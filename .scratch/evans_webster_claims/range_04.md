**Entry:** Formative Period (c. 1600 B.C.–A.D. 250)
**Source_lines:** 28395–28438 (abs. 2395–2438)
**Author:** John E. Clark
**Claim:** Archaeologists divide Mesoamerican history into Formative (also Preclassic), Classic, and Postclassic periods, each with Early/Middle/Late/Terminal subdivisions used for interregional comparison. When first proposed, the labels were developmental as well as chronological, with the Classic defined as climax of Lowland Maya civilization (carved monuments, calendrical inscriptions, stone temples); Formative was “nascent development” and Postclassic “denouement.” The scheme overgeneralizes Maya, Zapotec, and Teotihuacan trajectories; some scholars prefer Basin of Mexico “horizons” and numbered intermediate periods (Formative = Initial Ceramic, Early Horizon, First Intermediate), but most still use Formative terminology strictly as time rather than evolutionary rank.
**Quote:** "For descriptive and analytical convenience, archaeologists divide the long history of Mesoamerica into epochs designated as the Formative (also called the Preclassic), Classic, and Postclassic periods."

**Source_lines:** 28428–28480 (abs. 2428–2480)
**Author:** John E. Clark
**Claim:** Formative dates are given as 1600 B.C. to A.D. 250 in radiocarbon years. The start marks first ceramic technology and sedentary village life; the terminal date approximates the Maya emphasis on Long Count dates and calendrical monuments. Regional states arose mainly in Late and Terminal Formative from about 200 B.C.; early Olmecs of ~1100 B.C. may already have comprised three or more city-states. The Formative created Mesoamerica as a cultural area at near-maximal extension via spread of a “Mesoamerican pattern,” whereas the Classic mainly elaborated existing institutions without major spatial expansion.

### CLAIM | target: mesoamerica-culture-area | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** Formative Period (c. 1600 B.C.–A.D. 250)
**Source_lines:** 28489–28518 (abs. 2489–2518)
**Author:** John E. Clark
**Claim:** The Mesoamerican pattern that spread in the Formative comprised seven major elements: (1) sedentary village and town life; (2) basic cultigens (beans, avocados, chiles, maize); (3) intensive farming and water control; (4) public economies with surplus food and specialized luxury goods for long-distance exchange; (5) complex social/political systems (stratification, divine kingship, ceremonial centers, commemorative art); (6) a shared cosmology (quadripartite multi-layered cosmos, creation myths, pantheon, animism, life-death journey); (7) influence of these concepts on social norms. Genesis and spread of traits were temporally and spatially irregular; no single regional culture was responsible for all critical developments.
**Quote:** "The Formative period witnessed the creation of Mesoamerica as a cultural area and its near-maximal extension."

### CLAIM | target: mesoamerican-preclassic | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** Formative Period — Beginnings of Village Life
**Source_lines:** 28540–28587 (abs. 2540–2587)
**Author:** John E. Clark
**Claim:** Transition from seminomadic hunting/gathering to settled villages and ceramics occurred in the Early Formative, beginning c. 1600–1400 B.C. among cultures of northern Veracruz coastal lowlands, Gulf Coast Lowlands, coastal Chiapas/Guatemala (Soconusco), West Mexico highlands, and valleys of Oaxaca, Puebla, Morelos, and México. Toward end of Early Formative (~1000 B.C.), small agricultural villages appeared in highlands of Chiapas, Guatemala, El Salvador, and Maya Lowlands. Best data from Soconusco and Valley of Oaxaca; tropical lowland settlements were more extensive than highland ones. Highlands committed to maize agriculture, small villages, dispersed settlement, simple utilitarian pottery; lowland Soconusco villages larger/more clustered, with ranked societies by ~1400 B.C., mixed economy (root crops and maize plus wild fish/turtles/deer), fancy ceramics for feasting, and evidence for simple chiefdoms/shaman-chiefs (elaborate residences, ball courts, burials, differential imported goods).
**Quote:** "the tropical lowland societies were socially more complex than those of the highlands. In the Soconusco, villages were larger and more closely clustered"

### CLAIM | target: olmec | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** Formative Period — The Early Olmecs
**Source_lines:** 28588–28642 (abs. 2588–2642)
**Author:** John E. Clark
**Claim:** Olmec civilization arose by 1150 B.C. in the Gulf Lowlands; most spectacular capital was San Lorenzo Tenochtitlan (Veracruz), with massive earth-moving, multi-ton basalt sculpture, and possibly >10,000 inhabitants—large enough to be a true city. Unique “supernatural” art (were-jaguars, fire-serpents) spread across Mesoamerica c. 1100–1000 B.C. San Lorenzo–style objects (ceramic motifs, figurines, cylinder seals) appear spotty in Valley of Mexico, Guerrero, Oaxaca, Puebla, Tehuacan, northern Veracruz, Chiapas, coastal Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador—signaling Olmec ideological dominance. Most scholars concede San Lorenzo polity was a complex chiefdom if not Mesoamerica’s first state; contemporaries were smaller and may have allied with Olmecs. Nature/means of Olmec influence remain contested (exchange, marriage alliances, conquest all possible).
**Quote:** "Olmec civilization arose by 1150 B.C. in the Gulf Lowlands, with its most spectacular manifestation being the capital of San Lorenzo Tenochtitlan (Veracruz)."

### CLAIM | target: mesoamerican-preclassic | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** Formative Period — Regional Development of Complex Chiefdoms
**Source_lines:** 28652–28718 (abs. 2652–2718)
**Author:** John E. Clark
**Claim:** Middle–Late Formative saw complex chiefdoms centered on pyramid sites across Mesoamerica; most significant was spread of the Mesoamerican pattern into the Maya Lowlands. First pulse of chiefdoms linked to San Lorenzo–style influence (patchy clusters in Soconusco, Veracruz, possibly Oaxaca, Morelos, Puebla-Tlaxcala, Valley of Mexico), then demographic “infilling.” Maya Lowland Middle/Late Formative kept pace with or surpassed earlier-occupied regions—possibly because earliest large Maya communities remain deeply buried. Innovations: planned plazas with tall pyramids and ball courts; high-status pyramid burials with jade; beginning of “stela cult” and bas-relief narrative sculpture; shifts in luxury exchange. More productive maize (enlarged cob size) by Middle Formative enabled surplus; large chiefdoms sat on prime agricultural land. Polities typically had radius no greater than one day’s walk; rival equal-power chiefdoms changed toward Late Formative end with near-simultaneous regional-state formation via some interregional mechanism.
**Quote:** "Perhaps the most significant Middle Formative development was the spread of the Mesoamerican pattern to the Maya Lowlands."
