### CLAIM | target: mesoamerica-culture-area | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** Settlement Patterns and Settlement Systems  
**Source_lines:** 61445–61585  
**Author:** Gordon R. Willey  
**Claim:** After c. 1500 BCE, Mesoamerican settlement systems incorporated public mound platforms for temples/palaces as politico-religious centers, with commoner houses either concentrated (urban effect) or dispersed; concentration correlates with exceptionally fertile soils and intensive agriculture (irrigation, raised fields, terracing), while thinner/exhaustible soils favored dispersed farmsteads. Upland Central Mexico (Teotihuacan, Tula, Tenochtitlan) exemplifies dense urban settlement with deep soils and chinampa potential; Maya Lowlands exemplify forested thin soils, long fallow, and dispersed dwellings around politico-religious centers. Vaillant noted this basic dichotomy more than fifty years before the entry.  
**Quote:** "In regions where soils are exceptionally fertile and well suited to farming, or where intensive agricultural practices such as irrigation, raised field construction, or terracing were practical, dwelling units tended to be closely grouped around a center, producing an urban settlement effect."

### CLAIM | target: interregional-interactions-mesoamerica | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** South America, Interaction of Mesoamerica with  
**Source_lines:** 61771–61951  
**Author:** Karen Olsen Bruhns  
**Claim:** Contact between Mesoamerica and South America after initial settlement is rarely demonstrated archaeologically; overland down-the-line trade (from early Formative) is earliest; eastern coastal canoe trade grows Late Classic; Pacific deep-sea raft contact models lack Galapagos pre-Columbian presence and evidence that Ecuadorian rafts left sight of land willingly. No materials of Mesoamerican manufacture have been found in South America; southern gold at Copán, Altun Ha, and Chichén Itzá is small/portable. Metallurgy’s appearance in Mesoamerica Late Classic–Early Postclassic may reflect contact or value-system change; independent invention of West Mexican metallurgy remains possible.  
**Quote:** "On the basis of current archaeological and historical evidence, it appears that contact between Mesoamerica and South America was indirect"

**Source_lines:** 62300–62339
