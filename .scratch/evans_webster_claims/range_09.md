**Entry:** Toltec Culture — The Toltec Ideological Heritage
**Source_lines:** 70132–70161
**Author:** Mastache Flores and Cobean
**Claim:** Expansion of Toltec culture in the 9th–11th centuries reshaped ideology/religion across Mesoamerica. Toltecs introduced some Nahua gods among Maya and Central American peoples (esp. Quetzalcoatl; also Toltec-style Xipe Totec, Mictlantecuhtli). Gods and rites at Toltec Tula are presented as direct antecedents of Aztec religion; Tezcatlipoca (who supposedly defeated Quetzalcoatl at Tula) as prototype for Huitzilopochtli. **Quote:** "Tezcatlipoca, the god who supposedly defeated Quetzalcoatl at Tula, was the prototype for Huitzilopochtli, the paramount Aztec deity."

### CLAIM | target: west-mexico | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** West Mexico — Teuchitlán Classic
**Source_lines:** 74846–74879
**Author:** Weigand
**Claim:** Classic A.D. 200–700 circular architecture based on concentric circles from a radial center found nowhere else in Mesoamerica or world. Largest circles diameters >120 m or circumferences ≥400 m; precincts of 6–8 structures plus monumental ball courts (largest 135 m) and elite residential compounds. Best precincts: Teuchitlán, Loma Alta, Mesa Alta, Santa Quiteria (San Juan de los Arcos, Ahualulco badly damaged). Habitation zones often cover thousands of hectares of terraced countryside—marks nuclear area with hilltop fortifications at regular intervals/passes. Pseudo-cloisonné vessels may indicate early glyph-based recording. Wetland drained-field (chinampa-type) features ~32 km² documented. Obsidian mines: 1,265 at La Joya, >800 Primavera, ~150 San Juan de los Arcos; plus malachite/azurite and crystal quarries—Classic as regional civilization.
**Quote:** "This architectural format, based on concentric circles planned from a radical center, is found nowhere else in Mesoamerica or indeed in the world."

**Source_lines:** 72107–72383
**Author:** Helen Perlstein Pollard
**Claim:** Tzintzuntzan (“place of the hummingbird”) was capital of Tarascan Empire controlling large portion of West Mexico at Spanish contact; foremost Aztec enemy; civilization emerged after A.D. 1350; known mainly for last pre-Conquest century A.D. 1450–1520 under hereditary dynasty. **Source_lines:** 73989–74292

**Claim:** Projectiles (bows/arrows, spears, atlatls/darts, slings) vs shock weapons (swords, maces, staffs); before gunpowder projectiles typically secondary in complex warfare. Cacaxtla by A.D. 700: bladed spears + atlatlists; round forearm-strap shields. ### CLAIM | target: mesoamerican-writing-systems | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** Writing
**Source_lines:** 75432–75591
**Author:** Joyce Marcus
**Claim:** Restrictive definitions of writing (correspondence to speech, linear format, reading order, combinatorial grammar) preferred over broad ones that conflate writing with art (Kwakiutl, Olmec, Chavín). Writing invented multiple times; New World systems confined to Mesoamerica. Four principal systems: Zapotec c. 650 B.C. (earliest), Maya c. A.D. 100–200 (most complex, greatest speech correspondence), Mixtec c. A.D. 250–500, Aztec/Nahuatl c. A.D. 1200–1500; fifth poorly known (possibly Zoque) ~100 B.C. as Maya forerunner. All four combine pictographic, logographic, syllabic elements; no alphabetic system in Mesoamerica. Earliest signs: proper nouns and calendric day names; short assertions about captives, ancestors, genealogical position to legitimize rulers. Writing sometimes predated states: pre-state Zapotec 600–200 B.C. recorded names of sacrificed elite captives from endemic chiefly competition. Not all states had writing (Inca, Tarascans); do not define civilization by writing. Functions expand over time; early = political/military propaganda on stone; Aztecs expanded to economic tribute lists (Codex Mendoza). Ancient Mesoamerican literacy restricted to elite as esoteric knowledge distinguishing nobles from commoners; texts high propaganda content, not unbiased history.
**Quote:** "The four principal writing systems were Zapotec (c. 650 B.C.), Maya (c. A.D. 100—200), Mixtec (c. A.D. 250—500), and Aztec, or Nahuatl (c. A.D. 1200—1500)."

**Source_lines:** 71038–71303

### CLAIM | target: interregional-interactions-mesoamerica | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** Transport
**Source_lines:** 70605–70707
**Author:** Ross Hassig
**Claim:** Mesoamerican land transport limited to foot traffic—no wheels (except figurines) or draft animals. Professional tlamemes (Nahuatl) inherited occupation, trained from age 5; hired by town; stage of 5 leagues (~13–16 miles/one day); average load ~23 kg with packframe/tumpline; long-distance = relay system. Canoes only truly efficient transport: lakes (Valley of Mexico, Pátzcuaro, Atitlán) and coasts; square-bow dugouts 3.5–30 m; one man poles one-ton cargo at walking speed—~40× more efficient than porters in competing areas; no sails. Coastal shipping moved bulky goods (e.g., salt) hundreds of miles. Net effect: cities controlled relatively small economic hinterlands unless water-linked.
**Quote:** "a single man could pole a one-ton cargo at the same speed as a walking man, making canoes forty times more efficient than porters in the limited areas in which they competed."

### CLAIM | target: zapotec | action: UPDATE
**Entry:** Zapotec Culture and Religion
**Source_lines:** 76958–77014
**Author:** Joyce Marcus
**Claim:** Animistic religion: supreme uncreated creator (no images, no direct contact); principal interactive supernatural Lightning (Cociyo) with companions Wind (Pee/Pi), Clouds (Zaa), Hail (Quiezabi), Rain (Nica)—Lightning as angry face of Sky; Earthquake (Xoo) angry face of Earth.
