## Overview

Cartographic example:

![The First Farmers — global domestication and spread of agriculture](assets/atlases/snow-history-of-the-world-map-by-map-2018/snow-history-p025-first-farmers.png)

*Map from [[sources/snow-history-of-the-world-map-by-map-2018]]*

The Neolithic ("New Stone Age") is defined not by a date but by an **economy**:
the transition from foraging (hunting, fishing, gathering) to food production
(cultivating domesticated plants and herding domesticated animals). It is the
period in which the human relationship to the natural world inverted — the
balances "recognizable in the Pleistocene shifted in favour of the survival and
expansion of human populations at the expense of the rest of the planet"
(Barker & Goucher). Food production underwrote the rise of [[sedentism]],
villages, population growth from perhaps 6 million people at the end of the
Pleistocene toward the billions of today, and ultimately the cities and states
that dominate later world history.

Crucially, **the Neolithic is regionally diachronous**: it did not begin
everywhere at once. The conventional 9,500–3,000 BCE bracket reflects Southwest
Asia, the first and best-studied hearth. Elsewhere food production began later
(China and the Americas in the early–mid Holocene), continued diffusing into
the first millennium CE (much of sub-Saharan Africa and the Pacific), or — in
the case of pre-domestication cultivation — has roots reaching back into the
late Pleistocene. The period therefore overlaps the [[mesolithic]] before it and
the [[chalcolithic]] and [[early-bronze-age]] after it, differently in each
region. See [[agricultural-origins]] for the process and
[[why-did-agriculture-begin]] for the central debate.

## Major Developments

**The independent hearths of domestication.** Food production arose
*independently* in several "hearths," each with a distinct domesticate package
(Barker & Goucher; Childe's older single-origin diffusionism is rejected):
- **Southwest Asia** (the Fertile Crescent) — emmer and einkorn wheat, barley,
  legumes, sheep, goats, pigs, cattle. The classic model; see Cycle 3.
- **China** — broomcorn and foxtail millet in the north, rice in the Yangtze
  basin; pigs, soybeans. See [[early-agriculture-china]].
- **South Asia** — a northwestern Neolithic package (Mehrgarh) plus indigenous
  Ganges rice and southern millets/pulses; the zebu.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa** — Sahelian cattle (animals before crops), then millet,
  sorghum, African rice, tef, cowpea, oil palm. See
  [[early-african-food-production]].
- **The Americas** — maize (from teosinte) in Mexico; manioc in Amazonia; quinoa
  and camelids (llama, alpaca) in the Andes; squash/chenopod/sunflower in
  eastern North America.
- **New Guinea** — banana, taro, sugarcane, in an indigenous vegeculture/
  arboriculture system (Kuk). See [[early-agriculture-southeast-asia-pacific]].

**European farming spread (Champion et al. 1984).** The initial spread into Europe combined colonization (especially SE–NW axis and central Mediterranean) with indigenous contributions and local adaptation. Primary settlements favoured optimal soils: loess deposits for Linearbandkeramik (LBK) longhouse villages in temperate Europe ("well drained and easily tillable") and light fertile valley soils for substantial, long-lived Balkan tells (e.g. Karanovo, Azmak). "the apparent role played by direct colonization in many areas... Even with colonization there may regularly have been an indigenous contribution of population and skills; cattle for example seem to have been domesticated (at least on morphological criteria) earlier in Greece... than in the Near East." Secondary colonization later moved onto interfluves and poorer soils; plough agriculture (ard marks under barrows) facilitated this from the early fourth millennium. See dedicated [[processes/spread-of-farming-europe|Spread of Farming in Europe]] and [[concepts/linearbandkeramik|Linearbandkeramik (LBK)]].

Early European Neolithic subsistence was a "high-yielding, diverse and above all secure blend" of cereals (dominant) and livestock, with evidence for manuring, legume use, permanent fields, storage pits, and forest clearance (long-lived clearances up to 400 years documented in pollen). Animal management included stalling in some zones (Alpine foreland), castrated cattle for traction from the sixth millennium, and a primary early focus on meat (high juvenile culling); milk/wool secondary products gained significance later. Environmental side-effects (erosion, heath, peat) emerged with intensification. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch. 5)

**Domestication as a slow, two-way process.** Domestication was not a single
invention but a protracted coevolution. "Pre-domestication cultivation" — people
sowing, tending, and harvesting still-wild plants — preceded morphological
domestication by centuries or millennia in both Southwest Asia and China. And
domestication reshaped humans (diet, disease, settlement, social structure) as
much as it reshaped plants and animals — the "mutual domestication" theme. See
[[domestication]].

**Sedentism, villages, and households.** A developed commitment to farming was
usually associated with permanent agglomerated settlements, though the two could
occur separately (sedentary foragers without farming; farming without villages).
The nuclear-family household emerged as the characteristic residential and
labour unit across many regions, but communal and compound forms also flourished
(see [[neolithic-communities]]).

**The downside: health and inequality.** The transition to dense, sedentary,
cereal-dependent life carried heavy costs — nutrient deficiencies, infectious
disease, dental pathology, heavy repetitive labour, and (in some regions)
increased interpersonal violence. Agriculture was "not easy street" (Roberts).
At the same time, long-term investment in land created issues of ownership and
inheritance that became "the seedbed for emergent inequalities" (Bogaard).

**Pottery and secondary products.** Pottery appears very early among some late
Pleistocene foragers (China, ~18,000 BP; Jomon Japan, ~14,000 BCE) *before*
agriculture, and later as part of the "Neolithic package" elsewhere — its
association with farming is contingent, not necessary. The "secondary products
revolution" (Sherratt) — exploiting live animals for milk, wool, traction, and
riding — unfolded later and unevenly. See [[secondary-products-revolution]].

**Retrospective from Bronze Age sources (Bogucki & Crabtree eds. 2004 Range 01):** In central Europe, Neolithic crops (emmer, einkorn, barley + flax/peas/lentils) and animals (cattle dominant, then sheep/goats/pigs) continued with intensification in the Final Neolithic/Bronze transition: new species (spelt, millet, broad beans, oats); "an increase in the exploitation of the horse—which remained fairly rare after its introduction in the Final Neolithic"; and emphasis on secondary products (milk/dairy, wool, traction via ards/ploughs) enabling diversification, pastoralism/transhumance, and dispersed settlement. "The Bronze Age witnessed an intensification in the agricultural practices carried down from the Neolithic, a process that began in the Final Neolithic with the introduction of a number of important innovations sometimes termed the “Secondary Products Revolution”." Barley may have increased for alcoholic beverages in rituals. These changes produced the mixed farming base seen in MBA sites (e.g. Feudvar). (Grounded in Bogucki & Crabtree 2004, Range 01 Central Europe and SE Europe articles.) This reinforces the process page and links Neolithic foundations to later complexity.

**The Near Eastern pottery-Neolithic sequence (CAH detail).**
[[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. VII, Mellaart) lays out the
classic ceramic-culture succession of northern Mesopotamia and the Zagros that the
wiki had not previously detailed:
- **Aceramic beginnings** in the Levant ([[jericho|Jericho]] PPNA/PPNB) and the
  Zagros (Jarmo, Tepe Guran, Tepe Sarab), with the **earliest Near Eastern pottery
  produced on the Anatolian plateau** at [[catalhoyuk|Çatal Hüyük]] from ~6750 BCE.
- **Hassuna** culture of northern Iraq — the first widespread painted/incised
  pottery village culture of the Mesopotamian piedmont.
- **Samarra** culture — technically and artistically advanced painted pottery with
  naturalistic motifs; at **Tell es-Sawwan** it is associated with a possible
  temple and some of the **earliest evidence for irrigation**, carrying farming
  down onto the drier middle-Tigris plain.
- **Halaf** culture — a brilliant polychrome painted-pottery horizon spread across
  northern Mesopotamia and Syria (Arpachiyah, with its *tholoi*), immediately
  preceding the southern **Ubaid** expansion that leads toward the first cities
  (see [[chalcolithic]] and [[urban-revolution]]).
These cultures were knit together by long-distance **obsidian trade** from central
Anatolia, documented reaching the Levant as early as the late Natufian — see
[[trade-network-formation]].

## Key Actors

- **Natufian** communities (Levant) — semi-sedentary late-glacial foragers, the
  proximate antecedents of SW Asian farming (see [[mesolithic]]).
- **Pre-Pottery Neolithic (PPNA/PPNB)** communities of Southwest Asia —
  [[jericho|Jericho]], 'Ain Ghazal, Göbekli Tepe; and the great Anatolian town of
  [[catalhoyuk|Çatal Hüyük]].
- **Jomon** (Japan) and **Yayoi** (Japan) cultures *(Cycle 4)*.
- **Linearbandkeramik (LBK)** — the first farmers of temperate Europe *(Cycle 6)*. See [[concepts/linearbandkeramik|Linearbandkeramik (LBK)]] for details on loess settlement, longhouses, and colonization model (Champion et al. 1984, Ch. 5).
- **Starčevo–Criș and Vinča** cultures (central/eastern Balkans) — the
  southeast-European farming sequence documented by [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH
  Vol. III, Part 1]] (Garašanin, Dumitrescu), the bridge by which farming reached
  temperate Europe ahead of the LBK. Notably, in the **Iron Gates** the late
  Mesolithic **Schela Cladovei** culture had developed antler hoes and incipient
  cultivation that was *arrested by the arrival of Starčevo* — a clear case of the
  porous foraging–farming frontier. See [[balkans]].
- **Yangshao** and **Dawenkou** cultures (Yellow River, China) — the painted-pottery
  villages and lower-river mortuary-hierarchy societies that begin the sequence to the first
  Chinese state. See [[yangshao-culture]], [[dawenkou-culture]], and
  [[early-china-state-formation]] (from [[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Liu 2004]]).
  [[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook (2017)]] label the combined Middle and Late
  Neolithic of the [[east-asian-heartland-region|Heartland]] (c. 3500–2000 BCE) the
  **[[jade-age|"Jade Age"]]** — defined by ritual jade (the [[hongshan-culture|Hongshan]]
  pig-dragon, the [[liangzhu-culture|Liangzhu]] *cong*/*bi*) rather than metal, the long
  prelude to bronze ritual.
- Early **Bantu**-speaking farmer-herders, agents of agricultural spread in
  sub-Saharan Africa *(Cycle 5)*.
- **Khirokitia culture** (aceramic Neolithic I of Cyprus) — a *sui generis* island
  Neolithic of round **tholoi** houses with stone footings and mud-brick/domed
  upper walls, whose source CAH (Ch. IX(e), Catling) could not trace; an early case
  of insular colonization and divergence. The Nile valley's own Neolithic
  (Merimda, the Fayum) opens the [[predynastic-egypt|Predynastic Egyptian]]
  sequence.

## Geographic Scope

Genuinely global by the end of the period, but profoundly uneven. Southwest Asia,
China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes are the most intensively studied hearths;
Europe has by far the richest data set for *social* interpretation; sub-Saharan
Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific are the least understood and the latest
to transition. The "foraging–farming frontier" was porous and long-lived almost
everywhere: farmers and foragers coexisted, traded, and intermarried for
centuries (e.g. Brześć Kujawski, Poland).

## Transition

**Entry:** the Neolithic emerges out of the [[mesolithic]] as late-glacial
foragers in several regions intensify their use of wild cereals and begin
managing plants and animals, accelerated in Southwest Asia by the
[[younger-dryas]] climate stress *(see mesolithic page)*.

**Exit:** the Neolithic gives way to the [[chalcolithic]] (copper-working) and
[[early-bronze-age]] as metallurgy, the plough, the secondary-products complex,
and growing surpluses feed the urban revolution and the first
[[agriculture-and-urbanism|cities]]. Because the period is diachronous, this exit
occurs at very different absolute dates by region.

## Historiography

**Source quality.** The Neolithic is, by definition, a pre-literate period
everywhere it begins; **archaeology is the primary evidence**, now massively
augmented by archaeological science. The four transformative method-streams
(each a thematic chapter in this volume) are: **archaeobotany/zooarchaeology**
(carbonized seeds, animal bone, recovered by systematic flotation),
**[[archaeogenetics]]** (modern + ancient DNA of humans, plants, animals),
**stable-isotope analysis** (diet and migration from body chemistry), and
**organic-residue analysis** (milk, fat, cereal traces on pots and tools).
Starch-residue analysis has been a particular breakthrough for tropical root
crops (yam, taro, sweet potato) that leave no carbonized seeds.

**Dating.** Radiocarbon (14C), introduced in the mid-20th century and
calibrated since, is the backbone of Neolithic chronology and is what first
revealed the true antiquity and *independence* of the various hearths (the first
European Neolithic 14C dates pushed Greek sites from an assumed ~3000 BCE back
to ~6000 BCE). AMS dating of single seeds now allows direct dating of
domesticates. Dates in this period are generally reliable to the century or
better, but archaeogenetic and residue datings are revised frequently.

**Major debates.**
- *Why* foragers took up farming at all, given its costs — see
  [[why-did-agriculture-begin]] (climate "push" vs. social/ideological "pull").
- *Migration vs. adoption* — did farming spread by farmers moving (demic
  diffusion) or by foragers adopting domesticates (cultural diffusion)? The
  answer varies by region; the European "leapfrog colonization + assimilation"
  model is the best-supported by ancient DNA.
- The *unsatisfactory term "Neolithic"* itself — see [[neolithic-revolution]].
- Whether sedentism preceded or followed food production (it can do either).

**Recent revisionism (last 30 years).** The biggest shift is the collapse of the
old linear, one-way, single-origin "ladder of progress" model (Westropp 1872 →
Childe). Current scholarship emphasizes: multiple independent and *partial*
pathways; "failed" experiments and reversions to foraging; pre-domestication
cultivation deep in the late Pleistocene; the porousness of the forager–farmer
frontier; and the ritual/social rather than purely economic motivations of early
cultivators (cereals valued for feasting and beer as much as bread). See
[[neolithic-revolution]] and [[why-did-agriculture-begin]].

## Collection Coverage Note

**Strong.** *The Cambridge World History* Vol. II (*A World with Agriculture*) is
the wiki's keystone source for this period and gives genuinely global,
state-of-the-2015 coverage — including the regions the collection is otherwise
weak on (sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, the Americas). Per
CLAUDE.md, the African, Southeast Asian, and American material here is the
*best the collection will offer* for the Neolithic and is flagged as such on the
relevant regional pages; later phases (CAH, specialist prehistory in Phase 11)
will deepen Southwest Asia, Europe, and the Near East but will not match Vol. II's
breadth for the under-resourced regions. Honest caveat: archaeogenetics and
residue dating move fast, so specific first-appearance dates here are provisional.

**Champion et al. 1984 (Ch. 1) European contribution:** Foundational post-radiocarbon textbook for the European Neolithic specifically. Explicitly abandons Three Age / culture-historical organization in favour of thematic analysis of settlement, subsistence, exchange and social organization. Documents the radiocarbon lengthening of the Neolithic to "millennia rather than just centuries" and the consequent rethinking of Childean diffusionism. Provides the primary grounding for all European Neolithic pages on adaptive behaviour, site hierarchies, and the inseparability of economy and society. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~390-410, ~95-110, ~510-535)

## The Neolithic's Long Shadow (Diamond)

[[diamond-guns-germs-and-steel-1997|Jared Diamond]] makes the uneven geography of this
period the ultimate cause of world history's later divergences: the few regions with rich
domesticable biota (above all the [[near-east|Fertile Crescent]] and [[china|China]]) gained
food production earliest, and Eurasia's east–west axis let that package spread across shared
latitudes far faster than the north–south axes of the Americas and Africa allowed. The
resulting head start in population, germs, technology, writing, and states is, on his account,
why post-1492 collisions ran as they did. See [[guns-germs-and-steel]], [[continental-axes]],
and [[geographic-determinism]].
