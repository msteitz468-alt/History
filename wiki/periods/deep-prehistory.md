## Overview

Deep Prehistory encompasses all of Earth's history before the first known
stone tools, dated to approximately 3.3 million years ago (the Lomekwian
industry from Kenya, though this date is disputed; Oldowan tools are more
firmly dated to ~2.6 million years ago). This is the period before the
*Homo* genus in its familiar forms, covering the australopithecines and
earlier bipedal hominins.

The history of this period belongs primarily to paleoanthropology and
evolutionary biology rather than archaeology in any traditional sense. The
human lineage was not yet recognizably "human" in cultural terms. No
cumulative cultural learning — the defining feature of our species for
Christian and the Big History framework — is yet detectable in the
archaeological record.

## Major Developments

- Divergence of the human and chimpanzee lineages (~6–7 million BP), in Africa
- Evolution of bipedalism in australopithecines (Australopithecus afarensis;
  famous specimen "Lucy" dates to ~3.2 million BP)
- Brain size increase within the australopithecine lineages
- First appearance of the *Homo* genus ~2.5 million BP (Homo habilis)
  — this marks the transition into [[early-prehistory]]
- Appearance of the first stone tools: Oldowan industry ~2.6 million BP,
  associated with Homo habilis; some claims for earlier Lomekwian tools
  ~3.3 million BP

## Key Actors

No individual or group actors identifiable at this level of the record.
Key species: *Australopithecus afarensis*, *Australopithecus africanus*,
*Paranthropus* spp., early *Homo habilis*.

## Geographic Scope

All evidence for this period is from Africa, consistent with the scientific
consensus that the human lineage evolved in Africa. There is no evidence of
hominin presence outside Africa during this period.

## Transition

This period ends with the appearance of identifiable stone tool industries
and the *Homo* genus (~2.5–3.3 million BP). These developments define the
opening of [[early-prehistory]].

## Historiography

**Source quality**: There are no texts. Evidence comes entirely from
fossilized skeletal remains, dating by radiometric and stratigraphic methods.
The fossil record is extremely fragmentary and concentrated in East Africa
(Rift Valley sites) and South Africa (cave sites). Many discoveries reshape
the narrative; the field has been transformed in the last 30 years.

**Dating methods**: Potassium-argon and argon-argon radiometric dating for
volcanic strata; uranium-lead dating; paleomagnetism; comparative anatomy.
Confidence intervals are wide — often ±100,000 years or more.

**Major debates**: The taxonomic relationships between australopithecine
species; whether Australopithecus sediba is a direct human ancestor; the
date and number of transitions within the *Homo* lineage; whether
Lomekwian tools really precede Oldowan and what they imply for hominin
cognition.

**Recent revisionism**: The 2015 discovery of *Homo naledi* (South Africa)
and subsequent dating to 236,000–335,000 BP complicated the model of a linear
transition from Australopithecus to Homo. New evidence continues to emerge
rapidly; any summary risks near-term obsolescence.

**Collection coverage**: The Bibliotheca Alexandrina's archaeological section
likely covers this period moderately through Cambridge series and specialist
volumes, but it has not yet been ingested. This period page will be
substantially updated during Phase 11 ingestion (Prehistoric and
Archaeological Depth).

## Collection Coverage Note

[[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (ingested 2026-06-11) opens with
D. L. Linton & F. Moseley's geological framing — "the perspective of history
begins with the origin of the earth" — and the radiometric age of the earth
(~4,500 Myr), a useful articulation of deep-time perspective and dating method
(see [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]]). However, its treatment of
hominin populations (Ch. V, Hughes & Brothwell) uses mid-20th-century taxonomy
("Pithecanthropines," "Australopithecines") that is **superseded** and should
not be carried into this page as current; it is retained only as a record of the
1960s state of the field. No `[CONTRADICTION]` flag is warranted — the divergence
is one of vintage, not of live scholarly dispute.

Coverage of Deep Prehistory from ingested sources: **weak**. The *Cambridge
World History* Vol. 1 Introduction (ingested 2026-06-10) provides only a
sketch of this period; the focus of that volume is the Paleolithic era of
*Homo sapiens*. Phase 11 specialist sources will be required for fuller
coverage. The **[[cambridge-history-of-africa-v1|Cambridge History of Africa, Vol. 1]]**
(J. Desmond Clark, ed., 1982) adds an authoritative older treatment of the **origins and
evolution of the African Hominidae** — Africa as the hearth of human evolution — though,
like CAH I, its hominin taxonomy and dating predate the major late-20th- and 21st-century
discoveries.
