# Sengoku Period (the Warring States of Japan)

The Sengoku ("Warring States") period was the century-plus of endemic civil war that followed the
collapse of [[ashikaga-shogunate|Ashikaga]] central authority in the Ōnin War (1467–77). The shōgun and
the old provincial governors gave way to self-made warlords — the *daimyō* — who built castle-centered
domains and fought continuously for territory, while a culture of *gekokujō* ("the low overthrowing the
high") overturned the established order.

## Overview

The era is defined by the breakdown of the central order and the long, violent process of reunification
driven by the "three unifiers": [[oda-nobunaga|Oda Nobunaga]], who shattered the old powers and exploited
firearms; [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Toyotomi Hideyoshi]], who completed the military unification and reorganized
society; and [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu]], whose victory at [[battle-of-sekigahara-1600|Sekigahara]]
(1600) and [[siege-of-osaka-1614|destruction of the Toyotomi]] (1615) established the lasting
[[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa]] peace. It overlaps the [[azuchi-momoyama-period|Azuchi–Momoyama period]] in
its final decades.

## Transition

It opens with the failure of the [[ashikaga-shogunate|Ashikaga bakufu]] and closes with the Tokugawa
settlement that ended the wars and inaugurated the [[edo-period|Edo period]].

## Historiography

The standard narrative — represented in older form by [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937|Sadler]] — has
been enriched by social and military history (the "military revolution" debate over firearms at
[[battle-of-nagashino-1575|Nagashino]]) and by reassessment of the unifiers' state-building.

## Collection Coverage Note

The Cambridge History of Japan and the unification biographies give moderate coverage; the period is
better served than most of the Japan narrative in the collection.
