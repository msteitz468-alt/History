# Chalcolithic

## Overview

The **Chalcolithic** ("Copper-Stone Age," ~5,500–3,300 BCE) is the transitional
period between the [[neolithic|Neolithic]] and the [[early-bronze-age|Early
Bronze Age]] in the Near East and southeastern Europe, defined by the first
regular use of **copper** alongside continuing stone tools. It is less a clean
chronological block than a regionally diachronous threshold: it overlaps the Late
Neolithic at its start and the earliest cities at its end. In Mesopotamia it
spans the Ubaid and Uruk developments that culminate in the first cities; in the
Levant it includes distinctive cultures (Ghassulian); in the Balkans it includes
the spectacular metalworking of Varna.

The period's significance is that the social and economic preconditions of
[[urbanization]] — craft specialization, the [[secondary-products-revolution|
secondary products revolution]], surplus storage, long-distance exchange in
metals and prestige goods, and growing social differentiation — all intensify
here, immediately before the urban threshold treated in
[[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]].

## Major Developments

- **Copper metallurgy** — cold-working then smelting and casting of copper;
  Varna (Bulgaria, ~4600–4200 BCE) yields the world's oldest large gold hoard,
  signaling marked status differentiation.
- **Secondary products** — traction, wool, dairying, and riding intensify (the
  [[secondary-products-revolution]] documented in [[barker-cwh-v2-2015|CWH II]]).
- **Proto-urban aggregation** — in southern Mesopotamia the Ubaid temple-centered
  towns and then the Uruk expansion set the stage for [[uruk|Uruk]]'s growth into
  the first city by the end of the 4th millennium. [[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH
  Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. VIII, Mallowan) documents the mechanism at
  [[eridu|Eridu]], where **eighteen superimposed temples** (levels 18–6 spanning
  the whole Ubaid) show a shrine growing in place by continuous rebuilding on
  consecrated ground — the archaeological backbone of the temple-centred origin of
  the Sumerian city. The Ubaid ceramic sequence (Eridu ware = Ubaid 1 → Hajji
  Muhammad = Ubaid 2 → Ubaid 3–4) leads directly into the **Uruk/Protoliterate**
  horizon and the [[writing-and-record-keeping|invention of writing]].
- **Craft specialization and exchange** — pottery, metallurgy, and seal use point
  toward administrative complexity.

## Key Actors

- Ubaid and Uruk cultural traditions (southern Mesopotamia).
- Ghassulian (southern Levant); Varna and the Balkan Copper Age cultures.

## Geographic Scope

Historically active: Mesopotamia, the Levant, Anatolia, the Iranian plateau, and
southeastern Europe / the Balkans. Elsewhere the "Chalcolithic" label is applied
loosely or not at all; independent metallurgical and urban trajectories in East
Asia, the Andes, and Mesoamerica follow different chronologies.

## Transition

Follows the [[neolithic|Neolithic]] (agricultural village life; see
[[agricultural-origins]]). Gives way to the [[early-bronze-age|Early Bronze Age]]
when tin-bronze alloying and the first cities and states become established —
the [[urban-revolution]] threshold. The boundary is conventional and regionally
staggered.

## Historiography

**Source quality.** Archaeological only; no writing until the very end of the
period (proto-cuneiform at Uruk). Metallurgical and mortuary evidence (Varna)
and settlement survey carry the interpretation. Dating is radiocarbon-based.

**Debates.** Whether "Chalcolithic" is a useful period at all, given its
regional variability and overlap with Neolithic and Bronze Age; whether copper
use marks a genuine social threshold or merely a technological one; and how
directly the [[secondary-products-revolution]] drove social differentiation
(revised by lipid-residue dating — see [[barker-cwh-v2-2015|CWH II]]).

**Collection coverage.** Weak. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina covers the Chalcolithic
only at the edges — via [[barker-cwh-v2-2015|CWH II]] (secondary products, late
Neolithic) and the urban run-up in [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH III]]. Dedicated
Bronze/Copper-Age archaeology and the Cambridge Ancient History (Phase 2) will
deepen it; until then this page is a thematic placeholder, not a full overview.
