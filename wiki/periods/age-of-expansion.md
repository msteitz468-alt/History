# Age of Expansion (1700–1800 CE)

## Overview

The eighteenth century is the closing phase of the early modern construction of
a global world and the threshold of the industrial and revolutionary
nineteenth. It is the last age, in Jeremy Black's phrase, of "a truly diverse
and varied global military situation" — before the steam-and-steel asymmetries
of the 1800s — and at the same time the moment when a recognizably modern
international system, and a handful of dominant powers (Britain, Russia, Qing
China), began to take shape.

Two large movements define the period. First, the **maturing of the oceanic
empires and their wars**: the Anglo-French struggle from North America to India
made the Seven Years' War (1756–63) the first conflict often called a "global
war," and British naval mastery, public finance, and amphibious power-projection. National Atlantics reached peaks and crises; reform (Bourbons), revolution (Atlantic Revolutions), and shifts (Dutch to transit) mark the era's Atlantic dynamics (Greene & Morgan).
gave Britain the decisive edge by 1759–63. Second, the **eighteenth-century
reordering of the Asian land empires**: the near-total collapse of the Mughal
Empire, the fall of Safavid Persia, the rise of the Marathas, Afghan, Burmese,
Nepali, and other regional powers, and the Qing conquest of Xinjiang and Central
Asia — a landward transformation usually ignored in accounts centered on the
Atlantic.

Beneath the wars, the Atlantic slave and **plantation system reached its
apex** (the great majority of the some twelve million enslaved Africans were
transported after 1700), the silver-integrated world economy matured, and East
Asian "industrious revolutions" (Sugihara and Wong, treated in a later cycle)
generated labor-intensive growth on a different path from European
industrialization. The period closes with the Enlightenment and the **Atlantic
Revolutions** — American independence (1776–83), the Haitian revolution begun in
1791, and the French Revolution from 1789 — whose new ideas of rights began to
reshape forced-labor regimes and migration in the following century.

## Major Developments

- **The first global wars**: the War of the Spanish Succession, the Austrian
  Succession, and above all the **[[seven-years-war-1756|Seven Years' War]]**; British naval
  supremacy confirmed at Lagos and Quiberon Bay (1759) — fought under the logic of the
  **[[balance-of-power]]** (codified at Utrecht, 1713).
- **The [[enlightenment|Enlightenment]]**: the eighteenth-century "Age of Reason" — critical
  reason and science applied to religion, politics, and society — and the intellectual seedbed
  of the Atlantic revolutions.
- **The first successful colonial war of independence**: the **[[american-revolution|American
  Revolution]]** (1775–83), in which a [[continental-army|Continental Army]] under
  [[washington-george|Washington]] — surviving the disasters of 1776 and professionalized at
  [[valley-forge-1777|Valley Forge]] — held on until the [[franco-american-alliance-1778|French
  alliance]] and French sea power turned the war, ending at [[siege-of-yorktown-1781|Yorktown]].
  It also founded a national army and a durable settlement of civilian control over the
  military (see the [[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/american-revolutionary-war|campaign analysis]]).
- **Collapse of the Asian gunpowder empires**: Mughal disintegration after 1707,
  Safavid fall (1722), Maratha ascendancy, and the British conquest of Bengal
  (from 1757) and defeat of Tipu Sultan of Mysore (1799).
- **Qing expansion**: the destruction of the Zunghars and conquest of Muslim
  Central Asia in the 1750s.
- **Russian imperial expansion and Enlightenment**: Under **[[catherine-the-great|Catherine the Great]]**, Russia annexed Crimea (1783), won decisive victories against the Ottoman Empire (Treaty of Kuchuk Kainarji, 10 July 1774: cession of Azov and Black Sea strip, recognition of Crimean independence, right to build Black Sea fleet, merchant passage of Dardanelles, Orthodox representations in Constantinople), and partitioned Poland (1772, 1793, 1795) alongside Prussia and Austria (see **[[first-partition-of-poland-1772|Partitions of Poland]]**). Despite Catherine's Enlightenment rhetoric (manifested in the **[[legislative-commission-1767|Legislative Commission]]** and Bolshoi Nakaz), she brutally suppressed the **[[pugachev-rebellion-1773|Pugachev Rebellion]]** (1773–1774) — the largest 18th-c. uprising, which mobilized Cossacks, Turkic peoples, factory serfs, and central peasants under the Peter III pretender myth after the 1762 noble emancipation manifesto broke the reciprocal service justification for serfdom — and codified the absolute authority of the nobility over the serfs via the 1785 Charter. The Russian Enlightenment flourished with figures like **[[lomonosov-mikhail|Mikhail Lomonosov]]** (Academy of Sciences), Moscow University (1755), and proliferation of journals/private presses (1783), but ended in censorship and repression under the impact of the French Revolution, targeting critics like **[[radishchev-alexander|Alexander Radishchev]]** (*Journey from St Petersburg to Moscow*, 1790 → Siberian exile) and **[[novikov-nikolai|Nikolai Novikov]]** (imprisoned). Population grew from ~10–11M (1700) to >40M (1790s) through natural increase and annexations; serfdom intensified (5.6M male serfs, 56% of peasantry) with shift to corvée and New Russia colonization (Odessa founded 1794). Post-Pugachev provincial reforms (1775 Statute) addressed under-government. (Freeze 2002, ll. 4131–5073)
  From Lieven (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II, ch.1, ll. 4305–4317): Baltic provinces opened direct trade routes to Europe, contributing greatly to 18th-c. economy. Ukraine and steppe territories equivalent to New Worlds of British/Spanish empires; Odessa (founded 1794) became world grain-exporting port (population 630,000 by 1914). Poland “biggest thorn in Petersburg’s flesh” yet contributed to economy and served as glacis. “Finland was annexed to enhance the security of St Petersburg, and military and geopolitical factors were also behind initial acquisitions.” (ll. ~4307). Peter I and successors achieved effective military-fiscal state apparatus enabling great-power status 1700–1815 (ll. 4323). “Tsarist Russia was a worthy member of this imperial ‘club’... the tsarist regime directed one of the most successful examples of territorial expansion in history.” (ll. 4275–4277). “Until the emergence of Japan in the twentieth century, it was the only example of a non-Western polity which had challenged effectively the might of the European great powers.” (ll. 4295). Westernisation of ruling elites a core characteristic uniting the era (ll. 4155).
- **Apex of Atlantic slavery and plantation production**: peak volumes of the
  slave trade; the plantation complex at its most extensive.
- **Industrious revolutions**: labor- and household-intensive intensification in
  East and South Asia (treated in the production cycle).
- **The Atlantic Revolutions and the Enlightenment**: new concepts of rights and
  the person that would transform politics, slavery, and migration after 1800.

## Key Actors

- Maritime powers: Britain and France (and Spain); the
  [[english-east-india-company|English East India Company]] and
  [[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] as territorial as well as commercial powers.
- Land powers: Qing China, the Maratha confederacy, the Mughal successor states,
  Durrani Afghanistan, and an expanding Russian Empire under **[[catherine-the-great|Catherine the Great]]**.
- Revolutionary actors: the thirteen colonies/United States, the enslaved
  insurgents of Saint-Domingue, and Revolutionary France.

## Geographic Scope

Worldwide but with shifting centers: the **Atlantic** is the engine of the slave
and plantation economy; **India** becomes the central theater of European
territorial empire-building for the first time; **Central Asia** is absorbed by
the Qing; and the **Pacific** is opened to systematic European exploration
(Cook). North America's political future is settled in Britain's favor by 1763,
then reopened by revolution.

## Transition

**From the [[early-modern|Early Modern]] period:** the consolidation of oceanic
empires, the maturing plantation and silver economies, and the onset of the
Asian land empires' eighteenth-century crisis. **To the Long 19th Century
(1800):** the Atlantic Revolutions, the beginnings of industrialization, and the
shift from a multipolar global military order toward European (and especially
British) dominance mark the threshold. The standing transition **"1789 and the
Atlantic Revolutions"** belongs to the close of this period.

## Historiography

- **The Great Divergence**: the eighteenth century is the crux of the debate
  over when Europe (or northwest Europe) pulled decisively ahead of the most
  advanced regions of Asia. CWH VI's contributors (Black on warfare, Sugihara
  and Wong on industrious revolutions) generally locate the divergence late and
  emphasize Asian dynamism into the 1700s.
- **"The first global war"**: the appropriateness of that label for the Seven
  Years' War is itself debated, since the conflict largely excluded East Asia,
  where the militarily decisive event was the Qing conquest of the Zunghars.
- **Contingency of British ascendancy**: Black stresses how little was inevitable
  about British global dominance even in the 1740s–50s.

## Collection Coverage Note

As with the [[early-modern|Early Modern]] period, only **Book 2 of CWH VI**
("Patterns of Change") is in the collection; the empire-by-empire foundations
(Book 1) are absent, leaving the Mughal successor states, Safavid Persia, the
Qing, and the Russian and Iberian empires without dedicated pages here. The
standing collection map rates the post-1700 world as a critical gap; CWH VI's
thematic chapters give strong coverage of warfare, trade, slavery, production,
and religion but not of the period's political narratives region by region. The gap on Russia and Eastern Europe has been addressed by the ingestion of Riasanovsky (2000).
