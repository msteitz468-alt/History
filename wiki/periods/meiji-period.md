# Meiji Period (1868–1912)

The reign of Emperor **Meiji** — the era of **deliberate, state-directed modernization** in
which [[japan|Japan]] transformed itself from a [[edo-period|secluded feudal society]] into
the first non-Western industrial **great power**. "The change from unthinking acceptance of
traditional attitudes to deliberate espousal of new goals marked a decisive break with the
past." From Mason & Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]] (ch. 15).

## Overview

After the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Restoration of 1868]], a small, capable oligarchy (drawn
from the Satsuma–Chōshū samurai) set Japan the explicit goal of **"catching up" with the
West** — adopting industrial capitalism and constitutional government — under the slogan
***fukoku kyōhei*** ("enrich the country, strengthen the army"). The goals were "few and
clear-cut," authority concentrated in few hands with broad support, giving the era a
striking coherence of purpose. Borrowing was conscious and selective, then **domesticated**
— "as much creative as imitative."

## Major Developments

- **Abolishing the old order.** The *han* became **prefectures (1871)**; the **samurai**
  class was dissolved — stipends commuted, swords and topknots banned, a **conscript army**
  raised — provoking the **Satsuma Rebellion (1877)**, crushed by peasant conscripts.
- **State-led industrialization.** The government built model factories, railways
  (Tokyo–Yokohama 1872), telegraphs, mines, and shipyards; pioneered **textiles** (silk,
  cotton); developed **Hokkaidō**; created modern banking and a unified currency (the Bank
  of Japan, 1882; the **Matsukata deflation**); and then sold enterprises to private
  **zaibatsu** (Mitsui, **Mitsubishi**). The **land tax** funded modernization. See
  [[industrialization]].
- **Constitutional government.** From the **Charter Oath (1868)** through the **Liberty and
  Popular Rights Movement** to the **Meiji Constitution (1889)** — a Prussian-influenced
  document creating a **Diet** and independent judiciary while vesting sovereignty in the
  emperor and grounding the state in ***kokutai*** (national essence). Japan thus became the
  first non-Western constitutional state.
- **Treaty revision & empire.** The central diplomatic aim — ending the unequal treaties —
  was achieved in **1894**; victory in the **[[japanese-imperialism|Sino-Japanese
  (1894–95)]]** and **[[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese (1904–5)]]** wars won Taiwan,
  Korea (annexed 1910), and recognition as a great power (the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, 1902).

## Building the centralized state (Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 5)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v5-nineteenth-century|Cambridge History Vol. 5]] (Beasley,
ch. 10) details the institutional construction the Mason spine sketches. The foundational act
was the **abolition of the domains** (*haihan chiken*, 1871) — the daimyo persuaded to
surrender their *han*, replaced by centrally appointed prefectures, ending feudal
decentralization at a stroke. There followed a powerful **Home Ministry** appointing governors
and a national police; a uniform conscript army and school system; and, after a long
oligarchic debate, the **[[meiji-constitution-1889|Meiji Constitution (1889)]]** —
[[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi's]] Prussian-model settlement creating an Imperial Diet while
reserving sovereignty to the emperor. Jansen stresses that the leadership, by century's end,
was "largely satisfied that proper wisdom and institutions had been found," and during the
1890s **co-opted the popular-rights politicians** into the cabinet system (Itō forming his own
Seiyūkai party in 1900) — the consolidation of an oligarch-guided constitutional order rather
than genuine parliamentary supremacy.

## Key Actors

- The Meiji oligarchs (***genrō***): **Ōkubo Toshimichi**, **Itō Hirobumi** (architect of
  the constitution), **Yamagata Aritomo**; the rebel **Saigō Takamori**; the entrepreneurs
  Iwasaki (Mitsubishi) and Shibusawa.
- **Emperor Meiji** — the symbol in whose name the transformation was made.

## The conservative reaction (Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 5)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v5-nineteenth-century|Cambridge History Vol. 5]] (Kenneth Pyle,
ch. 11) traces a crucial countercurrent: the **conservative reaction against the early-Meiji
"enlightenment."** As the millennial optimism of *bunmei kaika* faded, conservatives — first
moralists defending Confucian and national values against wholesale Westernization (Motoda
Eifu; the **Imperial Rescript on Education, 1890**), then a new **bureaucratic conservatism**
(Yamagata's local-government and social programs) — built "a powerful conservative orthodoxy"
by 1900. Pyle stresses the ambivalence this bred: reformers like Tokutomi Sohō abandoned
liberalism; critics such as Tanaka Shōzō (the **Ashio copper-mine pollution** case) decried
modern civilization as destroying "moral structures … a thousand years" old; and a stifled
younger generation turned to despair or radicalism. The conservative settlement — emperor,
*kokutai*, family-state, and bureaucratic guidance — became the **ideological mold of the
twentieth-century Japanese state**.

## Geographic Scope

A unifying nation-state radiating from **Tokyo**, integrated by railway and telegraph;
expanding into a colonial empire (Hokkaidō, Okinawa, Taiwan, Korea, the Manchurian sphere).

## Transition

**Preceded by** the [[edo-period|Edo period]] (ended by the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji
Restoration]]). **Followed by** the **Taishō** era (1912–26) of "Taishō democracy," and the
drift toward militarism and the [[pacific-war-1941-1945|Pacific War]] (Cycle 4's later
chapters).

## Historiography

Mason and Caiger stress that industrialization was **gradual** ("it is easy, but mistaken,
to think that Japan industrialized overnight"), **state-led but completed by private and
village enterprise**, and built on **Tokugawa foundations** (commercial economy, literacy,
the rural entrepreneur, bureaucratic discipline). They emphasize the **creativity** of
adapting Western institutions to a non-Western society — Japan being the first to attempt
it — against readings that see only imitation. The era's success, not its mistakes, bred the
tensions of the 20th century.
