# Kamakura Period (1185–1333)

The period in which **government by warriors** began — the change that, in Mason and
Caiger's words, "determined Japan's character for the next seven hundred years." Power
passed from the [[heian-period|Heian]] court to the **[[shogunate-bakufu|bakufu]]**
(military government) at **Kamakura**, founded by [[minamoto-no-yoritomo|Minamoto no
Yoritomo]] — the beginning of [[japan|Japan's]] long feudal age. From Mason & Caiger's
[[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]] (chs 9, 11).

## Overview

The shift was internal — neither foreign threat nor foreign model, but a slow change in
the balance of power as provincial warriors ([[samurai]]) bound by **vassalage** displaced
the civil aristocracy. Yet the new order grew **within** the old: Mason and Caiger call
the era as much "the final rally and grand modification of the Heian political order" as
the start of feudalism. The governing reality was ***dyarchy*** — Kamakura and the Kyoto
court ruled in shifting balance, the court keeping its forms, ranks, and much provincial
authority while the bakufu guaranteed order.

## Major Developments

- **The Gempei War (1180–85) and the founding of the bakufu.** The Minamoto destroyed the
  [[shogunate-bakufu|Taira]] (Dannoura, 1185); [[minamoto-no-yoritomo|Yoritomo]] built a
  military government at Kamakura, won the right to appoint **shugo** (constables) and
  **jitō** (stewards) over provinces and estates (1185), and took the title
  ***sei-i-tai-shōgun*** (1192).
- **The Hōjō regency.** After Yoritomo's death (1199) real power passed to the **Hōjō**
  family as regents (*shikken*) for figurehead shoguns. They crushed the court's attempt
  to restore power in the **Shōkyū War (1221)** (the formidable **Hōjō Masako** rallying
  the vassals) and issued the **Jōei Code (1232)**, a model of warrior law that endured
  to 1868.
- **The [[mongol-invasions-of-japan-1274-1281|Mongol invasions (1274, 1281)]].** Kublai
  Khan's two great seaborne assaults on Kyūshū, repelled with the help of the **kamikaze**
  ("divine wind") typhoons — the Hōjō's finest hour.
- **The popular Buddhist revolution.** The age's most lasting legacy: Buddhism became a
  **mass religion** — [[japanese-buddhism|Pure Land]] (Hōnen, Shinran), Nichiren, and
  [[zen-buddhism|Zen]] (Eisai, Dōgen). A vigorous **Nara-revival** sculpture (Unkei)
  accompanied the rebuilding of war-damaged temples. As Sansom's
  [[sansom-short-cultural-history-japan|cultural history]] stresses, it was in Kamakura that
  **[[zen-buddhism|Zen]] was taken up by the warrior class** — beginning the fusion of the
  [[samurai|samurai]] ethos with Zen discipline that would define [[muromachi-period|Muromachi]]
  culture; Chinese masters (1214–1280) brought the "unadulterated" doctrine that produced the
  first Japanese "National Teachers" (Kokushi).

## The Hōjō regency and the bakufu as a court of justice (Cambridge History of Japan)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v3-medieval|Cambridge History]] (Jeffrey P. Mass) sharpens
the picture of what the Hōjō regency actually *was*. Its strength lay not in conquest but
in **adjudication**: the bakufu grew into a sophisticated machine for settling the land
disputes of its vassals. **Hōjō Yasutoki** ("the greatest of the Hōjō regents") built the
core institutions — the ***rensho*** (a cosigner alongside the regent) and the
***hyōjōshū*** (a board of councillors) — and promulgated the **Jōei (Goseibai) Code
(1232)**, warrior law emphasizing **procedure, precedent (*senrei*), impartiality, due
process, and the right of appeal** over abstract principle. Mass presses a **revisionist**
point (following Andrew Goble against Satō Shin'ichi's older "conciliar government" thesis):
the hyōjōshū was **"a bakufu invention and a vehicle of the regent,"** not a genuine
power-sharing council — the Hōjō *tokuso* (main line) tightened its grip throughout. The
[[medieval-shoen|shōen]] page traces the estate disputes that this judicial apparatus
existed to manage; the bakufu's documents survive in their thousands precisely because the
[[medieval-shoen|jitō]] were its constant object of litigation.

The decisive political turn was the **Jōkyū (Shōkyū) War of 1221**: the retired emperor
Go-Toba's attempt to overthrow Kamakura failed utterly, after which the bakufu confiscated
some 3,000 estates from the losers, planted eastern warriors as jitō across central and
western Japan, and installed a deputy (the *Rokuhara tandai*) in Kyoto — converting the
early dyarchy into a clear **Kamakura ascendancy**.

## The Mongol crisis and the slide to collapse (Cambridge History of Japan)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v3-medieval|Cambridge History]] (Ishii Susumu) reframes the
[[mongol-invasions-of-japan-1274-1281|Mongol invasions]] as the trigger of the regime's
decline. Repelling Kublai Khan let the bakufu *consolidate* power — extending its weak
western reach and tightening control over the Kyoto court — and from the **Shimotsuki
incident of 1285** the **tokuso** ruled autocratically, their private vassals (***miuchibito***)
displacing the older gokenin elite. But this peak proved "illusory." A *defensive* war
brought **no reward land**; many **gokenin**, already impoverished by partible inheritance
and the cash economy, bore their own war costs and lost their lands by sale or pawn. The
bakufu's radical remedy — ***tokusei* (debt-cancellation) decrees** annulling gokenin debts
and returning pawned land — gave only temporary relief, and lesser houses were swallowed by
shugo, miuchibito, and commercially enriched **akutō** ("evil bands"). Mounting warrior
dissatisfaction with Hōjō autocracy fed the antibakufu movement that **Emperor Go-Daigo**
would lead to the dynasty's destruction in 1333.

## Key Actors

- [[minamoto-no-yoritomo]] — founder of the bakufu; **Yoshitsune** (his brilliant,
  doomed brother) and **Kiyomori** of the Taira.
- The **Hōjō** regents and **Hōjō Masako**.
- **Emperor Go-Daigo** — whose revolt ended the period.

## Geographic Scope

A dyarchy split between **Kamakura** (the Kantō plain, the warrior east) and **Kyoto** (the
court, central/western Japan); the bakufu's reach strongest in the east, the court's forms
persisting in the west.

## Transition

**Preceded by** the [[heian-period|Heian period]] (the Gempei War ending the cloistered-
emperor order). **Ended** in 1333 when **Emperor Go-Daigo**, allied with defecting warrior
houses (Ashikaga, Nitta), destroyed the Hōjō — the brief **Kemmu Restoration** — which
collapsed into the [[muromachi-period|Muromachi/Ashikaga]] order.

## The culture of mappō (Cambridge History of Japan)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v3-medieval|Cambridge History]] (H. Paul Varley) reads the
era's elite culture as the product of ***mappō*** **pessimism** — the Buddhist sense of
living in "the latter days of the law," an age of decline — fused with a **nostalgia for the
[[heian-period|Heian]] past**. This tone pervades the period's masterworks: **Kamo no
Chōmei's *Hōjōki*** (the hermit's meditation on impermanence) and the great war tale the
***Heike monogatari***, the chronicle of the [[minamoto-no-yoritomo|Taira's]] fated
destruction. Varley credits *mappō* with shaping the age's central aesthetic precepts —
***yūgen*** (mystery and depth), ***sabi*** (loneliness), and ***wabi*** (the plain and
humble) — already visible in the last great imperial poetry anthology, the **Shinkokinshū
(1205)**, with its taste for "the weathered and withered, the desolate and lonely." (Varley
controversially traces these precepts to Heian roots rather than to [[zen-buddhism|Zen]] —
see [[zen-and-medieval-japanese-aesthetics]].) On religion, Ōsumi Kazuo frames the
[[japanese-buddhism|new Kamakura schools]] (Pure Land, Shin, Zen, Nichiren) as
**"revolutionary"** — the first time Buddhism was fully adapted to Japanese concerns and
rooted among the common people.

## Historiography

A central debate (Mass; Sansom ends his Vol. 1 at 1333, not 1185) is whether Kamakura
**replaced** or merely **supplemented** the court — the "dyarchy" model holding that the
two coexisted in shifting balance. The Hōjō's reputation for fair, consultative justice
(the Jōei Code) is praised, though the "consultative government" thesis has been contested
(Goble). The era is richly documented by war tales (the *Heike Monogatari*), legal records,
and painted scrolls.
