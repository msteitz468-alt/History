## Overview

The Early Middle Ages (600–1000 CE) is the "evening" of CWH IV's single day — the
period in which the post-Roman, post-Han world reorganizes around new universal
empires and a revived network of exchange. In the frame of [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]]
it is also the opening phase of the **[[middle-millennium|Middle Millennium]]**
(500–1500 CE): the first stretch of the long thickening of Afro-Eurasian
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|exchange webs]] that the volume traces, anchored on the
Abbasid–Tang–Byzantine "engines" and the [[silk-roads|second Silk Roads era]]. Its defining event is the **rise of
[[islam|Islam]]** and the explosive expansion of the **caliphates**:
within a century of the Hijra (622 CE) the Arab conquests had absorbed the entire
[[sasanian-empire|Sasanian Empire]] and Rome's Levantine, Egyptian, and North
African provinces, reaching the Indus and the Atlantic. The
**[[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]]** (from 750, capital [[baghdad|Baghdad]])
presided over a cultural and scientific "golden age" at the hub of Afro-Eurasian
trade. As detailed in Lapidus, the period built directly on late antique frameworks (Roman/Byzantine urban-bureaucratic and Sasanian hybrid central/feudal models with religious pluralism), with the Arab conquests and caliphal evolution producing new garrison cities (amsar), gradual conversions and Arabization, and the crystallization of Islamic law, theology, and communal forms (Lapidus 2014 Ch. 1, 6–10, 15–19; see [[lapidus-a-history-of-islamic-societies-2014]]). The umma, sharia, and Sufi currents took shape alongside imperial cosmopolitanism.

Simultaneously, **[[tang-dynasty|Tang]] China** (618–907) — perhaps "the
wealthiest and most powerful state thus far seen in world history" (Benjamin) —
anchored the **[[silk-roads|second Silk Roads era]]** at the eastern end, while

these great structures fragmenting (Tang collapse 907; Carolingian partition;

## Major Developments

**The rise of Islam and the conquests.** From the Arabian preaching of [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam|Muhammad]] (d. 632) the **[[rashidun-caliphate|Rashidun]]** and **[[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]]** caliphates conquered an empire stretching from Spain (711–718) to the Indus (711) within roughly ninety years — one of the most rapid imperial expansions in history. Key battles include the decisive **[[battle-of-yarmuk-636|Battle of Yarmouk]]** (636), which stripped Byzantium of Syria and the Levant, and the subsequent conquest of Egypt and Sasanian Persia. Caliph **[[abd-al-malik|Abd al-Malik]]** (r. 685–705) consolidated the state after the **[[second-fitna-680|Second Fitna]]**, initiating the **Arabization of the bureaucracy**, minting Islamic coinage, and constructing the **Dome of the Rock**. See also the **[[first-fitna-656|First Fitna]]**.

**The Abbasid golden age.** The **[[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]]** (750–1258) built a centralized, bureaucratic, cosmopolitan empire from [[baghdad|Baghdad]], sponsoring the translation movement, the preservation and extension of Greek and Indian science, and a commercial system spanning the Indian Ocean and the Sahara. See [[abbasid-caliphate]].

**Tang China and the second Silk Roads era.** After the [[sui-dynasty|Sui]] reunification (581), the **[[tang-dynasty|Tang]]** (618–907) created a prosperous, cosmopolitan, bureaucratic empire whose stability — paired with the Abbasid and Byzantine revivals — drove the **[[silk-roads|second Silk Roads era]]**: goods, religions (Buddhism, Nestorian Christianity, Islam, Manichaeism), and disease flowing across Eurasia. See east-asia-classical.

**Byzantium's survival and recovery.** Shorn of its richest provinces by the Arab conquests and pressed by Avars, Bulgars, and the iconoclast controversy, [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]] nonetheless survived behind Constantinople's walls (resisting the **[[siege-of-constantinople-626|Siege of Constantinople (626)]]** and later Arab sieges) and reorganized its administration under the **[[theme-system|Theme System]]**. The state enjoyed a 9th–10th-century "golden age" of stability and cultural revival.

**The Frankish Ascendancy.** In the Latin West, the Merovingian kingdoms consolidated under Chlothar II, but central power devolved to the Mayors of the Palace. Pippin II of Herstal secured Austrasian supremacy over Neustria at the **[[battle-of-tertry-687|Battle of Tertry]]** (687); after the family's near-collapse in 714, **[[charles-martel|Charles Martel]]** refounded its dominance and **[[pippin-iii|Pippin III]]** converted it into kingship, deposing the last Merovingian with papal sanction (**[[pippinid-coup-751|the coup of 751]]**) and sealing the Frankish–papal alliance. The **[[carolingian-empire|Carolingian Empire]]** reached its zenith under **[[charlemagne|Charlemagne]]** (768–814), crowned emperor in 800 (**[[coronation-of-charlemagne-800]]**), who assembled a realm from the Pyrenees to the Elbe and sponsored the **Carolingian Renaissance**. Under **[[louis-the-pious|Louis the Pious]]** (814–840) the ideology of imperial unity collided with Frankish partible inheritance; civil war (**[[battle-of-fontenoy-841|Fontenoy]]**, 841) produced the **[[treaty-of-verdun-843|Treaty of Verdun]]** (843), dividing the empire among **[[lothar-i|Lothar I]]**, **[[louis-the-german|Louis the German]]** (East Francia), and **[[charles-the-bald|Charles the Bald]]** (West Francia) and seeding the divergence of France and Germany. Whether Carolingian rule (through the **[[missi-dominici|missi]]** and **[[carolingian-capitularies|capitularies]]**) reconstituted Roman public authority or merely glossed a plunder-driven hegemony is debated: see [[nature-of-carolingian-government]]. See [[carolingian-empire]].

**The Mercian Supremacy.** In England, the kingdom of **[[mercia|Mercia]]** dominated the lands south of the Humber through the eighth century under Æthelbald (716–757) and **[[offa-of-mercia|Offa]]** (757–796), who built **Offa's Dyke**, reformed the silver coinage, and dealt with Charlemagne as a near-equal — the apogee of pre-Viking Anglo-Saxon kingship before Wessex's rise (Ellandun, 825) and the Viking Great Army. The **Tribal Hidage** is the key window onto this hegemony; NCMH II (Keynes) warns against the anachronistic constructs of the "Heptarchy" and the "Bretwalda."

**The British Isles and Christianisation.** The Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England involved competing Roman and Irish missions, resolved in favor of Roman practice at the **[[synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]]** (664). Archbishop **[[theodore-of-tarsus|Theodore of Tarsus]]** reorganized the English diocesan structure under Canterbury. Northumbrian hegemony in northern Britain was broken by the Picts at the **[[battle-of-nechtansmere-685|Battle of Nechtansmere]]** (685).

**Slav Expansions and Northern Europe.** Slavic migrations expanded into Central Europe and the Balkans, forming the first Slavic federation under the **[[empire-of-samo|Empire of Samo]]** (r. 623–658). In Scandinavia, the **Vendel Period** (c. 550–800) represented an era of elite boat graves and long-distance trade preceding the Viking Age. Around the millennium a wave of **Christian state-formation** transformed the eastern and northern periphery into durable kingdoms (NCMH III): the **[[boleslaw-i-chrobry|Piast]]** Polish state (Mieszko I's baptism, 966; the **[[synod-of-gniezno-1000|Act of Gniezno]]**, 1000), **[[premyslid-bohemia|Přemyslid Bohemia]]** (as an imperial fief), the **[[hungary-arpad|Árpád]]** kingdom of Hungary (**[[stephen-i-of-hungary|St Stephen]]**, crowned c. 1000/1001), and **[[kievan-rus|Kievan Rus′]]** — the latter taking Orthodox Christianity from Byzantium at the **[[conversion-of-vladimir-988|conversion of Vladimir I (988)]]**, setting eastern Europe on a confessional path distinct from the Latin west.

The Primary Chronicle (Laurentian Text, Cross trans.) supplies the foundational annalistic record for Rus' origins. Key events (lines 3000–6000): pre-852 tribal customs and Khazar tribute (Polyanians pay one sword per hearth; "the Russes rule over the Khazars even to this day"); 860–862 Varangian invitation after discord ("Our land is great and rich, but there is no order in it. Come to rule and reign over us") — Rurik at Novgorod (present inhabitants "descended from the Varangian race, but aforetime they were Slavs"), Askold/Dir at Kiev; 882 Oleg captures Kiev by ruse ("You are not princes... but I am of princely birth"), declares it "the mother of Russian cities"; 907/911 Oleg's massive raid and detailed treaty with Leo/Alexander (2000 ships, wheels on ships, brocade sails, Perun/Volos oath, full trade/legal stipulations); 912 Oleg's death by serpent from horse skull per prophecy; Igor raids (941 Greek fire repulse; 944/945 major treaty with Romanus et al., sworn at Perun and St. Elias church); 945 Igor's death by Derevlians collecting extra tribute; Olga's multi-stage revenge (envoys buried alive in trench, bathhouse burned, 5,000 massacred at funeral feast); 946 bird-fire attack takes Iskorosten; Olga's tax reforms (pogosti), Novgorod trading posts; c.955 Olga's baptism in Constantinople as Helena (Emperor's daughter reference; "Blessed art thou among the women of Rus’"); Svyatoslav's campaigns (965 Khazar Bela Vezha destruction, 967 Danube Pereyaslavets base, 968 Pecheneg siege of Kiev rescued by youth/ Pretich deception); 969 Olga death ("precursor of the Christian land"); 971 Svyatoslav Greek treaty (Perun/Volos, no attack on Kherson/Bulgaria); 972 Pecheneg ambush at cataracts (skull cup); 978–980 Vladimir's fratricide rise (kills Rogvolod, betrays/kills Yaropolk via Blud); sets Perun idol etc.; paganism (300 concubines per city); 983 Varangian Christian martyrs; 986 "test of the faiths" (Bulgars/Germans/Khazars/Greek scholar's full creation-to-judgment narrative); 987 envoys report Greek liturgy "we knew not whether we were in heaven or on earth"; 988 Kherson siege (Anastasius cuts water), baptism in St. Basil church (eye healing miracle), marriage to Anna, creed/7-councils instruction, relics return. See [[cross-primary-chronicle-laurentian-1953]] for full grounded quotes/lines. This establishes Varangian calling, Rurikid consolidation of "way from the Varangians to the Greeks", Olga's reforms/precedent, Svyatoslav expansion/decline, and Vladimir's conversion mechanics in detail.

**The formation of Japan and unified Korea.** [[japan|Japan]] built a Chinese-style imperial court (Nara 710, Heian 794) and Buddhist state; in Korea, Silla unified the peninsula (668) before yielding to Koryŏ (935). See east-asia-classical.

**African and Indian Ocean networks.** [[ghana-empire|Ghana]] rose on the trans-Saharan gold–salt trade; the [[swahili-coast|Swahili coast]] and [[aksum|Aksum/Ethiopia]] tied East Africa into a flourishing Indian Ocean commerce, intensified by Islam. See [[trans-saharan-trade]] and [[indian-ocean-trade]].

**Barbarian Law Codes and Legal Ideology.** The early Middle Ages produced the great **leges nationum** (laws of the peoples): the Salic Law (c. 507–511), the Lombard *Edictus Rothari* (643), and the Visigothic **[[liber-iudiciorum|Liber Iudiciorum]]** (654). These codes were organized around the central concept of **[[wergild]]** — monetized blood-price — calibrated to social status and (in the Frankish kingdoms) to ethnic identity, explicitly encoding a hierarchy between Franks and Romans. The ideology of kingship was simultaneously evolving from warrior-based to **biblical**: from Reccared's conversion (589) onward, the Old Testament royal model (*rex David*) provided the vocabulary of sacral kingship confirmed by ecclesiastical anointing.

**The North Sea Economy: Wics and Sceattas.** From the late sixth century, the economic geography of northwestern Europe underwent a structural shift away from the fragmented, inland, estate-based exchange of the post-Roman period toward a new interregional commercial network centered on the North Sea and the English Channel. Specialized trading settlements — **[[wic|wics]]** or **emporia** (Hamwic/Southampton, Lundenwic/London, Ipswich, Quentovic, Dorestad) — emerged along coastlines as planned, artisanal production centers controlled by royal authority and served by toll-collectors. The monetary instrument of this zone was the small silver **[[sceatta]]** — a direct product of the transition from the late Roman and Merovingian gold coinage to a low-denomination silver currency suited to everyday commercial transactions. The relationship between this northern commercial expansion and the Arab closure of the southern Mediterranean is the empirical core of the **[[pirenne-thesis|Pirenne thesis]]** controversy.

**Viking North Atlantic Colonization (from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004).** The Scandinavian contribution to the period included not only raids and Danelaw/Normandy settlement but also the *landnám* (first settlement) of the North Atlantic islands. Iceland was traditionally settled c. 874 and Greenland c. 985 by colonists from Scandinavia and the British Isles. Archaeological and paleoecological data show rapid landscape transformation: pollen evidence indicates ~90 percent of dwarf birch and willow forests removed in Iceland's first century of settlement, with soil erosion in places. Zooarchaeology documents a shift from high-status "ideal" farms (cattle and pigs prominent) to sheep-dominated strategies. Diversified resource use (including wild birds, walrus, fish, seals) underwrote survival. By A.D. 930 Iceland had established *thing* assemblies; Christianity was adopted (with some pagan tolerance) in 1000. Greenland developed chiefly society with a bishopric by 1127 and major stone churches. The colonies ultimately became extinct (western ~1350, eastern ~1450) amid climate cooling, Thule Inuit contact, and vulnerabilities rooted in landnám-era choices. Key sites include Birka (emporium with rich textile finds, A.D. 750–970), Oseberg (A.D. 834 elite burial with production tools), and Hofstaðir (large chieftain hall near Mývatn with ritual abandonment c. 1000). Material culture (clinker boatbuilding, warp-weighted looms, filigree/granulation jewelry, cloisonné) supported these networks.

**Archaeological detail on wics/emporia from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004 (range 05):** Ipswich (Gipeswic) "one of the four major craft production and trading settlements"; earliest phase ~15 ha seventh century with handmade pottery + Merovingian black wares and cemetery (77+ graves, some with Continental assemblages including sword/shield/spears); early eighth-century expansion "to a massive 50 hectares" with gridiron streets; "Ipswich ware was the only wheel-made and kiln-fired pottery produced in England between the seventh and ninth centuries" supplying East Anglia and exported as far as Yorkshire/Kent. "Local rulers’ involvement is apparent in elite-built and maintained fortifications" at Hedeby, Ipswich, Hamwic. International imports (Rhenish lava millstones, Frankish pottery, Norwegian hones); assumed wool/cloth export; wine in Mainz-dated barrels. "Gipeswic (Ipswich) served East Anglia and certainly was founded by the East Anglian royal house, the Wuffingas" (Sutton Hoo/Rendlesham nearby). Viking harbors (Birka, Hedeby/Haithabu, Wolin, Novgorod, Gotland sites e.g. Fröjel active late 6th c.–1180 with far-flung coins/jewelry) show "the system of harbors and trading centers in the Viking Age was far more complicated and intricate than one is led to believe from written sources." (Bogucki & Crabtree vol. 2 pp. 330–335, 354; [[places/ipswich]] created from this source). 

**State formation in the Early Middle Ages (grounded from same range):** Post-Roman "successor states" fused Germanic/Slavic/Romano-Celtic/Roman elements. Northern Europe (Germanic groups outside empire) built "more politically complex society" via Roman interaction without conquest. Scandinavians after imperial collapse "slowly acquired increasingly statelike qualities" (A.D. 500–1000): settlement hierarchy including cities/towns, runic monuments, coin minting, taxes, large labor projects (Danevirke earthwork from 737). Slavs/Wends showed "more political complexity; administrative centers, markets..." Celtic oppida (pre-Roman) as "archaic or emergent states" with nucleated populations, industries, elite residences; cut short by Roman conquest. England post-407 lost most Roman structures; unique flavor in successor polities. (vol. 2 pp. 346–349).

**Coinage transition:** Gold tremissis dominant 5th–7th c. (debased); 7th c. shift to silver sceattas (small, thick, often aniconic or runic; earliest beyond Roman borders at Ribe ~720). Carolingian/English reforms ~751–793 produced standardized silver penny (1.5–2g). Viking hoards mix dirhams, Byzantine silver, English/German pennies. Minting reached Bohemia 960s, Kiev ~990, Scandinavia ~950–1000. (vol. 2 pp. 357–360; updates [[sceatta]]).

**Gender and social identity (burial evidence):** "Traditional thinking... has held that weapons found in a grave indicate a male, and jewelry indicates a female." DNA at West Heslerton (5th–7th c.): majority female graves with jewelry, males with weapons/none; exceptions — 3 of 24 spear-buried identified female; one ~18yo amulet/jewelry grave DNA male (possible spiritual/"cunning" figure). "Tania Dickinson labeled a woman found in one such early Saxon burial as a 'cunning woman,' a practicer of magic, healing, and divination." Age/status fluid: older individuals often non-gender-specific goods. Textile tools (spindle whorls) common in female/domestic contexts. (vol. 2 pp. 362–364).

**Agriculture, animal husbandry, technology:** Rye spread post-Roman for marginal soils. Malting (oats/barley) and hops evidenced at Ipswich and Haithabu (Hedeby). Horizontal-wheeled mills widespread Ireland 7th–11th c. (dendro dates); English examples Tamworth (9th c.), Orkney Viking Age. Vertical-wheeled at Little Island (Cork, 7th c., paired with horizontal), Tamworth. Querns remained in peasant households; tentering for adjustment. Celtic emphasis on pigs (burials, mythology); steppe traditions (Scythian/Sarmatian/Avar/Magyar) high horse (stirrups introduced by Avars), cattle/sheep, lower pig/poultry initially; hippophagy declined with Christianization. (vol. 2 pp. 366–379).

**Migration Period Peoples (selected profiles):** Bede (Ecclesiastical History) attributes Anglo-Saxon settlers to "three very powerful Germanic tribes, the Saxons, the Angles and the Jutes." Continental links via pottery (standing arch/bosses), brooches (cruciform, equal-arm, saucer); dress styles (peplos with brooches for Anglian; supporting-arm/saucer for Saxon; inlaid for Jutish Kent). Feddersen Wierde (Lower Saxony) man-made mound longhouses until 5th c. Baiuvarii ethnogenesis polyethnic (Bohemian Friedenhain-Prestovice core + Roman/Alemannic/Ostrogothic/Langobard/Thuringian elements); Regensburg as royal capital from 770; Agilolfing dukes. Dál Riata: Gaelic from Ireland to Argyll ~500; cenéla (kindreds) per Senchus fer nAlban; Iona founded by Columba 563; center of Insular/Hiberno-Saxon art (Book of Kells/Durrow debates). **Church Structure and the Eigenkirche.** The organizational structure of early medieval Christianity differed radically from the late Roman episcopal model. While bishops retained theoretical authority over their dioceses, the practical reality was dominated by the **[[eigenkirche|Eigenkirche]]** (proprietary church): churches built, endowed, and controlled as private property by lay magnates, who appointed their own priests and collected tithes as personal revenue. The effective instrument of church governance was not the canonical provincial council but the **royal synod** — the assembly of bishops convened by and under the direction of the king (the Visigothic Councils of Toledo are the paradigmatic example). Episcopal office itself was dominated by noble families appointed by the crown.

**Jews in Early Medieval Europe.** The legal position of Jews in the successor kingdoms derived from the Roman concept of **[[religio-licita|religio licita]]** — Judaism as a tolerated, ancestral religion. This Roman framework was broadly maintained in Ostrogothic Italy (Theoderic's *civilitas*) and Merovingian Gaul, but was progressively destroyed in Visigothic Spain after 589 CE — culminating in the forced baptisms of Sisebut (616 CE) and the effective enslavement of all Spanish Jews decreed by the 17th Council of Toledo (694 CE) under King Egica.

**Christianisation of the Germanic and Anglo-Saxon Peoples.** The process of **[[christianisation-of-anglo-saxon-england|Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England]]** (c. 597–680 CE) was paradigmatic of the broader top-down conversion model: the Roman mission of Augustine of Canterbury (597 CE) and the Irish mission from Iona (Aidan at Lindisfarne, 635 CE) competed for the allegiance of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, resolved at the **[[synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]]** (664 CE) in favor of Rome. Archbishop **[[theodore-of-tarsus|Theodore of Tarsus]]** then built a fully organized English diocesan structure under Canterbury, with long-range consequences: the missionary movement to the Continent (Willibrord, Boniface) and the Northumbrian intellectual florescence (Bede, Aldhelm).

**Insular Art and Early Medieval Learning.** The fusion of Irish monastic culture, Germanic metalwork (*Tierstil*), and Mediterranean iconographic models produced **[[insular-art|Insular art]]** — the distinctive visual tradition of the Lindisfarne Gospels, the Book of Kells, the Ardagh Chalice, and the Tara Brooch. In the realm of learning, the early medieval West preserved and transmitted classical literacy through monastic and episcopal schools: **Cassiodorus** founded his Vivarium scriptorium (c. 554 CE); **Isidore of Seville** compiled the *Etymologiae* (c. 620 CE) — the most widely read reference work of the medieval period; **Aldhelm** and **Bede** raised Anglo-Latin scholarship to the highest level the post-Roman West had yet achieved. The Carolingian Renaissance of the late eighth century drew directly on this Insular and Visigothic intellectual inheritance.

**The Long Tenth Century: Post-Carolingian Fragmentation (c. 900–1000).** The closing century of the period is covered in depth by [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] as the **"long tenth century"** (c. 900–c. 1024) — for long disparaged as a "dark" or "iron" century, but reassessed as the formative era of medieval Europe's durable political geography. As the Carolingian patrilineal line failed in both east and west, **Carolingian traditions detached from the dynasty** and were taken up by new royal houses: the **[[ottonian-dynasty|Ottonians]]** in the east (Henry I from 919; the imperial coronation of Otto I in 962 reviving the western empire) and the **[[capetian-dynasty|Capetians]]** in the west (Hugh Capet, 987), while England was unified from Wessex as a precociously centralised "honorary Carolingian" realm. Janet Nelson's analysis (NCMH III) stresses that the **survival of these realms as units** — by no means assured around 920 — owed less to royal resources than to the **aristocracy's investment in a "regnal community,"** and that realms tended toward **indivisibility** as undivided successions congealed into custom. Three contemporaneous trends define the era's social history, all gathered into the contested **[[feudal-revolution-debate|"feudal revolution" (*mutation de l'an mil*)]]** model: the (disputed) **slavery-to-serfdom** shift and the squeeze on the free peasantry (see [[bipartite-manor]]); the nucleation of settlement and lordship (**[[encellulement]]**/*incastellamento*); and the **hollowing-out of public order** into privatised, castle-based banal lordship. The Church's key figure became the **bishop** (the papacy was muted); **monastic reform** flowered in many independent centres — **[[cluny|Cluny]]** (909/910), Gorze, Brogne — not one (see [[ottonian-renaissance]]); the **[[peace-of-god|Peace and Truce of God]]** mobilised relics and populace against private war; and a **commercial revival** (Harz silver, revived towns) began the medieval [[urbanization|urban renaissance]]. Reuter declines to synthesise: the *mutation* model fits west Francia and Catalonia far better than Germany, England, or León.

## Geographic Scope

The most fully integrated Afro-Eurasian system the world had yet seen: a continuous chain of large, commercially minded states (Tang–Central Asia–Abbasid–Byzantine–Carolingian) linked by the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]], the [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]], and the [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan]] caravan routes. Beyond this core, the Americas reach new heights (Maya Terminal Classic, [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan's]] fall, the rise of [[chaco-canyon|Chaco]] and Cahokia) and Polynesian voyagers complete the settlement of the Pacific (reaching Aotearoa/New Zealand c. 9th c.) — covered in the regional cycles.

## Transition

**Entry:** from [[late-antiquity]] at c. 600 CE, on the eve of Islam.

**Exit:** into the [[high-middle-ages]] at c. 1000 CE — as the Tang (907), Carolingian, and unitary Abbasid orders fragment and are succeeded by new configurations (Song China, the Fatimids and regional sultanates, the post-Viking European kingdoms). This is the chronological end of [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]'s coverage; [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] overlaps it from 500 CE with the "expanding webs of exchange and conflict" and carries the story forward through the [[high-middle-ages|High]] and [[late-middle-ages|Late Middle Ages]].

## Historiography

**Source quality.** Rich but very unevenly distributed. The Islamic world generates an enormous Arabic historiographical, legal, and geographical literature (though mostly written down a century or more after the conquests, raising acute source-critical problems about the earliest period — the "skeptical school"). Byzantium and the Latin West have chronicles, hagiography, charters, and conciliar records (the collection's *Translated Texts for Historians*, Phase 5, reaches into this period). China's dynastic histories are dense; Japan's first chronicles (*Kojiki* 712, *Nihon Shoki* 720) appear; sub-Saharan Africa enters the written record chiefly through external (Arabic) observers plus archaeology. For Kievan Rus', the foundational text is the composite *Russian Primary Chronicle* (Povest' vremennykh let, Laurentian redaction 1377, Sylvester colophon 1116); see cross-primary-chronicle-laurentian-1953.md. Earlier sections rely on oral traditions and retrospective dating; 11th-c. annals (incl. this range) are higher value but reflect pro-Rurikid/Kievan monastic bias. Cross-check with archaeology and foreign sources required. The chronicle itself is a product of Caves Monastery culture.

**Major debates.** The historicity and reliability of the early Islamic tradition (the dating and authenticity of the conquest narratives and the Qur'anic text); the nature and pace of conversion and Arabization; the **[[pirenne-thesis|Pirenne thesis]]** (whether it was Islam, not the Germanic invasions, that ended Mediterranean unity and made "the Middle Ages" — now partially resolved: the Germanic invasions did damage Mediterranean commerce; the Arab conquests accelerated but did not alone cause the reorientation toward the North Sea); the reality of the "Carolingian renaissance"; the mechanisms and pace of Christianisation; and the causes of the Tang collapse. For eastern Europe, the reliability gradient of the Primary Chronicle (composite annalistic compilation with legendary material for 9th–10th c., stronger for 11th c.; pro-Rurikid/Kievan bias; chronological inconsistencies; must be cross-checked with archaeology and Byzantine/Arabic sources).

**Recent revisionism.** A skeptical, source-critical reassessment of the earliest Islamic centuries (Crone, Hoyland and others — Phase 5/10 material); the "connected histories" and Indian Ocean turn that re-centers Afro-Eurasia on its maritime and caravan networks rather than on Europe; and growing archaeological attention to West and East Africa as participants in, not recipients of, these networks.

**The Merovingian century and the Carolingian myth (Geary 1988).** For the post-Roman West, Patrick Geary's
*Before France and Germany* argues that the institutions of medieval Europe — the duchies and principalities
([[aquitaine|Aquitaine]], [[provence|Provence]], [[bavaria|Bavaria]], Burgundy, [[austrasia|Austrasia]],
[[neustria|Neustria]]) — "first appeared in the Merovingian period," and that the negative image of the
[[rois-faineants|rois fainéants]] ("do-nothing kings") was **manufactured Carolingian propaganda** (Einhard
drawing on the *Fredegar* continuations sponsored by Charles Martel's kin) to legitimise the
[[pippinids|Pippinid]] usurpation of 751. The decline of [[merovingian-franks|Merovingian]] royal power is
better explained by a run of royal **minorities** than by hereditary degeneracy, and the real seventh-century
revolution was social: the [[christianisation-of-the-frankish-aristocracy|Christianisation of the Frankish
aristocracy]] and the [[charles-martel|Carolingian]] capture of the Church.

**Primary Chronicle (Laurentian text) and Rus' source criticism (from Cross & Sherbowitz-Wetzor 1953, lines 12001–16299).** The Laurentian redaction includes the Sylvester colophon: the work was redacted in 1116 by "Sylvester, Prior of St. Michael's in V§dobichi" ("For the relation of Sylvester, Prior of St. Michael's in V§dobichi, to the text of the Povest’"; "the colophon of Sylvester was on a separate leaf or on the binding, and was thus preserved," lines ~388, ~14506-14508). "In the hope of God’s grace, I Sylvester, Prior of St. Michael’s, wrote this Chronicle in the year 6624 (1116)" (referenced). The text is composite; some early passages (e.g., 852–920) restored from Hypatian. The famous "test of the faiths" conversion narrative for Vladimir is "a later invention, and, at that, in all probability, not even a Russian, but a Greek invention" (Golubinsky quoted, lines ~12229-12232); "the story ... must be regarded as apocryphal" (line ~12316); it fuses two traditions (Greek missionary icon + Kherson baptism/marriage, lines ~12257-12269). The Laurentian uniquely preserves as interpolation after 1096 the Testament (Pouchenie) of Vladimir Monomakh, his letter to Oleg, and prayer (lines ~14321 ff.). The Pouchenie "opens with excerpts from the Psalter and from patristic works (particularly the Asketika and Discourses of St. Basil the Great), followed, first, by extensive admonitions to Vladimir’s sons, and finally, by a somewhat incomplete summary of Vladimir’s campaigns and adventures in approximate diary form, beginning in 1073 and ending in 1117" and "represents a fusion of at least two intrinsically unrelated documents"; comparison shows "military activity against the nomads was more strenuous and constant than the annals themselves would indicate" (lines ~14519-14530). Composed shortly before Monomakh's death 19 May 1125 (lines ~14531-14535). Detailed notes supply identifications (e.g., Kherson topography, lines ~12330 ff.; Torchesk as Tork settlement, lines ~14074 ff.) and correct chronology (e.g., 971 for Svyatoslav-Tzimiskes, lines ~12003 ff.). Essential for 11th c. princely politics, Polovtsian wars, and Rurikid succession (e.g., Lyubech 1097 settlement details, lines ~14363 ff.). Must be used with archaeology and foreign sources; pro-Rurikid/Kievan bias noted.

## Collection Coverage Note

**Strong, with key gaps.** Per CLAUDE.md the collection is "very strong for western Eurasia" in this period and strong for the medieval Islamic world (the *Translated Texts*, the Edinburgh and Routledge Islamic series, Phase 5/10). [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] gives the global frame and good coverage of the Abbasid–Tang–Byzantine–Carolingian core plus West and East Africa. The *New Cambridge Medieval History* Vols. 1–3 (Phase 3) are the backbone for the European and Mediterranean material. 

Note a specific narrative gap: as explained in the volume's Preface (p. xiii), a planned chapter on Romans and Lombards in Italy in the seventh century was never submitted for NCMH Vol. 1, leaving a gap in the narrative of seventh-century Italy in our backbone reference source. The Primary Chronicle (ingested 2026) now supplies primary grounding for Kievan Rus' through the early 12th c., including 11th-c. political and military detail.

Gaps per CLAUDE.md: the interior of sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and South Asia remain comparatively thin; post-1500 material is outside this period. The gap on Russia and Eastern Europe has been addressed by the ingestion of Riasanovsky (2000) and now the Primary Chronicle (Laurentian Text, Cross & Sherbowitz-Wetzor 1953) as the essential primary source. Lines 3000–6000 ground the origins narrative (Rurik calling, Oleg unification and 907/911 treaty, Igor/Olga/Svyatoslav annals, detailed 988 conversion mechanics including "test of the faiths," Kherson siege, baptism in Cherson, and early pagan resistance) with direct quotes and line references; fills primary-voice gap for Kievan Rus' foundational events.
