## Overview

The Early Middle Ages (600–1000 CE) is the "evening" of CWH IV's single day — the
period in which the post-Roman, post-Han world reorganizes around new universal
empires and a revived network of exchange. In the frame of [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]]
it is also the opening phase of the **[[middle-millennium|Middle Millennium]]**
(500–1500 CE): the first stretch of the long thickening of Afro-Eurasian
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|exchange webs]] that the volume traces, anchored on the
Abbasid–Tang–Byzantine "engines" and the [[silk-roads|second Silk Roads era]]. Its defining event is the **rise of
[[islam|Islam]]** and the explosive expansion of the **[[caliphate|caliphates]]**:
within a century of the Hijra (622 CE) the Arab conquests had absorbed the entire
[[sasanian-empire|Sasanian Empire]] and Rome's Levantine, Egyptian, and North
African provinces, reaching the Indus and the Atlantic. The
**[[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]]** (from 750, capital [[baghdad|Baghdad]])
presided over a cultural and scientific "golden age" at the hub of Afro-Eurasian
trade.

Simultaneously, **[[tang-dynasty|Tang]] China** (618–907) — perhaps "the
wealthiest and most powerful state thus far seen in world history" (Benjamin) —
anchored the **[[silk-roads|second Silk Roads era]]** at the eastern end, while
**[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]]** held the center and the **[[carolingian-empire|Carolingian
Empire]]** briefly reunified the Latin West. The period ends with all three of
these great structures fragmenting (Tang collapse 907; Carolingian partition;
Abbasid devolution), opening onto the [[high-middle-ages]].

## Major Developments

**The rise of Islam and the conquests.** From the Arabian preaching of
[[muhammad|Muhammad]] (d. 632) the **[[rashidun-caliphate|Rashidun]]** and
**[[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]]** caliphates conquered an empire stretching from
Spain (711–718) to the Indus (711) within roughly ninety years — one of the most
rapid imperial expansions in history. See [[arab-conquests]] and [[islam]].

**The Abbasid golden age.** The **[[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasids]]** (750–1258)
built a centralized, bureaucratic, cosmopolitan empire from [[baghdad|Baghdad]],
sponsoring the translation movement, the preservation and extension of Greek and
Indian science, and a commercial system spanning the Indian Ocean and the Sahara.
See [[abbasid-caliphate]].

**Tang China and the second Silk Roads era.** After the [[sui-dynasty|Sui]]
reunification (581), the **[[tang-dynasty|Tang]]** (618–907) created a prosperous,
cosmopolitan, bureaucratic empire whose stability — paired with the Abbasid and
Byzantine revivals — drove the **[[silk-roads|second Silk Roads era]]**: goods,
religions (Buddhism, Nestorian Christianity, Islam, Manichaeism), and disease
flowing across Eurasia. See [[east-asia-classical]].

**Byzantium's survival and recovery.** Shorn of its richest provinces by the Arab
conquests and pressed by Avars, Bulgars, and the iconoclast controversy,
[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]] nonetheless survived behind [[constantinople|Constantinople's]]
walls and enjoyed a 9th–10th-century "golden age" of stability and cultural
revival.

**The Carolingian Empire.** In the Latin West the Frankish
[[carolingian-empire|Carolingians]] — [[charlemagne|Charlemagne]] crowned emperor
in 800 — briefly reunified much of Western Europe in alliance with the
[[papacy|Church]], fostering the "Carolingian renaissance" before partition
(843) and the Viking, Magyar, and Saracen raids dissolved central authority. See
[[carolingian-empire]].

**The formation of Japan and unified Korea.** [[japan|Japan]] built a Chinese-style
imperial court (Nara 710, Heian 794) and Buddhist state; in Korea, [[silla|Silla]]
unified the peninsula (668) before yielding to [[goryeo|Koryŏ]] (935). See
[[east-asia-classical]].

**African and Indian Ocean networks.** [[ghana-empire|Ghana]] rose on the
trans-Saharan gold–salt trade; the [[swahili-coast|Swahili coast]] and
[[aksum|Aksum/Ethiopia]] tied East Africa into a flourishing Indian Ocean
commerce, intensified by Islam. See [[trans-saharan-trade]] and [[indian-ocean-trade]].

## Geographic Scope

The most fully integrated Afro-Eurasian system the world had yet seen: a
continuous chain of large, commercially minded states (Tang–Central Asia–Abbasid–
Byzantine–Carolingian) linked by the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]], the
[[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]], and the [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan]]
caravan routes. Beyond this core, the Americas reach new heights (Maya Terminal
Classic, [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan's]] fall, the rise of [[chaco-canyon|Chaco]]
and Cahokia) and Polynesian voyagers complete the settlement of the Pacific
(reaching Aotearoa/New Zealand c. 9th c.) — covered in the regional cycles.

## Transition

**Entry:** from [[late-antiquity]] at c. 600 CE, on the eve of Islam.

**Exit:** into the [[high-middle-ages]] at c. 1000 CE — as the Tang (907),
Carolingian, and unitary Abbasid orders fragment and are succeeded by new
configurations (Song China, the Fatimids and regional sultanates, the post-Viking
European kingdoms). This is the chronological end of [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH
IV]]'s coverage; [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] overlaps it from 500 CE with the
"expanding webs of exchange and conflict" and carries the story forward through the
[[high-middle-ages|High]] and [[late-middle-ages|Late Middle Ages]].

## Historiography

**Source quality.** Rich but very unevenly distributed. The Islamic world generates
an enormous Arabic historiographical, legal, and geographical literature (though
mostly written down a century or more after the conquests, raising acute source-
critical problems about the earliest period — the "skeptical school"). Byzantium
and the Latin West have chronicles, hagiography, charters, and conciliar records
(the collection's *Translated Texts for Historians*, Phase 5, reaches into this
period). China's dynastic histories are dense; Japan's first chronicles (*Kojiki*
712, *Nihon Shoki* 720) appear; sub-Saharan Africa enters the written record
chiefly through external (Arabic) observers plus archaeology.

**Major debates.** The historicity and reliability of the early Islamic tradition
(the dating and authenticity of the conquest narratives and the Qur'anic text);
the nature and pace of conversion and Arabization; the "Pirenne thesis" (whether
it was Islam, not the Germanic invasions, that ended Mediterranean unity and made
"the Middle Ages"); the reality of the "Carolingian renaissance"; and the causes
of the Tang collapse.

**Recent revisionism.** A skeptical, source-critical reassessment of the earliest
Islamic centuries (Crone, Hoyland and others — Phase 5/10 material); the
"connected histories" and Indian Ocean turn that re-centers Afro-Eurasia on its
maritime and caravan networks rather than on Europe; and growing archaeological
attention to West and East Africa as participants in, not recipients of, these
networks.
