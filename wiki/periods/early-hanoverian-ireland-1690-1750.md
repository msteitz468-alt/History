## Overview

The half-century following the Battle of the Boyne was once portrayed as a period of consolidation and near-stasis in Irish political and social development — the [[protestant-ascendancy]] class lying supine under viceregal government, the economy a victim of English discrimination, the most memorable achievement being a code of Penal Laws against Catholics. D.W. Hayton's chapter in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]] notes that scholarship over the past thirty years has "transformed this picture": instead of torpor, historians now see "a changing, indeed a dynamic, society." Economic historians have demonstrated that Ireland enjoyed healthy trade balances and a growth spurt beginning in the second quarter of the century; work on parliament and press has uncovered vibrant political culture; social history has revised teleological readings of the period.

## Major Developments

### Constitutional Revolution

Charles II's Irish parliament had voted itself effectively out of existence through a grant of "additional" taxation for the king's lifetime. After 1691, Irish MPs were acutely aware of this precedent and the fragile financial state of the Irish treasury — kept chronically short by the cost of maintaining a large standing army (the English government's preferred location for its peacetime forces). Parliamentary supply, granted only for short periods, made the Dublin government dependent on the parliament. By 1715 a pattern of biennial sessions was established and a small national debt appeared. The Irish parliament had become a permanent institution.

The "heads of bills" procedure circumvented Poynings' Law (the fifteenth-century statute requiring prior English privy council approval of Irish legislation), with most statutes originating as draft bills in the Irish parliament. The Commons secured de facto recognition of their "sole right" to initiate supply bills. The key episode establishing this parliamentary culture was Lord Capel's viceroyalty (1695–6), which pioneered the "undertaker system" — contracting out parliamentary management to Irish politicians who could "undertake" satisfactory completion of parliamentary business. This system subsisted until the 1770s.

### Penal Laws

The Penal Laws against Catholics — the most important enacted between 1695 and 1709, with supplementary bills proposed throughout the period — were enacted by the Irish Protestant parliament against William III's personal preference for honouring the Treaty of Limerick's promises. They attacked the Catholic Church (banishing bishops and religious orders, making Catholic education illegal), the surviving Catholic political classes (depriving Catholics of office and public life, insisting estates be divided among children, preventing Catholic purchase of freehold land, excluding Catholics from the legal profession), and Catholic military capacity (the notorious provision preventing Catholics from owning horses worth more than £5 was specifically designed to forestall Catholic cavalry recruitment for a Jacobite invasion).

Edmund Burke's later characterisation of this legislation as a uniquely repressive "code" has been challenged by revisionist historians who note: (1) the laws were not enacted en bloc but piecemeal over time for different reasons; (2) the Irish situation was not unique by European *ancien régime* standards; (3) enforcement was patchy — the state lacked means for systematic enforcement against Catholic clergy, and the law could be evaded through collusion or nominal conversion. The most effectively enforced laws were those protecting Protestant control of land.

### Confessional and Partisan Politics

The chief political divisions of Queen Anne's reign (1702–14) crystallised around confessional issues rather than constitutional grievances:

- **Whigs**: Emphasised the continuing danger from Catholic-Jacobite conspiracy; argued for Protestant unity; supported restrictions on Catholics.
- **Tories**: Began to regard existing Penal Laws as sufficient; focused on defence of the established church against the Presbyterian threat.

The Sacramental Test Act (1704) obliged holders of crown and municipal office to take annual Church of Ireland communion, effectively excluding Ulster Presbyterians from municipal government. Presbyterian strongholds like Belfast and Derry fell to Anglican domination. Thereafter maintenance of the test became a central parliamentary battleground.

Irish Toryism collapsed rapidly after the Hanoverian succession (1714): the party's Stuart loyalism was incompatible with Irish Protestant property rights derived from the Revolution Settlement. The second Duke of Ormond's flight to the Jacobite court in 1715 completed the identification of Toryism with the Pretender. Moderate Tories gradually changed sides; a party system gave way to "connexion politics" based on kinship and patronage.

### Anglo-Irish Friction and Patriotism

Disputes with Westminster in the late 1690s demonstrated the limits of Protestant Irish power:

- The English House of Lords claimed final appellate jurisdiction in Irish legal cases.
- The Woollen Act (1699) prohibited Irish cloth exports.
- The Forfeitures Resumption Act (1700) compelled sale of Williamite grants, affecting Protestant purchasers.

These provocations inspired William Molyneux's *The Case of Ireland Stated* (1698), asserting Irish legislative autonomy — condemned by the English parliament. Two years later the Forfeitures Act prompted a country-wide petitioning campaign. Yet the fury was stifled without significant concessions: Irish Protestants accepted constitutional subordination in exchange for maintaining the Protestant settlement.

The Wood's Halfpence affair (1722–5) crystallised a mature "patriot" politics: Jonathan Swift's *Drapier's Letters* mobilised popular opposition to a patent for Irish copper coinage secured through corruption at the English court, forcing Walpole to withdraw the patent. Swift was established as a "patriot hero" despite opposing any Catholic relief and any challenge to Protestant ascendancy.

The Declaratory Act (1720), asserting Westminster's appellate jurisdiction and right to legislate for Ireland, produced "some puffing in the Dublin press, but a shattering silence in the Irish parliament" — illustrating the essentially pragmatic character of Protestant Irish patriotism, which protested constitutional subordination but accepted it rather than risk the English military guarantee of Protestant property.

### Economic and Social Development

Despite contemporary jeremiads about economic backwardness, Ireland enjoyed significant growth:

- The linen industry in east Ulster, under the statutory Linen Board (1711), became the brightest economic sector.
- The Atlantic provisioning trade (beef, butter) expanded Cork's prosperity.
- Dublin's population grew rapidly, reaching possibly 130,000 by mid-century, with an expanding commercial and professional middle class.
- After 1740, the Irish economy accelerated: Ulster farmer-weavers transformed by the nascent linen industry; Munster boosted by Atlantic trade.

The savage winter of 1740–1 caused massive mortality (contemporary accounts compared it to the Great Famine in scale, proportionally) but the economy recovered.

The Dublin Society (1731) — forerunner of the Royal Dublin Society — institutionalised the "improvement" movement, bringing together wealthy enthusiasts to publicise schemes for agricultural improvement, manufacture, and social reform. Jonathan Swift satirised the craze in *A Modest Proposal* (1729), recommending the raising of Irish Catholic children as livestock.

### Catholic Quiescence and Presbyterian Change

Catholic political interests remained quiescent through the period: the 1745 Jacobite rebellion in Scotland produced "no significant response." A House of Lords inquiry in 1731 found the number of Catholic priests had actually risen despite the Penal Laws — demonstrating the failure of conversion strategies. The charity-school movement had "run into the ground." A wealthy Catholic mercantile class was beginning to emerge by the 1740s, creating pressure for eventual relaxation of the Penal Laws (though this belongs to the next period).

Ulster Presbyterianism was reshaped by mass Scottish immigration in the 1690s (following bad harvests in Scotland), establishing the largest single wave of Scottish settlement since the original plantation. By the late 1710s, however, distress propelled the first waves of Presbyterian emigration from Ulster to North America. Theological divisions — notably the subscription controversy of 1719 — weakened Presbyterian institutional cohesion.

## Transition

By the 1740s, as Hayton notes, "the contours of Irish politics had changed." The principal issues that had agitated early Hanoverian politics — Catholic Jacobitism, Presbyterian encroachment, constitutional subordination — had been substantially settled or exhausted. The emerging challenges were different: accumulating revenue surpluses creating new constitutional questions about their disposal (prefiguring the 1750s Money Bills crisis), a harder-edged Protestant patriotism seeking broader constituencies, and incipient Catholic mercantile prosperity. This period transitions into the late Hanoverian and Revolutionary era of Irish politics (1750–1800).

## Historiography

S.J. Connolly's *Religion, Law and Power: The Making of Protestant Ireland 1660–1760* (1992) established the revisionist framework, placing early Hanoverian Ireland within a comparative *ancien régime* context. D.W. Hayton's own *Ruling Ireland 1685–1742* (2004) and C.I. McGrath's *The Making of the Eighteenth-Century Irish Constitution* (2000) are essential for parliamentary history. L.M. Cullen's economic history remains foundational. The period has been contested between revisionist readings stressing stability and normalcy within European norms, and post-colonial readings emphasising the colonial character of the confessional state.

## Collection Coverage Note

Coverage in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]] is moderate to strong for the political and constitutional dimensions, with particular depth for parliamentary history, the Penal Laws, and Protestant elite culture. Economic history and the experience of the Catholic majority are thinner. The collection as a whole is weak on post-1750 Ireland; this period represents one of the stronger sections.
