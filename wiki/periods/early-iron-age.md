## Overview

The Early Iron Age opens in the shadow of the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] (~1200 BCE),
the systemic failure of the Late Bronze Age palace civilizations of the eastern
Mediterranean and Near East. It is the period in which the world recovers from
that collapse on new terms — with iron rather than bronze as the working metal,
with smaller and often non-palatial polities, and with alphabetic rather than
syllabic or logographic scripts spreading in the west. In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|Benjamin]]'s
framing ([[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]), this is the runway from which the
"world of states, empires, and networks" takes off: 1200 BCE is the volume's
sunrise precisely because the collapse cleared the ground for the new political
forms that follow.

Mycenaean palaces are gone and Greece enters its so-called "Dark Age" of reduced
population, lost literacy, and village-scale life. In China the [[zhou-dynasty|Western Zhou]]
presides over a sophisticated bronze-age dynastic order that will itself fragment
toward 800–771 BCE. In India the Indus cities are long gone and the Vedic
[[vedic-age|Indo-Aryan]] world of cattle-keeping chiefdoms takes shape. Across
much of the world, however, the period is invisible in the textual record and
known only archaeologically.

## Major Developments

**Recovery from the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]].** The interlocking palace economies
of Mycenaean Greece, Hittite Anatolia, and the Levantine city-states failed
within a few decades around 1200 BCE; Egypt survived but contracted. The causes
are a standing wiki controversy ([[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|causes of the collapse]]). The Early Iron
Age is the centuries-long aftermath.

**The spread of iron metallurgy.** Iron working, known earlier but marginal,
became widespread as bronze-supply networks (especially the long-distance tin
trade) broke down. Iron ore was far more widely available than tin, democratizing
access to metal tools and weapons — a structural shift with military and
agricultural consequences. See iron working.

**Phoenician maritime expansion.** Freed from Bronze Age imperial overlordship,
the [[phoenicians|Phoenician]] city-states (Tyre, Sidon, Byblos) built a
commercial network across the Mediterranean between c. 1200 and 800 BCE, founding
colonies (Carthage traditionally 814 BCE) and — decisively — spreading the
**[[alphabet|consonantal alphabet]]** that underlies Greek, Latin, Aramaic, and
their descendants.

**The Greek "Dark Age" and its end.** Depopulation, the loss of Linear B
literacy, and village-scale life followed the Mycenaean collapse; recovery from
the 9th century produced the conditions for the [[city-state|polis]] and Homeric
epic. The period is reframed below under Historiography.

**The Balkan metal boom and Hallstatt A.** Beyond the literate Near East, the
**Carpatho-Danubian Balkans** entered the Iron Age through a spectacular
**Late Bronze Age → Hallstatt A** metallurgical climax. [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH
Vol. III, Part 1]] (Dumitrescu) reports that of ~25,000 catalogued metal
hoard-objects in Romania, **over 20,000 date to Hallstatt A** (12th–11th c. BCE),
Transylvania having become "an exceptional centre of bronze-working" whose products
reached the Baltic. **Hallstatt A (1200–1000 BCE)** is treated as the transition to
the Iron Age; the Palaeo-Balkan peoples (Thracians, Illyrians, Daco-Moesians,
Macedonians) who carry it forward are covered in CAH III.1 Chs 14–15 — see
[[balkans]].

**Western Zhou China.** The [[zhou-dynasty|Western Zhou]] (c. 1046–771 BCE) ruled north China
through a decentralized system of kin-based regional lords legitimated by the
[[mandate-of-heaven]], an idea of world-historical durability. Its fall in 771
BCE opens the long fragmentation of the [[archaic-period]] and
[[classical-antiquity]] in China.

**The Vedic age in South Asia.** After the Indus collapse, Indo-Aryan-speaking
pastoralists and the composition of the *Rigveda* define a world of cattle-wealth
chiefdoms (*jana*) in the Punjab and upper Ganges — the matrix of later Indian
religion, society, and the *varna* order. See [[vedic-age]].

## Liverani's interpretation

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] reads the Early Iron Age as an age of
**democratising innovation** in the west. Both **iron** and the **alphabet** already
existed in the Late Bronze Syro-Levantine world but were *suppressed* by the palace system
(bronze monopolised in palace workshops; writing monopolised by cuneiform scribal elites);
only the **collapse of the palaces** let them spread — iron because its ore is widespread
and workable in village smithies, the alphabet because it freed writing from professional
scribes (early alphabetic texts include ownership graffiti and ordinary funerary
inscriptions "with no significant difference" from royal ones). He maps a **conservative
east** (Mesopotamia/Egypt keeping complex scripts and political continuity) against an
**innovative west** (alphabet, iron, kinship states). Settlement **dispersed** from a few
big cities into many villages; landscape innovations (hillside terracing, *qanat*
irrigation, plastered cisterns) and the full domestication of the **camel/dromedary**
opened terracing and trans-desert trade, integrating Arabia and the Iranian deserts. The
defining political form was the **kinship ("nation") state** — membership by descent from a
common eponymous ancestor rather than residence in palace territory, its name "House of X,"
its army a charismatic "military population," its charters the aetiological origin-stories.
See [[ancient-near-east-environment-and-society]] and [[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]].

## Geographic Scope

Historically "active" (in the documentary sense) in the Near East, the Levant, the
Aegean fringe, and north China. Archaeologically legible but text-poor across the
Mediterranean, the Iranian plateau, South Asia, and the Eurasian steppe (where
mounted [[pastoral-nomadism]] is consolidating). Most of the Americas, Africa
south of Egypt, Australasia, and Oceania lie wholly outside the period's written
record and are treated in the regional cycles on their own archaeological terms.

## Transition

**Entry:** the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] (period 11) terminates the Late Bronze Age
palace world; the Early Iron Age is its recovery phase.

**Exit:** the period gives way to the [[archaic-period]] (800–500 BCE) as the
Neo-Assyrian Empire achieves true territorial dominance, the Greek polis and
overseas colonization take off, alphabetic literacy matures, and — in the
mid-first-millennium "Axial" ferment — new religious and philosophical
traditions begin to crystallize. See [[axial-age]].

## Historiography

**Source quality.** Thin and uneven. For the Near East, Assyrian and Babylonian
royal inscriptions resume through the period; for the Levant, the Hebrew Bible
preserves (heavily edited, theologically shaped) traditions about this era. The
Aegean is almost wholly archaeological. China has the transmitted *Book of
Documents* and *Book of Songs* plus bronze inscriptions, but the textual horizon
is shaped by later Zhou and Han editing. South Asia is known almost entirely
through the orally transmitted *Vedas*, undateable to the year.

**The "Dark Age" debate.** The label *Greek Dark Age* is increasingly questioned:
Lefkandi's monumental "heroön" (c. 1000 BCE) and continuous contact with the
Near East show the period was less impoverished and isolated than the old model
held. Many scholars prefer "Early Iron Age" precisely to avoid the value-laden
"dark." This is the period's central historiographical revision.

**Dating.** Near Eastern chronology is anchored by Assyrian eponym lists and
astronomical retrocalculation; Aegean and central-Mediterranean chronologies
rest on pottery seriation cross-linked to Egypt, and are under active revision by
radiocarbon (the "Iron Age chronology debate" in Levantine archaeology, with
direct bearing on Biblical history, is unresolved).
