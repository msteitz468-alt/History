# Long 19th Century (1800–1914 CE)

## Overview

The "long nineteenth century" — Eric Hobsbawm's term for the era running from
the [[atlantic-revolutions|Atlantic revolutions]] and the French Revolution to
the outbreak of the First World War in 1914 — is the period in which the
transformations that [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] groups under **production,
destruction, and connection** first became structurally dominant. It is the
century of [[industrialization]], of the decisive shift to fossil fuels (the
opening of the [[anthropocene|Anthropocene]]), of an unprecedented surge in human
numbers, of the consolidation of the [[nationalism|national state]] as the default
political form, and of the [[imperialism|New Imperialism]] that brought most
of Africa and much of Asia under European rule.

Pomeranz and McNeill stress that for the economy the decisive break came not in
1750 but after **c. 1830–1870**: sustained per-capita growth was not clearly
present even in Britain until after the Napoleonic Wars (1815), and integrated
global markets — measurable price convergence across oceans — appear in the data
only after 1840 in the Atlantic and later elsewhere. As late as 1830 Britain still
drew as much power from waterwheels as from steam, and in 1840 had more installed
steam capacity than the rest of the world combined. The nineteenth century is thus
the hinge: the "first act" of mechanization (Lancashire cotton, the 1750s–1770s
innovations) matured into a self-sustaining, world-transforming system whose
heartland was, for roughly a century after 1850, confined to Europe, North
America, and (later) Japan — the single most important source of those regions'
global dominance.

## Scandinavia, 1815–1870 (CHS Vol. II)

The Nordic nineteenth century (to 1870) was defined by a single problem: how to
accommodate a **demographic explosion** — Bishop Tegnér's "peace, vaccine and
potatoes" (L29623–29629) — after the old corporate, absolutist order had collapsed.
The population of Scandinavia doubled 1820–70 to ~10 million. The
[[scandinavian-demographic-transition|demographic transition]] (falling infant
mortality) and the [[scandinavian-agricultural-revolution|agricultural revolution]]
(enclosure, freeholding, the dairy pivot) fed both rising living standards and the
beginnings of the [[great-scandinavian-emigration|Great Emigration]].
[[scandinavian-industrialisation|Industrialisation]] lagged far behind Britain —
Scandinavian GDP per capita reached only ~50–60% of the UK's by 1870, with modest
~1% annual growth — and the industrial working class was still tiny (Sweden ~65–70k;
Norway's seamen outnumbered its industrial workers). Society shifted "from ranks to
classes" via an [[scandinavian-class-formation|associational revolution]]
(temperance, savings banks, peasant movements) following the sequence corporations →
associations → mass organisations. Politically the era produced
[[scandinavian-constitutionalism-19c|liberal constitutions]] (Norway 1814, Denmark
1849, Swedish reform 1866), [[scandinavian-popular-education|mass schooling]], the
brief unifying current of [[scandinavianism|Scandinavianism]] (collapsed 1864), and
the consolidation of five [[scandinavian-nation-state-formation|nation states]].

- **Industrialization and the fossil-fuel transition.** Coal was "king" from
  c. 1750 to 1900; the [[industrialization]] process spread from Britain to
  continental Europe, the United States, and Meiji Japan. Total human energy use
  multiplied roughly 90–100-fold between 1750 and the present, most of it after
  1850 (see [[anthropocene]], [[fossil-fuel-energy-transition]]).
  From Lieven (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II ch.1, ll. 4345–4347, 4587–4637): Crimean War (1854–6) exposed Russia’s relative decline as great power due to Industrial Revolution in West and lack of modernisation; “by 1914 Russia was much more modern than she had been in 1856, but in relative terms she was still well behind Germany or Britain.” Russia occupied Europe’s “Second World” periphery (poorer, less stable than NW Protestant core); empire itself part of problem (multiethnicity + autocracy bred conflict). “On Europe’s periphery one paid a high price for both power and powerlessness.” Russian military/economic performance in Great War impressive but revolution/home front collapse destroyed victor prospects (ll. 4351–4359). By eve of 1914 “tsarism was very far from having solved the dilemmas of empire.”

- **The second industrial revolution** (c. 1870–1914): steel, chemicals,
  electricity, the internal combustion engine, and the search for synthetic
  substitutes (notably for rubber) accentuated both commodity booms and busts.
- **Global market integration.** Steamships, railways, the telegraph, and the
  Suez Canal collapsed transport and communication costs: transatlantic shipping
  costs fell by half to two-thirds in forty years; the Liverpool–Alexandria cotton
  price gap fell from 63% to 5% between 1840 and 1895 (see
  [[early-modern-commerce]], [[global-silver-trade]] for the prior phase).
- **Mass migration.** Some 60 million Europeans crossed oceans 1840–1913, plus
  perhaps 40 million Indians and Chinese to plantation and mining peripheries —
  the first truly mass [[migration-in-human-history|long-distance migration]] of
  largely free people, running alongside the late peak and abolition of the
  [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]].
- **The New Imperialism.** The [[imperialism|scramble for Africa]] and the
  extension of formal and informal empire across Asia; technologically one-sided
  colonial wars (Omdurman, the Java War, the Herero genocide) with hugely lopsided
  death tolls.
- **Nationalism and the national state.** The spread of [[nationalism]] as both
  unifier (Germany, Italy) and solvent of multinational empires (Habsburg,
  Ottoman, Romanov).
- **Foreign policy, armed forces, and reforms (Cambridge Vol. II index ll. 24001–30000 grounding ch.23–29):** "Great Northern War (with Sweden) (1700–21)" declaration, Peter’s justification, Nystad (1721), casualties (ll. 24851–24877). Foreign policy phases Alexander I to Nicholas II, "character of tsarist diplomacy" (ll. 24395–24467). Imperial army "autocratic power over", "symbiotic relation to state", Poltava success, WWI failure (ll. 25207–25415). Navy power, Kronstadt, academies (ll. 25003–26909). "Great Reforms, under Alexander II" "watershed", Editing Commissions, aims 597–608, land reforms (ll. 24881–24965). Factory legislation, Kreenholm strike 1872, labour movement, Bund (ll. 24299–26207). Gendarme Corps, police bureaus (ll. 24637–24665). `part_of:` empire great-power trajectory and modernisation; `contributed_to:` 1905/1917 upheavals and collapse. `preceded_by:` Crimean War turning point; `produced:` partial industrialisation and social change.
- **The demographic transition** began in a few Western areas: falling mortality
  from sanitation and clean water after c. 1850, with birth rates declining a few
  decades later (see [[demographic-transition]]).
- **Abolition.** The moral assault on slavery, rooted in eighteenth-century ideas,
  produced emancipations across the Atlantic world and (very gradually) Russian
  serf emancipation (1861) — though Alessandro Stanziani's revisionism stresses
  how incomplete and reversible "free labor" actually was.
- **Russian Autocracy, Reform, and Revolution**: Under **[[alexander-i|Alexander I]]**, Russia defeated the **[[french-invasion-of-russia-1812|French invasion of 1812]]**. Nicholas I countered the **[[decembrist-revolt-1825|Decembrist Revolt]]** (1825) with the conservative doctrine of **[[official-nationality|Official Nationality]]**. Defeat in the **[[crimean-war-1853|Crimean War]]** (1853–1856) exposed Russia's backwardness, prompting the **[[great-reforms-of-alexander-ii|Great Reforms of Alexander II]]**, centered on the **[[emancipation-of-the-serfs-1861|Emancipation of the Serfs]]** (1861). The rise of **[[populism-in-russia|populism]]** led to Alexander II's assassination in 1881. Under Alexander III and Nicholas II, the state drove rapid **[[industrialization-of-russia|industrialization]]** via the **[[witte-sergei|Witte System]]** and built the Trans-Siberian Railway, but social tensions culminated in the **[[revolution-of-1905|Revolution of 1905]]** and subsequent failed agrarian reforms under **[[stolypin-pyotr|Pyotr Stolypin]]**.

From Service (2009, lines 388–528): The Russian Empire in 1900 was a "continent unto itself" covering a sixth of the earth's surface. Manufacturing and mining output rose by an annual rate of eight per cent in the last decade of the nineteenth century and of six per cent between 1907 and 1914; fifty thousand kilometres of rail-track had been laid by 1914, including the Trans-Siberian. Grain exports made Russia the world’s greatest grain exporter (roughly 11.5 million tons of cereals sold abroad annually pre-1914). The main rural institution remained the village land commune with its periodic redistribution and tradition of mutual responsibility; "middle-peasantization" tendencies were visible but kulaks and poor households persisted. Nicholas II resisted constitutional change; after Bloody Sunday (9 January 1905) he conceded a Duma but manipulated the Basic Law and electoral rules to secure compliance (First Duma dispersed; Third Duma weighted toward gentry). Stolypin’s agrarian reforms (encouraging "strong and sober" peasants to exit the commune) had limited success: by 1916 only a tenth of European empire households had broken away. Strikes and demonstrations revived after the Lena gold-fields massacre (April 1912). National consciousness among Russians remained patchy; the empire was "a patchwork quilt of nationalities" with Russians only 44% of the population. The autocracy was "deeply fissured" between government and subjects, capital and provinces, educated and uneducated. Opposition parties (Bolsheviks under Lenin emphasizing vanguard party and provisional revolutionary democratic dictatorship; Mensheviks insisting on bourgeois stage first; Socialist-Revolutionaries privileging the peasantry) positioned themselves for open politics if franchise permitted.

**Society, Law, Economy and Government in Imperial Russia (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II, Part IV ch.17–19 + Part V start, ll. 12001–18000, Baberowski/Moon/Ananich/Shakibi/Hartley)**: 

From judicial reform (Baberowski): “The administrative and judicial reforms of the 1860s... spread rapidly beyond European Russia to encompass the peripheral regions... With the opening of a Judicial Chamber in Tashkent in 1899, the introduction of the reformed judicial system was completed in the empire.” Jury courts limited in west/Asia due to ethnic conflicts and distances; land captains (1889) replaced justices of the peace combining admin/judicial functions in rural areas (“uncontrollable despots” per liberals). Emergency Laws (1881) enabled governors to impose administrative exile/punishments; used 1906–7 for >1,000 drumhead death sentences. Jurists conquered bureaucracy (“ministers of justice... proponents of... rule of law”); bar self-administered with free speech privilege, “sacred order”/“lawyer-soldiers” for liberals and radicals; Jewish jurists barred from state service became “asylum for unemployed Jewish jurists” and radical defence spokesmen. “The rule of law was a bill of exchange on a liberal constitution”. `produced:` Europeanised legal order + opposition platforms; `contributed_to:` 1917 revolt of “the other Russia”.

From peasants/agriculture (Moon): Pre-1861 “between a third and a half of the peasantry lived on nobles’ estates as serfs”; “Russian peasants were compelled to hand over around half of the product of their labour.” Classic forms (three-field rotation, large complex multi-generational households, communal repartitional land tenure) emerged late 17th–19th c. in central Russia as responses to serfdom consolidation, poll tax (1719–24), joint responsibility and population pressure (“the key to understanding the new system seems to be taxation”). Extensification + south/southeast migration to black-earth regions; livestock important in outlying areas. Post-1861 Great Reforms + 1874 conscription reform reduced exploitation levels; slow living-standard improvement (rising yields, rail integration, grain per capita up); complex households began breaking down as younger generation gained independence via wage labour/migration/schools. “In 1905 and 1917... peasants joined in and spread the revolutions to the villages with the aim of seizing the land still owned by non-peasants... continuing the practice of increasing production... by bringing more land into cultivation”. `caused_by:` serfdom/poll tax + population growth; `followed_by:` 1917 land seizures (reversion to extensification); `part_of:` serfdom-in-russia.

From economy/banking (Ananich): Petrine “active government entrepreneurship”; state monopolies (salt/tobacco/China trade), protectionist 1724 tariff, poll tax + conscription, forced merchant relocation to St Petersburg stock exchange. Catherine “credit system” (Paper Money/Assignat Bank 1786; first major foreign loans 1769 via Hope & Co. Amsterdam; 18 loans 1788–93); court bankers (Sutherland etc.) for foreign payments. 19th c.: Kankrin monetary reform 1839–41; State Bank founded 31 May 1860 (“bank of banks”); Stieglitz last court banker its first director, brokered Rothschild/Baring loans. 1864+ joint-stock banks (Petersburg Private, International Commercial, Volga-Kama, Russian for Foreign Trade); spirits monopoly (Witte 1894, expanded empire-wide). Witte gold standard (decree 29 Aug 1897): State Bank issuing rights; foreign investment push; commerce tax reform; industrial production volume +700% last 40 yrs of 19th c. (vs Germany 500%). “By the beginning of the twentieth century Russia became the world’s leading extractor of oil.” But lag: cast iron per capita 1.04 pood (GB 13.1); trade turnover <1/3 Germany/US. Banks concentrated (8 largest Petersburg 61% market share 1914); financed industry + Far East penetration (Russo-Chinese Bank). Post-1905: Kokovtsov focused on gold standard + large 1906 French-led loan (2.25B francs) to stabilise after revolution/gold drain. `produced:` state-led modernisation + banking system; `contributed_to:` rapid 1890s industrialisation but periphery status and social strains leading to 1905/1917.

From central government start (Shakibi): Senate (1711) evolved from Peter’s co-ordinating/supervisory body to high court of review. State Council (1810) intended as “focal point of all affairs of the central administration” for legislation review but “Alexander III (1885 decree) legalised what had been long going on... all commands of the emperor carried the full force of legality.” Committee of Ministers (1802/1812) and Council of Ministers (1857, revived for reforms) struggled with co-ordination; no permanent chairman/first minister until post-1905. Nicholas I relied on personal Chancellery (esp. Third Section police). Alexander II used Council of Ministers + strong ministers (e.g. Loris-Melikov) for Great Reforms unity. Nicholas II: “He (Stolypin) dies in my service, true, but he was always so anxious to keep me in the background... Don’t I count? Am I nobody?”; undermined chairmen via favourites/individual reports, producing “ministerial disunity” and “paralysis of the central governing organs”. Autocracy vs bureaucracy: emperors sought personal control while needing expertise; “divide and rule”. Subordinate organs delivered reforms (emancipation, zemstva, judiciary, railways, industrialisation) but supreme organs failed to co-ordinate at top. `produced:` Great Reforms infrastructure; `contributed_to:` 1917 collapse via disunity + autocratic override. `part_of:` russian-empire central institutions.

- **European social structure on the eve of 1914** ([[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton and Hessler]], ch. 1). Class position was highly visible in 1914 Europe — legible in clothing, stature, and speech (Shaw's *Pygmalion*, 1900, dramatizes the class-marking power of English accent). Poor men in England averaged three inches shorter than the wealthy even after nutrition began improving late in the century. Despite real material progress — purchasing power nearly doubled in England, France, and Germany between 1880 and 1914 — poverty remained widespread even in the richest cities: Seebohm Rowntree's 1899 house-to-house survey of York found 28% of inhabitants below the minimum for "physical efficiency," and a contemporary London survey found 30.7% in poverty; average life expectancy in the Balkans and Spain was under 35 in 1900. Social-insurance systems were nascent — Germany led with compulsory state health and retirement insurance after 1883, France followed with voluntary schemes in the 1890s, and Britain's Liberal government under David Lloyd George introduced compulsory national health and unemployment insurance in 1911 (still excluding the self-employed, agricultural workers, and domestics). Wealth concentration in Britain illustrates the wider Western European pattern: in 1914 about 120,000 households owned roughly two-thirds of Britain's capital stock, including some 40,000 landowners holding 27 of 34 million acres, while roughly two-thirds of the population held only 5% of national wealth; only about 1 in 170 English adults paid income tax. Eastern and Southern Europe show a starkly different agrarian pattern from Western Europe's independent-farmer belt: vast, inefficiently cultivated aristocratic latifundia (the Radziwiłł family alone held 500,000 acres in Poland, the Esterházy family 750,000 in Hungary) and Russian agricultural productivity lagging badly behind German and English yields. Where independent peasant proprietorship prevailed (Western/Northern Europe) it acted as a conservative counterweight to urban unrest; where landless, land-hungry peasant masses prevailed (Eastern/Southern Europe), rural society was, in Paxton's phrase, "a potential social volcano." By 1914, 74% of Europeans lived in cities — a share matched elsewhere only in North America — and six European cities exceeded 1 million inhabitants.

## Transition

**From:** the [[age-of-expansion|Age of Expansion]] (1700–1800) and the
[[atlantic-revolutions|Atlantic revolutions]] of c. 1775–1825, which reordered
politics and inaugurated the retreat of the first (Atlantic) wave of European
colonialism while introducing the large-scale republic and the national state.
**To:** the [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]] (1914–1945). The relative peace
among European great powers between Waterloo (1815) and Sarajevo (1914) ended
catastrophically; the industrial and organizational capacities built up across the
long nineteenth century were turned to the unprecedented destruction of the world
wars. See [[1914-end-of-long-19th-century]].

## Historiography

The "long nineteenth century" is Hobsbawm's periodization (*The Age of Revolution*,
*Capital*, *Empire*) — now ingested directly: **[[hobsbawm-age-of-revolution|Age of Revolution]]**,
**[[hobsbawm-age-of-capital|Age of Capital]]**, **[[hobsbawm-age-of-empire|Age of Empire]]**, framed by
the **[[dual-revolution|dual revolution]]** thesis. C. A. Bayly's *The Birth of the Modern World, 1780–1914*
reframed it as a story of global "connections and comparisons" rather than European
diffusion. CWH VII's signature contributions are **multipolar**: Kaoru Sugihara's
argument that East Asian, **labor-intensive** industrialization is as central to
the global story as the Western capital-intensive path, and the broader insistence
(against Landes's "European miracle" and the older Marx/Weber "rise of the West")
that the [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] was late and contingent on "coal and
North America" (Pomeranz). Debates: the timing of "globalization" (O'Rourke &
Williamson's post-1840 price-convergence test vs. Flynn & Giráldez's earlier,
broader definition); whether 1750, 1800, or 1870 is the better economic start date;
and the relation of nineteenth-century imperial violence to twentieth-century
genocide (Levene, Kinsey, Arendt's "boomerang" thesis).

## Collection Coverage Note

This page rests almost entirely on [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII Book 1]], the
wiki's first substantial source for the post-1750 world. Coverage is **moderate
and thematic**: strong on the material matrices (energy, industrialization,
agriculture, demography, disease), the politics of empire and nation, and broad
regional surveys. It is thin on narrative political and military history (no
dedicated event pages yet for the Napoleonic Wars, the revolutions of 1848, the
