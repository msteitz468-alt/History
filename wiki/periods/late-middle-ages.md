## Overview

The Late Middle Ages (1300–1500 CE) is the closing act of [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]]'s
**"Middle Millennium"** — the century-and-a-half in which the Afro-Eurasian
exchange system reached its maximum integration, suffered catastrophic shock, and
reconstituted itself on the threshold of genuine globe-spanning contact. Around
1250–1350 the **[[afro-eurasian-world-system|world system]]** described by
Abu-Lughod — eight interlocking commercial circuits from England to China,
knitted together by the **Pax Mongolica** — operated at its fullest extent. Then,
from 1347, the **[[black-death|Black Death]]** travelled those same webs, killing
perhaps a third of the population from China to Europe and exposing how
connection breeds vulnerability as well as wealth.

The recovery reshaped the political map. The Mongol order fragmented and was
briefly revived by [[timur|Timur]] (d. 1405); the **[[ottoman-empire|Ottomans]]**
rose to take [[constantinople|Constantinople]] in 1453, ending the
[[byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]]; **[[ming-dynasty|Ming]] China** turned the
treasure fleets of [[zheng-he|Zheng He]] (1405–1433) across the Indian Ocean and
then withdrew them; [[mali-empire|Mali]] reached its apogee under [[mansa-musa|Mansa
Musa]]; and in the Americas the **[[aztec-empire|Aztec]]** and **[[inca-empire|Inca]]**
empires assembled the largest states the hemisphere had known. By 1500 — the
volume's terminus — Europe's improving cartography and gunpowder navies were
poised to weld the two hemispheres into one world, the threshold that the next
volume ([[bentley-cwh-v6|CWH VI]]) takes up.

## Major Developments

**The integrated Eurasian system at its peak.** Under the Pax Mongolica, merchants,
missionaries, and envoys (Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta, Rabban Sauma) crossed Eurasia
end to end; a single silver-based monetary stratum and the
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|eight-circuit world system]] linked England to China.
See [[afro-eurasian-world-system]].

**The Black Death and the fourteenth-century crisis.** Plague erupted in the
1340s, spread along the trade and conquest routes, and triggered demographic
collapse, social upheaval, and economic restructuring across Afro-Eurasia. See
[[black-death]].

**The breakup of the Mongol empire and Timur.** The successor khanates (Yuan,
Ilkhanate, Golden Horde, Chaghatai) fragmented; [[timur|Timur]] briefly rebuilt a
Central Asian empire from Samarkand before his death in 1405.

**The rise of the Ottomans.** The [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman]] gunpowder state
absorbed the Balkans and Anatolia and took [[constantinople|Constantinople]]
(1453), extinguishing the last Roman state and mastering, with the Europeans, the
new gunpowder warfare that closes the period (Rogers).

**Ming China and the treasure voyages.** The [[ming-dynasty|Ming]] (1368) restored
a Han-Chinese empire; [[zheng-he|Zheng He's]] vast fleets reached East Africa
(1405–1433) before the voyages were discontinued and their records destroyed.

**The apogee of Mali and the Sahelian states.** [[mali-empire|Mali]] under
[[mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]] (whose 1324 pilgrimage placed him on the 1375 Catalan
Atlas) dominated the trans-Saharan gold trade; Gao expanded toward the Songhay
Empire. See [[western-sudan-states]].

**The rise of the American empires.** [[aztec-empire|Tenochtitlan's Triple Alliance]]
(from 1428) and the [[inca-empire|Inca]] (from 1438) built the largest
pre-Columbian states — the subjects of the volume's final two chapters.

## Geographic Scope

The full Old World belt operates as one connected system at the period's opening,
fractures under plague and political collapse, and reconsolidates around new
gunpowder empires (Ottoman, Ming, Timurid). West Africa is firmly tied into the
Mediterranean economy via the gold trade. In the western hemisphere the Aztec and
Inca empires integrate Mesoamerica and the Andes internally as never before, still
without contact between them — until, at the very edge of the period, European
navigation begins to close the hemispheric gap.

## Transition

**Entry:** from the [[high-middle-ages]] at c. 1300 CE, with the
[[mongol-empire|Mongol]]-integrated Eurasian system at its height.

**Exit:** at 1500 CE, the terminus of the "Middle Millennium" and of
[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]]. The threshold events are the European voyages of
oceanic exploration (Portuguese rounding of Africa from the 1480s; Columbus 1492)
and the mastery of gunpowder, which together open the [[early-modern|Early Modern]]
period and the genuine globalization taken up by [[bentley-cwh-v6|CWH VI]]. This is
a true [[transition]]: 1492 and the Columbian Exchange is a standing transition
page on the wiki's list.

## Historiography

**Source quality.** Very rich for the Eurasian core and now, increasingly, for the
connections between regions: the Mongol-era world historians (above all
[[rashid-al-din|Rashid al-Din]]'s *Compendium of Chronicles*, c. 1300 — the one
near-hemispheric history of the age), the great travel accounts (Marco Polo, Ibn
Battuta, Wang Dayuan), Ming and European archives, and abundant material evidence.
For the Americas, Spanish-recorded indigenous traditions (Aztec annals, Inca
narratives), surviving Maya and Mexica codices, the archaeological record, and the
still-undeciphered Inca *khipu* are the principal sources.

**Major debates.** The origins, mortality, and economic consequences of the Black
Death (and whether it was bubonic plague alone); whether the fourteenth-century
crisis was Malthusian, climatic (the onset of the Little Ice Age), or systemic;
the reasons China abandoned the Zheng He voyages on the eve of an oceanic age; and
the "Great Divergence" question of why European, not Chinese or Islamic, expansion
ultimately spanned the oceans.

**Recent revisionism.** Abu-Lughod's thesis of a thirteenth/fourteenth-century
world system "before European hegemony"; the reframing of 1500 not as the dawn of
an inevitable European-led modernity but as one contingent outcome of a long
Afro-Eurasian integration; and the insistence (Smith, MacCormack) that the
American empires be read on their own terms rather than as foils to the conquest.

## Collection Coverage Note

**Strong in potential, lightly ingested so far.** Per CLAUDE.md the collection is
strong for Late Medieval Europe and the Mediterranean and moderate for late
medieval China and the Islamic world, but thin for the Ottoman, Timurid, and
post-1500 Islamic worlds and for the Americas (archaeology only). Only
[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] has been ingested for this period to date, giving the
world-systemic frame and comparative coverage of the Mongol aftermath, the Sahelian
states, and the American empires. The *New Cambridge Medieval History* Vols. 6–7
(Phase 3) and [[bentley-cwh-v6|CWH VI]] are the principal sources still to be
ingested for narrative depth; Ottoman, Timurid, Ming, Aztec, and Inca specialist
material remains a gap.
