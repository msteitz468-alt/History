# World Wars Era (1914–1945 CE)

## Overview

The years **1914–1945** saw the return of genuinely global war after the century
of relative European great-power peace between Waterloo and Sarajevo. In
[[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]'s framing of **production, destruction, and
connection**, this is the period in which *destruction* reached its modern apogee:
the two world wars, waged with both the nineteenth century's high explosives,
rapid-fire arms, telegraphs, and railways and a host of distinctly twentieth-century
technologies — airplanes, poison gas, tanks, radios, and ultimately atomic weapons
— killed on a scale without precedent. The Second World War alone likely exceeded
**60 million dead**, the majority of them civilians, more of whom succumbed to
war-related food-supply failures than to bullets.

## Major Developments

- **[[first-world-war-1914|The First World War (1914–1918)]]** ended four dynastic empires (Habsburg,
  Ottoman, Romanov, Hohenzollern) and, with the post-war settlements, made the
  national state the supposed ideal — concentrating the era's genocides in the
  lands those empires had ruled. The **Armenian genocide** (1915) is its signal
  atrocity (see [[genocide]]). Its much-debated origins are treated on
  **[[causes-of-the-first-world-war]]** (framed by [[joll-origins-first-world-war|Joll]]).
- **[[russian-revolution-1917|The Russian Revolution (1917)]]** (including the **[[february-revolution-1917|February Revolution]]** and **[[october-revolution-1917|October Revolution]]**) and the creation of the **[[soviet-union|Soviet Union]]** (initially led by **[[lenin-vladimir|Vladimir Lenin]]** and **[[trotsky-leon|Leon Trotsky]]** of the **[[bolshevik-party|Bolshevik Party]]**) established the first state committed to Marxist-Leninist communism. This was followed by the **[[russian-civil-war-1918|Russian Civil War]]**, the establishment of **[[war-communism|War Communism]]** and the subsequent **[[new-economic-policy|New Economic Policy (NEP)]]**, and Stalinist consolidation under rapid **[[soviet-industrialization|Soviet Industrialization]]** and forced **[[soviet-collectivization|Soviet Collectivization]]**, leading to the **[[great-purge|Great Purge]]** and the forced-labor Gulag system (administered by the **[[nkvd|NKVD]]**).

From Service (2009, lines 541–1450): The World Wars Era opened for Russia with entry into the Great War (declared by Germany after Russian mobilization in support of Serbia, July–August 1914). Initial advances (East Prussia) collapsed at Tannenberg; the empire conscripted 14 million men by end-1916. Secret treaties with Allies promised the Dardanelles. Economic strains (peasant grain hoarding, transport breakdown, inflation from paper rouble emission, armaments priority starving consumer goods) combined with Rasputin scandal and Nicholas II's assumption of personal command (1915) to erode legitimacy. "Dual power" emerged after the February Revolution (spontaneous strikes/bread riots in Petrograd from 22 Feb 1917; garrison mutiny; Nicholas abdicates 2 March). Provisional Government (Lvov, then Kerenski) promised universal freedoms but deferred land reform and continued the war, alienating workers, soldiers and peasants. Lenin’s April Theses (3 Apr 1917) shifted Bolshevik strategy toward immediate soviet power and socialist transition. Kornilov mutiny (Aug 1917) was defeated by a united socialist front, boosting Bolshevik urban soviet majorities. The October Revolution (25 Oct 1917) was executed by the Petrograd Soviet’s Military-Revolutionary Committee (Trotsky): key points seized; Winter Palace fell; Second Congress of Soviets ratified transfer. Decrees on Peace (no annexations) and Land (expropriation to peasant committees) issued immediately. Constituent Assembly dispersed (5–6 Jan 1918) after SR plurality. Brest-Litovsk (Mar 1918) imposed by Lenin despite fierce internal opposition; lost Ukraine, Baltics and western borderlands.

Civil War (1918–1921) saw Komuch (SRs on Volga), White armies (Kolchak in Siberia, Denikin in south, Yudenich in NW), Czechoslovak Legion, and foreign interventions. Left SRs assassinated German ambassador Mirbach and staged July 1918 revolt. Red Terror followed Lenin assassination attempt (30 Aug 1918); Cheka executions (official 12,733 in 1918–20; higher estimates). Food-Supplies Dictatorship and grain quotas (razvërstka) imposed; kombedy created then abolished. Trotsky transformed the Red Army (ex-officers + political commissars; summary executions of deserters). Reds won key victories (Kazan Sept 1918; Kolchak 1919; Denikin/Yudenich repulsed). Romanovs executed 17 July 1918. By 1920–21 Whites defeated but regime faced Kronstadt mutiny and Tambov peasant revolt. NEP introduced at Tenth Party Congress (Mar 1921): tax-in-kind replaced requisitioning; small private trade and manufacturing re-legalized. Politburo/Orgburo (1919) and nomenklatura system centralized party control. Federal concessions and korenizatsiya used to secure non-Russian support. By late 1920s NEP produced substantial recovery but "scissors crisis" (industrial prices vs agricultural) and renewed grain procurement crises (Urals-Siberian method 1928) set stage for Stalin’s break with NEP. Lenin’s political testament (late 1922) criticized Stalin as "too crude" and warned of split; Lenin died 21 Jan 1924. Succession struggles featured Trotsky’s Left Opposition, Zinoviev/Kamenev Leningrad Opposition, United Opposition, and Stalin-Bukharin alliance advancing "Socialism in One Country" while defeating rivals. Shakhty trial (1928) initiated intimidation of specialists. Stalin’s Jan 1928 Urals/Siberia trip revived coercive grain seizures, fracturing the Stalin-Bukharin duumvirate and inaugurating the end of NEP.
- **Stalin’s Second Revolution (1928–1933) and Great Terror (1936–1938):** Constantly the Politburo quickened the projected pace of industrialization. Cheap labour was made available by peasants fleeing the villages (Service, line 1454). Compulsory collectivization initiated against Bukharin’s counsel; 25,000-ers dispatched; dekulakization (three categories, 5–7 million; sub-kulaks invented); “Dizzy with Success” (Mar 1930); 4–5 million dead in 1932–3 famine from de-kulakization and grain seizures; livestock slaughtered by peasants; kolkhoz lower than sovkhoz; MTS as control mechanism. Industry: First Five-Year Plan “completed” Dec 1932 (exaggerated claims of doubled output; ~10% annual growth, capital goods prioritized); defense sub-sector >6% investment. Kirov assassination (1 Dec 1934) pretext for NKVD emergency powers; show trials (Zinoviev/Kamenev 1936 executed; Pyatakov/Radek 1937; Bukharin 1938); Red Army purge (Tukhachevski et al. shot June 1937); Yezhov quotas (259k+ arrests target), troiki trials, torture sanctioned Aug 1937; 681,692 official executions 1937–8 (1–1.5m total repression deaths); only 1/30 17th Congress delegates survived to 18th; national deportations; Stalin elevated above all institutions by 1938 (Beria succeeds Yezhov). Service: Stalin’s “second revolution” not yet secure; terror hysterically disproportionate; clientelism and deceit integral (lines 1452–1768).
- **The Great Depression (1929–c. 1939)** and the interwar collapse of global
  trade and migration (restrictive legislation after 1920 curbed the great
  nineteenth-century flows).
- **The rise of fascism** in Italy and Nazi Germany; state-led and socialist
  [[industrialization]] programs of varying success across the interwar world.
- **The Second World War (1939–1945)** — the most destructive event in human
  history — and **the Holocaust**, the era's defining genocide. Key Soviet conflicts included the **[[battle-of-stalingrad-1942|Battle of Stalingrad]]** and the **[[battle-of-kursk-1943|Battle of Kursk]]**, following the **[[molotov-ribbentrop-pact-1939|Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact]]**.
- **Hiroshima and Nagasaki (1945):** the first use of atomic weapons. Josephson
  notes the bombs may have been used as much to demonstrate their power as from
  military necessity — the "golden spike" candidate (radioactive fallout, 1945–1963)
  for the geological [[anthropocene|Anthropocene]].
- **Acceleration of material life:** despite the wars and depression, population,
  energy use, and industrial capacity continued their climb toward the post-1950
  [[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]].

## Key Actors

- The **Allied** and **Axis** coalitions; the **[[soviet-union|Soviet Union]]** under **[[lenin-vladimir|Vladimir Lenin]]**, **[[trotsky-leon|Leon Trotsky]]**, and **[[stalin-joseph|Joseph Stalin]]** (with security apparatuses like the **[[cheka|Cheka]]** and **[[nkvd|NKVD]]**); **Nazi Germany** under Hitler; **Imperial Japan**; the **United States** (see [[united-states]]) emerging as the dominant industrial and military power.
- Movements and ideologies: [[marxism-leninism|Marxism-Leninism]], [[democratic-centralism|Democratic Centralism]], [[socialist-realism|Socialist Realism]], and fascism;
  anti-colonial [[nationalism]] gathering force in India, the Middle East, and East
  Asia as a prelude to post-war [[decolonization]].

## Geographic Scope

Global, but with European, East Asian (the Sino-Japanese war from 1937), and North
African/Near Eastern theaters at the center of the fighting; the Americas as the
"arsenal"; colonial populations mobilized for both world wars.

## Transition

**From:** the [[long-19th-century|Long 19th Century]]. The industrial and
organizational capacities, the nationalist ideologies, and the great-power rivalries
built up before 1914 turned the relative peace of the nineteenth century into total
war. See [[1914-end-of-long-19th-century]]. **To:** the [[cold-war|Cold War]]
(1945–1991). The 1945 settlement — the United Nations, the bipolar order, the
nuclear age, and the imminent wave of [[decolonization]] — defines the next period.
See [[1945-postwar-order]].

## Historiography

The standard accounts (Hobsbawm's "Age of Catastrophe," 1914–1945 — now ingested directly as
**[[hobsbawm-age-of-extremes|The Age of Extremes]]**) treat the two
wars, the Depression, and the totalitarianisms as a single systemic crisis of the
industrial-national order. CWH VII's distinctive moves are to set the era's
*destruction* in a long material frame — linking twentieth-century genocide to
nineteenth-century imperial violence, nationalism, and social-evolutionary thought
(Levene, Kinsey, drawing on Arendt and Hull) — and to read fascism and communism
comparatively as projects of violent social "cleansing" (Strayer). Debate persists
over the singularity vs. comparability of the Holocaust, the necessity of the atomic
bombings, and whether 1914 or 1945 marks the sharper twentieth-century break.

Robert Service's narrative history redefines the "Soviet compound" (one-party dictatorship, ideological monopoly, state economic dominance, legal nihilism, hyper-centralism) as a durable system assembled under Lenin and remodelled under Stalin that survived seven decades not only through terror but through graduated rewards, activation campaigns, and informal practices (corruption, clientelism, low effort, misinformation) as the necessary "lubricating oil" that kept the machinery from seizing up. Service stresses that leaders (especially Lenin and Stalin) mattered enormously; contingency was real; the system contained self-limiting features (clientelism, low productivity, nationalism) that eventually contributed to dissolution. He treats continuities with the tsarist past as real but secondary to the transformative (and ultimately self-undermining) effects of Bolshevik rule. For the Great Terror, Service emphasizes Stalin's personality (hyper-suspicious, in his element amidst chaos; fascination with Genghis Khan, Ivan the Terrible, Peter the Great) and the disproportionate reaction to real threats; official 681,692 executions 1937–8 but likely 1–1.5 million total repression deaths; only 1 in 30 17th Congress delegates survived; clientelism survived the terror. On WWII, Service details Stalin's multiple grave miscalculations (Barbarossa surprise despite warnings; initial no-retaliation orders; Order 270/227; separate peace feelers; Warsaw halt) alongside massive industrial evacuation and out-production of Germany, Lend-Lease significance (~1/5 GDP), and Allied conferences where Stalin secured eastern European spheres while complaining about second front delays. Postwar, Service portrays Stalin reimposing the pre-war compound despite popular hopes for relaxation, with renewed deportations, filtration of POWs (half sent to Gulag), atomic project using Gulag labor, Cominform/Tito break, Eastern European one-party imposition, and Doctors' Plot. Service's synthesis gives presumptive weight to high politics and leadership decisions grounded in post-1991 archives while noting the "Soviet compound" contained the seeds of its own limits.
