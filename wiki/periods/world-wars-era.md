# World Wars Era (1914–1945 CE)

## Overview

The years **1914–1945** saw the return of genuinely global war after the century
of relative European great-power peace between Waterloo and Sarajevo. In
[[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]'s framing of **production, destruction, and
connection**, this is the period in which *destruction* reached its modern apogee:
the two world wars, waged with both the nineteenth century's high explosives,
rapid-fire arms, telegraphs, and railways and a host of distinctly twentieth-century
technologies — airplanes, poison gas, tanks, radios, and ultimately atomic weapons
— killed on a scale without precedent. The Second World War alone likely exceeded
**60 million dead**, the majority of them civilians, more of whom succumbed to
war-related food-supply failures than to bullets.

## Major Developments

  Ottoman, Romanov, Hohenzollern) and, with the post-war settlements, made the
  national state the supposed ideal — concentrating the era's genocides in the
  lands those empires had ruled. The **Armenian genocide** (1915) is its signal
  atrocity (see [[genocide]]). Its much-debated origins are treated on
  **[[causes-of-the-first-world-war]]** (framed by [[joll-origins-first-world-war|Joll]]). - **[[russian-revolution-1917|The Russian Revolution (1917)]]** (including the **[[february-revolution-1917|February Revolution]]** and **[[october-revolution-1917|October Revolution]]**) and the creation of the **[[soviet-union|Soviet Union]]** (initially led by **[[lenin-vladimir|Vladimir Lenin]]** and **[[trotsky-leon|Leon Trotsky]]** of the **[[bolshevik-party|Bolshevik Party]]**) established the first state committed to Marxist-Leninist communism. From Service (2009, lines 541–1450): The World Wars Era opened for Russia with entry into the Great War (declared by Germany after Russian mobilization in support of Serbia, July–August 1914). Initial advances (East Prussia) collapsed at Tannenberg; the empire conscripted 14 million men by end-1916. Secret treaties with Allies promised the Dardanelles. Economic strains (peasant grain hoarding, transport breakdown, inflation from paper rouble emission, armaments priority starving consumer goods) combined with Rasputin scandal and Nicholas II's assumption of personal command (1915) to erode legitimacy. "Dual power" emerged after the February Revolution (spontaneous strikes/bread riots in Petrograd from 22 Feb 1917; garrison mutiny; Nicholas abdicates 2 March). Provisional Government (Lvov, then Kerenski) promised universal freedoms but deferred land reform and continued the war, alienating workers, soldiers and peasants. Lenin’s April Theses (3 Apr 1917) shifted Bolshevik strategy toward immediate soviet power and socialist transition. Kornilov mutiny (Aug 1917) was defeated by a united socialist front, boosting Bolshevik urban soviet majorities. The October Revolution (25 Oct 1917) was executed by the Petrograd Soviet’s Military-Revolutionary Committee (Trotsky): key points seized; Winter Palace fell; Second Congress of Soviets ratified transfer. Decrees on Peace (no annexations) and Land (expropriation to peasant committees) issued immediately. Constituent Assembly dispersed (5–6 Jan 1918) after SR plurality. Brest-Litovsk (Mar 1918) imposed by Lenin despite fierce internal opposition; lost Ukraine, Baltics and western borderlands.

**Detailed triggers and dynamics of Feb 1917 collapse (Cambridge Vol. II, Lohr ch. "War and revolution, 1914–1917" + Daly police ch., range 18001–18709)**: Heavy snows Feb 1917 slowed trains + flour supply; 19 Feb govt announced bread rationing for 1 Mar → panic buying. 23 Feb walkouts + International Women’s Day protests escalated to ~200k strikers 24 Feb; crowds on Nevsky, Marseillaise, anti-monarchy banners (ll. 18417–18425). 26 Feb loyal troops fired, killing hundreds (parallels 1905 Bloody Sunday); bulk of Petrograd garrison (180k+150k suburbs; mostly untrained recent recruits, convalescents, resentful primary breadwinners) mutinied 26–27 Feb, freed prisoners, sacked police HQs, took govt buildings. “The proximate cause of greatest weight in explaining the end of the monarchy was the mutiny of the Petrograd garrison” (ll. 18481). Duma committee + Petrograd Soviet EC negotiated Provisional Govt (Lvov et al.); universal rights, amnesty, Constituent Assembly proclaimed. Tsar (isolated in Pskov) abdicated 2 Mar (first for son, then brother Mikhail who refused); “on 3 March the 300-year-old Romanov dynasty came to an end” (cross-ref). Army commanders (Alekseev circular) unanimously backed abdication to save war/nation (Brusilov: choose Russia over tsar). Police/repression context (Daly): Zubatov-era informants (Azef 1909 exposure, Bogrov killed Stolypin 1911); Dzhunkovskii 1913–14 bureau abolitions; Lena 1912 (172 dead) unrest. Economic: prohibition cost 2.5bn roubles/10% war exp by mid-1917 (ll. 18523); money supply quadrupled post-gold abandonment 27 Jul/8 Aug 1914 (ll. 18533); foreign investment halt + blockade; grain hoarding from inflation/admin controls (“peasants... waiting for higher grain prices... store... or illicitly convert it into alcohol” ll. 18541); women industry share 27%→43% (ll. 18565). Progressive Bloc + war councils + Zemgor assumed parastatal functions 1915; Nicholas personal command Aug 1915 + Rasputin discredited regime. 4–10 Mar Provisional abolished security bureaus/Police Dept/Gendarme Corps. “the dismantling of the imperial police apparatus... was an invitation to takeover by political conspirators” (ll. 18387). `caused_by:` bread crisis + garrison mutiny + army commanders + economic war distortions + elite alienation. `produced:` end of Russian Empire/Romanovs, dual power. `part_of:` [[world-wars-era]] global imperial collapse. See [[february-revolution-1917]], [[russian-empire]], [[nicholas-ii]], [[stolypin-pyotr]]. Historiography: emphasises contingency of mutiny over deep structural inevitability alone; security police sophisticated yet unable to prevent mass spontaneous discontent (vs conspiracy).

Civil War (1918–1921) saw Komuch (SRs on Volga), White armies (Kolchak in Siberia, Denikin in south, Yudenich in NW), Czechoslovak Legion, and foreign interventions. Left SRs assassinated German ambassador Mirbach and staged July 1918 revolt. Red Terror followed Lenin assassination attempt (30 Aug 1918); Cheka executions (official 12,733 in 1918–20; higher estimates). Food-Supplies Dictatorship and grain quotas (razvërstka) imposed; kombedy created then abolished. Trotsky transformed the Red Army (ex-officers + political commissars; summary executions of deserters). Reds won key victories (Kazan Sept 1918; Kolchak 1919; Denikin/Yudenich repulsed). Romanovs executed 17 July 1918. By 1920–21 Whites defeated but regime faced Kronstadt mutiny and Tambov peasant revolt. NEP introduced at Tenth Party Congress (Mar 1921): tax-in-kind replaced requisitioning; small private trade and manufacturing re-legalized. Politburo/Orgburo (1919) and nomenklatura system centralized party control. Federal concessions and korenizatsiya used to secure non-Russian support. By late 1920s NEP produced substantial recovery but "scissors crisis" (industrial prices vs agricultural) and renewed grain procurement crises (Urals-Siberian method 1928) set stage for Stalin’s break with NEP. Lenin’s political testament (late 1922) criticized Stalin as "too crude" and warned of split; Lenin died 21 Jan 1924. Succession struggles featured Trotsky’s Left Opposition, Zinoviev/Kamenev Leningrad Opposition, United Opposition, and Stalin-Bukharin alliance advancing "Socialism in One Country" while defeating rivals. Shakhty trial (1928) initiated intimidation of specialists. Stalin’s Jan 1928 Urals/Siberia trip revived coercive grain seizures, fracturing the Stalin-Bukharin duumvirate and inaugurating the end of NEP.
- **Stalin’s Second Revolution (1928–1933) and Great Terror (1936–1938):** Constantly the Politburo quickened the projected pace of industrialization. Cheap labour was made available by peasants fleeing the villages (Service, line 1454). Compulsory collectivization initiated against Bukharin’s counsel; 25,000-ers dispatched; dekulakization (three categories, 5–7 million; sub-kulaks invented); “Dizzy with Success” (Mar 1930); 4–5 million dead in 1932–3 famine from de-kulakization and grain seizures; livestock slaughtered by peasants; kolkhoz lower than sovkhoz; MTS as control mechanism. Industry: First Five-Year Plan “completed” Dec 1932 (exaggerated claims of doubled output; ~10% annual growth, capital goods prioritized); defense sub-sector >6% investment. Kirov assassination (1 Dec 1934) pretext for NKVD emergency powers; show trials (Zinoviev/Kamenev 1936 executed; Pyatakov/Radek 1937; Bukharin 1938); Red Army purge (Tukhachevski et al. shot June 1937); Yezhov quotas (259k+ arrests target), troiki trials, torture sanctioned Aug 1937; 681,692 official executions 1937–8 (1–1.5m total repression deaths); only 1/30 17th Congress delegates survived to 18th; national deportations; Stalin elevated above all institutions by 1938 (Beria succeeds Yezhov). Service: Stalin’s “second revolution” not yet secure; terror hysterically disproportionate; clientelism and deceit integral (lines 1452–1768). Special Camps/katorga and exile system (mass from 1929, "temporary pen to hold sheep marked for slaughter"; ~15m peasants in "Peasant Plague" famine) emerged as core institutions (Solzhenitsyn lines 6647–6725). Kengir and other camp resistance foreshadowed limits.

## Geographic Scope

Global, but with European, East Asian (the Sino-Japanese war from 1937), and North
African/Near Eastern theaters at the center of the fighting; the Americas as the
"arsenal"; colonial populations mobilized for both world wars.

## Historiography

The standard accounts (Hobsbawm's "Age of Catastrophe," 1914–1945 — now ingested directly as
**[[hobsbawm-age-of-extremes|The Age of Extremes]]**) treat the two
wars, the Depression, and the totalitarianisms as a single systemic crisis of the
industrial-national order. CWH VII's distinctive moves are to set the era's
*destruction* in a long material frame — linking twentieth-century genocide to
nineteenth-century imperial violence, nationalism, and social-evolutionary thought
(Levene, Kinsey, drawing on Arendt and Hull) — and to read fascism and communism
comparatively as projects of violent social "cleansing" (Strayer). Debate persists
over the singularity vs. comparability of the Holocaust, the necessity of the atomic
bombings, and whether 1914 or 1945 marks the sharper twentieth-century break.

Totalitarian model undermined by social historians in 1970s but reappeared in 'neo-totalitarian' version drawing on darker readings of Enlightenment and modernity. 'The historiography of the USSR was divided by the Cold War chasm between East and West and by political passions in the West that kept Left and Right in rival camps.' Post-1991 archives enabled new empirical readings; 'the dynamic political conflicts among scholars in the past have actually enriched the field'. Robert Service's narrative history redefines the "Soviet compound" (one-party dictatorship, ideological monopoly, state economic dominance, legal nihilism, hyper-centralism) as a durable system assembled under Lenin and remodelled under Stalin that survived seven decades not only through terror but through graduated rewards, activation campaigns, and informal practices (corruption, clientelism, low effort, misinformation) as the necessary "lubricating oil" that kept the machinery from seizing up. Service stresses that leaders (especially Lenin and Stalin) mattered enormously; contingency was real; the system contained self-limiting features (clientelism, low productivity, nationalism) that eventually contributed to dissolution. He treats continuities with the tsarist past as real but secondary to the transformative (and ultimately self-undermining) effects of Bolshevik rule. For the Great Terror, Service emphasizes Stalin's personality (hyper-suspicious, in his element amidst chaos; fascination with Genghis Khan, Ivan the Terrible, Peter the Great) and the disproportionate reaction to real threats; official 681,692 executions 1937–8 but likely 1–1.5 million total repression deaths; only 1 in 30 17th Congress delegates survived; clientelism survived the terror. On WWII, Service details Stalin's multiple grave miscalculations (Barbarossa surprise despite warnings; initial no-retaliation orders; Order 270/227; separate peace feelers; Warsaw halt) alongside massive industrial evacuation and out-production of Germany, Lend-Lease significance (~1/5 GDP), and Allied conferences where Stalin secured eastern European spheres while complaining about second front delays. Postwar, Service portrays Stalin reimposing the pre-war compound despite popular hopes for relaxation, with renewed deportations, filtration of POWs (half sent to Gulag), atomic project using Gulag labor, Cominform/Tito break, Eastern European one-party imposition, and Doctors' Plot. Service's synthesis gives presumptive weight to high politics and leadership decisions grounded in post-1991 archives while noting the "Soviet compound" contained the seeds of its own limits.
