## Overview

The **Greek Dark Age** is the regional name for the Aegean from the fall of the
[[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean palace civilization]] (~1200 BCE) to the eve of
the eighth-century "Greek Renascence." It is the Aegean face of the
[[early-iron-age|Early Iron Age]] (period 12) and the aftermath of the
[[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]]. The label is conventional and
increasingly contested (see Historiography): "dark" denotes both the **drastic
decline** of the twelfth and eleventh centuries — depopulation, the loss of
writing, the end of monumental building — and the **darkness of our evidence**, an
almost wholly archaeological and (later) legendary record with no contemporary
texts.

[[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II.2]] devotes its final section (chapters
XXXVI–XL) to this period and treats it from four converging angles: the
**archaeology** of the Mycenaean collapse and Protogeometric recovery (Desborough),
the **migration legends** preserved by Thucydides and Herodotus (Hammond), the
**Greek settlement of Asia Minor** (Cook), the **prehistory of the Greek language**
(Chadwick), and the **Homeric poems and Greek religion** as windows onto the age
(Kirk, Guthrie).

## Major Developments

**The final collapse of the palace world (LH IIIC, c. 1200–1050 BCE).** A wave of
destructions around 1200 fell on the central and southern mainland — Mycenae
(twice), Tiryns, Pylos, Iolcus, Gla, Crisa, the Menelaion — breaking the central
political control. A **partial recovery** in earlier LH IIIC (the fine "Close Style"
pottery of the Argolid; thriving island and refugee centres in the Dodecanese,
Naxos, Perati, Lefkandi) gave way to a **final disintegration** (a last destruction
at Mycenae ~1130, then the impoverished **sub-Mycenaean** style ~1050). The hallmark
losses are stark: **Linear B literacy vanished entirely**, monumental stone-building
ceased, work in metal/ivory/precious stone collapsed, and population fell sharply,
with many sites simply abandoned and never reoccupied. (Desborough, Ch. XXXVI.)

**Depopulation and migration.** Refugees moved from the ruined heartlands (Messenia,
Laconia, the Argolid) to frontier and refuge zones — Achaea, Cephallenia, the
islands, Cyprus, and Crete — and ultimately overseas, the archaeological correlate of
the legendary [[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Dorian invasion]] and
[[ionian-migration-1050bce|Ionian migration]].

**The Protogeometric recovery (c. 1050–900 BCE).** From Athens — never abandoned, the
chief surviving centre — a **new ceramic style** arose out of sub-Mycenaean: the
**Protogeometric**, with a faster wheel, compass-drawn circles, and a new disciplined
sense of decoration. It is "a creative and dynamic style," and its spread across the
Aegean traces the resumption of seaborne contact. A **second cultural province**
(coastal Thessaly, Euboea, the northern Cyclades) developed in parallel; its key
sites are **Iolcus** (four Protogeometric levels of good stone houses) and
**Lefkandi** in Euboea (near-continuous occupation through the age).

**The spread of iron and new customs.** Iron-working entered the Aegean from Cyprus at
the sub-Mycenaean transition and, in Attica, the Argolid, and Euboea, **replaced
bronze almost entirely** for tools and weapons. Burial shifted from the Mycenaean
family chamber-tomb to **single inhumation in cist graves and pit graves**, with
**cremation** spreading in Athens and a few centres; new dress (the arched fibula and
long dress-pin) appears.

**From palace to village to polis.** The Dark Age unit was not the kingdom but the
**village** (*kome*, *damos*). Yet the period did **not** revert toward the
Mycenaean order; it moved instead "towards the conditions under which the independent
city-state developed." On the Asia Minor coast the
[[ionian-migration-1050bce|Ionian settlements]] produced, by the late eighth century
at Old Smyrna, "the first certain and unambiguous apparition of the organized
Hellenic *polis*."

## Key Actors

- **[[dorians|The Dorians / Heracleidae]]** — the West-Greek-speaking invaders from
  the northwest who seized the [[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Peloponnese]].
- **Athens** — the chief surviving urban centre, refuge of Mycenaean nobility
  (the Neleidae), home of the Protogeometric style, and metropolis of the
  [[ionian-migration-1050bce|Ionian migration]].
- **The Ionians and Aeolians** — the migrants who carried Greek settlement and the
  Homeric tradition across the Aegean to [[ionian-migration-1050bce|Asia Minor]].
- **[[homer|Homer]]** — the eighth-century Ionian monumental composer in whom the
  oral tradition of the age crystallized.

## Geographic Scope

Active in the southern and central Aegean (Athens/Attica, the Argolid, Euboea,
coastal Thessaly, the Cyclades, the Dodecanese), in **Crete** (treated as a partly
distinct unit, with its sub-Minoan sequence and strong Cypriot contacts), and — from
the tenth century — on the **west coast of Asia Minor** (Miletus, Smyrna, the Ionian
and Aeolic cities). Cyprus, never fully "dark," is the conduit through which iron and
eastern contact reached the Aegean.

## Transition

**Entry:** the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] and the fall of
the [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] palaces (the close of the
[[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze Age]]).

**Exit:** the recovery of the ninth and eighth centuries — the maturing of the
[[ionian-migration-1050bce|Ionian]] cities, the rise of the polis, the return of
alphabetic literacy (the [[alphabet|Phoenician alphabet]] adapted not long before
700), overseas colonization, and the monumental composition of the Homeric poems —
carries the Aegean into the [[archaic-period|Archaic Period]] (800–500 BCE).

## The Recovery and the Birth of the Polis (CAH III.1)

[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] (Chs. 16–18) carries the Aegean
through the **recovery** that ends the Dark Age, treating it region by region
(Snodgrass on Central Greece and Thessaly; Hammond on the Peloponnese; Cook on East
Greece; Boardman on the islands):

- **An Athens-led resurgence.** The revival is read off the **pottery sequence**
  (sub-Mycenaean → Protogeometric → Geometric), which does not arrive everywhere at
  once but spreads, region by region, **from Attica** outward to Thessaly, Boeotia,
  and the rest. Athens — never abandoned — leads, its richest Protogeometric burials
  (gold, imports) showing renewed wealth, underwritten by the **mastery of
  iron-working** (which filled the gap left by the bronze-metal shortage) and by
  **silver extraction at Laurium**. Population climbs steadily through the period in
  Attica, Euboea, and the Argolid.
- **[[euboea|Euboea]] takes the lead overseas.** The single most dynamic region is
  Euboea, above all **Lefkandi**, casting bronze tripods before 900 and importing
  Phoenician, Cypriot, and Egyptian goods through the ninth century. The **Euboeans —
  not easterners — took the first step in the orientalizing of Greek culture**,
  founding the trading post of **Al Mina** in Syria (~end of the 9th c.) and the
  western colony of **Pithecusae** on Ischia (~770) — driven by hunger for **metal**,
  not land. Through them flowed the eastern goods, motifs, and the
  [[alphabet|alphabet]] that powered the eighth-century renaissance.
- **From village to polis.** Hammond's chapter on the Peloponnese argues that the
  **city-state emerged directly out of the Dark Age**: scattered village-encampments
  (especially in the Dorian states) **combined** for corporate religious and political
  action — the cult of **Athena Poliouchos** ("guardian of the polis") on the Spartan
  acropolis being emblematic — producing the first poleis at **Sparta, Megara,
  Corinth, and Argos**. On the Asia Minor coast, Old Smyrna yields "the first certain
  and unambiguous apparition of the organized Hellenic *polis*." See
  [[city-state|the city-state]].

## Historiography

**Source quality.** The Dark Age is the test case for doing history without
contemporary texts. The evidence is **pottery seriation** (sub-Mycenaean →
Protogeometric → Geometric, anchored to Athens), **burial archaeology**, and the
**later Greek literary tradition** — Thucydides (I.12), Herodotus, and the
genealogies of the migrating royal houses — which Hammond (Ch. XXXVI) argues
preserves, through cult and family memory, a "fairly accurate foundation of folk
memory" for the sack of Troy, the Dorian invasion, and the Ionian settlement,
"correctly interpreted by Thucydides."

**The "Dark Age" debate.** The label is the period's central historiographical
question. The discovery of **Lefkandi**'s wealth and continuity, of continuous
Aegean–Cypriot contact, and of the creative Protogeometric style has led many
scholars to prefer the neutral "Early Iron Age" and to argue the age was less
impoverished and isolated than the old model held. Kirk (Ch. XXXIX(b)) makes the
point sharply from the literary side: the very survival and elaboration of the
**Homeric oral tradition** across these centuries proves that "social conditions then
were not... utterly chaotic" — singing, listening, and the conscious re-creation of a
heroic past went on continuously, providing "one of the most important elements of a
common culture for the whole of the Greek world."

**Dating and the migrations.** Two loose systems must be coordinated — pottery
sequence and genealogical reckoning (a generation taken as ~30 years) — each elastic
by ~50 years. Hammond's literary chronology (fall of Troy ~1200; Dorian invasion
~1120; Ionian migration begins ~1050) is set against the archaeological dates for the
sub-Mycenaean–Protogeometric transition (~1050) and the Dorian occupation of the
southeastern islands (little before the late tenth century). The two are broadly
compatible but cannot be fixed to the year.
