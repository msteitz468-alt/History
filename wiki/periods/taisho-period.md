# Taishō Period (1912–1926)

The reign of Emperor **Taishō** — the era of **"Taishō democracy,"** when [[japan|Japan's]]
[[meiji-constitution-1889|constitutional]] order moved closest to genuine parliamentary
government, with **party cabinets** governing the country, before the slide into
[[japanese-militarism|militarism]] in the 1930s. A short but pivotal interlude of liberal
politics, mass culture, and social ferment. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v6-twentieth-century|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 6]] (Mitani
Taichirō, ch. 1).

## Overview

After the Meiji oligarchs (*genrō*) faded, power passed to a "new generation" of party
politicians, bureaucrats, and military men whose authority was "narrower and less stable" than
the oligarchs' had been. In the **era of party cabinets (1918–1932)** — bracketing the Taishō
reign — Japan was governed largely by the leaders of the two great parties (the **Seiyūkai**
and the **Minseito**), the cabinet headed by the president of the majority party. Mitani's
central question is **how** party government emerged *despite* a constitution designed to
prevent it.

## The ambiguity of the Meiji constitution

Mitani argues that the **[[meiji-constitution-1889|Meiji constitution]] both hindered and
permitted** party rule. It hindered it through two devices: **imperial sovereignty** (*tennō
taiken* — [[ito-hirobumi|Itō]] held that "if one establishes parliamentary government, one
must reduce the authority of the emperor"), which rejected popular or parliamentary
sovereignty; and the **division of powers** among organs of state (cabinet, Diet, Privy
Council, military, *genrō*), none able to claim the whole. Yet the same ambiguity left a
**vacuum at the center** — the emperor "placed beyond politics by being placed above it" — that
the parties could fill by building Diet majorities and capturing the cabinet. Party government
was thus an **interpretation** of the constitution, not a change to it — which is why it could
be reversed in the 1930s by a different reading.

## Major Developments

- **The Taishō political crisis (1912–13)** and the **Movement to Protect Constitutional
  Government** — popular protest that toppled a cabinet and announced mass politics.
- **The rise of the parties.** [[ito-hirobumi|Itō's]] **Seiyūkai** matured into a governing
  machine; from **Hara Takashi (1918)**, the first commoner and true party prime minister, to
  1932, party presidents normally formed cabinets.
- **Mass society and "Taishō democracy."** Urban growth, a popular press, labor and tenant
  movements, the women's and socialist movements, and **universal manhood suffrage (1925)** —
  paired, tellingly, with the repressive **Peace Preservation Law (1925)**.
- **The economy and diplomacy.** A **World War I export boom** followed by the **postwar
  recession** and the **1923 Great Kantō earthquake**; abroad, Foreign Minister **Shidehara
  Kijūrō's** "cooperative diplomacy" of arms limitation and accommodation with the
  Anglo-American powers.

## Key Actors

- **Hara Takashi** (the first party prime minister); the **Seiyūkai** and **Minseito**
  parties; the constitutional scholar **Minobe Tatsukichi** (the "emperor-as-organ" theory);
  **Shidehara Kijūrō** (cooperative diplomacy).

## Geographic Scope

Centered on a rapidly urbanizing Japan (Tokyo, Osaka) integrated into the world economy and
the post–World War I Versailles–Washington order.

## Transition

**Preceded by** the [[meiji-period|Meiji period]] (the oligarchs' authority receding after
1912). **Followed by** the early **Shōwa** era, in which the party-cabinet order collapsed
into [[japanese-militarism|militarism]] after the [[manchurian-incident-1931|Manchurian
Incident (1931)]] and the assassination of Prime Minister Inukai (May 1932).

## Historiography

The CHJ Vol. 6 (Mitani; and Duus's introduction) frames Taishō democracy through the
**change-vs.-continuity** debate. Older readings treated it as a fragile liberal experiment
doomed by the militarist reaction; the volume stresses instead that party government was a
genuine, if **constitutionally precarious**, achievement built on the very ambiguities that
later allowed its reversal — making the 1930s collapse a *re-interpretation* of the same
constitutional order rather than its overthrow.
