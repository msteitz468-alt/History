## Overview

The Archaic Period (800–500 BCE) is when the "world of states, empires, and
networks" comes decisively into being. Three world-historical developments
converge: the maturation of the first true **territorial empires** (Neo-Assyrian,
Neo-Babylonian, and above all [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid Persian]]); the
explosion of Mediterranean civic life through Greek and Phoenician
**colonization** and the consolidation of the [[polis|polis]]; and the
intellectual ferment that Karl Jaspers called the **[[axial-age|Axial Age]]** —
the roughly simultaneous emergence, between c. 800 and 200 BCE, of the religious
and philosophical traditions (Hebrew prophecy, Greek philosophy, the Buddha and
Mahavira, Confucius and the early Daoists, Zoroaster) that still structure much
of human thought.

In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]'s scheme this is "mid-morning" of the volume's
single day: the period in which the largest agrarian state the world had yet seen
(the Achaemenid Empire, controlling perhaps 10% of the earth's land surface) arose
and reached toward both the Aegean and the Indus, binding the Afro-Eurasian world
zone more tightly than ever before.

## Major Developments

**The first territorial empires.** The [[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]]
(its apogee under Tiglath-Pileser III, Sargon II, Sennacherib, and Ashurbanipal,
8th–7th c. BCE) pioneered systematic conquest, mass deportation, provincial
administration, and a road-and-messenger system. After its fall (612 BCE,
Nineveh) the Neo-Babylonian empire briefly succeeded it, before [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]]
founded the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid Empire]] (550 BCE), which by 500 BCE
ran from the Aegean and Egypt to the Indus — administered through satrapies, the
Royal Road, an imperial Aramaic chancellery, and a tolerant, multi-ethnic
ideology. See [[empire]] and [[state-formation]].

**Greek colonization and the polis.** Between c. 750 and 550 BCE Greek
city-states planted colonies from the Black Sea to Spain, while the
[[polis|polis]] crystallized as a distinctive form of self-governing civic
community through *synoikism* (political fusion of villages). There were ultimately c. 1,500 poleis (most small, with only a few hundred to a few thousand adult male citizens); the polis was simultaneously city and state. [[coinage]] (invented in Lydia c. 600 BCE) and the mature
[[alphabet|alphabet]] spread with trade. Hesiod and the Homeric poems are written
down; lawgivers (Draco, Solon) and tyrants reshape civic order — the matrix from
which [[classical-antiquity|classical]] Athens and Sparta emerge.

**The [[axial-age|Axial Age]].** The defining intellectual claim of the volume's
Part I: a cluster of transcendence-oriented breakthroughs across Eurasia —
[[zoroastrianism|Zoroaster]] in Iran, the Hebrew prophets, the pre-Socratics in
Ionia, the [[buddha|Buddha]] and Mahavira in the Ganges, [[confucius|Confucius]],
Laozi, and the "Hundred Schools" in China — that reframed the relationship between
the human and the cosmic order. Whether these were truly connected or merely
coincident is contested ([[axial-age-controversy]]).

**Eastern Zhou China.** With the Zhou court's flight east in 771 BCE, China enters
the **Spring and Autumn period** ([[eastern-zhou]]): nominal Zhou suzerainty over
increasingly autonomous, competing states — the political disunity that incubated
[[confucianism]], Daoism, and Legalism.

**Mahajanapadas and the second urbanization of India.** In the Ganges plain,
sixteen "great realms" (*mahajanapadas*) — Magadha foremost — develop into the
territorial kingdoms that the [[mauryan-empire|Mauryas]] will later unify; cities,
coinage, and the Buddhist and Jain renunciant movements appear together. See
[[south-asia-classical]].

## Geographic Scope

The most "active" zone is a band from the Aegean across the Near East and Iran to
the Indus, plus north China and the central Mediterranean. The Eurasian steppe is
now dominated by mounted [[pastoral-nomadism|nomads]] (Cimmerians, Scythians/Saka)
whose interaction with settled empires becomes a permanent structural feature.
The Americas (Olmec decline, Chavín in the Andes), sub-Saharan Africa (early
ironworking, Nok), Australasia, and Oceania develop on independent trajectories
covered in the regional cycles.

**Rome and the western Mediterranean.** The Archaic period is also when
[[rome|Rome]] transforms from a cluster of hilltop villages into a monumental
city-state — the process documented by [[latial-culture|Latial Culture]]
archaeology and the regal tradition. Under the **Etruscan Tarquins** (c. 616–509
BCE), Rome acquired the Forum, the Capitoline temple (dedicated 509), and the
political institutions (*comitia centuriata*, senate) that made it a regional
power capable of claiming hegemony over the [[latium|Latin League]] by the end
of the period. The expulsion of Tarquinius Superbus (traditional 509 BCE) and
the foundation of the Republic coincide with the retreat of Etruscan power from
Latium after the battle of Aricia. See [[foundation-of-rome-753bce]] and
[[roman-kings]].

Simultaneously, [[carthage|Carthage]] consolidated its western Mediterranean
empire following the Persian conquest of Tyre (539 BCE): the **battle of Alalia**
(c. 535) with its Etruscan ally gave the Carthaginian-Etruscan alliance effective
control of western Mediterranean trade. The first **Romano-Carthaginian treaty**
(Polybius III.22, c. 509 BCE) inscribes these spheres of influence: Rome was
already part of the western Mediterranean diplomatic order at the Republic's
foundation.

## Transition

**Entry:** out of the [[early-iron-age]], as Assyria converts recovery into
territorial empire and the Mediterranean polis-world takes shape.

**Exit:** into [[classical-antiquity]] at c. 500 BCE — conventionally marked by
the Greco-Persian Wars (490/480–479 BCE), the high-water mark of Achaemenid
expansion, and the flowering of Classical Greek civilization. The Axial ferment
continues across the boundary.

## Historiography

**Source quality.** Far richer than the Early Iron Age. Assyrian and Babylonian
royal annals and administrative archives are abundant; the Hebrew Bible's
historical books reach their formative editing; Greek sources begin
([[herodotus|Herodotus]], writing in the 5th c., is the great narrative source for
the Persian Wars and is himself a standing wiki controversy on reliability,
[[herodotus-reliability]]). Persian self-representation survives in royal
inscriptions (Behistun) and Persepolis administrative tablets, but the primary Behistun text (Darius's own account of the rebellions and consolidation) is now directly available via [[behistun-inscription]].
Greco-Roman lens dominates and must be corrected for.

**Major debates.** The reality and meaning of the [[axial-age|Axial Age]]
(coincidence vs. connection vs. construct); the degree of Near Eastern influence
on the emergence of Greek civilization (the "orientalizing" question); the
historicity of the Biblical narratives for this period; and Achaemenid history's
long captivity to hostile Greek sources, only recently corrected by the
"Achaemenid history" school using Persian and Babylonian evidence.

**Recent revisionism.** A major rehabilitation of the Achaemenid Empire as a
sophisticated, tolerant, well-administered polity (against the Greek "oriental
despotism" trope); reassessment of Phoenician agency in the Mediterranean; and
sustained critique of the Axial Age as a Eurocentric and over-tidy category.
