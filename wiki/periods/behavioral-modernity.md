## Overview

Behavioral Modernity covers the great dispersal of fully modern *Homo sapiens*
across the globe, culminating in the occupation of every habitable continent
before the end of the last ice age. It begins with the emergence of the full
modern behavioral/cognitive package in East Africa (~70,000–50,000 BP) and
ends with the Mesolithic transition as warming climates reshaped human
ecologies worldwide (~12,000 BP).

This period represents ~95 percent of the history of behaviorally modern
*Homo sapiens*, yet it is documented far less richly than subsequent periods.

Most Paleolithic communities were small (typically fewer than fifty individuals,
with occasional gatherings of several hundred), foraging over familiar
territories, leaving behind only stone tools, occasional art and symbolic
objects, and skeletal remains. Yet by the end of this period, humans had
colonized every continent except Antarctica and possessed a full range of the
cultural and social capacities that distinguish our species.

## Major Developments

### Emergence of Behavioral Modernity (~70,000–50,000 BP)

Ehret's evidence: fully modern humans evolved somewhere in East Africa between
~70,000 and 48,000 BCE [that is, 50,000 years ago]. Indicators include:
- Later Stone Age technologies: carefully made small blades and tools from
  small flakes; bone tools; associated with art
- Modern pharynx form enabling full range of sounds for modern language
  (appears in skeletal record after ~70,000 BP)
- Evidence in East Africa for new types of stone points possibly used for
  projectile weapons and possibly early bows and arrows
- Rock art suggesting new religious beliefs possibly resembling shamanism
- Larger communities and more complex kinship systems enabled by language

A population bottleneck ~70,000 BP (possibly linked to the eruption of
Mt. Toba in Indonesia ~72,000 BP) reduced human populations to perhaps
10,000 individuals. Whether the bottleneck preceded, coincided with, or
followed the emergence of behavioral modernity is contested. Linguistic
evidence supports an East African origin: modern languages show a clear
trend away from complex consonantal systems as one moves outward from
eastern and southern Africa, suggesting all modern languages derive from
a single origin in this region ~70,000 BP.

### Out of Africa 2 (~50,000–60,000 BP)

Modern humans began spreading from East Africa from approximately 50,000 BP.
See [[out-of-africa-2-60kbp]] for the full event page.

The broad sequence:
- Through the Levant and into Eurasia (~50,000 BP)
- Sahul (Australia + New Guinea + Tasmania): by 50,000 BP, probably earlier
  (see [[colonization-australasia-50kbp]])
- Europe: ~42,000 BP (proto-Aurignacian industries)
- Japan: ~40,000 BP
- Siberia: ~40,000–50,000 BP
- Americas: via Beringia, arriving ~14,500 BP at the latest
  (see [[colonization-americas-14500bp]])

### Neanderthal Extinction (~42,000–30,000 BP)

Modern humans arrived in Europe ~42,000 BP. The [[neanderthals|Neanderthals]]
disappeared within ~10,000 years of modern human arrival, though the process was
not immediate. Evidence of limited interbreeding: non-African modern humans carry
~1–4% Neanderthal DNA (gene flow dated ~100,000 BP in the Near East, before modern
humans entered Europe). The mechanisms of Neanderthal extinction remain debated —
competition, disease, climate change, and interbreeding all may have played
roles. The decisive modern-human advantage was likely cumulative cultural
creativity (the Neanderthal record shows striking stasis over 200,000 years).

### The European Upper Paleolithic Sequence (Hoffecker, Ch. 16)

Modern humans entered Europe by land via the Bosporus and Caucasus (not across
Gibraltar). The European Upper Paleolithic — a "fundamentally different"
archaeological record reflecting the creative powers of the modern mind — unfolds
in stages:
- **Initial Upper Paleolithic** (~45,000+ BP): simple, in Central/SE Europe;
  linked to a sustained warm interval (Greenland Interstadial 12), implying
  climate aided the initial peopling.
- **Proto-Aurignacian** (~42,000 BP): via Bosporus/Caucasus; eyed needles
  (Mezmaiskaya Cave), hare/small-mammal trapping, personal ornaments (perforated
  shells, canine teeth), backed bladelets for composite weapons.
- **Aurignacian** (post-40,000 BP): spectacular visual art including the
  Löwenmensch (Hohlenstein-Stadel); linked to an extreme cold interval (Heinrich
  Event 4) that may have finished off the Neanderthals; the Campanian Ignimbrite
  super-eruption (~39,000 BP) devastated SE Europe and the East European Plain.
- **Gravettian** (~30,000–25,000 BP): settlements of 50+ people; fired-clay
  ceramics (kilns to 800°C); refrigerated storage; elaborate burial ritual;
  widespread Venus figurines (e.g., Venus of Dolní Věstonice). Boom ended ~25,000
  BP with the Last Glacial Maximum.
- **Magdalenian** (W. Europe) / **Epi-Gravettian** (E. Europe): successful
  reoccupation of the periglacial zone after ~20,000 BP; mammoth-bone houses;
  spear-throwers; complex artificial memory systems.

Notably, modern humans retained their **tropical anatomy** (elongated forelimbs)
for thousands of years at high latitudes — technology "buffered" them from cold
selection pressure, exposing them to higher risk of frostbite/hypothermia and to
fuel shortage in the treeless coldest zones.

### Paleolithic Lifeways

Most Paleolithic communities:
- Lived in groups of fewer than fifty individuals
- Foraged over familiar territories; highly mobile
- Mobilized little more energy than needed to survive, with a slight surplus
- Showed little change in population densities at the community level
- Were highly vulnerable — communities could vanish along with their knowledge

At the species level, however, ecological power was slowly increasing: humans
occupied progressively more diverse and challenging environments.

### Ice Age Climate and the Last Glacial Maximum (~22,000–18,000 BP)

Climate was the dominant environmental force shaping Paleolithic migration and
culture. The Milankovic cycles (variations in Earth's orbit, orbital shape,
and axial tilt) drove long cycles of cold/dry glacial periods (~100,000 years)
alternating with shorter warm interglacials (~10,000–20,000 years). Additional
shorter "Heinrich events" caused abrupt cold snaps lasting centuries or millennia.

The Last Glacial Maximum (~22,000–18,000 BP) was the coldest phase:
- Ice sheets covered northern Europe and North America
- Sea levels were ~125 metres below present
- Australia's land area was at its largest extent as Sahul
- Many northern archaeological sites were abandoned
- Climate instability accelerated after ~20,000 BP, beginning the transition
  toward the Holocene

### African Cultural and Linguistic Differentiation

Within Africa, the spread of Later Stone Age technology after ~48,000 BCE was
accompanied by the differentiation of fully modern populations into the ancestors
of Africa's four major language families (Khoesan, Niger-Kordofanian, Nilo-Saharan,
Afrasian), whose Last Glacial Maximum homelands and distinct kinship and religious
systems are reconstructible through historical linguistics (Ehret, CWH Vol. I,
Chs. 14–15). See [[african-language-family-origins]]. Archaic humans with Middle
Stone Age technology persisted alongside fully modern humans for thousands of years
in southern, central, and West Africa — in West Africa down to ~13,000–12,000 BCE
(the late Iwo Eleru skull, Nigeria, shows archaic morphology). Distinct rock art
traditions (Khoesan, Batwa) mark this differentiation; see
[[paleolithic-art-symbolic-culture]].

### Cultural Florescence

Despite material simplicity, Paleolithic cultures displayed remarkable
creativity (see [[paleolithic-art-symbolic-culture]]):
- Cave paintings (Chauvet ~30,000 BP, Lascaux ~17,000 BP, Altamira)
- Venus figurines (e.g., Venus of Willendorf, ~30,000 BP; Venus of Laussel)
- Ivory carvings (Löwenmensch from Germany, ~40,000 BP)
- Prestige burials suggesting embryonic social hierarchies
  (e.g., Sunghir burial, ~28,000 BP, near Moscow)
- Long-distance exchange networks of prestige goods (hundreds of kilometres)

Fernández-Armesto (CWH Vol. I, Ch. 13) argues the comparability of art across
widely separated regions makes the Ice Age "the last great era of a kind of
globalization" — culture was strikingly similar worldwide, changing slowly
(Chauvet to Lascaux spans 10,000 years with little change). The dominant
interpretation links this art to shamanic trance experience; Ehret (Ch. 14)
argues [[shamanism-paleolithic-religion|shamanism was the original religion]]
of modern humans.

### Ice Age Affluence and Foraging Lifeways

Contrary to older assumptions of precarious scarcity, Ice Age foragers were by
many measures *affluent*: well-nourished (often better than later agricultural
populations), leisured, and secure, exploiting the fat-rich megafauna of the
glacial tundra. See [[paleolithic-foraging-lifeways]]. Social organization may
have centered on matrilocal coalitions of close female kin, with "Iroquois"-type
kinship reckoning (Ehret, Ch. 14).

### Syntactic Language

Ehret (Ch. 14) argues the critical enabler of behavioral modernity was the
acquisition of *full syntactic language* ~70,000 BCE — coevolving with the
modern vocal tract (the descended pharynx, present in all humans since ~50,000
years ago). Syntactic language enabled abstraction, planning, large cooperative
networks, and complex kinship. See [[syntactic-language-origins]]. Complex
projectile technology (the African bow and arrow; the spear-thrower of the
Eurasian/Sahul dispersal) was a further decisive advantage.

### Megafaunal Extinctions (~50,000–10,000 BP)

As modern humans colonized each continent, large-bodied megafauna disappeared:
- Australia/Sahul: multiple large herbivore species disappeared ~50,000–45,000 BP
- Americas: mammoths, mastodons, giant ground sloths, and other species
  disappeared ~13,000–12,000 BP (coinciding with Clovis expansion and Younger Dryas)

The causes are debated: human hunting, climate change, or both. In Australia,
absence of kill sites (unlike the Americas) weakens the hunting hypothesis, but
the coincidence of arrival and extinction is striking.

### Transition Toward Agriculture

At the end of this period, some communities began experimenting with sedentism
and intensified food production:
- Natufians (Fertile Crescent): became sedentary, built villages, harvested
  wild grains ~15,000–11,500 BP
- Jomon (Japan): sedentary communities, pottery ~16,000 BP
- Eastern Sahara: early cattle herding evidence ~9th–8th millennium BCE
- Nile corridor: semi-sedentary communities harvesting and grinding wild grains
  ~13,000 BCE

These represent the threshold from which the Neolithic Revolution will emerge.

## Key Actors

- *Homo sapiens* (fully modern, behaviourally modern): dominant species globally
  by ~30,000 BP
- Neanderthals: extinct by ~30,000–25,000 BP
- Denisovans: identity still emerging; likely extinct by ~40,000 BP in most areas
- Clovis peoples: first clearly documented culture in the Americas, ~13,500–12,800 BP
- Natufian communities: precursors to agriculture in the Levant
- Jomon communities: early sedentism in Japan

## Geographic Scope

Genuinely global. By 12,000 BP, humans had colonized all habitable continents.
Europe and Asia: from ~42,000 BP. Australasia: from ~50,000 BP (possibly earlier).
Americas: from ~14,500 BP (possibly earlier at disputed sites). Africa and the
Near East throughout.

## Transition

The period ends as warming climates after the Last Glacial Maximum ~12,000 BP
transformed environments worldwide. Rising sea levels drowned coastal territories,
separated Tasmania from Australia and New Guinea, and isolated island populations.
The Holocene epoch begins. Some communities respond to these pressures by
intensifying food production — the seed of the [[neolithic]] transition.
This marks the opening of the [[mesolithic]].

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Stone tools, skeletal remains, ancient DNA, cave art, and
increasingly fine-grained climate records. The field has been transformed by:
(1) ancient DNA analysis since 2010, revealing the extent of interbreeding
between modern humans, Neanderthals, and Denisovans; (2) improved radiocarbon
and thermoluminescence dating; (3) paleoclimatology, which now enables
precise correlation between climate events and human movements.

**Dating methods**: AMS radiocarbon (reliable to ~50,000 BP), OSL/TL
optically stimulated luminescence), U-series, genetic molecular clocks.

**Scholarly debates**:
- When exactly did behavioral modernity emerge and was it gradual or sudden?
- Dates of colonization of Australasia (see [[australasian-colonization-timing]])
- Whether the Clovis culture was the first in the Americas or whether earlier
  arrivals left sparse evidence (see [[colonization-americas-14500bp]])
- Causes of megafaunal extinctions (human, climate, or both)
- The nature and extent of Neanderthal and Denisovan introgression into
  modern human populations

**Recent revisionism (last 30 years)**:
- The "Human Revolution" model (rapid behavioral change ~40,000 BP in Europe)
  has been replaced by a model of gradual, fitful accumulation of modern
  behaviors in Africa beginning ~100,000+ BP
- aDNA analysis has destroyed the clean "Out of Africa replacement" model;
  replaced by "mostly out of Africa with significant introgression"
- Australasian colonization dates have been pushed back, possibly to 65,000+ BP
