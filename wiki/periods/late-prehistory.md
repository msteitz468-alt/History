## Overview

Late Prehistory covers the period from the appearance of the first anatomically
modern *Homo sapiens* and Neanderthals (~300,000–250,000 BP) to the emergence
of behaviorally modern humans (~50,000 BP). It is defined by the coexistence
of multiple hominin species and the long, uneven transition from archaic to
fully modern human cognition and behavior.

This is the most contested period in the story of human evolution. The
distinction between "anatomically modern" and "behaviorally modern" *Homo
sapiens* is fundamental: skeletal remains show a modern-looking species
appearing in East Africa from ~200,000–150,000 BP, but the behavioral and
cognitive package associated with modern humans only fully consolidates
around 70,000–48,000 BP (see [[homo-sapiens-emergence-date]]).

## Major Developments

**Homo sapiens in Africa (~300,000–150,000 BP)**
Skeletal evidence for anatomically modern humans from East Africa: ~300,000 BP
(Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, revised dating 2017) and ~195,000 BP (Omo Kibish,
Ethiopia). The taxonomic status of the earliest specimens relative to fully
modern *H. sapiens* is debated. These populations used Middle Stone Age
technologies similar to those of Neanderthals.

**Neanderthals (~250,000–40,000 BP)**
Neanderthals evolved in Europe from *Homo heidelbergensis* ancestors no later
than ~250,000 BP. They were well adapted to glacial conditions, surviving in
Europe through periods of extreme cold. They depended heavily on meat from
large herbivores (mammoth, woolly rhinoceros). Key behavioral indicators:
- Stone tools: Mousterian tradition; sophisticated, including composite tools
  with adhesives (recently demonstrated)
- Probably buried some dead (debated as evidence of belief)
- Possessed the FOXP2 gene (associated with human speech), but the evidence
  of behavioral stasis over 200,000 years suggests they lacked the full
  modern linguistic capacity
- No evidence of sewn clothing or the ability to make fire from scratch
  (though they could maintain fires)

**Middle Palaeolithic Subsistence and Settlement in Europe (Champion et al. 1984)**
Faunal assemblages from sites such as Combe Grenal show changing proportions of
reindeer, red deer, bovids and horse interpreted as climatic effects on
availability within local exploitation territories. "Changes in the relative
importance through time of reindeer, red deer, bovids and horse bones are thought
to reflect a combination of climatic factors which led to different proportions
of these animals being taken from the area around the site." (Champion et al.
1984, Ch. 3 / range lines ~100-110)

Specific hunting episodes include stampeding at La Cotte de St. Brelade (English
Channel): layer 3 yielded nine mammoth skulls and woolly rhino remains from a
small herd of adult females and young driven over a headland. Layer 6 showed
selected carcass portions cached. Carnivore contributions (cave bear, hyena,
wolf, lion) must be distinguished at many cave sites; southern European sites
(e.g. Cantabrian Spain) show fewer such remains, making faunas more directly
representative of human activity.

Middle Palaeolithic groups showed more limited regional occupation during
climate deterioration compared with Upper Palaeolithic successors. "the upper
palaeolithic populations of central and eastern Europe had solved some of the
problems connected with exploiting ecologically specialized environments caught
in the grip of the last ice age." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch. 3 / range lines
~1740-1750) Burials appear (single, flexed, in caves; e.g. Combe Grenal infant
pit), but grave goods remain ambiguous.

**Levant "False Start" (~125,000–70,000 BP)**
Modern-looking humans appear in the Levant around 125,000–70,000 BP during a
warm interglacial period. Their disappearance and replacement by Neanderthal
populations as climate deteriorated suggests they lacked the behavioral
resilience of fully modern humans — the same basic skeleton but not yet the
full cognitive/cultural package. This population seems not to have contributed
to the subsequent Out of Africa 2 dispersal.

**Middle Stone Age in Africa (~300,000–50,000 BP, with survivals later)**
Across Africa, the dominant stone tool tradition is the Middle Stone Age (MSA).
Evidence of prepared-core technologies similar to Neanderthal Mousterian. But
increasingly in the later MSA, we see signs of behavioral modernity: small
blade tools, bone tools, early evidence of art and symbolic behavior, projectile
weapons (possibly early bows and arrows), rock art that may indicate shamanism-
like beliefs. The transition is slow and fitful, not abrupt.

**Fire management solidified**
Fire management was clearly established by Neanderthals by ~250,000 BP.
Evidence from Israeli sites suggests controlled fire perhaps 800,000 BP in
archaic *Homo* populations (contested). See [[processes/fire-management]].

## Key Actors

- *Homo sapiens* (anatomically modern, behaviorally archaic, ~200,000–70,000 BP)
- *Homo neanderthalensis* (~250,000–40,000 BP in Europe; contact with H. sapiens)
- *Homo heidelbergensis* (ancestral; fading from record by ~200,000 BP)
- Denisovans (identified 2010; present in Siberia and possibly Asia ~400,000–50,000 BP)

## Geographic Scope

Africa and western Eurasia. No evidence of hominin presence in Australasia or
the Americas during this period. East Asia: *Homo erectus* populations persisted
alongside possible early *H. sapiens* populations.

## Transition

Closes with the emergence of fully behaviorally modern *Homo sapiens* in East
Africa (~70,000–48,000 BP) and the beginning of the Out of Africa 2 dispersal
that defines [[behavioral-modernity]].

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Fossil remains, stone tools, ancient DNA (now available for
Neanderthals and Denisovans), and increasingly early symbolic/artistic evidence.
The ancient DNA revolution since 2010 has transformed understanding: we now know
that non-African modern humans carry 1–4% Neanderthal DNA, and some populations
carry Denisovan DNA, demonstrating interbreeding was real and ongoing.

**Dating methods**: Uranium-series, thermoluminescence, electron spin resonance,
aDNA molecular clocks. Precision improves but confidence intervals remain wide.

**Major debates**: Whether anatomically modern humans at Jebel Irhoud (~300,000 BP)
were behaviorally modern; what caused Neanderthal extinction (competition,
disease, climate, or combination); whether the Levant population was a failed
dispersal or left genetic descendants; the extent and nature of H. sapiens-
Neanderthal interbreeding; whether Denisovans contributed to Southeast Asian
or Pacific Islander populations (evidence suggests yes).

13–17 of this volume (Part II) and Phase 11 sources will expand substantially.

Coverage moderate. CWH Vol. 1 Introduction provides framework; actual chapters

**Champion et al. 1984 framing (Ch. 1):** This textbook supplies the principal European post-radiocarbon historiographic context for all prehistoric periods. It documents the replacement of culture-historical diffusionism and the Three Age System by thematic analysis of adaptive behaviour, settlement, subsistence and social organization. The radiocarbon revolution "greatly lengthened the time-span of European prehistory" and forced rethinking of all prior models. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~390-410) The source is cited on the relevant European period pages for methodological and interpretive shifts.

## From Tattersall, *The World from Beginnings to 4000 BCE* (2008)

[[tattersall-world-from-beginnings-2008|Tattersall (2008)]] treats this as the
"getting brainier" phase: [[homo-heidelbergensis|*Homo heidelbergensis*]] spreading
across the Old World, prepared-core (Levallois) technology (~300 kya), the Schöningen
spears and Terra Amata shelters, and the European [[punctuated-equilibria|adaptive
radiation]] that produced the [[neanderthals]]. Anatomically modern *Homo sapiens*
also first appears in Africa late in this span (Omo ~195 kya, Herto ~160 kya) — but
without yet showing [[symbolic-cognition|modern symbolic behavior]]. See
[[human-evolution]].
