# Scandinavian Prehistory

Regional prehistory of Scandinavia from the late-glacial reindeer hunters to the
emergence of Iron Age chiefdoms and emporia, drawing on Part I of
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003|The Cambridge History of Scandinavia, Vol. 1]]
(chapters by Sporrong, Siiriäinen, Myhre, and Barnes). It complements the
collection's general prehistory pages ([[neolithic]], [[early-bronze-age]],
[[early-iron-age]]) with the specifically Scandinavian sequence.

## Geographic Frame

The Fenno-Scandian Precambrian shield and the Caledonian mountains divide
Scandinavia into a cultivable south (sedimentary rock, glacial clays) and a
forested north of coarse till — a geological boundary that fixed where permanent
agricultural populations could establish themselves. Post-glacial **isostatic
rebound** has been greatest around the Swedish east coast (Ångermanland, land
risen >800 m since the Weichselian maximum c. 20,000 years ago) while southern
Sweden and Denmark are still sinking. The Baltic passed through successive stages
— Baltic Ice Lake, Yoldia Sea, Ancylus Lake, Littorina Sea — whose beach ridges
determined settlement on Öland and Gotland.

## Major Developments

**Mesolithic (c. 10,000–4000 BC).** Late-Palaeolithic reindeer hunters (Hamburg,
Ahrensburg, Federmesser cultures; northernmost Hamburg finds at Finjasjö in Skåne
c. 14,000 BP) gave way to the forest-adapted **Maglemose** culture (c. 8000–6000
BC) and the stable, largely sedentary **Kongemose–Ertebølle** coastal continuum
(c. 7000–3100 BC), sustained by rich marine resources, with pottery first appearing
in the Ertebølle phase. One of the world's oldest fishing nets (willow-bark fibre)
was found at Antrea Korpilahti and radiocarbon-dated to c. 7300 BC. Ertebølle
burials (red ochre, simple pits) suggest a mainly egalitarian society.

**Neolithic (c. 4000–1800 BC).** Agriculture, adopted by Ertebølle communities from
c. 4000 BC, became important from c. 3100 BC with the spread of the **Funnel Beaker**
culture; megalithic dolmens and passage graves mark a collective burial tradition.
About 2300 BC the **Corded Ware / Single Grave / Battle Axe** complex arrived from
central Europe with individual barrow burial, probably (debatably) Indo-European
immigrants. In the north and east the **Comb Ceramic** and **Pitted Ware** cultures
mark hunter-fisher societies and probable early proto-Finnic presence.

**Bronze Age (c. 1800–500 BC).** All copper and tin was imported, adding prestige
to metal and underpinning stratified chiefdoms whose elites controlled exchange
networks; rock carvings (ships, sun-symbols, human figures, painted with ochre)
reflect an agrarian ritual life. From c. 1500 BC Scandinavians established trading
posts on the south-west Finnish coast, introducing agriculture and an early layer
of Germanic loanwords into Finnish.

**Iron Age (c. 500 BC–c. 800 CE).** The earliest iron-working in Scandinavia
(c. 800–700 BC around Lake Mälaren and western Sweden) is up to 200 years older
than the oldest known sites in England, Germany, and Poland. The pre-Roman Iron Age
saw comparatively egalitarian village communities (Grøntoft); the Roman Iron Age
(AD 1–400) shows private land ownership (Vorbasse, replanned c. AD 200) and chiefdoms
sustained by Roman prestige-goods exchange via Danish middlemen, with peat-bog weapon
deposits attesting armies of 500–1,000 men. The Migration Period (AD 400–600) spread
Germanic animal-ornament Style I/II; Jordanes's *Getica* (551) names twenty-eight
Scandinavian tribes. Central places — **Old Uppsala**, the **Vendel/Valsgärde** boat
graves, **Borg** in Lofoten (83 m hall), **Gudme/Helgö** — and the first emporium,
**Ribe** (c. 704–710), set the stage for the [[viking-age|Viking Age]].

## Ethnic and Linguistic Groups

The **syncope period** (AD 550–750) marks the emergence of a distinctly Scandinavian
("Common Scandinavian") speech; an East/West Nordic dialect split is reckoned from
c. AD 700. The [[runestones|earliest runic inscriptions]] (AD 200–600) show an
archaic, homogeneous Germanic whose classification ("North-west Germanic" vs.
proto-Scandinavian) is debated. The [[sami-people|Sami]] and the Baltic-Finnic
peoples of [[finland|Finland]] are now generally seen as having formed gradually
*in situ* among northern hunting-fishing populations rather than by a single
migration.

## Historiography

Dating rests on radiocarbon, pollen analysis, dendrochronology (Ribe), and
typological chronology. Three live debates recur: the **"crisis hypothesis"** of
a c. AD 550–700 demographic collapse (plague, climate, soil exhaustion), now
questioned by continuous occupation at Vorbasse and Nørre Snede but "not refuted";
the hazard of **linking archaeological cultures to language/ethnicity**; and the
**Sami origins** migration-vs.-*in situ* models. See
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003]].
