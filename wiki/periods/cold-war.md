# Cold War (1945–1991 CE)

## Overview

The **Cold War** period (1945–1991) is, in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]'s
material reading, far more than a geopolitical standoff between the United States
and the Soviet Union. It coincides almost exactly with the **[[great-acceleration|Great
Acceleration]]** — J. R. McNeill's second phase of the [[anthropocene|Anthropocene]],
the "age of oil" and of nearly 2% annual population growth. Between 1945 and 2015
humanity burned roughly 50–150 million years' worth of fossil sunshine and added,
in the second half of the twentieth century alone, some 4.4 billion people. Global
population growth peaked around 1970 at just over 2% a year; the years 1950–1990,
when growth exceeded 1.75% annually, were "a global burst of reproduction and
survival, never before approached and never to be repeated."

Politically the period is defined by the **bipolar order**, the nuclear arms race
and the threat of mutual annihilation, and the worldwide wave of
**[[decolonization]]** that dismantled the European empires and multiplied the
number of national states — many of them sites of Cold War proxy conflict. It is
also the period in which [[industrialization]] diffused most rapidly beyond the
North Atlantic, above all to East Asia, in the labor-intensive form Sugihara
emphasizes, and in which the **Green Revolution** transformed agriculture in much
of the developing world.

## Major Developments

- **The bipolar order and the nuclear age:** the US–Soviet rivalry, **[[containment]]**
  (Kennan, the Truman Doctrine, NSC-68), the **[[marshall-plan-1948|Marshall Plan]]**,
  **[[nato|NATO]]** and the Warsaw Pact, the **[[berlin-blockade-1948|Berlin crises]]**, the
  **[[nuclear-arms-race|nuclear arms race]]** and MAD, and proxy wars — the
  **[[korean-war-1950|Korean War]]**, Vietnam, Afghanistan, Angola, and across Latin America,
  Africa, and Asia.
- **The Great Acceleration (from c. 1950):** the steepest rise in energy use,
  population, urbanization, consumption, and environmental impact in human history
  — and, in reaction, the birth of modern popular **environmentalism** after c.
  1965 (see [[great-acceleration]], [[anthropocene]]).
- **Decolonization:** the rapid end of the European overseas empires across Asia
  and Africa (c. 1945–1975), the high tide of Third World [[nationalism]], the
  Bandung Conference (1955), and non-alignment (see [[decolonization]]).
- **The demographic explosion and its politics:** sharp post-war mortality decline
  almost everywhere; intense, often coercive [[population-politics|population
  politics]] (India's sterilization campaigns, China's one-child policy from c.
  1980).
- **The Green Revolution:** scientifically engineered seeds, chemical fertilizers,
  pesticides, and irrigation spread worldwide after 1945, sharply raising yields
  while increasing dependence on industrial and fossil-fuel inputs.
- **The East Asian economic ascent:** Japan's post-war "miracle," then the "tiger"
  economies — the rapid diffusion of [[industrialization]] that began to close the
  [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]].
- **Smallpox eradication (certified 1980):** a Cold War collaboration across the
  ideological divide and a landmark of internationalist public health (see
  [[smallpox-eradication]]).

## Key Actors

- The two superpowers: the **United States** (see [[united-states]]) and the
  **Soviet Union**; the People's Republic of **China** as a third pole after 1949.
- The **non-aligned movement** and newly independent post-colonial states; figures
  of decolonization (Gandhi, Nasser, Nehru, Nkrumah, Fanon).
- International institutions: the **United Nations**, the WHO (smallpox), the
  Bretton Woods order.

## Geographic Scope

Global. The superpower heartlands (North America, the Soviet bloc) and their
European frontline; the decolonizing and "developing" world of Asia, Africa, and
Latin America as both arena and agent; East Asia as the engine of late-century
industrial diffusion.

## Transition

**From:** the [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]]. The 1945 settlement — the UN, the
bipolar division of Europe, the nuclear monopoly and then arms race, and the
imminent collapse of the colonial empires — opened the period. See
[[1945-postwar-order]]. **To:** the [[contemporary|Contemporary]] period (1991–present).
The collapse of Soviet communism and the end of the bipolar order in 1989–1991
closed it. See [[1991-end-of-cold-war]].

## Historiography

The diplomatic-military Cold War narrative is now ingested directly via **[[hobsbawm-age-of-extremes|Hobsbawm's
"Golden Age" and "Landslide"]]**, **[[westad-cold-war|Westad's global *Cold War*]]**, and
**[[judt-postwar|Judt's *Postwar*]]** (Europe since 1945). CWH VII subordinates that narrative (which it largely
leaves to its absent Book 2 chapter) to a structural account of the post-1950 world:
McNeill's Great Acceleration, Sugihara's multipolar industrial diffusion,
Livi-Bacci's demographic transition, Bashford's population politics, Duara's
decolonization, and Manela's smallpox eradication. The major interpretive questions
it raises are environmental and developmental rather than ideological: whether the
Great Acceleration is sustainable; why industrialization diffused so much faster
after 1950; and how far decolonization actually altered global economic hierarchies
(Cooper, Duara). The classic political historiography (Gaddis; revisionists;
post-revisionists) is largely outside this volume's frame.

## Collection Coverage Note

Originally rested on [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII Book 1]] (structural — demography,
energy, environment, decolonization, public health). The **political-diplomatic and
global narrative is now supplied** by **[[westad-cold-war|Westad's *The Cold War: A
World History*]]** (the conflict as a worldwide contest over modernity, fought largely
in the decolonizing Global South) and **[[judt-postwar|Judt's *Postwar*]]** (Europe
since 1945). The **diplomatic-structural backbone is now supplied directly** by the **Cambridge History
of the Cold War** (3 vols, Leffler & Westad, eds., 2010 — all ingested 2026-06-13:
[[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|I Origins]], [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v2|II Crises and
Détente]], [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v3|III Endings]]). This created the wiki's discrete
Cold War event/structure pages: **[[containment]]**, the **[[marshall-plan-1948|Marshall
Plan]]**, **[[nato]]**, the **[[berlin-blockade-1948|Berlin Blockade]]**, the
**[[korean-war-1950|Korean War]]**, the **[[nuclear-arms-race]]**, the
**[[cuban-missile-crisis-1962|Cuban Missile Crisis]]**, the **[[vietnam-war|Vietnam War]]**,
the **[[sino-soviet-split]]**, **[[detente]]**, the
**[[soviet-war-in-afghanistan-1979|Soviet–Afghan War]]**, and the
**[[revolutions-of-1989]]** (closing the gaps the period page had flagged). CWH VII **Book 2
remains absent from the collection**, but its gap is now well covered by Westad, Judt, and
the Cambridge History of the Cold War.

The **African theatre** of the Cold War is now supplied by the **[[gha-vol8-since-1935|UNESCO General
History of Africa, Vol. VIII]]** (Section VII): the entanglement of African decolonization and
**[[post-colonial-africa|post-colonial]]** politics in superpower rivalry — the **Congo crisis** and
Lumumba's murder, the Angola/Mozambique wars, the [[apartheid|Southern African]] liberation struggle (Soviet
and Cuban support vs. Western/apartheid intervention), and the proxy conflicts of the Horn — the Cold War
"fought largely in the decolonizing Global South" of which [[westad-cold-war|Westad]] writes.
