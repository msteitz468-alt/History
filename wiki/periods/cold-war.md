# Cold War (1945–1991 CE)

## Overview

The **Cold War** period (1945–1991) is, in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]'s
material reading, far more than a geopolitical standoff between the United States
and the Soviet Union. It coincides almost exactly with the **[[great-acceleration|Great
Acceleration]]** — J. R. McNeill's second phase of the [[anthropocene|Anthropocene]],
the "age of oil" and of nearly 2% annual population growth. Between 1945 and 2015
humanity burned roughly 50–150 million years' worth of fossil sunshine and added,
in the second half of the twentieth century alone, some 4.4 billion people. Global
population growth peaked around 1970 at just over 2% a year; the years 1950–1990,
when growth exceeded 1.75% annually, were "a global burst of reproduction and
survival, never before approached and never to be repeated."

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|*The Cambridge History of the Cold War*, Vol. I]] (Range 01): "The term 'Cold War' was first used by the British writer George Orwell in 1945 to deplore the worldview, beliefs, and social structure of both the Soviet Union and the United States, and the undeclared state of war that would come to exist between them after the end of World War II." "Russia’s Great October Socialist Revolution of 1917 triggered a confrontation between the Soviet Union and the United States that would last much of the twentieth century... this new form of struggle – the Cold War – was at its root a battle of ideas: American liberalism vs. Soviet Communism." The conflict globalized because "by the 1960s, the emergence of new states had done much to intensify the rivalry between the superpowers, and for the rest of the Cold War Asia, Africa, and Latin America stood at the center of the conflict." "The combination of capitalist crises and world wars was a key factor in the collapse of the European colonial empires, a chain of events that decisively influenced the Cold War, especially in its later stages."

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v2|CHCW Vol. II, Range 01 (Gaddis Ch. 1)]]: The middle period (c. 1962–1975) saw the conflict "seemed to stabilize after the Cuban Missile Crisis in 1962 and how this sense of increased stability evolved into the détente era of the early 1970s." "Conflicts in the Third World, as well as the interests and ideologies of the superpowers, eroded the détente process." Grand strategies evolved "much as competitive species do within common ecosystems." Post-1962 nuclear/ecological constraint produced "tacit" superpower agreement "not to use nuclear weapons again to try to break the Cold War stalemate." Khrushchev's "socialism with a human face" reforms + de-Stalinization produced "unintended" fragmentation (Hungary 1956 harsher than Stalin; Berlin Wall 1961; irreversible Sino-Soviet split). "Détente... instead destabilized the priorities of both superpowers." Soviet overstretch (Yemen/Ethiopia/Afghanistan interventions; Helsinki 1975 human-rights concession; integration into global economy) produced vulnerabilities that "handed enemies the means to break a long stalemate." (Gaddis locators throughout Ch. 1)

Politically the period is defined by the **bipolar order**, the nuclear arms race
and the threat of mutual annihilation, and the worldwide wave of
**[[decolonization]]** that dismantled the European empires and multiplied the
number of national states — many of them sites of Cold War proxy conflict. It is
also the period in which [[industrialization]] diffused most rapidly beyond the
North Atlantic, above all to East Asia, in the labor-intensive form Sugihara
emphasizes, and in which the **Green Revolution** transformed agriculture in much
of the developing world.

From Cambridge History of Russia Vol. III (Taubman/Hanson/Brown range ll. 7019–8424+): Khrushchev 'secret speech' (1956) denounced Stalin (ll. 7019–7027); 'Khrushchev’s indictment was neither complete nor unalloyed' (ll. 7029); birth 1894 Kalinovka, repentance (ll. 7129, 7151). Brezhnev social contract: 'job security, low prices for basic goods, the de facto toleration of a thriving “second economy”, a limited form of social mobility and the creation of tightly controlled spheres for the expression of non-Russian national identities' (ll. ~7765); rejection of Khrushchevism, détente (ll. ~7673, 7852); 'The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 was the single most disastrous decision of the Brezhnev leadership' (ll. 7971). Gorbachev 'in a minority of one in the Politburo... believing that the Soviet Union as well as the United States had to react to the realities of the nuclear age in a new way' (ll. 8424); contributed to ending Cold War. `preceded_by` / `produced` shifts in [[contemporary]]. ## Major Developments

- **The bipolar order and the nuclear age:** the US–Soviet rivalry, **[[containment]]**
  (Kennan, the Truman Doctrine, NSC-68), the **[[marshall-plan-1948|Marshall Plan]]**,
  **[[nato|NATO]]** and the Warsaw Pact, the **[[berlin-blockade-1948|Berlin crises]]**, the
  **[[nuclear-arms-race|nuclear arms race]]** and MAD, and proxy wars — the
  **[[korean-war-1950|Korean War]]**, the **[[vietnam-war|Vietnam War]]**, and the **[[soviet-war-in-afghanistan-1979|Soviet-Afghan War]]**. Within the **[[soviet-union|Soviet Union]]**, this era spanned late Stalinism (with the cultural purge of **[[zhdanovshchina|Zhdanovshchina]]**), the political liberalization and international crises under **[[khrushchev-nikita|Nikita Khrushchev]]** (including the **[[de-stalinization|De-Stalinization]]** campaign, the crushing of the **[[hungarian-revolution-1956|Hungarian Revolution]]**, and the **[[cuban-missile-crisis-1962|Cuban Missile Crisis]]**), the long stagnation and interventions under **[[brezhnev-leonid|Leonid Brezhnev]]** (the **[[brezhnev-stagnation|Brezhnev Stagnation]]** and the **[[prague-spring-1968|Prague Spring]]**), and the reform attempts of **[[gorbachev-mikhail|Mikhail Gorbachev]]** (through **[[perestroika|Perestroika]]** and **[[glasnost|Glasnost]]**), which led to the **[[chernobyl-disaster-1986|Chernobyl Disaster]]**, the **[[august-coup-1991|August Coup]]**, and the final **[[dissolution-of-the-soviet-union|Dissolution of the Soviet Union]]**.

From Range 02 (Pechatnov): Soviet postwar planning emphasized "security concerns" via territorial "glacis of 'friendly states'" and defense in depth; initial "popular front" incremental influence behind multiparty facade; after atomic bomb, Truman moves, and Marshall Plan (seen as "Trojan horse" for US-led bloc including western Germany), shift to "tenacity and steadfastness" and "two camps" via Cominform, accelerating full Sovietization and Berlin blockade (Stalin's aim: force reversal of western German decisions, but "careful to avoid a direct military confrontation"). "The Berlin blockade provided a stronger impetus than had ever existed for the Western powers to establish a separate West German state and to effect closer military cooperation." (links to [[marshall-plan-1948]], [[division-of-germany]], [[berlin-blockade-1948]], [[sovietization-of-eastern-europe]], [[containment]]).

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v2|CHCW Vol. II, Range 01 (Jervis Ch. 2)]]: Middle Cold War identities shaped rivalry. American: democracy, "individualism and voluntarism," opposition to concentrated power, "faith that this model... will be adopted by others." Soviet: proletariat/class conflict/transformation + "superpower, equal in status and rights." Symmetries (universalism, progress/modernity, second-image thinking, transformationism) "heightened rather than dampened the conflict." Asymmetries (Soviet top-down/future-oriented/explicit anti-capitalist ideology) made "domestic reverses... corrosive to the Soviet regime and identity" while American "left relatively unscathed." Third World was "uncommitted states and peoples"; "nothing less than each side's view of the rightness of its cause... whose side history was on." Khrushchev (Vienna 1961): "The Soviet Union is for change... challenging the capitalist system." Détente failed on "incompatible expectations": US for status quo maintenance, Soviets for "equal status and gains in the Third World." "The Cold War ended only when one side's identity did." (Jervis Ch. 2 quotes/locators)

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v2|CHCW Vol. II, Range 01 (Cooper Ch. 3 early)]]: Economic dimensions critical complement to deterrence. US used "building an international economic system conducive to economic prosperity," sanctions, and "a robust US economy... as a beacon for the benefits of a free, enterprise-based, market-oriented economy." 1960s "perhaps the highest decadal growth in history"; Western Europe/Japan "extraordinary growth"; US imports +170% 1960–70. USSR official ~7% 1960s (CIA 5.1%); "on a roll" per Khrushchev boasts but declining; 15–17% GDP military + large inefficient investment; standards rose "respectably" but Comecon trade limited until détente surge (industrialized exports to Comecon $2.8B 1960 → $34B 1975). Sanctions architecture (no MFN, COCOM controls, 26 US episodes); Jackson-Vanik blocked 1972 MFN. "In the end, the Soviet Union was not defeated in military combat, but rather it collapsed because of internal economic weaknesses" contrasting robust non-Communist performance. "cumulative comparative experience was at least as important in preventing the spread of Communism as was deterrence." (Cooper Ch. 3)

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v2|CHCW Vol. II, additional ranges 02–06]]: Cuban Missile Crisis (Hershberg): neither side wanted to risk repetition in Europe post-1962; success in Cuba may have facilitated disaster in Vietnam; Moscow resolved to catch up in nuclear competition never to be vulnerable again; US rationalized Soviet ICBM buildup as stabilizing MAD, enshrined in 1972 parity. Nuclear stalemate (Burr/Rosenberg): by late 1960s strategic parity provided context for détente; US deployed thousands ICBM/SLBM/MIRVs; Soviets matched; tactical nukes in Europe raised escalation issues but no rational plan (McNamara 1965); Nixon “nuclear umbrella... a lot of crap.” Détente as European project (Hanhimäki): Brandt Ostpolitik (Moscow/Warsaw/Basic treaties, UN entry 1973); CSCE Helsinki 1975 (Baskets I–IV, “living document,” Basket III human rights manifesto for dissidents; trade sixfold rise 1970–79); European challenge to bipolarity (de Gaulle independent line, Harmel Report). European integration (Ludlow): Cold War logic in UK 1961/1967 bids; “protective Atlantic cocoon” enabled EEC focus on customs union/CAP; German integration aided its foreign-policy emergence; Western success destabilized East (1989 transitions, EU 2004 enlargement as definitive end of division). Indochina/Vietnam (Logevall): US escalation driven by “psychological domino”/credibility (Tet 1968 inflamed opinion, LBJ no re-election run; Nixon Vietnamization + Christmas bombing; Paris Accords 1973 allowed NVA in South; collapse April 1975; dominoes did not fall outside Indochina). Middle East (Little): Suez 1956; Eisenhower Doctrine 1957; 1967 Six Day War; 1973 Yom Kippur (US airlift, DEFCON III, oil embargo); Kissinger shuttle → Camp David 1978/Egypt-Israel peace 1979 (Egypt changed sides). Cuban internationalism (Gleijeses): no other Third World country projected power extra-continentally on Cuba’s scale (36k to Angola 1975–76 halting SA advance; 12k to Ethiopia 1978); Castro refused US pressure on Africa policy. Great power politics structure (Trachtenberg): German nuclear ambitions blocked; US nuclear deterrent limits (“no rational plan” for Europe tactical nukes; Nixon umbrella “crap”); structural German incentive for Ostpolitik/détente (“our only option”); Soviet “Finlandization” temptation vs. conservative/bureaucratic caution + China factor; Nixon-Kissinger “shadow-boxing”/tacit alliance with China while maintaining formal symmetry with USSR; SALT/ABM had limited stabilizing effect. Social/economic history (Loth): Western high growth + consumer society/consensus stabilized democracy (Communists lost support post-Hungary); Eastern growth (USSR industrial rapid but ag/consumer lopsided; GDR consumption ~60% West); Berlin Wall 1961 showed East could not win competition; Eastern leaders’ consumption/welfare focus with Western loans led to debt crisis, missed microelectronics, growth collapse, frustration/protests, delegitimization and 1989 transitions (“the Cold War... was also made obsolete by the further development of that society”). Bib essay grounds historiography (post-revisionist balance, declassified sources, economic data, nuclear planning).

## From Range 03 (Naimark, Rajak, Niu Jun, Guthrie-Shimizu, Stueck in Leffler & Westad Vol. I)

- European bloc consolidation (Naimark): Marshall Plan rejection triggered rapid shift. Cominform Szklarska Poręba Sept 1947 with Zhdanov "two camps" speech ended "anti-Fascist, democratic revolution" and national roads; Comecon Jan 1949 tightened economic ties. "The intensification of the Cold War terminated any lingering restraints on the full-scale Sovietization of the region." Show trials/purges with NKVD advisers; "Little Stalins" (Bierut, Rákosi, etc.) strictly subordinate to Kremlin. Soviet advisers pervasive in politics, economy, culture, ideology ("report cards" on Marxism-Leninism). Self-Sovietization debate: East Europeans adapted models with agency.
- Balkans schism (Rajak): Tito partisans "fourth-largest army in Europe"; "empire by invitation" in Yugoslavia/Albania. Trieste 1945 standoff; Stalin forced withdrawal. Split: Cominform expulsion 28 June 1948 after Stalin attacks on federation etc.; economic blockade, 7,877 border incidents 1948–53, Goli Otok camp. "destroyed forever any view of the Communist movement as a monolith." Post-1953 normalisation aided de-Stalinisation.
- Asian turn (Niu Jun): PRC born 1 Oct 1949 after civil war. "lean to one side" policy; new Sino-Soviet Treaty 14 Feb 1950 after Mao-Stalin talks (Mao insisted despite initial Soviet reluctance). US viewed as "major external threat"; Taiwan blockade after June 1950 forced China redeploy from Taiwan to Korea frontier. Mao: "if the US imperialists were to succeed, they would be complacent, and would threaten us."
- Japan alignment (Guthrie-Shimizu): Reverse course 1947 prioritised economic recovery over reform (Strike Commission). San Francisco Peace + Security Treaty 1951 "in the midst of the Korean War". Korean War procurements "resuscitated the anemic Japanese economy"; by 1952 "well on its way to... high-speed growth". US-enforced PRC embargo; private trade deals pursued. 1960 revised treaty "more equal partnership".
- Korea origins (Stueck): 38th parallel division Aug 1945 (Truman proposal, Stalin agreement); bypassed Koreans. South: US refused Korean People’s Republic (declared Sept 1945), discouraged committees, favored conservatives/Rhee. North: Kim Il Sung (Manchuria guerrilla) installed via NKPPC Feb 1946. Trusteeship controversy polarised politics; Joint Commission failed 1946. South Autumn Harvest Uprising 1946 suppressed with US troops; hundreds dead, "suggest coordination".
- Overall: "The combination of capitalist crises and world wars was a key factor in the collapse of the European colonial empires..." but early Cold War "bipolar order" crystallised in Europe via Marshall/Cominform/division and turned hot in Asia with PRC and Korea. From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1]] (Range 05):
- **Culture (Gienow-Hecht):** The Cold War "was a war about two different Weltanschauungen, two ways to organize cultural life, two possibilities of defining modernity." Soviets positioned as "saviors of occidental culture (Abendlandkultur)" with high culture accessible via "heavy state subsidies"; Americans portrayed as lacking high culture, purveyors of "popular culture and jazz." US responded defensively with classical music (Van Cliburn Tchaikovsky win 1958), CIA/CCF book programs, Fulbright/exchanges, USIA; irony: American popular culture (Hollywood, jazz, rock) "successfully contributed to the defeat of Communist ideology" yet "did not enhance the image of the United States in Europe" and "continues to be a deep-seated anti-Americanism." In East, popular culture served as "silent protest"; "they never lost the argument that high culture flourished in the East." (Ch. 19, pp. 397–419).

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v3]] (main synthesis, Brown/Roberts/Chen/Lévesque/Pons/Mitchell ranges):
- **Endings (c. 1975–1991)**: "incredibly swift transition" (Roberts Ch. 24); "dramatic, decisive, and remarkably peaceful" — Berlin Wall Nov 1989 / USSR end Dec 1991. Two unique traits: two universalist/anti-colonial superpowers + nuclear shadow. Détentes "incomplete"; by 1987 Gorbachev cooperation ended talk of ongoing Cold War. All explanations "convincing"; no "end of history".
- **Gorbachev revolution (Brown)** decisive: "revolution from above" by "evolutionary means"; Dec 1984 "new political thinking" (to Thatcher: "Europe is our common home"; nuclear prevention "most acute ... problem"); evolved to "democratic socialist of a social democratic type"; Gromyko replaced by Shevardnadze (<1 yr, foreign easier than economic overhaul); "Cold War was over ... by the end of 1989" via tolerance / Brezhnev Doctrine abandonment.
- **China pivot (Chen)**: Deng "reform and opening"; Mao Three Worlds theory development-focused; US rapprochement shifted power balance vs Moscow; market turn "destroyed Moscow's claims that Communism remained a viable alternative"; "buried the last hope of international Communism".
- **Other Vol. III factors**: Eurocommunism "rise and fall" (Pons) challenged Soviet hegemony; W. Europe left adopted austerity consensus (Mitterrand reversal, Craxi, González "no alternative"); Carter "overcorrected" Manichean post-Horn/Afghan; transnational scientists (IPPNW Lown/Chazov) enabled uncensored Soviet nuclear education; consumerism "rather than the US nation-state, 'won'".
- **1989 hinge (Lévesque)**: "single most significant event"; "pace and scale ... shaped by the gradual discovery of the scope of Soviet tolerance". Non-use of force the hinge.

- **US domestic mobilization (McEnaney):** NSC 68 framed it as "a real war in which the survival of the free world [was] at stake" requiring "ingenuity, sacrifice, and unity" via "traditional democratic process" mobilizing schools, unions, media etc. "McCarthyism" was "primarily a top-down phenomenon... many McCarthyisms... collaborative project"; Taft-Hartley loyalty affidavits "wiped out an entire generation of activists"; FBI "designed and ran much of the machinery" ("Hooverism"); "national security state" a "state within a state" and "disciplinary state" generating "ambient militarism." Contradictions with liberalism: "Liberal intellectuals were willing to put the containment of communism ahead of the welfare state while conservative intellectuals were prepared to give antistatism a back seat to anticommunism." Civil rights used Cold War framing ("segregation harmed US foreign relations") but NAACP purged left; "national security was the dominant rationale" making reform "both urgent and limited." Gender/nuclear: containment ideal coexisted with openings; citizens "private pacifists but public militarists" (most rejected shelters). "the Cold War [was] not synonymous with American culture" (pp. 420–441).
- **Soviet domestic mobilization (Priestland):** Leadership "obsessed with the competition with the West" (Stalin on "foreign bastard"; Khrushchev "Stalin kept trying to convince us that we... were no good"); system "already well adapted" from 1917/1930s war-mobilization structures; "Cold War... did not demand a special set of approaches" unlike US. Guns/butter: heavy industry priority squeezed consumption (1948 real wages 80% of 1940); Khrushchev Virgin Lands then consumer shift but 1962 price hikes sparked "Novocherkassk" riots suppressed by tanks. Technocracy vs ideology (tekhnika/politika): postwar Stalin Zhdanov/Lysenko campaigns (but protected physics); Khrushchev revivalist party mobilization; "Western culture... helped to undermine the collectivism"; popular fear of war + patriotism but "dissatisfaction with... austerity" and stiliagi subculture (pp. 442–463).
- **Decolonization/global South (Bradley):** Rapid: UN 51→117 members 1945–65 "majority... states in the global South." Roots long pre-Cold War (WWI, Wilson self-determination "met with silence", Comintern, Pan-African 1919, Négritude, Gandhi, Vietnamese eclectic borrowing Jefferson/Gandhi/Sun + Marx). 1945 "almost unimagined contingency" for superpowers; Stalin/US limited early engagement. Post-1950: Korean War/Mao China intensified; US funded ~75% French Vietnam war; Khrushchev "world-historical significance" + aid to India/Egypt/Indonesia; US modernization theory as "universal model" with "racialized paternalism." "The Cold War both empowered and constrained" but "local particularities" and "transnational postcolonial visions" persisted beyond bipolar (pp. 464–476, links to [[decolonization]], [[soviet-union]], [[united-states]], [[world-wars-era]]).
  Gulag/katorga/exile central throughout: Special Camps hardened dehumanization (numbers, torture handcuffs); Kengir uprising (1954) 8,000 zeks seized internal freedom before betrayal; post-Stalin relaxations but "Archipelago Remains" with ongoing 25-yr sentences and identical conditions (Solzhenitsyn lines 5201–8600). Exile "temporary pen to hold sheep marked for slaughter"; 15m peasants in uncommemorated 1930s famine. Primary testimony exposes system as inherent, not aberration.

  Nuclear strategy evolved from US monopoly and early retaliation concepts through massive retaliation (Eisenhower/Dulles "New Look") to debates over limited war, counter-force vs. counter-value targeting, and stable second-strike deterrence. Civilian theorists (Brodie, Schelling, Kahn, Wohlstetter) emphasizing second-strike
  forces, the "reciprocal fear of surprise attack," and the logic of stable
  deterrence through invulnerable retaliatory capabilities (Polaris, Minuteman).
  See dedicated coverage in [[concepts/deterrence]], [[concepts/assured-destruction]],
  [[concepts/counterforce]], [[concepts/nuclear-strategy]], [[processes/nuclear-deterrence]],
  and [[sources/freedman-evolution-of-nuclear-strategy-1981]]. Technological
  competition (ICBMs, ABMs, MIRVs) and pre-emption pressures shaped doctrine on
  both sides even as the prospect of mutual destruction made all-out war
  increasingly unthinkable.

  **Formal Strategists and Arms Control (Freedman Range 05):** The "Formal Strategists" applied game theory (Prisoner's Dilemma, chicken) and bargaining theory. Schelling reframed military strategy as "the art of coercion, intimidation and deterrence" rather than victory; the stable balance of terror as "a massive and modern version of an ancient institution: the exchange of hostages." Stability favoured protected second-strike weapons over accurate counter-force ones ("Cities would be threatened, not weapons"). Arms control shifted from disarmament to "managing rather than eliminating the arms race" and "induced or reciprocated 'self-control'" based on "incomplete antagonism." Central theme: stability. Nuclear pacifists critiqued the moral basis but many conceded practical stable deterrence or supported arms control measures such as the 1963 Partial Test Ban. (Freedman 1981, ARMS CONTROL chapter, pp.194–207).

  **Bargaining, Escalation and City-Avoidance:** Intra-war deterrence/compellence via signaling and modulated threats; escalation as crossing "saliencies" (focal points such as national boundaries, nuclear vs conventional). Kahn's 44-rung escalation ladder with 6 firebreaks (including "no nuclear use," "city targeting"); escalation dominance as capacity for marked advantages at a rung. Schelling's "threat that leaves something to chance" and brinkmanship ("deliberate creation of a recognizable risk of war") in the game of Chicken; reputation and "face" as "the interdependence of a country’s commitments." McNamara's city-avoidance (Ann Arbor 1962) sought destruction of military forces not civilian population to give "the strongest possible incentive to refrain from striking our own cities," with damage limitation estimates (10M vs 100M US casualties) and second-strike targeting of used/empty sites. Shift in emphasis toward assured destruction. (Freedman 1981, BARGAINING AND ESCALATION and CITY-AVOIDANCE chapters, pp.208–239).

- **Proxy conflicts and the Third World:** Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan, and
  numerous smaller interventions and civil wars in which the superpowers
  competed for influence without direct clash. These wars tested limited-war
  theories and exposed the limits of nuclear deterrence in peripheral conflicts.
- **Alliance management and European nuclear debates:** NATO's reliance on US
  extended deterrence, French and British independent forces, the MLF controversy,
  and the distinct "dissuasion" theories of Gallois and Beaufre. See
  [[actors/gallois-pierre]], [[actors/beaufre-andre]].
- **Détente, stagnation, and the end:** From the Helsinki process and SALT through
  the renewed tensions of the early 1980s to Gorbachev's perestroika/glasnost,
  the 1989 revolutions in Eastern Europe, and the USSR's dissolution in 1991.

## Oil, Uranium, and Strategic Resources, 1945–1962 (from Leffler & Westad CHCW Vol. I, Painter ch.)

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|Leffler & Westad CHCW Vol. I]] Range 06 (Painter): "Control of strategic raw materials played a key role in the origins and outcome of World War II and continued to be a source of power and policy during the Cold War." (p. 486) "The United States and the Soviet Union were continent-spanning countries rich in oil and strategic minerals, and their control of these resources helped underpin their power." US looked globally (esp. Third World); Soviets to Eastern Europe sphere. (pp. 486–487)

Uranium control: Combined Development Trust (US-UK) "controlled an estimated 97 percent of world uranium production and 65 percent of the world supply of thorium" by war's end. Baruch Plan (1946) aimed to preserve US monopoly; Soviets blocked. Soviets developed own + Czechoslovakia + East German mines (forced labor; ~45% of Soviet demand by 1950 from East Germany). US relied on Belgian Congo (Hiroshima bomb source) then South Africa. Importance lessened mid-1950s with production increases, low-grade tech, and thermonuclear shift. (pp. 487–488)

Oil was "central to military power and economic life". WWII all major systems oil-powered. Postwar: "oil-powered forces remained central". "In the late 1940s, seven large oil companies [Seven Sisters: Exxon, Mobil, Chevron, Texaco, Gulf + BP + Shell] controlled over 90 percent of oil reserves outside North America and the Communist countries." (pp. 488, 491)

Soviet oil: net importer (Romania/Austria occupation zones) to 1953; production "from 154.9 million barrels in 1945 to ... 1.78 billion barrels in 1965"; net exporter 1954. Shift from Caucasus to Volga–Urals. Exports mainly to bloc; also "Soviet oil helped Cuba survive the cutoﬀ of oil from Western companies after 1960." Revenues bought Western tech/grain but "propped up a system that was in serious trouble". (pp. 489–490, 506)

US: world leader (66% 1945 production falling to 25.8% 1965); per capita consumption rose sharply; "oil increased its share of total US energy consumption from 30.5% in 1945 to 44.1% in 1955". (p. 490)

Middle East pivot: US mission 1943: "The center of gravity of world oil production is shifting from the Gulf–Caribbean region to the Middle East". Postwar "great oil deals" consolidated private management. Iran 1946 crisis: JCS warned loss of Persian Gulf access meant "oil-starved war" for US/allies; "concern over access to the region’s chief resource played an important role in the shift in US foreign policy" (Truman Doctrine). (pp. 493–495)

Marshall Plan: >$1.2 billion for oil purchases 1948–51 (>10% ERP total); shifted Europe from western hemisphere to Middle East (85% of imports by 1950 vs. 20% prewar). "helped maintain markets for US oil companies". (pp. 497)

Iran 1951 nationalization (Mossadeq/Anglo-Iranian) → boycott + 1953 CIA-orchestrated coup; post-coup US-led consortium. Baghdad Pact (1955). (pp. 499–500)

Suez 1956: Nasser nationalized canal (2/3 of Gulf-to-Europe oil transit); "Without Middle East oil, Eisenhower warned in early 1957, 'Western Europe would be endangered just as though there had been no Marshall Plan, no North Atlantic Treaty Organization.'" US pressure forced allied withdrawal; led to Eisenhower Doctrine bolstering conservative regimes. (pp. 501–502)

OPEC founded Sept 1960 in Baghdad (Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Venezuela) after posted-price cuts; "subordinated the broader Arab nationalist agenda ... to the narrower economic goal of protecting the revenues of the major oil-producing countries". ## NATO Conventional Defence and Flexible Response Debates (Freedman Range 07)

The Eisenhower New Look and massive retaliation produced a nuclear bias in NATO force structure. The 1952 Lisbon goals (96 divisions) were abandoned in favour of 30 divisions plus tactical nuclear weapons. Conventional defence was viewed as radical, requiring major manpower and doctrinal change. Helmut Schmidt argued that a credible local blocking capability "would be as good a deterrent as the threat of nuclear retaliation. In fact it would be better because the initial use of nuclear weapons would be such an irrational act that it could not be expected to influence another’s actions." Liddell Hart advocated militia-based deep defence using local forces in underground shelters for lower cost and higher resilience.

The "trip wire" or "plate glass" role for conventional forces was accepted: US troops in Europe created an involvement that linked local defence to the American strategic deterrent. Schelling noted that conventional forces could embody "the threat that leaves something to chance" — the combination of direct costs and the heightened risk of general war. Kissinger observed that stronger limited-war forces increased the scale required for victory and thus inhibitions on initiating hostilities.

The Kennedy-McNamara push for conventional balance met strong European resistance. Europeans feared that treating conventional war as limited would confine devastation to Europe while sparing North America, and that any "threshold" or "pause" would ease Soviet risk calculations. "All war had to be deterred." McNamara argued Soviet divisions were overstated (smaller, understrength, unreliable); after adjustments NATO and the Warsaw Pact had approximate ground equality. Enthoven and Smith concluded the conventional option had been viable all along but was blocked by "despair and hopelessness" as a self-fulfilling prophecy (e.g., only three days' ammunition stocked).

Brodie critiqued the mechanical "fire-break" between conventional and nuclear war, preferring reliance on opponents' aims, intentions or fears. The fire-break theory had worked to the West's advantage precisely because the Soviets had not accepted it.

Flexible response (codified in MC 14/3, 1967) emerged as the compromise: stronger conventional forces to raise the nuclear threshold plus continued escalation options. It did not produce the major European spending increases the US desired.

## The European Nuclear Option (Freedman Range 07)

US pressure against independent European deterrents clashed with British and especially French aspirations. Wohlstetter criticized national strike forces as expensive, lacking economies of scale, vulnerable, and prone to loss of control or hair-trigger postures. Small forces could only credibly threaten blunt city attacks. McNamara's 1962 Athens speech judged them "dangerous, expensive, prone to obsolescence and lacking in credibility as a deterrent."

Britain's force evolved through Skybolt cancellation to the Nassau Polaris agreement. Rationales shifted from filling gaps to political influence ("ticket of admission" to the peace table) and the "second decision centre" — an independent European capability that would force the Soviets to reckon with possible British use even if doubting US resolve. Britain remained closely tied to NATO and the US; the force was never presented as a full substitute.

France under de Gaulle made the force de frappe central to restoring national rank and independence from super-power hegemony. Gallois stressed "proportional deterrence" ("The thermonuclear force can be proportional to the value of the stake it is defending") and the superior credibility of national will over alliance guarantees; he viewed conventional defence as obsolete. Beaufre emphasized psychological risk manipulation through multiple decision centres that would "destabilise" a too-stable nuclear threshold and restore prudence. Ailleret and de Gaulle pursued "tous azimuts" (all points of the compass) reach. France withdrew from NATO integrated military command in 1966 while remaining in the political alliance. Later adjustments (Giscard/Mery "extended sanctuarization") showed some movement toward more Alliance-compatible concepts, but the "anti-cities strategy" remained the declared deterrent.

These debates exposed the core tension of extended deterrence: the difficulty of credibly threatening actions that would be suicidal for the guarantor once parity existed, and the European insistence that all war (not merely nuclear) be deterred on the continent.

## Geographic Scope

Truly global. The core confrontation was transatlantic and Eurasian, but
decolonization and proxy conflicts extended the period's effects to every
continent. The nuclear dimension created a de facto global strategic space even
where local conventional balances predominated.

## Transition

The Cold War ended with the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, the
reunification of Germany, and the rapid withdrawal of Soviet forces from Eastern
Europe. The bipolar structure dissolved into a unipolar moment dominated by the
United States, though nuclear arsenals, proliferation risks, and great-power
rivalries persisted into the Contemporary period. Many of the doctrinal and
alliance-management problems first articulated in the 1950s–1970s (credibility of
extended deterrence, the stability of mutual vulnerability, the political
economy of arms racing) remained relevant.

## Historiography

Nuclear strategy is unusually well documented through declassified US planning
documents, the extensive civilian literature (RAND studies, IISS Adelphi Papers,
public speeches by McNamara, Schlesinger, etc.), and the published Soviet and
Chinese doctrinal debates that Freedman synthesizes. The absence of actual
nuclear use after 1945 means all analysis is counterfactual and inferential;
historians and strategists continue to debate whether deterrence "worked," what
the role of nuclear weapons was in preventing World War III, and whether the
cyclical rediscovery of the same ideas reflects genuine learning or the inherent
intractability of the problem. European perspectives (Gallois, Beaufre, British
"second centre" arguments) are critical counterweights to the dominant American
civilian-strategist narrative. Collection coverage via this and related sources
is strong for the intellectual and policy history of the Western side; Soviet
archival material post-1991 has refined but not overturned the main lines.
