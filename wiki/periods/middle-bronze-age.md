# Middle Bronze Age

## Overview

The **Middle Bronze Age** (~2,100–1,550 BCE in the Near East) is the era of the
**second urbanization** and the consolidation of **territorial states and palace
economies** across the Fertile Crescent, after the fragmentation that closed the
[[early-bronze-age|Early Bronze Age]]. It is dominated by two great recoveries —
the **Sumerian bureaucratic revival under the [[mesopotamian-chronology|Third
Dynasty of Ur]]** and the **Egyptian [[middle-kingdom-egypt|Middle Kingdom]]** —
and, running through and beyond both, the spread of the **[[amorites|Amorites]]**,
the West-Semitic peoples who would found the kingdoms of [[mari|Mari]], Yamhad,
Assyria, and ultimately Babylon. This wiki page is anchored to
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]], which carries the story to
c. 1,750 BCE; the later Middle Bronze Age (Hammurabi, the Old Assyrian and Old
Babylonian worlds, the rise of the Hittites and the Hyksos) will be deepened from
CAH Vol. II.

## Major Developments

- **The Ur III revival (~2112–2004 BCE)** — after the [[gutian-invasion|fall of
  Akkad]] and Utu-khegal's liberation, [[ur-nammu|Ur-Nammu]] and [[shulgi|Shulgi]]
  build the [[third-dynasty-of-ur|Third Dynasty of Ur]], a centralized, intensely
  **bureaucratic** Sumerian state (the largest archival corpus of the ancient
  world) with deified kings and the earliest [[ur-nammu|law code]]. It falls under
  [[amorites|Amorite]] pressure and the [[fall-of-ur-2004bce|Elamite sack of Ur]]
  (~2004 BCE), opening the Isin–Larsa period.
- **Elam** — the highland-and-lowland civilization of [[elam|Elam]] (Awan,
  Simashki; capital [[susa|Susa]]) is Mesopotamia's perennial eastern rival and
  trading partner; it takes its revenge in the sack of Ur.
- **The Egyptian Middle Kingdom (~2040–1650 BCE)** — the
  [[reunification-of-egypt-2040bce|Theban reunification]] of Egypt under
  [[mentuhotpe-ii|Nebhepetre Mentuhotpe II]] ends the
  [[first-intermediate-period|First Intermediate Period]]; the
  [[middle-kingdom-egypt|Eleventh and Twelfth Dynasties]] build a classic age of
  Egyptian state, art, and literature, conquer **Lower Nubia** with a chain of
  fortresses, and reclaim the **Faiyum**. [[senwosret-iii|Senwosret III]] breaks
  the provincial nomarchs.
- **The Amorite ascendancy** — the [[amorites|Amorites]] (Sumerian *martu*, Akkadian
  *amurru*), long infiltrating from the Syrian steppe, become the dominant political
  force: Ur III builds a "Wall against Amurru," then falls to them; Amorite dynasts
  take over the cities of Mesopotamia and Syria, founding the kingdoms that
  Hammurabi's Babylon will later unify.
- **The second urbanization of the Levant** — after a non-urban
  [[first-intermediate-period|EB–MB intermediate]] interlude (Kenyon: nomadic
  pastoralists, single-burial tombs, no walled towns), the Levant is **re-urbanized**
  in the Middle Bronze Age proper as a network of fortified **Canaanite/Amorite
  city-states**, with [[byblos|Byblos]] again the great Egyptian-facing port.
- **The Old Assyrian trade network** — the [[old-assyrian-trade|Assyrian merchant
  colonies (*kārum*)]] at [[kultepe|Kanesh]] knit [[ashur|Ashur]] to the Anatolian
  metal market in **tin and textiles for silver** — the documented heir of the EBA
  [[early-bronze-age-anatolia|Anatolian metal economy]], and the moment Anatolia
  first enters written history. Out of [[ashur|Ashur]] the Amorite
  **[[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]]** (~1813–1781 BCE) built the **first Assyrian
  territorial empire** over Upper Mesopotamia, ruling from Shubat-Enlil with his
  sons over Mari and Ekallatum — the proof-of-concept for the later Assyrian
  conquest-state, and a contemporary of Hammurabi.
- **Indo-European arrivals.** The [[indo-european-migrations|Indo-European
  migrations]] become historically visible in this period: Indo-European names in
  the [[kultepe|Kültepe]] tablets and the Hittite-Luwian languages of Anatolia, the
  Indo-Iranian "Aryan" element among the Hurrians, and — on the Greek mainland —
  the **[[coming-of-the-greeks-1900bce|coming of the Greeks]]** (~1900 BCE: Middle
  Helladic, Minyan ware) out of the [[early-bronze-age-aegean|Aegean EBA]]. In
  Crete the Minoan palaces arise instead by unbroken local development.

## Key Actors

- The [[third-dynasty-of-ur|Third Dynasty of Ur]] (Ur-Nammu, Shulgi); the
  [[amorites|Amorite]] kingdoms; [[mari|Mari]]; Old Assyria
  ([[ashur|Ashur]], [[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]]).
- The [[middle-kingdom-egypt|Egyptian Middle Kingdom]] (Mentuhotpe II;
  Amenemhat I–III; [[senwosret-iii|Senwosret III]]).
- The Canaanite city-states of the revived Levant; the princes of [[byblos|Byblos]].

## Geographic Scope

Historically active: Mesopotamia, Egypt and Nubia, the Levant, Syria, Anatolia
(via the Assyrian colonies), and the Iranian plateau (Elam). The Aegean (Minoan
Crete's first palaces) and the Indus develop in parallel; long-distance exchange
links them all (the **Tod Treasure** in Egypt contains Aegean silver and
Mesopotamian cylinder-seals).

## Transition

Follows the [[early-bronze-age|Early Bronze Age]] across the ~2,200–2,000 BCE
horizon of collapse and reformation (the fall of Akkad, the Egyptian First
Intermediate Period, the EB–MB break in the Levant). Closes toward the
[[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze Age]] with the Hyksos in Egypt, the rise of the
Hittite Old Kingdom, the Kassites, and the international system of the later second
millennium. The boundary is conventional and regional.

## Historiography

**Source quality.** Far richer than the EBA: continuous royal and administrative
cuneiform (the vast Ur III bureaucratic archives; the later Mari archives), and in
Egypt a "golden age" of literary and didactic texts (the *Instruction for
Merykare*, the *Prophecy of Neferty*, the *Story of Sinuhe*, the *Instruction of
Ammenemes I*) that are simultaneously historical sources and royal propaganda.
Egyptian chronology rests on the king-lists (the Turin Canon), regnal dates, and
co-regency reckoning.

**Debates.** Whether the Twelfth Dynasty's reach into Syria-Palestine amounts to an
"**empire**" or only diplomatic-commercial influence (Posener: an "uneven but
vigorous" informal domination, evidenced by sphinxes at Ugarit/Qatna, resident
officials at Byblos and Megiddo, and the geographic detail of the **Execration
Texts** — though those are ritual, not a political map); the **Amorite question**
(invaders vs. long-term infiltrators; the scale of their role in the fall of Ur);
and the nature of the Levantine **EB–MB break** (Kenyon's sharp non-urban
discontinuity vs. continuity readings).

**Collection coverage.** Strong for Egypt and Mesopotamia in this volume (CAH
Vol. I.2) and forward into CAH Vol. II; the Levantine and Anatolian threads are
moderate and archaeological. The wider comparative MBA awaits later volumes and
monographs.
