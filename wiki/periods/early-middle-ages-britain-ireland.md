# Early Middle Ages — Britain and Ireland (c.500–c.1100)

A regional sub-period page nested under [[early-middle-ages|the Early Middle Ages]],
covering the **[[british-isles|Atlantic Archipelago]]** — Britain *and* Ireland —
between the end of Roman Britain and the consolidation of the Norman settlement. Its
analytical spine is [[sources/stafford-companion-early-middle-ages-britain-ireland-2009|Pauline Stafford's *Companion*]],
which insists the period be read "as it developed, rather than teleologically … from
the viewpoint of its later shape." That programme — set out further in
[[concepts/four-nations-british-history|four-nations British history]] — refuses the
Anglocentric "island story," integrates Ireland (whose sources are far richer than
England's before c.800), and treats *England, Scotland, Wales,* and *Ireland* as
modern terms that "correspond very imperfectly to the political geography, or to the
identities" of the period.

## Overview

Around 500 the archipelago was a patchwork of small polities emerging from the
collapse of Roman power: British (Brittonic) kingdoms descended from Roman *civitates*
in the west and the "Old North"; the new Anglo-Saxon kingdoms forming in the lowland
east; the Picts north of the Mounth; the trans-marine kingdom of [[actors/dal-riata|Dál Riata]]
spanning Argyll and Antrim; and a dense mosaic of Irish *túatha*. Over six centuries
this resolved — not inevitably — into a smaller number of larger kingdoms:
[[actors/kingdom-of-wessex|Wessex]] expanding into the
[[actors/kingdom-of-england-late-anglo-saxon|kingdom of the English]];
[[actors/kingdom-of-alba|Alba]] (Scotland) coalescing out of Pictland and Dál Riata;
Welsh hegemonies under the line of [[places/wales|Rhodri Mawr]]; and a contested Irish
high-kingship that [[actors/brian-boru|Brian Bóruma]] wrenched from the
[[actors/ui-neill|Uí Néill]].

**Comparative materialist frame (Wickham 2005).** [[sources/wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham's *Framing*]] places the archipelago inside a North Sea / "North" comparative league rather than a teleological English nation-story. From c.550 English and Welsh polities were typically **county-scale or smaller**; by 700 England had four dominant kingdoms (Northumbria, Mercia, Wessex, East Anglia) still **tiny and materially poor by Frankish standards**, though Offa of Mercia (757–96) was treated as Charlemagne's equal and Offa's Dyke was the largest human construction in Europe between Roman roads and early modern canals. England's structures originated differently from [[francia|Francia]]; local society long paralleled Wales; the tenth-century English "nation state" coherence arose from the **Viking crisis**, not from Wickham's 400–800 period. See [[tax-based-vs-land-based-states]], [[peasant-mode-of-production]], [[aristocratic-demand]], [[politics-of-land]].

## Major Developments

- **The end of Roman Britain (c.409–500).** British self-government after 409/410, the
  *adventus* of the Anglo-Saxons, and post-Roman [[concepts/ethnogenesis|ethnogenesis]]
  — the making of new peoples (Gildas and the migration debate). Wickham stresses
  **three structural differences** from the Continent (especially northern Gaul):
  (1) dramatic fifth-century **material-culture collapse**; (2) by c.600 highly
  **fragmented politics** with dozens of small autonomous "kingdoms," unlike
  Romano-Germanic continental kingdoms; (3) **land tenure** (from charters after
  c.670 in England / earlier SE Wales) without parallel in the Roman land law
  inherited by Romano-Germanic kingdoms — exclusive Roman-style tenure broke down
  in the early fifth century, not as a pre-Roman survival. Micro-regional tribal
  politics (Chertsey-scale ~300-hide units matching smallest Tribal Hidage polities)
  **preceded** Anglo-Saxon immigration and was taken over by the newcomers.
- **Anglo-Saxon immigration and flat hierarchy.** Wickham rejects continuity theories
  that reduce Anglo-Saxons to a tiny aristocratic conqueror elite: early settlements'
  material simplicity and the shift of lowland England to Germanic speech with almost
  no Brittonic influence require a **popular/peasant element** among immigrants, even
  though Anglo-Saxons remained a minority (ballpark ≥10:1 indigenous British under AS
  control by 500). Fifth–sixth-century settlements show **no archaeological hierarchy**
  in spatial structure or finds; cemetery hierarchy is limited outside Kent. Seventh-
  century written sources show more hierarchy but still extreme political fragmentation
  into tiny polities that do not fit Wickham's ideal type of the state.
- **Wales tribalized without Germanic conquest.** Wales was barely conquered by
  Germanic invaders yet by the seventh century lacked Roman cultural traditions beyond
  writing/Christianity and was divided into four major (Gwynedd, Powys, Dyfed,
  Glywysing) and several minor kingdoms. SE lowland Gwent (Llandaff charters from after
  550) shows king-dominated large units with low food dues — building-blocks of small
  tribal kingdoms, not exclusive estates — a rough model for what the eastern lowlands
  looked like **before** the Anglo-Saxons came. Socio-economic break equalled eastern
  lowlands: Roman tenure dissolved into small tribal units.
- **Conversion (5th–8th c.).** The [[processes/conversion-of-britain-and-ireland|Christianisation of Britain and Ireland]]:
  Palladius and Patrick in Ireland; the Roman mission of 597; the Irish/Columban
  mission from Iona; the [[events/synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]] (664).
- **English recomposition and Mercian state-building (c.550–800).** After c.550 the
  Anglo-Saxon crisis began reversing: late sixth-century funerary display; after 600
  rich separate burials (Sutton Hoo, Prittlewell) and "final phase" ordinary cemeteries
  with fewer goods — **status difference stabilizing**. Seventh-century settlements more
  structured (Cowdery's Down, Yeavering); after 700 coastal emporia (York, Ipswich,
  London, Hamwic) and richer eastern sites (Flixborough). Stabilization of status
  c.550/600; substantial wealth accumulations only after 700 — elite ability to control
  resources increasing ([[aristocratic-demand]]). Normative unconditional aristocratic
  loyalty (ASC 755/757 retainers; *Beowulf*) and preference for alternative lordship over
  local independence (unlike Wales/Ireland) meant expanding kingdoms did not dissolve
  into tribal parts: **slow growth of state power was a joint project of kings and
  aristocrats**. Eighth-century Mercian kings (Æthelbald, Offa, Cenwulf) controlled
  southern England through mixed hegemony/conquest/absorption; exercised port tolls,
  extended hospitality rights, church councils, and Offa's Dyke logistics — most
  elements of Wickham's ideal-type state **except fully exclusive land tenure**. By 800
  initial English state-building was largely completed; Offa shows coherent state power
  can develop when peasant subjection is only beginning.
- **Bookland and the peculiar English tenurial path.** Authentic Anglo-Saxon charter
  sequences begin in the 670s (Theodore of Tarsus period); early charters are rulers'
  permanent gifts to churches, later with exemptions; by mid-eighth century bridge-/
  fort-building and army service are retained royal obligations. Bookland/*ius
  ecclesiasticum* was a privileged form only rulers could grant — unlike documented
  Continental regions — and by the ninth century laymen sought it widely. Unchartered
  land (*folcland*) remains opaque. The crucial move from [[peasant-mode-of-production|peasant-mode]]
  to aristocratic economy in England was the **ninth century** — unusually total but
  probably too slow for peasants to perceive as a break, with no documented resistance
  (contrast clearer later Spanish takeovers).
- **Wales checked; Ireland at the low-aggregation end.** Seventh–eighth-century Wales
  saw some accumulation (Meurig ap Tewdrig in Glywysing; Gwynedd expansion) but stopped
  short of English-style capillary government. Geography, disruptive English interventions,
  and a decentralized church inheritance limited centralization. From the eighth century
  aristocrats alienated land without needing kings; royal and aristocratic power
  **rivalled rather than complemented** each other — full state-building checked until
  the thirteenth century; tribal political economy long after 800; incomplete victory over
  free peasant autonomies. Wickham's northern aggregation spectrum: **Ireland** (few scale
  increases for a long time; Iceland later analogous) → **Wales** (began small-scale
  dominance then stopped; maybe Saxony/Norway/Sweden) → **Denmark** (wider but intermittent
  hegemonies; maybe Frisia) → **England** (nearly unbroken aggregation to sub-regional
  states by 800). Not a league-table of success/failure but independent responses.
- **The Insular golden age (7th–9th c.).** A flowering of Latin learning (Gildas,
  Aldhelm, Bede) and [[concepts/insular-art|Insular art]] (Book of Durrow, Lindisfarne
  Gospels, Book of Kells), with Britain and Ireland briefly "the intellectual
  powerhouse of western Europe."
- **Exchange and demand.** Before the seventh century few ceramics moved short distances;
  Illington-Lackford / Sancton-Baston links more likely travelling potters or gift-exchange
  than specialist commercial networks; loom-weights imply household cloth — levels of demand
  like SE Spain but for a whole region. Early Anglo-Saxon demand was too low for professional
  production or real markets (West Stow/Mucking vs Goudelancourt: similar buildings, utterly
  different ceramic worlds). Exchange narrative tracks social development: East Anglia first
  proper landowner rents and commercial exchange by the eighth century; Offa's Mercia was
  the first English "state" but heartland archaeology shows **political power without East
  Anglian-style commercial networks** — exchange guides wealth accumulation, not distribution
  of political power.
- **The Viking Age (793–11th c.).** Raids from Lindisfarne (793); the Great Army (865);
  the [[processes/scandinavian-settlement-of-britain|Scandinavian settlement]] of the
  Danelaw, the Northern Isles, and Hiberno-Norse [[places/dublin|Dublin]].
- **State formation (9th–11th c.).** The making of [[places/england|England]] under
  Alfred, Æthelstan, and Edgar; of [[actors/kingdom-of-alba|Alba]] under Constantín son
  of Aed; Welsh overkingship under Hywel Dda and Gruffudd ap Llywelyn; and the Irish
  high-kingship "with opposition."
- **The Norman impact (1066–c.1100).** [[events/norman-conquest-of-england-1066|1066]]
  and its aftershocks across the whole archipelago — the harrying of the north, the
  Norman penetration of Wales, and dynastic ties reaching Scotland and Ireland.

## Transition

The period opens with the dissolution of Roman Britain (see
[[periods/late-antiquity|Late Antiquity]]) and the post-imperial state-formation shared
with the Danube and Slav worlds. It closes not at 1066 but at **c.1100**, because
Hastings "has more immediate relevance for England than for the rest of the area";
c.1100 captures the Norman impact across Ireland, Scotland, and Wales, opening onto the
[[high-middle-ages|High Middle Ages]].

## Historiography

The field is unusually self-conscious about its own nationalism. Nineteenth- and
twentieth-century Irish (Eoin MacNeill), Welsh (J. E. Lloyd), and Scottish scholarship
served "the task of nation building"; the "tribal/Celtic" stereotype placed Ireland and
Wales at a primitive evolutionary stage. Donnchadh Ó Corráin's 1978 article was a
turning point, relocating early Ireland within early medieval *Europe* rather than
ancient India. Recent work has "Gaelicized" early Scotland (Broun, Woolf), dissolved the
"Celtic church" and "Celtic kingship," and — following James Campbell's "English
exceptionalism" debate — questioned whether the precocity of the English state is real
or an artefact of differential charter survival. The sources are radically uneven:
clerically produced, overwhelmingly Latin and ecclesiastical, far richer for southern
England and south-eastern Ireland than for the north and west; archaeology is the one
expanding body of evidence. See [[controversies/british-national-identity-historiography]]
and the migration-scale, continuity-vs-catastrophe, and Clontarf debates flagged on the
relevant pages.

**Wickham's comparative North (*Framing the Early Middle Ages*, 2005).** Against both
English teleology (the "island story" ending in the tenth-century state) and pure
national genealogies, Wickham embeds Britain and Ireland in a **comparative spectrum of
northern responses to c.500 tribal fragmentation** — Ireland, Wales, Denmark, England —
as independent structural paths, not ranks of success. All northern societies were in
large part or entirely [[peasant-mode-of-production|peasant-mode]] c.600; by 800 most
were moving toward a feudal direction (England most, Ireland least). Without coercive
predominance of landowning aristocrats — including kings — over the peasantry, political
resources stayed too limited for fully fledged states. Lacking village-level documents
for the North, Wickham invents the speculative **"Malling"** model (lowland England, late
seventh century, with Scandinavian analogies) for peasantries under weak aristocratic
hegemony: relative free equality in ~12 household compounds, sharp free–unfree household
exploitation, limited commercial links, **tribute rather than exclusive Roman-style land
tenure**, and *gemot*-level patronage. Trajectories to 900 could produce thegnly ownership,
church tenancy, or continued free proprietorship. This is the regional application of
*Framing*'s seven trends (fiscal simplification; relative aristocratic weakness outside
Francia/Levant; peasant autonomy; militarized elite identity; regional divergence; social
fluidity; end of imperial unity) and of the concepts [[tax-based-vs-land-based-states]],
[[aristocratic-demand]], and [[politics-of-land]]. Stafford's *Companion* remains the
narrative and four-nations spine; Wickham supplies the materialist, cross-regional
economic-political structure.

## Collection Coverage Note

**Strong.** The collection is excellent for early medieval western Eurasia, and Stafford's
*Companion* supplies a dense, specialist-authored backbone for the whole archipelago —
including the Irish, Scottish, and Welsh material that the wider collection covers less
well. [[sources/wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham *Framing* (2005)]]
adds the comparative social-economic frame for England, Wales, and Ireland against
Francia, Denmark, and the Mediterranean. Gaps remain in narrative political history of
tenth–eleventh-century Scotland (source-poor in any case) and in Welsh and Pictish
material culture beyond the standard syntheses.
