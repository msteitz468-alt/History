# Nara Period (710–794)

The first period in which [[japan|Japan]] had a permanent Chinese-style capital and a
fully articulated **centralized imperial state**. Named for the capital **Nara**
(*Heijō-kyō*, founded 710), built on the grid plan of the [[tang-dynasty|Tang]] capital
Chang'an, it is the culmination of the [[taika-reform-645|Taika Reform]]'s drive to
turn the loose [[yamato-japan|Yamato]] chieftainship into a bureaucratic monarchy. Drawn
from Mason & Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]] (chs 3–4).

## Overview

The Nara state was the mature **[[ritsuryo-state|ritsuryō state]]** — the Chinese-modeled
legal-bureaucratic order — running on the **Taihō Code** (701–702), which created a Department
of Worship ([[shinto|Shintō]] rites) and a Department of State, a provincial structure of
~66 provinces and ~600 districts, a population census every six years, allotment ricefields
held in theory by the Crown, and taxation in rice, products, labor, and military service.
It was the high-water mark of the imported Chinese model of direct imperial rule —
though enforced by a small elite "without a strong element of armed force," and resting
on hereditary aristocratic office rather than the Chinese merit examination.

## Major Developments

- **The capital and state Buddhism.** Nara, never quite as grand as planned, anchored a
  national project: the **Tōdaiji** temple (16 city blocks; its Buddha Hall still among
  the largest wooden buildings on earth) and the **great bronze Buddha** (dedicated 752)
  ordered by **Emperor Shōmu** to propitiate the gods after the 737 smallpox epidemic —
  the centerpiece of a system of provincial temples meant to focus the realm on the
  imperial house. See [[japanese-buddhism]].
- **The first writings.** The **Nihon shoki** (720), the official national history in
  Chinese (with the *Kojiki*, 712), and the ***Man'yōshū*** poetry anthology — the
  beginnings of a Japanese literary tradition built on Chinese learning, taught at a
  Confucian **university** for aristocratic sons.
- **Frontier and defense.** Campaigns against the Kumaso/Hayato of southern Kyūshū and
  the **Ainu** of the north; a defensive posture toward Korea/China after the 663 defeat.
- **The monk-in-politics scandal.** The affair of Empress Shōtoku and the priest
  **Dōkyō** (who nearly gained the throne, 770) bred a lasting fear of politically
  ambitious monks — a prime reason the court soon abandoned Nara.

## Key Actors

- [[shotoku-taishi|Prince Shōtoku]] (earlier, but the ideological forerunner) · the
  reforming sovereigns and the Fujiwara forebears.
- **Emperor Shōmu** — patron of the Tōdaiji and state Buddhism.
- The hereditary court aristocracy who monopolized the new bureaucratic offices.

## Geographic Scope

The Yamato heartland (the Nara basin) and an expanding imperial reach over Honshū,
Kyūshū, and Shikoku; the northern frontier still contested with the Ainu.

## Transition

**Ended** by Emperor Kammu's move of the capital (to Nagaoka 784, then Heian/Kyoto 794) —
chiefly to escape the entrenched Nara monasteries — which opens the [[heian-period|Heian
period]]. **Preceded by** the [[yamato-japan|Yamato/Asuka]] era and the
[[taika-reform-645|Taika Reform]].

## Chinese learning and the conflict of native and alien (Sansom)

George Sansom's [[sansom-short-cultural-history-japan|cultural history]] frames the Nara age
as the wholesale import of Chinese civilization onto a materially "poor" native base — and
sounds his signature theme: a "young people in an early stage of rapid development" were
"likely to **borrow more than they could assimilate, to take over outward form rather than
inward significance**," so that the history of Japan after the [[taika-reform-645|Taika
Reform]] becomes "the history of a **conflict between native habits and alien principles**."
The imported package was not only [[japanese-buddhism|Buddhism]] but **Confucianism** — the
cult of the **family** and **filial piety** (the *Classic of Filial Piety* was part of every
school's curriculum by the end of the eighth century), and a theory of the state in which
the ruler holds Heaven's mandate by **virtue** (deposable if deficient) rather than, as in
Japan, by [[shinto|divine ancestry]] — a tension the Japanese resolved in their own way.
Sansom argues (against some) that Japanese **ancestor worship** grew chiefly from Confucian
doctrine, not from early Shintō. The Nara cultural legacy — the great monasteries
(Hōryūji, Tōdaiji), and the cosmopolitan treasures of the **Shōsōin** (textiles,
instruments, and goods from across Asia to Persia) — embodies this absorption of continental
high culture.

## Historiography

The period is unusually well documented for so early an age because the Japanese had
adopted writing: the Nihon shoki, the Shoku Nihongi court chronicle, and the
extraordinary survival of physical objects in the **Shōsōin** treasure-house (textiles,
instruments, medicines from across Asia, even Persia — preserved 1,200 years). The
long-running debate is whether the Taika–Taihō state was an "experiment doomed to
failure" (because Japan never matched the Chinese model) or the durable foundation of
later administration; Mason & Caiger insist Japanese history be read on its own terms,
noting the cultural borrowings "continued to flourish as elements of a distinctively
Japanese civilization."
