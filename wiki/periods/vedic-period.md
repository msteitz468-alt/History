# The Vedic Period (India)

The **Vedic period** (c. 1500–600 BCE) is the formative age of North Indian civilization between
the decline of the [[indus-valley-civilization|Harappan cities]] and the rise of the
[[magadha|Magadhan]] kingdoms — named for the **Vedas**, the religious corpus that is its principal
source. It is a regional sub-period of the wiki's [[early-iron-age|Early Iron Age]]/[[archaic-period|Archaic]]
framework. Documented through Romila Thapar's [[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*,
Vol. I]] (1966).

## Overview

After the Harappan decline, **Indo-Aryan-speaking pastoralists** (the term *Aryan* is **linguistic,
not ethnic/racial**, Thapar stresses) spread from the north-west into the Punjab and the upper
Ganges-Yamuna region. Over the period they shifted from **semi-nomadic cattle-keeping to settled
agriculture** — a transition decisively accelerated by the arrival of **iron** (c. 800 BCE), which
made clearing the Gangetic forests feasible and generated the surplus and leisure behind the period's
later religious-philosophical speculation. The early *Rig-Veda* world of the western Punjab gave way,
in the later Vedic age, to larger settled communities across the whole Indo-Gangetic plain.

## Major Developments

- **The Vedic corpus.** The *Rig-Veda* (1,028 hymns, parts pre-1000 BCE) is the earliest literary
  source; the *Sama*, *Yajur*, and *Atharva* Vedas, then the *Brahmanas* and *Upanishads* (from c. 700
  BCE) followed. The epics — the **Mahabharata** (the Kurukshetra war, perhaps c. 900 BCE) and the
  **Ramayana** (read by Thapar as a memory of Aryan penetration southward) — took final shape much
  later (1st millennium CE).
- **The origins of [[caste-varna|caste]].** From an early three-class scheme (kshatriya warriors,
  brahman priests, vaishya commoners) the four-*varna* order crystallised, the fourth (shudra) rooted
  in the conquered, darker-skinned **Dasas** (*varna* = "colour"). See [[caste-varna]].
- **Political forms.** Tribal kingship (the *rajan*, originally a war-leader with no land-right or
  regular tax) checked by the assemblies **sabha** and **samiti**, evolving toward **hereditary,
  divinely-sanctioned monarchy** and the king–priest interdependence — alongside non-monarchical tribes
  governed by their assemblies (the seedbed of the later [[magadha|gana-sangha republics]]).

## Key Actors

The Vedic tribes (Bharatas, Kurus, Panchalas) and their *rajans* and *purohitas* (chief priests);
the brahman composers of the hymns; the conquered **Dasas/Panis**. No individuals are securely
historical — the period is known through collective, religious texts.

## Geographic Scope

The **Punjab and the Indo-Gangetic plain**, expanding eastward and (in the epics) southward into the
Deccan. The wetter, more forested landscape of the time shaped the agrarian colonisation that defines
the age.

## Transition

It **succeeds** the [[indus-valley-civilization|Harappan civilization]] (with which Thapar sees *no*
political continuity) and **gives way** to the age of [[magadha|Magadha]], the cities, and the
heterodox sects (Jainism, Buddhism) from c. 600 BCE — the subject of Thapar's next chapter.

## Historiography

The Vedic age rests on a **religious literary corpus** (composed by and for the priestly elite) plus
tentative archaeology — above all the **Painted-Grey Ware** culture (c. 1100–500 BCE), provisionally
identified with the Indo-Aryans because its iron-using, horse-breeding agriculturists "broadly tally"
with the Vedic description. Thapar writes against two distortions: the colonial dismissal of India as
a "stagnant" spiritual culture, and the nationalist treatment of the Vedic age as a lost Golden Age.
The "Aryan invasion vs. migration vs. indigenous" question is treated under [[indo-european-origins]];
Thapar already prefers **migration and acculturation** over conquest.
