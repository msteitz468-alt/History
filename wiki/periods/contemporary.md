# Contemporary (1991–present)

## Overview

The **Contemporary** period opens with the end of the [[cold-war|Cold War]] and the
dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. In [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]'s terms,
it is the period in which the three great dynamics of the post-1750 world —
**production, destruction, and connection** — reach planetary saturation: a single,
deeply integrated global economy; an environmental crisis (above all climate change)
that the volume treats as the open-ended culmination of the
[[anthropocene|Anthropocene]]; and a *connectedness* — material and imagined,
increasingly digital — that makes "globalization" something qualitatively more than
the long-distance contact of earlier eras.

Recent US-focused narrative sources (e.g. [[sources/kruse-zelizer-fault-lines-2025|Kruse & Zelizer, *Fault Lines* (2025)]] and [[sources/jenkins-short-history-of-america-2025|Jenkins, *A Short History of America* (2025)]]) are strengthening coverage of domestic political culture, polarization, and the post-1991 US experience within the broader global frame.

CWH VII, published in 2015, writes the contemporary world as the still-unfolding tail
of the [[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]. Its leading edges: the **shift of
industrial and economic gravity back toward East Asia** (China surpassed the United
States as the world's largest energy user in 2010), partially closing the
[[great-divergence|Great Divergence]]; the **deceleration of population growth**
(UN projections of a 2050 growth rate slower than 1800's) even as absolute numbers
swell; **renewed mass [[migration-in-human-history|migration]]** with a wider variety
of sending and receiving countries than the nineteenth-century waves; and a
**climate system** that may already have passed self-reinforcing tipping points.

## Major Developments

- **The post-Cold-War order:** the brief "unipolar moment," the spread of the
  national state to the former Soviet space (formalized in the **[[dissolution-of-the-soviet-union|Dissolution of the Soviet Union]]**), and the proliferation of conflicts along the seams of former multinational empires (the Balkans, the Caucasus). In the new **[[russian-federation|Russian Federation]]**, the decade was defined by **[[yeltsin-boris|Boris Yeltsin's]]** presidency, the painful transition of **[[economic-shock-therapy|Economic Shock Therapy]]**, the **[[russian-constitutional-crisis-1993|1993 Constitutional Crisis]]**, the **[[first-chechen-war-1994|First Chechen War]]**, and the **[[russian-financial-crisis-1998|1998 Financial Crisis]]**. Service details the terminal phase: the failed August 1991 coup (lines 3466–3484), Yeltsin's Belovezha Accords dissolving the USSR (8 Dec 1991, lines 3510–3514), price liberalization ("shock therapy") on 2 Jan 1992 causing 245% inflation in January alone (lines 3539, 3543), voucher privatization enabling nomenklatura managers and "oligarchs" (lines 3565, 3704), the violent 1993 storming of the White House (lines 3623), and the rise of Putin after 1999 (lines 3758–3775) who reasserted state control over business and regions while presiding over oil-driven recovery and the 2008 Georgia war.

- **Hyper-globalization:** tightly integrated markets, global supply chains, and
  the connectedness that Thomas Zeiler's (absent Book 2) chapter calls
  "globalization" in the strong sense — competition intensified as distance ceased
  to protect producers.
- **The East Asian ascent:** China's rapid, coal-fired [[industrialization]] — "the
  fastest industrial revolution in world history" — and the broader return of Asia
  to the center of global production, the empirical core of the revisionist
  [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] story. This rise was the fruit of **Deng
  Xiaoping's post-1978 "reform and opening"** — the market turn within the one-party
  state built by the [[chinese-communist-revolution|Chinese Communist Revolution]] —
  which (as Jonathan Spence's [[spence-search-for-modern-china|*Search for Modern
  China*]] traces) carried China's long "search" from [[mao-zedong|Mao's]] catastrophes
  to superpower status.
- **The climate crisis and the mature Anthropocene:** accelerating fossil-fuel
  consumption, the highest species-extinction rate in 65 million years, tropical
  deforestation, and possibly irreversible climate change (see
  [[anthropocene]], [[great-acceleration]]).
- **Demographic transition completed in much of the world**, with sub-replacement
  fertility spreading and ageing populations — even as parts of Africa and South
  Asia continue to grow (see [[demographic-transition]], [[population-politics]]).
- **Persistent inequality and unfree labor:** by some counts more people are
  enslaved today, in absolute numbers, than in 1860 (Bales), though as a far smaller
  share of the labor force.

## Key Actors

- The **United States** as post-Cold-War hegemon (see [[united-states]]); a rising
  **China**; the **European Union**; resurgent regional powers (India, Brazil,
  Russia, the Gulf states).
- Transnational actors: multinational corporations, the UN system and its agencies,
  global NGOs, and the worldwide [[environmentalism|environmental movement]].

## Geographic Scope

Genuinely global and, for the first time, with no region untouched by the
integrated economy and the shared environmental predicament — though the volume
stresses that the *experience* of globalization remains profoundly uneven.

## Transition

**From:** the [[cold-war|Cold War]]. The collapse of Soviet communism and the end
of the bipolar order (1989–1991) opened the period — see [[1991-end-of-cold-war]].
**To:** open. CWH VII is the terminal volume of the Cambridge World History; it
leaves the period's outcome — climate, the global balance of power, the
sustainability of the Great Acceleration — explicitly unresolved.

## Historiography

As the present is, by definition, the hardest period to historicize, CWH VII writes
it forward from long-run structural trends rather than as settled narrative. Its
distinctive claims are environmental (the Anthropocene as the master frame) and
economic (the partial reversal of the Great Divergence, the multipolar future of
industrialization). The volume is self-conscious that "globalization," "the
Anthropocene," and even "the contemporary" are categories shaped by the very
processes they describe, and that the field of world history is itself a product of
this connected age (Wiesner-Hanks's preface). Periodization of the present remains
genuinely open: 1989/1991, 2001, and 2008 all have advocates as sub-period breaks.

From Freeze (2002, lines 11201–14900, esp. ch. 15): Yeltsin era (1991–1999) defined by "shock therapy" (2 Jan 1992 price liberalization: 2509% inflation 1992; GDP -43% 1991–97), voucher privatization (~70% enterprises by 1996), rigged "shares-for-loans" (1996–8; Yukos acquired for $159m vs $7–10bn value), 1993 constitutional crisis (White House stormed), First Chechen War (1994–6; Khasaviurt Accords), 1996 election (Yeltsin 35%→54% vs Ziuganov via oligarch funding/media), and 17 Aug 1998 meltdown (default on $40bn T-bills; ruble crash, approval 2%). "Family"/oligarch influence (Berezovskii, Potanin, Diachenko) and high turnover (5 PMs) accelerated state disintegration (tax revenue collapse, regional sovereignty assertions, "grab-ization"). Putin (PM Aug 1999; acting Pres 31 Dec 1999; elected 53% Mar 2000) rebuilt via 7 super-districts (plenipotentiaries), Council of Federation reform, 13% flat tax, land privatization (July 2001, non-ag), and "dual patriotism" (prerevolutionary symbols + Soviet anthem; positive Soviet legacy + Orthodox ties). Boom from 1998 devaluation + oil prices (11→30$/bbl); but persistent poverty (~31%), demographic crisis (male life expectancy 59 in 2000), inequality (decile 14:1). Complements Service with Freeze's emphasis on weak state, corruption, and social costs of transition. (Grounded exclusively in range.)
