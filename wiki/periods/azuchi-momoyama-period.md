# Azuchi–Momoyama Period (1568–1600)

The age of **reunification** — when [[oda-nobunaga|Oda Nobunaga]] and
[[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Toyotomi Hideyoshi]] ended a century of civil war and forged the
unified state that the [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa]] would inherit. Named for Nobunaga's
castle at Azuchi and Hideyoshi's at Momoyama (Fushimi), it is the threshold of early-modern
[[japan|Japan]]. From Mason & Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of
Japan*]] (ch. 12).

## Overview

In barely three decades the [[muromachi-period|warring-states]] anarchy was reversed.
**Nobunaga** seized the capital region, destroyed the militant Buddhist establishments, and
pioneered gunpowder warfare; **Hideyoshi** completed the conquest of the daimyo and fixed
the social order; the era closes with **Sekigahara (1600)**, which delivered the country to
[[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]]. It also saw the **first sustained contact with Europe** and a
brilliant, secular, power-displaying art.

## Major Developments

- **Reunification.** [[oda-nobunaga|Nobunaga's]] rise (Okehazama 1560; Kyoto 1568;
  Nagashino 1575) and [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Hideyoshi's]] completion (Kyūshū 1587; Odawara
  1590) — by force tempered with negotiation, the daimyo subdued but not abolished.
- **The end of militant Buddhism.** Nobunaga's burning of **Hieizan (1571)** and reduction
  of the **Ishiyama Honganji** broke the monasteries' political and military power.
- **The social settlement.** Hideyoshi's **land survey** and **sword hunt (1588)** disarmed
  the peasantry and **separated [[samurai|samurai]] from farmers** — freezing the class
  structure of early-modern Japan.
- **Korea.** Hideyoshi's two failed **invasions of Korea (1592, 1597)** — devastating Korea,
  weakening Ming China, gaining Japan nothing.
- **Europe and Christianity.** The **Portuguese** (from the 1540s) and Spaniards brought
  firearms, the Macao [[global-silver-trade|silk-for-silver]] trade, and Catholic missions
  (the Jesuits); Hideyoshi's expulsion edict (1587) and the 1597 martyrdoms opened the
  suppression completed under [[sakoku]].

## Momoyama art

The unifiers displayed power not in temples but in **monumental castles** (Azuchi, Osaka)
with soaring stone-and-plaster keeps (*tenshukaku*), and in **gold-ground screen and wall
painting** (the **Kanō school** — Eitoku — and Hasegawa Tōhaku) — a grand, colorful,
**secular** art for a confident new ruling class, alongside the refined **tea ceremony**
(Sen no Rikyū). The era marks the decisive **secularization** of Japanese high culture.

## Key Actors

[[oda-nobunaga]] · [[toyotomi-hideyoshi]] · [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] — the three unifiers.

## Geographic Scope

Centered on the Kinai (Kyoto–Osaka) heartland from which the unifiers projected power across
all Japan; the Iberian trade and mission concentrated in northern **Kyūshū** (Nagasaki).

## Transition

**Preceded by** the [[muromachi-period|Muromachi]] warring-states anarchy (Nobunaga
deposing the last Ashikaga shogun, 1573). **Ended** by **Sekigahara (1600)**, which opened
the [[edo-period|Edo period]] under [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]].

## Historiography

A short, pivotal period read as the birth of the early-modern state: Mason and Caiger trace
to it "the beginnings of the beginnings of modern Japan" — national reunification, growing
secularization, and the first encounter with the West. They caution against Western writers
exaggerating Christianity's role, which was real but regionally confined.
