## Overview

The Mesolithic (literally "Middle Stone Age") covers the transition from the
cold, highly mobile world of the Paleolithic to the agricultural settlements of
the Neolithic. Its character varies dramatically by region: in Europe it
is a recognizable transitional period between Paleolithic and Neolithic; in
other parts of the world the concept applies less neatly.

This period is defined by the Holocene warming (~11,700 BP): the end of the
last ice age. Rising temperatures, rising sea levels, and dramatically
transformed landscapes forced adaptations. Tasmania was separated from
mainland Australia; New Guinea separated. Many coastal Paleolithic sites were
drowned. The Americas megafauna — already diminished — disappeared entirely
during this transition (partly climate, partly hunting).

In some regions, communities were already experimenting with sedentism,
intensive plant harvesting, and early animal management — the preconditions
for the Neolithic Revolution.

## Major Developments

**Holocene warming and geographic transformation**
- Temperatures rose rapidly from the Last Glacial Maximum low (~22,000 BP)
- Sea levels rose from ~125 metres below present to within a few metres of
  current levels by end of this period
- Tasmania separated from Australian mainland; New Guinea separated from
  Australia; many Pacific islands became isolated
- Sahara region was relatively moist ("Green Sahara") in early Holocene,
  supporting savanna environments where now there is desert

**Younger Dryas (~12,900–11,700 BP)**
A sudden cold reversal at the start of this period, lasting ~1,200 years.
The Younger Dryas disrupted the gradual warming and may have accelerated
the transition to agriculture in the Near East by stressing forager
communities dependent on wild grain resources. The coincidence of the
Younger Dryas with American megafaunal extinctions (~12,800 BP) is notable
but causation is debated.

**Near East: Natufian culture and proto-agriculture**
The Natufians (Levant, ~15,000–11,500 BP) were among the first communities
to become semi-sedentary, building permanent or semi-permanent villages and
intensively harvesting wild grains. They are the proximate precursors of
the Neolithic Revolution in Southwest Asia. By the end of this period, some
Natufian communities were beginning to cultivate domesticated varieties of
wheat and barley — a process that accelerates in the Neolithic. See the dedicated
[[natufian-culture]] page.

[[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. III, Garrod & Clark) supplies
the classic regional sequence behind the Natufian. In the Zagros, the
**Baradostian** Upper Palaeolithic at Shanidar/Zarzi gives way after a
stratigraphic gap to the **Zarzian** microlithic industry (radiocarbon
~12,000 BP), which CAH judges *older* than the Natufian of the Levant. The Mount
Carmel caves (Tabun, Skhul/es-Sukhul, el-Wad) and Jericho's Natufian levels anchor
the Levantine record. Clark frames the transition by the onset of **Neothermal**
(early Holocene) forest conditions, tracked by pollen analysis — the environmental
shift (see [[postglacial-environment-near-east]]) that reshaped foraging across
Europe and Southwest Asia.

**East Africa and Northeast Africa**
Evidence from the eastern Sahara for early cattle herding in the 9th–8th
millennium BCE — the earliest cattle herding in world history (Ehret) — and
wild grain cultivation along the Nile corridor. Khoesan language communities in
southern/eastern Africa. Distinctive 'Batwa' cultural complex in the Congo Basin.
This period sees Africa's independent pathways toward food production: Afrasian
wild-grain collecting, Nilo-Saharan cattle herding, and Niger-Congo pre-
domestication cultivation with independently invented ceramics (Bandiagara, Mali,
before 9400 BCE). See [[early-african-food-production]] and
[[african-language-family-origins]]. Ehret's thesis: "Africa at the beginning of
the Holocene was not a place apart."

**Americas: Paleoindian transition to Archaic**
The highly mobile Clovis and post-Clovis cultures gave way (with megafaunal
extinction and the Younger Dryas) to new tool kits and signs of sedentism,
defining the Archaic period beginning ~8,000 BCE.

**Japan: Jomon culture**
Jomon communities were among the earliest anywhere to develop pottery
(~16,000 BP, predating this period) and to build semi-sedentary settlements.
The Jomon represent an unusually early transition to sedentism without
agriculture — a pattern made possible by the exceptional richness of
Japan's coastal and forest resources.

## Key Actors

- Natufian communities (Levant): key precursors to agriculture
- Jomon communities (Japan): sedentism without agriculture
- Post-Clovis Paleoindian communities (Americas)

## Geographic Scope

Global, with all inhabited continents covered. The Near East and East Asia
are the most historically consequential regions for this period, as they
are where proto-agricultural transitions are underway.

## Transition

Closes with the first domesticated crops and animals in Southwest Asia
(wheat, barley, sheep, goats) and China (rice, millet, pigs), marking the
opening of the [[neolithic]]. The Neolithic transition is not simultaneous
worldwide — it occurs at different times in different regions over thousands
of years. The Mesolithic intensification described here — Natufian wild-grain
harvesting, Jomon sedentism, pre-domestication cultivation — is the immediate
precursor of [[agricultural-origins]]; whether these foragers were "pushed" or
"pulled" into farming is the subject of [[why-did-agriculture-begin]].

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Stone tool assemblages, skeletal remains, plant and animal
remains (palaeobotany and zooarchaeology), ancient DNA. The archaeological
record is compressed and in some cases literally submerged (coastal sites now
under water from sea level rise). The Mesolithic is often understudied relative
to the Neolithic and Paleolithic.

**Dating methods**: AMS radiocarbon is reliable for this period; dendrochronology
begins to be usable toward the end.

**Major debates**: The causes and pace of the Neolithic transition; whether
agriculture was adopted or invented independently multiple times; the role
of climate (Younger Dryas) in driving the transition; whether sedentism
preceded or followed food production.

**Collection coverage**: Moderate. The CWH Vol. 1 Introduction covers this
period briefly; more detail will come from CWH Vol. 2 (agriculture origins)
and Phase 11 specialist prehistoric sources.

**Champion et al. 1984 (R03 range) addition:** Post-glacial adaptations (Ch. 4) framed farming spread as parallel adaptation across Europe and Near East. "the emergence of new resources and novel methods of exploiting these resources in the period 9000-7000 BC... can be seen from the European perspective not just as a foreign, isolated process but as a parallel one of adaptation to changing conditions." Mesolithic sites are explicitly mapped alongside early Neolithic ones. Social motives for agriculture (emulation/status via food) suggested in Ch. 4. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch. 5 / cache ~290-300, 1674+, 2380+)
