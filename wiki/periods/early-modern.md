# Early Modern (1500–1700 CE)

## Overview

The Early Modern period is when human history first became genuinely
**global**. Before 1500 the Afro-Eurasian world system (see
[[afro-eurasian-world-system]]) and the separate American world systems
developed in mutual isolation; after the Iberian oceanic voyages of the 1490s
the two hemispheres were permanently joined, and biological, commercial, and
cultural exchanges began to circulate on a planetary scale for the first time.
The Cambridge World History titles its volume on this era *The Construction of a
Global World* — and the verb matters: the global world was not discovered but
built, through migration, conquest, trade, missionary religion, and the violent
imposition of new labor regimes.

This was not a story of a developed European core acting on a passive world.
As Dirk Hoerder stresses, capitalist development, sophisticated commerce, and
producer migrations had emerged independently in Asian and eastern
Mediterranean economies; what changed was that European territorial states
fused **state power with mercantile investment** — armed trade backed by
public-funded soldiery — and projected it across oceans. The Ming and Qing,
the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires, the kingdoms of West Africa, and the
Aztec and Inca polities were all major actors in this world, not its objects.
European maritime dominance was real at sea by 1700 but remained thin on land:
the [[military-revolution|"military revolution"]] did not translate into
European superiority over the great Asian land empires until well into the
eighteenth or nineteenth century.

Within Europe, the period opens with the **[[italian-wars-1494|Italian Wars]]
(1494–1559)** — the Habsburg–Valois struggle over a wealthy, fragmented Italy — which
generalized the Italian balance-of-power model into a continental **state system** with
**resident diplomacy**, and drove the military revolution. This and the European
state-system story are now being built out from the **[[new-cambridge-modern-history-v1|New
Cambridge Modern History]]** (Modern series, 1493–1945; ingestion begun 2026-06-13) — which
also supplies the **[[protestant-reformation-1517|Reformation]]** and Counter-Reformation, the
**[[price-revolution]]**, the **[[thirty-years-war-1618|Thirty Years War]]** and Peace of
Westphalia, and the age of **[[absolutism]]** under Louis XIV.

The defining structural facts of the period are the
[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]], the catastrophic demographic
collapse of the Americas, the Atlantic slave trade and the plantation complex,
the silver-fueled integration of a world economy spanning Potosí, Seville,
Amsterdam, and Canton, the worldwide expansion of Christianity and Islam, and
the rise of the chartered company as a hybrid state-commercial actor.

## Major Developments

- **The joining of the hemispheres** (from 1492): permanent contact between the
  Americas, Africa, and Eurasia; see [[columbian-exchange-1492]].
- **American demographic collapse**: Eurasian and African pathogens, warfare,
  and forced labor (encomienda, repartimiento, the Potosí *mita*) reduced
  Indigenous populations by up to 90 percent in the worst-hit regions.
- **The Atlantic slave trade**: forced migration of millions of Africans into a
  plantation belt producing sugar, tobacco, and later coffee; see
  [[early-modern-migrations]].
- **The silver world economy**: New World and Japanese silver flowed toward
  China, integrating price systems across the globe (treated in later cycles).
- **Oceanic and gunpowder empires**: Portuguese and Dutch armed trade in the
  Indian Ocean; the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal land empires; Ming–Qing
  consolidation; Russian expansion across Siberia.
- **Confessional expansion and dialogue**: Catholic and Protestant Reformations,
  Jesuit missions in China, Japan, and the Americas, the spread of Islam through
  Sufi networks, and the emergence of syncretic forms (Sikhism, Latin American
  and African-Atlantic Christianities). See
  [[intercultural-relations-early-modern]].
- **Interpolity law**: shared practices of [[interpolity-law|protocol,
  jurisdiction, and protection]] that allowed cross-cultural exchange and treaty
  without a common law of nations.

## Key Actors

- Land empires: the [[mongol-empire|post-Chinggisid]] successor steppe orders,
  Ming and Qing China (see [[song-dynasty]], [[tang-dynasty]] for antecedents),
  the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires.
- American empires at contact: the [[aztec-empire|Aztec]] and [[inca-empire|Inca]].
- West African states: successors to [[mali-empire|Mali]] and
  [[songhay-empire|Songhay]], and the Kingdom of Kongo.
- Hybrid actors: the [[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] and
  [[english-east-india-company|English East India Company]], chartered companies
  wielding state-like powers of war and diplomacy.
- Religious orders and movements: the Jesuits, the Sufi orders, the Sikh gurus.

## Geographic Scope

Genuinely global for the first time: the **Atlantic world** (Europe, West and
Central Africa, the Americas) becomes a single interactive arena; the **Indian
Ocean** and **East Asian seas** are penetrated by European armed traders without
losing their own vitality; the **Pacific** is crossed by the Manila galleon. The
interior land empires of Eurasia — Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Ming–Qing, and
Muscovite — remain the demographic and military centers of gravity.

## Transition

**From the [[late-middle-ages|Late Middle Ages]]:** the period opens with the
Iberian voyages, the fall of Granada and expulsions of 1492, and the joining of
the hemispheres — a discontinuity sharp enough to anchor the
[[columbian-exchange-1492|1492 transition]]. **To the [[age-of-expansion|Age of
Expansion]] (1700):** the consolidation of oceanic empires, the maturing of the
plantation and silver economies, and the eighteenth-century reordering of the
Asian land empires (Mughal and Safavid collapse) mark the threshold to the next
period.

## Historiography

The period is the proving ground for **world and global history** as a field
(see [[world-history-as-field]], [[proto-globalization]]). Central debates:

- **The "rise of the West" and the Great Divergence**: when, why, and how far
  European power became dominant. CWH VI consistently pushes the divergence late
  and treats European ascendancy as contingent and uneven rather than structural
  before circa 1750–1800.
- **The [[military-revolution|military revolution]]**: whether early modern
  warfare underwent a revolution that explains European expansion — sharply
  contested (see [[military-revolution-thesis]]).
- **The origins of international law**: whether a universal law of nations
  emerged (Alexandrowicz) or only a "crude parallel morphology" of shared
  practices (Benton & Clulow); see [[interpolity-law]].
- **Eurocentrism**: the field's self-conscious effort to decenter Europe while
  acknowledging that the source base and the historiography remain
  overwhelmingly Anglophone and Europe-centered (see [[eurocentrism]]).

## Collection Coverage Note

CWH Volume VI is split into two books; **only Book 2, "Patterns of Change," is
present in the collection** (migrations, warfare, encounters, law, trade,
commodity flows, production, and religion). **Book 1, "Foundations"** — the
empire-by-empire surveys of the Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Ming–Qing, Russian,
and Iberian empires, and the crossroads regions (Caribbean, Indian Ocean,
Southeast Asia) — is **absent**, so this wiki currently lacks dedicated actor
pages for several of the period's central land empires (Mughal, Safavid,
Ottoman early-modern, Ming, Qing). These are flagged gaps. Per the standing
collection map, the post-1500 Islamic world (Ottoman/Safavid/Mughal), the
post-1500 Americas beyond archaeology, sub-Saharan Africa, and Russia/Eastern
Europe remain thinly sourced; CWH VI's thematic chapters partially offset this
but do not replace dedicated regional volumes.

A dedicated single-author synthesis of the religious transformation of the period is now ingested —
**[[eire-reformations|Carlos Eire, *Reformations: The Early Modern World, 1450–1650*]]** — covering the
[[protestant-reformation-1517|Protestant]], Radical and [[catholic-reformation|Catholic]] Reformations
and the long road from a sacralized Christendom toward secularity.
