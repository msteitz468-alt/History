# Strategy

**Strategy** is the deliberate linking of political ends to available means — military and non-military — in a contest of opposing wills. As defined in the comparative framework of the *Cambridge History of Strategy*, it is "**a comprehensive way to try to pursue political ends, including the threat or actual use of force, in a dialectic of wills**."

This page organizes the wiki's coverage of strategic *practice* across time and civilizations (as distinct from the operational details of warfare covered in the main [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy Hub]]). It centers on the major comparative synthesis:

## Recurring Features of Strategic Practice

Certain patterns appear across widely separated societies and eras (drawn from the comparative analysis):

- **Aspiration to universal monarchy or world dominion**: Assyria, Achaemenid and Sasanian Persia (*Shahanshah*), Macedon under Alexander, Rome, Byzantium (*Basileus Basileōn*), the Caliphate, the Mongols ("one empire under the Blue Heaven"), Ming/Qing China, the Mughals, and many Indic polities. This often resembled the *vijigishu* ideal in Kautilyan thought.

- **Peacetime deterrence by display**: Armed forces used to awe and deter rather than fight (Ming/Qing emphasis on "awe"; Prussian reviews; parades generally).

- **Dynastic marriage as alliance tool**: Used by the Habsburgs (archetype), ancient Chinese states, Macedonians, Roman Principate, Byzantium, Guptas, and European dynasties.

- **The wider non-kinetic toolkit**: Economic leverage (tribute, subsidies, trade denial), negotiated territorial concessions, alliances, propaganda, and triumphal narrative.

**High Middle Ages Western Europe (proprietorial pattern per France)**: Strategy was heavily conditioned by land as wealth and limited government. Castles (proprietorial homes and admin centres first) turned war toward ravaging the economic base and siege rather than battle. "The spread of castles intensified the basic character of war. The fact that a defender could take refuge in a castle decreased the attractions of battle and increased the need to ravage as an act of war." Networks of castles + field army (exemplified in Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem) was the effective combination; isolated strongpoints could delay but rarely decide. The 1184–85 Hainaut campaign shows a successful "castle strategy" of garrisoning, scorched earth, and political attrition without major battle or siege. ## Culture-Specific and Era-Specific Variation

## The European Dimension of Nuclear Strategy (Freedman Range 07: Conventional Defence and Independent Nuclear Options)

In the 1950s–1960s NATO confronted the problem of extended deterrence: how the United States could credibly threaten nuclear war on behalf of Europe once the Soviet Union possessed a secure second-strike capability. The Eisenhower-era nuclear bias (abandonment of Lisbon force goals, reliance on tactical nuclear weapons) made any serious conventional option appear radical. Proponents such as Helmut Schmidt argued that a local defence capable of blocking attack would deter as effectively as nuclear threats — and more rationally — because nuclear first use was inherently irrational. Liddell Hart proposed militia forces organised locally in underground shelters to create a deep, resilient, low-cost defence network less vulnerable to interdiction.

US ground forces in Europe were valued primarily for their nationality: they served as a "trip wire" or "plate glass" guaranteeing US involvement before any nuclear decision. Conventional forces could thus embody Schelling’s "threat that leaves something to chance" — imposing direct costs while exposing both sides to a heightened risk of general war. Kissinger noted that stronger limited-war forces would force any aggressor to contemplate a scale of effort approaching all-out war, thereby strengthening overall deterrence.

The Kennedy–McNamara drive for conventional balance and flexible response encountered fierce European resistance. Europeans feared that Americans would view a European conventional war as limited (sparing North America) while Europeans experienced it as total. Any explicit "threshold" or "pause" before nuclear use would ease Soviet calculations and weaken deterrence. "All war had to be deterred." McNamara’s team countered that Soviet conventional strength was overstated once paper divisions, relative costs, manpower, and effectiveness were adjusted; by the mid-1960s approximate ground equality existed. Enthoven and Smith diagnosed "despair and hopelessness" as the self-fulfilling reason NATO stocked only three days of ammunition.

Brodie attacked the "fire-break" theory as mechanically optimistic: it assumed both sides would observe a sharp conventional–nuclear distinction and stop at the limit. He preferred reliance on political aims, intentions, and fears, and noted that Soviet refusal to accept US ideas had worked to the West’s advantage. The 1967 MC 14/3 compromise (flexible response) strengthened conventional forces to raise the nuclear threshold while preserving escalation options, but delivered far less European spending than Washington hoped.

Parallel debates raged over independent European nuclear forces. Wohlstetter criticised small national strike forces as prohibitively expensive without super-power scale economies, highly vulnerable (encouraging hair-trigger postures), and limited to blunt city attacks with no scope for controlled or city-avoiding options. Control problems multiplied dangerously with decentralised decision-making. McNamara’s 1962 Athens speech judged such forces "dangerous, expensive, prone to obsolescence and lacking in credibility as a deterrent."

Britain’s independent force was justified on political rather than purely strategic grounds: influence in Washington and a "second decision centre" that would complicate Soviet calculations even if US resolve were doubted. The Nassau Polaris deal (after Skybolt cancellation) gave technical soundness while preserving ultimate British control conditional on supreme national interests.

France took the most radical stance. Gallois argued that only a nation-state possessed the unity of will necessary for credible retaliation; "The thermonuclear force can be proportional to the value of the stake it is defending." Conventional defence was futile once nuclear weapons existed; alliances could not be relied upon. Beaufre stressed the psychological dimension: multiple decision centres would introduce uncertainty that "destabilised" an overly stable threshold and restored deterrent effect. "There must be several centres of decision." De Gaulle and Ailleret pursued "tous azimuts" reach and withdrew France from NATO integrated military command (1966) while remaining politically in the Alliance, insisting that nuclear weapons were the prerequisite of commanding one’s own destiny.

These debates illustrated the enduring difficulty of making nuclear threats credible when execution would be suicidal for the guarantor, and the European conviction that the political costs of dependence outweighed the economic burdens of independence. The compromises (trip-wire conventional forces, second centres, flexible response) reflected the impossibility of fully resolving the underlying tensions.

## Late Cold War Shifts and the Verdict on Nuclear Strategy (Grounded in Freedman 1981 Range 08)

By the 1970s the consensus around mutual assured destruction eroded under Soviet numerical catch-up in ICBMs/SLBMs, persistent modernization, moral critiques, and technological changes (MIRV + accuracy improvements enabling hard-target counterforce). The Nixon Administration's "sufficiency" proved vague; Laird's "triad" concept served bureaucratic balance more than coherent strategy. Schlesinger sought to restore "essential equivalence" plus "a variety of limited pre-planned options" for controlled responses, including to European contingencies. This produced PD59 (1980) targeting Soviet leadership, military forces, and control assets with a withheld "secure strategic reserve" for prolonged limited war. (Freedman 1981, SELECTIVE OPTIONS / ICBM VULNERABILITY / CONCLUSION).

ICBM vulnerability became "the question of the decade": a Soviet counterforce strike could eliminate the US Minuteman force while leaving the attacker with reserve capacity, forcing any US response onto city targets and handing the USSR escalation dominance. MX was pursued as remedy. European theatre nuclear modernization (neutron bomb controversy, 1979 decision for 572 US missiles) attempted to address coupling without fully decoupling from central systems. (Freedman 1981, p.387-392).

Freedman's overall verdict (Conclusion, Range 08): after 35 years, "one is forced to the conclusion that there has been a move to the analysis of second- and third-order issues." Three basic responses had been tried: (1) exploit the weapons' destructiveness to render total war an obvious folly; (2) deny the enemy benefit via effective defence or disarming first strike (exhausted for the moment); (3) deny the essence of nuclear weapons via precision low-yield systems for wholly military targets under meticulous control (defeated by the difficulty of establishing rules "capable of comprehension and obedience in the most exceptional and fraught of circumstances"). 

No side gained decisive advantage. "The essence of the problem is the difficulty of attaching any rationality whatsoever to the initiation of a chain of events that could well end in the utter devastation of one’s own society." Any strategy requiring first use "suffers from incredibility"; even pure retaliation threats suffer it. "We do not know how political leaders will react to the news of even only a few nuclear weapons exploding on their territory." Stability since 1945 has rested on "the fear of the whole process getting out of control" — "the strongest source of caution in the modern world." "The Emperor Deterrence may have no clothes, but he is still Emperor." "Those who have responsibility for unleashing nuclear arsenals live by the motto that if they ever had to do so they would have failed. Remarkably, up to now they have succeeded. C’est magnifique, mais ce n’est pas la stratégie." (Freedman 1981, CONCLUSION, pp. 396–400).

This constitutes a profound challenge to the traditional understanding of strategy as the rational linking of means to ends. In the nuclear domain, the dialectic of wills risks producing an outcome "beyond human control and comprehension."
