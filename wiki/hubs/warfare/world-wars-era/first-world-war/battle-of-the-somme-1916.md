# The Somme, First Day (1 July 1916) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/battle-of-the-somme-1916|The Somme]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/first-world-war|First World War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college-level analysis of the bloodiest day in British military history, built on
[[keegan-face-of-battle-1976|John Keegan]]'s *The Face of Battle* (1976), where it is the third and
climactic case study — the type-case of modern **industrial battle**. Keegan's
[[face-of-battle-method|face-of-battle method]] is here at full stretch: the first day of the Somme
is reconstructed as a **contest of competing technologies** and as a human catastrophe visited on a
volunteer civilian army.

**Cartographic support from Banks, A Military Atlas of the First World War (1975):** Somme front lines 1 July 1916 (opening assault).

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**Britain (Rawlinson / Haig).** The Somme was the British contribution to the Allied agreement to
mount simultaneous 1916 offensives, forced forward by the German assault on **Verdun**. The
objective on 1 July was a **breakthrough** of the German lines on a ~15-mile front, after which
cavalry would pass through into open country. The deeper context was that this was the first mass
commitment of the **"New Armies"** — Kitchener's volunteers of 1914 — and the plan was deliberately
shaped to their inexperience.

**Germany (von Below).** The German Second Army's objective was simply to **hold** a position it had
fortified for eighteen months: deep dug-outs mined "thirty feet below the surface," thick wire, and
machine guns sited to sweep no-man's-land. Their operational problem was to survive the bombardment
and **win "the race for the parapet"** — to get their machine-gunners up the dug-out stairs and into

**French sector and planning (Doughty).** Joffre chose the Somme to join British and French forces at the army boundary and shift the main effort to Britain. After Verdun compelled reductions, Sixth Army (Fayolle under Foch) with 11 divisions was to "support" the British: XX Corps north (cover British right), I Colonial and XXXV south. French possessed superior heavy artillery (552 pieces vs. 467 British main effort) and applied methodical battle doctrine (artillery first; bounded objectives; small-group infantry in depth). On 1 July French forces seized all first-day objectives south of the river (and on XX Corps front) with far lower relative losses than British; 4,000+ prisoners day 1, 8,000+ by day 3; later gains to Maisonette. Exploitation limited by Joffre's priority to British assistance and shifting difficulties. Joffre repeatedly pressed Haig for broader, more vigorous action (tense 3 July meeting); French continued attacks into autumn but results remained incremental. See [[events/battle-of-the-somme-1916]] and [[events/battle-of-verdun-1916]].

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground.** The chalk uplands favoured the defender: good observation, dry deep dug-outs, and
  fields of fire across the open, gently rising no-man's-land the British had to cross.
- **The bombardment as intelligence failure.** The British **believed the artillery would do the
  work**: "the enemy's wire would have been scythed flat, his batteries battered into silence and
  his trench-garrisons entombed." The infantry's task was conceived as **administrative** — "merely
  to walk forward to the objectives which the officers had marked on their maps." The slogan-spirit,
  in Keegan's phrase, was "Artillery conquers, infantry occupies."
- **What the gunners could not know.** Many gunners were "amateurs," who "had largely to guess at
  where their real targets, the German machine-gun crews, were hidden, and then very often lacked
  the skill to put a shell where they wanted it to fall."

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | British (Fourth Army) | German (Second Army) |
|---|---|---|
| Assault strength | ~60,000 infantry, first-day assault | Garrison divisions in depth |
| Composition | 143 attacking battalions, **97 "New Army"**; 4 of 13 divisions regular | Long-service defenders in deep dug-outs |
| Artillery | ~50,000 gunners; ~1,500,000 shells over 7 days | Counter-battery + protected field guns |
| Key weapon | The longbow's heir was the rifle — but the **plan**, not the weapon | The **machine gun** + deep dug-outs + wire |
| Posture | Advance in **waves**, four lines per battalion, men 2–3 yds apart, **at walking pace**, ~60 lbs of kit | Hold; race to the parapet at zero |

*Deployment:* the leading waves were to climb the parapet on scaling-ladders, extend into four lines
a company each, and advance "to the German wire" behind a timed barbed. Fire-and-movement was judged
"too difficult to be taught to the Kitchener divisions." Maximum objectives were set at the range of
the barrage (~4,000 yards). Zero hour: **7.30 a.m.**

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **The seven-day bombardment (24 June – 1 July).** ~1,500,000 shells; visually devastating but
   lethally **mismatched** — of ~21,000 tons of shell, only ~900 tons was high explosive ("each ten
   square yards had received only a pound of high explosive"). Most were 18-pdr **shrapnel**, which
   "could do very little damage to earthworks" and often failed to cut the wire. The German garrisons
   endured seven days in the dark on iron rations — "but they were alive."
2. **The race for the parapet (zero, 7.30 a.m.).** When the barrage lifted to the rear, the Germans
   raced up the dug-out stairs: "It was of vital importance not to lose a second in reaching the open
   before the British infantry could arrive at the dug-out entrances." They won the race.
3. **Infantry versus machine guns, in no-man's-land.** The walking waves were caught in the open.
   The machine gun — "concentrated essence of infantry" — kept "a stream of bullets so dense that no
   one could walk upright… without being hit." Battalions were destroyed in minutes: a Tyneside Irish
   sergeant — "by the time I had gone twenty yards, I seemed to be on my own. Then I was hit myself."
4. **Infantry versus wire.** Where shrapnel had failed, men found the wire **uncut** — "I was
   absolutely amazed to see it intact, after what we had been told" — and were machine-gunned down
   searching for gaps.
5. **Local lodgements and the bombing fight.** A few units broke in (the Ulster Division at the
   Schwaben Redoubt; the London Scottish). Inside the **traversed** trenches the fighting was by
   grenade — "throwing of a hand-grenade over the top of the traverse, and running round to arrive
   just after it exploded" — an "extremely dangerous game." Cut off and out of bombs, the lodgements
   were mostly bombed or starved back out; the London Scottish came back 266 strong out of 856.

**Key mechanisms:** the **competing-technologies** contest (British shells vs. German dug-outs +
machine guns); the **walking-pace, full-kit, wave plan** designed for inexperienced troops; the
**race for the parapet**; and the total **collapse of communication** once the infantry crossed the
top.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | British | German |
|---|---|---|
| Casualties (1 Jul) | **~60,000**, of whom **~21,000 killed** | Light where defences held |
| Illustrative sector | 8th Division: **5,121 of 12,000** | 180th Regiment: **~280 of ~3,000** |
| Worst battalion | Newfoundland Regiment: **710 lost**, all officers | — |
| Source | Keegan, ch. 4 (Official History + war diaries) | Keegan, ch. 4 |

- **Strategic result:** near-total failure of the breakthrough on the British front (the French and
  the British far south did better). The bloodiest single day in British history; the offensive then
  degenerated into a four-month attritional grind to November 1916.
- **Consequence:** the destruction of the **Pals battalions** meant single towns and workplaces lost
  their young men in an hour — a social catastrophe — and the day became the enduring symbol of the
  futility of the war, fixed in its literature (Sassoon, Graves, Blunden).

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**German success factors.** (1) **Deep dug-outs** that survived the bombardment; (2) winning the
**race for the parapet**; (3) machine guns sited for interlocking fire across fixed lanes; (4) wire
belts the British guns could not cut; (5) defensive discipline — holding fire until the waves were in
the open.

**British failures.** (1) **Faith in a bombardment** that was the wrong type and quantity of shell
for the targets; (2) the **walking-pace, full-kit wave plan**, which surrendered all tempo; (3)
amateur gunnery that could not find or kill the machine guns; (4) **no means of command once the
infantry left the trench** — buried cables "stopped at the edge of no-man's-land," and "rockets, tin
triangles, lamps, flags, and carrier pigeons… none were to prove of real use"; (5) a medical chain
(32 stretcher-bearers per battalion against 300+ wounded) that left a third of the killed to die of
survivable wounds in the open.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — The British objective (breakthrough) was beyond the means provided; the German
  (hold) was exactly matched to theirs.
- **Offensive** — Britain held the offensive but forfeited its essence (tempo, surprise) through the
  walking advance; Germany's "offensive" was the lethal counter-stroke from the dug-outs.
- **Mass** — Britain massed **men and shells** but not **effect** (wrong shell, uncut wire); Germany
  massed **fire** at the decisive points.
- **Economy of Force** — German machine-gun teams economised force brutally: the 180th Regiment held
  off and destroyed a division at a cost of ~280 men.
- **Maneuver** — Essentially **absent** on the British side by design; the plan substituted a timed
  walk for maneuver.
- **Unity of Command** — Broke down completely at zero hour: Rawlinson and Haig "attempted to follow
  the battle from scraps of imprecise information several hours old. Neither made real sense of it."
- **Security** — German security (dug-outs, wire) was the battle's decisive factor; British security
  of its own attack (counter-battery, wire-cutting) failed.
- **Surprise** — **Forfeited** — a seven-day bombardment announced the attack precisely; the only
  surprise was the Germans' to the British (intact wire, surviving machine guns).
- **Simplicity** — The plan was simple to the point of rigidity; its simplicity (walk to the
  objective) was its fatal flaw, not its virtue.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

The first day of the Somme is the permanent doctrinal warning that **firepower must be matched to the
target, not the spectacle** — a lesson re-learned whenever a bombardment's apparent devastation is
mistaken for its effect (suppression is not destruction). It is the founding case for the modern
emphasis on **combined arms and tempo**: the absence of fire-and-movement, of responsive command,
and of any means to exploit local success doomed the assault, and the war's later innovations (the
true creeping barrage, infiltration/stormtroop tactics, tanks and ground-attack aircraft) were the
answer to exactly these failures. It is also a standing study in **command-and-control collapse**:
once the infantry crossed the parapet the generals were blind, the foundation of the modern doctrinal
obsession with communications and situational awareness.

## 9. The Face of Battle — Keegan's Human Reading

Keegan's deeper subject is the **human** event. The attackers were the **Pals** — "the clerks of the
White Star shipping company formed up as one platoon, those of Cunard as another" — bound by a
solidarity that "threatened home with a catastrophe of heartbreak" when those friends died together.
Their morale was civic and religious (Second-Lieutenant Engall taking Communion "with dozens of
others who are going over tomorrow"; killed at Gommecourt) and their advance often "solid and
thorough — just like the men themselves." **Compulsion** existed (battle police, an officer shooting
a fleeing soldier to turn the rest), but Keegan insists it was not the main spring; the architecture
of the trench and the bond of the group did more. He dwells, as his method demands, on **what shells
do to the body** (blast that killed "with no more visible mark… than some singeing of their
clothing"; fragments that "amputate limbs, decapitate, bisect"), and on the **abandoned wounded** —
men who "crawled into shell holes, wrapped their waterproof sheets round them, taken out their
Bibles, and died like that." His most contested move is the **Auschwitz analogy** for "those long
docile lines of young men… plodding forward… to their own extermination inside the barbed wire" — an
image he concedes is "unhistorical" but presses for its emotional truth.

## 10. Historiography and Primary Sources

The day is documented by the **British Official History** (whose deliberately emotionless prose
Keegan dissects in ch. 1 as the limiting case of the bloodless "battle piece") and by an enormous
deposit of **battalion war diaries, letters, and memoirs** — the first war in which "we hear the
voice of the common man." Keegan reads these critically (against the self-serving "Bullfrog Effect")
and supplements them with physical and statistical reconstruction.

**Caveat on this page.** Its spine is Keegan's single chapter — magnificent on the **first day** and
on the **experience and mechanics** of the assault, but by design **not** an operational history of
the whole Somme offensive (July–November 1916), still less of the war. The wider operational analysis
(the planning quarrel between Haig and Rawlinson, the attritional months that followed, the German
defensive doctrine, and the tactical "learning curve" that led to 1918) awaits the dedicated
operational literature flagged **outstanding** in the roadmap (Strachan, Doughty, Lupfer, Gudmundsson,
Griffith). Casualty and force figures here are Keegan's.
