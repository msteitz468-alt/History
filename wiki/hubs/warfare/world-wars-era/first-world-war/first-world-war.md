# The First World War — Campaign & Operational Hub

> **Narrative:** [[events/first-world-war-1914|First World War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

The warfare-hub entry point for the operational and tactical conduct of the **First World War**
(1914–1918) — the war that fixed the modern image of **industrial attrition**: the trench deadlock,
the primacy of artillery and the machine gun, and the long, costly search for a way to restore
movement to the battlefield.

**1914 war-planning and decision context (Strachan, Range 01).** Pre-war plans were incremental and contingent, not a rigid "Schlieffen Plan" betrayed by Moltke. Germany abandoned Russia-only options in 1913 and committed to two-front war (France first), yet the timing of the Austrian ultimatum was chosen to reduce two-front risk. Moltke's fatalistic preventive-war advocacy (Slav-Teuton struggle; "the sooner it came the better") and the 8 Dec 1912 "war council" (decisions conditional on military readiness, not policy objectives) militarized diplomacy. The "blank cheque" was blank; Bethmann pursued localization until Russian mobilization forced the issue. Conrad's pressure and Austria's dependence shaped the Balkan trigger. These political/strategic origins (detailed in [[sources/strachan-first-world-war-to-arms-2001]]) set the parameters for the 1914 campaigns covered in later hub pages. See also [[events/july-crisis-1914]] and [[events/first-world-war-1914]].

**French pre-war army transformation and Plan XVII (Doughty Ch. 1, exclusive range).** The French army that mobilized in 1914 differed dramatically from the 1870 forces due to post-Franco-Prussian reforms and especially the 1911 High Command centralization. After the débâcle of 1870–71, leaders emphasized preparation, organization, modern weapons/equipment, and a national conscript army in place of the professional long-service force of the Second Empire (1874 Assembly committee reports). High Command remained cumbersome: Minister of War dominant; no powerful unified peacetime commander; duality between Chief of Staff of the Army and Vice President of the Superior Council of War ("generalissimo"); advisory role only for the Superior Council of War (1872) and Superior Council of National Defense (1906). Change was difficult; short ministerial tenures and entrenched bureaucrats limited progress.

The July 1911 Agadir crisis (German *Panther* at Agadir) provided the catalyst. New Minister of War Adolphe Messimy arrived with a mandate for High Command reorganization. He lost confidence in General Victor Michel (vice president; authority "nil"; senior generals called him "incapable" and "a national danger"; Michel criticized Grandmaison's offensive à outrance and proposed a reserve-heavy strategy with "demi-brigades" integrating active and reserve units plus a "vigorous offensive" on the left toward Antwerp-Brussels-Namur while defending the right from Belfort to Mézières). The Superior Council rejected the demi-brigade concept (19 July 1911). Michel resigned; Gallieni (Messimy's first choice) refused (retirement timeline and role in ouster); Pau demanded excessive appointment powers. Messimy selected Joseph Joffre.

A 28 July 1911 decree unified the vice-presidency and chief-of-staff roles under Joffre as chief of the General Staff, placing the High Command "completely and without reserve under Joffre's direction." (Dubail chief of staff subordinate except personnel/logistics; Castelnau deputy.) A January 1912 decree further consolidated. Joffre gained authority over training, doctrine, regulations, mobilization, and concentration — "the first time that such powers were confided to a single man."

Joffre immediately revised Plan XVI (published 6 Sep 1911): front-line divisions increased from ~38 to 58 (second-line/reserves and Italian-border forces shifted forward); Fifth Army moved left to Mézières; Sixth Army positioned nearer Verdun and Belgian frontier west of Luxembourg. Emphasis shifted from defensive-offensive to immediate offensive. Political leaders (Caillaux, then Poincaré) refused French preemptive entry into Belgium absent a "positive menace" (fears Britain/Italy would join Germany); Joffre accepted the limit while preferring Belgian terrain for decisive action. 

Franco-Russian military convention (finalized 1892–94, staff talks 1910–13) required simultaneous offensives to force Germany to fight on two fronts ("defeat of the German armies remains... the first and principal objective"; France "vigorous and determined" offensive ~day 11; Russia day 14 into the "heart" of Germany). Wireless links established. Joffre communicated intention of attack from northeastern France with simultaneous Russian effort.

Doctrine: Intellectual roots in Ardant du Picq ("moral ascendancy"/resolution to advance), Foch (firepower favored attacker), Bonnal (first battle decisive for mass armies). Grandmaison (Operations Bureau head) Feb 1911 lectures criticized "nearly complete atrophy" of offensive spirit. Joffre integrated "offensive à outrance." October/December 1913 regulations: "Only the offensive yields positive results"; "Battles are above all moral contests"; artillery "supports" (not prepares) attacks; bayonet "supreme weapon" of infantry; extreme energy in execution. 

Artillery and materiel: Confidence in excellent 75-mm (4,076 pieces) but severe shortage of mobile heavy field artillery (only 308 vs. German 848 with better range/rate of fire). Efforts for 105-mm howitzers delayed by technical services resistance and preference for mobile war; Joffre not sufficiently forceful (optimistic capability comparisons in Jan 1914); heavies placed at army level as siege artillery, not with corps/divisions. Three-year service law (July–Aug 1913, after debate) enabled ~700,000 active troops (three classes) to reinforce covering force and improve quality for immediate offensive.

Plan XVII (concept presented to Superior Council 18 Apr 1913, unanimous support; main parts Feb 1914; complete 1 May): concentration of five armies northeast for attack "with all forces assembled." 1st–5th armies along frontier (Fifth on left with broad front to Hirson); Fourth in reserve. Specific instructions for attacks N/S of Metz-Thionville or into Belgium if Germans violated. Secret annex for British "Army W." Not a blueprint for operations; Joffre retained latitude and concealed details. French intel assessed Germans would use reserves like active but concluded insufficient corps for deep west-of-Meuse drive simultaneously with strong central Belgium force. Gallieni (Fifth Army commander until Apr 1914 retirement) and successor Lanrezac raised pre-war doubts about Fifth Army strength/mission for Belgian advance and force maldistribution (three-quarters south of Verdun).

1913 large-units regulations (presidential decree) formalized division: government fixes political objectives and distributes resources; C-in-C conducts strategy and operations. Confirmed by Messimy and Joffre August 1914. Overall, transformation enabled better performance than 1870 (Marne) but left army oriented to short, mobile, offensive war with doctrinal and materiel weaknesses for the prolonged conflict that followed. **British Tactical Learning Curve 1916–18 (Griffith, 1994 ingest):** [[sources/griffith-battle-tactics-of-the-western-front-1994]] supplies the detailed British counterpart to Lupfer’s German analysis. Core thesis: the BEF executed a sustained, army-wide tactical evolution from mid-1916 that produced modern platoon-level all-arms tactics (SS 143 Feb 1917 as a “stormtrooper’s handbook” applied to the entire force, not specialists), sophisticated artillery (shift from destructive to neutralising/predicted fire, mature creeping barrages, counter-battery via survey/air/flash-sound ranging), and flexible formations for both set-piece and mobile operations. 

Key claims grounded in the five ranges:
- Pre-Somme the BEF already grasped core stormtroop ideas theoretically (infiltration, forward automatics, directive command, smoke/grenades); Somme turned an inexperienced force into a battle-hardened one via hard lessons on hugging the barrage and re-emphasising rifle/bayonet over the “cult of the bomb.”
- 1917 set-pieces (Arras, Messines, bite-and-hold phases at Third Ypres) demonstrated mature methods when weather permitted; Haig’s switch from Gough to Plumer reflected recognition of methodical “siege” approach superiority.
- Training schools formed a “university of higher tactics”; Partridge’s GHQ printing machine (SS series, iterative manuals from 1916) and post-battle analysis disseminated lessons rapidly despite high turnover.
- By late 1917 the BEF could guarantee success against strong defences with careful all-arms preparation. Hundred Days showed the system working in semi-open conditions (deep penetrations feeding themselves; local initiative/soft-spot exploitation; choice of waves/worms/blobs/diamonds).
- Historiographical corrective: the “charmed circle” of German stormtroopers/colonials/tanks is illusory; tactical innovation was played by the whole BEF, including “woolly old cavalry generals.” German 1918 gains were often against unprepared straw men; British methods were frequently ahead or parallel (e.g., rejection of grenade-centric assault in favour of rifle + barrage + CB).

**1917 French "strategy of decisive battle" (Doughty Ch. 7, Range 5):** After Joffre's relief (Dec 1916 political crisis post-Somme/Romania), Nivelle (Verdun hero) promoted "formula": massed depth artillery + single continuous thrust (24-48 hrs to enemy guns) + lateral exploitation for decisive battle vs reserves. "We now have the formula." Secured British relief + Apr timing at London conference; temporary unity at Calais. German Hindenburg withdrawal (Feb-Mar) strengthened Aisne but Nivelle adapted (added Pétain Central AG auxiliary attack). Loose security lost surprise; Micheler, Pétain, Painlevé voiced doubts ("waters of Lake Geneva... Sahara Desert"). Compiègne 6 Apr compromise: proceed but halt if no early rupture. Execution 16-17 Apr: 5,350 guns, 11M+ rds, but weather/air/reverse slopes/logistics failed prep. II Colonial fixed by intact MGs on northern slope of Chemin des Dames; no breakthrough. By 20 Apr failure clear; shifted to limited ops. 16-25 Apr: ~134k French cas (30k killed). Morale collapse. Nivelle relief process; Pétain chief of staff 29 Apr. See full [[events/nivelle-offensive-1917]] and source claims. `produced:` command change and doctrinal shift; `contributed_to:` 1917 mutinies context.

## The tactical problem of the Western Front (from Keegan)

The Somme analysis page sets out the core dynamic in full; in brief, Keegan frames 1916 as a
**contest of competing technologies** — British artillery (shells) against German defensive
engineering (deep dug-outs) and the **machine gun**. The week-long bombardment failed to cut the
wire or kill the entombed garrisons, and the battle reduced to **walking infantry against automatic
fire**, with ~60,000 British casualties in a single day. This is the experiential baseline against
which the war's later tactical innovations (the creeping barrage perfected, infiltration tactics,
combined-arms with tanks and aircraft) must be measured — innovations this hub will cover as the
sources arrive.

**Naval and Pacific 1914 (Strachan Range 07, exclusive cache grounding):** Distant blockade adopted after close-blockade plans foundered on submarine/mine/torpedo realities. Heligoland Bight (28 Aug) — British victory (three light cruisers + one TB sunk) marred by coordination failures and gunnery defects; established moral edge at sea. German raids: Yarmouth (3 Nov), Scarborough/Hartlepool (16 Dec) — successful bombardment but Hipper escaped British trap (Room 40 warning incomplete, ambiguous signals, weather, command lapses). Von Spee’s East Asiatic Squadron cruise: *Emden* detached for Indian Ocean raiding; Coronel (1 Nov) defeated Cradock and forced detachment of three British battle cruisers from North Sea; Falklands (8 Dec) destroyed the squadron. Japan entered (23 Aug) to seize Tsingtao and German Pacific holdings (Kato policy); Tsingtao fell 7 Nov after methodical siege; NZ took Samoa (30 Aug); Australia took German New Guinea (11–15 Sept); Japan occupied Micronesia (early Oct). **1914 Economic and Strategic Context (Strachan Range 10):** The 1914 campaigns exposed the collapse of pre-war assumptions. Shell shortages hit all powers because battlefield consumption of quick-firer artillery vastly exceeded every pre-war model; "the shells crisis of the autumn of 1914 was emphatically not a manifestation of a belief that the war would be short." France (post-Marne) first: Joffre warned rear "exhausted... impossible to continue fighting for lack of munitions within fifteen days"; reserves of 75 mm shells slumped from 530k to 33k by 10 Sep. Germany at Ypres limited to 5 rds/gun/day in places; Britain 9 shells/gun; Russia ~7 days supply. Pre-war stocks (France ~1,400 rds/75 mm gun; Germany 750–987; Britain 1,500; Russia 1,000–1,500 target) proved inadequate for positional rates. The "short-war illusion" (3–9 months official; public "back before the leaves fell") persisted as "a series of short episodes" but material reality forced adaptation. Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE.

**1915–1916: Search for Alternatives and Adoption of Attrition (Doughty, Range 4):** After 1915 offensives (Artois, Champagne) failed to achieve rupture against German second defensive belts, Joffre and allies formalized attrition at the Chantilly conference (6–8 Dec 1915). Joffre's "Plan of Action" placed primary emphasis on principal theaters (Franco-British, Italian, Russian) with coordinated offensives "as soon as possible after March 1916"; "attrition of the enemy should be pursued with partial and local offensives, above all by the powers that still have abundant resources in men." France would "collaborate in this attrition as much as its resources in men will permit" while Britain bore the main burden on the Western Front. Joffre still hoped for "strategic rupture" after the enemy was worn down.

Parallel to Western Front focus, France pursued (and was pulled into) peripheral operations. Initial Dardanelles/Gallipoli commitment (Feb–1915 landings at Kum Kale/Cape Helles; heavy early casualties) shifted after Bulgarian entry (Sep 1915) to Salonika to aid Serbia. Joffre consistently opposed large diversions ("dispersion of our efforts will be true folly"; "supporting an excessively large expeditionary corps in the Near East will result in such a consumption of our resources in men and munitions that it will render uncertain the possibilities of decisive victory in the principal theater"). Political leaders (Briand, Millerand, later Gallieni) drove commitment despite military objections. Limited Allied force (French 156th, 57th, 122nd Divisions under Sarrail + British) advanced toward Krivolak/Strumica Station and the Tcherna but withdrew to fortified Salonika perimeter by mid-Dec 1915 after Serbian collapse. Salonika became a "bluff of limited duration" to fix Bulgarian forces and influence Romania/Greece; Joffre later (1916) ordered Sarrail to maintain threat of offensive but not to weaken Western Front. No significant link-up with Romanians materialized; Romania entered late (Aug 1916), was overrun, and Salonika remained largely static ("internment camp" per Germans). See [[events/battle-of-verdun-1916]] and [[events/battle-of-the-somme-1916]].

French doctrine shifted explicitly to the "methodical battle." GQG directive 26 Jan 1916: corps/army commanders must identify successive objectives and conduct operations "by a single leader who controls the means [of attacking] and [is] aware of the objective being pursued." Advance "step by step" with "powerful, slow, methodical, repeated" attacks: artillery preparation, infantry assault, forward displacement of batteries, repeat. Joffre abandoned hopes for single-thrust breakthrough; "one does not fight with men against materiel." Foch and Fayolle stressed artillery destruction before infantry advance in small groups in depth; "strict economy" of manpower. Pétain (initially training large units) emphasized depth, rapid counterattacks, and later limited offensives. See [[concepts/methodical-battle]] and [[processes/attrition-warfare-wwi]].

**Verdun 1916 (Doughty Ch. 6):** Falkenhayn chose Verdun to force France to "impale itself on Germany's sword" and "bleed to death" the French army at a point it would never abandon, without seeking decisive territorial results. French intelligence was accurate (Crown Prince directing; heavy concentration) but Joffre viewed it as a diversion. Defenses had been weakened by Aug 1915 policy treating forts as non-independent and stripping guns/artillery; autumn Champagne offensive drained labor. German attack opened 21 Feb with massive bombardment; rapid gains; Fort Douaumont fell 25 Feb amid command confusion and Woëvre withdrawal. Joffre sent Castelnau then assigned Pétain/Second Army (mission initially limited to rally if forced west of Meuse and prevent crossing). After Briand's intervention, hold east bank "no matter what the cost."

Pétain organized noria rotation (divisions relieved before combat effectiveness collapsed; ultimately ~68–78 divisions rotated), defense in depth (advanced line 300–500 m forward of principal resistance), daily artillery fire plans, and logistics via Sacred Way (Bar-le-Duc–Verdun road: 1,700 trucks/day peak, vehicle every 25 seconds; narrow-gauge rail). West bank fighting (Hill 304, Mort Homme) March–April; east bank pressure continued. Political discontent led to Gallieni's critical report (Mar), Roques as replacement minister, removal of Dubail and de Langle; Pétain promoted to Central Army Group (May), Nivelle to Second Army at Verdun. Pétain pressed for Somme relief; Joffre restricted reserves to preserve Somme striking power and demanded aggressive counterattacks. Germans took Fort Vaux (June), Fleury; pushed salient toward Souville. Nivelle's "Comrades, they will not pass!"

With Somme drawing German resources, French counteroffensives succeeded: Nivelle/Mangin recaptured Douaumont (24 Oct) and Vaux (2 Nov) with concentrated heavy artillery (incl. 370/400-mm), short intense prep, and rolling barrages (75-mm 50 m bounds/2 min; infantry 75 m behind). December push drove Germans back to 7.5 km from citadel. French claimed ~377k casualties (16% killed, 56% wounded, 28% missing); heaviest in first 45 days and May–June. See dedicated [[events/battle-of-verdun-1916]].

**Somme 1916 – French Sector (Doughty Ch. 6):** Joffre selected Somme to enable combined Franco-British attack (boundary between armies) and shift main burden to British while France rebuilt. Haig resisted Joffre's desire for British preliminary large offensives of attrition (preferring raids/bombardments). Feb 14 agreement: decisive attack on Somme with French on both banks, preceded by partial attack; target ~1 July. Verdun forced reduction of French commitment (from planned 39+ divisions to ~11 in Sixth Army under Fayolle/Foch). Mission of Sixth Army: "support" the British; XX Corps north bank (cover British right), I Colonial and XXXV south.

French applied methodical battle more systematically than British massed waves. Superior heavy artillery (552 pieces vs British 467 for main attack). 1 July: preparatory fire from 24 June; XX Corps and I Colonial seized all first-day objectives (1.5–2 km advance; 4,000 prisoners initially, 8k by day 3); British gains only on their right. Later: I Colonial to Maisonette (8.5 km by 9 July); priority remained assisting British north, limiting exploitation. Joffre visited 3 July, raged at Haig's shift of weight south ("I will not approve"), relented ("an English battle"). French continued attacks (Sept with 5 corps in Sixth Army) but fragmented and limited by weather/mud; ended Nov. French ~202.5k casualties (heaviest July 49.8k, Sept 76k). British official: 419k. Joffre's hope for vigorous coordinated offensive never fully realized; British "small, distinct attacks" drew French criticism ("infantile" tactics per Fayolle; "for amateurs by amateurs"). See [[events/battle-of-the-somme-1916]] and hub analysis.

**Strategic/Political Outcome (end 1916 per Doughty):** Despite optimism after Brusilov and Romanian entry (Aug 1916), year closed with Romanian collapse, Salonika stasis, Isonzo stalemate, and Austrians no longer "tottering." French suffered fewer deaths in 1916 (350k) than 1914 (454k) or 1915 (391k) despite Verdun + Somme. Nivelle's quick, artillery-heavy "thrusts" at Verdun (light casualties for forts) contrasted sharply with Somme's "slow, repetitive battering-ram attacks" and heavy cost, eroding political confidence in Joffre. France insisted on keeping Salonika force but accepted that "the Balkan theater has lost its importance." The "pyrrhic" character of eventual victory was already evident in the mortgaged human and material capital of 1916.


On finance: the gold standard fragmented. Britain stayed effectively on it (Empire gold, US peg); reserves rose overall. Germany suspended convertibility and created Darlehenskassen for liquidity; Austria-Hungary (pre-weakened) suspended its Bank act with extended moratoriums; France's sweeping moratoriums "carried an immense burden." Explosive note/credit expansion everywhere. On 13 Aug Germany stood up the Kriegsrohstoffsamt (KRA) under Rathenau for central raw-materials allocation ("bewitching in its suddenness"). France created regional structures and labour-return schemes despite losing its NE industrial base (74% coal, 81% pig iron). By 1916 shell output had surged dramatically (Russia 15x monthly), but heavy artillery/rifles/transport bottlenecks persisted. These realities fixed the transition from mobile 1914 warfare to industrial attrition. See full Range 10 on source page; new [[processes/war-financing-wwi]] and [[processes/industrial-mobilization-wwi]]; [[concepts/short-war-illusion]].

**Range 04 (Ch. 3, Marne through Aisne/Yser):** The Marne was a French victory achieved by manoeuvre and fighting. Joffre maintained resolution and purged ~1/3 of higher command by competence (not politics); French 75 mm excelled in defence ("far more effective against advancing infantry than any small-arms or machine-gun fire"). German right wing crippled by intel failure (cavalry deficient; Kluck "seeing things as they wanted them to be"), comms (info 24 hrs old; directives not orders), logistics (railheads 136–168 km back; 60% lorries broken; 84k horses per army needing 2M lbs fodder/day), and attrition (~265k losses by 6 Sep + corps detached to East Prussia/Maubeuge). French rail move brought 20 inf + 3 cav divisions west; on 6 Sep confronted 41 allied vs initial 24.5 German right. Kluck's SE turn + later shift of corps to Ourcq opened gap exploited by BEF. Hentsch authorised retreat after Bülow pulled back. Falkenhayn succeeded Moltke 14 Sep but pursued envelopment with same constraints. "Race" ended with Belgian Yser inundation (27 Oct) and First Ypres exhaustion (German ~80k; BEF 54k of 90k total losses in salient). By 25 Nov Falkenhayn ordered defensive positions: "trench warfare... a matter of expedience, not a foundation for strategy." **Eastern Front continuation (Strachan Range 06, Ch. 4 Serbia/Galicia/Poland):** After Tannenberg Samsonov defeat, Rennenkampf "had barely moved, advancing with excessive deliberation, and doing so in the wrong direction"; "the 1st army had fallen back 48 kilometres. Cer plateau 15-20 Aug: 5th AH crossed Drina, "toiled uphill in soaring temperatures... outstripped their supplies"; forced back, divisions lost 1/3 rifle strength. 2nd army diverted. Second offensives (Sep) produced bridgehead stalemate and positional war on Drina. "Austria-Hungary’s bid to use military might to re-establish its Balkan pre-eminence had been thwarted." See [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/serbia-campaign-1914]].

"In the east 1.5 German divisions occupied a front that would have been held by five divisions in the west." Exhaustion, not trenches, produced stasis. Independent commands yielded rival plans, not unity.

**Colonial and African campaigns (Strachan Range 08 exclusively):** The war was global from its first hours and lasted beyond Europe. "As much from its outset as beyond its formal conclusion, therefore, the First World War was far more than just a European conflict." "in 1914 the flow of major war was the reverse of that in the eighteenth century—from Europe to the colonies, rather than vice versa." "For the Great War in Africa... was fought principally by the Africans themselves. In all, somewhere over 2 million Africans served in the First World War as soldiers or labourers, and upwards of 200,000 of them died or were killed in action." "never before in the history of Africa had manpower been mobilized on such a scale." "Disease, not battle, disabled armies in Africa." "The difficulties of supply, rather than the experiences of battle, did most to disseminate the impact of the Great War throughout the African continent." "a company with its attendant porters mustered about 300 men and on the tracks of the equatorial rain forests of Central Africa constituted a column 1,500 to 2,000 yards long." "Fighting in Africa was therefore predominantly an infantry affair, the machine-gun being the heaviest and most significant weapon regularly deployed." "Although fought between European powers for objectives that were also European, the African campaigns of the First World War bore more relationship to the nineteenth-century campaigns of colonial conquest than they did to the Great War itself." "relatively the impact of the war on the dark continent was as great as that on Europe, that few black families were unaffected."

- **Togoland:** First British round 12 Aug 1914 by Alhaji Grunshi (WAFF). Improvised British attack seized Lome and Kamina wireless; Germans destroyed station and surrendered 25 Aug. "its principal strategic objective was accomplished in its first month."

Porter crises (2–3 per soldier); French/British coordination failures (Brazzaville/Duala conferences produced optimistic plans never matched on ground). Jaunde evacuated pre-fall; 15,000+ (6,000 askaris) to Spanish Muni; Mora surrendered 18 Feb undefeated.
- **South-West Africa (1914–Jul 1915):** Union Afrikaner rebellion (Maritz/De Wet/Beyers/Kemp). Botha invasion used mounted Boer envelopments and speed. Germans ~5,000 (scattered, cohesion issues, fodder famine). "The South-West African campaign was characterized by a maximum of movement and a minimum of casualties." Surrender 9 Jul 1915 (4,740 men) without climactic battle. "By biding his time in 1915, Botha laid the foundations for South Africa’s own brand of colonialism in 1918."
- **East Africa (1914–25 Nov 1918):** Lettow vs Schnee tension (coast neutrality). Tanga (2–5 Nov 1914): British IEF B (Aitken) landed exhausted after long sea voyage; bush, failed naval support, left brigade collapse; Lettow's reserves achieved local envelopment. "On 25 November 1918... at Abercorn in Northern Rhodesia Colonel Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck surrendered, the last German commander of the war to do so."

## French Strategy of “Healing” and Defense, Late 1917 + Response to German 1918 Offensives (Doughty Range 6)

From [[sources/doughty-pyrrhic-victory-2005]] (Ch. 8–9 exclusively, cache range_6_10738-13855.txt). After Nivelle's failure and mutinies (54 divisions affected Apr–Jun 1917), Pétain (Chief of GS 29 Apr; C-in-C 17 May) shifted France to defensive strategy of "healing" + limited offensives. Directive No. 1 (19 May): equilibrium made breakthrough impossible; limited attacks for min losses + attrition via massed arty on forward positions + short infantry advances then consolidate (not continuous battle). Rearmament (aircraft, 155mm arty, FT-17 tanks to 3,500, chemicals) + centers of resistance reforms. Manpower crisis forced conservation of lives (20–25 divs projected suppressed by end-1918). (Ch. 8, 355-369)

Limited ops: Passchendaele support (First/Anthoine); Verdun 20 Aug (Second/Guillaumat: rapid limited gains, e.g. 31st Div objectives with ~1.1k cas). Malmaison (23–26 Oct, Sixth/Maistre): model success — 6km max advance, Chemin des Dames heights recaptured, 11k POW/200 cannon for ~11.8k total French cas ("less than one-tenth" Nivelle's price for less gain). (Ch. 8, 378-389)

Russia collapse forced defensive priority (23 Oct Pétain to Haig: form 40+ div reserves; offensive plan secondary). Italy Caporetto: 4–6 French divs sent. Clemenceau (16 Nov) gov: "War. Nothing but war"; initially backed Pétain. (Ch. 8, 390-404)

1918: Pétain vs Foch clash (Foch: offensive spirit + counteroffensives for disengagement; Pétain: "period of waiting will last throughout 1918"; "battle of 1918 will be defensive... absence of means imposes this"). Doctrine Dec 1917 (Directive No. 4): position of resistance (bulk rear), islands/centers forward, reserves for counterattack; "economize our forces"; yield terrain if needed but tenacious "all-out defense." British/Americans often expected continuous lines. (Ch. 9, 408-428)

Michael (21 Mar): Pétain rushed reserves (Third + First Armies + Fayolle); mixed command; first priority prevent Paris drive. Doullens (26 Mar): Foch "coordinating" powers. Beauvais (3 Apr): "strategic direction." Lys (9 Apr): French northern moves; Kemmel loss criticized; British complaints over effort despite French front extension. Blücher (27 May Chemin des Dames): Duchêne violation of doctrine (main resistance on crest) enabled rapid German breakthrough to Marne (55km); 37 divs absorbed. June 9 (Noyon-Montdidier): better prep; early reserves + Mangin counter halted advance. French casualties modest due to husbanding. Americans stiffened morale. Ongoing Foch-Pétain friction; Clemenceau enforced Foch authority. By late Jun, Foch ascendant for counteroffensives. (Ch. 9, 429-470)

`produced:` restoration of French army post-mutinies; Malmaison exemplar; defense-in-depth + reserves that absorbed 1918 shocks with lower relative losses. `contributed_to:` Foch unity steps; holding until US readiness enabled Allied 1918 offensive. `caused_by:` mutinies + manpower + Russian collapse (not command failure). ## 1918 German Offensive Doctrine and Tactical Completion (Lupfer)

From [[sources/lupfer-dynamics-of-doctrine-1981]] (Ch. 2 completion + Ch. 3), when strategic conditions favored German offensive in spring 1918 on the [[places/western-front]], the Germans developed offensive doctrine "which incorporated many of the virtues of their elastic defense-in-depth" ([[concepts/elastic-defense-in-depth]]) "and which avoided several of the pitfalls into which the Allied tactical doctrine had fallen."

"The new offensive doctrine described the complementary use of firepower and maneuver, using infantry forces which avoided strongpoints and plunged deeply into enemy positions, aided by highly efficient and effective artillery fire." (See [[concepts/infiltration-tactics-1918]].)

Key: "These doctrinal changes were not invented by one mastermind. Rather, they were the product of a thorough collective effort which enlisted the advice and experience of every possible source." Disseminated "through a remarkable training program." The procedure recognized "that conditions and unique situations would always affect the application of the tactical doctrine."

Generalizations (Ch. 3): Germans treated doctrine inductively and tentatively. "The process of deriving the doctrine was inductive, and the application was in the same inductive spirit." "Evidence from the battlefield was more respected than the doctrine." "For the Germans all tactical solutions were tentative." Allied tendency to rigid deductive formulas contrasted with German "healthy curiosity and mental flexibility." "There is no panacea. A formula is harmful. Everything must be applied according to the situation." (Rupprecht, quoted in Lupfer).

Corporate direction by OHL ([[ludendorff]], [[hindenburg]]): talent used efficiently (e.g. Capt. Geyer); principles "sufficiently general ... but sufficiently precise ... Their flexibility was their strength."

This completed the German tactical evolution on the Western Front 1914–18 and directly informs analysis of Operation Michael and subsequent 1918 offensives.

## 1918: Foch’s Strategy of Opportunism and the Final Allied Counteroffensives (Doughty, Ch. 10 + Conclusion)

**Source grounding: exclusive range cache for Ch. 10 "A Strategy of Opportunism" (book pp. 461–507) and Conclusion "The 'Misery' of Victory" (pp. 508–518). All claims, quotes, and analysis here drawn solely from that text. Links only to established taxonomy.**

Foch’s most important service came after appointment as supreme Allied commander. He brought talent for French-British cooperation and "opportunism" (B. H. Liddell Hart characterization, akin to Gambetta’s political "art of the possible"). "Foch recognized that military advances could occur only when the opportunity for action appeared. As a strategist, Foch revealed himself to be a man who lacked any grand strategic theories but who had the vision, energy, and knack for seizing the opportunity for action when it appeared." (A Strategy of Opportunism l 461).

Unity of command enabled action. Doullens 26 Mar 1918: authority to "coordinate the actions of allied armies on the Western Front". Beauvais 3 Apr: "strategic direction of military operations". 17 Apr: title "general-in-chief of the allied armies in France". 26 Jun: War Committee "compelled Pétain to obey his directives". Powers restricted (relied on persuasion) but "gained sufficient authority between March and July to exert considerable influence over allied strategy and operations. This expanded authority enabled him to seize the initiative from the Germans in July 1918 and to drive them out of France with a series of relentless attacks." (l 461-462).

Operational methods: Learned 1915 Artois/Champagne "a breakthrough was impossible"; Somme 1916 and Passchendaele 1917 "a series of massive blows against the same point on the front would result only in high casualties." Recognized importance of destroying enemy reserves and that Pétain’s limited offensives "could inflict high casualties on the enemy while keeping friendly casualties low" but "promised no quick end to the war." Foch’s staff called Pétain the "great grub." Foch added twist: "a rapid series of blows to keep the Germans off balance and prevent them from shifting reserves." Illustrated "by punching with his right fist, then his left, and then again with his right and following these blows with a vigorous kick." (l 462).

Mid-June 1918 Foch still expected final campaign in 1919. Sequenced: (1) halt coming German offensive; (2) launch counteroffensive; (3) "advancing on all allied fronts with a series of relentless attacks"; (4) "ending the war with a decisive kick, a massive general offensive." "Although Foch did not adopt an offensive strategy immediately after he acquired his new power, he began the transition from a defensive to an offensive strategy the moment the opportunity appeared. In doing so, he enabled allied armies to fight the final, decisive campaign in 1918, not 1919." (l 462-463).

**Priority to halting German 5th drive (July).** 1 Jul memo to Haig/Pétain: hold Franco-British front; enemy 40 km Abbeville or 60 km Paris "could decisively affect the outcome of the war by splitting the French and British armies or threatening Paris." Concentrate reserves between Arras and Château-Thierry. Pétain opposed citing "extreme fatigue" of troops and British "density" advantage. Foch shifted Army Detachment of the North (six divisions) Flanders to French zone; transferred battered British IX Corps back to British zone. By late June procedures agreed for cross-zone dispatch. (l 463-464).

After halt, counterattack. Germans vulnerable in Marne and Somme salients (advanced beyond logistics, extended fronts). Not small op: prepare ~30 divisions (12 French, 12 American, 7-8 British). Directed Pétain "augment the offensive value of troops called to take part in the [coming] battle." Pétain published Directive No. 5 (12 Jul): "Henceforth the armies should envisage the resumption of the offensive. Commanders at all echelons will prepare for this; they will focus resolutely on using simple, audacious, and rapid procedures of attack. The soldier will be trained in the same sense and his offensive spirit developed to the maximum." Also covered training staffs/troops, surprise, exploitation. "After four years in the trenches and a year of preparing only for the defense, the French army required much coaching before it could conduct a large offensive operation." (l 464-465).

Foch anticipated limited attacks on each front (Western, Italian, Balkan) after more US arrivals to gain ascendancy and keep enemy off balance; strong general offensive possibly Sep 1918, decisive 1919. Concern over British: told Pershing liaison "When you and we go forward, we will pull the British along with us. If we are not near enough to do that we run the risk of leaving them behind. Guide left, then, is the command." (l 464).

**Marne salient counteroffensive (18 Jul).** Intelligence indicated twin-pronged German attack both sides Reims (initially east, later Marne crossing SW). Foch/Pétain cautious, feared feint to Paris/Abbeville. By 7 Jul expected formidable Champagne offensive 9-10 Jul as diversion. Reserves shifted; 7-9 Jul five infantry divisions into sector east Château-Thierry. Central Army Group (Maistre) with Sixth, Fifth, Fourth. Confirmed 10-12 Jul large offensive ~14 Jul. Pétain reserves poised; superiority artillery/munitions/heavy+light tanks/aviation. "With the Germans having lost operational and tactical surprise and with Pétain’s reserves poised for action, the situation favored the allies." (l 465-466).

Counter vs Marne salient apparent early (GQG staff studying shoulders 30 May). 14 Jun Foch directed Pétain bombard Soissons rail center + consider limited offensive toward Soissons to bring cannon closer. Tenth Army (Mangin from 16 Jun after 11 Jun counter) 28 Jun attack toward Soissons: advanced several km, thousands prisoners; revealed disorganization/weakness but did not alert Germans to larger. 5 Jul Mangin proposed ambitious: "A large attack on Soissons... had an excellent chance of success and could conceivably result in the reduction of the Marne salient." Pétain enthusiastic: "Beyond a doubt this operation presents not only the best chance of success but also the opportunity for a fruitful exploitation; additionally, it constitutes the most effective parry to the imminent German offensive." Foch aware, concurred; suggested combine Tenth with Sixth (right) and Fifth strike eastern face. Initially unwilling commit reserves (Flanders concern). Changed 11 Jul (not diversion): released reserves N Oise, asked Haig 2+4 divs. Haig objected 15 Jul (only if massive to Paris). Foch reassured returnable. (l 466-468).

Two-phased battle: halt German then unleash Soissons. Pétain expected counter 18 Jul. Committed everything: Champagne reserve 38 inf (incl 4 Br +5 US) +6 cav but only 1 div behind entire front Argonne-Switzerland +1 Br on Paris front. "At a time when the situation called for decisive measures, Foch and Pétain bet everything on the outcome of this battle." (l 468).

15 Jul German assault uneven 0415-0530: east Reims Fourth Army (40 km front) advanced 2-3 km then halted by noon, heavy casualties; later attacks no gains. SW Marne Fifth Army made progress (too many men on river vulnerable to arty); drove out main position; only 39th Fr + US 3rd held near Château-Thierry. Pétain 0930 suspended Tenth prep, diverted forces for strong counter vs bridgehead. Foch noon intervened: do not halt Tenth; obtain more (probably Br) but "he did not want them taken from Tenth Army." 1645 Pétain requested US 2nd from Tenth (delay Mangin 1 day); later canceled. Hard fighting; Germans advanced 5 km 35 km front but 17 Jul French counterattacks halted. 16 Jul Pétain letter to Foch on lack reserves behind Third Army (direct to Paris N Oise): "It is necessary to guarantee the solidity of our front north of the Oise." Foch refused weaken Tenth; diverted 2 Br (en route Fifth) to Third instead. "On the eve of the counteroffensive that would give the allies the initiative, Foch refused to let the tactical or operational demands of the battle subvert his strategic design and rejected all thoughts of diverting forces from Tenth Army. In contrast, Pétain’s pessimism colored his assessment... had Foch not intervened, he might have delayed, weakened, or canceled Tenth Army’s operation. In the final analysis, Foch’s optimism and his willingness to take a calculated risk earned him the credit for the subsequent victory." (l 468-470).

17 Jul final order for Foch’s counteroffensive (Tenth, Sixth, Ninth, Fifth, Fourth armies 18 Jul). Tenth main attack S Soissons (I, XX, XXX, XI corps; 10 div first line +6 second; cav corps reserve; 1,545 arty +301 tanks). US 1st/2nd in XX Corps (Foch’s old unit). Pershing memoir: "The [XX] corps, which was four-fifths American, had the honor of being the spearhead of the thrust against this vulnerable flank of the salient, an honor which it gallantly sustained." (l 470-471).

18 Jul 0435 Tenth attacked without arty prep (preceded tanks + dense rolling barrage). Quick advance. US 2nd 8 km (deepest first day any unit); US 1st interdicted Buzancy Château-Thierry-Soissons route, threatened entire salient position. By end day: Tenth 10,000 prisoners +200 cannon; Sixth 2,000+50. GQG colonel: "This is the greatest day since the Marne." American liaison: GQG staff celebrated pretending Ludendorff/Crown Prince complaints; "Without the Americans this would never have been possible." Toast with champagne. (Later US 1st Div monument Buzancy marks where "allies seized the initiative on July 18 and began their march toward victory.") Other Fr armies small gains; 1145 German commander ordered Marne bridgehead evac. Most troops across in two nights; Fr cleared S bank 20 Jul. Tenth/Sixth continued pressure W face; Château-Thierry liberated 21 Jul. By 22 Jul Germans strengthened, stiff resistance. (l 471-472).

Foch contended with Haig demands return 4 Br divs (moved S to augment Pétain reserves). Foch 15 Jul promised return if Br front threatened. After XXII Corps arrival, Foch placed two behind Third (Paris security), two to Fifth. 18 Jul morning Haig (expected little of Fr counter; feared Ypres attack) demanded return in writing (oral: remain if needed exploit success). Foch: operation near Marne "prevented the enemy from making a large attack on the British front 'for the moment'"; levy from quiet front if needed. Br divs eventually contributed to pushing Germans out salient. "Haig’s insistence on the return of the divisions on the crucial first day of the counterattack highlights his failure to comprehend the changing strategic situation. Neither Haig nor Pétain recognized as fully as Foch the strategic opportunity opening before the allies." (l 472-473).

Foch urged strengthen Tenth; Germans considered options. Crown Prince Wilhelm 24 Jul ordered withdrawal (cost excessive casualties); Ludendorff canceled (favored attack W Soissons); objections forced accept. Germans withdrew skillfully to strong Aisne-Vesle base by 3 Aug; Fr reached Vesle then halted. (l 473-474).

Foch counteroffensive "proved to be one of the most important operations of the war. It drove the Germans from the Marne salient, lowered their morale, raised the morale of allied troops, and gave the allies the initiative." Shortened front 45 km, removed Paris threat. Pétain forces captured 29,000 Germans, 612 cannon, 221 Minenwerfer, 3,330 MG. Casualties Tenth/Sixth/Ninth/Fifth: 95,165. 5 Aug Clemenceau informed Foch appointment Marshal of France. Decree: "Paris disengaged, Soissons and Château-Thierry reconquered by force, more than 200 villages freed... the high hopes proclaimed by the enemy before his attack shattered, the glorious allied armies advanced with a single victorious will from the edge of the Marne to the banks of the Aisne: those are the results of a maneuver admirably conceived by the high command and superbly executed by incomparable leaders." (l 473).

Failure German spring + success counter began German army disintegration (influenza epidemic; soldiers abandoning positions). Despite Rupprecht reports worsening morale/discipline, Ludendorff told Kaiser: "During the world war, so far I have had to withdraw my troops five times, only to beat the enemy in the end after all. Why should I not bring this off a sixth time?" Pétain pleased progress but foresaw difficulties (shortage 120,000 infantry; 103 divs 71 front/32 reserve; only 1 fresh div 31 Jul): "We are at the limit of our effort." (l 474).

**July 24 strategic shift.** Foch met Pétain/Haig/Pershing; Weygand read long memo: "The moment has come to abandon the general defensive attitude imposed upon us until now by [our] numerical inferiority and to pass to the offensive." Still expected final 1919 but "called for quick, successive blows to keep the initiative on the side of the allies and establish the best conditions for the final victorious campaign." First: attacks vs three large salients (Marne under way; Montdidier-Amiens; St.-Mihiel "as soon as they had the 'necessary means'"). Clearing salients secure rail lines indispensable later. Next: clear mining region N France, drive enemy Dunkirk/Calais vicinity. "By leaving only brief intervals between operations, the allies could prevent the Germans from using their reserves in counterstrokes and from rebuilding their depleted units." Became basis next month. (l 474-475).

**Montdidier-Amiens (8 Aug).** Idea fermented in Foch mind; 20 May told Pétain/Haig prepare Fr-Br limited offensive shoulders salient to "disengage" Paris-Amiens rail. Temporarily abandoned after 27 May Chemin des Dames but returned when Haig (seeking XXII Corps release) 17 Jul identified as most fruitful. 20 Jul Foch proposed Br Fourth + Fr First commanders meet details. 28 Jul to Haig: "disengage Amiens and the Paris-Amiens railway" and "drive back" enemy front of Amiens. To increase success/unity: placed Fr First under Haig +4 divs. Urged hasten possibly catch unprepared; Haig advanced date 10 to 8 Aug; formal order Rawlinson/Debeney. (l 475-476).

**Foch-Pétain doctrinal clash late Oct.** 25 Oct Foch wrote Pétain criticizing successive objectives (more static than mobile war). "Having lost patience with Pétain’s slow, methodical advance": "Troops launched into an attack should know only their direction of attack. In this direction they should go as far as they can, attacking and maneuvering [around] the enemy who resist without any concern for alignment, the units most advanced working to the advantage of those momentarily halted. They operate in this manner not according to lines established beforehand based on terrain, but according to the enemy." (l 502-503).

Pétain responded lecturing (without "methodical battle" term): operation "successive objectives" + "main direction" "zone of action." Procedures usable "fortified zone" or "open terrain." "series of efforts" ensured "close liaison between artillery and infantry and guaranteed a 'continuity of effort' far better than 'simple' directions of attack." "I believe that the method which consists of defining an offensive mission by successive objectives with a principal objective and directions of exploitation responds to the real character of the struggle. The enemy is undoubtedly very exhausted, but his resistance rests on extensive echelons in depth and a multitude of machine guns. Consequently, he yields only to coordinated attacks, relying on great amounts of firepower and the intervention of tanks, and when he yields, the exploitation always requires cannon. Under these conditions, every offensive still includes in some fashion a breakthrough phase in which successive objectives are imperative if one wants to ensure the precise cooperation of the different arms; and this breakthrough phase is followed by an exploitation phase, which can be successful only if one maneuvers across the successive lines or points to which enemy resistance clings." To show who commanded, sent note army-group commanders elaborating (Foch portrayed "conciliatory" but emphasized successive). "the final battles fought by the French bore a stronger resemblance to Pétain’s than Foch’s operational concept." (l 503-504).

**British criticism + French reality last weeks.** British official (Edmonds) "diminishing effort of the French armies" attributed "policy of leaving the fighting to the British and Americans." Fr held only 65 km "active front" 11 Nov 1918. Compared prisoners/weapons: "Which armies bore the brunt of the fighting in the last stage cannot, therefore, be doubted." (Colored by desire highlight Br contributions; but "no doubt that the French army’s effort and capability declined between March and September 1918 and that the French were on their last legs in the final two months of the war.") After magnificent spring defensive + counter, "French soldiers had little left to give; heavy casualties and mental and physical exhaustion finally took their toll." Week before 26-28 Sep offensives: GQG officer "The moral[e] of French troops is very good but they are physically tired as a result of constant combat for many weeks and, in the case of many divisions, for many months." Formal reports "splendid" morale "absolute" confidence victory but warned increasing fatigue/complaints. Month before armistice Col Laure (later Pétain biog/Operations Bureau book): "You have many of the characteristics which we had in the early part of the war—élan, impetuosity—you are willing to assault; alas, the great losses we sustained forced us to become more conservative—probably too conservative—and your élan carries us along." (l 504-505).

Massive equipment to Americans drained Fr combat power. Weapons US combat: Fr provided 3,532/4,194 arty, 227/289 tanks, 4,874/6,364 aircraft. 19 Aug Buat (Pétain COS) diary: order Foch provide Americans 24,000 horses "At times like this, you regret that the commander-in-chief of allied forces is French." Prevented transform "mechanical-warfare oriented army"; diverted at critical time (St-Mihiel/Meuse-Argonne prep). Pétain complained several times Foch "the French army did not have enough equipment 'to meet its own requirements.'" Final weeks "struggled to mount a powerful offensive on their own and required much assistance from the Americans and the British. Although they gamely organized an offensive into Lorraine, the chances of their achieving a significant victory were small." (l 505).

Broader: pre Mar 1918/Jul 18 Messimy privately "Without the American intervention, we definitely would have lost the war." Armistice day Gen Victor Duval (Ops Bureau Pétain HQ) to liaison: "let me say from my heart that we French, we will feel an eternal gratitude to Americans, for without your aid we would be Boche provinces instead of a free people. You came not one hour too soon. We were nearly finished... Take the all-important attack of July 18 [in which the allies gained the initiative]. Without the presence in that attack of your troops the attack would have been an utter impossibility—it would never have taken place. Your presence was absolutely essential." Month earlier "without you we would have been completely defeated this year." "Clearly, the Americans did not win the war, but without their assistance, as well as that of the British, the French probably would have collapsed amidst one of the German offensives in 1918 and certainly would not have seized the initiative from the Germans in mid-July." (l 505-506).

"A week is too long a time." 7 Nov 2015 German delegation (wireless asked Foch previous night) contact First Army advance guard. 8 Nov Compiegne forest N Paris Foch met, dictated terms. After departure told commanders: "The enemy, disorganized by our repeated attacks, is yielding on the entire front. It is urgent to maintain and accelerate our efforts. **Conclusion: The “Misery” of Victory.** News unleashed tumultuous celebration France. Paris crowds boulevards "La Marseillaise"; Place de la Concorde captured guns. Ripped crepe/mourning from Strasbourg statue, covered garlands/tricolor. Times correspondent: "Silence has fled, perhaps to Berlin... but certainly far from the Paris boulevards. There is no fraction of any second that is not filled with the deep cheers of men, the shrill cheers of women, the blowing of trumpets, the singing of songs... the spectacle is so extraordinary in relation to the occasion that one shrinks from the task of explaining the wild enthusiasm." Noted however "Practically every woman is in deep black." (l 508-509).

"France had repelled the invader and won a victory, but it had paid a terribly high price." ~1,400,000 killed/missing; another 4,000,000 wounded (800,000 severely/permanently maimed). Much N/NE France devastated (fighting + deliberate Ger destruction: cities/villages rubble; railways/roads/bridges/mines/wells destroyed; trenches/holes fertile fields; stumps/splinters forests/orchards; unexploded shells). "The specter of severe financial difficulties also cast a long shadow." Political leaders refused effective income tax most war; financed expedients (heavy borrowing, pay later). Wholesale prices quadrupled long before guns silent; "decades of currency stability were a thing of the past." Last phase: military complained lack discipline new conscripts (lost homes/communities; raised families no fathers; lacked character/resolve predecessors). "Much like these young recruits, France could never erase all the scars of the Great War." (l 509).

Most losses early. By end autumn 1915 offensive (15 months) >50% total casualties war. 1916 another 20% (primarily Verdun/Somme). 1917 lightest 10%. Not until Mar 1918 German attacks casualties rose; desperate Mar-Jun "threw everything... to halt the German offensive and suffered almost as many casualties as in 1917." After 18 Jul Marne counter + resumption, 1918 final toll 20% all Fr losses. (l 509-510).

"Throughout the ordeal the French sometimes wavered, but they never quit." Entered reluctantly grim determination drive Germans out + recover Alsace-Lorraine. After repeated attacks failed drive out, "more primitive emotions of hatred and revenge, as well as a reluctance to cast aside the sacrifices already made, sustained their will to fight." Public support: hope early; waned early 1917 war-weariness/strikes; steadied US entry. "After so much effort, so much sacrifice, so many dead and wounded, French families continued to send their sons into the army." Share refusing serve declined 2.59% 1915, 1.08% 1916, 0.88% 1918. (l 510).

"Buttressed by strong public support, every ministry from Viviani to Clemenceau remained committed to fighting the war to the finish." Entered because leaders accept risk support Russia Balkan crisis; remained because refused peace favorable Germans. "With much of northern and northeastern France occupied by the enemy, the politicians had no bargaining chips for use in negotiations, and even those critical of the conduct of the war saw no viable alternative to continuing to fight." Only one ministry overturned vote Chamber (Painlevé Nov 1917) — scandal/incompetence not war support. "At the lowest point... when the army was drained by casualties and weakened by mutinies, Clemenceau’s rise to power and his famous words 'War. Nothing but War' clearly demonstrated France’s refusal to accept the disastrous effects of an unfavorable peace or of ignominious defeat." (l 510-511).

Ordinary soldiers: press early "eager to fight and to die"; later survival/indifferent. "More recent scholarship emphasizes a more nuanced portrait, one in which ordinary soldiers, though often weary and discouraged, remained faithful to the ideals of defending their country and doing their duty." Cynical overly optimistic/exaggerated reports; angry poorly organized op/leader incompetence; "refused to abandon the defense of their country or to allow the sacrifices of their friends to be rendered meaningless." Pity some enemy same deprivation/hardships; but hated "'Boches,' who were—in the words of one soldier—'the bastards who have made me suffer so much for such a long time.'" Anger reinforced resolve/resilience. "Though not eager to enter battle, the soldiers endured almost unimaginable demands in order to drive the Germans from their country." (l 511).

"grim quest for victory placed extraordinary requirements on France." Mobilized larger % population + larger losses vs major powers: 168/1,000 citizens mobilized +34 lost (Germany 154/30; Britain 125/16). Huge demands industrial base. "In essence, France, which began the war with an antiquated industrial base, a backward financial system, and the loss of most of its key natural resources and factories, became the arsenal of the Entente." Soldiers began without much heavy arty/basic (wire cutters/trench mortars); "hastily built factories... pour out desperately needed weapons and munitions, allowing the army to transform itself from the light, highly mobile force of August 1914 to the massively heavy one of November 1918." Sophistication: 52,000 aircraft (significantly higher Ger 48,000 Br 43,000). End war air strength exceeded any: 247 squadrons 3,222 aircraft Western Front. Production rates increasing; supplied weapons/munitions all allies at times; Americans most (more than three-quarters all tanks, field artillery, airplanes used by Doughboys France). "Before the war no one had dreamed that France could achieve such levels of production." (l 511-512).

"Strengthened by the ordinary soldier’s desire to defend his country and by the nation’s outpouring of weapons and materiel, the army proved remarkably resilient." Especially: halting withdrawal + offensive decisive Marne Aug-Sep 1914 ("one of the most remarkable feats in twentieth-century warfare and amply merits the sobriquet 'miracle.'"). Series attacks Somme late 1916 after disastrous 1915 Artois/Champagne + terrible 1916 Verdun losses: "exceptional tenacity." Costly but "soldiers continued to endure incredible demands until Nivelle’s foolish offensive in April 1917 pushed them to mutiny." Another extraordinary Mar 1918: Pétain threw many into gap Somme between Br/Fr. "By July 1918, however, the French were staggering with exhaustion, and only the support of the British and Americans kept them going." (l 512).

"With Germany prostrate and in chaos, the French celebrated their victory; they had survived the war and had retained their independence." Mourned losses, erected monuments; "great hopes for the future and expected the peace treaty to disarm Germany, reduce the threat on their frontier, and provide reparations for damages and costs." "Despite Clemenceau’s best efforts, the Versailles treaty failed to alter the fundamental strategic imbalance between France and Germany." "In essence France’s political influence was too small to convince its allies to dismantle Germany, and its army too weak to enforce a draconian peace and destroy Germany’s military power." "However much the French people may have wanted revenge or security, they could not achieve them alone." Germany larger pop + industrial capacity; "misgivings about the future eventually overshadowed the euphoria of November 1918." (l 512-513).

"With peace no longer certain, France’s vision of a future war with Germany bore a strong resemblance to the recent conflict, for no nation can endure such an ordeal and then ignore it." "Gone were notions of battle as a glorious or uplifting experience." Movies/radio/newspapers/books reminded terrible battles/enormous losses; battle-scarred veterans grisly tales. Military journals/doctrine "annihilating," "crushing," "irresistible." "Unlike Messimy in late July 1914, when he responded 'bravo' to Joffre’s affirmation of the army’s readiness, no one expected a future war to be glorious, and no one was ready to rush to war and relive the horrific experiences of 1914–1918." "Such attitudes were not necessarily defeatist or decadent and did not indicate rampant pacifism, but they ensured that caution ruled over all diplomatic and military actions and barred any possibility of an aggressive strategy in the opening days of a war." Joffre Aug 1914 disastrous demonstrated disadvantages offensive start war; "political and military leaders had no desire to incur futile losses again in a rash offensive." Especially after late 1930s Little Entente unravel (countries carved Austro-Hungarian), "saw no reason to launch an impetuous attack into Germany." Preferred "more careful strategy, one that would provide time to mobilize the nation’s resources and commit soldiers to prudent, thoroughly prepared operations." (l 513).

"Great War had demonstrated the necessity for the entire nation to participate in a war against the Germans, and the French, though dreading the enormous destructiveness of another great war, believed they had no choice but to endure another long war should Germany attack." Leaders chastised 1914 shortsighted (thousands lives). "With few voices of dissent, political and military leaders organized a military force, consisting primarily of reservists and conscripted soldiers, capable of waging a long, destructive war." Elaborate mobilization marshal entire nation resources match/surpass Great War production. Remembering Briey iron/Lille coal losses, moved protect vulnerable N/NE frontier resources. "These efforts contributed to the building of fortifications on the northeastern frontier not only to protect valuable resources but also to provide the nation time to mobilize its entire resources for a long war." "Political and military leaders thought it wise first to defend against the enemy’s initial attacks and then to respond with the completely mobilized power of the entire nation." (l 513-514).

"Demonstrated the importance of allies." "At the end of the long, deadly war no one believed that France alone could defeat Germany." Paris peace sought cement allies. US/Br guarantees empty; looked E Europe (Little Entente + arrangements) but "did not provide the strategic advantages that the prewar alliance with Russia or the wartime alliance with the Entente powers had conferred." Late 1930s tension "concluded that they had no choice but to repudiate all obligations to Czechoslovakia and follow the lead of London." "In essence their desire for a powerful ally outweighed their desire for an independent policy." (l 514).

Military leaders searched better operational methods. Knew devoted time/effort war develop better; recalled disastrous early hasty/inadequate prep horrendous casualties. Remembered Nivelle failure + Pétain limited success + "costly transition from the continuous to the methodical battle." "Leaders in the Great War had adopted the methodical battle only after much debate and huge losses, and their successors refused to welcome new operational ideas that sounded suspiciously like the offensive à outrance or the continuous battle." Within methodical: "rigid centralization and strict obedience—not decentralization, initiative, or flexibility—became the bywords of the officer corps." "It proved easier to reject ideas from officers such as Charles de Gaulle than to replace the methodical battle with a new operational concept." (l 514-515).

Presence much Great War equipment worked against new methods. 5,412 75-mm cannon left; "trustworthy gun remained more numerous than any other artillery piece." 3,187 Renault FT-17 tanks (lightly armored 2-man 6.5 tons max 4.8 mph favorable). "Neither the 75-mm cannon nor the FT-17 tank could spark the imagination of young officers or create doubts about the combat-tested methods of the Great War." Not mid-1930s new weapons sufficient numbers foster mechanized ideas. (l 515).

"Veterans of the war dominated the development of new ideas." Officers/soldiers greater respect Pétain than Foch; "Pétain, not Foch, had the largest influence upon the postwar army." "His cautious methods dominated military thinking and planning." "One only has to read the minutes of the Superior Council of War’s meetings in the interwar years to weigh the different effects of the two men and to consider how different things could have been had Foch wielded the most influence." Eugène Debeney (First Army commander relatively mobile final months) especially important role. Postwar École de Guerre director; students study every aspect army’s mobile ops. "Students at the École de Guerre often walked the ground where his army had attacked in the final months of the war, and 'lessons' and insights from the study of those operations shaped multiple aspects of French doctrine." Member commission capstone doctrinal manual postwar army: "Debeney cemented the operational methods of the last months of the war into the army’s thinking." Chief General Staff 1923-1930: "played a key role in drafting the basic laws of 1927 and 1928 that shaped the organization of the army of the 1930s and 1940s." "Over the long term the methods of his army in the final months of the war provided the blueprint for the army of the future." (l 515-516).

"On the morrow of the armistice, the prestige of the Army reached its highest point. Military leaders basked in the glory of a victory that had required the best efforts from them and the entire nation." "They were neither hypnotized nor blinded by their experiences in the war, and they were not bound for defeat in 1940 because they succeeded in 1918. Instead, they were confident of the methods and weapons that had given them success in the war, and they saw no reason to abandon or modify them without careful thought, thorough analysis, and challenging tests." "None of them foresaw the disastrous defeat of 1940 that would inflict fewer casualties but greater damage to the ideals, institutions, and international stature of France than the Great War." (l 516).
