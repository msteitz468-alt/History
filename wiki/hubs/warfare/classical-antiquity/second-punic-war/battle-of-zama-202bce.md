# Battle of Zama (202 BC) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/battle-of-zama-202bce|Full event page]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/second-punic-war|Second Punic War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

Zama is the mirror and the answer to [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/battle-of-cannae|Cannae]]. Where [[hannibal|Hannibal]] had destroyed the Roman republic's largest army by envelopment at Cannae (216 BCE), [[scipio-africanus|Scipio]] now met Hannibal on African soil and reversed every structural asymmetry that had made Cannae possible. The cavalry balance — Carthage's decisive advantage from 218 to 216 — had inverted: [[masinissa|Masinissa]]'s Numidians gave Scipio the superior horse. Scipio pre-empted the elephant opening by engineering his deployment to swallow the charge harmlessly. When the cavalry returned from pursuit to strike Hannibal's final line in the rear, the Cannae template was reproduced in reverse, ending fifteen years of war and extinguishing [[carthage|Carthage]] as a great power. This page analyses the conduct of the engagement at staff-college depth; the broader narrative, causes, and political consequences belong to [[events/battle-of-zama-202bce]].

---

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

The African campaign of 204–202 had methodically stripped [[carthage|Carthage]] of every ally and field army. Scipio had burned the combined camps of Hasdrubal Gisgo and Syphax in a night attack (spring 203), shattered the reconstituted Punic–Numidian force at the Great Plains, dethroned Syphax through Laelius and Masinissa, and devastated the Tunisian interior. Peace negotiations had begun and collapsed when Carthage broke the armistice by seizing a storm-driven Roman convoy. Hannibal was recalled from Bruttium — where he remained undefeated in the field after fifteen campaigns — and landed with his veterans at Hadrumentum. His army was the last coherent Punic military resource.

**Rome ([[scipio-africanus|Scipio]])** — primary objective: force a pitched battle before Hannibal could train his scratch forces into a unified army and before political rivals in Rome could strip him of his command. Scipio had supporters in the Senate but faced determined opponents who had tried to recall him or reassign the African command twice. A decisive victory was the only irrefutable argument. Secondary: compel a peace on terms so harsh that Carthage could never threaten the western Mediterranean again. The condition enabling battle on good terms was Masinissa's cavalry reinforcement; Scipio sent repeated messages urging the king to arrive quickly, understanding that his own infantry and Masinissa's horse together represented a qualitatively complete army that Hannibal could not match.

**Carthage ([[hannibal]])** — primary objective: win, or at minimum survive, a single engagement that would either terminate the war on acceptable terms or at least buy time for further negotiation. Hannibal understood his structural disadvantages with clarity: he was weak in cavalry, his three-line infantry force comprised three *different* armies that had not trained together, and — unlike every previous engagement in Italy — he could not expect to outflank an adversary who had studied Cannae's mechanics. His plan was accordingly conservative: use the elephants to disorder the Roman front, feed three successive infantry waves into the Roman heavy foot to exhaust it line by line, and hold his own veterans intact for a final decisive blow against a spent Roman infantry. The plan assumed his cavalry could hold on long enough for this infantry attrition to work. That assumption was the plan's fatal weakness. As Goldsworthy notes, "Hannibal's veterans, experienced and confident both in themselves and in their officers, composed less than half of his total force. His first and second lines were each formed from the remnants of two different armies."

**The parley.** Before battle the two commanders met — the only recorded direct encounter between Scipio and Hannibal. Both ancient sources (Polybius and Livy) furnish speeches, but Goldsworthy dismisses these as literary inventions: "it is questionable whether the speeches attributed to them preserve anything of their actual conversation." The parley failed. Battle followed on the next day, both sides advancing promptly, "such a swift confrontation, without the usual days of skirmishing, indicates the eagerness of both commanders for battle."

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

**Ground.** The battlefield site is the most disputed factual question about the action. Polybius names the Roman camp outside a town called Magaron; Livy calls it Naragarra. Goldsworthy is unambiguous: "it has proved impossible to locate the battlefield with any certainty, although it was clearly somewhere to the west of Zama." The traditional identification "Zama" is a misnomer — the battle was fought on open plain far from the town of that name, which was merely Hannibal's staging area five days' march west of Carthage. What is clear from the sources is that the ground was a broad flat plain — essential for the elephant charge and for cavalry operations — with low hills available for each camp. Scipio held a hill with close access to a good water supply; Hannibal occupied "a stronger position but lacked a good water supply," a logistical deficit that may have increased his urgency to fight.

**Intelligence.** Hannibal sent spies to assess the Roman camp. Scipio captured three of them and, in a gesture of calculated confidence, gave them guided tours of the entire Roman position and sent them back to Hannibal. Whether this was pure bravado or a deliberate deception — Polybius implies Scipio may have wanted to suggest Masinissa had not yet arrived — is unclear. In the event, "Masinissa rode into the Roman camp on the next day," bringing 4,000 cavalry and 6,000 infantry. Hannibal's spies had assessed the Roman position before this reinforcement arrived; the battlefield intelligence picture they carried back to their general was therefore already out of date. Hannibal also obtained 2,000 Numidian horse under Tychaeus (a relative of Syphax) to shore up his own cavalry weakness, but Goldsworthy judges these as inadequate against Masinissa's superior numbers and quality.

**Season.** The battle was fought in autumn 202, after months of armistice and Hannibal's organisation period at Hadrumentum. No source mentions weather as a significant factor in the engagement itself, in contrast to the high summer heat cited for Cannae.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | Rome and Allies (Scipio) | Carthage (Hannibal) |
|---|---|---|
| **Total engaged** | ~29,000–35,000 (estimated; see §9) | ~36,000–50,000 (Appian's 50,000 treated with caution) |
| **Infantry, Line 1** | Hastati (front of triplex acies) | Ligurians, Gauls, Balearic slingers, some Numidians — remnants of Mago's force from Italy |
| **Infantry, Line 2** | Principes (behind hastati, *in column behind the lanes*, not covering intervals) | Libyans + Punic citizens ("making a rare appearance as a formed unit during the wars"); one tradition adds Macedonians — rejected by most scholars since Polybius does not mention them |
| **Infantry, Line 3** | Triarii (rearmost) | Hannibal's Italian veterans — ~15,000–20,000, "roughly equal in number to the entire Roman heavy infantry," held ~200 yards behind the second line |
| **Cavalry, left** | Roman and Italian cavalry (under Laelius) | Carthaginian and allied cavalry |
| **Cavalry, right** | Masinissa's Numidians (~4,000) | Numidian allies under Tychaeus |
| **Screening** | Velites in the lanes of the maniple deployment (with orders to deal with the elephant charge) | ~80 war elephants across the front |
| **Command arrangement** | Scipio commanded the centre; Laelius commanded left cavalry; Masinissa commanded right cavalry | Hannibal commanded overall; "he ordered two distinct sets of officers to speak to the first and second lines" — the disparate command structure reflecting the army's composite nature |

**The critical tactical innovation in Scipio's deployment.** Normal Roman triplex acies staggered the principes to cover the gaps between hastati maniples. Scipio reversed this: "instead of stationing the maniples of principes to cover the intervals between the maniples of hastati, they were drawn up directly behind them, with the triarii in turn behind them. This created a series of wide lanes running right through the Roman formation." Velites were posted in these lanes in skirmish order — possibly initially concealing the nature of the formation — with specific orders to engage the elephant charge with javelins, wound or unnerve the animals, and then shelter within and behind the maniple blocks. The lanes were the decisive pre-battle adaptation: an engineered solution to a known threat, placed before the first trumpet note sounded.

**Hannibal's three-line deployment** was itself a tactical innovation he had never used before. Previously Hannibal had always relied on cavalry superiority to mask and then envelop his centre. Now stripped of that superiority, he adopted the Roman model of multiple infantry waves. As Goldsworthy observes, this was the clearest evidence of "just how much the two military systems had learned from each other during the long years of war." The third line — the veterans — was held conspicuously apart, several hundred yards behind the second line, in part to avoid having fugitives from the forward lines disrupt their formation, in part to preserve their cohesion for the decisive blow.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

**Phase 1 — The Elephant Charge (and its failure, c. first hour)**

After both armies deployed and their commanders addressed the troops, both sides raised war cries and sounded trumpets in the traditional display of confidence. The sudden noise startled the elephants. This appears to have been premature — the "far larger than usual number of war elephants suggests that the vast majority had recently been rounded up and were probably poorly trained." On the left, a group of panicking elephants stampeded *back* through Hannibal's own Numidian cavalry, disordering them. Masinissa read the moment immediately and "led his men forward in an immediate attack on Hannibal's Numidian allies, routing them almost immediately."

The remaining elephants charged the Roman front. The velites skirmished forward, "throwing showers of javelins at the oncoming beasts. Wounded, or their crews killed, the elephants became even more inclined to panic." Most did not close with the heavy infantry. Instead, "most of them stampeded through the lanes deliberately left in the Roman formation. Later they were disposed of at leisure in the rear of the army." Some on the right swerved toward the Roman cavalry, but a volley of javelins turned them, and they burst back through the Carthaginian cavalry on that wing. Laelius then charged the disordered enemy horse on his front and routed it.

In a matter of minutes Hannibal had lost both cavalry wings without the elephants inflicting significant damage on the Roman infantry. The structural parallel with Cannae — where the Punic cavalry had *won* both wings — was now running in reverse. As at Cannae, both winning cavalry forces immediately pursued the fleeing enemy off the field, removing them from the infantry battle for an extended period.

**Phase 2 — The Infantry Engagement: First and Second Punic Lines (mid-battle)**

With the cavalry action concluded, Hannibal's first line — Mago's old veterans of the Italian campaign, now Ligurians, Gauls, and Balearic mercenaries — advanced. "Mago's old army attacked with great enthusiasm, inflicting significant losses on the hastati." The fighting was hard and sustained: "after each lull in the fighting they renewed the combat, but gradually their charges slackened, whilst the Romans kept steadily pushing forward." The principes moved up close behind the hastati but did not yet join the fighting line.

The Punic first line received little support from the second. "The Ligurians, Gauls and others in the Punic first line received little aid from their supports, the second line of Libyans and Carthaginians hanging back." This failure of co-operation was a direct consequence of the army's composite structure — men from three different forces who had not trained together. Matters deteriorated further: "our sources even claim that fighting broke out between the two lines as men from the first attempted to retreat through the second." Hannibal had apparently instructed the reserve lines not to allow fugitives through, as he had with his veterans. A confused fighting front formed where remnants of the first and second lines merged. At this point elements of the principes joined the hastati and the fresh impetus carried the combined first-and-second Punic line into rout. "The hastati, their order gone after two hard combats, surged forward in pursuit, hacking down at the enemy as they ran."

**Phase 3 — The Third Line: Hannibal's Veterans, and Scipio's Reformation (the crisis)**

Hannibal's veterans "refused to break their ranks and presented a levelled row of spear points at their comrades fleeing towards them. Officers bellowed at the men to go around the flanks of the third line and rally behind it." The Punic veterans remained "intact and apparently unperturbed." Between them and the pursuing hastati lay a battlefield carpeted with corpses and slick with blood — difficult ground for an intact formation to advance across.

The Roman army was now in its most vulnerable state. The hastati were in pursuit, their order dissolved. The principes had fought a short engagement and were also somewhat disordered. Had Hannibal counter-attacked at this moment, the confusion might have been exploitable. He chose instead to remain stationary in perfect order and wait — "hoping that in their current state their advance would be improperly coordinated and lack power." It was a reasonable calculation: "it is possible that the flight of the forward lines made it impossible for Hannibal to send the third line forward in a counter-attack."

Scipio then achieved the action that Goldsworthy considers most indicative of the Roman army's quality: not a brilliant manoeuvre, but the "even more difficult task of reforming in the middle of a battle." Trumpets recalled the hastati from their pursuit. The wounded were moved to the rear; men rested and the officers worked to reorder the formation. The hastati reformed in the centre. "Whilst the principes and triarii were brought up on either flank. For once the Roman legions reverted to the tactics of the old hoplite phalanx, forming a single dense line matching the enemy's." This extended line now confronted Hannibal's veterans on roughly equal terms in both numbers and equipment — Hannibal's men had been re-equipped with Roman arms captured in Italy.

**Phase 4 — The Final Slogging Match and the Cavalry Return (decision)**

The two lines closed. "It proved a hard struggle, for the two sides were roughly equal in number and similarly equipped. Some of the soldiers on both sides were veterans with over ten years' service." The Romans were more tired but carried the confidence of having already broken two Punic lines. Hannibal's veterans were fresh and stood on ground of their choosing. The outcome of this final engagement in isolation is genuinely unknown. Goldsworthy is explicit: "it is impossible to know which phalanx would eventually have prevailed if the Roman cavalry had not returned."

The cavalry returned. Both Laelius and Masinissa had driven their opponents from the field and now wheeled back — "the rallied cavalrymen who returned to the battlefield and took Hannibal's veterans in the rear, making them suffer as they had once made the legions suffer at Trebia and Cannae." The parallel was conscious and explicit even to contemporaries. The Punic veterans, struck from behind while engaged to the front, collapsed. "The Cannae legions won Rome's greatest success."

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | Rome | Carthage |
|---|---|---|
| **Killed** | ~1,500 (Polybius) | ~20,000 (Polybius) |
| **Captured / wounded** | No significant figure given | ~20,000 captured (Polybius) |
| **Source(s)** | Polybius (preferred by Goldsworthy); some later sources give ~2,500 Roman dead — "no need to prefer the higher figure" | Polybius XV; Livy XXX; Appian's figures for overall army sizes unreliable throughout |

Roman losses (~1,500 killed, roughly 5% of an army of ~30,000) were described by Goldsworthy as "a substantial loss for a victorious army, testimony to the hard fighting." The Punic figures represent near-complete destruction of Hannibal's last army. Hannibal himself escaped with a small staff to Hadrumentum. [[carthage|Carthage]] had no remaining military resource and opened immediate peace negotiations.

**Strategic result.** The defeat left Carthage with no realistic option but to accept Roman terms. Scipio imposed the Treaty of 201: surrender of all war elephants, reduction of the fleet to ten triremes, loss of all overseas possessions, acknowledgement of Masinissa's enlarged Numidian kingdom, and an indemnity of 10,000 silver talents payable over fifty years. Carthage retained its African territory and internal self-government but was forbidden from making war outside Africa and only within Africa with Roman permission. As Goldsworthy notes, even Hannibal accepted the terms, reportedly dragging a dissenting senator from the speaker's platform and urging his colleagues not to reject a peace "which in their position could have been far worse."

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Scipio: success factors**

1. **Pre-battle adaptation to the elephant threat.** Opening the maniple lanes before the battle began was a problem solved by doctrine and preparation, not improvisation. The velites' specific standing orders meant no commander decision was required in the chaos of the charge itself. This is preparation translating to execution.

2. **Timing of Masinissa's arrival as a precondition for battle.** Scipio refused to engage until Masinissa's cavalry reinforcement arrived. The cavalry balance was the structural asymmetry that made victory possible; Scipio recognised this and did not allow political pressure to move him before the condition was met.

3. **Recall and reformation of the hastati.** The sounding of recall during the hot pursuit of the Punic second line — preventing the hastati from becoming entirely disordered before the confrontation with Hannibal's veterans — was the single most difficult command act of the day. Armies rarely obey recall orders in the excitement of pursuit. That Scipio's men obeyed is a measure of their training and his authority over them.

4. **Flank extension for the final line.** Bringing the principes and triarii out to the wings for the confrontation with Hannibal's veterans, extending the line to match the veterans' frontage, prevented a straightforward frontal kill by Hannibal's fresh and equal force.

5. **Years of preparation and training.** The Cannae legions had served continuously for over a decade. Scipio had spent a full year in Sicily training and integrating his volunteers with the veteran nucleus. The tactical flexibility exhibited at every stage — from the maniple lanes to the mid-battle reformation — was the product of that preparation, not of battlefield inspiration.

**Hannibal: shortcomings**

1. **The cavalry imbalance was fatal and known in advance.** Hannibal understood this — he attempted to recruit Tychaeus's 2,000 Numidians to partially offset Masinissa's 4,000. The attempt failed numerically and qualitatively. Once the elephant discharge routed his own Numidians on the left, both cavalry wings were lost within minutes. His entire plan depended on the cavalry lasting long enough for infantry attrition to work; the plan was therefore unsound given the cavalry imbalance.

2. **The composite army could not function as a unit.** Three armies — Mago's Italian veterans/mercenaries, the newly levied African force, and Hannibal's own Italian veterans — had not trained together and had different officers. The breakdown between the first and second lines, including possible fighting between them as the first line tried to retreat, was a command-and-control failure that Hannibal's three-line deployment partly anticipated (by spacing the lines apart and ordering guards against fugitives) but could not solve.

3. **The elephant gamble.** Deploying 80 war elephants — described as "probably poorly trained" since most had recently been rounded up — against an enemy known to have studied and prepared for such a charge was a high-risk opening. The elephants routed Hannibal's own cavalry on the left before the battle had properly begun.

4. **The decision not to counter-attack after the Punic first and second lines broke.** The moment the Roman hastati were in pursuit disorder was the one window when Hannibal's intact veterans might have struck to devastating effect. He chose to wait on good ground and force the Romans to attack him. The calculus was defensible — the broken ground between them was genuinely difficult — but waiting ceded the tactical initiative to Scipio's cavalry return.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective.** Scipio maintained a clear and undeviating objective throughout the African campaign: destroy Hannibal's last army and force a peace. Every prior operation — the night attack on the camps, the Great Plains, the pursuit of Syphax — was in service of that end. Hannibal's objective was more ambiguous: he needed not a total victory (he had won those at Trebia, Trasimene, and Cannae without ending the war) but a *sufficient* victory to change Roman terms. The difference in objective clarity shaped the two commanders' risk tolerance.

- **Offensive.** Scipio seized and held the offensive throughout the African campaign; Zama was the culmination of a two-year offensive operation conducted at the end of a very long supply line. Hannibal was operationally on the defensive — he was reacting to Scipio's moves — even though tactically he chose to advance and fight. The initiative lay with Rome.

- **Mass.** Scipio massed his decisive element — combined cavalry — where it could act decisively against both Punic cavalry wings simultaneously. He did not dilute Masinissa's Numidians by distributing them along the front. Hannibal attempted to mass his strength in the third infantry line, but the two forward lines were consumed before the veterans could act, and when they finally did act the cavalry returned. Hannibal violated Mass by distributing his cavalry across two wings too weak to hold either.

- **Economy of Force.** Scipio economised on the infantry centre — the hastati bore the main burden for an extended period without premature commitment of the principes — to preserve fresh troops for the critical reformation phase. Hannibal economised intelligently in holding his veterans entirely out of the first two engagements, but the economy was rendered irrelevant by the cavalry collapse.

- **Maneuver.** The critical manoeuvre was the dual cavalry pursuit and return. Both Laelius and Masinissa drove the Punic cavalry from the field, rallied, and returned to strike the veteran third line in the rear — a manoeuvre that precisely replicated what Hasdrubal had achieved at Cannae against the Romans. Scipio's mid-battle lateral extension of the principes and triarii to match the veterans' frontage was a second significant manoeuvre. Both rewarded the Roman army's long training in complex battlefield evolutions.

- **Unity of Command.** Scipio's command was unified and personal — he rode the lines encouraging his men before battle; he sounded the recall that reordered the hastati. The contrast with Hannibal's divided command (different officers addressing each of the three lines separately, no common officer structure across the three armies) was stark and directly caused the breakdown between the first and second Punic lines.

- **Security.** Scipio's choice of camp with reliable water secured his logistical base before battle. His screening deployments during the approach march (Goldsworthy notes that 500 Carthaginian cavalry on reconnaissance were defeated easily during the earlier 204 landing) demonstrated habitual security consciousness. Hannibal's camp on a stronger hill was defensively secure but logistically weak — the water shortage was a vulnerability.

- **Surprise.** Scipio achieved tactical surprise in the maniple-lanes deployment: Hannibal knew an elephant charge was coming but could not have known the lanes were structured to swallow it until the animals were already galloping through them. The return of the cavalry struck Hannibal's veterans from a direction they could not counter once already engaged to the front. Hannibal attempted no significant surprise at Zama — his plan was attritional, not deceptive, a function of his weaker army composition.

- **Simplicity.** Scipio's plan was simple in execution: let the elephants through, neutralise both cavalry wings, wear down the Punic infantry lines, reform, and trust the returning cavalry to decide the veteran engagement. Each phase was self-contained and sequentially executable. Hannibal's plan was also conceptually simple but depended on multiple conditions holding simultaneously — elephants disrupting the Roman front, cavalry holding long enough, three infantry waves attriting the enemy in order. When the first condition (elephants) failed and the second (cavalry) failed immediately after, the entire plan collapsed into damage-limitation.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

**The answer to Cannae.** Zama is studied explicitly as the doctrinal *counter* to Cannae. Where Cannae demonstrated the annihilation potential of the double envelopment against massed infantry, Zama demonstrates that the solution is not tactical but structural: deny the enemy the cavalry superiority that makes the envelopment possible. Scipio did not out-manoeuvre Hannibal tactically; he out-structured him operationally by ensuring Masinissa's cavalry arrived before battle was offered. Modern doctrine recognises this as "shaping the battlefield" — creating the conditions for decisive action before the decisive engagement begins.

**Canalisation as counter-shock.** The maniple-lane solution to the elephant charge is a textbook example of what modern doctrine calls *canalisation*: rather than absorbing or deflecting a massed shock, the defender engineers passage routes that allow the shock to expend itself harmlessly. The concept appears in 20th-century anti-tank doctrine — using terrain, obstacles, or prepared defensive geometry to channel armour away from critical positions — and the psychological logic (deny the weapon its target mass) is identical. Scipio pre-empted the shock weapon not by trying to stop it but by re-engineering the target.

**The decisive arm and combined arms.** Zama reinforces the principle that combined arms integration — not numerical superiority in any single arm — determines the decision. Scipio's infantry could not have defeated Hannibal's veterans unaided; the veterans might well have prevailed in a straight fight. The cavalry was the decisive arm, but only in conjunction with infantry that had already ground down the first two Punic lines. Modern combined-arms doctrine (FM 3-0: combined-arms operations integrate mutually supporting capabilities) traces its intellectual lineage through engagements like Zama, where each arm enabled the other's success rather than operating independently.

**The mid-battle reformation.** The recall of the hastati, the reordering under fire, and the controlled lateral redeployment of the principes and triarii — executed in the middle of an ongoing battle — represents the kind of disciplined transition that modern battalion commanders study as the hardest single tactical problem: the *passage of lines* and *consolidation under contact*. Modern military education uses Zama to illustrate the difference between *training* as the prerequisite for tactical flexibility and *inspiration* as its pale substitute.

**Zama in military education.** The battle is standard curriculum at the U.S. Army War College and Leavenworth, not merely as classical history but as illustration of operational design (shaping before engagement), combined arms theory, and the limits of tactical genius against structural preparation. The Cannae-to-Zama arc is taught as the canonical case for why the same commander who achieved a textbook success cannot necessarily repeat it when the underlying conditions have changed.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Polybius (Book XV).** The primary and most reliable account. Polybius was a professional Greek soldier and historian who conducted research in Rome, had access to participants and official records, and understood military mechanics from personal experience. His account of Zama's tactical phases is coherent and analytically grounded. He gives Roman casualties as ~1,500 and Punic losses as 20,000 killed and 20,000 captured. Goldsworthy treats these figures with qualified acceptance and sees no reason to prefer the higher Roman casualty figure of 2,500 found in later sources. Polybius' text is acknowledged to be "slightly corrupted" at one point in the infantry engagement phase, meaning one passage describing the precise mechanics of the first Punic line's contact is textually uncertain.

**Livy (Book XXX).** Livy drew on Polybius but also on the Roman annalistic tradition, which was prone to rhetorical inflation and patriotic embellishment. He names the Roman camp town "Naragarra" rather than Polybius' "Magaron" — the discrepancy contributes to the unresolved site dispute. Livy preserves detail about the fighting mechanics (the shield-boss punching technique, the breakdown between Punic lines) that supplements Polybius. His version of the pre-battle parley speeches is almost certainly literary invention, as Goldsworthy notes.

**Appian (Libyca).** The least reliable of the three. Appian wrote in the 2nd century CE, many generations after the event, and his account is "generally unconvincing" in Goldsworthy's assessment. His figure of 50,000 men for Hannibal's army and 23,000 foot plus 1,500 horse for the Romans "needs to be treated with caution." Appian's style tends toward Homeric embellishment — a tendency Polybius himself noted (in his own account of the battle Polybius quotes from the Iliad to discuss the pre-battle noise of armies, and a similar literary heightening affects Appian's version throughout). Appian is used where Polybius and Livy are silent, but always with the qualification that his figures and narrative sequences are least trustworthy.

**The site dispute.** No ancient site identification has achieved consensus. The battle was fought "somewhere to the west of Zama" on a broad plain. Multiple modern scholars have proposed identifications near Naraggara (Saca), Sicca Veneria (El Kef), or other inland Tunisian locations, but as Goldsworthy states plainly: "it has proved impossible to locate the battlefield with any certainty." This is not merely an academic question — the terrain specifics (hill positions, water access, plain width) affect all tactical reconstruction.

**Disputed numbers.** The sizes of both armies are uncertain. Goldsworthy's estimate for the Romans is ~29,000–35,000, noting that Appian's figure of 23,000 foot plus 1,500 horse (plus Masinissa's 4,000 cavalry and 6,000 infantry) cannot be taken as exact. For Hannibal he suggests that the veterans "were roughly equal in number to the entire Roman heavy infantry, so perhaps there were between 15,000–20,000 of them," implying a total Punic infantry well in excess of 30,000. The key asymmetry all sources agree on is cavalry: the Romans were significantly superior, Masinissa's 4,000 Numidians being the decisive quantitative and qualitative edge.

**Modern scholarship.** [[goldsworthy-the-fall-of-carthage-2000]] provides the principal modern tactical synthesis used here. The battle should be read alongside the same author's *Cannae* (2001) for the full arc of the Second Punic War's decisive engagements. Lazenby's *Hannibal's War* (1978) and Scullard's work on Scipio remain important secondary treatments, though not directly used in this page.
