# Battle of the Trebia (218 BC) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second Punic War event page]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/second-punic-war|Second Punic War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

The Trebia is [[hannibal|Hannibal]]'s first great set-piece victory on Italian soil — a masterclass in provocation, terrain selection, and the combined use of ambush, superior cavalry, and chosen ground to destroy a larger army. Unlike [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/battle-of-cannae|Cannae]]'s elegant geometric envelopment, the Trebia is an operational ambush at army scale: Hannibal pre-positioned a concealed reserve under his brother Mago, lured the Romans across a freezing river without breakfast, and then crushed them on both flanks before Mago's force erupted into their rear. This page analyses the mechanics of that victory and what it reveals about Hannibal's command approach in the early Italian campaign.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**[[carthage|Carthage]] (Hannibal)** — primary objective: defeat a Roman consular army decisively enough to tip the wavering Gallic tribes of Cisalpine Gaul firmly into the Carthaginian alliance, thereby securing recruits, supplies, and a safe operating base for the long Italian campaign ahead. Secondary: exploit his cavalry superiority — the irreplaceable asset he had painstakingly preserved across the Alps — on chosen ground and under conditions of his own making. Constraint: his army was still lean from the Alpine crossing; 20,000 infantry and 6,000 cavalry were all that remained of a force that had numbered 50,000 foot and 9,000 horse at the start of the march. Every veteran was irreplaceable. A costly victory was no victory. As Goldsworthy observes, "what distinguished the two men was that Hannibal made sure that the battle would be fought on his own terms."

**[[roman-republic|Rome]] (Tiberius Sempronius Longus)** — primary objective: prove Roman arms superior before his term of command expired and new consuls took office in three months' time. He had two legions and allied forces that, combined with his wounded colleague's army, gave him a numerical advantage in infantry. He believed his infantry could overwhelm Hannibal's battered force; the Ticinus skirmish had been a cavalry reverse, not (in Roman eyes) a test of the legions. Secondary: political visibility — Longus wished to share or claim the credit of Hannibal's defeat before his colleague the consul Publius Scipio (the elder) recovered from his wound and re-entered active command. Constraint: his army was inexperienced, recently raised, and had had little time to operate jointly with Scipio's contingent.

The two sets of objectives produced a predictable collision on ground of Hannibal's choosing, at a moment of Hannibal's choosing, in weather that Hannibal had already accounted for.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground:** the plain west of the River Trebia — wide, flat, and open until it rose sharply at its southern and southwestern edges. The flat ground was ideal for Hannibal's cavalry arm and suited the linear deployment of a large, relatively inexperienced Roman army. Critically, Hannibal and his commanders had ridden across the plain before the battle, studying the terrain in detail. He identified a watercourse crossing the plain and running between two steep and heavily overgrown banks — ideal cover for a concealed force. "The plain west of the Trebia is wide, and as flat as any land ever is naturally, until it rises sharply at its southern and south-western edges."
- **The Trebia as a weapon:** the Roman camps lay on the east bank of the river. To engage, Longus' army would have to ford the Trebia. Polybius tells us the day was near the winter solstice; the waters were swollen by recent rain. The crossing guaranteed that the Roman infantry would arrive tired, cold, wet, and — because they were hustled out of camp before dawn without time to eat — hungry.
- **Weather / season:** December, near the winter solstice. The day brought sleet in sporadic showers, and the men were still wet from the ford when they formed up. The temperature favoured Hannibal's troops, who had breakfasted and prepared at leisure in camp. The Romans' physical condition before a blow had been struck was already seriously degraded.
- **Intelligence:** Hannibal had precise intelligence about the Roman dispositions and, more importantly, about Longus' temperament. He knew Longus was "of an aggressive temperament" and that a minor success — the recent skirmish in which the Romans had driven back his raiding party — had reinforced the consul's desire for an immediate pitched battle. Roman intelligence was comparatively poor: Polybius notes that the Romans were wary of ambush in wooded terrain (where the Gauls had often surprised them) but "did not believe an ambush possible in an open plain." Hannibal exploited exactly this assumption when he positioned Mago's concealed force in the overgrown watercourse on the open plain.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | Carthage | Rome |
|---|---|---|
| **Total** | ~30,000 (est.) | ~40,000–42,000 (est.) |
| **Heavy Infantry** | ~20,000 — Gauls in centre, Spanish and Libyan foot on the flanks of the centre | ~36,000–38,000 — 16,000 Roman legionaries (Polybius) or 18,000 (Livy) + 20,000 allied infantry incl. Cenomani Gauls |
| **Cavalry** | ~5,000 Gallic/Spanish heavy cavalry + ~5,000 Numidian light cavalry = ~10,000 total on the wings | ~4,000 (Roman, Latin, and allied horse) |
| **Missile/Light infantry** | 8,000 light infantry deployed as a forward screen once the Roman heavy infantry committed; Balearic slingers included | 6,000 velites, already partly exhausted by the morning cavalry chase |
| **Special arms** | 37 elephants, divided between the two wings near the heavy infantry | None |
| **Concealed reserve** | 1,000 infantry + 1,000 cavalry under Mago, hidden in the overgrown watercourse | None |
| **Command** | Hannibal, with Mago commanding the ambush force | Tiberius Sempronius Longus (consul) |

**Carthaginian deployment.** Hannibal waited until the Roman heavy infantry had fully committed to crossing the Trebia before sending out his 8,000 light infantry to support the retiring Numidians and form a screen. Only then did his main body advance approximately one mile from camp and form line. The centre was a single line of 20,000 close-order infantry, with the Gauls (approximately 8,000) forming the middle of the centre and the Spanish and Libyan foot on the flanks. The heavy cavalry was distributed on each wing, joined by the returning Numidians to give roughly 5,000 horsemen on each flank. The elephants were divided into two bodies and placed with the wings of the heavy infantry.

**Roman deployment.** The army deployed in the standard Roman formation: legions in the centre, allied troops on their flanks, cavalry on the wings. Three heavy infantry columns had marched the four to five miles from the camps and then wheeled into line — a laborious process taking several hours. The men were tired from the crossing and the march, cold and wet, and most were hungry. Longus recalled his weary cavalry from the morning skirmish and formed them onto the main line.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Phase 1 — The lure (dawn to mid-morning).** At dawn, Hannibal sent his Numidian cavalry across the Trebia to attack the Roman outposts and draw the enemy into a skirmish, with "strict orders to involve the Romans in a skirmish and then steadily withdraw, luring the enemy across the river." Longus responded exactly as Hannibal anticipated: he sent all his cavalry out against the Numidians, then 6,000 velites in their wake, and finally ordered his entire heavy infantry to muster and cross the river. The Numidians fought and fell back, fought and fell back — never closing to decisive combat, harassing the Roman horse with javelins, tiring men and horses through the whole morning. The velites, ordered to support the cavalry pursuit, used up most of their missile supply chasing an enemy who declined to stand.

2. **Phase 2 — The crossing and deployment (mid-morning).** Longus' heavy infantry waded the Trebia — swollen by rain, near-freezing — and then spent several hours wheeling from march column into a battle line perhaps two miles long. This was technically demanding for a large, partly inexperienced force under the tribunes' direction. By the time the Romans had deployed, their men had covered four to five miles on foot, forded a winter river, stood in the cold and sleet through the lengthy deployment process, and most had eaten nothing. The consul's cavalry returned to take post on the wings: horses and riders tired after a morning of fruitless pursuit of the Numidians, who had never stopped to let them charge home.

3. **Phase 3 — The skirmish line and the cavalry wings (morning into afternoon).** Longus advanced his whole line in good order. The skirmishers of both armies met first. The Romans fared badly: their velites were tired, had expended many of their javelins during the morning, and may have been outnumbered. Hannibal's light infantry included the renowned Balearic slingers, whose long range combined with the shorter javelinmen's weapons gave the Punic skirmishers a flexibility the Romans could not match. As the main lines closed, the Carthaginian light infantry fell back — to the flanks, according to Livy, to support the cavalry there. When Hannibal finally ordered his horsemen forward, the outnumbered and weary Roman cavalry "seems to have put up a feeble resistance." Livy adds that the Balearic slingers now sniped at the Roman horse and that the elephants frightened the horses (though Goldsworthy notes that Livy's account of the elephants' role "is hopelessly confused"). As the Roman cavalry broke, the Numidians and light infantry surged forward around the flanks of the Roman main line, enfilading the allied infantry on the wings with missiles from the flanks and rear.

4. **Phase 4 — The infantry clash and Mago's ambush (afternoon).** With the Roman wings now exposed and harassed, the main infantry lines collided. Despite everything — the cold, the hunger, the fatigue — the heavy engagement in the centre was long and contested: "the clash between the two main lines of close order foot was long and hard, its outcome uncertain." The Roman hastati and principes alone outnumbered the enemy foot by a large margin and were more heavily armoured. They outpushed the Gallic centre, eventually routing the Gauls and a unit of Libyans and breaking through the Carthaginian line entirely. But the wings were crumbling. The Numidians and light infantry continued to press the allied flanks, and then Mago's force — 1,000 infantry and 1,000 cavalry, rested and concealed all morning in the overgrown watercourse — burst from ambush into the Roman rear, "spreading confusion throughout the whole army." The wings of the Roman infantry eventually gave way. The Roman centre, beset by the elephants and Punic foot to their front and assailed from the rear by Mago's men, lost unit cohesion at the flanks even as the legionary heavy infantry in the middle drove through.

5. **Phase 5 — Breakthrough and rout.** Hannibal had deployed his infantry in a single line with no formed reserve. When approximately 10,000 Roman legionaries broke through the Gallic centre, there was nothing to stop them — but crucially, they did not turn to re-engage. Keeping their formation, they marched north, swung around the Punic army, recrossed the Trebia opposite Placentia, and took refuge in the colony. Hannibal made no attempt to intercept them: "his men were weary and his victory was already clear." The rest of the Roman army disintegrated in the rout, individuals and small groups making their way back to the camps or joining the force at Placentia.

**Key tactical mechanisms:** provocation to force a premature, unfed advance in cold weather · use of the river crossing as an attrition mechanism before a blow was struck · the concealed ambush reserve in terrain the Romans assessed as safe · cavalry superiority exploited to envelop the flanks once the centre was committed · combined use of light and heavy horse to pin, tire, and then break the Roman wings.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | Carthage | Rome |
|---|---|---|
| **Killed** | Moderate; heaviest among the Gallic centre (no figure given by Polybius or Livy) | Heavy; no precise total given in sources |
| **Post-battle attrition** | Severe — in the cold spell following the battle, many men, most of the horses, and all but one of the elephants died | Significant further losses as individuals and stragglers were rounded up |
| **Escaped as formed body** | — | ~10,000 legionaries from the centre to Placentia |
| **Source(s)** | Polybius Bk III; Goldsworthy *The Fall of Carthage* (2000) | Polybius Bk III; Livy Bk XXI |

- **Strategic result:** Hannibal achieved his primary operational objective. The Gallic tribes that had been wavering now embraced the Punic cause — "the victory persuaded those Gauls who had been wavering to embrace the Punic cause." The blow to Roman prestige was considerable, even if the actual physical losses were difficult to calculate precisely. The Romans' confidence in their own heavy infantry, dented but not destroyed, survived the battle.
- **Roman reaction:** Longus first attempted to portray the result as inconclusive, blaming the weather for preventing a Roman victory. It was some time before the Senate appreciated the true scale of the disaster. Responsibility was laid primarily on Longus' rashness. The escape of ~10,000 legionaries from the centre — fighting their way through Hannibal's line in good order — was taken as evidence that Roman courage had not failed, even when Roman generalship had.
- **Immediate operational consequence:** Hannibal now had sufficient momentum and Gallic support to sustain his campaign through the winter. The stage was set for the disasters of Lake Trasimene (217 BCE) and Cannae (216 BCE). See [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/battle-of-lake-trasimene-217bce]].

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Hannibal's success factors**

- **Reconnaissance and terrain selection.** Before the battle, Hannibal and his commanders personally rode the plain and identified both the ideal deployment ground and the concealed watercourse for Mago's ambush. He turned a "flat, featureless" plain into a killing ground with a prepared trap at its centre. The parallel with Napoleon's reconnaissance of the Pratzen Heights before Austerlitz was already noted by ancient observers.
- **The Numidian lure — exploiting a known enemy psychology.** Hannibal correctly predicted that Longus, elated by a minor success in the previous skirmish and eager for glory before his consulship expired, would send his entire force across the river in pursuit of a feigned withdrawal. The Numidians were given explicit and disciplined orders: skirmish, never close, withdraw steadily. This is not improvisation; it is a designed provocation requiring tight command and control of light cavalry — notoriously difficult to manage.
- **Choosing the hour and the weather.** By triggering the battle at dawn and near the winter solstice, Hannibal ensured his own men could eat, warm themselves, and prepare at leisure while the Romans crossed a frigid river and stood in sleet for hours before the first blow was struck. The physical asymmetry at the moment of contact — rested, fed Carthaginians against cold, wet, hungry Romans — was an operational choice, not a coincidence.
- **The Mago ambush.** Positioning 2,000 men (infantry and cavalry) in a concealed watercourse on an open plain was a bold and original idea. It exploited a blind spot in Roman tactical thinking: they feared Gallic ambush in woods, not on plains. The timing of Mago's emergence — once the Roman rear was already partly disorganised by the cavalry envelopment and the infantry was fully committed frontally — was exact.
- **Asymmetric cavalry concentration.** With approximately 10,000 horse against Rome's 4,000, Hannibal held nearly a 2.5:1 numerical superiority in the arm that decided the flanks. He did not dissipate this advantage by piecemeal commitment; instead he deployed virtually all his cavalry on the wings from the outset, ensuring the Roman horse was swept away before his infantry pressure and the Mago ambush reached their full effect.
- **Tight control, refusal to be drawn.** Before the battle, Hannibal had demonstrated exactly this quality in the preliminary skirmish over the Gallic villages: when that fight began to escalate, he rallied his men personally, formed a defensive line just outside his camp, and refused to let it develop into a battle on unplanned terms. Polybius explicitly praises "the tight control he exercised over his men and his unwillingness to let a battle occur by chance."

**Roman shortcomings**

- **Command susceptibility to provocation.** Longus was not reckless by the standards of his time — Goldsworthy is careful to note that "it is doubtful that any other Roman magistrate would have behaved differently in the circumstances." The problem was structural: Roman consular culture rewarded aggression and punished apparent timidity. Hannibal identified this and exploited it. The consul's wounded colleague had reportedly argued for delay; Longus overrode him, partly from genuine military judgment, partly from political calculation about sharing glory.
- **Failure to feed and prepare the men before committing.** The army was hurried out of camp before dawn without allowing time to cook a meal. The men were also still wet from fording the Trebia by the time they had to fight. These were not unavoidable conditions — they resulted from Longus accepting battle on Hannibal's timetable rather than his own.
- **Misreading the velites' exhaustion.** The 6,000 velites had spent the morning pursuing elusive Numidians and had largely expended their javelins. In the climactic skirmish phase they were outmatched by fresh Punic light infantry including the Balearic slingers. No tactical adjustment was made for this.
- **Cavalry inferiority accepted as a given.** With 4,000 horsemen against approximately 10,000, the Roman wings had no realistic chance of holding once Hannibal committed his full cavalry force. This inferiority was known before the battle; no countermeasures — refused flanks, defensive use of the velites to screen the wings, terrain anchoring — appear to have been attempted.
- **No answer to the Mago ambush.** Partly this was Hannibal's skill in deception; partly it was a failure of Roman reconnaissance and security doctrine. The watercourse was not investigated. The Romans assumed the open plain was safe.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — Hannibal's objective was precise and political: not merely to win a battle but to secure the Gallic alliance by demonstrating that Rome could be beaten in the field. Every tactical choice — the lure, the timing, the ambush — served this objective. Longus' objective was confused: winning a battle before his consulship ended was partly a personal ambition that partially obscured the actual military requirement, which was to destroy or neutralise Hannibal's army, not simply to offer and win a pitched engagement.
- **Offensive** — Longus believed he held the initiative because he chose to attack. In practice, Hannibal held the initiative throughout: he chose the ground, set the bait, dictated the timing, pre-positioned the reserve, and fed his men before the action started. Apparent offensive action — marching out to fight — does not constitute true initiative when the adversary has already designed the battle you are walking into.
- **Mass** — Rome held a significant numerical advantage in heavy infantry (~36,000–38,000 vs. ~20,000) but could not apply it decisively. The flanks gave way before the central mass could exploit its breakthrough. Hannibal massed *decisively where it mattered*: he concentrated virtually his entire cavalry superiority on both wings simultaneously, ensuring both Roman flanks collapsed before the centre could redeem the battle. The lesson echoes Cannae: mass that cannot be applied at the decisive point and moment is strategically inert.
- **Economy of Force** — Hannibal accepted significant risk at his Gallic centre — his weakest infantry — but this was a calculated economy: the Gauls were to absorb the Roman central push while the flanks and the ambush reserve decided the battle. Mago's 2,000 troops (a small fraction of the total force) were held back in complete concealment, contributing nothing in the skirmish phase, to be applied at the critical moment of greatest Roman disorganisation. Economy of force sustained as a timed, phased commitment is visible throughout Hannibal's plan.
- **Maneuver** — the battle is fundamentally a manoeuvre victory achieved before the main lines closed: the Numidian feint manoeuvred the entire Roman army across a freezing river in degraded condition; the cavalry superiority manoeuvred around both Roman wings; and Mago's concealed force manoeuvred into the Roman rear without detection. Physical manoeuvre before contact — not superior fighting quality in the infantry press — determined the outcome.
- **Unity of Command** — Hannibal exercised unified command, personally briefing his senior officers the morning of the battle and explaining the plan before the fighting started. He could also delegate precisely: Mago operated independently in the watercourse on a timed plan, while the Numidian cavalry commanders understood their role and executed it without deviation. Longus commanded without an effective second mind; the consul Publius Scipio (the elder) was wounded and inactive.
- **Security** — Hannibal's own camp and army were entirely secure during the preparatory phase. The Numidian lure operated close to the Roman position while Mago moved his force into the watercourse under cover of darkness. Roman security was poor: no effective screen covered the watercourse, and the Roman outposts were neutralised by the opening Numidian raid rather than reinforced and used to develop intelligence about Carthaginian movements. The Roman assumption that a flat plain precluded ambush was precisely the security vulnerability Hannibal exploited.
- **Surprise** — the tactical surprise was total at two levels. First, the overall battle was staged: the Numidian "attack" appeared to be a real aggressive move requiring an immediate response, concealing Hannibal's preparatory dispositions. Second, and more importantly, the emergence of Mago's ambush force from an apparently bare and open plain shocked an army that was already under pressure from three sides. Polybius records that the Romans did not conceive an ambush possible in open terrain — Hannibal used their own doctrine against them.
- **Simplicity** — Hannibal's plan was not simple: it required precise timing of the Numidian withdrawal, the sequenced commitment of light infantry, the cavalry charge on both wings, the simultaneous advance of the heavy infantry centre, and the timed emergence of Mago from concealment, all co-ordinated in real time across several miles of front, by multiple commanders of different nationalities. What made it work was not simplicity but the disciplined execution of a multi-part plan by veteran, well-led troops who understood their role. The lesson for commanders is the inverse of the standard formulation: complexity can be managed when training, mutual trust, and command clarity are high enough — but the risk of a single element failing (a Numidian commander who broke off too early, a Mago who emerged too soon or too late) would have unravelled the whole design.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

**The baited trap and the prepared battleground.** The Trebia is the earliest well-documented example of what modern doctrine calls a *shaping operation*: all the preliminary actions — the Numidian raid, the minor skirmish over the Gallic villages, the feigned withdrawal — were not random or opportunistic engagements but deliberate preparations designed to shape the enemy's decision-making before the decisive action. Modern manoeuvre doctrine (ADP 3-0) distinguishes between *enabling operations* and the *decisive operation*; Hannibal ran several consecutive enabling operations before committing to his decisive action.

**Provocation as an operational weapon.** The Numidian lure is a textbook use of the enemy's psychology as a force-multiplier: by correctly predicting Longus' response to a visible attack on the Roman outposts, Hannibal turned the consul's own aggressiveness into a tool that delivered the Roman army to him in a degraded physical state. This parallels the modern concept of *adversary decision cycle exploitation* — shaping not just the terrain or the tactical situation, but the enemy commander's mental model and reaction time. Staff-college analyses of Chancellorsville (1863) and the German Blitzkrieg opening of the Ardennes (1940) involve the same mechanism: the enemy is induced to commit in the wrong direction before the decisive blow falls.

**The concealed reserve.** Mago's force is a true operational reserve employed as a surprise element — not a pursuit force, not a garrison, but a formed body held out of sight and committed at the crisis point. Modern doctrine stresses the importance of retaining a commander's reserve precisely for contingencies; Hannibal inverted this: he *planned* the crisis (the Roman breakthrough of his centre) as the moment for the reserve's commitment, turning the most dangerous moment of the battle into a trigger for the decisive blow. This deliberate synchronisation of a concealed reserve with a planned enemy action is visible in the German stormtrooper tactics of 1918 and in Allied operational reserve doctrine in both world wars.

**Envelopment on a shoestring.** The Trebia achieved a partial envelopment — both Roman wings broken, the rear penetrated by Mago — without the geometric perfection of Cannae's double development. That ~10,000 legionaries escaped through the centre is the direct consequence: Hannibal had no second-line cavalry to seal the gap because his cavalry was already committed on the wings. The lesson taken from the comparison of Trebia and Cannae in military education is that a successful flank envelopment must be paired with a reserve capable of blocking the enemy's route of escape if it is to produce annihilation rather than defeat. At Cannae (216 BCE), Hasdrubal's lateral ride behind the Roman rear solved precisely this problem; at the Trebia it was not yet solved. See [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/battle-of-cannae]].

**Fighting on chosen ground.** Napoleon's reconnaissance of the Pratzen Heights before Austerlitz (1805) was a conscious parallel to Hannibal's own pre-battle study of the Trebia plain. The principle — identify the ground on which you want to fight, then manoeuvre the enemy onto it — is enshrined in modern operational art as *terrain appreciation and battle geometry*. Hannibal's reconnaissances before his major battles (Trebia, Lake Trasimene, Cannae) are among the earliest documented examples of a commander systematically using ground as a force-multiplier before the engagement begins.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Polybius (Histories, Book III)** is the principal narrative source. He was writing within two generations of the battle, with access to participants and to documents. His account of the Trebia is characteristically sober: he is precise about the mechanism of the Numidian lure, Hannibal's feeding and preparation of his own troops, the sequence of phases, and the escape of the Roman centre. His casualty figures are generally preferred over Livy's because he is more cautious about accepting round numbers. He notes Hannibal's tight control of his men in the preliminary skirmish — "Polybius praises Hannibal for the tight control he exercised over his men and his unwillingness to let a battle occur by chance, which Polybius believed demonstrated his wisdom as a commander." His bias: a close association with the Scipionic family inclines him to present the elder Publius Scipio's advice against battle (supposedly given to Longus) in a favourable light, and Goldsworthy correctly urges caution about accepting this Scipionic family tradition at face value.

**Livy (Ab Urbe Condita, Book XXI)** writes roughly 200 years after the battle for an Augustan audience. He provides additional detail (infantry numbers: 18,000 Roman legionaries vs. Polybius's 16,000; greater narrative colour on the cavalry fight and the elephants' role) but is explicitly less reliable on tactical mechanics. His account of the elephants' part in breaking the Roman cavalry is "hopelessly confused," per Goldsworthy, and Livy's dramatic sensibility leads him to sharpen the contrast between the sagacious (wounded) elder Publius Scipio counselling caution and the rash Longus rushing headlong to disaster. This binary is almost certainly a literary and family-tradition construct.

**Conflicting figures.** On Roman infantry strength: Polybius gives 16,000 Roman legionaries, Livy 18,000; both then agree on 20,000 allied infantry including the Cenomani. Goldsworthy notes that both writers "have clearly assumed an average size for each unit and multiplied this by four, so that at best this provides a rough guide to the actual number of troops" — and warns that if one of Longus' legions was the unit that had been ambushed by the Boii earlier in the year, it was likely still under strength. No precise casualty figure for the battle is given by either source, which is itself informative: the Roman losses were severe but the catastrophic disorganisation of the retreat made counting impossible. On the elephants: both sources agree they were divided between the wings; Livy's account of their role in the cavalry action is contradicted by other details in both narratives, and Goldsworthy treats it with appropriate scepticism.

**The Scipionic family tradition.** The tradition that the elder Publius Scipio warned against fighting and predicted the disaster was "most probably spread by the Scipionic family from the immediate aftermath of the battle onwards." Polybius, through his close association with Scipio Aemilianus, transmitted and reinforced this tradition. It fits too neatly — the wise Scipio foreseeing defeat; the rash new arrival Longus ignoring him — to be accepted without qualification. Goldsworthy notes that Scipio's earlier behaviour had been "just as aggressive" as Longus' and that his own wound and defeat at Ticinus may have accounted for his changed outlook, rather than superior strategic insight.

**Modern scholarship.** [[goldsworthy-the-fall-of-carthage-2000]] provides the fullest modern tactical treatment of the Trebia, contextualised within the broader Italian campaign. His analysis is the primary basis for this page. The battle receives less dedicated modern study than Cannae or Trasimene, partly because it left fewer sources and partly because the Roman central breakthrough complicates the narrative of a clean Carthaginian victory. The live scholarly questions are: (1) whether the Scipionic-tradition account of pre-battle counsel is credible; (2) the exact scale of Roman losses; and (3) whether the escape of the Roman centre reflects a planned Hannibalic decision or a tactical gap he could not fill.
