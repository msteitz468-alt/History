# Battle of Ilipa (206 BC) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second Punic War event page]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/second-punic-war|Second Punic War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college-level analysis of [[scipio-africanus|Scipio]]'s tactical masterpiece in Spain: the pre-battle deception, the dawn reversal of deployment, the wheeling advance that enveloped Hasdrubal Gisco's flanks, and the doctrinal significance of a manoeuvre that consciously inverted [[hannibal|Hannibal]]'s template at [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/battle-of-cannae|Cannae (216 BC)]]. For the operation that made Ilipa possible, see [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/battle-of-new-carthage-209bce|Capture of New Carthage (209 BC)]]; for the war's culmination see [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/battle-of-zama-202bce|Zama (202 BC)]].

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

By 206 BCE the Second Punic War in Spain had reached a crisis that was also an opportunity. Scipio had destroyed Punic Spain's strategic base at New Carthage in 209, won a tactical engagement at Baecula in 208 — from which Hasdrubal Barca escaped northward into Italy — and fought an inconclusive campaign in 207. The Carthaginians recognized that the situation was critical and responded with an all-out concentration of force. Hasdrubal Gisco massed the largest army yet seen in the Iberian theatre: Polybius gives 70,000 infantry, 4,000 cavalry, and 32 elephants; Livy records the more modest but still formidable figure of 50,000 foot and 4,500 horse. This army, encamped on readily defensible high ground near Ilipa (probably near modern Alcalá del Río, north of Seville), was a clear invitation to decisive engagement.

Scipio's own force was numerically at best a rough match, probably at a disadvantage. When he called in all his scattered detachments, he mustered "around 45,000 foot and 3,000 horse," of which only about half — two legions and two allied alae, the standard consular army — were Roman or Italian. The remainder were Spanish allied troops, "many recently raised from Rome's new allies amongst the Spanish tribes, similar to the warriors whose desertion had precipitated the disaster suffered by his father and uncle." The Spanish allied infantry were a known quantity in terms of courage and individual fighting skill, but they were less disciplined and less tactically flexible than the legions. This troop-quality asymmetry would define Scipio's tactical solution.

**Scipio (Rome)** — primary objective: a decisive battle that would eliminate Carthaginian military power in Spain entirely, not merely drive the enemy from a position. After years of campaigning without a truly war-ending result in the theatre, Scipio needed a battle that would produce a Punic collapse rather than another managed retreat. Secondary objectives: preserve the Roman and Italian core of his army (they could not be easily replaced at this distance from Italy) and prevent the Spanish allied contingents — whose loyalty was personal and conditional — from being exposed to pressure they could not resist. Constraint: fighting an army significantly larger than his own, with inferior allies forming half his force, on ground chosen by the enemy.

**Hasdrubal Gisco (Carthage)** — primary objective: a pitched battle on favourable terms that would reverse the tide in Spain before the theatre was lost entirely. The defensive camp on high ground, with an open plain in front, was, as Goldsworthy observes, "a clear message to the Romans that this time Hasdrubal was ready and willing to fight." Hasdrubal's deployment advantage was his Libyan heavy infantry — his best troops — which he stationed in the centre. His Spanish allied warriors, of similar quality to Scipio's Spanish contingents, held the flanks. Constraint: a force that was large but whose quality was concentrated in one arm (the Libyan centre), with Spanish allied flanks whose reliability under sustained Roman pressure was not guaranteed.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

**Ground.** The battle was fought on an open plain between the two camps, Hasdrubal's on high ground and Scipio's on a line of low hills. The plain was level enough for the complex manoeuvre Scipio planned, and wide enough to accommodate two armies of this size in line. There were no significant terrain features that would impede movement or provide natural anchors for either flank — a critical fact, because Scipio needed room to wheel his columns without them running into obstacles.

**The camp positions.** Hasdrubal had the superior camp site: high ground, defensible, with the army deploying downhill onto the plain. Scipio's low hills were defensible but offered no commanding advantage. The choice of ground was Hasdrubal's — and for several days he used it to control the tempo of the pre-battle manoeuvring, moving his army out to form up first each morning and thereby establishing the pattern of deployment Scipio would exploit.

**Weather and duration.** The battle was fought through the heat of the day, which proved significant. Goldsworthy records that Polybius mentions "hunger weakened the resilience of the Spanish warriors as the fight continued through the heat of the day." Hasdrubal's army had been denied breakfast by Scipio's predawn advance and was fighting through the Spanish summer heat without food, a cumulative degradation of fighting capacity that compounded the tactical defeat. The fighting ended not by Roman decision but when "a sudden deluge of rain" broke the action late in the day.

**Intelligence.** Scipio's intelligence work at Ilipa was primarily observational and temporal. Over the several days during which both sides deployed to offer battle without committing, he systematically catalogued Hasdrubal's fixed routine: the Carthaginian army marched out first each morning, forming up with Libyans in the centre and Spanish warriors on the flanks. Scipio responded each day with his own standard deployment: legions in the centre, allied alae on the flanks, Spanish foot on the outer wings. He allowed Hasdrubal to read this as a fixed pattern. The deception depended on Hasdrubal believing that Scipio would deploy the same way on the decisive morning, and on the psychological momentum of habit: "Each day the Carthaginians moved first, and it was only in apparent response that Scipio gave the order for his own columns to march out of the camp gates and form up."

**The deployment reversal — the critical innovation.** On all previous days, the standard formation on both sides had been: best troops (Libyans / Roman legions) in the centre, allied troops on the wings, cavalry on the extreme flanks. On the morning of the decisive battle, Scipio reversed his own arrangement completely: **the Roman legions and Italian alae moved to the wings, the Spanish allies to the centre.** This meant Scipio's best troops would face Hasdrubal's weakest (the Spanish warriors on the Punic flanks), while Scipio's weakest troops (his Spanish allies in the centre) faced Hasdrubal's best (the Libyan centre). The plan's logic was to destroy Hasdrubal's flanks before the Spanish centre came under serious pressure from the Libyan foot — a controlled economy of force in which the centre's role was to hold long enough for the wings to win.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

**Phase 1 — The predawn advance and the breakfast trap.** Scipio issued orders the night before for his soldiers to eat well and be ready in battle order at dawn. Well before first light, the Roman cavalry and velites were sent forward to engage the Punic outposts. Behind them, the army marched out and wheeled into line — with the new, reversed deployment. The Punic outposts were rapidly driven in. Hasdrubal, presented with the unexpected sight of the Roman army already formed in the plain, had no choice but to order his own troops out. They marched out before they had a chance to eat breakfast, and — crucially — in the same order as on every previous day. Hasdrubal had formed his line and pushed back the Roman light troops before he realized that Scipio's deployment was different. By then it was too late to alter his own orders.

Goldsworthy captures the Carthaginian commander's dilemma precisely: "It was one thing for the Romans to carry out a carefully planned and prepared manoeuvre, but far harder for him on the spur of the moment to issue new orders, sending some of his line forward to face them. If his Libyan foot had been sent forward to outflank the Roman wings, they would in turn have exposed their own flanks to the Spaniards forming the Roman centre." Hasdrubal did nothing. The two lines stood perhaps half a mile apart as the light troops continued to skirmish — possibly for hours — while the Punic army stood hungry in the Spanish summer heat.

**Phase 2 — Scipio recalls the velites and begins the advance.** Once the velite skirmishing had served its purpose (exhausting Punic light troop ammunition, pinning Hasdrubal in place, and running time off the clock against the unfed enemy), Scipio recalled his velites and ordered them to the wings of the army, adding their missile capacity to the flank attack that was coming. He then ordered a general advance — but not an even one. The Spanish allies in the centre were instructed to move slowly. The wings, composed of the legions and alae together with the velites and cavalry, would advance faster.

**Phase 3 — The column manoeuvre and the approach to contact.** The critical tactical movement now followed. Scipio had deployed in the triplex acies (the standard Roman three-line arrangement) facing the enemy. He ordered his right wing to turn 90° to the right, forming three columns marching parallel to the Punic line rather than toward it. The left wing mirrored this to the left. These columns then advanced rapidly — columns always move faster than line — outstripping the slowly moving Spanish centre. When the Roman columns had moved far enough along the Punic front to be level with Hasdrubal's Spanish flanking infantry, they wheeled again 90° inward, deploying back into triplex acies formation, now facing the Punic line but at a point well out toward the Punic flanks.

Goldsworthy stresses that this manoeuvre — performed within sight of and very close to a formed enemy — was extraordinary: "Scipio's manoeuvre was a variation on the normal Roman method of deploying an army, but it had never before been performed so close to and under the gaze of a formed enemy. Only the exceptionally high discipline and training of his army made this possible." The Punic army "watched, mesmerized, as the Roman columns had arrogantly come straight at them. Hasdrubal did nothing."

The result, once the Roman wings had completed their deployment, was that the Roman legions and alae were now overlapping and beginning to envelop Hasdrubal's Spanish flank infantry, with the velites and cavalry already outflanking the ends of the Punic line. The Punic Libyan centre — the best troops — remained unengaged. The Spanish centre — Scipio's weakest element — had not yet come into contact.

**Phase 4 — The engagement and the envelopment.** The Roman wings attacked with "great enthusiasm." The velites bombarded the elephants with missiles, "causing many to stampede" — disorganizing the Punic flanks further. The legions and alae drove into Hasdrubal's Spanish foot. In the centre, the pick of the Punic army — the Libyans — "remained unengaged for some time, inactive observers of the fighting, until the Romans' Spanish allies eventually came into contact." The Spanish warriors on the Punic flanks met the Roman legions "with considerable determination," but the Roman multiple-line system allowed fresh impetus to be injected into the fighting line as the engagement wore on through the heat of the day, while hunger and the mounting heat of the Spanish summer eroded Punic resilience. "Hasdrubal rode around the line urging his men on."

On the wings, the Romans gradually gained the upper hand. "At first the Spanish and then the whole Punic army went back step by step, still facing the enemy. Then the pressure grew too much and, as the Romans surged forward, they broke and fled." The Punic army rallied briefly "at the base of the hill beneath their camp," but as the Romans pressed forward again the rout resumed. A sudden deluge of rain ended the pursuit before the Roman camp could be stormed.

**Phase 5 — The collapse and dissolution.** That night, Hasdrubal's Spanish contingents began abandoning him in the rain. By morning, any thought of continuing the fight was gone. The retreat began, and with it the final dissolution of Punic military power in Spain. "Hasdrubal and Masinissa managed to make their way to the coast and took ship for North Africa, whilst Mago fled to Gades." The Spanish tribes that had followed Carthage flocked to Scipio. The Punic armies in Spain ceased to exist as organized forces.

The absence of casualty figures in the sources is notable. Goldsworthy does not supply numbers for killed or captured at Ilipa. The victory's significance was strategic rather than quantitative: the Punic army did not die in place — it dissolved, its Spanish components voting with their feet — and the Carthaginian commanders escaped. But the *effect* was total. Within months of Ilipa, Punic Spain was gone.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Scipio's success factors**

- **The deployment reversal as a solution to the troop-quality problem.** Scipio's fundamental challenge was that he had both Romans (good) and Spanish allies (unreliable under pressure) in his army, while Hasdrubal had both Libyans (good) and Spanish warriors (unreliable under pressure). The standard deployment — best troops in the centre — would have pitted Roman legions against Libyan foot in a frontal slugging match while Spanish contingents fought each other on the flanks, with no army having a decisive advantage anywhere. Scipio's inversion put his best troops against Hasdrubal's weakest, and his weakest troops against Hasdrubal's best — but in a design where the flanks would win before the centre needed to.
- **Days of deliberate pattern-setting.** Scipio's willingness to deploy conventionally for several days, giving the enemy a fixed expectation, was itself a tactical preparation. He manufactured the habit he intended to exploit. This required patience and discipline in holding back the decisive manoeuvre.
- **The breakfast denial.** By advancing before dawn, Scipio forced Hasdrubal to deploy before his men had eaten — a cumulative attrition that did not show in the opening phases but compounded over a long hot day, particularly against Spanish warriors who "lacked the organized supply system of the Roman army.
- **The column manoeuvre under observation.** Executing a 90°-wheel-into-column, march parallel to the enemy, then wheel again into line, while within view and perhaps half a mile of a formed Punic army, was an operation only the highest-trained force could manage without its ranks dissolving into confusion. Scipio had spent months after New Carthage "subjecting his army to a rigorous training programme." The Ilipa manoeuvre was the dividend.
- **Scipio's personal command of the critical wing.** He led the right wing himself. On the left, he entrusted the mirror manoeuvre to Silanus and Lucius Marcius — the equestrian who had salvaged the Spanish command after 211 and whom Scipio had "treated with great honour." The command assignments matched ability and seniority to the mission.
- **Exploitation.** The pursuit was pressed until the rain intervened. The Spanish contingents' overnight desertion shows the psychological completeness of the Roman victory; Scipio had not merely beaten Hasdrubal but broken the moral compact between Carthage and its Spanish allies.

**Hasdrubal Gisco's shortcomings**

- **Paralysis in the face of the unexpected.** Hasdrubal's decision to "do nothing" as the Roman columns wheeled and advanced in their unprecedented manner was the decisive failure. He recognized that Scipio had changed the deployment; he could see the Roman legions moving toward his flanks; and he could not find a counter-move. Any attempt to reorder his own line risked confusion; sending the Libyans forward to confront the Roman wings would expose their own flanks. The rigidity was partly structural — you cannot easily reorder a 70,000-man army in real time under observation — but it also reflects a commander who had been out-thought so completely that he had no options left.
- **Reliance on the habitual deployment pattern.** Hasdrubal deployed in the same order every day, building the very predictability Scipio exploited. Had he varied his formation even once, Scipio's plan would have required revision.
- **No contingency for the flank threat.** The Libyans in the centre were the Punic reserve as well as the best troops. But they were deployed in the centre, facing the Spanish allied infantry who were moving slowly. Once the Roman wings were in contact with Hasdrubal's Spanish flanks, the Libyans had no viable role — they could not wheel outward without exposing themselves. The centre-of-mass deployment that had served Hannibal at Cannae (different battle, different army, different context) was catastrophic for Hasdrubal at Ilipa because Scipio had constructed the battlefield specifically to neutralize it.
- **Logistics.** Allowing his army to be pinned in place on the plain — hungry — for hours while the Roman skirmish line controlled the tempo was a command failure. No attempt appears to have been made to rotate troops back to eat, to withdraw under Roman pressure, or to accept that the day's engagement was not yet necessary.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — Scipio's objective was total: not to defeat Hasdrubal on the day but to end Punic military power in Spain. The deployment reversal was designed not just to win a battle but to win it decisively enough that the Spanish allied components of both armies would resolve the question of loyalty definitively. He honoured the principle in its strategic as well as tactical dimension. Hasdrubal nominally shared the objective of a decisive battle but his defensive camp position and conventional deployment suggest he was primarily oriented toward not losing rather than winning on Roman terms.
- **Offensive** — Scipio seized the initiative at every phase: the predawn deployment, the advance that denied Hasdrubal time to respond, the column manoeuvre that dictated the terms of contact. Hasdrubal, despite his superior numbers, was entirely reactive after the first morning. The principle was violated chronically on the Punic side.
- **Mass** — Scipio violated this principle in the conventional sense: he placed his weakest troops (Spanish allies) at the centre, facing the enemy's strongest. But he *applied* mass in the decisive sense — concentrating his best troops against the enemy's weakest point. The reversal was mass applied *selectively and with design*, not mass in the crude aggregate. Hasdrubal had his best troops (Libyans) in the centre against Scipio's weakest, which might have been decisive had the flanks held long enough to let the Libyans engage — but the flanks did not hold.
- **Economy of Force** — the Spanish allied centre was assigned the minimum mission: stay in contact with the Libyan foot long enough for the wings to win. They did not need to defeat the Libyans; they needed to hold them in place. This is economy of force in its most disciplined doctrinal application. On the Punic side, the Libyans in the centre were essentially wasted — they "remained unengaged for some time, inactive observers of the fighting" — a catastrophic misapplication of the best available combat power.
- **Maneuver** — Ilipa is the manoeuvre battle of the Second Punic War on the Roman side. The 90°-wheel-into-column, lateral march, then second wheel back into line placed Hasdrubal in a tactical position from which there was no recovery. The physical manoeuvre was complemented by the temporal manoeuvre — days of habit-building — and the deception of the predawn advance. The three layers together (deception, timing, physical movement) constitute a manoeuvre operation in the fullest sense.
- **Unity of Command** — single command, single plan, clarity of mission to each element. Scipio assigned the left wing to Silanus and Marcius with clear instructions; the centre's mission (move slowly, hold) was equally clear. Hasdrubal's command appears to have been unified in name but without the internal clarity to respond when the plan broke down.
- **Security** — Scipio's camp on the low hills was defensible; the outpost screen gave warning of the Punic cavalry raid on arrival, for which a concealed cavalry reserve had been prepared. The predawn operation was secured by the rapid advance of the velites, which drove in Hasdrubal's outposts before they could report the changed Roman deployment. Hasdrubal failed to secure his flanks against the Roman column manoeuvre — once committed to the standard deployment, his Spanish flanks had no depth or reserve to counter the incoming legions.
- **Surprise** — not surprise of presence (both armies had been watching each other for days) but surprise of method. The reversal of deployment — Romans on the wings, Spanish in the centre — was the operational surprise; the predawn timing was the tactical surprise. Both were achieved. Hasdrubal recognized the first surprise too late to act and never recognized the second (the predawn advance) until the opportunity to respond had passed.
- **Simplicity** — the concept was simple: put the good troops against the weak troops, and keep the weak centre out of serious trouble long enough. The execution was complex — the column manoeuvre required exact timing and exceptional discipline — but the idea behind it was expressible in one sentence. Hasdrubal's situation, by contrast, had become fundamentally simple in the worst way: no viable options remained.
- **Offensive** (revisited as a post-battle lesson): Scipio also honoured this principle in the pursuit. He pressed the retreating Punic army back to its camp before the rain intervened, then resumed the next day. The failure to destroy the enemy army in detail — Hasdrubal Gisco escaped, Masinissa escaped, Mago escaped — would have been more costly in a different strategic situation. As it was, the Spanish tribes' overnight defection made pursuit less critical than the diplomatic consolidation that followed.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

**Ilipa as a Cannae inversion — and the doctrine of deception before the assault.** The most important doctrinal observation about Ilipa is the one Goldsworthy notes only in passing: that comparisons with Cannae are "a mistake, since they were very different battles fought in very different circumstances." This is historiographically careful but doctrinally incomplete. Ilipa was not Cannae reproduced; it was Cannae *understood and inverted*. At Cannae, Hannibal put his expendable troops in the centre (Celts and Iberians) to absorb and hold the Roman mass, while his best troops (Libyans) formed the closing jaws on the wings. At Ilipa, Scipio put his less reliable troops (Spanish allies) in the centre to hold and fix the Punic best (Libyans), while his best troops (the legions) formed the closing wings against the Punic worst (Spanish warriors). Both battles achieved flank envelopment through a centre that was deliberately not the decisive element. The difference is that Scipio engineered the entire pre-battle period — days of habit-setting, the predawn advance, the deployment reversal — to create the conditions for his manoeuvre, whereas Hannibal relied on the Romans' own tactical rigidity to walk into the trap. Scipio manufactured the rigidity in his opponent through deception.

Modern doctrine captures this distinction in the concept of **shaping operations**: the activities that create the conditions for the decisive operation, often by limiting the enemy's options before the main effort begins. Scipio's days of conventional deployment were shaping operations. His cavalry raid on the Punic outposts at the start of the campaign (for which he had a concealed reserve waiting) was a shaping operation. The predawn advance that denied Hasdrubal breakfast was a shaping operation. By the time the decisive manoeuvre began, Hasdrubal had already been defeated in the information environment, the logistics environment, and the tempo environment. The battle confirmed what had already been decided.

**The double envelopment through manoeuvre rather than mass.** Cannae achieved encirclement by holding a large force in the centre while superior cavalry won the wings. Ilipa achieved outflanking through disciplined infantry manoeuvre — columns wheeling into line close to the enemy — rather than cavalry superiority. This is the prototype of what modern doctrine calls the **flanking attack through manoeuvre**: the attacking force positions itself to envelop the enemy's flank not by a strategic outflanking movement (a wide turning movement) but by a tactical formation change close to the enemy that changes the relative orientation of the forces. The risk is high (the formation change is exposed to counteraction); the payoff is immediate and decisive if the enemy cannot respond.

**Economy of force and the weak-centre concept.** Ilipa is the clearest ancient example of what modern doctrine calls **economy of force**: assigning the minimum necessary combat power to secondary efforts to free maximum combat power for the decisive effort. The Spanish allied centre was the economy-of-force element; the legionary wings were the main effort. Modern planners frequently struggle with the economy-of-force assignment because commanders do not want to give any element a "hold and accept losses" mission. Scipio did so without apparent hesitation — because his design ensured the wings would win quickly enough that the centre's ordeal would be short.

**Deception as a force multiplier.** The pre-battle period at Ilipa is the most systematic ancient example of what modern doctrine calls **military deception (MILDEC)**: deliberately conveying false information to the enemy about one's intentions to cause him to take actions favourable to the deceiver. Scipio's deception was patient (several days), consistent (the same deployment each time), and credible (it matched what both sides considered conventional). Hasdrubal cannot be blamed for believing the pattern he had seen repeated every morning. He was the victim of a deception that exploited his own reasonable assumptions. Modern doctrine distinguishes between **operational deception** (misleading the enemy about the campaign's direction) and **tactical deception** (misleading the enemy about the day's battle). Scipio achieved both simultaneously: Hasdrubal did not know the decisive battle was coming on that particular morning *or* what form it would take when it arrived.

**The commander on the decisive wing.** Scipio commanded the right wing in person — the element that would deliver the enveloping attack on Hasdrubal's weakest flank. This reflects the ancient principle, validated repeatedly in modern case studies, that the commander belongs where the decisive action is, not at the army's geometric centre. The centre (Spanish allies) was led by subordinates with clear instructions; the decisive element was led by the army commander. Modern doctrine captures this in the concept of the **commander's critical information requirements (CCIRs)** and personal positioning: the commander should be where he can observe and influence the most consequential event.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Polybius (Histories, Book XI)** is the principal source and gives the higher figure for Punic strength (70,000 infantry, 4,000 cavalry, 32 elephants). His account of the manoeuvre — the predawn deployment, the column formation, the wheeling advance — is the most detailed we have and was presumably reconstructed from participants or Scipio's own explanations. Polybius had strong pro-Scipionic sources (through Scipio Aemilianus) and his admiration for Scipio's methods as the product of "careful preparation and planning" rather than improvisation is explicit. Goldsworthy finds this framework credible: "Polybius was at great pains to emphasize Scipio's skill as a commander, that the risks he took were the result of sober calculation and not unthinking rashness."

**Livy (Ab Urbe Condita, Book XXVIII)** gives the lower figure for Punic strength (50,000 infantry, 4,500 cavalry) and a parallel account. The discrepancy with Polybius is significant — a difference of 20,000 infantry. Modern scholars generally cannot resolve this with confidence; neither figure can be independently verified. The Punic order of battle was clearly very large, probably the largest concentration of force Carthage deployed in Spain during the war. Goldsworthy cites both figures without adjudicating decisively.

**The casualty vacuum.** Neither Polybius nor Livy preserves casualty figures for Ilipa. Goldsworthy notes this without resolving it. The absence is unusual for a major engagement and may reflect either source damage or the nature of the Punic collapse — a disintegration and dissolution rather than a discrete moment of slaughter at which losses could be measured. The Spanish contingents' overnight desertion after the battle effectively ended the campaign without a second engagement in which prisoners and dead might have been counted.

**Scholarly debate on the manoeuvre.** The precise mechanics of Scipio's column manoeuvre have been debated extensively. Goldsworthy acknowledges that "the details of the manoeuvre performed by the Roman army has, like so many other aspects of the war, been endlessly debated by scholars. All agree that it demonstrated a far higher standard of corporate discipline than Roman armies had possessed in the early years of the war." The debate centres on exactly where the columns halted, how far they had marched along the Punic front before wheeling back into line, and how close to the enemy this occurred. Goldsworthy's reconstruction — columns marched until the Roman legions were level with the edges of Hasdrubal's Spanish infantry, then wheeled 90° inward — is the most defensible reading of Polybius and is followed here.

**The Cannae comparison.** Goldsworthy explicitly cautions against comparing Ilipa to Cannae, arguing they were fundamentally different battles. This is correct as a historical observation — different armies, different terrain, different troop quality, no encirclement at Ilipa in the strict Cannae sense (the Punic army broke and retreated rather than being surrounded). But the *doctrinal* relationship — Scipio consciously modelling his deployment logic on Hannibal's principle while adapting it to Roman tactical capability — is supported by the pattern of decisions. That Scipio studied Cannae is attested by the outcome at Zama, where his manipulation of the battle space again reflected Hannibalic principles turned against a Hannibalic army.
