# Third Punic War (149–146 BCE)

**Narrative overview:** [[events/third-punic-war-149-146bce]]

Hub for the analysis of the final war — in practice a **single great siege**. Rome,
having decided that a revived Carthage must cease to exist (*Carthago delenda est*,
the refrain of [[cato-the-elder|Cato the Elder]]), extracted hostages and the city's
weapons by cynical ultimatum and then demanded that the Carthaginians abandon their
city and resettle inland. They refused, re-armed in weeks, and held out for nearly
four years behind 20+ miles of walls before [[scipio-aemilianus|Scipio Aemilianus]]
stormed and razed the city in 146.

## Strategic shape of the war

- **The cynical road to war (149).** Carthage, recovered economically and provoked
  into an "illegal" war with Rome's ally [[masinissa|Masinissa]] of Numidia, gave
  Rome its pretext. Rome escalated demands — hostages, all arms, then evacuation —
  until Carthage chose to fight from a position of near-total disarmament.
- **The frustrated siege (149–147).** The great fortifications and seaward access
  defeated the first consular armies; Roman assaults failed and disease and
  Carthaginian sorties (under Hasdrubal) prolonged the stalemate. Rome's military
  reputation suffered until Scipio Aemilianus — grandson by adoption of
  [[scipio-africanus|Africanus]] — was given the command.
- **The fall (147–146).** Scipio sealed the city off with siege lines and a mole
  across the harbour mouth, starved it, and then took it street by street in days of
  brutal urban fighting up to the Byrsa citadel. The survivors were enslaved and the
  city destroyed; its territory became the province of *Africa*.

## Significance

The Third War is the model of a war of **deliberate annihilation** and of
**siegecraft and urban assault** rather than field manoeuvre; per
[[goldsworthy-the-fall-of-carthage-2000|Goldsworthy]] it also shows the limits of
Roman engineering against a great fortified city until total blockade was achieved.
It completed Rome's western Mediterranean supremacy and supplied the wiki's
[[controversies/roman-grand-strategy|Roman grand strategy]] debate with its starkest
case of pre-emptive destruction.
