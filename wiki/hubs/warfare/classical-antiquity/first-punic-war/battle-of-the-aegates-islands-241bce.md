# Battle of the Aegates Islands (241 BC) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/first-punic-war-264-241bce|First Punic War event page]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/first-punic-war/first-punic-war|First Punic War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

The Aegates Islands ended twenty-three years of the costliest naval war in the ancient world. It was not decided by a subtler tactical design than its predecessors — there was no envelopment, no dramatic reversal. It was decided by a systematic asymmetry built before the first oar was pulled: Rome sent an experienced fleet in fighting trim against a Carthaginian relief force burdened by its own cargo. This page analyses how that structural advantage was created, how the battle was managed under adverse conditions, and what the engagement teaches about sea control as the lever of strategic decision.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

By late 243 BCE the First Punic War had reached operational stalemate. [[roman-republic|Rome]] had abandoned large-scale naval operations after catastrophic losses at the [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/first-punic-war/battle-of-the-bagradas-255bce|Bagradas]] and at Drepana (249), and to storms that had consumed hundreds of ships and tens of thousands of men. [[carthage|Carthage]], which had held naval superiority since Drepana, had made little aggressive use of it: a few coastal raids on Italy, minor skirmishing, nothing decisive. [[hamilcar-barca|Hamilcar Barca]]'s army remained in the field on western Sicily, containing Roman siege operations around Lilybaeum and Drepana but unable to break them. Both powers were exhausted; neither had a clear path to victory.

**Rome (Catulus)** — Rome's strategic objective was to force a decision. The State could not fund a new fleet from public revenues, so 200 quinqueremes were financed by private subscription from the Roman elite — an interest-free patriotic loan to be repaid from the eventual peace indemnity. All were modelled on the captured ship of Hannibal the Rhodian, a Carthaginian captain who had spectacularly run the Roman blockade of Lilybaeum and whose vessel was considered the finest design available. The political and military calculus was explicit: Polybius states that the main Roman objective was to provoke a major encounter with the Carthaginian fleet, since its defeat would deliver a greater blow than any further success in Sicily. Catulus crossed to the island with his new fleet in 242, seized the harbour at Drepana, and cut Hamilcar's army off from resupply by sea. This was an invitation to battle, not merely a blockade.

**Carthage (Hanno)** — Carthage's objectives for the relief fleet were sequential. First priority: deliver grain and supplies to Hamilcar's isolated army and the remaining Punic garrisons. Only once the supplies had been offloaded was the fleet to take on board the pick of Hamilcar's veteran soldiers as marines — and only then seek out and destroy the Roman fleet. This sequencing was the operational flaw the battle would expose. A Punic fleet that had run supplies first and fought second would have been a formidable force; the fleet that actually fought at the Aegates did neither.

The commander appointed to this mission, Hanno, may have been the same officer who had presided over the Carthaginian defeats at Agrigentum (261) and Ecnomus (256) — Goldsworthy notes the identification is uncertain but the possibility is pointed. What is not in doubt is that the fleet he brought was assembled hastily, crewed by men not recently in naval service, and committed to battle before the supply run it had been designed to complete.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

**Geographic setting.** The Aegates Islands (modern Egadi archipelago) lie off the extreme western tip of Sicily, roughly twelve miles from the port of Drepana. Their significance in the battle plan was topographic: they provided a staging point from which a fleet running before a westerly wind could reach Drepana or Eryx rapidly, before a Roman fleet based at Lilybaeum could intercept. The Carthaginians had used exactly this route in 250, when Hannibal son of Hamilcar had successfully run fifty ships into Lilybaeum from this anchorage. Hanno parked his fleet at the westernmost of the islands, called "the Holy Island," awaiting the right wind.

**Weather — the double-edged factor.** On 10 March 241 a strong wind blew from the west — precisely the breeze Hanno had waited for. For the Punic fleet, carrying sails, this was an opportunity: running before the wind, the supply-laden ships could reach Sicily quickly under canvas with minimal crew effort. For Catulus it was the opposite: his rowers would have to battle a heavy swell to intercept, and the seas were running high. Goldsworthy notes that Catulus faced a difficult decision — past Roman commanders who had treated the elements carelessly had presided over spectacular disasters. Yet delay meant the Carthaginians would link up with Hamilcar's veterans. Catulus chose to fight.

The weather that seemed to favour Hanno proved a trap. His ships, laden with grain, could not manoeuvre well in the high swell. Their crews, poorly trained and already strained by a crossing in difficult conditions, were in no state to fight an oared battle. The same wind that had hurried them to their intercept position made them ungainly targets.

**Intelligence.** Catulus received a report of the Carthaginian fleet's arrival at the Aegates before it moved. This gave him time to take on board extra marines drawn from the army and position himself at a second island in the group to intercept. The Carthaginians, by contrast, seem not to have known that Catulus had moved to cut them off: their plan relied on surprise and speed, and they got neither. The wind that rose on 10 March was their signal to act, but it also signalled Catulus to come out.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | Rome (Catulus) | Carthage (Hanno) |
|---|---|---|
| Total vessels | ~200 quinqueremes (Polybius); 300 (Diodorus — disputed) | ~250 ships (Polybius) |
| Ship condition | Lightened for battle; no supplies carried; masts probably left ashore | Laden with grain and military supplies |
| Crews | Experienced; intensively trained for approximately one year | Poorly trained; possibly undermanned |
| Marines | Substantial — extra contingent drawn from the army before battle | Few; the plan was to embark Hamilcar's veterans *after* delivering supplies |
| Command | Catulus; praetor Quintus Valerius Falto second in command | Hanno |
| Tactical posture | Seeking engagement; line formed to intercept | Seeking to reach Sicily before interception; sails raised |

**Deployment.** The Roman fleet formed a battle line to intercept the Carthaginian approach to Sicily. When the Punic ships realised interception was inevitable, they lowered their sails and unstepped their masts to prepare for an oared battle — a manoeuvre that cost time and revealed that even their preparation for action was reactive. Polybius says the Punic crews "yelled encouragement to each other as they bore down on the enemy" — a detail that implies disorder rather than confidence, men psyching themselves up for a fight they had not expected to have in these circumstances.

The Roman line was formed on the open sea, in a heavy swell, by crews that Catulus had spent nearly a year preparing for exactly this moment. The asymmetry in readiness was total.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Phase 1 — Interception and the Punic choice.** The Carthaginian fleet, sails set and running before a westerly, was intercepted by the Roman fleet before it could reach Sicilian waters and link up with Hamilcar. Realising that the Romans had anticipated their approach, Hanno's captains lowered sails and unstepped masts to prepare for battle. This transition — from an auxiliary-powered supply run to an oared naval engagement — was itself an indicator of the disparity: Roman ships had already stripped their masts and were configured for ramming; the Punic transition cost time and effort that a well-trained fleet would have managed more smoothly.

2. **Phase 2 — Main engagement.** The battle was decided quickly. Roman ships, faster and more manoeuvrable despite the swell — because they carried no cargo and their crews were fit and trained — pressed the attack. The Carthaginian ships were overburdened, their crews undertrained, their marines few. Polybius states the difference was "quickly apparent." The Romans combined the rowers' advantage in speed and manoeuvrability with a superiority in boarding action, given the additional marine contingent Catulus had taken on just before the battle. The Punic fleet had no answer to either form of engagement — it could neither ram effectively nor resist boarding.

3. **Phase 3 — The Punic collapse and the wind shift.** Fifty Carthaginian ships were sunk and seventy captured before the battle's character changed. Fortunately for the survivors, the wind shifted during the engagement, moving to an easterly. This allowed many of the remaining Punic ships to raise their masts and sails again and escape downwind. The Romans, who had deliberately prepared for an oared battle and were probably not carrying their masts, could not pursue under sail and were unable to follow far into the open sea against an easterly in heavy conditions. The pursuit was therefore limited. Goldsworthy notes that the excavation of ancient wrecks near Marsala (the Punic warship finds) suggests the Roman pursuit may nonetheless have been somewhat more effective than the literary sources imply, since those ships appear to have been sunk after the main engagement, possibly while fleeing.

4. **Phase 4 — Aftermath and strategic consequence.** Catulus returned to Lilybaeum with his prizes. The victory was immediately decisive at the strategic level: Hamilcar's army was now completely isolated, with no prospect of resupply or reinforcement. Carthage, which Polybius says lacked either the will or the resources to build another fleet and try again, opened peace negotiations.

**Key tactical mechanisms.** The Roman advantage operated at every level simultaneously: faster ships (through training, not design), superior crews (through sustained exercise), more marines (through last-minute reinforcement), and a fighting-trim configuration (through deliberate choice to carry no cargo). The Carthaginian disadvantage was equally total: every dimension of fighting power — speed, manoeuvre, boarding strength, crew readiness — had been sacrificed to the supply mission's requirements. In this sense the battle was won before it was fought.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | Rome | Carthage |
|---|---|---|
| Ships sunk | 30 (Diodorus only) | 50 (Polybius); 20 of 117 total lost (Diodorus) |
| Ships captured | 50 crippled (Diodorus) | 70 (Polybius); 97 (Diodorus) |
| Men killed | Not given by Polybius; implied by ship losses | Proportionally high — rough seas meant men drowned when ships were rammed and foundered |
| Prisoners | — | ~10,000 (Polybius); ~6,000 (Philinus, via Diodorus); ~4,040 (other sources cited by Diodorus) |
| Source(s) | Diodorus Siculus (Polybius silent on Roman losses) | Polybius Bk I; Diodorus Siculus |

The figures require careful handling (see §9). Polybius gives 50 Punic ships sunk and 70 captured but does not mention Roman losses, implying the Roman fleet took minimal damage. Diodorus adds 30 Roman ships sunk and 50 crippled, against 117 Punic ships lost total (20 sunk, 97 captured). The discrepancy in Punic losses (Polybius: 120; Diodorus: 117) is minor; the presence of Roman losses only in Diodorus — and his much larger Roman fleet figure (300 ships) — suggests either a different source tradition or that Diodorus is combining data from multiple engagements.

The low prisoner figures relative to the ships lost are notable and have been used to argue that the Punic crews were undermanned. Goldsworthy offers an alternative: the rough seas meant that when ships were rammed and sank in a swell, more men drowned than in a calm-water engagement, reducing the number available for capture. Both factors may have been in play simultaneously.

**Strategic result.** Hamilcar Barca's forces on Sicily were now utterly isolated. Carthage lacked the resources — or perhaps the manpower — to build another fleet. The Punic aristocracy, unlike Rome's elite, did not step forward to fund a replacement from private means. Carthage conceded defeat and opened peace negotiations. The terms, initially negotiated by Catulus (whose year of office was nearly expired) and subsequently toughened by a senatorial commission, required Carthage to evacuate all of Sicily, pay a war indemnity ultimately set at 3,200 Euboean talents (1,000 immediately, the rest over ten years), and surrender all Roman prisoners without ransom. Carthage had to pay a ransom for its own. Sicily became Rome's first overseas province — the template for what would become an empire.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Roman success factors**

- **The private-subscription fleet.** The decision to build 200 new quinqueremes by private loan when the State could not afford it was the single most consequential act of the entire war's final phase. It converted Rome's manpower depth and political will into naval form without requiring treasury funding. The patriotism of the Roman elite was materially decisive.
- **Training as a force multiplier.** Catulus refused to waste his sailors in siege-work at Lilybaeum. Goldsworthy is explicit: sailors were kept healthy, well-fed, and exercised at sea daily for the better part of a year. By 241 the Roman fleet was in "superb condition, its crews experienced and skilled." This was a deliberate command choice that reversed the chronic Roman disadvantage in seamanship.
- **Copying the best Punic design.** All 200 ships were modelled on Hannibal the Rhodian's captured vessel — the finest Carthaginian warship available. Rome competed on equal or superior terms technically, not just in numbers.
- **Stripping for battle.** On the day of the engagement, the Roman fleet was lightened for action: no cargo, extra marines from the army, masts likely ashore. Every capacity decision pointed toward fighting. The contrast with the laden Punic fleet was total.
- **The decision to intercept in bad weather.** Catulus chose to fight in a heavy swell when a cautious commander might have waited. His judgment that delay would allow the Carthaginians to link up with Hamilcar — and thus fight under far more favourable conditions — was correct and decisive. The swell that made his rowers work hard made the grain-laden Punic ships almost unmanoeuvrable.
- **Last-minute marine reinforcement.** Adding army marines at the Aegates anchorage just before battle gave the Roman fleet a boarding superiority that the Carthaginian fleet, with few marines, could not match. The increment was tactically decisive in every close-quarters action.

**Carthaginian shortcomings**

- **Sequential objectives, simultaneous problems.** The plan to deliver supplies first and fight second assumed the Romans would allow that sequence to run. They did not. The fleet arrived at the Aegates laden with grain and had to fight an oared battle while carrying cargo it had not yet delivered — the worst possible configuration.
- **Operational neglect.** After regaining naval superiority at Drepana in 249, Carthage made little use of its fleet and allowed it to atrophy. The navy that gathered in 243 was not a maintained force; it was an emergency mobilisation of ships and men who had not been in regular service. The training gap with the Romans was, for the first time in the war, reversed.
- **Manning shortfalls.** Whether the ships were literally undermanned or whether the low prisoner figures reflect drowning in rough seas, the Punic crews were insufficient for the task. Goldsworthy notes "it is also possible that many crews were under strength, although certainty is impossible."
- **The intelligence failure.** The Carthaginians did not know Catulus had advanced to the Aegates to intercept them. Their plan depended on arriving undetected and reaching Sicily before Roman reaction; when that assumption failed, they had no fallback. They fought a battle of interception — exactly the type most disadvantageous to a laden, undertrained fleet.
- **No private mobilisation of resources.** When the crisis was clear, the Punic aristocracy did not replicate what Roman elite families had done — commit private wealth to rebuild naval power. Whether this reflected exhaustion, political dysfunction, or a rational assessment that the situation was unwinnable, the result was that Carthage had no second fleet.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective.** Rome's objective was clear and singular: force a naval battle that would isolate Hamilcar and end the war. Catulus built every decision — training, ship design, the seizure of Drepana harbour — toward creating the conditions for that engagement. Carthage's objective was ambiguous: deliver supplies and *then* seek battle. The two-phase plan meant the fleet was optimised for neither mission when battle came.

- **Offensive.** Rome seized and held the operational initiative after 242 despite years of passivity: the new fleet, the seizure of Drepana harbour, and the advance to the Aegates anchorage were all offensive moves. Carthage reacted to Roman moves throughout the final campaign, arriving at the Aegates in response to the isolation of Hamilcar rather than as an initiative of its own choosing.

- **Mass.** Carthage had the larger fleet — approximately 250 ships to Rome's 200. That quantitative advantage was entirely negated by the condition of the ships and the quality of the crews. Rome honoured the true principle: not numerical mass but concentrated, effective combat power at the decisive point. The Roman fleet was smaller but every ship was ready to fight; the Punic fleet's mass was diluted by cargo.

- **Economy of Force.** Catulus allocated his forces with precision: the main fleet for interception and battle, the army contingent stripped out as marine reinforcement at the last moment, the remainder of the land force maintaining the blockade of Lilybaeum. Nothing was wasted. Carthage violated this principle by loading its fighting vessels with supplies — committing warship capacity to a logistics mission at the moment those ships were needed for combat.

- **Maneuver.** The Roman advance from Lilybaeum to the Aegates to cut off the Punic approach was the decisive manoeuvre of the engagement — executed before a single ship was rammed. On the tactical level, Roman ships were faster and more manoeuvrable than their laden opponents. The Punic fleet's forced transition from sailing formation to oared battle line was the antithesis of manoeuvre: reactive, slow, and conducted under pressure.

- **Unity of Command.** Catulus commanded the fleet with Falto as his praetor subordinate. The subsequent bickering between the two over credit for the victory — Falto later claiming Catulus was incapacitated by a thigh wound suffered outside Lilybaeum — suggests personal rivalry, but it did not impair the conduct of the battle. Hanno's command arrangements are not detailed in the sources, but the disorganised Punic response suggests either poor command and control or that the fleet's formation was irretrievably compromised by its cargo before any command decisions could matter.

- **Security.** Catulus maintained security of information sufficiently to move his fleet from Lilybaeum to the Aegates without the Carthaginians learning of his position in time to respond. The Punic fleet, by contrast, was caught in its transit configuration — a state of maximum vulnerability — because its commanders did not know the Romans were waiting. The failure of Punic intelligence was also a failure of security for the Romans to exploit.

- **Surprise.** Catulus achieved operational surprise: the Carthaginians arrived expecting to make a swift run to Sicily under sail and found instead a Roman battle line in their path. The reversal was complete — the force that had come expecting to set the terms of engagement found itself fighting on the enemy's terms in conditions that maximised Roman advantages. Hanno had surprise taken from him by the same intelligence failure that left him tactically vulnerable.

- **Simplicity.** The Roman plan was admirably simple: build a good fleet, train it thoroughly, position it to intercept, and fight. The Punic plan was comparatively complex — a two-phase operation requiring a successful supply delivery before battle — and its complexity proved fatal when circumstances prevented the first phase from completing. In naval warfare, as on land, plans with sequential dependencies are vulnerable to disruption at any hinge; the simpler single-purpose Roman plan had no such vulnerability.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

**Sea control as the decisive lever.** The Aegates Islands is an early and unusually clear historical example of what modern naval theory calls **sea control** — the ability to use a body of water for one's own purposes while denying its use to the enemy. Rome did not win the battle by some tactical genius at the point of contact; it won by achieving sea control around western Sicily before Hanno arrived. The seizure of Drepana harbour in 242 was the key move: it cut Hamilcar's logistical umbilical and forced Carthage to fight for the resupply line rather than choosing when and whether to fight. The battle of 10 March 241 was the inevitable result of that earlier sea-control operation.

This maps directly onto modern maritime doctrine. Alfred Thayer Mahan's foundational argument — that command of the sea, achieved by battle-fleet action, is the prerequisite for projecting power ashore — finds one of its earliest validations here. The Roman fleet in 242–241 was acting as a Mahanian force: it sought the decisive engagement not as an end in itself but to eliminate the Punic fleet as a threat and thereby secure the sea lines of communication that would starve Hamilcar into submission. The sequence — build the fleet, train the crews, seize the key harbour, force the decisive engagement — is recognisable in modern fleet doctrine.

**Logistics as strategy.** The Aegates engagement is also a case study in what modern doctrine calls **interdiction of supply lines.** Hamilcar's army on Sicily was the strategic centre of gravity of the Carthaginian war effort. Once his sea lines of communication were cut by the Roman seizure of Drepana, he was operationally dead regardless of his tactical skill on land — and Goldsworthy considers Hamilcar the ablest commander on either side in the entire war. The battle did not destroy Hamilcar's force; it made that force irrelevant. This is the logic of blockade and interdiction carried to its strategic conclusion: defeat the enemy not by engaging his main strength directly, but by cutting what sustains it.

Modern joint doctrine (JP 3-0) treats interdiction of enemy logistics as a primary task of air and maritime forces precisely because it can achieve strategic effect without the costs of a frontal engagement. The Roman strategy against Hamilcar in 241 anticipates this logic across two and a half millennia.

**The war-ending battle.** The Aegates also illustrates the conditions under which a single engagement can be genuinely war-terminating — a problem that preoccupies modern strategists precisely because such battles are rare. The engagement worked as a war-ender because: (1) it destroyed an irreplaceable asset — the Punic relief fleet — that Carthage could not rebuild; (2) it made the continuation of a contested ground campaign in Sicily physically impossible by severing Hamilcar's supply; and (3) it coincided with Carthage's political exhaustion after twenty-three years of attrition. The Romans had their decisive battle because they had engineered the conditions for one — not by tactical brilliance at the Aegates but by two years of patient reconstruction, training, and positional pressure.

Contrast this with the strategic disappointment of Cannae (216 BCE), where Hannibal achieved a far more spectacular tactical annihilation and gained nothing decisive. The difference was that Rome at Cannae retained its manpower reserves and allied network, so the battle was not war-terminating even though it was almost incomprehensibly bloody. At the Aegates, Carthage had neither reserve fleet nor the will to build one; the asymmetry of exhaustion made the battle decisive where Cannae was not.

**Training as a decisive operational variable.** The year Catulus spent drilling his crews before seeking battle is the most striking single lesson of the campaign from a modern force-development perspective. The Roman navy in 241 was materially no better than the fleet Carthage had been building for two years — both sides were drawing on the same Punic hull design. The difference was entirely in crew quality. Catulus deliberately insulated his sailors from the usual drain of siege labour and maintained their conditioning and skill. The result was a fleet that could fight in a heavy swell against an opponent that could not. Modern doctrine on pre-deployment training, readiness cycles, and the distinction between "forces in being" and "effective forces" all have their ancient parallel in this case.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Polybius (Histories, Book I)** is the primary source for the battle and the dominant authority for the First Punic War as a whole. Writing within two generations of the events, with access to both Roman and Carthaginian informants and to documentary sources, he provides the most coherent narrative of the late war: the Roman fleet-building by private subscription, Catulus' training programme, the Carthaginian assembly of the relief fleet, the conditions on 10 March, and the battle's outcome. His figures — 50 Punic ships sunk, 70 captured, ~10,000 prisoners — are the baseline. His silence on Roman losses is notable and almost certainly means Roman casualties were too small to report, not that he suppressed them.

Polybius' biases are familiar: pro-Roman in structural framing (Roman persistence and determination are his recurring theme), and inclined to explain Carthaginian failure through institutional character rather than specific command error. His famous observation that Romans rely on brute force (*bia*), throwing massive resources at problems and accepting high costs, occurs in the storm narrative of 255 BCE but colours the whole war's interpretation. His closing summary of Punic losses (nearer 500 warships) against Roman losses (about 700) underlines the paradox he develops throughout: Rome lost far more ships than Carthage, mostly to weather, yet won by refusing to stop.

**Diodorus Siculus** preserves a partially independent tradition, probably drawing on Philinus of Agrigentum (a pro-Carthaginian contemporary historian whose work survives only in fragments via Polybius and Diodorus). Diodorus' figures diverge significantly from Polybius: 30 Roman ships sunk, 50 crippled; 20 Punic ships sunk, 97 captured (total 117 vs Polybius' 120). He also records a Roman fleet of 300 ships, which is at variance with the 200 quinqueremes Polybius describes as built in 243. The 300-ship figure probably reflects either an error in Diodorus' source or the inclusion of older vessels added to the core fleet. His prisoner figures — 6,000 from Philinus, 4,040 from other (unnamed) sources — are lower than Polybius' 10,000, and the divergence across multiple ancient traditions on this single figure suggests genuine uncertainty rather than simple copying error.

**Reconciling the sources.** The two accounts are compatible in their broad outlines — both describe a decisive Roman victory with major Punic losses, rough-weather conditions, a wind shift that allowed Punic survivors to escape, and subsequent peace negotiations. The specific numbers are irreconcilable and should be treated as order-of-magnitude indicators rather than precise counts. Goldsworthy's approach — accepting Polybius as the more reliable structural narrator while noting Diodorus preserves data from a second (Philinus-derived) tradition — is the appropriate scholarly handling. The low prisoner figures across all sources, and Goldsworthy's suggestion that rough-water sinkings drowned more men than usual, is more plausible than the alternative (universal undermanning), though both factors probably contributed.

The dispute over Roman losses between Polybius (silent) and Diodorus (30 sunk, 50 crippled) cannot be resolved from the sources. Goldsworthy notes that Catulus and Falto both celebrated triumphs, which suggests Roman losses were not politically damaging — consistent with a battle in which the decisive outcome was entirely one-sided.

**The post-battle controversy.** Falto's later claim that Catulus had been incapacitated by a wound — suffered not in the Aegates engagement but in a preliminary skirmish outside Lilybaeum — was an element of the command-credit dispute between the two. Both were allowed to triumph. The wound story is difficult to verify and may be Falto's post-hoc attempt to claim that he had effectively commanded the fleet on the day. Goldsworthy treats it as probable that the wound existed but uncertain that it affected Catulus' direction of the battle.

**Polybius on the war's totals.** The closing summary in Polybius — ~700 Roman warships and ~500 Carthaginian warships lost across twenty-three years — has been doubted on the grounds that the numbers seem too large. Goldsworthy accepts them as broadly plausible given what is known of individual engagements: Roman losses were disproportionately from storms (the disasters of 255, 253, and 249), Punic losses more evenly distributed between battle and weather. The paradox — Rome losing more ships but winning the war — is central to Polybius' interpretation of Roman character and is one of the most significant strategic judgements in ancient historiography.
