# Battle of Ecnomus (256 BC) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/first-punic-war-264-241bce|First Punic War — full event page]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/first-punic-war/first-punic-war|First Punic War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

Ecnomus was, by any measure of combatants engaged, the largest naval battle in antiquity and perhaps in all of pre-modern history. It was fought in 256 BCE off the southern coast of Sicily as a Roman invasion fleet attempted to carry the war directly to [[carthage|Carthage]] on the African shore. The battle is analytically significant on three levels: it demonstrates the disruptive effect of a single technological innovation (the corvus boarding bridge) on an established naval tradition; it shows how a carefully designed tactical formation can survive being broken up and still convert local successes into decisive victory; and it illustrates the limits of superior seamanship when strategic boldness tips scale in favour of an operationally simpler opponent. This page analyses the conduct of the engagement in detail. The broader narrative of the First Punic War — causes, the African campaign, the storms of 255, and the war's conclusion — belongs to [[events/first-punic-war-264-241bce]] and [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/first-punic-war/first-punic-war]].

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

By 257 BCE the First Punic War had settled into attritional stalemate in Sicily. The [[roman-republic|Roman Republic]] had proved it could defeat Carthaginian land forces and even Punic fleets — the Battle of Mylae in 260 had delivered Rome's first naval triumph, won almost entirely by the corvus — but it could not force a decision so long as [[carthage|Carthage]] retained its fleet and its fortified strongholds in western Sicily. Rome's response was characteristically escalatory: mount a direct invasion of North Africa, threaten Carthage itself, and compel the enemy to negotiate on Roman terms rather than fight a grinding twenty-three-year war of attrition.

**Rome (Manlius Vulso and Marcus Atilius Regulus)** — primary objective: escort an invasion army across the sea to Africa and deliver it intact. Secondary: defeat whatever Carthaginian fleet was sent to intercept them, clearing the sea lane for subsequent supply and reinforcement. Both consuls sailed with the fleet, a measure of the Senate's commitment to a decisive result. The fleet loaded 120 marines per quinquereme — far above the normal complement — because a major encounter was anticipated and the marines would also constitute the first wave of the African landing force. Horse transports were towed alongside the warships to carry the cavalry component of the expeditionary force.

**Carthage (Hamilcar and Hanno)** — primary objective: prevent the Roman invasion fleet from reaching Africa. A Roman landing would threaten the city's immediate hinterland, force the recall of forces from Sicily, and potentially compel peace on Roman terms. The Punic commanders had resolved to fight a fleet action off Sicily rather than allow the Romans to cross unopposed. Their assembled fleet — on Polybius' figures the largest force Carthage had ever put to sea — gave them numerical superiority, and confidence in their crews' greater skill and shiphandling experience encouraged belief in the outcome. The Carthaginian strategic concept was to exploit that skill advantage by breaking up the compact Roman formation into a series of smaller, isolated engagements where ramming tactics could be used to best effect and the corvus neutralised.

The two objectives were directly incompatible. One fleet had to be destroyed.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

**Ground (sea conditions):** The battle was fought close inshore along the southern Sicilian coast near Cape Ecnomus. The proximity of the Sicilian shore mattered operationally in the battle's final phase: the Carthaginian left wing, in pressing the Roman third squadron, drove it against the coast, where the Romans were able to reform with their bows facing outward — a hedgehog position the Carthaginians declined to attack frontally for fear of the corvus. The shallow water and the shore ultimately became a trap for the Punic left when the Roman first and second squadrons arrived to close the pocket.

**Weather and season:** The battle was fought in summer, consistent with the Mediterranean sailing season and the rhythm of galley operations. No severe weather is recorded. Conditions were calm enough for the elaborate Roman formation — with its towed horse transports and four distinct squadrons maintaining geometric relationships — to be kept under control during the approach phase, which speaks to sea states permitting formation sailing.

**Intelligence:** Both fleets were aware of the other's general position and intentions. The Romans knew the Carthaginian fleet had moved from Lilybaeum toward Heraclea Minoa and were sailing to intercept them. The Carthaginians knew the Romans were heading south along the Sicilian shore with a full invasion force. What neither side could fully know in advance was the exact deployment the other would adopt. Hamilcar's decision to modify the Punic line of battle once the Roman formation came into view — bending the left wing toward shore and extending the right wing beyond the Roman flank — was a response to what he actually saw. On the Roman side, the consuls' identification of the Punic centre as "thinner" (Polybius' description) and their decision to attack it directly was an intelligence read made in the moments before engagement.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

### Fleet Strengths

| Element | Rome | Carthage |
|---|---|---|
| Total ships | ~330 (Polybius 1.25) | ~350 (Polybius 1.25) |
| Principal type | Quinqueremes (majority); two 'sixes' as flagships; some smaller vessels | Quinqueremes (majority); some smaller types |
| Marines per warship | 120 (elevated for expected battle and African landing) | Fewer than Roman complement; no recorded figure |
| Total personnel | ~140,000 (Polybius 1.26) | ~150,000 (Polybius 1.26) |
| Horse transports | Present, towed behind warships | None mentioned |

*Both totals are disputed: Tarn and Thiel reduced each fleet by approximately 100 ships, placing the engagement in the range of 230–250 ships per side. Goldsworthy accepts Polybius' broad figures as approximately correct while acknowledging the bias inherent in his sources.*

### Roman Formation

The Romans divided their fleet into four squadrons ("legions" in Polybius' account, though the term bore no relation to actual unit size). The arrangement was geometrically precise and tactically integrated:

- **First and Second Squadrons** — led by the two consuls' flagships (the 'sixes'), arranged in echelon: each ship's prow set behind and to the side of the ship ahead, forming two converging lines that together constituted the apex of a large triangle pointing toward the enemy.
- **Third Squadron** — drawn up in line abreast across the base of the triangle, with each ship towing a horse transport. This was the slowest and most encumbered element.
- **Fourth Squadron (the** ***triarii***)** — a deeper line arrayed behind the third, overlapping its flanks on both sides, acting as rear guard and ultimate reserve.

The result was a single compact wedge pointing forward, with its rear and flanks covered by the fourth squadron and with the cumbersome transports shielded inside the formation. Polybius praised this arrangement for its "practicality": it was dense enough to keep the fleet cohesive, but structured so the squadrons could wheel to face threats from multiple directions. That this sophisticated formation was achievable at all testified to how far Roman crews had developed since Mylae six years earlier.

### Carthaginian Deployment

| Wing / Element | Commander | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Left wing (~¼ of fleet) | Unnamed | Extended toward Sicilian shore; initially faced the Roman third squadron |
| Centre | Hamilcar | Main body; ordered to withdraw on first contact to draw Romans forward |
| Right wing | Hanno | Fastest ships; extended beyond the Roman formation's right flank |

The Punic line was originally in line abreast. On sighting the Roman formation, the Carthaginians modified it: the left wing angled toward the coast, the right wing (Hanno's fastest ships) was pushed outward to envelop the Roman flank, and the centre under Hamilcar was deliberately thinned — left with wider intervals between ships — with standing orders to make a fighting withdrawal once the Romans attacked, drawing the leading Roman squadrons forward and away from their rear.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

### Phase 1 — The Roman Charge and Hamilcar's Feigned Retreat

The Roman consuls, commanding the first and second squadrons from their 'six' flagships, identified the Punic centre as the weak point — Polybius notes it appeared "thinner" than the wings. They led a direct charge at this apparently vulnerable centre, the two consular squadrons driving straight ahead at the apex of their triangular formation. Hamilcar executed his planned withdrawal: the Punic centre ships turned and fled before the Romans, so that "a large gap swiftly developed between the consuls' ships and the third squadron" still encumbered by its towed horse transports. The Roman first and second squadrons surged forward into what they took to be a breaking enemy. In fact they were being drawn into open water, progressively isolated from their rear.

When Hamilcar judged the separation sufficient, he signalled his ships to reverse and engage. A fierce fight developed. Both consuls were personally active in the fighting — their presence and their 'sixes' were a rallying point that prevented the forward squadrons from losing cohesion as the Punic ships turned. The Carthaginian ships were faster and some may have threaded through gaps in the Roman line to deliver rams from the stern. But whenever a Punic ship came within range, the Roman corvi came into play: the heavy boarding bridges dropped onto enemy decks, their spiked beaks fixing into the planking, and Roman marines poured across.

### Phase 2 — The Three-Way Split

While the centre was engaged, the two Punic wings executed their assignments simultaneously:

- **Hanno's right wing** swept around the Roman formation and fell on the fourth squadron — the *triarii* — from beyond their flank, pressing them hard.
- **The Punic left wing** changed its alignment to face the approaching Roman third squadron and closed with it. The third squadron cast adrift its horse transports to free itself for battle, but it was now fighting without support against a fresh enemy on its own.

As Polybius observed, the battle had dissolved into three separate and widely spaced actions: the consuls' two squadrons fighting Hamilcar's centre in open water; the *triarii* under pressure from Hanno's wing in the north; and the third squadron being driven back against the Sicilian shore by the Punic left. This three-way separation was precisely what Hamilcar had designed. The question was whether the Romans, once split, could convert their local engagements faster than the Carthaginians could exploit the isolation.

### Phase 3 — Hamilcar's Centre Breaks

The first engagement to be resolved was the one in the open sea. Though the Punic ships were faster and some won individual ramming duels, the Roman boarding tactics — anchored by the corvus — proved decisive in the aggregate. Several Carthaginian ships were grappled and their crews overwhelmed. The superior numbers of Roman marines (120 per ship rather than the smaller Punic complement) meant that each boarding action tended to go in Rome's favour. Hamilcar's centre gave up the struggle and fled. At this point the Roman command demonstrated the quality that Goldsworthy singles out as genuinely remarkable: rather than remaining to secure prizes, Manlius Vulso supervised the collection of captured ships while Regulus "led as many ships as he could back to the aid of the rest of the Roman fleet" — a rapid regrouping under battle conditions that required both commanders to act independently and correctly without communication.

### Phase 4 — The Converging Counter-attack and Decision

Regulus' ships arrived behind Hanno's right wing while it was still pressing the *triarii*. The two Roman forces caught the Punic right between them and drove it off. Meanwhile Manlius Vulso, having secured the central prizes, moved to relieve the third squadron on the shore. The Punic left wing had succeeded in driving the Romans against the coast, but had failed to close in and destroy them — the Roman ships had formed a tight defensive perimeter with bows outward, and the corvus deterred the Carthaginians from making frontal attacks. When Vulso's ships appeared from one direction and Regulus' from another, the Punic left was trapped between the shore, the Roman third squadron facing them, and the converging Roman first and second squadrons. Fifty Carthaginian ships were captured in this final phase — the largest single haul of the battle — unable to escape. Another fourteen were captured, mainly from the earlier central engagement, and thirty were sunk across all phases.

Roman losses: twenty-four ships sunk; none captured.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | Rome | Carthage |
|---|---|---|
| Ships sunk | 24 | 30 |
| Ships captured | 0 | 64 (50 in final phase off shore; ~14 from centre) |
| Total ships lost | 24 | 94 |
| Crew and marine losses | Not separately recorded; proportional to ship losses | Not separately recorded; heavy given scale of captures |
| Source | Polybius 1.28 | Polybius 1.28 |

The Carthaginian total loss of 94 ships — roughly 27% of the fleet on Polybius' figures — was a strategic catastrophe. The three Punic divisions had retreated in different directions and were in no condition to renew the battle. The Romans returned to Sicily to rest crews, repair ships, and salvage prizes before crossing to Africa. The battle had achieved Rome's operational prerequisite: the sea lane to Africa was open.

The longer-term consequence was the African expedition and the eventual defeat of the Roman land force under [[atilius-regulus]] by the Spartan mercenary commander [[xanthippus]] at the Battle of the Bagradas River (255 BCE) — see [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/first-punic-war/battle-of-the-bagradas-255bce]]. Ecnomus thus won the crossing but did not end the war, which continued until the Battle of the Aegates Islands in 241 BCE — see [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/first-punic-war/battle-of-the-aegates-islands-241bce]].

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

### Roman Success Factors

1. **Formation design.** The triangular wedge with the *triarii* as a rear guard and the horse transports shielded inside the formation was a tactically sophisticated answer to the Carthaginian strength in ramming and flanking. It kept the fleet cohesive and gave the *triarii* a defined mission as a reserve that was actually executed.

2. **Aggressive centre attack.** The consuls' decision to charge directly at the "thin" Punic centre, rather than waiting passively, seized the initiative even though it created the gap Hamilcar was trying to engineer. The Romans accepted the separation because they trusted their ability to finish each engagement quickly — and were right.

3. **The corvus as force multiplier.** Against a numerically superior and more skilled enemy, the boarding bridge converted every ship-to-ship contact into a contest the Romans were likely to win. Even the beleaguered third squadron, driven against the shore and isolated, held its position because Carthaginian commanders would not attack into corvus range frontally. The technology did not require seamanship — it required infantry, which Rome had in abundance.

4. **High marine complement.** Loading 120 marines per ship rather than the normal forty was a deliberate pre-battle decision that paid dividends in every boarding action and in the defensive stand of the third squadron.

5. **Command adaptability after the split.** The speed with which both consuls independently redirected their squadrons after the central engagement — Vulso to secure prizes and then relieve the third squadron, Regulus to relieve the *triarii* — showed genuine operational judgment under conditions where central control was impossible. Goldsworthy regards this as "truly remarkable."

6. **Exploitation of the coastal trap.** When the Punic left drove the third squadron onto the shore, the Romans turned a bad position into a defensive asset. By forming with bows outward, they denied the Carthaginians room to use ramming tactics and made the shoreline their anchor until relief arrived.

### Carthaginian Shortcomings

1. **Failure to neutralise the corvus.** In the five years since Mylae (260 BCE), Carthage had devised no effective counter to the boarding bridge. As Goldsworthy notes, drawing on Lazenby, "an army or navy with a long tradition of success may well have difficulty in adapting to a novel tactic." The Punic commanders' plan assumed they could avoid the corvus by breaking up the Roman formation — a plan that partially succeeded but still could not prevent mass grappling.

2. **Hamilcar's feigned retreat succeeded too well.** The centre's withdrawal opened the gap the Carthaginian plan required, but it also exposed the Punic ships to boarding actions they consistently lost. Once the corvi began falling, the centre's faster, more manoeuvrable ships became a liability — their advantage was ramming and escape, neither of which was possible once grappled.

3. **Inferior marine complement.** There is no recorded Punic figure for marines per ship, but Goldsworthy notes that "there is no evidence for their successfully boarding and taking a Roman ship" throughout the battle — implying significantly fewer marines than the Roman 120 per ship. The expansion of the Carthaginian fleet had also reduced average crew quality.

4. **Failure to pursue advantage after the three-way split.** The plan succeeded in dividing the Roman fleet into three separated actions. But none of the three Carthaginian engagements was resolved before Hamilcar's centre was defeated. Hanno's right wing pressed the *triarii* but did not destroy them before Regulus arrived. The Punic left penned the third squadron against the shore but would not close.

5. **The size of the fleet as a disadvantage.** Goldsworthy observes that "the sheer size of the fleets may have made them clumsy and been better suited to the simpler boarding tactics favoured by the Romans." Carthage's skill advantage was most visible in small-scale engagements; mass fleet actions reduced the value of individual seamanship.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

**Objective** — Rome *honoured*. The Roman objective was clear, limited, and operationally coherent: defeat the Carthaginian fleet to open the sea lane to Africa. Everything — the formation, the elevated marine complement, the consular presence — was oriented toward that end. Carthage *partially violated* this principle: the intent to prevent the invasion was correct, but the tactical plan (breaking the Roman formation into isolated sub-engagements) was a means in search of an end state, with no clear concept for how winning three small battles would translate into sinking enough Roman ships to abort the crossing.

**Offensive** — Rome *honoured* at the strategic and tactical levels: the invasion itself was offensive; within the battle, the consuls' direct charge at the Punic centre seized and maintained the initiative. Carthage *violated* the offensive at the decisive moment: after successfully dividing the Roman fleet, the Punic wings failed to press their local advantages to destruction before Roman forces could concentrate in turn.

**Mass** — Rome *honoured* at decisive points. Though the overall fleet numbers were comparable, the Romans achieved local mass by concentrating their first and second squadrons against Hamilcar's centre, then sequentially redeploying that mass against Hanno and the Punic left. Carthage *violated* mass by distributing effort across three simultaneous engagements, none of which was reinforced when it stalled.

**Economy of Force** — Rome *honoured* this in the assignment of the *triarii*. The fourth squadron was allocated the minimum mission — hold the rear against Hanno, buy time — while the decisive effort was directed at the centre. When Regulus stripped away to relieve the *triarii*, he took only as many ships as needed. Carthage *violated* economy of force by committing Hanno's best ships (the fastest in the fleet) to what was essentially a holding action against the *triarii* — a mission that could have been assigned to lesser vessels, freeing the fastest ships for pursuit or exploitation of the central success.

**Maneuver** — Carthage *partially honoured*, Rome *honoured under constraint*. The Punic plan to split the Roman formation was a genuine maneuver concept. Hamilcar's feigned withdrawal and the simultaneous wing envelopments showed doctrinal sophistication. But Punic ships could not maneuver freely while corvi threatened them, and their speed advantage was neutralised in the shallow coastal water where the decisive final phase took place. Rome's maneuver was blunt — the triangular wedge was not elegant — but it preserved the fleet's ability to concentrate, and the consuls' lateral redeployment after the centre engagement was the decisive maneuver of the battle.

**Unity of Command** — Rome *honoured* through a dual-consul arrangement that worked because both men complemented rather than competed with each other: Vulso secured prizes and relieved the third squadron while Regulus took the fighting squadrons to the *triarii*. Effective unity without a single commander. Carthage *violated* unity of command at the tactical level: the three Punic divisions fought their engagements without evident coordination after the initial separation was achieved. There is no record of Hamilcar signalling Hanno or the left wing once the three-way split occurred.

**Security** — Carthage *violated* this principle most conspicuously. By committing the full fleet to an aggressive three-part envelopment, Hamilcar left no reserve and no fallback position. When the centre broke, the retreating ships could not rally to cover the wings; the three divisions dispersed "in different directions" and were "in no position to renew the fight." Rome *honoured* security through the *triarii* as an explicit reserve and through the horse transports being towed rather than sailed — keeping them under direct Roman control rather than leaving them as independent vulnerable targets.

**Surprise** — Neither side achieved strategic surprise; both knew a fleet action was imminent. At the tactical level, Hamilcar achieved a degree of surprise through his feigned retreat, which the Romans did not anticipate — the gap that opened between the consular squadrons and the third squadron was not planned. Rome achieved a form of reverse surprise in Phase 4: the Punic left wing, focused on penning the third squadron against the shore, did not expect to be caught between that squadron and two converging Roman fleets arriving from open water.

**Simplicity** — Rome *honoured* at every level. The triangular formation was complex to assemble but produced a simple, understandable doctrine: charge the centre with the best ships, hold the rear with the *triarii*, keep the transports shielded. The corvus itself was simplicity made material — a wooden plank with a spike, turning naval warfare into infantry warfare. Carthage *violated* simplicity in its plan: a coordinated three-part envelopment requiring simultaneous timing from three geographically separated squadrons, with no mechanism for the overall commander to adjust once the battle was joined. "It was difficult at the best of times to control a fleet in this period, with only the simplest signals and plans standing much chance of success" (Goldsworthy).

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

**The corvus as disruptive technology.** The boarding bridge is the clearest ancient parallel to a weapons system that neutralises an adversary's established advantage by changing the fundamental mode of engagement. Rome could not match Carthaginian seamanship, so Rome changed the game from seamanship to infantry fighting. The doctrinal parallel is to any asymmetric innovation that shifts the contest from the enemy's domain of competence to one's own: aircraft carriers made battleship gunnery irrelevant; precision munitions made mass artillery less decisive. The corvus did not make seamanship permanently irrelevant — it was abandoned after the storm losses of 255 suggested it made galleys dangerously bow-heavy — but for the critical middle phase of the war it was decisive. The parallel to precision-guided munitions is apt: a battlefield advantage that cannot be exported into all weather conditions.

**Ecnomus as a naval Cannae that failed to annihilate.** Hamilcar's plan had structural similarities to Hannibal's at Cannae (216 BCE): a weak, withdrawing centre to draw the enemy forward, flanking wings to envelop, a three-part action designed to destroy in detail. But where Hannibal's centre held long enough for the encirclement to close, Hamilcar's centre broke before the wings could achieve decision. The lesson — that a feigned retreat requires the centre to be strong enough to survive the initial contact, not merely skilled enough to disengage — is a recurring problem in complex envelopment schemes. Modern military analysts teaching Cannae often contrast it with Ecnomus precisely to illustrate why the same concept can succeed or fail depending on whether the centre can control its retrograde.

**Sea control vs. sea denial.** Ecnomus is a textbook case of the distinction between sea control and sea denial. Carthage was attempting sea denial — preventing Rome from using the sea lane to Africa without necessarily destroying the Roman fleet as a force. Rome was pursuing sea control — forcing a decisive engagement to establish the freedom of navigation required for the invasion. The historical weight of evidence from the First Punic War as a whole (and from subsequent naval history) favours the offensive, sea-control posture when one side has a technological equaliser: denial strategies tend to cede the initiative and allow the opponent to choose the time and place of engagement.

**Concentration after dispersion.** The Roman consuls' ability to regroup after the three-way split and converge on successive Carthaginian elements is an early example of what modern doctrine calls *defeat in detail* operated in reverse — instead of the Romans being defeated in detail, it was the Carthaginian wings that were taken sequentially by a reconcentrating Roman force. The analogy to interior lines doctrine is imperfect (the Romans did not hold a central position in the geometric sense), but the operational logic is similar: destroy the nearest threat first, then move mass to the next, faster than the enemy can coordinate. The consuls' independent decision-making — each correctly reading the operational situation without communication — corresponds to what modern doctrine calls *mission command*: subordinate commanders acting within commander's intent rather than waiting for orders.

**The limits of tactical skill in mass engagements.** Goldsworthy notes that Carthaginian seamanship advantage was "most marked in actions involving only a small number of ships" and that "the massed naval battles offered slight opportunity for subtlety." This generalises to a principle that appears across military history: elite, skill-intensive fighting systems tend to be more effective in small-unit engagements than in mass battles where systemic factors — numbers, logistics, training throughput, industrial capacity — dominate. The Roman response — investing in mass training and mass ship construction rather than elite seamanship — was a strategic choice that prioritised scale over quality, exactly as Polybius described in his famous comment on Roman "brute force" (*bia*).

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Polybius** is the sole detailed ancient source for Ecnomus. A Greek statesman and historian writing in Rome in the mid-second century BCE — within living memory of the war's veterans and with access to Roman senatorial archives and participants' accounts — Polybius is an exceptional source for the Punic Wars. His account of Ecnomus appears in Book I and is remarkable for its attention to formation, phase mechanics, and operational logic rather than merely heroic narrative. He provides the only fleet numbers (330 and 350 ships), the only personnel totals (~140,000 and ~150,000), the only casualty figures (24 Roman ships sunk; 30 Punic sunk, 64 captured), and the only description of the Roman four-squadron formation and the Carthaginian plan.

His reliability at Ecnomus is generally rated highly by modern scholars because his account is internally consistent, the tactical logic is coherent, and the result (a Roman victory enabling the African crossing) is confirmed by subsequent events. His known biases — pro-Roman sentiment rooted in his personal and intellectual relationship with Scipio Aemilianus, and a tendency to credit Roman institutional rationality — are less distorting here than in some other episodes. There is no obvious reason for him to exaggerate the Roman formation's sophistication since the result was a Roman win regardless.

**The fleet numbers are the central scholarly dispute.** Polybius' totals of 330 and 350 ships, with crew and marine complements of 140,000 and 150,000, have been systematically challenged. Tarn and Thiel both argued for reductions of approximately 100 ships per side, producing fleets in the range of 230 ships each. Goldsworthy's response is methodologically important: "acknowledging that this may be so does not provide any guidance as to what the actual figures may have been. Analysis of fleet numbers has tended to be very rigid in its methods, assuming that only when specifically mentioned by our sources were ships constructed, manned, lost or captured." He accepts Polybius' figures as "broadly" correct without asserting their precision. The present analysis follows that position.

**The corvus description** (Polybius 1.22) is our only ancient source for the device. Later sources treated it as a grapnel, generating scholarly doubt until H. T. Wallinga constructed a working model from Polybius' description and demonstrated its viability. Goldsworthy accepts Polybius' account as confirmed by this reconstruction.

**The Punic commanders at Ecnomus** are named only for the right wing (Hanno) and the centre (Hamilcar). The left wing commander is unnamed in Polybius. Hanno is identified as the general who had failed to relieve Agrigentum in 261 BCE, one of several indications that Carthaginian command assignments were driven by political and aristocratic considerations rather than purely by merit.

**Conflicting accounts and figures:** The battle's one major internal tension in Polybius is the claim that the Roman formation was sophisticated and deliberately designed ("there is no good reason to doubt Polybius' account or assume that he had misunderstood what was no more than an accidental formation" — Goldsworthy). Some scholars have suggested the formation was accidental, produced by the horse transports slowing the third squadron. Goldsworthy's counter — that achieving such a formation by accident still required the quality of crew needed to maintain it — is persuasive, though certainty is impossible. The tactical analysis in this page treats the formation as deliberate, consistent with Polybius.
