# Battle of the Bagradas (255 BC) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/first-punic-war-264-241bce|First Punic War — full event page]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/first-punic-war/first-punic-war|First Punic War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

The Bagradas engagement is the one pitched Carthaginian land victory of the First Punic War and one of the cleanest surviving case studies in **combined-arms restoration**: a professional Greek adviser recognising that a force's decisive advantages — elephants and superior cavalry — were being persistently forfeited by poor ground selection, then correcting that single error to achieve the complete destruction of a Roman consular army. It is equally a study in strategic overreach: [[atilius-regulus|Marcus Atilius Regulus]] had brought [[roman-republic|Rome]] to the gates of [[carthage|Carthage]] itself and then overplayed his hand at the negotiating table, giving his enemy the breathing room to recover. This page analyses how [[xanthippus|Xanthippus]] engineered the destruction and what the engagement teaches at the level of combined-arms doctrine, professional versus political command, and the culminating-point problem.

---

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**Carthage (Xanthippus / [[xanthippus]])** — After catastrophic naval defeat at Ecnomus (256 BCE) and the overrunning of its African territory, Carthage had lost the strategic initiative comprehensively. Regulus had taken Aspis, ravaged the peninsula, captured Tunis, and was raiding the suburbs of Carthage itself. The immediate objective was existential: stop the Roman army from devastating the homeland any further and prevent Carthage from being forced to accept a humiliating peace that amounted to unconditional surrender. Secondary: restore confidence in a citizen body that Goldsworthy characterises as "utterly despondent" after the fall of both their fleet and their field army within months. Constraint: the winter of 255 BCE had to be used to rebuild and re-drill the army before Regulus received fresh consular reinforcements or mounted a siege of the city itself.

**Rome ([[atilius-regulus|Regulus]])** — The primary objective going into 255 was to deliver a peace that forced Carthage to admit total defeat — "that the Carthaginians should give up both Sicily and Sardinia, release all Roman prisoners freely… pay the Romans an indemnity and annual tribute, only make war and peace on the approval of Rome, and only retain one warship for their own use" (Goldsworthy summarising Dio's version of the terms). This objective had *already failed* diplomatically before the battle was fought: Carthage had rejected the terms as harsher than their actual military situation warranted, and talks had collapsed. Regulus was therefore seeking to translate his operational dominance into outright capitulation through one further decisive military blow. Secondary: complete the African campaign before a successor consul arrived to claim the credit. Constraint: an understrength consular army — 15,000 infantry and only 500 cavalry, the latter reduced because of the difficulty of transporting horses by sea — facing a terrain that theoretically favoured his enemy's strengths.

The battle is therefore not merely a tactical collision but the climax of a strategic miscalculation: Regulus had refused Carthage reasonable terms when he held the stronger hand, and in doing so had given a desperate enemy the winter they needed to recover under professional tutelage.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground:** Polybius locates the battle on "level plains" near Tunis — the broad, flat lower Bagradas (modern Medjerda) valley. Goldsworthy is explicit that *Xanthippus identified this as the decisive correction*: previous Carthaginian commanders had positioned themselves on high, broken ground at Adys, where their cavalry and elephants were useless. "Xanthippus was openly critical of the Carthaginian commanders who had let their army fight a battle on unfavourable ground when their advantages in cavalry and elephants could easily have been employed to defeat the Romans in the open plains." The level plain was, in effect, the first tactical decision of the campaign — and the correct one.
- **Season:** Early 255 BCE, following a winter of re-training. The rebuilt Carthaginian army had had months to drill under Xanthippus beneath the walls of Carthage before taking the field; the Romans had spent that time raiding and had grown confident, perhaps overconfident, in the passivity of their enemy.
- **Intelligence:** Both sides had a reasonably clear picture of each other's approximate numbers. The Romans "were surprised by their opponents' renewed confidence" when the Carthaginians marched out — they expected a demoralised enemy still reeling from Adys. Regulus was "eager to inflict another defeat" and accepted battle readily when offered. There is no evidence of systematic Roman intelligence about the specific Carthaginian formation — the deployment of the elephant screen and the cavalry disposition appear to have registered only when the armies were already engaged. On the Carthaginian side, Xanthippus had clearly studied Roman tactical habits (the deep formation Regulus adopted was an explicit reaction to the elephant threat, and Xanthippus may have anticipated it) and understood the severe Roman cavalry disadvantage.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | Carthage (Xanthippus) | Rome (Regulus) |
|---|---|---|
| **Total** | c. 16,000 + c. 100 elephants | c. 15,500 |
| **Infantry** | 12,000 — citizen spearmen (phalanx) in centre; mercenaries on right flank and supporting wings | 15,000 — legions in triplex acies, drawn up **unusually deep** ("many maniples deep," Polybius) |
| **Cavalry** | 4,000 — divided between both wings | 500 — on the wings; outnumbered at least 4:1 |
| **Elephants** | c. 100 — formed in a **single line** a "suitable distance" in front of the main infantry line | None — velites deployed as skirmish screen against the elephants |
| **Special element** | Mercenary contingent on right (apparently thinner coverage — "it seems that they did not fully cover the mercenaries on the right," Goldsworthy) | Velites in front of the heavy infantry; maniples formed especially deep |
| **Command arrangement** | Xanthippus — single directing authority following his appointment as senior adviser; Punic generals formally in command but following his dispositions | Regulus — sole consular command; no subordinate noted |

**Carthaginian deployment (Polybius / Goldsworthy):** The citizen phalanx occupied the main centre line; a body of mercenaries extended the right; cavalry were divided between both wings with additional mercenaries, some possibly acting as skirmishers. The elephant line screened the entire front at a "suitable distance" ahead of the infantry — placed to absorb and disrupt the Roman advance before it reached the phalanx. Crucially, the cavalry was massed in force on both wings against a Roman mounted arm that numbered only 500.

**Roman deployment:** Regulus placed the cavalry conventionally on the wings and the legions in the centre — but formed the latter "many maniples deep" to absorb the elephant charge. Goldsworthy analyses this at length, suggesting it most likely meant each maniple was drawn up in more ranks than usual rather than a literal multiplication of the three standard lines, though the exact meaning of Polybius' phrase is debated. The logic was sound: a deeper formation made it harder for men in the front ranks to bolt when the elephants charged, and those to the rear created the physical pressure to hold forward. The fatal structural weakness was that this deepening of the centre *reduced frontage*, compressing the Roman line while leaving the already grotesquely outnumbered cavalry on the wings with no reinforcement and no solution.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Phase 1 — Approach and opening skirmish.** After the Romans advanced and camped "just over a mile (10 stades) away from the Punic camp and eagerly accepted battle when the next day the enemy marched out and deployed" (Goldsworthy / Polybius), both forces deployed. The Romans moved forward "raising their battle cry and rhythmically banging their weapons against their shields in what Polybius describes as their usual custom." Velites were pushed out ahead of the main line to skirmish against the elephant screen — a sound counter, since "elephants were vulnerable to missiles which might cause them to panic even if they did not inflict serious wounds." This was the Roman response to having been terrified by elephants in Sicily; Regulus was adapting his formation to the threat he had identified.

2. **Phase 2 — Cavalry destruction on both wings.** The moment the armies engaged seriously, Carthaginian cavalry swept the Roman horse from both wings. "The Roman horse, facing odds of at least four to one, were swiftly routed." This was not even a prolonged contest: the numerical disparity — 4,000 Carthaginian cavalry against 500 Roman — made the wing action effectively instantaneous in tactical terms. The wings were cleared early, leaving the infantry engagement to develop without Roman cavalry able to threaten the Carthaginian flanks or protect the Roman flanks and rear.

3. **Phase 3 — The elephant assault and the partial Roman breakthrough.** Xanthippus ordered the elephants forward. The mass of c. 100 animals struck the Roman centre. Despite the depth of their formation, "the Roman infantry reeled under the onslaught of the mass of elephants, but despite taking casualties, the depth of their formation prevented them from breaking." This was the intended effect of Regulus' deep deployment — and it partly worked. "A few maniples and small groups fought their way past the animals, and after reforming moved against the Carthaginian phalanx." Simultaneously, a significant Roman success occurred on the left flank: the 2,000 men there — "eager to avoid the elephants and contemptuous of the mercenaries who had been defeated in the previous battle" at Adys — "charged the units on the enemy right flank and routed them, chasing them back to their camp." This success, the one Roman bright spot of the engagement, tells us the mercenaries on the Carthaginian right were the weakest element and that the elephant screen did not fully cover that sector.

4. **Phase 4 — Envelopment and annihilation.** The Roman infantry that had fought through the elephants now faced the citizen phalanx "weary, their pila almost certainly gone, and greatly outnumbered." They were defeated in short order. In the meantime, the victorious Carthaginian cavalry, having swept the Roman wings, turned inward against the flanks and rear of the Roman infantry mass. "Their attacks robbed the Roman formation of what forward impetus it had left, as flanking maniples had to turn to face the new threat. Struck by missiles from the cavalry or trampled by the elephants the Romans were destroyed whether they stood their ground or turned to flee." The result was annihilation rather than defeat: "Regulus and 500 men initially made their escape, but were quickly captured." The only organised survivors were the 2,000 on the left who had broken through the mercenaries — "the only substantial part of the Roman army to escape" — who made their way back to Aspis and held it until evacuated by the Roman fleet.

**Key tactical mechanisms:** The sequencing was critical. The cavalry wings were stripped first; the elephant screen disrupted and fragmented the Roman centre; the fresh citizen phalanx delivered the coup de grâce against exhausted, weaponless survivors; and the now-free cavalry enveloped the flanks and rear to close the destruction. No single element could have achieved the result alone — the combination was what made the victory so complete. Goldsworthy's emphasis is clear: "it is important to note that the victory had not been achieved by the elephants alone and owed a great deal to the successful cavalry actions which had allowed the envelopment of the Roman infantry."

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | Carthage | Rome |
|---|---|---|
| **Killed** | 800 (the routed mercenaries on the right, per Polybius) | Near-total; most of c. 15,000 infantry killed or captured |
| **Captured** | None noted | Regulus + c. 500 (initially escaped but quickly taken); Polybius |
| **Survivors** | — | c. 2,000 (the left-wing contingent that broke through to Aspis) |
| **Source(s)** | Polybius (Bk I) — only figure preserved for Carthaginian losses | Polybius (Bk I); Goldsworthy, *The Fall of Carthage* (2000) |

**Note on Carthaginian losses:** Polybius records 800 killed among the routed mercenaries but gives no figure for losses in the elephant corps or phalanx. Goldsworthy does not supply an alternative. The 800 figure likely understates total Carthaginian casualties but is the only grounded number available.

**Strategic result:** The African expedition of 256–255 was annihilated. Regulus was captured — a figure who would become one of the most elaborated legends of the Roman tradition. The 2,000 survivors at Aspis were evacuated later in the year by the Roman fleet, which then suffered a catastrophic storm off Sicily destroying most of the ships. Rome made no further attempt to invade Africa for the remainder of the war. "The Carthaginians' victory restored their confidence, which had reached such a low ebb after Ecnomus and Adys, and began an upsurge in their fortunes." In the following years Carthage regained the initiative in Sicily and suppressed the Numidian princes. Goldsworthy describes this as "the most striking success achieved by elephants throughout the course of the Punic Wars" with "a great moral effect on the Roman armies in Sicily, who for the next few years did not dare contest control of the open ground with the Carthaginians for fear of these beasts."

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Xanthippus / Carthaginian success factors**

1. **The terrain correction.** This was the single most consequential act of the campaign. Previous commanders had fought on high, broken ground, negating Carthage's greatest assets. Xanthippus identified this error, argued publicly for moving to the plain, and was given command as a result. Before a sword was drawn on the day of battle, this decision had shifted the probability of the outcome dramatically.
2. **Combined-arms integration.** Xanthippus designed a formation in which each arm had a specific, coordinated role: the elephant screen to disrupt and channel the Roman infantry; the cavalry to clear the wings rapidly and then envelop; the phalanx to finish the weakened survivors. The three elements worked in sequence rather than independently. Contrast this with Carthaginian failures at Agrigentum and Adys, where "a recently created Carthaginian force failed to co-ordinate its different elements effectively."
3. **Winter re-training.** Polybius emphasises Xanthippus' "use of proper military commands and manoeuvres as he drilled the army beneath the walls of Carthage." The soldiers who fought at Bagradas were not the demoralised force of the previous season; they had been rebuilt professionally in drill, confidence, and familiarity with combined-arms operations. The morale effect was real: the army's "confidence was renewed."
4. **Matching the formation to the mission.** Placing the citizen phalanx in the centre was sound: it was the most reliable and cohesive element, given the task of delivering the final blow against an already-disrupted enemy rather than having to absorb the shock of the opening phase.
5. **Exploiting Roman cavalry weakness ruthlessly.** The 4,000:500 cavalry imbalance was exploitable only if Carthage actually committed its cavalry aggressively to both wings, which Xanthippus did. The wings were stripped before the infantry action had developed fully.

**Regulus / Roman shortcomings**

1. **Strategic overreach before the battle.** The most consequential Roman error preceded the engagement. Regulus had Carthage on the verge of negotiated peace and demanded terms that amounted to unconditional surrender — terms so harsh that Carthage chose to continue fighting rather than accept them. This gave Xanthippus his winter window. Goldsworthy: "the city was by no means at the end of its resources. Faced with a Roman refusal to grant any concession, the talks failed."
2. **The cavalry deficit with no answer.** Regulus had 500 cavalry against 4,000. He accepted battle on open ground knowing this disparity. His apparent hope — "to use his superior infantry to break the enemy's main line before their numerically superior cavalry could come into play" — was a reasonable gamble but it required his infantry to break the phalanx before the cavalry had finished with the Roman wings. There was no contingency if the infantry failed to break through quickly, which it did not.
3. **Deep formation reduced frontage.** The deepening of the maniples, designed to resist the elephant charge, shrank the Roman frontage. Goldsworthy notes this made it "easier for the Punic cavalry to envelop the infantry centre, even more so as its deeper formation can only have reduced its frontage." The tactical adaptation to the elephant threat created the vulnerability the cavalry envelopment exploited.
4. **The elephant threat had no adequate answer.** The velite screen was the correct response in principle, but against c. 100 elephants on an open plain it proved insufficient to prevent the main charge. The Roman left succeeded partly because the elephant screen did not fully cover the Carthaginian right — a structural gap in the Punic deployment that the Romans could not have planned for.
5. **No reserve and no flexibility.** Once the cavalry was stripped and the legions committed against the elephants, Regulus had no formed reserve and no capacity to respond to the envelopment. The Roman formation by its nature was a single committed mass.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective.** *Xanthippus: honoured; Regulus: violated at the strategic level.* Xanthippus had a clear, attainable military objective — defeat the Roman army in the field before it could besiege Carthage. His every decision served that aim. Regulus, by contrast, conflated his tactical objective (win the battle) with a political objective (force unconditional surrender) that the actual balance of power did not support, and in demanding terms Carthage could not accept, he prolonged the war that he needed to win quickly.

- **Offensive.** *Regulus: apparent honour; Xanthippus: real honour.* Rome had held the operational initiative throughout the African campaign, and Regulus readily accepted battle when offered. But accepting battle on terms dictated by Xanthippus — on the open plain, against a rebuilt combined-arms force — meant that Roman "offensiveness" delivered them into a prepared killing ground. True initiative belongs to whoever controls the conditions of the engagement.

- **Mass.** *Xanthippus: honoured decisively; Regulus: partially.* Carthage massed its cavalry on the wings in overwhelming force (4,000 against 500), its elephants in a concentrated line screen, and its best infantry (the citizen phalanx) as the final blow. Regulus massed his infantry, which was the right instinct — it was his strongest arm — but was unable to mass sufficiently on a wide enough front (the deepening reduced frontage) and had no way to mass against the cavalry threat.

- **Economy of Force.** *Xanthippus: honoured.* The mercenaries on the right, who were defeated by the Roman left, were the weakest element in the Carthaginian order of battle — and they faced the weakest sector of the Roman line (the 2,000 troops on Regulus' left who were trying to avoid the elephants). Xanthippus accepted the loss of the mercenary right as an economy of force, preserving the citizen phalanx and the cavalry for the decisive action. The mercenaries' defeat cost 800 men; the main action destroyed a Roman army.

- **Manoeuvre.** *Xanthippus: decisive; Regulus: absent.* The tactical manoeuvre at Bagradas was the cavalry envelopment — once both wings were cleared, the Carthaginian horse turned inward against the flanks and rear of the Roman infantry. This was the mechanism of destruction, identical in structural logic (though not in the tight choreography) to Cannae: a cavalry envelopment of an infantry mass that had been disrupted and channelled by a screening element. Regulus had no manoeuvre element; he had committed everything to a straight frontal push through the elephants.

- **Unity of Command.** *Xanthippus: de facto honoured; Carthage structurally awkward.* Xanthippus was appointed "as some sort of senior military adviser to the army" — not formally the commander, but the actual directing intelligence of the Carthaginian formation. The Punic generals retained formal authority. This worked because the other generals deferred to his professional judgment, but it was an inherently fragile arrangement. Regulus, as consul, exercised sole command — a structural advantage that was offset by his inexperience with the specific combined-arms threat he was facing.

- **Security.** *Regulus: violated; Xanthippus: honoured.* The Roman cavalry flanks were the decisive vulnerability and Regulus had no answer to their exposure. He apparently hoped to win before the cavalry imbalance could be exploited. This is the same structural failure as at Cannae sixteen years later: accepting battle on ground that maximises the enemy's decisive arm while your own decisive weakness is fully exposed. Xanthippus, conversely, secured his flanks with his strongest arm (the cavalry) and his front with the elephant screen.

- **Surprise.** *Xanthippus: achieved; Regulus: suffered.* Surprise here was not of contact but of capability. The Romans had spent the winter watching Carthage absorb defeats; they had no expectation of a rebuilt, redrilled, professionally organised field army emerging in spring 255. "The Romans were surprised by their opponents' renewed confidence." The specific surprise of the elephant mass on open ground — a very different proposition from the isolated elephant failures the Romans had observed in Sicily — appears to have exceeded Roman anticipation even though the threat was known.

- **Simplicity.** *Xanthippus: honoured; Regulus: partially.* Xanthippus' plan was not simple in conception — it required three arms to execute coordinated roles in sequence — but the *logic* was simple: use the open plain; use the cavalry to clear the wings and then envelop; use the elephants to disrupt and channel; use the phalanx to finish. Each element was assigned a clear, achievable task. Regulus' plan (break through the elephants with deep infantry before the cavalry was swept) was also simple, but it was simple in a way that left him one failure mode away from catastrophe — and that failure mode (the infantry not breaking through fast enough) is exactly what occurred.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

**Combined arms and the restoration of doctrine.** The Bagradas engagement is a textbook case for how neglecting combined-arms integration degrades a force's combat power, and how a single professional correction can restore it. Xanthippus did not give Carthage a new army; he gave an existing army the doctrine its assets demanded. Modern joint and combined-arms doctrine (U.S. ADP 3-0, FM 3-96) makes the same point: each arm protects the weaknesses of the others — cavalry and armour protect infantry from being enveloped; infantry protects cavalry and armour from close-in threats; indirect fires suppress the enemy's anti-armour assets. Disconnecting these arms by fighting in terrain that neutralises two of the three is not merely sub-optimal; it is, as Bagradas demonstrates, potentially lethal.

**The professional versus the political commander.** The contrast between Xanthippus and the Carthaginian generals he replaced is a recurring theme in professional military education. Goldsworthy characterises the previous commanders as having made a straightforward error — they fought on ground that negated their strengths — without the professional knowledge to recognise it. Xanthippus saw the error immediately and corrected it. The same contrast applies to Regulus: capable and aggressive but operating at the limits of Roman tactical doctrine, facing a problem (mass elephants on open ground combined with catastrophic cavalry inferiority) that required a professional response he did not have. The lesson for modern command education is that technical competence in one arm of service or at one command level does not automatically transfer to combined-arms operations at a higher level.

**Over-reach and the culminating point.** Bagradas is an exemplary case of a force reaching — and passing — its culminating point (Clausewitz's *Kulminationspunkt*): the moment at which continued offensive action shifts from strength to vulnerability. Regulus had reached his culminating point before the battle: the Roman army was too small to besiege Carthage, too undermanned in cavalry to contest the open plain, and too politically constrained (Regulus wanted to finish before his successor arrived) to wait for reinforcement. His correct move at the culminating point was negotiation at realistic terms — which the Carthaginians were willing to accept. By demanding unrealistic terms, he passed through his culminating point and allowed the enemy to recover. This maps directly onto modern operational doctrine's treatment of the culminating point (FM 3-0, §7-12): commanders must recognise when they have reached the limit of their offensive capability and shift posture accordingly, or risk transitioning from attacker to victim. Regulus failed this test; the battle was the result.

**The elephant-screen as an area-denial system.** Ancient commanders who combined elephants with cavalry used the elephant screen much as modern doctrine uses indirect fire support to disrupt and channel an advancing enemy before the manoeuvre element envelops — a suppression-and-envelopment sequence rather than a brute-force charge. Xanthippus' elephant line achieved what Carthage's elephant employment at Agrigentum had failed to achieve: it disrupted the Roman advance sufficiently to strip momentum from the legions before they could reach the phalanx, creating the conditions for the cavalry to complete the encirclement. The parallel is imperfect (elephants are far less controllable than fire support) but the structural logic — suppress, disrupt, channel, then envelop — is recognisable in modern combined-arms doctrine.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Polybius (Histories, Book I)** is the essential source. A Greek statesman writing within two generations of the events, with access to Roman informants and access to the tradition of Philinus of Agrigentum (a Carthaginian sympathiser who wrote a contemporary account). His narrative of the Xanthippus episode is the fullest surviving account. Goldsworthy notes that "Polybius takes obvious pride in recounting the achievements of this Greek soldier, whose actions confirmed the deeply held Hellenic admiration for the Spartan military system" — a bias toward Xanthippus and the Spartan system that colours his framing of the episode. Some of the stories around Xanthippus, notably the "romantic" tradition of his subsequent assassination by jealous Carthaginians, Polybius himself treats with scepticism. His casualty figures are sparse: he records 800 for the Carthaginian mercenaries and gives no single aggregate for Roman losses, leaving Goldsworthy to reconstruct the scale of destruction from the narrative detail (only 2,000 escaped; Regulus and 500 were captured; the rest of 15,000 were presumably killed or taken in the rout). This is unusual and likely reflects a gap in Polybius' sources for the Roman losses specifically.

**Diodorus Siculus** provides some supplementary detail, particularly on the Greek drafts sent to Carthage that winter — "either 100 or 50 soldiers according to Diodorus" — a tiny number that tells us Xanthippus' contribution was professional expertise, not manpower.

**Dio / Zonaras** preserve the tradition of Regulus' peace terms. Goldsworthy treats these with caution: "Dio claims to preserve the details of the terms dictated by Regulus, but their absence from all the earlier sources can only make their authenticity dubious." For what they are worth, the terms he summarises are more punishing than those eventually imposed in 241 BCE, which lends plausibility to the basic narrative if not to every clause.

**The Regulus legend.** The elaborate post-battle tradition — Regulus sent to Rome to negotiate for prisoners, choosing instead to advise against the exchange, then returning voluntarily to Carthage to be tortured to death — is "almost certainly mythical" by Goldsworthy's assessment, and crucially absent from Polybius. It belongs to the moralising Roman tradition of the mid-Republic and should not be taken as historical.

**Conflicting accounts — Xanthippus' subsequent fate.** Polybius sceptically discusses the claim that the Carthaginians arranged Xanthippus' drowning at sea out of jealousy after his victory. Goldsworthy accepts the scepticism: "Xanthippus departed after his success, aware according to Polybius of the jealousy of the Carthaginian nobility, and may subsequently have served under the Ptolemies." The later fate is unresolved; the victory itself is not in doubt.

**[[goldsworthy-the-fall-of-carthage-2000]]** is the key modern source for the land-war narrative. His analysis of the elephant employment at Bagradas is placed within a wider argument about Carthaginian combined-arms failures throughout the First Punic War: the army repeatedly failed to coordinate its different elements (as at Agrigentum and Adys), and Xanthippus' achievement was to impose the doctrinal coherence that prior Punic commanders lacked. Goldsworthy also provides the essential comparative analysis noting that at Trebia (218), a substantially larger Roman army was partly able to escape when its centre broke through the Carthaginian line — whereas at Bagradas, the much smaller Roman force (about one-third the size of the Trebia army) was completely enveloped because its deeper, narrower formation made it easier for the Punic cavalry to close the ring.
