A staff-college-level analysis of objectives, terrain, order of battle, the phased conduct of the engagement, the critical decisions, and the lessons — at greater depth than the standard `events/` page.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**United States (Sherman)** — primary objective: Destroy or neutralize the Army of Tennessee; capture Atlanta to cut Confederate supply lines and boost Northern morale before the 1864 election. Coordinated with Grant’s Overland Campaign.

**Confederate States (Johnston, later Hood)** — primary objective: Defend Atlanta; delay or defeat Sherman using defensive terrain and fortifications; hope for political victory in the North.

Sherman used a mix of maneuver and direct pressure; Johnston traded space for time.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground:** Mountainous north Georgia, rivers (Chattahoochee), dense woods, railroads. Good defensive terrain for Confederates.

- **Weather / season:** Summer 1864; rain affected movements.

- **Intelligence:** Sherman had superior numbers and used flanking; Johnston had good defensive positions.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | United States | Confederate States |
|---|---|---|
| Total | ~110,000 (three armies) | ~65,000 |
| Infantry | Army of the Cumberland, Tennessee, Ohio | Army of Tennessee |
| Cavalry | Large force | Wheeler’s cavalry |
| Missile / other | Artillery | Artillery |
| Command arrangement | Sherman overall | Johnston then Hood |

*Deployment (described as a map would show it):* Sherman advanced south along railroad; Confederates fell back to successive lines (Dalton, Resaca, etc.).

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Phase 1 — Advance to Atlanta (May–July):** Sherman flanked Johnston repeatedly (Resaca, Dallas, Kennesaw Mountain). Johnston avoided decisive battle.

2. **Phase 2 — Hood takes command (July):** Hood launched aggressive but costly attacks (Peachtree Creek, Atlanta, Ezra Church). Sherman besieged Atlanta.

3. **Phase 3 — Capture of Atlanta (Sep 2):** Sherman cut railroads; Hood evacuated. Union occupied Atlanta.

**Key tactical mechanisms:** Flanking maneuvers, railroad as axis, siege operations, Hood’s frontal attacks.

| Captured / wounded | ~27,000 total | ~32,000 total |

- Strategic result: Atlanta fell; major blow to Confederacy; boosted Lincoln’s re-election.

- Immediate follow-on: Sherman’s March to the Sea.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Union success factors:** Sherman’s operational art in flanking; persistence; destruction of Confederate logistics.

**Union shortcomings:** High casualties at Kennesaw and other points.

**Confederate success factors:** Johnston’s defensive skill delayed Sherman.

**Confederate shortcomings:** Hood’s aggressive tactics led to higher losses without gain.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — Sherman kept focus on Atlanta and enemy army.

- **Offensive** — Mix of offensive maneuvers and siege.

- **Mass** — Sherman used mass at decisive points.

- **Economy of Force** — Good use of multiple columns.

- **Maneuver** — Flanking was key.

- **Unity of Command** — Sherman had good control of three armies.

- **Security** — Railroad protection important.

- **Surprise** — Flanking achieved surprise.

- **Simplicity** — Direct advance on Atlanta via railroad.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Example of operational maneuver and logistics warfare. Sherman’s use of the railroad as a line of operation and the destruction of resources prefigures modern effects-based operations and counter-logistics. The campaign shows the political impact of military success.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **Primary sources** — Sherman’s memoirs; soldier accounts of the march and fighting.

- **Conflicting accounts** — Hood blamed others; Johnston criticized for caution.

- **Modern scholarship** — Seen as a masterpiece of operational art; critical to Union victory.
