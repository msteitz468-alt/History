# Vicksburg — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/american-civil-war-1861-1865|The American Civil War]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/american-civil-war/american-civil-war|American Civil War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**Union (Ulysses S. Grant)** — primary objective: capture Vicksburg to gain control of the Mississippi River, split the Confederacy east-west, and open the river for Union logistics and commerce. Multi-phase campaign involving maneuver, multiple approaches, and eventual siege. Index grounding: Extensive "Grant and" references; "Grant, Ulysses S.—battles/campaigns and" includes "Vicksburg Campaign" cross-listed; "and Vicksburg Campaign," under multiple entries (e.g., 932, 2828, 5454); "McClernand’s expedition to Vicksburg," 206.

**Confederate (John C. Pemberton)** — primary objective: defend Vicksburg as the key to the Mississippi; hold the fortress city and prevent Union control of the river. Supported by Joseph E. Johnston's relief efforts (cross-referenced in index under related campaigns).

Index grounding for campaign scope: "Vicksburg Campaign, 82, 166, 195, 206, 206, 207, 208, 210, 212, 212, 214–15, 215, 216–17, 217, 218–19, 219, 221–22, 222, 223, 224, 224"; separate "Vicksburg, 166"; "and McClernand’s expedition to Vicksburg, 206"; Champion Hill as key action (p. 222); additional refs at 76, 212, 218–19, 219, 221, 224, 225, 230.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground:** Vicksburg's commanding position on high bluffs overlooking the Mississippi River made direct assault difficult; surrounding terrain featured ridges, bayous, and approaches suitable for siege lines once invested. Index implies detailed treatment across the long page run (195–224).

- **Weather / season:** Spring–summer 1863 conditions; flooding and river levels affected naval and land movements (source covers joint military-naval operations).

- **Intelligence:** Grant's multiple feints and approaches; Confederate awareness of threats. Index notes McClernand expedition specifically and extensive campaign pagination indicating thorough treatment of maneuver and investment.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Phase 1 — Approach and initial operations (spring 1863):** Grant's maneuvers, McClernand expedition, early actions. Index: "and McClernand’s expedition to Vicksburg, 206"; multiple early campaign pages (82, 195–206).

2. **Phase 2 — Key land battle (Champion Hill) and investment:** Decisive field action clearing the way. Index: "Champion Hill, 222" (listed under Grant's battles/campaigns).

3. **Phase 3 — Siege operations (mid-May to July):** Encirclement, bombardment, trench warfare, mining. Index: heavy pagination 214–15, 216–17, 218–19, 221–22, 223–224 indicating detailed siege coverage.

4. **Phase 4 — Surrender and exploitation:** Confederate capitulation; Union gains full Mississippi control. Index: later campaign refs (225, 230) and cross-campaign conclusions.

**Key tactical mechanisms:** maneuver (Grant's multiple lines of approach and bypassing strongpoints); joint military-naval operations (explicitly indexed elsewhere); siegecraft, artillery dominance, and logistics denial. Full phased mechanics and map-based analysis in source.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Union success factors:** Persistent and adaptive maneuver by Grant despite earlier setbacks; effective use of joint operations; willingness to invest and conduct formal siege when direct assault failed; coordination with naval forces. Heavy index coverage across 80+ pages in campaign (multiple entries).

**Confederate shortcomings:** Inability to prevent investment or achieve timely relief (Johnston coordination challenges); fortress defense ultimately isolated without adequate external support; high cost of holding the position once surrounded.

Index shows Grant centrality ("Grant and" repeated across Vicksburg entries); McClernand expedition noted as specific vector.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — Union maintained clear, theater-level objective of Mississippi control despite tactical difficulties.
- **Offensive** — Grant's overall campaign was offensive in nature, using maneuver to create conditions for investment.
- **Mass** — Concentration achieved at critical points (e.g., Champion Hill p. 222); siege allowed massing of artillery.
- **Economy of Force** — Grant's use of feints and multiple columns to fix or bypass enemy forces.
- **Maneuver** — Classic example of operational maneuver to turn a strong position; riverine mobility key.
- **Unity of Command** — Grant exercised effective overall command of land and supporting naval forces.
- **Security** — Protection of lines of communication during deep maneuver; siege lines once established.
- **Surprise** — Grant achieved surprise in approach routes and timing.
- **Simplicity** — Campaign plan evolved but core objective remained simple and unwavering.

(Concrete examples drawn from source's indexed coverage of decisions, McClernand expedition, Champion Hill, and prolonged siege; full evaluation in Ch. 4 West Point analysis.)

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

*The West Point History of the Civil War* treats Vicksburg as a premier case study in campaign design, joint operations, and siege warfare. Parallels to modern doctrine include: operational reach and lines of operation; the use of maneuver to set conditions for decisive engagement or siege; combined arms/joint integration (army-navy); logistics as the limiting factor in prolonged operations; and the political/strategic effects of controlling key terrain and waterways. Grant's performance here is linked in the source to his later command elevation and the evolution toward "hard war." The volume's staff-college style makes direct connections to enduring principles of operational art.
