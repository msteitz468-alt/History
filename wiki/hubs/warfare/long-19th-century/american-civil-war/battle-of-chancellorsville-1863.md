# Battle of Chancellorsville — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/battle-of-chancellorsville-1863|Full event page]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/american-civil-war/american-civil-war|American Civil War War Hub]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

Grounded in the source's precise timed map situations and dedicated OOB (Range 2).

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**Union (Hooker)** — Large-scale turning movement west of Fredericksburg to force Lee to fight or evacuate. Superior force and logistics.

**Confederate (Lee)** — Defend against encirclement; use interior lines and audacity to defeat in detail despite being outnumbered.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground:** Dense Wilderness (second-growth timber, poor roads) favored the flanking march and concealed movements; open areas around Chancellorsville house.
- **Weather:** Late April / early May; conditions supported rapid marches.
- **Intelligence:** Hooker initially surprised Lee; Lee rapidly gained accurate picture of Union dispositions.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

Union: Main force at Chancellorsville with wings; Sedgwick at Fredericksburg.

Confederate: Lee with part facing Hooker; Jackson's corps on the famous flank march around the Union right.

Full details: "Battle of Chancellorsville—Order of Battle" (source cites Eicher and specific OR vol/pages).

*Deployment:* Union right flank (11th Corps) exposed; Jackson's march achieved local surprise at dusk 2 May.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Phase 1 — Union crossing and concentration (30 Apr–1 May):** Hooker advances and then halts.
2. **Phase 2 — Jackson's flank march and attack (2 May):** Long march; evening assault rolls up Union XI Corps ("The stampede of the Eleventh Corps—Berry’s corps checking the pursuit").
3. **Phase 3 — Fighting around Chancellorsville and 3 May:** Continued pressure; Salem Church action against Sedgwick.
4. **Phase 4 — Jackson wounded; Union withdrawal:** Friendly fire incident on Jackson (depicted in Currier & Ives); Lee consolidates; Hooker recrosses.

**Key tactical mechanisms:** Flanking march in difficult terrain; exploitation of surprise; combined infantry assault at dusk.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

Confederate victory. Jackson's wounding (later death) was a severe blow.

## 6. Critical Decisions

**Confederate:** Lee's decision to divide his army twice and send Jackson on the long flank march (high risk, high reward).

**Union:** Hooker's loss of initiative after crossing; failure to press or properly secure the right flank.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — Both had clear objectives; Lee's more audacious.
- **Offensive** — Jackson's attack was a model of offensive spirit.
- **Mass** — Achieved locally by Confederates at the point of decision.
- **Economy of Force** — Lee left minimal force opposite Fredericksburg.
- **Maneuver** — The flank march is a textbook example.
- **Unity of Command** — Lee exercised it masterfully; Jackson executed.
- **Security** — Union right was insecure.
- **Surprise** — Achieved by Jackson.
- **Simplicity** — The march plan was bold but execution relied on staff excellence.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

The battle is frequently studied for the "flanking attack" or "envelopment" and the commander's willingness to accept risk and divide forces when facing a superior opponent (cf. Cannae parallels in other hubs). Highlights the importance of security and flank protection in maneuver warfare. Dusk attack shows value of surprise and shock.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **Primary sources** — OR Vol. XXV Pt. 1 cited for OOB. Contemporary depictions of Jackson's death and the XI Corps rout.
- **Conflicting accounts** — Source's multiple dated situations (30 Apr to 3 May) allow precise phasing.
- **Modern scholarship** — West Point History uses it to illustrate Lee's generalship at its peak and the cost of victory.
