A staff-college-level analysis of objectives, terrain, order of battle, the phased conduct of the engagement, the critical decisions, and the lessons — at greater depth than the standard `events/` page.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**United States (Grant)** — press forward to Corinth after Forts Henry/Donelson victories to split the Confederacy and open the Mississippi corridor. Underestimated Confederate concentration.

**Confederate States (Johnston/Beauregard)** — concentrate scattered forces to achieve local superiority and destroy Grant’s army before Buell could join; seize initiative after cordon strategy failed.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

**Cartographic support:** Kentucky-Tennessee theater 1861 developments (Shiloh / Corinth area context).

- **Ground:** Pittsburg Landing; Shiloh Church; woods, ravines, sunken roads (Hornet’s Nest); smoke and thickets broke formations and artillery support.
- **Weather/season:** April; heavy rain night of April 6.
- **Intelligence:** Complete tactical surprise for Confederates on April 6; Union unprepared in camp despite proximity.

| Cavalry | Limited | Limited |

*Deployment (described as a map would show it):* Union forward camps; Confederates in dense woods for surprise attack in successive waves.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Phase 1 — April 6 surprise and initial collapse:** Confederates attack at dawn in 4 lines; Union driven back in confusion. Local successes but lines became jumbled in woods/ravines.
2. **Phase 2 — Hornet’s Nest:** Union stand in sunken roads/ravines; 62 guns massed against it; nearly reached the depot before dark. Johnston mortally wounded (leg wound, blood loss) while leading; never exercised full army-level control.
3. **Phase 3 — Night recovery:** Buell’s 28k and Wallace arrive; rain. Grant to Sherman: “We’ve had the devil’s own day, haven’t we?” “the army would 'lick ’em tomorrow, though.'”
4. **Phase 4 — April 7 counterattack:** Grant (now ~2:1) grinds forward; Confederates withdraw. No effective pursuit due to woods, casualties, and coordination limits.

**Key tactical mechanisms:** Surprise and local mass vs. defensive stand; successive waves breaking cohesion; arrival of fresh Union troops overnight.

- Strategic result: Union retained the field and initiative in the West; Confederates failed to destroy Grant.
- Immediate follow-on: Opened way to Corinth; Grant’s post-battle realization that only “complete conquest” would save the Union.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Confederate success factors (partial):** Concentration, surprise, aggressive initial assault.
**Confederate shortcomings:** 4-line stacking caused jumbling and loss of reserves; Johnston’s death removed overall commander; no exploitation of near-success.
**Union success factors:** Night reinforcement, Grant’s determination.
**Union shortcomings:** Complete lack of preparation for surprise; poor initial deployment.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — Confederates achieved local surprise but broader objective (destroy army before reinforcement) failed.
- **Offensive** — Honored by Confederates initially with great effect.
- **Mass** — Violated by Confederates through successive lines that lost cohesion.
- **Economy of Force** — Union fixed forces initially ineffective.
- **Maneuver** — Terrain (woods/ravines) defeated planned movements on both sides.
- **Unity of Command** — Poor on Confederate side after Johnston’s wounding; Grant absent at critical early moment.
- **Security** — Grossly violated by Union on April 6.
- **Surprise** — Achieved by Confederates but squandered by poor follow-up.
- **Simplicity** — Overly complex 4-line attack in difficult terrain.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Highlights the decisive effect of surprise and the difficulty of maintaining command and control in complex terrain with large citizen armies. Demonstrates the importance of fresh reserves and night reinforcement. Grant’s overnight resolve and shift in strategic thinking (from limited war to complete conquest) prefigure modern operational art lessons on adaptation.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- Primary: Neely chapter quotes emphasize tactical collapse despite operational concentration; the Grant-Sherman dialogue is iconic.
- Conflicting accounts: Range notes the shock of casualties compared to expectations.
- Modern scholarship: The West Point text stresses the battle as the moment both sides realized the war’s true nature.
