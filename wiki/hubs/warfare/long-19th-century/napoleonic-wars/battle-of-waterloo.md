> **Narrative & full sources:** [[napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars (event page)]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

The Battle of Waterloo, fought on 18 June 1815, was [[napoleon|Napoleon]]'s final battle and the last engagement of the [[napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars]]. Its outcome was determined by two interlocking factors: [[wellington-arthur-wellesley|Wellington]]'s use of the reverse-slope defensive position at Mont St. Jean to hold French assaults through a long afternoon, and the timely arrival of Blücher's Prussian army — three corps in all, against Napoleon's expectation that Grouchy's detachment would contain them — on the French right flank. As [[doughty-gruber-warfare-western-world-v1-1996|Doughty & Gruber]] summarize the outcome, "Wellington won the Battle of Waterloo because of the steadfastness of his troops and the arrival of Blücher's Prussian soldiers. Wellington asked for one Prussian corps, and Blücher brought three." The battle illustrates the final exhaustion of the [[napoleonic-warfare|Napoleonic system]] against an enemy that had learned defensive patience and inter-army coordination — themes continuous with the earlier Coalition victory at [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-leipzig|Leipzig (1813)]].

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

Napoleon's return from Elba in March 1815 — the Hundred Days — presented him with a strategic problem that had no clean solution. The Seventh Coalition mobilized immediately: Blücher moved into the Low Countries with 117,000 Prussians; Wellington commanded an Anglo-German-Dutch-Belgian force of 110,000; to the east, 210,000 Austrians under Schwarzenberg advanced toward the Rhine; 150,000 Russians under Barclay were marching across central Europe. Against this, France could field roughly 280,000 soldiers in uniform within two months of Napoleon's proclamation of mobilization in April, but only 120,000 were available for operations in Belgium after detachments for frontier defense and suppression of royalist insurrections. Napoleon had to act before these forces could coordinate and concentrate.

His strategic concept was characteristic: "he would move immediately, occupy a central position between Blücher and Wellington's armies, and defeat each separately." Speed and the initiative — the operational hallmarks of his earlier campaigns — were his only advantages. Once the full Coalition force assembled, the numbers would be overwhelming. The Belgian campaign was therefore a race: Napoleon had to destroy or neutralize Wellington and Blücher before the Austrians and Russians arrived.

His objectives on 18 June were direct: destroy Wellington's army at Mont St. Jean before Blücher's Prussians could arrive in force, then exploit that victory to split the Coalition politically and militarily. Wellington's objective was to hold his position long enough for Prussian support to arrive and transform a defensive holding action into a decisive allied counterattack.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

The position at Mont St. Jean was Wellington's chosen ground, selected in advance as "a favorable defensive position at the crossroads of Mont St. Jean, just south of Waterloo." The key terrain feature was the reverse slope: "with many of the allied soldiers on the reverse side of a slope at Mont St. Jean, where they were partially hidden and received some protection against enemy fire, Wellington concentrated his forces on a front of about 5,000 yards." This reverse-slope deployment — screening infantry from French artillery and obscuring unit dispositions from French observers — was Wellington's signature defensive technique and fundamentally shaped the battle's course.

Forward of the main line, Wellington placed troops "in the chateau of Hougoumont, on his right front; on his center in the walled farmhouse of La Haye-Sainte; and on his left in the farmhouses of Papelotte and La Haye." These fortified outposts forced attacking French infantry to reduce each obstacle under fire before engaging the main line, consuming men and momentum at each step.

Weather was operationally significant. The night of 17–18 June brought torrential rain. The rain-saturated ground caused Napoleon to delay his attack: "confident that Grouchy would prevent Blücher's Prussians from joining Wellington's army, Napoleon delayed his attack until the rain-saturated ground had dried enough to improve traction for horses and ensure a more deadly effect from ricocheting cannon balls." This delay — Napoleon would normally have attacked "soon after dawn, as was his custom" — proved catastrophic, as it gave Blücher's lead corps time to reach the battlefield. The ground conditions that motivated the delay directly enabled the Prussian arrival.

Intelligence failure was equally decisive. Napoleon sent Grouchy with 33,000 men — "almost one third of his total force" — to pursue and contain the Prussians after their defeat at Ligny. Grouchy "misinterpreted the movements of the Prussians, assumed the small enemy force in front of him was the entire Prussian force, and refused the pleas of his lieutenants to march to the sound of the guns." This error left Blücher with an open road to Waterloo. The French never developed accurate intelligence about Prussian movements during the critical morning hours.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Factor | France (Armée du Nord) | Coalition (Wellington + Blücher) |
|---|---|---|
| **Total at battle opening** | ~72,000 | Wellington: ~68,000 |
| **Prussian reinforcement** | — | 3 corps arriving progressively; ~30,000 engaged by 4:00 p.m., more by 7:00 p.m. |
| **Artillery** | 246 guns; more than 80 massed in front of Wellington's position | Wellington: 156 guns; Prussian batteries arriving with corps |
| **Infantry** | Mix of veterans and new recruits; Old Guard (9 battalions) held as last reserve | Allied infantry of varied quality; British line and Guards steady; some Dutch-Belgian units broke early |
| **Cavalry** | Available but misemployed — ~10,000 charged unsupported at allied squares | British heavy and light cavalry; Prussian cavalry arrived with Blücher |
| **Command** | Napoleon commanding overall; Ney entrusted with tactical decisions on battlefield; Grouchy commanding right wing detachment | Wellington commanding Anglo-allied army; Blücher commanding Prussians; strong inter-army coordination via dispatch |

Napoleon deployed his forces south of Wellington's ridge. The Imperial Guard was retained in reserve. Ney commanded the tactical execution of the main French attacks. Grouchy's 33,000-man detachment at Wavre represented a critical operational absence — one third of Napoleon's total force held away from the decisive point by a subordinate who failed to march to the guns.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

**Phase 1 — Morning: Hougoumont and the Artillery Bombardment (11:00 a.m.)**
Napoleon massed more than eighty cannon and opened bombardment of Wellington's position around 11:00 a.m. At 11:30, he sent a corps in a diversionary attack against the chateau of Hougoumont on Wellington's right, "hoping to induce Wellington to shift his reserves away from his center." Wellington's response was disciplined: "unwilling to commit his reserve to what was apparently a secondary effort, Wellington sent only a brigade to support the defenders at Hougoumont." The diversionary attack "quickly escalated into a major battle that occupied the attention of a corps for the entire day" — consuming far more French strength than intended while Wellington maintained his reserve intact.

**Phase 2 — Early Afternoon: D'Erlon's Corps Attack (1:30 p.m.)**
At 1:00 p.m., Ney launched the main French attack on La Haye-Sainte. General d'Erlon's four divisions advanced "in massive formations, each having a front of 160 yards. British artillery knocked down entire rows of soldiers in the massed divisions." The infantry reached Wellington's line but "concentrated fire from the allied infantry beat them back." In the pursuit, British heavy cavalry charged, routing d'Erlon's corps, then overextended toward the French artillery and were met with devastating fire, "losing 1,000 soldiers in minutes." Wellington used the window to reinforce his battered center. D'Erlon's attack had failed at enormous cost, but Wellington's cavalry had also been badly handled.

**Phase 3 — Mid-Afternoon: The Unsupported Cavalry Charges (3:00–6:00 p.m.)**
At approximately 3:00 p.m., knowing Grouchy would not arrive in time, Napoleon ordered an all-out attempt on La Haye-Sainte. Ney "led the attack and somehow became convinced the allies were retreating." What was ordered as a brigade cavalry charge escalated into a massive, unsupported cavalry assault: "more than 5,000 cavalrymen, unsupported by infantry or artillery, charged forward against the allied center, which quickly formed into twenty squares. Ultimately, other horsemen joined in the attack until almost 10,000 were charging across the fields." The cavalry swept over the allied artillery but crucially "failed to spike the pieces," allowing the guns to resume fire when the charges were repulsed. Without infantry support to hold ground, the cavalry could not exploit any success. Wave after wave broke against the allied squares.

**Phase 4 — Early Evening: La Haye-Sainte Falls and the Crisis (6:00–7:00 p.m.)**
After 6:00 p.m., French infantry and cavalry in combination finally captured La Haye-Sainte. "Heavy fire from an artillery battery rushed forward by Ney caused the allied center to waver, but Napoleon refused to commit the Old Guard." This was the moment of maximum allied vulnerability — had the Guard been committed immediately after La Haye-Sainte fell, the allied center might have broken. Instead Napoleon hesitated. Simultaneously, Blücher's troops had been pouring "out of the woods on Napoleon's right at 4:00 p.m."; a savage fight saw the Prussians capture the village of Plancenoit, then lose it to the Old Guard's Young Guard battalions and Old Guard, who "recaptured Plancenoit with bayonets." By 7:00 p.m., Napoleon's right was stabilized — but only temporarily, as a third Prussian corps was approaching.

**Phase 5 — Evening: The Guard's Final Attack and Collapse (7:00–8:00 p.m.)**
With a third Prussian corps approaching, Napoleon committed his last reserve — "nine battalions of the Old Guard." If they could exploit Ney's earlier success at La Haye-Sainte, "the British army would be cut in half and beaten." Wellington reinforced his line and deployed his infantry behind defensible terrain. "Unsupported by artillery or cavalry, the Guard received heavy allied artillery fire. When they reached Wellington's position, British infantrymen greeted them with concentrated, devastating musket fire. As the men in both armies watched, the Guard broke and began to retreat." The Old Guard had never before broken in battle. Its repulse destroyed French morale: "as shock and then panic swept through Napoleon's army, Wellington ordered his line forward; the Prussians also advanced." The French army disintegrated. "Napoleon attempted to halt the mass of fleeing soldiers, but he, too, soon had to flee."

The allied occupation army remained in France for three years. The post-Waterloo settlement reduced France to its 1790 boundaries — more restrictive than even the 1813 terms Napoleon had rejected. He was exiled to St. Helena, where he died in 1821.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**French errors:**

- *Detaching Grouchy with one third of the army.* Sending 33,000 men to contain a defeated Prussian force that turned out to be not contained at all was the campaign's single most consequential decision. Grouchy's failure to "march to the sound of the guns" compounded the error, but the root problem was Napoleon's underestimation of Blücher's ability to recover from Ligny and redirect toward Waterloo. The Prussian withdrawal toward Wavre (north) rather than Liège (east — toward the Prussian base) was a critical intelligence failure: Napoleon expected them to withdraw eastward along their lines of supply and be neutralized.

- *Delaying the morning attack.* The weather-driven delay of several hours gave Blücher time to reach the field. An early attack might have broken Wellington before the Prussians arrived. Napoleon's own assessment — that he would normally have attacked at dawn — implicitly acknowledges the cost of the delay.

- *Escalation at Hougoumont.* The diversionary attack on Hougoumont was supposed to draw Wellington's reserves; instead it consumed an entire French corps for the day without achieving its purpose. Operational discipline in limiting the diversionary effort was not maintained.

- *The unsupported cavalry charges.* Committing nearly 10,000 cavalry against infantry squares without infantry support or the ability to spike captured artillery was a fundamental tactical error. Ney's conviction that the allies were retreating appears to have been a misreading of Wellington's deliberate adjustment of his rear-area traffic. The charges expended irreplaceable cavalry strength for no gain.

- *Withholding the Guard at the crisis moment.* When La Haye-Sainte fell after 6:00 p.m. and the allied center wavered, Napoleon did not immediately commit the Old Guard. His hesitation — possibly caused by the developing Prussian threat on his right — allowed Wellington to stabilize the line. When the Guard was finally sent forward, it went without the combined-arms support (artillery, cavalry) that had made earlier Napoleonic assaults succeed.

**Coalition successes:**

- *Wellington's reverse-slope defense.* The deployment of infantry behind the ridge at Mont St. Jean was the battle's master tactical decision. It screened the allied infantry from French artillery, preserved Wellington's ability to shift reserves along interior lines unseen by French observers, and forced French attacking columns to crest the ridge into concentrated defensive fire at close range.

- *Blücher's commitment.* After the defeat at Ligny, Gneisenau's decision to withdraw north toward Wavre rather than east toward Liège kept the Prussian army in supporting distance of Wellington. Blücher's personal commitment to marching despite his own wounds, and his bringing three corps rather than the one Wellington had requested, was the decisive act of the campaign. "Wellington asked for one Prussian corps, and Blücher brought three."

- *Wellington's reserve discipline.* Throughout the battle — at Hougoumont, during d'Erlon's attack, during the cavalry charges — Wellington resisted committing his reserve to secondary threats. He held until the moment of the Guard's repulse, then ordered the general advance.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

**Objective** — *Honored by Coalition; lost by France.* Wellington's objective — hold until Prussian arrival — was clear and executed with consistent discipline. Napoleon's objective — destroy Wellington before Blücher arrived — was valid but the timeline was miscalculated. Every hour's delay in the morning moved the battle further from French viability.

**Offensive** — *Honored by France; ultimately futile.* Napoleon attacked throughout the day. The principle of offensive was honored, but the attacks were sequentially mismanaged (Hougoumont escalation, unsupported cavalry, Guard committed too late and without combined arms). Tactical offensive does not guarantee success if the execution degrades over successive phases.

**Mass** — *Coalition advantage; decisive when Prussians arrived.* French numerical superiority at battle opening (72,000 vs. 68,000) was marginal and eroded rapidly once Prussian corps began arriving. By evening Napoleon faced effective parity or inferiority on both his front and his right flank simultaneously — the same concentric mass pressure that had destroyed his army at [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-leipzig|Leipzig]].

**Economy of Force** — *Violated by France — the Grouchy detachment.* Sending one third of the army to contain a force that could not be contained was the most severe violation of economy of force in the campaign. The detachment accomplished nothing at Wavre while Blücher marched freely to Waterloo.

**Maneuver** — *Denied to France; exploited by Coalition.* Wellington's reverse-slope position denied Napoleon the ability to see and target his reserves. The Prussian approach from the east was maneuver at the operational level — a fresh force striking an engaged enemy's open flank — that the [[napoleonic-warfare|Napoleonic system]] had made famous but was now being used against its originator.

**Unity of Command** — *Stronger in Coalition than France.* Wellington and Blücher's coordination, maintained through dispatch riders and a prior agreement at Quatre Bras that Wellington would hold if supported, was operationally effective. On the French side, command was divided between Napoleon's operational direction and Ney's tactical execution; the disconnect between the two — evident in Ney's unauthorized escalation of the cavalry charges — was never resolved. Grouchy's failure to exercise judgment and march to the sound of the guns represents a subordinate command failure with catastrophic consequences.

**Security** — *Violated by France.* The failure to track Prussian movements after Ligny was the root intelligence failure of the campaign. Napoleon's assumption that Grouchy had neutralized the Prussians was never verified. Wellington's security was better: he received "a dispatch from Blücher, announcing that he was marching with at least two corps to support him."

**Surprise** — *Achieved by Blücher.* The Prussian arrival on Napoleon's right was not a surprise in the strategic sense — Napoleon knew they might come — but the timing, speed, and scale exceeded French assumptions. Napoleon "believed he could defeat Wellington's army before the Prussians could strike his flank." He was wrong. The surprise lay in Blücher's pace and commitment.

**Simplicity** — *Wellington's plan was simple; French plan degraded under pressure.* Wellington's concept — hold the ridge, protect the outposts, wait for the Prussians — was simple and robust. The French plan began with a reasonable concept (Hougoumont diversion, main attack on La Haye-Sainte) but fractured under the pressure of events: the diversion consumed a corps, d'Erlon's attack was repulsed, the cavalry charges were mishandled, and the Guard was committed too late.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Waterloo is one of the clearest historical examples of the combined-arms principle violated (French cavalry unsupported by infantry and artillery) against the combined-arms principle honored (Wellington's interlocked outpost defense, reserve infantry, and artillery). The French cavalry charges against unbroken infantry squares illustrate a lesson driven home repeatedly in military history: cavalry — or any single arm — cannot break prepared infantry positions without infantry and artillery support to neutralize defensive fires and hold captured ground. Modern doctrine's insistence on combined-arms integration across all echelons is in part a codification of lessons first starkly visible at Waterloo.

The reverse-slope defense remains a recognized technique in modern ground combat doctrine. Wellington's use of it at Waterloo is a canonical illustration of denying the enemy observation and direct fire while maintaining a defensible line. Contemporary doctrine recognizes the technique's continued relevance against direct fire weapon systems, with analogous applications in defilade positioning in mechanized warfare.

The Grouchy failure — a subordinate commander who refused to exercise initiative and march toward the decisive battle — is a case study in the tension between mission orders and hierarchical command. Prussian military reformers (Scharnhorst, Gneisenau, Clausewitz) drew explicit lessons from the Napoleonic era about the need for junior commanders capable of independent judgment. The German Auftragstaktik (mission-type tactics) that later characterized the Prussian and Imperial German army was partly a response to the systemic failure Grouchy exemplified: a command culture that waited for explicit orders rather than responding to unfolding operational reality.

The Waterloo campaign also illustrates the decisive effect of inter-army coordination — the same principle that underpinned the Trachenberg Plan in 1813. Coalition cohesion, maintained through political agreement and personal commitment between Wellington and Blücher, proved more robust than Napoleon's assumption that he could defeat each in turn.

## 8b. The Face of Battle — Keegan's Human Mechanics

[[keegan-face-of-battle-1976|John Keegan]] made Waterloo the central case of *The Face of Battle*
(1976), drawing on the unmatched deposit of British personal testimony to reconstruct the
[[face-of-battle-method|experience and mechanics]] beneath the operational narrative above. He
analyses the day not as five phases (which "were not perceived at the time by any of the
combatants") but by **categories of combat**:

- **Cavalry versus squares.** Steady infantry in square almost always beat cavalry, because the
  horses refused the steel: cuirassiers' mounts "came to a standstill, shaking and covered with
  foam, at about twenty yards distance." The cavalry "got a somewhat new direction, which carried
  them to either flank… which they all preferred to… riding on to our bayonets." Numbers explain it:
  a square's face presented ~140 muskets to a squadron's ~18 sabres.
- **Cavalry versus artillery.** Horsemen could "take" guns but not remove them, because "no
  cavalryman… seemed prepared" to dismount under fire; the crews fled to the squares and returned to
  fire into the cavalry's backs — "almost wholly one-sided."
- **Infantry columns broke from the rear.** Keegan's most striking finding: the French assault
  columns (and the Guard) collapsed because "it was at the back of the columns, not the front, that
  the collapse began." Men in the rear "could see nothing," felt only "tremors of movement, edging
  them rearward," and became a Canetti-style **"flight crowd."** "La Garde recule" was thus a
  crowd-psychology event, not a clash of physical mass.
- **Standing to be cannonaded.** The day's worst ordeal was passive: the Inniskillings lost ~450 of
  750 men "standing motionless to be cannonaded." Keegan calls this "about the most unpleasant thing
  that can happen to soldiers"; men begged, "are we to be massacred? Let us go and fight them."

On the **human face**, Keegan foregrounds factors the operational account omits: pervasive
**fatigue** (regiments that had marched fifty miles in two days and slept in the rain), suffocating
**smoke** ("we could not see ten yards off") and **noise** that left Mercer "almost deaf";
**drink** (gin guzzled in the ranks — though he judges alcohol had "no more than an indirect
effect"); and naked **compulsion** (British cavalry herding unreliable allied troops at sword-point,
officers and sergeants posted behind the ranks "to keep individual soldiers… from running away").
What ultimately held the British line, he concludes, was **group solidarity, the colours, and
officers' leadership by personal example** — for whom "honour" had become "an almost wholly abstract
ideal, a matter of comportment, of exposure to risk," demonstrated by **wounds received** rather
than enemies slain. The contrast with [[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/hundred-years-war/battle-of-agincourt-1415|Agincourt]] is explicit: at Waterloo smoke and gunfire dominate where Agincourt's noise
was "chiefly human and animal," and single combat is now an accident of broken formations rather
than the sought-after heart of battle.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

Waterloo is one of the most extensively documented battles in history, partly because so many participants wrote accounts and partly because its political stakes generated immediate controversy. Doughty & Gruber's account in chapter 9, grounded in the standard secondary literature, provides a clear operational narrative but does not adjudicate the detailed tactical controversies. Their interpretive line is clear: the battle was won by Wellington's steadfastness and Blücher's persistence, and lost by Napoleon's sequence of mismanaged attacks, the Grouchy detachment, and ultimately by the failure of the Guard.

The secondary literature cited in Doughty & Gruber's suggested readings includes Archibald Becke's *Napoleon and Waterloo* (1939), David Chandler's *Waterloo: The Hundred Days* (1980), David Howarth's *Waterloo: Day of Battle* (1968), and Jac Weller's *Wellington at Waterloo* (1967). The Hundred Days campaign more broadly is treated in Antony Brett-James's *The Hundred Days* (1964).

Key historiographical debates include: (1) whether Napoleon could have won had he attacked earlier in the morning; (2) whether the Guard's final attack was adequately supported — the question of whether Napoleon committed it "too late" and "without adequate combined-arms support" is central to assessments of French command; (3) the degree of Ney's responsibility for the mishandled cavalry charges versus Napoleon's; and (4) whether the campaign was winnable at all given the strategic situation — the view that Waterloo was structurally determined by Coalition mass (as at [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-leipzig|Leipzig]]) versus the view that specific command failures made the difference. Doughty & Gruber's account implicitly supports the latter: "he was defeated in the end by the prudent, persistent, and coordinated use of massive allied armies," but the specific failures at Waterloo — Grouchy, the late morning, the cavalry charges — were contingent, not inevitable.
