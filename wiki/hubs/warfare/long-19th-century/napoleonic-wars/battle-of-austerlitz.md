> **Narrative & full sources:** [[napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars (event page)]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/napoleonic-wars|Napoleonic Wars]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

Austerlitz, fought on 2 December 1805 — the first anniversary of [[napoleon|Napoleon]]'s coronation — stands as the most complete expression of the [[napoleonic-warfare]] system. Napoleon deliberately ceded the Pratzen Heights to bait the allied command into a flank attack, then struck the weakened allied center with overwhelming force while Davout's corps held the threatened right. In two hours of hard fighting on the heights, the allied army was split, enveloped from the south, and driven into the frozen Satschan and Menitz ponds. The destruction of roughly one-third of the allied force in a single day ended the War of the Third Coalition and reshaped the European balance of power, excluding Austria from both Italy and Germany.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

By October 1805 [[napoleon]] had already annihilated the Austrian army of General Mack at Ulm, capturing 27,000 men without a major engagement. He then marched toward Vienna, which French forces entered on 12 November. The allied strategic situation was dire: Russia's General Kutusov, recognizing he could not defend Vienna, withdrew northeast into Moravia, where the army was reinforced by Austrian remnants and additional Russian formations, eventually assembling near Olmütz at roughly 90,000 men.

Napoleon faced serious strategic vulnerability. His lines of communication stretched almost 400 miles back to France; the Prussians were wavering and could join the coalition; the army carried only eight days of rations; and the approaching winter would compound every difficulty. Doughty and Gruber note that the French "faced critical logistical and strategic problems: their long lines of communication were vulnerable to attack; they were menaced by a much larger enemy and a belligerent population." Napoleon recognized he could not force a strategic envelopment and resolved instead to "lure Alexander into a decisive engagement and end the campaign with one great battle."

His objective was to destroy the allied army in a single battle of annihilation — the decisive engagement that was the hallmark of the [[napoleonic-warfare]] system. Austria must be forced out of the war before Russia could regroup and before Prussia could be tempted off the fence.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

Napoleon reconnoitered the ground personally and chose the Pratzen Heights — a broad plateau between Brünn and Austerlitz rising some fifty to sixty feet above the surrounding plain — as the hinge of his deception plan. The plateau dominated the battlefield; whoever held it commanded fields of fire over both the northern and southern sectors. The Goldbach stream ran north-south west of the heights, channeling movement. To the south, the Satschan and Menitz ponds created a lethal obstacle with no viable exit for troops pressed against them. On the French left, Santon Hill anchored the northern flank and commanded the Brünn–Olmütz road.

The weather on the eve of battle was freezing, with fog thick in the low ground. On the morning of 2 December, that fog proved decisive: Doughty and Gruber describe Soult's two divisions, concealed by "thick fog and heavy smoke," as marching "out of the murk into the brilliant sun" to strike the Pratzen heights. Napoleon's intelligence operation was active and calculated. He sent an aide-de-camp to allied headquarters "ostensibly to appeal for an armistice" — a deception designed both to confirm allied dispositions and to suggest French weakness.

The allied commanders received poor intelligence in return. They observed that Napoleon had only thinly spread forces on his right flank, and they concluded — as Napoleon intended — that his army was too weak to hold the Pratzen if they attacked south of it. Kutusov had doubts about the plan but was overruled; the tsar "believed any shortcomings in their battle plans would be negated by deficiencies in Napoleon's army and dispositions. They had no idea they had been tricked."

| **Infantry** | Seven corps; battalions organized in brigades of ~3,600; six companies per battalion | Five columns; Russian and Austrian infantry in deep formations |
| **Cavalry** | Corps cavalry brigades plus large reserve cavalry corps (Murat) | Allied cavalry distributed across columns; Russian Imperial Guard cavalry |
| **Artillery** | 24–48 guns per corps; army artillery reserve; Gribeauval system | Approximately comparable; allied superiority partially offset by dispersal |
| **Command** | Napoleon (supreme); Soult (center), Davout (right), Lannes (left-center), Bernadotte (reserve), Imperial Guard reserve | Tsar Alexander I (political command); Kutusov (sidelined); Weyrother (plan); five column commanders operating semi-independently |

Deployment: Napoleon anchored his left on Santon Hill with a full corps straddling the Brünn–Olmütz road. He spread one division of Soult's corps thinly along six miles of the Goldbach stream — from the stream north through Sokolnitz to Tellnitz — to invite the allied flank attack. Davout's corps was marching from Vienna, nearly eighty miles away, and was counted on to reinforce the right as the battle developed. The Imperial Guard, additional artillery, and Bernadotte's corps were hidden behind a hill on the French left — the mass Napoleon intended to strike through the Pratzen.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

**Phase 1 — The Deception (30 November – dawn 2 December).** On 30 November French troops "feigning disorder, retired from the Pratzen heights." Allied forces moved up and occupied the high ground. By the eve of battle, Napoleon had confirmed that the allied main effort would strike his right flank near Tellnitz — he "soon concluded that the enemy would concentrate their attack about five or six miles south of the road, near Tellnitz" — and shifted Davout's arriving troops accordingly.

**Phase 2 — Allied Main Effort South (from ~8:30 A.M.).** Three massive infantry columns totaling ~34,000 men attacked Napoleon's weak right flank. "By 8:30 A.M., Russian troops had fought their way into the villages of Tellnitz and Sokolnitz on the French right." Allied generals stripped troops from their center on the Pratzen heights to feed the southern assault. The plan was working — but it was Napoleon's plan.

**Phase 3 — The Central Counterstroke (approximately 9:00 A.M.).** As the allied center weakened, Napoleon ordered Soult to advance with two divisions. Screened by the morning fog, they moved rapidly up the Pratzen heights. "Concealed by thick fog and heavy smoke, they marched out of the murk into the brilliant sun and fought their way up into the weakened enemy center." The fighting "raged fiercely with attacks and counterattacks unfolding in rapid succession." When the Russians committed their Imperial Guard in a counterattack, Napoleon responded by ordering the cavalry of the French Imperial Guard forward and feeding in a division from Bernadotte's corps. By 2:00 P.M. the allied center had collapsed.

**Phase 4 — The Southern Envelopment (afternoon).** Napoleon pivoted the Pratzen force southward. The allied columns attacking near Tellnitz and Sokolnitz now found French troops on their northern flank and the Satschan and Menitz ponds at their backs. "Hundreds of Russians surrendered, but thousands attempted to escape, some fleeing across the ice of the ponds. Napoleon personally ordered an artillery battery forward to bombard the fugitives. The cannonballs and the weight of fleeing troops broke the ice, and hundreds of allied troops fell into the freezing, waist-deep water."

**Phase 5 — Collapse and End of Action.** On the northern flank the allied right wing broke contact and retreated. Darkness ended the pursuit. The allied army had lost approximately one-third of its strength in a single day.

Strategic consequences: Tsar Alexander I withdrew his army toward Hungary. Austrian Emperor Francis II sought terms, meeting Napoleon on 3 December and agreeing to armistice. The Treaty of Pressburg (26 December 1805) excluded Austria from Italy and reduced its influence in Germany decisively. The Third Coalition dissolved. Doughty and Gruber note that "although Napoleon expected Russia to seek peace, the defeated and humiliated Russian tsar saw Austerlitz as only the first phase in a continuing struggle."

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**French decisions that succeeded:**

- *The deliberate surrender of the Pratzen Heights.* This was the master stroke. By withdrawing from dominating ground and spreading forces thinly on the right, Napoleon manufactured the allied plan for them. "He carefully avoided concentrating his entire army in this position, for he hoped to lure the allies into an attack." An opponent with better intelligence or a more skeptical headquarters would have detected the trap.

- *Timing the central strike to fog and allied commitment.* Soult's attack was held until the allied columns had committed to the southern flank and had weakened the Pratzen center — a judgment Napoleon made in real time as allied dispositions became clear.

- *Incorporating Davout's march of nearly eighty miles.* Counting on a corps arriving from Vienna by forced march to anchor the right, and positioning it precisely to meet the main allied blow when it arrived, demonstrates the operational flexibility the corps system enabled.

- *The southern pivot after securing the heights.* Turning Soult's divisions south to cut off the allied flank attack rather than pursuing northward was the decision that converted a tactical victory into a strategic annihilation.

**Allied decisions that failed:**

- *Dismissing Kutusov's doubts and concentrating planning in the tsar's personal staff.* "Rather than seek the advice of Kutusov or the other senior generals, he turned to his personal aides and the Austrian staff officer who had guided the Russians across Austria."

- *Stripping the Pratzen center to reinforce the southern attack.* This was the fatal error — abandoning commanding ground in response to apparent French weakness on the right.

- *No exploitation of the northern sector.* The allied 30,000 troops on the French left facing Santon Hill were effectively neutralized and accomplished nothing decisive.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

**Objective** — *Honored.* Napoleon's objective was specific and measurable: destroy the allied army in a single engagement to end the campaign before Prussian intervention or logistical collapse. Every operational choice served that goal. The allies' objective was less coherent — they sought to interpose between Napoleon and Vienna, a positional aim that dispersed effort.

**Offensive** — *Honored (French); Violated (Allies).* Napoleon seized the initiative from the moment he withdrew from the Pratzen heights, forcing the allies to execute his preferred plan. Though initially appearing defensive, the French were always setting conditions for the attack. The allied operation, despite moving forward, was reactive throughout.

**Mass** — *Honored (French) at the decisive point.* Napoleon concentrated Soult's two hidden divisions precisely where the allied center had weakened, achieving mass at the moment of opportunity. He also concentrated Bernadotte's corps and the Imperial Guard as exploitation force. Allied forces, attacking in five separate columns without unified command, achieved mass nowhere.

**Economy of Force** — *Honored.* The thin screen along the Goldbach, Sokolnitz, and Tellnitz was the economy-of-force element — a calculated sacrifice of ground to preserve strength for the decisive blow. Davout's corps performed the same function in a more active sense, holding the right against far superior numbers.

**Maneuver** — *Honored (French).* The southward pivot of Soult's forces from the Pratzen heights to envelop the allied southern columns is a textbook application of maneuver to achieve positional advantage. The corps system gave Napoleon the ability to shift mass rapidly. Doughty and Gruber observe that the [[napoleonic-warfare]] system required corps to "live off the countryside and concentrate swiftly with other corps when necessary."

**Unity of Command** — *Honored (French); Violated (Allies).* Napoleon exercised direct command throughout. Allied command was fractured: Alexander ignored Kutusov; Weyrother's complex plan required coordinated execution from five column commanders; when the situation deteriorated, no single commander could redirect the effort.

**Security** — *Honored (French) through active deception.* The dispatch of an aide to allied headquarters "ostensibly to appeal for an armistice," the feigned disorder before the Pratzen, and the concealment of reserves behind the ridge constituted an active security/deception operation. The allies violated security entirely — they believed Napoleon's weakness was real.

**Surprise** — *Honored.* The allies were completely surprised by the timing and direction of Soult's central assault. "They had no idea they had been tricked." The fog masked the movement; the prior deception masked the intent.

**Simplicity** — *Mixed.* The French plan was bold but required precise timing: the central strike could not come too early (before the allied center weakened) or too late (after the allied southern attack overwhelmed Davout). Allied plans violated simplicity — five columns, complex approach marches, uncoordinated axes of advance.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Austerlitz anticipates several features of twentieth-century operational doctrine. The deliberate shaping of the enemy's decision-making — creating conditions where the adversary adopts the course of action you want — parallels what modern doctrine calls shaping operations. Napoleon's use of the corps as independently capable combined-arms formations capable of fighting and fixing an enemy while the army concentrates elsewhere foreshadows the operational structure of twentieth-century armored warfare; the same principle of dispersed advance and rapid concentration appears in German Auftragstaktik and in the 1944 Normandy breakout.

The "inner game" at Austerlitz — winning by making the opponent execute a plan that serves your interests rather than theirs — also anticipates modern information operations and the doctrinal concept of cognitive advantage. The tsar's headquarters was working from a false intelligence picture that Napoleon had actively constructed. Doughty and Gruber's broader argument is that Napoleon "went completely beyond the operational and tactical conventions of [[limited-war]]" — the engagement of the entire [[nation-in-arms]] created armies large and fast enough to make this kind of deep deception feasible; no eighteenth-century force could have held its screen thin enough and reinforced fast enough to execute the Austerlitz plan.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

Doughty and Gruber treat Austerlitz as the paradigmatic expression of Napoleon's mature system. They ground the battle's meaning in the structural argument of Chapter 7: that [[napoleonic-warfare]] transformed "the scale, speed, and decisiveness of warfare" by fusing the [[nation-in-arms]] social base with the corps system's operational flexibility. Austerlitz is their primary exhibit for this thesis — the battle where every element of the system worked simultaneously.

The book cites David G. Chandler's *The Campaigns of Napoleon* (1966) as a key reference, along with Christopher J. Duffy's *Austerlitz, 1805* (1977) and John R. Elting's *Swords Around a Throne* (1988). Chandler remains the standard operational account in English. The controversy in the historiography is less about the battle itself (the outline is well-established) than about Napoleon's generalship: whether Austerlitz represents genius or the exploitation of an opponent so poor (Alexander ignoring Kutusov, Weyrother's complex plan) that the outcome was overdetermined. Doughty and Gruber acknowledge allied command failure — "Rather than seek the advice of Kutusov" — but clearly attribute the outcome to Napoleon's deliberate engineering of the situation rather than merely to allied mistakes.

The book's casualty figures (French ~8,000, allied ~27,000 including 13,000 prisoners) are conventional and consistent with most secondary accounts. Precise figures remain subject to dispute in specialist literature, as is typical for battles of this period.
