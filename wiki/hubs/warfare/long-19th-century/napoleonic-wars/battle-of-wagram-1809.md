# Battle of Wagram, 1809 — Campaign & Operational Analysis

> **Narrative:** Part of the 1809 Franco-Austrian War. **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/napoleonic-wars]] · **Period:** [[long-19th-century]]

The climactic two-day battle (5–6 July 1809) on the Marchfeld plain that ended the 1809 campaign. Napoleon’s last clear victory on the battlefield before the strains of later years. Epstein presents it as the moment when the Napoleonic system reached full operational maturity (corps-enabled distributed maneuver across theaters) while simultaneously displaying the attrition, scale, and resilience that would characterize "modern" 19th-century warfare.

## Strategic Context & Objectives

After Aspern-Essling (May 1809), Napoleon paused to rebuild artillery superiority and secure his southern flank. Eugene’s Army of Italy was ordered to neutralize Archduke John and effect junction with the main army. The French crossed the Danube from Lobau island in early July with the goal of forcing Charles into a decisive battle before Austrian reinforcements or British diversions could intervene.

Charles deployed in a long defensive arc centered on the Russbach heights, aiming to absorb the French attack, preserve his army, and (on 6 July) envelop the exposed French left with Kollowrat and Klenau.

## Terrain, Weather & Intelligence

The Marchfeld offered an enormous ~25 square mile / 14-mile front battlefield—far larger than prior Napoleonic fights—limiting direct commander influence and favoring corps-level initiative. Key features included the Russbach stream (Austrian main line), Wagram village, Aderklaa, and the villages of Aspern/Essling/Süssenbrünn to the south. Flat terrain favored artillery and cavalry but complicated command.

## Order of Battle & Deployment (per Epstein/Chandler)

French (Napoleon):
- Right: Grouchy cavalry + Davout III Corps (toward Markgrafneusiedl)
- Center: Oudinot II, Eugene’s Army of Italy, Bernadotte IX (later withdrawn), Massena IV
- Reserve: Bessières cavalry + Imperial Guard at Raasdorf

Austrian (Charles):
- Left wing on Russbach Heights (Rosenberg, Hohenzollern, Bellegarde + reserves)
- Right wing (Kollowrat, Klenau) for potential envelopment of French left
- Reuss V in reserve

## Course of the Battle (phases)

**5 July (first phase):** French attacks on the Russbach were largely uncoordinated as units were still arriving. Oudinot repulsed at Baumersdorf. Eugene gained ground on the plateau but became isolated and fell back in night confusion. Charles was satisfied and planned an envelopment for the next day.

**6 July:** 
- Bernadotte’s unauthorized withdrawal from Aderklaa allowed Austrians to seize the village; Napoleon sacked him and shifted Massena northward, exposing the French left/rear.
- Davout’s corps (with massive artillery superiority) crossed the Russbach, took Markgrafneusiedl, and began rolling up the Austrian left.
- Klenau and Kollowrat advanced aggressively against the French left/center. Napoleon committed Macdonald’s corps (in a deep box formation with heavy artillery support from Nansouty and the Guard) to stabilize and counterattack. The French center held; Davout and supporting attacks from Oudinot/Marmont/Eugene broke the Austrian left. Charles, with reserves exhausted and John arriving too late, ordered retreat.

## Outcome & Casualties

French victory on points but not annihilation. Neither army disintegrated in the classic Napoleonic sense. Wagram demonstrated the resilience conferred by corps organization on both sides.

Casualties (Epstein): French ~37,568; Austrian ~41,750. The Army of Italy alone lost over 6,000 of ~20,300 engaged.

## Critical Decisions

- Charles’s choice to stand on the defensive and his failure to commit V Corps (Reuss) decisively.
- Bernadotte’s withdrawal from Aderklaa and Napoleon’s reaction (sacking him mid-battle).
- Napoleon’s artillery buildup after Aspern and use of captured Austrian guns.
- Eugene/Macdonald’s deep formation and Davout’s turning movement.

## Lessons (Principles of War)

- **Mass / Economy of Force**: French artillery concentration and use of the Army of Italy as masse de rupture.
- **Maneuver**: Distributed approach from Italy + Germany; Davout’s flank attack.
- **Unity of Command**: Contrast between French (mission orders, initiative) and Austrian (more centralized after early defeats).
- **Security / Surprise**: French deception and rapid concentration; Austrian failure to exploit after Aspern fully.
- **Simplicity**: Napoleon’s verbal orders vs. Charles’s more static plan.

## Modern Doctrine Parallels

Corps-level distributed operations, sequenced battles on broad fronts, cumulative rather than single-battle decision, and the need for decentralized execution under a unifying operational concept all prefigure 19th- and 20th-century practice (Moltke, etc.). The relational nature of war (symmetric opponents) limits "genius" results.

## Historiography & Primary Sources

Epstein uses Chandler, Petre, Bowden/Tarbox, and primary correspondence (Napoleon’s notes to Eugene, etc.). Emphasizes that 1809 must be understood relationally: Austrian reforms + French corps system produced a different dynamic than 1805–1807. Wagram is presented as the first clear example of the "emergence of modern war."
