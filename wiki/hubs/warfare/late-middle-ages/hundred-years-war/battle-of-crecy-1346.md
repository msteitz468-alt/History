# Battle of Crécy (1346) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/battle-of-crecy-1346|Battle of Crécy]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/hundred-years-war/hundred-years-war|Hundred Years' War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college-level analysis of the battle that announced the English **missile-and-dismounted**
system to Europe. Drawn from the [[cambridge-history-of-war-v2|Cambridge History of War, Vol. 2]]
(Anne Curry, ch. 13; Kelly DeVries on the weapons, ch. 23), whose argument is that Crécy was the
first full vindication of a *recruited, paid, longbow-based army* against the larger but
"amorphous" feudal host of France.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**England (Edward III).** Crécy was the climax of a **chevauchée** — a mounted devastation raid
Edward had driven through Normandy in 1346 to ruin French finances, assert his claim to the crown,
and provoke or accept battle on his own terms. Curry calls it the campaign "prompted by the king's
desire to make a truly effective challenge to the French, which was achieved at Crécy." With the
Somme behind him and Philip pursuing, Edward chose to **stand and fight on prepared ground** rather
than be run down on the march — the recurring problem of the levied troops' "lack of mobility in
the chevauchée."

**France (Philip VI).** Philip's objective was to **catch and crush** the raiders who were burning
his kingdom and humiliating his kingship. He could raise far larger numbers — but, Curry stresses,
French armies were "amorphous and poorly coordinated between the different types of troops, which
helps to explain their vulnerability in battle against the English."

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground.** Edward deployed on a low **ridge between Crécy and Wadicourt**, his flanks anchored
  on the villages and broken ground, forcing the French to attack uphill across a constricted
  frontage that funnelled and disordered their charges.
- **Weather.** A rain shower before the action is traditionally said to have slackened the strings
  of the Genoese crossbows (the longbowmen could unstring and protect theirs) — consistent with the
  volume's repeated point that wet weather degraded bows.
- **Intelligence / tempo.** Edward had time to choose and prepare the position; the French arrived
  **piecemeal and late in the day**, committing to battle without assembling or resting — the
  coordination failure Curry identifies as endemic.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | England | France |
|---|---|---|
| Total | c. 14,000–15,000 (Curry/CHW Vol. 2) | Substantially larger; uncounted |
| Infantry | c. 8,000 foot, **the majority longbowmen** | Hired **Genoese crossbowmen** as the missile arm |
| Cavalry / men-at-arms | Men-at-arms **dismounted** to fight on foot beside the archers | Mounted **French chivalry** — the main striking arm |
| Command | Edward III in overall command; the **Black Prince** commanding the right "battle" | Philip VI — but control fractured as contingents arrived |
| Posture | Defensive, on a chosen ridge; archers on the wings/flanks of the men-at-arms | Repeated frontal attacks uphill |

*Deployment (as a map would show it):* English men-at-arms dismounted in divisions ("battles") along
the ridge, with **longbowmen massed on the flanks** to enfilade attackers; the French crossbowmen
sent forward first, then wave after wave of mounted men-at-arms charging the same narrow front.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **The crossbow duel.** The Genoese opened the action and were **outranged and overwhelmed** by
   the longbowmen's volume of fire — drawn "to the cheek," delivering "more power and distance than
   a shorter bow," at a far higher rate than the slow, pavise-dependent crossbow. The Genoese broke.
2. **The French men-at-arms charge through their own missile troops.** Contemptuous of the
   retreating Genoese, the mounted nobility pressed forward — into and over their own crossbowmen —
   beginning the disorder that defined the day.
3. **Successive uphill charges shot to pieces.** Wave after wave of French chivalry struck the
   English line and were broken by enfilading longbow fire and the dismounted men-at-arms holding
   the crest. The charges went in "amorphous and poorly coordinated," never massed.
4. **Attrition into the night.** The French renewed the assault repeatedly into darkness without
   result. Among the dead was the **blind King John of Bohemia**, who — the volume notes — had
   provided 500 men-at-arms and died at Crécy; his end became the chivalric epitaph of the battle.

**Key tactical mechanisms:** missile superiority (rate of fire × range × massed flanking position);
a chosen defensive ridge that constricted and channelled the attacker; and the self-inflicted
**disorder** of an uncoordinated host charging piecemeal.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | England | France |
|---|---|---|
| Killed | Light (consensus; figures unrecorded) | Very heavy among the mounted nobility |
| Notable dead | — | **John, blind king of Bohemia** (CHW Vol. 2); a large share of the French chivalry |
| Source(s) | Curry/CHW Vol. 2; Froissart | Curry/CHW Vol. 2; Froissart |

- **Strategic result:** a decisive English victory that freed Edward to march on and **besiege
  Calais**, taken after a twelve-month siege in 1347 — a permanent English bridgehead in France.
- **Consequence:** Crécy validated the longbow-and-dismounted system that would win **Poitiers
  (1356)** and **Agincourt (1415)**, and announced the late-medieval **infantry revolution**.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**English success factors.** (1) Choosing to **fight on prepared, constricting ground**; (2)
**dismounting** the men-at-arms to anchor the line; (3) **massing longbowmen on the flanks** for
enfilade; (4) the system behind it — a **paid, contract-raised army** Edward had deliberately built
(income-graded recruitment: "a 100s landowner was to be… an archer"); (5) fire discipline that
broke the Genoese and then each charge in turn.

**French shortcomings.** (1) **Attacking piecemeal and uphill** without assembling; (2) **no unity
of command** over an "amorphous" host; (3) riding down their own crossbowmen; (4) repeating failed
frontal charges instead of manoeuvring; (5) committing tired troops late in the day.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — England's was disciplined (survive and bloody the pursuer on chosen ground);
  France's degenerated from "catch the raiders" into uncoordinated charges with no scheme.
- **Offensive** — France held the nominal offensive but squandered it; England's "offensive" was
  the killing power of massed archery from a defensive posture.
- **Mass** — England **massed missile fire**; France had numbers but never massed them — the
  decisive distinction Curry draws.
- **Economy of Force** — Dismounting the men-at-arms and posting archers on the flanks economised a
  smaller army perfectly; France wasted its Genoese and its successive waves.
- **Maneuver** — England used **terrain** as maneuver; France attempted none, charging the same
  front repeatedly.
- **Unity of Command** — England's single, respected command vs. France's fractured control of
  contingents arriving piecemeal — **violated by France**.
- **Security** — England secured its flanks on villages and broken ground; France secured nothing,
  not even its own crossbowmen's line of retreat.
- **Surprise** — England's surprise was qualitative: the **volume and range** of longbow fire that
  the French missile arm could not match.
- **Simplicity** — England's plan was simple and rehearsed; France's was improvised in contact.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Crécy is the medieval archetype of **defensive fires breaking a superior attacker** — the chosen
position, the constricted engagement area, and the massed-flanking missile arm prefigure the logic
of the prepared killing ground. It is also the classic demonstration that **a coherent system beats
mass**: a smaller, paid, drilled, single-commanded force defeats a larger but unarticulated host —
the same theme Curry traces forward to Agincourt and that runs through the wiki's
[[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|warfare hub]] (combined arms and articulation as enduring multipliers).

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

The literary tradition is dominated by **Froissart**, whose *Chronicles* gave Crécy its enduring,
chivalric shape — vivid but late and shaped for a noble audience; the volume quotes his framing of
honour-in-war and should be read as interpretation, not field report. Modern scholarship
(Andrew Ayton, Clifford Rogers) has reconstructed the **logistics and recruitment** behind the
army from administrative records, the approach Curry champions.

**Caveat on this page.** CHW Vol. 2 is a **survey**. It firmly grounds the **army's size and
composition** (14,000–15,000, c. 8,000 foot, mostly longbowmen), the **French coordination
failure**, and the **death of the blind king of Bohemia** — but it does *not* give a
phase-by-phase reconstruction or hard casualty totals. The phase narrative above follows the
scholarly consensus the volume points to; precise numbers are deliberately left qualitative.
Full operational depth belongs to **Andrew Ayton & Philip Preston, *The Battle of Crécy, 1346***
(2005) and **Clifford Rogers, *War Cruel and Sharp*** — both cited by the volume and **flagged
outstanding** in the roadmap.
