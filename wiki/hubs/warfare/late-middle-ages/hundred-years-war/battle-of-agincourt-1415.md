# Battle of Agincourt (1415) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/battle-of-agincourt-1415|Battle of Agincourt]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/hundred-years-war/hundred-years-war|Hundred Years' War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college-level analysis of the English **missile-and-dismounted** system at its peak. Drawn
from the [[cambridge-history-of-war-v2|Cambridge History of War, Vol. 2]] (Anne Curry, ch. 13 —
Curry is the leading modern authority on the battle and on the soldiers who fought it). The volume
treats Agincourt less as a tale of chivalry than as proof of the **paid, recruited, longbow-based
army of the fiscal state** and of the chronic French failure to coordinate "the different types of
troops."

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**England (Henry V).** Agincourt closed a campaign that began with Henry's capture of **Harfleur**
(autumn 1415). With his army reduced by the siege and by dysentery, Henry chose a hard **march to
Calais** rather than re-embark — a calculated assertion of his claim and his soldiers' "high
reputation." The French moved to block him; Henry's object on 25 October was simply to **break
through and survive**, accepting battle on terms a worn, outnumbered army could exploit. (Curry and
Rogers debate whether Henry sought or merely accepted the battle — the volume flags this as a live
question about "the mindset of medieval commanders.")

**France (the royal army, Charles VI being incapacitated).** Led by the Constable **d'Albret** and
Marshal **Boucicaut**, with the great Armagnac lords, the French aimed to **destroy the invader**
and avenge a generation of humiliations. Their problem was the one Curry names throughout: a large
but **uncoordinated** host, here worsened by a command diluted among quarrelling magnates with the
king absent.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground.** The decisive factor. The French had to be fought in a **narrow defile between two
  woods** (Agincourt and Tramecourt) that compressed their wide superiority into a frontage no
  larger than the English line — negating their numbers exactly as the ridge had at Crécy.
- **Weather.** Heavy rain on **recently ploughed ground** turned the field to deep **mud**, fatal to
  the dismounted French men-at-arms who had to cross it in heavy armour under arrow fire.
- **Intelligence / tempo.** Henry **advanced his line** to within longbow range to provoke the
  French into attacking across the mud — seizing the initiative from a defensive posture, the
  signature of the English system.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | England | France |
|---|---|---|
| Total | c. 6,000, worn by Harfleur and the march | Substantially larger; numbers disputed |
| Infantry / missile | The great majority **longbowmen** | Dismounted men-at-arms as the main assault; crossbowmen present but marginal |
| Men-at-arms | A core of **dismounted** men-at-arms in the centre and "battles" | The chivalry of France, mostly **dismounted** for the assault, with mounted wings |
| Command | **Henry V**, single and personal | d'Albret & Boucicaut nominally, but command fractured among the lords |
| Posture | Defensive, in a chosen defile; archers on the flanks behind **sharpened stakes** | Massed assault across mud on a constricted front |

*Deployment:* English men-at-arms in the centre, **longbowmen massed on the flanks** behind a hedge
of planted stakes (a counter to cavalry learned over the war); the French packed into the narrow
gap in successive lines so deep they could not deploy or manoeuvre.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **The English advance and opening volleys.** Henry moved his line forward and the longbowmen
   opened a galling fire, provoking the French to attack.
2. **The mounted French wings fail.** The cavalry charges against the archers were broken by arrow
   fire and the **stakes**, and recoiled into the advancing dismounted men-at-arms — seeding
   disorder, the Crécy pattern repeated.
3. **The dismounted men-at-arms bog down.** The main French line struggled across deep mud under
   arrow storm, crowded into a front too narrow to use its numbers; the press became so dense that
   men could barely raise their weapons or recover if they fell.
4. **Collapse, slaughter, and the prisoners.** English men-at-arms and archers (the latter taking up
   hand weapons) fell on the stalled, exhausted mass and overwhelmed it. Vast numbers of French
   were killed or taken; at a fraught moment Henry ordered the **killing of prisoners** for fear of
   a renewed attack — the battle's enduring moral controversy.

**Key tactical mechanisms:** terrain-and-weather (defile + mud) nullifying numbers; massed flanking
archery; anti-cavalry **stakes**; and the lethal **self-crowding** of an over-deep attacker on a
narrow front.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | England | France |
|---|---|---|
| Killed | Light (consensus; debated) | Very heavy among the nobility |
| Notable dead / taken | — | The **Constable d'Albret** killed; the dukes of Orléans and Bourbon captured; many great lords dead or prisoner |
| Source(s) | Curry/CHW Vol. 2; *Gesta Henrici Quinti* | Curry/CHW Vol. 2 |

- **Strategic result:** a victory so complete it shattered the French command class and opened the
  way to Henry's **conquest of Normandy** and the **Treaty of Troyes (1420)**, which recognised him
  as heir to the French throne.
- **Consequence:** the apogee of the longbow system and of English fortunes in the war — the high
  point from which the French recovery (artillery, standing companies) would eventually reverse the
  verdict by 1453.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**English success factors.** (1) Accepting battle in a **defile** that cancelled French numbers; (2)
**advancing to provoke** the attack across mud; (3) **stakes + flanking archers** to defeat cavalry;
(4) the dismounted, disciplined, **single-commanded** line; (5) the underlying **system** — recruited
mixed retinues of men-at-arms and mounted archers raised by indenture.

**French shortcomings.** (1) Fighting in a **constricting defile** instead of forcing battle in the
open; (2) **no unity of command** with the king absent and the magnates at odds; (3) attacking across
**mud** in heavy armour under fire; (4) packing the front so deep that numbers became a **liability**;
(5) the failed mounted charges disordering their own assault.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — Henry's was clear and limited (break through to Calais); the French object
  dissolved into an uncoordinated rush.
- **Offensive** — England seized the initiative from the defensive by **advancing to provoke**;
  France held the nominal offensive and wasted it.
- **Mass** — England massed **fire**; France massed **bodies** on a front too narrow to use them —
  mass turned against its owner.
- **Economy of Force** — Stakes and flanking archers let a worn 6,000 defeat a much larger army.
- **Maneuver** — England used **terrain and weather** as maneuver; France attempted none in the
  defile.
- **Unity of Command** — England's single command vs. France's fractured magnate council — the
  recurring **violation** Curry stresses.
- **Security** — England secured its flanks (woods) and its archers (stakes); France secured neither
  order nor footing.
- **Surprise** — The English **advance** and the killing power of massed archery in the mud.
- **Simplicity** — A simple, rehearsed defensive-provocation plan vs. an improvised, crowded assault.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Agincourt is the canonical lesson that **terrain and conditions can cancel numerical superiority**:
the defile and the mud were force-multipliers worth more than any count of lances, a staple of
modern engagement-area design. It is equally the lesson that **mass without articulation is a
liability** — an over-concentrated force on a constricted front loses the ability to fight, the
medieval mirror of modern warnings about culmination and over-stacking. The prisoner-killing
episode anchors discussions of the **law of armed conflict** and command under perceived threat
(linking to the [[just-war|just-war]] and customs-of-war material in CHW Vol. 2 ch. 22).

## 8b. The Face of Battle — Keegan's Human Mechanics

[[keegan-face-of-battle-1976|John Keegan]] made Agincourt the first of his three case studies in
*The Face of Battle* (1976), and his reconstruction (by the [[face-of-battle-method|physical-evidence
method]], since no common soldier's voice survives) substantially supplies the phase-and-experience
layer the operational sources leave thin:

- **What the longbow actually did.** Against plate, not much: fifteenth-century armour "was composed
  almost completely of steel sheet" shaped "to offer a glancing surface," so high-trajectory arrows
  "cannot… have done a great deal of harm… to the men-at-arms." Their effect was **moral and
  acoustic** — "a weird clanking and banging on the bowed heads and backs of the French." Horses,
  armoured only on the face and chest, were far more vulnerable; the arrow-storm channelled and
  enraged the French rather than shooting them down.
- **The killing was done by the crush, not the arrow.** Keegan's central mechanism: the French
  first division, packed eight to ten deep, suffered "unrelenting pressure from the rear… driving
  them steadily into the weapon-strokes of the English," denying each man "that margin of room…
  essential if men are to defend themselves." Fallen men became "a very effective stumbling block,"
  and the **tumbling effect** "would have become cumulative."
- **The "wall of dead" demythologised.** Bodies "do not… pile into walls, but lie in shapeless
  sprawling hummocks"; the chronicle image of six-foot heaps is "ludicrous rather than grisly." What
  happened was **envelopment** — archers and men-at-arms "toppled them over on top of each other…
  and killed them on the ground." The unarmoured archers, swinging mallets and axes, did the close
  butchery "two or three against one," each "act of execution… only a few seconds."
- **The killing of the prisoners, re-read.** Comprehensible "in harsh tactical logic" (a feared
  third charge plus the baggage-raid), but Keegan argues two hundred archers could not have
  slaughtered well over a thousand armoured men; the order "was intended by its threat to terrorize
  them into abject inactivity" — in practice "a noisy and bloody cattle-drive to the rear," lethal
  but "not… in thousands, perhaps not even in hundreds."
- **Compulsion and the wounded.** Both armies were driven as much by **compulsion** as courage — the
  English by hunger and cold ("movement at least meant… body heat"), many French swept in "with all
  the free-will of a man… going the wrong way on a moving-staircase." The French wounded faced a far
  graver prognosis than the English: penetrating wounds meant peritonitis and sepsis, falls in
  armour meant "broken backs," and exposure overnight in near-freezing temperatures killed many
  where they lay.

This layer turns the page's earlier "the volume gives no phase detail" caveat from a gap into a
genuinely reconstructed account of the conduct of the fight.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

The chaplain's eyewitness *Gesta Henrici Quinti* is the indispensable English source, partisan to
Henry's providential image. **Anne Curry's** revisionist work — built on the financial and muster
records of the English army and on a critical re-reading of the French sources — has **narrowed the
traditional numerical gap** (the old "tiny English army vs. vast French horde" is now contested) and
reframed the battle as a triumph of system and ground rather than miracle. The **prisoner-killing**
remains the central moral and evidentiary controversy.

**Caveat on this page.** CHW Vol. 2 is a **survey**: Curry's chapter gives the *system*, the
strategic frame, and the coordination failure with full authority, but — by design — not a
phase-by-phase reconstruction or settled orders of battle within these pages. The phase narrative
and figures above follow the consensus of Curry's own dedicated studies; numbers are kept
qualitative where the volume does not fix them. Note that the **conduct and mechanics** of the fight
(section 8b) are now well grounded in [[keegan-face-of-battle-1976|Keegan]]'s reconstruction —
ingested 2026-06-24 — so the remaining gap is operational/numerical, not experiential. For the full reconstruction see **Anne Curry,
*Agincourt: A New History*** (2005) and **Curry, *The Battle of Agincourt: Sources and
Interpretations*** (2000) — **flagged outstanding** in the roadmap (note: held only via this survey
chapter).
