# The Burgundian Wars (1476–77) — Campaign Analysis

> **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/rise-of-the-swiss/rise-of-the-swiss|Rise of the Swiss]] · **Narrative:** [[events/burgundian-wars-1476|Burgundian Wars]] · **Actors:** [[valois-burgundian-state|Valois Burgundy]] · [[swiss-confederation|Swiss Confederation]]

The campaign in which the Swiss pike phalanx destroyed the most modern combined-arms army in Europe
— [[valois-burgundian-state|Charles the Bold]]'s Burgundy — in three battles, drawn from
[[delbruck-medieval-warfare-vol-3-1982|Hans Delbrück, *History of the Art of War*, Vol. III]], Book
V. Delbrück insists, against the patriotic myth, that this was an **offensive war** of Bernese
expansion bankrolled by Louis XI of France — "not… a war of liberation or even only a defensive
war… but an offensive war" — and that the Swiss were consistently the **larger** army.

## Strategic context

Charles the Bold's Burgundy was a rich, ambitious state fielding a deliberately modernised army of
**heavy horse, massed marksmen, dismounted spearmen, and a powerful artillery train**. The Swiss,
following Bern and paid by France, marched against him. The campaign tested the **disciplined
tactical body** (the Swiss square) against the era's most sophisticated **combined-arms** force —
and the square won three times.

## The three battles

### Grandson (2 March 1476)

Charles moved out of his fortified camp to intercept the Swiss debouching from a mountain pass along
Lake Neuchâtel. The Swiss **advance guard** (~8,000) emerged onto hilly vineyard ground before the
main army was up. Charles's men-at-arms charged: they "moved right up to the spear points, but…
were unable to penetrate into the massive square from which the long spears were extended against
them" — the seigneur de **Châteauguyon**, forcing his horse into the line, "was cut down." The
attack failed. Then, as a Burgundian rearward movement (to unmask the artillery) was mistaken for
flight, panic spread: "With the cry 'Sauve qui peut,' one unit after another took flight." Delbrück
accepts that **few actually died** ("only 200 Burgundians left on the field") — a general engagement
never developed — but the structural lesson is decisive: Charles's marksmen "could not stand up to
close combat with spearmen," and his army lacked "the firm steadfastness of a tactical body that
protects individuals from becoming infected by panic."

### Murten / Morat (22 June 1476)

The hinge of the campaign. Charles besieged Murten behind a fortified line (the **Grünhag** —
palisade and cannon on the rises); the garrison under **Adrian von Bubenberg** held. The Swiss
(~26,000) waited at Ulmitz for the Zurich contingent. On 22 June, after rain and a Swiss morning
reconnaissance that withdrew, **Charles convinced himself they would not attack** and let his
deployed main body return to camp — exactly when the Swiss advanced out of the forest, "between the
three squares… the knights, no fewer than 1,800, and the marksmen." A first rush was torn by cannon
("a few horsemen… shot in two in the middle"), but the Swiss found dead ground, stormed the Grünhag,
and rolled up the camp; units pinned against the town and lake "were all slaughtered or drowned."
Burgundian dead: ~**6,000–8,000** of ~20,000. Delbrück is scathing about Charles's generalship — he
"remained obstinately convinced that they would not risk an attack… and took no measures."

### Nancy (5 January 1477)

The end. **René of Lorraine** assembled ~20,000 (Lorrainers, Alsatians, Austrians, French, and
Swiss); Charles, after Murten, had at most ~6,000–10,000. Charles drew up his infantry between the
Meurthe and a forest, horse on the flanks, a stream and hedges in front. The allies formed three
masses: the rear guard demonstrated frontally while the **main body and advance guard enveloped both
flanks** through a snowstorm and difficult ground. "As soon as the two units with their greatly
superior numbers had arrived… they had, of course, already won." **Charles the Bold himself was
killed** — his body found days later in the frozen mud. Valois Burgundy ceased to be a great power.

## Why the phalanx beat combined arms

Delbrück's campaign verdict: Charles's army was modern in *composition* but still lacked the
**tactical body**. Marksmen and dismounted spearmen could not survive close combat with the Swiss
square; the heavy horse could not penetrate it; and without "the firm steadfastness of a tactical
body," panic ran through the whole. "As in the Persian wars, the foot troops with close-combat
weapons had been victorious… over the army of knights and marksmen." His epochal claim: **"The
Middle Ages in military history already came to a close on the day of Murten"** — medieval war
overcome "at the highest imaginable degree of [its] perfection."

## Lessons (Principles of War, FM 3-0) — campaign level

- **Objective / Offensive / Mass** — the Swiss repeatedly **massed the square** and took the
  decisive offensive (the assault at Murten, the double envelopment at Nancy), while Charles
  dispersed effort (siege lines, artillery, mixed arms) and never massed a war-winning blow.
- **Maneuver** — Nancy's **double envelopment** (a conscious Cannae) is the campaign's masterstroke.
- **Security & Surprise** — Charles's repeated **security failures** (Grandson's panic, Murten's
  stand-down, Nancy's exposed flanks) handed the Swiss surprise each time.
- **Unity of Command** — the Swiss square cohered; the Burgundian combined-arms army fragmented
  under shock — the absence of the tactical body made manifest.

## Modern Doctrine Parallels

The Burgundian Wars are the classic demonstration that **organisation beats sophistication**: a
force with a cohesive, disciplined core (the square) defeats a more "modern," better-equipped, but
**brittle** combined-arms army. Nancy's double envelopment is a staple Cannae-parallel; Murten is a
study in the catastrophe of a commander who **assumes the enemy will not attack**.

## Historiography

Delbrück leans on the **Milanese ambassador Panigarola** (an eyewitness with Charles) and corrects
the Bernese chronicler **Schilling**, who suppressed the awkward fact that the Murten supreme command
was held by an Austrian knight, **Wilhelm Herter** — "not… a Swiss, but a vassal of the dynasty with
which they had stood in deathly enmity until three years previously." He trims the inflated casualty
and strength figures throughout by [[sachkritik|Sachkritik]].
