# The Rise of the Swiss — Campaign & Operational Hub

> **Actors:** [[swiss-confederation|Swiss Confederation]] · [[valois-burgundian-state|Valois Burgundy]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

The operational arc by which the [[swiss-confederation|Swiss Confederation]]'s pike-and-halberd
phalanx **rebuilt the disciplined infantry tactical body** that had been absent from European
warfare since Rome — drawn from [[delbruck-medieval-warfare-vol-3-1982|Hans Delbrück, *History of
the Art of War*, Vol. III]], Book V. For Delbrück this is the climax of the whole medieval volume:
"A true infantry was not formed again until the period of the Swiss dominance."

## Why the Swiss phalanx worked

- **The formation.** The Swiss fought in the **tightly closed square** — "equally strong on all
  sides," spears protruding "on all sides," which they themselves nicknamed the **hedgehog**
  (*Igel*). Spearmen (armoured) held the exterior ranks; **halberdiers** (unarmoured) waited inside
  to do their work once the square "gained the pressure advantage"; marksmen skirmished alongside
  and withdrew into the square when pressed. They deployed in **three squares in staggered echelon**
  for mutual support — a formation of great flexibility because "the narrowness of its front" let
  even a 10,000-man square turn quickly.
- **It restored the tactical body.** Delbrück's core verdict: "The Swiss infantry formed tactical
  bodies, while the knights, marksmen, and spearmen of the Middle Ages did not have them." Knights
  could not penetrate the closed square ("they were unable to penetrate into the massive square from
  which the long spears were extended"), and a horseman who cannot move "derives no further
  advantage from his horse."
- **Discipline from constitution, not drill.** The Swiss "did not" practise common drill; cohesion
  came from the **free-peasant constitution** — the canton's civil authority *was* its military
  authority. "The linking with the civil authorities gave the Swiss general levies the basis of
  military obedience," where "in the knightly armies the habit of obedience was still very weak."
  A man who fled "could be struck down on the spot by his flanking comrade."
- **Numbers and no quarter.** Delbrück stresses (against the legend of plucky underdogs) that "from
  Morgarten to Nancy the armies of the Confederation were always considerably larger than those of
  their enemies, at times as high as double," and that the Swiss custom of **granting no mercy**
  amplified the panic that broke their enemies.

Delbrück draws the explicit through-line to antiquity: "With the battles of Laupen and Sempach,
Grandson, Murten, and Nancy we once again have foot troops comparable to the phalanx and the
legions" — and compares the Swiss commanders' defensive-into-offensive method to **Miltiades at
Marathon**.

**Laupen (1339)** — the first open-field win: Bern hired the forest cantons (the "first forerunner"
of the Swiss mercenary *Reisläufer* system) and, under **Rudolf von Erlach** (granted extraordinary
disciplinary power by oath), held the Bramberg heights, let the forest-canton square draw off the
knights, then "turned about, moved against the knights, and fell on them from the rear."

## The verdict: the Middle Ages end at Murten

Delbrück fixes the hinge precisely: "The Middle Ages in military history already came to a close on
the day of Murten, where… medieval methods of war were theoretically overcome — not by chance, not
in a moment of weakness… but… at the highest imaginable degree of their perfection." Knighthood, he
insists, was overthrown "by foot soldiers with cold steel," **not** by firearms — "These foot
soldiers have become infantry… Then knighthood would also necessarily become cavalry," the threshold
of the early-modern [[military-revolution]].
