# Battle of Morgarten (1315) — Tactical Analysis

> **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/rise-of-the-swiss/rise-of-the-swiss|Rise of the Swiss]] · **Narrative:** [[events/battle-of-morgarten-1315|Battle of Morgarten]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college-level analysis of the founding Swiss victory, drawn from
[[delbruck-medieval-warfare-vol-3-1982|Hans Delbrück, *History of the Art of War*, Vol. III]], Book
V. Delbrück's reading is sharply revisionist: Morgarten was **not** a spontaneous peasant uprising
but a **deliberately planned ambush** requiring "a well-functioning observation and communication
service" — generalship, not "popular instinct."

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**Habsburg (Leopold I).** Duke Leopold moved to crush the rebellious forest canton of **Schwyz**,
which had defied Habsburg overlordship. His objective was punitive: break into the canton, defeat
the peasants, and restore Habsburg authority. He assembled what was, Delbrück notes, "a considerable
force and an unusually large levy against a mere group of peasants."

**Confederates (Schwyz, with Uri and Unterwalden).** Unable to meet a knightly army in the open,
the Swiss objective was to **deny the mountain approaches** and destroy the invading column where
its cavalry advantage was worthless. They had long prepared the ground with the **Letzi** system —
stone walls blocking the valley entrances (the main Letzi ran ~5 km, "12 feet high, with gates… and
three mighty towers").

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground.** The decisive factor. Leopold chose the road along the **east bank of Lake Ägeri**
  through Morgarten — a defile pinched between a steep wooded slope and the lake. Delbrück's
  inference: the Schwyzers **deliberately left this approach unfortified** as a trap — "it is not at
  all impossible that in 1315 the Schwyzers intentionally neglected the Letzi of Schorno and left
  open the access by Ägeri Lake."
- **Intelligence and concealment.** The Swiss main body was hidden on the **Mattligütsch ridge**,
  screened from reconnaissance by a wooded ravine. Delbrück insists this required organised scouting
  and signalling — "There must have been on hand a well-functioning observation and communication
  service" — the opposite of a peasant mob.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | Confederates | Habsburg |
|---|---|---|
| Total | ~3,000–4,000 (locally superior at contact) | ~2,000–3,000 |
| Composition | Foot — halberd and improvised close-combat arms; a crossbow advance guard | Mounted knights + foot, strung out in march column |
| Command | Werner Stauffacher (field direction) | Duke Leopold I |
| Posture | Concealed ambush from high ground; a blocking detachment forward | Road-bound column, unable to deploy |

*Deployment:* a small **advance guard of crossbowmen** blocked the road at the Buchwaldli narrows
to halt and congest the head of the column; the **main body** lay concealed above on the
Mattligütsch ridge, poised to charge downhill into the jammed mass.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **The block and the jam.** When the Austrian foot tried to clear the crossbow advance guard at
   Buchwaldli, "the column behind them congested and jammed on the road and lakeshore." The
   knightly army lost all room to manoeuvre.
2. **The avalanche.** "Suddenly stones and logs rolled down the steep slope under the horsemen, and
   the entire confederated force stormed down from the ridge in powerful, closely formed units."
3. **The slaughter.** "The far superior mass drove into the confused pack of knights and soldiers,
   cutting and thrusting." Pinned against the lake, most knights "were either slaughtered by the
   Swiss or pushed into the lake and drowned."
4. **Collapse.** Habsburg cohesion never formed; Leopold "only barely managed to escape."

**Key mechanisms:** terrain that nullified cavalry; a planned **block-and-jam** to fix the column;
concealment and surprise; and the downhill shock of closely formed foot against a disordered mass.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | Confederates | Habsburg |
|---|---|---|
| Killed | Light | Heavy — cut down or drowned |
| Source | Delbrück (Vitoduran) | Delbrück; chronicle figure of 20,000 dismissed as "meaningless" |

- **Strategic result:** Schwyz's defiance succeeded; the battle became the **founding legend** of
  Swiss arms and the confidence on which the later phalanx victories were built.
- **Consequence:** the first proof that disciplined, well-led foot on chosen ground could annihilate
  a knightly army — the seed of the [[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/rise-of-the-swiss/rise-of-the-swiss|infantry rebirth]].

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Swiss success factors.** (1) **Choosing and preparing the ground** (the Letzi system; the baited
open approach); (2) **intelligence and concealment** (scouts, signals, the hidden main body); (3)
the **block-and-jam** that fixed the column; (4) **local numerical superiority** at the point of
contact; (5) downhill shock with closely formed units.

**Habsburg failures.** (1) Entering a **defile** where cavalry was useless; (2) no security against
ambush; (3) a strung-out **march column** that could not deploy; (4) underestimating the "mere
peasants" as incapable of an organised operation.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — Swiss object (destroy the column in the defile) was exact; Leopold's (punish
  Schwyz) ignored how to get there safely.
- **Offensive** — The Swiss seized a decisive local offensive from a prepared ambush; the nominal
  attacker (Leopold) never got to fight on his terms.
- **Mass** — The Swiss **massed** their whole force at the jammed point; the Habsburg mass was
  strung out and unusable.
- **Economy of Force** — A small crossbow advance guard economically created the decisive jam.
- **Maneuver** — Terrain *was* the manoeuvre; the downhill charge into a fixed enemy.
- **Unity of Command** — Swiss field direction was unified and obeyed; the Habsburg column had no
  means to coordinate once jammed.
- **Security** — Habsburg security **failed utterly** (no flank/route security in a defile); Swiss
  security (concealment, scouting) was the battle's foundation.
- **Surprise** — Total Swiss surprise — the hidden main body and the avalanche.
- **Simplicity** — A simple, rehearsed plan: block, jam, charge downhill.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Morgarten is the textbook **defile ambush**: fix the head of a road-bound column, deny it room to
deploy, and destroy it from dominating ground — the same logic as a modern linear ambush with a
blocking element and an assault from the high side. Delbrück's insistence that it required
organised reconnaissance and signals anticipates the modern truth that a successful ambush is a
**command-and-intelligence** feat, not luck.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

The main source is the monk **John of Winterthur (Vitoduran)**, writing 25–30 years later, whose
father served on the Austrian side. Delbrück uses Sachkritik to strip away the romantic accretions
(the warning knight von Hünenberg; the adviser Itel Reding) and the absurd 20,000 figure, and —
against the populist reading of Bürkli — insists the victory was an **organised military operation**:
"the community of many thousand souls can, in the final analysis, still not do what was done here"
without "a well-functioning observation and communication service." The remaining uncertainty is the
exact strengths, which the thin sources do not fix.
