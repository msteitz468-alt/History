A staff-college-level analysis of objectives, terrain, order of battle, the phased conduct of the engagement, the critical decisions, and the lessons — grounded in DeVries's reconstruction of Scottish infantry discipline, schiltrons, terrain engineering, and the English failure, with explicit ties to precursor Loudon Hill and comparanda at Courtrai.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**Scotland (Robert the Bruce):** Relieve the siege of Stirling Castle and prevent Edward II from re-establishing control. Bruce had consolidated power after Loudon Hill (1307) and Edward I's death. By late 1309 two-thirds of Scotland was under his control. Edward Bruce's siege of Stirling (May 1313) produced a truce: if no English relief force arrived by Midsummer's Day 1314, the castle would surrender. Bruce prepared a defensive infantry stand on chosen ground to meet the inevitable English response.

**England (Edward II):** Relieve Stirling Castle (a symbolic English foothold) and crush Bruce's rebellion with overwhelming force. Edward used the crisis politically against baronial opposition. The army assembled at Berwick and marched north — one of the largest hosts for Scotland. Overconfidence was high: "There were so many knights, that they believed that if they were to do battle, there would be no strength which could withstand their might." (Barbour via DeVries range 04).

The battle occurred because the English force reached within three miles of Stirling on 23 June 1314, one day before the truce expired, with Bruce's prepared army blocking the way.

**Precursor at Loudon Hill (10 May 1307, from DeVries range 03):** Bruce (then in rebellion after Methven/Dalry reverses) used terrain engineering (three large deep trenches across a flat dry plain bordered by "deep and wide" morass) + defensive infantry line of ~600 to defeat Aymer of Valence's ~3,000 (mostly cavalry). English charge deteriorated in the obstacles; Scots speared men and horses, advanced, routed the rearguard. Bruce personally fought with an axe. "Loudon Hill shows Bruce's successful use of infantry against cavalry, a feat which he would duplicate later at the battle of Bannockburn." Tactics (reconnoitre, prepare obstacles, hold defensive formation, target horses, personal leadership) were the model for 1314.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground (Bannockburn):** New Park (wooded rise chosen by Bruce); the carse (flat, marshy/boggy ground with the Bannock Burn). Scots dug extended ditches/pits "three feet deep and three feet wide... filling them with a brittle plait of twigs... and covering them with grass and weeds." "A contrivance full of evils is fashioned for the feet of horses." Infantry could pass knowingly; heavy cavalry could not. Later retreat into a large "foveam" (Bannockburn) trapped many. (DeVries ranges 03/04)
- **Weather/season:** June; ground conditions (wet marsh noted for English camp) favoured the prepared defenders. English horses and men "worn out with toil and hunger."
- **Intelligence:** Bruce knew the English approach and chose/prepared ground meticulously. Scots rested; English fatigued from march. Bruce received last-minute intelligence via defector Alexander Seton that the English were discouraged and planned a sudden attack. English underestimated the prepared obstacles and cohesion of the schiltrons.

Loudon Hill precedent: Bruce arrived first, reconnoitred, cut trenches leaving narrow gaps to limit cavalry, camped beyond, held line despite English display.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | Scotland (Bruce) | England (Edward II) |
|---|---|---|
| Total | ~5,000–8,000+ (contemporary claims ~30,000 exaggerated) | ~15,000–20,000+ (contemporary 100k+ with 40k cavalry; modern lower with heavy cavalry emphasis) |
| Infantry | Dense schiltrons (circular or packed spear formations; "solid formation"; "thick-set hedge"; front ranks kneeling with pikes planted, rear ranks holding weapons above); "back to back with their spear points outward" in some stands. Bruce and leaders dismounted to fight with the foot. | Archers (present but not yet the dominant massed longbow system of Crécy); supporting infantry |
| Cavalry | Minimal/none in main line (Bruce ordered infantry-only to avoid Falkirk-style defeat; some accounts note limited Scottish horse) | Massed knights and men-at-arms in divisions/"batailles"; expected to deliver decisive shock |
| Command | Bruce overall; divisions under Randolph (Moray), Edward Bruce, Walter Stewart + James Douglas, Bruce's own. | Edward II present/overall; vanguard Gloucester + Hereford; Clifford/Beaumont relief force (~800) |
| Notes | Four divisions (or three lines + rearguard per sources); "lances in their hands," shields thick among them; axes. High morale, prayer before action. | Classic cavalry-led host; "pride is remarked on by all sources." Infantry and archers secondary in plan. |

**Deployment (Bannockburn):** Scots anchored on New Park rise with obstacles forward and natural barriers (trees, marshes, burn) protecting flanks/rear. "back to the river" analogy in some descriptions. English deployed for assault, committing cavalry into/ across the difficult carse against the waiting schiltrons.

(Loudon deployment: Scots behind trenches on chosen flat field with morass flank; English two lines of cavalry.)

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

**Day 1 (23 June) — precursor actions (from DeVries range 03/04):**
- English advance and delay. Edward II sent Clifford/Beaumont's ~800 to relieve Stirling. Randolph's ~500 intercepted, formed defensive schiltron. English surrounded and attacked with spears, darts, maces, swords. Stalemate in heat/dust. "On both sides they became so fatigued / from the fighting and the sun's heat."
- Douglas requested/received permission to aid (initial Bruce refusal to risk main force). Bruce himself led divisions with axe. Bohun (English) charged Bruce personally; Bruce "buried his axe in Bohun's helmet, killing his foe with a blow so powerful that it broke the handle of the axe." Boosted Scots. English relief force broke; rearguard elements turned. Scots victorious in skirmishes.

**Night:** Scots slept armed and in order. Bruce speech: right on their side; gain from English riches; fighting for wives/children/freedom; hold spears vs. expected cavalry charges; no looting until battle over. Promised heirs property if killed. English miserable in "evil, deep wet marsh"; morale crushed after vanguard defeats.

**Day 2 (24 June) — main battle:**
- Scots gained intelligence (Seton): English discouraged, planning sudden attack. Bruce confirmed plan.
- English council: Edward surprised Scots were all infantry (expected cavalry array). Umphraville suggested feigned retreat to lure then counter; rejected. Edward saw Scots kneeling (prayer, mistaken for submission), ordered immediate charge despite objections (Gloucester disagreement noted in Vita). Edward joined line.
- Approach: trumpets, standards. Possible brief English archery. English cavalry charged (most sources) or Scots advanced like "thick-set hedge" (Vita variant). "The crash of the lances, the ringing of the swords... the groan of the dying... For a long time it was fought at the front of the lines." (Trokelowe)
- English attacks: first struck with great impact but "no penetration." Long melee. English "crowded together" and could not operate. Scots "attacked like madmen." Multiple charges failed against the solid line.
- Turning point: Gloucester (leading) fought fiercely ("as a bellicose boar"); "the points of the lances... stabbing several places, he was knocked to the ground, and his head was struck on all sides by the clubs of the enemy, until he breathed out his soul under the horse's feet." (Trokelowe/Vita). Seeing their lord slaughtered, his troops fled in panic; the rout spread. "Soon all of the English troops were fleeing... even the king, Edward II, fled."
- Pits/ditch role in collapse: English in retreat fell into the large foveam/Bannockburn; "multi nobiles et alii prae pressura cum equis in illam ceciderunt... multi nunquam se explicare de fovea potuerunt." (Lanercost). Many nobles and horses trapped.

**Key tactical mechanisms (DeVries):** Terrain + deliberate pits/ditches to canalise and disorder cavalry; dense, disciplined schiltrons (front kneeling planted pikes, rear layers) able to hold or press when not broken; Bruce's insistence on infantry commitment and discipline (no early looting); failure of English to clear obstacles, use archery effectively first, or maintain cohesion. Explicit parallel: "It is no wonder that both Thomas Gray and the Vita Edwardi II can see the comparison between this battle and that at Courtrai... 'Indeed I think it is unheard of in our time for such an army to be scattered so suddenly by infantry, unless when the flower of France fell before the Flemings at Courtrai.'"

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

Decisive Scottish victory. English host shattered. Edward II barely escaped capture. Scottish independence effectively secured in the south.

Casualties (exaggerated in sources; per DeVries analysis): Barbour claims 30,000 English killed. Nobles: 154 (Walsingham) to 700 (Barbour); focus on loss of chivalry. Many prisoners taken for ransom. Scottish losses light. English "rushing back to their own borders."

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Scotland success factors:**
- Terrain selection and engineering (pits, ditches, natural barriers) by Bruce.
- Discipline and cohesion of schiltrons (held line; "solid infantry formation... unable to be penetrated").
- Bruce's leadership: personal example (Loudon axe; Bannockburn axe incident and speech), enforced infantry discipline, exploited English disorder.
- Morale and cause (freedom, families, God).

**England shortcomings:**
- Overconfidence in cavalry numbers and "might"; ignored preparations and counsel.
- Fatigue from march into poor camp.
- Premature charge ordered by Edward into unsuitable ground without clearing obstacles or suppressing defenders.
- Crowding and loss of cohesion; critical leader loss (Gloucester) triggered panic.
- "they were not accustomed to fight on foot." (Gray)

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective:** Scots achieved (relieve Stirling, break English host). English failed their relief/punitive objective.
- **Offensive:** English pressed but violated by charging disordered into prepared position; Scots used defensive stand then exploitation.
- **Mass:** Scots concentrated in solid formations; English dispersed effort and lost mass in obstacles.
- **Economy of Force:** Bruce used limited numbers effectively on restricted/ prepared ground; English wasted superior numbers.
- **Maneuver:** Terrain nullified English cavalry maneuver; Scots used position + obstacles.
- **Unity of Command:** English undermined by overruled advice and Edward's direct order; Scots maintained cohesion under Bruce.
- **Security:** Scots secured position and flanks; English blind to hidden ditches despite map.
- **Surprise:** Scots achieved via prepared field and disciplined stand; English expected traditional fight.
- **Simplicity:** Scots "win-or-die" defensive line straightforward; English complicated by chivalric priorities and poor adaptation.

(Concrete examples drawn from DeVries verbatim reconstructions in ranges 03/04.)

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Classic example of prepared defensive infantry + terrain/obstacles defeating mounted shock (cf. Courtrai). English learning curve: adoption of dismounted tactics. Dupplin Moor (1332; DeVries range 06) provided the direct tactical model — small Disinherited force (~1,500, mostly dismounted + flanking archers in defensive posture) defeated much larger Scottish host; Scots charged, archers "blinded and wounded the faces" causing massing/disorder, English held and advanced uniformly once leaders fled. Halidon Hill (1333) explicitly modeled Edward III's formation on Dupplin (three infantry lines in arc at base of hill, archer wings; strictly defensive); Edward III cited Dupplin's success ("an even more disparate number of Scottish soldiers had been defeated quite handily") to his troops. These validated the combined dismounted line + flanking archery that matured in the HYW. Lessons in economy of force on restricted terrain, unit cohesion under pressure, leader personal example, and the cost of underestimating prepared foot. "the thick lines of Scottish troops were perhaps the greatest factor in their victory... infantry lines standing in defensive formation."

Crécy application (DeVries range 09): "established formation used by Edward III" (dismounted men-at-arms + archers on flanks) applied on ridge; English "stood solidly in their formation" from dawn to vespers; provoked uphill charges through archery + ditches "into his solid infantry formation." "As at Courtrai, the infantry soldiers stood in unity, without giving way." Baker confirms flanks ("a lateribus ... quasi ale astarent"); ditches 1 ft deep/wide. (DeVries range 09)

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primaries (DeVries analysis):** John Barbour (The Bruce, c.1375 — detailed, preferred for chronology on day 1; Scottish perspective); Thomas Gray (Scalachronica, c.1355 — unique eyewitness father captured at the battle; uses "schiltrom"); Vita Edwardi II (English, notes Gloucester disagreement and Scottish advance); Chronicon de Lanercost (ditch details); Geoffrey le Baker, John of Trokelowe, Walter Bower, Thomas of Burton, Walsingham, Adam Murimuth, Henry Knighton, Anonimalle Chronicle; poems (Robert Baston, Laurence Minot). No single contemporary Scottish account besides later Barbour.

**Source criticism (DeVries):** Contemporary numbers gross exaggerations; modern reductions accepted. Chronology disputes (Barbour vs. Gray on vanguard movement; most sources vs. Vita on who initiated main charge). Formation details vary (3 lines vs. 2+rearguard). Presence of Scottish cavalry (Barbour notes some; most sources claim all dismounted). Author relies heavily on Barbour for sequence but cross-checks.

**Modern:** DeVries 1996 provides the detailed tactical reconstruction used here. NCMH VI notes it "with Courtrai (1302) and Morgarten (1315) in military history." Explicit Courtrai parallel in English sources themselves.
