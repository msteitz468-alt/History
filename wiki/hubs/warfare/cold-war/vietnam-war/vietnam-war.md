# Vietnam War — Strategic / Campaign Analysis (Warfare Hub)

**Navigation**: Summary narrative and full historiography in the main [[events/vietnam-war]] page. This hub page owns the high-resolution strategic and operational analysis using West Point / staff-college standards, with particular emphasis on the application (and misapplication) of strategy, the principles of war, and the nature-of-war diagnosis.

Reciprocal link: [[events/vietnam-war]] (see "Related" or "Strategic Analysis" section there).

## Strategic Context & Objectives (Summers framework)

The decisive question, per Clausewitz and Summers, is the first and most comprehensive: "the kind of war on which they are embarking; neither mistaking it for, nor trying to turn it into, something that is alien to its nature."

U.S. (and South Vietnamese) objectives were never stated with the clarity required for military planning:
- Publicly framed as containing communism / preventing the fall of South Vietnam (domino theory).
- Militarily executed primarily as attrition of "VC" forces inside South Vietnam through search-and-destroy.
- Never clearly defined what "victory" or even "success" would look like in concrete, attainable terms (restoration of a stable non-communist South? destruction of the VC infrastructure? defeat of the NVA?).

North Vietnamese objective: reunification under communist control by whatever means and duration necessary (Giap: fight 10, 25, 50 years).

**Range 02 early engagement (Ch. 8 Ia Drang, Nov 1965):** 32nd, 33rd and 66th NVA regiments clashed with US 1st Cavalry Division. "On a strategic scale, a brilliant spoiling attack had completely derailed Hanoi’s hopes of earning a decisive victory before full American might could be deployed to South Vietnam... the NVA unit had been sent reeling in retreat. For the moment, at least, an adverse tide had been reversed." "Now was the time for the United States to take the offensive." Instead the response turned inward to the guerrilla symptom.

Summers' core diagnosis: the United States treated the war as a revolutionary insurgency (people's war) requiring counterinsurgency when it was in its decisive phase an interstate war waged by the regular forces of a sovereign enemy state (the DRV). The final 1975 conquest of Saigon was executed by four North Vietnamese Army corps, not by guerrillas.

## National Will: The People and the Congress (Part I, Ch. 1–2; Range 01)

Per Summers (grounded exclusively in front matter + Introduction + early Part I): "Vietnam was a reaffirmation of the peculiar relationship between the American Army and the American people. The American Army really is a people’s Army in the sense that it belongs to the American people who take a jealous and proprietary interest in its involvement. When the Army is committed the American people are committed, when the American people lose their commitment it is futile to try to keep the Army committed. In the final analysis, the American Army is not so much an arm of the Executive Branch as it is an arm of the American people. The Army, therefore, cannot be committed lightly." (Gen. Fred C. Weyand, lines ~710-720)

President Johnson made a conscious decision not to mobilize the national will: "According to his biographer, President Johnson’s decision not to mobilize the American people was based on his fears that it would jeopardize his 'Great Society' programs. As he himself said: '... History provided too many cases where the sound of the bugle put an immediate end to the hopes and dreams of the best reformers... And the generals. Oh, they’d love the war, too... That’s why I am suspicious of the military.'" (lines ~760-780)

This produced the strategic vulnerability the enemy exploited. "The failure to invoke the national will was one of the major strategic failures of the Vietnam war. It produced a strategic vulnerability that our enemy was able to exploit." (lines ~1060)

Instead of declaration of war, the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution: "President Johnson asked Congress for a resolution empowering him to 'take all necessary measures to repel an armed attack...' This Southeast Asia Resolution... passed the Senate by a vote of 88-2, and the House by a unanimous voice vote of 416-0." Sen. Javits: "The power of decision... had not been stolen. It had been surrendered." (lines ~1235-1280)

This recreated an 18th-century dynamic. Clausewitz: "In the eighteenth century... war was still an affair for governments alone, and the people’s role was simply that of an instrument... War thus became solely the concern of the government to the extent that governments parted company with their peoples..." (lines ~530-555)

**Consequence:** "It also explains why to many Americans the Vietnam war was 'Johnson’s war' or 'Nixon’s war' or 'the Army’s war' rather than the American people’s war." (lines ~555-560) The trinity (people/government/Army) was unbalanced; "A theory that ignores any one of them... would conflict with reality to such an extent that for this reason alone it would be totally useless." (lines ~570-580)

## Friction: The People and the Bureaucracy (Part I, Ch. 3–4; Range 01)

Clausewitz on friction: "Everything in war is very simple, but the simplest thing is difficult... Friction is the only concept that more or less corresponds to the factors that distinguish real war from war on paper." (lines ~1660-1670; extended to national environment)

**Friction: The People.** "Antimilitarism then was not something unique to Vietnam. It is part of what makes Americans what they are." "If we cannot be loved, we can be trusted and respected." (Weyand, lines ~1690-1700) "The student draft deferments... were part of a deliberate Presidential policy not to arouse the passions of the American people. The effect of this was that we fought the Vietnam war in cold blood." (lines ~1740-1750) Euphemisms concealed reality: "we did not kill the enemy, we 'inflicted casualties'; we did not destroy things, we 'neutralized targets.'" (lines ~1775) This made the war "intolerable to the American people" because "Vietnam was fought in cold blood" unlike prior wars "fought in the heat of passion." (lines ~1870-1880)

**Friction: The Bureaucracy.** Civilian systems analysts and PPBS dominated: "By default the military had allowed strategy to be dominated by civilian analysts... Instead of being experts in the application of military force to achieve the political ends of the United States, we became neophyte political scientists and systems analysts and were outclassed by the civilian professionals who dominated national security policy under Secretary of Defense Robert S. McNamara after 1961." (lines ~2080-2130) "It is especially ironic that this so-called new approach to strategy was actually two centuries old." (lines ~2140)

PPBS "was only half of the equation" — preparation for war, not war proper. "People who only ask how much is enough... tend to develop an instinct for the capillaries. That is not the instinct it is wisest to cultivate if you want to win real—not bureaucratic—battles." (Woolsey, lines ~2190) Clausewitz: "An irreconcilable conflict exists between this type of theory and actual practice." "They aim at fixed values; but in war everything is uncertain... They consider only the unilateral action, whereas war consists of a continuous interaction of opposites." (lines ~2210-2450)

Presidential access limited: "The Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff was the only member of the Joint Chiefs who saw the President with any regularity in the crucial years 1965-1968 [but even he] rarely saw the President alone... The individual members of the Joint Chiefs... seldom saw the President privately." (Baldwin via lines ~2320-2340) Military witnesses in Korea had not insisted on the constitutional point; same pattern repeated.

**Trinity and moral factors:** The enemy targeted the "moral element" (Clausewitz: "When we speak of destroying the enemy’s forces... nothing obliges us to limit this idea to physical forces: The moral element must also be considered." lines ~1055). "Instead of the passions of the American people strengthening and supporting us, the more vocal and passionate voices were too often raised in support of our enemies." (lines ~1450-1460)

## Nature of the War (critical misidentification)

- First Indochina War (1946–54): revolutionary war between France and Viet Minh.
- Second Indochina War (post-1954/1960): primarily conventional aggression by the North Vietnamese state using its army as the main force, with the southern insurgency (NLF/VC) as a supporting, economy-of-force effort and political front.
- North Vietnamese deliberately began with guerrilla action in part to avoid the overt conventional trigger that had brought massive U.S. response in Korea.
- Ho Chi Minh captured the nationalist mantle; Diem and successors carried the taint of French colonial inheritance.

**Range 02 grounding (Ch. 8, verbatim):** "it is essential to distinguish the First Indo-China war between France and the Viet Minh from the Second Indo-China war between North Vietnam and South Vietnam. The first was a revolutionary war. The second was not. The forces that besieged Dien Bien Phu grew out of the guerrilla movement, the forces that captured Saigon did not grow out of the Viet Cong but were the regular armed forces of North Vietnam. This critical difference validates the official U.S. government position that the Vietnam war was caused by aggression from the North."

**Misidentification of guerrilla role (Ch. 8):** "we saw their guerrilla operations as a strategy in itself... We attempted to counter it by using such models as the British model in Malaysia." Reality: "the guerrilla war was a kind of economy of force operation on the part of North Vietnam to buy time and to wear down superior U.S. military forces... 'In South Vietnam we responded mainly to Hanoi’s simulated insurgency rather than to its real but controlled aggression, as a bull charges the toreador’s cape, not the toreador.'"

**Counterinsurgency dogma error (Ch. 7, Range 02):** Westmoreland: "The Communists in Vietnam waged a classic revolutionary war." But Thompson defined revolutionary war as designed "to reach a decisive result on its own." "with hindsight it is clear that by Sir Robert Thompson’s own definition, he was exactly wrong... Even at the very end there was no popular mass uprising... Their actions fit precisely Sir Robert Thompson’s definition of partisan warfare—they harassed and distracted both the United States and South Vietnam so that North Vietnamese regular forces could reach a decision in conventional battles."

**Empirical shift (Ch. 7/8):** "by 1970 at least 80 percent of the day-to-day combat in South Vietnam was being carried on by regular People’s Army of Vietnam (PAVN) troops... Genuine black-pyjama southern guerrillas... had been decimated and amounted to no more than 20 percent of the communist fighting force" (Chalmers Johnson).

This misdiagnosis meant U.S. forces were optimized and employed against the wrong center of gravity.

## Application of the Principles of War (Part II analysis)

Summers systematically measures U.S. performance against the classic principles:

- **The Objective** (Range 02 / Ch. 9 grounding): Failed at the strategic level. Political objectives were never translated into clear, attainable military objectives that could produce a decision. "We succeeded in everything we set out to do" in logistics and tactics because the objectives set for the military instrument were themselves misdirected or impossible.

  **FM 100-5 (19 Feb 1962) definition:** "Every military operation must be directed toward a clearly defined, decisive and attainable objective. The ultimate military objective of war is the destruction of the enemy’s armed forces and his will to fight."

  **Evolution and loss (Ch. 9):** 1921 codification explicitly separated strategic ("the objective assigned military forces must be in consonance with the national objective") and tactical. Post-WWII the strategic dimension was dropped then partially restored (1954). During Vietnam changes obscured the link: "What had been a clear relationship between military strategy and political objectives was lost in an abstruse discussion of national objectives, rejection of aggression, deterrence..." The 1968 edition reduced the ultimate objective to "'defeat of the enemy’s armed forces'" (eliminating "and his will to fight").

  **Clausewitz core (Ch. 9):** "Our loss of focus on The Objective was particularly damaging, since this is the driving principle of war... 'the political object is a goal, war is the means of reaching it, and means can never be considered in isolation from their purpose.'"

  **MACV mission divergence (Westmoreland quote):** "To assist the Government of Vietnam and its armed forces to defeat externally directed and supported communist subversion and aggression and attain an independent South Vietnam functioning in a secure environment." "Even a cursory mission analysis reveals two divergent specified tasks" — the second political/nation-building rather than military.

  **US diffusion vs. enemy clarity:** Arnold: ~22 US rationales shifting 1949–68 (resist aggression → counterinsurgency → preserve commitments). NVA objective consistent: install communist regimes throughout Indochina (1952 Lao Dong document; Collins 1973: "simple, concise, and consistent... Controlling and communizing all of Indochina have always been the foe’s overriding objectives").

  **Ridgway 1954 analysis (intervention cost):** Professional military must not compromise judgment; cost of intervening at Dien Bien Phu would be "as great as, or greater than, that we paid in Korea."

  **Sanctuaries/self-inflicted:** Failure to focus on external source (North Vietnam); "limited our attacks on the North to air and sea actions only." Laos/Cambodia: "a self-inflicted wound. The myth of their neutrality gave North Vietnam an immense tactical and strategic advantage..."

  **Kinnard survey (1974):** "almost 70 percent of the Army generals who managed the war were uncertain of its objectives." "mirrors a deep-seated strategic failure: the inability of policy-makers to frame tangible, obtainable goals."

  **Post-1969 shift (Kissinger):** New mission "focused on providing 'maximum assistance' to the South Vietnamese to strengthen their forces, supporting pacification efforts, and reducing the flow of supplies to the enemy" — formal move to "preserving the integrity of American commitments" and withdrawal.
- **The Offensive**: Search-and-destroy was tactically offensive but strategically reactive and attritional inside South Vietnam. The strategic offensive against the true source of enemy power (North Vietnam's will and regular forces) was largely avoided for fear of Chinese intervention and nuclear escalation. (See detailed Range 03 expansion below with verbatim/OOB.)
- **Mass, Economy of Force, Maneuver**: Enormous mass applied to the wrong problem (body count in the South). Economy of force violated by the requirement to protect dispersed bases and by failure to interdict the Ho Chi Minh Trail effectively at source. Maneuver was tactical (airmobile) but rarely translated into strategic effect against the enemy's decision-making apparatus. (Detailed below.)
- **Unity of Command**: Fragmented. MACV vs. pacification/CORDS; military vs. political chains; U.S. vs. GVN; air vs. ground; multiple nations with different rules. No single commander with authority over all instruments. (Detailed below.)
- **Security and Surprise**: Tactical security often good; strategic surprise repeatedly suffered (Tet 1968 the most famous). North Vietnam repeatedly achieved operational surprise. (Detailed below.)
- **Simplicity**: Violated by complex theories of limited war, signaling, graduated response, and systems analysis that obscured rather than clarified. (Detailed below.)
- **Coalition Warfare**: Chronic problems with ARVN motivation, corruption, and dependence; allied contributions (ROK especially) tactically effective but politically and strategically marginal to the main effort.

## Detailed Principles Applications and Lessons (Summers Range 03 exclusive)

### The Offensive (strategic)
U.S. entered "on the strategic defensive" (containment; Korea status quo precedent). "our failure to appreciate what this strategic posture entailed contributed to our ultimate failure." NVA strategic offensive (conquest SV): 1964 tactical off (3 NVA divs Cambodia bases slice Pleiku-AnKhe-QuiNhon); post-IaDrang tac def (VC economy screen + select regulars preserve main force sanctuary); Tet68/Eastertide72 tac off; 1975 17-div conventional total victory. 1975 Dung: "strategic route ... more than 20,000 kms" + 5k km pipeline + >10k vehicles; "Army corps were gradually formed"; Politburo Oct74 assessed "United States could hardly jump back in"; Central Highlands main; Ban Me Thuot "nearly 3 divisions against ... one regular regiment ... 23rd division" +3 CG; ratios "5.5 ... infantry ... 1.2 to 1 ... tanks ... 2.1 to 1 ... heavy artillery." Four corps close: IV east (Xuan Loc), II SE, 5/8 SW, I/III NW/N; Saigon 30 Apr 1130 surrender.

U.S. "confused that posture with the strategic offensive." "The Americans had designed a purely defensive strategy for Vietnam. It was a strategy that was based on the attrition of the enemy through a prolonged defense and made no allowance for decisive offensive action." (Lung). Westmoreland phases: Phase II "reinstitute pacification" error (ARVN task). Graduated "slow squeeze" (McNaughton) over JCS "hit hard and fast." Alternatives (Vien isolation fortify 17th par + amphib; JCS Lao denial; Westmoreland Laos/Cambodia/amphib hook; Palmer 5-div DMZ-to-Thai + reserve "four fewer divisions ... wither on the vine") rejected (reserve mobilization). Clausewitz: "Defense without an active purpose is self-contradictory"; major victory "by positive measures ... never by simply waiting."

### Mass, Economy of Force, and Maneuver
FM defs (mass concentrate superior at critical; EoF measured alloc; maneuver relative disadvantage + flexibility). COG Clausewitz "hub ... all energies should be directed" (army/capital/protector army/community/leaders opinion).

U.S. wrong COG (VC separate not NVA instrument); Tet-68 "virtually destroyed" VC yet "war continued unabated." Global dilemma (Wheeler request for worldwide not just VN). "technical ability ... far exceeded" NVA (Ia Drang etc. frustrated 65/68/72) but "strategic failure ... fritter away our advantage in Mass, Economy of Force, and Maneuver in tactical operations."

NVA: 1964-65 "three full North Vietnamese divisions" "superior ... from secure bases in Cambodia"; Ia Drang defeat. 1968 "economy of force units—the Viet Cong guerrillas—to attack the population centers" (regulars Khe Sanh); "by attacking everywhere Giap had superior strength nowhere"; ~100k losses tac fail/strat success. 1972 "12 divisions—a total of about 150,000 men" tanks/arty/AA DMZ; "never been bombed like ... this time"; >100k deaths. 1975 "classic use"; "interior lines"; screen "kept the South Vietnamese Army deployed in a counter-guerrilla mode with little ability to mass"; II Corps "900 kms ... 18 days ... 2,000 vehicles ... six big rivers ... fight the enemy en route"; "could not draw maps quickly enough"; "5 divisions against our 15"; 4 corps closure.

ARVN "like the American militia"; "families with them ... inhibited their ability to maneuver and it proved impossible to mass"; 1972 held (US); 1975 "faulty disposition"; Xuan Loc 18th ARVN "fought heroically and virtually destroyed three North Vietnamese divisions." "flexibility ... most critical." (Clausewitz animate reacts.)

### Unity of Command
FM: "best achieved by vesting a single commander." Clausewitz cabinet or combined. NVA Giap "member of the ruling Politburo, a Deputy Premier ... Minister of Defense and Commander in Chief." 1975 "Political Bureau and the Central Military Party Committee worked together"; Le Duc Tho "would not be allowed to return if he was not successful"; "establish the specific combat commands ... name the field commanders." "seamless web ... highly centralized" (Hoopes).

U.S. Pres nominal; MacArthur 1932 head-of-state. DoD/JCS 1947 interservice success but political linkage fail. NSC "scrapped" (Kennedy); "irregular meetings ... Rusk, McNamara, and Bundy"; "piecemeal ... fragmented NSC" (Hoopes). Komer: "no Vietnam 'high command'"; "everybody and nobody was responsible"; "not a single senior level official ... full-time with Vietnam before 1969"; "business as usual" peacetime procedures. Theater CINCPAC "over 5,000 miles away"; Westmoreland "MACV functioned not directly under the Joint Chiefs ... through CINCPAC"; "not I but [Sharp] was the theater commander"; "responsibilities and prerogatives were basically confined within the borders of South Vietnam"; "three separate or only loosely related struggles." "Without a common goal it is impossible to have ... unity of command." Suggested Philippines external strategic HQ + forward tactical army.

### Security and Surprise
Reciprocal; surprise = EoF/Mass/Maneuver/Unity. "Basically surprise is a tactical device." Rare strat (buildup). VN three will-based: 1965 US (Hanoi "dead wrong"; Johnson statements; Ia Drang "thrown off the track"); Tet68 NVA ("gained complete surprise"; "victims of ... optimism and ... Giap’s ... deception"; "If we’d gotten the whole battle plan, it wouldn’t have been credible"; wedge on US opinion "resolve obviously wavered"); 1972 US ("I have the will in spades"; B-52s "full weight ... vital power centers"; 11-day "decisive factor" Jan73 peace). Paradox US surprise from "lack of strategic security" (public opinion misled NVA).

### Simplicity
"Sum of all the others." NVA simple (conquest objective; Politburo unity). U.S. "conflicting ... resisting aggression and counterinsurgency"; "confused the tactical offensive for the strategic offensive"; "strategic direction was fragmented among Washington, Honolulu and Saigon"; "command structure was convoluted and confused with overlapping authority and responsibility diffused among Military Assistance Command Vietnam, the Army Republic of Vietnam Joint General Staff, United States Army Vietnam and the 'Free World Military Forces'." "inability to explain in clear and understandable language ... undercut American support." "sitting in Saigon was like sitting inside the folded petals of a poisonous flower" (Herr); "base camp mentality"; Heiser standards "usually high" overload; Clausewitz "whole of military activity must ... relate directly or indirectly to [combat]"; soldier end "fight at the right place and the right time."

## Center of Gravity and Decisive Points

Per Summers (Clausewitzian):
- Enemy COG: the North Vietnamese regular armed forces and the political will of the Hanoi leadership (not the southern population or VC infrastructure alone).
- Proposed alternatives discussed in the literature (e.g., Laotian barrier/EL PASO plan associated with Summers' circle): cut the Trail with a substantial force to "wither" the VC on the vine while pressuring the North. Critiqued by others (Krepinevich et al.) on logistical, political, and force-structure grounds.
- U.S. never struck decisively at the true COG; instead fought a war of attrition on the enemy's chosen ground (the South) under rules that preserved the enemy's sanctuary and lines of communication.

## Outcome & Casualties

Tactical/operational U.S. and ARVN forces repeatedly defeated NVA/VC main-force units when they chose to stand and fight (Ia Drang, Tet urban battles, Linebacker air campaigns, etc.). The 1972 Easter Offensive was defeated. Yet the political objective was not secured. The 1973 Paris Accords bought a "decent interval"; the 1975 final offensive succeeded because the South Vietnamese state collapsed politically and militarily once U.S. combat support was withdrawn.

## Critical Strategic Decisions (selected)

- 1965: 44-battalion request and Americanization under attrition logic.
- Refusal to mobilize reserves or seek congressional declaration (neo-18th century cabinet-war mindset).
- Prioritization of "the other war" (pacification) only after Tet, and even then incompletely.
- Vietnamization without addressing underlying GVN legitimacy or NVA sanctuary.
- 1972–73 bombing campaigns (Linebacker) that achieved temporary results but were not sustained politically.

## Coalition Warfare (Ch. 15 — Range 04 exclusive)

U.S. overinvolvement in South Vietnam's internal problems was the decisive coalition failure. "We failed in Vietnam because we attempted to do too much. Instead of concentrating our efforts on repelling external aggression as we had done in Korea we also took upon ourselves the task of nation building. This was an error we never made in Korea." (NVA major in Hanoi, April 1975: "You have done more than enough.") 

In Korea, despite worse initial conditions (government in retreat, economy destroyed), "we did not intervene but left the resolution of their internal problems to the ROK government." In Vietnam, "What the United States could never do was 'solve the internal problems' of South Vietnam. Only the Vietnamese themselves could accomplish that task." South Vietnamese generals (Truong, Don) later confirmed: halting infiltration was "the most critical requirement"; once stopped, "everything else would have been 'easy.'"

**Basic error in 1965 deployment:** Instead of US focusing on external aggression (interdicting infiltration, isolating battlefield) while initially assisting ARVN with COIN, "the U.S. instead committed the United States forces in support of counterinsurgency." Westmoreland positioned arriving divisions to support prior advisory COIN effort; Army scattered for search-and-destroy; Marines pursued CAPs with tactical success but "at a fatal strategic price."

**CORDS (1967+):** Produced genuine pacification progress (Komer: "largely due to the expanded CORDS advisory effort... advising over 900,000 Vietnamese... cumulative impact... incalculable"). But "serious long-term disadvantages": post-1975 ARVN officer: "when American troops came to Vietnam, they try to do everything. And make the Vietnamese lose the initiative... So the Vietnamese don’t rely on themselves. They rely on the Americans."

**Vietnamization (1969+):** "essentially a strategy that would require the Vietnamese to survive with greatly reduced American participation." RVNAF made "no adjustments in doctrine, organization or training." Per Podhoretz: "the model or paradigm of a new strategy of retreat" and "containment through surrogate power" — but surrogate left without promised aid when tested by conventional invasion.

**Clausewitz on coalition limits:** "One country may support another’s cause, but will never take it so seriously as it takes its own. A moderately-sized force will be sent to its help; but if things go wrong the operation is pretty well written off, and one tries to withdraw at the smallest possible cost."

**Ironies:** Nixon Doctrine (1969) mirrored what USSR/PRC had done throughout (nuclear shield + massive materiel, no ground troops). Original rationale ("check Chinese communist expansion") produced the reverse: "China now has a hostile state allied with its most dangerous enemy (the Soviet Union) on its southern border and the independent nation of the Republic of Vietnam... has ceased to exist." "In war the result is never final."

## Lessons (Principles of War as Interrogatories — Epilogue & Appendix, Range 04 priority)

Summers treats the principles not as checklists or immutable rules but as "military planning interrogatories" — "a series of questions that provide a framework for analysis" (citing Paret/Hines). Their purpose: "not of telling [us] how to act but of developing [our] judgment."

1. **What are we trying to accomplish? (The Objective)** — "the first principle because all else flows from it. It is the strategic equivalent of the mission statement in tactics." Insist on "tangible, obtainable political goals" stated in "concrete terms," not platitudes. "we... need to have a definition of 'victory.' This victory need not be a total destruction of the enemy... It need only be the attainment of a political goal that prompted our involvement, such as the restoration of the status quo in the Korean war." (Hackett oil-protection example illustrates complexity: specify area degrees + 5 success requirements including seize/secure/restore/operate/guarantee passage.)

2. **How are we going to do it? (The Offensive)** — Maintain "initiative and freedom of action." Can be achieved on strategic defensive (Waterloo example). Requires delegation and mutual trust (Auftragstaktik); suffered after Truman-MacArthur.

3. **With what means, applied how? (Mass, Economy of Force, Maneuver)** — "relational action against the enemy’s center of gravity" ("the hub of all power and movement, on which everything depends"). US faces "bipolar confrontation with the Soviet Union" + "threats... from heavily armed Soviet surrogates." Geography: US insular power requires "strategic sealift and airlift" + base rights; POMCUS improves Europe readiness at cost to worldwide flexibility and home training.

4. **Who is going to control it? (Unity of Command)** — "For every objective, there should be unity of effort under one responsible commander." In limited war the peacetime preparation task and war-conduct task "work against each other." Recommendation: designate separate elements in contingency ops.

5. **How do we protect the force and achieve surprise? (Security & Surprise)** — Inherent conflict with "free and democratic American society." Media cannot preserve strategic security/surprise (NVA monitored US press closely); yet "the free and open American media acted as a kind of deception device" enabling initial intervention and Christmas bombing surprises. Presidential decision volatility (Truman on Korea: plans showed no interest; "the one place he couldn’t break into was the mind of President Truman") promotes both surprise and security. "Secrecy... cannot be maintained by hiding one’s intentions from subordinates... Such secrecy is not desirable, because any operation must be thoroughly trained and rehearsed if it is to be successful." (Krauss)

6. **Is our concept clear and executable? (Simplicity)** — "The American people must understand what we are about and why their sacrifices are necessary." "Overly complex and convoluted plans and operations should in themselves be a danger signal." "simple and direct plans and orders cannot compensate for ambiguous and cloudy objectives." (Sassoon "The General" poem; MacArthur 1935 on fundamental principles from history.)

**Epilogue on military nature and public support honesty:** "In the future we must take care to avoid jeopardizing American public support for their military with misstatements... of what we are about." "people will probably respond to arguments made on the basis of enlightened self-interest... The apocalyptic language of the past has tended to deceive those who used it as well as those who got the message." (Javits) "We cannot know the future"; avoid fixation on scenarios that lose flexibility.

**Quintessential lesson:** "The quintessential 'strategic lesson learned' from the Vietnam war is that we must once again become masters of the profession of arms. The American people deserve, demand and expect nothing less of their Army."

## Modern Doctrine Parallels

Principles as interrogatories remain core to staff-college education for diagnosing war type and linking ends/means before commitment.
