# Battle of Bunker Hill — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[battle-of-bunker-hill-1775|Battle of Bunker Hill (event page)]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/american-revolutionary-war|American Revolutionary War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college analysis of the first major battle of the [[american-revolution|Revolution]] — the
classic case of a **frontal assault on prepared field fortifications**, and of how a tactical victory
can be so costly as to constitute a strategic defeat. Fought, despite its name, mainly on **Breed's
Hill**.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

In June 1775 the British army lay besieged in [[siege-of-boston-1775|Boston]], penned by New England
militia after [[battles-of-lexington-and-concord-1775|Lexington and Concord]]. To break the
encirclement and dominate the harbor, the British command resolved to seize the **Charlestown
Peninsula** north of the city. Forewarned, the Americans moved first.

- **American objective (Putnam/Prescott).** Seize and fortify the high ground overlooking Boston, both
  to threaten the city and — explicitly — to **"bait the British into making an attack"** against
  prepared works, trading militia labor for British blood.
- **British objective (Howe).** Clear the peninsula and restore freedom of action. The *method* was the
  decision that mattered: [[clinton-henry|Clinton]] urged an **amphibious landing behind** the American
  position at the narrow neck, to cut the defenders off; Gage and [[howe-william|Howe]] judged it too
  risky and chose a **direct assault** up the slope.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground.** The peninsula offered three hills — Breed's (27 yds, nearest Boston), Morton's (the
  landing area), and Bunker (38 yds, highest, to the rear). Diverted by Putnam to **Breed's Hill** as
  closer to the city, Prescott fortified ground that was more provocative but also more **exposed and
  harder to retreat from** — a peninsula with a single narrow neck behind it.
- **The defenses.** Overnight and through 17 June the militia built a **square redoubt with a redan**, a
  **7-foot breastwork** extending ~55 yards to a swamp, and — critically — Stark's men buttressed a
  **rail-and-stone fence** along the beach with a stone wall and three V-shaped flèches, closing the gap
  between the breastwork and the water.
- **Intelligence.** The British could see the works being dug but underestimated their strength and the
  militia's steadiness; the Americans could see the methodical British landing and preparation, and chose
  their fire discipline accordingly.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| Total engaged | ~1,500 | ~2,300 |
| Redoubt (Breed's Hill) | Prescott's militia | Pigot's wing (demonstration → assault) |
| Breastwork → beach | militia; **Stark's** men on the rail fence & beach | Howe's wing: ~1,100 light infantry & grenadiers (beach flank) |
| Reserve | weak; no real reserve, short of powder | reinforcements called forward for the 3rd assault |
| Artillery | present but ineffective | present but ineffective |
| Command | Prescott (redoubt), Putnam (rear), Stark (beach) | Howe (right/beach), Pigot (left/redoubt) |

*Deployment (as a map would show it):* a **continuous but thin defensive line** — redoubt anchoring the
left toward Charlestown, breastwork extending right, and the fence/beach defenses closing the seaward
flank against Howe's intended envelopment.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **First assault (~3 p.m.).** Howe led from the front: a column on the beach to turn the flank, two
   infantry lines against the breastwork. **Stark's men, firing from the fence at ~50 yards, "shredded"
   the flanking column**; Prescott's militia held fire until close range and shattered the frontal
   advance. Pigot's demonstration against the redoubt, mistaking silence for abandonment, pressed an
   assault and was **repulsed at short range "with devastating effect."**

2. **Second assault.** Renewed and again broken by close-range volleys from the works — the British
   infantry could not carry the line by fire and maneuver alone.

3. **Third assault (~4 p.m.).** Howe **massed his reinforced infantry in column directly against the
   redoubt**. This time the Americans, **out of powder**, could not stop them; the redcoats closed and
   **carried the redoubt with the bayonet** — the arm the militia largely lacked. The defenders "fought
   stoutly but had no real chance and broke." The British, exhausted, could not pursue.

4. **Aftermath on the field.** The British had also fired shells to **burn Charlestown**, from which
   Americans were sniping at Howe's flank.

**Key mechanisms:** *fire discipline* (holding fire to lethal range) · *prepared field fortification*
defeating fire-and-maneuver · *the bayonet and powder supply* as the deciding material factors · a
*frontal method* chosen over an available *envelopment*.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

|---|---|---|

| Wounded | 278 | 828 |

| **Total** | **448** | **1,054** |

The British held the field but lost **nearly half their assault force**, "including numerous officers" —
the bloodiest day they would suffer in the war. A textbook **Pyrrhic victory**.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**American success factors:**
- **Choice of ground and prepared works** that forced the British into a costly frontal fight.
- **Fire discipline** — holding fire to short range — that wrecked the first two assaults.
- **Closing the seaward flank** (Stark's fence and beach wall), defeating Howe's intended envelopment.

**American shortcomings:**
- **No reserve and inadequate powder** — the line could not be sustained once ammunition ran low.
- **A forward, hard-to-retreat position** (Breed's rather than Bunker Hill) that risked the force.

**British shortcomings:**
- **The decision to assault frontally** rather than land at the neck to cut the defenders off — what
  subordinates called "gross ignorance of the most common … rules of the profession."
- **Frontal columns against fortified infantry** — repeated until sheer persistence and the bayonet (and
  the enemy's empty cartridge boxes) carried the day, at ruinous cost.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective.** Both sides achieved their nominal objective (the Americans baited an attack; the British
  took the hill) — but the British **objective was tactically met and strategically self-defeating**, a
  caution against confusing terrain capture with advantage.
- **Offensive.** British offensive spirit eventually prevailed, but **un-supported by maneuver** it cost
  more than it gained.
- **Mass.** The British massed in column for the third assault and broke through — but only after two
  unmassed, frontal failures.
- **Economy of Force.** The Americans economized brilliantly — ~1,500 inflicting 1,054 casualties — but
  squandered the advantage by **failing to resupply or reserve**.
- **Maneuver.** The **decisive lost opportunity**: Clinton's rejected envelopment at the neck would have
  trapped the defenders; the frontal assault forfeited maneuver entirely.
- **Unity of Command.** American command was improvised and divided (Ward, Putnam, Prescott, Stark) but
  cohered on the firing line; British command was unified but chose poorly.
- **Security.** American flank security (the fence/beach) was excellent; British security paid the price
  of the chosen method.
- **Surprise.** Little tactical surprise on either side; the Americans' fire discipline produced a
  *moment* of shock at close range.
- **Simplicity.** The American plan — fortify and hold fire — was simple and nearly sufficient; the
  British plan was simple but **wrong in conception**.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Bunker Hill is the enduring lesson on the **cost of frontal assaults against prepared defenses** and the
power of **fortification plus disciplined fire** to multiply weak troops — a foreshadowing of the
entrenched firepower problem that would dominate warfare from the American Civil War to 1914–18. It is
equally a study in **the difference between a tactical victory and a strategic result**: the British took
the ground and lost the campaign of attrition, because they could not replace a thousand veterans as
easily as the Americans could replace militia. Doctrinally it argues for **maneuver over attrition** —
Clinton's envelopment was the "right answer" the command refused — and it explains the **psychological
imprint** on Howe that produced his later caution at [[battle-of-long-island-1776|Long Island]], where
he declined to storm the Brooklyn lines. The modern principle that *you should fix and flank, not batter
frontally*, is written in the casualty returns of 17 June 1775.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **The victor's alarm — Gage.** The most telling primary witness is the British commander's own dismay.
  Gage warned London that the rebels "are now spirited up by a rage and enthusiasm as great as ever
  people were possessed of, and you must proceed in earnest or give the business up" — a victor confessing
  the battle had **revealed the scale of the task**, not reduced it.
- **The professional critique.** British subordinate officers' verdict — evidence of "gross ignorance of
  the most common … rules of the profession" — is a contemporary indictment of the frontal method, not a
  later historian's hindsight.
- **The myth and the lesson.** American memory mythologized Bunker Hill as a moral triumph ("Don't fire
  till you see the whites of their eyes"). Lengel keeps the analytical lesson central: disciplined infantry
  with bayonets will *eventually* carry earthworks held by ill-equipped militia — but the price can decide a
  war. He also flags the **double-edged consequence**: the battle gave Americans confidence but bred an
  **"overreliance on fixed defenses"** that Howe would exploit at New York.
