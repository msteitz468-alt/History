# Siege of Yorktown — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[siege-of-yorktown-1781|Siege of Yorktown (event page)]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/american-revolutionary-war|American Revolutionary War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college analysis of the engagement that ended the [[american-revolution|Revolution]]. Yorktown
is studied less as a feat of arms in the trenches — a textbook but unremarkable Vauban-style siege — than
as a **masterpiece of coalition and joint operations**: the synchronization of two armies and a battle
fleet, moving from New York, Rhode Island, and the West Indies, to converge on a single peninsula in a
single fortnight. Its decisive act was fought not in the siege lines but at sea, and its central lesson is
the dependence of all land operations, in this theater, on **command of the sea**.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

By the summer of 1781 the war hung in doubt. The Continental cause was fraying — mutinies over pay,
runaway inflation, a Congress without taxing power; Rochambeau "had even briefly contemplated withdrawing
the French expeditionary force from Newport," and Versailles was prepared, if pressed, to accept a
compromise peace leaving Britain the southern states. Into this fragile moment marched
[[cornwallis-charles|Cornwallis]], who — after the Pyrrhic ruin of [[battle-of-guilford-courthouse-1781|
Guilford Courthouse]] — abandoned the Carolinas for Virginia and, on [[clinton-henry|Clinton]]'s
instruction to secure a deep-water winter anchorage, fortified **Yorktown**.

- **Allied objective.** Trap and capture Cornwallis's army before it could be relieved or evacuated by
  sea — the decisive blow that could redeem the campaign and the alliance. The precondition was **temporary
  command of the Chesapeake**, without which no siege could be sealed.
- **British objective.** Hold the Yorktown lines until the Royal Navy and Clinton's relief expedition
  arrived from New York — a fundamentally **passive** objective that surrendered the initiative.

Conway's framing is essential: once Cornwallis "dug in at Yorktown, [the British] gave up the initiative
and became a fixed target, vulnerable to the kind of concerted attack by land and sea that had been
Clinton's nightmare since the French joined the war." The whole campaign turned on whether the allies
could, for a few weeks, **out-concentrate the Royal Navy in one place** — exactly the coordination that
had failed at [[siege-of-savannah-1779|Savannah]] (1779) and Newport (1778).

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground.** Yorktown sits on the south bank of the **York River**, on a low bluff in flat Tidewater
  country — defensible against a coup de main but, crucially, **commanded from the landward side** by ground
  on which besiegers could mount batteries, and dependent for survival on an **open seaward line of
  communication**. The river separated Cornwallis from a subsidiary post at **Gloucester Point**, splitting
  his force.
- **The sea as terrain.** The operational "key terrain" was not a hill but the **mouth of the Chesapeake**.
  Whoever controlled it controlled Cornwallis's fate — supply, reinforcement, and the only escape route.
- **Weather.** Weather twice intervened decisively: storms damaged ships through the campaign, and on the
  night of the British breakout attempt a sudden squall "made crossing the river impossible," extinguishing
  Cornwallis's last hope.
- **Intelligence and deception.** [[washington-george|Washington]] and Rochambeau ran a sustained
  **deception** to fix Clinton at New York: marching by routes "that suggested that Staten Island or Sandy
  Hook might be their objective." Clinton remained "convinced New York was the target until August 31" —
  too late. Cornwallis, for his part, did not learn until 2 September that Clinton had realized the truth,
  and "Cornwallis's last real chance of fighting his way out had passed" by 14 September.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | Allied (Washington / Rochambeau / de Grasse) | British (Cornwallis) |
|---|---|---|
| Besieging army | ~16,000+, a **French majority** of regulars | ~7,200 in the Yorktown/Gloucester works |
| American land contingent | Continentals + Lafayette's ~4,500 (the pinning force) | British regulars, German auxiliaries, Loyalists |
| French land contingent | Rochambeau's army (≈ equal to the Continentals) + 3,000 landed from de Grasse | — |
| Fleet | de Grasse: 28+ ships of the line (Chesapeake) | Graves's relief fleet: 19 ships of the line (driven off) |
| Siege train | heavy artillery brought down from Newport by **Admiral de Barras** | fixed garrison guns; two grasshopper guns insufficient |
| Notable units | 1st Rhode Island Regiment (incl. ~140 Black soldiers) | — |
| Command | Washington (allied C-in-C), Rochambeau, de Grasse, Lafayette | Cornwallis; O'Hara as second |

*Deployment (as a map would show it):* the allies invested the landward face of Yorktown in a classic
**siege arc**, with Lafayette's corps having first **pinned** Cornwallis to the peninsula; de Grasse's
fleet sealed the bay and the river mouth, and de Barras's siege guns gave the besiegers the metal to
batter the works. Cornwallis, having **retired from his outer defenses** to a tighter perimeter, ceded the
ground from which the allies opened their first parallel.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Phase 0 — The convergence (August–September).** Rochambeau's French left Newport (10 June) and joined
   Washington outside New York; the combined army feinted at the city, then "began to move south on August
   19." De Grasse sailed from the West Indies with 3,000 troops; de Barras brought the siege train from
   Newport. Lafayette's Virginia corps held Cornwallis on the peninsula. Four separate forces, on
   converging axes from hundreds of miles apart, arrived in the same window — the operational achievement of
   the war.

2. **Phase 1 — Decision at sea (5 September).** The **[[battle-of-the-chesapeake-1781|Battle of the
   Chesapeake]]**: de Grasse's 26-plus engaged Graves's 19 ships of the line. Tactically inconclusive — no
   ship lost — but Graves, outnumbered and with the rear division under Hood failing to engage, withdrew to
   New York to refit. With that, **British sea control of the Chesapeake was gone**, and with it Cornwallis's
   relief, resupply, and escape. Conway: "Perhaps this was the decisive moment of the whole campaign."

3. **Phase 2 — Investment and the first parallel (late September).** Cornwallis "pulled his men back from
   the outer defenses" — a choice Conway questions, since it "enabled the French and Americans to open a
   conventional siege, digging approach trenches to bring their guns within effective range." The allies
   closed in by 28 September and broke ground.

4. **Phase 3 — The bombardment (from 6 October).** "The allied artillery began its bombardment, and the
   remorseless shelling soon started to wear down the defenders." The German auxiliary **Johann Conrad
   Dohla** recorded its physical horror: shells "of 100 and 150 pounds, and also of 200 pounds … People were
   to be seen lying everywhere, fatally wounded, with heads, arms, and legs shot off."

5. **Phase 4 — The storming of Redoubts 9 and 10 (night of 14 October).** To open the **second parallel**
   on the river, two forward British redoubts had to be taken. The **French stormed Redoubt 9 "after a
   fierce fight"**; an American light-infantry column under **Alexander Hamilton** took **Redoubt 10** —
   "placing the allies within 350 yards of the main British defenses." The siege guns could now fire at
   point-blank range into the heart of the works.

6. **Phase 5 — The failed sortie and breakout (16–17 October).** Cornwallis tried a **sortie** to spike the
   allied guns — "only limited and temporary relief" — then attempted to **break out across the York to
   Gloucester** and march north; a storm wrecked the crossing. With his works collapsing and Dohla's
   comrades unable to "tolerate the enemy bombs … any longer," Cornwallis opened negotiations on **17
   October**, the fourth anniversary of [[battles-of-saratoga-1777|Saratoga]].

7. **Phase 6 — Surrender (19 October).** Cornwallis's ~7,200 marched out and grounded arms — "just as
   Clinton was setting sail from New York with the relief force." Cornwallis, "unwilling to participate,"
   feigned illness and sent **General O'Hara**, who tried to surrender to Rochambeau first; the French
   admiral, "merely an auxiliary of the Americans," directed him to Washington, who in turn — Cornwallis
   being absent — had his subordinate **Benjamin Lincoln** (the [[siege-of-charleston-1780|loser of
   Charleston]]) receive the sword.

**Key operational mechanisms:** *strategic deception* fixing Clinton at New York · *concentration from
dispersal* (four forces, one window) · *command of the sea* as the decisive enabler · *methodical siege
craft* (parallels, redoubts, second parallel) once the sea was won.

The siege casualties were modest; the result was total — the **loss of a second field army**, following
Saratoga. The *West Point History* does not enumerate the killed and wounded (the figures above are
conventional); its emphasis is on the **completeness of the capture** and its political effect.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Allied success factors:**
- **Strategic deception** that pinned Clinton at New York while the army marched 450 miles south.
- **Joint synchronization** of army and fleet — the war's supreme staff achievement — bringing de Grasse,
  de Barras, Rochambeau, Washington, and Lafayette together in one fortnight.
- **Winning the sea first.** Everything depended on the Chesapeake; once Graves withdrew, the siege was a
  matter of engineering.
- **Rochambeau's judgment and money** — he pressed the Virginia option over Washington's preference for New
  York, and **loaned Washington his war chest** to pay Continentals who would not otherwise have marched.
- **Methodical siege craft** — French engineers ran a copybook approach (parallels, the storming of 9 and
  10, the second parallel) that made the outcome inevitable.

**British shortcomings:**
- **A passive objective.** Fixing the army in a coastal fort surrendered the initiative and bet everything
  on naval relief that the enemy could deny.
- **Cornwallis's passivity.** "Previously one of the most daring commanders of the war, he remained
  remarkably passive." Conway argues he should have **attacked Lafayette before the trap closed** ("he would
  surely have had a better chance of extricating himself") or held his **outer defenses** to keep the
  besiegers' guns at bay.
- **Naval failure** — Hood's rear division at the Chesapeake "failed to bring the ships … fully into the
  battle"; Graves's withdrawal forfeited the campaign.
- **Coordination breakdown** — the fatal lag between Clinton's intentions and Cornwallis's knowledge, and a
  relief expedition that sailed the very day of the surrender.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective.** The allies held a single, clear, decisive objective — **the capture of Cornwallis's army**
  — and bent army and fleet to it. The British objective (passive survival pending relief) was inherently
  reactive.
- **Offensive.** The allies seized and **never relinquished the initiative** from the New York feint to the
  second parallel; Cornwallis's only offensive acts (the sortie, the breakout) came too late and failed.
- **Mass.** The campaign is a paradigm of **massing combat power at the decisive point** — naval and land
  force concentrated on one peninsula at one time, achieving better than two-to-one on land and superiority
  at sea.
- **Economy of Force.** **Lafayette's economy-of-force corps** fixed and shadowed Cornwallis for months with
  inferior numbers ("I am not strong enough even to get beaten"), holding him in place until the main effort
  could arrive.
- **Maneuver.** The **operational maneuver** — converging four forces from New York, Newport, and the
  Caribbean — placed Cornwallis in a position of irretrievable disadvantage before a siege gun was fired.
- **Unity of Command.** The harder for being a **coalition**: Washington as allied C-in-C, with Rochambeau's
  gracious subordination ("merely an auxiliary") and de Grasse's cooperation, produced genuine unity of
  effort — the rare allied operation in which national frictions did not fracture the plan.
- **Security.** Allied operational security (the deception of Clinton) was excellent; British security
  failed at the strategic level — they never divined the southern concentration in time.
- **Surprise.** Strategic surprise — Clinton "convinced New York was the target until August 31" — gave the
  allies the time margin the whole plan required.
- **Simplicity.** The plan was **operationally complex but conceptually simple**: win the bay, pin the army,
  besiege the port. The simplicity of the *concept* held a complex multinational operation together.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Yorktown is taught as the founding American example of **joint and combined operations** — the integration
of land and naval power, and of allied forces under unified command, toward a single operational objective.
Its central doctrinal lesson is the **primacy of sea control (today, of the joint enabler)**: an
expeditionary army ashore is only as secure as its lines of communication, and the [[battle-of-the-chesapeake-1781|
Chesapeake]] shows how a force can be rendered combat-ineffective without a shot at it on land, simply by
severing its sea line. The campaign also exemplifies **operational maneuver from dispersed bases converging
in time** — the synchronization problem at the heart of modern operational art — and **strategic deception**
as a force multiplier (the New York feint). Finally, it is a model of **coalition warfare**: the
subordination of a stronger partner's pride to unity of command (Rochambeau under Washington) is precisely
the behavior modern multinational doctrine prizes and rarely obtains. The siege craft itself — parallels,
the reduction of forward redoubts, the second parallel — is the Vauban system at its mature height,
unremarkable in method and all the more instructive for it.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **The view from inside the works — Johann Conrad Dohla.** The most vivid primary voice is a German
  auxiliary's diary, valuable precisely because it is **neither French nor American nor British**: Dohla had
  no stake in the victors' myth, and his account of bodies "lying everywhere … with heads, arms, and legs
  shot off," and of relief that the garrison surrendered "with a reasonable accord, because we always
  believed we would be taken by storm," conveys the bombardment's reality without national gloss.
- **Conway's revisionist emphasis — the French were decisive.** The *West Point History*, though a U.S.
  Military Academy text, is bracingly clear that **Yorktown was a French victory as much as an American
  one**: "the majority of the regular soldiers … were French," and "the vital ingredient … was the French
  warships; without their presence and victory over the Royal Navy, Yorktown would not have been possible."
  This corrects the patriotic memory that centers Washington and the Continentals.
- **What the volume omits.** Conway narrates the siege through its decisive turns and Dohla's testimony but
  **does not tabulate casualties** — a reminder that even excellent secondary accounts privilege the
  decisive and the human over the statistical. The casualty figures cited here are conventional, not drawn
  from this source.
- **The interpretive payoff.** For Conway, Yorktown vindicates his whole thesis on **why the British
  failed**: the war became unwinnable once "the American army [could cooperate] with the French navy to
  capture an exposed British outpost" — the nightmare Clinton had named in 1778 and that "finally, at
  Yorktown," came true. The battle did not so much *cause* British defeat as *consummate* a strategic
  position that French intervention had already made nearly hopeless.
