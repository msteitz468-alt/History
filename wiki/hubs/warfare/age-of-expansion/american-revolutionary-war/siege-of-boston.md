# Siege of Boston — Operational Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[siege-of-boston-1775|Siege of Boston (event page)]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/american-revolutionary-war|American Revolutionary War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college analysis of the war's **opening siege** — won not by assault but by **seizing commanding
ground and emplacing artillery to make the enemy's position untenable**, and the operational crucible in
which [[washington-george|Washington]] forged the **[[continental-army|Continental Army]]**.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

After [[battles-of-lexington-and-concord-1775|Lexington and Concord]], New England militia penned the
British garrison inside Boston. The bloody check at [[battle-of-bunker-hill-1775|Bunker Hill]] confirmed a
stalemate that lasted eleven months.

- **American objective.** **Drive the British out of Boston** — and, for Washington (who took command in July
  1775), do so by a **decisive, war-shortening blow** if possible, while building a real army out of a militia
  rabble.
- **British objective.** Hold the port pending reinforcement and a strategic decision; eventually, **extricate
  the garrison** rather than waste it.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground — the commanding heights.** Boston was a near-island dominated by surrounding hills. Whoever held
  the heights (Bunker/Breed's to the north, **Dorchester Heights** to the south) **commanded the city and
  harbor**. The siege turned on a contest for that high ground.
- **Winter and the guns.** The decisive operational act was logistical: **[[knox-henry|Henry Knox]]'s
  "noble train of artillery,"** dragging the heavy guns captured at Ticonderoga overland through the winter to
  arm the heights.
- **Intelligence/judgment.** Washington repeatedly sought a decisive stroke; his councils of war restrained
  him, and Howe, "perhaps reminded of Bunker Hill," correctly judged Dorchester Heights too costly to assault.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| Force | New England army → early Continentals (fluctuating, often ~16,000) | ~6,500–11,000 garrison |
| Key assets | **Knox's Ticonderoga guns**; the surrounding heights | the fortified town; the fleet in the harbor |
| Decisive ground | **Dorchester Heights** (seized 4–5 March 1776) | the city, now under the guns |
| Diversion of effort | **Arnold's Canada expedition** (drew off 1,000 men) | — |
| Command | Ward, then Washington (July 1775); Knox (artillery) | Gage, then Howe |

*Deployment (as a map would show it):* the Americans **ringed Boston from the land side**, the British held
the town and harbor; the siege's decision came when the Americans **occupied and fortified Dorchester
Heights**, training Knox's guns on the city and fleet.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Investment and army-building (summer–autumn 1775).** Washington arrived at Cambridge (2 July) to find a
   "ramshackle" force and set about creating a **disciplined Continental Army** — chains of command, rank
   distinctions, administration, supply. Congress diverted strength into **[[arnold-benedict|Arnold]]'s
   wilderness invasion of Canada**, which "came to grief before the walls of Quebec."

2. **Frustrated assault plans (winter).** Washington twice pressed for a **direct assault** — including a wild
   scheme to attack across the frozen harbor ice — and was **overruled by his councils of war**, which
   counseled patience.

3. **The decisive maneuver — Dorchester Heights (4–5 March 1776).** Instead, the Americans **seized and
   fortified Dorchester Heights overnight** and emplaced Knox's Ticonderoga guns, "from which artillery soon
   opened fire on the city." The British position became untenable in a stroke.

4. **The evacuation (17 March 1776).** Howe briefly considered storming the heights, then — "reminded of
   Bunker Hill" — **chose to evacuate**; the last British troops left on 17 March. Washington, who wanted a
   bloody, war-shortening victory, privately called the bloodless win a "disappointment."

**Key mechanisms:** *seizing commanding ground* to make a position untenable · *artillery emplacement* (the
Ticonderoga guns) as the decisive operational act · *patience over assault* (the councils restraining
Washington) · *army-building under the cover of a siege*.

## 5. Outcome and Casualties

| | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| Siege casualties (excl. Bunker Hill) | light | light |
| Result | took Boston **bloodlessly** | evacuated the garrison intact |

A clean American strategic victory — the **liberation of a major city** and the first proof that the new army
could conduct a successful operation — though Howe preserved his garrison for the
[[battle-of-long-island-1776|New York campaign]].

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**American success factors:**
- **The Knox artillery feat** — emplacing heavy guns on commanding ground, the operation's decisive act.
- **Seizing Dorchester Heights** to make Boston untenable **without an assault** — maneuver over attrition.
- **Washington's army-building** during the siege, creating the instrument for the rest of the war.
- **Sound councils of war** that restrained Washington's costly assault schemes.

**American shortcomings:**
- The diversion into the **failed Canada expedition** dissipated strength.
- Washington's **eagerness for a bloody assault** — checked, fortunately, by his subordinates.

**British shortcomings:**
- A **passive garrison posture** that ceded the initiative and the heights.
- Failure to seize and hold Dorchester Heights themselves before the Americans did.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective.** The American objective (clear Boston) was achieved by an **indirect approach** — the heights —
  rather than the costly assault Washington wanted; a lesson in choosing the means that achieves the objective
  at least cost.
- **Offensive.** The decisive offensive act was the **night seizure of Dorchester Heights**, not a frontal
  attack.
- **Mass.** The Americans massed **artillery** on the commanding ground — the decisive concentration of combat
  power.
- **Economy of Force.** The Canada expedition was arguably a **violation** — strength dispersed to a secondary
  theater that failed.
- **Maneuver.** The campaign's lesson: **maneuver (seizing key terrain) trumped assault** — the antidote to the
  Bunker Hill error.
- **Unity of Command.** Washington forged unity out of a militia confederation; his **councils of war**
  functioned as a healthy check on his aggressive instincts.
- **Security.** The overnight fortification of Dorchester Heights was a model of **operational security and
  surprise**.
- **Surprise.** The Heights were occupied **before the British could react** — operational surprise.
- **Simplicity.** A simple, decisive operational stroke (occupy the heights, emplace the guns) succeeded where
  Washington's riskier assault schemes were rightly refused.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Boston is the war's clearest lesson in the **indirect approach** and the power of **key terrain plus
artillery** to render a position untenable **without a costly assault** — the maneuver answer to the frontal
problem posed at [[battle-of-bunker-hill-1775|Bunker Hill]] just months before. Knox's overland movement of
the Ticonderoga guns is a classic study in **operational logistics as a decisive act**: the campaign was won
by *moving artillery*, not by fighting. It also models **the value of the council of war as a check on a
commander's aggression** — modern command climates that encourage candid staff dissent exist precisely to
prevent the costly assault Washington twice proposed. Finally, it shows a force **building and training itself
under the cover of an operation** (the creation of the Continental Army during the siege) — the integration of
force generation with active operations that modern armies still must manage.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **The army-building frame.** Lengel reads the siege less as a feat of arms than as the **forge of the
  Continental Army** — Washington's months of disciplining and organizing a "ramshackle" force are, in this
  telling, the campaign's most consequential work, more than the maneuver that ended it.
- **Washington's "disappointment."** The most revealing source-detail is Washington's private regret at the
  *bloodless* victory — he wanted a costly battle to weaken Lord North's ministry. It documents the
  **aggressive temperament** Lengel stresses (the un-Fabian Washington) and the gap between his instincts and
  the prudent counsel that restrained him.
- **Scale vs. significance.** Outside [[battle-of-bunker-hill-1775|Bunker Hill]], the siege saw little
  bloodshed; its importance is **operational and institutional**, not a matter of casualties — a reminder that
  decisive campaigns need not be bloody, and that the *creation of an army* can outweigh any single battle.
