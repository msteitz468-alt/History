# American Revolutionary War — Campaign & Operational Analysis

> **Narrative & political/diplomatic story:** [[american-revolution|The American Revolution (event page)]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]] · **Period:** [[age-of-expansion|Age of Expansion]]

This is the **campaign hub** for the military conduct of the War of Independence: the
strategy, operations, and logistics by which an initially disorganized
[[continental-army|Continental Army]] — backed by state militias and, decisively, by a
French alliance — defeated the world's foremost military and naval power. The broader
political, ideological, and diplomatic narrative lives on the **[[american-revolution]]**
event page; this hub owns the *war*. Battle-level staff analyses are filed beneath this page.

The wiki's source for this analysis is the U.S. Military Academy's cadet textbook,
*[[west-point-history-american-revolution-2017|The West Point History of the American Revolution]]* (2017).

## Strategic Framework

The war turned on an asymmetry the British never solved. Britain had to **reconquer and
hold** a vast, populous territory 3,000 miles across an ocean; the Americans had only to
**not lose their army** and outlast British political will. Washington, after the near-ruin
of 1776, converted this into a deliberate strategy of **protraction and survival** — a
[[fabian-strategy|Fabian]] husbanding of the Continental Army as the embodiment of the cause
— while seeking the foreign intervention that could offset British sea power. Saratoga (1777)
secured the **French alliance**; French money, troops, and above all the **fleet** made the
trap at [[siege-of-yorktown-1781|Yorktown]] possible. The British, by contrast, never
reconciled their political aim (restoring loyal allegiance) with their military means
(coercion that alienated the population), and pursued a succession of strategies — New
England, the Hudson, Pennsylvania, and finally the **southern strategy** — without unifying
command or sustaining any one to a decision.

## Theaters and Phases

1. **New England & the siege of Boston (1775–76).** [[battles-of-lexington-and-concord-1775|Lexington & Concord]], [[battle-of-bunker-hill-1775|Bunker Hill]], the [[siege-of-boston-1775|siege of Boston]]; the war begins and the Continental Army is created.
2. **The middle states (1776–78).** The disastrous [[new-york-campaign-1776|New York campaign]]; the recovery at [[battle-of-trenton-1776|Trenton]] and [[battle-of-princeton-1777|Princeton]]; Howe's Pennsylvania campaign ([[battle-of-brandywine-1777|Brandywine]], [[battle-of-germantown-1777|Germantown]]); [[valley-forge-1777|Valley Forge]] and the professionalization of the army under [[steuben-friedrich|Steuben]]; [[battle-of-monmouth-1778|Monmouth]].
3. **The northern turning point (1777).** [[battles-of-saratoga-1777|Saratoga]] — Burgoyne's surrender, and the diplomatic earthquake that brought France into the war.
4. **The southern war (1778–81).** The British [[siege-of-savannah-1779|move south]]; the [[siege-of-charleston-1780|fall of Charleston]] and [[battle-of-camden-1780|Camden]]; the partisan war and [[battle-of-kings-mountain-1780|Kings Mountain]]; Greene's campaign of attrition with [[battle-of-cowpens-1781|Cowpens]] and [[battle-of-guilford-courthouse-1781|Guilford Courthouse]].
5. **Decision in Virginia (1781).** Cornwallis to the Chesapeake; the Franco-American convergence and the [[siege-of-yorktown-1781|siege of Yorktown]].

## Why the British Failed

Conway's analysis (chapter 5) frames the British defeat as strategic and political, not a
catalogue of battlefield losses — Britain won most major engagements. The decisive factors:
the **logistical tyranny of distance** and dependence on seaborne supply; the failure to
**translate military victory into restored political allegiance**; the **manpower shortfall**
that forced reliance on Loyalists and Germans and on a southern strategy premised on
Loyalist support that never materialized at scale; **divided and hesitant command**; and the
**French naval and military intervention** that, by 1781, stripped Britain of the local sea
control on which its whole expeditionary posture rested.
