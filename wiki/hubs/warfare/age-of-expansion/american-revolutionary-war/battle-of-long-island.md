# Battle of Long Island — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[battle-of-long-island-1776|Battle of Long Island (event page)]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/american-revolutionary-war|American Revolutionary War]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college analysis of the **largest battle of the [[american-revolution|Revolution]]** — a
masterclass in **operational envelopment** by [[howe-william|Howe]], a near-catastrophe of American
deployment and command, and a study in how **enemy restraint and a skilled withdrawal** can preserve an
army that should have been destroyed.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

Having evacuated [[siege-of-boston-1775|Boston]], the British returned in overwhelming force to seize
**New York**, the great harbor and the strategic hinge of the continent. Washington, **ordered by
Congress to hold a city he judged untenable**, divided his army across Manhattan and Long Island.

- **British objective.** Defeat the American army and take New York as a base. Howe, a liberal Whig,
  sought **defeats that would bring the rebels to terms, not annihilation** — a political constraint that
  shaped his conduct.
- **American objective.** Hold the Brooklyn Heights line covering the East River and the city — against
  Washington's own military judgment.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground.** The wooded **Gowanus Heights** ridge, crossed by four passes, screened the Brooklyn
  fortifications. The American defense hinged on guarding those passes — and especially the eastern
  **Jamaica Pass**, the open flank.
- **The intelligence failure.** The Americans posted only **five mounted officers** to watch Jamaica Pass.
  Howe, exploiting local Loyalist knowledge, marched **10,000 men overnight** through it; the five officers
  were captured "without sounding the alert."
- **Weather as savior.** Fog on the night of 29–30 August would cloak the American evacuation across the
  East River.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | American | British |
|---|---|---|
| Total | ~9,000 (Long Island) | ~20,000 ashore |
| Forward (Gowanus Heights) | ~3,300 guarding three passes | demonstration force (center/right) + Germans |
| Jamaica Pass (open flank) | **5 mounted officers** | **Howe's 10,000-man turning column** |
| Right wing | Stirling's Maryland & Virginia Continentals | Cornwallis |
| Command | Putnam (by seniority), Sullivan, Stirling | Howe, Cornwallis |

*Deployment (as a map would show it):* a **forward line on the Gowanus ridge** with the **left flank
(Jamaica Pass) effectively open** — the fatal gap into which Howe poured his main column, exactly the
Bunker Hill template in reverse: demonstrate against the front, **turn the unguarded flank**.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **The night turning movement.** While two British brigades pressed the American right and 5,000 Germans
   demonstrated against the center, **Howe marched overnight to Jamaica Pass**, occupying it unopposed —
   "strict march discipline kept the troops cohesive and helped ensure surprise."

2. **The envelopment (9 a.m., 27 August).** Howe's column "drove practically unopposed into the American
   rear." Putnam's left and center "collapsed as soldiers fled or surrendered"; General **Sullivan was
   captured**.

3. **Stirling's stand.** On the right, **Lord Stirling's Maryland and Virginia Continentals** "stood and
   fought" against hopeless odds, then were "driven into Gowanus Creek; most surrendered" — buying time
   with their own destruction.

4. **Howe halts.** Decisively, Howe **did not storm the Brooklyn Heights fortifications**, halting to
   "conduct a formal siege" — out of memory of Bunker Hill, hope of reconciliation, and his Whig
   sympathies. This restraint saved the American army.

5. **The escape.** On the foggy night of 29–30 August, Washington **evacuated ~9,000 men across the East
   River "without loss."**

**Key mechanisms:** *operational envelopment* through an open flank · *fixing attacks* (front and center)
masking the turning column · a *sacrificial rear-guard stand* (Stirling) · *enemy restraint* and a *skilled
night withdrawal* preserving the force.

A severe American defeat — but **not the destruction it should have been**, thanks to Howe's halt and the
foggy evacuation. The army survived to fight at [[battle-of-trenton-1776|Trenton]].

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**British success factors:**
- **A textbook operational envelopment** through the unguarded Jamaica Pass, masked by fixing attacks.
- **March discipline and Loyalist guides** that delivered surprise.

**British shortcomings:**
- **Howe's halt** before the Brooklyn lines — politically motivated restraint that **let the trapped army
  escape**, the single decision that arguably cost Britain the war's best chance to end it.

**American shortcomings:**
- **A fatally open flank** — five officers watching the decisive pass.
- **Confused command** — Greene's illness, then Putnam taking over "by seniority," produced disorder.
- **Holding an untenable position** at Congress's insistence against Washington's judgment.

**American saving graces:**
- **Stirling's sacrificial stand** and **Washington's masterly night evacuation** under fog.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective.** Howe achieved the ground objective but, by **declining to destroy the army**, missed the
  decisive objective; Washington failed to hold New York but preserved the force — the more important aim.
- **Offensive.** British offensive maneuver was excellent up to the moment Howe **abandoned the offensive**
  before the fortifications.
- **Mass.** Howe massed his turning column (10,000) at the decisive point; the Americans dispersed their
  inferior numbers and left the flank uncovered.
- **Economy of Force.** The British fixing attacks economized force to free the main column; the Americans'
  five-officer screen was economy of force taken to negligence.
- **Maneuver.** The battle is a **paradigm of the turning movement** — victory won by maneuver onto the
  enemy's rear rather than by frontal assault.
- **Unity of Command.** American command confusion (illness, seniority disputes) contrasted with Howe's
  firm direction — until his halt.
- **Security.** The **catastrophic American flank-security failure** at Jamaica Pass is the battle's central
  lesson.
- **Surprise.** Achieved completely by the night turning march.
- **Simplicity.** Howe's plan was simple and well-executed; American dispositions were neither clearly
  conceived nor clearly commanded.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Long Island is the war's textbook **turning movement / envelopment**: fix the enemy frontally, mass a
turning force against an open flank, and strike the rear — the maneuver modern doctrine still prizes above
the frontal assault, and the mirror image of the costly frontal method at [[battle-of-bunker-hill-1775|
Bunker Hill]]. Its second, harder lesson is about **flank security and economy of force**: a single
uncovered avenue of approach (Jamaica Pass) unhinged an entire defensive position — the doctrinal warning
that you must cover the *decisive* gap, not merely the obvious ones. Finally, it is a study in **the
preservation of a beaten force**: Howe's failure to press to annihilation and Washington's night
withdrawal under fog together illustrate the difference between *winning a battle* and *destroying an
army* — the distinction on which the whole war turned, since an army that escapes lives to win at
[[battle-of-trenton-1776|Trenton]].

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **The "what if" of Howe's halt.** The central historiographical debate is **why Howe stopped**. Lengel
  attributes it to a convergence of motives — the trauma of Bunker Hill, his Whig hope for reconciliation,
  and a desire to inflict defeats without a bloodbath ("to bring the rebels to the peace table without
  rendering … reconciliation impossible"). Critics then and since see a decisive opportunity thrown away.
- **The numbers and the screen.** The figures (~9,000 American, ~20,000 British; ~1,000 captured) and the
  almost satirical detail of the **five-officer Jamaica Pass screen** come from Lengel's narrative; the
  flank failure is uncontested and damning.
- **The interpretive payoff.** Long Island sets the pattern Lengel traces through 1776 — **Howe's tactical
  and operational superiority repeatedly failing to convert into a decisive result** because of his
  political restraint — the gap that Washington exploited to keep the army, and the cause, alive.
