# Siege of Caizhou (1233–34) — Tactical Analysis

> **Narrative & full sources:** [[events/siege-of-caizhou-1234|Siege of Caizhou]] · **War hub:** [[hubs/warfare/high-middle-ages/mongol-conquests/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

A staff-college-level analysis of the Jin dynasty's terminal siege — the **nerge** isolation of
Caizhou, Emperor Ai-Tsung's refusal of defensible ground, starvation and currency collapse, the
Mongol–**Song alliance**, deliberate **flooding** of approaches, and Tachar Noyan's patient
consolidation before final assault. Drawn primarily from [[may-mongol-art-of-war-2016|Timothy May,
*The Mongol Art of War*]] (ch. 8, "Sieges"), which treats Caizhou as the ideal exemplar of Mongol
siege technique and ruthless operational method.

## 1. Strategic Context and Objectives

**Mongols (Tachar Noyan; campaign context of Sübedei).** By 1231 the Jin Empire had been reduced
to eastern Honan; Kaifeng, the former capital, lay under Sübedei's attention and fell in 1233. The
Mongol strategic objective in North China was **total elimination of the Jin polity** — a
twenty-year war of attrition reaching its final phase. Caizhou was not an isolated battle but the
**last knot in a net** already woven across North China. Tachar, whom May characterises as a less
famous but fully competent commander, inherited the closing act of a campaign Sübedei had opened.
Ögödei Khan's confidence in reassigning Sübedei to the Kipchak and European front before Jin
collapse was complete demonstrates the depth of the Mongol **generalship bench**: the system
expected subordinate noyans to finish wars without the senior field marshal present.

Tachar's operational objectives at Caizhou were: (1) **isolate** the city and its emperor; (2)
**overstretch** food supply by driving rural population inward via nerge; (3) **prevent relief**
through multi-column manoeuvre; (4) **alliance with the Song** to block southern resupply and
add breach capacity; (5) **breach and reduce** without culminating prematurely — patience as a
weapon.

**Jin (Emperor Ai-Tsung).** The Jin objective was survival of the dynasty — or at minimum
preservation of the imperial line until relief or favourable negotiation. Having fled Kaifeng as
Sübedei closed on that city, Ai-Tsung chose Caizhou over the counsel of multiple advisers. The
choice was strategically disastrous: Caizhou was **only 100 li from the Song frontier**, meaning
the enemy could ally with the Song for troops and grain — which occurred. It lacked strong walls
for prolonged siege, sat in hot rainy season terrain vulnerable to flooding, and offered **no
waterborne escape** if it fell (unlike the alternative Kuei-te, surrounded by water).

The Jin faced structural constraints beyond geography: **desertion** among soldiers and generals,
armies **overextended** defending territory so that commanders could not concentrate for the
emperor's relief, and a court that trusted astrology over alliance diplomacy. Retired general
Nei-tsu A-hu-tai urged Song alliance for grain and to keep the Song from joining the Mongols; the
advice went unheeded. The emperor instead sent 3,300 men to reinforce the Song border while
boasting that 3,000 armoured troops could march into Chiang and Huai — contempt that underestimated
both Mongols and Song.

## 2. Terrain, Weather, and Intelligence

- **Ground.** Caizhou lay in eastern Honan, the last Jin heartland fragment. Prince Yung-An had
  argued for **Kuei-te at Shang-Tung**, surrounded by water that would hinder Mongol attack, with
  local fish and vegetables for subsistence even if grain was short. Caizhou, by contrast, was a
  **hard spot turned vulnerable** — close to the Song line, poorly fortified, and exposed to
  Mongol nerge from multiple directions. The **Lien River** and **Ju River** defined western and
  southern approaches; in January 1234 Mongol and Song cooperation would burst embankments and
  flood these avenues, allowing penetration to the city's western edge.

- **Weather / season.** Yung-An's sixth counsel noted the **hot, rainy season** and already
  **flooded land**, which would impede reinforcements reaching Caizhou while Kuei-te remained
  reachable by water. The siege opened in **late October 1233** and deepened through winter; by
  December food prices reached astronomical levels. Flooding in January 1234 compounded starvation.
  The Jin court astrologer Wa Kang predicted Jin victory but siege lasting until the thirteenth day
  of the new year when "there would not be a single person or horse in the city" — wrong on victory
  and calendar day of fall (9 February, tenth day), eerily right on annihilation (Mongols left on
  the thirteenth).

- **Intelligence.** The Mongols knew Caizhou was the emperor's destination and targeted it
  deliberately. Jin intelligence of Mongol intent was adequate at the command level — by October
  all Jin commanders knew Caizhou was the target — but **strategic intelligence failed upward**:
  the emperor's pride prevented relocation. Jin messengers seeking New Year coordinated relief were
  **intercepted**; commanders who received word concluded rescue was doomed and preferred post-fall
  submission on favourable terms to Mongol-abhorred disloyalty mid-crisis.

## 3. Order of Battle and Deployment

| Element | Mongols (Tachar) + Song | Jin (Ai-Tsung) |
|---|---|---|
| Command | Tachar Noyan — patient encirclement, algincin ambush | Emperor Ai-Tsung; executed Kuan-nu; factional court |
| Cavalry | Mongol horse archers in multiple columns | Jurchen cavalry degraded by desertion and horse slaughter for food |
| Infantry / garrison | Song 10,000 (allied breach attempt) | Imperial Guard (deserted early); eunuchs and courtiers on walls in extremis |
| Engineers / hydraulics | Embankment breach Lien River; coordinated Ju River flood with Song | Civilians' houses dismantled for inner defences |
| Isolation force | Nerge columns taking surrounding cities | Yüan Chih's relief fought running battle to enter |

*Deployment:* Mongols approached in **many columns** (October 1233), forcing Jin to react
piecemeal and preventing consolidation. Nerge tactics **herded population into the city**,
accelerating famine — the siege's true centre of gravity. Song forces massed at the frontier,
captured Tang Prefecture (18 September 1233), and later joined the besiegers. Tachar positioned
algincin to ambush Wen-Tun Ch'ang Sun's daily fishing expedition from the city to Lien Chiang —
eliminating the emperor's privileged food source. By January 1234 Mongols held western portions
behind a **palisade** against counter-attack.

## 4. Course of the Battle — Phases

1. **Strategic prelude — wrong capital (1231–October 1233).** Sübedei reduced Jin territory;
  Ai-Tsung fled Kaifeng toward Caizhou. Kuan-nu urged Sui-yang; the emperor executed him for
  perceived slight. Prince Yung-An's detailed six-point memorandum (August 1233) foresaw Song–Mongol
  cooperation, flooding, and escape problems; rejected for distrust. Puxian found Caizhou too weak
  but the emperor was already en route. Kaifeng fell to Sübedei 1233; Caizhou bought **months**, not
  salvation.

2. **Nerge isolation (late October–November 1233).** Tachar advanced in nerge fashion, capturing
  other cities and sealing Caizhou. Yüan Chih arrived with reinforcements through a **running
  battle** — proof relief was possible but costly. Other commanders withheld troops to protect
  their own territories. Attack opened **19 November**; Imperial Guard deserted to Mongols shortly
  after. Grain requisition with promotions for hoarders coincided with **overnight currency
  devaluation** — economic collapse signalling loss of state credibility.

3. **Starvation and desperation (December 1233–January 1234).** Food prices astronomical;
  **cannibalism** emerged; executions of cannibals failed to stop it. The emperor still ate well
  until Tachar destroyed Ch'ang Sun's fishing column (November). Factions proposed magic potions and
  lion-masked horses with bells to frighten Mongol mounts; the emperor dismissed the horse scheme as
  folly. Song joined with **10,000 men** and briefly breached a wall; Jin drove them back. Nei-tsu
  A-hu-tai's ignored Song-alliance counsel materialised as nightmare.

4. **Flooding and western penetration (8–20 January 1234).** On 8 January Mongols burst the Lien
  River embankment while Song efforts flooded the Ju River protecting western and southern
  approaches. Water allowed Mongol penetration to the **western edge**; by 20 January western
  portions were captured. Tachar erected a **palisade** to secure gains — the doctrinal opposite
  of reckless pursuit of glory.

5. **Final reduction and dynastic end (January–9 February 1234).** Jin resisted: horses slaughtered
  for rations, houses destroyed for inner works, eunuchs and courtiers sent to walls. Coordinated
  relief promised for New Year's Day **never materialised** — messengers intercepted, commanders
  waiting to submit post-fall. On **8 February** Ai-Tsung abdicated to Nei-tsu Ch'eng-lin; **9
  February** Caizhou fell. The Jin dynasty, resisting the Mongols for over twenty years,
  ended.

**Key tactical mechanisms:** nerge isolation and population herding; multi-column fixation;
Mongol–Song **combined breach and flood**; algincin ambush of logistics; palisade consolidation;
interception of relief coordination; patience before final assault.

|---|---|---|
| Killed | Not quantified by May | Catastrophic civilian and military losses; cannibalism; emperor died with city |
| Captured / deserted | — | Imperial Guard deserted en masse; commanders submitted after fall |
| Strategic | Jin dynasty destroyed; Tachar completed Sübedei's war | Twenty-year state extinguished |

- **Strategic result:** Mongol objective achieved — Jin elimination. Song gained frontier
  advantage temporarily but armed a partner that would soon turn on them.
- **Immediate follow-on:** North China cleared for Mongol–Song confrontation; demonstration that
  nerge + starvation + flooding could reduce even an imperial capital without Sübedei present.
- **Longer-term consequence:** May stresses Caizhou as proof of Mongol **command depth** and
  systemic ruthlessness — the ideal teaching example of their siege approach.

## 6. Critical Decisions — What Went Right and Wrong

**Mongol success factors.** (1) **Nerge isolation** — herding population inward to collapse
  logistics; (2) **Multi-column operations** preventing Jin concentration; (3) **Song alliance**
  — diplomatic manoeuvre turning southern neighbour from potential relief into co-belligerent; (4)
  **Hydraulic breaching** — Lien embankment burst plus Song Ju River flood; (5) **Tachar's
  patience** — palisade before pursuit, securing western gains against counter-attack; (6)
  **Algincin ambush** of fishing sortie — targeting emperor's food security; (7) **Messenger
  interdiction** breaking New Year relief plan; (8) **Generalship pipeline** — war finished by
  Tachar after Sübedei's reassignment without loss of competence.

**Jin shortcomings.** (1) **Caizhou instead of Kuei-te** — pride over geography; (2) **Execution
  of Kuan-nu** — removing the commander who read the map; (3) **Rejection of Song alliance** while
  underestimating Song military role; (4) **Overextension** — armies guarding territory could not
  rescue emperor; (5) **Astrology over logistics** — Wa Kang's prophecy bred complacency; (6)
  **Desertion cascade** — Imperial Guard to Mongols; commanders awaiting post-fall submission; (7)
  **Failure of coordinated relief** — New Year offensive plan collapsed; (8) **Desperation
  tactics** (magic potions, lion masks) without addressing encirclement.

## 7. Lessons Learned — The Principles of War (FM 3-0)

- **Objective** — Mongols pursued the annihilation of the Jin state, not merely Caizhou's walls.
  Ai-Tsung confused personal survival with strategic position, choosing a city that extended life
  "a few months" only.

- **Offensive** — Tachar maintained continuous offensive pressure through columns, ambush, flood,
  and alliance — never allowing the Jin to stabilise. Jin offensives (Ch'ang Sun's fishing army,
  Chang-tu Tien victory over Song) were peripheral and could not break encirclement.

- **Mass** — Mongols achieved effective mass through **distributed columns** converging on one
  centre of gravity. Jin mass never formed — relief piecemeal, hoarded troops guarding provinces.

- **Economy of Force** — Song alliance outsourced part of the southern blockade and breach effort.
  Tachar used algincin — economy of force in a small, high-value ambush destroying imperial
  logistics.

- **Maneuver** — Nerge was strategic maneuver: shaping the battlefield by population movement and
  city isolation before assault. Flooding maneuvered Jin defenders off the western approaches.

- **Unity of Command** — Tachar's single-minded patience contrasted with Jin court faction,
  executed advisers, and commanders who individually rationalised non-intervention.

- **Security** — Mongol security through nerge and messenger interception; Jin insecurity through
  desertion, cannibalism, and currency collapse — the population no longer protected by state
  credibility.

- **Surprise** — Song alliance and January flooding may have surprised Jin hope of southern relief.
  Intercepted New Year messages surprised the emperor's last coordinated relief expectation.

- **Simplicity** — Tachar's method: isolate, starve, flood, breach, consolidate, wait, take. Jin
  response mixed magic, house demolition, and aborted alliances — complexity without coherence.

## 8. Modern Doctrine Parallels

Caizhou is May's **ideal type** of Mongol siege warfare and maps cleanly onto modern doctrinal
vocabulary. **Area isolation** — the nerge — anticipates modern **encirclement and interdiction**:
fix the defender, deny reinforcement, compress the problem in space and time. Driving rural
population into the city is a brutal form of **logistics warfare**, making the defender's food
supply the centre of gravity rather than the wall circuit. Modern blockade and sanctions theory
similarly targets systemic sustenance rather than frontal assault.

The **Mongol–Song alliance** illustrates **coalition warfare** and **combined arms at campaign
scale**: one partner floods, another breaches; roles differ but objectives align. For the Jin it is
a case study in **strategic encirclement** when a neighbour chooses the aggressor's side — the
failure mode Nei-tsu A-hu-tai identified and the court ignored.

**Flooding** remains a standard engineer operation; Caizhou shows deliberate **terrain
modification** to enable assault where walls held. Tachar's **palisade** after partial capture
exemplifies **consolidation on objective** — the discipline modern doctrine teaches against
"culminating before the culminating point" through reckless exploitation. His contrast with
commanders who sought glory through hasty assault makes Caizhou a **positive lesson in patience**,
unusual among narratives emphasising Mongol speed.

The **relief coordination failure** (New Year's Day attack) demonstrates **C2 fragility** under
encirclement: intercepted communications plus rational defectors awaiting post-collapse submission
produce **catastrophic single points of failure** at national level. The Jin commanders' calculus
— Mongols punish disloyalty if you desert now, reward patience if you submit after fall — is a
perverse **incentive structure** created by twenty years of Mongol policy.

Finally, Caizhou supports May's broader argument about **Mongol generalship depth**: Sübedei
initiated Jin destruction; Tachar finished it; Ögödei reassigned the senior marshal mid-war without
fear of collapse — analogous to modern **bench depth** and standardised operational doctrine
across theatre commands.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

May's treatment relies on Chinese dynastic histories and secondary synthesis; this analysis page
inherits his **evaluative judgment** that Caizhou is the clearest demonstration of nerge, flooding,
and ruthless patience. The emperor's quoted contempt for the Song — "weak and not martial, just like
women" — is a primary-voice window into **Jin hubris** that made alliance diplomacy toxic.

Prince Yung-An's memorandum is the strategic masterpiece the court rejected; its six reasons read
like a staff estimate: water obstacles, subsistence, Song proximity, seasonal flooding, escape
routes, and comparative defensibility. Historians should treat its inclusion as evidence that **Jin
military literacy existed** but was politically unusable under Ai-Tsung.

Wa Kang's prophecy offers historiographic contrast between **astrological battle rhythm** and
material siege timeline — fall on the tenth, Mongols gone by the thirteenth. Numbers and dates in
May should be cited with awareness that Song–Jin–Mongol chronologies can shift by source.

**Modern scholarship:** May (2016) positions Caizhou within twenty years of Mongol–Jin war and
emphasises systemic over heroic explanation. Force totals are **not firmly recorded** in the
chapter; this page avoids inventing magnitudes. Deeper reconstruction belongs to Jin dynasty
military histories and specialised studies of the 1230s North China campaigns.
