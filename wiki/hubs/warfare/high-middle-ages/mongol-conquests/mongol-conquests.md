# Mongol Conquests — Campaign & Operational Hub

> **Narrative:** [[events/mongol-conquests-13th-century|Mongol Conquests]] · [[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire (actor)]] · [[mongol-army|Mongol Army]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

The operational overview of the Mongol military system and its campaigns, drawn from the
[[cambridge-history-of-war-v2|Cambridge History of War, Vol. 2]] (Hautala, ch. 11; de la Vaissière
on the steppe inheritance, ch. 6). This hub holds the campaign-level analysis; the
[[hubs/warfare/high-middle-ages/mongol-conquests/battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ain Jalut]] page holds
the one battle so far worked to full tactical depth.

## The Mongol military system

**A society organised as an army.** "The entire Mongolian society was considered by Chinggis khan
as a single military organization." All nomadic subjects were divided into **minghan** ("thousands"
— communities liable to field ~1,000 warriors), grouped into **tümens** (nominally 10,000) and
sub-divided into hundreds and tens. The decimal structure was also a **political weapon**:
conquered tribes (Tatars, Keraits, Naimans) were broken up across units and lost their names, so
that "the military system of the Mongol empire suppressed separatist tendencies among nomadic
tribes" — the same logic de la Vaissière identifies in the earlier Turk decimal armies. Command
rewarded talent over birth: "ordinary nomads (such as Sübe'edei) could rise to the highest rank,"
and the most valued quality in a commander was "the ability to take care of the daily needs of his
subordinates."

**Mobility built on remounts.** Each warrior kept "from three to five battle horses," steppe
ponies "inferior… in speed of movement but [superior] in endurance," grazing even in winter. The
result: an army that "could move almost without stopping and cover long distances in a short time"
and could "continuously attack the enemy by changing to fresh horses during the battles." The
binding constraint was **forage** — Mongol horses "lacked food during the Hülegüid invasions of
Syria," forcing small armies and summer withdrawals, the structural reason the Ilkhans never held
the Levant.

**Discipline through the booty decree.** Before the Tatar campaign (1202) Chinggis ruled that
booty be shared only "after the complete defeat of the enemy," so "every Mongol warrior could…
focus on the pursuit of the fleeing enemy, not on the robbery of the abandoned train." Premature
looting — the classic way medieval pursuits broke down — was thus punished, not rewarded.

## Operational art

- **Intelligence first.** "Before the beginning of any campaign, the Mongols collected as much
  information as possible about the military strength of the enemy, its morale, fortresses' status,
  and ways of communication" — using merchants, defectors, dervishes, and recruited guides.
- **Multi-column invasion.** Armies entered enemy territory "divided into several columns," so "the
  enemy lost the opportunity to coordinate the actions of its units, since the field commanders of
  each of them thought that they were the main target." In the Khwarazmian campaign (1219–20) the
  Syr Darya garrisons "could not help Bukhara and Samarqand… because they, in turn, were attacked by
  separate units." After taking Vladimir (1238) the army split three ways and "captured fourteen
  Russian towns in one month."
- **Deception and terror.** Dummies on spare horses, women dressed as warriors, civilians driven
  forward under false banners; and the deliberate massacre of towns that resisted (Ürgench 1221,
  Kozelsk 1238) advertised to make the next town surrender.

## Tactics

The signature field method was the **crescent**: flanks pushed forward, centre holding, with
"alternating chains of light archers" sweeping the enemy flank and "giving way to a new chain" —
a deadly close-range "shower of arrows" that broke the enemy line and opened it to encirclement
"from three sides." When that failed to draw blood, the **feigned retreat** lured the enemy onto a
hidden ambush (the Rus' "pursued the Mongols for eight days" before the Kalka, 1223). The chain
manoeuvre demanded "high proficiency" — Hautala identifies it as the army's most exacting training
requirement. Mongols "avoided the use of infantry because of the slowness of its movement," but
**dismounted to shoot** when repelling cavalry (decisive at Hims/Wadi al-Khaznadar, 1299).

## Siege and the limits of innovation

"In the siege art… the Mongols, who brought with them to the west detachments of engineers from
north China, had no equal." Counterweight trebuchets "could hurl projectiles weighing 100–150 kg
for 150 m for several days," supplemented by naphtha incendiaries, mining, and prisoners forced to
fill ditches and climb first. Yet the western Mongols, "extremely susceptible to borrowing new
military technologies during [the] expansion, were equally conservative… thereafter," repeatedly
declining to adopt the field guns they faced (Vorskla 1399, Delhi 1397).
