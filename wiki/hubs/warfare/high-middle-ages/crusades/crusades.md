# The Crusades — Campaign & Operational Hub

> **Narrative:** [[events/first-crusade-1095|First Crusade]] · [[crusades|Crusades (process)]] · **Warfare hub:** [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy]]

The operational overview of crusading warfare, drawn from the
[[cambridge-history-of-war-v2|Cambridge History of War, Vol. 2]] (John France, ch. 10). France —
an operational historian of the First Crusade — frames the wars as a problem in **logistics and
siege** more than in pitched battle, fought by a Western army of slow but "ferociously effective"
cavalry and predominant infantry against Turkish horse-archers in a Near East that was, decisively,
politically **divided**.

## Why the crusaders won (when they did)

France is blunt: "in every engagement the crusaders were outnumbered, often heavily, so their
victories need explanation." Two structural answers:

1. **Enemy disunity.** "It is often, and quite rightly, said that they would never have been
   victorious if their enemies had not been divided." The Seljuq succession crisis after Malik
   Shah's death (1092) split Syria between **Ridwan of Aleppo** and **Duqaq of Damascus**, with
   ambitious governors (Yaghi Siyan at Antioch) pursuing their own ends. The Latins exploited the
   gaps; Alexios I of Byzantium had launched them into precisely that opportunity.
2. **A coherent, increasingly disciplined army.** The First Crusade's leaders "proved to be able
   military leaders," and "the army… became more experienced and disciplined as time went on, and
   able… to assume a complex formation." They "took logistics seriously," detouring through
   well-watered Pisidia and Armenian Cappadocia for supply.

## The logistics problem

Distance and supply dominated. "It is about 4,000 km from Paris to Jerusalem, and most of the
participants on the First Crusade walked virtually all the way." Horses wasted: by October 1097
a participant "was bewailing the lack of horses," and the army reached Jerusalem with "some 1,500
cavalry." Heat killed — "about 500 perished of thirst in a single day" crossing Anatolia. Above
all, **sieges starved the besiegers**: "siege posed the greatest challenge to the army because it
was forced to stay in one place, exhausting local supplies." At Antioch by Christmas 1097 "the army
was starving"; at Ma'arra (January 1099) "famine in the army was so acute that some crusaders
resorted to cannibalism." The decisive enabler was **sea power** — English and Genoese fleets
bringing food from Byzantine Cyprus to Antioch, and the timber and skilled labour for the
siege-towers at Jerusalem.

## Siege provoked battle

France's structural point: "siege provoked battle." The crusaders, forced to sit before a city,
drew relief armies onto themselves — and won the resulting open-field fights against the odds. The
First Crusade's major engagements: **Dorylaeum** (1 July 1097), the **Foraging Battle** near Albara
(31 December 1097), the **Lake Battle** (9 February 1098), the great sortie against **Kerbogha** at
Antioch (28 June 1098), and **Ascalon** (12 August 1099). At Jerusalem (June–July 1099) they shifted
to "very different tactics": a failed escalade on 13 June, then a "systematic siege" with mobile
towers, a ram, and catapults built from a Genoese fleet that put into Jaffa on 17 June, carried by
an all-out assault on 15 July "after very heavy losses."

## The Turkish opponent

"The Turks were a steppe people and their military tactics depended on disciplined horse-archers
whose strings of horses enabled them to sustain strategic movement across long distances or fast
tactical movement." Their compound bows were "powerful, yet short enough to use on horseback" — a
sharp contrast to the infantry-heavy West. The "paradigmatic battle was… the first collision in
the open field at Dorylaeum," where a crusader column on the march was ambushed and had to survive
the arrow-storm until its rearguard came up. Later, Saladin's elite **tawashi** cavalry (numbered
at 8,000 by William of Tyre) posed the mature Ayyubid challenge that **Richard I** defeated by
disciplined march-and-defence at **Arsuf** (7 September 1191).

## Warfare Characteristics, Command, and Adaptations (France 1999, Ch. 15)

Additional analysis from France (1999), Chapter 15 ("Crusading and warfare in the Middle East"), grounded exclusively in the exclusive cache range for this subagent. Focus on structural contrasts with western European proprietorial warfare, the impact of distance/sea power, persistent command problems, tactical adaptation vs conservatism, and the detailed anatomy of Hattin.

### Sea Power and Logistics

"Crusades were dependent from the first on sea-power." Urban II appealed personally to the Genoese. Fleets (Italian, English, Byzantine) were "indispensable to the success of the crusade" and thereafter "vital for communications with the West." After the Third Crusade, "the only route to the Holy Land was by sea, and even horses needed special transports."

The Italian city-states acquired "special privileges" (own quarters, own laws) in return; when monarchy weakened they plunged the kingdom into their quarrels (War of St Sabbas most disastrous). Muslims rarely challenged this supremacy: "The Islamic world lacked wood to sustain shipping. Only Egypt had a fleet, but its ships were smaller and they suffered from problems of water supply. This was one area where the crusaders enjoyed a clear technical superiority."

Sailors and fleets were decisive in sieges of the littoral cities "without which the crusader bridgeheads established in 1099 would never have been viable." Twice-yearly pilgrim fleets were "the lifeline of the kingdom," bringing trade wealth and emergency manpower.

Absence of fleet delivered Fourth Crusade leaders to Venice. Later major efforts (St Louis) absorbed local capacity.

### Command and Cohesion Problems

Crusading armies exemplified in acute form the general problems of medieval command. "The First Crusade was led by a committee of princes, and it was only in extreme circumstances that the leaders agreed to the appointment of a single commander — and then only for a limited period."

Examples of failure to cooperate: 1101 contingents defeated separately; Louis VII and Conrad III on Second; Richard and Philip on Third; Fourth under incident/circumstance; Fifth under Pelagius (poor decisions); Theobald/Richard of Cornwall "a burlesque of the crusades."

Even when successful, "uncertain and often divided leadership was inevitably accompanied by uncertain purpose." The First succeeded partly because of the "limited but specific objective — Jerusalem." Later efforts lacked this clarity.

Crusades raised "diverse and loosely organized armies" with high turnover, desertion, and international tensions. "On the First Crusade there was considerable tension between the Provençals and others. The French and the Germans quarrelled bitterly on the Second Crusade, while there was an enormous amount of bickering on the Third between nationalities."

Yet exceptions existed: the First Crusade army "became hardened by three years of fighting together, and by its later stages the army performed with exceptional skill and coherence." Richard's army was fine but faced tensions with other contingents. State machinery (Angevin under Richard; later Capetian under St Louis) helped organization and finance (Saladin Tithe).

### Tactical Contrasts: West and East

"Warfare in the Middle East was heavily conditioned by geographical and climatic factors. In general, the countryside was much emptier than in the West, and there were large tracts of desert and semi-desert. This placed a heavy emphasis on mounted warfare... Water was vital in an arid land and any army without it would perish."

Islamic armies "were largely ad hoc bodies" with small standing nuclei (Mamluks/slave soldiers core for Seljuks; Diwan administration). "Horsemen of all kinds were predominant and infantry played a limited role." There was "a tendency for Islamic armies to use more and more heavy cavalry" but the "really obvious difference was that Islamic armies used clouds of light cavalry and horse-archers."

Tactics differed sharply. "Everywhere, close-quarter confrontation was decisive, and the Western tradition was to bring that about as quickly as possible. In the East, light cavalry could outflank and unhinge formations by rapid movement... even more significant was the tactical use of large bodies of horse-archers who fired from the saddle. Their bows had a range of 50–80m, which enabled them to kill horses and men at a distance."

Western response: "The natural response to this kind of attack was an intensification of the Western tradition of close order. Knights in tight formation could protect one another from bombardment and threaten a charge, which could scatter the lightly armed archers..." The First Crusade used this at Dorylaeum. On later crusades, veterans of the East advised but were not always heeded (e.g., protests ignored at Gaza 1239; Robert of Artois at Mansourah).

### Frankish Adaptations and the Militarized Society

The Latin states suffered "a shortage of manpower." At Hattin the army had ~1,200 knights (Orders ~600); total upper class perhaps ~3,000 in the kingdom. Infantry could reach 15,000+ on full muster. "It points to the military nature of the Frankish settlement, where every man must have been a soldier." Total Frankish population surmised at 120,000–140,000 ruling ~500,000 natives.

Castles guarded proprietorial rights and were important, but "it was chiefly underpinned by the cities and the existence of a powerful field-army." In the thirteenth century cities remained essential.

Frankish tactics emphasized "mass and close order, with cooperation between infantry and cavalry." The march from Acre to Arsuf (1191) under Richard I exemplified: "cavalry in three divisions and threw around them a cordon of footsoldiers and crossbowmen, who held off the enemy. As infantry tired, so they retreated to the seaward side of the march, where the fleet shadowed their progress." "Conventional Frankish tactics... were brought to new heights in the Holy Land. This was possible because this was a heavily militarized society, whose members must have served together time after time."

Turcopoles (light cavalry, often mounted archers, native or Frankish) were "a useful adjunct" for raiding/harassing but "not numerous enough in pitched battle to face the light cavalry... and so were used simply as a supplement to the heavy cavalry."

Contemporaries noted Frankish cavalry power: "famous onset," seeking "a clear field for their own charge." Horses admired even in death.

### Hattin as Case Study (3–4 July 1187)

Detailed reconstruction: Guy mustered ~1,200 knights +15,000–18,000 foot/Turcopoles. Saladin ~12,000 first-rate cavalry + support (~30,000 total). "The crude overall figures indicate a huge numerical advantage to Saladin, but in reality it was far greater..."

Army gathered at Saffuriyah (good water). Saladin took Tiberias 2 July. Council debated. Fabian tactics (shadowing to let army disintegrate) had worked in 1182–83 but eroded reputation and Guy faced criticism. Guy left on 3 July intending battle (or fighting march toward water at Turan?); army in three blocks with infantry around cavalry.

"the crusader army was by then surrounded, with the main enemy attacks coming in from the rear... As they struggled the 4–5km up the slope to the vicinity of Maskana, it became clear that Saladin had enough men to harass them to the point at which they could not deploy for battle, while keeping his main force to the south, comfortably out of range of a sudden charge."

Night at Maskana (dry, surrounded). On 4 July Muslims held off until heat/thirst told. "The Muslims lit fires... the Templars tried to break out, Raymond of Tripoli broke through towards Tiberias, the demoralized infantry deserted and fled to the old volcano of the Horns of Hattin, Guy ordered tents to be pitched to create a fighting camp..." "the whole disaster seems to have ended with desperate cavalry charges from the Horns of Hattin."

Guy "disciplined and controlled his army well, or it would not have remained intact for so long." But ignored two canons: "He seems not to have made provision for water, nor to have recognized the strength of Saladin’s army." Saladin "harassed the crusaders throughout 3 July so that they could not deploy, and then holding off on the morning of 4 July."

Aftermath exposed the "fundamental weakness": "there was nobody left to defend Jerusalem and the cities, and they fell quickly to Saladin." "It took three great battles in 1066 to reduce the thegnly class of England to anything like this situation."

### Rules of Engagement and the Character of the Struggle

Although religious war, "to a degree at least some of the conventions of war were observed by both sides." Sieges followed "he who holds out dies" but many surrenders on terms (Arsuf 1101, Tyre 1124, etc.); exceptions when stormed early (Ma'arra, Jerusalem 1099, later Mamluk deceptions).

Capture in battle: ransom culture strong for "gentry." "Broadly, therefore, the kinds of rules that we have noted in the West applied here too, notwithstanding the rival clash of Crusade and jihad." Saladin spared knightly prisoners after Hattin (except Raynald); had to pay captors to execute Orders men. "The financial gain from ransom was in general sufficient to preserve the life of a captive of rank."

"Crusading was a pretty horrible experience. Distance, hunger, disease and brutal fighting made it a terrible undertaking. To live in the Holy Land was to be constantly at war."
