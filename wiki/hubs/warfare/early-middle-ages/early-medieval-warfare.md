# Early Medieval Warfare in the Barbarian West (c. 450–900)

> **Hub thematic overview** — not a single battle. This page is the operational and
> organisational synthesis of how war was raised, supplied, fought, and ended in western
> Europe between the fall of the Western [[late-antiquity|Roman Empire]] and the break-up
> of the [[carolingian-empire|Carolingian Empire]]. It is built almost entirely on
> [[sources/halsall-warfare-society-barbarian-west-2003|Guy Halsall's *Warfare and Society
> in the Barbarian West, 450–900*]] (Routledge, 2003), the standard scholarly synthesis.

## Why this period needs its own treatment

Early medieval warfare has been doubly misread: as a degraded shadow of Rome, and as a mere
prelude to the knights-and-castles warfare of the central Middle Ages. The classic art-of-war
histories (Oman, Delbrück, Verbruggen, Contamine, Beeler) either skipped it or treated the
five centuries as one static "Dark Age." Halsall's case is that the period **had a character
of its own** and that its warfare **changed dynamically** — with major transformations
**around 600** and again **in the ninth century** — and that it can only be reconstructed by
combining sparse, genre-bound texts with archaeology and art, read "substantively" (within the
mentalities of the society waging it) rather than by the "normalist" assumption of timeless
military principles. See [[grammar-of-warfare]] for the methodological frame; this page covers
the conduct of war.

The evidence problem is foundational. Early medieval writers, unlike classical ones, **did not
describe warfare in detail**: authority rested on moral and exegetical learning, not empirical
report, and battles were "supposed to be won by divine favour, not human agency." The eyewitness
participant Nithard gives barely a paragraph to the great battle of [[battle-of-fontenoy-841|Fontenoy
(841)]]. Grave-goods — the richest archaeological source — are a **ritual** record of age,
ethnicity and rank (following Heinrich Härke), not a transcript of real battlefield kit, and the
furnished-burial rite itself fades after c. 625.

## Raising the army — the hinge between war and the state

Halsall makes army-raising "one of the most important and dynamic points of contact between
local societies and royal authority." The master-process is the slow shift from the **tax-funded
standing army** of the late Roman state to the **obligation-based levy** of lords and their
landed retainers — a change with deep political consequences:

- A state that extracts **tax** can pay a standing army and dominate its aristocracy (late Rome).
- A state that raises troops by **obligation** from landholders and their followings depends on
  aristocratic **consent**, and is correspondingly weaker — defensive or internal campaigns are
  hard to mobilise for, foreign plunder-wars easy.

| Phase | How armies were raised | Political effect |
|---|---|---|
| Late/post-Roman (5th–6th c.) | Decaying Roman field army; barbarian generals become kings; soldiers paid by estate/tax "thirds" (*tertiae* — debated as land vs. tax revenue, the Goffart/*hospitalitas* controversy); royal/aristocratic bodyguards (*bucellarii*) as the core; the [[merovingian-army|*civitas*/*pagus* levy]] under royal officers | "Ethnicity became functional: barbarians fought, Romans paid taxes" — see [[frankish-ethnogenesis]] |
| 7th-century selectivity | The mass *civitas* levy fades (last clear Frankish reference 639); *scarae* (select warbands) appear; service "moderated by wealth, in turn manifested by landholding"; Visigothic call-ups run down chains of lordship and used **armed slaves**; Anglo-Saxon graded *fyrdwite* fines (Ine of Wessex) | Service narrows to a landholding warrior class |
| Carolingian (8th–9th c.) | Property qualifications in Charlemagne's capitularies (806–8): service from holders of "3, 4 or 5 *mansi* or more," poorer freemen clubbing together (*adiutorium*) — revised to a 4-*mansi* threshold (808); graded equipment (12-*mansi* holders owe a mail coat); three months' provisions; heavy penalties (*haribannus* fine; *herisliz* = desertion, death) | A smaller, better-equipped, partly mounted host; the burden of annual service becomes ruinous and evasion endemic |
| Late 9th c. | Charles the Bald's **Edict of Pîtres (864)**: everyone with a horse to serve, the *adiutorium* revived, attempts to bypass lordship and re-link king to lesser freemen; the English *trinoda necessitas* (army-, bridge-, fortress-work) | State strains to keep direct claims on manpower against aristocratic mediation |

Halsall **demolishes the old "birth of feudalism" story** (Brunner/Ganshof: [[charles-martel|Charles
Martel]]'s church-land seizures + *precaria* + vassalage + the stirrup producing land-for-service
cavalry). The real change came around 700 from *precaria*/*beneficia* and the rise of aristocratic
followings, not a legal land-for-service system. As Nicholas Brooks put it (approvingly quoted):
"Land … without being the source of military obligation, was the expected reward for loyal
military service."

**Allies, mercenaries, training.** Cash mercenaries were **rare before the ninth century** — the
post-Roman west was largely non-monetary and under-urbanised, with no pool of "footloose
underemployed young males," and a rewarded warrior entered an enduring social bond, not a
terminable contract. Earlier "mercenaries" were really exiles-with-retinues; true paid mercenary
use surged with the **Vikings** (hired by the king of Cornwall against Wessex in 838; paid in coin
by Charles the Bald, 860). Allied/tributary contingents were commoner, usually made to attack
**separately** to split the enemy. Training left little record: the **aristocratic household** and
apprenticeship-in-arms, and above all **hunting** (handling weapons coolly amid danger), were the
school of war — the dangerous small-unit war-games Nithard describes at Worms (842) are the key
text.

**The size of armies.** Halsall sides with the **low-thousands consensus** against the inflated
figures of the sources (which are biblical/numerological — Clovis's 3,000 echo Acts — or pure
genre). Source numbers are worthless ("no one ever counted an army"; the same 589 force is 5,000
dead in Gregory of Tours and 60,000 in Isidore). The reliable method is **socio-economic context
and logistics**: an army of 5,000 could be a hundred times the population of a settlement it
foraged in; Rigunth's column managed five miles a day "like a plague of locusts." Realistic
ceilings: generally small (a couple of thousand in Britain/Germany/Scandinavia/Ireland), **5,000–6,000
the early upper end**, and only later, with economic recovery, **occasional field armies of ~10,000**
(Reuter's image: an effect "analogous to the down-wind ellipse of fall-out from a nuclear weapon").
Calling out *every* man was undesirable — untrained farmer-soldiers "did more harm than good"
(the Metz annalist contrasts a "huge army … mixed with the common people" with Charles Martel's
"lesser host … men very well tested for battle").

## Campaigning, logistics, and strategy

"There was nothing random, or even particularly disorganised, about early medieval campaigning."
The army mustered in spring (the Frankish **Marchfield**, 1 March, later moved to 1 May) and
functioned as a **political assembly** as much as a fighting force — much was "fictive campaigning"
where the king redistributed patronage, gauged support, and passed law (the mass stay-away from
Louis the Pious's 830 Breton expedition "showed the writing on the wall").

- **Ravaging and harrying were the central strategic method** — but as a deliberate **challenge to
  battle**, attacking the enemy king's claim to be a good lord and protector, not a safe substitute
  for battle. Aridius's speech to Clovis (destroying fields, vineyards, olive-groves to extract
  tribute) is the model.
- **Objectives.** [[merovingian-army|Merovingian]] inter-kingdom war centred on seizing *civitas*
  capitals (administrative, not economic, prizes) by intimidation rather than crude siege.
- **Real techniques** (against the "normalist" caricature of mindless mobs): summer campaigning;
  **multi-pronged attacks** to split the enemy (three armies into Bavaria 787, against the Avars
  791/796; four into Saxony 774); stockpiled logistics (Charlemagne's *De Villis* and the Aachen
  capitulary — carts of wine and flour, three months' supplies, two-thirds of grass reserved as
  fodder); large baggage trains that themselves **capped army size**.
- **Attrition** did much of the killing without battle: dysentery wrecked Frankish invasions of
  Italy (539, 590) and killed [[charles-martel|Charles Martel]], Pippin I and Charles the Bald;
  horse murrain (910 Bohemia campaign lost 9 of every 10 horses); desertion punishable by death.
- **Vikings** waged strategically adept "land-based political piracy": the [[great-heathen-army-865|Great
  Army]] struck after harvest at the onset of winter, aimed at fortified political and ecclesiastical
  centres, timed assaults to Christian festivals, and exploited the winter dispersal of English
  forces. See [[viking-age]].

Halsall argues that, contrary to the "normalist" assumption that commanders always avoided the
gamble of battle, **battle was comparatively frequent** in this period — because martial valour
underpinned kingship and noble/ethnic identity, because beating a field army opened the country to
safe ravaging and rich loot (kings carried treasuries on campaign — Charles the Bald lost crowns
at Jengland and Andernach), and because battle could be read as **God's judgement**.

## Weaponry, equipment, and the cavalry question

Technology stayed "fairly constant … but this should not lull us into believing that the nature of
battle remained similarly constant."

| Weapon | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Spear** | The most common weapon, carried by nearly all warriors mounted or foot; the symbolic "spear side" of a family. Hugely varied (javelins, two-handed slashing spears, throwing types). |
| **Sword** | The most treasured item — a long two-edged cutting *spatha* needing room to swing ("may have been developed primarily for mounted use"); **pattern-welded**, expensive, a status marker. |
| **Ango / francisca / seax** | The *ango* (a *pilum*-derived barbed throwing spear) and the throwing **francisca** axe are élite 5th–6th-c. north-Gaulish weapons that **drop out in the 7th c.**; the single-edged **seax** was the poor man's sidearm, lengthening into a one-edged sword by c. 700. ("No double-headed axe has ever been found from early medieval Europe.") |
| **Bow** | Mandated by Visigothic, Lombard and Carolingian law; significant Ostrogothic archery. Halsall rejects a rigid cavalry-vs.-archer division — the trained warrior fought mounted or dismounted, near or far. |
| **Shield** | Round/oval board, central iron boss, single fist-grip; 6th-c. small shields with "golf-tee" blade-catching bosses imply **looser** early fighting; bosses lengthen after 600. Painted, expendable ("shield-sellers in Charles the Bald's baggage train"). |
| **Armour** | Mail byrnie and helmet, "common though far from universal," but a **status display**: of ~three dozen surviving helmets almost all are "top of the range" decorated pieces (Spangenhelme; ridge-helmets — Sutton Hoo, Benty Grange, Coppergate; the Avar-style Niederstotzingen). |

Halsall stresses that ornament was not vanity: the **moral/psychological effect** of fine war-gear
on the enemy was "as much" the point as physical protection.

**The stirrup thesis, rejected.** The Brunner / Lynn White Jr. claim — that the stirrup created
battle-winning shock cavalry, that [[saxon-wars-772-804|Frankish]] heavy cavalry won the day, and
that this produced feudalism, with Poitiers/Tours (732) as the hinge — is dismissed. Stirrups
(Avar-introduced, late 6th c.) "did not catch on everywhere," and experiment shows they "do not
materially affect the ability to perform" a charge; the real enabler of the couched-lance charge
was the **high-cantled saddle**, which post-dates this period. "It is difficult to see the evidence
for any dramatic upsurge, or change, in the use of cavalry between 450 and 900." Halsall likewise
demolishes the "Anglo-Saxons fought only on foot" construct: the dismounted cases (Hastings,
Maldon, Ashingdon) are late and special, while the English plainly fought mounted "when the
occasion demanded."

## The face of battle

Halsall reconstructs combat with a [[face-of-battle-method|Keegan-style]] ([[sources/keegan-face-of-battle-1976|Keegan, 1976]]) method, anchored on the
two least-bad texts — **Nithard on [[battle-of-fontenoy-841|Fontenoy (841)]]** and **Asser on
Ashdown (871)** — while warning that classical-modelled accounts (Asser's *testudo* and *turmae*)
carry no technical weight.

- **Pre-battle was dominated by morale and the sacred**: fasting, Mass, ordeals, relics and
  standards with numinous power; and by **single combat / champions** (Totila's armed "dance of
  death" before Busta Gallorum, 552). Battle itself could be framed as a judicial ordeal placed in
  God's hands.
- **Formations were dense masses** — "shield-walls" (*cuneus* meaning merely "wedged together,"
  not a true wedge) — advancing **slowly and steadily**: "the full cavalry charge appears to have
  been developed after the close of our period," and the rash gallop or "flight to the front"
  courted disaster.
- **Phases**: a missile exchange (heavier missiles before 600, to break shields and formation),
  the slow grind to contact with shouts and horns, then close hand-to-hand fighting beyond any
  leader's control. Combat came in **short, exhausting bouts separated by rest**, explaining
  recorded multi-day battles.
- **The rout, not the clash, did the killing.** "The most numerous casualties were those caused
  once one side was beaten and broke in flight" — men dropping shields, cut down from behind,
  trapped in the baggage. The palaeopathology of the **Eccles (Kent)** cemetery is the proof:
  some skeletons killed by a single frontal head-blow (close combat), others (Eccles II:
  defensive arm-wounds, eight post-fall cuts) showing the multiple hacking of pursuit.
- **Leaders died disproportionately** (12 of 22 seventh-century English battles killed a royal
  figure), yet **battle need not be decisive**: Fontenoy and Ashdown were strategically
  inconclusive but "written up as triumphs." The Vikings were especially hard to beat.

## Fortification and siege warfare

A seeming paradox — endemic warfare, yet "the fortification of settlements was relatively rare" —
which Halsall calls "more apparent than real," because harrying-to-provoke-battle, not siege, was
the strategic core.

- Distinguish **defended** from merely **defensible** sites: walls and ditches often served social
  control, legal/property demarcation (the *curtis*), or status display. Roman town walls decayed;
  Carolingians let many decline (Mainz demolished a section for river trade); settlement "drifted
  away from the often derelict walled zones" toward extramural shrines — "people do not appear to
  have wanted to live within walled zones."
- Other works: reused Iron Age **hillforts** in western Britain (South Cadbury), Frankish
  aristocratic *castra* in troubled times, frontier strongpoints under Charlemagne (Hamburg,
  Frankfurt), Italian hilltop sites (*incastellamento*), Irish raths and crannogs.
- **The Viking transformation** drove "a new concern with fortification": Charles the Bald's
  fortified bridges of the 860s (Pont-de-l'Arche) to block fleets, and — most successfully —
  Alfred's **burhs** (880s), a network sited so all Wessex lay within a day's walk, with permanent
  garrisons, against which the Great Army of the 890s "was able to make no significant inroad."
- **Siegecraft was basic**: harry into surrender, blockade/starve (risky — disease struck
  besiegers), or storm "with a minimum of finesse." Towns generally fell by **treachery or
  collapsed walls, not assault** — "there are no instances in Merovingian Gaul where a walled town
  was captured by straightforward assault." Artillery existed (Abbo's stone-throwers at Paris 886)
  but was rarely decisive.

## Making peace

Ending a war rarely meant a formal peace: usually **oaths, hostages, tribute, or de facto
conquest**, with endemic raiding soon resuming. Oaths required shared sacral values — which is why
pagan **Vikings** (not bound by imposed Christian objects) were branded oath-breakers, and why
kings worked to bring pagans into the Church. Formal treaties ([[treaty-of-verdun-843|Verdun 843]],
Alfred–Guthrum) were exceptional and often read like law-codes, with bishops as go-betweens.

## The two transformations — Halsall's conclusion

Early medieval warfare was **distinctive**, neither a decayed echo of Rome nor a simple prelude to
the central Middle Ages. Its hallmark was **harrying to provoke battle**, which made the field
encounter central and the siege marginal — the **reverse** of the siege-and-ransom warfare that
social and economic change would produce by the twelfth century. Halsall identifies two turning
points:

1. **Around 600** — an under-appreciated shift from the open, fluid, missile-heavy fighting
   inherited from the late Roman army (small blade-catching shields, the *ango* and *francisca*)
   to the **close-fighting infantry shield-wall** (larger shields, longer spearheads and seaxes),
   for reasons (growing army size? recruitment down vertical lordship chains?) Halsall cannot
   firmly pin down.
2. **The ninth century / Viking era** — distinctive Viking armies, the new emphasis on
   fortification, and the entrenchment of dismounted combat — though the full move toward castles
   and mounted shock warriors "do not seem to have been fully carried through until the tenth
   century."

Throughout, war was inseparable from society, politics and economy: the way an army was raised was
the most revealing measure of how power actually worked in the post-Roman West.
