## Warfare: The Long Arc of Transformation

Each era is defined less by *who* fought than by *what made armies and navies effective* — and every such system eventually generated a counter-system.

### Bronze & Early Iron Age — Chariots to Iron Infantry
Palace armies relied on massed chariots ([[battle-of-qadesh-1274bce|Qadesh]]). The Bronze Age Collapse swept this away; cheaper iron and broader social participation opened war to citizen-soldiers and larger forces.

### Archaic & Classical — The Hoplite Revolution and Its Limits
The Greek **[[hoplite-warfare|hoplite phalanx]]** fused military power with the polis. Tested in the [[greco-persian-wars-499bce|Greco-Persian Wars]] and [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]]. Superseded by Macedonian combined-arms ([[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II]], [[battle-of-gaugamela-331bce|Gaugamela]]).

### Roman — Discipline, Engineering, and Logistics
The manipular/cohort **legion** turned drill and field engineering into a decisive advantage. Rome's true edge was the political and fiscal capacity to raise, pay, and replace armies on a scale no peer could match. Decay visible from [[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople]] onward.

### Medieval — The Knight, the Castle, and the Siege
Heavy cavalry, stone fortifications, and the primacy of siege over field battle defined the period ([[military-history-middle-millennium|middle-millennium warfare]]). The [[mongol-army|Mongol army]] offered the devastating counter-example of steppe horse-archer mobility and operational speed, checked only at [[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ain Jalut]]. Infantry revival: longbow, pike, early gunpowder.

### Early Modern — The Military Revolution
Gunpowder, the *trace italienne*, drilled infantry, and vastly larger armies drove the rise of the **[[fiscal-military-state|fiscal-military state]]**. Gunpowder empires (Ottoman janissaries, Mughal, Qing) and European states reorganized to finance permanent forces. Globalized by the [[seven-years-war-1756|Seven Years' War]] and beyond.

### Industrial & Total War
The [[industrialization-of-warfare|industrialization of warfare]], the *levée en masse*, and total mobilization of economies and societies produced the world wars and nuclear standoff. Post-1945 warfare often displaced into proxy, limited, or asymmetric forms.

## Recurring Problems (Read Laterally)

These problems reset with each technological or social change:

- **Infantry vs. cavalry vs. missile / combined arms**: The eternal rock-paper-scissors (phalanx → legion → knight → horse-archer → pike-and-shot → mechanized forces).
- **Fortification and the siege**: From city walls to the *trace italienne* and modern bunkers. Siege usually decided wars until air power and maneuver changed the equation.
- **Sea power and power projection**: [[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]], [[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]], the age of sail, and carrier/strike warfare. Maritime supremacy enabled (or required) global empires.
- **Paying for war**: The deepest driver. Ties directly to [[hubs/themes/state-formation|state formation]], taxation, credit, and bureaucracy ([[fiscal-military-state]]).
- **Steppe vs. sown / nomadic vs. sedentary**: The longest-running asymmetry in Eurasian history. Nomadic mobility repeatedly overturned agrarian empires until gunpowder and settled logistics closed the gap.
- **Logistics, supply, and endurance**: Armies and fleets are limited by what they can feed, move, and resupply. Often more decisive than tactics.

## Strategy: Ends, Means, and Traditions

Strategy is the bridge between political purpose and the use (or threat) of force.

See the dedicated sub-hub:

**[[hubs/warfare/strategy|Strategy]]**

It organizes coverage of strategic practice, recurring features, major traditions, and a curated list of illustrative events/campaigns (with direct links to the event pages where they exist). The flagship resource remains:

**[[strategic-practice-across-civilizations|Strategic Practice across Civilizations]]** — the major cross-civilizational comparison (universal features vs. culture-specific ways of war). Key verdict: actual practice was usually "messy, frustrating, and often guided by luck" rather than the clean rational model of modern theory.

### Recurring Strategic Features (from the comparison)
- Aspiration to world dominion / universal monarchy (Assyria, Persia, Rome, Caliphate, Mongols, China, Mughals...).
- Peacetime deterrence by display and "awe".
- Alliance via dynastic marriage.
- The wider toolkit beyond battle: tribute, subsidies, trade denial, narrative control.

### Culture-Specific and Era-Specific Traditions
- Classical combined-arms (Macedonian phalanx + cavalry).
- Roman operational art and engineering.
- Steppe operational mobility and decimal organization ([[mongol-army]]).
- Medieval chivalric and siege-focused approaches.
- Early-modern "military revolution" state-building.
- Modern total war and nuclear deterrence.

## Domains of Warfare

Warfare is not one thing. Different environments and technologies produce distinct domains:

- **Land warfare** (infantry, cavalry, combined arms) — see [[hubs/warfare/tactics-and-battle-strategies|Tactics and Battle Strategies]] for detailed examples.
- **Naval and maritime power** (sea control, trade protection, power projection)
- **Siege and fortification warfare** (trace italienne, modern engineering)
- **Steppe and nomadic warfare** (mobility, horse-archers, operational tempo — see [[mongol-army]])
- **Asymmetric and irregular warfare** (guerrilla, insurgency, raiding)
- **Total and industrialized warfare** (mass mobilization, economies of war, air power, nuclear)

Each domain has its own logic, constraints, and historical arc.
