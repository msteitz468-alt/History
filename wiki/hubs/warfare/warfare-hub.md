# Warfare & Strategy Hub

> Warfare is one of the densest themes in the wiki, with hundreds of battle, war, siege, and conquest events plus the processes, institutions, and ideas that made organized violence possible. This hub treats **warfare** (the conduct, technology, and organization of armed conflict) and **strategy** (the linking of political ends to military and non-military means) as an integrated domain of historical analysis. See the dedicated sub-hub [[hubs/warfare/strategy|Strategy]] for the paradoxical logic of strategy, levels framework, armed suasion, and comparative practice (Luttwak, Cambridge History of Strategy, Millett/Murray military effectiveness framework, etc.).

## Detailed Hierarchical Structure

This section is being built as a comprehensive, professional-grade military history resource with the following organization:

**`hubs/warfare/` → `(Time Period e.g. classical-antiquity)` → `(Specific War e.g. second-punic-war)` → `(Specific Battle)`**

Each battle analysis page delivers **West Point / staff-college level examination** (and significantly more detailed than standard wiki event pages):
- Strategic objectives for both sides (primary + secondary)
- Order of battle and deployment (with formations)
- Terrain, weather, and intelligence factors
- Detailed phases of the engagement
- Critical tactical and operational decisions
- Outcome analysis with casualties and immediate follow-on
- In-depth lessons learned (tied to principles of war, with examples)
- Deeper historiography, primary source excerpts, and modern parallels

Use the enhanced template at `hubs/warfare/templates/battle-analysis-template.md` (aim for 1500–4000+ words per analysis, with tables where data allows).

The analytical charter for the **human/experiential layer** of these pages is [[face-of-battle-method|the face of battle]] ([[keegan-face-of-battle-1976|Keegan, 1976]]): what combat did to the body, and why men stood or ran — recovered by attending to all participants and reconstructing from physical evidence (Delbrück / "Inherent Military Probability"), not by repeating the conventional "battle piece."

Narrative/historiography lives in the main `events/` page; the `hubs/warfare/` page provides the dedicated analytical deep dive.

## Warfare: The Long Arc of Transformation

Each era is defined less by *who* fought than by *what made armies and navies effective* — and every such system eventually generated a counter-system.

### Bronze & Early Iron Age — Chariots to Iron Infantry
Palace armies relied on massed chariots ([[battle-of-qadesh-1274bce|Qadesh]]). The Bronze Age Collapse swept this away; cheaper iron and broader social participation opened war to citizen-soldiers and larger forces.

### Archaic & Classical — The Hoplite Revolution and Its Limits
The Greek **[[hoplite-warfare|hoplite phalanx]]** fused military power with the polis. Tested in the [[greco-persian-wars-499bce|Greco-Persian Wars]] and [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]]. Superseded by Macedonian combined-arms ([[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II]], [[battle-of-gaugamela-331bce|Gaugamela]]).

### Roman — Discipline, Engineering, and Logistics
The cohort-based **legion** turned drill, visual theater, and field engineering into a decisive operational advantage. In addition to Rome's strategic capacity to raise and replace armies at scale, its battlefield performance was driven by [[concepts/roman-legionary-tactics|flexible cohort tactics]], systematic [[concepts/roman-campaign-methods|campaign methods]] (including the marching camp), and a highly developed system of [[concepts/roman-battle-morale|battle psychology and morale]] (analyzed in [[sources/goldsworthy-roman-army-at-war-1996|Goldsworthy, 1996]]). Decay became visible from [[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople]] onward.

### Medieval — The Knight, the Castle, and the Siege
Heavy cavalry, stone fortifications, and the primacy of siege over field battle defined the period ([[military-history-middle-millennium|middle-millennium warfare]]; operational layer in [[medieval-warfare]]). Battle was the exception: the *Vegetian* norm was to control fortresses, devastate the enemy's land (the *chevauchée*), and avoid pitched battle. The [[mongol-army|Mongol army]] offered the devastating counter-example of steppe horse-archer mobility and operational speed, checked only at [[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ain Jalut]] ([[hubs/warfare/high-middle-ages/mongol-conquests/mongol-conquests|Mongol Conquests hub]]). Crusading warfare was a problem of logistics and siege ([[hubs/warfare/high-middle-ages/crusades/crusades|Crusades hub]]; [[hubs/warfare/high-middle-ages/crusades/siege-of-antioch-1097|Antioch]]). The infantry/missile revival — longbow, pike, early gunpowder — broke the knight at Crécy and Agincourt ([[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/hundred-years-war/hundred-years-war|Hundred Years' War hub]]). Delbrück's deep reading ([[delbruck-medieval-warfare-vol-3-1982|Vol. III]]): the knight-as-individual **dissolved** the disciplined tactical body, which was **reborn** in the Swiss pike square ([[hubs/warfare/late-middle-ages/rise-of-the-swiss/rise-of-the-swiss|Rise of the Swiss]]) — the Middle Ages in war "came to a close on the day of Murten" (1476).

### Early Modern — The Military Revolution
Gunpowder, the *trace italienne*, drilled infantry, and vastly larger armies drove the rise of the **[[fiscal-military-state|fiscal-military state]]**. Gunpowder empires (Ottoman janissaries, Mughal, Qing) and European states reorganized to finance permanent forces. Globalized by the [[seven-years-war-1756|Seven Years' War]] and beyond.

### Industrial & Total War
The [[industrialization-of-warfare|industrialization of warfare]], the *levée en masse*, and total mobilization of economies and societies produced the world wars and nuclear standoff. Post-1945 warfare often displaced into proxy, limited, or asymmetric forms.

German tactical adaptation on the [[places/western-front]] (1916–18) offers a key case of doctrinal dynamics in industrial attrition: shift to [[concepts/elastic-defense-in-depth]] (1917) then [[concepts/infiltration-tactics-1918]] (spring 1918). Per [[sources/lupfer-dynamics-of-doctrine-1981]] Ch. 3 (Range 3): "incorporated many of the virtues of their elastic defense-in-depth"; "complementary use of firepower and maneuver"; "product of a thorough collective effort"; disseminated via "remarkable training program." Process inductive/tentative: "Evidence from the battlefield was more respected than the doctrine"; "all tactical solutions were tentative"; "no panacea. A formula is harmful" (Rupprecht). Corporate OHL direction under [[ludendorff]] and [[hindenburg]] used talent (e.g. Geyer) with "remarkable tolerance of dissent" and produced flexible yet unifying principles. See [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/first-world-war]], [[hubs/warfare/tactics-and-battle-strategies]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].

## Recurring Problems (Read Laterally)

These problems reset with each technological or social change:

- **Infantry vs. cavalry vs. missile / combined arms**: The eternal rock-paper-scissors (phalanx → legion → knight → horse-archer → pike-and-shot → mechanized forces).
- **Fortification and the siege**: From city walls to the *trace italienne* and modern bunkers. Siege usually decided wars until air power and maneuver changed the equation.
- **Sea power and power projection**: [[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]], [[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]], the age of sail, and carrier/strike warfare. Maritime supremacy enabled (or required) global empires.
- **Paying for war**: The deepest driver. Ties directly to [[hubs/themes/state-formation|state formation]], taxation, credit, and bureaucracy ([[fiscal-military-state]]).
- **Steppe vs. sown / nomadic vs. sedentary**: The longest-running asymmetry in Eurasian history. Nomadic mobility repeatedly overturned agrarian empires until gunpowder and settled logistics closed the gap.
- **Logistics, supply, and endurance**: Armies and fleets are limited by what they can feed, move, and resupply. Often more decisive than tactics. Van Creveld (*Supplying War*) demonstrates across eras (magazine system through motorized) that detailed pre-planned supply often proves inadequate; "most armies seem to have prepared their campaigns as best they could on an ad hoc basis"; Overlord's elaborate logistic plans were "an exercise in conservatism, even pusillanimity"; Allied 1944 victories "were due as much to their disregard for the preconceived logistic plans as to their implementation." Optimum service-to-combat ratios are campaign-specific (theoretically up to 100:1); calculation is "an almost impossible task." Ultimately, "the willingness ... to override the plans, to improvise and take risks, that determined the outcome." The moral is to the physical "as three to one" (Napoleon); intellect alone is insufficient — hubris to believe otherwise. Links: [[van-creveld-supplying-war-1977]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **Command, control, and information management (C3)**: The challenge of directing forces and managing uncertainty on the battlefield. Van Creveld (*Command in War*) traces how command systems have struggled with the balance between centralization and decentralization, from the standardized drill of the Roman legion to the French autonomous corps system and the Prussian doctrine of decentralized mission command (*Auftragstaktik*), often bypassing formal chains with a *directed telescope*. Links: [[sources/van-creveld-command-in-war-1985]], [[concepts/auftragstaktik]], [[concepts/directed-telescope]].
- **Integrated technological systems vs. flexibility (van Creveld *Technology and War* Range 3)**: In the Age of Systems, armored divisions integrated tanks + mechanized infantry + SP artillery + engineers + signals + logistics into potent but logistically voracious combined-arms systems (300–1500+ tons/day). Two-way radio (Guderian/Fellgiebel) provided "very great tactical and operational flexibility" that "constituted the true core of the new style". Strategic air and battleship fleets became cumbersome integrated systems (coordination with ground facilities, beams, escorts, antiradar) at cost of flexibility; "the price to be paid for “mechanical” efficiency was, as usual, a loss of flexibility." Aircraft, subs, and tactical fighter-bombers retained decisive flexibility precisely because they did not form the core of such large systems. Radar required full "coordinated and integrated" networks to realize potential. Nuclear devices "suspend war rather than contribute to its conduct." Technology is "not just... an assemblage of hardware but a philosophical system" shaping the framework for thinking about war. See [[sources/van-creveld-technology-and-war-1989]]. Links: [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- **Linear logic of technology vs. paradoxical logic of war (van Creveld *Technology and War* Range 5 / Conclusions)**: War is permeated by technology in every element (causes, goals, command, strategy, tactics, conceptual frameworks), but every part of technology—including non-military infrastructure (roads, vehicles, communications, timekeepers)—affects war and "goes a long way to dictate the character of organization, logistics, intelligence, strategy, even the concept of battle itself." Weapons effects remain "limited mainly to tactics"; nuclear weapons impact strategy precisely because "too powerful to use," turning war toward "an exercise in make-believe, a game." Technology is "an abstract system of knowledge, an attitude towards life and a method," not hardware alone. The modern idea that "war is primarily a question of technology" (technicians, superiority) is "neither self-evident, nor necessarily correct, nor even very old" and "dangerous" at extremes; tech impact includes "nonutilitarian and nonfunctional" psychological aspects "paramount" in war. Technology's logic is **linear** (objective physical world: repeatable one-to-one cause-effect, repetitiveness, specialization, integration into streamlined systems, certainty by eliminating uncertainty, efficiency). War's logic is **paradoxical** (contest of independent wills; "the same action will not always lead to the same result. The opposite, indeed, is closer to the truth"; uncertainty "forms a necessary part of war and can be eliminated only at the cost of turning conflict into a charade"; success requires "an ability to tolerate uncertainty, cope with it, and make use of it"). Immutable elements: war as "an act of violence" producing "hardship and of suffering, of stress and fear and pain and death" — "primarily an affair of the heart" (courage, honor, duty, loyalty, sacrifice independent of tech level, from cavemen to future); core functions (supplying, communicating, intelligence, securing, fixing, maneuvering, protecting, striking) "rooted in the very nature of war and thus immune to technology." Applying linear tech principles (centralized, no-redundancy, hyper-specialized systems) to war "would be useless or worse" — vulnerable to decapitation and cascade failure; war demands redundancy and tolerance for friction. "To explain the way technology relates to the logic of war, and hence should be used in it, is... the most important one." Future: tech "will continue to help alter the shape of war" but "little prospect that it will bring war to an end." "War will remain what it has always been, namely a horrible, messy affair in which some people are killed and others mutilated... not... altered by any kind of technology and with which, accordingly, society will have to come to terms." Crosses Luttwak on paradoxical logic ("brilliant"). See [[sources/van-creveld-technology-and-war-1989]], [[concepts/paradoxical-logic-of-strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], [[hubs/warfare/logistics]].

## Deep-Time Evolutionary Baseline (Gat 2006)

Azar Gat's *War in Human Civilization* (2006) supplies the ultimate-cause foundation for the hub's long arc:

- War predates agriculture and the state by the full span of Homo evolution (~99.5% of genus history spent as hunter-gatherers). Violent death rates in many documented forager societies reached 15–35% of adult males (raids and ambushes the lethal norm; open battles rarer). Motivations: resources (especially meat), reproductive access (women as primary "prize"), status, revenge, and coalitional psychology rooted in kin selection and ethnocentrism. See [[concepts/hunter-gatherer-warfare]] and [[concepts/evolutionary-origins-of-war]].
- On the steppe, the Neolithic package (herding + limited cultivation) + horse riding (late 2nd millennium BCE) produced fully nomadic pastoralists whose entire way of life (herding/hunting/movement) was lifelong training for war. They enjoyed overwhelming mobility (70+ km/day), lived off the land, used the composite bow with lethal accuracy from horseback (including backward while fleeing), and had no fixed targets to defend—enabling hit-and-run raids that shifted to systematic extortion ("protection money") when possible. Agrarian responses included monumental walls (e.g., Great Wall precursors) and the shift from predominant pre-state raids to sieges and set-piece battles. Steppe polities often formed as specialized "extortion conglomerations" (Barfield). See [[gat-war-in-human-civilization-2006]], [[processes/evolution-of-war]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- In the modern era the calculus flipped among affluent liberal democracies: Kantian factors (democracy + economic interdependence + international organizations) plus nuclear weapons made major war between them irrational and rare. Conquest ceased to pay in industrial societies; liberal polities proved superior at total-war mobilization due to economic base and alliances, yet face constraints in small wars from norms and casualty sensitivity. Risks persist in non-liberal "zones of turmoil," failed states, terrorism, and WMD proliferation. See [[gat-war-in-human-civilization-2006]] (esp. Ch. 16–17 synthesis), [[processes/evolution-of-war]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
- Agriculture/sedentism scaled violence (storable wealth, population density, fortifications as sedentism response rather than war "invention"). Tribal raiding endemic; pastoralists often more predatory than foragers due to mobility + numbers.
- The state (Leviathan) is largely a "spin-off" of war-making: superior war performers suppressed internal feuds while projecting force, enabling dense ordered societies, division of labour, and civilization — at the cost of hierarchy and exposure to larger wars. Per-capita violent death often fell under effective states except during their most destructive conflicts.
- Modernity (guns + markets → fiscal-military states; industrial explosion; liberal democracy + ultimate weapons) flipped the calculus for affluent liberal polities: positive-sum growth and nuclear stakes made major war between them irrational and rare. Pre-modern participatory polities (Athens, Rome) still voted for material agrarian gains through war. Risks persist via failed states, nationalism, and proliferation.

## Domains of Warfare

Warfare is not one thing. Different environments and technologies produce distinct domains:

- **Land warfare** (infantry, cavalry, combined arms) — see [[hubs/warfare/tactics-and-battle-strategies|Tactics and Battle Strategies]] for detailed examples.
- **Naval and maritime power** (sea control, trade protection, power projection)
- **Siege and fortification warfare** (trace italienne, modern engineering)
- **Steppe and nomadic warfare** (mobility, horse-archers, operational tempo — see [[mongol-army]])
- **Asymmetric and irregular warfare** (guerrilla, insurgency, raiding) — historically conceptualized in Carl von Clausewitz's [[concepts/peoples-war-clausewitz|Volkskrieg]] doctrine as a strategic means of defense, serving as the theoretical origin of modern [[guerrilla-warfare|guerrilla warfare]] and partisan theory.
- **Total and industrialized warfare** (mass mobilization, economies of war, air power, nuclear)

Each domain has its own logic, constraints, and historical arc.

### Military innovation (the interwar laboratory)

How militaries develop new doctrine, technology, and organization is its own analytical problem —
why, under shared austerity and rapid technological change, some interwar (1918–1939) services
innovated brilliantly and others failed. The framework and its seven case studies (from
[[sources/murray-millett-military-innovation-interwar-1996|Murray & Millett, *Military Innovation
in the Interwar Period*]], 1996):

- **Framework:** [[military-innovation]] — concrete strategic problem + institutional culture +
  honest doctrine and experimentation; changing the *context* of war beats raw technical lead.
- **Case studies:** [[armored-warfare]] · [[amphibious-warfare]] · [[carrier-aviation]] ·
  [[close-air-support]] · [[radar-and-air-defense]] (Dowding's system, the premier success) ·
  [[submarine-warfare]] ("innovation ignored") · [[strategic-bombardment|strategic bombing]].
- Complements the [[military-effectiveness]] framework and the [[revolution-in-military-affairs]] debate.
