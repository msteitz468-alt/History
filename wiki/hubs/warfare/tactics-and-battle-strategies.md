# Tactics and Battle Strategies

This page focuses on the **tactical** (battlefield) and **operational** levels of warfare — how forces were actually employed in combat and campaigns — as distinct from (but closely related to) higher-level strategy.

See the broader context in the [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy Hub]] and the dedicated [[hubs/warfare/strategy|Strategy]] sub-hub.

## Core Tactical Principles Across Eras

Many principles recur, even as technology changes the specific tools:

- **Combined arms**: Getting different troop types (infantry, cavalry, missile, artillery, engineers) to support each other rather than fight in isolation.
- **Shock vs. fire**: The balance between closing for melee shock and using ranged fire to degrade the enemy before contact.
- **Maneuver and terrain**: Using ground, flanking, envelopment, and deception.
- **Morale and cohesion**: Discipline, leadership, and unit cohesion often mattered more than numbers or weapons.
- **Reserves and exploitation**: Holding forces back to commit at the decisive moment or to exploit success.

**High Middle Ages infantry and missile support (France Ch. 6)**: Knights held initiative but required substantial foot support. "if we allow for the demands of status, it is likely that there were two or three servants for every cavalryman" who reinforced sergeants and specialists. Archers were "a formidable instrument of war" (Hastings shieldwall, Brémule 1119, Bourgthéroulde 1124, Northallerton 1138; Baldwin I's *magister sagittariorum*; Richard on Third Crusade). Archery skill diffused through population (often depicted as hunters); raised from lords' followings rather than national specialities. Urban sergeants (esp. French cities, Latin Kingdom, Italian communes, Flanders) supplied bulk foot; English Assizes of Arms systematised arming of free men producing large (but variable-quality) infantry by Edward I's time. Ad hoc nature meant poor cohesion: "any group of soldiers, cavalry or infantry, lacked cohesion and were likely to be of uneven quality." Commanders therefore relied heavily on mercenaries despite elite ideology and Church condemnation. See [[concepts/medieval-infantry]], [[medieval-warfare]], [[proprietorial-warfare]], and [[sources/france-western-warfare-in-the-age-of-the-crusades-1999]]. (Grounded exclusively in Ch. 6 cache range.)

## Selected Tactical and Operational Case Studies

- **Peloponnesian War hoplite battles (Hanson, *A War Like No Other*, Ch. 5 Armor, range 5501-8500; Kagan *The Peloponnesian War*, range 13001-17000)**: Delium (424 BCE) and Mantinea (418) as rare traditional collisions amid attritional war. 

  Delium (Kagan verbatim): Pagondas (Theban commander, >60) massed Thebans to 25-deep (usual 8); used 300 elite trained hoplites (first recorded professional corps separate from militia). Cavalry and light-armed on flanks. Athenian right routed Boeotian left (Thespiae/Tanagra/Orchomenus); Theban right slowly pushed Athenians. Pagondas sent two cavalry squadrons unseen around hill to appear in Athenian rear, causing panic. "This broke the momentum of the Athenian charge." Nightfall halted pursuit. Athenian losses: ~1,000 hoplites + general Hippocrates (heaviest of Ten Years' War) plus light troops. Boeotians later used "enormous flamethrower" to burn Delium fort. Famous for Socrates (hoplite) and Alcibiades (cavalry) fighting. First deep wing tactic foreshadowing Epaminondas/Philip/Alexander.

  Amphipolis (422, Kagan): Brasidas used deception (sacrifices + Clearidas at northern/Thracian gate) to lure Cleon south, then elite 150 from southern gate struck disordered Athenian center during withdrawal maneuver; Clearidas flanked. ~600 Athenian dead incl. Cleon; 7 Spartans incl. Brasidas (mortally wounded in victory).

  Mantinea campaign (418, Kagan): Agis forced battle after Eleans withdrew 3,000 hoplites over target dispute (Tegea vs Lepreum). Terrain: Mantinean plain ~18 mi N-S / 11 mi E-W at ~2,200 ft, slopes N, narrows 3 mi S of Mantinea to ~2-mi gap between Mytikas (W) and Kapnistra (E) ridges; sinkholes (Zanovistas, Sarandapotamos). Agis collected allies at Tegea, marched to Heracleum SE of Mantinea. (Approved links: [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/]], [[alcibiades]], [[pericles]], [[brasidas]], [[nicias]])
- **Detailed Analysis**: [[hubs/warfare/classical-antiquity/second-punic-war/battle-of-cannae|Battle of Cannae – Tactical Analysis]]
  - Narrative: [[battle-of-cannae-216bce|Cannae (216 BCE)]] — Hannibal's double envelopment. Classic example of using terrain, feigned weakness, and coordination of infantry and cavalry.
- **[[battle-of-gaugamela-331bce|Gaugamela (331 BCE)]]** — Alexander's oblique order and cavalry hammer-and-anvil against the Persian center and flanks.
- **Mongol operational art** — [[mongol-tactics]] (arrow storm, caracole, feigned retreat, double envelopment, open-the-end, Fabian avoidance, deception, weather magic) and [[mongol-siege-warfare]] (circumvallation, engineer corps, conscripted levies); grounded in [[may-mongol-art-of-war-2016|May 2016]]. See also [[mongol-army]], [[battle-of-kalka-river-1223]], and [[mongol-conquests]] for campaign context; operational strategy (columns, annihilation, leader pursuit) in [[hubs/warfare/strategy|Mongol mobility and columns]].
- **Infantry revival in the Late Middle Ages** — English longbow + dismounted men-at-arms (e.g., Crécy, Poitiers, Agincourt) and Swiss pike squares defeating feudal cavalry. Per DeVries conclusions: infantry armies 1302–1347 (Courtrai through Crécy/Neville's Cross) exhibited "the uniformity in tactics used by all these infantry armies throughout such a large geographical region." Armies "primarily, and in most cases entirely, fought on foot"; cavalry present "dismounted and fighting as infantry"; "it was, in almost every battle, the cavalry of these armies charging against infantry opponents which led directly to their defeat." Longbow "not as 'a killing machine' [...] but instead to narrow their opponents' charges"; defeats of foot armies occurred "when the attacks made by the defending troops" (i.e. when infantry abandoned the stand and charged, as at Mons-en-Pévèle and Cassel). Ditches, terrain choice forced frontal assaults only. English dismounting "against the ancient tradition of their fathers" but "that ancient tradition was completely supplanted by the new, victorious tactics of the English army." (DeVries range 10) See [[sources/devries-infantry-warfare-early-fourteenth-century-1996]], [[hubs/warfare/strategy]], Courtrai/Bannockburn/Crécy analyses.
- **Early gunpowder tactics** — The shift from massed shot to combined pike-and-shot formations, and the development of volley fire and drill.

For many more specific battles, see the live event lists in the main [[hubs/warfare/warfare-hub|Warfare & Strategy Hub]].

## Evolution of Battle

- Chariot and phalanx eras: emphasis on formation and shock.
- Legionary era: flexibility, engineering support, and reserves.
- Medieval: cavalry dominance and the siege as the decisive act. Infantry revival (late 13th–14th c.): dense close-order foot (pikes, goedendags/club-pikes, schiltrons) + terrain neutralising cavalry on chosen ground. Verbruggen (infantry section): Flemish at Courtrai 1302 "the first to defeat a great knightly army since Roman times"; "massed close-order foot with anti-cavalry weapons"; "pikemen in their tightly packed formation" (cf. Falkirk schiltrons: front rank kneeling pikes planted, rear ranks above); knights dismounted "with the foot-soldiers at Courtrai". Factors: economic development, national feeling/cohesion in urban/peasant forces (Flemings, Scots, Swiss, Dithmarschen), knights past peak. Source criticism: "what is good in poetry does not necessarily mean good tactics". See [[events/battle-of-courtrai-1302]], [[hubs/warfare/high-middle-ages/courtrai-war/battle-of-courtrai-1302]], [[sources/verbruggen-art-of-warfare-western-europe-middle-ages-1997]].
  Grounded additions from France (Ch. 12–13): armies formed in "battles" of retinues with cavalry conroi sub-units; "Control of troops was the key... difficult to achieve in armies made up of men who lived by an individualistic ethos." Templar Rule: small units "gathered close around a banner" with reserves for repeated charges (not single all-out shock); Bouvines used this technique. "It is much easier to find occasions... when footsoldiers played a vital role than it is to find instances of classic shock tactics by cavalry en masse." Infantry mass ("Thick and tight!") held cavalry; urban specialization (Italian sections, Flemish guilds). Knights prized for mobility + training. "no neat pattern of evolution." 13th-c. professionalism uneven; "little sense of conscious development"; "each victory was sui generis" due to no institutional memory or staff. Command personality decisive (examples: Guérin at Bouvines, Simon at Muret vs. failures of control). See [[medieval-campaign-style]], [[limits-of-tactical-evolution-medieval]], [[proprietorial-warfare]], [[sources/france-western-warfare-in-the-age-of-the-crusades-1999]], [[hubs/warfare/high-middle-ages/bouvines-campaign/battle-of-bouvines-1214]].
- Early modern: linear tactics, drill, and the growing importance of artillery.
- Modern: dispersion, combined arms teams, and the impact of machine guns, tanks, and air power.
  - WWI Western Front (1916–18): [[concepts/elastic-defense-in-depth]] (1917: depth, economy of force, counterattack primacy over holding terrain; inductive from Somme experience) and [[concepts/infiltration-tactics-1918]] (1918: complementary firepower + maneuver; avoid strongpoints, deep penetration by specialized assault groups; short intense artillery support; decentralized initiative). Per [[sources/lupfer-dynamics-of-doctrine-1981]] Ch. 3 / Range 3: completed via collective effort and "remarkable training program"; process was inductive and tentative ("all tactical solutions were tentative"; "evidence from the battlefield was more respected than the doctrine"); "no panacea. A formula is harmful" (Rupprecht). OHL ([[ludendorff]], [[hindenburg]]) directed talent efficiently with "remarkable tolerance of dissent." Principles flexible yet precise for "common understanding and unity of effort." Enriches [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/]] and [[places/western-front]] analysis. See also [[hubs/warfare/strategy]].
