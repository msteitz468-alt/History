# Augustine of Hippo — Detailed Biography

> **Summary & network:** [[actors/augustine-of-hippo|Augustine of Hippo (actor page)]] · **Biography hub:** [[hubs/biographies/biographies-hub|Biographies Hub]] · **Source:** [[sources/brown-augustine-of-hippo-2000|Brown, *Augustine of Hippo*]]

A graduate-level analytic study of Augustine's formation, the mechanics of his major
decisions, his intellectual methods, and the structural vs. contingent character of his
legacy — built on Peter Brown's biography and the post-1967 evidence (the Divjak letters
and Dolbeau sermons). The compact network summary lives on the [[actors/augustine-of-hippo|actor page]];
this page owns the depth.

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Augustine was born on 13 November 354 at **Thagaste**, a small farming town on the Numidian
plateau — 200 miles inland, 2,000 feet up — administratively dependent on
[[carthage|Carthage]]. His father **Patricius** was a *tenuis municeps*, a burgess of
slender means: free, civilized, hot-tempered, unfaithful to his wife, and proud enough of
his clever son to beggar the household to fund an education far beyond his own station.
His mother **[[monica|Monica]]** (b. c. 331) was an austere provincial Christian who kept
the "primitive" African usages — fasts, meals at the tombs of the dead — and whose
"devouring love" for Augustine he later recognized as containing "an element of unspiritual
desire." A wealthy Thagaste patron, **Romanianus**, supplemented Patricius's savings.

Augustine's education was the standard late-Roman rhetorical training: purely literary,
built on the intensive memorization of Vergil, [[cicero|Cicero]], Sallust, and Terence, to
the exclusion of philosophy, science, and history. Greek was taught so badly that Augustine
came to hate it and remained, in Brown's phrase, "the only Latin philosopher in antiquity
to be virtually ignorant of Greek" — a fact that shaped his whole intellectual life, since
he met the Greek philosophical and Christian traditions only through Latin translation. He
studied at Madauros (town of the second-century Platonist Apuleius), spent one idle,
sexually awakening year at Thagaste (the setting of the *Confessions*' pear-theft), and
arrived at **Carthage** aged seventeen in 371, "where the cauldron of illicit loves leapt
and boiled around me." There he took an unnamed concubine who bore his son **Adeodatus** and
lived faithfully with him for some fifteen years; read Cicero's lost *Hortensius* in 373,
his "conversion to philosophy"; and, seeking a rational religion that exculpated God for the
existence of evil, became a **[[manichaeism|Manichaean]] Hearer**.

What was contingent here versus structural? The rhetorical curriculum, the concubinage of an
ambitious provincial "on the make," and the appeal of Manichaeism to the Carthage
intelligentsia (Brown likens it to Communism among 1930s English intellectuals) were
structural features of his world. What was contingent — and decisive — was the particular
temperament formed by them: an introspective intensity, a hunger for certainty crossed with
a sceptic's restlessness, and the lifelong friendships (**Alypius**, Nebridius) that carried
his intellectual life.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Augustine's "rise" was double: first the ordinary cursus of a rhetor, then its abandonment
for the church. Disillusioned with Manichaeism after the Manichaean bishop **Faustus of
Milevis** proved unable to answer his cosmological doubts (383), he left Carthage for Rome
(384), deceiving Monica to sail without her, and within a year obtained — through the pagan
Prefect **Symmachus**, lobbied by Manichaean friends — the professorship of rhetoric at
**Milan**, seat of the imperial court. This was the summit of a rhetor's career: effectively
the court's minister of propaganda, deliverer of official panegyrics.

At Milan the machinery of ascent reversed. The immaterial conception of God in
[[ambrose-of-milan|Ambrose]]'s learned preaching, and then a reading of "some books of the
Platonists" (Plotinus, in Marius Victorinus's Latin) in 386, resolved the two problems
Manichaeism had left him: how God could be spiritual rather than a subtle material force,
and how evil could exist without a co-eternal evil principle (Plotinus: evil as privation, a
turning-away, not an active substance). Monica arranged a marriage to a Catholic heiress and
his concubine was dismissed — "a blow which crushed my heart to bleeding" — but the crisis
that followed the visit of Ponticianus (who told him of the desert monk Antony) ended not in
marriage but in renunciation: the garden scene of August 386, the child's voice chanting
*tolle lege*, and the opening of Paul at Romans 13. He resigned his chair, retired with a
small circle to the villa at **Cassiciacum** to write philosophical dialogues, and was
baptized by Ambrose at Easter 387.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**The *servus Dei* and the reluctant priest (387–395).** After Monica's death at Ostia
(387) and the deaths of Adeodatus and Nebridius, Augustine returned to Thagaste to live a
contemplative communal life on the family estate as a "Servant of God." Visiting **Hippo
Regius** in 391 to recruit for his community, he was seized by the congregation and forced
into ordination as priest under the elderly Greek bishop Valerius — who, exceptionally, let
him preach. He debated the Manichee Fortunatus in the baths of Hippo (392), expounded the
Creed to the assembled African bishops (393), and about 395 — answering Simplicianus's
questions on Paul (*Ad Simplicianum*) — reached the turning point of his thought: grace, not
free choice, has "the upper hand."

**Bishop and controversialist (395/6–410).** Made co-adjutor and then bishop of Hippo amid
scandal (the Numidian primate Megalius accused him of Manichaeism and of sending
love-philtres), Augustine ran his household as a monastery that became a seminary for
Numidian bishoprics, arbitrated lawsuits he "heartily resented," and wrote the *Confessions*
(c. 397). The dominant struggle was against **[[donatism|Donatism]]**, the majority African
church. Initially opposed to compulsion (fearing insincere converts, *ficti*), he came by
the imperial Edict of Unity of **405** to accept and then theorize state coercion of
schismatics — *disciplina*, "a teaching by inconveniences," *compelle intrare* ("compel them
to come in") — a step Brown calls making him "the first theorist of the Inquisition," though
he consistently opposed the death penalty and insisted instruction accompany compulsion. The
mechanics of that reversal repay attention: as late as the Catholic council of 404 Augustine had
asked only for police protection, fearing that forced converts would be *ficti*; what moved him was
partly his own developing doctrine of grace (which could work even on the insincere, since "God is
able to graft them in again") and partly the observed fact that, in the town of Hippo, imperial
pressure produced peaceful conformity as local Donatist landowners lost office and inheritance
rights. In the countryside, by contrast, coercion met the Circumcellions, and Augustine watched the
"vicious circle... familiar in modern history — doctrinaire persecution from above, which, in turn,
can only be resisted by mounting violence from below." He refused, when the Spanish priest Consentius
proposed planting *agents provocateurs* among the heretics, to "hunt with lies," and pleaded (often
in vain) with imperial officials against executing captured Circumcellions. Brown's careful verdict
is that he "may be the first theorist of the Inquisition, but he was in no position to be a Grand
Inquisitor": he saw coercion as the corrective treatment of a body of Christians who resembled his
own congregation, not the crushing of a hated alien sect.

**The *City of God* and the Pelagian crisis (410–420).** The **[[sack-of-rome-410|sack of
Rome]]** by Alaric in 410 sent pagan aristocratic refugees to Carthage blaming Christianity;
Augustine's answer was the *[[city-of-god|City of God]]* (first books 413, completed c. 426),
the great work of the two cities. Its real target was not popular cult but the literary neo-paganism
of aristocrats like Volusianus, "a paganism that existed only in libraries," which Augustine chose
to meet through those same libraries — citing Cicero some 119 times, treating Porphyry as a worthier
opponent than any heretic, and setting "your Vergil" against "our Scriptures" at every turn. From it
came the enduring framework of the two cities, drawn partly from the Donatist exegete Tyconius: since
the Fall, humanity is divided between the city built by love of God and the city built by love of
self, mixed inextricably in this life and separated only at the Last Judgment; the Christian is a
*peregrinus*, a resident alien, grateful for the earthly city's order yet never finally identified
with it. Simultaneously the **[[pelagianism|Pelagian controversy]]**
erupted: [[pelagius|Pelagius]] and his disciple Caelestius denied inherited guilt and
asserted human perfectibility. Augustine built the *causa gratiae* against them — insisting that God
gives not merely commands and sanctions but the *love* that fulfills them, and that baptism begins a
lifelong convalescence rather than completing a cure — securing their condemnation at Carthage and
Milevis (416), through Rome (418, after Pope Zosimus's brief reversal), and by imperial edict.

**Old age, Julian, and the end (420–430).** The controversy's last and ablest phase pitted
Augustine against **[[julian-of-eclanum|Julian of Eclanum]]**, who charged him with
crypto-Manichaeism and defended the goodness of sexuality and the justice of God against the
doctrine of infant damnation. The dispute became "a dingy, rushed affair" of volume answering
volume, forwarded from Rome by Alypius in installments, running to Augustine's death. It
overlapped a "revolt of the monasteries" at Hadrumetum in Africa and at Marseilles and Lérins in
Gaul, where monks recoiled from the implications of predestination for ascetic effort — forcing
Augustine to write *On the Predestination of the Saints* and *On the Gift of Perseverance* (sent
across the Mediterranean at the end of 429, as the Vandals advanced). He also composed the
*Retractationes* — a chronological self-catalogue and correction of his own works, prompted by the
scriptural warning that "from much speaking thou shalt not escape sin" — assembled a personal
library of 93 works in 232 "books," reversed his earlier scepticism about contemporary miracles
after Orosius brought the relics of St Stephen to Africa (recording some seventy cures at Hippo in
two years), and nominated the priest Eraclius as his successor in 426. He died on 28 August 430
during the siege of Hippo, having asked to be left alone with the penitential psalms, the *Opus
Imperfectum* against Julian unfinished on his desk.

Alongside the great controversies the *Confessions* (c. 397) stand as the hinge of his life and
work: thirteen books in which the narrative of sin and conversion (I–IX) opens into an
examination of the present self — memory, temptation, the opaque vastness of the inner life (X) —
and then into exegesis of Genesis (XI–XIII). Written as a sustained prayer, saturated with the
Psalms, it broke with the conventional conversion-autobiography (which ended in achieved security)
by dwelling on unresolved struggle: "a convalescent, not a cured man." Brown's later hypothesis is
that it was composed partly as a "thank-offering" for Augustine's unexpected vocation as a
preacher, the Dolbeau sermons showing him at Carthage in 397 as "a preacher of repentance,
conversion and grace" in the very months the book took shape.

## 5. Intellectual and Religious Style and Methods

Augustine thought autobiographically and psychologically. His signature move was to make
the interior life — memory, will, "delight" (*delectatio*), the divided self — the ground of
theology: "Command what You will, and give what You command." He worked by *confessio*
(self-accusation joined to praise of God) rather than by the achieved serenity of the
philosophers; the *Confessions* present "a convalescent, not a cured man." His method in
controversy was juxtaposition and tenacity — in the *City of God*, setting "your Vergil"
against "our Scriptures" for a learned pagan audience, meeting "the last pagans through their
libraries." He read Scripture allegorically (theorized in *De doctrina christiana*), treating
the text as deliberately "veiled" by God to exercise the seeker's mind — a hermeneutic Brown
compares to Freudian dream-interpretation, both presupposing a "fault" across consciousness.
Handicapped in Greek, he was nonetheless an original digester of Plotinus, transforming
Neoplatonic procession-and-return into a Christian account of the soul's dependence on an
"invisible doctor." His psychology of habit (*consuetudo*) and unconscious consent to desire
repeatedly anticipates modern accounts of the unconscious.

Two mechanisms drove his intellectual development, and both are instructive because they show
thought moving under pressure rather than in the abstract. The first is the resolution of the
Manichaean dilemma: Manichaeism had held him for nine years by exculpating God for evil (an
external, co-eternal evil principle) and by treating the "Kingdom of Light" as a subtle *material*
force. Ambrose's insistence that "when God is thought of, our thoughts should dwell on no material
reality whatsoever" gave him the target — an immaterial God — but not the means to think it;
Plotinus supplied the means, teaching that the Good flows out without being diminished and that
evil is not a substance but a privation, "a turning away." The shift is exactly datable in the
*Confessions*: Augustine ceased to identify himself with a fragment of God and accepted that he was
"separate and different from God." The second mechanism is his discovery, in pastoral and
controversial practice, of the limits of the will. His early anti-Manichaean works had defended
free will so vigorously (*De libero arbitrio*) that Pelagius would later quote them against him;
but debating the Manichee Fortunatus (392), preaching against habitual swearing, and above all
answering Simplicianus's questions on Paul (c. 395) drove him to make *delectatio* — "delight," the
involuntary spring of the will — the center of his account of grace. He thought, characteristically,
by writing: "I am the sort of man who writes because he has made progress, and who makes progress —
by writing."

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

The sources give an unusually intimate portrait, but they are self-authored and must be read
critically. Augustine valued friendship to the point of theology — "of many, made us one" —
and his life is a chain of relationships: Monica; the fused friendship with the unnamed
Thagaste friend whose death "darkened his heart"; his lifelong *alter ego* **Alypius**; his
concubine, whose departure he mourned even as he made it for ambition; his son Adeodatus.
The 2000 evidence complicates the older image of a severe authoritarian. The Dolbeau sermons
show him shouted down by his own Carthage congregation (the "Missa sint" episode of January
404) and preaching "dialogues with the crowd" that betray "how little authority Augustine
actually wielded." The Divjak letters show a seventy-year-old bishop interviewing a
terrified kidnapped girl and asking to see a teenage boy's school exercises — an "inspired
fussiness," not a burnt-out old man. In dress and table he was deliberately austere (silver
spoons but plain crockery; verses on the table against gossip; a forfeited wine-glass for
swearing), and he refused a gift of silk as unfit for "a poor man, born of poor parents."

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

In 429 the Arian **[[vandal-kingdom|Vandals]]** under Genseric crossed from Spain and
overran Mauretania and Numidia; Roman Africa "simply collapsed," its bishops "divided and
demoralized." Count Boniface, again reduced to commanding Gothic mercenaries, held the
fortified port of Hippo against a siege by land and sea in the winter of 429–430. Augustine,
having refused (in Letter 228) to let bishops flee their flocks, fell ill with fever in
August 430, asked to be left alone with the penitential psalms copied onto his walls, and
died on 28 August 430, aged 75, weeping. **[[possidius|Possidius]]** — who had taken refuge
with him — recorded the death and compiled the *Indiculum* of his works. Hippo was evacuated
and partly burnt within a year, but Augustine's library reportedly survived: the
*Retractationes* and Possidius's index had already fixed his corpus, and it was that corpus,
not the ruined African church, that carried him into the Middle Ages.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Augustine's structural legacy is threefold. **Theologically**, his doctrines of original
sin, prevenient grace, and predestination — victorious over the Pelagian view of human
perfectibility — became the frame within which the Latin church, the medieval schoolmen, and
the sixteenth-century Reformers (Luther an Augustinian friar; Calvin an Augustinian on grace)
all argued. **Politically**, the *City of God* detached Christian hope from the survival of
Rome at the precise moment the Western empire failed, giving medieval Christendom a theology
of the church-in-the-world (the *peregrinus*, the resident alien) rather than a sacralized
empire. **Culturally**, the *Confessions* created the genre of Western interior autobiography
and a model of the divided, self-scrutinizing self that runs through Petrarch, the Reformers,
Rousseau, and modern psychology. His endorsement of coercion against the Donatists supplied,
against his own more careful qualifications, a text quarried by later persecutors. Brown
warns against the opposite exaggeration — "demonizing" Augustine as the "evil genius of
Europe" responsible for Western sexual guilt — as "an act of egregious cultural narcissism."

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources.** Augustine is his own chief witness, and the danger is ventriloquism.
The *Confessions* (c. 397) are a theological construction of the self written a decade after
the events, not a diary: their conversion narrative is shaped by the doctrine of grace he had
just reached in *Ad Simplicianum*, and the serene Cassiciacum dialogues of 386 diverge
sharply from the anguished garden of Book VIII. The *Retractationes* fix the canon and dating
of his formal works; Possidius's *Vita* supplies the contemporary external view, deliberately
gentler than Paulinus's near-contemporary *Life of Ambrose*. The two great post-1967
discoveries transformed the evidence base: the **Divjak letters** (found by Johannes Divjak,
1975; ed. 1981) — the vivid letters of 418–428 revealing the tax revolts, the
[[slavery-in-early-christianity|slave trade]], and the limits of episcopal power — and the
**Dolbeau sermons** (found by François Dolbeau, 1990; ed. 1996), preached at Carthage in 397
and 403–404 to real, resistant crowds and to a still-living paganism.

**Conflicting accounts and reconciliation.** The chief interpretive tension is internal to
Augustine's self-presentation: the confident *magister* of the formal treatises versus the
harried, shouted-down preacher of the sermons. Brown's own 2000 Epilogue is a model of
reconciling them — he explicitly retracts his 1967 portrait of a "severe and aggressive
figure of authority" and a late Augustine whose "energy has burnt itself out" (a verdict he
had taken from John Burnaby's *Amor Dei*, 1938), judging that he had over-relied on the
formal theology and neglected "the more muted, background tones of his day-to-day life as a
bishop."

**Major scholarly lives and debates.** Peter Brown's *Augustine of Hippo* (1967) is the
foundational modern biography and the founding text of Late Antiquity as a field; Brown wrote
it using the seventeenth-century Maurist edition and Tillemont's chronology, translating
Augustine for secular readers through the "chemical equivalents" of psychoanalysis (for the
theology of grace) and social psychology (for the ecclesiology) — an approach Henry Chadwick
fairly called "a biography without the theology." Serge Lancel's *Saint Augustin* (1999) is
the first full biography to use the new evidence; Claude Lepelley recovered the robustly
secular life of African cities; Neil McLynn's *Ambrose of Milan* (1994) dismantled the older
picture of a tolerant "Milanese circle of Christian Platonists," showing Augustine's 386
Platonism as "already poised for battle." W. H. C. Frend's socio-ethnic reading of Donatism
(1952) — Romanized elite against indigenous poor — Brown rejects, arguing no significant
class or cultural gap separated Augustine from the Donatist bishops. The old "Janus figure"
thesis (a sinister break between a sunnier classical Christianity and a grace-haunted Middle
Ages) is now widely qualified, Brown included.

**Methodological note.** Because so much of the record is autobiography and hagiography, the
biography of Augustine is a permanent exercise in reading a brilliant self-witness against
the grain — using the sermons and letters, the archaeology of Christian Africa (Lepelley,
Ennabli), and the recovered Donatist and Manichaean voices (the Conference of Carthage of
411; the Cologne Mani-Codex; the Kellis letters) to see the man his own masterpieces both
reveal and stylize.
