## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Sophia Augusta Fredericka of Anhalt-Zerbst was born at 2:30 a.m. on 21 April 1729 in Stettin to a mismatched, penurious Junker marriage. Her mother Johanna Elizabeth (Holstein-Gottorp) had wanted a son and rejected the daughter, handing her to wet nurses after a difficult delivery and later lavishing affection on a handicapped brother Wilhelm. Her father Christian Augustus (Anhalt-Zerbst regiment commander) was simple, honest, pious, and thrifty; he viewed his daughter as an angel but deferred to his wife. The result, as Catherine later wrote in her Memoirs, was a permanent wound: “I was merely tolerated and often I was scolded with a violence and anger I did not deserve.”

Her governess Babet Cardel (Huguenot) recognized the child’s belligerence as loneliness, taught her La Fontaine, Corneille, Racine, and Molière, and modeled noble self-respect despite inferior station. Catherine later credited her: “She had a noble soul, a cultured mind, a heart of gold; she was patient, gentle, cheerful, just, consistent—in short the kind of governess one would wish every child to have.” Pastor Wagner’s rigid catechism and threats produced the opposite: “All my life I have had this inclination to yield only to gentleness and reason—and to resist all pressure.” Johanna repeatedly told Sophia she was ugly and impertinent, forbade uninvited speech, and required her to kneel and kiss the hems of visiting women of rank. Sophia learned concealment of pride as a survival tactic.

At ten she met the eleven-year-old Peter Ulrich (Holstein heir presumptive to Sweden, grandson of Peter the Great) during a Brunswick visit; the title “queen” fell sweetly on her ears and she grew accustomed to the idea of being his wife. At thirteen Count Gyllenborg publicly validated her mind to her mother. Frederick II of Prussia, seeking to block a Saxon candidate, wrote Johanna proposing the match with Grand Duke Peter of Russia. Christian Augustus reluctantly approved and wrote his daughter to respect Peter and Elizabeth above all. The winter journey 1744 (no sledges at first, heavy carriages over ruts, bitter cold) brought her to Moscow and then St. Petersburg, where Elizabeth appeared in silver and diamonds with a black feather. Sophia was astonished: “It was quite impossible on seeing her for the first time not to be astonished by her beauty and the majesty of her bearing.”

Within days Peter told her he loved another but was resigned. Sophia studied Russian at night, contracted pneumonia (bled sixteen times in twenty-seven days), and was nursed personally by Elizabeth. Near death she asked for Simon Todorsky (Orthodox instructor) rather than a Lutheran pastor. This won sympathy. Johanna’s intercepted letters (with La Chétardie) insulting Elizabeth led to the French ambassador’s expulsion and Johanna’s marginalization. On 28 June 1744 Sophia publicly recited the creed in Russian, was anointed, and received the name Catherine (Ekaterina) chosen by Elizabeth. Betrothal followed in the Assumption Cathedral; rings exchanged (hers worth 12,000 rubles). A Kiev pilgrimage ended in permanent estrangement between Johanna and Peter after a jewel-case quarrel.

Peter’s smallpox (1751) left him disfigured; Catherine could not hide horror. The wedding (21 August 1745) was an ordeal of heavy gown and crown; Peter arrived reeking of alcohol and tobacco and fell asleep without consummation. The marriage remained unconsummated for nine years. Catherine resolved: “If you allow yourself to love that man, you will be the unhappiest creature on this earth… attention, please, and keep on a leash any affection you might feel.”

**Family rejection and the permanent wound (contingent vs. structural).**  
The mismatch between Johanna’s vivacious, extravagant, court-bred expectations and Christian Augustus’s stern, thrifty, reclusive Junker piety created a loveless provincial garrison household in Stettin. Johanna, married at fifteen to a man nearly three times her age whom she scarcely knew, viewed the birth of a daughter as bitter disappointment. Sophia was handed to wet nurses; no nursing or cradle-watching occurred. Eighteen months later the longed-for son Wilhelm arrived (later diagnosed with dislocated hip/crippling illness). Johanna “idolized” him with “gentle kisses, whispered endearments, tender caresses”; Sophia watched and “hated him.” The Memoirs, composed ~forty years later, still simmer with resentment while treating Wilhelm’s death clinically:

> “It was told me that I was not very joyfully welcomed.… My father thought I was an angel; my mother did not pay much attention to me. A year and a half later, she [Johanna] gave birth to a son whom she idolized. I was merely tolerated and often I was scolded with a violence and anger I did not deserve. I felt this without being perfectly clear why in my mind.”  
> (Catherine, Memoirs, ch. 1 Sophia’s Childhood)

Massie: “The harm done to this small daughter by Johanna’s open display of preference marked Sophia’s character profoundly.” Even at the height of power Catherine sought the “elemental creature warmth” her brother had received. This was contingent on Johanna’s personal frustrations and character (shallow charm souring to irritability when thwarted) yet structural within minor German princely marriage markets where surplus daughters faced spinster convents or poor matches. Counterfactual: absent this rejection, the later “leash” discipline and hunger for validation might have been less acute; Catherine might have entered the Russian court with greater emotional dependence rather than the self-contained ambition that sustained her through eighteen years as Grand Duchess.

**Education: Babet Cardel’s gentleness vs. Wagner’s pressure.**  
Babet (Huguenot exile) supplied the warmth Johanna withheld and instilled permanent love of French language and literature (La Fontaine → Corneille, Racine, Molière). Catherine later wrote of her: “She had a noble soul, a cultured mind, a heart of gold; she was patient, gentle, cheerful, just, consistent—in short the kind of governess one would wish every child to have.” To Voltaire she identified as “the pupil of Mademoiselle Cardel.” In 1776 (age 47) she advised Grimm: “one should not scold children too much but should make them trustful, so that they will not conceal their stupidities from us.”  

Pastor Wagner’s pedagogy—memorize, repeat, threats of cane and eternal damnation—produced terror (“every night at dusk I would go and cry by the window”) and lifelong resistance: “All my life I have had this inclination to yield only to gentleness and reason—and to resist all pressure.” “I am convinced in my inmost soul that Herr Wagner was a blockhead.” Johanna reinforced the lesson in humiliation: Sophia forbidden to speak unless spoken to, required to kneel and kiss hems of visiting women of rank, repeatedly told she was “ugly as well as impertinent.” Sophia obeyed outwardly and “smothered her own opinions.” Babet modeled an alternative: dignity preserved despite governess status.  

This binary (gentleness producing trust and curiosity; pressure producing concealment and rebellion) became diagnostic of Catherine’s later style. It explains her preference for reason-based governance rhetoric and her skill at drawing people out by listening (the opposite of her mother’s anxious self-importance).

**Early ambition, marriage as escape, and Peter Ulrich as symbol.**  
Marriage was the only sanctioned exit for a minor princess from both maternal control and the fate of “surplus daughters” (one aunt with sixteen pug dogs and parrots in one room). At ten, during a visit to her uncle the Prince-Bishop of Lübeck (guardian of the orphaned Peter Ulrich), Sophia met the eleven-year-old boy. She later recalled:

> “I knew that one day he would become king of Sweden, and although I was still a child, the title of queen fell sweetly on my ears. From that time on, the people around me teased me about him and gradually I grew accustomed to thinking that I was destined to be his wife.”

Peter Ulrich was in reality “small, delicate, and sickly, with protuberant eyes, no jaw, and thin, blond hair,” emotionally underdeveloped and already showing a liking for drink. Johanna and aunts whispered of a match. Count Gyllenborg (Hamburg, age ~10 for Sophia) publicly corrected Johanna: “Madame, you do not know the child. I assure you she has more mind and character than you give her credit for.” Sophia never forgot. She was discovering the skill of making others talk about themselves—precisely the technique that would later disarm courtiers. A brief adolescent infatuation with her uncle George Lewis (ten years older) almost led to acceptance of his proposal, but the Russian letter arrived first. Sophia chose the higher path without hesitation.

**The summons, Frederick II’s intrigue, and the journey as trauma/adventure.**  
Brümmer’s 1 January 1744 letter (explicitly excluding the father) and Frederick’s follow-up framed the fourteen-year-old as pawn to block Bestuzhev’s Saxon candidate and give Prussia indirect influence via Johanna. Johanna was euphoric (“lacked only wings”); Christian Augustus secluded himself, produced cautious advice (“Next to the empress... respect the Grand Duke... win by care and tenderness... Your Lord and his will are to be preferred to all the pleasures...”), then yielded. Sophia was largely ignored in the frantic wardrobe preparations for her mother; her trousseau was three old dresses and used sheets.  

Her response to the announced risk was pragmatic: “God would provide for stability, if such was his will; and that I had sufficient courage to face the risk, and that my heart told me that all would be well.” To her father she promised to preserve the Lutheran faith (letter from Königsberg). The journey itself (mid-January 1744, no snow, heavy carriages over frozen Baltic ruts, whistling wind through floor cracks, feet so numb she had to be carried) was physical ordeal. Postal stations were unheated; Johanna described one as pigsty-like. Sophia, however, saw comet 1744 as “very grand” and “very close to earth,” drank too much beer once (and was stopped), and treated the whole as “great adventure.” Frederick in Berlin judged her favorably: “The little princess of Zerbst combines the gaiety and spontaneity natural to her age with intelligence and wit surprising in one so young.” She sat beside him at table while her parents did not.

**Arrival, Elizabeth’s majesty, and Peter’s immediate confidences.**  
Elizabeth in silver and diamonds with upright black feather was overwhelming: “It was quite impossible on seeing her for the first time not to be astonished by her beauty and the majesty of her bearing.” Peter (now fifteen) rushed in: “I could wait no longer.” He promptly confided he loved another (a disgraced lady-in-waiting’s daughter) but was “resigned” because his aunt wished the match. Sophia “blushed... and... was astonished at his imprudence and want of judgment.” She saw at once “that he would have parted from me without regret” if expulsion came. She adapted: playmate and comrade rather than romantic object. This mismatch was not of her making; it was structural (Peter’s own brutalized Holstein childhood under Brümmer + smallpox trauma) and contingent (Elizabeth’s dynastic sentimentality).

**Pneumonia, Elizabeth as maternal surrogate, and the Todorsky choice.**  
Night study of Russian on cold stone floors produced acute pneumonia. Johanna refused bleeding (citing her brother’s death); Elizabeth overruled, ordered sixteen bleedings in twenty-seven days, and nursed personally—cradling the head, stroking hair, kissing forehead. Near death, when Johanna proposed a Lutheran pastor, Sophia whispered: “Why do that? Call Simon Todorsky instead. I would rather talk to him.” The story spread: the little foreign princess nearly died because she loved Russia enough to study barefoot at night. Public sympathy surged; Johanna’s stock fell. On her fifteenth birthday (21 April 1744) the emaciated girl received a diamond necklace and earrings worth 20,000 rubles from Elizabeth. The episode forged a surrogate maternal bond that Catherine never forgot, even through later jealousies and humiliations.

**Conversion as strategic performance and name choice.**  
Father’s consent was extracted by Frederick and a compliant Lutheran minister arguing “no essential difference.” On 28 June 1744, fasting, Sophia recited the Orthodox creed “like a parrot” in Russian in a scarlet dress matching the empress’s. She remained “quite in control.” Elizabeth chose the name Catherine (her own mother) rather than Sophia (Peter the Great’s rival half-sister). Betrothal the next day in the Assumption Cathedral lasted four hours; rings were valued at 12,000 and 14,000 rubles. Johanna was humiliated by separate alcove seating; Elizabeth prevented her kneeling with “Our situation is the same; our vows are the same.” Johanna’s report mixed complaint and pride. Catherine had crossed the Rubicon.

**Kiev pilgrimage, court vanity, and early wedge with mother.**  
The Kiev pilgrimage (to delay wedding until Peter showed maturity) became a lark for the young people. A jewel-case tipping incident (accidental, Peter claimed) produced a violent quarrel; Catherine, caught between, sided with Peter and wept. Johanna called Peter an “ill-bred little boy”; Peter called Johanna an “unjust, over-bearing shrew.” From that moment Peter “took a great dislike to my mother” and never forgot; Johanna bore an “unforgiving grudge.” Catherine noted: “In truth, at that time, the grand duke opened his heart to me more than to anyone else.” She observed Elizabeth’s church magnificence (gold, silver, pearls on icons) with aesthetic admiration but without personal devotion—“the majesty of architecture, art, and music merged into a splendid unity of inspired—but still man-made—beauty.” Transvestite balls (Elizabeth displaying legs) and the opera-box public scolding over “debts” (actually gifts to mother and Peter) taught the lesson of not outshining the empress’s vanity: “in dealing with a massive ego such as Elizabeth’s, all other women at court had to beware of succeeding too well.”

**Smallpox, the “hideous” moment, and Catherine’s failure of spontaneous affection.**  
Peter’s smallpox (after measles) ravaged his face. Elizabeth nursed him six weeks. On reunion in semi-darkness Catherine saw “almost with terror” the swollen, pitted face under an enormous wig; she called him “hideous” internally, stammered congratulations, and fled to collapse. Peter asked, “Do you recognize me?” The missed moment of warm, unrestrained compassion was never recovered. Peter internalized “hideous” and unlovable; he retreated to drilling servants and mocking Catherine. Catherine, proud and wounded, met coolness with reserve. Massie notes this as the precise point where a compassionate smile “might have ensured some kind of amicable future.” The smile was not given.

**The wedding night vow and its self-fashioning bias.**  
The 21 August 1745 wedding was physical ordeal (heavy gown, crushing crown, three-hour service, banquet with old men). In the nuptial chamber Peter arrived late, reeking of alcohol and tobacco, laughed nervously about what it would amuse his servants to see, and fell asleep. The marriage remained unconsummated for nine years. In the Memoirs (hindsight) Catherine records the private resolution that became doctrine:

> “If you allow yourself to love that man, you will be the unhappiest creature on this earth. With your temperament, you will expect some response whereas this man scarcely looks at you, talks of nothing but dolls, and pays more attention to any other woman than yourself. You are too proud to complain, therefore, attention, please, and keep on a leash any affection you might feel for this gentleman; you have yourself to think about, my dear girl.”  
> “This first scar made upon my impressionable heart remained with me forever; never did this firm resolution leave my head...”

Massie flags the voice: “This was the voice of an older, wiser Catherine, looking back on the difficulties of the young woman she had been many years before.” The vow was real in effect; whether fully articulated at age sixteen is the self-fashioning question. Ambition alone sustained her: “In my inmost soul there was something that never for a single moment allowed me to doubt that, sooner or later, I would become the sovereign Empress of Russia in my own right.” Peter told her of love for Mlle Karr; he continued drilling soldiers in his room.

**Johanna’s departure and the Zhukova signal of caprice/surveillance.**  
Johanna left after a purportedly loving farewell (ambassador’s account was harsher). Catherine was distraught and briefly homesick. Maria Zhukova, her lively Russian favorite among the young ladies, was dismissed on the claim that Johanna had requested it to prevent too-close friendship; Catherine’s attempts to send money or arrange a marriage were blocked (brother posted away, prospective husband to Astrakhan). Catherine concluded people “were being gratuitously ruined out of mere caprice, with no shadow of reason.” This was Bestuzhev/Elizabeth policy: isolate the couple, curb independent attachments. It was also a warning of autocratic arbitrariness.

**Seeds of Rise/Path to Power visible in Range 01.**  
By departure of Johanna and the early marriage months, Catherine had internalized: (1) concealment and performance as tools; (2) ambition as non-negotiable north star overriding personal humiliation; (3) reading of power dynamics (Elizabeth’s generosity/jealousy alternation; Peter’s immaturity as structural weakness); (4) the leash doctrine as protection for long-term self-interest; (5) cultivation of useful relationships (opening Peter’s heart, Elizabeth’s maternal surge). These were not yet political instruments, but the psychological and tactical repertoire that would later enable the eighteen-year wait and the 1762 coup. Contingent personal formation (rejection + Babet + journey trauma + missed smallpox moment) intersected with structural realities (autocratic court, serf empire, dynastic fragility) to produce a woman who could survive Peter and Elizabeth while accumulating the networks (Orlovs, Panin) that would matter later. Counterfactuals at this stage: had Peter responded to the smallpox disfigurement with spontaneous warmth from Catherine, the marriage might have been less poisonous and the coup less likely; had Elizabeth’s jealousy hardened permanently rather than relenting, Catherine might have been marginalized or sent home before betrothal.

Eighteen years of boredom and surveillance followed under Empress Elizabeth and Chancellor Bestuzhev. Bestuzhev imposed Maria Semenovna Choglokova (Elizabeth’s cousin, age 24, idolized her husband and “produced a child with almost annual regularity”) as watchdog: “simple-minded … uneducated … cruel … malicious … capricious … self-serving” (Catherine’s Memoirs). Catherine was forbidden letters or private conversations; all remarks vetted (“such talk would displease the empress”). She became “a royal prisoner.” In 1747 Choglokov was promoted to Peter’s governor; servants Peter liked were dismissed, rooms restricted, “the grand duke and I were now forced to remain inseparable.” Peter turned to Catherine for talk (“the only person with whom he could talk without every word being turned into a crime”), pacing for hours on military minutiae while she listened, exhausted.

Peter’s character revealed itself in the bedroom and daily life. He played with toy soldiers (blue Holstein uniforms) in the marital bed until 2 a.m., Madame Krause (in her fifties, native of Holstein) locking the door and joining the maneuvers; heavy toys covered the bed so Catherine “could not move.” He scraped his violin or trained and whipped dogs from 7 a.m. to late (“horrible barking and howling of the dogs whom he cudgeled and thrashed”). One day Catherine found him beating a small English King Charles Spaniel suspended by collar and tail: “I tried to intercede for the poor animal, but this only made him redouble his blows. I returned to my room in tears. After the dog, I was the most miserable creature in the world.” He refused Russian customs (Lent bath: “I would like to see what she can do to me. I am not a child any more”). Catherine: “Never did two minds resemble each other less. We had nothing in common in our tastes or ways of thinking.” Possible phimosis or deep psychological inhibition (orphanhood under martinet tutors, no peers, smallpox scars); nine years Peter “never slept elsewhere than in Catherine’s bed” yet no consummation. Catherine wryly: “It seems to me that I was good for something else.”

Grief compounded isolation. Catherine’s father Christian Augustus died March 1747 (stroke, age 56); she had been forbidden personal letters. Elizabeth via Choglokova: a grand duchess “was not permitted to mourn for more than a week ‘because, after all, your father was not a king.’” Catherine replied it was true he “was not a reigning sovereign, but he was my father.” After a private Elizabeth outburst (accusing betrayal to Prussia, fault for non-consummation, “I know quite well that you alone are to blame if you have no children”), Catherine took a knife to her heart; a maid stopped her (“the knife was not very sharp and would not have penetrated my corset”). A house collapse at Razumovsky’s estate (May 1748) left her bruised and shocked; Elizabeth was displeased she did not treat it as “a mere trifle.” Catherine developed severe headaches and insomnia.

Escape came through reading and secret physical freedom. “In those days I read romances only”; favorite *Tiran the Fair* (princess whose “skin was so transparent that when she drank red wine, you could see it pass down her throat”). Peter preferred “highwaymen eventually hanged.” Later: Sévigné letters, Barre’s ten-volume *General History of Germany* (a volume weekly), Bayle’s *Dictionnaire Historique et Critique* (read cover to cover). Riding became “dominant passion”; she designed a sidesaddle allowing her to ride astride out of sight (“the grooms were happy to keep her secret”; “the more violent this exercise, the better I liked it”). She shot ducks before dawn in man’s clothing at Oranienbaum.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

The path from isolated Grand Duchess to autocrat ran through eighteen years of boredom, surveillance, reading, calculated affairs that produced an heir, and the accumulation of a personal network (Bestuzhev, Panin, Orlov brothers, Dashkova) while Peter’s behavior alienated army, church, and elite.

After Paul’s birth (1754) Elizabeth appropriated the child. Catherine turned to books (Tacitus produced “a singular revolution in my brain”; Montesquieu gave her a “republican soul”; Voltaire’s clarity and Beccaria’s humanity shaped her). Sergei Saltykov gave her first sexual experience and a son; Poniatowski gave cultivated companionship. When Elizabeth collapsed (1757) and Bestuzhev fell (1758), Catherine confronted the empress directly over intercepted letters and survived with composure. Peter’s accession (1761) brought open pro-Prussian betrayal: peace ceding all conquests, “eternal” alliance with Frederick, plans to march Russian troops for Prussia against Austria and to attack Denmark for Holstein, Prussian uniforms and drill imposed on the Guards, mockery of Elizabeth’s funeral, and public insults (“Dura!”) to Catherine.

Peter’s divorce threats and arrest order crystallized the plot. The Orlovs (Gregory her lover since summer 1761, pregnant by him in 1762) controlled key Guards elements; Panin provided counsel and manifesto language; Dashkova supplied information and zeal. Passek’s arrest (27 June) forced immediate action. At 5 a.m. 28 June Alexis Orlov fetched Catherine from Peterhof in a hired carriage (later peasant cart); she wore a black dress. At the Izmailovsky barracks the regiment swore allegiance; Semyonovsky followed. At Kazan Cathedral the archbishop proclaimed her Catherine II and Paul heir. She accepted the colonelcy of the Preobrazhensky, received Potemkin’s sword knot, and led the march toward Peterhof. Peter, drilling Holsteiners at Oranienbaum, learned late, sent Vorontsov with orders, found Kronstadt already loyal, and signed abdication. He was taken to Ropsha under Alexis Orlov. On 6 July, after a drunken dinner quarrel, he was overpowered and killed (“We ourselves know not what we did”). Catherine announced “hemorrhoidal colic”; postmortem found no poison.

By the time Johanna departed and the unconsummated marriage settled into its nine-year pattern, Catherine possessed a repertoire—emotional self-containment, performance of deference, reading of autocratic moods, and unshakeable conviction of future sovereignty—that would allow her to endure surveillance, boredom, and Peter’s provocations while quietly building the networks that Range 02–04 would activate. These seeds are contingent on the specific traumas and models of 1729–1745 yet structural to the position of a foreign Grand Duchess in Elizabeth’s court.

In early 1758, the arrest of Chancellor Bestuzhev (Feb 14) — Catherine’s key political ally — and interception of her correspondence via jeweler Bernardi and the horn player plunged her into acute danger. Poniatowski’s note warned of the arrests (including her former teacher Adadurov and Elagin). “With a dagger in my heart, so to speak,” Catherine dressed for Mass where “most of the faces were as long as my own.” She quickly had valet Vasily Shkurin bring all papers and account books; alone, she burned everything, including the 1744 “Portrait of a Fifteen-Year-Old Philosopher” manuscript. Shkurin was instructed as witness: “You are a witness to the fact that all my papers and accounts are burned.” Stambke (Holstein administrator) relayed Bestuzhev’s secret message that papers were safe; the former chancellor kept communication via brick pile. The vague lèse-majesté manifesto (“attempting to sow discord”) convinced no one; the commission’s absurd orders for twenty-year-old dispatches went unheeded. Bestuzhev was exiled without trial to his estate (until Catherine’s accession three years later). Catherine’s involvement in Peter’s Holstein affairs ended; the empress formally asked Poland to recall Poniatowski. Catherine’s calculation: destroy evidence first, display composure, prepare for worst while gambling on Elizabeth’s attachment to the succession (Ivan VI still alive).

On the last day of Carnival 1758 Catherine made her bold theater gamble. Excluded from Peter’s society and aware he wanted privacy with maid of honor Elizabeth Vorontsova, she ordered a carriage for a Russian play Peter disliked. Peter forbade it in “a fearful passion.” Catherine replied she would walk if necessary and would write the empress complaining that Shuvalov enabled Peter’s rendezvous by keeping her confined, that she was “alone and neglected in my room, hated by the grand duke and disliked by the empress,” and begged to be sent home. The letter was strategic: thanked Elizabeth for past kindnesses; noted children “in your hands and could not be better placed”; “I will do as well as I did before you did me the honor of choosing me”; asked first for waters to recover health, then Germany. Shuvalov relented on the carriage; Catherine attended, Peter rose (unprecedented). The empress did not appear but granted another interview. Catherine later reflected she did not actually want to leave — “she had invested sixteen years... in her ambition to become ‘a queen’” — but the plea undermined any real plan to intimidate or banish her. Publicly, she kept reminding Shuvalov of her wish to have fate decided.

<!-- Range 03 -->
The April 13, 1758 confrontation (week before her 29th birthday) was the climax. Escorted at 1:30 a.m. by Alexander Shuvalov, Catherine found Peter already there. She fell on her knees begging to go home. Elizabeth, “more sad than angry”: “Why do you wish me to send you home? Remember that you have children.” Catherine: “My children are in your hands and could not be better placed. I hope you will not abandon them.” Elizabeth pressed on haughtiness (“You imagine that there is no one so clever as you”) and meddling (Apraksin letters in a basin on the dressing table). Catherine admitted the three letters (interest in the general, congratulations on son, New Year’s greetings) but swore no orders and that Bestuzhev lied about more. “If Bestuzhev says this, he lies.” Elizabeth: “since he is lying about you, I will have him put to torture.” Catherine stood her ground against Peter’s outbursts (“She is dreadfully spiteful and very obstinate”): “I am indeed spiteful to people who advise you to inflict injustice, and that I have become obstinate because I have seen that, by yielding, I have gained nothing but your hostility.” Screens concealed Ivan Shuvalov. Elizabeth wavered, whispering to Catherine: “I have many more things to say to you, but I do not want to make things worse between the two of you than they are already.” Catherine whispered back her desire to open her heart. Dismissed at 3 a.m.; later Shuvalov relayed Elizabeth’s reassurance not to worry and promise of a second private audience. On May 23 Catherine saw her children briefly at 3 p.m.; the second audience followed. Elizabeth accepted the three letters only; asked details of Peter’s mode of life. (Catherine’s Memoirs break off here.) The episode demonstrated Catherine’s readiness with answers, composure under accusation, and ability to exploit Peter’s emotional clumsiness against him.

<!-- Range 03 -->
Catherine inserted in her Memoirs (during this 1758 narrative) a remarkable self-portrait of resilience, ambition, and the court’s temptations: “Whatever happened... ‘I felt myself possessed of sufficient courage either to rise or fall without being carried away by undue pride on the one hand, or being humbled and dispirited on the other.’ ... My natural pride made the idea of being miserable intolerable to me. I used to say to myself that happiness and misery depend on ourselves. If you feel unhappy, rise above it and act so that your happiness may be independent of all outside events. ... I was a true gentleman with a mind more male than female, but, together with this, I was anything but masculine and, combined with the mind and character of a man, I possessed the attractions of a loveable woman.” On emotion vs. morality: “to tempt and to be tempted are closely allied and, in spite of all the finest maxims of morality, whenever emotion has anything to do with the matter, one is already much further involved than one realizes. ... Flight, perhaps, is the only remedy. But there are cases and circumstances, in which flight is impossible. For how can one escape, fly, or turn one’s back, in the middle of a court? ... one cannot hold one’s heart in one’s hand, tightening or relaxing one’s grasp at will.” This candid confession (self-laudatory yet insightful) framed her survival strategy: concealment, performance, and refusal to be broken.

<!-- Range 03 -->
Poniatowski resisted recall by feigning illness and continued secret visits to Catherine’s private pavilion at Oranienbaum while the young court was there (Elizabeth’s court at nearby Peterhof). In July 1758 Peter (half-drunk with Elizabeth Vorontsova) intercepted the “tailor” carriage; Poniatowski was seized, questioned (“whether I had slept with his wife”), lied, confined briefly, then rescued from scandal by Shuvalov. Catherine placated Vorontsova; at a ball Poniatowski danced with her and was invited to Mon Plaisir. Peter welcomed him effusively: “Are you not a great fool not to have been frank with me from the beginning?” Later Peter fetched Catherine from bed (“loose robe over her nightgown and a pair of slippers on her bare feet”), presented Poniatowski, and wrote the vice-chancellor requesting his prompt return (Vorontsova countersigned). Suppers followed; Peter left them: “Well, my children, you do not need me any more, I think.” He repeated the arrangement four times. Peter felt triumph (“for the first time in his life, he felt himself master of a situation”); he spread the scandal openly. Catherine, who “believed in love,” found the ménage degrading and the equating of Poniatowski with “trollop” Vorontsova offensive. On departure she wept more than ever in her life. Years later to Potemkin: “Poniatowski was loving and beloved from 1755 to 1758 and the liaison would have lasted forever if he himself had not got bored by it. On the day of his departure, I was more distressed than I can tell you. I don’t think I ever cried so much in my life.” (Poniatowski’s later memoirs as King of Poland gave a damning but understanding sketch of Peter as “poltroon, a guzzler... not stupid, but mad.”) Counterfactual seed: had the liaison continued without scandal forcing departure, Poniatowski’s presence might have altered 1762 network dynamics or provided a different diplomatic channel; his later Polish throne (imposed by Catherine) was a direct legacy of this relationship.

<!-- Range 03 -->
Paul remained separated in Elizabeth’s household. By 1761 the empress permitted Catherine Sunday drives from Oranienbaum to Peterhof “to spend the day watching her son play in the palace garden.” On return she often stopped at the Vorontsov dacha. The child (born 1754) had been taken immediately; Catherine noted in the letter she “never saw them, although they lived in the same building only a few yards away.” The separation reinforced isolation and political marginalization while binding her fate to the heir.

<!-- Range 03 -->
As Elizabeth’s health deteriorated (1760–61), Catherine assessed futures: wife/adviser to Peter (if he kept her), regent for Paul, or supreme power. “Whatever happened, she would need allies. People were coming to her.” The significant trio: Nikita Panin (42, Bestuzhev protégé, sophisticated ex-ambassador to Sweden and Denmark; returned 1760, appointed tutor to Paul despite Peter’s displeasure; favored removing Peter for boy-emperor Paul with Catherine regent; Catherine told him “I had rather be the mother than the wife of the emperor” but harbored higher ambition; shared Enlightenment tastes (Montesquieu) and Bestuzhev loyalty; safe to discuss ideas; bond without concrete plan). Gregory Orlov (24, five years younger; hero of Zorndorf 1758 — 44k Russians vs. 37k Prussians, 10k+ dead each side, Orlov wounded three times yet held position; grandson of Streltsy survivor pardoned by Peter I for contempt of death; one of five Orlov Guard officer brothers — Ivan, Gregory, Alexis “Scarface” (saber-cut face, most intelligent), Theodore, Vladimir — tight clan, brave, reckless, popular; Gregory “the head of an angel and the body of an athlete”; affair began summer 1761 in secret Vassilevsky Island house; “uncomplicated physical passion”; Catherine pregnant by August 1761; brothers proud; Guards rumors positive). Princess Catherine Dashkova (b. 1744; Elizabeth Vorontsova’s younger sister; met Catherine 1758 age 15; precocious, read Enlightenment authors overnight; married Prince Michael Dashkov 1760; resumed intimacy 1761 at dacha between Peterhof and Oranienbaum; conversations on books/politics; “not two women in the empire except the grand duchess and myself who occupied themselves at all in serious reading”; convinced Catherine was “savior of the nation”; supplied gossip from Peter/Vorontsova circle while adoring; stood up to Peter at dinner — challenged disproportionate punishment, “your august aunt still lives and sits on the throne”; Peter stuck out tongue; Catherine delighted, “gained me a high degree of notoriety”). Catherine compartmentalized: Panin saw levelheaded politician; Orlov the warm-blooded woman; Dashkova the philosopher. “Each of them knew a different Catherine.” All Russians — useful for the German-born grand duchess.

<!-- Range 03 -->
Peter’s behavior accelerated the crisis. He openly betrayed Russian plans to Frederick via English ambassador Sir Robert Keith (copies via Berlin); discussed ending the war, eternal Prussian alliance, using Russian armies for Frederick against current allies, then war on Denmark for Holstein; planned to divorce Catherine and marry Vorontsova; imposed Prussian drill and uniforms. “The king my master.” Everyone expected his first act as emperor would be peace with Frederick. Elizabeth’s decline made the succession “all-important.” Catherine’s resolve hardened: invest in allies while maintaining position through calculated risk and performance.

<!-- Range 03 -->
Affairs functioned as tools and outlets in the power vacuum: Saltykov (post-1754) had secured the heir and political favor via Bestuzhev; Poniatowski (1755–58) brought cultivated European courtship and diplomatic entrée (British ambassador Hanbury-Williams) but ended in degrading public ménage that threatened reputation more than Shuvalov hostility. Orlov (1761) was different — physical, military, tied to the Guards whose support would prove decisive; pregnancy occurred as Elizabeth faded and Peter’s reign loomed. Catherine’s calculation (evident in composure, paper-burning, letter rhetoric, and selective alliance-building) balanced survival, emotional need, and long-term positioning. Counterfactual seed: had Elizabeth sent Catherine home after the 1758 confrontation (as Catherine gambled she would not), the 1762 coup under Catherine’s leadership would have been impossible; Peter III might have ruled longer (or been removed by others), Holstein/Danish war pursued, Prussian alliance entrenched, and Russian expansion or internal reform delayed or altered. The confrontations demonstrably built the “sufficient courage either to rise or fall” she claimed; resilience under surveillance and humiliation prepared the nerve for the June 1762 action.

The eighteen years produced not only an heir but the political education and character traits that enabled the later rise. Isolation after the 1754 birth of Paul (Elizabeth took the swaddled infant immediately; Catherine left on the floor “with only Madame Vladislavova as company... to which I had not the strength to crawl”; denied water and linen change for hours; gift of 100,000 rubles plus “a poor little necklace with earrings and two miserable rings which I would have been ashamed to give my maids”; money borrowed back because Peter complained) forced Catherine to books and calculation. Post-birth winter 1754–55: Tacitus *Annals* “caused a singular revolution in my brain... I began to take a gloomier view of things and to look for deeper and more basic causes”; Montesquieu’s *L’Esprit des Lois* led her to attribute to herself a “republican soul” (even as autocrat she later sought “efficiency... guided by intelligence” and called it “the Breviary of every sovereign of common sense”); Voltaire supplied wit and the model of enlightened despotism possible if “reasonable.” These readings tempered autocracy with reason while sharpening her sense of structural causes beneath court intrigue.

Early affairs supplied both personal awakening and political lessons. Sergei Saltykov (handsome, “making the seduction of women his life’s purpose”) courted via Choglokov island hunt (1752); a westerly gale marooned the party; he declared “heaven itself was favoring his suit.” Catherine: “I had thought that I would be able to govern both his passion and mine, but now I realized that this was going to be difficult and perhaps impossible.” Consummated August/September 1752. Maria Choglokova, after arranging Peter’s sexual initiation with widow Mme Groot (to resolve “little imperfection” or inhibitions), took Catherine aside: “I do not doubt that in your heart you have a preference for one man over another. I leave you to choose between Sergei Saltykov and Lev Naryshkin... You will see that I shall not put difficulties in your way.” Thereafter the Choglokovs “stood aside when Sergei Saltykov entered Catherine’s bedroom.” Peter guessed and treated it as “a joke on the foolish Choglokov.” To keep Saltykov, Catherine overcame seven years of enmity and sent Bestuzhev “a few words that would allow him to believe that I was less hostile to him than before.” The chancellor was delighted, offered to “make Madame Vladislavova as gentle as a lamb,” and advised Saltykov. Two miscarriages preceded Paul; Saltykov was posted to Sweden (announcing the birth) then Hamburg; the affair ended with his cooling and indiscretions.

Stanislaus Poniatowski (introduced 1755 by Hanbury-Williams) offered cultivated companionship: 23, virgin (“severe education had kept me out of all vulgar debauchery”), “my whole life was devoted to her” after seeing her in “a little gown of white satin with a light trimming of lace, threaded with a pink ribbon.” Catherine at 25: “black hair, a complexion of dazzling whiteness, large, round, blue, expressive eyes... a mouth that seemed to ask for kisses... graceful, supple, and yet of the most dignified nobility... a laugh as merry as her temperament.” Lev Naryshkin arranged secret visits (meowing at the door, disguises as musicians). Poniatowski represented the Enlightenment world her reading had prepared her for.

Seeds of signature decisions appeared: information gathering (Vladislavova “a living archive... from her, I learned more about what had happened in Russia over the past hundred years than anywhere else”); image management (dressing simply for private balls to please Elizabeth; “if a gown attracted everyone’s praise, she never wore it again”; later parties to “disarm my enemies”); concealment and pragmatism (secret astride riding; helping Peter with Holstein affairs despite contempt while building independent networks; cautious response to Bestuzhev’s risky co-ruler ukase draft as “premature”); preference for reason over force (“yield only to gentleness and reason—and to resist all pressure”); political calculation (reconciling with Bestuzhev for Saltykov’s career, confronting Brockdorff over unjust arrests). Resilience: endured tooth extractions shattering jawbone, Moscow fires, repeated illnesses, public humiliation, yet “I resolved to defend myself” after Paul’s birth (“to make those who had caused me so much suffering understand that I could not be offended and mistreated with impunity”).

These years transformed the lonely grand duchess into a woman who could navigate court intrigues, maintain position despite Peter, and prepare a network while Peter’s behavior (Holstein troops, insults, Prussian worship) alienated the Guards and elite. The “republican soul” reading and early lovers (personal/political) were direct preparation for the 1762 mechanics and later style.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Grand Duchess Years and Path to Power (1745–1762):** Eighteen years of surveillance, intellectual self-education, calculated liaisons (Saltykov for heir and access; Poniatowski for cultivated companionship amid isolation), motherhood (Paul 1754 taken by Elizabeth immediately; later limited Sunday visits), and political crises. Key turning points from Range 03: Bestuzhev arrest and paper-burning (1758); theater gamble and manipulative letter to force audience (Carnival 1758); midnight confrontation with Elizabeth (Apr 13 1758) where Catherine defended herself on knees, exposed Peter’s spite, and survived with composure (“With a dagger in my heart...”); degrading ménage à quatre that ended Poniatowski liaison but demonstrated Peter’s weakness; formation of compartmentalized network (Panin as Paul’s tutor favoring regency, Gregory Orlov as lover and Guards bridge with pregnancy by Aug 1761, Dashkova as zealous informant and defender). Peter’s pro-Prussian betrayals, divorce threats, and self-destructive policies during Elizabeth’s decline clarified options. Catherine maintained position through performance and selective alliances while Peter alienated the army and elite. The phase produced the resolve (“sufficient courage either to rise or fall”) and infrastructure for the 1762 coup. (<!-- Range 03 --> detailed verbatim from Massie confrontation, ménage, network sections; early phases from prior ranges.)

**Accession and Consolidation (1762–1766):** Coronation in Moscow (22 Sept 1762) with personal crowning; lavish rewards to coup participants (18,000 serfs gifted to supporters in first month alone); manifesto and policy gestures (noble service freedom retained; salt tax relief). Early friction with Dashkova (expected co-ruler status) and Panin (imperial council proposal buried). Industrial serfs crisis: August 8, 1762 decree forbidding purchase of serfs for factories/mines without land and requiring agreed wages; strikes followed, troops (Vyazemsky) sent with cannon in Urals, but Catherine instructed: “In a word, do everything you think proper for the satisfaction of the peasants; but take suitable precautions so that the peasants should not imagine that their managers will be afraid of them in the future. If you find managers guilty of great inhumanity, you may punish them publicly, but if someone has exacted more work than is right, you may punish him secretly; thus you will not give the common people grounds to lose their proper dutifulness.” Serfdom reality: chattel sales at < prize hunting dog prices (males 200–500 rubles); families bartered/gambled; ads e.g. “For sale: a girl of sixteen trained in lace-making... Anyone wishing to buy an entire family...” Catherine intellectually opposed (earlier plan: free serfs on estate sales) but rewarded coup backers with 18,000 crown/state peasants. <!-- Range 04 -->

**Enlightenment Experiment and Limits (1767–1774):** Nakaz and Legislative Commission (1767–68/69) — 564 delegates (nobles 142, towns 209, state peasants 56, Cossacks 44, non-Russian tribes 54; serfs unrepresented) heard grievances on Volga trip and in Moscow/St. Petersburg but produced no code; serfdom debates suppressed after nobles shouted down proposals. Nakaz proclaimed “Russia is a European state”; “The sovereign was absolute... for there is no authority but that which centers in his single person that can act with a vigor proportionate to such a vast dominion.” Borrowed 294 articles from Montesquieu, 108 from Beccaria; condemned torture verbatim: “The use of torture is contrary to sound judgment and common sense... Humanity itself cries out against it, and demands it to be utterly abolished.” “What right can give anyone authority to inflict torture upon a citizen when it is still unknown whether he is innocent or guilty?... The rack, therefore, is a sure method of condemning an innocent person whose constitution is weak, and of acquitting the guilty...” Serf language tentative after excisions: “Since the Law of Nature commands Us to labor... to avoid reducing the people to a state of slavery, unless urgent occasion indispensably obliges us to do it... Of whatever kind subjugation may be, the civil laws should prevent the abuse of slavery...” Commission: Volga voyage revealed diversity (“There are in this city twenty different peoples who do not resemble each other at all. We have, nevertheless, to design a garment to fit them all.”); delegates debated noble/merchant privileges, serf property rights; Bibikov marshal; titles debate (“Catherine the Great” most votes; she refused “All Wise”); prorogued Dec 1768/Jan 1769 on Turkish war. First Turkish War (1768–74) triggered by Polish dissidents + French/Turkish fears: Rumyantsev victories (Larga, Kagul 1770); fleet to Mediterranean via Britain; Chesme Bay (June 1770) annihilation of Turkish fleet (15 ships of line burned by fireships, 9,000 Turkish dead); First Partition of Poland (1772): Russia 36,000 sq mi (White Russia/Belorussia, 1.8M Orthodox), Prussia smallest slice for Baltic link, Austria Galicia; Catherine: “Why shouldn’t we both take our share?” Smallpox inoculation (Catherine first Oct 1768 via Dimsdale; Paul next; 20k+ by 1780). 1771 Moscow plague: 55k–120k dead; Archbishop Ambrosius lynched for hiding icon; Orlov dispatched, imposed quarantines/humanely, deaths dropped with cold. Pugachev rebellion (1773–75) — most serious internal challenge: illiterate Don Cossack Emelyan Pugachev (ex-soldier, deserter) proclaimed “Tsar Peter III” at Yaitsk Sept 1773; manifesto: “I give eternal freedom to you and your children... You will no longer work for a lord... If God permits me to reach St. Petersburg, I shall put my wicked wife Catherine into a convent. Then I will free all the peasants and exterminate the nobles down to the last man.” Attracted Bashkirs, Kalmucks, factory serfs (44 Urals foundries seized), peasants; atrocities (nobles flayed/burned/hanged, wives raped then killed, children mutilated); camp “married” Yustina Kuznetsova as “Her Imperial Majesty”; played tsar at Berda with titles for lieutenants (Count Orlov etc.). Catherine initially to Voltaire: “this impudent Pugachev” “a common highway robber” “Marquis de Pugachev who is giving me a little trouble in the Urals.” Then empowered Bibikov; no-torture interrogation; after defeat, Mirovich-style secret trial? public beheading of Pugachev + amnesty/oblivion. Post-Pugachev hardening: no further serf-reform rhetoric. “A general emancipation from the unbearable and cruel yoke will not ensue... if we do not agree to the diminution of cruelty... then, even against our will, they themselves will seize it sooner or later.” <!-- Range 04 -->

**Potemkin Era and Imperial Apogee (1774–1791):** <!-- Range 05 --> Potemkin (1739–1791) as lover, political partner, and viceroy of New Russia (governor-general, College of War president, field marshal). Early passion 1774: Catherine’s “Sincere Confession” detailing prior lovers (Saltykov necessity, Poniatowski loving, Orlov boredom, Vasilchikov desperation); “my heart is loath to be without love even for a single hour”; “If you want to keep me forever, then show as much friendship as love”; “dear husband” / “devoted wife” language and possible secret marriage June 8 1774 (St. Sampsonovsky, 5 witnesses; supporting evidence in Joseph II 1782 remark “she could not easily get rid of him” and Ségur “sacred and inalienable rights”). Rapid ascent: Privy Council, New Russia, diamond sword, St. Andrew; 100k rubles/year + jewels/furs. Separation after ~2.5 years (1776): repeated quarrels (“we quarrel about power, never about love”); Potemkin “angry wolf” moods, sensitivity to “blind eye”; Catherine “stop your raging so that my natural tenderness might return, otherwise you will be the death of me”; Potemkin chooses power over exclusive intimacy (“I shall go through fire for you”) yet retains supreme influence (“I am unto death your faithful slave”). Physical passion fades but collaboration endures. Potemkin builds southern empire: Kherson (shipyard/port, stone buildings, fortress, barracks, 64-gun ship 1780); Sebastopol (1783 deep protected bay fleet base); Ekaterinoslav (planned cathedral/university/factories on Dnieper bend); Nikolaev (1789). Army reforms: loose comfortable uniforms, wide breeches, no hair powder/curling (“Is that a solider’s business? They have no personal valets”). Black Sea Fleet command. Crimea annexation (July 1783): Potemkin proposal “The acquisition of the Crimea can make us neither stronger nor richer, but it will ensure our peace”; peaceful, personal malaria cost. 1787 Crimean journey (Jan–July, 4000+ miles, sledge then 7 red/gold galleys with brocade bedroom/library): guests Joseph II (incognito Falkenstein), Ségur, Ligne (“pleasantest company”), Fitzherbert; awkward Stanislaus reunion at Kaniev (“It was thirty years since I’d seen him... we found each other changed”; “The king bores me”; he claims second “hat”/crown); Kherson launch of Vladimir + St. Joseph; Sebastopol review (“the most beautiful port I have ever seen... The truth is that it is necessary to be here to believe what I see”); fleet salute; real development (villages, cattle, plantings, cities) validated by eyewitnesses Joseph/Ségur/Ligne (debunks later “Potemkin villages” cardboard myth). Catherine: “you serve me and I am grateful... you rapped your enemies across the knuckles”; gifts Prince of Tauris title + pearls. System of acknowledged favorites as buffer/companionship (Zavadovsky buffer then ousted by Potemkin ultimatum; Zorich 10mo; Rimsky-Korsakov “Pyrrhus” caught with Bruce; Lanskoy 1780–84 “angel... kind, gay, honest”, maternal affection, 1784 diphtheria death prostrates Catherine “I thought that I myself would die... my best, dearest, and kindest friend”; Yermolov 17mo dismissed after criticizing Potemkin; Mamonov “beyond price... inexhaustible source of gaiety”, 1789 betrayal with pregnant Scherbatova, Catherine magnanimous 100k+estate “God grant them happiness” but “I cannot express how I have suffered”). Potemkin nieces liaisons (Varvara “goddess”, Alexandra devoted, Ekaterina). Art “voracity” (4,000 paintings); building “a sickness like being addicted to alcohol”; Bronze Horseman 1782 (“To Peter the First, from Catherine the Second”).

**Twilight and Reaction (1791–1796):** <!-- Range 05 --> Potemkin’s death (5 Oct 1791) leaves Catherine without principal partner (“more than a real mother to me”). French Revolution seeds shift from initial openness (Estates-General/Bastille sympathy) toward censorship/Voltaire confiscation/1796 press controls (Radishchev *Journey* 1790 “worse than Pugachev”, exile). Final partitions of Poland (1793, 1795) after 1791 constitution and Kościuszko: join Prussia/Austria to erase state; Russia gains Belarus/Ukraine/Lithuania, Pale groundwork, confessional burdens. Paul excluded from power (Gatchina Prussian-drill private army, “inaction to which I am condemned”) yet succession ambiguous (Catherine considers Alexander; Paul dictates will to secure papers). Catherine maintains routine/sociability until stroke 5 Nov 1796 in closet; dies 6 Nov. Paul succeeds, reburies Peter III beside her. Post-Pugachev hardening seeds (no further serf-elimination talk; focus expansion/culture). (Verbatim letters, eyewitnesses, and mechanics from Range 05 cache.)

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military / Religious Style and Methods

Catherine combined voracious reading (Montesquieu “prayer book”, Beccaria, Voltaire, Tacitus) with pragmatic autocracy suited to a vast, multi-ethnic, serf-based empire. She preferred “undermine what cannot be overthrown,” gathering information (Nakaz, Commission petitions, ambassador reports, Volga voyage) before acting. Explicit: “Monsieur Diderot... you work only on paper which accepts anything, is smooth and flexible... while I, poor empress, work on human skin, which is far more sensitive and touchy.” Image projection central: letters to Voltaire (“ardent disciple”; “the Semiramis of the North”); Diderot library purchase (16k pounds + salary); first smallpox inoculation (Oct 1768) as example (“My objective was, through my example, to save from death the multitude...”); Volga trip to impress Old Russia and Europe (“Perhaps at the moment when you least expect it, you will receive a letter from some corner of Asia”); refused “All Wise” but pleased “Catherine the Great” received most votes; Manifesto of Silence to end gossip. On absolutism: “The sovereign was absolute... for there is no authority but that which centers in his single person...” Yet accepted “fundamental laws” (traditions, religion, noble privileges) defining “moderate autocracy.” Military: close daily war councils + encouragement letters; relied on Rumyantsev (Larga/Kagul), Orlov/Greig (Chesme fireships), but recognized limits (“at the risk of repeating myself... Serf policy: intellectual opposition + pragmatic rewards and force (Vyazemsky public/secret punishment distinction); post-Pugachev “A general emancipation... will not ensue”. ## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Catherine suffered a stroke in her water closet at the Winter Palace on 5 November 1796 and never regained consciousness; she died at 9:45 p.m. on 6 November. Paul (at Gatchina) was notified immediately; Zubov informed him. Paul’s manifesto asserted his rights. He had Peter III’s coffin opened at Nevsky Monastery; the remains were processed beside Catherine’s; both interred 5 December in the Cathedral of St. Peter and St. Paul (Alexis Orlov forced to carry Paul’s crown behind Peter’s coffin). No immediate policy rupture announced, but Paul reversed some of Catherine’s inner circle (Zubov dismissed) and later restored strict primogeniture. The empire’s institutions and southern gains survived the transition intact.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Catherine extended Peter the Great’s project: a Baltic window became a Black Sea window (Crimea, Sevastopol, Odessa, grain export); imported technology became imported philosophy, art, architecture, and education. The Bronze Horseman (1782) and her self-epitaph asserted equality with Peter. The empire reached its greatest contiguous extent under the Romanovs; the Pale framework, confessional hierarchies, and noble corporate rights (1785 Charter) shaped 19th-century Russia. Cultural Westernization produced the golden age of Pushkin, Tolstoy, etc. The failure to address serfdom structurally (post-Pugachev hardening) contributed to the crises that produced 1861 emancipation and later revolution. In European memory she embodied enlightened absolutism at its most successful and most contradictory.

This description is, of course, both idealized and excessively modest. She always refused extravagant titles, whether from the Legislative Assembly in 1764, which wished to name her Catherine the Great; from Voltaire, who filled his letters with flowery tributes; or from Grimm, who called her Catherine the Great in a letter in 1788. Replying to Grimm, she wrote, “I beg you no longer to call me Catherine the Great, because … my name is Catherine II.” It was after her death that Russians began speaking of her as “Catherine the Great.”

She was a majestic figure in the age of monarchy; the only woman to equal her on a European throne was Elizabeth I of England. In the history of Russia, she and Peter the Great tower in ability and achievement over the other fourteen tsars and empresses of the three-hundred-year Romanov dynasty. Catherine carried Peter’s legacy forward. He had given Russia a “window on the West” on the Baltic coast, building there a city that he made his capital. Catherine opened another window, this one on the Black Sea; Sebastopol and Odessa were its jewels. Peter imported technology and governing institutions to Russia; Catherine brought European moral, political, and judicial philosophy, literature, art, architecture, sculpture, medicine, and education. Peter created a Russian navy and organized an army that defeated one of the finest soldiers in Europe; Catherine assembled the greatest art gallery in Europe, hospitals, schools, and orphanages. Peter shaved off the beards and truncated the long robes of his leading noblemen; Catherine persuaded them to be inoculated against smallpox. Peter made Russia a great power; Catherine magnified this power, and advanced the nation toward a culture that, during the century that followed, produced, among others, Derzhavin, Pushkin, Lermontov, Gogol, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Turgenev, Chekov, Borodin, Rimsky-Korsakov, Mussorgsky, Glinka, Tchaikovsky, Stravinsky, Petipa, and Diaghilev. These artists and their work were a part of Catherine’s legacy to Russia.

> Day before yesterday, on February ninth, it was fifty years since I arrived with my mother in Moscow. I doubt if there are ten people living today in St. Petersburg who remember. ## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources details (grounded in Range 06 back matter):** Catherine’s Memoirs form the primary source for the first half of her life (1729–1762, up to age twenty-nine under Elizabeth); they begin with her earliest recollections and continue to 1758. Catherine wrote them in French. At least four English translations exist: Alexander Herzen (London, 1859); Katharine Anthony (New York and London, 1927); Dominique Maroger (original French ed. Paris) translated by Moura Budberg (New York, 1955); and Mark Cruse and Hilde Hoogenboom (Modern Library, 2005, which put the reminiscences in correct chronological sequence, which Catherine and previous translators never achieved). Massie used the first three of these; they are cited as Memoirs (Maroger/Budberg), Herzen, and Memoirs (Anthony). “Naturally, her memoirs display the subjective perspective of any memoir writer; even so, they are invaluable.”

Correspondence is central: with Voltaire (flattery and political exchange); with Grimm (informal correspondence of more than fifteen hundred letters; Grimm became her agent in Paris after Diderot and acquired the Houdon Voltaire statue for her; she felt more at ease with Grimm); with Potemkin (intimate, sometimes raw; Massie drew heavily on Douglas Smith’s translations in *Love and Conquest*). Princess Dashkova’s *Memoirs* (two volumes, London: Henry Colburn, 1840) provide participant account of the coup and later tensions. Ambassadorial reports and dispatches (Philippe de Ségur, James Harris, etc.) supply court texture and foreign views. Legislation (Nakaz text, provincial statutes) and contemporary published accounts (Masson’s *Secret Memoirs*, Radishchev’s *Journey*) round out the base. Smith’s *The Pearl* is also used for serfdom and performing-arts descriptions.

**Bias notes on self-fashioning in Catherine’s writings:** The Memoirs are retrospective and selective (focus on early years; written decades after events). The self-epitaph is “both idealized and excessively modest.” Letters to Grimm “are performance for European audience.” Catherine herself articulated pragmatic image management: “What I despair of overthrowing, I undermine.” She examined circumstances, consulted “the enlightened part of the people,” and issued orders only when “convinced in advance of good approval,” then observed the “blind obedience” that followed. “Before I became what I am today, I was thirty-three years the same as other people. It is only thirty years since I have become what they are not, and that teaches one to live.” Her collecting admitted “voracity”: “It is not love of art… I am a glutton.” Building was “a sickness like being addicted to alcohol.”

- **John Paul Jones incident (1789):** Three versions. Accusation: ten-year-old girl (daughter of German dairy woman) claimed Jones locked her in, knocked her down, assaulted her; she ran home. Ségur’s defense (in memoirs and at time): the girl came asking about linen to mend, made “indecent gestures”; Jones gave money and dismissed her; she then ripped her dress, screamed, and fell into mother’s arms. Jones to Potemkin: “The charge against me is an unworthy imposture… I love women, I confess, and the pleasures that one only obtains from that sex, but to get such things by force is horrible to me… if the girl in question has not passed through hands other than mine, she is still a virgin.” To chief of police: “The accusation against me is false. It was invented by the mother of a depraved girl who came to my house several times and with whom I have often badine,* always giving her money, but whose virginity I have positively not taken. I thought her to be several years older… each time she came to my house she lent herself very willingly…” Supported by three witnesses’ affidavits (girl left quietly, no blood/bruises/tears). “In any case, if not a crime, this encounter between a restless, lonely, middle-aged man and an underage girl was tawdry. Nobody knew exactly what had happened, but Jones was ostracized… Criminal charges… dropped, but the offer of a command… also evaporated.” Footnote: “badiner” can mean “played with,” “bantered,” “joked,” “toyed,” or “trifled”; “No one will ever know now how intimate this encounter became. Jones, however, was not denying that something had happened.”

- **Potemkin death and character (1791):** Detailed deathbed: malaria symptoms, refusal of quinine initially, “I’m not going to recover… God’s will be done… I am not a bad man and not the evil genius of our mother, the Empress Catherine, as has been said.” Last words “Matushka, oh how sick I am!”; died in field on Persian carpet with copper five-kopeck coin over eye (no gold found); Catherine: “Now I have no one left on whom I can rely. How can anybody replace Potemkin? Everything will be different now. He was a true nobleman.” Ligne’s eyewitness portrait of Potemkin at Ochakov (verbatim long passage): “I here behold a Commander in Chief who looks idle and is always busy; who has no other desk than his knees… What then is his magic? Genius, natural abilities, an excellent memory, artifice without craft, the art of conquering every heart…” Reconciles legends (subterranean marble hall, theater, harem) with the man’s actual eccentricity and effectiveness.

- **Coup mechanics and other:** “The whole thing is rather a miracle than a pre-arranged plan” (Catherine to Poniatowski). Second Turkish War “had been Potemkin’s war; he had borne responsibility for strategy, command, and logistics. Catherine had sustained him. She was the more stable… Neither could have achieved victory without the other.”

**Massie’s method vs. other scholars (Range 06 Biblio + narrative):** Massie’s approach is narrative biography emphasizing personal psychology, court texture, relationships (especially Catherine–Potemkin partnership), contingencies, and verbatim primary texture from Memoirs and letters (with explicit awareness of self-fashioning). He thanks libraries (Yale Sterling, NYPL) and Douglas Smith for translations and serf/performing-arts material; acknowledges pleasure in the subject after eight years. Selected Bibliography lists: Catherine Memoirs (four ed./trans.); Dashkova Memoirs; Madariaga, *Russia in the Age of Catherine the Great* (1981) and *Catherine the Great* (1990); Dixon, *Catherine the Great* (2001); Smith, *Love and Conquest* (2005) and *The Pearl* (2008); Montefiore, *Prince of Princes: The Life of Potemkin*; plus ambassadors, legislation, Radishchev, etc. Complements Madariaga (legal/institutional), Dixon (concise scholarly), Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II (structural, empire/periphery, serfdom, state capacity), and Freeze (social history over great-woman narrative). Massie excels at restoring the human scale and daily mechanics of power that structural accounts background.

**Overall legacy assessment (Range 06 Coda):** Catherine and Peter tower over other Romanovs. She refused “Great” in life; title posthumous. Epitaph and 1794 letter to Grimm show self-assessment as sociable, hardworking, art-loving, still childlike in play and laughter. “She was the greatest collector and patron of art in the history of Europe.” The guillotine discussion and French Revolution reaction illustrate limits of her Enlightenment stance once order was threatened.
