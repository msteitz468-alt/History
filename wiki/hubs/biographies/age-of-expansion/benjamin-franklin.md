# Benjamin Franklin — Detailed Biography

> **Summary & network:** [[actors/benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin]] · **Biography hub:** [[hubs/biographies/biographies-hub|Biographies Hub]]

A graduate-level analytic study of Franklin's formation, the mechanics of his signature decisions, his method as printer-scientist-diplomat, and the balance of contingency and structure in a life that helped invent the American type. Synthesized from [[sources/isaacson-benjamin-franklin-2003|Isaacson, *Benjamin Franklin: An American Life* (2003)]].

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Franklin was, in Isaacson's phrase, "a product of lineage rather than of land": the youngest son of a youngest son for five generations, descended from a line of English freeholder "franklins" — dissenting, nonconformist, but pragmatic artisans from Ecton, Northamptonshire. His father Josiah, a tallow chandler who emigrated to Boston in 1683, distilled the Puritan fusion of religious and economic motive ("industriousness is next to godliness") without passing on its introspective spiritual anxiety. Josiah's favorite maxim — "Seest thou a man diligent in his calling, he shall stand before Kings" (Proverbs 22:29) — Franklin would literalize, wryly noting at 78 that he had stood before five actual kings.

Born January 17, 1706, Franklin was briefly destined for the ministry (Boston Latin School, 1714), but Josiah abruptly withdrew him — ostensibly over cost, more likely judging him unsuited to the clergy. After a year at a writing academy (where he excelled at prose and failed at arithmetic), he was apprenticed at twelve to his brother James, a printer, on an unusually long nine-year indenture. This was the decisive formative environment: James's *New-England Courant* (1721) was colonial America's first genuinely independent newspaper, and it was there, in 1722, that the sixteen-year-old slipped his anonymous "Silence Dogood" essays under the shop door. Isaacson argues that the *Autobiography*'s portrait of James as a harsh tyrant is retrospective mythmaking — "he deserved better" — and that James was in fact Franklin's most important journalistic influence.

Two self-taught methods formed in these years defined him for life. His **prose style** he trained by rewriting *Spectator* essays from memory and comparing them to the original. His **argumentative style** shifted, after losing debates to a friend, from "abrupt contradiction" to gentle Socratic questioning — a technique he later codified into the etiquette of his Junto club and deployed across a lifetime of persuasion. His father's redirection of him from poetry was characteristically practical: Josiah "discouraged me by ridiculing my performances and telling me verse-makers were generally beggars; so I escaped being a poet, most probably a very bad one." Franklin's later attribution of his political temperament to James's severity — "I fancy his harsh and tyrannical treatment of me might be a means of impressing me with that aversion to arbitrary power that has stuck to me through my whole life" — Isaacson reads as exactly this kind of retrospective self-fashioning: emotionally resonant, chronologically convenient, and unfair to the brother who was in fact his teacher. What was contingent here was the redirection from pulpit to print shop; what was structural was the dissenting-artisan inheritance that made self-improvement a moral duty rather than mere ambition.

His religious development in these years was equally formative and equally pragmatic. Raised Calvinist, he drifted young toward deism — "the arguments of the deists which were quoted to be refuted appeared to me much stronger than the refutations" — but then applied to belief itself the utilitarian test that would govern his whole life. Seeing friends whom he had converted to freethinking behave badly, "I began to suspect that this doctrine, though it might be true, was not very useful," and settled into a morally-fortified deism that valued virtue and good works over grace. The self-aware irony that is his signature note appears already in the young man who, abandoning vegetarianism to eat fish, reflected: "So convenient a thing it is to be a reasonable creature, since it enables one to find or make a reason for everything one has a mind to do."

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Franklin's rise was the self-conscious construction of a public self — "the most interesting thing that Franklin invented," Isaacson argues, "was himself." At seventeen he ran away from Boston (September 1723), arriving in Philadelphia with a Dutch dollar and a shilling, buying the three puffy rolls that became the founding image of his own myth. A false promise from Governor William Keith sent him to London (1724–26), where he worked in printing houses and wrote a hard-deist tract he later suppressed as an "erratum." Returning in 1726 aboard ship, he drafted a "Plan for Future Conduct" — his first self-improvement credo.

By 1728 he had his own print shop; in 1729 he bought and relaunched *The Pennsylvania Gazette*; in 1732 he began *Poor Richard's Almanack*. His pride in self-made enterprise was intense and enduring — the first five shillings a customer paid at his own shop "gave me more pleasure than any crown I have since earned." He built, deliberately, a full-stack media operation — production (franchised partner shops in other cities), content (the Gazette, the almanac, pseudonymous essays), and distribution (later leveraged by the postmastership). Crucially, he cultivated the *appearance* of industry as much as the substance: "I took care not only to be in reality industrious and frugal," he wrote, "but to avoid all appearances of the contrary," carting his own paper through the streets to be seen doing it. His "Apology for Printers" (1731) states the press philosophy underlying the enterprise: "when men differ in opinion, both sides ought equally to have the advantage of being heard by the public; and… when Truth and Error have fair play, the former is always an overmatch for the latter."

In 1727 he founded the **Junto**, a mutual-improvement club of tradesmen that became the engine of Philadelphia's civic institutions and the practical expression of his conviction that "the good men may do separately is small compared with what they may do collectively." His self-improvement was systematized in the famous thirteen-virtue "Moral Perfection Project," which Isaacson cautions should be read with the wry distance of the seventy-nine-year-old who recorded it, not as naive Babbittry — the man who wrote of the last virtue, humility, "even if I could conceive that I had completely overcome it, I would probably be proud of my humility." He retired from the printing business in 1748 at forty-two — the exact midpoint of his eighty-four-year life — turning operations over to a foreman for half the profits, freeing himself for science, then politics.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

### Public Citizen and Scientist (1731–1756)
From the Junto flowed a chain of voluntary institutions: the Library Company (1731, America's first subscription library), the Union Fire Company (1736), the American Philosophical Society (1743), a volunteer militia (1747), the Academy that became the University of Pennsylvania (1749/1751), and the Pennsylvania Hospital (funded by his invented matching-grant mechanism). This associational civic culture — voluntary, non-governmental organization for public goods — is what Tocqueville would later identify as quintessentially American. In parallel came the **electrical experiments** (1744–1752): the single-fluid theory, the conservation of charge, the kite, and the lightning rod. The French confirmation of his "sentry-box" experiment at Marly (May 1752) made him internationally famous; the Royal Society awarded him the Copley Medal in 1753. Isaacson's verdict: "no Galileo or Newton" but a practical experimenter and popularizer whose theory was nonetheless fundamental. His politics sharpened in the same period — the [[events/american-revolution|imperial]] federalism of the **Albany Plan of Union** (1754), accompanied by the "Join, or Die" cartoon (America's first), and the bitter tax fight with the Penn proprietors that ended his self-cast role as mediator.

### The Imperial Agent's Radicalization (1757–1775)
Franklin spent most of these eighteen years in London as colonial agent, lodging with the Stevenson family on Craven Street — a surrogate household to which he was demonstrably warmer than to his own. He arrived a proud British royalist; Lord Granville's blunt lesson that colonial assemblies had no independent authority ("You Americans have wrong ideas of the nature of your constitution… [the governors' instructions are] the law of the land") was, he wrote, a "rude surprise." His long fixation on stripping the Penns of their proprietorship — which Edmund Morgan called a "prolonged fit of political blindness" — was, Isaacson argues, driven by wounded pride more than strategy; a leaked letter describing Thomas Penn's "triumphing, laughing insolence, such as a low jockey might do when a purchaser complained that he had cheated him in a horse" permanently poisoned the relationship and crippled Franklin as a lobbyist.

His radicalization was gradual and reactive, propelled less by ideology than by a sequence of personal humiliations. His pivotal 1766 testimony before Parliament on the Stamp Act had at first restored his standing and made him America's leading spokesman; there he issued the prophetic warning that troops sent to America "will not find a rebellion; they may indeed make one." But the deeper break came through rejection: Hillsborough's rebuff (1768), the icy confrontation over his agency (1771), and above all the **Cockpit** (January 29, 1774), where Solicitor General Wedderburn delivered an hour-long "furious Philippic," declaring that Franklin "will henceforth call it a libel to be called a man of letters," while Franklin stood, as an eyewitness recorded, "conspicuously erect, without the smallest movement of any part of his body," in blue Manchester velvet. The next day he was dismissed as deputy postmaster. The **Hutchinson letters affair** (1772–74) — his leak of a governor's letters, intended to show that bad advice rather than innate hostility drove British policy, but published by Samuel and John Adams to inflame it — had produced "the opposite of what Franklin had intended." By March 1775, after a last, tearful day with Joseph Priestley, he sailed home a revolutionary.

### Independence and the French Alliance (1775–1778)
At sixty-nine, Franklin was the oldest delegate to the Second Continental Congress. His private break with his loyalist son William at Trevose (May 1775) ran, Isaacson argues, as the personal analog of the coming public declaration. His fury at the war was captured in the deliberately circulated but never-sent letter to his old friend, the MP William Strahan: "You and I were long friends: You are now my enemy, and I am, Yours, B. Franklin." He served on the Declaration's drafting committee, made its single most consequential edit (see §4), and consoled Jefferson over Congress's cuts with the "John Thompson, hatter" parable. At the August 1776 signing — an act of treason — he supplied the canonical bon mot, answering Hancock's call to unity: "Yes, we must, indeed, all hang together, or most assuredly we shall all hang separately." To Lord Howe, a former negotiating partner, he wrote his most famous metaphor for the rupture: "Long did I endeavor… to preserve from breaking that fine and noble china vase, the British empire; for I knew that, being once broken, the separate parts could not retain even their share of the strength or value that existed in the whole." Sent to France in late 1776, he executed what Van Doren called "a diplomatic campaign equal in results to Saratoga." He fused realism (a balance-of-power memo to Vergennes) with idealism (casting the American cause as "the cause of all mankind") and a calculated "backwoods sage" persona — fur cap, plain dress, no wig — that exploited French Enlightenment romanticism. After Saratoga's news reached Passy (December 1777), he personally drafted the assurance that pushed France to proceed, and the **Treaties of Amity and Commerce and of Alliance** were signed in February 1778 — Franklin deliberately wearing the same velvet coat from the Cockpit, "to give it a little revenge."

### Peacemaker and Sage (1778–1790)
Franklin remained in Paris through 1785 as America's minister, living at Passy amid salon society and his *amitié amoureuse* friendships with Madame Brillon and Madame Helvétius, composing his printed bagatelles. He led (until incapacitated by gout and kidney stones) the negotiation of the **Treaty of Paris (1782–83)**, working through tensions with the blunt, jealous John Adams and the France-distrustful John Jay; his response to Vergennes over the unilateral signing has been called "a diplomatic masterpiece." Returning to Philadelphia in 1785, he was elected almost unanimously to the presidency of Pennsylvania's Executive Council — Benjamin Rush said his presence, "like oil upon troubled waters, [had] composed the contending waves of faction." At eighty-one he was the oldest and, as symbolic host under his mulberry tree, among the most steadying delegates to the **Constitutional Convention (1787)**, brokering rather than authoring the Great Compromise and closing with his great plea for humility and consent. His final public acts were antislavery: the presidency of the abolition society, a petition to Congress (February 1790), and a deathbed satire rebutting a pro-slavery congressman. He died April 17, 1790; some 20,000 mourners attended.

## 5. Intellectual and Political Style and Methods

Franklin's distinctive method was the **empirical, experimental temperament carried from science into politics and social observation** — the population calculations of the 1751 "Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind" (later cited by Adam Smith and Malthus) are of a piece with his weather deductions and his charge theory. He preferred **indirect, oblique persuasion**: pseudonymous dialogues, satire, and self-effacement, deliberately hiding his authorship of proposals ("put myself as much as I could out of sight") to disarm rivals — a technique a contemporary critic likened, damningly, to "the slyness of a pickpocket." He built through **voluntary association** rather than command, and he was **utilitarian in science** ("What signifies philosophy that does not apply to some use?"), declining patents on both the stove and the lightning rod. In diplomacy he practiced suasion and charm over assertion, and, per Bernard Bailyn, originated a durable American mode in which "realism and idealism have been combined." His French mission illustrates the fusion: he had American state constitutions translated and published to cast the cause as "the cause of all mankind," performed a calculated "backwoods sage" persona (fur cap, no wig) that exploited French romanticism, and turned the certainty of surveillance into a diplomatic instrument. His response to a warning about spies is the programmatic statement of his transparency-as-strategy: "to be concerned in no affairs I should blush to have made public, and to do nothing but what spies may see and welcome… If I was sure… that my valet de place was a spy, as he probably is, I think I should probably not discharge him for that, if in other respects I liked him." He used known double agents to ensure the British learned how close America was to a French deal, and the French learned of his British contacts, accelerating both. His one persistent through-line, Isaacson insists, was a genuine "faith in the wisdom of the common citizen" and an opposition to all arbitrary and inherited authority — the roots of which he traced to the "aversion to arbitrary power" bred by his brother's harsh treatment.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

The great tension in Franklin's character, in Isaacson's reading, is between his immense sociability and his "few inner chambers." He kept many friendships but often lost the deepest ones (Collins, Ralph, Keimer, Meredith), and he was demonstrably closer to surrogate family — the Stevensons and Shipleys in England, his grandsons Temple and Benny — than to his biological family. His common-law marriage to Deborah Read (1730) was an affectionate practical partnership, but he spent fifteen of their last seventeen years apart and was absent at her death (1774). His illegitimate son **William**, whom he raised and advanced to the royal governorship of New Jersey, became the sharpest wound of his life. William chose Crown over father — "No attachment or connections shall ever make me swerve from the duty of my station," he pledged to Lord Dartmouth — and Franklin came to see him, in a bitter 1774 letter, as "a thorough courtier [who] see[s] everything with government eyes." Their rupture over independence "turned into ice"; Franklin's coldness — refusing to aid William's imprisoned family, insisting on loyalist non-compensation in the peace treaty — reveals a capacity for personal callousness in service of public commitment. The *Autobiography*, begun in 1771 and formally addressed to William, is itself an instrument in this tension: a celebration of self-made, tradesman origins aimed implicitly at a son who had embraced the aristocratic pretensions his father distrusted. Only the grandson Temple received, in Isaacson's phrase, "unalloyed affection." His self-composed 1728 epitaph — the body "like the cover of an old book… lies here, food for worms" but the work to "appear once more, in a new and more elegant edition" — captures the ironic, self-aware serenity that was his abiding temper.

## 7. Death, Immediate Aftermath

Franklin died in Philadelphia on April 17, 1790, at eighty-four, after roughly ten days of fever and a burst lung abscess; his last words, to his daughter Sally's hope of recovery, were "I hope not." Some 20,000 mourners followed the procession to Christ Church burying ground. His final petition for the abolition of slavery, presented to Congress weeks earlier, had been rebuffed; his last written act was the satirical Algiers-divan parody defending it. The institutions he founded — the library, the Philosophical Society, the University of Pennsylvania, the postal system — long outlived him, as did the Constitution he had helped legitimize. His estrangement from William was never healed; the will left him little.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Isaacson's summative claim is that "the roots of much of what distinguishes the nation can be found in Franklin: its cracker-barrel humor and wisdom; its technological ingenuity; its pluralistic tolerance; its ability to weave together individualism and community cooperation; its philosophical pragmatism; its celebration of meritocratic mobility." He was, Isaacson concludes, "the most accomplished American of his age and the most influential in inventing the type of society America would become." His most important civic contribution was, per Garry Wills, his apostleship of **tolerance** — the pluralism that "made the United States a new thing on earth." His reputation has cycled sharply: Max Weber's "spirit of capitalism," D. H. Lawrence's 1920s attack on his "Babbittry," Depression-era rehabilitation (Van Doren), postwar self-help canonization, and a late-20th-century reduction to a "figure of fun." Against the charge that a compromiser makes no hero, Isaacson answers: "Compromisers may not make great heroes, but they do make democracies."

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **Primary sources.** The *Autobiography* (begun 1771 at Twyford, addressed as a letter to William, and continued in installments to 1789) is the indispensable and treacherous foundation — a consciously shaped self-portrait written across decades, whose ironies and elisions must be read against the grain (its portrait of brother James, Isaacson shows, is retrospective mythmaking). The Yale *Papers of Benjamin Franklin* (40+ volumes) provide the corrective documentary base. Contemporary counter-voices are essential: John Adams's jealous diary ("Franklin's cunning will be to divide us… he will provoke, he will insinuate, he will intrigue"); Hutchinson's diary (the only account of the Trevose break with William); Wedderburn's Cockpit speech; and Bancroft's eyewitness note of Franklin standing "conspicuously erect" under it.
- **Conflicting accounts.** The parentage of William is genuinely unresolved; Isaacson weighs the theories (Deborah, a servant, an unnamed woman) and favors the last while flagging it as speculation. The authenticity of the kite experiment has been challenged as a hoax and defended (I. Bernard Cohen); the Trevose confrontation survives only third-hand through a hostile source.
- **Major scholarly lives and debates.** The live interpretive axis runs between the "bourgeois moralist" reading (Weber, Lawrence) and the civic-republican / self-inventing reading (Van Doren, Isaacson). Gordon Wood (*The Americanization of Benjamin Franklin*, 2004; *The Radicalism of the American Revolution*) stresses Franklin's genuine, unabashed royalism into the 1760s and calls him "one of the most aristocratic of the founding fathers" — a claim Isaacson finds "too sweeping." Edmund Morgan (*Benjamin Franklin*, 2002) supplies the "political blindness" reading of the anti-Penn crusade.
- **Methodological notes.** Isaacson writes popular narrative biography, not academic revision: reliable on documented fact, synthesizing rather than overturning the scholarship, and organized around his own thematic argument that Franklin "invented" the American middle-class character. That interpretive frame — practicality over profundity, tolerance as the core contribution, compromise as democratic virtue — is the author's, and should be read as such.
