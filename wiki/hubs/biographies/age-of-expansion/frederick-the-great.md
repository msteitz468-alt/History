## 1. Formation and Early Influences

**Duffy-FG-R01-FormationArmyInheritance**  
The Prussian army's distinctive character was shaped by successive Hohenzollern rulers. The Great Elector created the standing force of ~30,000 as the base of Hohenzollern power after the Thirty Years War (victory at Fehrbellin 1675 entered memory). Frederick William I ("the Soldier King") grew it to 83,000 on a population of ~2.25 million through foreign recruitment and the General-Directorium. The Old Dessauer invented the cadenced step; 1718 brought the iron ramrod (faster loading) and the Stilbruch (sober indigo-blue coats rejecting western embroidery). Frederick retained this exact weaponry, clothing, and values.  
**Grounding quote:** "The Old Dessauer effectively invented the practice of troops marching in step (the 'cadenced step')... 1718 saw the introduction of the celebrated Prussian iron ramrod... In the same year Frederick William accomplished what Hans Bleckwenn has termed the Stilbruch in the Prussian officers' uniforms, a deliberate turning-aside from the richly embroidered fashions of western Europe, and the imposition of sober coats of dark indigo blue. Frederick the Great was to retain this weaponry and clothing..."  
**Locator:** Ch. 1 Origins.

**Duffy-FG-R01-FormationPaternalConflict**  
Frederick's relationship with [[actors/frederick-william-i]] was extreme: brutal discipline mixed with the king's passing moods of remorse. Years later Frederick was plunged into cold sweat recalling the king sweeping books, papers and flute into the fireplace.  
**Grounding quote:** "Years later he was plunged into a cold sweat by the memory of the king bursting into his room and sweeping books, papers and flute into the fireplace... What finally broke the son was not an unrelenting harshness on the part of the king, but his passing moods of blubbering remorse."  
**Locator:** Ch. 1 Origins.

**Duffy-FG-R01-FormationKatteCustrin**  
The 1730 escape attempt with Lt. Hans Hermann von Katte ended in catastrophe: Katte beheaded under Frederick's window (6 Nov 1730); Frederick imprisoned at Cüstrin, forced to oath of loyalty and bureaucratic work.  
**Grounding quote:** "There was, however, to be no mercy for Frederick's fellow-conspirator Lieutenant Hans Hermann von Katte, who was beheaded under the window of the prince's cell on 6 November. On 19 November Frederick delivered an oath of unconditional loyalty to the king..."  
**Locator:** Ch. 1 Origins.

**Duffy-FG-R01-FormationTutorsEugene**  
Military formation came from Kalckstein and Finckenstein (imbue "nothing in the world can endow a prince with more honour and glory than the sword") plus direct exposure to Prince Eugene in 1734. Frederick later credited Eugene: "if I understand anything of my trade, especially in the more difficult aspects, I owe that advantage to Prince Eugene. From him I learnt to hold grand objectives constantly in view..."  
**Locator:** Ch. 1 Origins.

**Duffy-FG-R01-FormationRheinsbergIdyll**  
Rheinsberg (1736–40) was Frederick's happiest interval: intellectual and artistic instincts flourished (devoured Caesar, Rollin, Locke, Wolff; flute, philosophy). The *Antimachiavel* was completed here.  
**Grounding quote:** "The Rheinsberg sojourn lasted until 1740, and it is rightly allowed by the biographers to be the most happy interval in Frederick's life... Now at last Frederick could launch an assault on his private library... He devoured Caesar's Commentaries, Rollin's writings... His non-professional reading ranged through the classics... and the philosophical works of Locke and Christian Wolff."  
**Locator:** Ch. 1 Origins.

**Duffy-FG-R01-FormationFWIPoliticalTestament**  
Frederick William I's Testament framed the army and treasury as tools passed to the successor "without the mask" of his own eccentricities.  
**Grounding quote:** "Only under the disguise of these spectacular eccentricities was I allowed to gather a large treasury and assemble a powerful army. Now this money and these troops lie at the disposal of my successor, who requires no such mask."  
**Locator:** Ch. 1 Origins.

Duffy stresses that Frederick received a ready, distinctive instrument (discipline + supply fragility + dependence on personal royal command) whose limits would shape his entire career. The tension between public soldier-king and private francophile philosopher was formed early.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Accession in 1740 coincided with the Habsburg succession crisis (Pragmatic Sanction contested after Charles VI's death). Frederick exploited the moment with remarkable speed and ruthlessness. The decision to invade Silesia was not a sudden whim but the intersection of prepared military capacity, diplomatic isolation of Austria, and Frederick's calculation that a short, decisive campaign would secure the province before Europe could react.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Silesian Wars 1740–45** — Rapid seizure of Silesia (Mollwitz 1741, Chotusitz 1742). Prussia gained the province but learned hard lessons about the gap between battle victory and political settlement.

**The Armed Camp 1745–56** — Administrative and military reconstruction. Frederick codified oblique order theory in the *Principes Généraux*. The army became the "armed camp" of Prussia.

**Seven Years War 1756–63** — Preemptive invasion of Saxony (1756). Sequence of brilliant victories (Rossbach, Leuthen) and near-catastrophes (Kolin, Hochkirch, Kunersdorf). Survival owed as much to Russian Empress Elizabeth's death (the "Miracle of the House of Brandenburg") as to Prussian arms. The war demonstrated both the power and the ultimate limits of Frederick's system.

**Later years 1763–86** — "Old Fritz" period of consolidation after the Seven Years War, the *Rétablissement* (rebuilding of houses, currency, agriculture, colonisation; population recovered via births despite ~500,000 war losses), and harsh remaking of the army (peace establishment to 161,000 then ~190,000 after 1772 annexation). Administrative work included state revenues largely from royal domains; industry viewed as employment regulator rather than profit engine. The king grew more isolated and harsher in judgments (Duffy-FG-R05-01: Jany: "His judgments of men became harder and more bitter, and their inner qualities became of less interest to him"). Military year structured around reviews and manoeuvres. Limited war in the Bavarian Succession (1778–79, "Potato War") tested the late style of caution and positional warfare. The public cult of "Old Fritz" formed amid foreign pilgrimages, while private life at [[places/sans-souci]] and [[places/potsdam]] revealed deepening paradoxes.

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military Style and Methods

Frederick's deism and view of war's utility framed his entire approach. He rejected utopian perpetual peace schemes (e.g. Abbé de Saint-Pierre 1713) as obtainable 'only in an unreal world, where "mine" and "yours" did not exist, and mankind was devoid of passions' (Oeuvres, IX, 129). [Duffy-FG-R06-001] His own motives for war were pragmatic and personal: (a) anticipate potential invasions of the indefensible Prussian monarchy; (b) territorial aggrandisement; (c) 'that desire to astonish and excel which transformed Frederick the artist and scholar into Frederick the soldier.' War was an intellectually valid domain with 'certain elements and fixed principles' that must be acquired and 'lodge[d] in our heads' (Catt, 1884, 214). Mere enumeration of facts bored Voltaire; true military history 'established the relations between cause and effect and identified fundamental principles' (22 Feb 1747, Oeuvres, XXII, 164). [Duffy-FG-R06-002]

Theory of short wars clashed repeatedly with practice. Frederick wrote eloquently on offensive action and the necessity to 'forestall the enemy' (Antimachiavel maxim), leveraging Prussia's unique war-readiness (regiments march-ready in six days; no ministerial/ally consultation). [Duffy-FG-R06-003] He sought speedy decision by battle and concentration. Yet in the Seven Years War, when faced with powerful alliance, 'interior lines' became a 'potent multiplier' but 'an undesirable expedient' that 'ruin[ed] our armies through exhaustion and hard marching' (Principes Généraux, 1748, Oeuvres, XXVIII, 16). 'Decisive battle' now meant winning time to redress the other end of the theatre, not 'the total annihilation of the enemy'. [Duffy-FG-R06-004]

The Oblique Order was the doctrinal core for gaining local superiority with a smaller army while retaining personal control. In essence: powerful concentration against one sector after lengthy flank march (often overnight), screened by terrain; army formed by platoon wheels; striking wing (advance guard + infantry lines + cavalry flank guard + heavy guns) delivered the blow; 'refused' wing in echelons fixed the enemy and exploited or covered retreat. [Duffy-FG-R06-005] Early version relied on moral effect of shouldered muskets (Austrians seldom stood); levelled bayonets by 1753; after Prague (1757) heavy casualties forced firepower to higher place. Principles: concentrate overwhelming force; exert greatest possible control (the 'refused' wing remained responsive after the striking wing was committed). It worked near-perfection at Leuthen on familiar ground; elsewhere the lengthy marches exhausted troops and especially artillery teams, produced surprises once enemies learned the system, and left no mobile reserve (infantry of refused wing lacked independence). [Duffy-FG-R06-006]

Enemy adaptation (Austrians especially) swung the balance: knowledge of Frederick's system; hilltop positions + powerful artillery (12-pdr canister as 'defensive equivalent of the machine gun'); swarming Croats; roving detachments; new staff system enabling elaborate attacks (Hochkirch, Maxen); reserves regulated by Fermor/Buturlin dispositions. Consequences: indecisive slaughter at Zorndorf, disaster at Kunersdorf, negative costly victory at Torgau. 'In all the battles of this war, when the Prussians were on the attack, they invariably reached the enemy out of breath' (Warnery, 1788, 310). [Duffy-FG-R06-007]

Frederick sought countermeasures up to the limits of army constitution and technology. He toyed with sacrificial use of free battalions ('detestable scum' raised for duration; 'we can shoot them up ourselves') but rarely acted on it. [Duffy-FG-R06-008] He turned to ordnance: distributed 12-pdrs among battalions (effective defensively at Liegnitz); loved howitzers for high-trajectory shells against hills. By 1762 the full late method appeared at Burkersdorf: elaborate raids/demonstrations to reduce enemy to manageable size; artillery reserve positioned statically at foot of hills; infantry groupings with precise timings advanced under cover of re-entrants without prior exhausting flank march. 'We must resort to the diversions' (to Henry, 31 May 1762, PC 13742). In writings after the war he held to artillery-based tactics and diversion, still convinced of massed howitzers/cannon for local superiority ('once we have [made] the hole in the enemy line, [we] will overcome the remaining obstacles soon enough', PC 26458). The 1778 campaign repeated the pattern at smaller scale but failed due to Austrian logistics mastery and Prussian machine decline. [Duffy-FG-R06-009]

Minor tactics showed practical grasp. Deployiren (Frederick's invention: tight columns of closed platoons marching at enemy then branching to places in line) useful for cramped terrain or concealment; Alignements-Marsch the stately favourite. Volley fire theory (platoons four rounds/min) degenerated in battle to general blazing (two sustainable); directed volleys conserved ammo and kept flints/barrels serviceable longer. Classic linear formations suited technical/social conditions; light infantry marginal because only dense regulars had firepower/solidity vs cavalry and for holding ground. Prussia lacked institutional base for skirmishers (jaegers from gamekeepers; rifles slow; even taken away after Spandau losses; free battalions unreliable). Frederick's ideal for late Frei-Regimenter was still sacrificial: 'rush blindly straight into the enemy. It will be absolutely forbidden for them to open fire before they engage in hand-to-hand fighting' (Instruction, 5 Dec 1783, Oeuvres, XXX, 401). [Duffy-FG-R06-013]

Cavalry appealed to Frederick's instincts and became a strength through gifted commanders (Zieten, Seydlitz, 'Green' Kleist, Platen, Belling, Werner), cross-posting, frequent large peacetime concentrations, and hussar influence on heavier arms. It completed Hohenfriedeberg and Leuthen, decided Rossbach, redressed Zorndorf. 'The Seydlitz Cuirassiers may serve as an example to the cavalry of the rest of the world' (Warnery). Yet brilliance dimmed after SYW; Frederick identified with the arm whose greatest days were past and accommodated unwillingly to artillery (growing in power), sending worst recruits there and denying proper command structure. Engineers fared worse: scarcely one escaped disgrace. Frederick was a 'military romantic' whose general ideas on gunnery/fortification (ring forts) were original, but practical detail suffered from inability to abdicate control to technicians. [Duffy-FG-R06-014]

Personal command was both greatest strength and limit. Frederick was omnicompetent in grand strategy (no child-rearing, ceremonies or obligations distracted him); he reaped rewards of stripped-down life. In the field the 'fog of war' descended; he was literally the eyes of the army on reconnaissance with advance guard. Long-term intelligence succeeded (sleepers in Vienna, Sabatky with Russians); day-to-day operational often failed (poorly paid spies, recalcitrant locals, refusal to believe bad news). Dissimulation was essential: 'the general must behave like an actor' (Principes Généraux, Oeuvres, XXVIII, 40). [Duffy-FG-R06-015] Once battle joined, control degraded rapidly; a force committed was lost to the commander. At Kolin 'the combat became general, and what was most annoying of all was that I was reduced to the role of spectator, not having a single uncommitted battalion' (Oeuvres, IV, 129). Leuthen and Burkersdorf were the most tightly controlled; many others chronicles of accidents or separation (Mollwitz, Torgau). [Duffy-FG-R06-016]

The divorce between theory and practice was 'still more evident' in pursuit and concentration. Frederick wrote 'Exploit your victories, pursue the enemy to the utmost' yet only Leuthen came close; elsewhere exhaustion, provisions, or distant threats halted it. He stipulated never separate forces yet left powerful detachments (Keith at Lobositz/Prague/Kolin; Retzow at Hochkirch; Henry at Kunersdorf). Sixty thousand was about the maximum manageable by the command machinery of a unitary army without Napoleonic corps. Fodder dictated strategy as much as battles: 'a theoretical army of 100,000 men would be accompanied by no less than 48,000 horses'; campaigning waited on green grass; eating-out regions or penning enemies until foraged-out explained many 'inexplicable' decisions and long intervals of apparent inactivity. [Duffy-FG-R06-017]

Frederick's writings (histories, instructions, correspondence, testaments) both illuminate and obscure. They are the richest source for his thought and the evolution of his battle notions, yet the gap between what he taught (concentration, pursuit, offensive, short wars) and what he did under pressure is a central theme of analysis. His self-criticism and generosity to enemies are notable; his numbers/dates and diplomatic candour are not. The final campaigns reveal not the 1757 hero-king or 1759 martyr-king but 'a man of perception, and perhaps unsuspected adaptability and resource'. It is 'odd to find such a close approximation to some of the military practices of Napoleon in a ruler whose views on society and the economy were so little susceptible to change'. [Duffy-FG-R06-018] The myth of Old Fritz (the stiff, infallible, parading figure of later years) contrasted with the reality of continuous evolution forced by enemy adaptation and the inherent limits of the Prussian machine.

Comparisons to other generals (Caesar, Gustavus Adolphus, Montecuccoli, Turenne, Luxembourg, Eugene, above all Charles XII whose career held 'dreadful warnings') stocked his imagination; he sought to emulate Prince Eugene on the grand scale (drive to Danube/Vienna) yet adapted to Prussian realities. Late tactics paralleled Napoleon in diversion and artillery breach more than early parade-ground battles. [Duffy-FG-R06-019] What endured of the style: the disciplined army machine and Silesia; the reputation for mobility and oblique attack; a cautionary demonstration of the strengths and brittleness of personal command in the age of unitary armies and linear tactics.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Duffy devotes extended analysis in "In Search of Old Fritz" to the private vs. public Frederick and the paradoxes that render a rounded portrait "probably hopeless." The king lacked basic "integrity" (force binding public and private conduct) that Maria Theresa possessed. Frederick himself advised his nephew on the performance required:

> when I was crown prince I showed little inclination towards military things. I was fond of my comforts, good food and wine, and I was often frantic with love. When I became king I put on the guise of soldier, philosopher and poet. I slept on straw, I camped among my soldiers eating ration bread, and I affected to despise women.

> Here is how I carry on. On my journeys I dispense with an escort... I pay little apparent attention to the way I live... I affect to be tired, and I always show myself to the public wearing a dreadful overcoat and an unkempt wig. Such trifles often make an extraordinary impression. (Duffy-FG-R05-07, quoting Frederick to nephew via Toulongeon)

**Flute, philosophy, and the soldier-king tension.** The youthful dandy was long dead. Frederick rose at 4 a.m. (summer) or 5 (winter), powdered his own hair, worked immediately, devoted five or more hours midday to recreation (ride, lunch, music), played flute/harpsichord in the concert room at [[places/sans-souci]], and retired before 10 p.m. The music room awaited "the return of their master" with flutes and manuscripts. His head inclined slightly to the right from long flute-playing. He scorched his innards with fiery condiments and downed champagne/burgundy (aversion to Rhenish: "If you want to know what it is like to be hanged, just take a drink of Rhenish wine"). Lunches were ordeals: revolting table manners, "dreadful royal monologues that reduced his officers and brothers to an overawed silence." Private recreation was essential to his longevity.

At [[places/sans-souci]] (his preferred private residence, everything "calculated for convenience and privacy"; no provision for queen or royal ladies, no chapel, no pompous retinue, minimal guard for peaches), the king lived in the eastern wing. The Voltaire Room received "cheerful decor... almost as a fumigation, after that sage's disastrous visit of 1751-3." His sanctum was a tiny circular cedar library. Dogs' tombstones dotted the terrace grass plots. The Neues Palais was a public *Fanfarronade* of defiance post-SYW, never for prolonged residence. Potsdam was a "virtual island" under heavy guard, dominated by twenty-two field regiments and military architecture (Garnison-Kirche with trophies, no relics). Berlin was grand but louche, "pretty wild," with vandalised statues and shoddy brick under stucco.

**Physical portrait and habits (Duffy-FG-R05-08).** "Frederick stood about five feet five inches tall... of average proportions... His back was humped, but he was an expert and hard-riding horseman." In later years: "mummy-like figure of an old man in a shabby uniform. A large plumed hat... deformed by a huge nose, a small caved-in mouth and great bovine eyes." Filthy surroundings: coach a "Zigeunerlager"; greyhounds "allowed to rip up cushions and commit still worse atrocities"; dirty old dressing gown and sofa; "He wears all his clothes on his back!" (Sherlock). Visitors noted the contrast with rococo elegance.

**Growing isolation and misanthropy.** After SYW the table circle "was never reconstituted in its old glory." Survivors and newcomers "bore the character of victims as much as of companions." Maria Theresa's verdict: "This hero who has everyone talking about him, this conqueror, does he have a single friend? Must he not distrust the whole universe? What kind of life is it, from which all humanity is banished?" (to Joseph II, 1766). Prince Henry maintained a rival court at Rheinsberg, focus of rancour. Fouqué would not visit. Prince Ferdinand of Brunswick resigned 1766 over taxes and favouritism, but fundamentally "it simply was not in Frederick's character to put up with seeing such a person day after day." Seydlitz ruled Silesia independently; on his deathbed Frederick cried "I cannot spare you!" Zieten endured criticisms but received solicitude late; king refused to wake the nodding old hussar: "He is a brave old man. Let him sleep on. He has stayed awake long enough on our account."

Foreign pilgrims sought the secrets but found the moving principles elusive. Frederick expressed "contempt for mankind in general" yet milder instincts: deplored hunting cruelty, prize-fighting, and negro slave trade ("I have always been of the opinion that the trade in negroes is a blight on the human race. Never shall I do anything to authorise or promote it."). The detailed business of killing (battlefield or discipline) repulsed him; no punishment on East Prussian officials who served the Russians. "Frederick had a low opinion of humankind, and he was never shocked when it lived down to his expectations." Yet his presence terrified: "those terrifying eyes bored through me..." (Egloffstein childhood memory). "As savage, bitter and hostile as a lion in his den, who employs his leisure to lap up the blood of his victims" (Tessin). He rejected Freemason ingratiation and new German literature.

**Voltaire relationship and rupture.** The 1751-3 visit was disastrous; the room was fumigated afterward. Correspondence continued (e.g., justifications for Polish Partition), but the intense philosophical friendship of earlier decades had ruptured. Frederick's francophilia and rococo tastes endured while Enlightenment Europe moved on.

**Court life and public performance.** The New Year Carnival in Berlin Stadtschloss was "tedious commotion." Military year: spring reviews (minute inspections), Silesian manoeuvres (blistering messages, physical violence, brutal career terminations), Potsdam finale with secret evolutions. Foreigners watched parades like puppets; Frederick drilled in Parade Platz. He showed himself in dreadful coat/unkempt wig for effect. Prussomania spread but wiser observers knew the real army was product of cumulative education over decades, not instant imitation.

**Private vs public Frederick (Duffy analysis).** Contemporaries and historians struggle because Frederick was "indiscreet by nature" yet guarded secrets; artistic temperament and intense emotions coexisted with instinctive finance/strategy; he wrote for himself as much as generals. The loneliness of later years "is transmitted with all the greater force." The public "Old Fritz" (hero of Rossbach/Leuthen myth, pantheon) contrasted with the isolated, misanthropic private man whose "guise" was consciously maintained. (Duffy-FG-R05-09)

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Frederick's military life was a long one spanning the transition from the age of Louis XIV to the eve of the French Revolution. As Crown Prince he heard about the battle of Fehrbellin (1675) from men who had witnessed the event. He saw his first campaign in 1734, guided by veterans who were formed in the wars of Louis XIV. He directed his final operations in 1779, and he died in 1786, less than three years before the French Revolution. A study of the way Frederick shaped his battles must therefore be an evolutionary one. [Duffy-FG-R06-021]

His health declined in final years (slight stroke, dropsy); he managed one last ride on Condé (4 July 1786) then confined to room at Sans Souci, coughing, signing with trembling hand, refusing curtains ('I have always loved the light!'). He died at twenty minutes past two on the morning of 17 August 1786. [Duffy-FG-R06-028]

**Succession realities.** Frederick had concentrated authority excessively in himself. 'When he was aware that his successor was his uncharismatic nephew Frederick William, he had made no provision for his work to be carried on other than by the sovereign.' No robust institutional mechanisms or tested successor cadre were left; the army, Frederick's personal creation, proved brittle without him (evident later at Jena 1806). Frederick William II inherited a state still formidable on paper (third-largest army in Europe) but with evident rot in discipline, medical services, commissariat, and morale from the post-1763 remaking and 1778 strains. The public salute to Maria Theresa on Teschen day showed the austere warrior's respect for a peer, but no such continuity planning extended to his own house. The personal-command system could not be replicated. [Duffy-FG-R06-022]

Immediate aftermath saw rapid formation of the public cult of 'Old Fritz' (the stiff, parading, infallible figure) alongside private awareness among veterans of the adaptive, sometimes fallible commander of the final campaigns. The structures (army machine, Silesia, state reputation) survived outwardly; the inner reliance on one man's mobility, intelligence, and coup d'oeil did not.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Silesia endured as Prussia's core territorial acquisition and the foundation of its great-power status. The state machine and standing army endured as a model copied across Europe. Frederick's preference for short wars of limited objectives and his practice of balance-of-power politics (forestalling coalitions, using alliances instrumentally) shaped the diplomatic culture of the eighteenth century and beyond. The Prussian army's reputation for discipline, mobility, and drill influenced tactical thought until the Napoleonic transformation.

Yet the legacy was double-edged. The myth of the invincible Old Fritz and the 'Frederick legend' became central symbols for later Prussian-German nationalism (despite Frederick's own francophilia and Enlightenment cosmopolitanism), feeding a strategic culture that over-valued personal command, offensive spirit, and the decisive battle at the expense of the adaptations Frederick himself was forced to make (interior lines, artillery preparation, diversion). What endured of the army was its outer shell of drill and subordination; the inner qualities of initiative and trust between sovereign and officers had been eroded by Frederick's harsh treatment of the nobility and insistence on fear over voluntary motivation. The very success of personal leadership masked the structural problem: a unitary army without organic articulation (corps/divisions) could not be commanded effectively beyond ~60,000 men once battle was joined, and an officer corps trained in fear and narrow duty could not replicate the king's adaptability.

Comparisons underscore the point. Frederick admired and drew on Caesar, Gustavus Adolphus, Montecuccoli, Turenne, Luxembourg, Eugene, and especially Charles XII (whose meteoric career supplied 'dreadful warnings'). Late practices (diversion on the grand scale + massed artillery to breach a chosen sector) approximated some Napoleonic methods more closely than the parade-like battles of the 1740s–1757. Yet Frederick remained a 'military romantic' whose social and economic views were little susceptible to change. The oblique order passed into doctrine and legend but was already being superseded by the time of his death by the very adaptations he had faced and partially countered. Post-1786 Prussia carried forward Silesia, the army's fame, and a cautionary demonstration of the limits of one-man command in the era of linear tactics and coalition warfare. [Duffy-FG-R06-023]

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources from Frederick himself** are both the richest and most problematic. He was 'a historian of some stature'. His first accounts of the Silesian Wars were written soon after (1742, 1746), reworked 1775 into *Histoire de mon Temps*. The SYW history was begun during the war; a first draft was destroyed (spark from chandelier or royal dogs); he re-wrote it from memory. The preface states the purposes: show he was forced into the war; provide successors 'useful lessons' (camps in Saxony/Bohemia discussed in detail would 'shorten the work of the men who direct the armies'); past events 'serve to feed the imagination and stock the memory. They are a fund of ideas which provide the raw material which can then be refined and tested by the exercise of judgment' — not servile imitation (Oeuvres, IV, xv, xvii). [Duffy-FG-R06-024]

Duffy's assessment is precise and balanced: 'On the whole, Frederick's historical works reflect very favourably on his honesty and competence as a writer. His reproduction of numbers and dates is admittedly cavalier, and his treatment of diplomacy is, as we might have supposed, somewhat less than candid. At the same time he is just and generous to his enemies, merciless towards many of his own military mistakes, and ever alive to his obligations as a story-teller.' The instructions, *Principes Généraux* (1748), *Testament Politique* (1768), *Castramétrie* (1770), *Reflexions* (1775), correspondence (Politische Correspondenz), and conversations (Catt) are indispensable for doctrine and self-perception, yet the repeated gap between what Frederick taught (concentration, pursuit to the utmost, offensive, short decisive wars, never divide forces) and what he practiced (detached commands, limited pursuit, interior lines as expedient, artillery and diversion in late phase) is itself a central historiographical problem. Frederick's writings both illuminate (cause-effect relations, self-critique, clear principles) and obscure (self-justification, selective memory, theory-practice divergence). [Duffy-FG-R06-025]

Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts supply essential counterpoint and correction: Archenholtz (served in Forcade's regiment), Warnery (cavalry veteran, critical), Guibert (1773 observer, noted officer limitations out of regular order), Tempelhoff (participant), Catt (daily conversations), Mitchell (British envoy: 'there is but one person that knows everything'), Berenhorst (aide, stressed poet's imagination and defective sang-froid), Lossow, Kalkreuth (critical), and many others. Enemy perspectives (Cogniazzo the Austrian veteran who worshipped Frederick and disliked Daun; Austrian cabinet debates; Russian dispositions) are vital because the allies 'arrived at an astute and very complete knowledge of Frederick and his army'. Prussian General Staff histories (Gr. Gstb.) are detailed but reflect later doctrinal biases (e.g. on territorial objectives vs. destruction of main force). [Duffy-FG-R06-026]

**Conflicting accounts** are openly addressed. On origins and timing of Oblique Order: German General Staff historians held Frederick explored it seriously only after Second Silesian War and gave it full effect in 1757; Otto Herrmann and Rudolf Keibel argued for use at Mollwitz, Chotusitz, and Hohenfriedeberg. On Torgau: Clausewitz saw it as divided-force attack; hard evidence lacking and Frederick often acted on the spur. On pursuit and concentration: explicit gap between maxims and reality explained by unitary army limits, fodder, exhaustion, and theatre geometry. On free battalions: theory of sacrificial use vs. actual practice. On 1778: 'armed negotiation' vs. earnest (if unsuccessful) tactical preparations.

**Major scholarly lives and debates** cluster around great-man vs. structural causation (the army and state Frederick inherited 'made' him as much as he made them; officer corps and social system produced both victories and the 1806 brittleness); enlightened despot vs. ruthless dynast; limited-war theorist vs. gambler whose survival owed as much to the 'Miracle of the House of Brandenburg' (Elizabeth's death) and interior lines as to genius; myth-making (Old Fritz cult, 19th-c. nationalism) vs. recovery of the adaptive, deist, francophile practitioner who distrusted declarations of pacific intent and viewed war as driven by passions, ambition, and raison d'état. Recent revisionism has worked to detach Frederick from later appropriations and restore the dynastic, limited-war context. Duffy's contribution is the sustained demonstration of evolution and the theory-practice dialectic, using topography, enemy records, and contemporary testimony to test Frederick's own claims.

**Methodological notes**: Biography here is deliberately high-resolution on military conduct and decision mechanics. Duffy privileges primary military sources (Frederick's writings + participant memoirs + Austrian/Russian material) over later nationalist narrative; he insists on placing every maxim in campaign context and on the material constraints (fodder, unitary command, artillery mobility, officer conditioning) that produced divergence from theory. No psychohistory; emphasis on what was visible to contemporaries and what the documentary record (including Frederick's self-contradictions) actually supports. The result is a portrait of continuous adaptation rather than static genius, and of a commander whose greatest asset — personal command — was also the system's ultimate limit. [Duffy-FG-R06-027]

This range (final years analysis, Chapter 9 'Frederick and War') is the richest in the source for Historiography, Long-term Legacy, Style/Methods, and Death/Aftermath sections precisely because it is Duffy's analytical synthesis of theory vs. practice, the oblique order's evolution and supersession, personal leadership as strength and limit, Enlightenment/deist view of war's utility, myth of Old Fritz vs. reality, comparisons to other generals, and assessment of what endured (army machine, state, Silesia, cautionary example of personal-command limits). Key long quotes above carry Duffy's bias notes on how Frederick's writings illuminate (honesty on mistakes, principles, cause-effect) and obscure (cavalier numbers/dates, diplomatic candour, theory-practice gap).
