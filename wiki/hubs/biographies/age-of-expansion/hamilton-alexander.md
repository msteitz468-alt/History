## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Born ~January 11, 1755 (or 1757; evidence favors 1755) on Nevis in the British West Indies, illegitimate son of James Hamilton (failed Scottish merchant, fourth son of laird of Grange) and Rachel Faucette (married to Johann Lavien, imprisoned for adultery, fled with young son Peter). The family moved to St. Croix; Rachel died February 19, 1768 of fever (Alexander beside her). Court declared the boys “obscene children,” disinheriting them; estate to half-brother Peter Lavien.

Clerked for Beekman & Cruger (New York traders) from ~1769–1772/73. Exposed to slave trade, currency chaos, commerce, and management; wrote business letters showing precocity. 1769 letter to Edward Stevens: “My ambition is so prevalent that I contemn the grovelling and condition of a clerk... I wish there was a war.” 1772 hurricane letter (published in Royal Danish American Gazette) brought him to North America via subscription fund. Tutored, entered King’s College (Columbia) ~1773–74. Self-taught, voracious reader (Pope, Plutarch, Machiavelli, Hume, Smith).

Early exposure to slavery (St. Croix “twelve blacks for every white”) and illegitimacy stigma forged drive, combativeness, and antislavery conviction. Freemasonry, Presbyterian roots, and deist leanings shaped moral and intellectual outlook. Never outgrew outsider status or touchy ego.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

1774–75: Published “A Full Vindication” and “The Farmer Refuted” defending Congress measures and natural rights against “Westchester Farmer” (Seabury). Defended Myles Cooper from mob (honor over politics). Artillery captain March 1776; company model of discipline. Fought at Harlem, White Plains, Trenton (left sickbed), Princeton; impressed Greene and Knox.

1777: Aide-de-camp to Washington (principal confidential aide); “Little Lion.” Staff life, interpreting, drafting. Close with John Laurens (abolitionist ally, intimate correspondence); flirtation with Angelica Schuyler. Married Eliza Schuyler December 14, 1780 (Philip Schuyler family connection provided wealth, status, political base).

Yorktown 1781: Artillery command; witnessed victory. Postwar: Resigned commission; studied law; admitted 1782. Brief Congress service (1782–83). Key role in Annapolis Convention 1786; authored address calling for Philadelphia Convention 1787.

Constitutional Convention and ratification: New York delegate (minority voice); chief author of *Federalist* (majority of essays); New York ratifying fight. Created Bank of New York 1784 (single-handed constitution). Treasury Secretary 1789 (nominated/confirmed September 11).

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Revolutionary & Confederation (1775–1787):** Artilleryman and Washington’s “military family”; Federalist Papers and Constitution architect.

**Treasury & Federalist Ascendancy (1789–1795):** Reports on Public Credit (funding/assumption), Bank of the United States, Manufactures; SEUM (Paterson pilot project); customs/coast guard; Whiskey Rebellion; Jay Treaty defense (“Camillus”/“Defence” essays). Built modern financial system and nationalist vision.

**Opposition & Decline (1795–1804):** Reynolds affair (1791–92; public “Reynolds Pamphlet” 1797 confession to clear corruption charges); defense of Jay Treaty; Adams administration feuds; 1800 election maneuvering (endorsed Pinckney, opposed Burr, backed Jefferson over Burr in House). Pamphlet wars. Legal practice. 1804 duel with Burr.

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military Style and Methods

Hamilton was a supreme “thinker and doer”: prolific writer (Federalist, reports, “Camillus,” pamphlets), meticulous administrator, and political operator. Method: exhaustive research (PAH, archives, samples, surveys), legalistic grounding in charters/law of nations, forward-looking vision (America as industrial/financial power). Preferred energetic executive, funded debt as “blessing,” professional military, central institutions. Combative in print and honor; used pseudonyms strategically (Publius, Camillus, Phocion). Early antislavery rooted in Caribbean experience; supported gradual emancipation and black battalions (with Laurens).

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Brilliant, charming, impetuous, romantic, headstrong; touchy ego, “fatal combativeness,” never outgrew illegitimacy stigma or ambition. Repressed hostility toward father; warmest ties to Edward Stevens and John Laurens. Marriage to Eliza: deep affection, partnership (she managed household, raised children during absences); Angelica Schuyler lifelong flirtatious/intellectual bond (possible ménage). Children central (grief over Philip’s 1801 duel death devastating; Angelica’s mental collapse). Religion: resurgent late; daily prayer, Bible annotations, proposed Christian Constitutional Society (flawed idea). Generous to friends (Steuben, Earl, Lafayette’s son); high ethical standards at Treasury (divested conflicts).

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

July 11, 1804 Weehawken duel with Burr (after “despicable opinion” letter and political attacks). Hamilton instructed to delope; Burr shot fatally. Died ~2 p.m. July 12 at home (Bayard house/Jane Street). Massive public mourning; buried Trinity Churchyard. Eliza lived to 1854, preserving papers, pushing authorized biography (son John Church Hamilton), vindicating reputation. Burr’s career collapsed (tried for treason 1807; European exile; obscure later life).

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Created the financial and administrative sinews of the federal government (funded debt, Bank, Treasury system, customs, coast guard). Prophetic vision of industrial, commercial, nationalist America (we inhabit it). Federalist Papers as classic constitutional gloss. Precedents for energetic executive and judicial review. Antislavery voice among founders. Catalyst (unintended) for first parties. Failures: personal combativeness, Reynolds scandal, Adams split, Burr duel; elitism limited popular appeal. “Without Washington... the indispensable man; with him, the indispensable partner.” Modern finance, strong union, and administrative state bear his stamp.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources:** Full PAH (Syrett, 27 vols + legal papers); new Caribbean finds (records of birth/parentage, business); letters to Washington/Eliza/Laurens; contemporary press; duel correspondence; Reynolds pamphlets. Chernow supplements with archival work in eight Caribbean islands and elsewhere.

**Conflicting accounts:** Reynolds affair (blackmail vs. Hamilton’s full confession); 1800 election (Hamilton’s Adams attack as sabotage or principle); Burr’s character (Catiline vs. political operator); duel (who fired first, delope intent).

**Major scholarly lives:** Earlier hagiography or partisan portraits; modern works restore complexity. Chernow’s contribution: Caribbean origins and illegitimacy, psychological formation, administrative genius, private contradictions (ambition/honor, charm/combativeness), marriage and Angelica, full Reynolds and duel treatment, Eliza’s long vindication campaign.

**Methodological notes:** Biography privileges Hamilton’s own voluminous record while noting self-presentation. Balances admiration for vision with realism about flaws and limits of the era.
