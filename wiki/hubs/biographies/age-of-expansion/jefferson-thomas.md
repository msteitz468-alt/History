> **Summary & network:** [[actors/jefferson-thomas|Thomas Jefferson]] · **Biography hub:** [[hubs/biographies/biographies-hub|Biographies Hub]]

A graduate-level analytic study of Thomas Jefferson's formation in Virginia gentry society, the mechanics of his signature decisions from the Declaration through the early national period, his distinctive intellectual and political style, primary-source foundations, and the structural versus contingent dimensions of his legacy. Based primarily on Dumas Malone's *Jefferson the Virginian* (1948) with cross-references to the wider founding literature.

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Thomas Jefferson was born April 13, 1743 (N.S.; April 2 O.S.) in a simple wooden house at Shadwell in what became Albemarle County, Virginia, then on the fringe of western settlement overlooking the Rivanna River. "The place was called Shadwell. Peter Jefferson, who owned it, had named it for the parish in London where his wife, Jane Randolph, had been christened..." The prospect westward to the Blue Ridge was finer; "the vastness of this physical setting was not lost upon Thomas Jefferson; it was reflected in the largeness of his mind."

When two or three he was carried on a pillow by a mounted slave to Tuckahoe on the James (Randolph connections), an older and better house in a more thickly settled district; this was the home of his earliest memories. Peter Jefferson (huge stature, legendary strength, mid-thirties at Thomas's birth) was an enterprising planter, surveyor, and local leader who acquired the Shadwell tract (including the later Monticello site) through grants and the famous bowl-of-arrack-punch deal with William Randolph of Tuckahoe. He performed the Fairfax Line survey (1746) and, with Joshua Fry, the Virginia-North Carolina extension (1749), producing the important 1751 map. Peter died August 17, 1757 (under 50); his will was equitable and without entail. Thomas (nearly 14) inherited choice of Rivanna or Fluvanna lands, slaves, livestock, books, mathematical instruments, and the slave Sawney. "Peter was the chief early influence and more than anyone else caused his son to be well schooled."

After the move back to Shadwell, Thomas's English schooling was at Tuckahoe; at nine he entered the Latin school of Rev. William Douglas (1752–1757). In early 1758 (age 14) he began at Rev. James Maury's small log school near the Southwest Mountains, boarding with the family; he read Greek and Latin authors in the original and formed his closest early friendship with Dabney Carr. Maury provided sound classical scholarship; Jefferson credited his father as first cause but Maury with deep impressions. He did not derive his later political or religious views from Maury.

In 1760 (nearing 17) Jefferson entered the College of William and Mary in Williamsburg. With the single important exception of William Small (professor of natural philosophy, the only layman on the faculty), the members were Anglican clergymen. Small gave the first lectures in ethics, rhetoric, and belles-lettres; "Small probably fixed the destinies of his life." Jefferson became a daily companion through lectures and informal talk. Small opened the door to George Wythe's law office. Jefferson studied law under Wythe (then ~35, already eminent) primarily from 1762, reading Coke upon Littleton (initially heavy; later preferred its "crabbed text and uncouth learning" to Blackstone's "honeyed" words). He kept commonplace books of cases, pursued a rigorous program, and was a careful draftsman from the start. He regarded Latin, French, mathematics, and natural philosophy as essential foundations.

He dined regularly at the Governor's Palace as fourth member of the Small–Wythe–Fauquier circle and heard "more good sense, more rational and philosophical conversations" than at any other time. He participated in weekly amateur concerts (violin or cello); music was "the favorite passion of his soul" and he later reported playing three hours a day for twelve years before the Revolution. Social life was conventional (Raleigh Tavern dances, plantation visits, weddings) but he often expressed boredom during long stays at Shadwell ("All things here appear to me to trudge on in one and the same round").

A youthful romance with Rebecca Burwell ("Belinda") in 1763–64 ended indecisively; he stammered at the Apollo Room dance and sensed her lack of interest in a planned England trip. She married Jacquelin Ambler. Jefferson copied antifeminist and anti-marriage passages into his literary notebook and temporarily sought satisfaction in male friendships. "Jefferson carried on this rather absurd affair mostly in his imagination." He was sensitive, craved affection and honors, yet abhorred controversy unless centered on an important idea. "This is not the sort of stuff out of which partisan leaders are generally made." He assumed responsibilities "just as George Washington, George Mason, and George Wythe did."

He observed the House of Burgesses and General Court; through Wythe he met Edmund Pendleton and heard Patrick Henry speak against the Stamp Act (May 30, 1765) "spellbound." He regarded Henry as pre-eminent in the forensic field from that moment while later noting Henry's intellectual limitations and laziness. In 1766 he traveled to New York and Philadelphia (inoculated against smallpox with no ill effects; lodged with Elbridge Gerry). The years in Williamsburg were decisive for intellectual formation and friendships that endured.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

By the time he began to practise law (1767), Jefferson had already named the little mountain across the Rivanna "Monticello" (Italian for "little mountain") and started grafting cherry trees. He made an agreement for leveling the top and, by his twenty-fifth birthday, had decided to build a house there. The choice of an elevated, inaccessible site defied local precedent and common sense; the difficulties (thick woods, stubborn clay, bringing water uphill) were immense, yet he worked them out in practice. The decision revealed "a sensitiveness to natural beauty which he did not often express in words" and a desire for an extended view eastward over rolling forest and westward to a lavender-blue mountain wall.

He turned to architectural books (more than others locally) and was most influenced by English imitators of Palladio and by Palladio himself, who returned to Roman antiquity. He valued simplicity, serenity, reserve, and a dignity suited to the majestic site. "Liberty was never anarchy to him, but freedom to seek the truth." He preferred chaste models, worked proportions mathematically before inking plans, and possessed a rare gift of adaptation. Early plans showed a central mass with wings; he later developed L-shaped blocks of rooms connecting to the main house and terminating in square outbuildings, sinking service rooms below terraces on the slope to conceal the meaner parts. "The first structure that he erected was one of the outbuildings, often referred to as 'the southeast pavilion.' He moved to this simple brick building in the November after the house at Shadwell burned."

About the time he moved to the pavilion he began regular visits to The Forest in Charles City County. On New Year's Day 1772 he married Martha Wayles Skelton, the widowed daughter of John Wayles. "This was a real romance, nevertheless, and his marriage... ushered in the happiest period of his life... He never had the slightest doubt that he was well mated." Martha was slight and well-formed, with large hazel eyes and luxuriant auburn hair; she played harpsichord and pianoforte skillfully. Music was a special bond. The homeward journey was delayed by visits; they arrived at Monticello late at night in late January through three feet of snow to find the fires out and servants in bed. "Martha's introduction to her unfinished home may have been discouraging," but they enjoyed uninterrupted months together. Their first child, Martha ("Patsy"), was born late September 1772 and nearly lost in the first six months; she "was recovered almost instantaneously by a good breast of milk."

Before his own marriage Jefferson (then single) had made improper advances to his neighbor's wife, Betsey Moore (Mrs. John Walker), during Walker's absence. He later privately admitted: "that when young and single I offered love to a handsome lady. I acknolege its incorrectness." He deeply regretted it and accepted all blame. There is no convincing evidence of continuation after his marriage; he was notably loyal to friends and devoted to his wife. The episode later resurfaced for partisan purposes.

The Wayles inheritance (1773–74) roughly doubled his estate: more than 11,000 acres and 135 slaves (including the Hemings family), with Poplar Forest, Elk Island, and Elkhill retained after sales. "At no time in his entire life did he appear to be as prosperous as he was from 1774 until depreciation ensued." The associated heavy debt to British merchants (Farrell & Jones) colored the rest of his life; he had to provide for it "all over again" and it later forced slave sales and mortgages.

In March 1773, with Patrick Henry, Richard Henry Lee, Dabney Carr and others meeting privately at the Raleigh Tavern, Jefferson helped draft the resolves creating the first colony-wide Committee of Correspondence. He gained for Carr the privilege of introducing them. The resolves were designed to tie colonial patriots together.

News of the Boston Port Act reached Williamsburg in May 1774. Jefferson was among those who helped opposition spread. At the Virginia convention of July–August 1775 he was elected a delegate to Congress (85 votes) and received instructions that contributed to the movement for independence.

In Congress (June 1775 onward) he was constant in attendance but silent in debate. Adams described him as "prompt, frank, explicit, and decisive" on committees and in conversation; their friendship dated from this period. He brought a reputation for literature, science, and composition and had learned French, Italian, and Spanish. Added to the committee revising the unsatisfactory Declaration on Taking Up Arms, his draft was judged "too harsh" by Dickinson, though some expressions were incorporated.

He returned to Virginia in time to help draft the reply to Lord North's conciliatory motion and to send a constitution draft via Wythe to the convention. On June 11, 1776, Congress appointed the Committee of Five (Jefferson, Adams, Franklin, Sherman, Livingston); Jefferson was asked to draft the Declaration. He consulted no book or pamphlet, drew the charges against the King from his earlier Virginia paper, and produced a document whose philosophical paragraph echoed ideas already in his mind (with similarity to Mason's language). He was a fastidious craftsman, weighing phrases ("self-evident" for "sacred and undeniable"; "inalienable"). Congress deleted the slave-trade charge and reduced the final paragraph; Jefferson believed Congress had weakened it, but the changes (primarily deletions) generally strengthened the document.

In the seventeen days between June 11 and June 28 he drafted the Declaration. It was debated July 2–4 after the independence resolution passed on July 2. The document asserted that when it becomes necessary for one people to dissolve the political bands connecting them with another, a decent respect to the opinions of mankind requires a declaration of causes. Charges were directed against the King (cutting the last tie); parliamentary grievances were included under "pretended Legislation." Jefferson approached the task as a literary craftsman and a revolutionary statesman whose job was to grasp the essence and set forth the justification in the required tone. The Declaration became the founding charter whose universal language shaped later liberty movements worldwide.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Virginia Reformer and Legislator (1776–1779):**  
Returned from Congress to serve in the House of Delegates and on the committee (with Wythe, Mason, Pendleton) to revise the laws of the Commonwealth. Produced enduring reforms on inheritance (abolition of primogeniture and entail), religious freedom (the 1786 statute), and education. The revisal work was done largely at Monticello; he purchased libraries of deceased leaders (Peyton Randolph, Richard Bland) and collected old laws and newspapers. He gave more time to the bill for proportioning crimes and punishments than to all other Revisal bills combined — a meticulously crafted document seeking humane and rational penalties while limiting capital punishment to treason and murder. Public labor on roads was the chief alternative (later judged ineffective for reform). The bill for religious freedom asserted that "it is error alone which needs the support of government. Truth can stand by itself" and prohibited compulsion to support any worship while protecting civil capacities. It was the bitterest contest of his Assembly career and one he later rated second only to the Declaration. The bill for the more general diffusion of knowledge proposed primary "hundred" schools (three years gratis for all white children) and selective grammar schools to "rake from the rubbish" superior talent for higher public education — a realistic and discriminating approach to republican leadership drawn from native ability rather than birth or wealth. As a visitor to William and Mary he criticized its failure to meet expectations and introduced professorships of law (to Wythe), medicine, and modern languages.

**Wartime Governor (1779–1781):**  
Elected June 1, 1779 (age 36) on the second ballot (67–61). He viewed the office as an onerous constitutional burden ("burdens to those appointed... which it would be wrong to decline") and soon felt like "a public slave." He scrupulously observed limits, acting generally only with Council advice (Council included Page, Madison — the beginning of their intimate friendship — and Walker). He was a strong unionist, sending Virginia troops south after Charleston losses and dispatching Monroe as commissioner. Faced Henry Hamilton (captured at Vincennes) as a "war criminal," advising irons and close confinement in the public jail dungeon to deter Indian barbarities (later somewhat relaxed on Washington's advice). Planned a major expedition against Detroit under George Rogers Clark that aborted due to timing, militia reluctance, and local invasion threats. The Arnold raid (Jan 1781) caught the government initially off guard (disbelief delayed full militia call by ~2 days); Jefferson then acted with energy (store removals at Westham, incessant riding, reports). Tarleton's raid (June 4, 1781) forced flight from Monticello; Jack Jouett rode ahead to warn. Jefferson evacuated deliberately (guests first, family carriage hours later, himself last when enemy approaching); no sign of terror. Family taken to Poplar Forest; Jefferson fell from his horse and was confined ~6 weeks. The Assembly elected Thomas Nelson governor (June 12); George Nicholas moved an inquiry into the Executive's conduct for the last twelve months. Jefferson was deeply wounded, requested specifications, and prepared detailed answers focused on the Arnold raid. Nicholas presented no charges. In December 1781 the General Assembly unanimously thanked him for his "impartial, upright, and attentive administration" and declared its "high opinion" of his "ability, rectitude and integrity." The most conspicuous failure was of Virginia's governmental structure (ill-suited to war) rather than of Jefferson personally; "of all the war governors he was incomparably the greatest mind and the greatest man."

**Congress, Notes, and France (1783–1784):**  
Elected to Congress 1783. Key contributions included the report on government for the western territories (principles that shaped the Northwest Ordinance) and Virginia's cession of claims north of the Ohio. Commissioned (with Adams and Franklin) to negotiate treaties of commerce; sailed for France July 5, 1784, aboard the Ceres with his daughter. While still governor and in the months of "retirement" he assembled answers to Marbois's queries; the manuscript of *Notes on the State of Virginia* was sent in late March/early April 1782 and later enlarged and published in Paris (1785). It was his only book-length work — a wide-ranging survey of natural history, society, law, religion, and politics that included his famous strictures on slavery and proposals for gradual emancipation. At age 41 he left behind a distinguished career of patriotic service and personal achievement; he expected "more quiet and greater richness" in the new phase. "When he ferried across the Potomac on his way to the Continental Congress, he crossed his Rubicon." Now he crossed the Atlantic.

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Political Style and Methods

Jefferson's characteristic method was the carefully drafted state paper: precise, historical in argument, moral in grounding. He preferred committee work and written formulation to oratory. He combined radical principle (natural rights, religious freedom, generational sovereignty) with pragmatic attention to what Virginia society would accept at a given moment. Architecture and empirical observation (*Notes*) were extensions of the same habit of mind: measure, record, improve.

He distrusted standing armies, concentrated financial power, and hereditary privilege. He believed in the capacity of ordinary people for self-government when educated and informed. His style was often indirect in personal politics but uncompromising in written principle. In the revisal he was a meticulous legal craftsman (beautiful penmanship, multilingual notes) yet capable of sweeping philosophical assertions ("it is error alone which needs the support of government"). As governor he was scrupulous about constitutional limits and unionist in outlook even when local pressures were intense. He took a long-range view: "It may have been his weakness but unquestionably it was his genius to take a long-range view of things." He assumed that war should be waged like gentlemen and that the amenities of civilized life (music, philosophy, hospitality) should continue even amid invasion — until necessity compelled ruthlessness (Hamilton case, attainder).

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Intense, controlled, reserved in public, passionate in private correspondence and building projects. The marriage to Martha was the emotional center of his life; her death produced a grief from which he never fully recovered. He maintained warm (sometimes flirtatious) friendships with women in France but formed no second permanent attachment. Deeply attached to his daughters and grandchildren; Monticello was both sanctuary and ongoing construction site reflecting his mind.

Contemporary observers noted his charm in small groups, aversion to personal confrontation, and omnivorous curiosity. The central contradiction — eloquent enemy of tyranny who held people in slavery — was visible to him and to his contemporaries; he never resolved it in practice.

He was kind to servants "to the point of indulgence" and they were devoted. He loved young men full of enthusiasm, information, and ideas and exerted on them "an irresistible appeal" throughout life. He was more sensitive than average and appears to have been diffident toward women until after he had acquired a wife and family. In home matters he was "least disposed to count the labor and the cost." He combined craving for public honors and affection with deep sensitivity that led him to abhor controversy unless it centered on an important idea. "Of all men living he loved Dabney Carr the most." The Wayles inheritance first enriched and then impoverished him; the necessity of paying the associated debt "all over again" shaped his later philosophy of economy and hostility to public debt.

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Died at Monticello on July 4, 1826, the same day as John Adams. Last words reportedly "Is it the Fourth?" or similar. He was buried under the epitaph he wrote himself: author of the Declaration, the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom, and founder of the University of Virginia.

His papers and influence passed to family, Madison, and a generation of younger Virginians and national figures. The University opened in 1825 and became his most tangible institutional legacy.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Jefferson's words in the Declaration provided the moral language of American nationhood and later of anti-colonial and civil-rights movements worldwide. His presidency demonstrated that a large republic could acquire territory peacefully and transfer power partisanly. The University of Virginia embodied his belief in education as the only sure foundation of republican government.

His failures on slavery and his racial views have become central to modern reassessments. The tension between his universalist rhetoric and the particularities of his life as a Virginia slaveholder remains the most productive and painful site of Jefferson scholarship.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

Malone's six volumes (this one the foundation) set the scholarly standard for a generation through exhaustive documentary reconstruction. The Boyd edition of the Papers (beginning 1950) made the full record available and corrected some details. Peterson's *Jefferson and the New Nation* synthesized; Ellis highlighted the "American Sphinx"; Gordon-Reed brought the Hemings family to the center. Debates continue over the sincerity of his anti-slavery proposals, the nature of his relationship with Sally Hemings, and whether his thought was fundamentally consistent or opportunistic.

**Primary sources in this volume:** Jefferson's early letters and Account Books; Virginia legislative journals and county records; contemporary newspapers and pamphlets; the *Summary View* and *Notes*.
