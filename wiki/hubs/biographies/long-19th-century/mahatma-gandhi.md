A graduate-level analytic study of Mohandas K. Gandhi's formation, the South African laboratory in which satyagraha was invented and tested, the mechanics of his key decisions 1893–1914, character in context, primary-source foundations, and the structural vs. contingent elements that produced the leader who returned to India in 1915. Draws primarily from Ramachandra Guha's *Gandhi Before India* (2013).

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi was born 2 October 1869 in Porbandar, Kathiawar (Saurashtra), Gujarat, into a Bania (Vaishya) family of the Modh sub-caste. His father Karamchand (Kaba) Gandhi served as diwan (chief minister) to several small Kathiawar states; his mother Putlibai was pious, strong-willed, and deeply observant in the Vaishnava and Jain-influenced traditions of the region.

Gandhi's caste position was "middle rank" — above Sudras and untouchables, below Brahmins and Kshatriyas. Bania culture emphasized trade, moneylending, frugality, hard work, adaptability, and a "soft and persuasive way of speech." These traits would later serve both his legal practice and his political method.

The first Gandhi in public service (Lalji) migrated to Porbandar; Karamchand (Kaba) became diwan at 25. Putlibai bore Mohandas after three children; family provided multiple generations of administrative service unusual for Banias elsewhere. School record (Taluk School 1879, Kattywar High from 1880) was mediocre: 40% matric, ranked 404/823 provincially; spotty attendance (110/238 days one year). Family memory (sister Raliat) later claimed consistent first rank — contradicted by records.

Early experiences of caste rules (child-marriage to Kasturba at ~13, black-water crossing to London) exposed him to the constraints he would later challenge. At marriage he exhibited intense possessiveness and sexual preoccupation that later caused profound guilt after his father’s death during his absence.

London (1888–1891) was decisive: vegetarianism (initially for economy and identity, later principled via the London Vegetarian Society), exposure to Theosophy, Christianity (Sermon on the Mount), and a wide circle of non-conformists. He read the Gita in Edwin Arnold's translation. He was called to the bar at Inner Temple in 1891 but returned to India a shy, unsuccessful barrister.

South Africa (from 1893) transformed him. Racial insults (the Pietermaritzburg train incident, the Durban mob attack) forced a political awakening. Employment by Muslim merchants (Dada Abdulla) placed him in an ecumenical Indian diaspora. Friendships with Henry Polak (Jewish radical, vegetarian), Hermann Kallenbach (architect, Tolstoyan), Sonja Schlesin (secretary), and others broadened his circle beyond caste and religion. Tolstoy's *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (read ~1894) and Ruskin (read 1904 on a train) supplied philosophical backbone.

*(Contingent: the specific train conductor in 1893; the vegetarian networks in London and Johannesburg; Kallenbach's land gift for Tolstoy Farm. Structural: the racial state in post-Boer War South Africa, the presence of a literate Indian merchant class with resources, the British Empire's legal and petitioning traditions that Gandhi first used then transcended.)*

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Gandhi arrived in Durban May 1893 on a one-year contract. Within months he was organizing the Natal Indian Congress (1894) and leading petitions against the Franchise Bill. He returned briefly to India in 1896–97 to fetch his family and recruit support, surviving a white mob attack on his return to Durban.

The real ascent began with the 1906 Transvaal Asiatic Registration Act (the "Black Act"). On 11 September 1906 ~2,000 Indians packed the Empire Theatre in Johannesburg and passed a solemn vow to submit to imprisonment rather than comply (explicitly drawing on hartal and Indian precedents rather than Thoreau). The term "satyagraha" (truth-force) was coined in 1906–1907 by his cousin Maganlal Gandhi during a competition in *Indian Opinion* (refining "sadagraha"). The 16 August 1908 Johannesburg mass burning of registration certificates outside Fordsburg Mosque (3,000+ participants, Chinese joining) marked the public birth of the new method. Ram Sundar Pandit was the first prosecuted (Nov 1907); Gandhi himself was sentenced Jan 1908 to two months in Fort Prison (native section, reading Tolstoy, Ruskin, Gita). A February 1908 Pathan attack (sticks/iron rod) during voluntary registration left him unconscious; he was nursed at Joseph Doke’s home and recovered using earth poultices.

By 1910–1913 he commanded a coalition that included Gujarati merchants, Tamil labourers, women (including Kasturba), and even Chinese participants. In 1913 the campaign revived over the Searle judgment (invalidating non-Christian marriages) and the £3 tax. Kasturba independently offered to court imprisonment ("Could I not, then, join the struggle and be imprisoned myself?"). Thousands of indentured workers struck; women crossed borders illegally; Gandhi led the epic 11-day march of ~2,000 (including women/children) from Newcastle toward Tolstoy Farm. Police shootings, mass arrests, and international attention (including Viceroy Hardinge) forced the Enquiry Commission and the 1914 Indians’ Relief Bill (repeal of £3 tax; recognition of religious marriages). Gandhi adopted indentured-labourer dress (shaved head, white clothes) after the shootings and declared victory. The settlement was incomplete but sufficient; he sailed from Cape Town 18 July 1914.

His power derived not from office or wealth but from personal example, organizational stamina, willingness to suffer, and an uncanny ability to dramatize injustice for both local and imperial audiences.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Phase 1: Loyalist Lawyer and Community Organizer (1893–1906)**  
Context: post-1893 Natal/Transvaal restrictions on Indians. Objectives: defend existing rights through petitions, test cases, and imperial appeals. Tools: legal skill, *Indian Opinion*, personal diplomacy. Results: some concessions, but growing recognition that petitions alone were insufficient.

**Phase 2: Invention and First Tests of Satyagraha (1906–1909)**  
The Black Act provoked the decisive break with mere constitutionalism. Phoenix Settlement (1904) and simple living provided the institutional base. Jail terms (1908, 1909) tested and publicized the method. Tolstoy Farm (1910) deepened the communal experiment. Gandhi's reading of Tolstoy and Ruskin fused with Jain ahimsa and his own experiments with diet, celibacy (brahmacharya after 1906), and truth-telling.

**Phase 3: Mass Campaign and Negotiated Settlement (1913–1914)**  
The trigger was the £3 tax on ex-indentured labourers and the invalidation of non-Christian marriages. A general strike, women's participation in illegal border crossing, and the epic march produced a crisis for the Union government. Smuts and Gandhi reached a settlement (repeal of the tax and recognition of marriages). Gandhi left for India in July 1914.

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Religious Style and Methods

Gandhi's style combined:
- **Truth as method**: satya (truth) was both goal and instrument; one must be willing to suffer for it and to revise one's own position when shown to be in error.
- **Non-violence as active force**: ahimsa was not passivity but the refusal to harm the opponent while refusing to accept injustice. Suffering was purifying for the sufferer and potentially converting for the oppressor.
- **Ecumenism without relativism**: deep personal roots in Hinduism (Gita), selective appropriation from Christianity (Tolstoy, Sermon on the Mount) and other traditions; pragmatic political alliances with Muslims; rejection of religious coercion.
- **Personal example as primary instrument**: diet, dress, celibacy, manual labour, and jail-going were political acts.
- **Dramatization**: the ability to make a local grievance (pass laws, salt tax) stand for a universal moral claim.

He consistently sought "points of agreement" even with opponents (Smuts) while refusing to compromise on core principles.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Guha's portrait is of a man of intense self-discipline who drove himself and those close to him hard. Kasturba endured repeated separations, jail, and the experiments in celibacy and diet; their relationship was deep but not without tension. Gandhi's closest male friendships were with Polak (intellectual partner) and Kallenbach (emotional and practical support; the two lived in a "simple life" ménage at times).

He was capable of great charm and persuasiveness but also authoritarian control within his organizations. He demanded high standards of personal conduct from followers and was unforgiving of lapses (including his own sons' struggles). His lack of personal bitterness toward whites who attacked or legislated against him was repeatedly noted by contemporaries as remarkable and sometimes unnerving.

Women played unusually prominent roles in his South African campaigns (both European secretaries and Indian volunteers), and he explicitly credited their courage with shaming and inspiring the men.

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

This volume ends in 1914 with Gandhi's departure. The "death" of this phase was the end of the South African chapter. He left behind a settled (if imperfect) agreement and a community that had learned satyagraha. The technique and the man were exported to India. Kasturba and several associates (including some Europeans) would later join him in India.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

The South African years produced:
- The fully formed method of mass non-violent resistance that would be deployed on a civilizational scale in India 1915–1947.
- A model of inter-religious and inter-caste living and struggle that prefigured Gandhi's later Harijan and Hindu–Muslim work.
- An international reputation that made the Indian nationalist leader immediately credible to both supporters and opponents upon his return.
- The personal transformation from shy London-trained barrister into the figure contemporaries already called "Mahatma" by 1909–1910 (Mehta, Kallenbach, Cachalia).

Guha quotes a South African friend: "You gave us a lawyer; we gave you back a Mahatma."

Key synthesis (Guha): "As writer, editor, leader, bridge-builder, social reformer, moral exemplar, political organizer and political theorist, Gandhi returned to India in 1915 fully formed and fully primed..." South Africa gave India "the idea of civil disobedience." Hofmeyr (1931): "India had given to South Africa one of the most difficult of its problems; South Africa in turn gave to India the idea of civil disobedience." Gandhi himself (June 1914): satyagraha was "perhaps the mightiest instrument on earth." Counterfactual: had Milner or Smuts compromised earlier, "Gandhi would have returned home without ever having thought of civil disobedience." The 1913 campaign demonstrated scalability through indentured labourers, Tamils, and women; "potent... though passive resistance was as an instrument for winning reforms – perhaps the mightiest instrument on earth."

Gandhi's "capaciousness was not complete": he forged no real friendships with Africans despite labouring alongside them at Tolstoy Farm and knowing John Dube; his understanding widened but remained limited.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources** (per Guha's Note on Sources):
- *Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi* (Vols 1–12) — indispensable but require critical use for attribution of unsigned *Indian Opinion* pieces.
- Letters *to* Gandhi (National Gandhi Museum black-bound volumes, "S.N." series) — under-used before Guha; reveal personality, pressures, and advice from Polak, Gokhale, Mehta, etc.
- Papers of associates (Gokhale at NAI; Polak at Rhodes House/BL; Kallenbach in Haifa/transferring to India; Doke papers).
- Government archives (South Africa, India, UK) for the view "from the other side."

Key verbatim from Guha's synthesis range (for Legacy/Historiography):
- "You gave us a lawyer; we gave you back a Mahatma." (South African friend)
- Hofmeyr (1931): "India had given to South Africa one of the most difficult of its problems; South Africa in turn gave to India the idea of civil disobedience."
- Gandhi (June 1914): "potent, however, though passive resistance was as an instrument for winning reforms – perhaps the mightiest instrument on earth."
- Gandhi to Tolstoy (1909): "the struggle of the Indians in the Transvaal is the greatest of modern times, inasmuch as it has been idealised both as to the goal as also the methods adopted to reach the goal."
- A. M. Cachalia (1908): "the passive resister is higher in the moral scale... Passive resistance is a matter of heart, of conscience, of trained understanding."
- Polak (1910): "our programme will remain... one of suffering on the part of our people, who intend to go on enduring these hardships until they make the authorities ashamed of themselves."

**Conflicting accounts and bias**:
- Gandhi's own *Autobiography* (1920s) is retrospective, selective, and shaped by later political needs; Guha prefers contemporary letters and files.
- Official records (Milner, Smuts, Indian Office) reveal contempt, racial panic, and sometimes grudging respect.
- Associate memoirs (Polak, Doke, Andrews) are hagiographic but contain unique detail.
- Contemporary white press and Muslim merchant critics (e.g. P. S. Aiyar calling Gandhi a "failure" and "Czar") provide unflattering counter-narratives.

**Major scholarly debates addressed by this volume**:
- The relative weight of "South Africa" vs. "India" in the making of Gandhi-the-leader.
- Whether satyagraha was a fully formed philosophy in SA or an improvised tactic later theorized.
- Gandhi's attitudes toward violence, race, and empire in these years (Guha stresses continuity of non-violence and the search for "unity of hearts" alongside tactical confrontation).
- Role of women and indentured labourers in making the method scalable (often under-emphasized in elite-focused accounts).

**Methodological notes**: Guha's approach is classic critical biography — priority to documents written closest to the events, triangulation across multiple archives, and explicit attention to what the subject later mythologized. It avoids both uncritical adulation and reductionist debunking. The volume repeatedly flags that popular accounts over-emphasize the 1893 Pietermaritzburg train incident while under-playing the 1897 Durban mob attack and the 1913 mass participation. "Haste and teleology... do injustice to both man and place."
