# Nikola Tesla — Detailed Biography

> **Summary & network:** [[actors/tesla-nikola|Nikola Tesla (actor page)]] · **Biography hub:** [[hubs/biographies/biographies-hub|Biographies Hub]] · **Source:** [[sources/seifer-wizard-tesla-1996|Seifer, *Wizard* (1996)]]

A graduate-level analytic study of Tesla's formation, the mechanics of his decisive choices, his intellectual style, and the structural vs. contingent shape of his legacy — drawn from Marc Seifer's archival biography *Wizard* (1996). Tesla is the figure who made [[concepts/alternating-current|polyphase AC power]] practical and so helped electrify the modern world, yet died impoverished, the central puzzle this biography works.

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Nikola Tesla was born during a summer storm in 1856 in **Smiljan**, a hamlet in the province of Lika in the Austro-Hungarian Military Frontier (modern Croatia), the son of a Serbian Orthodox priest. The family were ethnic Serbs; Seifer notes the trade-name origin of the surname — "a trade name like Smith…or Carpenter," describing a woodworking ax. His father **Milutin Tesla** (b. 1819) was a multilingual priest, poet, and political journalist with "a large library"; his mother **Djouka Mandić** (b. 1821), though never taught to read, memorized Serbian epics and, in Tesla's telling, "conceived of many household appliances."

Two formative wounds shaped him. The death of his gifted elder brother **Dane** (c. 1863, thrown by the family horse) left the household permanently grieving: "Anything I did that was creditable merely caused my parents to feel their loss more keenly. So I grew up with little confidence in myself." And he was subject to intense **eidetic imagery** and out-of-body episodes — the same faculty that let him "mold his various creations, and even run and modify them in his mind, before committing them to paper." This capacity for complete mental visualization is, in Seifer's reading, the engine of his inventive method and inseparable from his lifelong compulsions.

A near-fatal bout of cholera produced the decisive bargain of his youth: bedridden, he extracted his father's promise — "'You will go to the best technical institution in the world,' he solemnly replied." Tesla studied at the Higher Real Gymnasium in **Karlovac**, then enrolled in 1875 at the **Polytechnic in Graz**, where a confrontation with Professor Poeschl over a Gramme dynamo planted the obsession of his life. Tesla argued the commutator was unnecessary; Poeschl ridiculed it publicly: "His plan is simply a perpetual motion scheme." Tesla never graduated — he lost his tuition gambling — and after a term at the **University of Prague** (1880, under the philosopher Carl Stumpf) drifted into telephone work in **Budapest**.

The founding moment came there in early 1882. Recovering from a severe nervous collapse (his senses grown so acute he claimed to hear "the ticking of a watch…three rooms away"), Tesla was walking at sunset reciting Goethe's *Faust* when the principle of the **[[concepts/rotating-magnetic-field|rotating magnetic field]]** arrived whole: "the truth was [suddenly] revealed. I drew with a stick on the sand the diagrams shown six years later in my address before the American Institute of Electrical Engineers." Within two months, he claimed, "I evolved virtually all the types of motors and modifications of the system now identified with my name."

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Tesla's path ran through the Edison organization. Working for the **Compagnie Continental Edison** in Paris and then **Strasbourg** (1882–83), he secretly built his first physical AC motor in a machine-shop closet, achieving "rotation effected by alternating currents of different phase, and without sliding contacts or commutator" — to investor indifference. Denied promised compensation, he sailed for **New York** in 1884 and went straight to [[actors/edison-thomas|Edison]]'s Goerck Street works, "thrilled to the marrow by meeting Edison."

The relationship was brief and bruising. Tesla redesigned Edison's DC dynamos (earning the line "Batchelor, this is a damn good man") but could not interest Edison in AC, and left when a $50,000 bonus he believed promised was dismissed as "an American joke." After being forced out of an arc-lighting company that bore his name — reduced for a winter to digging ditches, "with no other possession than a beautifully engraved certificate of stock of hypothetical value" — he found backers in **Charles Peck** and **Alfred Brown**, and in spring 1887 set up the **Tesla Electric Company** at 89 Liberty Street.

His ascent was then astonishingly fast. He filed the foundational polyphase patents, and on **15 May 1888** delivered "A New Alternating Current Motor" before the **AIEE**, having already filed fourteen of the forty fundamental AC patents. The lecture's significance was structural: where Elihu Thomson's single-circuit AC still needed a commutator, Tesla's used "two or more circuits out of phase with each other" and made the commutator obsolete. Within a week (21 May 1888), [[actors/westinghouse-george|George Westinghouse]] sent his manager to Tesla's lab, and the alliance that would electrify America was struck.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**The Westinghouse alliance and the War of the Currents (1888–1895).** Westinghouse licensed the patents on lavish terms (see §4) and brought Tesla to Pittsburgh to develop the [[concepts/induction-motor|induction motor]]. The contest with Edison's direct current — the **[[processes/war-of-the-currents|War of the Currents]]** — turned ugly: the Edison camp staged animal electrocutions and promoted AC for the first electric-chair execution (the botched 1890 Kemmler killing) to brand it "the executioner's current." AC's transformer-based long-distance economy won anyway, sealed by two demonstrations: the **[[events/worlds-columbian-exposition-1893|1893 World's Columbian Exposition]]**, lit by Westinghouse AC, and the **[[events/niagara-falls-power-project-1895|Niagara Falls power project]]** (first power 1895; to Buffalo 1896), which chose AC over DC and compressed air for one of the world's great energy sources.

**Celebrity and the high-frequency years (1891–1899).** Dazzling lectures in New York (Columbia, 1891), **London** (Royal Institution / IEE, 1892) and **Paris** made Tesla a scientific celebrity; he became a U.S. citizen and a fixture of Gilded-Age society (close to the poet [[actors/morgan-john-pierpont|Robert Underwood Johnson]] and his wife Katharine, and to Mark Twain). A devastating **lab fire** (13 March 1895) destroyed his uninsured work (~$250,000). He pursued X-ray ("shadowgraph") imaging, demonstrated a radio-controlled boat (**teleautomaton**) at Madison Square Garden in 1898, and in **1899** built his **[[places/colorado-springs|Colorado Springs]]** station, generating millions of volts and 135-foot artificial lightning and claiming to have detected terrestrial stationary waves — and possibly signals from space.

**Wardenclyffe and ruin (1900–1906).** Financed by [[actors/morgan-john-pierpont|J. P. Morgan]], Tesla began **[[places/wardenclyffe|Wardenclyffe]]** on Long Island. After [[actors/marconi-guglielmo|Marconi]]'s December 1901 transatlantic signal, he secretly enlarged the plan from a "modest-sized transmitter" to a global power-and-telegraphy system, ran out of money, and was refused further funds by Morgan. The project collapsed; in 1906 Tesla suffered a nervous breakdown so severe "his handwriting completely disintegrated."

**Decline and late inventions (1907–1943).** Tesla pivoted to a **bladeless (disk) turbine**, the **Hammond** remote-control partnership, and small royalty deals, sliding from the Waldorf-Astoria through ever-cheaper hotels into genteel poverty, sustained late by a Westinghouse consulting stipend ($125/month, from 1934) and a Yugoslav endowment ($600/month, from 1937). From 1931 he used annual birthday press conferences to announce grand, unverifiable inventions — culminating in the particle-beam **[[concepts/death-ray-teleforce|"teleforce" / death ray]]**. He died alone in the Hotel New Yorker on 7 January 1943.

The **release of the Westinghouse royalty** is the hinge of the whole life. Westinghouse had agreed to pay $2.50 per horsepower; by Seifer's accounting the contract could have yielded a colossal fortune. When bankers pressed Westinghouse to shed the obligation, Tesla — to keep his system alive in the company best able to deploy it — gave it up. It was a structurally rational act (AC survived; the world electrified) and a personally ruinous one (he kept neither the royalty nor, later, control of his wireless patents under Morgan). The man who electrified the planet thereby ensured he would not profit from it.

## 5. Intellectual Style and Methods

Tesla's distinguishing method was **complete mental prototyping**: he designed, ran, and refined machines entirely in his visual imagination before building them, contrasting himself explicitly with Edison's brute trial and error — "if he had a needle to find in a haystack he would…examine straw after straw," where "a little theory and calculation would have saved him 90 per cent of the labor." His genius was for *systems*: the polyphase insight was not a single device but a complete generation-transmission-motor architecture, and his wireless work likewise aimed at a planetary "World System."

That same intuitive, image-driven cast of mind produced his weakness: a tendency to treat vividly imagined results as accomplished facts. The Colorado Springs "received signals," the cosmic-ray motor, the faster-than-light particle claims (he asserted solar particles "exceeding fifty times that of light"), and the death ray were all announced with the conviction of demonstrated engineering. He worked by resonance and visualization, not by the falsification-minded experimental culture of the rising physics establishment, and increasingly spoke past it. His superstitions (fixation on the number three, compulsive routines, germophobia) were of a piece with the same obsessive, pattern-saturated mind.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Seifer's Tesla is courtly, abstemious, and solitary — an "absolute celibate" (in friend Kenneth Swezey's phrase) who slept little, dressed immaculately, and formed his deepest attachments to a few intimates and, notoriously, to pigeons. His closest emotional bond in America was with the **Johnsons** — Robert Underwood Johnson, editor of *The Century*, and especially Katharine, whose death in 1925 he mourned deeply. He was admired by women of society (Anne Morgan among them) but never married.

His patron relationships structured his fate. With **Westinghouse** he was generous to his own ruin; with the elder **Morgan** he was proud and supplicating by turns ("there has been hardly a night when my pillow is not bathed in tears…but you must not think me a weak man"); with **Marconi** he was embittered, convinced (with cause) of stolen priority. Rivalries with **Pupin** (reconciled only at Pupin's deathbed) and the broader establishment that wrote him out of textbooks (Steinmetz removing his name from a republished treatise) left him increasingly isolated. The late birthday press conferences were, in part, a lonely man's bid to remain visible.

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Tesla died on **7 January 1943**, aged 86, found the next morning by a maid in the Hotel New Yorker. His Serbian-rite funeral filled the Cathedral of St. John the Divine; Mayor La Guardia read a eulogy on the radio and Eleanor Roosevelt sent condolences. Within a day the **Office of Alien Property** (the one agency able to seize "enemy assets" without a court order), with the FBI and military intelligence, impounded his papers — motivated largely by his death-ray claims and fear that his heir **Sava Kosanović** might pass material to the Soviets. MIT's **John G. Trump** examined the trunks (26–27 January 1943) and judged them "nothing of value for the war effort"; the famous death-ray "model" in a hotel vault proved to be a common resistance box. The papers stayed under federal control about a decade before 80 trunks went to the **Tesla Museum in Belgrade** (1952).

Months after his death, in 1943, the **U.S. Supreme Court** upheld key prior wireless patents — Tesla's among them — over Marconi's, the vindication he had not lived to see (see [[events/marconi-radio-priority-dispute-1943]]).

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Tesla's structural legacy is enormous and largely anonymous: the [[concepts/alternating-current|polyphase AC system]] and the [[concepts/induction-motor|induction motor]] are the basis of modern power generation, transmission, and industrial drive — the motive core of [[processes/electrification|electrification]] and the [[processes/second-industrial-revolution|Second Industrial Revolution]]. B. A. Behrend's 1917 tribute captured it: "Were we to seize and to eliminate…the results of Mr. Tesla's work, the wheels of industry would cease to turn, our towns would be dark." His foundational radio patents underwrote the [[processes/development-of-radio|development of radio]], legally recognized only posthumously.

His visionary program — global wireless power — was never realized and is generally held unworkable on the terms he proposed; expert reassessment (Marincic, Golka) judges the planetary scheme impracticable while crediting some of his terrestrial-resonance measurements (Corum) as essentially sound. The SI unit of magnetic flux density, the **tesla**, was named for him in 1956. Posthumously he became one of the most mythologized figures in the history of technology — the lone genius cheated by financiers, the suppressed prophet of free energy — a legend Seifer treats as a historical object in its own right, distinguishing the documented engineer from the speculative inventions that fuel the cult.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **Primary sources.** Tesla's autobiography *My Inventions* (serialized 1919) supplies much of the early narrative but is self-shaped and retrospective; his *Colorado Springs Notes* (1899–1900, recovered from Belgrade) are a richer technical record. The Tesla–Morgan correspondence (quoted at length by Seifer) is decisive for Wardenclyffe; the FBI/OAP files document the seizure. The contemporary technical press (*Electrical Review*, *Electrical Engineer*) and patent record let Seifer adjudicate priority disputes.
- **Conflicting accounts.** Priority is contested at every turn: Baily, Deprez, and Ferraris on the rotating field (a court ruled Tesla "transform[ed] the toy of Arago into an engine of power"); Tesla vs. Thomson, Shallenberger, Stanley on AC; Tesla vs. Marconi, Lodge, Fessenden, Stone on radio. Seifer generally credits Tesla's priority while noting genuine independent work by others.
- **Major scholarly lives and debates.** Seifer's *Wizard* (1996) is the archival corrective to the hagiographic earlier lives — O'Neill's *Prodigal Genius* (1944) and Hunt & Draper's *Lightning in His Hand* (1964). The central interpretive divide is **great-man vs. structural**: was AC's triumph Tesla's contingent genius or an over-determined outcome of transformer economics and Westinghouse capital? Seifer leans to genuine contingency in the rotating-field insight while showing how structural forces (finance, patents, the establishment) governed Tesla's fate.
- **Methodological notes.** Seifer, trained in psychology, frames Tesla psychobiographically — reading the compulsions and visualizations as both creative engine and social handicap. This is illuminating but interpretive; the speculative-technology and conspiracy material (death ray, missing papers, Cold-War interest) is reconstruction from fragmentary evidence and should be read as such, not as established fact.
