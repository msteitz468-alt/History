# Nikola Tesla — Detailed Biography

> **Summary & network:** [[actors/tesla-nikola|Nikola Tesla (actor page)]] · **Biography hub:** [[hubs/biographies/biographies-hub|Biographies Hub]] · **Source:** [[sources/seifer-wizard-tesla-1996|Seifer, *Wizard* (1996)]]

A graduate-level analytic study of Tesla's formation, the mechanics of his decisive choices, his intellectual style, and the structural vs. contingent shape of his legacy — drawn from Marc Seifer's archival biography *Wizard* (1996). Tesla is the figure who made [[concepts/alternating-current|polyphase AC power]] practical and so helped electrify the modern world, yet died impoverished, the central puzzle this biography works.

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Nikola Tesla was born during a summer storm in 1856 in **Smiljan**, a hamlet in the province of Lika in the Austro-Hungarian Military Frontier (modern Croatia), the son of a Serbian Orthodox priest. The family were ethnic Serbs; Seifer notes the trade-name origin of the surname — "a trade name like Smith…or Carpenter," describing a woodworking ax. His father **Milutin Tesla** (b. 1819) was a multilingual priest, poet, and political journalist with "a large library"; his mother **Djouka Mandić** (b. 1821), though never taught to read, memorized Serbian epics and, in Tesla's telling, "conceived of many household appliances."

Two formative wounds shaped him. The death of his gifted elder brother **Dane** (c. 1863, thrown by the family horse) left the household permanently grieving: "Anything I did that was creditable merely caused my parents to feel their loss more keenly. So I grew up with little confidence in myself." And he was subject to intense **eidetic imagery** and out-of-body episodes — the same faculty that let him "mold his various creations, and even run and modify them in his mind, before committing them to paper." This capacity for complete mental visualization is, in Seifer's reading, the engine of his inventive method and inseparable from his lifelong compulsions.

A near-fatal bout of cholera produced the decisive bargain of his youth: bedridden, he extracted his father's promise — "'You will go to the best technical institution in the world,' he solemnly replied." Tesla studied at the Higher Real Gymnasium in **Karlovac**, then enrolled in 1875 at the **Polytechnic in Graz**, where a confrontation with Professor Poeschl over a Gramme dynamo planted the obsession of his life. Tesla argued the commutator was unnecessary; Poeschl ridiculed it publicly: "His plan is simply a perpetual motion scheme." Tesla never graduated — he lost his tuition gambling — and after a term at the **University of Prague** (1880, under the philosopher Carl Stumpf) drifted into telephone work in **Budapest**.

The founding moment came there in early 1882. Recovering from a severe nervous collapse (his senses grown so acute he claimed to hear "the ticking of a watch…three rooms away," and that a fly alighting on a table "would cause a dull thud in my ear"), Tesla was walking at sunset reciting Goethe's *Faust* when the principle of the **[[concepts/rotating-magnetic-field|rotating magnetic field]]** arrived whole: "the truth was [suddenly] revealed. I drew with a stick on the sand the diagrams shown six years later in my address before the American Institute of Electrical Engineers." Within two months, he claimed, "I evolved virtually all the types of motors and modifications of the system now identified with my name…It was a mental state of happiness as complete as I have ever known in life. Ideas came in an uninterrupted stream."

Two cautions on this celebrated scene frame the whole biography. First, the entire early narrative descends largely from Tesla's own autobiography *My Inventions* (serialized 1919, nearly four decades later), a self-shaped and frequently mythologizing text — the storm at birth, the static-electricity sparks off the family cat ("Is nature a cat? If so, who strokes its back? It can only be God, I concluded"), the *Faust* recitation. Seifer corroborates what the patent and institutional record allows and flags the rest as Tesla's retrospective self-fashioning. Second, the priority of the rotating-field idea was genuinely shared: Walter Baily had demonstrated a crank-driven proto-version before the Physical Society of London on 28 June 1879, Marcel Deprez had calculated one in 1881, and Galileo Ferraris reached it independently (~1885). What was Tesla's was the practical, commutator-free *system* — a later U.S. court ruling captured the distinction by saying it "remained for the genius of Tesla to…transform the toy of Arago into an engine of power."

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Tesla's path ran through the Edison organization. Working for the **Compagnie Continental Edison** in Paris and then **Strasbourg** (1882–83), he secretly built his first physical AC motor in a machine-shop closet, achieving "rotation effected by alternating currents of different phase, and without sliding contacts or commutator" — to investor indifference. Denied promised compensation, he sailed for **New York** in 1884 and went straight to [[actors/edison-thomas|Edison]]'s Goerck Street works, "thrilled to the marrow by meeting Edison."

The relationship was brief and bruising. Working hours of "10:30 A.M. till 5 A.M. the next day," Tesla redesigned twenty-four of Edison's DC machines and repaired the dynamos on the lighting steamer *Oregon* overnight, earning Edison's praise — "Batchelor, this is a damn good man." But he could not interest Edison in AC, and left when a $50,000 bonus he believed promised for the redesign was dismissed as "an American joke" — "When you become a full-fledged American, you will appreciate an American joke." The encounter crystallized Tesla's lifelong critique of Edison's empirical method: "if he had a needle to find in a haystack he would…examine straw after straw," where "a little theory and calculation would have saved him 90 per cent of the labor." It is the contrast between the two electrical cultures of the age — Edison's industrial trial-and-error against Tesla's theory-first visualization — embodied in a single failed apprenticeship.

The wilderness that followed nearly ended his career. He filed his first U.S. patents (an improved arc lamp, 1885) with the attorney Lemuel Serrell, formed an arc-lighting company in Rahway, New Jersey, and was then forced out of it by his backers Vail and Lane, left "with no other possession than a beautifully engraved certificate of stock of hypothetical value." Bankrupt, he dug ditches through the winter of 1886–87, his "high education in various branches of science, mechanics and literature" seeming "like a mockery." Only then did he find the backers — **Charles Peck** and **Alfred Brown** — who, in spring 1887, set up the **Tesla Electric Company** at 89 Liberty Street.

The breakthrough with Peck and Brown was as much theatrical as technical. To win the skeptical lawyer Peck, Tesla staged a "Columbus's egg" demonstration: a copper egg set inside a polyphase circular winding, which — when he "turned on the current, the egg began to spin" and "stood on its end." The trick made the invisible rotating field visible to a financier, and it secured the laboratory at 89 Liberty Street. The episode is characteristic: Tesla's gift for spectacle was a real instrument of persuasion, and it would later both build and undermine his reputation.

His ascent was then astonishingly fast. He filed the foundational polyphase patents, and on **15 May 1888** delivered "A New Alternating Current Motor" before the **AIEE**, having already filed fourteen of the forty fundamental AC patents. The lecture's significance was structural: where Elihu Thomson's single-circuit AC still needed a commutator, Tesla's used "two or more circuits out of phase with each other" and made the commutator obsolete. Independent validation followed quickly — Professor William Anthony of Cornell tested the motors and found the larger models ran "a little over 60%" efficiency, comparable to the best DC apparatus, and reversed direction so fast "it was almost impossible to tell when the change took place." Within a week (21 May 1888), [[actors/westinghouse-george|George Westinghouse]] — who had already bought the Gaulard-Gibbs transformer rights and watched his AC central-station sales outrun all the DC companies combined — sent his manager Col. Byllesby to Tesla's lab. Westinghouse's strategic calculus was blunt, as he wrote his lawyer: "If the Tesla patents are broad enough to control the alternating motor business, then the Westinghouse Electric Company cannot afford to have others own the patents." The alliance that would electrify America was struck.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**The Westinghouse alliance and the War of the Currents (1888–1895).** Westinghouse licensed the patents on lavish terms — by Seifer's accounting a $5,000 option, a $10,000 purchase, notes, shares, and a royalty of **$2.50 per horsepower** that could have made Tesla one of the richest men of the age — and brought him to Pittsburgh to develop the [[concepts/induction-motor|induction motor]] and resolve the system frequency. The contest with Edison's direct current — the **[[processes/war-of-the-currents|War of the Currents]]** — turned ugly: the Edison camp staged public animal electrocutions (coining the verb "to Westinghouse") and promoted AC for the first electric-chair execution, the botched 1890 Kemmler killing, to brand it "the executioner's current." Then, around 1890, with Westinghouse Electric squeezed by bankers hostile to the royalty, Tesla made the most consequential financial choice of his life: he released the company from the per-horsepower royalty to keep his system alive in the firm best able to deploy it (see §4). AC's transformer-based long-distance economy won anyway, sealed by two demonstrations: the **[[events/worlds-columbian-exposition-1893|1893 World's Columbian Exposition]]**, lit by Westinghouse AC after an underbid against the Edison/GE DC interests, and the **[[events/niagara-falls-power-project-1895|Niagara Falls power project]]** (first power 1895; to Buffalo 1896), where the International Niagara Commission under Lord Kelvin chose AC over DC and compressed air for one of the world's great energy sources — the capstone confirmation of the technology Tesla had given away.

**Celebrity and the high-frequency years (1891–1899).** Dazzling lectures in New York (Columbia, 1891), **London** (Royal Institution / IEE, February 1892) and **Paris** made Tesla a scientific celebrity who lit lamps held in his bare hands and passed high-frequency currents through his body; he became a U.S. citizen and a fixture of Gilded-Age society, close above all to the poet [[actors/morgan-john-pierpont|Robert Underwood Johnson]], editor of *The Century*, and his wife Katharine, and to Mark Twain, who visited the laboratory. He formed the Nikola Tesla Company in 1895. A devastating **lab fire** on 13 March 1895 — originating in a dry-cleaning establishment below — destroyed years of uninsured apparatus (~$250,000) and forced a fresh start at Houston Street. He pursued X-ray ("shadowgraph") imaging in nine articles within two years of Röntgen's 1895 announcement, and in 1898 unveiled a radio-controlled boat (**teleautomaton**) at the Madison Square Garden Electrical Exposition — a demonstration of remote control so far ahead of its audience that it read as magic. Backed by $100,000 from [[actors/morgan-john-pierpont|John Jacob Astor IV]], he went west in 1899 to build his **[[places/colorado-springs|Colorado Springs]]** station, where free power and dry mountain air let him generate voltages exceeding three to four million volts, throw 135-foot artificial lightning bolts that shorted out the town's dynamo, and record terrestrial "stationary waves" — and what he took to be regular signals possibly from space. (Seifer's reconstruction: the three-beat signal Tesla received on ~28 July 1899 plausibly coincided with Marconi's Morse-S during English Channel naval trials.)

**Wardenclyffe and ruin (1900–1906).** Financed by [[actors/morgan-john-pierpont|J. P. Morgan]] under a 1 March 1901 contract ($150,000 for 51% of the patents), Tesla began **[[places/wardenclyffe|Wardenclyffe]]** on Long Island, designed by Stanford White. After [[actors/marconi-guglielmo|Marconi]]'s December 1901 transatlantic signal — achieved, Tesla noted bitterly, by "secretly employing" his own apparatus — he abandoned the "agreed-upon scheme of building a modest-sized transmitter" for a 600-foot global power-and-telegraphy system, and the costs spiraled. Morgan's refusals came in a remorseless series ("I should not feel disposed at present to make any further advances," July 1903; "I am not willing to advance you any more money as I have frequently told you," December 1904), and his weight scuttled a rival rescue by Thomas Fortune Ryan. Tesla's appeals grew anguished: "there has been hardly a night when my pillow is not bathed in tears, but you must not think me a weak man for that." Having spent "about $250,000 in all," he fired the silent tower briefly in July 1903, then watched it fall silent for good. In 1906 he suffered a breakdown so severe that "his handwriting completely disintegrated."

**Decline and late inventions (1907–1943).** Tesla pivoted to a **bladeless (disk) turbine** (claiming "ten horse power to a pound of weight…twenty-five times as powerful as the lightest weight engines in use today"), the **[[actors/marconi-guglielmo|Hammond]]** remote-control partnership (the steering of an unmanned boat from shore), and small royalty deals (a Waltham "Air-Friction Speedometer"; turbine contracts with Pyle National, Allis-Chalmers, and Budd). None made him solvent: Allis-Chalmers' engineer found the turbine only 38% efficient with disks that fatigued and cracked at 10,000–35,000 rpm. He slid from the Waldorf-Astoria — which he left in 1917 owing nearly $19,000, signing Wardenclyffe over against the debt before it was dynamited that year — through the St. Regis, the Pennsylvania (evicted in 1930, "$2000 behind in his rent," partly over his pigeons), the Governor Clinton, and finally the Hotel New Yorker, sustained late by a Westinghouse consulting stipend ($125/month, from January 1934) and a Yugoslav endowment ($600/month, from 1937). He received the Edison Medal in 1917 and, in 1915, the false front-page news that he and Edison would share a Nobel Prize neither ever received. From 1931 he used annual birthday press conferences to announce grand, unverifiable inventions — a cosmic-ray motor, particles "exceeding fifty times" the speed of light, and the particle-beam **[[concepts/death-ray-teleforce|"teleforce" / death ray]]** ("it will bring down a fleet of 10,000 enemy airplanes at a distance of 250 miles"). He died alone in the Hotel New Yorker on 7 January 1943, found the next morning by a maid.

The **release of the Westinghouse royalty** is the hinge of the whole life. Westinghouse had agreed to pay $2.50 per horsepower; by Seifer's accounting the contract could have yielded a colossal fortune. When bankers pressed Westinghouse to shed the obligation, Tesla — to keep his system alive in the company best able to deploy it — gave it up. It was a structurally rational act (AC survived; the world electrified) and a personally ruinous one (he kept neither the royalty nor, later, control of his wireless patents under Morgan). The man who electrified the planet thereby ensured he would not profit from it.

The **Wardenclyffe escalation** is the hinge's mirror image: where the royalty release sacrificed money to save the technology, the decision to enlarge Wardenclyffe sacrificed a feasible plan to an infeasible vision. Tesla had Morgan's money for a defined deliverable — transatlantic telegraphy. Stung by Marconi's success and convinced he could leap past mere signaling to the wireless transmission of *power*, he secretly redrew the project around a goal no contemporary financier would knowingly fund and no contemporary physics could deliver. The counterfactual is pointed: a modest tower built on the agreed plan might have given Tesla a profitable wireless concern and a durable second act; the grandeur ensured the opposite. The two hinge decisions together explain the central paradox — twin failures of *financial control*, one altruistic and one hubristic, that left the era's most consequential inventor dependent on a Westinghouse stipend and a foreign pension.

## 5. Intellectual Style and Methods

Tesla's distinguishing method was **complete mental prototyping**: he designed, ran, and refined machines entirely in his visual imagination before building them, contrasting himself explicitly with Edison's brute trial and error — "if he had a needle to find in a haystack he would…examine straw after straw," where "a little theory and calculation would have saved him 90 per cent of the labor." His genius was for *systems*: the polyphase insight was not a single device but a complete generation-transmission-motor architecture, and his wireless work likewise aimed at a planetary "World System."

That same intuitive, image-driven cast of mind produced his weakness: a tendency to treat vividly imagined results as accomplished facts. The Colorado Springs "received signals," the cosmic-ray motor, the faster-than-light particle claims (he asserted solar particles "exceeding fifty times that of light," and that his oscillations reached the earth's antipode at "471,200 kilometers per second—57% greater than that of the so-called Hertz waves"), and the death ray were all announced with the conviction of demonstrated engineering. He worked by resonance and visualization, not by the falsification-minded experimental culture of the rising physics establishment, and increasingly spoke past it — rejecting even Einstein's curved space-time as "self-contradictory," reasoning that "the curved spaces must react on the bodies and, producing the opposite effect, straighten out the curves." His superstitions (fixation on the number three, compulsive routines, an extreme germophobia, an aversion to pearls and round objects) were of a piece with the same obsessive, pattern-saturated mind that could hold an entire machine in view at once.

A second methodological signature was his command of *spectacle and prophecy* as instruments. From the spinning egg that won Peck, to the body-as-conductor lecture demonstrations, to the teleautomaton, to the annual birthday press conferences, Tesla understood that a vivid public image could substitute for — and sometimes outrun — a finished product. This served him brilliantly in the AC and high-frequency years, when the demonstrations were backed by real, patentable engineering, and disastrously later, when the spectacle (death ray, free energy, communication with Mars) had no demonstrable apparatus behind it. The continuity between the genius and the crank lies precisely here: the same faculty drove both.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Seifer's Tesla is courtly, abstemious, and solitary — an "absolute celibate" (in friend Kenneth Swezey's phrase) who slept little, dressed immaculately, and formed his deepest attachments to a few intimates and, notoriously, to pigeons. His closest emotional bond in America was with the **Johnsons** — Robert Underwood Johnson, editor of *The Century*, and especially Katharine, whose death in 1925 he mourned deeply. He was admired by women of society (Anne Morgan among them) but never married.

His patron relationships structured his fate. With **Westinghouse** he was generous to his own ruin; with the elder **Morgan** he was proud and supplicating by turns, capable in the same crisis of self-abasement and grandeur — "I came to enlist your genius and power, not because of money…You are a big man, but your work is wrought in passing form, mine is immortal." With **Marconi** he was embittered, convinced (with cause in the patent record) of stolen priority. Rivalries with **Pupin** — estranged since the First World War, reconciled only at Pupin's deathbed in a scene of mutual weeping — and the broader establishment that wrote him out of textbooks (Steinmetz quietly removing his name from a republished AC treatise) left him increasingly isolated.

His most revealing late attachment was to the pigeons he fed in New York parks and hotel rooms, and to one white female bird in particular: "I loved that pigeon as a man loves a woman, and she loved me…When that pigeon died, something went out of my life. I knew that my life's work was finished." Friends recorded an ascetic, courtly recluse — Kenneth Swezey called him "an absolute celibate" who slept less than two hours a night — who refused to die rich ("I'll never die rich unless the money comes in the door faster than I can shovel it out the window"). The late birthday press conferences were, in part, a lonely and impoverished man's bid to remain visible to a world that had absorbed his inventions while forgetting him; King Peter of Yugoslavia, visiting shortly before the end, wept with him "for all the sorrows that had torn apart [their] homeland."

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Tesla died on **7 January 1943**, aged 86, found the next morning by a maid in the Hotel New Yorker. His Serbian-rite funeral filled the Cathedral of St. John the Divine; Mayor La Guardia read a eulogy on the radio and Eleanor Roosevelt sent condolences. Within a day the **Office of Alien Property** (the one agency able to seize "enemy assets" without a court order), with the FBI and military intelligence, impounded his papers — motivated largely by his death-ray claims and fear that his heir **Sava Kosanović** might pass material to the Soviets. MIT's **John G. Trump** examined the trunks (26–27 January 1943) and judged them "nothing of value for the war effort"; the famous death-ray "model" in a hotel vault proved to be a common resistance box. The papers stayed under federal control about a decade before 80 trunks went to the **Tesla Museum in Belgrade** (1952).

The famous death-ray "working model" Tesla had lodged as collateral for unpaid hotel rent — telling the staff it was worth $10,000 — proved, when MIT's Trump opened locker no. 103, to be a common multidecade resistance box "found in every electric laboratory before the turn of the century." Trump concluded the last fifteen years of Tesla's work were "primarily of a speculative, philosophical and somewhat promotional character." Yet the secrecy persisted: the 1937 teleforce treatise remained classified for decades, and FBI exhibits alleged Tesla had sold particle-beam plans to a Soviet Amtorg agent — the kernel of an enduring Cold-War conspiracy literature that Seifer treats as reconstruction, not fact.

Months after his death, in 1943, the **U.S. Supreme Court** upheld key prior wireless patents — Tesla's among them — over Marconi's, the vindication he had not lived to see (see [[events/marconi-radio-priority-dispute-1943]]).

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Tesla's structural legacy is enormous and largely anonymous: the [[concepts/alternating-current|polyphase AC system]] and the [[concepts/induction-motor|induction motor]] are the basis of modern power generation, transmission, and industrial drive — the motive core of [[processes/electrification|electrification]] and the [[processes/second-industrial-revolution|Second Industrial Revolution]]. B. A. Behrend's 1917 Edison Medal tribute captured the scale of dependence: "Were we to seize and to eliminate…the results of Mr. Tesla's work, the wheels of industry would cease to turn, our towns would be dark, our mills would be dead and idle." That this debt is invisible to most users of electric power — who have heard of Edison and not of the man whose motor turns in their appliances — is itself part of the legacy, and a driver of the compensatory mythology. His foundational radio patents underwrote the [[processes/development-of-radio|development of radio]], legally recognized only posthumously, and his remote-control work prefigured a century of teleautomation.

His visionary program — global wireless power — was never realized and is generally held unworkable on the terms he proposed; expert reassessment at the 1990 International Tesla Conference (Marincic, Golka) judged the planetary power scheme impracticable, while a narrower technical claim (Corum) — that Tesla's 1900 figures imply genuine terrestrial-resonance measurements rebounding from the earth's antipode — was credited as essentially sound, and Leland Anderson argued the Colorado Springs "stationary waves" were probably local reinforcement effects off Pikes Peak. The SI unit of magnetic flux density, the **tesla**, was adopted in his honor in 1956, the year of a centennial congress addressed by Niels Bohr; a Liberty ship, a U.S. commemorative stamp (1983), and statues at Niagara and Gospić followed.

Posthumously he became one of the most mythologized figures in the history of technology — the lone genius cheated by financiers, the suppressed prophet of free energy and death rays, even (in the occult fringe Seifer opens the book with) an avatar "not born of this planet." That mythology has a long reach: in 1995 followers of the Aum Supreme Truth cult infiltrated the Belgrade museum seeking schematics for a Tesla "earthquake machine." Seifer's distinctive move is to treat this legend as a historical object in its own right — tracing its growth through O'Neill's 1944 hagiography, the Cold-War secrecy around the seized papers, and the free-energy subculture — while insisting on the separation between the documented engineer who electrified the world and the speculative inventions that fuel the cult.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **Primary sources.** Tesla's autobiography *My Inventions* (serialized 1919) supplies much of the early narrative but is self-shaped and retrospective; his *Colorado Springs Notes* (1899–1900, recovered from Belgrade) are a richer technical record. The Tesla–Morgan correspondence (quoted at length by Seifer) is decisive for Wardenclyffe; the FBI/OAP files document the seizure. The contemporary technical press (*Electrical Review*, *Electrical Engineer*) and patent record let Seifer adjudicate priority disputes.
- **Conflicting accounts.** Priority is contested at every turn, and the wiki should hold these as live disputes rather than settled facts. On the rotating field: Baily (1879), Deprez (1881), and Ferraris (~1885) all have claims, though a U.S. court ruled Tesla "transform[ed] the toy of Arago into an engine of power." On AC: Thomson (single-circuit, commutator-dependent), Shallenberger (an induction meter built weeks before Tesla's AIEE lecture), and Stanley (the Great Barrington transformer work) all worked the same ground. On radio the dispute is sharpest: Tesla's foundational tuned-circuit patents predate Marconi, the Patent Commissioner in 1900 derided Marconi's "pretended ignorance of the nature of a 'Tesla oscillator'," a French court (Bonjean) ruled for Tesla, and John Stone Stone testified in 1915 that "we all misunderstood Tesla…he was so far ahead of his time that the best of us mistook him for a dreamer" — yet Lodge, Braun, Fessenden, Stone himself, and Marconi's practical engineering all have legitimate stakes, and Tesla had let a key British patent lapse. Seifer generally credits Tesla's priority while documenting the genuine independent work of others; a sceptic would weight the others more heavily.
- **Major scholarly lives and debates.** Seifer's *Wizard* (1996) is the archival corrective to the hagiographic earlier lives — O'Neill's *Prodigal Genius* (1944) and Hunt & Draper's *Lightning in His Hand* (1964). The central interpretive divide is **great-man vs. structural**: was AC's triumph Tesla's contingent genius or an over-determined outcome of transformer economics and Westinghouse capital? Seifer leans to genuine contingency in the rotating-field insight while showing how structural forces (finance, patents, the establishment) governed Tesla's fate.
- **Methodological notes.** Seifer, trained in psychology, frames Tesla psychobiographically — reading the compulsions (the number three, germophobia, the photographic visualization, the love of the pigeon) as both creative engine and social handicap. This is illuminating but interpretive, and the usual psychohistory cautions apply: a retrospective diagnostic vocabulary is laid over a self-mythologizing autobiography. The book's other methodological strain is genuinely strong — archival reconstruction of finance and patents from the Tesla Museum, Library of Congress, Columbia, the Westinghouse and Morgan papers, and the declassified FBI/Office of Alien Property files Seifer obtained under FOIA. The speculative-technology and conspiracy material (death ray, missing papers, Soviet Amtorg sale, Cold-War interest) is reconstruction from fragmentary evidence and should be read as such, not as established fact. The wiki's stated next step is to cross-check Seifer against a more sceptical modern life — W. Bernard Carlson's *Tesla: Inventor of the Electrical Age* (2013) — and, if the divergence on the late inventions and the Tesla legend proves substantial, to promote it to a dedicated controversy page.
