A graduate-level analytic study of Vincent van Gogh's formation, the mechanics of his artistic decisions, the "fanatic heart" as driver of both failure and breakthrough, primary-source foundations (the letters), and the structural vs. contingent elements of his legacy — at substantially greater depth than the standard `actors/` page.

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Vincent Willem van Gogh was born 30 March 1853 in the Dutch Reformed parsonage at Zundert, North Brabant, exactly one year after his parents had buried a stillborn son of the same name. The coincidence was never lost on the family or on Vincent himself. Anna Carbentus van Gogh brought a dark, fearful worldview shaped by floods, wars, and personal loss (“disappointments will never cease”); she schooled her children in garden symbolism—violets for courage, ivy for resurrection. Dorus van Gogh, the persistent “sower,” preached Galatians in the tiny Protestant outpost on the Catholic “papist heath.” The family lived by the “holy trinity” of Duty, Decency, and Solidity (degelijkheid). Vincent grew up as the strange, obstinate, “least pleasant” child—obsessively collecting and labeling beetles, flowers, and nests in the attic, already turning the world into catalogued types.

Schooling was a series of failures and flights: beatings and truancy in Zundert, misery at Provily’s in Zevenbergen (likened to an asylum), and a walk-out from Tilburg. At sixteen he entered Goupil & Cie in The Hague through uncle Cent’s influence and threw himself into the work with fanatic energy, mastering thousands of prints daily and beginning his own collection. The 1872 walk to Rijswijk with Theo became the mythic founding of their lifelong correspondence. But by 1876 the ground gave way: dismissal from Goupil in Paris was felt as personal catastrophe (“the ground gave way under my feet”). Religious fervor followed—first sermon in England on “I am a stranger on the earth,” punishing pilgrimages, self-mortification in Amsterdam while cramming Latin and Greek for the ministry, identification with the Prodigal Son. Family viewed him as “always strange” and “daffy with piety.” The Borinage evangelical mission (1879–80) ended in failure and humiliation among the miners. At twenty-seven, after one last family explosion at Christmas 1881, he chose drawing over the Church and the respectable path. “I have no other choice.”

The structural inheritance was clear: parsonage intensity without the parish, collecting habit without a museum, religious language that survived loss of orthodoxy, and a father’s model of patient sowing through adversity. The contingent fuel was repeated vocational rejection plus the unconditional monthly allowance from Theo that turned survival into a full-time artistic vocation. Without that allowance the volume and speed of work would have been impossible; without the early pattern of all-or-nothing zeal the work would not have carried its particular voltage.

From the subagent range 00 material: Vincent’s childhood in Zundert was marked by the shadow of the stillborn brother who shared his exact name and birthdate a year earlier. Anna van Gogh’s worldview was shaped by repeated family tragedies and a Calvinist sense that “disappointments will never cease.” She used the garden behind the parsonage as a living catechism—violets stood for courage, ivy promised life after death, and the sun itself was “the Sweet Lord.” Vincent absorbed this symbolic language early. By age ten or eleven he was already a compulsive classifier, catching beetles along the Grote Beek, pinning them in labeled boxes with Latin names, and spending hours in the attic studying them. Family visitors and siblings described him as “een oarige” (a strange one), obstinate, unruly, and self-willed. He received harsher punishments than his siblings and was often the least pleasant child at the table. Formal schooling repeatedly failed him: truancy and beatings at the local school, misery at the Provily boarding school in Zevenbergen (which he compared to an asylum), and a sudden walk-out from the secondary school in Tilburg in March 1868. 

At sixteen, through the influence of his wealthy uncle Cent, he entered Goupil & Cie in The Hague as a clerk. He threw himself into the work with the same fanatic energy that would later define his painting. He mastered the vast stock of prints, advised customers, and began building his own collection. Yet the same intensity that made him effective also isolated him. Even during his relatively successful early years at Goupil, he preferred solitary walks to social life and carried the “unshakable isolation” of the Zundert moors with him. The pivotal August 1872 walk along the towpath to Rijswijk with the younger Theo—drinking milk at the mill, talking until late—became the mythic origin of their lifelong correspondence and Theo’s role as both brother and patron. By 1876 the ground had given way under him. Dismissal from Goupil’s Paris branch felt like total personal catastrophe. Religious fervor rushed in to fill the void: the first sermon in England on the text “I am a stranger on the earth,” punishing long-distance walks as moral penance, and a period of extreme self-mortification in Amsterdam while cramming for theological exams—sleeping outdoors in winter, living on bread, beating himself with his walking stick. Family letters record their alarm: he was “always strange,” “daffy with piety,” a “kerkdraver.” The Borinage mission of 1879–80 among the Belgian miners ended in humiliation and physical breakdown. At twenty-seven, after the final Christmas 1881 explosion with his father (“Get out of my house”), Vincent made the irrevocable choice for art. (Drawn from R00–R01 claims and Prologue/early chapters.)

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

After the Borinage, Vincent declared himself an artist in 1880 and moved to Brussels, then Etten, then The Hague. Cousin Anton Mauve gave initial watercolor lessons; Vincent drew live models from the streets and almshouses (“orphan man” Zuyderland) with obsessive intensity. The relationship with pregnant prostitute Sien Hoornik (1882) produced the drawing *Sorrow* and a brief domestic “nest,” but ended under family and Theo pressure. Expelled from the Etten parsonage on Christmas Day 1881 (“Get out of my house”), he broke with Mauve over plaster casts vs. living models and with Tersteeg over the commercial verdict “you are no artist.”

In Nuenen (1883–85) he produced the weaver series and *The Potato Eaters* (1885), defended by color laws from Charles Blanc yet attacked by Rappard for anatomical errors. A pregnancy scandal involving model Gordina and the local priest’s ban on posing drove him out. Antwerp academy classes were brief. The decisive rise occurred in Paris (1886–88) living with Theo: exposure to Impressionism, Seurat, Japanese prints, and the shift from “green soap” earth palette to broken color and complementary contrasts. In the rue Lepic apartment the brothers achieved a rare period of unity while Vincent painted urban views and still lifes and absorbed the latest color theories. The move to Arles in February 1888 was the bid for independence and the founding of the “Studio of the South.” He arrived in the middle of the harshest winter in a decade and immediately began the “mercenary frenzy” of letters urging Theo on commercial strategy while producing the first orchard and Langlois bridge studies. The invitation to Gauguin was both a plain business proposition (“my brother would send 250 francs a month for both of us”) and a utopian fantasy of artistic brotherhood modeled on medieval guilds and Japanese bonzes. The 200+ works of the first Arles months—Sunflowers, the Yellow House interiors, the harvest series, the Roulin portraits—established the scale, speed, and chromatic signature that would define his contribution. Theo’s continuing 150–250 francs monthly remained the material base; the letters functioned as the running theoretical laboratory in which every technical decision was argued out. (Drawn from R01–R04 claims.)

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Dutch Phase (1880–1886)**: After the Borinage collapse, Vincent committed to drawing “from life” among the poor. In The Hague he produced hundreds of figure studies under Mauve’s early guidance and lived briefly with Sien. Nuenen saw the weaver drawings, the large *Potato Eaters*, and the first systematic color theory. Objective: to be a “peasant painter” in the line of Millet. Constraint: family shame and poverty. Turning point: the Christmas 1881 expulsion and the 1885 priest ban. Result: a coherent body of dark, socially committed work and the irreversible break with respectable bourgeois life.

**Paris Phase (1886–1888)**: Living with Theo on the rue Lepic, Vincent absorbed Impressionist and Neo-Impressionist color, Japanese prints, and the example of the modern art market. He lightened his palette, adopted divided touch, and painted urban views, still lifes, and portraits. Objective: catch up with the avant-garde. Constraint: the overstimulation of the city and dependence on Theo. Turning point: the decision to seek the “real South” and Japanese light of Provence. Result: the technical foundation for the Arles explosion and the first sales through Theo.

**Arles Phase (February 1888 – May 1889)**: In the Yellow House Vincent produced over 200 works in fifteen months. He painted blooming orchards in every available style (Monet-like, Seurat dots, Bernard Cloisonnism), the Sunflower series, the Night Café, Bedroom in Arles, and multiple versions of the sower and harvest. He rented the dilapidated yellow building at 2 place Lamartine explicitly as a “Studio of the South” and a refuge for “poor cab horses of Paris.” The arrival of Gauguin on 23 October 1888 at first seemed to vindicate the dream—Gauguin was in robust health and immediately began working from memory indoors while Vincent painted en plein air with furious speed. They argued about abstraction versus direct observation; Gauguin painted the Night Café entirely from imagination, while Vincent countered with the Yellow Chair and rapid Roulin family portraits as assertions of tangible reality. Tension mounted over money (Gauguin received a large sale while Vincent had none), artistic authority, and daily habits. The rupture came in late December. Vincent mutilated his ear, was hospitalized, and the Yellow House project collapsed. Gauguin left. The two months produced some of Vincent’s greatest paintings and also the crisis that led directly to voluntary commitment at Saint-Rémy. Objective: found a “Studio of the South.” Constraint: loneliness, debt, local hostility, and incompatible temperaments. Turning point: the ear incident and hospitalization. Result: the core of the mature style and the first institutionalization. (Drawn from R04–R05 subagent claims.)

**Asylum and Final Phase (May 1889 – July 1890)**: At Saint-Rémy (Saint-Paul-de-Mausole) Vincent painted from the garden and escorted fields, copied Millet and Delacroix, and produced *Starry Night*, *Irises*, cypresses, and olive groves between attacks of epilepsy/hallucination. In Auvers under Gachet he painted at least seventy final canvases in seventy days, including the double-square wheatfields and *Wheatfield with Crows*. Objective: continue working under supervision while seeking greater independence. Constraint: recurring illness and isolation. Turning point: the 27 July 1890 shooting. Result: the last great series and posthumous legend. (Phases synthesized from R04–R08 claims.)

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military / Religious Style and Methods

Vincent’s method was total identification of life and work: “As my work is, so am I” and “I want to paint what I feel, and feel what I paint.” From the very beginning of his artistic career he insisted that “in general, and more especially with artists, I pay as much attention to the man who does the work as to the work itself,” directly echoing Zola’s demand to look for “the man” in a picture. Color was treated as an autonomous emotional language governed by laws of simultaneous contrast (Blanc) yet ultimately driven by passion rather than rules. He repeatedly declared that he painted “in one rush” or “premier coup,” producing serial studies of the same motif—Sunflowers, orchards, wheatfields—to capture changing light, weather, and inner states. The nearly 900 surviving letters to Theo functioned as a parallel artwork and running theoretical treatise—often written the evening before or the morning after a major canvas, describing the next day’s planned attack on color in advance.

Copying after Millet, Delacroix, and Rembrandt served as dialogue, discipline, and consolation during illness. He absorbed Japanese prints (which he called “as beautiful as flowers”), Impressionist division of color, and Gauguin’s advocacy of memory and abstraction, then swung back to direct observation when abstraction felt like a “brick wall.” In Arles he told Theo he had “allowed myself to be led astray” by Gauguin’s methods and then had his “fill of that.” Risk was embraced as moral necessity: “There is safety in the very heart of danger.” Moderation, steady employment, and social compromise were repeatedly rejected as betrayals of the self. The religious language of sowing, pilgrimage, and resurrection survived the loss of orthodox faith and was transposed wholesale into the language of color and labor. Contrasted with Gauguin’s slower, memory-based, “head-centered” method (Gauguin insisted his artistic center was “in my brain and not elsewhere”), Vincent’s practice remained rooted in observed reality even at its most visionary. The subagent extractions show this tension playing out in real time: Gauguin painting the Night Café entirely from imagination while Vincent raced to finish his version from the actual café; Gauguin working three or four careful canvases while Vincent blazed through a dozen. (Synthesized from R04–R06 claims and Prologue.)

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Contemporary witnesses described Vincent as strange, obstinate, difficult, and “vraiment la laideur personnifiée” to some Arlesians, yet capable of boisterous jokes, infinite sympathy, and encyclopedic enthusiasm. Theo, in the Prologue, recalled the early bond as “adoration” and described Vincent as adventurous guide, droll critic, and transfixing eye. The letters reveal a proud, witty, theoretically articulate man who could also be domineering, self-pitying, and impossible in daily life. He demanded absolute loyalty and absolute truth from everyone around him and gave the same in return. Attempts at domesticity (Sien in The Hague, the hoped-for marriage to Kee Vos, the Yellow House experiment) repeatedly collapsed under the demands of the work and his temperament. Gauguin found him both inspiring and exhausting—“Vincent is working himself to death,” Gauguin wrote his wife—while Vincent envied Gauguin’s social success with Arlésiennes. Gachet was judged “sicker than I am”; Vincent concluded that “when one blind man leads another blind man, don’t they both fall into the ditch?” Mental crises (hallucinations, attacks, institutionalization) are documented in medical notes and letters, but the sources insist the “fanatic heart” was present from childhood and not merely the product of illness.

From the claims: in Zundert Vincent was already the collector and categorizer who would later paint serial Sunflowers and wheatfields. The Etten and The Hague breaks (Kee’s “never, no, never,” the Mauve rupture over plaster casts versus living models, Tersteeg’s blunt verdict “you are no artist”) hardened the identification with the marginalized that produced the weavers and *The Potato Eaters*. In Arles the same intensity that produced 200 works in 15 months also made cohabitation with Gauguin impossible within two months. In Saint-Rémy the discipline of Millet copies coexisted with attacks of eating dirt, drinking kerosene, and painting while confined. In Auvers the obsessive campaign for Theo, Jo, and the baby to relocate showed both desperate need for connection and the inability to moderate emotional or practical demands. The “fanatic heart” was not an illness but the constant engine; illness only removed the remaining brakes. Theo’s own collapse six months after Vincent’s death proved the symbiosis was mortal. (Drawn from Prologue, R00, R05, R08, and Epilogue.)

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

On 27 July 1890 Vincent returned from the fields to the Ravoux Inn wounded in the abdomen (“Je me suis blessé”), was examined by the doctors (small oblique entry wound, no exit), and lingered until the early hours of 29 July, dying in Theo’s arms with the words “I want to die like this.” The gun was never found; no one had witnessed the shooting. Theo organized the modest funeral (no church service or parish hearse), displayed recent canvases around the bier, and immediately began the campaign that “he will not be forgotten.” Within weeks Theo’s own health collapsed; he was institutionalized in October and died 25 January 1891. The paintings and letters passed to Jo Bonger, who later edited the correspondence and mounted the exhibitions that secured the reputation. Vincent’s own statements were hedged (“Yes, I believe so”); the traditional suicide narrative sits uneasily with his prior condemnation of suicide, the physical evidence, and later local testimony favoring an accidental shooting involving local youths and the Ravoux pistol. (R08 + Epilogue + Note on the Fatal Wounding.)

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Vincent’s work helped define Post-Impressionism and opened the path to Fauvism and Expressionism. Color as autonomous emotional force, visible brushwork as signature of authenticity, and the ordinary (peasants, chairs, wheat) as epic subject changed what painting could be. The myth of the suffering modern artist—ear incident, suicide, posthumous recognition—became cultural property more powerful than any single canvas. Major institutions (Van Gogh Museum, Kröller-Müller) and market valuation followed Jo’s editions of the letters and early exhibitions. The 2011 Naifeh & Smith revision on the manner of death (accident rather than suicide) has reopened biographical debate. Influence extends far beyond art history into popular imagery, tourism, and the archetype of the dedicated, tormented creator. The letters remain among the greatest artist documents ever published.

From the subagent extractions: the “fanatic heart” thesis runs from the Prologue through the Epilogue. Vincent’s collecting passion in Zundert prefigured the serial production of Arles. The Etten expulsions and The Hague breaks hardened the identification with the marginalized that produced the weavers and Potato Eaters. Paris supplied the technical tools; Arles the subject matter and velocity. Saint-Rémy demonstrated that the work could survive the illness. Auvers showed both the final serenity and the fragility of the support system. Theo’s collapse proved the symbiosis was mortal. The reburial in 1914 with matching stones sealed the legend of the two brothers. (R00–R08 + Epilogue synthesis.)

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

The nearly 900 letters between Vincent and Theo (plus family correspondence) are the unmatched primary spine—intimate, theoretical, and often written the day before or after the canvases they describe. Key verbatim passages (e.g., “I am a fanatic! I feel a power within me…”, “As my work is, so am I”, the last “I want to die like this”) anchor every phase. Naifeh & Smith (2011) is the current exhaustive documentary life, using the full correspondence, medical records, and re-interviewed local testimony (Rewald-era oral history, Adeline Ravoux, Secrétan family) to argue the 27 July 1890 shooting was almost certainly accidental (Secrétan boys + Ravoux pistol) rather than suicide. Earlier accounts (Irving Stone’s *Lust for Life*) amplified the mad-genius and suicide myths for dramatic effect. Art-historical studies (Hulsker chronology, Van Uitert iconography, Zemel social context, Silverman religious reading) separate formal and thematic analysis from biography. The ear incident and death remain subjects of continuing dispute; the 2011 revision has been influential but not universally accepted. Methodologically the book combines exhaustive letter quotation with psychological framing (“fanatic heart”) and detective work on the death; it gives presumptive weight on the life narrative while cross-reference with catalogues raisonnés remains necessary for painting-by-painting questions. The subagent extractions confirm that the letters’ self-documenting density makes this one of the best-sourced artistic lives of the 19th century. (R00–R08 + Epilogue + source page reliability notes.)

## Additional Depth from Subagent Claims (R00–R08)

R00 established the Zundert formation in rich detail: the stillborn brother who shared Vincent’s exact name and birthdate one year earlier; Anna Carbentus van Gogh’s dark, fearful worldview shaped by family tragedies (“disappointments will never cease”) and expressed through the garden behind the parsonage (violets for courage, ivy for resurrection, the sun as “the Sweet Lord”); the “strange obstinate boy” (“een oarige”) who obsessively collected and classified wildflowers, birds’ eggs, and especially beetles along the Grote Beek, pinning them in labeled boxes with Latin names and studying them for hours in the shared attic; the repeated educational failures and flights (truancy and beatings in Zundert, misery at Provily’s in Zevenbergen which he compared to an asylum, and walking out of Tilburg school); the Goupil apprenticeship in The Hague where he worked with fanatic energy yet carried “unshakable isolation”; the August 1872 walk to Rijswijk with Theo that became the mythic foundation of their lifelong bond; and the core artistic conviction, stated in the Prologue, that “as my work is, so am I” and that biography was the truest key to understanding art.

R01 traced the religious phase with painful specificity: the first sermon in England on the text “I am a stranger on the earth,” the punishing long-distance walks across the English countryside as moral penance, the extreme self-mortification in Amsterdam while cramming Latin, Greek, and mathematics for the ministry (sleeping outdoors in winter to increase suffering, living on bread, beating himself with his walking stick), the family’s alarmed verdict that he was “always strange” and “daffy with piety,” the obsessive identification with the Prodigal Son and Ary Scheffer’s painting, and the final, irrevocable Christmas 1881 explosion with his father Dorus (“Get out of my house, the sooner the better”) that made art the only remaining vocation.

R02 covered the Dutch years of commitment and repeated rupture: the return to Etten and immediate copy of Millet’s *Sower*, the intense and sometimes coercive model work among local peasants, the disastrous proposal to cousin Kee Vos and her categorical “never, no, never,” the manic multi-week “aimer encore” letter campaign, the November 1881 family explosion with his father, the move to The Hague and lessons with Mauve, the relationship with pregnant prostitute Sien Hoornik (including support during the difficult birth of her son), the break with Mauve over the use of living models versus plaster casts, Tersteeg’s blunt commercial verdict that Vincent had “started too late” and was “no artist,” the defense of *The Potato Eaters* using Charles Blanc’s scientific color laws, the pregnancy scandal with Gordina de Groot in Nuenen, the local Catholic priest banning parishioners from posing, and the bleak departure through the industrially ravaged peat moors of Drenthe.

R03 captured the Paris color revolution: the move to live with Theo on the rue Lepic, the decisive exposure to Impressionism, Neo-Impressionism, and Japanese prints, the rapid lightening and brightening of his palette away from the dark “green soap” earth tones of the Dutch period, the decision to seek the “real South” and Japanese light of Provence, and the departure from Paris in February 1888.

R04 detailed the Arles arrival and productivity explosion: stepping off the train in the middle of the coldest winter in a decade with snow deep enough to cover his shoes, the immediate “mercenary frenzy” of detailed commercial advice to Theo, the series of blooming orchards painted in every style he had absorbed in Paris, the obsessive return to the Langlois bridge as an image that reconnected him emotionally to his past, the renting of the dilapidated Yellow House at 2 place Lamartine as “paradise,” the elaborate invitation campaign to Gauguin framed simultaneously as sound business (“my brother would send 250 francs a month for both of us”) and utopian artistic brotherhood, and the first major series of Sunflowers, harvest scenes, and sower paintings.

R05 reconstructed the Gauguin crisis with day-by-day texture drawn from the letters: Gauguin’s arrival on 23 October 1888 in robust health that visibly surprised Vincent (“He even seems to me better than I am”); the stark contrast in work methods (Vincent flinging himself at canvases en plein air with loaded brushes while Gauguin worked slowly indoors from memory and sketches); Gauguin’s advocacy of abstraction and “pure thought”; the vineyard memory experiment in which Vincent accepted Gauguin’s challenge to paint the recent grape harvest from memory; Vincent’s rapid swing back to direct observation in the six Roulin family portraits and the assertive Yellow Chair; the growing money tensions (Gauguin receiving a large sale while Vincent had none); and the final rupture that produced the ear mutilation, hospitalization, and collapse of the Yellow House project.

R06 recorded the Saint-Rémy year in clinical and artistic detail: voluntary commitment to the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole (the former monastery), permission to paint in the walled garden and, with an attendant, in the surrounding countryside; the production of *Starry Night* as a personal cosmic vision using swirling spirals and a new palette of violet and ocher; the extensive project of copying Millet’s *Les travaux des champs* and other prints as both discipline during recovery and a way “to console myself”; and the long, violent attacks of summer 1889 involving hallucinations, vertigo, eating dirt, drinking kerosene, and confinement.

R07 covered the move to Auvers and the final works: departure from Saint-Rémy in mid-May 1890 (“CURED” noted by Dr. Peyron), a brief Paris stop with Theo and Jo, arrival in Auvers on 20 May under the nominal supervision of Dr. Paul Gachet; immediate disillusionment (“We must not count on Dr. Gachet at all… when one blind man leads another blind man, don’t they both fall into the ditch?”); the obsessive production of at least seventy canvases in roughly seventy days, including the double-square wheatfields and *Daubigny’s Garden*; and the intense letter campaign urging Theo, Jo, and the newborn baby to relocate to Auvers for the child’s health and a family reunion.

R08 supplied the precise mechanics of the death and its immediate aftermath: Vincent’s return to the Ravoux Inn on the evening of 27 July 1890 holding his belly and limping; the admission to Gustave Ravoux “Je me suis blessé” and the lifting of his shirt to show the small hole under the ribs; the doctors’ findings of a small-caliber pistol wound that had not exited and had entered at an oblique angle from some distance; Vincent’s hedged and protective statements to the police (“Yes, I believe so” but “Do not accuse anyone—it is I who wanted to kill myself”); the deathbed conversation in Dutch lasting through the night; the last words “I want to die like this” spoken while cradled in Theo’s arms around 12:30 a.m. on 29 July; Theo’s immediate, obsessive legacy mission (“He will not be forgotten”) and rapid physical and mental collapse; the modest funeral on 30 July without church service or parish hearse; and the full accident reconstruction advanced by the authors (involving the teenage Secrétan brothers and the pistol acquired from Gustave Ravoux, with Vincent apparently choosing to protect the boys while welcoming death on his own terms). The 1914 reburial organized by Jo placed the two brothers side by side in the Auvers cemetery with matching stones reading “ici REPOSE VINCENT VAN GOGH” and “ici REPOSE THEODORE VAN GOGH.”

These claims ground the “fanatic heart” thesis from the Prologue through the Epilogue and demonstrate that the same temperament that produced vocational disaster also generated the serial intensity and emotional directness of the paintings. Counterfactuals recur at every major turning point: without Theo’s monthly allowance the sheer volume and speed of the output would have been impossible; without the Gauguin rupture the asylum years and the final Auvers burst might never have occurred; a decision to remain at Saint-Rémy might have extended his life and changed the character of the late work. The letters remain the unmatched primary source; Naifeh & Smith supply the contextual density, the medical and local-witness research, and the death revision that most shapes current biographical understanding. The subagent extraction process made visible how every major phase was already latent in the formation: the Zundert collector and classifier became the artist who painted serial Sunflowers and wheatfields; the religious absolutist who could not compromise became the man who painted exactly “what I feel”; the figure who was repeatedly expelled from family and social structures became the artist whose work could not be ignored once the world finally saw it.
