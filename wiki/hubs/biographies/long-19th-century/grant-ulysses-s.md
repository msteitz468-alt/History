## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Grant was born Hiram Ulysses Grant on 27 April 1822 in Point Pleasant, Ohio, the eldest son of Jesse Root Grant, an ambitious and overbearing tanner and businessman, and the reserved Hannah Simpson Grant. The family moved to Georgetown, Ohio, where Grant grew up with a love of horses and a dislike of the tannery work. His father secured an appointment to West Point (the name "Ulysses S. Grant" resulted from a clerical error that Grant accepted).

At West Point he was an indifferent cadet who excelled only in horsemanship and mathematics; he read novels, showed little enthusiasm for military life, and graduated 21st in a class of 39 (1843). The Mexican-American War provided his first real professional experience. Serving under Zachary Taylor and then Winfield Scott, he performed competently in logistics and combat (he was mentioned in dispatches) but was repelled by the human cost and the political dimensions of the war. He observed Scott's operational art and Taylor's calm under fire; both influenced his later style.

After the war, postings to the Pacific Northwest and other remote garrisons separated him from his wife Julia Dent (married 1848, daughter of a slaveholding Missouri family) and their growing children. Lonely, bored, and drinking to excess on at least some occasions, he resigned in 1854 rather than face a court-martial for an alleged episode of intoxication on duty (the exact circumstances remain disputed; Chernow treats it as part of a recurring solitary binge pattern rather than career-ending alcoholism at the time).

The next six years were the "lost years." Grant tried farming on land given by Julia's father ("Hardscrabble"), failed as a real-estate agent and bill collector in St. Louis, and finally took a clerkship in his father's Galena, Illinois, leather goods store in 1860. He lived modestly, was regarded locally as a failure, and was viewed by some in-laws as socially inferior. These years of humiliation, poverty, and dependence forged the stubborn persistence and empathy for the underdog that later characterized his generalship and presidency. They also gave him intimate knowledge of the western theater terrain and river systems that proved invaluable in 1862–63.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

When the Civil War began, Grant's prewar acquaintance with Congressman Elihu B. Washburne of Galena proved decisive. Washburne championed the obscure former captain; Illinois Governor Richard Yates appointed him colonel of the 21st Illinois. Grant quickly demonstrated organizational talent and calm in small actions, assisted by the fiercely loyal John Rawlins, who joined his staff as assistant adjutant general in late August 1861 after the death of his wife.

Grant's first combat test came at Belmont, Missouri (7 November 1861), a muddy steamboat landing opposite the Confederate stronghold at Columbus, Kentucky. Ordered only to make a "demonstration," Grant landed some 3,000 men, overran the rebel camp, but was surprised by reinforcements ferried from Columbus. He showed boldness bordering on impetuosity and preternatural coolness under fire—personally rallying men, ordering "Stop the men, they are wasting ammunition," and escaping a close call when his horse slid down the bank onto a departing transport while rebel bullets flew. "My horse put his fore feet over the bank without hesitation or urging," Grant later wrote in his *Memoirs*, "and with his hind feet well under him, slid down the bank and trotted aboard the boat." Belmont was not a territorial victory and exceeded instructions, previewing Grant's tendency to focus on his offensive while remaining vulnerable to enemy countermoves. Yet it served as his "baptism of fire," broke the mystique of invincible Confederate troops, gave his men lasting confidence, and brought his first national press notice. It also removed lingering civilian doubts about his capacity; he informed Washburne that Confederate losses "proves to be greater and the effect upon the southern mind more saddening." On the same day, [[actors/grant-julia|Julia]] experienced a vision of her husband in peril.

Halleck's assumption of command in November 1861 brought friction. Grant chafed at the department commander's brusque, by-the-book style. Halleck rudely rebuffed Grant's January 1862 presentation of plans for Forts Henry and Donelson, leaving him "crestfallen." Grant persisted, conferring with Flag Officer Andrew Foote and sending a terse telegram: "With Permission I will take Fort McHenry on the Tennessee and hold & establish a large camp there." Halleck finally approved on 30 January. Grant moved with secrecy and dispatch. On 2 February he embarked from Cairo, cuffing Rawlins on the shoulder in rare exuberance once beyond recall: "Now we seem to be safe... We will succeed, Rawlins; we must succeed."

Fort Henry fell easily on 6 February after Foote's gunboats pounded the flooded, low-lying works; its commander evacuated most of the garrison to Fort Donelson. Grant then turned immediately to Donelson, a far stronger position on the Cumberland. After an initial naval repulse that wounded Foote, Grant settled into siege. On 15 February the Confederates under Pillow, Floyd, and Buckner launched a breakout that caught Grant by surprise while he was aboard Foote's flagship (he had missed the racket due to wind direction). Returning to find McClernand's division in retreat, Grant set his jaw and declared: "Gentlemen, the position on the right must be retaken." He deduced from captured haversacks containing three days' rations that the enemy meant to escape rather than stand, focused on their demoralization ("the enemy must be more so"), and ordered an immediate counter. He exploited weakness on the Union left by sending C. F. Smith to attack "a very thin line," personally rallied men ("Fill your cartridge-boxes, quick... the enemy is trying to escape"), and recouped lost ground. That evening he showed humanity, giving brandy to a wounded Union lieutenant and Confederate private alike and ordering stretcher-bearers: "Take this Confederate, too... the war is over between them." He quoted Burns: "Man’s inhumanity to man / makes countless thousands mourn."

On 16 February Buckner requested terms. Grant, sitting on the floor of a farmhouse, composed the historic reply in his *Memoirs*: "Sir; Yours of this date proposing Armistice, and appointment of commissioners, to settle terms of capitulation is just received. No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender can be accepted. I propose to move immediately upon your works." Buckner called the terms "ungenerous and unchivalrous" but accepted. Grant captured over 13,000 men—the largest on the continent to date—treated prisoners mercifully (food, sidearms retained, no humiliating parade), enlisted captured slaves as contraband laborers, and wrote jubilantly to [[actors/grant-julia]]: "This is the largest capture I believe ever made on the continent." To Washburne he compared it favorably to European battles. The North celebrated wildly; Grant was instantly a national hero, dubbed "Unconditional Surrender Grant," promoted to major general, and showered with cigars that became his trademark. Rumors of drinking briefly subsided.

Shiloh (6–7 April 1862) tested Grant's resilience as nothing before. After Henry-Donelson he had moved up the Tennessee toward Corinth, Mississippi, a vital rail junction. Despite skirmishing, Grant and Sherman discounted a major attack ("I have scarcely the faintest idea of an attack... there will be no fight at Pittsburg Landing"). Grant decided against entrenchments, prioritizing drill and discipline for green troops over fortifications, and was absent at Savannah when the Confederate blow fell at dawn on 6 April. A horse fall days earlier had left him on crutches. He heard the cannon at breakfast, hastened to Pittsburg Landing despite injury, was hoisted onto his horse, and rode the battlefield all day "in the midst of a shower of cannon and musket balls," issuing orders "as though he was simply reviewing the troops," cigar in mouth. He organized stragglers, coordinated the Hornets' Nest defense that bought critical time, and rejected any thought of retreat when Buell arrived: "I haven’t despaired of whipping them yet!" "Not beaten yet by a damn sight." "They can’t break our lines tonight. Tomorrow we shall attack with fresh troops..."

That night under an oak in the rain, Sherman found him: "Well, Grant, we’ve had the devil’s own day, haven’t we?" Grant replied, "Yes... Lick ’em tomorrow though." He had already ordered a 4 a.m. counteroffensive. On 7 April, reinforced by Buell and (belatedly) Lew Wallace, Grant's forces regained lost ground with relentless pressure. Beauregard withdrew to Corinth. Grant viewed the first day's stout resistance by half-green troops as the real triumph. Shiloh cost ~24,000 casualties (more than all prior U.S. wars combined) and "peeled away any lingering aura of romance," convincing Grant that only total defeat of southern society would end the war. He had rescued his army from his own errors through personal leadership and indomitable will; "If [Shiloh] had been lost the war would have dragged on for years longer."

Yet the victory brought vilification. Grant was accused of being surprised, arriving late, and failing to pursue. Reporter Whitelaw Reid (AGATE) popularized myths of soldiers bayoneted in tents and Grant's luxurious absence. Drinking charges resurfaced. Grant told Julia he was "as sober as a deacon no matter what is said to the contrary." Contemporaries on the ground (Jacob Ammen, Col. Webster, William Rowley) attested his sobriety. Rawlins was incensed and polled witnesses. Grant confided to an old friend he had "not drunk any for several years" and would not let liquor regain the upper hand. Nevertheless, the storm was fierce; politicians called for his removal. Grant endured through the same persistence forged in failure.

The twin victories at Henry-Donelson and survival at Shiloh propelled Grant from obscure colonel to the Union's most conspicuous western commander within months, setting the stage for Vicksburg. As he wrote Julia after Donelson, "by following up our success we can go anywhere."

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Prewar and Early War (to Shiloh):** See Formation and Rise. Grant entered the war as colonel of Illinois volunteers with a reputation clouded by prewar drinking and failure. Belmont (Nov 1861) was his baptism—bold but imperfect raid that gave troops confidence and removed his own self-doubts while exposing vulnerability to surprise. With Foote's navy he seized the soft underbelly of Albert Sidney Johnston's line at Fort Henry (easy river victory, 6 Feb 1862) then fought the desperate, bloody contest at Fort Donelson. The breakout attempt on 15 Feb caught him by surprise (he had been with Foote), but his rapid personal rally, deduction of enemy intent from haversacks, and counterattack produced the first major western victory and 13,000+ prisoners. "Unconditional Surrender" fame followed. At Shiloh (6–7 Apr) his decision against entrenchments, overconfidence despite skirmishing, and absence at the moment of attack produced a near-catastrophe; half his men had never fought. Yet through all-day personal leadership, equanimity ("Not beaten yet by a damn sight"), the night resolve under the oak ("Lick ’em tomorrow though"), and use of arriving reinforcements for a coordinated counter, he turned it into a strategic Union victory that opened the path to Corinth and Vicksburg. The 24,000 casualties stripped war of romance for him and began his conversion to attrition and total war. Persistence through Halleck's rebuffs, post-battle vilification (Reid's myths, drinking charges refuted on the ground), and personal injury demonstrated the "dogged persistence and iron will" forged in the lost years. Emphasis on learning riverine and expeditionary warfare, combined arms with navy, rapid exploitation of victory, and resilience under political fire.

**Vicksburg and the West (1862–63):** The campaign that demonstrated Grant's operational maturity—deception, logistics innovation (living off the country after Holly Springs), rapid exploitation, and siege craft. Living off the country and splitting Confederate commands (Pemberton vs. Johnston) prefigured later total-war methods. After Shiloh, Grant advanced on Corinth then targeted Vicksburg as the key to opening the Mississippi and splitting the Confederacy. Political intrigue with McClernand (Lincoln's favored independent commander) forced Grant to outmaneuver him; he had Sherman take troops and later assumed personal command after McClernand's diversion to Arkansas Post. Holly Springs raid (Van Dorn, Dec 20 1862) destroyed Grant's supply depot, forcing retreat and the invaluable lesson that "his men could subsist for days, even weeks, off the produce of local farms." Sherman’s solo attack at Chickasaw Bayou failed disastrously amid the blackout. 

To keep his idle, disease-ridden army (floods, smallpox, malaria) occupied while awaiting favorable conditions and to probe approaches, Grant pursued multiple engineering projects: the Williams Canal across the Vicksburg peninsula (collapsed under rising river despite 4,000 black laborers); Yazoo Pass (Lt. Wilson blew levee spectacularly but Fort Pemberton and vegetation blocked it); Lake Providence; and Steele’s Bayou (snakes, alligators, sniper fire). Most failed, drawing Northern mockery ("Grant is getting along at Vicksburg with such rapidity..."), yet they bought time and tested ingenuity amid criticism and drinking rumors. Lincoln stood firm: “what I want… is generals who will fight battles and win victories. Grant has done this, and I propose to stand by him.”

Dana arrived April 6 as Stanton's observer (ostensibly pay service, actually to report on drinking and plans); Grant won him over with candor, disclosing the emerging plan. Dana reported Grant “the most modest, the most disinterested, and the most honest man I ever knew, with a temper that nothing could disturb.” After gunboats tested the batteries (Lancaster sunk, Switzerland passed), Grant executed the master stroke: on the night of April 16, Porter’s fleet (ironclads, gunboats, transports) ran past Vicksburg’s guns under starlight; hay, cotton bales, and sandbags protected boilers; only one transport (Henry Clay) lost, 14 wounded total. A second run April 22 brought 100k rations each. Troops marched down the flooded west bank (mud, alligators, corduroy roads built on the fly). 

Grand Gulf bombardment (April 29) failed; Grant pivoted to Bruinsburg landing (April 30), informed by a local slave of unguarded shore. “When this was effected I felt a degree of relief scarcely ever equalled since… I was on dry ground on the same side of the river with the enemy.” He cut loose from base, marched inland northeast to Jackson rather than straight at Vicksburg, to defeat the enemy in detail and interpose between Pemberton and Johnston. Grierson’s 600-mile cavalry raid (April 17) sowed confusion and destroyed rails; Sherman feinted at Haynes’ Bluff. Victories followed in rapid succession: Port Gibson (May 1), Raymond (May 12), Jackson (May 14—Sherman wrecked the transportation hub and arsenals), Champion Hill (May 16, Hovey’s bloody ridge fight; Grant: “If the enemy has driven them he is not in good plight himself”), Big Black River (May 17, Lawler’s impetuous charge yielding 2k prisoners). In 18 days, five battles, 130 miles traversed, ~4,300 Union casualties vs. 7,200 Confederate; Pemberton bottled in Vicksburg. 

Two assaults (May 19, May 22) failed with heavy loss (~4,200 total); McClernand’s false reports of captured forts diverted troops and inflated casualties, leading Grant to remove him for insubordination and self-promotion. Grant then settled into siege: parallel trenches (some 50 yards from enemy), 220 land + 100 naval guns, systematic starvation. Grant personally directed gun emplacement under sharpshooter fire, smoking serenely atop the embankment. Sherman guarded against Johnston to the east (“I never had a moment’s care while Sherman was there”). Vicksburg surrendered July 4. Grant’s equanimity, audacity in cutting loose, mastery of deception and combined arms, and refusal to despair through months of failure and political pressure defined the campaign. **General-in-Chief (1864–65):** Appointed lieutenant general and given command of all Union armies in March 1864 (with promotion references and strategic planning context in range). Grant established headquarters with the Army of the Potomac under Meade while retaining overall direction. His grand strategy featured simultaneous advances by far-flung armies: he would pin Lee in Virginia while Sherman advanced on Atlanta (and later the Carolinas), Sheridan operated in the Valley, Butler up the James toward Richmond/Petersburg, and other columns (Crook, Hunter, Sigel) disrupted Confederate supply lines and interior. "It had never happened before in the history of war that one man directed so completely four distinct armies, separated by thousands of miles, and numbering more than a quarter of a million soldiers; ordering the operations of each for the same day, and receiving at night reports from each that his orders had been obeyed."

The Overland Campaign opened 4 May 1864 with the crossing of the Rapidan. Grant sought to march through the Wilderness quickly to open ground, but Lee struck first in the dense second-growth thickets (5–6 May). Grant demonstrated coolness under fire ("puffed through twenty cigars... wrote orders... chatted with Meade"), personally formed mental picture of battle without overview, and issued orders to "take the initiative whenever the enemy could be drawn from his intrenchments." After inconclusive slaughter (~17,500 Union casualties), Grant refused to retreat. He told a panic-stricken officer: "Oh, I am heartily tired of hearing about what Lee is going to do... Go back to your command, and try to think what we are going to do ourselves, instead of what Lee is going to do." That night he suffered a private catharsis ("threw himself face downward on his cot, and gave way to the greatest emotion... never before seen him so deeply moved"), then slept "as soundly and as peacefully as an infant." Next morning the army turned south instead of north; soldiers cheered "Grant is moving to Richmond." Grant: "If you see the President, tell him, from me, that, whatever happens, there will be no turning back." Lincoln praised the "perfect coolness and persistency of purpose... grit of a bull-dog." Lee to his officers: "Grant is not retreating; *he is not a retreating man*." Grant viewed Wilderness not as defeat but opening act: "Our victory consisted in having successfully crossed a formidable stream... and in getting the army together as a unit." He had forced Lee onto the permanent defensive.

Grant then executed a stealthy flank to Spotsylvania Court House (8–21 May). Elaborate Confederate earthworks (loopholes, "in a manner unknown to European warfare") forced bloody frontal assaults. Grant coordinated a major cavalry raid by Sheridan that defeated Stuart at Yellow Tavern (11 May), depriving Lee of his premier cavalryman. Sedgwick was killed by sniper. On 12 May Hancock's assault at the "Bloody Angle" produced horrific close combat. After further fruitless attacks, Grant abandoned the position for another leftward shift. He had forced Lee to react throughout.

At North Anna (21–26 May) Lee established a strong inverted-V position; Grant probed but avoided a decisive trap, recognizing the danger of divided forces. Sheridan returned from raid. Grant continued south toward Totopotomoy and Cold Harbor.

Cold Harbor (1–12 June) became the campaign's nadir. Grant, hoping to exploit perceived Confederate exhaustion and secure a breakthrough before public disillusion or summer disease, ordered a massive dawn assault on 3 June against Lee's entrenched right (Hancock, Wright, Baldy Smith). After delays from exhaustion and coordination failures, 60,000 men charged into prepared defenses; ~7,000 Union casualties in the first hour (vs. ~1,500 Confederate). Grant later wrote in *Memoirs* (added as afterthought): "I have always regretted that the last assault at Cold Harbor was ever made." A Confederate general called it "not war but murder." Grant was visibly depressed ("Bad—very bad"). The "butcher" label stuck: "They call me a butcher... I sometimes could hardly bring myself to give an order of battle? When I contemplated the death and misery that were sure to follow, I stood appalled." Yet Lincoln stood firm: "Grant is this evening... in a position from whence he will never be dislodged until Richmond is taken."

Grant then executed a stunning strategic volte-face: a secret night march (12–14 June) south across the Chickahominy and James River to link with Butler and strike Petersburg, the rail hub supplying Richmond and Lee. The 2,100-foot pontoon bridge (longest in military annals) and massive crossing (115,000 men, wagons, cattle swimming) was a logistical masterpiece that completely fooled Lee. Grant to Julia: "one of the most perilous movements ever executed by a large army" involving "crossing two rivers over which the enemy has bridges and railroads whilst we have bridges to improvise." He felt "no doubt about holding the enemy in much greater alarm than I ever felt in my life." On 15 June, Baldy Smith and Hancock overran Petersburg's outer works; the city "was clearly at the mercy of the Federal commander, who had all but captured it." But hesitation (Smith's illness, Hancock's wound, delayed exploitation) allowed Beauregard to reinforce; Lee rushed troops south. Repeated assaults (15–18 June) cost >12,000 Union casualties without breakthrough. Grant settled into the nine-month siege of Petersburg, extending lines westward to stretch Lee thin while maintaining simultaneous pressure elsewhere. "If he gets there it will become a siege, and then it will be a mere question of time" (Lee's fear, realized).

The grand strategy of coordinated, multi-theater pressure—attrition of Lee's army + destruction of Confederate logistics and will—prevented concentration and wore down the South, even at terrible cost. Sherman took Atlanta; other columns disrupted supplies. Grant's willingness to accept casualties to fix Lee while others maneuvered proved decisive. **Presidency (1869–77):** Two full terms. Major focus on Reconstruction enforcement. Created the Department of Justice (1870); Attorney General Amos Akerman and Solicitor General Benjamin Bristow brought thousands of Klan indictments; federal troops and marshals protected black voters and officeholders in the South. Signed the 15th Amendment (1870). The 1875 Civil Rights Act (public accommodations) was the high-water mark of federal civil rights legislation until 1964. Scandals (Whiskey Ring exposed by Bristow; Belknap War Department; Credit Mobilier hangover) and the Panic of 1873 overshadowed achievements for contemporaries and later historians. Grant's Indian policy mixed peace initiatives with force (Red Cloud, Modoc). Foreign policy successes included the Alabama claims arbitration and resolution of the Virginius affair. He declined a third-term bid in 1876.

**Post-Presidency and Final Campaign (1877–85):** Triumphant round-the-world tour (met Li Hongzhang, Bismarck, Queen Victoria, etc.). Financial ruin in the Grant & Ward swindle (1884). Terminal throat cancer diagnosed 1884; race to complete *Personal Memoirs* with Mark Twain's firm as publisher. Finished manuscript 16 July 1885; died one week later.

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military Style and Methods

From the range material, Grant's early-war style already showed clear offensive objectives, rapid exploitation ("by following up our success we can go anywhere"), and a willingness to improvise when plans went awry. He was not a brilliant tactician but excelled at sensing enemy weakness (attuned to "the mentality of defeat" from his own background of failure), coordinating with the navy (Foote at Henry-Donelson), and maintaining relentless pressure. At Donelson he deduced enemy breakout intent from haversacks and shifted instantly to the counter; at Shiloh he coordinated fresh troops across the line on day 2 while personally leading a charge at one point. Decentralized execution appeared early—he gave division commanders freedom and focused on theater-level decisions.

He showed personal bravery and hands-on involvement: ranging Fort Henry's guns himself aboard the *Essex*, riding the Shiloh battlefield all day on crutches "in the midst of a shower of cannon and musket balls," issuing orders conversationally yet seeing everything. Secrecy was a trademark ("I am quietly making preparations for a move, without as yet having created a suspicion"). He dispensed with baggage trains for mobility. His humanity coexisted with ruthlessness: impartial aid to wounded on both sides at Donelson ("the war is over between them"), quoting Burns on inhumanity, yet rejecting chivalric terms for rebels ("No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender").

He disliked ceremony; avoided shaming prisoners at Donelson. In writing, the *Memoirs* passages on these fights are lean, direct, and self-critical on his own errors (e.g., the surprise at Shiloh). He credited horses and subordinates (Sherman as "hero of Shiloh") readily.

On alcohol in this period: Chernow's analysis, grounded in letters and staff testimony, portrays a solitary binge drinker who abstained for long stretches (especially with Julia present or in Galena) but was vulnerable when idle, stressed, or separated. Rawlins served as explicit watchdog and conscience, with Grant's consent imposing draconian rules at headquarters. Rawlins' pledge to Washburne (which Grant approved as "exactly right") vowed to notify him or resign if Grant became intemperate. The pattern never demonstrably impaired performance in these campaigns; witnesses confirmed sobriety at Shiloh and Donelson despite rumors. Grant himself told old friends he had "not drunk any for several years" and would not let it regain the upper hand. The prewar resignation and early war charges (Kountz after Belmont) loomed as background threat, but persistence and Rawlins' monitoring carried him through.

Politically and in command, early signs of nationalism and pragmatism: refusal to return contraband slaves at Donelson ("We want laborers, let the negroes work for us"), rebuffing his father's contract schemes to preserve efficiency and reputation.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Contemporaries in the range describe Grant in early war as "indifferently dressed, and did not at all look like a military man" yet possessing a "firm, determined gaze." In battle he went about "quietly, speaking in a conversational tone; yet he appeared to see everything that went on, and was always intent on business." His jaw "set upon the other" when angered or resolute. He was the "soul of honesty" yet shaded the truth after Shiloh to claim he had foreseen the attack, writing later accounts that contradicted his own April 5 correspondence and Sherman's. He quoted poetry (Burns) unusually on the Donelson battlefield amid carnage. He was modest to a fault, crediting his horse for the dramatic embarkation at Belmont ("My horse put his fore feet...") and subordinates (anointing Sherman "the hero of Shiloh").

The marriage to [[actors/grant-julia]] was a genuine partnership and emotional anchor. Julia's vision of him "on horseback and in great peril" the day of Belmont matched his own thoughts; she rushed to Cairo. She took pride in the bands playing "Hail to the Chief" for "my General," though she disliked the long beard and the desolate look of Cairo. Grant wrote her jubilant, self-assured letters from the front ("You will soon hear if my presentiment is realized"; "This is the largest capture I believe ever made on the continent"). She cured a whiskey temptation by pouring it down the drain. Grant's wrath flared when family criticized her.

Grant was loyal and generous even to former foes: after Donelson surrender he bantered warmly with old acquaintance Buckner ("If I had got [Pillow] I’d let him go again"), offered the use of his purse (declined), and treated Confederate wounded identically. He prevented souvenir hunting and vain ceremonies that would "mortify and injure the spirit of brave men, who, after all, are our own countrymen." Yet he rejected outdated chivalry for rebels in official terms.

The relationship with John Rawlins was that of commander and "right-hand man and alter ego," "resident conscience." Rawlins, a fierce temperance advocate whose own father had drinking issues, laid down rules with Grant's consent, berated headquarters drinking, and enjoyed "special license to be frank and even scold." Rawlins' long defense to Washburne after Belmont (which Grant reviewed and approved "exactly right") declared: "I love him [as] a father... I say to you frankly and I pledge you my word for it, that should General Grant at any time become a intemperate man or an habitual drunkard, I will notify you immediately, will ask to be removed from duty on his staff... or resign my commission." Rawlins later showed broken pledges privately to new staff while publicly defending Grant's sobriety at Shiloh. Grant tolerated Rawlins' oaths and allowed him unique access.

With Sherman the bond solidified "in the midst of death and slaughter" at Shiloh; Grant needed to give him "scarcely... any advice." Sherman found him under the oak after the first day and received the laconic "Lick ’em tomorrow though." Grant protected Sherman's reputation and credited him fully.

Grant's character was marked by dogged persistence and indomitable will, "perhaps owed something to having survived the ups and downs of his own improbable life before the war." "The most dangerous situations brought out his indomitable will." He focused on enemy defects rather than his own, demystifying foes from his background of failure. He showed equanimity under fire and in adversity that "communicated itself to his officers." At the same time he was staggered by the human scale of suffering (amputations in the Shiloh hospital "more unendurable than encountering the enemy’s fire"; he returned to the rainy tree). He could not abide blood, turned pale at injuries, had been repelled by the tannery and Mexican War costs.

On drinking: Chernow's analysis from Range 02-03 material portrays a solitary binge pattern—abstinent for long periods (Galena, with Julia, under Rawlins' watch), vulnerable when "away from Julia," idle, or under extreme stress (migraines, criticism, separation during Vicksburg buildup). During Memphis stay Julia applied hot poultices for his headaches; Grant refused liquor offered by doctor: “he says he will not die, and he will not touch a drop upon any consideration.” Episodes were private. Grant limited officers' drinking. At Shiloh he was sober per witnesses. During Vicksburg canal phase and siege stress, allegations intensified (Hamilton, Kountz, Halstead letters to Lincoln/Chase describing foolish/idiotic drunkenness). Lincoln refused to act on them. Dana, sent to investigate, reported categorical negative on drinking at first and became an ally shielding Grant.

The most famous incident: after doctor prescribed wine for illness, Rawlins on June 6 wrote a searing 3 a.m. rebuke: “The great solicitude I feel for the safety of this army leads me to mention what I had hoped never again to do—the subject of your drinking… I have heard that Dr. McMillan… induced you, notwithstanding your pledge to me, to take a glass of wine, and today… I find you where the wine bottle has just been emptied, in company with those who drink and urge you to do likewise… Had you not pledged me the sincerity of your honor early last March that you would drink no more during the war, and kept that pledge during your recent campaign, you would not today have stood first in the world’s history as a successful military leader.” Grant accepted the rebuke; Rawlins claimed it was heeded. A second episode June 6 on the Yazoo River steamer to Satartia (Dana present): Grant became ill/unresponsive (“not in a condition to conduct an intelligent conversation”), blacked out, and Dana turned the boat back. Dana later described Grant “as stupidly drunk as the immortal nature of man would allow; but the next day he came out as fresh as a rose.” Cadwallader’s later memoir embellished a similar tale (he was not present) with horse chase and bottle-tossing heroics; it shares motifs with other accounts (childlike state, sudden sobriety on return) but is not taken at face value. Dana and Rawlins both chose to protect the irreplaceable commander rather than expose him. Grant invited Julia and children to camp in June to stabilize him.

Vicksburg further revealed Grant’s equanimity and persistence: “man of iron” standing motionless on deck under 500 shots (“no word escaped his lips, no muscle of his earnest face moved”); Dana’s horse-stumble test—“instead of going over the animal’s head… he kept his seat… without a word or sign of impatience.” He personally oversaw gun placement atop embankments under sharpshooter fire, smoking serenely. Through canal fiascos, disease, press scorn (“the confidence of the army is greatly shaken”), family political meddling (General Orders No. 11 issued in pique over father Jesse’s cotton scheme with Mack brothers; rescinded by Lincoln after Jewish protests; Grant later regretted it deeply even on deathbed), and command friction he never wavered. His jaw “set upon the other” in resolve. After defeats he slept amid corpses and was staggered by suffering but kept the army moving. “I saw new energies in Grant,” Rawlins observed. The prewar humiliation continued to forge the “dogged persistence and iron will” and attunement to the “mentality of defeat.”

Modesty and lack of self-promotion persisted: he was surprised by fame, refused to exploit his position for family contracts, and lived simply (during the march: “neither a horse nor an orderly or servant, a blanket or overcoat or clean shirt, or even a sword… entire baggage consists of a tooth-brush”). His prewar humiliation forged empathy and stubbornness. "I saw new energies in Grant," said Rawlins early on. The solitary, determined core—taciturn in public, vivid in private storytelling—carried him through criticism that would have broken others. ## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Grant finished the *Memoirs* on 16 July 1885 and died on 23 July at the cottage on Mount McGregor. The funeral in New York was one of the largest in American history up to that time; both Union and Confederate generals served as pallbearers. Julia survived until 1902 and saw the publication of her own memoirs.

The immediate aftermath included the enormous commercial success of the *Memoirs* (Twain's firm cleared substantial debts for the Grant family). Politically, the "Stalwart" wing of the Republican Party lost its natural leader; the party moved toward the compromises that ended Reconstruction.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Militarily, Grant's 1864–65 campaigns established the model of coordinated, multi-theater pressure and acceptance of attrition to achieve political ends that influenced later American strategic thought (though the "butcher" label persisted in popular memory for decades). The *Memoirs* remain a canonical text of military literature and American autobiography.

On race and citizenship: Grant was, in the words Chernow and earlier Douglass cite, the president who did most to translate emancipation into enforceable rights. The rollback after 1877 was structural (Northern fatigue, Southern violence, Supreme Court decisions, economic priorities) but Grant's enforcement efforts delayed and limited the damage for a generation. Modern historians rank him far higher on civil rights than the scandals narrative suggested.

Institutionally, his presidency reinforced the precedent of two terms and the norm of civilian control while expanding federal administrative capacity (Justice Department).

The personal rehabilitation effected by 20th- and 21st-century scholarship has moved Grant from "worst presidents" lists toward the middle or upper tier, recognized as a flawed but consequential figure who got the largest questions of his era right.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources:** The *Personal Memoirs* provide the central first-person account of these early campaigns, written decades later under terminal cancer with Mark Twain's firm. Verbatim passages include the Donelson surrender note ("No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender can be accepted. I propose to move immediately upon your works."), the horse sliding onto the Belmont transport, the open field at Shiloh "so covered with dead that it would have been possible to walk across the clearing, in any direction, stepping on dead bodies, without a foot touching the ground," the night exchange with Sherman ("Lick ’em tomorrow though"), the post-Bruinsburg relief ("When this was effected I felt a degree of relief scarcely ever equalled since… I was on dry ground on the same side of the river with the enemy"), Jackson fire account ("Joe Johnston set fire to every store house in Jackson before I reached it"), and battle aftermath reflection (“While a battle is raging one can see his enemy mowed down by the thousand, or the ten thousand, with great composure; but after the battle these scenes are distressing...”). Grant's letters to [[actors/grant-julia]] and Washburne (e.g., "by following up our success we can go anywhere"; Belmont losses "proves to be greater and the effect upon the southern mind more saddening") supply immediate, less polished texture. Rawlins' defense letter to Washburne (approved by Grant as "exactly right") and his extraordinary June 6, 1863 rebuke during Vicksburg (“The great solicitude I feel for the safety of this army leads me to mention… Had you not pledged me the sincerity of your honor early last March that you would drink no more during the war... you would not today have stood first in the world’s history as a successful military leader”) are key contemporary documents. Julia's memoirs record her Belmont vision and Cairo impressions. Dana dispatches and letters (on Grant’s modesty, equanimity, initial sobriety report), Cadwallader’s later memoir (Satartia embellishments), Official Records, newspapers (*New York Times* criticism of Grant at Vicksburg; *Vicksburg Whig* premature celebration), and eyewitness accounts (Eaton on Holly Springs poker-face; Washburne on toothbrush march; soldiers on “man of iron” under fire) provide the public and critical record. Bias analysis: Grant's *Memoirs* are lean and often self-critical on errors (Shiloh surprise, Lew Wallace route) but shade the record on preparedness: he later claimed foreknowledge contradicted by his own 5 April letter to Halleck ("I have scarcely the faintest idea of an attack") and Sherman's assurances. Written for family security and reputation, they defend his generalship without score-settling; they also pass over General Orders No. 11 in “embarrassed silence” (Fred later flagged the omission; Grant called it “a matter long past and best not referred to”). Rawlins' letters are partisan advocacy by a devoted subordinate and temperance zealot who had a vested interest in Grant's success and sobriety; he later privately showed staff a "broken pledge" yet publicly defended Grant. Reid's influential account drew from demoralized skulkers and stragglers, fabricating or exaggerating bayoneting-in-tents myths and Grant's "late arrival" while containing genuine reporting on lack of fortifications. Dana dispatches are initially positive (as observer turned admirer) but later candid on the Satartia blackout; his account is contemporaneous and less embellished than Cadwallader’s. Cadwallader’s 1955 memoir (written decades later while sheepherding) contains the most dramatic Satartia details (horse chase, bottle tossing) but is undermined by his confirmed absence from the trip (Dana was present); it serves self-aggrandizement and defense of Grant while sharing consistent motifs (slurred speech, staggering, childlike state, sudden sobriety before Rawlins). Soldier letters and diaries (e.g., "We thought he was hard-hearted, cold and indifferent, but it was only the difference between a real soldier and amateur soldiers"; “There’s General Grant… That fellow doesn’t look like he has the ability to command a regiment”) often capture the contrast between Grant’s sangfroid and volunteers’ emotionalism or initial underestimation. Contemporary rumors (Kountz post-Belmont, Hamilton/McClernand, Halstead, post-Shiloh “pity he drinks”) reflect political enemies, rivals (McClernand spied and spread tales), and the shadow of the 1854 resignation more than observed impairment during operations.
