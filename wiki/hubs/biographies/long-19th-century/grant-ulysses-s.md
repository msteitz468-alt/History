## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Grant was born Hiram Ulysses Grant on 27 April 1822 in Point Pleasant, Ohio, the eldest son of Jesse Root Grant, an ambitious and overbearing tanner and businessman, and the reserved Hannah Simpson Grant. The family moved to Georgetown, Ohio, where Grant grew up with a love of horses and a dislike of the tannery work. His father secured an appointment to West Point (the name "Ulysses S. Grant" resulted from a clerical error that Grant accepted).

At West Point he was an indifferent cadet who excelled only in horsemanship and mathematics; he read novels, showed little enthusiasm for military life, and graduated 21st in a class of 39 (1843). The Mexican-American War provided his first real professional experience. Serving under Zachary Taylor and then Winfield Scott, he performed competently in logistics and combat (he was mentioned in dispatches) but was repelled by the human cost and the political dimensions of the war. He observed Scott's operational art and Taylor's calm under fire; both influenced his later style.

After the war, postings to the Pacific Northwest and other remote garrisons separated him from his wife Julia Dent (married 1848, daughter of a slaveholding Missouri family) and their growing children. Lonely, bored, and drinking to excess on at least some occasions, he resigned in 1854 rather than face a court-martial for an alleged episode of intoxication on duty (the exact circumstances remain disputed; Chernow treats it as part of a recurring solitary binge pattern rather than career-ending alcoholism at the time).

The next six years were the "lost years." Grant tried farming on land given by Julia's father ("Hardscrabble"), failed as a real-estate agent and bill collector in St. Louis, and finally took a clerkship in his father's Galena, Illinois, leather goods store in 1860. He lived modestly, was regarded locally as a failure, and was viewed by some in-laws as socially inferior. These years of humiliation, poverty, and dependence forged the stubborn persistence and empathy for the underdog that later characterized his generalship and presidency. They also gave him intimate knowledge of the western theater terrain and river systems that proved invaluable in 1862–63.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

When the Civil War began, Grant's prewar acquaintance with Congressman Elihu B. Washburne of Galena proved decisive. Washburne championed the obscure former captain; Illinois Governor Richard Yates appointed him colonel of the 21st Illinois. Grant quickly demonstrated organizational talent and calm in small actions.

His first major success came in February 1862 with the capture of Forts Henry and Donelson on the Tennessee and Cumberland rivers. The phrase "No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender" made him a national figure overnight. Lincoln promoted him to major general.

Shiloh (April 1862) was a near-disaster that Grant survived through personal leadership on the first day and the timely arrival of Buell and Lew Wallace on the second. The heavy casualties produced a storm of criticism and rumors of drunkenness; several senior officers and politicians demanded his removal. Lincoln's famous reported reply ("I can't spare this man—he fights") preserved Grant's command.

The Vicksburg campaign (autumn 1862–July 1863) was the turning point of Grant's career and the strategic hinge of the war in the West. After failed attempts (Chickasaw Bluffs, the canal projects), Grant cut loose from his supply line, crossed the Mississippi south of Vicksburg, won five battles in eighteen days against divided Confederate forces (Port Gibson, Raymond, Jackson, Champion Hill, Big Black River), and besieged the city. Vicksburg fell on 4 July 1863, yielding an entire army and control of the Mississippi. Grant was now the obvious choice for higher command.

After Chattanooga (November 1863), Lincoln promoted him to lieutenant general (the first since Washington) and gave him command of all Union armies in March 1864. Grant moved his headquarters to the Army of the Potomac while leaving Sherman in the West. The rise from failed clerk to general-in-chief in less than three years remains one of the most remarkable ascents in American military history.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Prewar and Early War (to Shiloh):** See Formation and Rise. Emphasis on learning riverine and expeditionary warfare.

**Vicksburg and the West (1862–63):** The campaign that demonstrated Grant's operational maturity—deception, logistics innovation, rapid exploitation, and siege craft. Living off the country and splitting Confederate commands prefigured later total-war methods.

**General-in-Chief (1864–65):** The Overland Campaign (Wilderness, Spotsylvania, North Anna, Cold Harbor) and Petersburg siege. Grant accepted high casualties to fix Lee while Sherman advanced on Atlanta and the Carolinas, Sheridan cleared the Valley, and Thomas destroyed Hood in Tennessee. The simultaneous pressure across theaters prevented Confederate concentration and wore down the South's capacity to continue. Appomattox terms (April 1865) were generous: officers kept sidearms and horses; paroled men returned home.

**Presidency (1869–77):** Two full terms. Major focus on Reconstruction enforcement. Created the Department of Justice (1870); Attorney General Amos Akerman and Solicitor General Benjamin Bristow brought thousands of Klan indictments; federal troops and marshals protected black voters and officeholders in the South. Signed the 15th Amendment (1870). The 1875 Civil Rights Act (public accommodations) was the high-water mark of federal civil rights legislation until 1964. Scandals (Whiskey Ring exposed by Bristow; Belknap War Department; Credit Mobilier hangover) and the Panic of 1873 overshadowed achievements for contemporaries and later historians. Grant's Indian policy mixed peace initiatives with force (Red Cloud, Modoc). Foreign policy successes included the Alabama claims arbitration and resolution of the Virginius affair. He declined a third-term bid in 1876.

**Post-Presidency and Final Campaign (1877–85):** Triumphant round-the-world tour (met Li Hongzhang, Bismarck, Queen Victoria, etc.). Financial ruin in the Grant & Ward swindle (1884). Terminal throat cancer diagnosed 1884; race to complete *Personal Memoirs* with Mark Twain's firm as publisher. Finished manuscript 16 July 1885; died one week later.

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military Style and Methods

Grant's military style was characterized by clear strategic objectives, decentralized tactical execution by trusted lieutenants, and relentless pressure once a campaign was launched. He was not a brilliant tactician in the Lee or Jackson mold but excelled at seeing the theater as a whole and at logistics and coordination. The 1864 plan of simultaneous advances was the first coherent Union grand strategy.

He disliked ceremony and ostentation; his headquarters were functional. He wrote crisp, direct orders and the *Memoirs* in a lean, unadorned prose that Twain and later critics praised as among the best in American literature.

Politically, Grant was a nationalist who believed the war had created a new constitutional order with stronger federal authority, especially to protect citizens' rights. He was pragmatic rather than ideological on Reconstruction—willing to use force where necessary but not a Radical in the Sumner or Stevens mold. His approach to scandal was often loyal to subordinates until evidence became overwhelming ("Let no guilty man escape" was sincere but applied unevenly).

On alcohol: Chernow documents a consistent pattern of solitary binges every few months, especially when idle or under stress. Grant abstained for long periods and relied on staff (especially Rawlins) to monitor him. The problem never demonstrably affected his performance in major commands after early incidents.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Contemporaries described Grant as taciturn in public ("Ulysses the Silent") yet a superb private storyteller with a dry wit and vast fund of anecdotes. He was loyal to a fault to friends and staff, sometimes to the point of political blindness (Babcock in the Whiskey Ring). He adored Julia and their children; the marriage was a genuine partnership.

He could not abide the sight of blood or suffering animals yet sent thousands of men into battle when he judged it necessary to shorten the war. He was personally modest, hated self-promotion, and was surprised by the intensity of the adulation that followed Appomattox and the world tour.

His relationship with Lincoln was one of mutual respect and non-interference; Lincoln gave Grant wide latitude and protected him from political enemies. With Sherman the bond was deep and affectionate; with Sheridan more instrumental. With Rawlins, a temperance advocate, the relationship was that of commander and indispensable conscience.

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Grant finished the *Memoirs* on 16 July 1885 and died on 23 July at the cottage on Mount McGregor. The funeral in New York was one of the largest in American history up to that time; both Union and Confederate generals served as pallbearers. Julia survived until 1902 and saw the publication of her own memoirs.

The immediate aftermath included the enormous commercial success of the *Memoirs* (Twain's firm cleared substantial debts for the Grant family). Politically, the "Stalwart" wing of the Republican Party lost its natural leader; the party moved toward the compromises that ended Reconstruction.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Militarily, Grant's 1864–65 campaigns established the model of coordinated, multi-theater pressure and acceptance of attrition to achieve political ends that influenced later American strategic thought (though the "butcher" label persisted in popular memory for decades). The *Memoirs* remain a canonical text of military literature and American autobiography.

On race and citizenship: Grant was, in the words Chernow and earlier Douglass cite, the president who did most to translate emancipation into enforceable rights. The rollback after 1877 was structural (Northern fatigue, Southern violence, Supreme Court decisions, economic priorities) but Grant's enforcement efforts delayed and limited the damage for a generation. Modern historians rank him far higher on civil rights than the scandals narrative suggested.

Institutionally, his presidency reinforced the precedent of two terms and the norm of civilian control while expanding federal administrative capacity (Justice Department).

The personal rehabilitation effected by 20th- and 21st-century scholarship has moved Grant from "worst presidents" lists toward the middle or upper tier, recognized as a flawed but consequential figure who got the largest questions of his era right.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources:** The *Personal Memoirs* (2 vols., 1885) are the central text—written under extreme duress, remarkably free of self-justification or score-settling, and literarily excellent. The Ulysses S. Grant Association Papers project (John Y. Simon, John F. Marszalek et al.) provides the documentary foundation. Julia's memoirs add domestic perspective. Official Records and contemporary newspapers supply the public record.

**Chernow 2017 contribution:** Uses the full modern Papers, close reading of the *Memoirs*, and a psychological lens on the drinking pattern and the "lost years." Explicitly frames Grant as the link between military victory and Reconstruction enforcement. Quotes Grant, Rawlins, Sherman, Twain, and black observers (Douglass, Martin) to ground assessments.

**Major debates:** Extent of Grant's drinking and whether it affected command (Chernow: real but managed); responsibility for scandals (loyalty vs. naiveté vs. corruption); quality of Reconstruction policy (feasibility given white resistance vs. insufficient vigor); military reputation vs. Lee (grand strategy vs. battlefield tactics).

**Methodological notes:** Biography as corrective to Lost Cause memory and Gilded Age dismissal. Chernow's work participates in the broader post-1960s re-evaluation of Reconstruction (Foner et al.) and the rehabilitation of Grant as a military and political figure. Primary-source texture is strongest on the war years and the final illness; presidential administration detail relies more on secondary synthesis.
