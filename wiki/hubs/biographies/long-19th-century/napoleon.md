A graduate-level analytic study of Napoleon’s formation, the mechanics of his signature decisions, character in context, the tension between personal agency and structural forces, and his institutional legacy — at substantially greater depth than the standard `actors/` page. Primary spine for this page: Andrew Roberts (2014) with its access to the unexpurgated correspondence, supplemented by operational sources for military phases.

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Napoleone di Buonaparte was born 15 August 1769 in Ajaccio on a heap of tapestry after his mother Letizia felt labour pains returning from church. The Buonaparte family were minor Corsican gentry of Florentine origin; Carlo (Enlightenment-influenced) and strong-willed Letizia had eight surviving children. After Ponte Nuovo (1769), Carlo refused exile with Paoli and took the oath to Louis XV, retaining positions and later representing Corsican nobility in Paris. No evidence Napoleon despised his father for this collaboration; he later named a son Charles.

Sent to France at nine for Gallicization (bursary), he learned French rapidly (thick Corsican accent lifelong). At Brienne (1779–1784) and École Royale Militaire (1784–85) he excelled in mathematics (artillery path) and devoured Plutarch, Caesar, Arrian on Alexander, Polybius, Rousseau, Voltaire. Nicknamed “the Spartan”; identified with Caesar over Pompey and emulated ancient heroes. Early writings mixed Corsican nationalism (“The Corsicans… may do the same with that of the French”) and Rousseauian melancholy (“My fellow-countrymen are weighed down with chains”). 13 Vendémiaire (1795) “whiff of grapeshot” ended mob power and launched his high command.

Josephine relationship began via Directory circles; overwhelming crush on his side, her early infidelity (Hippolyte Charles) revealed in Egypt devastated him (“I have a great, great deal of domestic sorrow”). Contingent (Corsican outsider + artillery + Revolutionary merit openings) met structural (post-1789 openings, nation-in-arms). Formed the soldier-statesman who would identify himself with the Revolution’s best elements.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Allied with Sieyès (who sought a “sword”), Napoleon was appointed commander of the 17th District; legislature moved to Saint-Cloud. On 19 Brumaire he faced hostile Five Hundred (“Down with the tyrant! Cromwell!”); grenadiers cleared the Orangery after Lucien’s theatrical oath (“I swear that I will stab my own brother…”). Rump legislature named provisional Consuls. No popular resistance.

Year VIII Constitution centralized real power with the First Consul (Senate amendable via sénatus-consultes; Tribunate/ Legislative Body discussion/vote only). Lucien falsified the plebiscite (~900k phantom votes added; true ~1.55M yes). Early decrees healed wounds (pensions, released hostages, non-juring priests, mourning for Washington). “The Revolution is established on the principles which began it. It is finished.” Prefectorial system abolished local democracy for central appointees; Banque de France stabilized credit. Marengo (1800) near-disaster reversed by Desaix/Kellermann; propaganda turned it into trap narrative. Amiens (1802) gave brief respite. Code and Concordat (1801–04) codified equality + patriarchal limits and reconciled Church on state terms. “Women should not be looked upon as equals of men. They are, in fact, only machines for making babies.” Empire (1804) and self-coronation followed Enghien execution and plots. “They seek to destroy the Revolution by attacking my person. I will defend it, for I am the Revolution.”

## 3. Major Phases of Career

- **Consulate (1799–1804):** legal/administrative revolution (Code, Concordat, prefects, Banque, lycées, Conseil d’État); Marengo recovery and Amiens peace; plebiscite manipulation and centralization.

- **Empire to Zenith (1804–1809):** Austerlitz, Jena/Auerstädt (Davout independent), Eylau bloodbath (no decisive result), Friedland, Tilsit (lenient on Alexander I, punitive on Prussia); Continental System launch (Berlin Decrees); Wagram after Aspern-Essling stalemate (Lannes killed); family thrones (Joseph, Louis, Jérôme) and merit nobility.

- **Overreach and Collapse (1810–1815):** Iberian “ulcer” (Bayonne, Bailén, “Spanish ulcer”); Russia 1812 (typhus epidemic 80k+ early losses, Vitebsk hesitation, Borodino Guard refusal, Moscow fire, Maloyaroslavets northern route choice, Berezina “miracle”); Leipzig (Saxon defection + premature bridge destruction); 1814 abdication, suicide attempt (degraded poison), Elba reforms; 1815 return (Ney defection, “On my march… I was an adventurer; in Grenoble I once again became a sovereign”); Waterloo (Ligny/Quatre Bras misdirection, Grouchy detachment, Ney cavalry charges, Guard refusal, mud delay); second abdication, Bellerophon surrender (“like Themistocles”).

- **Captivity and legend-making (1815–1821):** St Helena; Mémorial dictation (12 hrs/day, “Arcole, Rivoli… these are granite”); Hudson Lowe restrictions; stomach cancer decline and death 5 May 1821; autopsy (“ulcerated all over like a honeycomb”). “What a novel my life has been!”

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military Style and Methods

Compartmentalization (“drawers in a cupboard”): on eve of battle could dictate girls’ school rules or Comédie-Française regulations while aides arrived with orders; after Moscow issued regulations for the theatre. Relentless micromanagement (22+ letters/day on boots, bean types, bell-ropes, prostitutes in theatres, obscure priests’ sermons) coexisted with corps delegation (Berthier staff work; “I have destroyed the Austrian army simply by marches”). Meritocracy (Légion d’Honneur “baubles” + new counts/barons drawn from middle/working classes) replaced privilege. Preference for central position, fixing + envelopment (Ulm, Jena). Propaganda (bulletins, self-crowning with Charlemagne replica, “I am the Revolution”). Humor constant (“I’m sorry you are angry with me” to Decrès; wounded-horse quip at Marengo). Capacity to separate political/military in crisis. “I will conquer the sea through the power of the land.” “I have never seen a man… with so much personal activity and restless perseverance.”

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Josephine: overwhelming initial crush (her side less so); early infidelity in Egypt (“the veil has been completely lifted”); reconciliation and a decade of contentment despite his mistresses; dynastic divorce (1809) left her desolate but friendly relations continued (“She was the wife who would have gone with me to Elba”). Family: integrated Beauharnais (Eugène, Hortense); promoted siblings to thrones (mixed success). Marshals: loyalty mixed with ambition/betrayal (Marmont “ragusard,” Ney’s 1815 defection then execution). Volcanic temper (hat thrown at Metternich; “Troops? Where would you like me to find them?” at Waterloo) but rapid recovery. Self-control cultivated (“it’s taken me years… Without all this self-control, do you think I could have done all I’ve done?”). Letters to Josephine affectionate yet detached; constant joking even in extremis. “When I receive the monthly reports… I take greater pleasure reading them than a young lady does in reading a novel.” “My health is good” repeated to Marie Louise amid catastrophe. Malet plot shocked him because no one thought of the King of Rome. “I have more than four hundred million… and would gladly have given the whole for the ransom of my faithful companion-in-arms” (Ney).

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession/Aftermath

Second abdication after Waterloo; fled to Rochefort, surrendered to Maitland on Bellerophon (“I come, like Themistocles, to seat myself at the hearth of the British people”). St Helena (Oct 1815): Hudson Lowe restrictions; dictated Mémorial (“Arcole, Rivoli, the Pyramids, Marengo, Austerlitz, Jena, Friedland – these are granite”); “crown of thorns” greater than imperial one. Health decline (swellings, vomiting, depression); autopsy 6 May 1821: stomach “ulcerated all over like a honeycomb” at pylorus. “How Napoleon could have existed for any time with such an organ was wonderful.” Remains returned to Les Invalides 1840 under Louis-Philippe/Hitler (1940). Myth outlasted empire; institutions prevented full Ancien Régime restoration.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Civil Code (and derivative codes) basis of law in France, Belgium, Netherlands, Italy, Spain, Latin America, etc. — “the greatest codification of laws since the fall of the Roman Empire.” Prefects, Banque de France, Conseil d’État, lycées, Légion d’Honneur, rational administration, abolition of feudalism, meritocracy survived 1815 and 1830. “Thanks in large part to the legal and political reforms undertaken by Napoleon, the Bourbons were unable to reintroduce the old ways.” Unintended: provoked German, Spanish, Russian nationalisms that defined the nineteenth century. Redrew map (end of Holy Roman Empire, Confederation of the Rhine, Grand Duchy of Warsaw). Model of the modernizing soldier-statesman who “commence[d] its history” after the “romance of the Revolution.” Archetype for Great Man vs. structural debate. “I am the Revolution” was egotistical but substantively correct on the survivals he codified and exported.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

Pre-2004 biographies relied on Napoleon III’s censored two-thirds edition. Las Cases *Mémorial* (dictated on St Helena) is deliberate self-mythologizing (“the historian… must persuade”); many memoirs compromised by pensions/jobs under Bourbons (Rémusat, Constant, d’Abrantès, Fouché ghosted). Post-1940 Hitler analogies distorted. Roberts (2014) leverages full Fondation correspondence + 53 battlefield visits (with John Lee) for re-evaluation: charm/humor/self-appraisal from letters; institutions > conquests; Continental System as key error; Russia not inevitable catastrophe. Complements (does not duplicate) Chandler’s campaign operational detail, Muir’s soldier experience, Rothenberg’s army comparison, Epstein’s 1809 modernity thesis. “More books have been written with Napoleon in the title than there have been days since his death.” Collection now strong on personal/institutional layer; operational and economic quantification gaps remain for cross-reference to other sources. “The teeth of envy are powerless here.”
