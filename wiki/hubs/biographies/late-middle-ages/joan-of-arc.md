## 1. Formation and Early Influences

The political and religious crisis that shaped Joan’s world began with the English victory at Agincourt (1415), interpreted by the English as unambiguous divine vindication of Henry V’s claim (“the ‘true elect of God’”). French observers read the defeat instead as God’s punishment for France’s sins, especially the Armagnac–Burgundian civil war ignited by the 1407 murder of Louis of Orléans by John the Fearless of Burgundy. Chroniclers (the Parisian journal-writer, monk of Saint-Denis, Burgundian and Armagnac voices) debated responsibility and divine will amid rumor and shifting allegiances.

John the Fearless’s vast cross-border power (Burgundy, Flanders, Artois) and dual allegiances exemplified the paralysis that enabled English gains. The 10 September 1419 assassination of John on the Montereau bridge (struck with a war axe by Tanguy du Châtel in the dauphin Charles’s presence, under safe-conduct) made reconciliation impossible and drove the new duke Philip the Good into the English alliance. The 1420 Treaty of Troyes disowned Charles as the “so-called dauphin” for his “horrible and enormous crimes,” recognized Henry V as regent and heir, and arranged his marriage to Catherine so their son would unite the crowns. Charles’s parents participated; the oriflamme and regalia were symbolically yielded. Reims (traditional site of Clovis’s Holy Ampulla coronation) now lay in enemy hands.

Charles (the disinherited dauphin) was described as “not a warlike man” with a “puny frame and unsteady gait,” irreplaceable as the last surviving Valois son and thus unable to lead armies personally. He maintained intense piety (daily masses) and interpreted events through divine will. His court was riven by faction (executions of favourites by Richemont, clashes with La Trémoille, Yolande of Aragon’s temporary withdrawal). Yolande, “dowager duchess of Anjou (‘queen of Jerusalem and Sicily’),” served as mentor and “second mother,” brokering alliances and counsel (“by the good advice and counsel of our dearest and most beloved mother”).

Scottish aid brought euphoria at Baugé (1421, Clarence killed; Buchan hailed as “another Messiah”) but collapse at Verneuil (1424; Buchan, Douglas, and most Scots killed). English forces under Salisbury then Bedford advanced. By 1428–29 the court at Chinon confronted the grinding siege of Orléans (key Loire crossing), loss of leaders, internal betrayals, and the “massacre at Rouvray” (Battle of the Herrings, 12 February 1429), leaving the regime at nadir. Charles continued daily masses while rumours flew of retreat to the Dauphiné or safe havens in Scotland or Castile.

Charles adopted St Michael (protector of Mont-Saint-Michel) as patron, replacing Armagnac symbols with the white cross of St Michael and banners of the archangel slaying the serpent. The English under the child-king Henry VI (grandson of both Henry V and Charles VI after their 1422 deaths) claimed the double crown; Bedford was regent in France. This premature succession crisis and contested sacral kingship (Reims unction vs English coronation in Paris) created the precise vacuum in which a figure claiming direct divine authorization could appear. Castor’s sources (Parisian journal-writer with shifting partisanship, monk of Saint-Denis, *Gesta Henrici Quinti*, Burgundian chroniclers, Armagnac voices, Basin) repeatedly flag rumor, contradictory divine interpretations, and civil-war bias colouring all accounts.

On 23 February 1429, eleven days after Rouvray, a little band of six armed men arrived at Chinon with a girl “dressed as a boy, her dark hair cut short.” Her name was Joan, and she had come with a message from God. Castor stresses that the records of the later 1456 nullification trial (often used for early life) are themselves deeply retrofitted by political necessity once Charles had won. (Grounding throughout from Castor’s reconstruction via the anonymous Parisian journal-writer, monk of Saint-Denis, *Gesta*, Burgundian/Armagnac chroniclers, Thomas Basin, and the precise sequence of Agincourt–Montereau–Troyes–Bourges–Baugé–Verneuil–Orléans siege.)

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Joan’s journey from Vaucouleurs (over 270 miles through hostile territory) succeeded because Robert de Baudricourt was persuaded to send her (after the duke of Lorraine audience) with a royal messenger (Colet de Vienne) already preparing the way. At Chinon she was incongruous—a village girl in black-and-grey men’s tunic, doublet, hose and breeches—but the clarity of her message (“drive the English out of France, and lead Charles to Reims for his coronation”) quickly spread. The guiding hand of Yolande of Aragon ensured access despite the risks.

At Poitiers (three-week examination under Regnault de Chartres using Gerson’s discretio spirituum), theologians found “no evil… only goodness, humility, virginity, piety, integrity and simplicity.” Unable to reach a definitive verdict on her voices, they proposed the practical sign of relieving Orléans. Success would vindicate her; failure would condemn her with minimal loss. “The king… should not prevent her from going to Orléans with his soldiers, but should have her escorted there honourably, placing his faith in God.” A final virginity examination was supervised by Yolande. She was styled “the Maid.”

She dictated a letter (22 March 1429, headed “Jhesus Maria”) challenging Bedford, Suffolk, Talbot and Scales to submit to the king of heaven or face a war-cry “greater than France had heard for a thousand years.” She requested a sword from Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois (found inside a coffer at the high altar) and received armour and banners (fleurs-de-lis, Jhesus Maria, Annunciation, Christ in judgement). She carried the banner to avoid killing (“I never did take anyone’s life”).

At Orléans (arrived ~29 April) she clashed with commanders (Bastard of Orléans, La Hire, de Gaucourt) who delayed her preferred direct attack. She wrote to Talbot demanding surrender; the English replied with contempt. On 4 May she took Saint-Loup. On 6 May the Augustins fell. On 7 May at the Tourelles an arrow struck her “between her neck and shoulder”; she pressed forward from the ditch, brandishing her standard. Glasdale fell into the Loire and drowned; panic broke the defence. The siege was raised in four days of fighting after six months of stalemate.

After Orléans she insisted on clearing the Loire before Reims. At Jargeau (11 June) she overruled caution, led the assault (struck by a stone on the head), and rose shouting God was with them; Suffolk was captured. At Beaugency Richemont joined after swearing loyalty. On 18 June at Patay the English trap was sprung prematurely by a stag; Talbot and Scales were captured, Fastolf fled. Her presence had “greatly changed and weakened” English hearts.

She led the army to Reims despite counsel preferring Normandy. Troyes capitulated after she prepared an assault and dictated a direct letter (“submit yourselves… or… we will enter with God’s help”). On 17 July 1429 Charles was crowned and anointed in the cathedral; Joan stood at his side in shining armour holding her white banner. Afterwards she knelt weeping: “Noble king, God’s will is done.” She wrote to Philip the Good urging peace (“with my hands clasped, I pray…”). Absent were Richemont (barred by La Trémoille) and Burgundy.

She pushed for immediate attack on Paris. On 8 September (Nativity of the Virgin) she led the assault on the Porte Saint-Honoré, calling “Surrender to us quickly, in the name of Jesus!” A crossbow bolt ripped through her thigh; her standard-bearer was killed; the retreat was sounded. She continued insisting the city could be won as she was dragged away. The king ordered withdrawal. She left armour at Saint-Denis as an offering.

In May 1430 she rode to relieve Compiègne (besieged by Burgundy). After a successful sortie she was cut off; the town gate was shut. Burgundians pulled her from her saddle. She offered formal submission. Philip rode to view the prize and wrote that her capture proved “the error and foolish credulability” of those who believed her. Jean de Luxembourg held her; the English later bought her.

(Grounding drawn from Castor’s reconstruction via contemporary letters, chronicles, and the sequence of Chinon/Poitiers/Orléans/Loire/Reims/Paris/Compiègne.)

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Pre-mission (to Feb 1429):** Political/military nadir for the “kingdom of Bourges.” Civil war, English advances, loss of leaders (Verneuil), Orléans siege, internal court violence (Montereau aftermath, Richemont/La Trémoille clashes), and contested sacral legitimacy after Troyes.

**Mission (Feb–July 1429):** Chinon access (Yolande), Poitiers examination (practical sign of Orléans), relief of Orléans (4–7 May, four days of fighting), Loire campaign (Jargeau, Beaugency, Patay), march to Reims, coronation (17 July). Towns submitted spontaneously; momentum shifted.

**Post-Reims campaigns (Aug 1429–May 1430):** Paris assault failure (8 Sept, wounding and retreat), continued operations, capture at Compiègne (23 May 1430). Mission effectively over after Reims; court abandoned her.

**Capture, trial, execution (May 1430–30 May 1431):** Sold to English, tried at Rouen by Cauchon-led tribunal (Paris clerics, English pressure), abjuration under fear of fire (24 May), relapse (28 May, voices and clothing), condemnation as relapsed heretic, burned in the old market square (“Jesus. Jesus. Jesus.”). English propaganda and body disposal to prevent relics.

**Aftermath (1431 onward):** Immediate English narrative control and Charles silence. Long-term English decline (Arras 1435 realignment, Paris 1436, Normandy 1449–50, Gascony 1453). 1450 Bouillé inquiry, 1452 Estouteville reopening, 1455–56 full nullification (Isabelle and Pierre d’Arc plaintiffs; verdict “corruptly, deceitfully, slanderously, fraudulently and maliciously”; cross erected in old market). 1920 canonization after Quicherat transcripts and Pius X miracles. Orléans annual commemorations, impostor episodes, de Rais-sponsored Mystery play (ended by his crimes). Castor notes both 1431 and 1456 processes were predetermined by the questioners’ political/theological assumptions; “in gaining a saint, however, we have lost a human being.” (Grounding from Castor’s narrative of the sequence and contemporary voices.)

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military / Religious Style and Methods

Joan presented herself as a military leader (“I am the military leader”) while carrying her banner to avoid killing (“I never did take anyone’s life”). She dictated letters in a rambling yet electrifying style (“wherever I find your men in France, I will make them leave, whether they want to or not, and if they will not obey, I will have them all killed”). Her voices (St Catherine and St Margaret, seen “with my bodily eyes, just as well as I see you”) spoke French and gave practical as well as spiritual direction. At Poitiers she accepted the theologians’ practical sign rather than insisting on immediate Reims. In battle she overruled cautious commanders, pressed forward after wounding, and shifted morale (“hearts... greatly changed and weakened”). She insisted on male clothing for riding through dangerous country and protection against assault (“it pleases God that I wear it”). Under interrogation she showed singular self-possession (“You can leave the matter”), resisted the full oath (“I’ve sworn twice; that’s enough... You burden me too much”), and gave the famous conditional answer on grace (“If I’m not, may God put me there; and if I am, may God keep me in it”). She preferred her banner and direct action over strategy debates (“why need she think in those terms when she had the will of God?”). Gerson praised her chastity, piety, and inspiration of faith/fear; the University of Paris condemned male clothing as abomination, incitement of war, and possible sorcery/idolatry. Castor notes her incandescent fury when delayed and practical adaptation (learning to hold a pen, signing uncertainly). (Grounding from Castor’s trial dialogue and contemporary assessments.)

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

From the trial records and contemporary voices: singular self-possession and certainty (“You can leave the matter”; “If you were well informed about me, you would wish that I were out of your hands”). She took pride in sewing/spinning, insisted on God’s direct command over human strategy, and preferred her banner to avoid killing. She formed close bonds with captains (Alençon: gifted horse and later wanted her for Normandy; Bastard, La Hire, de Rais, Xaintrailles; received Orléans crimson/green livery from the captive duke). She impressed young nobles (“a gift from heaven... that I was seeing her and hearing her”). She maintained distance from court factions (La Trémoille) while pushing unification and was adamant that the king must be anointed. Under pressure she showed fury when blocked, weeping at Reims after delivering the king (“Noble king, God’s will is done”), and practical adaptation in captivity. She formed no romantic attachments; maintained chastity (no desire, possible non-menstruation per witnesses). In the cell she fought off grabs at her breasts/hands with force (Aimon de Macy testimony). She was incandescent when delayed at Orléans (“why need she think in those terms...”) and preferred death to perpetual irons (“I would rather do penance once and for all by dying”). Castor draws on trial self-descriptions of voices/visions/sign and the contrast between her roaring partisan voice and later sanitized saint. (Grounding from Castor’s reconstruction of trial records, Alençon, Bastard, d’Aulon, Lavenu, Massieu, de la Pierre, Aimon de Macy, and contemporary letters.)

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

After capture she was sold to the English. At Rouen (from Christmas 1430) she faced Bishop Cauchon and dozens of Paris-trained clerics. She described her voices (first heard at age 13 in her father’s garden; later St Catherine and St Margaret crowned with diadems; St Michael earlier; seen “with my bodily eyes”). The sign at Chinon: an angel brought a rich golden crown (“Sire, here is your sign; take it”). She carried her banner to avoid killing. She repeatedly resisted the full oath (“I’ve sworn twice; that’s enough... You burden me too much”), swearing only on matters of faith. On men’s clothes: necessary for riding and protection (“it pleases God that I wear it”). On grace: “If I’m not, may God put me there; and if I am, may God keep me in it.” She told judges “Take care what you do, for in truth I am sent by God.”

On 24 May 1431 at Saint-Ouen she abjured publicly after the sentence of abandonment to the secular arm was read, fearing the fire. Sentenced to perpetual imprisonment in women’s clothes with shaven head. Three days later (28 May) she relapsed in her cell: voices told her she had damned her soul to save her life; the preacher had accused her falsely; she preferred men’s clothes among men and had not understood the oath as permanent. “I told you the truth about everything in the trial... I would rather do penance once and for all by dying than suffer prison any longer.”

On 30 May 1431 she was condemned as relapsed heretic and burned in the old market square. English soldiers held her; she prayed ceaselessly “Jesus. Jesus. Jesus.” No rescue came. Immediately after, the executioner raked back the fire to expose her charred naked body to prove she was a woman, then burned flesh and bones to ashes “so that the people could see beyond any doubt” and to prevent relics. Official letters in Latin and French were dispatched across Europe recounting her “outrageous heresies.” In Paris on 4 July the inquisitor preached of demons (St Michael, Catherine, Margaret) who had abandoned her. Cauchon tasked notaries with an official Latin transcript (five sealed copies) to stand as “an open testament to the diligence of the judges and the enormity of the girl’s heresy.” From Chinon there was “an echoing silence”; no comment from Charles VII.

From the lips of the king there came no comment while his enemies produced a torrent of words. Another claimed envoy (William the Shepherd, stigmata, rode side-saddle) appeared; captured with Xaintrailles, shown in Henry VI’s Paris entry procession, then reportedly dumped in the Seine. On 16 December 1431 Henry VI was crowned “King Henry II of France” at Notre-Dame (not Reims, not with Clovis’s oil); Cauchon was among the celebrants. The event was lavish but criticized for poor food and organization. (Grounding from Castor’s reconstruction of contemporary accounts, official letters, journal-writer, and trial records.)

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Joan’s death briefly reinforced the Anglo-Burgundian alliance, but Anne of Burgundy’s death (1432) frayed ties; Bedford’s remarriage insulted Philip. At Arras (1435) Burgundy negotiated with Armagnacs and swore peace with Charles on 21 September, touching the host and cross and receiving absolution from his Troyes oath. “This was not peace... But it was a movement of tectonic plates that utterly transformed the landscape of the war. The necessity that Yolande of Aragon had always seen, and that Joan the Maid had so forcefully demanded... had finally come to pass.” Bedford died the same month.

Armagnac forces retook Paris (13 April 1436) with minimal resistance; the journal-writer noted the “very great miracle” and rapid loyalty shift (“before the day was out every man in Paris would have risked his life and goods to destroy the English”). In November 1437 Charles made his royal entry (first since 1418), riding in shining armour with son Louis; captains including Richemont, Bastard (now count of Dunois), La Hire, Poton de Xaintrailles. Jean d’Aulon (Joan’s former squire) walked holding Xaintrailles’s bridle. The canopy service recalled the English coronation but was now for the French king.

By 1449–50 Normandy was reconquered; on 12 August 1450 the last English holdings except Calais were gone. On 10 November 1449 Charles entered Rouen in state (Pot on carrying Charlemagne’s sword Joyeuse); gave thanks at Notre-Dame near the Seine where Joan’s ashes had been thrown. In 1450 (four months after Rouen’s fall) Charles spoke of the “very cruelly, iniquitously and against reason” trial and commissioned Guillaume Bouillé to inquire. In 1452 Cardinal d’Estouteville (papal legate, Norman, royal cousin) and Inquisitor Bréhal reopened targeting the trial itself.

In 1455–56 Isabelle (Joan’s mother) and son Pierre appeared in Notre-Dame Paris as plaintiffs before papal commissioners (Juvénal des Ursins, bishop of Paris, Bréhal), petitioning on grounds of hatred-driven false heresy accusation and trial injustice (war had prevented earlier action). 101 articles; extensive testimony on childhood/character/mission and trial/death. On 7 July 1456 in Rouen the judges declared the twelve articles “drawn up ‘corruptly, deceitfully, slanderously, fraudulently and maliciously’”; truth silenced, fabrication substituted; record and sentence “utterly null, invalid and void.” Joan innocent and justified; a cross ordered in the old market square “to preserve her memory forever.”

Orléans never forgot her (annual 8 May rites). In 1435 Gilles de Rais sponsored the enormous *Mystery of the Siege of Orléans* (hundreds of actors, 20,000 lines); ended by his later crimes. In 1436 a dark-haired woman resembling Joan appeared at Metz; recognized by brothers, received honors and gold in Orléans “for the good that she did the town during the siege,” but denounced as fraud in Paris 1440. Another envoy (William the Shepherd) was shown in Henry VI’s entry then reportedly drowned.

In 1869 Bishop Dupanloup petitioned after Quicherat’s publication of the transcripts (“She is a saint. God was in her”). Canonization faced obstacles (killed by the Church for political reasons; anger/arrogance/luxury; abjured out of fear). Devil’s advocate arguments; defender countered with revelations and heroic virtues. Pius X concurred (1904); accepted three miracles plus her salvation of France as fourth (“Joan of Arc has shone like a new star destined to be the glory not only of France but of the universal Church as well”). Beatified 1909; canonized 16 May 1920 (feast 30 May). Great War delayed proceedings. By 2011 Benedict XVI could speak of her seeking “a just Christian peace,” a sanitization that clashes with her actual words.

Castor’s central verdict: “In gaining a saint, however, we have lost a human being. This ferocious champion of one side in a complex and bloody war has been robbed of her context and her roaring voice.” Both 1431 and 1456 processes were predetermined by the questioners’ assumptions; “it all came down to the gift of vision... the ability to see which facts conformed with God’s plan for the world.” Documents from the tribunals pose a double challenge (elusive rules plus the vivid girl); partisan judgement transformed her into legend/icon/saint. Even in the legal records humanity persists (Aimon de Macy’s ugly testimony of force used against her, closing “And he believes she is in paradise”). The 1456 cross and 1920 feast preserved a sanitized memory; the source record itself requires reading “in the knowledge of how those documents came to be made.”

The cumulative effects of her brief career (sacral legitimacy for Charles, momentum shift, towns submitting, English hearts weakened) contributed to the long expulsion of the English (Arras as tectonic validation, Paris, Normandy, Gascony). Charles (who “learned, from unpromising beginnings, how to believe in his God-given sovereignty, how to take the fight to his enemies and how to unite his people around him”) stood in Rouen 1449 with captains who had shared her victories. (Grounding from Castor’s narrative of Arras, entries, expulsions, inquiries, canonization, and source critique.)

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

Primary sources are the condemnation trial records (1431, Rouen, under English pressure and Cauchon with Paris clerics) and nullification trial records (1456, under victorious Charles). Both processes were shaped by predetermined political/theological assumptions: what was “right” in 1431 English Rouen (persuade abjuration for salvation) was wrong in 1456 (victorious France). “There was no possibility that the hearings of 1456 would uphold the sentence of 1431: the information they sought and the purpose for which they sought it would not allow that conclusion, just as there had been no chance that the trial of 1431 would exonerate Joan...”

Castor reconstructs the pre-Joan crisis and Joan’s career primarily from the anonymous Parisian journal-writer (initially hostile to Armagnacs, later disillusioned with Burgundian/English rule), the monk of Saint-Denis, *Gesta Henrici Quinti*, Burgundian chroniclers (Chastellain, Monstrelet), Armagnac voices (Juvénal des Ursins), Thomas Basin, Scottish chroniclers, and contemporary letters. She repeatedly flags shifting partisanship, rumor, contradictory divine interpretations, and civil-war bias. The 1456 witnesses (Manchon, de la Pierre, Massieu, Lavenu, Alençon, Bastard, d’Aulon, villagers, etc.) reinterpreted roles under the new political reality; some used weasel words or elaborated stories (Loiseleur as St Catherine). Only Beaupère remained less reverent.

Scholarly/textual debates: Beveridge/Randall downplayed the debates; Jaffa, Zarefsky, Ericson, and Fehrenbacher elevated them. No wholly reliable debate text (Lincoln scrapbook vs hostile/friendly press; Holzer vs Johannsen approaches). Fehrenbacher demolishes the Freeport-1860 legend and explains 1858 malapportionment. Castor’s central historiographical argument is that “in gaining a saint, however, we have lost a human being” and that “it all came down to the gift of vision... the ability to see which facts conformed with God’s plan for the world.” Documents require reading “in the knowledge of how those documents came to be made.” Later sanitization (Benedict XVI on “just Christian peace”) clashes with her actual partisan words. Even the legal records preserve humanity (Aimon de Macy testimony; executioner’s remorse; heart unconsumed; tears including reportedly from Beaufort).
