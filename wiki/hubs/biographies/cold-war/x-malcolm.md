## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Malcolm Little was born May 19, 1925, in Omaha, Nebraska, the fourth of seven children of Earl and Louise Little. His father, a Baptist preacher and committed Garveyite (UNIA organizer), embodied black nationalist self-reliance and was frequently targeted for his activism. The family faced constant white harassment; Earl Little was killed in 1931 (officially a streetcar accident, widely believed by family and community to be murder by white supremacists). 

Louise Langdon Little (Grenadian-born, fair-skinned, educated) struggled after her husband's death. She was eventually committed to a mental institution; the children were dispersed to foster homes and relatives. Malcolm lived briefly with half-sister Ella in Boston.

These early experiences — Garveyite ideology from his father, family destruction by racism and state institutions, the Great Migration and urban Northern Black communities — formed the substrate for his later politics. Prison would be the next decisive forge.

*(Contingent elements: survival of specific family members; choice of Boston/Harlem over staying in Michigan; the particular NOI message reaching him in prison. Structural: Jim Crow violence, economic marginalization of Black migrants, collapse of Garvey movement after Garvey's deportation.)*

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

While incarcerated (1946–1952) for burglary, Malcolm encountered the teachings of Elijah Muhammad and the Nation of Islam through correspondence and family. He underwent a profound personal transformation: self-education (dictionary copying), rejection of his "slave name," adoption of "X," strict discipline (no pork, no alcohol, no tobacco, sexual restraint per NOI).

Upon parole he quickly rose in the NOI hierarchy. Appointed to build Temple No. 7 in Harlem. His intelligence, oratorical power, and personal charisma made him the organization's most effective recruiter and spokesman. The 1959 *Mike Wallace* documentary "The Hate That Hate Produced" catapulted him (and the NOI) to national visibility.

By 1961–63 he was the public face of militant Black separatism: articulate, fearless, unapologetic. He attracted thousands to rallies and was under intense FBI surveillance.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Phase 1: NOI Minister and Ideological Enforcer (1952–1963)**  
Primary objective: build the Nation, defend and extend Elijah Muhammad's authority and message. Tools: fiery oratory, disciplined organization, moral example. Achieved spectacular growth of the NOI. Maintained strict loyalty publicly while privately beginning to question.

**Phase 2: Crisis and Break (1963–March 1964)**  
The Kennedy assassination remark ("chickens coming home to roost") provided the pretext for suspension. Discovery of Elijah Muhammad's extramarital affairs and children with secretaries shattered the moral authority of the Messenger in Malcolm's eyes. He formally left the NOI in March 1964. This was the most existentially risky decision of his life.

**Phase 3: Internationalist Sunni Muslim and OAAU Leader (April 1964–February 1965)**  
Hajj (April 1964) produced a genuine religious and political reorientation: acceptance as a Muslim by the global ummah, rejection of blanket "white devils" theology, embrace of orthodox Islam. Founded Muslim Mosque, Inc. (religious) and Organization of Afro-American Unity (political, modeled partly on OAU). Conducted extensive tours of Africa and the Middle East. Sought to internationalize the Black American struggle and build alliances.

## 5. Intellectual / Religious / Political Style and Methods

Malcolm's style was characterized by:
- **Direct, uncompromising rhetoric** rooted in lived Black experience rather than abstract theory.
- Rapid ideological adaptation while retaining core commitments to Black dignity, self-defense, and critique of white supremacy.
- Use of religion (first NOI, then Sunni) as both personal discipline and political vehicle.
- Internationalist framing: connecting US racism to colonialism and imperialism.
- Mastery of media and public performance; willingness to debate anyone.

He evolved from separatist theology toward a more universalist (yet still race-conscious) humanism informed by orthodox Islam and Third World solidarity.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Marable portrays a complex man: intellectually curious, personally disciplined (especially after conversion), capable of great warmth and loyalty but also authoritarian control within the NOI. Relationship with Betty Shabazz was important but strained by his absences and the demands of leadership. Deeply affected by the destruction of his family of origin.

His break with Elijah Muhammad was both political and deeply personal (sense of betrayal by a father-figure).

Contemporary views were polarized: hero to many Black Americans and radicals; dangerous demagogue to white America and moderate civil rights leadership.

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Assassinated February 21, 1965, at the Audubon Ballroom in Harlem by gunfire from multiple assailants while beginning a speech. Three men (Talmadge Hayer / Thomas Hagan and two others) were convicted; Hayer later identified other NOI members. Persistent questions remain about possible broader conspiracy (FBI COINTELPRO knowledge, other factions).

Immediate aftermath: shock and mourning in Black communities; rapid mythologization. The *Autobiography* (published posthumously) became a foundational text. His widow Betty and daughters carried aspects of the legacy. The OAAU did not long survive as an organization.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Malcolm X became a global icon of resistance. His image and words influenced:
- Black Power and radical wings of the civil rights movement (SNCC, Black Panthers).
- Hip-hop culture and later generations of activists ("by any means necessary").
- International discourses on race, Islam, and anti-imperialism.
- Ongoing debates within African-American politics between integrationist and nationalist traditions.

His life dramatizes the possibility (and cost) of personal and political reinvention under extreme pressure. The tension between his NOI phase and post-Hajj phase remains a live interpretive site.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources**:
- The *Autobiography* (with Haley) — powerful but edited with Haley's agenda; Malcolm died before completion.
- Malcolm's speeches (many recorded and transcribed).
- FBI files (extensive surveillance).
- Marable's new interviews and archival discoveries.

**Marable's intervention**: Uses the new materials to complicate the standard narrative, particularly around the depth of Malcolm's break with separatism, internal NOI corruption, and the mechanics of the assassination plot.

**Ongoing debates**:
- Extent of Malcolm's evolution toward "mainstream" civil rights or persistent radicalism.
- Responsibility and broader context for the assassination.
- Role of sexuality and personal life in his politics (Marable's treatment was controversial).
- Use and misuse of his image by later movements.

**Methodological note**: Biography here serves both individual life reconstruction and the history of Black radicalism and American Islam in the mid-20th century. Heavy reliance on one major scholarly life (Marable 2011) supplemented by the Autobiography and primary documents.
