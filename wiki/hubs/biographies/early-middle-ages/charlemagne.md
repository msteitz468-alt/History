# Charlemagne (Charles the Great) — Detailed Biography

> **Summary & network:** [[actors/charlemagne|Charlemagne — actor page]] · **Biography hub:** [[hubs/biographies/biographies-hub|Biographies Hub]] · **Source spine:** [[sources/nelson-king-and-emperor-2019|Nelson, *King and Emperor* (2019)]]

A graduate-level analytic life of Charles the Great (748–814), synthesised principally from **Janet L. Nelson's *King and Emperor* (2019)** — the foremost Anglophone authority's deliberately anti-teleological, evidence-first biography — set against the established Carolingian cluster ([[carolingian-empire]], [[carolingian-renaissance]], [[carolingian-capitularies]], [[coronation-of-charlemagne-800]]). The governing methodological commitment of this page follows Nelson's own: to read Charles's life **forward, as he lived it "in hope but also in ignorance of what would or could follow a given perception, decision or act,"** rather than backward from the retrospective image of the "Father of Europe." Nelson refuses even the name "Charlemagne" as anachronistic — "none of Charles's contemporaries used it" — and calls her subject **Charles**.

---

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Charles was born **2 April 748**, not 742 — a correction with cascading consequences. The old date rested entirely on Einhard's claim that Charles died "in the seventy-second year of his age," a figure Karl Ferdinand Werner (1972/73) and Matthias Becher (1995) showed Einhard had **modelled on Suetonius's statement that Augustus died at seventy-two**. The reliable evidence — an annalistic note "Et ipso anno fuit natus Karolus rex" dated from Easter, and a Lorsch manuscript reading "IIII. Non. Apr. Nativitas domni … Karoli imperatoris" — fixes 2 April 748 and reduces his lifespan from seventy-two to "not quite sixty-six." Every chronology of his adult decisions compresses accordingly: Charles was younger, and in a hurry, more often than the legend allows.

He was born into a usurping dynasty mid-coup. His father [[pippin-iii|Pippin III]] and grandfather [[charles-martel|Charles Martel]] were Arnulfing/Pippinid mayors of the palace whose claim to royalty was still being manufactured. Nelson stresses that Pippinid power was "**defined by women**" — Itta, Begga, Gertrude of Nivelles — and that the genealogical link binding the Pippinids to Bishop Arnulf of Metz was "a two-layered genealogical fudge confected by eighth-century scholars." The family Charles grew up in was inventing its own legitimacy, and Charles was an instrument of that invention from toddlerhood.

The **[[pippinid-coup-751|deposition of the Merovingians in 751]]** consecrated Pippin *and* Bertrada as king and queen at St-Médard, Soissons — the queenly consecration "an unfamiliarly new feature" with "no biblical warrant," which Nelson reads as "the shared brainchild of Pippin and Bishop Chrodegang of Metz." Childeric III was tonsured and incarcerated at the same site: dynastic obliteration staged as liturgy. Charles, "a little over three and a half," was probably present and "trusted … to play out his part well." Around 753–4 the five-year-old was sent nearly 100 miles to **escort Pope Stephen II** — a real event, attested by Nibelung and the *Vita Stephani*, by which "Charles's parents, staging events for an audience … put the spotlight on this child." The anointing of Pippin "and his two sons" created a spiritual compaternity between the papacy and the Frankish royal house that would structure Charles's whole life.

Two formative stories Nelson defends as psychologically true. The **first-tooth story** (St-Germain, 755), transmitted by Charles himself in old age to Abbot Irmino, shows "an alert, responsive and engaging personality" who "confidently connected with the saint." And his military apprenticeship — the **Aquitaine campaigns of 761–768** — was "no war of battles between armies" but a war of systematic devastation; Nibelung records "over twenty-five instances of 'burning', 'laying waste', and 'ravaging'." Charles learned war as the methodical ruin of a country's productive capacity. That schooling would govern Saxony for thirty years.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Pippin died in 768 having divided the realm between Charles (Austrasia) and his brother **Carloman** (Burgundy, Provence, Gothia, Alsace, Alamannia — "a preponderance of archbishops"). The brothers were consecrated **the same day, 9 October 768**, at separate sites — Charles at Noyon, Carloman at Soissons — "separate but identical rites … could suggest some wariness and a sense of competition." It was a poisoned inheritance: a shared kingdom with overlapping claims and a contested Aquitaine.

Charles moved at once to dominate. His **first charter (D.55, 13 January 769)** introduced two innovations absent from Pippin's: the title *gratia Dei rex* and a royal monogram, "inspired by Byzantium via Italy" — and "neither title nor monogram could possibly have been introduced without Charles's say-so." When Carloman abandoned the joint Aquitaine campaign at Moncontour (769), Charles finished it alone and reissued Pippin's Aquitaine capitulary in his own name: "Charles had decided to shove his brother out of the frame." The rivalry was literalised in their sons: Himiltrud bore Charles a son named Pippin in 769; Carloman's wife bore a son also named Pippin in 770 — "fraternal rivalry could hardly have been more tellingly expressed."

The contingency that made Charles sole ruler was **Carloman's death at twenty, 4 December 771**. Charles reacted within days, riding to Corbeny to receive his dead brother's bishops, counts and magnates; the *Annales Mettenses Priores* — which Nelson argues was commissioned by Charles's sister Gisela at Chelles — uniquely says he was there anointed and "felicitously obtained *monarchia* over the whole kingdom." Carloman's widow and sons fled to the Lombard king. Cathwulf's letter (c.775) listed Charles's blessings with revealing frankness: "God preserved you … from your brother's plots, as Jacob from Esau … and exalted you … without any bloodshed." It was, Nelson notes, "a frank account of consistent fraternal rivalry" sayable only after Carloman was safely dead.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

### 3a. The Italian turn and the Lombard kingdom (771–4)

The crisis began with a marriage and a repudiation. Bertrada's diplomacy (770) had married Charles to a daughter of the Lombard king [[desiderius|Desiderius]]; in 771–2 Charles **repudiated her**, an act Einhard calls the only thing that ever caused discord with his mother, and which Nelson frames as "the most blatant of insults to her and her family's honour." When Carloman's sons took refuge with Desiderius, Charles offered cash — **14,000 solidi** — which Nelson, in her single most distinctive reading of these years, interprets not as a payment for papal territory but as "**a pair of wergilds — the price of two princes, 7,000 solidi apiece**": Charles trying to buy custody of his nephews. Only "Desiderius's absolute refusal" — "what father does deals with the man who has repudiated his daughter?" — forced the war.

Nelson insists the conquest was **not pre-planned**: the decision to seize the kingdom "evolved while he was en route for Pavia," crystallising during the siege. Pavia fell not by storm but to **epidemic** — Cathwulf says Charles took it "without any shedding of blood." Carloman's sons surrendered at Verona and then vanish from every source: "The history of monarchy is stained with the blood of close kin. The rest is silence." Charles became *rex Langobardorum* (first attested 5 June 774); a charter of 16 July 774 issued jointly with [[hildegard|Hildegard]] put the new queen's name where the old queen Ansa's had stood, "stripped of royal dignity … at the heart of the charter." The "conquest" left Lombard law, army and identity largely intact — and the conqueror was "himself conquered" culturally by Lombard scholars like Paul the Deacon. (See [[carolingian-conquest-of-lombard-italy-774]].)

His **Easter 774 visit to Rome** was entirely his own initiative; Pope [[pope-hadrian-i|Hadrian I]] was "thrown into great bewilderment and consternation." Nelson reads the visit as religion in its most material sense — the confessio of St Peter "like a numinous oven," the donation-document left inside it "like a battery on permanent charge." The **Donation of Quierzy** was "the very reverse of a watertight legal … document": Hadrian thought in whole territories, Charles's missi in estates — a structural mismatch that produced a "cat-and-mouse game" between Charles's agents (cultivating Spoleto and Ravenna) and an increasingly "near-hysterical" pope.

### 3b. The Saxon wars (772–804)

The longest, bloodiest, and most ideological commitment of the reign (see [[saxon-wars-772-804]]). The 775 campaign set the template: two armies, forced river crossings, sequential dealings with Eastphalians, Angrarians and Westphalians, hostage-taking as the mark of victory. At **Paderborn 777**, the first Frankish assembly held on Saxon soil, the fidelity oath was for the first time extended "to his sons and to the Franks." Then came the reverses: the **Süntel disaster (782)**, where Frankish *missi* Adalgis and Gailo charged without scouting because "they feared Theoderic would get the glory" (*veriti sunt ne ad nomen Theoderici victoriae fama transiret*), and were "killed almost to a man."

The notorious **massacre at Verden (782)** — 4,500 executed — Nelson treats sceptically: the figure is "suspiciously large, and its tidiness increases suspicion. It could well have resulted from a scribe … adding a zero." She also reverses the usual causation, arguing the brutal *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae* may have been a **response** to revolt rather than its cause — "Charles and his advisers were driven less by practical considerations than by ideology." The 785 baptism of the resistance leader [[widukind]] was Charles's own initiative (he sent envoys, gave hostages first, and stood as godfather), and the annals' triumphant "**then all Saxony was subjugated**" was written before the 793 revolt exposed it as wishful.

From **795** the strategy turned to **deportation** — "a new strategy centred on the taking of hostages and captives, and the rounding-up of deportees." The 799 Paderborn transfer settled Saxons "through various regions," redistributing "their land among his faithful men … bishops, priests, counts and … vassals." Nelson is precise on the logic: Charles "was not interested in guilt or innocence … He wanted to deprive the land of its elite." A petitioner's later word for it survives: "*transmigratio* was a euphemism for *deportatio*." Yet the **Capitulare Saxonicum of 797** softened the regime — equal 60-solidus fines for Saxon and Frank, compensation over death, no tithe — "the beginning of the end."

### 3c. Bavaria, the Avars, and the apparatus of correctio (787–94)

The **annexation of Bavaria** removed the last autonomous Frankish duchy. Duke [[tassilo-iii|Tassilo III]] submitted at the Lechfeld on **5 October 787** (a date preserved only in the Chesne Fragment), surrendering "with a staff … carved [with] the likeness of a man" and becoming Charles's *vassus*. Nelson dismantles the *Annales regni Francorum* account of the **788 Ingelheim trial** as a "retrospective construction" — a "shadow-biography of Tassilo as 'Charlemagne's significant other'," in which oath-charges from 757, 763, 781 and 787 were accumulated after the fact. A letter discovered by Bernhard Bischoff shows Charles seizing the duke's "women and treasure" *before* the formal arrest ("*mando, non peto*" — "an order, not a request"). The condemnation was "systematically and ruthlessly planned."

The **Avar war (from 791)** opened with the army divided across the Danube and supplied by river; the ArF Reviser uniquely records an **equine pestilence** that killed "barely one-tenth" of the horses — information the strictly contemporary annals suppressed, just as Charles "coped … by suppressing information" about inconvenient portents. The campaign foreshadowed the later capture of the fabulous Avar treasure.

The intellectual engine of these years was **correctio**. The *Epistola generalis* (787) and the **[[carolingian-capitularies|Admonitio generalis]] (23 March 789)** were, Nelson argues, products of a **small committee at Aachen** (traceable hands: Alcuin and Theodulf), not a grand assembly — and they carry Charles's own voice, as if "dictating … ideas as they tumbled from his lips." The proof of intent is in the copying: the *Admonitio* survives in 36 manuscripts against the *Epistola*'s one, evidence of "his ability to set so many scribes to work at Aachen … and so quickly." The **canal (fossa Carolina) of 792–3**, Nelson argues, was driven chiefly by the **famine** of those years — to move food "as quickly as possible from a region unaffected by famine to famished Francia" — and its failure was framed *naturaliter*, a rationalist explanation chosen over a supernatural one.

The **revolt of Pippin the Hunchback (792)** aimed, Nelson argues, at Bavaria precisely "because it offered a perfect opportunity to cut out his rivals, his half-brothers"; she reads the Lorsch Annals' syntax to implicate Pippin's mother Himiltrud. The queen [[fastrada|Fastrada]], later libelled by Einhard and the ArF Reviser as the cruel cause of revolts, Nelson rehabilitates: "she was retrospectively made a scapegoat for the actions of others — especially those of Charles himself." Her surviving role — sole charge of the victory litanies in 791, presiding over a legal judgment at Frankfurt before Charles arrived — shows "a personal relationship, and a strong emotional bond."

The **Council of Frankfurt (794)** was the doctrinal capstone: it condemned Spanish Adoptionism (Nelson, following Cavadini, defends Felix of Urgell as a "subtle and original theologian" partly lost in translation), rejected the image-decrees of Nicaea II via the *Opus Caroli* (whose Tironian-note marginalia, Ann Freeman showed, record **Charles's own spoken comments** — "bene" 24 times), imposed grain price controls in the famine's wake (royal stock sold "at least a quarter lower"), and confirmed the new silver penny.

### 3d. The road to empire (799–800)

The crisis that produced the imperial title was an assault on a pope. On **25 April 799** Pope [[pope-leo-iii|Leo III]] was ambushed by insiders — "Paschal at his head, Campulus at his feet" — who "tried to put out his eyes … cut off his tongue." Leo's miraculous recovery, Charles's calm decision to "make for Paderborn and await Leo's arrival," and Alcuin's discovery of the canon "the apostolic see judges but cannot be judged" set up the journey to Rome. Nelson's central interpretive claim about the coronation is unambiguous: "there is plenty of evidence that **Charles and Leo between them … carefully planned and stage-managed the sequence of rituals. Any argument to the contrary simply can't be sustained.**" Einhard's famous claim that Charles "would never have entered the church that day … if he had known the pope's plan" is dismissed as a topos — though Nelson allows residual mileage in the unease over the *order* of events (coronation before acclamation), which "did not accord with 'Roman' (Byzantine) tradition." (See [[coronation-of-charlemagne-800]].)

### 3e. The Aachen years and imperial governance (801–814)

After 801 Charles ruled from a fixed capital. **[[aachen|Aachen]]** — never "among the seats of the ancient Caesars but … a new place of power, of *nova antiquitas* and *antiqua novitas*" — became the *sedes regni*, a centre of governance, intellectual life (the "recovering of Aristotelian dialectic and its application to theology"), and sociability (baths where "over a hundred men might be in the water together"). Against Ganshof's post-war thesis of "*l'échec de Charlemagne*" and "decomposition," Nelson reconstructs a **functioning communications system**: the Thionville Double Capitulary (806) surviving in 30+ manuscripts; a *missus* letter telling a count to "**re-read your capitularies!**"; the 811 report on military-service evasion read not as crisis but as "carefully gauged checks and balances"; the *Breve Commemoratorium* census of the Holy Land's churches (808) enabling an alms levy promulgated at Aachen in 810. "It is hard to infer decomposition from Charles's activities in 813."

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Religious Style and Methods

Charles's distinctive method was the **fusion of profound belief with smart calculation** — a combination Nelson insists was not contradictory for him: "for the same mind to contain profound belief and smart calculation" was "perfectly possible." He governed less by command than by **presence and association**. The *missi dominici* and capitularies projected his will, but the system "fostered trust at and between all levels"; his rulership style "was more sociable and associative than controlling, more naturally familiar than aloof." He summoned troops through a **selective-service** system in which *pares* pooled resources to equip one man — evidence, Nelson argues, that "he cared about the *pares*," wanting "a sense of comradeship among the led."

He was an active **manager of information and media**. He suppressed inconvenient data (the equine pestilence of 791; the portents of 786; the ArF's complete silence about Tassilo's 794 Frankfurt appearance, which "can only have occurred on Charles's orders"). He commissioned the *Annales regni Francorum* as, in part, a "justificatory dossier." And he engaged personally with doctrine: the Tironian marginalia of the *Opus Caroli* record his own voice, and Frankfurt's canon 52 — permitting prayer "in every language" — Nelson hears as Charles's personal religious conviction breaking through the committee Latin. He had "acquired a basic grasp of the liberal arts, and of Latin." His religion powered everything: "Charles's driving energy, his consciousness of the divine blessing that powered it, and his trust in his faithful ones (*fideles*)."

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Nelson's Charles is "a practical, down-to-earth man with a down-to-earth sense of humour" who "*se sentait bien dans sa peau*" — "felt good in his own skin." Notker's cloak joke (complaining of short Frisian cloaks that left "freezing-cold legs") and the self-told first-tooth story render a vivid, sociable personality. Physically he was imposing — about 6'3"; his first partner **Himiltrud**, whose remains at Nivelles show she stood "just under 6 feet," made "an imposing figure … a regal pair."

The biography is, unusually, **a family history in which the women act**. Nelson dismantles the modern category of *Friedelehe* ("a figment of … modern wishful thinking") and shows Charles's partners as agents, not adjuncts. **[[hildegard|Hildegard]]** (married 771, d. 783 aged 25 after nine children in eleven years) appears at the Pavia siege camp, co-issues charters, co-commissions the Godescalc evangelistary, and shares the decision to rename a son Pippin in 781. **Fastrada** (d. 794), libelled as a wicked stepmother, in fact held independent court and managed the kingdom's litanies. Einhard's tidy sequence of "queens then mistresses" is contradicted by the 777 manumission of an *ancilla* that "perhaps reflected a prior or existing sexual connection." Charles fathered, by Nelson's count, at least nineteen children by some nine partners — and kept his daughters unmarried at court, reversing that policy only in the **Divisio of 806**.

His emotional life is legible in unexpected documents: the epitaph he commissioned for an infant daughter ("Little girl … you pierce as with a spear the royal heart of your father"); the perpetual lights endowed for Hildegard's tomb; the anxious second-person inquiries about Fastrada's health in his 791 letter. The Roncesvaux disaster of 778 left a wound that "overclouded … in his heart a large part of what had gone successfully in Spain" — a grief that, on a source close to the old king, "lasted until death."

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession, and Aftermath

The succession Charles designed in the **Divisio Regnorum (6 February 806)** — a tripartite division among Charles the Younger, Pippin of Italy, and [[louis-the-pious|Louis]] — was overtaken by mortality. **Pippin of Italy died 8 July 810; the Young Charles died 4 December 811** ("No poet wrote his elegy"). Louis was left sole heir. In summer 813 Charles "kept him by his side … and instructed him on all matters," and on **11 September 813 crowned him himself in the Aachen church** as *consors imperialis nominis* — pointedly without a pope. Nelson reads Louis as a collaborator in this, not the passive figure of the *Astronomer*. The same day, Charles invested his grandson Bernard as king of Italy. Capitulum 18 of the 806 Divisio — protecting nephews from uncles' violence — Nelson reads as "a very belated flicker of a guilty conscience" about the fate of Carloman's sons in 772.

Charles **died 28 January 814**, seven days after taking to his bed with pleurisy, "at 9 a.m., after taking Holy Communion," singing "Into your hands, Lord, I commend my spirit." He was buried the same day in the Aachen church, in an antique Roman sarcophagus carved with the **Abduction of Proserpina** — which Nelson now believes Charles and his daughters "together chose … and planned some time before," reading it through Augustine's Christianised allegory of the soul's resurrection. His body was wrapped in gold-and-purple Constantinopolitan silk. Louis arrived on the thirtieth day, staged his *adventus*, and at once "removed the *coetus qui permaximus erat femineus*, 'the crowd of women which was very large'" — Charles's daughters and companions — a swift moral repudiation of his father's household. The undivided empire did not survive a generation (see [[treaty-of-verdun-843]]).

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

What endured was structural and cultural rather than territorial. Charles bequeathed the template of a **Latin Christian Europe bound to the papacy**; the **imperial idea** later revived as the Holy Roman Empire; and, through the *correctio* and its copying machinery, the **educational and scriptural infrastructure** of medieval learning, including the clarified Caroline minuscule (see [[carolingian-renaissance]], [[caroline-minuscule]]). His title's careful evasion — *Romanum gubernans imperium*, "governing the Roman empire," never "emperor of the Romans" — produced, after the Byzantine recognition of **812**, a settled fact of "two emperors": one of the Romans at Constantinople, "the other, just plain emperor, based in Aachen."

Nelson's most pointed legacy argument is **against the legacy industry**. The "Father of Europe" was the confection of "an idiosyncratic poet" (the so-called Paderborn Epic), not Charles's own self-understanding: "**Charles himself had no such vision, no truck with teleology.**" The twentieth-century "longing for Charlemagne" — the 1950 Charlemagne Prize, the EU icon — is a use Charles "would not have recognized," and Nelson explicitly distances her biography from it. The durable Charles is the contingent one: an energetic, pious, sociable warlord-administrator who built an empire decision by decision, "in hope but also in ignorance" of what would follow.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources and their biases.** Nelson's method is a sustained source-criticism that this page inherits:

- **Einhard, *Vita Karoli* (c.829).** Not disinterested piety but a bid "to recover some of his old influence" after the 828 crisis at Louis's court. Structurally indebted to Suetonius — the fabricated Augustan age-at-death, the reluctance topos at the coronation. "All biographies are authorial constructs," yet beneath "ninth-century spice" lie "nutritious grains of historicity."
- **Annales regni Francorum & the Reviser.** Not a contemporaneous chronicle but compiled "in 789/790," in part a "justificatory dossier" against Tassilo. The Reviser sometimes preserves suppressed data (the 791 horse plague, the Roncesvaux wound). Silences are political: the ArF's omission of Tassilo's 794 appearance "can only have occurred on Charles's orders."
- **Annales Mettenses Priores.** Nelson's case that Gisela of Chelles commissioned it, with "a quasi-authorial role," makes it a dynastic family-memory text to be read for its angle.
- **Codex Carolinus.** The surviving papal letters once had a "now lost" Byzantine counterpart; the **771–4 gap** Nelson attributes to a deliberate "cover-up" around the nephews and the repudiation.
- **Charters (164 surviving).** Nelson's signature evidential move: place-and-date of issue as a map of political relationships and itinerary — used to rebut McKitterick's hypothesis of king-absent charter production ("it would destroy the ground-rules of diplomatics").
- **Notker, *Gesta Karoli* (c.886).** Late and anecdotal, but Nelson defends select stories (the Regensburg revolt scene) as preserving "grains of historicity."

**Major debates and Nelson's positions.**
- *Great Man vs. structural causation.* Nelson's whole project is anti-"Great Man": she refuses "Charlemagne," refuses "Father of Europe," and foregrounds contingency, family, and the agency of women.
- *The coronation of 800.* Against any "Charles was surprised" reading — co-planned and stage-managed, "any argument to the contrary simply can't be sustained."
- *The success or failure of Carolingian government.* Nelson's sustained rebuttal of **Ganshof's "*l'échec de Charlemagne*"** and Fichtenau's pessimism, which she attributes partly to their writing "amid the still all-too-visible ruins … of the immediate post-war years." She reads the late capitularies and *missi* correspondence as a working, monitored system. (See [[nature-of-carolingian-government]].)
- *Verden and the Saxon wars.* Scepticism toward the 4,500 figure (a possible scribal zero) and reversal of the *Capitulatio*'s causal place.
- *The women of the dynasty.* Rejection of *Friedelehe*; rehabilitation of Fastrada from the "wicked stepmother" libel; restoration of Hildegard, Bertrada, and Gisela as political agents.

**Method.** Nelson's organising conceit in her conclusion — Isidore of Seville's six ages of man — is a deliberate refusal of teleological career-narrative in favour of a contemporary frame. Her stated aim is modest and anti-heroic: "I have made a journey towards the Other. I have not found him … But perhaps I have got nearer to him."
