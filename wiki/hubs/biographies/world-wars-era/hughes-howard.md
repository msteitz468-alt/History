A graduate-level analytic study of Howard Hughes' formation, the mechanics of his image-driven empire building and subsequent collapse, signature decisions in aviation/film/business/politics, character, relationships with aides and proxies, and the structural versus personal dimensions of his rise and legend-making.

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Howard Robard Hughes Jr. was born December 24, 1905, in Houston, Texas, to Howard R. Hughes Sr. (inventor and founder of Hughes Tool Company) and Allene Gano Hughes. The family was wealthy; the tool company provided a steady, highly profitable revenue stream from the Texas oil boom after Hughes Sr. and partner Walter Sharp commercialized the revolutionary "rock eater" roller-cone drill bit (patents 1909, purchased for $150 from Granville Humason and sketched on a breadboard).

Allene smothered her only child with obsessive health monitoring—nightly mineral oil, frequent doctor visits for feet/teeth/digestion, removals from Houston during outbreaks. Relatives noted Howard “idolized” her. “Howard also learned from her that the best way to attract attention or to escape unpleasant situations was to complain of illness.” (Ch. 1 Mother/Tragedy)

Tragedy struck early. Allene died suddenly March 29–30, 1922 (age 39, complications from surgery); 16-year-old Howard was told the truth by uncle Rupert in Los Angeles. Hughes Sr. suffered a fatal heart attack January 14, 1924 (age 54). “At the time of his father’s death, Howard did not have the slightest notion of what to do in life.” The losses triggered lifelong obsession with his own health and early death.

Less than a month after his father’s death, the 18-year-old dropped out of Rice, bought out relatives’ 25% interest for $325,000 using company reserves, and petitioned the court to remove his “disabilities of minority.” “The thing I knew was that I would never be able to get along with my relations… I was determined to buy them out and go it alone.” Judge Montieth granted adulthood December 26, 1924. He signed an early will directing the tool company to operate as a permanent monument to his father and leaving the bulk to a proposed medical research laboratory.

Hughes used the fortune to leave Texas for California and pursue two glamorous fields—motion pictures and aviation—rather than steady management of the family business. The drive to surpass his father was explicit and lifelong.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

Hughes leveraged inherited capital into visible successes that generated massive publicity. In Hollywood he produced and directed the epic *Hell’s Angels* (1930), a hugely expensive WWI aviation spectacle. He assembled the world’s largest private air force (87 planes), fired directors, and took over direction himself. Cost reached $3.8 million for a 15,000-foot movie; he used extravagant promotion (airplanes over Grauman’s). “It had become the movie colony’s favorite joke… The cost was no joke—$3.8 million.” Hughes later admitted: “Making Hell’s Angels by myself was my biggest mistake.” After Greta Nissen proved unsuitable for sound, he launched Jean Harlow; her line “Do you mind if I slip into something more comfortable?” helped make the film a hit.

He fought the Hays Office and censors over *Scarface* (1930–32), publicly denouncing suppression: “It has become a serious threat to the freedom of honest expression in America…”

In aviation he designed (with Richard Palmer and Glenn Odekirk) the H-1 Racer with flush rivets and fully retractable landing gear. On September 13, 1935 he flew four passes averaging 352 mph, breaking the French record despite later forced landing. “It’ll go faster,” he muttered. “I only flew a perfect machine.”

In 1938 he piloted a modified Lockheed 14 with a small crew (Lund, Stoddart, Thurlow, Connor) from New York via Paris, Moscow, Omsk, Yakutsk, Fairbanks, Minneapolis in 3 days 19 hours 17 minutes, halving Lindbergh’s time. “I hope we get to Paris before we run out of gas…” Houston turned out 250,000; the airport was renamed. This was his last visit to his hometown. The publicity machine turned technical accomplishments into the image of the brilliant, adventurous American individualist.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power (continued from claims)

World War II brought government contracts for aircraft (including the massive wooden HK-1 Flying Boat / Spruce Goose and the XF-11 reconnaissance plane). Hughes' companies received hundreds of millions; results were mixed to poor (the Flying Boat flew only once, briefly, in 1947 after Kaiser partnership drama, overruns, and cancellation threats saved only by Jesse Jones). The same pattern—ambitious vision, technical partial success, massive cost overruns, secrecy, and image management—repeated at ever larger scale.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**Phase 1: Hollywood and Heroic Aviation (1925–1939)**  
Film production and direction; record flights; construction of personal legend. Image largely matched substance in this period.

**Phase 2: War Contracts and Senate Scrutiny (1940–1947)**  
Hughes Aircraft and related entities win large contracts. XF-11 crash (1946) and the near-fiasco of the Flying Boat lead to Senate War Investigating Committee hearings (1947). Hughes appears, defends himself dramatically, and emerges with reputation intact or enhanced in public eyes despite evidence of mismanagement.

**Phase 3: RKO Takeover, Political Turn, Breakdown (1948–early 1960s)**  
Hostile RKO takeover (1948, $8.8M for 24%); rapid clash with Dore Schary, mass firings (~700), plummeting production, mounting losses ($12.4M in three years). Increasing political contributions. Deepening phobias and rituals. Marriage to Jean Peters (1957, secret Tonopah ceremony under assumed names; separate bungalows thereafter). Loss of TWA control via voting trust (1960) after jet-financing failures.

**Phase 4: Las Vegas Empire and Proxy Rule (1966–1970)**  
Secretive acquisition of Desert Inn (April Fool’s Day 1967, $13.2M for lease only) then Sands, Frontier, Castaways, etc. Spent ~$178,000/day; hotels = 20% of Strip rooms. Maheu as front man; PR blitz positioning Hughes as Las Vegas savior. Behind scenes: internal rivalries, Maheu empire-building with relatives/friends at high salaries using Hughes funds for personal projects, growing isolation. Antitrust pressure from Justice; continued acquisitions anyway.

**Phase 5: Exile, CIA Exploitation, Decline, Death, and Estate Fight (1970–1976)**  
Ouster via November 1970 proxy giving Gay/Davis/Holliday control of Nevada operations; Hughes secretly flown to Bahamas on Thanksgiving Eve. Increasing codeine/Valium dependence, sealed rooms, bizarre rituals. CIA used Summa/Hughes Tool as cover for Project Jennifer (Glomar Explorer recovery of Soviet sub); Hughes and aides believed the mining cover story. “Hughesgate” burglary of Romaine Street “Bastille” (June 1974). Death April 5, 1976 in Acapulco (93 lbs, malnourished, dehydrated, high codeine); flown to Houston; private burial <48 hours. “Mormon Will” (forged per experts) triggered massive probate battle. Summa had already lost >$131M 1971–Sept 1976; liquid assets collapsed.

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military / Religious Style and Methods

Hughes’ style was intensely personal, visual, and perfectionist. He read aircraft manuals and technical documents with photographic retention and could quote specifications verbatim. He dictated long, detailed memoranda (sometimes repeating the same text days later from memory). He insisted on personal control and testing (flying his own planes, directing his own films).

He had little interest in or aptitude for institutional management at scale. Once organizations grew beyond his direct oversight, he substituted secrecy, proxies (Maheu, then Mormon “palace guard”), and image control. Risk tolerance was high for technical or publicity stunts, low for personal exposure or conventional accountability. No coherent political ideology—money flowed to whoever could deliver favors or protection (both parties, Nevada regulators, CIA cutouts via Bennett/Mullen).

Core method: legend construction—do something spectacular, control the narrative, use the resulting reputation as leverage. When substance faltered, the legend and lawyers carried him for decades. Later: compulsive environmental control, germ rituals with Kleenex “paddles,” paper “insulation,” and elaborate protocols that aides executed while he regressed.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Contemporary and later accounts describe Hughes as shy yet exhibitionistic, charming when he chose, ruthless when crossed, and increasingly paranoid. He was a loner who craved the spotlight on his own terms. Relationships were transactional or controlled: Hollywood romances, deeply private marriage to Jean Peters (separate living, seen by appointment), reliance on a small circle of male aides.

In later years the inner circle became the Mormon “palace guard” (Bill Gay et al.) who controlled access and, according to the authors, ultimately the empire’s direction. Robert Maheu served as the highly visible front man in Las Vegas and Washington; their relationship ended in acrimony, ouster, and lawsuits. Maheu’s memoir and interviews became important sources.

Personal habits became grotesque: codeine addiction (multiple grains daily for decades, supplied via aides’ names and later direct pharma shipments), refusal to cut hair/nails, Kleenex-box shoe covers, fear of germs leading to sealed environments. A psychologist assessment described him as resembling “a chronic psychotic patient in the very worst mental hospitals,” “continuously drugged,” “incapable of caring for himself,” and unable to make informed complex business judgments. He never sought psychiatric help.

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Hughes died April 5, 1976, aboard an air ambulance from Acapulco to Houston. He weighed 93 pounds, was severely malnourished and dehydrated, had an open scalp tumor, bedsores, needle tracks, and high codeine levels. Aides and doctors (including Thain, Gay’s brother-in-law) had managed a bizarre procurement and ritual system for years. No vigorous investigation of negligence followed; preliminary announcement cited “chronic renal disease”; full details (codeine, needles in arms on X-ray, etc.) were not released. Burial private in Glenwood Cemetery <48 hours later.

The will situation was chaotic. Extensive searches found only an unsigned 1947 draft. A “Mormon Will” (three-page yellow pad, dated March 19, 1968, with 16+ misspellings including “revolk,” “devided,” “Las Vagas”) surfaced; handwriting experts and content errors (use of hated “spruce goose”) declared it a forgery. Melvin Dummar’s pickup story and later recantation (steamed envelope left at his station) added farce. Years of probate litigation followed.

The immediate aftermath exposed how little direct control Hughes had retained; aides and intermediaries (Gay circle especially) had operated in his name. Summa had already lost over $131 million 1971–Sept 1976; liquid assets collapsed from hundreds of millions to ~$94 million.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Hughes’ most durable contributions are in the realm of myth and archetype: the reclusive billionaire genius, the aviator-playboy, the eccentric who could buy anything (including politicians and cities). The reality—technical gifts squandered at scale, decades of business losses papered over by PR, a CIA proprietary used for Project Jennifer, a Las Vegas interlude that demonstrated the limits of personal rule—has been less romanticized.

Aviation records and early film work left minor marks. The military-industrial contracting pattern he exemplified became standard. His political money and “Hughes connection” to Watergate added one data point to the story of money in American politics. The CIA’s use of his companies illustrated privatization of covert action.

The cautionary tale—wealth + secrecy + legend + decline—remains potent. Barlett & Steele conclude that if he had simply invested the tool company profits, he would have died far richer; instead he chased legend and reputation, and “history is made by reputation, not reality.”

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

- **Primary sources**: The Barlett & Steele foundation is the paper trail (court, SEC, congressional, corporate filings, Hughes internal memos/logs). Aide interviews (especially Maheu and post-death accounts) supply color and mechanics. CIA documents on Project Jennifer add one concrete operational link. Probate records document the end-state chaos. Medical testimony and logs detail the final decline.
- **Conflicting accounts**: Hughes’ own memoranda are often self-serving or delusional in later years. Aides’ memoirs differ on responsibility for decline (Maheu vs. Gay circle). The “Mormon Will” authenticity remains disputed; Dummar recanted.
- **Major scholarly / journalistic lives and debates**: Barlett & Steele (2004 reissue of 1979 *Empire*) is the essential demythologizing work based on exhaustive public records. Earlier popular biographies and press often amplified the legend. Later works draw on the same records plus declassified material. Debate centers on agency vs. enablers in the decline, the extent of Hughes’ active direction in the Las Vegas years, the authenticity and implications of the Mormon Will, and whether Hughes was primarily a victim of his own pathologies or of the sycophantic circle that enabled them.
- **Methodological notes**: This is investigative journalism biography at its strongest—document first, legend second. It avoids psychohistory speculation while presenting observable deterioration with evidence. The authors are explicit that they are correcting a record that Hughes himself spent a lifetime distorting. The CIA’s exploitation of his compromised state (“best-kept secret”) is one of the book’s sharper correctives.
