## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Alan Mathison Turing was conceived in late 1911 at Chatrapur (an imperial station on India’s eastern coast) and born 23 June 1912 in a Paddington nursing home; baptised 7 July. His parents (father Julius Mathison Turing, ICS officer in the Madras Presidency; mother Ethel Sara Stoney, daughter of a railway engineer) returned for leave. The family spent time in Italy before the father returned to duty; the mother stayed until September 1913. The paternal line traced to Turins of Foveran, Aberdeenshire (14th c.), with a baronetcy, Civil War losses, and revival via Indian fortune; the junior branch included Alan’s grandfather (Trinity Cambridge maths, later ordained). Julius (b. 1873) took a literature/history degree at Oxford and entered the ICS (high placements 1895/96); Ethel (b. 1881 at Podanur) was sent “home” for Irish education and met Julius on the 1907 voyage.

As a young child at Baston Lodge, St Leonards-on-Sea (with the Wards and Nanny Thompson), Alan taught himself to read in about three weeks from *Reading without Tears*, showed obsessive recognition of figures and numbers, made a “knowing spot” on his left thumb, and displayed early experimental behaviour. He was initially “extremely vivacious – even mercurial” but became “unsociable and dreamy.” At Hazelhurst preparatory school he arrived with crazes for paper-folding, maps, and recipes; the book *Natural Wonders Every Child Should Know* (Edwin Tenney Brewster, gifted ~end 1922) “opened his eyes to science,” framing the body as a “vastly complex machine” (a “gas engine”) made of living “bricks” (cells) and evolutionary processes without invoking soul. This contributed to his later mechanistic views.

At Sherborne School (arrived 3 May 1926 during the General Strike; cycled ~60 miles from Southampton), he endured the “nation in miniature” system of esprit de corps, fagging, games, and hierarchy. Reports noted he was “self-contained and... apt to be solitary,” “not a ‘normal’ boy,” with “his own furrow to plough.” He clashed with languages and organised routine but showed promise in mathematics and science. He became a prefect (loyal but initially “weak”), discovered running stamina, experimented with codes and a “K-ray System” formula for a friend’s family invention, and won the Christopher Morcom science prize.

The decisive early relationship was with Christopher Morcom (a year older, fair-haired, slight; first noticed early 1927). They shared science and mathematics (setting “pet problems,” discussing relativity from Einstein’s popular account, astronomy, the striking iodate-sulphite “clock” reaction). They worked experiments, corresponded in holidays (comet observations, plasticine/adding-machine ideas), and attended the December 1929 Cambridge scholarship exams together. Alan “worshipped the ground he trod on.” Christopher died at noon, 13 February 1930, after operations for bovine tuberculosis (contracted as a boy from infected milk). Alan had premonitions and later wrote: “I feel sure that you could not possibly have had a greater loss.” He visited the Clock House (Morcom family home with laboratory, telescope, goats), sorted papers, and composed a note “NATURE OF SPIRIT” (mind/will acting on atoms in a small portion of the brain; body as amplifier; spirit-matter connection; drawing on Eddington’s quantum reconciliation of determinism and free will). Christopher “even from the grave” induced communication and competition; the loss shaped Alan’s drive, philosophical interests, and sense of difference.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

At King’s College Cambridge (from ~1933), Turing joined the Anti-War Council (politically rather communist in programme, focused on strikes by munitions and chemical workers) but was not a committed organiser or enthusiast for the Soviet system; he valued individual questioning of authority. The King’s environment (Keynes/Forster/Lowes Dickinson influence) emphasised moral autonomy: “We entirely repudiated a personal liability on us to obey general rules. We claimed the right to judge every individual case on its merits.” Cambridge was a “lovely backwater” detached from Empire and power politics.

He maintained the emotional connection to Christopher Morcom (Easter visits to Clock House, letters to Mrs Morcom on anniversaries) while forming other relationships, including with James Atkins (1933 Lake District) and frank discussions of sexuality with Fred Clayton. He resented having been circumcised and recounted early memories.

The intellectual breakthrough came in 1935 (early summer, Grantchester meadow). From Newman’s Part III lectures on the Foundations of Mathematics (Hilbert programme, Gödel), Turing abstracted “mechanical process” into a precise model: machines with a finite number of states (“configurations”), operating on an infinite tape of squares, performing read/write/erase/move according to a finite “table of behaviour.” A “universal machine” could simulate any other by reading encoded “description numbers” (tables rendered as numbers, in “alphabetical order”) and executing them. “Computable numbers” (and sequences) were those whose decimal expansions a machine could produce from a blank tape (e.g., π by finite rules for operations). Using a diagonal argument on the list of computable numbers (plus analysis of “satisfactory” description numbers), he proved there is no “definite method” that can decide, for every mathematical assertion, whether it is provable — the Entscheidungsproblem is unsolvable. “Alan Turing had dealt the death-blow to the Hilbert programme.”

He justified the model by analysing what a human “computer” actually does: writing symbols on (one-dimensional) paper in finite symbols, with finite states of mind, bounded observation, and behaviour determined by observed symbols + state. “Computing is normally done by writing certain symbols on paper... I assume then that the computation is carried out on one-dimensional paper, i.e. on a tape divided into squares.” The physical electric multiplier he later began building (relays, machined and wound himself with Malcolm MacPhail) and the 1938–39 zeta-function machine design (with Donald MacPhail) showed the drive to connect abstract logic with physical mechanisms.

The main paper was submitted 28 May 1936 to LMS Proceedings. Learning of Church’s independent lambda-calculus solution, Turing added an appendix proving equivalence. He wrote to his mother: “I have just got my main paper ready... Meanwhile a paper has appeared in America, written by Alonzo Church... Mr Newman and I have decided however that the method is sufficiently different to warrant the publication of my paper too.”

During 1936–38 at Princeton (Procter Fellowship, then a second year), he attended Church’s lectures, met von Neumann, worked on group theory, considered applications of his logic to “the most general kind of code or cipher possible,” built stages of the electric multiplier, and pursued the zeta machine. He returned to Britain with the paper’s ideas and early cryptologic interests that would soon meet the emergency of 1939.

“Quote: ‘By a mechanical process’, Newman had said. So Alan Turing dreamed of machines.” (3 New Men).

“Quote: ‘It would have to retain the typewriter’s quality of having a finite number of configurations... his machines were to be finitely defined, but they would be allowed an unlimited amount of space on which to work.’” 

“Quote: ‘With this done, it followed that one particular machine could simulate the work done by any machine. He called it the universal machine.’”

## 3. Major Phases of Career

### Logical / Pre-war and Early War
See Rise section above for 1936 paper and transition. By 1938–39 Turing’s cryptologic interests (general codes/ciphers) met the emergency; he joined GC&CS/Bletchley Park work (initially at Broadway, then Hut 8 for naval Enigma).

### Bletchley Park (1939–1945)
Turing was the chief scientific figure in the British effort. Drawing on pre-war ideas and Polish foundations, he drove the development of the “bombe” machines for testing Enigma settings at scale, “crib” methods, Banburismus for naval traffic, and organisational systems that allowed daily reading of high volumes of traffic. The range material emphasises that without the sequence keeping Enigma on the Allied side in 1943, the war of 1945 might have been very different (first atomic weapons possibly reserved for U-boat pens). Post-war he could not speak of it directly but drew on the Bletchley model (10,000 operators, remote stations, elite programmers, “librarian types”) for the ACE organisation.

In July 1945 (with Flowers) he visited Germany and viewed a captured Fish-type machine whose designers claimed mathematical impregnability for only two years; Alan and Flowers “just blinked and said, ‘Really!’”

By summer 1940 Turing was in charge of (or directing) work in Hut 8 on naval Enigma — for the first time since school telling others what to do. Early Bombe prototypes entered operation (May 1940; more with Welchman’s diagonal board after August). He developed the mathematical theory (inverse probability / Bayes formalised into “weight of evidence,” units “ban,” “deciban,” “sequential analysis”) that mechanised guessing and enabled Banburismus. Captures turned theory into practice: Krebs (Feb 1941) for February traffic; München (May) for June; Lauenburg (June); U-110 (May, short-signal codebook and Offizier settings). April/May 1941 traffic was broken by cryptanalytic methods without further captures. By spring 1941 the system was in production-line operation (Bombes + punched cards + “big room girls”).

In October 1941 Turing (with Welchman, Alexander, Milner-Barry) wrote the direct letter to Churchill detailing staffing bottlenecks; it had an “electric effect” and produced “ACTION THIS DAY” priority. GC&CS grew four-fold; it operated as “creative anarchy” in which the “new men” (Turing par excellence the “undisciplined” person) invaded the field of appreciation. He recruited Joan Clarke to Hut 8; they developed a close friendship (cinema, leave days, proposal and engagement that continued through summer 1941 before being broken off after a North Wales holiday).

Hut 8 work gave command of day-to-day tactics and enabled interceptions (e.g., seven of eight Bismarck advance ships). Alan’s persona (“the Prof”) — gas mask for hay fever, bent-spoke bicycle, combination-locked mug — coexisted with intense focus. He continued pure mathematics on the side (theory of types reform with Newman; papers received 9 May 1941).

### Post-war Computing (1945–1948)
Recruited June 1945 by J.R. Womersley to NPL Mathematics Division (Temporary SSO, £800 p.a.) after Womersley saw the EDVAC report and recognised “Turing in hardware.” Turing wrote the ACE report (completed end-1945) framing the machine as a practical universal Turing machine with ~200 mercury delay lines (1024 pulses each, average 512 access), Logical Control reading 32-pulse instructions at 1M pulses/sec, minimal hardware (OR/AND/NOT primary), and maximum use of stored instructions. He demonstrated instruction modification (arithmetic on instructions for conditional branching), master/subsidiary tables (BURY/UNBURY/EXPAND), and foresaw non-numerical uses (chess, circuit analysis). The pilot machine was costed ~£10k; full ~£50k–£100k+.

He tested delay-line principles at Hanslope with cardboard tube + super-regenerative amplifier; collaborated with Post Office (Flowers/Colossus engineers) but grew frustrated with bureaucracy, lack of electronic engineers, and “brain and hand” separation. The OBE arrived by post and was kept in his toolbox.

By 1948 the Manchester “baby” (Williams/Kilburn/Tootill, single CRT store initially 1024 bits, “one-address” design) ran the world’s first working stored-program electronic digital computer program on 21 June 1948 (highest-factor trial by brute force, written by Kilburn). Turing arrived too late to direct it but wrote a long-division routine immediately and accepted a Reader post. He criticised rival designs (e.g. Wilkes) for solving difficulties with extra equipment rather than thought/programming.

In 1947–48 he wrote “Intelligent Machinery,” advocating teaching unorganised machines via pleasure/pain stimuli to develop initiative and learning (beyond fixed tables), using chess as paradigm: “If the untrained infant’s mind is to become an intelligent one, it must acquire both discipline and initiative.”

### Final Years and Collision with the State (1950–1954)
By 1950 Turing had bought Hollymeade in Wilmslow (fields and Peak District at the back), ending suitcase living. He continued Manchester computing work (input routines, Programmers’ Handbook, random number generator hardware with Tootill, zeta-function calculations on the prototype, impregnable 16-figure cipher demo with 1000-unit storage, paper on the word problem for cancellation semi-groups submitted 1949). He attended Ratio Club meetings and gave the June 1949 “Checking a Large Routine” talk at the EDSAC conference. In June 1950 the machine checked Riemann zeta zeros up to t=1540. He published “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” in *Mind* (October 1950), introducing the imitation game as operational test for thinking and predicting machines with ~10⁹ storage would pass a 5-minute test with 70% success by ~2000.

He developed a reaction-diffusion theory of morphogenesis (chemical concentrations diffusing and reacting to produce spontaneous patterns — gastrulation, Fibonacci phyllotaxis, animal dappling/stripes/spots, starfish/flower symmetry) and ran early numerical simulations (“rather resembling that of a Jersey cow”).

In March 1951 he was elected FRS (citation for the 1936 work; sponsors Newman and Russell). BBC Third Programme talk “Can Digital Computers Think?” went out 15 May 1951. At the Festival of Britain he beat the Ferranti NIMROD Nim machine.

In late 1951 on Oxford Street, Manchester, he met 19-year-old Arnold Murray (recently on probation after a Festival theft). They began a relationship; Turing gave gifts/loans (£3 then £7 for a suit debt, pen-knife). On 23 January 1952 his Wilmslow house was burgled (shirt, fish-knives, trousers, shoes, shavers, compass, opened sherry — total ~£50). He reported it; two days later detectives called. He initially gave a false description of Arnold (“about twenty-five... five foot ten... black hair”), then blurted out the affair and volunteered a five-page statement. On 2 February Arnold confronted him over the letter demanding repayment; the relationship re-established that night (Turing even put away a glass with Arnold’s fingerprints). Arnold was arrested; both made statements. Committal 27 February at Wilmslow (full statements read; £50 bail for Turing). Trial 31 March 1952 at Knutsford Quarter Sessions before Mr J. Fraser Harrison: 12 counts of gross indecency (Section 11, Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885). Both pleaded guilty.

Character witnesses: Hugh Alexander (“a national asset”), Max Newman (“particularly honest and truthful,” “one of the most profound and original mathematical minds of his generation”; he had already received Turing in his home). Turing was placed on probation for one year on condition of “organo-therapic treatment” (hormone/oestrogen injections) at Manchester Royal Infirmary. He was rendered impotent and developed gynaecomastia. University (Blackett, Newman, Stopford) protected his position. He continued work immediately (Ratio Club morphogenesis talk the day after arrest; defended against Prigogine 29 Feb; submitted zeta work 15 March).

He told key people loudly and directly (Newman at lunch, letter to brother John, visit to mother in Guildford, letters to friends). Contemporary context: five-fold rise in prosecutions 1931–1951; 1952 “Evil Men” press series; positive vetting (1952) explicitly treated homosexual tendencies as automatic security risk (blackmail, emotional instability); Montagu trials and “drive against male vice” (1953–54); parliamentary debates linking homosexuality to the Atomic Energy Bill. Turing accepted he could no longer do cryptanalytic work “because there was no room for homosexuals in that field.”

On 7 June 1954 (Whit Monday, exactly ten years after D-Day) he died by cyanide (apple). Coroner recorded suicide (balance of mind disturbed). Body cremated 12 June at Woking; ashes dispersed with his father’s. Postscript in the biography opens with Whitman’s “camerado” lines on restlessness, confronting “peace, security, and all the settled laws,” and urging onward without knowing the destination.

In February 1954 he had made a new will (Nick Furbank executor; mathematical books/papers to Robin Gandy; legacies including housekeeper; remainder divided among mother + friends). He had continued research to the end (morphogenesis with Bernard Richards on fir cones/daisies/Radiolaria; joint plans with Gandy on theory of types; Penguin Science News word-problem article; revived physics interests; “Messages from the Unseen World” postcards). A mid-May 1954 Blackpool fortune-teller visit left him “white as a sheet.”

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Turing maintained intense, loyal friendships (Christopher Morcom as early ideal; Robin Gandy as later close intellectual and personal confidant; others including Nick Furbank, David Champernowne, Neville Johnson). He found conventional hierarchy and small talk difficult (“self-contained,” “apt to be solitary,” clashes with Sherborne system and NPL bureaucracy). Loyalty to colleagues was strong (farewells at Bletchley, helping Hugh Alexander post-war). Sexuality was a central, openly acknowledged fact for him and a source of state collision after 1952.

In 1952 the “Arnold story” (relationship with Arnold Murray) led to conviction for gross indecency. He accepted hormone treatment (oestrogen/organotherapy) as alternative to prison and may have given a promise not to repeat the offence. He told Don Bayley he could no longer do cryptanalytic work “because there was no room for homosexuals in that field.” He accepted it as fact. His brain, filled with knowledge of British cryptographic work, “mingling with street life” horrified the official/security mind. Holidays abroad (including the “Kjell crisis” of March 1953 and summer 1953 “act of defiance”) became security nightmares.

He continued scientific work to the end (morphogenesis with student Bernard Richards — no decline even at the very end; computer experiments on fir cones/daisies/Radiolaria; joint plans with Robin Gandy on theory of types; Penguin Science News article on the word problem early 1954; revived interest in theoretical physics/spinors/quantum foundations; “Messages from the Unseen World” postcards). In February 1954 he made a new will (Nick Furbank executor, mathematical books/papers to Robin Gandy, legacies including housekeeper, remainder divided among mother + friends). John Turing was “amazed and appalled.”

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession/Aftermath

Alan Turing died by suicide on Whit Monday, 7 June 1954 (exactly ten years after D-Day; the coldest and wettest Whit Monday for fifty years). He ate an apple filled with cyanide. The coroner referred to the “balance of his mind” and him becoming “unstable.” The symbolism of the apple “filled with the poison of the 1940s” was not perceived at the time. The verdict of suicide did not affect certain superannuation/estate payments.

He had called on the Greenbaum family two days before (they were out). In mid-May 1954 he visited a Blackpool fortune-teller (“Gypsy Queen”) with the family; he emerged “as white as a sheet” and would not speak on the return bus.

Body cremated 12 June 1954 at Woking Crematorium. Mother, brother, and Lyn Newman attended. Ashes dispersed in the gardens at the same place as his father’s.

Immediate scientific colleagues (Gandy, others) carried on work. Public/official recognition of Bletchley role was delayed for decades; the personal story remained private or stigmatised.

**Postscript framing (Whitman):** “As I lay with my head in your lap camerado... I know I am restless and make others so... For I confront peace, security, and all the settled laws, to unsettle them... Dear camerado! I confess I have urged you onward with me, and still urge you, without the least idea what is our destination, Or whether we shall be victorious, or utterly quell’d and defeated.”

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

- Theoretical: universal machine / computability as foundation of computer science.
- Practical/wartime: concrete contribution to breaking Enigma and related systems; model of organisation drawn from Bletchley experience applied to ACE.
- Post-war computing: ACE design principles (stored-program modification, instruction hierarchies, minimal hardware + programming); presence at the first working stored-program electronic computer (Manchester baby, 21 June 1948).
- Cultural/symbolic: emblem of the scientist in war, the birth of the digital age, the cost of secrecy, the persecution of homosexuals in the early Cold War security state, and the “imitation game”/Turing test as test of machine thought.
- Counterfactual: different timelines for WWII intelligence and the emergence of general-purpose computers; what further contributions to morphogenesis, theoretical physics, and machine intelligence he might have made had he lived.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

Hodges (mathematician with family access and long engagement, including the turing.org.uk site) combined technical accuracy on the 1936 paper, Enigma work, and early computers with deep personal and social context. The book integrates letters, papers, veteran accounts, and emerging declassifications. It is the standard scholarly biography; later cultural works (e.g. *The Imitation Game*) have popularised the story while taking liberties.

Range material highlights the collision between Turing’s life and the shift to explicit “positive vetting” (1952 onward), American/British policies treating homosexuals as security risks (blackmail susceptibility, emotional instability per 1950 Senate findings), the Montagu trials and “drive against male vice” (1953–54), parliamentary debates linking homosexuality to blackmail and the Atomic Energy Bill, and the mechanisation of trust/loyalty in the Cold War security state.

Primary sources drawn on: family papers, Gandy and other colleagues’ holdings, Bletchley veterans, Turing’s own writings and reports, trial/medical records, government files. The 2014 edition reflects post-film recognition (Obama 2011 mention) while underscoring secrecy in Turing’s lifetime.

Hodges frames the story as one of individual freedom of mind (including sexuality) colliding with the state it served, set against the timelessness of pure mathematics and the emergency of 1939. The book insists on both the universal (the machine) and the personal tragedy.
