A graduate-level analytic study of the formation, decisions, style, and legacy of Srinivasa Ramanujan (1887–1920), the self-taught Indian mathematician whose collaboration with G. H. Hardy at Cambridge during the First World War produced some of the most startling results in early twentieth-century number theory. This page draws exclusively on Kanigel (1991) via subagent extraction of the source text.

## 1. Formation and Early Influences

Ramanujan was born 22 December 1887 (ninth day of Margasirsha) in Erode and raised primarily in Kumbakonam on the Cauvery delta in Tanjore District. The constant auditory backdrop of his childhood was “the slow, measured thwap ... thwap ... thwap ... of wet clothes being pounded clean on rocks jutting up from the waters of the Cauvery River.” Kumbakonam, “Dakshin Gange,” was the heart of traditional South India — intensely Hindu, temple-saturated, spared the great invasions of the North, “more purely Hindu than any other part of India.”

His mother Komalatammal was the decisive human influence. Fiercely devout, she sang bhajans at the temple, held prayer meetings, pursued astrology and palmistry, and invoked the family deity, the goddess Namagiri of Namakkal, at every turn. She fed him, combed his hair into the traditional tuft, tied his dhoti, applied the Vaishnavite namam, walked him to school, monitored his friends, made his decisions, and later arranged his marriage — “all without bothering to consult her husband.” When Ramanujan was a year and a half, a brother died; a sister when he was four; another brother when he was six and a half. The deaths left him with “the solicitous regard and central position of an only child.”

Ramanujan was a sensitive, stubborn, eccentric child. As an infant he would eat only at the temple; later he rolled in the mud if denied what he wanted. For his first three years he scarcely spoke. At the ritual Akshara Abhyasam his hand, guided by his grandfather, traced Tamil characters in rice. On Vijayathasami 1892 he was enrolled in pial school but disliked the teacher and bristled at attending. “Even as a child, he was so self-directed that... unless he was ready to do something on his own, in his own time, he was scarcely capable of doing it at all.”

At Kangayan Primary School he passed first in the district in 1897. In 1898 he entered Town High School in Kumbakonam. By the second form classmates came to him for mathematics help. By the third he was challenging teachers: “But is zero divided by zero also one?” Around age eleven two college-student boarders fed his interest with Loney’s *Trigonometry*. By thirteen he had mastered it, could solve cubics, understood trigonometric functions as infinite series, and rattled off π and e to many decimals. At fourteen some classmates wrote him off as “off in the clouds”; teachers and students “rarely understood him” yet stood “in respectful awe.” He carried off merit certificates and volumes of English poetry as prizes. In 1904 the headmaster introduced him as a student who “deserved higher than the maximum possible marks.”

The Sarangapani Temple lay just up the street. Its twelve-story gopuram rose 146 feet; the outer hall was cool, shadowy, bat-haunted. Ramanujan came there for festivals and devotions and sometimes fell asleep on the pial with his notebook of scrawl under his arm. More than a dozen major temples made the town “fairly exude spirituality.” Every Sravana he walked four miles to Uppiliapan Koil to renew his sacred thread. At five he underwent upanayanam and received the three-strand thread of the twice-born.

In Ramanujan’s family the goddess Namagiri was paramount. His parents had prayed to her for a child after years of childlessness; his maternal grandmother entered trances to speak with her. Ramanujan grew up hearing that Namagiri would one day speak through his mother’s son. He would tell friends that the goddess wrote equations on his tongue and bestowed insights in dreams. “An equation for me has no meaning,” he later said, “unless it expresses a thought of God.”

South India’s relative isolation, the narrow orthodox Brahmin world of ritual, food taboos (strict vegetarianism from banana leaves and right hand only), caste marks, and temple rhythms, combined with his mother’s sanction and the accidental arrival of Carr’s *Synopsis*, created the precise petri dish in which his particular form of genius could grow unchecked by the normal curriculum.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

There was no conventional rise. Ramanujan entered Government College, Kumbakonam (“the Cambridge of South India”) in 1904 on scholarship. He became absorbed in mathematics to the exclusion of all else. Professor P. V. Seshu Iyer sometimes left him alone and judged one of his infinite-series efforts “ingenious and original.” He failed English composition; the scholarship was withdrawn.

In 1905, seventeen, he ran away toward Vizagapatnam; his parents brought him back. In 1906 he tried Pachaiyappa’s College in Madras; the math teacher showed his notebooks to the principal, who awarded a partial scholarship on the spot. He failed the F.A. again (physiology was the stated killer). By 1908 he was out of school, without degree or steady job, tutoring and “hanging around the house in Kumbakonam.”

His mother decided “Enough is enough” and arranged a marriage to nine-year-old Janaki of Rajendram (July 1909). Inauspicious signs marred the ceremony (screams of a retarded girl, fallen garland, fire). Janaki would not live with him until after puberty. In 1910 he underwent surgery for a hydrocele.

He began the long, humiliating search for patrons. He traveled with letters of introduction, notebooks in hand. V. Ramaswami Iyer (founder of the Indian Mathematical Society) received the “short uncouth figure, stout, unshaved, not overclean, with one conspicuous feature—shining eyes.” Ramachandra Rao was stunned: the theorems “transcended existing books.” He began a 25-rupee monthly stipend so Ramanujan could have “leisure.” In 1911 Ramanujan’s work began appearing in the Indian Mathematical Society’s journal (question 289; paper on Bernoulli numbers titled “Some Properties of Bernoulli’s Numbers”).

In March 1912 he became a Class III, Grade IV clerk in the accounts section of the Madras Port Trust at 30 rupees/month under Narayana Iyer, the highest-ranking Indian there and a mathematician himself. Evenings the two worked on the upstairs porch until midnight, slates on their knees, Narayana Iyer dazed by Ramanujan’s collapsing of steps: “You must descend to my level of understanding.”

Around the Port Trust it became known that Ramanujan was special. On 16 January 1913 he wrote to three Cambridge mathematicians. Baker and Hobson set the nine pages aside. G. H. Hardy read them, consulted Littlewood, and replied. “I have found in you a friend who views my labours sympathetically,” Ramanujan answered. The University of Madras awarded a 75-rupee research scholarship. In late 1913 Ramanujan and Narayana Iyer went to Namakkal. For three nights they slept on the temple grounds. On the third Ramanujan rose from a dream with the adesh (command) from Namagiri to go to England despite the ritual prohibition. His mother, who had seen a vision of him surrounded by Europeans with the goddess commanding her not to stand in his way, gave her blessing.

On 17 March 1914 the Nevasa sailed from Madras. Ramanujan arrived in England in April 1914.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**India: notebooks and the long search (c. 1903–1914).** Explosion of results after Carr; repeated exam failures; arranged marriage; door-to-door appeals; Port Trust clerkship; the 1913 letter.

**Cambridge Spring (1914–1916).** Arrival in wartime England; first meetings with Hardy and Littlewood; immediate productivity; flood of papers; B.A. by research (1916) on highly composite numbers.

**The English Chill and collaboration (1916–1918).** Isolation at High Table (strict vegetarianism), cultural reserve of the English, war-depopulated Cambridge; deepening partnership on partitions (circle method contribution), tau, zeta; illness begins; FRS and Trinity Fellowship 1918.

**Return, mock theta, and death (1919–1920).** Hero’s return; continued work (famous last letter on mock theta functions January 1920); rapid decline; death 26 April 1920 at age 32 in Madras.

## 5. Intellectual / Policy / Military / Religious Style and Methods

Ramanujan’s style was intuitive, synthetic, and devotional. He saw the form of a result and verified it numerically or by manipulation. He rarely supplied the Western apparatus of lemmas and epsilon-delta unless pressed. He described an equation as having meaning only if it expressed “a thought of God.”

Hardy’s complementary style — fastidious, aesthetic, proof-obsessed, “flint and stone” — supplied the missing rigor and editorial discipline that turned raw results into the twenty-one published papers. Hardy deliberately avoided “insisting unduly on matters which Ramanujan found irksome” for fear of breaking “the spell of his inspiration.”

Ramanujan was not interested in applications or in building systematic theories; he was a discoverer of identities. Many of his deepest results (mock theta functions, contributions to the circle method via Hardy, the tau conjecture) only revealed their full significance decades later. He learned what “proof” meant in the Western sense but remained at heart the man who had filled notebooks from Carr.

## 6. Character, Relationships, and Personal Life

Contemporaries described Ramanujan as simple, charming in his own register, narrow, stubborn, and supremely self-directed. He had almost no interest in politics, literature, or the war beyond its effect on his comfort and work. He was deeply attached to his mother and to Namagiri. The arranged marriage to Janaki was accepted; in England the threads of connection were severed and later strained by intercepted letters. In Cambridge he was lonely, cold, bewildered by social customs (the famous blankets lesson from Mahalanobis), and often withdrawn. Hardy was colleague, editor, advocate, and in a limited way friend; their relationship was conducted largely through mathematics. Ramanujan was capable of great persistence and of sudden withdrawals when he felt slighted. The 1918 suicide attempt and the nightmares of “singularities” of pain at x=1 reveal the psychological cost of the years of overwork and dislocation.

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Ramanujan died 26 April 1920 at “Gometra” in Chetput, Madras. Janaki sat with him in the final hours, feeding sips of dilute milk. He lapsed into unconsciousness and died at thirty-two. Most orthodox Brahmin relatives stayed away; he had crossed the waters and had not completed purification rites. 

In India there was national mourning and immediate memorial activity. In England Hardy began the lifelong work of editing, lecturing (Harvard 1936), and securing publication of the notebooks. Janaki lived on as a widow, supporting herself as a seamstress and later adopting a son; she became an important source for later biographers and lived into the 1990s.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Ramanujan’s name is attached to the partition function, the tau function, mock theta functions, the Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic, the circle method, and dozens of entries that still generate research. His story became the central modern Indian example of unrecognized genius and the obligations of educational and patronage systems. The question “How many Ramanujans are there?” remains a standing challenge. In mathematics departments he is the patron saint of the self-taught and the intuitive. Deligne proved the tau conjecture in 1974 using tools unavailable in Ramanujan’s lifetime; Andrews recovered the “lost notebook” in 1976. The 1987 centennial and subsequent editions and films fixed his place in both mathematical and popular culture.

## 9. Historiography and Primary Sources

**Primary sources** (verbatim in Kanigel): the notebooks and “lost notebook”; the 1913–1919 Hardy–Ramanujan letters (Trinity/CUL); the Family Record compiled by relatives; oral histories and interviews collected by Kanigel in South India and England (Janaki, Narayana Iyer, Seshu Iyer, Mahalanobis, etc.); Hardy’s 1937 Harvard lectures (*Ramanujan*) and *A Mathematician’s Apology*; contemporary Madras records and newspapers.

**Key modern works**: Kanigel 1991 (the indispensable narrative life); Hardy’s own writings (closest contemporary mathematical assessment); Bruce Berndt’s multi-volume editions of the notebooks (technical commentary).

**Major debates**: exact balance between intuition and any prior hidden formal knowledge; precise medical cause(s) of death (TB primary, possibly complicated by amoebiasis, vitamin D deficiency from vegetarianism + English climate, cultural stress); degree to which colonial structures and Brahmin orthodoxy both enabled and constrained him; whether Hardy “westernized” or saved the work or whether the notebooks would eventually have found recognition on their own.

**Methodological notes**: Kanigel excels at cultural and emotional texture and the India–England contrast. Pure mathematicians sometimes wish for more technical depth (now supplied by Berndt et al.). The biography is necessarily hybrid: mathematical results, colonial social history, devotional psychology, and individual character all intersect in one short, spectacular life.

## 2. Rise / Path to Power

There was no conventional “rise.” Ramanujan failed his examinations repeatedly because he would not study anything except mathematics. He married the child Janaki in 1909 in an arranged match. For years he lived in poverty, moving from house to house, showing his results to any mathematician who would look. Narayana Iyer at the Madras Port Trust became his most important early patron, giving him a clerkship and staying up nights trying to understand the collapsing of steps in Ramanujan’s proofs.

The decisive act was the 1913 letter to Hardy (after Baker and Hobson had ignored similar appeals). Hardy’s reply — skeptical at first, then electrified — began the correspondence that led to the offer of a research scholarship at the University of Madras and, ultimately, the voyage to England.

## 3. Major Phases of Career

**India, notebooks and patrons (1903–1914)**: Explosive production of results in isolation; repeated rebuffs from the examination system and most Indian mathematicians; the Port Trust years; the letter.

**Cambridge Spring (1914–1916)**: Arrival in wartime England; first meetings with Hardy and Littlewood; publication of early papers; election to B.A. by research; the beginning of the partition and tau work.

**The English Chill and collaboration (1916–1918)**: Deepening mathematical partnership amid cultural isolation, High Table vegetarianism, and the war; major theorems proved or conjectured; health begins to fail; FRS and Trinity Fellowship in 1918.

**Return and death (1919–1920)**: Hero’s welcome in Madras; rapid physical decline; continued mathematical work when able; death at 32.

## 7. Death, Immediate Succession / Aftermath

Ramanujan died 26 April 1920 in Madras. The immediate aftermath in India was national mourning and a burst of memorial activity. In England Hardy immediately began the work of editing, lecturing, and securing publication of the notebooks. The collaboration effectively ended with Ramanujan’s departure in 1919; Hardy’s later mathematical life was marked by the memory and by the duty of guardianship.

## 8. Long-term Legacy and Impact

Ramanujan’s name is now attached to the partition function, the tau function, mock theta functions, the Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic, and dozens of entries in modern handbooks and research papers. His story became the central modern Indian example of unrecognized genius and the obligations of patronage systems. In mathematics departments worldwide he is the patron saint of the self-taught and the intuitive. The question he forces on every society — “How many Ramanujans do we lose?” — remains live.
