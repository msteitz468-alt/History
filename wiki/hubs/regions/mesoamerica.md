### Historiography of the field (Adams Ch.1 — brief)

Americanist archaeology inherits anthropology’s premises: patterned, culturally determined
behavior with material correlates; cultural institutions (kinship, economy, politics, ideology,
warfare, settlement, technology, communication) analyzed in ecological context
(**cultural ecology**; processual “New Archaeology”). Adams rejects postprocessual nihilism for
field Mesoamerican work. Three long trends: growing realization of **time depth**; increasing
perception of **complexity**; since ~1960, more persuasive **explanatory secondary theory**—but
Adams insists **data methods** (stratigraphy from Gamio 1911; Type:variety ceramics; obsidian
sourcing; settlement-pattern studies from Willey) ultimately drive the field more than theory
fashion. Analogy debate: restrict ethnographic parallels to 16th-c. Mesoamerica only, or cast
nets wider.

## From CHNPA II.2 — Colonial and modern native Mesoamerica (MacLeod 2000)

### Fluid frontiers and Part 2 regional map

MacLeod keeps geographical definitions **deliberately fluid**. Classical Mesoamerican frontiers
were extended especially over what is today the **Mexican north**, because those areas
interacted with or felt influences from sedentary centers. The editor did not impose rigid
boundaries among essayists; overlaps and some neglected interstitial areas are acknowledged.

Post-conquest history is organized by regional chapters under shared colonial institutions
([[encomienda]], tribute, missions, cofradías, [[hacienda|haciendas]],
[[congregacion|congregación]]) and shared nineteenth–twentieth-century national pressures
(Liberal Reforma, coffee/henequen export regimes, Revolution, [[indigenismo]], late
neoliberalism):
