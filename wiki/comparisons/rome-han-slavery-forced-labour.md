# Slavery and Forced Labour in Rome and Han China Compared

Walter Scheidel's contribution to Kim et al. (2017, ch. 6) asks how differently the two
great ancient empires — the [[roman-empire|Roman Empire]] and Qin–[[han-dynasty|Han]] China
— mobilised **unfree labour**, and why. His answer: Rome was one of history's rare
**slave societies** (market chattel slavery), whereas Han China leaned on a vast **state
system of conscript and convict forced labour** — a divergence he reads as consistent with
Rome's higher level of economic development.

## The Divergence on One Axis

| | Roman Empire | Qin–Han China |
|---|---|---|
| Core unfree institution | Market **chattel slavery** | State **corvée + penal servitude** |
| Chief source of slaves | Mass enslavement of war captives (attested for centuries) | Kin of condemned criminals (the "only truly legitimate source") |
| War captives | Sold into the private market | Killed, made convict-labourers, or absorbed into the army — **not** mass-enslaved |
| Slave share of population | ~10% empire-wide (15–25% in Italy) | disputed; Scheidel argues low seven figures / several %, vs Wilbur's oft-cited ~1% |
| State labour | Little regularised civilian conscription; *opus publicum* mostly municipal | Universal ~1 month/year corvée; huge, mobile convict workforce |
| Incentives | *peculium*, motivational manumission, freedmen agents | little evidence of comparable incentive structures |
| Revolts | Slave revolts (Spartacus etc.) | No slave revolts reported; **convicts** rose in force instead |

## Rome: A Market Slave Society

Roman law split everyone into free and slave, with slave-like categories (arena fighters,
*servi poenae*). Mass enslavement of war captives supplied a private market; slave prices
(~4 tons of wheat equivalent) were comparable to Chinese ones. Roman elites, who
"effectively owned the state," deployed slaves in Mediterranean cash-crop agriculture
(viticulture, *pastio villatica*) and household/commercial roles, supported by *peculium*
incentives, manumission, and freedmen acting as agents. Roman penal labour (*opus
publicum*; convictions *ad metallum*) existed but was, in Scheidel's phrase, "only a pale
shadow of the much more extensive gulag-like Chinese system."

## Han China: A Forced-Labour State

In early China the fundamental split was "good" (free commoners) versus "base." War
captives were converted into convicts or conscripts rather than sold, so relatively few
reached a private market. Most adult civilians owed **corvée** (~1 month/year); **penal
servitude** (commonly 1–5 years) furnished a huge, fungible workforce that "built palaces,
dug tombs, shored up dikes... mined copper and iron ore, cast iron tools, minted coins,
boiled brine into salt" (Barbieri-Low) — 700,000 convicts and slaves reportedly built the
First Emperor's palace; Han tomb-cemeteries hold convict labourers buried in iron collars
and leg fetters. Archaeology (the Fenghuangshan tombs, where a minor official owned 40–50
slaves, 25–43% in farming) leads Scheidel to reject Martin Wilbur's ~1% slave-share figure
as too low.

## Scheidel's Explanation

Rome's heavier slave reliance follows from (a) captives reaching the **private market**
rather than state service, (b) elites who "owned the state" versus more state-constrained
Chinese wealth-holders, and (c) Mediterranean cash-crop agriculture and high commercial
connectivity absent in continental China. Chattel slavery, he suggests, let a society draw
in "outside" labour without disturbing internal arrangements; Han China achieved a similar
effect internally, by turning condemned criminals into "outsider" convicts and mobilising
duty-based conscription. The whole pattern, he concludes, aligns with **Ian Morris's**
finding that Chinese social development did not reach Roman levels until the Song, "almost
1,000 years later" — offered as an interpretive thesis, not a settled verdict.
