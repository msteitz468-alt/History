# Unfree Labor Systems Compared

"Unfree labor" names wildly different institutions. This page compares them on one structural axis that explains much of the variation: **what was the binding relation — debt, capture, birth, or service — and was the society's scarce, power-conferring resource *people* or *land*?** Anthony Reid's insight about maritime Southeast Asia — that where land was abundant and labor scarce, **control of people, not territory, was the basis of power** — is the hinge of the comparison.

## Five Forms on One Axis

| System | Binding relation | Scarce resource controlled | Heritable? | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SE Asian debt-bondage** | Debt to a master/patron | People (labor scarce) | Sometimes | Bondage as clientage; absorbable into the household |
| **Atlantic chattel slavery** | Capture + purchase; property | Capital/plantation output | Yes (matrilineal status) | Total commodification of the person |
| **European serfdom** | Birth + attachment to the land | Land (labor tied to estate) | Yes | Unfreedom *of place*, not ownership of person |
| **Islamic military slavery** | Purchase/levy of youths for service | Loyal manpower for the state | No (often manumitted) | Elite, administrative, upwardly mobile |
| **Andean *mita*** | Rotational obligation to the state | Labor service (reciprocity) | No (rotational) | Corvée framed as reciprocal duty |

## The Comparison

### Where people are scarce: debt-bondage (Southeast Asia)
In land-abundant maritime [[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce|Southeast Asia]], wealth and power meant **commanding people**. [[slavery|Bondage]] was overwhelmingly *debt-based*: a person pledged their labor (or was pledged) against a debt, becoming a dependent within a patron's household, often able to marry in and rise. It shaded into clientage rather than chattel ownership — unfreedom as a relation of dependence, not a thing owned. (Reid, *Age of Commerce*.)

### Where labor is capital: chattel slavery (Atlantic)
The [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic plantation system]] represents the opposite pole: the enslaved person was **legal property**, racialized and heritable, abstracted from kin and place to maximize extractable plantation output. Here the scarce resource was capital and tropical staple production, and labor was commodified to serve it — the most total form of the person's reduction to a thing.

### Where land is the prize: serfdom (Europe)
European **serfdom** bound labor not by ownership of the person but by attachment to the **land**: the serf was unfree *in place*, owing dues and labor to the lord of an estate they could not leave. Where (as in early-modern Eastern Europe) land was being commercialized for export grain, lords intensified this bondage — the "second serfdom." Unfreedom here is a property relation in *land* that captures the people on it.

### Where the state wants loyalty: military slavery (Islam)
[[military-slavery-islamic|Islamic military slavery]] (mamluks, ghilman, later the janissaries) inverts the usual hierarchy of unfreedom: youths were bought or levied, converted, and trained as an **elite** corps loyal to the ruler precisely *because* they were severed from local kin networks. Frequently manumitted, sometimes ruling (the Mamluk sultanate), it was unfreedom as a path to power — the scarce resource being trustworthy manpower for the state.

### Where the state wants service: the mita (Andes)
The Andean **[[mita-and-andean-reciprocity|mita]]** was rotational labor owed to the state, rooted in pre-Columbian traditions of [[encomienda|reciprocity]] and brutally repurposed by the Spanish for the Potosí mines. Not ownership and not lifelong, it was *corvée* — obligatory service framed (originally) as reciprocal duty to a redistributive state.

## Assessment

The forms array cleanly once you ask *what is scarce*. Where **people** are the scarce resource (Southeast Asia, the service-states of Islam), unfreedom takes the form of bonds *over persons* — debt clientage, military dependence — often porous and even upwardly mobile. Where **land or capital** is the prize (European estates, Atlantic plantations), unfreedom attaches people *to* land or *commodifies* them outright, and tends to be heritable and rigid. The mita sits apart as rotational state service. The comparison cautions against a single category of "slavery": the chattel-property model of the Atlantic was historically extreme, not the norm, and most unfree labor was a relation of dependence or obligation rather than ownership.
