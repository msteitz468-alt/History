# Frontier Societies and Responses to Core Expansion

The model established in the comparison of the Celtic lands under English pressure can be extended across multiple frontiers. In each case a consolidating core exerted sustained pressure on a peripheral society or set of societies; the outcomes were shaped by geography, social structure, political will, and chance, producing conquest and incorporation in some cases, successful assertion of independence or autonomy in others, and enduring division or hybrid societies in still others.

Drawn from NCMH volumes (especially the frontier chapters in V, VI and VII), CWH material on Chinese and Russian expansion, and the wiki's existing process and actor pages on the Reconquista, Teutonic Order, and steppe interactions.

## The Shared Structural Situation

In each instance the peripheral zone exhibited internal dualism (more "core-like" or colonised south/east vs. more indigenous or resistant north/west or steppe), and faced simultaneous tasks of negotiating with an expansionary neighbour while managing its own internal divisions. The same external pressure produced radically different results depending on inheritance practices, aristocratic cohesion, geography, and the accidents of succession or leadership.

## Cases

### British Isles (extension of the Celtic comparison)
Already treated in detail: Wales saw conquest and incorporation under Edward I, secured by castles and partible inheritance that allowed English intervention. Scotland asserted independence through developed institutions and native aristocratic loyalty (Bannockburn, Declaration of Arbroath). Ireland settled into enduring division between English Pale and Gaelic/Gaelicised lordships, with delegated rule to great earls.

### Iberian Reconquista
The Christian kingdoms of northern Iberia exerted pressure on al-Andalus. The fragmentation of the Caliphate into taifas enabled the parias tribute system that funded Christian advances; the fall of Toledo (1085) precipitated Almoravid and Almohad interventions that temporarily reversed gains. Las Navas de Tolosa (1212) broke the last major Muslim counterweight. Outcomes: rapid incorporation in the south (Córdoba 1236, Seville 1248) but the survival of Granada as a tributary until 1492. The result was a distinctive frontier society of repoblación, mudéjares, and convivencia that persisted into the late period (see NCMH V and VII). The Reconquista was assimilated to the crusade but remained a peninsular process with its own rhythms.

### Baltic Frontier (Teutonic Order)
The Teutonic Knights, invited to defend against pagan Prussians, built a theocratic conquest-state using papal and imperial privileges. Winter campaigns, fortresses, and Ostsiedlung (German settlement on favourable terms) produced incorporation of Prussia. The reysa against Lithuania continued the pressure. Outcome: successful incorporation of the Prussian periphery into a monastic state, but the Order's raison d'être collapsed when Lithuania converted (1385/6) and allied with Poland, leading to defeat at Tannenberg (1410) and eventual secularisation in 1525. The Baltic case produced a durable (if ultimately transformed) colonial structure rather than either full independence or simple division.

### Chinese Southern and Steppe Frontiers
Chinese cores repeatedly expanded into southern "barbarian" zones and faced steppe nomads. Southern frontiers (e.g., under Tang-Song and later dynasties) often saw gradual incorporation through settlement, sinicisation, and administrative extension, producing hybrid societies. The steppe frontier was more cyclical: tribute, walls, or conquest alternated with nomadic pressure. Unlike the Celtic or Baltic cases, the Chinese core possessed overwhelming demographic and bureaucratic resources, so outcomes skewed toward incorporation or managed coexistence rather than peripheral independence (with notable exceptions such as Vietnam). The model of partible inheritance weakening the periphery applied in reverse: Chinese partible systems sometimes aided central intervention.

### Russian Steppe and Eastern Frontiers
The rise of Moscow involved expansion into steppe and forest zones previously under Mongol (Golden Horde) suzerainty. Outcomes varied: incorporation of Slavic principalities, co-optation or subjugation of steppe peoples, and the creation of a hybrid imperial structure. The "gathering of the lands" produced a core that absorbed rather than was absorbed by the frontier, with service nobility and later serfdom as instruments of control. Divergence from the Baltic case is striking: the Russian core eventually reversed the steppe dynamic.

## Significance

The same logic of external pressure plus internal variation produces conquest, independence, or partition depending on local conditions. The Celtic page's insight—that the thirteenth-century English state acted as an expansionary frontier power—generalises. Consolidating cores (Latin Christendom's monarchies, the Chinese bureaucratic state, Muscovy) interacted with peripheral societies in ways that reveal the contingency of empire. These cases complement the broader theme of frontier societies (Iberia, Baltic, Russian steppe, Chinese marches, and later the Americas) and show why some peripheries were absorbed while others retained or asserted distinct identities.

The comparison also highlights the role of inheritance, aristocratic cohesion, and religious/ideological framing in determining outcomes.
