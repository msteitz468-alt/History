# Legal Legitimation Styles Compared

This page asks a narrower question than the wiki's other legal comparisons: **on what authority does law's bindingness ultimately rest, and what does that source of legitimacy do to the law's capacity to change, to be codified, and to relate to the state?** It builds on — rather than repeats — the moralism-versus-formalism contrast already drawn in [[islamdom-and-occident-high-medieval|Islamdom and the Occident]] and the law-and-community axis in [[religious-authority-and-institutions-across-major-traditions|Religious Authority Across Traditions]], extending the question to the *grounds* of legitimacy and adding the Chinese case.

## Three Grounds of Legal Authority

### Jurists' law grounded in revelation — Islamic shari'a
[[sharia|Shari'a]] is grounded in revelation (Qur'an and Sunna) but elaborated by **jurists** ('ulama) through interpretation (ijtihad, qiyas, consensus). Its legitimacy is therefore simultaneously divine *and* scholarly: the law is God's, but its content is what qualified jurists derive, and the [[caliphate-legitimation-middle-periods|ruler does not make it]]. This produced an enormously sophisticated, mobile, trans-territorial jurists' law (see the wiki's large fiqh cluster — [[law-and-politics-formative|formative law and politics]], the schools, [[codification-of-islamic-law|the late codification debate]]) but one held, in Hodgson's reading, to be "relatively rigid and incapable of adaptation" because no sovereign could simply legislate it. The [[ottoman-law-kanun|Ottoman kanun]] grew up *alongside* shari'a precisely to supply the ruler-made, situational law that shari'a's grounding excluded.

### Jurists' law grounded in reason and precedent — Roman and canon law
[[roman-private-law|Roman law]] rests its authority not on revelation but on **juristic reason and the accumulated authority of precedent and the texts** (later, the rediscovered Corpus Juris), with canon law adding the Church's institutional authority. Legitimacy is formal and corporate: rights flow from charters, statuses, and precedents specific to corporate bodies, and legal fictions (the corporation as legal person) flourish. This is the "formalistic" pole of the Islamdom comparison — but the deeper point here is its *ground*: because authority lay in human reason and revisable precedent rather than fixed revelation, the tradition proved adaptable, codifiable by sovereigns, and assimilable to the [[natural-law|natural-law]] and later statutory traditions of the European state.

### Sovereign command under a cosmic mandate — Chinese imperial law
Chinese imperial law (*fa*) was, by contrast, primarily **the command of the sovereign**, legitimated from above by the [[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate of Heaven]] and tempered by Confucian *li* (ritual propriety). Law was an instrument of the state's moral order rather than an autonomous jurists' science: codes were promulgated and revised by dynasties, administered by magistrate-officials, and answerable to the ruler's cosmic responsibility rather than to an independent legal profession. Its legitimacy was thus neither revealed-and-juristic nor reason-and-precedent but **mandated-and-administrative**.

## What the Ground of Legitimacy Produced

| | Islamic shari'a | Roman/canon law | Chinese imperial law |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ultimate ground | Revelation, via jurists | Juristic reason + precedent | Sovereign command under Heaven's mandate |
| Who makes the law | Scholars (not the ruler) | Jurists, sovereigns, the Church | The dynasty/state |
| Capacity to change | Constrained; kanun added beside it | High; revisable, codifiable | High, but top-down |
| Relation to the state | Autonomous of it | Interpenetrating | An organ of it |

The axis explains divergent later trajectories. A law grounded in revelation-via-jurists resisted sovereign codification, so modernising states had to either bypass it (kanun) or force [[islamic-law-and-modernity|the wrenching codifications of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries]]. A law grounded in reason and precedent flowed naturally into sovereign codes and the nation-state. A law that was already sovereign command codified easily but never generated an autonomous legal profession to check the state.

## Assessment

The moralistic/formalistic contrast (treated in detail on the [[islamdom-and-occident-high-medieval|Islamdom–Occident]] page) is a *symptom* of a deeper difference in where each tradition located legal authority. Grounding law in revelation produced jurists independent of rulers but a law hard to change; grounding it in reason and precedent produced an adaptable, codifiable, state-assimilable law; grounding it in sovereign mandate produced efficient codes without an autonomous bar. None is simply "more legal" than the others — but the *ground* of legitimacy, more than any surface feature, shaped each civilisation's path into legal modernity.
