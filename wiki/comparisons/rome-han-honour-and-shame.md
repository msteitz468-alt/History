# Honour and Shame in Rome and Han China Compared

Two chapters of Kim et al. (2017) treat honour and shame as a **socio-institutional
technology** — a system for regulating behaviour — and place the [[roman-republic|Roman
Republic]] (Frederik Vervaet, ch. 4) beside [[han-dynasty|Han China]] (Mark Lewis, ch. 5).
The comparison turns on **where each society lodged honour**: in a competitive aristocratic
and military caste, or in a bureaucratic-moral order fused to the person of the ruler.

## The Two Systems on One Axis

| | Roman Republic | Han China |
|---|---|---|
| Primary locus | Competing aristocratic *gentes*; the offices literally called *honores* | Officialdom whose honour is tied to the ruler's; the moral-scholarly self |
| Family register | *patria potestas* — the father's near-absolute power (incl. life and death) | Filial duty (*si*, private) subordinated in office to public duty (*gong*) to the ruler |
| Military register | An honour-bound soldiery; *spolia*, crowns, decimation, barley-rations for cowards | Mass peasant levies controlled by graded **reward and punishment**, not caste honour |
| Elite death | Suicide/single combat to win or preserve glory | Permitted **suicide in lieu of public execution** to preserve court honour |
| Relation to ruler | Aristocrats competed *against* one another for finite *gloria* | Officials defined as the emperor's "servants/slaves"; surname surrendered in office |

## Rome: Honour as Aristocratic Competition

Vervaet treats the Republic as "a plutocratic outfit" in which esteem (*honos*, *dignitas*)
was "a public verdict" (Lendon) granted or withdrawn by the peer community, and its loss
(*infamia*) was catastrophic. Honour operated at three linked levels — family (the
Lucretia and Verginia legends; historical honour-killings such as Aulus Fulvius executing
his Catilinarian son in 63 BCE), Senate (rank/seniority contests, the *gradatim* speaking
order), and army (Polybius 6 on battlefield rewards and the fear of disgrace). It **drove
outcomes**: Marius converted his men's shame into fighting anger before Aquae Sextiae
(102 BCE); the Arausio disaster (105 BCE) followed Caepio's refusal to obey a "new man"
consul; Pompey's senatorial peers shamed him into giving battle at Pharsalia. The
Fabius–Marcellus rivalry and the 29 BCE **Spolia Opima** affair (Octavian blocking
Crassus's claim) show honour-competition escalating into constitutional consequence — the
public triumph became an imperial monopoly precisely to deny the aristocracy its highest
honour.

## Han: Honour as Bureaucratic-Moral Order

Lewis traces a Chinese arc from a Spring-and-Autumn warrior ethic, through the Confucian/
Mencian redefinition of shame as tied to **moral and intellectual virtue** rather than
combat or wealth, to a Han bureaucratic system in which (per Jia Yi) the ruler's honour and
his officials' honour were bound together — hence the "privilege" of suicide over
mutilating public punishment. Officials surrendered their surname and were rhetorically the
emperor's "slaves"; public-minded duty (*gong*) was set explicitly above private familial
interest (*si*). A parallel **vengeance ethic** (kin/lord revenge, sometimes protected by
officials) persisted, culminating in the Eastern Han "partisan clique" self-sacrifices of
the 160s CE, framed as the height of the scholar's moral integrity.

## The Contrast That Matters

Lewis states the key divergence directly: "Unlike in Rome, where the army... emerged as a
group apart that preserved the military focus of honour, Warring States armies were based on
mass peasant levies" run by reward and punishment. Rome kept honour in a **caste**; Han
diffused it into a **moral-bureaucratic order** answerable to the throne — a difference that
maps onto the two empires' whole political architectures.
