# Managing Religious Pluralism Compared (Dhimma, Millet, Convivencia)

Premodern states almost never aimed at equality among faiths; the live question was *how a dominant religion incorporated subordinate ones*. This page compares the legal-institutional arrangements by which that was done, and the structural trade-off each embodied: **toleration purchased through legalised subordination, versus assimilation, expulsion, or open conflict.** It is distinct from the [[plural-society]] concept, which is J. S. Furnivall's *colonial* category (markets without a shared social will); here the comparison is the *premodern, legally structured* management of confessional difference.

## The Arrangements

| Arrangement | Legal basis | Status of minorities | Tendency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Islamic *dhimma*** | Qur'anic "People of the Book"; the pact of protection | Protected, tax-paying (*jizya*) subordinates with communal self-rule | Durable subordinated toleration |
| **Ottoman *millet*** | Imperial recognition of confessional communities | Self-governing religious "nations" under their own clergy/law | Institutionalised, scalable pluralism |
| **Iberian *convivencia*** | De facto coexistence under Christian (and earlier Muslim) rule | Tolerated then increasingly pressured *mudéjares* and Jews | Coexistence collapsing into expulsion |
| **Latin Christendom (general)** | Canon law; ideal of a single *corpus christianum* | Jews as a tolerated-but-precarious exception; heresy policed | Low tolerance; assimilation or expulsion |

## The Comparison

### The dhimma — toleration as legal subordination
Classical Islamic law incorporated Jews and Christians as **[[dhimmi|dhimmis]]**: protected, internally self-governing communities that paid the *jizya* and accepted defined social subordination. This produced a genuinely durable pluralism — far more so than contemporary Latin Europe — but it was *hierarchical* toleration, not parity. It is the institutional backdrop to the [[caliphate-legitimation-middle-periods|caliphal order]] and the legal autonomy traced in [[religious-authority-and-institutions-across-major-traditions|Religious Authority Across Traditions]].

### The millet — pluralism institutionalised
The Ottomans scaled the dhimma logic into the **[[millet-system|millet system]]**: recognised confessional communities (Orthodox, Armenian, Jewish) governed their own family law, education, and courts under their own religious leaders, answerable to the sultan. This was the most *administratively elaborated* form of managed pluralism — pluralism as a stable structural feature of a multi-confessional empire, not a grudging exception.

### Convivencia — coexistence and its collapse
Iberian **[[convivencia|convivencia]]** is the case where managed pluralism *failed*. Under both Muslim and then Christian rule, Muslims, Christians, and Jews long coexisted productively. But as the [[reconquista|Reconquista]] advanced, toleration of *mudéjares* and Jews narrowed into pressure, forced conversion, and finally the expulsions of 1492 and the Moriscos after. Convivencia shows that legalised coexistence was reversible — and that a society organised around religious *unity* (the Latin-Christian ideal) tended to resolve plurality by elimination rather than incorporation.

### Latin Christendom — the low-tolerance baseline
By contrast the western Christian norm was a single *corpus christianum*: Jews were a precarious tolerated exception and heresy was policed, with assimilation or expulsion the usual end-states. The comparison makes clear that the Islamic world's subordinated-but-durable toleration was, for most of the medieval period, structurally *more* accommodating of religious difference than Latin Europe's.

## Assessment

Ranked by the durability of managed pluralism, the order runs roughly millet > dhimma > convivencia > Latin Christendom. The decisive variable is **whether the dominant order had a legal category for the permanent incorporation of religious others** (dhimma, millet did; the *corpus christianum* did not). Where it existed, plurality was stable but subordinated; where it did not, plurality was resolved by conversion or expulsion. None was tolerance in the modern liberal sense — all rested on a settled hierarchy — but the presence or absence of an incorporating legal form, more than any general "spirit of tolerance," determined whether plural societies endured.
