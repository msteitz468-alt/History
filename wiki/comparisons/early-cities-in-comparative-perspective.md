# Early Cities in Comparative Perspective

A deliberate experiment in cross-cultural comparison of early urbanism, drawn from [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|Norman Yoffee (ed.), *The Cambridge World History, Volume III: Early Cities in Comparative Perspective, 4000 BCE–1200 CE*]] (2015). Rather than a regional or chronological survey, the volume isolates six *features* of early urban life and tests each against three or four city case studies drawn from across the world and across four and a half millennia. Its provocative juxtapositions (Rome with Tenochtitlan, Athens with Jenne-jeno, Jerusalem with Cahokia) are the point: early cities *can* be compared without either essentializing "the city" or denying each city its distinct evolutionary trajectory.

## The Comparative Experiment

The editor's premise (Ch. 1) is that early cities should be compared following V. Gordon Childe's "urban revolution" (1950) but moving beyond trait-lists toward questions of performance, information, landscape, power, creation, and empire. Each of the six thematic parts presents specialist city case studies followed by a co-authored synthesis chapter. The volume is the direct successor to the agriculture-and-urbanism thread and the foundation for much of the wiki's places/ directory and the [[urbanism]] / [[urbanization]] concepts.

## Cities as Arenas of Performance

Early cities were stages for ritual, procession, and the enactment of cosmic order. Egyptian cities (Baines) emphasized monumental display and divine kingship. Classic Maya centers (Houston & Garrison) were "dedicated cities" built around cosmograms and elite performance. SE Asian urbanism at Angkor Borei (Stark) and related sites used ritual processions and water-management spectacles. Synthesis: performance was not secondary to "function" — it *was* the urban function for many early polities.

## Information Technologies

Writing and record-keeping originated primarily as tools of economic administration and legibility, not literature or religion. [[uruk|Uruk]]'s proto-cuneiform grew out of tokens, seals, and ration bowls. Early Chinese writing at [[anyang|Anyang]] is shot through with bookkeeping even in oracle-bone contexts. The Inka **khipu** demonstrates that a complex empire could be administered without phonetic writing at all. This is the volume's strongest cross-cultural claim.

## Early Urban Landscapes

Urbanization was always also ruralization. The Mesopotamian urban process (Emberling) involved central-place hinterlands and ruralization. [[tiwanaku|Tiwanaku]]'s qochas and raised fields integrated city and countryside. [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] had planned neighborhoods (Axotlan) and a dispersed urban fabric. The urban–rural dichotomy dissolves in these cases.

## Distribution of Power

Large, dense, complex cities did not all concentrate power in a single despotic ruler. [[heterarchy]] is the key analytical advance. [[jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]] and Teotihuacan show heterarchical organization alongside (or instead of) hierarchy. Harappan cities (Sinopoli), Greek poleis (Morris & Knodell), and West African urbanism (R. McIntosh) further complicate top-down models inherited from Childe and the "oriental city." This overturns assumptions built into the [[urban-revolution]] concept and supplies the [[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]] and [[what-defines-a-city]] controversies.

## Cities as Creations

Many early cities were consciously founded or imagined as cosmic or political statements. [[baghdad|Baghdad]] (762) was planned as a round city at the center of the Islamic world. Jerusalem's layered sacred geography (Killebrew) was repeatedly re-created. [[cahokia|Cahokia]] was a founded and re-imagined center in the Mississippi valley (Pauketat et al.). The [[founded-cities]] concept captures this intentionality.

## Early Imperial Cities

Imperial capitals concentrated power, people, and problems. Neo-Assyrian capitals (Otto) used forced migration and monumental display. Mexico-Tenochtitlan (Gutiérrez) was the core of a tribute empire with its own environmental and demographic stresses. The rise of [[rome|Rome]] (Terrenato) shows the transformation from city-state to imperial city. Imperial cities amplified both the achievements and the fragilities of earlier urban forms.

## Key Cross-Cultural Insights

- **Information technology** as administration (not literature) recurs across Mesopotamia, China, and the Andes.
- **Heterarchy** offers a robust alternative model to Childean despotism, especially visible in Jenne-jeno and Teotihuacan.
- **Ruralization** is the necessary counterpart of urbanization; cities never stand alone.
- **Performance and creation** were primary urban functions in many cases, not mere epiphenomena.
- **Imperial cities** scaled up earlier experiments while introducing new vulnerabilities (disease, migration, environmental overreach).

## Assessment

Yoffee's wager succeeds: early cities are comparable at the level of *features* without forcing a single evolutionary sequence or denying local trajectories. The volume supplies the wiki with the structural vocabulary (heterarchy, performance, legibility, founded cities, ruralization) that lets individual place pages connect into a larger conversation. Its limits are those of the archaeological record — evidence is strongest for literate or monument-rich societies — but the comparative method itself is portable.
