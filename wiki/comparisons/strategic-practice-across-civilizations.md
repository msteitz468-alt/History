# Strategic Practice Across Civilizations

A comparative reading of how **strategy was practised** — political ends linked to military
and non-military means — across the world's major civilizations and empires, drawn from
[[cambridge-history-of-strategy-v1|Duyvesteyn & Heuser's *Cambridge History of Strategy*]]
(2024). The series' central question is whether strategic practice exhibits **universal
features**, as strategic studies has long assumed, or is largely **culture-specific**. The
answer is **mixed**: certain patterns recur with striking regularity across unconnected
societies, while much else is contingent on culture, technology, and circumstance. See
[[strategy]] for the underlying concept.

## Recurring (near-universal) features

- **The aspiration to universal monarchy / world dominion.** Independently, many of the
  more powerful civilizations developed a claim to rule "the known world," casting whatever
  lay outside as chaos inviting pacification: **Assyria**, **Achaemenid** and **Sassanian**
  Persia (the *Shahanshah*, "King of Kings"), **Macedon** under Alexander, **Rome** (most
  explicitly, from the 2nd c. BCE through Augustus and Virgil), **Byzantium** (*Basileus
  Basileōn*), the **Caliphate** (one worldwide Islamic polity), the **Mughals**, the **Indic
  polities** of South and Southeast Asia, the **Mongols** ("one empire under the Blue
  Heaven" — the basis of the largest land empire in history), and **China**. The editors note
  how closely this resembles the world-conquering *vijigishu* ideal in
  [[raja-mandala|Kautilya's]] Indian thought. Every such universal claim "raised hackles" and
  provoked counter-balancing.
- **Peacetime deterrence by display.** Armed forces served to deter in peace, not only to
  fight in war — through parades and displays of power. The Ming and Qing prized inspiring
  **"awe"** (Ch. 20); Frederick II of Prussia staged impressive reviews to deter; the same
  logic animates any military parade. A genuinely cross-cultural constant.
- **Dynastic marriage as a tool of alliance.** Cementing alliances and confirming peace
  through marriage was used not only by the **Habsburgs** (the paradigm) but by the ancient
  **Chinese**, the **Macedonians**, **Rome** under the Principate and Dominate, **Byzantium**,
  the **Guptas**, and all later European dynasties.
- **The wider toolkit.** Economic leverage (tribute, subsidies, trade denial), propaganda
  and triumphal narrative, and negotiated surrender of territory recur everywhere as
  instruments of strategy alongside the army.

## Culture-specific variation

Against these constants, the volumes stress **diversity**: configurations of force (cavalry
vs. infantry vs. navy) shaped by ecology and economy; distinctive "ways of war" tied to
[[pastoral-nomadism|nomadic]], agrarian, or maritime bases (e.g. the
[[mongol-army|Mongol]] decimal horse-army vs. the Roman legion vs. Ottoman gunpowder
forces); and divergent relationships between the warrior and the state. Clausewitz's dictum
that "every age has its own kind of war" holds across space as well as time.

## The deflationary verdict

The comparison also undercuts the **stylised, rationalist picture** of strategy. Across
periods and cultures, practice was "messy, frustrating and often guided by luck"; objectives
were unstable and ends–means alignment frequently faulty; "muddling through and ad hockery"
describes much of it better than far-sighted planning. The recurring *aspirations* (universal
monarchy, deterrence, alliance) were thus pursued through a practice far more improvised than
either the participants' rhetoric or modern theory suggests.

## Assessment

The Cambridge History's contribution is to put **non-Western strategic practice on the same
analytic footing as the Western canon** and to show both real cross-cultural convergence and
deep variation — qualifying strategic studies' assumption of a single "unity of strategic
calculations" without abandoning the idea that deliberate ends–means reasoning is a human
universal. The evidence is uneven (skewed toward literate, record-keeping states), so the
"universal" patterns are best read as **widespread among complex polities** rather than
strictly universal.
