# Polycentric World-Systems and Trade Networks Compared

Long-distance trade systems are not all built the same way. This page compares three historically connected ones along a single structural axis: **whether the system had a dominant core that organised and coerced exchange, or whether it was polycentric — multiple cores, no hegemon, integration without command.** The contrast reframes the "rise of the West" not as the arrival of trade but as a *change in the structure* of trade.

The three cases are the thirteenth-century Afro-Eurasian world system mapped by [[abu-lughod-before-european-hegemony-1989|Janet Abu-Lughod]], the [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean world]] as treated by [[hall-empires-of-the-monsoon-1996|Hall]] and [[alpers-indian-ocean-in-world-history-2014|Alpers]], and the [[atlantic-world|Atlantic system]] that formed after 1500. They are linked in time: the second underlies the first, and the third partly displaced both.

## The Three Systems

### The thirteenth-century Afro-Eurasian system (Abu-Lughod)
Abu-Lughod's central claim is that a sophisticated world economy operated c. 1250–1350 — under the [[mongol-empire|Mongol]] peace and monsoon shipping — that was **polycentric and non-hegemonic**: eight overlapping circuits grouped into three subsystems (European, Middle Eastern, Asian), with multiple commercial "cores" (cities and regions, not states) and *no single dominant core or periphery*. It integrated Italian city-states, [[afro-eurasian-world-system|Mamluk Egypt]], the Indian Ocean ports, and Yuan China. Its restructuring after the [[black-death|Black Death]] and Mongol fragmentation, not any European superiority, opened the way for later divergence. The system "failed to hegemonize," and that was its defining feature.

### The Indian Ocean world
The Indian Ocean was the archetypal polycentric maritime zone: a segmented, cosmopolitan network of ports ([[melaka|Melaka]], Calicut, Aden, the Swahili coast) tied together by the monsoon and by diasporic merchant communities (Gujarati, Arab, Persian, Chinese), operating largely **without armed coercion of trade** before 1500. No state controlled the sea; rulers taxed and hosted commerce rather than monopolising it. The Portuguese intrusion after 1498 introduced, for the first time, the attempt to *control* the ocean by force (the *cartaz* pass system and fortified factories) — a structural mutation rather than a mere new participant.

### The post-1500 Atlantic system
The [[atlantic-world|Atlantic system]] was organised on the opposite principle: a **single, European, coercive core**. It was built on conquest, the [[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]], plantation slavery, and chartered monopoly companies, with the Americas and West Africa structured as peripheries supplying bullion, sugar, and labour to European cores. Here the directional, hierarchical "modern world-system" associated with Wallerstein actually applies — which is exactly Abu-Lughod's point: it was a later and *different* kind of system, not the natural maturation of the polycentric one.

## The Axis: Hegemony and Mode of Integration

| Feature | 13th-c. Afro-Eurasian | Indian Ocean (pre-1500) | Atlantic (post-1500) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cores | Many; no hegemon | Many ports; no hegemon | One (Europe) |
| Integration | Commercial, reciprocal | Diasporic, low-coercion | Conquest + monopoly |
| Coercion of trade | Low | Low (until Portuguese) | High (built in) |
| Periphery | No fixed periphery | None | Americas, West Africa |
| Labour | Local/customary | Local + some slavery | Plantation slavery at scale |

## Significance

Read together, the three cases dissolve the assumption that intensive long-distance trade *is* European-style capitalism in embryo. Two of the three were polycentric systems that integrated vast regions **without a hegemon and without coercing exchange**; the European Atlantic system was historically unusual precisely in centralising and militarising trade. This supports Abu-Lughod's reframing of the [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]]: what changed after 1500 was less the *existence* of a world economy (it long predated Europe, along the [[silk-roads|silk roads]] and monsoon routes) than its *structure* — from many cores to one, from accommodation to coercion. The comparison is the structural counterpart to the [[commercial-revolutions-compared|commercial-revolutions comparison]], which looks at the booms; this one looks at how the networks linking them were organised.
