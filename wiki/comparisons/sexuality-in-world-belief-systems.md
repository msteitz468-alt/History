# Sexuality in World Belief Systems

A comparative survey of how the world's major **intellectual and religious traditions**
conceived, valued, and regulated sexuality, drawn from
[[cambridge-world-history-of-sexualities-v2|*The Cambridge World History of Sexualities*,
Vol. 2]]. Read together, the traditions confirm the central thesis of the
[[history-of-sexuality|history of sexuality]]: there is **no single, natural human
sexuality**, but a wide range of culturally specific frameworks for organizing desire, the
body, marriage, and identity.

## Greco-Roman antiquity: act, status, and *eros*

Greek and Roman thought organized sexual life less by the **gender** of one's partner than
by **role and status**: the citizen adult male was expected to be the *active/penetrating*
partner, with women, youths, and slaves as acceptable *passive* objects — so the modern
hetero/homosexual binary does not map onto it (the classic constructionist case, *Before
Sexuality*). Greek lyric and epic explored ***eros*** as an overwhelming, often destructive
divine force (Archilochus "pierced through the bone"; Virgil's Dido), while philosophy
(Plato) and later Stoic and medical writing debated its proper governance.

## South Asia: *kama* between *dharma* and asceticism

Traditional South Asian thought gave sexuality a **legitimate, even celebrated** place:
**kama** (pleasure/desire) is one of the recognized aims of life (the *purusharthas*,
alongside dharma, artha, and moksha), systematized in texts like the *Kamasutra* and
visible in temple art — yet held in tension with powerful **ascetic/renunciatory** ideals
(brahmacarya, the world-renouncer). Sexuality was woven into cosmology, caste regulation of
marriage, and devotional (bhakti, tantric) practice.

## China: cosmology, medicine, and the "bedchamber arts"

Chinese traditions framed sex within a **cosmological-medical** logic of **yin and yang**
and the balance of vital energies (*qi*, *jing*): the "arts of the bedchamber" treated
sexual practice as a matter of **health, longevity, and harmony** rather than sin, while
Confucian concern centred on **family continuity, lineage, and propriety**. Same-sex love
between men is amply attested in the literary record without a fixed "homosexual" identity.

## The Abrahamic religions: procreation, law, and regulation

The **Jewish, Christian, and Islamic** traditions share a broadly **procreative,
marriage-centred** ethic backed by religious law, but differ sharply:

- **Judaism** treats marital sexuality as a positive duty (a husband's obligation; the
  *mitzvah* of procreation) governed by *halakha* (purity laws, *niddah*).
- **Christianity** developed a strong **ascetic** strand (Paul, Augustine on concupiscence
  and original sin) that exalted **celibacy and virginity** above marriage and narrowed
  licit sex to procreative marriage — the most sex-anxious of the major traditions.
- **Islam** affirms sexuality as a **God-given good** within marriage (and, historically,
  concubinage), regulated by *sharia* and *fiqh*, with a rich erotic and juridical
  literature; it lacks Christianity's exaltation of celibacy.

## Buddhism, and the traditions of Africa, Oceania, and the Americas

**Buddhism** subordinates sexual desire to the goal of liberation (the monastic *vinaya*
enjoins celibacy; lay ethics counsel restraint), while tantric currents reinterpret desire
as a vehicle. The volume also stresses the **diversity** of African, Oceanic, and Indigenous
American systems — including widely attested **third-gender and gender-variant** roles — and
their disruption by colonialism and missionary Christianity.

## The modern reframings

From the 19th century, new "systems of thought" reframed sex: **sexology / sexual science**
(Krafft-Ebing, Havelock Ellis, Freud) recast it as a domain of medical-scientific
classification; **Marxism and socialism** linked sexual oppression to property and class;
**feminism** politicized it as a site of power and patriarchy; and **post-colonial** critique
exposed how European powers used sexuality to define and rule the "other."

## Assessment

The comparison's payoff is the demonstration of **deep variation** — procreative regulation
(Abrahamic) vs. health-and-harmony (China) vs. legitimate-aim-in-tension-with-asceticism
(South Asia) vs. status-and-act (Greco-Roman) — against which a few recurring concerns
(marriage, fertility, the management of desire, gender hierarchy) stand out. It anchors the
constructionist argument of the [[history-of-sexuality]] in concrete cross-cultural evidence.
