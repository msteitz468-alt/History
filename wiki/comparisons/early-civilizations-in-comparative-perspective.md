# Early Civilizations in Comparative Perspective

A systematic cross-cultural comparison of the seven best-documented early civilizations, drawn from [[sources/trigger-understanding-early-civilizations-2003|Bruce G. Trigger, *Understanding Early Civilizations: A Comparative Study* (2003)]].

Trigger examines ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, Shang China, the Aztecs (and neighbors in the Valley of Mexico), the Classic Maya, the Inka, and the Yoruba. With no historical connections between the cases, shared features are attributed to panhuman patterns of thought and behaviour or similar functional and environmental constraints (parallel development or coevolution). Differences largely reflect specific cultural traditions and historical particulars.

The study deliberately balances similarities and differences across sociopolitical organization, economic systems, religion, and culture. It challenges both extreme cultural relativism and overly simplistic materialist or ecological determinism.

## The Seven Civilizations

- **Ancient Egypt** (primarily Old and Middle Kingdom periods): territorial state with strong royal centralization.
- **Mesopotamia** (Early Dynastic through Old Babylonian): predominantly city-state system.
- **Shang China**: territorial state with lineage-based delegation.
- **Valley of Mexico (Aztecs and predecessors)**: city-state system with hegemonic elements.
- **Classic Maya**: city-state system.
- **Inka (Tawantinsuyu)**: large territorial state.
- **Yoruba (and related Benin)**: city-state / urbanized polities with councils sharing power with kings.

## City-States versus Territorial States

One of Trigger's most important structural findings is that early civilizations exhibited only two basic forms of political organization:

**City-states**: Smaller polities centered on an urban core with surrounding farmland. High proportion of population lived in cities (often 50%+ in some cases, including farmers for defense). More heterarchical elements, power-sharing with councils or lineages, and often more market-oriented exchange. Examples: Mesopotamia, Classic Maya, Valley of Mexico, Yoruba.

**Territorial states**: Larger polities controlling broad territories through multi-level administration. Capitals and provincial centers had smaller, more elite-focused populations; farmers lived dispersed near fields. More hierarchical or delegational administration and stronger central control of long-distance trade and elite goods. Examples: Egypt, Shang China, Inka.

These were stable alternatives rather than sequential stages. The distinction correlates with many other features (settlement patterns, economic organization, degree of ruler sacralization, stylistic uniformity in art).

## Sociopolitical Organization

**Kingship**: Universal in the sample. Sovereignty embodied in an individual ruler. Kingship was hereditary in practice (and usually in theory). Divinity claims were strongest in territorial states where rulers were more remote (Egypt, Inka); weaker in urbanized city-state systems where subjects could observe rulers closely (Mesopotamia).

**Class Systems**: All had a tiny ruling upper class supported by systematic appropriation of agricultural surpluses from a much larger farming population, plus full-time specialists and (in most cases) slaves or bound laborers. Class displaced kinship as the primary organizing principle.

**Family, Gender, and Administration**: Masculine bias everywhere, with variation in descent systems (patrilineal in many, endogamous groups in others, nuclear-family emphasis in Egypt). Women's status generally declined over time. Administration mixed local self-regulation with delegational or bureaucratic oversight from the center. City-states tended toward more council/lineage power-sharing; territorial states toward more formal hierarchies.

**Law and Military**: Law added a hierarchical layer reinforcing class distinctions. Military relied on peasant corvées plus professional specialists and guards. Rulers could never deploy enough force to suppress a significant portion of the population; legitimacy and perceived normality of hierarchy were more important.

## Economy

Greater diversity than in sociopolitical forms:

- Agricultural systems, tools, and intensity varied enormously depending on environment and prior traditions (chinampas and intensive raised fields in Mesoamerica vs. simpler digging-stick agriculture elsewhere; animal traction and ploughs limited mostly to Egypt and Mesopotamia).
- Land ownership: collective (kin or community), institutional (state/temple/patrimonial), and private forms all existed; no single evolutionary sequence.
- Trade and craft: City-state systems often featured more independent merchants and broader access to exotic goods via markets. Territorial states tended to centralize control of long-distance trade and elite craft production.
- Wealth appropriation: Multiple overlapping mechanisms (taxes, rents, corvée, tribute, fees). Upper classes consistently captured most surplus.

Trigger notes that economic variation was greater than sociopolitical regularities would predict.

## Cognitive and Symbolic Systems

**Striking regularities** (one of the book's most surprising findings):

- No sharp distinction between natural, supernatural, and social realms. The universe was seen as animated by conscious forces.
- Deities as personifications or manifestations of natural forces (often partially anthropomorphized), with diffuse power concepts alongside specific gods.
- Mutual dependence: humans depended on gods for the functioning of the world; gods depended on humans (especially through sacrifice and offerings) for sustenance and energy.
- Kings and upper classes as primary mediators channeling energy back to the gods.
- Sacrifice as a core mechanism projecting the earthly taxation and class system into the cosmic realm, while also imposing moral limits on exploitation (if farmers were mistreated, the gods would go hungry and cosmic order would fail).

**Cosmology**: Shared broad structure (flat earth, encircling ocean, multi-level vertical cosmos with sky above and underworld(s) below, privileged center, connections via axis mundi or sacred places). Cities, temples, and royal centers often functioned as cosmograms.

**Variation**: Specific myths, iconography, and details of cult were highly idiosyncratic and rooted in local traditions. Values and ideal lifestyles (e.g., the calm, self-controlled Egyptian official vs. the competitive, militaristic Aztec warrior ideal) differed strongly and endured.

## Overall Assessment and Theoretical Implications

Trigger finds far more cross-cultural uniformity than pure relativists would expect, but less than strict ecological or practical-reason determinists predict. Regularities are especially strong in sociopolitical forms (only two state types) and religious conceptions (the single model of mutual human-god dependence and sacrifice). Economic and artistic/valuative features show more idiosyncratic, culturally specific variation.

These patterns cannot be explained by historical diffusion (the cases are independent). They result from a combination of:

- Functional constraints (limited viable ways for complex societies to operate).
- Panhuman cognitive and psychological tendencies (including primate-derived acquisitiveness and dominance behaviors that emerge when public opinion no longer constrains leaders).
- Culturally constructed traditions that resist random change and provide meaning and identity.

The book rejects both extreme relativism and overly narrow materialism. It calls for holistic explanations that integrate biological, psychological, social, cultural, and historical factors rather than privileging one at the expense of others.
