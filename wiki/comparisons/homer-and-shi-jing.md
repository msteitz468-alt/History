# Homer and the Shi Jing as Imperial Texts

Alexander Beecroft's chapter (Kim et al. 2017, ch. 7) compares the **Homeric epics** and
the Chinese **Shi Jing** (*Book of Songs*) not as timeless objects to be dated, but as
texts that passed through successive "ontologically distinct" incarnations — oral, variant,
and finally **canonized** — and focuses on their *late* lives as instruments of imperial
regimes: the Hellenistic Greek kingdoms (Ptolemaic Alexandria, Pergamon) for Homer, the
Western and Eastern [[han-dynasty|Han]] for the Shi Jing.

## Two Corpora, Two Modes of Curation

| | Homer | Shi Jing |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Long, narratively continuous epic | Anthology of short lyric/imagistic poems |
| Imperial patrons | Hellenistic kingdoms (library rivalry) | Han (and Tang) dynasties |
| Dominant scholarly act | **Text criticism** — establishing/athetizing lines | **Allegorical interpretation** — assigning moral-political meaning |
| Institutional form | Diffuse, never-canonized scholia | Standardized commentary; the dominant **Mao school** |
| Political use | Cultural capital contested between rival courts | Legitimation by linking a new dynasty to the Zhou |

Beecroft's central point is that the **shape of the text determined the mode of imperial
scholarship**. A long coherent narrative invited editorial battles over which lines
belonged (Zenodotus athetizing *Iliad* 3.423–426 as unseemly for a goddess); a collection
of short poems invited selection, ordering, and above all **allegoresis** of individual
pieces.

## Homer under the Hellenistic Kingdoms

The great Alexandrian editors — Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium, Aristarchus — plus
Crates of Mallos at Pergamon produced rival editions (none surviving directly; known
through the *Venetus A* scholia). Rival kingdoms treated Homeric scholarship as **cultural
capital**: Alexandria reportedly blocked papyrus exports to Pergamon and even detained
Aristophanes of Byzantium. Yet the transmitted text shows little direct Alexandrian
influence — Margalit Finkelberg's compelling hypothesis is that "our" Homer descends from a
later **Byzantine** (Constantinopolitan, possibly Antiochene) recension, itself an imperial
"Age of Homer."

## The Shi Jing under the Han

Of the four Han interpretive schools (Lu, Qi, Han, Mao), the **Mao** edition (c. 150 BCE)
prevailed — canonically enshrined only under the child-emperor Ping (with regent **Wang
Mang**, who styled himself the Duke of Zhou), fixed by the imperial bibliographers Liu Xiang
and Liu Xin, layered by **Zheng Xuan** (127–200 CE), and made definitive by **Kong
Yingda's** Tang edition (637–642, for Tang Taizong). The Mao reading works by "judicious
misreading": poem 2, "Ge Tan" ("Kudzu"), read literally in the pre-300 BCE *Kongzi Shilun*
as praise of linen-making, is allegorized by the Mao preface into Queen Taisi's wifely
thrift — Kong Yingda's commentary on that single poem runs ~5,000 characters. Newly
excavated bamboo texts (Kongzi Shilun; Tsinghua strips) are reshaping the dating debate,
which ranges from an 11th-c. BCE Western Zhou origin (Shaughnessy) to a late-4th-c. BCE
floor (the Brookses).

## Why It Matters

Both empires reached back to a prestigious inheritance and **re-fixed** it to serve present
authority — Tang Taizong's sponsored classics edition tying his dynasty to Han and Zhou, the
Hellenistic and Byzantine courts competing to own Homer. The comparison exemplifies the
volume's method of setting Mediterranean and Chinese developments in a single Eurasian
frame; Beecroft notes modern readers remain, for the Shi Jing, "prisoners of the Mao school
of interpretation."
