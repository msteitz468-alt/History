# Mumun Culture

The **Mumun** ("plain," undecorated pottery) culture of the Korean Peninsula, **~1450–300 BC**,
succeeded the [[chulmun-culture|Chulmun]] and carried the Peninsula into farming, megalithic burial and
metal. It is the decisive stage in [[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes's]] account of
[[pen-insular-state-formation|Korean state formation]] and the source of the agricultural "package"
that crossed the Korea Strait to create the Japanese [[yayoi-culture|Yayoi]].

## Three Transformations

Barnes identifies three changes from Chulmun: (1) loss of ceramic decoration and new vessel forms;
(2) the appearance of **dolmen burials** in the north in the late 2nd millennium BC; (3) the intrusion
of diagnostic **Manchurian-Basin bronzes** from the 7th century BC. Early Mumun continued Chulmun
millet-farming, hunting and marine exploitation (eleven Mumun shellmounds are known); leading into the
transition, Chulmun millet farmers appear to have **reverted to mobile foraging**, perhaps easing the
spread of rice-farming from Liaodong.

## Rice and the Field System

**Rice cultivation joined millet, sorghum, beans and peas around 1000 BC**, entering via the Shandong→
Liaodong→Peninsula route (see [[rice-agriculture-spread-east-asia]]); permanent paddy fields with bunds
and irrigation canals are known from ten southern sites (e.g. the 7th-century-BC Okhyon fields beside a
60-house village). The lunate/triangular **polished stone reaper**, inherited from Shandong's Yueshi
culture, became the standard Pen/Insular reaping knife.

## Burial and Society

**Tens of thousands of dolmens** dot the Peninsula (413 excavated cemeteries). Northern "table" dolmens
are smaller and poorer; southern "go-board" dolmens grow monumental, often over rich pit or cist
burials — concentrated around Kwangju and possibly tied to the emergence of southern-coast chiefs late
in the Mumun. Middle Mumun (850–550 BC) society was **community-oriented**: dolmens are communal works,
cemeteries mix dolmen, cist and jar burials without strong size differentiation, and Liaoning bronzes
"probably indicated the integration of community heads into incipient-elite networks." Three-tiered
site hierarchies appear in the southwest; central places (often ditched and palisaded) lasted only a
few generations. **House-burning** is widespread (93.5% of excavated Mumun houses around Pyongyang;
25–36% in the south) — Barnes's evidence of competition. Key sites: **Taepyong-ri** (some of the
earliest ridge-and-furrow dry fields; a bronze plaque depicting foot-plough farming), **Komdan-ri** (a
hilltop ditched enclosure where the richest houses cluster inside the ditch), and **Songguk-ri** —
whose round, central-depression pit-building defines the "Songguk-ri culture" that coalesced ~650 BC and
spread across the southwest after 450 BC; at its peak Songguk-ri covered 61 ha with ~400 pit-buildings.

## Metal: Liaoning Daggers to Slender Bronze Daggers

Liaodong bronzes — above all the **Liaoning dagger** (a short double-edged sword with a pear-shaped,
centre-bulging blade and separate hilt) — infiltrated the Peninsula from the early 7th century BC. They
are rare and precious: ~100 Liaoning-style artifacts are known, over half daggers, many resharpened to
the rib or refashioned, "suggesting bronze was in short supply." In the Late Mumun / Early Iron Age
(500–200 BC), the narrower **Slender Bronze Dagger culture** (developed in Liaodong ~5th c. BC, not
invented locally) brought technically superb bronze rattles and compass-drawn fine-line mirrors,
produced at one or two concentrated southwestern workshops. Three burial assemblages — ritual bronzes
(a "shaman-king"), weapons-only (warriors), and weapons-plus-mirror — mark a new **individualisation**
of society. **Cast iron** from the [[zhou-dynasty|Zhou]] state of Yan arrived in the 4th century BC, but
**iron did not replace bronze** as the prime prestige good; smithing is attested from ~200 BC (north)
and ~150 BC (south). Mumun gave way to the [[samhan|Samhan]] in the south.
