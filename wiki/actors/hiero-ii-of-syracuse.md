# Hiero II of Syracuse

## Overview

**Hiero II** (c. 308–215 BCE) was king of Syracuse from c. 269 until his death —
the longest-reigning Sicilian monarch of antiquity and the most politically successful
Greek ruler of the western Mediterranean in the third century. His career spans the
transition from independent Sicilian power to Roman protectorate; his fifty-year
loyalty to Rome after 263 BCE was foundational to the Roman position in Sicily
throughout the [[first-punic-war-264-241bce|First Punic War]] and beyond.

## Rise to Power

Hiero was a strategos (general) who rose to prominence through his military ability.
He made two attempts to deal with the **Mamertines** — the Campanian mercenaries who
had seized Messana and were plundering eastern Sicily. After an initial defeat (c.
275/4), he captured Halaesa and Tyndaris, defeated the Mamertines decisively on the
river **Longanus** (c. 269 or 265 BCE), and assumed the royal title of **basileus**
("king"). He advanced on Messana — but was blocked by a Carthaginian admiral who
installed a Punic garrison in the citadel.

## The Decisive Alignment: 264–263 BCE

When Rome intervened in the Mamertine crisis in **264 BCE**, Hiero allied with
Carthage against what appeared to be a third-power intrusion into Sicily.
Carthaginian and Syracusan forces jointly blockaded Messana. But Appius Claudius
Caudex crossed the Straits and the campaign began.

After a second year (263) in which both Roman consuls crossed with 40,000 men and
sixty-seven Sicilian towns submitted or were taken, Hiero made a rational reassessment:
Rome was more likely to prevail than Carthage, and his Punic allies had already
abandoned him once at Messana. He sought peace. The Romans, anxious about supplies
and eager to secure their eastern Sicilian logistics base, accepted.

Treaty terms (263 BCE): Hiero paid an indemnity of **100 talents**, returned all
Roman prisoners without ransom, and retained his kingdom — a rump of c. 30 miles
radius around Syracuse including Acrae, Leontini, Megara, Helorus, Netum, and
Tauromenium. The treaty was ratified by the Roman people and renewed permanently in
**248 BCE**.

## Fifty Years of Loyalty

For the remaining fifty years of his life and reign, Hiero II was the most reliable
partner Rome had in the Mediterranean world. His contributions to Roman war-making
during the First Punic War were substantial:

- Maintained reliable food supply to Roman forces in Sicily, especially during the
  difficult years after Rome lost her fleet
- Sent Syracusan troops to assist Roman forces (Zonaras attests this)
- Expressed his confidence in Rome's ultimate victory by renewing his alliance in 248
  "on a permanent basis" even during the nadir of Roman fortunes
- Refused the Mamertines and others who would have drawn him away

Ptolemy III of Egypt similarly recognized Rome — granting *amicitia* c. 273 BCE and
declining to lend Carthage the 2,000 talents she requested during the war.

## Statecraft and Culture

Hiero II was a significant ruler in his own right, not merely a Roman client:

- He transformed Syracuse into a major Hellenistic capital — new fortifications,
  the great altar of Hiero (the largest altar in the Greek world), civic architecture
- He patronized the poet **Theocritus** (*Idylls* XVI addressed to Hiero c. 275/4,
  before his victories)
- He developed the **Hieronic law** (*lex Hieronica*) — a tax-collection system for
  Sicilian grain tribute that Rome later adopted wholesale for the province of Sicily,
  making it the administrative template for Rome's first overseas province
- He corresponded with [[archimedes|Archimedes]], his court's greatest scientific mind
  (Archimedes' *Sand-Reckoner* is addressed to Hiero's son Gelon)

## Death and Consequence

Hiero died in **215 BCE**, two years into the Second Punic War. His death — at
roughly 93 years old — removed the one figure who might have maintained Syracusan
loyalty to Rome. His grandson and successor Hieronymus immediately defected to
Hannibal. After Hieronymus's assassination (214 BCE) and two years of war, Rome
besieged Syracuse; Archimedes' famous war-machines prolonged the defense before the
city fell to Marcellus in **212 BCE**.

## Counterfactual Significance

Hiero II's fifty-year alliance was not structurally inevitable — it was a contingent
choice repeatedly reaffirmed. Without his reliable logistics base at Syracuse, Rome's
ability to sustain large armies in Sicily through the years of naval disaster (255,
253, 249) would have been severely compromised. His early defection from Carthage in
263 — rational but not mandatory — was the single act most consequential for the war's
early momentum. The contrast with his grandson Hieronymus's defection to Hannibal
illustrates how fragile the alliance always was under its royal-personal character.
