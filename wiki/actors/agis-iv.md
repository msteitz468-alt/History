## Role

Agis IV (c. 265–241 BCE), Eurypontid king of [[sparta|Sparta]], was the first of the two
**revolutionary kings** (the other being [[cleomenes-iii|Cleomenes III]]) who tried to
arrest Sparta's decline through radical social reform. His attempt failed and cost him his
life, but it opened the great Spartan revolution of the late third century. He is known
chiefly through Plutarch's *Agis* (drawing on the sympathetic [[hellenistic-historiography|Phylarchus]]).

## The Crisis and the Reform

By the mid-third century Sparta faced a deep structural crisis: wealth (partly mercenary
earnings) had concentrated in few hands — half the land, says Phylarchus, in the hands of
women — and the number of full **Spartiates had collapsed to about 700**, only a hundred of
them rich. The shrunken citizen body could no longer field an effective army.

Agis' programme (presented as a restoration of the ancient "Lycurgan" order) aimed to
**rebuild the citizen body**:
- **redistribution of land** into 4,500 lots for citizens, "inferiors" (*hypomeiones*),
  *perioikoi*, and even resident foreigners, plus 15,000 lots for landless *perioikoi*;
- **cancellation of debts**;
- revival of the common messes (*syssitia*) and the *agoge* (the Spartan training).

His ally Lycurgus (as ephor) and his uncle Agesilaus pushed the measures through against
the opposition of the Agiad king Leonidas II (whom they deposed). But the reform was
**fatally half-completed**: the debt cancellation was carried out, but the
land-redistribution was *postponed*. Once relieved of debt, the poorer citizens lost
interest in sharing citizenship; the rich resisted losing their estates.

## Downfall

When the [[achaean-league|Achaean]] leader [[aratus-of-sicyon|Aratus]] called for Spartan
help against the Aetolians (241), Agis marched north — but Aratus, alarmed at the
revolutionary example of his well-drilled army (and the social unrest it might inspire),
**dismissed him**. Returning to find his enemies in power, Agis was tricked out of
sanctuary and **executed**, along with his mother and grandmother. The Spartan revolution
was postponed for over a decade — until [[cleomenes-iii|Cleomenes III]] revived and
radicalised it.

## Significance

Agis IV is the tragic prototype of the **Hellenistic social revolution from above** — a
king turning the slogans of land-redistribution and debt-cancellation (the perennial fears
of the propertied [[achaean-league|Achaean]] class) into a programme of state revival. His
failure exposed the structural weakness of Sparta and the alarm such movements caused
across Greece; his martyrdom made him a model for Cleomenes and a lasting symbol. See
[[sparta]], [[cleomenes-iii]].
