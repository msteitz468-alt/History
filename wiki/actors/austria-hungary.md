# Austria-Hungary

**Austria-Hungary** (the "Dual Monarchy") was created by the 1867 *Ausgleich* (Compromise),
which divided Habsburg rule into two co-equal states — the Kingdom of Hungary and the
Austrian ("Cisleithanian") lands — sharing a monarch (Franz Joseph I, 1848–1916), a
common army, and joint ministries for foreign affairs, war, and finance, while each half
retained its own parliament and administration. It was one of the great powers of Europe
until the First World War, but a state structurally strained by its extraordinary ethnic
diversity: Germans and Magyars held disproportionate political power over Czechs, Poles,
Ruthenians, Romanians, Croats, Serbs, Slovenes, and Slovaks, and unresolved nationality
demands (the "nationalities question") were a chronic source of instability.

## Role and Decline

Foreign policy after 1878 (occupation, then 1908 annexation, of Bosnia-Herzegovina) put
Austria-Hungary on a collision course with Serbian and South Slav nationalism, which the
army's Chief of the General Staff [[actors/franz-conrad-von-hotzendorff|Conrad von Hötzendorf]]
saw as an existential threat requiring preventive war. The assassination of Archduke Franz
Ferdinand at Sarajevo (June 1914) triggered the July Crisis and the ultimatum to Serbia
that began the First World War. Austria-Hungary's war effort was hampered by an
under-resourced, multilingual army, dependence on German support, and internal nationalist
disaffection; military and economic collapse in 1918 was followed by the disintegration
of the empire into successor nation-states (Czechoslovakia, the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats
and Slovenes, an independent Hungary and Austria, and territorial cessions to Poland,
Romania, and Italy) — see [[events/dissolution-of-austria-hungary-1918-1919]].
