## Overview

Cenwealh, son (or possibly grandson — see Historiography) of [[cynegils-of-wessex]], was king of the Gewisse/West Saxons in the mid-seventh century. Still a pagan at his accession, he was converted to Christianity during an exile at the [[kingdom-of-east-anglia|East Anglian]] court of King Anna. He established a second episcopal see at Winchester in Hampshire, dividing the diocese from Dorchester-on-Thames. His reign saw the high-water mark of Gewissian territorial expansion, followed by the reassertion of Mercian control over the upper Thames valley. On his death the kingdom fragmented among sub-kings for approximately ten years.

## Paganism and Exile

Cenwealh was still a pagan in the 640s despite his father's conversion. He had married a sister of [[penda-of-mercia|Penda]], king of Mercia, cementing a relationship with the dominant power of midland England. When he repudiated her and remarried, Penda expelled him from his kingdom. Cenwealh spent three years in exile at the court of Anna, king of the eastern Angles (HE III, 7), where he was converted. Anna was slain by Penda before the battle of the Winwaed (654 or 655), and Cenwealh was likely unable to return until after Penda's own death at the Winwaed (655 or 656).

## Territorial Expansion

After his return, Cenwealh reconstituted much of the western Saxon territorial base. The Chronicle A records (for c. 660, though the annal date is slightly too early) a campaign in which he fought the Britons at Peonnum (near Penselwood) and drove them to the River Parrett, implying control of Wiltshire and beyond. He granted 3,000 hides of land in Berkshire to his kinsman Cuthred. Kirby concludes that "he must have re-assembled some of the territories which had once been under the domination of Ceawlin."

He also maintained contacts with Northumbrian court circles — with Oswiu's son Alhfrith and with Wilfrid — and possibly with Benedict Biscop, in the period after the Winwaed.

## Winchester See and the Division of the Diocese

Bede describes the founding of a second episcopal see at Winchester as a division of the Gewissian diocese (HE III, 7). The Frankish bishop Agilbert, whom Cenwealh had appointed to Dorchester, was not consulted and quarrelled with the king over it, departing for Gaul (eventually becoming bishop of Paris). Wine, who replaced him at the Winchester see, can be dated to c. 662–665: he was still bishop when he assisted at the consecration of Chad c. 665. Wine was himself later expelled by Cenwealh and acquired the see of London from Wulfhere of Mercia.

Kirby reads the Winchester foundation and the effective abandonment of Dorchester-on-Thames not as a theological reorganisation but as a reflection of territorial loss: the upper Thames valley was falling to Mercian domination. "What Bede describes as a division of Cenwealh's kingdom into two dioceses with the founding of Winchester (HE III, 7) was in reality part of the slow abandonment of Dorchester-on-Thames."

## Death and Fragmentation

On Cenwealh's death, sub-kings "presumed to divide the kingdom of the western Saxons and ruled it for about ten years" (Bede, HE IV, 12). Kirby argues this statement of Bede — probably derived from Daniel, bishop of Winchester — represents the partisan viewpoint of the supporters of Caedwalla and Ine, who viewed Cenwealh's immediate successors as dynastic rivals rather than legitimate kings. The sub-kings were real enough: Seaxburh (his widow) reigned for a year; Aescwine fought Wulfhere of Mercia at Biedanheafde in 675; Centwine reigned from c. 676 and drove the Britons to the sea (Chronicle A, s.a. 682).

The date of Cenwealh's death is disputed. The Chronicle places it in 672, but Bede links it to Benedict Biscop's return from the continent, which occurred in both 672 and 675. Kirby suggests death c. 676 is possible if a ten-year period of sub-kings terminates with Caedwalla in 686.

## Counterfactual Significance

Cenwealh's conversion and his establishment of the Winchester see were decisive for the long-term Christianisation of the western Saxons. Without the Winchester see, the missionary infrastructure in the west would have collapsed with the loss of Dorchester. His reign also demonstrated the structural vulnerability of the Gewisse to Mercian expansion — a vulnerability his successors were unable to remedy until the rise of Caedwalla in 686.

## Historiography

The principal source is Bede (HE III, 7; IV, 12), supplemented by the Chronicle and the Vita Wilfridi. Kirby raises the possibility that Cenwealh was Cynegils' grandson rather than son, on the grounds that Cuthred, son of Cwichelm, son of Cynegils, is described as Cenwealh's contemporary and grantee. The thirty-one-year regnal figure in the West Saxon Genealogical Regnal List is "regarded with suspicion" by Kirby. The date of death is uncertain, and Kirby considers both 672 and c. 676. The question of whether it was Centwine or Caedwalla who ended the period of sub-king fragmentation is raised by Barbara Yorke (cited by Kirby) as a genuine open problem.
