# Oda Nobunaga (1534–1582)

The first of the **three unifiers** who ended Japan's century of civil war
([[muromachi-period|sengoku jidai]]) and began the reunification completed by
[[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Hideyoshi]] and [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]]. A daimyo of ruthless
energy whose motto was **"Rule the Empire by Force,"** he is the opening figure of
[[azuchi-momoyama-period|early-modern Japan]]. From Mason & Caiger's
[[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]] (ch. 12).

## Career

From his base in Owari, Nobunaga won his decisive early victory at **Okehazama (1560)** —
a surprise attack destroying the much larger army of Imagawa Yoshimoto — then allied with
[[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]] and others and **entered Kyoto (1568)**, installing (then in
1573 expelling) the last Ashikaga shogun and so ending the [[muromachi-period|Muromachi
bakufu]]. He controlled the capital region, using the emperor's sanction to legitimize his
conquests, and expanded by force and innovation.

## Decisions and significance

- **Crushing the militant Buddhists.** Nobunaga ended the centuries-old political power of
  the great monasteries with shocking violence: in **1571** he surrounded and **burned
  Mt. Hiei** (Hieizan), slaughtering tens of thousands of [[japanese-buddhism|Tendai]]
  monks and destroying a great treasure-house of culture; he reduced the Shinshū
  fortress-temple **Ishiyama Honganji** after a ten-year siege (1570–80). This broke the
  independent power of the sects — a precondition of the unified secular state.
- **The military revolution.** At **Nagashino (1575)** he deployed massed, disciplined
  **firearms** (the matchlock, introduced by the Portuguese) in volleys that annihilated
  the Takeda cavalry — a landmark in the [[military-revolution|gunpowder transformation]]
  of warfare.
- **Administration.** Within his domains he built great stone **castles** (Azuchi),
  standardized currency, abolished customs barriers, and promoted trade.

He was killed in 1582 when his general **Akechi Mitsuhide** turned on him at the Honnōji
in Kyoto; [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Hideyoshi]] avenged him and inherited his project.

## Counterfactual significance

Nobunaga did not unify Japan, but he **made unification possible** — by smashing the two
forces (the militant monasteries and the strongest rival daimyo) that had kept the country
fragmented, and by pioneering the gunpowder warfare and castle-based administration that the
later unifiers used. He is the hinge between medieval fragmentation and the early-modern
state.
