# The Holy Roman Empire in the Late Middle Ages

The late-medieval **Holy Roman Empire** was a firmly **elective monarchy**, its kings made by the
prince-electors and ruling less through imperial institutions than through their own **dynastic
power-base (*Hausmacht*)**. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Peter Herde, Ch. 14a–b) traces it from
the contested elections after [[rudolf-of-habsburg|Rudolf I]] to the great reign of **Charles IV**
and the **Golden Bull of 1356**.

## The Elective Monarchy and Its Dynasties

After Henry VI's failed bid to make the Empire **hereditary** (on the French and English model), the
crown remained elective, and the **electors** guarded that right jealously — choosing weak
candidates they hoped to control, and switching dynasties to prevent any one house entrenching
itself. The fourteenth-century kingship rotated among houses: **Adolf of Nassau** (1292, "the
insignificant count" elected as a tool of the archbishop of Cologne, deposed and killed 1298);
**Albert I of Habsburg**; **[[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Henry VII of Luxemburg]]** (whose Italian
expedition, 1310–1313, failed to revive imperial rule); **Lewis of Bavaria** (Wittelsbach,
1314–1347); and then the **Luxemburgs**. Royal power rested not on the dwindling imperial domain
but on each dynasty's own lands — the **Hausmacht** principle the [[german-interregnum-1250|Interregnum]]
had established.

## Lewis of Bavaria and the Papacy

The reign of **Lewis of Bavaria** renewed the old **papal–imperial conflict**: excommunicated by
**[[papacy|Pope John XXII]]** at Avignon, Lewis gathered at his court the Franciscan and
philosophical opponents of papal claims — **Marsilius of Padua** and **William of Ockham** — and the
electors declared at **Rhens (1338)** that an elected king of the Romans needed no papal
confirmation (see [[late-medieval-political-thought]]). This asserted the **autonomy of the secular
empire** from the papacy.

## Charles IV and the Golden Bull (1356)

The Empire's late-medieval zenith came under **Charles IV of Luxemburg** (king 1346, emperor
1355–1378), who made **Prague** and **Bohemia** the centre of his power, founded its university
(1348), patronised the arts (Karlstein), and pursued an **eastward, Hausmacht** policy in Bohemia,
Brandenburg and Silesia rather than in Italy. His monument was the **Golden Bull of 1356** — "the
basic law of the empire for several centuries." It fixed the **election of the king of the Romans**
in a college of **seven hereditary electors** (the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne and Trier; the
king of Bohemia, the count-palatine, the duke of Saxony, and the margrave of Brandenburg), now
"de jure accorded full sovereign rights," and confirmed **Frankfurt** (election), **Aachen**
(coronation) and **Nuremberg** (first diet). It made no mention of the **pope's** role — a decisive
omission. Herde reads it as "a compromise between the central power and the electors," which
sidelined the lesser estates and the **towns** (whose leagues it sought to ban).

## Significance

The Golden Bull crystallised the Empire's distinctive late-medieval form: not a centralising
national monarchy like [[valois-dynasty|France]] or [[edward-iii-of-england|England]], but a
**federal, elective polity** of great princely territories, in which the emperor's real power was
his family's *Hausmacht* and the constitutional initiative lay with the electors. This decentralised
constitution — confirmed against the towns and the lesser nobility — set the framework of German
politics into the early-modern period. Charles IV's weak son **Wenceslas** was deposed (1400) and
replaced by **Rupert of the Palatinate**, before the crown returned to the Luxemburgs and then the
Habsburgs.
