# Agesilaus II

## Overview

**Agesilaus II** (c. 444–c. 360 BCE), Eurypontid king of [[sparta]] from about **400**
to his death, was the dominant Spartan of the fourth century and "**a powerful figure in
the Greek world**" — the king under whom the [[spartan-hegemony|Spartan hegemony]]
reached both its height and its ruin. He is unusually well documented because he was the
hero, benefactor, and biographical subject of **[[xenophon]]**, whose *Agesilaus* is the
first surviving Greek encomiastic biography — a closeness that makes the king's image,
in [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Chs. 1–2, Hornblower and Lewis), both vivid and
suspect.

## The exponent of "congenial oligarchies"

Hornblower presents Agesilaus as "**the exponent *par excellence***" of the system of
friendly oligarchies through which Sparta controlled the Peloponnesian League — the
network of personal *xenia* and patronage that was the real machinery of Spartan power.
Lame from birth, he was raised in the ordinary Spartan *agoge* (unusual for a king) and
came to the throne with the backing of [[lysander]], who expected to manage him;
Agesilaus quickly outgrew and sidelined his patron.

## The Asian campaign and the limits of panhellenism

In **396** Agesilaus crossed to Asia Minor to "liberate" the Greeks from Persia, posing
as a new Agamemnon — the panhellenist gesture Xenophon admired. CAH VI uses the campaign
to puncture the myth: unlike [[cyrus-the-younger|Cyrus the Younger]] in 401, Agesilaus
**could not secure Ionia and Caria** and so could never have struck inland with an
unprotected rear; he would have been "bottled up in the interior." His more realistic
aim may have been to foment a "cordon of rebel satraps." In **394** he was recalled to
fight the [[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]] at home, winning the bloody land
battle of **Coronea** against a Theban-led coalition.

## Overreach and Leuctra

Through the period of the [[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]] (386) Agesilaus made Sparta
the enforcer of Persia's "autonomy" settlement, using it to break up rival leagues.
[[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC* (2000)]]
credits Agesilaus specifically, not the Persian King, with deciding how the Peace's
autonomy clause would be applied — he used it to dissolve both the Corinth–Argos union
and the **Boeotian confederacy**, the clearest evidence that Spartan rather than Persian
interests actually shaped the Peace's consequences (see
[[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]]). Sparta then pressed the same logic against
her own allies: in **385** she besieged and demolished **Mantinea**, breaking it into
four or five villages, on the pretext that it had aided Argos; around **380** she
besieged **Phlius** over a dispute concerning restored exiles' property. His implacable
hostility to **Thebes** drove the worst Spartan blunder of the age: the
seizure of the Theban citadel, the **Cadmea**, in **382** by the Spartan commander
Phoebidas (fined but not disgraced at home, while the pro-Athenian Theban leader Ismenias
was executed) — which Xenophon himself read as the impiety the gods later punished at
**Leuctra (371)**, where [[epaminondas]] shattered the Spartan army (see
[[thebes-hegemony-371bce]]). Agesilaus survived the disaster and spent his last years
defending a diminished Sparta and serving as a mercenary commander in **Egypt** to raise
funds, dying on the voyage home.

## Counterfactual significance

Agesilaus personifies the contradiction at the heart of the
[[spartan-hegemony|Spartan hegemony]]: a king of great energy, charisma, and tactical
skill whose narrow vindictiveness — above all toward Thebes — converted Spartan
supremacy into the coalition and the catastrophe that ended it. His career is the case
study for the editors' thesis that the early fourth century was "full of ideas and
innovation" even as its political achievement proved "ultimately unimpressive."
