# Ramesses III

"The last of the great warrior kings of Egypt" — the Twentieth-Dynasty pharaoh who
**defeated the [[sea-peoples|Sea Peoples]] on the very threshold of Egypt** in the great
land-and-sea battle of his eighth year, the only major power to ride out the
[[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]]. After him "the glory departed, and Egypt was never
again an imperial power." Treated in [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]]
(Ch. XXIII, Faulkner). Reign 1198–1166 (volume) / ~1184–1153 BCE.

## The wars

His reign's three great wars (recorded at his **Medinet Habu** funerary temple) all
faced the migrations crashing in from the west and north:
- **Year 5 — Libyans.** A Libu/Meshwesh coalition, recovered from
  [[merneptah|Merneptah's]] defeat, again sought to seize the Delta; routed.
- **Year 8 — the Sea Peoples.** "The foreign countries made a plot in their
  islands... no land could stand before their arms, **Khatti, Qode, Carchemish,
  Arzawa and Alashiya**." A confederation of **Peleset (Philistines), Tjekker,
  Sheklesh, Sherden, Weshesh and Denyen** (perhaps the Danaoi), having **destroyed
  the Hittite empire**, advanced down the Syrian coast — with their **families in
  ox-carts**, to settle — escorted by a fleet. Ramesses stopped the land army with
  his Palestinian garrisons and **destroyed the enemy fleet** in a Nile-mouth
  harbour, the Egyptians lining the shore with archers. This is the **decisive
  defeat of the Sea Peoples** (see Section 4); but two of the peoples, the
  **Philistines** and **Tjekker**, stayed to settle in Palestine.
- **Year 11 — Libyans again**, who had already infiltrated the western Delta;
  crushed.

## Decline at home

The later reign darkened. The **Great Harris Papyrus** records the immense wealth
poured into the temples — above all **[[amun-priesthood|Amun]] of Thebes** — "bleeding
the nation white." In Year 29 the necropolis workmen of **Deir el-Medina** went
unpaid and staged the **first recorded strikes in history**. And a **harem
conspiracy** — Queen Tiy plotting to put her son Pentwere on the throne, using wax
figures and drawing in courtiers — was uncovered and tried (the conspirators
condemned to death or suicide; some of the judges themselves disgraced for carousing
with the accused). His mummy shows no wound, but the plot's outcome for the king
himself is uncertain.

## Shaw (2000): the temple economy that broke the state

Jacobus Van Dijk's chapter sharpens the internal story. Ramesses III's father **Sethnakht**
had seized a throne left in chaos — the Great Harris Papyrus recalls a Syrian "upstart"
(**Irsu**) whose confederates had plundered Egypt. Ramesses' year-8 victory succeeded because
"Egypt had fortified the Nile Delta mouths and moved forces to Djahy," so "his troops were
well prepared." But his **donations to the temples** were ruinous: by the end of the reign
"a third of cultivable land was temple-owned, three-quarters of that belonging to Amun of
Thebes" — the fiscal imbalance that left the treasury unable to pay the
[[deir-el-medina|Deir el-Medina]] workmen, producing the **year-29 strikes**, "the first
recorded organized strikes in history." He also mounted an expedition to
[[places/punt|Punt]], "perhaps the first since the famous venture … in the days of
Hatshepsut." (See [[concepts/egyptian-temple-economy]].)

## Significance

Ramesses III is the **breakwater of the Bronze Age Collapse** — the ruler whose
victory spared Egypt the fate of Hatti, Ugarit and the Mycenaean world, while the
**Philistine settlement** he could not prevent reshaped the Levant (and the biblical
world). His reign is also a study in **internal decline**: the over-mighty Amun
priesthood, fiscal strain, labour unrest and palace intrigue that would dissolve the
Ramesside state (Section 5). With him the **New Kingdom's imperial age ends**.

## Historiography

Documented by the **Medinet Habu** reliefs and inscriptions (the Sea-Peoples scenes),
the **Harris Papyrus**, the **Deir el-Medina** strike records, and the **harem
conspiracy** trial transcripts — an unusually rich social as well as military
archive. The Medinet Habu "Syrian war" scenes are partly **anachronistic copies** of
Ramesses II's, a caution against reading them literally. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
