# Titus Quinctius Flamininus

The Roman commander who defeated **[[philip-v|Philip V]]** at Cynoscephalae and
proclaimed the **[[freedom-of-the-greeks|"freedom of the Greeks"]]** — the architect
of Rome's philhellenic diplomacy. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 8
(Errington).

## Role and career

Of meteoric ascent (consul 198 before he was thirty, without the praetorship — a
case the [[roman-aristocratic-politics|cursus-honorum]] reforms responded to),
Flamininus had learned Greek and Greek ways in Magna Graecia. Given command in the
[[second-macedonian-war-200-196bce|Second Macedonian War]], he secured his own
prorogation through powerful senatorial backing, forced Philip from the Aous gorge,
detached his Greek allies, and won at **Cynoscephalae** (197). He then staged the
**Isthmian proclamation of 196** — the herald's declaration of Greek freedom — and,
over two years, evacuated the "Fetters of Greece" (Demetrias, Chalcis, the
Acrocorinth), returning the Acrocorinth to the Achaeans in a calculated *coup de
théâtre* (194).

He remained Rome's chief "eastern expert," confronting **[[antiochus-iii-the-great|
Antiochus III]]** diplomatically (Lysimacheia 196, Rome 193) with the same technique
of demands framed to be rejected, and intervening repeatedly in Greek affairs
(Sparta, Boeotia, the murder of Brachylles, the favouring of Philip's son Demetrius).

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Flamininus invented the doctrine that **Greek gratitude was a cheaper and more
durable substitute for legions** — "freedom" as an instrument of indirect control.
Errington reads his philhellenism as largely **instrumental**: the freedom slogan
won the war and structured the peace, but its logic (Rome as benefactor and arbiter)
hardened into the demand for obedience that, by 146, ended Greek freedom outright.
He is the embodiment of the [[nature-of-roman-imperialism|debate]] over whether Rome's
eastern policy was generous or manipulative.

## Historiography

[[polybius]] gives a broadly admiring but not uncritical account; [[livy]] dramatises
the Isthmian scene. Errington stresses the cynical stage-management beneath the
benefaction. See [[freedom-of-the-greeks]].
