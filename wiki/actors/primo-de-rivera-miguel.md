# Miguel Primo de Rivera (1870–1930)

General and dictator of Spain 1923–1930 — the "authoritarian alternative" that ended the Restoration parliamentary system by pronunciamiento (September 1923) with the acquiescence of Alfonso XIII. His regime (Military Directory 1923–25, then civilian Directory) ended the Moroccan war (Alhucemas landing, 1925, in which [[franco-francisco|Franco]] distinguished himself), pursued state-led public works and corporatist labor arbitration, and founded a single official party (Unión Patriótica) — but failed to institutionalize itself, and fell in January 1930 when the army and king withdrew support; he died in Paris weeks later.

## Significance for the Franco regime

Payne (1987) stresses that the Primo de Rivera dictatorship "made a major impression" on Franco's thinking: Franco regarded himself as continuing the same policies while avoiding the basic "Primo de Rivera mistake" — failure to institutionalize an authoritarian nationalist regime. The dictatorship's collapse also discredited the monarchy and opened the way to the [[second-spanish-republic]]. His son [[primo-de-rivera-jose-antonio|José Antonio]] founded the [[falange|Falange]] partly to vindicate his father's memory.

## Detail (Payne 1987)

The pronunciamiento of 13 September 1923 succeeded "mainly because the Liberal government did nothing to defend itself"; Alfonso XIII transferred power "without invoking constitutional limits or procedures" — the first direct military dictatorship in modern Spanish history. Framed as a 90-day emergency, the Military Directory governed 27 months; Primo denied being a dictator ("No one can with justice apply that term to me"). Achievements: the Moroccan victory (1925–27), Calvo Sotelo's tax reform ("proportionately the most significant to occur until after the end of the Franco regime"), CAMPSA, the 1920s public-works boom. Mussolini hailed him as "head of Spanish fascism" and Primo called Mussolini his teacher, but Payne classes the regime with the south/east-European military takeovers (Portugal, Poland, Greece), not fascism: no fascist mass movement, and the Unión Patriótica (claiming a million members, slogan "Monarchy, Fatherland, and Religion") remained a civic front. The 1926 corporatist labor decree (27 corporations, comités paritarios) covered only ~15% of the labor force by 1929. The 1929 Pemán constitutional draft pleased no one, and Primo — diabetic and abandoned by army and king — resigned in January 1930. Prieto's verdict, endorsed by Payne as largely true: "a dictator without corpses," later recalled nostalgically as the last "good times" before the Civil War.

## Historiography

Shlomo Ben-Ami's *Fascism from Above* poses the classificatory question; Payne treats the regime as pre-fascist authoritarian nationalism, not fascism (cf. [[concepts/payne-typology-of-authoritarian-nationalism]]).
