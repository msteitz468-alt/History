# Erwin Rommel

Field Marshal Erwin Rommel commanded Axis forces in North Africa (as commander of the Deutsches Afrikakorps / DAK and later Panzerarmee Afrika) and later Army Group B in Normandy. This page draws exclusively on logistic analysis from van Creveld's examination of his campaigns.

## Role and Commands

In North Africa (1941–1942): Initially commanded German contingent supporting Italian forces in Libya. Elevated to full General and given command of combined Panzerarmee Afrika (31 July 1941). Conducted offensive operations from Sirte toward Egypt, capturing Tobruk on 22 June 1942, then advancing to El Alamein where halted in early July 1942 and again after Alam Haifa in late August/early September 1942.

In Normandy (1944): Commanded Army Group B under OB West (Rundstedt); responsible for defending against the Allied invasion (Operation Overlord).

## Logistic Constraints in North Africa (Sirte to Alamein)

Rommel's operations were shaped by extreme supply limitations rooted in port capacity, internal distances, and fuel.

- Port capacities fundamentally limited forces that could be sustained: Tripoli was primary but distant; Benghazi closer but limited; Tobruk theoretically capable of 1,500 tons/day but "in practice rarely exceeded 600." The German navy "dismissed it as a disembarkation port for large ships" and advised relying exclusively on Tripoli and Benghazi.

- Internal lines of communication were "out of all proportion to those that the Wehrmacht had met in Europe, including Russia." Example: retreat to positions near Benghazi still left long hauls; advance beyond Tobruk required supply over hundreds of additional miles.

- Fuel consumption: "fully ten per cent of his precious fuel simply to transport the other ninety per cent." Approximately 35% of vehicles constantly out of repair due to 1,000-mile round trips. "If Panzerarmee's fuel is put at about one third of its total requirements... then it would be a reasonable guess that thirty to fifty per cent of all the fuel landed in North Africa was wasted between Tripoli and the front."

- Shipping and unloading: Even when sea losses were not decisive, "the capacity of the North African ports... restricted the size of convoys, making the business of escorting them impossibly expensive." In July–October 1941, Italians succeeded in putting "an average of 72,000 tons... across the Mediterranean in each one of the four months" — "just above Rommel’s current consumption." Difficulties "stemmed less from a dearth of supplies from Europe than from the impossible length of his line of communications inside Africa."

- 1942 "Annus Mirabilis" and collapse: After Tobruk capture, "supplies disembarked dropped from 150,000 tons to a disastrous 32,000" due to fuel shortage for navy; unloading forced back to Tripoli. "Tobruk... could handle barely 20,000" of the "100,000 tons needed each month." At start of El Alamein battle, "Rommel’s troops were down to three basic loads of fuel — instead of the thirty or so which he claimed were needed in Africa — and eight to ten of ammunition."

- Malta vs. internal factors: "the Axis decision of summer 1942 not to occupy Malta was of far less moment to the outcome of the struggle in North Africa than the fact that the port of Tobruk was so small and hopelessly exposed to the attacks of the RAF operating from Egypt." "More significant even than the above factors... were the distances that had to be overcome inside Africa."

Rommel repeatedly demanded more trucks (e.g., another 8,000) and forces (four armoured divisions for Tobruk), but these were refused as impossible given Russian front commitments and port limits. He accused Italians of inefficiency and preference for Tripoli; inter-Allied bickering intensified as situation worsened.

## 1944 Normandy Context

As commander of Army Group B, Rommel was involved in the defense against Overlord. German plans debated between coastal defense (Rommel emphasis) and mobile counterattack with Panzers held back (Rundstedt). Detailed Allied logistic planning and actual performance shaped the campaign's pace.

## Assessment from Supply Perspective

"Rommel was given all the forces that could be supported in North Africa, and more, with the result that, as late as the end of August 1942, his intelligence officer estimated that Panzerarmee was actually superior to the British in the number of tanks and heavy artillery." "To support these forces he was given a complement of motor-trucks incomparably more generous than that of any other German formation of similar size and importance."

"Given that the Wehrmacht was only partly motorized and unsupported by a really strong motor industry; that the political situation necessitated the carrying of much useless Italian ballast; that the capacity of the Libyan ports was so small, the distances to be mastered so vast; it seems clear that, for all Rommel’s tactical brilliance, the problem of supplying an Axis force for an advance into the Middle East was insoluble."

"Hitler’s original decision to send a force to defend a limited area in North Africa was correct. Rommel’s repeated defiance of his orders and attempts to advance beyond a reasonable distance from his bases, however, was mistaken and should never have been tolerated."

After the war Rommel claimed that with fraction of the resources later sent to Tunisia he could have thrown the British out of Egypt many times; the analysis notes this ignores the changed basis after Allied Torch landings and capture of French ports/shipping.

## Historiography (per this source)

Van Creveld emphasizes that Rommel's difficulties were primarily internal African logistics rather than solely the "battle of the convoys" (losses at sea decisive only perhaps Nov–Dec 1941). The "battle of the convoys" importance "is grossly exaggerated." Rommel's own demands and advances exacerbated the problem. The source stresses quantitative data on tonnages, port throughput, vehicle wastage, and fuel calculations over narrative of individual brilliance or betrayal.
