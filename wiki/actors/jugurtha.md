# Jugurtha

## Overview
Jugurtha (c. 160–104 BCE) was king of **Numidia** and Rome's antagonist in the
[[jugurthine-war-112-105bce|Jugurthine War]] (112–105 BCE). A grandson of the
Numidian king Masinissa, he was raised at the Numidian court and served with
distinction alongside Roman forces at the siege of Numantia, where — according to
the tradition preserved by Sallust — he learned both Roman military methods and
Roman venality. After the death of King Micipsa, Jugurtha seized sole power by
murdering his cousins Hiempsal and Adherbal, the latter after the sack of Cirta
(112 BCE), in which Italian traders were massacred — the act that forced Rome to
war.

## Role and Significance
Jugurtha's importance to the history of the late Republic is less military than
political. His war exposed the corruption of the Roman senatorial oligarchy: he
repeatedly bought off Roman commanders and senators, prompting his famous
reported verdict on Rome as "a city for sale, and doomed to quick destruction if
it should find a buyer." The scandal of the war's mismanagement:
- discredited the senatorial establishment and empowered the *populares* and the
  *equites* who prosecuted corrupt nobles (the Mamilian commission);
- raised [[marius|Gaius Marius]] to the consulship of 107 BCE, when he campaigned
  against the aristocratic conduct of the war and superseded his own commander
  Quintus Caecilius Metellus Numidicus;
- gave the young quaestor **Sulla** his first fame, since it was Sulla who
  negotiated Jugurtha's betrayal and capture by the Numidian-Mauretanian king
  Bocchus — a coup Sulla advertised for the rest of his life, planting the seed
  of the [[sulla|Sulla]]–[[marius|Marius]] rivalry.

Captured in 105 BCE, Jugurtha was paraded in Marius's triumph on 1 January 104
BCE and then starved to death in the Tullianum prison.

## Counterfactual Significance
Jugurtha was not a threat to Rome's survival; his lasting effect was to serve as
the occasion through which Marius rose and the Marius–Sulla antagonism began.
Without the Jugurthine scandal, Marius's meteoric ascent — and the [[mos-maiorum|norm-breaking]]
career that followed — would have needed another trigger.

## Historiography
Jugurtha is known overwhelmingly through Sallust's monograph *Bellum
Jugurthinum*, a moralizing account written a generation later that uses the war
as a vehicle for indicting senatorial corruption; its portrait of Jugurtha and
its speeches are shaped by that agenda and must be read critically. Plutarch's
lives of Marius and Sulla supply the personal rivalry material with their own
biographical bias.
