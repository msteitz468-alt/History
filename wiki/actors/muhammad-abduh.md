## Overview

Muhammad Abduh (1849–1905) was the most influential Muslim religious thinker of the nineteenth century and the systematic theologian of [[islamic-modernism]]. Egyptian by birth and formation, he was the primary Arab disciple of [[al-afghani]]; but where Afghani was peripatetic and incendiary, Abduh was institutional and pedagogical, working carefully within the structures of Islamic legal and scholarly life to demonstrate that ijtihad (independent reasoning) could produce a reformed Islam adequate to modern conditions. He eventually became Mufti of Egypt — the supreme expositor of the Sharia — and used that position to reintroduce principles of Islamic legal adaptability he found precedent for in classical sources.

Hodgson describes his comprehensive influence as "making the classical Muslim-Arab heritage, in its most distinctive aspects, respectable among Arabs with European interests as a legitimate alternative foundation for a Modern society."

## Formation and Early Career

Abduh was educated at al-Azhar, the great mosque-university of Cairo, but was transformed by Afghani's brief presence in Egypt in the 1870s. Afghani introduced him to the tradition of Falsafa (philosophical Islam) and to the urgency of Muslim political and intellectual revival. He collaborated with Afghani on the journal *al-'Urwa al-wuthqa* (Paris, 1884) — the most important Arabic periodical of Islamic Modernism. Before that, he was involved with the Urabi movement (the Egyptian nationalist uprising of 1882); after its suppression by the British, he was exiled and then forgiven, returning to Egypt to pursue reform through institutional channels.

## Theological Method

Abduh's most fundamental commitment was the attack on *taqlid* — not merely in its technical legal sense (following established juridical opinions without re-examination) but in the broader nineteenth-century sense in which it had become "a conscious rejection of all the new and culturally alien ways of the Occident." He opposed this not with Westernization but with Muslim *ijtihad*: "the free exploration, within the originally established rules of legal inquiry and the moral norms of Islam, of what was best here and now."

His intellectual debts were multiple:
- **Comte's positivism**: Abduh acknowledged the influence of Auguste Comte, whose positivism "exalted scientific objectivism even in the analysis of human culture, yet who called for a new religious system to meet a persisting human need, provided it be consistent with science." Abduh was convinced Islam — not Comte's invented religion — could fill this role.
- **Al-Ghazali**: He chose to "follow Ghazâlî in his attitude to revelation and to the Muslim community, deprecating not only legal taqlîd but the old kalâm, and relying on the continuing and more open witness of spiritually sensitive individuals." This meant a relatively open reception of modern science (assimilated to Falsafa), combined with maintained commitment to revealed faith.
- **Mu'tazilism**: He "took a Mu'tazilî view of the ability of reason to show a priori what must be the good, which was therefore incumbent on God" — a rationalist strain that his more strictly Shari-minded successors (the Salafis) would later criticize.
- **Ibn Taymiyya**: In legal reasoning he invoked *maslaha* (consideration of public welfare) as a criterion in applying *qiyas* (analogical reasoning) — drawing on Ibn Taymiyya's precedent, but in a reformist rather than a traditionalist direction.

### The Quranic Commentary

His Quranic commentary (*Tafsir al-Manar*, completed posthumously by Rashid Rida) demonstrated the possibility of "a cautious but firm reinterpretation of the sacred text in line with Modern needs." He used the old technique of *bi-lā kayf* (accepting anthropomorphic expressions without asking "how") and recognized that the meaning of textual words was a function of the Arab cultural context of the seventh century. He "found it unnecessary to introduce any radically new principles, such as a general principle of historical cultural relativity which would put in question all the imagery of the texts." This was both the achievement and the limit of his method: he preserved Islamic authenticity but also the ceiling on how far reinterpretation could go.

## Institutional Work

**Mufti of Egypt** (appointed c. 1899): As the supreme legal authority in Egypt, he "worked carefully within the context of the historical tradition, finding it sufficient to encourage all tendencies to restore the adaptability and moral concern that had characterized the law in its formative period." He invoked maslaha in courts applying Western law as well as Islamic law — "insisting on his right to ijtihad and to the use of the criterion of maslaha, despite technical precedents. Thus he Islamized even the Western law in spirit."

**Al-Azhar reform**: He attempted to reform the instruction at al-Azhar, the chief institution forming Sunni Arab ulama. He "did not succeed very far, but he introduced many of the students there to wider currents of thought" — including secular classical Arabic literature and some Western subjects. He also insisted on high-level journalism in pure classical Arabic, himself setting the example of a "simple Arabic prose which yet conformed to the ancient grammatical and lexical forms."

**Voluntary associations**: He helped renew the tradition of institutional charity disrupted by government seizure of waqfs, through modern voluntary associations — "performing in his own Muslim way rather than the British way."

**Personal moral influence**: He fought superstition and corruption, "his vigilance able, for instance, to restore, in certain departments of which he had charge, a high level of expectation of personal integrity and honesty."

## Relationship to Afghani

Abduh differed from Afghani in emphasis and temperament. Where Afghani's pan-Islamic vision was international — "revival of each Muslim nation internally as part of a general pan-Islamic movement in which the reviving nations were to co-operate" — Abduh "when he was forgiven his involvement with 'Urâbî and allowed to resume activity in Egypt, was equally zealous; but he concentrated his efforts on Egypt itself, and stressed immediate moral reforms, enlightened education, and careful reinterpretation of religious doctrine." He also liked to visit Europe "to restore his faith in mankind" — an openness Afghani shared but expressed very differently in public.

## Legacy and Divergence

Abduh's followers diverged sharply:
- **The [[salafiyya]]** proper (led by Rashid Rida): moved toward stricter Shari-mindedness, eventually aligning with Wahhabism.
- **Liberal social reformers** (Qasim Amin, Taha Husayn): emphasized women's emancipation and social Westernization, using the Arabic classical heritage as cultural foundation but reducing the specifically religious content.
- **Egyptian nationalism**: Sa'd Zaghlul, "a close disciple of Muhammad Abduh and of Afghani himself," led the nationalist Wafd party and the 1919 uprising against British occupation.

This divergence reflects both the richness and the instability of Abduh's synthesis: it was broad enough to inspire very different trajectories but specific enough that none of his successors could simply continue his project unchanged.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Abduh: the [[islamic-modernism]] movement would have lacked its systematic theological expression; al-Azhar, however imperfectly, would not have been exposed to modernist currents; the classical Arabic literary revival would have lacked its most prestigious religious sanction; and the tradition of informed ijtihad as a reformist strategy would have had no institutional foothold in Egypt. His influence shaped the entire trajectory of Arab Islamic thought through the first half of the twentieth century.
