# Mao Zedong (1893–1976)

The principal leader of the **Chinese Communist Revolution** and founder of the **People's
Republic of China** — who reunified China after a century of crisis and then convulsed it with
the **Great Leap Forward** and the **Cultural Revolution**, among the most consequential and
catastrophic of twentieth-century leaders. From Jonathan Spence's
[[spence-search-for-modern-china|*The Search for Modern China*]].

## The rural revolutionary

A Hunan peasant's son radicalized in the [[chinese-communist-revolution|May Fourth]] era, Mao
was a founding member of the **Chinese Communist Party (1921)**. After the Guomindang's bloody
**1927 purge** of the Communists shattered the urban movement, Mao — censured by the party
leadership — fled into the **Jinggang Mountains** and developed his distinctive strategy:
**peasant-based rural revolution** built in the border zones between administrative regions
(safest from a fragmented state), resting on land redistribution and a force drawn from the
"dispossessed and classless." Spence stresses Mao's **pragmatism over theory** — he abandoned
Comintern orthodoxy when local realities required, even allying with bandit chiefs. Driven from
the Jiangxi Soviet, the Communists undertook the epic **Long March (1934–35)**, during which Mao
emerged as paramount leader; from the **Yan'an** base he built a disciplined party-army and a
peasant-nationalist movement strengthened by resistance to the [[japanese-imperialism|Japanese
invasion]].

## Founder of the People's Republic

After Japan's defeat, the Communists won the **civil war (1945–49)** against
[[japanese-imperialism|Chiang Kai-shek's]] Guomindang, and on **1 October 1949** Mao proclaimed
the **People's Republic of China** — "the Chinese people have stood up." The new state imposed
land reform, allied with the USSR, fought the United States in the **Korean War (1950–53)**, and
carried out the **First Five-Year Plan** and the collectivization of agriculture.

## The catastrophes: Great Leap and Cultural Revolution

Mao's utopian radicalism then brought disaster. The **Great Leap Forward (1958–61)** — mass
mobilization into giant **people's communes** and backyard steel, driven by the faith that human
will could "vanquish all natural and technical challenges" (his people "poor and blank," a "blank
sheet" for "the newest and most beautiful words") — produced the **deadliest famine in human
history** (tens of millions dead), as cadres reported fictitious harvests and the state
over-procured grain. Sidelined after the failure, Mao struck back with the **Cultural Revolution
(1966–76)** — mobilizing the **Red Guards** in a cult-driven assault on the party establishment
and "the four olds," bringing a decade of persecution, chaos, and cultural destruction. The
**Sino-Soviet split** (from the late 1950s) and the opening to the United States (the Nixon
visit, 1972) reordered the Cold War. Mao died in **1976**; his successors purged the Gang of Four
and, under **Deng Xiaoping**, reversed his economics (see
[[chinese-communist-revolution]]).

## Counterfactual significance

Mao is the central figure of modern China's "search": he **reunified the country and ended the
century of foreign humiliation**, building a strong centralized state — and he caused, through
the Great Leap and Cultural Revolution, **human catastrophes on an immense scale**. The Chinese
Communist Party's official verdict ("70% right, 30% wrong") captures the duality. The state he
founded, transformed by Deng's reforms, became the world's second superpower.
