# Chandragupta Maurya

## Overview

**Chandragupta Maurya** (r. 321–297 BCE) founded the **[[mauryan-empire|Mauryan Empire]]**,
South Asia's first and largest ancient empire. In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Kaul,
Chs 18–19) he is the obscure-born conqueror — guided by the "wily but moralistic" minister
**Kautilya Chanakya** — who took the Magadhan kingdom "to the heights of empire."

## Rise to Power

Of "obscure origins," Chandragupta succeeded, according to tradition, through the strategy of
his preceptor **Chanakya** (Vishnugupta), the brahmana wronged by the last Nanda king and
credited with authoring the **Arthashastra**. Together they overthrew the powerful but
unpopular **Nanda** dynasty and seized **[[pataliputra|Pataliputra]]** (c. 321 BCE) — a
story dramatized in the later play **Mudrarakshasa**, with its web of spies enacting the
Arthashastra's stress on espionage.

## The Conqueror

Chandragupta defeated **Seleucus Nikator**, Alexander's successor in the northwest, gaining
**Gandhara** and a Seleucid marriage alliance. His empire stretched from **Afghanistan** to
**Gujarat**, **Bengal**, and into **Karnataka**, straddling the Vindhyas — Plutarch's
"Sandrocottus" who "overran the whole of India" with 600,000 men. He embodies the
Arthashastra's ideal of the **vijigishu**, the would-be world-conqueror.

## End

By tradition Chandragupta **abdicated**, embraced the **[[jainism|Jain]]** faith, and
traveled to Karnataka in the south at the end of his life — passing the throne to his son
**Bindusara** and, in time, his grandson **[[ashoka|Ashoka]]**.

## Significance

Chandragupta unified the subcontinent for the first time and founded the dynasty that
produced Ashoka and launched Buddhism's expansion. His career — and the Chanakya partnership —
became the archetype of empire-building and statecraft in the South Asian tradition.

## Historiography

His history is heavily **legendary**, drawn from later Sanskrit drama, Jain tradition, and
Greco-Roman chroniclers (Plutarch, the synchronism with Seleucus that anchors ancient Indian
chronology). The blurred line between the historical Chandragupta and the literary figure of
the Arthashastra's ideal king is itself a theme of CWH IV's treatment. The
**[[kautilya-arthashastra|Arthashastra]]** ascribed to his minister **[[kautilya|Kautilya]]** is now
ingested directly: it presents Chandragupta's reign as the realization of the *[[raja-mandala|vijigishu]]*
("would-be conqueror") who masters the [[kautilyan-statecraft|seven limbs of sovereignty]] — though,
on the modern dating, the text may post-date him by centuries, making it his *ideal* portrait rather
than his record.

## Avari's Account (Avari, *India: The Ancient Past*, 2007)

Avari's treatment in Chapter 6 adds several dimensions:

**Dynastic origin**: 'The term "Maurya" is derived from moriya (Skt. Mayura), or peacock. According to a Jain tradition, the founder of the empire, Chandragupta, came from a clan of peacock-tamers who owed allegiance to the kingdom of Magadha.'

**The identification by William Jones**: Until the late eighteenth century, much uncertainty surrounded who Chandragupta was. In 1793, Sir William Jones of the Asiatic Society of Bengal identified 'Sandrocottus' (known to the Greeks through Plutarch and Megasthenes) with Chandragupta, identifying the River Erannoboas as the Son (which merged into the Ganga until the fourteenth century) and Palimbothra as Pataliputra. Jones's discovery may be considered 'one of the great chronological anchors of Indian history' (Drekmeier).

**Defeat of the Nandas and succession**: 'After defeating the last of the nine Nandas of Magadha he assumed power in 321 BC. By the time his reign ended in 297 BC he had, by war, threats of war, and aggressive diplomacy, reduced the kingdoms and republics of north India to impotence.'

**Defeat of Seleukos Nicator**: In 305 BCE, Seleukos Nicator moved to reclaim Alexander's Indian heritage. Chandragupta 'confronted him with victorious outcome — and forced him to surrender the Indus basin, Baluchistan and all lands east of Kabul. Seleukos made a dignified retreat from India in 302 BC, after giving his daughter in marriage to Chandragupta, establishing diplomatic relations at the Mauryan court in Pataliputra and accepting Chandragupta's modest gift of 500 war elephants, which he later used against his western rival Antigonos.'

**Conversion to Jainism and death**: 'Chandragupta's interest in Jainism is the first significant indication of the influence that the heterodox religions were to have on the future rulers of India for the next few centuries. If a Jain tradition is to be believed, Chandragupta ended his life as a pilgrim and recluse in Karnataka, in the vicinity of Sravana Belgola, a major centre of Jain pilgrimage.'

**The [[arthashastra|Arthashastra]] relationship**: Kautalya (Chanakya) advised Chandragupta 'in the arts of war and peace.' The Arthashastra's vision of espionage-backed governance, a six-form diplomatic policy, and royal self-discipline all correspond to the Megasthenes account of Chandragupta's actual administration — corroborating the text's relevance to the early Mauryan world even though Chandragupta's name does not appear in it.

**The Megasthenes account**: The Greek ambassador Megasthenes (at court 302–298 BCE) described the municipality of Pataliputra as organised by six major committees covering arts and crafts promotion, care of foreigners, registration of births and deaths, supervision of weights and measures, quality control over manufactures, and collection of duties. Pataliputra's palisade formed 'a great oblong, 9 miles long and 1.8 miles in width. All along the palisade were 570 towers and sixty-four gates. Outside the palisade ran a ditch, 60 feet deep and 200 yards wide, serving as both defence and public sewer.' The court was considered 'more sumptuous than those of Susa and Ecbatana in Iran.'
