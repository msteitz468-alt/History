# Lelang Commandery

**Lelang** (K. *Nangnang*) was the [[han-dynasty|Han]] commandery planted in the northern Korean
Peninsula after the conquest of Ko-Choson in **108 BC**, and the **central institution of the
[[yellow-sea-interaction-sphere|Yellow Sea Interaction Sphere]]** for four centuries until
[[koguryo|Koguryo]] destroyed it in **313 AD**. For [[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes]] it is
the hinge of [[pen-insular-state-formation|Pen/Insular state formation]]: the conduit through which
Mainland administration, prestige goods and the tribute idea reached Korea's [[samhan|Samhan]] chiefs
and Japan's [[yayoi-culture|Yayoi]] chiefs.

## Ko-Choson and the Conquest

Before the conquest, **Ko-Choson** in the northern Peninsula was "a small centralized polity,
hierarchically organized," with a king, prime ministers, high ministers and generals — its kingship
seized in 196 BC by **Weiman (Wiman)**, a refugee from Yan. Han's campaign overran it in 108 BC and
established four commanderies; Lelang was the enduring one.

## Headquarters and Function

Lelang's headquarters was the **31-ha earth-walled site of Tosong-ri** on the south bank of the Taedong
River (now part of Pyongyang). 1934–35 excavations exposed brick-paved lanes, drains, wells, latrines,
building foundations, and roof tiles naming a "Lelang Ceremonial Palace"; bronze coins and inscribed
clay sealings mark its administrative-economic role, and bronzes, iron and glass were made on site.
"Lelang archaeology" became its own sub-discipline. Lelang "first fulfilling a military role, soon
acquired an economic function while also serving as a staging point for surrounding peoples wishing to
submit to the Han Court." Its *heqin* diplomacy drew Yayoi chieftains into the Han tributary order and
enticed southern Korean chiefs to act as agents extracting local goods — stimulating the southern
**iron** industry.

## Tombs and Hybrid Identity

Over **3,000 tombs** lie in the hills around the headquarters. 1st-century-BC wood-coffin tombs reflect
Iron Age Ko-Choson heritage (narrow bronze daggers, iron tools, chariot gear); after Emperor Guangwu's
reconquest (30 AD) a 1st–2nd-century "Golden Age" of Han imports follows (bronze vessels, lacquerware,
mirrors, coins, jades). The **Pujo Yegun tomb** — an indigenous Okcho leader buried with a silver seal
reading "Pujo Yegun," 62 Han artifacts and 19 local objects — shows how local elites were folded into
the commandery's territorial administration. (Because the body of a deceased Han official was returned
to his homeland, few imperial officials were actually buried at Lelang.)

## End

When Han lost its eastern grip in the late 2nd century, the **Gongsun** family of the Liaodong
governorship took over the southern Lelang area and founded the **Daifang** commandery (202–204 AD);
the **Wei** Dynasty acquired Daifang in 238. Koguryo, which had long raided the commanderies, finally
**conquered Lelang in 313 and Daifang in 314** — opening the power vacuum that the Three Kingdoms filled.

**Historiography note:** North Korean scholarship denies a Han base in the northern Peninsula and reads
the Pyongyang remains as purely Korean — a nationalist position Barnes records as contradicted by the
archaeological evidence.
