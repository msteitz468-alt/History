## Overview

Fujiwara no Michinaga (966–1028) was the head of the Northern House of the [[fujiwara-clan|Fujiwara clan]] and the dominant political figure of the Heian court for approximately three decades. "The domination of the court by the Fujiwara leader reached its height with Michinaga, especially in the years from 1016 to 1028" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 5). He was the fifth son of Fujiwara no Kaneie (929–990) and came to power through a succession dispute rather than as the expected heir, but proved to be among the most effective political operators of the [[fujiwara-regency|Fujiwara Regency]].

## Rise to Power

After his elder brother Michikane died mere days after being appointed regent in 995, the succession lay between Michinaga and his nephew Korechika (Michitaka's son). Korechika was eight years Michinaga's junior but considered senior in direct line of descent. The contest was resolved when Michinaga received *nairan* ("private inspection") regental powers — a title that gave him powers mostly identical to those of a *kampaku*, but crucially permitted direct participation in the Council of State's business and meetings, unlike the more isolated *sesshō* and *kampaku* offices.

The immediate background was Michinaga's favor with Emperor Ichijō, whose mother Senshi was Michinaga's sister. Korechika's position at court was subsequently undermined by a scandal: in 996 both Korechika and his brother Takaie were accused of shooting arrows at the carriage of a Retired Emperor (Kazan), believing the emperor to be visiting a woman they had an interest in. The court, "led by Michinaga, launched an investigation" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 70), and Korechika and Takaie were exiled. Korechika was recalled in 998 but never recovered political influence.

## Apogee: Marriage Politics at Maximum Scale

Michinaga's dominance rested on deploying marriage politics at a scale and with a success rate unmatched before or after. His strategy: place daughters as imperial consorts; ensure their sons became crown princes and then emperors; become regent as the maternal grandfather of successive emperors.

**With Emperor Ichijō** (r. 986–1011): Michinaga placed his eldest daughter Shoshi (also called Akiko; 988–1074) as consort "shortly after birth" (effectively, from 999 when she entered the palace). Ichijō already had a beloved consort, Empress Teishi (daughter of the deceased regent Michitaka), and Teishi's son was Ichijō's eldest child. Michinaga worked systematically to secure the crown prince designation for Shoshi's son instead: he gave the emperor "as a consort one of his daughters" while simultaneously maneuvering to have the existing crown prince, Atsuakira — who was descended through Michinaga's cousin — abdicate his position. Atsuakira "probably succumbing to pressure from Michinaga, renounced the crown princehood" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 71), and was replaced by Shoshi's son, who became Emperor Go-Ichijō.

**With Emperor Sanjō** (r. 1011–1016): Sanjō's relationship with Michinaga was the most difficult of his regental career. Sanjō was "less closely connected with Michinaga because his mother, Michinaga's sister, had been dead for nearly thirty years." Relations were so strained that, in the CHJ's understated language, "it seemed to several contemporaries as if this was the worst period" since Kaneie's day. Michinaga gave Sanjō a daughter as consort but also applied persistent pressure on the emperor to abdicate, citing Sanjō's failing eyesight. Sanjō finally abdicated in 1016.

**With Emperor Go-Ichijō** (r. 1016–1036): Michinaga was appointed *sesshō* on the day of Go-Ichijō's accession. Go-Ichijō was a son of Shoshi and a grandson of Michinaga — the perfect configuration for the regental system. Three years later Michinaga gave another daughter as consort to the crown prince (who would become Emperor Go-Suzaku), while his eldest son Yorimichi married still another daughter to Go-Ichijō concurrently. "A contemporary poet composed what was perhaps the most famous poem of his age to record Michinaga's reaction to this latest triumph" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 72):

> *Kono yo wo ba / waga yo to zo omou / mochizuki no / kaketaru koto mo / nashi to omoeba*
> ("This world — I think it is mine, / since the full moon / lacks nothing, / as I find it.")

At Michinaga's death in 1028, twenty-two of twenty-five senior ministers (*kugyo*) were Fujiwara, the remaining three Minamoto all closely related to him by marriage.

## Political Method: The Nairan Advantage

Michinaga preferred the *nairan* title for most of his regental career rather than the more prestigious *sesshō* or *kampaku*, precisely because *nairan* gave him freedom to exercise direct administrative leadership over the Council of State. He "appeared at court almost daily and presided over most important Council meetings. There was consequently little of importance that went on in the government of which he was not directly aware, which no doubt helps explain the extraordinary power he enjoyed as regent" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 78).

He resigned the *sesshō* title in 1020 (having held it since 1016), with his son Yorimichi succeeding him — an extremely early transition even by the standards of the period. He took Buddhist orders in 1019 following serious illness but "made a good recovery" and devoted most of the last decade of his life to the construction of the Hōjōji temple.

## The Hōjōji Temple

The Hōjōji (founded 1019, Golden Hall dedicated 1022) was Michinaga's defining architectural legacy and a statement of the wealth and piety that characterized the regency at its height. Located east of his Tsuchimikado mansion in the northeastern quarter of the capital, the site covered approximately 14 acres — the same size as the ancient East and West Temples. It contained over a dozen major halls and chapels, elaborate gardens, artificial hills, a lake, and over a hundred golden buddhas sixteen feet tall "arranged in a row with a passageway running from north to south in front of them" (CHJ Vol. 2, pp. 100–101, quoting a contemporary source).

The construction was a spectacle of coordinated labor. Daily levies of 500 to 1,000 men came from the sustenance households and private estates of Michinaga's male connections and imperial family members; provincial governors competed to provide labor, timber, cypress bark, and tiles, "even at the cost of falling behind with their rental taxes and tribute commodities." Master joiners, image-carvers (a crew of 100), carpenters (200–300 raising beams), plasterers, roofers, excavators, and artisans from near and far participated.

The dedication of the Golden Hall was attended by Go-Ichijō. Some of the foundation stones of the long-since-collapsed Rampart Gate (the ancient southern gateway of the Heian capital) were incorporated into the temple's construction — "a bit of ironical symbolism that even Michinaga may have appreciated" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 101).

## Role and Income

Michinaga's income came from several streams that together constituted an extraordinary private accumulation:
- **Official emoluments**: rank and office stipends
- **Shoen**: the largest holder of privately commended estates in the country; though a contemporary's claim that "there was not a needle's breadth of land in the country that did not belong to him" was hyperbolic, and most commendations "appear to have been largely fictitious arrangements," the shoen portfolio was vast
- **Provincial governor gifts**: "Michinaga received a nearly continuous stream of horses and oxen from provincial officials," and much of the financial responsibility for rebuilding his Tsuchimikado mansion and constructing Hōjōji was assigned to provincial governors. The governor of Iyo, Minamoto no Yorimitsu (948–1021), "supplied all the furnishings for the reconstructed Tsuchimikado mansion, which were so fabulously extravagant that they left even the wealthiest nobles agog" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 79)

## Ritual-Ceremonial School

Michinaga was also the founder of the "Mido school" of ritual and ceremony — a "mixture of Ononomiya and Kujo elements with Michinaga's own particular contributions" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 100). This blended lineage derived from Fujiwara no Mototsune's ritual observances, which had descended through his sons Saneyori (Ononomiya school) and Morosuke (Kujo school). Ritual-ceremonial expertise was central to courtly success; "when a man was called learned and able, it often had to do with his knowledge of ritual and ceremony."

## Counterfactual Significance

Michinaga's dominance was simultaneously the apex and the structural limit of the [[sekkan-system|sekkan system]]. His success depended on an improbable confluence: the early deaths of his elder brothers, the exile of his nephew Korechika, the willingness of multiple emperors to accept Fujiwara consorts in preference to others, and extraordinary personal political skill. His son Yorimichi inherited the regency and the shoen holdings but not his father's ability to compel marital arrangements — and without those arrangements, the structural foundation of the regency collapsed within a generation. Without Michinaga, the sekkan period would likely have ended earlier; with Michinaga, it peaked in a form that made the subsequent reassertion of imperial power through [[insei]] appear not as a revolution but as a restoration.

## Primary Source

Michinaga's diary, *Mido kampaku ki*, survives in large part and has been translated by Francine Hérail as *Notes journalières de Fujiwara no Michinaga, ministre à la cour de Heian* (two volumes). His is among the best-documented careers of any Heian figure, and the diary is a primary source of the first order for the political and cultural history of the period.

## Historiography

Michinaga is sometimes treated as emblematic of the entire Fujiwara era, which can distort understanding of the regency as an institution. The CHJ's treatment emphasizes that he was an exceptional figure within a system that both constrained and enabled him: "not even the most powerful regent was able to govern autocratically in complete disregard of the emperor and the Council of State" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 75). His preference for the *nairan* title over the more traditional *sesshō*/*kampaku* positions suggests a deliberate effort to avoid the isolation and honorific drift that threatened to hollow out regental power.
