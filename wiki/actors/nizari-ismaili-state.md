## Overview

The Nizari Ismaili state was an independent polity established by the Nizari branch of Ismaili Shii Muslims, centered on the mountain fortress of Alamut (in the Daylami mountains north of Qazvin in northern Iran) and lasting from 1090 until its destruction by the Mongols in 1256. It was the most coherent and sustained challenge to the [[military-patronage-state]] system of the [[ayan-amir-society]], and the movement known to Crusader history as the "Order of Assassins." See [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v2-1974]].

## Origins and Founding

The Nizari state grew out of a schism within the Fatimid Ismaili movement. On the death of the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir (1094), the Fatimid vizier Badr al-Jamali's son substituted a younger, more compliant son as caliph, excluding the designated heir **Nizar**. The Ismailis of the Seljuk domains — already effectively autonomous under their own leadership centered on the da'i of Isfahan — "insisted on Nizar's rights as imam and then (after his death) on the rights of some one — which one was not yet revealed — of his descendants." They thus became the **Nizaris**, formally independent of Cairo.

The physical beginning of the state was the seizure of the **fortress of Alamut** in 1090 by the da'i Hasan-i Sabbah, followed by the capture of various towns in Quhistan (mountainous region south of Khurasan). From 1092, after the death of Malikshah triggered Seljuk fragmentation, the Nizaris broke into "full-scale revolt throughout Iran and the Iraq, and later in Syria as well."

## Methods

The Nizari revolt was "not carried out (as in the Fatimid or Abbasid risings) through a major army aiming at overthrowing the central government but, in a spirit appropriate to the regime of the many amirs, through a piecemeal seizure of strongholds and through the elimination of individual amirs (or politically minded 'ulama') by assassination when they proved personally dangerous."

Hodgson emphasizes that the assassination technique was strategic, not random: "The assassinations were aimed at single prominent enemies who caused them special damage (or at turncoats) and were seemingly calculated to avoid bloodshed among ordinary people, whose champions, in the name of justice, the Ismailis felt themselves to be — for the elimination of one man in time could obviate a bloody battle." Nizari youths "gladly sacrificed their lives in such acts (necessarily without recourse to debilitating hashish as the modern legend would have it!)."

The technique exploited the key feature of the [[ayan-amir-society]]: that everything depended on the prestige of individuals rather than of bureaucratic office. The assassinations proved "frightening to all" precisely because individual notables were the pivots of local stability.

## Ideological Challenge

The Nizari state represented a principled rejection of the entire settlement of jama'i-Sunni amirs and 'ulama: "They attempted to introduce a religious reform precisely in opposition to the whole pattern of Jama'i-Sunni amirs and 'ulama alike." The Ismaili doctrine of the imam provided an alternative political ideal — a legitimate authority grounded in the inspired imamate rather than in military power sanctioned by a symbolic caliphate.

The Nizari claim was that the outer law (Shari'ah) was only meaningful through the inner sense (batin) revealed by the imam. Without access to the living imam, Shari'ah observance was, at best, provisional. This made any pledge of allegiance to a Seljuk sultan "ambivalent" — which was why the Sunni 'ulama insisted the Nizaris could not be accommodated as just another iqta'-holding group within the [[military-patronage-state]] framework.

## The Revolt and Its Failure

The Nizaris initially found support "especially among Shiis, in some rural areas, in most towns, and even in the lower ranks of the armies." Their revolutionary moment came from the popular discontent with Turkic rule. But they overplayed their challenge. The frank policy of assassination "proved frightening to all," and enemies spread horror tales that won popular credence. "The 'ulama carried not only the military decision but the mind of the people. Few might care about the Seljuk state, but the unity of the Muslims proved a decisive political value."

The sultan Muhammad Tapar (1104–18) led systematic campaigns against Nizari strongholds. The critical moment was the fall of the Nizari fortress just outside Isfahan (1107), held by a da'i who had argued — remarkably — that he should be recognized as a proper Seljuk amir holding his own iqta', provided he paid tribute to the sultan. The Sunni 'ulama rejected this: Nizari acceptance of an "inner sense" superseding the outer Shari'ah made any such accommodation impossible.

## Survival and Character

Despite Seljuk pressure, the Nizaris survived in several mountain refuges:

- **Daylaman and Quhistan** (northern Iran): Where they had successfully identified their cause with local independence from outside interference
- **Syrian mountains** north of the Lebanon: Established after their failure to hold Aleppo and other Syrian cities

These scattered, seemingly defenseless districts formed "a single state, marked for a century and a half by outstanding solidarity and stability as well as independent local spirit." Unlike the Fatimid state in Egypt (whose mercenary army became the occasion of decay) and unlike the Turkic amirs around them, the Nizari state was "based on the local militias that had established it; and it seems to have retained its vigour to the end" — until the Mongol destruction of Alamut in 1256.

Hodgson notes the Nizari state paradoxically expressed the logic of the [[military-patronage-state]] era even while opposing it: "in the decentralized political mood of the time, in which the central bureaucracy was losing control of the iqta' assignments anyway, the Ismailis were often able to gain a temporary advantage by helping one amir against another."

## Legacy

The Nizari revolt's failure had ambiguous effects on subsequent Islam:

- It "discredited the Shii opposition generally, and assured the allegiance of moderate men, even of Twelver Shiis, to the amirs and the Jama'i-Sunni society they maintained"
- The Ismaili doctrine "helped to form the intellectual synthesis of the great jama'i-Sunni, Ghazali, who in turn helped Sunnism to find itself in the new age"
- Imaginatively, the Nizari movement "became the stimulus for numerous legends" of devoted men free from conventional restrictions — legends that circulated even among Sunnis

The Nizari state survived the Seljuk campaigns and continued as an independent polity until the Mongol onslaught of 1256. The Nizari Imamate continued after Alamut's fall; the contemporary Aga Khan is the hereditary imam of this tradition (now centered among the Khojas of India and East Africa).
