# Theodore of Tarsus

**Theodore of Tarsus** (602 – September 19, 690 CE; Archbishop of Canterbury 669–690 CE) was a Byzantine-born Greek scholar who served as the eighth Archbishop of Canterbury. Sent by Rome to rebuild the English Church, Theodore undertook a comprehensive reorganization of the diocesan structure, established the first national councils, and introduced a high level of classical and biblical education to England, transforming the fragmented missionary outposts into a unified national church.

## Background and Appointment

Theodore was born in Tarsus (Cilicia, modern Turkey) and educated in the Greek intellectual traditions of Athens and Antioch. Fleeing the Sasanian and Arab invasions of the Eastern Roman Empire, he moved to Rome, where he became a monk and an advisor to the Pope on Eastern theology (playing a role in the anti-Monothelite Lateran Council of 649, see [[monothelite-controversy|Monothelitism]]).

In 667, the Archbishop-elect of Canterbury died of plague in Rome. Pope Vitalian selected the 66-year-old Theodore as replacement, accompanied by the African scholar **Hadrian**, to ensure that Theodore did not introduce any Greek theological eccentricities to England. They arrived in Canterbury in 669.

## Ecclesiastical Reforms and Organization

Upon arrival, Theodore found the English Church in disarray, with several vacant sees and no national organization. He undertook a systematic visitation of the kingdoms:
- **Diocesan Division**: Theodore divided the massive, kingdom-sized bishoprics (such as Northumbria and Mercia) into smaller, manageable dioceses based on tribal boundaries. This decentralization was opposed by powerful bishops, notably **Wilfrid of York**, whose vast Northumbrian diocese was partitioned.
- **National Synods**: In 672/3 CE, Theodore convoked the **Council of Hertford**, the first national council of all Anglo-Saxon bishops. The council passed ten canons, regulating the power of bishops, the stability of clergy, the calculation of Easter, and the prohibition of divorce.
- **Theological Concord**: In 679/80 CE, he held the **Council of Hatfield**, which confirmed the English Church's orthodoxy, rejecting Monothelitism and accepting the decrees of the Lateran Council.

## The School of Canterbury

Theodore and Hadrian established a famous school at Canterbury, which became the leading intellectual center of Anglo-Saxon England.
- **Curriculum**: The school taught Greek, Latin, biblical exegesis, Roman law, metric poetry, astronomy, and computation.
- **Legacy**: The school educated a generation of scholars, including Aldhelm of Malmesbury, and established the intellectual foundation that enabled the writings of **Bede** at Wearmouth-Jarrow.

## Historical Significance

Theodore is regarded as one of the most successful administrators in English history. By organizing the church on a national basis, transcending the borders of the rival Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, his reforms created the first national institution in England. This unified ecclesiastical structure preceded and directly contributed to the political unification of the English kingdoms under a single monarchy centuries later.
