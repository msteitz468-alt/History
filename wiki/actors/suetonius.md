# Suetonius (Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus)

Roman scholar, equestrian courtier and biographer (c. 70–after 130 CE), author of the
*[[suetonius-lives-of-the-caesars|Lives of the Caesars]]* — the biographies of Julius Caesar and the
first eleven emperors that are, with **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]**, our principal literary source for the
early Roman Empire, and the model of imperial biography for all later European writing about rulers.

## Life

Of an **equestrian** family (perhaps from Hippo Regius in North Africa), Suetonius trained in the
rhetorical schools and entered public life under the patronage of **Pliny the Younger**, who secured
him a (declined) military tribunate and other favours. He rose instead as a learned **court
bureaucrat**, holding a succession of palace secretaryships — *a bibliothecis* (the imperial
libraries), *a studiis*, and finally ***ab epistulis*** (in charge of the emperor's correspondence)
under **Hadrian** — posts that gave him **privileged access to the imperial archives**. He dedicated
the *Lives* to the praetorian prefect Septicius Clarus; according to the later *Life of Hadrian*, both
men were **dismissed (c. 122)** for some disrespect toward the empress Sabina. He was a prodigiously
learned antiquarian, author of *On Illustrious Men* (lives of literary figures) and many lost works on
Roman games, customs, terms and "bodily defects."

## Method

Suetonius is the great practitioner of **biography organized by topic (*per species*) rather than
chronology**. Each Life typically proceeds by rubric — ancestry and birth (with omens), public career
to accession, then a thematic catalogue of the emperor's appearance, character, virtues and **vices**,
building toward the manner of his death. He explicitly rejects narrative order (*Aug.* 9) so that
each ruler's qualities "may be more clearly perceived and assessed." The result is a fund of vivid
**anecdote and gossip** — Caligula's horse, Nero fiddling as Rome burns — unreliable as chronology but
revealing of Roman debates about how imperial power should be exercised.

## Significance

Suetonius created the **moral typology of the emperor**: later rulers might aspire to be "another
Augustus or Titus" and dread being "another Caligula or Nero." Where Tacitus anatomizes the corruption
of the system, Suetonius assembles the man trait by trait. His twelve Lives became the template for
medieval and Renaissance "mirrors of princes" and for Einhard's *Life of Charlemagne*; Robert Graves's
*I, Claudius* descends directly from him.
