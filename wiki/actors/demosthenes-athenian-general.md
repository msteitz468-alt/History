# Demosthenes (Athenian General)

> **Disambiguation:** this page covers the **fifth-century Athenian general** (fl.
> 425–413 BCE, the Pylos/Sphacteria and Sicilian-relief strategist). He is a **distinct
> individual** from the fourth-century Athenian orator and statesman Demosthenes,
> chief opponent of [[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II]], covered at
> [[demosthenes-orator]]. Ancient and modern sources sometimes use the bare name
> "Demosthenes" for either man; do not conflate them.

## Overview

**Demosthenes** was an innovative Athenian general who, together with Hippocrates, devised and executed the bold offensive operations of 424 against Megara and Boeotia. His plans marked a clear departure from strict Periclean strategy toward the use of permanent fortified bases in enemy territory and coordinated multi-front attacks.

## Actions in 425–424 (Pylos and new strategy from Kagan range)

- **Pylos and Sphacteria (425)**: Key planner of the landing and light-troop tactics on Sphacteria. “Demosthenes divided his troops into companies of 200 who seized all the high places on the island... The key to the strategy was the use of light-armed troops, for they ‘were the most difficult to fight, since they fought at a distance with arrows, javelins, stones and slings... Such was the plan with which Demosthenes first conceived the landing’” (4.32.4). Messenian Comon’s flanking path completed encirclement; Spartans surrendered after truce. “the most unexpected event in the war” per Greeks. Cleon bore political responsibility; Demosthenes the technical execution.

- **Megara assault**: With Hippocrates, Demosthenes planned to seize the long walls to Nisaea at night using Megarian democratic conspirators who would open the gates. The plot involved a three-pronged approach with hidden troops. Brasidas' timely arrival and Megarian hesitation foiled full success; Athens retained a garrison at Nisaea but the city remained Spartan-aligned under restored oligarchs.

- **Boeotian plan**: Demosthenes was to land at Siphae (port of Thespis) and Chaeronea with allied troops from the northwest to betray those sites to Athens simultaneously with Hippocrates seizing Delium. The goal was to create fortresses on Boeotia's borders and perhaps trigger anti-Theban democratic revolts. Secrecy was paramount but traitors betrayed the plan. Demosthenes arrived too early at Siphae; the western prong failed.

- **Delium contribution**: The plan involved a large Athenian force at Delium to build a fort while deterring Boeotian response. Though the main battle occurred after construction, the campaign as a whole was part of the new aggressive posture.

Kagan notes: "In this less optimistic form the plan would be part of the new strategy that placed permanent fortified bases in enemy territory, which was already working so well in Laconia."

## Sicilian Expedition relief force and destruction (413 BCE)

In 413, after Athenian reverses in Sicily, Demosthenes was chosen (with Eurymedon) to lead the main relief armada. He arrived with a theatrically impressive force of 73 ships and nearly 5,000 hoplites plus light troops. Immediately recognizing that delay had been fatal, he sought to exploit the panic caused by his arrival: “Knowing that at that moment he was most frightening to the enemy, he wanted as swiftly as possible to take advantage of their present panic” (7.42.3). He planned a direct night assault on the Syracusan counterwall on Epipolae.

The attack initially succeeded — Euryalus fort surprised, counterwall sections torn down — but pursuit in moonlight produced fatal confusion among the Athenians. Unfamiliarity with the plateau, indistinguishable paeans (Dorian allies on both sides), and a Boeotian rally turned advance into rout. “they attacked one another in many different parts of the battlefield, friends against friends and citizens against fellow citizens” (7.44.8). Two to two-and-a-half thousand killed — the greatest single disaster yet for Athens in the war. Demosthenes then advocated immediate withdrawal while sea control lasted.

Outvoted by Nicias (supported by the temporary generals), he remained. After the final harbor defeat and morale collapse, he commanded one division of the land retreat. His force was trapped and surrendered on terms near the Cacyparis; he was prevented from suicide. Both he and Nicias were executed at Syracuse.

## Significance

Demosthenes exemplified the post-Periclean willingness to take risks for decisive gains. The failure at Megara and Delium (heavy Athenian losses of nearly 1,000 hoplites) discredited the aggressive faction temporarily and contributed to the mood for peace. His approach influenced later operations. In Sicily his bold initiative came too late to reverse Nicias’ errors; his plan for rapid action was correct but the window had closed. Kagan presents him as the clear-sighted professional whose counsel was ignored at the decisive moments.
