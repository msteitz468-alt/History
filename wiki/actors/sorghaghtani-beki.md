# Sorghaghtani Beki

## Overview

**Sorghaghtani Beki** (Sorkhokhtani; c. 1190–1252), a Kereyid princess—niece of [[togrul-ong-khan|Ong Khan]]—and widow of [[tolui|Tolui]], was among the most consequential political actors of the mid-thirteenth-century Mongol world. Weatherford’s “Warring Queens” chapter centers her as the hinge between civil-war regencies and Toluid restoration. The area where [[ogedei-khan|Ögödei]] later built [[karakorum|Karakorum]] had belonged to the Kereyid and to Ong Khan’s family, including her.

After Tolui’s death (~1232), Ögödei tried to annex Tolui’s lands by marrying her to Güyük; she refused, insisting her four sons needed undivided attention—Weatherford: “a decision that later proved one of the most important in the history of the empire.” Despite rivalry with Ögödei she ruled northern China and eastern Mongolia, including the family homeland where Genghis had grown up.

## Role (Weatherford 2004)

- **Against Güyük.** After refusing remarriage, she faced Güyük’s inquiry into Tolui’s estate and orders to surrender warriors assigned to her and her sons; she secretly warned Batu of Güyük’s planned surprise attack.
- **Engineering Möngke’s election.** After Güyük’s death, widow Oghul Ghaimish took control as regent but lacked [[toregene-khatun|Töregene]]’s skills; her own sons set up rival courts. Sorghaghtani, with four capable sons and lifetime preparation, moved with Batu’s alliance. At her instigation Batu called a 1250 *khuriltai* near Lake Issykul (Tian Shan), outside Mongolia; it elected her eldest son [[mongke-khan|Möngke]], but the Ögödei family boycotted (demanding Mongolia/Karakorum). Second plan: *khuriltai* on the sacred family homeland (birth, election, and burial of Genghis), which she controlled; Batu sent a 30,000-man bodyguard under brother Berke; on 1 July 1251, age-43 Möngke was proclaimed Grand Khan.
- **Purge of rival women.** While Möngke tried men, Weatherford reports she ordered the arrest of Oghul Ghaimish; captors sewed rawhide around hands, stripped her for ridicule, wrapped her in felt and drowned her and another senior woman; a third was wrapped in a blanket and kicked to death—full-fidelity atrocity record from the claims layer.
- **Culmination.** Celebration of Möngke’s election marked “the culmination of Sorkhokhtani’s lifework.” Her four sons each became khans: Möngke, Ariq Böke, and Khubilai as Great Khan variously; Hülegü as Ilkhan of Persia and dynasty founder—conquests spanning Persia, Baghdad, Syria, Turkey, Sung China, and mainland SE Asia; destruction of the Assassins and execution of the caliph (see [[sack-of-baghdad-1258]], [[hulegu]], [[kublai-khan]]).
- **Death.** Near Mongol New Year festival, February 1252 (end of Pig / start of Rat year). The decade of ruling women begun 1241 with Töregene’s regency ended; Weatherford credits those women with bringing outside talent, monasteries, schools, books, and exchange of ideas—**as his thesis on female administration**, not as uncontested wiki fact.

## Historiography

European and Persian observers (Rubruck, Rashid al-Din) singled her out for intelligence and statecraft. Weatherford (2004) is the main popular English synthesis for the “warring queens” arc; specialist care needed separating political achievement from his broader modernity framing. Christian Kereyid background is repeatedly noted in court religion discussions under Möngke.
