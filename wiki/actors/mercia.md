# Mercia (Anglo-Saxon kingdom)

## Overview

**Mercia** was the dominant Anglo-Saxon kingdom of the eighth and early ninth centuries,
based in the middle Trent valley (Tamworth, Lichfield, Repton) and exercising hegemony over
most of England south of the Humber. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Keynes, Ch. 2a)
the **Mercian supremacy** under [[æthelbald-of-mercia|Æthelbald]] (716–757) and
[[offa-of-mercia|Offa]] (757–796) is the central thread of southern English political history
before the Viking age.

## The Tribal Hidage and Mercian Hegemony

The clearest window onto Mercian power is the **Tribal Hidage**, a survey of the lands south of
the Humber listing some thirty-four peoples and their assessments in **hides**. Beginning with the
Mercian heartland (30,000 hides) and sweeping through the East Angles, East Saxons, Kent, the South
Saxons, and the West Saxons (100,000 hides), it preserves an "extraordinary impression of the
number of different peoples" still distinguishable in central England. Keynes uses it as an
**antidote to the received view of the "Heptarchy"**: whether it is a Mercian tribute-list or a
scholar's compilation, it shows both the reach of Mercian overlordship and the underlying
fragmentation of the English peoples.

## Supremacy and Decline

Under Æthelbald and Offa, Mercia absorbed or dominated the Hwicce, Kent, Sussex, Essex (with London),
and East Anglia, built **Offa's Dyke** against the Welsh, and issued a reformed silver coinage. The
supremacy continued under [[coenwulf-of-mercia|Coenwulf]] (796–821) but collapsed thereafter: the West
Saxon king [[ecgberht-of-wessex|Ecgberht]] defeated Mercia at the
[[battle-of-ellendun-825|Battle of Ellendun (825)]], and the **Viking Great Army** overran and partitioned eastern
Mercia in the 870s. Western "English Mercia" survived under ealdormen allied to Wessex and was finally
absorbed into the emerging kingdom of the English (918).

## Significance

Mercia's hegemony was the most sustained attempt to dominate southern England before the West Saxon
ascendancy. Its collapse cleared the way for Wessex and Alfred the Great to become the nucleus of a
unified English kingdom.

## Historiography

Keynes (NCMH II) warns against two "deeply engrained" but anachronistic constructs — the
**"Heptarchy"** (a 16th-century rationalisation of Bede) and the **"Bretwalda"** (an overlordship title
extrapolated from Bede's list of seven kings). Evidence is radically uneven: charters and law-codes
survive for Kent and Wessex but not for Northumbria or East Anglia, so the relative obscurity of some
kingdoms reflects the accidents of preservation, not their real importance.
