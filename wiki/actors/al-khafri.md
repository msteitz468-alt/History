# Shams al-Dīn al-Khafrī

## Overview

Shams al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad al-Khafrī (d. 1550 CE) was the most prolific Islamic astronomer of the sixteenth century and one of the most mathematically sophisticated figures in the entire hay'a tradition (see [[ilm-al-hayya]]). He was simultaneously a renowned religious scholar and, at a point in his career, the official Shiʿite jurist (*mujtahid*) of Safavid Iran. Khafrī exemplifies Saliba's argument that scientific and religious authority were deeply integrated in Islamic civilization rather than in conflict (see [[science-and-religion-in-islam]]).

## Astronomical Work

### *Al-Takmila fī Sharḥ al-Tadhkira*

Khafrī's major astronomical work was *al-Takmila fī Sharḥ al-Tadhkira* ("Completing the Commentary on the Tadhkira") — a commentary on [[nasir-al-din-tusi|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]]'s foundational *al-Tadhkira fī ʿilm al-hayʾa*. His biographers — who wrote primarily about his religious stature — mention this astronomical work only marginally, a fact that itself illustrates the integrated world in which he operated.

> "This brilliant astronomer was also a renowned religious scholar in his own right. His biographers, who speak of him mainly as a religious scholar, only marginally mention his most famous astronomical work al-Takmila fī Sharḥ al-Tadhkira." — Saliba, *Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance*, ch. 5

### Mathematics as the Language of Science

Khafrī's *Takmila* represents "the best examples of the use of mathematics as a language of science" (Saliba). He produced four mathematically equivalent models for Mercury's motion — demonstrating that the same observational phenomena could be described by different but equally valid mathematical structures, and thereby showing that the relationship between mathematical models and physical reality was more complex than simple one-to-one correspondence.

### Near-Definition of Limits and Acceleration

In analyzing the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī Couple]] and the linear motion it produces, Khafrī observed that the point of tangency moving along the diameter of the larger circle does not move at constant speed, even though the circular motion causing it is uniform. The speed of the linear component varies across the diameter. In pursuing this observation, his analysis "came very close to defining the concepts of limits and of acceleration, but did not do so in the strict sense" (Saliba). He concluded that what the Couple produces is not the same as the uniform linear motion Aristotle discussed, and therefore cannot be used directly to disprove the Aristotelian principle — but the mathematical investigation itself approached the threshold of early calculus concepts.

This represents the furthest extension within the Islamic hay'a tradition of the philosophical consequences first drawn by [[qutb-al-din-al-shirazi|Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī]] from Ṭūsī's theorem.

## Religious Career

Khafrī was not merely a religious scholar in a secondary sense. In the early Safavid period, before the arrival of the Lebanese jurist al-Muḥaqqiq ʿAlī b. al-Ḥusain al-ʿĀmilī (d. 1553) in Iran, Khafrī fulfilled the function of the official Shiʿite jurist, issuing juridical opinions (*fatwas*) on matters of Shiʿi doctrine. This was among the highest religious-legal offices in the emerging Safavid state, whose Twelver Shiʿism was being actively institutionalized in this period.

> "At one point in his career he apparently fulfilled the function of the official Shiʿite jurist in Safavid Iran. The same biographers also report his issuing juridical opinions (fatwas) on matters pertaining to the Shiʿī doctrines before the arrival of al-Muḥaqqiq ʿAlī b. al-Ḥusain al-ʿĀmilī (d. 1553) from Lebanon." — Saliba, ch. 5

## Significance

Khafrī stands at the chronological end of the classical Islamic hay'a tradition — the sixteenth century, contemporary with Copernicus (*De Revolutionibus*, 1543) — and he represents that tradition at its mathematical peak. His work demonstrates that Islamic theoretical astronomy was still producing sophisticated original contributions at precisely the moment when European astronomy was beginning to incorporate the mathematical tools developed within that tradition. The question of whether Copernicus's use of the Ṭūsī Couple and related Islamic mathematical devices represents direct transmission from scholars like Khafrī and his contemporaries remains an active research question.

His dual career — cutting-edge astronomer and highest religious jurist — is Saliba's clearest single instance of the claim that scientific and religious excellence were not only compatible in Islamic civilization but were sometimes found in the same individual simultaneously.
