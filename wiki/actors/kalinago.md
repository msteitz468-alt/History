## Overview

The **Kalinago** (also called Island Caribs in colonial sources, and sometimes simply "Carib") are the indigenous people of the Windward Islands of the Lesser Antilles at the time of European contact. Their identity and origins are among the most contested topics in Caribbean archaeology and history.

## Name and Colonial Misrepresentation

The name "Carib" entered the historical record through Columbus's diario (1492), where it appears in at least four distinct senses: the people of the Grand Khan (Caniba), mythological beings, an island of men, and a general term for enemies. Columbus's usage was almost certainly not a reference to the Kalinago people, but the term was applied to them retroactively by Spanish colonists and became fixed in European discourse. The colonial-era binary of "peaceful Arawak vs. cannibal Carib" is now largely rejected by archaeologists as a colonial projection.

The people call themselves **Kalinago** (singular: Kalinago; the term "Island Carib" is a colonial exonym).

## Origins

Kalinago origins are debated. The dominant colonial-era model held that Kalinago were South American mainlanders who invaded the Lesser Antilles shortly before European contact, displacing or killing earlier Saladoid/Suazoid populations. Archaeological evidence partially supports South American connections in material culture (particularly in the southern Lesser Antilles) but does not support the "recent invasion" model for the northern Windward Islands. The Kalinago ceramic tradition (Suazoid) shows continuity with earlier island traditions. Keegan and Hofman (2017) argue for in-place development with ongoing mainland exchange rather than wholesale population replacement.

## Archaeological Evidence

The **Argyle site** on St. Vincent provides the most detailed Kalinago-period archaeology. Kalinago ceramics and settlement patterns show both continuity with earlier Lesser Antillean traditions and distinctive features associated with mainland connections.

## Survival

The Kalinago survived European colonisation longer than most Caribbean indigenous peoples due to the defensive terrain of the Windward Islands and their military resistance. A Kalinago community survives today on Dominica (Kalinago Territory).

## Historiography

Colonial historiography portrayed the Kalinago as fierce cannibals who displaced peaceful Taíno/Arawak populations. Modern archaeology substantially revises this: the archaeological record for "Carib raids" and Kalinago replacement of earlier populations is weak or absent in most of the northern Windward Islands, though South American connections in the southern Windwards are real.

**Key controversy:** The origins of the Kalinago and the validity of "Carib raids" displacing Taíno populations — colonial accounts of Carib cannibalism are now largely treated as European construction; the archaeological record for actual Kalinago replacement of earlier island populations is weak or absent in most of the northern Windward Islands.
