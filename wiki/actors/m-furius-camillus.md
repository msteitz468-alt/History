# M. Furius Camillus

## Overview

**Marcus Furius Camillus** is the dominant Roman figure of the period 396–367 BCE
and the most celebrated general of the early Republic. He is credited with five
dictatorships (an unprecedented number), the capture of Veii, the rescue of Rome
from the Gauls, and the reconciliation of the Licinian-Sextian constitutional
settlement. The Roman tradition hailed him as the **"second founder of Rome"** and
*pater patriae* (Father of the Fatherland).

Cornell's analysis in CAH VII.2 provides the most rigorous modern assessment: Camillus
was a **genuine historical figure** whose real career was very substantial, but whose
legendary dimensions have grown to the point where separating historical core from
accretion requires sustained source-critical work. The famous "rescue from the
Gauls" scene is legendary; the Veii capture is genuine.

## Historical Core

### The Capture of Veii (396 BCE)
The most firmly attested act of Camillus's career is his command as dictator at the
**[[capture-of-veii-396bce|fall of Veii]]**. He was appointed dictator for the final
assault, conducted the *evocatio* ceremony to transfer Juno Regina to Rome, and
oversaw the distribution of booty and the eventual settlement of the ager Veientanus.

The tradition also records that he **dedicated a tithe of the Veii booty to Apollo
at Delphi** — an action confirmed by a story in later sources about the Romans
sending a golden bowl to Delphi. Whether this specific dedication occurred is uncertain,
but it reflects the scale of the victory.

One dark note in the tradition: Camillus's **first exile** is associated with the
Veii episode — he allegedly took a disproportionate share of the booty (bronze doors
from Veii) or failed to distribute the ager Veientanus fairly. He was condemned in
absentia and went into exile at Ardea. This tradition, though possibly embellished,
suggests that the actual distribution of Veii's spoils was politically contested.

### The Wars of the 380s–360s BCE
After the Gallic Sack, Camillus appears in the tradition as the commanding figure
in Rome's military recovery: campaigns against the Volsci, the Latins (Tusculum),
various Etruscan cities, and the Gauls themselves. Some of these campaigns are
plausible; others may be duplications or inventions. His role as the stabilizing
military force of the generation after 390 is broadly credible even if specific
engagements cannot be verified.

He held his dictatorships in:
- **396 BCE**: Capture of Veii
- **390 BCE** (or shortly after): Traditional date of the "rescue" from the Gauls
- **389 BCE**: Campaign against the Volsci and Aequi
- **368 BCE**: Operations during the Licinian-Sextian period
- **367 BCE**: Final settlement of the Licinian-Sextian laws (traditional)

Five dictatorships is extraordinary — no other Roman held more than two. Cornell
accepts the number as probably genuine but notes that the specific contexts assigned
to some of them are likely embellished.

### The Licinian-Sextian Settlement (367 BCE)
The tradition holds that Camillus reconciled the Licinian-Sextian constitutional
settlement by **dedicating a temple to Concord** in the Forum, symbolizing the
resolution of the Struggle of the Orders. He is presented as a patrician statesman
who supported the plebeian constitutional gains — a remarkable tradition for a member
of the most conservative political class.

Cornell accepts that Camillus was active in the political events of the late 360s
but notes that the temple of Concord dedication may be a later retrojection (the
temple's archaeological evidence is Augustan-era at earliest for the form we see).
His role as reconciler of the orders reflects a tradition of emphasizing his
trans-partisan greatness that was probably developed in the later Republic.

## The Legend: The Rescue from the Gauls

The most famous scene in the Camillus tradition: at the moment when the Romans were
paying the ransom to the Gauls, Camillus arrives from Ardea with a newly raised army,
refuses to allow the gold to be weighed, defeats the Gauls, and recovers the gold.
His reported exchange: *"Not with gold but with iron shall Rome be ransomed"*.

**Cornell's verdict**: this story is **entirely legendary** and should be rejected.
The Romans paid the ransom; Brennus received the gold. The Camillus rescue narrative
was invented to transform a humiliating ransom into a Roman triumph. Its function
is psychological and ideological — it effaced the reality of Roman weakness and
Gallic superiority at that moment.

The story's structural implausibility: Camillus had been in exile at Ardea, a small
Latin town. Raising a substantial army there without Roman authorization, in the middle
of a military crisis, is constitutionally and logistically impossible. The tradition
is aware of this and gets around it by having Camillus appointed dictator during the
crisis — a device that highlights the fictional character of the whole episode.

## Role and Significance

**Military.** Camillus was Rome's most effective commander of the generation that
established Roman dominance in central Italy. The wars against the Volsci, the Aequi,
the Etruscans (Tarquinii, Falerii), and the Gauls were largely won under his
leadership, real or attributed. Whatever the legendary distortions, the broad
military narrative — that Rome recovered from the Gallic trauma under the leadership
of a single dominant general — reflects a genuine historical pattern.

**Political.** Camillus represents the **transitional generation** of the Roman
aristocracy — patrician by birth but serving in a period when the patrician monopoly
was being eroded by the Licinian-Sextian settlement. The tradition's presentation
of him as a patrician who supported plebeian constitutional gains is probably
ideologically constructed, but it reflects the historical reality that the post-367
settlement required patrician acquiescence to succeed.

**Counterfactual significance.** Without a dominant personality to provide military
stability in the 380s–360s, Rome's recovery from the Gallic Sack might have been
slower or less complete. The Volsci and Aequi could have exploited Roman weakness
more effectively; Etruscan cities might have recovered their independence. This
counterfactual is impossible to evaluate precisely, but the concentration of
dictatorships in Camillus's hands suggests that the Roman political system chose to
vest unusual authority in a single figure — a structural response to an extraordinary
threat.

## Historiography

Camillus is the hardest figure in early Roman history to assess because he exists
at the point where historical memory and legendary construction intersect most
densely. His dictatorships, campaigns, and political significance are attested
in multiple sources (Livy IV–VI; Plutarch's *Life of Camillus*); the legendary
elaborations (the rescue, the exile stories, the Concord temple) are visible on
the surface of the tradition. Modern scholars generally accept the historical
Camillus while rejecting the legendary Camillus, but the line between them is
sometimes unclear.

Plutarch's *Life of Camillus* is the fullest narrative but is explicitly biographical
and was written to illustrate moral and political virtues; it cannot be used as a
source without accounting for its Plutarchan aims.
