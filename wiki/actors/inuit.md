# Inuit

Indigenous peoples of Arctic North America and Greenland, properly designated **Inuit** from their own word for “people.” Biologically, linguistically, and culturally descended from [[thule-culture|Thule]] populations that expanded from Alaska across the Canadian Arctic to Greenland after the tenth century. Related Eskimo-Aleut relatives include [[yupik|Yuit]] speakers of the Bering Sea and Alaskan Pacific coasts and Aleut speakers of the Aleutians—**not** all correctly called Inuit (Damas).

## Terminology and identity

Damas (CHNPA I.2 ch. 14 bibliographic essay): the chapter deals with Arctic North American inhabitants properly designated Inuit. The 1970s-on tendency to replace “Eskimo” wholesale with “Inuit” for all Eskimo-Aleut and prehistoric Arctic populations has two shortcomings: (1) linguistic/genetic affiliations of most archaeological cultures (except Thule) are uncertain; (2) Yuit and Aleut speakers lack *Inuit* in their vocabularies. Historic ethnonyms in the literature (Central Eskimo, Polar Eskimos/Arctic Highlanders, Mackenzie Eskimos/Inuvialuit, Copper Eskimos, Caribou Eskimo, Iglulik, Netsilik, Aivilingmiut, Kalaallit) mark regional groups; large *-miut* (“inhabitants of”) labels are often outsiders’ extensions of small locale names. Helm’s Athapaskan-based “tribe” concept (greatest extension with sufficient intermarriage for multi-sided communication) fits major groupings such as Netsilik, Copper, and Iglulik.

Constitution Act 1982 defines “Aboriginal peoples of Canada” as Indians, Inuit, and Métis. Canadian census 1991: 49,255 Inuit among combined Aboriginal-origin counts (Washburn).

## Role and overview

Inuit societies were maritime- and ice-adapted hunter-fisher-gatherers with regional variation: bowhead and beluga whaling where ecology allowed; ringed-seal breathing-hole hunting under Little Ice Age conditions; caribou, fish, and (in specialized cases) inland year-round adaptation (Caribou Eskimo west of Hudson Bay). Historic contact stretched from Norse Greenland and Labrador through Frobisher/Davis, Danish–Norwegian and Moravian missions, commercial whaling, HBC fox trade, and mid-twentieth-century state centralization to land claims and circumpolar organizations. `part_of: [[arctic-native-history-norse-to-modern]]` · `preceded_by: [[thule-culture]]`

## Contact history (selected)

### Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries
Martin Frobisher’s three voyages (1576–78) provide the first historical Inuit descriptions since brief Norse references: southern Baffin Island mixture of warfare and trade; mutual kidnapping; Dionyse Settle’s ethnocentric ethnography of Frobisher Bay (skin tents, winter “ovens”/qarmat, kayaks and umiaks, iron tips from meteoric or unrecorded European sources). John Davis (1585–87) mixed orchestral welcome, theft, violence, and a 39-word vocabulary attempt; pattern: cordial trade could flip to hostility; Inuit used any method to acquire metal. Seventeenth-century eastern Arctic continued hostility and mass kidnapping (~30 Eskimos 1605–60 for European exhibition); captives studied as non-Norse “Orient-resembling” people (Olerius).

### Eighteenth century
Hearne reached Coppermine mouth July 1771—first European penetration of Central Arctic coast; Athapaskan companions’ premeditated attack below Bloody Falls shaped later Inuit wariness. Mackenzie 1789 to the Arctic Ocean recorded abandoned villages and permanent winter houses without direct Inuit contact; Indian ironware implied Siberian trade networks. By 1800 Greenland and Labrador were pacified under missions; the Central Arctic remained largely unaffected.

### Nineteenth-century ethnography and whaling
Parry and Lyon (1821–23) wintering at Winter Island and Iglulik produced classic Central Arctic ethnography (breathing-hole sealing, snowhouse, dog traction). Franklin, Ross (Netsiliks), Beechey (Point Barrow), and others refined regional pictures; named Inuit interpreters/hunters emerged (Augustus, Ooligbuck, Hans Hendrick, Ebierbing/Joe and Hannah). Eastern commercial whaling brought whaleboats, firearms, and intergroup trade without clear large-scale depopulation. **Western Arctic whaling at Herschel Island (1889–1906)** devastated Mackenzie Eskimos (2,000–2,500 → 200–300). Caribou Eskimo inland orientation west of Hudson Bay endured into the twentieth century.

### Early twentieth century
Fur traders replaced whalers as chief northern/western Alaska contact; Alaskan migrations repopulated Mackenzie delta (never to pre-whaler levels). Domesticated reindeer provided food 1920s–30s but never rivaled fur as income. Fox trade, RCMP, and missions remade Central Arctic seasonal rounds and settlement.

### WWII–1960s
Alaska wartime boom and centralization (Barrow, Kotzebue growth); Anti-Discrimination Act 1945; education lag—by 1960 only a handful of Eskimos (including Inuit, Aleut, and Yuit) had post-secondary education. Canadian family allowances, DEW line, and mid-1950s town centralization; soapstone art industry from Cape Dorset 1949.

## Modern political organizations

- **Inuit Tapirisat of Canada (ITC)** formed 1971 as umbrella for regional orgs (LIA, COPE, others); initiated [[nunavut]] proposal.
- **Labrador Inuit Association (LIA)** 1973—culture and land claims; admitted Settlers 1975; land-use study accepted Ottawa 1978; relinquished aboriginal rights/self-government claims in concessions; ICC membership.
- **COPE** (Committee for Original Peoples’ Entitlement) 1970 over pipeline threat; **Western Arctic Claim** ratified 5 June 1984 (Inuvialuit): 91,000 of 435,000 km² traditionally used (11,000 km² with surface and subsurface rights); $45 million 1984–97.
- **Northern Quebec Inuit Association** 1971; **James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement 1975** with Makivik Corporation; dissidents (Povungnituk, Ivujivik, some Sugluk) formed Inuit Tungavingat Nunami against 1% exclusive land.
- **[[alaska-native-claims-settlement-1971]]:** Arctic Slope Regional Corporation, NANA, and part of Bering Straits (shared with [[yupik|Yuit]]) among Inuit-speaking corporations.
- **Inuit Circumpolar Conference (ICC)** founded 1977; assemblies every third year; UN NGO status by 1983; environmentalism preserving wildlife as resources (opposition to Greenpeace-style seal-hunt bans); research clearance and remains protections; assertive pan-Inuit image under economic dependency.

Washburn: Canadian pan-Aboriginal bodies included Inuit Committee on National Issues alongside Assembly of First Nations and Métis National Council.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Thule radiation there is no historic Inuit dialect continuum from Greenland to northern Alaska. Without nineteenth-century western whaling the Mackenzie coastal demographic collapse is hard to imagine; without postwar welfare centralization, land-claim politics of the 1970s–90s would have different settlement geography and leadership. Without ICC and national umbrella orgs, circumpolar bargaining power and hunting-rights diplomacy would be far weaker.

## Historiography

Primary strata: Norse sagas and archaeology; sixteenth–seventeenth-century voyage literature; mission and trade company records; explorer ethnographies; Boas *Central Eskimo*; Rasmussen Fifth Thule Expedition; Jenness; modern community studies and land-claims literature. Damas tracks image shifts: early east/west “bellicose thieving xenophobes” → more benign central portraits → passive fur-trade hunter-trapper → late-twentieth-century assertive, articulate political actors. Source bias strong for contact zones (Greenland, Labrador, whaling nodes) and thinner for interior High Arctic until twentieth-century ethnography. Collection: strong via CHNPA I.1–I.2 Arctic chapters; terminology discipline mandatory.
