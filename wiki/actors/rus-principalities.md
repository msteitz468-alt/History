# The Rus′ Principalities under the Mongols

In the thirteenth century the lands of the **Rus′** were conquered by the
**[[mongol-empire|Mongols]]** — the symbolic end of "old, strained, but once-glorious **Kievan
Rus′**" and the beginning of the "Tartar yoke." [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Simon Franklin,
Ch. 18d) stresses that thirteenth-century "Rus′" "existed either no longer or not yet": it was
"not a polity nor a place" but "various polities and places which had less and less relationship
with one another."

Volga–Baltic trade (Nizhnyi Novgorod founded 1221; monopoly over Novgorod's princes), the
south-west torn between old Rus′ and the Carpathian kingdoms, and Smolensk/Chernigov the "true
Riurikid traditionalists" who needed the old unified Rus′ to thrive.

## The Mongol Conquest

The Mongols first appeared in 1223, destroying a Rus′–Polovtsian coalition at the **river Kalka**,
then vanishing. They returned in force under **Batu**: **Riazan** (1237), **Vladimir** (February
1238, where Prince Iurii's family burned in the cathedral), Iurii himself killed at the **river
Sit′** (1238), then **Chernigov** and finally **Kiev (6 December 1240)** — the great tithe church
collapsing "with the sheer weight of people crowding into it for sanctuary." Franklin notes the
Rus′ "were not alone: everybody from China to Croatia fell rapidly to the Mongols."

## The "Tartar Yoke" and the Continuity Debate

The Rus′ princes became tributaries of the **Golden Horde**, traveling to Sarai for patents to
rule. Franklin frames the central historiographical question as a **pendulum between catastrophe
and continuity**: native chronicles speak in "apocalyptic formulae" and Carpini reported Kiev
reduced to 200 houses, yet "an alternative version" holds the impact "outwardly traumatic" but
"superficial," with the Mongols accelerating rather than causing change. The lasting effects were
a **reorientation of power northward and eastward** — away from Kiev toward Vladimir, Novgorod
(under figures like Alexander Nevsky) and the rising Moscow — and the long subordination of the
eastern Slavs to a steppe overlord, while the south-western lands (Galicia-Volyn) gravitated
toward Catholic Poland and Hungary.

## The Fourteenth Century: The Rise of Moscow and Lithuania (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Nancy Shields Kollmann, Ch. 19) carries the story forward: "the
state centred at Moscow that became Russia emerged from one of the Rus′ principalities over the
course of the century." In c. 1300 the lands were still marked by "political and cultural diversity"
under the overlordship of the **Golden Horde** (the Kipchak khanate at Sarai). Two powers then rose
to gather the Rus′ inheritance: in the west, the **[[grand-duchy-of-lithuania|Grand Duchy of
Lithuania]]**, which absorbed Smolensk, Kiev and the western principalities (the future Ukrainian and
Belarusian lands); and in the north-east, the **principality of Moscow**, whose princes won the
khans' favour, the office of grand prince, and the seat of the Orthodox **metropolitan**. Moscow's
prestige rose sharply when **Dmitrii Donskoi** defeated a Horde army at **Kulikovo (1380)** —
though Tatar overlordship was soon reimposed. By 1400 the Rus′ world was effectively partitioned
between a **Lithuanian** west and a **Muscovite** north-east — the configuration from which modern
Russia, Ukraine and Belarus would emerge.
