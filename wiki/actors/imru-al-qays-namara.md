## Overview

**Mara al-Qays** (also rendered **Imru al-Qays**) is the Arab ruler commemorated by the
**al-Namara inscription**, discovered in 1901 by René Dussaud and Frédéric Macler near the
Roman fort at al-Namara in southern Syria. Written in Nabataean Aramaic script but in the
Arabic language, and set within a Roman-style *tabula ansata*, it is one of the oldest surviving
Arabic-language texts. The inscription forms the lintel of the eastern doorway of his tomb (now
in the Louvre) and is traditionally dated 328 CE, via a reading of the Roman Province of Arabia
era as "223"; a recent alternative reading proposes 227/332 instead. The tomb itself, some 18.5
square meters and probably pyramid-roofed, sits beside a small Roman observation post capable of
housing at most about fifty men, on an island amid irrigated wadis.

## The Inscription and Its Claims

The text celebrates Mara al-Qays's rule over Syria; a campaign against Najran, called "the city
of Shammar" after the [[himyar|Himyarite]] king Shammar Yuharish; a campaign against Madhhij;
and rule over Nizar and Maadd. It states that he appointed his own sons as rulers over "the
communes" — implying authority over settled populations, not merely nomadic groups — acting as
"proxies" for both Rome and Persia, a phrase read as evidence of client status carrying material
or financial support from both empires simultaneously.

Fisher reads the Najran campaign as a plausible response to Himyarite expansion into Arabia
Deserta under Shammar Yuharish, since the Madhhij were themselves Himyarite clients through
[[kinda|Kinda]]. The find-site, adjacent to a Roman fort, suggests Mara al-Qays was a Roman
ally, possibly campaigning on Constantine's behalf; Michael Macdonald has suggested the tomb's
unusual placement reflects death in battle while fighting for Rome. A rival interpretation holds
he was instead, or additionally, a Persian client: genealogical links have been proposed to
"Amru king of the Lahmids" in the Paikuli inscription (from the reign of the Sasanian king
Narseh) and to "Amaro, king of the sons of Lahim" in a Manichean text. Christian Robin has
argued that the [[himyar|dhu-Yazan]] campaigns of this period were partly meant to intimidate
Persia's Arab allies, which would imply Mara al-Qays was in the Persian camp rather than Rome's.

Fisher situates Mara al-Qays within a broader comparative frame of "hybrid" frontier rulers
straddling Roman and non-Roman identity — Germanic *foederati* warlords such as the half-Vandal
Stilicho, the Ostrogothic king [[theoderic-the-great|Theoderic]] (whose mausoleum at Ravenna
resembles a yurt), Masuna of Altava in Mauretania (styled "king of the Moorish people and the
Roman people" on a 508 funerary inscription), and the grave goods of [[clovis|Clovis]]'s father
Childeric. In this reading, Mara al-Qays's deliberate choice to inscribe his tomb in Arabic,
despite its otherwise Roman visual framing, marks a conscious assertion of Arabic-speaker
identity at the edge of empire.

## Historiography

**The "king of all the Arabs" controversy.** Part of the inscription's first line has often
been translated as proclaiming Mara al-Qays "king of all the Arabs," a reading that would imply
an overarching pan-Arab ethnic kingship in the early fourth century. Michael Macdonald and
Michael Zwettler have "persuasively argued," in Fisher's assessment, that "Arab" in this context
refers instead to a regional toponym attested in Hatran and Edessan inscriptions — a place-name,
not an ethnonym. Fisher endorses this reading and states there is "no basis," either textually
or in the surrounding fourth-century political and cultural context of the Middle East, for the
older pan-Arab-kingship interpretation. This remains a live point of translation and
interpretation rather than a settled question, and the wiki should not adjudicate it beyond
noting Fisher's own clearly stated position.

**Possible identity with al-Tabari's king of al-Hira.** Fisher states explicitly that there is
"no clinching evidence either way" as to whether Mara al-Qays of the al-Namara inscription is
the same figure as al-Tabari's "Imru al-Qays, king of al-Hira, son of Amr," a ruler otherwise
associated with the [[lakhmids|Lakhmid]] line at al-Hira. The two figures share a name common
enough in this period that identity cannot be assumed; they may be the same person, related
figures, or entirely unconnected rulers whose names happened to coincide. Any wiki cross-links
between this page and Lakhmid material should flag this as an open identification, not a
resolved fact.
