# Miklós Horthy

Admiral Miklós Horthy (1868–1957), last commander-in-chief of the
Austro-Hungarian navy, served as regent of Hungary from 1919/1920 to 1944 —
a landlocked admiral heading a kingdom whose throne remained deliberately
vacant.

## Role

Horthy emerged as the most eminent figure of the counterrevolutionary
movement gathered at Szeged in 1919, where junior officers (the "circle of
the twelve captains," including Gyula Gömbös) organized a volunteer
anti-Bolshevik army under his command against [[kun-bela|Béla Kun's soviet
republic]]. After Romanian forces expelled Kun from Budapest
(1 August 1919), forces from the two counterrevolutionary centers — Szeged
and Count István Bethlen's anti-Bolshevik committee in Vienna — occupied
the capital. The retaking of Budapest was accompanied by a "White Terror"
that killed roughly a thousand alleged socialists or Jews
([[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]]).

## Character of the regime

Paxton's survey stresses that Horthy's Hungary was formed *in*
counterrevolution but settled, by 1921, into oligarchic parliamentarism
rather than fascism proper: Horthy made the Calvinist aristocrat Bethlen
prime minister, who cut the eligible electorate to 27 percent, restored
public balloting in rural precincts so landlords could supervise peasants'
votes, and permitted urban but not agrarian labor unions. The more
radical, racialist "Szeged idea" current — represented by Gömbös, prime
minister from 1932 and self-described "national socialist" as early as
1919 — coexisted with and pressured this conservative order. A. J. P.
Taylor called Hungary "the first breeding ground of fascism," a
characterization Paxton reports with qualified approval; the Horthy regime
itself is better classed with authoritarian conservatism (see
[[para-fascism]]).

Horthy twice blocked attempts to restore the Habsburg ex-emperor Karl to
the Hungarian throne in the 1920s (a cause pressed by legitimist gentry
but opposed by Gömbös's radicals and by the Little Entente).

## Counterfactual significance

Had the crown been restored or a republican settlement consolidated in
1919–1920, interwar Hungary's revisionism might have been channeled
through conventional great-power diplomacy rather than through the
alignment with Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany that Horthy's regime pursued
in the 1930s in pursuit of overturning the Treaty of Trianon.

## Historiography

The foundational English-language treatment of the Szeged milieu is István
Deák's essay in Rogger and Weber, *The European Right* (1965), which
Paxton follows closely. Debate centers on whether the Horthy system was
"fascist," "para-fascist," or conservative-authoritarian — see
[[generic-fascism]] and [[payne-history-of-fascism-1995]], which treats
Hungary as authoritarian-conservative with fascist challengers.
