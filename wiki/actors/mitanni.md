# Mitanni (Khanigalbat)

The great **[[hurrians|Hurrian]] kingdom of northern Mesopotamia and Syria** — the
**fourth "great power"** of the Late Bronze Age, alongside Egypt, Hatti, and
Babylonia. Built on a Hurrian population ruled by a small **Indo-Aryan chariot
aristocracy**, it dominated the Fertile Crescent's north in the 16th–15th centuries
before the Hittites and Assyria destroyed it. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. X, Drower).

## Hurrians and Indo-Aryans

The state arose from the **[[hurrians|Hurrian]]** expansion that filled the vacuum
left by the [[fall-of-babylon-1595bce|fall of the Amorite dynasties]] (~1600). But
its **ruling class** — more than a hundred attested princes and officials — bore
**Indo-Aryan** (Vedic-Indian, not Iranian) names, and worshipped the Vedic gods. The
evidence is striking:

- The treaty between the Hittite **Šuppiluliuma I** and **Kurtiwaza (Mattiwaza)** of
  Mitanni invokes as divine witnesses **Mitra, Varuna, Indra, and the Nasatyas** —
  the gods of the Rig Veda.
- A **horse-training manual** attributed to the Hurrian **Kikkuli** uses Indo-Aryan
  technical and numeral terms (the turns of a chariot-course).
- The chariot nobility — the **maryannu** — bears a name equated with Vedic *márya*,
  "young warrior." Royal names mean things like "owner of terrible chariots"
  (Tushratta) or "possessing great horses" (Biridashwa).

CAH reads this as a **small, vigorous horse-and-chariot warrior elite** that imposed
itself on the Hurrian-speaking majority — the clearest historical instance of the
[[indo-european-migrations|Indo-Aryan]] expansion, distinct from (and later than) the
broad Hurrian movement. (For the wider debate, see [[indo-european-origins]].)

## The kingdom

Mitanni ("Maitani"), called **Khanigalbat** by the Assyrians and "the Hurrian land"
in its own tongue, was centred on the steppe of the upper Khabur, its capital
**Washshukanni** (perhaps Tell Fakhariyah, unlocated). It was not a centralized
empire but a **confederacy of feudal Hurrian princes** under a "Great King." Its
kings: **Kirta**, **Shuttarna I**, **Parattarna** (overlord of [[alalakh|Alalakh]]
and Kizzuwatna), and above all **Saustatar**, under whom the kingdom reached its
**greatest extent — from the Taurus to the Zagros, from the Amanus to the Biqa'** —
ruling east of the Tigris (Nuzi/Arrapkha), arbitrating his vassals' disputes, and
holding Assyria itself in vassalage.

## Rivalry and rapprochement with Egypt

Mitanni was Egypt's chief antagonist in Syria. It stiffened the resistance of
Qadesh, Tunip, and the other city-states against **[[thutmose-iii|
Thutmose III]]**, whose campaigns reached the Euphrates against "Naharin"
(Mitannian territory). But the rise of the **[[hittites|Hittites]]** drove the two
powers together: under **Thutmose IV** a **marriage alliance** was sealed (a
daughter of king Artatama entering the Egyptian harem), and Mitanni and Egypt stood
together as allies through the reign of **[[amenhotep-iii|Amenophis III]]** — the
relationship vividly documented in the Amarna letters (Tushratta of
Mitanni to the pharaoh), which open at the very end of this volume's coverage.

## The destruction (narrated in CAH II.2)

What the first volume foretold, [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|Vol. II, Part 2]] (Ch. XVII,
Goetze) recounts. In two Syrian wars **[[shuppiluliuma-i|Šuppiluliuma I]]** of Hatti
stripped Mitanni of Syria and struck its capital **Waššukanni**; king **Tushratta**,
already at odds with a rival "Khurri" line under Artatama, was finally **murdered by
his own son**. His successors then "completed the surrender to Assyria," bribing
[[ashur-uballit-i|Ashur-uballit I]] and Alše with the wealth of the royal palace to
win allies against Hatti. Šuppiluliuma took up Tushratta's son **Kurtiwaza
(Mattiwaza)**, married him to a Hittite princess, and reinstalled him at Waššukanni
as a **client king** (the two surviving versions of their treaty — invoking the
Vedic gods — are the showcase document of the Indo-Aryan question above). Mitanni
survived only as a shrunken buffer, soon squeezed out of existence between Hatti and
the rising Assyria. Its fall **freed Assyria** — "unwished for and of little
consequence at the moment," but the hinge of the next two centuries. The rump
kingdom of **Khanigalbat** (the house of Šattuara) fought Assyria into the reign of
Shalmaneser I, who annexed it (see Section 3).

## Liverani's reading

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] sees Mitanni as the **unification of
previously fragmented Hurrian city-states**, with the Indo-Iranian wave linked to the
horse-drawn chariot at its heart (the royal family consistently bore Indo-Iranian names).
Under **Shaushtatar** it reached its peak, holding [[assyria-middle|Assyria]] as a vassal
(symbolised by carrying off the gold and silver doors of Ashur) and treating Egypt as an
equal — though marriages flowed **one way only** (Mitannian princesses to Egypt, never the
reverse). Mitanni's lasting contribution was spreading the **maryannu** chariot aristocracy
and Hurrian culture across its sphere, surviving the kingdom's fall. Liverani stresses that
the capital **Washshukkanni** (Tell Fekheriye) lies submerged in groundwater and
unexcavated — the single most important **missing archive** of the Late Bronze Age, which is
why Mitannian society must be reconstructed from peripheral sites like **Nuzi**
(Yorghan Tepe, in the kingdom of Arrapha) and Alalah — where private archives reveal a
society in advanced social disintegration (50%-interest loans, *tidennutu* labour-servitude,
and the fictitious **adoptions** by which men like Tehip-tilla, "adopted" ~100 times,
amassed thousands of hectares as customary inalienability broke down). See
[[ancient-near-east-environment-and-society]].

## Significance

Mitanni is the **missing fourth corner** of the Late Bronze great-power system and
the institutional carrier of two world-historical things: the **light war-chariot
and the maryannu warrior order** (which spread across the Near East and into Egypt),
and the **Indo-Aryan presence** in the Near East — the northern, documented edge of
the same migrations that carried Indo-Aryan into India. Its destruction (by the
Hittite Šuppiluliuma I and then Assyria, in the 14th–13th centuries, beyond this
volume) removed the buffer between Hatti and Assyria and reshaped the Late Bronze
world.

## Historiography

A kingdom known almost entirely **from its enemies and vassals** — Egyptian campaign
records, the Hittite treaties, the [[alalakh|Alalakh]] and Nuzi tablets, the Amarna
letters — never from its own capital, which has not been excavated. Its internal
chronology and the order of its early kings are uncertain. The **Indo-Aryan
onomastic evidence** is a famous and still-debated dataset in Indo-European studies.
See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
