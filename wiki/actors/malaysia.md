# Malaysia

The federation grown from **[[british-malaya|British Malaya]]** — the Straits Settlements and the
protected Malay states — whose tin and rubber economy drew massive Chinese and Indian immigration,
producing the classic colonial **[[plural-society]]** of Malays, Chinese, and Indians that has defined
its politics ever since. British rule was interrupted by the [[japanese-occupation-southeast-asia|Japanese occupation]]
and contested after 1948 by the communist insurgency of the Malayan Emergency.

Independence came in 1957 (Malaya) and the wider federation of Malaysia in 1963; Singapore separated in
1965. Communal balance among its ethnic communities remains the central theme of the Malaysian state.
It is a founding member of [[asean-and-regionalism|ASEAN]].

The Malay peninsula's Muslim identity predates British Malaya by centuries: Chiara Formichi
([[formichi-islam-and-asia-2020]]) identifies [[melaka|Melaka]] as "the first port of entry for
Islam to Southeast Asia," from which Malay rulers' conversion (15th c.) fused with retained
Hindu-Buddhist court titulature and law under the Melaka Sultanate — the template later
Malay states (Johor, Patani) inherited.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

**Isthmian geography.** The Siamo-Malay Peninsula is ~1,500 km long; narrowest (~50 km) at the Isthmus
of Kra (~10°N). From Kra to ~6°N the peninsula is crowded with early cosmopolitan trading sites;
several trans-peninsular routes linked the Bay of Bengal and South China Sea by the second century BCE
(probably two centuries earlier). Latitude 6°N coincides with the tropical→equatorial climate
transition and closely with the modern Thailand–Malaysia border (linguistic boundaries fuzzier — Malay
widely spoken in four Thai provinces north of the line).

**Early Classic ports.** Kedah (Chinese Jiecha; Indian Kataha/Kadaram) was the principal navigational
landmark of the northern Melaka Straits and Malaysia's largest rice area; Yijing sailed Kedah–
Tamralipti and treated Kedah as the Seasia embarkation for India. Sites include Pengkalan Bujang,
Sungai Mas (≥20 ha Early Classic with local, Tang–Song Chinese, and West Asian ceramics; glass-bead
rejects indicate local production), Bukit Meriam, and Candi Bukit Batu Pahat. Kuala Selinsing (~150 km
south) yielded a carnelian seal of Sri Visnuvarman (~7th c.) plus metalworking and bead-making. West-
coast isthmian peers (Ko Kho Khao/Takuapa, Laem Pho–Chaiya — partly in modern Thai territory) shared
Persian Gulf glass, late Tang pottery, and Indian beads; Takuapa holds the oldest known Tamil
inscription in Seasia (9th c., Maṇigramām guild). Chinese-named polities include Chitu ("Red Earth,"
Sui envoys 608, south of Langkasuka/Patani) and poorly located Dandan (Terengganu/Kelantan or Java?).

**Islam and Postclassic Melaka.** The earliest evidence for Islam on the Siamo-Malay Peninsula is the
fourteenth-century Terengganu inscription (Arabic script, Malay with Sanskrit *dewata mulia raya* and
Allah; legal fines by social rank). Oldest Malaysian funerary inscription: 1442–1443, Makam Diraja
Kampung Langgar, Kedah. [[melaka|Melaka]] became the new centre of the largest southern-Straits
mandala c. 1400 after Angkor, Malayu, Majapahit, and Bagan faded; Portuguese destroyed the sultanate
in 1511, but Miksic notes little historical archaeology has been done there. The Melaka Legal Code
(*Undang-Undang Melaka*) is largely maritime law; under Mansur Shah (1459–1477) Pengkalan Kempas
inscriptions shift from Kawi to Arabic-script Malay within five years. Wrecks off Malay coasts
(Turiang; Royal Nanhai hybrid Seasian–Chinese vessel with >21,000 mostly Sawankhalok ceramics)
document the ceramic trade that filled the Ming gap.
