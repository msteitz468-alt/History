# Enkidu

## Overview

**Enkidu** is the wild man created by the gods as a counterpart and companion to **[[gilgamesh|Gilgamesh]]**, king of Uruk, in the *Epic of Gilgamesh*. He embodies the untamed steppe — hairy, animal-like, living with the beasts — and serves as both foil and equal to the semi-divine but arrogant Gilgamesh. Through his friendship with Gilgamesh, Enkidu becomes the vehicle for exploring themes of civilization versus nature, the redemptive power of companionship, and the inevitability of death.

In the Standard Babylonian version of the epic ("He who saw the Deep"), Enkidu is fashioned by the goddess Aruru from clay as a double for Gilgamesh. In the older Sumerian poems, he appears in slightly different roles (e.g., servant or ally in Gilgamesh and Huwawa). His arc — from wild innocence to civilized wisdom to tragic death — is one of the most moving in ancient literature.

## Career (from the Epic)

- **Creation and wild life**: The gods create Enkidu to check Gilgamesh's tyranny. He lives among the animals of the steppe, eating grass and drinking from waterholes, protecting the herds from trappers. "The goddess Aruru, she washed her hands, / took a pinch of clay, threw it down in the wild. / In the wild she created Enkidu, the hero, / offspring of silence, knit strong by Ninurta. / All his body is matted with hair, / ... with the gazelles he grazes on grasses, / joining the throng with the herd at the water-hole" (ll. 1155–1177). His creation **produced** by Aruru at Anu's command **to counter** Gilgamesh's oppression (ll. 1141–1149).
- **Civilization by Shamhat**: A trapper brings the prostitute Shamhat (or Shamkatum) to "tame" him on Gilgamesh's instruction (ll. 1277–1287). After six (or seven) days and nights of sexual intercourse, Enkidu loses his animal nature: the beasts no longer recognize him, and he gains human understanding and language. Shamhat teaches him the ways of civilization — clothing, bread, beer, and the city. "For six days and seven nights / Enkidu was erect, as he coupled with Shamhat. / When with her delights he was fully sated, / he turned his gaze to his herd. / The gazelles saw Enkidu, they started to run, / the beasts of the field shied away from his person. / Enkidu had defiled his body so pure, / ... but now he had reason and wide understanding." (ll. 1343–1361). The taming **contributed_to** Enkidu's transformation from wild to human; "a woman’s counsel struck home in his heart" (ll. 1622).
- **Journey to Uruk and friendship**: Enkidu travels to Uruk. He and Gilgamesh wrestle (a test of strength) and become instant friends ("like brothers"). Enkidu's presence humanizes Gilgamesh. Enkidu blocks the wedding house door upon learning of Gilgamesh's practice (ll. 1816–1822); after combat "Gilgamesh knelt, one foot on the ground, / his anger subsided" (ll. 1834–1836 parallel); Enkidu then affirms "High over warriors you are exalted, / to be king of the people Enlil made it your destiny!" (ll. 1846–1848). The wrestling **preceded_by** the friendship bond (concurrent_with initial rivalry).
- **Adventures**:
  - **Cedar Forest expedition**: Enkidu guides Gilgamesh to the distant Cedar Forest guarded by the monstrous Humbaba (Huwawa). Despite warnings from the gods and Enkidu's own fears (he knows the forest from his wild days), they slay Humbaba. Enkidu's knowledge of the wild is crucial. Enkidu initially resists: "So to keep safe the cedars, / Enlil made it his lot to terrify men. / ... Humbaba, his voice is the Deluge." (ll. 1960–1970); "I knew him, my friend, in the uplands" (ll. 1930–1932). Gilgamesh insists; they arm at the forge (ll. 2018–2034). During journey Enkidu interprets Gilgamesh's dreams as omens of success (ll. 2576–2588, 2760–2762). In combat Enkidu urges haste: "finish him, slay him, do away with his power!" (ll. 3381–3383, 3480–3481); after victory he begins felling: "Enkidu was seeking out the best timber" and fashions a door for Enlil's temple (ll. 3569–3585). Enkidu's wild knowledge **enabled** the approach and **contributed_to** the slaying; the joint action **part_of** their friendship. The curse by Humbaba **preceded_by** later consequences (ll. 3503–3505).
  - **Bull of Heaven**: When Ishtar propositions Gilgamesh and is rejected, she sends the Bull of Heaven to ravage Uruk. Enkidu helps Gilgamesh kill it. He tears off its thigh and taunts Ishtar. This act seals his fate.
- **Death and grief**: Enkidu falls ill after a dream vision of the underworld (the "House of Dust"). He curses the hunter and Shamhat for bringing him to civilization, but Shamash (the sun god) reminds him of the joys of friendship with Gilgamesh. He dies in Gilgamesh's arms. Gilgamesh's inconsolable grief ("like a lioness robbed of her cubs") drives the rest of the epic. In Tablet VII (ll. 4095–4222), Enkidu first curses the hunter ("may the hunter not get as much as his friend!") then Shamhat ("A home to delight in [you shall not] acquire... The dust of the cross-roads shall be where you sit!"), before Shamash's intervention causes him to bless her instead ("My mouth [that] cursed you shall bless [you] as well!"; ll. 4225+). His netherworld dream (ll. 4251–4348): "to the house which none who enters ever leaves, / on the path that allows no journey back" where "dust is their sustenance and clay their food" and he sees crowns of ancient rulers, priests, Etana, Shakkan, Ereshkigal (ll. 4293–4330). Enkidu lay sick twelve days and died (ll. 4357–4385); his death **produced** Gilgamesh's grief and **preceded_by** the funeral. Enkidu's vision **contributed_to** the epic's meditation on mortality.
- **Afterlife**: In Tablet XII (an appendix) and the Sumerian *Gilgamesh and the Netherworld*, Enkidu descends to the underworld (or is sent as Gilgamesh's agent) and reports on the fates of the dead. His descriptions of the netherworld are among the most vivid in Mesopotamian literature.

Enkidu represents the "noble savage" who is both improved and doomed by entering human society. His death teaches Gilgamesh (and the audience) that even the strongest bonds end in loss, and that immortality must be sought in lasting achievements rather than literal eternal life.

## Historiography and Value

Enkidu is a purely literary creation, though he may echo older motifs of wild men or "hairy ones" in Mesopotamian thought. His story provides the emotional core of the epic and a profound reflection on what it means to be human: the trade-offs between nature and culture, instinct and wisdom, freedom and society.

In Sumerian poems, Enkidu (or his analogues) plays different supporting roles, showing the fluid evolution of the tradition. Andrew George's edition carefully distinguishes the layers.

**Grounded details from this range (OB fragments + apparatus, ll. 10001–12899)**:
- Glossary explicitly: "ENKIDU ‘Lord of the Pleasant Place’: in the Babylonian tradition a wild man created by the gods as Gilgamesh’s equal, in the Sumerian his favoured servant." (ll. 12511–12). **Contrasts_with** the two layers.
- From OB curse episode (Ur tablet): Enkidu "decided to curse the harlot also. ‘Come, Shamhat, I will fix your destiny, I will curse you with a mighty curse...’" followed by specific deprivations ("A home to delight in you shall not acquire!... The dust of the potter’s cross shall be where you sit!... Briar and thorn shall skin your feet!... [The rabble] of the street shall throng to your brothel!") (ll. 11176–11214). Then reversal after Shamash: blessings ("Governors shall love you and noblemen too!") (ll. 11238–42). **Part_of** deathbed sequence **produced_by** his taming and entry to civilization.
- Sealand tablet: "The harlot said to him, to Ea:..." with substitution of Ea for Enkidu (and Sîn for Gilgamesh) in civilizing and dream scenes (ll. ~11985+). **Concurrent_with** name fluidity in early traditions.

**Grounded claims from Sumerian "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld" and "Death of Gilgamesh" (exclusive range: Andrew George trans., ll. 7001–10000)**:
- Enkidu volunteers to retrieve ball/mallet from Netherworld; ignores Gilgamesh's precise instructions (dress not clean, anoint not, no noise, no sandals, "Do not kiss the wife you loved, do not strike the wife you hated... the outcry of the Netherworld would seize you" ll. 8534–8562). "Enkidu paid no attention to the word of his master" (ll. 8576); seized: "From that wicked day to the seventh day thence, from the Netherworld his servant Enkidu came not forth." (ll. 8608–10). This **produced** Enkidu's entrapment **caused_by** disobedience in Sumerian version. Gilgamesh laments: "My favourite servant, my steadfast companion, the one who counselled me – the Netherworld seized him!" (ll. 8614).
- Enkidu's shade (brought via Ea/Enki and Utu opening chink in Netherworld, ll. 9032–48) reports detailed rules: fates vary by sons ("‘Did you see the man with seven sons?’ ‘I saw him. Second only to the gods he sits on a throne and listens to the proceedings.’"; no heir "eats a bread-round like a kiln-fired brick"; eunuch "propped in the corner"; various deaths (mooring-pole, battle, lion, leper, roof-fall, god-struck, accursed parents, no offerings eat scraps; stillborn "play amid syrup and ghee at tables of silver and gold"; burnt "I did not see him. His ghost was not there, his smoke went up to the heavens.") (ll. 8698–8800). The dialogue **produced** Enkidu as source of netherworld knowledge **part_of** [[enkidu|Enkidu]]'s afterlife role.
- In Ur recension: "‘Did you see the sons of Sumer and Akkad?’ ‘I saw them. They drink water from the place of a massacre, dirty water.’ ‘Did you see where my father and mother dwell?’ ‘I saw them. The two of them drink water from the place of a massacre, dirty water.’" (ll. 8826–32). This **contributed_to** Gilgamesh's filial piety (statues, mourning rites).
- In "Death of Gilgamesh", Enkidu is reunited in vision: the gods promise Gilgamesh "your friend Enkidu, the young man your companion! (There) in the Great City, dwell governors and kings..." (ll. 9363–65, repeated). Enkidu shares the special status in the Netherworld with Gilgamesh. This **followed_by** Enkidu's seizure in the poem's chronology.

For the wiki, Enkidu anchors the theme of **friendship** as a civilizing and humanizing force in early Mesopotamian literature. He complements the institutional and archaeological picture of Uruk with the personal dimension of power and mortality.
