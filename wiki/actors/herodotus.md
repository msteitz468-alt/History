# Herodotus of Halicarnassus

The "**Father of History**" (Cicero) — author of the *[[herodotus-histories|Histories]]*, the earliest
surviving work of historical enquiry in the Western tradition. His own term for his project,
**historiē** ("enquiry, investigation"), named the discipline. As analysed by Carolyn Dewald in the
Oxford World's Classics edition (trans. Robin Waterfield, 1998).

## Life

Herodotus was born around the time of the [[greco-persian-wars-499bce|Persian Wars]] in
**Halicarnassus** (modern Bodrum), a Dorian Greek town of mixed Greek, Carian and Persian population
under nominal Persian suzerainty; his family probably had a Carian element and was exiled in the
troubled years after the wars, when he may have lived on **Samos**. He **travelled widely** around the
eastern Mediterranean (Egypt, the Levant, the Black Sea, Mesopotamia, mainland Greece), gathering the
material he later delivered as **public recitations** in Greek cities and at **Athens** at the height
of its imperial power — where his contemporaries may have included Sophocles, Pericles, Protagoras and
the young Thucydides. He joined the Panhellenic colony of **Thurii** in southern Italy (c. 444 BCE).
His narrative stops before 425 and mentions nothing after, so he likely died in the 420s, early in
the Peloponnesian War; his tomb was claimed at Thurii (also reported at Pella in Macedonia).

## Achievement: the Invention of History

Dewald stresses that the magnitude of Herodotus's accomplishment is hard to appreciate precisely
because the genre he invented became so basic to later thought. He was not the first to write about
Persia or the wars (Ionian *logographoi* like **Hecataeus of Miletus** preceded him), but he was, as
the ancient critic Dionysius of Halicarnassus saw, **the first to see many separate stories of the
past as parts of a single whole** — "to tell, really to tell, the story of the Persian Wars." Out of
this came a "polycentric" narrative moving among many peoples, voices and points of view.

Formed **before** the rhetorical and educational revolution of the Athenian sophistic, Herodotus
wrote in the **paratactic "old-style" prose** (Aristotle) of Ionian enquiry — short, self-contained
*logoi* strung together, bound by **ring composition** and association, with constant **digression**
("my work seeks out digressions," 4.30) into ethnography, geography and *thomata* (marvels). His
younger contemporary **Thucydides** took the chronological-causal narrative of political events from
Herodotus but stripped away the digressions and the tales, founding the austere, analytic strand of
history-writing; the two together created the field. (See [[classical-greek-historiography]].)

## Method and Outlook

- **Critical enquiry.** Herodotus reports "the best version or versions" he could find, names or
  cites his informants ("it is said," "they say"), and intervenes in his own voice hundreds of times
  to doubt, compare, or reject — famously: "I am obliged to record the things I am told, but I am
  certainly not required to believe them" (7.152). This self-conscious, source-citing apparatus is
  why he most "deserves the title father of history" — and also what his radical critics (Fehling)
  attack as fabricated (see [[herodotus-reliability]]).
- **A moral and cosmic order.** His narrative is shaped by recurring patterns: **reciprocity** and
  *tisis* (retribution) flowing across generations; **freedom vs. despotism** (the poor, free Greek
  resisting the wealthy autocrat — "soft lands breed soft men"); and **hubris and limit** — the
  overreaching king (Croesus, Cambyses, Xerxes flogging the Hellespont) brought low by the gods and
  by his own failure to heed *warners* (Solon, Demaratus, Artabanus) and to seek out accurate
  information. Power, in the *Histories*, repeatedly fails to listen to facts that would save it.

## The founding of war as history's subject

[[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC* (2000)]]
adds a striking measure of Herodotus's long shadow: he established **war as the proper
subject of history** so thoroughly and so early that centuries later the Roman historian
**Tacitus** felt compelled to apologize for being forced to write about events other than
war. The convention Herodotus set — that a "real" history narrates conflict between
peoples — shaped the genre's expectations for the rest of antiquity, including
[[thucydides|Thucydides]]'s explicit restriction of his own subject "rigorously to war."

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Herodotus "we would know comparatively little of Greek history as a coherent narrative of
events before c. 450 BCE" (Dewald): he is the **only continuous narrative** for the rise of Achaemenid
Persia, the Ionian Revolt, and the Persian Wars, and a unique ethnographic window onto Egypt, Scythia,
Lydia and the wider non-Greek world. He invented not only a literary genre but the very idea that the
human past can be systematically investigated, narrated and explained.

## Hall (2014) — method and source criticism

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] treats Herodotus as a standing lesson in Archaic source criticism (not as a biography of the man):

- **Secondary, not primary, for Archaic events.** Writing ca. 431 about 480–479, the *Histories* are strictly secondary (reliant on others’ reports); modern narratives based on him are more like tertiary products — reserve “source” for most proximate entry-points into the ancient world.
- **Intentionality:** insistence that tyranny ended in 510 (not 514) reveals widespread contrary popular belief — itself a historical fact.
- **Persian Wars:** emphasis/motivations for Persian policy may tell more about later Greek commemoration than Persian foreign policy; Spartan ethnographic portrait and Athens–Sparta polarities already participate in the “Spartan mirage”; silence on Solon/Alcmaeon/Cleisthenes of Sicyon in First Sacred War traditions is troubling for those late narratives.
- **Possible back-projection:** Eretria–Miletus alliance cited at 5.99 may have been invented to justify Eretrian intervention in 499.

## OHCW (Murray) — father of history and total enquiry

[[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]], Ch. 8 (Oswyn Murray), affirms Herodotus as “father of history”: earliest intact Greek prose book (~600 pages, nine books). Opening program: preserve achievements of Greeks and barbarians and **why they fought**; ultimate scale-frame is Xerxes’ 480 expedition (source numbers doubtful but Greeks heavily outnumbered). Final version published **shortly before 425**, when [[aristophanes|Aristophanes]] parodied his war-causes account in the *Acharnians*.

**Origins of the genre.** Writing history as a distinct cultural activity arose independently only in **Judaea, Greece, and China**. Greek and Jewish historiography emerged roughly together from national identity under imperial pressure (Persian defeat for Greeks; Assyria/exile for Jews), but with opposite presuppositions: for Jews, God’s covenant; for Greeks, **great deeds of men under human control** — “The Greeks indeed taught the West how to create and write history without God.” Greek historians **sign** opening sentences; they seldom quote documents yet were paradoxically strong at distinguishing fact from fiction.

**Predecessors.** Earliest prose literary work was philosophical — Anaximander *On Nature* c.550 with first maps. Hecataeus of Miletus c.500 wrote *Description of the Earth* and *Genealogies* (“the stories told by the Greeks are various and in my opinion absurd”) — a “curious false start” that rationalized myth without yet distinguishing myth from history. Geography and ethnography remain core to the Greek historical view.

**Method and shape.** War narrative hangs ethnographic *logoi* (esp. Egypt Book 2), yielding a **total picture** of the known world — Mediterranean egalitarian city-states vs oriental despotisms — making Herodotus, Murray argues, “more modern than any other ancient historian” toward total history. He practised **oral history** (sight and hearing), knew only Greek, travelled widely; Ionian/Delphic folk-tale moral patterning shapes the Persian Wars as divine thunderbolt on the tall. [[thucydides|Thucydides]] dismisses him as a “logos-writer.” After Herodotus, local/ethnic histories and chronography (Hippias’ Olympiad list to 776; Hellanicus’ *Atthis*) professionalized the grammar of time.

Griffin (OHCW Ch. 3) uses Herodotus’s natural explanations (Nile flood) as the fifth-century **rational** foil to mythical explanation, and quotes Herodotus on Homer/Hesiod as makers of Greek theogony.

## Freeman (2014) — rationalism and its limits

[[sources/freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014|Freeman (2014)]] cites Paul Cartledge's
observation that Herodotus can be seen **explicitly rejecting myths as insufficient
evidence** on at least three occasions, and uses empirical reasoning on the Nile-flood
question (Book II) — evidence for a more "scientific" habit of mind than his reputation
for credulity suggests. Freeman also flags Herodotus's own **propaganda function** (Greek
liberty vs. Persian despotism as an explanatory frame) and notes that this contrast was
later given a **racialized/climatic reading** by Hippocrates of Cos and Aristotle
(Europeans "spirited but unintelligent," Asians "intelligent but lacking spirit," Greeks
uniquely combining both). On numbers: Freeman treats Herodotus's figures for Xerxes'
invasion force (1,700,000 troops, 1,300 triremes) as likely deliberate exaggeration,
against modern estimates of roughly 200,000 troops and 600 triremes.
