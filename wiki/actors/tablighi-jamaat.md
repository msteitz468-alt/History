## Overview

The Tablighi Jamaat (TJ) is a transnational grassroots piety and da'wa (revival/outreach) movement founded in 1926 by Muhammad Ilyas Kandahlawi in India, as a Deobandi offshoot. Deliberately apolitical, TJ pursues individual religious renewal rather than political engagement, a strategic choice that allowed it to survive and expand under regimes hostile to organized political Islam.

## Role and Key Developments

TJ's commandment of "not getting involved with politics" let it operate relatively unmolested under Pakistan's Ayub Khan and later Zia ul-Haq regimes, even as explicitly political Islamist organizations like [[jamaat-i-islami]] faced restriction. This apoliticism became the movement's defining transnational asset: TJ expanded across Asia through personal networks of preaching and travel rather than institutional or state channels.

TJ reached Soviet Central Asia in the 1960s–70s via Soviet students who had studied in India, with a second wave of expansion in the early 1990s after Soviet collapse; treatment by post-Soviet states varied sharply — tolerated and even prominent in Kyrgyzstan (a Tablighi leader became state mufti by 2014) but persecuted in Uzbekistan. TJ also reached China in 1986 via the Malegaon (India) network, becoming, alongside Salafiyya, one of the two major post-Mao Islamic revivalist movements there — both reject "Chinese Islam" as insufficiently orthodox but differ sharply in method, TJ emphasizing ritual practice and personal piety over doctrine.

## Counterfactual Significance

TJ's deliberate abstention from politics is the mechanism that let it survive multiple hostile state regimes (Ayub Khan's Pakistan, Soviet and post-Soviet Central Asia, post-Mao China) where politically engaged Islamist organizations could not — making apoliticism itself a strategy of institutional survival and transnational reach.

## Historiography

Formichi (2020) uses TJ as a recurring case demonstrating that "political Islam" and "Islamic revival" are not synonymous — much of 20th-century Asia's Islamic resurgence was driven by explicitly apolitical piety movements rather than Islamist parties.
