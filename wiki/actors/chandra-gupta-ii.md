# Chandragupta II (Vikramaditya)

## Overview

**Chandragupta II** (r. 375–415 CE) — son and successor of [[samudragupta|Samudragupta]] — brought the [[gupta-empire|Gupta Empire]] to its height of power and cultural brilliance. He is best known by his title **Vikramaditya** ('Sun of Valour'), adopted after his conquest of the western Shakas, and as the patron of the legendary court of the **Nine Gems** (Navaratna), whose most celebrated member was the poet-dramatist **Kalidasa**. His reign is the reference point for what historians call India's **Classical** or 'Golden' age.

## Conquest of the Western Shakas

The defining military achievement of Chandragupta II's reign was the **conquest of the Shakas of western India**. The Shakas (Scythians), who had entered India after the Mauryan collapse, held their main power base in Malwa and Saurashtra with their capital at Ujjain. 'Their defeat at the hands of Chandragupta II opened the way to the Gupta control of key ports in western India, such as **Broach, Cambay and Sopara**, which produced greater revenues from their overseas trade.' Possession of Ujjain also gave dominance over the internal trade routes that crossed through that great central Indian city.

After this conquest, Chandragupta II came to be known as **Vikramaditya**, meaning 'Sun of Valour', 'a title highly esteemed in Brahmanic India.' The title carried strong legitimating weight, associating him with a legendary king celebrated for justice and generosity — an association that served him well in consolidating the new western territories.

## Marriage Diplomacy

'Like his grandfather [Chandragupta Maurya], Chandragupta II was shrewd enough to understand the value of marriage alliances for enhancing the influence and dignity of his own dynasty.' He married **Kuvera-Naga**, a princess from the Naga family, extending his patrimony to the east. More consequentially, he married his daughter **Prabhavatigupta** to **King Rudrasena II of the Vakataka dynasty** of Maharashtra and the western Deccan. 'When Rudrasena II died prematurely, Prabhavatigupta ruled as regent for her minor sons, thereby bringing the Vakatakas under Gupta influence.' The late fourth century 'brought the two families closer. Although the Gupta remained the senior partners, the fifth century may rightly be described as the Gupta–Vakataka Age.'

## The Navaratna Court

Chandragupta II held **three courts** — at Pataliputra, Ayodhya, and Ujjain — 'but the one at Ujjain was particularly lustrous. It was a court where a circle of poets, commonly known as the "Nine Gems" (Navaratna), is said to have flourished under the patronage of Chandragupta and composed verses in his praise.' The most celebrated of the Nine Gems was **[[gupta-classical-culture|Kalidasa]]**, the great dramatist and poet, whose play *Shakuntala* would later — after translation by Sir William Jones in 1789 — captivate European Romantics including Goethe, Schiller, and Herder.

## What "Vikramaditya" Signifies

The title Vikramaditya ('Sun of Valour' or 'Sun of Power') was not a personal name but an **honorific bestowed in recognition of outstanding royal achievement** in the Brahmanic tradition. Its adoption after the Shaka conquest signals Chandragupta II's claim to supreme sovereignty across the subcontinent, including territories that had long resisted Gupta authority. The title also situated him within a prestigious legendary tradition of Indian kingship — a legitimating strategy combining military proof of power with cultural-religious prestige.

## Fa-hsien's Account

The Chinese Buddhist pilgrim **Fa-hsien** visited India under Chandragupta II's reign (c. 399–411 CE), spending nearly seven years travelling Buddhist sites from Peshawar and Gandhara to Pataliputra, eventually departing by sea from Tamralipti. Though Fa-hsien 'never had an audience with Chandragupta II, and neither was he interested in the politics of his kingdom,' his recollections provide a remarkable external account. He called India the 'Middle Kingdom' (a term normally used for China) and described 'the free and liberal society of India that he found practically everywhere.' He was particularly impressed by 'the facilities of welfare and care for the weaker members of society and, secondly, the ordinary people going about their daily life in an atmosphere of peace and order, with little interference from the state. There was no capital punishment, and the public officers were held to be non-corrupt.'

Avari cautions, however, that 'Fa-hsien's portrayal of Chandragupta II's India is uncritical and adulatory, and we need to be more circumspect before accepting his observations as reliable: he only saw what he wanted to see. For example, there is evidence for the prevalence of alcohol-drinking and meat-eating.'

## Significance

Chandragupta II combined military expansion (the Shaka conquest and western ports), diplomatic consolidation (Vakataka marriage alliance), and cultural patronage (the Navaratna court and Kalidasa) in a reign that is the historical anchor of India's 'Classical' age. The gold and silver coins of his reign 'reflect the strength and prosperity' of the empire. His conquest of the western ports gave the Guptas access to the lucrative Indian Ocean trade with Rome and the Levant, funding the cultural efflorescence of his court. The Gupta-Vakataka partnership that he established also produced the greatest phase of Buddhist cave art at **[[gupta-classical-culture|Ajanta]]**, completed under the Vakataka king Harishena (460–78 CE).

## Historiography

Chandragupta II is known through: the Allahabad pillar (inherited from Samudragupta, partially relevant to him); coins bearing his image and title; the Chinese pilgrim Fa-hsien's account; and later tradition. The 'golden age' label is substantiated by the convergence of evidence from literary, epigraphic, numismatic, and Chinese sources, but historians since Thapar have qualified it as a benefit mainly accruing to the upper classes of northern India and to creative individuals. The association of Kalidasa and the Navaratna court with Chandragupta II rests on tradition rather than direct contemporary documentation. The Vakataka dimension of the period has received increasing scholarly attention as the Ajanta caves are recognized as primarily a Vakataka achievement enabled by Gupta alliance.
