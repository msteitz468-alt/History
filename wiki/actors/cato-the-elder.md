# Cato the Elder

**M. Porcius Cato** — the "new man" from Tusculum who became the emblematic
self-regulating Roman aristocrat of the second century: censor, moralist, orator,
agricultural writer, and relentless advocate of the destruction of Carthage.
Treated across [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Chs 1, 5, 6 & 7.

## Role and career

A *novus homo* advanced by the patrician **Valerii Flacci**, Cato rose through the
*cursus* to the **consulship of 195** (campaigning in Spain) and the **censorship of
184**, which he and L. Valerius Flaccus sought on a declared platform of severity —
"to cut and sear the hydra-like luxury and softness" they saw afflicting Rome. As
censor he expelled senators and *equites* (including a former consul, L. Quinctius
Flamininus), penalised conspicuous luxury, and contracted vast public works. He was
a tireless **prosecutor and defendant** (prosecuted 44 times, always acquitted), a
foe of provincial extortion (he backed the move against Galba in 149, the year of
the *lex Calpurnia* repetundae court), and, by tradition, the voice of *Carthago
delenda* — agitating for the [[third-punic-war-149-146bce|Third Punic War]] until his
death in 149.

## Writings

Cato's **De agri cultura** is the earliest surviving Latin prose work and the
central document of the [[agrarian-crisis-roman-republic|second-century agrarian
transformation]]: a manual for the absentee owner of a market-oriented,
slave-worked villa — its prologue praising the small farmer-soldier while its
substance describes the new estate economy. His **Origines** (lost) pioneered Latin
historical prose and, unusually, took serious interest in the histories of the
**Italian peoples**. His speeches (the *ORF* fragments) are a prime source for the
politics of *mores*.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Cato personifies [[roman-aristocratic-politics|the mid-Republican system's
self-regulation]] — the censorial and sumptuary attempt by the elite to discipline
its own new wealth — and, in his anti-Carthaginian agitation, the
[[nature-of-roman-imperialism|aristocratic appetite for war]]. Astin cautions
against the old caricature of Cato as a blanket enemy of Greek culture or of the
Scipios: his cultural outlook was more complex, and the "trials of the Scipios"
cannot be reduced to a Cato-led factional vendetta. Rawson (CAH VIII Ch. 12) makes
him the central case of the **ambivalence** of the
[[hellenization-of-rome|Hellenization of Rome]] — warning his son that Greek
literature would "prove the city's ruin" while himself learning Greek, studying with
Ennius, and building in Greek forms (the Basilica Porcia).

## Historiography

Known from his own fragments (*De agri cultura* survives; *Origines* and speeches in
fragments), from [[livy]], **Plutarch**'s *Life*, and **Cicero**. The standard modern
study is Astin's *Cato the Censor* (1978), on which CAH VIII Ch. 6 draws.
