# Prince Shōtoku (Shōtoku Taishi, 574–622)

The semi-legendary regent who became the **patron saint of Japanese statecraft and
Buddhism** — "the Prince Charming of Japanese history," in Mason and Caiger's phrase —
revered less for documented deeds than for what he came to symbolize: the deliberate
adoption of [[tang-dynasty|Chinese]] civilization to build a centralized imperial
[[japan|Japanese]] state.

## Role and significance

Descended from both the imperial and the **Soga** lines, Shōtoku served as regent for
Empress Suiko after the Soga's victory (587) over the anti-Buddhist Mononobe had made
Buddhism the court's favored religion. The traditions credit him with:

- The **"Seventeen-Article Constitution"** — a set of **Confucian** moral-political
  precepts urging that subjects accept imperial rule to achieve social **harmony**; the
  first Japanese statement of principles for a centralized state under the throne.
- A **system of court ranks** on the Chinese pattern, cutting across the old hereditary
  uji offices.
- Adoption of the **Chinese calendar** and the dispatch of the **first official embassies
  to the Sui court** — opening the channel of scholars and ambassadors who would bring
  back the knowledge underpinning the [[taika-reform-645|Taika Reform]].
- Patronage of **Buddhism** as student and teacher, founding temples (the **Hōryūji**,
  the oldest surviving wooden temple complex in the world).

## Counterfactual significance

Shōtoku is venerated "not so much for what he did as for what he stood for." Whether or
not the traditions exaggerate, he marks the moment when the Yamato court **chose** to
remake itself on the Chinese model — the ideological seed of the centralized imperial
state realized two generations later in the [[taika-reform-645|Taika Reform]] and the
[[nara-period|Nara]] order. He became, and remains, the emblematic founder-figure of
Japanese Buddhism and civilized government.
