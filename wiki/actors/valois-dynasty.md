# The Valois Dynasty (the Late-Medieval French Monarchy)

The **Valois** were the cadet branch of the [[capetian-dynasty|Capetians]] who took the French
throne in 1328 and ruled through the catastrophes and recoveries of the
**[[hundred-years-war-1337|Hundred Years War]]**. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Michael Jones,
Ch. 12a, on 1314–1364; Françoise Autrand, Ch. 12b, on Charles V and VI) traces the French monarchy
from "the culmination of the medieval French monarchy" under [[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV]] to its
near-destruction and partial reconstruction.

## The Valois Succession (1328)

The premature death of Philip IV (1314) "proved to be a major turning-point": his three sons —
Louis X, Philip V, Charles IV — followed one another in quick succession and died without surviving
male heirs (the first failure of the direct Capetian line in three and a half centuries). In 1328
the crown passed to **Philip VI of Valois**, Philip IV's nephew — over the rival claim of
**[[edward-iii-of-england|Edward III]]** of England through his mother, the dynastic spark of the
Hundred Years War. The change of dynasty re-exposed the "fissures in the body politic" that Philip
IV's authoritarian rule had disguised.

## Disaster (1337–1364)

The Valois inherited the **[[late-medieval-crisis|crisis]]**: the **Great Famine of 1315–1317**,
then the **[[black-death|Black Death]]**, halved the population of Paris over a century. War
compounded it: the destruction of the French chivalry at **Crécy (1346)**, the loss of **Calais
(1347)**, and the capture of **King John II** at **Poitiers (1356)** — a king ransomed and held in
London. In the chaos came the **Jacquerie (1358)**, a savage peasant revolt against a nobility
"blamed for dereliction of duty and recent military defeat," and the Parisian revolt of Étienne
Marcel. Petrarch's 1363 lament contrasted the ruined France he saw with "the bountiful riches he had
known in his youth."

## Recovery and Madness (1364–1415)

- **Charles V "the Wise" (1364–1380)** was "considered a model king... a time of reconstruction." With
  the constable **Bertrand du Guesclin** and a strategy of avoiding pitched battle, he recovered
  almost all the English gains by 1380, rebuilt royal finance and administration, and cultivated a
  learned, sacral image of kingship ("the vicar of Christ in his temporality").
- **Charles VI (1380–1422)** began promisingly but descended into recurrent **madness from 1392**.
  His incapacity opened a vicious struggle between the **Armagnac** and **Burgundian** factions for
  control of the realm — a civil war that left France fatally divided on the eve of Henry V's
  invasion and the catastrophe of **Agincourt (1415)**, the point at which this volume closes.

## Significance

The Valois century is the story of the late-medieval French state pushed to the brink and back: a
monarchy that, even "in the kingdom's darkest days," built the household, council, chancery, and
*parlement* that "decisively shaped the *ancien régime*," and forged — through the affective bond
of "his dear and well-beloved" subjects and the trauma of the war — a French **national monarchy**.
Autrand stresses that the king remained "the mainspring of political action," the sacral and human
"fount of grace" at the apex of the developing state.
