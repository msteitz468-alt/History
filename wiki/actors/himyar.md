## Overview

**Himyar** (dhu-Raydan), centered on the highland capital **Zafar**, unified the principal
kingdoms of South Arabia — Saba, Hadramawt, and Main/Qataban's remnants — into a single
polity between the late 3rd and early 4th centuries CE, and remained the dominant power
in the southern Peninsula until its conquest by Sasanian Persia in the 570s. Himyar's kings
never called themselves "Arabs" on their own inscriptions, reserving the term for the
tribal groups they controlled and campaigned against.

## Unification and Northern Expansion

Kings **Yasirum Yuhanim** (c. 265–87) and his son **Shammar Yuharish** (c. 286–311)
conquered Saba (275–300) and subjugated Hadramawt (c. 300), adopting the composite royal
title "kings of Saba, of dhu-Raydan, of Hadramawt, and of Yamnat." Under
**Tharan Yuhanim** (c. 324–c. 375) Himyarite armies, directed largely by the Hadrami
princely house of **dhu-Yazan** and fielding Arab allies [[kinda]], Murad, and Madhhij,
pushed north into Arabia Deserta as far as the Hijaz and Najd. The **Abadan inscription**
(360 CE) records twelve such campaigns in detail, including plunder totals and
irrigation works; the eighth reached al-Kharj near modern Riyadh, the twelfth targeted
the broad tribal collectivity "Maadd." By the mid-5th century, under **Abikarib Asad**
(c. 400–45) and his son **Hassan Yuhamin** (c. 445–50), Himyar's titulature had expanded
to include "of the Arabs of the Upper Country and the Coast" (later softened to "their
Arabs"), and control over central Arabia was exercised through client tribes — Kinda,
Maadd, and Mudar (the tribal group that included the Quraysh, Muhammad's own tribe).

## Judeo-Monotheism

Under Tharan Yuhanim, Himyar broke definitively with polytheism. Polytheist content in
royal inscriptions declines through the 4th century and vanishes entirely by **380 CE**.
Christian Robin's term for the resulting state religion is **"Judeo-Monotheism"** —
a "minimalist" monotheism invoking "the Lord of Heaven" or "the God of Israel," distinct
from and not equivalent to Levantine rabbinic Judaism. The change was formalized under
Tharan Yuhanim's son **Malkikarib Yuhamin** (r. 380–384) at Zafar, where an inscription
records the establishment of a *mikrab*, a site of the new religious practice. The Great
Temple at Marib apparently received no more noble dedications after 380 and may have been
converted to a synagogue; Himyarite aristocrats began asserting membership in "Israel"
rather than their traditional communes. A 2017 inscription discovery records an appeal
to the "God of Israel," reinforcing the epigraphic pattern.

Motives suggested by scholars include personal piety, the political utility of a
unifying state cult across a large, newly-consolidated territory, and a deliberate
counterweight to newly Christian [[aksum]] (whose king Ezana became the first Christian
Aksumite ruler in the 340s and claimed titular authority over Saba and Himyar). A Roman
mission under "Theophilus the Indian," sent by Constantius II to Tharan Yuhanim around
339–344 with the political aim of securing a Christian ally against Persia, won only
grudging permission to build three churches — Himyar's religious trajectory was already
set toward Judaizing monotheism, not Christianity.

## Persian and Aksumite Contest, and Collapse

Himyarite kings cultivated ties to both empires: an embassy under Tharan Yuhanim's son
went to Ctesiphon and to "the land of Tanukh" (al-Hira) around 310, and separately to
Rome in 311. By the late 5th century Himyarite kings had begun persecuting the Christian
community of [[najran]], setting the stage for catastrophe.

Around 500 CE Himyar fell under Aksumite control: king **Kaleb Ella Asbeha** conquered the
kingdom and installed the Christian client **Marthadilan Yanuf** (500–515), commemorated
on an Aksumite stela recording both the campaign and the founding of a Christian
"sanctuary" in Himyar. His successor **Madikarib Yafur**, a staunch Christian ally of
Aksum and Rome, campaigned in 521 as far as southern Iraq against **al-Mundhir III**
(see [[al-mundhir-iii-lakhmid]]) with Mudar and "Thalabite" Arab auxiliaries.

Madikarib's death in 521/2 brought **Joseph** (Dhu Nuwas / Yusuf / Masruq) to the throne.
Joseph quickly rebelled against Aksumite overlordship: he massacred the Aksumite garrison
at Zafar, sacked its church, and in 523 besieged and massacred the Christian community of
Najran — see [[najran-massacre-523]]. The massacre triggered a renewed Aksumite invasion,
Joseph's overthrow, and an anti-Jewish pogrom in Zafar. Aksum's appointee
**Sumuyafa Ashwa** ruled briefly before being overthrown after 531 by **[[abraha]]**, who
established a lasting, largely independent Christian kingdom under nominal Aksumite
suzerainty.

After Abraha's death (c. 565), his sons **Yaksum** and **Masruq** failed to hold the
kingdom together. A Persian invasion under the general Miranes, sent by Khusrau I,
captured Masruq and sacked the Himyarite capital, ending Himyar as an independent state
and installing direct Sasanian rule (the semi-legendary tradition of **Sayf b. dhu-Yazan**
petitioning Persia for aid against "the Ethiopian ravens" belongs to this final phase).

## Historiography

Himyar is known almost entirely through South Arabian (Sabaic) royal inscriptions, cross-
referenced with Aksumite Geez inscriptions, Syriac Christian martyrological texts (the
*Book of the Himyarites*, the letters of Simeon of Beth-Arsham), and later Arab-Islamic
historical tradition (al-Tabari, Ibn Hisham). The "Judeo-Monotheism" transformation is
debated between an older "abrupt shift" reading and Iwona Gajda's argument (based on
newly published texts) for a more gradual process. Christian Robin's epigraphic corpus
underpins most modern reconstruction; casualty and campaign figures in the Najran
massacre illustrate the wide gap between epigraphic and later literary traditions (see
[[najran-massacre-523]]).
