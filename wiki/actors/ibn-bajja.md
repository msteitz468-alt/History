## Overview

Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Yahya ibn al-Sa'igh, known in the Islamic world as Ibn Bajja and in Latin as Avempace, was the first Muslim philosopher in Andalusia to adopt wholeheartedly the Aristotelian style of philosophy that had been cultivated in Baghdad. He lived under the [[almoravids|Almoravid]] dynasty and died c. 1138, probably in Fez. A physician, mathematician, logician, musician, and philosopher, Ibn Bajja was the foundational figure of Muslim [[andalusi-philosophy|Andalusi philosophy]] in the strict sense — the predecessor from whom both [[ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]] and [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] drew essential inspiration.

## Intellectual Position

Ibn Bajja "harks back to a pre-Avicennan phase in the history of philosophy, adhering more to the style of thought we found in [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]]" (Adamson, ch. 24). While Avicenna dominated the eastern Islamic tradition of his day, Ibn Bajja's philosophical allegiance was to the stricter Aristotelianism of the Baghdad school. In this he anticipated Averroes, who likewise took al-Farabi rather than Avicenna as his primary Islamic authority, and who shared Ibn Bajja's commitment to the demonstrative character of philosophy compared to the rhetorical nature of religious discourse.

## Major Works

**Rule of the Solitary** (*Tadbir al-mutawahhid*): Ibn Bajja's most famous work and the one with the clearest political and philosophical significance. Like al-Farabi's major works, it combines metaphysical speculation with political philosophy. Ibn Bajja accepts al-Farabi's idea of the "virtuous city" as theoretically possible, but focuses on the more realistic situation of the philosopher living in an imperfect, morally corrupt society. He applies the term "weed" to such a philosopher, "indicating the philosopher's tendency to undermine the values of the society in which he lives" (Adamson, ch. 24). Some attention is given to how a philosopher might come to rule, but Ibn Bajja does not treat this as a realistic prospect. The solitary philosopher is his characteristic subject.

This work directly influenced [[ibn-tufayl|Ibn Tufayl]]: the island narrative of *Hayy ibn Yaqzan* is explicitly a development of the "solitary philosopher" theme, even as Ibn Tufayl diverged sharply from Ibn Bajja's intellectualism toward mystical Avicennism.

**On the Soul / Theory of the Intellect**: Ibn Bajja wrote on the nature of the intellect, producing an account that shaped Averroes' subsequent, more developed and controversial theory. Ibn Bajja associated the human "material intellect" (the capacity for potential universal knowledge) with the imagination — the faculty that stores and manipulates particular sensory images. When the imagination has been sufficiently prepared through experience, the human soul undergoes "unification" with the universal Agent Intellect, achieving actual universal knowledge. Averroes admired this theory initially and adopted it in his *Epitome* of Aristotle's *On the Soul*, before rejecting it in his more mature commentaries as inconsistent with the immaterial nature that the intellect must have.

## Relation to Ibn Tufayl and Averroes

Ibn Tufayl knew Ibn Bajja's work well but was critical of it, seeing him as "a limited mind, incapable of the mystical heights reached by Avicenna and al-Ghazali" (Adamson, ch. 24, paraphrasing Ibn Tufayl's preface to *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*). This is somewhat unfair: Ibn Bajja was doing a different kind of philosophy — rigorously Aristotelian and political, not mystically inclined. Ibn Tufayl was the "chorizo" in the Peripatetic paella of Andalusi philosophy; Ibn Bajja and Averroes were the paella itself.

Averroes acknowledged the influence of Ibn Bajja's theory of the intellect on his own early position. His eventual rejection of Ibn Bajja's identification of the material intellect with imagination was a crucial step toward the notorious "unity of the human intellect" doctrine.

The intellectual lineage is: **Ibn Bajja → Ibn Tufayl (reaction) → Averroes (fulfillment)**. But the more philosophically coherent continuity is **Ibn Bajja → Averroes**, with Ibn Tufayl as an interlude representing a different, Avicennan-mystical strand.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Ibn Bajja, the reception of al-Farabi's strict Aristotelianism in Andalusia might have been significantly delayed or attenuated. He established the standard of philosophical rigor — demonstrative argument from Aristotelian premises — against which subsequent thinkers in the west defined themselves. Averroes' enormous project of Aristotelian commentary is intelligible as the fulfillment of what Ibn Bajja began.

## Historiography

Ibn Bajja's works survive only partially in Arabic; some are available in modern editions. He has received less scholarly attention than Averroes or Ibn Tufayl in Western scholarship, partly because his major work *Rule of the Solitary* was not translated into Latin or Hebrew and thus did not shape the scholastic reception. Adamson's treatment positions him as foundational rather than derivative, correcting an underestimation that follows from focusing too narrowly on transmitted influence.
