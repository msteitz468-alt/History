# Emperor Kammu (737–806, r. 781–806)

Emperor Kammu was the fiftieth sovereign of Japan and the last of what the
*Cambridge History of Japan* calls "a line of puissant, capital-building monarchs
who were able to mobilize the entire country's wealth and military power for
national or dynastic purposes." His reign initiated the [[heian-period|Heian period]]
and fixed its capital at [[heian-kyo|Heian-kyō]] (modern Kyoto). He is widely
considered the most powerful ruler the imperial line ever produced.
[[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]]

## Origins and Accession

Kammu was an obscure official in his mid-thirties when dynastic politics elevated
his father, Kōnin (702–782, r. 770–781), to the throne in 770. His elevation to
crown prince three years later was complicated: his mother's patriline, the
Korean-descended Yamato lineage, was considered of "humble immigrant origins,"
and this may have initially worked against his selection as heir apparent.

He came to the throne on his father's abdication in 781 — a "learned peer of his
illiterate contemporary Charlemagne," in the chapter's formulation — after a
period of consolidation under Kōnin that had already weakened Buddhist and
secular opposition to imperial authority.

## Path to Power

Kammu's freedom to rule was enabled by a structural vacuum at court. After the
downfall of Fujiwara no Nakamaro in 764 and the reconstitution of government
under the monk Dōkyō (d. 772), men of lesser, "bureaucratic" clans entered the
central councils, reducing the Council of State's power to check the emperor.
After his accession, Kammu saw to it that a new generation of Fujiwara leaders
was not given the opportunity to establish hegemony at court. He kept leading
governmental offices either vacant or filled with imperial family members:
the post of Minister of the Left remained vacant throughout all but the first year
of his reign; after 796 the post of Minister of the Right was held by his cousin
Prince Miwa; and "there was not a single Fujiwara officeholder above the level
of Middle Counselor during the final decade of the reign (796–806)."

He also built a tight circle of personal loyalists through kinship, marital alliances,
and large land grants — "particularly generous in his treatment of relatives of his
mother and grandmother."

## Capital Transfers

Kammu presided over two capital moves. In 784 he relocated the court to
**Nagaoka**, near the Katsura River about twenty miles northwest of Nara. The
move was led by his chief adviser Fujiwara no Tanetsugu (737–785), whose
maternal family (the Korean-descended Hata) had roots in the area. Nagaoka
was plagued by political catastrophe almost immediately: Tanetsugu was
assassinated in autumn 785, and the heir apparent Prince Sawara — implicated
in the murder — was deposed and died in exile or fasting. The subsequent
disasters (famine, floods, epidemic, deaths in the imperial family) were
interpreted as the vengeance of Sawara's angry spirit.

After ten years at Nagaoka, acting on the recommendation of Wake no Kiyomaro
(733–799), Kammu in early 793 accepted the choice of a new site at Heian in
the Kadono area. He moved into the new palace at [[heian-kyo|Heian-kyō]] in
the tenth month of 794.

## The Emishi Wars

The most sustained military challenge of Kammu's reign was the series of
campaigns against the **Emishi**, a non-subject people inhabiting the northeastern
part of Honshu (in what are now Fukushima, Niigata, and further north). Their
name and ethnic identity remain disputed — the *Cambridge History* notes that
"even their name is a matter of dispute." They ranged from hunter-gatherers to
agriculturalists; some held Japanese provincial appointments.

The immediate trigger was the revolt of Koreharu no Azamaro in 780, which
led to the burning of Taga Fort. Major campaigns in 780–781, 788–789, and
794 met with defeat or inconclusive results. Not until 801–802 did Japanese
forces under **Sakanoue no Tamuramaro** (758–811) — described as a
"red-visaged, yellow-bearded warrior giant" of about five feet ten inches —
defeat the main Emishi leader Aterui and permanently occupy Isawa. A final
limited action by Fun'ya no Watamaro in 811 brought large-scale fighting to
a close, and in 812 most northeastern armies were demobilized.

In 805, Kammu terminated the campaigns specifically because of cost. Fujiwara
no Ōtsugu (774–843) asserted that "the wars against the Emishi and construction
work in the new capital at Kyoto were the two chief causes of distress among
the peasantry."

## Administrative and Fiscal Reforms

Kammu addressed the [[ritsuryo-state|ritsuryō system's]] financial deterioration
with sustained attention. He tinkered constantly with tax laws and focused
heavily on provincial compliance with central law. In 798 he redefined imperial
clan membership to exclude fifth-generation descendants (restoring the original
statutory definition), and in 805 reduced more than one hundred princes and
princesses to noble status with clan names, thereby removing them from the
imperial family's expensive support obligations.

He also dismantled the military conscript system outside Kyushu, northern
Honshu, and Sado in 792, replacing it with small rotating bands of "stalwart
youths" (*kondei*) from district officials' families — a shift that contributed to
the later growth of private provincial warrior forces.

## Character and Governing Style

The official chronicler recorded that Kammu "had no use for 'literary floweriness'"
and was equally uninterested in the "extravagant Buddhistic devotion that had
nearly bankrupted the state under Shōmu and Kōken/Shōtoku." His reign
is remembered "chiefly not because of grand temples, magnificent art, or
superlative literature, but for its accomplishments in city building, war, and
governmental reform." He was trained in the Confucian pedagogic tradition
and appears to have been a deliberate, unsentimental ruler — though he stopped
short of ruthless manipulation of noble lives (no evidence supports his direct
role in the Tanetsugu–Sawara affair, even if he benefited from it).

## Legacy and Counterfactual Significance

After Kammu's death in 806, the Fujiwara and other leading clans began
immediately to re-enter the highest offices. His three successors — Heizei,
Saga, and Junna — each had Fujiwara mothers yet managed relatively
independent courses for several decades, partly because of rivalries within the
clan. The structural shift toward Fujiwara dominance became irreversible only
after Saga's death in 842.

Counterfactually: without Kammu's personal authority and deliberate exclusion of
Fujiwara clan leadership from top posts during 796–806, the Fujiwara regency
might have been established a generation earlier. His reign created a window of
unchallenged imperial rule that was never replicated. He was also the last emperor
for whom the final decision in major matters "was his, both in name and in fact."
