# Abdallah 'Azzam (1941–1989)

Palestinian scholar and member of the Jordanian Muslim Brotherhood who became the
**most influential ideologue and recruiter of the anti-Soviet Afghan jihad**, and a
principal architect of the transnational "Afghan-Arab" movement out of which
[[al-qaeda]] emerged.

## Role

'Azzam traveled to Islamabad in 1981 and provided, in Formichi's words, "the
theoretical scaffolding for the anti-Soviet resistance to be presented as an
individual duty for all Muslims" (*fard 'ayn*) — obligating Muslims worldwide, not
just Afghans, to fight. He framed the liberation of Afghanistan as the first step
toward reclaiming other Muslim lands ("Palestine, the Philippines, Kashmir, Lebanon,
Chad, Eritrea, etc."). In 1984 he opened the **Services Bureau** (Maktab al-Khidamat)
in Peshawar to channel foreign volunteers and funds into the
[[soviet-war-in-afghanistan-1979|Afghan jihad]] — the organizational seed of the
international jihadi network. By 1986 'Azzam, Osama bin Laden, and Ayman al-Zawahiri
were the international face of the [[afghan-mujahideen|Afghan resistance]]. 'Azzam also
co-founded Lashkar-e Toiba (1990, posthumously credited), later active in Kashmir.

His jihad theory originated in his views on Palestine: he condemned Soviet involvement
in the Palestinian cause and Yasser Arafat's secular PLO leadership.

## Death and Consequence

'Azzam was killed by a roadside bomb in Peshawar in 1989. His death removed the more
"classically" anti-occupation voice from the movement and, per Formichi, allowed
al-Zawahiri's more militant, globally offensive approach — and bin Laden's
[[al-qaeda]] — to dominate the Afghan-Arab legacy.

## Counterfactual Significance

'Azzam is the pivotal figure who converted a territorial anti-Soviet insurgency into a
*global* obligation. Without his doctrine of jihad as individual duty and his Peshawar
recruitment infrastructure, the internationalization of the Afghan jihad — and the
network that became al-Qaeda — is hard to reconstruct.

## Historiography

Formichi (2020) treats 'Azzam within her broader argument that Asia (here, the
"heart of Asia," Afghanistan) became the arena where transnational Islamic solidarity
was mobilized. She is critical of the post-9/11 "terrorism studies" literature that
reads this history backward from al-Qaeda.
