# False Dmitry I (Gregory Otrepiev)

## Biography / Description
False Dmitry I was the first and most successful of the pretenders during the [[time-of-troubles-1598|Time of Troubles]]. Most historians identify him as Gregory Otrepiev, a young man of service-class origin who became a monk and later fled to the Cossacks. Strongly believing himself to be the true Prince Dmitrii of Uglich (the son of Ivan IV who had supposedly escaped the 1591 assassination), Otrepiev appeared in Lithuania in 1603 seeking support (Lines 2239-2245).

Dmitry obtained backing from the Jesuits (by promising to bring Catholicism to Russia) and from Polish-Lithuanian aristocrats, notably marrying Marina Mniszech, the daughter of a Polish magnate (Line 2249). In October 1604, he invaded Russia with a small army. Benefiting from widespread social discontent and the sudden death of Tsar Boris Godunov in April 1605, Dmitry entered Moscow in triumph on June 20, 1605, and was crowned tsar. His rule lasted less than a year. He was killed during a boyar-led coup on the night of May 26, 1606 (Lines 2251-2269).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Mobilization of Discontent:** Dmitry successfully capitalized on the social and political grievances of the southern Russian borderlands, rallying Cossacks, runaway peasants, and townspeople who resented the Godunov regime (Line 2251).
- **Consolidation of Power:** Upon his accession, Dmitry recalled exiled boyars, including Philaret (Theodore) Romanov, whom he elevated to Metropolitan of Rostov (Line 2257). He replaced Patriarch Job with Ignatius, a Greek cleric (Line 2253).
- **Independent Stance:** Once on the throne, Dmitry refused to act as a Polish puppet. He did not introduce Catholicism as promised, but instead proposed a grand European crusade to drive the Ottoman Turks out of Europe (Line 2259).
- **Cultural Friction:** Dmitry alienated conservative Muscovite society by ignoring court etiquette, refusing to take afternoon naps, dressing in Polish attire, and surrounding himself with a loud, Catholic Polish entourage (Lines 2261-2263).

## Historical Significance
False Dmitry I's brief reign demonstrated the extreme fragility of the Muscovite autocracy in the absence of a legitimate dynasty. His rise was heavily facilitated by Russian boyar factions who utilized the "Dmitry" myth as a tool (a "boyar stratagem") to destroy the Godunov family (Line 2249). The violent coup that ended his life, led by Basil Shuisky, marked the transition of the Time of Troubles from a dynastic succession crisis into a destructive, multi-layered social and national war (Line 2275-2281).
