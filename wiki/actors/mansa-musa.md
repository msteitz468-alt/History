# Mansa Musa

## Overview

**Mansa Musa** (Musa I of Mali, r. c. 1312–1337) was the ruler under whom the
[[mali-empire|Mali Empire]] reached its territorial and economic apogee, and the
West African figure most visible in the medieval Mediterranean and European
imagination. [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Conrad, Ch 22) treats his reign as the high
point of the [[western-sudan-states|Western Sudanic]] states' integration into the
wider [[afro-eurasian-world-system|Afro-Eurasian]] and [[islam|Islamic]] worlds.

## The Pilgrimage of 1324

Musa's defining act was his **hajj to Mecca in 1324**, a journey of legendary
opulence: a vast caravan carrying so much gold that his lavish distribution and
spending in **Cairo** reportedly depressed the value of gold there for years. The
pilgrimage announced Mali's wealth across the Islamic world, drew scholars,
architects, and books back to the empire, and strengthened Mali's ties to the
broader *dar al-Islam*. It is the principal reason Musa entered European geographic
knowledge: the **Catalan Atlas of 1375** depicts him enthroned, crowned, and holding
a golden nugget — "the richest and most noble king" of the lands of the Black gold.

## Significance

Musa consolidated [[islam]] among Mali's ruling and merchant elite (he patronized
mosque construction, including the Djinguereber mosque at **Timbuktu**, and the city's
rise as a center of trade and Islamic learning) while ruling over a population that
remained largely committed to indigenous religious practice. His reign exemplifies the
[[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan]] gold economy at its height and the way West
Africa, a commercial "cul-de-sac" in Richard Smith's typology
([[afro-eurasian-world-system]]), nonetheless fed one of the medieval world's most
important bullion flows. His historical visibility — far greater than that of most
medieval West African rulers — owes much to the convergence of the Arabic written
record (al-Umari's account of the Cairo visit) with the [[mali-empire|Mande oral
tradition]].
