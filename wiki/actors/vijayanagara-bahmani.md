# Vijayanagara and the Bahmani Kingdom

**Vijayanagara** and the **Bahmani kingdom** were the two great rival states of the **Deccan and the
south** that emerged from the retreat of the [[delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate's]] over-extended
southern conquests — one Hindu, one Muslim, divided by the **Krishna/Tungabhadra** frontier and locked
in centuries of conflict. Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## Common origin in the Sultanate's collapse

Both states were "indirectly" founded by the **withdrawal of Sultanate power** from the Deccan after
Muhammad bin Tughluq's failures. The **Bahmani dynasty** (1347) arose when the **Turkish governor of
the Deccan revolted**; **Vijayanagara** (the "City of Victory," near Hampi, capital from 1343) was
founded further south, where the Hoysalas had ruled, by **Harihara and Bukka** — two princes whom the
Sultanate had captured and **converted to Islam**, who then returned south, **reverted to Hinduism**
(a brahman removing the taboo by declaring Harihara the earthly agent of the god Virupaksha), and
built an independent kingdom.

## Vijayanagara: a "Hindu revival"?

Thapar **questions** the common claim that Vijayanagara represented a **Hindu revival** against Islam.
Its kings patronised temples (embellishing old ones, building new) partly to **appease religious
authority** lest the founders' apostasy be used against them — but they did **not** stir anti-Muslim
sentiment, form a Hindu alliance, or hesitate to **attack other Hindu kings** (crushing the Hoysalas
in 1346, conquering Madurai in 1370) when it suited them. Vijayanagara was a powerful, centralising
state: clearing forest and settling new land, building **irrigation tanks and dams**, tightening
revenue, importing horses and **Turkish mercenaries** to improve its cavalry — the south's answer to
the military pressures of the age.

## The Bahmani kingdom and the Deccan rivalry

The **Bahmani kingdom** (capital Gulbarga, later Bidar) was the Muslim power of the northern Deccan;
its conflict with Vijayanagara over the **Raichur Doab** and the east-coast deltas was "unavoidable"
(hostilities from 1358), the frontier shifting with each campaign. Its ablest figure was the minister
**Mahmud Gavan** (15th c.), who recaptured **Goa** (diverting its trade revenue back) — but the
kingdom was wrecked by the factional split between **Deccanis** (domiciled immigrants and local
converts) and **Pardesis** (newly-arrived foreigners), to which Gavan fell victim. The Bahmani realm
fragmented (by 1527) into the five **Deccan sultanates** — which would later combine to break
Vijayanagara at **Talikota (1565)**.

## Significance

The two kingdoms carried the political life of the **peninsula** into the early-modern era and embody
Thapar's recurring **north–south** and **Hindu–Muslim** themes — not as a simple religious war (which
she is at pains to deny) but as a **regional power struggle** in which both sides used mercenaries of
the other faith and fought co-religionists freely. Vijayanagara was the last great Hindu empire of the
south; its capital Hampi remains one of India's most spectacular ruins.

## The Bahmani sultanate in detail (CHI Vol. III)

The [[cambridge-history-of-india-v3|Cambridge History of India, Vol. III]] (Haig, 1928) devotes
several chapters to the **Bahmani kingdom** itself. Founded in 1347 when the Deccan governors
revolted from [[muhammad-bin-tughluq|Muhammad bin Tughluq]] and raised **Ala-ud-din Hasan Bahman
Shah** to the throne, it was the first independent Muslim power of the Deccan, ruling from Gulbarga
and later **Bidar**. Its high point came under the great Persian minister **Mahmud Gawan**
(d. 1481), who expanded the kingdom to its widest extent and reformed its administration — but the
Bahmani state was riven by a bitter factional rivalry between the **"foreigners" (afaqis** —
Persian and other immigrant Muslims) and the **"Deccanis"** (locally born Muslims and Abyssinians),
and Mahmud Gawan's execution on a forged charge accelerated its decline. In the decades after 1490
the kingdom fragmented into the **five Deccan sultanates** (Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmadnagar, Bidar,
Berar), which in 1565 combined to crush Vijayanagara at **Talikota**. (CHI Vol. III is dated, mined
here for the Bahmani dynastic narrative.)
