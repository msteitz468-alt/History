# The Coptic Church

The **Coptic Church** is the native Christian church of Egypt — "Coptic" deriving (via Arabic
*qibt*) from Greek *Aigyptios*, "Egyptian." Emerging from the Christianisation of Egypt in the
fourth and fifth centuries and the **non-Chalcedonian** stance taken after 451, it became, and
remains, the church of Egypt's Christian population, surviving the Arab conquest as a permanent
minority down to the present.

## Origins and Christianisation

Coptic tradition attributes the coming of Christianity to Alexandria to **St Mark the Evangelist**.
Egypt's large Jewish community provided the early milieu; papyrus fragments of the New Testament
show a Christian presence by the third century, but it was in the **fourth century** that
Christianity made serious inroads into the countryside — visible in the papyri as churches, an
ecclesiastical hierarchy of bishops, priests, and deacons, and a decisive shift in personal names
from pagan to biblical. By the fifth century, "Egypt had become a Christian land," its villages
full of churches and its deserts full of monasteries (see [[egyptian-monasticism]]).

## The Chalcedonian schism and the dual patriarchate

The church's defining rupture came with the **[[council-of-chalcedon-451|Council of Chalcedon]]
(451)**, which defined Christ as existing "in two natures." The dominant Egyptian position rejected
this in favour of **Miaphysitism** — the belief that the incarnate Christ had **one united
nature** (divine and human conjoined). The result was a lasting split and a **dual patriarchate**
in Alexandria: a **Chalcedonian** patriarch (later called **Melkite**, "royal," because backed by
the emperor) and a **non-Chalcedonian (Miaphysite)** patriarch — the Coptic line. Both claimed
descent from St Mark; both outlasted the Arab conquest.

This was not a marginal squabble but the central religious-political fault line of late-antique
Egypt. The imperial church in Constantinople repeatedly tried, by force and by compromise formulae,
to bring Egypt back into communion; the failure of these efforts left Egypt religiously estranged
from the empire on the eve of the Persian and Arab invasions.

## Significance

- **A national church.** The Coptic Church fused Egyptian Christianity with the Coptic language and
  the monastic movement; its literature (above all [[shenoute|Shenoute]]) and its monasteries
  (Wadi Natrun) carried Egyptian Christian identity through the centuries.
- **Survival past the conquest.** The conversion of Egypt to Islam after 641 was "even more gradual
  than its earlier conversion to Christianity, and likewise never complete." The Coptic Church
  endured as the institution of the Christian minority that "remains an important factor in Egypt
  down to the present day."
- **Counterfactual.** Egypt's Miaphysite estrangement is often cited (cautiously) as one reason the
  province offered limited loyalty to Byzantium during the [[arab-conquest-of-egypt-641|Arab
  conquest]] — though Bagnall-Rathbone-style documentary history warns against reducing a complex
  conquest to religious grievance alone.
