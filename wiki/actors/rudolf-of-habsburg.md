# Rudolf I of Habsburg (r. 1273–1291)

**Rudolf**, count of Habsburg, was elected king of the Romans in 1273, ending the
**[[german-interregnum-1250|Interregnum]]** and laying "the foundations for the long-lived
Habsburg predominance in the south-east." [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Michael Toch,
Ch. 9a) sets him between two readings: the traditional one of a "hopeful new beginning," and
a less enthusiastic recent view that makes him "the first of a line of 'small' kings" (*reges
Allamanniae*) working within an underequipped, electorally constrained monarchy.

## The Election and the Towns

Elected at Frankfurt (October 1273) by all the electors except the absent Ottokar of
Bohemia, Rudolf had a "real rapport with the towns": his first acts were charters for the
cities that had agitated for him, and he liked to lodge with rich burghers (leaving "behind
unpaid debts"). For the first time **ecclesiastics of urban origin** sat among the king's
closest advisers (Heinrich of Isny). Yet he also demanded regular and special taxes, and when
direct property levies and stationed troops provoked resistance — and **pseudo-emperors**
claiming to be Frederick II appeared in the Rhine valley — he shifted course, ending direct
levies and, in 1287, calling town representatives to deliberate on imperial finances. He
worked to **regain the crown lands** lost since 1245, reorganising them into *Landvogteien*
under bailiffs.

## The Defeat of Ottokar II

Rudolf's great achievement was the destruction of **Ottokar II of Bohemia**, who at the
height of his power had seized **Austria, Styria and Carinthia** and refused homage. Outlawed
(1276), Ottokar was forced into a "poignant scene of submission," kneeling "in kingly
splendour before Rudolf, who was clad in a simple leather jerkin." When the accord broke
down, Rudolf — with Hungarian help and "somewhat un-knightly tactics" — overpowered the
Bohemian army at the **battle of Dürnkrut/Marchfeld (August 1278)**, where Ottokar was killed.
This was "the breakthrough": Bohemia passed to Ottokar's son, Carinthia to the count of Tyrol,
and **Austria and Styria to Rudolf's own sons** — the territorial nucleus of Habsburg power.
The electors' formal consent to this disposition (the *Willebriefe*, 1282) became a
**constitutional precedent**: later grants of imperial fiefs needed electoral endorsement.

## Significance and Limits

Rudolf "could not go beyond the inherent constraints of the political system." He failed to
secure the **imperial coronation** that might have guaranteed a Habsburg succession, and his
Italian plans came to nothing (he renounced the imperial claim to the Romagna to the papacy).
His reign exemplifies the new **Hausmacht kingship** of the post-Interregnum Empire: real
power built on a consolidated regional power-base rather than the whole of Germany. His death
(1291) "vividly expressed the duality between the old political legacy and the novel
circumstances" — the old man riding to Speyer to be buried among the Salian and Hohenstaufen
emperors, his tomb effigy "the first one in Germany to show a king's portrait in a naturalistic
manner." His son **Albrecht I** would (after the interlude of **Adolf of Nassau**, 1292–1298)
make the Habsburg claim good by force.
