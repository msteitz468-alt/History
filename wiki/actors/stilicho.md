# Stilicho

The **half-Vandal, half-Roman generalissimo** (*magister utriusque militiae*) who governed the western
empire as guardian of the child emperor **Honorius** from 395 to 408 — the dominant figure of the West in
the years after [[theodosius-i|Theodosius I]]. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 4
(**R. C. Blockley**).

## The Theodosian regent

On Theodosius' death (395), Stilicho — married to Theodosius' niece Serena — claimed the guardianship of
**both** young emperors, but his insistence on the **unity of the empire** was emphatically rejected by
the eastern court, and his career was consumed by friction with Constantinople and by the problem of
**[[alaric|Alaric]]**, whom he repeatedly fought but never destroyed (and at times tried to employ).
His greatest success was the annihilation of **Radagaisus'** invasion of Italy (406); but the crossing of
the Rhine by the Vandals, Sueves and Alans (406/7), the usurpation of Constantine III in Gaul, and his
payment of **4,000 pounds of gold to Alaric** (408) eroded his standing.

## Fall

The faction of **Olympius** exploited the army's anti-barbarian mood; at Ticinum the troops massacred
Stilicho's supporters, and — refusing to let his barbarian soldiers resist — Stilicho took sanctuary and
was **executed at Ravenna (22 August 408)**. The ensuing massacre of the families of barbarian federates
drove some 30,000 of them to join Alaric — directly precipitating the march on Rome.

## Significance

Blockley defends Stilicho against the charges of treason and incompetence, locating his real failures in
**policy**: his insistence on imperial unity proved divisive, his **reliance on barbarian federates**
inhibited the rebuilding of a Roman army and ultimately destroyed the western governing consensus, and
his prioritizing of the **defence of Italy and the emperor at Ravenna** led to the loss of the northern
provinces. "In a very real sense the failure of Stilicho was the failure of Theodosius" — the
working-out of the Theodosian legacy of federate dependence and dynastic guardianship. His death removed
the one figure who might have managed Alaric, and the sack of Rome followed within two years.
