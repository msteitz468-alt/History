## Role and Background

Kuroda Jōsui, also known as Kuroda Kanbei, was a veteran general, strategist, and advisor to both Oda Nobunaga and [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Toyotomi Hideyoshi]], and one of [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu's]] most important and trusted supporters. He was the father of Kuroda Nagamasa, who served in the Eastern army at Sekigahara and whom Ieyasu singled out as "most responsible for today's victory." Jōsui himself conducted a parallel campaign in Kyushu during the Sekigahara campaign of 1600 that extended Eastern control across southern Japan.

He is characterized by Sadler as perhaps the only figure of the era who might plausibly have contested Ieyasu for dominance had circumstances been different: "though he was as capable in many ways as Ieyasu and now perhaps the only one who might have contested the Empire with him, yet he really stood no chance of success as affairs had turned out."

## The Kyushu Campaign (1600)

While his son Nagamasa fought at Sekigahara, Jōsui organized a parallel campaign in Kyushu against Western partisans, beginning before Ieyasu had even left for the east. With all able-bodied samurai already in the field with Nagamasa, Jōsui raised a volunteer force of about 3,600 men from ronin, old men, retired men (*inkyo*), traders, farmers, and artisans. He greeted them personally with appropriate compliments — praising older men for their experience, younger men for their vigor — and distributed pay without investigating the several who came forward more than once: "on such an occasion it was not advisable to make any invidious investigations."

He organized a coordinated Kyushu campaign:
- Secured the castle of Kitsuke (belonging to Hosokawa Yusai's Kyushu fief, which Ishida had arranged to divide and weaken)
- Assisted Kato Kiyomasa's wife, who had escaped from Osaka, providing her clothes and escort home
- Sent news of the fall of Gifu to Kato Kiyomasa, prompting him to take the offensive
- Seized the strong places of Takaku, Usuki, Saeki, and Kokura
- Moved into Chikuzen province
- Assisted in the siege of Yanagawa, the capital of Tachibana Muneshige

His naval forces intercepted some of Shimazu's retreating transports after Sekigahara, though the engagement — which resulted in the sinking of ships carrying mostly women and children — drew his sharp rebuke: "What's the use of this sort of thing? Quite an unnecessary piece of work. And killing women and children is inhumane, too!" He recovered his composure quickly, acknowledging that "it was difficult to blame men who had fought so toughly."

He negotiated the surrender of Tachibana Muneshige at Yanagawa — Jōsui and Kato were old campaign companions of Muneshige from the Korean war and were reluctant to destroy him — persuading him to surrender with a guarantee of Ieyasu's pardon, which was fulfilled.

## Ambition and Self-Restraint

The Kyushu campaign raised a sharp strategic question. Ieyasu sent letters — along with similar ones from Ii Naomasa and Honda Tadakatsu — expressing concern for Jōsui's health if he ventured on a winter campaign in Satsuma, which has a warm climate. The letters were, as Sadler notes, "merely Ieyasu's way of hinting that he saw through Jōsui's energetic fishing in troubled waters." Jōsui may have intended to conquer Kyushu and then operate in the Kansai if anything happened to Ieyasu, leveraging his connections and recently won territory.

He recognized the signal: "'Aha, he knows!' he is reported to have exclaimed when told that Ieyasu's enthusiasm for his continued activity in the field was not very apparent." He then withdrew from active campaigning and "became the very complete altruist philosopher." When Ieyasu offered him large rewards in territory and court rank, Jōsui declined: "I am old and my health is poor... All I wish for is permission to spend my remaining days in peace." Ieyasu was delighted to grant this.

His son Nagamasa was given Chikuzen with 523,000 koku. Jōsui later retired to a villa in the Ichijo district of Kyoto, then to Dazaifu in Kyushu, where he built a small tearoom before the shrine of Tenjin (the deified poet Michizane) and "made tea and poems and diverted himself with the simple pleasures of the scenery."

## Character

Jōsui combined strategic brilliance with aestheticism and philosophical temperament. He practiced Zen at the Daitokuji under the master Haruya Osho, "considering the origins of life and death and thereby gaining extreme peace of mind." He interceded with Ieyasu for the lives of Otomo Yoshimune and Shimazu Yoshihiro. He supervised the early construction of Fukuoka (the new castle town his son built, named by Jōsui after his ancestral home in Bizen), which became one of the strongest places in the country.

His maxim on his own career: "I served Nobunaga and Hideyoshi, and three times I had differences of opinion with them and went into retirement... I used not to think very deeply before acting, for I rushed in with a sandal on one foot and a clog on the other." He died with "claims on both the Buddhist and Christian heavens, as well as on Shinto deification" — reflecting the religious syncretism of the era. He is commemorated at the Ryuko-in chapel in the Daitokuji.

The source notes his health was declining, with "contemporary opinion blaming the spirochaete" (a reference to syphilis), which may have made his graceful retirement more sincere than purely tactical.
