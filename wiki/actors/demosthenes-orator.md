# Demosthenes (Orator and Statesman)

> **Disambiguation:** this page covers **Demosthenes the fourth-century Athenian orator
> and statesman**, chief opponent of [[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II]]. He is a
> **distinct individual** from the fifth-century Athenian general Demosthenes of the
> Peloponnesian War (the Pylos/Sphacteria strategist and Sicilian relief commander), who
> is covered at [[demosthenes-athenian-general]]. Ancient sources use the same name for
> both men; do not conflate them.

## Overview

**Demosthenes** (384–322 BCE) was the leading Athenian orator and statesman of the
mid-to-late fourth century BCE, the most persistent and effective voice organizing Greek
resistance to **[[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II of Macedon]]**. His surviving speeches
are, per [[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC*
(2000)]], the **single most important contemporary window onto Philip's reign**, since no
independent ancient history of Philip survives.

## The anti-Macedonian campaign

Through the 340s Demosthenes delivered a series of speeches — the **Philippics** and
**Olynthiacs** among them — warning Athens of the danger Philip posed and urging military
and diplomatic resistance. Osborne notes that Demosthenes's own portrayal of Philip in
these speeches casts the Macedonian king as a scheming **"barbarian"** who fought by
trickery and bribery rather than open, honorable Greek warfare, with Demosthenes himself
cast as the **champion of Greek freedom**. Despite the temporary **Peace of Philocrates**
(346), which Demosthenes helped negotiate but came to regret, he continued working to
build an anti-Macedonian coalition, finally succeeding in forging the joint
**Athenian–Theban alliance** that met Philip at **[[battle-of-chaeronea-338bce|
Chaeronea]]** (338) — where the coalition was decisively crushed.

## The contested reputation: Aeschines and Isocrates

Osborne stresses that Demosthenes's self-presentation should not be taken at face value.
His chief political rival, **Aeschines**, and other opponents, portray a very different
Demosthenes: **inconsistent**, prone to reversing position, and motivated by personal
ambition and factional advantage rather than principled Panhellenic idealism. The
contemporary orator **Isocrates** represents a third position entirely — his pamphlet
*Philippus* (346) called on Philip himself to become the **champion of Greek concord**
and lead a Panhellenic expedition against Persia, a pro-Macedonian Panhellenism that
directly contradicts Demosthenes's framing of Philip as Greece's enemy. Historians must
therefore read the run-up to Chaeronea through **three competing, self-interested
Athenian voices**, not a single reliable narrative.

## After Chaeronea

Demosthenes survived Chaeronea and continued in Athenian public life, later delivering
his most celebrated speech, **On the Crown** (330), a defense of his career against
Aeschines's prosecution of Ctesiphon. He was later implicated in the **Harpalus affair**
(324), fined and exiled, but was recalled after Alexander's death (323) to help organize
the Athenian-led revolt against Macedon (the Lamian War). When the revolt failed, he fled
and took his own life at **Calauria** in 322 rather than fall into Antipater's hands.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Demosthenes's decades of agitation, Athens might have accommodated Macedonian
power far earlier and with less resistance; his career is the textbook demonstration of
principled (or, per his critics, self-serving) opposition failing against superior
Macedonian military and diplomatic resources. His reputation as the paradigmatic defender
of civic freedom against tyranny long outlived the immediate failure of his policy.

## OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Hornblower (Ch.6), Murray (Ch.9)

[[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]]:

**Near-success at Chaeronea (Hornblower).** Amid Greek weakness that enabled [[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II]], Hornblower notes that “Demosthenes’ propagandist and political efforts almost succeeded” — shown by the closeness of Philip’s final victory at Chaeronea (338). League of Corinth then entrenched possessing classes; closing line of the political chapter: “Imperialism had after all proved incompatible with democracy.” Demosthenes stands as the last major democratic-imperial voice against Macedonian monarchy.

**Family wealth (Murray Ch.9).** The father of the politician Demosthenes owned two manufactories—one making swords with over thirty slaves, the other making couches with twenty—illustrating craft “factory” scale available to elite political families (Lysias’ shield factory at 120 slaves was the largest known). Portraiture advances later include a Demosthenes type (~280; Boardman Ch.12/R7 art notes).
