# Sallust (Gaius Sallustius Crispus)

Rome's **first major historian whose work survives** (c. 86–35 BCE) — the creator of the Latin
**historical monograph** and the analyst of the Republic's moral and political decline whose terse,
moralizing, Thucydidean manner became the model for **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]**. Author of the
*[[sallust-conspiracy-jugurtha-histories|Catiline's Conspiracy, the Jugurthine War, and the
Histories]]*.

## Life

A *novus homo* from the Sabine town of Amiternum, Sallust entered politics as a **populares** partisan:
tribune of the plebs in **52 BCE** (active against Milo after Clodius's murder), he was **expelled from
the Senate by the censors in 50** — ostensibly for immorality, in reality for his politics. He attached
himself to **Julius Caesar**, served in the civil war, and was rewarded with the governorship of the
new province of **Africa Nova (46)**, where he enriched himself notoriously (the lavish *Horti
Sallustiani*, the Gardens of Sallust, in Rome). After Caesar's murder he withdrew from public life and
**turned to history**, writing in retirement until his death c. 35.

## The Works and the Method

Sallust wrote two **monographs** — *Catiline's Conspiracy* and *The Jugurthine War* — and an
unfinished annalistic *Histories* (surviving only in fragments). His hallmark is the focused
monograph: a single episode chosen because it illuminates the deeper sickness of the state, prefaced by
a moralizing *proem*, studded with set speeches and sharp character-sketches (Catiline, Jugurtha,
Marius, Sulla, Cato and Caesar). His **style** — deliberate brevity, archaism, asymmetry, the pointed
epigram — was modelled on **Thucydides** and in turn shaped Tacitus.

## The Thesis: Decline After 146

Sallust's controlling idea is the **moral and political decline of the Republic**, which he dates to
the **destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE**. So long as Rome faced the ***metus hostilis*** ("fear of
the enemy," specifically *metus Punicus*), civic discipline and concord held; once Carthage fell, "greed
for money… then greed for power" — *the root of all evils* — tore the state between *optimates* and
*populares*, "and the Republic, which had been our common ground, was mutilated." This conjunction of
**luxury and faction** is his explanation for the century of civil war he lived through.

## Significance

Sallust fixed the form (the monograph) and the moralizing, analytical voice of Roman historiography,
transmitted the *optimates/populares* framework, and supplied later ages a powerful narrative of
**republican decline through the loss of external threat** — an idea revived from Montesquieu to
modern political science. His direct heir in manner and pessimism is **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]**.

## OHCW (Lintott Ch. 26)

**Lintott**: C. Sallustius Crispus (born 86 BCE), Caesarian partisan who turned to history around Caesar’s murder; terse epigrammatic style indebted to early annalists (esp. Cato); major *Histories* (to 67 BCE) survive only in fragments; monographs *Catiline* and *Jugurtha* remain. Contrasted Roman military glory with moral corruption attending imperial expansion; aristocracy’s greed and ambition destroyed themselves and corrupted the poor; fundamental causes of decadence were prosperity and lack of foreign enemies — a second-century notion transmitted memorably by Sallust.
