# Virgil (Publius Vergilius Maro)

Rome's greatest poet (70–19 BCE) and the central literary voice of the Augustan age, author of the
**Eclogues**, the **[[virgil-georgics|Georgics]]**, and the national epic the **Aeneid** — the most
influential poet in the whole later Western tradition.

## Life

Born near **Mantua** in Cisalpine Gaul to a modest landowning family, Virgil studied rhetoric and
Epicurean philosophy and entered the literary **circle of Maecenas**, Octavian's cultural minister. His
family land was reportedly caught up in the **confiscations** that resettled veterans after Philippi
(41 BCE) — an experience that shadows the *Eclogues*. He spent the rest of his life on his three great
works, dying at **Brundisium in 19 BCE** on his way back from Greece, having (the tradition says) asked
that the unfinished *Aeneid* be burned; Augustus overrode the request.

## The Works

- **Eclogues** (*Bucolics*, c. 42–39 BCE) — ten pastoral poems after Theocritus, mingling idyll with
  the upheavals of the civil wars and including the "messianic" Fourth Eclogue.
- **[[virgil-georgics|Georgics]]** (c. 36–29 BCE) — four books of didactic poetry on farming, his most
  perfect work, read to Octavian on his return from Actium.
- **Aeneid** (29–19 BCE) — the twelve-book epic of Aeneas's flight from Troy and the founding of the
  Roman race, fusing Homer with Roman destiny and providing the principate its foundational myth.

## Significance

Virgil gave Rome an epic to set beside Homer and gave the West its model of the poet as national and
moral voice. The *Aeneid* became the core school text of the Latin world; in the Middle Ages Virgil was
revered as sage and proto-prophet (Dante's guide through Hell and Purgatory). His shaping of Augustan
ideology — *pietas*, destiny, the cost of empire — and his influence on later epic (including
**[[ovid-metamorphoses|Ovid]]**, who reworked him) are without parallel in classical literature.
