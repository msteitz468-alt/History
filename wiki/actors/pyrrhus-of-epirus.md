## Role

Pyrrhus (319–272 BCE), king of the **Molossians of Epirus** and a kinsman of
[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] (a "descendant of Achilles"), was the brilliant,
restless general whose name gives us the phrase **"Pyrrhic victory."** In CAH VII.1
(Will, ch. 4) he appears chiefly in his Greek and Macedonian role, 301–272; his famous
western campaigns against Rome and Carthage in Italy and Sicily (280–275) are reserved
for **CAH VII.2** as more properly part of the rise of Rome.

## Decisions and Significance

Twice exiled in childhood, Pyrrhus attached himself to the Antigonids (fighting at
[[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]], 301, as a young man) and then, as a hostage in
Alexandria, became a friend and son-in-law of **[[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]]**, who
restored him to Epirus (c. 298) as a deliberate **anti-Macedonian** instrument —
guarding Macedon's western flank, reinforced by a marriage alliance with
[[agathocles-of-syracuse|Agathocles of Syracuse]] (whose daughter Lanassa brought
Corcyra as dowry). Will treats Pyrrhus as, in this phase, an unwitting agent of
Ptolemaic "containment" of Macedon.

He intervened repeatedly in the Macedonian succession struggles: allied with the
[[aetolian-league|Aetolians]] against [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius]], he helped
drive Demetrius from Macedon (288) and briefly held its western half and Thessaly —
before being squeezed out by [[lysimachus]]. His Macedonian ambitions cost him his
allies, and he turned back to Epirus to await his great western temptation: the appeal
of Tarentum against Rome (280).

## Counterfactual Significance

Pyrrhus is the archetype of the **brilliant tactician without strategic patience** — the
"man of the present moment" who, like [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius]], could win
battles but never consolidate a lasting kingdom. His costly victories over Rome
(Heraclea 280, Asculum 279) coined the proverb; his ultimate failure in the West
helped clear the way for Roman mastery of Italy (CAH VII.2). He died in a street-fight
at Argos (272), still chasing the Peloponnese. See [[hellenistic-monarchy]],
[[wars-of-the-diadochi-323]].
