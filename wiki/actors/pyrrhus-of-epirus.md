# Pyrrhus of Epirus

## Role

Pyrrhus (319–272 BCE), king of the **Molossians of Epirus** and a kinsman of
[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] (a "descendant of Achilles"), was the brilliant,
restless general whose name gives us the phrase **"Pyrrhic victory."** In CAH VII.1
(Will, ch. 4) he appears chiefly in his Greek and Macedonian role, 301–272; his western
campaigns against Rome and Carthage in Italy and Sicily (280–275) are covered by CAH
VII.2 (Franke, Ch. 10) as part of the [[pyrrhic-war-280-275bce|Pyrrhic War]].

## Greek and Macedonian Career (319–280 BCE)

Twice exiled in childhood, Pyrrhus attached himself to the Antigonids (fighting at
[[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]], 301, as a young man) and then, as a hostage in
Alexandria, became a friend and son-in-law of **[[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]]**, who
restored him to Epirus (c. 298) as a deliberate **anti-Macedonian** instrument —
guarding Macedon's western flank, reinforced by a marriage alliance with
[[agathocles-of-syracuse|Agathocles of Syracuse]] (whose daughter Lanassa brought
Corcyra as dowry). Will treats Pyrrhus as, in this phase, an unwitting agent of
Ptolemaic "containment" of Macedon.

He intervened repeatedly in the Macedonian succession struggles: allied with the
[[aetolian-league|Aetolians]] against [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius]], he helped
drive Demetrius from Macedon (288) and briefly held its western half and Thessaly —
before being squeezed out by [[lysimachus]]. His Macedonian ambitions cost him his
allies, and he turned back to Epirus to await his great western temptation.

## The Western Campaigns (280–275 BCE)

See [[pyrrhic-war-280-275bce]] for the full narrative. Key decisions:

**Accepting Tarentum's appeal (280 BCE).** The Tarentines needed a military
commander against Rome after their attack on Roman ships triggered a confrontation
they could not face alone. Pyrrhus had just been frustrated in Macedonian politics;
the prospect of a western empire encompassing southern Italy and Sicily — matching the
Hellenistic kingdoms of the east — was too attractive to refuse. Franke emphasizes
this as Pyrrhus's own project, not merely a service to Tarentum.

**Battle of Heraclea (280 BCE).** First victory; Roman army under P. Valerius Laevinus.
Pyrrhus's reaction after inspecting the Roman dead: *"With such soldiers as these
I could conquer the world."* His first assessment of Roman military quality.

**Refusal of peace after Heraclea.** Pyrrhus sent **Cineas** (his most eloquent
associate) to Rome with peace terms — full withdrawal, Roman recognition of Greek
independence in south Italy. **[[appius-claudius-caecus|Appius Claudius Caecus]]**
had himself carried blind to the Senate and delivered the speech that reversed a
gathering majority for peace. Cineas's famous report to Pyrrhus: the Senate was *"an
assembly of kings"* — the Greek world's first recognition of the Roman aristocracy as a
ruling class comparable to Hellenistic monarchs.

**Battle of Ausculum (279 BCE).** Second costly victory. The famous proverb — *"One
more such victory and I am undone"* (Plutarch) — is placed here; its original Greek
form is attested only in Plutarch's later biography, but the sentiment captures the
military reality.

**Sicily (278–275 BCE).** With both Rome and Carthage checked, Pyrrhus crossed to
Sicily. He expelled the Carthaginians from most of the island within two years,
reducing their control to Lilybaeum (which resisted siege). But his autocratic
methods — executing Thoenon, demanding Sicilian troops — alienated the Greeks. His
famous remark on leaving: *"What a wrestling-ground we are now leaving for
Carthaginians and Romans to fight in."* (Plutarch, *Pyrrhus* 23.8.)

**Battle of Beneventum (275 BCE).** Return to Italy; night attack on M'. Curius
Dentatus's camp near Beneventum went wrong in the woods; dawn engagement saw Roman
firebrands rout Pyrrhus's elephants; Curius won decisively. Pyrrhus returned to
Epirus with most of his army, leaving garrisons.

**Death at Argos (272 BCE).** Now chasing Peloponnesian ambitions, Pyrrhus was killed
in street-fighting at Argos — a roof-tile thrown by an old woman (according to
Plutarch) struck his neck or head; he was beheaded while stunned on the ground.

## Counterfactual Significance

Pyrrhus is the archetype of the **brilliant tactician without strategic patience** — the
"man of the present moment" who, like [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius]], could win
battles but never consolidate a lasting kingdom. His costly victories over Rome
(Heraclea 280, Ausculum 279) coined the proverb; his ultimate failure in the West
helped clear the way for Roman mastery of Italy (Franke, CAH VII.2).

More specifically: if Pyrrhus had accepted Rome's peace offers from a position of
his Heraclea victory, or if he had resolved the Sicilian situation before returning
to Italy, he might have established a lasting Epirote-Italic kingdom. His inability
to choose a strategic object and see it through — the brilliant tactician without
patience — is the consistent assessment of the ancient and modern sources alike.
