# The Roman Republic

## Overview

The **Roman Republic** (traditionally 509–27 BCE) grew from a central-Italian
[[city-state|city-state]] into the dominant power of the entire Mediterranean basin.
In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Ch 12) Rome is the supreme case of a polity that
mastered the integration of conquered peoples — extending citizenship, binding Italian
allies, and converting military success into a self-reinforcing imperial machine — even
as that very expansion destroyed the Republican constitution and produced the
**[[roman-empire|Roman Empire]]**.

## The Republican Constitution

After expelling its kings, Rome built a mixed constitution balancing the aristocratic
**Senate**, annually elected magistrates (the two **consuls** at the apex), and popular
**assemblies**. The long internal **Struggle of the Orders** gradually admitted the
plebeians to office and law (the Twelve Tables), producing a broadly stable, militarily
formidable, and expansionist civic order. Roman openness to incorporating outsiders —
unusual among ancient states — was, CWH IV stresses, central to its growth.

## Mediterranean Conquest

Rome unified Italy, then defeated **[[carthage|Carthage]]** in the three **Punic Wars
(264–146 BCE)** — the existential **Second War against Hannibal** (218–201) the turning
point — winning the western Mediterranean and provinces in Spain and Africa. It then
absorbed the **[[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic kingdoms]]** of the east (Macedon,
Greece, Anatolia, Syria), becoming master of the Mediterranean ("**mare nostrum**").

## Crisis and Fall of the Republic

Conquest brought wealth, mass [[slavery]], and provincial command that overwhelmed
civic institutions. Land concentration displaced the peasant-soldier; the
**Gracchi**'s reforms ended in violence; armies grew loyal to generals (Marius, Sulla)
rather than the state. A century of crisis and **civil war** — Caesar against Pompey,
then Octavian against Antony and **Cleopatra** — ended the Republic. The victory of
**Octavian** (Actium, 31 BCE) and his settlement as **Augustus** in 27 BCE founded the
[[roman-empire|Principate]].

## Significance

The Republic created the institutional, legal, and military foundations of Rome's
empire and bequeathed to the Western tradition its core political vocabulary —
*senate, magistrate, republic, citizen, dictator, veto*. Its self-destruction under the
weight of its own conquests is the classic case of an expansionist state outgrowing its
constitution.

## Historiography

Rich but partisan sources (Polybius, Livy, Cicero, Sallust, Caesar) wrote largely to
explain or lament the Republic's fall, shaping a moralizing decline narrative. Modern
debate centers on whether the collapse was structural (the strains of empire,
militarization, the agrarian crisis) or driven by the ambitions of "great men" — a
direct instance of the [[great-man-vs-structural-causation|Great Man vs. structural
causation]] controversy.
