# Bernard of Clairvaux

**Bernard of Clairvaux** (1090–1153) was the most influential Latin churchman of the second quarter of the twelfth century: abbot of Clairvaux for thirty-eight years, architect of explosive [[cistercian-order|Cistercian]] expansion, kingmaker in the **Innocent II / Anacletus II** schism, prosecutor of **[[peter-abelard|Peter Abelard]]**, preacher of the **Second Crusade**, and the age’s foremost Marian and affective spiritual voice. [[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] places him at the centre of both the new monasticism and the church’s “devotional, affective” side in the twelfth century.

## Role and Career

### Cîteaux and Clairvaux

Around 1112, at about age twenty-two, Bernard entered **Cîteaux** (founded 1098) with a train of relatives and friends. In **1115** he founded **Clairvaux**, remaining its abbot for **38 years** and generating **68 daughter houses**. Under his impulse Cistercian growth became Europe-wide: **333 abbeys by 1152** (a temporary halt to further foundations), **525 by the end of the century**. Logan calls him “Europe’s most influential churchman” of his generation.

### Papal politics

In the eight-year schism after **1130**, northern Europe (Louis VI, Lothar, Henry I; Suger; Peter the Venerable; Norbert; **especially Bernard**) backed **Innocent II** against majority-elected **Anacletus II** — moved, Logan notes, not by conviction of Innocent’s canonical validity but by belief that Innocent was **better suited** to be pope. Bernard’s former Clairvaux monk **Eugenius III** (1145–53) later received Bernard’s half-mocking line that people said “it is not you but I who am pope.” During the **1130s and even 1140s** Logan judges Bernard “the most powerful ecclesiastic in western Europe.”

### Abelard and Sens (1141)

Bernard accepted the mysteries of the faith by **faith alone** and treated rational scrutiny of the Trinity as arrogance; Abelard held that reason applied to God was the fullness of faith. The clash was personal and profound. Bernard’s invective charged Abelard with defiling the church, infecting simple minds, examining God with reason before believing, and being “a monk only in habit” who “consorts with women.” At **Sens (June 1141)**, before Louis VII, nobles, bishops, and abbots, Bernard prosecuted **nineteen** allegedly heretical propositions; he had **pre-dined** the bishops into condemning them. Abelard appealed to the pope and left; the council condemned the propositions but **not Abelard’s person** — some see episcopal resistance to Bernard’s de facto power. **Innocent II** (dependent on Bernard) condemned Abelard and, in one account, burned his books in St Peter’s; Abelard later **reconciled** with Bernard, was absolved, and died **21 April 1142**.

### Other interventions

- **Military orders:** Bernard supported the early **Knights Templar** (founded 1119 under Hugh de Paynes).
- **Marian devotion:** he preached Mary as **mediatrix**; every Cistercian house bore her title. He (with later Thomas Aquinas) **opposed** the theology of the **Immaculate Conception** even as popular devotion to Mary surged.
- **Eucharistic miracle lore:** Logan notes a healing story of Bernard curing a bewitched man with a vessel of hosts — typical of twelfth-century host miracle preaching.
- **Hildegard:** at the **Council of Trier (Nov. 1147–Feb. 1148)** Eugenius III read part of unfinished *Scivias* aloud; Bernard urged him to encourage Hildegard, who completed the work within three years.
- **Cluniac–Cistercian polemic:** when Bernard’s cousin left Clairvaux for Cluny, Bernard’s angry letter denounced Cluny’s prior as a wolf and scorned feasts versus fasts, poverty, and labour; **Peter the Venerable** answered, calling Cistercian critics “a new race of pharisees” who neglected Benedictine humility.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Bernard, Cistercian expansion, the resolution of the 1130s schism in Innocent’s favour, the public defeat of Abelard’s theological programme, and the moral authority behind the Second Crusade would all look different. Logan’s larger point is structural as well as personal: Bernard embodies the twelfth-century church’s capacity to combine **institutional power** (orders, popes, crusade) with **affective devotion** (Mary, Eucharist, personal conversion).

## Historiography

Logan’s Further Reading privileges Bredero and McGuire on Bernard, Constable’s *Reformation of the Twelfth Century*, and Lawrence on monasticism. Modern debate still balances Bernard the saint and spiritual classic against Bernard the ruthless ecclesiastical politician and crusade preacher. The Sens trial is often called a “show trial” in Logan’s own wording.
