# Classical Greece (The Polis World)

## Overview

**Classical Greece** is the world of the **[[city-state|polis]]** — the
self-governing Greek city-states that, from c. 800 BCE, produced a civilization
"never unified" politically yet of immense and lasting cultural force
([[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|Benjamin & Wiesner-Hanks, CWH IV Ch 12]]). Its achievements
in drama, philosophy, history, art, architecture, and politics — none Greek
"inventions" but all flowering here — shaped world culture, while its political
disunity led to its "virtual self-destruction" in civil war.

## From Bronze Age to Polis

Greece's classical civilization grew from the wreck of the Bronze Age. The
**Minoans** (Crete, c. 2700–1450 BCE) — wealthy maritime traders, goddess-worshippers,
their Linear A undeciphered — were overtaken by mainland **Mycenaeans** (palace-states
under the king of Mycenae, Linear B, warrior values), who collapsed with the broader
[[bronze-age-collapse]] (c. 1200–1100). The ensuing **Greek "Dark Age"** (c. 1100–800)
of depopulation, lost literacy, and reduced trade gave way, with the adoption of iron
and the [[alphabet|Phoenician alphabet]], to the polis.

## The Polis and Colonization

By 800 BCE the **polis** had become the heart of Greek life — governed variously by
monarchy, aristocracy, oligarchy, "tyrants" (not always oppressive), and (at Athens)
democracy. Rapid population growth and limited land drove **colonization** (c. 750–500
BCE) from the Black Sea to Iberia, building on Phoenician/Minoan trade routes and
spreading the [[city-state|polis]] model and [[coinage]] across the Mediterranean.
The Ionian colonies became the cradle of Greek [[ancient-science-and-technology|natural
philosophy]] and a flashpoint with Persia.

## Sparta and Athens

- **Sparta**: a militarized polis built on the enslavement of the conquered Messenian
  **helots** (who outnumbered Spartiates ~10:1); lifelong military training from age
  seven; austerity; unusual property rights and public roles for women (who ran estates
  while men were at war). Admired and feared as a "totalitarian" machine.
- **Athens**: enriched by trade and the Laurion **silver mines**; reformer **Solon**
  (594 BCE) abolished debt-slavery; by the early 5th century all adult male citizens
  voted in the assembly — "the most democratic form of government thus far seen in
  world history" (excluding women, slaves, and metics). Its 5th-century apogee under
  **Pericles** is the volume's regional case study ([[athens-fifth-century]]).

## The Persian and Peloponnesian Wars

The Ionian revolt drew **[[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid Persian]]** invasions, repulsed
at **Marathon (490)**, **Thermopylae/Salamis/Plataea (480–479 BCE)** — a rare display of
Greek collective action and the formative event of Greek identity (see [[herodotus]]).
Athenian power then alarmed Sparta, producing the ruinous **[[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian
War]] (431–404 BCE)** — disease, civil war, famine, and the exhaustion that opened the
way for **[[macedon|Macedon]]** and [[alexander-the-great|Alexander]].

## Significance

The polis world produced the first European democracy, the foundations of Western
philosophy, science, and historiography, and — through its self-destructive disunity —
a standing lesson in the fragility of fragmented state systems. Its culture was carried
across Afro-Eurasia by the [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]] kingdoms.

## Historiography

Documented as no earlier society — [[herodotus|Herodotus]], [[thucydides|Thucydides]],
Xenophon, the dramatists and philosophers — but the record is Athenocentric and elite.
Debates: the reality and limits of Athenian democracy and its dependence on
[[slavery]]; the causes of the [[peloponnesian-war]]; the degree of Near Eastern
("orientalizing") influence on Greek culture; and the rehabilitation of the
[[achaemenid-empire|Persian]] adversary.
