# The Kassites

A people of the **Zagros mountains** who, after the [[fall-of-babylon-1595bce|
Hittite sack of Babylon]] (~1595 BCE), took the vacant throne and ruled Babylonia
for the longest dynasty in its history — by the king-lists, **576 years**.
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II.1]] (Ch. V §VII, Gadd) reaches only their obscure
**beginnings**; their flourishing "great-power" century is now covered in
[[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II.2]] (Ch. XVIII) — see below.

## Out of the mountains

Like the [[gutian-invasion|Gutians]] before them, the Kassites were "as alien" to
the old land as any highland people. They first appear in a bare date-formula of
**Samsuiluna** (year 9, ~1740 BCE): "Samsuiluna the king [defeated] the Kassite
host" — and again, thirty years later, under his son. "An appropriately slow
beginning" to a movement that, unlike the Gutian raid, would end by **governing**
Babylonia. Their homeland was the Zagros (where later Assyrian kings still harried
them); their language is an isolate; their first kings — **Gandash/Gaddash** and his
successors — "have scarcely any existence outside the later king-lists" and "no
history."

## The seizure of Babylon

The First Dynasty of Babylon decayed slowly across its last four reigns until
**[[fall-of-babylon-1595bce|"the Hittite came to the land of Akkad"]]** under
**Mursili I**, sacking the city and carrying off the statues of **Marduk and
Sarpanitum**. The Kassites filled the vacuum. The decisive evidence is an inscription
of the ninth Kassite king, **Agum II (Agum-kakrime)**, recording that he **brought
Marduk and his consort home** from "the distant land of Khani" — most naturally the
**ransom of the gods from their Hittite captors**, received at a half-way point on
the Euphrates. Whether the first king Gandash should be placed at Samsuiluna's time
or only after the Hittite sack is one of the period's tangled chronological problems;
a small Kassite-named kingdom of **Khana** (the Kashtiliash of Terqa) is the one
early trace of Kassite power, oddly remote from the Zagros.

## Significance

The Kassite takeover marks the **transition from the Old Babylonian to the Late
Bronze world**: the "land" of Sumer and Akkad, exhausted by two centuries of
[[amorites|Amorite]] turbulence, "had lost its force of reaction and recovery —
another [[gutian-invasion|Utu-hegal]] never arose." The Kassites gave Babylonia
unprecedented **stability** and made it one of the **Late Bronze "great powers"**
that corresponded as equals with Egypt, [[hittites|Hatti]], and [[mitanni|Mitanni]]
in the Amarna age. Their arrival is one face of the broad ~1595 BCE reshuffling
(Hittites in Anatolia and Syria, Hurrians/Mitanni in the north, Kassites in
Babylonia, Hyksos then the New Kingdom in Egypt) that opens the
[[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze Age]].

## The Middle Kassite age (CAH II.2)

[[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|Vol. II, Part 2]] (Ch. XVIII, Gadd) reaches the dynasty's
**flourishing century** — the reigns from **Burnaburiaš II** through **Kaštiliaš
IV**, when Kassite Babylonia corresponded with Egypt as a "great king" in the
[[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] (Burnaburiaš demanding gold, escorts and respect,
and protesting Egypt's recognition of the upstart [[ashur-uballit-i|Assyrians]]).
Two threads dominate:
- **The Assyrian intervention.** Burnaburiaš's house intermarried with
  [[ashur-uballit-i|Ashur-uballit I]] of Assyria; when Kassite factions murdered the
  half-Assyrian king **Kadashman-Harbe**, Ashur-uballit marched in, killed the
  usurper, and installed his own great-grandson **Kurigalzu II** — the beginning of
  the long **[[assyria-middle|Assyria]]–Babylonia rivalry** that runs (via
  Enlil-nirari, Adad-nirari I) to [[tukulti-ninurta-i|Tukulti-Ninurta I]]'s eventual sack of Babylon.
- **Middle Kassite society.** This is the great age of the **kudurru
  ("boundary-stones")** — royal land-grants guarded by carved divine symbols and
  curses — and of a distinctive officialdom (land-sale by proclamation and royal
  survey) shared with Assyria and Nuzi. The volume rehabilitates Kassite **literary
  culture**, the era of the textual fixation, serialization and Akkadian translation
  of the Babylonian classics — its scholars "the Alexandrians of Babylonian
  literature."

## Liverani: marginality, standardisation, and crisis theology

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] stresses how **opaque** the Kassite
seizure of Babylon is (the conqueror's name is unknown) and how **marginal** Kassite
Babylonia became once the LBA centre of gravity shifted to the Syria-Levant axis: it
struggled to hold "great kingdom" status and traded formal punctilio about rank for "an
almost uncouth avidity to receive Egyptian gold." Internally he documents a **social
inversion** of the Old Babylonian order — relatively free functionaries now in the cities,
servile dependents in the countryside; servile labour, permanent dependence, and rations
replacing paid labour, contracts, and salaries — amid Lower Mesopotamia's worst demographic
crisis since Ur III (Diyala down ~50%). Administration was reorganised into **20 provinces**
under *šaknu* governors and **family "Houses"**, and land was conveyed by the **kudurru**
boundary-stone (temple-displayed grants with divine-symbol curses). Culturally the Kassites
left little of their own (only Shuqamuna and Shumaliya survived in the pantheon) but their
**scribes were decisive**: they created **Standard Babylonian**, fixed the canonical
editions of the literary corpus (later cited by name in Ashurbanipal's library), and
produced a literature of **pessimism and individualism** (the Babylonian Theodicy, the
Dialogue of Pessimism, *Ludlul bel nemeqi*) that Liverani reads as the ideological symptom
of genuine social crisis — alongside a religious shift from social gods (justice, heroism)
to **personal protective deities** (Marduk the exorcist, Nabu, Gula) and guardian spirits.
The dynasty fell not to the Bronze Age Collapse but to **[[elam|Elam]]** (Shutruk-Nahhunte
and Kutir-Nahhunte). See [[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]] and
[[ancient-near-east-historiography]].

## Historiography

The early Kassites are a near-blank: no contemporary inscriptions, only later
king-lists, chronicles, and the (disputed but probably genuine) Agum II text. Their
beginnings are inseparable from the **[[mesopotamian-chronology|chronology debate]]**,
since the date of the Hittite sack of Babylon anchors the whole second-millennium
sequence. The "Kassite dark age" over both Babylonia and [[elam|Elam]] after ~1595
reflects a real thinning of sources as much as a real decline. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
