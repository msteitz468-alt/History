# Hantili I

The **brother-in-law and assassin** of [[mursili-i|Mursili I]], husband of Mursili's
sister Harapsili, who seized the [[hittites|Hittite]] throne c. ~1590 BCE and opened
the half-century of regicide and dynastic violence that the Proclamation of
[[telipinu|Telipinu]] later narrated as a morality tale of divine vengeance. He is the
hinge-figure of the Old Kingdom's catastrophic middle period: a king who could justify
his seizure of power only through successful military campaigns, yet who watched his
family destroyed by the same logic of violence he had introduced. Treated in Trevor
Bryce, *The Kingdom of the Hittites* (2005), Ch. 5.

## The Assassination of Mursili I

Hantili acted with the support of his son-in-law Zidanta. The Proclamation of Telipinu
records tersely: "Zidanta conspired with Hantili, and they made a wicked plot. They
murdered Mursili. They shed blood" (Telipinu Procl. §11, i 32–4). The murder probably
occurred "within a few years" of Mursili's triumphant return from the sack of
[[fall-of-babylon-1595bce|Babylon]] — possibly before he had fully reestablished his
authority at home after such a prolonged absence. Bryce suggests Hantili's assassination
illustrates "the ultimate failure of Hattusili's attempt to ensure the security of his
successor by the provisions he had made in his Testament."

## Reign: Campaigns and Catastrophes

Despite the criminal nature of his accession, Hantili maintained Hittite ambitions in
Syria, conducting military operations that reached **Carchemish** on the Euphrates — very
likely directed against the [[hurrians|Hurrians]], who were expanding rapidly to fill the
vacuum left by Mursili's destruction of [[iamkhad|Aleppo]] and [[babylon-first-dynasty|
Babylon]].

On his return journey, at the city of Tegarama (probably modern Gürun), "the gods sought
(revenge for) the bloodshed of Mursili" (Telipinu Procl. §13, i 42). From that point the
Proclamation presents his reign as an unbroken sequence of disasters — the
theological idiom for Telipinu's political argument that murder contaminates the kingdom.
The [[hurrians|Hurrians]] invaded the Land of Hatti at will but were driven back before
taking [[hattusha|Hattusa]] itself.

## The Fate of His Family

The most dramatic episode of Hantili's reign was the fate of his wife and sons. Queen
Harapsili (Mursili's own sister, now Hantili's wife) and two sons were taken to the city
of Sugziya in the Euphrates region — either deposited there by Hantili during his
Hurrian campaigns for their safety, or captured by the retreating Hurrians. In either
case, Harapsili fell ill in Sugziya and died there. Her sons apparently shared her fate.
Foul play was suspected; those held responsible were eventually arrested and "brought to
justice."

## The Succession's Perversion

Hantili had expected his son **Piseni** to succeed him, and Piseni already had
grandchildren. But Hantili "lived long enough to see his son and his grandsons murdered
by the man who had helped him seize the throne from Mursili many years before — his
son-in-law Zidanta." The instrument of divine retribution for Mursili's blood turned out
to be Hantili's own partner in the original crime.

Hantili's career thus encapsulates the self-defeating logic of succession-by-assassination:
the act that brought him to power destroyed every precedent that might have protected
his own dynastic claim.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Hantili not murdered Mursili I, the Old Kingdom might have stabilised the Hittite
state rather than entering its fifty-year regicide cycle. The vacuum left by the
assassinations — with Hurrian aggression intensifying precisely in these decades —
directly enabled the rise of [[mitanni|Mitanni]] to fill the Syrian power vacuum. It is
at least plausible that a surviving Mursili, or a legitimate succession from him, would
have left Hatti better positioned against Hurrian expansion. Hantili's reign is thus a
critical node in the causal chain leading to the [[telipinu-edict-1500bce|Edict of
Telipinu]].

## Historiography

Hantili I is known almost entirely from the historical preamble of Telipinu's
Proclamation, written at least a generation after his reign. Telipinu's framing is
explicitly theological and didactic — the narration of past bloodshed as a series of
sins followed by divine punishment — making it an unreliable chronological source but
a highly significant political document. The earliest reference to Mursili's conquests
of Aleppo and Babylon that survives is in "a prayer of the conqueror's later namesake
Mursili II," composed well after Hantili's own reign. The king who opens the era of
regicide is thus memorialized almost exclusively by the reformer who sought to end it.
