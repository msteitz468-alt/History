# Old Kingdom Egypt (Dynasties III–VIII)

The **"Pyramid Age"** — the first great flowering of the centralized Egyptian
state (c. 2686–2160 BCE), from Djoser's Step Pyramid to the disintegration that
opens the First Intermediate Period. It is the period of the Giza pyramids, the
fullest expression of pharaonic [[divine-kingship|divine kingship]], and a
bureaucratic state of remarkable reach — followed by one of history's classic
state collapses. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch.
XIV, W. Stevenson Smith). Continues from [[early-dynastic-egypt|Dynasties I–II]].

## Third Dynasty — the stone revolution

Early in Dynasty III, **[[djoser|Djoser (Netjerykhet)]]** and his architect
**Imhotep** built the **Step Pyramid at Saqqara** — the first monumental building
in cut stone, evolved from the flat mastaba by piling stepped layers in inward-
leaning courses. Smith stresses this was not the invention of stone architecture
(known from Dynasty I) but a leap in *scale and technique*, and a moment of
"barbaric" experimental freshness (stone imitating wood, reed, and matting).
Successors (Sekhemkhet, Khaba, Huni) began but rarely finished step pyramids; the
dynasty's chronology and king-list equations (Sanakhte/Nebka) remain tangled.

## Fourth Dynasty — the great pyramids

- **Sneferu** perfected the form across **three pyramids** (Maidum; the **Bent
  Pyramid** at Dahshur, whose angle was lowered mid-build; and the **North/Red
  Pyramid**, the first true pyramid). Remembered in later literature as a genial,
  accessible king.
- **Khufu (Cheops)** built the **Great Pyramid of Giza** — the supreme monument
  of the age, surrounded by a planned "city of the dead" of family cemeteries. The
  intact tomb-cache of his mother **Hetepheres** survived. Later tradition
  (Herodotus, the Westcar Papyrus) cast Khufu as a detested autocrat against
  Sneferu's geniality.
- **Khafre (Chephren)** — a slightly smaller Giza pyramid, the granite valley
  temple, and the **Great Sphinx**. **Menkaure (Mycerinus)** — the third, much
  smaller Giza pyramid. **Shepseskaf** abandoned the pyramid for the
  sarcophagus-shaped **Mastabat Fara'un**.

The Fourth Dynasty perfected **centralization**: the king ruled through a
**vizier** and high offices held by **members of his own immediate family**; able
princes who might aspire to the throne were bought off with the vizierate.

## Fifth Dynasty — the solar state

Power devolved subtly. The **cult of Re of Heliopolis** became central: kings
took the title **"Son of Re,"** built distinctive **sun-temples** with masonry
obelisks (Userkaf, Nyuserre), and the Westcar Papyrus mythologizes the first
three kings as the sun-god's own sons. Pyramids shrank; private tomb reliefs grew
ever richer in **scenes of daily life**. Critically, **Fifth-Dynasty viziers were
no longer princes**, and a new **"Governor of Upper Egypt"** office signals
growing concern to control the south — early signs of weakening central grip. The
**Abusir Papyri** (temple accounts of Neferirkare) survive from this dynasty. The
last king, **Unas**, was the first to inscribe the **Pyramid Texts** — spells to
aid the king in the afterlife — in his tomb.

## Sixth Dynasty — the long decline

**Teti, Pepi I (Phiops I), Merenre, Pepi II (Phiops II).** Pepi I made a
**political marriage** into the provincial nobility of the Thinite nome (the two
queens both named Ankhesenpepi/Meryreankhnes). Officials' autobiographies now
illuminate the state: **Weni (Uni)** led an army (with Nubian mercenaries)
against the Asiatic "sand-dwellers" and into southern Palestine; **Harkhuf**,
governor of Elephantine, ran trading caravans deep into Nubia (the famous royal
letter about a **dancing dwarf**). **Pepi II** reputedly reigned **~94 years** —
the longest in recorded history — but the long reign "spelled the end of the Old
Kingdom": the **provincial nobility (nomarchs)** turned hereditary, crown lands
fragmented through inheritance, and tax-exempt funerary endowments **exhausted the
crown's resources**.

## Collapse

After Pepi II the monarchy fractured: the ephemeral **Seventh and Eighth
Dynasties** at Memphis left almost no record (Manetho's "seventy kings in seventy
days"), propped up by powerful provincial families (Shemay at Koptos), until
**Heracleopolis revolted** and ended the Old Kingdom — the
[[fall-of-old-kingdom-2160bce|onset of the First Intermediate Period]]. The
collapse is mirrored in a **pessimistic literature** of lament foreign to the
confident earlier age.

## Government and society

A meritocratic, **non-caste** administration: "innate ability and the favour of
the king were the determining factors" (Smith) — practical men of public works
(Imhotep, the Senedjemib family of master-builders) rose high. No standing army or
navy: men were levied and ships commandeered for raids that were "more often
connected with mining or trade" (Sinai turquoise, Nubian diorite, Byblos cedar,
Punt incense) than war. Wealth was agricultural and irrigation-dependent — famine
reliefs (Unas) and the legendary "seven years' famine of Djoser" show the
vulnerability.

## Kemp: the provider state and the bureaucratic mind

Barry Kemp ([[kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy of a Civilization*]], Chs. 4–5)
reads the Old Kingdom as the first full working of the **[[provider-state|redistributive
provider state]]** run by the **[[egyptian-bureaucracy|bureaucratic mind]]**. Surplus was
taxed in, accounted in units of bread, beer and grain, and redistributed as rations; the
**phyle** work-sharing system (each ~20-man subdivision serving one month in ten) spread
entitlements across a large population. The earliest archives belong here: the **Wadi
el-Jarf papyri** (year 27 of Khufu, the inspector Merer's logbook of hauling Tura limestone
to the Great Pyramid) and the **Abusir papyri** of Neferirkara's cult. Labour was mobilized
through occupation lists (the Fourth-Dynasty **Gebelein papyri**, ~300 *neferu*) and crews
with nautical names ("Friends of Khufu") daubed on Giza blocks.

Pyramid **settlement** confirms the model: the Giza workers' town (**Heit el-Ghurab**) shows
both self-organized housing and a planned barracks zone of galleries that could sleep
40–50 men each (~2,000 in all, only part of the workforce), with a meat-rich diet. The
Menkaura valley temple gradually filled with granaries and houses, an officially sanctioned
"pyramid town." Foreign recruitment ran deep: the official **Weni** (reign of Pepi I)
levied soldiers from five Nubian homelands and Libya (see
[[egyptian-ethnicity-and-identity]]).

## Shaw (2000): the narrative spine and the seeds of collapse

Jaromir Malek's chapter in the [[sources/shaw-oxford-history-ancient-egypt-2000|Oxford
History]] supplies the connected dynasty-by-dynasty narrative for the individual reigns
now treated on their own pages — **[[actors/sneferu|Sneferu]]** (the greatest builder of
the age, perfecting the true pyramid at Dahshur), **[[actors/khufu|Khufu]]** (the Great
Pyramid at **[[places/giza|Giza]]**, ~2.3 million blocks), and **[[actors/pepy-ii|Pepy
II]]** (the ~94-year reign that shaded into collapse). Malek frames the **"Old Kingdom"**
itself as a modern label ("the ancient Egyptians never used it") and reads pyramid-building
as the **economic and social catalyst** of the age — diverting labour, stimulating
administration, and (through permanent tax-exempt endowments) sowing the
[[events/fall-of-old-kingdom-2160bce|fiscal seeds of the eventual collapse]]. The earliest
royal religious corpus, the **[[concepts/pyramid-texts|Pyramid Texts]]**, first appears in
the pyramid of Unas at the close of the 5th Dynasty.

## Historiography

Smith warns the Old Kingdom Egyptians were "extremely laconic" in recording
events; with daily papyrus records lost, history is reconstructed from **tomb
genealogies, titles, and royal monuments**, supplemented by later (often
unreliable) sources — the Westcar Papyrus, Manetho, Herodotus, the Palermo Stone
annals. Chronology rests on the **biennial cattle-count** (dates must usually be
doubled) and the Turin Canon. The structural reading of the collapse (decentral-
ization + fiscal exhaustion) is Smith's; the competing **climatic** explanation
(low Niles / drought) is treated under
[[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism]]. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].

## Egypt in its African context (GHA II)

The **UNESCO [[gha-vol2-ancient-civilizations|General History of Africa, Vol. II]]** treats Pharaonic Egypt
deliberately **as an African civilization** — rooted in the Saharan and Nile-valley Neolithic and in
continuous exchange with Nubia and the lands to the south — against the older European habit of detaching it
from the continent. The volume opens with **Cheikh Anta Diop's** argument that the ancient Egyptians were a
**Black African people**, the signature debate now recorded on **[[peopling-of-ancient-egypt]]**. Whatever
one makes of Diop's strongest claims, the broader reframing of Egypt within its African world is now
mainstream.
