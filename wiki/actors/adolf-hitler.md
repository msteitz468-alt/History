## Overview

Adolf Hitler (1889–1945) was the Austrian-born politician and leader (Führer) of the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) who became Chancellor of Germany on 30 January 1933 and subsequently consolidated total dictatorial power as head of [[nazi-germany]]. He bears primary personal responsibility for the initiation of [[second-world-war-1939]] and for [[the-holocaust]]. His political career was inseparable from the specific crisis conditions of the [[weimar-republic]] and the economic and political legacies of [[total-war-germany-1914-1918]].

Source: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], chs. 18 and 19.

## Early Career and WWI Context

A famous photograph of the Odeonsplatz in Munich on 2 August 1914 shows Hitler among an exuberant crowd celebrating Germany's declaration of war against Russia — "the crowd is almost exclusively made up of men, and most of them are clearly marked as middle-class by their straw hats, white collars, and suits" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 17). Hitler served in WWI as a messenger (Meldegänger), receiving the Iron Cross. His political radicalization occurred in the context of the defeated, humiliated Germany of 1918–1919.

Hitler participated in Erich Ludendorff's failed Beer Hall Putsch in Munich (November 1923), an attempt to seize power modeled on Mussolini's March on Rome. The putsch's failure and Hitler's subsequent imprisonment (during which he dictated *Mein Kampf*) redirected the Nazi movement toward electoral politics.

## Rise to Power

The ascent of the Nazi movement began "in the second half of the 1920s and accelerating at a breathtaking pace after 1930." Tooze (ch. 18) and Mergel (ch. 19) of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]] converge on two crucial points:

1. **The Nazi surge preceded the Great Depression**: It began before the economic crisis reached full force, though it benefited enormously from it.
2. **The NSDAP was not a class movement**: It was "a people's party of protest, which could attract members of all social groups, including the workers." Contemporaries appreciated its political radicalism as "an indication of its determination to replace the failing political classes and to reunite the people" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 19).

The Depression destroyed the international economic framework underpinning Weimar's stabilization (Dawes Plan, Wall Street investment, multilateral trade). Six million unemployed by winter 1931–1932 drove mass support to extremes. Hitler's appointment as Chancellor on 30 January 1933 resulted from a miscalculation by the conservative establishment (Papen, Hindenburg) that they could control him.

## Economic Governance

As Chancellor and Führer, Hitler presided over the most dramatic peacetime military-economic mobilization in capitalist history. Within six years of taking power he boasted of spending 65 billion Reichsmarks on rearmament. Key features of his economic governance (per Tooze, ch. 18):

- He embraced military spending as the priority from day one, contrary to the myth of a civilian recovery phase (1933–1936) followed by militarization.
- He encouraged factional competition between Nazi agencies rather than building a unified command structure — a deliberate anti-bureaucratic ideology in which "teams of technical experts, engineers, and officers, guided by the Fuehrer's vision, would exercise direct command over the resources of the Volk."
- He was driven by an apocalyptic conviction from autumn 1938 onward that "the forces of international Jewry" in the City of London and Wall Street were orchestrating a coalition against Germany — an antisemitic conspiracy theory that colored his interpretation of military-economic data and drove the decision for war in 1939.
- His regime achieved genuine full employment and upward mobility within the blue-collar hierarchy, generating substantial popular support that should not be reduced to terror and manipulation.

## Strategic Decisions and the War

Hitler's major strategic gambles — each analogous to the January 1917 submarine warfare decision of the Kaiser's regime — were shaped by the same logic of resource asymmetry: Germany could not win a long war against global empires; therefore it had to win quickly or not at all.

- **1939**: The decision for war, despite Schacht's warnings of imminent foreign exchange crisis and Wehrmacht analysis showing Germany would lose whatever armaments lead it had gained. Hitler read the data through his antisemitic-conspiratorial framework as confirming the necessity of "headlong aggression."
- **1940**: The victory over France — achieved with a materially inferior German army — opened "an unprecedented space for the imagination of power." The subsequent decision to simultaneously prepare the invasion of the Soviet Union AND a long-range war against Britain and America was strategically irrational by any objective resource calculation.
- **1941**: The moment of "grand synthesis" — the only year in which Nazi agencies formulated something approaching a coherent multi-front war plan. It was simultaneously the moment of the Hunger Plan, Heydrich's orders for the "Europe-wide solution to the Jewish Question," and the Generalplan Ost. Detail on [[the-holocaust]] is at that page.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Hitler's specific antisemitic obsession and willingness to accept strategic risks that his own military-economic experts considered suicidal, the German state's trajectory through the 1930s would most likely have been authoritarian nationalist but not genocidal, and might not have launched a general European war in 1939. The business community, the Reichswehr, and even most senior Nazi officials were more cautious about the scale and timing of military confrontation. The Holocaust was not an inevitable product of German society's antisemitism — it required Hitler's specific ideological commitment to make systematic extermination a state priority even when it contradicted military-economic logic (e.g., diverting rail transport for deportations that could have carried ammunition). On this, see [[the-holocaust]].

## Role in the Nazi State

The Nazi state was neither a conventional totalitarian command economy nor a normal capitalist state. It was characterized by deliberate "hollowing out" of legal and administrative formality in favor of direct command by technical-political networks guided by Hitler's "vision." Franz Neumann's 1942 study titled this structure *Behemoth* — the "unstate." Hitler consciously encouraged factional competition and institutional duplication as a governance technique, ensuring all roads led back to him for final arbitration. This produced administrative chaos alongside ideological radicalization: the most extreme proposals tended to be the ones adopted.

## Historiography

**Source**: ch. 18 (Tooze) and ch. 19 (Mergel) of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]].

**Key debates**:
- **Intentionalism vs. functionalism** in Holocaust causation: Whether Hitler intended the genocide from early in his career (intentionalist position) or whether it emerged from the cumulative radicalization of competing agencies (functionalist/structuralist). Current scholarship favors "moderate intentionalism" — Hitler's personal commitment to racial antisemitism was necessary but not sufficient; the specific form of the Holocaust emerged from the military-economic crisis of 1941.
- **Hitler's role vs. structural factors**: Mergel (ch. 19) stresses that the Nazi movement was not simply Hitler's personal creation — it was "a people's party of protest" that responded to genuine structural failures of the Weimar Republic. But the specific ideological and strategic choices after 1933, especially after 1938, were shaped decisively by Hitler's personal convictions.
- **Popular support**: The source rejects the "Hitler's willing executioners" model of German society as uniformly antisemitic while also rejecting the "ordinary Germans were victims of the regime" exculpatory narrative. Genuine popular support for rearmament and full employment coexisted with ignorance of, indifference to, or active participation in persecution of Jews.

**Collection coverage**: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]] provides strong analytical framing. Tooze's *Wages of Destruction* (2006) is the definitive economic study. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection is thin on post-1933 German history in general.
