## Overview

Adolf Hitler (1889–1945) was the Austrian-born politician and leader (Führer) of the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) who became Chancellor of Germany on 30 January 1933 and subsequently consolidated total dictatorial power as head of [[nazi-germany]]. He bears primary personal responsibility for the initiation of [[second-world-war-1939]] and for [[the-holocaust]]. His political career was inseparable from the specific crisis conditions of the [[weimar-republic]] and the economic and political legacies of [[total-war-germany-1914-1918]].

Sources: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]] (structural overview); primary spine now [[sources/kershaw-hitler-hubris-1998|Kershaw, Hitler: Hubris (1998)]] for rise to 1936 (esp. formation and 1919 discovery of talent). See also detailed biography.

## Formation and Early Life (Kershaw, Hubris Range 01)

Adolf Hitler was born 20 April 1889 at 6:30 p.m. in the Gasthof zum Pommer, Braunau am Inn (Vorstadt Nr. 219), on the Austro-German border. "In the very first sentences of *Mein Kampf*, Adolf was to emphasize ... how providential it was that he had been born in Braunau am Inn, on the border of the two countries he saw it as his life’s task to unite." Father Alois (illegitimate Schicklgruber until name change 1876; authoritarian customs official, bee-keeper, social climber) was distant and domineering. Mother Klara (née Pölzl, second cousin to Alois) provided compensating affection. Frequent moves (Braunau to Passau 1892, then Linz area). Father died 1903; mother of breast cancer 21 December 1907 — Hitler (age 18) was "devastated".

School failures at Realschule in Linz left him without qualifications. Artistic ambitions clashed with father's civil-service expectations. Vienna years (1907–1913) were decisive for worldview. Rejected twice by the Academy of Fine Arts ("I was so convinced that I would be successful that when I received my rejection, it struck me as a bolt from the blue"). Lived in Men's Home Meldemannstraße (from 9 Feb 1910), painted postcards/watercolours "only to earn a bare living and so that he 'could study'" (~12 RM max per picture); voracious reading, political arguments. "The Vienna years were over. They had indelibly marked Hitler’s outlook." (Kershaw Hubris Range 06: Hanisch, Honisch, Schroeder.)

Here his antisemitism crystallized from pan-German and völkisch influences (Schönerer, Lueger's pragmatic antisemitism, Lanz von Liebenfels). "Wherever I went, I now saw Jews, and the more I saw, the more sharply they set themselves apart in my eyes from the rest of humanity." Shifted from social resentment to biological-racial worldview; Jews linked in his mind to both Marxism and finance capital as existential racial threat. Social Darwinist "struggle" framework gelled. In May 1913 he left for Munich "to where his 'childhood secret love' for Germany drew him" and to evade Austrian conscription.

## Early Career and WWI Context

A famous photograph of the Odeonsplatz in Munich on 2 August 1914 shows Hitler among an exuberant crowd celebrating Germany's declaration of war against Russia — "the crowd is almost exclusively made up of men, and most of them are clearly marked as middle-class by their straw hats, white collars, and suits" (cross-ref Kershaw for personal reaction; [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 17 for crowd composition). "Overpowered by stormy enthusiasm, I fell down on my knees and thanked Heaven from an overflowing heart for granting me the good fortune of being permitted to live at this time." Enlisted in Bavarian List Regiment (16th Bavarian Reserve Infantry); served as Meldegänger (dispatch runner). Awarded Iron Cross Second Class (1914) and rare Iron Cross First Class (4 August 1918, recommended in part by Jewish Lt. Hugo Gutmann).

Wounded on the Somme (1916); gassed near Ypres sector (14 Oct 1918); hospitalized in Pasewalk. There he learned of the armistice and revolution. "I knew that all was lost... What was the pain in my eyes compared to this misery?" The "stab in the back" by "November criminals" became personal mission. He resolved: "I had become a politician."

Returned to Munich 21 Nov 1918. The city was under soldiers' councils; Kurt Eisner (Jewish USPD) led the revolutionary Bavarian government. After Eisner's assassination, Munich briefly became a Soviet-style Räterepublik (April 1919) before bloody Freikorps/Reichswehr suppression. Hitler witnessed the chaos at close quarters but gave a laconic, likely obfuscating account in *Mein Kampf*. His first "more or less purely political activity" came post-"liberation" on a regimental investigation commission.

Retained by the Reichswehr as an "education officer" and informant, he was sent to Lechfeld camp (Aug 1919) to lecture returning POWs against Bolshevism. Talent was immediately recognized: "Herr Hitler especially is, I might say, a born popular speaker who, through his fanaticism and his populist style in a meeting, absolutely compels his audience to take note and share his views." One listener: "Goodness, he’s got a gob. We could use him." Hitler himself: "I was offered an opportunity of speaking before a larger audience; and the thing that I had always presumed from pure feeling without knowing it was now corroborated; I could 'speak'."

In September 1919, attending a meeting of the tiny German Workers' Party (DAP) in the Sterneckerbräu as an army observer, he spoke and was recruited by founder Anton Drexler. This marked the discovery of his political vocation and entry into the movement that would become the NSDAP. See [[hitler-dap-entry-1919]], [[actors/drexler-anton]].

Hitler participated in Erich Ludendorff's failed Beer Hall Putsch in Munich (November 1923), an attempt to seize power modeled on Mussolini's March on Rome. The putsch's failure and Hitler's subsequent imprisonment (during which he dictated *Mein Kampf*) redirected the Nazi movement toward electoral politics.

**Kershaw (Hubris, Range 02) on 1921–1925 mechanics**: In the early 1920s Hitler presented himself as 'nothing but a drummer and rallier (Trommler und Sammler).' He built the SA via Röhm's paramilitary networks and weapons caches. The 1923 putsch was forced by his own 'all-or-nothing' temperament and the need to prevent supporter enthusiasm ebbing: he chose the Bürgerbräukeller meeting of 8 November to confront the wavering triumvirate (Kahr, Lossow, Seißer). The march the next day ended in gunfire at the Odeonsplatz (14 putschists dead). At his lenient trial he turned the courtroom into a propaganda platform. In Landsberg the adulation and writing of *Mein Kampf* completed his transformation: he was no longer the drummer but the predestined Leader. The 13-month 'leaderless time' proved his indispensability; on release (20 Dec 1924) he refounded the NSDAP on 27 Feb 1925 as a pure Führer party, crushing the Strasser challenge and converting Goebbels. The 'Führer principle' was now institutionalised. See [[beer-hall-putsch-1923]], [[nsdap-refoundation-1925]], [[actors/gregor-strasser]], [[hubs/biographies/world-wars-era/adolf-hitler]].

## Rise to Power

The ascent of the Nazi movement began "in the second half of the 1920s and accelerating at a breathtaking pace after 1930." Tooze (ch. 18) and Mergel (ch. 19) of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]] converge on two crucial points:

1. **The Nazi surge preceded the Great Depression**: It began before the economic crisis reached full force, though it benefited enormously from it.
2. **The NSDAP was not a class movement**: It was "a people's party of protest, which could attract members of all social groups, including the workers." Contemporaries appreciated its political radicalism as "an indication of its determination to replace the failing political classes and to reunite the people" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 19).

The Depression destroyed the international economic framework underpinning Weimar's stabilization (Dawes Plan, Wall Street investment, multilateral trade). Six million unemployed by winter 1931–1932 drove mass support to extremes. Hitler's appointment as Chancellor on 30 January 1933 resulted from a miscalculation by the conservative establishment (Papen, Hindenburg) that they could control him.

**Consolidation to total power (Kershaw Hubris, Range 05):** Post-1933: Röhm purge (30 June–2 July 1934) eliminated SA threat and other rivals (Schleicher, Strasser, etc.); SS elevated directly under Hitler. Hindenburg death (2 Aug) + pre-emptive law + army personal oath to Führer (Blomberg/Reichenau initiative) + 89.9% plebiscite secured institutional absolutism. Public acclaimed the murders as 'restoration of order.' By 1936: 'working towards the Führer' (Willikens 21 Feb 1934: 'duty of every single person to attempt, in the spirit of the Führer, to work towards him') drove cumulative radicalization without constant orders. SS-police empire (unified June 1936), party interventions, economic agencies competed to anticipate Hitler's broad aims. Foreign coups (Poland pact 1934; Saar 91% return Jan 1935; conscription 36 divisions 16 Mar 1935; Rhineland 7 Mar 1936 — 'greatest triumph to date') were Hitler's timing/bluff atop shared revisionism; massively boosted domestic myth and his self-belief ('sleepwalker' quote 14 Mar 1936). Economic mobilization prioritized rearmament from outset despite food/raw-material crises; Four Year Plan (1936) as new direct agency. 'The Führer state was established.'

**Kershaw (Hubris, Range 04) — 1933–1934 Consolidation (Machtergreifung to total power):** On 30 January 1933 the day was marked by torchlight procession: Hindenburg watched respectfully from his window; Hitler received wild acclaim. 'Power had indeed not been “seized”. It had been handed to Hitler...' The 'Day of Potsdam' (21 March) staged Prussian tradition with Hindenburg in field-marshal uniform; two days later in the Kroll Opera House (surrounded by armed SA/SS/Stahlhelm) Hitler secured the Enabling Act. Promises of untouched Reichstag, President, Länder and Churches were made; Zentrum voted yes after verbal assurances under duress. 'With 441 votes to the ninety-four votes of the Social Democrats, the Reichstag, as a democratic body, voted itself out of existence.' The Enabling Act went into effect the next day. Hitler told cabinet he had obtained 'that which we could have taken anyway.'

During spring/summer 1933 *Gleichschaltung* ('coordination') swept institutions. 'What is striking is not how much, but how little, Hitler needed to do.' Reich Governors law (7 April) undermined Länder sovereignty. 'By the autumn, the Nazi dictatorship – and Hitler’s own power at its head – had been enormously strengthened.' Civil liberties extinguished within a month; Reichstag powers surrendered within two; trade unions dissolved within four; all opposition parties gone within six months; Länder sovereignty formally abolished January 1934.

By end 1933 two obstacles remained: the SA (outlived purpose, 'politics of hooliganism') and Röhm's ambitions vs Reichswehr. Röhm pushed 'second revolution'; rank-and-file resentments ran deep. Hitler wavered publicly. On 28 February 1934 he declared 'the Wehrmacht must be the sole bearer of weapons of the nation.' Röhm (overheard, reported by Lutze): 'What the ridiculous corporal declared doesn’t apply to us. Hitler has no loyalty...'

Papen Marburg speech (17 June) criticised chaos; Hitler threatened 'the fist of the nation'. On 21 June at Neudeck, Blomberg warned martial law if tension not relieved; Hindenburg ordered Hitler 'to bring the revolutionary trouble-makers finally to reason.' Action forced before SA leave ended 1 August. On 30 June Hitler flew to Munich in rage ('the blackest day of my life'). At Ministry of Interior ripped badges from Schneidhuber and Schmid: 'You are under arrest and will be shot.' At Bad Wiessee (6.30 a.m.) pistol in hand arrested Röhm ('denounced him as a traitor'); Heines found in bed with a young man. At Brown House midday in frenzy ('spittle dribbling from Hitler’s mouth') he declared 'the worst treachery in world history' and 'He would have them all shot.'

On 13 July to Reichstag: 'I gave the order to shoot those most guilty of this treason, and I further gave the order to burn out down to the raw flesh the ulcers of our internal well-poisoning…' Army reaction (Reichenau guidelines 28 Aug): 'The Reich Chancellor kept his word when he nipped in the bud Röhm’s attempt to incorporate the S A in the Reichswehr. We love him because he has shown himself a true soldier.'

The rapidity 'between Hitler’s takeover of power on 30 January 1933 and its crucial consolidation and extension at the beginning of August 1934, after Reich President Hindenburg’s death and following close on the major crisis of the “Röhm affair”, was astounding.' See [[nazi-seizure-of-power-1933]], [[night-of-the-long-knives-1934]], [[hubs/biographies/world-wars-era/adolf-hitler]].

## Economic Governance

As Chancellor and Führer, Hitler presided over the most dramatic peacetime military-economic mobilization in capitalist history. Within six years of taking power he boasted of spending 65 billion Reichsmarks on rearmament. Key features of his economic governance (per Tooze, ch. 18):

- He embraced military spending as the priority from day one, contrary to the myth of a civilian recovery phase (1933–1936) followed by militarization.
- He encouraged factional competition between Nazi agencies rather than building a unified command structure — a deliberate anti-bureaucratic ideology in which "teams of technical experts, engineers, and officers, guided by the Fuehrer's vision, would exercise direct command over the resources of the Volk."
- He was driven by an apocalyptic conviction from autumn 1938 onward that "the forces of international Jewry" in the City of London and Wall Street were orchestrating a coalition against Germany — an antisemitic conspiracy theory that colored his interpretation of military-economic data and drove the decision for war in 1939.
- His regime achieved genuine full employment and upward mobility within the blue-collar hierarchy, generating substantial popular support that should not be reduced to terror and manipulation.

## Strategic Decisions and the War

Hitler's major strategic gambles — each analogous to the January 1917 submarine warfare decision of the Kaiser's regime — were shaped by the same logic of resource asymmetry: Germany could not win a long war against global empires; therefore it had to win quickly or not at all.

- **1939**: The decision for war, despite Schacht's warnings of imminent foreign exchange crisis and Wehrmacht analysis showing Germany would lose whatever armaments lead it had gained. Hitler read the data through his antisemitic-conspiratorial framework as confirming the necessity of "headlong aggression."
- **1940**: The victory over France — achieved with a materially inferior German army — opened "an unprecedented space for the imagination of power." The subsequent decision to simultaneously prepare the invasion of the Soviet Union AND a long-range war against Britain and America was strategically irrational by any objective resource calculation.
- **1941**: The moment of "grand synthesis" — the only year in which Nazi agencies formulated something approaching a coherent multi-front war plan. It was simultaneously the moment of the Hunger Plan, Heydrich's orders for the "Europe-wide solution to the Jewish Question," and the Generalplan Ost. Detail on [[the-holocaust]] is at that page.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Hitler's specific antisemitic obsession and willingness to accept strategic risks that his own military-economic experts considered suicidal, the German state's trajectory through the 1930s would most likely have been authoritarian nationalist but not genocidal, and might not have launched a general European war in 1939. The business community, the Reichswehr, and even most senior Nazi officials were more cautious about the scale and timing of military confrontation. The Holocaust was not an inevitable product of German society's antisemitism — it required Hitler's specific ideological commitment to make systematic extermination a state priority even when it contradicted military-economic logic (e.g., diverting rail transport for deportations that could have carried ammunition). On this, see [[the-holocaust]].

## Role in the Nazi State

The Nazi state was neither a conventional totalitarian command economy nor a normal capitalist state. It was characterized by deliberate "hollowing out" of legal and administrative formality in favor of direct command by technical-political networks guided by Hitler's "vision." Franz Neumann's 1942 study titled this structure *Behemoth* — the "unstate." Hitler consciously encouraged factional competition and institutional duplication as a governance technique, ensuring all roads led back to him for final arbitration. This produced administrative chaos alongside ideological radicalization: the most extreme proposals tended to be the ones adopted.

## Historiography

**Source**: ch. 18 (Tooze) and ch. 19 (Mergel) of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]].

**Key debates**:
- **Intentionalism vs. functionalism** in Holocaust causation: Whether Hitler intended the genocide from early in his career (intentionalist position) or whether it emerged from the cumulative radicalization of competing agencies (functionalist/structuralist). Current scholarship favors "moderate intentionalism" — Hitler's personal commitment to racial antisemitism was necessary but not sufficient; the specific form of the Holocaust emerged from the military-economic crisis of 1941.
- **Hitler's role vs. structural factors**: Mergel (ch. 19) stresses that the Nazi movement was not simply Hitler's personal creation — it was "a people's party of protest" that responded to genuine structural failures of the Weimar Republic. But the specific ideological and strategic choices after 1933, especially after 1938, were shaped decisively by Hitler's personal convictions.
- **Popular support**: The source rejects the "Hitler's willing executioners" model of German society as uniformly antisemitic while also rejecting the "ordinary Germans were victims of the regime" exculpatory narrative. Genuine popular support for rearmament and full employment coexisted with ignorance of, indifference to, or active participation in persecution of Jews.

**Collection coverage**: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]] provides strong analytical framing. Tooze's *Wages of Destruction* (2006) is the definitive economic study. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection is thin on post-1933 German history in general.
