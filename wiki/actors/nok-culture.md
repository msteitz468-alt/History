# The Nok Culture

## Overview

The **Nok culture** of central **Nigeria** (c. 500 BCE – c. 200 CE) was, in
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Burstein, Ch 23), the **earliest recognized complex society of
West Africa** — long known only for its striking art, now understood as a widespread
iron-using farming civilization.

## Terracottas and Early Iron

For decades the Nok culture was represented solely by **remarkable terracotta figures**
(probably shrine dedications) unearthed by tin miners. Radiocarbon dates from the settlement of
**Samun Dukiya** and the industrial site of **Taruga** revealed that it extended over some
**75,000 km²** and was created by **iron-using farmers** over roughly **700 years**. Nok is
central to the case that **ironworking in sub-Saharan Africa was an independent achievement** —
its dates rival or precede the earliest North African and Nile-Valley iron, undercutting the
old diffusionist assumption (see [[bantu-expansion]]).

## Significance

Nok demonstrates that **complex, technologically sophisticated society arose indigenously in
West Africa** centuries before any documented contact across the Sahara. With the
**Tichitt Tradition** of Mauritania and the proto-urban cities of the Niger Delta
([[jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]]), it forms the deep background to the later Sahelian states such as
**[[ghana-empire|Ghana]]**.

## Historiography

Known entirely through **archaeology**, Nok exemplifies CWH IV's argument that Africa's
"dark centuries" were dark only for lack of **written** sources — archaeology and radiocarbon
dating have steadily pushed back both the antiquity and the independence of sub-Saharan
complexity.
