# The Nok Culture

## Overview

The **Nok culture** of central **Nigeria** (c. 500 BCE – c. 200 CE) was, in
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Burstein, Ch 23), the **earliest recognized complex society of
West Africa** — long known only for its striking art, now understood as a widespread
iron-using farming civilization.

## Terracottas and Early Iron

For decades the Nok culture was represented solely by **remarkable terracotta figures**
(probably shrine dedications) unearthed by tin miners. Radiocarbon dates from the settlement of
**Samun Dukiya** and the industrial site of **Taruga** revealed that it extended over some
**75,000 km²** and was created by **iron-using farmers** over roughly **700 years**. Nok is
central to the case that **ironworking in sub-Saharan Africa was an independent achievement** —
its dates rival or precede the earliest North African and Nile-Valley iron, undercutting the
old diffusionist assumption (see [[bantu-expansion]]).

## Significance

Nok demonstrates that **complex, technologically sophisticated society arose indigenously in
West Africa** centuries before any documented contact across the Sahara. With the
**Tichitt Tradition** of Mauritania and the proto-urban cities of the Niger Delta
([[jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]]), it forms the deep background to the later Sahelian states such as
**[[ghana-empire|Ghana]]**.

## The Saharan barrier and the absence of a Bronze Age

[[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of West Africa* Vol. I]] (Thurstan
Shaw, Ch. 2) frames Nok within a structural fact of African deep history: **sub-Saharan
Africa had no Bronze Age.** After the **desiccation of the Sahara from c. 3000 BCE** the
desert became an increasingly impenetrable barrier (until the camel became common, c. 3rd
c. CE), sealing the sub-Saharan world off from the Bronze Age urban revolution of Egypt
and Mesopotamia at the very moment it was unfolding. Where Europe received copper, bronze,
the wheel, and then iron in sequence, sub-Saharan Africa **skipped copper/bronze
metallurgy and the wheel and re-entered wider contact through iron** — a sequence Shaw
insists is "no reflection on Africans." The Nok smelters are, with Ntereso (Ghana), the
earliest evidence of that iron age.

The same chapter notes an **artistic continuity**: features of the Nok terracottas
(disproportionately large heads, distinctive eyes) reappear in the later terracotta and
stone sculpture of **[[ife|Ile-Ife]]**, suggesting Nok stands at the head of the figurative
tradition that culminates in the great art of Ife and [[benin-kingdom|Benin]].

## Historiography

Known entirely through **archaeology**, Nok exemplifies CWH IV's argument that Africa's
"dark centuries" were dark only for lack of **written** sources — archaeology and radiocarbon
dating have steadily pushed back both the antiquity and the independence of sub-Saharan
complexity. The **UNESCO [[gha-vol2-ancient-civilizations|General History of Africa, Vol. II]]** (ch. 24,
West Africa before the 7th c.) treats Nok and the early ironworking at **Taruga** as the West African
anchor of the continent's early Iron Age (see [[bantu-expansion]] on the diffusion-vs-independent-invention
debate over African iron).

**Revisionism on iron origins.** The diffusion-vs-independence question has *moved* over the
half-century these three sources span. Writing in 1966 (Ajayi & Crowder), **Shaw leaned toward
diffusion**, weighing two external sources — **Meroe** ("the Birmingham of Africa," with its
huge iron-slag heaps, c. 4th c. BCE) reaching Nok via Kordofan and Darfur, and **Carthage**
passing iron to its Berber hinterland and thence south — while flagging a Ghanaian claim of
ironworking before 1000 BCE that, *if* confirmed, would force independent invention to be taken
seriously. The later syntheses ([[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]; GHA II) tilt the other way,
treating sub-Saharan ironworking as plausibly **independent**, its dates rivalling or preceding
North African iron. The shift illustrates how radiocarbon accumulation steadily favoured African
autochthony — part of the broader rejection of the [[hamitic-hypothesis-african-states|Hamitic
Hypothesis]].
