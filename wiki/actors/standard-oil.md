# Standard Oil Company

The Standard Oil Company, incorporated in Ohio in 1870 by [[actors/rockefeller-john-d|John D. Rockefeller]] and partners (notably [[actors/flagler-henry|Henry Flagler]]), became the archetypal American [[concepts/business-trust|trust]] and the dominant force in the world petroleum industry. Through secret railroad rebates and drawbacks, horizontal consolidation of refining (the [[events/cleveland-massacre-1872|Cleveland Massacre]] of 1872), and later vertical integration into pipelines, crude production, and marketing, it controlled roughly 90% of American refining capacity at its peak. Reorganized as the Standard Oil Trust in 1882 — a legal-organizational innovation — and later as a New Jersey holding company, it was dissolved into 34 successor firms by the U.S. Supreme Court in [[events/standard-oil-dissolution-1911|Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States (1911)]].

## Mechanisms of Control

Standard's dominance rested on **transport leverage** rather than superior refining alone: secret railroad rebates and "drawbacks" (rebates paid on *rivals'* shipments), with Standard acting as the traffic "evener" allocating shares among competing railroads. As railroads weakened, it shifted to outright **pipeline ownership** (controlling nearly the entire network by 1879), then to **vertical integration** into crude production (after the Frasch desulfurization breakthrough on Lima crude, 1888). Acquired firms kept their old names and secret accounts, feigning independence; sellers were pressed to take appreciating Standard stock. The **committee system** (executive committee at 26 Broadway plus specialized committees) unified dozens of partially owned subsidiaries while circulating performance figures to drive internal competition.

## Organizational Forms and Peak

Incorporated 1870 (Ohio, $1M capital, ~10% of US refining); the **Standard Oil Trust** (Jan 2, 1882) consolidated ~40 companies under nine trustees controlling ~90% of refining; after the 1892 Ohio quo warranto ruling it reorganized as the **New Jersey holding company** (1899) with control unchanged. At its zenith (1890s) it marketed ~84% of US petroleum products. By 1899 it ran a third of US crude — its highest share — eroding to 14% by 1911 as new fields (Spindletop 1901, Texas, Oklahoma) and foreign rivals (Nobel/Rothschild Baku, Royal Dutch, Shell) emerged.

## Decline and Successors

The Sherman Act prosecution (filed 1906) ended in the [[events/standard-oil-dissolution-1911|1911 Supreme Court dissolution]] into 34 successor firms — ancestors of Exxon, Mobil, Chevron, Amoco, and Conoco — whose shares then soared, paradoxically multiplying Rockefeller's fortune.
