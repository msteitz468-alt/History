# Al-Mansur bi-Nasr Allah

**Isma'il al-Mansur bi-Nasr Allah** ("the One who is Victorious by the Victory of God," r. 946–953) was the
**third [[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] Imam-caliph** — the first born in Ifriqiya (January 914) — who broke the
**[[khariji-revolt-of-abu-yazid-943|revolt of Abu Yazid]]**, rebuilt a devastated state, and founded the
capital **[[mansuriyya|al-Mansuriyya]]**. He was raised as the close companion of his grandfather
[[al-mahdi-billah|al-Mahdi]] and named heir on the deathbed of his father [[al-qaim-bi-amr-allah|al-Qa'im]].

## Victory over Abu Yazid

Taking the field as crown prince (his father's death concealed), al-Mansur counter-attacked the Kharijite
armies, relieved the siege of **Sousse**, and won back **[[qayrawan|Qayrawan]]**, whose people locked their
gates against Abu Yazid chanting "obedience only to Isma'il." In a decisive series of battles south of Qayrawan
in **947** he personally rallied his forces — refusing to lower the caliphal parasol that marked his position —
and routed the rebels; **Abu Yazid** was cornered in the Hodna mountains and died of his wounds in August 947.
Only then did al-Mansur announce al-Qa'im's death and take the regnal title al-Mansur.

## Reconstruction and governance

Ifriqiya lay wrecked — refugees, plundered markets, ruined farmland. Al-Mansur's recovery measures became
hallmarks of Fatimid rule: he **cancelled a year's taxes** (land tax and alms taxes alike) for Muslims and
*dhimmis* through his aged chamberlain Ja'far al-Hajib, and — in what the source calls "a momentous development
in Islamic governance" — appointed a **Sunni Maliki chief judge** over Qayrawan rather than an Ismaili qadi,
formally recognising a legal school different from the ruling dynasty's. On the battlefield of his victory he
built **al-Mansuriyya**, a round city like Baghdad, settling 14,000 Kutama families there; it became the
prototype for **[[cairo|Cairo]]**.

He summoned **[[qadi-al-numan|Qadi al-Nu'man]]** as chief justice of all Ifriqiya (948), continued the
Fatimid–Byzantine and Fatimid–Umayyad wars over **Sicily** through the **Kalbid** governors, and himself
composed the ***Tathbit al-Imama*** ("Foundations of the Imamate"), one of the earliest surviving works by a
Fatimid Imam, arguing the necessity of a divinely chosen guide of the Prophet's progeny. An antiquarian, he
was fascinated by the Roman and Punic ruins of **Carthage**. He died of a chronic liver illness in **953**,
aged 39, having groomed his son **Ma'add** ([[al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Mu'izz]]) for the succession.

## Counterfactual significance

Al-Mansur is the restorer without whom the 909 foundation would have been a footnote. His pragmatic
accommodation of the Sunni Maliki majority — a Maliki chief judge, tax relief for all communities — set the
template of pluralist governance the dynasty carried into Egypt.
