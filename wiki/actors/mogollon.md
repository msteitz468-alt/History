# Mogollon

One of three major prehistoric cultural traditions of the Greater Southwest, the Mogollon occupied the mountains of eastern Arizona and western New Mexico, where the Colorado Plateau gives way to lower deserts along the Mogollon Rim. Their most celebrated achievement is the Mimbres pottery tradition, among the most visually sophisticated ceramics in North American prehistory. The Mogollon were intermediate in position — geographically, temporally, and culturally — between the Hohokam to their west and the Ancestral Pueblo to their north.

## Overview

The Mogollon tradition developed slightly later than the Hohokam, with the first villages appearing around 200 BCE on high ground in rugged eastern Arizona and western New Mexico. Early communities consisted of fewer than twenty houses on ridges and bluffs of the Mogollon Rim. The tradition contributed importantly to neighboring cultures: Hohokam sites to the west and Ancestral Pueblo sites to the north show Mogollon influences, and the Mogollon themselves adopted and modified pottery, architectural forms, and ceremonial structures from neighboring traditions over centuries.

## Geographic Distribution

The Mogollon heartland was the mountain zones of eastern Arizona and western New Mexico — the highlands where the Colorado Plateau descends to the Chihuahuan and Sonoran deserts. This rugged terrain offered cooler temperatures and somewhat more reliable moisture than the surrounding deserts. The Mimbres branch occupied the Mimbres Valley in southwestern New Mexico. The late regional center Paquimé (Casas Grandes) lay in northwestern Chihuahua, Mexico, at the southern edge of the tradition's influence.

## Dating and Chronology

- First villages: ~200 BCE (high-ground pithouse clusters)
- Mogollon Pithouse period: 200 BCE–1000 CE (circular, then rectangular pithouses)
- Mimbres Classic: 1000–1130 CE (largest surface pueblos, peak pottery production)
- Decline: 1130–1150 CE (Mimbres pottery disappears; climate disruption)
- Dispersal and transformation: 1150–1450 CE (refugee communities, Salado phenomenon)
- Paquimé Medio Period: 1200–1450 CE

## Economy and Subsistence

Like the Hohokam and Ancestral Pueblo, Mogollon subsistence rested on the Upper Sonoran Agricultural Complex: maize, beans, squash, and bottle gourd, supplemented by tobacco. Early Mogollon probably used simpler irrigation than the large-scale Hohokam canal systems. Hunting and gathering supplemented the agricultural base, serving as backup in poor crop years. The Mimbres branch shows increased agricultural dependence during the Classic period (1000–1130 CE), coinciding with pueblo aggregation and the elaboration of pottery production.

## Architecture and Technology

Early Mogollon pithouses were circular, built into the ground to knee or waist height, and typically oriented east. Their partial subterranean construction provided thermal insulation in the cool mountain environment. Excavated entrance ramps on the east side captured morning sun. Over time pithouses transitioned from round to rectangular forms — a change driven by the structural requirements of surface construction.

Mogollon communities lived primarily in pithouses before 1000 CE but transitioned to aboveground rooms thereafter. The change from round to square housing units was accompanied by a shift from residential pithouses to dedicated ceremonial kivas. In historic Pueblo villages, kivas serve as men's clubs modeled on older pithouses — places where male members of clan segments could gather in matrilocal societies where residences were dominated by related females.

Large square great kivas appeared in some (not all) Mogollon villages after 850 CE. These very large ceremonial structures accommodated whole communities rather than small kin groups, indicating a scale of communal integration beyond household-level ceremony. The bow and arrow appeared before 1000 CE, though Mogollon hunters retained spear throwers as well.

Beginning around 650 CE, Mogollon potters began producing decorated ceramics — red-painted designs on brown backgrounds. This was an important regional contribution to the vast range of pottery types produced across the Greater Southwest.

## Mimbres Pottery

The Mimbres branch of the Mogollon, occupying the Mimbres Valley of southwestern New Mexico, is best known for the extraordinary decorated pottery bowls produced at the height of their development, 1000–1130 CE. Mimbres black-on-white pottery is particularly appealing to modern aesthetic sensibilities, a fact that has led to severe site destruction through systematic looting — sometimes using bulldozers — rendering many Mimbres sites archaeologically unusable.

The earliest Mimbres pottery dates to 200–550 CE: brown and undecorated. Later types were red-slipped, then red-on-brown. Around 750 CE Mimbres potters applied white slip before decorating with red paint, creating more complex designs. Still later, vessels were fired in reducing atmospheres, causing red paint to turn black — the classic black-on-white tradition.

Classic Mimbres bowls (1000–1130 CE) feature stylized human, animal, and other designs on their interiors. Symbols range from figurative to nonfigurative, depicted in positive or negative views. Vessels used as burial offerings were ritually "killed" by puncturing their bottoms. While exquisitely made — probably by craft specialists — there is no evidence they were produced exclusively for an elite minority.

Mimbres settlements in the early phases were clusters of circular pithouses on steep hilltops and ridges. By 1000 CE, communities had aggregated into larger surface pueblo communities with some having substantial ceremonial rooms. Construction of new pueblos and Mimbres pottery manufacture ended around 1130 CE when climate change disrupted the region.

## Paquimé (Casas Grandes)

Located in northwestern Chihuahua, Mexico, Paquimé was a large, influential community during its Medio Period (1200–1450 CE). Its multistory adobe domestic structures contained 1,000 or more rooms. Paquimé was simultaneously a political and ritual center: it combined Pueblo domestic architecture with Mesoamerican ceremonial facilities, iconography, and goods. Large ball courts, massive ovens for communal food preparation, and many small platform mounds (some in animal shapes) are present. More than four million shell artifacts, copper items, and the remains of hundreds of macaws and parrots were recovered. Paquimé is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site administered by the Mexican government.

## Decline and Legacy

Some Mogollon communities dispersed as early as 1150 CE; others persisted until 1450 CE. Mimbres pottery disappeared by 1150 CE. Gila Polychrome ceramics, associated with the Salado phenomenon, appear in Mogollon areas as Ancestral Pueblo refugees migrated southward into still-viable farming zones, producing ethnogenesis and cultural amalgamation. After 1450 CE, the last Mimbres people disappeared from the region; the area was subsequently occupied by Apache hunter-gatherers. No single modern community claims direct descent, though Pueblo peoples of the Rio Grande recognize cultural continuity with ancient southwestern traditions.

## Cordell & Smith (CHNPA I.1 ch. 4)

*part_of:* [[southwest-agricultural-development]]; *concurrent_with:* [[hohokam]]. Mogollon designates ancient inhabitants of the rugged mountainous area immediately east of the Hohokam region, encompassing mountains and low-lying basins from central Arizona/New Mexico south into Chihuahua. Pre-700 remains strongly resemble Hohokam (simple palettes, figurines, plain brown/red and red-on-brown pottery); may have taken up farming somewhat later than the Hohokam, or at least depended on hunting and gathering longer; inhumations relatively more common vs Hohokam cremations.

**After ~700:** more differentiated but internally variable; sites move from ridges to near streams and arable land. A.D. 700–1000: deep pithouses with ramp entries. The Mogollon did **not** build platform mounds or ball courts, but some villages had a very large communal pit structure — a **great kiva** (sometimes multi-community). After ~1000–1150: single-story contiguous stone pueblos with plazas. After ~1000: black-on-white ceramics including Classic Mimbres (often “killed” bowls in burials). Craft/trade lag behind Hohokam until late flourishing at [[casas-grandes-paquime]]. *followed_by:* late Paquimé craft peak in the broader Mogollon-related south.

Cordell & Smith note that through most of its history the Casas Grandes region **resembled Mogollon**; fourteenth-century reorganization into Paquimé is treated on that place page. Late Mogollon–Zuni and some Mogollon–Piro links are proposed for post-contact continuity (less secure than Ancestral Pueblo–Pueblo).

## Historiography

The Mogollon were recognized as a distinct tradition in the twentieth century, distinct from both Hohokam and Ancestral Pueblo. Key debates include the origin and spread of Mimbres pottery (craft specialization vs. household production; the role of looting in destroying the evidentiary base), the nature of great kivas and their social function, and the relationship between Paquimé and the broader Mogollon world (whether Paquimé should be classified within Mogollon or as a separate tradition with Mesoamerican characteristics). The Mattocks site study has been important for revealing internal social diversity — not all Mimbres communities had the same social structure or access to trade goods.
