# Solomon

Solomon (r. c. **960–930 BCE**), son of [[david|David]] and **Bathsheba**, was the
third and last king of the [[hebrew-kingdom|united Hebrew monarchy]], remembered as
the builder of the **Temple at Jerusalem** and the "enthroned merchant" whose reign
was the material zenith of Israelite kingship — but whose burdens precipitated the
**division of the kingdom** at his death. He is treated in CAH II.2 (chapter XXXIV,
Eissfeldt).

## Five Features of the Reign

The volume organises Solomon's reign — which cannot be set in chronological order
from the sources — under five themes:

1. **Chariotry.** Solomon re-armed Israel around **chariotry** (an average ~1,400
   chariots, 4,000 horses), the decisive arm his father David had pointedly declined
   to adopt, posting garrisons in fortified "chariot cities." Excavated stables at
   **Megiddo** (and works at Hazor and Gezer) illustrate the programme.
2. **Administrative districts.** He divided the kingdom (chiefly the northern part,
   Israel) into **twelve districts**, each provisioning the court and garrisons for
   one month a year — cutting across the old tribal lines and including former
   Canaanite city-territories. That **Judah was apparently exempted or treated
   specially** became a northern grievance.
3. **Commerce as royal monopoly.** Solomon ran trade as a crown enterprise: a
   **Red Sea fleet from Ezion-geber to Ophir** (gold, silver, ivory, apes), the
   "**Tarshish ships**" with [[phoenicians|Tyre]], copper and iron works in the
   Arabah, the caravan trade through Tadmor (Palmyra) and to the Queen of **Sheba**,
   and a near-**monopoly in horses (from Cilicia) and chariots (from Egypt)** resold
   to "the kings of the Hittites and of Aram."
4. **Building.** The **Temple and palace** complex at Jerusalem (built with
   [[phoenicians|Tyrian]] craftsmen and Lebanese cedar supplied by **Hiram of Tyre**),
   plus fortified store- and chariot-cities. The Temple — entrance-hall with the
   pillars **Jachin and Boaz**, *hekal*, and innermost *debir* for the Ark — followed
   a **Syro-Phoenician temple type**, confirmed by the excavated 9th-century temple at
   **Tell Tainat** in the Amuq, which corresponds closely to the biblical plan.
5. **Court culture.** A cosmopolitan court on the international model — a large
   diplomatic harem (the **Egyptian princess** as principal wife, whose dowry brought
   the city of **Gezer**), and a reputation for **"wisdom"** literature (proverbs and
   songs, including incipient botanical/zoological lore — the riddle-contests with the
   Queen of Sheba and with Hiram).

## Foreign Position and Decline

Solomon could not hold all of David's conquests: **Aram-Damascus** (under Rezon) and
**Edom** (under Hadad, who had sheltered in Egypt) loosened Israelite control, and he
**ceded the district of Cabul in Galilee to Tyre** in payment for building materials.
The reign also marks a **re-awakening of Egyptian interest** in Palestine (the pharaoh
— probably Siamun — taking Gezer and ceding it as the princess's dowry; later Egypt
sheltering both Hadad and Jeroboam).

## The Burden and the Division

Solomon's splendour rested on **heavy taxation and corvée** that profited only the
court, officials, and merchants while pressing the mass of the people — sowing the
social grievances that the prophets Amos and Isaiah would later denounce. The
northern district overseer **Jeroboam ben-Nebat** rebelled and fled to Egypt; at
Solomon's death his son **Rehoboam** refused relief, and Israel seceded under
Jeroboam, leaving the **Davidic dynasty** to rule only **Judah**. The united monarchy
thus ended; the religious tradition it had centred on Jerusalem did not.

## Significance

- `produced:` the **First Temple** and the centralisation of the Yahweh cult at
  Jerusalem — a decisive step in the development of Israelite religion (while also
  accelerating, the volume notes, the adoption of Canaanite cult forms).
- `caused_by:` the secure throne and empire bequeathed by [[david|David]].
- `produced:` the **division of the kingdom** into [[hebrew-kingdom|Israel and Judah]],
  through the taxation and labour burdens of his programme.
- `affiliated_with:` [[phoenicians|Tyre (Hiram)]] and Egypt; `opposed_by:`
  [[arameans|Aram-Damascus]] and Edom.

## Historiography

The Solomon narrative (1 Kings 3–11) mixes documentary material of the "very best
kind" (court and district lists, building accounts) with legend (the judgement of
Solomon, the Queen of Sheba, the wisdom traditions). Eissfeldt extracts the
administrative and architectural core while flagging the folkloric overlay; the
**Tell Tainat** temple gives the building account independent support. As with
[[david|David]], later scholarship debates how grand the historical reign really was
given limited archaeology; the collection reflects the confident mid-century
reconstruction.
