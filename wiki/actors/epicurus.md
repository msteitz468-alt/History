# Epicurus

Founder of [[epicureanism|Epicureanism]] (341–271 BCE): born of Athenian parents on **Samos**, settled in [[athens|Athens]] in **307 BCE**, and taught until his death in **271**. His system — ethical hedonism, atomic physics, empiricist logic — became one of the two great practical philosophies of the Hellenistic and Roman worlds.

## Role

Epicurus organized philosophy as a **way of life** centred on the Garden community. **Jonathan Barnes** (OHCW, 1986) summarizes the system: in ethics, hedonism — pleasure is the sole good; in physics, atomism; in logic, empiricism. He wrote hugely — three letters preserved, Herculaneum fragments, plus the full Latin restatement by [[lucretius|Lucretius]] (*De rerum natura*, 1st c. BCE), who still called him “the father, the discoverer of things.” Followers were doctrinally **conservative** relative to the evolving Stoa: first-century Lucretius remains faithfully Epicurean.

Key ethical maxims Barnes highlights: refer all choice to health of body and tranquillity of soul; pleasure as absence of pain (not revelries); virtue required for pleasant life; abjured politics but valued friendship; “death is nothing to us.” Physics subordinate to ethics: bodies + void; anti-teleology; gods without anger/gratitude; soul dissolved at death; **clinamen** to free volition.

## Significance

Without Epicurus there is no durable Greco-Roman alternative to Stoic virtue-ethics as a mass elite programme: atomism without teleology, gods without providence, death without terror, pleasure redefined as freedom from disturbance. Counterfactual: a Hellenistic intellectual world dominated only by Stoic and Academic lines would lack the physics-to-ethics pipeline Lucretius later gave Latin culture. Barnes pairs him with [[zeno-of-citium|Zeno]] as co-architect of the “narrower and more introspective notion of the Hellenistic schools” that displaced Aristotelian unity of science and philosophy.

## Historiography

OHCW (Barnes, 1986) gives the standard tripartite summary and stresses continuity to Lucretius; later papyrology and specialized monographs refine the letters and *On Nature*. Polemical ancient sources (including Cicero) systematically distorted “pleasure.”
