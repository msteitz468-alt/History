## Note on Disambiguation

This page covers **Abū l-Barakāt al-Baghdādī** (c. 1080–c. 1165), the Jewish-Muslim philosopher and physician from Baghdad. He is not to be confused with **ʿAbd al-Laṭīf al-Baghdādī** (d. 1231), a near-contemporary and also a critic of Avicenna who also bore the nisba "al-Baghdādī." ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]])

## Overview

Abū l-Barakāt al-Baghdādī was among the earliest and most original of the post-Avicennan philosophers in the Islamic East. Jewish by birth and formation, he converted to Islam towards the end of his life under circumstances that remain unclear — Muslim sources disagree on the motive, ranging from a desire for greater professional respect to a crisis following the death of the sultan's wife in his care as physician. Whatever the circumstances, he retained enough leverage to set conditions: his daughters could still inherit his property without converting.

He flourished at the caliphal court in Baghdad during the Seljūq period. The main scholarly publication on him — a collection of articles by Shlomo Pines (1979) — was already four decades old when Adamson wrote, and research has advanced little since. He is thus "an outstanding example of the problem of understudied later thinkers" ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## Principal Work

His philosophical masterpiece is the ***Kitāb al-Muʿtabar*** (Book of What Has Been Carefully Considered), an ambitious reconsideration of all major departments of Avicenna's philosophy. The title signals his method: he has "carefully considered" both the ancient authorities (Aristotle) and the modern ones (Avicenna), found both wanting, and sets out the fruits of his own reflection. This rhetoric of independence echoes al-Ghazālī, Avicenna, and Abū Bakr al-Rāzī — all of whom made the same claim. As a Jewish thinker not trained in Islamic law, Abū l-Barakāt approaches philosophy from outside the standard Avicennan formation of jurist-theologian. He nonetheless demonstrates mastery of kalām dialectical method, recalling the earlier Jewish use of kalām by Saadia Gaon.

## Physics: Inclination and Motion

Abū l-Barakāt engages with Avicenna's adaptation of John Philoponus' "inclination" (*mayl*) theory of projectile motion. Avicenna argued that a thrown stone receives an inclination to move upward, gradually diminished by air resistance, until the natural inclination to fall reasserts itself — an anticipation of impetus theory.

Abū l-Barakāt refines this with a "dual inclination" theory: throughout the stone's upward arc, *both* inclinations are simultaneously active. The natural downward inclination, though present from the start, cannot immediately overcome the externally imposed upward one. The stone's motion slows as the downward inclination gains ground, then accelerates downward as the imposed inclination fades. Crucially, there is no moment of rest at the apex — the two forces simply shift their equilibrium continuously. Adamson calls this "the best explanation of these phenomena anyone managed to offer prior to the modern concept of gravity."

This dual-inclination theory was later invoked by the theologian al-Ījī (fl. Shīrāz, 14th c.) in his speculative argument that the earth might itself be revolving, with the earth having two simultaneous inclinations — one toward its natural center, one in rotation.

He also affirms, against Avicenna, that **void is possible** and that **time is independent of motion**. On time: "We do not need to see anything moving to be aware of time passing. Rather, it is something that is just obvious to us." He uses the Arabic idiom "may God let you go on longer" to show that ordinary speakers use time to measure *existence*, not motion — an Avicennan strategy of appealing to linguistic intuition, applied to a non-Avicennan conclusion.

## Psychology: The Soul as Unified Self-Awareness

Abū l-Barakāt's most radical departure from Avicenna concerns the soul. The Aristotelians analyzed the soul as a bundle of distinct faculties: vegetative, animal, rational. Abū l-Barakāt rejects this entirely. The soul is not a bundle of capacities; it is a *single seat of awareness*: "the same soul that sees and hears, that imagines and thinks and dreams." It is the principle of first-person perspective — necessarily a unity, since it is identical with awareness itself.

He argues for this unity by pointing to Arabic expressions such as "my soul is pleased," which identify the soul as the subject of all mental states. He further argues that if the soul were the body, one would lose part of it by losing a limb — which is absurd. The body conditions which objects come before awareness (your eye faces the object in good lighting), but the unified self that is aware remains immaterial.

Adamson observes that this theory is developed primarily for theological purposes: by showing that a single unified self can be aware of an external multiplicity without itself becoming multiple, Abū l-Barakāt enables a parallel argument about God. God can know the entire universe by knowing Himself, yet remain perfectly one — solving the problem that had dogged Avicenna's account of divine knowledge.

## Relation to Jewish Philosophy

Abū l-Barakāt wrote a commentary on Ecclesiastes and cited the Hebrew Bible occasionally in his philosophical writings, though his Jewish background is generally not prominent in the *Muʿtabar*. His views on time and void parallel those of the later Jewish philosopher Ḥasdai Crescas (14th c.) — a connection not yet fully resolved by scholars, though Crescas follows Abū l-Barakāt chronologically, not vice versa. The two may draw on common sources including Philoponus.

## Historical Significance

Abū l-Barakāt exemplifies the strategy that defines post-Avicennan Islamic philosophy: philosophical innovation achieved by critical engagement with Avicenna from within his own framework. "There was nothing more Avicennan than questioning the traditional authorities and adopting new and innovative positions when it seemed like a good idea." His dual-inclination theory of motion, his unified theory of the self, and his view that time measures existence rather than motion all influenced later thinkers, including [[fakhr-al-din-al-razi]], who found his discussion of time "intriguing" and devoted extensive attention to questions Abū l-Barakāt had raised.
