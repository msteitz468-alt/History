# Monica

**Monica** (c. 331–387), the mother of **[[augustine-of-hippo|Augustine of Hippo]]** and,
through the *Confessions*, one of the most vividly portrayed women of antiquity. Her name
may derive from a local Numidian deity (a speculation Brown flags as such), a hint of the
Berber substrate beneath African Christianity.

Raised in an austere Christian household, Monica kept the "primitive" usages of the African
church — Sabbath fasts, commemorative meals at the tombs of the martyrs — that educated men
disdained. She married the hot-tempered, unfaithful, pagan burgess **Patricius**, managing
him by patience and eventually converting him before his death; she bore at least three
children (Augustine, Navigius, and probably a daughter). She believed a classical pagan
education would ultimately make Augustine a better Christian, and placed great confidence in
prophetic dreams, claiming to discern authentic ones instinctively.

Augustine's own portrait is double-edged. In the earlier books of the *Confessions* she is
"a relentless figure," possessive — "she loved to have me with her... far more than most
mothers" — who shut him out of her house when he became a **[[manichaeism|Manichee]]** and
followed him to the coast when he fled for Rome in 384 (he deceived her and sailed by night
while she prayed at a shrine of St Cyprian). She rejoined him at Milan in 385, arranged his
marriage to a Catholic heiress (prompting the dismissal of his concubine), became a devoted
follower of **[[ambrose-of-milan|Ambrose]]**, and took part in the philosophical dialogues at
Cassiciacum as the voice of "primitive Catholic piety." Shortly before her death, she and
Augustine, "leaning in a window" over a garden at Ostia, briefly shared a mystical ascent
toward divine Wisdom — the famous **Vision of Ostia** (*Confessions* IX). She died at Ostia
within a fortnight, aged about fifty-six, telling Augustine she had never in her life heard
"one sharp word" from him. Augustine, writing a decade later, recast her not as an "oracle of
God" but as "an ordinary human being... a sinner like himself, equally in need of mercy." A
fragment of her original marble epitaph was rediscovered by two boys in a courtyard at Ostia
in 1945.
