# The Shang Dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE)

The first **historically attested** Chinese dynasty — a Bronze Age polity of the Yellow River
basin whose **oracle-bone inscriptions** are the earliest known Chinese writing and the
beginning of China's documentary history. From John Keay's
[[keay-china-a-history|*China: A History*]].

## Authentication: the oracle bones

The Shang's historicity (like the legendary Xia's) was once doubted — until the discovery of
the **oracle bones**. From 1899, "dragon bones" sold as medicine in Beijing were found to be
**ox scapulae and turtle plastrons** incised with archaic Chinese characters; traced to
**Anyang** (Henan), they led to excavations (from the late 1920s) that uncovered the Shang's
last great cult-centre — palace foundations, vast royal tombs (the intact tomb of **Lady Hao**),
and the bones themselves. The Shang were "handsomely authenticated," and the names of kings on
the bones matched later texts.

## Divination and the king

The Shang elite were **literate**, and Chinese script today is "unique in being the direct
descendant of one used in the second millennium BC." The bones record **royal divinations**:
the king posed "charges" (questions phrased as statements) on everything from war, weather, and
harvests to toothache and childbirth, applied heat to produce cracks, "read" the answer, and had
a scribe **engrave and file** the result — China's documentary history beginning "with an
official and distinctly bureaucratic archive." Keay stresses the king as "the pivotal persona
in a transcendental bureaucratic hierarchy," reporting to his ancestors and the supreme deity
***Di*** much as his officers reported to him. David Keightley notes a "characteristic
**this-worldliness**" — gods and ancestors were practical participants in human affairs, "to be
consulted, activated and used," not transcendent creators or lawgivers — a trait that would
color later Chinese philosophy.

## A "segmentary state"

Despite its ritual grandeur, bronze-casting genius, and despotic power, the late Shang was a
**"segmentary state"** — a small core directly ruled by the king, surrounded by kin-linked
subordinate domains and by "scattered and despised communities" of full autonomy. Linked by
**kinship, not territory**, its realm reached no farther than northern Henan and southeastern
Shanxi by c. 1000 BCE; the Shang were "at best first among equals" among rival
bronze-casting, possibly literate, polities. Kingship was **peripatetic**, the capital moved
when "inauspicious," and Shang power depended on the king's restless energy (hunting, fighting,
inspecting, divining).

## Significance

The Shang demonstrated, more than a thousand years before unification, "many of the cultural
traits … seen as typically Chinese": the emphasis on **lineage and ancestor-worship**, ritual
and the calendar, bronze ritual vessels, and above all a **bureaucratic literacy** in which
"authority and history went hand in hand." It was overthrown by the **[[zhou-dynasty|Zhou]]**
c. 1046 BCE — who justified their conquest with the new doctrine of the
**[[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate of Heaven]]**. The Shang's selection into the "legitimate"
dynastic succession reflects its role as the recognizable seedbed of [[china|Chinese
civilization]].
