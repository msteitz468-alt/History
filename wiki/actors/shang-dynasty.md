# The Shang Dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE)

The first **historically attested** Chinese dynasty — a Bronze Age polity of the Yellow River
basin whose **oracle-bone inscriptions** are the earliest known Chinese writing and the
beginning of China's documentary history. From John Keay's
[[keay-china-a-history|*China: A History*]].

## Authentication: the oracle bones

The Shang's historicity (like the legendary Xia's) was once doubted — until the discovery of
the **oracle bones**. From 1899, "dragon bones" sold as medicine in Beijing were found to be
**ox scapulae and turtle plastrons** incised with archaic Chinese characters; traced to
**Anyang** (Henan), they led to excavations (from the late 1920s) that uncovered the Shang's
last great cult-centre — palace foundations, vast royal tombs (the intact tomb of **Lady Hao**),
and the bones themselves. The Shang were "handsomely authenticated," and the names of kings on
the bones matched later texts.

## Divination and the king

The Shang elite were **literate**, and Chinese script today is "unique in being the direct
descendant of one used in the second millennium BC." The bones record **royal divinations**:
the king posed "charges" (questions phrased as statements) on everything from war, weather, and
harvests to toothache and childbirth, applied heat to produce cracks, "read" the answer, and had
a scribe **engrave and file** the result — China's documentary history beginning "with an
official and distinctly bureaucratic archive." Keay stresses the king as "the pivotal persona
in a transcendental bureaucratic hierarchy," reporting to his ancestors and the supreme deity
***Di*** much as his officers reported to him. David Keightley notes a "characteristic
**this-worldliness**" — gods and ancestors were practical participants in human affairs, "to be
consulted, activated and used," not transcendent creators or lawgivers — a trait that would
color later Chinese philosophy.

## A "segmentary state"

Despite its ritual grandeur, bronze-casting genius, and despotic power, the late Shang was a
**"segmentary state"** — a small core directly ruled by the king, surrounded by kin-linked
subordinate domains and by "scattered and despised communities" of full autonomy. Linked by
**kinship, not territory**, its realm reached no farther than northern Henan and southeastern
Shanxi by c. 1000 BCE; the Shang were "at best first among equals" among rival
bronze-casting, possibly literate, polities. Kingship was **peripatetic**, the capital moved
when "inauspicious," and Shang power depended on the king's restless energy (hunting, fighting,
inspecting, divining).

## Significance

The Shang demonstrated, more than a thousand years before unification, "many of the cultural
traits … seen as typically Chinese": the emphasis on **lineage and ancestor-worship**, ritual
and the calendar, bronze ritual vessels, and above all a **bureaucratic literacy** in which
"authority and history went hand in hand." It was overthrown by the **[[zhou-dynasty|Zhou]]**
c. 1046 BCE — who justified their conquest with the new doctrine of the
**[[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate of Heaven]]**. The Shang's selection into the "legitimate"
dynastic succession reflects its role as the recognizable seedbed of [[china|Chinese
civilization]].

## Additional detail from Maisels, *Early Civilizations of the Old World* (1999), Chapter 5 (Range 05 extraction)

**Clanship, conical clan structure and origins of the territorial state (Longshan → San Dai):**
Complex society developed in Longshan Chalcolithic. "complex and stratified society certainly had a long development in the Longshan Chalcolithic cultures of the Dawenkou (East Coast) ceramic tradition. Human sacrifice, ritual bronzes and scapulimancy are all present there". Burial classes at Chengzi (Longshan) show four grades within lineage clusters: large graves with ledges/caskets/goods (incl. thin-stem cup + pig mandible) to narrow unfurnished pits. Earliest clear stratified lineage burial pattern. (lines ~1280-1295)

"Clans and [their component] lineages... not only continued to serve as primary groups governing social interaction but also provided a genealogical basis for the differentiation of their members into political and economic classes through the mechanism of hierarchical segmentation." "A clan consists of a number of lineages all supposedly descended from a common ancestor, but not all the lineages are of equal standing or rank... Those deemed to be nearest to the founding ancestor of the clan are the most senior and politically powerful. They control the most important fecundity rituals because they are invested with potency by dint of proximity to the ancestors". (lines ~1295-1310)

State formation: "the process of state-formation thus takes place when the apical lineages (at the top of the cones) no longer mix and marry with their clansmen. Instead they interact with other elevated lineages from other clans, who likewise draw their support and revenues from, but are no longer answerable to, inferior lineages." The "conical principle" produces differential membership degrees. King (wang) heads senior lineage with most direct ancestor access to Di. (lines ~1319-1335)

Zu (lineage) core: "The lineage (zu) was probably the most important social framework for coercion; zu rules were the society’s fundamental law." Original graph "composed of an arrow beneath a flag, indicating that it served as the basic military unit, composed of 100 adult men from 100 households." (lines ~1302, ~1369-1370)

**Bronze Age urbanism and capitals (Erlitou / Zhengzhou / Anyang):**
Fundamental unit: "The fundamental social unit throughout the Bronze Age [c.2200 to c. 500 BC] was the walled town [yi] thousands of which dotted the loessic landscape of north China." "A state, guo, consisted of a network of towns, yi, under a royal capital, du." Lineage hierarchy = hierarchy of towns. (lines ~1715-1740)

Erlitou (possible Xia centre, Henan, c.1800–1500 BC): "Palace {compound} No:1... was 108 by 100 metres in size." Stamped-earth platforms, elite tombs with ritual slaughter, early cast bronzes (ritual, weapons, few tools), scapulimancy without inscriptions on bones. "the radiocarbon profile... grouping between 1800 and 2100 BC are too early for Shang (c. 1700–1100 BC) but just right for Xia." Upper phases (III–IV) show strong continuity with early Shang Erligang at Zhengzhou. (lines ~1845-1940)

Zhengzhou (early/mid Shang capital, probably Bo): Large walled enclosure between rivers; massive bronze production (fang ding up to 1 m / 86.4 kg); "Zhengzhou has yielded a vast number of bronze vessels in a wide variety of shapes". Workshops suggest surplus for exchange. (lines ~2070-2155)

Anyang (late Shang capital district, Yinxu): "Anyang is not a single site but the capital district extending over about 24 square kilometres". At Xiaotun: palaces/temples/workshops/servitor housing in distinct sections; "Section B is thought to comprise the royal temple sector". Xibeigang royal tombs: "eleven large tombs... grouped into a western cluster of seven and an eastern cluster of four, happily coinciding in number with the eleven kings from P’an Geng to Ti Yi". Abundant furnishings, human/dog sacrifice, chariots. "the first Chinese civilizations were shamanistic; their art was in fact shamanistic paraphernalia, which were not only the trappings but also the very instruments of political power." (lines ~1990-2065)

**Western Zhou and territorial expansion:**
Zhou used "branching lineage system (zā ngfā)" for expansion: king enfeoffed brothers/younger sons/allies who replicated "small lineage" courts in new territories. "The general pattern of Zhou expansionism…was for the population to follow a vassal prince to a new locality... In the new land... they superseded and were superimposed on the native population." Reinforced guo-ren vs ye-ren distinction. (lines ~1695-1710, ~1380-1395)

Zhou capitals followed similar dispersed pattern; introduced roof tiles and fired bricks. (lines ~2230-2240)

**Childe checklist implications and trade (per Maisels):**
China's "cities" were dispersed clusters, not nucleated. No regular foreign trade in formative periods ("0" on checklist #9): "There is no Chinese equivalent to Gulf or Levant trade... nothing that China wanted or thought it needed from overseas. Thus silk traders made their way to China when they could. Until the Tang Dynasty (AD 618–906), the Chinese did not go abroad to trade." (lines ~2385-2400, ~2655-2665)
