## Overview

The **Antigonids** were the royal dynasty that ruled **Macedon** from 276 to 168 BCE,
the third of the great [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]] houses alongside the
[[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemies]] and [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]]. Descended from
[[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus I Monophthalmus]] (the first Successor to take
the royal title, 306) through his son [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius
Poliorcetes]] (who briefly held Macedon, 294–288), the line secured the Macedonian
throne permanently only with **[[antigonus-ii-gonatas|Antigonus II Gonatas]]** in 276.

**Simon Price** (OHCW, 1986): Alexander’s ancient Argead dynasty was **exterminated**;
possession of Macedon was fiercely contested until **Antigonus Gonatas** secured power
in **276**; his heirs, the Antigonid dynasty, ruled until Roman conquest in **168 BCE**.

## Founding Conditions

CAH VII.1 (Will, ch. 4) traces the difficult birth of Antigonid Macedon. After
[[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]] (301) the family held only a fleet and scattered bases;
Demetrius seized Macedon in 294 but lost it in 288 to [[lysimachus]] and
[[pyrrhus-of-epirus|Pyrrhus]] and died a Seleucid captive (283). The kingdom then
passed through the apogee and collapse of Lysimachus ([[battle-of-corupedium-281|Corupedium]],
281), the murderous reign of [[ptolemy-ceraunus|Ptolemy Ceraunus]], and the
**[[celtic-invasion-of-greece-279|Celtic invasion]]** that left Macedon kingless and
ravaged. Gonatas earned the throne by **destroying a Gaulish army at Lysimacheia
(277)** — presenting himself not as a discredited pretender but as a true *Soter*
("Saviour"). By 276 he was master of Macedon and Thessaly, and "the pattern of the
great Hellenistic kingdoms was finally fixed."

## Structural Features

- **A "national" monarchy?** A long scholarly tradition held the Antigonid kingship
  uniquely limited — *primus inter pares*, constrained by the Macedonian army-assembly.
  [[hellenistic-monarchy|Walbank rejects this]]: Macedon differed only in nuance — a
  closer king–people bond, the occasional title "king of the Macedonians," more
  treatises *On Kingship* solicited, and notably **no official dynastic cult** — but
  was not constitutionally distinct from the other monarchies.
- **The "Fetters of Greece."** Antigonid power over Greece rested on garrisons in the
  key fortresses — **Corinth (the Acrocorinth), Chalcis, Demetrias** — the "fetters"
  that recur throughout the dynasty's history, plus (at times) the Piraeus.
- **A smaller, poorer kingdom** than Ptolemaic Egypt or the Seleucid east, but with a
  cohesive Macedonian national army and a strategic position controlling Greece.

## How They Shaped Events

The Antigonids were the perpetual counterweight in mainland Greece: opposed by the
rising federal leagues (the [[aetolian-league|Aetolian]] and [[achaean-league|Achaean]]
confederations) and by Ptolemaic money and sea-power. Major episodes treated in this
volume include the [[chremonidean-war-267|Chremonidean War]] (Gonatas vs. an
Athenian–Spartan–Ptolemaic coalition); under **[[antigonus-iii-doson|Antigonus III Doson]]**,
the recall of Macedon to the Peloponnese (the surrender of the Acrocorinth, 224), the
founding of the **Hellenic Symmachy** (a "League of Leagues"), and the crushing of
[[cleomenes-iii|Cleomenes III]] of Sparta at [[battle-of-sellasia-222|Sellasia]] (222); and
under **[[philip-v|Philip V]]**, the [[social-war-220|Social War]] (220–217) and the Peace
of Naupactus, after which Philip turned west toward Rome (see chs. 7 & 12). The dynasty
ended when Rome defeated Perseus at Pydna (168), abolishing the monarchy.

## Principal Kings

- [[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus I Monophthalmus]] (king 306–301) — founder of
  the royal claim.
- [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius I Poliorcetes]] (king of Macedon 294–288).
- **[[antigonus-ii-gonatas|Antigonus II Gonatas]]** (276–239) — true founder of the
  stable dynasty.
- **Demetrius II** (239–229), **Antigonus III Doson** (229–221), **Philip V** (221–179),
  **Perseus** (179–168) — treated in ch. 12.

## The End of the Dynasty (CAH VIII, Chs 8–9)

The Antigonid kingdom was destroyed by Rome. **[[philip-v|Philip V]]**, defeated at
Cynoscephalae (197) in the [[second-macedonian-war-200-196bce|Second Macedonian War]],
was confined to Macedon and then, after Apamea, steadily humiliated by Roman orders
(stripped of his Thracian gains, his son Demetrius destroyed in a dynastic tragedy).
His son **[[perseus-of-macedon|Perseus]]** revived Macedon's standing in Greece —
which proved fatal, since an alternative arbiter was incompatible with Roman
supremacy. Defeated by [[aemilius-paullus|Aemilius Paullus]] at
**[[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|Pydna]]** (168), Perseus was the last Antigonid
king: the monarchy was abolished and Macedon broken into **four republics**, becoming
a Roman province after Andriscus' revolt (148). See
[[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce]], [[perseus-of-macedon]].

## Manpower, garrisons, and navy (CHGRW)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] sharpens the
military base. Macedon alone among Hellenistic kingdoms retained a **pre-existing manpower
base**: gymnasium training, territorial + age-class mobilization, military obligation to age
55; demographic impact of Alexander’s casualties/settlement abroad remains disputed (Sekunda
I.11A). From 276 the Antigonids ruled a manpower-weakened Macedonia still able to field
~20,000 levy but forced toward mercenaries; navy cut back. Serrati (I.14): destruction by
Rome was **not** from lack of men or money but Roman military superiority. Billows (I.10):
**Antigonus Gonatas** eschewed goodwill strategy in southern Greece, installing partisan
tyrants and garrisons, earning ill-will but holding key positions against Ptolemaic-encouraged
disaffection. Dynastic intermarriage created shared *progonoi* claims: Antiochus III in 219
cited Antigonus Monophthalmus among ancestors justifying claim to Syria/Palestine (Polyb.
5.67); mid–late second-century Seleucids used the Antigonid name Demetrius. Late-fourth-
century size race led by Antigonus Monophthalmus: by 315/14 ~240 warships in Phoenicia
including 210 *cataphracts* (97 triremes, 90 fours, 10 fives, 3 nines, 10 tens); Demetrius at
Cypriot Salamis 306 fielded mixed threes–sevens; later elevens–sixteens; Philip V barred from
*cataphracts* except the sixteen after 197 (Polyb. 18.44.6). See [[hellenistic-warfare]].
