# The Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization

The **Indus Valley Civilization** (the **Harappan culture**, c. 2500–1500 BCE) was the first
urban civilization of the Indian subcontinent and, by area, the **most extensive of the ancient
Bronze Age civilizations** — spanning the Indus plain (Punjab and Sind), northern Rajasthan, and
Kathiawar/Gujarat. Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## The cities

Harappan civilization was "essentially a city culture," with its great centres at **Mohenjo-daro**
and **Harappa** (and, in later-discovered sites, Kot Diji, Kalibangan, Rupar, and the port-town of
**Lothal**). The cities show an "advanced sense of civic planning" — each divided into a raised
**citadel** (housing the civic and religious institutions) and a residential lower town, with grid
streets, drainage, and elaborately constructed **granaries** that imply control over an agricultural
surplus. Its wealth rested on that surplus plus a flourishing trade, both within the subcontinent and
with **Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf**.

## The seals and the script

The most distinctive — and tantalising — Harappan remains are the ~2,000 **seals**: small flat
objects bearing animal or human motifs and an **undeciphered script**. Thapar reads them as merchants'
tokens or markers for goods brought into the cities; their writing "holds promise of interesting
information when it can be finally read." This undeciphered script is the central reason the
civilization's language, polity, and beliefs remain conjectural.

## Decline and legacy

By ~1500 BCE the Harappan culture had **declined** — in Thapar's 1966 account, "the intrusion of
less civilized peoples" broke political continuity, roughly coinciding with the
[[indo-european-origins|Indo-Aryan migration]] from Iran (whose Painted-Grey Ware sites are sometimes
found *above* the older levels). She stresses there was **no political continuity** between Harappa
and the later Aryan culture — "from some points of view the coming of the Aryans was a backward step,"
since northern India had to **re-evolve urbanism** from agrarian and nomadic systems. Yet a cultural
legacy survived: Harappan **fertility worship** — the Mother Goddess, the Bull, a horned deity, sacred
trees — Thapar argues "continued in Hindu worship," one strand of the pre-Aryan/Aryan synthesis that
became [[hinduism|Hinduism]].

## Historiography

Known **entirely through archaeology** and an unread script, the Indus civilization is the prime case
of the limits of reconstruction without decipherable text. Thapar's 1966 datings (mature phase c.
2500 BCE, decline c. 1500 BCE) and her "intrusion/invasion" framing of the decline have since been
substantially revised by later scholarship (gradual decline, climatic and hydrological causes, no
violent "Aryan" destruction) — see [[indo-european-origins]]. The deeper antiquity and continuity of
South Asian urbanism is an active field; this page will be refined as the Cambridge History of India
and other sources are ingested.

## Phases of the Harappan Civilisation (Avari 2007)

Avari identifies three broad phases, with Mehrgarh's earliest strata (c. 7000 BCE) as the ultimate root:

- **Baluchistan Phase (c. 7000–4500 BCE):** Neolithic farming settlements at [[mehrgarh]] and linked sites; indigenous development of agriculture, pottery and craft.
- **Age of Transition (c. 4500–3500 BCE):** Progressive development of proto-urban features across Baluchistan, Sind, Punjab, and the Ghaggar–Hakra valley.
- **Early Harappan / Era of Regionalisation (c. 3500–2600 BCE):** Proto-urban settlements at Amri, Kot Diji, Kalibangan, and Mehrgarh; emergence of urban planning features (fortified citadels, standardised bricks, wheel-turned pottery).
- **Mature Harappan (c. 2600–1900 BCE):** The specifically urban phase; five first-tier cities (Harappa, Mohenjo Daro, Dholavira, Rakhigari, Ganveriwala), each covering 80–100 hectares; standardised weights and measures, undeciphered script, long-distance trade.
- **Late/Post-Harappan (c. 1900–1700 BCE and after):** Gradual decline and dispersal into regional post-Harappan cultures.

## Urban Planning and Major Structures

Harappan settlements were organised in a consistent pattern: a raised citadel mound in the west containing public buildings, and a lower town in the east with residential blocks on a grid-street plan. Baked kiln bricks were standardised at a ratio of 1:2:4 (thickness:width:length). Both Harappa and Mohenjo Daro covered approximately 100 hectares each. The Great Bath at Mohenjo Daro — unique in the ancient world — was approximately 39 feet long by 23 feet broad, sunk 8 feet below the courtyard, and waterproofed with bitumen.

Harappa is distinctive in lacking a citadel mound but possessing a unique set of circular brick platforms, possibly for threshing grain. Dholavira was built of stone rather than brick and uniquely contained three sectors (castle and bailey, middle, and lower town) with elaborate gateways, stairs, corridors and guard chambers.

## Standardised Weights and Mathematics

The Harappans possessed a highly standardised system of cubical stone weights using a base unit (the "gunja" seed = 0.109 gm) in ratios of 1:2:4:8:16:32:64 and higher series. A standard weight of 27.2 gm (uncia) with multiples of 5 uncia (136 gm) and fractions as fine as one-sixteenth uncia (1.7 gm) suggest a decimal system. The "Indus inch" (1.32 in, equal to two Sumerian shushi) found on a shell ruler at Mohenjo Daro indicates commercial and computational exchange with Mesopotamia.

## Trade Networks

The Harappan economic zone was largely self-sufficient but maintained long-distance trade with Afghanistan, Turkmenistan, Iran, Mesopotamia and Oman. The only Mature Harappan site outside the subcontinent was at Shortugai (Afghanistan), used as a transit point for lapis lazuli and tin. A Mesopotamian text from King Sargon of Akkad refers to boats from "Meluhha" — now identified as the Indus valley — bringing cargo to Sumer. Exports included textiles, beads, bangles, hard woods, pearls, ivory, exotic animals and utility artefacts; imports included copper from Oman, shells, dates, honey and incense.

## Social Organisation

Whether the settlements collectively formed an empire, perhaps run from Harappa or Mohenjo Daro, remains debated. Avari concludes that "in the absence of substantial archaeological evidence of a central political authority, one can only conclude that there was a great deal of cultural but not political uniformity." No palaces or rich tombs have been found. The most probable elite was "a coalition of rich merchants, priests and bureaucrats." The unicorn appears on the largest number of seals and may have been the power symbol of a dominant clan.

## The Script

The [[harappan-script]] remains undeciphered. Nearly 4,200 inscribed objects exist; the script contains 419 signs and is mostly logo-syllabic. The average inscription is five signs; the longest is twenty-six. Direction is right to left (boustrophedon on second lines). Professors Mahadevan, Parpola, and Fairservis Jr. have all failed to crack it. No bilingual text has been found.

## Religion and Proto-Hinduism

Harappan religion cannot be established with certainty but shows significant resonances with later Hinduism: the Great Bath suggests ritual purification; a male figure seated in padmasana (yoga posture) with horned headdress appears on seals; pipal and banyan tree symbols, fertility goddess figurines, swastikas, and endless-knot motifs are attested. Avari notes that "some of the features of the Harappan Culture can be detected within Vedic Culture," but the former "was essentially pre-Vedic and non-Vedic, but indigenous" — not the same as Vedic Hinduism, as some modern Hindu nationalists claim.

## Collapse

See [[harappan-collapse-1900bce]] for full analysis. The current consensus is a multi-causal collapse involving tectonic disturbance (shifting river courses, flooding), rainfall decline, deforestation from brick-kiln charcoal burning, and socio-economic cascade. The Wheeler "Aryan invasion" explanation has been largely rejected. After the urban collapse, post-Harappan regional cultures continued at village level and eventually came into contact with Aryan migrants entering India from c. 1700 BCE.
