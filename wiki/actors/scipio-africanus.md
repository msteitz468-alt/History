# Scipio Africanus

The Roman commander who conquered Punic Spain and defeated [[hannibal]] at
[[battle-of-zama-202bce|Zama]], ending the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second
Punic War]]. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 3 (Briscoe).

## Role and career

**P. Cornelius Scipio**, son and nephew of the two Scipios killed in Spain in 211,
was elected by the *comitia* to an extraordinary Spanish command as a *privatus cum
imperio* (210), still in his mid-twenties. He stormed **[[new-carthage|New
Carthage]]** in a single day (209) by exploiting the ebbing lagoon — an exploit
folded into the "Scipionic legend" of divine favour (the dream of Neptune). He won
at **Baecula** (208) and decisively at **Ilipa** (206), driving Carthage out of
Spain, and opened relations with the Numidian princes **Syphax** and
**[[masinissa]]**.

As consul (205) he forced through, against [[fabius-maximus|Fabius]] and Fulvius
Flaccus, the strategy of **invading Africa**. He burned the camps of Syphax and
Hasdrubal Gisgo, won the **Great Plains**, secured Masinissa's defection, and
defeated the recalled Hannibal at **[[battle-of-zama-202bce|Zama]]** (202),
dictating the peace of 201.

Later he and his brother **Lucius** ("Asiaticus") commanded against
[[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III]], the war settled at Magnesia (190) and
Apamea (188). Political enemies (the "trials of the Scipios") drove him into
embittered retirement; he died at Liternum in 183, the same year as Hannibal.

## Counterfactual significance

Scipio is the structural answer to Hannibal: where Hannibal could not break the
Roman system, Scipio adapted Carthaginian methods (cavalry, manoeuvre, the
reversal of Cannae at Zama) within a state whose manpower and alliances let it
absorb defeat. CAH VIII's factional analysis makes him the head of the
"**Scipionic group**" whose "forward strategy" — fighting in Spain and Africa
rather than waiting in Italy — won the war and, in victory, accelerated the
personalisation of Roman command prestige that later troubled the Republic.

## Historiography

[[polybius]], close to the family through **Scipio Aemilianus** (the adoptive
grandson), is the prime and sympathetic source; his Scipionic bias colours the
treatment of Cannae (absolving Paullus), Zama, and the legend. The factional
reading of war-time politics (Scullard) centres on the Scipios and is contested as
prosopographical inference. See [[second-punic-war-218-201bce]].

Mary Beard ([[sources/beard-spqr-2015]]) notes that Scipio and his family
(Aemilianus in particular) were not merely military men but patrons of Rome's
earliest Latin literature (Ennius, ties to Terence) — framing the beginning of
Roman overseas empire and the beginning of Roman literature as "two sides of the
same coin."
