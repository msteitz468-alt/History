# Gabriele D'Annunzio (1863–1938)

Italian poet, aesthete (long resident in Paris, associated with Marinetti's
futurist milieu), war hero, and *Comandante* of the
[[fiume-expedition-1919|Fiume expedition]] (September 1919 – December 1920).
At Fiume he promulgated the **Carta del Carnaro** (28 September 1920) —
drafted by the national syndicalist [[de-ambris-alceste|Alceste De Ambris]],
whom he appointed secretary for civil affairs, with D'Annunzio adding his
"philosophic-aesthetic interpretation of life" — a corporatist-productionist
constitution in which only producers were full citizens, "claimed by many to
be one of the main prefigurations of Fascist corporatism." His political
theatre — balcony dialogues with the crowd, Roman salutes, legionary ritual —
supplied [[generic-fascism|fascism]]'s liturgy before Mussolini, who showed
"lack of enthusiasm" for Fiume, advised D'Annunzio (via De Ambris's own
hands) *not* to march on Rome, and abandoned the Regency for the Treaty of
Rapallo. Giolitti ended the regency by force in December 1920.

## Griffin (2007): D'Annunzio as Modernist Propheta

*Modernism and Fascism* charts D'Annunzio's transformation from the decadentism of *Il Piacere* (1889), via Nietzsche, into Dionysian vitalist of national rebirth (*La vergine della roccia*, *Il fuoco*); his 1907 Carducci funeral oration is quoted as pure palingenetic expectancy ("Here is the new world... the whole of the world is being stretched like a bow ready to release its arrow"). The Quarto speech (5 May 1915, "Here a Greater Italy is being born") galvanized intervention; at Fiume he ruled a "state [that] was now a 'total work of art'," adopting De Ambris's syndicalist corporatism and dedicating a corporation to "il nuovissimo uomo" — though "it was Fascism, not 'Dannunzianism', that won the day for 'combattentismo'." Griffin also places him among the fin-de-siècle "Healer" cult figures (with Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Ibsen, Goethe). (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)
