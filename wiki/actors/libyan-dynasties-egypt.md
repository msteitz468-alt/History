# The Libyan Dynasties of Egypt (22nd–24th, Third Intermediate Period)

The **Libyan dynasties** ruled Egypt through the middle and later **Third Intermediate
Period** (c. 945–715 BCE) — a "weak" Egypt of decentralized power that nonetheless,
under its founder **Shoshenq I**, briefly reasserted itself abroad (the biblical
**Shishak** of the [[shoshenq-campaign-925bce|925 BCE Palestine campaign]]) before
fragmenting into rival lines and falling to the Kushites. [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH
Vol. III, Part 1]] (Ch. 13, I. E. S. Edwards) traces the Twenty-second to Twenty-fourth
Dynasties.

## The Rise of Libyan Supremacy

The Libyan presence in Egypt grew out of **Ramesside-era policy**: Mashwesh (Ma) and
Libu tribesmen, taken as prisoners or hired as mercenaries from Sethos I and Ramesses
II through Ramesses III, were settled in the **Delta** and paid in land. Over the
Twenty-first Dynasty these military settlers — keeping their own chiefs and militias —
accumulated large territorial holdings and formed self-governing communities. Thebes
in Upper Egypt watched warily (the fortified wall and watch-tower at El-Hiba guarded
the southern approach), but the transition was managed peacefully: by the reign of
**Psusennes II**, the **Great Chief of the Ma, Shoshenq**, was powerful enough to
secure royal favours at Thebes (the Abydos stela of his father Nimlot's mortuary cult)
and was positioned to inherit the throne.

## Shoshenq I and the Twenty-second Dynasty

**Shoshenq I** founded the **Twenty-second ("Bubastite") Dynasty** around 945 BCE,
ruling from the eastern Delta (Bubastis and Tanis). He briefly **re-centralized**
Egypt: he installed his own sons in the great offices — one as **High Priest of Amun
at Thebes**, another (Nimlot) as governor of Herakleopolis — binding the dangerous
Theban priesthood to the crown by family ties. On this restored footing he campaigned
abroad, the **[[shoshenq-campaign-925bce|invasion of Palestine in 925 BCE]]** recorded
both at Karnak and in the book of Kings — the high-water mark of Libyan Egypt's
external power. His successors **Osorkon I, Osorkon II, Takeloth, Shoshenq III** (the
royal burials excavated at Tanis) maintained the dynasty for two centuries but could
not preserve its unity.

## Fragmentation

The Libyan polity carried within it the seeds of disintegration: a **decentralized,
quasi-feudal structure** of hereditary military chiefdoms ill-suited to unified rule.
The strategy of placing royal sons in regional priesthoods and governorships, meant to
secure control, instead created **rival hereditary power centres**. By the later
eighth century central authority had splintered: a parallel **Twenty-third Dynasty**
ruled alongside the Twenty-second, and beyond them a patchwork of local "great chiefs"
and kinglets divided the Delta and Middle Egypt. Into this fragmentation rose
**Tefnakht of Sais** (founder of the **Twenty-fourth Dynasty**, with his successor
Bakenranef/**Bocchoris**), attempting to reunify the north — just as the **Kushite**
kingdom of **Napata** pressed up from the south. The Nubian king **Piankhy (Piye)**
swept north and subdued Egypt, ending Libyan rule and inaugurating the Kushite
Twenty-fifth Dynasty (narrated in CAH III.2).

## Significance

- `produced:` the [[shoshenq-campaign-925bce|Shoshenq I campaign]] that stripped
  Jerusalem's Temple and left a destruction horizon across Palestine — Libyan Egypt's
  one moment of regional power.
- `enabled:` (by its weakness) the **independence of the Levantine states** —
  [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]], [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]], the
  [[phoenicians|Phoenician]] and [[neo-hittite-states|Syro-Hittite]] cities — through
  the period, since Egypt was usually "normally inactive" toward Asia.
- `caused_by:` the long Ramesside settlement of Libyan mercenaries in the Delta; its
  decentralization in turn `produced:` the fragmentation that opened Egypt to the
  Kushite conquest.

## Shaw (2000): a Libyan chiefdom-federation

John Taylor's chapter in the [[sources/shaw-oxford-history-ancient-egypt-2000|Oxford History]]
reframes the [[processes/third-intermediate-period|period]] not as mere "decline" but as the
imposition of a **semi-nomadic political logic** on Egypt — "a federation of semi-autonomous
rulers, nominally subject (and often related) to an overlord-king." Tellingly, the Libyans
"had adopted the trappings of Egyptian kingship without fully understanding it," tolerating
"two or more 'kings' simultaneously, each entitled 'king of Upper and Lower Egypt.'" Their
distinct identity persisted as it never had for earlier immigrants: Libyan **names** (Osorkon,
Sheshonq, Takelot) endured for centuries, chiefly titles were retained, "a feather worn in the
hair survived as a distinguishing mark," and long oral **genealogies** — rare in Egyptian
texts — became a hallmark. The founder [[actors/sheshonq-i|Shoshenq/Sheshonq I]] and his line
ruled from the Delta ([[places/tanis|Tanis]], Bubastis), and the system fragmented precisely
because kings kept appointing their own sons to the Theban priesthood and provincial offices.
Power eventually passed to the Kushite [[actors/piye|Piye]].

## Historiography

Edwards reconstructs the period from a **difficult, fragmentary record** — royal
genealogies pieced from stelae, temple inscriptions, the Tanis burials, and Manetho's
king-list — within the loose "**Third Intermediate Period**" framework (Twenty-first
to Twenty-fifth Dynasties). Reign-lengths and even the sequence of identically-named
kings (multiple Osorkons, Shoshenqs, Takeloths) are uncertain, and the chronology has
remained a specialist battleground (the foundational synthesis being Kitchen's). The
collection is strong on the Egyptological monumental record; the broader narrative of
decline-through-decentralization is secure even where individual dates are not.
