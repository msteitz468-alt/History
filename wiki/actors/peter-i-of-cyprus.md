# Peter I of Cyprus

**Peter I** (Pierre I de Lusignan), king of Cyprus (1359–69) and the most aggressive
crusading monarch of the later fourteenth-century Latin East. His western tour, the
**[[sack-of-alexandria-1365|sack of Alexandria (1365)]]**, and subsequent raids made Cyprus
temporarily the initiator rather than the client of crusading policy — until his murder by
vassals on 16 January 1369 opened the crisis that culminated in the
**[[genoese-invasion-of-cyprus-1373|Genoese invasion of 1373–74]]**.

## Path to the throne

Born 9 October 1329 (Alice of Ibelin), son of Hugh IV. Created count of Tripoli mid-1340s.
In 1349 he and brother John secretly left for the West against Hugh's will; recovered and
imprisoned in Kyrenia. Founded the Order of the Sword (per Machaut) before accession. Hugh
had him co-crowned king of Cyprus on **24 November 1358** to pre-empt grandson Hugh of
Lusignan (son of deceased first-born Guy). Hugh IV died 10 October 1359; Peter was crowned
king of Jerusalem at Famagusta by legate Peter Thomas on Easter 5 April 1360. A pension
settlement (50,000 bezants; later prince of Galilee) neutralized the pretender.

## Anatolian policy and western tour

August 1361: stormed Satalia (Antalya) with mainly Cypriot forces — a "second Smyrna"
under sole Cypriot credit. Took Gorhigos under protection (1360). October 1362–August 1365:
western tour with Peter Thomas and Philip of Mézières (Venice, Genoa, Avignon, France,
England, Germany, Bohemia, Poland). After John II of France's death (1364) crusade headship
effectively passed to Peter. Western recruitment was meagre; Mézières reports Peter Thomas
comforting the downcast king.

## Alexandria and the Mamluk war

October 1365: sack of Alexandria (see event page). Peter wanted to hold the city; westerners
and (per Mézières) Hospitallers and his brothers insisted on withdrawal (16 October). War
continued to 1370 with raids (Tripoli, Tortosa, Ayas) and failed peace drafts whose commercial
clauses reveal, for Edbury, Peter's real aim: preferential facilities for Cypriot merchants
in Mamluk ports, not recovery of Jerusalem. The gamble failed and accelerated commercial
decline.

## Murder (16 January 1369)

After an empty-handed second western trip (1367–68), chroniclers describe erratic conduct:
mistresses, quarrels over greyhounds, imprisonment of vassals, foreign favourites with
independent force. Early hours of **16 January 1369**: princes John of Antioch and James
with knights released the Jubails; Philip of Ibelin of Arsur, Henry of Jubail and John of
Gaurelle struck Peter down. Western writers hold the brothers complicit; Cypriot chroniclers
disassociate them — Edbury finds circumstantial evidence hard to clear. Same-day ordonnance
constrained war, recruitment (>100 men-at-arms), and taxation without vassal assent.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Peter's crusade, Famagusta's mid-century commercial crisis might have been managed
without Mamluk war and without the fiscal–feudal explosion that made the Genoese invasion
possible. With him, Cyprus briefly set the crusading agenda — and then lost the capacity to
set any eastern agenda at all.

## Historiography

Mézières and Machaut are panegyrical; Makhairas is fuller on internal politics; Edbury
strips Holy Land rhetoric to expose commercial strategy
