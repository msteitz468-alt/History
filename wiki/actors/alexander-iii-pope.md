# Pope Alexander III (Roland Bandinelli)

> **Naming note:** The slug `[[alexander-iii]]` in this wiki is the **Russian Tsar Alexander III** (r. 1881–94). This page is **Pope Alexander III** (Roland Bandinelli, r. 1159–81). Link the pope as `[[alexander-iii-pope]]`.

**Alexander III** (r. 1159–81), the former legate **Rolando Bandinelli**, was the long-reigning reform-era pope who survived a **~20-year schism** with imperial antipopes, allied with the **Lombard League** against **[[frederick-i-barbarossa|Frederick Barbarossa]]**, reformed papal elections at the **Third Lateran Council (1179)**, and condemned Henry II’s **[[constitutions-of-clarendon-1164|Constitutions of Clarendon]]** while managing the **[[thomas-becket|Becket]]** crisis. [[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] places him at the centre of twelfth-century “politics rather than religion” in pope–emperor Italy contests, while still treating his electoral reform as a lasting institutional achievement.

## Role and Career

### Election and schism

After **Hadrian IV**’s death (1 Sept. 1159), Bandinelli’s election as Alexander III against the imperial candidate **Victor IV** opened roughly **twenty years** of schism. Alexander spent much of the pontificate in **France**, needing the goodwill of both the **Angevin** and **French** kings.

### Lombard League, Legnano, Venice

Alexander encouraged the **Lombard League**; the imperial army was **routed at Legnano**; peace with the pope followed in **July 1177** (Venice). Logan ranks Legnano among the major battles of medieval history for its political effect on the Hohenstaufen–papal struggle.

### Third Lateran Council (1179)

The council’s chief long-term act was electoral reform: each cardinal an **equal vote** and a **two-thirds majority** required — stabilising elections for about **200 years** (with a brief 1328 hiccup). The council also forbade **tournaments** and denied Christian burial to those dying in them.

### Becket and Clarendon

Alexander **refused** Henry II’s request to depose Becket, **condemned the Constitutions of Clarendon**, and **released Becket from his oath**. His anti-pope crisis and dual need to woo Angevin and Capetian courts help explain the length of Becket’s exile.

### Afterlife of the Hohenstaufen problem

Frederick drowned on the **Third Crusade** (1190); the **1186** marriage of Henry VI to Constance tied empire to Sicily against papal interest — Logan’s coda to the twelfth-century papal–imperial story.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Alexander’s endurance of schism and the Legnano settlement, the twelfth-century [[papal-monarchy]] might have fractured under Barbarossa; without Third Lateran’s two-thirds rule, papal elections would have remained more vulnerable to minority coups. On Becket, a pope more dependent on Henry alone might have abandoned the archbishop.

## Historiography

Logan judges twelfth-century pope–emperor disputes as **political** (control of Italy), with religion little involved — papal defence would claim territorial independence as necessary for the sacred mission. True religious change, for him, lay beyond Rome in **orders and devotion**. Specialist literature (Morris *Papal Monarchy*; NCMH Hohenstaufen chapters) elaborates the legal and diplomatic detail.
