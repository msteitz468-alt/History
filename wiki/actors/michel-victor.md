# Victor Michel

French general; Vice President of the Superior Council of War (appointed 1910). Ousted in July 1911 by Minister of War Adolphe Messimy amid the Agadir crisis; his proposed strategy and army reorganization were rejected, clearing the way for Joseph Joffre's appointment and the centralization of High Command powers.

## Views and Proposed Reforms

Michel had reservations about Plan XVI and greater concerns than his predecessor (de Lacroix) about a possible German advance through Belgium. He foresaw the Germans seeking a "definitive solution in the very heart of Belgium" (concentrating in the north due to French defenses in Lorraine, obstacles in eastern Belgium, and rail networks). 

His alternative strategy (submitted before Messimy took office):
- Defend on the right from Belfort to Mézières.
- Launch a "vigorous offensive" on the left toward Antwerp, Brussels, and Namur.

To enable this across a vast front, Michel proposed reorganizing the army via the "demi-brigade" (reviving a Revolutionary Wars formation): pair each active regiment with a reserve regiment to double the size of brigades, divisions, and corps, fully integrating reserves.

He presented these ideas to the Superior Council of War on July 19, 1911. The council rejected the demi-brigade reorganization, making his strategic shift impossible. An undersecretary in the Ministry of War referred to him as a "looney."

Michel also publicly criticized the offensive à outrance as articulated by Lt. Col. Louis Loyseau de Grandmaison. Messimy and senior generals shared faith in the offensive; this difference contributed to the loss of confidence in Michel ("incapable of commanding"; "a national danger").

## Ouster and Replacement

Messimy met with Michel and informed him that colleagues on the Superior Council lacked confidence. Michel countered that Messimy had not yet attended a council meeting. Messimy agreed to let Michel present his plan but the rejection sealed his fate. Michel resigned two days after the July 19 presentation.

Messimy's replacement choices bypassed Michel's allies; Joseph Gallieni (first choice) refused, leading to Joffre.

## Significance

Michel represented a more cautious, reserve-heavy approach and skepticism toward the extreme offensive doctrine. His removal removed the last major internal check on the "offensive à outrance" and enabled the unification of High Command authority under Joffre (ending duality between chief of staff and vice president/generalissimo). The episode illustrated tensions between political control, republican distrust of a powerful military hierarchy, and the urgent need for effective command preparation revealed by the 1911 crisis.
