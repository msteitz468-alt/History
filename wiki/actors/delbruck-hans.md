Hans Delbrück (1848–1929) was a German military historian whose reading of
[[clausewitz-carl-von|Clausewitz]] produced the most influential modern framework
for sorting [[strategy|military strategy]] into two basic forms. Writing "at the
start of the twentieth century," he argued, in Freedman's words, "that all
military strategy could be divided into two basic forms" — *Niederwerfungsstrategie*,
the strategy of annihilation, and *Ermattungsstrategie*, the strategy of exhaustion.
This pairing is treated at length on the concept page
[[annihilation-and-exhaustion|Annihilation and Exhaustion]]; this page covers the
man and his method.

## Strategic Thought

Delbrück's distinction rested on a close reading of Clausewitz — specifically the
1827 note. The first form, annihilation, "conform[ed] to the majority view of the
day" and "demanded a decisive battle to eliminate the enemy's army." The second
"drew on Clausewitz's note of 1827 which recognized the possibility for another
type of war when the available military means could not deliver a decisive battle."
Where annihilation had "just one pole, the battle," exhaustion "had another pole,
involving a variety of ways to achieve the political ends of war, including
occupying territory, destroying crops, and blockading."

Crucially, Delbrück denied that exhaustion was merely the resort of the weak. As
Freedman records, "Delbrück did not intend to imply that the strongest was bound
to be attracted by annihilation whereas the weak were fated to do what they could
through exhaustion. Exhaustion was not about a single decisive battle but about an
extended campaign that would wear the enemy out." He insisted battle was never
wholly avoidable, mocking "the idea of a 'pure maneuver strategy that allows war
to be conducted without bloodshed.'" His exhaustion was "more operational than an
anticipation of the later concept of attritional war," which placed more weight on
"underlying economic, industrial, and demographic factors."

Delbrück's critical method led him into a famous fight with the official
historians of the German general staff. Per Freedman, "Delbrück's analysis led
him into furious arguments with the historians of the German general staff,
especially when Delbrück argued that Frederick the Great had practiced limited war
rather than decisive battle." Freedman judges "the history was on his side, in
that Frederick had become wary of battle and careful in his ambition," while
conceding "there was still a problem with the dichotomous presentation of complex
options." The general staff could not abide the suggestion that anything other
than annihilation counted as real strategy; "the challenge for Delbrück at this
time … was to get German generals to contemplate anything other than a swift
offensive leading to the annihilation of the enemy army in a decisive battle."

## In Freedman's argument

Delbrück functions in Freedman's narrative as the diagnostician of the German
general staff's fatal monomania. The staff's "successors' determination to avoid
exhaustion" — "they could not bring themselves to prepare for an inevitable
stalemate" — drove the Schlieffen design and the offensive of August 1914, a
strategy of annihilation with "neither the appetite nor the resources for a
prolonged war of exhaustion." Delbrück's framework named exactly the alternative
they refused to consider.

His later trajectory confirms the point. When the August 1914 offensive stalled,
Delbrück revised his thinking in real time: "Delbrück had applauded the initial
offensive and thought it would succeed, but once it stalled he quickly revised his
thinking. If Germany could not annihilate it would have to exhaust the enemy,
although Delbrück struggled to assess the relative economic impacts on the
belligerents." He "argued for a deal with Britain and France in order to
concentrate on Russia," and despaired at the leadership's intransigence. In 1917
he wrote that Germany had "in a sense the whole world leagued against us," adding
that "fear of German despotism is one of the weightiest facts with which we have
to reckon, one of the strongest factors in the enemy's power." Freedman thus casts
Delbrück as the realist whose two-pole scheme exposed the recklessness of the
single-pole faith in the decisive battle.

## Primary Text — History of the Art of War, Vol. IV: The Dawn of Modern Warfare (1985 trans.)
Delbrück's final volume traces the rebirth of disciplined infantry in the Renaissance (Swiss squares → Spanish and German imitators, firearms integration), the refinement of linear and volley tactics under Maurice of Nassau, the full realization of combined-arms mobile warfare under Gustavus Adolphus, the institutionalization of permanent standing armies (France under Le Tellier/Louvois, Brandenburg-Prussia), the tactical and strategic debates of the eighteenth century, and the revolutionary shift to national armies and Napoleonic strategy (with the Prussian response led by Scharnhorst, Gneisenau, and Clausewitz).

Key themes carried forward from earlier volumes:
- Military organization as an expression of political constitution ("within the framework of political history").
- The tension between annihilation (Niederwerfungsstrategie) and exhaustion/attrition (Ermattungsstrategie).
- Frederick the Great as a practitioner of limited war and maneuver rather than pure decisive battle (a position that set Delbrück against the Prussian General Staff historians).
