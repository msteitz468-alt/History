# The Fujiwara Clan

The aristocratic family that **dominated the [[heian-period|Heian]] court for over two
centuries** by governing through puppet emperors — the supreme example of the
**familism** that, in Mason and Caiger's reading, eclipsed the centralized imperial state
in [[japan|Japan]] without ever abolishing it.

## Origins

The Fujiwara descended from **Nakatomi no Kamatari** (614–669), the noble who, with
Prince Naka no Ōe, engineered the 645 coup against the Soga and drove the
[[taika-reform-645|Taika Reform]]; his service earned his line the name Fujiwara. Through
the Nara and early Heian periods they were prominent bureaucrats and advisers.

## The regency (850–1068)

After ~850 the Fujiwara converted **marriage politics** into near-dictatorial power:
they made their daughters the mothers of emperors, then persuaded sovereigns to abdicate
in favor of Fujiwara-blooded children for whom the Fujiwara chief served as **regent** —
*sesshō* for a minor, *kampaku* ("spokesman") for an adult. Polygamy and the absence of
strict primogeniture made the system workable. The clan head simultaneously held the
**public** office of regent and the **private** office of clan head (*uji no chōja*),
controlling the family's corporate property — a fusion of state and family power that
typified the age. Their wealth rested on **[[heian-period|shōen]]** estates larger than
any rival's. The apogee came under **Michinaga** (in power 996–1027), whose era was also
the golden age of court literature (Murasaki Shikibu served his daughter, Empress Akiko).

## Patrons of the classical age

The Fujiwara ascendancy coincided with — and underwrote — the **[[heian-court-culture|golden
age of Heian culture]]**. Michinaga's daughters' salons hosted **Murasaki Shikibu** and
**Sei Shōnagon**; the clan's wealth built the supreme monument of the age, the **Byōdōin
Phoenix Hall** (the regent Yorimichi, 1052); and Fujiwara aristocrats were themselves noted
painters and calligraphers (one is held the originator of the *yamato-e* **Tosa** style). The
Fujiwara peace was the soil of the *Genji*.

## Decline

The regency depended on supplying emperors' mothers; it broke in **1068** when
**Go-Sanjō**, born of a non-Fujiwara mother, took the throne determined to rule —
attacking the shōen and provincial appointments. The imperial house then reasserted
itself through **cloistered (retired) emperor** rule (*insei*), catching the Fujiwara in
the very web they had spun: they kept the formal offices but no longer controlled the
puppet. Real power drifted thereafter toward the cloister and, ultimately, the warrior
houses.

## Significance

The Fujiwara are the textbook case of the Japanese pattern whereby the **sacred imperial
institution endured while secular power was captured** by those who married into or served
it — a template repeated by the cloistered emperors and then the shoguns. Their patronage
also sustained the literary and religious efflorescence of the Heian court.
