# Helmuth von Moltke the Younger (1848–1916)

Chief of the German General Staff 1906–1914 (succeeding Schlieffen). Central military figure in the July Crisis; embodied the fatalistic and militarized strand in German decision-making.

## Views and role

Moltke viewed war as endemic in international relations. He spoke in general terms of a coming "struggle between Slav and Teuton" and was "both pessimistic and fatalistic." He had advocated preventive war as early as the 1909 Bosnian crisis exchanges with Conrad. At the 8 December 1912 "war council" he told the Kaiser that "war was inevitable, and that the sooner it came the better for Germany."

In July 1914 he initially stayed on leave. On 30 July he bypassed Bethmann Hollweg to urge Conrad to mobilize against Russia (not Serbia) and assured German support. He resisted early Falkenhayn calls for preliminary steps but by 30 July agreed a decision on general mobilization by noon 31 July. He was clear that the Tsar's equivocation on partial vs general mobilization could make no difference. On 1 August he despaired when a (false) report suggested Britain might keep France neutral, because Germany's plan required attacking west first: redirecting the army east "would not have an army prepared for the attack but a barren heap of armed men disorganised and without supplies."

## War planning context

Moltke abandoned plans for war against Russia alone in April 1913; the army committed to a two-front war. Yet the timing of the Austrian ultimatum was chosen to minimize two-front risk. Moltke's operational focus was not matched by grand-strategic integration with Conrad or political leadership. He "did little to co-ordinate those plans with those of Conrad, and he did not attempt to formulate what we would now call a grand strategy."

## Historiography

Strachan stresses that Moltke's preventive-war advocacy was not Fischer-style deliberate use of foreign policy to resolve domestic crisis, nor a precise operational timetable driving July events from the start. It reflected prevailing social-Darwinist orthodoxy and accustomed civilian leaders to the possibility of war. The clash between Moltke's assumptions and Bethmann's localization efforts illustrated the absence of guiding authority in Wilhelmine Germany.
