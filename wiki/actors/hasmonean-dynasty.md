# The Hasmonean Dynasty

The priestly house, sprung from the **Maccabean revolt**, that turned a small
Judaean temple-state into an **independent, expansionist Jewish kingdom** — and then
tore itself apart in a succession war that handed Judaea to Rome. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 8b (Tessa Rajak).

## From rebels to rulers

The sons of Mattathias — **Judas Maccabaeus**, then **Jonathan**, then **Simon** —
parlayed military leadership against the Seleucids into **permanent dynastic
authority**, exploiting Seleucid dynastic chaos by selling their support to rival
kings in exchange for the **high priesthood** and privileges. The breakthrough came
under **Simon**: in **142** "the yoke of the Gentiles was taken away," and in **140**
a popular decree at Jerusalem made him **high priest, commander, and ethnarch "for
ever, until a trustworthy prophet shall arise"** — the powers now internally
sanctioned, no longer needing Seleucid approval. The decree, inscribed in bronze and
set in the Temple, marked the new state as **visibly Hellenistic in its public
forms**.

## Expansion and monarchy

- **John Hyrcanus I** (135–104): after the death of Antiochus VII Sidetes (129) ended
  the last Seleucid threat, Hyrcanus conquered widely — **Medeba**, **Shechem and the
  Samaritan temple on Mt Gerizim**, and **Idumaea** (whose people he **forcibly
  circumcised** and Judaized). He minted the **first Hasmonean coinage** (aniconic,
  in archaic Hebrew script, styling himself "high priest and the *hever* of the
  Jews").
- **Aristobulus I** (104–103): took the title **king**, conquered the Ituraeans of
  the Galilee (again with conversion).
- **Alexander Jannaeus** (103–76): king and high priest, a relentless warrior who
  overran the **coast** (Gaza), **Transjordan**, and beyond — but provoked a savage
  **civil war** at home (see below).
- **Salome Alexandra** (76–67): Jannaeus' widow, the one peaceful reign; she conciliated
  the **Pharisees**, who came to dominate public life, and enlarged the army.

## Hellenization

Far from anti-Greek, the later Hasmoneans took **Greek names**, hired **foreign
mercenaries**, built **Hellenistic palaces** (Jannaeus' colonnaded pool-palace at
Jericho) and tombs (the seven-pyramid mausoleum at Modiin), and ruled in a
Graeco-oriental idiom — while carefully keeping their **coinage image-free** to avoid
offending the pious. Rajak's theme: the dynasty **hellenized the Jews even as it
judaized the region**.

## The civil war and the fall (67–63)

Jannaeus' brutality (the crushing of a popular/Pharisaic revolt — reportedly 50,000
dead, and **800 opponents crucified** as he feasted) prefigured the dynasty's
self-destruction. After Salome, her sons **[[pompeian-eastern-settlement-63bce|Hyrcanus
II]]** (the weak elder, backed by the Idumaean **Antipater**, father of Herod) and
**Aristobulus II** (the forceful younger) fought a **succession war**. Both **courted
Pompey** — who, arriving in Syria (64), settled it by force: a **three-month siege of
the Temple Mount**, the storming of Jerusalem on the Day of Atonement (63), and his
entry into the **Holy of Holies** (a desecration mourned in the *Psalms of Solomon*).
He installed Hyrcanus as **high priest and ethnarch**, imposed tribute, and stripped
away the Greek cities — ending Judaean independence.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

The Hasmoneans show a successful **war of national liberation hardening into a
Hellenistic monarchy** — and then succumbing to the same dynastic and religious
strains as its neighbours. Rajak argues the **internal sectarian divisions** (and the
princes' fratricide) opened the door to Rome, but that even a united Judaea could
hardly have resisted Pompey's settlement. The dynasty's structures were inherited and
reconstructed by **Herod the Great** under Augustus.

## Historiography

Rajak stresses the **exceptional richness** of the evidence — **1 and 2 Maccabees**,
the **dynastic chronicle behind 1 Maccabees**, and above all **Josephus** (drawing on
**Nicolaus of Damascus**, Strabo), checked against **archaeology**, **coinage**, and
the **Dead Sea Scrolls**. The pro-Hasmonean bias of 1 Maccabees and Josephus' shifting
sources demand caution; the "destruction" of Greek cities is partly **Pompeian
restoration-propaganda**.
