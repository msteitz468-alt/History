# Thomas Becket

**Thomas Becket** (c. 1120–1170), royal chancellor and then Archbishop of Canterbury,
was the central figure in the most famous Church–state confrontation of the twelfth
century. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (editors' Introduction; Keefe, Ch. 18) places
his quarrel with [[henry-ii-of-england|Henry II]] in the long line of disputes — like the
[[investiture-contest|Investiture Contest]] — over the boundary between royal power and
the **liberty of the Church**.

## Career and Conflict

A Londoner of merchant stock, Becket rose as a brilliant administrator to become Henry
II's **chancellor** and close friend, serving the king's interests energetically. When
Henry had him made **Archbishop of Canterbury (1162)**, expecting a pliant primate,
Becket instead became an uncompromising champion of ecclesiastical rights. The breach
came over the **Constitutions of Clarendon (1164)**, by which Henry sought to subject
"criminous clerks" to royal courts and to limit appeals to Rome and the Church's
jurisdictional independence. Becket's resistance drove him into **exile in France
(1164–1170)**, under the protection of Louis VII and (cautiously) Pope Alexander III.

A negotiated return in 1170 quickly collapsed over Becket's excommunication of the
bishops who had crowned Henry's heir. Henry's furious words ("Will no one rid me of this
turbulent priest?") prompted four knights to murder Becket at the altar of **Canterbury
Cathedral on 29 December 1170**.

## Aftermath and Significance

The murder horrified Christendom. Becket was **canonised in 1173**, and his shrine at
Canterbury became one of the greatest pilgrimage sites of medieval Europe (the
destination of Chaucer's pilgrims). Henry was forced to public **penance** and to
abandon the most contentious of the Constitutions, though the crown retained much
practical control over the English Church. Becket became the archetype of the
**martyr for ecclesiastical liberty** against the encroaching power of the centralising
monarchy — a potent symbol in the wider age of [[gregorian-reform|reform]] and the
[[papal-monarchy|papal monarchy]].

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan’s second “twelfth-century profile” supplies denser narrative and legal detail than the NCMH summary above.

### Name, formation, chancellorship

Born on the feast of St Thomas the Apostle (**21 December**, year ~**1120**); both parents Norman-born in post-Conquest England; “Becket” likely a diminutive from *bec* (beak/nose), a boyhood nickname — “a Becket” is a later confection; parents at Cheapside; father a successful merchant in Norman elite circles. Education: Merton Priory from ~10, a city school (possibly St Paul’s); two sources place him at Paris (John of Salisbury’s silence noted); ~three years money-market training after family reverses; ~age 25 entered **Archbishop Theobald**’s household — a cradle of future bishops (four archbishops, six bishops) including John of Salisbury and Vacarius; possible Bologna canon-law sabbatical; minor orders to subdeacon. **1154**: archdeacon of Canterbury and, within weeks, **chancellor** under newly crowned [[henry-ii-of-england|Henry II]] on Theobald’s recommendation. Logan warns “prime minister” is both too modern and too weak — right hand of an autocratic king holding ~half of France via Eleanor of Aquitaine. Chancellor’s magnificence: 1158 Paris embassy of enormous retinue (“What a great man the king of England must be”); 1159 still archdeacon, led 700 knights plus thousands of mercenaries in Aquitaine; intimate friendship (hunting, hawking, chess).

### Archbishop, Clarendon, exile

After Theobald’s death (18 April 1161) — Becket failed to visit the dying benefactor — Henry chose his loyal chancellor over Gilbert Foliot. Consecutive days June **1162**: ordained priest and consecrated bishop; almost at once **resigned the chancellorship**, startling the king. New lifestyle: early prayer, washing feet of the poor, hair shirt, scourging; historians debate conversion of soul vs role-consistent performance; Logan affirms a dramatic change either way.

At **Clarendon** (near Salisbury) January **1164** Henry demanded solemn assent to Henry I’s customs; after reluctant oath under threat Henry ordered customs **written** — a blunder. Sixteen constitutions; six immediately unacceptable (advowson in king’s court; criminous clerks first in king’s court then church then king’s punishment; senior clergy leave kingdom only with leave; no excommunication of tenants-in-chief without leave; no final appeal to pope without consent; royal income from vacant sees). Becket refused to seal; Alexander III later condemned them. On criminous clerks Logan: clash of two jurisdictions each with law and custom, “not one right and another wrong”; Becket echoed current canon-law teaching (clerical crime wholly ecclesiastical; no double jeopardy after degradation).

At **Northampton** October 1164 Henry piled charges including embezzlement already discharged; 13 Oct. Becket appealed to the pope (violating Const. 8), said St Stephen Mass, entered castle bearing processional cross, escaped midnight in Gilbertine habit via rain to Lincoln, then Sandwich, into **six-year exile**. Length owed to characters of king and archbishop, Alexander III’s anti-pope crisis (need to woo both Angevin and French kings), and Louis VII’s sanctuary. Condemnation of Clarendon; release from oath; two years at Cistercian **Pontigny** until Henry threatened English Cistercian lands (left Nov. 1166); then Benedictine **St Columba** outside Sens four more years. Episcopal opposition led by **Gilbert Foliot** (London), Hereford, and **Roger of York** (former colleague and predecessor as archdeacon).

### Return and murder

Failed reconciliations 1169 (*salvo ordine suo*); Palm Sunday 1169 excommunications of Foliot, Jocelin of Salisbury and others; Montmartre nearly settled but Henry refused the kiss of peace. **14 June 1170** young Henry crowned at Westminster by York — violating Canterbury’s reserved right (confirmed 1166). Within six weeks, peace near **Fréteval**: return, property restored, constitutions quietly buried, Becket may punish coronation bishops; Henry still refused kiss of peace. Late Nov. Becket from Wissant sent papal letters re-excommunicating London and Salisbury; landed **Sandwich 1 December 1170** after six years one month; barefoot entry to Canterbury; Christmas sermon reiterated excommunications.

Christmas 1170 at Bures near Bayeux: inflamed by officials and the three bishops; York: “You will never have peace as long as Thomas is archbishop”; king’s words essentially “Will no one of my men rid me of this contemptuous, low-born priest?” Four knights — **Reginald FitzUrse, Hugh de Moreville, William de Tracy, Richard le Breton** — set out intent on murder (later “seize only” claim contradicted by accounts); Henry’s knowledge disputed. **29 December 1170** (Tuesday): confrontation in chamber; pursuit into Canterbury Cathedral during vespers; Becket forbade bolting doors (“the church is not a fortress”); answered “Where is the traitor?” with “I am here, not a traitor to the king but a priest of God”; FitzUrse first blow; le Breton cut off the crown of the head, broke sword on pavement; companion scattered brains; five eyewitnesses.

### Aftermath and memory

Christendom’s revulsion immediate; papal week of mourning and personal interdict on Henry; Sens and French hierarchy interdicted continental lands. At **Avranches 1172** Henry allowed appeals to Rome, restored Canterbury property, promised crusade (never fulfilled), abrogated anti-liberty customs, later exempted clerics from secular courts; privately denied knowing such customs; admitted words may have provoked. **21 February 1173** canonized St Thomas of Canterbury, feast 29 December. July **1174** barefoot penance: 80 monks × three strokes. Shrine thrived until **Henry VIII** destroyed it in the 1530s. Logan situates modern memory via T.S. Eliot’s *Murder in the Cathedral* and Anouilh’s *Becket* (1964 film) and insists the historian sift wheat of truth from chaff of fiction, hagiography, and propaganda. (Celestine III was a friend of Becket as well as a Sens defender of [[peter-abelard|Abelard]].)
