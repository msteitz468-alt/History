# Alfred von Schlieffen

Count Alfred von Schlieffen (1833–1913) was Chief of the German General Staff from 1891 to 1906, the principal architect of German strategic planning for a two-front war against France and Russia in the decades before the First World War.

Confronting Germany's central strategic problem — the 1894 Franco-Russian alliance meant any future war would likely require fighting both powers simultaneously — Schlieffen developed what became known as the [[concepts/schlieffen-plan|Schlieffen Plan]]: a concept for rapidly defeating France with an overwhelming right-wheel offensive through neutral Belgium before Russia's slower mobilization could bring its full weight to bear, after which German forces would be shifted east. The plan existed in various memoranda and staff studies rather than a single fixed operational order, and was significantly modified by his successor [[helmuth-von-moltke-the-younger|Helmuth von Moltke the Younger]], who weakened the decisive right wing before 1914.

Schlieffen retired in 1906 and died in January 1913, before his ideas were tested; the German offensive of August 1914 that bore his name's legacy failed at the Marne, and historians continue to debate how faithfully — or how wisely — Moltke executed Schlieffen's original conception.
