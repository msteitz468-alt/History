# Börte (Borte)

## Overview

**Börte** (Borte Üjin; c. 1161–c. 1220s), of the Onggirat, was the primary wife of [[genghis-khan|Temüjin / Genghis Khan]] and mother of his four recognized sons [[jochi|Jochi]], [[chagatai-khan|Chagatai]], [[ogedei-khan|Ögödei]], and [[tolui|Tolui]]. Yesügei and young Temüjin stayed with her family; the children liked each other and the fathers betrothed them. After Yesügei’s death Temüjin left in haste; in 1178 (age 16) he returned for Börte (then 17–18), who had still waited though nearly past marriage age. Her bride-gift black sable coat—normally for the father-in-law—Temüjin redirected to [[togrul-ong-khan|Ong Khan]] to revive Yesügei’s *anda* bond and claim ceremonial sonship.

The Merkid night raid on the isolated Kherlen *ger* is the formative crisis of Temüjin’s early career: Temüjin fled with companions, mother, and sister, leaving Börte, Sochigel, and an old woman as booty (utilitarian calculation that captives would slow pursuers). Börte was given as wife to an older Merkid warrior. Joint raid by Ong Khan and [[jamuka|Jamuka]] routed the Merkid; Temüjin searched camps calling her name; she jumped from a cart and seized his reins. She was pregnant when rescued; first son Jochi was born 1179 and named “visitor/guest”—the name and Merkid captivity fed a century of paternity politics. Weatherford follows the *Secret History* rescue timeline while noting scholarly debate on implications for legitimacy.

## Role

- **Political counselor.** After ~1.5 years following Jamuka, in mid-May 1181 Börte “interrupted and insisted angrily” that Temüjin break with Jamuka over white-bone/black-bone herd assignments; the night flight began two decades of civil rivalry. Weatherford: she remained the only family member Genghis still heeded during the Teb Tengeri crisis, demanding a break with the shaman as she had once demanded the break with Jamuka—warning what Teb Tengeri would do to sons or widows after the khan died.
- **Senior wife (*qatun*).** Only official wife until aristocratic Tatar wives Yesügen and Yesui; Onggirat kin remained a preferred source of imperial brides. Relatives through Börte deserted Ong Khan for Temüjin after Baljuna.
- **Legal and domestic memory.** Weatherford reports that Genghis’s first new law forbade kidnapping of women, “almost certainly a reaction to the kidnapping of his wife Borte,” while the uncertainty over Jochi’s paternity “would cause increasingly more severe problems as Genghis Khan grew older.”
- **Mother of Ögödei.** Tradition: she treated young Ögödei with water from the Avarga spring to cure disease—the site later of the Yellow Palace warehouses.

## Death and later tradition

Oral tradition (Weatherford): she continued to live at Avarga on the Kherlen and “likely died there, or in the vicinity, sometime between 1219 and 1224” while Genghis was on the western campaigns—earlier than some older popular dates (c. 1230).

## Historiography

Primary: *Secret History*. Popular synthesis: [[weatherford-genghis-khan-making-modern-world-2004|Weatherford 2004]]. Paternity of Jochi remains a standing interpretive problem (factual uncertainty + political weapon among Chinggisids). Persian chroniclers later sanitized or blanked the succession quarrel that grew from that doubt; the *Secret History* preserved it.
