# Elymais

The **Hellenistic-and-Parthian-era successor of [[elam|Elam]]** — a semi-independent
kingdom in highland Khuzistan and the Bakhtiari mountains, c. 147 BCE–224 CE. Its name is
decisive for [[potts-archaeology-of-elam-2016|Potts's]] central thesis: **"Elymais is
nothing but the Graecized form of the more ancient name Elam"** — the same polity that
late Babylonian cuneiform sources still called *Elam* — proving that "Elam and the
Elamites periodically underwent transformation" long after the conventional terminus at
[[sack-of-susa-646bce|646 BCE]]. Older syntheses (Cameron, Hinz, Carter & Stolper) ended
Elamite history with the Assyrians or Achaemenids; Potts insists the Elymaeans are
**latter-day Elamites**.

## Emergence and the temple-state

Independent Elymais became possible only after the Seleucid defeat at Magnesia (190 BCE)
and the death of **Antiochus IV** (164 BCE). The kingdom is famous for its **wealthy
temples**, which tempted bankrupt Seleucid kings to their deaths: **Antiochus III** was
killed in 187 BCE attacking the **temple of Bel in Elymais** (seeking funds for the
Apamea indemnity), and **Antiochus IV** died at Tabae in 164 BCE after the Elymaeans
repelled his raid on a temple of Artemis/Nanaya. Strabo names three provinces (Gabiane,
Massabatene, Korbiane); the warlike **Kossaeans** of the Zagros (possibly Kassite
descendants) were the Elymaeans' mountain neighbours and soldiers. The Elymaean king,
Strabo notes, refused "to be subject to the king of the Parthians like the other tribes."

## Coinage, reliefs and the survival of Elam

The dynasty is known **almost entirely from coinage**. The earliest issues are Seleucid-
style silver tetradrachms of kings named **Kamniskires** (a name compared to the
Achaemenid Elamite title *kapnuskira*, "treasurer") — first attested in Babylonian
astronomical diaries as "Kammashkiri" in 146/145 BCE. Over time **Aramaic legends replace
Greek**, and small denominations carry Aramaic ideograms that may mask an underlying
Iranian or even surviving **Elamite** vernacular — Henning suggested the Khuzistan
lowlands spoke Aramaic while an Iranian-or-Elamite tongue persisted in the highlands.
Elymaean **rock reliefs** (Hung-i Azhdar, Tang-i Sarvak, Bard-e Nechandeh, Masjid-i
Solaiman) consciously **perpetuate the ancient Elamite relief tradition** of Kurangun and
Naqsh-e Rustam, dated by Mathiesen (1992) to four groups c. 250 BCE–225 CE.

## Counterfactual significance

Elymais matters less as a power than as **evidence against the centre–periphery model**:
a region written off as marginal in fact sustained its own kings, temples, art and
language for nearly four centuries between the Seleucids and the [[parthian-empire|Parthians]].
It ended c. 224 CE when **[[ardashir-i|Ardashir]]** of Fars — having defeated an unnamed
"king of Ahwaz," possibly the last Elymaean ruler — folded the region into the rising
[[sasanian-empire|Sasanian]] state. The Elamite name itself, however, outlived even
Elymais, surviving as the Nestorian **ecclesiastical "province of Elam"** until c. 1400 CE.

## Historiography

Elymaean chronology "remains, in many respects, contested": the coin sequence is anchored
mainly by the Babylonian astronomical diaries, and the relationship of homonymous
Kamniskires kings (one ruler in two phases, or father and son?) is unresolved. Rock-relief
dating is stylistic and loose; settlement survey for the Seleuco-Parthian period is
compromised (Wenke mistook Achaemenid forms for Seleucid). Only Susa and Masjid-i Solaiman
yield much excavated material.
