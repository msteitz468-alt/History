## Overview

The Later Hōjō clan (Go-Hōjō, also called the Odawara Hōjō to distinguish them from the earlier Kamakura-period regents of the same name) controlled the eight provinces of the Kantō from their base at Odawara castle for nearly a century (c. 1493–1590). They were among the most durable and administratively sophisticated daimyo of the Sengoku period, governing their territory with bureaucratic consistency and maintaining hereditary loyalty among their retainers. Their conservatism, which served them well in the earlier Sengoku period, became a fatal liability in the era of Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi's rapid political consolidation.

## Territorial Extent and Government

At their peak, the Hōjō controlled Sagami, Izu, Musashi, Awa, Kazusa, Shimōsa, Kōzuke, and Shimotsuke — the eight provinces of the Kantō plain, anchored by the formidable castle city of Odawara. The Hōjō were notable for their structured administration, their system of land surveys, and a legal code (the Hōjō Sōkoku-ji) that attempted to regularize the relationship between lord and retainer. Sadler characterizes the Odawara of the siege era as "a strong and populous city, a ri long, and well furnished with every kind of shop and trade able to supply the luxuries as well as the necessities of life" — a prosperous urban center maintained through decades of stable Hōjō rule.

## Relations with the Tokugawa

During the confused aftermath of Nobunaga's death (1582), Hōjō Ujimasa moved a large army into Kai to contest Ieyasu's seizure of the province. Ieyasu had only 7,000 men to Ujimasa's 50,000, but ran the Hōjō force in circles through the Shinano mountains until Ujimasa sued for a negotiated settlement: Ieyasu kept Kai and Shinano; the Hōjō received Kazusa; Ujimasa's son Ujinori married Ieyasu's daughter. This marriage alliance created a personal connection that persisted through the Komaki-Nagakute campaign (where Hōjō nominally aligned with Ieyasu's coalition) and into the Odawara campaign itself.

During the Komaki-Nagakute campaign (1584), Hōjō sent no material support to Ieyasu, who was waging war against Hideyoshi at least partly in Ieyasu's interest. The Hōjō's trademark conservatism — "infatuated conservatism and easy-going arrogance," in Sadler's phrase — made them easy to flatter and difficult to draw into decisive action. Ieyasu exploited this during his 1586 visit to the Kisegawa frontier, where he performed elaborate self-abasement (taking a lower seat than his son-in-law Ujinao, playing an absurd role in a Noh performance, even ordering the demolition of a frontier fortress in front of Hōjō's councilor as a gesture of trust) to reassure the Hōjō that he was no threat.

## Defiance and Fall

When Hideyoshi presented his ultimatum over the Sanada border dispute, Hōjō Ujimasa refused to submit, believing (as Sadler puts it) that "if Hideyoshi had treated Ieyasu, whom he regarded as a tributary, with such condescension, this threat was no more than bluff." He did not understand how completely the political situation had changed since the days when the Hōjō's sheer regional weight could deter intervention.

During the Odawara siege (1590), the castle held firmly and the garrison inflicted casualties. The Hōjō besieged proved that their castle was militarily formidable. But Hideyoshi simply waited, maintaining a comfortable siege camp of 150,000 men supplied by sea, while gradually reducing the subsidiary fortresses. Hōjō confidence eroded as treachery appeared among their own councilors. Ujinao and Ujifusa ultimately went out to Ieyasu to negotiate; Ujimasa committed seppuku on the eleventh day of the seventh month, aged fifty-three. His farewell poem:

> "Autumn wind of eve, / Blow away the clouds that mass / O'er the moon's pure light / And the mists that cloud our mind, / Do thou sweep away as well."

Ujinao, Ieyasu's son-in-law, was exiled to Mount Koya and later given 10,000 koku in Kawachi, where he died of smallpox at twenty-nine. Ujinori received 3,000 koku at Sayama.

## Significance

The fall of the Hōjō completed the unification of Japan under Hideyoshi. The confiscation of their eight Kantō provinces and their transfer to Ieyasu was the decisive act that would shape Japanese history for the next 250 years — placing the Tokugawa clan in the geographic base from which they would eventually build the Edo shogunate. The Hōjō's administrative legacy, including their tradition of systematic land governance, arguably influenced the Tokugawa approach to Kantō administration.

## Historiography

The Hōjō's defeat is narrated in multiple Tokugawa-period chronicles (including the Kwan-hasshu-kosen-roku and the Azuma Kagami excerpts preserved at Odawara). Sadler characterizes Ujimasa's political judgment as "strange obtuseness" and treats the Hōjō throughout as admirable administrators who were constitutionally incapable of adapting to a new political reality — a judgment reflecting Tokugawa historiography's need to portray Ieyasu's eventual supremacy as inevitable. Modern historians are more sympathetic to the difficulty of the Hōjō's position and the rational calculation, if ultimately mistaken, behind their defiance.
