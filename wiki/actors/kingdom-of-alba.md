# Alba (the Kingdom of the Scots)

The kingdom that coalesced in the tenth century out of **Pictland** and
**[[actors/dal-riata|Dál Riata]]**, ancestor of the medieval kingdom of
[[places/scotland|Scotland]]. Alex Woolf's chapter in
[[sources/stafford-companion-early-middle-ages-britain-ireland-2009|Stafford's *Companion*]]
is the wiki's anchor for its formation and for the dismantling of the old "Kenneth
MacAlpin" origin-myth.

## Overview

The **Picts disappear** from the record between 875 and 900, amid Scandinavian disruption
(Halfdan's 875 expedition) and a linguistic shift toward Gaelic. The term **"king of
Alba"** (*rí Alban*) first appears in Irish chronicles from c.900 — **Domnall mac
Constantín** (889–900) is the first so styled — replacing the older *rex Pictorum*, a
change Woolf links partly to "the linguistic switch from Latin to the vernacular." The
making of the kingdom is now placed firmly in the **tenth century**, not with Cinaed mac
Ailpín.

## Cinaed mac Ailpín (Kenneth MacAlpin): myth and reality

Cinaed (d. 858) was described by contemporaries simply as *rex Pictorum* — "no hint that
he was of Gaelic origin." His later reinvention as a Dál Riatan Scot, and the legend of
his **genocide of the Picts**, are "early-eleventh-century retrospective construction[s]"
by Gaelic historians who made him the apical figure of a royal genealogy traced (with a
"major disjuncture") to the Cenél nGabráin of Dál Riata. "Early medieval dynasties were
not above tampering with their pedigrees."

## Constantín son of Aed (900–943)

"Perhaps the most significant" reign in early Scottish history. Constantín defeated the
Norse at Strathearn (904), presided over the **Council of Scone (906)** — swearing with
Bishop Cellach to uphold church law, the first record of Scone as a royal centre — fought
Rægnald at Corbridge (918) to a stalemate, submitted to Edward the Elder (920), and allied
with the Hiberno-Norse against **Æthelstan at Brunanburh (937)** before abdicating to the
monastic community at St Andrews in 943. His exceptionally long reign gave the nascent
kingdom structural stability.

## Structure and the Alternating Kingship

From the 870s to 1005 the **mac Alpín** dynasty alternated the kingship between the lines
of Cinaed's sons Constantín and Aed — like the [[actors/ui-neill|Uí Néill]] alternation at
Tara, held together by intermarriage, providing stability but also the eventual cause of
collapse. Below the king, regional rulers held the title **mormaer** (Latin *comes*) — an
institution perhaps of Pictish root; by the twelfth century most territory north of the
Forth was organised into mormaer-ruled provinces (Moray, Fife, Atholl, etc.). The **clann
Ruaidrí of Moray** — whose number included **Macbethad** (Macbeth, king 1040–57) —
repeatedly challenged the Alpinid kings.

## Mechanisms of Decline and Transition

The dynastic crisis of the early eleventh century (Donnchad vs. Macbethad; Macbethad vs.
Máel Coluim) closed with **Máel Coluim III** (Malcolm Canmore), whose marriage to the
English refugee **[[actors/margaret-of-scotland|Margaret]]** opened the Anglo-Norman era;
from his reign "the affairs of the kingdom become increasingly easy to discern."
