# Lysias

The greatest of the Athenian **speechwriters** (*logographoi*) (c. 445–c. 380 BCE) and a canonical
**Attic orator**, whose surviving speeches are a uniquely vivid primary source for Athenian law,
society, and the crisis of the **[[thirty-tyrants|Thirty Tyrants]]**. His works are now ingested as
**[[lysias-oratory|Lysias]]** (Oratory of Classical Greece).

## Life

Lysias was a wealthy **metic** (resident alien) of Athens, son of a Syracusan invited by Pericles; the
family ran a successful shield-factory. Under the **[[thirty-tyrants|Thirty]]** (404/3 BCE) the regime
seized the family's wealth and **murdered his brother Polemarchus**; Lysias escaped, funded the
democratic resistance, and returned with the restored democracy in 403. Barred as a non-citizen from
speaking in the assembly, he made his living **writing speeches for others** to deliver in the courts.

## The Orator

Lysias is the model of the **plain, lucid Attic style** — apparent simplicity concealing great art — and
the master of ***ēthopoiia***, crafting a speech to fit the character and voice of the ordinary client who
would deliver it. About thirty-five speeches survive, mostly **forensic** (court) pieces, the most famous
being **Against Eratosthenes** — his own prosecution of one of the **Thirty** for Polemarchus's death, a
searing first-person indictment of the tyranny and the only Lysianic speech he delivered himself.

## Significance

Lysias's speeches are a **prime source for Athenian daily life, law, and democracy**: they open windows
onto inheritance and property disputes, the scrutiny (*dokimasia*) of officials, the experience of
**metics**, sycophancy, and the politics of the restored democracy after 403. Stylistically he became a
classic, studied as the standard of pure Attic prose from antiquity onward.
