# Fort Ancient Culture

## Overview

In the **Fort Ancient** area of the Middle Ohio River Valley, the **A.D. 1000** transition to maize farming was accompanied by changes in material culture and social organization. Many technological and material changes were clearly **influenced by Mississippian societies** of the Lower Ohio River Valley and beyond (shell-tempered large jars, curvilinear incised designs, triangular points, stone discoidals/“chunky,” weeping-eye motifs) — *analogous_to* (influence, not identity): [[mississippian-culture]]; *contrasts_with:* [[hopewell-culture]] dispersed farmsteads. Cordell & Smith treat Fort Ancient as a **northern non-Mississippian** maize society; Mississippian ranked chiefdom depth is owned by ch. 5.

**Naming note:** the Fort Ancient *earthwork* in Ohio is Hopewell; the later *culture* was misnamed after the site.

## Settlement A.D. 1000–1200

Numerous terrace villages larger and more organized than Late Woodland (e.g. Waterplant). Often stockaded with storage-trash pits, burials, rectangular single-family houses around plazas; burial mounds common; **few grave goods / little status differentiation** in burials — ranking weaker than Mississippian chiefdoms.

### Incinerator site (eleventh century)

Small village, ~25-year occupation: central post complex, plaza ringed by concentric burials, storage/trash pits, wattle-and-daub thatch houses, stockades. ~14 structures in half the habitation zone → estimate **20–30 households, 150–200 people**; 3–4 household clusters (possible matrilocal lineage segments); two west-side community structures in a solstice-aligned “sacred area” for assemblies/councils/ritual storage. Internal post and hearth aligned with central post on **April 24** (earliest safe frost-free planting) and **August 20** (120-day corn “green”) — suggests green corn ceremony focus. Combines sacred and domestic space in the village (*contrasts_with* Hopewellian sacred centers spatially separated from dispersed households).

## After A.D. 1200

Fewer Fort Ancient villages documented (population decline or amalgamation into larger villages); efficient below-ground grass-lined cylindrical maize silos (1 m diameter, 1–2 m deep, 1–1.5 m³ / 30–40 bushels). By **A.D. 1450**, earlier plaza/central-post plan lacking in remaining large villages — lineage single-family clusters replaced by large multifamily structures without overall village organization. Possible parallel to Lower Ohio–Mississippi “empty quarter” demographic decline (ch. 5) or to Iroquoian longhouse developmental trends (*analogous_to:* [[haudenosaunee-confederacy]] longhouse trajectory parallels; light *concurrent_with:* [[mississippian-culture]] empty quarter).
