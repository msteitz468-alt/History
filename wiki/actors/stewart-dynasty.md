# The Stewart Dynasty (Scotland, 1406–1513)

The royal house of late-medieval Scotland, from the death of Robert III (1406) to the catastrophe of
Flodden (1513). Treated by Jenny Wormald in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 22b). Wormald
argues against viewing Scotland as "a smaller-scale version of England": it was a successful and
distinctive kingdom whose **peripatetic, personal kingship** worked on its own terms, even as the
dynasty suffered a unique structural flaw.

## The Stewart Flaw and Distinctive Kingship

The defining feature of the century is the **recurrent royal minority**, "unparalleled in any
European kingdom": the early, often violent deaths of the Stewart kings — none living to see his son
grow up — repeatedly collapsed royal authority into faction. Yet, strikingly, the kings made almost
no use of the **propaganda and ideology** their mightier neighbours deployed: no royal touch, no
cultivated antiquity (the dynasty frankly recalled its origins as household *stewards*), little
patronage of the "nationalist" literature (Barbour, Bower, Blind Hary) that lesser nobles
sponsored. Wormald reads effective Scottish kingship through an early-medieval model of **centre and
periphery**: government remained tied to the **royal circuit**, not to a fixed capital, so that the
most successful kings were the most mobile.

## The Reigns

- **James I** (personal rule 1424–37): after eighteen years' English captivity, ruthlessly imposed
  his authority and seized magnate lands; murdered at Perth (1437).
- **James II** (d. 1460): broke the over-mighty **Black Douglases**; an enthusiast for **guns** — and
  killed by an exploding cannon at the siege of Roxburgh.
- **James III** (1460–88): the exception who tried to govern statically **from Edinburgh** (which
  became the undisputed capital in his reign) and pursued peace with England; his failure and death in
  battle against rebellious subjects at **Sauchieburn (1488)** illustrate, for Wormald, the cost of
  abandoning peripatetic rule.
- **James IV** (1488–1513): the "glore of princelie governyng" — energetic, charismatic, restlessly
  itinerant; suppressed the semi-independent **Lordship of the Isles** (1490s), built a navy (the
  *Great Michael*) and a brilliant court (William Dunbar; the royal entry of 1503 with his bride
  Margaret Tudor). His one fatal mistake was to offer the English battle: he died with an unusually
  large national army at **Flodden (1513)** — "a testimony, if ultimately a tragic one, to the
  success of his kingship."

## Highland and Lowland

Wormald cautions against reading back a sharp **Highland–Lowland** divide: it was an "expansionist
crown bent on extending its control" (James I against the Lord of the Isles, 1428; James IV's western
expeditions) that *began to create* the later "Highland problem." Lowland and Gaelic society shared
the workable justice of the **feud**, a bilingual legal vocabulary, and (in James IV) royal patronage
of Gaelic culture.

## Significance

A poor but confident kingdom that "took its success for granted," Scotland preserved its independence
and the **Auld Alliance** with France through a century that destroyed dynasties elsewhere — only for
the senior Stewart line's fatal pattern of early death to deliver yet another minority after Flodden.
