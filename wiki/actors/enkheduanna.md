# Enkheduanna

Daughter of [[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon of Akkad]] and **the first holder known to
history of the office of high-priestess (*entu*) of the Moon-god [[ur|Nanna at
Ur]]** — an office that thereafter became, for nearly two millennia, the
prerogative of royal sisters and daughters. She is widely regarded as the
**earliest author known by name in world history**, though CAH (1971) treats her
more cautiously, as a historical priestess attested by her own monument and by the
poetry attributed to her. Introduced in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]]
(Ch. XIX, §II, C. J. Gadd).

## A political-religious appointment

Sargon's installation of his daughter as *entu* at **Ur** was a deliberate
instrument of [[akkadian-empire|empire]]: placing a royal woman at the head of the
most venerable Sumerian cult bound the conquered south's religious life to the
Akkadian house. Enkheduanna left a **monument of her own** (the alabaster
"disk of Enheduanna," showing her at a temple rite) and her name on the
**cylinder seals of her servants** — the material proof CAH rests on.

## The lament and the revolt

CAH connects her to the **Sumerian revolt that met the accession of Sargon's son
Rimush**: when Ur rose and was crushed, a **lamentation ascribed to
Enkheduanna** depicts her as the victim of the disaster — driven into exile,
powerless to appease the Moon-god's anger or her own brother's wrath against the
rebel city. This poem (the **Exaltation of Inanna**, in the fuller modern reading)
is both a personal cry and a piece of imperial theology, exalting Sargon's patron
goddess Inanna/Ishtar over the local order.

## Significance

Enkheduanna stands at three intersections at once: the **fusion of Akkadian power
with Sumerian cult**; the **beginnings of named authorship** (and of devotional
literature) in the [[writing-and-record-keeping|written record]]; and the long
institution of the **royal *entu*-priestess** that would persist to the last days
of Babylonian Ur. Her texts are among the earliest in which an individual
religious voice — and a woman's — can be heard at all.

## Historiography

The attribution of the surviving hymns (the *Temple Hymns*, the *Exaltation of
Inanna*) to Enkheduanna rests on later copies and is debated: she may be author,
patron, or traditional ascription. CAH is deliberately reserved, treating the
**office and the monument** as secure and the **lament** as connected to but not
certainly composed by her. Her modern fame as "the world's first named author" runs
ahead of what the third-millennium evidence strictly proves. Dates approximate;
[[mesopotamian-chronology|middle chronology]]. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
