# Charles Lanrezac

French general. Commander of Fifth Army (1914) during the opening campaign. Key figure in the Battle of the Frontiers (Sambre/Charleroi) and the battle of Guise; relieved by Joffre on 4 September 1914 and replaced by Franchet d’Espèrey.

## Narrative

Lanrezac commanded the French Fifth Army, initially positioned to cover the left of Fourth Army and prepared to operate against enemy forces debouching between Mézières and Mouzon. As intelligence mounted of strong German forces in central Belgium, Lanrezac repeatedly expressed concern that the Germans would cross the Meuse on the west bank and outflank him. Joffre initially dismissed these concerns to preserve arrangements with the British and because intelligence assessments still placed the main German effort between Liège and the northwest corner of Luxembourg. 

On 14–15 August Joffre authorized Fifth Army to extend slightly left and prepare to advance north on the west bank of the Meuse. Lanrezac moved the army into the angle formed by the Meuse and Sambre at Namur (19–20 August). The position resembled an upside-down “V,” with I Corps (Franchet d’Espèrey) on the right along the Meuse, X and III Corps along the Sambre, and XVIII Corps extending the left. A cavalry corps screened the far left.

Leading elements of Bülow’s German Second Army seized Sambre crossings relatively easily on 21 August because Lanrezac had ordered only small forces forward to protect the bridges while main units remained on the heights of the south bank. Lanrezac did not immediately grasp the significance and reported only that outposts were engaged and Namur was being invested. He delayed his own offensive until the British arrived, then attempted on 22 August to drive the Germans back across the Sambre. By mid-afternoon German attacks had pushed the center corps back; by evening all three center/right corps had been driven from the river line. 

To cover his right rear, Lanrezac shifted I Corps north toward Namur. Hausen’s German Third Army forced additional bridgeheads at Dinant (north of Givet). On 23 August the British were heavily engaged at Mons. Fearing an attack in his rear, Lanrezac informed Joffre of his intention to withdraw toward Givet. This decision ended the battle of the frontiers and yielded the initiative to the Germans.

Despite the withdrawal, Joffre ordered Lanrezac to launch a counterattack to relieve pressure on the British and to slow the German pursuit. After tense exchanges (Lanrezac preferred to withdraw farther to Laon before counterattacking; Joffre traveled to headquarters and wrote a direct order), Fifth Army attacked on 29 August from the Guise area toward St.-Quentin. The left made limited progress; the center came under heavy German pressure; Franchet d’Espèrey’s I Corps on the right launched a vigorous counterattack at 1700 hours that drove the enemy back with assistance from adjacent corps. The action (battle of Guise) slowed the German advance but did not halt it. Sir John French’s British forces did not cooperate as hoped.

Joffre lost confidence in Lanrezac after repeated challenges to orders and the cumulative difficulties of the campaign. On 4 September (after the government had left Paris and as the Marne concept crystallized), Joffre relieved him. The relief was “intensely painful” to Joffre, who had known Lanrezac for years and esteemed his intellectual gifts, but he judged it necessary. Franchet d’Espèrey replaced him and immediately demonstrated the energy and willingness to execute the coming counteroffensive that Joffre required.

## Key Decisions and Consequences

- Persistent warnings about German strength and depth in Belgium (mid-August) — partially validated but initially overridden by Joffre’s assessment; contributed to delayed adjustment of Fifth Army position.
- Decision to keep main forces on south bank of Sambre and send only small bridge guards (21 Aug) — allowed easy German crossings; produced the crisis that ended the frontier battles.
- Counterattack at Guise (29 Aug) — slowed German pursuit and bought time for Sixth Army assembly and further retreat planning; demonstrated value of I Corps under d’Espèrey but insufficient to restore the situation or convince British cooperation.
- Repeated objections to Joffre’s orders and preference for deeper withdrawal before counterattacking — eroded Joffre’s confidence and led directly to relief on 4 September.

## Historiography

Doughty presents Lanrezac as a perceptive but difficult subordinate whose strategic instincts about the German right-wing depth were largely correct, yet whose execution and relations with higher command (and the British) contributed to the frontier disasters and the necessity for command changes. The relief of Lanrezac (like that of Ruffey) is framed as part of Joffre’s ruthless weeding of commanders who lacked the faith and self-mastery required for the coming battle.
