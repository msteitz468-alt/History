# Hezekiah

**Hezekiah** (Hebrew *Ḥizqiyahu*, "Yahweh has strengthened"; reigned **716/715–687/686
BCE**) was the reforming king of [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]] who reversed the apostasy
of his father Ahaz, attempted to **concentrate all worship in the Jerusalem Temple**,
cut the famous **Siloam tunnel**, led the western revolt against Assyria, and survived
**[[sennacherib-siege-jerusalem-701bce|Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem (701)]]** when
the Assyrians withdrew without taking the city. He is treated by [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|
CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 29, T. C. Mitchell).

## Inheritance: the apostasy of Ahaz

Hezekiah's father **Ahaz** had made Judah an Assyrian vassal during the
**Syro-Ephraimite War** (when [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] and Aram-Damascus attacked
Judah, c. 734), appealing to [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]] with the
words "I am your servant and your son" against the prophet **Isaiah's** advice. Ahaz
copied a Syrian-style altar for the Temple, closed the Temple, built cult platforms
across Judah, and even "passed his sons through the fire" in the Valley of Hinnom.
Hezekiah, who had watched all this from boyhood, reversed it at once.

## The religious reform

In his first month Hezekiah **reopened and cleansed the Temple**, restored the
sacrificial cult, and made a **covenant renewal** marked by a great **Passover** to
which he invited even the Jews of the former [[kingdom-of-israel|northern kingdom]]
under Assyrian occupation (Chronicles). He then **destroyed the cult platforms, pillars,
and the bronze serpent Nehushtan** — a Mosaic relic now treated as an idol — throughout
Judah, an early move toward the **centralization of worship in Jerusalem** later
completed by his great-grandson [[josiah|Josiah]]. Archaeology may attest the reform in
the dismantled horned altar at **Beersheba**.

## The Siloam tunnel and the revolt

As Assyrian power passed to a new king ([[sennacherib|Sennacherib]], 705), Hezekiah
joined the western rebellion — seizing Philistine territory and holding the deposed
pro-Assyrian king of Ekron, **Padi**, prisoner. He prepared Jerusalem for siege:
repairing the walls, reinforcing the **Millo**, and above all cutting the **Siloam
tunnel**, a 533-metre conduit driven through solid rock by two gangs working from
opposite ends to bring the **Gihon spring's** water inside the walls while denying it
to besiegers — commemorated by the **Siloam inscription**, one of the finest monuments
of early Hebrew. The ***lmlk* ("belonging to the king") stamped storage-jar handles**,
found in a defensive arc around Jerusalem, probably reflect his provisioning of
garrisons for the coming war.

## 701: Sennacherib's invasion

In **701** Sennacherib's third campaign devastated Judah — the annals claim **46
fortified cities taken and 200,150 deported**, and the reliefs depict in detail the
**siege of [[sennacherib-siege-jerusalem-701bce|Lachish]]**. The Assyrian *rab-šāqê*
harangued Jerusalem's walls demanding surrender. But the city held: Sennacherib
**withdrew without taking Jerusalem** (the Bible attributes 185,000 dead in his camp
to the angel of Yahweh; Mitchell canvasses plague, a rumour of trouble in Babylonia,
and a textual crux on "185,000"). Hezekiah paid heavy tribute (30 talents of gold;
800 or 300 talents of silver), but Judah survived as an independent state. See
**[[sennacherib-siege-jerusalem-701bce]]**.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the apostasy of Ahaz, which Hezekiah set out to reverse; the wider revolt
against [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]] at Sennacherib's accession.
produced: the **first attempt at cultic centralization in Jerusalem** (the model for
[[josiah|Josiah's]] reform); the survival of [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]] through 701.
contributed_to: the **theology of Zion's inviolability** — Jerusalem's deliverance in
701 became a touchstone of Judaean faith, with lasting consequences (and dangers, when
later kings counted on it against Babylon).

## Counterfactual significance

Mitchell's account makes Hezekiah the hinge between Ahaz's surrender and Josiah's
reform: had Jerusalem fallen in 701 as Samaria had in 722, the Davidic line and the
Temple cult — the matrix of the Hebrew scriptures — might have ended a century early.
The survival of 701 instead fixed the conviction that Yahweh would defend his city.

## Historiography

Known from the unusually full **Kings, Isaiah, and Chronicles** accounts (the last
two preserving the "Vision of Isaiah"), the **Sennacherib annals** (Taylor/Chicago
Prisms), the **Lachish reliefs**, the **Siloam inscription**, the ***lmlk* jar stamps**,
and the bullae of his officials (Eliakim, Shebna). The central unresolved questions are
the **chronology** (the date of his accession and of the 701 events) and **whether the
Bible conflates two Assyrian campaigns**; Mitchell follows a single 701 campaign. See
[[neo-assyrian-empire]] and [[assyrian-mass-deportation]] for the Assyrian side.
