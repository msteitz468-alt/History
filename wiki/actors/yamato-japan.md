# Yamato Japan

## Overview

**Yamato** (early Japan, also written **Wa**) is the polity from which the Japanese imperial
state emerged between the third and eighth centuries CE. In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]
(Holcombe, Ch 15) Japan is a far edge of the **[[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian
cultural sphere]]** that adopted Chinese writing, [[buddhism|Buddhism]], and statecraft while
ultimately asserting an **independent, rival imperial identity** rather than accepting
subordination to China.

## From Yayoi to Centralized State

Migration from the **Korean Peninsula** (from c. 400 BCE) brought wet-rice agriculture, iron,
and bronze in the **Yayoi period** (3rd c. BCE–3rd c. CE). A third-century Chinese history
describes a coalition of communities under the priestess-queen **Himiko**. The **Kofun**
("old tomb") period (c. 250–552 CE) of giant keyhole-shaped tumuli marks emerging political
consolidation; a genuinely centralized state appeared in western Japan around the sixth
century.

## Adopting and Resisting the Chinese Model

**[[buddhism|Buddhism]]** arrived from **Paekche** (538 or 552 CE) and became a vehicle for
continental learning. As the **[[sui-dynasty|Sui]]** reunification (589) made China a looming
superpower, Japanese rulers responded by **centralizing**: adopting Chinese-style imperial
titles (the **tennō**), law codes (from 668), and a continental-style capital, **Nara**
(built 708–712), giving its name to the **Nara period (710–784)**, the apogee of
Chinese-style empire-building in Japan. Yet Japanese monarchs **stopped accepting subordinate
titles** from China by the seventh century and instead claimed **rival** supreme imperial
status, grounding legitimacy in **divine descent from the Sun Goddess** rather than the
Chinese [[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate of Heaven]]. The defeat of a Japanese fleet by the
**[[tang-dynasty|Tang]]**–Silla alliance at the **Paekchon River (663 CE)** ended major
Japanese involvement on the continent for nearly a thousand years.

## A Distinct Japanese Culture

By the tenth century Chinese-style imperial institutions waned, imperial power atrophied, and
a uniquely Japanese culture coalesced — **kana** phonetic scripts, the rise of provincial
warriors (incipient **samurai**), and **Lady Murasaki's** *Tale of Genji* (c. 1000 CE).

## Significance

Yamato Japan exemplifies CWH IV's theme of **selective adoption** within the East Asian
sphere: borrowing Chinese script, religion, and statecraft to build a state, while
constructing an independent imperial ideology and, ultimately, a distinct civilization.

## Historiography

Reconstructed from Chinese histories, Korean records, archaeology (the Kofun tombs), and
Japan's own earliest chronicle the **Kojiki** (712) — which pointedly used the Chinese phrase
"all Under Heaven" in reference to Japan **without mentioning China**, a deliberate assertion
of parity. CWH IV cautions against retrojecting later national categories onto this formative
era.
