# Corneliu Zelea Codreanu (1899–1938)

Founder and Căpitanul ("the Captain") of the Romanian
[[iron-guard|Legion of the Archangel Michael]], the most religiously mystical
of Europe's fascist leaders. A student antisemitic agitator acquitted of
murdering a police prefect in 1924, he founded the Legion in 1927 around
Orthodox spirituality, ascetic discipline, rural evangelism, and the
sanctification of violence and martyrdom.

## Role and Significance

Codreanu built the only mass fascist movement whose core appeal was
explicitly religious — [[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|Payne]] treats him as
the test case for fascism's relationship to traditional religion. Arrested
in King Carol II's 1938 crackdown, he was strangled with thirteen comrades
on the night of 29–30 November 1938 ("shot while attempting to escape"),
becoming the movement's supreme martyr; the Legion's subsequent turn to
mass reprisal killing under Horia Sima followed his cult of death to its
conclusion.

## Historiography

Treated across the fascism literature as the extreme pole of "clerical" or
religious fascism against the secular-palingenetic Italian and German cores;
his posthumous cult persists in Romanian and international far-right
subcultures. Griffin ([[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]]) treats the
Legion's spirituality as fully palingenetic — Legionary recruitment language:
"the new man or the renewed nation presupposes a great spiritual renewal...
the Legionary Movement is a spiritual school, in which if a man shall enter,
a hero must come out at the other end" — and quotes the Legionary Moța,
killed fighting for Franco in Spain, dying "for the happiness of the
Romanian people, for its rebirth which is being made possible by our
Captain's reconstruction of it." See [[generic-fascism]] and
[[palingenetic-ultranationalism]].

## Payne (1980)

From [[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]]: founded the Legion in 1927 out of the extreme-religious antisemitic milieu of the National Christian Party but "became a sort of religious mystic"; Legionaries identified totally with their reading of Romanian Orthodoxy. Nolte (quoted by Payne) called Codreanu "the other European nationalist leader most like Hitler" in single-minded fanaticism and personal magnetism — but the movement's martyr complex bred "self-destructiveness rarely equaled." Payne (ch. 5, on Poland's Falanga): the maxim "God is the highest end of man" was "more appropriate for Codreanu than for José Antonio Primo de Rivera" — placing Codreanu at the religious-extreme pole of European fascism.
