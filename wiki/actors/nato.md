# NATO (the North Atlantic Treaty Organization)

**NATO** is the transatlantic military alliance founded by the **North Atlantic Treaty**
(Washington, 4 April 1949) to deter Soviet aggression against Western Europe — the
**military arm of [[containment]]** and the institutional core of the Western bloc in the
[[cold-war|Cold War]]. From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|*The Cambridge History of the Cold
War*, Vol. I]].

## Founding and purpose

NATO grew out of the insecurity of postwar Western Europe — the [[berlin-blockade-1948|Berlin
Blockade]] (1948–49) crystallized the fear of Soviet pressure — and out of European demands
for a binding American security guarantee to complement the economic
[[marshall-plan-1948|Marshall Plan]]. Its founding members (the US, Canada, Britain, France,
the Benelux, Italy, Norway, Denmark, Iceland, Portugal) committed under **Article 5** to
treat an armed attack on one as an attack on all. As Lord Ismay's famous quip had it, the
alliance existed "to keep the Russians out, the Americans in, and the Germans down."

## From treaty to military command

The treaty of 1949 was at first largely a political pledge. The **[[korean-war-1950|Korean
War]]** (1950) transformed it: fearing a similar Soviet stroke in Europe, the allies built an
**integrated military command** (SHAPE, under Eisenhower), committed standing US troops to
Europe, and — most divisively — moved toward **West German rearmament**, achieved when the
Federal Republic joined NATO in **1955**. The Soviet response was the **Warsaw Pact** (1955),
formalizing the bipolar military division of Europe. NATO strategy rested increasingly on the
US **[[nuclear-arms-race|nuclear]]** guarantee (massive retaliation, later flexible response),
which repeatedly strained the alliance (the French withdrawal from the integrated command
under de Gaulle, 1966; the Euromissiles crisis of the 1980s).

## Significance and afterlife

NATO was the most durable and successful Cold War alliance, holding the central front in
Europe without a shot fired between the blocs. It **outlived its adversary**: the Warsaw Pact
dissolved in 1991, but NATO persisted and **enlarged** eastward into former Soviet-bloc
states after the Cold War — making the alliance, and the question of its expansion, a central
issue of the [[contemporary|post-Cold-War]] order.

## From Paxton & Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century*

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton & Hessler]] add institutional prehistory:
NATO's founding (April 4, 1949) built on the **Treaty of Brussels** (March 1948, UK,
France, and the Benelux states) — itself reacting to the Prague coup and the run-up to
the Berlin blockade — which in turn built on the **Anglo-French Treaty of Dunkirk**
(March 1947). The **Vandenberg Resolution** (June 1948) marked the decisive US Senate
shift away from isolationism that made the treaty possible. West Germany's accession
(October 1954 by treaty, effective May 1955) followed the French parliament's June 1954
rejection of the European Defence Community, which forced the fallback to national West
German rearmament within NATO. De Gaulle's withdrawal from NATO's integrated command
proceeded in stages — 1959 (Mediterranean fleet and air squadrons), 1963 (Atlantic/
Channel fleets), and 1966 (full withdrawal from the integrated military structures while
remaining in the Atlantic Alliance) — after which NATO headquarters moved from near Paris
to Brussels.
