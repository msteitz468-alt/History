## Overview

**Abd al-Jabbar ibn Ahmad al-Hamadhani** (c. 935–1025 CE) was the foremost Mu'tazilite theologian of the High Medieval period and, by common scholarly consent, the greatest systematic exponent of [[mutazilism|Mu'tazilism]] in its mature form. Known honorifically as *Qadi al-Qudat* (Chief Judge of Judges), he produced the most comprehensive surviving statement of Mu'tazilite theology: the *al-Mughni fi abwab al-tawhid wa-l-adl* ("The Sufficient Work on the Chapters of Divine Unity and Justice"), a multi-volume summa that remained the reference point for Mu'tazilism in both Sunni and Shi'ite polemics for centuries.

Abd al-Jabbar represents the moment at which Mu'tazilism had been decisively expelled from Sunni intellectual life by the [[asharism|Ash'arite]] synthesis yet found a second home within [[shia-islam|Twelver and Zaydi Shi'ite]] theology. His thought is the pivot between the formative Mu'tazilite school of Baghdad and Basra and the later Shi'ite rational theology.

---

## Life

Abd al-Jabbar was born in Hamadhan (present-day western Iran) around 935 CE. He received his early theological education within the Mu'tazilite tradition and eventually settled in Ray (near modern Tehran), which under the Buyid dynasty functioned as a centre of heterodox intellectual activity. The Buyid vizier **al-Sahib ibn Abbad** (938–995), himself a committed Mu'tazilite and major patron of learning, appointed Abd al-Jabbar **Chief Qadi of Ray** around 977 — an extraordinary elevation for a member of a school by then marginalised among Sunnis. Al-Sahib ibn Abbad's patronage gave Abd al-Jabbar the institutional platform and scholarly freedom to produce his enormous theological output.

He died in 1025, having outlived his patron and the height of Buyid power, but his students continued the school's tradition, especially in Zaydi Yemen where Mu'tazilism maintained a continuous living presence.

## Theological System

Abd al-Jabbar's theology is organised around the Mu'tazilite **Five Principles** (*al-usul al-khamsa*) first formulated by Abu'l-Hudhayl al-'Allaf (c. 753–841) and developed through the Baghdad school:

### 1. Divine Unity (*Tawhid*)

God is absolutely one and unique. Nothing whatsoever may be compared to Him (Qur'an 42:11). Abd al-Jabbar systematised the Mu'tazilite position on the divine attributes more rigorously than any predecessor. He held that God's attributes of knowledge, power, and will have no independent existence separate from God's essence — they are ways of speaking about God's action, not real subsisting entities. To posit coeternal attributes alongside God's essence would compromise divine unity, constituting a covert polytheism.

This placed him in direct opposition to the [[asharism|Ash'arites]], who insisted the essential attributes were real, coeternal, and "neither God Himself nor other than God." The Abu Hashim al-Jubbai (d. 933) theory of "states" (*ahwal*) — which Abd al-Jabbar inherited and developed — was an attempt to describe what grounds the truth of attributive statements about God without positing real subsisting attributes.

### 2. Divine Justice (*'Adl*)

God is perfectly just and is bound by His own declaration of justice. This is the doctrine with the most far-reaching practical consequences. Abd al-Jabbar's formulation: God can will and do only what is *good* (*aslah* — most beneficial for His creation). He cannot create evil, cannot unjustly punish, and cannot reward sin. The doctrine of the *aslah* (God must always do what is most beneficial) was the most controversial Mu'tazilite teaching, since it appeared to constrain divine freedom. Ash'arite opponents charged that a God bound by such a principle was less than omnipotent.

The justice doctrine entails **genuine human free will**: if God determines all human actions, then His punishment of sinners is itself unjust — punishing creatures for actions they could not have avoided. Abd al-Jabbar's free-will theory holds that God creates in humans the power (*qudra*) to act, but humans exercise that power independently. God's empowerment precedes the act; the act itself is genuinely the human's own creation.

### 3. The Promise and the Threat (*al-Wa'd wa-l-Wa'id*)

God has promised Paradise to the righteous and threatened Hell to the impenitent wicked. These promises and threats are binding on God by His own justice: He cannot rescind them out of arbitrary mercy. This eliminated the hadith doctrine of prophetic intercession (*shafa'a*) from Abd al-Jabbar's system, since allowing the Prophet to intercede to spare impenitent sinners would violate the justice of the divine threat. This was a major point of Sunni-Mu'tazilite divergence.

### 4. The Intermediate State (*al-Manzila bayn al-Manzilatayn*)

The Muslim who dies an unrepentant grave sinner is in an "intermediate state" — neither a full believer nor an unbeliever. This position (first formulated by Wasil ibn Ata, d. 748) distinguished Mu'tazilism from both the Kharijite (sinner = apostate) and Murji'ite (sinner = believer) positions. In Abd al-Jabbar's systematic treatment the intermediate state is primarily an eschatological category: the grave sinner faces eternal punishment in Hell, consistent with the justice principle and the divine threat.

### 5. Commanding Right and Forbidding Wrong (*al-Amr bi-l-Ma'ruf wa-l-Nahy 'an al-Munkar*)

The fifth principle enjoined active intervention against injustice and sin, up to and including armed revolt under specified conditions. Historically this had given Mu'tazilism its politically activist edge; in Abd al-Jabbar's more quiescent Buyid context it was intellectually maintained without political expression.

## The *Al-Mughni*

The *al-Mughni fi abwab al-tawhid wa-l-adl* (preserved in part; sections discovered and published in the twentieth century) is the most systematic exposition of Mu'tazilite theology produced in the classical period. It covers:
- Epistemology and the foundations of knowledge (*'ilm*)
- The nature of being and existence
- Divine unity and the attributes
- Divine justice and human free will
- Obligation (*taklif*) and moral theory
- Prophecy and imamate
- Eschatology

The work's recovery and publication from Yemeni manuscript libraries in the mid-twentieth century substantially transformed scholarly understanding of Mu'tazilism. Before its recovery, Mu'tazilite theology was known mainly through Ash'arite refutations — an inherently distorting lens.

## Significance in the Tradition

Abd al-Jabbar's position in Islamic intellectual history is structurally anomalous. He was a Sunni-educated theologian (trained in the Shafi'i legal tradition) who headed a Mu'tazilite school operating under Shi'ite Buyid patronage in a context where Sunni Ash'arism had already triumphed in the major western Islamic centres. His theology fed into:

1. **Zaydi Shi'ism**: Zaydi scholars adopted Mu'tazilite rational theology wholesale, and Abd al-Jabbar's texts became standard curriculum in Zaydi Yemen.
2. **Twelver Shi'ism**: Twelver theologians in the Buyid period (especially al-Shaykh al-Mufid, d. 1022, and al-Sharif al-Murtada, d. 1044) engaged intensively with Abd al-Jabbar's system, often adopting its methods while modifying its imamate theology.
3. **Post-Ash'arite Sunni kalam**: Even Ash'arite theologians were forced to define themselves against Abd al-Jabbar's systematic challenges, especially on the divine attributes and free will.

The Winter *Cambridge Companion* (2008) places Abd al-Jabbar within the trajectory from Wasil ibn Ata through Abu'l-Hudhayl to the mature school: a tradition of "quasi-rationalistic defence of the faith" whose culmination was the *Mughni*. He represents Mu'tazilism at its most systematically developed — and most institutionally marginalised within Sunni Islam.

---

## Counterfactual Significance

Had the Abbasid *mihna* (833–851) permanently institutionalised Mu'tazilism, Abd al-Jabbar would likely be regarded as the systematiser of Islamic orthodoxy rather than a defender of a marginalised school. The accident of political reversal under al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861) sent Mu'tazilism into permanent Sunni exile; Abd al-Jabbar's career in Buyid Ray represents what Mu'tazilism looked like when given patronage — rigorous, comprehensive, and philosophically sophisticated.

---

## Historiography

Abd al-Jabbar was largely unknown to western scholarship until the Cairo publication of parts of the *al-Mughni* beginning in the 1960s. Richard Frank's studies of early [[kalam]] and George Hourani's work on Mu'tazilite ethics (*Islamic Rationalism*, Oxford, 1971) brought Abd al-Jabbar to wider scholarly attention. The source coverage in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection on this figure is indirect: Winter's *Cambridge Companion* provides the framework; dedicated studies of Abd al-Jabbar himself are not in the collection.
