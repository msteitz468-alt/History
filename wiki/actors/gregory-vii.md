# Pope Gregory VII (Hildebrand)

**Hildebrand of Sovana**, who reigned as **Pope Gregory VII** (1073–1085), was the
most radical and emblematic figure of the [[gregorian-reform|church reform]] that
bears his name. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (I. S. Robinson, Ch. 9; Cowdrey,
Ch. 8) presents him as the culmination of a reform party that had gathered at Rome
since the 1040s, and as the pope who first pressed the *libertas ecclesiae* to the
point of open war with the empire.

## Role and Career

Born in Tuscany c. 1015, Hildebrand rose through the reform circle around Leo IX and
his successors, serving as a key administrator and (from 1058) archdeacon of the
Roman Church before his acclamation as pope in 1073. As pope he pursued reform with
exceptional intensity, attacking **simony** and **clerical marriage**, asserting the
supremacy of the *sacerdotium* over the *regnum*, and condemning **lay investiture**.

His **[[dictatus-papae|*Dictatus Papae*]]** (1075) — a set of twenty-seven terse
propositions entered in his register — distilled his conception of papal authority:
the Roman Church was founded by God alone; the pope alone could depose bishops and
emperors, be judged by no one, and was rendered holy by the merits of St Peter. This
maximalist programme precipitated the **[[investiture-contest|Investiture Contest]]**
with **[[henry-iv-holy-roman-emperor|Henry IV]]**: the mutual depositions of 1076,
Henry's penance at **Canossa (1077)**, the renewed breach and second excommunication
(1080), and the schism of the antipope **Clement III**. Driven from Rome by Henry's
forces and the destructive intervention of his Norman allies (the sack of Rome,
1084), Gregory died in exile at Salerno in 1085, reportedly saying, "I have loved
righteousness and hated iniquity; therefore I die in exile."

## Counterfactual Significance

Gregory's significance is structural as much as personal. By converting the reform
ideal into a doctrine of papal **monarchy** over a unitary Christendom — and by
daring to depose an emperor — he set the terms of the papacy–empire conflict for two
centuries and supplied the ideological foundation of the [[papal-monarchy|high
medieval papacy]]. Whether the institutional papacy would have developed as it did
without his intransigence is the implicit counterfactual: NCMH IV cautions against
reading the *Dictatus* as a finished constitution, and against treating the
investiture quarrel as the whole of reform, but treats Gregory as the indispensable
radicaliser of both.

## From Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

[[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] offers a sharply revisionist personal judgement: Gregory’s twelve-year pontificate was a **failure, “perhaps even a monumental failure.”** He “disturbed the forward progress of reform by picking unnecessary fights with secular rulers” — the perennial scourge of “the priest in politics.” Logan stresses that Gregory’s **irregular popular election** at Alexander II’s funeral inverted Nicholas II’s 1059 electoral decree and later fuelled enemies’ attacks.

On the investiture fight, Logan notes that Gregory **added lay investiture** to the older simony/celibacy agenda and inverted Gelasian dual-key imagery so that both keys pass through the pope, implying power to depose rulers. After delayed support for anti-king **Rudolph of Swabia** only in **1080** (too late), Henry defeated Rudolph, marched on Rome, installed a new pope, and was crowned emperor; a majority of cardinals defected. Gregory fled to Norman support and died at **Salerno 25 May 1085**. Last words, echoing Psalm 44: “I have loved justice, hated iniquity and, therefore, die in exile” — to which Logan adds, “And I die a failure.”

This “failure” thesis sits in deliberate tension with NCMH’s structural reading of Gregory as indispensable radicaliser of the [[papal-monarchy]]; both framings are retained here as positions.
