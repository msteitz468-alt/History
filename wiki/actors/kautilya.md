# Kautilya (Chanakya / Vishnugupta)

**Kautilya** — also known as **Chanakya** and **Vishnugupta** — is the brahmin
statesman and political theorist traditionally credited with engineering the rise of
[[chandragupta-maurya|Chandragupta Maurya]], founding the [[mauryan-empire|Mauryan
Empire]] (c. 321 BCE), and authoring the **[[kautilya-arthashastra|Arthashastra]]**, the
foundational treatise of Indian statecraft. He is the archetype of the
*power-behind-the-throne* in Indian tradition and is often called the **"Indian
Machiavelli"** — though he precedes Machiavelli by some eighteen centuries and his work
is far more comprehensive, encompassing administration, economics, law, and welfare as
well as realpolitik.

## The traditional figure

In tradition (the play *Mudrarakshasa*, Buddhist and Jain sources, and the Puranas),
Chanakya was a brahmin of [[pataliputra|Pataliputra]] humiliated by the last **Nanda**
king. Vowing revenge, he found and trained the young Chandragupta, and through
stratagem, alliance, and war overthrew the Nandas and installed his protégé on the
Magadhan throne, then served as his chief minister (*amatya*) and preceptor, guiding the
new empire "to the heights of empire" and repelling **Seleucus Nikator**. The
partnership of king and minister-strategist is the prototype of the Arthashastra's ideal
relationship between the ruler and his counsel.

## The thinker

Whether or not the historical Chanakya wrote the surviving text (see the dating debate
below), the **Arthashastra** attributed to him is his monument. Its doctrine is the
**secular, pragmatic** conception of politics: the king's task is *artha* (the material
prosperity and security of the state), achieved through *danda* (coercion) wielded with
calculated proportion — neither too harsh (which breeds hatred) nor too lax (which breeds
contempt). His name became a byword for cunning statecraft (*Kautilya* was popularly
glossed as "crookedness," though it more properly derives from his *gotra*). See
**[[kautilyan-statecraft]]** for the theory and **[[raja-mandala]]** for the foreign
policy.

## The authorship and dating debate

Modern scholarship complicates the traditional picture. The Arthashastra was lost for
centuries and only rediscovered in **1905**; text-critical work (notably **Thomas
Trautmann's** statistical study) argues it is a **composite text** reaching final form
in the early centuries CE, not the unitary work of a 4th-century-BCE minister. On this
view "Kautilya" may be a school or a literary persona, and the treatise an ideal-type of
the Indian state rather than a record of Chandragupta's actual government. The figure of
Chanakya thus stands at the uncertain border of history and tradition — but his *role* as
the brahmin architect of the Mauryan state and father of Indian political science is
secure in the cultural memory.

## Significance

Kautilya is the founder of the **realist tradition** in Indian political thought, the
named author of its central text, and — as Chandragupta's minister — the legendary
mind behind South Asia's first great empire. He defines one pole of Indian kingship
(amoral *danda*-based pragmatism), against which the other pole — [[ashoka|Ashoka's]]
Buddhist *dhamma* of righteous, non-violent rule — is the deliberate counterpoint.
