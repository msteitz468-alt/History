## Overview

Eadberht, son of Eata and son of Leodwald, was king of Northumbria from c. 738 to 758 — the last warrior-king of Northumbria before the kingdom's slide into uncontrolled dynastic violence. The brother of Archbishop Ecgberht of York, he reversed the quiescent policies of [[ceolwulf-of-northumbria|Ceolwulf]] and extended his kingdom's frontiers, revived a high-quality silver coinage, and abdicated to become a cleric at York. His reign ended the phase of relative stability and inaugurated the accelerating instability of later eighth-century Northumbria.

## Accession

Kirby corrects the accession date: an eclipse in January 753 is described in the Northumbrian annals as occurring in Eadberht's fifteenth year, and an attack on Dumbarton in 756 is placed in his eighteenth year. Both dates are consistent with accession in early 738 rather than 737. Ceolwulf "resigned the kingship at the very end of 737 and Eadberht's accession occurred in early 738."

## Military Expansion

"In contrast to Ceolwulf, was a warrior-king who reversed the quiescent policies of his immediate predecessors and extended the bounds of his kingdom." In 750 he annexed the plain of Kyle in Ayrshire together with other territories. In 756, in alliance with Óengus son of Fergus, king of the Picts, he besieged Dumbarton — the stronghold of the Britons of Strathclyde. The alliance represented a revival of "seventh-century northern imperial ambition." However, the campaign ended catastrophically: "almost the whole of Eadberht's army perished at Newburgh on the Tyne, perhaps at the hands of the Britons."

**Mercian attack**: While Eadberht was attacking the Picts in 740, [[æthelbald-of-mercia|Æthelbald, king of the Mercians, devastated part of Northumbria]] — a demonstration of Mercian reach that presumably constrained Northumbrian southern ambitions.

## Coinage and Prosperity

The reign saw "a revival (at an unknown date) of a substantial regal silver coinage in Northumbria together with an archiepiscopal coinage combining Eadberht's name with that of his brother, Archbishop Ecgberht, which has been described as 'ahead of its age' in its fineness." Only the Northumbrians at this period, and then only temporarily, minted coins of pure silver; the south was experiencing progressive debasement of sceattas. The co-produced coinage with the archbishop reflects the unusual concentration of power in the Leodwald family.

## Dynastic Violence

Eadberht's reign contained evidence of the continuing rift between the descendants of Oswiu and the Leodwald kings. In 750 he imprisoned Cynewulf, bishop of Lindisfarne, at Bamburgh, and besieged [[aldfrith-of-northumbria|Aldfrith's]] son Offa in Lindisfarne church until "almost dead with hunger, he was dragged from sanctuary." Kirby sees in this episode "a deep and continuing rift between the descendants of Oswiu and the kings of Leodwald's line."

## Abdication and Aftermath

In 758 Eadberht abdicated voluntarily, "handing the kingdom over to his son, Oswulf, and becoming a cleric at York (where he died in 768)." Oswulf was killed within the year by his own household, "replaced by Aethelwald Moll, who is likely to have been associated with those who were responsible for Oswulf's murder." Eadberht's descendants did not recover royal power for twenty years. Kirby interprets Oswulf's overthrow as "the consequence of challenge to the monopoly of royal power by the descendants of Leodwald as dynamic new forces entered the arena of Northumbrian dynastic politics."

## Historiography

Primary sources: the eighth-century Northumbrian annals; the Irish annals; Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. Bede's *Ecclesiastical History* ends in 731, so Eadberht's reign lies entirely beyond Bede. Kirby's chronological corrections are anchored by the eclipse of January 753 (identified in the annals as his fifteenth year) and the Dumbarton attack (his eighteenth year in 756).
