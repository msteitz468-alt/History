## Overview

Nguyen Hue was the second of the three Tay Son brothers and the dynasty's decisive military talent, celebrated in later Vietnamese nationalist historiography as the Quang Trung emperor and as a founding national hero for his victories over Siam (1785) and the Qing Empire (1789). He commanded the campaign that destroyed the Nguyen Phuc royal house at Saigon in 1777, defeated a Siamese invasion fleet at the Battle of Rach Gam-Xoai Mut in 1785, conquered the north and ended [[trinh-lords|Trinh]] rule in 1786 (initially in the name of "restoring the Le"), then proclaimed himself emperor at Phu Xuan and routed a Qing intervention army near Ke Cho at the 1789 lunar new year — the celebrated victory associated with Dong Da/Ngoc Hoi. He died suddenly in 1792 at age 40, reportedly while preparing an ambitious and likely unrealistic plan to invade the Qing provinces of Guangdong and Guangxi.

K. W. Taylor's account is deliberately unromantic about Nguyen Hue's governance: "his brilliance on the battlefield was not matched by his ability to govern... unlike the men who became founders of dynasties, he had no mind for the details of administration." He left the routine business of law and taxation to the same class of scholar-officials who had served the Trinh, built a new capital near Vinh that was never completed, and by Taylor's account drew a steady income chiefly from incorporating Qing pirate fleets into his navy rather than from systematic administration. His decisive break with his elder brother Nguyen Nhac — marching on Ke Cho in 1786 without authorization, then fighting Nguyen Nhac for control of the movement in 1786–1787 — fractured Tay Son unity permanently and, in Taylor's reading, reflects Nguyen Hue's growing absorption into the perspective of northern educated men who saw him as a potential dynasty founder, in contrast to Nguyen Nhac's provincial indifference to ruling beyond Binh Dinh.

## Major Decisions and Consequences

- 1777: led the campaign that captured Saigon and killed nearly the entire Nguyen Phuc royal family, sparing only the fugitive Nguyen Phuc Anh (later [[gia-long|Gia Long]]).
- 1785: destroyed a Siamese rescue fleet supporting Nguyen Phuc Anh at Rach Gam-Xoai Mut, exploiting Siamese unfamiliarity with Mekong tidal currents.
- 1786: without consulting Nguyen Nhac, marched on and took Ke Cho, ending Trinh rule — a decision that both completed the destruction of the old northern regime and permanently split the Tay Son leadership.
- 1789: proclaimed himself emperor at Phu Xuan (reign title Quang Trung) and routed the Qing intervention army sent to restore the Le king, then negotiated a face-saving peace (reportedly sending a body double, Pham Cong Tri, to Qianlong's court in his place in 1790).
- 1790–1792: pursued plans to invade Guangdong and Guangxi rather than move against Nguyen Phuc Anh's rebuilding base at Saigon — a decision Taylor treats as evidence of Nguyen Hue's restless search for new battlefields over sustained governance, and one that left his enemy time to consolidate.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Nguyen Hue lived longer, or focused his energies on destroying Nguyen Phuc Anh's Saigon base rather than dreaming of a Qing campaign, the Tay Son dynasty might have consolidated lasting rule rather than collapsing within a decade of his death. His death in 1792, leaving an 11-year-old heir and a regency riven by generals' feuds, is treated by Taylor as the single most consequential contingency of the whole war — opening the path for [[gia-long|Nguyen Phuc Anh]]'s systematic reconquest.

## Historiography

Sourced from K. W. Taylor, *A History of the Vietnamese* (Cambridge, 2013). Taylor explicitly positions his portrait against mainstream Vietnamese nationalist historiography, which reveres Nguyen Hue/Quang Trung as a founding national hero; Taylor instead reads him as a battlefield genius without administrative vision, whose regime's few institutional innovations (reported use of vernacular Nom in official documents) cannot be verified because Gia Long later destroyed the relevant records. This is a source-reliability caveat worth flagging explicitly: the post-1802 Nguyen dynastic record, hostile to the Tay Son, shapes much of what survives.
