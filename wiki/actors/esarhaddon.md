# Esarhaddon

**Esarhaddon** (reigned 680–669 BCE), younger son and chosen heir of
[[sennacherib|Sennacherib]], pushed Assyrian arms to their **furthest extent —
the conquest of Egypt** — while reversing his father's policy by **rebuilding
Babylon**. His reign, unusually, foregrounds cultural and religious endeavour as
much as war. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 23, A. K.
Grayson) is the authority.

## Accession

Designated heir over elder brothers, Esarhaddon survived their slander and, after
his father's [[sennacherib#Assassination|assassination (681)]], defeated the
rebel brothers in a 42-day civil war and took the throne. His name means "Ashur
has given a brother."

## The conquest of Egypt

The culmination of the western drive begun by [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-
pileser III]]. After an early failure (an Assyrian army was **defeated in Egypt in
674**), Esarhaddon mounted a second invasion: in **671**, with Arab camels
carrying water across the Sinai, he fought three battles and on 22 Du'uzu
**captured Memphis**. **Taharqa**, the [[kush-meroe|Kushite (25th-Dynasty)]]
pharaoh, fled south; his family and crown prince were taken. Esarhaddon appointed
client kings and governors over Egypt — "the high point in Assyria's imperialist
expansion." It proved ephemeral: Egypt was unsubdued, and Esarhaddon **died at
Harran in 669 while marching to reconquer it**, leaving the problem to
[[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]].

## Phoenicia, Anatolia, the steppe

- **Sidon** rebelled and was **destroyed (677/676)**; its king Abdi-milkutti was
  caught "like a fish" and beheaded, his territory partly handed to **Baal of
  Tyre**, who later defected to Taharqa and was besieged in 671. (Phoenician detail
  is treated under [[phoenicians]].)
- He defeated the **[[cimmerians|Cimmerian]]** leader Teushpa (c. 679), the first
  Assyrian check on the steppe raiders ravaging Anatolia.
- On the northern frontier he faced the **Medes, Mannaeans, and the newcoming
  [[scythians|Scythians]]**; the Scythian king **Bartatua (Herodotus's
  Protothyes)** sought an Assyrian princess in marriage. Many **Median** chiefs
  became sworn vassals — the oath-tablets of the **succession treaty of 672**, by
  which the empire swore loyalty to his heirs Ashurbanipal (Assyria) and
  **Shamash-shuma-ukin (Babylon)**, survive in Median copies.

## The rebuilding of Babylon

Esarhaddon's signature reversal of [[sennacherib|Sennacherib's]] sacrilege. He
recast the 689 catastrophe as **Marduk's anger** (cleverly "reading" the god's
70-year sentence as 11 years by transposing two cuneiform signs), rebuilt
**Esagila and Etemenanki** and the city walls, freed and repatriated enslaved
Babylonians, and restored Babylon's privileged status — a policy of appeasement
continued by Ashurbanipal. (Babylonian affairs proper: [[babylonia-iron-age]].)

## Naqia and the substitute king

Two distinctive features:
- His Aramaean mother **Naqia (Zakutu)** wielded unprecedented power in the harem
  and as queen mother, even building a palace and issuing a royal-style
  inscription — a rare documented female power in Assyria.
- The reign repeatedly used the **substitute-king ritual**: when astrologers
  predicted the king's death from an eclipse, a substitute was enthroned for up to
  100 days and then put to death, diverting the omen. It is the best-attested case
  of this ancient Mesopotamian institution.

## Significance

- `produced:` the brief Assyrian conquest of Egypt — the empire at maximum extent
  ("from Susa to Thebes" was realized by him and his son).
- `produced:` the restoration of Babylon, reversing the central error of
  [[sennacherib|Sennacherib's]] reign.
- The **succession arrangement of 672** (dual monarchy for two brothers)
  `contributed_to:` the catastrophic [[ashurbanipal#Elam and Babylonia|Shamash-
  shuma-ukin civil war]] a generation later.

## Historiography

His royal inscriptions are mostly of the uninformative "Display" type, so the
**Babylonian Chronicle** and the **oracle inquiries** (extispicy queries, often
about Cimmerian/Median/Scythian threats) carry much of the historical weight; the
self-serving **Esarhaddon Chronicle** must be read against the more neutral
Babylonian Chronicle. His assassinated father and his own contested accession make
the reign a key case for Assyrian royal-succession politics.
