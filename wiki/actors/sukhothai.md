# Sukhothai

**Sukhothai** was an early **Tai** kingdom in the upper Chao Phraya basin that rose in the mid-thirteenth century from a former [[khmer-empire|Khmer]] outpost and became, with [[ayutthaya|Ayutthaya]], one of the two best-known polities of Late Classic central Thailand. It was the first kingdom in the territory of modern Thailand to use **Tai as its language of state**. Primary archaeological-historical synthesis for the wiki: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]] Ch. 7 (Late Classic).

## Overview

Sukhothai was a main Khmer outpost southeast of Chiang Mai until the **1240s**, when it fell to an alliance of Thai chiefs. Bang Klang Hao became the first Thai king; his son **Ramkhamhaeng** reigned **1279–1298**. Under Ramkhamhaeng the [[mandala|mandala]] grew to include Sawankhalok, Uttaradit, Kamphaengphet, and Tak. The famous **1292 inscription** calls him “sovereign of all the Tai” and has been treated—especially after 1767 Bangkok-dynasty nationalism—as marking the “birth of the Thai nation.” By the end of the Late Classic, central Thais had become overlords of the former Khmer mandala; Sukhothai and Ayutthaya were the two best-known kingdoms of that transition.

[[jayavarman-vii|Jayavarman VII]]’s authority had earlier reached as far north as Sukhothai—so the mid-thirteenth-century Thai seizure sits on a landscape already integrated into Angkor’s outer orbit.

## Ramkhamhaeng and the 1292 Inscription

The 1292 text describes walled Sukhothai; royal support of **Theravada** Buddhism, including appointment of a **Sangharaja** from Sri Dharmaraja (Tambralingga / Nakhon Si Thammarat)—“clearly stated… most knowledgeable in the Buddhist teachings in Sukhothai”; the Aranyik / Wat Saphan Hin complex; a five-storey pyramid centre; and two 18-cubit Buddha images. Betty Gosling argues the “golden” images were **stucco**; bronze-casting appears only about fifty years later.

Ramkhamhaeng conducted a biweekly ritual from throne to the sacred hill **Wat Saphan Hin**—cited by Miksic and Goh as parallel to Srivijayan *siddhayātra* hill ritual, an analogy of practice rather than political descent.

Relations with northern [[pagan-kingdom|Bagan]] shaped policy and art: some Bagan–northern Thai artistic traits were appropriated by thirteenth-century Sukhothai art; Ramkhamhaeng’s social-welfare policies reflected “ancient Tai political traditions and adapted the utopian Buddhist visions of King Kyanzittha.”

## Contraction under Lo Thai and Lu Thai

**Lo Thai** (1298–1346/7) lacked the prowess to hold the vast mandala; by **1320** it had contracted to a small area. Lo Thai and **Lu Thai** (1346/7–1374) were strong Theravada patrons; a distinct art style emerged under them. Lu Thai produced the *Traibhūmikathā* / *Trai Phum Phra Ruang* in **1345** as viceroy of Si Satchanalai (date contested by Michael Vickery; the cosmological concept remains important in fourteenth-century thought).

What has come to be known as the classic **Sukhothai walking Buddha** style is characteristic of **late fourteenth-century** Sukhothai. Gosling questions whether a coherent Sukhothai artistic style existed in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries; Lan Na may have influenced fourteenth-century art.

## Ceramics and Economy

Sukhothai was a major ceramic centre **1300–1450** (underglaze brown and white; five-spurred pontils). Chaliang produced brown glaze in the eleventh–fourteenth centuries; Sawankhalok green-glazed wares; high-quality products were exported in the Postclassic. Martaban jars later became an important trade commodity—linking the kingdom to wider [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]] and South China Sea markets.

After the Early Classic, coinage had disappeared in the old [[dvaravati|Dvaravati]] zone; Thais preferred cowries, and Sukhothai inscriptions present the king as sanctified centre of a redistributive network rather than a minting sovereign.

## Subordination to Ayutthaya

Ayutthaya’s rise from **1351** threatened weakened Sukhothai. Borommaracha I (1370–1388) campaigned northward; by **1378** Sukhothai reluctantly acknowledged subordination; in **1412** an Ayutthayan chief resident was installed; the capital moved to Phitsanulok in **1430**; in **1438** Sukhothai became a province of Ayutthaya. Causal link: **caused_by** Ayutthayan military-political expansion acting on a mandala already contracted after Ramkhamhaeng—not mere temporal succession.

## Historiography

The 1292 inscription’s authenticity and national-myth loading are among the most sensitive debates in Thai history (beyond Miksic and Goh’s brief; they report the nationalist reading). Gosling’s revision of early Sukhothai art chronology and Vickery’s challenge to the 1345 *Traibhumi* date exemplify source criticism. Miksic and Goh place Sukhothai firmly in a Late Classic frame of Classic-culture disintegration plus urban and ceramic growth, not as an isolated “Thai dawn.” Collection coverage for Sukhothai is moderate in this source relative to Angkor or Java.
