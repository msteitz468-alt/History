# Comanches

The **Comanches** (self-name *Nermernuh* / *Nʉmʉnʉʉ*, "the People"; Ute-derived exonym *Koh-mats* / *Komantcia*, "anyone who is against me all the time") were the dominant mounted military and political power of the southern Great Plains from the early eighteenth century until U.S. military and ecological destruction of their independence in 1874–75. Their sphere of control — [[places/comancheria|Comanchería]] — stretched across the high plains of present-day Texas, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Kansas, and Colorado, with the [[places/llano-estacado|Llano Estacado]] and canyon systems such as [[places/palo-duro-canyon|Palo Duro]] as strategic core. Gwynne frames them as the only peer in acreage among plains peoples to the western Sioux, and as having caused more havoc under successive Spanish, French, Mexican, Texan, and American occupations than any other tribe of the region.

## Overview

Comanche power rested on three interlocking systems: the **horse** (remount herds of exceptional size and quality; true breeding of geldings preferred), the **buffalo** economy of the southern plains herds, and a **raiding-trading complex** that extracted captives, livestock, and goods from Spanish/Mexican frontiers while supplying horses and buffalo products into wider networks (including Comanchero middlemen after the 1780s Anza peace). Band structure was fluid; no single permanent capital or bureaucratic state existed, but coordinated war power and territorial exclusion of rivals — especially Apache groups driven south and west in the eighteenth century — produced what Gwynne and specialists call an **empire of the summer moon**: seasonal, mobile, and lethal.

Major bands and leaders in the nineteenth-century crisis include [[actors/buffalo-hump|Buffalo Hump]] (Penateka), [[actors/peta-nocona|Peta Nocona]] (Nokoni/Quahadi orbit), and [[actors/parker-quanah|Quanah Parker]] (Quahadi war leader; later principal reservation chief). Spiritual-political mobilization under [[actors/isa-tai|Isa-tai]] helped trigger the 1874 war that ended free life on the plains. Gwynne’s narrative arc treats the Parker family as bookends of Comanche–U.S. history: the kidnapping of [[actors/parker-cynthia-ann|Cynthia Ann Parker]] (1836) and Quanah’s 1875 surrender.

## Origins and Horse Transformation

Per Gwynne’s synthesis: pre-horse Comanches were a Shoshonean people of the high country (Wind River / modern Wyoming above the Arkansas headwaters) — short, dark mountain people, descendants of deeper Bering migrations; culture extremely simple by later plains standards (no agriculture, pottery, fixed houses, warrior societies, priest class, or Sun Dance); roughly ~5,000 people with dog-travois mobility, driven into the Rockies as relatively weak foot warriors. Late 1600s southward migration sought the southern plains buffalo. First Spanish record: July 1706 (Ulibarri reporting Comanches with Utes preparing to attack Taos).

Horses were absent in the Americas until the Spanish sixteenth century. Oñate brought ~700 horses to New Mexico (1598); Apaches adapted to mounted raiding by the 1650s; the 1680 Pueblo Revolt drove the Spanish out for a decade and triggered the Great Horse Dispersal of feral Iberian mustangs (small, ~14 hands, desert-adapted — ideal for arid plains). Spanish law banned native riding, but ranch-labor knowledge transmitted the skill. By ~1625–1750 Comanches transformed from peripheral mountain people into the dominant plains power. Gwynne’s comparative frame: Comanches as mounted archers comparable to Mongols, Parthians, and Magyars; the first true horse Indians most Texans ever faced (no soldier or settler east of the Mississippi had fought mounted Native warriors of this type).

## Band Structure

Not a single tribe with a head chief: **band system only**, no national institutions. Whites repeatedly mistook camp headmen for “tribal” spokesmen — a structural cause of failed treaties. Gwynne’s five major bands c. 1800 (counts varied: Spanish thought three; Neighbors in 1860 thought eight; up to thirteen named):

| Band | Meaning / role (Gwynne) |
|---|---|
| **Yamparika** | “Yap Eaters”; northern border fights vs Cheyennes/Arapahoes |
| **Kotsoteka** | “Buffalo Eaters”; main raiders of Spanish New Mexican settlements |
| **Penateka** | “Honey Eaters”; largest/southernmost; drove Lipan Apaches into Mexican borderlands; first Texan battles; closest white contact |
| **Nokoni** | “Wanderers”; attacked Parker’s Fort (1836) |
| **Quahadi** (Quahada) | “Antelopes”; Quanah’s band; last free fighters vs U.S. Army |

Nation at zenith estimated ~20,000. Dual civil/war chiefs with limited power; **anyone** could lead a raid as war chief by recruiting followers. Peace with Kiowas (1790) and Wichitas opened multipolar alliances and French Louisiana trade; by mid-nineteenth century Kiowa–Comanche joint camping, hunting, and raiding was routine (Kiowa + Plains Apache never >~1,800 vs Comanche peak numbers).

### Quahadis

Gwynne’s most detailed band portrait: most remote, “primitive,” and irredeemably hostile; hunted the southernmost high plains of Comanchería; shunned Anglo contact, preferred Mexican **Comanchero** traders; first appear in ethnographies only in **1872**; largely avoided the cholera plagues of 1816 and 1849 that Gwynne says destroyed fully half of all Comanches; **virtually alone among North American bands never signed a treaty** until 1875 surrender; richest plains band in horses (~**15,000** after the Civil War) plus “Texas cattle without number.” Preferred camps: [[places/palo-duro-canyon|Palo Duro]], headwaters of the Pease and McClellan’s Creek, Blanco Canyon — within ~100 miles of present Amarillo. After Red River War they were the last major free southern-plains band; by 1874 Quahadi culture/vernacular was beginning to dominate a dissolving multi-band structure.

### Penateka collapse (1840s)

Penatekas were largest and most powerful band and the one in closest constant white contact; northern bands stayed more aloof on the high plains. Proximity brought cultural “pollution” (Spanish/English, cloth, metal, scavenged trade goods) and progressive dependence as buffalo left southern ranges. Within a decade of peak power at the Parker’s Fort moment: Lamar-era battlefield losses (~¼ of fighting men 1836–40), game collapse, and epidemic disease (smallpox 1816 from Mexico + syphilis; smallpox 1839 via Kiowas/Mandan; cholera 1849 from Forty-niner trails on the Canadian) reduced Penateka from ~**8,000 to ~2,000** in <30 years (Gwynne’s soft estimate); half of still-surviving Penateka died in 1849 cholera; major camp headmen died; Pah-hah-yuco withdrew north; Buffalo Hump chosen without real common leadership; early 1850s many starving.

## Military and Social Practice

- **Mounted warfare:** Only Comanches and Kiowas among plains tribes fought fully mounted; only Comanches systematically bred horses. A warrior might keep 100–200 mounts, a chief 1,500 (vs a Sioux chief ~40). They could loose ~20 arrows in the time of one musket load, lethal at ~30 yards; Catlin recorded the hanging-sideways riding stratagem. Navigation: war parties planned routes with sand maps; Dodge’s account (via Gwynne) of teenage raiders riding Brady’s Creek (near San Angelo) to Monterrey 350+ miles without a wrong turn.
- **War as social system:** After Apache conquest, war became the core status-producing activity (scalps, captives, horses) — Gwynne’s “Spartan” comparison. Inter-Indian no-quarter warfare (Lehmann Apache fight; Blackfeet clash reclaiming 3,000 horses) largely unseen by whites preceded the same ethos against frontier farmers.
- **Raid logic (Gwynne, full fidelity):** men killed; captive men tortured to death; captive women gang-raped, some killed/tortured, some young spared; babies invariably killed; preadolescents often adopted; same practices on rival Indians. Parker’s Fort (1836) judged a success — no Indian casualties, five ransomable captives. Revenge after Council House (1840) included roasting and butchering thirteen American captives (per captive Booker Webster), including Matilda Lockhart’s six-year-old sister. Lehmann’s 1870s account of Comanche torture of wounded Tonkawas after finding them roasting a Comanche leg: scalped, limbs amputated, tongues cut out, living and dead piled on a campfire while dancing.
- **Tactical generalization (Gwynne):** plains Indians almost never pressed advantage by accepting heavy casualties to take a position (unlike Euro-American assaults); this reluctance “saved countless thousands of white lives” (e.g. Col. John Moore’s 1839 foot-retreat force could have been annihilated).
- **Camp personality (Dodge via Gwynne):** noisy, jolly, gamblers, singers, horse racers, vain about hair, doted on sons; buffalo central for food/lodging/fuel/tools/weapons; did not eat buffalo heart; might drink blood if short of water. Rachel Plummer’s proto-ethnography: move every 3–4 days; men danced nightly; some worshipped pet crows/deerskins; pre-battle morning vomiting of water; taboo on human shadow across cooking food.
- **Moral/cultural frame (Gwynne’s interpretive thesis — attribute, do not adopt as wiki voice):** no unified single-God religion; *puha*/medicine from spirits in nature; no absolute good/evil as Europeans defined it — actions and consequences; enemy torture as millennia-old reciprocal norm (Ute↔Comanche); blood vengeance; Gwynne’s “time warp” / “low-barbarian” comparison to Celts of the 5th c. BC.

## Role and Structure

- **Military:** light cavalry unmatched on the southern plains for most of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; hit-and-run raiding, village mobility, mastery of canyon and llano geography. By 1750 few tribes dared the southern plains without Comanche leave.
- **Political:** multi-band confederative practice; war chiefs rose by prowess and following. Treaties with “the Comanche nation” (e.g. Houston 1844 with only Old Owl and Buffalo Hump) were structurally meaningless for free northern bands.
- **Economic:** buffalo hunt; horse breeding and trade; captive incorporation and ransom; Comanchero guns/whiskey for livestock/hides/captives; later reservation cattle and allotment politics. Hide economics (Gwynne): individual might eat ~6 buffalo/year but kill ~44 and rising; more wives → more hide production → polygamy pressure and demand for female captives.
- **Apache wars (1706–1760s):** Spanish watched Apachería collapse as Comanches destroyed half a dozen major Apache bands from NM mountains to Kansas/Oklahoma plains to the Nueces; 1724 Jicarilla raid carried off half the women (69 members left); 1724 nine-day battle El Gran Cierro de La Ferro (Wichita River, SW Oklahoma) vanquished Lipans from southern plains (per Texas gov. Cabello); by 1748 sweep largely complete; 1760s driving Apaches across the Rio Grande into Mexico.
- **End of independence:** commercial hide-hunter slaughter of the southern herd + Mackenzie’s winter campaigns ([[events/red-river-war-1874|Red River War]]) → surrender to [[places/fort-sill|Fort Sill]] agency system (1875). Late summer 1874 force balance per Gwynne: ~3,000 Comanches left (~2,000 on reservation; ~1,000 untamed with ≤300 fighters) plus ~1,000 untamed Southern Cheyenne and comparable Kiowa/Kiowa Apache hostiles — ~3,000 hostiles / ≤800 warriors — hit by ~46 companies / ~3,000 U.S. troops, the largest force Gwynne says was ever sent against Native Americans.

## Nineteenth-Century Crisis and Reservation

- **Anglo ignorance long persisted:** Lewis and Clark knew Comanches only by hearsay (“Padouca”); 1821 Santa Fe Trail ~80 wagons/year; 1832 Houston failed peace trip; 1834 Dodge’s 250 dragoons + Catlin observed horsemanship; even 1852–53 Marcy Red River expedition treated core Comanche country as unexplored.
- **Disease:** 1816 and 1849 cholera (and recurrent smallpox) gutted southern bands; Quahadis largely escaped.
- **Civil War vacuum:** wild Comanches (sometimes with reservation Penatekas) raided Chickasaws, Choctaws, Creeks, Wichita farmers after U.S. troops withdrew 1861; ~100 Tonkawas killed 1862. Spring 1865: Gwynne’s “old power” returned with maybe ~4,000 still holding the advance of settlement; Quahadis thrived as cattle clearinghouse — Charles Goodnight estimated **300,000** head rustled during Civil War years, traded via Comancheros to NM contractors/U.S. Army (Carleton), sometimes selling the army its own cattle for revolvers/carbines.
- **Kit Carson at Adobe Walls (1864):** federal force from Santa Fe nearly destroyed by Comanches (distinct from [[events/second-adobe-walls-1874|Second Adobe Walls 1874]]).
- **Medicine Lodge / Peace Policy failure:** farming program futile — men refused planting, plundered demonstration fields. 30 June 1869: ~916 Comanches on reservation, none self-supporting farmers; seasonal winter-on-reservation / spring-to-plains-or-Texas-raids pattern. “Comanches remained Comanches” (Gwynne). Lawrie Tatum found ~two-thirds off-reservation 1869; reservation as sanctuary (troops barred) for Texas raids across the Red River.
- **North Fork (1872) and identity crisis:** multi-band prisoner mix (34 Kotsoteka, 30 Quahadi, 18 Yamparika, 11 Nokoni, 9 Penateka) showed structures dissolving; Mackenzie ordered ~3,000 captured horses shot after night stampedes recovered most of them — thereafter wild captured herds routinely destroyed. Winter 1873–74: little game, failed rations, forced slaughter of own horses/mules for food; white horse thieves and whiskey peddlers unpunished. Reservation system as “shattering”: combined Comanche-Kiowa annuities ~$30,000/year (~$10/person); beef issue assumptions failed in winter; warriors ran down ration cows with bows and pistols as pathetic diversion. By 1874 bands disintegrating/merging; white material goods even among Quahadis; adoption of Cheyenne feathered headdress (incl. Quanah), scaffold burials, later Kiowa Sun Dance; reservation Comanches (~1,000 drawing Fort Sill annuities) already lost hunter identity — “a form of slavery” in Gwynne’s phrasing of the loss.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Comanche military dominance, Spanish and Mexican settlement of the southern plains and Texas frontier would likely have advanced earlier and deeper; the Republic of Texas and early U.S. Texas would not have faced the same decades-long “open wound” of raiding. Conversely, without the post–Civil War U.S. Army’s logistical reach and the hide trade’s ecological shock, Comanche autonomy might have persisted longer as a negotiated borderland power rather than collapsing in a few seasons (1874–75). Gwynne’s structural claim: Comanches stopped Spanish northward advance in the eighteenth century and, post–Civil War, rolled the Anglo frontier back on a larger scale (e.g. Wise County population 3,160 → 1,450, 1860–70; settlements driven back a hundred miles in places).

## Historiography

Popular narrative (Gwynne 2010; Fehrenbach earlier) stresses martial prowess and mutual atrocity, often in civilizational-contrast language that academic ethnohistory rejects. Academic revision (esp. Pekka Hämäläinen, *The Comanche Empire*, 2008 — not yet ingested) reframes Comanchería as an **indigenous empire** reshaping the Southwest borderlands, not merely a barrier to white expansion. Ethnographic synthesis: Thomas W. Kavanagh; U.S.–Comanche policy: William T. Hagan. Wiki pages derived from Gwynne should be read as **narrative entry points** with attributed interpretive theses — not final ethnography. Gwynne’s moral “time warp” framing and absolute rankings (“most dominant and influential tribe in American history”) are authorial, not scholarly consensus.

### Fowler, CHNPA I.2 ch. 9 (append — preserve Gwynne narrative above)

Loretta Fowler’s Plains chapter places Comanches within multiethnic expansion rather than as a standalone Texas story:

- **Preeminence:** became preeminent raiders of Southwest settlements, displacing Apaches early eighteenth century, and dominated upland Plains below the Platte until the 1830s; traded horses/mules to Caddoans and after 1740s directly to French for goods including firearms; **by 1750 horse trading was probably more important to the Comanche economy than bison hunting.**
- **Diplomacy and livestock industry:** played Spanish–French rivalry—trading hides/captives for livestock in Spanish/Pueblo settlements and stealing when trade inconvenient; trading captives/stock to Caddoan allies and French for arms Spain forbade; displaced Kiowas from upper Platte 1730–1770 then made peace **1790** and allied to block northern nomads; together controlled southern uplands between Arkansas and Cimarron. After American settlement in Texas, raided deep into Mexico for stock traded in Texas—a **livestock industry crucial to regional economies and Comanche prosperity until Americans no longer needed the trade.**
- **1820s pressure:** Cheyennes and Arapahos drove Kiowas, Kiowa-Apaches, and Comanches **south of the Arkansas**; thereafter Comanches and Kiowas operated south of Arkansas amid Canadian River emigrant traffic and Texas settlement, with treaty provisions often undelivered, making cattle/supply raiding necessary from their viewpoint; Texas Rangers and federal troops retaliated late 1850s.
- **Southern war to confinement (Fowler does not name “Red River War” or Adobe Walls):** Comanches and Kiowas camped near agency for issues then left to raid when supplies ran out; sporadic Army engagements 1868–1875; 1870s commercial hide hunters with repeating rifles (meat left to rot) became attack targets; federal **winter campaigns** destroyed winter-camp provisions; by **1875 hunger drove ~2,500 Comanches and Kiowas** back to their reservation between the Canadian and Red Rivers, **this time to stay**.
- **1865/1867 reservation agreements** with Southern Arapahos, some Cheyennes, Comanches, and Kiowas ([[events/medicine-lodge-treaty-1867]])—government renegotiated in 1867 when it realized it could not keep 1865 terms.
- **Horse-source geography:** peoples closest to Spanish frontier horses (Comanches) and Plateau traders (Crows) were better supplied than northerly Crees/Assiniboines.
- **Southwest chapter cross-ref (range 02):** mounted Comanches with French guns displaced Eastern Apaches; San Saba destruction; Anza peace then continued ascendancy into Mexico; commercial buffalo hunting increased American enmity on Santa Fe Trail; Republic of Texas and U.S. campaigns ended with Quanah’s Quahadi surrender.

Fowler names no individual Comanche leaders in ch. 9 body (Quanah only in related SW narrative). Pair Gwynne’s operational detail with Fowler’s regional political-economy frame.
