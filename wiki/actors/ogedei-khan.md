# Ögödei Khan (Ogodei)

## Overview

**Ögödei** (Ogodei; c. 1186–1241), third son of [[genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]] and [[borte|Börte]], was the second Great Khan of the [[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] (r. 1229–1241). Designated successor as the fraternal compromise after [[jochi|Jochi]]–[[chagatai-khan|Chagatai]] rivalry, he consolidated the imperial capital at [[karakorum|Karakorum]], expanded commercial and postal infrastructure, and oversaw campaigns that destroyed the Jin and struck deep into Europe (1241). His death on 11 December 1241, “reportedly in a drunken stupor,” pulled European armies home for the succession once news crossed ~4,000 miles in four to six weeks.

## Role

Third son chosen over the elder Jochi and Chagatai; known in Persian and European sources for open-handedness, heavy drinking, and administrative patronage. Childhood tradition: Börte treated young Ögödei with Avarga spring water for disease—later site of the Yellow Palace warehouses. Post-1206 he received only ~5,000 followers (with [[tolui|Tolui]] and mother)—less than friends’ full *tümens*. Regency after his death passed to widow [[toregene-khatun|Töregene]].

## Key events and processes (Weatherford 2004)

- **Succession compromise.** Chagatai proposed “the mellow, good-natured, hard-drinking Ogodei”; Jochi endorsed rather than face war. Genghis separated the elder brothers’ camps.
- **1229 inauguration at Avarga.** Emptied his father’s treasury (pearls, silks, color-coded court dress, continuous drinking); family took the name Golden Family/Golden Lineage. Juvaini (via Weatherford): Ögödei “was ever spreading the carpet of merrymaking… in constant appreciation to wine and the company of beautiful women.” Drunken revelry “set the standard and the model for his rule.”
- **Karakorum.** Abandoned mobile rule for a permanent capital on the Orkhon in former Kereyid/Turkic lands (his territory; Onon-Kherlen to Tolui). Convinced—contrary to his father—that “a kingdom conquered on horseback could not be ruled on horseback.” Chinese architects; warehouse/workshop function; multi-faith worship houses; daily free distribution of merchandise (Rashid al-Din). Weatherford’s **thesis** (not wiki assertion): dismounting at stone walls began a four-decade co-optation from mounted warriors toward sedentary court “decadence.”
- **Commercial policy.** Paid merchants high prices (often 2× asking or asking +10%), standardized weights and measures, paper-money exchanges, permanent road garrisons, abolition of complex local taxes/extortion, trees and stone pillars on roads (Juvaini)—institutional layer of the [[pax-mongolica|Pax Mongolica]].
- **By 1235** had squandered most of his father’s wealth; new conquest required for survival—leading to the dual-front wars and the European campaign under Batu guided by [[subedei|Sübe’etei]] ([[mongol-invasion-of-europe-1241]]). Mutual dislike/mistrust with Sübe’etei; Jochi’s line (Batu) strongly backed the Europe plan; Ögödei sent Güyük as “least-favorite and most-annoying son.”
- **Banquet quarrel.** When news of Batu/Güyük/Büri quarrel reached him he threatened to execute Güyük—“May he rot like an egg!”—reprimanded him for breaking army spirit, then sent the princes back to Europe.
- **Tolui’s widow.** After Tolui’s death tried to annex Toluid lands via Güyük–Sorghaghtani marriage; [[sorghaghtani-beki|Sorghaghtani]] refused.
- **Late reign.** Often too drunk to lead; conveyed administration to Töregene. Women administered much of the empire outside the heavy European fighting (Weatherford). Nominated a grandson as successor; Töregene blocked that path for Güyük.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Ögödei’s election and support for dual-front war, the 1241 European strike and final destruction of Jin are hard to imagine on the same scale; the empire’s administrative “settling” at Karakorum is largely his. His death is a classic contingency that froze the European campaign.

## Historiography

Weatherford (*Making of the Modern World*, 2004) emphasizes generosity, alcohol, Karakorum’s sedentarization, and the civilizational shock of the European campaign—flag modernity/“decadence” language as thesis. Persian chroniclers (Juvaini) and the *Secret History* supply the core narrative; specialist treatments in [[morgan-the-mongols|Morgan]] and [[may-mongol-conquests-2012|May]]. After Möngke’s rise, Karakorum and surrounding territory were seized from Ögödei’s descendants amid a bloody purge of the Ögödeid lineage.
