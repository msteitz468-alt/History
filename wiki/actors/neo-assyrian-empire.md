# Neo-Assyrian Empire

## Overview

The **Neo-Assyrian Empire** was the **first true territorial empire of antiquity**
([[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|Daryaee, CWH IV Ch 10]]) — the "Mesopotamian core" that, in
the [[early-iron-age|Early Iron Age]], converted recovery from the
[[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age collapse]] into systematic conquest. Growing from the Middle Assyrian
state (Tukulti-Ninurta I, Ashur-Dan I) through the consolidating reigns of
**Ashurnasirpal II** and **Shalmaneser III** (9th c.), it reached its apogee under
the late-8th- and 7th-century "conqueror-builder kings" — **Tiglath-Pileser III,
Sargon II, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, and Ashurbanipal** — stretching at its height
"from Susa in Iran to Thebes in Egypt," an Afro-Asiatic empire that confronted the
nascent Greek states emerging from their own Dark Age.

## Foundation and Ninth-Century Consolidation (CAH III.1)

[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] (Ch. 6, A. K. Grayson) supplies the
detailed **foundation narrative** that the CWH IV overview compresses — the climb
from the tenth-century nadir to the threshold of the imperial age (934–745 BCE):

- **The nadir and the founder.** After [[tiglath-pileser-i|Tiglath-pileser I]],
  Assyria had been driven into a tenth-century "eclipse" by the **[[arameans|Aramaean]]
  influx** that overran Syria and the Jezirah. Grayson dates the **birth of the
  Neo-Assyrian Empire** to **Ashur-dan II (934–912)**, the first king in a century to
  campaign regularly, who "regained territory lost during Assyria's eclipse" and
  repatriated Assyrians who had fled famine. Adad-nirari II (911–891) and
  Tukulti-Ninurta II (890–884) extended the recovery.
- **Ashurnasirpal II (883–859) — the first "great" king.** Fourteen-plus campaigns,
  the first real **provincial administration** (fortified supply depots with fixed
  tribute), a calculated reputation for **terror** (graphically recorded atrocities),
  and the wholesale rebuilding of **Calah (Nimrud)** as the new capital — a walled
  city with the North-West Palace, its rooms lined with inscribed reliefs, dedicated
  at a banquet for tens of thousands whose menu was inscribed on a stela. He reached
  the Mediterranean and washed his weapons in the sea. Grayson flags two future
  threats already "burgeoning in this reign": **Babylonia and Urartu**.
- **Shalmaneser III (858–824).** Thirty-four campaigns over a 35-year reign; annexed
  **Bit-Adini** as Assyria's first trans-Euphrates province (Til-Barsib →
  Kar-Shalmaneser). His decade-long struggle with a **central-Syrian coalition** under
  **Adad-idri (Hadadezer) of Damascus** and Irhuleni of Hamath climaxed at the
  **[[battle-of-qarqar-853bce|battle of Qarqar (853 BCE)]]** — an indecisive clash whose
  inscription gives history its **first mention of [[kingdom-of-israel|Ahab of Israel]]**
  and of an Arab (Gindibu, with 1,000 camels). The coalition dissolved only when
  **[[hazael|Hazael]]** usurped Damascus; the **Black Obelisk** then depicts
  **[[kingdom-of-israel|Jehu of Israel]]** paying tribute (841) — the only contemporary
  image of an Israelite king.
- **Decline into the "Interval" (782–745).** Shamshi-Adad V and his queen
  **Sammuramat** (the historical kernel of the legendary **Semiramis**, regent for the
  young Adad-nirari III) preceded a reign — **Adad-nirari III (810–783)** — whose
  outward successes (the fall of Damascus, tribute from Israel/Joash) masked a fatal
  internal shift: power devolved onto **over-mighty provincial governors** (Nergal-erish;
  the *turtānu* **Shamshi-ilu**) who erected royal-style stelae of their own. Under the
  three weak kings of the **Interval** — Shalmaneser IV, Ashur-dan III, Ashur-nirari V —
  Assyria shrank, plague and revolt spread, and **[[urartu|Urartu]] under Sarduri II**
  became the dominant power in the north. (The eclipse of **15 June 763 BCE**, recorded
  in the Eponym Chronicle, is the anchor that fixes all Assyrian dates to the modern
  calendar.) The recovery under **[[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]]** from 745
  — the true beginning of the imperial age — is treated in CAH III.2.

## The Sargonid Zenith (CAH III.2)

[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Chs. 21–25, Grayson/Brinkman/Oates)
narrates the empire's hundred-year climax and sudden fall through the five great
reigns. Grayson's striking framing: the "Assyrian empire" in the full sense
**flourished for little more than a century** — only from [[tiglath-pileser-iii|
Tiglath-pileser III]] did Assyria *enforce* control rather than merely raid — and
its "fall" was less a collapse "like that of the Third Dynasty of Ur or of the
Roman empire" than a **transfer of power from Nineveh to Babylon**, an "adjacent
and ethnically related kingdom" whose administration and geography stayed largely
intact.

- **[[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]] (744–727)** — usurper and
  re-founder; broke [[urartu|Urartu]], conquered the Levant to the Egyptian border,
  destroyed Aram-Damascus (732), took the Babylonian crown (729), and institutional­
  ized the **provincial system** and **two-way [[assyrian-mass-deportation|mass
  deportation]]**.
- **[[sargon-ii|Sargon II]] (721–705)** — founder of the **Sargonid dynasty**;
  completed the [[fall-of-samaria-722bce|destruction of Samaria]], annexed
  [[neo-hittite-states|Carchemish]] (717), broke Urartu in the famous **eighth
  campaign (714)**, made peace with **[[phrygians|Midas]]**, built **Dur-Sharrukin**
  — and died on the battlefield (705), his body never recovered.
- **[[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] (704–681)** — besieged
  [[sennacherib-siege-jerusalem-701bce|Jerusalem (701)]], **destroyed Babylon
  (689)** in a fateful act of sacrilege, rebuilt **[[nineveh|Nineveh]]** as the
  imperial metropolis; assassinated by his sons.
- **[[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]] (680–669)** — **conquered Egypt** (Memphis 671),
  rebuilt Babylon, destroyed Sidon, checked the [[cimmerians|Cimmerians]].
- **[[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]] (668–c.631)** — last great king; **sacked
  [[sack-of-thebes-663bce|Thebes]] (663)**, won the pyrrhic civil war against his
  brother Shamash-shuma-ukin (652–648), **destroyed Susa and Elam** (647–646), and
  assembled the **Library of [[nineveh|Nineveh]]**. The destruction of the Elamite
  buffer `enabled:` the rise of the Medes and Persians who, with the Chaldaeans,
  would bring the empire down a generation later (see [[fall-of-nineveh-612bce]]).

This century of conquest is treated thematically in [[assyrian-civilization]]
(monarchy, army, deportation, religion, the libraries).

## Territorial Extent and Governing Structure

At peak the empire controlled Mesopotamia, the Levant, much of Anatolia, western
Iran, and (briefly) Egypt. It pioneered the administrative repertoire of later
[[empire|empires]]: provincial organization, a standing and increasingly
professional army, royal annals as propaganda, and monumental capital-building —
successive new capitals at **Kalhu (Nimrud)**, **Dur-Sharrukin (Khorsabad)**, and
finally **[[nineveh|Nineveh]]** (Esarhaddon), with their celebrated palace reliefs
(see [[art-and-power]]).

## Mass Deportation

Its signature instrument of control was **systematic [[assyrian-mass-deportation|mass
deportation]]** — uprooting conquered populations (Israelites, Arameans,
Babylonians) to clear hostile regions and to supply skilled and menial labor where
the empire needed it. Begun in earnest by [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser
III]] as a **two-way exchange** of whole populations (some half a million removed
from Babylonia alone, 745–702), it was inherited by the
[[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonians]] (the "Babylonian captivity" of the Jews)
and shaped the demographic map of the Near East — while leaving the empire a legacy
of disloyal subject peoples that hastened its own [[fall-of-nineveh-612bce|fall]].
The full institutional system is treated in [[assyrian-civilization]].

## Mechanisms of Decline

CWH IV reads the collapse as **structural over-extension**: the agricultural base of
the Assyrian heartland could no longer feed gigantic capitals (Nineveh, Kalhu,
Dur-Sharrukin), forcing unreliable food imports; the military class outgrew the
local population, filled with Cimmerian and **Median** mercenaries who came to
control the officer corps; the **Aramean** rural population spread restively; and
the destruction of **Susa** (Ashurbanipal's invasion of Elam) was "the straw that
broke the camel's back." A coalition of the **Chaldean** Nabopolassar of Babylon and
**Cyaxares the Mede** sacked Kalhu and then **Nineveh in 612 BCE**, ending the empire
(last stand at Harran, 609 BCE). Compare the comparative collapse analysis in
[[empire]].

## Liverani: structure, ideology, and the secular state

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] analyses the empire as a system. The
9th-century "revival" was **reconsolidation, not expansion** — recovering land framed as
always Assyria's, treating resistance as "rebellion." The decisive structural change was
**Tiglath-pileser III's provincial reform**, converting the old two-tier order (the inner
*māt Aššur* under governors, the outer *nīr Aššur*, "yoke of Assyria," of tribute-paying
vassals with only a resident *qēpu*) into uniform **provinces** with Assyrian palaces,
garrisons, cults, and target-based extraction (*iškaru* quotas; *biltu/maddattu* and
*nāmurtu* tribute, fossil terms of the old gift-exchange). **Mass deportation** (royal
inscriptions total well over a million quantified deportees) served three functions —
repopulating a war-thinned Assyrian core, harvesting skilled labour, and erasing conquered
identities by "crossover" resettlement — but only *displaced* the agrarian crisis to the
periphery, producing (Liverani argues) a "genetic mutation" of the Assyrian population that
explains its sudden disappearance after the fall. The **army** was essentially a corvée host
(one campaign a year straining the farmers), run by palace-titled grandees and loyalty-bound
eunuchs.

Ideology he breaks into four **dualisms** (space, time, humanity, resources): an ordered
civilised centre against a chaotic periphery of "monocultures," conquest reframed as the
**civilising completion of the gods' creation**, raw materials repaid with "protection,
justice, and order." The **celebrative system** addressed three audience circles (scribes /
city crowds / distant villagers) through matched channels, rewriting the past "the way
totalitarian regimes do"; palace reliefs intimidated foreign envoys. Statecraft ran on a
dual human/divine intelligence system in which **astrology** (*Enūma Anu Enlil*) became
supreme and the **substitute-king ritual** absorbed bad omens — yet Liverani insists the
state was fundamentally **secular**, the divine "a hypostasis of Assyrian kingship." Two
unsolved problems doomed it: **Babylonia** (too prestigious to provincialise) and a
7th-century **saturation point** (no easy conquests left; Egypt and Elam fragmented rather
than annexed), so that **Ashurbanipal's destruction of Elam** removed the buffer against the
Medes and Persians — the peak that began the end. See [[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]],
[[ancient-near-east-historiography]], and [[divine-kingship]].

## Historiography

Known through abundant **royal annals and administrative archives** plus palace
reliefs and the (hostile, Judah-centric) Hebrew Bible. The annals' boastful
self-representation must be corrected against archaeology. CWH IV places Assyria as
the founding case in the period's comparative study of [[empire]] and imperial
collapse; its capitals are treated as a place type in [[nineveh|Nineveh and the
Neo-Assyrian Capitals]] and [[imperial-city]].
