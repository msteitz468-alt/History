## Overview

Abū l-Ḥasan Muḥammad ibn Yūsuf **al-ʿĀmirī** (c. 936–992 CE) was a Persian philosopher from Nīshāpūr in Khurāsān, widely regarded as the most significant thinker within the **Kindian tradition** — the philosophical lineage descending from [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] through his Khorasanian students. Working in the eastern Islamic lands during the period of [[buyid-dynasty|Būyid]] and Sāmānid patronage, al-ʿĀmirī was a key figure in the Islamicization and eastward transmission of the Neoplatonic philosophy introduced to Arabic culture by the Kindī circle. Source: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]].

## Lineage and Training

Al-ʿĀmirī stands third in a chain of intellectual descent:
- [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] → Abū Zayd al-Balkhī (student) → **al-ʿĀmirī** (student of al-Balkhī)

Abū Zayd al-Balkhī was himself from Balkh in Khorasan — the hometown of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna's]] father — and had written on ethics and philosophical topics in the manner of his master al-Kindī. Al-ʿĀmirī continued this tradition while adding more explicitly Islamic theological framing.

## Philosophical Work

### Reworking of Proclus / the *Liber de Causis*

Al-ʿĀmirī's most distinctive contribution was his further reworking of the Arabic Proclus text that Adamson describes as a "Telephone" chain: the Greek Proclus's *Elements of Theology* had been transformed by the Kindī circle into a Plotinian-style Arabic text (later known in Latin as the *Liber de Causis*, or Book of Causes); al-ʿĀmirī then transformed this transformation again, with a still more explicit Islamicization.

In al-ʿĀmirī's version:
- The Neoplatonic hierarchy's Intellect and Soul are given **Koranic names**: "Pen" (*Qalam*) and "Tablet" (*Lawḥ*), drawn from Quranic cosmological imagery
- The emanation of all things from God — previously presented in Plotinian fashion as automatic or natural, "like light from a source or water from a fountain" — is recast as issuing from a **divine command** (*amr*), echoing the Quranic "Be!" (*kun*)

This move represents a significant theological revision: it introduces an element of voluntarism into a scheme that had been thoroughly necessitarian in its Plotinian original.

### Islam-Compatible Philosophy

Like [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] and later [[miskawayh|Miskawayh]], al-ʿĀmirī saw philosophical inquiry as fully compatible with Islam and directed at a Muslim audience. His work is characteristic of the Kindian tradition's project of making Hellenic Neoplatonism into an Islamic intellectual resource rather than a foreign import.

## Cultural Context

Al-ʿĀmirī's career illustrates the eastward shift of philosophical activity under the "Iranian intermezzo." He visited Baghdad, the traditional capital of Arabic philosophical culture, where he encountered prejudice against Eastern (non-Iraqi) scholars — he reportedly complained of this discrimination and was glad to return home to Khorasan. This anecdote captures a cultural tension within the Islamic philosophical world: the Baghdad Peripatetic school (see [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]]'s students and successors) maintained a metropolitan prestige that eastern thinkers had to contend with, even as the political and cultural center of gravity shifted eastward under the Sāmānids and Būyids.

## Relationship to Ismaili Philosophy

Al-ʿĀmirī's Islamicizing use of Neoplatonic hierarchies — especially his application of Koranic epithets to the Intellect and Soul — runs parallel to (and may have influenced) the similar strategy pursued by the [[ismaili-neoplatonism|Ismaili philosophers]] of the same era, such as al-Sijistānī. Both traditions drew on the Kindī-circle Neoplatonism and both sought to align it with Islamic theological sensibilities, though for very different purposes: al-ʿĀmirī was a philosopher in the courtly tradition, while the Ismailis were missionary theologians.

## Significance

Al-ʿĀmirī represents the bridge between the Kindian tradition and the later, more systematic philosophy of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]], who was born in the same eastern cultural milieu. His project of Islamicizing Neoplatonism — giving divine command language to what had been automatic emanation — prefigures Avicenna's more thoroughgoing metaphysical synthesis, even if Avicenna ultimately went in very different directions. Miskawayh, al-ʿĀmirī's most prominent successor in the Kindian lineage, is the figure who carried this tradition into the eleventh century.
