# Inoue Kowashi (1844–1895)

Legal scholar and bureaucrat who served as [[ito-hirobumi]]'s chief intellectual assistant in the
drafting of the [[meiji-constitution-1889]] and as the principal drafter of the
[[imperial-rescript-on-education-1890]]. Described by contemporaries as "Itō's brain," Inoue was
the most important legal and ideological architect behind the documentary foundations of the Meiji
constitutional order. Linked to [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]].

## Role in the Meiji Constitutional Settlement

Inoue was recognized in the Meiji government as "an expert in intellectual matters" — Keene's
source explicitly identifies him as "said to be Itō Hirobumi's 'brain.'" He participated in the
drafting of the Meiji Constitution alongside Itō and the German adviser Hermann Roesler. His
intellectual contribution was in mediating between European constitutional models (especially
Prussian) and Japanese political conditions, translating constitutional ideas into workable
Japanese institutional design.

## Drafting the Imperial Rescript on Education

When the government sought to produce a set of educational precepts to complement the
constitutional settlement, Inoue was asked for his opinion of Minister of Education Yoshikawa
Akimasa's first draft. He rejected it on several grounds:

1. A rescript on education should not read like a political command or a military order
2. It was inadvisable to risk "arousing religious disputes by mentioning in the rescript respect
   for heaven or worship of the gods"
3. It should be "clear and easy to understand, and the tone should not be such that it pleased
   one faction and angered another"
4. It should not be "abstrusely philosophical or colored by a political tinge"

He was then tasked with producing a better draft. After showing it to Motoda Nagazane, revising
it based on Motoda's criticism, and submitting a temporary draft to [[emperor-meiji]] for
inspection — who read it with great care and found the treatment of the Confucian virtues
inadequate — Inoue and Motoda conferred many more times, "adding a character here and deleting a
character there." The final text was approved by the emperor on October 24, 1890, and promulgated
six days later.

Inoue's drafting principles help explain the Rescript's character: it is simple, non-sectarian
(carefully avoids naming Shintō or Buddhist concepts), and non-partisan — which made it both
broadly acceptable and broadly weaponizable. By grounding moral obligation in the imperial lineage
and the inherited virtue of generations of loyal subjects rather than in any specific religious
or philosophical tradition, Inoue produced a document that could not be challenged on
theological grounds but could be used to demand any level of loyalty in the emperor's name.

## Later Career

Inoue served as minister of education from 1893 to 1894, implementing educational policy in
accordance with the framework he had helped design. He died in 1895, shortly after the conclusion
of the [[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895]].

## Counterfactual Significance

Inoue's drafting principles for the Rescript on Education were precisely calibrated to avoid the
controversies that a more explicitly Shintō or Confucian document would have provoked, both
domestically and internationally. The result was a text that was harder to criticize and easier
to abuse. A different drafter who had embedded the Rescript in explicit Confucian or Shintō
doctrine would have limited its reach but also limited the quasi-religious authority it ultimately
acquired. Inoue's elegant, non-sectarian formulation inadvertently made the Rescript into a more
powerful instrument of state coercion than its author may have intended.
