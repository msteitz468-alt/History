# The Elbe Slavs (Polabian Slavs, Wends)

The **Elbe Slavs** (Polabian Slavs; German *Wenden*) were the pagan West-Slavic peoples settled between the **Elbe/Saale and the Oder**, against whom the [[ottonian-dynasty|Ottonian]] kings waged a sustained campaign of conquest, tribute, mission, and (repeatedly) failed subjugation across the tenth and eleventh centuries. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Müller-Mertens, and the chapter on Saxony and the Elbe Slavs) makes them the eastern frontier of the Reich.

## The Peoples

The principal groupings were the **Abodrites** (north, in Mecklenburg/Holstein), the **Liutizi** (or Wilzi, a confederation with the **Redarii** at its core, between the lower Elbe and Oder), and the **Sorbs** and **Hevelli** (Havelli) in the south, around the middle Elbe and Saale. They were organised in tribal units centred on fortified strongholds and pagan cult-sites (the temple of Rethra/Riedegost among the Liutizi).

## Ottonian Conquest and the March System

After the **[[battle-of-lechfeld-955|Lechfeld]]** [[otto-i|Otto I]] turned on the Slavs, defeating the Abodrites at the **Recknitz (955)** and pressing **direct lordship** over the Polabian and Sorbian lands. He organised the frontier through two great marches under **Hermann Billung** (lower Elbe) and **Gero** (middle Elbe/Saale; subdivided into six smaller marches on his death in 965), beneath which lay the **burgward** — a fortified district of some five to twenty villages grouped around a stronghold (built and maintained by the Slav population) that served as the cell of administration, tribute extraction, and lordship. Otto crowned the structure with a **church organisation**: bishoprics at Brandenburg and Havelberg (948), then Merseburg, Zeitz, Meissen, and Oldenburg, all subordinated to the new archbishopric of **Magdeburg** (967/968) as the centre of eastern mission. Otto's aim, NCMH III stresses, was **immediate lordship over land and men** — renders, services, and the eventual manorialisation of the conquered — which the Abodrites explicitly rejected, offering tribute but demanding to keep "freedom and lordship over their own land."

## The Great Revolt of 983 and the Liutizi

The system collapsed in the **Great Slav Revolt of 983**: exploiting the disasters of Otto II's reign (the defeat at Cotrone, 982), the newly-formed **Liutizi confederation** and the Abodrites rose together, **swept away the Saxon march and church organisation**, and burned Hamburg; the rising was checked only west of the Elbe at the Tanger. The bishoprics of Brandenburg and Havelberg became titular only; Saxon expansion was thrown back to the Elbe for over a century. Repeated campaigns under the regent **Theophanu** and **[[otto-iii|Otto III]]** (often in alliance with Piast Poland) failed to recover the lost lands; the **Liutizi and Abodrites retained their independence**.

## Henry II's Reversal

In one of the most criticised acts of his reign, [[henry-ii-holy-roman-emperor|Henry II]] **allied with the still-pagan Liutizi against the Christian** [[boleslaw-i-chrobry|Bolesław Chrobry]] of Poland (from 1003) — abandoning the Ottonian project of reconquest and outraging Saxon churchmen (Thietmar of Merseburg, Brun of Querfurt). The Liutizi, content to balance Reich and Poland, gave him no decisive help. Definitive conquest and Christianisation of the Elbe Slavs would wait until the twelfth-century **Wendish Crusade** and the *Ostsiedlung*.

## Significance

The Elbe Slav frontier shaped the Ottonian Reich as profoundly as Italy did: it absorbed Saxon military energy, generated the **march and burgward institutions** of German eastern lordship, and made the archbishopric of **Magdeburg** a missionary metropolis. The revolt of 983 is a reminder that Ottonian expansion was **contested and reversible**, not a steady *Drang nach Osten*.
