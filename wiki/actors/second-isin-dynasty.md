# Second Dynasty of Isin

The Second Dynasty of Isin (named in the king-lists for the city of **Isin**) was
the native Babylonian line that ruled c. **1156–1025 BCE**, between the fall of the
[[kassites|Kassite dynasty]] (extinguished by the [[elam|Elamite]] Shutrukids) and
the long Aramaean-driven dark age of Babylonia. It is the political vehicle of the
post-Kassite Babylonian revival treated in CAH II.2 (chapter XXXI, Wiseman). Its
founder was **Marduk-kabit-ahheshu**, and its eleven kings ruled for some 132 years.

## A Babylonian Revival

After the catastrophe of the Elamite sack — the loss of the cities and of the
statue of Marduk — the nobles of the south rallied around Isin to
re-establish a native Babylonian kingship. The dynasty's high point came with its
fourth king, **[[nebuchadnezzar-i|Nebuchadnezzar I]]** (r. 1124–1103 BCE), who
defeated [[elam|Elam]] on the Ulai near Susa, recovered the Marduk statue, and is
associated in the volume with the **elevation of Marduk to the supreme god of
Babylon**. Babylon itself was reoccupied as the capital and its temples refurbished.

Other kings — Itti-Marduk-balatu, Ninurta-nadin-shumi, and later
**Marduk-nadin-ahhe** — repeatedly contested the upper Diyala frontier and the
East-Tigris border districts with [[assyria-middle|Assyria]]. Marduk-nadin-ahhe
raided across the Lesser Zab and carried off the gods Adad and Shala from Ekallate,
provoking a reprisal by [[tiglath-pileser-i|Tiglath-pileser I]], who eventually
sacked Babylon's palaces — though without lasting territorial gain on either side.

## Decline

The dynasty's later reigns coincided with the disaster that overtook both
Mesopotamian powers: famine, the loss of trade, and above all the relentless
pressure of the [[arameans|Aramaean]] tribes settling the Euphrates and East-Tigris
country. One late king, **Adad-apla-iddina** (c. 1062 BCE), is described in a
chronicle as himself an Aramaean usurper. Contemporary records then almost cease,
and Babylonia, like Assyria, sank into one of the weakest and darkest phases of its
history.

## Significance

- `produced:` the post-Kassite Babylonian revival and (via
  [[nebuchadnezzar-i|Nebuchadnezzar I]]) the theological elevation of **Marduk**.
- `caused_by:` the [[elam|Elamite]] destruction of [[kassites|Kassite Babylon]],
  which created the vacuum it filled.
- `opposed_by:` [[assyria-middle|Assyria]] (the perennial frontier struggle) and,
  fatally, the [[arameans|Aramaeans]].

## Historiography

Reconstructed almost entirely from king-lists, chronicles, and *kudurru* stelae;
the absence of building and economic records for much of the period reflects the
genuine impoverishment of the age rather than mere loss. See
[[nebuchadnezzar-i|Nebuchadnezzar I]] for the dynasty's one well-documented reign.
