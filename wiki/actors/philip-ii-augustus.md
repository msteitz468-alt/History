# Philip II Augustus

**Philip II "Augustus"** (1165–1223; king of France from 1180) was the
[[capetian-dynasty|Capetian]] king who transformed the French monarchy from the weak
overlordship of his predecessors into the dominant power of western Europe.
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (John W. Baldwin, Ch. 17a, "Crown and government";
Michel Bur, Ch. 17b, "The seigneuries") treats his reign as the decisive turning point
in the recovery of royal authority — building on the patient consolidation begun under
Louis VI ("the Fat") and his minister **Suger** of Saint-Denis, and Louis VII.

## Career

Inheriting a crown whose effective domain was still little more than the
[[capetian-dynasty|Île-de-France]], Philip systematically expanded royal power. He
reorganised royal administration (the *baillis* and *prévôts*, a salaried bureaucracy,
written records — the beginnings of the royal archive), increased crown revenue, and
fortified Paris as a true capital. His central achievement was the **destruction of the
[[angevin-empire|Angevin empire]]**: exploiting the feudal defaults of King **John** of
England (his vassal for the continental lands), Philip conquered **Normandy, Anjou,
Maine, and Touraine in 1204**, multiplying the size of the royal domain at a stroke. He
confirmed his dominance by defeating a coalition of John, the Emperor Otto IV, and the
count of Flanders at the **Battle of Bouvines (1214)** — a victory that secured the
Capetian ascendancy.

He also joined the **Third Crusade (1190)** alongside Richard I (returning early), and
his reign coincided with the flowering of [[gothic-architecture|Gothic]] Paris and the
recognition of the [[medieval-university|University of Paris]].

## Counterfactual Significance

Philip Augustus is the architect of the medieval French state: by quadrupling the royal
domain and defeating the Angevin and imperial coalition, he made the Capetian monarchy
the strongest in Europe and set France on the path to the centralised kingdom of the
thirteenth century ([[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]]). His reign is the mirror image of
the Angevin collapse — the same events that ruined King John made Philip "Augustus."
