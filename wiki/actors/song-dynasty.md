# Song Dynasty

## Overview

The **Song dynasty** (960–1279) reunified China after the fragmentation that followed
the [[tang-dynasty|Tang]] collapse and presided over what is widely regarded as the
most **commercialized, urbanized, and technologically advanced** society of the
[[middle-millennium|Middle Millennium]]. [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Richard von
Glahn, Ch 19) places it at the centre of the volume's account of Chinese state
formation: with the Sui reunification of 589 a **unified empire became the political
ideal and — with brief interruptions — the historical reality**, inspiring China's
neighbours to emulate the model and engendering a multi-state East Asian order. The
Song is also the commercial **"engine"** whose internal market, in Richard Smith's
phrase (Ch 9), "rippled through much of the rest of Eurasia," and the Chinese pole of
the [[cultural-crystallization|tenth-to-thirteenth-century cultural crystallization]].

## The Reunified Empire and the Examination State

After the founding emperor **Taizu** (Zhao Kuangyin) reunified the realm in 960, the
Song built a centralized, civilian-dominated state that deliberately subordinated the
military to a **scholar-bureaucracy** recruited through an expanded
[[educational-institutions|civil-service examination]] system. Von Glahn stresses two
structural transformations:

- **A revitalized Confucian model of imperial sovereignty and civil bureaucracy** —
  the ideological and institutional core that became
  [[neo-confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]].
- **A rapid monetization** of taxes, rents, and economic life, driven in part by
  government intervention — the world's first use of **paper money**, vast bronze and
  iron coinage, and a tax system increasingly assessed and paid in cash.

## The Song Economic Revolution

The Song is the classic case of a medieval "economic revolution": explosive growth in
agriculture (early-ripening Champa rice), a doubling of population between the Tang
and Song, the largest cities on earth (the Northern Song capital **Kaifeng**; the
Southern Song capital **Hangzhou**, "the largest city in the world of its time" with
over a million inhabitants), the busiest ports (Quanzhou, Guangzhou), and a cluster of
transformative technologies — **gunpowder, the magnetic compass, woodblock and
movable-type printing, advanced iron and steel production**. Its commercial energy
turned China's external trade seaward, anchoring the Chinese end of the
[[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]] and [[afro-eurasian-world-system|Afro-Eurasian]]
systems even as [[confucianism|Confucian]] ideology disdained merchants.

## Northern and Southern Song

Despite its wealth, the Song never matched the Tang's military reach and "faced
challenges from within and without." It ceded the far north to the Khitan **Liao**
and paid tribute to keep the peace; in 1127 the Jurchen **Jin** overran the north and
captured Kaifeng, forcing the court south to **Hangzhou** (the **Southern Song**,
1127–1279). The dynasty's economic and cultural brilliance continued in the south
until the [[mongol-empire|Mongol]] conquest completed in **1279** — "the most
enriching and empowering conquest" of the Mongol expansion (Arnason).

## A "new society" of scholar-officials (Kuhn)

[[kuhn-die-song-dynastie|Dieter Kuhn's *Die Song-Dynastie*]] (1987) — read here to fill the Song
gap left by the Chinese [[cambridge-history-of-china|CHC]] compilation — frames the dynasty as
**"a new society reflected in its culture."** Its argument: the Song marks the decisive
**[[tang-song-transition|Tang–Song transition]]** in which the hereditary medieval **aristocracy**
was replaced by a new elite of **scholar-officials** (*shi-da-fu* / *Beamten-Gelehrte*) defined by
classical education and success in the **[[chinese-examination-system|examinations]]**, not by
birth. The Song "embodied like no other dynasty the **state of the scholar-officials**," and its
distinctive culture — Neo-Confucian philosophy, landscape painting, print learning, antiquarianism —
is the mirror of that transformed society. This social reading complements the economic
("commercial revolution") and world-systemic (von Glahn) accounts above.

## Significance

The Song embodies several of the volume's central themes at once: the Chinese
**unified-empire ideal** that structured the East Asian state order; the commercial
**engine** driving Afro-Eurasian exchange; and the **cultural crystallization** of
Neo-Confucianism that would shape East Asia into the modern era. Its combination of
economic and technological precocity with relative military weakness makes it a focal
case in the "Great Divergence" debate over why an industrial breakthrough did not
follow.

## The Dai Viet frontier (Taylor 2013)

K. W. Taylor argues the Song was a "new kind of empire" — led by scholar-officials distrustful of military
men, "lacking the martial prowess and strategic vision of the old Han–Tang aristocracy" — and that this,
as much as declining capacity, shaped its southern frontier. Its 980–981 invasion to reconquer the newly
autonomous Vietnamese failed, and thereafter Song adopted an "appeasement control" posture toward
[[dai-viet|Dai Viet]]. That policy reversed under Wang Anshi, precipitating the
[[ly-song-war-1075-1077|Ly–Song War (1075–1077)]]; after the Jurchen drove the Song south in the 1120s, the
weakened Southern Song posed little threat to Thang Long. See [[ly-dynasty-vietnam]].
