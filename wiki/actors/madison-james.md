# James Madison

James Madison (1751–1836) was a Virginia statesman, principal architect of the U.S. Constitution, co-author of the Federalist Papers, fourth President of the United States (1809–1817), and Jefferson's closest political collaborator and intellectual partner for nearly fifty years.

## Life and Career

Born in Port Conway, Virginia, to a prominent planter family. Educated at the College of New Jersey (Princeton) under John Witherspoon; influenced by Enlightenment thought and Scottish common-sense philosophy. Early career in Virginia politics; served in Continental Congress and Virginia House of Delegates.

At the Constitutional Convention (1787) he was the leading figure, keeping the most complete notes and advancing the Virginia Plan. Co-authored the Federalist Papers with Hamilton and Jay to secure ratification in New York. As Congressman from Virginia in the First Congress he introduced the Bill of Rights and helped organize the new government.

From the early 1790s he became Jefferson's chief lieutenant in opposition to Hamilton's financial system and in the formation of the Republican party. Drafted the Virginia Resolutions (1798) against the Alien and Sedition Acts. Served as Jefferson's Secretary of State (1801–1809), then as President during the War of 1812. Later reconciled with aspects of federal power while remaining committed to republican principles and religious liberty.

## Key Relationships

- **Thomas Jefferson**: Lifelong closest friend and collaborator. Their correspondence is one of the great intellectual exchanges in American history. Madison managed many of Jefferson's political interests while Jefferson was in France and later served as his successor.
- **Alexander Hamilton**: Ally during ratification; sharp opponent over Bank, funding, and executive power in the 1790s.
- **George Washington**: Respected colleague; served in the Convention and early administration.

## Significance and Counterfactual

Madison's work at Philadelphia gave the United States its constitutional framework. His partnership with Jefferson defined the first party system and the strict-constructionist tradition. Without Madison the Constitution might have been less balanced or failed ratification; the Virginia Resolutions might not have articulated the compact theory that influenced later states-rights thought.

## Historiography

Modern scholarship (especially the Papers of James Madison project) has illuminated his role as "Father of the Constitution" while noting his evolution and the limits of his nationalism. Malone's treatment in *Jefferson and His Time* emphasizes the Jefferson-Madison axis in the 1780s–90s.
