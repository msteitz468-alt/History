## Overview

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (c. 1839–1897) was the catalyzing figure of the first generation of [[islamic-modernism]] and pan-Islamic political thought. Originally from near Hamadan in the Qajar kingdom (he claimed Afghan origins, hence the epithet "Afghani," possibly to obscure his Shii background and broaden his Sunni appeal), he spent his life as a peripatetic agitator — in India, Egypt, Iran, the Ottoman empire, Russia, and France — seeking to arouse Muslim peoples to organized resistance against European hegemony. He originated relatively few systematic ideas but possessed exceptional political intelligence and the capacity to crystallize developing sentiment at decisive moments.

His most influential Arab disciple was [[muhammad-abduh]].

## Background and Intellectual Formation

He is said to have studied at Najaf, the great Shii intellectual center near ancient Kufa. He gained early political experience in Afghanistan, being exiled in 1868 when he found himself on the losing side of a succession dispute. Contact with Muslim reform movements in India seems to have converted him to the view that passive accommodation to Western domination was spiritually and politically disastrous.

Intellectually, his roots lay in the tradition of *Falsafa* (philosophical Islam), in which revealed religion functioned primarily as a political force — a framework for mobilizing and cohering communities rather than merely a system of doctrinal beliefs. This gave him freedom from the political caution of strict Shari-mindedness and allowed him to combine intellectual venturesomeness with populist Islamic rhetoric. He denounced the Sufi tradition for its quietism and the conventional ulama for their obscurantism. Yet "with his admiration for the technical and intellectual vigour of the Occident, he condemned it vituperatively (at least on the level of the unphilosophical public for whom he wrote) for its godless materialism."

## Major Activities

### Egypt (1870s)

Afghani first evoked active response in Egypt in the 1870s, "winning some of the best Egyptian youth to a militant anti-Western Muslim Modernism." His brief presence before the Urabi revolt of 1882 launched a vigorous intellectual and political ferment. Among those he influenced was the young Muhammad Abduh.

### *Al-'Urwa al-wuthqa* (Paris, 1884)

After the British suppression of the Urabi movement, Afghani published in Paris (with Abduh collaborating) the most influential Arabic journal of the period — *al-'Urwa al-wuthqa* (The Firmest Bond). It was "violently anti-British" and was "prevented by the British in Egypt and India from reaching a sufficient paying audience to keep it going; yet it was eagerly read wherever copies could be smuggled." Its circulation was brief (eighteen issues in 1884) but its impact extended far beyond its print run.

### Iran and the Tobacco Boycott (1889–92)

Invited to Tehran by Nasir al-Din Shah in 1889, Afghani quickly broke with the Shah, who had "no intention of reforming under his guidance." Expelled to the Shii sanctuary near Tehran, he agitated against the Shah's concession of a tobacco monopoly to a British company (1890). He wrote to the recognized chief mujtahid in Iraq urging action; the mujtahid issued a fatwa that smoking was contrary to religion until the monopoly was withdrawn. "The decree was obeyed throughout the Qājâr dominions: tobacco disappeared even from the palace." The concession was cancelled. This is perhaps the single most spectacular demonstration of the pan-Islamic political potential Afghani sought to harness: the alliance of ulama, bazaar tradesmen, and Westernizing intellectuals against a European commercial penetration backed by a collaborationist ruler.

After his expulsion he continued agitation from Iraq. A close disciple, after consulting Afghani in Istanbul, assassinated the Shah in 1896.

### Istanbul and Captivity under Abdülhamid II

Abdülhamid II recognized Afghani's usefulness as a pan-Islamic publicist and lured him to Istanbul. There, however, the Sultan "kept him carefully under control." The two were incompatible: Afghani's pan-Islamic vision was ultimately hostile to despotism, while Abdülhamid's pan-Islamism was a legitimating ideology for autocratic rule. Afghani died in 1897 "in circumstances which led the Iranians to believe Sultan Abdülhamid had had him done away with."

## Ideas

Afghani's core political program involved three elements:
1. **Internal Muslim reform** — purging quietism, superstition, and accommodation to foreign domination from Islamic practice.
2. **Pan-Islamic solidarity** — Muslim peoples revitalizing themselves individually and then cooperating in a common front against European hegemony.
3. **Reception of modern science** while rejecting Western cultural and moral claims — he taught "that each Muslim people should bestir itself and strengthen itself to co-operate with other reviving Muslim peoples in a 'pan-Islamic' movement of political solidarity against the West."

His pan-Islamic notion "had ultimately little to do with that of Abdülhamid." Where the Sultan used pan-Islamism to legitimize his own autocracy and claim spiritual suzerainty over all Muslims as caliph, Afghani's vision was of politically revitalized, internally reformed Muslim nations cooperating as equals.

## Counterfactual Significance

Afghani's significance lies less in any specific political outcome than in the intellectual climate he created. Without his peripatetic agitation: the Egyptian generation that produced Muhammad Abduh would have lacked its catalytic figure; the tobacco boycott — the most successful example of organized popular resistance to European commercial penetration in nineteenth-century Muslim history — might not have occurred; and the pan-Islamic idiom in which subsequent Islamist reformers worked would have lacked its most vivid historical precedent.

His limitations were structural: "he could not achieve a new philosophical basis for his new politics." His Falsafa-based willingness to manipulate public doctrine (telling different things to different audiences) served him politically but prevented him from building a coherent movement.

## Later Court Alignment and Asian Reach (Formichi)

Chiara Formichi's [[formichi-islam-and-asia-2020|*Islam and Asia: A History*]] (2020) adds a coda to
the Istanbul years: Afghani formally joined the **Ottoman caliphal court in 1892**, lending
intellectual legitimacy to Abdülhamid II's pan-Islamic diplomacy even as the relationship soured.
Formichi (2020) stresses that Afghani's pan-Islamism was, in its origin, "a solidarity network
primarily meant to help individual nations to achieve independence from European colonialism"
rather than a program of political unification under a single Caliph — the Hamidian, caliph-centered
reading was a later, temporary transformation of his idea rather than its original content.

Formichi also traces the transmission of Afghani-Abduh-Rashid Rida reformism — via *al-'Urwa
al-wuthqa* (1884) and its successor journal *al-Manar* (Cairo, 1898) — deep into Asia: to Russia's
Volga-Tatar community (*al-Shura*), to Southeast Asia (*al-Imam*, Singapore 1906; *al-Munir*,
Sumatra), to South Asia (*al-Hilal* and *al-Balagha*, edited by Abu al-Kalam Azad), and to China
(*Yuehua*, 1930s, via students sent to al-Azhar from Shanghai and Beijing Islamic schools). See
[[islamic-modernism]] for this wider network.

## Historiography

Afghani is a contested figure in both Western and Muslim historical scholarship. Western historians have debated whether his espousal of pan-Islamic solidarity was sincere or instrumental; Iranian historians have focused on his role in the constitutional movement and the tobacco boycott; Arab historians have emphasized his influence on Abduh and the Modernist tradition. His self-constructed persona (obscuring his Iranian Shii origin) complicates source criticism. Hodgson presents him as a genuine intellectual force whose structural dilemma — how to combine Falsafa-style elite politics with popular mobilization — was symptomatic of the broader dilemma facing Muslim intellectuals confronting European hegemony.
