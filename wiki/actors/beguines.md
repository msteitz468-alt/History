# Beguines (and Beghards)

## Overview

The **Beguines** (women) and **Beghards** (men) were **lay quasi-religious communities** that arose in the Low Countries and Rhineland in the early thirteenth century. They did not form a traditional order: members took **no permanent vows**, promised celibacy only while living in community, remained free to leave, were **not enclosed**, and often worked in towns. Logan treats them as another outlet for the same voluntary-poverty impulse that produced [[waldensians|Waldensians]], [[humiliati|Humiliati]], and [[francis-of-assisi|Francis]] — but one that for a time won high ecclesiastical praise.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan stresses that Beguines were **falsely accused of heresy** more often than they earned the charge. Their structure was deliberately liminal:

- Laywomen, not nuns  
- No vows in the canonical sense  
- Promise of celibacy **while in community** only  
- Free to leave and marry  
- Not enclosed; some worked in urban economies  

**Honorius III** gave verbal approval in **1216**; **Gregory IX** formally took them under protection in **1233**. Houses appear in rapid sequence: Cologne (1223), Leuven (1232), Mainz (1233), Namur and Paderborn (1235). By **1309** Cologne alone had about **164 houses / ~1,000 Beguines**; Strasbourg about **600**. The English scholar-bishop **Robert Grosseteste** warmly approved their life as “**perfect and holy**” — high endorsement from a leading pastoral intellectual. Reliance on [[mendicant-orders|friars]] for spiritual direction, however, subjected them to anti-friar criticisms.

Decline set in after the **Council of Vienne (1312)**, whose two hostile decrees opened a long period of suspicion. Logan links the Beguine world as a forerunner of later currents of interior piety (including the *Devotio Moderna*). Male **Beghards** followed a similar, less documented path.
