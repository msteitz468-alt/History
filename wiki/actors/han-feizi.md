# Han Feizi (Han Fei)

The great systematizer of **[[legalism|Legalism]]** (c. 280–233 BCE) — the political philosophy of law,
power and bureaucratic control that underpinned the **[[qin-dynasty|Qin]]** unification of China. His
writings are now ingested as **[[han-feizi-watson|Han Feizi: Basic Writings]]** (trans. Burton Watson).

## Life

A prince of the royal house of the small state of **Han**, Han Fei studied under the Confucian
**Xunzi** (from whom he took a dark view of human nature) but rejected Confucian moralism for a
hard-headed philosophy of statecraft. A stammerer who wrote rather than spoke, he produced essays of
striking force. His fellow student **Li Si** had risen to power in **Qin**; when Han Fei was sent there
as an envoy, the **First Emperor** admired his writings, but Li Si — jealous, and fearing a rival —
contrived his imprisonment and forced suicide in **233 BCE**.

## Thought

Han Feizi synthesized the earlier Legalist strands into a complete theory of rule:

- ***Fa* (law)** — clear, public, uniformly enforced laws, the same for high and low, replacing personal
  judgment and Confucian ritual.
- ***Shu* (statecraft / technique)** — the hidden administrative methods by which the ruler controls,
  tests and manipulates his officials, keeping his own intentions concealed.
- ***Shi* (positional power)** — authority that flows from the **position** of ruler, not from personal
  virtue or charisma.
- **The "two handles"** — **reward and punishment** — are the ruler's essential instruments; men are
  motivated by self-interest, so a wise ruler relies on **strict, certain penalties and rewards**, not
  on benevolence.
- A **historicist** rejection of the Confucian golden age: times change, so government must suit present
  conditions, not imitate the ancient sage-kings.

## Significance

Han Feizi gave the most rigorous statement of the **amoral, statist** alternative to Confucianism. His
principles, applied with severity by the **First Emperor** and **Li Si**, enabled the **Qin** to unify
China (221 BCE) — and the harshness of that application was blamed for the dynasty's rapid fall. Though
Legalism was thereafter publicly reviled, its institutions (centralized bureaucracy, codified law,
standardized administration) were quietly absorbed into the imperial system, making Han Feizi one of the
most consequential of all Chinese thinkers.

## Note: Han Fei without "Legalism" (Major & Cook 2017)

[[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook (2017)]] confirm the biography — Han Fei studied with
**Xunzi** alongside **Li Si**, was invited to [[qin-dynasty|Qin]], accused of being a Han agent, and
**coerced into suicide** — and quote his advice that the ruler be "remote and aloof, trusting no
one," wielding reward and punishment as "the two handles of government." But they argue his work
draws **eclectically** across Warring States thought, including the *Laozi*, and that the very
label **"[[legalism|Legalism]]" should be dropped** as obscuring the differences among Han Fei,
Shang Yang, Shen Buhai, and Shen Dao — part of their broader case that tomb texts reveal overlapping
**trends, not bounded schools**. **Source:** [[major-cook-ancient-china-2017]].
