# Harsha (Harshavardhana)

**Harsha** (r. 606–647) was the last ruler to build a substantial empire in **northern India** before
the coming of Islam — a transitional figure between the [[gupta-empire|Gupta]] "classical" age and the
era of regional kingdoms. Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## Career

Of the **Pushyabhuti** family of Thanesar, Harsha came to power in **606** after the murder of the
last Maukhari king of Kanauj left the throne to him; he made **Kanauj** his capital, which "remained
the focus of political activity in northern India" for centuries. Over a 41-year reign he welded
together a realm across the Ganges plain (and into the Punjab, Kashmir, Nepal, Valabhi) — an
"energetic ruler who frequently toured" to keep in touch with his subjects. His **southward expansion
was checked** decisively when the Chalukya **Pulakeshin II** defeated him on the **Narmada** (~630),
fixing the enduring frontier between the northern and Deccan powers. His kingdom **disintegrated
rapidly** at his death, having no clear heir — illustrating Thapar's theme that these post-Gupta
realms depended heavily on the **personal** energy of the ruler rather than durable institutions.

## Sources and significance

Harsha is unusually well documented: by **Bana**, his court poet, in the *Harshacharita* ("Life of
Harsha") — an early example of Sanskrit courtly biography — and by the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim
**Hsuan Tsang (Xuanzang)**, who travelled India during his reign and left a detailed account
(including the curious episode in which a Chinese embassy, finding the throne usurped after Harsha's
death, raised a force that defeated the usurper and carried him to China). Himself a poet (credited
with Sanskrit plays) and a patron of both Buddhism and the Hindu cults, Harsha epitomises the
cultured, personally-centred kingship of the age. His reign marks the effective **end of the
"classical" north-Indian imperial tradition**; thereafter power fragmented into the
[[rajputs|regional states]] Thapar treats next.

## Avari's Treatment (Chapter 9)

Burjor Avari's *India: The Ancient Past* (2007) adds significant detail to the Thapar account. Avari explicitly frames Harsha as a ruler who played "three different roles: as a conqueror, an administrator and a man of intellect and culture." After unifying the Pushyabhutis and Maukharis and establishing himself at Kanauj, "he went on to conquer Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, Gujarat and Punjab." Though stopped on the Narmada by Pulakeshin II, he was nonetheless recognised as master of "the five Indies." He employed the *samanta* policy successfully: "Even the powerful kings of Kashmir, Nepal and Valabhi counted among his tributaries."

Avari underscores Harsha's **administrative style** as described by Hsuan Tsang: royal land was divided into four categories — expenses of government and state worship, endowments to ministers, rewards to persons of great ability, and charity to various faiths. Land was measured in plough units; a minor *samanta* was entitled to roughly 1,333 acres. Harsha's "secular grants provided a considerable boost to the growth of [[indian-feudalism|feudalism]] and a feudal mentality in north India." One religious endowment comprised "a donation of 100 villages of 1,000 ploughs each to brahmans."

On **religious syncretism**: Avari follows Hsuan Tsang's account that Harsha organised "grand assemblies and festivals" at which Buddhism was "feted and honoured," yet Hsuan Tsang also "found Buddhism in great decline" across much of India, with "ruins of monasteries and stupas" attesting to decay. The challenge to Buddhism came from "Vaishnavism and Shaivism" rather than from Vedic Brahmanism directly — the same forces that would eventually extinguish Buddhism from the subcontinent.

On **literary achievement**: Harsha authored three Sanskrit dramas — *Ratnavali*, *Priyadarshika*, and *Nagananda*. His biographer Banabhatta wrote the *Harsha-charita*, "what is called an Akhyayika" (historical biography rather than romance). The Chinese pilgrim's *Si-Yu-Ki* is described as "the great account of India" of the period — "one of the great geographical and social documents of the ancient world."

On **fragmentation after death**: "Shortly after Harsha's death in 647 his kingdom broke up. No one of comparable stature appeared on the scene, and the ensuing anarchy lasted for more than seven decades." This opened the period of Yashovarman of Kanauj (r. c. 725–752) and Lalitditya Muktapida of Kashmir (c. 724–760), whose conflicts with each other and with Tibetan, Chinese, and Arab powers, along with the rising Gurjara-Pratihara, formed the immediate political background to the [[gurjara-pratihara|Tripartite Struggle]].
