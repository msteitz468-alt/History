## Note on Disambiguation

This page covers **Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī** (1149–1210), the Ashʿarite theologian and Avicenna commentator. He is distinct from the earlier unorthodox philosopher and physician **Abū Bakr al-Rāzī** (c. 854–925), who held the heterodox "five eternal principles" doctrine. Both names derive from the northern Persian city of Rayy. Adamson explicitly flags this confusion: "the great theologian of the twelfth century, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, [is distinct from] the earlier unorthodox philosopher and doctor al-Rāzī" ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## Overview

Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī was born in Rayy in northern Persia and traced his intellectual lineage to al-Juwaynī (teacher of al-Ghazālī) and through him to al-Ashʿarī himself. He was the foremost Ashʿarite theologian-philosopher of the twelfth century and arguably the most influential single thinker in the eastern Islamic world of his era. His method was forensic dialectic: every topic he addressed received exhaustive treatment in the form of arguments, counter-arguments, counter-counter-arguments, with his own view appearing only as a terse conclusion — if at all.

[[nasir-al-din-tusi]] aptly and archly called him "prince of the controversialists." By the time of his death in 1210, he had requested burial in a secret location to prevent a group of outraged opponents from desecrating his grave.

## Career and Patronage

Fakhr al-Dīn flourished under Seljūq patronage at various courts. He undertook an extended tour of Transoxiana in central Asia specifically to debate local scholars, documenting the results in a work titled *al-Munāẓarāt* (Debates), which opens: "As to the city of Bukhārā, when I arrived at it, I argued with a number of people." He was, as Adamson notes, "a man with a nearly unprecedented capacity for argument" whose opponents won arguments less often than they won his personal ill-will.

## Philosophical Method

Al-Rāzī's dialectical procedure is characterized by:

1. **Exhaustive enumeration**: he lists all known arguments on both sides of every question, often providing proofs he himself finds unconvincing, to the point that critics complained he "was far better at explaining the arguments in support of heretical views than he was at refuting them."
2. **Stringent epistemic standards**: following Avicennan scholasticism, he holds all claims to the highest standard of demonstration. His skeptical rigor sometimes induces what Adamson calls "uncertainty, or at least bewilderment." An anecdote — probably apocryphal — has a friend finding him weeping because he had just discovered that a belief he had held as certain was false, leading him to doubt whether any belief can be certain.
3. **Conceptual precision**: he delights in "the deft distinction, the counter-example, the terminological clarification that will defeat an opponent," most famously distinguishing necessary from sufficient conditions — a move al-Ghazālī had failed to make in a famous debate with a Shiite opponent.

## Principal Works

- **Commentary on Avicenna's *Pointers and Reminders*** (*Sharḥ al-Ishārāt*): a massive engagement with Avicenna from the Ashʿarite perspective; immediately provoked a counter-commentary by [[nasir-al-din-tusi]].
- ***Maṭālib al-ʿāliya*** (Exalted Topics of Inquiry): his most systematic philosophical work, organized around disputed questions in Avicennan physics and metaphysics. Contains the extraordinary discussion of time: twelve proofs that time has no real existence, twenty-one arguments that it needs no proof, four proofs that it does exist.
- **Koranic commentary**: so full of philosophical digressions that [[ibn-taymiyya]] tartly noted it "contains everything, apart from exegesis."

## Key Philosophical Positions

### On the Essence–Existence Distinction

Al-Rāzī defends Avicenna's real distinction between essence and existence against Suhrawardī's critique. He argues that if existence were merely mental, we could not explain how different things are actually distinguished from one another. "All things that exist have something in common, namely, the very fact that they exist." But things are differentiated from one another by their *essences* — not by existence, which is shared equally by everything. This places him in the "univocal existence" camp, against [[nasir-al-din-tusi]]'s "analogical" position.

A consequence: God's existence is in the same sense as created existence; what distinguishes God is his *essence*, which guarantees his existence, whereas creatures must receive existence from an outside cause. Al-Ṭūsī objected that this implies God's essence *causes* His existence — a troubling self-causation — while his own view preserves God's complete simplicity by identifying essence and existence in God.

### On Divine Knowledge of Particulars

Al-Rāzī presents a "battery of arguments on all sides" of the question of whether God knows individual particular things. He ultimately invokes the pragmatic argument that prayer to God must presuppose particular divine knowledge: "I don't pray that there are giraffes, but that Hiawatha will recover from her recent neck-reduction surgery." This sort of conclusion — "that, al-Rāzī says, is good enough for him" — is typical: powerful dialectical machinery followed by a somewhat underwhelming personal verdict.

### On Time

Al-Rāzī's treatment of time is strikingly skeptical. Following earlier discussions by Abū Bakr al-Rāzī and [[abu-l-barakat-al-baghdadi]], he considers seriously whether time exists only mentally. He assigns to the skeptic the default position: the burden of proof falls on whoever claims time has *external* reality. His discussion yields twelve arguments against real existence, twenty-one arguments that time is self-evident, and four proofs that it is real.

### On Ethics

Early in his career al-Rāzī follows Ashʿarite divine-command ethics. As his thought matures under the influence of Avicenna, he moves toward a consequentialist-hedonist position: moral terms like "good" and "bad" mean nothing except reference to what people find pleasant or painful. He connects this to Islamic law by arguing that obeying God's commands is rational precisely because the punishments and rewards attached to them make obedience the pleasure-maximizing strategy. He anticipates twentieth-century rule-consequentialism in arguing that even apparently selfless honesty is rational because a general policy of honesty maximizes collective benefit.

## Historical Significance

Al-Rāzī was, Adamson concludes, "one of the leading commentators on the Koran" and arguably "the most significant and influential thinker in the eastern lands of Islam in the twelfth century." His works were so widely read that later philosophers and theologians engaged with Avicenna *through* al-Rāzī even when not writing directly about him. Many commentaries were written on his philosophical works. He exemplifies "Avicennan scholasticism" — the distinctive mode of later eastern Islamic philosophy in which Avicenna's system is the inescapable reference point for both defenders and critics.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without al-Rāzī, the synthesis of Ashʿarite kalām and Avicennan philosophy might have proceeded along different lines or more slowly. His encyclopedic treatment of metaphysics set the agenda for al-Ṭūsī's defense of Avicenna, and the Rāzī–Ṭūsī debate on existence and essence parallels the contemporaneous Aquinas–Scotus debate in Latin Europe — both triggered by Avicenna, both producing analogical and univocal positions, but without direct cross-influence.
