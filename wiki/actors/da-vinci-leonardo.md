# Leonardo da Vinci

Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519) was a Florentine artist, engineer, scientist, and inventor whose work epitomized the Renaissance ideal of the polymath. He is best known for the *Mona Lisa* and *The Last Supper*, but in his own estimation he was equally (or more) an investigator of nature whose notebooks record obsessive empirical studies of anatomy, optics, water, flight, geology, and mechanics. His iconic *Vitruvian Man* drawing embodies the belief that the human body and the cosmos share mathematical proportions.

## Life and Career

Born 15 April 1452 in Vinci near Florence, the illegitimate son of notary Piero da Vinci and a young peasant woman (Caterina). Raised in his father’s household. Around 1466 entered the workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio in Florence as an apprentice; remained until ~1477. Early independent commissions in Florence were limited; he had difficulty finishing works and sought broader opportunities.

In 1482 he wrote the famous letter to Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan (de facto ruler), offering his services primarily as military engineer, architect, and designer of festivals and only secondarily as painter. He spent ~17 years in Milan (1482–1499), producing theatrical machinery, the great (never completed) Sforza Horse monument, *Virgin of the Rocks*, portraits of the Sforza court, and the *Last Supper* (c. 1495–1498) in the refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie. After the French invasion ousted Ludovico, Leonardo returned briefly to Florence (1500–1506), where he began the *Mona Lisa* (c. 1503–), competed with Michelangelo on the Battle of Anghiari mural (abandoned after 1505 storm and technical failures), and served briefly as military engineer to Cesare Borgia. Father Ser Piero died July 1504 (notebook note with minor inaccuracies; no inheritance).

He returned to Milan under French rule (1506–1513), settling a Virgin of the Rocks payment dispute but staying under protection of Louis XII and governor Charles d’Amboise (Florentine obligations repeatedly extended by French diplomacy). Adopted ~14-year-old Francesco Melzi (1507) as pupil, scribe, assistant, and intellectual heir (fond but sometimes frustrated letters survive; “peace not war” note re household with Salai). Conducted major second anatomy campaign (centenarian dissection revealing arteriosclerosis; aortic valve vortices discovery via glass model experiments; wax-injected brain ventricles; >30 bodies total). Heavy work on water, geology, and fossils in the Codex Leicester (evaporation/rain cycle for mountain springs; fossils as ancient seabed uplift, not Deluge; trace fossils). Notebook analogies (body/machine, eddies/hair, heart/seed) and astronomy notes (“Il sole nó si muóve”). Pageants included a mechanical lion for Louis XII. Fought (and partially won with French help) an inheritance dispute with half-brothers over uncle Francesco’s property.

He moved to Rome (1513–1516) under Giuliano de’ Medici patronage, then accepted Francis I’s invitation (1516) and spent his final years at Clos Lucé near Amboise. ## Key Relationships and Patrons

- **Andrea del Verrocchio**: master in whose workshop Leonardo trained; collaborative projects.
- **Ludovico Sforza**: primary early patron; provided court position and resources.
- **Francesco Melzi**: young nobleman who joined the household ~1507 and became Leonardo’s closest companion, heir, and the principal preserver of the notebooks.
- **Salai (Gian Giacomo Caprotti)**: entered household at age 10 (~1490); long-term companion and model.
- **Cesare Borgia**: employed Leonardo as military architect and cartographer in 1502.
- **Michelangelo Buonarroti**: younger rival; public competition in Florence.
- **Francis I**: final generous patron who brought Leonardo to France with his notebooks and paintings (including the *Mona Lisa*).

Contemporaries included Niccolò Machiavelli (encountered during Borgia period) and members of the Este and Medici families.

## Major Works and Inquiries

Leonardo left a relatively small number of finished paintings but thousands of pages of notebooks filled with drawings, mirror-script notes, and inventions. Iconic completed or near-completed works include *The Last Supper*, the *Mona Lisa*, *Virgin of the Rocks* (two versions), *Saint Anne* cartoon and painting, and portraits. His scientific and technical studies encompassed:

- Human anatomy (dissections, layered drawings of skull, heart, muscles of expression).
- Optics, perspective, and the “science of art” (light, shadow, atmospheric perspective — *sfumato*).
- Hydraulics, geology, and fossils (used to question biblical flood chronology).
- Flight (bird studies, designs for ornithopters and parachutes).
- Mathematics, proportion, and engineering (ideal cities, canals, armored vehicles, bridges).

He repeatedly noted “Tell me if anything was ever done,” reflecting both his perfectionism and the vast number of projects left incomplete or only on paper.

## Significance and Counterfactual

Leonardo’s integration of empirical observation with artistic imagination and engineering fantasy made him the archetype of the “Renaissance Man.” His work helped establish dimensionality, anatomical accuracy, and naturalistic expression as central to Western art. His notebooks, though unpublished in his lifetime and scattered after his death, became foundational for later studies of anatomy, hydrodynamics, and flight.

Without Leonardo the historical record of the Italian Renaissance would lack its most famous visual symbols and its most compelling demonstration that rigorous science and transcendent art could spring from the same mind. His unfinished projects and “failures” (the Sforza horse destroyed, battle mural abandoned, flying machines never built) paradoxically enhanced his mythic status while also showing the limits of individual genius against material and political constraints.

## Historiography

Primary sources are dominated by Leonardo’s own notebooks (surviving in multiple codices) and a smaller body of contracts, letters, and contemporary testimony. Giorgio Vasari’s *Lives* (1550, revised 1568) is the most influential early biography but is hagiographic and sometimes inaccurate; it established the “divine genius” trope that later writers qualify.

Modern scholarship (Martin Kemp and others) emphasizes the notebooks as the central evidence and stresses the continuity between Leonardo’s artistic and scientific projects. Isaacson’s 2017 biography synthesizes this tradition with clear narrative and expert consultation. Debates persist over the exact dating and attribution of some works, Leonardo’s sexuality and personal life (largely undocumented), and the degree to which his “inventions” were practical versus visionary.
