# Itō Hirobumi (1841–1909)

The foremost statesman of [[meiji-period|Meiji Japan]] — chief architect of the
**[[meiji-constitution-1889|Meiji Constitution]]**, Japan's first prime minister, and the
leading figure of the oligarchy that built the modern Japanese state. A
[[samurai|low-ranking samurai]] of **Chōshū**, he rose from the *sonnō jōi* loyalist
underground to the summit of power. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v5-nineteenth-century|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 5]]
(Beasley; Jansen).

## Career

A protégé of the Chōshū reformers, Itō was one of a handful of young samurai **smuggled
abroad to study in Britain (1863)** before the Restoration — an experience that turned the
xenophobic loyalist into a convinced, pragmatic Westernizer. After the
[[meiji-restoration-1868|Restoration]] he rose fast in the new government, joined the
**[[meiji-modernization|Iwakura mission]] (1871–73)** to America and Europe, and emerged in
the 1870s — after the deaths of Ōkubo and Kido — as a (with Yamagata) dominant **genrō**.

## The constitution

Itō's central achievement was the **[[meiji-constitution-1889|Meiji Constitution]]**. Against
**Ōkuma Shigenobu's** call for a rapid British-style party government, Itō forced Ōkuma out
(1881), committed Japan to the **Prussian model**, studied constitutional government in
Germany and Austria (1882), and then **drafted the document in seclusion** with German
advisers. He built the institutions that surrounded it — a **European-style cabinet** (1885,
becoming **first prime minister**), a reformed peerage to stock the House of Peers, and the
**Privy Council** (which he chaired to ratify the constitution). His guiding maxim was to
"relax our hold over government but not yield it" — conceding the forms of representative rule
while keeping sovereignty with the throne and real power with the oligarchy.

## Later career and Korea

Itō served four terms as prime minister and, frustrated by obstruction in the Diet, founded
his own party, the **Seiyūkai (1900)** — co-opting the popular-rights politicians into the
governing system. After the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]] he became the
first **resident-general of Korea (1906)**, presiding over the protectorate that preceded
annexation; his **assassination by the Korean nationalist An Jung-geun at Harbin in 1909**
removed the leading restraining voice and was followed by Japan's formal **annexation of
Korea (1910)**.

## Counterfactual significance

Itō is the indispensable institution-builder of the Meiji state: more than any other figure
he **chose the Prussian constitutional path** that defined Japan's modern polity — its
strengths (a stable, "enlightened" framework that won treaty revision and great-power
recognition) and its fatal flaw (an executive and military insulated from parliamentary
control, which the [[world-wars-era|1930s militarists]] would exploit). His career embodies
the Meiji pattern: a loyalist samurai turned cosmopolitan bureaucrat remaking Japan from
above.
