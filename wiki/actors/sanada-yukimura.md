## Overview

Sanada Yukimura (1567–1615), also known as Sanada Nobushige, was the outstanding military commander of the Osaka garrison during the campaigns of 1614–1615. A veteran warrior who had been associated with the Toyotomi cause after years of conflict with the Tokugawa, he became the symbolic champion of resistance to Tokugawa hegemony and the most celebrated figure among the Osaka defenders. He was one of the ronin — masterless samurai — who answered Hideyori's call.

## Military Role

During the Winter Campaign Sanada constructed the celebrated *Sanada no Dejiro* (Sanada Barbican), a forward defense work "in front of the Hachome Gate with a wall and an empty moat, and threefold palisades, one on each side of the moat and one in the middle, with towers and embrasures to command this part of the wall." He advocated the bold offensive strategy of sallying out to seize Uji, Seta, and Fushimi, capturing and burning Kyoto to shock wavering daimyos into the Osaka camp — a plan the majority rejected.

When Ieyasu attempted to bribe key Osaka commanders, Sanada was among those who refused.

In the Summer Campaign of 1615 his charge was the military highpoint of the entire battle. After the Echizen division wavered and broke on the Tokugawa left, Sanada's men drove into the gap and pressed so hard that many Tokugawa troops fled as far as three ri. The Satsuma Chronicle recorded: "When Sanada attacked Ieyasu's men they gave way, and many fled as far as three ri to save their skins." Ieyasu himself, watching with only a single attendant, was forced to personally direct his staff to hold the line. The situation was only stabilized by the weight of numbers and the eventual rallying of the Echizen leaders.

## Death

Sanada fought until exhausted. He dismounted, sat on his campstool to rest, and then removed his helmet, saying: "I am Sanada Yukimura, an adversary no doubt quite worthy of you, but I am too exhausted to fight any more." He bared his head and was killed by Nishio Nizaemon of the Echizen matchlock troops. Hosokawa Tadaoki, noting this in his diary, observed that "since he was wounded and weary it was no great feat" — a backhanded acknowledgment of how hard he had fought to reach that state.

## Historical Reputation

The contemporary verdict on Sanada crossed factional lines. The Satsuma Chronicle (a Tokugawa source) called him "without doubt the first warrior in the Empire, and in the ancient histories we hear of none greater." Hosokawa Tadaoki, also on the Tokugawa side, listed him among those who "fell gloriously." He became one of the central heroic figures of Japanese popular culture in subsequent centuries, the archetype of the loyal warrior of a lost cause.

## Counterfactual Significance

Sadler's account strongly implies that had Sanada's bold offensive strategy been adopted in the Winter Campaign — seizing Kyoto before the Tokugawa forces arrived in strength — the outcome might have been genuinely uncertain. The defensive choice made Osaka's cause dependent on the impregnability of a castle whose outer defenses the Winter Campaign stripped away. Sanada's Summer Campaign charge also demonstrates that the battle itself was far closer than the historical outcome implies.
