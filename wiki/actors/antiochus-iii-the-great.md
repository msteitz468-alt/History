## Role

Antiochus III "the Great" (*Megas Basileus*, c. 242–187 BCE) was the greatest of the
later Seleucid kings — the restorer who brought the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucid
kingdom]] back "almost to the boundaries which it possessed at the death of its founder"
— and the king who first felt the heavy hand of Rome. He dominates CAH VII.1 ch. 6
(Musti) as the figure in whom the kingdom's possibilities and limits are clearest.

## Decisions and Significance

- **Restoring the centre**: he came to the throne (223) facing the revolts of the
  governors **Molon** (the upper satrapies) and **Achaeus** (Asia Minor), which he
  crushed, reasserting royal authority over a fragmenting realm.
- **The [[syrian-wars|Fourth Syrian War]] and [[battle-of-raphia-217|Raphia]] (217)**: his
  bid for Coele-Syria against **Ptolemy IV Philopator** — after first crushing the revolt
  of Molon in the east — ended in **defeat at Raphia**, the battle whose Seleucid order of
  battle ([[polybius|Polybius]] v.79–85), a multi-ethnic army with Indian elephants, is the
  single best description of a Hellenistic army (and the terminal event of CAH VII.1's
  narrative).
- **The *Anabasis* (212–205)**: his great eastern expedition through Media, Parthia, and
  Bactria — recovering tribute and formal submission (he recognised **Euthydemus of
  [[greco-bactrian-kingdom|Bactria]]** as king and renewed contact with India) but **not**
  reconquering the seceded east. It won him the title **"the Great"** and overawed his
  subjects "by his courage and his efforts" (Polybius).
- **The collision with Rome**: after seizing Coele-Syria (Panium, 200) and expanding into
  Thrace, he drifted into war with Rome over the cities Seleucus I had won from Lysimachus
  — and was beaten at **Magnesia (190)**, the [[peace-of-apamea-188|Peace of Apamea]]
  (188) stripping him of Asia Minor west of the Taurus. He died the next year plundering a
  temple in Elam to pay the indemnity.

## Counterfactual Significance

Antiochus III is the test-case of [[hellenistic-monarchy|spear-won kingship]]: his
*anabasis* shows that **victory itself was the title to rule** (it "made him appear
worthy of the throne"), and his restoration proved the Seleucid state could be
reconstituted by a capable king. But his defeat by Rome marks the structural turning
point — the moment the Hellenistic kingdoms passed from masters of their own balance of
power to subjects of Roman hegemony. His reign frames Musti's recurring theme: a vast,
multi-ethnic military monarchy, brilliant under a strong king but perpetually precarious.
