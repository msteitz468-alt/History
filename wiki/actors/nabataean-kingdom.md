## Overview

The Nabataeans were an Arab tribal people first attested in Hebrew and Assyrian sources by
the 7th century BCE as nomads of northwestern Arabia, possibly originating from further
south. According to David Ball (*Rome in the East*), they spoke a dialect akin to modern
Arabic, and the Nabataean script is a forerunner of the Arabic script; Ball places the
Nabataeans within the broader Arab civilizational continuum that later produced the
Umayyads, Abbasids, and Fatimids. Their kingdom, centered on [[petra|Petra]] and later
[[bosra|Bosra]], controlled the overland incense and spice trade of Arabia for roughly two
centuries before becoming, in 106 CE, the Roman Provincia Arabia.

## King List and Major Events (per Ball)

- **Harithath (Aretas) II** — conflict with Alexander Jannaeus of Judaea, c. 100 BCE.
- **'Obaydath (Obodas) I** — defeated and killed Antiochus XII of the Seleucids at the
  Battle of Cana (87 BCE), enabling major Nabataean territorial expansion; the rise of the
  Nabataean kingdom in southern Syria/Jordan contributed to the decline of the
  [[seleucid-empire|Seleucid Empire]] by capturing its trade routes ahead of Pompey's
  annexation (64/63 BCE).
- **Harithath III** (86–62 BCE) — extended borders to Damascus (84 BCE), likely incorporating
  the future [[decapolis|Decapolis]] cities; Damascus later lost to Tigranes of Armenia
  (72 BCE).
- **Maliku (Malichus) I** (from c. 54 BCE) — fought an indecisive war with
  [[herod-the-great|Herod the Great]]; preserved good relations with Octavian after Actium.
  Nabataeans burned a fleet Cleopatra had assembled in the Red Sea (intended to transport
  Antony and Cleopatra's forces to the Persian Gulf after their defeat at Actium), acting at
  Roman request.
- **'Obaydath II** (from 30 BCE).
- **Harithath IV** "lover of his people" (8 BCE–40 CE) — reign marks Nabataea's greatest
  prosperity and territorial extent (Sinai, southern Negev, Jordan, Hauran, Wadi Sirhan to
  Jawf, Red Sea coast to Hegra); possibly briefly deposed or annexed by Augustus (3 BCE–1 CE)
  amid post-Herod instability, with Hegra (Mada'in Saleh) argued by Ball as a capital-in-
  exile, before reinstatement following Gaius Caesar's fact-finding mission (1 CE). Allied
  with [[kingdom-of-edessa|Abgar V of Edessa]] in a war against Antipas (Herod's son),
  defeating him; Nabataeans may have briefly held Damascus again around the time of Paul's
  escape "in a basket" there (37 CE, per the biblical account). Tiberius planned punitive
  action (ordering the governor of Syria, Vitellius, to prepare an expedition) but this was
  called off on Tiberius's death.
- **Maliku (Malichus) II** (40–70 CE).
- **Rabbel II** (70–106 CE) — moved the capital from Petra to Bosra (93 CE), oversaw expanded
  irrigation, agriculture, and urbanization; died 106, after which Trajan annexed the kingdom.

Herod the Great's father Antipater was an Idumaean, married to Cypros, a member of the
Nabataean royal family, making Herod half-Idumaean, half-Nabataean by birth. After the
Parthian invasion of Judaea (40 BCE) installed the anti-Roman Hasmonaean Antigonus, Herod
fled first to the Nabataean king Maliku (Malchus) II at Petra before continuing to
Cleopatra's court and then Rome.

## Relations with Rome

First Roman incursions came via Pompey's lieutenant Scaurus (63 BCE, reaching only Pella)
and Gabinius (55 BCE), both inconclusive; Nabataea thereafter became an unofficial Roman
client kingdom, a status never explicitly stated in the sources, only implied. Aelius
Gallus's expedition to south Arabia (26 BCE) included 1,000 Nabataean auxiliaries; the
Nabataean guide Syllaeus's intrigues led to renewed war with Herod, ended by Roman
intervention (9 BCE). Titus's forces during the [[first-jewish-roman-war-66-74|First
Jewish–Roman War]] included a Nabataean contingent alongside those of Commagene and Emesa.

Ball describes Rome's rule of the Near East as a "patchwork of client kingdoms" — chiefly
Emesa, Judaea, Nabataea, Palmyra, and Edessa — governed by native client kings left largely
to their own affairs, a system Ball compares explicitly to the British Raj's Princely
States, ending in "creeping conquest": nominal independence followed by growing dependency
and eventual bloodless annexation. Trajan annexed the Nabataean kingdom peacefully in 106,
on Rabbel II's death, to form the Roman Province of Arabia (capital Bosra). Little source
material survives explaining the annexation's rationale; theories per Ball include
safeguarding Trajan's rear before his Parthian campaign, or opportunistic land-grabbing,
since the kingdom posed no real security threat — it never revolted and never bordered Iran.
For two centuries (Trajan to Diocletian) Arabia was defended by a single legion (III
Cyrenaica). The Via Nova Traiana (Aqaba to Bosra) was built in 111.

Ball identifies five distinct regions called "Arabia" in Roman sources: Arabia of the
eastern Nile delta (Egypt), Arabia of northern Mesopotamia, Nabataea/Arabia Petraea (the
only officially designated Roman province, Provincia Arabia), plus extra-imperial Arabia
Deserta and Arabia Felix (Yemen).

## Political Structure and Stability

Ball characterizes Nabataean political stability as exceptional: three long, peaceful
successive reigns (Harithath IV, 48 years; Maliku II, 30 years; Rabbel II, 46 years) without
the fratricidal violence typical of other Near Eastern monarchies. He cites Strabo's
description of Nabataea as "exceedingly well-governed," with a tribal structure — a king
ruling as shaikh, supported by tribal elders — rather than a fully Hellenized state.

## Economy and Trade

The Nabataean economy rested on the overland incense and spice trade routed through Petra.
This land-based trade declined over the 1st century CE as sea routes via Roman Egypt became
more competitive; south Arabian trading kingdoms shifted correspondingly from inland desert
polities (Ma'in, Saba/Sheba, Qataban) to highland Himyar oriented to the Red Sea. Despite
this, Nabataean hydrological and agricultural expertise sustained Petra's prosperity. The
shift of the political center to Bosra in 93 CE may reflect a trade-route shift toward the
Wadi Sirhan; Ball notes it was ultimately [[palmyra|Palmyra]], not Nabataea, that captured
the benefit of this northward shift in trade.

## Architecture and Religion

Petra's major monuments — the theatre, the Temple of Dushara (Qasr al-Bint), and probably
the Khazneh — date to the reign of Harithath IV. Ball argues Petra's architecture, though
blending Hellenistic, Roman, Egyptian, Assyrian, and Persian elements, should be classified
as "Nabataean," not "Roman," since virtually all major building there predates the 106 CE
annexation. Petra's famous baroque, Classical-influenced façades are only one of two
categories at the site; a larger group shows predominantly native Semitic/Arabian features
(Assyrian-style crow-step merlons, Egyptian-style flaring cornices, Nabataean pilasters)
uninfluenced by Classical elaboration, paralleled at Medain Saleh (Hegra). Rock-cut tomb
façades, per Ball, are not typical of Nabataean architecture generally — they occur only at
Petra and Medain Saleh, nowhere else in Nabataean territory.

Nabataean religion centered on the god Dushara, represented abstractly as a plain square
block of stone ("god-blocks"), which Ball parallels (as analogy, not literal descent) with
the pre-Islamic square Ka'ba at Mecca. Ball also notes scholarly disagreement over the
function of Petra's rock-cut "high places": the traditional reading is blood sacrifice, which
Ball argues is over-read from thin evidence; an alternative, minority interpretation (dating
to the 1960s) proposes some high places and monumental tomb façades functioned as exposure
platforms and ossuaries for secondary burial, by analogy with Zoroastrian/Sasanian Iranian
practice, citing Strabo's report that Nabataean kings' bodies were "left on refuse heaps
after death." Ball treats this as speculative but underappreciated; actual burial practice
at Petra likely varied. A Nabataean dedicatory inscription to the Sun god has been found in
Rome, and Ball speculates Christian veneration of "holy water" may derive as plausibly from
Nabataean ritual practice (water was central to Nabataean/Atargatis worship) as from the
Iranian Anahita cult — a **THESIS**-level claim, not established fact.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Nabataean kingdom's control of the incense trade and its stable middleman role
between Arabia and the Mediterranean, the routing and profile of Roman-era eastern commerce
would likely have developed earlier around Palmyra or the Red Sea sea-lanes; its
uncontested, peaceful annexation in 106 also set a template Rome would not always be able to
replicate with its more fractious client states (contrast [[palmyra|Palmyra]] under
[[zenobia|Zenobia]]).

## Historiography

The main modern source for this page is David Ball's *Rome in the East* (2000), whose
central and contested thesis is that Roman rule left only a superficial "veneer" over an
enduringly Semitic/Arab substrate — a framing under which the Nabataean kingdom is read as
a case of Arab political and cultural continuity later obscured by conventional Greco-Roman
narratives of the region. Mainstream scholarship (e.g., Fergus Millar, *The Roman Near
East*, 1993) generally reads the region as more thoroughly Graeco-Roman in character; see
[[orientalization-of-rome-thesis|the Orientalization of Rome thesis]] for the fuller debate.
