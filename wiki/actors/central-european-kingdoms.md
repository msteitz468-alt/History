# The Central European Kingdoms (Bohemia, Hungary, Poland)

The thirteenth century was decisive for the three **central European kingdoms** — Přemyslid
**Bohemia**, Árpád **Hungary**, and Piast **Poland** — which by 1300 differed "much less...
from their western counterparts than they had done in 1200." [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]]
(S.C. Rowell, Ch. 18c) treats them as a connected "community of central European realms"
undergoing a "second, consolidatory round of **westernisation**."

## A Connected Region

The *Chronicle of Greater Poland* expressed a myth of common Slavonic descent (the brothers
Lech, Rus and Czech) and of Germano-Slav symbiosis ("oxen ploughing a common furrow"). Their
shared experience was **large-scale German and western immigration** of farmers, artisans and
clergy; **competition between crown, nobility and Church**; a common need to reform the rural and
urban economy; and a common defence against the **[[mongol-invasion-of-europe-1241|Tatars]]**. By
1300 the Bohemian kings had "arrived" as imperial pretenders (Ottokar II and Vaclav II appear in
Dante's *Purgatorio*).

## The Three Kingdoms

- **Bohemia (Přemyslids).** Acknowledged as **kings** from 1198, the Přemyslids presided over
  "economic, political and cultural growth" — silver mines, the Prague groat (1300), and the
  great territorial expansion of **Ottokar II** into Austria, Styria and Carinthia, until his
  defeat and death by [[rudolf-of-habsburg|Rudolf of Habsburg]] at Dürnkrut (1278). The dynasty
  briefly united Bohemia, Hungary and Poland under Vaclav III (1305) before its extinction.
- **Hungary (Árpáds).** Like the England of John and Henry III, the last Árpád century was
  dominated by the **barons' struggle to wrest power from the crown**. **Andrew II** relied on the
  lesser nobility and parcelled out castles as "perpetual inheritances," turning royal counties
  into noble counties; the **Golden Bull of 1222** — central Europe's **Magna Carta** — guaranteed
  noble liberties, barred foreigners and Jews from office, and (article 31) gave the nobility a
  right of resistance. **Béla IV** rebuilt the kingdom after the Tatar catastrophe and deepened the
  cult of **St Stephen** and his crown.
- **Poland (Piasts).** Fragmented since 1138 into rival duchies, Poland was reunited only
  gradually; the **archbishopric of Gniezno** preserved the idea of a single Polish *gens* (a
  "shadow kingdom"), and a restored kingdom would emerge at **Cracow in 1320**. The cult of the
  martyr-bishop **St Stanisław** supplied a new national focus.

## The Tatar Trauma and Significance

The **[[mongol-invasion-of-europe-1241|Tatar invasion of 1241]]** — Legnica (where Henry II the
Pious died), the devastation of Hungary at Mohi — made "perhaps the greatest impact" on the
region, dominating its political and religious life for a century and spurring castle-building and
reform. Rowell stresses the period's **baronial constitutionalism**: the Golden Bulls of Hungary
(1222, 1231), the Cienia pact in Poland, and annual royal-noble assemblies parallel the
[[magna-carta-1215|English]] and Jerusalemite developments — the same "new general European
order" of military Orders, reformed chapters and a powerful high nobility, now embracing the
Slavonic and Magyar east.
