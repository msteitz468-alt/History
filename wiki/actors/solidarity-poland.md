## Overview

Solidarity (Solidarność) was the independent Polish trade union founded at the Gdańsk
shipyards on August 31, 1980, after a wave of strikes over food prices forced the
Communist government to negotiate. At its peak it claimed roughly 10 million
dues-paying members plus 3 million more in an affiliated peasant branch — out of a
national population of 36 million — making it, in effect, the voice of the Polish
nation rather than a conventional labor union. It was suppressed under martial law
from December 1981 but survived underground, reemerged to negotiate Poland's 1989
roundtable transition, and its leader Lech Wałęsa became the country's first
postcommunist president in December 1990.

## Role and Formation

Solidarity's roots lay in the distinctive conditions of Polish communism: private
peasant farming (never collectivized after 1956), and an autonomous, popular Catholic
Church that published its own press, ran the underground "Flying University," and —
after the October 1978 election of Karol Wojtyła as Pope John Paul II — fused Polish
national identity with Catholic identity even more tightly. The 1976 strikes produced
the Committee for the Defense of Workers (KOR), which supplied legal aid to arrested
strikers and opened a dialogue between labor activists and intellectual dissidents that
had not existed in 1970 or 1976.

When strikes broke out again in August 1980 over renewed food-price increases, KOR
activists helped formulate demands, but leadership fell to Lech Wałęsa, a shipyard
electrician fired after the 1976 strikes. Wałęsa's dramatic assertion of leadership —
climbing onto a bulldozer to take charge of the Gdańsk shipyard strike — became a
founding image of the movement. On August 31, 1980, Wałęsa and the government signed
the **Gdańsk Accords**, granting the right to form independent unions and to strike,
release of political prisoners, press access for diverse viewpoints, and broadcast of
Catholic Sunday Mass on state radio. In exchange, Solidarity formally accepted the
"leading role" of the Polish United Workers' Party (the Communists) — a concession many
rank-and-file members considered a betrayal.

## Fifteen Months of Legal Existence, 1980–1981

Between the Gdańsk Accords and martial law, Solidarity gave Poles their first experience
of democratic practice since the interwar period: free, contested union elections; open
debate at assemblies and conferences; and its own newspaper (from April 1981). Wałęsa
generally argued for moderation against a more radical rank and file. The union's
October 1981 congress adopted radical resolutions — free local elections, a second
legislative chamber representing nonparty interests, private ownership of small
businesses, and transfer of large enterprises to employee ownership — that invited
confrontation.

## Suppression and Underground Survival

General **Wojciech Jaruzelski**, newly combining the posts of defense minister, prime
minister, and party first secretary, declared martial law on the night of
December 12–13, 1981. Security police arrested nearly the entire top leadership
(conveniently gathered at one Gdańsk hotel), full censorship was reimposed, and military
courts were empowered to jail people for "spreading false information." Solidarity was
unprepared for the loss of its leaders, press, and freedoms; strikes flared but were
easily quashed for lack of coordination.

What survived was Solidarity as **symbol**: of democratic experience and — since the
crackdown was popularly attributed to Soviet pressure — of Polish national resistance to
foreign domination. Jaruzelski's attempts to sponsor alternative, regime-loyal unions in
the mid-1980s made little headway.

## Return to Legality and the 1989 Transition

By the late 1980s, Poland's deepening debt crisis forced the Communists to seek
Solidarity's cooperation to prop up the regime's faltering legitimacy. Roundtable talks
in spring 1989 won Solidarity the right to contest the senate and a third of the lower
house in elections on June 4, 1989 — the first free elections in Poland since 1939, and
anywhere in Eastern Europe since 1948. Solidarity won all but one contested seat.
Jaruzelski, unwilling or unable to reimpose martial law, appointed Solidarity activist
Tadeusz Mazowiecki as prime minister; Solidarity in turn supported Jaruzelski's own
reelection to the presidency to avoid provoking Soviet intervention — the opening move
of the wider [[revolutions-of-1989]].

Solidarity soon split into competing factions once in power. Wałęsa became president of
Poland in December 1990 but was defeated in the November 1995 presidential election by
former Communist official Aleksander Kwaśniewski, part of a broader Eastern European
pattern of ex-Communists returning to power through free elections amid the economic
pain of market transition.

## Historiography

Solidarity is among the most heavily studied movements of the late Cold War. Timothy
Garton Ash's *The Polish Revolution: Solidarity, 1980–82* (1984) is the classic
contemporary account; David Ost's *Solidarity and the Politics of Anti-Politics* (1990)
and Jadwiga Staniszkis's *Poland's Self-Limiting Revolution* (1984) offer competing
interpretive frameworks (the latter stressing Solidarity's deliberate avoidance of
outright revolutionary confrontation). Padraic Kenney's *A Carnival of Revolution:
Central Europe 1989* (2002) situates the underground survival of Solidarity within
broader Central European dissident networks.
