## Role

Perdiccas was the senior Macedonian officer who held [[alexander-the-great|Alexander's]]
empire together — for himself — in the two years after the king's death, and the first
of the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|Successors]] to fall. As **chiliarch** (the
Persian-derived rank meaning "first after the king"), inherited at Hephaestion's death,
he received Alexander's signet ring on the deathbed and presided over the **Babylon
settlement** of 323: the compromise that made the retarded Philip III Arrhidaeus and
the unborn Alexander IV joint kings, with Perdiccas as regent (*epimeletes*) in Asia.

## Decisions and Significance

Perdiccas embodied the **unitary idea** — the determination to preserve Alexander's
empire intact against the centrifugal ambitions of the satraps (certainly for his own
ultimate benefit). He completed the conquest of Cappadocia for [[eumenes-of-cardia|Eumenes]]
(322), the one marshal genuinely loyal to him; claimed the office of *prostates*; and,
fatally, entangled himself in dynastic marriage politics — repudiating Antipater's
daughter Nicaea to marry Alexander's sister Cleopatra, an open bid for the throne. This
united [[antipater]], Craterus, [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy]], [[lysimachus]], and
[[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus]] against him.

His downfall came over **Egypt**: when Ptolemy diverted Alexander's funeral cortège to
Memphis and refused his authority, Perdiccas invaded (321). The expedition stalled at
the Nile near Memphis with heavy losses, and his own officers — among them
[[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus]] — assassinated him. His death ended the first attempt to
hold the empire together and led directly to the reorganisation at **Triparadisus**.

Perdiccas' career reveals the structural lesson Will draws for the whole period: the
empire was a personal union with no institutional centre, and the man who tried to be
that centre could not command the loyalty the role required. See
[[wars-of-the-diadochi-323]], [[hellenistic-world]].
