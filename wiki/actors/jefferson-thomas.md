# Thomas Jefferson

Thomas Jefferson (1743–1826) was a Virginia planter, lawyer, revolutionary statesman, diplomat, third President of the United States, and principal author of the Declaration of Independence. He was the central political and intellectual architect of American independence and the early republic's commitment to liberty, religious freedom, and territorial expansion. His long public career spanned the House of Burgesses, Continental Congress, governorship of Virginia, diplomatic service in France, Secretary of State, Vice Presidency, and two terms as President (1801–1809). In retirement he founded the University of Virginia and renewed his correspondence with John Adams. No other American of his era matched the range of his achievements across government, law, education, architecture, science, and political philosophy.

## Life and Career

Born April 13, 1743 (O.S.) at Shadwell in what became Albemarle County, Virginia, to Peter Jefferson (surveyor, planter, magistrate, mapmaker) and Jane Randolph. Early childhood spent partly at Tuckahoe on the James (Randolph connections) before returning to Shadwell. Educated by local tutors (including James Maury) and at the College of William and Mary in Williamsburg (1760–1762), where he came under the decisive influence of Professor William Small (natural philosophy, Enlightenment) and began his legal apprenticeship with George Wythe. Admitted to the bar 1767; practiced in General Court and county courts while managing inherited lands and beginning construction of Monticello (1769 onward).

Marriage to Martha Wayles Skelton (January 1, 1772) brought additional lands and slaves via the Wayles inheritance (1773–74) and established the household at Monticello. Six children, of whom two daughters (Martha "Patsy" and Mary "Maria") reached adulthood. Domestic happiness was intense but brief; Martha died September 6, 1782, after the birth of their last child. Jefferson never remarried and was devastated.

Political entry: elected to House of Burgesses 1769 (age 25). Active in resistance to Stamp Act and Townshend duties. In 1774 drafted *A Summary View of the Rights of British America* for the Virginia Convention — a radical statement denying parliamentary authority over the colonies and grounding rights in nature and the Saxon migration. Elected to Continental Congress 1775; absent for much of the early sessions because of wife's health and father's death, but returned in time to draft the Declaration of Independence (Committee of Five; July 1776 adoption). Returned to Virginia to serve in the House of Delegates and on the committee (with Wythe, Mason, Pendleton) to revise the laws of the Commonwealth — producing enduring reforms on inheritance (abolition of primogeniture and entail), religious freedom (the 1786 statute), and education.

## The Great Contradiction: Slavery, Expatriation, and Bimodal Views

Ellis (2025) identifies Jefferson's position on slavery as having "hardened into a bimodal pattern during the mid-1780s." In *Notes on the State of Virginia* (1785) he wrote with passion: "a perpetual exercise of the most boisterous passions, the most unremitting despotisms on the one part, and degrading submissions on the other. Indeed, I tremble for my country when I reflect that God is just." Yet the other side concluded Blacks "inferior to whites in the endowments of both mind and body" and that "when freed they must be removed beyond the reach of mixture." In his unpublished autobiography (1821): "Nothing is more certainly written in the book of fate than that these people are to be set free. Nor is it less certain that the two races, equally free, cannot live in the same government. Nature, habit, opinion have drawn indelible lines of distinction between them."

The 1784 Ordinance (drafted by Jefferson) represented a high-water mark: territories to be republican states on equal footing; slavery to end no later than 1800. It "lost by one vote." Thereafter, ending slavery was held hostage to "expatriation" — a recipe for paralysis. At Monticello during Lafayette's 1824 visit, Jefferson kept the guest busy with University of Virginia tours to avoid the topic. Light-skinned enslaved people were visible ("mongrel negros... neither in point of colour or features showed the least trace... but their mothers being slaves, they retain the same condition"). After his death, >100k in debts led to the 1827 public auction of 130 "valuable Negros," breaking families and dispersing them "down the river." His arguments (via Thomas Dew) helped close Virginia's 1831 debate against gradual emancipation without removal. "All white men are created equal" became the effective reading.

Governor of Virginia 1779–1781 during the most dangerous phase of the war in the South. Faced British invasions under Benedict Arnold (1781) and Banastre Tarleton; the state government collapsed temporarily, the Assembly fled, and Jefferson narrowly escaped capture at Monticello. After leaving office he faced a legislative inquiry into his conduct; he was fully vindicated. The death of Martha in 1782 left him shattered.

Elected to Congress 1783. Key contributions included the report on government for the western territories (1784 ordinance principles) and Virginia's cession of its claims north of the Ohio. Commissioned (with Adams and Franklin) to negotiate treaties of commerce in Europe; sailed for France July 1784 as Minister Plenipotentiary. While in Paris he published *Notes on the State of Virginia* (1785), his only book-length work, a wide-ranging survey of natural history, society, law, religion, and politics that included his famous strictures on slavery and proposals for gradual emancipation.

## Key Relationships

- **Martha Wayles Jefferson**: Profoundly happy marriage; her death in 1782 left a permanent scar. Jefferson destroyed most of their correspondence.
- **John Adams**: Ally and collaborator in 1776 on independence and the Declaration; political rival in the 1790s; reconciled correspondent in retirement. Died the same day (July 4, 1826).
- **James Madison**: Closest political lieutenant and intellectual partner from the 1780s onward; co-author of the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions; successor as president.
- **George Washington**: Colleague in Virginia politics and the Revolution; served loyally as Secretary of State despite disagreements with Hamilton.
- **George Wythe**: Law mentor, colleague on the revisal committee, and lifelong model of classical republican virtue.
- **William Small**: William and Mary professor who opened the door to the Enlightenment and to Wythe.
- **Daughters**: Martha Randolph (manager of household and Monticello in later years) and Maria Eppes.

## Significance and Counterfactual

Jefferson gave the American Revolution its most memorable and philosophically resonant statement in the Declaration. As governor and legislator he translated revolutionary principles into concrete Virginia reforms (disestablishment, inheritance, education) that influenced other states. As president he doubled the territory of the United States (Louisiana Purchase) and demonstrated that a republic could survive partisan transfer of power and conduct a major territorial acquisition without war. His lifelong projects — religious freedom, public education, westward expansion under republican institutions — shaped the trajectory of the United States more than any other single founder except perhaps Washington.

Without Jefferson the Declaration might have been less eloquent and universal in phrasing; the Virginia model of church-state separation less decisive; the purchase of Louisiana more contested or delayed; the University of Virginia (his architectural and educational capstone) never founded. His contradictions on slavery — author of some of the strongest anti-slavery passages in the founding era yet a lifelong slaveholder who freed very few — remain central to the American paradox.

## Historiography

Malone's six-volume *Jefferson and His Time* (1948–1981) remains the most comprehensive scholarly narrative, especially strong on the Virginia years covered in Volume 1. The modern standard edition of the papers (Boyd et al., Princeton) transformed research after Malone wrote Vol. 1. Later biographers (Peterson, Ellis, Gordon-Reed on Sally Hemings and the family, Meacham, Ferling) have emphasized different facets: the "American Sphinx" elusiveness, the Sally Hemings relationship and its implications, the tension between liberty rhetoric and slaveholding practice, architectural genius, and scientific curiosity. Recent scholarship continues to debate the sincerity and consistency of Jefferson's anti-slavery views, the degree of his radicalism versus pragmatism, and the balance between his universalist language and his particular Virginia planter identity.

Collection note: Excellent coverage of the founding era via multiple biographical sources (Adams, Hamilton, Washington) and reference works. This Malone volume supplies the essential detailed grounding for Jefferson's formative period.

**Hartz (1955):** [[hartz-louis|Louis Hartz]] read Jefferson as divided between "a love of Europe and fear of its 'contamination,'" and argued his celebrated attacks on primogeniture and the Virginia Anglican establishment (1785) succeeded so easily because Virginia's operative premises were already Franklin's, not Bishop Laud's — "he had won his argument... long before he began to speak." Hartz also flagged the Progressive-school habit of pairing "a Jefferson for every Hamilton" as a comforting dualism masking the underlying [[lockean-consensus]] ([[hartz-liberal-tradition-1955]]).

**Hayek (1960):** In [[hayek-constitution-of-liberty-1960|*The Constitution of
Liberty*]] Hayek sorts Jefferson (after his French sojourn) into the
rationalist rather than the evolutionary liberal tradition (see
[[classical-liberalism]], [[constructivist-rationalism]]), preferring
[[madison-james|Madison]] as the American "Old Whig" exemplar — and notes the
illiberal streak: Jefferson wanted the political principles taught at his
University of Virginia "prescribed by authority" lest a professor prove "one
of the school of quondam federalism," which Hayek links to his opposition to
judicial independence.
