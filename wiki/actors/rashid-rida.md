## Overview

Rashid Rida (1865–1935) was a Syrian-born Islamic reformer and publicist who served as the primary institutional transmitter of [[islamic-modernism]] from its founding generation (represented by [[al-afghani]] and [[muhammad-abduh]]) to the inter-war period. As the editor and chief author of *al-Manar* (The Lighthouse, Cairo, 1898–1935), the most widely circulating Arabic journal of Islamic religious reform, he shaped the intellectual formation of Muslim modernists across the Arab world and into Southeast Asia. Over the course of his career he moved from the relatively liberal and rationalist Modernism of Abduh toward the stricter Shari-minded purism of the [[salafiyya]], eventually aligning with the Wahhabi-Saudi movement after Ibn Saud conquered the Hijaz (1924–25).

## Role in Islamic Modernism

Rida was Abduh's closest Syrian disciple and his literary executor. After Abduh's death (1905) he compiled and completed the *Tafsir al-Manar* (Quranic commentary begun by Abduh), ensuring that the Modernist hermeneutic reached a mass audience. He also systematized Abduh's legal reformism and pan-Islamic politics in a more explicitly Salafi direction — emphasizing return to the textual sources (*Quran* and authenticated *hadith*), rejection of *taqlid*, opposition to Sufism and popular devotional practices, and Arabic as the privileged Islamic language.

## *Al-Manar* and Its Reach

The journal was the primary vehicle through which Islamic Modernism reached the Maghrib, West Africa, and Southeast Asia — regions beyond the direct institutional influence of al-Azhar. Students who had attended al-Azhar or other Egyptian institutions carried it home; local reformers in Malaysia, Indonesia, West Africa, and elsewhere drew on it for a reformist vocabulary they could deploy against local religious establishments. Hodgson notes the movement Rida represented "eventually spread throughout the Arab lands and even to Malaysia."

## Trajectory: From Modernism to Salafism

The evolution of Rida's thought reflects the broader trajectory of Islamic reform under pressure:
- **Early phase** (1898–1914): Close adherence to Abduh's program — ijtihad, maslaha, engagement with modern science, cautious Quranic reinterpretation.
- **WWI and its aftermath** (1914–1924): The destruction of the Ottoman caliphate, the Sykes-Picot partition of the Arab east, and the failure of Arab political aspirations radicalized Rida's critique of Western power and Muslim accommodation. He became a proponent of restoring the caliphate and of tighter Islamic solidarity.
- **Post-Hijaz** (1924–1935): After the Saudi-Wahhabi conquest of the Hijaz, Rida found in the Wahhabi movement a practical ally. He endorsed the Wahhabi program as representing the kind of strict Shari-minded Islam, free of superstition and Sufi accretions, that he had long advocated. This convergence — noted by Hodgson — effectively aligned Salafism with the Saudi state: "this movement was shy of any radical social changes. Eventually it found itself in close sympathy with the Wahhâbî movement in Arabia in its more conciliatory twentieth-century form."

## Arab Nationalism and Islam

Rida's pan-Islamism had an Arabic inflection: "their potential pan-Islamism, calling for a federation in which Arabic would be the universal language, tended to amount to a demand only for a pan-Arab nationalism on a Muslim basis." He exalted Arabs as the special heirs and guarantors of Muhammad's mission — a theological claim that dovetailed with the cultural Arab nationalism emerging from the literary revival. In the inter-war period, however, as inter-faith Arabism and social reform agendas gained ground, the strict Salafi-Islamic basis lost popular traction.

## Significance

Rida is the pivot figure between nineteenth-century Islamic Modernism and twentieth-century Salafi-Islamism. His career illustrates how the same reformist impulse — purging Islam of accretions, reopening ijtihad, asserting Arab-Islamic authenticity — could be directed either toward liberal accommodation with modernity (Abduh's direction) or toward strict Shari-minded conservatism (the Wahhabi direction). The path taken depended on political context: the mounting failures of Arab national projects under European hegemony made the more radical, uncompromising position more attractive.
