# Zimri-Lim of Mari

The last great king of **[[mari|Mari]]** on the middle Euphrates (reigned
~1775–1761/1759 BCE), an [[amorites|Amorite]] dynast whose reign produced the
**Mari archive** — some 20,000 tablets, the richest documentary window onto the
[[middle-bronze-age|Middle Bronze Age]] Near East. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. I, Kupper).

## Exile and reconquest

Zimri-Lim was heir of the native Mari dynasty (his father Yahdun-Lim had built
Mari's greatness) whom **[[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]]** had dispossessed,
installing his own son **Yasmah-Adad** as viceroy. Zimri-Lim took refuge at
**[[iamkhad|Aleppo]]**, married the daughter of king **Yarim-Lim**, and — on
Shamshi-Adad's death and the collapse of his empire — **regained his father's
throne** (~1775), almost certainly with Aleppo's military backing. "Truly it is my
father[-in-law] who has caused me to regain my throne," he wrote.

## A king of the second rank

Zimri-Lim's kingdom was the **middle Euphrates and Khabur valleys** — a "line of
communication" rather than a territorial heartland, with no natural defences and a
desert frontier. He governed by **diplomacy more than conquest**: drawing the petty
kings of the "High Country" of Upper Mesopotamia into alliance (he lacked the
resources to annex them), maintaining resident ambassadors at the great courts, and
keeping permanent loyalty to **[[iamkhad|Aleppo]]** and to **[[hammurabi|Babylon]]**
— the powers that guarded the two ends of his trade route. His correspondents'
candid reports are the source of the famous "no king is mighty by himself"
assessment of the [[interpolity-law|Mari-age balance of power]]. Much of his energy
went on the unending struggle with the **nomads** of the steppe (the Benjaminites,
Sutians; see [[amorites]]).

## The palace, the archive, and the wealth of Mari

Zimri-Lim built the latest phase of the **great palace of Mari** — more than 260
rooms over six acres, frescoed (the famous "Investiture" scene of the king before
Ishtar), with a scribal school, archive rooms, magazines and workshops; a prince of
distant **Ugarit** sought an introduction merely to see it. Mari's prosperity rested
not on its irrigated fields (in a war-famine Zimri-Lim had to import grain from
Syria) but on its position astride the **Gulf-to-Mediterranean caravan route**:
Anatolian and Elamite **tin** moving west toward Aleppo, Qatna, Carchemish and Hazor;
Cypriot copper, Lebanese timber, even an object "from Crete" sent on to Hammurabi.
The *kārum* of Sippar kept a trade mission at Mari. "The whole of western Asia at
that time shared a common civilization," through which "men and merchandise could
move from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean."

## The fall

Zimri-Lim's friendship with **[[hammurabi|Hammurabi]]** lasted only "so long as
Hammurabi was kept occupied on other frontiers." After helping Hammurabi against the
Elam-Eshnunna coalition, Zimri-Lim was discarded: Hammurabi defeated Mari in his
33rd year (~1761) and **dismantled the city** two years later. Mari "survived but
went into complete decline"; out of its ruins rose the small successor **land of
Khana**, centred on Terqa. "The middle Euphrates would never again seem a political
factor of any importance."

## Significance

Zimri-Lim matters less for what he did than for **what his archive lets us see**: a
multipolar diplomatic world of roughly equal kings, resident ambassadors, dynastic
marriage, espionage, and constant coalition-building — the documentary foundation
for the modern, deflated understanding of [[hammurabi|Hammurabi]] and of the whole
[[middle-bronze-age|Middle Bronze Age]] political system. He is also the wiki's
clearest case of a **trade-route kingdom**: rich, brilliant, and fragile, "more
brilliant than solid," doomed once a real military power had its hands free.

## Historiography

Almost everything known about Zimri-Lim comes from the **Mari palace archive**
(French excavations under Parrot from 1933), whose letters are datable only with
difficulty because the year-names cannot be securely ordered. His absolute dates ride
on the [[mesopotamian-chronology|middle-chronology]] synchronism with Hammurabi. The
archive's vividness gives Mari a documentary prominence out of proportion to the
kingdom's real weight — a sampling bias worth flagging. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
