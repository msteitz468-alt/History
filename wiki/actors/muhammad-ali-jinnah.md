# Muhammad Ali Jinnah

**Muhammad Ali Jinnah** (1876–1948), the **Quaid-i-Azam** ("Great Leader"), was the architect of
**Pakistan** — the lawyer-politician who turned the Muslim League into the vehicle of a separate Muslim
nationhood. Documented for the wiki through Percival Spear's
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965).

## From "ambassador of unity" to two-nation theory

Jinnah began as a Congress moderate and a champion of **Hindu–Muslim cooperation** (the architect of
the 1916 Lucknow Pact), opposed to separate electorates. Disillusioned with the Gandhian mass movement
and with Congress's claim to speak for all India, he came to embrace the **two-nation theory** — that
India's Muslims (the "hundred millions," as he liked to say) were a distinct nation, not a minority.
Finding in the moribund Muslim League a vehicle, he revived it as the **1935 Act** took shape and, in
**1940**, proclaimed **Pakistan** the League's goal.

## Tactics and the winning of Pakistan

Spear paints a vivid portrait: behind "the impassive face, the slow and measured voice, the neat Bond
Street tailored figure" lay "an iron will and nerve, a supreme tactical skill … and the ability to
stir the masses almost as great as Gandhi's own" — remarkably, "the man who could not speak Urdu could
move the Muslim multitude." His method was **consistent obstruction**: "time was on his side," every
week of deadlock deepening middle-class Muslim commitment and his mass support. He met "Congress
negation to the British with Muslim negation to the Congress," called the ruinous **Direct Action Day
(1946)**, and "not yielding an inch," ultimately "succeeded in presenting the Congress with the choice
of **Pakistan or chaos**" — a choice "only Gandhi was willing to face." He became Pakistan's first
Governor-General, dying barely a year after its creation.

## Significance

Jinnah is the indispensable maker of [[partition-of-india-1947|Partition]] and of Pakistan — a figure
Spear treats with considerable (if cool) respect as a master tactician whose intransigence, more than
any positive blueprint, forced the division of the subcontinent. His legacy is a nation founded on
religious nationalism and the long India–Pakistan antagonism that followed.
