# The Fatimid Caliphate

The **Fatimid Caliphate** (909–1171) was the great **Ismaili Shi'a** empire that arose in **North Africa**
and conquered **Egypt**, founding **Cairo** and challenging the Sunni Abbasids for leadership of the Islamic
world. It is the central North African power of the **UNESCO [[gha-vol3-7th-11th-century|General History of
Africa, Vol. III]]** (ch. 12, Hrbek; ch. 7, Bianquis) — an empire **born in Africa**, not imported into it.

## Berber beginnings

The Fatimids rose not in the Arab heartland but among the **Kutama Berbers** of Ifriqiya (modern Algeria/
Tunisia), won over by the **Ismaili** missionary **Abu Abd Allah al-Da'i**. In **909** the movement
overthrew the Sunni Aghlabids and proclaimed **'Ubayd Allah al-Mahdi** — who had slipped into Sijilmasa
disguised as a merchant — as **caliph**, claiming descent from the Prophet's daughter **Fatima** (hence
"Fatimid") and the imamate. They built a new capital, **Mahdiyya**, on the Tunisian coast. The Fatimids are
thus, GHA stresses, a case of **Berber agency** raising a caliphate that dominated the Mediterranean — the
counterpart in the east to the [[almoravids|Almoravid]] movement in the west.

## The rise, 909–969: the four Ifriqiyan Imam-caliphs (Jiwa)

[[jiwa-fatimids-rise-2018|Shainool Jiwa's *The Fatimids: 1*]] traces the dynasty's first six decades in detail
from the recovered **Ismaili sources**. The clandestine [[ismaili-movement|Ismaili]] [[dawa|da'wa]], directed
from concealment (*dawr al-satr*) at **Salamiyya** in Syria, was carried to the [[berbers|Kutama Berbers]] of
[[ifriqiya|Ifriqiya]] by the da'i [[abu-abd-allah-al-shii|Abu Abd Allah al-Shi'i]] from c. 893. Four
Imam-caliphs ruled from North Africa before the move to Egypt:

- **[[al-mahdi-billah|al-Mahdi bi'llah]]** (909–934) — founder; freed from Aghlabid arrest at
  [[sijilmasa|Sijilmasa]], proclaimed at [[qayrawan|Qayrawan]] (910), executed his own founding da'i (911),
  and built the fortress-capital [[mahdiyya|Mahdiyya]]. See [[foundation-of-the-fatimid-caliphate-909]].
- **[[al-qaim-bi-amr-allah|al-Qa'im]]** (934–946) — led the first Egyptian expedition (914); survived the
  near-fatal **[[khariji-revolt-of-abu-yazid-943|revolt of Abu Yazid]]**.
- **[[al-mansur-fatimid|al-Mansur]]** (946–953) — crushed Abu Yazid, rebuilt the state, appointed a **Sunni
  Maliki chief judge**, and founded [[mansuriyya|Mansuriyya]].
- **[[al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Mu'izz]]** (953–975) — codified Ismaili law through [[qadi-al-numan|Qadi
  al-Nu'man]]'s *Da'a'im al-Islam*, reconciled the eastern da'wa, won the Sicilian wars with Byzantium through
  the Kalbids, and ordered the **[[fatimid-conquest-of-egypt-969|conquest of Egypt]]** (969) and the founding
  of Cairo.

Jiwa's corrective emphasis is that the Fatimid record — long dominated by hostile Abbasid-Sunni chronicles
after the destruction of the Cairo libraries — can now be reconstructed from **insider Ismaili texts** (Qadi
al-Nu'man's *Iftitah al-da'wa* and *Da'a'im*, the *Sirat al-Ustadh Jawdhar*, Idris Imad al-Din).

## Conquest of Egypt and the founding of Cairo

After earlier failures, the general **Jawhar** conquered **Egypt in 969** for the caliph **al-Mu'izz** and
founded a new royal city beside Fustat — **al-Qahira ("the Victorious"), Cairo** (970) — with the mosque-
university of **al-Azhar**, which became (and remains) a great centre of learning. From Egypt the Fatimids
at their height (later 10th–11th c.) ruled North Africa, the Hejaz with the holy cities, and much of Syria,
and controlled both ends of the **Red Sea–Indian Ocean** and **trans-Saharan gold** trades — the bullion
that underpinned their famously pure gold dinar.

## Decline and end

The Fatimids' western base unravelled when their **Zirid** viceroys in Ifriqiya **renounced Shi'ism for
Sunni Abbasid allegiance** (mid-11th c.); the Fatimids retaliated by loosing the nomadic **Banu Hilal**
Arabs westward — the **Hilali invasion** that GHA treats as a watershed, accelerating the **Arabization** of
the Maghrib's countryside. Weakened by factional strife and the **Crusades**, the caliphate was finally
**abolished by Saladin in 1171**, who restored Egypt to Sunni Abbasid allegiance.

## Peak, crisis, and the Frankish war (Cobb)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|*The Race for Paradise*]] situates the Fatimids as one pole of a
**triple caliphate** map by 1000 (with Abbasids of Baghdad and Umayyads of Córdoba): domains including
Sicily, western Arabia, Yemen, and much of Syria–Palestine. Peak “hot” sectarian reach included
**al-Basasiri** holding Baghdad for months (1059) and proclaiming the Fatimid name in its pulpits.
After ~1000, Persian Gulf–India trade rerouted via the Red Sea under Fatimid control; Egypt/Cairo
became a commercial prism linking Mediterranean and Indian Ocean, drawing western European (*Ifranja*)
markets into Fatimid-dominated Mediterranean commerce. Socially they remained a small Ismaʿili ruling
minority over mostly Coptic countryside and urban Sunnis; administration employed Christians, Jews,
and Muslims — “you didn’t have to be Ismaʿili to make it big.”

**Institutions and crisis.** Fatimid Egypt was exceptional among c. 1050 Islamic polities for a
powerful navy (while timber lasted) and for recruiting *mamluk*/*ghilman* from Africans and Slavs as
well as Turks. Caliph **al-Hakim** ordered the Church of the Holy Sepulcher leveled (1009; later
rebuilt with Byzantine help) — a memory-laden act for later Latin holy-war discourse (possible papal
appeal of Sergius IV, if genuine). The 1060s **Great Calamity** (*al-shidda al-ʿuẓma*) — low Nile
floods, near-uninterrupted famines for roughly two decades, Turkish vs black-slave army faction
fighting in Cairo streets — ended when **Badr al-Jamali** (from Acre) became vizier (1073) with
Armenian troops, militarizing the state until 1171.

**Al-Afdal (fold) and the First Crusade.** Badr’s son **al-Afdal** succeeded as vizier but mishandled
succession: he proclaimed youngest son **al-Mustaʿli** over older **Nizar**, crushed Nizar’s Alexandria
rebellion, and murdered him — producing the **Nizari** schism. In 1071 Fatimids had hired Turcoman
warlord **Atsiz** against Bedouin in Palestine; unpaid, he occupied Jerusalem and southern Syria and
took Damascus (1075), sacking Jerusalem after a 1078 revolt. After Antioch fell to the Franks (1098),
al-Afdal’s army retook Jerusalem (siege “somewhat over forty days,” 40+ trebuchets; surrendered Sept
1098; Saljuq representatives treated well and allowed to leave). News that al-Afdal marched “with a
mighty army to engage in jihad” pushed Franks to take Jerusalem before relief (1099). At **Ascalon**
the Egyptians “had no intelligence” of the Frankish approach; the camp was routed and al-Afdal
retreated to Cairo; Ascalon citizens negotiated a costly truce. Egypt was “the biggest loser” early
on: failure at Ascalon forced a coastal-foothold policy; a later Egyptian army (said ~10,000) toward
Ascalon was defeated, though Ascalon itself remained unconquered for decades.

**Final phase.** Fatimids retained Ascalon as a Palestine bridgehead until Baldwin III took it (1153);
by 1155 Egypt paid tribute to Jerusalem. Mid-12th-c. caliphs after al-Hafiz (d. 1149) were child
puppets; the vizierate was the real prize. Shawar–Dirgham strife and Amalric’s invasions opened the
door to Nur al-Din’s intervention under **Shirkuh**; Shirkuh’s nephew **Saladin** became last Fatimid
vizier, phased out the Fatimid army, and after al-ʿAdid’s death (13 Sept 1171) proclaimed the Baghdad
Abbasid caliph in Friday prayer — Egypt’s first Sunni caliphal naming in over two centuries.

## Significance

The Fatimids show **North Africa as a maker, not merely a recipient, of Islamic history**: a Berber-based,
Egypt-centred empire that founded Cairo and al-Azhar, rivalled Baghdad, and tied the Mediterranean to the
gold of [[sub-saharan-africa|sub-Saharan Africa]]. Their Hilali reprisal reshaped the ethnic and linguistic
map of the Maghrib. In Cobb’s Mediterranean frame they were also the Near East’s principal early
**naval–coastal** counterweight to the Franks — and the first major Muslim power structurally crippled by
the First Crusade’s coastal losses.
