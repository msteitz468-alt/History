# Edward Lansdale

U.S. Air Force officer and [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] operative who played a prominent role in the American advisory and psychological warfare effort in Vietnam, especially in support of Ngo Dinh Diem in the mid-1950s. He is often (though not exclusively) identified as a model for Graham Greene's Alden Pyle in *The Quiet American*. In Alfred McCoy's account he became the archetype of the "man on the spot" — an individual field operative wielding outsized personal autonomy in Cold War covert operations.

## Role

Lansdale advocated civic action, psychological operations, and the building of a non-Communist Vietnamese alternative to both the French and the Viet Minh. He was deeply involved in the consolidation of Diem's power after Geneva and in early counterinsurgency thinking.

## Career (per McCoy)

He served in the Philippines with OSS/intelligence duties in 1945–48, returned as a CIA officer in 1950 to lead a six-person advisory team supporting Philippine defense secretary Ramon Magsaysay against the communist-led Huk rebellion, funneling roughly $1 million in CIA cash and $250,000 in corporate funds to Magsaysay's successful 1953 presidential campaign. He was sent to Saigon in 1954 on Secretary of State [[dulles-john-foster|John Foster Dulles]]'s instruction — "I want you to do what you did in the Philippines," per McCoy's quotation — where he built a close relationship with President [[ngo-dinh-diem|Ngo Dinh Diem]], helped defuse a 1954 coup attempt by General Nguyen Van Hinh, and won over Cao Dai commander Trinh Minh The. In this period he unwittingly recruited Pham Xuan An, later revealed as a senior Hanoi intelligence officer operating inside Saigon throughout the [[vietnam-war|Vietnam War]]. In November 1961 Lansdale was placed in charge of Operation Mongoose, a $50 million covert campaign of sabotage, psychological warfare, and (per a CIA memo McCoy cites) assassination planning against Fidel Castro's Cuba, disbanded after the onset of the October 1962 [[cuban-missile-crisis-1962|Cuban Missile Crisis]]. He was buried at Arlington National Cemetery in 1987.

**Attribution dispute (documented in sources).** McCoy explicitly credits Lieutenant Colonel Charles Bohannan, not Lansdale, as the originator of the Philippine psywar tactics popularly associated with Lansdale's legend; Bohannan is quoted: "I was the one who developed the use of superstitions here in the Philippines that Lansdale described in his RAND Corporation report." McCoy frames Lansdale's reputation as resting substantially on self-promotion rather than sole authorship of the tactics and outcomes credited to him — a revisionist reading of a canonical Cold War figure, not a settled biographical consensus (see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]]).
