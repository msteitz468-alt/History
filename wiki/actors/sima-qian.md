# Sima Qian (c. 145–86 BCE)

The **founder of Chinese historiography** — author of the ***Shi ji*** (*Records of the Grand
Historian*), the monumental history of China from legendary antiquity to his own day that
defined the form and standards of Chinese history-writing for two thousand years. Among readers
of Chinese he commands "a respect and admiration comparable to that accorded **Herodotus or
Thucydides**" in the West. From his own [[sima-qian-records-qin|*Records of the Grand
Historian*]] (trans. Burton Watson).

## Life and the great catastrophe

Sima Qian succeeded his father **Sima Tan** as *Taishi* ("Grand Historian") at the court of
**Emperor Wu of Han**, an office concerned chiefly with astronomy and the calendar, and took up
his father's dying charge to complete a comprehensive history. After some seven years' work his
life was shattered by the **Li Ling affair (99 BCE)**: when the young general Li Ling, having
fought desperately against the Xiongnu, surrendered, the enraged emperor condemned him, and Sima
Qian — alone — spoke in his defense. Charged with deceiving the ruler, he was imprisoned,
tortured, and sentenced to **castration**. A man of honor was expected to commit suicide rather
than suffer such disgrace; but, as he explains in his famous **Letter to Ren An**, Sima Qian
chose to **endure the shame in order to finish his history**. That choice — "ghastly as it was"
— secured both the *Shi ji* and his place among the world's great historians. (He was afterward
made Palace Secretary and served on.)

## The *Shi ji* and its form

Sima Qian devised "an entirely new historiographical form," organizing 130 chapters into five
divisions:

- **Basic Annals** (*benji*) — the dynasties and reigns;
- **Chronological Tables** — events dated in graph form;
- **Treatises** — topical essays (rites, music, astronomy, religion, water control, economics);
- **Hereditary Houses** — the feudal states;
- **Biographies** (*liezhuan*) — famous individuals and foreign peoples.

This structure became the template for the official "Standard Histories" of every later dynasty.
A hallmark is the closing personal verdict — "**The Grand Historian remarks**" — in which Sima
Qian renders a candid **moral and political judgment** on his subjects, the historian's voice
made explicit.

## Method, purpose, and bias

Sima Qian's purpose was **didactic and moral**: "to preserve for posterity the moral and
political lessons of the past even when he could not always be certain of the facts." Where his
sources were thin — the [[qin-shi-huang|First Emperor]] had **burned the records** of the rival
states — he drew on dramatic, semi-fictional material (the *Intrigues of the Warring States*),
producing accounts "closer to historical fiction than true history" yet "highly effective as
literature." His judgments are shaped by **Confucian** ethics and a loathing of **Legalism** and
its harsh penal methods — a loathing surely deepened by his own ordeal in the palace prison.
Crucially, his hostile portrait of the Qin is also an **indirect critique of his own master,
[[han-wudi|Han Wudi]]**, who (Sima Qian saw) resembled the First Emperor in his expansionism,
harsh laws, ruinous projects, and quest for immortality. His chapters on Wu's reign (in
[[sima-qian-records-han-ii|*Records of the Grand Historian: Han Dynasty II*]]) sharpen the point
through the **collective biographies** — the **"Biographies of the Harsh Officials"** (a quiet
indictment of Legalist cruelty under Wu), the sympathetic **"Wandering Knights,"** and the
appreciative **"Money-makers"** — a method of moral commentary by juxtaposed lives that became a
model for Chinese historiography.

## Significance

Sima Qian is the **father of the Chinese historical tradition** — the most read and influential
of early Chinese historians, who gave Chinese civilization both its master narrative (from the
legendary sages through the [[zhou-dynasty|Zhou]], [[qin-shi-huang|Qin]], and [[han-dynasty|Han]])
and the **literary form, moral seriousness, and critical voice** of its historiography. The
*Shi ji* is at once the principal primary source for early China and a literary masterwork.
