# Kingdom of Judah (the Southern Kingdom)

The **Kingdom of Judah** — the smaller, poorer, but far more **dynastically stable**
southern kingdom — retained **Jerusalem**, the **Temple**, and the unbroken
**Davidic line** after the schism of 931 BCE, and outlived its northern neighbour by
nearly a century and a half. It is one of the two [[hebrew-kingdom|Hebrew kingdoms]].
[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] (Chs. 10–11, T. C. Mitchell) covers it
from Rehoboam to the age of Uzziah.

## A Stable Davidic Realm

Where [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] cycled through dynasties by assassination, Judah
was ruled by **one dynasty — the House of David — for its entire existence**, a
continuity rooted in the legitimacy of Jerusalem and the Temple cult. It was the
weaker partner: smaller, more arid, off the main trade arteries, and frequently in
Israel's shadow or under its influence (the two royal houses intermarried, most
fatefully when a Davidic prince married **Athaliah**, daughter of the
[[omride-dynasty|Omride]] line). Judah's history is accordingly quieter and, for the
period of this volume, less eventful than Israel's.

## Early Reigns and the Athaliah Crisis

**Rehoboam** lost the north and then, in his fifth year (**925 BCE**), suffered the
invasion of **[[shoshenq-campaign-925bce|Shoshenq I]]**, surrendering the Temple
treasures. He fortified a ring of cities in the Judahite hills. His successors (Abijah,
Asa, Jehoshaphat) fought or allied with Israel and with [[hazael|Damascus]] by turns.
The dynasty's gravest crisis came after the [[jehu-revolt-841bce|revolt of Jehu]]
(841): Jehu killed the visiting Judahite king Ahaziah, whereupon the queen mother
**Athaliah** — an Omride — seized the throne and tried to **exterminate the Davidic
heirs**. The infant **Jehoash (Joash)** was hidden in the Temple for six years by the
priest **Jehoiada**, then enthroned in a coup that killed Athaliah and restored the
Davidic line — the dynasty's narrowest escape.

## Prosperity under Uzziah

In the early eighth century, in parallel with **Jeroboam II** of Israel, Judah's king
**Uzziah (Azariah)** presided over a long, prosperous reign: he maintained a equipped
standing army, expanded toward Edom and the Philistine coast, and shared in the
trade-borne wealth of the age. Mitchell connects the archaeological signs of growth —
and of widening inequality — at Judahite and Israelite sites with the social critique
of the contemporary prophets **Amos and Hosea**, who indicted Judah as well as Israel
for injustice and for tolerating the rural cult "high places." Beyond the volume's
horizon, Judah would survive the Assyrian destruction of Israel, endure Sennacherib's
701 invasion, and fall at last to [[neo-babylonian-empire|Babylon]] in 587 BCE.

## Judah under Assyria and Babylon (CAH III.2, Chs 29–31)

[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Chs. 29–31, T. C. Mitchell) carries
Judah from the coming of Assyrian domination to the Exile — the kingdom's last century
and a half, lived in the shadow of two empires:

- **Ahaz and the Syro-Ephraimite War (c. 734).** When [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] and
  Aram-Damascus attacked Judah to force it into an anti-Assyrian coalition (aiming to
  replace **Ahaz** with a puppet, "Ben-Tabeel"), Ahaz **appealed to
  [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]]** — "I am your servant and your son" —
  against the prophet **Isaiah's** counsel, making Judah an Assyrian vassal and importing
  pagan cult (a Damascus-style altar, child sacrifice in the Valley of Hinnom).
- **[[hezekiah|Hezekiah]] (716/715–687).** Reversed Ahaz's apostasy, attempted to
  **centralize worship in Jerusalem**, cut the **Siloam tunnel**, and led the revolt that
  brought **[[sennacherib-siege-jerusalem-701bce|Sennacherib's invasion of 701]]** — from
  which Jerusalem, alone, emerged unconquered.
- **Manasseh and Amon (687–640).** Mitchell's long treatment of **Manasseh** (45 years)
  records the deepest apostasy — pagan altars and a cult-image *in the Temple itself*,
  child sacrifice, sorcery — and his loyal vassalage to **[[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]]** and
  **[[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]]** (he paid tribute and supplied troops; Chronicles has
  him briefly taken in chains to Babylon). His son **Amon** was assassinated after two years.
- **[[josiah|Josiah]] (640–609).** The great reformer: the discovery of the **Book of the
  Law** (Deuteronomy) in 623/622, the purge of pagan cult, the **centralization of worship
  in Jerusalem**, and the brief **reannexation of the former northern provinces** as Assyria
  collapsed — ended by his death at **[[battle-of-megiddo-609bce|Megiddo (609)]]**.
- **The last kings (609–587).** **Jehoahaz** (deposed by Necho after 3 months); **Jehoiakim**
  (Egyptian then Babylonian vassal, who burned Jeremiah's scroll and rebelled); **Jehoiachin**
  (deported 597); **Zedekiah**, whose revolt brought the **[[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|
  destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple (587/586)]]** and the **[[babylonian-exile|
  Babylonian Exile]]**. The archive of the **Lachish ostraca** documents the final siege.

The kingdom's destruction was not its end: the exiles **returned** under
[[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]] (538) and rebuilt the Temple (516) — see [[babylonian-exile]].

## Significance

- `caused_by:` the schism of 931 (see [[hebrew-kingdom]]); `affiliated_with:` the
  Davidic dynasty and the Jerusalem Temple, the sources of its exceptional stability.
- `produced:` the **continuity of the Davidic tradition** — the institutional and
  ideological seedbed of later Judaism, messianism, and (through them) Christianity;
  and, after 587, the **Babylonian Exile** that crystallized the Hebrew scriptures.
- `contrasts_with:` [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] — same religion and origin, opposite
  political trajectory (one dynasty vs. serial usurpation).

## Historiography

Judah is the **home perspective of the book of Kings and of Chronicles**, so it is
better and more sympathetically documented than Israel — but the same Deuteronomistic
theological framing applies, judging kings by cultic fidelity. External anchors are
fewer than for Israel until the eighth century (Joash named by Adad-nirari III, 802;
Ahaz by Tiglath-pileser III, 732). The **chronology** rests on E. R. Thiele's
reconstruction of the Kings synchronisms (with co-regencies and two regnal-dating
systems), the framework Mitchell adopts. The collection is strong on the biblical text
and Albright-school archaeology.

## Dever (2017): the eighth–seventh-century state

[[sources/dever-beyond-the-texts-2017|Dever (2017)]] documents Judah's maturation through its
administrative archaeology. Eighth-century population peaked at ~137,000 (307 sites), nearly
half in the Shephelah, ~90% rural. The **lmlk** ("belonging to the king") system — ~1,700
stamped four-handled store jars from 70+ sites (densest at [[lachish|Lachish]] 407, Jerusalem
275+, Ramat Rahel 160), petrographically made in one Shephelah workshop — marks royal
provisioning for the Assyrian invasions (see [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]]). A standardized
**Judahite sheqel weight system** (~11.33 g standard, ~350 weights, mostly post-721) implies
"the regulation of commerce by a centralized government," and Kletter uses the weights to map
Judah's borders. **Judean pillar-base figurines** (hundreds, confined to Judah, late 8th–early
6th c.) are now widely read as **Asherah** images. After 701 the Shephelah was gutted, but —
Faust argues — Judah's **maximum population and prosperity actually came in the seventh
century**: "The gloomy description of that period is a result mainly of our biased
perspective." The Jerusalem **House of the Bullae** (51 sealings, incl. "Gemariah son of
Shaphan," a scribe of Josiah's reign) ties epigraphy to the late-monarchy narrative.

### Noll (2013)

[[sources/noll-canaan-israel-antiquity-2013|K. L. Noll (2013)]] reads Judah as a late-maturing
polity in the [[canaan|Canaanite cultural sphere]] whose full statehood comes in the eighth–
seventh centuries; he favours a Low/Middle Chronology that deflates the tenth-century Jerusalem.
He treats [[hezekiah|Hezekiah]]'s and [[josiah|Josiah]]'s reforms as the **temple-centralization**
push from henotheism toward exclusive [[yahwism-and-israelite-religion|monolatry]] — the
worship, not yet the sole existence, of Yahweh — within the wider
[[divine-patronage-ancient-near-east|divine-patronage]] system.
