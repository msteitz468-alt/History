# The First Bulgarian Empire

## Overview

The **First Bulgarian Empire** was the dominant power of the early medieval Balkans, ruled
from the great fortified camp of **Pliska** by the **khans** of a Turkic Bulgar warrior elite
set over a largely Slav population. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Shepard, Ch. 9) it
is the chief non-Frankish state of the volume's eastern horizon, locked in a long contest with
[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]] and, after its conversion, a prize fought over by Rome and
Constantinople.

## The Khanate and Krum

Founded when Khan Asparuch's Bulgars crossed the Danube and were recognised by Byzantium (681),
the khanate combined a fortified Danubian heartland with a tribute-based military overlordship. Its
power peaked under **Khan Krum** (c. 802–814), who exploited the Frankish destruction of the Avars,
annihilated a Byzantine army and killed the emperor Nicephorus I (811), and campaigned to the walls
of Constantinople, devastating Thrace and recording his commanders' hierarchy on inscribed stone
pillars at Pliska. His successor **Omurtag** stabilised the frontier with Byzantium.

## Conversion and the Church Question

Under **Khan Boris** (852–889) Bulgaria converted to Christianity ([[conversion-of-boris-i-864]]),
Boris taking the baptismal name Michael. The decision touched off a diplomatic tug-of-war: Boris
played **Rome (Pope Nicholas I)** against **Constantinople**, seeking an autonomous Bulgarian church,
and his correspondence with the pope (866) is a key source. The dispute over jurisdiction in Bulgaria
was a principal flashpoint of the **Photian schism** between the eastern and western churches. Bulgaria
ultimately attached itself to the Byzantine ecclesiastical sphere — and became the haven for the
disciples of [[cyrillo-methodian-mission|Cyril and Methodius]], where the **Slavonic liturgy** and a
**Slavic literary culture** took root after being expelled from Moravia.

## Significance

The conversion and ecclesiastical alignment of Bulgaria pulled the southern Slavs decisively into the
Byzantine-Orthodox cultural orbit and made Bulgaria the cradle of Slavonic Christian literature, with
consequences for the whole later Orthodox Slavic world.

## Historiography

NCMH II (Shepard) draws on a difficult combination of Byzantine chronicles (often hostile or
tendentious — e.g. claiming Boris converted under duress), the stone inscriptions of Pliska, and the
papal-Bulgar correspondence. The date and circumstances of Boris's baptism (variously 863–866) remain
contested.
