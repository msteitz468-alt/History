# The Peloponnesian League (the Spartan Alliance)

The **Peloponnesian League** — more accurately "**the Lacedaemonians and the allies**,"
the Spartan Alliance — was the hegemonic system of bilateral alliances through which
[[sparta|Sparta]] dominated the Peloponnese and, by the mid-sixth century, all of
mainland Greece. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 42, N. G. L.
Hammond) reconstructs its Archaic formation.

## From conqueror to protector

Sparta built the alliance out of **necessity**: through the sixth century its policy was
"dominated by the fear of a Messenian or Helot revolt being instigated by one or more of
her neighbours" — Argos, Arcadia, Pisatis. Its first ally was oligarchic **Elis** (helped
to crush Pisatis, 590–580). The decisive shift came at **Tegea**: after repeatedly
failing to conquer the tough Arcadians by the sword (the Spartans "wore the fetters they
had brought" and worked the land as prisoners), Sparta — on Delphic advice, recovering
"the bones of [[orestes|Orestes]]," symbol of pre-Dorian hegemony — abandoned aggressive
Dorianism and reinvented itself as **protector** rather than overlord (c. 560). The
Tegean treaty's surviving terms — "to expel the Messenians from the land" and not to
execute Laconizers — show the logic: a *cordon sanitaire* against Helot subversion,
secured through friendly oligarchies rather than annexation.

## The system

The alliance was a set of **separate treaties, each ally bound to Sparta, not to one
another** ("the Lacedaemonians and the allies"). Its terms (inferred from later practice)
gave Sparta **unrestricted command (*hegemonia*)** in joint war — the king need not even
reveal a campaign's objective — in exchange for **defence of the ally's territory**
(above all against Argos) and the expulsion of Messenian refugees. The cost to Sparta was
negligible (it kept its army on a war footing anyway); the cost to the ally was
acceptance of Spartan political influence in favour of a "Laconizing" oligarchy. Posing
as **liberator from [[greek-tyranny|tyranny]]** (Thucydides: Sparta "put down most of the
tyrants... in the rest of Greece"), Sparta drew in **Elis, Arcadia, Sicyon, Corinth and
Megara** (perhaps Phlius and Cleonae) by c. 550, keeping Argos isolated. The ephor
**Chilon**, one of the Seven Sages, is credited with shaping the policy.

## The triumph over Argos

The capstone was the **defeat of Argos at Thyreatis in 546** — the "Battle of the
Champions" (300 a side, then the full armies) — after which Sparta was "acknowledged as
the leading power in Greece." Backed by the alliance's combined land power (and the naval
reach of Corinth, Sicyon and Megara), Sparta now overthrew tyrants as far afield as
Phocis, Thessaly, Naxos and Miletus, and the expedition against [[polycrates|Polycrates of
Samos]] (c. 524); it became ally of [[lydia|Croesus]] and [[saite-egypt|Amasis]] and
self-appointed champion of the Greeks against [[achaemenid-empire|Cyrus]].

## Why it mattered

- The **first durable hegemonic alliance** in Greek history — Hammond notes the
  "fortunate coincidence" that as Cyrus built the Persian empire, Sparta was creating "a
  loosely-articulated system of power based on a coalition of free states and a
  detestation of despotism."
- The **organizing structure of the Greek resistance to Persia** and one pole of the
  Sparta–Athens bipolarity that would dominate the Classical age.

## Discipline and decline in the fourth century (CAH VI)

[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 6, Seager) shows the alliance turned, after 386,
into an instrument of [[spartan-hegemony|Spartan]] coercion of its own members. Freed by
the **[[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]]** of any external check, Sparta "turned her
attention to the conduct of her friends in the war," **punishing allies for disloyalty**:
in **385** it forced restive **Mantinea** to pull down its walls and then, citing the
peace's autonomy clause perversely, **broke the city up into villages** (the *dioikismos*)
— a calculated humiliation that also yielded more compliant troops. It restored friendly
exiles at **Phlius**, and increasingly extracted **money in lieu of men** from allies
weary of Spartan wars (first seen in the Olynthus campaign, 382).

These strains help explain why the alliance could not survive defeat. After
**[[battle-of-leuctra-371bce|Leuctra]]** (371), [[epaminondas|Epaminondas]] invaded the
Peloponnese, liberated **Messenia**, and fostered the **Arcadian League** and
**Megalopolis** — and the old members fell away. The Spartan Alliance, the first durable
hegemonic system in Greek history, **dissolved by 366**, leaving Sparta a second-rank
power in a peninsula it had dominated for two centuries.
