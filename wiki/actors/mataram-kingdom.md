# Mataram Kingdom (Central Java)

**Mataram** (Medang) was the Early Classic agrarian kingdom of **central Java** that produced the temple landscapes of Dieng, the [[sailendra|Sailendra]] Buddhist complex including [[borobudur|Borobudur]], and the Hindu [[prambanan|Prambanan]] (Loro Jonggrang) complex. Chinese sources knew related polities as **Heling** then **Shepo**. Between **919 and 929** the centre of Javanese rule moved to **eastern Java**; for the following centuries permanent-stone temple building nearly ceased—Miksic and Goh’s Middle Classic “dark age” in Java. Primary synthesis: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]].

## Overview and Chinese Framework

Heling sent embassies to the Tang from **640**; last known Heling embassy **818**; from **820** Javanese embassies appear under the name Shepo. The Early Classic frame of **600–900 CE** in [[seasian-classic-periodization|Miksic & Goh’s periodization]] “matches closely the careers of both of these polities.” Archaeology at **Batujaya** (brick/stone foundations, clay votive tablets of non-Esoteric Buddhism, sixth–seventh centuries) may relate to the Heling that Yijing described as a Hinayana country—either Heling moved to central Java or Chinese continued an old name.

About **3,000** Classic and Postclassic Indonesian inscriptions survive (Sanskrit, Old Malay, Old Javanese, Old Balinese, Arabic, Tamil), mostly from Java; the vast majority are **sima** records (tax relief for religious service). Archaic Kawi before **832** was used mainly for Sailendra edicts (including one of 775 in southern Thailand); after 832 the script evolved into standard Kawi.

## Foundation Traditions and Early Kings

The **Tuk Mas** inscription (~650, oldest central Java script) shows Viṣṇu attributes at a Merbabu spring. The **Canggal** (Mount Wukir) inscription of **732** records Sanjaya erecting a *lingga*, praising Sanna then Sanjaya. Sanjaya set up his kingdom in **716** (per later Kayuwangi ~880); later kings invoked him as founder of Mataram. Sanjaya bears a Sanskrit name, but his uncle Sannaha is a Sanskritized Javanese name—parallel to early Sanskritization processes elsewhere (e.g. Kutai). Analogous to [[khmer-empire|Jayavarman II]] about fifty years later, Sanjaya functions as a dynastic founder figure.

After Sanjaya, **Rakai Panangkaran** may have ruled ~**746–784** (also *dyah* Pañcapana), expanding from the Kedu toward the north coast. Chinese sources for 742–755 report a capital move east to Pulo Giasi. Late in his reign the Buddhist complexes **Kalasan** and **Sewu** were inaugurated, credited to King Indra Saṅgrāmadhanañjaya—possibly the same person as Panangkaran—under Sailendra higher authority (see [[sailendra]]).

East Java enters history with the **Dinoyo** inscription near Malang (**760**), founding a Hindu temple by the king of Kanjuruhan, coexisting with Panangkaran without known contact. The next major east Java inscription appears only in **929**—169 years later.

## Sailendra Apex and Sanjaya Culmination

From ~**780–832** the Sailendra Buddhist lineage dominated monumental patronage (Borobudur, Mendut, Pawon, etc.). By **842** the **Raka of Pikatan**, a Hindu of the Sanjaya line, was supreme ruler. An inscription of **856** describes consecration of an “abode of Śiva,” possibly the Prambanan group; the same Hindu king contributed to Buddhist shrines with a Buddhist queen (Plaosan). Part of the Ratu Boko complex was converted from Buddhist to Hindu use.

**Balitung** (c. 899–913) was the first to use the name **Mataram** for central Java and may have incorporated east Java; he was succeeded by Daksa, Tulodong (919–921), and Wawa (by 927–28). **Daksa** (c. 913–919), last Classic central Java king in this sequence, inaugurated the Sanjaya era from **18 March 717**. Later tradition ascribes the **transfer of government to east Java** to Wawa’s successor **Sindok** (~20 inscriptions, last 948); the oldest surviving Javanese texts on perishable materials (a *Rāmāyaṇa* version; *Sang Hyang Kamahāyānikan*) date from his reign.

## Economy and the Wonoboyo Hoard

About **200** sima inscriptions reveal a cash economy with specialized occupations (goldsmiths to pimps). By about **800**, when the Khmer were abolishing money, the Javanese began stamping silver and gold coins in several denominations—**contrasting with** the Angkorian monetary path. Mid-ninth-century uniform weights and measures appear; late ninth-century villagers paid taxes in gold; debtors fled to sima sanctuaries.

The **Wonoboyo hoard** (1990): ~**6,000 gold coins** weighing 20 kg plus jewellery and ritual utensils in Chinese jars, under Merapi volcanic sand ~4 km from Prambanan—likely a royal hermitage buried ~900; possibly the place to which the Raka of Pikatan retreated after abdicating in 856. Seplawan Cave is a second royal hermitage with gold statues and *lingga/yoni* temples.

## End of Central Java Mataram

“The construction of the Śiva complex at Prambanan marked the culmination of Javanese architecture…. thereafter the light of Early Classic civilization suddenly died.” Later inscriptions refer vaguely to a *pralaya* (disaster). Gold jewellery and coin caches suggest many people died or left quickly and never returned. **Last central Java inscription: 919.** After **929**, inscriptions appear only in east Java. For the next **350 years**, no more than six monuments of modest size are found in Java; for the entire Middle Classic, no permanent-material temples and very few inscriptions—“a dark age in Java” in Miksic and Goh’s phrasing.

### Causal Hypotheses for the Eastward Shift

Several factors were **probably** responsible (not mutually exclusive; none proven as sole cause):

1. **Military threats** from [[srivijaya|Srivijaya]] (hostility is attested later; **no evidence** that a Srivijayan attack *caused* the central Java demise).
2. **Heightened volcanic activity** of Mount Merapi (Wonoboyo and Sambisari under volcanic sand support eruption as *part* of the explanation).
3. **Growing maritime trade** for which the **Brantas** was better suited than any river or bay in central Java.

The rupture was “not voluntary or peaceful.” After demise, a new centre appeared near modern Surabaya. Later east Java kingdoms (Kediri, [[singhasari|Singhasari]], [[majapahit|Majapahit]]) inherit the Mataram royal tradition without restoring central Java’s Early Classic temple density.

## Historiography

King lists disagree; titles multiply; Old Malay vs Old Javanese epigraphy fuels Srivijaya-intervention theories. The “dark age” label is relative to stone temples and inscriptions—not a claim of demographic emptiness. Relationship to Chinese Heling/Shepo names remains partly opaque. Sanjaya–Sailendra dualism is overstated when Sanjaya kings fund Buddhist monasteries and Hindu kings fund Buddhist shrines.
