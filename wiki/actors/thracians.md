# The Thracians

The **Thracians** were the large group of Indo-European tribes who occupied the
**Balkan hinterland** between the Aegean and the Danube, and the Black Sea and the Axius
(Vardar) — a rural, tribal, horse-breeding people, never a unified nation, who supplied
the Greek world with mercenaries, slaves, the **gold and silver of Mt Pangaeum**, and the
myth of **Orpheus**. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 33b, G.
Mihailov) treats them. **Note:** Mihailov's chapter is largely a **tribal and
geographical catalogue** built from scattered, late, and Greek-filtered sources; firm
historical conclusions "in most cases" are not possible.

## "Thracians" — a Greek name for many tribes

There was **no common Thracian name or national identity**: "Thracians" and "Thrace"
were Greek labels for a host of tribes "of the same ethnic origin" speaking Thracian
dialects. Mihailov stresses that information thins sharply away from the Greek-contacted
coast — the northern tribes appear "very widespread but not at all numerous," the
southern ones "more numerous and smaller" simply because they were better known. The
major groupings: the **Getae** (north, on both Danube banks, who "immortalize" their
dead and fight as horse-archers), the **Triballi** (middle Morava), the **Moesi**, the
**Odrysae** (the lower/middle Arda — the future ruling tribe), the **Bisaltae, Edones,
Maedi, Bessi, Cicones, Bistones, Dolonci** (the Chersonese), and many more. Some tribes
(Bithyni, Mysi/Moesi, Maedobithyni, Thyni) had **migrated to Asia Minor**, traditionally
before the Trojan War.

## The Cimmerian problem and Greek colonization

Mihailov treats the seventh-century **[[cimmerians|Cimmerian]] passage** through the
Balkans (with allied Treres and Edones) as uncertain — the "Cimmerian-type" horse-trappings
once cited as proof may instead reflect Near Eastern art reaching Thrace across the
Dardanelles, and the changes may owe more to the **Phrygian migration** (c. 800). The
decisive external force of the period was **[[greek-colonization|Greek colonization]]** of
the Thracian coast (Abdera, Maronea, the Chersonese, Pontic Mesambria and Apollonia) —
the Greek towns "set up on earlier Thracian sites" — drawing Thrace into Aegean trade and,
in the west, the **Macedonian conquest** that pushed the Thracian tribes (Pieres, Bottiaei,
Edones) eastward across the seventh and sixth centuries.

## Society and civilization

Mihailov's portrait: a **rural, non-urban** society — "Thrace possessed only fortified
areas," and true towns appeared only from Philip II and Roman times; the population lived
in villages and hamlets (even underground "Troglodyte" houses among some Getae). The
economy was **agricultural and pastoral** — wheat, barley (made into the beer *bryton*),
renowned **wine and viticulture**, and above all **horse-breeding** (Homer's Thracians are
"herders of horses"); plus the **gold and silver of Pangaeum, Dysoron, and Thasos**.
**Coinage** appeared only c. 525–500; before that metal was held as ingots and ornaments.
Burial was by **inhumation and cremation** under **tumuli**, with dolmens and rock-cut
beehive tombs in the south-east and the burial of **horses** beside the dead; Herodotus
reports the **sacrifice of a favoured wife**. The pantheon (Bendis, the Thracian Apollo
Deraenus, the myths of **Orpheus** and Thamyris) was polytheistic; the art was a
conservative **geometric** "national style" in bronze (fibulae, cult-axes with animal
heads, horse-trappings), with Asiatic influence only from the late sixth century.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the Late Bronze Age migrations that consolidated the Thracian tribes in the
Balkans.
produced: a reservoir of **mercenaries, slaves, metals, timber, and horses** for the
Greek world; the **Orphic** religious tradition that entered Greek culture.
contributed_to: by Greek colonization and Macedonian and Persian pressure, the eventual
formation of the **Odrysian kingdom** (fifth century) — the first Thracian state — beyond
this volume's horizon.

## Historiography

The sources are "neither rich nor of consistent value": Homer and Hesiod, fragments of
Archilochus and Alcaeus, Hecataeus, and the fuller but limited Herodotus and Thucydides,
plus thin archaeology. Mihailov works mainly by **tribal prosopography and toponymy**,
constantly cautioning that the evidence is irregular and the tribal locations shifting.
The flowering of Thrace, he concludes, **began only at the end of the sixth century**,
coinciding with **Persian interest in Europe** — Darius's Scythian expedition (c. 513) —
which is reserved for CAH IV.
