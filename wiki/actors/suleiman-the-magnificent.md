# Süleyman I, "the Magnificent" (r. 1520–1566)

The tenth Ottoman sultan, under whom the empire reached its **apogee** — its greatest
international power, territorial reach, and cultural splendour. Known to Europeans as "the
Magnificent" and to his own subjects as ***Kanunî***, "the Lawgiver." From Colin Imber's
[[imber-ottoman-empire-1300-1650|*The Ottoman Empire, 1300–1650*]].

## Conquests in Europe and the Mediterranean

Succeeding [[selim-i|Selim I]] peacefully (he was the only son), Süleyman immediately succeeded
where Mehmed II had failed: he took **Belgrade (1521)** — the key to Hungary — and **Rhodes
(1522)**, expelling the Knights of St John. In **1526** he annihilated the Hungarian army at
**Mohács**, killing King Lajos; the disputed Hungarian succession drew him into decades of war
with the **Habsburgs**, including the failed **siege of Vienna (1529)**. Hungary was
ultimately partitioned, central Hungary becoming an Ottoman province. At sea, his admiral
**Hayreddin Barbarossa** won the battle of **Preveza (1538)** and dominated the Mediterranean;
Süleyman even allied with **Francis I of France** against the Habsburgs.

## Wars with Iran and the wider world

Against the **[[safavid-empire|Safavids]]**, Süleyman took **Tabriz** and conquered **Baghdad
(1534)**, adding Iraq and a second outlet to the Gulf, though Safavid scorched-earth tactics
repeatedly frustrated a decisive victory (settled by the Treaty of Amasya, 1555). His reach
extended to the **Red Sea and Indian Ocean** (contesting the Portuguese; garrisoning Yemen and
Aden), and his prestige drew appeals from Muslim rulers in India, Ethiopia, and Aceh. After
the 1547 treaty styled the Habsburg Charles V merely "King of Spain," Süleyman claimed the
title of **"Emperor of the Romans" / Caesar**.

## The Lawgiver and the limits of empire

Imber stresses both Süleyman's grandeur and its **constraints**: against the Habsburgs he met
"an enemy whose resources matched his own," the Iranian borderlands defeated even unopposed
campaigns, and the isthmus of Suez and an inability to build ocean-going ships blocked victory
over the Portuguese. At home his reign is the classic age of the **[[ottoman-law-kanun|kanun]]**
— his jurists (above all the chief mufti **Ebussuud**) systematized Ottoman secular law and
reconciled it with the *shari'a*, earning him the title *Kanunî*. His later years were marred
by **succession struggles**: he executed his sons Mustafa (1553) and (via the shah) Bayezid,
leaving the throne to Selim II. He died on campaign at the siege of **Szigetvár (1566)**.

## Counterfactual significance

Süleyman's 46-year reign marks the **summit of Ottoman power** — the empire at its maximum
international weight, courted by France and the German Protestants alike. Imber notes that "the
Empire never again played the international role that it had done at the height of Süleyman's
power": his reign both displayed and disclosed the geographical and military limits that would
constrain his successors.
