# Labarna

The **founding warrior-king** of the [[hittites|Hittite]] royal dynasty and the
originator of the dynastic title *Labarna* that his successors bore as a throne-name,
much as Roman emperors bore the title *Caesar*. He is known exclusively from later
sources, above all the **historical preamble** to [[telipinu|Telipinu's]] Proclamation,
composed about a century after his death. Treated in Trevor Bryce,
*The Kingdom of the Hittites* (2005), Ch. 4.

## The Problem of Labarna's Historicity

All information about Labarna comes from sources composed well after his reign; no
texts survive from it. The Proclamation of Telipinu begins: "Formerly Labarna was the
Great King. Then were his sons, his brothers, his relations by marriage, his (blood)
relations and his troops united. And the land was small" (Telipinu Procl. §§1–4, i
2–12). Bryce argues convincingly for his historical reality: the near-identical
formulaic praise given to Labarna, [[hattusili-i|Hattusili I]], and then
[[mursili-i|Mursili I]] is a deliberate rhetorical device in the Proclamation linking
royal unity with military expansion — not evidence of confusion between two persons.
The alternative view — that Labarna and Hattusili were one and the same king — is
not convincing, since "the tradition of a new king, or king-designate, assuming the
name Labarna was already established by Hattusili's reign, and the tradition persisted
after his reign" (Bryce, Ch. 4). Telipinu was not likely to confuse a throne-name
title with a separate ancestor's personal name.

## The Conquests

From a power base at **Kussara** (ancestral home of the dynasty of Pithana and
Anitta), Labarna launched a programme of military conquests extending from central
Anatolia south to the Mediterranean Sea. The Proclamation records: "he held the lands
of the enemy in subjection by his might. He kept devastating the lands, and he deprived
the lands of power; and he made them boundaries of the sea" (Telipinu Procl. §§1–4).
The conquered lands were then distributed among his sons as governors: "Hupisna,
Tuwanuwa, Nenassa, Landa, Zallara, Parsuhanta, Lusna—these (were the) lands they
governed. The large towns were assigned (to them)."

Bryce suggests a northward as well as southward expansion. The later episode of a
rebellion at **Sanahuitta** — a city north-east of [[hattusha|Hattusa]] — during his
reign implies Labarna had won territory in that direction too, even though the
Proclamation only records the southern campaigns explicitly.

## Relationship to Hattusili I

Bryce's most likely reconstruction places Labarna as the **grandfather** of
[[hattusili-i|Hattusili I]]. The Testament of Hattusili refers to "a rebellion against
his grandfather" in Sanahuitta, where his grandfather had attempted to appoint a son
(also called Labarna, by then a dynastic name-in-waiting) as regional governor.
"His servants and the great men defied his word and placed Papahdilmah (on the
throne)" (Hattusili's Testament §20, iii 41–5). The rebellion split the kingdom:
a rebel regime sat in Sanahuitta even at the start of Hattusili's own reign. The
son Labarna who was displaced by this rebellion may never have become king; he may
not have survived it.

## The Title Labarna

The personal name of the dynasty's founder became a **royal title** borne by
subsequent kings — comparable to *Caesar* among the Roman emperors. Hattusili I
used *Labarna* (or *Tabarna*) as a throne-name alongside his personal name, as did
later kings. This dual-name practice, while initially creating confusion for later
readers, is in fact strong evidence for the historical reality of an original personal
Labarna distinct from Hattusili.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Labarna — or without the first military unification he achieved from Kussara
— the scattered petty states of post-Anitta central Anatolia might never have been
welded into the coherent polity that [[hattusili-i|Hattusili I]] then transformed
into the Hittite state. His conquests provided the territorial base and the ideological
model (the warrior-king who "made the boundaries of the sea") that shaped every
subsequent Old Kingdom ruler's self-presentation.

## Historiography

Labarna survives only through the Proclamation of Telipinu and the incidental
references in Hattusili's Testament — documents composed one to two generations
after his likely reign. The Proclamation's near-identical language for Labarna and
Hattusili led earlier scholars to question whether he was a literary fiction; Bryce
dismisses this and treats him as genuine. No texts from his reign, no seal impressions,
and no contemporary witnesses have been recovered, leaving chronology, exact
territorial extent, and genealogical position all approximate. The [[hittites|Hittite]]
Old Kingdom's own records of its own foundations are thus filtered through the
retrospective political argument of Telipinu's constitutional document.
