# Nez Perce

Sahaptian-speaking people of the Columbia basin / Plateau country, alongside Klikitats, Teninos, Yakimas, Umatillas, Walla Wallas, and related groups. Interior rather than coastal Northwest: smaller populations, greater mobility, looser social organization than coast ranked societies—within Fisher’s coast–interior fundamental division ([[pacific-northwest-coast]]; process [[northwest-native-history-to-1880s]]).

## Role and overview

**Horse revolution (early eighteenth century):** Acquisition of horses from the Plains transformed Plateau life—mobility, hide tipis, buckskin clothing, diet shift from largely fish toward mostly land-animal flesh. Fisher notes the impact has been likened to the automobile on twentieth-century North America. Many aspects of Nez Perce (and Kootenay) culture resembled Plains patterns through Rockies buffalo-hunt contact.

## Key episodes (Fisher; Hoxie)

- **Walla Walla council, late May 1855:** Yakimas, Cayuses, Walla Wallas, Nez Perces with Governor Isaac Stevens—treaties drawn before genuine negotiation; Chinook jargon for complex provisions; Stevens played pliant leaders off resistants and threatened holdouts would “walk in blood knee deep”; **>4 million hectares** signed away for few reservations; leaders’ authority often overstated; settlement pressed while ratification lagged—produced further violence.
- **War of 1877:** Nez Perces sought peace but treaty land insecure (government could move groups if “interests of the territory require it”); officials displaced them from the cherished **Wallowa Valley**. Young men seeking revenge killed settlers → war. Multi-month heroic fighting retreat under **Chief Joseph** and others; harried from homeland to the **Bear Paw Mountains**; surrendered just **~50 km short of the Canadian border**. Like Kamiakin, believed Canada would be different—Fisher: distinctions more apparent than real; techniques of dispossession differed, end result the same (insufficient land for livelihood).
- **Allotment resistance (1889):** When Smithsonian anthropologist Alice Fletcher supervised Nez Perce allotment/sale, boundary markers for new Indian homesteads were regularly removed by angry Indians (Hoxie)—local sabotage within [[allotment-policy]] / [[dawes-act-1887]] era.
- **Religious milieu:** Plateau prophet movements (Smohalla Dreamers) and broader “adjustment cults” of the settlement era form the cultural background to late-century resistance and adaptation.

## Counterfactual significance

Wallowa dispossession and the near-escape to Canada dramatize Fisher’s Canada/U.S. comparison: cross-border hope was rational given different surface techniques (fewer BC “Indian wars,” Douglas piecemeal reserves) but structural settler land hunger produced convergent dispossession by the 1880s.

## Historiography

Josephy’s Nez Perce history is Fisher’s cited exemplar of strong particular-group historiography south of the border. Military narrative of 1877 is extensively worked; Fisher embeds it in regional land-policy comparison rather than isolated “last stand” romance.
