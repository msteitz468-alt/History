## Overview

The **Lucayans** were the indigenous people of the Bahama Archipelago at the time of European contact. They are the people Columbus first encountered on 12 October 1492 at Guanahaní (San Salvador). Their name derives from "Lucayo," the indigenous term for the Bahamas.

## Origins and Settlement

The Lucayans descended from Ostionoid/Chicoid-series peoples who colonised the Bahamas from Hispaniola and Cuba beginning around AD 700. Settlement proceeded from south to north through the archipelago. Key archaeological evidence: Coralie site (Grand Turk, c. AD 700–1100), Governors Beach site (Grand Turk, c. AD 1100–1300), MC-6 site (Middle Caicos, c. AD 1400–1600).

## Culture

Lucayan material culture belonged to the Chicoid ceramic tradition, linking them to the Taíno cultural complex of the Greater Antilles. They practised manioc horticulture, fishing, and inter-island exchange. Social organisation featured caciques (chiefs) at the local level but appears less hierarchically developed than the large cacicazgos of Hispaniola. Population at contact estimated in the tens of thousands.

## Colonial Destruction

Following Columbus's landfall, the Lucayans were enslaved in large numbers by the Spanish. Amerigo Vespucci's expedition (1499) reportedly transported hundreds of Lucayans to Spain. By c. 1520, the Lucayan population had been effectively destroyed through enslavement, deportation, and disease — making the Bahama Archipelago one of the first regions in the Americas to be depopulated by European colonialism.

## Historiography

Because the Lucayans were destroyed so rapidly, primary sources are sparse. Archaeological work in the Turks & Caicos and Bahamas (Keegan's decades-long fieldwork) provides the primary evidence base. Columbus's diario is the main contemporary account.
