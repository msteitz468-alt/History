# Songhai Empire

## Overview

The **Songhai Empire** (c. 1350–1591 CE) was the third and largest of the great
**Western Sudanic commercial empires**, superseding
[[mali-empire|Mali]] in the fifteenth century and controlling a vast territory
"extending farther eastward, toward Lake Chad" (Davidson). At its apogee under
**Sunni Ali** (r. 1464–1492) and **Askia Muhammad I** (r. 1493–1528), Songhai
dominated the middle Niger basin and the [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan trade]]
routes, making Timbuktu and Gao into the intellectual and commercial centres of
the West African savanna. Gold was both the source of Songhai's power and,
ultimately, the cause of its destruction — attracting the Moroccan invasion of
1591 that shattered the empire and permanently disrupted the trans-Saharan
commercial system.

## Origins and Emergence

The Songhai polity centred on **Gao** on the Niger bend emerged as a distinct
regional power around 1350, initially within the framework of a declining Mali
Empire. [[davidson-african-kingdoms-1966|Davidson (1966)]] dates the empire from
roughly 1350 to 1600 and notes that it "slowly usurped the territory of Mali" and
extended further east than any of its predecessors. The origin of the Songhai state
is rooted in the Gao kingdom, which had its own earlier history as a subordinate
Niger city, but rose to dominance by filling the vacuum left by Mali's fourteenth- and
fifteenth-century contraction.

## Sunni Ali and Military Expansion

**Sunni Ali** (r. 1464–1492) transformed Songhai into a military empire through
relentless campaigning. His 28-year reign secured the capture of **Timbuktu** (1468)
and **Djenné** (c. 1473) — the two greatest commercial cities of the western Sudan —
and established Songhai as the dominant power from the Atlantic approaches to the
Hausa city-states. Sunni Ali's relationship with Islam was ambiguous: he was a ruler
of the savanna and riverine peoples as much as the Muslim merchant cities, and the
learned scholars of Timbuktu, whose patronage he needed, regarded him as impious.

## Askia Muhammad and the Learned City

The empire's cultural and administrative zenith came under **Askia Muhammad Touré**
("Askia the Great," r. 1493–1528), who deposed Sunni Ali's son and reoriented the
empire firmly toward orthodox Islam and scholarly patronage. [[davidson-african-kingdoms-1966|Davidson]]
opens his chapter on the western Sudanic empires with a folk singer in the new
Republic of Guinea reciting a lineage of heroes culminating in Askia Muhammad, whose
memory was still potent six centuries later.

Under Askia Muhammad, **Timbuktu** became a centre of Islamic learning with an
international reputation. The Muslim scholar **Leo Africanus** visited Timbuktu
with a diplomatic mission and reported that it had "a splendid and well-organized
royal court, many craftsmens' shops, and markets where European cloth is sold."
Leo also noted that Timbuktu's **book trade** "provided a better source of profit
than any other kind of commerce" — a striking index of scholarly density. Erudite
Muslims travelled to Timbuktu and Jenne to study with African scholars who "took
their doctrines from Cairo but their ideas were their own," writing prolifically
in biography, history, religious commentary, legal dissertation, and royal
correspondence. Some of their work "acquired an almost classical authority and is
still used today in Koranic schools in the Western Sudan" (Davidson).

**Mahmud al-Kati** (b. c. 1468) — Soninke by descent, therefore heir to the oldest
West African tradition — was among Askia Muhammad's protégés: he accompanied the
king on his pilgrimage to Mecca and served in various official capacities, earning
the title *alfa* (doctor of law). In 1519, then approximately 51, he began
collecting notes for the *Tarikh al-Fettash* ("chronicle of the researcher"), one
of the two principal Songhai chronicles. Al-Kati "witnessed the whole of the 16th
Century, when the Songhai empire was at the peak of its fame" (Davidson) — and as
a Soninke could also draw on Ghana's earlier oral tradition.

Askia Muhammad rationalized administration, standardized weights and measures,
and cultivated Islamic scholars as legitimating partners, in contrast to Sunni Ali.
He undertook a pilgrimage to Mecca, providing international Islamic legitimacy to
the dynasty.

## Gao: The Commercial Capital

[[davidson-african-kingdoms-1966|Davidson]] reproduces **Leo Africanus**'s eyewitness
account of **Gao**, Songhai's political capital, which Leo visited while Columbus
was sailing to the New World:

> "Gao is a very large city similar to Kabara, that is, without surrounding walls.
> The city is about 650 kilometers from Timbuktu to the southeast. Most of its
> houses are ugly; however, a few, in which live the king and his court, have a
> very fine aspect. Its inhabitants are rich merchants who travel constantly about
> the region with their wares. A great many Blacks come to the city bringing
> quantities of gold with which to purchase goods imported from the Berber country
> and from Europe, but they never find enough goods on which to spend all their gold
> and always take half or two-thirds of it home."

Leo further describes Gao as "well-policed," with abundant bread, meat, rice,
melons, and freshwater wells, but "neither wine nor fruit." The king maintained a
large harem, a guard of horsemen and archers, and conducted affairs through
secretaries, advisors, captains, and stewards — a bureaucratic state by any measure.
A slave market operated on market days: "A fifteen-year-old girl is worth about six
ducats and a young man nearly as much."

## Structure and Economy

Songhai was organized around the **Niger River** as an arterial highway. Agricultural
surplus from the inner Niger delta, combined with [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan
trade]] in gold, salt, and slaves, underpinned imperial revenues. The empire was
larger geographically than either Ghana or Mali; Davidson locates it as part of the
"four trading empires" of the Sudan alongside Ghana, Mali, and **Kanem-Bornu**
(800–1800 CE), the last growing "separately in the interior" (i.e., around Lake
Chad) and lasting longest of the four.

Gold production — "entirely an African development" in its technology, with neither
prospecting nor mining methods imported from outside — fed both the Saharan caravans
moving north and the coastal trade opening westward after the 1440s. Davidson
estimates the total gold mined from antiquity to 1500 at approximately 3,500 tons.
By around 1400 a second and "eventually more important" gold region had opened in
the forest lands of modern Ghana, driven by expanding European demand.

## European Discovery and the End of Isolation

Songhai's commercial world first entered European consciousness through Leo
Africanus's account, published by Venetian Giovanni Battista Ramusio in 1550 and
circulated widely as an incitement to trade: "go and do business with the king of
Timbuktu and Mali." Portugal was buying gold at several West African coastal points;
in 1555 an English expedition returned to the Thames with 400 pounds of gold and
250 ivory tusks, "comfortably multiplying its capital investment many times over."
Queen Elizabeth herself invested in African trade in 1561, clearing "a profit on one
voyage of £1,000." Davidson's argument is that "the prosperity of the African trading
empires between the 13th and 16th Centuries was in a large measure caused by Europe's
demand for more gold."

## Decline and Fall: The Moroccan Invasion (1591)

Internal dynastic conflicts weakened the empire after Askia Muhammad's deposition
(1528). The fatal blow came in **1591** when a **Moroccan army** — equipped with
firearms that the Songhai cavalry could not withstand — crossed the Sahara and
shattered the empire. Davidson states that gold "was the reason for the rise of
Songhai, and also the cause of its fall—attracting a catastrophic Moroccan invasion
in 1591 which left the empire in ruins." See the Battle of Tondibi (1591).

The empire fragmented into successor states. The trans-Saharan commercial system
was permanently disrupted. Timbuktu's scholarly infrastructure — the libraries,
the schools, the trade in books — was devastated. The western Sudan did not recover
its pre-1591 commercial and intellectual density.

## Counterfactual Significance

The Moroccan invasion was a decisive contingent event: without it, Songhai might have
continued its gradual administrative consolidation and maintained the trans-Saharan
commercial system into the seventeenth century. The destruction of the commercial
cities depressed the entire western Saharan trade system for generations and
impoverished the scholarly infrastructure that had made Timbuktu the intellectual
capital of sub-Saharan Islam. The Moroccan intervention itself was ephemeral — Morocco
could not hold the Sudan — leaving a power vacuum that was filled only slowly and
partially by smaller successor states.

## Historiography

Songhai history relies primarily on two **17th-century Arabic chronicles** written
within the Timbuktu scholarly tradition: the *Ta'rikh al-Sudan* and the
*Ta'rikh al-Fattash* (the latter begun by al-Kati in 1519, completed by descendants).
Both privilege the urban elite and the Muslim scholarly networks; rural and non-Muslim
populations are largely invisible. **Leo Africanus** (c. 1510s) provides the key
eyewitness external account of Gao and Timbuktu. Ibn Battuta did not visit Songhai
but his account of the trans-Saharan routes illuminates the commercial infrastructure
Songhai commanded.

[[davidson-african-kingdoms-1966|Davidson's 1966 popular synthesis]] reflects the
mid-twentieth-century rehabilitation of African state history: his account emphasizes
the sophistication and cosmopolitanism of the commercial system against older colonial
dismissals of African state capacity. As a Time-Life "Great Ages of Man" volume, it
is illustrative rather than analytical — treat as a data point on 1960s popular
historiography, not as primary scholarship.
