# Ludwig Beck

## Overview

General Ludwig Beck (1880–1944) served as Chief of the Army General Staff from 1935 until his resignation on 18 August 1938, and thereafter became the central, if increasingly titular, figure of the internal-Army resistance to Hitler, culminating in his designation as the conspiracy's intended head of state after 20 July 1944. He shot himself (unsuccessfully, twice) in the Bendlerstrasse courtyard on the night of the failed plot and was finished off by a sergeant.

## Formation and Career

Beck opposed "Case Green," the 1938 plan for war against Czechoslovakia, initially on narrow professional grounds — that Germany was unprepared for a war against Britain, France, and possibly Russia simultaneously — rather than moral grounds. His 5 May 1938 memorandum to Brauchitsch judged Germany's "military-economic situation... worse than it was in 1917–18"; his 30 May memorandum called the revised Green directive "militarily unsound." A 16 July 1938 memorandum went further, demanding Hitler halt war preparations and asserting that a soldier's conscience could override orders: "There are limits to one's allegiance to the Supreme Commander where conscience, knowledge and responsibility forbade carrying out an order" — a passage Shirer notes anticipated, and implicitly rebutted in advance, the "superior orders" defense later used by dozens of German generals at Nuremberg.

Beck summarized his own political awakening in mid-1938 as: "For the Fuehrer, against war, against boss rule, peace with the Church, free expression of opinion, an end to the Cheka terror, restoration of justice, reduction of contributions to the party by one half, no more building of palaces, housing for the common people and more Prussian probity and simplicity" — a program Shirer calls politically naïve, since Beck did not yet grasp that Hitler himself was the cause of what revolted him.

He resigned on 18 August 1938 after failing to rally fellow generals — above all the wavering Brauchitsch — to a collective stand at a secret 4 August meeting and a 10 August Berghof confrontation, where Hitler publicly shouted down General von Wietersheim over the West Wall's true weakness. Hitler suppressed public mention of the resignation. Beck's bitterness at Brauchitsch's failure to back him ("Brauchitsch left me in the lurch") persisted; Hassell judged him "pure Clausewitz, without a spark of Bluecher or Yorck" — a man of principle rather than action.

## Role in the Resistance, 1938–1944

Beck was the moral and organizational anchor of the conservative-military conspiracy through its many failed phases: the "Zossen Conspiracy" of October–November 1939 (with Brauchitsch, Halder, Goerdeler, Oster, and General Georg Thomas), the winter 1939–40 peace-feeler channels to Britain via the Vatican and Bern, and — after Barbarossa's failure — the reactivated Hassell–Goerdeler–Popitz–Oster circle. He was formally installed as head of the conspiracy in March 1942, though Hassell privately doubted his capacity for decisive action, judging him "very theoretical... a man of tactics but little will power," an assessment Shirer calls prophetic of the eventual failure.

During the Stalingrad crisis, the plotters attempted to use Beck's personal appeal, flown in by an Air Force officer, to induce General Paulus to turn the surrounded Sixth Army against Hitler; Paulus instead sent "a flood of radio messages of devotion to his Fuehrer," only turning against the regime after Soviet captivity.

By 1943–44 Beck had become nominal leader of the conspiracy in name only; the practical, operational leadership passed to the younger, more radical [[actors/stauffenberg-claus-von|Claus von Stauffenberg]], who — unlike Beck — insisted on including Socialist figures such as Julius Leber and Wilhelm Leuschner in the planned post-Hitler cabinet.

## Death

On the night of 20–21 July 1944, following the failed assassination attempt and the collapse of the Berlin coup, Beck was given the opportunity to shoot himself. His first attempt only grazed his head; a second attempt also failed to kill him outright, and he was finished off by a sergeant with a shot to the neck in the Bendlerstrasse courtyard, alongside the summary executions of Stauffenberg, Olbricht, Mertz von Quirnheim, and Haeften. See [[events/july-plot-1944]] for the full sequence and its aftermath.

## Historiography

Shirer is skeptical of the generals' postwar self-exculpation — including claims traceable to Halder — that "Chamberlain saved Hitler" from a 1938 coup that Beck's own circle had supposedly readied; he notes the conspirators had substantial force available (Witzleben's Berlin garrison, Brockdorff-Ahlefeldt's Potsdam division, Hoepner's panzer division, armed police under Helldorf) yet hesitated through the Munich crisis. Beck's case is a recurring reference point in Shirer's broader argument about the German officer corps' capacity for, and failure of, timely resistance — see [[controversies/german-sonderweg]] for the historiographical debate this feeds, presented there as a contested position rather than settled fact.
