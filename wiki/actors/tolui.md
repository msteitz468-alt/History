# Tolui

## Overview

**Tolui** (c. 1191–c. 1232), youngest son of [[genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]] and [[borte|Börte]], was the empire’s greatest field general among the sons and, as *otchigin* (Prince of the Hearth), inherited the Mongol homeland on the Onon and Kherlen—including Burkhan Khaldun—while [[ogedei-khan|Ögödei]] built [[karakorum|Karakorum]] farther west on his own territory. Married to [[sorghaghtani-beki|Sorghaghtani Beki]] (Kereyid niece of [[togrul-ong-khan|Ong Khan]]), he fathered [[mongke-khan|Möngke]], [[kublai-khan|Khubilai]], [[hulegu|Hülegü]], and Ariq Böke—the Toluid line that seized the great khanate in 1251 and produced Yuan and Ilkhanid rulers.

## Role (Weatherford 2004)

- **Post-1206 share.** Received ~5,000 followers (with mother and Ögödei)—meager compared with loyal friends’ full *tümens* of 10,000.
- **Succession *khuriltai*.** Remained quiet “and seemed to have disappeared into the folds of the tent while his older brothers dominated center stage” when [[jochi|Jochi]] and [[chagatai-khan|Chagatai]] fought over the succession and Ögödei was chosen compromise Great Khan.
- **Military reputation.** Standard narrative (outside Weatherford’s detailed Western focus) credits him with heavy lifting in Jin and Khwarazmian theaters; his sons’ later commands build on that prestige.
- **Death.** Weatherford’s account: about age forty, “staggered drunk out of his tent one morning after a drinking binge and dropped dead” (~1232, three years before the Europe-target decision). (Other traditions circulate ritual self-sacrifice for Ögödei’s illness—flag as alternate source strand.) Ögödei immediately moved to annex Tolui’s property by arranging marriage between son Güyük and widow Sorghaghtani; she refused, citing her four young sons—Weatherford calls that refusal one of the most important decisions in imperial history.
- **Posthumous elevation.** In 1252 [[mongke-khan|Möngke]] retroactively awarded Tolui the title Great Khan on the legal claim that as youngest son and *otchigin* he was entitled to inherit his father’s titles and homeland. [[kublai-khan|Khubilai]] later conferred the office of Chinese emperor on him posthumously and included him in Chinese-style ancestral tablets and portraits.

## Historiography

*Secret History*, Persian chronicles, and Chinese Yuan materials; Weatherford (2004) foregrounds drunken death, homeland inheritance, and Sorghaghtani’s refusal of levirate as the hinge of Toluid restoration. Ritual-sacrifice vs. alcohol death remains a source-critical fork.
