# The Christian Kingdoms of Spain (León, Castile, Navarre, Catalonia), c. 900–1035

In the long tenth century the Christian polities of northern Iberia — the **kingdom of León** (heir to [[kingdom-of-asturias|Asturias]]), the emergent **county of Castile**, the kingdom of **Pamplona/Navarre**, and the **Catalan counties** — consolidated along the frontier of the [[caliphate-of-cordoba|Caliphate of Córdoba]]. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Roger Collins) stresses that this was an age of **localisation and repopulation** rather than dramatic conquest, the seedbed of the later *Reconquista* but not yet its main act.

## The Kingdom of León

The **Asturian** realm (from c. 718) shifted its centre south from Oviedo to **León** under **Ordoño II** (early tenth century), reflecting the southward expansion onto the Meseta and a greater sense of security as Umayyad power waned. Collins emphasises the kingdom's **weak governmental apparatus** (perhaps no permanent writing-office, kingship elective among the royal house among the magnates, comital offices becoming hereditary from the 930s) and a "fundamental flaw": its inability to become "more than the sum of its parts." Its central activity was the **repoblación** — the resettlement of the Duero frontier by settlers from the northern mountains, Basques, and **Mozarabs** (Christians from al-Andalus, who carried Andalusi material and intellectual culture north). The Leonese inflicted a major defeat on **al-Nasir** at **Simancas/al-Khandaq (939)** but suffered devastating raids under **al-Mansur** (León itself sacked).

## Castile, Navarre, and Catalonia

- **Castile** emerged as a frontier **county around Burgos** in the later ninth century, its hereditary comital dynasty (Fernán González and successors) growing in military weight on the contested upper Duero; it would later eclipse León.
- **Pamplona/Navarre**, the Basque kingdom of the western Pyrenees, rose to dominate Christian Spain under **Sancho III "the Great"** (c. 1004–1035), who at his death held hegemony over León, Castile, and the Pyrenean counties — though his realms were divided among his sons.
- The **Catalan counties** (the "Spanish March," chief among them **Barcelona** under the house of Wifred) were nominally within west Francia but effectively autonomous (the count of Barcelona never did homage to the [[capetian-dynasty|west Frankish king]]); Barcelona was sacked by **al-Mansur in 985**. Catalonia's full archives make it (with Latium) a classic ground of the [[feudal-revolution-debate|*mutation*]] debate.

## The Eleventh-Century Reversal (NCMH IV)

[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Simon Barton, Ch. 6, "Spain in the eleventh century")
carries the story into the age of Christian ascendancy. The dominant early figure was
**Sancho III "the Great" of Navarre** (c. 1004–1035), who briefly held hegemony from
Barcelona to Gascony and styled himself *imperator* — but at his death **divided his
realms among his sons** (Fernando → Castile, Ramiro → Aragón, García → Navarre),
permanently splitting Christian Iberia into the kingdoms of **Castile, Aragón, Navarre,
León, and Portugal** (and the Catalan counties). Sancho's son **Fernando I** reunited
**Castile and León** and, exploiting the fragmentation of [[al-andalus|al-Andalus]] into
*taifas*, made the **[[reconquista|parias]]** (protection tribute) a systematic
instrument of domination. His son **Alfonso VI** (1065–1109) brought this to its climax
with the capture of **Toledo in 1085** — the first great Christian seizure of a major
Andalusi city — which precipitated the [[almoravids|Almoravid]] intervention and the
crushing Christian defeat at **Sagrajas (1086)**. The era's emblematic warrior was
**[[el-cid|El Cid]]** (Rodrigo Díaz), exiled by Alfonso VI, who carved out an independent
Valencia (1094). With the parias and Toledo, the **[[reconquista|Reconquista]] proper**
had begun.

## The Fourteenth Century: Trastámara Castile and Portuguese Independence (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (the "Castile, Navarre and Portugal" chapter) carries the Christian
kingdoms into the late Middle Ages. **Castile**, the dominant peninsular power, was convulsed by a
**civil war** between **Pedro I "the Cruel"** and his half-brother **Henry of Trastámara** — a
conflict drawn into the [[hundred-years-war-1337|Hundred Years War]] (the Black Prince fighting for
Pedro, du Guesclin's companies for Henry) that ended with Pedro's death (1369) and the founding of
the **Trastámara dynasty**, which would later inherit [[crown-of-aragon|Aragon]] (Caspe, 1412). The
fourteenth century also saw **Portugal** secure its independence and identity: the **1383–1385
revolution** brought the Avis dynasty (João I) to the throne and, at the **battle of Aljubarrota
(1385)**, Portuguese footsoldiers (with English archers) destroyed a Castilian army — confirming
Portugal's separate destiny and inaugurating the Anglo-Portuguese alliance. **Navarre** remained a
small Pyrenean kingdom drawn between France, Castile and Aragon.

## Significance

The tenth-century Christian kingdoms were militarily overshadowed by Córdoba at its height, but their **repopulation of the frontier** and the hardening of Castile, Navarre, and Catalonia laid the demographic and political foundations for the eleventh-century reversal — when the *fitna* shattered the Caliphate, the *parias* (tribute gold, already reaching Barcelona by the early eleventh century) began flowing north, and the *Reconquista* proper began. By the late Middle Ages the peninsula had settled into the configuration — **Castile, Aragon, Portugal, Navarre, and Nasrid [[nasrid-granada|Granada]]** — that would carry into the early-modern union of the Spanish crowns.
