# First Dynasty of Babylon

The **[[amorites|Amorite]] dynasty (~1894–1595 BCE)** that raised the obscure town
of Babylon into the permanent metropolis of southern Mesopotamia, peaked under its
sixth king **[[hammurabi|Hammurabi]]**, and decayed across five further reigns until
the **[[fall-of-babylon-1595bce|Hittite sack]]** opened the way for the
[[kassites|Kassites]]. Also called the "Old Babylonian" dynasty. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. V, Gadd).

## Obscure beginnings and the peak

Founded by **Sumu-abum**, the dynasty's first five kings ruled a "diocese of about
fifty miles radius," occupied with canals and defensive building amid the general
"equilibrium of weakness." **[[hammurabi|Hammurabi]]** transformed it — defeating
Rim-Sin of Larsa (~1763) to win "the kingship of Sumer and Akkad," crushing
Eshnunna and Elam-led coalitions, and sacking [[mari|Mari]] — but, as the
[[interpolity-law|Mari letters]] show, his empire was modest and held together by
his person alone.

## Samsuiluna and the long decline

The empire "crumbled in his son's grasp even more quickly than it had sprung up."
**Samsuiluna** (~1749–1712) faced a great southern revolt led by a **Rim-Sin II**
and "twenty-six usurping kings" (including the king of Eshnunna, executed); he
defeated it, but **lost the south permanently** to the new **"Dynasty of the
Sealand"** under **Iluma-ilum**, lost Nippur, and lost the middle Euphrates, where a
small kingdom of **Khana** arose. His ninth year records the first clash with **"the
Kassite host."** **Abi-eshuh**, **Ammiditana**, and **Ammiṣaduqa** (whose accession
*mīšarum* edict and the astronomical "**Venus tablets**" survive, both key to the
[[mesopotamian-chronology|chronology debate]]) reigned long but within ever-narrower
bounds — "an age sinking slowly into decline and spinning itself out only because
there was no neighbour with force enough to cut even so thin a thread."

## The fall

The destroyer came "with a speed and from a distance as of lightning": under the
last king **Samsuditana**, **[[fall-of-babylon-1595bce|"the Hittite came to the land
of Akkad"]]** — **Mursili I** sacked Babylon (~1595 BCE) and carried off the gods
**Marduk and Sarpanitum**, ending the dynasty. The [[kassites|Kassites]] inherited
the throne; Marduk was later ransomed home by the Kassite king **Agum II**.

## Significance

The dynasty's lasting achievement was not its brief empire but **making Babylon the
metropolis of Mesopotamia** — a primacy that endured fifteen centuries — and
producing the **Code of [[hammurabi|Hammurabi]]** and the rich "Old Babylonian"
documentary culture (contracts, letters, *mīšarum* edicts, the *awīlum/muškēnum/
wardum* social order). Its slow strangulation and abrupt end exemplify the period's
fragility: even the leading southern power could neither dominate its rivals nor
defend itself against a raid from beyond the horizon.

## Historiography

Reconstructed from date-formulae, the few royal inscriptions, the
[[hammurabi|Hammurabi]] and successor letters, later king-lists and chronicles, and
the Mari archive for the peak. The chronology of the whole dynasty — and with it the
entire second millennium — hangs on the **disputed date of the Hittite sack** (high
~1651, middle ~1595, low ~1531); this volume uses the middle chronology. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
