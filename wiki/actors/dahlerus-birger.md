# Birger Dahlerus

Swedish businessman and personal friend of Hermann Göring who served as an unofficial back-channel between Göring and the British government during the final week of peace before the invasion of Poland (25 August – 3 September 1939). He is not a member of the Nazi regime but a private intermediary whose activity is documented almost entirely through his own later Nuremberg testimony and postwar memoir.

## Role and Activity

Dahlerus was summoned to Berlin from Stockholm on 24 August 1939 and used by Göring as an unofficial conduit to Lord Halifax throughout the last week of peace, flying repeatedly between Berlin and London (25, 27, 29, 30 August) carrying informal messages, including a set of "six points," from Hitler's camp. Göring maintained this back-channel to Halifax outside regular diplomatic channels, a fact kept from Ribbentrop, who favored war outright. On 3 September 1939, minutes before the British ultimatum expired at 11 a.m., Dahlerus made a final futile call proposing Göring fly personally to London to negotiate; Halifax's office rejected it flatly.

[EYEWITNESS, via Dahlerus's own Nuremberg testimony] Dahlerus witnessed a bizarre outburst by Hitler after midnight on 26–27 August 1939 at the Reich Chancellery: Hitler, "excited and nervous," repeatedly shrieked disconnected phrases — "If there should be war, then I shall build U-boats, build U-boats, U-boats, U-boats, U-boats," then "I shall build airplanes... and I shall annihilate my enemies" — described by Dahlerus as behaving "like a phantom from a storybook than a real person," while Göring "did not turn a hair."

Dahlerus's role was kept secret from contemporaries — including William Shirer, then reporting from Berlin — and was omitted from the British Blue Book and Nevile Henderson's memoirs; it was confirmed only when Dahlerus testified at Nuremberg on 19 March 1946, and via the 1954 publication of *Documents on British Foreign Policy*, Vol. VII. He later published a memoir of the episode, *The Last Attempt* (Swedish edition 1945, English 1948).

## Counterfactual Significance

Dahlerus's back-channel represented Göring's own last hope of averting war independent of Hitler and Ribbentrop's fixed determination to attack Poland (see [[invasion-of-poland-1939]]); it never had any realistic chance of altering the outcome, since Hitler's Case White timetable and the 23 August [[molotov-ribbentrop-pact-1939|Nazi-Soviet Pact]] had already committed Germany to invasion regardless of British signals. The episode is chiefly significant as evidence of the divided and often chaotic character of Nazi decision-making in the final days of peace, and of Göring's own persistent, ultimately ineffectual preference for a negotiated settlement over Ribbentrop's push for war.

## Historiography

The documentary record is almost entirely retrospective — Dahlerus's Nuremberg testimony and his own memoir — since his activity was deliberately concealed from contemporary observers and omitted from the standard British diplomatic record until the 1950s. Shirer, drawing on this later-surfaced material for *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960), treats Dahlerus's account as broadly credible on factual sequence (dates, flights, meetings) while noting its self-serving framing of Dahlerus's own role as peacemaker.
