# Murasaki Shikibu (c. 978 – c. 1014/1025)

The [[heian-period|Heian]]-period court lady who wrote the ***[[tale-of-genji|Tale of
Genji]]*** — often called the **world's first novel** — and a perceptive *Diary*. One of the
supreme figures of [[heian-court-culture|Japanese classical culture]] and, with her rival
Sei Shōnagon, the embodiment of the era's extraordinary **women's literature**. Her life is
known almost entirely from her own *Diary*, as preserved in Arthur Waley's
[[waley-tale-of-genji|introduction]].

## Life

Born about 978 into a **minor branch of the great [[fujiwara-clan|Fujiwara]] clan**, she was
the daughter of **Tametoki**, a provincial governor and Chinese scholar. As a girl she
learned **Chinese** by overhearing her brother's lessons — so quickly that her father
lamented "if only you were a boy" — but, in a society that thought Chinese unwomanly, she
**hid her learning** (she was mockingly nicknamed "Dame Annals"). Widowed around 1001 after a
brief marriage to a kinsman, she was placed (c. 1005) in the service of the young, "serious-
minded" **Empress Akiko (Shōshi)**, daughter of the all-powerful regent **Fujiwara no
Michinaga** — whom Murasaki's *Diary* shows half-courting her, and to whom she secretly
taught Chinese poetry (Bai Juyi). Reserved and observant, she recorded sharp judgments of her
fellow court women — dismissing **Sei Shōnagon's** "extraordinary self-satisfaction" and
"patchwork of blunders," and **Izumi Shikibu** as "not really a poet at all." Nothing is
known of her after about 1025.

## Significance

Murasaki is the author of the central monument of Japanese literature. The *Tale of Genji*
(written c. 1001–1020, in the new phonetic **kana** that freed Japanese-language prose) is a
work of profound **psychological realism** that, Waley argued, anticipates the modern novel
by centuries. Her *Diary* is, in turn, the prime window onto the inner life of the
[[heian-court-culture|Heian court]] at its Fujiwara apogee. That its supreme literary
achievement came from a **woman**, writing in the vernacular while men wrote in Chinese, is
one of the defining facts of Japanese cultural history.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Murasaki the Heian court would still have produced a brilliant women's literature
(the *Pillow Book*, the *nikki* diaries), but not the *Genji* — the work that became the
touchstone of Japanese taste (*[[heian-court-culture|mono no aware]]*), the inexhaustible
source of later poetry, drama (Noh), and painting (the *yamato-e* Genji scroll), and Japan's
single greatest claim in world literature.
