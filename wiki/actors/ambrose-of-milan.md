# Ambrose of Milan

**Aurelius Ambrosius** (c. 339–397), bishop of Milan, the most powerful churchman of the
Latin West in his generation and the decisive intellectual influence on the conversion of
**[[augustine-of-hippo|Augustine]]**. A provincial governor of Liguria, he was acclaimed
bishop by the Catholic populace of Milan in 373/374 though not previously a cleric, and
governed the church of the imperial capital for over two decades.

Ambrose combined a Roman aristocrat's command of power with the most learned preaching in the
Latin world: he read Greek fluently, drew on Origen and on Plotinus (the "gold of the
Egyptians"), and taught a radically immaterial conception of God and the soul — the body "a
tattered garment," the soul "the one thing in the universe nearest to God." He defended the
Old Testament against **[[manichaeism|Manichaean]]** ridicule by reading the Patriarchs as
philosophers, directly answering the objections that had held Augustine in the sect. In the
basilica crisis of 386 he refused, with his congregation, to surrender a church to the Arian
court of the empress Justina, introducing Eastern-style congregational hymnody to sustain the
besieged crowd — "we priests have our own ways of rising to Empire" — and shortly after
discovered the relics of Saints Gervasius and Protasius. Against his own relative, the pagan
senator **Symmachus**, he blocked the restoration of the Altar of Victory; against the
emperor **[[theodosius-i|Theodosius I]]** he exacted public penance for the massacre at
Thessalonica, a landmark assertion of church over state.

For Augustine, Ambrose was remote as a man but transformative as a teacher: his immaterialism
and his allegorical reading of Scripture dissolved Augustine's Manichaean materialism, and he
personally directed Augustine's baptismal preparation, baptizing him at Easter 387. Augustine
made Ambrose his model of the episcopal office and urged the deacon Paulinus to write his
*Life*. Modern scholarship (notably Neil McLynn's *Ambrose of Milan*, 1994) has stressed
Ambrose the ruthless ecclesiastical politician over the older image of a serene mediator of
Christian Platonism.
