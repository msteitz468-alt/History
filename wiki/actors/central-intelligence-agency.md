## Overview and Role

The Central Intelligence Agency was created by the National Security Act of 1947, initially chartered for intelligence collection. In 1948, National Security Council Directive No. 10/2 authorized it to conduct covert action, ordering that "the overt foreign activities of the US government must be supplemented by covert operations" including sabotage, subversion against hostile states, and "support of indigenous anti-communist elements" (McCoy, citing the declassified directive). Under President [[eisenhower-dwight-d|Eisenhower]], the Agency employed roughly 6,000 covert-action personnel worldwide on an $82 million budget, and Eisenhower is reported to have authorized some 170 major covert operations in 48 nations during the 1950s (figures per Alfred McCoy, *[[mccoy-cold-war-five-continents-2024|Cold War on Five Continents]]* (2024), not individually footnoted in the cited excerpt).

The Agency became the central institutional actor in the covert dimension of the [[cold-war|Cold War]], running paramilitary, propaganda, and intelligence operations across Europe, Southeast Asia, Latin America, the Middle East, the Congo, and southern Africa. See [[covert-action|covert action]] for the doctrine and toolkit, and [[cold-war-proxy-wars]] for the broader process this activity fed into.

## Major Covert Operations (selected)

- **Western Europe, 1948** — denied Communist Party victory in the 1948 Italian elections and worked to split French socialists from communists.
- **[[iran-coup-1953|Iran, 1953]]** ("Operation Ajax") — with British MI6, overthrew Prime Minister [[mossadegh-mohammad|Mohammad Mossadegh]] and restored the Shah.
- **[[guatemala-coup-1954|Guatemala, 1954]]** ("Operation PBSUCCESS") — overthrew President [[arbenz-jacobo|Jacobo Árbenz]].
- **[[congo-crisis-1960|Congo, 1960s]]** — station chief Larry Devlin's operations against [[lumumba-patrice|Patrice Lumumba]] and cultivation of [[mobutu-sese-seko|Mobutu Sese Seko]].
- **[[bay-of-pigs-1961|Cuba, 1961]]** — the Bay of Pigs invasion, and the subsequent Operation Mongoose covert campaign against Castro under [[edward-lansdale|Edward Lansdale]].
- **[[vietnam-war|Vietnam]]** — the Phoenix Program against the Viet Cong underground, and the CIA's "Secret Army" paramilitary operation in Laos, which the Agency itself called "the largest paramilitary operations ever undertaken by the CIA."
- **[[indonesian-mass-killings-1965|Indonesia, 1965–66]]** — provision of PKI leadership lists to the Indonesian military and coordination with British psywar operations during the mass killings following the countercoup led by [[suharto|Suharto]].
- **[[chile-coup-1973|Chile, 1970–73]]** — "Track I/Track II" destabilization of [[allende-salvador|Salvador Allende]]'s government, culminating in the 1973 coup and continued liaison with [[pinochet-augusto|Augusto Pinochet]]'s DINA and Operation Condor.
- **[[angolan-civil-war-1975|Angola, 1975–91]]** — covert aid to the FNLA and, after the 1985 repeal of the Clark Amendment, to UNITA under the Reagan Doctrine.

## The Cultural Cold War

McCoy documents an extensive CIA effort to shape non-communist opinion, run through Deputy Director of Operations [[dulles-john-foster|Allen Dulles]] (distinct from his brother, Secretary of State John Foster Dulles), Office of Policy Coordination chief Frank Wisner (whose operation was nicknamed the "Mighty Wurlitzer"), International Organizations Division chief Thomas W. Braden, and Cord Meyer. The Agency laundered funds through roughly 170 foundations (real and fictitious), subsidized the Congress for Cultural Freedom (founded 1950, active in 35 countries, funding magazines such as *Encounter*), and — per historian Frances Saunders — reached "few writers, poets, artists, historians, scientists, or critics in postwar Europe whose names were not in some way linked to this covert enterprise."

The Agency also promoted Abstract Expressionist art internationally (via MoMA touring shows from 1952), recruited media assets in American journalism (Carl Bernstein's estimate of ~400 in the 1950s, later revised upward to ~800 by a *New York Times* fact-check), worked through Hollywood studios via a resident Hollywood officer, and by 1967 had "sponsored, subsidized, or produced" over 1,000 books according to the 1967 Senate Church Committee hearings; by 1978 its mass-media propaganda budget reportedly reached $265 million per year. Cord Meyer separately attempted to suppress Alfred McCoy's 1972 book *The Politics of Heroin* — an episode McCoy recounts in the first person; treat as an uncorroborated author claim about his own experience, included here for attribution rather than as independently verified fact.

Domestically, the Agency's Operation CHAOS compiled a surveillance index of roughly 300,000 names, and CIA/Army facilities in West Germany reportedly employed former Gestapo personnel and Nazi-era doctors for unsuccessful mind-control research — part of the broader MKUltra/Project Artichoke program (see [[covert-action]]).

## Historiography

McCoy's account is thesis-driven: he frames the CIA as the operational core of a "covert empire" and argues individual field officers ("men on the spot") were frequently more causally decisive than Washington policy direction. This interpretive framing — along with McCoy's causal claims linking specific CIA coups to later civil wars and kleptocratic regimes — is recorded, not asserted in wiki voice, on [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]]. The underlying operational facts (dates, personnel, budgets, declassified quotations) are attributed to McCoy's archival research and are treated here as well-documented, while McCoy's larger interpretive architecture remains a contested position.
