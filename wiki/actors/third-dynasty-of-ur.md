# Third Dynasty of Ur (Ur III / Neo-Sumerian Empire)

The **"Neo-Sumerian" empire** (~2112–2004 BCE) — the last and greatest age of
Sumerian-language power, founded by **[[ur-nammu|Ur-Nammu]]** out of the
[[gutian-invasion|liberation of Sumer]] by Utu-khegal, raised to its height by
**[[shulgi|Shulgi]]**, and destroyed by [[amorites|Amorite]] and [[elam|Elamite]]
pressure under Ibbi-Sin. Its defining trait, and the richest single body of
documents from the ancient Near East, is its **hyper-bureaucratic temple-and-state
economy**. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XXII, C. J.
Gadd).

## A Sumerian revival

After the [[akkadian-empire|Akkadian]] interlude and the
[[gutian-invasion|Gutian]] "dark age," Ur III consciously **revived old Sumerian
forms** — the city-god theology, the *ensi* governorship, Sumerian as the language
of administration and literature. Ur-Nammu took the title **"king of Sumer and
Akkad"**; his successors revived the Akkadian universalist claim **"king of the
four regions."** Yet the revival was political theatre over an Akkadianizing
reality: the population was already thoroughly mixed and Semitic-speaking, and the
empire's organization owed much to Agade.

## The bureaucratic state

Ur III is the **archetype of the over-administered ancient state**. Under
[[shulgi|Shulgi]], from about his 21st year, a "**meticulous system of
book-keeping**" was imposed: every transaction — down to "half-a-dozen lines
registering the receipt of a goat" — was recorded on finely-made tablets, checked
by overseers, and filed. The huge cattle-clearing-house at **Drehem
(Puzrish-Dagan)** near Nippur processed tens of thousands of animals; great
temple **workshops** (clothiers, carpenters, smiths, jewellers, leather- and
reed-workers) ran on rationed labour, including women and children at the mills.
Shulgi also **standardized weights, the calendar, and a bureau of standards**, and
issued the earliest substantial **[[ur-nammu|law code]]** (the *Laws of Ur-Nammu*,
predating Hammurabi by three centuries). The resulting "business documents of the
Third Dynasty" are by far the most numerous cuneiform tablets in modern
collections — a "soulless industry" of officials and serfs in which the private
citizen is barely visible.

## Deified kingship

The Ur III kings, like [[naram-sin|Naram-Sin]] before them, were **deified**: from
Shulgi's middle years the king wrote the divine determinative before his name,
received his own temple and cult in each city, and was addressed as a god. CAH
reads this partly as policy — only a *god*-king could legitimately appoint a human
*ensi* as the city-god's steward (see [[divine-kingship]]). Royal **hymns** (some
perhaps composed by Shulgi himself, who boasted of his literacy and musicianship)
and flattering **royal letters** were copied for centuries in the scribal schools.

## Reach and limits

The empire's core was Sumer and Akkad; its persistent **military front was the
northeast** — endless campaigns against the Zagros peoples (Lullubi, Simurrum,
Urbilum/Irbil, and the still-resented Gutians), with **Ashur** and the Assyrian
borderland held as vassals (the governor Zariqum dedicated an inscription "for the
life of Amar-Sin"). [[elam|Elam]] (Susa) was subordinated and Akkadianized. The
**northwest** (Mari, Syria) was mostly beyond effective control — and increasingly
the source of danger.

## Collapse

Under **Shu-Sin** the [[amorites|Amorite]] pressure grew acute enough to require a
~170-mile defensive line, the **"Wall against Amurru" (Muriq-Tidnim)**, northwest
of Baghdad — a measure of despair that, like all such walls, failed. Under
**Ibbi-Sin** the empire disintegrated: the Amorites breached the wall, cities
defected (above all under **Ishbi-Erra**, the "man of Mari" who seized Isin and
founded an independent dynasty), famine prices soared, and finally **[[elam|Elam]]
and the Subarians sacked Ur**, leading Ibbi-Sin captive to Anshan. See
[[fall-of-ur-2004bce]].

## Significance

Ur III is the high-water mark of the **command-economy temple-state** and the model
of bureaucratic kingship that later Mesopotamian states inherited. Its
**Sumerian-language renaissance** was the last: after its fall, Sumerian survived
only as a learned, liturgical, and scribal language while Akkadian (and then
Amorite-Akkadian) dynasties — Isin, Larsa, and ultimately **Babylon** — ruled the
land. Its law code begins the Mesopotamian legal tradition that culminates in
Hammurabi.

## Liverani: direct management and the fiction of the records

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] identifies Ur III's central economic
innovation as **direct management of provincial production** — a step beyond the Early
Dynastic and Akkadian systems, which controlled trade and political relations without
directly running the provinces. Provinces were governed by *ensi* who were now **appointed,
uniform, and interchangeable** functionaries of the king (see [[ur-nammu]]), and the temple
was absorbed into the state, employing propertyless full-time urban workers rather than
"free" village labour (see [[temple-economy]]).

His most pointed argument is that the famous Ur III paperwork records a **theoretical, not a
real, economy**. Agricultural yields were estimated *before* harvest from fixed parameters
(field area × soil/salinity/sowing coefficients), ~two-thirds of land left fallow; at the
Drehem (Puzrish-Dagan) cattle office "a cow never died and gave birth to half a calf a year,
alternating male and female." Merchant accounts were likewise administrative fictions — once
the merchant left the centre, invisible barter, gift-exchange, transit taxes, and
subcontracts occurred entirely outside the record. The parameters were deliberately set as
**low estimates** leaving workers a margin (slim for farmers/craftsmen, generous for
merchants). Ur reached perhaps **200,000** people — the highest population of the entire
pre-Classical period in Lower Mesopotamia — as smaller settlements emptied into fortified
cities. The **"Wall of the Martu"** against the [[amorites|Amorites]] was built, Liverani
notes, roughly contemporaneously with Egypt's "Prince's Wall," both responses to the same
expanding West-Semitic pastoral group. The deified king functioned not as Akkadian-style
**hero** but as **cultic-administrative head** replacing the city-gods atop the
redistributive system. See [[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]].

## Historiography

A paradox of evidence: **superabundant administrative tablets** but a "**tantalizing
want**" of narrative — the dynasty was strangely "unwilling to record even the
triumphs, much less the failures, of its kings." History is reconstructed mainly
from **date-formulae**, royal hymns, and the late-copied royal letters (whose
reliability is uneven). The famous **Lamentations over the Destruction of Ur and
Sumer** are literary, not documentary. Dates follow the
[[mesopotamian-chronology|middle chronology]]. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
