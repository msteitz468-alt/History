# The Moche

## Overview

The **Moche** (or Mochica) of the **north coast of Peru** (c. 200 BCE–600 CE) were, in
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Begun & Brashler, Ch 20), "among the best understood of the
early state-level civilizations" of the Andes — despite leaving **no writing** — because their
**ceramics served as a recording system**, depicting in extraordinary detail almost every
aspect of Moche life: hunting, warfare, weaving, sexuality, and ritual.

## Society and Economy

A [[andean-state-formation|First Horizon]] society of the desert river valleys, the Moche
combined heavy reliance on **Pacific marine resources** with **irrigation agriculture** on the
limited arable land, supporting a growing population. Whether they constituted an
**aggressive expansionary chiefdom** or a **true state** was long debated.

## Ceramics and the Sacrifice Ceremony

Moche pottery is famous — even infamous — for its vivid, realistic modeling. From it
archaeologists reconstructed the **"Sacrifice Ceremony,"** in which an elite figure receives a
chalice (of blood) from a subordinate, accompanied by depictions of **human sacrifice**.

## Sipán and the Question of the State

The 1987 discovery of the unlooted **royal tombs of Sipán** transformed the debate: their
**extravagant grave goods** (gold and other precious metals) and accompanying **human
sacrifices** revealed a far higher concentration of power than a simple chiefdom, supporting
the view of the Moche as a genuine **state** — and Sipán's "Lord" as one of the richest burials
ever found in the Americas.

## Significance

The Moche demonstrate CWH IV's point that **complex statehood arose independently and
repeatedly in the Andes**, and that a society can be richly legible to history through
**imagery rather than text**. They prefigure the later north-coast Chimú and the imperial Inca.
