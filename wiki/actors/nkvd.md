# The NKVD
## Biography / Description
The **NKVD** (People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs) was the central state security and law enforcement agency of the Soviet Union from 1934 to 1946. It absorbed the OGPU (the successor to the Cheka) and assumed responsibility for regular police work, border guards, state security, and the administration of the Gulag concentration camp system. The NKVD was successively headed by Genrikh Yagoda (1934–1936), Nikolai Ezhov (1936–1938), and Lavrentii Beria (1938–1945). It was renamed the MVD (Ministry of Internal Affairs) in 1946.

The OGPU lost its separate institutional status, and its activities and personnel were transferred under the control of the People’s Commissariat of Internal Affairs (NKVD). Thus the state’s mechanisms of arbitrary repression appeared to have been weakened. Yet the changes for the better were nugatory. Massive instrumentalities of violence remained intact, and the NKVD’s engorgement of the OGPU had the result of constructing an even mightier centralized organ for policing and security (line 1666). Yezhov enjoyed the technical chores of administering repression, devising instructions that anticipated most practical snags... He was ‘short of stature, almost a dwarf, with a piercing voice and bandy legs’. His associates... dubbed him the Iron Hedgehog (line 1704).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Execution of the Great Purge (1936–1938):** Under Nikolai Ezhov, the NKVD executed Stalin's orders to purge the Party, military, and general population. Stalin sacked Yagoda in September on the grounds that he was four years behind in catching enemies of the people. His replacement was Nikolai Yezhov (line 1688). On 2 July, at Stalin’s instigation, the Politburo passed a resolution ‘On Anti-Soviet Elements’, and Yezhov scuttled back to the Politburo on 31 July with the scheme for the NKVD to arrest 259,450 persons over the following four months. In mid-August 1937 torture was sanctioned as a normal procedure of interrogation in Soviet prisons (line 1704). Victims were tried by trios (troiki), typically consisting of the local NKVD chief, party secretary and procurator. Trials were derisorily brief and sentences were carried out without right of appeal (line 1708). According to official central records, 681,692 persons were executed in 1937–8... Between one million and one and a half million persons, it is tentatively reckoned, were killed by firing squad, physical maltreatment or massive over-work in the care of the NKVD in those two years alone (line 1710).
- **Administration of the Gulag:** The NKVD managed a vast empire of forced-labor camps (the Gulag), utilizing millions of prisoners for large-scale economic projects such as mining, logging, and building canals and railways (e.g., the White Sea Canal). By 1939 the total number of prisoners in the forced-labour system – including prisons, labour camps, labour colonies and ‘special settlements’ – was 2.9 million (line 1720). Cattle-trucks were commandeered for journeys to the labour camps of the Gulag in Siberia, Kazakhstan and arctic Russia... On arrival at their camp they sawed timber, dug for gold, mined coal and built towns. Their meals left them constantly famished (line 1716).
- **Mass Deportations and Repression:** The NKVD conducted mass deportations of "suspect" ethnic minorities... The Poles were removed from Soviet Ukraine by a secret decree of April 1936... the Kurds were driven out from the North Caucasus, and the Koreans from eastern Siberia (line 1724). Vans and lorries marked ‘Meat’ or ‘Vegetables’ could carry the victims out to a quiet wood, such as the one near Butovo... (line 1716). Beria... became Yezhov’s deputy in July 1938. He had a collection of canes in his office, and Red Army commanders ruefully talked of such interrogations as occasions when they went ‘to have a coffee with Beria’ (line 1748).

## Historical Significance
The NKVD was the primary institutional arm of Stalinist terror, responsible for some of the worst human rights abuses of the twentieth century. The NKVD’s star had risen while the party’s had fallen; and Beria, when replacing Yezhov, entered the small circle of Stalin’s close advisers. The ‘organs’, as the security police were known, were at Stalin’s elbow whenever he needed them. Fearsome as it was, moreover, the NKVD itself operated in dread of Stalin (line 1768). Ezhov himself and many of his collaborators were eventually executed in the purge, illustrating the "permanent purge" dynamic of the era. Its state security functions were later split off and ultimately reconstituted in the KGB. An immense punitive industry was developed with guaranteed employment for torturers, jailors, stenographers, van-drivers, executioners, grave-diggers and camp-guards (line 1712).
