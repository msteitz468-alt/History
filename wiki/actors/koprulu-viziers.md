# The Köprülü Viziers (1656–c. 1710)

A sequence of grand viziers from the Albanian Köprülü family who dominated the [[ottoman-empire]] from 1656 to the early eighteenth century and presided over the empire's last major territorial expansion as well as the most serious attempt to restore the classical absolutism after its seventeenth-century erosion. Hodgson in [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974|*The Venture of Islam*, Vol. 3]] identifies the Köprülü period as the "secondary peak" of Ottoman power — a partial restoration that bought decades but could not reverse the structural transformation of the empire.

## Context: The Crisis of the Mid-Seventeenth Century

By the 1640s–1650s the [[ottoman-classical-system|classical system]] was in advanced decay. The central army had been civilianized; the Janissary corps had been diluted; the devshirme had effectively been abandoned; provincial notables (*derebey*) were consolidating semi-autonomous power in Anatolia and Rumelia; and Christian military vessels had entered the Thracian straits — the inner waters of the empire. The empire suffered "two decades of ignominy" at the start of the seventeenth century and a "second period of ignominy" at mid-century.

## Mehmed Köprülü (Grand Vizier 1656–1661)

The elder Köprülü took power under conditions of near-imperial collapse and restored central authority through ferocity:

> "The father, Memed Köprülü (1656–61), employed bloody ferocity against the grandees of the empire to suppress the more pernicious forms of corruption; he offended even the pādishāh's harem by laying hands on their funds and executing their favourites, and the 'ulamā' by expropriating waqf foundations, though some of the more established breaches of military discipline he left untouched."

His methods were those of the absolutism at its most direct: summary execution of corrupt officials, reassertion of central financial control, and military reorganization. He did not attempt to revive the devshirme or restore the classical institutions wholesale — he worked within the transformed structure while reimposing discipline on it.

## Ahmed Köprülü (Grand Vizier 1661–1676)

The son inherited a partially restored empire and ruled more conciliatorily while maintaining his father's standards. Under Ahmed:
- Crete was finally taken from Venice (1669), completing Ottoman control of the Aegean
- The empire pushed into Ukraine and maintained pressure on Poland
- A second siege of Vienna was planned

Hodgson: "So far recovered was the empire that even after Amed's death it could proceed to a second siege of Vienna (1683) even more threatening than the first under Süleymān."

## The Second Siege of Vienna and Aftermath (1683)

The second Vienna campaign (1683) ended in catastrophic defeat — the city was relieved by a joint German-Polish force. The subsequent Holy League alliance (Austria, Venice, Russia) drove the Ottomans out of all Hungary and temporarily seized the Morea and Azov. The Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) — the empire's first major territorial concession — formalized these losses. Henceforth the Ottomans were on the defensive in Europe.

The Köprülü restoration had extended the empire's reach to its maximum extent but could not prevent the structural weaknesses from reasserting themselves once the extraordinary personal energy of the viziers was gone.

## Later Köprülüs

Subsequent members of the family — Kara Mustafa Köprülü (executed after Vienna), Fazıl Mustafa (killed in battle 1691), Hüseyin (vizier 1697–1702) — continued to play significant roles, but the momentum of the restoration faded. The Treaty of Passarowitz (1718) extended Ottoman territorial losses further. The empire entered the [[tulip-age|Tulip Age]] under conditions of structural decentralization that the Köprülüs had temporarily masked but not resolved.

## Significance

The Köprülü period illustrates both the resilience and the limits of the [[ottoman-classical-system|classical synthesis]]. The empire could still produce remarkable institutional energy — a secondary peak of territorial reach and military effectiveness — but only through exceptional personal leadership working within, not against, the transformed social structure. The devshirme was not revived; the provincial notables were not eliminated; the Janissaries were not reformed at root. The Köprülü achievement was imposing discipline on a decentralized system, not reconstructing the centralized one.

Hodgson's assessment is characteristic: "the absolutist tradition was still strong enough to impose a partial reform when it had good viziers; and such reform could appear very effective for a time."
