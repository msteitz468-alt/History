## Overview

Abū ʿAlī Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad **Miskawayh** (c. 932–1030 CE) was a Persian philosopher, historian, and court intellectual who embodied the philosophical culture of the [[buyid-dynasty|Būyid age]]. Born in Rayy — the home city of the physician-philosopher [[abu-bakr-al-razi|Abū Bakr al-Rāzī]] — he served as librarian to a Būyid vizier at Rayy, giving him access to an exceptional collection of books. He represents what Adamson calls the "cultured, popular understanding of philosophy that was current in the tenth and early eleventh centuries" — Hellenistic ethics and Neoplatonic metaphysics blended with Islamic religious themes and displayed with literary style. Source: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]].

## Biography and Context

Miskawayh's career was shaped entirely by the Būyid patronage network. His position as keeper of a vizier's private library at Rayy made him one of the most widely read scholars of the era. Like the Būyids themselves, he was a Shīʿī from Persia, and his philosophical work reflects the cultural synthesis of the "Iranian intermezzo" — a period when Persian courtly culture, Hellenistic philosophy, and Islamic religious commitment interpenetrated. He remained intellectually active well into the eleventh century, dying around 1030, making him a contemporary of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] (though Avicenna regarded Miskawayh's style of philosophy as insufficiently rigorous).

He was not merely a philosopher but also an accomplished historian — Adamson compares him to Plutarch in this respect — and his historical works are an important source for Būyid-era events. He engaged in a celebrated series of philosophical exchanges with the great literary figure [[abu-hayyan-al-tawhidi|Abū Ḥayyān al-Tawḥīdī]], a set of questions and answers that still survives.

## Philosophical Position

Miskawayh worked within the **Kindian tradition**: the line running from [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] through his Khorasanian students to the eastern Islamic lands. He was extremely well-read in Aristotle's ethics, the Neoplatonic texts produced in the Kindī circle, Galen, and Islamic religious literature, and his characteristic move was to weave these together into a coherent but not highly original synthesis.

### Ethics as Medicine

His most significant work, *Tahdhīb al-Akhlāq* (Refinement of Character — the same title used by the Christian Baghdad philosopher [[yahya-ibn-adi|Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī]]), draws heavily on the Galenic tradition of psychological medicine. Like al-Rāzī and al-Kindī before him, Miskawayh treats ethics as a medicine for the soul. The tripartite Platonic soul (reason, spirit, desire) is the framework: virtue consists in reason's domination of the lower faculties. This is not purely Platonic, however: he also draws on Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* more thoroughly than most of his contemporaries, referencing the Arabic translation of that work repeatedly. He thereby occupies a position between Platonic medicine-of-the-soul and Aristotelian virtue ethics, using each to supplement the other.

Miskawayh distinguishes two ethical ideals:
- A **worldly virtue** of moderation along Aristotelian lines (the golden mean, a life balanced between excess and deficiency)
- A **contemplative ideal** of pure intellectual activity, though (unlike the Christian ascetic tradition embraced by Ibn ʿAdī) he does not require radical renunciation of bodily life for this higher path

Both ideals are compatible with reason's governance, only at different levels.

### Metaphysics

His treatise *On Soul and Intellect* presents and refutes an unnamed materialist philosopher who argued that all phenomena — including life itself — could be explained by the physical forces of heat and light alone (the opponent offered the beating heart of an excised animal placed in hot ash as evidence for heat as the principle of life). Miskawayh's response reasserts standard Aristotelian and Neoplatonic doctrines rather than breaking new ground, illustrating both his learning and his limits as an original thinker. He saw philosophy as fully compatible with Islam, in the tradition of [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]].

## Historical Significance

Miskawayh marks the high point of **philosophical adab**: the interpenetration of Hellenistic philosophical content with refined Arabic literary culture. This synthesis made philosophy socially respectable and culturally accessible in Būyid courtly circles, at the cost of some philosophical rigor. Adamson notes: "A court philosopher could be a kind of status symbol — the eleventh-century equivalent of an expensive artwork that is admired, but not necessarily understood."

His influence would be overshadowed by the advent of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]], whose more technically demanding and original philosophy rendered Miskawayh's synthesis dated. Nevertheless, the ethical tradition he represented — Galenic, Aristotelian, and moderately Islamic — continued to exert influence on the formation of Islamicate gentlemanly culture.

## Relationship to al-Tawḥīdī

Abū Ḥayyān al-Tawḥīdī, one of the great Arabic prose stylists of the age, recorded stories involving Miskawayh and left a series of philosophical questions that Miskawayh answered in an exchange that survives. Al-Tawḥīdī had attended the lessons of [[yahya-ibn-adi|Ibn ʿAdī]] in Baghdad, making him a philosophically informed interlocutor; his "gossipy" works are among our best sources for the intellectual atmosphere of the Būyid court.

## Key Works

- *Tahdhīb al-Akhlāq* (Refinement of Character) — ethical treatise
- *On Soul and Intellect* — metaphysical treatise defending Aristotelian-Neoplatonic positions
- *Tajārib al-Umam* (Experiences of Nations) — historical chronicle

## Historiography

Miskawayh is often treated by historians of Islamic philosophy as a minor figure eclipsed by Avicenna. More recent scholarship has reclaimed him as important for understanding the social circulation of philosophical ideas — how Hellenistic ethics became part of courtly culture rather than remaining a technical specialty. His historical works are primary sources for the Būyid period.
