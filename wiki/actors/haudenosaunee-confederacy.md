# The Haudenosaunee Confederacy (Iroquois League)

The **Haudenosaunee** ("People of the Longhouse"; the **Iroquois League** or **Five Nations**) were "the
greatest indigenous polity north of the Río Grande" in the centuries around contact — an alliance of the
**Seneca, Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida, and Mohawk** (joined c. 1720 by the **Tuscarora** to make Six). Mann
gives them the **coda** of [[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]] (Ch. 11, "The Great Law of Peace") because they
embody his most provocative argument: that **Native American traditions of liberty and consensual
self-government** were not a footnote to history but a current that fed into the modern democratic world.

## Deganawidah and the Great Law of Peace

After maize-beans-squash agriculture reached the Finger Lakes (~1000 CE), rising populations fell into a
spiral of revenge warfare. From it emerged **Deganawidah, the Peacemaker** — a shamanic outsider with a
stutter — who, with the Onondaga orator **Ayenwatha** (the historical "Hiawatha") as his voice, persuaded
the warring nations to unite. The recalcitrant Onondaga warlord **Tododaho** yielded after Deganawidah's
parable of the arrows (one snaps; a bundle of five cannot) and a solar eclipse; the Onondaga still keep
the council fire, and **Tododaho** remains the title of the league's speaker. The alliance's constitution,
the **Great Law of Peace**, was preserved orally on wampum and **Condolence Canes**.

## A consensual, checked, matrilineal government

The Great Law is striking for **limiting** power as much as granting it. Fifty **sachems** (chosen by the
female clan heads, impeachable by them) deliberated by **unanimous consensus**; the council's authority
was confined to **relations among the nations and with outsiders**, leaving internal affairs to each
nation. It could make peace but **not declare war** (left to each nation), and "an especially important
matter" had to be **referred back to the people**. Power, in short, rested on the **consent of the
governed** — "a libertarian dream" beside the despotisms of Europe and Asia. It was also, Mann argues, a
**feminist** order in its own idiom: the **clan mothers** held title to the land and its produce, set the
council's agenda ("men could not consider a matter not sent to them by the women"), and could veto and
remove leaders — a "separate-but-equal" arrangement that so impressed nineteenth-century U.S. feminists
(Stanton, Mott, Gage, all living in Haudenosaunee country) that it shaped early American feminism.

## How old? The 1142 eclipse

Haudenosaunee tradition holds the league predates Europeans. Long dismissed by skeptics, this is now
supported by independent estimates: Barbara Mann and Jerry Fields, counting 145 Tododahos and modeling
average tenures, dated the founding to the mid-twelfth century — and noted that the **last total solar
eclipse over upstate New York before 1600 fell on 31 August 1142**, a candidate date for Tododaho's
assent. If so, the Confederacy would be the world's **second-oldest continuously functioning
representative parliament**, after Iceland's Althing (930). The dating is still debated.

## The "Native American example": liberty and the influence thesis

Mann's larger claim is that the **personal autonomy and social equality** pervasive among the
eastern-seaboard peoples — "their whole constitution breathes nothing but liberty" (James Adair on the
Cherokee); they "allow of no Kind of Superiority of one over another" (Cadwallader Colden, a Mohawk
adoptee, 1749) — **impressed and infected the colonists**. On a porous frontier, captured or curious
colonists repeatedly **chose Indian life and refused to return** (Franklin lamented exactly this in
1753), so colonial societies could not become too coercive: their members "always had the option to vote
with their feet." Mann argues Enlightenment writers (Locke, Hume, Rousseau, Paine) drew **illustrations
of liberty from Indian examples**, and the Boston Tea Party's colonists dressed as "Mohawks." On the
**direct** claim that the **Great Law inspired the U.S. Constitution**, Mann is careful: taken literally
it is "implausible" (the Constitution is majoritarian not consensual, bicameral, property-protecting, and
denied women and the enslaved the vote — all unlike the Great Law), but in the **larger sense** the
framers "were pervaded by Indian ideals and images of liberty." It is one of the book's most contested
arguments.

## Significance

The Haudenosaunee are Mann's culminating refutation of [[holmbergs-mistake|Holmberg's Mistake]]: not
timeless primitives but the authors of a sophisticated, enduring, **constitutional** order — and, in his
boldest formulation, a wellspring of the modern idea of liberty itself, so that "everywhere that liberty
is cherished... people are children of the Haudenosaunee and their neighbors." The claim is debated, but
it reframes Native America as a **full participant** shaping the colonial and modern world, not merely its
victim.

## Colonial era (Salisbury, CHNPA I.1 ch. 7)

**Naming:** Salisbury (and most colonial sources) use “Iroquois,” “Five Nations,” and after 1722 “Six Nations.” This wiki’s canonical slug is [[haudenosaunee-confederacy]] (anti-duplicate rule).

### Power concentration and Beaver Wars

Through the 1610s–1660s political power in the East concentrated in European colonies **plus** the Five Nations, each dependent on ties with other Native groups. [[mohawk]] Dutch access (after defeating Mahicans by 1630) funneled guns and wampum; [[beaver-wars-17th-century]] destroyed the [[huron-wendat]] as a political entity (1648–49) and dispersed Petuns, Neutrals, Eries. Captive adoption replaced disease and raid dead—structural mourning-war demography. Mid-1660s setbacks (Susquehannock 1663 repulse; Mahican 1664; French Mohawk raids 1666) forced peace with France 1665–67; Christian factionalism and Kahnawake migrations followed.

### Covenant Chain and Grand Settlement

After [[king-philips-war-1675]], Governor Andros’s 1677 Albany treaties forged the **Covenant Chain**—a “silver” chain vs older “iron”—making Iroquois intercolonial brokers (including Susquehannock refugee settlement and tributary claims over Chesapeake groups). Renewed western war 1680s–90s met French-allied defeats (losses ~1689–98: 500 of 2,000 fighting men; 1,600 of ~8,600 population). **Grand Settlement 1701:** neutrality in future Anglo-French wars for French hunting/trade access to Detroit; English Albany privileges retained; Covenant Chain redefined to exclude Iroquois military activity for English.

### Eighteenth century: Six Nations, neutrality, southern mourning wars

[[tuscarora]] incorporation as sixth nation (1722) after Tuscarora War. Reaffirmation of dual friendship with British and French (1717) along Grand Settlement lines; traditionalist revival; military activity directed south vs [[catawba]] and Cherokee (~seventy years intermittent Iroquois–Catawba warfare after ~1707 Shawnee enlistment of Iroquois). 1722 Albany treaties with VA/PA assumed Iroquois hegemony over unrepresented [[tuscarora]], [[lenape-delaware]], [[shawnee]], Conestoga—revived Covenant Chain as joint Anglo-Iroquois instrument. Walking Purchase (forged deed + myth of prior Iroquois Delaware conquest) removed Delawares with Six Nations cooperation. Ohio Mingos grew independent of the Confederacy 1730s–40s.

### Seven Years’ War, Stanwix, Revolution

Most Iroquois resisted British enlistment early in [[seven-years-war-1756]]; few Mohawks under William Johnson. Easton 1758: Ohio Indians accepted Iroquois suzerainty as anti-French instrument. **Fort Stanwix 1768:** Johnson persuaded Iroquois to cede vast Shawnee, Delaware, and Cherokee Ohio hunting lands the Six Nations had never occupied or controlled. Revolution split the Confederacy: [[oneida]] (and many Tuscarora) leaned Patriot under Samuel Kirkland; most [[mohawk]] followed Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea) for Britain; Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca mostly royal. Sullivan–Clinton 1779 destroyed forty additional Six Nations villages; >5,000 fled to Canada; at war’s end only ~2 of ~30 towns intact (Green/Salisbury). Haldimand Canadian reserves (Bay of Quinte; Grand River).

### Post-1783 (Green, CHNPA ch. 8)

Fort Stanwix 1784 and related right-of-conquest treaties treated Six Nations as defeated British allies; U.S. policy claimed title without Native consultation at Paris 1783. Confederacy endured as political identity despite territorial catastrophe.

## Historiography (colonial)

Richter *Ordeal of the Longhouse*; Jennings Covenant Chain volumes; Graymont/Wallace on Revolution; Trigger comparative Iroquoian work; Salisbury ch. 7 synthesis. Wampum’s role in Deganawida–Hiawatha condolence and as commercialized trade good is both emic and etic load-bearing. “Iroquois empire” theses vs limited-hegemony/Covenant Chain interpretations remain live debates.
