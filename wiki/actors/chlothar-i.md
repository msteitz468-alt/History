# Chlothar I

**Chlothar I** (c. 497–561), called "Lothar" in Thorpe's translation, was the youngest son
of **[[clovis]]** and [[clotild|Clotild]], king at **Soissons** from the partition of 511.
He outlived all his brothers and in 558 reunited the entire Frankish realm under one ruler
for the first time since Clovis — only for it to be divided again among his own sons at his
death. He is the dynastic bridge between Clovis's generation and the civil wars that
**[[gregory-of-tours-history-of-the-franks-thorpe-1974|Gregory]]** lived through.

## Career

Chlothar shared in the Burgundian wars and the murder of [[chlodomer|Chlodomer]]'s
children — it was he who "jabbed his dagger into his armpit and so murdered" the elder boy,
rounding on [[childebert-i|Childebert I]] when his brother wavered ("It was you who thought
of this business!"). From the Thuringian campaign he took the captive princess
**[[radegund-of-poitiers|Radegund]]** as his share of the booty and later married her,
afterwards murdering her brother — the act that drove Radegund to found her convent at
Poitiers. Gregory credits Clotild's prayers to St Martin with repeatedly saving Chlothar
from his brothers' ambushes ("No drop of rain fell on Lothar").

He had **seven sons by various wives**, including — by Ingund — Charibert, **[[guntram|Guntram]]** and **[[sigibert-i|Sigibert I]]**; by Ingund's sister Aregund, **[[chilperic-i|Chilperic I]]**; and by Chunsina, **Chramn**. His son Chramn revolted in alliance with Childebert; Chlothar finally captured him in Brittany and had him "strangled with a piece of cloth" before the hut was burnt down over him and his wife and daughters. Having absorbed the kingdoms of his brothers and nephews, Chlothar reunited Francia in 558; he died of a fever at Compiègne in the fifty-first year of his reign (561) and was buried at Soissons. His realm was then re-partitioned among his four surviving sons — the division that set the stage for the wars of Sigibert and Chilperic.

## Counterfactual Significance

Chlothar's long survival and reunification show that Merovingian division was contingent,
not structural: the realm could be made whole when one heir outlived the rest. But his
immediate re-partition among four sons, and the lethal Chramn revolt, demonstrate the
dynasty's inability to institutionalise unity — the direct prelude to the Brunhild–
Fredegund civil wars. See [[merovingian-franks]].
