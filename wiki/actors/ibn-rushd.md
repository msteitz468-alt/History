## Overview

Abu al-Walid Muhammad Ibn Rushd (1126–1198), known to the Latin Occident as Averroës, was the greatest philosopher of the Western Islamic world and the most systematic commentator on Aristotle ever produced by the Islamic tradition. Born in Córdoba under the Muwahhid (Almohad) dynasty, he served as grand qadi (chief judge) and at points as the Muwahhid caliph's personal physician, while composing the extensive Aristotle commentaries that became the primary vehicle through which Aristotelian philosophy was transmitted to medieval Europe.

His historical significance bifurcates sharply between his influence in Islamdom—relatively limited, especially after his disgrace—and his influence in the Latin Occident, where his commentaries established him as "The Commentator" par excellence, and where his philosophical positions launched the controversial movement known as "Averroism" that shaped Western intellectual history well into the early modern period.

## The Muwahhid Context

Ibn Rushd worked under the Muwahhid dynasty, whose political tradition differed from the Seljuk-dominated central Islamic lands. The Muwahhids maintained a more direct alliance between the ruling dynasty and intellectual culture, with philosophers serving as courtiers. Ibn Rushd was brought into this circle by the philosopher Ibn Tufayl. Under this patronage, "Falsafah and Shari'ah were to share the field exclusively between them"—the philosopher's private wisdom reserved for the elite, public religious life confined to the strict Shari'ah ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v2-1974]]).

## Philosophical Position

Ibn Rushd's central move was a return to stricter Aristotelianism after Ibn Sina's synthesis, which he saw as having made too many concessions to Sufi and Islamic theological categories. He engaged directly with al-Ghazali's *Incoherence of the Philosophers* in his *Tahafut al-Tahafut* (Incoherence of the Incoherence), defending Aristotelian positions by "cautiously shifting some primary categories."

Key positions:
- **On creation**: Every particular thing is originated by God, as Islamic theology requires; but neither "origination" nor "eternity" properly applies to God's relationship with the whole world. The debate's terms are inadequate to the ultimate reality.
- **On divine knowledge**: God does not know particulars in the way humans know them, nor universals in the way humans know them; His knowledge is "of a still more comprehensive kind than either." This retreats to positions that "had the virtue of not being refutable, as their meaning could hardly be known."
- **On Falsafah and religion**: Philosophical reasoning (Falsafah) and revealed law (Shari'ah) occupy "two different realms of truth, answering largely different sorts of questions." Falsafah addresses metaphysical reality through demonstrative reason; religion provides images adequate for governing the masses. Kalâm (theological speculation) and Sufi metaphysics are to be rejected as dangerous distortions that mislead those not ready for pure reason.

Ibn Rushd "rejected much of Ibn Sina's attempt to give Philosophical standing to Sufi experience and to prophethood; he went back to a stricter Aristotelianism" and "could not accept" the position that religion could claim any understanding of metaphysical reality—that domain was reserved for Falsafah.

## Averroism in the Occident

Ibn Rushd's influence in the Latin Occident vastly exceeded his influence in Islamdom. His Aristotle commentaries, translated into Latin in the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries, were the gateway through which medieval Europe encountered Aristotle in depth. He became "The Commentator," the authoritative interpreter of "The Philosopher." Thomas Aquinas engaged extensively (and critically) with his positions.

The movement known as "Averroism" in the Latin Occident drew from Ibn Rushd's sharp dichotomy between philosophical reason and revealed faith. In Islamdom, where mysticism complicated the dichotomy, this division "played a secondary role at most." In Christendom, "his attitude, under the name of 'Averroism,' served rather to justify an accommodation between a radically rationalistic Philosophy and Christian theology itself." The most important consequence was "to raise Occidental thinking to a higher level of sophistication."

Hodgson identifies a far-reaching, if unintended, consequence: the Averroist division between "philosophy" based on "reason" about "nature" and "religion" based on "faith" in "revelation" became foundational for Western intellectual self-understanding—and has been applied retrospectively "as if it were a natural division in all human thought, with confusing results." In modern Arabic, *falsafah* now means philosophical thinking generally, not a specific school, "in a confused contrast to dogmatic 'religious' thinking"—itself a trace of this Averroist transmission.

## Disgrace and Legacy

Around 1195, Ibn Rushd was disgraced—apparently for having composed philosophical works that reached too wide an audience—and exiled briefly to a town near Córdoba. He was restored before his death in 1198. The episode illustrates the tension inherent in his own position: as public censor he condemned too popular a promulgation of Aristotelian ideas; as private thinker he pursued those same ideas. "Ibn Rushd himself was grand qadi and chief censor" under a dynasty that simultaneously patronized philosophy and persecuted popular intellectual speculation.

In Islamdom, later Falsafah developed along different lines—above all through the Iran-centered synthesis of Falsafah, kalâm, and Sufism associated with Nasir al-Din Tusi and later figures—rather than following Ibn Rushd's rigorously Aristotelian and dualistic path.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Ibn Rushd, the Latin Occidental engagement with Aristotle would have been significantly delayed and differently shaped. Whether the specific distinction between "reason" and "faith" as alternative grounds for two separate disciplines would have been articulated so sharply without his mediation is genuinely unclear; the tendency was latent in the Abrahamic tradition, but his systematic formulation gave it a particular form that proved historically durable.
