# The Pallavas, Chalukyas and Rashtrakutas (c. 500–1000)

Between the Guptas and the [[chola-empire|Cholas]], the **Deccan and Tamil south** were dominated
by three great dynasties locked in a recurring, **geographically-driven** conflict: the **Chalukyas**
of Badami (the western plateau), the **Pallavas** of Kanchipuram (Tamil-nad), and — after they
overthrew the Chalukyas — the **Rashtrakutas**. Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## A geographic, not merely dynastic, conflict

Thapar reads the southern political pattern as a structural clash of two zones — the **western
Deccan plateau** and the fertile **Tamil-nad** plain south of Madras — each seeking to control the
Krishna–Godavari waterway, with **Vengi** the perennial bone of contention. The conflict "continued
through the centuries despite the rise and fall of particular dynasties." Because the south lacked
the vast fertile plains of the Ganges, large unitary kingdoms were hard to sustain, and **regional
loyalties and smaller polities** formed far earlier than in the north.

## The dynasties

- **The Pallavas** of Kanchipuram (probable foreign — perhaps Parthian — origin, dressed up with a
  Naga-princess legend) became "the first Tamil dynasty of real consequence." **Mahendravarman I**
  (600–630), a dramatist and temple-builder (the rock-cut temples of **Mahabalipuram**), shifted from
  Jainism to Shaivism; **Narasimhavarman I** sacked the Chalukya capital Badami (642). The Pallava
  age saw the flowering of the **"Tamil personality"** and Dravidian temple architecture.
- **The Chalukyas of Badami**: **Pulakeshin II** famously **defeated [[harsha|Harsha]]** on the
  Narmada (~630), checking northern expansion into the Deccan, before the long see-saw of
  Chalukya–Pallava wars (neither able to hold the other's land — a sign of matched strength).
- **The Rashtrakutas**: the Chalukya feudatory **Dantidurga** overthrew his overlords (~753);
  **Amoghavarsha** (814–880, "Ashoka of the South") patronised Jainism and Kannada literature. The
  Chalukyas of Kalyani later revived and ended Rashtrakuta rule — "the wheel come full circle."

## Southern village self-government

Thapar highlights the south's distinctive **local autonomy**: a dense network of **assemblies** —
the village **sabha** (managing land, irrigation, justice, records, its committees chosen by lot and
working closely with the informal **ur**), district *nadu* councils, and assemblies of merchant
guilds, craftsmen, students, and priests. This tradition, "actively maintained for many centuries" in
Tamil-nad far more than in the north, underpinned the later [[chola-empire|Chola]] state. Thapar
cautions that the term "feudatory" here means **political vassalage**, not the economic feudal tenure
of the European model — that came later.

## Religion and culture

The age was one of intense **Aryanisation** of the south (brahmans as keepers of the Vedic tradition,
the rise of Sanskrit colleges and *mathas*, the eclipse of Jainism and Buddhism for lack of royal
patronage) and, in reaction, the surge of the **Tamil devotional cult** — the *bhakti* hymns of the
Shaiva and Vaishnava "saints" — and the philosophy of **Shankara** (Advaita Vedanta) and, later,
**Ramanuja** (Vaishnava). The Malabar (Chera/Kerala) coast meanwhile received the **Arabs** (the
ancestors of the Mappila Muslims), the fleeing **Parsis**, and an early **Jewish** community — Thapar's
reminder of the south's cosmopolitan trade world. See [[hinduism]].
