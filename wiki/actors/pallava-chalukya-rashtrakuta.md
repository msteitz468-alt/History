# The Pallavas, Chalukyas and Rashtrakutas (c. 500–1000)

Between the Guptas and the [[chola-empire|Cholas]], the **Deccan and Tamil south** were dominated
by three great dynasties locked in a recurring, **geographically-driven** conflict: the **Chalukyas**
of Badami (the western plateau), the **Pallavas** of Kanchipuram (Tamil-nad), and — after they
overthrew the Chalukyas — the **Rashtrakutas**. Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## A geographic, not merely dynastic, conflict

Thapar reads the southern political pattern as a structural clash of two zones — the **western
Deccan plateau** and the fertile **Tamil-nad** plain south of Madras — each seeking to control the
Krishna–Godavari waterway, with **Vengi** the perennial bone of contention. The conflict "continued
through the centuries despite the rise and fall of particular dynasties." Because the south lacked
the vast fertile plains of the Ganges, large unitary kingdoms were hard to sustain, and **regional
loyalties and smaller polities** formed far earlier than in the north.

## The dynasties

- **The Pallavas** of Kanchipuram (probable foreign — perhaps Parthian — origin, dressed up with a
  Naga-princess legend) became "the first Tamil dynasty of real consequence." **Mahendravarman I**
  (600–630), a dramatist and temple-builder (the rock-cut temples of **Mahabalipuram**), shifted from
  Jainism to Shaivism; **Narasimhavarman I** sacked the Chalukya capital Badami (642). The Pallava
  age saw the flowering of the **"Tamil personality"** and Dravidian temple architecture.
- **The Chalukyas of Badami**: **Pulakeshin II** famously **defeated [[harsha|Harsha]]** on the
  Narmada (~630), checking northern expansion into the Deccan, before the long see-saw of
  Chalukya–Pallava wars (neither able to hold the other's land — a sign of matched strength).
- **The Rashtrakutas**: the Chalukya feudatory **Dantidurga** overthrew his overlords (~753);
  **Amoghavarsha** (814–880, "Ashoka of the South") patronised Jainism and Kannada literature. The
  Chalukyas of Kalyani later revived and ended Rashtrakuta rule — "the wheel come full circle."

## Southern village self-government

Thapar highlights the south's distinctive **local autonomy**: a dense network of **assemblies** —
the village **sabha** (managing land, irrigation, justice, records, its committees chosen by lot and
working closely with the informal **ur**), district *nadu* councils, and assemblies of merchant
guilds, craftsmen, students, and priests. This tradition, "actively maintained for many centuries" in
Tamil-nad far more than in the north, underpinned the later [[chola-empire|Chola]] state. Thapar
cautions that the term "feudatory" here means **political vassalage**, not the economic feudal tenure
of the European model — that came later.

## Religion and culture

The age was one of intense **Aryanisation** of the south (brahmans as keepers of the Vedic tradition,
the rise of Sanskrit colleges and *mathas*, the eclipse of Jainism and Buddhism for lack of royal
patronage) and, in reaction, the surge of the **Tamil devotional cult** — the *bhakti* hymns of the
Shaiva and Vaishnava "saints" — and the philosophy of **Shankara** (Advaita Vedanta) and, later,
**Ramanuja** (Vaishnava). The Malabar (Chera/Kerala) coast meanwhile received the **Arabs** (the
ancestors of the Mappila Muslims), the fleeing **Parsis**, and an early **Jewish** community — Thapar's
reminder of the south's cosmopolitan trade world. See [[hinduism]].

## Avari's Treatment (Chapters 9–10): Detailed Accounts

Burjor Avari's *India: The Ancient Past* (2007) provides substantially more detail on all three dynasties and their interactions.

**The Chalukyas**: Three distinct Chalukya families are distinguished — Early Chalukyas of Badami (525–757), Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi (624–1020), and Later Chalukyas of Kalyani (973–1200). Pulakeshin II's victories are inscribed "in the renowned Aihole prasasti by his court poet, Ravikirti, and inscribed on a slab of the eastern wall of the great Meguti Temple of Aihole in Karnataka." Hsuan Tsang's description of their army: "if a general loses a battle they do not inflict punishment, but present him with women's clothes, and so he is driven to seek death for himself." The Chalukyan cultural legacy is concentrated at three Karnataka sites: **Aihole** (first capital, earliest free-standing temples, including the Meguti Temple dated to 634), **Badami** (second capital, rock-cut cave temples), and **Pattadakal**.

**The Pallava kingdom**: The dynasty dated itself to ad 275 but "its great epoch straddled the seventh and the eighth centuries." Two theories of origin: descendants of Parthians from Iran, or descendants of north Indian brahman migrants. The capital was **Kanchipuram**, "one of the seven sacred cities of Hinduism," and the port city of **Mamallapuram** (Mahabalipuram). The **Chalukya-Pallava conflict** lasted a full century with neither side able to hold the other's territory: Pulakeshin II was killed in Narasimhavarman I's attack on Badami (642); Vikramaditya I took Kanchipuram from 655; Vikramaditya II captured it again in 740, leaving an inscription recording "both his conquests and his generosity towards the people of the vanquished city" — which the Pallavas left unerased when they regained the city. Pallava architecture at Mamallapuram included the **Five Rathas** (miniature temples demonstrating the evolution of the southern temple style), a great frieze of Arjuna's penance 28 metres long and 12 metres high cut into granite, and the **Shore Temple** facing the Bay of Bengal. The Pallava irrigation system introduced huge earthwork tanks called *eri* on *eripatti* land; the kingdom was divided into 24 *kottam* localities, each a "unique zone of agrarian, pastoral and village-based economy." Land grants to brahmans (the *brahmadeya* villages) were used to increase agricultural productivity through the technical skills of brahman settlers.

**The Rashtrakutas**: The word means "peaks among kingdoms." They ruled from **Manyakheta** (Mankhed, southeast of Sholapur, Maharashtra), bearing the Sanskrit title *Vallabha-Raja* ("beloved lord") — "from a Prakrit term, Ballaha-Raya, the Arabs came to call the Rashtrakutas Al-Ballahara." Founded by Dantidurga, the dynasty "produced some of the most outstanding monarchs of India": Krishna I (760–75), Dhruva (780–93), Govinda III (793–814), **Amoghavarsha I** (814–80), Indra III (914–28), Krishna III (939–67). Arab travellers Al-Masudi and Ibn Khordadbih described the Rashtrakuta king as King of Kings whose armies dominated from Konkan to Sind; "most of the kings of Hindustan turned their faces towards the Ballahara king while they were praying." The Rashtrakutas "controlled the Deccan vigorously, but the north and the south had to be fought over continually" — the classic Deccan dilemma of balancing northern campaigns against possible southern losses.

The Rashtrakutas' greatest cultural monument is the **Kailashanatha Temple at Ellora** — "the world's largest single free-standing structure, carved out of living rock, and covering an area more widespread than the Parthenon of Athens." Art historian Percy Brown described it as "the most stupendous single work of art ever executed in India." Nearly 3 million cubic metres of rock were excavated. The temple is attributed to King Krishna I and remains standing today as a UNESCO heritage site. The 34 rock-carved cave temples at Ellora (half Hindu, 12 Buddhist, 5 Jain) mostly date to the 8th–9th centuries. **Amoghavarsha I** wrote the *Kavirajamarga*, the first great literary manual in **Kannada** — "the first great literary figure in Kannada was the Rashtrakuta king Amoghavarsha."

The Rashtrakutas participated centrally in the **Tripartite Struggle** for Kanauj alongside the [[pala-empire|Palas]] and [[gurjara-pratihara|Gurjara-Pratihara]]. Indra III finally captured Kanauj in the early tenth century. "Continual fighting drained their resources; and, in the eleventh century, they were overthrown by a feudatory who established a new dynasty, known as the **Western Chalukyas of Kalyani**."
