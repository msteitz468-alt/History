# Galen

**Galen of Pergamum** (Aelius Galenus, b. September 129 CE in Pergamum, Asia Minor,
d. c. 216) was the most influential physician of the ancient world and the dominant
authority in Western and Islamic medicine for the following fourteen centuries. For
the history of the Roman Empire he is also an irreplaceable eyewitness: his vast
clinical corpus is the single most important contemporary testimony to the
**[[events/antonine-plague-165|Antonine Plague]]**.

## Career

Born in the middle of the reign of **Hadrian**, Galen trained in [[places/alexandria|Alexandria]],
where the rare chance to study real human bones left a lasting mark ("for this reason,
if for no other, try to visit Alexandria"). He came to Rome in **162 CE**, the first
year of the joint rule of [[actors/marcus-aurelius|Marcus Aurelius]] and
[[actors/lucius-verus|Lucius Verus]], and rose to serve as physician to the imperial
household. He liked to quote the saying that "Rome was the epitome of the whole world."

## The Antonine Plague: clinical witness

Galen's value to environmental and epidemiological history rests on his detailed,
if humorally framed, descriptions of the great pandemic that struck the empire from
165 CE:

- In *The Method of Medicine* he recorded a young victim whose cough turned violent,
  who expelled dark scabs from throat ulcerations, and whose body was wrapped head to
  foot in a **black rash** — symptoms that have anchored the modern consensus that the
  disease was **smallpox** (*Variola major*).
- His notes describe fever, a black pustular rash, conjunctival irritation, deep
  windpipe ulceration, and black or bloody stools. The Littman and Littman study
  concluded that Galen had seen the **hemorrhagic expression of smallpox**, the very
  black stool signalling the worst outcome.
- Galen **fled Rome in 166 CE** as the disease approached, an act he admitted in
  *On My Own Books* was prompted by the "great plague." His flight, and his later
  recall by Marcus Aurelius to the plague-ravaged winter camp at **Aquileia** (where
  Lucius Verus died), help historians track the pandemic's westward advance.

Tellingly, Galen — for all his clinical range — **betrays no familiarity with bubonic
plague**, a silence Kyle Harper uses as evidence that *Yersinia pestis* had not yet
reached the Mediterranean before the [[events/justinianic-plague-541|Justinianic
Plague]] of 541.

## Significance

Galen's writings let modern historians and paleo-epidemiologists reconstruct an ancient
pandemic from contemporary clinical detail rather than later rhetoric — a rare bridge
between the textual and biological records of antiquity. See
[[sources/harper-fate-of-rome-2017|Harper, *The Fate of Rome*]] and
[[concepts/roman-climate-optimum|the Roman environmental and disease regime]].
