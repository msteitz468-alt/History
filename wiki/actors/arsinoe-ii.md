## Role

Arsinoe II (c. 316–270 BCE), daughter of [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]] and Berenice,
was the most powerful woman of the early Hellenistic age — successively queen of three
kingdoms (Thrace, Macedon, Egypt) — and, as wife of her own brother Ptolemy II, a
co-ruler of Ptolemaic Egypt and a cornerstone of its [[ruler-cult|dynastic cult]]. Will
(CAH VII.1, ch. 4) makes her the central figure of the dynastic "vipers' knot" that
brought down [[lysimachus]].

## Career and Significance

- **Queen of Thrace**: married c. 300 to the aged [[lysimachus]], she bore him sons
  and schemed to secure their succession against the heir-apparent Agathocles (her
  stepson, married to her half-sister Lysandra). She is held responsible for
  manoeuvring Lysimachus into **murdering Agathocles** (c. 283) — the dynastic crime
  that shattered his support and brought on [[battle-of-corupedium-281|Corupedium]].
- **Queen of Macedon**: after Lysimachus' death she escaped to Cassandreia and married
  her half-brother [[ptolemy-ceraunus|Ptolemy Ceraunus]] — who promptly murdered two of
  her sons. She fled to Samothrace, then to Egypt.
- **Queen of Egypt**: she married her full brother **[[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Ptolemy
  II]]**, the first of the [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic]] **brother–sister marriages** (the
  *Theoi Adelphoi*, "Brother–Sister Gods" — and the epithet *Philadelphos*, "sibling-loving,"
  was hers before it was his). A "masterful personality," she received **full deification
  during her lifetime** — the first "western" queen so honoured — with her own priestess
  (a *kanephoros*), a city cult, and spontaneous private worship as the **Marine
  Aphrodite**; cities and Fayyum villages were named for her, and a tax (the *apomoira* on
  vineyards) funded her cult. Her death left [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Philadelphus]],
  Turner suggests, missing "a guiding personality."

## Counterfactual Significance

Arsinoe demonstrates the **dynastic, personal character of Hellenistic politics**, in
which royal women could be decisive agents — her ambition arguably triggered the chain
(Agathocles' murder → Corupedium → Ceraunus → the Gauls) that destroyed Lysimachus'
kingdom and reshaped the north. In Egypt she helped fix the Ptolemaic pattern of
sibling marriage and living-ruler cult that defined the dynasty to Cleopatra VII. See
[[hellenistic-monarchy]], [[ruler-cult]], [[ptolemaic-egypt]].
