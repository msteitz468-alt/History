# Medieval Christian Ethiopia (Zagwe and Solomonid)

After the fall of [[aksum|Aksum]], Christian power in the Ethiopian highlands revived under two great
dynasties — the **Zagwe** and then the **Solomonids** — sustaining one of the world's oldest Christian
states through the medieval centuries amid an encircling Muslim world. Documented for the wiki through the
**UNESCO [[gha-vol4-12th-16th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. IV]]** (ch. 17, Taddesse Tamrat).

## The Zagwe and Lalibela

The **Zagwe dynasty** (c. 1137–1270), centred farther south than Aksum in the Lasta highlands, is
remembered above all for **King Lalibela** (c. 1181–1221) — "the Saint Louis of Ethiopia" — and the
extraordinary **rock-hewn churches** of his capital (renamed **Lalibela**), eleven monolithic churches
carved down into the living rock (the cruciform **Bete Giyorgis**, St George's, the most famous). Cut off
from Jerusalem, Lalibela conceived his churches as a **"New Jerusalem"** for Ethiopian pilgrims — a high
point of indigenous African Christian art and engineering.

## The Solomonid restoration (1270)

In **1270 Yekuno Amlak** overthrew the Zagwe and founded the **Solomonid dynasty**, which legitimized
itself through the *Kebra Nagast* ("Glory of the Kings") — the national epic claiming the dynasty's descent
from **Solomon and the Queen of Sheba** via their son **Menelik I**, who was said to have brought the Ark of
the Covenant to Ethiopia. This sacred genealogy underwrote the **"Solomonic" emperors** for seven centuries
(to Haile Selassie, 1974). The medieval Solomonids were a **mobile, military monarchy** without a fixed
capital, expanding the Christian realm and tying church and crown tightly together.

## The Christian–Muslim frontier

Ethiopia's medieval history turned on the long struggle with the **Muslim sultanates** of the eastern
lowlands — **Ifat** and then **Adal**. The warrior-emperor **Amda Seyon** (r. 1314–44) crushed the
sultanates and secured Christian dominance; **Zara Yaqob** (r. 1434–68), a great reforming and centralizing
ruler, consolidated the church and the state. The balance broke in **1529–43**, when **Ahmad ibn Ibrahim
al-Ghazi ("Gran")** of Adal — with Ottoman firearms — overran most of Christian Ethiopia, until a
**Portuguese musketeer expedition** helped the emperor defeat and kill him (1543). The wars left both
powers exhausted on the eve of the **Oromo** migrations into the highlands.

## Significance

Medieval Christian Ethiopia is, with [[christian-nubia|Christian Nubia]], one of **Africa's two enduring
medieval Christian civilizations** — and the only one to survive into modern times. Its rock churches,
its Ge'ez literature and the *Kebra Nagast*, and its self-image as a chosen Christian island ("Prester
John's" kingdom in European imagination) make it a unique synthesis of African and biblical traditions.
