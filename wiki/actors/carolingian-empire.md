# The Carolingian Empire

## Overview

The **Carolingian Empire** was the Frankish state that, under **Charlemagne**
(r. 768–814 CE), united most of Western and Central Europe into the largest political
unit the region had seen since [[roman-empire|Rome]] — and revived the **imperial title**
in the Latin West on **Christmas Day 800 CE**, when Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne
*Imperator*. In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Ch 14) the Carolingians close the
volume's western arc: the moment Latin Europe ceased to be a fragmented Roman afterglow
and forged its own distinct Christian-Germanic synthesis — politically fragile but
culturally foundational.

## From Mayors to Emperors

The Carolingians rose as **mayors of the palace** under the fading Merovingian kings.
**[[charles-martel|Charles Martel]]** halted Muslim raids at Tours/Poitiers (732) and
refounded the family's dominance after its near-collapse in 714; his son
**[[pippin-iii|Pippin III]]**, with papal sanction, deposed the last Merovingian and took
the crown (751: [[pippinid-coup-751]]), sealing the **Frankish–Papal alliance**.
**[[charlemagne|Charlemagne]]** then conquered the Lombards (774), the Saxons (forcibly
Christianized, 772–804), Bavaria (787–788), and the Avars (791–796), assembling a realm
from the Pyrenees to the Elbe and from the North Sea to central Italy.

## NCMH II: The Carolingian Achievement Reassessed

[[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (1995) is the wiki's principal authority for the empire's
long eighth and ninth centuries. It deliberately abandons the old "blow-by-blow account of
conquests and coronations" for an analysis of **how power and authority actually worked**, and
poses the period's central question: was the Carolingian state *objectively different* from its
Merovingian predecessor? Its answers are characteristically cautious. Fouracre (Ch. 3) stresses
that **custom and continuity outweighed innovation** — "in 814 as in 714, power on the ground lay
in the hands of counts and bishops" — and frames the [[nature-of-carolingian-government|sharp
debate]] over whether [[missi-dominici|the missi]], [[carolingian-capitularies|capitularies]], and
the apparatus of reform amounted to a genuine reconstitution of Roman public authority (the
"optimistic" view) or merely glossed a personal, plunder-driven hegemony (Reuter's "pessimistic"
view). The volume's overall verdict: the empire's enduring achievement was **cultural and ideological
rather than territorial** — the synthesis of Latin, Christian, and Frankish elements that gave Europe
a common civilisation, realised above all in the [[carolingian-renaissance]].

## Empire, Church, and the Carolingian Renaissance

Charlemagne's coronation as emperor expressed a claim to **renewed Roman and Christian
universal rule** in the West — distinct from, and implicitly rivalrous with,
**[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]]**. He governed through counts, *missi dominici*
(traveling inspectors), and the Church, and sponsored the **[[carolingian-renaissance|Carolingian
Renaissance]]**: a revival of Latin learning, manuscript copying (preserving much classical
literature), and the clear **[[caroline-minuscule|Caroline minuscule]]** script — the ancestor of
modern lowercase letters. Its leading scholar was **[[alcuin|Alcuin of York]]**.

## Fragmentation

The empire depended on the person of the ruler and on continuous conquest. The reign of
**[[louis-the-pious|Louis the Pious]]** (814–840) — the failed **Ordinatio imperii** of 817,
the civil wars, the "Field of Lies" and public penance of 833 — exposed the irreconcilable
tension between the ideology of imperial unity and Frankish **partible inheritance**. On his
death his sons fought the **[[battle-of-fontenoy-841|battle of Fontenoy]]** (841), and the
**[[treaty-of-verdun-843|Treaty of Verdun]]** (843) divided the empire among
**[[lothar-i|Lothar I]]** (the Middle Kingdom and imperial title),
**[[louis-the-german|Louis the German]]** (East Francia), and
**[[charles-the-bald|Charles the Bald]]** (West Francia) — seeding the long divergence of
**France** and **Germany**. Renewed **Viking, Magyar, and Saracen** raids accelerated the
breakup, completed when Charles the Fat was deposed (887) and the empire fragmented for good
into the kingdoms and lordships of the post-Carolingian age (888; see [[feudalism]]).

## Significance

Though short-lived, the Carolingian Empire created the durable template of a **Latin
Christian Europe** bound to the Papacy, the imperial idea later revived as the **Holy
Roman Empire**, the cultural and scriptural infrastructure of medieval learning, and the
political fault lines of France and Germany. It is the institutional birth of "Europe"
as a self-conscious civilization distinct from both Byzantium and Islam.

## Historiography

Carolingian sources are largely ecclesiastical and court-centered (Einhard's *Life of
Charlemagne*, the royal annals), shaping a flattering, Rome-centric image. Debates: how
"real" or merely symbolic the 800 coronation was; the depth and reach of Carolingian
administration; the economic basis of the realm (Pirenne's thesis tying its rise to the
Arab closure of the Mediterranean); and whether the "renaissance" was genuine revival or
narrow clerical reform.
