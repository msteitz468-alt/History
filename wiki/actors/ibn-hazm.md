## Overview

Abu Muhammad Ali ibn Ahmad ibn Said ibn Hazm (994–1064 CE) was an Andalusi Muslim polymath — jurist, logician, literary author, theologian, and polemicist — active during the final decades of the western Umayyad caliphate and the early taifa period. He is the primary exponent of the Zahiri legal school in the Islamic west and the first major intellectual figure of [[andalusi-philosophy|Andalusi philosophy]]. His career spanned both political ambition and scholarly retreat: he held the office of vizier under Umayyad claimants, was imprisoned during political conflicts, and spent the latter portion of his life in scholarly seclusion after his political projects failed.

Ibn Hazm came to legal study out of embarrassment: having failed to follow correct funeral prayer rituals, he sought a teacher and studied Maliki jurisprudence, then the dominant school of [[fiqh|Islamic law]] in Andalusia. He subsequently moved toward the Shafi'i school before adopting Zahirism. He "tried several times to support claimants to the Umayyad throne. For his trouble he sometimes found himself in prison, while at other points he rose to the level of vizier. Ultimately, though, Ibn Hazm's political ambitions came to nothing, and the latter part of his life was spent in relative seclusion, devoted to scholarship" (Adamson, ch. 23).

## Legal Theory: Zahirism

Ibn Hazm is the major defender of the [[zahirism|Zahiri]] (*zahir* = "evident," "manifest") school of Islamic jurisprudence. The school was founded by a student of al-Shafi'i named Dawud ibn Khalaf (nicknamed "al-Zahiri"), who held that only the *surface* or *manifest* meaning of Quran and sound hadith could serve as the basis of legal rulings.

Ibn Hazm pushed this principle with exceptional rigor:

- **No independent legal reasoning** (*ra'y*): Unlike the Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanafi schools, which permit analogical reasoning (*qiyas*) to extend the law to cases not explicitly covered by the canonical texts, Ibn Hazm rejected even this moderate use of judgment. All legal decision must be grounded in an explicit text.

- **No legal consensus** beyond the unanimous judgments of the immediate Companions of the Prophet.

- **Permissibility by default**: If God wished to prohibit or require something, He would have said so explicitly in revelation. What is not explicitly commanded or forbidden is therefore permitted. Ibn Hazm supported this with Quranic verses stating the book is "complete" or "comprehensive" (6:38, 16:89).

**Liberalizing effects of a stringent literalism.** Ibn Hazm's approach, though extreme in its insistence on textual basis, could produce unexpectedly liberal outcomes because it severely restricted the scope of legally actionable prohibitions. His analysis of homosexuality illustrates this: although disapproving remarks appear in Quran and hadith, explicit capital punishment is not prescribed in the texts, and analogical reasoning (which would have extended the penalty for adultery) is inadmissible. Therefore homosexuality cannot be a capital offense under Zahiri law, though Ibn Hazm held it to be sinful (Adamson, ch. 23).

**Epistemological underpinning.** Ibn Hazm argued that once metaphorical or extended meanings of scriptural language are permitted, the door opens to skepticism. Literal reading is the only guarantor of certainty in legal interpretation. This connects his jurisprudence to a broader Islamic preoccupation with demonstrative certainty also seen in al-Farabi and [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazali]].

## Logic and Philosophy

Ibn Hazm studied logic with teachers trained in the Baghdad Peripatetic tradition — specifically, scholars who had studied with or under Yahya ibn 'Adi (a student of al-Farabi). He composed a work on logic that shows only indirect knowledge of Aristotle's logical writings themselves. This indirect Baghdad-school influence makes Ibn Hazm the first transmitter of eastern Islamic Aristotelianism to Andalusia, anticipating the more thoroughgoing Aristotelianism of [[ibn-bajja|Ibn Bajja]] and [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]].

He wrote a refutation of al-Kindi's *On First Philosophy*, objecting that calling God a "cause" implied that God necessarily creates the universe — an unacceptable limitation on divine freedom.

## Literary and Polemical Works

**Ring-Collar of the Dove** (*Tawq al-hamama*): Ibn Hazm's most celebrated literary work, a sophisticated meditation on the nature and experience of love, drawing on personal experience. It is a tour de force of Andalusi literary culture, in which many intellectuals were also poets.

**Polemic on book burning**: When a political enemy had his books destroyed, Ibn Hazm wrote verses arguing that though paper burns, ideas persist in the soul.

**On the Sects of Religion** (*al-Fisal fi al-milal*): A comparative heresiography discussing the errors of Jews, Christians, Muslim theological sects, and others. Ibn Hazm was unusually severe in his view of the inauthenticity of Jewish and Christian scriptures, arguing they had been corrupted. He engaged in debate with Jewish thinkers in Andalusia.

## Significance and Legacy

Ibn Hazm exemplifies several features that will recur through the Andalusi tradition:

1. **Fusion of legal and philosophical inquiry**: His legal epistemology anticipates the concerns of Averroes, who likewise combines the roles of jurist and philosopher.

2. **Baghdad school transmission**: He is an early vector for Farabian ideas in the west.

3. **Jewish-Muslim intellectual rivalry**: His contentious engagement with Jewish thinkers in a context where Jews held significant social standing is a harbinger of the complex interreligious intellectual ecology of al-Andalus.

4. **Literary culture**: His *Ring-Collar of the Dove* is a model of the Andalusi fusion of philosophy with literary aesthetics.

The Zahiri school did not survive as a major legal tradition after Ibn Hazm. However, his precise textual method and his insistence on epistemological standards left a mark on later thinkers engaged with the problem of knowledge and legal certainty.

## Historiography

Ibn Hazm is well-attested in Arabic sources and modern scholarship on Islamic law and Andalusi culture. His *Ring-Collar of the Dove* is available in English translation and widely studied in literary history. His legal writings are less well known outside specialist Islamic jurisprudence scholarship. Adamson's treatment presents him as a philosophically significant figure — not merely a legal curiosity — whose epistemological commitments link him to broader Islamic debates about certainty.
