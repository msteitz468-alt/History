# Mikhail Gorbachev
## Biography / Description
Mikhail Sergeevich Gorbachev was born in 1931 in Privolnoe, a small village of Stavropol region, to a peasant family of mixed Russian and Ukrainian background. Relatives were persecuted during collectivization. He worked in the fields during and after the war; in 1949 received the Order of the Red Banner of Labour for industriousness. He graduated from Moscow State University Faculty of Jurisprudence in 1955 with first-class marks (silver medal at school). Returning to Stavropol he joined Komsomol and party apparatus. By 1966 he headed the City Party Committee; in 1970 the entire Stavropol Region (under 40). In 1971 Central Committee membership; 1978 head of Agricultural Department in Secretariat; 1979 candidate, 1980 full Politburo member. He proposed a State Agro-Industrial Committee in Brezhnev's time but it was rejected. Andropov promoted him; he and Ryzhkov elaborated economic measures for Andropov. On 11 March 1985, after Chernenko's death, the Politburo unanimously chose him as General Secretary (lines 3075–3089, 3069–3071).

Gorbachev was a brilliant dissimulator who had attended Brezhnev's court without seeming an unsettling reformer. In a December 1984 speech he used words soon associated with radicalism: "acceleration", "the human factor", "stagnation", "glasnost", "democratization". By temperament a gambler, he stated privately the night before his selection: "Life can't be lived like this any longer." He had visited Italy, Belgium, West Germany and France; capitalism was not moribund. He had studied Lenin's last works on bureaucracy and concluded the problems of the 1920s persisted. His wife Raisa (political partner) wrote on rural relationships. He was comfortable with dual Russian/Ukrainian identity but this dulled sensitivity to national tensions (lines 3085–3087, 3200).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Personnel Revolution (1985):** Within months average Politburo age fell from 69 (1980) to 64. Ryzhkov, Ligachëv and Shevardnadze promoted to full Politburo; Romanov sacked; Yeltsin and Zaikov to Secretariat; Gromyko moved to head of state; Tikhonov retired. "Everything's rotten. There must be change" (Shevardnadze to Gorbachev, 1984). Allies shared sense of urgency (lines 3089–3091).
- **Early Economic Measures:** Gosagroprom (State Committee for the Agro-Industrial Complex) formed November 1985. "Acceleration" (uskorenie); anti-alcohol campaign (vineyards hacked down; nicknamed "Mineral Secretary"). Initial focus on machine-building investment for the Twelfth Five-Year Plan (lines 3099–3103, 3097).
- **Glasnost (from mid-1986):** Glavlit rules relaxed June 1986. New radical editors (Yegor Yakovlev at Moscow News, Vitali Korotich at Ogonek). Sakharov lifted from Gorki exile by phone 16 December 1986. History re-examined: Repentance film, Shatrov drama. "Blank spots" to be filled (lines 3160–3170, 3174).
- **Perestroika Economic Reforms:** Law on State Enterprise adopted at June 1987 plenum (effective Jan 1988): factories to decide production after state orders, set wholesale prices, elect directors; self-financing banks; allowance for private sector in services and small industry. "The country was in a 'pre-crisis' condition"; over-centralized planning had to be abandoned (lines 3178–3180).
- **Political Restructuring (1987–1988):** January 1987 plenum called for democratization, multi-candidate elections, non-party members in high office. Yeltsin (Moscow First Secretary) pushed faster, criticized privileges, was forced to resign from candidate Politburo and sacked as Moscow secretary October 1987 after hospital episode. Nineteenth Party Conference June 1988: Congress of People's Deputies (2,250 members), Supreme Soviet as working parliament; party economic departments to be disbanded; local first secretaries to stand for soviet chairmanships. Amendments rammed through despite resistance (lines 3182–3186, 3230–3238).
- **Chernobyl and Its Impact (26 April 1986):** Reactor explosion exposed chain of negligence, incompetence and disorganization. Winds carried radiation; Politburo itself struggled for rapid accurate information. Gorbachev concluded administrative tinkering was insufficient; ventilation of problems was crucial for survival as superpower. "The lethal atmosphere over Chernobyl was a metaphor for the conditions in Soviet public life" (lines 3129–3135).
- **Foreign Policy "New Thinking":** Non-interference announced to Warsaw Pact leaders at Chernenko's funeral (1985). Reykjavik 1986 (near-agreement on eliminating nuclear weapons in 10 years); INF Treaty December 1987. Afghanistan withdrawal announced April 1988. UN speech 7 December 1988: "common human values" over class struggle; unilateral Soviet Army cut by a tenth and six divisions recalled from Eastern Europe (lines 3113–3115, 3250–3256).

- **Perestroika (Restructuring):** Gorbachev launched an ambitious program to modernize the Soviet economy, initially focusing on a "speed-up" (uskorenie) and labor discipline (such as the 1985 anti-alcohol campaign) before moving toward marketization, enterprise autonomy, and limited private business. By 1989–90 co-operatives flourished in services but aggravated shortages and required bribes ("Illegality had to be accepted as companion to the re-emergence of private economic activity", lines 3301–3303). Miner strikes in 1989 were settled without repression (lines 3307–3308).
- **Glasnost (Openness):** To overcome bureaucratic inertia, Gorbachev permitted unprecedented freedom of speech and press. This led to a re-examination of Soviet history (including the crimes of Stalinism) and allowed citizens to openly criticize the government. By 1989 live TV of Congress debates stopped work nationwide (line 3317); previously banned works like Solzhenitsyn's *Gulag Archipelago* and Grossman's *Forever Flowing* appeared, attacking Lenin and Stalin (lines 3343–3345).
- **Democratic Reforms:** In 1989, Gorbachev established the Congress of People's Deputies, introducing competitive elections. In March 1990, he oversaw the abolition of Article 6 of the Soviet Constitution, ending the Communist Party's monopoly on political power, and was elected President of the USSR. At the 28th Party Congress (June 1990) he secured ratification of a de-Leninized platform ("Towards a Humane, Democratic Socialism", lines 3404–3408).
- **Non-intervention in Eastern Europe (1989):** Gorbachev decided against using military force to defend communist regimes in the Warsaw Pact, allowing the peaceful collapse of communism in Poland, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Hungary, Romania, and Bulgaria, and accepting German unification. "The dominoes were tumbling fast"; Romania's Ceaușescu was executed in December; "great was the work of his hands" (lines 3369–3377).
- **Ending the Cold War:** Gorbachev established a close working relationship with US President Ronald Reagan and signed historic treaties to reduce nuclear arms. He also ordered the withdrawal of Soviet troops from Afghanistan in 1988–1989.
- **Union Treaty and the Coup (1991):** To prevent the disintegration of the USSR from rising nationalisms, Gorbachev negotiated a new Union Treaty scheduled for 20 August 1991. This prompted a hardline communist coup. On 18 August plotters (Shenin, Baklanov, Varennikov, Boldin) visited Foros demanding he cede power to Yanaev; Gorbachev refused ("swore at them lustily", line 3466). The State Committee for the Emergency Situation (Yanaev, Pavlov, Kryuchkov, Yazov, Baklanov, Pugo, Starodubtsev, Tizyakov) declared emergency 19 August. Yeltsin rallied resistance from the White House; three civilians killed 20–21 August (Komar, Krichevski, Usov). Coup collapsed by 21 August; plotters arrested (lines 3470–3484). "A delighted Yeltsin travelled around the RSFSR urging the autonomous republics to 'take whatever helping of power...'" (line 3446). Gorbachev resigned 25 December 1991 after Belovezha Accords (line 3514). 

Gorbachëv's "intuitive brilliance did him little good"; he "remained hampered by his background from foreseeing where his path of transformation was leading" and "induced chaos in governance" while "always more slowly than the pace of the deepening crisis" (lines 3396–3397). "I'm doomed to go forward, and only forward..." (line 3398).

## Historical Significance
Gorbachev was the solitary fore-rider of reform who set out to renovate the Soviet compound but instead accelerated its dissolution. He hated obstacles from upholders of the Brezhnev period and was in his element in debate. Initially he could count only on Shevardnadze as unconditional ally. He remained a Marxist-Leninist believer who assumed the renovated Soviet order would demonstrate superiority and that Eastern European communists would voluntarily follow. He was a disorganized thinker with rudimentary economic understanding and superficial grasp of the national question (comfortable with his own dual identity, he spoke of Russia and the USSR as coextensive in Ukraine, outraging sensitivities). By battering the system he hoped to secure popular approval while preserving the one-party state and hallowed Lenin/October symbols. Instead glasnost and perestroika reinforced localism, nationalism, corruption, illegal profiteering and distrust of authority — the very phenomena he sought to eliminate. He was Russia's "holy fool" (lines 3137–3141, 3198–3200, 3260).

Counterfactual: If Gorbachev had not been General Secretary the decisions of summer 1986 would have been different. The long-lasting Soviet order would have endured for many more years, and the eventual collapse would almost certainly have been much bloodier. His attempt to prevent general crisis proved instrumental in bringing it forward and destroying the USSR.

## Historiography
Service stresses that Gorbachev had no grand plan; external pressures (Reagan's SDI), unpredictable events (Chernobyl) and his own personality were decisive. He began as buoyant optimist confident Soviet science could match the USA. The 19th Conference marked the shift of power base from party to state. Nationalism had been "the hatred that dared not speak its name"; glasnost and anti-corruption campaigns in the republics while granting press and assembly freedom were explosive. Gorbachev overestimated the attractiveness of his ideas and underestimated inter-ethnic enmities frozen by the dictatorship (lines 3139, 3202, 3260).

## Counterfactual Significance
A more cautious successor to Chernenko might have continued Andropov-style discipline without opening the floodgates of glasnost or political restructuring. The compound might have muddled through for another decade. Gorbachev's wager on the intelligentsia and society, combined with his personal dominance in initiating change, made the 1985–1991 transformation possible — and made the peaceful end of the Cold War and Eastern European communism possible as well. Without him the system would not have reformed itself out of existence so rapidly.
