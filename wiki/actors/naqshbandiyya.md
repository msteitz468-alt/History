## Overview

The Naqshbandiyya is the most widely spread and politically consequential of the major Sufi orders, characterized by its sobriety, its insistence on strict adherence to the *sharīʿa*, and its preference for **silent (*khafī*) dhikr** over audible or ecstatic performance. Where other orders made music, *samāʿ*, and whirling dance central spiritual tools, the Naqshbandiyya largely rejected these as innovations. In some respects it is closer in spirit to the Shādhiliyya (see [[shadhiliyya]]) than to the eastern orders with which it shares geography.

The order traces its lineage through the "Khwājagān" (Masters) of Central Asia, attributed ultimately to the first Caliph Abū Bakr rather than through ʿAlī — a distinction marking it off from most other Sufi orders. It is named for **Bahāʾuddīn Naqshband** (d. 1389) of Bukhara.

## Key Features from Chapters 4–5

**Silent (heart) dhikr**: A hallmark of the order. Schimmel notes: "The Naqshbandī tradition has taught the dhikr of the five *laṭāʾif*, five subtle points in the body" as nodes for directed silent recollection. The goal is **khalwat dar anjuman** ("solitude in the crowd"): "complete absorption in proximity to God in the midst of a crowd" — an ideal already approximated by Abū Saʿīd ibn Abīʾl-Khayr's perfect saint who "goes in and out amongst the people... and never forgets God for a single moment."

**Tawajjuh (concentration on the sheikh)**: "Later orders, mainly the Naqshbandiyya, considered necessary for the successful performance of the dhikr" the technique of *tawajjuh* — "to establish a tie" (*rabita kurmak* in Turkish) between master and disciple. The sheikh "enters the door of the disciple's heart" spiritually to guard him at every moment.

**Sobriety and samāʿ**: The Naqshbandiyya rejected *samāʿ* (spiritual music and dance). "Even a sober mystic of the Naqshbandiyya in Delhi like Mīr Dard felt compelled to write a book in defense of music and used to gather musicians once or twice a week to play melodies for him and his followers — without the dancing movement, however, and in strict discipline."

**Indo-Persian poetry**: Around 1700, "most of the leading poets were under the influence of the Naqshbandiyya *ṭarīqa*" — the word *shikast* ("broken") became a favored poetic term, reflecting the period's sense of Muslim India breaking down.

**Sirhindī and qayyūmiyya**: Aḥmad Sirhindī (d. 1624), the great Naqshbandī reformer, claimed for himself and three of his descendants the rank of **qayyūm** (upholder of the universe), superior even to the *quṭb*. He built his program for Islamic renewal around speculations on the two *m*'s of Muhammad's name and critique of Ibn ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd*.

**Tomb visitation**: Despite sobriety, "Naqshbandī masters considered it useful to visit the tombs of deceased saints" — *ziyāra* as a form of spiritual concentration rather than folk devotion.

**Political activity**: The order had "grave political consequences in Central Asia in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries." It became the primary vehicle for Islamic resistance in Central Asia under Russian colonialism and for reform movements in India.

## Geographic Expansion (Formichi 2020)

Formichi (2020) frames the Naqshbandiyya as a central through-line in her thesis that Asia — not just the Arab heartland — shaped global Islam. Nasir al-Din 'Ubaydullah Ahrar of Tashkent (1404–1490) drove the order's first major expansion beyond Transoxiana, sending deputies outward; it subsequently took root among the elite of [[timur|Timur's]] [[timurid-empire]]. In India, **Baqi Billah** (1564–1603) established the first Naqshbandi khanaqa in Delhi under Mughal patronage, the base from which Sirhindi later launched his *mujaddidi* reform. Eastward, the order reached Qing China and Tibet via **Khoja Afaq** of Kashgar (d. 1694), then took root in Gansu through **Ma Laichi** and **Ma Mingxin**, who founded rival Naqshbandi sub-orders (*menhuan*) after studying in Mecca, Yemen, and Central Asia — Ma Laichi's hereditary-succession model reportedly mirrored Chinese lineage structures (see [[islam-in-china]]). By the eighteenth century the order had reached, in Formichi's account, Central Asia, South Asia, China, Arabia, and the Ottoman lands as a single interconnected network.

## Relationship to Other Orders

The Naqshbandiyya is structurally most similar to the Shādhiliyya in its rejection of ecstatic practices and emphasis on sobriety — a parallel Schimmel explicitly notes. It superseded the Kubrāwiyya in India from ca. 1600, and became the dominant order in Ottoman Turkey's educated and court circles. ʿAlāʾuddaula Simnānī (d. 1336), who critiqued Ibn ʿArabī from a Kubrāwī perspective, became "a favorite saint of the later Naqshbandiyya."
