# Benjamin Franklin

> **Detailed biography:** [[hubs/biographies/age-of-expansion/benjamin-franklin|Benjamin Franklin — Detailed Biography]]

American polymath (1706–1790): printer, scientist, inventor, civic organizer, writer, and diplomat — the oldest of the Founding Fathers and, in Walter Isaacson's reading, the exemplar who helped "invent" the American middle-class character of practicality, self-improvement, tolerance, and civic virtue. Youngest son of a Boston tallow chandler, apprenticed to his printer brother, he ran away to Philadelphia at seventeen (1723), built a full-stack publishing operation (the *Pennsylvania Gazette*, *Poor Richard's Almanack*), and retired from business at forty-two to pursue science and public life.

## Role and Significance

- **Scientist/inventor.** His electrical experiments (1744–1752) — the single-fluid/conservation-of-charge theory, the kite, and the lightning rod (unpatented) — won him the Royal Society's Copley Medal (1753) and European fame. Also the Franklin stove and bifocals. Isaacson's verdict: "no Galileo or Newton," but a fundamental practical experimenter.
- **Civic architect.** Through the [[actors/united-states|American]] associational tradition he pioneered — the Library Company (1731), Union Fire Company (1736), American Philosophical Society (1743), the academy that became the University of Pennsylvania, and the Pennsylvania Hospital — he built the voluntary civic culture Tocqueville later called distinctively American.
- **Revolutionary and diplomat.** After radicalizing during eighteen years as a colonial agent in London (culminating in the 1774 Cockpit humiliation), he served in the Second Continental Congress, made the "self-evident" edit to the [[events/declaration-of-independence-1776|Declaration of Independence]], and secured the 1778 French alliance — "the greatest diplomatic victory the United States has ever achieved" (Morgan). He then helped negotiate the [[events/treaty-of-paris-1783|Treaty of Paris (1783)]].
- **Sage.** At eighty-one he steadied the [[events/constitutional-convention-1787|Constitutional Convention (1787)]], lending prestige to the Great Compromise; his final acts were antislavery, as president of the Pennsylvania abolition society.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Franklin's celebrity and diplomatic craft, the open French alliance of 1778 — and with it Yorktown and independence on the terms won — is far less likely. His scientific fame was itself a diplomatic instrument. At the level of national character, Isaacson argues Franklin's genuine "faith in the wisdom of the common citizen" and his apostleship of tolerance were formative, not incidental — the roots of American pluralism, pragmatism, and meritocratic mobility. His signature edit to the Declaration reoriented its philosophical ground from religious assertion to rational certainty.

## Historiography

The interpretive axis runs from the "bourgeois moralist" reading (Weber's "spirit of capitalism," D. H. Lawrence's attack on his "Babbittry") to the civic-republican, self-inventing reading (Van Doren, Isaacson). Gordon Wood (2004) stresses Franklin's unabashed royalism into the 1760s and his aristocratic streak; Edmund Morgan (2002) supplies the "political blindness" reading of his crusade against the Penn proprietors. His *Autobiography* is the indispensable but consciously shaped foundation, corrected by the Yale *Papers* and hostile contemporaries (John Adams's diary, Hutchinson's diary).
