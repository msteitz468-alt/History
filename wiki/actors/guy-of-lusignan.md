# Guy of Lusignan

**Guy of Lusignan** (French *Guy de Lusignan*), Poitevin noble, king of Jerusalem by marriage
to Sibylla, and first Latin lord of **[[cyprus|Cyprus]]** (1192–94). His disaster at
**[[battle-of-hattin-1187|Hattin (1187)]]** cost Jerusalem; his acquisition of Cyprus from
Richard I (via the Templars) founded the **[[kingdom-of-cyprus|Lusignan kingdom]]**.

## Background and rise

The lords of Lusignan traced association with the Latin East to 1102 (Hugh VI at Ramla).
Guy's father Hugh VIII came to Syria in 1163 and died in Muslim captivity. As Poitevin
nobles the family were from 1154 vassals of the English crown — a connection that may help
explain Richard's later support for Guy — though Aimery (1168) and Geoffrey and Guy (1173)
had all rebelled against Henry II.

In 1180 Aimery persuaded Guy to come to Jerusalem; at Easter Guy married Sibylla and Aimery
became constable. William of Tyre thought Baldwin IV could have found someone of greater
importance, wisdom and wealth: Guy as a younger son lacked western reserves and reputation.
In 1183 he was appointed then dismissed as king's lieutenant; after Baldwin V's death
(1186) Sibylla and Guy were anointed and crowned in a coup against Raymond of Tripoli.

## Hattin, Acre, and the crown dispute

At **Hattin (4 July 1187)** Guy fell captive and the field army was destroyed. Released
summer 1188, he found Conrad of Montferrat controlling Tyre. Denied entry, he opened the
siege of Acre in August 1189. In autumn 1190 Sibylla and her daughters died; opponents
argued Guy's kingship (derived from his wife) had lapsed and elevated Isabella to Conrad.
Guy refused to give way. On 11 May 1191 at Limassol he did homage to Richard and assisted
the Cyprus campaign; after Acre fell (12 July), Philip secured for Conrad Tyre's revenues
and the expectancy of the crown on Guy's death.

## Cyprus

In April 1192, after the Templars returned Cyprus to Richard, Richard sold the island to
Guy for 100,000 bezants (60,000 down — raised by chancellor Peter of Angoulême from
Tripoli merchants in under a month). Guy never paid the remaining 40,000. He brought
disinherited knights, burgesses and clergy from Syria; settlement met no internal
opposition after Richard and the Templars had broken resistance. A thirteenth-century
tradition (Continuation of William of Tyre) has him enfeoffing 300 knights and 200 mounted
sergeants on Saladin's advice to "give it all away" — Edbury discards the Saladin-advice
and artisan-enfeoffment motifs as Ibelin-hostile partisanship but accepts the substance:
Guy recruited widely and the settlement succeeded. Conrad's murder (28 April 1192) and
Isabella's marriage to Henry of Champagne left Guy irreconcilable; he claimed Jerusalem
until death (~end 1194) and soured relations with Richard via a Pisan plot to seize Tyre.
He designated brother Geoffrey as heir; Geoffrey preferred Poitou, and the vassals chose
**[[aimery-of-lusignan|Aimery]]**.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Guy's purchase and settlement, Cyprus might have remained a Templar or English
dependency, or slipped back toward Byzantine recovery. The durable Latin regime of nearly
three centuries rests on his recruiting a settler class with a vested interest in the new
order — the lesson of the Templars' under-manned interlude.

## Historiography

Narrative sources are "violently partisan." An English writer called Guy naive in intrigue
but praised his Acre siege; Imad al-Din noted good administration in Cyprus. Edbury's
critical reading of the settlement tradition is the modern baseline
([[edbury-kingdom-cyprus-crusades-1991|Edbury 1991]]).
