# Humiliati

## Overview

The **Humiliati** were a northern-Italian lay and clerical movement — concentrated near **Milan** and **Verona**, mostly of artisan background — who forswore luxuries, refused oaths, and pursued a life of voluntary simplicity. Condemned with other groups at the Council of Verona (1184), they were among the dissident currents that [[innocent-iii|Innocent III]] chose to **channel rather than destroy**.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan places the Humiliati beside [[waldensians|Waldensians]] and [[catharism|Cathars]] as part of the wider lay hunger for gospel poverty around Innocent’s accession. In **1199** they approached the newly elected pope; after inquiries he sanctioned **three orders** of Humiliati:

1. **Clerics** living in community  
2. **Lay men and women** in separate communities  
3. **Married people and others** remaining in their own homes  

Growth was rapid. By **1216** Jacques de Vitry (among others) reported that “this religion has so grown in the diocese of Milan that they now have **one hundred and fifty houses**.” [[innocent-iii|Innocent III]]’s settlement is a case study in papal discrimination among voluntary-poverty movements: the same pontificate that condemned unlicensed Waldensian preaching and launched the [[albigensian-crusade-1209|Albigensian Crusade]] could institutionalize a parallel artisan piety when it accepted hierarchical oversight.

Later history was less expansive. **Innocent IV (1246)** centralized the order; over time it became a localized Italian body and was **extinguished in the sixteenth century**. Logan treats the Humiliati as a successful short-run experiment in containing dissent by recognition — the institutional cousin of the [[mendicant-orders|mendicants]], but without their pan-European trajectory.
