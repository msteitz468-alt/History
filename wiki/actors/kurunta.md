## Overview

Kurunta was the second son of [[muwatalli-ii]] and king of [[tarhuntassa]], the appanage kingdom in southern Anatolia created for him (or for his predecessor) by Muwattalli when the Hittite capital was moved to Tarhuntassa during the conflict with Egypt. He is one of the most intriguing and elusive figures of the Hittite New Kingdom: simultaneously the most privileged vassal in the Hittite world and — if the seal evidence is read correctly — possibly a usurper who briefly seized the throne at [[hattusha]] itself.

Kurunta's position was from the outset anomalous. His father had been Great King; his half-brother [[urhi-teshub]] had been Great King before being deposed by his uncle Hattusili III; yet Kurunta himself was confined to a subordinate kingdom, however generously endowed. His relationship with his cousin [[tudhaliya-iv]] — cordiality reinforced by reciprocal need — defined the political architecture of Hatti's final decades.

## The Bronze Tablet Treaty

Shortly after [[tudhaliya-iv]] acceded, he concluded a formal treaty with Kurunta recorded on the famous Bronze Tablet, discovered in 1986 beneath the pavement near the Yerkapı Gate at [[hattusha]]. The tablet (which was signed by Masturi of the Seha River Land among the witnesses, dating it to early in Tudhaliya's reign) granted Kurunta concessions going "significantly beyond" those in earlier treaties his father had drawn up with Kurunta:

- Additional territories not included in previous agreements
- Freedom to choose his own successor in [[tarhuntassa]]
- Further reduction of taxes and corvées imposed on his kingdom
- Formal acknowledgement of his status as "equivalent to the Syrian viceroys and second only to the Great King in Hattusa"

Egyptian medical expertise was even dispatched to treat Kurunta when he fell ill, with the pharaoh sending the scribe and doctor Pariamahu along with medicines: "place Kurunta, the king of the Land of Tarhuntassa, in his charge so that he may prepare medicines for him" (KUB iii 67, obv. 12'–rev. 12). Bryce notes dryly that "Tudhaliya may subsequently have had cause to regret the skills of the Egyptian doctors."

## Possible Usurpation

The burning question of Kurunta's story is whether he eventually attempted to seize the Hittite throne. The evidence consists of two finds:

1. **Seal impressions at Hattusa** bearing within the royal aedicula the inscription: "Kurunta, Great King, Labarna, My Sun."
2. **The Hatip rock relief** (17 km southwest of Konya): "Kurunta, the Great King, [the Hero], the son of [Mu]wattalli, the Great King, the Hero."

These inscriptions strongly imply that Kurunta at some point was recognized — or recognized himself — as Great King. To achieve this, he would almost certainly have had to seize [[hattusha]] by force from [[tudhaliya-iv]]. Archaeological evidence (Professor Neve) indicates destruction of parts of the city — walls and the temple quarter — during Tudhaliya's reign, possibly caused by armed conflict between the two cousins. If a coup occurred, it appears to have been short-lived: Tudhaliya seems to have regained his throne and then undertaken "an ambitious restoration and rebuilding project in the upper city."

Neve has proposed that after Tudhaliya regained Hattusa, he performed a "de-sacralizing burial" of the Bronze Tablet under newly constructed pavement — intending to conceal forever all trace of his compact with Kurunta. This would explain both the unique find-spot and the absence of any other reference to the treaty's execution.

Alternative interpretations exist. Some scholars argue that Kurunta's "Great King" title was entirely legitimate — conferred by imperial authority, as ruler of [[tarhuntassa]] — and that no violent usurpation occurred.

## Fate and Legacy

"Kurunta's ultimate fate remains a mystery. If he had rebelled against his overlord he may, like his brother Urhi-Teshub, have spent his remaining days in exile." He is almost certainly not Ulmi-Teshub (sometimes identified with him); if a distinct figure, Ulmi-Teshub probably replaced him as ruler of [[tarhuntassa]] following the presumed coup. The kingdom of Tarhuntassa appears to have broken from its Hittite allegiance after Kurunta's removal, becoming openly hostile — a state later reflected in [[suppiluliuma-ii]]'s Südburg campaigns to reconquer it.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Kurunta's initial loyalty — and the elaborate concessions Tudhaliya made to secure it — Hatti's eastern and southern flanks would have been far more exposed earlier. Yet the very generosity of those concessions, by elevating Kurunta's status to near-parity with the Great King, may have encouraged him to press his dynastic claim and thereby accelerate the internal fragmentation that weakened the kingdom in its final decade.

## Historiography

Kurunta was largely invisible to scholarship until two decisive finds: the Bronze Tablet (1986) and the Hatip rock relief (1990s). The tablet transformed understanding of the Tarhuntassa appanage and the scale of Hittite dynastic concession-making. The seal impressions and Hatip relief have generated ongoing debate about whether the "Great King" title represents legitimate regional status or evidence of a violent coup. Bryce treats the coup hypothesis as the more likely but admits that "all theories about a violent coup in Hattusa must remain speculative until we have more direct evidence."
