## Overview

The Sultanate of Aceh, at the northwestern tip of Sumatra, was a major Islamic scholarly and commercial hub often styled a "verandah of Mecca" — a first point of contact for Muslim traders from Arabia, Persia, and South Asia and a center of Islamic learning in maritime Southeast Asia. It reached its territorial and commercial peak under Sultan Iskandar Muda and later underwent a distinctive era of female rule sanctioned in explicitly Islamic terms.

## Role and Key Developments

Marco Polo reported that the inhabitants of Ferlec, on Sumatra's coast, "have all been converted to the law of Mahomet" through contact with Saracen merchants — among the earliest Western notices of Islam in the archipelago. By the 14th century, Muslim graveyard headstones at Pasai attest that Islam had become a established "local religion" in the region. Ibn Battuta, visiting Samudera-Pasai in the mid-14th century, described its sultan as "a lover of theologians," engaged in jihad against "infidels," yet humble enough to walk to Friday prayers.

Aceh's golden age came under Sultan Iskandar Muda (r. 1607–1636), during which it was dubbed a "bulwark of Islam" and dominated the regional pepper trade. Its 17th-century Indrapuri mosque, with a square floor plan and multi-tiered thatched roof, exemplified the pre-colonial Southeast Asian mosque form — evoking "more the idea of a pagoda than a Middle Eastern mosque."

From 1641, Aceh entered an unusual era of queenship: Safiatuddin Syah was appointed "Great Sultan and Illustrious King" of Aceh, the first of four successive female rulers. Her legitimacy was harnessed to both Islam and adat (customary law), and the ulama shaykh-ul-Islam Abdu Ra'uf al-Sinkili recognized her explicitly as "deputy of God" (khalifat Allah) — a formulation later analyzed by historian Sher Banu Khan as a distinctive Acehnese "queenship" model, contrasted with the "cruelty of their male predecessors' coercive and absolutist regimes" and credited with sustaining Aceh's independence amid growing European incursion.

After the Aceh War, Dutch colonial authorities built the Baiturrahman Great Mosque (1881) in Kutaraja (Banda Aceh), deliberately breaking from traditional Acehnese architectural style; locals mocked it as looking like "a gourd" and "an exotic abnormality."

## Counterfactual Significance

Aceh's queenship era demonstrates that Islamic legitimation of female sovereignty was possible and stable for over half a century in a major Muslim polity — a case that complicates any assumption that Islamic political theory categorically excluded women from supreme rule.

## Historiography

Formichi (2020) draws on Sher Banu Khan's reframing of Acehnese queenship as a deliberate, Islamically-legitimated governance model rather than an anomaly, situating Aceh within the broader debate over syncretism and orthodoxy in Southeast Asian Islamization (Ricklefs's "mystic synthesis," and the critique that "if everything is syncretistic, nothing is syncretistic").
