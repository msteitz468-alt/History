# Paddy Griffith (1947–2010)

British military historian and analyst specializing in tactical and operational history. Best known in the warfare hub context for *Battle Tactics of the Western Front: The British Army’s Art of Attack, 1916–18* (1994; paperback 1996), a detailed revisionist study arguing that the BEF underwent a profound, army-wide tactical learning process from the Somme onward.

## Background and Approach
Griffith approached the Western Front after prior work on earlier periods (including the American Civil War and Napoleonic warfare, see his *Forward into Battle*). He explicitly set out to counter the dominant popular and some academic historiography that portrayed British generalship and tactics 1914–18 primarily through the lens of incompetence, futility, and horror (the “blighted absolute”). Instead he focused on technical and organizational achievements of the infantry and supporting arms in the “larger second half of the war.”

His sources include official histories, unit records (with recurrent attention to the 2nd Royal Welch Fusiliers and 9th (Scottish) Division), training manuals (notably SS 143), papers of key figures such as Ivor Maxse and George Lindsay, and personal accounts. He is critical of both “university” military history that avoids gritty technical detail and of selective focus on élite or “innovator” formations at the expense of the broader BEF.

## Core Thesis (from *Battle Tactics of the Western Front*)
- Tactical innovation on the Western Front was not the monopoly of German stormtroopers, ANZACs, Canadians, or the Tank Corps.
- By early 1917 the essentials of modern platoon-level all-arms tactics had been worked out and codified for the entire BEF (SS 143 as a “stormtrooper’s handbook” applied army-wide rather than to specialists only).
- Artillery evolved from crude destructive bombardments toward precision neutralising fire, counter-battery work, and deep battle support.
- The British often led or paralleled German developments; the hand-grenade, for example, was analysed and largely de-emphasised as a primary assault weapon in favour of rifle, bayonet, and creeping barrage.
- “Failure” in late 1917 and spring 1918 is better explained by force ratios, exposure, and preparation time than by doctrinal backwardness.
- Training schools, doctrine dissemination (Captain Partridge and others), and bottom-up experience combined with GHQ direction produced a genuine “university of higher tactics.”
