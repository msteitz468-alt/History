# George Watson MacGregor Reid

Scottish-born religious and political radical (origins obscure; first documented in Glasgow docks in 1888; died 1946), founder and chief of the **Universal Bond** (Universal Bond of the Sons of Men), the first distinctively twentieth-century Druid order. A former sailor, health reformer, socialist agitator, and self-styled "messenger from Tibet" (under the name Ayu Subhadra), Reid brought robed, turbaned Druids to [[places/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] from Midsummer 1912, repeatedly clashing with the landowner, the Office of Works, and professional archaeologists over the right to perform ceremonies at the monument.

In Hutton's analysis Reid is the third of three pivotal catalysts of the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]] (with [[actors/stukeley-william|Stukeley]] and [[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]]): he made the name "Druid" into a vehicle and metaphor for English cultural radicalism, founding a tradition that — by both example and personal succession — continues to the present. He also fabricated an elaborate mythical history for his order. Hutton notes his obscured biography: Reid told several stories of his early life, all false where they can be checked.

## Career and the Universal Bond

Reid first surfaces in the historical record in 1888 at the Glasgow docks, and by 1889 was a "fiery orator" organising seamen's strikes on the Clyde before moving to New York where he tried to unite dock workers in an international union. After political and labour failures (he stood for Congress in 1892 for the short-lived Populist Party and polled 1 per cent), he disappeared from the record until September 1906 — now a middle-class man of comfortable means, married, and living in Burgess Hill, Sussex. He had meanwhile taken the additional name "MacGregor", directly echoing that of Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers, the most powerful figure in the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, whom Reid acknowledged as "our teacher and friend of many dream studies." Reid himself was initiated into the Golden Dawn, writing poetry in a book of Aleister Crowley's as "Frater A. I. of the Golden Dawn Temple Isis-Urania."

From 1906 Reid poured his new fortune into a **British Nature Cure Association** promoting vegetarianism, anti-vaccination, natural medicine and socialist politics, through a journal (*Nature Cure*) that quickly became a vehicle for his universalist theology: "God is too big and too good to belong to any church." He passed through infatuations with Indian mysticism, the Kosmon Church, Buddhism (under Allan Bennett, a fellow Golden Dawn alumnus) and mystical Islam (through Charles Rosher, a champion of the Senussi Order). In 1910 he published *The Path That Is Light* as "the Tathagata Ayu Subhadra", a fictitious Tibetan monk-sage he had himself invented, serving as the sacred text of the "Universalist Churches and Temples."

On **Midsummer's Eve, 23 June 1912**, Reid appeared at [[places/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] with four companions, dressed in white robes and tall white turbans and introducing himself as "Ayu Subhadra, the messenger from Tibet." They called themselves the **Universal Bond of the Sons of Men** and had invited press photographers; the event was reported in both local and national newspapers. They were not yet presenting as Druids — their liturgy was "a remarkable mixture of Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Buddhism and Zoroastrianism," and journalists called them "sun-worshippers."

The term "Druid" attached progressively through the Stonehenge confrontations. When the landowner Sir Edmund Antrobus barred them from the monument in 1913, Reid paid his way in alone and chanted his liturgy; in 1914 he led ten men and two women inside until ejected by police, then harangued thousands outside. By 1915 he was publicly styling himself "the chief of all the Druids of England." In 1916 the new owner Cecil Chubb let them hold four ceremonies, and Reid's evening sermon that year defined Druidry as "the one root of religious life," calling men back to nature and thence to "Nature's God" and "the Brotherhood of Man." In 1918 the Universal Bond formally renamed itself **An Druidh Uileach Braithreachas** — Gaelic for "the Universal Druid Brotherhood" — and its holy places shifted from Tibet and Mecca to Stonehenge (renamed "Cathair Ghall"), Iona and Glastonbury. Its members shed their Eastern robes for white robes with crimson hoods, modelled on those of the Ancient Order of Druids (the fraternal friendly society founded 1781).

## The Office of Works Conflict, 1919–1932

When Cecil Chubb donated Stonehenge to the nation in 1918, Reid expected greater security. Instead the Office of Works treated his Druids as "curious persons" and friction was immediate. The Bond demanded ever-expanding ceremony schedules (four days in 1919, six in 1920, eight in 1921), free entry as a special priesthood, and the replacement of the official guidebook with one written by itself. In retaliation for a soldier's burlesque of a 1922 ceremony — "a group of young officers from a nearby army base staged a burlesque … wearing white sheets and false beards" — Reid severed relations for two years.

Reid simultaneously fabricated a mythical history on a spectacular scale. He claimed the Bond's records went back to 1643 (the date carved on an antique table in his Clapham temple), that it had held Stonehenge services since Tudor times, that William Stukeley had been its chief in 1720, and that [[actors/aubrey-john|John Aubrey]], [[actors/toland-john|John Toland]], [[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]], Charles Dickens, Francis Bacon and the Duke of Kent were former members. He warned the Office that his curse on Antrobus had been followed by the latter's death.

The 1924 crisis over the interment of cremated ashes — agreed by the sympathetic Labour First Commissioner Frederick Jowett — provoked a national protest campaign. Archaeologists, Oxford dons and the Wiltshire Archaeological Society bombarded Parliament and newspapers with objections; the Universal Bond claimed 68,000 world members and declared archaeologists inferior authorities. Jowett capitulated and withdrew permission. "The one and only appearance of the order in the national consciousness had proved to be a disaster." The Bond was left with only the right to hold four midsummer services at half-price entry.

From 1928 to 1932, under an amiable new custodian and with Robert Reid (the founder's son) handling negotiations, the ceremonies ran smoothly: just over fifty members each year, drawing crowds of one to five hundred. The ceremonies blended Christian hymns ("Lead Kindly Light"), the "Rite of the Golden Dawn," bread-and-wine communion from a silver grail at the Altar Stone, burning of oak leaves on a charcoal brazier, chanting of five gong notes (for the five trilithons), and Reid's apocalyptic evening orations to up to three thousand people at the "Double Circle" on Lady Glenconner's land to the south.

The final rupture came when the Office banned sale of the Bond's annual magazine at the stones — it could only sell the guidebook by Frank Stevens, which denied any Druid connection with the monument. Reid withdrew from Stonehenge in 1932, never returned, and gradually shed all Druidic identity. By the mid-1930s his Clapham temple was the "South London Universalist Church," its liturgy wholly Christian. He disinherited his son Robert in favour of Arthur Peacock, quarrelled with the Labour Party, and died in August 1946; his death certificate listed his occupation as "Minister of Religion."

Hutton's verdict: Reid was "a bully, a braggart and a charlatan, but also a visionary of heroic and generous ideals, capable of inspiring great devotion in others" — and "the most fantastic fabulator" in the history of modern Druidry, whose archive was entirely destroyed by a wartime flying bomb.

## Post-1946 Schism

On Reid's death the Universal Bond split. Arthur Peacock led the Universalist Church strand but gave it up in 1951, becoming the last Universalist minister in Britain. G. W. Smith led the **Ancient Order of Druid Hermetists** (AODH), which had been active since the late 1930s and had quietly held ceremonies at Stonehenge throughout the Second World War. Robert MacGregor-Reid (the son) gathered a rival group at Bristol in December 1946, declared the Leamington Spa lodge the "Mother Lodge," proclaimed a fabricated founding lineage from John Toland in 1717, and named his body "The Druid Order." After Smith's sudden death in 1954, Robert became the sole successor leader and from 1955 held the coveted dawn ceremony at the summer solstice. He died unexpectedly in January 1964; a contested succession between Thomas Maughan and Philip Ross Nichols produced another schism. Maughan retained the Universal Bond name; Nichols founded the **Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids** (1964), which dissolved on his death in 1975 before being revived by Philip Carr-Gomm in 1988. The main Universal Bond continued Stonehenge ceremonies under Maughan until English Heritage banned all access at the solstice in 1985.

## Significance

Reid represents Hutton's third great pivot of the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]] (with [[actors/stukeley-william|Stukeley]] and [[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]]): he made "Druid" a vehicle for English countercultural radicalism and gave modern Druidry its public ceremonial form at Stonehenge. His Universal Bond was also, as Hutton notes, a study in "the invention of tradition": it generated successive layers of fabricated history in response to each political adversity. The 1924 controversy inadvertently gave professional archaeologists a platform to consolidate their authority over prehistoric sites. Stuart Piggott's 1968 book *The Druids* — prompted by the Druids' victory at Stonehenge in 1964 — became the scholarly instrument of dismissal; but by the 1970s some archaeologists were cautiously restoring a Druid connection with megalithic religion that the Universal Bond had never abandoned.

## Historiography

Primary documentation is sparse: the Bond's entire archive was destroyed by the flying bomb of 1944. External sources — newspaper reports, Office of Works memoranda, a few surviving letters, Reid's published pamphlets and the memoir of Arthur Peacock — are the indispensable base. Alan Seaburg and Bruce Aubry provided early reconstructions; Adam Stout's research (cited by Hutton as "the indispensable launch-pad") provides the most comprehensive account. Hutton, in [[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|*Blood and Mistletoe*]] (2009, ch. 11–12), argues that the Universal Bond can be studied entirely through external sources and that doing so reveals as much about British archaeology and officialdom as about the Druids themselves.
