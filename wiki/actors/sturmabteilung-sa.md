# Sturmabteilung (SA)

The Nazi Party's brownshirt paramilitary: the street-fighting force of the
movement's rise and the shock troops of the 1933 seizure of power, running the
first wave of wild concentration camps and anti-Jewish boycotts. Under Ernst
Röhm it swelled to some three million men and pressed for a "second revolution"
and absorption of the army — ambitions ended by the murder of its leadership in
the [[night-of-the-long-knives-1934|Night of the Long Knives]] (June 1934),
after which it survived as a marginalized mass organization, resurfacing as the
main perpetrator crowd of [[kristallnacht-1938|Kristallnacht]].

## Evans (2005)

Enrolled as auxiliary police by Göring in early 1933, the SA ran the wild
terror of the seizure of power — the Köpenick "blood-week" of June 1933 alone
saw 500+ arrests and 91 tortured to death — and the first anti-Jewish boycotts
and street violence (over 40 Jews murdered by end June 1933). Swollen to ~3
million (4.5m with the absorbed Steel Helmets) against a 100,000-man army, it
lost its role once the streets were won: stripped of police status (August
1933), then decapitated in the [[night-of-the-long-knives-1934|purge]].
Membership fell from 2.9 million (August 1934) to 1.2 million (April 1938)
under the colourless Viktor Lutze. Its later function was as the shock crowd
of antisemitic escalation — the 1935 boycott waves whose pressure fed the
[[nuremberg-laws-1935|Nuremberg Laws]], and the synagogue-burning and
house-wrecking squads of [[kristallnacht-1938|Kristallnacht]].

## Evans (2003): The Street War of the Rise

- **Stennes revolt** (April 1931): Walther Stennes, SA leader in the
  north-east, occupied the Berlin party HQ, beat up SS guards, and denounced
  the bosses' extravagance; there is "some indication" (hedged, per Evans)
  that Brüning's government secretly subsidized him to divide the movement.
  Hitler sacked Pfeffer von Salomon, recalled [[rohm-ernst|Röhm]] from
  Bolivia, and imposed a personal oath to Hitler on every SA man.
- **Boxheim documents** (Nov 1931): the Hessian Nazi official Werner Best's
  unauthorized contingency plans for a violent SA putsch (food rationing,
  abolition of money, death penalty for disobedience) — Hitler distanced
  himself; the scandal produced Brüning's uniform ban.
- **Death tolls**: Nazis reported 17 activists killed in 1930, 42 in 1931, 84
  in 1932 (~10,000 wounded that year); Communist counts ran higher; a
  Reichstag estimate put 300 political dead nationally in the year to March
  1931; in Prussia in 1932, 155 died in political clashes, 105 in June–July
  alone. Repeated amnesties (the last, 20 January 1933) put convicted
  paramilitary killers back on the streets.
- **The martyr cult**: after Horst Wessel — an SA storm leader shot on 14
  January 1930 by Ali Höhler, a Red Front-affiliated criminal drawn into a
  rent dispute involving Wessel's landlady — died on 23 February,
  [[goebbels-joseph|Goebbels]] staged the funeral ("Through sacrifice to
  redemption") and made Wessel's song the party anthem, later the de facto
  second national anthem. The SA's own songbook was blunter: "Only when Jews
  bleed, are we liberated" (Song of the Storm Columns).

## Historiography

Longerich's SA study and Höhne on the Röhm affair anchor the literature; the
degree to which the "Röhm putsch" threat was real (it was not) is settled.
