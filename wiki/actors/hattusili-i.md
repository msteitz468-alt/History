# Hattusili I

The first fully historical king of the **[[hittites|Hittite Old Kingdom]]** (r.
~1650 BCE) — the ruler who moved the capital to **[[hattusha|Hattusha]]**, launched
the Hittite drive into **north Syria**, and whose own family rebellions and bitter
**death-bed testament** open the archive's record of Hittite kingship. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. VI, Gurney).

## Names and the move to Hattusha

He bore the dynastic name **Labarna** (so probably "Labarna II") together with the
surname **Hattusili**, "man of Hattusha" — a title he most likely adopted to
commemorate his transfer of the royal seat from **Kushshar** to the long-deserted,
Anitta-cursed site of [[hattusha|Hattusha]]. In his own bilingual annals he calls
himself "Great King... man of Kushshar... brother's son of Tawananna" — a filiation
whose obscurity reflects the **matrilineal Hattian residue** in early Hittite
succession (see [[telipinu-edict-1500bce]]).

## The Syrian wars

His surviving **annals** (Hittite and Akkadian versions, recovered 1957) narrate
six years of campaigns. After local fighting (Zalpa, Sanahuitta) he struck
boldly across the Taurus into the Syrian plain, **destroying [[alalakh|Alalakh]]**
(the end of its level VII, ~1650–1630, an important chronological anchor), then
raiding **Urshu** on the Euphrates. The implication is that the **Cilician Gates
and Cilicia were already in Hittite hands**. His great adversary was the Amorite
"Great Kingdom" of **[[iamkhad|Aleppo (Yamhad)]]**: campaigning against its ally
Hashshum he won a battle at **Mt. Adalur**, crossed the Euphrates on foot (boasting
that only **Sargon of Akkad** had done the like), and "made the sea his frontier."
A later treaty records that "Hattusili filled the days of the kingdom of Aleppo" —
i.e. began to break it — but he met a reverse, perhaps a mortal wound, and **passed
the unfinished war against Aleppo to his successor**.

## The death-bed testament

The single most remarkable Old Hittite text is **Hattusili's testament**, delivered
when he lay sick at Kushshar. His family had betrayed him at every turn — a son
governing Tapassanda, "the daughter" who led a revolt at Hattusha, another son who
rebelled at Zalpa, and finally the **nephew he had adopted as heir**, the young
Labarna, who "showed no tears, no pity... the word of his mother, the serpent, he
laid to heart." Hattusili **disinherited the nephew and adopted his grandson
[[mursili-i|Mursili]]** instead, charging the assembled "fighting men and
dignitaries" to keep his word. The speech — embittered, intimate, and unique in
cuneiform literature (its nearest analogue the Egyptian *Instructions*) — both
attests the founding of Hittite cuneiform writing in his reign and lays bare the
**chronic succession crisis** that the [[telipinu-edict-1500bce|Telipinu edict]]
would later try to resolve.

## Significance

Hattusili I is the **architect of Hittite power**: he gave the kingdom its capital,
its Syrian ambitions, and its first written literature, and his designation of
Mursili set up the [[fall-of-babylon-1595bce|sack of Babylon]]. His reign also
exposes the structural flaw that nearly destroyed the kingdom — a militarized royal
**aristocracy whose feuds the monarchy could not yet master**.
