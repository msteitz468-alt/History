## Overview

Sukarno (1901–1970) was the founding president of the Republic of Indonesia, proclaimed on 17 August 1945. He remains one of the defining figures of Asian decolonization — the pre-eminent orator, symbol, and mass mobilizer of Indonesian independence. His political career falls into three distinct phases: prewar nationalist agitator, wartime collaborator-leader under Japanese occupation, and postwar president who evolved from constitutional head of state to architect of "Guided Democracy" before his effective deposition by General Suharto in 1966–67.

## Role and Major Decisions

**Prewar nationalism**: Sukarno rose to prominence in the late 1920s as a nationalist leader and orator with unmatched capacity for mass mobilization. He was repeatedly imprisoned by the Dutch colonial authorities (1929, 1933), which only enhanced his nationalist credentials. His political skill lay in synthesizing the three main currents of Indonesian political life — nationalism, Islam, and Marxism — into a populist anti-colonial coalition.

**Wartime collaboration**: During the Japanese occupation (1942–45), Sukarno worked within Japanese structures, delivering speeches on behalf of the occupation and mobilizing Indonesian labor for Japanese projects. The *Cambridge History of Southeast Asia* (Tarling, ed., Vol. 2) notes that "in the immediate postwar world, it was not practical for Sukarno — tainted by collaboration with the Japanese — to deal with foreign powers." This meant that in the early years of the revolution, the diplomatic work of engaging the Dutch and British fell to Prime Minister Sjahrir (noted for his non-collaborationism) and Vice-President Mohammad Hatta, while Sukarno concentrated on propaganda, mobilization, and maintaining mass revolutionary energy.

The Sukarno-Hatta partnership was itself a political structure that bridged Indonesian social divisions: "Broadly speaking, Sukarno represented the Javanese syncretistic religious strain; Hatta was Sumatran and more emphatically a Muslim. Sukarno was skilled at oratory, agitation and mass politics, whereas Hatta, an economist by training, was interested in restoring economic and administrative order to the chaos created by revolution."

**The revolution (1945–49)**: Sukarno declared Indonesian independence on 17 August 1945. During the revolutionary struggle against the Dutch — who launched two "Police Actions" (1947, 1948) — Sukarno was arrested in the second (1948). The Dutch action proved counterproductive: international condemnation and American pressure (including threats to withhold Marshall Plan funds) forced the Netherlands to the negotiating table. Indonesian sovereignty was recognized at the Round Table Conference (December 1949).

**Parliamentary democracy (1950–57)**: The early independence period saw Sukarno as constitutional president within a parliamentary system. This period was marked by cabinet instability (the *Cambridge History* notes the chronic succession of short-lived coalition governments), regional revolts, and the failure of the 1955 constitutional assembly to agree on a basis for the Indonesian state. Sukarno grew increasingly impatient with what he called the "50 per cent plus one" democracy.

**Guided Democracy (1959–65)**: In 1956, Sukarno proposed a new political framework he called "Guided Democracy" (*Demokrasi Terpimpin*), which was formally proclaimed in 1959. He dismissed the Constituent Assembly and dissolved the elected parliament in 1960, banning parties including the Masjumi and the Socialist Party (PSI). In a 1959 speech reproduced in the *Cambridge History*, he explained his diagnosis: "The spirit of the Revolution has been almost extinguished, has already become cold and without fire... each and every party lays down its own principles, so that there are those who have departed from even the principles of the Pantja Sila."

Guided Democracy balanced the army (which received civilian administrative roles and economic positions under martial law) against the Indonesian Communist Party (PKI), which provided mass mobilization in support of Sukarno's campaigns — recovery of West Irian, confrontation against Malaysia, agitation against the West — in exchange for protection from military persecution. This balancing act became increasingly unstable as the PKI's mass land reform program (*aksi sepihak*) alienated Muslim landowners and polarized rural Java.

**Fall (1965–67)**: The abortive coup of 1 October 1965 — in which six generals were murdered — was blamed on the PKI by the military, though Sukarno's role and the coup's full origin remain debated. General Suharto, commanding the army's strategic reserve, moved against the PKI (which Sukarno refused to ban) and against Sukarno himself. After an extended power struggle in which student organizations played a significant role, Sukarno was effectively deposed on 11 March 1966. He was named acting president in absentia by Suharto and died under house arrest in 1970.

## Counterfactual Significance

The Sukarno-Hatta partnership during the revolution was, according to the *Cambridge History*, more than a personal relationship: it was "a significant political structure that fashioned consensus during the revolution" in a society that "was sorely lacking in unity because it was a matrix of communal, ethnic, religious and cultural segmentation." Without this bridging function, it is plausible that the Indonesian revolution would have been more fragmented — more regionally varied in outcome — and potentially more easily suppressed by Dutch military operations. The revolution's successful completion "at one go" (contrasted with the protracted Vietnamese struggle) owed much to this structural consensus.

Sukarno's turn to Guided Democracy, by contrast, arguably foreclosed an Indonesian path toward more accountable governance. The conditions he identified — elite division, party fragmentation, constitutional deadlock — were real; but Guided Democracy's resolution (authoritarian presidentialism balanced by army and communist mass party) generated the polarization that produced the 1965 coup and the mass killings of 1965–66.

## Historiography

Sukarno is one of the most-studied figures in Southeast Asian history. Indonesian nationalist historiography has treated him as the *Bapak Bangsa* ("Father of the Nation"). Critical scholarship has focused on his wartime collaboration, the authoritarian evolution of Guided Democracy, and the role of his political balancing in enabling PKI expansion that ultimately provoked military backlash. The debates about the 1965 coup — Sukarno's complicity, the army's orchestration, the scale of subsequent killings — remain active. The *Cambridge History* (1992) treats Sukarno primarily as a creator of political structures rather than as an ideological figure, analyzing Guided Democracy as a form of "maximum government."
