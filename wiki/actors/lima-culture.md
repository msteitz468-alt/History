## Overview

The Lima culture (also called Maranga, after its principal surviving monument complex) was the dominant ERD polity on the **central coast of Peru**, centred in the Rímac and Lurín valleys near the site of the later colonial capital of Lima. It flourished broadly within the Early Intermediate Period (c. 400 BCE–CE 550) and overlapped in its final phase with the Wari Middle Horizon intrusion. The Lima tradition is also characterised by "interlocking" ceramic designs, a diagnostic central-coast style.

Shimada's chapter in [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]] is explicit that Lima is among the least understood ERD cultures: "There is no good current synthesis of the Lima culture on the central coast." Most data come from a handful of excavated sites, dominated by the Rímac Valley, where the Maranga site cluster and the oracle centre of Pachacamac (later associated with a powerful pan-coastal cult) provide the best evidence.

## Monumental Architecture

The most concrete evidence for Lima sociopolitical complexity is architectural. The Lima (Maranga) culture "erected adobe-and-fill platform mounds that were comparable in size" to those of the [[moche]] on the north coast — notably "a mound measuring about 270 × 100 m and 15 m high" within the Maranga Complex. This is identified as the largest such construction on the central coast in this period.

Platform mounds (huacas) built with adobe bricks were the standard form of corporate labour expression across ERD coastal cultures. The structural analogy with [[moche]] mounds is noted, though "little is known of their specific functions and associated settlements."

## Ceramics

Lima ceramics are characterised by "negative (resist) painting" in common with contemporaneous [[recuay]], Gallinazo, and Vicús styles on the north coast and north highlands — a widespread ERD technique in which organic resist paint is applied over a fired slip to produce dark designs on a lighter background. The chapter notes their affiliation within a broader "White-on-Red Horizon" phenomenon that spans multiple north and central coastal cultures around CE 0 and the first century or two CE.

Shimada observes that a key distinction of [[moche]] ceramics from their "Lima and Nasca counterparts" is the large-scale use of two-piece press moulds, a technique Lima does not share: "What sets Mochica ceramics apart from their Lima and Nasca counterparts" in terms of industrial organisation.

Early transitional evidence: "small and limited quantity of copper and gilded copper personal ornaments found in some burials at the necropolis at Tablada de Lurín just south of the city of Lima (dated to c. 300 BCE to CE 100; transition to an early Lima culture) pale in comparison to what we see on the north coast." This confirms that Paracas-Lima contact zone activity was ongoing during the formation of the Lima tradition.

## Relation to Pachacamac

The oracle centre of Pachacamac (Pacha Kamaq) in the Lurín Valley eventually became the most influential pre-Inca religious centre on the Peruvian coast, but its early phases belong to the Lima cultural sphere. Shimada's chapter, combined with the broader source, clarifies "the nature of Lima occupation at Pacha Kamaq and its relationship" to the Lima culture. The centre apparently served as a pilgrimage-trade-craft production node comparable to Cahuachi (Nasca) and Pacatnamu (Mochica).

## Sociopolitical Organisation

Shimada does not assign a specific political designation (state vs. chiefdom) to Lima in this chapter; the absence of detailed settlement-pattern or mortuary data makes the question unresolved. The scale of the Maranga mound complex implies significant labour mobilisation and centralised organisation, but whether this reflects a unitary polity or a confederation of valley-level groups is not known.

## Transition and End

The Lima culture's corporate ceramic style was overwritten during the Wari Middle Horizon (c. CE 600 onwards). The chronological table in Shimada's chapter places the Lima sequence (Pachacamac I, II, etc.) as transitioning into Wari-influenced Pachacamac phases and then into the Chancay and later traditions. The persistence of Pachacamac as a sacred centre through the Wari period and into the Late Intermediate Period suggests institutional continuity even after the Lima ceramic style was eclipsed.

## Historiography

The Lima culture is among the most poorly documented of the Peruvian ERD traditions. Shimada writes that "there is no good current synthesis of the Lima culture on the central coast either in Spanish or English," and cites the analysis of the Maranga Complex (Stothert; Shimada's own historical analysis) and Lima occupation at Pachacamac as "critical to current perceptions of the Lima culture." Most publications have limited circulation outside Lima. Residential settlements of the Lima culture, like most ERD cultures, remain "desperately await[ing] archaeological attention."
