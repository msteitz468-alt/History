## Overview

Katsura Tarō (1848–1913) served as Prime Minister of Japan during the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]] (1904–05), presiding over the war's prosecution and the subsequent peace negotiations. A Chōshū army general and protégé of [[yamagata-aritomo|Yamagata Aritomo]], he served three terms as prime minister (1901–06, 1908–11, 1912–13) and alternated in power with Saionji Kinmochi in what became known as the "Katsura-Saionji era" of Meiji-Taisho politics.

## Role During the Russo-Japanese War

As Prime Minister throughout the war, Katsura managed the diplomatic and domestic dimensions of Japan's most consequential modern conflict. He was among the Japanese officials who told British minister Sir Claude MacDonald in December 1904 that "if Japan was successful in war, she would seek for a closer alliance with England" — signaling the intention to renew the [[anglo-japanese-alliance-1902|Anglo-Japanese Alliance]] (Keene, ch. 56).

When Foreign Minister [[komura-jutaro|Komura Jutarō]] telegraphed Tokyo on August 26, 1905 announcing his intention to break off the Portsmouth peace negotiations, Katsura convened the decisive council of cabinet members and genrō ([[yamagata-aritomo|Yamagata]], Itō, and Inoue Kaoru) in the presence of [[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]] on August 28. This council concluded that Japan's financial and military exhaustion made peace essential even without an indemnity or all of Sakhalin, and directed Komura to yield. When the [[hibiya-riots-1905|Hibiya Riots]] broke out on September 5 at news of the treaty terms, Katsura "rushed to the palace to report on the situation" to the emperor personally (Keene, ch. 55).

Katsura resigned in January 1906, succeeded by Saionji Kinmochi. Emperor Meiji "commanded Saionji Kinmochi to form a cabinet after Katsura Tarō resigned" — Keene notes the emperor was probably pleased to have a noble (Saionji) as prime minister for a change, as nobles had recently played a minor role in government (Keene, ch. 57).

## Historical Significance

Katsura's tenure encompassed Japan's transformation into a recognized great power: the Russo-Japanese War, the renewal of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, and the establishment of the Korean Protectorate all occurred under his premiership. He represented the Yamagata faction's vision of a state guided by military and bureaucratic elites rather than party politicians — a governing philosophy that would prove increasingly contested in the Taisho era.
