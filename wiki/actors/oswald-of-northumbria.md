## Overview

Oswald was king of [[bernicia|Bernicia]] and [[deira|Deira]] from 634 or 635 until his death in 642 or 643. Son of [[æthelfrith-of-bernicia|Æthelfrith]] and Acha (sister of [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]]), he ended the destructive interregnum following Eadwine's fall by defeating and killing the British king Cadwallon at the [[battle-of-heavenfield-634|battle of Heavenfield]], reconstituting the northern Anglian overkingship on a Bernician basis. His establishment of the Ionan mission under Bishop Aidan at Lindisfarne redirected the northern church away from the Roman/Kentish model and towards Celtic ecclesiastical tradition, with consequences resolved only at the [[synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]] a generation later. Bede counted him as the fifth overlord south of the Humber. He was slain by [[penda-of-mercia|Penda]] at the battle of Maserfelth, his body dismembered. He was 37 at death (HE II, 9), pointing to birth c. 604/5.

## Exile and Return

On [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]]'s accession, Æthelfrith's sons were driven into exile: "Oswald and Oswiu finding refuge in Scottish Dál Riata (HE III, 3, 25)." Oswald was "brought up as a Christian prince in Celtic lands from the age of 12." He may have fought in Ireland, reportedly in the retinue of an Uí Néill king of Tara. The Dalriadic connection gave him both a Christian formation and military networks he would draw on at the critical moment.

When Eadwine fell at Hatfield Chase (633 or 634) and the subsequent kings Osric of Deira and Eanfrith of Bernicia were both killed by Cadwallon, the sons of Æthelfrith "were nearby to take advantage of the situation." Eanfrith's slaying "may have been an important turning-point": until then the Bernician aethelings may have been in alliance with Cadwallon; but Cadwallon "was prepared to tolerate neither the family of Eadwine nor that of Aethelfrith as rulers of the northern Angles," advancing north toward the Bernicians.

## Battle of Heavenfield and Kingship

Oswald "commended himself and his men to God and his victory over Cadwallon established a new Christian king over the northern Angles." According to Adomnán's Life of Columba, he had a vision of St Columba on the eve of battle. He slew Cadwallon near Hexham in the autumn of 634 (if Eadwine died in 633) or 635 (if Eadwine died in 634) — see [[battle-of-heavenfield-634]]. He "assumed the kingship of both the Deirans and the Bernicians (HE III, 1, 2)."

## Hegemony

Bede regarded Oswald as ruling "within the same bounds as Eadwine," though Kirby is cautious: there is "no evidence that Oswald was ever the presence in north Wales which Eadwine appears to have been." Oswald certainly re-established Eadwine's Humbrian confederacy, asserting overlordship in Lindsey and re-engaging the eastern Angles. He stood sponsor to the West Saxon king Cynegils at his baptism at Dorchester-on-Thames c. 640, jointly granting Dorchester to the missionary bishop Birinus — a clear expression of hegemony in southern England. He married Cyneburh, Cynegils's daughter. The Deiran royal princes were, however, seen as threats by Queen Aethelburh, who sent them to the Frankish king Dagobert I "for fear specifically of Oswald and Eadbald."

Oswald's northward expansion included a probable siege of Edinburgh (638, from Irish annals), interpreted as annexing the Votadini stronghold, and possibly the extension of the Anglian frontier to Stirling (Iudeu/Giudi).

## Ionan Church and Celtic Christianity

As soon as established as king, Oswald "sent a request to Columba's monastic foundation on Iona among the Dalriadic Scots for a bishop, receiving in due course Aidan (HE III, 3)." Aidan's see was placed at Lindisfarne, near the Bernician stronghold of Bamburgh. "There was no attempt to revive the church of Paulinus at York." This brought the northern Anglian church within the familia of Iona, "disseminating throughout northern England the idiosyncratic customs of the clergy of the Celtic regions of the British Isles" — including a different Easter calculation and a different ecclesiastical order — which Rome would eventually contest.

## Death

In 642 or 643, Oswald was killed fighting Penda at the battle of Maserfelth (Maes Cogwy), "traditionally though not certainly Old Oswestry." Possible allies of Penda included Cynddylan ap Cyndrwyn of Powys. His brother Eowa, king of the northern Mercians, also died in the same battle — whether as Oswald's ally or Penda's subject is contested. Penda dismembered Oswald's body. The immediate consequence was that "the northern Humbrian confederacy fragmented" and Oswiu could initially only reign over the Bernicians.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Oswald's victory at Heavenfield, Cadwallon might have destroyed the Bernician dynasty entirely, leaving no obvious successor to reunite the northern Angles. His adoption of the Ionan mission, rather than the Canterbury one, had lasting ecclesiastical consequences: it created the divergence in Easter observance and ecclesiastical custom that polarised northern English Christianity until Whitby.

## Historiography

Kirby is sceptical of Bede's inflated account of Oswald's hegemony. Bede "allowed his awareness of the different native languages of Britain to lead him into defining Oswald's Anglo-British imperium in excessive terms," and there is evidence only for domination of the Picts under Oswiu rather than Oswald. The vision of Columba is attested only in Adomnán's Life — a Iona source with an obvious interest in the story. The date of death (642 vs. 643) depends on whether Eadwine died in 633 or 634, Kirby preferring the later dates throughout.
