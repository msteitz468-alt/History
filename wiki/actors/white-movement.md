# White Movement
## Biography / Description
The **White Movement** (whose forces were known as the White Army or Whites) was a loose political and military coalition of anti-Bolshevik forces that fought against the Red Army in the Russian Civil War (1918–1922). It brought together a wide array of political factions, including monarchists, liberal Constitutional Democrats (Kadets), moderate socialists (Socialist Revolutionaries), and nationalist Cossack hosts. The movement was characterized by its anti-communist stance, its commitment to the principle of "Russia, One and Indivisible," and its reliance on foreign military aid from the Allied powers.

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Military Campaigns (1918–1920):** White forces launched several major offensives against the Bolshevik-controlled core:
  - In the east (Siberia), Admiral Alexander Kolchak established a dictatorship at Omsk and advanced toward the Volga.
  - In the south, the Volunteer Army led by Generals Kornilov, Denikin, and later Wrangel occupied Ukraine and advanced to within 200 miles of Moscow in late 1919.
  - In the northwest, General Nikolai Yudenich advanced from Estonia to the outskirts of Petrograd in October 1919.
- **Evacuation (1920):** Following defeat on all fronts, the main southern White Army and thousands of civilians were evacuated on Allied ships from the Crimea to Constantinople in November 1920.

## Historical Significance
The White Movement was the primary military challenger to Bolshevik rule, but its political fragmentation and strategic weaknesses led to its defeat. Riasanovsky highlights several fatal flaws: their inability to present a cohesive positive program beyond anti-communism; their refusal to compromise on national independence for Baltic states, Ukraine, and Finland (which cost them vital local support); and the peasants' fear that the Whites would return nationalized land to the old gentry landlords (Lines 5809–5815). The defeat of the Whites paved the way for the consolidation of the single-party Soviet state.
