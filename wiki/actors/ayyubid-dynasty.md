# Ayyubid Dynasty

The **Ayyubids** were the dynasty founded by **[[saladin|Salah al-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub]]** after the abolition of the **[[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]]** caliphate in Egypt (1171). Centered in Egypt, they governed — at various points — Syria, northern Mesopotamia, western Arabia, and Yemen until the effective takeover by the **[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]]** around 1250.

## Origins (Cobb)

The dynastic eponym is older than Saladin’s sultanate. After capturing Baalbek (breaking safe-conduct; flaying/crucifying the garrison), **[[imad-al-din-zangi|Zangi]]** appointed the Kurdish commander **Ayyub ibn Shadhi**—future eponym of the Ayyubids—as governor. Ayyub’s son Saladin and brother Shirkuh rose in Zangid service; Saladin’s succession as Fatimid vizier (1169) and abolition of the Fatimid caliphate (1171) made the house Egypt’s ruling dynasty. ([[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]])

## Structure and peak

Saladin’s personal empire was the dynasty’s high point: Egypt + Syria after the defeat of Zangid rivals, victory at **[[battle-of-hattin-1187|Hattin]]** (1187), recovery of **[[fall-of-jerusalem-1187|Jerusalem]]**, and survival of the **[[third-crusade-1189|Third Crusade]]**. On his death (1193) the realm was partitioned among kin (**al-Afdal**, **al-ʿAdil** / “Saphadin,” and others).

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] insists the Ayyubid realm was **not a centralized kingdom** but a collection of Seljuk/Zangid-style **appanages** ruled by branches of the house; one member might achieve preeminence as sultan, and amirs could oust unsuitable princes. Saladin named al-Afdal (Damascus) heir; brother al-ʿAdil mediated and became sultan in Cairo by 1200; succession ran through his son al-Kamil then al-Salih Ayyub.

## After Saladin: diplomacy over reconquest

Post-Saladin Ayyubids faced little serious threat from the local Levantine Franks (contained after Zangi/Saladin reconquests and Third Crusade failure); focus shifted to diplomacy, commerce, and fending off European-launched crusades. Franks increasingly saw Egypt’s conquest as prerequisite for the Holy Land.

**Al-ʿAdil:** appointed sons to major provinces; during drought/famine/earthquakes sent soldiers to work fallow lands; fostered Italian commerce (controversial Genoese friendship raised intelligence fears); truce with Christian Georgia; 1197 Germans captured Sidon and Franks took Beirut, but Muslims recaptured Jaffa and secured a three-year truce with Acre. After Acre raids near Nazareth he arranged a truce granting Lydda, al-Ramla, Nazareth, and Jaffa back to the kingdom of Jerusalem; a 1207 force vs. Tripoli/Hospitallers failed at Crac and Tripoli—truce, prisoner release, annual tribute; fortress built at Mt. Tabor to watch Acre.

**Fifth Crusade paradox:** the Frankish strike at the Ayyubid heart caused normally fractious princes to unite around al-Kamil; Frankish threat silenced (almost) all dissent. Al-Kamil’s generous offer to cede nearly all the former kingdom of Jerusalem (Holy City included) was rejected by Pelagius and the Military Orders; later he negotiated Jerusalem’s temporary return to Frederick II (Treaty of Jaffa, 1229) with Temple Mount remaining Muslim and the city unfortified.

**Al-Salih Ayyub** (sultan by 1240 after fraternal fighting) recruited **Bahri mamluks** on Rawda island in the Nile; challenged by uncle al-Salih Ismaʿil of Damascus, who counterallied with Franks promising “all the lands that Saladin had reconquered,” including Jerusalem. October 1244 battle of al-Harbiyya (La Forbie) near Gaza: al-Salih Ayyub defeated the Franco-Syrian army—for the Franks a military blow second only to Hattin—opening most of Syria including lands recently ceded to Franks.

## Mechanisms of decline

Partible inheritance and amiral autonomy repeatedly fragmented Ayyubid power; the Mamluk regiments that the sultans themselves had built became the agents of dynastic replacement after the murder of Turanshah (1250)—last of the line to rule Egypt—after Louis IX’s Damietta crusade. Cobb: the Mamluk order was a “by-product of inter-Muslim rivalries and Frankish intervention.”

## Historiography

Long overshadowed by Saladin’s biography and by Mamluk institutional history; modern work (Humphreys, Eddé, and survey treatment in [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]]) restores the Ayyubid decades as a distinct phase of post-Fatimid, pre-Mamluk order. Cobb’s thesis on Ayyubid diplomacy: decentralized appanages meant each prince had local interests; Franks participated in inter-Ayyubid rivalries; **holy war is bad for business** (Acre/Tyre/Beirut depots; Damascus/Aleppo hubs); greater jihad and religious foundations (Damascus alone: 63 Ayyubid madrasas—as many as all dynasties a century before and after combined) show commitment to religious struggle not weaker than Saladin’s different policy, only differently expressed.
