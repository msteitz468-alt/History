# Liangzhu Culture

The **Liangzhu culture** of the **lower Yangzi** valley (around Lake Tai) is treated by
[[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Li Liu (2004)]] mainly as a **contrast case** to the
[[yellow-river-valley|Yellow River]] cultures: a jade-rich Neolithic society whose burial
logic differs fundamentally from the kin-solidarity pattern of the north. This page is an
anchor node; the Bibliotheca Alexandrina's coverage of Liangzhu is thin and Liu predates its
fullest publication, so later specialist sources should substantially enrich it.

## Burial Pattern and Jade

Where Yellow River cemeteries keep elite and commoner graves within a shared kin/lineage
space, Liangzhu placed **only elite graves in platform mounds** (e.g. Fanshan, Zhejiang) —
a more thoroughly segregated, individual-oriented elite. Liangzhu is central to the
trans-regional distribution of ritual jades: the **_cong_ tube, _bi_ disk, and _yazhang_
tablet** recur across the Hongshan, Liangzhu, Shandong/[[dawenkou-culture|Dawenkou]],
[[taosi|Taosi]], and Qijia spheres. Liu reads the *cong* and *bi* as cosmological
symbols (heaven and earth) controlled by elites who held ritual/cosmological power, and
their inter-regional spread as evidence of **elite interaction**, not a single political or
artistic horizon.

## Significance

Liu's key methodological point is that Neolithic China had **no unified artistic horizon**
comparable to the Olmec or Chavín of the New World: "cosmological power was not centralized;
instead, there were many regional centers." Liangzhu thus exemplifies the **multi-regional**
character of Chinese Neolithic complexity (see
[[origins-chinese-civilization-monocentric-vs-multiregional]], [[quxi-leixing]]) and an
alternative, jade-and-elite trajectory distinct from the Central Plains'
public-architecture path.

## The Liangzhu Complex and its Collapse (Barnes 2015)

[[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes]] details Liangzhu (from ~3300 BC, out of Songze) as a
"southern powerhouse" stratified into aristocrats and commoners **from its founding**. Its middle phase
(3000–2600 BC) produced an urban-like complex around **Mojiaoshan**, an artificial earthen terrace 10 m
high and 30 ha in area, with the **Fanshan** platform cemetery (11 elite tombs, **1,100 jades**) 150 m
away; jades were standardised into sets of **bi disks, cong tubes and yue axes**, the full set reserved
for the highest rank (and withheld even from elites outside the core — a limit on access). A late-phase
mounded wall enclosed 290 ha plus flood-control earthworks. Barnes reads the **epi-Liangzhu** (2400–2200
BC) spectacular burials as a last florescence before collapse, which she attributes to "loss of
ideological control" over the jade system **plus the 4.2 kya climate event** — flood-sands over the
Maoshan paddy fields are direct evidence. **Source:** [[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015]].
