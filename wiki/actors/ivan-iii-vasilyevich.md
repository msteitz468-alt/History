# Ivan III "the Great" and the Rise of Muscovy

Grand Prince of Moscow (r. 1462–1505), under whom the grand principality of Muscovy "achieved clear
dominance" in north-east Rus′ and laid the institutional and ideological foundations of the Russian
state. Treated by Nancy Shields Kollmann in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 28), who calls the
fifteenth century "one of the most significant, and underappreciated, centuries in Russian history."

During the period between 1462 and 1533, Muscovy underwent substantial growth in land and population, virtually tripling in size (see Map 9.1 from source). (ll. 5519-5521)

## The Gathering of the Lands

Moscow's rise rested less on geography than on the **means — institutional and symbolic — it devised
to consolidate authority and mobilise resources** (means that "endured for at least the next two
centuries"). After the brutal **dynastic war** of his father Vasilii II's reign (Vasilii was blinded
by rivals), Ivan III completed the absorption of the north-eastern principalities:

- **Novgorod.** The great merchant republic, ruled by a "myopic" boyar oligarchy, leaned toward
  Lithuania; after defeat at the **Shelon river (1471)** and a final campaign, Ivan **annexed
  Novgorod outright in 1478**, dismantling its government, deporting hundreds of boyar and merchant
  families, confiscating ~80% of seignorial property, and closing the Hanseatic *Kontor* (1494).
- **Tver** was conquered in **1485**; Viatka (1489), the Perm lands and the Urals followed, securing
  the fur-rich north. The rival **grand duchy of Lithuania** was pushed back as Orthodox Upper Oka
  princes defected to Moscow.

In 1462, Muscovy was one of a number of independent Rus’ principalities and republics. "Riazan’, to the south-east on the other side of the Oka River from Muscovy, had maintained its viability and sovereignty despite being located in the northern reaches of the western steppe and often caught in battles between the Qipchaq (Kipchak) khans and Muscovite grand princes. The grand prince of Tver’, just to the west of Muscovy, was nominally a vassal of the Muscovite grand prince, but he could still manoeuvre relatively independently in diplomatic relations. An alliance of Tver’ with Lithuania against Muscovy was an ongoing possibility... Novgorod further to the west of Tver’ was a prosperous merchant republic that held nominal possession of vast lands to the north and north-east all the way to the White Sea and coast of the Arctic Ocean." (ll. 5620-5646) `preceded_by:` [[kievan-rus]].

The Muscovite state annexed: Iaroslavl’ (1471), Perm’ (1472), Rostov (1473), Tver’ (1485), Viatka (1489), Pskov (1510), Smolensk (1514) and Riazan’ (1521). "But by far its greatest acquisition was through the annexation of Novgorod in 1478." (ll. 5525-5529)

## Domestic Policies and Boyar Duma

"The domestic policies of both Ivan III and Vasilii III focused on reducing the power of their brothers and on maintaining good relations with the boyars and the Church." Treaties with brothers Boris and Andrei the Elder required them to acknowledge Ivan and his son as ‘elder brothers’ and prohibited independent diplomatic or military relations without Ivan’s knowledge. (ll. 5820-5826)

Muscovy’s internal operation "relied on reaching decisions through institutional consultation and consensus-building among the elite and envoys. The ruler was thereby prevented from making agreements with foreign powers without the knowledge and approval of the boyar duma." Formulas such as "the Grand Prince decreed with the boyars..." indicate boyars’ approval was required for major acts (law codes, foreign treaties, precedent-setting measures). (ll. 5695-5708, 5851-5855) `part_of:` [[boyar-duma]].

## Military and Pomest’e

Since the grand prince had no standing army, "his armies had to be gathered anew for each campaign, and demobilised after that campaign was over. The Muscovites of this period seem to have fought using steppe tactics and weapons, which depended on mounted archers with composite bows... by the second half of the fifteenth century, Muscovy was already beginning to take part in the gunpowder revolution of the West. The chronicles describe the Muscovites using arquebuses against the Tatars in 1480. The men shooting these weapons were the forerunners of the strel’tsy (musketeers) of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries." (ll. 5710-5712)

Pomest’e introduced: "the grand prince could choose to which of his servitors he would grant a pomest’e estate... Pomest’e was similar to votchina in most respects. It, like votchina, could be and was inherited... The condition for inheritance was that someone in the family, a son or brother, could continue to provide service to the grand prince... A pomeshchik could not, as a votchinnik could do, sell his estate; nor could he mortgage it... or give it away... Its similarity to iqta landholding among the Muslims has led to the suggestion that Ivan III borrowed the principles..." This "gave the grand prince, if not a standing army, at least a military force available to be called up quickly..." (ll. 5966-5993) `produced:` service gentry underpinning Muscovite expansion; `contributed_to:` [[serfdom-in-russia]].

## Foreign Policy and End of Mongol Yoke

In **1480** the **Great Horde** (remnants of Qipchaq khanate), "Khan Ahmed of the Great Horde advanced with a large force to the south-west of Muscovy. He was apparently hoping for military help from Casimir... That help never arrived." Ivan convened war council with boyar duma, Church prelates (Metropolitan Gerontii, Archbishop Vassian Rylo), mother Mariia. Armies faced across Ugra two weeks; "On 11 November Ahmed retreated and peace was restored." (ll. 6067-6073) `followed_by:` "the last time the Great Horde attacked Muscovy". `produced:` full sovereign independence for Muscovy.

Friendly relations with Crimean khan Mengli Girey under Ivan III ("brother"); deteriorated under Vasilii III with Muhammed Girey. Muscovy’s first direct diplomatic contact with Ottoman Empire 1496. (ll. 6086-6036)

## Ivan III and Vasilii III Personal

"We have little historical evidence concerning the personal characteristics of Ivan III. Perhaps the only contemporary evidence is Ambrogio Contarini’s description of Ivan when he was thirty-seven years old: ‘he is tall, thin, and handsome.’" (ll. 5759) He endured then turned on Novgorod–Moscow heretics, aided Church in 1504 trials.

Vasilii III "strove to expand his own personal power along with that of the state... Within two months of succeeding to the throne in October 1505, he had Kudai Kul, a Kazanian tsarevich... convert to Christianity as Peter Ibraimov... married Vasilii’s sister Evdokhiia. From then until his death in 1523, Kudai Kul/Peter was Vasilii’s closest associate... Only after Kudai Kul/Peter’s death did Vasilii III begin proceedings to divorce his wife Solomoniia because she had not produced an heir. On 28 November 1525, she went to the Pokrov monastery in Suzdal’ and was veiled as a nun. Within two months, Vasilii married Elena Glinskaia, who produced two sons – Ivan in 1530 and Iurii in 1532. Vasilii III died on 21 September 1533, from a boil on his left thigh that had become infected." (ll. 5812-5815)

## Church Affairs

" In Church affairs, this period saw the dominance of councils, beginning with councils in 1447 and, especially, 1448, where the Rus’ bishops chose their own metropolitan." (ll. 5912-5914)

"Both Ivan III and Vasilii III were actively involved with the Church as befitted their positions as head of state. They presided with their respective metropolitans over Church councils." Ivan decided jurisdictional disputes, investigated processions. (ll. 5939-5941)

Nil Sorskii and Iosif Volotskii represented complementary monastic forms with mutual respect; later factions created opposition narrative. Iosif credited with 1504 heretics council. (ll. 5927-5931)

## Historiography

From Ostrowski (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. I): Emphasizes institutional consultation (boyar duma), pomest’e as service land (iqta parallel), non-"manifest destiny" explanation for expansion, steppe military continuity with gradual gunpowder adoption, complex Church-state "symphony" ideal not always attained. Rejects simple autocracy narrative; stresses consensus-building and elite support. Collection (Cambridge vol) provides authoritative multi-author synthesis integrating Russian/Western scholarship on this pivotal growth phase; strong on political economy and Church. Prior sources (e.g. NCMH) complement on ideological "Third Rome" seeds. No major contradiction with existing; adds granular detail on 1478 Novgorod confiscations, 1480 Ugra council, pomest’e prohibitions, trade volumes.

## Significance

Ivan III is the founder of the unified Russian state — the "continuum that extended to the Russian
Empire, the Soviet Union and modern Russia." His gathering of the lands, defeat of the great rival
republics, and construction of an autocratic and imperial-Orthodox ideology set the pattern of
Russian governance for centuries.
