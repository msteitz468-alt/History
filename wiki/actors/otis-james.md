# James Otis Jr.

## Overview

**James Otis Jr.** (1725–1783), brilliant and unstable Boston lawyer, articulated
early constitutional opposition to writs of assistance and parliamentary taxation
and was the formative mentor of the circle around [[adams-samuel|Samuel Adams]].
[[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Stacy Schiff]] (2022) treats him as
pathbreaker and cautionary partner in the 1760s resistance: impulsive where Adams
was even-keeled, a five-hour courtroom orator who later clogged the “political
engine” with hour-long anecdotes, and eventually removed by mental collapse from
the leadership he had opened.

## Role

Constitutional theorist and courtroom star of the early resistance; House member;
public antagonist of [[hutchinson-thomas|Thomas Hutchinson]]. Three years behind
Adams at Harvard; by the 1750s a brilliant lawyer. Adams served as his “lieutenant,
wingman, accomplice, ghostwriter, and editor,” sharing an aversion to Hutchinson.
Together they ran what Schiff calls “what may qualify as the first province-wide
political campaign in American history,” publishing a blacklist of thirty-two
“governor’s tools” (nineteen of whom lost their seats).

## Career

**Writs of assistance (24 February 1761).** Nearly five-hour uninterrupted argument
for sixty-three merchants before Hutchinson, presiding for the first time as chief
justice (an appointment Otis had sought for his father—the origin of the feud).
Otis contended Parliament might regulate trade but not tax it; a man’s house was
his castle; life, liberty, and property were inherent and inalienable. He lost the
case but made an indelible constitutional point. By spring 1763, newly elected to
the House, he was snapping publicly at Hutchinson; a full-page *Evening Post*
parody (28 March 1763) mocked him; Hutchinson complained Otis flattered then
libeled.

**Sugar Act and Stamp years.** In a much-read 1764 pamphlet Otis argued that taking
property without consent stripped liberty: “If a shilling in the pound may be taken
from me against my will, why may not twenty…?”—while still affirming Parliament’s
undoubted power to legislate for America and the colonies’ enrichment of Britain.
On the Sugar Act he noted it “has set people a thinking, in six months, more than
they had done in their whole lives before.” With Adams he drove early House and
town opposition through the [[stamp-act-crisis-1765|Stamp Act crisis]].

**Townshend fracture and decline.** Early nonimportation saw Otis and Cushing break
toward submission while Adams pressed on; Bernard crowed that opposition was
rebuffed. [[adams-john|John Adams]], watching Sunday Gazette work, saw Otis “clog”
the political engine—three-minute anecdotes consuming an hour. Mental instability
worsened; by the early 1770s he was no longer the movement’s center. Schiff’s Adams
inherits Otis’s constitutional vocabulary and merchant networks but not his
volatility.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Otis’s writs argument and 1764 pamphlet, the early Massachusetts language of
rights-against-taxation would have been thinner and later; without the mentorship
structure, Adams’s path from Caucus back rooms to House leadership would have been
slower. His collapse cleared space for Adams’s cooler, more sustained organization.

## Historiography

Schiff (2022) is the present grounding: Otis as Adams’s mentor and foil (impulse vs.
discipline), writs set-piece, blacklist campaign, and decline. Classic treatments
center the 1761 argument as a founding text of American constitutionalism; modern
work also stresses his instability and the historiographical inflation of single
speeches. Collection still lacks a dedicated Otis monograph.
