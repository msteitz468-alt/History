# Friedrich A. Hayek (1899–1992)

Austrian-born economist and political philosopher; the twentieth century's
most consequential intellectual opponent of central planning, and author of
[[hayek-road-to-serfdom-1944|*The Road to Serfdom*]] (1944), the founding
text of the [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]]. Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics,
1974. *(This page covers Hayek as a historical actor; his economics is
treated in the sibling Economics wiki.)*

## Role

A young Viennese economist of the Austrian School (student of Ludwig von
Mises, participant in the post-WWI socialist-calculation debate), Hayek came
to the **London School of Economics** in 1931 on the strength of his
*Prices and Production* lectures, took the Tooke Chair in 1932, and
immediately fell into the famous controversy with **Keynes** over the
*Treatise on Money*. His March 1933 inaugural lecture — delivered the
morning after news of the Reichstag fire — blamed the German Historical
School economists around Bismarck for the intellectual climate that made
utopian planning respectable.

That spring he wrote the memo that became his life's pivot: **"Nazi-Socialism"
(1933)**, addressed to LSE director William Beveridge, rebutting the
prevailing British view that Nazism was capitalism's last-ditch defense
against socialism and asserting instead that it was "a genuine socialist
movement" grown from post-Bismarckian anti-liberalism. The memo grew into
"Freedom and the Economic System" (1938/39), then into the never-finished
"Abuse of Reason" project, and finally — against the wartime planning
consensus crystallized by the [[beveridge-report-1942]] — into *The Road to
Serfdom*.

## Major decisions and consequences

- **Publishing the polemic instead of the treatise** (1940–43): abandoning
  the scholarly Abuse of Reason project for a "tract for the times" made him
  a public figure. The Reader's Digest condensation (April 1945, circulation
  ~8.75 million) turned a scholarly hit into a cultural icon — and, Caldwell
  argues, a symbol read by admirers and enemies who had never opened it.
- **Institution-building**: the 1945 US tour brought contact with funder
  Harold Luhnow, seeding the **Chicago School** nucleus (Director, Friedman,
  Stigler); in 1947 Hayek founded the **Mont Pèlerin Society**; the
  condensation inspired Antony Fisher to found the **Institute of Economic
  Affairs**, prototype of the postwar free-market think tank. Samizdat copies
  circulated behind the Iron Curtain.
- **Answering his critics with system-building**: Keynes's where-do-you-draw-
  the-line objection was met over decades by
  [[hayek-constitution-of-liberty-1960|*The Constitution of Liberty*]] (1960)
  and *Law, Legislation and Liberty* (1973–79).

## The scholarly corpus behind the polemic

The essays collected in [[hayek-individualism-economic-order-1948|
*Individualism and Economic Order*]] (1948) show the scholarly spine of the
public argument: "Economics and Knowledge" (1936) and "The Use of Knowledge
in Society" (1945) formulated the [[knowledge-problem]]; the three
socialist-calculation surveys (1935, 1935, 1940) wrote the Austrian side's
history of the [[socialist-calculation-debate]] against
[[lange-oskar|Lange]] and Dickinson — the 1940 essay already stating "that
socialism is bound to become totalitarian"; the Finlay Lecture (Dublin,
December 1945) constructed the [[true-and-false-individualism]] genealogy;
"'Free' Enterprise and Competitive Order" was his opening policy paper at
the founding Mont Pèlerin meeting (April 1947), distinguishing a reformist
"competitive order" from mere defense of privilege; and the 1939
[[interstate-federalism]] essay carried the anti-planning argument into the
interwar federalist movement.

## The Systematic Statement: *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960)

Written at Chicago, finished on his sixtieth birthday (8 May 1959), published
9 February 1960: the constructive counterpart to the 1944 polemic — what
freedom is (absence of coercion), why civilization depends on it (the
[[knowledge-problem]] generalized: "the case for individual freedom rests
chiefly on the recognition of the inevitable ignorance of all of us"), and
what institutional order preserves it (the [[rule-of-law]], Part II's grand
genealogy from *isonomia* to the *Rechtsstaat*). Part III applies the test to
the [[welfare-state]] sector by sector. Hayek declares himself no ordinary
economist: the pressing social questions demand a synthesis "outside the
scope of technical economics or of any other single discipline," with Hume as
his "constant companion and sage guide." The Postscript, "Why I Am Not a
Conservative," fixed his self-identification as an "unrepentant Old Whig" and
his liberal-conservative-socialist triangle in place of the left-right
spectrum (see [[classical-liberalism]]). Initial reception was thin and cool
(Sidney Hook's hostile NYT review; Viner: "far too doctrinaire"); its stature
grew over decades into the foundational text of post-war classical liberalism.

## The Trilogy: *Law, Legislation and Liberty* (1973–79)

Nearly twenty years in the making and interrupted by "a weakening of my powers, which fortunately proved to be temporary," the trilogy is his self-correction of the 1960 book: not a substitute but a filling of "the gaps which I discovered after I had made an attempt to restate … the traditional doctrines of classical liberalism." Its diagnosis is constitutional — "the first attempt to secure individual liberty by constitutions has evidently failed," because Montesquieu-style separation of powers left all law-making and governing in one omnipotent assembly. Vol. 1 (*Rules and Order*, 1973) lays the foundations: [[spontaneous-order|cosmos vs. taxis]] and [[nomos-and-thesis|nomos vs. thesis]]. Vol. 2 (*The Mirage of Social Justice*, 1976) delivers the attack on [[social-justice]] — he recounts realizing mid-composition "that the Emperor had no clothes on, that is, that the term 'social justice' was entirely empty and meaningless" — and coins [[catallaxy]]. Vol. 3 (*The Political Order of a Free People*, 1979) proposes the two-assembly model constitution and the term [[demarchy]], with the frank self-placement: "if democracy is taken to mean government by the unrestricted will of the majority I am not a democrat." Its Epilogue, "The Three Sources of Human Values" (instinct, evolved tradition, reason), is the bridge to *The Fatal Conceit*: "Man did not adopt new rules of conduct because he was intelligent. He became intelligent by submitting to new rules of conduct." The closing line of the trilogy: "Man is not and never will be the master of his fate: his very reason always progresses by leading him into the unknown."

## Counterfactual significance

Without the Reader's Digest condensation, *The Road to Serfdom* likely
remains an academic succès d'estime and the postwar Anglo-American
free-market counter-establishment loses its founding text and much of its
network (Mont Pèlerin, IEA, Chicago funding all trace through the book's
celebrity). The underlying ideas had other carriers (Mises above all), but no
comparably legible public statement.

## Historiography

The account here derives from Bruce Caldwell's editorial introduction to the
Definitive Edition — sympathetic but source-rich (Press archives, Hayek
papers, the Howson discovery confirming the Beveridge memo). Hostile
contemporary readings ran from Finer's *Road to Reaction* ("the most
sinister offensive against democracy to emerge from a democratic country for
many decades") to the durable "inevitability thesis" misreading Hayek spent
decades protesting (see [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]]). The collection lacks a
full biography (Caldwell's *Hayek's Challenge* is cited but not held) and
anything on Hayek's later Chicago/Freiburg career or the 1974 Nobel.

## Late Capstone: *The Fatal Conceit* (1988)

In his final major work (Collected Works Vol. I, ed. Bartley), Hayek (then ~89) presents the mature evolutionary and demographic restatement of his lifelong critique. The “fatal conceit” is the constructivist-rationalist belief that reason can deliberately redesign morals, law, language, and the economic order to satisfy instinctual demands for visible solidarity and “social justice.” The extended order (several property, trade, abstract rules, prices as discovery) arose through cultural/group selection, not design; it sustains (and was selected for sustaining) populations impossible under tribal morality or central direction. Religion and “symbolic truths” often preserved the counter-instinctual rules long enough for selection to operate. “Socialism… endangers the standard of living and the life itself of a large proportion of our existing population.”

Personal notes (Hayek’s voice): describes his own early adherence to the rationalist outlook common to his generation and the long struggle to escape it; adopts an explicit agnostic stance while conceding that non-factual religious beliefs helped transmit the traditions that enabled civilisation; imposes a self-denying ordinance against the words “society”/“social” (except in quotes) and proposes “catallaxy” (exchange + reconcile enemy into friend) as the better term for the market order. Dated April 1988, Freiburg; thanks assistant Charlotte Cubitt and editor Bartley (who completed after illness).

Key additions to prior corpus: explicit population-survival argument; cultural evolution as between instinct and reason; critique of animistic vocabulary; the duty, if one does not wish to condemn millions to starvation, to resist creeds that destroy several property and the abstract rules of the extended order. Links directly to the 1944 polemic and 1948 scholarly essays while broadening the frame to evolutionary epistemology and the demographic consequences of the conceit.
