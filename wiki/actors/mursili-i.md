# Mursili I

The **[[hittites|Hittite]]** king (r. ~1620 BCE) who made the most spectacular
march in Bronze Age history — **down the whole length of the Euphrates to sack
[[fall-of-babylon-1595bce|Babylon]]** — and whose **murder** plunged the Old
Kingdom into half a century of regicide. Adopted grandson and successor of
**[[hattusili-i|Hattusili I]]**. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II,
Part 1]] (Chs. VI & XV, Gurney).

## Avenging Aleppo

Mursili's first task was to finish his grandfather's unfinished war. He **destroyed
Aleppo**, ending the "Great Kingship" of **[[iamkhad|Yamhad]]** that had dominated
north Syria since the days of Hammurabi — "Hattusili filled the days of Aleppo's
kingdom; Mursili destroyed it" (the later Aleppo treaty) — and "avenged his father's
blood." This broke the chief Amorite power of Syria and cleared his flank for the
greater adventure.

## The sack of Babylon (~1595 BCE)

Mursili then marched some **five hundred miles down the Euphrates** and fell on
**[[babylon-first-dynasty|Babylon]]**, ending the **First Dynasty of Babylon**
under its last king Samsuditana — the one event of early Hittite history confirmed
by an external source (the Babylonian Chronicle: *"in the time of Samsuditana the
men of Hatti marched against Akkad"*). The march is best explained through the
middle-Euphrates district of **Khana** and a likely **alliance with the rising
[[kassites|Kassites]]**: Mursili could not have passed unopposed unless the corridor
powers were complicit, and since he did **not** defeat the Kassites (who took the
Babylonian throne after him), they were probably his allies — or he sought them as a
bulwark against the Hurrians. He carried off the statues of **Marduk and
Sarpanitum** and rich booty to Hattusha but made **no attempt to hold Babylon**,
abandoning it to the Kassites. See [[fall-of-babylon-1595bce]].

## Assassination and aftermath

The victory was never consolidated. Within a year or two of his return Mursili was
**struck down by an assassin** at the instigation of his brother-in-law **Hantili**
(abetted by Hantili's son-in-law Zidanta), who seized the throne. The murder "ended
the first period of Hittite expansion" and began a chain of usurpations and
counter-killings (Hantili → Zidanta → Ammuna → Huzziya) that nearly destroyed the
kingdom — the chaos the **[[telipinu-edict-1500bce|Telipinu edict]]** later set out
to cure.

## Significance

In a single campaign Mursili **ended both surviving Amorite great kingdoms** —
Aleppo and Babylon — and so closed the Old Babylonian age and helped open the
[[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze]] world of the Kassites, Hurrians and Hittites. Yet
his career is also the textbook lesson of the Old Kingdom: **conquest without
consolidation**. He took no steps to secure the succession or his gains, and his
assassination shows the murderous instability of a kingship not yet bound by the
rule of law.
