## Overview

*Homo heidelbergensis* ("Heidelberg man," named from the 1908 Mauer jaw) is the
large-brained Middle Pleistocene hominid that, in
[[tattersall-world-from-beginnings-2008|Tattersall's]] account, became widespread
across the Old World (Africa, Europe, Asia) ~600–200 kya and is the probable common
stock from which both the [[neanderthals]] and *Homo sapiens* later diverged in
separate regional radiations. African examples (Bodo, ~600 kya, ~1250 cc; Kabwe)
and European/Asian ones (Arago, Petralona, Dali, Jinniushan) form a relatively
coherent group despite real variation.

## Anatomy and Capabilities

- **Big brain, archaic skull.** Brain approaching but below the modern average;
  large projecting face, bulbous double-arched brow ridges, chinless jaw.
- **Possible vocal potential.** The flexed cranial base (best seen at Bodo) hints at
  a partly modern vocal tract by ~600 kya, but the unretracted face makes full
  human speech doubtful, and nothing else suggests symbolic language.
- **Technology.** Mode 1 and Acheulean (Mode 2) handaxes; the **prepared-core
  (Levallois)** technique appears in this broad period (~300 kya) — a marked
  cognitive advance, implying intricate (if intuitive) reasoning.

## Behavioral Record (Europe)

The richest lifeway evidence comes from a handful of French and German sites:
- **Terra Amata** (S. France, ~400 kya) and **Bilzingsleben** (Germany): the
  earliest known artificial **shelters**, with hearths — among the earliest firm
  evidence for habitual control of [[fire-management|fire]] (consistently attested
  from ~400 kya; possible earlier instances).
- **Schöningen** (Germany, ~400 kya): superbly preserved wooden **throwing spears**,
  suggesting hunting more sophisticated than long assumed.
- **Sima de los Huesos / "Pit of the Bones"** (Atapuerca, Spain, ~500 kya): ≥28
  individuals with incipient Neanderthal-like features — a classic case of European
  diversification after the first occupation of the subcontinent.

## Significance (Counterfactual)

As the likely last common ancestor of Neanderthals and modern humans, *H.
heidelbergensis* is the hinge between the early dispersals and the Late Pleistocene
hominid world. Its European descendants radiated (the Steinheim, Sima, and
Neanderthal lineages), illustrating Tattersall's theme of repeated local
[[punctuated-equilibria|adaptive radiation]] following each successful incursion
into new territory.

## Fagan & Durrani (2020) — Schöningen and the Ancestor Question

Fagan & Durrani (2020) date *H. heidelbergensis* ca. 700,000–300,000 BP
(brain 1,100–1,400 cc), found across the East African Rift Valley, North and
South Africa, and Europe. At **Schöningen, Germany** (Hartmut Thieme
excavations since 1983), a lakeshore camp dated 400,000–380,000 BP preserves
a butchery site (elephants, birds, fish) and at least nine wooden spears
(spruce, 1.82–2.5 m) — quoted as "currently the world's oldest surviving
wooden artifacts" — alongside the remains of a complete herd of ~20 wild
horses hunted and butchered (over 90% of some 25,000 recovered bone
fragments). At **Atapuerca, Spain**, a 2010 find nicknamed "Elvis" — an
individual with a severe spinal pathology implying he could only have
survived with group care — and a 2011 find of a malformed 12-year-old's
skull at the same site, are both cited as evidence of compassionate care
within the species.

Fagan & Durrani flag a live **revision to the ancestor question**:
*heidelbergensis* was long treated as the last common ancestor of
Neanderthals and *H. sapiens*, but newer DNA evidence places the
modern-human/Neanderthal/Denisovan split before ~750,000 BP — too early for
*heidelbergensis* (whose earliest secure dates are ~700,000 BP) to fill that
role. **Homo antecessor** (~850,000 BP, Gran Dolina, Atapuerca) is now
proposed instead as the shared ancestor, which would make *heidelbergensis*
a descendant lineage rather than the ancestral hinge. Fagan & Durrani also
note that some sub-Saharan African genomes show signs of archaic
introgression from an unidentified source — possibly *H. heidelbergensis* or
*H. naledi* — distinct from the Neanderthal/Denisovan introgression found
outside Africa.

## Historiography

The species is partly a "category of convenience": its boundaries, and whether the
African and Eurasian samples are one species, are debated; the related label
"archaic *Homo sapiens*" is criticized here for disguising real diversity. The
status of the European "first wave" (*Homo antecessor*/*mauritanicus* at Gran
Dolina, ~780 kya, with possible cannibalism) relative to *heidelbergensis* is
unsettled. Most key specimens are poorly dated. African coverage thins markedly
after ~1.5 mya (few researchers, vast terrain); the European behavioral record is
unusually good for its date.
