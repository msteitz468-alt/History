# Yangshao Culture

The **Yangshao culture** (~5000–3000 BC) is the great **painted-pottery** tradition of the
middle [[yellow-river-valley|Yellow River]] in Henan, Shanxi, and Shaanxi — the Neolithic
predecessor that developed, through the Miaodigou II intermediate phase, into the
[[longshan-culture|Longshan]]. It was the first Chinese Neolithic culture excavated
scientifically, by **J. G. Andersson at the site of Yangshao in 1921**; Andersson's
proposal that its origins lay in the **Near East** was rejected by Chinese nationalist
scholars and became a founding controversy of [[nationalism-and-archaeology|Chinese
archaeology]]. About 800 Yangshao sites are known in Henan, ~2,000 in Shaanxi.

## Settlement and Society

[[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Liu (2004)]] reads the Yangshao as a sequence from egalitarian
village society toward emergent complexity:

- **Early Yangshao (~5000–4000 BC):** centripetal villages enclosed by ditches, divided into
  residential sectors each with one large house and several smaller ones. At **Jiangzhai**
  (5 ha, Banpo phase) houses ring a central plaza with doors facing inward; population
  ~80–112 (mean density 53.5/ha). Large houses lack cooking features and were **communal
  facilities**, not residences. Part-time craft specialisation (concentrated stone-axe
  production in one sector) and inter-village lithic trade existed, but **no social
  stratification** — these were segmentary, egalitarian societies sharing broad belief
  systems (the anthropomorphic "fish-mask" basins of Banpo, Jiangzhai, Beishouling).
- **Middle Yangshao / Miaodigou phase (~4000–3500 BC):** dramatic growth — sites up to **90
  ha** appear in western Henan (Lingbao). At **Xipo** (40 ha) a 516 m² ritual building
  (F105), plastered with **cinnabar**, required multi-village labour; feasting (abundant pig
  bone) and competition for scarce cinnabar opened the **first pathways to prestige**. This
  is where Liu locates the **earliest social hierarchy** in the region (a three-tiered
  settlement system), though formal mortuary stratification is still absent.
- **Late Yangshao (~3500–3000 BC):** ground-level square houses and the **first walled
  town** in the Yellow River valley (Xishan). **Dadiwan** (Gansu) grows to ~100 ha with a
  palace-like central building (F901) for redistribution and feasting; **Anban** (70 ha)
  yields fertility-ritual deposits and clay figurines, some with apparent Caucasoid
  features — hints of pre-Silk-Road contact that Liu judges **did not drive** indigenous
  social change. Climatic deterioration and declining community health (caries, enamel
  hypoplasia) form the backdrop to intensified ritual feasting.

Household material possessions were sparse (the richest Jiangzhai house held only 23
artifacts, vs. 91 in the wealthiest Longshan house), and gender-specific tools made up only
~34% of burial tool types (vs. ~80% in Dawenkou/Longshan), an index of the still-limited
division of labour.

## Historiography

The Yangshao anchors the old **Central Plains "nuclear-area" model** of Chinese civilisation
and the Andersson diffusion debate; both are treated under
[[origins-chinese-civilization-monocentric-vs-multiregional]] and [[nationalism-and-archaeology]].
Marxist readings cast Yangshao cemeteries as "matrilineal," but recent osteology and
mtDNA do not support a matrilineal/matrilocal burial pattern. See
[[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Liu (2004)]] and [[cambridge-history-of-ancient-china]].
