# Neo-Babylonian Empire (Chaldaean Empire)

The **Neo-Babylonian** or **Chaldaean Empire** (626–539 BCE) was the short, brilliant
imperial successor to [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]] — the last native Mesopotamian
empire, and the one that built the wonder-city of [[babylon|Babylon]], destroyed
[[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|Jerusalem]], and carried Judah into the **Babylonian
Exile**. Founded by the [[chaldeans|Chaldaean]] [[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]] out of
the long [[babylonia-iron-age|struggle of Babylonia against Assyria]], raised to its
height by his son **[[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]]**, and lost a generation
later under **[[nabonidus|Nabonidus]]** to [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Persian]], it
lasted less than ninety years. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]]
(Ch. 27, D. J. Wiseman) is the authority for the political narrative; its civilization
is treated in [[neo-babylonian-civilization]].

## Overview

The dynasty Nabopolassar founded in **626** is variously called the "Chaldaean"
dynasty, the **dynasty of Bit-Yakin**, or the **Third Dynasty of the Sealand** —
though Wiseman notes it was not the first time the southern tribes had united
Babylonia against the north (Nebuchadrezzar I, Eriba-Marduk, and
[[merodach-baladan|Marduk-apla-iddina II]] had each done so before). What was new
was its **durability and reach**: in under thirty years Nebuchadnezzar held "an
empire larger than that lost by the last major king of Assyria, [[ashurbanipal|
Ashurbanipal]]," stretching "from the Upper to the Lower seas" — the Mediterranean
to the Persian Gulf. It inherited Assyria's provincial machinery, its
[[assyrian-mass-deportation|deportation policy]], and its claim to universal
kingship, but governed in a Babylonian idiom of temple-centred cities and an
ideology of **Marduk's favour** rather than relentless military conquest.

## Foundation and the alliance with Media

[[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]], a Chaldaean once an Assyrian official in the Sealand,
seized the Babylonian throne in **626** and, with **[[medes|Cyaxares of Media]]**,
destroyed Assyria — sacking **Ashur (614)**, **[[fall-of-nineveh-612bce|Nineveh
(612)]]**, and **Harran (610)**. The alliance was sealed dynastically by the
marriage of Nabopolassar's eldest son to **Amytis of Media** — a treaty that
guarded the eastern frontier and freed Babylon to fight Egypt for the former
Assyrian west. From the outset Nabopolassar began rebuilding [[babylon|Babylon]] as
the capital of the new independent state.

## The reign of Nebuchadnezzar II (605–562)

The empire is essentially the achievement of **[[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]]
(Nabu-kudurri-usur)**. As crown prince he annihilated Necho II's army at the
**[[battle-of-carchemish-605bce|battle of Carchemish (605)]]**, then raced home on
his father's death to take the throne. Over a forty-year reign he:

- **Subdued the Levant**, reducing Judah, Tyre, Sidon, and the Philistine cities to
  tribute; an indecisive open battle with Egypt near Pelusium (**601**) cost him so
  heavily that he spent a year re-equipping at home, but it ended Saite power in Asia.
- **Captured Jerusalem twice** — in **597** (deporting Jehoiachin and the elite) and,
  after Zedekiah's revolt, **destroyed it in 587/586** ([[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce]]),
  beginning the **Babylonian Exile**.
- **Besieged Tyre for thirteen years** (c. 587–572) and claimed authority from
  Cilicia and Lydia to Egypt.
- **Rebuilt [[babylon|Babylon]]** into the greatest city of its age — its double
  walls, the Processional Way, the Ishtar Gate, Esagila and the ziggurat Etemenanki,
  and the "hanging gardens" built (tradition says) for his Median queen.

His inscriptions, unusually, **lack the military boasting** of Assyrian annals,
stressing instead piety toward Marduk and a self-image as **"king of justice"** —
a reformer who suppressed bribery and protected the weak. He died in 562 leaving
"Babylonian prestige at its highest point."

## The unstable succession (562–556)

After Nebuchadnezzar the throne passed through three short, contested reigns that
Wiseman reads as **inter-family and inter-tribal discord**:

- **Amel-Marduk (Evil-Merodach, 562–560)** — Nebuchadnezzar's son; released the
  captive [[kingdom-of-judah|Jehoiachin of Judah]] from prison (a clemency
  remembered in II Kings/Jeremiah); reportedly ruled "in a lawless fashion" and was
  assassinated.
- **Neriglissar (Nergal-sharra-usur, 560–556)** — a great landowner and officer
  (Nebuchadnezzar's *rab mag*) married to the king's daughter Kashshaya; campaigned
  successfully in Cilicia against Appuwashu of Piriddu, securing the buffer between
  Lydia and Media.
- **Labashi-Marduk (556)** — Neriglissar's son; reigned about three months before
  conspirators slew him.

## Nabonidus, Belshazzar, and the fall

**[[nabonidus|Nabonidus]] (555–539)** — chosen by the conspirators, not of the
Chaldaean royal line — devoted himself to the cult of the moon-god **Sin** (above
all rebuilding Ekhulkhul at Harran) and spent ten years at the Arabian oasis of
**Tema**, leaving his son **Belshazzar** as regent in Babylon. Whatever its causes
(religious reform, trade strategy, illness, or flight from a hostile priesthood),
his absence and his demotion of Marduk's New Year festival alienated the priesthood
and citizens. While Babylon was thus divided, **[[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]]** — having
already taken Media, Lydia, and Elam — marched down the Diyala, smashed the
Babylonian army at **Opis**, and entered Babylon "without a battle" on 13 October
**539** ([[fall-of-babylon-539bce]]). Belshazzar was slain; Nabonidus surrendered.
The empire passed to Persia, and Cyrus's decree allowed the **exiled Jews to return**.

## Governing structure and economy

Power was decentralized relative to Assyria: the king's own land-fund was small, and
the **temples and great private business houses** (above all the **Egibi** of Babylon)
dominated the economy. The privileged cult-cities (Babylon, Borsippa, Sippar,
Nippur, Uruk) held tax-and-corvée exemption (*kidinnūtu*) and a measure of self-rule
through their assemblies; the inscriptions of the kings boast of **temple-building,
not conquest**, because the clergy who spoke for those cities had to be courted.
The fuller institutional picture — society, law, the temple economy, slavery, trade,
and the great flowering of mathematics, astronomy, and literature — is treated in
[[neo-babylonian-civilization]].

## Historiography

Uniquely well documented for its **economy** — "tens of thousands" of legal and
administrative tablets from temple and private archives (Dandamaev) — but poorly
documented for its **political history**, since the Babylonian Chronicle runs out
after 594/3 and the kings' own inscriptions are building-texts, not annals. Wiseman
reconstructs the reigns from the Chronicle where it survives, dated contracts,
later classical sources ([[herodotus-reliability|Herodotus]], Berossus, Josephus),
and the Hebrew Bible — the last hostile, having lived through the destruction of
Jerusalem. The image of Nebuchadnezzar as the archetypal tyrant is a **Biblical
inheritance**; "at home he was traditionally the king who rebuilt Babylon." The
suddenness of the fall, Wiseman concludes, "stands as a testimony to the
debilitating effects of division among the political and economic leaders who
followed" Nebuchadnezzar.
