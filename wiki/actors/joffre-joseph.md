# Joseph Joffre

French general and Commander-in-Chief (GQG) from 1911/1914 to late 1916. Central figure in the transformation of the French army before 1914 and the conduct of the war through the Marne campaign and the attrition battles of 1915–1916.

## Pre-War Transformation and High Command (Doughty Ch. 1, exclusive range)

A graduate of the École Polytechnique and an engineer, the fifty-nine-year-old Joffre came from a middle-class family and considered himself a moderate republican. Colonial service (Formosa, Indochina) and major expeditions (led force 500 miles across Sudan to Timbuktu Dec 1893; organized Diégo Suarez port and fortifications in Madagascar) demonstrated exceptional organizational talent and earned Gallieni's praise that Joffre "should rise to the highest levels." In France he commanded 6th Division and II Corps, served as director of engineers, and from Jan 1910 as director of support services (gaining logistics/rail expertise and a seat on the Superior Council of War as its youngest member). He was noted for republican (not conservative/Catholic) views and political acumen with civilians, in contrast to officers such as Pau, Foch, or Castelnau.

In the Agadir crisis (1911), Messimy sought a reorganization of the High Command. After losing confidence in Michel (authority "nil"; called "incapable" and "a national danger" by Dubail, Durand, Gallieni; criticized Grandmaison's offensive à outrance and favored heavy reserve use), and after the Superior Council rejected Michel's demi-brigade plan (July 19), Messimy chose Joffre over Gallieni (who refused due to retirement timeline and role in Michel's ouster) and Pau (who demanded power to appoint all generals). 

A July 28, 1911 decree named Joffre chief of the General Staff and placed the High Command "completely and without reserve under Joffre's direction," uniting the vice-presidency and chief-of-staff functions previously divided. Dubail became chief of staff (subordinate except personnel/logistics); Castelnau (familiar with prior plans and ministry procedures) became deputy (Foch was Messimy's veto). A further January 1912 decree abolished the separate chief-of-staff-of-the-army post. Joffre now possessed authority over "the training of the army, its doctrine, its regulations, its mobilization, its concentration" — unprecedented peacetime power for the future wartime commander (Joffre). 

Joffre immediately revised Plan XVI (Sep 6 1911 publication): increased immediately-available divisions from 38 to 58 by shifting second-line/reserve units and forces from the Italian border into the front line; moved Fifth Army left to Mézières and Sixth Army nearer Verdun/Belgian frontier west of Luxembourg. This emphasized an *immediate offensive* over prior defensive-offensive posture and positioned two armies (seven corps) to cover more of the Belgian frontier. He continued analysis of German options through Belgium and became convinced of a deep envelopment threat. Political leaders (Caillaux then Poincaré) refused preemptive French entry into Belgium absent a "positive menace," though Joffre argued the terrain advantages there. 

**1915–1916: Balkans Alternatives, Attrition Strategy, Verdun, and Somme (Doughty Ch. 5–6, Range 4):** Joffre consistently opposed diverting significant forces from the Western Front to peripheral theaters. On Balkans/Salonika: "We are simply changing the point of application of the forces we have in the Near East; we are maneuvering, not withdrawing." "Given the actual state of our resources, this dispersion of our efforts will be true folly." "supporting an excessively large expeditionary corps in the Near East will result in such a consumption of our resources in men and munitions that it will render uncertain the possibilities of decisive victory in the principal theater of operations." Despite political pressure (Briand, Millerand), he limited French contribution and ensured Salonika remained a secondary "bluff." He supported unity of command and was elevated to commander-in-chief of all French armies (Dec 1915 decree) after Briand/Gallieni government; only colonial forces excluded.

At Chantilly (Dec 1915) he secured allied agreement to attrition via coordinated offensives, with France temporarily reducing effort. He drove adoption of methodical battle (Jan 1916 directive): successive "powerful, slow, methodical, repeated" attacks from objective to objective after artillery preparation. On Somme planning: chose region to join with British and shift burden ("the allies, who still have considerable forces... have the duty henceforth to provide the principal effort"); resisted British delay; accepted reduced French role due to Verdun but insisted on 1 July start to relieve pressure. Visited Haig 3 July; exploded at southward weight shift but relented ("an English battle").

**Siege Warfare and 1915 Offensives (Doughty Ch. 3–4 exclusive):** After Marne/Ypres, Joffre confronted continuous front and ammo crisis (telegram halting attacks). On 29 Sep liaison informed Poincaré of new phase; "we are condemned to wage a long war of siege." Joffre expected temporary siege until reserves and "special equipment" restored mobility. He refused passive defense ("absolutely negative") and from 17 Sep ordered "maintain an aggressive attitude" on static sectors to prevent German disengagements; mid-Oct demanded "vigor" and "will" to "maintain the spirit of the offensive" and publicly "nibbled" (*grignoter*). Partial offensives also aimed to aid Russia and seize terrain (e.g. Aubréville heights). 

He dominated high command (Millerand accepted division of responsibilities); created army groups (Foch Northern, Dubail Eastern formalized June); purged dozens of generals ("all-powerful"). Doctrine evolved: artillery must prepare every attack ("infantry should be held back"); future ops "slower" and "prepared down to the smallest detail" (Jan 1915 memo). 

Dec 1914–Mar 1915: principal attacks Artois (Tenth/Pétain XXXIII for Vimy) and Champagne (Fourth/de Langle "continuous battle" feeding troops); supporting St-Mihiel. Inadequate heavy arty (110 guns vs later needs); rain/mud; narrow fronts; German counters. Gains hundreds of meters to 1 km at cost of tens of thousands (43k in one Champagne phase for ~1 km avg). Joffre: failure from "insufficient and too brief artillery support; too few personnel engaged on fronts that were too narrow." Total period ~268k French dead. Opposed early Salonika ("It is not Austria... it is Germany").

1915 offensives (post Gorlice-Tarnów): May–June Artois (May 9 + June 16 renewals, doubled heavy ammo) 102.5k cas main for ~3 km; no breakthrough. Sept–Oct double (Artois + Champagne): initial 4 km/3 km gains (Pétain's XIV Corps, II Colonial) and 16k+ prisoners but second band halted; false breach wasted forces; rain. Total autumn ~192k cas for 2–4 km. Joffre adopted "attrition" language ("drain the enemy and attrit him") but persisted with offensive ("We’re not the people to let go"). After failure blamed "insufficient artillery"; ordered reconstitution and local actions only. "This is the end of our offensive." `produced:` shift to explicit attrition and methodical preparation.

On Verdun: initially saw German attack (21 Feb) as diversion ("I ask only one thing, that the Germans attack me and... that it be at Verdun" yet expected main effort elsewhere). Approved east bank defense after Briand intervention and Castelnau's assessment. Balanced reserves with Somme needs; restricted flow to Pétain while demanding aggressive counterattacks ("This is the only way you can impose your will on the enemy"). Clashed with Pétain over levies of troops/artillery ("needless" appeals to minister); used Verdun to train/rotate but protected Somme striking power. Political fallout from Verdun and Somme contributed to loss of confidence in him by late 1916.

`produced:` elevation of Pétain and Nivelle; methodical doctrine that shaped 1916 operations; `contributed_to:` eventual political replacement. See [[events/battle-of-verdun-1916]], [[events/battle-of-the-somme-1916]], [[processes/attrition-warfare-wwi]], [[concepts/methodical-battle]].


Joffre also drove integration of the offensive à outrance (Grandmaison's Feb 1911 lectures criticizing "nearly complete atrophy" of offensive spirit provided the catalyst). Under Joffre the 1913 regulations (Oct for large units; Dec for smaller) codified: "The French army, returning to its traditions, accepts no law in the conduct of operations other than the offensive"; "Only the offensive yields positive results"; battles as "moral contests"; artillery supports rather than prepares attacks; infantry bayonet as "supreme weapon." He bore ultimate responsibility for embedding the "mystique of the offensive" and "cult of the offensive."

Joffre and staff developed Plan XVII (concept to Superior Council Apr 18 1913, unanimous support; main parts Feb 7 1914; complete May 1). It was a concentration plan stating the C-in-C's intention "to deliver, with all forces assembled, an attack against the German armies." Five armies in the northeast with Fourth in reserve; options for attack N or S of Metz-Thionville or into Belgium (Neufchâteau/Arlon). Fifth Army on left flank. Not a fixed operations script; Joffre deliberately kept final decisions open and concealed full intent from politicians to avoid "meddling" (e.g., response to Dubail: "That's your plan, not mine").

## Major decisions and consequences (from Doughty Ch. 2, War of Movement 1914)

Appointed under Messimy's reforms; shaped Plan XVII (flexible deployment allowing offensive on both sides of Metz-Thionville once enemy intentions clearer). In July–early August 1914 pressed for covering forces (10 km restriction accepted to appear non-provocative) and urged simultaneous Russian offensive into East Prussia; gained "absolute liberty of movement for executing his plans" after small German frontier crossings (pp. 54-55). 

Ordered small Alsace demonstration (Mulhouse, 7–10 Aug) to show readiness to Russians and French public; formed Army of Alsace under Pau to secure right flank. Issued General Instructions No. 1 (8 Aug): two-pronged attack — right jab (1st/2nd Armies into Lorraine south of Metz-Thionville) to fix and draw enemy south; main left blow (3rd/4th into Belgium/Lux north of the fortifications) to strike the "most sensitive point" and unhinge the German advance. Intelligence assessment (9 Aug) focused on Metz/Luxembourg concentrations and underestimated depth of German right through Belgium (pp. 57-59, 64).

Lorraine offensive (14 Aug) launched to coincide with Russian attack; divergent fronts and widening sectors proved difficult. Early reports showed artillery outranged and infantry halted by unseen entrenched fire and machine guns (Castelnau note 15 Aug); Joffre still praised "admirable offensive qualities of our infantry" while noting need for better preparation. German counterattack 20 Aug shattered uncoordinated 1st/2nd Armies; French fought separate battles and fell back, then stabilized via Charmes Gap counter (pp. 59-63).

After frontier defeats (21–23 Aug in Ardennes and on Sambre), Joffre ordered general withdrawal (24 Aug) to Maubeuge–Mézières–Verdun line and began shifting forces left. Gen. Instr. No. 2 (25 Aug) extended the line to Amiens-Reims-Verdun and envisaged envelopment of the German right near Amiens with a newly formed left group (6th Army). Issued tactical instructions for "intimate combination of infantry and artillery." Formed Foch detachment to close gap between 4th/5th Armies (pp. 76-77). 

Formed 6th Army (Maunoury) 26–27 Aug from shifted corps/reserves and Lorraine/Alsace remnants. Visited British HQ 26 Aug; explained new strategy but left concerned about British "fragility." Ordered Lanrezac counterattack (Guise, 29 Aug) despite objections; visited Sir John French to urge cooperation (pp. 78-81). 

Gen. Instr. No. 4 (1 Sep) ordered withdrawal to Seine-Aube line; 6th Army to cover Paris. Advised government to leave Paris (2 Sep). Relieved Ruffey (30 Aug, "very nervous") and Lanrezac (4 Sep, "intensely painful" but lost confidence after repeated challenges); replaced Lanrezac with Franchet d’Espèrey. Intelligence (1–3 Sep) confirmed Kluck turning southeast of Paris. Modified instructions 2 Sep: "Simultaneously, the garrison of Paris will attack in the direction of Meaux" (pp. 82-86).

On 4 Sep telegraphed d’Espèrey for readiness to counterattack "tomorrow or the day after"; received assurance after d’Espèrey–Wilson meeting. Met Sir John French 5 Sep in emotional appeal: banged fist and declared "The honor of England is at stake, Marshal." Sir John committed ("all that men can do our fellows will do"). Published attack order 2200 hrs 4 Sep (date advanced to 6 Sep morning after Gallieni call) (pp. 88-91).

During battle displayed "strong judgment and a remarkable degree of composure" responding to near-disasters on flanks of 4th/9th/3rd/2nd Armies without diverting from left. Credit for "miracle of the Marne" belonged to Joffre: kept forces under control, recognized Kluck turn, prepared counteroffensive, refused to let flank crises divert resources. Ordered energetic pursuit ("victory depends on the legs of our infantry"); Germans slipped away and halted on Aisne ~14 Sep. Complained of "slowness and the lack of skilled maneuvering" by flank armies and 5th Army in exploitation. Shifted forces west by rail (corps in 5–6 days via 100+ trains); elevated Foch to coordinate northern group (pp. 96-101, 1597-1600). 

By mid-November mobile warfare ended; Joffre had prevented decisive German victory despite bankrupt initial strategy and terrible losses. 

Relieved of command December 1916 (detailed Ch. 7 range): discontent boiled over after Somme failure and Romania collapse. Chamber closed session (28 Nov–7 Dec) after Roques' Salonika report (favorable to Sarrail, discrediting Joffre/Briand control). Briand reorganized: Joffre named "general-in-chief of the French armies, technical military advisor to the government, consultative member of the War Committee" (13 Dec decree); Nivelle appointed commander of armies north/northeast. Joffre initially objected and commented "This is not what they promised me"; powers reduced to studies/advice only (new minister of war controlled operations; Nivelle reported exclusively to minister). Elevated marshal of France 26 Dec but facade to satisfy army/allies/country; Joffre requested relief. (A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 319-321)

Pre-ouster: Oct 1916 assessment called for winter pressure on all fronts and "coordinated offensives in spring 1917 similar to those undertaken in 1916 but which will be more powerful and more fruitful." Final operational methods letter (1 Nov) urged broad-front attacks reaching enemy artillery, "divide their attacks into different phases and fight a series of battles with delays between each successive battle being as short as possible," reserves close, "Attacks prepared and conducted in this manner will always succeed if those making the attacks have confidence in their success." Chose continued Verdun pressure over winter despite morale crisis (soldiers concluding costs exceeded gains; "we do not want responsibility for continuing the slaughter"). (l 311, 315-316, 191-201)

**Role in high command evolution**: Benefited from and extended the strengthened High Command powers created 1911–12. Purged underperforming subordinates ruthlessly after early defeats. Emphasized methodical preparation, artillery, and reserves as the war progressed.

**Counterfactual significance**: Without Joffre's calm resolve and left-flank conception at the Marne, the initial German advance might have achieved greater success or forced a different armistice dynamic. His persistence kept France in the war through the worst attritional phases.

**Historiography note**: Portrayed variously as the savior of France (Marne) and as wedded to costly offensives. Doughty presents a more nuanced view of pragmatic adaptation within severe constraints.
