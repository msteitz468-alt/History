# Hudson's Bay Company

Chartered English (later British) joint-stock fur-trade company—Ray’s through-line institutional actor for the Northern Interior and a principal European partner (then settler-era obstacle) on the Northwest Coast under Fisher. Archives (Provincial Archives of Manitoba; Hudson’s Bay Record Society volumes) are the documentary spine of Subarctic ethnohistory.

## Formation and Bay-side system (Ray)

- **Idea and charter:** Radisson and Groseilliers (winter 1659–60) concluded the cheapest route to northern fur country was via Hudson/James Bays. English *Nonsuch* voyage; **charter from Charles II, 2 May 1670**—title effectively to all lands drained into Hudson Strait (**Rupert’s Land**) and monopoly trading privileges therein.
- **Protocol:** Gift exchange (alliance renewal) + barter; “trading captains” leading gangs; unit of account **Made Beaver (MB)**—so well suited it remained in use in many northern areas until after WWI.
- **Early posts:** Charles Fort/Rupert 1668; Moose 1673; Albany 1675; York Factory 1682 (greatest inland impact via Hayes–Echimamish); Churchill/Prince of Wales 1717 (Chipewyan trade). Local [[cree]] and Assiniboine allies used firearms to control Bay access and expand as middlemen.
- **“Sleeping by the frozen sea”:** Deliberate refusal to move inland against French interior posts—higher costs without guaranteed extra furs; left inland trade to Indian entrepreneurial partners—wise business despite political critics. Volumes fell under French advance; profits remained.

## Destructive competition and monopoly (1763–1870)

- **1774:** Cumberland House—first inland post; end of frozen-sea policy. Rivalry with Montreal Nor’Westers / **North West Company** built >600 posts; by 1821 most Indians S/E of Peace River lived near a post—middleman roles collapsed there.
- **1821 amalgamation:** Mutual financial ruin + British alarm at lawlessness/alcohol → combined firm, 20-year monopoly on Rupert’s Land and Northwest (Mackenzie + **Pacific drainage**). **George Simpson** governor-in-chief (1839 all Canadian ops).
- **Conservation and “fur desert”:** Quotas, trap bans, closed/open posts to nurse beaver; “fur desert” along U.S. border E of Rockies and in Oregon to block Americans. Initial failure; tighter 1839 rules → beaver rebound 1840s.
- **Social order after 1821:** Gift austerity; alcohol cut (tea/flour rose); rigid racialized hierarchy blocking mixed-ancestry officers; officer marriage norms shifted against Indian wives (Simpson exemplar).
- **1870:** Relinquished monopoly and Rupert’s Land title for $1.5 million, land grants, and post lands; Canada assumed sovereignty and legal welfare responsibility formerly tacitly the company’s.

## Northwest Coast operations (Fisher)

Only after 1821 amalgamation were fur resources west of the Rockies systematically exploited. Under Simpson, **John McLoughlin** managed from **Fort Vancouver**; chain of coastal and interior forts; 1830s company vessels to eliminate maritime competitors. **Fort Victoria** 1843 as potential new western HQ.

- Intended monopoly failed through the 1840s: American vessels and Russian American Company kept alternative markets; McLoughlin maintained **three price levels** (interior; coast; coast with competitors present). Snake Country expeditions of the 1820s virtually eliminated SE mountain-man competition; coast harder.
- Simpson found Indians “tiresome in their bargaining”; high coastal prices affected interior chains; inland peoples sold to coastal middlemen when forts underpriced.
- Native role in land-based trade matched maritime pattern: company men few, enclave-limited, influence overstated; **reciprocity** not conquest. McLoughlin 1843: better economy by good terms with Indians than variance.
- Douglas (chief factor Fort Victoria; colonial governor 1851) applied fur-trade experience to early colonial land policy—piecemeal reserves, selective force—until retirement 1864; company and settlement interests, he wrote in 1838, “will never harmonize.” Oregon Treaty 1846 split the old preserve at 49°.

## Industrial age and decline as fur monopolist (Ray)

CPR 1885 and telegraph broke price monopoly; cash buyers, Revillon Frères, Northern Trading, Lamson & Hubbard; 1914 “black year” (London market closed, credit cut). By WWII eve HBC obtained barely **~1/4** of Canadian wild fur. Dual role as trader and de facto welfare provider later reimbursed by the state; aviation and rail displaced Native transport labor (Moose Works closure devastating).

## Counterfactual significance

Without HBC’s Bay entry, French St. Lawrence systems and later American Pacific capital would have structured Subarctic and Northwest trade differently; the 1821 monopoly and Simpson conservation regime shaped beaver ecology and Native credit dependence for two generations; Douglas’s fur-trade-trained governance briefly distinguished BC land practice from U.S. wholesale treaty violence—without preventing long-run dispossession.

## Historiography

E.E. Rich on mercantile HBC to 1867; Innis *Fur Trade in Canada*; Ray *Indians in the Fur Trade*, *“Give Us Good Measure”* (with Freeman), *Canadian Fur Trade in the Industrial Age*; Van Kirk and Brown on women and company families. Fisher *Contact and Conflict* for west of Rockies. Company records enable quantitative ethnohistory unmatched for most colonial frontiers.
