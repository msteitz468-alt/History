# Rikken Seiyukai (Friends of Constitutional Government)

The **Rikken Seiyūkai** (立憲政友会, "Friends of Constitutional Government"), founded
**15 September 1900** by [[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]] with the backing of [[emperor-meiji|
Emperor Meiji]], was the most important political party of the Meiji and Taishō eras. It
absorbed the defunct [[kensei-to|Kenseito]] and provided the organisational base for Itō's
fourth cabinet (October 1900). Its founding represented the culmination of the struggle to
create a constitutional party capable of bridging the gap between the elected Diet and the
genrō-dominated executive. From Donald Keene's [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002|*Emperor
of Japan: Meiji and His World*]] (chapters 50–51).

## Founding

After the collapse of the [[kensei-to|Kenseito]] party cabinet in October 1898, Itō Hirobumi
concluded that constitutional government required a party with a firm popular base and direct
imperial support. In September 1900 he announced the formation of a new party. The emperor not
only granted Itō permission but on **14 September** sent Iwakura Tomosada to Itō's residence
with a gift of 10,000 yen and rolls of red and white silk crepe, together with a message of
"abiding eagerness to benefit by Itō's frank advice" — an unprecedented show of imperial favour
for a party formation. Cynics called it "the Imperially Sanctioned Party."

The Kenseito immediately dissolved itself and joined the Seiyūkai as its nucleus. Itō was
elected president.

## Itō's Fourth Cabinet

On 7 October 1900 Itō accepted the emperor's command to form a government — the fourth Itō
cabinet, invested 19 October. This was the first cabinet to consist entirely of members of a
named political party. The House of Peers denounced party government, but Itō pressed on,
dealing with the aftermath of the [[boxer-rebellion|Boxer Rebellion]] and the financial
strains of military build-up since the [[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895|Sino-Japanese War]].

The cabinet faced persistent revenue crises. Tax increase bills passed the House of Representatives
but were blocked by the House of Peers — controlled by Yamagata's faction, which viewed party
government as a violation of the Meiji constitution's spirit. On 12 March 1901 the emperor
issued a rescript rebuking the House of Peers for obstructing government revenue measures — an
unusual instance of direct imperial intervention in legislative-executive conflict, obtained by
Itō but unauthorised in the strict constitutional sense (the rescript lacked the emperor's
signature and countersignature of a minister, as Itō's enemies noted).

Itō resigned as prime minister in May 1901 citing ill health, replaced by Yamagata's protégé
**Katsura Tarō**, who governed in a more conservative direction. But the Seiyūkai continued as
the leading party.

## Historical Significance

Keene presents the Seiyūkai's founding as the point at which Japanese parliamentary politics
matured from unstable factional coalitions to a durable party organisation. Its formation
signalled that even the genrō most hostile to party government (Yamagata, Kuroda) could not
simply eliminate the parties; the question had become whether to work with them or against them.

The Seiyūkai continued to dominate Taishō-era party politics, alternating in power with the
Kenseikai (later Minseitō), until the rise of military dominance in the 1930s.
