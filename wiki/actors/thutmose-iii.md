# Thutmose III

The great conqueror-pharaoh of the [[new-kingdom-egypt|Eighteenth Dynasty]] (reigned
~1479–1425 BCE; sole ruler from ~1458) — in CAH's judgement "incontestably the
greatest pharaoh ever to occupy the throne of Egypt," whose ~17 campaigns built the
Egyptian empire from the Fourth Cataract to the Euphrates. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. IX, Hayes).

## The shadow of Hatshepsut

Son of Thutmose II by a minor wife, he came to the throne a child and spent two
decades as the nominal co-regent of his stepmother **[[hatshepsut|Hatshepsut]]**,
probably with the army, "training himself as a soldier." Only in his ~22nd regnal
year (~1458) did he become sole master of Egypt. Late in his reign he had her
monuments **erased** — long read as personal vengeance, treated by CAH as a sober
dynastic act of legitimation.

## The empire-builder

Freed of Hatshepsut and facing a revolt of the Syrian principalities, Thutmose III
launched the campaign that opened his ~20 years of conquest. The **Battle of Megiddo**
(~1457) — his decisive defeat of a Syro-Canaanite coalition led by the prince of
Kadesh, after a daring march through the narrow Aruna pass — is the first battle in
history recorded in tactical detail, in his **Karnak Annals**. Repeated campaigns
carried him to the **Euphrates**, where he set up a victory stela beside that of his
grandfather Thutmose I and clashed with the rising power of **[[mitanni|Mitanni]]**;
in the south he fixed Egypt's frontier at **Napata** near the Fourth Cataract. His
method — annual campaigns, the taking of hostage-princes educated in Egypt, garrisons
and oaths of loyalty — created a durable system of imperial control over
Syria-Palestine, after which "one or two campaigns usually sufficed" for his
successors.

## The administrator and patron

Thutmose III is CAH's archetype of the **"military king"**: he divided his
independent reign "equally between his conquests abroad and his administrative tours
of inspection at home," personally driving a vast building programme (above all at
Karnak), exploiting the empire's resources, and patronizing the arts. His vizier
**Rekhmire**, whose tomb preserves the famous "Installation of the Vizier" and a
picture of the whole Karnak temple-economy under vizieral supervision, exemplifies
the tightly-run administration. A physically small man (his mummy is under 5'4"),
he was also celebrated as a champion archer, charioteer, and athlete.

## Shaw (2000): the mechanics of empire

Betsy Bryan's chapter details how the conquest was run. The **[[events/battle-of-megiddo-1457bce|Battle
of Megiddo]]** (year 23) yielded "894 chariots … 200 suits of armour … over 2,000 horses, and
25,000 animals"; "at the end of some seventeen years of military campaigns, Thutmose III had
successfully established Egyptian dominance over Palestine and had made strong inroads into
southern Syria." The control system was a **vassal order** (see
[[processes/egyptian-imperialism]]): the children of city-rulers were taken to Egypt "to be
Egyptianized," and per the Annals "whoever died from among these chiefs, his Majesty caused
that his son stand in his place." Bryan distinguishes one-time **booty** from recurring
**tribute** (*inu*) — the latter from Retenu and Djahy, while powerful **Nahrin (Mitanni)**
does not even appear in the inscriptions before the **eighth campaign (year 33)**, "regarded
as too powerful to be mentioned." The **Annals** themselves were inscribed at Karnak only in
**year 42**. Syrian war-booty (glass, fine metalwork "of the workmanship of Djahy") was "copied
(and frankly improved upon) in Egypt," reshaping elite material culture. He took
[[actors/amenhotep-ii|Amenhotep II]] as co-regent in his 51st year.

## Counterfactual / analytical significance

Thutmose III is the architect of the **Egyptian empire** and thus of Egypt's
standing as a Late Bronze "great power." His Syrian conquests created the very
frontier — against [[mitanni|Mitanni]] and soon the [[hittites|Hittites]] — that
defined the [[interpolity-law|international system]] of the next two centuries
(diplomatic marriage with Mitanni would follow under his grandson). Without his
sustained campaigning the New Kingdom might have remained, like the Middle Kingdom, a
Nile-and-Nubia state rather than an Asian empire. His career is the classic case for
**individual agency** in this volume — a single exceptionally capable ruler
converting Egypt's potential into hegemony.

## Historiography

Exceptionally well documented for an ancient king: the **Karnak Annals** (extracts
from the day-books of his campaigns — a genuine military record, unusually sober in
tone), boundary and victory stelae, the tombs of his officials (Rekhmire, Menkheper-
reseneb), and an astronomically-anchored chronology. The records are royal but, CAH
notes, "for their period moderately phrased and sincere." See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
