# Shen Shixing (Shen Shih-hsing, 1535–1614)

Senior Ming official who served as First Grand Secretary (1583–1591) during the critical years around 1587. Embodiment of the conscientious, diplomatic but ultimately constrained civil bureaucrat whose "human touch" and indirect management achieved limited technical successes (Yellow River control, frontier stabilization) while the larger constitutional deadlock between throne and bureaucracy persisted. Central figure in Huang's Chapter 4 "The Living Ancestor."

## Early Career and Succession to Zhang Juzheng

Shen Shih-hsing had been treated by Chang Chü-cheng more as a novice than partner, yet this subordinate position had allowed him "to grow and to progress." He acknowledged Chang as "a very talented man" capable of "remembering thousands of things in minute detail." But Chang's "greatest fault had been that he could not practice humility, and because of that he tended to overlook the larger issues and in the end evolved into the kind of man Mencius had described as capable of seeing the barbs of a feather but unable to visualize a cartload of faggots."

When Wan-li ascended in 1572, "Chang Chü-cheng seemed to be a much better choice" than the deceitful Kao Kung. Yet Chang "tried to impose a system on an organization the complexities of which could not be tidied up, and which, also by nature, had to operate under contradictory principles. He was bound sooner or later to meet opposition. He managed to chastise his critics in his lifetime; but his entire enterprise collapsed when he was dead and buried."

## Management of Technical and Frontier Crises (1583–1591)

Shen took quiet pride in averting major Yellow River disasters and frontier wars. When dikes burst in 1587 he recalled the experienced but politically tainted P'an Chi-hsun (discharged 1584 for alleged siding with Chang Chü-cheng's family) to lead rehabilitation under the "channel contracting theory." P'an narrowed channels for self-scouring, built parallel dikes with retention basins, and used massive fascine bundles (rarely less than twenty feet in diameter and 150 feet long) maneuvered into place by several hundred workers, with ships scuttled alongside for immediate stoppage. Central government provided only token grants-in-aid; most costs (materials, labor, transport) came from local levies coordinated across counties. Shen gave P'an full political cover in the capital through published dialogue with the emperor. P'an served successfully as long as Shen was first grand-secretary but was censured again soon after Shen's removal.

In 1590 an Ordos Mongol ambush killed a Chinese deputy commander near the Kansu-Kokonor border after pursuit of raiders; court hawks and even the emperor demanded war against Prince Cürüke's all-Mongolian confederation. Shen convinced the throne to treat the incident as local, preserve the 1570–71 Altan Khan peace settlement (trading privileges and annual subsidies), maintain civil supremacy over the military, focus on defense and filling vacancies/warehouses rather than campaigns, and delegate to a high commissioner (Cheng Lo appointed 4 days after the key 25 August 1590 audience). The crisis stabilized without full war; Cürüke withdrew his hordes. Shen quietly dismissed charges in a concurrent 1587 Liaotung incident involving the ambitious Chien-chou chieftain Nurhaci, avoiding a split that would benefit the future Manchu founder: "Because Shen Shih-hsing kept quiet about the Liaotung incident, no one remembered that he had had anything to do with the founder of the Manchu dynasty."

## The 1590 Palace Audiences and Intimate Tutor–Sovereign Relationship

Shen maintained an intimate instructor-disciple tie with Wan-li dating to childhood. The recorded interview of 25 August 1590 (last on record) shows Shen using humility and diplomacy to secure commitments: no removal or punishment of frontier governors, long-term delegation for morale, civil supremacy intact, peace framework preserved, and focus on defense. A high official was dispatched as commissioner with the emperor's consent. The published record served as a declaration of policy "that should have silenced the hawks." Shen felt gratified by the dynasty's design, which tied academicians (assistants to senior courtiers) to princes who became emperors, creating access "unreachable by others."

## The Eastern Depot Incident and Its Consequences (1587)

A Peking magistrate's illegal beating of ceremonial performers at the Court of Imperial Sacrifices raised jurisdictional conflict. Minister of Justice Li Shih-ta obstructed two Silk Robe Guard agents sent to observe the metropolitan court trial (precedent favored Eastern Depot intervention). The emperor viewed exclusion as contempt of the throne and curtailment of prerogative, threatening transfer of the case to the Depot. Shen mediated an apology from Li and a two-month salary fine for the sergeant-at-arms; the case stayed civil. Short-lived throne "moral victory." Civil officials soon forced dismissal of Eastern Depot director Chang Ching (accused of receiving money and offending officials by disclosing secrets). A junior remonstrator accusing the emperor of being bribed by Chang received sixty strokes. "This unhappy incident plunged the young sovereign into a deeply downcast mood for a long time. In court politics it marked a point of no return. Already tired of his morning audiences and study sessions, the Wan-li emperor was henceforth to suspend them permanently."

## Return of Imperial Gifts and Self-Assessment in Retirement

After forced retirement (Shen lived several days beyond seventy-nine), on a special birthday the Wan-li emperor (whom Shen had not seen for twenty-three years) conveyed greetings via envoy and sent fifty ounces of silver, a crimson python-embroidered robe, and four bolts of satin. Shen returned the gifts unopened, struggling with age to kneel toward Peking while reading the message. He explained he could not accept because "as tutor and chief counsellor to the throne he felt he had failed in his duties. If not, why was the eldest imperial son, now in direct line of succession, not being instructed by the Han-lin academicians? And how could it be that both inside and outside the capital numerous governmental positions remained unfilled?" The emperor was disturbed but set the parcels aside. In copious posthumously published writings Shen reflected without qualms or remorse, viewing his record as better than surface appearances suggested and his preventive style as keeping things "as they should be."

## Role as First Grand Secretary: Peacemaker and Compromiser (pre-1587 context)

By 1587 Shen occupied the same desk. He had survived the immediate post-Chang purges of 1583–1585 only through "lack of positive evidence that the young emperor was ready to discharge" him. His "quiet and peaceful disposition had been well accepted." He positioned himself explicitly as peacemaker. He persuaded the emperor to discontinue Chang Chü-cheng's efficiency control system. In the 1587 evaluation he limited changes to an absolute minimum (only 33 doctorates demoted, none from vital departments). To protect officials' security he obtained consent "to suspend his personal command of the army, to limit his travel outside the capital, and to restrain eunuch Chang Ching."

The Kao Ch'i-yu crisis (1583–85) tested him severely; he suspended himself but the emperor retained both grand secretaries. He understood the bureaucracy's dual character ("logic for its professed virtue; it also had an underlying urge for self-gain") and preached "sincerity, to be arrived at through ritualistic exercises." Critics called him "a great compromiser"; Shen saw this as necessary to solidify position before restoring workable order.

## The Succession Crisis and Forced Resignation (1590–1591)

While in office the succession issue emerged. Shen was the first to advise declaring Ch'ang-lo heir soon after Ch'ang-hsün's birth (he had participated ceremonially in Lady Cheng's investiture as imperial consort). He waited patiently, believing Wan-li sensible. Critics later accused him of postponing the installation; in reality timing and his indirect style prevented decisive action. A joint petition while on sick leave (name included without consent) led to impeachment for duplicity. The emperor initially protected him but protest engulfed the court; Shen resigned repeatedly until retirement authorized. "The tragedy of the Year of the Rabbit was that nobody emerged a victor."

## Personality, Philosophy, and Institutional Limits

Sensitive and gifted at reading minds, Shen believed absolute monarchy served bureaucratic purpose by providing authoritative decisions without the sovereign becoming personally entangled. The emperor must personify "the universal spirit, which was perfect but neutral." His own approach relied on moral inducement and diplomacy because "few problems which came his way could be institutionally or organizationally solved." He had no legislative/judicial tools. In retirement he remained serene, clear conscience; his office required the human touch, which he applied sincerely. Ultimate outcome was beyond his control.

In Huang's reading, Shen's experience confirmed that "the empire's transportation and communication could never be sufficiently improved to be systematized." No administrator could "sacrifice the human touch to neat organizational principles." The bureaucracy "was a source of power in itself and, in a sense, was master." Shen had predicted as early as 1587 that "under such conditions the state could not long remain in peace and order."

## Historiography

Huang presents Shen not as weak or a total blank but as correctly diagnosing institutional limits once the throne turned cynical. Traditional accounts often condemned him as pliant; Huang reframes his caution and indirectness as the only viable posture. The source draws on Shen's own voluminous writings (poetry, letters, tomb inscriptions) and Veritable Records to show the gap between ritual expectation and operational reality, and the genuine (if limited) successes in flood control and frontier management.
