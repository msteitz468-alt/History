## Overview

Akechi Mitsuhide was a senior general of [[oda-nobunaga]] who in the fifth month of 1582 launched the coup known as the Honnōji Incident, surprising Nobunaga at his lodgings at the Honnōji temple in Kyoto, setting fire to it, and forcing him to commit seppuku. Akechi's own revolt lasted only thirteen days before he was defeated and killed at the battle of Yamazaki by Toyotomi Hideyoshi — giving rise to the popular epithet "the thirteen-day ruler" (Jusan-nichi Tenkabito).

## The Honnōji Incident (1582)

The motivation for Akechi's revolt remains contested. Sadler records one traditional explanation: that during the reception organized for Tokugawa Ieyasu's visit, Nobunaga inspected Akechi's prepared banquet, declared that the fish and fowl "stank," and "kicked the lot over all about the apartment, while he abused Akechi and finally relieved him of his office." Whether a personal humiliation of this kind was the cause, a pretext, or an embellishment added later is unclear. The rebellion was organized in secret; Akechi mobilized his forces on a campaign supposed to be directed against Mōri and turned them on the Honnōji where Nobunaga was sleeping with only a small guard.

Nobunaga's heir, his son Nobutada, was also surrounded in his lodgings and committed suicide after a fierce fight.

## Consequences for Ieyasu

Akechi's revolt found [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] at Sakai with only a small retinue, far from his Mikawa base. The Honnōji Incident thus directly caused the [[iga-flight-1582]] and set in motion the chain of events — Ieyasu's seizure of Kai and Shinano, the Komaki-Nagakute campaign, and eventually the Kantō transfer — that culminated in the Tokugawa shogunate.

## Defeat and Death

[[toyotomi-hideyoshi]], campaigning against Mōri in the west, received news of the revolt, made a rapid truce with Mōri, and marched east at extraordinary speed. At the battle of Yamazaki (Tenmōzan) on the first of the seventh month of 1582, Akechi's forces were defeated. Akechi was killed — by bandits during his flight, according to the most common account — thirteen days after the Honnōji Incident. Hideyoshi's speed in avenging Nobunaga positioned him as the legitimate successor to Nobunaga's unification project.

## Historiography

Akechi Mitsuhide is one of the most debated figures of the Sengoku period. The "real" motive for his revolt — personal grievance, political ambition, external instigation (possibly by the Ashikaga, the Mōri, or even Hideyoshi himself in some theories), or religious/ethical objection to Nobunaga's policies — has generated extensive scholarship. Sadler presents the humiliation-at-the-banquet story as one explanation while acknowledging it is disputed. The episode demonstrates a structural vulnerability of the Nobunaga system: the delegation of vast military power to subordinates without institutional constraints on their loyalty.
