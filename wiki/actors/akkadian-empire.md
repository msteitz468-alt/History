# Akkadian Empire (Dynasty of Agade)

The **first empire in world history**: the dynasty founded by
[[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon of Akkad]] (c. 2334 BCE) that for the first time welded
the city-states of [[early-dynastic-sumer|Sumer]] and the Semitic-speaking north
into a single state ruled from a single new capital, **Agade** (Akkad, site still
unidentified), and projected its arms from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean
and the Taurus. Its five principal kings — Sargon, his sons **Rimush** and
**Manishtusu**, his grandson [[naram-sin|Naram-Sin]], and **Shar-kali-sharri** —
held power for roughly 180 years before the state disintegrated under the
[[gutian-invasion|Gutian invasion]]. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XIX, C. J. Gadd).

## What was new: the first territorial empire

Earlier Sumerian hegemons (Eannatum, Lugalzaggisi) had at most raided beyond the
zone of Sumerian institutions. Sargon did something categorically different: he
**built a new capital** to house his own warriors and their families (not a
captured city), and from it governed a standing territorial state. The empire's
inscriptions boast a permanent military household — "5,400 men ate bread daily
before him" — and the placing of Akkadian governors (and dispossession of local
landholders in their favour) across conquered cities. With Naram-Sin the rulers
claimed the new universalist title **"king of the four regions"** (*šar kibrāt
arbaim*), a claim to dominion over the whole earth. This is the archetype to which
all later Mesopotamian empires looked back: see [[imperialism]] and the parallel
later case of [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]].

## A society divided

CAH (Gadd) stresses that the empire introduced a **division** into the land that
had not been felt before. "Sumerian" and "Akkadian" were never racial categories —
Semitic-speakers had been intermixed in the south from the earliest times (see
[[early-dynastic-sumer]]) — but the Agade kings ruled as a self-conscious
**northern, military caste**, and every one of their accessions was met by a
**bitter revolt of "the cities of Sumer."** The patriotic Sumerian resentment of
the "alien yoke" prefigured the still fiercer hatred later directed at the
[[gutian-invasion|Gutians]], and arguably weakened the state from within.

## Administration and economy

- **A more secular order.** The business archives of the Agade period show **no
  trace of the all-embracing [[temple-economy|temple-estate economy]]** typical of
  the old Sumerian cities; land and tenure had moved toward a more royal/secular
  basis. The **Obelisk of Manishtusu** records the crown's large-scale **purchase**
  of fields in four townships (at a fair, non-confiscatory price) to redistribute
  to royal "veterans" — a monument to the dispossession of older owners in favour
  of the king's adherents.
- **Akkadian becomes a written language.** Under Agade, the
  [[writing-and-record-keeping|cuneiform script]] — invented for Sumerian — was
  first used to write a **Semitic language (Old Akkadian)**, beginning cuneiform's
  long career as the common written medium of the whole Near East. Agade tablets
  are instantly recognizable: rectangular, finely prepared, with beautifully ruled
  and spaced signs.
- **Art at a peak.** The same mastery shows in the great **cylinder seals** and in
  the **Victory Stele of Naram-Sin**, one of the masterpieces of ancient art.

## Long-distance reach: Magan, Meluhha, and the Indus

Agade marks the **high point of Mesopotamia–Indus contact**. Sargon boasted that
"the ships of **Meluhha**, **Magan**, and **Tilmun** moored at the quay of Agade."
Magan (the Oman coast, source of copper and *mes*-wood) and **Meluhha** (most
likely the **Harappan** Indus civilization, source of carnelian, ivory, gold, and
lapis) appear in maritime trade more in this period than in any other; Indus seals
and etched carnelian beads cluster at Ur and Eshnunna in Agade-period strata. (Yet
the empire also shows a curious **falling-off in tin and lapis** supply, perhaps
because conquest disrupted the very trade it sought to control.)

## Collapse

The dynasty unravelled under [[naram-sin|Naram-Sin's]] son **Shar-kali-sharri**,
caught between the **Amorites** pressing from the northwest (he "overcame
the Amorite in Basar," the Jebel Bishri) and the **Gutians** descending from the
Zagros, with **Elam** breaking away under Kutik-In-Shushinak. The king-list marks
the chaos with its one rhetorical cry — *"Who was king? Who was not king?"* — as
four claimants seized the throne within three years. See
[[gutian-invasion|the fall of Akkad and the Gutian supremacy]].

## Liverani: structure, trade, and the limits of conquest

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] reads Agade as a structurally
**intermediate** form — "halfway between the system that supported trade in the Uruk phase
and the more compact, territorial organisation" of the [[third-dynasty-of-ur|Third Dynasty
of Ur]] — which is also its weakness: it gave the Sumerian south too many openings to
rebel and the periphery too much autonomy. Control was zoned: the **centre** (Akkad to the
Gulf) ruled *indirectly* through local *ensi* under Akkadian oversight; the **periphery**
through treaties, appointed *šakkanakku*, or strongholds (Naram-Sin's palace at Nagar/Tell
Brak). He emphasises that settlement distribution in Lower Mesopotamia barely changed
between Early Dynastic III and the Akkadian period — the big demographic and economic
shifts came with the empire's **crisis**, not its formation.

He rejects the old **"Semitic conquest"** reading: Semites were long present in Mesopotamia,
ethnic difference did not drive the wars, and Sargon was no ethnic champion. Akkad's rise he
ties instead to an **environmental** trend (the pull northward to control water as Upper
Mesopotamia was more exploited) and an **ethno-linguistic** one (Akkadian belonged to the
wide Semitic family from Syria to Arabia, better placed to absorb incoming groups than
isolated Sumerian). His sharpest interpretive claim concerns trade: by **directly seizing**
commercial networks ([[ebla|Ebla]], [[elam|Elam]], Magan) rather than working through
intermediaries, the Akkadian kings **damaged** them — those regions had their own
extraction, processing, and exchange systems that conquest disrupted without replacing (the
cut-off of Afghan lapis and Central Asian tin via a shattered Elamite confederation is his
prime example). Sargon's conquest of Lower Mesopotamia was still mainly political/military;
**Manishtusu and Naram-Sin** made the shift to absorbing the commercial networks directly.
See [[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]].

## Historiography

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014|*Egypt, Greece, and Rome*]], 2014)
frames this textual/archaeological gap as historiographically significant in its own
right: "the extent of surviving written texts for the Akkadian empire marks an
important moment in history, when a civilization is known more fully through its
texts than its archaeology" — the site of Agade itself remains unidentified despite
abundant cuneiform records.

The Sargonic kings are "vast but dim": their own inscriptions survive mostly as
**later copies made at Nippur**, and most of what is "known" comes through
**omen-literature and didactic legend** (the birth-legend of Sargon set adrift in
a basket; *King of the Battle*; *The Curse of Agade*) composed centuries later
with a moralizing "philosophy of history." Gadd's account carefully separates the
contemporary inscriptional core from this tradition. Dates follow the
[[mesopotamian-chronology|middle chronology]]. The empire's place as a template for
[[imperialism]] — and the question of whether its fall was political, ethnic, or
[[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism|climatic]] — are live. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
