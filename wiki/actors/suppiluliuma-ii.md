## Overview

Suppiluliuma II was the last Great King of [[hittites|Hatti]], son of [[tudhaliya-iv]] and successor — after a brief reign of perhaps only a year — to his brother Arnuwanda III, who died without issue. His reign, roughly c.1209–1190 BCE (dates very approximate), is documented only in fragments: two cuneiform texts copied from hieroglyphic originals, a handful of Ugaritic administrative documents, and the Südburg inscription at [[hattusha]]. He succeeded to a kingdom already under severe structural stress and presided over the final collapse, though the precise circumstances of that collapse remain contested.

His accession was accompanied by unrest. An oath of loyalty records that the scribe swore allegiance to "only the descendants of my lord Suppiluliama" and explicitly excluded descendants of Suppiluliuma I, Mursili, Muwattalli, and Tudhaliya — testimony to the dynastic fractures still unresolved (KUB xxvi 32+ iii 10'–14'). Suppiluliuma's first priority appears to have been restoring internal political stability.

## The Südburg Inscription and Western Campaigns

The most vivid document of Suppiluliuma II's reign is an inscription in hieroglyphic script on Chamber 2 of a cult complex discovered at [[hattusha]] in 1988, located on the city's so-called Südburg, just south of the royal acropolis. The chamber is adorned with reliefs of a deity and the king, and was probably a symbolic entrance to the Underworld (kaskal.kur) rather than a tomb. Following Hawkins' interpretation, the inscription records Suppiluliuma's conquest and annexation of Wiyanawanda, Tamina, Masa, Lukka, and Ikuna — all within or near Lukka territory in southwestern Anatolia — and also the conquest and annexation of [[tarhuntassa]].

These western campaigns have been interpreted two ways: as determined maintenance of Hittite authority in the west, or as rearguard actions to protect the homeland's southern and southeastern flanks. A recent suggestion holds that [[tarhuntassa]]'s ruler was Suppiluliuma's chief opponent and had actually seized the listed lands, forcing Suppiluliuma to reconquer them. "Rather than reflecting self-confidence and security," in Singer's words, his focus on these campaigns and on the mortuary shrine of his father was "a mute plea to the gods and spirits to grant salvation where the sceptre and the sword have failed."

The Südburg inscription also documents that Suppiluliuma dealt with defiant vassals: the new king of [[ugarit]], Ibiranu, had failed to send messengers or tokens of loyalty after his accession, provoking royal anger communicated through "Pihawalwi, son of the king" (RS 17.247). Syrian affairs were largely delegated to the viceroy at [[carchemish]], Talmi-Teshub, son of Ini-Teshub, with whom Suppiluliuma drew up a treaty (surviving only in fragments).

## The Sea Battles off Alasiya

The most dramatic episodes of Suppiluliuma II's reign are three naval engagements fought off the coast of Alasiya (Cyprus), recorded in a cuneiform copy of a second hieroglyphic inscription from his reign. The Hittite forces — using a war fleet almost certainly supplied by [[ugarit]] — engaged enemy ships described as "ships of Alasiya" in three consecutive battles:

> "The ships of Alasiya met me in the sea three times for battle, and I smote them; and I seized the ships and set fire to them in the sea. But when I arrived on dry land(?), the enemies from Alasiya came in multitude against me for battle." (KBo xii 38 iii 1'–13', trans. Güterbock)

Whether these "enemies from Alasiya" were native Alasiyans, foreigners using Alasiyan ports, or elements associated with what Egyptian records call the [[sea-peoples]] is unclear. Bryce identifies them tentatively with the broader disruption of eastern Mediterranean supply routes that was threatening Hatti's grain supply. Tudhaliya IV had mounted an earlier campaign against Alasiya with only temporary effect; Suppiluliuma had to repeat the operation.

## The End of Hattusa

Historical records of the Hittite kingdom "finish abruptly in Suppiluliuma II's reign with the account of the naval battles off the coast of Cyprus and the record of military events in the Südburg inscription." The physical end of [[hattusha]] followed, probably in the very early years of the twelfth century BCE. Archaeological evidence shows widespread devastation by fire on the royal acropolis, in the temples of both Upper and Lower Cities, and along stretches of the fortifications.

However, the current director of excavations at Hattusa, Dr Seeher, proposes a less dramatic scenario: a gradual abandonment of the capital by the royal family and palace bureaucracy, who took with them "all their valuable and portable possessions, including the kingdom's most important official records." The remaining population scavenged through what was left. Only after the city was largely derelict was it put to the torch. "The decline, abandonment, and final destruction of Hattusa probably occurred in the very early years of the twelfth century. The whole process may have taken no more than a few months."

Whether the [[kaska]] people delivered the coup de grâce — as they had sacked the capital in an earlier era — is possible but unproven.

## Aftermath: Continuity at Carchemish

Suppiluliuma II's viceroy at [[carchemish]], Talmi-Teshub — great-great-grandson of [[shuppiluliuma-i]] — was succeeded by his son Kuzi-Teshub, who styled himself "Great King," acknowledging the extinction of the central dynasty at [[hattusha]]. The Hittite royal line thus survived in the collateral Carchemish branch, providing the genealogical and cultural bridge to the [[neo-hittite-states]] that persisted through the Iron Age. Inscriptions from Arslantepe extended the genealogy further, naming two brothers, kings of Melid (Malatya), as grandsons of Kuzi-Teshub.

## Counterfactual Significance

Suppiluliuma II's sea victories off Alasiya may have temporarily preserved the eastern Mediterranean grain routes crucial to Hatti's survival. Had he failed those battles, Hattusa might have collapsed even sooner — though the structural crisis of grain shortage, internal fragmentation, and hostile Tarhuntassa made collapse appear near-inevitable regardless of any single military outcome.

## Historiography

Suppiluliuma II is among the most poorly documented of Hittite Great Kings: the Südburg inscription and the Alasiya sea-battle tablet are the primary administrative testimonies to his reign, supplemented by a handful of Ugaritic texts (RS 17.247 etc.). Hawkins' hieroglyphic readings of the Südburg inscription have been central to reconstructing the western campaigns, but remain subject to scholarly debate about scope and sequence. The question of whether the Südburg campaigns were offensive or defensive remains open. Seeher's abandonment hypothesis for Hattusa, based on recent excavation evidence, has significantly revised older "catastrophe" narratives.
