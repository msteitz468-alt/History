# Edward III, King of England (r. 1327–1377)

**Edward III** was the great warrior-king of fourteenth-century England, whose long reign opened
the **[[hundred-years-war-1337|Hundred Years War]]** and rebuilt the authority of the crown after
the disasters of his father. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (W. Mark Ormrod, Ch. 11a) presents him
as "a very different king" from Edward II — one who "fitted perfectly into the contemporary image
of kingship" and whose honour "became a byword... Edward III was a man who could be trusted."

## Accession after the Fall of Edward II

Edward came to the throne prematurely in 1327, after the **deposition and murder of his father
[[edward-ii-of-england|Edward II]]** — the king whose "obsessive favouritism" (Piers Gaveston, the
Despensers) and humiliating defeat at **[[battle-of-bannockburn-1314|Bannockburn]] (1314)** had
produced civil war and his overthrow by Queen Isabella and Roger Mortimer. The young Edward III
threw off the Isabella–Mortimer regency in 1330 and made his reign a "conscious reaction" to his
father's: a conventional, chivalrous, art-patronising king who used Windsor (the "Versailles of its
age") and court ceremony to enhance "the mystique and majesty of his office."

## War and Chivalry

Edward "excelled at the arts of war," discovering his talent on the **Scottish campaigns** of the
1330s before exploiting it in France. He launched the **Hundred Years War** (from 1337) on his
claim to the French crown and the defence of Aquitaine, winning the era's most celebrated
victories — **Sluys (1340), Crécy (1346), Calais (1347), Poitiers (1356)** — through the longbow
and dismounted men-at-arms, and forcing the **Treaty of Brétigny (1360)**. He institutionalised the
chivalric ethos in the **Order of the Garter (1348)**, the model [[chivalry|princely order of
chivalry]].

## Domestic Success

Ormrod credits Edward with "a remarkably successful domestic policy that considerably enhanced the
authority of the crown" — but one "carefully tailored to accommodate the interests and enthusiasms
of the political community." The vast cost of war was met by bargains with the realm: regular
**parliamentary taxation** in exchange for concessions, which matured **[[english-parliament|parliament]]**
(and the **Commons**) as a taxative and legislative assembly. His later years were clouded by the
**Black Death** (from 1348), military stalemate, the dominance of his mistress Alice Perrers, and
the **Good Parliament (1376)**, but his reign is the high-water mark of the late-medieval English
monarchy's prestige.

## Significance

Edward III is the model of the successful late-medieval warrior-king: he turned the energies of a
chivalric aristocracy outward into a national war, built consent at home, and forged — through both
the war and the partnership with parliament — a more cohesive English polity and a sharpened sense
of English national identity. His failure to secure a stable succession (the early death of the
Black Prince) left the throne to his grandson **[[richard-ii-of-england|Richard II]]**.
