## Overview

Shihâbuddin Yaḥyâ Suhravardî, called Shaykh al-Ishrâq ("Master of Illumination"; c. 1154–1191), was the founder of the Ishrâqi (Illuminationist) school of Islamic philosophy — one of the two great Sufi metaphysicians of the late twelfth century alongside [[ibn-arabi]]. He is to be distinguished from his contemporary 'Umar Suhravardî (1145–1234), the conservative Sufi teacher and political adviser to the caliph al-Nasir; the two shared a town of origin (Suhraward in northwestern Iran) but not a philosophical orientation. He died at Aleppo, executed on suspicion of Isma'ili sympathies, at approximately 36 years of age.

## Philosophical Program

Suhrawardi's major work, *Hikmat al-Ishraq* (The Wisdom of Illumination), presents a comprehensive metaphysical system grounded in the concept of Light as the universal substance of being. The system is "tightly worked out — nothing fails to support his distinctive vision" (Hodgson).

**The ontology of Light.** For Suhrawardi, "at every level of being, and not only on the level of human moral consciousness, God intervenes to draw creatures toward His own norms." This divine activity is Light — simultaneously the medium of perception, the energy of physical processes, and the substance of rational consciousness. He identifies "three seemingly different sorts of clarity: that of logical rational consciousness; that of the formal essences of whatever is; and that of the physical medium of perception — light." The darkness of inert materiality is merely potentiality, into which Light shines to produce all that appears.

**Reconciliation of Plato and Aristotle.** Suhrawardi reinterpreted Aristotle's "forms" as identical with Plato's "ideas" — both pointing to transcendent essences that are simultaneously embodied in things. He used this reconciliation not to show philosophical harmony but to subordinate Aristotelian analysis to a higher Platonic-Mazdean vision. Aristotle's system was "of a lower order" — more accessible exoterically but pointing toward insights that exceeded it.

**Mazdean angels and neo-Platonic intellects.** Suhrawardi innovated by interpreting the Mazdean tradition of angelic guides (each substance and person having a guardian angel representing its ideal form) within a neo-Platonic framework, identifying the greater angels with the neo-Platonic intellects and the planets. The result was neither orthodox Mazdean nor standard neo-Platonic, but Suhrawardi's own synthesis.

**Eschatology.** His treatment of the soul combined Islamic monotheistic judgment with a doctrine resembling transmigration: souls entrapped in matter must purify themselves or face "ever darker entrapment as they are reborn in lesser beings." This is distinctively Islamic in its sense of urgency and individual judgment while incorporating cosmological elements from Mazdean and Platonic traditions.

## Sources and Claims

Suhrawardi claimed to be reviving ancient wisdom suppressed since Aristotle — the "unitive wisdom of ancient ages" that he traced through Plato back to the Hermes of the Hermetic corpus (associated with the pre-Platonic priestly traditions). He positioned himself as the heir of both Plato and the esoteric Iranian tradition. His insistence that Falsafa, taken seriously as a preparatory discipline, was necessary for mystical insight — that "mystical experience without the underpinning of the metaphysical understanding offered by Falsafah could lead the speculative mystic sadly astray" — placed him between the strict Sufis and the Faylasûfs while belonging fully to neither.

## Death

Suhrawardi was "an intellectually self-confident young man, eager to persuade the rulers of the world of his opinions" (Hodgson) who settled at Aleppo in his thirties. There he encountered a Shari'ah-minded revivalism energized in part by the Crusades. The 'ulama hostile to speculative Sufism "were able to win the ear of Saladin, whose son was amir at Aleppo," and Suhrawardi was executed on suspicion of being in league with the Isma'ilis, at 36 or 38.

## Legacy

The Ishrâqi school that Suhrawardi founded survived him and became a significant strand in later Islamic philosophy, particularly in Safavid-era Iran (16th–17th centuries). His influence was distinct from but parallel to [[ibn-arabi]]'s: where Ibn al-'Arabi generated the dominant vocabulary of Sufi metaphysics (wahdat al-wujud and the perfect man), Suhrawardi generated the distinctive philosophical tradition of the "philosophers of light" who sought to integrate mystical experience with rigorous metaphysics.

Hodgson treats Suhrawardi and Ibn al-'Arabi together as the period's two "great Sufi metaphysicians" who "developed two contrasting forms of unitive doctrine." The contrast: Suhrawardi was more systematic and cosmological, grounding unity in Light; Ibn al-'Arabi was more comprehensive and personally experiential, grounding unity in Love.
