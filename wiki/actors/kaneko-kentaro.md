## Overview

Kaneko Kentarō (1853–1942) was a Harvard-educated Japanese statesman dispatched to the United States during the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]] to maintain pro-Japanese sentiment among American opinion-makers and to cultivate President Theodore Roosevelt as a mediator. His personal relationship with Roosevelt, dating from their Harvard days, made him an indispensable backchannel in the diplomacy that led to the [[treaty-of-portsmouth-1905|Treaty of Portsmouth]]. Alongside Suematsu Kenchō (dispatched to Britain), he represented the Meiji government's sophisticated approach to managing international public opinion during a major war.

## Role and Relationship with Roosevelt

Kaneko's first White House meeting with Roosevelt occurred on March 26, 1904. Despite thirty or more persons waiting to see the president, Roosevelt came out immediately on seeing Kaneko's card, ushered him into his office, and told him "he had been eagerly awaiting his visit and had wondered why Kaneko had not appeared earlier" (Keene, ch. 54). This warmth reflected Roosevelt's genuine admiration for Japan, shaped in part by his reading of *Bushidō* by Nitobe Inazō — "one of his favorite books."

Kaneko was in New York when news of [[battle-of-tsushima-1905|Tsushima]] arrived. He immediately sent Roosevelt a note declaring it "the greatest naval victory of the world's history." Roosevelt replied: "This is the greatest phenomenon the world has ever seen. Even the battle of Trafalgar could not match this. I could not believe it myself…. I grew so excited that I myself became almost like a Japanese, and I could not attend to official duties" (Keene, ch. 55).

### The Bear Skin Exchange

One of the more colorful moments in the diplomacy involved Kaneko's visit to Roosevelt in March 1905, shortly after the Japanese victory at Mukden. Learning that Roosevelt was about to leave for Colorado to hunt bears, Kaneko told the president: "The Russian fleet is about to enter the Pacific, and there is certain to be a great naval battle with our fleet in the near future. If you should kill a bear, this will be an augury of victory for the Japanese fleet." Roosevelt said simply, "I fully intend to." After the conclusion of peace, the president gave Kaneko the skin of a bear he had shot, with a handwritten note, and asked Kaneko to present it personally to [[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]] (Keene, ch. 54).

### Mediating Peace

After Tsushima, Kaneko was the Japanese channel through which the formal request for Roosevelt's mediation was transmitted. He kept Roosevelt informed of Japanese intentions and helped Roosevelt understand the limits of what Japan would accept in peace terms. His role as a trusted interlocutor — rather than a formal diplomat — gave him unusual latitude to communicate frankly in a way that formal diplomatic channels could not.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Kaneko's personal relationship with Roosevelt, the mediation that produced the Portsmouth Treaty might have fallen to French initiative (France was Russia's ally and was actively trying to organize its own conference). Roosevelt's active, sympathetic mediation was not inevitable — it depended on personal trust. Kaneko cultivated and sustained that trust throughout the war.
