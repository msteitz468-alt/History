# The Avars and the Slav Settlement of the Balkans

The steppe-nomad **Avar khaganate** and the **Slav** peoples whose combined pressure broke the Roman
**Danube frontier** in the later sixth century and **permanently de-Romanized the Balkans** — one of the
great demographic transformations of late antiquity, and the immediate prelude to the seventh-century
collapse. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 23 (Whitby, **The Balkans and Greece**).

## The long Balkan crisis

The Balkans had been under pressure since the late fourth century — the [[huns|Huns]] reaching the Danube by
c. 420, the loss of **Pannonia** (the strategic corridor between East and West, its capital Sirmium decaying
to "an impoverished refuge"), and a settlement pattern already shifting from open villages to **walled towns
and upland refuges**. The accommodation of tribes under [[theodosius-i|Theodosius I]] left semi-autonomous
groups south of the river, eroding the frontier from within. Prosperity survived better the further one went
from the Danube (the Thracian plains, fed by Constantinople's demand).

## The Avars

The **Avars**, a steppe confederation fleeing westward, reached the Danube under Justinian and his
successors, extracting **subsidies** and demanding land. Allying with the **Lombards**, they **destroyed the
Gepids (567)** and seized the Carpathian basin (Pannonia) — whereupon the Lombards, fearing them, departed
for [[lombards|Italy (568)]]. Under their **khagan** the Avars built a tribute empire over the Danubian
peoples, and clashed with the empire over the key fortress of **Sirmium**, which **fell to them in 582**
after a long siege — opening the Balkans.

## The Slavs

The **Slavs** were, in Whitby's judgement, "the most significant of the new groups" — agriculturalists
(archaeologically elusive) living north of the lower Danube, whom Byzantine writers divided into **Slavs and
Antes**. From the 540s they **raided ever deeper** into Thrace, Illyricum and Greece; by the later sixth
century, often driven or led by the Avars, their incursions became **settlement**. The empire's resources,
consumed by the Persian war and Justinian's western commitments, could not hold the line; the
[[successors-of-justinian|emperor Maurice's]] hard Balkan campaigns (recorded with the *Strategikon*) won
victories but no lasting security.

## Collapse

When **Maurice was overthrown (602)** and the Danube army withdrawn, the frontier **collapsed**. Avars and
Slavs poured across; the cities of the interior were largely abandoned or destroyed; and over the following
decades the **Slavs settled the Balkans** permanently, displacing or absorbing the Romanized provincial
population (which survived as enclaves and as the ancestors of the Vlachs, while Greek urban life clung to
the coasts and islands). The Balkans ceased to be a Roman land.

## Significance

The Avar–Slav transformation is, with the [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam|Arab conquests]], one of the two
great ruptures CAH XIV's terminus c. 600 sits before. It **stripped the eastern empire of its European
hinterland** and reshaped the ethnography of south-eastern Europe for good — the origin of the modern Slavic
Balkans. It is the clearest northern instance of the volume's theme: an over-extended empire unable to defend
all its fronts at once.
