# The Nazi Concentration Camp System (KL), 1933–1945

This is the canonical page for the SS concentration-camp system (the **KL**,
from *Konzentrationslager*) across its whole history. The founding and pre-war
sections below (Evans/Shirer) cover 1933–1939; the Wachsmann synthesis that
follows carries the system through the war, mass extermination, and collapse.
Individual camps have their own pages ([[dachau]], [[sachsenhausen]],
[[buchenwald]], [[ravensbruck]], [[auschwitz]], [[majdanek]]); the genocide of
European Jewry is on [[the-holocaust]].

## The Founding Moment: Dachau, March 1933 (Evans, 2003)

Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]) on the origin:
[[himmler-heinrich|Himmler]], made Provisional Police President of Bavaria on
9 March 1933, announced on 20 March "a concentration camp for political
prisoners" at Dachau; the first ~200 prisoners were trucked in from
Stadelheim and Landsberg on 22 March as townspeople watched. The SS took over
in early April under Hilmar Wäckerle: on 11 April the guards took four Jewish
inmates out and shot them as "escaping"; twelve inmates had been murdered or
tortured to death by end May — Munich medical staff, hearing a survivor's
account, called in the public prosecutor. Conditions in the SA/SS camps and
cellars of spring 1933 were "a makeshift sadistic anarchy" — fists, jackboots,
rubber truncheons rather than refined torture (the beating of Friedrich
Schlotterbeck and of the Köpenick councillor Marie Jankowski are Evans's
exemplary cases). Turnover was rapid: at Oranienburg 35% of inmates were held
1–4 weeks and under 0.4% over a year — rolling terror touching far more people
than any headcount shows.

**Long-planned, and publicly advertised**: Evans is explicit that "Dachau was
not... an improvised solution... but a long-planned measure." Hitler had
spoken in 1921 of putting Jews in "concentration camps" on the British model;
paragraph 16 of the intended 1923 putsch constitution assigned "security
risks and useless eaters" to "collection camps," resisters to be killed; the
Nazi press promised camps for SPD/KPD functionaries in August 1932; Frick
repeated the threat openly on 8 March 1933. The precedents Evans cites are the
British camps of the Boer War and the German army's camps for 14,000 Herero
(1904–7, 45% death rate; ~500 dying monthly at Swakopmund and Lüderitz Bay,
justified as eliminating "unproductive elements" — see
[[herero-nama-genocide-1904]]). Foundation announcements were widely
publicized — deterrence was the point.

## From wild camps to the SS system (Evans, 2005)

At least 70 improvised camps existed by early 1933, holding ~45,000 prisoners
(overwhelmingly Communists, Social Democrats, trade unionists); several hundred
died of maltreatment. Amnesties shrank the population — a third released on
31 July 1933 alone; only ~3,000 inmates system-wide by mid-1934 — and the SS
under [[himmler-heinrich|Himmler]] consolidated control in 1934–35. By August
1937 only four camps remained: Dachau, Sachsenhausen, Buchenwald, and
Lichtenburg (women; replaced by Ravensbrück, May 1939).

**Theodor Eicke** (Dachau commandant from 26 June 1933; Inspector of
Concentration Camps from 4 July 1934, days after personally shooting
[[rohm-ernst|Röhm]]) made Dachau the template: comprehensive regulations
(October 1933) prescribing hanging for "incitement," 25 strokes of the cane,
solitary on bread and water, tying to posts — the death-penalty clauses kept
confidentially as intimidation. Guard "Death's Head" units (from December
1934) were trained in "hardness"; the SS secured effective immunity from
prosecution for anything "behind the perimeter fence," and by 1936 the camps
were institutions beyond the law.

## The shift from political repression to "racial-social purification"

From February 1936 Hitler approved reorienting the SS/Gestapo mission from
suppressing the (crushed) left toward "purging the German race": habitual
criminals, "asocials," beggars, tramps, the "work-shy," homosexuals, Jehovah's
Witnesses, Sinti and Roma. By July 1937, 57% of Dachau's inmates were
non-political. Mass round-ups: the September 1933 "Reich beggars' week"
(~100,000 arrests); Himmler's March 1937 order seizing 2,000 "professional
criminals" without legal process; the 14 December 1937 "asocial" decree; the
April/June 1938 raids under [[heydrich-reinhard|Heydrich]] (>10,000 arrests —
Heydrich ordered 200 per district; Hamburg delivered 700). The triangle-badge
system was standardized in winter 1937–38 (red political, green criminal,
black asocial, violet Jehovah's Witness, pink homosexual, yellow overlay
forming a Star of David for Jews).

Death tolls climbed as the system hardened and swelled: Dachau 24 deaths
(1933) → 10 (1936) → 69 (1937) → 370 (1938); Buchenwald 48 (1937) → 771
(1938) → 1,235 (1939). After [[kristallnacht-1938|Kristallnacht]] ~30,000
Jewish men were forced in within a week, doubling Buchenwald's population;
Dachau deaths jumped to 115 in November and 173 in December 1938 alone. Total
camp population grew from ~7,500 to ~21,000 on the eve of war. New camps
Flossenbürg and Mauthausen (1938) were founded explicitly around quarry labour
for the SS "German Earth- and Stoneworks Company," feeding Hitler and
[[speer-albert|Speer]]'s building projects; Mauthausen, near Linz in annexed
Austria, was the harshest camp in the pre-1941 system.

Evans stresses a comparative point: through the mid-1930s the **state prison
system, not the camps, handled the bulk of political repression** (~14,000
designated political offenders in state prisons in summer 1937), with the
Justice Ministry cooperating in Gestapo re-arrests of released prisoners into
"protective custody."

## The System as a Whole (Wachsmann, *KL*, 2015)

Wachsmann's governing argument is that **"the main constant of the KL was
change"**: the camps had no fixed blueprint and no straight-line development,
but lurched through distinct phases driven by the shifting obsessions of the
Nazi leadership and its Camp SS managers. Over its whole history the SS ran
**27 main camps and over 1,100 satellite camps**; an estimated **2.3 million
people** passed through them and **over 1.7 million died** — nearly a million
of them Jews murdered at [[auschwitz|Auschwitz]]. Some 60,000+ men and women
served as Camp SS staff. Unlike the ~90% survival rate of the Soviet Gulag,
fewer than half of registered KL prisoners survived; Wachsmann argues the KL
were "largely made in Germany," not copied from the Gulag.

**Phases.** (1) *Early "wild" camps* (1933) and consolidation under
[[eicke-theodor|Eicke]]. (2) *The pre-war SS system* (1936–39), the pivot from
political repression to "racial-social purification," and the founding of the
purpose-built model camps [[sachsenhausen]], [[buchenwald]], and [[ravensbruck]].
(3) *The early-war descent into mass death* (1939–41) as new camps opened in
occupied Europe and mortality soared. (4) *1941, the turn to mass extermination*
— Action 14f13 (the "euthanasia"/T-4 murder of infirm prisoners, first at
Sachsenhausen in April 1941) and the mass execution of Soviet POWs (~40,000+),
during which the Camp SS became "professional executioners" and, at Auschwitz,
**invented the Zyklon B gas chamber**. (5) *The Holocaust enters the KL*
(1942–43): after the WVHA took over the camps ([[pohl-oswald|Pohl]], March 1942)
and Himmler substituted Jews for Soviet POWs as slave labour, Auschwitz and
[[majdanek]] became hybrid labour/death camps, while western camps were made
"free of Jews." (6) *[[forced-labor-nazi-germany|Slave labour]] and the
satellite explosion* (1943–44): after Hitler's September 1942 decision to let
prisoners work in outside factories, a vast web of *Außenlager* spread across
the Reich, driven by [[annihilation-through-labor|"annihilation through labour"]].
(7) *Climax and collapse* (1944–45): the system peaked at a record **714,211
prisoners** on 15 January 1945, then broke apart in the [[death-marches-1944|death
marches]] and the disaster camps (Bergen-Belsen, Buchenwald's "little camp"),
in which perhaps 300,000 died in four months.

**Structure.** From the mid-1930s each camp had a five-department Commandant
Staff (staff office; political office; protective-custody camp / camp compound
leader; administration; medical office), the Dachau model exported system-wide;
guard duties were strictly separated from camp administration. Prisoner
"self-administration" ran through the **Kapo system** — work, block, and
administrative prisoner-functionaries up to the camp elder — which the SS
increasingly leaned on as staff ratios worsened (Auschwitz reached 23 prisoners
per SS man by 1944). Prisoners were sorted by the standardized **coloured-triangle
badge system** (red political, green criminal, black "asocial," purple Jehovah's
Witness, pink homosexual, yellow overlay for Jews). The administrative nerve
centre was the **T-Building** at Oranienburg (the IKL, later WVHA Office Group D).

**Wachsmann's key correctives.** By the end of 1941 the essential *mechanics*
of the Holocaust — deportation straight to death, camouflage with fake showers,
poison gas, selection on arrival, gold-tooth extraction — were already in place
in the KL; yet he insists the murder of invalids and Soviet POWs was **not a
dress rehearsal** for the Holocaust ("reading history backward") — those
killings had their own logic, and no KL was designated to kill large numbers of
Jews until 1942. He likewise distinguishes the KL from the single-purpose
Operation Reinhard death camps (Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka), while showing the
two were more entangled than usually assumed.

## Historiography

Wachsmann (*KL*, 2015) is now the standard history; Evans's account
synthesizes the earlier Broszat/Orth institutional literature. Prisoner
memoirs (Walter Poller on Buchenwald; [[hoss-rudolf|Höss]]'s self-serving
commandant's memoir on the perpetrator side; Primo Levi and the buried
"Scrolls of Auschwitz" of the Sonderkommando on the victims') supply the
interior evidence. Wachsmann's own contribution is to treat the *entire* KL
system chronologically as one evolving organism — correcting both the
liberation-photograph image fixed on 1945 and the fragmentation of the field
into single-camp studies (a "giant unassembled puzzle").

## Shirer (1960) Additions

- Roughly 50 camps existed by end of 1933, mostly SA-run extortion/beating operations ("blackmail... ransom them to their relatives"); four documented cold-blooded murders (whipping, strangulation) at Dachau in spring 1933 came to light at Nuremberg, drawing protest even from the Munich public prosecutor — corroborating Evans's account of the April 1933 Dachau killings above.
- Dachau camp regulations, drafted 1 November 1933 by first commandant Theodor Eicke, are quoted by Shirer in full: Article 11 prescribed hanging for "agitators," including anyone documenting camp conditions for outside "atrocity" propaganda; Article 12 prescribed shooting or hanging for "mutineers," including for shouting during work.
- Camp population is estimated by Shirer at 20,000–30,000 at any time during the 1930s (pre-war); named camps: Dachau, Buchenwald, Sachsenhausen (replacing Oranienburg), Ravensbrück (women, Mecklenburg), Mauthausen (after the 1938 Austrian annexation) — broadly consistent with Evans's totals above, though Shirer's range runs somewhat higher for the mid-1930s. A Christmas Eve 1933 Hitler-announced amnesty for 27,000 inmates was largely subverted by Göring and Himmler; Frick's April 1934 attempt to restrict *Schutzhaft* arrests was overridden by Himmler.
- Mauthausen, established during the first weeks of the Anschluss (1938), recorded 35,318 officially listed executions over its six-and-a-half-year existence — Shirer's superlative, "the German concentration camp... with the largest number of officially listed executions," explicitly excludes the extermination camps in the East from the comparison.
