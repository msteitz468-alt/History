# Nicholas II of Russia

**Nicholas II** (1868–1918; r. 1894–1917) was the last Emperor of Russia. His reign witnessed rapid industrialization and agricultural reform under Witte and Stolypin, alongside military defeats in the Russo-Japanese War and World War I. His refusal to yield autocratic power and his mismanagement of the state during World War I led to the collapse of the Romanov dynasty in the February Revolution of 1917.

## Biography / Description

Nicholas was the eldest son of Emperor Alexander III. He was educated under the supervision of the conservative theorist Constantine Pobedonostsev, from whom he inherited a belief in the divine right of autocracy. Nicholas possessed a gentle, charming personal manner but lacked the strength of character, political flexibility, and intellectual depth required to govern a vast empire in a time of rapid social transformation. 

Upon his accession in 1894, Nicholas declared that hopes for a consultative assembly were "senseless dreams" and pledged to maintain autocracy as his father had. He was heavily influenced by his reactionary wife, Empress Alexandra, and later fell under the influence of the Siberian mystic Grigori Rasputin, who gained enormous political influence over the imperial family.

In 1915, during the military crises of the First World War, Nicholas assumed personal command of the Russian armies at the front. This fateful decision made him personally responsible for military defeats, while leaving the daily management of the government in Petrograd to Alexandra and Rasputin. As inflation, food shortages, and military losses mounted, the capital erupted in spontaneous strikes and bread riots in March 1917. When the reserve garrison mutinied and joined the demonstrators, Nicholas was forced to abdicate on March 15, 1917, ending three centuries of Romanov rule.

## Key Decisions and Achievements

Nicholas II inherited a rapidly industrializing but socially fissured empire. He supported Sergei Witte’s drive for factories, mines and banks; fifty thousand kilometres of rail were laid by 1914, including the Trans-Siberian. Agriculture remained dominated by the village commune; Stolypin’s reforms (encouraging exit from the commune by "strong and sober" households) had limited success. Nicholas reluctantly conceded a Duma after the 1905 Revolution but manipulated the Basic Law and electoral rules to secure a compliant assembly (dispersed First and Second Dumas; Third weighted toward gentry). He viewed the "labour question" as more threatening than the national question. Repression included field courts-martial after 1905 (Stolypin’s "necktie") and administrative exile. In foreign policy he pursued expansion in Asia, leading to humiliating defeat by Japan (1904–5, Portsmouth treaty). Alliance with France (1893) and 1907 Entente with Britain were balanced by trade with Germany, but rivalry with Austria-Hungary and Germany over the Balkans and Ottoman decline proved decisive.

In 1914 Nicholas entered the Great War on Serbia’s side after Austrian ultimatum; he anticipated a short victorious war to bind society. Secret treaties with Allies claimed the Dardanelles. He assumed personal command at Mogilëv in 1915, cutting himself off from Petrograd politics. Rasputin’s influence and Alexandra’s German origins fed treason rumours. By 1916 even the United Gentry reconsidered loyalty. Strikes resumed after Lena massacre (1912) and escalated in 1914 and 1916–17. Nicholas prorogued the Duma on 26 Feb 1917 as Petrograd erupted in general strike. Garrison troops joined demonstrators. On 2 March 1917, travelling from Mogilëv, he abdicated (first offering throne to son Aleksei, then Grand Duke Mikhail). The Romanov dynasty ended after 300+ years.

## Historiography

Service portrays Nicholas as hard-working but lacking vision or political skill; "hen-pecked" rumours exaggerated his independence from Alexandra yet he remained "a tsar" too austere to rabble-rouse. The regime’s failure to constitutionalize left it exposed; even Octobrists turned against him after Stolypin’s humiliation. Nicholas’s decision for war in 1914, despite warnings (e.g. Durnovo), was driven by dynastic honour. The collection’s coverage of tsarist Russia is strengthened by Service’s synthesis of political, social and economic strains leading to 1917 collapse. Gaps remain on non-Russian nationalisms and deeper social history from below.

### The Russo-Japanese War and the 1905 Revolution
- Driven by expansionist aims in the Far East (promoted by Witte's railway projects and imperial policy), Nicholas's government drifted into the **Russo-Japanese War (1904–05)**. The conflict resulted in catastrophic Russian defeats on land and sea, destroying public confidence in the regime.
- The defeat triggered the **Revolution of 1905**. In response to widespread strikes, peasant uprisings, and mutinies, Nicholas signed the **October Manifesto (1905)**, which promised civil liberties and a national legislative assembly (the **State Duma**).

### The Duma Period and Stolypin's Reforms
- Nicholas quickly limited the power of the new legislature by enacting the **Fundamental Laws of 1906**, which reaffirmed his autocratic authority and excluded the ministers from responsibility to the Duma.
- Following clashes with the first two reform-minded Dumas, Nicholas supported Prime Minister Pyotr Stolypin in the **Coup of June 1907**, changing the electoral law by imperial decree to favor the gentry and ensure a conservative Duma.
- He backed Stolypin's agrarian reform (1906–1911), which aimed to break up the peasant communes and establish a class of conservative peasant landowners.

### World War I and Autocratic Collapse
- In August 1914, Nicholas brought Russia into the **First World War** against Germany and Austria-Hungary. The war strained the empire to its absolute limits, mobilizing 15.5 million men and causing staggering casualties (1.65 million killed, 3.85 million wounded, and 2.41 million captured).
- Nicholas's refusal to collaborate with the Duma's Progressive Bloc or the public organizations (such as the Union of Zemstva and Towns), and his reliance on incompetent ministers (such as Ivan Goremykin and Vladimir Sukhomlinov), alienated both the liberal gentry and the military command.
- In March 1917 (February OS), his authority collapsed completely in Petrograd, leading to his abdication.

## Historical Significance

Nicholas II's historical significance lies in his role as the final ruler of imperial Russia, whose political rigidity and administrative isolation facilitated the downfall of his dynasty. While he presided over a period of significant economic development and a rich cultural revival (the Silver Age), his inability to integrate the rising middle class, the industrial proletariat, and the peasantry into a constitutional political system ensured that the strains of World War I would produce a revolutionary explosion rather than evolutionary reform. His abdication cleared the way for the Provisional Government and the subsequent Bolshevik seizure of power.
