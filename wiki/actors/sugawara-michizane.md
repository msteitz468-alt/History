# Sugawara no Michizane (845–903)

Sugawara no Michizane was a scholar-official and poet who rose to the second-highest
regularly filled ministerial office at the Heian court — Minister of the Right — under
Emperor Uda's patronage, before being driven into exile by Fujiwara no Tokihira in
901 and dying in Kyushu in 903. His career and fall are the most dramatic illustration
of the limits of non-Fujiwara advancement in the late [[heian-period|Heian court]]
and of the structural barriers the [[fujiwara-regency|Fujiwara regency]] placed before
rival talents. He is also known for his 894 memorial recommending the termination of
official Japanese embassies to Tang China. [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]]

## Career under Uda

Michizane was the scion of the scholarly Sugawara clan, which had no claim on
the highest ministerial offices in an age where "clan and family membership had
come generally to determine the offices for which a man was eligible." He had
served in provincial posts and held no central court position when Fujiwara no
Mototsune's death in 891 opened the way for Emperor Uda (r. 887–897) to reassert
a degree of imperial independence. Uda promoted him rapidly:

- By 896, when a daughter entered Uda's harem, Michizane had reached Middle Counselor.
- Before Uda's abdication, he was appointed Provisional Major Counselor.
- When Uda abdicated in 897 in favor of the twelve-year-old Emperor Daigo (885–930),
  Michizane and Fujiwara no Tokihira (871–909) were directed to share supervision of
  the government with powers similar to a *kampaku* (*nairan*).
- In 899 he was appointed **Minister of the Right** — "the second highest regularly
  filled office in the government and a totally unprecedented honor for a member of the
  Sugawara clan."

Michizane was reportedly alarmed by his own prominence: "even he considered [it]
beyond his station in life."

## The Embassy Proposal of 894

In 894 Michizane was chosen to lead a diplomatic mission to the Tang court, probably
partly in response to a request relayed from Chinese officials. Before the embassy
could depart, he submitted a memorial to Emperor Uda and the Council of State
recommending that official relations with Tang China be terminated. His stated
justifications were the chaotic disorder of the declining Tang empire and the
mounting hazards of the sea voyage. He quoted a letter from the monk Chūkan in China:

> "The Great T'ang is in a state of decline, and reported that the emperor is not at
> court [because of the rebellion] and foreign missions have ceased to come… those who
> arrived safely in China have never yet had to suffer there from hunger and cold.
> According to Chūkan's letter, however, that which has never yet happened now seems
> likely to occur."

The Council of State accepted his recommendation. In retrospect, the cancellation —
though not intended as a permanent policy — became the end of official Japanese
embassies to China for centuries, a turning point in East Asian diplomatic history.
The *Cambridge History* notes that Michizane's reasoning "was fundamentally sound
and especially convincing, very likely, to a somewhat impecunious court that may have
been less than eager to undertake the huge expenses."

## Fall and Exile

After Uda's abdication, Michizane's political protection dissolved. In 901 Tokihira
maneuvered Daigo into issuing an order demoting Michizane to a minor post in Kyushu.
He died in exile in 903.

## Posthumous Cult

Michizane became a vengeful spirit (*goryō*) in popular belief, and the succession of
disasters that struck the Fujiwara after his death — including deaths of Tokihira and
other court figures — were attributed to his curse. He was eventually deified as
**Tenjin**, the patron deity of scholarship, and enshrined at the Kitano Tenman-gū
in Kyoto. His case is one of the most prominent examples of the *goryō* (angry spirit)
belief system that was prominent in Heian court culture.

## Historical Significance

Michizane's career demonstrates both the possibilities and the hard ceiling of
merit-based advancement in Heian society. His rise under Uda represented the
most serious non-Fujiwara challenge to Fujiwara court dominance between Kammu's
reign and the rise of the warrior clans. Its failure consolidated the Fujiwara monopoly.
