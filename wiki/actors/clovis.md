# Clovis and the Frankish Kingdom

Founder-king of the **Merovingian Frankish kingdom** (r. c. 481–511), the barbarian ruler whose conquests
made the Franks masters of Gaul and whose conversion to **Catholic (Nicene) Christianity** — unique among
the major Germanic kings, who were Arian — set the Frankish kingdom on the path to becoming the most durable
of the successor states and the nucleus of medieval France. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]]
Ch. 5 (**Ian Wood**), drawing on the partisan narrative of **Gregory of Tours**.

## From Childeric to a Gallic kingdom

A Frankish presence had grown in north-eastern Gaul since the mid fourth century; as central Roman authority
dissolved, Frankish leaders built up personal followings. The most successful was **Childeric** (whose rich
furnished grave at Tournai survives); on his death (c. 481) his son **Clovis** took over a kingdom centred on
**Soissons**. Through campaigns whose chronology (from Gregory) is contentious, Clovis subdued the Alamans,
intervened in Burgundian dynastic wars, and made himself **master of most of northern Gaul and Aquitaine.**

## Vouillé and the defeat of the Visigoths

Clovis's best-known war was against the **Visigoths** in **507**. Allied with the Burgundian Gundobad, he
killed **Alaric II** at the **battle of Vouillé** near Poitiers and overran nearly all the Visigothic
territories in Gaul; only **[[theoderic-the-great|Theoderic's]]** Ostrogothic intervention (508) saved the
Mediterranean coast (Septimania) for the Visigoths and confined them thereafter largely to Spain. The
victory made Clovis the dominant power in Gaul; the emperor **Anastasius I** awarded him an **honorary
consulship (508)**, a mark of imperial recognition.

## The conversion

Clovis is famed as the **first Frankish ruler to accept Catholic Christianity** — decisively important
because the other great Germanic kings (Visigoths, Vandals, Ostrogoths, Burgundians) were **Arian**.
Gregory's dramatic story (a battlefield vow against the Alamans, baptism at Reims by Bishop Remigius) places
the baptism in the 490s, but it is now usually dated to **c. 508**, linked to the Visigothic war; Wood notes
recent arguments that Clovis flirted with — or even briefly held — **Arianism** first. Whatever the date and
route, his Catholicism made him an **acceptable partner to the Catholic Gallo-Roman aristocracy and
episcopate** and to Constantinople, and spared the Frankish kingdom the confessional fracture between Arian
ruler and Catholic ruled that weakened the Vandal and Visigothic regimes. (His attribution as author of the
*Lex Salica* is, Wood cautions, largely later myth.)

## The Merovingian succession

At Clovis's death (511) the kingdom was **partitioned among his four sons** (Theuderic, Chlodomer,
Childebert I, Chlothar I) — establishing the enduring Merovingian pattern of **divided kingship and
fratricidal rivalry**, in which each king had to win land and treasure by war to reward his followers. The
brothers nonetheless continued expansion, conquering the **Burgundian kingdom** (534) and absorbing
Provence, so that by mid-century the Franks dominated Gaul.

## Significance

Clovis is the **founder of the most successful barbarian successor kingdom.** His Catholicism aligned crown,
Gallo-Roman aristocracy and Church, giving the Frankish state a cohesion the Arian kingdoms lacked; his
conquests created the territorial core of medieval Francia. CAH XIV ends with the Merovingians as the rising
western power — the seed of the Carolingian order that the New Cambridge Medieval History takes up.
