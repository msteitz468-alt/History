## Role

Archimedes of Syracuse (287–212 BCE) was the greatest mathematician and physicist of
antiquity — a "creative mathematical genius of a far higher order than Euclid" (CAH VII.1
ch. 9, Lloyd) — and the supreme figure of [[hellenistic-science|Hellenistic science]].
Related to the ruling family of Syracuse under Hiero II, he worked there (not at
Alexandria, though he corresponded with Alexandrian scholars) as a man of means and leisure.

## Achievements

- **Pure mathematics**: in *Measurement of the Circle* he approximated π by inscribing and
  circumscribing polygons (Eudoxus' method of exhaustion); in *The Sand-Reckoner* he devised
  a notation for vast numbers; in his *Method of Mechanical Theorems* he used the idea of a
  figure "composed of indefinitely close parallel lines" balanced against a known figure — in
  effect an **anticipation of the integral calculus** — while insisting that the method
  *discovered* results which still had to be proved rigorously (e.g. the area of a parabolic
  segment).
- **Mechanics made geometry**: in *The Equilibrium of Planes* he gave the first rigorous
  deductive proof of the **law of the lever** and the theory of **centres of gravity**; in
  *On Floating Bodies* he founded **hydrostatics**, proving "Archimedes' principle" — treating
  statics and hydrostatics, in Lloyd's phrase, "as a branch of geometry... on the model of
  Euclid's *Elements*."
- **Inventions** (traditionally): the **Archimedean screw** for raising water, compound
  pulleys, and the war-engines that defended Syracuse against the Roman siege.

## Significance and Death

Archimedes embodies the tension at the heart of Hellenistic science between **theory and
application**. Plutarch reports that he "despised" engineering as "ignoble and vulgar" and
prized pure theory — yet his mechanical ingenuity was famous, above all in the **defence of
Syracuse** against Rome (213–211), where his cranes and catapults long held the besiegers at
bay. He was killed when the city fell (212), reputedly cut down by a Roman soldier while
absorbed in a geometrical diagram. His work — recovered and transmitted (notably via the
medieval *Archimedes Palimpsest*) — was foundational for the mathematics and physics of the
Scientific Revolution. See [[hellenistic-science]], [[syracuse]].
