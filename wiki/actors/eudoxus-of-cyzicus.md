# Eudoxus of Cyzicus

**Eudoxus of Cyzicus** (active c. 120–100 BCE) was the Greek navigator who first used the **monsoon sea-route** from the Red Sea to India — a hinge between the late [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]] and early Roman Indian Ocean trades. The narrative is preserved via **Posidonius** and retold by [[thonemann-hellenistic-age-2016|Thonemann]] (*The Hellenistic Age*, ch. 5). Not to be confused with the fourth-century astronomer **Eudoxus of Cnidus**.

## The India Voyages

Around **120 BCE**, late in the reign of Ptolemy VIII Physkon, Eudoxus came to [[alexandria|Alexandria]] as ambassador announcing a Cyzicus festival. An Indian shipwreck survivor, taught Greek at court, guided a royal expedition; Eudoxus was among those selected. He and his crew were the first Greeks to sail to India by the open-ocean monsoon route and returned about a year later with perfumes and precious stones.

**Monsoon mechanics:** March–September the south-west monsoon blows steadily from Somalia to India; in winter the winds reverse, allowing return to the mouth of the Red Sea.

A second voyage in **116/115 BCE** was blown to the Somali coast; the crew traded bread, wine, and figs for water and pilots, and found a horse-prow wreck later identified with the fishing-boat type of Gadeira (Cadiz) — evidence of Atlantic–Indian Ocean connections already in motion. Later Ptolemies and Romans richly exploited the route: vast Roman coin finds in southern India and Sri Lanka; Red Sea ports received Indian pepper, ivory, spices, and fabrics.

## West African End

Eudoxus later sold possessions to attempt West African circumnavigation; around **100 BCE** he set out again with two ships, tools, seeds, and carpenters, and was never heard of again.

## Significance

Before Eudoxus, Greek knowledge of the Indian Ocean was coastal and episodic (Alexander's admiral Nearchus had explored the Indus–Euphrates coastal route in 325 BCE after Greeks first grasped that the Indian Ocean was open sea). The monsoon link collapsed the journey from a coastal crawl into a seasonal ocean highway and underwrote the later Roman–Indian luxury trade. Thonemann pairs the voyage with [[ai-khanoum|Aï Khanoum]], [[olbia|Olbia]], and the Villa of the Papyri as four "encounters" at the edges of Hellenistic civilization.
