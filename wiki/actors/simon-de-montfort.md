# Simon de Montfort (c. 1208–1265)

**Simon de Montfort**, earl of Leicester, was "the first leader of a political movement in
English history to seize power and govern the country in the king's name."
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (D.A. Carpenter, Ch. 12) gives him two characteristics
that made him unique: an **unwavering adherence to the Provisions of Oxford** and a
**genius as a general**. He passed "in and out of history like a comet, trailing admiration
and debris in his wake."

## Career

A younger son of the **Simon de Montfort who led the [[albigensian-crusade-1209|Albigensian
Crusade]]**, he was a French noble who made good a claim to the earldom of Leicester and
married Henry III's sister Eleanor (1237). Eloquent, charismatic and demanding, he became
the leading figure of the **revolution of 1258**, which vested government in a council of
fifteen (the **Provisions of Oxford**). When [[henry-iii-of-england|Henry III]] overthrew
the Provisions, Montfort alone refused to accept the king's recovery of power, declaring he
would "rather die landless than depart from the truth."

Returning to lead a renewed reform movement, he defeated and captured Henry at the **battle
of Lewes** (May 1264) and became *de facto* ruler of England under conciliar control. But,
unable to keep the support of the great magnates — repelled by his "arrogance and ambition"
— his regime proved unsustainable. The defection of the new earl of Gloucester and the
escape of the **Lord Edward** ended it: Montfort was defeated and killed at **Evesham**
(August 1265), his body mutilated. He was thereafter revered by some as a saint and hated
by others "as the Devil incarnate."

## Significance: The First Elected Commons

Montfort's lasting importance lies in **representation**. In 1264 he ordered each county to
elect **two knights** to represent "all of the county" at parliament; in **1265** he
summoned knights again, this time **with burgesses from the towns** — "the first meeting of
the embryo House of Commons" (see [[english-parliament]]). To his enthusiasts his cause was
not constitutional but **national**: he had come to rescue the English from "extirpation by
foreigners," channelling the xenophobic tide of the 1250s–1260s (resentment of the
Lusignans, the Savoyards, Italian clerics and bankers) into a movement that, in 1263, forced
Henry to a "statute" banning foreigners from office. This period was "central to the
development of English national feeling."

His power rested on a small but devoted personal affinity, the resources of **London**
(where a revolution installed the middle and lower orders), the **gentry** (the "community
of the bachelery of England"), and many **reforming ecclesiastics** of the circle of
**Robert Grosseteste**. The lesson of 1258–1265 was double-edged: it showed "the
impossibility of keeping power from the king unless the magnates were united" (which they
never long were), but also taught "how the realm might be reformed and how it might be
represented in parliament."
