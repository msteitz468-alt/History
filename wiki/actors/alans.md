# Alans

An Iranian-speaking steppe people of the western Eurasian plains, classed by ancient
writers with the [[sarmatians|Sarmatians]] and the descendants of the
[[scythians|Scythians/Saka]] (Seneca, *Thyestes* 630; Ptolemy, *Geographia* 6.14).
Before the Hunnic upheaval they occupied the grasslands between the Don and the Volga.

## Overview and Role

When the [[huns|Huns]] surged onto the western steppe **c. 370 CE**, opening the
Migration Period, the Alans split into two streams (Kim et al. 2017, ch. 9): the
**European Alans**, who moved west among the displaced peoples of the *Völkerwanderung*,
and the **Caucasian Alans**, who relocated from north of the Black Sea into the Crimea
and the northern Caucasus, becoming mobile cattle-breeders and agro-pastoralists
concentrated in the Kuban drainage. They were renowned cavalry, serving at various times
alongside Romans, Parthians and Sasanians (Josephus, *Jewish Wars* 7.244–251).

The 10th-century Arab historian **al-Masʿūdī** described the Alan king as able to muster
30,000 horsemen over a densely settled kingdom: "The kingdom consists of an uninterrupted
series of settlements; when the cock crows (in one of them), the answer comes from the
other parts of the kingdom." In **450 CE** the Alans devastated the Iberian (Georgian)
kingdom under the boy-king Vakhtang, who confronted them six years later (456 CE), with
Persian aid, in a battle on the Aragvi (Rapp 2014).

## Archaeology and Identity

Alanic material culture in the northern Caucasus is centred on **catacomb (shaft-and-
chamber) tombs**, sometimes with horse burials, at sites such as Klin-Yar, the Zmeiskaia
necropolis (North Ossetia), and Beslan/Zilgi. Bone-chemistry study at Klin-Yar tracked an
Alanic-period migration of both men and women (contrasting an earlier male-only Sarmatian
infiltration), while the elite burial plot shows social continuity across the transition
(Härke and Belinskij 2012). Distinctive **hexagonal-section swords** are thought to have
arrived with Alanic groups migrating back from Europe after Attila's death (453 CE).

The equation of any given assemblage with "the Alans" is contested even in the Caucasus
heartland — M.P. Abramova cautioned that "the Alan culture of the North Caucasus cannot be
interpreted as something stable." Whether the new burial elements at the
[[samtavro-cemetery|Samtavro cemetery]] in Georgia reflect Alanic *people* moving south or
only the diffusion of styles is the subject of a dedicated study (see
[[samtavro-cemetery]]); the Sagona team argued cautiously for a "trickling" of Alanic
people, not fashion alone. Non-Christian Alanic religion (a solar-deity cult) is inferred
from grave amulets and from **Ossetian** folklore and language, the Ossetians being
regarded as Alanic descendants.

## Counterfactual Significance

As one of the peoples set in motion by Hunnic expansion, the Alans are a case study in the
volume's argument (Kim et al. 2017) that Inner Asian steppe dynamics propagated outward
across Eurasia — carrying cavalry technique, cranial-modification custom, and steppe
ritual (e.g. the deliberate breaking of bronze mirrors) from the Pontic-Caspian steppe into
both Latin Europe and the Caucasus frontier.

## Historiography

The Alans are known almost entirely through outside observers (Graeco-Roman, Armenian,
Georgian, Arab) and archaeology; they left no literature of their own. Ethnic
identification from grave form is methodologically fraught — the same catacomb type is
cautioned against as an ethnic marker (Barca 2012). See [[pastoral-nomadism]] and
[[hunnic-migrations]].
