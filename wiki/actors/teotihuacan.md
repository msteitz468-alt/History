# Teotihuacan

## Overview

**Teotihuacan** was the first multiethnic metropolis of central Mexico and the dominant
force in Mesoamerica during the Classic period (c. 250–550 CE). Located in the northeast
of the Basin of Mexico, it grew from a modest Formative-period settlement to become the
sixth-largest city in the world within three centuries, with a population of approximately
100,000–150,000 at its peak, occupying roughly 20 km² — an area comparable to downtown
Washington, DC. It rose to preeminence partly as a consequence of the volcanic destruction
of its rival **Cuicuilco**, which was buried by lava flows from the volcano Xitle around
the turn of the first century CE, shifting the political center of gravity in the Basin of
Mexico decisively northeastward (Snow, Gonlin, and Siegel 2020, Ch. 13).

## Geographic Setting and Rise

Teotihuacan was situated in the Basin of Mexico at an elevation just under 2,240 m,
surrounded by plentiful springs for irrigation, fertile soils, and abundant raw materials
including the prized green obsidian of the Pachuca Range — a resource it appears to have
controlled and leveraged as a foundation of its economy. Agricultural populations were
drawn to the area, especially after the fall of Cuicuilco. The city's layout followed a
precise grid, oriented 15°30' east of true north. Its central axis is the broad **Avenue of
the Dead** (Street of the Dead), along which the largest pyramids were built.

## Architecture and Monuments

The city's three monumental structures define its ceremonial core:

- **Pyramid of the Moon**: begun around 100 CE; built in seven stages, each covering the
  previous. Humans were sacrificed at each new construction episode; some victims were
  lavishly dressed, others naked and bound or decapitated.
- **Pyramid of the Sun**: begun around 150 CE; one of the largest structures in the New
  World. Its flat, plastered top suggests open-air ritual use rather than an enclosed temple.
- **Temple of Quetzalcoatl**: constructed inside the huge walled **Ciudadela** compound
  around 200 CE. Its construction involved the sacrifice and burial of approximately 200
  individuals. The talud-tablero façade, later exposed by archaeologists, is one of
  Teotihuacan's most celebrated architectural features. The Ciudadela was large enough to
  hold the entire city's population on special occasions.

Beyond the ceremonial core, the Teotihuacan archaeological mapping project (Millon 1973)
documented approximately 2,000 apartment complexes, each housing 60–100 residents, arrayed
around the urban core. Surrounding these were neighborhood centers managed by an intermediate
elite class. The outer ring of the city contained **foreign ethnic enclaves** from Oaxaca,
Michoacán, and Veracruz, making Teotihuacan a genuinely multiethnic city.

## Political and Social Organization

Teotihuacan's form of government remains incompletely understood — how it was ruled is
described by specialist Linda Manzanilla as "one problem still unsolved." The city appears to
have had a **corporate organization** at both the base (corporate groups within apartment
compounds) and the summit (possibly a ruling council rather than a single dynastic ruler),
unlike the overtly dynastic Maya city-states. The city may have been divided into four
districts. This corporate, non-dynastic character is what makes Teotihuacan exceptional in
Mesoamerica.

Manzanilla describes it as "the octopus type" of state: the city as head, with corridors of
allied sites linked to sumptuary-goods provisioning regions as tentacles, including a site
controlling the green obsidian of the Pachuca Range and a major center of pilgrimage.

## Influence on the Maya and Zapotec

Teotihuacan exerted significant influence across Mesoamerica. Zapotec inscriptions at
[[zapotec|Monte Albán]] depict leaders receiving visitors wearing distinctive Teotihuacan
tassel headdresses, indicating diplomatic contact. The city indirectly influenced the
development of Puuc region Maya cities from 300–600 CE. The [[maya-civilization|Classic
Maya]] city of Tikal shows evidence of Teotihuacan influence in its political iconography.

## Collapse

Two main construction phases define the city's history: the **Tlamimilolpa phase**
(200–350 CE) with polychrome paintings in public areas, and the **Xolalpan phase**
(350–550 CE), characterized by buildings painted predominantly in red. Around 550 CE, the
city's central public buildings were systematically burned and religious shrines destroyed.
Palaces were burned, idols smashed. No evidence of an external invasion has been found;
the burning appears to have been the result of **internal revolt**. The resident population
dropped significantly, though the city was not entirely deserted; many residential compounds
survived. Teotihuacan then declined from its position as the preeminent Mesoamerican center.
Nearby polities met similar ends: Xochicalco was sacked around 900 CE; Tula around 1150 CE.

## Historiography

Teotihuacan lacks the deciphered writing system that illuminates Maya dynastic history.
The large **Teotihuacan Archaeological Mapping Project** (Millon 1973) produced the
foundational description of the city's extent and structure. Specialist research by Linda
Manzanilla on the apartment compound system and the corporate social organization has
reshaped understanding of the city's social structure. Karl Taube and James Langley have
worked on the notational system (approximately 120 signs on murals and pottery) that may
constitute a proto-writing system, though it lacks the elaborated calendrics of contemporary
Maya culture. Approximately 120 such signs have been identified. The question of dynastic
versus corporate rulership remains the central unresolved debate. See
[[snow-gonlin-siegel-archaeology-native-north-america-2020]] for the synthesis used here.
