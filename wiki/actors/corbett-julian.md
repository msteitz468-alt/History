# Julian S. Corbett

Sir Julian Stafford Corbett (1854–1922) was a British civilian naval historian
and strategic theorist, lecturer at the Royal Naval War College, and the author
of *Some Principles of Maritime Strategy* (1911) — the foundational statement of
the British or "maritime" school of strategy and the principal English-language
counterweight to [[mahan-alfred-thayer|Mahan's]] doctrine of [[sea-power]]. His
theory of [[maritime-strategy]] is the subject of its own page; this page treats
the man, his standing, and the controversy that surrounded his work.

## Role

Corbett never served in the navy. He came to the subject as a historian and man
of letters, and according to the editorial framing of his work "had never been
able to surmount completely the naval service's distrust of civilian experts."
His career was built through lecturing at the Royal Naval War College (the "War
Course") and through a sequence of scholarly histories: *Drake and the Tudor
Navy* (1898), a study of the Seven Years' War (1907), and a study of *Trafalgar*
(1910). The last of these, "with its broad strategic analysis of the entire 1805
campaign and not just the single battle off Cape Trafalgar, appeared to denigrate
that action's meaning and thus Nelson's image" — and so provoked the hostility
that would dog *Some Principles*.

His standing in the service rested above all on "his close connection to Lord
John Fisher," whose naval reforms divided the officer corps. Fisher's critics —
"the 'Syndicate of Discontent,' as Fisher described the critics of his reforms" —
"resented" Corbett as a Fisher partisan. He would later become the official British
historian of the naval side of the First World War. His biographer (Donald
Schurman) judged that it is in the "conception of the joint function of army and
navy … that his reputation ought chiefly to rest."

## Major Ideas and Their Consequences

Corbett's central achievement was to deny that naval strategy is autonomous. As
the editorial summary of his thought puts it, "naval strategy was not an autonomous
concept. It was, instead, part of maritime strategy" — "a broader approach to the
use of naval force in conjunction with both other military components and other
elements of national power to achieve political ends. This connection was Corbett's
most important achievement." When the hostile "Captain R. N." complained that a
civilian could not "assess at their proper value the influences which purely naval
considerations had," the editor notes that "Corbett's point, of course, was that
there was no such thing as 'purely naval considerations.'"

Reviews were "generally enthusiastic," and "one reviewer even dubbed the British
theorist 'Super Mahan.'" But the book also drew "the most vitriolic attack," and
its de-emphasis of the decisive fleet action and its emphasis on limited war were
warned to have "a disastrous effect upon the Navy."

## Thought

Corbett built his theory on a Clausewitzian foundation. He insisted that for "a
world-wide maritime Empire" naval and military strategy are not "distinct branches
of knowledge," but that "embracing them both is a larger strategy which regards
the fleet and army as one weapon." He defended theory itself against a service
hostile to abstraction, denying that it could give recipes for victory:

> "It does not pretend to give the power of conduct in the field; it claims no
> more than to increase the effective power of conduct. Its main practical value
> is that it can assist a capable man to acquire a broad outlook whereby he may
> be the surer his plan shall cover all the ground."

From Clausewitz he took the doctrine that war is a continuation of policy, and the
classification of wars as [[limited-war|limited]] and unlimited. He argued that the
sea gave Britain a unique capacity to wage limited war, concluding:

> "that limited war is only permanently possible to island Powers or between Powers
> which are separated by sea, and then only when the Power desiring limited war is
> able to command the sea to such a degree as to be able not only to isolate the
> distant object, but also to render impossible the invasion of his home territory."

He took Wellington in the Peninsula as the model of the British way — "in perfect
conditions he was applying the limited form to an unlimited war" — and he redefined
"command of the sea" as "nothing but the control of maritime communications," not
the conquest of water. (See [[maritime-strategy]] for the full development.)

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Corbett, the British strategic tradition — the use of a small army "as a
projectile to be fired by the navy," combined operations, the fleet in being, and
limited war by maritime power — would have lacked its theoretical articulation, and
the field would have been left to the Mahanian doctrine of decisive battle and the
concentrated fleet that "too many writers both at home and abroad quietly assume"
to be the only school. Schurman's verdict captures the contingency: before Corbett,
naval history "consisted of little more than a record of the glorious exploits of
the broadside bashers"; after twenty years' work "he left the two firmly connected"
— sea power and the growth of empire — "through his illumination of the way
statesmen and sailors had worked out a patter of seapower."

## Historiography

Corbett's work was "controversial … in its time," and the controversy was as much
about his person as his argument. The *Trafalgar* dispute was "reenergized by the
new book," led by Henry Spenser Wilkinson "of the Syndicate of Discontent," who
warned in 1912 that the book would have "a disastrous effect upon the Navy." The
sharpest assault came from an anonymous "Captain R. N.," who held that *Some
Principles* was "the 'crowning mistake' of Corbett's career," and complained that
"as a civilian he was obviously incompetent to assess at their proper value the
influences which purely naval considerations had" — the recurring charge that a
man who never served could not theorize about the service. The editor records that
a special Admiralty Committee had earlier been formed to investigate charges arising
from the *Trafalgar* controversy and that "the committee exonerated Corbett."

The editorial introduction also flags a substantive weakness later borne out in war:
"the reading … reflects Corbett's underestimation of the importance of convoys,
which would be the source of so much criticism in the next war." Corbett himself
hedged here, observing that "Modern developments and changes in shipping and naval
material have indeed so profoundly modified the whole conditions of commerce
protection, that there is no part of strategy where historical deduction is more
difficult or more liable to error." (The later Admiralty disclaimer blaming his
philosophy for the caution of the Jutland strategy, and Fred T. Jane's "you cannot
tell whether an egg is bad or good unless you are a hen" riposte, are part of the
wider Corbett historiography but are not contained in this source slice.)
