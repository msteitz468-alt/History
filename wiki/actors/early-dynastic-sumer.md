# Early Dynastic Sumer (the City-State System)

The world's first literate civilization: the network of competing Sumerian (and
Semitic-speaking) **city-states** of southern Mesopotamia during the Early
Dynastic period (c. 2900–2334 BCE) — Kish, Uruk, Ur, Lagash, Umma, Nippur,
Eridu, Shuruppak, Adab, Mari and others. It is the political world that emerged
from the Protoliterate ([[uruk|Uruk]] IV / Jamdat Nasr) and ended with its
absorption into the first empire under [[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon of Akkad]]. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XII, Frankfort, on the
Protoliterate; Ch. XIII, C. J. Gadd, on the cities).

## From prehistory to history

Frankfort's framing (Ch. XII): in prehistoric times the future centres of
civilization "showed no signs of being exceptional" — each shared a wide cultural
province. What set Sumer (and Egypt) apart was the **aggregate** of innovations
that crystallized in the Protoliterate: monumental temple architecture, the
cylinder seal, the wheel and wheel-made pottery, advanced metallurgy, the
[[temple-economy|temple-household economy]], and above all **[[writing-and-record-keeping|writing]]**.
"Mesopotamian history may be said to begin in the Protoliterate period even
though 'historical' texts do not reach back so far." Where Egypt's monuments
celebrate the [[divine-kingship|divine king]], Sumer's earliest monuments are
**temples** — the relationship between man and the gods is the organizing fact.

## The city-state and its offices

The Sumerian polity rested on a theology of **divine ownership**: each city
belonged to a god, whose temple-estate was managed on his behalf by a human
steward. The key titles:
- **lugal** ("great man," king) — often carrying the implication of an overlord;
  not attested before Early Dynastic times.
- **ensi** — city-governor/steward, "called with reference mainly to his
  stewardship in the service of the god"; could be sovereign or subordinate.
- **en** — "lord," but also "high-priest"; the latent rivalry between temporal
  and priestly power surfaces at Lagash.

Offices were hereditary in practice; governors pursued the "normal ambitions of
leaders," aspiring to extend rule over neighbours and, ultimately, to the
**nam-lugal** (supremacy over "the land") that the
[[sumerian-king-list|king-list]] imagined as passing from one city to another.

## Sumerians and Semites

A central CAH clarification (Gadd): "Sumerian" and "Semite" "refer exclusively to
language," not race. Semitic names appear in the earliest dynasties (notably Kish,
and entirely at Mari) **mingled** with Sumerian ones; skeletal and costume
evidence shows "no visible cleavage." The "Sumerian problem" (whether Sumerians
were the first inhabitants and inventors of writing) is real but, Gadd argues,
does not diminish that the *development* of cuneiform was wholly their work. From
the earliest discernible beginning the two peoples were "inextricably mixed."

## History: the struggle for supremacy

Contemporary inscriptions are sparse; the narrative spine is the
[[sumerian-king-list|Sumerian King List]] (overlapping "dynasties" of Kish, Uruk,
Ur) supplemented by the uniquely full records of **[[lagash|Lagash]]** (a city
the king-list omits entirely). Landmarks:
- **Enmebaragisi of Kish** — earliest ruler attested by his own inscription.
- **Gilgamesh of Uruk** and his war with **Agga of Kish** — the first glimpse of
  the inter-city struggle for supremacy (and a key text in the
  [[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities|"primitive democracy" debate]]). In the Standard Version (ll. 771–4000) Gilgamesh as "shepherd of Uruk-the-Sheepfold" (ll. 784, 1091) tyrannizes the people, prompting divine creation of Enkidu (ll. 1141–1161); he addresses the assembly of elders ("Hear me, O elders of Uruk-the-City!", ll. 2052–2066) and young men to announce the Cedar Forest expedition despite warnings, then instructs regents during his absence (ll. 2447–2453). These episodes from the Standard Version **concurrent_with** the legendary traditions of Early Dynastic Uruk. The Sumerian poem "Gilgamesh and Akka" details assemblies debating submission to Kish; Gilgamesh follows the young men. This **preceded_by** (in Sumerian tradition) the Huwawa/Cedar Forest expedition. These poems **part_of** the legendary corpus for Early Dynastic Uruk. Additional from range apparatus (ll. 10001–12899): the five Sumerian poems listed with editions (Akka, Huwawa A/B, Bull of Heaven, Netherworld, Death of Gilgamesh; ll. 12732–80); "HUWAWA An old form of the name Humbaba" (glossary, ll. 12545). OB fragments detail Uruk's sevenfold gates, elders' counsel against Huwawa ("You are young, Gilgamesh, borne along by emotion...", ll. 10518–36), and the assembly setting.
- **The First Dynasty of Ur** (Mesannepada) — where the king-list's reign-lengths
  first shrink to human scale and agree with contemporary inscriptions; the era of
  the [[ur|Royal Tombs of Ur]].
- **The Lagash dynasty** (Ur-Nanshe → Eannatum → Entemena → ... → Urukagina) and
  the long [[lagash-umma-border-war|border war with Umma]].
- **Lugalzaggisi of Umma/Uruk** — briefly unified "the land" from the Lower to the
  Upper Sea, then fell to [[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon]] — the
  [[akkadian-empire|transition to empire]].

## The archaeological record (CAH XVI, Mallowan)

The material backbone of the period comes from the **Diyala valley** sites east of
Baghdad — **Tell Asmar (Eshnunna)**, **Khafajah**, **Tell Agrab** — excavated by
the Oriental Institute, which yielded the three-stage Early Dynastic sequence and
its diagnostic **plano-convex ("plump") brick**. The signature find is the
**Square Temple of Abu at Tell Asmar**: a pit beside the altar held a **cache of
votive statues** — stiff, wide-eyed worshippers and gods with hands clasped and
enormous inlaid eyes, deposited in the temple to stand in perpetual attentive
prayer before the deity on the dedicant's behalf. These statues are the supreme
expression of the Sumerian relationship of man to god, and (with the figures from
**Mari** and the **archaic Ishtar temple at Assur**) show how thoroughly Sumerian
cult forms reached the city's far outposts. Mallowan notes that antiquities were
**cherished across generations** — one statue's feet were found in the E.D. II
Square Temple, its head and trunk in the later Single-Shrine Temple.

## Warfare

The age earned "an ill repute for militarism." The Stele of the Vultures and the
Standard of Ur show a disciplined **phalanx** of heavily armed, helmeted
spearmen, supported by four-wheeled solid-disc **battle-cars** drawn by onagers.
Notably, **bow and arrow were not used in Sumerian warfare** (only for hunting);
armies "depended wholly upon weight." Cities had massive walls; sieges were
pressed by earthen ramps.

## Historiography

The Sumerian record is overwhelmingly **archaeological plus administrative**, with
royal/literary cuneiform thin and the king-list a much-later, schematizing
compilation (see [[sumerian-king-list]]). Gadd's reliance on the Lagash temple
archives means the picture of the [[temple-economy|economy]] is drawn from "one,
and that by no means the greatest" city — a sampling caveat he stresses himself.
Absolute dates follow the [[mesopotamian-chronology|middle chronology]]; CAH's
uncalibrated radiocarbon is superseded. The "primitive democracy" reading of early
kingship (Jacobsen) is a live interpretive question, not settled fact. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].

**Grounded claims from Sumerian Gilgamesh poems (exclusive range: Andrew George trans., ll. 7001–10000)**:
- Huwawa poem end and Bull of Heaven locate heroic exploits in the city-state world (e.g., "the sons of his city who had come with him" lop branches, Gilgamesh calls "O city, he who has a wife, to his wife!" in Huwawa B, ll. 7478–84; Bull fills Uruk and Kullab, ll. 7951–53). **part_of** [[early-dynastic-sumer|Early Dynastic Sumer]] legendary kingship.
- "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld" and "Death of Gilgamesh" show ED Sumerian views on netherworld governance, mourning rites, tomb construction (Euphrates diversion in Uruk, ll. 9665–83), and legacy through statues ("how their names are pronounced will never be forgotten!", ll. 9807) and offspring (Aruru "for the sake of their names gave (men) offspring", ll. 9811). These **produced** cultural practices of funerary piety in the city-state period.
- Gilgamesh petitions Enki at Eridu and goes "alone to Ur, the house of Sîn" (ll. 9002–4); Ur recension alludes to Amorite disruption of Sumerian netherworld offerings post-Ur III (ll. 8820–32). **concurrent_with** historical memories of ED/Ur III transitions.
