# Alexios I Komnenos

**Alexios I Komnenos** (c. 1057–1118; emperor from 1081) rescued the
[[byzantine-empire|Byzantine empire]] from near-collapse and founded the
[[komnenian-restoration|Komnenian recovery]]. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]]
(Michael Angold, Ch. 8) makes him the architect of "astonishing" recovery in "years of
political turmoil, financial crisis and social upheaval."

## Career

A scion of the great military aristocracy, Alexios seized the throne in 1081 amid the
chaos that followed [[battle-of-manzikert-1071|Manzikert (1071)]] — the Seljuk conquest
of Anatolia, the Pecheneg occupation of the Danube, and the Norman invasion of the
Balkans. He met each threat in turn: he fought the Normans **Robert Guiscard and
Bohemond** to a standstill in Epirus and Macedonia; he annihilated the **Pechenegs at
Levounion (1091)**; and he rebuilt the army, fiscal system, and coinage, binding the
aristocracy to his house through a web of marriage alliances and new court titles — the
**family-based Komnenian state**.

His most consequential dealings were with the West. Having appealed to the Latins for
mercenary aid, he found himself in 1096–97 confronted by the vast, unruly armies of the
**[[first-crusade-1095|First Crusade]]**. He extracted oaths from the crusade leaders to
restore former imperial territory, ferried them across the Bosphorus, and used their
victories to **recover the western coast of Anatolia** (Nicaea, 1097) — but his refusal
to support the siege of Antioch, and Bohemond's seizure of that city, sowed the
Byzantine–Latin mistrust that would grow across the twelfth century. His reign is
vividly (if partially) recorded by his daughter **Anna Komnene** in the *Alexiad*, one
of the great works of Byzantine historiography.

## Counterfactual Significance

Alexios is the indispensable figure of Byzantine survival: without his improvised,
family-centred reconstruction, the empire might well have gone under in the 1080s. He
restored Byzantium as a great power for another century — but the instruments of that
recovery (dependence on Latin mercenaries and Italian fleets, the manipulation of the
crusade) also deepened the entanglement with the West that would end at
[[fourth-crusade-1204|Constantinople in 1204]].
