# The Ottonian Dynasty (Liudolfings) and the Reich

The **Ottonians** (the Saxon **Liudolfing** house) ruled the east Frankish kingdom and, from 962, the revived western empire, from **919 to 1024**. They transformed the fragmenting post-Carolingian east Francia into the most powerful polity of Latin Europe — the **Reich** — and gave it a durable structure built on itinerant kingship, the Saxon heartland, and a uniquely tight alliance with the imperial church. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Müller-Mertens, "The Ottonians as kings and emperors") is the volume's keystone political chapter.

## From Duchy to Kingship

With the death of the last east Frankish Carolingian, **Louis the Child** (911), and the failure of the Franconian **Conrad I** (911–918) to master the new ethnic duchies, the Saxon duke **Henry I "the Fowler"** (919–936) was elected king by Frankish and Saxon magnates at Fritzlar. Henry redefined kingship for a world of entrenched regional power: he ruled as **primus inter pares**, binding the dukes of Swabia, Bavaria, and Lotharingia by **friendship-pacts (*amicitiae*)** and vassalic bonds rather than crushing them, and pointedly **refused royal anointing** to mark a break with Conradine/Carolingian practice. He recovered Lotharingia (925), defeated the Magyars at the Riade (933), and — decisively — declared the kingship **indivisible**, designating a single heir (Otto) rather than partitioning the realm. This indivisibility, taken up across the post-Carolingian world (see [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|Nelson, NCMH III]]), is one of the dynasty's most consequential legacies.

## The Imperial Liudolfings

- **[[otto-i|Otto I "the Great"]]** (936–973): broke with his father's consensual style for an assertive, sacral kingship; crushed family revolts; annihilated the Magyars at the **[[battle-of-lechfeld-955|Lechfeld (955)]]**; conquered the kingdom of Italy; was crowned emperor in Rome in **[[coronation-of-otto-i-962|962]]**, reviving the western empire; founded the archbishopric of **Magdeburg** as the hinge of eastward mission; and built the **[[reichskirche|imperial church system]]**.
- **Otto II** (973–983): defended the realm but suffered the catastrophic defeat at **Cotrone** (982) against the Sicilian Arabs in southern Italy, followed by the great **Elbe Slav revolt of 983** (see [[elbe-slavs]]); adopted the title *imperator Romanorum*.
- **[[otto-iii|Otto III]]** (983–1002): a minor under the regencies of his Byzantine mother **Theophanu** and grandmother **Adelaide**; on majority pursued the visionary **Renovatio Imperii Romanorum** from a palace on the Palatine, with the popes Gregory V and **Sylvester II ([[gerbert-of-aurillac|Gerbert of Aurillac]])**, and at **[[synod-of-gniezno-1000|Gniezno (1000)]]** sponsored the new Christian kingships of Poland and Hungary.
- **[[henry-ii-holy-roman-emperor|Henry II]]** (1002–1024): the Bavarian Liudolfing line; abandoned Otto III's Rome-centred vision for a *renovatio regni Francorum* focused north of the Alps; founded the bishopric of **Bamberg** (1007); allied even with the pagan Liutizi against Christian Poland; intensified the Reichskirche to its apogee; died childless and was later canonised — the only sainted German emperor.

## Structure of the Reich

Müller-Mertens analyses the Reich as a polity of **itinerant kingship** organised around three **central regions** where the king periodically resided and held assemblies: the **Harz/eastern Saxony–north Thuringia** (the new Ottonian base domain, enriched by the Goslar silver), the **Rhine–Main** area (Frankfurt), and the **lower Rhine/Meuse around Aachen** (the Carolingian core). Power radiated outward through "transit" regions to "distant zones" (Bavaria, Swabia, Lotharingia) whose magnates met the king in the central regions. The dynasty styled itself "kings of the Franks and Saxons" (*Francia et Saxonia*); by Henry II's reign contemporaries (in Venice and southern Italy) had begun to call the realm north of the Alps a *regnum Teutonicum* and the ruler *rex Teutonicorum* — an early marker of "German" identity.

## Significance

The Ottonians:

- **revived the western Roman empire** (962), permanently linking Germany and Italy and creating what would become the Holy Roman Empire;
- secured Latin Europe's eastern frontier by destroying Magyar raiding at Lechfeld;
- forged the **[[reichskirche|imperial church]]** as the administrative backbone of monarchy — a model whose collision with the reform papacy would detonate the eleventh-century **Investiture Contest**;
- and presided over the **[[ottonian-renaissance]]** in art, learning, and imperial ideology.
