# Constantius II

Middle son of **[[constantine-the-great|Constantine the Great]]** and the dominant figure of the
generation after him — **Augustus of the East from 337**, sole emperor from 353 to his death in 361.
A capable, suspicious, tirelessly mobile ruler who held the empire together against Persia and usurpers
while pursuing a lifelong (ultimately divisive) quest for Christian doctrinal unity. Treated in
[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 1 (**David Hunt**).

## The dynastic massacre and the division

Constantine's death (May 337) was followed by a "promiscuous massacre" (Gibbon): the troops at
Constantinople butchered the descendants of Constantine's stepmother Theodora — nine of the future
[[julian|Julian's]] relatives, including his father — leaving the succession to Constantine's three
sons. Hunt judges that Constantius, the first Caesar on the scene, had "some part" in it. The three
brothers (**Constantine II, Constans, Constantius II**) met in Pannonia and divided the empire;
Constantius took the **East**. Constantine II died invading Constans' Italy (340); Constans held the
whole West until the usurper **Magnentius** killed him (350). After defeating Magnentius at the
**battle of Mursa (351)** — a ruinously bloody victory — and his suicide (353), Constantius was **sole
ruler**, elevating his cousins **Gallus** (executed 354) and then [[julian|Julian]] as Caesars.

## The defensive Persian war

Constantius' defining external struggle was with **[[sasanian-empire|Shapur II]]** over Armenia and
Mesopotamia (lost to Persia under Diocletian's treaty of 299, which Shapur sought to reverse). Based at
**Antioch** (337–350), Constantius fought a deliberately **defensive war** — holding the fortified
towns, above all **Nisibis** (besieged three times, 337/346/350), avoiding pitched battles (the
exception, **Singara** c. 344, a victory squandered). Hunt stresses this **un-glamorous strategy was
vindicated**: Constantius "lost no Roman territory," and the disaster of [[julian|Julian's]] offensive
of 363 would confirm its wisdom. He kept **Armenia** in the Roman fold by diplomacy (restoring king
Arsaces, 338).

## Religion: the search for a creed

A devout but **Homoian ("Arian"-leaning)** Christian, Constantius made **ecclesiastical politics affairs
of state**. He repeatedly deposed **Athanasius** of Alexandria, backed the anti-Nicene party of Eusebius
of Nicomedia, and after his brother Constans' death pursued a personal campaign to impose doctrinal
unity — the council of **Sirmium** and the "search for a creed" (357–359) that shifted the language away
from the Nicene *homoousios*. His Caesar Julian's secret apostasy and rebellion (360) cut this short: as
Constantius marched west to confront him, he **died in Cilicia (November 361)**, leaving the empire — and
the unresolved religious quarrel — to the pagan Julian.

## Significance

Constantius is the ruler who **consolidated the Constantinian Christian empire** across a generation of
civil war and Persian pressure — building the bureaucratic, ceremonial monarchy his father had founded,
and embedding the principle that the emperor arbitrates the faith. Hostile sources (Ammianus, the
Julianic tradition) caricature him as suspicious and unwarlike, but his patient Mesopotamian defence and
control of the army held the empire together. His childlessness made the elevation of Julian necessary —
and so made possible the last pagan reaction.
