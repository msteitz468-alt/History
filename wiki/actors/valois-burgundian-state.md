# The Valois Duchy of Burgundy (the Burgundian State)

The composite principality assembled by the four Valois dukes of Burgundy across the fifteenth
century — "nearer to being the promised land than any other," in Commynes's phrase — and its
spectacular collapse in 1477. Analysed by Bertrand Schnerb in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]]
(Ch. 20). Straddling the Franco-imperial border, it became "a vital element within the very heart of
western Europe," a great power that nearly crystallised into a third monarchy between France and the
Empire before its ruin.

## Territorial Extent and Structure

The four dukes — **Philip the Bold** (d. 1404), **John the Fearless** (d. 1419), **Philip the
Good** (d. 1467) and **Charles the Bold** (d. 1477) — ruled two great blocks: the **two Burgundies**
(the duchy, a French fief; and the county/Franche-Comté, an imperial fief) in the south, and the
**Burgundian Low Countries** ("les pays de par-deça") in the north. Philip the Good's relentless
acquisitions (Namur 1421/29; Hainault, Holland and Zeeland from Jacqueline of Bavaria; Brabant 1430;
Luxembourg 1441/43) created, within two decades, the richest urbanised territory in Europe — Bruges,
Ghent and Antwerp each over 20,000 souls; a third of the north's population town-dwellers.

Government was **bipolar, not centralised**: parallel councils, *chambres des comptes* and receivers
at **Dijon** (for the Burgundies) and **Lille** (for the north), topped by "central" institutions
that travelled with the duke (the *grand conseil* under the great chancellor **Nicolas Rolin**,
1422–56). The duchy remained legally subject to the French crown — the "appeal to France" to the
Paris *parlement* being "the most tangible evidence of royal sovereignty over Burgundy."

## A European Power

The dukes were **French princes** first: Philip the Bold and John the Fearless dominated the court of
the mad **Charles VI**, and John's assassination of **Louis of Orléans (1407)** opened the
**Armagnac–Burgundian civil war**. After John himself was murdered at Montereau (1419), **Philip the
Good** allied with England (the **Treaty of Troyes, 1420**, creating the dual monarchy) — then
switched sides at the **Treaty of Arras (1435)**, abandoning the English and ending the civil war in
exchange for the Somme towns and other concessions. Thereafter Philip "put himself on the periphery
of the Anglo-French conflict" and pursued expansion eastward into the Empire, though his bid to have
his lands raised to a **kingdom** never succeeded. The fall of Constantinople (1453) rekindled the
dukes' **crusading** ideal (the theatrical **Oath of the Pheasant**, Lille 1454), but the promised
crusade never sailed.

## Court, the Golden Fleece, and Culture

The Burgundian court was "one of the most brilliant in Europe" — an instrument of government and of
**political propaganda** through sumptuous feasts and ceremony. Philip the Good founded the **Order
of the Golden Fleece (1430)** to bind the aristocracy of all his lands to himself. Ducal and
courtier patronage produced an artistic golden age: **Claus Sluter** at the Chartreuse de Champmol;
the painters **Jan van Eyck** and **Rogier van der Weyden** (Rolin's commissions — the Beaune *Last
Judgement*, the *Madonna* at Autun); the composer **Gilles Binchois**; and a vast historical and
chivalric literature (official "historiographers" Chastellain and Molinet) projecting a
**Burgundian** view of the past against the French crown.

## Charles the Bold and the Fall (1467–1477)

**Charles the Bold** set out to forge a **centralised, independent** state. He reformed its
institutions (1473: a single *parlement* and *chambre des comptes* at Malines; standing **compagnies
d'ordonnance** modelled on the French army; multi-year taxation), crushed Liège and Dinant, and tried
to break the legal bond to France (the Treaty of Péronne, 1468, forbidding "appeals to France"). But
his aggressive **expansion toward the Rhine** (Upper Alsace, Guelders, the siege of Neuss 1474–5, the
conquest of Lorraine) provoked a broad coalition. The **Swiss** shattered his army at **Grandson** and
**Morat (1476)**; abandoned by his allies, Charles was killed before **Nancy on 5 January 1477**.

His sole heir **Mary of Burgundy** married **Maximilian of Habsburg (August 1477)** to survive the
French onslaught; the estates forced the "Grand Privilège" on her. The **Treaties of Arras (1482) and
Senlis (1493)** divided the inheritance: France kept the **duchy of Burgundy** and Picardy, while the
**Habsburgs** retained **Franche-Comté and the Low Countries** — a geopolitical settlement that shaped
European history (the Habsburg–Valois wars, the later Spanish Netherlands) for two centuries.

## Historiography

Schnerb poses the central question: did Charles the Bold **create** the Burgundian state or **dig its
grave**? His reforms brought it to apogee and destruction almost simultaneously. The chapter also
traces the **late, uneven growth of a "Burgundian" identity** — loyalty to the dynasty plus hatred of
the French enemy — redirected after 1477 to the house of Habsburg ("Rather, cry 'Long live
Burgundy,'" said Margaret of Austria in 1493).
