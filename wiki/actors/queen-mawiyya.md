## Overview

Mawiyya (Mavia in the Latin sources) was a queen of the [[tanukhids|Tanukhid]] Arab
confederation who led a major revolt against Rome in the second half of the 4th
century. **Her revolt dates to c. 375–378**, not the "fifth century" figure that appears
in one passage of Ball's text (p. 448) — the historical Mawiyya's war is securely dated
to the reign of Valens (r. 364–378); Ball's own text elsewhere is consistent with this
earlier dating, and the "fifth century" reference appears to be an error or typo rather
than a considered redating. Her death is separately recorded, by inscription, in 425.

## Background: The Breakdown of the Tanukh Alliance

Roman relations with the Syrian Tanukh, established as *foederati* allies guarding the
desert frontier, had already soured under the emperor Julian, who withdrew Arab-ally
privileges during his 363 Persian campaign — a decision Ball suggests may have cost
Julian his life, since his fatal wounding was blamed on an Arab auxiliary, possibly for
religious reasons (Christian Arabs' hostility to Julian's pagan apostasy) or as
reprisal for the loss of privileges. When the unnamed "king of the Saracens" (possibly
the Tanukhid al-Hawari, a grandson of Imru'l-Qays) died around 375 without an heir,
Emperor Valens abrogated Rome's treaty with the Tanukh. Ball attributes this chiefly to
religion rather than politics: Valens was an Arian Christian, the Tanukh Arabs were
staunch Monophysites, and Valens's attempt to impose an Arian bishop on them triggered
open revolt.

## The Revolt

Mawiyya, widow of the dead shaikh, took over leadership of the Tanukh confederation,
withdrew into the desert, and rallied desert Arabs and much of Arabia and Syria to her
cause. Her forces conducted devastating guerrilla raids deep into Roman Palestine and
as far as Egypt, reaching the Nile — exploiting Rome's inability to fight a mobile,
desert-based enemy without a fixed target, in contrast to [[zenobia|Zenobia's]] earlier
revolt, which had given Rome the fixed objective of [[palmyra|Palmyra]] itself. Mawiyya
defeated an initial Roman force commanded by the governor of Phoenicia and Palestine,
and won sympathy from Monophysite townspeople hostile to the Arian emperor.

Constantinople then dispatched a larger force under Rome's eastern military commander.
Mawiyya personally commanded the Arab army in the field and won a decisive victory, her
forces combining discipline learned from Roman service with traditional Arab cavalry
tactics using the long lance. Ball notes that Rome had not faced such lance-armed
cavalry in battle since the Battle of Magnesia over 550 years earlier — where
disciplined Roman infantry had prevailed over the Seleucid phalanx — and that on this
occasion, deployed by mobile Arab horsemen rather than a static phalanx, the same
weapon proved devastating against Roman forces. Facing an imminent Gothic war, Valens
was compelled to sue for peace.

## Peace Terms and the Gothic War

Mawiyya dictated the terms of peace: she chose the Monophysite Arab ascetic Moses as
bishop, a choice Valens had no real option but to accept; had the Tanukh's allied status
and privileges reinstated; and sealed the settlement by marrying her daughter, Princess
Chasidat, to the Roman officer Victor, a veteran of Julian's campaign and a committed
Monophysite. Ball quotes the settlement's outcome approvingly: "Under their Queen, the
Arabs had achieved a just and lasting peace."

Mawiyya's Arab auxiliaries subsequently fought for Rome in its disastrous war against
the Goths, struggling in the unfamiliar terrain of Thrace but, according to Ammianus
Marcellinus, helping to save Constantinople itself from the Goths, though at heavy cost.
After Valens was killed at the Battle of Adrianople in 378, the new emperor Theodosius
favored the Goths over the Arabs, and anti-Arab agitation by the Antioch orator
Libanius — a friend of the dead Julian — helped provoke the withdrawal of Arab
privileges once again, triggering a second, quickly suppressed revolt in 383. This
second revolt ended Rome's alliance with the Tanukh; by the close of the century Rome
had transferred its patronage to the Salih tribe, a precursor to the later rise of the
[[ghassanids|Ghassanids]]. Mawiyya's own role in this second revolt is not recorded; no
alternate Tanukhid leader is named in the sources.

## Later Life and Death

Mawiyya apparently lived to old age, retiring to Anasartha east of Aleppo; an
inscription records her death in 425.

## Counterfactual Significance and Historical Weight

**THESIS.** Ball frames Mawiyya's revolt as historically pivotal well beyond its
immediate outcome: it demonstrated to Arabs more broadly how readily provincial
populations would defect to an Arab side in wartime, and how devastatingly effective
disciplined, mobile Bedouin cavalry could be against conventional Roman military forces
— a lesson, in Ball's reading, that would be fully realized generations later in the
Arab-Muslim conquests of the 7th century: "The next time this was to happen, the Arabs
would be triumphant." This is Ball's interpretive framing of the episode's long-term
significance, not a claim of direct causal continuity established by contemporary
sources.

## Historiography

The principal narrative source for Mawiyya's revolt is Ammianus Marcellinus,
supplemented by later Christian ecclesiastical historians interested in the bishop
Moses. As with much pre-Islamic Arab history, named individual actors are sparse in
Graeco-Roman sources, and Mawiyya's prominence in the surviving record — a named,
militarily and diplomatically decisive queen — is itself unusual. Ball situates her
firmly within his broader "orientalization" thesis about the growing Arab role within
the late Roman military and political system; see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]]
for that debate.

## Fisher (2020): Revolt as Negotiation

Greg Fisher reads Mawiyya's revolt (which he dates c. 377–378) less as a war than as a
**negotiating tactic** — the archetypal case of an Arab tribal leader deploying the one
asset the state most needed, tribal manpower, as leverage. In his anthropological frame
(tribe/state relations; see [[super-phylarchate]] and [[band-tribe-chiefdom-state]]),
once Mawiyya's core demand was met the rationale for fighting evaporated and cooperation
resumed: her forces promptly re-entered Roman service and helped defend Constantinople
after Adrianople (378) — Ammianus records, sensationally, an Arab soldier sucking blood
from a slain Goth's neck. Fisher stresses that Roman–Arab alliances at this level were
understood as **personal, ruler-to-ruler pacts**, which is why they lapsed violently on
the death of a partner (the pattern later echoed by Khusrau II's invasion to "avenge" the
murdered Maurice).

For Fisher the **main source is the ecclesiastical historian Sozomen**, who called it a war
"by no means... contemptible... although conducted by a woman" and noted it was "still held
in remembrance... celebrated in songs by the Saracens" (five ancient authors report it in
all). He details the bishop dispute: Mawiyya demanded the ordination of the hermit-monk
**Moses**, complicated because the officiating Alexandrian patriarch **Lucius** was an Arian
(a creed condemned at Nicaea 325 but favoured by Valens) whom Moses regarded as heretical.
Fisher groups Mawiyya with Semiramis, Cleopatra, [[zenobia|Zenobia]], Boudicca, and the
Kahina as rare female military leaders of antiquity, and — unlike some earlier scholars —
regards attempts to link the 425 "Mavia" dedication at Anasartha to the queen as
"heroically futile."
