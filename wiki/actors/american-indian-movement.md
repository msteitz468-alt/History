# American Indian Movement (AIM)

Twentieth-century pan-Indian radical organization of individual Indians that, in Washburn’s CHNPA I.2 typology, often challenged existing tribal governments rather than speaking as their official organ. Contrasted with the [[national-congress-of-american-indians]] (NCAI), which he treats as the loose organization of tribes and individuals closest to official tribal-government spokesman.

## Role and structure (Washburn frame)

Washburn groups AIM with the rise of national/pan-Indian bodies that phrase demands for all Indians rather than single tribes. AIM’s signature example in his chapter is the 1973 siege of the Oglala Sioux village of Wounded Knee, South Dakota ([[wounded-knee-occupation-1973]]). Related 1970s actions in the same activist cluster: Trail of Broken Treaties (1972) and Longest Walk (1978) marches on Washington; Yellow Thunder Camp; anthropologist involvement that tribal governments did not always welcome. Named leaders: Dennis Banks and Russell Means.

**AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn):** Political turmoil of the 1960s–70s challenging tribal-government legitimacy gradually gave way to conventional debate over particular tribal policies; Banks and Means “ceased their attacks on tribal governments as political entities and sought rather to influence the course of those governments,” leaving John Collier “in effect, vindicated.” Treat this as Washburn’s evaluative thesis, not consensus history of AIM’s later decades.

AIM activists also appear among external critics of Navajo chairman Peter MacDonald during the late-1980s leadership crisis (secondary mention only).

## Counterfactual significance

Without AIM’s high-visibility confrontations, Washburn’s narrative of a political “renaissance” would rest more exclusively on NCAI, courts, and federal statutes; the chapter uses AIM to mark the radical pole of pan-Indian politics and its purported taming under [[tribal-self-determination]].

## Historiography

CHNPA I.2 supplies a short, policy-oriented characterization—not founding chronology, chapter structure, or criminal-case history (e.g. Banks/Means trials). Founding date “1968” is conventional external knowledge used only to replace `unknown` in frontmatter; expand from dedicated sources. Washburn does **not** link AIM to [[occupation-of-alcatraz-1969]] (Alcatraz absent from ch. 15).
