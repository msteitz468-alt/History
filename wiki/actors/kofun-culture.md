# Kofun Culture (Mounded Tomb Culture)

The **Kofun** period (~250–710 AD) — Barnes's **Mounded Tomb Culture (MTC)** — is the era in which the
first Japanese state, [[yamato-japan|Yamato]], formed around the construction of monumental keyhole-
shaped tombs. In [[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes's]] scheme the debut of monumental tomb-
building is the **archaeological marker of statehood** across the [[pen-insular|Pen/Insulae]] (see
[[pen-insular-state-formation]]).

## From Himiko to Hashihaka

The *Weizhi* records that after the Wa disturbances (147–189) the people made a woman, **Himiko**, ruler
of **Yamatai**; she sent embassies to the Wei court (238, 242, 247) and received bronze mirrors. Her
death (after 247) and burial "in a large tomb 100 paces across" is now used to **define the start of the
Kofun period at 250**, superseding the older date of 400. Scholarly consensus has shifted Yamatai from
North Kyushu to the **Nara Basin** (the 1997 Kurotsuka excavation was pivotal); the first monumental
keyhole tomb is **Hashihaka** (~270–280). Himiko is absent from the chronicle king-lists — Barnes
attributes this to the Confucian patriarchy under which the *Nihon Shoki* was compiled.

## The Keyhole Tomb and Its Spread

The keyhole tomb (round rear mound + trapezoidal front) first appeared at the **Makimuku** cluster in the
southeastern Nara Basin and spread across nearly all Late Yayoi mound cultures (the Izumo region's
square four-tailed mounds resisted it). Early keyhole tombs come in three size classes; the **Daisen
Tomb** (Osaka, 486 m long, 33 m high, three moats) built under the 5th-century **Kawachi Court** exceeded
all others. **Haniwa** — clay cylinders set at the tomb perimeter, evolving into representational
sculptures (shields, parasols, houses; humans from the 5th century) — replaced sacrificial victims (none
of which have been found in Japan, unlike in [[kaya|Kaya]]). Barnes argues the MTC spread "without
coercion": the **527 Iwai Rebellion** is the single attested military episode in Yamato's expansion.

## Continental Connections

Major early grave goods were Han and Wei **bronze mirrors** (including the triangular-rimmed
Queen-Mother-of-the-West type), which Barnes argues rulers redistributed to subordinates to cement
alliances. Iron, at first an agricultural import, became a **strategic military good** after the Early
Kofun system's mid-4th-century collapse. **Steppe-derived horse-riding armour** from [[koguryo|Koguryo]]
was adopted by all Pen/Insular elite and eventually transformed into samurai armour; a tide of refugees
and craftspeople from [[paekche|Paekche]] and Kaya in the early 5th century brought **stoneware
(Sué-ware), gold-working, saddle-making and horse-trappings**, revolutionising elite production. Barnes
**rejects** the old "horse-rider conquest" theory of Yamato's origins: the record shows migration,
prestige exchange and refugee craft production, not invasion. Late Kofun saw **corridor-chamber tombs**
(reopenable, multi-burial) cluster into cemeteries — Barnes suggests their dark sealed space gave rise to
the Japanese concept of *yomi*, the underworld. The MTC ended as **temple-building superseded tomb-
building** in the 7th century.
