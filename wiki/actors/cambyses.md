# Cambyses II

Second Achaemenid Great King (r. 530–522 BCE), son and successor of Cyrus the Great. Conquered Egypt (525 BCE), incorporating it as a satrapy and adopting pharaonic titulature. His reign is marked in Greek sources (especially Herodotus) by tales of hubris, sacrilege in Egypt, and mental instability, contrasted with Cyrus' more measured image. Died (or was killed) during or after a campaign against a usurper (Gaumata / "False Smerdis"); succeeded after a period of turmoil by Darius I.

## Significance
Completed the initial rapid expansion of the Persian Empire into Africa; his Egyptian venture tested the limits of Achaemenid rule over ancient, culturally distinct kingdoms. Source of major historiographical controversy (Herodotus' reliability on Cambyses).

## Briant's Rehabilitation

[[sources/briant-from-cyrus-to-alexander-2002|Briant]] dismantles the "mad Cambyses" tradition as the product of two hostile information pools — Egyptian anti-Persian popular memory and Persian aristocratic circles. The Egyptian campaign was **strategically predetermined** by Cyrus's annexation of Trans-Euphrates, not a deranged whim; Cambyses was in fact "the real creator of the Persian navy." The murder-of-Apis story is **archaeologically refuted** by the Serapeum epitaph (524 BCE) showing Cambyses presiding over Apis's funeral in pharaonic costume, and the Udjahorresnet inscription shows him commissioning Egyptian pharaonic titulature and restoring the temple of Neith at Sais. His revenue restrictions on Egyptian temples reflect fiscal control over powerful institutions (paralleled in Babylonia), not anti-Egyptian animus; even the Nubian expedition may not have been the catastrophe Herodotus reports (Achaemenid occupation at the Dorginarti fortress). On the [[gaumata-magush|Bardiya]] problem, Briant reads Darius's "Gaumata" as propaganda transforming a legitimate claimant into a usurper.
