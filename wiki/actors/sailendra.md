# Sailendra

**Śailendra** (Sanskrit “lord of the mountain”) was the name of a **staunchly Buddhist royal lineage** that ruled central Java from around **780 to 832 CE**. The name first appears in the **Kalasan inscription of 778**. Associated with the great Mahayana monuments of Early Classic Java—above all [[borobudur|Borobudur]]—the Sailendras also surface in peninsular and Sumatran epigraphy and, centuries later, as a family name for Kedah/Srivijaya rulers in Indian grants. Primary synthesis: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]] Early Classic Java chapter.

## Overview

Within the [[mataram-kingdom|Mataram]] political world of central Java, the Sailendras represent the high-water mark of royal Buddhist sponsorship. Their reign was “short… but glorious.” Temples associated with the line include Candi Sari, **Borobudur**, Mendut, Pawon, Bubrah, and Lumbung. They coexisted—and eventually intermarried—with the Hindu **Sanjaya** line that later consecrated [[prambanan|Prambanan]]. After the early ninth century the Sailendra name shifts off Java: Balaputra of the mid-ninth-century **Nalanda inscription** belonged to this family and ruled in Sumatra; later Middle Classic Tamil grants still call Srivijaya/Kedah rulers “of the Sailendra family.”

## Epigraphy and Kingship

**Kalasan (778):** Maharaja Panangkaran, despite his grand title, acted under a higher authority described as the “ornament of the Sailendra family,” founding a sanctuary dedicated to the bodhisattva **Tārā** at the behest of religious teachers. De Casparis argued that Panangkaran was **not** himself a Sailendra but a vassal of the Sailendra Viṣṇu Dharmatuṅga.

**Ligor (Nakhon Si Thammarat) inscription, side B (782 or shortly after):** refers to a Sailendra king **Viṣṇu** with titles *rājādhirāja* and Maharaja; he may be identical with Saṅgrāmadhanañjaya of Kelurak. It is **unknown** whether the Sailendras already held power in [[srivijaya|Srivijaya]] at this date—side A of the same stone (775) is Srivijayan.

**Samaratuṅga** (last known Sailendra king of central Java, attested 824) supposedly married Princess Tārā of Srivijaya, mother of **Balaputra**. Their daughter Pramodawardhanī likely married the Raka of Pikatan of the Hindu Sanjaya family. One interpretation: son Balaputra rebelled, was defeated, and moved to Srivijaya. An 824 inscription associates Samaratuṅga with a “place for the conqueror Buddhas” in ten parts—often linked to Borobudur’s programme (see that page).

**Mantyasih (907) and Wanua Tengah III (908)** king lists **omit** Sailendra rulers thought members of that line, while recording Sanjaya kings—even as Buddhist monastery donors—complicating any simple Buddhist Sailendra vs. Hindu Sanjaya dualism.

## Overseas Ambitions and Later Sailendra Name

Cham sources and Vietnamese annals suggest late eighth-century designs on the Cham area: invasion from Java in **767**; at Nha Trang, ships stole a *lingga* and Satyavarman defeated them; in **787** another Javanese raid burned a temple. These are best read as concurrent maritime projection, not proven permanent occupation of [[champa|Champa]].

In the Middle Classic, the **Larger Leiden Grant (1006)** records that Mara Vijayottuṅgavarman “of the Sailendra family with the *makara* crest,” ruler of Śrīvijaya and Kaṭāha (Kedah), built the Chūḷāmaṇivarma vihāra at Nagapattinam. A ruling group called Sailendra is mentioned only in Java inscriptions, Ligor face B, and Indian inscriptions naming the Kedah ruler. The **Smaller Tamil Leiden Grant (1089–90)** has Kulottuṅga give a village to the Sailendra temple at the request of the king of Kidara (Kedah)—long after central Javanese Sailendra rule had ended.

## Historiography of Lineages

It is “possible, though not certain,” that Sailendra and Sanjaya belonged to **separate lineages**. One reading: amalgamation through intermarriage around **832**; alternative: different geographic power bases (no clear evidence). “There is no consensus among historians regarding the relationships between the rulers of central Java in the Early Classic” because of complex titles and multiple names per person. Speculation that Sailendras descended from Funan *parwatabhūpāla / śailarāja* (“Lord of the Mountain”) titles has **no confirming evidence**.

Old Malay inscriptions in Java include the early seventh-century **Sojomerto** genealogy of Dapunta Selendra, a Śiva devotee—here *Selendra* is a **personal name**, not proof of the later Buddhist dynasty. Seven Old Malay inscriptions on Java have been explained as former Malay-speaking populations, temporary Srivijayan rule, or other contact; Chinese sources report Java–Sumatra warfare.

## Key Events and Processes

- **part_of** [[mataram-kingdom|Mataram]] central Javanese kingship and the Early Classic temple boom.
- **concurrent_with** [[srivijaya|Srivijaya]], [[borobudur|Borobudur]] construction, and Indian Ocean–Tang diplomatic cycles (including the era of the [[belitung-shipwreck|Belitung]] cargo).
- **produced** (as patronage) the Sailendra Buddhist monumental landscape.
- **followed_by** Sanjaya-line supremacy under the Raka of Pikatan and the Hindu culmination at Prambanan; Sailendra name persists in Sumatran/Kedah contexts.

## Historiography and Source Notes

Sources are almost entirely epigraphic (Sanskrit and Old Javanese/Kawi), with Chinese notices of Heling/Shepo providing external frames and Cham/Vietnamese annals for raids. No Sailendra “chronicle” survives. Key debates: identity of Panangkaran; whether Ligor B proves Sailendra control of Srivijaya; Balaputra’s career; and how far later Kedah “Sailendra” continuity is genealogical versus prestigious title reuse. King lists that omit Sailendras may reflect Sanjaya damnatio, genre bias, or genuine non-inclusion in certain ritual genealogies.
