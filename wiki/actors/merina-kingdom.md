# The Merina Kingdom (Madagascar)

The **Merina Kingdom** unified most of **Madagascar** in the nineteenth century — an indigenous Malagasy
state that pursued its own programme of Christianization and modernization before falling to France.
Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO [[gha-vol6-19th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. VI]]**
(ch. 16, Mutibwa).

## Unification and expansion

From the highland kingdom of **Imerina** (capital **Antananarivo**), **Andrianampoinimerina** (r. c.
1787–1810) united the central plateau, and his son **Radama I** (r. 1810–28) — recognized by Britain as
"King of Madagascar" — used a European-trained army to conquer much of the island, subduing the coastal
**Sakalava** and **Betsimisaraka** kingdoms. The Merina state rested on intensive **irrigated rice**
agriculture and a hierarchical society including a large class of slaves.

## Christianization, reaction, and modernization

Radama welcomed **British (London Missionary Society) missionaries**, who created a written Malagasy
language, schools, and printing. His successor **Ranavalona I** (r. 1828–61) reversed course, **expelling
missionaries and persecuting Christian converts** in a long nativist reaction that also resisted European
encroachment. Later monarchs and the powerful prime minister **Rainilaiarivony** re-embraced
**Christianity (declared the state religion, 1869)** and a programme of **modernization** — codified law,
schools, a modern army — making the Merina court a notable case of African/Malagasy **self-strengthening**.

## French conquest

Despite its modernization, the kingdom could not withstand French imperial pressure: after the
Franco-Hova wars, **France annexed Madagascar in 1896**, abolished the monarchy, and exiled the last queen,
**Ranavalona III** — folding the island into the French colonial empire.

## Significance

The Merina Kingdom is the great case of **indigenous state-building and modernization on Madagascar** — a
centralized Malagasy monarchy that unified the island, engaged Christianity and European technology on its
own terms, and resisted (ultimately unsuccessfully) the colonial conquest, paralleling the
self-strengthening projects of Egypt and Ethiopia.

> **GHA VII note.** The [[gha-vol7-colonial-1880-1935|General History of Africa, Vol. VII]] (ch. 9) covers
> Madagascar's **resistance and reaction to the French conquest** (1895–96) and the subsequent risings (the
> *menalamba* revolt) and nationalist stirrings under colonial rule, 1896–1935.
