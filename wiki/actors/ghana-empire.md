# The Empire of Ghana (Wagadu)

## Overview

The **Empire of Ghana** — called **Wagadu** by its own **Soninke** people, "Ghana" by Arab
geographers after the title of its king — was, in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Burstein,
Ch 23; Austen, Ch 24), the **first great empire of the West African Sahel** and the southern
anchor of the [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan trade]]. It marks the moment when "urban-based
states... spreading south of the desert" tied West Africa into the wider Afro-Eurasian world.

## Wealth from the Gold–Salt Trade

By the tenth century CE Ghana ruled a core territory **between the Niger and Senegal rivers**,
with authority extending over numerous **vassal states**. Its wealth came from controlling the
**sub-Saharan terminus of the caravan trade**: gold from the mines of **Bambuk and Buré** (and
Guinea) and slaves moved north, exchanged for **Saharan salt and copper** and North African
manufactures. This control of urban trade centers — heir to the proto-urban tradition of
**[[jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]]** and the Niger Delta — was the foundation of Sahelian imperial
power, later inherited by **Mali**.

## A Sacral Monarchy and the Double City

Arab geographers describe Ghana as a **sacral monarchy**: the king's relations with his
subjects were "hedged around with elaborate ceremonial," and kings were buried with members of
their court in lavishly furnished tombs — alongside a large standing army of infantry, archers,
and cavalry. Most striking is the capital, **Kumbi (Saleh)**, a **double city**: one town
inhabited by **Muslim merchants and scholars**, the other the **fortified residence of the
non-Muslim ("pagan") king**.

## Islam by Conversion, Not Conquest

CWH IV stresses that, unlike Egypt and North Africa, Islam reached the Sahel **not by conquest
but by the conversion of rulers** — who then accommodated the religions of their non-Muslim
subjects. The Kumbi double-city embodies this: Muslims "resided and traded on terms granted them
by the kings of Ghana." The result was a distinctive Sahelian Islam, retaining pre-Islamic
practices, that puzzled later visitors such as **Ibn Battuta**. By the later eleventh century
Ghana's rulers had themselves become Muslim, under pressure from the **Almoravids**.

## Wagadu in the Western Sudanic Sequence (CWH V)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Conrad, Ch 22) places Ghana — known to its own Soninke
descendants as **Wagadu** — as the first in the sequence of
[[western-sudan-states|Western Sudanic]] states (Wagadu → [[mali-empire|Mali]] →
[[songhay-empire|Songhay]]), all drawing power from trans-Saharan trade and control of
production and trading centers. Conrad's chapter foregrounds the methodological point
that this history is reconstructed by combining Arabic written sources with
**archaeology, epigraphy, linguistics, and West African oral tradition** — and that
the nearby trade city of **[[jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]]** reveals a *heterarchical*,
non-despotic, "self-organizing" urbanism (Roderick McIntosh) without kings or
monumental power-symbols, a distinctively West African path that complicates older
state-centric models of how trade cities arise.

## Significance

Ghana is CWH IV's prime example of indigenous West African **state formation** driven by
**long-distance trade**, and of the peaceful, ruler-led **Islamization** that integrated the
Sahel into the Afro-Eurasian commercial and religious world by 900 CE. It set the template for
the medieval Sudanic empires of [[mali-empire|**Mali**]] and [[songhay-empire|**Songhay**]].

## Historiography

Ghana is known almost entirely through **Arabic geographers** (al-Bakri and others) plus
archaeology at Kumbi Saleh and the Niger cities — its own people left no written records.
CWH IV warns against the old bias of dating African developments to their **first mention in
foreign sources**: Ghana was clearly powerful well before the surviving descriptions.

## GHA III: al-Bakri's Ghana and the Almoravid question

The **UNESCO [[gha-vol3-7th-11th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. III]]** (ch. 14, Devisse; ch. 6,
de Medeiros) supplies the classic eleventh-century portrait drawn from **al-Bakri (1068)**: the **Soninke**
empire of **Wagadu**, its twin capital at **Kumbi Saleh** (a royal/pagan town and a separate Muslim
merchants' town), the king's gold monopoly and bureaucracy, and the Saharan emporium of **Awdaghust**. GHA
also reframes the long-told story that the **[[almoravids|Almoravids]] conquered Ghana c. 1076**: recent
scholarship (Devisse and others) treats this as **overstated** — relations between the desert Sanhaja and
the Soninke were as much commercial and gradual as military, and Ghana's decline owed more to shifting
trade routes and the rise of successor states than to a single conquest. The **Islamization** of Ghana, as
elsewhere in the Sahel, came chiefly **through the merchant class (the Wangara)**, not the sword (see
[[islamization-of-africa]]).
