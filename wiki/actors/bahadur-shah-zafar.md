# Bahadur Shah Zafar (Bahadur Shah II)

**Bahadur Shah Zafar** (1775–1862), the last of the [[mughal-empire|Mughal emperors]], was a
mystic, calligrapher, and one of the finest **Urdu poets** of his age, who presided over a
brilliant cultural twilight in a Delhi that had long since lost real power — and who, in his
eighties, was swept up as the reluctant figurehead of the **[[indian-rebellion-1857|Rebellion of
1857]]**, with whose defeat the dynasty of [[babur|Babur]] came to an end. From
[[dalrymple-the-last-mughal|William Dalrymple's *The Last Mughal*]] (2006).

## A powerless emperor

By Zafar's accession in 1837 the "King of Delhi" was a pensioner of the [[english-east-india-company|
East India Company]], his authority confined to the Red Fort and its environs while the Company
ruled India in his nominal name. His court was nonetheless the centre of a remarkable late-Mughal
renaissance in poetry and the arts — the age of **Ghalib** and **Zauq** — and Zafar, a Sufi by
inclination and Poet Laureate's patron (and poet himself), embodied the cosmopolitan Indo-Islamic
culture of old Delhi.

## 1857

When the mutinous sepoys of Meerut rode to Delhi in **May 1857** and forced their way into the
palace, they proclaimed the aged Zafar their emperor and leader. Dalrymple's account, drawn from
the Persian/Urdu **Mutiny Papers**, shows him initially horrified by the rough rebels and an
ineffectual, vacillating figurehead over a chaotic rebel administration torn between the sepoys,
the *jihadi* preachers, and the city's people. He nonetheless lent the revolt the one thing that
could unite Hindu and Muslim rebels: the **legitimacy of the Mughal crown**.

## Fall and exile

The British **recaptured Delhi in September 1857** after a bloody siege and subjected the city to
ferocious **reprisals** — a general sack, mass executions, and the shooting of Zafar's sons and
grandson by Captain Hodson. Zafar himself was captured at Humayun's tomb, put on **trial** (1858)
as a rebel, and **exiled to Rangoon**, Burma, where he died in obscurity in 1862 and was buried in
an unmarked grave — the deliberate, total extinction of the dynasty.

## Significance

Zafar's deposition ended the **Mughal Empire** in name as 1707 had ended it in substance, and
cleared the way for the transfer of India to the **British Crown (1858)**. As a symbol he has had a
long afterlife: a figure of pathos and, later, of anti-colonial and composite-culture nostalgia —
the poet-king whose own lines ("how unlucky is Zafar, that for burial / he could not get even two
yards of land in the land of his beloved") became his epitaph.
