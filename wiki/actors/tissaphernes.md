# Tissaphernes

## Overview

**Tissaphernes** (d. 395 BCE) was the leading western [[achaemenid-satrapy|satrap]] of his
generation — of Lydia and Caria — and the chief Persian agent in the endgame of the Peloponnesian
War and its aftermath. [[sources/briant-from-cyrus-to-alexander-2002|Briant]] uses him to
illustrate the empire's real western strategy: not matching Greek hoplites in pitched battle but
**financial warfare and the calculated manipulation of Greek rivalries** — a rational policy
repeatedly misread by Greek sources as Persian weakness or treachery.

## Role and Key Events

- **Funding the war (412–411).** Tissaphernes negotiated the subsidies that let Sparta build a
  fleet against Athens, while — on Alcibiades' advice — keeping both Greek powers balanced rather
  than handing Sparta a quick victory, so as to exhaust them all in Persia's interest.
- **Eclipse and revenge.** When [[cyrus-the-younger|Cyrus the Younger]] received supreme command
  of the west (c. 407), Tissaphernes lost ground; he became Cyrus's enemy and warned
  [[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes II]] of the revolt, letting the king prepare for
  [[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|Cunaxa]] (401), where Tissaphernes commanded cavalry.
- **Restoration and strategy.** Rewarded with the satrapy of Lydia and Caria and tasked with
  bringing the Ionian cities under Persian control, he faced the Spartan king Agesilaus (396–394).
  His refusal to risk a pitched battle was, Briant argues, deliberate calculation, not timidity —
  Persia's leverage lay in gold and diplomacy.
- **Fall (395).** Blamed for the failures in Asia Minor, he was lured to Celaenae under pretext of
  negotiation and beheaded on the orders of the queen mother Parysatis, who had never forgiven his
  role against her favoured son Cyrus.

## Counterfactual Significance

Tissaphernes' career encapsulates why the Greco-Persian frontier looked the way it did: Persian
"passivity" on land was a strategic choice that worked — the [[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]]
(386) ultimately delivered Persia everything Tissaphernes had aimed at. Read as weakness, his policy
fed the Greek "decadent empire" narrative; read as strategy, it shows the empire mastering Greece
without conquering it.

## Historiography

The portrait depends almost wholly on Thucydides and Xenophon (Hellenica, Anabasis) — partisan,
Athenocentric, and personally hostile after Cunaxa. Briant's rehabilitation reads against their grain,
recovering the rationality of a satrap they cast as the archetypal treacherous Oriental.
