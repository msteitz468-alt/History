# Dene

Collective label for Northern Athapaskan-speaking peoples of the boreal forest and adjacent tundra from northwest of the Churchill River through the Mackenzie basin to the Yukon Valley and interior Alaska. Ray (CHNPA I.2) uses the spelling “Athapaskan” for the linguistic family and regional ethnonyms (Chipewyan, Dogrib, Slavey, Hare, Kutchin, Beaver, etc.); this page treats them as the Dene actor set for Subarctic postcontact history. Coastal Tlingit Na-Dene links are noted under Northwest Coast pages, not here.

## Overview — contact map

At European contact Athapaskan speakers inhabited the boreal forest northwest of the Churchill; Algonquians held the Churchill Valley and woodlands eastward.

| Group (Ray’s terms) | Approximate contact location |
|---|---|
| **Chipewyan** | N of Churchill along forest edge and adjacent tundra |
| **Beaver** | SW of Chipewyans (distinct dialect; least defined regional variant) |
| **Dogrib / Yellowknife** | NW of Chipewyans (common dialect with Slavey and Hare) |
| **Slavey** | Liard and Mackenzie as far N as Great Bear Lake |
| **Kaska / Mountain Indians** | SW / NW of Slavey country |
| **Hare** | N/W of Great Bear (two groups) |
| **Eastern / Western Kutchin** | Lower Mackenzie to Chandalar (upper Yukon tributary); dialect often unintelligible to neighbors |
| **Yukon Valley** | Tutchone, Han, Tanana, Koyukon |
| **South of Yukon Valley** | Ahtna, Tanaina, Kolchan, Ingalik |

**Subsistence variation:** lower Mackenzie and east/south toward Churchill—caribou + fishing + berries; Yukon headwaters/Liard—heavier moose; Alaska plateau—big game; Peace–Athabasca—bison and moose. Fishing dominant for upper Yukon/Liard Athapaskans. Religion: animal spirits, vision/dream contact; most Athapaskans would not touch river otter (spirits of the dead). Chipewyans used **chute-and-pound** drives on barren-ground caribou; winter local bands on northern forest fringes west of Hudson Bay numbered 400–1,000 at initial contact.

## Delayed fur-trade integration and middleman phase

Athapaskans remained peripheral while Assiniboine–Cree middlemen advanced to Athabasca (~1715). **Fort Churchill / Prince of Wales (1717)** enabled Chipewyan regular trade and armed pushback that stopped Cree advance on the lower Churchill by mid-century. Before 1763 Chipewyans primarily served as middlemen to more distant Yellowknives and Dogribs because their own territory was poorly stocked with furbearers.

**1694–1720 Cree wars** on middle/lower Churchill and upper Churchill toward Athabasca pushed Chipewyan and Beaver frontiers back—Knight heard reports of as many as 6,000 men dead (Ray: undoubted exaggeration, indicates intensity). **Post-1781–82:** after Cree foes were decimated by smallpox, trader-encouraged Chipewyans shifted into full boreal forest; by 1821 held most country N of Churchill and E of Athabasca as three clusters—**Caribou Eaters** (forest–tundra), **Athabascas** (Lake Athabasca), and upper Churchill occupants.

Peace River **Beavers** (and lower-valley Crees) were strategic provisioners of wood bison/moose/elk for Athabasca–Mackenzie brigades; NWC used force including to block HBC 1802–15, and debt collection abuses (seizing wives) led temporary Beaver withdrawal.

Hearne 1771 (Coppermine) and Mackenzie 1789 reached Arctic coasts with Athapaskan companions—Bloody Falls attack on Inuit shaped later wariness (`concurrent_with: [[inuit]]`; arctic narrative primarily under [[arctic-native-history-norse-to-modern]]). Chipewyan desertion of Keewatin interior after smallpox is one factor in the inland “Caribou Eskimo” adaptation on west Hudson Bay.

## Interior Alaska and far-western Subarctic

Russian coastal expansion late 1780s sent goods inland via Native routes; face-to-face trade only with **Nulato (1839)** and HBC **Fort Yukon (1847)** at Yukon–Porcupine confluence → heavier fur involvement, accelerated culture change; Russian Orthodox impact slight until mid-20th c. US Alaska takeover 1867 and Yukon steamboats 1869: repeating rifles depleted caribou, increased moose, communal hunting yielded to individualistic; ~1900 fish wheels → fish camps then larger river settlements; outboards and tech forced wage work; schools 1940s–50s nucleated settlement. Late 1960s Kolchans joined Alaska Federation of Natives; **ANCSA 1971** entitled Athapaskan-speaking groups to cash share and portion of 15.6 million acres via regional/village corporations (`part_of: [[alaska-native-claims-settlement-1971]]`).

## Modern Mackenzie claims and Berger Inquiry

1971 multinational $7 billion gas pipeline (Alaska + Mackenzie delta south) → **Mackenzie Valley Pipeline Inquiry** (Justice Thomas Berger, 1974–76 community hearings). Dene, Métis, and Inuit spoke against construction until claims settled. Berger 1977: no Yukon line; Mackenzie delay ≥10 years; project would not solve Native employment; social costs high until institutions and claims settled.

During hearings Dene obtained a **1973 caveat** stopping resource exploration; NWT court doubted Treaties Eight and Eleven extinguished Dene title (chiefs may not have understood terms). **1975 Dene Declaration** demanded equitable land claims settlement and self-government within Canada. Changing public opinion (partly Berger-fostered), s.35 Constitution Act 1982 aboriginal/treaty rights, and more flexible 1986 federal claims policy facilitated talks. **1988** Mackenzie Valley Dene and Métis AIP; **1990** annual assembly rejected the accord (partly *Sparrow* suggested more extensive rights) but allowed regional groups separate agreements on 1988 principles. Yukon umbrella AIP: ~16,000 sq mi settlement lands with continuing aboriginal rights. 1982 NWT division plebiscite separated western (Dene, Métis, Euro-Canadian) and eastern Inuit-majority (Nunavut) constitutional paths—western claims harder due to divergent interests.

Washburn renaissance chapter: Berger commission also opened fundamental legal/political questions of Native status beyond its formal pipeline remit (`concurrent_with: [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]]`).

## Counterfactual significance

Later and more uneven entry into Bay-centered trade meant Athapaskan groups retained different bargaining sequences than Cree middlemen—until firearms and posts reorganized the Churchill–Athabasca frontier. Without Berger-era testimony and the Dene Declaration frame, Canadian northern claims and pipeline regulation timelines shift; ANCSA corporate model remains the distinct US path for Alaska Athapaskans.

## Historiography

Yerbury and Abel (*Drum Songs*) span fur trade through modern social-economic responses; Ray’s bibliographic essay flags Athapaskan monographs as thinner than Cree/Ojibwa fur-trade studies. Contact ethnonym/dialect maps are approximate; Kutchin intelligibility and Beaver dialect boundaries poorly defined in early sources. Treaties 8/11 extinguishment remains legally and historiographically contested after the 1973 caveat period. Na-Dene/Tlingit coastal relationships belong to Northwest Coast historiography, not this Subarctic actor summary.
