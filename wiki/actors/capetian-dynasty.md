# The Capetian Dynasty and Late-Carolingian West Francia

In the long tenth century the west Frankish kingdom — the future France — passed from the failing **late [[carolingian-empire|Carolingians]]** to the **Robertine/Capetian** house, while real power devolved to the great **territorial principalities**. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Jean Dunbabin) frames the century as "crucial in the evolution of the west Frankish kingdom": in 888 its future was wholly uncertain (reabsorption into empire or disintegration both possible); by 1000 a single king was recognised "from the northernmost tip of Flanders to the Pyrenees," even if his real authority was thin.

## The Last Carolingians (898–987)

The Carolingian kings — **Charles the Simple** (898–922), **Louis IV d'Outremer** (936–954), **Lothar** (954–986), and **Louis V** (986–987) — held a shrinking "royal principality" around **Laon and Rheims**, repeatedly outmanoeuvred by the **Robertines** (descendants of Robert the Strong) who controlled Neustria from the Seine to the Loire (the "Robertine wall," Werner) and twice took the crown themselves (**Robert I**, 922–923; **Radulf/Raoul** of Burgundy, 923–936). Dunbabin stresses that the **declining numbers of royal charters** (five a year under Charles the Simple, fewer than two under Lothar) and the rise of recipient-produced and princely charters index the contraction of royal power — though recent regional scholarship reframes the princes not as rebels but as **legitimate partners in rule**, making the century "an interesting constitutional experiment in decentralised government."

A landmark of the period was the **[[treaty-of-saint-clair-sur-epte-911|Treaty of Saint-Clair-sur-Epte (911)]]**, by which Charles the Simple settled the Viking leader **Rollo** on the lower Seine — creating the nucleus of **Normandy** and establishing that the king's authority to *make peace* still mattered even as princes (Robert of Neustria, Richard le Justicier of Burgundy) did the actual fighting against Vikings and Magyars.

## The Capetian Accession (987)

On the death of the childless **Louis V** (987), Archbishop **Adalbero of Rheims** and the magnates **rejected the Carolingian claimant Charles of Lorraine** and elected **Hugh Capet** (duke of the Franks, the leading Robertine) king. As [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|Nelson (NCMH III)]] notes, this was *not* forced by the lack of a Carolingian alternative — Charles was deliberately passed over (Richer reports Adalbero commending Hugh as a "protector of private as well as public interest"). Hugh at once had his son **Robert II "the Pious"** consecrated co-king (Christmas 987), founding the practice of **associative succession** that would let the Capetians cling to the throne for centuries. By 1024 only one king, Robert II, was recognised across west Francia.

## The Territorial Principalities

The substance of power lay with the princes: **Normandy** (the dukes from Rollo), **Flanders** (the Baldwins), **Burgundy**, **Aquitaine/Poitou** (the dukes William), **Toulouse**, the **county of Barcelona/Catalonia**, and **Anjou/Blois**. In the south especially (the Peace of God heartland; see [[peace-of-god]]) royal authority was nominal, homages slow or withheld (the count of Barcelona never performed one). The fragmentation of these principalities into castellanies in the decades around 1000 is the empirical core of the [[feudal-revolution-debate|*mutation de l'an mil*]] debate — for which west Francia (with Catalonia) is the classic ground.

## Significance

The Capetian accession of 987 founded the dynasty that would, over three centuries, rebuild a strong French monarchy from the tiny royal demesne around **Paris and Orléans**. In the tenth century itself, however, the Capetian king was weaker than his [[ottonian-dynasty|Ottonian]] or [[kingdom-of-england-late-anglo-saxon|English]] contemporaries — the crown's survival owed less to royal resources than (Nelson argues) to the aristocracy's continuing investment in a "regnal community" and to the drift toward indivisible, associative succession.
