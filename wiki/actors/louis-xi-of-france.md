# Louis XI of France

King of France (r. 1461–1483), the ruthless and devious monarch who broke "the princely polyarchy"
and consolidated the recovered French state. Treated by Bernard Chevalier in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|
NCMH VII]] (Ch. 19b). Chevalier's revisionist reading replaces the legend of the all-controlling
"universal spider" with a king who more often "lived in fear of being overthrown or assassinated,"
yet who, by surviving and outlasting his enemies, secured the triumph of the monarchy over the great
principalities.

## Role and Decisions

Succeeding his father **[[valois-dynasty|Charles VII]]** in 1461, Louis — who had himself led the
princes in rebellion as dauphin — quickly antagonised everyone with arrogant reforms, provoking the
aristocratic **War of the Public Weal (1465)**, led by Burgundy, Bourbon and Brittany on behalf of
his brother Charles. Saved only by the loyalty of the **bonnes villes** and his own near-abject
surrender, Louis spent the next decade in a "life-and-death struggle" with the princes:

- **The Burgundian duel.** Humiliated at **Péronne (1468)** by **[[valois-burgundian-state|Charles
  the Bold]]**, Louis bided his time; the coalition of 1475 fizzled, letting him buy off Edward IV of
  England at **Picquigny (1475)** and execute the conspirators (the constable Saint-Pol). Charles's
  death at **Nancy (1477)** delivered Louis from his greatest danger.
- **Aggrandisement.** He seized the **duchy of Burgundy** (1477), fought Maximilian of Habsburg for
  the rest of the inheritance (settled at Arras, 1482), absorbed the **house of Anjou's lands** in
  1481 (Anjou, Maine, Provence, and the claim to Naples), and recovered Roussillon and other
  territories — leaving the monarchy vastly enlarged.
- **The instruments of power.** He doubled taxation to fund an arms race and an enlarged standing
  army (a costly permanent infantry from 1480), built the royal postal system, abolished the
  Pragmatic Sanction in favour of a **concordat** regime with the papacy, asserted himself as
  *empereur dans son royaume*, and promoted the Lyons fairs against Geneva.

## Counterfactual Significance

Chevalier insists the monarchy was **"fragile" in mid-century**, the principalities (Burgundy,
Brittany, Bourbon, Anjou) being "genuine, well-organised states," not feudal relics. Much of Louis's
success owed to **luck** — above all Charles the Bold's self-destruction "with which the king had
little to do." But Louis's relentless intimidation broke the princes' capacity to resist; after his
reign "no princely coalition would in future be capable of effective resistance," and the aristocracy
came to believe "its best chance of self-preservation lay within the bosom of the monarchy." He is a
central figure in the rise of the **[[new-monarchy|"new monarchy"]]**.
