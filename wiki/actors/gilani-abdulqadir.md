## Overview

'Abdulqâdir Gîlânî (1077–1166) was the Baghdad Sufi preacher who became the most widely revered Sufi pîr of the Middle Periods — the foundational figure around whom the Qadiriyya, the most widespread and enduring of the [[tariqa-orders|tariqa orders]], was posthumously organized. Born to a family of sayyids (claimed descendants of 'Ali) in Gilan, south of the Caspian, he came to Baghdad as a youth for religious studies and spent decades in spiritual discipline before emerging as a public teacher at approximately fifty years of age. His sermons attracted "crowds" that required Baghdad's outdoor festival prayer grounds to contain them (Hodgson).

## Life and Spiritual Formation

His family background as sayyids — a designation that carried both religious prestige and expectations of religious vocation — oriented him toward Islamic learning from boyhood. His mother, sending him off to Baghdad with his inheritance "in the form of eighty gold coins sewed up in his coat," extracted a promise "never to tell a falsehood." The legendary robber-conversion story — in which Gilani disclosed his gold to a robber who then converted at his honesty — circulated as the first of many such tales and became paradigmatic of his saintly character: moral rectitude as the ground of spiritual authority.

At Baghdad he chose as his spiritual guide a syrup vendor "extraordinarily severe in disciplining him" — deliberate submission to a non-prestigious pîr, demonstrating humility as a spiritual practice. His subsequent years of solitary discipline (in Khuzistan and then Baghdad), "until he was about fifty years old," followed the classical Sufi pattern of intensive self-purification before public ministry.

## Preaching and Public Life

Upon achieving "the spiritual maturity he was seeking, and under what he believed was divine guidance," Gilani combined public teaching with institutional activity. He received a [[madrasa]] of his own, "where he lectured on all the standard religious subjects: Qur'an, hadîth, fiqh jurisprudence, etc." — representing the integration of Sufi piety with 'ulama learning that [[al-ghazali]] had legitimized. He also married (four wives; forty-nine children), treating marriage not as a hindrance but as a social duty appropriate to a public figure. His observance of the Hanbali legal madhhab — "in Baghdad still very popular and was associated at once with Shari'ah rigor and concern for the religious good sense of ordinary people" — grounded his Sufism in the legal tradition closest to popular piety.

His daily routine combined ascetic fasting (year-round, not only in Ramadan) with massive public engagement: Friday and Wednesday morning preaching in the outdoor prayer grounds; afternoon fatwa decisions; daily bread distribution to the poor.

## Teaching: Accessible Mysticism

His spiritual teaching avoided the speculative metaphysics that alarmed Shari'ah-minded 'ulama. Instead, he offered accessible moral counsels organized around ten virtues that could "lead to full spirituality" if followed with consistency. Hodgson summarizes:

> "Each of the virtues is at least seemingly very simple, accessible to anyone... But each of them is inward-looking and can lead far if carried through consistently."

The virtues include: not swearing by God either truthfully or falsely; not speaking untruth even in jest; not breaking promises; not cursing or harming anything; not praying for harm to anyone; not accusing anyone of religious infidelity. The progression moves from practical self-control to comprehensive moral awareness without requiring any metaphysical speculation.

He is said to have converted many non-Muslims and won Muslim sinners to repentance. "The perfect saint whom he described was a humble man content with whatever God might assign him either in this world or in the next."

## The Qadiriyya (Posthumous)

Gilani "does not seem consciously to have founded any order, or to have appointed a successor in that sense." After his death, one of his sons took control of his assembly hall and the endowments that had accumulated in his name. As disciples' disciples in turn acknowledged his authority through their pîrs, the recognizable structure of a [[tariqa-orders|tariqa order]] emerged: a silsilah chain traced through him, a central authority at his tomb in Baghdad, and the annual gathering of followers at the tomb anniversary.

The standard biography composed a century after his death transformed the practical preacher into a supreme miracle-worker, "omitting all his practical work and teaching, in favour of an imaginary exalted figure in which the common person could take refuge for intercession and consolation" (Hodgson). The tension between the historically accessible Gilani and the legendary quṭb of hagiography illustrates the general dynamic of Sufi saint veneration.

The Qadiriyya became "the most famous and perhaps always most widespread" of all tariqa orders, spreading eventually throughout the Islamic world from West Africa to Southeast Asia.

## Cosmic Status

In later Sufi cosmology, Gilani was held to have been the [[qutb-saint|quṭb]] (cosmic axis) of his time. Nizamuddin Awliya (a major Delhi Sufi of the late thirteenth century) narrated how a saint who flew over Gilani's worship service "without doing obeisance was immediately cast to the ground" — the story encoding the quṭb's cosmic authority.
