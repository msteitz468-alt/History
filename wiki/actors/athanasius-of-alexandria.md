# Athanasius of Alexandria

**Athanasius** (c. 296–373 CE) was **Bishop of Alexandria for forty-five years from AD 328** and
the dominant literary and ecclesiastical figure of fourth-century Egyptian Christianity. He was the
great champion of **Nicene orthodoxy** against Arianism and, through his *Life of Antony*, a
principal populariser of monasticism.

## Role and significance

Athanasius devoted most of his career to defending the doctrines of the
**[[council-of-nicaea-325|Council of Nicaea]] (325)** — the full divinity and consubstantiality of
Christ — against the followers of **Arius**, who held the Son to be a created and subordinate
being. The struggle made him one of the most embattled bishops in history: he was repeatedly
**exiled** (five times) as the imperial court swung toward Arian or compromise positions, and he
fought equally against the schismatic Meletians who contested his episcopate. His tenacity — "his
struggles with emperors and other bishops made him a larger-than-life figure" — fixed the Nicene
formula at the centre of Christian orthodoxy.

His most historically influential work, however, was the **_Life of Antony_**, the spiritual
biography of the hermit [[antony-of-egypt|Antony]] that, in Latin translation, "has inspired
ascetics everywhere" and carried the ideal of [[egyptian-monasticism|Egyptian monasticism]] into
the Greek and Latin worlds. Athanasius thus linked the two great forces of fourth-century
Christianity — episcopal orthodoxy and desert asceticism — into a single tradition, allying the
Alexandrian see with the prestige of the monks.

## Counterfactual significance

Athanasius is a strong case for individual agency in doctrinal history. The Nicene settlement was
politically precarious for decades after 325; a less obstinate Alexandrian bishop, or one willing
to accept the "Homoian" compromises pressed by emperors such as Constantius II, might have left
Arianism or a semi-Arian formula as the imperial norm. His refusal — *Athanasius contra mundum*,
"Athanasius against the world" — helped ensure that the eventual orthodoxy of the empire was
Nicene. His see's later prestige underwrote the assertiveness of the
[[coptic-church|Alexandrian patriarchate]] down to Chalcedon.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Attributed claims from [[sources/mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015|Stephen Mitchell, *A History of the Later Roman Empire* (2015)]].

- **Melitian schism and violent consolidation of Alexandrian power.** Melitius rejected Peter of Alexandria’s wartime leniency; Nicaea allowed coexistence under Alexandrian authority, but Athanasius refused power-sharing (Nicaea can. 6 on Alexandrian control of Libya/Egypt/Pentapolis) and “went into battle against them in word and deed,” using ascetics and intimidation. Melitian charges (Ischyras’s chalice; Arsenius “murder,” exposed as false) plus papyrus evidence of kidnapping, beating, and imprisonment of Melitian clergy. Tyre 335 deposed him; he intercepted Constantine; a grain-fleet conspiracy charge by Eusebius of Nicomedia’s faction led to exile to Trier without formal deposition.
- **Nicene champion and East–West conflict.** For ~50 years he defended [[council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea]] against Arians and [[constantius-ii|Constantius]]; 339 refuge at Rome drew Julius and Constans into conflict culminating in the failed joint Serdica council (western/eastern split to Philippopolis).
