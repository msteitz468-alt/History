# John Hancock

## Overview

**John Hancock** (1737–1793), merchant heir, Massachusetts House member, and later
president of the Continental Congress and governor of Massachusetts, was the wealthy
public face of Boston resistance and a close—sometimes uneasy—political partner of
[[adams-samuel|Samuel Adams]]. Crown warrants targeted both men before Lexington;
[[revere-paul|Paul Revere]]’s ride aimed in part to protect them.
[[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Stacy Schiff]] (2022) emphasizes Hancock’s
pomp and resources beside Adams’s studied poverty: Hancock traveled with “the pomp
and retinue of an Eastern prince,” in gold-trimmed crimson velvet and embroidered
white vest, while Adams fretted over “political idolatry.”

## Role

Fortune, visibility, and ceremonial leadership more than doctrinal invention. Orphaned
young, raised partly in the Clarke parsonage, adopted by a Boston uncle whose fortune
he inherited in his mid-twenties, making him one of the richest men in the province.
Even an indulgent biographer (quoted by Schiff) called him a “foppish
pseudo-aristocrat.” Adams recruited him correctly surmising he would “revel in glory
as in frivolity,” open his coffers, and be esteemed. Hancock underwrote patriot
banquets and processions, served as master of ceremonies, and later showered Boston
with fire engines, libraries, church bells, streetlights, and free firewood—a visitor
dubbed him “the King of the Rabble.”

## Career

**Entry and Stamp–Townshend years.** Adams tapped twenty-nine-year-old Hancock for
the House (spring after Stamp Act politics; Hancock had failed an earlier bid),
gesturing toward the Beacon Hill mansion: “perhaps another John might prove more
useful… His fortune would please the party.” Hancock was the largest donor (nearly a
quarter) to the ~£1,100 subscription that settled Adams’s tax-collector arrears. In
June 1768 customs impounded his ninety-ton sloop (silencing a critic as much as
chastening a smuggler); a crowd burned a customs barge—Adams insisted Hancock had
personally dispersed the throng. Hancock initially held back from the 1767 luxury
boycott (Revere agreed; Hancock did not) but entered nonimportation with the bulk of
merchants in August 1768.

**Rift and reconciliation with Adams.** After the Massacre aftermath and nonimportation
collapse, the partnership frayed. Late 1771 Hutchinson heard Hancock had “sworn off all
connection with Adams.” Hancock accepted captaincy of an eighty-man ceremonial guard
(gold-buttoned uniforms) while declining a Council seat; April 1772 he pushed a town
finance inquiry meant to embarrass Adams, then after mutual-friend reconciliation
picked Adams up in his coach—yet they “rarely voted the same way” (“John Puff” and
“the psalm-singer”). Family lore: Hancock sealed peace by arranging Adams’s Copley
portrait for display in the Beacon Hill mansion. When Hancock contemplated resigning
after a slight, Adams stiffened him: “You say you have been spoken ill of. What then?”

**Tea Party to Lexington.** Hancock joined Adams, Edes, and others on tea-ship watch;
at the 30 November meeting he vowed he would “spend his fortune and life itself” for
American liberty (Hutchinson found none who would swear they heard it). Rotch’s inquiry
named Adams, Hancock, Young, Molineux, and Warren as prime movers. Adams tapped him for
the 1774 Massacre oration—Hancock’s first public address; Tories said Cooper and
Samuel composed it. With Adams he was at the Lexington parsonage when Revere arrived
(~12:30 a.m., 18–19 April 1775); aflutter cleaning his gun, he later sent for aunt,
fiancée, and “excellent first-of-season salmon.” Both fled by coach hours before the
[[battles-of-lexington-and-concord-1775|engagement]]. Gage’s martial-law amnesty
exempted only Adams, Hancock, and abettors.

**Congress and later friction.** Hero’s progress south: “no person could possibly be
more noticed than myself.” Mortified when John and Samuel Adams engineered
Washington’s command nomination (Hancock had assumed the tribute for himself)—a slight
that haunted the partnership. Later rumor-mongering (Conway Cabal) and contrasting
styles (“you found the money, and Sam Adams the brains”) kept the alliance
transactional. As governor he pardoned Shays’ rebels whom Adams would have hanged.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Hancock’s fortune, social reach, and signature visibility, Boston resistance
would have looked poorer and less respectable to other colonies; without Adams’s
recruitment and spine-stiffening, Hancock’s commitment was intermittent. The pair
embodied the coalition of purse and propaganda that made Massachusetts leadership
credible outside New England.

## Historiography

Schiff (2022) is the main source here: Hancock as Adams’s prize recruit, money man,
occasional rival, and ceremonial co-star—not as independent strategist. Primary
color from Hutchinson’s sneers (“master of the puppets” aimed at Adams using Hancock),
town and House records, and later family lore (Copley commission). Full merchant and
congressional biography is thinner in the collection until a dedicated Hancock source
is ingested.
