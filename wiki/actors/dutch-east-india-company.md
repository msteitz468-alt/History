# Dutch East India Company (Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie, VOC)

## Overview

The VOC (founded 1602) was the most powerful and best-capitalized **chartered
joint-stock company** of the seventeenth century, and the prototype of the modern
corporation — the first commercial entity financed **entirely via the stock
market**, with permanent tradable shares, separation of ownership and management,
and limited liability for managers and investors alike (Trivellato and Parker,
CWH VI). It was created when the States General forced competing Dutch "pre-
companies" to merge into a single corporation organized into six regional
**"chambers."** It is treated more fully as an institution at
[[early-modern-commerce]].

## A Quasi-State

The VOC was not merely a firm but the **military and administrative arm of the
Dutch Republic** in Asia: granted a national monopoly and the right to wage war,
make treaties, and govern territory with little state intrusion. Its directors
and governors held legal jurisdiction over employees, ships, and subjects in its
outposts. Whatever economic supremacy it achieved "derived at least as much from
military force as from managerial and financial organization." It built its
capital, **Batavia** (1619, modern Jakarta), expelled the Portuguese from Japan
and Taiwan, and inserted itself into the lucrative **intra-Asian "country
trade"** — exchanging Japanese silver, Indian textiles, and East Indies spices —
to fund its purchases without draining bullion from Europe.

The company's monopoly was enforced with notorious violence. To secure control of
nutmeg, cloves, and mace, Governor-General **Jan Pieterszoon Coen** waged a
**punitive campaign against Banda in 1621**, destroying crops and massacring or
deporting the local population — the starkest instance of "empire-building serving
the interests of commerce." In Indonesia the VOC built a **slave-plantation
economy** for spice production resembling that of the New World.

## In the Wiki's Framework

The VOC exemplifies the [[interpolity-law|interpolity-law]] dynamics of the era —
its agents notoriously posed as envoys of the fictitious "king of Holland" to
satisfy diplomatic protocol, and it traded **protection** to weaker rulers (the
sultan of Ternate accepted the company as *beschermheer* in 1607 in exchange for
a clove monopoly). It is a central actor in [[early-modern-commerce]],
[[indian-ocean-trade]], and [[global-silver-trade]], and a counterpart to the
[[english-east-india-company|English East India Company]]. Its eighteenth-century
decline tracked the crises of the Amsterdam stock market (1763, 1773) and the end
of the Dutch Golden Age; it was dissolved in 1799.

James Tracy (CWH VI) qualifies the VOC's apparent dominance: even with its
profitable country trade, the company still paid for **up to 80 percent of its
European exports with silver shipped out from home**, because the balance of trade
ran heavily in Asia's favor and there was little Asian demand for European goods —
and its total shipments to Europe were a small fraction of overall Asian trade
volume. The VOC depended on the Asian commercial world far more than it commanded
it (see [[global-silver-trade]], [[english-east-india-company]]).

## Historiography

Long hailed as the "first modern corporation," the VOC is now more often read as
a **transitional** form — bridging medieval partnership and the modern
corporation — and as a hybrid **company-state** whose sovereignty and violence
were as constitutive as its finance. See [[trade-diaspora]] for Curtin's
provocative grouping of the companies with stateless merchant networks.
