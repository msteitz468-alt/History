# Society of Jesus (Jesuits)

The Roman Catholic religious order founded by Ignatius of Loyola and confirmed in 1540,
which became the most important missionary institution on the frontiers of colonial South
America. In the [[guarani-jesuit-missions|Guaraní mission province]], western
[[western-amazonia-colonial-1540-1820|Amazonia]] ([[mainas-mission|Maynas]]), the
[[chaco-frontier-1573-1882|Chaco]], and the Llanos, Jesuits gathered indigenous peoples
into [[reduccion|reductions]] — self-governing mission towns combining evangelization,
communal production, and (among the Guaraní) armed defense against
[[bandeirantes|bandeirante]] slaving.

This is the concise summary; the order recurs across the colonial chapters of
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-2|CHNPA III Part 2]].

## Role and Significance

- **Frontier missionization** — the Jesuits built the most elaborate and autonomous mission
  systems in South America, shielding (and also controlling) indigenous populations.
- **Economic enterprise** — mission estates and the yerba-mate and cattle economies made
  the order a major economic actor, fueling suspicion from settlers and the crown.
- **Expulsion (1767)** — the Bourbon expulsion of the Jesuits from Spanish (and 1759
  Portuguese) America dismantled the missions, exposing their populations to settler
  predation and accelerating dispersal.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Jesuit reductions, the colonial demographic and territorial map of the La Plata
and Upper Amazon frontiers — and the survival of peoples like the [[guarani|Guaraní]] as
distinct communities — would have differed substantially; the order's expulsion was itself
a turning point that left many mission peoples newly vulnerable.
