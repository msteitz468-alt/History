## Overview

The Buyids (in Arabic, Buwayhids) were a Shi'i Persian dynasty from Daylam, a small rugged sub-Caspian highland territory, who rose from military captains to become the dominant power across western Iran and Iraq between 934 and 1055. Their occupation of [[baghdad]] in 945 marked the formal end of the [[abbasid-caliphate]] as an independent empire, though they chose to maintain the caliphs as ceremonial figureheads. They were the most consequential of the successor dynasties that arose during the [[dissipation-of-the-high-caliphate]]. Source: [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v1-1974]].

## Origins and Rise

Three brothers of the Buyid family rose to high military captaincies in the service of the Ziyarids, the military leaders who had become independent in the lands southeast of the Caspian by 928. The Buyids exploited the political vacuum left by the collapse of effective Abbasid control, making themselves independent — "with the help of Daylami troops, at once of the Ziyarids and of the caliphs." By 934 they controlled the greater part of western Iran, including Shiraz (Fars province), which became a Buyid seat.

Their origin was not as governors appointed by Baghdad (as were the Tahirids and Tulunids) but as **condottieri** — military entrepreneurs filling a political vacuum. Hodgson: "Not having risen, like the Tahirids or the Tulunids for instance, as regularly appointed governors but as condottieri who were filling a political vacuum, they had no special ties to the caliphs' government."

## Religious Affiliation

The Buyids were Shi'i — specifically inclined toward Twelver Shi'ism, though not exclusively. This was characteristic of their Daylami soldiers, who "had been converted largely by Shi'i rebels using their mountains as base." The Buyids gave official status in their territories to Shi'i holy days, including the commemoration of the martyrdom of Husayn at Karbala — making these public observances a state matter for the first time. Their Shi'i affiliation made their toleration of the Sunni Abbasid caliphs politically anomalous but practically necessary.

## The Occupation of Baghdad (945)

In 945 one of the Buyid captains (whose brothers already controlled western Iran above the Iraq) occupied Baghdad. Despite his Shi'ism, the Buyid chief — Mu'izz al-Dawla — took the title **amir al-umara'** (commander-in-chief) and acknowledged the theoretical position of the Abbasid caliphs, who maintained a local court with considerable local authority but no independent political power. "The Iraq was now merely a province under the new Buyid power."

The caliphs were retained for their residual legitimizing function. The Buyids, like the Hamdanids before them, "made no attempt actually to replace the caliphs with other supreme figures: they merely nullified effectively their power and authority in whatever lands they could seize."

## Governing Structure

The Buyid state was not a unified empire but a family confederation of principalities — three main centers in Shiraz (Fars), Ray (western Iran), and Baghdad (Iraq) — typically held by different Buyid brothers or cousins simultaneously. This fragmentation produced:
- Chronic intra-dynastic competition and occasional conflict
- Dependence on professional Daylami soldiers and (increasingly) Turkish mercenaries — "they could not ignore the good pleasure of their men — professional soldiers, Daylamis and latterly Turks also"
- Administration by the inherited Abbasid bureaucratic apparatus, "though in a simplified form"

## Cultural Significance

The Buyid period, despite political fragmentation, was a significant era of Islamicate high culture:

**Hamdanid parallel**: The Hamdanids of Aleppo (Shi'i like the Buyids) patronized al-Mutanabbi' (the poet), al-Farabi (the philosopher), and al-Isfahani (author of the great Kitab al-Aghani anthology). Their court at Aleppo attracted poets and scholars despite their military burdens against Byzantine reconquest.

**Buyid Baghdad**: The caliphal court at Baghdad retained local cultural authority and continued as a center of learning under Buyid overlordship. Twelver Shi'i scholarship flourished under Buyid patronage in this period.

**Samanid comparison**: The Samanids (875–999) in Transoxania and Khurasan, who were the most loyal of the successor dynasties to the caliphate, provided a contrasting model: sophisticated patronage of both Arabic and new Muslim Persian letters. Under Nasr II (913–942), Samanid rule brought "unquestioned peace and prosperity." The Samanids upheld dihqan (landed gentry) privileges and received the praise of the lettered classes. The first great classical Persian poet, Rudaqi, died in 940 at the Samanid court.

## Military Basis

The Buyids' military strength rested on Daylami infantry, who had a reputation as ferocious fighters from the sub-Caspian highlands. But Daylami soldiers, like the Turkic ghulam troops elsewhere, constituted a social force that the Buyid rulers could not simply command — "they could not ignore the good pleasure of their men." As the Buyid period progressed, Turkish mercenaries were also incorporated into the military, making the same dynamic that had undone the Abbasids a structural feature of Buyid rule as well.

## Decline and End

The Buyid dynasty was displaced by the Seljuk Turks in 1055. The Seljuks, Sunni Muslims, entered Baghdad and ended the anomaly of a Shi'i dynasty maintaining a Sunni caliphate. With the Seljuks the caliphate gained a new kind of protector — Sunni, and ideologically aligned — but the pattern of military overlords controlling ceremonial caliphs persisted.

## Historiography

Hodgson situates the Buyids within the broader argument that the political vacuum of 945 was not simply filled by new powers but left an ideological vacuum as well: "The partisans of the universal absolute monarchy had failed to build a viable power structure... it was the whole heritage of Irano-Semitic absolutism that was in question." The Buyid arrangement — maintaining the Abbasid caliphate as a ceremonial institution while exercising real power — became a template repeated by later dynasties (Seljuks, various sultanates). H.A.R. Gibb's analysis of the Abbasid collapse (cited by Hodgson) provides the main scholarly framework for understanding the transition to Buyid rule.
