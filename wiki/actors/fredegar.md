# Fredegar (the Chronicle of Fredegar)

**An anonymous seventh-century world chronicle — misnamed for an author who never existed — that is the
single most important narrative source for the later Merovingian period.** As Helmut Reimitz notes
(Oxford Handbook, Ch. 22), "the Chronicle of Fredegar received its 'author' only in the early modern
period, and we do not know the identity of the seventh-century historian who wrote it." The name is a
convention; the text is real and indispensable.

## Date, Milieu, and the "Chain of Chronicles"

The oldest extant redaction was compiled "around 660" in the eastern Merovingian kingdoms, "in a milieu
with strong connections to the members of the [[pippinids|Pippinid]] family and networks." Structurally
it is a **"chain of chronicles"**: the *Liber generationis* of Hippolytus, a chronological computation
(Adam to Sigibert II), Isidore on creation, a reworked Jerome and Hydatius, a six-book version of
[[gregory-of-tours|Gregory of Tours]]'s *Histories* (to the death of Chilperic, 584), and finally the
compilers' own **"Fourth Book"** continuing the narrative into the early 640s.

## Historiographical Innovation

Fredegar's compilers "fundamentally reworked the texts of their predecessors," and their key move
reversed Eusebius: Jerome's synoptic columns of peoples became "a linear text," so that "every story
about a community or a people could be considered as a discrete history or 'file'" — organizing "world
history as a series of peoples and kingdoms which Roman imperial history never absorbed." The **Trojan
origin myth** of the Franks is prominent (though, unlike the *Liber Historiae Francorum*, without an
uninterrupted king-list from Troy). The chronicle is critical of the Merovingians and warm toward the
Pippinid network (Pippin I, Arnulf of Metz, Grimoald), with an earlier **Burgundian** stratum showing
through. It tracks Byzantine affairs with unusual sophistication — its compilers "may have understood
[Byzantium] all too well, through the eyes of disgruntled… Byzantines" (see
[[merovingian-byzantine-relations]]).

## Transmission and Legacy

The single Merovingian exemplar (early eighth century) does not survive in any later medieval copy; about
a century after compilation, "the Carolingian descendants of the Pippinid family rearranged the 'chain of
chronicles' and continued it" to 768, hoping the work "would help to legitimate the usurpation of the
Frankish throne." Fredegar is thus both a primary witness to the seventh century — Samo, Dagobert,
Wogastisburg (see [[early-slavs]]) — and a monument of [[merovingian-historiography|Frankish historical
identity-making]] in Pippinid and Carolingian hands.
