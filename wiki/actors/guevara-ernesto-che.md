# Ernesto "Che" Guevara

Argentine-born physician, Marxist revolutionary, guerrilla commander, and theorist (1928–1967). Iconic figure of the Cuban Revolution and 20th-century armed internationalism. Known as "Che" (Argentine vocative); full name Ernesto Guevara de la Serna. Central military and political actor in the overthrow of Fulgencio Batista (1956–1959); later Minister of Industries and President of the National Bank in revolutionary Cuba; proponent of the "new man" (hombre nuevo) socialist ethic; architect of *foco* (foquismo) theory of revolutionary warfare. Led unsuccessful guerrilla expeditions in the Congo (1965) and Bolivia (1966–1967), where he was captured and executed.

## Formative Years and Radicalization (to 1955)

Born 14 May 1928 (birth certificate falsified to 14 June to conceal premarital pregnancy) in Rosario, Argentina, to an upper-middle-class family of partly Irish and Basque descent. Severe asthma from childhood shaped his character and limited conventional sports; he compensated with intellectual voracity, rugby, and long travels. Family moved frequently; formative years in Alta Gracia and Córdoba. Early exposure to literature, chess, and left-leaning household politics.

From [[sources/anderson-che-guevara-revolutionary-life-1997|Anderson]]: The 1952 motorcycle journey across South America (with Alberto Granado) exposed him to extreme poverty, indigenous marginalization, and the human cost of underdevelopment. At Chuquicamata copper mine he observed "the arid arms of the mechanical shovels that devour their entrails, with their obligatory condiment of human lives." Work in a leper colony deepened his sense of social mission; on his 24th birthday he toasted "for Peru and for a united America."

The 1954 CIA-backed coup against Jacobo Árbenz in Guatemala (which he witnessed) was the decisive politicization: he concluded that armed resistance and international solidarity were required against imperialism. Fled to Mexico, where he met Raúl Castro and, through him, Fidel Castro in 1955. The two recognized each other as kindred spirits committed to action.

## Granma Landing and Early Sierra (from Anderson)
At Alegría de Pío (5 Dec 1956) after the disastrous Granma landing, Che reached for a box of ammunition rather than a first-aid kit. Hit in the neck by a ricochet, he experienced a moment of fatalism ("I lost hope for a couple of minutes"). Only ~22 of 82 survived to regroup. He personally executed the traitor Eutimio Guerra (Feb 1957) with a .32 pistol shot to the head, recording it matter-of-factly in his diary. These moments fixed his combatant identity.

## Cuban Revolution (1956–1959)

Joined the 26th of July Movement. The *Granma* landing (2 December 1956) was a near-disaster; the survivors retreated to the Sierra Maestra. Che (initially the expedition's doctor) quickly became a combat commander. His column participated in key actions that expanded rebel control. Tensions between the Sierra (rural guerrilla) and Llano (urban) wings of the movement were real; Che aligned with the Fidelista military core.

His most celebrated military achievement was the capture of Santa Clara (December 1958), which cut the island and precipitated Batista's flight on 1 January 1959. Che entered Havana days later.

## Revolutionary Power and the "New Man" (1959–1965)

Appointed commander of La Cabaña fortress prison; oversaw revolutionary tribunals and executions of Batista-era officials and war criminals. Served as President of the National Bank (signing currency "Che") and Minister of Industries. Advocated rapid industrialization, moral (rather than material) incentives, and the creation of the "new man" — a selfless, conscious socialist citizen whose work would be driven by revolutionary duty rather than profit or coercion.

His radical economic vision clashed with Soviet advice and more pragmatic Cuban approaches. On 1 April 1965 he wrote his famous farewell letter to Fidel, renouncing Cuban offices and citizenship to pursue revolution abroad. He departed for the Congo in 1965 under an assumed identity.

## Internationalist Campaigns and Death (1965–1967)

In the Congo (with a small Cuban force supporting the Simba rebels against the Mobutu regime and white mercenaries), the mission failed due to lack of local support, poor coordination with Congolese forces, and superior counterinsurgency. Che withdrew.

In November 1966 he entered Bolivia clandestinely to open a *foco* in the Ñancahuazú region, aiming to ignite a continental revolution. The Bolivian Communist Party (pro-Soviet) withheld support. Terrain, supply problems, and rapid Bolivian Army response (aided by U.S. Green Berets and CIA) isolated the small band. After the ambush that killed Tania and others, Che was wounded and captured near La Higuera on 8 October 1967. He was executed the next day (9 October) on orders of the Bolivian high command in the presence of CIA operative Félix Rodríguez. His body was secretly buried; hands amputated for identification. Remains recovered and repatriated to Cuba in 1997.

## Ideas and Legacy

Che systematized the *foco* theory (small, mobile guerrilla vanguard creates the subjective conditions for revolution) in works such as *Guerrilla Warfare* and the "Message to the Tricontinental." He insisted on absolute moral commitment, internationalism, and the primacy of consciousness over economic determinism. His personal asceticism, asthma-defying endurance, and willingness to risk everything made him the archetype of the heroic guerrilla for later generations.

Critics (including some on the left) note the human cost of his methods, the over-optimism of foco theory in practice, and the authoritarian aspects of early revolutionary justice. Anderson documents both the "passionate idealist and the cold-hearted disciplinarian."

## Historiography

Pre-Anderson biographies were polarized between Cuban state hagiography and Cold War demonization. Anderson's 1997 (rev. 2010) work remains the scholarly standard in English, grounded in unique primary access (diaries, Aleida March, Argentine contemporaries, Bolivian participants) and on-the-ground reporting that contributed directly to the 1997 exhumation. Later studies of foco theory, Cuban economic policy, and Third World internationalism routinely cite it. The myth (Korda photograph, T-shirts) has largely floated free of the documented biography; Anderson's portrait insists on the paradoxes.
