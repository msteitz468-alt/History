## Role and Background

Hosokawa Yusai (court name Fujitaka) was a veteran general and the most famous poet and scholar among the military nobility of late Sengoku Japan. He was the father of Hosokawa Tadaoki and a supporter of [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu]]. At the age of sixty-seven, during the Sekigahara campaign of 1600, he conducted a remarkable two-month defense of Tanabe castle (also called Maizuru) in Tango province with only five hundred men against a Western besieging army of fifteen thousand.

## Siege of Tanabe Castle (1600)

When the Sekigahara campaign opened, Yusai entered his chief castle at Tanabe (Tango province) and put it in order for defense, though with only five hundred men against fifteen thousand besiegers. What followed was one of the more remarkable episodes of the campaign: many of the enemy captains had been Yusai's pupils in verse-making, and their attacks were "very half-hearted in consequence, many of them absentmindedly omitting to put the projectiles into the guns before firing." Many of the besieging troops were also personally sympathetic to the Eastern cause.

Anxious about rare and ancient poetry volumes in his possession — including a particularly rare edition of the *Kokinshū* — Yusai sent a letter to Prince Tomohito (younger brother of the Emperor) asking him to send Maeda Gen-i to receive them for presentation to the Emperor and the Court. The Court, alarmed lest anything happen to "so eminent a scholar and poet," issued an Imperial Edict ordering the castle to be surrendered and the defenders to march out unharmed. Two months after the siege began, the Western forces withdrew — too late to take part in the Battle of Sekigahara.

## Strategic Significance

The two-month diversion of fifteen thousand Western troops at Tanabe — along with the fifteen thousand tied down at the siege of Otsu and the forces delayed in Ise — meant that a combined total of perhaps forty-five thousand Western troops never reached Sekigahara. Their absence contributed significantly to the balance of forces. Yusai's defense was thus strategically important independent of its romantic literary dimensions.

## Relationship with Ieyasu

Ieyasu had anticipated that [[ishida-mitsunari|Ishida Mitsunari]] might try to use Yusai's divided Kyushu fief at Kitsuke to weaken his forces. Ieyasu told Yusai: "Ishida has divided your fief in this way, thinking to weaken your forces by taking some away to the west, but there in Kyushu I have only one supporter, and that is Kuroda Jōsui. Now your new fief is next his, so be sure you send a few good men down there so that you can combine to some effect when the need arises." Yusai duly sent captains Matsui and Ariyoshi to Kitsuke, where they coordinated with [[kuroda-josui|Kuroda Jōsui]] during the Kyushu campaign.
