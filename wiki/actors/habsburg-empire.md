# The Habsburg Empire (of Charles V)

The **empire of Charles V** (r. 1516–56) was the **composite monarchy** that made the House
of Habsburg the dominant power of sixteenth-century Europe — an unprecedented agglomeration of
Spain, the Burgundian Netherlands, the Holy Roman Empire, much of Italy, and the new Spanish
empire in the Americas, "the empire on which the sun never set." From
[[new-cambridge-modern-history-v2|*The New Cambridge Modern History*, Vol. II]].

## A composite monarchy by inheritance

Charles's empire was assembled not by conquest but by **dynastic inheritance**: from his
Burgundian grandmother the **Netherlands and Franche-Comté**; from his Spanish grandparents
(Ferdinand and Isabella) the crowns of **Castile and Aragon** (with Naples, Sicily, Sardinia,
and the **American conquests**); and from his Habsburg grandfather Maximilian the Austrian
lands and the claim to the **Holy Roman Empire**, to which he was elected in **1519** (over
Francis I, with Fugger banking money). It was, as NCMH II stresses, never a unified state but a
**personal union of kingdoms**, each with its own laws, institutions, and jealousies — the
union of the Spanish crowns itself "had not meant the union of the Spanish kingdoms."

## Governing structure and strains

- **Territorial extent at peak:** Spain, the Netherlands, the Empire, the Habsburg
  hereditary lands, most of Italy, and the American viceroyalties.
- **Economic base:** Castilian taxation and, increasingly, **American silver**
  ([[global-silver-trade]]); Netherlandish commerce and credit; financed (and indebted) by
  the Fugger and Genoese bankers.
- **Military capacity:** the leading army of the age (the Spanish *tercios*), driving the
  [[military-revolution]].
- **The three great struggles** that consumed it: against **[[italian-wars-1494|Valois
  France]]** for mastery of Italy and Europe; against the **[[ottoman-empire|Ottomans]]** in
  the Mediterranean and on the Danube (the siege of Vienna, 1529); and against the German
  **Protestant princes** ([[protestant-reformation-1517|the Reformation]]; the Schmalkaldic
  War and the failure to restore religious unity).

## Decline and division

The empire's very scale was unsustainable: simultaneous war on multiple fronts, the religious
schism Charles could not heal, and crushing debt exhausted it. Defeated in his aim of a
Catholic, universal monarchy — and forced to concede the **Peace of Augsburg (1555)** with its
*cuius regio, eius religio* — an exhausted Charles **abdicated in 1556**, dividing the
inheritance: his son **Philip II** took **Spain, the Netherlands, Italy, and the Americas**;
his brother **Ferdinand I** took the **Austrian lands and the imperial title**. The two
Habsburg lines — Spanish and Austrian — would dominate European politics for the next century
and a half.

## Significance

The Habsburg empire of Charles V is the classic case of the **early-modern composite
monarchy** and of the **bid for European hegemony** that the emerging balance-of-power system
(born in the [[italian-wars-1494|Italian Wars]]) repeatedly thwarted. Its silver-fuelled wars
helped forge the modern fiscal-military state and the global economy, while its failure to
crush the Reformation fixed the confessional division of Europe.
