# Mōri Terumoto

Daimyō of the powerful Mōri clan and grandson of the renowned strategist
Mōri Motonari, who served as the nominal supreme commander of the
[[western-coalition-sekigahara|Western Coalition]] at the Battle of
Sekigahara (1600), the decisive engagement that determined mastery of
Japan and paved the way for the Tokugawa shogunate.

## Role and Major Decisions

Despite holding nominal overall command of the Western army, Terumoto
remained at Osaka Castle throughout the battle rather than taking the
field, leaving actual command in the hands of Ishida Mitsunari and other
subordinates; his kinsman Mōri Hidemoto's contingent at Sekigahara also
failed to engage, having secretly negotiated with the Eastern side before
the battle. Following the Western defeat, Tokugawa Ieyasu drastically
reduced Mōri territory from roughly 1.2 million to about 300,000 koku,
confining the clan to Chōshū — a humiliation that fed a lasting Mōri
grievance against the Tokugawa order, later cited by Chōshū samurai as
historical justification for their leading role in overthrowing the
shogunate in the 1868 Meiji Restoration.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Terumoto actively committed the full Mōri army at Sekigahara, the
numerically strong Western Coalition might plausibly have prevailed;
his passivity is frequently cited as one of several critical Western failures
(alongside Kobayakawa Hideaki's defection) that handed Tokugawa Ieyasu
victory and, with it, national hegemony.

## Historiography

Terumoto's conduct at Sekigahara is documented through Japanese chronicle
and clan-record traditions compiled after the fact, often shaped by later
Chōshū domain interests in narrating the clan's grievance against Tokugawa
rule; the precise motivations behind his and Hidemoto's inaction remain
debated among specialists.
