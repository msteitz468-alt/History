# Shāh ʿAbduʾl-Laṭīf of Bhit

Shāh ʿAbduʾl-Laṭīf (1689–1752), known as "the Shah of Bhit," is the supreme master of Sindhi mystical poetry and one of the foremost Sufi poets of South Asia. His collected poems, the *Shāha jō risālō* ("The Book of Shāh"), remain living liturgy in Sind; dervishes perform his music on Thursday nights at his tomb in Bhit Shah to this day.

## Life

Born in 1689 in Hala, near present-day Hyderabad-Sind, into a family connected to Suhrawardiyya mystical leadership (Makhdūm Nūḥ is the most prominent 17th-century ancestor). In his youth he wandered through Sind in the company of a group of yogis, possibly reaching the sacred mountain Hinglaj in Balochistan — "the goal" in Sindhi mystical geography. He then settled permanently at Bhit, where his dargāh "exudes serenity and peace" with its exquisite blue-and-white tilework of the Hala style ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

He is reported to have recognised the future Sachal Sarmast as a child, foreseeing that he would "open the lid from those things" kept secret.

## The Risālō

The *Shāha jō risālō* contains thirty chapters (*sur*), organised by musical mode. Shāh Laṭīf both adapted traditional Indian *rāgas* (Kalyān, Kabōdh) and invented new modes. The best edition is Kalyan B. Adwani (Bombay, 1958).

The *surs* fall into several types:
- **Folk-tale allegories**: Sassuī-Punhun, Sohnī-Mehanwal, ʿUmar-Maruī, Nūrī-Tamāchī, Sorathi — each heroine's longing and peril allegorises the soul's journey toward union
- **Direct mystical odes**: Asā, Yaman Kalyān — in traditional Sufi vocabulary
- **Nature imagery**: camel (trained base faculties), seafarer's waiting wife, the dry tree in the desert, the wild grouse
- **Prophetological praise**: *Sur Sārang*; the Prophet as rain cloud, symbol of divine mercy
- **Shia mourning**: *Sur Kēdārō* — laments for Ḥusayn's death at Karbala
- **Mystical syncretism**: *Sur Ramakālī* — the most controversially syncretic section, but to be read in conjunction with the Prophetic praise *surs*

### Characteristic technique: folk-tale extremity as mystical moment

Laṭīf enters each folk tale at its most dramatic moment and elaborates the heroine's interior experience. When Sohnī discovers her unbaked jar (substituted by a jealous sister-in-law) and knows she will drown crossing the Indus: the poem turns outward catastrophe into inward discovery — Rūmī's line "Not only the thirsty seek the water, but the water seeks the thirsty as well" (*Mathnawī* 1:1741) is inserted at the moment of mortal crisis. Sassuī, dying in the desert, finds the beloved in her own heart, as Majnūn did.

Maruī — kidnapped by the ruler ʿUmar of Omarkot, confined in his castle — refuses to beautify herself, waiting in her ugliness for a letter from her homeland. She is "the oyster, which would rather die from thirst than drink the salty water that surrounds it; one day it will open to receive the raindrop that will become a pearl in its womb."

## Mystical Stance

Laṭīf maintained "a certain reluctance in unveiling the mysteries of divine love" — speaking "in allusions, in difficult and opaque words, in oblique expressions." This distinguishes him from his successor Sachal Sarmast's intoxicated directness. He acknowledged deep debts to Rūmī and his work requires knowledge of the full Sufi tradition of Iran and India for full appreciation.

The soul is systematically figured as feminine — "Hīr is our soul" — an inheritance from Hindu devotional poetry but here fully Islamicised: the beloved of the feminine soul is always ultimately the Prophet or the divine Reality.

## Relationship to Sufism and Islam

Unlike many Hindu interpreters who read the *Risālō* as crypto-Vedanta, Schimmel insists: "This is the point at which most of the Hindu interpreters, and even an excellent British scholar like Herbert Tower Sorley, have not done full justice to him." Laṭīf grounded his imagery in classical Sufism, included sustained Prophetic praise and Shia mourning poems, and operated within the framework of the suhrawardiyya.

## Reception and Influence

His poetry entered the Sindhi literary canon within a generation. His successor Sachal Sarmast (1739–1826) extended the tradition with greater intoxication and directness. The *Risālō* was first published in print by Ernst Trumpp (1866), who "thoroughly disliked Sufism" but preserved the text. H. T. Sorley produced the main English scholarly study (1940; repr. 1966).
