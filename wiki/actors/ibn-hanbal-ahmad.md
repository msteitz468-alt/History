## Overview

**Ahmad ibn Hanbal** (780–855) was the supreme exemplar of Hadith folk piety and the founder of the Hanbali madhhab school of [[fiqh]]. His resistance to the **Mihna** (the 'Abbasid inquisition of 833–848) made him the defining hero of the [[sunni-islam|Jama'i-Sunni]] scholarly tradition and established the principle that the [[ulama]] held doctrinal authority independent of caliphal command.

## Career

Ibn Hanbal dedicated his life from youth to Islamic learning: "listening to all possible hadith-reporters and memorizing prodigious numbers of reports. He long lived in abject poverty, which was further complicated by scruples against admitting any personal practice which he could not base on Muhammad's example as transmitted in hadith" (Hodgson). His major contribution was a vast collection of hadith (*Musnad*) and the articulation of a legal-devotional stance that relied as exclusively as possible on Qur'an and hadith, minimizing qiyas (analogy). His followers, the Hanbalis, "tried to use hadith (and Qur'an) as exclusively as possible" (Hodgson).

He taught in Baghdad and drew his strongest following from the lower urban classes — "the simpler folk of the cities, especially of Baghdad" — who responded to his populistic, textualist piety and his personal austerity.

## The Mihna and Its Significance

Under the caliphs al-Ma'mun (813–833) and his successors, the Mu'tazilis were given caliphal backing to enforce a doctrine that the [[quran|Qur'an]] was a *created* thing (like all of God's other creations), not eternal or coextensive with God. This doctrinal position — intended to assert divine transcendence and human rationality in religious matters — struck at the devotional core of the Hadith folk, who held that in reciting the Qur'an they were in direct contact with God's own uncreated speech.

Al-Ma'mun selected Ibn Hanbal as the most prominent figure to make an example of. "His steadfast refusal gave heart to the others. His life was saved by al-Ma'mun's own timely death, but he underwent a long imprisonment and was hailed as a martyr to the cause" (Hodgson).

When al-Mutawakkil abandoned the Mihna in 848, Ibn Hanbal's position was vindicated. The 'Abbasid caliphs never again attempted to assert doctrinal authority over the 'ulama as a class. The Hadith folk — identifying themselves with the entire Sunni jama'ah — had effectively established that caliphal power stopped at the boundaries of 'ulama-defined orthodoxy.

## The Hanbali School

The madhhab school named after Ibn Hanbal was the most consistently hadith-based of the four: rejecting the broader use of analogical reasoning (qiyas) that the Hanafi and Shafi'i schools accepted. Hanbali fiqh was later the doctrinal base for the Wahhabi reform movement in 18th-century Arabia and remains the official school of Saudi Arabia.

## Historical Significance

Ibn Hanbal's significance was not primarily theoretical but exemplary. He was "a man of humble background and the piety which he represented appealed to the simpler folk of the cities" (Hodgson); he embodied — for his followers and for subsequent Sunni tradition — the possibility that the 'ulama could resist caliphal power on grounds of conscience and scholarly authority. His willingness to endure persecution rather than recant became the model for later assertions of scholarly independence.
