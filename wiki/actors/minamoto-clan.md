## Overview

The Minamoto clan (源氏, Genji in Sino-Japanese reading) was a noble warrior lineage created when imperial princes were removed from the imperial family for cost-cutting reasons and given the surname Minamoto. "Minamoto" (meaning "source of water, origin" or, in a Sinophile reading, "of the blood") was bestowed by eleven emperors from Saga (r. 809–23) to Sanjo (r. 1011–16), producing multiple clan lines distinguished by the name of their ancestral emperor: Saga Genji, Murakami Genji, Seiwa Genji, and so on.

The militarily dominant line — the Seiwa Genji — descended from a prince named Tsunemoto (d. 961), reputedly a grandson of Emperor Seiwa (though a document discovered in the 19th century suggests Tsunemoto was actually a grandson of the criminally inclined Emperor Yozei; "not all students of the subject accept the testimony of the document, but if it is correct, we may suspect that the name of the criminally inclined and finally deposed Yozei was avoided by family historians in favor of a more auspicious genealogical origin" — [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]], p. 651). This line founded the [[kamakura-period]] in 1185–92.

## Origins and Early History

The Minamoto clan illustrates a recurring pattern in the Heian court: excess princes, removed from imperial status and given surnames, sometimes remained in the capital pursuing court careers while others sought their fortunes in the provinces through the profession of arms. In the case of the Seiwa Genji, Tsunemoto's son Mitsunaka (912–97) served the Fujiwara regents as their military arm in overawing rivals. For three generations until the end of the 11th century, this line held court positions in the guards, led punitive expeditions, and rotated as provincial governors, "gaining a reputation as the most formidable warriors in the land" (p. 651).

## Key Leaders and the Rise to Eastern Dominance

**Minamoto no Yorimitsu (948–1021):** Founded a major sublineage of the Seiwa Genji; exemplified the warrior ethos of attributing martial prowess to ancestral lineage and divine favor.

**Minamoto no Yorinobu (968–1048):** Kawachi Genji branch. Established Minamoto dominance in the Kanto by suppressing the Tadatsune revolt (1028–31) without battle — Tadatsune, who was Yorinobu's kenin (house man), surrendered upon Yorinobu's approach. Initiated Minamoto worship of Hachiman, god of archery, at Iwashimizu Shrine, claiming the deity as tutelary patron — "shrines to Hachiman soon sprang up throughout the country wherever there were Minamoto to invoke his name" (p. 669).

**Minamoto no Yoriyoshi (988–1075):** Yorinobu's son. Commanded court forces in the Earlier Nine Years' War (1051–62) against the Abe clan in Mutsu. First established Minamoto presence in Sagami through land gifts from Taira no Naokata. Victory came only through alliance with the Kiyohara clan of Dewa.

**Minamoto no Yoshiie (1039–1106), "Hachiman Taro":** Yoriyoshi's son. Led the Later Three Years' War (1083–87) as a private war after the court refused commission, compensating retainers from his own resources. "Yoshiie was acknowledged as the peerless warrior (musha) of the realm. In the words of a popular song, 'Of all the Minamoto, Hachiman Taro is the most feared'" (p. 673). His victories deepened lord-vassal bonds and expanded the [[bushidan]]. However, the court — alarmed by his power — curbed his influence, forbidding provincial troops from entering the capital and prohibiting landowners from commending land to him.

**Minamoto no Tameyoshi (1096–1156):** Yoshiie's grandson; succeeded as toryo after family violence weakened the clan. Led the Minamoto force on the losing side at the [[hogen-heiji-disturbances-1156|Hogen Disturbance]] (1156) under ex-emperor Sutoku; was executed by his own son Yoshitomo on Go-Shirakawa's orders.

**Minamoto no Yoshitomo (1123–60):** Tameyoshi's son. Fought on the winning side at Hogen (1156) under Go-Shirakawa, then launched the Heiji Disturbance (1159) in alliance with Fujiwara no Nobuyori, attempting to curb Taira power. Defeated by Kiyomori's Taira, killed while fleeing east. His sons were executed; the thirteen-year-old Yoritomo was spared due to the plea of Kiyomori's stepmother and exiled to Izu.

**Minamoto no Yoshinaka (1154–84):** Cousin of Yoritomo. Rose in the Genpei War (1180–85), captured Kyoto in 1183 after driving out the Taira, but was subsequently destroyed by Yoritomo's forces.

**Minamoto no Yoshitsune (1159–89):** Yoshitomo's youngest son, spared as an infant after Heiji and assigned to the priesthood. Became the military genius of the Genpei War, destroying the Taira at Dan-no-ura (1185). Later destroyed by his brother Yoritomo.

**Minamoto no Yoritomo (1147–99):** Yoshitomo's third son; exiled to Izu after Heiji. Raised the Minamoto banner in 1180 and led the Genpei War to victory, destroying the Taira and establishing the [[kamakura-period]] in 1185. See [[minamoto-no-yoritomo]].

## Warrior Organization

The Minamoto developed the [[bushidan]] as a personal retainership system centered on the eastern provinces, especially the Kanto. Retainers (roto, kenin) were drawn from provincial landholders who sought the protection of the Minamoto against provincial exactions. The clan maintained its double character throughout: connections at court (through guard offices, governorships, and service to the retired emperors) combined with dominance over eastern warrior networks. Land, court rank, and martial reputation were mutually reinforcing.

## Decline and Recovery

After Yoshiie's death in 1106, the Minamoto clan entered a period of factionalism and court neglect: "the generation of Yoshiie and his sons and nephews left a record of violence, lawlessness, and mutually destructive behavior that cost the family its reputation as the leading samurai" (p. 684). Retired emperor Shirakawa deliberately fomented Minamoto factionalism to check their power and elevated the [[taira-clan]] (Ise Heishi) as counterweight. The Minamoto suffered heavy losses at Heiji (1159) and appeared destroyed. Their recovery through Yoritomo (1180–85) was the decisive reversal.

## Historiography

The Minamoto are among the best-documented lineages in Heian Japan, owing to the literary tradition of the period (Heike monogatari, Hogen monogatari, Heiji monogatari, Mutsu waki) and to diary evidence. However, these literary sources contain semifictional elements and were compiled generations after the events. The genealogical controversy over Tsunemoto's parentage (Seiwa vs. Yozei) is a reminder that warrior families manipulated lineage claims. The chapter in [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] draws on Takeuchi Rizo's foundational Japanese scholarship alongside the literary texts.
