# Nur al-Din Mahmud

**Nur al-Din Mahmud ibn Zangi** (1118–1174), son of **[[imad-al-din-zangi|Imad al-Din Zangi]]**, ruler of Aleppo and eventually most of Muslim Syria, is the pivotal figure of the mid-twelfth-century Muslim counter-offensive against the Franks. He combined military pressure on the [[crusader-states|crusader states]] with a programme of **Sunni revival** — madrasas, patronage of jihad ideology, and nominal deference to the [[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid]] caliph — that later historiography treated as the model for **[[saladin]]**.

## Career outline

After Zangi’s murder (1146), Nur al-Din secured Aleppo, retook Edessa when the Franks briefly re-entered it, and weathered the **[[second-crusade-1147|Second Crusade]]** (failed siege of Damascus, 1148). Over the next decades he extended authority over Damascus (1154), intervened repeatedly in **[[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] Egypt**, and cultivated a reputation as *mujahid* and just ruler. His commanders — including **Shirkuh** and the young Saladin — conquered Egypt for the Zangid interest; after Nur al-Din’s death (1174), Saladin built the **[[ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubid]]** order on that foundation, sometimes in open rivalry with Nur al-Din’s heirs.

## Succession and early rule (Cobb)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] stresses that there was no succession plan: Zangid domains were family appanages. The true heir was the elder son **Sayf al-Din Ghazi** (Mosul/Upper Mesopotamia, appointed by the sultan); Nur al-Din took Aleppo/Syria. The Kurdish amir **Shirkuh** led officers rallying around Nur al-Din. Nur al-Din formally recognized his brother as overlord and was charged to continue Zangi’s jihad against the Franks—focusing Crusade histories on Nur al-Din alone, Cobb notes, distorts the partnership with Mosul.

After Zangi’s death Armenians rebelled; Joscelin recaptured Edessa’s town but not the citadel. Nur al-Din forced-marched from Aleppo; Joscelin abandoned the city; Nur al-Din sacked Edessa with free hand to the troops. Ibn al-Athir held that *this* sack, not Zangi’s 1144 conquest, was when the city was plundered and depopulated.

Unlike his father, Nur al-Din lacked Upper Mesopotamian resources (his brother’s domain) and needed Damascene agricultural lands to feed, pay, and supply his men. Spring 1147: Franks invaded the Hawran after a local governor invited them; Nur al-Din allied with Unur of Damascus and compelled withdrawal. A 1147 marriage alliance sealed Aleppo–Damascus rapprochement, alarming the Franks.

## Second Crusade and Damascus

With Edessa no longer viable after Nur al-Din’s hold and army losses, the newly arrived Second Crusade forces redirected to Damascus (July 1148: Louis VII, Conrad III, Baldwin III). Unur prepared defenses and blocked wells; after four days the Franks lifted the siege. Cobb argues that the threat of combined Zangid–Unur resistance was instrumental in that failure. Subsequent joint Muslim campaigns followed (Tripoli fort vs. Bertram; Battle of Inab, June 1149—Raymond of Poitiers killed; Apamea recaptured; truce forced on Antioch).

Unur died August 1149 (then Sayf al-Din); Abaq of Damascus allied with Jerusalem. Nur al-Din grandstanded as protector of the Damascenes. Turcomans captured Joscelin; by autumn 1150 Tall Bashir and remaining Edessan Frankish territory were retaken with Rum Seljuk help—the first Latin state created was the first permanently expunged; borders of *dar al-islam* shifted west from the Euphrates to the Orontes. Tartus fell 1152, a wedge between Tripoli and Antioch. In 1154 Nur al-Din took Damascus after blockade, food-price crisis, and popular disillusion with Abaq’s Frankish alliance: he entered virtually unopposed, negotiated Abaq’s exit to a Homs governorship, and announced tax cuts and clemency—“every inch of it was theater.”

## Ideology and Sunni programme

Nur al-Din carefully used the epithet *mujahid* in decrees and inscriptions, but Cobb argues jihad against the Franks was **one small aspect** of a broader pious-just-ruler image (Circle of Equity). Inscriptions pair *mujahid* with *zahid* (ascetic) and caliphal loyalty; he often used treaties and tribute; lands taken from Muslim rivals (Syria, Mesopotamia, Egypt) outweigh Frankish gains; he endowed madrasas and mystic meetinghouses on an unprecedented scale. Under him came the final melding of a frontier Turcoman military worldview with Sunni urban kingship and religious reform; at Aleppo he forbade the Shiʿite call-to-prayer formula and built madrasas/ribats; after Damascus 1154 titles stress *al-Malik al-ʿAdil* (“The Just King”) more than the warrior role.

**Ibn ʿAsakir** (b. 1105), leading Damascene hadith scholar, became a natural ally after 1154—patronage for Sunni restoration via jihad vs. Franks and Shiʿites plus legal/theological reform; he wrote a jihad hadith collection, Ascalon merits, and a massive *History of Damascus* arguing Syria’s Sunni centrality. Ibn ʿAsakir’s Nur al-Din entry balances Frankish victories with an elaborate social programme (madrasas, hospitals, orphans, Qurʾan copyists, pilgrimage 1161, infrastructure)—jihad “but one plank in a much broader platform,” challenging a narrow “counter-Crusade” frame.

Post-1154 northern stasis: Harim treaty (fort Frankish; produce shared); 1157 Jerusalem broke the Hawran treaty—public jihad sentiment and a failed Banyas attack; 1157 earthquake on both sides; 1159 Manuel–Frank menace of Aleppo negotiated away (Nur al-Din to aid vs. Rum Seljuks; release of thousands of Second Crusade prisoners). Concurrent Christian expansion in al-Andalus and Norman North Africa made the Zangids, in Cobb’s Mediterranean framing, seem “Islamdom’s only hope.”

## Egypt, Shirkuh, and Saladin

Fatimid Ascalon fell to Baldwin III (1153); by 1155 Egypt paid tribute to Jerusalem. Mid-century Fatimid caliphs after al-Hafiz (d. 1149) were child puppets; the vizierate was the most coveted office. In 1163 Shawar seized the vizierate, was ousted by Dirgham, and fled to Nur al-Din. Nur al-Din’s decision to intervene solved multiple problems: victory after defeat at Crac des Chevaliers; a blow for Sunni orthodoxy vs. Shiʿi Fatimids; wealth and lands; preeminence overshadowing Zangi’s legacy; prevention of Egypt falling to Amalric.

He chose the Kurdish amir **Shirkuh** to lead the Zangid army. Cairo was taken April 1164; Dirgham executed; Shawar then bargained with Amalric against Shirkuh; October armistice and mutual withdrawal. A 1167 return was cat-and-mouse without result; a Frankish resident adviser and small force were left in Cairo. Late 1168 Amalric invaded again and massacred Bilbays (November); the Fatimid caliph wrote for help; Franks withdrew as Shirkuh arrived; Shirkuh executed Shawar, was named vizier, and died weeks later of natural causes (sources say). His nephew **Salah al-Din Yusuf (Saladin)**—former Alexandria governor and right-hand man—succeeded as last Fatimid vizier and, by 1171, abolished the Fatimid caliphate and restored Egypt to the Sunni Abbasid fold.

Nur al-Din reacted with a mixture of delight (Levant unity Mesopotamia-to-Egypt; Sunni restoration surrounding the Frankish states; joint Transjordan campaigns 1171 and 1173 vs. al-Shawbak and al-Karak) and anxiety as Egypt slipped to Saladin’s kin (father Ayyub joined; Turanshah to Nubia and 1174 Yemen; small remittances). He sent tax men and mustered troops, but died in May 1174 before confrontation—buried in the Nuriya madrasa, Damascus. Cobb’s dry summary: “Nur al-Din gave Saladin one final gift: he died.”

## Counterfactual significance

Nur al-Din’s unification of Muslim Syria and the Egyptian intervention created the single power bloc without which Hattin (1187) is hard to imagine. Whether Saladin continued or betrayed Nur al-Din’s programme is a standing historiographical question. Cobb’s epilogue places Nur al-Din among the three “most successfully persuasive” leaders (with Zangi and Saladin) whose sequential careers create the illusion of a continuous countercrusade.

## Historiography

Arabic panegyric (Ibn al-Athir, Abu Shama, Ibn ʿAsakir) and modern Islamic historiography often elevate Nur al-Din as the true architect of jihad; Western crusade historians long treated him as Saladin’s forerunner. [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] places him within a broader Mediterranean Frankish problem, stresses inter-Muslim politics and the just-king programme alongside anti-Frankish warfare, and warns against reading his career as pure “counter-Crusade.”
