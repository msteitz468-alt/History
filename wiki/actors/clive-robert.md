# Robert Clive

**Robert Clive** (1725–74), "Clive of India," was the
[[english-east-india-company|East India Company]] soldier-administrator whose
conspiracies and victories converted the Company from a Bengal trading concern into
a territorial sovereign. He embodies the wiki's recurring theme of the **autonomous
overseas agent** ([[company-state]]): a man "on the spot" who made war and kings in
defiance of, or ahead of, the directors in London.

## Role and Key Decisions

- **Carnatic Wars.** Clive made his reputation in the Anglo-French struggle for the
  Coromandel ([[madras]]), where the French governor **Dupleix** sought "to establish
  French hegemony in Coromandel by military means." The Company backed Muhammad Ali
  against the French-backed Chanda Sahib; Clive's defence of Arcot (1751) was central
  to the British recovery. Per [[roy-east-india-company-2016|Roy]], the Carnatic Wars
  "had little effect on the political future of India, but… changed the character of
  the Company in Madras from a trading to a military entity."
- **[[battle-of-plassey-1757|Plassey (1757)]].** After Siraj-ud-Daula seized Calcutta,
  Clive retook it with 1,400 men from Madras and engineered the coup that installed
  **Mir Jafar** — "not overburdened by ethics," he forged a false contract to
  neutralize the broker Amir Chand. The "battle" was decided by betrayal. Clive took a
  personal fortune (Spear: £234,000 plus a £30,000-a-year *jagir*) and remitted
  several hundred thousand pounds home via Dutch Company bills and Golconda diamonds.
- **The diwani (1765).** Returning as governor of Bengal, Clive secured from the
  defeated Mughal emperor Shah Alam II the **diwani** (revenue authority) of Bengal,
  Bihar and Orissa after [[battle-of-buxar-1764|Buxar]] — limiting commitments rather
  than marching on Delhi, and making the Company the Mughal revenue agent and virtual
  ruler of India's richest province.

## Counterfactual Significance

Clive is the classic test case for **Great Man vs. structural causation** (see
[[great-man-vs-structural-causation]]). [[roy-east-india-company-2016|Roy]] reads his
career as evidence *against* the view that conquest expressed a coordinated,
London-directed strategy: "the conquest of Bengal was a sign of weakness of the firm,
not a sign of its strength," pushed by traders and soldiers in India against the
directors' caution. The plunder he organized became, for nationalist historians, the
foundational act of the [[drain-of-wealth-colonial-india|"drain of wealth"]] — though
Roy argues the looted money "came from the treasury of the nawab," not from ordinary
Indians, and was too brief and small to explain Indian poverty.

## Historiography

Clive is among the most morally contested figures in the wiki's modern coverage —
celebrated by imperial historians as the founder of British India, condemned in
Indian nationalist memory as its arch-plunderer, and the subject of the Parliamentary
corruption inquiries of the 1770s that also targeted the Company itself. His suicide
in 1774 coincided with the political reckoning that produced the **Regulating Act
(1773)** and the reordering of Company rule under [[hastings-warren|Warren Hastings]].
