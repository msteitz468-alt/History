## Overview

Komura Jutarō (1855–1911) served as Japan's Foreign Minister during the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]] and as chief plenipotentiary at the [[treaty-of-portsmouth-1905|Portsmouth peace negotiations]] (1905). He was also foreign minister during the conclusion of the [[anglo-japanese-alliance-1902|Anglo-Japanese Alliance]] in 1902 and played a key role in implementing the [[korea-protectorate-1905|Korean Protectorate]] (1905). A Harvard-trained diplomat, he was the principal architect of Japan's great-power diplomacy in the critical decade 1900–1910.

## Major Decisions and Consequences

### The Anglo-Japanese Alliance

As Foreign Minister, Komura worked closely with Prime Minister Katsura Tarō and British minister Claude MacDonald in the years leading to the 1902 alliance. In December 1904, during the Russo-Japanese War, he told MacDonald that "if Japan was successful in war, she would seek for a closer alliance with England" (Keene, ch. 56). This foreshadowed the 1905 renewal of the alliance. At the third anniversary dinner of the alliance in February 1905, he publicly expressed hope that the alliance would "grow in strength and solidity" — a signal the British took seriously.

### The Portsmouth Negotiations

As Japan's chief plenipotentiary at Portsmouth, Komura operated under enormous tension between his own diplomatic instincts and the constraints imposed by Tokyo. The Japanese cabinet had privately determined that there were only three "absolutely indispensable" peace terms: Russian recognition of Japanese primacy in Korea, mutual evacuation of Manchuria, and transfer of the Port Arthur leasehold. An indemnity and cession of all Sakhalin were "items not absolutely indispensable but to be secured insofar as possible."

Komura exceeded these instructions by insisting aggressively on both the indemnity and full Sakhalin, and critically, he failed to inform the Tokyo government that the czar was willing to divide Sakhalin at the 50th parallel. On August 26 he telegraphed Tokyo announcing his intention to break off negotiations entirely. The genrō council (Itō, [[yamagata-aritomo|Yamagata]], and Inoue) convened by the prime minister concluded that Japan's financial and military exhaustion made peace essential even without these items, and directed Komura to yield.

The Japanese delegation "began to weep and sob" at receiving these instructions, which "seemed to be a confession of defeat at the conference table" (Keene, ch. 55). On August 29, Komura accepted Witte's offer of the southern half of Sakhalin; at the formal session the next morning, he performed the theater of asking for the whole island, being refused, and then "for the sake of peace and humanity" accepting the division — Keene notes this was "just so much playacting for the benefit of spectators." The treaty was signed September 5, 1905.

Komura and his colleagues returned to Japan to face the [[hibiya-riots-1905|Hibiya Riots]]. They had signed the treaty "quite against their own wishes" and could foresee the stormy domestic reception.

### Role in the Korean Protectorate

Komura played a significant role in shaping the diplomatic framework for the [[korea-protectorate-1905|Korean Protectorate]], ensuring at Portsmouth that Russia formally recognized Japan's "paramount political, military, and economic interests in Korea" — the legal foundation for the protectorate treaty signed in November 1905.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Komura been more forthcoming with Tokyo about Russian flexibility on Sakhalin, or had he been willing to accept the three "absolutely indispensable" terms without pressing for the indemnity, the Portsmouth negotiations would have been concluded more smoothly and Japan would have obtained the same essential terms without the near-breakdown. His obstinacy nearly caused the conference to collapse and the war to resume at great cost. The near-failure illuminates the gap between front-line diplomatic judgment and the strategic realities known to Tokyo.

## Historiography

Keene presents Komura as a man "driven by a concept of honor" similar to the czar's — both parties' inflexibility on points of pride nearly derailed the negotiations that both governments actually needed to succeed. Komura's failure to keep Tokyo fully informed of Russian positions is presented as the critical misjudgment of the conference.
