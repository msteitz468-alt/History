# William Tecumseh Sherman (1820–1891)

## Overview

Union general who rose from a shaky start (a 1861 nervous breakdown after Kentucky command led press to brand him "insane") to become, alongside Grant, the architect of Union victory in the West and of the "hard war" strategy that broke Southern will to fight. Commanded the Western Theater from 1864, capturing Atlanta (September 1864) in time to bolster Lincoln's re-election, then conducted the **March to the Sea** (Nov–Dec 1864) and the Carolinas Campaign (1865), deliberately targeting Confederate infrastructure, railroads, and supply capacity rather than fighting set-piece battles against Confederate field armies. Accepted Joseph E. Johnston's surrender at Bennett Place (April 1865), effectively ending large-scale Confederate resistance.

## Role and Key Decisions

- Early Western Theater command alongside Grant (Shiloh, Vicksburg campaigns).
- Atlanta Campaign (1864): a grinding maneuver campaign against Joseph E. Johnston (then John Bell Hood after Confederate command change) culminating in the fall of Atlanta.
- March to the Sea: deliberate strategy of "hard war" — destroying railroads, factories, and agricultural capacity across Georgia to break the Confederacy's ability and will to continue fighting, while largely sparing civilian lives. Controversial among contemporaries and historians for its targeting of civilian infrastructure.
- Post-war: Commanding General of the U.S. Army (1869–1883), overseeing much of the Indian Wars-era U.S. Army.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Sherman's Western command, Union coordination of simultaneous multi-front pressure (Grant's 1864 strategy) loses its Western component; Atlanta's fall — critical to Lincoln's re-election chances against a war-weary electorate and George McClellan's peace-leaning candidacy — becomes far less certain, with significant downstream effects on whether the war continues to unconditional Confederate surrender or ends in a negotiated peace.

## Historiography

Sherman is central to the historiographical debate over "hard war"/total war in the Civil War — see [[sources/hagerman-american-civil-war-origins-modern-warfare-1988|Hagerman (1988)]] on the doctrinal and organizational development of Union "hard war" capacity, and [[sources/weigley-the-american-way-of-war-1973|Weigley (1973)]] on Sherman as a transitional figure between limited war and the strategy of annihilation/exhaustion. Popular memory (particularly in the "Lost Cause" tradition) has emphasized Sherman's destructiveness; modern scholarship generally credits him with unusual restraint toward civilian life relative to 20th-century "total war," while affirming the deliberate, strategic character of infrastructure destruction.
