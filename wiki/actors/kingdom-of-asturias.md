# The Kingdom of Asturias

## Overview

The **Kingdom of Asturias** was the first enduring Christian polity to emerge in the mountainous
north of the Iberian peninsula after the Muslim conquest of 711. In
[[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Collins, Ch. 11) it is the nucleus from which the later
medieval Christian kingdoms of Spain — and the ideology of the **Reconquista** — would grow.

## From Pelagius to the Alfonsos

The kingdom traces its origin to the rising of **Pelagius (Pelayo)** in the eastern Asturias and his
victory over a Muslim force at **Covadonga** (c. 722) — an event heavily mythologised in the later
*Chronicle of Alfonso III*. Under **Alfonso I "the Catholic"** (739–757) and his successors the
kingdom expanded over Galicia in the west and contested the eastern marches with the **Basques**,
exploiting Berber withdrawals and depopulating the Duero basin as a buffer. The reign of
**Alfonso II** (791–842), based at the new capital of **Oviedo**, consolidated the monarchy and
cultivated a **"neo-Gothic"** ideology presenting the Asturian kings as heirs of the vanished
Visigothic kingdom. **Alfonso III** (866–910) extended the realm southward toward the Duero; on his
death the centre of gravity shifted south and the kingdom was refounded as the **Kingdom of León**.

## Significance

Asturias gave the Christian north a royal dynasty, a Visigothic-restorationist ideology, and the
beginnings of southward expansion — the institutional and ideological foundations of the centuries-long
**Reconquista**. The (later) discovery of the shrine of **Santiago de Compostela** in Galicia would add
a powerful religious focus.

## Historiography

The Asturian past is known chiefly from the late-ninth-century **Asturian chronicles** (the *Chronicle of
Alfonso III* in its "Rotensis" and "erudite/Sebastián" versions, and the *Albeldense*), which are
court-produced, retrospective, and ideologically charged — projecting a continuous Gothic-Christian
mission of reconquest back onto the murky events of the eighth century. Collins (NCMH II) is notably
cautious about how much of the Covadonga/Pelagius narrative is recoverable as history.
