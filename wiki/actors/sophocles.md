# Sophocles

Second of the three canonical Athenian tragedians (c.496–406 BCE): rich citizen, public figure, and unmatched festival competitor — twenty-four victories, never lower than second, ~forty-one tetralogies.

## Role

Defeated [[aeschylus]] in 468 at about twenty-eight; in the year of his own death paraded his chorus in mourning for [[euripides]]. Comparatively rich, took part in public life, treated gods with respect; helped introduce **Asclepius** to Athens (effectively the first public hospital, in Levi’s phrasing). Wrote ~forty-one tetralogies, won twenty-four times, never lower than second. Three style periods end in a supple, character-fitted late manner; no plays from his first twenty-five theatre years survive. ***Ajax*** and ***Antigone*** (probably 441) already show mastery — Levi reads *Antigone* also as Creon’s tragedy (absolute duty to bury the brother leads Creon to condemn her; “his own destruction begins” and the end is “like an avalanche”); if there was one supreme hour in the fifth century, Levi proposes, it was that of the *Antigone*, composed while the [[parthenon|Parthenon]] was being built. ***Oedipus at Colonus*** centres on a holy wood near Colonus (Sophocles’ birthplace); when produced posthumously the Spartans and allies were visible around Athens’ walls near the sacred wood — “of course the trees were cut down in the end.” Murray (OHCW Ch. 9) cites Sophocles’ *Tereus* fr. 583 on sold brides as evidence of tragic belief in female emotional extremes under the ideology of seclusion.

## Significance

Sophocles perfected character-centred tragic form and the image of the dignified civic poet-priest of the classical city.

## Historiography

OHCW (Levi Ch. 7) is appreciative mid-1980s essay; dating of individual plays remains partly conjectural.
