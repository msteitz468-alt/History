# Gaius Julius Caesar

The general and statesman whose **conquest of Gaul** and victory in civil war made him **dictator for life** and
brought the Roman Republic to its end. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Chs.
9–10 (Wiseman) and Ch. 11 (Rawson).

## Rise and the road to power

A patrician of the **Julii**, nephew by marriage of **[[marius]]** and son-in-law of
**Cinna** (he defied Sulla's order to divorce her), Caesar built a **popularis**
reputation through lavishly borrowed generosity: the restored Marian trophies (65),
the games of his aedileship, and his shock election as **pontifex maximus** (63) over
Catulus. In the Catilinarian debate he argued (vainly) against the death penalty.
After a Spanish governorship, he returned in 60 to find the Senate, led by **Cato**,
blocking his triumph and his consular candidacy — driving him to forge the
**[[first-triumvirate|compact with Pompey and Crassus]]**.

## The consulship of 59

Caesar's first consulship was a constitutional storm. He carried an **agrarian law**
for Pompey's veterans by **mob force**, overriding his colleague **Bibulus** (who
retreated home to "watch the skies," giving the year its joke: "the consulship of
Julius and Caesar"). He published the Senate's proceedings (*acta senatus*),
ratified **Pompey's eastern settlement**, refunded the **publicani** (for Crassus),
passed a lasting **extortion law**, sealed the alliance by marrying his daughter
**Julia to Pompey** — and secured, via the tribune Vatinius, a **five-year command in
Gaul and Illyricum** (later extended). He also unleashed **Clodius** on his enemies,
notably exiling [[cicero]].

## The conquest of Gaul (58–50)

In the **[[caesar-gallic-war-58-50bce|Gallic War]]** Caesar conquered all Gaul to the
Rhine and the Ocean, crossed into **Germany** and **Britain**, and crushed the great
revolt of **Vercingetorix** at **Alesia** (52) — building an army of devoted veterans
and a vast fortune, and broadcasting his deeds to Rome in his **Commentaries**. The
alliance was renewed at the conference of **Luca** (56), but unravelled with the
death of **Julia** (54) and of **Crassus** at **[[battle-of-carrhae-53bce|Carrhae]]**
(53), leaving Caesar and **Pompey** — now drawn to the optimates — as rivals.

## The final crisis

Through 51–50 Caesar's enemies (Cato, the Marcelli, Lentulus) sought to **strip him
of his command** before he could pass straight to a second consulship — which he
needed to avoid prosecution. His tribunes (Curio, then **Antony** and Cassius) and
the ambiguity of his command's end-date drew out the deadlock until, on **7 January
49**, the Senate passed the *senatus consultum ultimum*; the tribunes fled to him; and
Caesar **crossed the Rubicon** with the Thirteenth Legion. "Are we talking about a
general of the Roman people," asked Cicero, "or Hannibal?"

## Civil war, dictatorship, and the Ides (49–44)

In the **[[roman-civil-war-49-45bce|civil war]]** Caesar overran Italy and Spain,
crushed **[[pompey|Pompey]] at Pharsalus** (48) — Pompey was then murdered in Egypt —
installed **Cleopatra**, beat Pharnaces at Zela, and destroyed the Republican
coalition at **Thapsus** (46, Cato's suicide) and **Munda** (45). Master of the
world, he ruled through an escalating **[[caesars-dictatorship|dictatorship]]**,
reforming the calendar, the colonies, the citizenship, debt, and the Senate — but his
**perpetual dictatorship** and the rumours of kingship drove a conspiracy of some
sixty senators (led by **Brutus** and **Cassius**) to **assassinate him on the Ides
of March, 44** (see [[julius-caesar-assassination-44bce]]).

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Wiseman frames Caesar as both agent and symptom: his career exploited every strain in
a Republic whose constitution had become "unworkable" — the over-large electorate,
the bribery, the over-mighty commands. His refusal to be destroyed in the courts, and
his army's loyalty to him over the state (the lesson of [[sulla|Sulla]]), made civil
war the only exit. Rawson's verdict, with the ancients: Caesar was driven by **glory
and the refusal to set bounds to his pre-eminence** — and his murder, far from
restoring the Republic, opened the further civil wars from which **Octavian/Augustus**
emerged (CAH X).

## Historiography

Caesar's own **Commentaries** (the *Bellum Gallicum*, written largely year-by-year as
political propaganda for the Roman voters, with Labienus prominent and casualties
softened) are a primary and self-interested source, alongside **Cicero's letters**
(the richest contemporary record of 59–49) and the later Plutarch, Suetonius, Appian,
and Dio. The "legal question" of when his command expired is an old scholarly battle
Wiseman treats as insoluble — and rendered moot by the invasion itself.
