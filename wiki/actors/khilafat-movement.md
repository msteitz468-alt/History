## Overview

The Khilafat Movement was a mass political mobilization of Indian Muslims, organized through the All-India Khilafat Committee founded in 1919, dedicated to pressuring the British government to preserve the Ottoman Sultan-Caliph's authority and territorial integrity after the Ottoman defeat in the First World War. It became one of the most consequential episodes of pan-Islamism intersecting with Indian nationalism, forging a temporary Hindu-Muslim political alliance that underpinned [[indian-independence-movement|the Indian independence movement]]'s non-cooperation phase before collapsing with the abolition of the Caliphate itself.

## Role

The movement was ideologically broad, drawing Sunni, Shi'i, Ahmadi, and even Hindu participants under a shared anti-British, pro-Ottoman banner. Its key figures included Abu al-Kalam Azad, brothers Muhammad Ali and Shawkat Ali, Abdul Bari, and Mahmud al-Hasan. Between 1919 and 1921 the Khilafat Committee allied with [[indian-national-congress|the Indian National Congress]] and Mahatma Gandhi, whose non-cooperation campaign against British rule drew crucial mass support from Khilafat mobilization — a rare and short-lived convergence of Muslim pan-Islamic and Hindu-led nationalist agendas under one movement. Women participants, notably Abadi Banu Begum (mother of the Ali brothers), broke purdah norms to address mixed political crowds, lifting her burqa's veil to speak publicly — a notable instance of nationalist mobilization reshaping gendered public participation.

## Key Events and Processes

The movement's political logic depended entirely on the survival of the Ottoman Caliphate as an institution worth defending. That logic collapsed when Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk) abolished the Sultanate and then the Caliphate itself in March 1924, severing religion from politics in the new Turkish Republic. With no Caliphate left to champion, the Khilafat Movement lost its unifying cause and rapidly disintegrated, and the Hindu-Muslim alliance it had sustained frayed in the following years.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Ottoman Caliphate crisis, it is unlikely Gandhi's non-cooperation movement would have achieved the same scale of Muslim mass participation in the early 1920s; the Khilafat Movement supplied a religiously resonant grievance that made Congress-led non-cooperation a genuinely cross-communal phenomenon rather than a Hindu-led campaign alone. Its collapse after 1924 also removed a rare structural bridge between Indian Muslim and Hindu nationalist politics, arguably accelerating the communal fragmentation of Indian politics in subsequent decades.

## Historiography

Chiara Formichi (*Islam and Asia: A History*, 2020) treats the Khilafat Movement as a paradigm case of how pan-Islamic sentiment, generated by [[ottoman-empire|the Ottoman Empire]]'s wartime collapse, was absorbed into and reshaped local nationalist politics across Asia rather than remaining a unified transnational project. The movement is generally read by historians as evidence that pan-Islamism functioned less as coherent political unification than as a mobilizing idiom available to diverse local actors for their own ends.
