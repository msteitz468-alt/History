# Henry V of England

King of England (r. 1413–1422), the warrior-king whose victories in France brought the Lancastrian
dynasty and English fortunes in the [[hundred-years-war-1337|Hundred Years War]] to their zenith.
Treated by Edward Powell in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 21a), who judges him "better
prepared for kingship than any English monarch since Edward I" — already a seasoned soldier and head
of his father's council before his accession.

## Role and Decisions

Henry inherited the instability of his father **Henry IV's** usurpation (1399). His early reign
restored **"bone governance"**: strict exchequer controls re-established royal credit, vigorous
judicial visitations curbed disorder, and he reconciled the noble heirs disinherited under Henry IV
(making restoration conditional on **service**, above all in war). He survived the Lollard rising of
Sir John Oldcastle (1414) and the Cambridge plot (1415).

- **Agincourt (1415).** Exploiting the French civil war between **Armagnacs and Burgundians**, Henry
  invaded Normandy; his "astonishing triumph" at **Agincourt** was "a decisive turning-point,"
  shattering the Armagnac leadership and burying the question of Lancastrian legitimacy "for a
  generation" in a tide of national euphoria.
- **The conquest of Normandy (1417–1419)** and the methodical reduction of the duchy (Rouen fell
  January 1419), facilitated by the neutrality of **John the Fearless** of Burgundy.
- **The Treaty of Troyes (1420).** After John's murder at Montereau (1419) drove
  **[[valois-burgundian-state|Philip the Good]]** into alliance, Henry secured the **dual monarchy**:
  declared "Heir of France," married Charles VI's daughter Catherine, and disinherited the dauphin —
  "an achievement which had eluded even Edward III."

Henry died of dysentery in 1422, aged 36, leaving the nine-month-old **Henry VI** to inherit both
crowns.

## Counterfactual Significance

Powell frames Troyes as **the source of later Lancastrian ruin**: a triumph that committed England to
an "open-ended... war of conquest and attrition against dauphinist France" far beyond its resources.
Henry's own abilities could defer but not resolve the underlying problem of inadequate finance; under
his incapable son the strategy collapsed (see the [[hundred-years-war-1337|final phase]] and the
[[wars-of-the-roses-1455|Wars of the Roses]]). His reign is the high-water mark of medieval English
power in France and the model of the active warrior-king against which Henry VI was found so
disastrously wanting.
