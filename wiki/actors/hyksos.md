# The Hyksos (Fifteenth Dynasty)

The **Asiatic rulers of Lower Egypt** during the
[[second-intermediate-period|Second Intermediate Period]] (~1650–1550 BCE) —
*Hikau-khoswet*, "Rulers of Foreign Countries," whence the Greek "Hyksos." A
[[amorites|West-Semitic]] people who came to power not by a single invasion but by
**gradual infiltration** of the Delta, they ruled from **[[avaris|Avaris]]** until
the Theban war of liberation drove them out. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. II §§III–IV, Hayes).

## Not an invasion

CAH (1973) firmly sets aside the old picture of a sudden barbarian conquest. The
Hyksos were the leaders thrown up by **western Asiatic peoples, chiefly Semites**,
who had been settling the eastern Delta through the declining
[[middle-kingdom-egypt|Middle Kingdom]] — the same *Amu/Setjetiu* the Egyptians had
always known on the north-east border. The 400-year stela of Ramesses II dates the
"accession" of **Seth of Avaris** — the founding of the Hyksos principality — to
about **1720 BCE**; Memphis and the old residence of Itj-towy fell about **1674**,
breaking the Middle Kingdom apart.

## The Great Hyksos

The **Fifteenth Dynasty** — six "Great Hyksos" over ~108 years — included **Salitis**
(the founder, who fortified Avaris), **Khyan** ("Embracer-of-Regions," whose objects
turn up from Knossos to Baghdad, attesting wide trade), and the long-reigning
**Apophis I (Auserre)**, in whose 33rd year the **Rhind Mathematical Papyrus** was
copied and under whom Thebes still acknowledged Hyksos sovereignty. Lesser chiefs
made up the nominal Sixteenth Dynasty. The Hyksos adopted Egyptian kingship wholesale
— hieroglyphic titularies, the cult of an Egyptianized **Seth** (equated with their
own Asiatic storm-god), patronage of Egyptian scribal learning — and ruled through
local Egyptian officials, "not unduly harsh," far from the "ruthless barbarians" of
later Theban propaganda.

## The bridge to Asia

The Hyksos did Egypt "two things": they destroyed its old **insular complacency**,
and, being Asiatics whose realm reached into Palestine, they opened Egypt to
**continuous contact with western Asia**. Through them (or the wars against them)
Egypt acquired the **horse-drawn chariot**, the **composite bow**, improved bronze
weapons, and other innovations — first attested in war in the texts of **Kamose**.
This military and cultural transfer "laid the foundations and largely determined the
character" of the imperial [[new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom]].

## The fall

The Theban **17th Dynasty** turned on them: **Seqenenre Tao II** (killed in battle,
his mummy's skull split by Asiatic axes), **Kamose** (whose Karnak stelae record a
raid to the walls of Avaris), and finally **[[ahmose-i|Ahmose I]]**, who took
**[[avaris|Avaris]]** (~1550) and pursued the Hyksos to **Sharuhen** in Palestine,
besieging it three years. With them the [[second-intermediate-period|Second
Intermediate Period]] ended and the [[new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom]] began.

## Significance

The Hyksos are the catalyst that converted Egypt from a self-contained Nile kingdom
into an **outward-facing, militarized empire**. They are also a historiographical
lesson: the gulf between the **contemporary evidence** (an Egyptianized, tolerated
foreign administration) and the **later legend** (Manetho's tale of a brutal
conquest and a negotiated exodus of 240,000) shows how completely New Kingdom
ideology rewrote the memory of national humiliation — a rewriting that also colours
the later biblical "sojourn in Egypt" traditions.

## Shaw (2000): the view from Tell el-Dabʿa

Janine Bourriau's chapter rests the modern picture on Manfred Bietak's excavation of
**[[places/avaris|Avaris (Tell el-Dabʿa)]]** — which "revolutionized" Hyksos studies. Key
refinements:

- **Who they were.** The name *hekau khasut*, "rulers of foreign countries," applied only to
  the **rulers**, not the population (called *aamu*, "Asiatics"); and every traceable
  personal and royal name is **West Semitic** ("earlier suggestions that some were Hurrian or
  even Hittite have not been confirmed"). The community at Tell el-Dabʿa — Syrian house
  plans, donkey burials, intramural tombs, non-Egyptian pottery and weapons — is "the only
  convincing archaeological evidence for a population of Asiatics within Egypt … during the
  Middle Kingdom," growing out of generations of Levantine settlement, not a single invasion.
- **A real dynasty.** A Turin Canon fragment gives the 15th Dynasty "6 rulers of foreign
  countries ruling for 108 years"; at its height under **Aauserra Apepi** (c.1555 BCE, a
  ~40-year reign) Avaris covered nearly 4 km² — "three times larger than Hazor," the biggest
  Middle Bronze site in Palestine. Apepi patronized Egyptian scribal learning (the **Rhind
  Mathematical Papyrus** was copied in his year 33) and styled himself King of Upper and
  Lower Egypt, ruling Nile traffic and taxation "on the model set up by the 12th-Dynasty
  kings in their rule over Nubia."
- **A telling technological detail.** Late Hyksos weapons at Tell el-Dabʿa were of
  **unalloyed copper**, while Upper Egyptian (Theban) weapons stayed **tin bronze** — "this
  would have given the Thebans a clear advantage in hand-to-hand fighting." On the horse and
  chariot, generally credited to the Hyksos, there is as yet "no firm evidence" at Avaris
  itself.

## Historiography

Known from a thin and one-sided record: scarabs and seal-impressions (the chief
source for the kings' names and their wide distribution), the Theban war-monuments
(Kamose's stelae, the Ahmose son-of-Ibana inscription), the Turin Canon, and
Manetho via Josephus — all either fragmentary or hostile. The "Hyksos forts" and
distinctive material culture once attributed to them have largely been
**disattributed**, leaving little evidence of a culture of their own. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
