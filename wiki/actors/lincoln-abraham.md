# Abraham Lincoln

> **Detailed biography:** [[hubs/biographies/long-19th-century/lincoln-abraham|Abraham Lincoln — Detailed Biography (Biography Hub)]]

Abraham Lincoln (1809–1865) was the 16th President of the United States. He led the Union through the American Civil War, issued the Emancipation Proclamation, delivered the Gettysburg Address, and was assassinated at the moment of victory. This page summarizes his life as the central actor in the preservation of the Union and the destruction of slavery.

## Overview

Born in frontier Kentucky and raised in Indiana and Illinois, Lincoln rose from poverty through self-education, law, and Whig/Republican politics. His 1858 debates with Stephen Douglas made him a national figure. Elected in 1860 on a platform opposing slavery's expansion, his presidency was defined by the secession crisis, total war, and the transformation of American society and government.

## Key Developments

- Early life and self-making: Kentucky/Indiana frontier, loss of mother (milk sickness, 1818), stepmother Sarah Bush Johnston’s transformative care, New Salem years (postmaster, surveyor, Black Hawk War captaincy—proudest moment), move to Springfield, legal partnerships, marriage to Mary Todd.
- Political apprenticeship: Illinois legislature (Whig), one term in Congress (opposed Mexican War), return to law, after Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) reentered politics. "House Divided" speech (1858); Lincoln-Douglas debates (Freeport Doctrine confrontation); lost Senate but gained national name. 1860 Republican nomination as “Rail Splitter” compromise candidate; victory with ~40% popular vote; Southern secession begins before inauguration.
- Presidency and Civil War: Fort Sumter crisis (chose to provision despite cabinet divisions and Scott advice; advance notice to place responsibility for first shot on Confederates; 75,000 militia call; Upper South secedes); suspension of habeas corpus (Taney ignored); initial military setbacks and general turnover; border-state management (Kentucky neutrality preserved via arms to Unionists); Emancipation evolution (initial resistance to black troops, Frémont overruled to protect border loyalty, preliminary Sept 1862, final Jan 1863 with black soldiers authorized); Gettysburg Address (“new birth of freedom”); 1864 politics (Wade-Davis pocket veto and 10% plan flexibility, Niagara “To Whom It May Concern,” Chase resignation and Fessenden appointment, Blair resignation bargain for Radical support); Grant appointed lieutenant general with wide authority (“not easily excited... grit of a bull-dog”); Wilderness/Petersburg persistence (“fight it out on this line if it takes all summer”); Atlanta fall shifts momentum; Second Inaugural (“malice toward none”); Richmond visit (April 4 1865, magnanimity to freedmen, Virginia legislature proposal later rescinded); Appomattox terms generous; assassination April 14 1865 at Ford’s Theatre by Booth (triggered in part by April 11 suffrage remarks).

**Cabinet “team of rivals” (Goodwin, 2005):** Lincoln deliberately appointed his three chief 1860 rivals—Seward (State), Chase (Treasury), Bates (Attorney General)—plus Stanton (War), Welles (Navy), and Blair (Postmaster). He rejected Seward’s early bid for “premier” control (April 1 memo) with “I must do it.” Through storytelling, humor, patience, and assumption of blame he converted initial skeptics into loyal colleagues. Seward became his closest confidant; even Chase and Stanton came to revere him. Lincoln managed repeated resignation threats, exposed Chase’s disloyalty in the “Gordian knot” crisis while retaining him, and waited for military success (Seward’s advice) before issuing the Emancipation Proclamation. The cabinet never fractured the government.
- Legacy: Union preserved as indivisible nation. Slavery abolished (13th Amendment). 13th–15th Amendments and expanded federal power. Model of democratic leadership in crisis. Icon of American identity. Debates over racial views, war leadership, and “greatness” continue.

## Significance and Counterfactuals

Lincoln's leadership prevented the permanent division of the United States and ended legal slavery in the process. Counterfactually, a different president might have allowed compromise on slavery's expansion, accepted Southern independence, or pursued different war policies (e.g., no emancipation or earlier negotiated peace). His combination of moral clarity on slavery's wrongness with political pragmatism was decisive.

**Cabinet and political genius (Goodwin emphasis):** After the 1860 nomination Lincoln made the unprecedented decision to bring his principal rivals (Seward, Chase, Bates) and other strong figures (Stanton, Welles, Blair) into the cabinet. Initially viewed with skepticism or contempt by several, Lincoln established undisputed leadership through self-confidence, timing, storytelling, humor, and willingness to share credit and absorb blame. Seward became his closest friend and counselor; even Chase and Stanton came to recognize his unmatched qualities. This “team of rivals” managed the war, emancipation, and 1864 politics without fracturing the government.
