## Overview

The Neanderthals (*Homo neanderthalensis*; some authors prefer *Homo sapiens
neanderthalensis*) were the characteristic archaic human population of Pleistocene
Europe and western Asia. Hoffecker (CWH Vol. I, Ch. 16) calls them "the only true
Europeans among the human family" — they *evolved in Europe*, reflecting local
environmental conditions plus the consequences of isolation and genetic drift.
They are the archaic humans best known to science and the principal foil against
which the distinctiveness of modern *Homo sapiens* is measured.

## Origin and Range

- **Ancestry**: Evolved in Europe from *Homo heidelbergensis* (which invaded
  Europe from Africa via the Bosporus ~600,000 BP). Characteristic Neanderthal
  traits are visible from ~600,000 BP; "classic" Neanderthals emerge in the fossil
  record by ~250,000 BP. The Sima de los Huesos fossils (Atapuerca, Spain) show
  Neanderthal traits possibly by ~500,000 BP (dating debated).
- **Sister population**: ancient DNA shows the [[denisovans|Denisovans]] were a
  related archaic population that diverged from the Neanderthal line; some
  "cousins of the Neanderthals" lived in the Altai region and perhaps
  elsewhere in Asia.
- **Range**: Europe, the Near East (Levant), and Central Asia. They were the first
  humans to inhabit the cold East European Plain (artefacts at Sukhaya Mechetka,
  Volgograd; the move east took place 350,000–150,000 BP per aDNA). They survived
  in refugia such as the Iberian peninsula and the Urals.

## Physical Adaptation

Stocky, barrel-chested, with powerful musculature; shortened forearms and lower
legs (an evolved cold-adaptation reducing heat loss in the extremities). Massive
heads with brain volume *slightly larger* than living humans, but with a low, flat
cranial vault and prominent brow ridge; protruding face with a formidable nose and
enlarged front teeth. Hoffecker stresses that most characteristic features were
consequences of **drift and isolation**, not purely cold adaptation.

## Subsistence and Technology

- **Hunting**: special skill at hunting large mammals — mammoth, woolly rhinoceros,
  red deer, reindeer, bison. Stable-isotope analysis shows most protein came from
  meat; specific isotope techniques reveal mammoth and rhinoceros as favoured prey.
  At La Cotte de St Brelade (Jersey), mammoths and rhinos were apparently driven
  over a cliff.
- **Stone tools**: skilled stone-workers, comparable to modern humans for the most
  part. Their toolkit is the **Mousterian**, based on **Levallois prepared-core
  technique** (the beginning of the Middle Paleolithic, ~300,000 BP). François
  Bordes devised the classic typology of Mousterian retouched forms.
- **Composite tools**: recently demonstrated — pointed/beveled stone blanks hafted
  onto wooden shafts using adhesives (pine resin and **bitumen**). The Levallois
  technique may have served partly to produce interchangeable standardized blanks.
- **Limitations**: apparently lacked **sewn clothing** (no eyed needles, unlike
  modern humans) and probably **could not make fire** (hearths are present but
  erratic; they likely captured naturally-generated fire). See [[fire-management]].

## The Neanderthal Mind (contested)

The central interpretive question is how similar Neanderthals were to us:
- **Burial of the dead**: Neanderthals dug graves and intentionally buried some
  dead (probably without grave goods). Often read as evidence of belief in an
  afterlife / religion — but some argue it was mere corpse disposal. **Unresolved.**
- **Language**: They possessed the **FOXP2** gene (associated with speech), found
  in the draft Neanderthal genome (2010). Efforts to reconstruct the vocal tract
  have not demonstrated a decisive difference from modern humans, and it is unclear
  major differences would preclude language. **Unresolved.**
- **Social structure**: aDNA from El Sidrón (Spain) — twelve individuals who died
  together (a social group) — shows the three adult females derived from different
  maternal lineages while adult males shared one lineage, suggesting a **patrilocal
  mating pattern** (contrast the matrilocal female-coalition model proposed for
  early modern humans; see [[paleolithic-foraging-lifeways]]).
- **Cultural stasis**: The decisive contrast with modern humans. The Neanderthal
  archaeological record is "essentially devoid of creativity and innovation" — in
  their final millennia they made the same artefacts as 200,000 years earlier.
  Since modern human language is inherently creative (generating novel sentences
  daily), the lack of creativity in Neanderthal artefacts may mirror a lack of
  *linguistic* creativity. See [[syntactic-language-origins]].

## Relationship with Modern Humans and Extinction

- **Interbreeding**: The draft Neanderthal genome (2010) confirmed limited gene
  flow: non-African modern humans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal DNA. The interbreeding
  is thought to have occurred ~100,000 BP in the Near East, *before* modern humans
  entered Europe. At least one Neanderthal skeletal trait appears in early modern
  human remains from Romania.
- **The Châtelperronian question**: Neanderthal skeletal remains are associated
  with ornaments and bone tools in southwest France (the Châtelperronian industry).
  This may indicate Neanderthal imitation of modern human behavior — or the layers
  may be **mixed** deposits of late Neanderthals and earliest modern humans
  (growing suspicion favors mixing). See the broader debate at
  [[homo-sapiens-emergence-date]].
- **Extinction**: Modern humans filtered into Europe after ~50,000 BP; Neanderthals
  were gone by ~40,000–30,000 BP. Proposed causes: ecological competition, outright
  warfare, disease, or some combination. The cultural/creative gap likely gave
  modern humans the decisive advantage.

## Counterfactual Significance

The Neanderthals are the clearest test case for what "behavioral modernity" means.
Anatomically capable (large-brained, FOXP2-bearing, skilled tool-makers) yet
behaviorally static, they demonstrate that anatomy alone does not produce the
cumulative cultural creativity of modern humans — sharpening the argument that
[[syntactic-language-origins|full syntactic language]] was the critical threshold.
Had Neanderthals not gone extinct, the very concept of a single "human" species
would be different.

## Fagan & Durrani (2020) — Genomic Update

Fagan & Durrani (2020) confirm and sharpen the genomic picture: Svante Pääbo's
team sequenced the full Neanderthal genome in 2010, establishing that ~1–4%
(later refined to ~2%) of the DNA of all non-sub-Saharan-African modern humans
is Neanderthal in origin — interbreeding is dated to roughly 50,000 BP (some
estimates 60,000–40,000 BP), most likely in the Middle East, before the
non-African founder population split into its European/Asian/Pacific branches
(shared roughly equally by French, Chinese, and Papua New Guinean genomes). No
Neanderthals ever lived in tropical Africa. A 2017 finding complicates the
tidy sequence further: H. sapiens DNA was detected in a **124,000-year-old**
Neanderthal fossil from Germany — surprising, since modern humans were not
thought to have reached Europe until ~60,000 BP, implying an earlier and more
complex history of contact than the standard model allows. Fagan & Durrani
date the Neanderthal–sapiens lineage split (from DNA) to ~400,000 BP,
consistent with the revised Jebel Irhoud dating of early *H. sapiens* (see
[[../periods/behavioral-modernity|Behavioral Modernity]]). They also confirm
the 2010 identification of the **Denisovans** (see [[denisovans]]) as a
sister population from the same research, and note the low genetic diversity
of the shrinking Neanderthal population (Spain-to-Siberia range) from
~100,000 BP, with extinction dated ~39,000 BP by genetic data (some
archaeologists prefer outlying survival ~10,000 years later). Neanderthal
burials at La Ferrassie (two adults + four children interred together) and La
Chapelle-aux-Saints (an elderly, toothless man implying caregiving) are cited
as evidence for group care and possibly afterlife belief; the shared FOXP2
speech gene is again noted as consistent with (but not proof of) speech
capacity.

## Historiography

Neanderthals were the first archaic humans recognized by science (19th-century
Europe), and European archaeology's dominance long warped understanding of the
Paleolithic (see [[eurocentrism]] and the European-origin-of-humans assumption
corrected only in the 20th century). The field has been transformed since 2010 by
**ancient DNA** (the Neanderthal genome; El Sidrón social structure; Denisovan
identification). Major debates remain genuinely open: Neanderthal language,
symbolic capacity, the meaning of burials, and the precise mechanism of extinction.

[[tattersall-world-from-beginnings-2008|Tattersall (2008)]] represents the strong
**separate-species, replacement** position: *Homo neanderthalensis* was a distinct,
non-symbolic species (anatomy described in detail neck-up and neck-down), William
King (1864) was right to name it separately, its distinctness is confirmed by mtDNA
falling well outside the modern range, and it was essentially *replaced* rather than
absorbed by the [[cro-magnons]] (~40 kya), with at most marginal "Pleistocene
hanky-panky." Writing before the 2010 nuclear genome, he minimizes interbreeding —
the chief point on which the book is now dated, since non-Africans carry ~1–2%
Neanderthal ancestry. See [[modern-human-origins-debate]] and [[symbolic-cognition]].
