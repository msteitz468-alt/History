# Nicholas I of Russia

**Nicholas I** (1796–1855; r. 1825–1855) was Emperor of Russia, whose reign represented the apex of autocratic conservatism, militarism, and bureaucratic centralization in 19th-century Russia. Guided by the doctrine of Official Nationality, his administration sought to freeze social reform and isolate Russia from Western revolutionary influences, a policy that ultimately culminated in the disastrous Crimean War.

## Biography / Description

Nicholas was the third son of Emperor Paul I and the younger brother of Alexander I. Because he had not been expected to reign, he received a primarily military education and did not share the liberal training of Alexander. He was a militarist who loved parade-ground discipline, order, and details of administration. 

Nicholas ascended the throne in December 1825 under chaotic circumstances. Alexander I died suddenly, and a confusion over the succession between Nicholas and his older brother Constantine (who had secretly renounced his rights to the throne) left a power vacuum that reformist military officers exploited to launch the **Decembrist Revolt** on December 26, 1825. Nicholas personally commanded the loyal troops that crushed the mutineers in St. Petersburg. This traumatic introduction to his reign left Nicholas with a lifelong suspicion of social initiative, a dread of revolution, and a determination to maintain absolute autocratic control.

Throughout his reign, Nicholas was a devoted bureaucrat, working tirelessly and supervising the state directly through his personal chancellery. He died in 1855 during the siege of Sevastopol in the Crimean War, deeply disillusioned by the military collapse of the system he had spent his life building.

## Key Decisions and Achievements

### Administrative Centralization and the Third Section
Nicholas bypassed normal administrative channels in favor of his **His Majesty's Own Imperial Chancellery**, which he expanded into several specialized sections:
- **The Second Section:** Tasked with codifying Russian law, a monumental task successfully carried out under the direction of the rehabilitated Michael Speransky, resulting in the publication of the *Complete Collection of Laws of the Russian Empire* (1830).
- **The Third Section:** Created in 1826 as the political police and intelligence-gathering body of the empire. Assisted by the Corps of Gendarmes, it monitored public opinion, managed censorship, and prosecuted political dissidents.

### Ideology of Official Nationality
To counter Western liberal and revolutionary ideas, Nicholas's Minister of Education, Count Sergei Uvarov, formulated the doctrine of **Official Nationality** (defined by the triad: **Orthodoxy**, **Autocracy**, and **Nationality**). This doctrine asserted that Russia's strength lay in its devotion to the Orthodox Church, absolute autocracy, and the unique, submissive character of the Russian people. It served as the basis for severe censorship, restriction of university enrollments, and political repression.

### Social and Agrarian Policies
Although Nicholas recognized that serfdom was "an evil, palpable and obvious to all," he feared that any major reform would provoke a peasant uprising or alienate the gentry. Consequently, he limited reform to minor, half-hearted adjustments, such as Count Pavel Kiselev's administration of state peasants, while leaving landowners' serfdom untouched.

### Foreign Policy and the "Gendarme of Europe"
Nicholas pursued a highly active and conservative foreign policy:
- **Repression of Revolutions:** Nicholas crushed the Polish November Uprising (1830–31), abrogated the Polish constitution granted by Alexander I, and integrated Poland directly into the Russian Empire. In 1849, obeying the principles of legitimism, he sent Russian armies to crush the Hungarian Revolution and restore Habsburg rule in Austria, earning Russia the title of the "Gendarme of Europe."
- **Imperial Expansion:** Nicholas waged war against Qajar Persia (1826–28), securing the Treaty of Turkmenchay, and fought the Ottoman Empire (Russo-Turkish War of 1828–29), expanding Russian influence in the Balkans and Caucasus.
- **The Crimean War (1853–1856):** Triggered by disputes over the custody of the Holy Places and Russian claims to protect Orthodox subjects in the Ottoman Empire, Nicholas's aggressive diplomacy led to war with Turkey, Britain, France, and Sardinia. The war exposed the technological, logistical, and economic backwardness of Russia, culminating in the siege of Sevastopol and Russia's defeat.

## Historical Significance

Nicholas I's historical significance lies in his role as the ultimate defender of the old regime in Russia. His system of bureaucratic autocracy, military parade-ground discipline, and political policing succeeded in suppressing revolutionary change for thirty years but failed the test of international conflict. The defeat in the Crimean War demonstrated that Russia could not maintain its great-power status without fundamental economic and social modernization, forcing his successor, Alexander II, to launch the Great Reforms.
