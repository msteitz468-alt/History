# Lucius Cornelius Sulla Felix

The patrician who twice marched a Roman army on Rome, won the Republic's first
full civil war, and made himself **dictator to refound the constitution** — the
single most consequential figure between the Gracchi and Caesar. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Chs. 6–7 (Seager).

## Role and career

A patrician of an impoverished, long-obscure branch of the Cornelii, Sulla rose
through war: quaestor to [[marius]] in the Jugurthine War (he took the surrender of
Jugurtha, a coup he never let Marius forget), then a leading commander in the
**[[social-war-91-88bce|Social War]]**, which won him the consulship of **88** and
the command against **[[mithridates-vi|Mithridates]]**.

### The march on Rome (88)

When the tribune **P. Sulpicius**, allied with Marius, transferred the Mithridatic
command to Marius by popular vote, Sulla did the unprecedented: he persuaded his
army at Nola to **march on Rome** — the first Roman general to take the city by
force. Marius and Sulpicius fled (Sulpicius was killed); Sulla had twelve men
declared *hostes* and passed measures strengthening the Senate (no bill to the
people without prior senatorial approval; the *comitia centuriata* restored to its
"Servian" form). Seager stresses this fused genuine constitutional conviction —
that tribunician legislation defying the Senate must not stand — with naked
self-interest. He then left for the East.

### The Mithridatic War and the return

In Sulla's absence the consul **Cinna** and Marius seized Rome (87), massacred
Sulla's friends, and declared Sulla a *hostis* (see the **Cinnan domination**,
87–84). Sulla meanwhile won the **[[first-mithridatic-war-89-85bce|First Mithridatic
War]]**, sacked Athens, crushed Mithridates' armies at Chaeronea and Orchomenus, and
made the **Peace of Dardanus** (85) — accused even then of letting Mithridates off
to free his hands for civil war. He returned to Italy in **83** with the booty,
prestige (*Felix*, "the fortunate"), and a veteran army.

### Civil war, dictatorship, and abdication

Sulla won the **[[sullan-civil-war-83-82bce|civil war of 83–82]]**, sealed at the
**battle of the Colline Gate** (1 Nov. 82). He then instituted the
**[[sullan-proscriptions-82bce|proscriptions]]** and had himself made **dictator
*legibus scribundis et rei publicae constituendae*** — dictator to write laws and
set the state in order, with no fixed term. He used the office to enact the
**[[sullan-constitution|Sullan settlement]]**, then — astonishingly — **resigned**
(probably late 81), held an ordinary consulship in 80, and retired to Campania,
where he died in 78 of liver failure after a lifetime of hard drinking.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Sulla is the hinge of the late Republic. His march on Rome **proved that an army
loyal to its general could override the state** — the lesson Caesar would draw
("the young Caesar learned, like Sulla, to despise the Senate"). His
constitution tried to entrench senatorial supremacy permanently, yet his own
career had shown how to destroy it; within a decade its key restraints (the
crippled tribunate) were dismantled (see [[pompey]] and Crassus, cos. 70). His
**voluntary abdication** — the road not taken by later dynasts — became the great
puzzle of his life. Seager: his new Senate "was to show itself no more deserving
of respect," trapped in "a sterile conformism which exalted mediocrity."

## Historiography

The sources are **saturated in Sulla's own *Memoirs*** (dedicated to Lucullus),
"diffused without competition after his victory" — Appian and Plutarch's *Sulla*
both depend heavily on this apologia, which stressed his *felicitas* and divine
favour (the goddess Ma-Bellona, Apollo, Venus/Aphrodite — his Greek surname
*Epaphroditos*). Modern scholarship (Badian, Keaveney, Gabba) debates how far the
constitutional reforms of 88 prefigured those of the dictatorship, the chronology
of his abdication, and whether his settlement was reactionary or genuinely
reconstructive.
