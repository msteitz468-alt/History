# Lucius Cornelius Sulla Felix

The patrician who twice marched a Roman army on Rome, won the Republic's first
full civil war, and made himself **dictator to refound the constitution** — the
single most consequential figure between the Gracchi and Caesar. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Chs. 6–7 (Seager).

## Role and career

A patrician of an impoverished, long-obscure branch of the Cornelii, Sulla rose
through war: quaestor to [[marius]] in the Jugurthine War (he took the surrender of
[[jugurtha|Jugurtha]], a coup he never let Marius forget), served in the
[[cimbric-war-113-101bce|Cimbric War]] (transferred to Catulus's command, he shared
in the victory at Vercellae in 101), then became a leading commander in the
**[[social-war-91-88bce|Social War]]**, which won him the consulship of **88** and
the command against **[[mithridates-vi|Mithridates]]**.

### The march on Rome (88)

When the tribune **P. Sulpicius**, allied with Marius, transferred the Mithridatic
command to Marius by popular vote, Sulla did the unprecedented: he persuaded his
army at Nola to **march on Rome** — the first Roman general to take the city by
force. Marius and Sulpicius fled (Sulpicius was killed); Sulla had twelve men
declared *hostes* and passed measures strengthening the Senate (no bill to the
people without prior senatorial approval; the *comitia centuriata* restored to its
"Servian" form). Seager stresses this fused genuine constitutional conviction —
that tribunician legislation defying the Senate must not stand — with naked
self-interest. He then left for the East.

### The Mithridatic War and the return

In Sulla's absence the consul **Cinna** and Marius seized Rome (87), massacred
Sulla's friends, and declared Sulla a *hostis* (see the **Cinnan domination**,
87–84). Sulla meanwhile won the **[[first-mithridatic-war-89-85bce|First Mithridatic
War]]**, sacked Athens, crushed Mithridates' armies at Chaeronea and Orchomenus, and
made the **Peace of Dardanus** (85) — accused even then of letting Mithridates off
to free his hands for civil war. He returned to Italy in **83** with the booty,
prestige (*Felix*, "the fortunate"), and a veteran army.

### Civil war, dictatorship, and abdication

Sulla won the **[[sullan-civil-war-83-82bce|civil war of 83–82]]**, sealed at the
**battle of the Colline Gate** (1 Nov. 82). He then instituted the
**[[sullan-proscriptions-82bce|proscriptions]]** and had himself made **dictator
*legibus scribundis et rei publicae constituendae*** — dictator to write laws and
set the state in order, with no fixed term. He used the office to enact the
**[[sullan-constitution|Sullan settlement]]**, then — astonishingly — **resigned**
(probably late 81), held an ordinary consulship in 80, and retired to Campania,
where he died in 78 of liver failure after a lifetime of hard drinking.

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]]) flags Sulla's 88 BCE march as "the
first time" a Roman army is known to have crossed the *pomerium* against fellow
citizens, and gives the proscription lists a range of **2,000–9,000** equestrians and
senators — wider than some other sources' figures, worth noting as a range rather than a
fixed count — alongside the renewed disruption caused by confiscating land for veteran
settlement.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Sulla is the hinge of the late Republic. His march on Rome **proved that an army
loyal to its general could override the state** — the lesson Caesar would draw
("the young Caesar learned, like Sulla, to despise the Senate"). His
constitution tried to entrench senatorial supremacy permanently, yet his own
career had shown how to destroy it; within a decade its key restraints (the
crippled tribunate) were dismantled (see [[pompey]] and Crassus, cos. 70). His
**voluntary abdication** — the road not taken by later dynasts — became the great
puzzle of his life. Seager: his new Senate "was to show itself no more deserving
of respect," trapped in "a sterile conformism which exalted mediocrity."

## Historiography

The sources are **saturated in Sulla's own *Memoirs*** (dedicated to Lucullus),
"diffused without competition after his victory" — Appian and Plutarch's *Sulla*
both depend heavily on this apologia, which stressed his *felicitas* and divine
favour (the goddess Ma-Bellona, Apollo, Venus/Aphrodite — his Greek surname
*Epaphroditos*). Modern scholarship (Badian, Keaveney, Gabba) debates how far the
constitutional reforms of 88 prefigured those of the dictatorship, the chronology
of his abdication, and whether his settlement was reactionary or genuinely
reconstructive.

**Beard ([[sources/beard-spqr-2015|SPQR]], 2015)** argues the violence cannot be
reduced to one man's "motives" or dismissed as partisan exaggeration in the
sources: the two marches on Rome, the burning of the Temple of Jupiter, and the
proscription lists reflect a structural collapse — "the violence was much more
widespread than could possibly be put down to the influence of one man." She
identifies Sulla as "the first dictator in the modern sense of the term" (his
dictatorship broke precedent in having no time limit and granting unchecked,
immune lawmaking power), calling it "one of Rome's most corrosive legacies" and
naming Caesar its direct heir ("the second"). She also credits Sulla as the first
Roman known to have officially branded fellow citizens *hostes* (88 BCE) — a term
swiftly turned back on him — reading it as evidence of a deeper erosion of the
citizen/foreign-enemy distinction that followed directly from the
[[social-war-91-88bce|Social War's]] blurring of ally and citizen lines.

### OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Griffin & Crawford

Miriam Griffin (Ch. 19): “the age of Cicero was, in many respects, the legacy of
Sulla.” Sallust dated the final moral turning-point to Sulla’s Eastern return, armed
seizure of the city, and vindictive victory after Carthage’s fall removed external
discipline. The dictator fixed the *cursus honorum* (~20 years’ senatorial
socialization before top commands), a Senate of 600 (20 quaestors/year), 2 consuls and
8 praetors, senatorial juries, and savage competition driving *ambitus* laws. He made
senatorial dominance explicit as Optimate bulwark; year 70 undid tribunician
restrictions under Pompey and Crassus. Crawford (Ch. 16): Sulla did **not** undo
Italian enfranchisement; post-Sulla Romanization advanced amid bilingual→Latin
language shift.

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010)

From [[flower-roman-republics-2010]]. Flower makes Sulla the **founder of a sixth republic**, not the restorer of an older one. After the last *nobiles* republic collapsed in the early 80s (Social War + citizen civil war), Sulla’s two marches on Rome, Cinna’s *dominatio*, and the dictatorship mark a clean break; his settlement of **81** is a revolutionary **New Republic** imposed by force (courts and *lex* over *contiones* and *mos maiorum* — see [[sullan-constitution]]). Self-presentation as victory-celebrant and inaugurator of a new age supports founder-not-restorer reading; he freed ~10,000 able-bodied male slaves of the proscribed as *Cornelii* agents in the city; he cut through the equestrian order by killings and by promoting hundreds of survivors (many Italian townsmen) into the enlarged senate as jurors. Flower stresses that **ambition and achievement outside a republican framework** set the most powerful (and devastating) example for a community already stripped of traditional political culture and divided into civil-war winners and losers — the shadow under which the next generation lived “after the shipwreck.” Periodization frame: [[roman-republics-periodization]]; fall debate: [[fall-of-roman-republic]].

## Woolf, Rome: An Empire's Story (2012)

From [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]] Ch. IX.

- **March on Rome (88).** Rivalry with Marius compounded by Jugurtha coup and popularis/aristocratic split; consul 88 with Mithridatic command; refused tribune’s transfer to Marius; marched army into the city; Sulpicius killed; Marius exiled; Senate compelled to reassign eastern command.
- **Proscription and dictatorship.** Returning from the east: invaded Italy, seized Rome, dictatorship, proscription lists—“Sulla invented proscription. His violence and the feuds it stirred up haunted Rome for a generation.” Constitutional laws, veteran colonies, resigned (80), died natural causes (79).
- **Lethal precedent.** Grim legacy less broken laws than example: first general to attack Rome with a Roman army; dictatorship as tool for suspending civil society; politics incurably partisan and personal. Later fear of Pompey returning “like Sulla”; Caesar dictator; Octavian/Antony proscriptions.
- **Landless soldiers.** Increased recruitment of landless citizens made them depend on generals for resettlement: Sulla’s army marched for eastern booty, then for land. No Republican reform (including Sulla’s) solved this; Augustus would, via military treasury and standing army.
- **Quotes:** “Sulla was the first general to attack Rome with a Roman army.” / “Sulla invented proscription.”
