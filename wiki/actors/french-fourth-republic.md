## Overview

The French Fourth Republic (1946–1958) was the parliamentary regime that succeeded Vichy France and the wartime provisional government of Charles de Gaulle. Created by referendum after French voters rejected (nearly 20 to 1, October 1945) any return to the discredited Third Republic, it combined a reaffirmation of French parliamentary and libertarian traditions with an ambition — never fully realized — to be more efficient and socially progressive than its predecessor. It governed France through reconstruction, the onset of the Cold War, and the early stages of decolonization before collapsing under the strain of the Algerian War and giving way to de Gaulle's Fifth Republic in 1958.

## Role

General Charles de Gaulle headed the provisional government during the constitution-drafting period but resigned in January 1946, disgusted by the revival of party bickering; the Fourth Republic he handed off resembled the Third Republic he had criticized, with a weak executive and a dominant Chamber of Deputies elected by party list. Three parties born of the Resistance coalition — the Communists, the Socialists, and the Christian Democratic Mouvement républicain populaire (MRP) — divided the vote roughly equally and governed in *tripartisme* until May 1947, when the onset of the Cold War forced the Communists out of government (Premier Paul Ramadier dismissed Communist ministers after they voted against government wage policy). Thereafter the Republic was chronically unstable: twenty-six cabinets in twelve years, with governments sometimes taking weeks to form.

Despite this instability, the Fourth Republic built lasting institutions: nationalization went further than in Britain (Bank of France, major insurers, coal, steel, electricity, gas, and — after Louis Renault's death awaiting a collaboration trial — the Renault automobile firm); the social security system of 1931 was extended to universal medical insurance; and the Commissariat du Plan under Jean Monnet introduced "indicative" economic planning that modernized French productive capacity (French GNP rose 14 percent above 1938 levels by the end of the First Plan, 1947–1952). The regime obtained renunciation of French war debts and a $650 million U.S. reconstruction loan in 1947 (negotiated by Monnet and Léon Blum), a decision entangled with pressure to exclude Communists from government.

## Key Events and Decisions

- 1946 constitutional referendum and founding of the parliamentary republic (weak executive, list-based elections).
- May 1947: expulsion of Communist ministers from government, ending *tripartisme* and splitting the French Left and its trade-union federation (Force ouvrière vs. the Communist CGT).
- Participation in the [[marshall-plan-1948]] and the Organization for European Economic Cooperation (OEEC) framework.
- Adoption of the First Plan (1947–1952) under Jean Monnet's Commissariat du Plan.
- Chronic cabinet instability (twenty-six governments, 1946–1958) culminating in collapse under the Algerian War crisis of 1958.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had the Fourth Republic's constitution given the executive de Gaulle's preferred strength, French governmental instability during reconstruction and early Cold War crises might have been reduced — but the multiparty compromise reflected genuine popular rejection of both wartime authoritarianism and prewar drift. The regime's economic planning apparatus (Monnet, the Commissariat du Plan) proved durable and shaped French dirigisme long after 1958, surviving into the Fifth Republic largely intact.

## Historiography

Jean-Pierre Rioux, *The Fourth Republic* (1989), is the standard account; Frank Giles, *The Locust Years: The History of the Fourth French Republic, 1946–1958* (1995), offers a well-informed political narrative; Robert Gildea, *France since 1945*, situates it in longer context. Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century* (5th ed., 2011), treat the regime as a case study in the postwar Western European pattern of resistance-coalition government giving way to Cold War polarization — see [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].
