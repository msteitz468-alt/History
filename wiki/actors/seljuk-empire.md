# The Seljuk Empire (the Great Seljuks)

The **Seljuks** were a Turkic dynasty, originally leaders of the Oghuz Turkmen, who
built the dominant power of the eleventh-century Islamic east. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|
NCMH IV]] (Michael Brett, Ch. 22, "Abbasids, Fatimids and Seljuqs") sets them at the
centre of the transformation of the Near East that confronted both
[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]] and the [[crusades|Crusaders]].

## The Great Seljuk Sultanate

Under **Tughril Beg**, the Seljuks conquered Iran and entered **Baghdad in 1055**, where
they were recognised as **sultans** and protectors of the Sunni
[[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid caliphate]] — restoring Sunni political power against the
Shi'ite [[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] and Buyids. Under **Alp Arslan** and **Malik
Shah** (with the great Persian vizier **Nizam al-Mulk**, author of the *Siyasatnama* and
founder of the *nizamiyya* madrasas) the empire reached its height, stretching from
Central Asia to Syria. The Turkmen advance into Anatolia, sealed by Alp Arslan's victory
over Byzantium at **[[battle-of-manzikert-1071|Manzikert (1071)]]**, opened Asia Minor to
permanent Turkish settlement.

## Fragmentation and the Seljuks of Rum

The Great Seljuk empire fragmented after Malik Shah's death (1092) into competing
successor principalities (*atabegs*) — among them the lines from which **Zengi** and the
[[saladin|Ayyubid]] consolidation of Syria would emerge. The
**[[battle-of-hattin-1187|Sultanate of Rum]]** (the Seljuks of Anatolia), centred on
Konya, became a durable Turkish-Muslim state that bordered Byzantium and the crusader
routes and survived (after Mongol subjection) into the early fourteenth century — the
matrix from which the Anatolian beyliks, and ultimately the Ottomans, would arise.

## Syria, appanage politics, and the First Crusade (Cobb)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|*The Race for Paradise*]] reframes the Great Seljuks as a
**family appanage system** rather than a centralized provincial empire: the sultanate was
family property open to contest by brothers with armies; military households (*ʿaskar*,
often *mamluk*), *iqṭāʿ* tax-land grants, and the **atabeg** office (“Father-Lord”) as
tutor/regent made the system work only while recruitment, kinship, and patronage overlapped
— “very occasionally, in crucial moments, it crashed.” Cobb also hedges the label “Sunni
Revival”: sultanic patronage of *madrasas* and *ulema* is better read as reciprocal
reward/punishment than a concerted top-down program.

**Entry into Syria.** Local openings preceded imperial conquest: in 1064 Mirdasid claimants
at Aleppo hired Turcoman freelancers — the first Turks settled into Syrian/Levantine
politics — an invitation Cobb analogizes to Norman Sicily and Alexios I’s 1095 appeal:
hosts “got more from their guests than they had bargained for.” Great Seljuk follow-on was
**conquest**, not mere pasturage. In 1070 Mahmud of Aleppo publicly rejected Fatimid
suzerainty for **Alp Arslan** and the Sunni Abbasid caliph; Aleppo submitted 5 May 1071,
days before Alp Arslan diverted north to [[battle-of-manzikert-1071|Manzikert]]. A later
messenger anecdote cast the Euphrates crossing as ending the “reign of the Arabs” and
beginning the “reign of the Turks.”

**Tutush (fold).** After Fatimid-hired warlord **Atsiz** occupied Jerusalem and southern
Syria and took Damascus (1075), **Tutush** — brother of **Malikshah**, holding Syria as
appanage — killed Atsiz, installed his own man in Damascus, and by July 1086 secured
Aleppo’s “last” surrender: nearly all Syria under Saljuq control after roughly two decades
of war. When Malikshah died (1092), civil war followed; Tutush’s bid against Barkiyaruq
failed and he died in battle in Iran (1095). Sultans never regained firm control of Syria;
**Ridwan** (Aleppo) and **Duqaq** (Damascus, with atabeg **Tughtakin**) inherited Tutush’s
legacy on the eve of the Frankish invasion.

**Ridwan, Duqaq, and the early Frankish war.** Summer 1097: Ridwan, [[yaghi-siyan|Yaghi-Siyan]]
of Antioch, Karbuqa of Mosul, and Sukman of Diyar Bakr were campaigning in central Syria
when news of Franks toward Antioch arrived; the coalition argued and dispersed — Ridwan to
Aleppo, Yaghi-Siyan to Antioch. News that **Qilij Arslan (Kilij Arslan)** and the
Danishmendids had failed to stop the Frankish advance across Anatolia already framed the
crisis. Ridwan’s and Duqaq’s relief attempts during the siege failed; after Antioch’s fall
(1098), Ridwan’s messages against Duqaq contributed to Turcoman desertions from Karbuqa’s
relief army. Early response zones, per Cobb, were five: Fatimid Egypt; Damascus
(Duqaq/Tughtakin); Aleppo (Ridwan); Upper Mesopotamia/Diyar Bakr (Artuqids foremost); and
formal sultanic jihad campaigns of “tens of thousands,” usually via Mosul, with little
positive effect.

Ridwan later accepted Fatimid al-Mustaʿli’s invitation of loyalty — Fatimid names in Friday
prayers across his pulpits “for four Fridays” only, then reverted to Abbasid/Barkiyaruq —
an illustration of “shifting political sands.” He managed sizable Shiʿite/Ismaʿili/Nizari
subjects, allowing a Nizari *dār al-daʿwa* in Aleppo; after his death (1113) a prefect’s
pogrom killed or imprisoned Nizaris, who dispersed across Syria but were “rarely subdued.”
In 1102–3 he cleared Franks from his domains and threatened Antioch; April 1105 his jihad
contingent for Tripoli was routed by Tancred at Artah. Sept 1108 near Tall Bashir: the
“first of many ironic tangles” — Tancred of Antioch + Ridwan vs the sultan’s man Jawuli of
Mosul + Baldwin of Edessa. 1104: Duqaq died; Tughtakin ruled as “an atabeg without a
prince” after Tutush II’s death without heir.

**Rum and 1101.** The Crusade of 1101 was wiped out when Saljuqs of Rum and Danishmendids
allied with Ridwan; the Anatolian land route was denied for decades. The Qilij
Arslan–Danishmend alliance broke by 1103. By Cobb’s reading, Oghuz/Saljuq migration was
“the most important engine of change in Islamdom since the career of the Prophet Muhammad
himself” — structural fragmentation, not mere Frankish luck, framed the First Crusade
shock.

## Significance

The Seljuks reshaped the medieval Near East: they restored Sunni political dominance,
patronised a flowering of Persianate Islamic culture and the madrasa system, opened
**Anatolia** to Turkish and Muslim settlement (the long-term Turkification of Asia
Minor), and, by shattering Byzantine power at Manzikert, helped precipitate the western
[[crusades|Crusades]]. Cobb stresses that the same appanage volatility that made Syria
hard for any sultan to hold also made a unified Muslim response to the Franks nearly
impossible until later Syrian consolidators (Zangi, Nur al-Din, Saladin).
