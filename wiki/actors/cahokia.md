## Overview

Cahokia stands alone at the upper end of Mississippian scale and complexity — the largest and most complex Native American polity to develop north of Mesoamerica (Bruce D. Smith, CHNPA I.1, ch. 5). It occupied the [[american-bottom]] floodplain east of the Mississippi opposite modern St. Louis. Over roughly five centuries (~A.D. 900–1400) the area witnessed a cycle of growth, florescence, and decline below archaeological visibility, with Cahokia central throughout. When Europeans first passed through in the late seventeenth century, the once-dense valley in this region was all but empty — part of the broader pre-contact **Vacant Quarter**.

## Site Scale and Architecture

Smith’s figures: site estimated ~**13 km²** with ~**100 earthen mounds** (burial or building platforms); mound clusters and plazas as civic-ceremonial foci of subcommunities.

**Monks Mound** — largest prehistoric mound north of Mexico: construction begun ~A.D. 900, completed by A.D. 1150, still used as late as A.D. 1300; **316 × 240 m** base, **30 m** high, four platforms; base area larger than the whole of Powers Fort ([[powers-phase]]). Smith also cites 6.5 ha base area / 30 m height for the same structure. Most Mississippian mounds are far more modest.

**Central precinct**: an **83-ha** core enclosed by a large palisade wall, including Monks Mound, other mounds (**including Mound 72**), residential areas, and plazas. **≥5 woodhenge** post circles (up to 130 m diameter) likely tracking sunrises/sunsets. Palisade construction is relatively late in the occupational sequence (reactive enclosure of the core, not an original layout element in all phases).

Interspersed among the mounds were residential areas of pole-and-thatch houses, maize fields, and water features; an abandoned river channel/oxbow crossed the site. Mill Creek (Illinois) chert agricultural hoes link Cahokia’s region into long-distance utilitarian exchange (mostly within ~200 km of the quarry; some up to ~700 km).

## Population Estimates

Peak florescence ~**A.D. 1050–1150**. High-end population estimates of **26,000–43,000** (commonly 30,000–40,000 in popular literature) were derived by projecting dense “downtown” residential-block excavation density over the entire site area. Smith: much of the site had much lower density, so high-end estimates are likely **four to five times greater** than actual peak population. Even at more reasonable estimates **in the thousands rather than tens of thousands**, Cahokia was still the largest population concentration north of Mexico before Europeans. Milner and others’ lower ranges (often cited ~3,000–8,000 in secondary surveys) sit in the same “thousands” band Smith defends. Mean age at death among Middle Mississippians ~25 (Cahokia/St. Louis area; disease-pool comparative claim).

Inflated population figures underwrite empire scenarios that require nonlocal bulk food provision — which Smith rejects (below).

## Political Organization and the States Debate

Some prehistorians cast Cahokia as a great city — urban capital of a powerful **state-level** society controlling vast eastern territories. Smith judges claims of vast size, complexity, and socio-political reach **often greatly overdrawn**, while acknowledging that the site is indeed impressive. He operationalizes Cahokia as a midrange **[[chiefdom]]** / complex chiefdom within the [[band-tribe-chiefdom-state]] framework — unstable regional integration rather than state or empire. Positions on both sides live on [[north-american-states-debate]]; this page records them without adjudication.

**American Bottom landscape** (Smith): smaller town/mound centers include East St. Louis, Mitchell, Lohmann, Grossmann/GrossLake, Lunsford-Pulcher, and Mathews — from ~20 km north to ~65 km south of Cahokia — with diverse occupational histories. Many people lived in short-lived farmsteads; favorable levee loci show specialized corporate structures (small circular sweatlodges; large rectangular dual-hearth structures). Landscape pattern: generally equivalent mound-centered societal groups with Cahokia central; **politically independent to varying degrees**; economically self-sufficient; interlocking cycles of florescence/decline — “rather than forming a rigid, centralized, and highly organized system.”

Cahokia’s elite directly controlled adjacent floodplain and exercised varying **indirect** administrative/tributary oversight of other American Bottom mound communities. More elaborate integration is evidenced at BBB Motor and Sponemann nodal sites (elaborate mortuaries; fertility figurines of nonlocal bauxitic clay, e.g. Birger Figurine). Paramount status is shown by ability to draw **tribute labor** for Monks Mound, central-precinct fortifications, woodhenges, and other public structures. Integration mechanisms are unknown but, by analogy to other midrange chiefdom groups, most likely involved extension of kinship status and obligation networks — acknowledging lower-order elite and inserting Cahokia functionaries locally.

**Developmental outline (Smith)**:
- ~A.D. 950–1000: Cahokia rose to paramountcy among roughly equal American Bottom mound societies; large-scale Monks Mound and other construction began.
- By A.D. 1100: woodhenges and central palisades built (command of large regional labor); elaborate mortuary programs (Mound 72) reflect growing elite power.
- Concurrent landscape shift: households dispersed from large villages into scattered single-family farmsteads — read as expansion of central authority and short-term normalization of relations among polities.
- Kinship-based structure defined both limits of expansion and vulnerability: each mound-centered society retained latent desire and ability to escape central control.

## Mound 72 — Elite Mortuary and Retainer Sacrifice

**Mound 72** (just south of Monks Mound), built over a disused woodhenge, hosted central-precinct elite mortuary ritual in related burial episodes (Smith, full archaeological framing):

- A **personage of paramount importance** distinguished by location and burial furniture.
- Accompanied by other **lower-status elite** personages.
- Accompanied by a group of **young female retainers who were sacrificed and interred in a mass grave** placed over a post pit of the woodhenge.

Lower-order elite facilities elsewhere in the system (e.g. **Wilson Mound** communal facility west of Cahokia: bundle burials with marine whelks and shell beads) are clearly differentiated from nonelite burials by location and degree of elaboration of burial goods and programs. Secondary literature (Snow et al. and excavation reports) also reports large marine-shell bead caches with high-ranking individuals and additional sacrificial pit assemblages (including decapitated and de-handed victims in some accounts); counts and pit inventories vary by report — record both Smith’s retainer-sacrifice statement and the broader sacrificial mortuary program without softening.

## Economy and Prestige Exchange

Intensive maize agriculture on fertile levees, plus fish and waterfowl from oxbows and the Mississippi flyway, underwrote surplus. Long-distance exchange almost exclusively moved lightweight **prestige** items among elites (copper, shell, galena, mica, bauxite figurines) — not bulk foodstuffs. Distinctive A.D. 1000–1200 Cahokia design motifs and artifact forms at mound centers across the eastern U.S. attest to broad elite contact, but scenarios of a far-flung empire extracting deer skins and agricultural surplus (even from western New York) depend on inflated population estimates. Smith: neither capacity for long-distance political control nor need for bulk agricultural surplus is present; **Cahokia was neither the core of a broad empire nor the prime mover of Mississippian development elsewhere**; its sphere of influence and control rarely extended much beyond the American Bottom; basic organization was in most aspects comparable to much smaller and far more abundant societies such as the [[powers-phase]].

## Ceremonial Life

Cahokia was a major generative and redistributive node for [[southeastern-ceremonial-complex]] materials and styles. Red bauxite fertility/agriculture figurines and shell/copper prestige goods link the site to [[spiro]], Shiloh, [[etowah]], and other centers. Woodhenges, Monks Mound, and the enclosed precinct framed public ritual. Chunky and related plaza activities are part of the broader Mississippian ceremonial package.

## Decline and Legacy

Complex chiefdoms are unstable. Cahokia’s florescence had **largely fragmented by A.D. 1150–1200**, marked by the beginning of large-scale depopulation of the American Bottom. Suggested contributing factors (Smith): rising groundwater reducing farmland; firewood depletion. The aftermath A.D. 1200–1300 was **not** a simple scaling back to preexisting equal polities but **large-scale abandonment of the river floodplain** and the appearance of smaller-scale polities in upland stream-valley settings. By ~A.D. 1400 the site and the broader central valley fall within the **Vacant Quarter** — dramatic interior decline **before** European explorers or introduced diseases, causes unknown (do not collapse into post-contact epidemic narratives alone). Secondary climate literature also correlates flood-frequency shifts and Medieval Climate Anomaly ending with rise/decline dynamics.

Former Cahokia-area populations are often linked in secondary literature to Siouan-speaking groups who later fragmented; some historic Upper Midwest and Plains nations are proposed as partial descendants — treat as interpretive, not proven one-to-one.

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Most intensively excavated Mississippian site; mound layout, residential density, subsistence, and elite mortuary/sacrifice are extensively documented. Population estimates and political classification remain contested.

**Debates** (do not resolve here):
- [[north-american-states-debate]]: city/state/empire vs. complex [[chiefdom]].
- Population projection methods (dense-block extrapolation vs. density-corrected thousands).
- Degree of American Bottom interpolity control and tribute.
- Vacant Quarter causes.

**Key works** (Smith’s bibliographic essay and later): George Milner, “The Late Prehistoric Cahokia Cultural System…” (*Journal of World Prehistory* 1990) — primary reference for Smith’s American Bottom coverage; Fowler, *Cahokia Atlas* (1989); FAI-270 project multi-volume reports; Bareis & Porter, *American Bottom Archaeology* (1984); BBB Motor (Emerson & Jackson); Milner, *The Cahokia Chiefdom*; Pauketat and Alt, eds., *Medieval Mississippians*.
