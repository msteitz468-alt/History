## Overview

Cahokia was by far the largest prehistoric settlement in the United States north of Mesoamerica. Located in the Mississippi River floodplain in what is now Illinois (across from modern St. Louis), it was the premier site of [[mississippian-culture]] and the largest of all Middle Mississippian centers. Cahokia rose to prominence in the eleventh century CE and was largely abandoned by around 1400 CE. When Europeans first passed through the area in the late seventeenth century, the once-densely populated Mississippi Valley in this region was all but empty.

## Geographic Distribution and Regional Variants

Cahokia occupied the "American Bottom," the rich floodplain of the Mississippi River just east of the river, encompassing approximately 10 km². The site was positioned to exploit rich bottomland farmlands and a network of oxbow lakes — natural impoundments of catfish and other fish formed when spring floods receded. Control of Mill Creek chert hoe manufacture and distribution gave Cahokia an economic reach extending to other Mississippian centers throughout the region.

## Political Organization

Cahokia was the paramount Mississippian chiefdom. Archaeologists debate whether it constituted a chiefdom or an incipient state, but scholarly consensus leans toward a complex chiefdom structured similarly to its contemporaries elsewhere in eastern North America, albeit at considerably larger scale. Peak population is estimated at 3,000–8,000 people based on representative excavation samples, though some estimates run several times higher. The site functioned as a political and ritual attractor drawing immigrants to sustain its population — like all preindustrial urban centers, death rates were too high for natural increase alone to maintain numbers.

The mound complex was enclosed by a substantial wall of stout posts defining the core precinct of elite architecture. Mound 72 yielded clear evidence of elite human sacrifice: two high-ranking individuals buried with thousands of marine shell beads, accompanied by at least twelve sacrificed adults in nearby pits, and four additional decapitated and de-handed sacrificial victims.

## Economy and Subsistence

Cahokia's wealth rested on intensive maize agriculture on the permanently fertile Mississippi bottomlands, supplemented by fishing from the oxbow lakes flanking the site. Control of Mill Creek chert hoe trade was an additional economic pillar. The hoes were in high demand by farmers throughout the Mississippian world, and Cahokia's role in their manufacture and distribution probably contributed directly to its rise as the largest Mississippian center.

Waste disposal and food supply presented significant urban management challenges without draft animals, wheeled vehicles, or sanitary infrastructure. Subterranean storage pits and above-ground granaries served residential food storage. The site depended on social and political attractions to maintain the immigration flow necessary for population stability.

## Architecture and Technology

Cahokia's mounds once numbered over 100, constructed from the ninth century through approximately 1400 CE. About half the total earthwork fill was concentrated in **Monks Mound**, the largest earthen structure north of Mesoamerica: covering approximately 7 hectares and rising approximately 30 m, it dominated the site. Monks Mound served as the central focus of the urban core, adjacent to an elongated plaza extending to the south — the venue for public ritual, political assembly, and the game of chunky.

An abandoned river channel (oxbow lake) ran east-west across the site. Most occupation was concentrated on the south side of this feature, on a natural levee of well-drained high ground. Interspersed among the mounds were densely packed residential areas of pole-and-thatch houses, socially and ritually important precincts, maize fields, and bodies of water.

A log palisade — later in Cahokia's occupational history than the earliest settlement — passed through established neighborhoods, indicating it was a reactive defensive measure rather than an original design element. The palisade enclosed the core precinct.

Surrounding the mound complex, "Woodhenge" posts (large timber circles) have been identified archaeologically; these are hypothesized to function as solar calendars marking seasonal ritual observances, though this interpretation remains debated.

## Ceremonial Life and Art

Cahokia was the generative center of the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex. Distinctive figurines and pipes in the "Cahokia style" have been found in elite burials at Caddoan Mississippian sites hundreds of kilometers distant, documenting direct long-range exchange connections. The ceremonial core of the site — Monks Mound, the grand plaza, the enclosed precinct — was the landscape setting for major public rituals. Chunky, a game played with a pill-shaped stone disk and throwing spears on a maintained flat field, was a prominent social institution.

## Decline and Legacy

Cahokia began to be abandoned within a few centuries of its founding. Increased periodic flooding of the Mississippi River is one documented factor in its abandonment (paleoclimate data from lake sediment cores shows a correlation between flood frequency shifts and Cahokia's rise and decline). Climatic deterioration beginning ~1250 CE — ending the favorable Medieval Climate Anomaly — brought drought, crop failure, and political instability. By approximately 1400 CE the site was abandoned.

Former Cahokia-area populations were primarily Siouan speakers, who subsequently fragmented and some eventually moved westward onto the Great Plains, adopting horses and mounted bison hunting. The Oto, Iowa, and Missouri nations are among their probable descendants. The historic nations of the Upper Midwest trace at least partial ancestry to the Middle Mississippian chiefdoms centered on Cahokia and the American Bottom.

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Cahokia is the most intensively excavated Mississippian site. Extensive archaeological investigation from the twentieth century onward has documented the mound layout, residential density, subsistence patterns, and evidence of elite ritual and sacrifice. Population estimates remain contested — the 3,000–8,000 figure (Milner 1998) reflects the most representative excavated samples; significantly higher estimates exist in the literature and remain debated. The nature of Cahokia's political organization (chiefdom vs. incipient state) is an active scholarly discussion.

**Dating methods**: Primarily radiocarbon; dendrochronology where applicable; ceramic typology for relative sequences. Paleoclimate proxy data (lake sediment cores, tree rings) has been integrated to correlate population dynamics and climate.

**Key scholarly works**: Fowler (1997) *The Cahokia Atlas*; Milner (1998) *The Cahokia Chiefdom*; Pauketat and Alt, eds. (2015) *Medieval Mississippians*; Munoz et al. (2015) on flood frequency and Cahokia's emergence/decline.
