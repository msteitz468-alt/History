# Charles E. Mitchell (1877–1955)

Chairman and CEO of National City Bank (forerunner of Citibank/Citigroup) and its securities affiliate National City Company during the late 1920s boom and 1929 crash. Prominent public face of aggressive commercial-bank involvement in stock promotion and margin lending. Central figure in Sorkin's narrative of the human decisions behind the bubble.

## Role in 1929

Mitchell aggressively expanded National City's securities business, defended speculative practices publicly, and was present at key emergency meetings in the days around the crash. His personal finances and the bank's aggressive posture made him a lightning rod for post-crash blame and later Senate investigations.

**Buildup (Feb–Sept 1929):** Appointed to the NY Fed board, Mitchell participated in the February 1929 meetings where the Fed attempted “moral suasion.” On 26–27 March, after a landslide of selling and spiking call rates, he issued the public pledge: “So far as this institution is concerned, we will continue to loan money on the Stock Exchange at the going rate, no matter how high that rate may go.” The statement was immediately labeled “dynamite in a sentence”; the market rallied vigorously the next day, but Senator Carter Glass accused him of slapping the Fed “in the face.”

Late September, after the headline National City–Corn Exchange merger (largest bank in the country at the time), Mitchell sailed for Europe. At the dock, with Andrew Mellon present, he told reporters: “Money is all right” and “There is nothing to worry about.”

**Crash days:** On Black Tuesday he was summoned to an emergency meeting at Bernard Baruch’s Fifth Avenue home after the close. National City participated in support buying.

**Aftermath and accountability:** Mitchell became the chief public target of the Pecora investigations. He resigned February 1933. In Senate testimony he admitted selling stock to his wife “frankly, for tax purposes.” He was arrested on tax-evasion charges (allegedly ~$858k for 1929–30), tried in June 1933, and acquitted amid courtroom tears and an ovation (“Mitchell Cleared, Weeps at Verdict”). On the stand he testified he had held positions through the panic “to sustain morale” and described the experience as “a nightmare.” Later analysts (including Huertas & Silverman) and Sorkin debate how far he was scapegoated versus emblematic of the era’s practices.

## Significance

Symbol of the 1920s fusion of commercial banking and investment banking that reformers later targeted. His story illustrates the incentives, culture, and information environment on Wall Street immediately before and during the crash.

## Historiography

Contemporary press and congressional critics cast Mitchell as a chief culprit. Later scholarship (e.g. Huertas & Silverman) has examined whether he was scapegoated. Sorkin (2025) uses new primary material (including trial transcripts and internal records) to reconstruct his motivations and actions in detail without simple villainization.
