# The Nasrid Kingdom of Granada

The **Nasrid kingdom of Granada** was the last Islamic state in Iberia — "the only Islamic
political entity of any size in Europe" before the Ottomans — surviving from the 1230s until
1492. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (David Abulafia, Ch. 17b) explains its emergence from
the wreck of [[almohads|Almohad]] al-Andalus and the secret of its long survival.

## Origins

The defeat of the Almohads at **Las Navas de Tolosa (1212)** ended their expansion and
exposed al-Andalus to "a process of fission" — a new generation of **taifa** statelets, far
weaker and more exposed to confident Christian kings than their predecessors. As Almohad power
collapsed and **Córdoba fell to Castile (1236)**, the petty lords loyal to the rival Ibn Hud
were left adrift. One of them, **Muhammad Ibn Yusuf Ibn Nasr ("Ibn al-Ahmar")**, lord of
**Arjona**, played "a delicate game" of deals with Muslim and Christian factions alike to win
**Granada (1237)**, **Almería** and **Málaga**, building the **Alhambra** as his palace in a
naturally defensible high-sierra capital.

## Survival as a Client State

In its early days Granada "could only survive as a client state of Castile-León." Muhammad I
**submitted to [[christian-kingdoms-of-spain|Ferdinand III]]**, even **aiding the Castilians in
the capture of Seville (1248)** and surrendering **Jaén (1246)** — for Ladero Quesada the
"birth certificate" of the Nasrid state, guaranteeing Castile would not press further south for
the moment. Yet L.P. Harvey stresses that the Granadans were inconstant vassals who often
ignored their status and withheld tribute; the western vocabulary of vassalage "had no real
equivalent in Arabic," and Muhammad felt his submission to the distant **Abbasid caliphs** more
keenly than the "protection money" paid to Castile.

The keys to survival, Abulafia argues, were two: the **natural defences** of the high sierra
(Granada "had natural frontiers in a way that few other Muslim states in Spain had them"), and
the **lack of outside support** that doomed Granada's neighbours — Murcia, Niebla, Minorca and
the Valencian enclaves were absorbed one by one, while occasional talk of aid from Hafsid Tunis
came to nothing against the Catalan-Aragonese naval blockade.

## Significance

Granada became the refuge of Andalusi Islam: its population swelled with Muslim refugees as
Castilian Islam went into "dramatic decline," and Muhammad I made it "a home of strict but
straightforward Maliki doctrine." Its survival for two and a half centuries — as a small,
mountainous, tribute-paying but resilient frontier emirate — is the great exception to the
thirteenth-century [[reconquista|Reconquista]], and the seedbed of the late-medieval
Granadan civilisation of the Alhambra.
