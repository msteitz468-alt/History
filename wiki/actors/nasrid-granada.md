# The Nasrid Kingdom of Granada

The **Nasrid kingdom of Granada** was the last Islamic state in Iberia — "the only Islamic
political entity of any size in Europe" before the Ottomans — surviving from the 1230s until
1492. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (David Abulafia, Ch. 17b) explains its emergence from
the wreck of [[almohads|Almohad]] al-Andalus and the secret of its long survival.

## Origins

The defeat of the Almohads at **Las Navas de Tolosa (1212)** ended their expansion and
exposed al-Andalus to "a process of fission" — a new generation of **taifa** statelets, far
weaker and more exposed to confident Christian kings than their predecessors. As Almohad power
collapsed and **Córdoba fell to Castile (1236)**, the petty lords loyal to the rival Ibn Hud
were left adrift. One of them, **Muhammad Ibn Yusuf Ibn Nasr ("Ibn al-Ahmar")**, lord of
**Arjona**, played "a delicate game" of deals with Muslim and Christian factions alike to win
**Granada (1237)**, **Almería** and **Málaga**, building the **Alhambra** as his palace in a
naturally defensible high-sierra capital.

## Survival as a Client State

In its early days Granada "could only survive as a client state of Castile-León." Muhammad I
**submitted to [[christian-kingdoms-of-spain|Ferdinand III]]**, even **aiding the Castilians in
the capture of Seville (1248)** and surrendering **Jaén (1246)** — for Ladero Quesada the
"birth certificate" of the Nasrid state, guaranteeing Castile would not press further south for
the moment. Yet L.P. Harvey stresses that the Granadans were inconstant vassals who often
ignored their status and withheld tribute; the western vocabulary of vassalage "had no real
equivalent in Arabic," and Muhammad felt his submission to the distant **Abbasid caliphs** more
keenly than the "protection money" paid to Castile.

The keys to survival, Abulafia argues, were two: the **natural defences** of the high sierra
(Granada "had natural frontiers in a way that few other Muslim states in Spain had them"), and
the **lack of outside support** that doomed Granada's neighbours — Murcia, Niebla, Minorca and
the Valencian enclaves were absorbed one by one, while occasional talk of aid from Hafsid Tunis
came to nothing against the Catalan-Aragonese naval blockade.

## Significance

Granada became the refuge of Andalusi Islam: its population swelled with Muslim refugees as
Castilian Islam went into "dramatic decline," and Muhammad I made it "a home of strict but
straightforward Maliki doctrine." Its survival for two and a half centuries — as a small,
mountainous, tribute-paying but resilient frontier emirate — is the great exception to the
thirteenth-century [[reconquista|Reconquista]], and the seedbed of the late-medieval
Granadan civilisation of the Alhambra.

## Cobb: diplomacy, Merinids, golden age, and the end

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|*The Race for Paradise*]] calls the Nasrids “the last, but in
many ways the greatest, kings of al-Andalus.” Post-Almohad fragmentation saw Christian
armies overrun remaining Muslim lands after Ibn Hud’s 1230 defeat destroyed his credibility;
Balearics, Valencia, Córdoba (1236), and Seville (Nov 1248, Fernando III) fell city by city.
For the next ~2½ centuries “Muslim Spain” was a hemmed-in toehold at Granada under the
Nasrids. Founder **Ibn al-Ahmar** secured Granada as capital from 1237; the Alhambra became
the Nasrid seat. Power was maintained **less by restless jihad than by deft alliances**
(Andalusi rivals, Hafsids, Abbasid caliph, Castile) — even sending soldiers to help Fernando
take Córdoba and Seville. 1264–66 mudejar revolt under some Granadan suzerainty was crushed
by Aragon and Castile; refugees crowded into Granada as few Muslims remained in Christian
territory by the late 1260s.

**Merinids.** After Ibn al-Ahmar’s death (1273), Merinids of Fez (replacing Almohads in
Morocco) were ideologically jihad-inclined — merit plus keeping Zanata Berber armies occupied
abroad. Nasrids balanced Merinid manpower promise against the threat of an obstreperous
neighbor. 1309 Castile–Aragon threatened the Straits (Gibraltar taken; Almería besieged);
Nasr made peace with Merinids, who pressured Aragon to withdraw; Ismaʿil deposed Nasr
(1314); 1319 Granada routed Castilians outside the city, killing both regents — stunning
victory without Merinid help. 1340: Muslim navy (Moroccan/Tunisian) defeated a
Castilian–Catalan fleet, but on land Alfonso XI and Portugal crushed the joint Nasrid–Merinid
army at **Rio Salado**; Merinid withdrawal from al-Andalus was permanent thereafter.
Algeciras fell 1344 after international holy-war assault (England, France, Spain) — first
recorded cannon use on the peninsula by Muslim defenders; Gibraltar saved only when Alfonso
died of plague (1350) while besieging it.

**Alone yet durable.** By mid-14th c. Granada was alone facing Christendom-backed foes yet
stood another century and a half: Christian internal divisions plus capable rulers who made
and kept peace with Castile. Golden age under **Muhammad V** (1354–59, 1362–91) —
administration, patronage, Alhambra embellishment.

**Fall (summary).** Last Muslim victory in the peninsula: late Dec 1481 Nasrid capture of
Zahara (~100 garrison prisoners reported). Kingdom fell when internal division met reunited
militant Christian monarchs: Fernando V of Aragon and Isabella of Castile planned protracted
war from 1479. July 1482 Abu al-Hasan ousted by son Abu ʿAbd Allah (**Boabdil**), splitting
the realm; 1483 Boabdil captured raiding Castile and extracted humiliating concessions; uncle
Muhammad al-Zaghal held Malaga then surrendered Almería and Guadix (end 1489) and sailed to
Algeria. Vizier surrendered Granada at Santa Fe **25 Nov 1491**; months later Boabdil
formally relinquished the Alhambra. Ferdinand broke the capitulations, forced conversions →
mudejar revolts and emigrations to North Africa and the [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman Empire]];
some Andalusis at Salé became maritime jihadis. Cobb treats 1492 as the **end-point of his
Islamic history of the Crusades**: Sicily and al-Andalus under Franks; Near Eastern Frankish
states and Cyprus destroyed; Byzantium eliminated; only Ottomans (and Barbary corsairs) still
much cared about Franks — now as a European regional power, not primarily as foes of
crusading Franks.
