# Christian Nubia (Nobatia, Makuria, Alodia)

For nearly a thousand years — from the sixth century to about 1500 — the **middle Nile** (modern Sudan) was
ruled by **Christian Nubian kingdoms**, the long medieval successor to [[kush-meroe|Kush/Meroë]] and a
major African Christian civilization almost entirely absent from popular history. Documented for the wiki
through the **UNESCO [[gha-vol2-ancient-civilizations|General History of Africa, Vol. II]]** (chs. 8–12).

## From Meroë to the "Group X" interlude

After Meroë's fall (c. 350 CE), Lower Nubia entered the archaeologically named **"Group X" / Ballana
culture** — petty kings buried under great tumuli at **Ballana and Qustul** with diadems, silverware and
caparisoned horses, still using Meroitic pottery (cultural continuity across the millennia). Their peoples
were the **Nobades** (Nubians) and the nomadic **Blemmyes** (Bedja), whom Diocletian had settled as a
buffer for Egypt's southern frontier and who long kept visiting the temple of **Isis at Philae** — until
Justinian's general Narses closed it (c. 535–537).

## Conversion and the three kingdoms

The **evangelization of Nubia** began at once: per John of Ephesus, the **Monophysite missionary Julian**,
backed by the empress **Theodora**, **converted the king of the Nobades in 543** — outpacing the
emperor's rival Melkite envoys, and tying Nubia to the **Coptic/Monophysite** church of Egypt rather than
to Byzantine Orthodoxy. The Nobatian king **Silko** boasts in a Kalabsha inscription of crushing the
Blemmyes. Three Christian kingdoms emerged along the river:

- **Nobatia** in the north (capital **Faras/Pachoras**, famous for its cathedral and painted murals);
- **Makuria** in the centre (capital **Old Dongola**), which absorbed Nobatia and became the dominant power;
- **Alodia / Alwa** in the south (capital **Soba**, near modern Khartoum).

## Survival beside Islam: the Baqt

When Arab armies conquered Egypt and pushed south, they failed to subdue Makuria; instead the parties
struck the **Baqt (652)**, a remarkable centuries-long treaty regulating trade and an exchange of goods
(including enslaved people) that let Christian Nubia **coexist with Muslim Egypt** for some 600 years — one
of the longest-lasting accords in medieval diplomacy. Nubian Christianity, literate in **Greek, Coptic and
Old Nubian**, sustained cathedrals, monasteries and bishoprics throughout.

## Decline and Islamization

From the late thirteenth century, **Mamluk** intervention, Arab nomadic immigration, and internal weakness
eroded the kingdoms: **Dongola** fell in the fourteenth century (its throne hall became a mosque), and the
southern kingdom of **Alodia/Soba** finally collapsed around **1504** before the rising **Funj** sultanate
— completing the Islamization of the Sudan.

## Significance

Christian Nubia is the **missing millennium** of middle-Nile history: proof of a durable, literate African
Christian state-civilization bridging ancient [[kush-meroe|Meroë]] and the modern Islamic Sudan, and a case
of long Christian–Muslim coexistence. Its recovery (above all the salvage archaeology of **Faras** ahead of
the Aswan High Dam) is a signal achievement of the GHA generation of scholarship.
