# Odysseus

**Odysseus** (Latin Ulysses) is the central hero of the *Odyssey* and a major figure in the *Iliad*. King of rocky Ithaca, son of Laertes, husband of Penelope, father of Telemachus. Renowned for his **mêtis** (cunning intelligence, craftiness, ability to endure and deceive) rather than raw strength like Achilles. The "man of many turns" (polytropos) who sees the cities of many men and suffers greatly at sea while trying to bring his companions home.

In the *Iliad* he is a key Greek leader: eloquent speaker, strategist, and the man who devises the Trojan Horse. In the *Odyssey* he is the hero of survival and homecoming: ten years of war followed by ten years of wandering (Calypso's island, Phaeacians, the apologoi tales of the Cyclops, Circe, the Underworld consultation with Teiresias, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the Cattle of the Sun). He returns disguised as a beggar, tests his household, slaughters the suitors who have been consuming his estate, and is reunited with Penelope after the famous bed-token recognition.

His character embodies the tension between heroic glory (kleos) and the desire for home (oikos), between the warrior abroad and the husband/father at home. He lies brilliantly when necessary, weeps for his lost companions, and ultimately chooses the human world over immortality with Calypso.

## Historiography and Significance

The "historical" Odysseus is as elusive as the "historical" Homer. The poems fuse Late Bronze Age memories, Dark Age realities, and 8th-century Ionian concerns. Odysseus appears in other traditions (e.g., as a trickster figure). Later Greeks and Romans reinterpreted him variously as the ideal of endurance and wisdom or as a deceitful rogue.
