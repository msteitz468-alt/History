## Overview

General Nogi Maresuke (乃木希典, 1849–1912) was the Japanese military commander who led the
siege of Port Arthur during the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) and became one of the most iconic
figures of Meiji-era martial culture. He served as president of the Gakushū-in (Peers School)
from 1907 until his death. On the night of Emperor Meiji's funeral, September 13, 1912, he and
his wife Shizuko committed *junshi* — ritual suicide following one's lord — becoming the
defining cultural symbol of samurai-inflected loyalty to the imperial house.

## Military Career

Nogi's military career was marked by episodes of catastrophic loss paired with extraordinary
public adulation. During the Satsuma Rebellion (1877), he lost a regimental flag in battle —
an act of shame that, by his own account in his farewell note, he had never expiated. He
subsequently sought death in combat during both the Sino-Japanese War and the Russo-Japanese
War. At Port Arthur (1904–05), the sieges he ordered resulted in the deaths of tens of thousands
of Japanese soldiers, including his own two sons. Keene notes that Nogi "felt deeply ashamed
about the loss of His Majesty's 'children.'" Foreign governments decorated him; the Japanese
public idolized him as the hero of the Russo-Japanese War.

Emperor Meiji's relationship with Nogi was complicated. After the war, Yamagata Aritomo
recommended Nogi as chief of the general staff — the highest post a military man could aspire
to — but Meiji declined, citing concern about finding a successor at the Gakushū-in where the
emperor's grandsons studied. Keene interprets this obliquely: "Perhaps the emperor still had not
forgiven Nogi for the enormous loss of life at Port Arthur. Although Nogi was idolized by the
Japanese public as the hero of the Russo-Japanese War and foreign governments had decorated him,
he had been shunted aside to an educational post for which he had no qualifications apart from
the excellence of his character."

Nogi himself, on the day of his triumphal return to Tokyo after the Russo-Japanese War, had
asked the emperor's permission to commit seppuku to atone for the Port Arthur losses. The
emperor refused, saying: "I understand very well the feelings that make you want to apologize by
committing seppuku, but this is not the time for you to die. If you insist on killing yourself,
let it be after I have departed this world."

## The Junshi

On the night of September 13, 1912, as Emperor Meiji's funeral cortege departed the palace
for the Aoyama parade grounds, Nogi and his wife Shizuko committed suicide at their residence.
Nogi set a small desk beside a window facing the palace, covered it with white cloth, placed
above it a portrait of the late emperor and an offering of sakaki. He left a farewell poem:

> *utsushiyo wo / kamisarimashishi / ōkami no / mi ato shitaite / ware wa yuku nari*
> ("In longing for / The great god who, as a god, / Has departed from / This transient world
> of ours, / I shall follow his traces.")

Nogi slit his abdomen with his military sword and then stabbed his throat. His wife stabbed her
heart with a dagger. News reached the funeral hall and "everyone was stunned by his display of
nobility and unswerving loyalty."

In his farewell note, Nogi explained that the loss of the regimental flag in 1877 had made him
court danger ever since, hoping to die in battle as amends; that the Russo-Japanese War had again
denied him death; and that "the depth of the emperor's solicitude" in appointing him to the
Gakushū-in had made him regret he had "so little time left to repay this kindness."

## Reception and Criticism

Nogi's suicide provoked immediate and polarized reactions. Some newspapers initially criticized
him for abandoning his duties (receiving Prince Arthur of Connaught; serving the new emperor).
Within days the tone shifted: by September 16, journalist Kuroiwa Ruikō called him "a god,"
and the *Tokyo Nichinichi Shimbun* compared him to Kusunoki Masashige. Thereafter, Nogi became
"the incarnation of loyalty to the emperor, a legendary hero whom it was impossible to criticize."

Critics were numerous and sharp. Katō Hiroyuki (the last member of the Meirokusha enlightenment
circle) called the suicide an anachronism and asked why Nogi had not offered his loyalty to the
new emperor instead. Mushakōji Saneatsu of the Shirakaba school called it "an act that could be
praised only by the warped intelligence of men who have been nurtured on thought shaped by a
warped age through a misuse of nature," contrasting it unfavorably with Van Gogh's suicide.
Shiga Naoya's diary for September 14 recorded simply: "What an idiot!"

The military establishment attempted to suppress Nogi's stated motivation, attributing the
suicide to mental derangement — fearing other officers might emulate him.

The writer Mori Ōgai was moved to write *Okitsu Yagoemon no isho* (The Last Testament of
Okitsu Yagoemon) within four days, followed by *Abe no Ichizoku*, exploring increasingly
ambivalent attitudes toward *junshi* as samurai tradition. Natsume Sōseki's novel *Kokoro*
(1914) drew directly on the cultural shock of Nogi's death.

## Role and Historical Significance

Nogi crystallized in his death the central tension of late Meiji culture: between a modernizing
state that had formally prohibited *junshi* since 1782 (under the Laws for the Military Houses)
and a military ideology that glorified precisely this kind of self-sacrifice. His suicide was
simultaneously illegal, archaic, and — to many contemporaries — moving. It marked the moment at
which Meiji-era martial culture began to be canonized as myth rather than lived practice.

**Counterfactual significance:** Had Nogi lived, he would have continued as president of
Gakushū-in and provided guidance to Emperor Taishō's three sons (the future Emperor Shōwa
among them). Emperor Meiji had specifically chosen Nogi for this role because of "the excellence
of his character." His death deprived the next generation of imperial princes of that influence —
a loss the court explicitly mourned.
