## Role and Background

Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban served [[actors/louis-xiv|Louis XIV]] as Commissioner General of Fortifications and, from 1703, Marshal of France, becoming the era's preeminent military engineer. He designed or upgraded well over a hundred fortresses, most famously the chain of star-shaped forts along France's northern and eastern frontiers (the *pré carré*), using geometric bastion systems designed to eliminate dead ground and maximize interlocking defensive fire.

## Method and Influence

Vauban systematized siege warfare as much as fortification, developing the method of parallel trenches (successive lines of approach connected by zigzag saps) that reduced attacker casualties and made siege outcomes far more predictable — a technique that remained standard European siege practice for over a century. He also wrote extensively on taxation, economics, and social policy, including a proposal for a single unified tax that anticipated later fiscal reform debates.

## Significance

Vauban's fortification and siege systems defined the "war of positions" that characterized much of late seventeenth- and eighteenth-century European warfare, in which campaigns often turned on sieges rather than field battles; his work exemplifies the professionalization and engineering rigor of the [[concepts/standing-army|standing-army]] era of French military administration under Louis XIV.
