# Liu Bang (Emperor Gaozu of Han) (c. 256–195 BCE; r. 202–195 BCE)

The **commoner founder of the [[han-dynasty|Han dynasty]]** — a minor [[qin-dynasty|Qin]]
official who rose through the anti-Qin rebellions to defeat the aristocratic warlord **Xiang Yu**
and establish the dynasty that fixed the enduring shape of imperial China. From
[[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook (2017)]].

## From Bailiff to Emperor

Liu Bang held a **minor Qin administrative post**, roughly a bailiff — a man of no elite military
pedigree, in pointed contrast to his rival **Xiang Yu**, scion of the Chu aristocracy. He emerged
in the rebellions that followed [[qin-shi-huang|the First Emperor's]] death (the
[[qin-unification-221-bce|Qin]] collapse from 209 BCE), and in **206 BCE** took the title **king
of Han** — the date conventionally taken as the dynasty's foundation, though four more years of
civil war followed. At the **Battle of Gaixia (202 BCE)** Xiang Yu, broken out of encirclement,
committed suicide, leaving Liu Bang undisputed ruler with the title **Han Gaodi, "High Emperor of
Han."**

## The Commoner's Settlement

Major & Cook stress that Liu Bang **won the empire as the head of a tenuous coalition**, not as
sole commander of a unified army — which dictated his central compromise. He kept the western
"area within the passes" under direct imperial rule but parcelled the fertile, populous **east into
semi-independent kingdoms** rewarded to senior allies. This **commandery-kingdom hybrid** built the
structural instability that would erupt in the [[han-dynasty|Rebellion of the Seven Kings]] (154
BCE) into the dynasty's foundations. He then methodically **eliminated the non-Liu kings**: by 195
BCE all but one had been ousted and replaced by **Liu-clan members**, and an imperial edict
restricted territorial kingship to the family.

His populism was partly theatre: his wartime promises to **soften the hated Qin legal code** were
"simply propaganda" — the **Qin code was retained almost unchanged** — though he did cut taxes and
proclaim a general amnesty. The early Han even claimed to rule by **Phase Water (black)** with the
[[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate]] passing directly from [[zhou-dynasty|Zhou]] to Han, **writing Qin out
of the legitimate succession** (a stance later reversed under [[han-wudi|Emperor Wu]] in favour of
Phase Earth/yellow).

## Death

Liu Bang was **fatally wounded by an arrow** while personally suppressing **Ying Bu's rebellion**
in 196 BCE; the wound became infected and he died in 195 BCE, having just installed a son as king of
Huainan. Power passed into a long contest of **consort clans** (beginning with his formidable widow,
Empress Lü) that would shape the Former Han.

## Counterfactual Significance

Liu Bang is the archetype of the **dynastic founder of humble origin** — proof, against the older
aristocratic order the [[zhou-dynasty|Zhou]] and Xiang Yu represented, that the
[[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate]] could fall to a commoner. By keeping the **Qin bureaucratic machine**
while clothing it in conciliation and (eventually) [[confucianism|Confucian]] legitimacy, he
authored the durable **Confucian-in-appearance, Legalist-in-substance** imperial state. Had Xiang Yu
prevailed, the likely outcome was a restored aristocratic multi-state order rather than a centralized
empire.

## Historiography

Liu Bang is known chiefly through [[sima-qian|Sima Qian's]] *Shiji* and Ban Gu's *Hanshu* —
sympathetic to the founder and hostile to Xiang Yu and to Qin, the contrast that anchors the
moralized **Han narrative of legitimate succession**. The image of the rough, shrewd, hard-drinking
commoner-emperor is partly a literary construction serving that narrative.
