# John Adams

John Adams (1735–1826) was a Massachusetts lawyer, revolutionary leader, diplomat, first vice president, and second president of the United States. A central architect of American independence, he was the “Colossus of Independence” in the Continental Congress, a key negotiator of the Treaty of Paris (1783), and the president who avoided full-scale war with France in 1800. With his wife Abigail Adams he formed one of the most remarkable partnerships in American history.

## Life and Career

Born October 30, 1735 (O.S.) in Braintree (later Quincy), Massachusetts, to a modest farming family. Harvard graduate (1755); taught school in Worcester while studying law. Admitted to bar 1758. Early character: ambitious to excel yet developed “habitual contempt” for fame/fortune; honest, independent, voracious reader (classics, Shakespeare, Swift); devout Christian and independent thinker. <!-- Range 01 --> Father Deacon John was his lifelong idol—“the honestest man” he ever knew; inspired by father’s honesty, independent spirit, and love of country. The first fifteen years of life “went off like a fairytale” roaming Braintree fields and marshes. Marriage to Abigail Smith (1764) the most important decision of his life; she his intellectual equal and “Dearest Friend,” the vital center of his world. Defended British soldiers after Boston Massacre (1770) on principle of fair trial for all despite public fury. Gained prominence defending British soldiers after Boston Massacre (1770) on principle of law applying to all. Through essays and Braintree Instructions (1765), became leading voice for American rights. In Continental Congress 1774–1776 emerged as “Colossus of Independence” and “Atlas” of the hour: forceful July 1, 1776 speech; drove July 2 vote declaring independence; Committee of Five (defended Jefferson draft wording); authored *Thoughts on Government* (bicameral legislatures, education, independent judiciary). Led Board of War (regular army preference, bounties, Articles of War). Between missions (1779) drafted the Massachusetts Constitution (as sub-subcommittee of one): Preamble/social compact (“The end of the institution... of government is to secure the existence of the body politic... the individuals who compose it with the power of enjoying... their natural rights”); Declaration of Rights (“born equally free and independent”, jury, conscience); separation of powers with strong elected executive/veto and life-tenure “good behavior” judiciary; education clause requiring diffusion of “wisdom and knowledge, as well as virtue” via schools and university and inculcation of benevolence, industry, frugality, “social affections, and generous sentiments.” One of the oldest functioning written constitutions. Re-appointed minister plenipotentiary for peace and commerce (1779); second voyage (Sensible leaks, Spain El Ferrol Dec 1779, mule overland to Paris Feb 1780); early Paris challenges with Vergennes (secrecy demands, “astonished and deeply insulted”; currency dispute); moved to Holland for “militia diplomacy” (recognition April 1782, 5M guilder loan, commerce treaty); returned for Paris peace talks (harmony with Franklin/Jay); preliminary treaty Nov 30 1782 (fisheries: “If Heaven in the Creation have a right, it is ours at least as much as yours”); definitive Sept 3 1783 (“acknowledges the said United States... to be free, sovereign and independent states”). Abigail/Nabby joined Europe 1784.

Diplomatic service 1778–1788: France (with Franklin), Netherlands (critical loans after brutal North Sea crossing), Treaty of Paris 1783. First U.S. minister to Court of St. James’s (1785 audience with George III: speech on epoch and friendship; King responded he was last to consent to separation but first to friendship). Vice president under Washington (1789–1797): marginal role (“most insignificant office ever contrived”); titles controversy (“His Rotundity”); 31 tie-breaking votes (always supporting administration); happy private life contrasting public isolation. 1796 election: narrow 71–68 win over Jefferson despite Hamilton’s scheme for Pinckney and smears as monarchist.

Presidency (1797–1801): retained Washington’s cabinet (High Federalist-leaning Pickering “tall, lean... severe... utterly humorless,” Wolcott, McHenry “trace of an Irish accent,” Lee; “Washington had appointed them”); understated inauguration (grey broadcloth, two-horse carriage; “republican President in earnest”); May 1797 address (“olive branch... emblems of defense”; “Great is the guilt of an unnecessary war”); bipartisan peace commission (Gerry, Marshall, Pinckney); XYZ Affair (Talleyrand agents X/Y/Z demanded $250k douceur + $10M loan; Pinckney “No! No! Not a sixpence”); dispatches release April 1798 sparked patriotic surge (“Hail Columbia,” navy bills, 10k provisional army); Alien & Sedition Acts (signed as war measures; Abigail: “unfounded calumny”; Adams never invoked Alien deportations); Quasi-War naval ops + Toussaint L’Ouverture diplomacy (first Black dinner guest at President’s House); Oct 1798 Gerry at Quincy: “The French wanted peace” (“more positive... than prior”); Feb 18, 1799 Murray nomination despite cabinet revolt (“bravest act”); Hamilton “mad” army schemes (Florida/Louisiana); cabinet purge (McHenry/Pickering May 1800: “Hamilton is an intriguant... devoid of every moral principle—a bastard”); 1800 loss (Jefferson 73, Adams 65). Left President’s House 4 a.m. March 4, 1801. <!-- Range 04 -->

Health decline (nearly blind, deaf, rheumatic, no horse) but mental vigor and love of life intact. “The last fourteen years have been the happiest of my life.”

Died July 4, 1826, Quincy ~6:20 p.m. (“Thomas Jefferson survives”; Jefferson died ~1 p.m. same day at Monticello; "It is a great day. ## Key Relationships

- **Abigail Adams**: His “Dearest Friend.” Political partner, farm manager, and emotional anchor during years of separation. Their letters are among the greatest in American literature.
- **Samuel Adams** (second cousin): See **Schiff 2022 — cousin and Massachusetts politics** below. John swore Samuel was “born to sever the cord between Great Britain and America” and that “without the character of Samuel Adams… the true history of the American Revolution can never be written.”
- **Thomas Jefferson**: Ally in 1776, rival in the 1790s, reconciled correspondent in old age.
- **George Washington**: Colleague and president; Adams served loyally as vice president despite frustrations.
- **Alexander Hamilton**: Bitter political opponent within the Federalist Party.
- **Benjamin Franklin**: Fellow diplomat in France; complex personal and professional relationship.
- **John Hancock** / **James Otis Jr.**: By 1817 John had settled on Hancock, Otis, and Samuel Adams as the Massachusetts “founding triumvirate” (Schiff 2022).

## Significance and Counterfactual

Adams insisted that the true American Revolution was the change in “the minds and hearts of the people” before the fighting began. He helped create the intellectual and institutional foundations of the republic: commitment to independence, balanced government, civilian control of the military, and diplomacy over unnecessary war. His decision to pursue peace with France in 1800, at great political cost, preserved the young nation’s resources and independence.

Without Adams the drive for independence in 1776 might have been slower or more divided; the diplomatic achievements of 1783 less certain; the precedent of peaceful transfer of power in 1801 weaker. His voluminous writings remain essential primary sources for understanding the founding era.

## Schiff 2022 — cousin and Massachusetts politics

*Edit-append from [[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Stacy Schiff, *The Revolutionary: Samuel Adams* (2022)]]. Complements McCullough; does not replace it. Full Samuel life on [[adams-samuel]] and the detailed bio hub.*

**Formation and cousinship.** Thirteen years younger and a country relation, John was “little acquainted until adulthood with his more worldly cousin.” He found Boston’s “rattle gabble” bewildering. Relying on others’ memories he later insisted the Land Bank caused greater ferment than the Stamp Act. He claimed Harvard—and Samuel’s town meetings—“had set the universe in motion,” and reported that Samuel looked “much like William the Conqueror” (if so, Schiff notes, every extant portrait is inaccurate). On Monday Night Club Samuel: “always for softness, and delicacy, and prudence where they will do, but is staunch and stiff and strict and rigid and inflexible in the cause.”

**Into the engine (1765–70).** At Samuel’s urging John moved Braintree→Boston (1768) and declined dangled government posts that would be “the first step in the ladder of royal favor,” unwilling to put duty and inclination “so much at variance.” He watched Samuel at the *Gazette* “cooking up paragraphs, articles, occurrences, etc.—working the political engine!” while Otis clogged it with hour-long anecdotes. 1765 was “the most remarkable year of my life”; the people were “more attentive to their liberties… than they were ever before.” On 5 March 1770 he was among Monday Night Club members streaming toward King Street thinking fire, then learned soldiers had fired on civilians. He deemed Samuel’s showdown with [[hutchinson-thomas|Hutchinson]] over the troops “worthy of Livy or Thucydides.”

**Massacre defense (already central on this page).** Schiff supplies Hutchinson’s capsule on John: “vain, and of overvaulting ambition,” nearly bought off; “as zealous as and more talented than his cousin, but lacked Samuel’s stamina.” In court John reduced the crowd to a “motley rabble of saucy boys, Negroes and mulattos, Irish teagues and outlandish Jack Tars”; painted Attucks as formidable and largely responsible; argued homicide could be justifiable after assault by a riotous mob. Samuel likely knew of the defense (Quincy Jr. conferred with Samuel first); Samuel helped select prosecution and withheld the *Short Narrative* from local circulation while aiming its pages at London. John applauded cousin’s March 5 memorials as engines that brought young gentlemen to notice—and later proposed that July 2 “ought to be solemnized with pomp and parade… from one end of this continent to the other.”

**Paper, secrecy, Congress.** In 1770s Philadelphia John watched Samuel feed whole handfuls of papers to the fire; asked if he was overreacting; Samuel: “Whatever becomes of me, my friends shall never suffer by my negligence.” Before First Congress John brooded “We have not men fit for the times… deficient in genius, in education, in travel, in fortune”; by August 1776 he had revised—“In solid abilities and real virtues” New Englanders exceeded the continent—and planted his Rubicon at Lexington and Concord. He proposed Washington as commander; Samuel seconded (mortifying Hancock). He needled Samuel into finally mounting a horse near Worcester, 2 September 1775 (flannel “biking shorts” from a Connecticut tailor). In France he was first mistaken for the famous cousin, then, “it being settled that he was not the famous Adams,” treated as “a man of no consequence—a cipher.”

**Later judgment.** 1789 Boston: overheard as one of “three genuine pivots of the Revolution” with Washington and Samuel. On Samuel as governor: “He always had a contracted mind—though a subtle and a bold one”; Abigail: “I respect his virtues, though I pity his weakness.” 1817: would not attempt even the outlines of Samuel’s biography—“Who can attempt it?” 1776 footnote on the dread of the word: “Independency is a hobgoblin of so frightful mien that it would throw a delicate person into fits to look it in the face.”
