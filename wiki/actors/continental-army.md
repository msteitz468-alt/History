# The Continental Army (1775–1783)

The standing land force of the United States during the **[[american-revolution|Revolution]]**,
created by the Continental Congress on **14 June 1775** and placed under
[[washington-george|George Washington]] the next day. Its founding overcame deep republican
suspicion of standing armies — many delegates "believed [they] both enabled and practically
required governments to impinge on liberty." The army was the institutional ancestor of the
United States Army.

## Organization

The basic unit was the **regiment** (8–10 companies, 600–800 men at full strength); brigades
held 3–6 regiments, divisions 2–4 brigades. Infantry were recruited by colony/state; cavalry
and artillery regiments existed but played limited battlefield roles, and the army "lacked
trained engineers," depending on foreign and French expertise for siegecraft. The standard
weapon was a smoothbore flintlock musket like the British Brown Bess — though early on "many
lacked bayonets," a grave handicap in close combat. A few units carried the accurate but
slow-firing long rifle, unsuited to the battle line but devastating in skirmish (see
[[morgan-daniel|Morgan]]'s riflemen at [[battles-of-saratoga-1777|Saratoga]]).

## The structural weakness: short enlistments and supply

The army's near-fatal flaw was **annual enlistment**: through 1775–76 most terms expired at
year's end, forcing Washington to "disband the old army and build a new one from scratch" in
the middle of a war; only in 1777 were enlistments fixed at three years or the duration.
Administrative departments — quartermaster, commissary, hospital — existed largely on paper and
bred "incompetence and corruption." These ad-hoc systems "collapsed under the strain of
protracted war," culminating in the [[valley-forge-1777|Valley Forge]] winter, where over
2,000 soldiers died of disease and privation out of ~12,000. This chronic fiscal-military
weakness — Congress could not tax — is the through-line from the army's sufferings to the
push for the federal **Constitution** of 1787 (see [[fiscal-military-state]]).

## Professionalization

At Valley Forge the army was remade. [[greene-nathanael|Greene]] overhauled the quartermaster
department, and the Prussian [[steuben-friedrich|Friedrich von Steuben]], as inspector general,
drilled it in "the principles of drill, discipline, and maneuver," codified in his "Blue Book"
*Regulations*. The result showed at [[battle-of-monmouth-1778|Monmouth]] (June 1778), where for
the first time the Continentals "could fight British regulars toe-to-toe and stand their
ground." By 1781 the army was a credible European-style force — though at
[[siege-of-yorktown-1781|Yorktown]] "the majority of the regular soldiers in the combined army
that trapped Cornwallis were French." Conway's verdict: "The Continental Army did not win the
war in America; it was on the winning side" — its achievement was to **survive** until foreign
intervention tipped the balance.

After the war the army was almost entirely disbanded (briefly just 80 men, mostly artillerists
at [[west-point|West Point]]); the First American Regiment (1784) became the seed of the
permanent U.S. Army.
