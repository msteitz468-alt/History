# The Huns

The **steppe-nomad people** whose intrusion into eastern Europe from c. 370 "completely overturned the
established political order beyond the Danube" — setting off the chain of displacements (the **Gothic
crisis**, [[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople]], the Rhine crossing of 406) that reshaped the late-Roman
West. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 16 (**Peter Heather**), whose account substantially
**revises the traditional picture**.

## Origins and society

Ammianus placed their origin in the far north near the "ice-bound ocean"; scholars debate whether they were
**Finno-Ugrian** (like the later Magyars) or the first **Turkic** nomads to reach Europe. They were
**steppe nomads** — practising a specialized animal husbandry that demanded movement between defined
pastures — organized in **small, largely autonomous units under many chiefs**, with **no overall ruler** in
the 370s (Ammianus) and only a loose "internal ranking" among the *reges* Olympiodorus met in 412. Their
sudden westward expansion was a **deliberate decision** with unknown cause (a guess: population growth, a
decline in steppe productivity in the documented fourth-century "warm period," or socio-political
revolution — as in the later Arab conquests).

## The revisionist chronology

Heather's central correction: the Huns did **not arrive *en masse* on the Danube in 376**. Rather they
exerted **slow, often indirect pressure** over a decade or more:

- They first subdued the **Alans** (Iranian nomads) east of the Don, then the **Goths**, after the deaths
  of the Greuthungi kings Ermenaric and Vithimer.
- The Gothic arrival of 376 was a **"domino effect"** — chaos generated *in front of* the Huns by displaced
  groups and small raiding parties — not a solid wall of Huns reaching the river. The **Tervingi** (under
  Alavivus and Fritigern) and a body of **Greuthungi** sought asylum across the Danube; the Goths could
  wait weeks beside the river while embassies travelled to [[valentinian-and-valens|Valens]] at Antioch,
  unharassed.
- Many Huns were still **far to the east** — their destructive raid of **395 came through the Caucasus**
  into Asia Minor and Persia, not across the Danube. **Independent Gothic kingdoms survived** north of the
  Danube into the 400s (Odotheus 386, Radagaisus 405).
- A **second convulsion (405–407)** — Radagaisus' invasion of Italy and the **Rhine crossing of Vandals,
  Alans and Sueves (31 Dec 406)** — reflects a renewed outpouring from *west* of the Carpathians, again
  plausibly driven by Hunnic pressure. By **c. 410** the Huns themselves were established on the **middle
  Danube** (the Great Hungarian Plain), the future heartland of Attila's empire.

## Transformation into an empire

The move west and the building of an empire **transformed** Hunnic society: the decentralized chiefs of
Olympiodorus gave way, by Attila's day, to a **much more authoritarian system**; the economy shifted from
herding toward **war and the exploitation of subject peoples' agricultural surpluses**, with horse-breeding
(the animal of war) favoured over the old flocks. From c. 400 successive Hunnic bands were **employed by
both halves of the Roman state** — by [[stilicho|Stilicho]] (Uldin), then Constantius and **Aetius** — above
all to control the very Germanic invaders the Huns had themselves displaced. By the 430s Hunnic power had
grown enough to make war independently against Rome; Attila's empire (the 440s–450s, beyond this volume)
was built on the subjection of many peoples, including separate groups of Goths.

## Significance

The Huns are the **prime mover of the late-fourth/early-fifth-century transformation**: without their slow
pressure on the steppe, the Goths would not have come to the Danube, there would have been no
[[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople]] and no Gothic settlement inside the empire, and the Rhine frontier
might not have given way in 406. Heather's revision matters because it makes the western crisis a
**protracted process** of Hunnic-driven displacement rather than a single barbarian flood — and because it
shows the Huns, for a generation, as **Rome's indispensable mercenaries** even as they reshaped the world
beyond the frontier. Their own empire, and its collapse, belong to [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]].

## Political organization (Kim 2017)

Hyun Jin Kim (in Kim et al. 2017) argues the Hunnic political system directly replicated the earlier [[xiongnu|Xiongnu]] model rather than being an ad hoc "primitive" formation as Ammianus Marcellinus's ethnography implies — he treats Ammianus's account as pseudo-ethnography drawing on stock Greco-Roman literary tropes about steppe nomads, not eyewitness reporting. On Kim's reading the Huns retained a supreme ruler with subordinate sub-kings (*reguli*) bound by oath — e.g. Uldin was a *regulus*, and the sub-king Donatus was vassal to the overlord Charaton, "the first of the kings" (Olympiodorus fr. 19) — and a dualistic East/West kingship (Ruga and his brother Oktar ruled jointly; their nephews Bleda and Attila succeeded them; Attila assigned the East to his son Ellac; Dengizich and Ernakh again split West/East after 453). A tier of high officials called *logades* (Priscus's Onegesius, Edeco, Berik), functionally comparable to the Xiongnu Gudu marquesses, served as envoys and commanders over vassal tribes, with seating at Attila's banquets reflecting rank. The Huns also collected regular tribute in agricultural produce from subject populations — attested by the Hun officer Chelchal's account to Gothic troops during Dengizich's 460s invasion — continuing a Xiongnu-derived fiscal pattern.

Kim's central and more far-reaching thesis — presented as his own interpretive argument, not settled consensus — is that this Xiongnu-derived "quasi-feudal" political model (dual kingship, dynastic fief-allocation to royal kin, oath-bound vassalage, marquess-equivalent officials) was transmitted via Hunnic military involvement in the rise of Childeric and Clovis to the nascent [[merovingian-franks|Merovingian Frankish kingdom]], shaping what became European feudalism, and that the Hunnic Empire's own fragmentation after 453 followed the same Xiongnu pattern of an unresolved succession dispute over territorial "fiefs." See [[steppe-empire-political-organization]] for the full argument and its relation to other steppe empires.
