## Overview

Motoda Nagazane (1818–1891) was the emperor's chief Confucian tutor (*jidoku*) from 1871 until his death in 1891, and by many contemporary accounts the most influential person on the emperor's moral character. Soejima Taneomi stated: "The man who deserves the greatest credit for the magnitude of His Majesty's moral virtue was Motoda sensei. I would have no choice but to give his name as the subject who contributed most to the Meiji era." Ōkubo Toshimichi, who "rarely had a good word for anyone," said: "As long as this man is by His Majesty's side, my mind is at ease." Motoda is today largely forgotten.

## Background and Career

Born in Kumamoto of middle-level samurai status, Motoda determined at fifteen to study Confucian texts and make himself useful to the state. He became associated with Yokoi Shōnan, the reformist Confucian scholar later assassinated for his apparent sympathy toward Western learning. Motoda's early political position, shaped by Yokoi's influence, was liberal for the era: he favored "respect the emperor and open the country" (*sonnō kaikoku*) rather than the more prevalent *sonnō jōi* (expel the barbarians).

By 1858 Motoda was an adviser to the daimyo of Kumamoto. His reputation grew when the domain ignored his advice against participation in the second Chōshū war and suffered severe losses — outcomes he had predicted. After the Restoration, he served the Kumamoto governor in Tokyo and was appointed the governor's tutor in 1871. Ōkubo Toshimichi, looking for an imperial tutor, was impressed by a memorial Motoda had drafted (submitted under the governor's name), which proposed two things: that legislation be discussed in the emperor's presence so he could decide it personally, and that hereditary officeholding be abolished. Ōkubo arranged Motoda's appointment as the emperor's chief tutor. Motoda delivered his first lecture before the emperor on July 21, 1871, on the *Analects*.

## Intellectual Position

Motoda's philosophy of *jitsugaku* (practical learning) traced to Chu Hsi (1130–1200) and stressed that Confucian study was valuable not as an abstract contemplation of virtue but as preparation for service to the state — "the unity of thought and action." He admitted the value of Western science and technology, urging Japanese to study them in the spirit of *kakubutsu* ("investigation of things" from the *Great Learning*). But he held that "with respect to human relations, the West had nothing to offer, that guidance could be found only in the Six Classics." He was especially concerned about what he called "an indiscriminate mania for Western culture, an unspeakable perversion of learning."

This position — Eastern ethics as the moral framework, Western science as the practical toolkit — is identified by Keene as characteristic of Emperor Meiji's own mature worldview, directly attributable to Motoda's instruction.

## Influence on the Emperor

Motoda lectured before the emperor twelve times per month throughout his tenure, covering the *Analects*, *Mencius*, and *Nihon gaishi* (a Japanese history in classical Chinese). His influence expressed itself in the emperor's consistent attendance at cabinet and council sessions even when he said nothing — Motoda had urged that legislation be discussed in the emperor's presence — and in the emperor's personal frugality and distaste for ostentation. When the imperial palace burned in 1873, the emperor declined to rebuild it, declaring: "It must not happen that for the sake of Our dwelling, public finances sustain losses and the people are made to suffer."

Motoda's most lasting institutional legacy was his major role in drafting the Imperial Rescript on Education (1890), which codified Confucian ethical ideals and the principle of *chūkun aikoku* (loyalty to the sovereign and love of country) as the moral foundation of the national school curriculum.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Motoda, the emperor's education would have been less coherently grounded in classical Confucian ethics. The specific synthesis of Confucian loyalty and Western practical knowledge that characterized Meiji's approach to governance — and that the Rescript on Education institutionalized — might have been less distinctly conservative in its moral content. The balance between Westernizing reformers like Itō Hirobumi and Mori Arinori on one side and the Confucian-Shintō conservative synthesis on the other might have tipped further toward the Western pole without Motoda's persistent presence at the emperor's side.
