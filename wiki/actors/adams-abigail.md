# Abigail Adams

Abigail Adams (1744–1818), née Smith, was the wife of John Adams and his indispensable intellectual, emotional, and political partner—“Dearest Friend.” Through their more than 1,000 surviving letters (only about half previously published at the time of McCullough’s work), she left one of the greatest bodies of personal correspondence in American history, providing unmatched candid detail on the Revolutionary and early national era.

She managed the Braintree/Quincy farm and household during John’s long absences (1774–1783 Europe; vice presidency and presidency), raised their children (Nabby, John Quincy, Charles, Thomas), handled shortages, refugees, epidemics, and finances with remarkable competence and cheer. In 1776 she famously urged John to “remember the ladies” in any new code of laws and not put “unlimited power into the hands of the husbands,” warning that women would “foment a rebellion” if denied voice and representation. She also noted the contradiction of fighting for liberty while depriving others (slaves).

In Europe (1784–1788): joined John in Paris/Auteuil (shock at French servants, dirt, prostitution, arranged marriages; growing appreciation for theater including *Marriage of Figaro* and Opera; close friendship with Adrienne de Lafayette; “I make a little America of my own”); London court as minister’s wife (detailed observations of protocol, 4-hour standing at Queen’s drawing room, “what a fool I do look like”). Homesick but resilient (“body in one place and the soul in another”).

In America during John’s vice presidency and presidency: stayed in Quincy for economy and health during second term; joined in Philadelphia (Bush Hill) and Washington (President’s House in “most deplorable condition”); handled social duties (“I am obliged every day to devote two hours for the purpose of seeing company”); provided counsel and ballast (“splendid misery”). Supported peace mission despite political cost.

Later years: mastectomy and death of Nabby (1811–1813, age 49; extraordinary fortitude); managed family crises (Charles’s alcoholism and early death); died October 28, 1818 (typhoid); Adams said he wished to lie down beside her. Her letters remained a sustaining force for John in retirement and for the family legacy.

Abigail was an avid reader (Milton, Pope, Shakespeare), strong-minded, frugal, hardworking, and shared John’s creed of duty, honor, and independence. McCullough presents the Adams marriage as one of the most remarkable partnerships in American history.
