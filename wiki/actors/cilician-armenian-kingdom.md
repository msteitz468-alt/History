# The Cilician Armenian Kingdom (Roupenid–Hetumid Armenia)

The **Cilician Armenian Kingdom** was the only Armenian state ever to sit on the
**Mediterranean** — a polity built by refugees from the lost Bagratid homeland of
[[armenia|Greater Armenia]] in the fortresses and plain of [[cilicia|Cilicia]] between the
late eleventh century and 1375. It began as a cluster of autonomous Armenian
**baronies** carved out of [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine]] territory as the empire reeled
from [[battle-of-manzikert-1071|Manzikert (1071)]] and the [[seljuk-empire|Seljuk]]
advance, matured into a **kingdom** crowned by the Latin West in 1199, and was extinguished
by the [[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]] three centuries later. Its history is, in
[[ghazarian-armenian-kingdom-cilicia-2000|Ghazarian's]] reading, inseparable from the
[[crusades|Crusades]]: the kingdom rose by **integrating with the Latins**
([[armenian-latin-integration|see the process]]) and fell, in part, when that integration
fractured Armenian society over union with Rome.

## Founding Conditions

When Byzantium absorbed the **[[armenia|Bagratid kingdom]]** — summoning King Gagik II to
Constantinople (1042) and accepting the surrender of Ani in **1045** — and the Seljuks then
sacked Ani (1064) and broke the Byzantine army at **Manzikert (1071)**, the Armenian
nobility and population drifted south and west into the imperial military zones of
Cappadocia and Cilicia, where Armenians had long served as the **backbone of the Byzantine
army** (producing emperors: Leo V, Basil I, John I Tzimiskes). In the resulting vacuum,
two rival Armenian houses established themselves:

- the **Hetumid (Hetumian) House**, west of the Cilician Gates around **Lampron** and
  **Barberon** — pro-Byzantine and often **Chalcedonian** in religion;
- the **Roupenid (Roupenian) House**, east of the Gates in the Taurus — **traditionalist**
  (non-Chalcedonian) and anti-Byzantine.

Their rivalry structured the kingdom's first century and a half until the marriage that
fused them in 1226.

## Structure and Governing Base

The kingdom was **feudal but clan-based**, inheriting the customs of the *nakharar*
nobility of Greater Armenia: great aristocratic families did not submit to royal autocracy,
and succession passed to the firstborn male — but, exceptionally, could pass through a
woman (Queen **Zabel/Isabel**, 1219). Its economic base was **transit trade**: the port of
**Ayas** linked the overland routes to Persia, India, and China to the Italian maritime
republics, and successive kings granted commercial charters to **Genoa, Venice, and Pisa**.
Under [[hetum-i-of-cilician-armenia|Hetum I]] the kingdom adopted Frankish feudal law
wholesale, translating the **Assizes of Antioch** from Latin into Armenian — one of many
markers of its Latinization. A **multilingual chancery** (Armenian, Latin, French, Arabic,
Italian) handled treaties and charters.

## The Kingdom (1198/99–1375)

In **1199** the baron Levon II was crowned **[[levon-i-of-cilician-armenia|King Levon I]]**
(see [[coronation-of-levon-i-1199|the coronation]]) by the Armenian patriarch and the
archbishop of Mainz, severing the Byzantine connection and binding the kingdom to the
Roman West — at the price of a promised **church union**. Levon "the Magnificent" forged a
unified Christian state, pursued (and briefly held) Antioch through his grandnephew
Raymond-Roupen, and on his death (1219) left only his daughter **Zabel**. Her forced
marriage to **[[hetum-i-of-cilician-armenia|Hetum I]]** (crowned 1226) united the Roupenid
and Hetumid houses and ended their rivalry.

**Hetum I (1226–69)** is the kingdom's pivotal figure: anticipating that the [[mongol-empire|Mongols]]
would reshape the Near East, he travelled in person to the Great Khan Möngke (1253) and
made Cilician Armenia the Mongols' **voluntary Christian ally** (see
[[mongol-latin-crusade-alliance|the Mongol–Latin crusade alliance]]). The alliance
delivered the sack of Baghdad (1258) and the fall of Aleppo (1260), and briefly restored
lost Cilician lands — but it earned the lasting enmity of the **Mamluks**, whose sultan
**Baybars** invaded in 1266 (killing Hetum's son Toros, capturing his son Levon).

The **Hetumid kings** (Levon II 1270–89, Hetum II 1289–1303, Toros III, Levon III
1303–07, Oshin I 1308–20, Levon IV 1320–41) presided over a long decline: the loss of the
patriarchal seat at **Hromkla** (1292), repeated Mamluk invasions and crushing tribute, and
a deepening internal split between **pro-Roman "unionists"** and **traditionalists** — a
split that produced a string of royal murders. After Levon IV's assassination (1341) the
crown passed, through marriage, to the Cypriot **[[lusignan-dynasty|Lusignan]]** house
(Constantine II–IV), whose pro-Latin policies further alienated Armenian opinion.

## Decline and Fall

The kingdom expired under the **Mamluks**: incursions from the 1320s, the annexation of
Adana and Tarsus by mid-century, and finally the siege and capture of **Sis in 1375**. The
last king, the half-Lusignan **Levon V**, fled to Gaban, was taken to Cairo (13 April
1375), ransomed (1382), and died in exile in **Paris (1393)**, buried at Saint-Denis. On
his death the Lusignans of Cyprus assumed the empty title "King of Armenia." See
[[fall-of-cilician-armenia-1375|the fall of Cilician Armenia]].

## Historiography

The dedicated synthesis here is [[ghazarian-armenian-kingdom-cilicia-2000|Ghazarian
(2000)]], which is **openly thesis-driven and pro-Armenian**: it casts the Cilician
Armenians as the indispensable enablers of the Crusades and the Latin West as the ally
that "conspired to suffocate" them through the union question. The medieval source base is
itself partisan — Armenian chroniclers **Matthew of Edessa, Kirakos of Gandzak, Sempad the
Constable, Vahram**, and the pro-crusade **[[hayton-of-korikos|Hayton of Korikos]]** — and
must be read against the Latin chroniclers (William of Tyre, Albert of Aix) and Byzantine
and Arabic accounts. Dynastic dates are well established; the deeper interpretive
question — whether church union was a sincere conviction or a recurring political tactic,
and whether it caused the kingdom's fall — is treated at
[[armenian-church-union-question|the union controversy]].
