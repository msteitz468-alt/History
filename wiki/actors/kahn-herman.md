# Herman Kahn

Herman Kahn (1922–1983) was the RAND strategist and later Hudson Institute founder famous (and notorious) for his elaborate analyses of nuclear war-fighting, civil defence, and escalation. A physicist by training, he brought systems analysis, operations research, and a willingness to discuss "unthinkable" scenarios in clinical detail to the strategic debate.

## Major Works and Ideas

- *On Thermonuclear War* (1960) — laid out scenarios for fighting and "winning" (in the limited sense of suffering less damage) a nuclear war; introduced the famous "escalation ladder" metaphor.
- Emphasis on civil defence, active defences (ABM), counterforce targeting, and damage limitation as ways to make nuclear war less catastrophic and therefore more usable as an instrument of policy.
- Strong belief that technological progress and ingenuity could solve strategic problems; less emphasis on the political and psychological limits that concerned Schelling and Brodie.

Kahn described as "one of the most startling things that ever happened to me" his participation in 1950 on a Technical Advisory Board when he "first came in contact with the philosophy which is willing to ask any question and tackle any philosophy". The final one-third of *On Thermonuclear War* (virtually a transcript of popular lectures) consisted of predictions for the future. He described eight wars, each one a "technological revolution ahead of its predecessor". The first two were World Wars I and II; the final four speculations. Date for World War VIII put at 1973; he expected at least four technological revolutions in the means of modern warfare in the coming thirteen years. Some predictions remarkably accurate (e.g. moon landing/take-off by 1969), others premature. His major error: assuming dramatic scientific breakthroughs and their immediate rigorous exploitation was the natural state of affairs, not requiring extraordinary effort. (Freedman 1981, THE TECHNOLOGICAL ARMS RACE, pp.157-158).

## Credible First-Strike Capability and Civil Defence

Kahn recognized certain feasible developments would be eschewed because they "cost a little too much" or from failure to perceive need due to misguided doctrine. He considered it quite likely the US would content itself with a limited deterrent capability providing no options for taking the nuclear initiative, failing by neglect to do enough in boosting air defence, air offence and civil defence. By 1961, given "possible Soviet lead in missiles, their interest in a cheap but useful civil defense program and a sober attitude towards the risks of war and peace", the Russians could have the upper hand. Eventually less inhibitions about exploiting technology; he described and prescribed a "frantic search for methods for improving all capabilities". (Freedman 1981, THE IMPORTANCE OF BEING FIRST / THE TECHNOLOGICAL ARMS RACE, pp.133-134, 158).

Kahn's basic assumption: thermonuclear war an ever-present possibility; though results tragic if it came, "the amount of tragedy could be reduced by the proper precautions". Any avenue promising amelioration of effects of enemy attack worth exploring. "Sober study shows that the limits on the magnitude of the catastrophe seem to be closely dependent on what kinds of preparations have been made, and on how the war is started and fought." (Freedman 1981, THE IMPORTANCE OF BEING FIRST, p.133).

He refused to accept that the threat of mutual suicide would guarantee deterrence. US would want capability to prevail if it did "fail". Thus he argued for a "Credible First-Strike Capability". Complementary to counter-force: adequate civil defence and "a suitably sacrificial frame of mind". "Credibility depends on being willing to accept the other side’s retaliatory blow. It depends on the harm he can do, not on the harm we can do. It depends as much on air defense and civil defense as air offense. It depends on will as well as capability." (Freedman 1981, THE IMPORTANCE OF BEING FIRST, pp.133-134).

He reminded readers that US obligations to NATO required ability to strike against the Soviet Union even if not attacked itself. "The agonizing decision to start an all-out thermonuclear war would be ours." Not taking precautions seemed "the height of irresponsibility". "We must still be able to fight and survive wars just as long as it is possible to have such a capability... we must also be able to stand up to the threat of fighting, or credibly to threaten to initiate a war ourselves... These remarks will distress all who very properly view the thought of fighting a war with so much horror... But I believe it borders on the irresponsible." (Freedman 1981, THE IMPORTANCE OF BEING FIRST, pp.133-134).

## Reception and Role in Freedman's History

Kahn was a polarizing figure. Critics saw his work as morally grotesque ("a moral tract on mass murder") and strategically misguided in its confidence that nuclear war could be controlled and survived in any meaningful sense. Supporters credited him with forcing policymakers and the public to confront the realities of the weapons they possessed rather than taking refuge in slogans.

Freedman ranks Schelling as the more substantial theorist but treats Kahn as a central, provocative participant whose work exemplified one pole of the debate — the pole that refused to accept that nuclear weapons had rendered strategy (in the traditional sense of using force for political ends) impossible. Kahn's scenarios and ladders fed directly into later flexible-response and selective-options thinking.
