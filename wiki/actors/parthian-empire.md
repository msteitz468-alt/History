# Parthian (Arsacid) Empire

## Overview

The **Parthian (Arsacid) Empire** (247 BCE–224 CE) was, with [[roman-empire|Rome]]
and [[han-dynasty|Han]] China, one of the **three great regional powers** whose
simultaneous strength created the [[silk-roads|first Silk Roads era]] — and, in
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]'s view, "the region and ruling power that has been
missed in the coverage of world history," overshadowed by Rome and China. Built by
the nomadic Iranian **Parni** under **Arsaces I** (crowned 247 BCE) out of the
eastern provinces of the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucid]] realm, it ruled Iran, Iraq,
and parts of Central Asia and the Caucasus for nearly 400 years.

## Origins: the secession from the Seleucids (CAH VII.1)

CAH VII.1 ch. 6 (D. Musti) reconstructs the Parthian breakaway as a two-stage process
within the wider secession of the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucid]] east. First the
Graeco-Macedonian governors of the peripheral provinces rebelled — Diodotus in
[[greco-bactrian-kingdom|Bactria]] and **Andragoras** in Parthyene. Then, in the last
years of Antiochus II, the nomadic Scythian **Parni** under **Arsaces** moved out of the
steppe, killed the already-rebel governor, and founded an independent state (the Parthian
era reckoned from **1 Nisan 247 BCE**), consolidated under Seleucus II. Musti characterises
this as "primarily the victory of a **nomadic over a settled element**" — and contrasts
Parthyene (where the settled population was *displaced* by the nomads, producing a new
Iranian dynasty) with [[greco-bactrian-kingdom|Bactria]] (where the Greeks persisted).

The exact date is a noted controversy between a **"high" chronology** (the process begins
under Antiochus II, c. 250) and a **"low" chronology** (Seleucus II, the "War of the
Brothers," c. 240–237). Musti argues for the high dating: Arrian's *Parthica*, Justin's
independent consular anchor, and the 247 era-epoch all point to Antiochus II, with the
*discordia fratrum* of the 230s belonging to the *consolidation* rather than the
beginning of the revolt. See [[hellenistic-historiography]].

## Territorial Extent and Structure

Under **Mithridates I** (171–131 BCE) the Parthians took Mesopotamia and revived the
old Persian title **King of Kings**, with [[zoroastrianism|Zoroastrian]]
legitimating ideology (the "Kingly Glory," akin to Roman *fortuna*). The empire was
**decentralized and quasi-feudal**: eighteen provinces under *shahrdars*; great noble
houses on landed estates (*dastkirt*, comparable to Roman *latifundia*) who mustered
cavalry from the lower nobility (*azats*) — a structure CWH IV likens to European
feudalism. Early Parthia was steeped in **Hellenistic** culture; over time the
Mesopotamian-Iranian and Achaemenid heritage was re-emphasized.

## Rome, Trade, and the Silk Roads

The Parthians checked Roman eastward expansion: in **53 BCE the general Surena
annihilated Crassus's legions at Carrhae**, after which Rome and Parthia
acknowledged rough parity in the east, with regulated **border trade** (Armenia,
Mesopotamia) and a ban on merchants crossing into the other's territory. Parthia
grew rich both from its own vineyards and grain and as the **indispensable middleman**
of the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] — deliberately blocking direct Roman access to
Chinese silk until the late 2nd century. The *Parthian Stations* of Isidore of
Charax documents its caravan and Persian Gulf trade (Palmyra, Hatra, Seleucia,
Spasinu Charax), tying the [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]] to Mesopotamia.

CWH IV (Liu, Ch 17) adds that the Parthians did more than relay Chinese silk: drawing
on their woolen-textile expertise and steppe pastoral base, **Persian artisans learned
to weave their own silk textiles** from Chinese yarn, creating a distinctively Persian
brocade that became a rival commodity in the Eurasian market — a tradition the
[[sasanian-empire|Sasanians]] would carry to its height. Their middleman interest is
vividly attested c. 97 CE, when the [[han-dynasty|Han]] envoy **Gan Ying**, sent to
reach [[roman-empire|Rome]], was turned back at the Persian Gulf — most likely by
Parthian merchants determined to prevent direct Han–Roman contact.

## A Cosmopolitan Religious Landscape

Less interested in promoting religion than its [[sasanian-empire|Sasanian]]
successors, Parthia tolerated an exceptionally cosmopolitan Mesopotamia — pagans,
Zoroastrians, Jews, Christians, and others side by side. Its large Jewish community
produced the **Babylonian Talmud**.

## Decline

Weakened by Roman wars and internal noble rivalry, the Arsacids were overthrown in
**224 CE by Ardashir I**, founder of the [[sasanian-empire|Sasanian Empire]], who
recast the Iranian state on a more centralized, religiously unified model. To the
east stood the [[kushan-empire|Kushans]] (former [[yuezhi]]).

## Historiography

Source-poor by classical standards: known largely through hostile/external Greco-Roman
accounts, coins, and limited indigenous evidence — part of why, as CWH IV notes, it is
"missed" in world history. Recent scholarship (Shayegan and others) reconstructs its
ideology of "post-Hellenistic" Iranian kingship. Compare [[empire]] and
[[sasanian-empire]]. For the third-century origins see [[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|CAH
VII.1]] (Musti, ch. 6).

## Parthia as Silk Roads Middleman (Benjamin 2018)

[[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Craig Benjamin]] frames the Arsacid Parthians
(247 BCE–224 CE, ~471 years) as one of the **four great powers** of the
[[silk-roads|First Silk Roads Era]] and its indispensable **western-central
intermediary**. Descended from the Parni (an eastern Indo-Iranian pastoral confederation),
they inherited the Achaemenid satrapal model in decentralized form (18 provinces plus
semi-autonomous kingdoms; *azat* nobility supplying cavalry) and practised notable
**religious tolerance** — sheltering Jews and Christians fleeing Rome (the Babylonian
Talmud was codified under Parthian rule).

- **First Roman contact**: the **Sulla–Orobazus treaty (96 BCE)** fixed the Euphrates as
  the boundary of spheres. **[[mithridates-vi|Mithridates II]]** (r. c. 121–91 BCE), "the
  greatest of all Parthian kings," extended control from the Caspian to the Euphrates and
  took the Persian Gulf hub of Charax.
- **Carrhae (53 BCE)**: the [[battle-of-carrhae-53bce|catastrophic Roman defeat]] using
  the **"Parthian shot,"** establishing Parthian dominance east of the Euphrates; ~10,000
  Roman prisoners settled at [[merv|Merv]].
- **First Chinese knowledge**: the *Shiji's* account of **"Anxi"** (from [[zhang-qian|Zhang
  Qian's]] report) describes a settled state of "several hundred cities" with **silver
  coinage** reissued at each succession; a Han caravan c. 106 BCE was met by **20,000
  Parthian cavalry**.
- **Guarding the monopoly**: Parthia deliberately blocked direct Roman–Chinese contact —
  in **97 CE** discouraging the Han envoy **Gan Ying** from reaching Rome — to protect its
  middleman profits. Yet commerce moderated conflict: even Caracalla proposed a
  trade-based alliance to Vologases IV.
- **Fall**: weakened by endless Roman wars and internal disunity, Artabanus V (IV) was
  killed by Ardashir I at **Hormozdgan (28 April 224 CE)**, ending the empire and beginning
  the [[sasanian-empire|Sasanian]] era — part of the
  [[collapse-of-first-silk-roads-era-250|collapse of the First Silk Roads Era]].
