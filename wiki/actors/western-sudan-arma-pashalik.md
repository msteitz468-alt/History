# The Arma (Ruma) Pashalik of Timbuktu

The **Arma** (Arabic *Ruma*, "musketeers") were the Moroccan-descended soldier-caste who ruled
the Middle Niger from **Timbuktu** after the [[moroccan-invasion-of-songhay-1591|conquest of
1591]] — the regime that replaced the [[songhay-empire|Songhay Empire]]. Documented for the wiki
through John Ralph Willis's chapter in [[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of
West Africa* Vol. I]] (1971), which spans 1591 to the death of the Kunta shaykh al-Mukhtar
al-Kunti (1811).

## Origins and the Makhzan

The conquering force — ~3,000 troops, mostly **enslaved Andalusian mercenaries** — took Songhay
wives, and their offspring became the **Ruma/Arma**, a hereditary military caste. They governed
through the **Makhzan**, a self-contained administration for the Moroccan community (who answered
to their own courts, not to local officials): at its head a **basha** (military governor, in
theory rotating between the Fas, Marrakush, and Sharaqa divisions), assisted by the **amin**
(fiscal agent of the Moroccan caliph) and district **hakims**. Over the indigenous population the
Arma practised **indirect rule**, investing loyal traditional officials — including puppet
**askias** for the Songhay — and at first refraining from interference with the existing
political, judicial, and religious order.

## Independence from Morocco and structural weakness

Distance defeated the empire: the recall of Judar (1599) and the death of Caliph al-Mansur (1603)
loosened Marrakush's grip, and a confirmation from the metropole could take six months. The army
**appointed its own basha in 1612**; by 1632 the amin (the last link to Morocco) was named by the
basha himself; and in **1660** the basha dropped the Moroccan caliph's name from the Friday
*khutba* and substituted his own. The regime was thus **functionally independent** but chronically
weak: the basha was a "weak *primus inter pares*," real power lay with the divisional *qa'ids*, and
the office was so unstable that almost every new basha reshuffled the lesser offices — against the
notable **stability of the religious offices** (qadis and imams of Timbuktu and Jenne held for
life). Willis stresses the Arma's **mercenary dependence** (Arab, Tuareg, Fulbe, Bambara hired
guns; the basha's personal slave-corps, the Legha) ultimately turned on them: from the 1720s–30s
Timbuktu suffered interregna and endless pillaging raids.

## Revising the "time of troubles"

Willis sets out to **test** the long-held view that 1600–1800 was a "time of troubles" of political
chaos, famine, and a **decline of Islamic learning**. His verdict is nuanced: the Arma kept their
core function — **securing the trade routes** — and were patrons rather than predators of the
Timbuktu and Jenne **merchants** (the *maks* "contributions" they levied were modest); their rule
"does not appear to have been oppressive," and oppression, when it came, issued from rebels and
unpaid soldiery rather than the government. The **capture of Ahmad Baba** (1591) was political
expediency, **not religious persecution** — al-Mansur was himself a noted patron of scholars. The
decline was real but **administrative and military**, not a wholesale collapse of Sudanese
civilisation.

## The Kunta realignment (al-Mukhtar al-Kunti)

The volume ends on the **shift from soldierly to clerical power**. The **Kunta**, a *zawaya*
(arms-renouncing) **Qadiri** Berber clerical group, rose from secondary status when **Sidi
al-Mukhtar al-Kunti** (d. 1811), from his base at Mabruk in the Azawad, **reunited the feuding
Qadiri factions** and made himself the supreme spiritual master and impartial **peacemaker** of
the Middle Niger after c. 1770 — gaining over the Arma, Tuareg, and Arab powers an authority of
*baraka* that the bankrupt military regime had lost. This realignment toward a **trans-political
clerical brotherhood** prefigures the religious revival that would erupt in the
[[west-african-jihads|nineteenth-century jihads]]. The Arma states were finally overrun by the
Massina caliphate in the 1830s.
