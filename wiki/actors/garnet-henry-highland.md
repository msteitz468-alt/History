# Henry Highland Garnet

African American minister, abolitionist, and political orator. Ellsworth (*Midnight on the Potomac*, 2025) centers Garnet's February 1865 address in the hall of the U.S. House of Representatives — widely remembered as the first time a Black preacher delivered a sermon there — as a hinge of Black civic assertion in the war's final months.

## Overview

Born enslaved in Maryland (1815); family escaped north. Educated in New York and New Hampshire; rose as a radical voice in the antebellum Black convention movement (notably the 1843 call that some contemporaries heard as endorsing slave resistance). By 1864–65 he was a prominent Northern Black leader supporting Union victory, emancipation, and full citizenship. Later served as U.S. minister to Liberia; died 1882.

## Role in 1864–65

Garnet's Capitol sermon praised congressional anti-slavery measures and articulated a Christian-republican vision of freedom beyond mere legal emancipation. Ellsworth pairs this moment with the Booth conspiracy thread to show two futures contending in the same capital: multiracial democracy vs. white supremacist restoration through violence.


## February 12, 1865 House Sermon (Ellsworth 2025)

On Sunday 12 February 1865 Garnet delivered a nearly hour-long address from the Speaker's desk in the House of Representatives—Ellsworth stresses that by end of 1864 no African American had addressed Congress from the floor. He condemned slavery, praised the Thirty-Eighth Congress for the Thirteenth Amendment, and demanded enfranchisement, equal citizenship, justice for Black soldiers, and education: **"Emancipate, Enfranchise, Educate."** Witnesses (William J. Wilson; Lois Adams) called it revolutionary—"the spectacle of a black man preaching against slavery from the Speaker's desk."

Biographical beats in Ellsworth: born enslaved Maryland 1815; family escape; African Free School NYC; NH academy burned by anti-abolitionists; Shiloh Presbyterian Manhattan; family nearly killed in July 1863 draft riots.

## Historiography

Garnet has often been overshadowed in popular Civil War narrative by Douglass; specialist Black abolitionist historiography restores him as a major radical voice. Ellsworth is popular synthesis — use for the 1865 House moment and wartime framing; deepen with dedicated Garnet scholarship for the full career.
