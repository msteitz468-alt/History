## Overview

The Chishtiyya is the dominant Sufi order of the Indian Subcontinent, playing an unrivaled role in the Islamization of northern India through its combination of spiritual authority, extreme hospitality, patronage of music, and use of vernacular languages. The order traces its origins to Chisht in Afghanistan; its Indian history begins with Muʿīnuddīn Chishtī (d. 1236) at Ajmer.

## Key Features from Chapters 4–5

**Samāʿ (spiritual music)**: The Chishtiyya was the Indian order most thoroughly identified with the practice of *samāʿ*. Schimmel states directly: "In India, *samāʿ* was advocated by the Chishtī order." One 17th-century saint in Sind even "dared to equate *samāʿ* with ritual prayer."

**Hospitality**: "Orders like the Chishtīs in India were extremely hospitable, and foreign visitors were always admitted" — in contrast to orders with stricter visitor policies. The Chishtiyya operated on *futūh* (unsolicited donations), refusing government stipends to maintain independence from political power.

**Vernacular devotion**: The order strongly promoted Urdu, Panjabi, and Sindhi as literary vehicles for mystical expression, contributing significantly to the development of these languages. The Chishtī saint Gīsūdarāz (d. 1422) appears in Schimmel's discussion of letter mysticism around Muhammad's name.

**Classification of seekers**: A tripartite classification of seekers — *ṭālib ad-dunyā* (seeker of the world), *ṭālib al-ākhira* (seeker of the afterlife), and *ṭālib al-maulā* (seeker of God) — was attributed to the Chishtī saint Jamāluddīn Hānswī (late 13th c.) and was used "by almost every Indo-Muslim mystic."

**Place in the Indian hierarchy**: The Chishtiyya "remained for centuries the most influential order in the Subcontinent" (from the detailed sections beyond this agent's range). It eventually competed with and was in many regions superseded by the Naqshbandiyya (from ca. 1600) and the Qādiriyya and Suhrawardiyya.
