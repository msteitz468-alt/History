# Pelopidas

## Overview

**Pelopidas** (c. 410–362... d. 364 BCE) was the Theban soldier and statesman who, with
his friend **[[epaminondas]]**, made Thebes great — the **leader of the
[[liberation-of-thebes-379bce|liberation of 379]]**, commander of the elite **Sacred
Band**, and Thebes' principal diplomat in the north. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]]
(Chs. 6–7) presents him as the bolder, more impetuous complement to Epaminondas' strategic
genius.

## Career

- **Liberation of Thebes (379).** An exile at Athens, Pelopidas led the daring return that
  killed the polemarchs of the pro-Spartan junta and expelled the Spartan garrison from
  the **Cadmea** — the act that launched the Theban resurgence (see
  [[liberation-of-thebes-379bce]]).
- **The Sacred Band.** He commanded the **Sacred Band**, the 300-strong corps of paired
  warriors, whose charge against the Spartan right at
  **[[battle-of-leuctra-371bce|Leuctra]]** (371) helped break the enemy before it could
  deploy.
- **Thessaly and the tyrant of Pherae.** Pelopidas repeatedly campaigned in Thessaly
  against **Alexander of Pherae**, who once held him prisoner. In **364**, marching to his
  allies' aid (the army first dismissed because of a solar eclipse on 13 July), he won a
  **hard-fought victory at Cynoscephalae but was killed** in the fighting — mourned so
  intensely by the Thessalians that they begged to conduct his funeral and dedicated a
  statue to him at Delphi.
- **Embassy to Persia (367).** Pelopidas led the mission to **[[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes
  II]]** that won a royal rescript favouring Thebes — the "medizing" Xenophon sourly noted
  (Spartan medizing being no less eager).

## Significance

[[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC* (2000)]]
confirms Pelopidas alongside Epaminondas as one of the two "**architects of an
unprecedented Theban predominance**" — and notes that this special status did not
protect either man from domestic political attack: both were, at different points,
taken to court by opponents at home, a symptom of Boeotia's annually-rotating Boeotarch
magistracy, which gave no institutional base from which to sustain a long-term
hegemonic policy (see [[epaminondas]]).

Pelopidas embodied the **military daring** of the [[thebes-hegemony-371bce|Theban
hegemony]], as Epaminondas embodied its strategy. His death at Cynoscephalae in 364
removed one of the two men on whom Theban supremacy depended; Epaminondas would fall at
**[[battle-of-mantinea-362bce|Mantinea]]** two years later, and the hegemony with him. The
pair are the textbook case of a great-power moment that **rested on individuals rather
than institutions**.
