## Overview

Alcide De Gasperi (1881–1954) was the dominant political figure of postwar Italy, leader of the Christian Democrats (Democrazia Cristiana) and prime minister from December 1945 to 1953. A veteran of the Catholic Popolari party of the 1920s, he spent the Fascist years in quasi-internal exile as a librarian at the Vatican. He emerged after 1945 as the principal architect of Italy's transition from monarchy and Fascism to a Christian Democratic parliamentary republic anchored firmly in the Western Cold War bloc.

## Role

De Gasperi led the Christian Democrats to a plurality in the constituent assembly elections of June 1946 (alongside Socialists and the powerful Italian Communist Party under Palmiro Togliatti, which by 1947 had grown to roughly 2 million members, the largest Communist Party outside the Soviet sphere). When the Communists were forced from the governing coalition in 1947 — part of the general Cold War split of the Western European Left also seen in France — De Gasperi emerged as the dominant power in Italian politics. In April 1948, in the first elections under Italy's new republican constitution, his Christian Democrats won an outright majority, the only single-party majority in modern Italian parliamentary history; the extension of the vote to women (1946) is credited with contributing to this result. De Gasperi's party benefited from American political and economic backing and clerical support, and remained the anchor of every Italian government from 1945 to 1993.

Under De Gasperi, Italy stabilized a currency wrecked by inflation (the lira was pegged at roughly one-fiftieth its prewar value), used Marshall Plan aid for reconstruction, and pursued a heavily state-mediated, neocorporatist economic model that preserved Fascist-era institutions such as the Institute for Industrial Reconstruction (IRI), a state holding company that controlled large shares of Italian metallurgy, chemicals, shipbuilding, and airlines into the 1960s. His government also carried out a limited land-reform program in southern Italy (1.75 million acres redistributed to roughly 85,000 owner-farmer families), though far short of southern land hunger.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had the Italian Communist Party (with its resistance-derived legitimacy and mass membership) remained in government or won power outright, Italy's postwar trajectory — and NATO's southern flank — would have looked very different. De Gasperi's success in channeling Catholic, anti-Communist, and American support into a durable parliamentary majority set the pattern of Christian Democratic dominance that lasted, with De Gasperi's own party continuously in government, until 1993.
