# La Malinche (Doña Marina / Malintzin)

**La Malinche** — baptised **Doña Marina**, called **Malintzin** in Nahuatl — was the Nahua woman who
served as **Hernán Cortés's interpreter, guide, and consort** through the
[[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519|conquest of Mexico]], and one of the most consequential (and most
contested) figures in the history of the Americas. Her story is told most fully by the conquistador
**Bernal Díaz del Castillo** in [[diaz-conquest-of-new-spain|*The Conquest of New Spain*]], who knew her
personally and called her "a person of great importance… obeyed without question by all the Indians of
New Spain."

## Life

By Bernal Díaz's account, Marina was **born a noblewoman** — daughter of *caciques* (lords) of Paynala,
near Coatzacoalcos. After her father's death, her mother remarried and, to secure the inheritance for a
new son, **secretly gave the girl away** to traders from Xicalango (giving out that she had died); she
passed to the people of Tabasco, who in **1519 handed her to Cortés** among twenty women — the first
converts baptised in New Spain. She proved "good-looking, intelligent, and self-assured," and her gift
to the Spanish soon proved priceless.

## The interpreter

Marina's value was **linguistic**: she spoke both **Nahuatl** (the Mexica language) and **Maya**, and so
formed a translation chain with the shipwrecked Spaniard **Jerónimo de Aguilar** (Maya↔Spanish) — and
soon Spanish directly. Through her the Spanish could **negotiate, gather intelligence, and detect plots**
(she is credited with uncovering the planned ambush at **Cholula**). She was inseparable from Cortés,
bore him a son, **Don Martín Cortés**, and was later married to the *hidalgo* Juan Jaramillo. Bernal
Díaz's most striking anecdote is her **reunion** with the mother and half-brother who had sold her:
finding them terrified she would take revenge, she **forgave them**, gave them gifts, and said she would
rather serve Cortés and her husband than rule all the provinces of New Spain — a scene Bernal Díaz
likens to **Joseph forgiving his brethren** in Egypt.

## Significance and the contested legacy

Without Marina, the conquest as it happened is hard to imagine: she made Cortés's **diplomacy and
alliance-building** possible — and indigenous disunity, channelled through her translation, was the
decisive cause of the Mexica defeat. Her legacy is fiercely **contested**, especially in Mexico: long
vilified as *la chingada* and the archetypal **traitor** (giving the word *malinchista* — one who
prefers the foreign), she has been reclaimed by later (especially feminist and Chicana) scholarship as a
**survivor and cultural mediator** — a woman twice sold into bondage who navigated an impossible position,
and, as mother of an early **mestizo**, a symbolic mother of modern Mexico. Bernal Díaz's portrait —
admiring, detailed, and sympathetic — is the fullest near-contemporary source.
