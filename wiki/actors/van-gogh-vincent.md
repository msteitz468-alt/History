# Vincent van Gogh

**Summary & detailed biography:** [[hubs/biographies/long-19th-century/van-gogh-vincent|Detailed analytic biography]]

Dutch Post-Impressionist painter (1853–1890). Author of some of the most recognizable and emotionally charged images in Western art: *The Potato Eaters*, the Arles Sunflowers, *The Bedroom*, *The Night Café*, *Starry Night*, *Irises*, and the final Auvers wheatfields. In a career of barely ten years he produced over 2,000 works (900+ paintings). His life of repeated failure, poverty, mental crises, and fraternal devotion became the archetypal modern narrative of the artist as suffering outsider whose work was recognized only after death.

## Narrative

Born in the parsonage at Zundert to a Dutch Reformed minister's family, Vincent failed successively as an art dealer (Goupil), lay preacher in the Borinage mining district, and bookseller before committing to drawing and painting in 1880 at age 27. Early Dutch work (The Hague with Sien, Nuenen weavers) was dark, thick, and socially committed (*The Potato Eaters*). In Paris (1886–88) with Theo he absorbed Impressionism and Japanese prints and lightened his palette. In Arles (1888) he produced a torrent of work intending to found a "Studio of the South"; the two-month visit of Paul Gauguin ended in crisis and the famous ear mutilation. After asylum at Saint-Rémy (1889–90) he moved to Auvers under Dr. Gachet, painted at least 70 final canvases, and died on 29 July 1890 from a gunshot wound to the abdomen. Theo arrived in time; both brothers are buried in Auvers. Theo's own death six months later left the letters and the paintings in the hands of Johanna Bonger, who later secured Vincent's reputation.

## Causal Analysis

A "fanatic heart" — unrelenting single-mindedness, inability to compromise, and identification of life with art — drove both vocational collapses and the intensity of the painting. Religious upbringing, early exposure to peasant life, and the model of Millet shaped his choice of subjects. The letters to Theo record a continuous dialogue about color, technique, and meaning; Theo's unconditional financial support (despite his own modest means) was the enabling condition. Paris and Arles exposed him to modern color theories and the example of Gauguin's Synthetism; the rupture with Gauguin coincided with a mental break that institutionalization could not fully contain. The final months in Auvers combined renewed productivity with deepening isolation.

## Consequence Analysis

Vincent's work helped define Post-Impressionism and opened the path to Fauvism, Expressionism, and much of 20th-century painting. His use of color as emotional language, visible brushwork, and direct emotional address became foundational. The myth of the unrecognized genius who cut off his ear and shot himself has shaped popular ideas of the modern artist more than any other single life. In reality the letters show a highly articulate, theoretically aware painter whose work was beginning to find buyers through Theo even before his death. The 2011 Naifeh & Smith biography re-opened the question of suicide vs. accidental shooting.

## Historiography

The letters (first edited by Jo van Gogh-Bonger 1914, later complete editions) are the indispensable primary source. Early biographies (including Irving Stone's *Lust for Life*) mythologized madness and suicide. Hulsker and others established chronology. Naifeh & Smith (2011) provide the most exhaustive documentary life and the strongest case for accidental death based on local testimony and ballistics context. Art-historical studies (Van Uitert, Zemel, Silverman) focus on religious iconography, peasant imagery, and Japanese influence. The "ear incident" and death remain subjects of debate and popular fascination.
