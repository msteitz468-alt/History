# Cyprian

**Thascius Caecilius Cyprianus** was the bishop of Carthage from 248 CE until his martyrdom in 258 CE. He was a leading figure in early Latin Christianity, whose pastoral leadership, theological treatises, and letters represent the primary historical witness to the mid-third-century crisis in North Africa—particularly the devastating pandemic that came to bear his name: the **[[events/plague-of-cyprian-249|Plague of Cyprian]]**.

---

## Life and Bishopric

Cyprian was born around 200 CE into a wealthy, pagan senatorial family in North Africa. He was trained in classical rhetoric and legal advocacy. Following his conversion to Christianity (c. 246 CE), he was rapidly elevated to the bishopric of Carthage in 248 CE (Harper 2017, line 1706). 

His tenure was defined by severe external shocks:
*   **Imperial Persecution:** Under the emperors Decius (r. 249–251 CE) and Valerian (r. 253–260 CE), the Roman state launched systematic persecutions of Christians. Cyprian went into hiding during the Decian persecution to direct his diocese remotely, which drew criticism but preserved his leadership.
*   **The Lapsed Controversy:** Following the persecutions, the church split over how to receive the *lapsi* (Christians who had sacrificed to pagan gods under duress). Cyprian advocated for a moderate path, requiring penitence overseen by the bishop, thereby strengthening episcopal authority.
*   **Martyrdom:** In 258 CE, during the persecution of Valerian, Cyprian was arrested, tried, and beheaded outside Carthage, becoming the first bishop-martyr of North Africa.

---

## Witness to the Pandemic

Cyprian's sermon *De mortalitate* ("On Mortality"), composed around 252 CE, is the most famous clinical and social description of the mid-third-century pandemic:
*   **Clinical Detail:** Cyprian described the horrific symptoms of the disease, including continuous vomiting, esophageal ulcerations, ocular hemorrhaging, and necrosis of the extremities requiring amputation (Harper 2017, line 1722).
*   **Demographic Relief:** Rather than viewing the plague as a sign of divine abandonment, Cyprian exhorted his congregation to view the mortality as a "trial of faith" and an opportunity for Christian charity, organizing practical care for both Christian and pagan victims in Carthage.
