## Overview

Mīr Dāmād (d. 1631) was the most important Safavid philosopher apart from his student [[mulla-sadra]], and "the greatest thinker of the period apart from Ṣadrā himself" in Peter Adamson's assessment. He held the prestigious post of *shaykh al-islām* — the foremost legal-scholarly position — under Shah ʿAbbās I, and rode alongside the Shah in courtly company. He is best known philosophically for his doctrine of **"perpetual creation" (*ḥudūth dahrī*)**, a sophisticated metaphysical solution to the problem of how a timeless God can create a temporal universe.

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Ch. 53.

---

## Doctrine of Perpetual Creation

Mīr Dāmād's central philosophical contribution, set out in his treatise *al-Qabasāt* (Blazing Embers), concerns the relationship between God's eternity and the created universe's temporality. The problem: if God is timeless, how can He relate to events that occur at specific times? And does the eternal necessity of God's nature force the universe to be eternal (Avicenna's position), or can there be genuine creation?

Mīr Dāmād's solution distinguishes three ontological levels:

1. **Sarmad (eternity)**: God's timeless, utterly atemporal existence
2. **Dahr (perpetuity)**: A transitional mode, neither fully temporal nor fully timeless, describing the relationship between God and the created universe
3. **Zamān (time)**: Ordinary temporal succession — "giraffes, Arsenal's chance at winning the league title, things that begin to be and pass away" (Adamson's paraphrase)

God acts "perpetually" (*dahrī*) rather than temporally. The universe as a whole has always been here as the result of God's perpetual creative act, even though nothing within the universe lasts forever. The perpetual differs from the eternal in that it involves a kind of causal "after" (the universe depends on God) but not a temporal "before and after" (the universe has no temporal beginning). This clarifies what was implicit in Avicenna — the distinction between causal priority and temporal priority.

[[mulla-sadra]] later criticized this doctrine, arguing (via the Indian philosopher Maḥmūd Jawnpūrī's parallel critique) that it incoherently requires the same thing to be created twice — once "perpetually" and once in time.

## Relationship to Mulla Sadra

Mīr Dāmād taught [[mulla-sadra]] philosophy (and possibly Sufism) in Qazwīn. Their relationship is the primary example within the [[school-of-isfahan]] of creative philosophical opposition: Ṣadrā initially accepted Mīr Dāmād's position on the primacy of essence but later decisively rejected it in favor of the primacy of existence. Ṣadrā's mature metaphysics can in part be understood as a radicalization of what was best in Mīr Dāmād (the emphasis on perpetual, continuous change) combined with rejection of what Ṣadrā saw as his error (essence primacy).

## Historical Context

Mīr Dāmād and Shaykh Bahāʾī were the intellectual ornaments of Shah ʿAbbās I's court. Anecdotes preserved by later writers show them in royal company: when the Shah teased Mīr Dāmād that Shaykh Bahāʾī's horse was outpacing his, Mīr Dāmād replied the Shaykh's horse "was just running fast with joy to have such an eminent rider." Shaykh Bahāʾī responded (when the Shah remarked on Mīr Dāmād's corpulence slowing his horse) that "the steed was simply having trouble carrying the weight of so much knowledge."

## Historiography

Mīr Dāmād's works were influential beyond Safavid Iran — they were widely read in Mughal India (see [[philosophy-in-islamic-india]]) — and his name "is still one to conjure with among scholars in modern-day Iran" (Adamson). He has received much less attention in European scholarship than [[mulla-sadra]]. The *Qabasāt* has been studied by modern scholars of Islamic philosophy but remains underanalyzed compared to Ṣadrā's *Four Journeys*.
