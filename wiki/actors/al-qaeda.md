## Overview

Al-Qaeda ("the base") is a transnational jihadist organization that grew out of the foreign-fighter infrastructure of the [[afghan-mujahideen|Afghan jihad]] against the Soviet Union. Its nucleus was the Services Bureau (Maktab al-Khidamat), founded in Peshawar in 1984 by [[abdallah-azzam|Abdallah 'Azzam]] (1941–1989) and Osama bin Laden (1957–2011).

## Origins

'Azzam, a Palestinian member of the Jordanian Muslim Brotherhood, arrived in Islamabad in 1981 and became the Afghan jihad's most influential ideologue and recruiter, arguing that resisting the Soviets was an individual religious duty (*fard-ul-'ayni*) incumbent on all Muslims — theoretical scaffolding that opened the war to foreign volunteers rather than Afghans alone. 'Azzam viewed liberating Afghanistan as a first step toward reclaiming other Muslim lands, naming Palestine, the Philippines, Kashmir, Lebanon, Chad, and Eritrea. Osama bin Laden, from a wealthy Hadrami Saudi family, arrived in Peshawar in 1979, met 'Azzam, made his first trip into Afghanistan in 1982, and co-founded the Services Bureau in 1984. By 1986 bin Laden, 'Azzam, and Egyptian militant Ayman al-Zawahiri (b. 1951) had become the international face of the Afghan resistance. The name "al-Qa'eda" first surfaced as an administrative term — a list ("the database") of trained, battle-ready foreign fighters in Afghanistan.

## Development

After the 1989 Soviet withdrawal, the international jihadi contingent solidified its alliance with the emerging [[taliban]] against Ahmad Shah Massoud's (1953–2001) traditionalist mujahidin faction. 'Azzam was killed in a roadside bombing in 1989, clearing the way for al-Zawahiri's more militant orientation to dominate the organization's direction. Bin Laden returned to Saudi Arabia before Iraq's 1990 invasion of Kuwait, offered al-Qaeda's fighters to repel Saddam Hussein, was rebuffed in favor of US troops, became an outspoken critic of the Saudi monarchy, and was pressured to leave the kingdom in 1992. He relocated to Sudan and, after roughly four years, to Taliban-protected Afghanistan.

Starting in 1992, Arab-Afghan veterans of the anti-Soviet war flowed into the Balkans to fight for Bosnian Muslims amid Yugoslavia's collapse — an early demonstration of the jihadi-internationalist logic of belonging to a global umma unconstrained by imperially drawn borders.

## Operations

Al-Qaeda-linked or -directed operations across the 1990s–2000s included the 1993 World Trade Center bombing, the 1998 bombings of US embassies in East Africa, the 2003–2005 Saudi Arabia attacks, the 2004 Madrid train bombings, the 2005 London bombings, and the September 11, 2001 attacks. Bin Laden was killed by US forces in Abbottabad, Pakistan, in May 2011.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without 'Azzam's doctrine of individual jihad duty and the Peshawar Services Bureau infrastructure, the foreign-fighter networks of the Afghan war would likely have dispersed without institutional continuity; al-Qaeda's subsequent operations, and the September 11 attacks specifically, reshaped US foreign policy and triggered the invasions of Afghanistan (2001) and, indirectly, Iraq (2003).

## Historiography

This page is drawn from Chiara Formichi, *Islam and Asia: A History* (2020), Ch. 7, "Islam as Resistance." Formichi explicitly situates al-Qaeda's post-9/11 prominence within a critique of the "global terrorism literature" that mushroomed after 2001: she argues this literature often collapses distinct, locally rooted jihadi-framed struggles (Kashmir, Xinjiang, southern Philippines, southern Thailand) into a single al-Qaeda-centric narrative, obscuring their separate ethno-nationalist and political origins. Her account treats al-Qaeda's rise as inseparable from Cold War great-power patronage of the Afghan mujahideen rather than as a spontaneous eruption of religious extremism.
