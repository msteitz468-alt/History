# Kublai Khan

**Kublai Khan** (1215–1294; Weatherford: **Khubilai**), grandson of **Genghis Khan** and son of
Tolui and [[sorghaghtani-beki|Sorghaghtani Beki]], was the Mongol ruler who completed the conquest
of China, founded the **[[yuan-dynasty|Yuan dynasty]]**, and presided over the eastern half of the
[[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] after the Toluid civil war. From the [[cambridge-history-of-china|
Cambridge History of China]], Vol. 6 (Rossabi's chapter on Kublai), enriched by Weatherford 2004.

## From Great Khan to emperor of China

On the death of his brother Möngke, Kublai won a **civil war (1260–64)** against another brother,
**Ariq Böke**, to become **Great Khan** — but the contest shattered Mongol unity: the western
khanates went their own way, and Kublai's authority became effectively that of an **East Asian
emperor** rather than ruler of a single empire. He moved the centre of gravity from Mongolia to
China, building a new capital at **Dadu (Beijing)** and, in **1271**, proclaiming a Chinese-style
dynasty, the **Yuan**. In **1279** his armies completed the conquest of the **Southern Song**,
reuniting all China under Mongol rule.

## Ruling China

Kublai is the CHC's model of the Mongol ruler **straddling two worlds**: he adopted Chinese
imperial forms, patronized Tibetan Buddhism, employed Confucian advisers and a multi-ethnic
administration (Mongols, Central Asian *semu*, and Chinese), and sought to govern China as a Chinese
emperor while preserving Mongol identity and the privileged position of the conquerors. His court —
visited and famously described by **Marco Polo** — was the cosmopolitan hub of the
[[pax-mongolica|Pax Mongolica]]. His ambitions overreached, though: the great seaborne **invasions
of Japan (1274, 1281)** and campaigns into Đại Việt, Champa, and Java failed at huge cost.

## Weatherford (2004) — Khubilai narrative

[[weatherford-genghis-khan-making-modern-world-2004|Weatherford]] fills early career and
institutional detail (popular narrative; check against Rossabi/CHC for contested numbers):

- **Army of the Left (1253):** Möngke assigned him the Song front despite limited field
  experience but deep knowledge of Chinese culture; lived a luxurious court south of the Gobi;
  Möngke's investigators later executed many of his administrators for fraud and stripped
  financial prerogatives before a public forgiveness at Karakorum.
- **Civil war:** Ariq Böke proclaimed Great Khan at Karakorum (June 1260) with most of the
  Golden Family; Khubilai held a thin *khuriltai* in his own territory, proclaimed Great Khan
  and Chinese emperor (title *Zhongtong*, "central rule"); cut food shipments Karakorum needed;
  Ariq Böke surrendered at Shangdu 1264 ("We were then, and you are today"); died 1266 —
  Weatherford: "almost certainly been poisoned." Four-zone empire: Khubilai (China, Tibet,
  Manchuria, Korea, eastern Mongolia); Golden Horde (refused recognition); Ilkhanate; Moghulistan
  under Qaidu. Karakorum looted under Khubilai; Silver Tree carted away after only ~three decades
  as capital.
- **Sinicizing strategy:** Chinese capital, Chinese names, Chinese dynasty, Chinese
  administration — "appear more Chinese than the Sung"; reign name *Zhiyuan* (1264), dynastic
  *Da Yuan* (1271); ancestral temple (1263), posthumous Chinese names (1277); Pacification
  Commissioners repairing temples; **Khanbalik/Dadu** on former Jurched Zhongdu (1272), with
  Forbidden City as miniature steppe filled with gers; Muslim architects and Central Asian
  craftsmen.
- **Law and administration (ascribed figures):** capital offenses reduced 233→135; rare
  executions (<2,500 claimed over 30+ years); 1291 code: reason before torture; abolished
  exam-selected mandarins in favor of foreigners, ethnic quotas, and *she* household units;
  paper money expansion (mulberry-bark notes; death for refusal — Polo); Phags-pa script (1269)
  as official but not forced; public schools (record: 20,166 — Weatherford notes possible
  exaggeration); golden age of Chinese drama (~500 new Yuan plays).
- **Song and Japan:** Hangzhou 1276; residual resistance wiped in following years; Japan 1274
  (~900 ships, ~23,000 Korean/Chinese infantry) and 1281 (main Chinese fleet ~3,500 ships /
  100,000 soldiers claimed) wrecked by storms; Weatherford on impact: pushed Japan toward
  cultural unification and militaristic government.
- **Late life:** too fat and gouty to ride; hunted from silk chamber on elephants; Weatherford's
  judgment: "only three generations after its founding, it had lost its way."
- **Diplomacy:** Rabban Bar Sawma's 1287–88 European mission (~7,000 miles to Edward I and
  Pope Nicholas IV) secured no treaties, only a papal promise of teachers; Polo brothers 1269
  had already carried a request for 100 priests.

Okada (via Weatherford): "The greatest legacy of the Mongol Empire bequeathed to the Chinese is
the Chinese nation itself" — uniting Chinese-dialect areas with Tibetans, Manchurians, Uighurs,
and dozens of smaller polities.

## Significance

Kublai made the Mongol conquest of China **durable and Chinese in form**, founding a dynasty that
reunified the country and enlarged the imperial template later inherited by the Ming and Qing. He
also embodied the transformation of the Mongol enterprise from a unitary world-empire into
regional successor states. The CHC's treatment is central to the **sinicization debate** — how far
Kublai and the Yuan were absorbed by Chinese civilization versus how far they remained a steppe
conquest elite ruling over it.
