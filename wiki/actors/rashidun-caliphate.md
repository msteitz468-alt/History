# Rashidun Caliphate

The **Rashidun Caliphate** (632–661 CE) was the first Islamic state established after the death of the Prophet [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam|Muhammad]]. It was ruled by the first four caliphs (successors) to Muhammad, known in Sunni tradition as the *Rashidun* ("Rightly Guided") caliphs: **Abu Bakr**, **Umar**, **Uthman**, and **Ali**. During its twenty-nine years of existence, the caliphate expanded rapidly from its center in Medina, conquering the Sasanian Persian Empire and stripping the Byzantine Empire of its southern Mediterranean provinces (Syria, Palestine, and Egypt), creating a vast empire.

## History and Dynastic Succession

### 1. Abu Bakr (632–634 CE)
Following Muhammad's death, Abu Bakr was selected as the first caliph at Medina. His immediate challenge was the **Ridda Wars** (Wars of Apostasy), during which several Arabian tribes declared independence or followed rival prophets. Abu Bakr successfully crushed these rebellions, unified the Arabian Peninsula under centralized Muslim rule, and initiated the first raids into Byzantine Syria and Sasanian Iraq.

### 2. Umar ibn al-Khattab (634–644 CE)
Selected as Abu Bakr's successor, Umar directed the caliphate's massive expansion. Under his rule, the Muslim forces achieved decisive victories over the Byzantines at the [[battle-of-yarmuk-636|Battle of Yarmouk]] (636) and over the Sasanians at the Battle of Qadisiyya (637). These campaigns resulted in the conquest of Syria, Palestine, Mesopotamia, and Egypt. Umar established the basic military-administrative structures of the empire, including the *diwan* (pension system for warriors) and the *amser* (military garrison cities, such as Fustat, Basra, and Kufa). He was assassinated in 644 by a Persian captive.

### 3. Uthman ibn Affan (644–656 CE)
Uthman, from the wealthy Umayyad clan of Quraysh, was selected by a committee. Under Uthman, conquests continued into Persia, North Africa, and the Caucasus, and the first Muslim navy was established, capturing Cyprus. His most significant domestic achievement was the **standardization of the Qur'anic text**, resolving regional variations in recitation. However, Uthman faced accusations of nepotism, favoring his Umayyad relatives with lucrative provincial governorships. This provoked growing discontent in Kufa, Egypt, and Medina, culminating in his assassination by mutinous soldiers in 656.

### 4. Ali ibn Abi Talib (656–661 CE)
Ali, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, was proclaimed caliph in Medina. His legitimacy was immediately challenged by a faction led by Muhammad's widow A'isha, Talha, and al-Zubayr, and by Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, the Umayyad governor of Syria and relative of Uthman. This split erupted into the **[[first-fitna-656|First Fitna]]** (Civil War). Ali moved his capital to Kufa. Following the indecisive Battle of Siffin (657) and a controversial arbitration, a group of Ali's former supporters (the Kharijites) seceded from his camp. Ali was assassinated by a Kharijite in 661, ending the Rashidun era and enabling Mu'awiya to establish the [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]].

## Administrative and Social Structures

- **Military Towns (*amsar*)**: To prevent the conquering Arab forces from dispersing into the local populations, the caliphs settled them in newly founded garrison cities (such as Kufa and Basra in Iraq, and Fustat in Egypt).
- **The *Diwan***: A registry that distributed pensions to the Arab warriors based on their priority of conversion to Islam and service in the conquests.
- **Treatment of Non-Muslims**: The conquered populations of Christians, Jews, and Zoroastrians were classified as **dhimmi** (protected peoples). They were allowed to practice their religions and retain their property in exchange for paying the *jizya* (poll tax) and *kharaj* (land tax). Because the early Muslim state made no effort to convert these populations, local administrative bureaucracies (operating in Greek, Coptic, and Persian) remained intact.
