# Sun Tzu

Sun Tzu is the traditional author of *The Art of War*, "the enduring model of the sage warrior" in Lawrence Freedman's *[[sources/freedman-strategy-a-history-2013|Strategy: A History]]*. Freedman is careful about the man: "Little is known about the author, or even if there was a single author. According to tradition, he was a general who served the king of Wu in Eastern China around 500 BCE, toward the end of China's Spring and Autumn period, although no contemporary references to him have been found." The text itself "seems to have been written or at least compiled over the subsequent century during the Warring States period." Its context was decisive for its content: "a competition for influence among a set of individually weak kingdoms at a time when central authority in China had collapsed."

Freedman treats Sun Tzu less as a biography than as the canonical articulation of one pole of strategic thought — the East Asian counterpart to the Greek *mētis*, the intelligence of the weaker party who outsmarts rather than overpowers. He sits, in Freedman's scheme, on the side of cunning and the indirect, alongside Odysseus, against the force of an Achilles or a [[clausewitz-carl-von|Clausewitz]].

## Strategic Thought

Sun Tzu's influence, Freedman writes, "lies in the underlying approach to strategy." Influenced by Taoist philosophy, *The Art of War* "covers statecraft as well as war." Its core claim is the economy and avoidance of force: supreme excellence was not winning "one hundred victories in one hundred battles" but rather "to subdue the enemy without fighting." The great strategist "had to be a master of deception, using force where it was most effective: 'Avoid what is strong to strike what is weak.'" Freedman lays out the hierarchy of generalship from the text: defeating "the enemy's strategy" (to "balk the enemy's plans") was "the 'highest form of generalship,'" followed by preventing "the junction of the enemy's forces," then attacking "the enemy's army in the field," and — "worst of all — besieging walled cities."

Deception, for Sun Tzu, "was simply a matter of doing the opposite of what was expected — look incapacitated when capable, passive when active, near when far, far when near." This paradoxically "required good order and discipline. Simulating cowardice, for example, required courage." It also required reading the opponent's character: a "choleric" general "could be easily upset"; one "obstinate and prone to anger" could be enraged by insults into impetuousness; the arrogant "could be lulled into a false sense of superiority and a lowered guard."

What "really made the difference," Freedman stresses, "was 'foreknowledge.'" This could not "be 'elicited from spirits, nor from gods, nor by analogy with past events, nor from calculations. It must be obtained from men who know the enemy situation'" — that is, from spies and informants. The enemy's coalition was itself a target: "Sometimes drive a wedge between a sovereign and his ministers; on other occasions separate his allies from him. Make them mutually suspicious so that they drift apart. Then you can plot against them."

## In Freedman's argument

Freedman places Sun Tzu as the "ideal type of a particular sort of strategy, based on outsmarting the opponent rather than overwhelming him with brute force" — the same problem, he notes, "that stimulated most strategists, including [[machiavelli-niccolo|Machiavelli]]: how to cope with the potentially greater strength of others." He links the indirect approach in war to the indirectness of Chinese rhetoric, citing François Jullien: a "disinclination to engage in high-risk, potentially destructive direct confrontations" mirrored in "circuitous, subtle forms of expression," contrasted with the Athenians' taste for "decisive" battle and transparent argument.

Crucially, Freedman flags the limits of Sun Tzu's applicability — his "strong tendency to point out what one should strive for, rather than explain how one should achieve one's aim." The approach "worked best when followed by only one side: if both commanders were reading Sun Tzu, the maneuvers and deceptions could lead to no decision at all or else an unexpected collision that caught them both unaware." A "reputation for deception would lead to a lot of double-guessing, just as one for avoiding battle could turn into a presumption of weakness." Against "a strong and coherent adversary, clever mind games could take you only so far." This is the underdog/cunning thesis with its own built-in ceiling — the recurring tension in Freedman between guile and force.
