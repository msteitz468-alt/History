# Marcus Licinius Crassus

The **richest man in Rome**, whose vast wealth and quiet web of obligations made him
"a man whom nobody liked to cross" — the indispensable financier of the late Republic
and the third partner of the **[[first-triumvirate|First Triumvirate]]**. This page
covers his career to 60 BCE; his death at Carrhae (53) belongs to later cycles.
Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 9 (Wiseman).

## Career to 60 BCE

A **Sullan** who rebuilt and vastly enlarged his fortune in the proscriptions and
through shrewd dealing (property, mining, slaves), Crassus crushed the
**[[spartacus-revolt-73-71bce|Spartacus revolt]]** (71) — and resented that
[[pompey]], mopping up the fugitives, claimed the credit. The two rivals shared the
**consulship of 70**, jointly restoring the tribunate. As **censor (65)** Crassus
pushed (against his colleague Catulus) to enroll the **Transpadanes** as citizens and
to **annex Egypt** under Ptolemy X's will — both blocked, and the censors resigned.

Wiseman stresses how **little we know** of Crassus' moves: he worked through others
(probably backing the tribune **Rullus** and the young **Caesar**, whose enormous debts
he helped underwrite), exercised influence "disproportionate to" any office, and
preferred the leverage of money to open leadership. In **60** his support of the
**publicani's** tax-contract demand (which Cato blocked) was one of the grievances
that drew him into alliance with Caesar and Pompey.

## The triumvirate and Carrhae (59–53)

In **59** Crassus backed [[julius-caesar|Caesar's]] consulship and was refunded the
publicani's tax money; at **Luca** (56) the alliance was renewed, giving him a
**second consulship with Pompey (55)** and a **five-year command in Syria**. Craving
**military glory** to match his partners, the sixty-year-old led an **invasion of
Parthia** — and was destroyed with his son and his army at the
**[[battle-of-carrhae-53bce|battle of Carrhae]] (53)**. His death (with Julia's the
year before) **dissolved the [[first-triumvirate|triumvirate]]**, leaving Caesar and
Pompey as unbalanced rivals.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Crassus embodies the new **political weight of wealth**: patronage, credit, and
obligation as instruments of power parallel to Pompey's legions. His rivalry with
Pompey, and his need (like theirs) to overcome Cato's obstruction, made him the
natural third party of the triumvirate — and his fatal craving for military glory
removed the balance-wheel that had kept Caesar and Pompey from open war.

## Historiography

Plutarch's *Crassus* (with the famous fortune and the proverbial avarice) and
Cicero's letters; Wiseman repeatedly notes the **thinness of the record** for so
powerful a man — a sign of his deliberately unobtrusive style.
