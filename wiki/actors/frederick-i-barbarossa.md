# Frederick I Barbarossa

**Frederick I "Barbarossa"** (c. 1122–1190; king of Germany from 1152, emperor
1155–1190) was the greatest of the [[hohenstaufen-dynasty|Hohenstaufen]] emperors and
the central figure of the twelfth-century empire. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]]
(Benjamin Arnold, Ch. 14, "The western empire, 1125–1197") presents his reign as the
attempt to restore imperial authority — drawing on a revived Roman-law conception of
*imperium* — against the two great obstacles of the age: the self-governing
**[[commune-movement|communes]]** of northern Italy and the reform **[[papacy]]**.

## Career

A Staufen duke of Swabia with Welf blood, Frederick was elected in 1152 as a
reconciler of the **Welf–Staufen (Guelf–Ghibelline)** feud that had divided Germany
since 1125. In Germany he stabilised the realm by accommodating the princes — above all
his Welf cousin **Henry the Lion**, duke of Saxony and Bavaria — and by recasting royal
lordship in territorial terms. His great struggles were in **Italy**:

- He asserted imperial *regalia* over the northern cities at the **Diet of Roncaglia
  (1158)**, provoking the resistance of the Lombard towns led by **Milan** (destroyed by
  Frederick in 1162).
- His support of a series of imperial antipopes against **Pope Alexander III** opened a
  **papal schism (1159–1177)** that fused with the Italian struggle.
- The cities formed the **[[lombard-league|Lombard League]]** (1167) under papal
  blessing and decisively defeated Frederick's army at the **[[battle-of-legnano-1176|
  Battle of Legnano (1176)]]**.
- Frederick then made peace: with the papacy at **Venice (1177)** and with the cities at
  the **Peace of Constance (1183)**, which recognised the communes' autonomy under
  nominal imperial overlordship.

He recovered prestige in his last years, brought down Henry the Lion (1180), and married
his son **Henry VI** to **Constance**, heiress of the **[[kingdom-of-sicily|kingdom of
Sicily]]** — uniting the empire's German and Sicilian fortunes. Frederick died in 1190,
drowning in a river in Anatolia while leading the **Third Crusade**.

## Counterfactual Significance

Barbarossa's reign is the high-water mark of the medieval empire's attempt to make its
universalist claims a working reality in Italy — and its failure (Legnano, Constance)
confirmed the **independence of the Italian communes** and the limits of imperial power
south of the Alps. His dynastic union with Sicily, however, set up the Hohenstaufen
"encirclement" of the papacy that would dominate the thirteenth-century struggles of
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]]. In German memory he became a legendary
sleeping-emperor figure.
