# Sennacherib

**Sennacherib** (reigned 704–681 BCE), son of [[sargon-ii|Sargon II]], is best
remembered for two acts at opposite poles of memory: the **destruction of Babylon
(689)** and the **[[sennacherib-siege-jerusalem-701bce|siege of Jerusalem (701)]]**
that the Hebrew Bible turns into a miracle of deliverance. He made
**[[nineveh|Nineveh]]** the greatest city of the ancient world.
[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 23, A. K. Grayson) is the
authority.

## The Babylonian question

The "predominant theme" of his reign. The Chaldaean
**[[neo-babylonian-empire|Merodach-baladan II]]**, backed by Elam, repeatedly
seized Babylon; Sennacherib tried every solution — a native puppet (Bel-ibni),
his own son **Ashur-nadin-shumi** on the throne — until in **694** the Elamites
invaded northern Babylonia and **kidnapped Ashur-nadin-shumi**, who was never seen
again. The war became a blood-feud, fought through the great but indecisive
**battle of Halule (691)** and a grinding siege. In **689 Babylon fell**, and
Sennacherib **razed the sacred city**, diverting canal-water over its ruins and
carrying off the statue of Marduk to Assyria. Grayson: "It was a black deed that
ruined his own reputation for posterity and was the starting-point for the ruin of
the empire." Its theological aftermath — the elevation of **Ashur over Marduk**,
the cancellation of the *akītu* festival for twenty years — runs deep in Sargonid
religion.

## The third campaign and Jerusalem (701)

The Babylonian revolt had a western arm: **Hezekiah of [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]]**,
encouraged by Merodach-baladan's embassy (II Kings 20) and by Egypt and Kush,
renounced Assyrian allegiance. Sennacherib's **third campaign (701)** took Sidon
and Ashkelon, defeated an Egyptian–Kushite force at **Eltekeh**, and besieged
**Jerusalem** — but the annals record only a vast tribute, not a capture, while
the Bible (II Kings 18–19) tells of a divine slaughter that lifted the siege.
Grayson's reconstruction posits **two western campaigns** (701, and a later one
c. 688–681 to which the Lachish reliefs and the deliverance-tradition belong).
See the dedicated event page: **[[sennacherib-siege-jerusalem-701bce]]**.

## Nineveh

His "most outstanding achievement" was transforming **[[nineveh|Nineveh]]** into
the imperial metropolis — the **"Palace Without a Rival"** (the South-West Palace,
its "two miles of bas-reliefs"), eighteen-gated walls and a moat, a great royal
park, and an elaborate system of **sixteen canals** drawing mountain spring-water
to the city. He personally supervised the engineering and boasted of a new method
of casting bronze colossi.

## Assassination

On 20 Tebet **681** Sennacherib was **murdered by one or more of his sons** while
at worship — "one of the great mysteries of ancient history." The Bible names
"Adrammelech and Sharezer"; Parpola argues the chief assassin was the passed-over
eldest son **Arda-Mulissi**. The younger son **[[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]]**, whom
Sennacherib had designated heir, defeated the rebels and took the throne after a
42-day civil war.

## Character

Grayson finds a rare individuality: a builder of genius personally absorbed in
engineering; a son **haunted by Sargon's unburied death**, who erased his father's
name and abandoned his father's city; and a king dragged by the Babylonian
vendetta into an act of sacrilege he then had to justify to his own people.

## Significance

- `produced:` the destruction of Babylon (689) — a wound that `contributed_to:`
  the later Babylonian war of independence and the empire's fall.
- `produced:` imperial [[nineveh|Nineveh]], the capital sacked in 612.
- The Jerusalem deliverance `produced:` a foundational motif of biblical theology
  (Zion's inviolability; Isaiah's prophecy) — see
  [[sennacherib-siege-jerusalem-701bce]].

## Historiography

Richly documented (royal annals numbering the campaigns, the Babylonian Chronicle,
the reliefs, the Bible, Berossus, Herodotus's mouse-legend), but the **Jerusalem
problem** — reconciling the Assyrian tribute-list with the biblical deliverance —
is one of the most debated puzzles in ancient history, and his reign's chronology
(three competing first-regnal-year dates) is unusually tangled.
