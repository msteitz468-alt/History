## Overview

U Nu (1907–1995) was Burma's first prime minister following independence in January 1948, serving as the country's democratic leader (with one interruption) until the military coup of 1962. He succeeded Aung San after the latter's assassination and attempted to build a post-colonial Burmese state on a synthesis of Buddhism and socialism. His tenure was characterized by communist insurgency, ethnic minority pressures, and progressive party fragmentation — conditions that ultimately provided the military's justification for removing him.

## Role and Major Decisions

**Succession and early challenges**: U Nu became prime minister in July 1947 following Aung San's assassination. He led Burma through independence (4 January 1948) and the immediately following crisis: in March 1948, the communist party (White Flags) revolted, joined by a People's Volunteer Organization. The ensuing civil war between communists and Rangoon produced the collapse of the central administration: "In the districts, local bosses (*bo* in local parlance) emerged, exercising control with their own paramilitary bands to defend their fiefs."

**Buddhist-socialist synthesis**: U Nu pursued a political framework combining Buddhism and socialism, with, as the *Cambridge History of Southeast Asia* (Tarling, ed., Vol. 2) observes, "an especially heavy dose of the former." He saw Buddhism as the means of making socialism possible: "the economic system could not be changed unless human hearts were first transformed." The Buddhist hierarchy constituted a substantial political structure in Burma — providing a refuge from state laws while posing as the conscience of the people. U Nu worked to institutionalize this relationship: the Buddha Sasana Council Act (1950) created a state-financed agency for Buddhist promotion; in 1954, instruction in Buddhism began in state schools.

This program "appealed to many Burmese who were Buddhists, but it also aroused the fear of the ethnic minorities who suspected that they would be marginalized since they were not Buddhists."

**AFPFL split (1958)**: The AFPFL, founded by Aung San as a broad coalition, was never a unified party but an alliance of mass organizations, ethnic groups, and personal followings. At the third All-Burma AFPFL Congress in January 1958, U Nu declared the coalition would become a unitary party with all affiliates subordinate to party hierarchy. This produced a split into a U Nu faction and a Kyaw Nyein-Ba Swe faction. Facing a potential no-confidence vote, U Nu invited General Ne Win to form a caretaker government to restore order.

**1960 elections and return to power**: Ne Win's caretaker government ran for 18 months. When U Nu's faction — renamed the Pyidaungsu (Union Party) — won the February-March 1960 elections, it was a resounding victory and "the first peaceful transition from military to civilian rule." However, the Union Party then "succumbed to squabbling among the leaders," dividing into leftist Thakins and more conservative U Bo's.

**Buddhism as state religion (1961)**: After 1960, U Nu made two decisions that precipitated his downfall. First, he declared his intention to make Buddhism the state religion — "because of his own strong desire to perform this deed of merit, among other reasons." Though Buddhism was already widely practiced, the announcement incensed non-Buddhist ethnic minorities and alarmed the army, which feared the divisive consequences. Second, U Nu promised separate statehood to the Arakanese and Mons as an election pledge, then delayed delivering on it after victory — alienating these groups and intensifying insurgency. By the end of 1961, U Nu admitted that minority rebels controlled one-tenth of the country.

**Coup and aftermath**: Ne Win's coup of 2 March 1962 removed U Nu from power. He was imprisoned until 1966, then lived in exile, organizing resistance movements. He returned to Burma in 1980 under an amnesty. He died in 1995.

## Counterfactual Significance

U Nu represented the democratic path for Burma — imperfect, fragile, and beset by challenges, but nonetheless a functioning parliamentary system for over a decade. The *Cambridge History* suggests that U Nu's specific policy errors (Buddhism as state religion; the ethnic statehood reversal) were the proximate causes of his downfall, which raises the question of whether different political choices could have preserved the parliamentary framework. Structurally, however, Burma's ethnic divisions, communist insurgency, and the army's accumulated political role created conditions that would have tested any civilian government severely.
