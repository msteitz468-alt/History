# Minamoto no Yoritomo (1147–1199)

The warrior who founded the **[[shogunate-bakufu|Kamakura bakufu]]** — Japan's first
enduring **military government** — and so inaugurated nearly seven centuries of rule by
the [[samurai|warrior class]]. From Mason & Caiger's
[[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]] (ch. 9).

## Role and career

Head of the **Minamoto (Genji)**, Yoritomo had been spared as a boy by the Taira leader
**Kiyomori** after the Minamoto defeat of 1160 and exiled to the eastern province of Izu.
There he built a following among the provincial warriors of the **Kantō** plain. When the
[[kamakura-period|Gempei War]] broke out (1180), he established a base at **Kamakura** —
less a fortress than an administrative center — while his brilliant brother **Yoshitsune**
won the battles that destroyed the Taira (Dannoura, 1185).

Yoritomo's genius was **institutional**, not martial. He built the organs of warrior
government (the *samurai-dokoro*, etc.), and in **1185** secured the court's grant of the
right to appoint **shugo** (constables) and **jitō** (stewards) nationwide — order imposed
without overthrowing the [[heian-period|court or the shōen]]. In **1192** he was made
***sei-i-tai-shōgun***, redefining the title as permanent, heritable authority to govern.

## Decisions and their consequences

- **Ruling from Kamakura, not Kyoto** — keeping his warrior government rooted in the east,
  apart from the enervating court, set the template for the bakufu.
- **Destroying Yoshitsune** — Yoritomo hounded his too-popular, court-favored brother to
  death (1189), eliminating a rival and absorbing the northern Fujiwara, but his cold
  ruthlessness (he killed many of his own kin) is the foil to Yoshitsune's tragic glory in
  the *Heike Monogatari*.
- **Building within the old order** — by securing his and his vassals' place *inside* the
  imperial system rather than abolishing it, he created the durable **dyarchy** of court
  and bakufu.

## The shugo and jitō: the durable institutional legacy (Cambridge History of Japan)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v3-medieval|Cambridge History]] (Jeffrey P. Mass) locates
Yoritomo's lasting achievement precisely in the offices he created in the **Gempei
aftermath**: the **shugo** (provincial constable) and especially the **[[medieval-shoen|jitō]]**
(estate steward). The jitō — a bakufu-appointed, bakufu-protected, heritable shiki holder
planted inside the [[medieval-shoen|shōen]] — was the institutional wedge by which warrior
power penetrated the estate economy and, over the next three centuries, hollowed out the
capital proprietors. Mass stresses that the bakufu's authority over these stewards was
exercised above all **judicially**: jitō were "the primary objects of complaint and
control," and the maturing Kamakura courts existed largely to adjudicate the land disputes
they provoked. Yoritomo thus bequeathed not just a military government but the **legal
machinery of warrior landholding** — the framework within which Japanese feudal society
actually grew.

## Counterfactual significance

Where Kiyomori had tried to dominate as a court insider and failed, Yoritomo created a
**separate, self-standing warrior government** — the decisive institutional innovation of
Japanese history. The bakufu he founded outlived his own line (the **Hōjō** soon ruled as
regents) and shaped Japanese politics until 1868. Historians credit him, not the romantic
Yoshitsune, as the true architect of the warrior age.
