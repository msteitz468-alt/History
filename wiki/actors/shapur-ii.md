# Shapur II

## Overview

**Shapur II** ("the Great"; r. 309–379 CE) had the **longest reign in Sasanian history** —
acclaimed king while still an infant (reputedly *in utero*, by crown placed on his
mother's womb) and ruling for seventy years. Under him the
[[sasanian-empire|Sasanian Empire]] recovered the initiative against
[[roman-empire|Rome]], beat back [[julian|Julian]]'s great invasion, and imposed the
**humiliating peace of 363 CE**. His reign is also the setting for the first major
**persecution of Christians** in Iran — which [[sources/wiesehofer-ancient-persia-2001|
Wiesehöfer]] reads as politically, not doctrinally, driven.

## Role and Key Events

- **The Roman wars.** After decades of frontier conflict with Constantius II, Shapur
  destroyed the eastern campaign of the emperor **[[julian|Julian]]** (363 CE); the
  ensuing treaty, which Wiesehöfer calls a "humiliating peace," returned eastern
  Mesopotamia — including the key trade city of **Nisibis** — to Sasanian control,
  reversing the losses of the peace of Nisibis (298/299).
- **Arabia.** Responding to Arab incursions into Fars, Shapur "took revenge by a brutal and
  extensive expedition into Arabia, by building massive defence lines in southern Iraq, and
  by deporting Arabs to Kerman and Ahvaz" — earning the Arabic epithet *Dhū l-Aktāf*
  ("he of the shoulders").
- **Persecution of Christians (from 344/345 CE).** The trigger was fiscal and political,
  not theological: Christians were assessed a special **war tax**, and when the
  Metropolitan **Shemʿōn bar Ṣabbāʿē** refused to collect it he was charged with disloyalty.
  Shapur's recorded accusation is explicitly political — "Simeon wants to make his followers
  and his people rebel against my Majesty and become slaves of the emperor who shares their
  faith" — i.e., the suspicion that Iran's Christians were a fifth column for newly
  Christian Rome. Jews, by contrast, "were decidedly loyal" and largely spared.
- **Coinage and clergy.** His drachma was minted in great quantity, "probably for the sake
  of recruiting mercenaries from Central Asia"; his reign (a period of crisis and war) is
  among those Wiesehöfer identifies with strong clerical influence (see [[kartir]],
  [[zoroastrianism]]).

## Counterfactual Significance

Shapur II's long reign stabilized the empire at a moment of acute Roman pressure and reset
the western frontier in Iran's favour for a generation. His handling of the Christian
question shows the recurring Sasanian dilemma — a large, growing minority whose co-
religionists ruled the enemy empire — and helped push the Church of the East toward the
autocephaly (410 CE) and Nestorianism (484 CE) that would distance it from Byzantium (see
[[sasanian-empire]]).

## Historiography

Shapur's wars are unusually well documented from the Roman side by the eyewitness
**Ammianus Marcellinus**; the persecution is known chiefly through Syriac **Acts of the
Martyrs**, whose hagiographic aims Wiesehöfer weighs against their value as evidence for
royal religious policy.
