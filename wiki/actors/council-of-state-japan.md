## Overview

The Council of State (*Daijokan*, literally "Great Council") was the apex of the statutory (*ritsuryo*) administrative structure of premodern Japan, established formally under the Taiho Code of 701. Under the code it was the supreme organ of civil government, standing between the emperor and the eight ministries that directed all central administration. All governmental action passed through, or was authorized by, the Council; the most important decisions required the emperor's direct personal approval or that of his regent.

The Council was the institutional home of the [[kugyo-aristocracy]] — the senior nobles of the Third Rank and above plus Fourth-Rank Consultants (*sangi*) — who constituted its membership when in active deliberation. The [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] notes that "currently active" (*gennin*) nobles — those holding appointment at Consultant level or above — were those who took part in the Council's deliberations.

## Structure

The statutory structure of the Council placed it under the authority of the Grand Minister of State (*Daijo-daijin*), a title rarely filled; practical leadership fell to the Ministers of the Left (*Sadaijin*) and Right (*Udaijin*), with the Minister of the Center (*Naidaijin*) as an additional senior post. Below them ranked Major Counselors (*Dainagon*), Middle Counselors (*Chunagon*), Minor Counselors (*Shonagon*), and Consultants (*sangi*).

Adjacent to the Council but formally extra-statutory were the Regent (*sessho*) and Chancellor (*kampaku*) positions, which from 858 onward were held by senior Fujiwara Northern House leaders and superseded the Council as the real locus of governmental authority during the height of the [[fujiwara-regency]].

The Council's administrative compound at [[heian-kyo]] lay directly east of the Court of Government (Chodoin), a "walled compound lying directly east of the Court of Government" containing "the three connected buildings of the Council of State." This was "the administrative heart of the government."

## Physical Location and Ceremonies

The Council of State was originally conceived as the body that would meet in daily audience with the emperor in the Court of Government (Chodoin) at the Greater Imperial Palace (Daidairi). The Chodoin — also called the Court of the Eight Ministries (*Hasshoin*) — was designed with the Great Hall of State (Daigokuden) at its northern end, flanked by twelve symmetrically arranged buildings where the bureaucracy assembled when government business was conducted. Foreign envoys were received here; the great state ceremonies were held in its precincts.

In practice, as the center of governmental activity shifted from the Chodoin to the Emperor's Residential Compound (*Dairi*) in the mid-Heian period, formal deliberation in the Council became increasingly ceremonial. Actual decisions were made informally by the regent and a small inner circle in the Chamberlains' Office (*Kurododokoro*) or at the regent's mansion.

The Great Hall of State burned in 876 and was rebuilt; it burned again in 1058 and remained unrestored for fourteen years. Its final destruction came in the great Kyoto fire of 1177. The Court of Abundant Pleasures (Burakuin), the Court of Government's ceremonial counterpart, burned in 1063 and was never rebuilt, leaving growing vacant space within the palace walls by the twelfth century.

## General Authority: Extra-Statutory Offices

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] diagram of major Heian governmental organs (c. CE 1000) distinguishes the Council's "specific Yoro Code authority" — the eight ministries and their subordinate bureaus — from its "general authority" over several critical extra-statutory offices:

- **Board of Censors** (*Danjodai*) — oversight and audit
- **Six Guards Headquarters** (*Rokuefu*) — palace and capital military forces
- **Imperial Police** (*Kebiishi*) — extra-statutory police authority for the capital, eventually replacing the Capital Offices as the principal organ of city administration
- **Chamberlains' Office** (*Kurododokoro*) — administration of the Emperor's Residential Compound; created 810, staffed by Fourth- and Fifth-Rank nobles, became the critical channel of access to the emperor
- **Provincial Administrations** and the **Capital Offices** (*Kyoshiki*)
- **Kyushu Government Office** (*Dazaifu*)

## The Eight Ministries

Under the Council, the statutory administrative structure comprised eight ministries:
1. **Central Affairs Ministry** (*Nakatsukasasho*) — including the Palace Storehouse Bureau, Bureau of Divination, and Bureau of Imperial Attendants
2. **Ministry of Ceremonial** (*Shikibusho*)
3. **Civil Affairs Ministry** (*Jibusho*) — including the Bureau of Music
4. **Popular Affairs Ministry** (*Minbusho*) — including the Bureau of Computation and Tax Bureau
5. **War Ministry** (*Hyobusho*)
6. **Punishments Ministry** (*Gyobusho*)
7. **Treasury Ministry** (*Okurasho*)
8. **Imperial Household Ministry** (*Kunaisho*) — including the Palace Table Office, Carpentry Bureau, Bureau of Grounds, Bureau of Housekeeping, and Imperial Table Office

The two temples (Saiji and Toji) and Shinto affairs fell under the Department of Shrines (*Jingikan*), which was relegated to the southeastern edge of the palace site despite its theoretical co-equality with the Council.

## Decline

The contraction of the statutory regime in the ninth and tenth centuries emptied the Council of much of its operative content. Many offices were abolished or became paper organizations; the Seventh and lower ranks ceased to be used. The Kebiishi office, which operated outside the Council's statutory framework, took over actual city administration. By the mid-Heian period the Council's most important function was the ceremonial authorization of decisions already reached informally, and the senior nobles who staffed it were increasingly occupied with their own household economies and the patronage relationships that bound the court together.

## Historiography

The Council of State is well documented in the compiled regulations (*Ryō no shūge*, *Engi shiki*) and in the diaries of senior nobles who participated in its deliberations. The distinction between the Council's statutory role and the regent's extrastatutory authority is a central question in the historiography of Heian governance. The [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] provides the clearest English-language account of the Council's structure and its subordination to the regency system.
