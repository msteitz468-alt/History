# Ancient Order of Druids

A British fraternal order founded in London in **1781**, the Ancient Order of Druids (AOD) was the first large-scale organization to adopt the name and imagery of Druidry for the purposes of mutual aid, fellowship, and ceremony. It became the most successful of a family of Druid fraternal orders and underwent repeated schisms as regional lodges in industrializing England asserted their independence.

## Founding and Purpose

The AOD was founded in London in 1781 on the model of the Freemasons: a closed organization with graded initiations, symbolic regalia, and a meeting structure of lodges. Its core purpose was mutual financial support — members paid weekly dues that guaranteed medical care in sickness and a good funeral — combined with music, ceremony, and fellowship. It appropriated the name "Druid" as a prestigious identity rooted in the ancient British past rather than as a theological commitment: the name carried connotations of learned wisdom, patriotism, and love of nature that served the fraternal purposes well.

## Ideology and Ritual

The AOD's ceremonies blended Masonic ritual forms with imagery drawn from the Georgian image of the ancient Druid: robes, wands, cups, flagons, and regalia. Its lodges stressed charity, sobriety, and non-denominational religious inclusivity — 'religion and politics could not be discussed' at meetings, in keeping with most closed societies of the period. An account of the Mona Lodge's public outing to the stone circles at Stanton Drew in July 1856 shows 150 members, wives, and friends processing in 'robes and insignia, carrying wands, cups and flagons', with 'a bible on a velvet cushion and a representation of Noah and his Ark' among the displayed objects; after a gorsedd ceremony, chanting, and an ode, the company adjourned for mead and a meal.

Several lodges adopted the language and ceremonial ideas of [[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]]: the Loyal Order of Druids (founded 1829; revived 1843), an offshoot, used the term *gorsedd* for its meetings and declared Iolo's motto 'The Truth Against the World'. This made it 'the first English group to have adopted' Iolo's ideas, according to Hutton, blending them with Masonic initiation ritual.

The religious tone of the orders was characteristically inclusive and non-dogmatic: alongside affirmations of belief in 'a Great Supreme, revealed in the natural world' and in the biblical Jehovah, the burial service of the Loyal Order spoke of the levelling power of death and eternal reward for virtue, representing 'an alternative form of spirituality, which could exist within Christianity but was not part of it.'

## Schisms and Growth

The AOD underwent its first major division in 1833, when lodges seceded to form the **United Ancient Order of Druids**, primarily as a friendly society without the old order's emphasis on secrecy. A second secession occurred in November 1858, when the Cheshire lodges broke away to form the **Order of Druids**, which in 1861 issued a set of lecture books — the only instance Hutton finds of a Druid order with a formal 'party line' on ancient Druidic tradition. This order's lectures taught that Noah had founded Druidry upon leaving the Ark, that the new Bard's duty was to emulate Iolo's hero Hu Gadarn, and that ancient Druidic wisdom had been transmitted from Noah through his European descendants.

Alongside these large affiliated orders, smaller bodies multiplied: an **Independent Order of Ancient Druids** (1824, revived 1843); a **Loyal Order of Druids** (1829); a **Noble Order of Female Druids** at Leigh, Lancashire (1853), whose spectacular initiation ceremonies featured a symbolic leap toward 'a double row of sharp steel spikes' replaced at landing by 'a soft green surface'; and an **Independent Order of Modern Druids** in Liverpool (1858). The Druid Friendly Society at the George Inn, Amesbury (1853) had so little Druidic content that Hutton concludes its members chose the name solely because Stonehenge lay within their catchment area.

By mid-century the fraternal Druid orders represented, in Hutton's words, 'highly successful, energetic and multiplying organizations', concentrated especially in the industrial communities of north-west England (Lancashire and Cheshire). The lodges' surviving records are frustratingly thin on social composition but show vigorous ceremonial life and a characteristic tendency to combine mutualism with a loose, inclusive religiosity compatible with but not identical to Christianity.

## Significance

The AOD and its derivatives were a major vehicle for the diffusion of Druidic imagery into working- and middle-class urban culture during the Long 19th Century, independent of the antiquarian and literary tradition. Their adoption of Iolo's concepts — the gorsedd, the triads, Hu Gadarn — created unexpected bridges to the Welsh national revival: at the Abergavenny eisteddfod of 1838, 'members of the Ancient Order of Druids, led by two Archdruids in full ceremonial dress' joined the procession, formally linking English fraternal Druidry with the Welsh cultural movement for the first time.
