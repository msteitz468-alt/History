## Overview

Urhi-Teshub was the son of [[muwatalli-ii]] by a secondary wife (DUMU EŠERTI), who occupied the [[hittites|Hittite]] throne for approximately five years (c.1272–1267 BCE) under the reign-name Mursili III. He is the central figure in one of the most dramatic succession conflicts in Hittite history: placed on the throne by his uncle [[hattusili-iii]], he was eventually overthrown by him, exiled, and spent the remainder of his life in flight — first to Syria, then to Egypt, then at large across the Near East, a perpetual source of legitimacy anxiety for the usurper who displaced him.

## Role and Accession

On Muwattalli II's death without a first-rank heir, Urhi-Teshub held the right of succession under the principles set down by the [[telipinu-edict-1500bce|Telipinu edict]], but his birth from a secondary wife was a vulnerability. His uncle Hattusili — next to the king, the most powerful figure in Hatti — chose to honour his brother's intention rather than claim the throne himself. He later described this in the *Apology* with a tone that implies the decision was his to make: "Out of esteem for my brother, I did no evil against him. And since he had left no son of the first rank, I took Urhi-Teshub, the son of a secondary wife (by him), and placed him on the throne of Hatti. I put all Hattusa into his hands, and he was Great King in the lands of Hatti" (Apol. §10b, iii 38'–44'). At least one vassal ruler, Masturi of the Seha River Land, contested even this: "Should I protect a (mere) second-rank son?" (Bryce, citing text).

On accession Urhi-Teshub adopted the name Mursili (III), the name of his grandfather — likely an implicit claim to legitimate succession. Hattusili, significantly, never used this name for his nephew.

## Reign and Actions

Despite his vulnerable position, Urhi-Teshub took several bold administrative initiatives:

- He reinstated [[hattusha|Hattusa]] as the Hittite royal capital, reversing Muwattalli's decision to relocate to [[tarhuntassa]]: "He took up the gods from Tarhuntassa and brought them back to Hattusa" (KUB xxi 15 i 11–12). The return met with little opposition and may have been a deliberate bid to secure broader support.
- He recalled the exiled former king Manapa-Tarhunda of the Seha River Land, contrary to Muwattalli's order.
- He probably restored the vassal Benteshina to the throne of [[amurru]] — though Hattusili later claimed credit for this.
- He reinstated the queen Danuhepa.
- He recalled Sippaziti, son of Hattusili's bitter enemy Arma-Tarhunda, from exile — a move almost certainly intended to build a counterweight to Hattusili's influence.

In foreign affairs, Urhi-Teshub faced the Assyrian expansion. The Mitannian king Shattuara I had attacked [[assyria-middle]] and been defeated and made tributary by Adad-nirari (c.1295–1264). Wasashatta's subsequent rebellion against Assyria failed; Hanigalbat was absorbed into Assyrian territory and its borders now touched [[carchemish]]. Adad-nirari wrote to the Hittite king claiming the title of "Great King" and requesting to be addressed as "Brother." Urhi-Teshub's angry reply survives: "Were you and I born from one mother? As my grandfather and my father did not write to the King of Assyria about brotherhood, you shall not keep writing to me about brotherhood and Great Kingship. It is not my wish!" (KUB xxiii 102 i 5–19). This refusal to concede diplomatic parity was consistent with Hittite tradition but left Hatti's eastern flank unresolved.

## Overthrow

The relationship between Urhi-Teshub and his uncle deteriorated as the king began acting independently and stripping Hattusili of his powers. Over seven years the uncle submitted; then Urhi-Teshub moved to take Hakpis and Nerik from him — the last remaining bases of Hattusili's authority. Hattusili described this as the breaking point: "For seven years I submitted. But at a divine command and with human urging, Urhi-Teshub sought to destroy me. He took Hakpis and Nerik from me. Now I submitted to him no longer" (Apol. §10c, iii 62–79).

Hattusili framed the conflict as a legal-divine contest rather than outright rebellion: "You are a Great King, while I am a small king. Let us go in judgement before the Storm God My Lord and Shaushga (Ishtar) of Samuha My Lady." Urhi-Teshub marched north into the Upper Land with Sippaziti tasked to raise troops. Sippaziti failed; Hattusili gathered substantial forces including Kaska allies and disaffected Hittite nobles. Urhi-Teshub was trapped at [[hattusha|Samuha]], besieged "like a pig in a sty," and forced to surrender. He was deposed and returned to Hattusa as his uncle's prisoner.

Hattusili excluded Urhi-Teshub's descendants from the succession explicitly: "Do not take anyone of other descent. No-one should look for a son of Urhi-Teshub" (KUB xxi 37 10–14).

## Exile and Flight

Rather than execute the deposed king, Hattusili sent him into honourable exile in the Nuhashshi lands of Syria, where he was given administrative responsibility over a number of fortified cities — partly to keep him occupied, partly relying on the restored vassal Benteshina of Amurru to watch him. The arrangement proved a catastrophic miscalculation.

Urhi-Teshub quickly began surreptitious dealings with the Babylonians and may have opened communications with the new Assyrian king Shalmaneser I (c.1263–1234) — almost certainly seeking foreign backing to restore his claim. On receiving intelligence of these contacts, Hattusili ordered his transfer to a coastal location or to the Land of Alasiya (Cyprus). Urhi-Teshub eluded his custodians and made his way to the court of [[ramesses-ii]], pharaoh of [[new-kingdom-egypt]].

Hattusili wrote to Ramesses demanding extradition. Ramesses refused, as Hattusili described in a letter to the Babylonian king Kadashman-Enlil II: "My enemy who fled to another country went to the king of Egypt. When I wrote to him 'Bring my enemy', he did not bring my enemy. Then I and the king of Egypt became enemies of one another" (KBo i 10 + KUB iii 72, obv. 67–9). Urhi-Teshub's presence in Egypt complicated negotiations for the [[egyptian-hittite-treaty-1259bce]]. Ramesses found the deposed king a useful repository of intelligence about Hatti.

Eventually Urhi-Teshub appears to have left Egypt — perhaps sensing his usefulness was ending — and made his way back toward Syria. Both Egyptian and Hittite searches failed to locate him. Ramesses wrote: "[He has flown like a bird]" (KBo i 15 + 19 rev. 22–5). Hattusili, in a striking reversal, then asked Ramesses to recapture Urhi-Teshub and keep him in Egypt rather than extradite him — calculating that remote Egyptian exile was safer than freedom within the former king's own territory. Urhi-Teshub apparently still commanded substantial popular support: "the very fact that Urhi-Teshub succeeded in eluding the most determined efforts by his pursuers...suggests that he did indeed have many supporters willing to shelter him" (Bryce). He may have established a kingdom in exile in southern Anatolia. He remained alive at the close of Hattusili's reign and had sons who might renew claims to the throne.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Hattusili allowed Urhi-Teshub to remain in Hattusa — or executed him — the persistent legitimacy crisis that shadowed the entire reign of Hattusili III would have been avoided or resolved. The decision to send him into honourable Syrian exile was the decisive error: it gave the deposed king proximity to foreign courts and scope for diplomatic activity. Urhi-Teshub's flight to Egypt directly complicated the Egyptian-Hittite rapprochement, and his continued freedom forced Hattusili to invest heavy diplomatic capital in persuading foreign rulers that Hatti had a single, legitimate sovereign. The Bronze tablet treaties granting Kurunta extraordinary privileges were also partly driven by the need to neutralize Muwattalli's other son while Urhi-Teshub remained at large.

## Historiography

Our knowledge of Urhi-Teshub derives almost entirely from hostile sources: the *Apology of Hattusili III* (CTH 81), which is "a largely self-laudatory and self-justificatory account" and "presents a very one-sided view of Hattusili's actions and achievements, and those of his enemies" (Bryce); and various administrative texts whose provenance and dating are disputed. A curious text recording Urhi-Teshub's reversals of his father's decisions may have been composed by Hattusili to highlight the "offences" of his predecessor and justify the coup. Bryce (2005) argues that Urhi-Teshub's counter-measures against Hattusili — stripping his powers — were not mere jealousy but rational moves to neutralize a dangerous rival whose base of support had grown threatening. The historical record of his reign was subject to revision by his successors; positive actions were credited to Hattusili, negatives attributed to him.
