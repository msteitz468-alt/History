# Paul Revere

## Overview

**Paul Revere** (1734–1818), Boston silversmith, engraver, and courier, was craft-elite
infrastructure for the resistance network around [[adams-samuel|Samuel Adams]]:
Massacre propaganda, intercolonial express rides, nightly military surveillance, and
the April 1775 warnings that saved Adams and [[hancock-john|John Hancock]].
[[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Stacy Schiff]] (2022) stresses his speed,
loyalty, and role in keeping “the people’s minds focused,” while noting how few
written traces survive—Adams destroyed papers, and only two notes between the men
remain despite constant collaboration.

## Role

Artisan-propagandist and trusted messenger rather than doctrinaire leader. Member of
the Long Room Club over his shop (with Warren, [[otis-james|Otis]], Adams, and
others); agreed early to the 1767 luxury boycott when Hancock held back. Belonged to
a thirty-man Boston surveillance team that patrolled nightly, swearing on a Bible to
report largely to Warren, Adams, and Hancock—intelligence that impressed Gage.
Goldsmith’s range included false teeth as well as silver and prints.

## Career

**Massacre image (1770).** Lent Pelham’s engraving, then issued his own hand-colored
version on sale before the original; relabeled the customs house “Butcher’s Hall”
and inserted a second-floor musket with smoke puffs. Pelham accused him of cheating
“as truly as if you had plundered me on the highway.” Schiff: accuracy was not the
point—Revere aligned the image with Adams’s version of the
[[boston-massacre-1770|Massacre]]. On the first anniversary (5 March 1771), thousands
flocked to Revere’s North End home for a brilliantly illuminated triptych; “the stage
management was all Adams’s.”

**Tea Party express (December 1773).** Morning after the
[[boston-tea-party-1773|Tea Party]], Adams dispatched Revere to New York and
Philadelphia with the news; despite foul weather he made “record-shattering time,”
with accounts in print within a week. Later rides carried Suffolk Resolves and other
urgent packets—Schiff’s Adams uses Revere as the human telegraph of New England
extremism and its reception abroad.

**Lexington night (18–19 April 1775).** Two days earlier Revere had ridden in daylight
to confer with patriot leaders and, on return, arranged North Church lantern signals
(one by land, two by sea). After midnight on the 18th, on a borrowed horse after a
near-encounter with officers, he clattered up to the Lexington parsonage ~12:30 a.m.;
guards barred the way—he reportedly huffed “Noise! You’ll have noise enough before
long” and “The regulars are coming out” (closest he came to the later slogan). With
Dawes and others he later rode into a British ambush midway to Concord; of the party
he alone had a pistol aimed at his head. Sent a third time that evening for Hancock’s
trunk of papers, he heard but did not see the “continual roar of musketry.” He waited
twenty-three years to set down the full arrest story; Longfellow (1860) later trapped
him “in tetrameter, a mythic figure.”

**Legend after the war.** Lived until 1818. Legend holds that Green Dragon tradesmen
overflow, reported by Revere, helped secure Adams’s support for the Constitution:
“More… than the Green Dragon could hold.”

## Counterfactual significance

Without Revere’s engraving and anniversary spectacle, Massacre memory would have been
less visual and less standardized; without his express network, intercolonial
coordination after Tea Party and Suffolk Resolves would have been slower; without the
Lexington ride, Adams and Hancock might have been seized—altering the opening
political symbolism of the war even if fighting still erupted.

## Historiography

Schiff (2022) integrates Revere as Adams’s operational arm—print, ride, watch—while
crediting Longfellow for myth. Revere’s own 1790s deposition remains the primary
corrective to the poem. Collection note: page grounded in Schiff; dedicated Revere
biographies (Fischer *Paul Revere’s Ride*, etc.) not yet ingested.
