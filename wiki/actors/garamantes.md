# The Garamantes

> **GHA II note.** The UNESCO [[gha-vol2-ancient-civilizations|General History of Africa, Vol. II]] (ch. 20)
> treats the Garamantes as a Saharan **[[berbers|Libyco-Berber]]** people and pioneers of the early
> **[[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan routes]]** — a mixed population the classical authors counted among
> the desert "Ethiopians."

## Overview

The **Garamantes** of the **Fezzan** (southern Libya) were, in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]
(Burstein, Ch 23; Austen, Ch 24), the **first state to arise within the Sahara itself** — a
desert kingdom that Roman writers dismissed as raiders but that archaeology has revealed as a
sophisticated irrigation civilization and the likely **precursor of trans-Saharan trade**.

## A Desert State

References to the Garamantes run through Greek and Roman texts from the fifth century BCE
(Herodotus) into late antiquity. Recent excavations (under David Mattingly) at the heartland of
the **Wadi al-Ajal** and the capital **Garama** show that, in the first three centuries CE, the
kingdom occupied some **250,000 km²**, used the (still-undeciphered) **Libyan script**,
supported a population of **50,000–100,000**, and sustained a massive agricultural system
through **foggara** — underground irrigation channels tapping fossil aquifers. The Garamantes
**imported large quantities of luxury goods from Roman North Africa**, wealth far exceeding
what their exports of semi-precious "carbuncles" could explain.

## Slaving and the Trans-Saharan Question

Herodotus describes the Garamantes using **chariots to chase the "Troglodyte Ethiopians,"** and
inscriptions record them delivering captives to Roman frontier posts. CWH IV argues (with
Andrew Wilson) that the Garamantes likely needed and traded large numbers of **slaves** — to
maintain their irrigation works and to sell north — making them the strongest candidate for an
early, smaller-scale **[[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan trade]]** centuries before the
Islamic caravans (see [[pre-islamic-trans-saharan-trade]]). Their fortifications and watchtowers
align with the later caravan routes toward the Niger Bend.

## Decline and Legacy

From the early 300s CE the foggara system faltered (climate, aquifer exhaustion, or overuse),
settlements were fortified, and the kingdom decayed amid Berber raiding and the collapse of
Roman order. After the **Arab conquest** of the Fezzan, the Berber **Mazata** took over the old
Garamantian capital of **Jarma** and the new entrepôt of **Zawila**, "reinventing and
expanding" the cross-desert trade that the Garamantes had pioneered.

## Significance

The Garamantes overturn the image of the pre-Islamic Sahara as an empty barrier. They were an
indigenous **state, irrigation society, and trade broker** straddling the Mediterranean and
sub-Saharan worlds — the institutional bridge that later [[trans-saharan-trade|Islamic caravan
commerce]] would inherit.
