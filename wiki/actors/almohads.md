# The Almohads (al-Muwahhidun)

The **Almohads** (Arabic *al-muwahhidun*, "those who affirm the unity [of God]") were
the second Berber empire to rule the Maghreb and Muslim Iberia, succeeding the
[[almoravids|Almoravids]] in the mid-twelfth century. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]]
(Hugh Kennedy, Ch. 16) contrasts them with their predecessors as a movement of
**religious** as well as military leadership.

## Origins and Empire

The movement was founded among the **Masmuda Berbers** of the Atlas by **Ibn Tumart**
(d. c. 1130), a charismatic reformer who proclaimed himself the *mahdi* and preached a
rigorous doctrine of divine unity (*tawhid*) against what he condemned as the
anthropomorphism and laxity of the Almoravids. Under his successor **Abd al-Mu'min**,
the Almohads conquered the Almoravid Maghreb — taking **Marrakesh in 1147** — and built
an empire stretching from Tripolitania to the Atlantic, the first to unite the whole of
North Africa west of Egypt under a single indigenous power.

## Rule in al-Andalus

Crossing into Spain after the collapse of Almoravid authority and a second taifa
period, the Almohads reunited al-Andalus under their rule and defeated Castile at
**Alarcos (1195)**, a serious Christian reverse. But a coalition of the Iberian
Christian kingdoms, summoned as a [[crusades|crusade]] by the papacy, decisively broke
Almohad power at **Las Navas de Tolosa (1212)** — the turning point that opened the
great thirteenth-century [[reconquista|reconquests]] (Córdoba 1236, Seville 1248). The
Almohad empire then fragmented, surviving in Morocco until the Marinids took Marrakesh
in 1269.

## Culture

Despite their rigorism, the Almohad period saw a high point of Andalusi intellectual
life: the philosopher **Ibn Rushd (Averroes, 1126–98)** and the physician-philosopher
**Ibn Tufayl** served the Almohad court, and Averroes' Aristotelian commentaries —
written in Almohad Córdoba and Seville — would shape the Latin
[[twelfth-century-renaissance|universities]]. The Almohads were also patrons of
monumental architecture (the Giralda of Seville, the Kutubiyya of Marrakesh).

## The Collapse (NCMH V)

[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Michael Brett, Ch. 17c; David Abulafia, Ch. 17b) traces the
**collapse** after **Las Navas de Tolosa (1212)**. Brett stresses that the empire had "two feet
of clay" — subjects divided by religion, region, tribe, and the townsman/nomad split, held
together only by allegiance to the caliph. The defeat broke the army (the caliph an-Nasir "barely
escaped" and died in disgrace); succession disputes, Arab tribal insubordination, and the
repudiation of Ibn Tumart's doctrine shattered the regime in the 1220s–1230s. The empire
fragmented into the **[[maghrib-successor-states|tripartite Maghrib]]** — **Hafsids** (Tunis),
**Ziyanids** (Tlemcen), **Marinids** (Morocco) — while **al-Andalus** fell away: **Córdoba**
fell to Castile (1236) and the Christian advance left little Muslim Spain beyond
**[[nasrid-granada|Nasrid Granada]]**.

## Significance

The Almohads were the last great Berber empire to dominate Iberia; their defeat at Las
Navas de Tolosa (1212) marked the irreversible turning of the [[reconquista|Reconquista]]
in the Christians' favour and the beginning of the end of al-Andalus. Their fall removed the
**North African counterweight** that had twice reversed the reconquest, and their fragmentation
gave European merchants greater access to the Saharan gold trade.

## Cobb: rise, Andalusi high-water, and contrast with Saladin

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|*The Race for Paradise*]] names the Almohads (*al-Muwaḥḥidūn*,
“those who affirm Divine Unity”). **Ibn Tumart** (claimed study with Turtushi in Alexandria)
challenged Almoravids as backsliders; 1121 proclaimed Mahdi; ~decade Moroccan war; failed
Marrakesh assault 1130, died that year; successor **ʿAbd al-Muʾmin** as “caliph”; by the
1140s rebuilt; took Fez then **Marrakesh 1147**; invited to al-Andalus by Ibn Qasi (Silves);
spring 1147 crossed to Tarifa/Algeciras, took the Algarve then Seville as capital. Concurrent
Second Crusade–linked Iberian operations (Lisbon 1147 with English-route crusader fleet;
Almería 17 Oct 1147 by Genoese/Catalans/Alfonso VII — Almohads retook 1157; Tortosa Dec
1148; Lérida Nov 1149) showed Iberian kings integrating European crusading resources while
Almohads remained relatively isolated from broader *dār al-islām*. Norman North Africa fell
to Almohad reconquest: Sfax 1156, Tripoli 1158, Tunis July 1159 (illegal convert-or-die for
non-Muslims reported), Mahdiya Jan 1160.

**Consolidation after 1150.** Granada 1154; Almería retaken 1157 (Alfonso VII died on
retreat); ʿAbd al-Muʾmin base at Gibraltar 1160; Arab nomad army for Andalusi jihad; Córdoba
re-centered as symbolic caliphal capital (bureaucracy from Seville; city nearly empty ruin).
Yusuf I vs “Wolf-King” Ibn Mardanish; Badajoz 1170 with Fernando II of León vs Portuguese;
Andalusi war enthusiasm depended on the caliph’s presence. Yusuf died 1184 after failed
Santarém. **al-Mansur:** Silves lost 1189 to Sancho of Portugal with Third Crusade en-route
volunteers, retaken 1191; **Alarcos 1195** vs Alfonso VIII high-water mark. **al-Nasir**
defeated **16 July 1212** at al-ʿIqab / Las Navas de Tolosa — Almohad power effectively
ended; one chronicler blames unpaid troops’ intentional non-fight.

**Jihad and structural comparison.** As with Saljuqs and Ayyubids in the east, jihad was for
Almohads one aspect of broader social reform and state legitimation (building, fortification,
policing orthodoxy — e.g. trial of Averroes/Ibn Rushd at Córdoba under al-Mansur). Cobb
contrasts Almohad failure with [[saladin|Saladin]]’s better structural position: Franks in
Syria more isolated from European support; Saladin had Baghdad caliph backing and had
subdued Muslim foes; “The Almohads never had such luxuries,” hence less satisfying
anti-Frank results.

## Persecution of Jews and Christians (Cohen)

[[cohen-under-crescent-cross-1994|Mark Cohen's]] *Under Crescent and Cross* treats the
Almohad regime as the great **exception** to the classical Islamic norm of relatively
secure [[dhimmi|dhimmī]] status — and as the single most severe persecution of Jews
under medieval Islam. From the **mid-1140s**, the Almohads abolished the dhimma
altogether and offered Jews and Christians the choice of **conversion to Islam or
death**; most chose conversion. The persecution:

- shattered the Jewish communities of the Maghrib and al-Andalus, killing many, forcing
  mass (often nominal) conversion, and sending thousands fleeing to Christian territory
  or to Muslim lands beyond Almohad reach — among the refugees, the family of
  [[maimonides|Maimonides]];
- at **Sijilmasa (1148)**, per the eyewitness-derived letter of Solomon ha-Kohen,
  produced ~150 Jewish deaths (*[[kiddush-ha-shem|kiddush ha-Shem]]*) alongside many
  apostasies (that letter's far larger claimed tolls — 100,000 at Fez, 120,000 at
  Marrakesh — are contemporary rhetorical figures, not corroborated);
- recurred in a **second wave under Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb al-Manṣūr (r. 1184–99)**, targeting descendants of the first forced converts suspected (often
  rightly) of secret Judaism.

Cohen notes the Almohad catastrophe elicited one of the very few Hebrew persecution
elegies from the Islamic world — Abraham ibn Ezra's *"Aha Yarad ʿal Sefarad"* — and
helped generate the refugee saying "**Better under Edom [Christendom] than under Ishmael
[Islam]**," marking the erosion of the classical dhimma equilibrium. See
[[jews-under-islam-myth-and-countermyth]] and [[jewry-islamic-world]].

## The African / Maghribi view (GHA IV)

The **UNESCO [[gha-vol4-12th-16th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. IV]]** (chs. 2–5, Saidi, Talbi,
Hrbek, Idris) frames the Almohad century as the **apogee of Maghribi-Islamic civilization** — a North
*African* empire (not merely an appendage of al-Andalus) whose unification of the Maghrib, urban
florescence, and patronage of thought (Averroes, Ibn Tufayl) marked the region's high point before
fragmentation into the **[[maghrib-successor-states|tripartite Maghrib]]** (Hafsids, Ziyanids, Marinids).
GHA also stresses the Almohads' stake in the **[[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan gold trade]]**: control
of the desert termini and the West African gold that flowed through them underwrote Maghribi prosperity and
tied the Almohad world to the empire of [[mali-empire|Mali]] rising to the south.
