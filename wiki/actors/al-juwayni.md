## Overview

**Imām al-Ḥaramayn** ("Imām of the Two Holy Cities") was the honorific title of Abū al-Maʿālī ʿAbd al-Malik al-Juwaynī (1028–1085 CE), the most prominent Ashʿarī [[kalam|kalām]] scholar of the generation before [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]], whose teacher he was. Born in Juwayn in Khurāsān, he brought the Ashʿarī tradition to a high point of rational sophistication, preparing the ground — inadvertently — for its supersession by al-Ghazālī's more radical re-evaluation.

## Biography

His father had been head of the Shāfiʿī legal madhhab in Nīshāpūr; when the father died, the eighteen-year-old al-Juwaynī succeeded him at the madrasa — demonstrating exceptional gifts already evident. He had also studied with an Ashʿarī teacher, giving him dual credentials in Shāfiʿī fiqh and Ashʿarī kalām.

He was forced to leave Nīshāpūr when the Seljuk vizier al-Kundurī ordered a ban on all Muʿtazilī teaching — in practice extending to other kalām as well. Al-Juwaynī took refuge in Mecca and Medina (hence the honorary title "Imām of the Two Holy Cities"), where he gained an exceptional reputation despite still being in his twenties. When Niẓāmulmulk replaced al-Kundurī as vizier under Alp-Arslān, scholars of kalām were readily restored to favour. Al-Juwaynī returned and received appointment at Niẓāmulmulk's new Niẓāmiyyah madrasa in Nīshāpūr, where he taught until his death — with [[al-ghazali|Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī]] as his most brilliant student.

## Intellectual Achievement

### Sophistication and Its Limit

Al-Juwaynī's achievement was to bring Ashʿarī kalām to mature philosophical sophistication by engaging seriously — though not always explicitly — with the logical and metaphysical standards of the Falsafah tradition. His work shows "a degree of sophisticated detail to which the disputation on every controverted point had been refined," and "categories [of the Faylasūfs] were everywhere present" (Hodgson, *Venture of Islam*, Vol. 2).

He established the subsequent standard form of an Ashʿarī treatise, introducing serious philosophical prolegomena on abstract reasoning and showing interest in the three-term Aristotelian syllogism (though in practice he used the more convenient two-term form customary in kalām).

### The Problem of God's Omnipotence

The old Ashʿarī doctrine of *kasb* ("acquisition") — that humans morally acquired their acts while God remained their sole real cause — was, al-Juwaynī acknowledged, "unintelligible" by rationalistic standards. His solution sought a middle position satisfying both Ḥadīthī insistence on God as sole real agent and Muʿtazilī insistence on human moral responsibility. Hodgson notes that in substance this returned to earlier Muʿtazilī positions that the Ḥadīth folk's pressure had forced modification of.

### The Paradox of Kalām's Triumph

Hodgson identifies a structural irony in al-Juwaynī's work: "in the very moment of its triumph, in the act of perfecting its own tradition, the Ashʿarī kalâm was near losing sight of its very purpose." The original kerygmatic force of kalām — defending the unique revelatory significance of specific historical events (Muhammad's prophethood, the Qurʾān) — was being replaced by a rationalistic spirit that could receive "its fullest expression only in Falsafah or its equivalent."

Al-Juwaynī, for instance, could no longer see why the doctrine of "commanding the right and forbidding the wrong" had ever been given primary kalām status alongside divine unity and prophethood. For earlier scholars, this had concerned the historical commitment of the faithful; for al-Juwaynī it seemed merely a minor rule of fiqh. The progressive rationalization was subtly undermining the kerygmatic foundation it was meant to defend.

## Legacy

Al-Juwaynī "dominated the Ashʿarī school of his time" — his formulations became the standard. More importantly, he trained [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]], whose more radical re-evaluation of kalām's limits would prove the next generation's defining intellectual event.
