# Richard II, King of England (r. 1377–1399)

**Richard II**, grandson of [[edward-iii-of-england|Edward III]], is the late-medieval English king
whose attempt to escape the fate of his deposed great-grandfather [[edward-ii-of-england|Edward
II]] led him, [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Caroline M. Barron, Ch. 11b) argues, to "provoke his
great magnates into taking exactly that course which he most feared." His deposition in 1399 closed
the direct Plantagenet line and opened the Lancastrian usurpation.

## A King of the Wrong Abilities

Barron presents Richard as a man of "strong character" — brave, loyal, intelligent, with "long-term
objectives" — but lacking "certain qualities which were essential for medieval kings": he "did not
enjoy real war and... got on badly with the English nobility as a group." Medieval monarchy "was
not yet sufficiently developed to carry incompetent kings," and "Richard II had the wrong abilities
for his inherited task." His suspicious, secretive temper and his exalted, sacral conception of
kingship bred insecurity in those around him.

## A Reign of Conflict and Experiment

The reign was crowded with novelty: the great **Peasants' Revolt of 1381** (the young king facing
the rebels at Smithfield); the attacks of **John Wyclif and the Lollards** on the Church; the rise
of a competitive London **merchant class**; the development of the **[[english-parliament|Commons]]**
as the voice of urban and gentry interests; and the "meteoric expansion in the use of written
English" in business and politics. Endemic plague held the population down, raising real wages and
spreading prosperity, while churches rose in the "quintessentially English **Perpendicular
Style**." It was, Barron writes, "a period of opportunity, competition and experiment."

## Crisis and Deposition

Richard's quarrels with his magnates produced the **Appellant crisis (1387–1388)**, when the Lords
Appellant purged his court in the "Merciless Parliament." After a decade of apparent reconciliation,
Richard struck back in his "tyranny" of 1397–1399, destroying the Appellants and ruling
arbitrarily. His banishment of **Henry Bolingbroke** and seizure of the Lancastrian inheritance
provoked Bolingbroke's invasion (1399); Richard was deposed and imprisoned, dying at Pontefract in
1400. Bolingbroke took the throne as **Henry IV**, founding the **Lancastrian** dynasty.

## Significance

Richard's fall — the second deposition of the century after Edward II's — demonstrated again that
late-medieval English kingship depended on the king's ability to manage the political community, and
that even a king with "long-term objectives" could not impose a sacral absolutism against a united
nobility. The Lancastrian usurpation he provoked set up the dynastic instability that would
culminate in the Wars of the Roses.
