## Overview

Thule culture was the immediate ancestor of all modern Inuit peoples. It originated on the northwest coast of Alaska around 900–1000 CE and then spread eastward across the entire Arctic, reaching Greenland and displacing or absorbing the [[dorset-culture]] people by around 1100 CE. Thule's rapid expansion — at least 15 km (10 mi) per year, covering as much as 3,200 km (2,000 mi) in only a century and a half — was enabled by superior technology, particularly whale-hunting equipment and efficient dog sleds, and was facilitated by the warmer climate of the Medieval Maximum.

## Geographic Distribution

Thule spread from its origin on the northwest Alaskan coast eastward across northern Canada to Greenland and Newfoundland. At its greatest extent, Thule populations occupied the entire Arctic coastal strip, including the High Arctic of Ellesmere Island (the northernmost point in Canada), Banks Island, the Kazan River region of Nunavut, and coastal Greenland. The southernmost extension reached Newfoundland on Canada's east coast. The Inuit descendants of the Thule persist in their Arctic homelands today; the territory of Nunavut (created in 1999 and covering 1,877,787 km²) is a contemporary Inuit homeland with deep Thule roots.

## Dating and Chronology

Thule culture is placed in Arctic Period 5, spanning approximately 600 (±500) CE to 1850 CE. The culture originated c. 900–1000 CE in northwestern Alaska. Eastward expansion across the Canadian Arctic occurred rapidly after 1000 CE. Collision with and absorption of [[dorset-culture]] was largely complete by 1100 CE. The onset of the Little Ice Age around 1400 CE forced significant adaptations: some bands abandoned the use of umiaks and large kayaks, shifting from open-water summer hunting of bowhead whales to winter ice hunting of ringed seals. Polar Inuit in northern Greenland became so isolated during this period that European explorers in the nineteenth century encountered them with mutual astonishment.

Dating method: radiocarbon.

## Economy and Subsistence

Thule was fundamentally a maritime hunting culture. People hunted sea mammals of all kinds — seals, walrus, and most significantly bowhead whales — from kayaks and umiaks in open water and through sea ice. The bowhead whale may have been the primary attraction drawing Thule hunters eastward through the Canadian Arctic, as whale populations there were abundant during the Medieval Maximum. Thule hunters also pushed up rivers into interior forests to fish and hunt caribou seasonally. This flexibility — maritime hunting combined with interior foraging — served them well as climate conditions changed. When the Medieval Maximum gave way to the Little Ice Age after 1400 CE, some bands shifted emphasis from open-water whale hunting to ice hunting of ringed seals.

## Architecture and Technology

Thule technology was the most sophisticated of all Arctic cultures. Houses were highly variable, constructed from whatever local materials were available. Soapstone (steatite) oil lamps provided heat and light sufficient to make winter life possible even in igloos — the lamps were more reliable than the cruder pottery ones sometimes used by predecessors. Artificial light from soapstone lamps enabled carving, sewing, and other indoor tasks during the long Arctic winter nights.

Key technological innovations:
- **Toggling harpoon**: Attached to a long tether, designed to turn sideways in a wounded animal after detaching from the foreshaft, preventing escape. Large sealskin floats tied midway along the tether forced a wounded seal or walrus to pull against the float rather than the kayak, allowing the hunter to wait for exhaustion and blood loss.
- **Kayak**: A closed one-person skin boat providing an ideal hunting platform for sea mammal hunting with harpoons and sealskin floats.
- **Umiak**: A large open skin boat for transporting whole families, food, equipment, and supplies during open-water months.
- **Dog sled**: Thule dog sleds of efficient built-up design, running on iced runners of bone or ivory, are still used in modern dogsled racing. Dogs were harnessed in a fanlike arc for open sea ice travel or in tandem pairs for narrow trail travel.

Other tools included: specialized harpoon forms, soapstone lamps, snow goggles, labrets, wound plugs (to stop blood loss from game animals), needle cases, wrist guards, harness toggles, and a vast array of gadgetry from bone, antler, fiber, feathers, gut, skin, copper, wood, baleen, chert, and meteoric iron.

## Society and Identity

Each Thule house was typically built for a nuclear family or small extended family. Males and females had significant complementary roles; age-graded roles were also defined by custom. Bands depended on mutual support for insurance, marriage partners, and cooperative undertakings. Band membership was fluid — bands could split, shrink, or grow as demographics required.

The rapid spread of Thule culture from west to east across the Arctic produced a string of closely related Inuit–Iñupiaq dialects spoken from the Bering Sea to eastern Greenland. Speakers of adjacent dialects communicate easily, but those at opposite ends of the continuum are mutually unintelligible. The deep split between Eskimo-Aleut branches reflects the much earlier divergence of Aleutian populations; the shallower Inuit–Iñupiaq dialect continuum reflects the rapid Thule expansion.

## Art and Symbolism

Thule artisans continued and elaborated the artistic tradition of earlier Arctic cultures. Tools were often beautifully decorated, reflecting northern mythology and the natural world. Labrets, lip plugs, and clothing were used for more than protection — fine stitching, waterproofing treatments, dyed threads, and featherwork ornamentation all express aesthetic sensibilities beyond mere function. Long winter confinements promoted games and pastimes: cat's cradle, cup-and-ball, singing, storytelling, dancing, wrestling, and (in larger gatherings) the blanket toss.

## Decline and Legacy

Thule culture did not decline as such — it transformed into modern Inuit culture. The onset of the Little Ice Age (~1400 CE) forced significant adaptive changes: retreat from parts of the former range, abandonment of open-water whale hunting in favor of ice seal hunting, and the isolation of some bands (notably the Polar Inuit of northern Greenland). Some bands died out entirely. Where conditions remained viable, Thule adaptations persisted into modern times and continued through European contact. The Inuit descendants of the Thule today manage Nunavut and other northern territories; Fall Caribou Crossing National Historic Site on the Kazan River (Nunavut) documents continuous Thule-ancestry Inuit use from pre-contact times through the historic era (c. 1650–1920 CE).

## Historiography

Thule culture is one of the best-studied Arctic cultures due to the preservation of organic materials in permafrost and the availability of oral traditions connecting archaeological findings to modern Inuit communities. Radiocarbon dating has been crucial in establishing the rapid pace of Thule expansion and correcting earlier assumptions (e.g., the Head Hill site on Banks Island was assumed to date only from 1853–1895 CE due to presence of salvaged wood and iron from HMS Investigator; radiocarbon dating of muskox bones showed occupations spanning c. 1650–1920 CE). The collection bias of the source is strong for Thule relative to earlier Arctic cultures; the relationship between Thule and the Dorset populations it replaced remains a subject of debate (displacement vs. absorption vs. admixture). Genetic research is increasingly informing these questions.
