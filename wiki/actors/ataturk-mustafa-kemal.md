## Overview

Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (1881–1938) was the founder of the Republic of Turkey and the leader who carried [[ottoman-westernization]] to its most radical conclusion: the identification of Modernization with Westernization, the abolition of Islamic institutions as public entities, and the construction of a Turkish national identity defined linguistically and territorially rather than religiously or imperially. Born in Salonika (Thessaloniki), he rose through the Ottoman officer corps, became a national hero defending Gallipoli (1915), and then led the successful war of national resistance against the Allied partition of Anatolia (1919–22). The political and cultural revolution he imposed thereafter — abolition of the sultanate, the caliphate, the Sufi orders, the madrasa system, Sharia law, the fez, and the Arabic script — constitutes the most thoroughgoing Westernization of any Muslim society.

Hodgson frames Kemal as the answer to the question: "what does coming to terms with Modernity mean?" His answer was unequivocal: "to become Modern was to join the West as one nation among other Western nations, sharing unreservedly in whatever was Western, for better or for worse."

## Military Career

Kemal distinguished himself as a divisional commander at the Gallipoli campaign (1915), successfully repelling the British naval assault on the Dardanelles — an action that made him a national hero while his political rivals (notably Enver Pasha, the Young Turk leader) kept him on peripheral fronts. In 1918 he was placed in command of the beaten Ottoman armies retreating through Syria and kept them intact through the armistice — ensuring Turkey retained an organized military force when the peace settlement threatened to eliminate Turkish sovereignty in Anatolia.

## War of Independence (1919–22)

When the Allies allowed Greek forces to occupy Izmir (Smyrna) and begin expanding into western Anatolia — with Greek and Turkish atrocities on both sides — and when the sultan's government appeared to acquiesce in the partition, Kemal defied the Istanbul government, assumed command of nationalist forces in central Anatolia, and called the Grand National Assembly at Ankara. He fought four simultaneous campaigns: against the sultan's "armies of the caliphate," against the Armenian Republic (which was pushed back to the former Russian frontier), against French forces in northern Syria (who were eventually persuaded to limit their aims), and finally against the Greeks. The Turkish counteroffensive of 1922 expelled the Greek forces from Izmir and Anatolia "amid massacres, in turn, of Greeks by Turks" and produced the historic exchange of populations: Greeks (Christians) from Turkey and Turks (Muslims) from Greece — "perhaps more than any other circumstance, that confirmed the identification of religion with ethnic nationality."

A new peace treaty eliminated all "the apparatus of Occidental privilege, intervention, and control which had grown up in the nineteenth century" — extra-territoriality, Capitulations, foreign financial controls.

## Political Construction of the Republic

The Grand National Assembly accepted the accomplished fact of a republic. Kemal secured his position through the Republican People's Party, whose membership rolls he controlled and which alone could make effective nominations for the Assembly. The Party was "more than an electoral device; it was a society for propagating the reforms," operating "people's houses" (*halk evleri*) as community centres, libraries, and adult education schools.

The republic's principles were formalized as **six words**:
1. **Republicanism** — elective constitutional government
2. **Nationalism** — specifically Turkish national culture
3. **Populism** — dignity and needs of the common people, including universal adult suffrage and women's rights
4. **Étatism** — state responsibility for economic development (adopted somewhat later, partly because Turkish capital had been concentrated in non-Muslim hands who had mostly left)
5. **Laicism** — rejection of communal religious privilege; Islam as private matter
6. **Revolutionism (Reformism)** — continuing adoption of the new over the merely traditional

## The Religious Revolution

Kemal's disestablishment of Islam was systematic:

- **Caliphate abolished** (1924) — the pan-Islamic fiction was eliminated.
- **Sufi orders suppressed** — all tariqa orders banned, properties seized; khanakahs turned into museums; Sufi ceremonies driven underground.
- **Madrasa colleges closed** — Sharia training ceased; "the authority of what was regarded as the relatively sober and dependable aspect of Islam declined" as no new trained men appeared.
- **Sharia law replaced** — entirely superseded by European codes, especially Swiss personal law.
- **Call to prayer in Turkish** — Arabic replaced by Turkish in mosque worship (later reversed).

Kemal himself was an unbeliever, but "on this level, even Kemal, unbeliever as he was, was loyal to the Muslim community as such" — he would not let a Muslim-born girl marry an infidel, and the population exchange with Greece was organized on religious rather than strictly linguistic lines.

Islam was not destroyed but transformed in unexpected ways: the Sharia-minded tradition withered as the madrasa system collapsed, but Sufi Islam "could be reaffirmed and perhaps even strengthened spiritually" because suppression of outward forms could not prevent a *pir* from conversing with disciples in private. After World War II, when religious expression was again permitted, "the vitality of Islam seemed to lie chiefly on the Sufi side."

## The Hat Law and Alphabet Reform

Two gestures especially: the **hat law of 1925** and the **alphabet reform of 1928**.

The brimmed hat was "explicitly Occidental" — and in Turkish idiom "to put on a hat" had meant "to Europeanize, that is, 'to desert Islam, or the state' (which came to the same)." The law forced a psychological crisis: "Either they must resist now, or acknowledge defeat and henceforth hold their peace." Those most disposed to resist did so and were crushed; the rest were implicitly committed to Westernization.

The Latin alphabet replaced the Perso-Arabic script. Beyond phonetic convenience, it severed the younger generation from Ottoman literary heritage and made the reform "psychologically complete and irreversible." Combined with language reform (purging Persianisms, Arabisms; replacing them with Turkic roots or French/Latin borrowings), the alphabet change completed the civilizational shift: younger Turks could no longer readily follow the vocabulary of the old literature even transcribed into new letters.

## Legacy and Limits

By Kemal's death in 1938, Kemalism was "well established among the great majority of the educated youth." Turkey was the first Muslim state to achieve a sovereign Westernization (the only comparator is Japan). But the completeness of the answer carried its own problems. Islam did not disappear; after WWII the religious question became "the great public question." A tariqa order that had been conservative and pro-French in the Maghrib became, on arrival in Turkey, "a spearhead for a religious drive to undo the work of Atatürk." The secularism Atatürk had installed had not introduced genuine religious liberty; the Sharia-minded, when allowed to re-organize, took for granted that the terms of Ottoman religious tolerance should apply — some even questioning whether Shia Muslims could be tolerated. "Eventually one of the most active points of debate became just the question of religious liberty, which neither Atatürk's state nor the Sufi leadership had made familiar to the bulk of the population."

Whether Kemalism was "tentative or superficial" or genuinely adequate to the challenge was still an open question at the time of Hodgson's writing.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Kemal: the Turkish nationalist movement would have lacked its decisive military commander at Gallipoli (the most credible claim to a counterfactual gap); without that victory, and his subsequent leadership of the Anatolian resistance, the peace settlement might have eliminated or reduced Turkish sovereignty to a rump. A Turkish state without Kemal's authority and determination might have attempted a more moderate Westernization (on the model of Egypt or Iran), retaining Islamic institutional forms while adopting some Western practices — with unpredictable consequences for the subsequent Turkish-European alignment.
