# Hiero I of Syracuse

## Overview

**Hiero I** (r. c. 478–467 BCE), brother and successor of [[gelon]], was the second of the
**[[western-greek-tyranny|Deinomenid]]** tyrants of [[syracuse]] — victor over the Etruscans
at the **[[battle-of-cumae-474bce|battle of Cumae]]** (474) and patron of the most brilliant
literary court of the age. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 7, D. Asheri) treats his
reign as the apogee of Sicilian tyranny, after which "le déluge."

## Reign

Succeeding Gelon by the Deinomenid rule of brother-to-brother succession, Hiero managed a
tense dynastic balance — his brother **Polyzalos** (who styled himself lord of Gela and
dedicated the famous **Delphic Charioteer**) was a rival barely contained, and war with
**Theron of Acragas** (the allied Emmenid dynasty) was averted only by the mediation of the
poet Simonides. Hiero pursued Gelon's policy of **mass deportation and refoundation**: in
476 he cleared **Naxus and Catana**, resettling 10,000 Dorian colonists and renaming Catana
**Aitna**, of which he had himself proclaimed Founder (with a hero-cult in view) — a city
celebrated by Pindar, Bacchylides, and Aeschylus' lost *Aitnaean Women*, but which barely
outlived him. Notably modest in titulature, Hiero never used an official title or put his
name on coins, signing himself "Hiero son of Deinomenes, and the Syracusans."

## Cumae and panhellenic propaganda

Hiero's signal achievement was the **[[battle-of-cumae-474bce|naval victory at Cumae]]**
(474), where a Syracusan fleet, answering an appeal from Campanian Cumae, destroyed an
**Etruscan** fleet — a battle modern scholars regard as a turning point in the **Etruscan
decline**. Hiero made the most of it: bronze helmets dedicated at Olympia (one survives), a
golden tripod at Delphi beside Gelon's, and odes by Pindar and Simonides representing the
Deinomenids as "conquerors of barbarian nations" who gave Greece "a mighty allied hand for
freedom" — propaganda that, Asheri notes, also served to obscure Gelon's earlier refusal to
aid the Greeks against Persia and Hiero's own forced **de-Chalcidization** of Sicily.

## The literary court

Hiero's court — developed especially during the years of his illness (he suffered for years
from gall-stones) — was "pre-eminently a salon of literary men": **Pindar** (who set
one-third of his extant odes in Sicily), **Aeschylus** (whose *Persians* was performed at
Syracuse in 471, and who returned to die at Gela), **Simonides**, **Bacchylides**, and
**Xenophanes**, alongside the Sicilian **Epicharmus**. Later tradition was divided on
Hiero: a wise and generous king to his court poets, but "violent and avaricious" in the
Timaean tradition, remembered for his secret police of *ptakoustai* ("eavesdroppers").

## Significance

With Hiero's death in **467** the **age of archaic Sicilian tyranny ended**; within five
years all three western dynasties had fallen (see [[western-greek-tyranny]]). His reign
fixed the pattern of **Syracusan Tyrrhenian ambition** (Cumae, the abortive Syracusan
garrison on Pithecusae) that the democratic republic would resume, and his court made
Syracuse a magnet of Greek high culture.
