## Overview

**Abū al-Rayḥān Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad al-Bīrūnī** (973–after 1050 CE) was the most wide-ranging scientific intellect of the Earlier Middle Period — a "universal man of science" (Hodgson, *Venture of Islam*, Vol. 2) whose work spanned chronology, mathematics, astronomy, optics, pharmacology, mineralogy, geography, and comparative religion. Born in Khwārazm (modern Uzbekistan/Turkmenistan), his native tongue was Khwārazmian (Iranian but not Persian); he eventually preferred Arabic for scholarly work.

## Biography

Al-Bīrūnī was patronized by the last Sāmānids at Bukhārā and early in his career travelled as far west as Rayy. He returned to Khwārazm and served the last autonomous Khwārazmshāhs as both scholar and diplomat until Maḥmūd of Ghazna's conquests brought Khwārazm under Ghaznavid control. Maḥmūd brought him to Ghazna as court astrologer. His contemporary [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā]] made a point of avoiding Maḥmūd's court; al-Bīrūnī had no such option and served the Ghaznavid dynasty throughout the rest of his active life.

He accompanied Maḥmūd on his Indian campaigns — using the opportunity for the field research that would produce his most remarkable work. He had by one point enumerated 113 of his own treatises; a number of additional works were published under his name by scholarly friends, presumably based on his material. He refused a reported elephant-load of silver offered for his astronomical compendium.

## Major Works and Contributions

### *Kitāb al-Āthār al-Bāqiya* (Chronology, 1000 CE)

"Remaining Traces of Bygone Ages" — an extensive chronological study handled from a mathematical viewpoint but placing Muslim history in a broad world-historical perspective, treating diverse calendar systems with rigorous comparative analysis.

### *Kitāb al-Hind* (India, 1030 CE)

Al-Bīrūnī's most remarkable achievement: a comprehensive survey of Indian life, philosophy, science, and religion based on field study and on his learning of Sanskrit. He approached Indian thought "almost from an anthropological viewpoint" — not seeking truth in the systems but understanding what made Indians think as they did. He was explicitly conscious of the cosmopolitan character of Islamicate culture: finding the Greek and Islamicate intellectual traditions kindred in the face of the genuine *alienness* of Indic thought, while also stressing that Islamdom's greater geographic scope had enabled more extensive scholarship than ancient Greece could achieve.

### Astronomy and Mathematics

His astronomical observations and tables contributed to the tradition of observatory-based astronomy. He reportedly considered — and rejected — both axial rotation and heliocentrism as astronomical hypotheses. His work in mathematics included contributions to trigonometry and the calculation of the earth's circumference.

### Pharmacology and Mineralogy

Works on gems and drugs that combined systematic classification with practical knowledge of sources and properties.

## Scientific Attitude

Al-Bīrūnī privately rejected judicial astrology — the prediction of specific future events from celestial configurations — as epistemically unsound, even while serving as court astrologer (where prediction was expected). He admired [[ibn-sina|Ibn Sīnā]]'s near-contemporary **Ibn Zakariyyāʾ al-Rāzī** despite that figure's philosophical independence, suggesting an appreciation for rigour over doctrinal conformity.

His stated refusal to write in Persian for extended periods — insisting on Arabic for scholarly work, despite Persian being the courtly language and his own native Khwārazmian being unrelated to either — reflected a commitment to the universality that Arabic as scholarly medium provided.
