# Franz Halder

Chief of the German Army General Staff (1938–1942). Primary planner of the 1940 Western campaign and Operation Barbarossa. Advocated for decisive armored thrusts but clashed with Hitler over strategy, resource allocation, and the decision to divert forces to Kiev. Dismissed after disagreements during the 1941-42 winter crisis; later involved in anti-Hitler resistance circles (imprisoned after 20 July plot).

See: [[adolf-hitler]], [[world-war-ii-1939-1945]], [[operation-barbarossa-1941]].

**From West Point WWII Vol 1 (range 3 extraction - Barbarossa context):**
- Halder early optimism and realities: "Franz Halder remarked that the campaign had been won in the first two weeks." By early August: recorded total casualties of 179,500. On Aug 11: "If we smash a dozen [divisions], the Soviets simply put up another dozen." Logistics failures: "The infantry was not keeping up with the armor... Railroad conversion proceeded far behind schedule... The poor state of the roads was taking an enormous toll on German motor vehicles. Dust clogged air filters... The number of functional transport vehicles dropped by 25 percent to 30 percent in the first month alone."
- Planning conflicts and Moscow vs. Kiev: Halder and staff believed Moscow the key; pushed "minimum" advance line east of Moscow. Hitler overruled for economic targets (Leningrad + Ukraine). Halder "quietly disregarded" some of Hitler's early instructions and shaped plans around his concepts.
- Winter crisis: Senior commanders proposed pullback during Dec 1941 Soviet counteroffensive; Hitler insisted on holding (no prepared rear positions). ## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Shirer's principal documentary spine for internal German high-command deliberations is Halder's captured diary, running from Sept 1939. Halder was the key military conspirator in the abortive 1938 "X-Day" coup plot against invading Czechoslovakia (arranged 48-hour advance notice of any attack order to enable a Chancellery seizure) and, with Brauchitsch and backed by Beck/Goerdeler/Oster, the "Zossen Conspiracy" of Oct–Nov 1939 to depose Hitler rather than attack the West — it collapsed after Brauchitsch's Nov 5, 1939 confrontation with Hitler left him "in such a state of shock" (Shirer 1960). In spring 1940 Halder personally rejected the resistance's Vatican-channel "X Report," telling Goerdeler a compromise peace was "senseless"; Hassell judged him "a weak man with shattered nerves." [SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer disputes Halder's own postwar self-portrayal as a reluctant, morally troubled subordinate: his diary for early April 1940 shows him in a "buoyant mood" while helping draft the invasion of neutral Belgium/Holland/Denmark/Norway, and his Dec 1940–Feb 1941 entries show "genuine enthusiasm" for Barbarossa rather than the opposition he claimed after the war. Interrogated at Nuremberg (Feb 1946), Halder was "exceedingly fuzzy" about the conspirators' August 1939 paralysis, telling his questioners: "You must excuse me if I smile. If I hear the word 'irrevocable' connected with Hitler, I must say that nothing was irrevocable" — evasiveness Shirer reads as self-deception rather than candor.

Halder's diary is also Shirer's source for the disputed Dunkirk "stop order" (May 24, 1940) — Rundstedt proposed the halt at Army Group A HQ, corroborated by a July 19, 1957 letter Halder sent Shirer directly clarifying Hitler's stated (and Shirer judges partly genuine) political motive of sparing Britain humiliation. On the Eastern Front, Halder recorded the Aug 11, 1941 admission "we underestimated the strength of the Russian colossus... When a dozen of them are destroyed the Russians throw in another dozen," and by July 3 had prematurely judged the "Feldzug against Russia... won in fourteen days." He was dismissed Sept 24, 1942 after warning Hitler — accurately — that Stalin could still muster 1–1.25 million fresh troops; Hitler "flew at the man... with clenched fists and foam in the corners of his mouth," telling him "we need National Socialist ardor now, not professional ability." Halder's diary ends on that date, a gap in Shirer's documentary record thereafter.
