## Overview

**Ptolemaic Egypt** was the kingdom ruled by the dynasty of [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy
I Soter]] from the death of Alexander (323 BCE) to the death of Cleopatra VII (30 BCE)
— the wealthiest, most centralised, and most durable of the [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]]
kingdoms. CAH VII.1 ch. 5 (E. G. Turner) gives it a deliberately **revisionist**
treatment that overturns the older picture (M. I. Rostovtzeff's "planned economy")
and stresses the costs of Ptolemaic government beneath its brilliant surface.

A defining feature, Turner argues, was the rule of **a thin Graeco-Macedonian
stratum over a vast native Egyptian majority** — perhaps 100,000 immigrants among
seven million Egyptians. The statesman's task was "to create the illusion" that the
immigrants' contribution justified their privileged share; [[ptolemy-i-soter|Soter]]
and Ptolemy III Euergetes managed it, [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Philadelphus]] (in
Turner's reading) over-pressed and "frittered away his assets."

## Governing Structure

- **Two countries in one.** The Greek cities (Naucratis, Ptolemais, and above all
  **[[alexandria|Alexandria]]**) had their own laws and notional self-government,
  set apart from the *chora* — the Egyptian countryside — which was governed in the
  Pharaonic manner through nomarchs, royal and village scribes, and a parallel
  *financial* hierarchy under the **dioiketes** and his local *oikonomoi*. Turner
  insists the system was **not** the seamless centralised machine of legend: four
  different dating systems ran concurrently in the king's own bureaux.
- **The army of settlers (the cleruchy).** Soter's foundational policy was to settle
  foreign professional soldiers (**cleruchs**, later *katoikoi*) on land-holdings
  throughout Egypt — a self-funding reserve army with a stake in the country, drawing
  on Macedonian, Pharaonic, and Athenian precedents. Cleruchs and the Egyptian
  **priesthood** were the "two complementary groups" dispersed through the villages
  that the crown had to keep in counterpoise.
- **The royal economy.** Turner distinguishes **two models**: (Model I) the corn crop,
  grown on royal land by royal cultivators (*basilikoi georgoi*) in a traditional,
  largely unwritten partnership; and (Model II) the tax-farmed monopolies on other
  produce (oil, beer, linen, etc.) codified in the **"Revenue Laws"** of Philadelphus.
  Following Bingen, he argues Model II was **not** state socialism but a pragmatic
  adaptation of Greek tax-farming to Egyptian conditions — see
  [[ptolemaic-economy|the controversy]].

## Major Phases

1. **[[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I Soter]]** (satrap 323, king 305–283) — founder;
   secured Egypt, built the settler army, founded [[alexandria|Alexandria's]] greatness
   and the cult of [[sarapis|Sarapis]].
2. **[[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Ptolemy II Philadelphus]]** (283–246) — the apogee of
   wealth and prestige (the Museum, the Library, the Ptolemaieia, the
   [[arsinoe-ii|Arsinoe]] marriage) — but, Turner argues, also the "total mobilisation"
   that sowed the seeds of later crisis: *"It was Philadelphus, not Philopator, who
   bankrupted Egypt."*
3. **Ptolemy III Euergetes I** (246–222) — recalled from his triumphal march in the
   [[syrian-wars|Third Syrian War]] (to the Euphrates and Babylonia) to crush a palace coup
   and native revolt; restored order, built temples (Edfu, the Serapeum), and is honoured in
   the trilingual **Canopus decree** (238).
4. **Ptolemy IV Philopator** (222–204) — victor of [[battle-of-raphia-217|Raphia]]
   (217), after which (per [[polybius|Polybius]]) the Egyptians "were no longer able to
   tolerate an imposed system" — the onset of native nationalism, the Thebaid revolts
   (206–186 under Harmachis and Chaonnophris), currency inflation, and long decline.
5. **The second-century decline** — minor kings, unscrupulous regents, the
   **Rosetta decree** (196), and growing subservience to Rome, to the absorption of
   the kingdom in 30 BCE.

## How They Shaped Events

The Ptolemies were the great naval and financial power of the eastern Mediterranean,
holders of Cyprus, Coele-Syria (the prize of the [[syrian-wars|Syrian Wars]] with the
[[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]]), and a web of Aegean possessions; the chief paymasters
of anti-Macedonian coalitions in Greece. Their alliance with the Egyptian priesthood
(the temple-building, the trilingual priestly decrees) was the hinge of internal
stability — and its breakdown the measure of their decline.

## Historiography

Turner's chapter is itself a landmark of revisionism. Against Rostovtzeff's image of a
rationally planned, nationalised "state socialism," he and the Brussels school (Claire
Preaux, Jean Bingen) substitute a pragmatic, often improvised fiscalism that
progressively over-exploited the cultivator. He also rejects the thesis of an
"Egyptianizing" early Ptolemaic policy and endorses **Preaux's conclusion that "the
expansion of the Greeks... did not create a new mixed civilisation"** — there was
*interpenetration* but not fusion (the *interpretatio Graeca* and *interpretatio
Aegyptiaca* in counterpoint). The evidence is the discontinuous, place-skewed
[[ptolemaic-papyri|papyrus record]]. See also [[ptolemaic-economy]],
[[hellenistic-monarchy]], [[ruler-cult]].

## The Sixth Syrian War and Roman protection (CAH VIII, Ch. 10, Habicht)

By the second century Egypt was the weakest of the great kingdoms, riven by
**regency factions** and dynastic strife (the joint rule of **Ptolemy VI
Philometor**, his brother **Ptolemy VIII**, and their sister **Cleopatra II**). Its
bid to recover Coele-Syria — the **Sixth Syrian War** (170–168) — was a disaster:
**[[antiochus-iv-epiphanes|Antiochus IV]]** overran almost all of Egypt and besieged
Alexandria. Egypt was saved not by its own arms but by **Rome**: at the
**[[day-of-eleusis-168bce|Day of Eleusis]]** (168), days after Pydna, the envoy
Popillius Laenas forced Antiochus to withdraw. Egypt survived thereafter as
effectively a **Roman protectorate**, its dynastic quarrels increasingly arbitrated
at Rome — the long prelude to annexation in 30 BCE. See [[day-of-eleusis-168bce]],
[[antiochus-iv-epiphanes]].

## The later Ptolemies and the slide into Roman dependence (146–31 BCE) — CAH IX, Ch. 8c (Thompson)

The last century of Ptolemaic rule is a story of **dynastic self-destruction** played
out against the rising shadow of Rome. **Ptolemy VIII Euergetes II** ("Physcon,"
146–116) murdered his nephew, married successively his sister and his niece (the two
**Cleopatras**), persecuted the Alexandrian Greek intelligentsia, and fought a **civil
war** that devastated the country (the *amixia* of 132–124) — turning increasingly to
**native Egyptian support** (the priesthoods, Egyptian troops settled as cleruchs).
Thompson's theme, drawn from the **papyri** (the Kerkeosiris land-surveys), is that
**weak central control translated directly into agricultural collapse, banditry, and
the chronic revolt of the Thebaid**, which Ptolemy IX Soter II finally crushed,
obliterating Thebes.

**Rome's pre-eminence** advanced not by conquest but by **testamentary bequest**: the
kings, lacking heirs or seeking to forestall dynastic strife, **willed their realms to
Rome** — Ptolemy Apion left **Cyrene** (96), and Ptolemy X Alexander I left
**Egypt and Cyprus** (a will Rome long declined to enforce but never forgot). The
recurrent nomination of Rome as legatee is "the clearest indication of her
pre-eminence in Mediterranean politics."

**Ptolemy XII Auletes** (80–51, "the Flute-player") survived only by **buying Roman
favour**: declared "friend and ally" in **59** for **6,000 talents** to
**[[pompey|Pompey]] and Caesar**; driven out by his own people when Rome annexed
**Cyprus** (58, Cato); and **restored by force in 55** by **Gabinius**, proconsul of
Syria, for a promised **10,000 talents** — leaving the **Gabiniani**, the first Roman
garrison, in Egypt. He drained the kingdom's wealth (even melting Alexander's golden
sarcophagus) to pay these debts. On his death (51) he left Egypt jointly to **Ptolemy
XIII** and **Cleopatra VII**, naming the Roman people as witness to his will.

**Cleopatra VII** (51–30) — the last and ablest Ptolemy, the first to speak Egyptian —
allied with **Caesar** (who restored her, fathering **Caesarion**) and then with
**[[mark-antony|Antony]]**, sharing his eastern realm (the **Donations of
Alexandria**, 34). When Antony lost **[[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]]** (31) to
Octavian, the dynasty's last hope fell with him: at the
**[[death-of-antony-and-cleopatra-30bce|fall of Alexandria]] (30)** both took their
own lives. **Egypt was annexed** as the personal domain of [[augustus|Octavian]] (ruled
by an equestrian prefect, closed to senators) — its vast treasure funding his veterans
and his new order. So ended three centuries of Ptolemaic rule (CAH X, Ch. 1).

Thompson's underlying argument: beneath the dynastic melodrama, **overseas debt,
failing Niles, and a weak unsalaried bureaucracy** hollowed out even Egypt's
proverbial agricultural wealth, while **reciprocal Greek–Egyptian acculturation**
(intermarriage in the cleruchies, bilingual families, the dynastic cult fused with
village temples) slowly advanced — but "not even the strength and power of the gods of
Egypt could resist the force of Rome."
