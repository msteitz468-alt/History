# Victor Davis Hanson

Victor Davis Hanson is a military historian, classicist, and farmer specializing in ancient Greek warfare and its lessons for the present. He is best known for applying a "face of battle" / experiential approach to Greek history, emphasizing the realities of combat, economics, terror, and societal impact over purely political or strategic narratives.

## Background and Approach

Hanson comes from a family of farmers in California's Central Valley and has long worked his own small farm while writing. This agrarian perspective informs his emphasis on the economic devastation of war (ravaged farms, lost labor) and the experience of ordinary hoplites and rowers. He has held academic posts including at California State University, Fresno (classics, emeritus), and fellowships at the Hoover Institution, the Center for Advanced Study in the Behavioral Sciences, and the U.S. Naval Academy (visiting professor of military history).

His prior works include *The Western Way of War* (hoplite battle as direct, decisive infantry clash), *The Other Greeks* (agrarian roots of Greek culture), and *Carnage and Culture*. *A War Like No Other* (2005) applies and extends these ideas to the Peloponnesian War, using Thucydides as a base but shifting focus to "how the Athenians and Spartans fought" — the actual mechanics, suffering, and innovations in land, sea, siege, and irregular warfare.

## Contribution via *A War Like No Other*

The book is explicitly a companion to Thucydides, not a replacement. It organizes the twenty-seven-year conflict thematically around the lived experience of fighting:

- Causes rooted in fear (Spartan insecurity vs. Athenian power and empire).
- The "fire" of annual invasions and economic attrition.
- Plague as a force multiplier.
- Terror, raids, and assassinations (the "war in the shadows").
- Traditional hoplite battles (Delium, Mantinea): right-wing dominance, othismos, low lethality, Spartan pipes/discipline vs confusion/panic; Pericles avoided as “a terrible thing.”
- Sieges and walls: Plataea (ramp/fire/circumvallation/breakout/execution/razing 431-427); ~21 sieges; Athenian fleet specialty (Melos, Mytilene); “poliorkia”; “The Thirty Years Slaughter”; one surrender chance then no quarter.
- The Sicilian disaster (naval + land combined arms catastrophe; 415 Syracuse hoplite battle tactical win but cavalry decisive, no strategic gain).
- The naval war and final Aegean climax (Aegospotami).
- Postwar "ruin" and whether Athens or Greece was truly destroyed.

Hanson stresses that the Peloponnesian War was a brutal civil war among Greeks, involving total economic mobilization, atrocities, disease, and innovation in terror and naval power — "a war like no other" in its length, savagery, and consequences for the Greek world. It challenges romantic views of decisive hoplite clashes and highlights Athens' resilience and Sparta's ultimate reliance on Persian money and brutal proxies. (Grounded claims from Ch. 5-6 in range 5501-8500.)
