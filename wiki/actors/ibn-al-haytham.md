# Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen)

**Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥasan ibn al-Ḥasan ibn al-Haytham** (c. 965 – c. 1040 CE), known in Latin as **Alhazen**, was an Islamic polymath whose work in optics, mathematics, and astronomical theory made him one of the most consequential scientists of the medieval world. He is the dominant figure in the [[shukuk-tradition|*shukūk* tradition]] of critiques against Ptolemaic astronomy.

## Reception in the Latin West and Islamic East

Ibn al-Haytham's fame in Latin Europe rested entirely on his *Kitāb al-Manāẓir* (Book of Optics), translated into Latin as *De aspectibus* (or *Perspectiva*). His astronomical work, however — the most radical and systematic critique of Ptolemaic astronomy in the Islamic tradition — was **never translated into Latin**. The *al-Shukūk ʿalā Baṭlamyūs* (Dubitationes in Ptolemaeum) survived only in Arabic, and its influence was confined to the Islamic intellectual world.

This asymmetric transmission has created a distorted picture of Ibn al-Haytham in Western historiography: the optician is celebrated; the astronomer has been largely invisible.

## The *Shukūk ʿalā Baṭlamyūs*

Ibn al-Haytham's *Shukūk* is the most extensive and systematically argued text in the [[shukuk-tradition]]. It is organized around three Ptolemaic works read **in conjunction**: the *Almagest*, the *Planetary Hypotheses*, and the *Optics*. The deliberate grouping signals Ibn al-Haytham's method: Ptolemy's works must be read together as a coherent scientific system, and the contradictions that arise from that joint reading are more damning than those visible in any individual text.

His central methodological principle was the **demand for physical consistency**:

> "No motion exists in this world in any perceptible fashion except the motion of [real] bodies." (*al-Shukūk ʿalā Baṭlamyūs*, quoted in Saliba ch. 3)

Any mathematical model that required imaginary lines or points to "move" in ways that no physical body could actually move was, for Ibn al-Haytham, not merely imprecise but **scientifically inadmissible**.

### Critique of the Lunar Model

Ibn al-Haytham targeted the *prosneusis* point in Ptolemy's lunar model: Ptolemy had the moon's epicyclic diameter always directed toward a point diametrically opposite the center of the deferent from the center of the world. But the epicyclic diameter is an imaginary line:

> "The epicyclic diameter is an imaginary line, and an imaginary line does not move by itself in any perceptible fashion that produces an existing entity in this world."

A single epicycle cannot simultaneously produce the moon's anomalistic motion and ensure that its diameter always points toward the prosneusis point — this would require a single sphere to move in two independent motions simultaneously, which is physically impossible.

### Critique of the Equant

In *Almagest* IX.2, Ptolemy stipulated uniform circular motion for the upper planets. But by IX.5–6, he had introduced the equant: the epicyclic center moved uniformly not around the deferent's own center but around a separate equant point. Ibn al-Haytham's verdict:

> "What we have reported is the truth of what Ptolemy had established for the motion of the upper planets; and that is a notion that necessitates a contradiction."

The contradiction: if the epicyclic center moves uniformly around the equant point, by Ptolemy's own proof (Almagest III), it cannot simultaneously move uniformly around the deferent center. Therefore the deferent moves non-uniformly around its own center — violating Ptolemy's foundational assumption of uniform circular motion. The equant sphere itself is "fictitious" (has no physical existence), so the only real moving body — the deferent — is forced into non-uniform motion. Physical impossibility.

This equant problem, Ibn al-Haytham concluded, was not incidental: it "permeated almost all of the models" in the Almagest.

### The Condemnation

Ibn al-Haytham did not merely criticize Ptolemy — he called for the **replacement of Ptolemaic astronomy** on new principled foundations:

> "The configuration, which Ptolemy had established for the motion of the five planets, was a false configuration (*hayʾa bāṭila*), and that the motions of these planets must have a correct configuration, which included bodies moving in a uniform, perpetual, and continuous motion, without having to suffer any contradiction, or be blemished by any doubt. That configuration must be other than the one established by Ptolemy." (*al-Shukūk*, as translated and discussed by Saliba, ch. 3)

Ibn al-Haytham's summary conclusion — that Ptolemy had been "obliged to follow that route for he could not devise another" — acknowledges Ptolemy's competence while declaring his system fundamentally inadequate, requiring a wholly new astronomy built on consistent physical-mathematical foundations.

### Critique of the *Planetary Hypotheses* and Optics

By comparing the *Almagest* and *Planetary Hypotheses*, Ibn al-Haytham found that Ptolemy had *changed* his models between the two works — dropping motions, substituting slices of spheres for full spheres — without resolving the underlying inconsistencies. The spherical slices (*manshūrāt*) of the *Planetary Hypotheses* were, in Ibn al-Haytham's assessment, worse than the full spheres of the *Almagest*: they required bodies to "empty one space to fill another" and to "move in different and contrary motions."

The extension of the *shukūk* to Ptolemy's *Optics* signals the pervasiveness of Ibn al-Haytham's critical program: the Greek scientific tradition was flawed as a whole, not just in its astronomy.

## Limitation: Critique Without Construction

A decisive limitation acknowledged by the tradition itself: Ibn al-Haytham articulated the problems with unparalleled clarity but **proposed no alternative astronomical models**. Muʿayyad al-Dīn al-ʿUrḍī (d. 1266) explicitly criticized this failure — it was not enough to condemn Ptolemy; one had to build something better. The constructive work was left to later astronomers of the [[maragha-observatory|Maragha school]] and to [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]].

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Ibn al-Haytham's astronomical work been translated into Latin alongside his optics, the Copernican revolution's intellectual antecedents would have been far more legible to European historians. The astronomical *Shukūk* is the most explicit statement of the theoretical demand that drove Islamic astronomers toward the models that Copernicus would later adapt — and yet it remained invisible to the Latin tradition.
