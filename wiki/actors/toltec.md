# Toltec Culture

## Overview

The **Toltec** culture of central Mexico occupied the Early Postclassic period
(c. 900–1175 CE), centered on their capital **Tula** (Tollán). They represent the first
major state formation in the Mexican highlands following the collapse of
[[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] and the decline of the Classic Maya city-states
([[snow-gonlin-siegel-archaeology-native-north-america-2020]], Ch. 13).

## Geographic Setting

Tula was located on the northern fringe of the desert in central Mexico — on the edge of the
Gran Chichimeca, the territory of northern hunter-gatherers. Agriculture along this northern
edge of the Basin of Mexico required irrigation. The location placed Tula at the cultural and
ecological boundary between the Mesoamerican agricultural zone to the south and the arid
north.

## Name and ethnicity

“**Toltec**” is polysemic: often it means urban, urbane, skilled craftsmen rather than one
ethnicity; “**Tollan**” may mean archaeological Tula, Teotihuacan, other sites, or mythical
abstractions (Cowgill, CHNPA II.1 Ch. 6). The archaeological capital is
[[tula-hidalgo|Tula, Hidalgo]].

## Rise

Tula rose after [[teotihuacan-collapse-550ce|Teotihuacan’s decline]]; Tula itself could not
have destroyed Teotihuacan, though ancestors of its builders may have entered as the older
state weakened (Cowgill 2000). Northwest ceramic affinities from the **Prado phase**
(~A.D. 700–800 or earlier; hilltop sites possibly defensive) precede Tula’s importance;
**Corral-phase Coyotlatelco** (~800–900 or as early as 600–800) supports substantial
northwest migration into the highlands — consonant with linguistic arguments that Nahua
speakers entered central Mexico in numbers about this time, though not ironclad.

Two precincts: earlier **Tula Chico** (Coyotlatelco); later **Tula Grande**. The city becomes
a major center only in the **Tollan ceramic phase** — traditionally ~950–1150/1200 but more
likely ~**800–1000** per Cowgill (Basin dates; Tohil Plumbate). Coverage ~12–13 km²; rough
population ~**60,000**. Continuities with the past (e.g., Atetelco-echoing feline/coyote
frieze) without complete cultural break. Secondary sources also describe early specialized
settlement (e.g., lime production ~700 CE) before full urban florescence
([[snow-gonlin-siegel-archaeology-native-north-america-2020]]).

Charlton treats the Toltec state as the **first crystallization of a macroregional secondary
state** in the continuum Teotihuacan → Tula → Tenochtitlan: processes after Teotihuacan
(warfare, tributary formation strongest outside but adjacent to the Basin) underwrote Tula,
then the Aztecs. Grandeur in art, architecture, and size did not equal Teotihuacan or
Tenochtitlan.

## Architecture and Material Culture

Toltec architects drew upon design ideas shared across a "long strip of Mesoamerica,"
extending from the Basin of Mexico to the northern Yucatan Peninsula. Their architectural
tastes ran to:

- **Modest stone pyramids**
- **Ball courts** (see [[mesoamerican-ball-game]])
- **Spacious colonnaded halls**
- Low-relief wall carvings of **jaguars, eagles, and warriors**; warrior columns, *coatepantli*, chacmools

A few undecipherable glyphs have been documented; lengthy inscriptions have not been
recovered, making Toltec political history difficult to reconstruct from internal sources.

## Influence, horizon, and the Maya

Specific architectural/iconographic links with **Chichén Itzá** (~1,150 km) plus varied
evidence for central Mexican–connected people. Kubler’s reverse-priority (Chichén first,
Tula imitation) lacks good reason — Chichén larger/finer due to masons, limestone, and labor,
not temporal primacy; contact more complex than simple Toltec replacement of Maya traditions
(Cowgill 2000). Diehl (1993) argues a **Toltec Horizon** spread by merchants; Calnek notes
distant imperial control is not archaeologically obvious; Weaver argues **limited integrated
territory** with extended sphere of influence. Charlton notes the Aztec empire looks equally
ephemeral archaeologically.

## Collapse

See full event page [[fall-of-tula-1150]]. Working date c. **1150 CE** (Charlton; range
~1100–1200); process more than single event, with migrations before and after. Residential
evidence consistent with massacre; Aztec later digging at Tula was charter-driven respect,
not the original fall (Cowgill). Parallel in form (not date) to selective burning of
Teotihuacan’s core and other Epiclassic/Postclassic sackings (e.g. Xochicalco traditions).

The Aztec (Mexica) later attributed great cultural prestige to the Toltecs, claiming ancestry
and depicting Tula as origin of arts and refined urban life — idealization that inflates
Tollan’s wonders relative to modest remains and muddies historical reality.

## Historiography

Idealized in Aztec tradition; poorer archaeological documentation relative to prestige.
Identification of Náhuatl “Tula” with Hidalgo archaeology is generally accepted after Jiménez
Moreno/Acosta corrected Teotihuacan-as-Tollan confusion. Open questions: political reach,
Toltec–Chichén mechanism, absolute chronology of Tollan phase vs. traditional 950–1150
bracket, and how far “Toltec” is ethnic vs. cultural honorific. Sources:
[[snow-gonlin-siegel-archaeology-native-north-america-2020]];
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1]] (Cowgill Ch. 6; Charlton Ch. 11).
