# Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus

The tribune of **133 BCE** whose agrarian law and violent death opened the era of
political crisis that ended the Republic. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]]
Ch. 3 (Lintott).

## Role and career

Of the highest nobility (his father consul twice and censor; his mother Cornelia,
daughter of Scipio Africanus), Tiberius was scarred by the Numantine affair (137),
where as quaestor he negotiated a treaty the Senate repudiated, surrendering the
consul Mancinus — an experience Cicero saw as turning him against the *optimate*
cause. As **tribune (133)** he proposed a law to recover **public land**
(*ager publicus*) held above the 500-*iugera* limit and redistribute it to the poor
through a three-man commission — addressing the [[agrarian-crisis-roman-republic|
agrarian crisis]] and the shrinking pool of peasant-soldiers.

When his colleague **M. Octavius** vetoed the bill, Tiberius had him **deposed** by
popular vote — an unprecedented act asserting that a tribune's office was conditional
on serving the people's will. He seized the **Pergamene legacy** to fund the
distribution and stood for **re-election** (also unprecedented). His enemies cried
tyranny; **Scipio Nasica** led a mob of senators who clubbed Tiberius and many
followers to death on the Capitol — the first political bloodshed of the late
Republic.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Lintott treats Tiberius as **the initial moment of a new political process**, while
cautioning that he acted within the *popularis* tradition and aristocratic value-system
(driven by a moral vision and the noble's love of *gloria*, not by revolution). His
key innovations — the **deposition of a tribune** and the **ruthless assertion of
popular sovereignty** — gave the tribunate an ideological edge; his murder taught
later *populares* that **superior force and magistrates with *imperium*** were
needed to survive. His land commission's boundary stones (with Appius Claudius, Carbo,
Fulvius Flaccus) and the rising census figures of 131–125 attest the law's real effect.

## Historiography

**Beard ([[sources/beard-spqr-2015|SPQR]], 2015)** is skeptical of the traditional
economic-crisis reading (mass displacement of smallholders by slave-worked ranches):
archaeology instead shows "widespread survival of small-scale farms," so "many
historians now think that, if his motives were sincere, Tiberius seriously misread
the situation." She also treats the 133 crisis as crystallizing an already-latent
*populares*/*optimates* divide rather than shattering a prior consensus — Cicero's
picture of a calm pre-133 harmony between rich and poor is, in her words, "at best a
nostalgic fiction."

The friendly tradition in **Plutarch** and **Appian** derives ultimately from his
brother [[gaius-gracchus|Gaius']] memoir; hostile accounts (Florus, via Posidonius)
cast him as an incipient tyrant. The reliability of the speeches and the legality of
his acts (the deposition, the re-election bid) are central debates. See
[[optimates-populares]].

### OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Crawford & Griffin

Crawford (Ch. 16): with Po colonization ended, landless men built up before 133; as
tribune 133 Tiberius limited public-land holdings and redistributed surplus, deposed
Octavius’ veto, interfered with Pergamum settlement, and was murdered with 300
supporters seeking re-election. Italian elites also hurt by resumption of public land;
floating citizenship compensation exploded in 91. Griffin (Ch. 19): Optimates/
Populares map ideology not parties; *popularis* denotes assembly/tribune use against
senatorial authority and/or land, debt, corn — the Gracchan line of the label.

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010)

Harriet I. Flower ([[flower-roman-republics-2010|*Roman Republics*]]) treats 133 as a
lethal watershed in civic space rather than as the sole start of one long “crisis”
of 133–49. She stresses reciprocal norm-breaking: when fellow tribune **M. Octavius**
persisted in vetoing the agrarian bill against the popular will, Tiberius had the
people depose him and physically remove him — “neither Octavius nor Gracchus acted
according to the existing rules of the political game.” The killing itself, led by
*pontifex maximus* **Scipio Nasica** (cousin of Tiberius) during an inaugurated
electoral assembly before Jupiter Optimus Maximus, was especially shocking: a
sacrosanct tribune slain by the senior priest, under an archaic *consecratio*, with
virtually no legal or societal precedent for death as a penalty in internal political
conflict (exile was the standard extreme). Flower argues that Tiberius’s
**unwillingness to play by traditional political rules** mattered more in unleashing
deadly violence than the substance of the agrarian law.

Against the tendency to treat the brothers as a single Plutarchan pair-unit, she
insists they be read separately: **despite the murder, the agrarian reform went into
effect**, land commissioners redistributed public land actively before the quarrel with
dispossessed Italian landowners peaked in 129, and later agrarian laws continued to
~111. The program itself aimed to rebuild an army of small peasant farmers with a
tangible stake in the community; Tiberius’s speeches invoked Spanish slave-chain gangs
and the demoralized Numantine army. Flower places the episode inside her multi-republic
map ([[roman-republics-periodization]]): conventional “beginning of the end” in 133
marks citizen-on-citizen violence in civic space (Cicero: *mors Tiberii Gracchi…
divisit populum unum in duas partes*), but she prefers secret ballot (139) as the more
useful chronological marker of the unstable final *nobiles* republic, and counts only
**fifty years** from Tiberius’s death to Sulla’s invasion of Italy after the Peace of
Dardanus — not an eighty- or hundred-year continuous crisis. See also
[[fall-of-roman-republic]].

## Woolf, Rome: An Empire's Story (2012)

From [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]] Ch. 7 (populares / empire crisis).

- **Populares program as radical empire solutions.** Late-second-century populares (Tiberius 133; Gaius 123; later Saturninus; later associated with Caesar) sought assembly support when Senate blocked them. Program: public-land distribution; colonies outside Italy; subsidized then free grain; secret ballot—not a constitutional revolution (kept property-weighted assemblies and senatorial monopoly of offices). Addressed diplomacy, revenues, courts, Italian relations.
- **Political murder new.** Both Gracchi died in street battles between senatorial/client mobs—“political murder was something new.”
- **Inconsistencies.** Land reform hurt Italian tenants; Asia tax-farming handed provincials to equestrians governors feared to restrain.
- **Quotes:** “What united their proposals was a willingness to form radical solutions to the crisis of the empire, and the oratorical skill to persuade the assemblies to back them when the Senate would not.” / “Both Gracchi brothers died in pitched battles in the streets of Rome… But political murder was something new.”
