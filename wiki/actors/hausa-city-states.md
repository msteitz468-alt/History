# The Hausa City-States (Hausa Bakwai)

The **Hausa city-states** were a constellation of independent **walled cities** in what is now northern
Nigeria — Kano, Katsina, Zazzau (Zaria), Gobir, Daura, Rano and Biram, the legendary "**Hausa Bakwai**"
(seven true Hausa states) — that flourished as centres of **trade, crafts, and Islamic learning** between
the western Sudanic empires and Kanem-Bornu. Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO
[[gha-vol4-12th-16th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. IV]]** (ch. 11, Adamu).

## Origins

GHA IV reviews competing theories of Hausa origins and rejects the old **Bayajidda legend** reading (an
"Arab from Baghdad" founder) as myth — though the legend may encode real **Kanem-Bornu** influence on Hausa
kingship. The modern view (Sutton, Adamu) is that the **Hausa language and people developed *in situ***
around the Daura–Kano–Rano core and spread westward, absorbing immigrants — Wangara (Dyula) Muslim traders
and, later, Fulani — rather than arriving from outside.

## A city-state civilization

Unlike the great Sahelian *empires*, Hausaland was a **decentralized world of rival city-states**, each
under its ***sarki*** (king) and walled for defence. Their wealth rested on:

- **Crafts and manufacture** — above all **indigo-dyed cotton cloth** and **leatherwork** ("Morocco
  leather" was in fact often Hausa), traded across the Sahara and the Sudan;
- **Trade** — a position athwart the routes linking the [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan]] termini, the
  forest gold and kola of the south, and [[kanem-bornu|Bornu]] to the east; **Kano** and **Katsina** became
  the great commercial cities.
- **Islamic learning** — Islam arrived with the Wangara merchants in the **fourteenth century** (the *Kano
  Chronicle* records the conversion of Kano's court), and the cities became centres of scholarship, though
  rural populations long kept indigenous religion.

The states were chronically **disunited**, frequently warring with one another and intermittently tributary
to Songhay, Kanem-Bornu, or the Jukun of Kwararafa — a disunity that left them vulnerable.

## Significance and end

The Hausa cities are the great example of **non-imperial urban civilization** in the West African
savanna — a durable network of commercial city-states (an African analogue, loosely, to the Italian or
Swahili city-states). Their independent era ended with the **Fulani jihad of Usman dan Fodio (1804–08)**,
which conquered the Hausa kingdoms and folded them into the **Sokoto Caliphate** — but **Hausa** language
and culture became, and remain, one of the largest in Africa.
