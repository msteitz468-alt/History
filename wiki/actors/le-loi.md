## Overview

Le Loi (1385–1433), posthumously Le Thai To, was a Thanh Hoa strongman from the Mount Lam region (where Kinh and Trai peoples mingled) who led the decade-long [[lam-son-uprising-1418-1428|Lam Son uprising]] against Ming occupation and, in 1428, founded the [[le-dynasty-vietnam|Later Le dynasty]], reigning until his death in 1433.

## Role

Head of a prominent landholding family that gained influence during Tran dynastic decline, Le Loi initially swore allegiance to the Ming (1413) after the defeat of the last Tran claimant, Tran Quy Khoang, then broke with Ming authority following a personal feud in which a rival strongman denounced him. From 1418 he waged a guerrilla insurgency in the Thanh Hoa uplands that, after a serious defeat and forced submission in 1422–1423, transformed after 1424 — on news of the Yongle emperor Zhu Di's death — into a rapid, methodical campaign that swept south into Nghe An, then north into the Red River plain, placing all major Ming strongholds (Dong Kinh, Tay Do, Co Long, Chi Linh, Xuong Giang) under siege by early 1427 and annihilating a major Ming relief army near Xuong Giang. He took the throne in 1428 after the Ming withdrawal.

As king, Le Loi rebuilt central administration (family registers, literary examinations from 1428, a schedule of "three proscriptions" for officials, ten disciplinary prohibitions for soldiers), restored Tran-era ritual continuity (rebuilding Tran tombs and shrines, reinstating the blood-oath ceremony and royal birthday festival abolished by [[ho-quy-ly|Ho Quy Ly]]), demobilized four-fifths of his army in 1429 to return soldiers to agriculture, and dismantled the remaining slave/serf-worked great estates in favor of a free peasantry — building on land reforms begun by Ho Quy Ly and continued under Ming administration. He began minting new coinage to replace the destroyed metal currency, and in 1431 secured Ming recognition as king, restoring normal tributary relations.

## Major Decisions and Consequences

- **Founding the Later Le dynasty on an alliance between Trai uplanders and Kinh lowlanders** — a fragile but durable arrangement; his own military following (the ninety-three men on the 1429 roll of merit, nearly all southern, illiterate, and given the honorific Le surname) became the "great lords" who dominated the court for three decades after his death.
- **Passing over his eldest son Le Tu Te** (deemed "rustic, rash, and foolish") in favor of the 10-year-old Le Nguyen Long as successor — set the pattern of minor/adolescent kingship and great-lord factional dominance that characterized the dynasty until [[le-thanh-tong|Le Thanh Tong]]'s accession in 1460.
- **Sparing the retreating Ming army (1427)**, on Nguyen Trai's advice, rather than annihilating it — secured a peaceful Ming withdrawal and paved the way for the 1431 recognition.

## Counterfactual Significance

Le Loi's decisive break with passive survival strategy after 1424 — described by Taylor as showing "forethought and a bold, aggressive spirit" rather than mere opportunism — turned a marginal upland insurgency into a dynasty-founding movement within three years. Without the change in Ming imperial priorities after Zhu Di's death, or without Le Loi's rapid exploitation of that opening, Ming Giao Chi might have persisted much longer. Later Vietnamese historiography reads his war purely as national liberation, but Taylor stresses it was equally "a civil war between the Kinh and the Trai, between the Red River plain and the southern provinces" — an alliance of convenience whose centrifugal tensions reemerged a century later in the [[seventy-years-war-vietnam-1533-1592|Seventy Years War]].

## Historiography

Later Vietnamese historiography, especially the *Complete Book of the History of Great Viet*, elevated Le Loi's war into a foundational myth of resistance to foreign aggression (including the legend of the magical sword returned to the Lake of the Returned Sword in Hanoi). Taylor treats this as historically true in outline but incomplete: the uprising's true character as a regional/ethnic civil war as much as an anti-Ming war is obscured by nationalist framing. The relative contribution of his advisor **Nguyen Trai** to strategy is, per Taylor, likely overstated by posterity, resting more on Nguyen Trai's surviving literary corpus than on firm evidence of a "great strategist" role.
