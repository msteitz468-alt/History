# Zhang Qian

## Overview

**Zhang Qian** (d. 119/118 BCE) was the [[han-dynasty|Han]] envoy whose mission to the
[[yuezhi|Yuezhi]] — though it failed in its objective — produced China's first reliable
intelligence on the lands west of the Pamirs and, in [[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin's]]
reading, effectively **inaugurated the eastern [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]]**. Sima Qian
(possibly an eyewitness to his report at court) called him "a man of great strength,
determination and generosity" who "trusted others and in turn was liked by the
barbarians." Both the *Shiji* and the *Han Shu* credit him personally with opening
relations with the northwest; later envoys "relied upon his reputation to gain them a
hearing."

## Career

An obscure member of the lower nobility serving as a palace attendant, Zhang Qian
answered [[han-wudi|Wudi's]] 139 BCE empire-wide summons and was appointed "envoy to the
Yuezhi" to seek an anti-[[xiongnu]] alliance (see [[zhang-qian-mission-138bce]]). Departing
[[chang-an|Chang'an]] c. 138 BCE, he was almost immediately captured by the Xiongnu and
held in "gentlemanly detention" for **over ten years**, given a wife, but keeping his
Han emblems of authority. Escaping, he reached **Dayuan (Ferghana)** — the first Chinese
ever to see the valley — then the Yuezhi court (c. 128 BCE), who declined alliance. He
spent ~a year gathering intelligence in [[bactria|Bactria]], was recaptured on the return,
and reached Chang'an c. 126/125 BCE with only his Xiongnu wife, child, and slave Ganfu.

Subsequent service:
- Delivered his intelligence report to Wudi, arguing the western states "prized Han
  wealth and goods" and could extend the empire "by 10,000 li"; promoted to supreme
  counselor of the palace.
- c. 124 BCE — sent to find a southern route to India; blocked by the Kunming tribes.
- 123 BCE — guide for **Wei Qing's** Xiongnu campaign; his knowledge of water and pasture
  "saved the army from hardship," earning the title **Bowang ("Broad Vision Marquis")**.
- Later sentenced to death for arriving late to a rendezvous with Li Guang; bought a
  pardon, reduced to commoner, then restored.
- Led the great **Wusun mission** (300 men, 600 horses, vast silk-and-gold tribute),
  bringing Wusun envoys to Chang'an in 120 BCE; appointed **Grand Messenger** (one of the
  nine highest ministers) before his death in 119/118 BCE.

## Counterfactual Significance

[CONTROVERSY] Benjamin uses Zhang Qian as his test case for **great-man vs. structural
causation**: he argues that history "does on occasion seem to hinge upon... the
astonishing personal endeavors of individuals," and that had Zhang Qian not escaped
captivity, Han expansion into Central Asia "might have been stopped in its tracks." See
[[great-man-vs-structural-causation]].

## Historiography

The sole substantial sources are **Sima Qian's *Shiji* ch. 123** (Watson trans.) and the
***Han Shu* chs. 61 and 96A** (Hulsewé & Loewe). Sima Qian may have personally witnessed
Zhang Qian's report to the court, giving the account exceptional authority; dates are
nonetheless approximate ("probably 138 BCE," "in or about 128 BCE").
