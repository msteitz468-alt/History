## Role

Ptolemy II "Philadelphus" (308–246 BCE), son of [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]], presided
over the apogee of [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic Egypt]] — the wealthiest and most
brilliant of the Hellenistic courts — but is the subject of CAH VII.1 ch. 5's most
pointed **revisionism**: E. G. Turner argues that the dazzling show of his reign "was
attained by disastrous fiscal and social policies" that, by the 250s, amounted to a
"total mobilisation" of Egypt's resources and sowed the seeds of later collapse —
*"It was Philadelphus, not Philopator, who bankrupted Egypt."*

## The Reign

- **The sibling marriage.** He married his full sister **[[arsinoe-ii|Arsinoe II]]**
  (the epithet *Philadelphos*, "sibling-loving," was first hers), divorcing his first
  wife to do so. The first Ptolemaic brother–sister marriage scandalised the Greek
  world but kept power in the family and was defended by court poets (Zeus and Hera);
  it became a dynastic custom and a pillar of the [[ruler-cult|dynastic cult]] (the
  *Theoi Adelphoi*, "Brother–Sister Gods" — the first deification of *living* rulers).
- **Pageantry and prestige.** He inaugurated the quadrennial **Ptolemaieia** festival
  (claiming parity with Olympia) and the famous **Grand Procession** described by
  Callixeinus, deployed (Turner stresses) as deliberate instruments of international
  prestige and royal power.
- **Alexandria's golden age.** Under him [[alexandria|Alexandria]] became "a wonder of
  the world": the **Pharos** lighthouse, the **Museum and Library**, the patronage of
  Callimachus, Theocritus, and Apollonius of Rhodes, and the beginnings of the
  **Septuagint** translation.
- **The fiscal machine.** His reign produced the **Revenue Laws** and the tax-farmed
  monopolies of [[ptolemaic-egypt|"Model II"]], and the dense administration visible in
  the **[[ptolemaic-papyri|Zenon archive]]** — the papers of the estate manager Zenon,
  agent of the powerful *dioiketes* **Apollonius**. Turner reads the archive's "success
  story" as concealing mounting strain: salary and ration cuts, forced re-negotiation
  of contracts with cultivators, and progressive over-exploitation.
- **Foreign affairs.** He fought the First and Second of the [[syrian-wars|Syrian Wars]]
  against the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]] over Coele-Syria, and faced the Antigonid
  [[chremonidean-war-267|Chremonidean War]] in Greece — expensive struggles that, in
  Turner's view, drove the fiscal pressure: "without his competitive dynastic wars the story
  could have been different."

## Counterfactual Significance

Philadelphus fixed the institutional and cultural shape of Ptolemaic Egypt — the
sibling monarchy, the dynastic cult, the Museum and Library, the fiscal apparatus. The
revisionist case makes him a cautionary figure: a king with "every advantage in his
favour" whose relentless extraction, far from being the admired triumph of Greek
administrative genius, set up the native revolts and currency collapse of the following
century. See [[ptolemaic-egypt]], [[ptolemaic-economy]].
