# Artaxerxes II Mnemon

## Overview

**Artaxerxes II** ("Mnemon," r. 404–358 BCE) was the Persian King for almost the whole of
the early fourth century — the monarch who **defeated his brother [[cyrus-the-younger|
Cyrus the Younger]] at Cunaxa (401)** and who **dictated the [[kings-peace-386bce|King's
Peace]] (386)**, making himself arbiter of the Greek world. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol.
VI]] (Ch. 3, Hornblower) treats his reign as the focus of the unjust charge of Persian
"**decadence**" — a reputation projected from "an excessive interest in the personality of
one man" onto the whole empire.

## Reign

Artaxerxes succeeded Darius II in 404. His brother **Cyrus the Younger**, the over-mighty
commander of western Anatolia, marched against him with a Greek mercenary army; at
**[[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|Cunaxa]]** (near Babylon, 401) Cyrus was killed and
the coup collapsed, though the famous retreat of the **Ten Thousand** followed. As King,
Artaxerxes:
- Exploited Greek divisions in the **[[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]]**, funding
  the coalition against Sparta and then, through the satrap **Tiribazus** and the Spartan
  **Antalcidas**, imposing the **[[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]]** — recovering the
  Greek cities of Asia and establishing the **Common Peace** under royal sponsorship.
- Faced and contained the long-running **Revolt of the Satraps** (c. 366–360) in the
  west — which Hornblower argues the sources exaggerated, and which "flexibility at the
  margins" of a quasi-feudal system (see [[achaemenid-satrapy]]) could absorb.
- **Failed, repeatedly, to reconquer Egypt**, in revolt since c. 404 — the one count of
  the "decadence" indictment Hornblower allows as genuine weakness, since Egypt "mattered
  to Persia economically."
- Conceded to **Evagoras of Salamis** the right to be "subject as king to king" — a
  telling instance of the empire's pragmatic accommodation of powerful clients.

## Significance

For all the later caricature of an uxorious, mother-dominated weakling, Artaxerxes II
**reigned for 46 years**, recovered Asia Minor by diplomacy, and made the Persian King
the indispensable broker of Greek politics. Hornblower's verdict is that Persian
"decadence" in the first half of the fourth century "is something of a myth" — and that
the empire's eventual fall is to be laid at the door of **Darius III personally**, not at
the system Artaxerxes presided over. He died in 358 and was succeeded by the far harsher
**Artaxerxes III Ochus**.

## Briant's additions

[[sources/briant-from-cyrus-to-alexander-2002|Briant]] concurs that "decadence" is a myth and
sharpens two points. He reinterprets the **Revolt of the Satraps** not as a coordinated western
conflagration but as "a series of limited local revolts over a decade" — Orontes betrayed the
coalition, Datames and Ariobarzanes were eliminated by treachery, and [[mausolus|Mausolus]] may
never have rebelled at all — evidence of imperial resilience, since the king dealt with each rebel
individually. He also stresses Artaxerxes II's **religious innovation**: the first explicit naming of
**Anahita and Mithra** alongside Ahura-Mazda in royal inscriptions, and an edict establishing Anahita
cult-statues across the capitals (Babylon, Susa, Ecbatana, Persepolis, Bactra, Damascus, Sardis) —
read by Briant as binding the Persian imperial diaspora to the crown rather than imposing Iranian
worship on subjects. The empire's strategic instrument in the west was **[[tissaphernes|financial
warfare]]** ("Persian gold was more effective than Persian arms"), not battlefield supremacy.
