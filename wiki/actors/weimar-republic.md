# Weimar Republic

Germany's first democracy, born of the [[november-revolution-1918|November Revolution]] of 1918 and
constituted at Weimar in 1919. Its parliamentary republic carried a heavy birth-burden: the "stab in
the back" legend, the punitive Treaty of Versailles, and assault from both the revolutionary left
(the KPD, the Spartacist rising) and the nationalist right (the Kapp and Beer Hall putsches).

After surviving the 1923 hyperinflation and Ruhr crisis, the Republic enjoyed a "golden" stabilization
in the mid-1920s — a flowering of [[german-modernism|cultural modernism]] in Berlin — before the
**Great Depression** destroyed its parliamentary basis. Government by presidential emergency decree
under Hindenburg from 1930, mass unemployment, and the electoral surge of the Nazis and Communists
hollowed out the centre. In January 1933 Hindenburg appointed **[[adolf-hitler|Hitler]]** Chancellor;
the Enabling Act of March 1933 ended the Republic and inaugurated [[nazi-germany|Nazi Germany]]. Why
Weimar failed — structural flaws, the Depression, or contingent elite choices — is a core question of
the [[german-sonderweg|Sonderweg debate]].

**The fiscal reading (Flynn 1944, attributed)**: [[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944|Flynn]]
argues the "golden" 1924–29 recovery was itself deficit-financed — federal and
state deficits of 20.8bn marks 1926–31, defended by a new academic doctrine
that internally held public debt is costless — with the true gap concealed in a
"double budget" of "extraordinary" items exposed by Reparations Agent-General
S. Parker Gilbert in 1928. On his account the dictatorship apparatus predated
Hitler: Article 48, drafted for public-safety emergencies, was stretched by
Chancellor Luther to enforce tax measures and then used systematically by
Brüning from 1930 to govern without a Reichstag majority — the fiscal-collapse
causal chain continued at [[nazi-seizure-of-power-1933]].

**Payne's assessment (1995)**: against doom-narratives,
[[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|Payne]] calls Weimar "the first fully
democratic political system in Germany and one of the most progressive
states in the world... the first democratic proto-welfare state in a large
country." Its fall he explains by the "concatenation of crises" 1914–33
rather than German character or economics alone — German unemployment was
no worse than democratic Norway's — and by Linz's comparative factors for
democratic survival (development level, length of liberal experience,
pre-1914 resolution of religious/regional issues, a social-democratic
rather than revolutionary left, victor/neutral status in WWI), on most of
which Weimar scored badly. Of the new post-1919 states, only Finland,
Ireland, and Czechoslovakia kept constitutional government; by 1939
authoritarian regimes outnumbered constitutional ones in Europe sixteen to
twelve.

**Berlin vs. Munich in the Republic's political geography (Evans 2015,
ch. 5, attributed)**: the Constituent Assembly deliberately convened in
Weimar to distance the democracy from Berlin's Prussian-militarist
associations. Pre-war modernism had centered on Munich's Schwabing; after
the Free Corps' 1919 "bloodbath" crushed the Munich council republic and
the Kapp Putsch pushed Bavaria's SPD government aside for Kahr's
right-tolerant "centre of order," cultural radicalism relocated to Berlin
(Grosz, Döblin, Brecht/Weill, *Die Weltbühne*) while Munich incubated the
young Nazi Party. Berlin stayed a left stronghold to the end: in July 1932
the Nazis took 37.4% nationally but only 28.7% in Berlin, where SPD+KPD
nearly doubled their vote; in November 1932 the Berlin KPD (861,000)
outpolled the Nazis (721,000). Goebbels's Gauleiter years built the Berlin
party through calculated street violence (Kurfürstendamm attacks, the
Horst Wessel martyrdom cult); after the Reichstag fire, ~200 makeshift SA
"unofficial concentration camps" operated across the city.

## The Great Coalition and Mid-1920s Stabilization (Paxton & Hessler 2011)

The Republic's recovery from the catastrophic 1923 crisis — hyperinflation,
the Ruhr occupation, Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch, and Communist risings in
Hamburg, Saxony, and Thuringia — rested on three conservative figures
working substantially outside the parliamentary framework:
[[gustav-stresemann|Gustav Stresemann]] (who built the "Great Coalition" of
Social Democrats, Democrats, Centre, and his own People's Party,
August–November 1923), General Hans von Seeckt (army commander 1920–1926,
who used Article 48 emergency powers to suppress both the Saxon/Thuringian
Left and, indirectly, the Nazi Putsch), and [[schacht-hjalmar|Hjalmar
Schacht]] (whose "miracle of the Rentenmark" — a new currency backed by a
notional mortgage on all German land, industry, and commerce rather than by
scarce gold reserves — ended the hyperinflation from November 1923). Paxton
and Hessler frame the deeper causal claim, quoting Godfrey Scheele: "the
inflation was the real German Revolution" — unlike the political revolution
of 1918–19, the 1923 inflation permanently altered German economic and
social relationships, reducing much of the middle class to poverty and
creating a constituency later available to "any savior in the event of
another economic crisis," a load-bearing precondition for later Nazi appeal.

By the late 1920s the officer corps was demonstrably *more* socially
exclusive than the pre-war imperial army: almost every officer was an
officer's son (versus 1 in 4 in the imperial army), and 1 in 4 officers was
a nobleman by 1932 (versus 1 in 5 in 1920) — evidence the "republic" never
democratized its own military.

President Ebert died in 1925 and was succeeded by Field Marshal
[[hindenburg-paul-von|Paul von Hindenburg]] — a divided Left vote, with
Communist Ernst Thälmann drawing votes from the centrist republican
candidate, let this happen. Government majorities shifted rightward again
in 1927 with the inclusion of the monarchist-nationalist DNVP.

New cartels formed during the mid-1920s boom: United Steel (Vereinigte
Stahlwerke, 1926, roughly half of German steel), Krupp, and I. G. Farben
(1925, the largest corporation on the European continent) — evidence of the
industrial concentration that would later interact with the Nazi war
economy. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

## Constitutional Anatomy (Evans 2003, attributed)

Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]) gives the fullest structural
account now ingested:

- **Constitution**: adopted 31 July 1919, "essentially a modified version of
  the constitution established by Bismarck... nearly half a century before" —
  the Kaiser replaced by a popularly elected President with independent
  legitimacy and Article 48 emergency powers. [[ebert-friedrich|Ebert]]
  invoked Article 48 on 136 occasions, deposing the elected Saxon and
  Thuringian governments and retroactively legitimizing summary executions in
  the 1920 Ruhr fighting; enforcement was asymmetric — against the left,
  "virtually unused" against the right. Because the President could threaten
  dissolution (Article 25) if a decree was rejected, Article 48 had no real
  legislative check.
- **Party system**: Evans rejects proportional representation as the chief
  destabilizer (fringe parties never exceeded ~15% combined). Twenty cabinets
  in fourteen years (average 239 days), but the deeper structure was the
  inherited political-cultural *milieux* — Social Democratic, Catholic,
  liberal, nationalist subcultures each with their own press, clubs, and
  funds. Continuity ministers (Stresemann at the Foreign Ministry across nine
  cabinets; Brauns at Labour across twelve; Gessler at the Army Ministry
  across thirteen) preserved policy coherence despite the churn.
- **Legitimacy, not machinery**: the Weimar coalition's 76.2% of January 1919
  was a permanent minority from 1920 (48% June 1920 … 43% Sept 1930). Evans:
  the constitution "was no worse than the constitutions of most other
  countries in the 1920s, and a good deal more democratic than many"; and
  "even in 1928, the Republic was as far away from achieving stability and
  legitimacy as ever" — the "Golden Years" as an illusion of hindsight.
- **State apparatus disloyalty**: the Ebert–Groener pact preserved the old
  officer corps; after the [[kapp-putsch-1920|Kapp Putsch]] (Seeckt: no army
  units against the putschists) the army served the abstract "Reich," not the
  Republic, and trained secretly in the USSR. The judiciary carried over from
  the Wilhelmine era ("party, class and bastard law," per the Judges'
  Confederation chairman on democratic legislation); Gumbel's statistics —
  flagged by Evans himself as possibly imprecise — recorded 354 right-wing
  political murders drawing 24 convictions and no executions against 22
  left-wing murders drawing 38 convictions including 10 executions. The
  irremovable 1.6-million-strong civil service worked against the Kapp
  putschists in 1920, but Evans notes the same duty-to-the-state abstraction
  "would also keep them working when the state was overthrown."
- **Federalism**: Prussia (57% of the population) under moderate SPD-led
  governments was a stabilizer, not a flaw.

## Griffin (2007): Weimar's Aufbruch and Nomic Crisis

*Modernism and Fascism* frames Weimar through Kracauer's **Aufbruch**: the early-Weimar mood of "departure from the shattered world of yesterday towards a tomorrow built on the grounds of revolutionary conceptions," in which people "believed in international socialism, pacifism, collectivism, aristocratic leadership, religious community, life, or national resurrection, and frequently presented a confused mixture of these variegated ideals as a brand-new creed." Behind it, the Sonderweg's "stressed society": belated unification plus hyper-rapid industrialization made pre-1914 Germany "the modernist nation par excellence" (Eksteins), teeming with völkisch cults, monism, occultism, life reform, eugenics, and Expressionism. Defeat, Versailles, and post-1918 chaos intensified liminoid conditions — Safranski's portrait of the early Weimar climate ("charismatics and prophets," "millennium and apocalypse," "messianism and redemption doctrines flourished on the left and the right") — breeding Broch's longing for "a Leader... the Healer who by his actions will give meaning to the incomprehensible events of the Age, so that Time can begin anew." The Depression then "triggered not just an economic and political crisis but a nomic crisis": NSDAP 2.6% (May 1928) → 37.3% (July 1932), with over half the electorate backing revolutionary anti-democratic parties. (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)
