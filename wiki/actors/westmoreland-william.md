# William C. Westmoreland (1914–2005)

U.S. Army general who served as Commander, United States Military Assistance Command, Vietnam (COMUSMACV) from 1964 to 1968, overseeing the massive escalation of American ground forces and the implementation of the attrition strategy.

## Role in Vietnam

Westmoreland inherited an advisory and limited-commitment mission and, in 1965, requested and received the deployment of large numbers of U.S. combat troops (famously the "forty-four battalions" cable of June 1965). He directed a campaign centered on large-unit "search-and-destroy" operations intended to find, fix, and destroy main-force Viet Cong and North Vietnamese Army units, using superior firepower and mobility (especially airmobile operations) to inflict unsustainable casualties.

Under his command the Army applied the "Army Concept" at scale: emphasis on body counts as the primary metric of success, generous use of artillery and air support, and short one-year tours that limited unit cohesion and local knowledge. Pacification and the "other war" received secondary priority and resources, even after the creation of CORDS in 1967.

**Specifics from intervention phase (Krepinevich Ch. 6–7):** June 1965 44-battalion request: "The GVN cannot stand up successfully to this kind of pressure without reinforcement." "No more niceties about defensive posture and reaction"; "had to forget enclaves and take the war to the enemy." Explicit concept: U.S. forces "against the hardcore DRV/VC forces in reaction and search and destroy operations" to free Vietnamese for populated areas. Ia Drang (Nov 1965) hailed as proof: "the ability of the Americans to meet and defeat the best troops the enemy could put on the field of battle was once more demonstrated beyond any possible doubt, as was the validity of the Army’s airmobile concept." Argued big units "had to be pounded with artillery and bombs and eventually brought to battle"; enemy focus on Highlands to draw Americans from lowlands. Resisted Marine CAP approach ("They just wouldn’t play"); preferred search-and-destroy. Supported short tours ("good for morale" / "look after its long-term interests"). Body count as "the criterion for measuring success." After PROVN and McNamara doubts, continued emphasis on attrition. "it was not enough merely to contain the big units."

**Daddis reassessment (Range 03, Ch. 4–5 implementation)**: Westmoreland's strategy explicitly combined search-and-destroy (as principal tactic within the attrition/containment mission) with concurrent population security and pacification ("the parallel war"). Post-Tet: "Basically, I see no requirement to change our strategy. Friendly forces still must find, fix, fight, and destroy the enemy, and concurrently provide the necessary security for the population." Border battles served to prevent NVA expansion of bases that would support guerrillas "in the vital battle for the people." The "parallel war" was integral: CORDS integration directives, nine-step 1968 pacification program using civil/military teams to supplement Revolutionary Development, emphasis on GVN commitment and RF/PF holding after US/ARVN clearing. "US forces are more aggressive... will inevitably find themselves involved in many of the tougher fights but by no means all of them." Planned "to make a major effort to fill in behind US and ARVN forces in areas to be pacified with regional and popular forces and pacification cadre." Daddis shows the elements often worked at cross-purposes ("The elements of the parallel war in fact tended to work against one another"), and implementation faced advisory friction and GVN limits, but the concept was not reducible to attrition alone. Komer: "There must be a parallel pacification offensive..." Tet caused major regression (38% hamlet holdings lost, 2.7M people from secure category), exposing fragility despite main-force successes. Westmoreland could not ignore main-force destruction without endangering the population security pillar.

## Tet and Aftermath

At the start of 1968 Westmoreland's faith in attrition remained unshaken; he sought approval for intensified operations (unrestricted bombing of North, Laotian incursion, Cambodian drive) and had predicted 1968 as the year allies "would finally turn the corner." Pre-Tet diversion of forces (1st Cav + 101st brigade) to counter NVA at Khe Sanh (a position Taylor called "the last place you would want to have a large garrison") left populated areas exposed. The Tet Offensive (30-31 Jan) saw 100,000 VC/NVA troops assault Saigon and 36 of 43 provincial capitals. MACV counted 37,000+ enemy dead (VCI losses up to 30%); Westmoreland compared it to the Battle of the Bulge. "the Communists had utterly failed"; from MACV perspective Tet advanced attrition goals.

Politically the offensive was catastrophic. Combined with reinforcement requests it triggered civilian review. Wheeler (with Westmoreland) reported enemy "has the will and the capability to continue" and "RD program had suffered a severe setback" yet recommended "regain the initiative through offensive operations" plus options for +206k troops and cross-border relaxation. Westmoreland back-channel: top priority "clear enemy main forces out of the populated regions" followed by "hard hitting offensive campaigns to invade base areas, interdict... eliminate or evict VC/NVA." ISA alternative (demographic frontier) rejected in favor of more of the same. Outcome: troop ceiling frozen at 549,500 (no 200k augmentation), Westmoreland recalled to become Army Chief of Staff in face-saving move, partial bombing halt, Johnson non-candidacy. "The Army approach to the war in Vietnam had lost its credibility with the political leadership."

From the insurgent perspective Tet succeeded on decisive dimensions despite military losses: "substantial setback" to pacification, 800,000 refugees, 1,001 U.S. KIA that eroded American will "more quickly than MACV could wear down the insurgent forces," and demonstration that even urban areas were not safe. "Tet was a victory for Hanoi." The Army "had convinced itself that it had won the Tet Offensive; therefore, no soul-searching was necessary."

**Vietnamization transition:** With ceiling imposed, policy shifted to Vietnamization and U.S. withdrawal. Westmoreland's departure marked the end of the high-tide attrition phase under the Concept he had implemented at scale.

The 1968 Tet Offensive was a military defeat for the Communists in terms of forces destroyed, but it shattered American public confidence and is widely viewed as the turning point of the war. Westmoreland was replaced by Creighton Abrams in mid-1968; the change in command coincided with a doctrinal and resource shift toward pacification, though the fundamental strategic situation remained difficult.

## Assessment (per Krepinevich)

Krepinevich portrays Westmoreland as both a product and an enforcer of the Army Concept. He was a capable conventional commander operating within an institutional framework that made successful counterinsurgency extremely difficult. His requests for more troops and his focus on attrition reflected the service's preferred way of war rather than a tailored response to the political-military realities of Vietnam.

## Daddis Reassessment (Range 01 grounding from Preface, Introduction, Ch. 1, early Ch. 2; plus Range 04 Ch. 6 + Conclusion exclusive)

Daddis (*Westmoreland's War*, 2014) offers a direct counter-narrative: the "attrition" portrayal is a "myth" that "evolved into an unquestioned truth." Westmoreland "developed a comprehensive military strategy for Vietnam, one not confined to simple attrition of enemy forces." "It is possible... to devise a sound strategy and still lose." 

Westmoreland's three missions per his intelligence chief: "not one battle, but three to fight: first, to contain a growing enemy conventional threat; second, to develop the Republic of Vietnam’s Armed Forces (RVNAF); and third, to pacify and protect the peasants in the South Vietnamese countryside." "Political, economic, and psychological victory is equally important..." MACV tracked far more than body counts (population security, HES, development metrics). 

**ARVN/RVNAF pillar (Ch. 6 Range 04):** The RVNAF development mission was central: "the bulk of the military support of Revolutionary Development must rest on the shoulders of the ARVN." ARVN performed concurrent conventional, territorial, and RD roles. Training/advisory evolution included MATs post-1965. Persistent challenges included leadership pathologies, desertions, and dependence. Tet exposed mixed results; "the problem of ARVN leadership did not go away" into Vietnamization.

**Conclusion limits (Range 04):** "It is possible... to devise a sound strategy and still lose a war." US "attempted to do too much" (external defense + nation-building), creating dependence. "There was no magic solution." Post-colonial GVN weaknesses and Hanoi's advantages meant even comprehensive strategy (three pillars) could not prevail. Vietnamization sometimes viewed as retreat paradigm. The "uncomfortable truth" that sound strategy is not always sufficient.


Cold War context mattered: Westmoreland personally advanced counterinsurgency education as West Point superintendent (Counterinsurgency Committee; 54 hours integrated instruction) and "openly brought perspectives gained from his tenure at West Point to his command of MACV." Pre-1965 army doctrine and officers engaged Maoist theory, decolonization, civic action, "revolutionary development," and broad definitions of strategy and counterinsurgency ("political, social, and economic rather than military problems"). Early transition (MAAG/McGarr "clearing and holding"; CIP) already reflected holistic plans; "by the time William Westmoreland arrived in Saigon in mid-1964, key US officials had been debating strategic issues for nearly five years and most understood that any policy for Vietnam required more than simply military might." Westmoreland warned escalation required a "reasonably firm political, military, and psychological base" for the GVN.

## Legacy

Westmoreland's name remains closely associated with the American conduct of the ground war in Vietnam and with debates over whether a different strategy (more population security, different force ratios, earlier Vietnamization) could have produced a better outcome. After Vietnam he served as Army Chief of Staff (1968–1972) during the transition to the all-volunteer force.

## Daddis Reassessment (2014; Ch. 2–3 Range 02 grounding): Comprehensive Concept vs. Attrition Myth

Daddis (grounded exclusively in range) provides the essential counter to the dominant portrayal. Westmoreland "had not one battle but three to fight: first, to contain a growing conventional threat; second to develop the Republic of Vietnam’s Armed Forces (RVNAF); and third, to pacify and protect the peasants in the South Vietnamese countryside."

MACV mission (1965): "to assist and support the RVNAF in their efforts to defeat communist subversive insurgency, and to accelerate effective GVN control over the country." "Westmoreland did not subscribe to an attrition strategy aimed solely at killing the enemy. ... Westmoreland and his staff unmistakably incorporated nonmilitary means into their strategic planning."

"defeating the enemy’s ground forces served only as a means to the larger end of securing the south’s population." "the insurgency in South Vietnam must eventually be defeated among the people in the hamlets and towns." "the main purpose of defeating the enemy through offensive operations against his main forces and bases must be to provide the opportunity through RD to get at the heart of the matter, which is the population of South Vietnam." "Neither facet could be ignored" (termites/bully boys). "the war in Vietnam is a political as well as a military war."

**Three-phase sustained campaign ("one war"):** Phase I halt losing trend (bases, centers, RVNAF); Phase II offensive + pacification support; Phase III remaining destruction. "all political, military, economic, and security (police) programs must be completely integrated..."

"Westmoreland’s strategy rested on the three pillars of attrition, pacification, and ARVN training." "one part of the three-pillared strategy was being mistaken for the whole." "catchphrases like 'attrition strategy'... fail to convey the true nature of Westmoreland’s approach." "The claim that 'several years were to pass before the American command began to accept the crucial role of population security' simply does not hold up when one scrutinizes the documentary evidence."

**"Long pull" & metrics:** "we are in for the long pull." Attrition language signaled protracted war, not sole focus. Honolulu (1966): +10% secure pop, +20% roads/rail, +30% base destruction, +235k pacified; enemy attrition at replacement rate. "body counts... constituted only one of many statistical indicators." HES (1967): graded "hamlets in both security and development areas" (NLF activities, local defense, health/education/welfare); 220 advisors, 13k hamlets, 90k pages/mo.

**Vs. critics (enclaves, PROVN, Marines):** Enclaves (Gavin) rejected as initiative loss/"magnets"/"inglorious, static"; "No one ever won a battle sitting on his ass." PROVN: "we were already doing pretty much all"; 53/140 implemented, 45 partial. Marines "balanced strategy" "remarkably similar" to MACV multipronged.

**Context:** "unless a 'fairly effective government' existed in Saigon, 'no amount of offensive action by the United States... has any chance by itself of reversing the deterioration underway.'" US faith in mil power for political change + recognition "force alone could not achieve their objectives." No deliberate OOB cover-up (debates known to Johnson/McNamara/JCS).

While execution shortfalls and limits of external power in post-colonial setting are acknowledged, Daddis demonstrates the concept was far more comprehensive than the "attrition general" caricature. `contrasts_with:` Krepinevich/Nagl/Summers on the wiki.
