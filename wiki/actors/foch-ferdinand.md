# Ferdinand Foch

French general (1851–1929; Marshal 1918). Commanded 9th Army at the Marne (1914); chief of General Staff 1917; pivotal in 1918 unity of command (Doullens coordinator, Beauvais strategic direction); directed Allied 1918 counteroffensives.

**1914–1915 Siege/Offensives (Doughty Ch. 3–4 exclusive range):** Post-Marne, assistant to C-in-C directing left-wing armies/coordination with British/Belgians (later Northern Army Group). Emphasized "character of a siege with its method and its slowness" for Vimy. March 1915 note to Joffre: offensive form always "general action along the entire front; a decisive action... breaking through the enemy" (Vimy decisive). Coordinated May–June Artois and Sept–Oct 1915 double (Artois+Champagne); pushed British. Furious at d’Urbal Oct corps shift but left in command; ordered 10 Oct attack; blamed "insufficient" prep fire. Insisted army not remain "inactive" 100–150 m from crest.

**Somme 1916 (Doughty Ch. 6, Range 4):** Commander of Northern Army Group; directed the French portion of the Somme offensive (Sixth Army under Fayolle primary). Had long-standing reservations: as late as 31 May advised against offensive on Somme in 1916 (compartmented terrain, villages, natural obstacles after 10–15 km; enormous heavy artillery required for limited results). At 31 May meeting with Poincaré/Briand/Roques relented and agreed to support offensive to assist Verdun but "not to break through enemy lines." Privately continued expressing doubts about large French commitment or British cooperation. Provided Sixth Army 552 heavy artillery pieces (more than British main attack). Note to battalions: infantry advance only after artillery destruction; "small groups, arrayed in depth"; "strict economy" of infantry; "Real combat is a struggle of long duration." On 1 July French (XX Corps north, colonials south) achieved all first-day objectives while British largely failed; later pushed to Maisonette. Frustrated with British (shifted priority south; criticized "infantile" tactics). Joffre lectured him on broad-front principles after visit to Fayolle. See [[events/battle-of-the-somme-1916]] and [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/battle-of-the-somme-1916]].

## Narrative

Pre-war and early war: removed from Northern Army Group late 1916 after Somme criticism (Joffre); minor planning cell, then Eastern Army Group temp, Italy coordination. Appointed chief of the General Staff May 1917 alongside Pétain as C-in-C. Smaller formal role initially than Pétain but attended War Committee meetings; demonstrated ability to simplify concepts; curried favor with Lloyd George (e.g. Italian theater interest). Language skills and broad vision aided coalition role.

**1917–early 1918 strategic vision.** Advocated flexible allied strategy with offensive flavor: counterattacks and "counteroffensives for disengagement"; "seize every opportunity to impose their will on the adversary by resuming as soon as possible the offensive, the only means of obtaining victory." Opposed passive waiting; cited Verdun halted by Somme. Clashed with Pétain over 1918: Foch saw powerful offensive as best halt to enemy attrition; Pétain insisted "the period of waiting will last throughout 1918" and "the battle of 1918 will be defensive... The absence of means imposes this on us." (Ch. 9, ~408-411)

President of Executive War Board (Supreme War Council); pushed allied general reserve (~30 divs) and "agent" for coordination. Opposed by Pétain and Haig. At Doullens (26 Mar 1918) appointed to "coordinate the action of the allies’ armies on the Western Front" (limited initially); at Beauvais (3 Apr) given "strategic direction of military operations" + coordination powers. Bypassed Pétain/Haig to issue direct guidance (e.g. to Gough, Fayolle, Debeney); pushed limited French offensives south of Somme while insisting on reserves north. (Ch. 9, 437-442)

**During 1918 offensives.** After Michael, urged no further withdrawal; directed French reserves north. For Lys, yielded to Haig demands for reliefs but believed main threat remained Somme-Arras. Insisted on preparing counteroffensives on shoulders of salients (Kemmel, Amiens/Montdidier). Clashed with Pétain over pace/ambition of French attacks and allocation of reserves (north vs Paris sector). Note of 16 Jun on doctrine (strengthen forward vs infiltration) provoked Pétain refusal to forward it. Clemenceau ultimately enforced subordination of Pétain to Foch. By late June, Foch directed resumption of allied offensive planning. (Ch. 9)

Optimism and conviction contrasted Pétain's pessimism; key to political acceptance of expanded powers.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` effective (if imperfect) unity of command on Western Front by Apr 1918; coordination of reserves and counteroffensive planning that contributed to halting Germans and transition to Allied offensives. `contributed_to:` final victory but also inter-Allied frictions (British criticism of French performance). `enabled:` Foch to direct 1918 campaign.

## Historiography

Doughty (French archival) presents Foch as visionary coalition figure whose optimism proved politically decisive and whose coordination prevented worse separation of armies, while acknowledging friction with Pétain and limits of initial Doullens powers. Contrasts British views of Foch as sometimes "amazingly ignorant" or peremptory early on. Emphasizes evolution from 1917 staff role to de facto strategic direction. **Range 7 (Ch. 10 "A Strategy of Opportunism" + Conclusion, exclusive cache):** Foch's strongest attribute "opportunism": "Foch recognized that military advances could occur only when the opportunity for action appeared. As a strategist, Foch revealed himself to be a man who lacked any grand strategic theories but who had the vision, energy, and knack for seizing the opportunity for action when it appeared." Unity of command (Doullens 26 Mar "coordinate the actions of allied armies on the Western Front"; Beauvais 3 Apr "strategic direction"; 17 Apr "general-in-chief"; 26 Jun compelled Pétain obey) gave "sufficient authority... to exert considerable influence... enabled him to seize the initiative from the Germans in July 1918 and to drive them out of France with a series of relentless attacks." (l 461-462).

Operational: learned 1915 breakthrough impossible; 1916/17 mass blows same point high cost. Pétain "great grub." Foch twist "rapid series of blows to keep the Germans off balance"; "punching with his right fist, then his left, and then again with his right and following these blows with a vigorous kick." Mid-Jun still expected 1919 final; "enabled allied armies to fight the final, decisive campaign in 1918, not 1919." 1 Jul memo halt 5th drive (40 km Abbeville/60 km Paris decisive); shifted forces despite Pétain "extreme fatigue." Pétain Directive No. 5 12 Jul per Foch: "Henceforth the armies should envisage the resumption of the offensive... simple, audacious, and rapid procedures of attack... offensive spirit developed to the maximum." (l 462-465).

Marne salient (18 Jul): early GQG study; Foch 14 Jun Soissons rail/limited; Mangin proposal enthusiastically backed by Pétain "best chance of success but also the opportunity for a fruitful exploitation; additionally... most effective parry." Foch concurred (Sixth combine; Fifth east); initially reserved reserves Flanders but 11 Jul released + Haig asks. 15 Jul German attack; Pétain 0930 suspended Tenth prep. Foch noon intervened: do not halt Tenth; "did not want them taken from Tenth Army." 16 Jul diverted Br to Third. "Foch’s optimism and his willingness to take a calculated risk earned him the credit for the subsequent victory." 17 Jul order: Tenth main (10+6 divs, 1545 guns 301 tanks; US 1st/2nd XX). 18 Jul 0435 surprise (tanks + rolling); US 2nd 8 km deepest; "greatest day since the Marne." Germans evac 1145; to Aisne-Vesle 3 Aug. 29k prisoners +612 cannon etc.; 45 km shorten; Paris threat removed; 95,165 Fr casualties. 5 Aug Marshal ("maneuver admirably conceived... superbly executed by incomparable leaders"). "one of the most important operations of the war" (drove salient, lowered Ger morale, raised allied, gave initiative). (l 466-474).

24 Jul: "The moment has come to abandon the general defensive attitude imposed upon us until now by [our] numerical inferiority and to pass to the offensive." Quick successive blows (salients first; then mining/Dunkirk). Brief intervals prevent Ger reserve use/rebuild. 8 Aug Amiens: "Enlarge the battle, go forward as much as I can." Fayolle concentrate important points "whose possession will result in the general recoil." "black day" (Ludendorff "limits of our endurance"). Converging to Mézières (30 Aug Pershing: cut 3 main rails). US tensions (Pershing "destroys the American army"; chose Meuse-Argonne). Sep offensive + Balkans (Franchet d’Espèrey collapse 30 Sep) multifront success forced peace offers. Slow progress: Clemenceau "much blood"; "demand that Foch take the situation 'in hand'"; 21 Oct "criminal" continue attrition "indefinitely"; "immobility of our right wing"; "the truth, the whole truth" to Wilson on Pershing; "final decision." Foch ignored ("Don Quixote"); "Your business is war, and everything that pertains to the peace... belongs to us and only us." Later "very large" contribution but "Fame is a troublesome temptress." (l 474-496).

Continued pressure: 10 Oct directive 3 converging (Maubeuge "most advantageous"; broadened Mézières 4 Fr armies). Pétain "cautious and deliberate"; "no reason to incur high casualties." 15-26 Oct 1st Army Hunding penetration; 26 Oct broke positions. Ludendorff 24 Oct "demand for unconditional surrender... challenge"; resigned 26 Oct. 19 Oct Meuse directive. Lorraine prep (Mangin Tenth "rupture"; assembled 11 Nov). Italian armistice 3 Nov. 25 Oct clash Pétain: Foch "know only their direction of attack... go as far as they can... without any concern for alignment... according to the enemy"; Pétain successive ("breakthrough phase... followed by an exploitation phase"). "final battles fought by the French bore a stronger resemblance to Pétain’s than Foch’s operational concept." (l 497-504).

Fatigue: "physically tired as a result of constant combat"; supplied US most arty/tanks/air (Buat "regret that the commander-in-chief of allied forces is French"). Duval 11 Nov "without your aid we would be Boche provinces... Without the presence in that attack of your troops the attack would have been an utter impossibility... absolutely essential." "without you we would have been completely defeated this year." "French probably would have collapsed... and certainly would not have seized the initiative from the Germans in mid-July." Final drive 31 Oct "pushed forward methodically"; US Sedan race; 5 Nov Ger withdrawal; Compiegne 8 Nov; 11 Nov signed 0510/cease 1100. Foch 7 Nov: "The enemy, disorganized by our repeated attacks, is yielding on the entire front. It is urgent to maintain and accelerate our efforts." (l 504-507).

Conclusion "Misery": victory at terrible price; shaped interwar (Pétain cautious dominant; Debeney final mobile methods blueprint via École/CGS 1923-30, 1927-28 laws; methodical battle centralization; no early aggressive; fortifications/ally reliance). "On the morrow of the armistice, the prestige of the Army reached its highest point." Confidence in 1918 methods. (l 508-516).
