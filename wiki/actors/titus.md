# Titus (Titus Flavius Vespasianus)

The **second Flavian emperor** (79–81) — Vespasian's elder son, the conqueror of Jerusalem and
*particeps imperii*, whose brief, popular reign was overshadowed by the eruption of Vesuvius and
who became, in the Roman tradition, "the delight and darling of the human race." Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 1.

## Particeps imperii and the conquest of Jerusalem

Born in **39 CE** and raised in the court of Claudius and Nero, Titus was made by his father the
**military arm of the regime**: he completed the Jewish War with the **storming of Jerusalem
(70)** and the destruction of the Temple, celebrated in the joint **triumph of 71** and the
**Arch of Titus**. Uniquely, he served as his father's **colleague in the censorship**, held the
praenomen **Imperator**, shared the tribunician power, and — controversially — became
**praetorian prefect**, in which role he did the regime's "dirty work" (eliminating opponents)
while shielding Vespasian's reputation for clemency. This gave him a reputation for **cruelty and
rapacity** (and the scandal of his affair with the Jewish queen **Berenice**) that he had to
overcome on accession.

## The transformation on accession (79)

CAH XI stresses Titus' deliberate **transmutation** from "obedient but dissolute son" — a
"Prince Hal" feared as a second Nero — into the "pious and responsible successor." He **dismissed
Berenice**, renounced his revels, deified his father, and made *clementia* his keynote: he
pledged on assuming the pontificate not to put any citizen to death (and kept his word, "the
shortness of his reign helping him"), **banned capital cases against senators** and the *asebeia*
(treason-by-defamation) charges, expelled the *delatores*, and confirmed his predecessors'
*beneficia* unasked. His **generosity** (Martial's *De Spectaculis*) and the completion of the
**Colosseum** and his **Baths** burnished the dynasty's image — though, like his father, he was a
shrewd financier who funded reconstruction from new revenue (the estates of the heirless).

## Disasters and death

Two months after his accession came the **eruption of Vesuvius (24 August 79)**, destroying
Pompeii, Herculaneum, and Stabiae (the death of the elder Pliny; the younger Pliny's letters); a
three-day **fire** then ravaged Rome, and an **epidemic** followed. Titus responded with
conspicuous relief and reconstruction. He died of fever in **81** at the family villa, his short
reign leaving his **clemency unblemished** — "preserving his reputation," as Dio's verdict had
it, "only by dying young." His relations with his brother **Domitian** were tense: Titus called
him *consors et successor*, but withheld from him the tribunician power, military command, or
governorship, so that Domitian (lacking a son of Titus' to displace him) succeeded.

## Significance

Titus' reign demonstrated the **peaceful hereditary succession** Vespasian had engineered (the
first emperor to follow his father by blood) and the Flavian cultivation of the **good-emperor
image** — clemency, generosity, *pietas*. Hailed in the West as the ideal *princeps*, he was
remembered very differently in the **East** (the false-Nero risings) and among the **Jews**, who
held that his early death was punishment for the destruction of the Temple.
