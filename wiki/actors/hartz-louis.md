# Louis Hartz (1919–1986)

American political scientist, professor of government at Harvard, and the
most systematic theorist of the 1950s "consensus school" of American
historiography. His *The Liberal Tradition in America* (1955) —
[[hartz-liberal-tradition-1955|source page]] — argued that the United
States, settled as a fragment of European liberal society **without a
feudal past**, developed a single, unanimous, largely unconscious Lockean
liberalism in place of a genuine ideological spectrum: see
[[lockean-consensus]]. He extended the fragment logic comparatively to
Latin America, Canada, South Africa, and Australia in *The Founding of New
Societies* (1964) — [[hartz-founding-new-societies-1964|source page]] —
whose general theory is the [[fragment-thesis]]: feudal, liberal, and
radical fragments each freeze at their moment of departure from Europe,
bury their ideology under a substitute nationalism, and lose the
ideological "future" along with the past.

## Role and significance

- Framed the canonical post-war answer to why America produced **no genuine
  conservatism and no genuine socialism** ("no Maistre, no Marx"), and gave
  the Sombart question ("Why is there no socialism in the United States?")
  its most influential structural answer.
- With Richard Hofstadter and Daniel Boorstin, defined
  [[consensus-history]] against the Progressive (Beard/Turner)
  conflict school — while, unlike Boorstin, treating the consensus as a
  *problem*: the root of conformity, "Americanism," and McCarthy-era
  intolerance, not a virtue to celebrate.
- Supplied a foundational statement of [[american-exceptionalism]] in its
  analytic (non-celebratory) form.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Hartz, consensus historiography would still exist (Hofstadter,
Boorstin), but its comparative-theoretical spine — the fragment thesis, the
feudalism–socialism linkage — is distinctively his; the republicanism
debates of the 1960s–80s (Bailyn, Wood, Pocock) and Rogers Smith's
"multiple traditions" critique were framed explicitly *against* Hartz,
making him the negative pole around which two generations of American
political thought scholarship organized itself.

## Historiography

Hartz's thesis dominated the field for roughly two decades, then was
displaced by the republican-synthesis school and by critics noting that a
polity built on slavery, Indian removal, and nativism was never simply
"Lockean" (Smith, *Civic Ideals*, 1997). Periodic revivals continue to
treat the book as the indispensable starting point for the question of
American ideological narrowness. He suffered a breakdown in the mid-1970s,
left Harvard, and died in Istanbul in 1986.
