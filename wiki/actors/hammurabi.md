# Hammurabi of Babylon

The **sixth king of the [[babylon-first-dynasty|First Dynasty of Babylon]]**
(reigned ~1792–1750 BCE, middle chronology), the [[amorites|Amorite]] ruler who
unified southern Mesopotamia and gave his name to the most famous law-collection
of the ancient world. He is the central figure of [[middle-bronze-age|Middle
Bronze Age]] Mesopotamia — but, as the Mari archives have revealed, a far smaller
figure in his own lifetime than later fame made him. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. V, Gadd).

## The deflation of a legend

Older scholarship cast Hammurabi as the lone "great lawgiver" towering over his
age and founder of a Babylonian "world-empire." The **Mari letters** — diplomatic
dispatches written by foreign envoys who watched him candidly, with no motive to
flatter — have **cut him down to size**. For most of his forty-three years he was
"no more than a struggling aspirant," one of a half-dozen near-equal kings in a
world of shifting coalitions. The decisive text is the envoy Itur-Asdu's report of
a speech to the desert shaikhs: *"There is no king who is mighty by himself. Ten or
fifteen kings follow Hammurabi the man of Babylon, a like number Rim-Sin of Larsa,
a like number Ibal-pi-el of Eshnunna, a like number Amut-pi-el of Qatna, and twenty
follow [[iamkhad|Yarim-Lim of Yamhad]]."* In this register Babylon ranks **below**
a distant king of Aleppo; "there could be no empire of a Sargon again in the days
of Hammurabi."

## Events of the reign

Babylon under Hammurabi's first five predecessors was a "diocese of about fifty
miles radius." His own early wars (the capture of Uruk, Isin, Malgium, Rapiqum) may
have been fought as a junior partner of **[[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]]** of
Assyria, in whose oath a Babylonian contract of his tenth year associates him. His
middle years (11th–30th) were given to **canals and defensive building** — the
canal "Hammurabi-is-the-abundance-of-the-people" and its fortress. Then, in a swift
late burst:

- **Year 29 (~1764 BCE):** a great defensive victory over a coalition of **Elam,
  Assyria (Subartu), Gutium, Eshnunna and Malgium** "who had collected their forces."
- **Year 30/31 (~1763 BCE):** the defeat of his old neighbour **Rim-Sin of Larsa**
  ("king of Emutbal"), bringing all the old southern cities — Ur, Uruk, Larsa,
  Eridu, Nippur, Isin — under Babylon. Hammurabi now claimed the traditional
  **"kingship of Sumer and Akkad."**
- **Years 32–38:** campaigns up the Tigris against Eshnunna (destroyed by an
  engineered flood, year 38) and Assyria, and the **sack of [[mari|Mari]]** (year
  33 victory, year 35 dismantling) — ending the kingdom of his former ally
  **[[zimri-lim|Zimri-Lim]]**.

His real dominion never exceeded "the normal extent of a Mesopotamian unity" up the
Tigris to Assyria and up the Euphrates to about the Balikh, and it **crumbled in his
son's grasp even more quickly than it had sprung up** (see
[[babylon-first-dynasty]]).

## The administrator and the "Code"

The ~150 surviving letters of Hammurabi (to his officials Sin-iddinam and
Shamash-hazir at Larsa) show "a system too much absorbed in day-to-day detail,"
its agents denied real initiative, the king "oddly complaisant" toward complainants
— the posture, Gadd suggests, of a régime nervously **appeasing newly conquered
subjects.** His famous **law-stele** is no longer "without comparison": it stands in
a series with the laws of **Lipit-Ishtar of Isin**, the **laws of Eshnunna**, the
**"stele of righteousness" of Attakhushu of [[elam|Elam]]**, and the *mīšarum*
("righteousness") debt-remission edicts that every Old Babylonian king issued on
accession. Since "no evidence exists for the actual application of Hammurabi's
laws" in the contracts, and the code omits much of criminal law, its standing is
debated: probably a **monument of royal justice and a gesture of uniform rule** over
a freshly conquered realm rather than a working statute-book. On the Code's
threefold society — *awīlum* ("gentleman"), *muškēnum* ("subject/commoner"),
*wardum* ("slave") — see below.

## Counterfactual / analytical significance

Hammurabi is the **second realization of the Amorite empire** (after
[[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]], whom he outlived and whose Assyria he briefly
took): an Amorite warlord welding the patchwork of city-kingdoms into a personal
state. His significance is less in the size of his empire — modest and ephemeral —
than in **making Babylon the permanent metropolis of southern Mesopotamia** (a
status it kept for fifteen centuries) and in fixing the **Code** as a touchstone of
later legal and political memory. He illustrates the period's central lesson, drawn
from the Mari archive: in the Old Babylonian world, **no single power could
dominate the whole**; supremacy was always provisional, a brief tipping of a
"delicately poised scale" ([[interpolity-law|the Mari-age balance of power]]).

## Historiography

Hammurabi is the rare ancient king seen **both** through his own self-presentation
(inscriptions, date-formulae, the Code) **and** through the unflattering external
eyes of the Mari correspondents — a contrast that makes him a textbook case of how
documentary discovery can overturn a historical reputation. His absolute dates ride
on the unresolved **[[mesopotamian-chronology|middle/low/high chronology]]** debate
(the "Venus tablets" of his descendant Ammiṣaduqa are a key, inconclusive datum);
this volume uses the middle chronology (1792–1750). See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
