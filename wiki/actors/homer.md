# Homer

**Homer** is the name attached by the Greeks to the **monumental composer of the
*Iliad* and the *Odyssey***, the two great epics that crystallized the oral heroic
tradition of the [[greek-dark-age|Dark Age]] and became the foundational texts of
Greek culture. He stands at the end of this volume — the figure "into whose world"
the entire [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II.2]] leads. He is treated by Kirk
(Ch. XXXIX(b), *The Homeric poems as history*) not as a biography but as the focal
point of an oral tradition and as a source of evidence for three different ages.

## A real poet behind the tradition

Kirk treats "Homer" as a genuine individual — **the monumental composer** of each
poem — while accepting that the details of his life are largely fictitious. The poems
themselves are **traditional and oral**: the product of "one main contributor in each
case... and scores or even hundreds of preceding subsidiary singers" whose
contributions can no longer be disentangled. What distinguishes Homer is the
**building-up, for the first time, of a massive unified epic** — an act of scale and
ambition that gave him "an authority quite unparalleled," causing his arrangement and
language to be transmitted with unusual care.

The broad tradition placing him in **Smyrna or Chios** (where the **Homeridae**, the
"descendants of Homer," later claimed special rights in reciting the poems) Kirk finds
credible: the poems are "in a real sense Ionian poems," their dialect "fundamentally
and not merely superficially Ionic." Composition belongs to the **eighth century**
(before ~700, "at the very latest 675") — the world of the small, reviving
[[ionian-migration-1050bce|Ionian cities]].

## The oral singer

Homer worked in the technique of the **aoidos** (oral singer), the system illuminated
by Milman Parry and A. B. Lord's study of the **Yugoslav *guslari***. Its hallmark is
the **formula** — fixed phrase-units ("goodly Odysseus," "swift-footed Achilles,"
"wine-dark sea") shaped to fill the divisions of the hexameter line, a system of
"scope and economy" that no literate poet would build and that proves a long,
illiterate tradition behind the poems. Writing had **disappeared** from Greece with
[[aegean-scripts|Linear B]] at the Bronze Age's end and did not return (in alphabetic
form) until close to Homer's own time. The poems were thus carried — first by singers,
then by reciting **rhapsodes** — and reached a fixed text only gradually, through the
sixth-century Athenian (Panathenaic) recension and finally the Alexandrian scholarship
of **Aristarchus**. Our text is close to, but not identical with, what the monumental
composers sang.

## The three ages in the poems

The epics fuse material from three periods (see
[[homeric-poems-as-history|the Homeric poems as history]] for the full argument):

- the **Late Bronze Age** of the formal subject — the [[trojan-war-1250bce|Trojan
  War]] and the [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] palace world (boar's-tusk helmet,
  body-shield, the **Catalogue of Ships**);
- the **Dark Age** of transmission (iron as a rare prize; cremation; the absence of
  the Dorians);
- the **Ionian eighth century** of composition (the similes' world of fishing,
  farming, and seafaring; hoplite tactics; the Gorgon motif).

## Significance

Homer is, in Kirk's words, the source of "one of the most important elements of a
common culture for the whole of the Greek world." The *Iliad* and *Odyssey* gave the
Greeks their picture of the **heroic age**, their **Olympian** gods in human form
(decisively shaping [[greek-religion-and-mythology|Greek religion]]), and a shared
canon of language, value, and story that underlay Panhellenic identity from the
Archaic period on. For the historian the poems are "a wonderful... picture of a Greek
Heroic Age — an age that we should otherwise have barely guessed at."

## Historiography

The "Homeric Question" — authorship, unity, the relation of the poems to the Bronze
Age — is itself a major controversy, recorded at
[[homeric-poems-as-history|the Homeric poems as history]]. Kirk's chapter represents
the **oral-traditional** synthesis (post-Parry/Lord) that displaced the older
**Analyst** approach (which sought to dissect an *Ur-Ilias* and its redactors).
Whether Homer dictated his poem to a literate scribe, whether one poet composed both
epics, and how much genuine Bronze Age memory the verse preserves remain open. This
page treats Homer as the historically real monumental composer the tradition
describes, without resolving those disputes.

## OHCW (Taplin) — poems, craft, and historical use

[[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|Boardman/Griffin/Murray (eds.), *Oxford History of the Classical World* (1986)]], Ch. 2 (Oliver Taplin), approaches Homer *Homeron ex Homerou* — from the poems, not pocket biography. Ancient lives are largely fiction; modern consensus is meagre (date roughly **750–650 BCE**, northern Aegean Asia Minor / Smyrna area, craft learned in oral bardic tradition). For Taplin, “Homer” **is** the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*: extremely long yet highly selective (Aristotle praised Homer for not narrating the whole war as chronicle; Epic Cycle poems were shorter yet covered more events).

**Craft.** The *Iliad* picks almost the only stretch of the war when Trojans have the better of fighting; a frame of ~21 days encloses a core of roughly four days and two nights (Books 2–23) that stand for the whole war from Paris’s crime to Troy’s ashes. Achilles (adventurer for loot/glory, semi-divine knowledge of a short life) is balanced against Hector (civic responsibility before family and city; dies of shame and duty). Whether one poet wrote both epics is left open; they **complement** — *Iliad* toward destruction and tragic inscrutability, *Odyssey* toward homecoming, tested hospitality, and clearer crime/punishment.

**Homeric Question.** From F. A. Wolf (1795), “Analysts” treated the poems as multi-poet compilations against “Unitarians.” Taplin’s unitarian lean: subject to minor reservations, present form is the best stage, shaped by a poet so superior he is *The Poet*. **Milman Parry** showed oral-formulaic composition (extension and economy of noun-epithet systems) collapses classic analytic criteria based on inconsistencies and “original vs derivative” repetitions. Homeric *Kunstsprache* is artificial Ionic with Arcado-Cypriot outcrops linked via Linear B to **Mycenaean** Greek ~500 years earlier — multi-period amalgam.

**History vs poetry.** Material culture is likewise amalgam (bronze weapons + Iron Age cremation): aesthetically coherent, not Schliemann-style verification. Against M. I. Finley’s *World of Odysseus* as a stable Dark-Age social record, Taplin argues the poems are **inconsistent** on kingship, inheritance, monogamy, and the “Heroic Code” is **argued over** inside the *Iliad*, not simple consensus. Gods and Fate (e.g. Zeus’s golden scales in *Iliad* 22) are **poetic elevation of turning-points**, not extractable systematic theology. Homer **shaped** Greek historical culture for a millennium more than he records one time: “Homer affected history.”

Griffin (OHCW Intro/Ch. 3) adds: after Mycenaean collapse, poetry alone kept gold-rich Mycenae and Agamemnon in memory; Greek myth’s distinctive concern with **heroes** (not only gods) and the absence of a single orthodox version (Stesichorus/Euripides on Helen) are Homeric inheritance. Levi (Ch. 7): [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]]’s “crumbs from the great table of Homer” — tragedy as substitute for epic. Parker (Ch. 11): Homer (with [[hesiod|Hesiod]]) as informal theologians who “created a theogony for the Greeks” without creed or heresy.

## Freeman: West's revisionist position and the Oxyrhynchus evidence

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014|*Egypt, Greece, and Rome*]], 2014)
cites **M. L. West** (a leading modern Homeric scholar) for a sharper revisionist
position than Kirk's synthesis above: "Homer" may itself be a later constructed
name; a single poet worked on the *Iliad* over a lifetime, with a different author
responsible for the *Odyssey*; and the dominant Ionic dialect may point to **western
Ionia (possibly Euboea)** rather than the traditional eastern Aegean (Chios/Smyrna)
origin story. West also argued for Near Eastern epic influence on Homer (*The East
Face of Helicon*, 1999). Freeman also notes the **Oxyrhynchus papyri** (~100,000
fragments) show Homer leading all classical authors in surviving fragment count
(~1,000) — concrete evidence of his central and disproportionate role in later Greek
education, where Athenian boys memorized the epics as moral and behavioral training.
