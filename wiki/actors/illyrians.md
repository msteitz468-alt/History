# Illyrians

The **Illyrians** were the cluster of Indo-European, non-Greek-speaking peoples of the
**western Balkans** — the Adriatic hinterland from Montenegro and Albania up into
central former Yugoslavia — whose distinctive Iron Age culture (tumulus burial,
spectacle fibulae, the **Glasinac** complex) and southward expansion shaped the Early
Iron Age of the south-west Balkans. [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]]
(Ch. 15, N. G. L. Hammond) treats them alongside the Epirotes and Macedonians.

## A Pastoral, Tumulus-Building World

The Illyrians belonged to the **transhumant pastoralist** societies of the mountainous
south-west Balkans, moving flocks twice yearly between coastal and upland pastures and
congregating on the high summer pastures of the lakeland and North Pindus — the
gathering-grounds where, Hammond argues, the shepherd peoples formed their centres.
Their archaeology is known overwhelmingly from **burials under tumuli**: cemeteries of
dozens of mounds (Glasinac, Cinamak, Krume, the upper Drin), each tumulus typically
~20 m across, with a central founder-burial and later interments added down shafts.
Hammond links the **tumulus rite** to the heroic burials of the *Iliad* (Patroclus,
the double tumulus) and treats it as the diagnostic marker of these northern peoples —
its spread into Italy (Peucetia, Picenum) he reads as evidence of Illyrian settlement
across the Adriatic.

## The Great Iron Age Expansion

A central theme of Hammond's chapter is the **large-scale southward pressure of the
Illyrian tribes** during the Early Iron Age. From the "great reservoir of Illyrian
peoples" in the central Balkans they pushed:
- into the **lakeland** (Ochrid, Prespa, Maliq) and the strategic **lower and middle
  Vardar (Axius) valley**, reaching Skopje, Titov Veles, and Radanja;
- into **Epirus**, leaving typically Illyrian dedications at the shrine of **Dodona**
  and Glasinac-style bronzes in the interior;
- and even into **Thessaly** as raiders.

This expansion accelerated as the **[[phrygians|Phrygian (Brygi)]] power** in west
Macedonia receded after c. 800 BCE: "when the Phrygians left Macedonia, the country
became open to expansion of Illyrian tribes." Illyrian movement is thus part of the
same Balkan turbulence that, a few centuries earlier, had helped set the
[[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Greek migrations]] in motion.

## Significance

- `part_of:` the long [[indo-european-migrations|Indo-European settlement]] of the
  Balkans; the Illyrians are the western, non-Greek branch beside the Greek-speaking
  Epirotes and the Macedonians.
- `produced:` (probably) the tumulus-burial intrusions into south-east Italy
  (Peucetia, Picenum) — an Adriatic cultural bridge.
- `contributed_to:` the ethnic map of the historic Balkans: the Illyrian kingdoms of
  the Hellenistic age (the Ardiaei of the Zeta valley) and, in much later argument, the
  contested ancestry of the Albanians descend from this world.

## Historiography

Hammond's reconstruction is **archaeology-driven and migration-centred**, built on the
tumulus cemeteries and on bold correlations between pottery/metalwork styles and named
peoples (Illyrians, Brygi, Taulantii, Liburni) drawn from much later Greek and Roman
sources. This "**pots-and-peoples**" method — reading material-culture distributions as
direct tribal movements — is exactly the approach that later prehistorians treat with
caution (compare [[indo-european-origins]] and the Garašanin culture-history of the
[[balkans]]). The ethnic labels and the scale of the migrations are therefore debated,
but the underlying picture of a pastoral, tumulus-building western-Balkan world
expanding southward in the Early Iron Age is sound. The south-west Balkans are a region
where the collection is, unusually, moderately strong (the Albanian and Yugoslav
excavations Hammond synthesizes).
