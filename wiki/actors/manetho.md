# Manetho

**Manetho** was an Egyptian priest of the early Ptolemaic period (fl. early 3rd
century BCE) who composed the *Aegyptiaca*, a history of Egypt in Greek organized
into the sequence of royal **dynasties** that remains the backbone of
[[concepts/egyptian-dynastic-chronology|Egyptian chronology]] to this day.

## The Aegyptiaca and its survival

Manetho drew on native records — the [[concepts/palermo-stone|Palermo Stone]] type
of annal, day-books, temple-wall king-lists, and papyri from temple and palace
archives — to divide the pharaonic past into numbered dynasties, each a group of
kings "of the same family or the same town, or sometimes defined by political
criteria such as the nature of the king's relationship to his predecessors … or the
location of their principal royal residence."

Critically, **his original text does not survive.** It is known only through later
excerpts and epitomes in Josephus, Sextus Julius Africanus, Eusebius, and George
Syncellus, which often disagree. As Ian Shaw puts it, "we neither know his sources
nor have his original text." Modern Egyptology therefore uses Manetho's dynastic
skeleton while continually correcting it against contemporary monuments and the
Turin Canon.

## Significance and limitations

Manetho's scheme gave the field its enduring vocabulary (the numbered dynasties; the
later Old/Middle/New Kingdom overlay is modern). But it carries built-in distortions:
it assumes "that only one king could occupy the throne of Egypt at any one time,"
flattening co-regencies and periods of rival parallel rule into a misleadingly
continuous line — one root of the modern problem of reconciling political
chronology with the archaeological record.
