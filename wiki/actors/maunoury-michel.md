# Michel Maunoury

French general. Commander of the newly formed Sixth Army (late August 1914). Sixth Army, assembled from units shifted to the left and the former Army of Lorraine staff, was tasked with covering Paris and, from early September, with executing the critical left-flank attack against the German right wing during the Battle of the Marne (Ourcq fighting).

## Narrative

After the collapse of the initial French offensives and the British difficulties at Le Cateau, Joffre formed Sixth Army under Maunoury on the night of 26–27 August. It received the four reserve divisions and the corps shifted to the extreme left for the planned envelopment near Amiens. Joffre abolished the Army of Lorraine and sent its divisions and staff to Maunoury (also abolishing the Army of Alsace).

As the Germans drew closer to Paris, Joffre ordered Sixth Army to withdraw toward the capital. When Maunoury proposed attacking the Germans near Compiègne, Joffre replied: “Your role is to cover Paris. Therefore, withdraw toward the capital and make contact with the military governor.”

By 2–3 September intelligence (including captured maps and British/French aerial reconnaissance) confirmed that Kluck’s First Army was moving southeast of Paris. Joffre’s modified instructions of 2 September directed that “the garrison of Paris will attack in the direction of Meaux.” Gallieni ordered Maunoury on 4 September to prepare Sixth Army to march east. Leading elements moved that afternoon.

On 5 September Sixth Army marched east from Paris. Maunoury intended to position northeast of Meaux and attack along the north bank of the Marne on 6 September. Unknown to the French, the German IV Reserve Corps had moved south that morning to a position just north of Meaux. When reports arrived of French forces advancing, the German commander attacked boldly. Fighting began at 1300 hours on 5 September northwest of Meaux with 77-mm fire on French leading elements. The French made no gains that day; the Germans held the heights north of Meaux but withdrew 10 km east overnight to a stronger position.

On 6 September Kluck, reacting to the threat on his flank, ordered II Corps north to assist IV Reserve, and later IV Corps. After dark he directed further shifts. This movement opened the gap between German First and Second Armies. Maunoury’s forces pressed but made little net progress beyond the temporary gain on 6 September when the German withdrawal allowed an advance; the French struggled to hold positions and identified fallback lines. Aerial observation on 7 September revealed significant German forces shifting from south to north to face Sixth Army. As Gallieni had predicted, the engagement revealed Joffre’s maneuver.

Sixth Army’s attack, while not achieving the deep envelopment Joffre initially hoped, fixed German attention and forces on the right, enabling the British and Fifth Army exploitation of the gap that compelled the German retreat. Joffre later highlighted Maunoury’s success in protecting Paris and halting German attacks against the French left.

## Key Decisions and Consequences

- Formation and positioning of Sixth Army west of the main front (late Aug–early Sep) — created the instrument for the left-flank counteroffensive.
- Execution of the eastward march and attack on the Ourcq (5–9 Sep) — despite limited gains and the early contact revealing the plan, forced Kluck to strip corps from his center-right and open the decisive gap.
- Identification of second withdrawal line (8 Sep) — reflected the fragility of the position but the army held long enough for the broader success.

**produced:** threat that compelled Kluck’s northward shift; **enabled:** exploitation of the 1st–2nd Army gap by British and Fifth Army; **part_of:** Joffre’s Marne counteroffensive on the left.

## Historiography

Doughty gives Maunoury and Sixth Army credit for initiating the decisive flank pressure, while noting the practical difficulties (early engagement, limited progress, need for strong Paris corps that were not fully provided). The account stresses the interplay between Gallieni’s local orders, Joffre’s approval, and Kluck’s reaction rather than portraying the Ourcq fighting as independently decisive. The range underscores the risks of the early contact and the importance of Joffre not diverting resources from the left despite crises elsewhere.
