# William "Billy" Mitchell

William "Billy" Mitchell (1879–1936) was the United States' foremost early prophet of [[air-power|air power]] and the most famous American advocate of an independent air arm in the interwar years. His campaign — conducted as much through the popular press and congressional testimony as through doctrine — culminated in his 1925 court-martial and his resignation from the Army, and his vision of independent air forces was substantially vindicated only after his death by the U.S. air arm of the Second World War.

## Role

Mitchell rose through the U.S. Army Signal Corps and then its Air Service. In the First World War he commanded American air operations with the AEF, organizing "the greatest combined force of aviation ever brought under one command during our St. Mihiel and Argonne operations," and reckoned that had the war run into 1919 American airmen would have commanded all Allied air forces on the Western Front. After the war he became Assistant Chief of the Air Service.

His signature achievement was the series of demonstration bombing tests off the Virginia capes in 1921, in which his First Provisional Air Brigade sank a succession of ever-larger warships — the submarine U-117, the destroyer G-102, the cruiser Frankfurt, and finally the German dreadnought *Ostfriesland*, the "unsinkable ship" and a survivor of Jutland — culminating with two-thousand-pound bombs on 21 July 1921. For Mitchell, "The demonstration was absolutely conclusive": it proved "the ability of aircraft to destroy ships of all classes on the surface of the water."

As his public attacks on the Army and Navy grew more strident, the Secretary of War in March 1925 relieved him as Assistant Chief of the Air Service, calling his conduct "lawless," "lacking in reasonable teamwork," and "indicative of a personal desire for publicity." That August his magazine articles were collected as *Winged Defense*. After two naval aviation disasters in September 1925 he issued a nine-page indictment of "the incompetency, criminal negligence, and almost treasonable administration of the National Defense by the Navy and War Departments." President Coolidge himself preferred the charges; Mitchell was court-martialled, found guilty on 17 December 1925, and suspended from active duty. He resigned from the Army on 1 February 1928 (the editor notes a 1926 resignation date is sometimes given) and continued his campaign as a private citizen until his death.

## Argument and Consequences

Mitchell's case was that "the advent of air power has completely changed all former systems of national defense." Aircraft had abolished the protective value of America's oceans — "The former isolation of the United States is a thing of the past" — and only an independent air force, run by airmen, could exploit the new arm. His impact in his own lifetime was muted: *Winged Defense* sold only about forty-five hundred copies in the last five months of 1925, the peak of the court-martial sensation, and the isolationist climate (which gave him no credible threat to point at) undercut his attempts before the Morrow Board. His influence was therefore largely domestic and posthumous, validated by the strategic air forces and the eventual creation of an independent U.S. Air Force after the Second World War.

## Counterfactual Significance

Mitchell's counterfactual weight lies less in original theory than in agitation: he forced air power onto the American political agenda a generation early and martyred himself to the cause of an autonomous air force. Without his publicity — the *Ostfriesland* spectacle, the Saturday Evening Post articles, the trial — the institutional fight for an independent air arm would likely have come later and more quietly. His specific predictions (continent-spanning bombers, cities surrendering to the mere threat of attack) outran the technology of his day, so his significance is organizational and rhetorical rather than predictive.

## Thought

Mitchell defined air power simply as "the ability to do something in or through the air," noting that "as the air covers the whole world there is no place that is immune from influence by aircraft." He insisted that the new arm had abolished the old frontier: "In a trice, aircraft have set aside all ideas of frontiers. The whole country now becomes the frontier." He believed air battles fought "miles away from the frontiers will be so decisive" that a defeated nation "will be willing to capitulate without resorting to a further contest on land or water," yet he qualified this with the realist caveat that "everything begins and ends on the ground." Unlike pure offensive theorists, he retained a robust role for the defensive: "the only effective defense against aerial attack is to whip the enemy's air forces in air battles." He demanded organizational revolution — a Department of National Defense over co-equal departments of the Air, Army, and Navy — and held that "a man must be an airman to handle air power," his "air-going people" forming "a separate class" as different from landsmen "as those of seamen are from those of land men."

## Historiography

The editor of *Roots of Strategy* (see [[sources/jablonsky-roots-of-strategy-4-1999]]) treats Mitchell as more agitator and propagandist than systematic theorist: *Winged Defense* is "a somewhat repetitious and, in his own words, 'hastily compiled collection'" whose "strong point was not systematic or rigorous thinking." Mitchell's text is riddled with internal contradictions — claiming both "the vulnerability of the whole country to aircraft" and that "America could entirely dispense with her sea-going trade if she had to" — and laced with exaggerations for the popular press ("transportation is the essence of civilization"). The editor stresses that his thinking *drifted* over time: his 1921 book allotted 60 percent of resources to pursuit and only 20 percent to strategic bombardment, but by 1925 the emphasis was on independently applied bombers paralyzing an enemy's "vital centers," and after 1926 — confirmed by a 1927 European trip — his predictions inflated and his views drifted toward [[douhet-giulio|Douhet]]'s ("to convert his opponents by killing them first," and the 1930 gas-bombing scenario in which a city has "only one alternative and that is surrender"). The editor also draws the irony of a striking parallel between Mitchell's emerging air-power doctrine and [[mahan-alfred-thayer|Mahan]]'s [[sea-power|sea power]] — both demanded aggressive command of their medium and grounded national strength in geography, technology, and public support. The recurring assessment, like the comparison sometimes made to Woodrow Wilson, is of a visionary undone by an inability to compromise.
