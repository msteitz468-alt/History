# Russian Empire

The **Russian Empire** was a historical state that existed from 1721 to 1917, ruling vast territories across Europe, Asia, and North America.

From Wirtschafter (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II Part IV, ll. 9299–9645): Raznochintsy incorporated "social and economic relationships that lay outside the framework of official ‘society’", facilitating and undermining control; pre-1861 illicit entrepreneurship common (e.g. serf Nikolai Shipov 1813–1844). Table of Ranks (Peter the Great 1722) institutionalised mobility: rank 8 hereditary nobility, 9–14 personal. Military service spawned "floating population" of soldatki and soldatskie deti. Civil society evolved from pre-political print/sociability (18th c.) to politically organised via noble assemblies (Catherine era) and especially zemstvos (1864) post-emancipation, which "helped to fill the vacuum created by the removal of seignorial authority" while linking localities to national issues. Professions (physicians ~3/4 public sector, teachers, lawyers) gained relative autonomy via zemstvos; activist minorities politicised but rank-and-file + paraprofessionals (street advocates, fel’dshery, uncertified teachers) bridged to ordinary people, forming "a Russian civil society distinct from official society" in everyday services and market relations. `produced:` raznochintsy/intelligentsia/professions strata; `contributed_to:` pre-1905 civil society; `part_of:` long-19th-century social dynamics. 

From Evtuhov (ll. 9657–10098): Nizhnii Novgorod vignette illustrates provincial imperial society: kremlin/fair (largest in Europe, 200m roubles turnover, 1.5m attendance summer); mixed economy (agriculture + kustar industry); Old Believer communities; 1861 emancipation experienced as religious procession 12 March; dynamic zemstvo (schools/hospitals/roads/cadaster); merchant duma; cultural flourishing (theatre, Gubernskie vedomosti, music circles, astronomy, archival commission, museums). Post-reform "demographic upsurge" + institutions transformed "relatively quiet provincial town" into "bustling and growing city". `produced:` example of Great Reforms local impact; `analogous_to:` other guberniia capitals. 

From Engel (ll. 10805–11000): Women/family: Petrine reforms (1701 dress laws applied widely; 1702/1722 marriage consent/betrothal; Smolnyi 1764); persistent patriarchy ("unlimited obedience"); soldatki marginalised by conscription (freed from serfdom but landless/vulnerable, many to prostitution/domestic service); limited non-elite education; reform-era "woman question" (Pirogov post-Crimean nurses essay; Alexander II girls' schools for "future mothers"). `contrasts_with:` European separate spheres (domestic extended for noblewomen via property mgmt); `contributed_to:` long-19th-century gender debates.

From Marrese (ll. 11335–11750+): Gender/law: 1753 noblewomen gained right "to alienate and manage their property during marriage"; by 19th c. "noblewomen controlled as much as one-third of the land and serfs"; active in markets (similar to men). Inheritance: statutory 1/14 immoveable + 1/8 personal for daughters. But family law codified "unlimited obedience" (1782 Statute, 1832 Svod art. 107); criminal law post-1861 emphasised female dependence/vulnerability ("like children and the mentally incompetent"). 18th-c. law muted gender in property vs. 19th-c. innovations highlighting difference. Women litigated/controlled assets despite patriarchal norms; transactions with husbands debated/allowed by 1825. `produced:` distinctive noblewomen property rights (vs. European codes); `contrasts_with:` 18th-c. property autonomy vs. family/criminal subordination; `part_of:` judicial system pre/post 1864 reforms.

## Biography / Description
The Russian Empire was proclaimed by Peter the Great on November 2, 1721, following the Treaty of Nystad which concluded the Great Northern War. Peter was declared "Emperor" by the Senate, succeeding the Tsardom of Russia. 

The empire was governed as an absolute monarchy by the Romanov dynasty. It grew to be the third-largest empire in history, stretching from the Baltic Sea to the Pacific Ocean. It was dissolved during the February Revolution of 1917, when Nicholas II abdicated.
  From Lieven (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II ch.1–3 + intro, ll. 4155, 4277, 4295, 4395, 4501–4502, 4583–4585): “Imperial Russia” 1689–1917 from Peter proclamation to March 1917 fall. “Tsarist regime directed one of the most successful examples of territorial expansion in history.” “Until... Japan... the only example of a non-Western polity which had challenged effectively the might of the European great powers.” “Much of the history... viewed through the prism of its position on the periphery of the European continent... crucial source of models, emulation and challenges.” “Dilemma of modern empire”: continental scale + multiethnicity vs nationalism; “tsarism was very far from having solved the dilemmas of empire” by 1914; “Non-Russian nationalism would only become a major danger if Russia entered a European war...” which happened 1914–17. Empire “huge burden on the Russian people.” 1721 formalised as European empire (Bassin ll. 5141). “Europeanising” + “national empire” visions competed (ch.3). From Weeks ch.2: nationalities policy evolved from co-opting aristocracies to post-1863 Russification (Valuev on Ukrainian; 1907 Duma “Russian in spirit”). From ch.7 Martin: 1812 reinforced xenophobic nationalism + paranoid style; contributed to autocratic stabilisation and  Decembrist reaction. From Hughes/Bartlett (ch.4–5): St Petersburg embodiment of Westernisation/culture; 19th c. national culture flowering amid empire strains. Collapse 1917 amid WWI (Lieven).

## Governing Institutions
* **Governing Senate (1711):** Established by Peter I as the supreme administrative and judicial organ of the empire, overseen by an Ober-Procurator ("the sovereign's eye").
* **Collegia / Colleges (1717):** Board-based ministerial departments that replaced the old Muscovite *prikazy*.
* **Holy Synod (1721):** A state-controlled commission that replaced the Patriarchate, placing the Russian Orthodox Church under direct secular oversight.
* **Provinces:** The empire was subdivided into provinces (gubernias), reorganised by Catherine the Great in 1775 into 50 provinces with class-based courts and Marshals of the Nobility.

From Hartley (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II, provincial/local government, ll. 14205–14499 exclusive range): The most serious conflict between the zemstvos and the central bureaucracy focused on the scope for inter-zemstvo contact and co-operation, "a conflict which became part of the liberal opposition movement to the tsar up to and including the year 1905". Zemstvos restricted to provincial/district matters out of fear they would seek "the ‘crowning edifice’ of a national body". Governors "always trod a difficult line, responsible for the conduct of local affairs whilst at the same time they were representatives of both the tsar and the central government" with "over 100,000 papers per year by the 1840s". Russia "seriously under-manned at all levels"; 1763: 16,500 officials vs Prussia's 14,000 in 1% land area; 6 admins per 10k inhabitants 1796, rising only to 17 by 1857. "The conclusion has to be that Russia was under- rather than over-governed throughout the imperial period." Corporate institutions (noble assemblies, urban dumas) failed to develop strong independence due to service obligations and collective responsibility; "all-estate" attempts included 1775 boards of social welfare and 1864 zemstvos (elected on property curia, noble dominance, "Third Element" professionals ~70k by ~1900 radicalised). Zemstvos advanced healthcare/primary education (20k schools turn of century to over 40k by 1914) but faced governor oversight and peasant resentment over taxes/burdens. `part_of:` [[russian-empire|Russian Empire]] provincial and local government; `contributed_to:` civil society and pre-1905 opposition; `contrasts_with:` over-governed assumptions. See also [[serfdom-in-russia]], [[great-reforms-of-alexander-ii]].

From Waldron (State finances, ll. 14501–14700+): Military expenditure "consistently accounted for more than half of the Russian state’s spending and, at times, more than 60 per cent of the budget" in 18th c., growing to 820m roubles by 1913 (from 420m 1900). Poll tax Peter the Great "levied this tax on most of the male population" replacing household tax; rose from 4m to 10.4m roubles 1726-1796; still 59m annually late 1870s. Liquor: tax farming peaked 43% revenue 1780, abolished 1863; state monopoly post-1894 yielded gross >950m roubles 1913. Salt monopoly loss-making, abolished 1880. State peasants obrok standardized by Peter, converted to redemption 1886 boosting revenue (32m avg 1880-5 to 43m 1887-90). Railways: Ministry of Communications from 2.5% budget 1885 to 20% 1908; Trans-Siberian cost >1bn roubles. Education: central share of rural schools 11% 1879 to 45% 1911; total education 2.69% budget 1881 to 7.2% 1914. Crises (wars, poor harvests, cholera, banking) met with paper money and loans; 1859 debts 441m domestic +365m foreign. "The absence of proper cabinet government... also contributed to the lack of a clear direction in financial policy." `caused_by:` wars + expansion + growing state role; `produced:` high military-fiscal burden; `part_of:` Demographics & Finance. 

* **Fiscal Structure:** Driven primarily by the introduction of the poll (head) tax (1718–1722) which merged slaves and serfs into a single tax bracket. A massive portion of imperial spending (often 46%) was dedicated to maintaining the army and navy. Military outlays 70-80% under Peter; railways/education/liquor monopoly added major 19th-c. calls.

## 1900–1917 (from Service lines 388–528, 541–599)
In 1900 the empire covered a sixth of the world’s surface, stretching from Baltic/Black Seas to Pacific. It was "a continent unto itself" with prospective enemies west (Germany/Austria-Hungary), south and east (Japan). Nicholas II supported Witte’s rapid industrialization (8% annual manufacturing/mining growth late 1890s; 6% 1907–1914; 50,000 km rail by 1914, Trans-Siberian). Grain exports made it the world’s greatest exporter. The village land commune remained the core rural institution. Social structure was "deeply fissured": peasants (90% population) alienated by Emancipation terms; workers tied to villages yet militant; intelligentsia anti-tsarist. Russians were only 44% of population; national consciousness among them patchy. Bloody Sunday (9 Jan 1905) sparked revolution; Duma conceded but manipulated. Stolypin’s reforms limited (10% households exited commune by 1916). Lena massacre (Apr 1912) revived strikes. WWI (1914) exposed strains; Feb Revolution (1917) ended the dynasty 2 March. The empire dissolved into revolution and civil war.

## Demographics & Finance
* **Population Growth:** The empire grew from 13 million subjects in 1725 to 19 million in 1762, and 36 million in 1796 (spurred by the partitions of Poland and conquest of Crimean territories). By 1917, the population exceeded 170 million.
* **Fiscal Structure:** Driven primarily by the introduction of the poll (head) tax (1718–1722) which merged slaves and serfs into a single tax bracket. A massive portion of imperial spending (often 46%) was dedicated to maintaining the army and navy.

## Empire and Periphery (from Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II, Lieven ch. 1, lines ~4267–4666)
Tsarist Russia as "worthy member of this imperial ‘club’" despite "relatively unfavourable location". "the only example of a non-Western polity which had challenged effectively the might of the European great powers." "Tsarist history belongs not just to the overall history of empire but also, more specifically, to the modern story of the expansion of Europe." Expansion "depended on imported European institutions, technologies and even cadres"; ideology "European civilising mission". (ll. 4275–4301)

Key acquisitions: "the Baltic provinces, Ukraine and Poland". Baltic opened trade; Ukraine/"New Russia" core of agriculture/industry (Odessa "looked just like an American city"); Poland "biggest thorn" but economic contributor and glacis. "Being recognised as the rulers of a European great power and empire ... was central to the Romanovs’ self-esteem and identity". Objective reasons: "predator states" subjecting globe; alternative unappetising. (ll. 4308–4321)

Success 1700–1815 via "effective military and fiscal state apparatus". Peter I and successors; advantages over Western Europe (less feudal baggage; "second wave" not lumbered by venal institutions). "autocracy and its alliance with a serf-owning nobility was an exceptionally effective (and ruthless) mechanism for mobilising resources from a vast and pre-modern realm". Russia "premier iron producer" in 18th c. (ll. 4323–4337)

Turning point mid-19th c.: "onset of the Industrial Revolution in Western Europe, and its extension to Germany". "Russia’s economic backwardness was cruelly evident during the Crimean conflict." British/French used industrial tech (steamship/rail); Russian finances collapsed; serf army lacked reserves. "modernisation ... was essential if Russia was to survive as a great power." Post-1815 decline vs Germany (unification on border; Austro-German alliance ended prior rivalries benefiting Russia). (ll. 4337–4393)

Peripherality dilemmas: "A gap had emerged by 1900 between ... ‘First World’ ... and the ‘Second World’ countries of Europe’s southern and eastern periphery." Russia "poorer and politically less stable". "Building a political nation" harder on periphery. Empire "huge burden on the Russian people". "shedding the Soviet empire ... opens up more hopeful perspectives for Russia’s future." Ottoman comparison: Ottomans' power failure led to "disastrous consequences for millions of their Muslim subjects" (expulsions, colonisation); Russia protected its people but "at a great price in terms of economic exploitation and the denial of rights and freedoms". (ll. 4587–4449)

**Security Police and 1917 Collapse (from Cambridge Vol. II range 18001–18709, Daly + Lohr)**: Nicholas I created Third Section + gendarmerie post-1825 Decembrist revolt “to conduct censorship, oversee the bureaucracy, keep track of the public mood and preserve state security” (ll. 18041–18043). 1866 reforms after attempt on Alexander II: special security bureaus (Okhrannoe otdelenie). Zubatov developed sophisticated informant/surveillant system (filery trained in physiognomy; “treat these people as a beloved woman...” ll. 18151); penetrated revolutionaries, experimented with police worker unions. Azef exposed 1909; Bogrov assassinated Stolypin (1 Sept 1911). Dzhunkovskii reforms abolished most provincial security bureaus 1913–14. Lena massacre 1912 (172 workers) revived unrest. WWI martial law temporarily calmed.

Economic drivers of collapse: prohibition cost 2.5 billion roubles (10% of war exp) by mid-1917 as state lost ~quarter of revenue (ll. 18523); money supply quadrupled after gold standard abandoned 27 Jul/8 Aug 1914 (ll. 18533); foreign capital (nearly half new industry investment 1890s–1914) frozen by war; blockade severed suppliers. Grain reached cities poorly due to peasant hoarding (“waiting for higher grain prices... store their grain, feed it to their livestock or illicitly convert it into alcohol” ll. 18541) amid inflation and admin interventions. Petrograd garrison (unreliable new recruits, convalescents) mutinied 26–27 Feb after troops fired on crowds; “The proximate cause of greatest weight in explaining the end of the monarchy was the mutiny of the Petrograd garrison” (ll. 18481). Commanders (Alekseev) forced Nicholas abdication 2 Mar to preserve war effort. Progressive Bloc, war councils, Zemgor assumed parastatal roles; Nicholas personal command Aug 1915 and Rasputin discredited tsar. 4–10 Mar 1917 Provisional Government abolished security bureaus, Police Dept, Gendarme Corps. “the dismantling of the imperial police apparatus... was an invitation to takeover by political conspirators” (ll. 18387). `caused_by:` mutiny + commanders + economic dislocation (prohibition/inflation/grain) + elite alienation. `produced:` abdication, end of empire. `part_of:` [[world-wars-era]]. See [[february-revolution-1917]], [[nicholas-ii]], [[stolypin-pyotr]].

Pre-modern vs modern: "Before the middle of the nineteenth century almost all empires had been federations of aristocratic elites." Post-1850 "dilemma of modern empire": continental scale + multiethnicity vs nationalism. Strategies included civilising mission, supra-ethnic identities (e.g. Abdul Hamid II Islam), core ethno-national bloc (Russians/Magyars redefining Ukrainians/Belarusians as "Russians who merely spoke a local dialect"), or multiethnic federalism (Habsburg Austria). "In 1914 minority nationalism was no immediate threat" (peasants beyond reach; capitals/rail control). Danger only with European war + heartland revolt. (ll. 4499–4585)

"the tsarist regime never entirely abandoned this policy" of alliance with local aristocracies. But "inadequate in a modernising empire". Poland elite refused co-option, led 1831/1863 rebellions. Russian society pushed "closer identification ... with ethnic Russians’ interests". "consolidating a sense of Russian nationhood in as large as possible a core population". Blocked Ukrainian language/culture. "relatively optimistic view of the empire’s viability could be based even in 1914." (ll. 4567–4573)

"minority nationalism was no immediate threat to the regime" in 1914; "only become a major danger if Russia entered a European war or revolt erupted in the Russian heartland." "government which controlled [capitals and railways] could reassert its power". (ll. 4583–4585) `caused_by:` WWI + internal revolt; `produced:` 1917 collapse.
