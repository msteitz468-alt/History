# Nero (Nero Claudius Caesar Augustus Germanicus)

The **fifth and last Julio-Claudian emperor** (54–68 CE) — the philhellene artist-emperor
whose extravagance, dynastic murders, and fiscal exactions after the **Great Fire of Rome**
provoked the provincial revolts that ended his life and his dynasty. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Chs. 5–6 (Wiedemann).

## Accession and the *quinquennium*

The grandson of Germanicus and great-great-grandson of Augustus, adopted by **[[claudius]]** at
the urging of his mother **Agrippina**, Nero succeeded at sixteen (54), guided by his tutor
**Seneca** (whose *De clementia* set out the duties of an absolute but self-restrained ruler)
and the praetorian prefect **Burrus**. His reign's "good" early years gave rise to the later
legend of a **quinquennium Neronis** — five years of good government. Wiedemann argues this
construct served chiefly to explain why so many senators who later reviled Nero as a monster
had supported him for so long: dynastic murder ran *through* the "good" years (Britannicus,
55; Agrippina, 59).

## Matricide and the artist

Nero's personal tastes drove politics. His love for **Poppaea Sabina** and the question of
divorcing **Octavia** (Claudius' daughter, whose dowry was, Agrippina warned, the empire) lay
behind the bungled shipwreck and then **murder of his mother Agrippina (59)** — after which
the dissident senator **Thrasea Paetus** walked out of the Senate. Increasingly Nero indulged
his "uncontrollable desire" to perform in public as **lyre-player and charioteer** — winning
genuine popularity in the Greek cities but appalling the Roman elite, for whom it was
un-Roman degradation.

## Fire, conspiracy, and revolt

The **Great Fire of Rome (18–19 July 64)** was the hinge. The vastly costly reconstruction —
including Nero's **Golden House**, which expropriated aristocratic land — created a "direct and
unresolvable conflict of interest" with the senatorial elite, while the search for funds
(confiscations, debased coinage, temple treasures, provincial exactions) bred resentment from
Britain (it contributed to **Boudica's revolt**, 60) to Judaea (the exactions of Gessius
Florus that sparked the **Jewish War**, 66). The **Pisonian conspiracy (65)** — a plot to
replace him with C. Calpurnius Piso, "nothing republican about it" — was followed by a purge
that claimed **Seneca**, Lucan, and the Stoic martyrs Thrasea Paetus and Barea Soranus. In the
East, the long Armenian war was settled by **Corbulo** (whom Nero then forced to suicide, 67).

## Fall (68)

While Nero toured Greece, **Gaius Julius Vindex**, legate in Gaul, and then **Galba** in Spain
threw off allegiance. Wiedemann stresses their coinage invoked not a restored Republic but the
**"Liberty of the Roman People"** — the right of Senate and People to recognise who truly held
power. Abandoned by the praetorians (bribed by Nymphidius Sabinus) and declared a public enemy
by the Senate, Nero **took his own life (9 June 68)** — his reported last words, *"qualis
artifex pereo"* ("what an artist dies in me"), epitomizing a ruler more concerned with his
public image than with governing. With him the **Julio-Claudian dynasty ended**.

## Significance

Nero's fall opened the **[[year-of-the-four-emperors-69ce|civil war of 68–69]]** and revealed
the *arcanum imperii* — that emperors could be made by provincial armies. After his death the
"false Neros" and the persistence of his popularity with the plebs and the Greek East testify
to how differently his reign was judged at the bottom and the top of Roman society.

## Beard (SPQR, 2015)

Mary Beard surveys modern revisionist scholarship recasting Nero as more a victim of
Flavian-dynasty propaganda than a self-obsessed pyromaniac — noting that Tacitus himself
credits Nero with effective relief measures after the Great Fire, and that at least three
"false Neros" appeared after his death claiming to be him, evidence (she argues) of real
popular affection: "no one seeks power by pretending to be an emperor universally hated."
But she judges this revisionism "misses the bigger point": whatever Nero's true character,
it mattered little to most inhabitants of the empire or to the empire's underlying
structures — see [[roman-empire]] for her fuller argument that individual emperors "filled
the same slot."

## OHCW (Stockton; Jenkyns; Wilson)

**Stockton** (Ch. 22): early “good” years under Seneca and Burrus; murdered Agrippina 59; after Burrus’ death Tigellinus rose, Octavia murdered, Great Fire 64 and Golden House, Christian scapegoats; Piso conspiracy 65; alienated ruling class and army; Vindex–Galba 68 → suicide 9 June ended the Julio-Claudian line — “cardinal sin of unsettling his own ruling class and army commanders.”

**Jenkyns** (Ch. 28): Silver Latin under Neronian pressure — Lucan forced to suicide at 26; Petronius (usually identified with the *arbiter elegantiae*) suicide AD 66 — literary culture of the court cut short by the same insecurity Stockton stresses.

**Wilson** (Ch. 32): Golden House (AD 64–68) as architectural watershed — octagonal room with first surviving dome in the capital; concrete enables circular/ovoid/apsidal interiors; post-64 fire promotes brick-faced multi-storey apartment planning.

## Nero in the Graffiti Record

[[ancient-graffiti|Graffiti]] complicate the uniformly hostile literary tradition
on Nero. At Pompeii, eight painted *iudicia* graffiti acclaim the "judgements" of
Nero and his wife **Poppaea Sabina** (of Pompeian connection), probably thanking
him for lifting the senatorial ban on gladiatorial games after the 59 CE
amphitheatre riot — pro-Neronian sentiment absent from the histories. Other
graffiti record his gifts to Pompeian Venus. In Rome, by contrast, Suetonius
(*Nero* 45) preserves anti-Nero statue graffiti (the "curl," the "sack"/*poena
cullei*, the Gauls-singing pun) and Dio the anti-Agrippina statue tags after her
murder ("Nero, Orestes, Alcmaeon — matricides"). After Nero was declared *hostis*,
even his name in Pompeian graffiti suffered the mutilation of *damnatio
memoriae*. Per [[keegan-graffiti-in-antiquity-2014|Keegan, *Graffiti in
Antiquity*]].

## Freeman, *Egypt, Greece, and Rome* (2014)

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]]) reads Nero as consciously
modeling himself on the Hellenistic ideal of the cultural-patron king — he founded
the Neronia, a Greek-style games competition (60 CE), and competed in it himself.
The **Golden House (Domus Aurea)**, built after the 64 fire, was funded partly
through coinage debasement. On the execution of the successful general
**Corbulo** (67): Freeman comments "Nero must have sensed how vulnerable his lack
of military experience left him" — reading the killing as insecurity rather than
mere caprice.

## Woolf, Rome: An Empire's Story (2012)

From [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]] (Principate stability / fiscal fossils).

- **Suicide and institutional snap-back (68–69).** Nero’s suicide left no heirs; short civil war <2 years (Year of Four Emperors). After Vespasian, institutions snapped back; bronze tablet (Dec 69) grants powers citing Augustus/Tiberius/Claudius precedents—will of all sides for status-quo restoration. Nero pretenders show dynasty still a family affair.
- **Fiscal fossil under Nero.** Ephesus customs-law inscription of Nero’s day: Pergamene royal tariffs still levied “exactly… more than two centuries after the Roman takeover”—Woolf’s case for opportunistic harvest of institutional fossils and continued tax-farming of indirect taxes.
- **Quote:** “An inscription of Nero’s day found recently at Ephesus shows that the various customs duties created by the kings of Pergamum were still being levied in exactly the same way they had done more than two centuries after the Roman takeover.”
