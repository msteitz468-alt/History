# Thailand (Siam)

The one Southeast Asian polity to escape formal colonization. Wedged between British Burma/Malaya and
French Indochina, the Chakri kings **Mongkut** and **Chulalongkorn** preserved independence through
shrewd buffer-state diplomacy and a programme of defensive, centralizing modernization that built a
modern bureaucratic state from above — ceding peripheral territories to keep the core sovereign.

The 1932 revolution ended the absolute monarchy; thereafter Siam/Thailand alternated between military
and civilian rule, allied with Japan in WWII and with the United States during the Cold War. Its
uncolonized path makes it the key comparative case in modern Southeast Asian history.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

**Geography and early centres.** The Chao Phraya ("Grand Duke") watershed (~160,000 km², ~35% of
modern Thailand) mothered most of Thailand's ancient capitals and important cities (Nakhon Sawan,
Chainat, Singburi, [[ayutthaya|Ayutthaya]]). Rainfall is under 1,500 mm/year against a ~1,750 mm
minimum for reliable rain-fed rice; flood irrigation was hazardous — a century of data shows flooding
optimal or barely adequate ~55 years, insufficient one year in three, excess one in six; in low-flood
years >40% of the rice crop could fail. Early and Middle Classic main centres lay in what is now
northern Thailand, linked overland to Yunnan and the Shan Plateau rather than the South China Sea;
ninth-century shipwreck and geomorphological evidence suggest the Chao Phraya delta was swamp, not
open sea, in the Classic. The Khorat Plateau (~one third of Thailand, nearly Cambodia's area, ~15% of
the Mekong basin) has Classic remains related to the lower Mekong rather than the Chao Phraya; Phimai
in the southwest was a major population centre, and Mun-basin salt trade reached Yunnan and central
Thailand from prehistory.

**Late Classic mandalas.** Tai descent into the Chao Phraya and Mekong lowlands in the Late Classic
exploited combined highland/lowland resources and Yunnan trade. Thai history c. 1250–1440 is
characterized as "muang pluralism." [[sukhothai|Sukhothai]] fell from Khmer outpost to Tai chiefs in
the 1240s and became the first kingdom on modern Thai territory to use Tai as its language; under
Ramkhamhaeng (1279–1298) the mandala expanded (Sawankhalok, Uttaradit, Kamphaengphet, Tak), and the
1292 inscription calls him "sovereign of all the Tai" (later national-myth material; Wyatt notes
parts may be posthumous). By 1320 the mandala had contracted; Ayutthaya's rise (capital 1351 at the
Chao Phraya–Pasak–Lopburi confluence under Ramathibodi I of U Thong) forced Sukhothai subordination
by 1378, Ayutthayan residency by 1412, and provincial status by 1438. Borommaracha II conquered
[[angkor|Angkor]] by 1432. In the 1450s three major mandalas dominated: Ayutthaya, Lan Na, and Lan
Sang. Sukhothai was a major ceramic producer 1300–1450 (underglaze brown/white; Sawankhalok celadon);
Thai wares (Sukhothai, Si Satchanalai, Sawankhalok, Suphanburi, etc.) dominate many "Ming gap"
fifteenth-century shipwrecks (e.g. Turiang: 46% Sukhothai, 11% Si Satchanalai). Sixteenth-century
Ayutthaya was little affected by European contact; only there did the Portuguese still find a
self-identified Chinese community in 1509.

**Historiography of Thai sources.** Primary Thai-language sources begin in the thirteenth century.
Charnvit Kasetsiri's three genres: *tamnan* (Buddhist legend, pre-fifteenth to eighteenth century),
*phongsawadan* (court-centred Thai kingdom foundations), and *prawatsat* (nineteenth-century modern-
academic history). Until the 1960s Thai archaeology was largely "royal art history."
