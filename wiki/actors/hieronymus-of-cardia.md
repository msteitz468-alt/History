## Role

Hieronymus of Cardia (c. 350–c. 250 BCE) was the foremost contemporary historian of
the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|wars of the Successors]] and, by Walbank's judgement,
"by far the most important of the lost historians" of the Hellenistic age — and its
"most serious casualty." A Greek from Cardia in the Thracian Chersonese and a
compatriot of [[eumenes-of-cardia|Eumenes]], he was no mere scholar but a soldier and
official across the whole period he chronicled: archivist to Alexander, then officer
and diplomat under Eumenes (whom he served at the siege of Nora), and after Eumenes'
death under [[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus I]],
[[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius Poliorcetes]], and Antigonus II Gonatas. He is
said to have lived past 100.

## The Histories

His *Histories* (the exact title is uncertain) covered the Successors from Alexander's
death in 323 down probably to the death of Pyrrhus in 272. They are lost, but were the
chief source of **[[diodorus-siculus|Diodorus]] Books XVIII–XX** — our only sustained
continuous narrative of the period to the [[battle-of-ipsus-301|battle of Ipsus]] —
and a source for Plutarch's *Eumenes*, *Demetrius*, and *Pyrrhus*, for Nepos, Arrian's
*Events after Alexander*, and Trogus/Justin. As a participant of broad military
experience he grasped the central significance of his subject: the breakup of
Alexander's empire into the separate kingdoms.

## Reliability

Pausanias accuses him of bias toward Antigonus, whom he served; Walbank judges the
charge hard to sustain, though Antigonus does receive marked attention. As far as the
abbreviated later versions allow judgement, his work was "serious and intelligent." He
wrote against the sensational, anti-Macedonian *Macedonica* of Duris of Samos. Because
nearly everything known about the Diadochi descends through him via Diodorus, the
shape of the modern narrative — its attention to Eumenes, Antigonus, and Cassander —
is in large part Hieronymus' shape. See [[hellenistic-historiography]].
