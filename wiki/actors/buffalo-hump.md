# Buffalo Hump

**Buffalo Hump** (Penateka Comanche war chief; Nermernuh name **Po-cha-na-quar-hip**, vulgarly “erection that won’t go down”—prudishly untranslated by contemporary whites) was the charismatic leader of the great **1840** retaliatory raid deep into Texas after the Council House Fight at San Antonio—a campaign Gwynne treats as a high-water mark of Penateka striking power and, in outcome, a hinge toward relative southern-band decline. Spirit Talker’s nephew; Spanish-fluent survivor elevated after smallpox and Council House leadership losses.

## Rise after Council House (1840)

Buffalo Hump was **absent** from the Council House Fight (March 1840) that killed twelve Comanche leaders Texans billed as “leading chiefs” (Gwynne: Spirit Talker was a small-group leader; major figures including Buffalo Hump were not there). Afterward he had an apocalyptic dream-vision of driving treacherous Texans into the sea. Leadership vacuum (1816 smallpox estimates; ≥4 headmen lost in 1839 smallpox; Council House dead) enabled his rise from lower war-chief recruiter to visionary expedition leader.

## The 1840 vision raid: Victoria, Linnville, Plum Creek

**July–August mobilization:** Messengers to Yamparika, Kotsoteka, Nokoni—few northern recruits (bad medicine; Cheyenne/Arapaho pressure on Arkansas–Canadian range; Penateka seen as degraded). Most other Penateka chiefs (Isimanica, Little Wolf, Santa Anna) plus some Kiowas joined. Midsummer: **400+ warriors and ~600 camp followers (~1,000 total)** rode from the Balcones/Blanco toward the coastal bend (1 August); night-moving by Comanche moon from 4 August.

**6 August ~4 p.m. — Victoria** (~100 mi SE of San Antonio): killed about a dozen; circled the town “as though it were a herd of buffalo” rather than house-to-house extermination; stole ~1,500–2,000 horses (later up to ~3,000 with the coastal sweep); carried off a small Black girl. Next day, discouraged by rifle fire, rode for Matagorda Bay following the vision “to the sea.”

**Linnville:** Coastal warehouse town sacked in a “saturnalia” of loot (umbrellas, hats—Linn description); Buffalo Hump lost control as war plan collapsed into a haul home. Captive killings and related atrocities: full fidelity on event pages; not softened here.

**Plum Creek:** Texan pursuit hit the overloaded column. Casualty traditions diverge—Texan: 1 killed / 7 wounded; Indian dead estimates 25–138 but recovered bodies ~12–25; most trade goods lost but many of ~3,000 horses retained (Linn: only “several hundred” recovered). **Historiography:** Ranger-victory tradition vs. Schilz (quoted in Gwynne): tactical draw—feints screened families; Buffalo Hump brought most people home after a coastal raid. Tonkawa cannibalism of dead Comanche after the fight is recorded in sources at full fidelity.

**October 1840 coda:** Col. John Moore’s dawn horseback attack on a ~60-lodge Colorado River camp (some accounts Buffalo Hump’s)—butchery against the river; Moore claimed ~130 dead in ~30 minutes, 34 prisoners, 500 horses, village burned.

## Later career (summary)

Post-1840 Mexican Trace raiding with Santa Anna and others killed far more people south of the Rio Grande than in Texas even in “dying days,” returning with cattle, horses, and captive children. After Pah-hah-yuco’s withdrawal and 1849 cholera deaths of headmen, Buffalo Hump was chosen successor without real pan-band power. **1855:** most of remaining Penatekas fled north with him rather than enter the Brazos reservation (~400 of ~1,200 came in). **15 September 1858:** Earl Van Dorn’s Second Cavalry (with Sul Ross friendlies) massacred his village next to the Wichitas after Captain Prince had just treated with the same band—~70 killed, 120 tipis burned, ~300 horses taken; Buffalo Hump escaped with most warriors; fall 1858 frontier raids partly reprisals. Gwynne later analogizes the 1874 Isa-tai/Quanah Sun Dance revenge raid to Buffalo Hump’s 1840 expedition as the tribe’s “second large-scale revenge raid against the white man.”

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the 1840 raid’s depth and coastal reach, Texas memory of Comanche power and the Ranger origin myth of Plum Creek would look thinner; without post-Council House elevation of survivors like Buffalo Hump, Penateka leadership after 1840 is harder to narrate. Disease and demographic collapse still constrained southern bands regardless of one expedition’s tactics.

## Historiography

Gwynne synthesizes Texan campaign narratives, Linn, and secondary (Schilz) for Plum Creek draw thesis. Population and body-count figures are estimates—flag. Name translation often bowdlerized in older Anglo sources. Do not conflate 1840 Plum Creek with later plains wars under Mackenzie.
