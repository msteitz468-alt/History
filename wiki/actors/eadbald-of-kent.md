## Overview

Eadbald, son and successor of [[æthelberht-of-kent]], reigned over Kent from 616 (or 618) until approximately 640. His accession triggered a pagan reaction across south-east England that endangered the Gregorian mission at [[canterbury]]. He eventually converted to Christianity — Kirby argues this happened not under Archbishop Laurentius (as Bede states) but under Archbishop Justus, probably not long before c. 624. He then consolidated Kent's position through a Frankish marriage and maintained a degree of regional overlordship, though reduced compared to his father.

## Pagan Reaction

On his accession Eadbald was a pagan — either apostate or never converted. He married his stepmother (Æthelberht's widow) in violation of Church law, a union Bede characterises as a manifestation of his paganism (HE II, 5). Simultaneously, the sons of Sæberht, king of the East Saxons, expelled Mellitus, bishop of London. Mellitus fled to Gaul with Justus, bishop of Rochester. Laurentius, Augustine's successor at Canterbury, contemplated abandoning the mission entirely.

Kirby reads the pagan reaction as having a political dimension absent from Bede's account: it represented a broad shift across south-east England, "attendant perhaps upon a rejection by Eadbald of his father's Frankish connections." The East Saxon kings were probably acting in concert with Eadbald rather than independently.

<!-- Grounding: "Eadbald as king of Kent and the heir to Aethelberht's imperium was a powerful figure with whom the rulers of the eastern Saxons were probably acting in concert when they expelled Mellitus." -->

## Conversion

Bede attributes Eadbald's conversion to a miraculous scourging of Archbishop Laurentius by St Peter (HE II, 6) and implies the conversion happened within a year of his accession. Kirby demonstrates, using surviving papal letters of Boniface V (619–25), that Bede was wrong. Boniface's letters to Archbishop Justus, to King Edwin, and to Eadbald's sister Æthelburh all reference the recent conversion of Eadbald and were written c. 624 — after Justus had succeeded Mellitus as archbishop. Since Mellitus held the archbishopric for five years after Laurentius, and Laurentius died not before 617–19, Eadbald cannot have been converted until c. 623–4. The pagan reaction therefore lasted five to eight years, not one year as Bede implies.

<!-- Grounding: "the pagan reaction under Eadbald was more protracted than Bede's single year. It lasted some five or six years (if Eadbald succeeded in 618) or seven or eight (if Eadbald succeeded in 616)." -->

Kirby also argues that Bede's claim that Eadbald recalled Mellitus from the continent after a year (HE II, 6) was a false inference from the mistaken conversion date, and that Frankish influence — not a miraculous event — was probably decisive: "the most decisive force at work in the Kentish court was again probably Frankish."

## Frankish Remarriage and Later Reign

The conversion of Eadbald was accompanied by his repudiation of his first wife and marriage to a Frankish noblewoman, likely a deliberate recreation of the Frankish connection established by his father's marriage to Bertha. This "recreat[ed] a relationship established by Eormenric and Aethelberht forty years earlier."

Eadbald remained a significant regional power. He minted gold coins at London in his own name (AVDVARLD), implying continued economic weight in south-east England. The pope's letter to Edwin c. 624 notes that Eadbald's and Edwin's lands were "close to one another," and Edwin's request for Eadbald's sister Æthelburh in marriage is evidence of Eadbald's continuing dynastic prestige. However, Eadbald could not restore Mellitus to London because, as Bede notes, "he had less royal power than Aethelberht" (HE II, 6), and the East Saxon kings had permanently broken from Kentish overlordship.

His son and successor Eorcenberht (640–64) was the first ruler among the Germanic peoples in Britain to order the destruction of pagan idols and the observance of Lent (HE III, 8), suggesting the Christianisation Eadbald began was completed in the next generation.

## Counterfactual Significance

If Eadbald had maintained his paganism indefinitely, the Gregorian mission would likely have collapsed. Canterbury survived the reaction only because it retained its base in the heart of Kent despite the departure of Mellitus and the near-flight of Laurentius. Frankish pressure and the eventual marriage alliance may have been the structural forces that brought Eadbald's conversion, which in turn reopened the possibility of the northern missions (including Æthelburh's journey to Northumbria and the eventual conversion of Edwin).

## Historiography

Bede's account of Eadbald is demonstrably defective on its main claim — that the conversion followed within a year and was effected by Laurentius. Kirby's source-critical analysis of the Boniface V letters (preserved in HE II, 7–11) demonstrates the correct sequence: Eadbald was converted by Justus, not Laurentius, c. 623–4. The scourging legend is identified as early Canterbury hagiography from the monastery of St Augustine. The political dimension of the pagan reaction — rejected by Bede's ecclesiastical framing — is Kirby's own interpretive addition.
