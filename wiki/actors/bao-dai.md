# Bao Dai

Last emperor of Vietnam (Nguyen dynasty), born 1913, reigned 1926–1945 (abdicated), then head of the State of Vietnam (1949–1955) under French auspices. Central symbolic figure in the French attempt to create a non-communist Vietnamese alternative to Ho Chi Minh's DRV.

## Role

Abdicated in 1945 in favor of the Viet Minh government but later returned from exile in France as head of the "Associated State." The French and later the Americans hoped his prestige could rally non-communist nationalists. In practice his government was widely seen as a French puppet; real power often lay with French officials or later with Ngo Dinh Diem. At Geneva the State of Vietnam delegation refused to sign the accords.

## Significance

Embodied the failure of the "Bao Dai solution" — the attempt to use a traditional monarch as the vehicle for a modern anti-communist state. His deposition by Diem in the 1955 referendum marked the end of French influence and the consolidation of the Diem regime.

## 1947–1949: The French Courtship and Élysée Accords (per Logevall)

French policymakers courted the 34-year-old ex-emperor (living in Hong Kong then Europe) as a malleable alternative. Bao Dai initially demanded full independence and dissolution of the Cochin China government. He reacted cautiously to High Commissioner Bollaert and announced he stood “neither for nor against” Ho but “above partisan squabbles.”

He eventually accepted the heavily hedged Baie d’Along protocol (December 1947) and later the June 1948 accord. Both times he suffered buyer’s remorse and fled back to Europe for bridge, tennis, and cabaret life in Geneva, Cannes, and Paris.

On 8 March 1949 the Élysée Accords were concluded by exchange of letters with President Vincent Auriol: reconfirmed “autonomy” and “Associated State” status within the French Union, but France retained control of the army’s equipping and direction, foreign relations, defense, and ultimate sovereignty. The announcement aroused scant enthusiasm inside Vietnam. The DRV issued a warrant for Bao Dai’s arrest on charges of high treason. Many Vietnamese viewed the arrangement as a “sham.”
