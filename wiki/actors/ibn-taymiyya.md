## Overview

Ahmad ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328), full name Taqi al-Din Ahmad ibn Taymiyya al-Harrani, was a Hanbali jurist and reformist theologian based in Damascus, the most controversial and influential Islamic legal thinker of the Later Middle Period. Born into "an old family of Hanbali scholars," he mounted a many-pronged campaign against the prevailing extra-Sharia tendencies of Mamluk-era Islam — attacking Sufi mystical practice, the doctrine of *taqlid* (uncritical adherence to legal precedent), the tolerance of customary law by the official *ulama*, and practices of saint veneration. He was imprisoned repeatedly and died in prison in Damascus when the Mamluk authorities denied him pen, ink, and paper.

## Historical Context

Ibn Taymiyya lived in Damascus under the [[mamluk-sultanate]], at a period when the Jamali-Sunni *ulama* had made comprehensive accommodations with the military-patronage state. The Mamluk government relied on scholars for legitimation but also required their practical cooperation with policies that deviated from Sharia norms. Most legal scholars justified this accommodation through the doctrine of taqlid — following established precedent rather than exercising independent legal reasoning (*ijtihad*). Meanwhile, Sufism had become the dominant form of popular piety throughout the Islamic world, with shrine veneration, intercession of saints, and Sufi brotherhoods (tariqahs) integral to religious life.

Damascus itself was strongly Sunni in orientation, with a popular tradition of loyalty to the Jamali-Sunni position and numerous venerated tombs of ancient prophets (Syria being "known to be the land of prophecy"). This environment gave Ibn Taymiyya his popular base while setting him at odds with the established authorities.

## Legal and Theological Programme

Ibn Taymiyya's central innovation was his use of the concept of *maslaha* (public interest or welfare) to give the Sharia a broader social reach than it had possessed under either the caliphate or the military amirs. Where other Hanbalis had used maslaha cautiously, Ibn Taymiyya deployed it as "the basis of an incisive attack on the established order."

His campaign operated on several fronts simultaneously:

**Against taqlid**: He insisted that the right of general *ijtihad* (independent legal reasoning) still held for any competent jurist, such as himself, who was qualified to go back to the Quran and Hadith sources. He condemned the other ulama for a doctrine of taqlid that, in his view, justified their accommodations with non-Sharia piety and with the government. Crucially, he made this radical demand from within technically conservative Hanbali ground, a madhhab already recognized as one of the legitimate four Sunni schools.

**Against Sufism**: Ibn Taymiyya rejected the mystical and intercessory dimensions of Sufism that had become dominant in popular religion — saint veneration, tomb visitation, the idea of a spiritual hierarchy of saints (*awliya*) over against secular authority. This brought him into conflict not only with Sufi orders but with the popular piety of much of the Muslim world.

**Against extra-Sharia government**: He attacked the Mamluk government directly for its anti-Sharia practices, a dangerous posture given his popularity. His combination of scholarly credentials and popular following made him politically threatening:

> "Ibn-Taymiyyah was greatly revered by the people of Damascus, most of whom were not Hanbalîs... But Ibn-Taymiyyah's programme was too radical for the established authorities. He attacked the anti-Shari'i and extra-Shari'i practices taken for granted by the Mamluk government, and his popularity made him dangerous." (Hodgson, *Venture of Islam* Vol. 2)

**Against kalâm**: Like Ibn Rushd before him, he was deeply skeptical of the more abstract disciplines of scholastic theology (*kalam*), though for different reasons. Where the Falasifa regarded kalam as epistemologically inferior to philosophy, Ibn Taymiyya regarded it as having outlived whatever limited apologetic utility it had possessed.

## Imprisonment and Death

Ibn Taymiyya was imprisoned more than once by the Mamluk authorities. His final imprisonment was the most significant: when authorities not only imprisoned him but refused him writing materials, he is said to have died of heartbreak. His funeral in Damascus drew an extraordinary popular demonstration:

> "It is said (doubtless with the usual chroniclers' exaggeration) that 200,000 men and 15,000 women took part." (Hodgson, *Venture of Islam* Vol. 2)

This popular response illustrates the gap between his formal institutional isolation — he was imprisoned by the authorities he attacked — and his mass following among ordinary Damascenes.

## Immediate Followers and Later Influence

In his own time, "Ibn-Taymiyyah had few followers to continue his work." More immediately influential were the philosopher-theologians of the Taftazani school, who dominated subsequent centuries. However, Ibn Taymiyya's program became the foundation for later Hanbali reformist movements, including the Wahhabi movement of eighteenth-century Arabia, which drew explicitly on his critique of saint veneration and his insistence on return to Quran and Hadith sources. In this sense his historical consequential impact far exceeded his immediate influence.

Hodgson characterizes him as "a reaffirmation of the oppositional character of the Sharia-minded tradition, even in its Jamali-Sunni form" — distinguishing him from the Shii and Batini forms of protest that were more common in the Later Middle Period.

## Gender and Education Note

Among his students was at least one woman scholar of religious disciplines — Hodgson notes this as evidence that even in the Later Middle Period, "when the earlier Muslim tolerance of public roles for women had long since receded, women could be prominent teachers of the religious disciplines."

## Historiography

Henri Laoust's *Essai sur les doctrines sociales et politiques de Taqi-d-din Ahmad b. Taimiya* (Cairo, 1939) is cited by Hodgson as "the most important of Laoust's works on Ibn-Taymiyyah" and contains rich documentation. Hodgson places Laoust alongside Louis Massignon's *La Passion d'al-Hallaj* as together providing "a fairly comprehensive sense of the issues debated in Islam."

The scholarly debate over Ibn Taymiyya concerns whether to read him primarily as a medieval Hanbali traditionalist, as a proto-reformist whose ideas are continuous with eighteenth-century Wahhabism and twentieth-century Islamism, or as a more nuanced figure whose significance has been distorted by his later adoption as a founding authority by Salafi movements. Hodgson's treatment emphasizes his embeddedness in the specific intellectual and political context of Mamluk Damascus rather than reading him as a precursor of modernity.
