# Artaxerxes I Longimanus

## Overview

**Artaxerxes I** ("Longimanus," r. 465–424 BCE) was the third son of [[xerxes-i|Xerxes]],
raised to the throne by the palace conspiracy that murdered his father. His long reign —
the longest of the later Achaemenids — is reconstructed by [[sources/briant-from-cyrus-to-alexander-2002|
Briant]] as a period of consolidation, not the "decline" of the Hellenocentric tradition:
the empire suppressed a dangerous Athenian-backed Egyptian revolt, settled its western
frontier, and maintained its administrative machinery intact, as the Murašû
tablets from Nippur show the [[achaemenid-satrapy|satrapal]] land-grant economy
functioning smoothly to the end of his reign.

## Role and Key Events

- **Bloody accession (465).** Xerxes was killed by Artabanus, captain of the guard;
  Artaxerxes then killed Artabanus with the help of [[achaemenid-kings-men|the general]]
  Megabyzus. A revolt by his brother Hystaspes, satrap of Bactria, was crushed only after
  two pitched battles.
- **The revolt of Inaros (c. 464–454).** The most serious crisis of the reign: the Libyan
  Inaros, allied with Athens, defeated and killed the king's uncle Achaemenes and seized
  Memphis. Megabyzus, satrap of Syria, reconquered Egypt by forcing the Athenian fleet to
  capitulate on the island of Prosopitis (454) — a catastrophic Athenian defeat. The later
  execution of Inaros, in breach of Megabyzus's safe-conduct, drove Megabyzus himself into
  a (negotiated) revolt.
- **The western frontier.** The [[peace-of-callias-449bce|Peace of Callias]] (allegedly
  449) is treated by Briant as a contested problem — thin, contradictory evidence, and
  more plausibly a Persian-dictated settlement than the Greek diplomatic triumph of Athenian
  tradition.
- **Judah and Elephantine.** Artaxerxes' firmans authorizing Ezra and Nehemiah to reorganize
  Judah under its ancestral law fit standard Achaemenid provincial practice — recognizing
  local custom as "royal law" to bind a community to the crown. The [[achaemenid-egypt|
  Elephantine]] Jewish garrison flourished, corresponding directly with the governor of Judah.

## Counterfactual Significance

Briant's reading makes the reign structurally important precisely because nothing structural
broke: the reconquest of Egypt and the survival of the hatru system show
that the mid-fifth-century empire retained full fiscal and military capacity. The "decline
begins after Xerxes" narrative is, on this evidence, a Greek literary construct rather than a
description of Achaemenid reality.

## Historiography

The narrative rests heavily on Ctesias, Diodorus, and Thucydides for the western and Egyptian
fronts — all hostile or Athenocentric — checked against the Babylonian Murašû archive and the
Aramaic Elephantine papyri, which Briant treats as the firmer evidence. The end of the reign
saw a sharp succession crisis: Xerxes II reigned only weeks before being murdered by Sogdianus,
who was overthrown in turn by the illegitimate Ochus ([[artaxerxes-ii|Darius II]]), backed by
the queen Parysatis.
