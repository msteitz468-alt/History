# Van Diemen's Land (colony)

**Van Diemen's Land** (renamed **Tasmania** in 1856) was the second major British
penal colony in [[australia|Australia]], founded from 1803 as an offshoot of
[[new-south-wales-colony|New South Wales]] and ultimately receiving between **67,000 and
69,000 convicts** between 1803 and 1853 — slightly fewer than New South Wales but a
comparable order of magnitude (CHA vol. 1, ch. 3, fn. 2).

## Founding and Early Settlement (1803–1810)

Settlement was driven partly by fears of French incursion into Bass Strait and the
desire to secure rich sealing and whaling resources discovered after 1798. Governor
King despatched **David Collins** with 308 convicts and a handful of free settlers in
1803, originally to Sorrento (Port Phillip Bay). The site failed through want of food
and water, scurvy, deaths, and Aboriginal hostility; Collins moved the group to
**Sullivan's Cove** on the Derwent River — now **Hobart**. Simultaneously, northern
settlement was established at Port Dalrymple; after multiple failed locations, Lieutenant
Governor **William Paterson** moved upstream to **Launceston** around 1807.

The period 1805–c.1820 was shaped by what historians (drawing on James Boyce,
*Van Diemen's Land*, 2008) call the "dog and kangaroo economy": Collins allowed the
Commissariat to buy kangaroo meat in 1805, enabling convicts and ex-convicts with
hunting dogs to live largely independently in the hinterland. This "mitigated violent
exchange" with Aboriginal people for roughly two decades, producing a longer period of
relatively peaceful relations than on the mainland, though violence was "sporadic and
localised" throughout.

## Norfolk Island Settlers Transferred (1804–1814)

The British government decided in 1803 to close the Norfolk Island settlement and
transfer settlers to Van Diemen's Land, which it considered strategically preferable.
Male settlers petitioned to remain on their cleared farms but were overruled. The last
settlers departed in 1814; they burned the buildings and left the island to animals
and forest. The resettled Norfolk Islanders founded **Norfolk Plains** and **New Norfolk**
but "found it hard to start again, and the lieutenant-governors there were unable to
fulfil the promises made to them" (CHA vol. 1, ch. 4). The transferred settlers
"spoke of the change with regret and sadness" for years.

## Governing Structure and Economy to 1822

Van Diemen's Land operated under a lieutenant governor subordinate to the governor of
New South Wales. The early economy was dominated by small grain farming (wheat was the
staple crop, maize not growing well in the climate) and a distinctive hunting-pastoral
mix. Hoe-farming, learned in New South Wales and Norfolk Island, was transplanted as a
tradition. Sealing at Bass Strait islands was a major early industry; Hobart emerged
as a whaling port.

After Collins' sudden death in 1810, bushranging increased dramatically; **Michael Howe**
and others challenged the colony's governance. Most were eventually caught and hanged
or transported to secondary punishment sites. The penal station at **Macquarie Harbour**,
opened in 1821 on the wild west coast — "a bleak anchorage…at the back of a
wind-blasted, rain-soaked shore" — represented an intentional escalation toward
inescapable confinement as the assignment-and-settlement system struggled with disorder.

## Relationship to Convict Transportation System

Van Diemen's Land was central to the [[convict-transportation-australia|convict
transportation]] system, eventually receiving a higher proportion of convicts per capita
than New South Wales. The [[convict-transportation-australia|assignment of convicts]] to private masters
was the dominant mode of convict labour management here as on the mainland. The
**Bigge Report** (1823) recommended expanding large-estate grazing worked by assigned
convicts — a model that would intensify in Van Diemen's Land through the 1830s and
eventually produce the "model prison" at Port Arthur (1830).

## Counterfactual Significance

Van Diemen's Land demonstrates that colonisation in early Australia was not linear.
Its founding involved multiple failed sites; its early economy took an unexpected
hunting-pastoral shape rather than the planned agrarian form; and its more isolated
geography produced both longer peaceful coexistence with Aboriginal people and, later,
the most complete documented genocide of an indigenous population in the colonial period
(the Black War and its aftermath, beyond this chapter's range).

## Historiography

Grace Karskens (*The Colony*, 2009) and James Boyce (*Van Diemen's Land*, 2008) are the
principal authorities drawn on by CHA vol. 1, ch. 4, for the early settlement period.
Both emphasise convict and ex-convict agency rather than simple top-down penal management.
