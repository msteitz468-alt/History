# Ḥasan al-Baṣrī

Ḥasan al-Baṣrī (642–728) is the "patriarch of Muslim mysticism" (Schimmel) — the earliest figure
whose life and preaching define the temper of the [[early-sufism|formative period]] of
[[sufism|Sufism]]. He witnessed the great Arab conquests at their height (711 CE saw the crossing of
Gibraltar, the reach to Sind, and the advance into Transoxania), and he understood the spiritual
danger in a community becoming drunk on conquest and wealth while forgetting the Quranic summons to
accountability.

## Life and Context

Born in Medina, Ḥasan settled in Basra, which was then becoming one of the most important centers
of Muslim learning and piety. He came of age during the early Umayyad period (after 661), when the
dynasty's worldliness generated intense reaction among the pious. He was "sober and clearsighted"
in his assessment of the dangers; Schimmel describes him as the embodiment of a "realistic critical
tendency" (following Louis Massignon's analysis), as opposed to the more "idealistic tradition" of
Kufa.

## The Ascetic Vision

Ḥasan's preaching was saturated with the fear of the Day of Judgment. He is said to have lived as
if hellfire had been created exclusively for him. His oratory in "beautiful sonorous Arabic"
expressed radical disengagement from the world:

> "Be with this world as if you had never been there, and with the Otherworld as if you would never
> leave it."

And, on individual accountability before God:

> "O son of Adam, you will die alone and enter the tomb alone and be resurrected alone, and it is
> with you alone that the reckoning will be made!"

These sayings "still echo in Persian, Turkish, and Pashto mystical verses" many centuries later.
He and his circle were known as "those who constantly weep" (*al-bakkāʾūn*), for both the
miserable state of the world and meditation on their own shortcomings moved them to tears.

Louis Massignon characterized Ḥasan's Basran circle as representing a "realistic critical tendency"
in contrast to the speculative tendencies elsewhere — their asceticism was almost exclusively
devotional, lacking interest in speculative thought. The emphasis was on strict *waraʿ*
(abstinence), *khawf* (fear of God), and *ḥuzn* (sadness) — the three dominant notes of the
earliest ascetic movement.

## Historical Significance

Ḥasan al-Baṣrī stands at the fountainhead of the Sufi tradition in two senses:

**As the representative type**: He embodies the mode of piety that the Sufi tradition would
transform — fear-based, world-renouncing, oriented toward the Last Judgment. When
[[rabia-al-adawiyya|Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya]] introduced selfless love as the motive of religious
life, she was, in part, moving beyond the Ḥasan-type of piety while remaining in the Basran
tradition.

**As the source of institutional continuity**: His disciple ʿAbdul Wāḥid ibn Zayd (d. 794) founded
an ascetic settlement at Abbadan on the Persian Gulf, spreading Ḥasan's ideals to Syria — where
Abū Sulaymān ad-Dārānī (d. 830) and Aḥmad ibn Abīʾl-Ḥawārī (d. 851) carried on the Basrian
ascetic movement. This chain of transmission connects the earliest circle to the developed Sufi
tradition of the ninth century.

## Theological Positions

Ḥasan al-Baṣrī was involved in the early theological disputes about free will and predestination
— debates generated by the moral dilemmas of Umayyad rule. His scrupulosity extended to his
position on the Umayyads: he opposed their worldliness while generally avoiding open political
confrontation.

## Historiography

The boundaries between the historical Ḥasan and the legendary Ḥasan — onto whom later generations
projected ideals of early piety — are difficult to establish. The Sufi tradition attributed to him
a founding role that may be partly retrospective. Massignon's analysis of his role remains
foundational. See [[sufism-historiography]] and [[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]].
