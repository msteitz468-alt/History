# Ur-Nammu

The founder of the **[[third-dynasty-of-ur|Third Dynasty of Ur]]** (reigned ~18
years), who turned the city of Ur from a deputyship under Utu-khegal into the seat
of the last great Sumerian empire. He is remembered above all for two enduring
achievements: the **earliest substantially preserved law code** and the **Great
Ziggurat of Ur**. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch.
XXII, C. J. Gadd).

## From deputy to king

Ur-Nammu first appears as **Utu-khegal's deputy at Ur**, restoring its temple. A
boundary dispute — in which Utu-khegal ruled *against* the "man of Ur" in favour of
Lagash — may have spurred his break from Uruk. He then supplanted his overlord (the
king-list says "by conquest," though no war is recorded), defeated **Nammakhni of
Lagash**, and took the title **"king of Sumer and Akkad."** Much of his reign was
spent repairing the [[gutian-invasion|Gutian]] devastation — re-surveying
city-state boundaries, and above all **restoring the canals** and water-supply on
which Sumerian agriculture depended (the reopening of the Magan sea-trade to Ur is
credited to him).

## The Laws of Ur-Nammu

Ur-Nammu (or his son Shulgi) issued the **earliest law collection known** — three
centuries before Hammurabi. Its fragmentary prologue celebrates his establishment
of "**justice**" (and his victory over Lagash), and its surviving clauses, framed
casuistically ("if a man..."), deal with sexual offences, the protection of women,
runaway slaves, and wrongs against others' land — notably prescribing **monetary
compensation** for several injuries where the later Code of Hammurabi would impose
talionic mutilation. It inaugurates the long Mesopotamian tradition of royal
law-giving.

## The Great Ziggurat

His greatest monument is the **ziggurat (temple-tower) of the Moon-god Nanna at
Ur** — the best-preserved ziggurat in Mesopotamia, "impressive alike by its plan,
its mass, and the excellence of its construction." Begun by Ur-Nammu and continued
by Shulgi, it was still being repaired by Nabonidus seventeen centuries later. The
**Stele of Ur-Nammu** depicted, scene by scene, the religious and practical stages
of its building. He raised comparable temple-towers at Uruk, Nippur, and Eridu.

## Liverani: the organisational innovation

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] locates Ur-Nammu's true innovation not
in the *extent* of his realm but in its **organisation**. His title — "strong man, king of
Ur, king of Sumer and Akkad" — abandoned Sargon's method of accumulating a separate title
for each conquered city and instead treated **Sumer and Akkad as ethno-linguistic wholes**.
The decisive structural change was converting the *ensi* from a local ruler governing on
behalf of the city-god into a **royal governor appointed by Ur and acting for the king** —
cities kept the title but lost their traditional autonomy. The law code was less a set of
reform edicts than an **organic reorganisation of justice**, standardising the *sila*
(capacity), the *mina* and *shekel* (weight), and fixed compensations for crimes, and
presenting the king as bringer of justice, freedom, and fairness. The contemporaneous
writing of the [[sumerian-king-list|Sumerian King List]] served the new doctrine that only
**one** king could legitimately rule at any time — eliminating the old logic of simultaneous
city-state rulers. See [[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]].

## Significance

Ur-Nammu is the **institutional founder** of the Neo-Sumerian state: the man who
restored order after the Gutian collapse, re-established the irrigation
infrastructure, monumentalized the cult, and began the legal and administrative
apparatus that [[shulgi|Shulgi]] would perfect. His law code and ziggurat are the
two most tangible legacies of the [[third-dynasty-of-ur|Ur III]] revival.

## Historiography

Documented by his own inscriptions (bricks, cones, the building stele) and by
hymns — including a striking lament on his **death and funeral**, consoling Ur with
the gifts that secured him a place among the shades — but, as throughout Ur III,
the **narrative of events is thin** and the praise-poetry "destitute of real
information." The attribution of the law code (Ur-Nammu vs. Shulgi) is debated.
Dates follow the [[mesopotamian-chronology|middle chronology]]. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
