# The British Raj (British Rule in India)

The **British Raj** was the regime by which Britain ruled the Indian subcontinent — first as the
**[[english-east-india-company|East India Company's]]** territorial dominion (from
[[battle-of-plassey-1757|Plassey, 1757]]) and, after the [[indian-rebellion-1857|Rebellion of 1857]],
directly under the **Crown (1858–1947)**. Documented for the wiki through Percival Spear's
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965), which (controversially)
treats the Raj as the continuation and heir of the [[mughal-empire|Mughal]] imperial state.

## Building the state: Cornwallis, Wellesley, Dalhousie

Spear traces the Company-state's consolidation through three architects:
- **Cornwallis** (Gov.-Gen. 1786): created a salaried, **Europeanized** covenanted service (excluding
  Indians from higher office) and the **Permanent Settlement of Bengal (1793)** — fixing the land
  revenue in perpetuity with the **zamindars** as proprietors. Spear's verdict is critical: it turned
  the zamindar into "a Calcutta rentier" and the peasantry into "a rustic proletariat," and its
  "idyllic" improving-landlord hopes were never realised.
- **Wellesley** (from 1798): aggressive expansion via the **subsidiary alliance** — guaranteeing a
  ruler's "independence" in return for a British-controlled garrison paid by ceded territory — which
  subordinated Hyderabad, Awadh, the Marathas (after their defeat), and much of India.
- **Dalhousie** (1848–56): the convinced westernizer of railways, telegraph, roads, and the first
  universities, but also of the **doctrine of lapse** and outright **annexation** (eight states,
  including **Oudh, 1856**) — alarming the whole upper class on the eve of 1857.

## The "new policy": anglicism and reform

Spear charts the ideological shift from the early Company's conservative respect for Indian custom to
the reforming, **anglicist** confidence of the 1820s–50s — driven by Evangelicalism, Utilitarianism,
and free trade. **Bentinck** (1828–35) abolished **suttee (1829)** and suppressed **thuggee**, and —
siding with **Macaulay** against the orientalists — launched **English education** (Macaulay's 1835
Minute) and began the codification of Indian law. The aim, in Macaulay's notorious phrase, was a class
"Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes" — a policy whose "seed was to bear unexpected
fruit" in the very nationalism it later nourished.

## The imperial heyday after 1857

The [[indian-rebellion-1857|1857 Rebellion]] ended Company rule (Crown takeover, **1858**; Victoria
proclaimed **Empress of India, 1877**) and reset the Raj's character for sixty years. Convinced the
revolt sprang from **specific mistakes, not inherent weakness**, the British grew more cautious:
respecting the **"established classes"** (princes kept as "decorative survivals," landlords courted),
slowing aggressive westernization ("public works rather than public morals"), and recasting the
**army** into the form that lasted to independence. Beneath the confident surface, Spear notes, the
deepest legacy of 1857 was a shift of Indian hopes to the **English-educated class** — the seedbed of
the [[indian-national-congress|national movement]].

## Significance and historiography

Spear's Raj is an essentially **continuist and relatively benign** picture — the British inheriting
the Mughal imperial mantle, schooled by the Company's competitive origins, governing through Indian
agency. He is candid about its costs (the "financial bleeding" of early Bengal, the Permanent
Settlement's social damage) but writes from within the mid-20th-century British liberal-imperial
tradition, **before** the economic-"drain," subaltern, and nationalist revisionisms that would stress
deindustrialization, famine, and exploitation far more heavily. The wiki treats his narrative as a
spine to be balanced by those later readings as further sources are ingested.
