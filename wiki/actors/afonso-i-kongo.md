# Afonso I of Kongo (Mvemba a Nzinga)

**Afonso I** (baptismal name; born Mvemba a Nzinga, r. **1509–c. 1542/43**) was the most celebrated *manikongo* (king) of the [[kingdom-of-kongo]], the ruler who made Christianity the state religion, corresponded as an equal with European monarchs, and presided over Kongo's transformation into the Atlantic world's most sophisticated African Christian polity — while simultaneously failing to halt the slave trade that hollowed out his kingdom.

## Rise to Power

Son of João I (Nzinga a Nkuwu), he served as governor of Nsundi province, traditionally the seat of the heir apparent. He converted to Christianity before his accession. On his father's death in 1509, he faced a succession struggle against a pagan rival, Mpanzu a Kitima; his victory in the resulting battle — which Afonso attributed to miraculous Christian intervention (an apparition of Santiago/Saint James) — became foundational myth for Kongo's Christian identity.

## Christian State-Building

Under Afonso, [[mbanza-kongo|Mbanza Kongo]] was renamed São Salvador, a cathedral and churches were built, and schools were established for the elite. His son **Henrique** was educated in Portugal and became the first sub-Saharan African bishop (Bishop of Utica, 1518). Afonso corresponded in literate Portuguese with the kings of Portugal, the Pope, and other European rulers — producing one of the earliest substantial documentary records of an African ruler's voice.

## Struggle with the Slave Trade

Afonso's most famous letters protest the **depopulation** caused by Portuguese (and São Tomé islanders') trafficking in enslaved people, including the free subjects and even nobles of Kongo. His letter to João III of Portugal (1526) is among the most remarkable documents in early Atlantic history. Yet Kongo itself participated in the slave trade — exporting war captives and using Atlantic trade revenues to fund the state — creating the central contradiction of his reign.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Afonso's extraordinary combination of Christian piety, political skill, and diplomatic initiative, the Kongo-Portuguese relationship would likely have remained shallower. His correspondence established a model of literate African diplomacy that persisted for generations. His failure to stop the slave trade, however, reflects structural rather than merely personal limits: the trade's economic logic overrode diplomatic protest even from a king.

## Historiography

Afonso I is the best-documented African ruler of the early sixteenth century by far, thanks to his own letters preserved in Lisbon (Arquivo Nacional da Torre do Tombo) and missionary accounts. Historians have debated how "genuinely" Christian his court was versus how strategically Christianity was deployed; Thornton (1984, 1992) and MacGaffey (1986b) represent different poles. The KongoKing project (Bostoen & Brinkman 2018) provides new archaeological and linguistic context for the kingdom he ruled.
