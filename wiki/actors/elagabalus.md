## Overview

Elagabalus, born Bassianus, was the grandson of Julia Maesa (sister of
[[julia-domna|Julia Domna]]) and son of Julia Soaemias. Like his Emesene forebears he
was hereditary high-priest of the Temple of Elah-Gabal — "God of the Mountain," a name
cognate with the Arabic *jabal* and related to *Allah* — at [[emesa|Emesa]], and it is
from this priestly title ("Elagabalus," Latinized also as "Heliogabalus") that he takes
his name. Julia Maesa spread the rumor that the boy was the illegitimate son of
Caracalla, and disaffected troops proclaimed him emperor at Emesa in 218, at age
sixteen. His reign lasted, in the ancient sources' precise phrasing, "three years, nine
months and four days"; he was murdered together with his mother by the Praetorian
Guard in 222, their bodies dumped in a sewer, and was succeeded by his cousin Severus
Alexander.

## The Black Stone and the God's "Marriages"

Elagabalus brought his cult object to Rome: a large black stone (a baetyl, probably of
volcanic or meteoric origin, with no accompanying anthropomorphic statue) representing
Elah-Gabal. He built a Temple of Elagabal on the Palatine Hill, near an earlier Temple
of Adonis, and staged ritual "marriages" of the god — first to the Roman/Trojan goddess
Pallas (said to have been brought to Rome by Aeneas), then to the Carthaginian/Phoenician
goddess Urania. Ball reads these unions not as personal eccentricity but as "sound
religious and political policy": a deliberate act of syncretism between eastern and
western cults. Elagabalus is also credited with associating Jews, Samaritans, and
Christians with the cult in an apparent attempt to make it universally inclusive.

He gave women — his mother Julia Soaemias and grandmother Julia Maesa — seats in the
Senate, and promoted freedmen to high office, both practices that provoked severe
censure among contemporary Roman elites.

## Ball's Defense Against the Hostile Portrait

**THESIS.** Ball argues (explicitly as his own interpretive position) that the lurid
depiction of Elagabalus transmitted by the *Historia Augusta* — "frenzied orgies," a
"frenzy of arrogance and madness" — is hostile propaganda comparable in kind to Roman
slanders once directed against Christian rites, rather than reliable reportage.
Behaviors read by hostile Roman authors as depraved — ecstatic ritual dancing, carrying
a parasol — were, in Ball's reading, normal features of eastern royal and religious
practice: ritual dance as an accepted element of eastern religion, the parasol as "the
almost universal oriental symbol of kingship." Ball notes that the sole ancient author
to write of Elagabalus with any warmth was the 6th-century chronicler John Malalas. Even
the *Historia Augusta* itself, quoted by Ball, concedes uncertainty about its own
material: "both these matters and some others which pass belief were, I think, invented
by people who wanted to depreciate Heliogabalus to win favour with Alexander." This
defense is Ball's own revisionist reading and is not the consensus position among
Roman historians; it should be attributed to him rather than stated as settled fact.

## The Sun Cult's Afterlife

Elagabalus's coinage, like that of the other Severan emperors and of
[[philip-the-arab|Philip the Arab]], frequently depicted Syrian deities such as Hadad
and Atargatis. Though the emperor's personal cult of Elah-Gabal did not survive his
death, Ball connects it to a longer trajectory: the cult was later "resurrected" with
full Roman respectability by [[aurelian|Aurelian]], who attributed his 272 victory over
[[zenobia|Zenobia]] outside Emesa to the intervention of the Emesene Sun god, paid
respects at its temple, and built a grand Temple of the Sun in Rome decorated with loot
from [[palmyra|Palmyra]]. Ball traces a line (THESIS) from this Sun cult through
Aurelian's state religion to Constantine's earlier devotion to Sol Invictus, arguing
that "from Sol Invictus to Christianity the step was almost negligible" — citing the
eastward orientation of churches, the shift of holy observance to "the day of the Sun,"
and 25 December (the Sun's traditional "birthday") becoming Christmas as accommodations
to a cult Elagabalus had first tried, and failed, to install at the center of Roman
religious life. This causal chain from the Emesene Sun cult to Christian practice is
Ball's interpretive argument, not an uncontested finding.

## Counterfactual Significance

Elagabalus's brief, scandal-ridden reign is, in Ball's broader argument, one of the
clearest instances of Near Eastern religious practice being imposed — even if
unsuccessfully and briefly — at the very center of Roman public life. Had the Emesene
priest-emperors never come to the throne, the direct transplantation of the Baal cult
to the Palatine would not have occurred, though Ball argues the underlying eastward
religious drift (culminating in Christianity's establishment) had causes well beyond any
one emperor's personal actions.

## Historiography

The principal ancient source for Elagabalus's reign is the unreliable *Historia
Augusta*, supplemented by Cassius Dio and Herodian; all three are hostile, and none is
contemporary in the strict sense with unbiased motive. Ball's rehabilitative reading —
recasting reported "madness" as normal eastern royal and religious behavior
misunderstood by Roman elites — is a deliberately revisionist position within his larger
"orientalization" thesis; see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]] for the standing
debate with the mainstream Graeco-Roman-continuity view associated with Fergus Millar.
