## Overview

Joseph Albo was an Aragonese Jewish philosopher and theologian of the early fifteenth century, a student of [[hasdai-crescas]] and participant in the Tortosa Disputation (1413–1414). His *Sefer ha-ʿiqqarim* (*Book of Principles*) is his major surviving work, written to defend the foundations of Judaism against both Christian polemics and internal Jewish philosophical debate ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## The Tortosa Disputation

Between February 1413 and November 1414, sixty-nine sessions of formal debate were held in the Spanish city of Tortosa, convened by an antipope of Avignon. The Jewish representatives debated a learned convert from Judaism, Jerónimo de Santa Fe, who used his knowledge of the Talmud to argue that it itself proved Jesus was the already-arrived Messiah. Some rabbis eventually signed documents abandoning the Talmud; others, including Albo, did not. The experience directly shaped Albo's *Book of Principles*.

## The Book of Principles

Albo's work contributes to what Adamson calls "the dominant debate within later medieval Jewish philosophy": the question of which beliefs are truly foundational to Judaism. This debate runs from [[maimonides]]' thirteen principles through [[hasdai-crescas]]' three "roots" to Albo's own reduction.

### Three Fundamental Roots

Albo identifies three primary *ʿiqqarim* (principles/roots):
1. God exists
2. The Torah is revealed from heaven
3. Reward and punishment in the next life

These three ground everything else; derived doctrines (divine unity, incorporeality, belief in the Messiah) are subordinate to one or another of them.

### Narrowing Orthodoxy's Boundaries

Where Maimonides treated denial of bodily resurrection and denial of the Messiah's future arrival as heresies, Albo is more flexible. Belief in the Messiah, he argues, should not be a litmus test for Jewish faith — partly because it was precisely this claim that Christian polemicists exploited (arguing that the Messiah had already come). Belief in *some* form of afterlife reward and punishment is required; its exact form is not.

> "The laying down of principles and non-negotiable beliefs can widen the boundaries of orthodoxy, just as much as it can tighten them." — Adamson

### On Rational Proof

Following his teacher Crescas, Albo is skeptical of reason's ability to establish the wide doctrinal portfolio Maimonides promised. Of the three claims central to Maimonides' philosophical theology — God exists, is one, is immaterial — Albo thinks only the first (God's existence) can be proven demonstratively, by a regress argument from Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics*.

That matter might be eternal rather than created (Gersonides' position) is, for Albo, a possibility that reason cannot eliminate — even if he himself does not believe it.

### Law as Demonstrative Science

Despite his criticisms of Maimonides, Albo shares the Maimonidean conception of the Torah as structured like an Aristotelian demonstrative science, with principles playing the role of axioms. He explicitly cites Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics* in explaining the relationship between his three roots and derived doctrines. In this sense he remains a Maimonidean even while criticizing the number and content of Maimonides' principles.

## Legacy

Albo represents a conservative reduction of the philosophical project of grounding Judaism — accepting the structural Maimonidean approach while relaxing some of its specific claims and trimming its rationalist excess. His *Book of Principles* was widely read in subsequent generations. His successor [[isaac-abravanel]] pushed the logic of reduction further, eventually abandoning the entire principle-project.
