# Aeneas

**Aeneas**, son of the goddess Venus (Aphrodite) and the mortal Anchises, is the Trojan hero who survives the fall of Troy and becomes the legendary founder of the Roman people. In Homer's *Iliad* he is a minor but noble figure, a survivor destined to rule the Trojans after the war. Virgil elevates him in the *Aeneid* to the central protagonist of Rome's origin myth: the pious exile who carries his household gods (*penates*) from burning Troy, wanders the Mediterranean, loves and loses Dido in Carthage, consults the Underworld, and fights to establish a foothold in Italy.

His defining quality is **pietas** — duty to the gods, to his father Anchises, to his son Ascanius/Iulus (ancestor of the Julian line), and ultimately to the future of Rome. He subordinates personal happiness (his love for Dido) and even mercy (the killing of Turnus at the poem's end) to *fatum*, the destiny that will lead to the founding of Lavinium, Alba Longa, and Rome itself.

The poem presents him as both Homeric hero and Roman ideal — a man of action and of suffering (*lacrimae rerum*, "the tears of things").

## Historiography and Significance

The "historical" Aeneas is a construct. Greek traditions already linked Trojan survivors to Italy (e.g., via Elymus or other figures). Virgil synthesizes these into a coherent national myth that justifies Roman imperialism while acknowledging its human cost. Later ages revered him as the model of the dutiful leader (Dante makes him Virgil's guide in part because of this).
