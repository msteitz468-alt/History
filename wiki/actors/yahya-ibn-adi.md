## Overview

Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī (893–974) was the leading philosopher of the [[baghdad-school-aristotelians|Baghdad school of Christian Aristotelians]] in the mid-tenth century. A Monophysite Christian, he was nicknamed "the logician" (*al-manṭiqī*) in recognition of his preeminence in Aristotelian logic. He was both a student and colleague of [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]] — who, as a Muslim in a predominantly Christian school, studied with Ibn ʿAdī rather than the reverse in terms of seniority — and the teacher of later Baghdad Peripatetics.

New manuscript discoveries in Tehran have recently expanded the known corpus of his writings; full scholarly assessment is ongoing.

## Philosophical Works

### Logic

**On the Nature of the Possible**: Ibn ʿAdī's most sophisticated logical work, addressing Aristotle's sea-battle argument from *On Interpretation*. This argument holds that if there are present truths about future events ("there will be a sea battle tomorrow"), then those events cannot fail to occur — eliminating contingency. Ibn ʿAdī combines an independent treatise with a commentary on the relevant Aristotelian chapter, an unusual format. His solution draws on the Neoplatonist Iamblichus (via the Greek commentary tradition): the features of knowledge belong to the knower, not to what is known. God can know necessarily what is in itself contingent, because necessity characterises God's eternal act of knowing, not the contingent events He knows. (Boethius arrived at a similar solution in Latin by the same Greek route — not through influence on Ibn ʿAdī but from common ancient sources.)

**On the Relative Merits of Logic and Grammar**: Written in the aftermath of Abū Bishr Mattā's public disputation with al-Sīrāfī. Ibn ʿAdī defends logic against the grammarian's claim that expertise in Arabic suffices for correct reasoning. His argument: grammar ensures correct conventional usage, and its goal is nothing beyond this. Logic's goal is truth and demonstration. A grammatically correct sentence ("X is a giraffe") need not be true; logic determines whether statements are actually sound, while grammar simply determines whether they conform to the rules of a given language. Grammar is therefore the study of the parochial and conventional; logic is universal and indispensable.

### Theology and Apologetics

**Defence of the Trinity**: Ibn ʿAdī wrote multiple apologetic treatises defending the Monophysite conception of the Trinity against Muslim critiques (including [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]]'s treatise against the Trinity, which survives only because Ibn ʿAdī quoted it in his counter-refutation) and against the rival Nestorian view. He uses Aristotelian philosophical categories to articulate Monophysite Christology — the view that Christ is a full unity of human and divine — and to criticize the Nestorian position that Christ has two distinct natures and two *hypostaseis*. The apologetic writings thus represent Aristotelian philosophy directly in service of Christian doctrine, in contrast to al-Fārābī's ostentatiously religion-neutral approach.

**Engagement with kalām**: Ibn ʿAdī wrote works engaging Islamic rational theology ([[kalam]]), including a refutation of the Ashʿarite doctrine of "acquisition" (*kasb*) — the view that God creates human actions which humans then "acquire" so as to be morally responsible. He seems unimpressed by this solution to the problem of divine creation and human moral responsibility. His engagement with kalām arguments is more extensive than one might expect from a Christian Aristotelian; the reasons are not fully understood.

**Correspondence with a Jewish philosopher**: Ibn ʿAdī exchanged polite and learned letters with Ibn Abī Saʿīd al-Mawṣilī on philosophical topics. This correspondence — which can still be read today — represents one of the clearest instances of interreligious philosophical dialogue in the Baghdad school's environment.

## Place in the Baghdad School

Ibn ʿAdī represents the school's mid-tenth-century maturity. He translated Aristotelian texts into Arabic (working from Syriac, not Greek — demonstrating the continued importance of the Syriac scholarly tradition). His logical and apologetic works show a thinker thoroughly grounded in the Alexandrian commentary tradition, able to deploy Aristotelian categories with precision across philosophical and theological contexts. Where he falls short of [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]] is in systematic ambition: he writes occasional works on defined topics without providing the large-scale integration of metaphysics, cosmology, ethics, and politics that made al-Fārābī the dominant figure of the school's legacy.

## Historiography

Ibn ʿAdī is less well studied than al-Fārābī despite his importance in the Baghdad school. The discovery of new manuscripts in Tehran is reshaping scholarly understanding of his output. His dual identity as Aristotelian logician and Christian apologist raises interesting questions about the relationship between philosophical method and theological commitment in the Baghdad milieu — questions that his works address more directly than those of al-Fārābī, who avoids explicit religious identification. His solution to the sea-battle problem is philosophically comparable to Boethius's, illustrating how independent thinkers working from the same late-ancient Greek sources converged on similar positions without mutual knowledge.
