# Umayyad Caliphate

The **Umayyad Caliphate** (661–750 CE) was the second of the four major Islamic caliphates established after the death of Muhammad. It was ruled by the **Umayyad dynasty**, originating from the Quraysh tribe of Mecca. Establishing their capital at **Damascus** in Syria, the Umayyads centralized the imperial administration, built a professional military and navy, and directed the second great wave of Arab conquests, extending the empire from the Iberian Peninsula in the west to Sind and Transoxiana in the east.

## History and Dynastic Phases

### 1. The Sufyanid Period (661–684 CE)
Following the assassination of Caliph Ali in 661 (ending the [[first-fitna-656|First Fitna]]), **Mu'awiya I** (r. 661–680), the governor of Syria, was recognized as caliph. Mu'awiya consolidated the state, stabilized the frontiers in Anatolia, and created a professional naval force that launched the first Arab siege of Constantinople (674–678). He broke with the elective tradition of the [[rashidun-caliphate|Rashidun]] era by nominating his son, **Yazid I** (r. 680–683), as his successor, transforming the caliphate into a hereditary monarchy.

Yazid's accession sparked the **[[second-fitna-680|Second Fitna]]** (680–692). The rebellion began with the martyrdom of Ali's son, Husayn, at the **Battle of Karbala** (680) by Umayyad forces, permanently cementing the Sunni-Shi'a division. In Arabia, Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr declared a rival caliphate in Mecca. Yazid's death and the brief reign of Mu'awiya II led to a succession crisis, resolved when the senior Umayyad prince **Marwan I** (r. 684–685) seized the throne, initiating the Marwanid branch of the dynasty.

### 2. The Marwanid Consolidation (685–743 CE)
Marwan's son, **[[abd-al-malik|Abd al-Malik]]** (r. 685–705), defeated Ibn al-Zubayr, suppressed regional revolts, and consolidated the empire. Abd al-Malik and his successor, **al-Walid I** (r. 705–715), oversaw the zenith of Umayyad power, including:
- The **Islamic conquest of Spain** (711), led by Tariq ibn Ziyad, which destroyed the Visigothic Kingdom.
- Expansion into Central Asia (Transoxiana) under Qutayba ibn Muslim and into the Indus Valley (Sind) under Muhammad ibn Qasim.
- The construction of the **Dome of the Rock** in Jerusalem.

### 3. Decline and Fall (743–750 CE)
The dynasty faced mounting internal pressures:
- **Mawali Discontent**: The empire's fiscal structure depended on the *jizya* (poll tax) paid by non-Muslims. As conversions to Islam surged, the Umayyads refused to grant these new non-Arab converts (*mawali*) equal tax status and military salaries, violating Islamic principles of equality.
- **Tribal Rivalries**: The Syrian military elite was factionalized by ancient tribal rivalries (the Qays vs. Yaman factions), crippling royal stability.
- **Abbasid Revolution**: Exploiting these grievances, the **Abbasids** launched a covert propaganda campaign in Khorasan. Led by Abu Muslim, the Abbasid forces defeated the last Umayyad caliph, Marwan II, at the **Battle of the Zab** in 750. The Umayyad family was systematically slaughtered, though one prince, Abd al-Rahman I, escaped to Spain, founding the independent **Emirate of Córdoba** (756).

## Administrative Reforms and Arabization

Under Caliph [[abd-al-malik|Abd al-Malik]], the Umayyads undertook a major reorganization of the state, replacing Byzantine and Sasanian administrative holdovers with a distinct Islamic imperial apparatus:
- **Arabization of the Bureaucracy**: Greek and Middle Persian were replaced by Arabic as the sole language of government administration (*diwan*), forcing non-Arab scribes to learn Arabic to retain their positions.
- **Islamic Monetary System**: The Umayyads ceased using modified Byzantine and Sasanian coins. In 696/7, Abd al-Malik introduced a standardized, epigraphic gold dinar and silver dirham featuring Qur'anic inscriptions and no human images, asserting the state's religious and economic sovereignty.
- **Postal Network (*Barid*)**: Reorganized as a highly efficient communication and espionage network linking Damascus with the provinces.
