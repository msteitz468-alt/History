# Roman Kingdom (Regal Period)

The Roman Kingdom is the traditional first phase of Roman history — the era of the **seven
kings**, from the founding by Romulus (traditionally 753 BCE) to the expulsion of the last
king and the establishment of the Republic (traditionally 509 BCE). In Mary Beard's *SPQR*
the regal period is treated as **reconstruction and myth**: the surviving narrative is a
later Roman schema, culturally revealing but not a reliable political chronology. See
[[sources/beard-spqr-2015]].

## The Traditional Scheme

Roman tradition remembered seven kings, each associated with a defining institutional
contribution:

- **Romulus** — founder; the Senate, the army, the asylum, the absorption of the Sabines.
- **Numa Pompilius** — the religious institutions, calendar, and priesthoods (Rome's peace-king).
- **Tullus Hostilius** and **Ancus Marcius** — war and expansion; the port at Ostia.
- **Tarquinius Priscus** (Tarquin the Elder) — Etruscan-associated; building programmes, the
  Circus, the great drain (Cloaca Maxima).
- **Servius Tullius** — the "Servian" reforms: the census, the division of the citizen body
  into property classes, and the centuriate organisation that structured both army and
  assembly.
- **Tarquinius Superbus** (Tarquin the Proud) — the tyrannical last king, whose overthrow
  (linked in legend to the rape of Lucretia) inaugurated the Republic.

## Significance (Analytical)

Beard's point is less about the individual kings than about what the scheme *does*. It
projects backward, onto a line of founder-kings, the institutions that mattered to later
Romans — Senate, priesthoods, census, army — giving each a clean origin story. The pattern of
**absorbing outsiders** (Sabines, Latins, an Etruscan dynasty) and the graded ordering of
citizens by the "Servian" census already encode themes central to the later Republic: a
composite citizen body and a hierarchy of political weight tied to wealth and military
obligation. The transition to the Republic is remembered as a reaction against monarchy so
formative that *rex* (king) became a permanent term of political abuse.

## Beard's Reading (SPQR, 2015)

Mary Beard sharpens the skeptical case. She notes the sheer chronological implausibility of
the scheme: fitting seven kings into the ~250 years the tradition allows gives each an average
reign of more than three decades — "no modern monarchy has ever equalled that consistent level
of longevity." She argues Rome's historians "systematically" modernised and aggrandised the
period, retrojecting the senate, popular assemblies, "foreign policy," and pitched legionary
battles onto what was in reality a settlement of a few thousand people (Fabius Pictor's claimed
80,000 adult male citizens she calls "a ludicrous figure," archaeologically unsupported; the
best modern estimate is 20,000–30,000). She suggests it may be truer to speak of a **"chiefly"
rather than "regal" period** — early *reges* as "chiefs or big men," warfare as small-scale
raiding fought within a dozen miles of Rome. The decisive evidence that Rome *had* once had
kings at all is the archaic **RECEI inscription** ("to/for the king") found under the Forum's
black stone by Giacomo Boni in 1899, which ended the most radical nineteenth-century skepticism
even though its text is largely untranslatable.

Beard also foregrounds a startling **alternative tradition**: in the emperor Claudius's 48 CE
speech to the senate (preserved on the Lyon bronze) and the François Tomb paintings at Vulci,
Servius Tullius appears not as a constitutional reformer but as **Mastarna**, an Etruscan
warlord and follower of Caelius Vivenna who seized the Caelian Hill with the remnants of a
militia — "rival militias, more or less itinerant warlords, personal loyalty, shifting
identities," a world utterly unlike Livy's. She treats the "Servian" census-and-centuriate
constitution as "a flagrant projection into the past" of much later practice, while allowing
that its retrojection preserves authentic Roman values (wealth should bring political weight;
citizenship and military service belong together). The calendar attributed to **Numa**, by
contrast, she thinks plausibly rooted in genuine early practice — the modern Western month-names
are "a direct descendant" of it.

## Historiography

No contemporary written evidence survives for the regal centuries; the tradition took shape
in later Republican historiography and antiquarianism. Archaeology confirms a real,
growing early settlement — with monumental building and Etruscan cultural contact in the
sixth century that lends some plausibility to the "Tarquin" phase — but cannot underwrite the
king-list, the regnal dates, or the individual reforms as narrated. Beard uses the regal
material chiefly as a window onto Roman self-understanding. See [[sources/beard-spqr-2015]].
