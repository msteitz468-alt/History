## Overview

Kevin Michael Rudd was Prime Minister of Australia from November 2007 to June 2010, when he was removed by the Labor caucus — the first sitting Labor prime minister to be so deposed. His government navigated Australia through the Global Financial Crisis, ratified the Kyoto Protocol, and issued the Stolen Generations apology. He served a second brief prime ministership in 2013 before leading Labor to defeat in the September 2013 election.

## Role and Major Decisions

Rudd's "popularity in the opinion polls saw him replace Kim Beazley as Labor's leader in 2006" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). He defeated Howard in November 2007, with Howard losing his own seat — a rare and symbolically powerful outcome.

**Stolen Generations apology (February 2008)**: Rudd's "first act as prime minister" was ratifying the Kyoto Protocol; his second was the apology to Indigenous Australians, "which came to be seen both as a repudiation of Howard's mean-spiritedness and a symbolic new beginning" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). The parliament presented two apologies: Rudd's and an equivocal one from Opposition leader Brendan Nelson.

**GFC stimulus package**: The Rudd government went into debt to limit the GFC's impact, deploying $14.7 billion for school buildings, $12.7 billion in cash handouts, $6.6 billion for public housing, $2.7 billion for home insulation, and a first home buyers' grant. "Although his economic policies had helped shield Australia from global recession, his popularity with the public and among his close colleagues had plummeted" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). Australia avoided recession while virtually all other advanced economies contracted.

**Kyoto ratification**: Rudd ratified the Protocol immediately on taking office. He subsequently negotiated the Carbon Pollution Reduction Scheme with Liberal leader Malcolm Turnbull in 2009; after Turnbull lost the Liberal leadership to Tony Abbott (a climate-change sceptic), the Greens and Coalition combined to defeat the legislation. "Returning from the United Nation's climate conference in Copenhagen later in December 2009 without an international agreement, Rudd abandoned the legislation" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8).

**China policy**: Rudd spoke Mandarin on his first visit to China and promised in the spirit of "true friendship" to raise Tibet. With the Chinese taking unkindly to this, he backed off. A 2009 Defence White Paper foreshadowed the biggest increase in defence spending since World War 2, naming China "as the main source of threat," while bilateral engagement on security and defence continued.

**Removal**: The Resources Super Profits Tax (2010) triggered a mining-industry advertising campaign. "Exaggerating their contribution helped the mining companies mobilise public opinion against Labor's proposed Resources Super Profits Tax in 2010 and sealed Rudd's fate" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). Julia Gillard succeeded him in June 2010.

## Counterfactual Significance

The GFC fiscal stimulus is Rudd's most consequential decision. Counterfactual analysis is complicated by Australia's structural advantages (commodity boom, banks unexposed to US subprime assets), but the stimulus package was widely credited as the decisive factor in avoiding recession. Without it, unemployment would likely have risen substantially above the 5 per cent level it reached and remained.
