## Overview

Jalal al-Din Muhammad Rumi (1207–1273), known universally as Rumi or Mawlana ("Our Master"), is the most significant Persian Sufi poet and one of the most widely read poets in world literature. Born in Balkh (in present-day Afghanistan) and educated in Khurasan and Syria, he settled in Konya (in the Seljuk-successor sultanate of Rum, present-day Turkey), where he spent most of his productive life under Turkic patronage—a biographical fact that perfectly illustrates the Persianate cultural process: a Persian poet working in Anatolia under Turkic rulers, composing works that would be read from Sumatra to the Balkans.

Rumi's two major works are the *Mathnawi-ye Ma'nawi* (Spiritual Couplets, often simply "the Mathnawi"), an enormous didactic Sufi poem in six books totaling roughly 25,000 couplets, and the *Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi* (Collected Poems of Shams of Tabriz), a vast collection of ghazals and other shorter forms composed in states of ecstatic inspiration associated with his spiritual master Shams-i Tabrizi.

## Background and Education

Rumi's family fled the Mongol advance westward when he was a child. His father Baha' al-Din Walad was himself a religious teacher and mystic. Rumi received a thorough traditional education in religious sciences and was deeply influenced by the Sufi poet Farid al-Din 'Attar, whom he is said to have met as a child (and from whom, Hodgson notes, "he learned much"). After his father's death, he came under the guidance of a Sufi master named Burhan al-Din Muhaqqiq. He was established as a religious teacher in Konya when the encounter with Shams-i Tabrizi transformed his life.

## Shams-i Tabrizi and the Lyric Explosion

In 1244, the wandering dervish Shams-i Tabrizi arrived in Konya and entered into an intense spiritual friendship with Rumi that overturned the latter's orderly religious career. Rumi's disciples resented the newcomer, and Shams eventually disappeared—perhaps killed, perhaps driven away. The loss shattered Rumi and was the generative catastrophe behind the *Divan-e Shams*: poems of longing, ecstasy, and mystical union composed as if addressed to Shams as the human mirror of the divine. The Divan contains approximately 35,000 verses and is among the most extensive bodies of lyric poetry composed by any single figure.

## The Mathnawi

Rumi's *Mathnawi-ye Ma'nawi* is his most systematic and philosophically ambitious work. Composed at the request of his disciple Husam al-Din Chalabi, it extends over six books in the mathnawi couplet form pioneered by [[firdawsi]] and elaborated by [[nizami-of-ganjah]] and [[attar-farid-al-din]]. Hodgson describes it as explicitly esoteric: "Rumi was quite explicit that his mathnawi, also, must be read esoterically." Rumi himself says in the text (citing II, 3602 ff.) that stories constructed to mislead the literal-minded reader will indeed mislead him—that the stories' literal surface is a veil, not the content.

The Mathnawi is not a systematic treatise but a spiraling accumulation of tales, digressions, Qur'anic commentary, Sufi doctrine, humor, psychology, and lyric interludes. Its organizing logic is associative rather than argumentative: one story spawns another, a verse triggers a digression, a doctrinal point is illustrated by a joke. The work draws extensively on earlier Persian poetry, Qur'anic material, hadith, folk tales, and Sufi anecdote. Its opening lines—the "Song of the Reed" (Ney nema) lamenting separation from the reed bed—became one of the most famous passages in Persian literature, understood as the soul's longing for reunion with the divine origin.

Hodgson situates the Mathnawi within the broader development of "mythic-visional writing" in Islamdom: works that attempt "a comprehensive vision of the totality of life" through "direct, if symbolic and mythic, description of the world as a whole." Such works "will be misunderstood if read simply as lyrical outpourings, or as pseudo-science or metaphysics, or as fiction" ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v2-1974]]).

## Sufi Significance

Rumi stands at the intersection of the literary Sufi tradition and the more technical Sufi metaphysics of Ibn al-'Arabi. He was a practicing Sufi master with disciples, a man whose spiritual life organized around the direct experience of divine love rather than abstract theological formulation. Yet he also engaged the intellectual traditions of Falsafah and kalam, and his Mathnawi is philosophically sophisticated.

After his death, his son Sultan Walad organized his disciples into the Mawlawiyya tariqah (known in the Ottoman world as the Mevlevi order, the "Whirling Dervishes"), which institutionalized the sama' (spiritual listening, especially music and movement) that Rumi had practiced. The Mevlevi order became one of the most prestigious Sufi institutions of the Ottoman empire.

## Persian Poet in Anatolia

The biographical fact that Rumi did "most of his work under Turkic rule in Anatolia far to the west of his Iranian birthplace" (Hodgson) is culturally significant. It demonstrates that the Persianate literary tradition was not geographically confined to the Iranian plateau but had become a genuinely Islamdom-wide cultural language. Rumi composed almost entirely in Persian despite living in a primarily Greek- and Turkish-speaking environment. His Persian works were read across the entire Persianate zone during his lifetime and long after.

## Translation and Reception

Rumi's Mathnawi was translated into Turkish and other Islamicate languages during his lifetime and shortly after. In the modern period, English-language versions—especially loose renderings by Coleman Barks—have made Rumi the best-selling poet in the United States, though Barks's renderings are very far from the original. Hodgson's general caution about Persian poetry translation ("any translation omits much of value, and many translations import very alien elements") applies forcefully here. The original Persian depends on complex metrical patterns, rhyme schemes, Qur'anic and hadith allusions, and Sufi technical vocabulary that disappears in loose paraphrase.

## Counterfactual Significance

Rumi's role in the Persianate tradition is difficult to imagine away: he is both its culminating mystical expression and the founder of a durable institutional Sufi order. Without him, the mathnawi form would still have been available for extended Sufi narrative (as 'Attar had already used it), but the specific synthesis of philosophical depth, lyric intensity, and narrative variety that defines the Mathnawi is singular. The Mevlevi order that perpetuated his influence into Ottoman times would not have existed.

## Historiography

The scholarly study of Rumi has grown enormously since the twentieth century. Key contributions include Reynold Nicholson's critical edition and translation of the Mathnawi (8 vols., 1925–1940), still the standard scholarly reference, and the work of Annemarie Schimmel on the mystical dimensions of Persian poetry. Franklin Lewis's *Rumi: Past and Present, East and West* (2000) provides a comprehensive scholarly treatment. The popular reception of Rumi in the West, largely through Barks's versions, has been extensively criticized by scholars for decontextualizing the Islamic spiritual content. Hodgson's treatment in [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v2-1974]] situates Rumi correctly within the broader history of Islamicate cultural life rather than treating him as a decontextualized mystical poet.
