# Visigoths

**The western Goths**, a people created — not merely relocated — during forty years of
wandering inside the Roman Empire (376–416), who built the **kingdom of Toulouse** in
Aquitaine and later the Visigothic kingdom of Spain. Geary treats them as the classic case
of [[ethnogenesis]]: "just as the Sinai period created the Hebrew people from the disparate
group of refugees who left Egypt, the forty years of uncertainty and wandering within the
Empire transformed the Tervingian refugees… into what history knows as the Visigoths."

## Origins: from Tervingi to Visigoths

The Visigoths emerged from the **Tervingian confederation** of the lower Danube — itself "hardly
a tribe in the sense of a group of common origin but rather the political constellation of
smaller groups, or *kunja*," united by common cult and led by their individual *reiks*. The Balt
leader **Ariarich** concluded the first *foedus* with Constantine in 332. Tervingian elites deeply
imitated Roman forms: a medallion from Szilágysomlyó bears the images of Valentinian I and Valens
with the legend *Regis Romanorum*. Bishop **Wulfila** (c. 311–383), consecrated "Bishop of the
Christians in the Getic land" (341), translated the Bible into Gothic; his theology refused to
address the substance of the divine, making him neither Nicene nor strictly Arian, though his
followers were later classified as Arians.

In **376** the arrival of the [[huns|Huns]] shattered the Tervingi under Athanaric; the majority
followed Fritigern and Alaviv across the Danube and "henceforth entered history as the Visigoths."

## Inside the Empire

After [[barbarization-of-the-roman-army|Adrianople (378)]], hunger forced Fritigern to a treaty with
**Theodosius (382)**, settling the Goths in Dacia and Thrace as federates with their command intact.
[[alaric|Alaric]]'s whole career was a quest for legitimacy as a supreme Roman commander (named
*magister militum* of eastern Illyricum in 397); the **sack of Rome (410)** was not his aim — he
needed food and had sought to lead his people to North Africa. Athaulf led the Goths into Aquitaine
in 413 "at once a barbarian people and a Roman army"; his successor Walia settled them there as Roman
federates, creating the **kingdom of Toulouse**. The Visigothic state was thus "the adaptation of the
'Gothic system' within the context of a Roman administrative and military system," not the importing of
barbarian society into the West.

## Decline north of the Pyrenees

At [[battle-of-vouille-507|Vouillé (507)]] the Franks under [[clovis|Clovis]] defeated and killed
**Alaric II**; the following year Toulouse fell and the Gothic presence north of the Pyrenees was
reduced to a coastal strip as far east as Narbonne, pushing the Visigothic centre of gravity into Spain.

## Significance / Counterfactual

The Visigoths demonstrate Geary's central claim that barbarian "peoples" were political constructions
forged in interaction with Rome. Their Arianism — unlike Frankish orthodoxy — kept them at a distance
from the Gallo-Roman and Hispano-Roman populations they ruled, a structural weakness that contrasts
with the [[merovingian-franks|Frankish]] amalgamation and helps explain why the Frankish kingdom proved
the most durable successor state.
