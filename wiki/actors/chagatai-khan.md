# Chagatai Khan

## Overview

**Chagatai** (Ča’adai / Chaghatai; c. 1183–1242), second son of [[genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]] and [[borte|Börte]], received the central Asian portion of the empire that became the [[chaghatay-khanate|Chaghadaid Khanate]]. He was known as a stern guardian of Chinggisid custom and law ([[the-mongol-yasa|Yasa]] / jasagh tradition). After 1206 decimal reorganization he received 8,000 followers—more than the younger brothers’ 5,000 but less than a full *tümen*; Genghis appointed overseers because, Weatherford quotes, Chagatai was “obstinate and has a petty, narrow mind.”

## Role (Weatherford 2004)

- **Succession crisis catalyst.** At the family *khuriltai* before the [[khwarazmian-campaign-1219|Khwarazmian war]], when Genghis invited [[jochi|Jochi]] to speak first, Chagatai publicly denied Jochi’s legitimacy: “How could we allow ourselves to be ruled by this bastard son of a Merkid?” The brothers fought. Outwardly submitting to Genghis’s command that Jochi remained eldest, Chagatai grinned that “game killed by mouth cannot be loaded onto a horse. Game slaughtered by words cannot be skinned”—and engineered the compromise: succession to third brother [[ogedei-khan|Ögödei]]. Genghis ordered the two camps far apart.
- **Urgench.** Joint siege with Jochi nearly became fratricidal war over tactics—Jochi protecting a city marked as his patrimony, Chagatai prioritizing Mongol lives over salvage. Flood and fire left nothing for Jochi’s inheritance.
- **Death.** Weatherford places Chagatai’s death at about the same time as Ögödei’s (late 1241 / into 1242), so that all four of Genghis’s sons were dead within fourteen years of their father—opening a decade of succession struggle that, in Weatherford’s narrative, spared the world further Mongol invasion for that interval.

## Historiography

*Secret History* preserves the paternity confrontation that Juvaini omitted and Rashid al-Din half-erased. Weatherford (2004) uses Chagatai as the voice of aristocratic legalism and fraternal hostility that made Ögödei the compromise Great Khan. Later Möngke’s purges (1251–) targeted Chaghadaid as well as Ögödeid supporters (see [[mongke-khan]]).
