## Role and Background

François-Marie Arouet, known by his pen name Voltaire, was the most prolific and internationally famous of the French Enlightenment philosophes — a playwright, poet, historian, and polemicist whose satirical wit and relentless attacks on religious intolerance and arbitrary authority made him both celebrated and repeatedly persecuted, including spells in the Bastille and periods of exile (notably in England, 1726–29).

## Career and Ideas

Voltaire championed deism, religious toleration, and freedom of expression, memorably summarized in his call to "écrasez l'infâme" (crush the infamous thing — institutionalized religious fanaticism). His campaigns on behalf of victims of judicial abuse, most famously the executed Jean Calas, made him an early and influential public intellectual advocate for legal reform. His philosophical tale *Candide* (1759) remains his best-known work, satirizing providentialist optimism in the wake of the 1755 Lisbon earthquake. From 1750 to 1753 he lived at the court of [[actors/frederick-the-great|Frederick the Great]] in Potsdam, an intellectually charged but ultimately strained relationship between two brilliant, difficult personalities that ended in personal rupture.

## Significance

Voltaire's vast correspondence and body of work made him a central node connecting Enlightenment thinkers across Europe, and his advocacy for toleration and against judicial abuse had lasting influence on later reform movements and on the intellectual currents feeding into the French Revolution.
