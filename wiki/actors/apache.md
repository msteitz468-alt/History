# Apache

Umbrella for Athapaskan-speaking peoples of the southern Southwest and southern Plains whom Spaniards labeled with the Pueblo word **“Apache” (enemy)**. Includes Western Apache bands, Mescalero, Eastern groups (Jicarilla, Lipan, Kiowa-Apache), historically overlapping with early [[navajo-dine|Navajo (Apache de Navihu)]]. Primary colonial synthesis: Lamar & Truett, [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-2|CHNPA I.2]] ch. 10. Related frontier page: [[apache-mexico-frontier]].

## Origins and naming

Relative newcomers who migrated south from central/western Canada between **A.D. 800 and 1500** by a still-debated route. At Coronado’s time, nomadic and seminomadic groups lay south and east of Pueblo villages. In the century after first contact Spanish were unsure who hostile peoples were, using “Apache” as catch-all; **Apache de Navihu** designated those later called Navajos. Sixteenth-century survivors known today as **Western Apaches, Mescalero Apaches, and Navajos**.

Mid/late sixteenth-century Spanish explorers met pedestrian hunters (probably Apaches) using dogs; by late seventeenth century horses from Spanish/Pueblo settlements transformed hunting and transport.

## Western Apaches

Five Spanish-named bands: **Mimbrenos, Chiricahuas, Pinalenos, Coyoteros, Tontos**. Seasonal food cycle included summer planting/irrigation, mescal, saguaro, acorns. After livestock introduction they raided Spanish and other Indian settlements for cattle; learning to ride Spanish horses, they became “almost legendary” for swift daring raids.

By **1685** Western Apache havoc produced presidios (Janos 1685; Altar 1690) and a ~**250-mile “Apache corridor”** from southeastern Arizona to the New Mexico–Texas border—blocking Spanish expansion north of the Gila, middle Rio Grande (Albuquerque–El Paso), and around Laguna/Conchos. By **1760**, a century of Spanish control efforts had failed.

Spanish (and Pueblos/Pimas) treated Navajos and Western Apaches as implacable enemies of a raiding economy and sold captives to mines rather than fully incorporating them—yet horse culture (and for Navajos, sheep/wool) radically changed Apachean life.

### Bourbon peace and Mexican collapse

**José de Gálvez** urged separate peace treaties with each Apache band, settlement near presidios, food, alcohol, and inferior firearms, plus trade to break inter-Apache hostility; **Bernardo de Gálvez** implemented. By **1800** many Western Apaches lived near presidios and stopped raiding—some became “hangers-on and drunkards”—until Mexican independence cut funds (**~1811**) and a new devastation killed **~5,000 Mexicans** and drove nearly as many from northwest settlements.

Mexican Arizona/New Mexico: missions ruined by corrupt overseers and Apache raiders; Western and Eastern Coyotero Apaches swept Arizona into Sonora; Mogollon, Mimbreno, Warm Springs, Natage, Mescalero paralleled across SW New Mexico into Chihuahua—**still in 1845**. Causes: no funds for Gálvez-style gifts; worse Mexican mistreatment; Mexico City civil wars; unpaid under-mounted presidials; Americans sold Indians better weapons than soldiers had.

## Eastern Apaches and Comanche displacement

By **1700**, Jicarilla, Kiowa-Apache, and Lipan occupied northern NM and west Texas plains. Expanded with early horse use and Staked Plains game, then pressed by mounted [[comanches|Comanches]] with superior French guns, Spanish punishment for raiding, and Wichitas. By **1730s** Apaches north of San Antonio raided to survive and ate horsemeat; **1749** San Antonio conference produced “nominal friendship.”

**1757 San Saba:** ~**2,000 Comanches** destroyed mission and presidio, seized **700 livestock**, killed many Apaches—opening Comanche–European warfare in Texas until the [[red-river-war-1874|Red River War of 1874–5]].

**Kiowa-Apaches** affiliated with Kiowas by mid-nineteenth century, adopting much Kiowa culture; raided Spanish then Americans until 1870s reservations. **Mescaleros** of SE New Mexico hunted and gathered without farming; extended-family bands; range from Chihuahua/Coahuila north to 34th parallel and Rio Grande east beyond Pecos—some Plains (buffalo, tipis), some mountain (deer, agave, wickiups). From [[onate-new-mexico-1598|Oñate]] era, Mescaleros faced Spanish attacks and slave raids; remained hostile into Mexican era; briefly aided U.S. takeover then faced land-hungry American settlers.

French guns gave Pawnees, Wichitas, and others an edge against Spain’s Apache allies on the eastern flank (`concurrent_with:` early Plains gun trade).

## U.S. period: forts, Camp Grant, Geronimo

Sumner’s Fort Union perimeter (1850s) faced Jicarilla attacks on Burgwin and herds near Fort Union; Mangas Coloradas’s Chiricahuas raided Tucson to Rio Grande; 1855–56 reservation treaties failed ratification.

**Arizona Territory (1863):** ~5,000 Anglo/Mexican vs ~30,000 Indians. Early treaties (Coyotero 1852; Mimbreno reservation 1853; Cochise’s Overland Mail arrangement) unraveled in the Civil War after Army tried to arrest Cochise and Anglo-Americans murdered chiefs including **Mangas Coloradas (1862)**. Gov. **John N. Goodwin** praised Pima/Papago/Maricopa allies and called Apache a “murderer by hereditary descent—a thief by prescription,” asserting “the Indian and the antelope must disappear together before the Anglo-Saxon race”—stark contrast to Spanish-Mexican incorporation ideology. **1865:** Tubac deserted; Tucson ~200 souls; roads north of Gila blockaded.

**Grant Peace Policy (1869)** sought fewer posts and reservation concentration. **1871 Camp Grant massacre:** 104 Tucson citizens and Papago Indians invaded Camp Grant and killed **115 Arivaipa Apache men, women, and children**. Howard attracted peaceful Apaches to San Carlos and White Mountain; **1872** Cochise ceased warfare. **Crook** ordered all off-reservation Indians pursued and killed by 1872, using Indian scouts; by **1874** sustained warfare almost ended under agents like John P. Clum.

**1885–88 Chiricahua crisis:** dissidents under **Geronimo** fled San Carlos (mixed incompatible groups, corrupt agents, inadequate rations, miner/Mormon land invasions); **Miles** captured them (**1886**); Chiricahua shipped to Fort Marion (Florida) then Fort Sill (Oklahoma)—Carleton-style removal applied “with a vengeance.” Lamar & Truett: military defeat, reservation confinement, and education of youth still did **not** destroy Apache culture or spirit—“incomplete conquest,” as under the Spanish.

Texas after statehood: Lipans, Mescaleros, Kickapoos raided Mexican border; free-ranging Apaches, Kiowas, Comanches reduced via Medicine Lodge and Red River War attrition into late 1870s.

## Ideology contrast

Spanish inclusion of Indians (even as near-slaves) in a wider colonial scheme vs American territorial ideology of extinction or assimilation and settler manifest destiny. American policy mixed ruthless features (if Indians would not be peaceful) with “benign” reservation assimilation—mirroring Spanish dualism in a harsher settler-colonial key.

## Counterfactual significance

The Apache corridor delayed Spanish (and early Mexican) occupation of vast tracts of Arizona and mid-Rio Grande for generations; without it, California overland links and northern Sonora settlement patterns would look different. Incomplete conquest thesis: even final U.S. military victory failed to erase Apache identity—undercutting extinctionist ideology of Goodwin’s type.

## Historiography

Lamar & Truett emphasize corridor geopolitics, Gálvez peace experiment, Camp Grant atrocity, and Geronimo removal as incomplete conquest. Ethnonym “Apache” is Spanish/Pueblo exogenous category over multiple bands—modern tribal distinctions (Western Apache, Chiricahua, Mescalero, Jicarilla, Lipan, Plains Apache) must not be collapsed uncritically. Spicer *Cycles of Conquest* remains synthetic backbone; specialist war literature sits alongside CHNPA bibliographic essay.
