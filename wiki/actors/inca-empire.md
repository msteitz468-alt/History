# Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu)

## Overview

The **Inca Empire** — **Tawantinsuyu**, "the four parts together" — was the largest
imperial state of pre-Columbian America, stretching at its height over **4,000 km**
along the Andes and the Pacific coast of South America, from modern Colombia to
central Chile. [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Sabine MacCormack, Ch 24) analyzes it
through the **fusion of state and religion**: the Inca built and legitimated their
empire as much through creation myths, the Sun cult, and the ritual incorporation of
their subjects' religions as through war, roads, and administration.

## Formation: Pachacuti and the Chanca War

Inca origins, as preserved in the sixteenth-century Spanish *crónicas* drawn from Inca
informants, center on a gradual expansion around **Cuzco** under the first eight
rulers. The decisive turn came with the **Chanca war**: a great Chanca assault drove
the Inca Viracocha and his heir to flight, but his younger son **Pachacuti**
("Turning of the Time," r. 1438–1471) received in a vision the Creator's promise of
aid — the *pururaucas*, stones that rose up as soldiers — and defeated the Chancas.
MacCormack stresses the striking **contrast between the person-and-event–centered
historical narratives and the archaeological record** of gradual settlement change,
agricultural refinement, and resource accumulation: both are needed to understand
state formation. After his victory Pachacuti reconceived Inca rulership, claiming the
title **Capac** ("very much more than king") for himself alone and launching
Andean-wide conquest.

## State and Religion

MacCormack's central theme is that Inca rulers "deliberately portrayed themselves as
the founders and originators of civilization in the Andes, giving order to human
society just as the Creator had given order to the cosmos." The Inca claimed descent
as **"sons of the Sun" (Inti)**, anchoring the claim by bringing the Lake Titicaca
region and **[[tiwanaku|Tiahuanaco]]** — where local sacred history held the Sun had
first risen — under their control and weaving the Tiwanaku origin of the Sun into
their own myth of emergence from Pacaritambo. Imperial cult, the network of *huacas*
(sacred markers), and the participation of Inca rulers in the religions of their
subjects bound the empire together.

## Administration and Expansion

The Inca expanded through a flexible repertoire: **marriage alliances** with
neighboring lords, **wars** of plunder and incorporation, **diplomacy and
negotiation**, and the projection of administration into the **four suyos** (the
quarters of Tawantinsuyu) from Cuzco. The state was knit by an extraordinary system of
**roads with way stations (*tambos*)**, by resettlement (*mitmaq*) policies, by labor
tribute (*mit'a*), and by record-keeping on knotted-cord **[[khipu|khipus]]** (still
only partly deciphered). Notably, the Inca economy operated **without markets or money**
over most of its territory, organizing exchange through state redistribution and
reciprocal labor obligation rather than commerce.

## Significance

The Inca Empire is the volume's prime case that **the largest, most integrated state
of the western hemisphere arose entirely independently** of the Old World, on
distinctive principles (reciprocity, redistribution, vertical ecological control)
rather than the market-and-coin logic of Afro-Eurasia — a reminder, with the
[[aztec-empire|Aztecs]], that the Americas constituted a parallel world of state
formation, "worlds apart" (Olstein) from Eurasia until the Spanish arrival of 1532.

## The Conquest and the Columbian Exchange (CWH VI)

As with the [[aztec-empire|Aztecs]], the Inca conquest is inseparable from the
[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]]. Noble David Cook (CWH VI) notes
that **smallpox, racing ahead of the Spanish down Indigenous trade networks**,
killed the Inca **Sapa Inca Huayna Capac** (and his designated heir) around
1527 — setting off the **fratricidal succession war** between Huáscar and
Atahualpa that left the empire fractured and "led to the ease of Spanish
conquest" when Pizarro's tiny band arrived in 1532. Disease, dynastic civil war,
and the burdens of [[early-modern-migrations|mita labor]] in the silver mines
that followed (the deadly **Potosí mita** from 1574) compounded the Andean
demographic collapse charted at [[pre-columbian-american-population]].
