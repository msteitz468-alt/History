# Hopi

Western Pueblo people of the northern plateau of what is now northern Arizona. Uto-Aztecan speakers; culturally similar neighbors of Zuni but linguistically distinct. Living descendants of western [[ancestral-pueblo|Ancestral Pueblo]] populations. Primary colonial synthesis: Lamar & Truett, [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-2|CHNPA I.2]] ch. 10.

## Overview

Coronado’s party met independent Western Pueblos as well as Rio Grande villages. Hopi language belongs to the widely distributed **Uto-Aztecan** family; Zuni is framed as perhaps related to Californian Penutian. Hopis practiced the broader Pueblo pattern of intensive religion, agriculture, and craft production within mesa villages remote from the Rio Grande colonial core.

## Spanish contact without submission

One of [[coronado-expedition-1540|Coronado]]’s lieutenants successfully **attacked the Hopis and forced surrender in 1540**—yet the remote Hopis **never really submitted** to the Spanish. Sustained Spanish relations west of New Mexico lagged a century after Rio Grande colonization; aside from early mission attempts on the northern plateau, Arizona colonization began with Father Eusebio Kino among Pima (O’Odham) of northern Sonora in **1687** (Pimería Alta).

Western Pueblos differed sharply from the Rio Grande experience:

- **Zuni:** no missionaries until 1629; mission abandoned by 1821 due to [[navajo-dine|Navajo]] raids
- **Hopi:** resisted missionaries to the point that they **may well have killed off the few pro-missionary Hopis** among them so there would never be a call for a missionary; rejected **Anza’s 1780s** drought food-for-allegiance offer; **held off the Spanish, the Mexicans, and the Americans until the 1880s** despite internal divisions

(`contrasts_with:` Rio Grande [[pueblo-peoples]] under continuous colonial pressure after 1598)

## U.S. period and later politics

Hopi autonomy into the 1880s sits at the end of the [[greater-southwest-california-colonial-1880s]] process endpoint—cultures persisting in villages as the free-ranging “frontier” closed. Thereafter Hopi and Navajo began a **century-long struggle over shared grazing and hunting territory** even as both avoided major land cessions typical of Plains allotment.

Under the [[indian-reorganization-act-1934|IRA (1934)]], BIA constitutions with anthropological advice allowed for preexisting structures (e.g., **Hopi village religious leaders**), but Collier’s tribal-unity model **subsumed autonomous village, band, and clan units** under federally recognized “tribal” identity—generating legitimacy challenges among Hopis (and Sioux). 1970s anthropologist advocacy engaged the **Hopi–Navajo relocation/boundary dispute** alongside other pan-Indian causes. Washburn notes pan-Indian gloss can encompass historic enemies including Hopi–Navajo.

## Counterfactual significance

Hopi geographic remoteness and deliberate elimination of pro-mission factions help explain why Western Pueblo ceremonial and political autonomy survived Spanish and Mexican centuries that remade the Rio Grande. Without that non-submission, the comparative case that structures Lamar & Truett’s Western vs Eastern Pueblo contrast collapses.

## Historiography

Lamar & Truett treat Hopi primarily through the resistance narrative (1540 attack; mission rejection; Anza refusal; holdout to 1880s). Precontact Western Pueblo archaeology and katcina ceremonialism debates sit on [[ancestral-pueblo]] and Cordell & Smith. IRA village-vs-tribe tensions appear in Washburn’s renaissance/self-determination chapters (CHNPA I.2). Spicer *Cycles of Conquest* remains the synthetic backbone for Spanish–Mexican–U.S. impact frames.
