## Overview

The **Natchez** were a Late Mississippian chiefdom people of the lower Mississippi River near present-day Natchez, Mississippi. Bruce D. Smith (CHNPA I.1, ch. 5) notes that this chiefdom **survived through the seventeenth century and well into the eighteenth**, to be described in detail by Antoine Simon Le Page du Pratz as the “Natchez” — one of the last archaeologically and ethnohistorically accessible Mississippian-type polities after most southeastern chiefdom-level organizations had unraveled under European disease and domination.

## Archaeology

Archaeological data on the **Grand Village of the Natchez (Fatherland Site)** and outlying dispersed “villages” complement written accounts but also underscore difficulties of detailed archaeological inference during periods of considerable social transformation (Smith). The settlement pattern — a principal ceremonial/political center with dispersed supporting communities — fits the broader Mississippian farmstead-and-mound-center model rather than dense urban nucleation of the Cahokia type.

## Social Organization (ethnohistoric)

French accounts (including Le Page du Pratz) describe a ranked society with strata often rendered as Sun, Noble, Honored, and Stinkard/commoner; strictly matrilineal descent; the paramount chief (the **Great Sun**) succeeded by his sister’s son to maintain the ruling matrilineage. Secondary Mississippian surveys treat this as a living illustration of ranked-clan chiefdom structure of the kind Smith reconstructs archaeologically for earlier polities. Classification as complex [[chiefdom]] rather than state follows the general Mississippian framing; see [[north-american-states-debate]] and [[chiefdom]].

## Contact-Period Trajectory

Smith places the Natchez among Late Mississippian lower Mississippi survivors south of the pre-contact **Vacant Quarter**, after interior mound centers had already collapsed. Sixteenth-century Spanish entradas disrupted neighboring polities (e.g. Nodena/Pacaha and related groups north of the Natchez zone); the Natchez themselves enter the documentary record most fully under French Louisiana.

Colonial-era claims from CHNPA I.1 later chapters (Salisbury and related contact narrative):

- French establishment of Biloxi (1699) and Mobile (1701) brought contact with Choctaws, Chickasaws, Natchez, and others.
- **Fort Rosalie (1716)** bitterly split the Natchez: one faction welcomed the post; another moved closer to the Chickasaws in opposition.
- **Natchez uprising 1729**: after French demanded village removal for expanded tobacco production, the Natchez rose, initially driving French from Fort Rosalie, amid New Orleans slave-rebellion context and Chickasaw–English overtures to the Choctaws. French resecured Choctaw support; Choctaws played the principal role in suppressing the Natchez in **1730**. Survivors were killed, enslaved, or dispersed (often among Chickasaws and other neighbors); as a sovereign Mississippian-descended chiefdom the Natchez polity ended.

## Ceremonial Continuity

Regional variants of [[southeastern-ceremonial-complex]] symbolism and mound-centered ritual are inferred to have persisted among lower Mississippi groups into the French period; Natchez ethnohistory is a primary window on surviving southeastern elite ceremonialism (temple/mound cult, ranked office, solar symbolism) after most interior SECC networks had dissolved.

## Historiography

Smith’s ch. 5 treatment is brief — a terminal Late Mississippian survival note pointing to Le Page du Pratz and the Fatherland Site. Fuller social description depends on French colonial ethnography, which is rich but filtered through colonial categories and factional politics of the 1710s–30s. Archaeological–ethnohistoric matching during rapid social transformation is difficult (Smith’s explicit caveat). Post-contact political history is better covered in colonial-encounter chapters of the same volume than in the agricultural-chiefdoms chapter.
