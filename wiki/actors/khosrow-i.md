# Khosrow I Anushirvan

## Overview

**Khosrow I** ("Anūšīrvān," *of the immortal soul*; r. 531–579 CE) was the great
**reforming king** of the [[sasanian-empire|Sasanian Empire]] and, in
[[sources/wiesehofer-ancient-persia-2001|Wiesehöfer]]'s account, the ruler under whom the
empire's long-deferred **centralization** finally crystallized. He suppressed the
[[#The Mazdakite movement|Mazdakite]] upheaval, overhauled taxation and the army,
fostered a new service nobility (the *dehqāns*), fixed the official idea of
[[eranshahr|Iran]] in writing, and presided over a celebrated cultural flowering — while
fighting and negotiating with Justinian's [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]].

## Major Reforms

- **Taxation.** He completed a fiscal reform (begun under his father Kavad) replacing
  variable crop-share levies with a more predictable land-and-poll tax — the basis of the
  state's revenue and, later, a model for the early Islamic *kharāj*.
- **The four-spahbed army reform.** Khosrow "replaced the hitherto single *spāhbed* with
  four officers of this title, each entrusted with the military command of a quarter of
  the empire," breaking the old supreme command into regional quarters and curbing the
  danger of an over-mighty generalissimo (a "generalissimo" rank had been created by
  Mihr-Narseh early in the 5th century). Beneath them stood *pāygōspānān* (provincial
  commanders) and *marzbānān* (border-march commanders).
- **A recruited cavalry and the dehqāns.** He created a stratum of paid "cavaliers"
  (Arab. *al-asāwira*) who, "if they had no fortune, were provided with a horse, equipment
  and money," with detailed muster and pay records; border troops received "soldier's
  fiefs." The reforms produced a dependent **service nobility** at court — the lower landed
  gentry (*dehqāns*) who would survive the empire and become the backbone of rural Iran
  under Islam.

## The Mazdakite movement

After the catastrophic Hephthalite wars of King Peroz, a peasant rising — "a section of
the peasant population had risen against the aristocracy and forcibly taken possession of
their property and women" — drew on the social-religious teaching of **Mazdak**. King
**Kavad** temporarily backed Mazdak to weaken the great nobles; Khosrow, by contrast,
"used the chaos to reform the state to his own benefit," crushing the movement and allying
with the aristocracy and Zoroastrian clergy. Wiesehöfer cautions that **all surviving
sources are hostile** (their authors "were Khosrow I and the Zoroastrian authorities, who
emerged as victors"); the *Dēnkard* preserves the least-mediated account of Mazdakite
doctrine (communal sharing of food, forms of communal kinship). Mazdakism survived
underground into the early Islamic period.

## Foreign Policy and Economy

- **Byzantium.** The **562 CE** treaty with Justinian regulated frontier trade to three
  designated cities (Nisibis, Callinicum, Artaxata) and the supervision of Arab merchants;
  Byzantium meanwhile worked to bypass the Persian transit monopoly through sericulture,
  Axum, and contacts with the Türks.
- **Yemen.** He dispatched an expeditionary force under **Vahrīz** that dislodged the
  Ethiopian (Byzantine-allied) garrison and installed a vassal at Sanaa — "Persian
  supremacy there did not end until Muhammad's last years."

## Cultural Patronage

Khosrow's reign was remembered as a golden age of learning. He sponsored translations of
Greek philosophy into Persian and **received the Neoplatonist philosophers expelled from
[[justinian|Athens]]** after the closing of the Academy (529 CE), negotiating their safe
return in his peace talks with Byzantium; Agathias (sceptically) reports him versed in
Aristotle and Plato. He commissioned the rendering of the Sanskrit *Pañcatantra* (via the
physician Burzoy) that became the Arabic *Kalīla wa-Dimna*, and under him the
**Xwadāy-nāmag** received its first authoritative version (see [[eranshahr]]) and the
*mōbadān mōbad* Weh-Šābuhr published the 21 *nasks* of the Avesta.

## Sarris: the Persian half of a parallel reform

**Peter Sarris** ([[sarris-empires-of-faith-2011|*Empires of Faith*]], 2011) reads
Khusro's reforms as the **mirror image of [[justinian|Justinian]]'s** — two empires
responding to the same superpower warfare by extracting themselves from aristocratic
dominance. Where the early Sasanian shahs had leaned on the armed retinues of the Iranian
nobility, Khusro taxed his subjects more aggressively to fund a properly imperial,
salaried army, provoking the wrath of both Mazdakite radicals and the great nobles. The
result, visible in the numismatic record, was "a period of rapid fiscal, monetary, and
bureaucratic expansion very similar to that which had taken place in the Roman Empire in
the age of [[diocletian|Diocletian]] and [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]," drawing a new
service elite from the urbanised civic notables of **Mesopotamia (Iraq)** — many of them
Christian. Sarris also stresses what bounded Khusro's western ambitions: the **Eurasian
steppe**. His climbdown from Lazica in 561–2, despite sixteen years of investment, is
explained by the rise of the **Turk Khaganate** on his north-eastern flank — the same
nomad pressure that would later doom his grandson Khusro II against
[[heraclius|Heraclius]].

## Counterfactual Significance

Wiesehöfer treats Khosrow's reforms as the decisive turn toward a centralized Sasanian
state — the institutional peak from which the empire fought Byzantium to a standstill. Yet
the same reforms concentrated power in ways that left the realm vulnerable to dynastic
turmoil after Khosrow II, and the heavy cavalry his system perfected would prove unable to
counter the mobile Arab armies a few decades later (see [[sasanian-empire]]).

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Attributed claims from [[sources/mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015|Stephen Mitchell, *A History of the Later Roman Empire* (2015)]].

- **“Sassanian Justinian”: tax/army reform and peer ideology.** Sixth-century Persian power rose above all under Khusro I (Khusro Anurshivan, 531–579). Procopius presented him as a Sassanian counterpart to [[justinian|Justinian]], “an extraordinary lover of revolutionary innovations”; Middle Persian/Arabic sources indicate comprehensive land and poll-tax reorganization and a more centralized elite cavalry from the nobility. Diplomatic ideology cast the two empires as “two eyes/lights of the world” (Peter the Patrician; letter of Khusro II to Maurice). Forces were closely matched in Mesopotamian campaigns. Related: [[sasanian-empire|Sasanian Empire]]; [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]].
- **540 invasion: Antioch sacked; protection-money peace logic.** The “endless peace” of 532 lasted only to 539; Procopius blames Khusro. Spring 540 exposed hopeless Roman city-by-city defense: Sura massacred; Beroea fell; Antioch stormed after troops fled to Daphne — many killed, survivors deported to a new city near Ctesiphon (only fourteen years after the 526 earthquake). Khusro demanded 5,000 lb gold indemnity + 500 lb/year, framed as hiring Persians as mercenaries (like Hun/Saracen subsidies), not tributary status. Belisarius returned 541; plague, not Roman strength, deterred the 542 advance toward Palestine — “they had not been deterred by the Romans but by a far more devastating enemy, the plague.” Related: [[justinianic-plague-541|Justinianic Plague]].
