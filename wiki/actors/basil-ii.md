# Basil II "the Bulgar-Slayer"

**Basil II** (958–1025; sole emperor from 976) brought the [[macedonian-dynasty|Macedonian]] Byzantine empire to its medieval apogee, extending its frontiers to the Danube, the Euphrates, and the Caucasus and ruling for nearly half a century. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Shepard) places his reign at the summit of "Byzantium expanding."

## Survival and the Aristocratic Revolts

Basil's early reign was consumed by civil war against the great Anatolian **military magnates**: the rebellions of **Bardas Skleros** and then **Bardas Phokas** (who had himself proclaimed emperor) threatened to topple him. The crisis was resolved by a momentous foreign alliance: Basil secured the military aid of **Vladimir of [[kievan-rus|Kiev]]** — the famous **Varangian Guard** — in exchange for the unprecedented marriage of his sister, the porphyrogenita **Anna**, to Vladimir, which precipitated the **[[conversion-of-vladimir-988|conversion of Rus′ (988)]]**. Victorious, Basil turned on the *dynatoi*: he issued severe novels against "the powerful," confiscated over-mighty estates, and — distrustful of the aristocracy that had nearly destroyed him — ruled as a famously austere, unmarried, soldier-emperor.

## The Bulgar-Slayer

Basil's defining achievement was the destruction of the **[[first-bulgarian-empire|First Bulgarian Empire]]** of **Samuel** in a grinding, decades-long war. After an early disaster (Trajan's Gate, 986) he ground down Bulgarian power; the decisive victory came at the **Battle of Kleidion (1014)**, after which — in the act that gave him his epithet ***Boulgaroktonos* ("Bulgar-Slayer")** — he reportedly blinded some 14,000 prisoners, leaving one in a hundred one-eyed to lead the rest home. Bulgaria was **annexed in 1018** (see [[conquest-of-bulgaria-1018]]), the Balkan frontier restored to the Danube for the first time since the seventh century.

## The Empire at Its Height

By Basil's death in 1025 the empire stretched from southern Italy and the Danube to Syria and Armenia (whose annexation he negotiated), with a full treasury and a professional army. He died planning a reconquest of Arab **Sicily**. NCMH III stresses, however, that his settlement was fragile: his suppression of the military aristocracy and his personal, heirless style left no durable institutional framework, and the empire unravelled rapidly under his weak successors — the road to **Manzikert (1071)** and the eleventh-century crisis.
