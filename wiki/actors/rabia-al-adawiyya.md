# Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya

Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya (c. 714–c. 801) is the pre-eminent woman saint of early [[sufism|Sufism]] and the
figure credited with introducing **selfless divine love** (*maḥabba*) as the animating core of the
mystical tradition. She transformed what had been an asceticism oriented principally toward fear of
judgment and hope of paradise into a mysticism of pure, disinterested love — love of God for His
own sake, with no admixture of desire for reward or fear of punishment.

The classical biographer ʿAṭṭār called her "that one set apart in the seclusion of holiness, that
woman veiled with the veil of sincerity, that one enflamed by love and longing… lost in union with
God, that one accepted by men as a second spotless Mary" (T 159).

## Life

Rābiʿa was born in Basra and was, by tradition, a slave girl who was set free by her master. The
biographical sources are largely hagiographic, and reliable details are few. She remained celibate
throughout her life — an unusual choice that later commentators connected to the overwhelming
exclusivity of her love for God, which left no room for any human attachment. She died around 801 CE
in Basra, a few years after her contemporary Shaqīq al-Balkhī, the Khurasanian ascetic who had also
moved toward speaking of "the light of pure love of God" (Schimmel, citing W 228).

## The Central Teaching: Selfless Love

Rābiʿa's most celebrated act of teaching is the image of the torch and the ewer. Once, in the
streets of Basra, she was seen carrying a torch in one hand and a ewer in the other; asked why, she
answered: "I want to throw fire into Paradise and pour water into Hell so that these two veils
disappear, and it becomes clear who worships God out of love, not out of fear of Hell or hope for
Paradise." (T 1:73)

This saying crystallizes the Sufi doctrine — elaborated by all subsequent mystical poets — that the
true lover must be entirely free of ulterior motive:

> "The lover must be in the way of love so that he does not remember Hell or Paradise." (N 597, via Schimmel)

Rābiʿa distinguished the Love of God from legal obedience and ascetic self-denial: "A few houris
and castles" promised in Paradise are merely veils hiding the eternal divine beauty. The ascetic
who seeks paradise remains, like one who fears hell, at a remove from God.

Jāmī articulated the distinction that Rābiʿa embodied:

> "The ascetics regard the Beauty of the Otherworld with the light of faith and certitude and
> despise the world, but are still veiled by a sensual pleasure, namely the thought of Paradise,
> whereas the true Sufi is veiled from both worlds by the vision of the Primordial Beauty and
> Essential Love." (N 10)

## The Theology of Love

Rābiʿa meditated on the Quranic passage "He loves them and they love Him" (Sūra 5:59) — the
Koranic foundation for the doctrine of mutual love between Creator and creature. She understood
God's love as preceding and undergirding human love: "Love has come from Eternity and passes into
Eternity and none has been found in seventy thousand worlds who drinks one drop of it until at last
he is absorbed in God." (T 1:67)

She also introduced into Sufism the image of the **jealous God** — not the orthodox jealousy that
tolerates no worship of other beings, but a jealousy that "will suffer none to share with Him that
love which is due to Him alone." This is a God who demands the whole of the heart, not merely
correct ritual performance.

Her night prayers show a love that persists even if rejected:

> "O God, the night has passed and the day has dawned. How I long to know if Thou hast accepted
> my prayers or if Thou hast rejected them. Therefore console me for it is Thine to console this
> state of mine. Thou hast given me life and cared for me, and Thine is the glory. If Thou want to
> drive me from Thy door, yet would I not forsake it, for the love that I bear in my heart towards
> Thee." (T 1:73)

## Significance and Influence

Rābiʿa occupies a unique position in the history of [[sufism|Sufism]]. Schimmel describes her as
"generally regarded as the person who introduced the element of selfless love into the austere
teachings of the early ascetics and gave Sufism the hue of true mysticism." Before her, the
dominant tone was that of fear, sadness (*ḥuzn*), and weeping — the mode of [[hasan-al-basri|Ḥasan
al-Baṣrī]] and the Basran ascetics. She turned the orientation toward love without displacing the
rigor.

Later mystical poets in Persian, Turkish, and Urdu extensively cited and elaborated her teachings.
Rūmī retold her story in the *Mathnawī* (M 4:1357), rendering her refusal to look at the spring
flowers — preferring to contemplate He who created them — into one of the canonical statements of
mystical interiority. Her principle was: "The gardens and the fruits are inside, in the heart."

She was accepted as a model even by those who otherwise dismissed women's spiritual authority, on
the grounds that "in the unity of God the lovers no longer have a separate existence, no distinction
can be made between man and woman" (T 1:59).

## In Western Scholarship

Rābiʿa was the first major figure from Sufi history to enter European consciousness. She was
introduced to European literature by Joinville, chancellor of Louis IX, in the late thirteenth
century, and her story was used in a seventeenth-century French treatise on pure love. Margaret
Smith produced the foundational modern study of her life and teaching. She remains one of the most
widely discussed figures in the [[sufism-historiography|Western study of Sufism]]. See also
[[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975|Schimmel (1975)]].
