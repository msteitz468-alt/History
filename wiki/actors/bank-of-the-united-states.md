# (First) Bank of the United States

Federally chartered national bank proposed by Treasury Secretary [[hamilton-alexander|Alexander Hamilton]] and signed into law by President [[washington-george|George Washington]] in 1791, modeled partly on the Bank of England. It served as a depository for federal funds, issued a stable national currency, extended credit to the government and commerce, and was central to Hamilton's broader financial program of assumption of state debts and funding of the national debt at par.

## Role

The Bank provoked one of the young republic's sharpest constitutional disputes: [[jefferson-thomas|Jefferson]] and [[madison-james|Madison]] argued a national bank exceeded Congress's enumerated powers, while Hamilton defended it under the "necessary and proper" clause and a doctrine of implied powers that Washington ultimately accepted. The dispute helped crystallize the emerging Federalist–Democratic-Republican partisan divide.

## Counterfactual Significance

The Bank's twenty-year charter was allowed to lapse in 1811 amid continued opposition, but its constitutional reasoning (implied powers) and its practical success in stabilizing public credit set precedents invoked in the later, more consequential fight over the Second Bank of the United States and in broader debates over federal economic power.
