## Overview

Fujiwara no Yorimichi (992–1074) was the eldest son of [[fujiwara-michinaga|Fujiwara no Michinaga]] and the last regent in whom the [[fujiwara-regency|Fujiwara Regency]] exercised genuine political dominance. He served as regent for over five decades (1020–1068), outlasting by many years the conditions that had made the regency powerful. His long tenure has often been characterized primarily as a decline from his father's apogee, but the CHJ argues for a more nuanced assessment: Yorimichi "was not, apparently, the inconsequential figure that historians have sometimes painted him" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 72).

## Failure of Marriage Politics and Political Decline

The structural vulnerability of the [[sekkan-system|sekkan system]] was its dependence on placing Fujiwara daughters as imperial consorts. Yorimichi failed to achieve this consistently. When Emperor Go-Reizei abdicated in 1045 without a Fujiwara-born son as heir, the former emperor was able to install as crown prince a son born of an imperial princess. The resulting accession of Go-Sanjō (r. 1068–1073) — the first emperor in 170 years whose mother was not a Fujiwara — marked the end of the regency's structural basis. Neither Yorimichi nor his brother Norimichi, who succeeded him as regent in 1068, was "an able enough politician to maintain the position of the Northern House without the support of blood ties to the emperor" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 72).

## The Fiscal Reform of the 1040s

In the 1040s, probably under Yorimichi's leadership, the court enacted an important reform of the land and tax system in response to provincial unrest. Cultivators and landholders in provinces near Kyoto had been in open conflict with provincial governors since the late tenth century, in some cases abandoning tillage in the public realm or commending holdings to Kyoto patrons as privately established shoen to escape heavy taxation.

The chief features of the reform:
1. **Fixed uniform tax rates** not subject to change by provincial administrators (set at 6 *soku* per *tan*)
2. **Formal recognition of existing shoen**: shoen established with tax immunities by provincial authority before a cutoff date were officially recognized, representing a "retreat from the court's long-standing effort to restrict shoen to those officially established before 902" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 73)
3. **Return to public realm** of the most recently and informally established shoen

The reform also initiated the transformation of the statutory subprovincial district (*gun*) system, leading eventually to the direct subordination of lesser land units to provincial governors' offices. Whether it actually changed total revenue flows is unknown, but it apparently stabilized conditions in the near-capital provinces.

## The Byōdōin

In 1052 Yorimichi converted his Uji villa into the Byōdōin Buddhist temple. The surviving Phoenix Hall (*Hōōdō*) remains one of the masterpieces of Heian architecture and the image on modern Japanese 10-yen coins. Like his father's Hōjōji, the Byōdōin expressed the Amidist piety of the late Fujiwara period, though it was built on a smaller scale and survived where the Hōjōji did not.

The Byōdōin's founding encapsulates the late regency's paradox: Yorimichi commanded the resources to build one of the century's great temples but not the political skill to maintain his family's constitutional dominance.

## Household Administration

By Yorimichi's day the regent's household administrative office had grown to at least twenty court-ranked officials holding a wide variety of specialized titles. From his time onward, the number of provincial administrators on the household staff decreased and posts tended to become hereditary — reflecting both the expanded role of shoen in the regental economy and the loss of regental control over provincial appointments that occurred under Go-Sanjō.

## Counterfactual Significance

Yorimichi's long regency illustrates the extent to which the sekkan system was person-dependent: the same structural position, the same clan, the same inherited shoen holdings — but without his father's marriage-politics success or political skill, the system atrophied. A more effective Yorimichi who had managed to marry daughters into the imperial succession would likely have extended the regency period considerably, delaying or preventing the rise of [[insei]] as the dominant power mechanism.
