# Robert F. Kennedy

**Summary page.** Robert Francis Kennedy (1925–1968) was U.S. Attorney General (1961–1964), U.S. Senator from New York (1965–1968), and a leading figure in the Kennedy family political dynasty. This page summarizes his role as both ally and antagonist to Lyndon B. Johnson.

## Overview

Younger brother of President John F. Kennedy. Rose to national prominence as JFK's campaign manager (1960) and Attorney General. His relationship with Lyndon Johnson began with visceral mutual hostility during the 1960 convention (RFK's attempt to remove LBJ from the ticket) and intensified during the vice presidency, when White House staff (led in derision by RFK allies) mocked Johnson as "Rufus Cornpone" or "Uncle Cornpone." After the Dallas assassination, positions reversed dramatically: Johnson became President with power over the Kennedy circle; RFK viewed the transition with deep resentment and was widely seen as a potential future challenger.

## Key Developments (relevant to LBJ)

- 1953 Senate cafeteria encounter with Majority Leader Johnson (early hostility noted by witnesses).
- 1960 Democratic Convention: RFK's aggressive efforts to persuade LBJ to withdraw from the VP slot; lasting bitterness on both sides.
- As Attorney General: centralized power in Justice Department; pursued civil rights cases and organized crime (Hoffa, etc.). Johnson perceived slights and exclusion (e.g., from key ExComm decisions during Cuban Missile Crisis).
- Vice Presidency years: enabled or participated in slights that reinforced Johnson's powerlessness and humiliation.
- Dallas and transition: RFK's grief and anger at the sudden reversal; early clashes with the new President over continuity, staff, and the "Kennedy legacy."
- Later: resigned as AG 1964, elected Senator from New York; 1968 run for presidency ended by assassination.

## Significance and Links

Central to understanding the personal dimension of the Kennedy-Johnson relationship and the difficulties of the 1963–1964 transition. The feud is a major thread in Caro, *The Passage of Power*.
