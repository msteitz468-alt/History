# Akbar

**Akbar** (r. 1556–1605) was the true architect of the **[[mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]]** and, for
Percival Spear, one of the **two Indian statesmen of world rank before the twentieth century** —
alongside [[ashoka|Ashoka]]. Documented for the wiki through
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965).

## From boy-king to emperor

Born during his father Humayun's exile, Akbar succeeded at thirteen under the guardianship of the able
**Bairam Khan**, who secured the throne at the **second battle of Panipat (1556)** against the Hindu
general Hemu. Taking personal control in 1560, Akbar proved an outstanding general with a "Napoleonic
gift of rapid movement" (his 600-mile dash to crush the Gujarat revolt in 1573), conquering Gujarat,
Bengal, Kashmir, Orissa, Sind, and part of the Deccan — "the richest and most vigorous as well as the
largest territorial share of India."

## The four achievements

Spear credits Akbar with raising himself "from a leader of a minority Indo-foreign group to the
accepted ruler of all Hindustan" through four works:
1. **Territorial empire** — a broad, waterway- and highway-knit realm from Bengal to Kandahar.
2. **The Rajput / Hindu partnership** — after breaking [[rajputs|Rajput]] power at Chitor and
   Ranthambor (1568–69), he co-opted the Rajput chiefs as governors, generals, and privy councillors
   (Raja Man Singh), married a Jaipur princess (his heir half-Rajput), granted them honours and
   autonomy, and **abolished the *jizya*** (the poll-tax on non-Muslims) — so that "through them the
   whole Hindu community came to accept the Mughal government as in some sense their own."
3. **The mansabdar service** — a graded, cash-paid, non-hereditary imperial bureaucracy/officer corps
   (the "steel frame"), a career open to talent that channelled ambitious energy into the state.
4. **Imperial sanctity** — he "re-grew the divinity that doth hedge a king," adopting the nimbus in
   Mughal painting and the *sijdah* prostration.

## The Din-i-Ilahi

Akbar's famous **"Divine Faith" (Din-i-Ilahi)** — an eclectic court cult (c. 1580) drawing on
Zoroastrian, Hindu, Jain, and Christian as well as Islamic ideas, after years of religious discussion
(into which he summoned Jesuits from Goa) and a personal mystical turn — Spear reads **politically**:
not the eccentricity of old age (it was proclaimed at forty, in full vigour) but a deliberate **cult
of the monarch** as a semi-divine figure whom it was a religious duty to obey, providing a basis of
loyalty transcending the Hindu–Muslim divide. The cult never spread beyond a small circle (the
philosopher Abul Fazl, the poet Faizi, Raja Birbal) and lapsed at his death, but it deeply **offended
the orthodox ulema** — sowing the reaction (Sirhindi, later Aurangzeb) that would eventually pull the
other way.

## Significance

Akbar gave India its first Muslim dynasty to win the **free allegiance of Hindus as well as Muslims**;
the loyalty he created outlasted Mughal power by a century (a factor in the [[indian-rebellion-1857|1857
Rebellion]]). Formally illiterate but of "prodigious memory and keen intellect," humane and tolerant
beyond his age, he is, in Spear's estimate, the rare conqueror whose **creative leadership** transformed
a situation rather than merely dominating it.
