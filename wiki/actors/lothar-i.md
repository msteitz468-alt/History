# Lothar I (emperor 840–855)

## Overview

**Lothar I** (795–855), eldest son of [[louis-the-pious]], was the chief beneficiary and chief
casualty of the struggle over Carolingian unity. Made co-emperor in the **Ordinatio imperii** of
817, he spent two decades defending the principle of indivisible empire under his own primacy — and
lost it at the [[battle-of-fontenoy-841]], salvaging at the [[treaty-of-verdun-843]] only the imperial
title and a long, narrow **Middle Kingdom** stretching from Frisia through Lotharingia and Burgundy to
Italy. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Nelson, Ch. 4; Fried, Ch. 5) he personifies the
doomed imperial ideal.

## Co-Emperor and Rebel

The 817 Ordinatio made Lothar co-emperor with supremacy over his brothers — and made him the natural
leader of the faction that, after the birth of [[charles-the-bald|Charles the Bald]] in 823 threatened
to dilute his inheritance, repeatedly rebelled against their father. Lothar was central to the revolts
of 830 and 833 (the "Field of Lies"), but was twice pushed back toward Italy by his brothers.

## Fontenoy and Verdun

On Louis the Pious's death (840) Lothar tried to make good a unitary, empire-wide claim on the 817
model. His brothers **[[louis-the-german]]** and **[[charles-the-bald]]** allied against him, swearing
mutual fidelity in the **Strasbourg Oaths** of February 842 (recorded by Nithard in early Romance and
Old High German — a landmark of vernacular language history). After his defeat at **Fontenoy** (25 June
841), a year of hard diplomacy produced the **Treaty of Verdun** (843): Lothar kept the imperial title
and the Middle Kingdom, but the unitary empire was abandoned. He continued to assert his rights as
family head and protector of the empire-wide church until his abdication and death in 855, when the
Middle Kingdom was itself divided among his three sons (Louis II getting Italy and the empire, Lothar II
giving his name to **Lotharingia**, Charles getting Provence).

## Significance

Lothar's career is the measure of how completely the Frankish aristocracy and partible custom defeated
the ideology of imperial unity. The fragmentation of his Middle Kingdom — a politically incoherent strip
with no natural cohesion — created the long-contested borderland (Lotharingia/Lorraine) between the
emerging French and German worlds.

## Historiography

NCMH II reads the wars of 840–843 not as dynastic squabbling but as the structural collision of the 817
unity-principle with Frankish inheritance norms and aristocratic self-interest. Nithard — a participant,
illegitimate Carolingian, and partisan of Charles the Bald — is the indispensable but biased source for
Lothar's defeat.
