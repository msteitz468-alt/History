# Maximus the Confessor

One of the **greatest theologians of the Greek Church** (c. 580–662) and the leading champion of
**dyotheletism** (two wills in Christ) against the imperial doctrine of Monotheletism — for which he was
mutilated and died in exile, earning the title "the Confessor."

## Life

Of a Constantinopolitan (or, in another tradition, Palestinian) background, Maximus served briefly in
the imperial administration before becoming a monk. Fleeing the Persian and then Arab advance, he moved
through Asia Minor to **North Africa**, where he emerged as the intellectual leader of opposition to the
**Monothelete** compromise promoted by **[[heraclius|Heraclius]]** and his successors. He helped shape
the condemnation of Monotheletism at the **[[booth-cubitt-lateran-synod-649|Lateran Synod of 649]]** under
**Pope Martin I**.

For this defiance of imperial religious policy **Constans II** had him arrested and tried at
Constantinople; refusing to recant, he was, according to tradition, **mutilated** (his tongue and right
hand cut off, to silence his speech and writing) and exiled to **Lazica** in the Caucasus, where he died
in **662**. The doctrine he defended triumphed at the **Sixth Ecumenical Council (680–681)**.

## Thought

Maximus was a profound and systematic thinker who synthesized the Greek patristic, Neoplatonic and
ascetic traditions. His dyothelete Christology — that Christ's full humanity entails a genuine **human
will**, freely conformed to the divine — was grounded in a larger vision of creation, the cosmic role of
Christ (the *Logos* and the *logoi* of things), human freedom, and *theosis* (deification). His
*Ambigua*, *Mystagogia* and ascetic works made him one of the most influential of all **Byzantine
theologians**, deeply shaping later Eastern Orthodox thought and respected in the Latin West.
