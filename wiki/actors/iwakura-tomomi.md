# Iwakura Tomomi

Court noble (*kuge*) who was the principal architect of the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji Restoration]] on the imperial side — organizing the Satsuma-Chōshū-court alliance, drafting (or inspiring) the imperial edicts that overthrew the [[tokugawa-shogunate]], and dominating the Restoration night meeting of January 4, 1868. He later led the Iwakura Mission (1871–1873), the great diplomatic tour of the West.

## Career

Iwakura first rose as a court official under Emperor Kōmei, but his involvement in the 1862 scheme to send the imperial princess Kazunomiya to Edo as the shogun's consort led to accusations of complicity with the shogunate. He was placed under house arrest in the Iwakura district north of Kyōto in 1862, where he remained — nominally — until the Restoration.

During his house arrest Iwakura continued to operate as a "born machinator" ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]): he received visitors from the Satsuma and Chōshū domains, maintained contact with sympathetic nobles, and developed the political framework for overturning the shogunate. In October 1867 he showed Ōkubo Toshimichi and Shinagawa Yajirō his design for the brocade pennant (*nishiki no mihata*) that would mark imperial forces as authorized defenders of the emperor — a weapon that proved psychologically decisive at Toba-Fushimi.

He drew up the secret imperial edict of November 9, 1867, ordering Satsuma and Chōshū to "slaughter the traitorous subject Yoshinobu" — an unusually violent document signed by three nobles rather than the emperor, which some historians have labeled a forgery or pseudoedict inspired by Iwakura and composed by his adviser Tamamatsu Misao. Iwakura maintained that it was shown to the emperor and approved before dispatch.

On January 4, 1868, the morning of the Restoration proclamation, Iwakura received his pardon from house arrest, appeared at court with his head still shaven (as required during penance), and delivered the Restoration proclamation to the assembled emperor and dignitaries. That evening, at the crucial meeting of nobles and daimyos, Iwakura dominated the proceedings — invoking the threat of violence (via Saigō's remark that "one short sword" would settle the debate), threatening to shed blood in the emperor's presence, and one by one turning even the strongest supporters of Yoshinobu. The meeting ended at midnight with unanimous agreement to strip Yoshinobu of rank and land.

In 1871–1873 Iwakura led the Iwakura Mission, the major diplomatic and fact-finding tour of the United States and Europe that shaped Meiji modernization policy.

## Role

Iwakura's contribution to the Restoration was organizational and rhetorical rather than military. He operated in the political space between the court and the domains, knitting together actors who had conflicting interests (Satsuma vs. Chōshū, nobles vs. samurai, conservatives vs. reformers) into a temporary coalition sufficient to overthrow the shogunate.

His methods — intrigue, manipulation, the assertion that the emperor approved of his actions regardless of whether this was true — made him feared and controversial. The Nobel laureate Nezu Masashi accused him of poisoning Emperor Kōmei (this remains unresolved and unproven). Other scholars argue that Iwakura sincerely respected Kōmei and was devastated by his sudden death.

Keene's assessment in [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]] emphasizes "remarkable diplomatic skill at playing off one man against another" in the Restoration night meeting, and notes the unanswerable question of whether the emperor in fact approved Iwakura's assertions to that effect.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Iwakura's management of the court side of the Restoration coalition — his ability to convert the nobles' diffuse anti-shogunate sentiment into specific political acts (the petition of the twenty-two nobles, the Restoration proclamation) — the Satsuma-Chōshū military alliance would have lacked imperial legitimacy. The brocade pennant that paralyzed the shogunate army at Toba-Fushimi was Iwakura's conceptual contribution. His role was irreplaceable in linking samurai military power to court ceremonial authority.
