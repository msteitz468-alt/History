## Overview

The Afghan Mujahideen were the array of Islamic resistance factions that fought the December 1979 Soviet invasion of Afghanistan and the Soviet-backed Kabul government, ultimately forcing the 1989 Soviet withdrawal. The term itself was a deliberate framing choice: a 1980 CIA memo recommended using "Mujahidin" rather than "insurgents," and President Carter's 1980 letter to Pakistani president Zia ul-Haq cast the resistance as serving both Islamic and Western democratic interests against Soviet aggression. In 1984 the Saudi Crown Prince likewise described the struggle explicitly as jihad, pledging continued Saudi support.

## Origins and Factions

Afghan Islamism predated the invasion: a first wave surfaced in the late 1950s–early 1960s among professors at Kabul University's Faculty of Theology (many al-Azhar graduates) and government madrasa graduates, forming Jamiyyat-i Islam, inspired by Pakistan's Jama'at-e Islami. After a failed 1975 communist coup attempt, the movement split. Burhanuddin Rabbani (1940–2011, al-Azhar graduate) sought to unify Muslim opposition through long-term infiltration of the state; Gulbuddin Hekmatyar (b. 1949) founded the more radical Hizb-i Islami (1976), favoring popular uprising. Both went into exile in Pakistan. At the invasion's outset Afghan society was fragmented among royalists, nationalist officers, Islamists, traditional ulama, and Sufi tariqa networks operating separately from one another; Islamic factions gradually took control of the resistance as the war progressed. Ahmad Shah Massoud (1953–2001) led a prominent traditionalist mujahidin faction, later opposed by the internationalist jihadi contingent aligned with the Taliban.

## Foreign Sponsorship

Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, and the United States jointly funded and armed the mujahideen. A 1980 US National Security Council memo detailed a covert plan to arm fighters via Afghan refugee camps on the Pakistan border while maintaining public deniability, envisioning US agents embedded "for refugee relief purposes" but principally to ensure weapons reached the Mujahidin. Roughly three million Afghan refugees crossed into Pakistan, and the border camps' proliferating madrasas — swelling from 900 registered Pakistani madrasas in 1971 to 8,000 by 1988 under Zia ul-Haq's Islamization policies — became a recruiting and training ground later associated with the [[taliban]].

## Internationalization

The Afghan jihad drew foreign volunteers and became a template for transnational jihadism, chiefly through [[abdallah-azzam|Abdallah 'Azzam]], who arrived in 1981 and provided the theoretical case that anti-Soviet resistance was an individual religious duty (fard-ul-'ayni) for all Muslims, and Osama bin Laden, who co-founded the Peshawar-based Services Bureau (Maktab al-Khidamat, 1984) with 'Azzam. This apparatus and its foreign-fighter networks became the nucleus of [[al-qaeda]].

## Counterfactual Significance

Without sustained US–Saudi–Pakistani sponsorship, the mujahideen's capacity to sustain a decade-long war against a superpower is doubtful; without 'Azzam's ideological framing of individual duty and the Peshawar infrastructure, the conflict likely remains a localized Afghan-Soviet war rather than the seedbed of transnational jihadist networks including al-Qaeda and elements later absorbed into the Taliban.

## Historiography

This page draws entirely on Chiara Formichi's *Islam and Asia: A History* (2020), Ch. 7, "Islam as Resistance," a chapter-length survey synthesis rather than an archival monograph. Formichi highlights the deliberate Cold War-era framing of the resistance as "Mujahidin" (not "insurgents") by US policymakers, and situates the mujahideen as the point of origin for later transnational jihadist organization — a genealogy she traces critically rather than through the post-9/11 "terrorism studies" literature she elsewhere questions for flattening ethno-nationalist and religious motives together.
