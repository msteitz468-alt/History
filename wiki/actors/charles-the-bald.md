# Charles the Bald (r. 843–877, emperor 875–877)

## Overview

**Charles the Bald** (823–877), youngest son of [[louis-the-pious]] by his second wife Judith,
became the first king of **West Francia** — the political ancestor of medieval France — at the
[[treaty-of-verdun-843]], and briefly revived the imperial title (875). His reign, the
best-documented of the ninth century, shows a Carolingian king governing through assemblies,
capitularies, and aristocratic consensus while contending with Viking raids, an over-mighty nobility,
and the rival claims of his nephew Pippin II in Aquitaine. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]]
(Nelson, Ch. 4) he is the central figure of the western kingdom.

## Verdun and the Western Kingdom

Charles's birth in 823 had detonated the succession crisis of Louis the Pious's reign. Allied with
[[louis-the-german]] against [[lothar-i|Lothar]], he won at **Fontenoy** (841), bound the alliance with
the **Strasbourg Oaths** (842), and at **Verdun** (843) received the lands west of the Scheldt, Meuse,
Saône, and Rhône — though he was not yet fully in control of Aquitaine, where Pippin II's cause revived
after 844. His kingdom's open frontiers (the Spanish March, Brittany) and long coastlines exposed it to
both external aggression and intensifying **Viking** raids up its river estuaries.

## Government and Renaissance

Charles ruled in the high Carolingian style: frequent assemblies, a steady output of
**[[carolingian-capitularies|capitularies]]** — including the **Edict of Pîtres (864)**, a major measure
on coinage, defence, and fortification against the Vikings — and a court that was a leading centre of the
**[[carolingian-renaissance]]**, patronising scholars such as John Scottus Eriugena and Hincmar of Reims.
He depended heavily on conciliating powerful magnates (his marriage to Ermentrude, niece of the
kingmaker Adalhard, was a calculated alliance) while struggling to contain ambitious figures such as his
brother-in-law Boso, who in 879 (after Charles's death) made himself king of Provence.

## Emperor

On the death of Lothar's son the emperor Louis II (875), Charles raced to Italy and was crowned emperor
by the pope — reviving the imperial dignity in the West Frankish line. He died in 877 recrossing the
Alps; his weak successor Louis the Stammerer and the subsequent failure of the West Frankish line opened
the way to the deposition of Charles the Fat (887) and the final fragmentation of 888.

## Significance

Charles the Bald gave West Francia its enduring shape and demonstrated both the sophistication and the
limits of late-Carolingian government: an articulate ideology of Christian kingship and a real apparatus
of assemblies and written law, set against a centrifugal aristocracy and a mounting external threat.

## Historiography

The reign is illuminated by the *Annals of St-Bertin* (continued by Hincmar), by a rich capitulary record,
and by Nithard. NCMH II (Nelson) uses Charles to argue that Carolingian kingship remained a genuine,
functioning system of negotiated power, not a hollow shell — a reading that informs the
[[nature-of-carolingian-government]] debate.
