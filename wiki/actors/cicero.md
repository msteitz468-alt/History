# Marcus Tullius Cicero

The greatest **orator** of Rome, a "new man" from Arpinum who reached the consulship
on talent alone, crushed the **[[catilinarian-conspiracy-63bce|Catilinarian
conspiracy]]**, and spent his life defending a senatorial Republic that was dying
around him — and whose **letters and speeches** are our richest window onto its fall.
Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 9 (Wiseman) and Chs. 11–12 (Rawson).

## Rise to the consulship

A wealthy **equestrian** of Arpinum (Marius' home town), Cicero rose entirely through
**forensic oratory**. His prosecution of the corrupt governor **Verres** (70) made
his name; he then climbed the *cursus* at the head of every poll — praetor (66),
when he gave his first speech from the rostra **for the Lex Manilia** (backing
[[pompey|Pompey's]] eastern command) — and in **63** he became the first **novus
homo** consul in a generation, the optimate establishment ultimately preferring his
talent (and the chance to put it under obligation) to the disreputable noble
**[[catilinarian-conspiracy-63bce|Catiline]]**.

## The consulship of 63

Cicero's year was dominated by crisis. He destroyed the **agrarian bill of Rullus**
(probably backed by Caesar and Crassus) with a brilliant demagogic speech turning
the *populus* against its own tribunes; defended the old senator **Rabirius** against
Labienus' archaic *perduellio* charge (a proxy fight over the
[[senatus-consultum-ultimum|SCU]]); and then exposed and suppressed the
**[[catilinarian-conspiracy-63bce|Catilinarian conspiracy]]** — securing the
**execution of five conspirators without trial** under the SCU, on Cato's motion and
against Caesar's. He was hailed *pater patriae*, "father of his country" — but the
executions made him a lasting target: barred from addressing the people on laying
down office, he swore only that he had "saved the Republic."

## The politics of "harmony"

Cicero's ideal was the ***concordia ordinum*** — harmony between Senate and
equestrian order in defence of property and the constitution — and the *consensus
omnium bonorum*. But Wiseman shows him increasingly **isolated**: distrusted by the
optimate "fish-pond fanciers" (the *piscinarii*), unable to draw [[pompey|Pompey]]
firmly to the Senate, and powerless as the **First Triumvirate** formed (60). He
declined Caesar's invitation to join it, choosing — like "Hector for Troy" — to fight
for a Republic whose constitution was, as he saw, "fast becoming unworkable."

## Exile, Caesar's dictatorship, and the end (58–43)

Clodius exploited the Catilinarian executions to drive Cicero into **exile (58)**;
recalled in **57** through Pompey and the *tota Italia* vote, he was thereafter
politically marginal, forced (at Luca's aftermath) to defend Caesar's allies and
retreating into **philosophy and rhetoric** (the *De Republica*, *De Officiis*). In
the [[roman-civil-war-49-45bce|civil war]] he followed Pompey from loyalty, was
pardoned by Caesar, and under the **[[caesars-dictatorship|dictatorship]]** wrote and
quietly hoped for a restored Republic.

The **[[julius-caesar-assassination-44bce|Ides]]** gave him his last great role.
Through the **fourteen *Philippics*** he led the Senate's war against **Antony**,
backing the young **[[second-triumvirate|Octavian]]** as a weapon against him —
"praised, rewarded, and raised up" (or "removed," as the pun ran). When Octavian
turned and joined Antony and Lepidus in the **[[second-triumvirate|Second
Triumvirate]]**, Cicero was **proscribed** (listed by Antony) and **killed at Caieta,
7 December 43** — "he died bravely." His head and hands were displayed on the rostra.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Cicero is the supreme spokesman of the senatorial Republic and the measure of its
impotence: a man of unmatched eloquence and genuine principle who could win cases and
coin slogans but could not command legions, and so could only watch — and finally die
as — the dynasts divided the state. Rawson's verdict: he "analysed the *res publica*
more profoundly than any other Roman thinker," yet was "slow to realize the danger
from great generals," and knew at the end that "we are the playthings of the whims of
the troops and the arrogance of their generals."

## Historiography

Cicero is at once our **chief source** for the late Republic (the *Letters to
Atticus*, the *Verrines*, the *Catilinarians*, *Pro Murena*, *De Imperio*) and a
deeply **interested witness** — Wiseman repeatedly notes his self-dramatization and
"brilliant chicanery." The "First Catilinarian Conspiracy" of 66/65 is largely his
and others' later invention. The whole reconstruction of the 60s depends on him,
checked against Sallust and Asconius.
