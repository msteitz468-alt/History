## Overview

Taira no Kiyomori (平清盛, 1118–1181) was the dominant political figure of late Heian Japan, the first warrior to reach the pinnacle of the court hierarchy, and the architect of Taira supremacy from 1160 to his death in 1181. Born to Taira no Tadamori and — according to a persistent rumor recorded in [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] — possibly the natural son of Retired Emperor Shirakawa, Kiyomori combined his father's courtly skills with formidable military ability, shrewd marriage politics, and ruthless elimination of rivals. His death by illness in 1181, before the [[genpei-war-1180]] was resolved, left the [[taira-clan]] without the political genius needed to survive the Minamoto onslaught.

## Birth and Parentage

Kiyomori was officially the son of Taira no Tadamori. However, [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] records the rumor "that his natural father was actually Emperor Shirakawa. The source considered the most reliable says that the emperor, upon the death of Kiyomori's mother, gave the two-year-old child for adoption to Taira no Tadamori, who thus became his genealogical father" (p. 465). If true, this imperial blood would explain the court's unusual tolerance for his rapid elevation to heights no warrior had previously reached. His mother may have been Shirakawa's concubine, or the younger sister of Shirakawa's favorite consort (the Gion Consort) — sources differ.

## Early Career

Kiyomori inherited Tadamori's position as the leading warrior at court. He served in the hokumen (the retired emperor's north-face guard), held provincial governorships, and built the Taira's retainer network in the western provinces and Inland Sea coastal area. By the time of the Hogen Disturbance (1156) he was the foremost Taira military figure, commanding roughly three hundred mounted men.

## The Hogen Disturbance (1156)

When succession rivalries split the imperial family — pitting Go-Shirakawa against his brother Sutoku, and dividing the Fujiwara regental house between Tadamichi and Yorinaga — the ready availability of warrior forces made military resolution tempting to both sides. Kiyomori aligned with Go-Shirakawa's faction (the reigning emperor's party). On the night of the 11th day of the 7th month of Hogen 1 (1156), Kiyomori led approximately three hundred mounted men in a nighttime assault on Sutoku's Shirakawa palace: "They set fire to the palace, and Sutoku's men fled. Within a few hours both the battle and the war were over" (p. 690). The aftermath was remarkable: for the first time in three and a half centuries of bloodless precedent, warriors condemned as prisoners were executed by imperial order — including Tameyoshi (the Minamoto chieftain, killed by his own son Yoshitomo) and Kiyomori's uncle Tadamasa (killed by Kiyomori on Go-Shirakawa's orders). See [[hogen-heiji-disturbances-1156]].

## The Heiji Disturbance (1159)

In 1159, Kiyomori's chief Minamoto rival Yoshitomo allied with the disaffected courtier Fujiwara no Nobuyori to seize control of the capital while Kiyomori was away on pilgrimage to Kumano. Yoshitomo and Nobuyori forced Go-Shirakawa into the Greater Imperial Palace under house arrest, burned Shinzei's residence, and removed Kiyomori from his official posts. Kiyomori returned at the head of a small force — eventually about four hundred men — and waited at his Rokuhara residence. When Emperor Nijo escaped custody disguised as a woman and joined Kiyomori at Rokuhara, the Taira acquired the legitimacy of an imperial army. Kiyomori's son Shigemori (1138–79) launched an attack on the Greater Imperial Palace and, after the Minamoto were lured out, occupied it. The dispossessed Minamoto advanced on Rokuhara and were crushed. Yoshitomo fled east and was killed; his sons were executed. The thirteen-year-old Yoritomo was spared at the plea of Kiyomori's stepmother, Ike no Zenni, and exiled to Izu. See [[hogen-heiji-disturbances-1156]].

## Rise to Supreme Power (1160–1179)

With the Minamoto eliminated and the court dependent on his military strength, Kiyomori's advancement was rapid:

- **1160:** Received the Third Rank — "the first of a warrior family to join the senior nobles (kugyo)" (p. 1160)
- **1164:** His eight-year-old daughter married the future regent Motozane, establishing Fujiwara-style marriage politics
- **1167:** Rose to Chancellor (daijo daijin) and First Rank — the pinnacle of the court hierarchy. He held the chancellorship only briefly, citing illness, but continued to dominate appointments and policy
- **c. 1168–70:** His Taira sister-in-law Shigeko (Kensh mon-in) bore Go-Shirakawa a son, later enthroned as Emperor Takakura (r. 1168–80) — making Kiyomori a de facto grandfather of the reigning emperor
- **c. 1172:** His daughter Tokushi (Kenreimon-in) became Takakura's consort
- **1180:** Their infant son was enthroned as Emperor Antoku by Kiyomori

The Heike monogatari's description of the clan's dominance at its height: "Not only did Kiyomori himself attain the pinnacle of worldly success, but his entire family shared his prosperity. . . . Sixteen Taira ranked as senior nobles, more than thirty were courtiers, and more than sixty held appointments as provincial governors, guards officers, or members of the central bureaucracy. It was as though there were no other people in the world" (p. 697, quoting Heike, kan 1.5).

Kiyomori's methods were, until 1177, those of a skilled civil politician: marriage politics, commendation of estates to high patrons, court appointments, and factional alliance management. He was an adroit exploiter of the rivalry between Go-Shirakawa and Emperor Nijo.

## Shishigatani Plot (1177) and Military Coup (1179)

Kiyomori's growing power generated serious resistance, particularly from Go-Shirakawa, who sought to check him. The Shishigatani plot of 1177 was an organized attempt by the retired emperor's household officers, acting with military figures, to move against the Taira. Kiyomori discovered and crushed it before it could act, leaving him "more powerful than ever but with a heightened enmity toward Go-Shirakawa and his court" (p. 498).

In 1179, disputes over estate inheritance — particularly Kiyomori's maneuvering to retain regental house holdings — provoked Go-Shirakawa to confiscate Taira properties. Kiyomori responded with an open military coup: "He led several thousand warriors from Fukuhara and paraded them in the capital. He demanded the dismissal of thirty-nine officials, retainers of Go-Shirakawa, and replaced them with Taira adherents. The retired emperor was placed under house arrest and moved to the Toba-dono" (p. 515). Three months later Go-Shirakawa's son Takakura was forced to abdicate in favor of Kiyomori's infant grandson, Emperor Antoku.

## The Genpei War and Death (1180–1181)

In 1180, Minamoto no Yorimasa — an aged Minamoto warrior-courtier who had loyally served the court — raised rebellion in support of Prince Mochihito's call to arms against the Taira. Yorimasa was defeated and died, but his action ignited the broader [[genpei-war-1180]], with Yoritomo raising the Minamoto banner in the east and Yoshinaka in the north. Kiyomori died of sudden illness in the intercalary second month of 1181, before the war was decided. His death removed the one leader capable of providing unified strategic direction to the Taira.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Kiyomori lived another decade, Taira political dominance might have been consolidated before the Minamoto could mobilize the east. The Taira's fatal strategic error — leaving Yoritomo alive in Izu and failing to eliminate him quickly after his 1180 rebellion — was arguably a failure of Kiyomori's successors, not Kiyomori himself, who died before the severity of the eastern uprising was clear.

## Historiography

Kiyomori is one of the most written-about figures in Japanese history, but heavily filtered through the Heike monogatari and related war tales (gunki monogatari), which were compiled in the Kamakura period when the Minamoto-descended shogunal regime had every reason to present the Taira as villains. [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] notes that contemporary sources (court diaries, official records) are considerably less critical of Kiyomori's rise than the later literary tradition. The rumor of imperial parentage appears in several sources and is assessed carefully: it may be true, may be a Taira self-serving claim, or may be a Kamakura-period explanation for the inexplicable elevation of a warrior. Takeuchi Rizo's scholarship (Bushi no tojo) is the foundational Japanese secondary source for this chapter.
