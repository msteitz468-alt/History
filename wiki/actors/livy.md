# Livy (Titus Livius)

The great Augustan historian of Rome, whose *Ab Urbe Condita* is — with
[[polybius]] — the principal narrative source for the middle Republic and thus for
much of [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]]. Introduced as a source in CAH VIII Ch. 1
(Astin).

## Role

Livy (59 BCE–17 CE) devoted his life not to public affairs but to a single
**142-book history** of Rome from its origins to his own day, written across the
reign of Augustus. **Thirty-five books survive**: I–X (to 292 BCE) and **XXI–XLV**
(219–167 BCE) — the latter covering the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second Punic
War]] and Rome's wars against the Hellenistic powers, the core narrative of CAH
VIII. For lost books there survive the **Periochae** (brief summaries of all but
two books) and an Oxyrhynchus epitome.

## Method and reliability

Astin's assessment (CAH VIII Ch. 1): Livy's aim was **artistic** — a grand,
polished, dramatic narrative — not primary research. His method was to follow one
chosen source per episode, rewritten in his own style. For this period he relied:
- on **[[polybius]]** for eastern affairs (often closely, though unacknowledged),
  so that much of Livy preserves Polybius' second-century account; and
- on the **Sullan annalists** Valerius Antias and Claudius Quadrigarius for Roman
  detail — writers "prone to exaggeration and elaboration."

The practical rule (Briscoe, Ch. 3): **where Polybius is a control, Livy is
reliable; where not, his casualty figures and dramatic battle-scenes need caution**,
but his broad framework — drawn ultimately from contemporary records via the
annalists, including the *annales maximi* (elections, provinces, levies, triumphs,
prodigies) — is generally sound. The breaking-off of Livy's continuous text at 167
sharply degrades the quality of our knowledge thereafter.

## OHCW (Lintott Ch. 26)

**Andrew Lintott**: only 35 of 142 books survive (Greek historians fared better under Byzantine scholarship after the western cultural hiatus). Born Patavium 59 BCE; wrote from about age thirty after twenty years of civil war and conversion of the Republic into monarchy; last great Latin annalistic history of the Republic, carried down to 9 BCE; conservative, patriotic, pro-senatorial against tribunes. Notable exception among non-senators to the ideal that the historian of public affairs should be a participant. Like [[sallust]], argued Rome had succumbed to the weight of her own success; as causal interpreter unoriginal/defective, substituting vivid human sympathy and “tragic” historiography for Polybian “pragmatic” explanation. Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]].

## Historiography

Livy is both a source and an object of study. His relationship to Polybius
(Nissen's foundational analysis) lets scholars measure his fidelity where the two
overlap. His treatment of early Rome is central to
[[reliability-of-early-roman-tradition]] and [[early-roman-source-criticism]]. His
own *Histories* are now ingested directly in Phase 4: **[[livy-hannibals-war|*Hannibal's War* (Books
21–30)]]** (the Second Punic War) and **[[livy-mediterranean-empire|*Rome's Mediterranean Empire*
(Books 41–45) and the Periochae]]** (the Third Macedonian War and the summaries of the lost books).
