# Ranald Slidell Mackenzie

**Ranald Slidell Mackenzie** (27 July 1840 – 18 January 1889) was a U.S. Army officer — first in his West Point class of 1862, Civil War brevet brigadier general (regulars) and major general (volunteers), later colonel of the Fourth Cavalry — who became the most effective U.S. operational commander against free [[actors/comanches|Comanches]] and allied southern plains peoples. Comanches called him **No-Finger Chief** or **Bad Hand** from war wounds that took the first two fingers of his right hand. S. C. Gwynne frames him as the "**anti-Custer**": obscure in victory where [[actors/custer-george-a|George Armstrong Custer]] was famous in disaster.

## Overview

Dispatched under [[actors/william-t-sherman|Sherman]]'s post–Peace Policy hard line (from the October 1871 campaigns onward), Mackenzie learned canyon and llano warfare through failure (Blanco Canyon 1871 and a horse-stealing counter-raid by [[actors/parker-quanah|Quanah]]'s people) and success (North Fork / McClellan Creek 1872; village and horse-herd destruction in the [[events/red-river-war-1874|Red River War]], especially [[places/palo-duro-canyon|Palo Duro Canyon]]). After a diversion into Mexico against Kickapoo/Apache villages, he later struck Northern Cheyenne under Dull Knife (1876). Mental and physical collapse ended his career; he died in New York in 1889 at forty-eight.

## Formation and Character

- **Family:** Elite eastern connections — grandfather John Slidell (Manhattan bank president/power broker); uncle John Slidell Jr. (Louisiana politics, U.S. senator, top Buchanan adviser); aunt Jane married Commodore Matthew Perry; father Alexander Mackenzie Slidell (naval commander/writer, court-martialed for hanging the secretary of war's son for mutiny); mother from Hamilton-era treasury bloodlines.
- **Youth:** Father died when Mackenzie was eight → permanent family financial difficulty. Frail, shy, smallish, pale Scottish coloring; speech impediment (lisp or slight stutter). Williams College two years (hoped to be a lawyer); transferred to West Point 1858 for free education/salary. Graduated **first of 28 cadets** (class of 1862), considered by many "the all-around ablest man in it"; adult height slim 5′9″ (cavalry limit); assistant math professor while a cadet.
- **Civil War:** Engineer at Second Manassas, Fredericksburg, Chancellorsville, Gettysburg; command at Cold Harbor June 1864 (brevetted lieutenant colonel, Second Connecticut Volunteer Artillery) at age 23; by Appomattox brevet brigadier (regulars) and major general (volunteers) — highest-ranking officer of West Point 1862, age 24; seven brevets in under three years (beat Custer's five, same final rank). Grant: "I regarded Mackenzie as the most promising young officer in the army… upon his own merit and without influence."
- **Wounds that hardened him:** Six Civil War wounds — shot through both shoulders (.50+ cal) at Manassas, lay 24 hours; artillery shell cut horse in half and hit thigh at Winchester; chest shrapnel; shell took first two fingers of right hand. Pain never left; personality change — lost affability/prankishness; became "brutally demanding: the boss from hell." Men of Second Connecticut hated him (some plotted to shoot him) until Winchester bravery ended mutiny talk. Nicknames: Three-Finger Jack; Comanche **Bad Hand** and **No-Finger Chief**.

## Role and Key Decisions

- Translate Civil War staff-college competence into **winter/logistical war** against mobile villages.
- Target **horse herds and food stores**, not only warriors in open fight — after North Fork 1872 horse-recovery fiasco, institutionalize the **horse-shot policy** (Sergeant John Charlton: thereafter "No effort… ever made to hold a herd of wild captured Indian ponies. They were all shot.").
- Persist on the Llano after earlier Spanish and U.S. forces had failed or barely escaped (cf. Kit Carson at Adobe Walls 1864).
- Learn from Blanco Canyon failure; map water/Comanchero roads across the [[places/llano-estacado|Llano Estacado]] (1872) so hostiles could be kept constantly on the run.

## Operational Career (southern plains and after)

### 1867–1871: Path to the Fourth Cavalry
Reverted to captain after the war; 1867 colonel, Forty-first Infantry (black regiment) → Texas; limited Indian skirmishes 1869–70. **1871:** command of the Fourth Cavalry — direct consequence of Grant's impatience with the peace policy; "most aggressive and effective officer… placed squarely in the path of Comanche war parties." On 3 October 1871 ~600 soldiers and ~20–25 Tonkawa scouts moved from the Clear Fork of the Brazos under orders to kill Comanches; Sherman's chosen agent after Peace Policy failure.

### Blanco Canyon, fall 1871 (failure and learning)
Largest force yet sent to pursue Indians on this front; men with Colts, Spencer carbines, hundreds of rounds each. Night of 10 October: Quanah's raiders crashed into the sleeping area (nearly over Mackenzie's tent), screaming/shooting/cowbells; stampeded horses to put soldiers afoot — could have been "disaster of epic proportions." **Sixty-six to ~seventy** best horses and mules lost, including Mackenzie's gray pacer. Village under young Quanah (not principal chiefs Bull Bear/Wild Horse); Gwynne: "remarkable tactics… his and his alone." Next day: Capt. E. M. Heyl's dozen pursued decoys into high-ground ambush; Quanah (black war paint, bear-claw necklace, six-shooters) shot Private Seander Gregg in the head at feet/inches; Robert Carter later awarded Medal of Honor. Gwynne's judgment: good news for Comanches (humiliation of a top combat officer) but "beginning of the end of the old empire" — penetration of the heartland signaled intent to destroy raiders at source.

### Summer–autumn 1872: Llano roads and North Fork / McClellan Creek
Still barred from chasing reservation Indians across the Red River, but scorched earth for off-reservation hostiles. Captured Comanchero Polonio Ortiz revealed east–west wagon road with water/grass (Comanchero cattle trail Texas→NM). Mackenzie scouted Caprock, Prairie Dog Town Fork, region of Clarendon/Turkey/Matador/Roaring Springs; crossed the Llano twice by different routes; permanent water ≤30 miles apart (Tucumcari→Canyon area) — "possible to keep the hostile Indians constantly on the run."

**29 September 1872 — North Fork of the Red River / McClellan Creek:** 222 soldiers + 9 Tonks hit village of 175 large + 87 small tipis ~5 miles from present Lefors at 4 p.m.; complete surprise; fight ~half hour; "shooting gallery" / theater-pit metaphor (W. A. Thompson); blood-red pool from dead/wounded (captive Clinton Smith). **52 Indians killed, 4 soldiers lost; 124 prisoners** (mostly women/children) — unprecedented since horse culture; lodges burned. Members of all five major bands present; ~1–2 of Quanah's Quahadi wives among prisoners. Captive Herman Lehmann later described Tonkawa mutilation of Batsena and disembowelment of daughter Nooki. ~3,000 captured horses held under Tonk/lieutenant guard → Comanches stampeded most back; next night took most remaining; only 50 horses + 9 mules left → **thereafter shoot the herds**. Prisoner band mix (34 Kotsoteka, 30 Quahadi, 18 Yamparika, 11 Nokoni, 9 Penateka) showed dissolving band structures. June 1873: 116 women/children (+ few old men) returned Fort Concho → [[places/fort-sill|Fort Sill]]; many soon back out raiding.

### Mexico diversion (1873)
Mackenzie diverted to Mexico (Kickapoo/Apache villages ~80 miles across the border, unofficial Sheridan orders); rheumatism kept him out of the field until January 1874.

### Red River War and Palo Duro, 1874
Fielded 23 August 1874: 560 enlisted, 47 officers, 3 surgeons, 32 scouts (642). Free to follow hostiles to agencies and take command at Fort Sill (Augur orders). Night of 26–27 September: expected stampede — horses hobbled + cross-sidelined + iron stakes + sleeping parties; Comanche attack under Shaking Hand, Wild Horse, Hears the Sunrise failed (lesson of 1871 learned). Feint up Tule Canyon then night march north; Comanchero tortured on wagon wheel gave camp location.

**28 September 1874 — [[places/palo-duro-canyon|Palo Duro Canyon]]:** dawn, 7 companies at the rim — five villages, ~200 lodges, large horse herd over 3 miles along stream (O-ha-ma-tai Comanches majority; Maman-ti Kiowas; Iron Shirt Cheyennes). Maman-ti's medicine said no bluecoat attack → no sentries. Precipitous goat trail ~900 ft; ~1 hour to get companies down. Running fight ~4 miles; 4 Comanches killed. Villages burned (buffalo robes/meat + white goods: blankets, china, breechloaders, flour, sugar). **1,424 horses** captured; marched to Tule Canyon; best to scouts; **more than 1,000 shot** until ~3 p.m. — "as a military tactic, a way to take away the Indians' means of survival"; rotting pile became landmark then fertilizer; legend of phantom herd. Survivors afoot without shelter/food/clothing facing winter; most straggled to Fort Sill "never to roam off the reservation again."

### Fort Sill command and Quahadi surrender, 1875
16 March 1875: Mackenzie took Fort Sill command. December 1874 scout: 17 days, 255 miles, deep snow/ice, only 3 Indians killed (Quahadi holdouts still free SE of Lubbock). 23 April 1875: dispatched Dr. Jacob J. Sturm + 3 Comanches incl. Wild Horse; 1 May found Quanah's camp. **Noon 2 June 1875: 407 Quahadis surrendered** at Signal Station a few miles west of Fort Sill with ~1,500 horses and arms. Mackenzie to Sheridan: "I think better of this band than of any other on the reserve… I shall let them down as easily as I can" — kept many horses; none of Quanah's band in icehouse/guardhouse. Pre-arrival 19 May letter inquiring after Cynthia Ann and Prairie Flower → learned both dead; start of Mackenzie–Quanah friendship. Firm administrator: 1876 renegade ultimatum (20 minutes or kill all) worked.

### Northern plains, 1876–1881
After Little Bighorn (June 1876), Sheridan sent Mackenzie + Fourth Cavalry north; commanded District of Black Hills / Camp Robinson; dawn surround of a Sioux village → 239 men + 729 horses surrendered. **Powder River Expedition vs Dull Knife's Northern Cheyenne** (Custer fight participants): 25 November 1876, 818 soldiers + 363 scouts, snow/subzero, killed 25, 500 horses captured, 6 own dead. Dull Knife later: "You are the one I was afraid of…." Two weeks later Crazy Horse + 889 Sioux surrendered to Mackenzie at Red Cloud Agency — Gwynne's "bookend to the twinned fates of Custer and Mackenzie." Later: border bandits 1877; Utes 1879/81; Apache uprising New Mexico; **brigadier general October 1881** with Grant's help.

### Collapse and death, 1882–1889
Odd reassignment request (poor handwriting, possible stroke; "much harder in the last two years"); Department of Texas command, San Antonio; age 43 began heavy drinking (had forsworn alcohol); engaged Florida Sharpe 17 December 1882; 18 December drunk fight, beaten, tied to cart; **29 December Bloomingdale Asylum, NYC**; 5 March army board declared him insane over protests. Lived with sister, Morristown NJ; **died New York hospital 18 January 1889**, age 48. Theories Gwynne canvasses: syphilis unlikely (no army treatment record); PTSD from Civil War wounds/battles; 1875 Fort Sill cart fall head injury (3-day stupor); childhood sunstroke. Death notice brief, no career news story — obscurity contrast with Custer fame. Quanah ~40 then; no recorded reaction.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Mackenzie's operational learning curve, southern plains subjugation might have taken longer or cost more army disasters; structural forces (railroads, hide hunters, demography) still favored the U.S., but he was the instrument that made hard policy executable. The anti-Custer frame is analytical, not hagiographic: success through logistics, scouts, horse destruction, and refusal of celebrity narrative.

## Historiography

Army reports; Robert G. Carter and other officer memoirs; Grant's praise; S. C. Gwynne, *Empire of the Summer Moon* (2010) — popular rehabilitation vs. Custer myth, operational detail on Blanco Canyon, North Fork, Palo Duro, and mental collapse. Less celebrity biography than Custer; operational reputation among military historians is high. Atrocity and horse-slaughter details are load-bearing to his method — record with attribution, not euphemism. Date of death: Gwynne gives **18 January 1889** (some reference works list 19 January — prefer Gwynne for this source pass; flag if contradicted).
