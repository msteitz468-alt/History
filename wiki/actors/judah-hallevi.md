## Overview

Judah ben Samuel Hallevi was an Andalusian Jewish poet, physician, and philosopher, born in Tudela (then in northern Spain) around 1075 and dying in the Holy Land in 1141. He is the most important medieval Jewish critic of philosophical rationalism. His *Kuzari* (*Kitāb al-Radd wa-l-Dalīl fī l-Dīn al-Dhalīl* — "Book of Refutation and Proof on Behalf of the Despised Religion") presents a sustained defense of Judaism against philosophy, Christianity, and Islam, set within a fictional account of the conversion of the Khazar king ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## Life

Hallevi came from Tudela, a contemporary and friend of Abraham Ibn Ezra. He trained as a physician and eventually rose to a court doctor in Toledo. The Almoravid invasion of Andalusia forced him to become peripatetic, moving through Toledo, Córdoba, and Almería. At the end of his life he traveled to Egypt and finally to the Holy Land, dying there in 1141. The theme of Jewish exile pervades both his poetry (hundreds of surviving Hebrew poems) and his philosophical writing.

The *Kuzari* was written in Judeo-Arabic and later translated into Hebrew. In the work's own description Hallevi is responding to a "heretic," likely a Karaite opponent.

## The Kuzari: Structure and Argument

The *Kuzari* dramatizes the Khazar king's search for religious guidance following a divine dream in which an angel tells him his *intention* pleases God but his *action* does not. This distinction is central: it is not right belief but right practice that needs amendment — a critique of philosophy's claim to provide the decisive truth.

**Philosophy's failure**: The king first hears from a philosopher who presents a highly rationalist system committed to the eternity of the universe and an impersonal God ignorant of particular events. Hallevi specifically targets Avicenna's claim that God lacks knowledge of particulars (36, 198, 200). The king is unimpressed; he has already received a personal divine communication, and the philosopher offers him a phone-book when he has a hotline to God.

**Christianity's failure**: The king finds Christian doctrines (Incarnation, Trinity) incoherent — ones that could be accepted only by someone raised in the faith, not by rational evaluation.

**Islam's failure**: Islam's main claim rests on the miraculous nature of the Koran, which the king (a non-Arabic speaker) cannot appreciate. More broadly, Muḥammad's other miracles were witnessed by only small numbers; the Jewish tradition commands miraculous events witnessed by entire peoples.

**Judaism's case**: Hallevi's defense of Judaism rests on three pillars:
1. An unparalleled arsenal of mass-witnessed miracles (the parting of the Red Sea)
2. Historical primacy — all wisdom traces back to Adam and the Hebrew patriarchs, transmitted to Persia and Chaldea and only then to Greece; Aristotle's errors stem from this indirect transmission
3. The necessity of tradition: human reason cannot discover how God wishes to be worshipped; only prophetic revelation and the oral tradition of the rabbis (against the Karaites) can do so

### On the Limits of Reason

Hallevi's position is not blanket anti-rationalism but a careful diagnosis of reason's limits. He cites Greek sources to undermine Greek claims: Hippocrates' "life is short, art is long" (248); Aristotle's metaphor of bats blinded by the sun (214, quoting *Metaphysics* 993b); Plato's *Apology* on merely human wisdom (218, 272). Like the ancient Skeptics, reason perpetually disagrees with itself. The remedy is not less reason but access to a tradition grounded in divine revelation.

> "Hallevi delivers a nuanced critique of Aristotelian philosophy: like the king himself, it is admirable in its intentions, but arrogant and misguided in carrying out these intentions." — Adamson

Adamson notes the strong parallel with [[al-ghazali]]: the *Kuzari* was likely shaped by al-Ghazālī's works, which were known in Muslim Spain. Like al-Ghazālī, Hallevi accuses the philosophers of *taqlīd* (uncritical acceptance of authority), even as Hallevi himself insists on submission to prophetic tradition.

### Against the Karaites

Against Karaite Jews (who rejected the oral rabbinic tradition and relied on individual Scripture interpretation), Hallevi argues that unaided reason cannot discover the proper forms of divine worship, and that the resulting Karaite disagreements mirror the interminable disputes of the philosophers.

## Relation to Contemporaries

Hallevi debated Abraham Ibn Ezra on astrology: where Ibn Ezra saw the Jews' exile as an astral phenomenon governed by Saturn, Hallevi's epistemological framework left little room for such naturalistic explanations. Their relationship was remarkable given how sharply they differed intellectually.

Hallevi's critique of the universalist Neoplatonism exemplified by [[ibn-gabirol]] helped establish the terms of debate for later Jewish philosophy.

## Legacy

The *Kuzari* became a canonical text of Jewish thought. Hallevi's emphasis on the experiential and communal bases of religious truth, his suspicion of purely theoretical philosophy, and his poetic rendering of Jewish exile and longing all exercised lasting influence. His poems are among the finest in the Hebrew language.

## Historiography

The *Kuzari*'s philsophical arguments were long overshadowed by its poetic and theological dimensions in Jewish reception. Modern scholarship has revisited his engagement with Islamic *kalām* and Sufi thought, and has traced his parallel to al-Ghazālī more precisely. Adamson places Hallevi at the rationalist–anti-rationalist fault line that would define medieval Jewish philosophy after him.
