# Ibn al-Shāṭir

ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Ibrāhīm ibn al-Shāṭir (ca. 1304–1375) was the foremost mathematical astronomer of 14th-century Damascus and the most comprehensive reformer of Ptolemaic planetary theory in the Islamic tradition. Working as a *muwaqqit* (timekeeper) at the Umayyad Mosque of Damascus — formally a minor institutional position — he produced in his "spare time" a set of planetary models whose mathematical structure was, in several cases, identical to models later published by Copernicus, a central fact in the argument for the transmission of Islamic astronomy to Renaissance Europe.

## Intellectual Context

Ibn al-Shāṭir stands at the end of the reform tradition inaugurated at the [[maragha-school|Maragha school]] a century earlier. He inherited the two new mathematical tools — [[urdi-lemma|ʿUrḍī's Lemma]] and the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī couple]] — introduced by [[al-urdi|al-ʿUrḍī]] (d. 1266) and [[nasir-al-din-tusi|al-Ṭūsī]] (d. 1274), and the multi-model work of Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī (d. 1311). He explicitly referred to previous solutions as *uṣūl* (principles/hypotheses) in his *taṣḥīḥ al-uṣūl* (correction of principles) — the subtitle of his major work — placing himself in a tradition of cumulative reform rather than isolated innovation.

What distinguished him was his willingness to re-examine the *cosmological* foundations rather than merely correcting individual models. Where his predecessors had reformed models while leaving some eccentrics in place, Ibn al-Shāṭir rejected eccentrics entirely and required strict geocentrism throughout.

## The Rejection of Eccentrics and the Defence of Epicycles

Ibn al-Shāṭir's first move was to insist that all his models would be strictly geocentric — no eccentric spheres at all. He justified this on Aristotelian grounds: the eccentric violated the principle that the Earth is the unique center of heaviness in the universe.

This left the problem of epicycles, which Aristotle's followers had condemned as introducing an alien center of heaviness into the celestial domain. Ibn al-Shāṭir's response was original and, as Saliba notes, he "was the first, and probably the only one, to insist on making" this argument: he pointed out that the fixed stars emitted light while the spheres carrying them did not — a visual observation everyone could verify. If spheres and stars were both composed of the simple element ether, how could one emit light and the other remain dark? He concluded that "at least the lower celestial spheres of Aristotle, by which the stars and the planets were supposed to be moved, had to admit some kind of composition" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]). Therefore, the complexity introduced by epicycles was no greater than the complexity already admitted by the visible difference between stars and their carrier spheres. Epicycles were permissible.

Having "solved" the epicycle problem in this fashion, he proceeded to banish all eccentrics by substituting epicycles in their place — following the Apollonius theorem, which proved that the eccentric and epicyclic representations were mathematically equivalent.

## The Unified System

The result was a set of planetary models **unified by strict geocentrism**, using the same two mathematical principles throughout:

1. **The Apollonius equation** (eccentric → epicycle substitution)
2. **[[urdi-lemma|ʿUrḍī's Lemma]]** (to resolve the equant via a small additional epicycle)

With the exception of Mercury, all of Ibn al-Shāṭir's models had identical constructions, differing only in the sizes and speeds of epicycles. For Mercury, he added a use of the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī couple]] as a final step — the same structure later adopted by Copernicus for the same planet.

Saliba notes the crucial consequence: "One additional advantage resulted from this systematic use of geocentricity, which was to come in handy later on during the European Renaissance: the unification of all the Ptolemaic geocentric models under one structure that lent itself to the simple shift of the centrality of the universe from the Earth to the sun, thus producing heliocentrism, without having to make any changes in the rest of the models" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]). The Copernican shift from geocentrism to heliocentrism required only a reidentification of the center — the mathematical apparatus was already in place from Ibn al-Shāṭir.

## Specific Model Innovations

### Solar Model
Conducted his own observations of the apparent sizes of the Sun (and Moon) — results "which varied considerably from the values given by Ptolemy" — and constructed a new solar model fitting those observations. His major surviving work, *Nihāyat al-sul*, explicitly states these observations and their use in model construction, demonstrating "quite clearly Ibn al-Shāṭir's ability to construct theoretical models that were based on observational results, just as was done by Ptolemy, but without committing the inconsistencies of Ptolemy" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

### Lunar Model
Ibn al-Shāṭir's lunar model eliminated both the Ptolemaic equant and the *prosneusis* problem (the oscillating reference line). It reduced the variation in the Moon's apparent size at quadrature — Ptolemy's model had predicted the Moon at quarter phase should appear nearly twice as large as the full Moon, which Ibn al-Shāṭir rejected as untenable since "the moon was never seen as such (*lam yurā kadhālika*)" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]). His lunar model is mathematically **identical** to the lunar model in Copernicus's *Commentariolus* and *De Revolutionibus*.

### Upper Planets (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Venus)
Used ʿUrḍī's Lemma with the Apollonius substitution to produce models entirely free of eccentrics and equants, with all spheres rotating uniformly around their own centers.

### Mercury
The most complex case — which al-Ṭūsī had acknowledged as unsolved, and which Shīrāzī had addressed through a double Ṭūsī couple. Ibn al-Shāṭir's Mercury model adds a use of the Ṭūsī couple as the final adjustment to his standard construction, successfully eliminating the Mercury equant. Saliba notes that "Copernicus ended up relying so heavily on the works of Ibn al-Shāṭir" precisely for the Mercury model, using "the same Ṭūsī Couple, in the same fashion as was done by Ibn al-Shāṭir, in order to account for the motion of Mercury" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Relationship to Observations

Ibn al-Shāṭir was the only Islamic astronomer known to have devoted an entire book — *Taʿlīq al-arṣād* (Accounting for Observations) — to the relationship between observational results and the construction of predictive models. The book is unfortunately lost, but its existence, as Saliba notes, is "extremely significant." The surviving evidence from the *Nihāyat al-sul* shows that he conducted original observations and used them to constrain model-building — the methodology Ptolemy had used, but pursued without Ptolemy's inconsistencies.

## Institutional Position

Ibn al-Shāṭir worked as *muwaqqit* (timekeeper) at the Umayyad Mosque of Damascus — responsible for determining prayer times, lunar visibility, and related calendrical and astronomical tasks required by Islamic religious law. This was a modest institutional position compared with court astronomers, yet it provided both the practical observational motivation and apparently sufficient leisure for sustained theoretical work. Saliba notes that "we do not know much about the details of his job description," but the scope of the surviving astronomical corpus suggests he treated the position as a platform for original research ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Works

- ***Nihāyat al-sul fī taṣḥīḥ al-uṣūl*** (The Final Quest Regarding the Rectification of [Astronomical] Principles): his major theoretical work, containing the reformed planetary models and the observational results for the apparent sizes of Sun and Moon.
- ***Taʿlīq al-arṣād*** (Accounting for Observations): on the relationship between observations and predictive models. **Lost.**
- Sundial construction and instrument-making works associated with his position at the Umayyad Mosque.

## Significance

Ibn al-Shāṭir represents the completion of the reform programme of the [[maragha-school|Maragha school]] within Islamic astronomy. The mathematical identity of his lunar model and Mercury model with those of Copernicus is the single most striking piece of evidence in Saliba's argument for the transmission of Islamic mathematical astronomy to Renaissance Europe. The argument is reinforced by the structural and contextual similarities: the same problems (equant, prosneusis, Mercury model), the same mathematical tools (ʿUrḍī's Lemma, Ṭūsī couple), the same sequence of application.

As Saliba observes, Copernicus "ended up relying so heavily on the works of Ibn al-Shāṭir when he used, among other things, a lunar model that was identical to that of Ibn al-Shāṭir" — and the shift from geocentrism to heliocentrism that distinguished Copernicus from Ibn al-Shāṭir may be understood as a reidentification of center rather than a reconstruction of models ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Historiography

Ibn al-Shāṭir's mathematical identity with Copernicus was established by Victor Roberts (1957) for the lunar model and extended to the planetary models by subsequent scholars. His *Nihāyat al-sul* has been studied by E. S. Kennedy, Roberts, and Willy Hartner. Saliba situates Ibn al-Shāṭir within the continuous reform tradition originating at Maragha, correcting earlier accounts that treated him as an isolated figure.
