# Trajan (Marcus Ulpius Traianus)

The **second of the "Five Good Emperors"** (98–117) — the great soldier-emperor under whom the
Roman Empire reached its **greatest territorial extent**, and whom the later tradition crowned
**"Optimus"**, the model *princeps* against whom all successors were measured (*felicior Augusto,
melior Traiano*). The first emperor from the **provinces** (Italica in Spain). Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 2.

## Accession and image

A Spanish-born commander of the legions of Upper Germany (loyal to Domitian during the revolt of
Saturninus), Trajan was **adopted by [[nerva]] (97)** to give the weak regime the army's backing,
and succeeded peacefully in **98**. CAH XI stresses that his overwhelmingly **favourable
tradition** (Pliny's *Panegyricus*, Dio Chrysostom's *Orations on Kingship*) is broadly
trustworthy — its few negative notes (drinking, the love of boys) being minor — but that it
**exaggerates Domitian's failures to magnify Trajan's**, obscuring the real **continuity** between
the reigns (in building, foreign policy, even the Capitoline Games). The volume highlights an
**"ambiguous image"**: the modest soldier sharing his men's toil and respecting the Senate
(*civilitas*, *moderatio*) versus the **demagogic conqueror** of the colossal **Forum of Trajan**
(larger than all earlier imperial fora combined), the column, and the four new provinces — a
"devouring ambition behind the discipline and modesty."

## The Dacian Wars and the greatest extent

Trajan's reign was defined by **conquest** — "the most extensive and innovative since Augustus."
In the **[[dacian-wars-101-106|Dacian Wars]] (101–102, 105–106)** he destroyed the kingdom of
**Decebalus** beyond the Danube and annexed **Dacia** as a province (its gold mines funding the
vast building programme; the conquest commemorated on **Trajan's Column**). He also annexed
**Arabia** (Nabataea, 106). His coins proclaimed the new provinces (*Dacia, Arabia adquisita*).

## The Parthian War

Late in his reign Trajan launched the **[[trajans-parthian-war-114-117|Parthian War]]
(114–117)** — annexing **Armenia** and **Mesopotamia**, taking the Parthian capital **Ctesiphon**,
and reaching the **Persian Gulf** (where, Dio reports, he wept that he was too old to go on to
India like Alexander). But the conquests proved unsustainable: a great **revolt erupted in his
rear** — the **Jewish diaspora revolt of 115–117** across Cyrene, Egypt, Cyprus, and Mesopotamia
— and the new eastern provinces could not be held. Trajan died in 117 on the return journey, at
Selinus in Cilicia.

## Civic works and significance

Beyond conquest, Trajan was remembered for **public works and benefaction**: the Forum and
markets, harbours, roads and bridges, the **alimenta** (the Italian child-support scheme,
developed and documented under him), and his celebrated correspondence with **Pliny** as governor
of Bithynia (the model of conscientious provincial administration, and the earliest official
exchange on the **Christians**). His reign marked the **apogee of the Principate's expansion and
stability** — and the limit of expansion: his successor **[[hadrian]]** at once **abandoned the
eastern conquests**, recognizing the empire had over-reached. Trajan's adoption (real or
arranged on his deathbed by Plotina) of Hadrian continued the adoptive succession.

## Beard (SPQR, 2015)

Mary Beard, in **[[sources/beard-spqr-2015|SPQR]]**, reads the Trajan–Pliny correspondence
from Bithynia as evidence of a genuinely new imperial administrative style: governors as
officials directly answerable to the emperor, with Trajan himself as the sole
decision-point of a thinly staffed provincial bureaucracy — since, on Beard's reading,
**[[pliny-the-younger|Pliny]]** "got no decision from the emperor unless [he] asked him
for one." She also flags the pair's exchange on the Christians (10.96–97) as "the
earliest surviving discussion of Christianity outside Jewish or Christian literature."
