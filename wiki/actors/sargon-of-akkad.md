# Sargon of Akkad

The founder of the [[akkadian-empire|Akkadian Empire]] (*Sharrum-kin*, "the king
is legitimate/true") and the first empire-builder in recorded history — "vast but
dim to later generations of Babylonians hardly less than to us." With Sargon, CAH
notes, "the historical memory of the people was enriched with its most abiding
treasure": he became the model of the world-conqueror for two thousand years,
his name proudly reused by Assyrian kings. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XIX, C. J. Gadd).

## The legend of his origins

Sargon is the first ruler around whom a **mysterious-origin legend** crystallized
— the prototype of a long line (Moses, Cyrus, Romulus). In a text cast as his own
words, he was the secret child of a priestess and an unknown father, born at
**Azupiranu** ("Saffron Town") on the Euphrates; his mother set him adrift in a
pitch-sealed basket; he was rescued by **Aqqi the water-drawer**, raised as a
gardener, favoured by the goddess **Ishtar**, and rose to be **cupbearer to
Ur-Zababa, king of Kish**, before seizing power. The details are legendary, but
they encode a genuine memory of **humble, Semitic, upper-Euphrates origins** and a
usurper's rise from the Kish court.

## The conquest of Sumer

Sargon's decisive act was the overthrow of **Lugalzaggisi of Umma/Uruk**, who had
briefly unified "the land" from the Lower to the Upper Sea (the
[[early-dynastic-sumer|culmination of the Sumerian city-state era]]). Sargon
stormed Uruk, "destroyed its wall," defeated Lugalzaggisi and the host of "fifty
governors," and led him in a neck-yoke to the gate of Enlil at **Nippur**. He then
reduced Ur, Lagash (E-Ninmar), and Umma in turn — "washing his weapons in the
sea" — claiming **thirty-four battles** and mastery of the whole Sumerian
homeland, with the Persian Gulf trade (Tilmun, Magan, Meluhha) now mooring at his
quays.

## The wider conquests

- **The west:** a march up the Euphrates to **Tuttul (Hit)**, where Dagan "gave
  him the upper land — Mari, Iarmuti, Ebla — up to the cedar forest (Amanus) and
  the silver mountains (Taurus)." The interest was in **timber and metals**.
- **The northwest:** the legend *King of the Battle* has Sargon march deep into
  Anatolia to **Purushkhanda** (near Kayseri) to protect Akkadian merchants —
  prefiguring the historical [[kultepe|Old Assyrian trade colonies]] four
  centuries later. Whether historical or retrojected, it shows the early
  importance of the Anatolian metal market.
- **The east:** campaigns against **Elam**, **Barakhshe**, and **Awan**, taking
  tribute and leaving his sole surviving original monument at **Susa**; and
  northward over **Subartu** (including the future Assyria — Nineveh and Ashur).

## End of the reign

Tradition holds that Sargon's old age was "clouded": "all the lands revolted
against him" and "beset him in Agade," but the old warrior still "cast them in
heaps." Later moralizing chronicles blame a **sacrilege** — building Agade too
near, or too like, holy Babylon — for the wrath that would eventually fall on his
house. He was succeeded by his sons **Rimush** and **Manishtusu**.

## Liverani: the honest conqueror who broke the networks

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] stresses how candid Sargon's own
inscriptions are about the **limits** of his reach: he held political control (the *ensi*
office) only "from Tuttul to the Persian Gulf," conceding that Elam and Mari remained
independent, while his **commercial** network stretched far wider, "from the Mediterranean
to Magan and Meluhha." The distinction between a modest political core and a long commercial
reach is, for Liverani, the key to the Akkadian system. He also argues Sargon's
interventionism was **self-defeating**: by destroying [[mari|Mari]] (~a decade after
[[ebla|Ebla's]] fall) he triggered the crisis of the Syrian commercial network and the rise
of the nomadic groups that eventually collapsed it. The later tale *King of Combat* (Sargon's
march to Purushhanda) he reads not as evidence of early Sargonic trade but as a **didactic
model for Old Assyrian commerce** — its moral being that a ruler should follow divine omens
over the cautious advice of merchants (see [[old-assyrian-trade]] and
[[ancient-near-east-historiography]]).

## Counterfactual significance

Sargon is the hinge between the **city-state world** and the **age of empire**.
Without his fusion of the Semitic north and Sumerian south into one militarized
territorial state, the very idea of universal kingship — "king of the four
regions," inherited by Ur III, Babylon, and Assyria — might have had no early
exemplar. His career also fixed the **dynastic-conquest narrative** (and the
foundling-hero legend) as enduring cultural templates. Yet the speed with which
Sumer revolted at each accession shows how **contingent and shallow** the
integration was — an empire of conquest atop an unreconciled population.

## Historiography

Almost nothing of Sargon's reign can be dated or sequenced: there are no
date-formulae, his inscriptions survive as **Nippur copies**, and the rest is
**omen-lore and legend** of much later composition, "having a didactic bent."
Gadd treats the legendary material as "based upon authentic tradition" but not
literal. The figure is therefore historically secure in outline (the conquest of
Sumer, the new capital, the empire) and almost wholly uncertain in detail. See
[[mesopotamian-chronology]] and [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
