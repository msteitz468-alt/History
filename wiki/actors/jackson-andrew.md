# Andrew Jackson (1767–1845)

Seventh president of the United States (1829–1837), general (New Orleans,
1815), and namesake of Jacksonian democracy — the mass democratic politics
of the 1820s–40s. This page currently reflects Hartz's analysis of the
Jacksonian movement in *The Liberal Tradition in America*
([[hartz-liberal-tradition-1955]]); a fuller narrative treatment awaits a
dedicated source.

## Hartz's American democrat: "Hercules with the brain of a Hamlet"

The Jacksonian democrat is, for Hartz, an unprecedented social hybrid: the
peasant transformed into a capitalist farmer and the laborer into an
incipient entrepreneur — "a great new democratic hybrid unknown in any
other land, America's unique contribution to the political culture of the
West" (Tocqueville: what astonishes in America is "the innumerable
multitude of small" undertakings). Because he is simultaneously landed
man, factory man, and forge man, he is immune to every European political
insult — neither cringing shopkeeper, backward peasant, dangerous
proletarian, nor landed aristocrat: "There is... always something in the
American democrat which redeems him from something else." Hartz:
"Whatever might be said about Jackson, he is not a Benjamin Disraeli; and
whatever might be said about Leggett, he is something short of a Friedrich
Lasalle."

**The transplant thought-experiment.** Set down in 1830s England, Webster
would flourish "almost as much as Macaulay"; Jackson "would not have known
where to stand" — not with Disraeli and the land, not with Cobbett and the
workers — "a lost giant from another world... a massive misfit in the old
society of Europe." Giant power riven by inner contradiction: "not quite a
Hercules but a Hercules with the brain of a Hamlet."

**Structural role.** By absorbing into one personality the groups European
Whiggery played off against each other, the American democrat "took the
props from under Whiggery and left it helpless in a liberal world" — then,
sharing Whiggery's "individualist fear" and "capitalist lust," was
re-conquered by the democratized Whiggery of 1840 and after: "the tables
of the American liberal community were turned upon the American democrat"
(see [[lockean-consensus]]).

**Majority rule and the courts.** Jacksonian attacks on judicial review
(Taney for Marshall) were real but shallow — neurotic fear of majority
tyranny in "one of the tamest, mildest, and most unimaginative majorities
in modern political history," never actually threatening the
constitutional fetish.

**The Bank War as theory vs. practice.** The hard-money assault on
Biddle's Bank was driven (per Bray Hammond, cited by Hartz) by Western
farmers and local bankers who wanted to *expand* credit — acquisitive
democracy overwhelming credit control. Orestes Brownson's 1840
disillusionment — discovering that "the people" themselves had built the
paper-money system — drove him toward conservatism and Catholicism, an
inverted mirror of Europe's post-1789 conservative flight (there caused by
too much radicalism; here by too little).

## Indian wars and Removal (Green, CHNPA I.1 ch. 8)

Hartz’s frame omits Native dispossession; Michael D. Green’s synthesis supplies the military and policy core.

**Creek War / Horseshoe Bend.** As Tennessee/Georgia/Mississippi militia commander with Choctaw, Cherokee, and Creek accommodationist allies, Jackson crushed Creek Nativists after **Fort Mims** (Aug 1813: Nativist slaughter of “civilized” Creeks and families). **March 1814 Horseshoe Bend:** some **800** Nativist warriors with prophets killed. **August 1814** dictated peace: forced cession of **twenty million acres** (including Alabama Black Belt cotton lands). Combined with [[tecumseh]]’s death (1813), shattered effective Indian military power resisting Appalachian-crossing settlement for the first time since the 1760s. **First Seminole War 1818:** Jackson led Army + Creek warriors into Spanish Florida.

**Sovereignty challenge (post-1815).** As southern military district commander (early 1817), attacked Indian sovereignty: reject “absurd” treaty system, legislate boundaries—Indians subjects not sovereigns. Congress still adhered to treaties. Calhoun/Monroe shared pressure; Monroe clung to expansion-with-honor language while doubting community independence.

**President and Removal.** Election 1828; first annual message **8 Dec 1829** aimed at Cherokee constitutional republic: only options emigrate beyond Mississippi or submit to Georgia/Alabama laws; emigration should be “voluntary” yet remainers “must be subject to their law.” Refused federal enforcement of protective treaty provisions against **state jurisdiction laws**—treaties defend from private acts not sovereign state power. With federal connivance, states made life miserable enough to force “voluntary” treaties (Creek Council cave-in winter 1831–2; Alabama terror including grave robbery and starvation). **[[indian-removal-act-1830]]:** House 102–97, Senate 28–19, signed **28 May 1830**. Operational priority: five large southern nations—[[choctaw-nation|Choctaws]], [[chickasaw-nation|Chickasaws]], [[creek-muscogee|Creeks]], [[cherokee-nation|Cherokees]], [[seminole|Seminoles]]. Franklin TN Aug 1830: only Chickasaws attended. Aggregate aim: expel **>100,000** eastern Indians. Non-enforcement of [[worcester-v-georgia-1832]]; path to Treaty of [[new-echota]] and [[trail-of-tears-1838]] (roundup under Van Buren but within Jacksonian policy). **[[second-seminole-war-1835]]** and **[[black-hawk-war-1832]]** on the same regime.

**Green’s interpretive note (do not erase violence):** U.S. policy “was not designed to exterminate Native Americans, but it was created to meet the needs and wishes… of its citizens.” Stockades, forced marches, Fort Mims/Horseshoe Bend death tolls, Choctaw ~2,500 removal deaths, and Seminole War costs remain full-fidelity facts under that policy complex.

## Historiography

Jacksonian democracy is a classic battleground: Schlesinger's *Age of
Jackson* (1945) as proto-New-Deal class politics; Hartz's consensus
reading (above) as intra-liberal faction fight; Hammond's entrepreneurial
thesis; later ethnocultural and, more recently, settler-colonial/Indian
removal-centered readings that put dispossession and slavery, absent from
Hartz's frame, at the center. Washburn & Trigger note Schlesinger celebrated democratic spirit without discussing Jackson’s Indian dealings; Bancroft praised Jackson for planning removal so land east of the Mississippi passed to “exclusive possession of cultivated man.” Green, Satz, Wallace (*Long, Bitter Trail*), Prucha, and Foreman are core Removal guides.
