# The Empire of Nicaea and the Byzantine Recovery (1204–1261)

The **empire of Nicaea** was the most successful of the Byzantine **successor states in
exile** after the [[fourth-crusade-1204|Fourth Crusade]], and the regime that recovered
**Constantinople** in 1261. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Michael Angold, Ch. 16b)
treats the fall of the City as a "cosmic cataclysm" that destroyed "the myth of Byzantine
invulnerability" — and shaped the exile ideology that would haunt the restored empire.

## Byzantium in Exile

Three successor states emerged: **Trebizond** (the Grand Komnenoi), the despotate of
**Epiros** (Michael Doukas), and **Nicaea** in western Anatolia. At Nicaea **Theodore I
Laskaris**, having secured the Bithynian cities, had himself acclaimed emperor and crowned by
a newly elected Orthodox patriarch (1208) — recreating "a Byzantine empire in exile." His
successor **John III Vatatzes** (1222–1254) reduced the Latin hold in Anatolia, took
**Thessalonica** (1246), and built a prosperous, well-governed state. The historian **Niketas
Choniates** and the polemicist **Constantine Stilbes** forged an **ideology of exile**:
intensely **anti-Latin**, casting exile as punishment for sin (the Babylonian captivity), the
defence of **Orthodoxy** against papal primacy and the crusade as the empire's sacred task.
Power, meanwhile, became **localised** (immunities, *pronoiai*, urban privileges) — a
devolution the restored empire could never reverse.

## Michael VIII Palaiologos and the Recovery of 1261

**Michael VIII Palaiologos** seized the Nicaean throne in 1258 (usurping and blinding the
boy-emperor John IV Laskaris) and, on **25 July 1261**, his general entered Constantinople
"by ruse" while the Venetian fleet was away — ending the [[latin-empire-of-constantinople|Latin
empire]]. The recovery rested on the **[[republic-of-genoa|Genoese alliance]]** of the **Treaty
of Nymphaion (1261)**, which expelled the Venetians and gave Genoa Pera. Hailed as the "New
Constantine," Michael restored the City, rebuilt the fleet, and conducted a diplomacy
stretching "from Aragon and France in the west to the Il-khans of Persia."

## The Tragedy of the Restoration

Angold argues Michael "was never able to capitalise on these early successes." His
**autocratic** style and **usurpation** alienated the Church (the **Arsenite schism**); and to
fend off **[[charles-of-anjou|Charles of Anjou's]]** planned crusade against Constantinople he
imposed the **Union of Lyons (1274)**, accepting papal supremacy. This "earned him the hatred
of all sections of Byzantine society" — for the exile years had made any compromise with the
Latins feel like betrayal ("You have become Franks!"). Salvation came not from his diplomacy
but from the **[[sicilian-vespers-1282|Sicilian Vespers]]** (1282), which forced Charles to
abandon his eastern expedition. Michael died in 1282 excommunicated by his own Church, which
refused him burial; his legacy was "schism, poverty and rapid decline."

## Significance

Nicaea preserved Byzantine statehood and Orthodoxy through exile and won back the capital, but
"the city that Michael Palaiologos recovered was but a husk." The restored Palaiologan empire
remained "a conglomeration of independent or semi-independent political units" — Epiros and
Thessaly unreconciled, Anatolia alienated and soon lost to the Turks (including the nascent
**Ottoman** beylicate). 1204 had broken the centralising prestige of Constantinople for good.
