# Gregory A. Daddis

**Gregory A. Daddis** (b. 1967) is a U.S. Army colonel (retired), military historian, and professor specializing in the Vietnam War and American strategy. He is a leading voice in the revisionist historiography that challenges the dominant post-war narrative of U.S. failure in Vietnam as primarily a story of strategic incompetence, institutional rigidity, and a narrow focus on attrition.

## Major Works

- *Westmoreland's War: Reassessing American Strategy in Vietnam* (Oxford University Press, 2014) — his most influential book. Argues that General William C. Westmoreland and MACV developed and implemented a comprehensive campaign strategy that included conventional operations against NVA forces, development of the Army of the Republic of Vietnam (ARVN), and a substantial pacification/"parallel war" effort. Directly contests the "myth of attrition" and the portrayal of Westmoreland as a one-dimensional body-count general.
- *No Sure Victory: Measuring U.S. Army Effectiveness and Progress in the Vietnam War* (Oxford, 2011) — examines the metrics and reporting systems used by the U.S. Army and shows that MACV tracked far more than simple body counts.

## Key Arguments (from *Westmoreland's War*)

Daddis maintains that the standard critique (often associated with Andrew Krepinevich, John Nagl, and others) oversimplifies a complex political-military campaign. This work "is a work of historical revisionism." "In truth, I undertook this project because historical evidence continually forced me to question the traditional storyline..." Questions driving the book: "If army officers cared only about killing the enemy, why were 'body counts' supplemented with metrics that measured population security, economic development, political stability, and scores of nonmilitary programs? Was it possible that Americans understood that victory or defeat in Southeast Asia turned on more than just military factors? Was it possible that US strategy in Vietnam was more than just one of attrition?" (Preface)

Westmoreland faced three simultaneous missions:
1. Containing the conventional threat posed by North Vietnamese Army units.
2. Building and advising the RVNAF/ARVN so that South Vietnam could ultimately defend itself (Ch. 6: ARVN as pillar for RD support; "bulk of the military support of Revolutionary Development must rest on the shoulders of the ARVN"; MATs; diverse concurrent missions; leadership/desertion/corruption barriers; "training an uncertain army").
3. Pacifying the countryside and protecting the rural population (the "parallel war").

He shows that MACV used a wide array of metrics — Hamlet Evaluation System (HES), security indicators, economic and political development measures — alongside body counts. "Central to this work’s argument is the contention that Westmoreland not only devised a sound military strategy..." "It is possible... to devise a sound strategy and still lose." While acknowledging real shortcomings in execution and the ultimate limits of American power in a post-colonial revolutionary environment, Daddis insists that Westmoreland's strategy was far more comprehensive than the "attrition" label allows. "Talented American generals can develop and implement a comprehensive political-military strategy and still lose a war." (Preface, Introduction)

Ch. 6 details the advisory effort's focus on making the war Vietnamese; Conclusion synthesizes that even comprehensive strategy confronted insurmountable nation-building and post-colonial obstacles (dependence created; "no magic solution"; "attempted to do too much" vs. Korea precedent). Range 01 grounding also details the Cold War doctrinal context (containment, decolonization, Maoist theory, flexible response, broad counterinsurgency doctrine) and Westmoreland's personal role advancing COIN education at West Point that informed his command. (Ch. 1)
