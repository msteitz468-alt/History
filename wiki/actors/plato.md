# Plato and the Academy

## Overview

**Plato** (c. 428–348/7 BCE), the Athenian philosopher and founder of the **Academy**, is
the towering intellectual figure of the fourth century — the thinker whose **theory of
Forms** and whose school did more than anything to ensure that, in
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]]'s words (Preface; Ch. 12a, Ostwald & Lynch), "**the
formation of the great schools assured to Athens in her political decline a future as a
cultural centre** which was to last physically for 900 years and intellectually a great
deal longer." His career also exemplifies the volume's theme that **Sicily was the
laboratory of Greek political experience**: "much of Plato's political experience is
Sicilian experience."

## From Socrates to the Academy

A young aristocrat repelled from politics by the violence of the
**[[thirty-tyrants|Thirty]]** (whose leader Critias was his kinsman) and then by the
democracy's **execution of Socrates (399)**, Plato made philosophy his life's work. His
earliest dialogues (*Apology*, *Crito*, *Ion*) preserve the Socratic conception of
philosophy as "**tendance for one's soul**," exercised through *elenchos*
(cross-examination) and the Socratic paradoxes (no one does wrong willingly; it is better
to suffer than to do wrong; virtue is knowledge). Around **387** he founded the **Academy**
in a grove outside Athens — not a chartered university but an informal **community
(*synousia*)** of inquirers under Plato as **scholarch**, who "determined its structure and
set problems." Mathematics was central (the later motto "let no one ignorant of geometry
enter"); no orthodoxy was imposed (the mathematician **Eudoxus** and Plato's own successor
**Speusippus** both dissented from his doctrines). Ostwald and Lynch stress that the
Academy was conceived in deliberate **rivalry with Isocrates' school** of rhetoric — the
*Gorgias* attacks rhetoric as a mere "knack" of flattering the ignorant mob, against
Isocrates' practical *politikoi logoi*.

## The theory of Forms

Plato's central doctrine, developed across the *Meno*, *Phaedo*, *Symposium* and
*Republic*, holds that beyond the changing objects of sense there exist eternal,
intelligible **Forms** (*eide*) — "the just itself," "the equal itself," "the beautiful
itself" — which alone are truly knowable and in which physical things "participate."
Knowledge is **recollection (*anamnesis*)** of Forms the soul knew before birth. The
*Republic* crowns the theory with the **Form of the Good**, "beyond being," the source of
both the intelligibility and the existence of all else — the foundation of Plato's
metaphysics, epistemology, psychology, ethics and politics, including the famous proposal
that philosophers should be kings.

## Sicily and the limits of the philosopher-king

Plato three times crossed to **Syracuse**, drawn by his friend **Dion** and the hope of
educating a ruler into a philosopher-king — first at the court of **[[dionysius-i|Dionysius
I]]** (c. 388) and twice under **[[dionysius-ii|Dionysius II]]**. The ventures ended in
disillusionment and danger (the *Seventh Letter* is the chief witness), and Plato withdrew
from active politics, judging that "nothing could be done without suitable friends and
associates." The Sicilian failure shadows the political philosophy of the *Republic* and
the *Laws*.

## Significance

Plato created the institutional form of the **philosophical school** and posed the
questions — about knowledge, justice, the soul, the ideal state — that have defined Western
philosophy ever since. Through his pupil **[[aristotle]]** and the Academy's nine-century
life, his thought became, with Aristotle's, the intellectual core of the **Hellenistic,
Roman, Islamic and medieval** worlds. He is the supreme instance of the fourth-century
paradox: as Athens declined politically, it rose to lasting cultural and intellectual
supremacy.
