# The Merovingian Army

**Not a standing army but a militarized society in arms.** Laury Sarti's chapter (Oxford Handbook,
Ch. 12) overturns the older model of a professional Frankish military (Bachrach 1972), arguing that
"the Merovingian military consisted of temporary levies recruited and led by local officers, while
more permanent services were much smaller and restricted to the *civitates* and the royal courts."
The army is best understood not as an institution apart but as the armed face of Frankish society.

## Composition and Recruitment

Roman military pay ended gradually in fifth-century Gaul (Eugippius's *Life of Severinus* records
soldiers' stipends that "never returned"). Thereafter forces were "recruited ad hoc, straight from
the local population… only for the time of a specific purpose or campaign." [[clovis|Clovis]] and his
sons commanded personally; from the third generation command passed to *patricii* (Burgundy,
Provence), *duces*, and *comites*, with civitas-level levies averaging perhaps **2,000 men**. The host
was multiethnic — "Alamanni, Armoricans, Bretons, Burgundians, Goths, Saxons, Taïfals, and
Thuringians" alongside Franks and Romans. Not all men served at once: a Formulary of Angers document
has a father thank his son for serving "in my place to fight the Bretons and Gascons," proving
substitution.

## Obligation, Pay, and the Problem of "Soldier"

Military obligation was enforced by the **herbannum**, a fine for failing to join the royal host
(Ribuarian Law 68; a 694 *placitum* fines one Ibbo 600 solidi). Its legal basis is debated — Bachrach
sees continued Roman land obligations, Goffart and Esders unpaid *munera/servitia*, Halsall an
identity-based liability to the king. Service was **unpaid**, rewarded by "royal thanks, renown, and
a share of the booty." Sarti's sharpest argument is lexical: there was no standard word for "soldier."
*Armatus* meant any armed man; the term ***miles***, in sixth- to early-eighth-century Gaul, referred
"only to men in charge of the supervision and execution of prisoners… inside a *civitas*, never to men
on a military campaign" — because "every man could be considered as a potential fighter," a
specialized designation was superfluous.

## A Militarized Society

The military was not segregated in camps but "integrated into the civilian population." Armies "lived
on local resources, which included raids committed on their own domestic population"; weapons recur in
ritual and judicial contexts (the *antrustiones*' oath sworn on their own weapons; the shield in
judicial assembly; Guntram placing a spear in Childeric II's hand "to symbolize transfer of *regnum*").
By the seventh century *pax* had shrunk from "long-lasting absence of military activity" to a "short
time lacking violence." Weapon burials, Sarti cautions, express social identity and aspiration (see
[[gender-in-merovingian-gaul]], [[merovingian-childhood]]) more than they document real war.

## The Military Elite and Episcopal Militarization

The secular elite was defined by its military role: *patricius*, *dux*, and *comes* combined military,
administrative, and advisory functions, making the military elite "involved to a significant extent in
the core functionalities of Merovingian governance" (see [[merovingian-polity]]). Bishops too bore
arms: Nicetius of Trier built a fortified *castrum*, the brothers Salonius and Sagittarius fought the
Lombards (and were condemned), and by the seventh century many bishops had been *dux* or *comes* before
ordination (see [[merovingian-episcopate]]).

## Counterfactual Significance

Sarti's revision matters for the larger argument of the Handbook: a Frankish army that was a temporary
levy of a militarized populace, not a professional successor to the Roman army, explains both the
kingdom's striking military reach (Italy, Septimania, the Avar frontier) and its lack of a standing
fiscal-military apparatus — and undercuts attempts to read the Franks as merely "an evolved form of
the late Roman army."
