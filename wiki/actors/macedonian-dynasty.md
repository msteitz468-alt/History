# The Macedonian Dynasty (Byzantium, 867–1056)

The **Macedonian dynasty** presided over the political, military, and cultural apogee of medieval [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]]. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Jonathan Shepard, in two chapters) traces its arc across the long tenth century from a **defensive "equilibrium" (886–1025)** under Leo VI and Constantine VII to an aggressive **expansion (944–1025)** under the soldier-emperors and Basil II — for Byzantium, the period was "as much a golden age as an age of iron."

## Equilibrium (886–c. 960)

Under **Leo VI "the Wise"** (886–912) the empire was on the defensive: Shepard stresses Leo's **law-books and manuals of governance** (the *Novels*, the *Book of the Eparch* regulating Constantinople's guilds, the *Tactica*) and a strategy that was essentially defensive against incessant Arab sea- and land-raiding (the sack of Thessalonica, 904; the Cappadocian cave-refuges). After the regency and the usurpation-by-marriage of the admiral **Romanos I Lekapenos**, **Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus** (sole rule 945–959) embodied the dynasty's **scholar-emperor** ideal — commissioning the *De administrando imperio*, the *Book of Ceremonies*, and the *De cerimoniis* — projecting a static, "Solomonic" kingship in which the palace's accumulated learning and ceremonial mystique screened the throne against usurpers.

## Expansion (c. 960–1025)

The catalyst was the Hamdanid emir **Sayf al-Dawla** of Aleppo, whose raids provoked Constantine VII to assemble a larger army and appoint the general **Nikephoros Phokas**, who **reconquered Crete (961)** — ending two centuries of Arab piracy in the Aegean — and stormed Aleppo (962). The military momentum proved irresistible: Nikephoros (as **Nikephoros II Phokas**, 963–969) took Cilicia, Cyprus, and **Antioch (969)**; **John I Tzimiskes** (969–976) campaigned into Syria and Palestine and crushed the Rus′ in Bulgaria; and **[[basil-ii|Basil II]]** (976–1025) brought the empire to its greatest extent since the Arab conquests, annexing **[[conquest-of-bulgaria-1018|Bulgaria (1018)]]** and pushing the frontier to the Danube, the Euphrates, and Armenia.

## Social Tension: "the Powerful" and "the Poor"

A central theme of the dynasty's legislation was the effort to protect **small peasant proprietors and "military lands"** (the fiscal base of the theme armies) against engrossment by **"the powerful"** (*dynatoi*) — the great military aristocratic houses (Phokas, Skleros, Maleinos) whose estates and private retinues were expanding. Romanos I, Constantine VII, and above all Basil II issued *novels* against the *dynatoi*; the struggle between the central state and the provincial military magnates runs through the whole period and prefigures the eleventh-century crisis.

## Significance

The Macedonian century made Byzantium again the dominant power of the eastern Mediterranean and Balkans, a beacon of law, learning, and ceremonial monarchy, and the missionary metropolis that drew **[[kievan-rus|Rus′]]** (988), Bulgaria, and the southern Slavs into the Orthodox world. Its apogee under Basil II was followed, within fifty years, by the rapid unravelling of the eleventh century (Manzikert, 1071) — making the dynasty's reign the high-water mark of medieval Byzantine power.
