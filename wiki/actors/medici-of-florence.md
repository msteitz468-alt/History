# The Medici of Florence

The banking family that dominated fifteenth-century Florence without formally abolishing its
republic, treated by Michael Mallett in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 23a) and Wendy Childs
(Ch. 7). Their ascendancy — Cosimo, Piero and Lorenzo — was, in Mallett's reading, less a princely
coup than an **alliance between the family and a broad cross-section of the Florentine elite**,
working through the manipulation of an ostensibly republican constitution.

## The Ascendancy

- **Cosimo de' Medici** (returned from exile, 1434) became "the acknowledged leader of the regime,"
  the *primus inter pares* of a generation of politicians who had weathered the war years. His
  banking fortune (the [[early-modern-commerce|Medici bank]], with branches across Europe and the
  papal account) and "consciously populist attitude" let him defeat the **Albizzi** oligarchy.
- **The machinery of control.** Power was secured not by abolishing republican forms but by
  distorting them: permanent ***accoppiatori*** controlling the sortition bags, emergency councils
  (***balìe***), and after 1454 new permanent bodies (the Council of One Hundred, 1458; the Council
  of Seventy, 1480) that "absorbed oligarchic trends into constitutional forms."
- **Lorenzo "the Magnificent"** succeeded as a youth of twenty (1469) yet established an authority
  exceeding Cosimo's — partly because, "in a world increasingly dominated by princes," he was bound
  to be seen and to behave somewhat like one, while the restraints of Florentine custom and his own
  political intelligence held the tendency in check.

## Role in the Italian System

The Medici were central to the **[[italian-renaissance-state-system|Italian balance of power]]**.
Cosimo's financial and diplomatic backing made **Francesco Sforza** duke of Milan (1450) and forged
the lasting **Florence–Milan entente** behind the Peace of Lodi (1454). The **Pazzi conspiracy**
(1478) — backed by Pope Sixtus IV — murdered Lorenzo's brother Giuliano but failed; the ensuing
**Pazzi War** (1478–80) against papal–Neapolitan forces was resolved by Lorenzo's bold personal
**journey to Naples** (1479), restoring the Milan–Florence–Naples axis. His later ascendancy over
Pope Innocent VIII (his son Giovanni made cardinal — the future Leo X) gave the years before 1492
their deceptive "serenity" (Guicciardini).

## Significance and End

The Medici embodied the broader Florentine drift from **commune to oligarchy** and the fusion of
political dominance with **Renaissance cultural patronage** (the great library, the patronage of
artists and humanists — Ficino's circle). Yet their rule rested on faction and constitutional
manipulation, breeding "political disillusionment with oligarchy." On Lorenzo's death (1492) his son
**Piero** inherited "a new and dangerous situation"; his mishandling of the French invasion led to the
Medici's **expulsion from Florence in 1494** (and Savonarola's republic), though the family would
return.
