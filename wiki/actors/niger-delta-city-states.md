# The Niger Delta City-States (Bonny, Kalabari, Okrika, Nembe)

The **Niger Delta city-states** — **Bonny**, **Elem Kalabari**, **Okrika**, and **Nembe** — were
**Ijo (Ijaw)** trading polities of the eastern Niger Delta mangrove swamps that turned the
forbidding environment of creeks and salt-water swamp into the brokerage hub of the eastern
**[[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]]**. They are the wiki's clearest worked example of
the trade-driven [[state-formation|transformation of a stateless society into a state]] — the very
case **Robin Horton** drew on for his model (see [[stateless-societies]]). Documented for the wiki
through E. J. Alagoa's chapter in [[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of West
Africa* Vol. I]] (1971).

## Origins

Ijo monarchy is **ancient** — Nembe's first kings reigned c. 1400, and the Portuguese captain
Pereira already noted a trading "large village of some 2,000 inhabitants" (probably Bonny) c. 1500.
The founding traditions derive the states from migrations out of the **central delta** (some
looping through the Ibo hinterland before turning back to the coast). Scattered **bronze objects**
and traditions of obtaining titles at **[[benin-kingdom|Benin]]** show early contact, but — like
the Tiv apprenticing chiefs to the Jukun — this was prestige-borrowing, **not subjection**: even
the western delta lay only on the edge of the Benin polity. Small bands of **Itsekiri** migrants
left religious marks (Nembe's war-god Ogidiga).

## From fishing village to trading state

The states are famous under two labels: **K. O. Dike's "city-states"** (a central city radiating
authority to hinterland trading stations) and **G. I. Jones's "trading states."** Alagoa accepts
both but insists on the **Ijo cultural base**: citizenship shifted from *descent* to *residence*,
yet the decisive qualification remained **acculturation** to the state's variety of Ijo culture.
The ordinary eastern-delta fishing village was governed by an assembly under an *amanyanabo*
("owner of the town"), a ritual head little more than the *pere* of the western delta. The
**Atlantic trade transformed this office into a monarchy**: the *amanyanabo* became the people's
representative to the European traders, who paid him **comey** (protection money) and channelled
**credit ("trust")** through him — exactly Horton's mechanism for the birth of kingship from trade.

## The War Canoe House

The most original institution was the **House (*wari*)** — the old Ijo lineage segment
**re-engineered as an "open" corporation** for trade and war. An ambitious man built a following by
marriage, clientage, and above all by **buying slaves**; when wealthy enough he equipped a **war
canoe** (~30 fighting men with guns) and was recognised as a **House head** and member of the
King's Council. Crucially, **succession to House headship went by capacity in trade and leadership
and the vote of adult members — not heredity** — so that **a slave could rise to head a House**.
The House actively **assimilated** its imported slaves: ritual shaving and a new name to symbolise
rebirth, a senior woman as "mother," and induction into prestige societies — the **ekine /
sekiapu** masquerade society (advancement by artistic skill and mastery of the drum-language) and
the warriors' *peri ogbo*. The result was a genuinely **meritocratic, open** social order built, in
a grim paradox, on the slave trade that fed it.

## Significance

The delta states show, in miniature and with unusual documentary clarity, the volume's central
thesis: **commerce, not conquest, makes states in West Africa**, and the transition from
stateless to state organisation is one of **degree, not rupture** (see [[state-formation]],
[[stateless-societies]]). They also expose the human core of the [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic
trade]]: the same institution that turned slaves into citizens within the delta depended on
shipping other captives — drawn largely from the **Ibo (Igbo) hinterland** — across the ocean.

## Historiography

Their history is reconstructed by combining **Ijo oral tradition and kinglists** (dated by Alagoa's
average ~21-year reign-length method) with **European trade records** (Pereira c. 1500; Dapper and
Barbot in the 17th c., who name kings and "trading chiefs"). Alagoa, a pioneer of delta oral
history, treats the 1600–1800 window as the formative period of the states' distinctive
institutions.
