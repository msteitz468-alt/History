# Aeschylus

First of the three great Athenian tragedians; veteran of the [[greco-persian-wars-499bce|Persian Wars]]; author of the only surviving complete trilogy, the ***Oresteia*** (458 BCE).

## Role

Seven complete plays survive (many scholars now doubt *Prometheus Bound* is his). Only from him do we have a complete trilogy, the ***Oresteia***; *Agamemnon* has the most powerful impact of any ancient tragedy and opened the 458 festival when he was about sixty-seven (two more years to live). Fragments show satyr-play humour and surprising treatments (e.g. Achilles and Patroclus as full homosexual lovers); he was champion of the **satyr-play** genre (*Net-Fishers* fragments).

***The Persians*** (472) makes the Persian king the tragic hero of Salamis — only losers fit tragic form for a Greek victory; Aeschylus, likely an eyewitness, spares none of the massacre; his epitaph wanted remembrance only as a Marathon hoplite. In ***Eumenides***, scene shifts (most unusually) to Delphi then Athens; Athena institutes the most venerable Athenian law court and, by reversing an ancient cursing formula, converts the Furies into Eumenides (“kindly ones”) — a political blessing on Athens with the weight of the trilogy behind it.

## Significance

Aeschylus fixed tragedy as Homeric justice on the civic stage (“crumbs from the great table of [[homer|Homer]]”) and linked Persian-war memory to democratic institutions. Counterfactual without him: no complete trilogy model, thinner bridge from epic to classical theatre.

## Historiography

Levi ([[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW, 1986]], Ch. 7) is literary-essayistic; attribution debates (esp. *Prometheus*) and performance reconstruction continue.
