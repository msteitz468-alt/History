# Funan and Zhenla (the Pre-Angkorian Khmer States)

**Funan** and **Zhenla** are the names (from the Chinese annals) for the **pre-Angkorian states** of
the lower Mekong — the [[indianization-of-southeast-asia|Indianized]] delta chiefdoms and kingdoms
from which the [[khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] of Angkor would later emerge. Documented for the wiki
through Michael D. Coe's [[coe-angkor-khmer-civilization-2018|*Angkor and the Khmer Civilization*]]
(2003).

## Funan and the Oc Eo maritime culture

The first phase, known to the Chinese as **"Funan,"** centred on the **Mekong delta** and its great
port-and-entrepôt **Oc Eo** (excavated by the French from 1942). Oc Eo was a planned, canal-bisected
town tied into the **Indian Ocean trade** that linked the Mediterranean, India, and China: among its
finds are **Roman gold medallions** (one of the emperor Antoninus Pius), Indian seals and Sanskrit
finger-rings, glassware, and the figures of **Vishnu, Shiva, and Brahma** in gold leaf. It was here,
through resident **Indian merchants**, that the **earliest writing** of the Khmer area (in a
Sanskrit-derived script) and the first **Hindu cults** took hold — the concrete beginning of the
Indianization Coe traces. (Funan is the polity Indian tradition associated with the brahman
**Kaundinya**; see [[indianization-of-southeast-asia]].)

## The inland shift to Zhenla

From around the 6th century the centre of gravity **shifted inland** from the maritime delta towns to
the **Zhenla** kingdoms of the Chinese annals — "highly stratified" Indianized states along the middle
Mekong and around the Great Lake. Their kings (Bhavavarman, Chitrasena/Mahendravarman,
**Ishanavarman**) built the first great brick temple-complexes — above all **Sambor Prei Kuk**
(ancient **Ishanapura**, the 7th-century capital), whose east-facing towers prefigure Angkor. Royal
religion centred on **Shiva** (in the form of the **linga**), **Vishnu** and his avatars, and the
combined deity **Harihara** (Vishnu-and-Shiva) — the sacral-kingship cult that would mature into the
Angkorian **devaraja**.

## Significance

Funan and Zhenla are the **foundation** of Khmer (and mainland Southeast Asian) civilization: the
moment when [[indian-ocean-trade|maritime trade]] and Indian religion, script, and statecraft were
selectively adopted by delta societies to build the first kingdoms — the local seedbed from which
[[khmer-empire|Angkor]] grew. They are the wiki's earliest worked example of the
[[indianization-of-southeast-asia|Indianization process]] on the ground.

## Historiography

Known through **Chinese annals**, **Sanskrit/Khmer inscriptions**, and **archaeology** (Oc Eo; Sambor
Prei Kuk), reconstructed by the French **EFEO** tradition (Cœdès) that Coe synthesises. The labels
"Funan" and "Zhenla" are Chinese exonyms imposed on what was a shifting mosaic of polities, and modern
scholarship is cautious about treating them as unified "empires."

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

Miksic and Goh treat Funan as a Chinese exonym (characters for a Mekong-delta kingdom, 3rd–7th c.; ancient pronunciation ~*biunâm*, close to Khmer *bnam*/*phnom* “mountain”); the local name is unknown. Rulers used Sanskrit titles *parwatabhupala* / *sailaraja* (“Lord of the Mountain”)—analogous to later [[sailendra]] usage, though any descent link is unconfirmed. The Kaundinya–naga-princess origin myth is a classic “stranger-king” template shared with south India and early [[champa]].

**Scale and structure.** Maximal 1960s maps (southern Vietnam + central Mekong + Menam + Malay peninsula) are rejected: Chinese sources over-assumed a centralized, China-like state. Funan was probably a **mandala / cluster of delta polities**. An extensive canal network supports territorial organization over a large share of the delta, but not beyond; archaeology cannot settle unified state vs. cluster. Pottery at [[oc-eo]] and Angkor Borei aligns more with later Cambodian ceramics than Sa Huynh Austronesian traditions—hence a Mon-Khmer rather than Cham core (the Vocanh inscription is more likely Cham than a Funan vassal).

**Chinese diplomacy and society.** After the Han fall, Funan sent a mission to Wu (226–231); further Chinese envoys (245–250) reported walled villages, palaces, books/archives, Indic script, and tax in gold, silver, and perfume. Under the Southern Dynasties (420–589) Funan sent 17 of 99 foreign tribute missions (incense, ivory, glass, re-exports); Seasians mainly sought Chinese silk. Southern Qi History: ruler Jayavarman (Śaiva, d. 514); expert smiths and shipbuilders; large family groups built ponds (a later Khmer affinity). Queen Kulaprabhawati and son Gunavarman left Viṣṇu-devotional inscriptions; Rudravarman sent embassies 517–539. The *Sui Shu* credits early-7th-c. Funan with ~30 enclosed settlements of ≥1,000 households each. Ships (Liang History) carried up to 100 paddlers; cloves from eastern Indonesia and Philippine iron reached China via this network.

**[[oc-eo]] and the delta urban system.** Oc-èo is the largest known Protoclassic settlement in Southeast Asia (~450 ha; five exterior rectangular moats; four ramparts ~1.5 × 3 km; canal to shore; Ta Kèo as possible outer harbor). Malleret’s 1940s excavations recovered brick foundations, Hindu/Buddhist sculpture, stilt-house pillars, and >30 wooden Buddhas (¹⁴C 4th–5th c.). Roman finds include an Antoninus Pius medallion (152 CE), coins, and intaglios; Chinese mirrors are scarcer—possibly Ptolemy’s Kattigara. Crafts: largest glass-bead assemblage in Seasia (mostly imported Indo-Pacific/*mutisalah* from India); pottery kilns; tin/gold work; dammar resin (caulking/incense). [CONTRADICTION] Miksic & Goh state coins (local and imported) indicate a monetized economy at Oc-èo, whereas their Early Classic chapter also notes “no evidence that coins were made in Funan; only foreign coins” at Oc-èo and contrasts that with extensive [[dvaravati]] coinage—preserve both formulations as an internal source tension (local vs. foreign minting). Oldest writing in Seasia appears here as Brahmi on imported seals/intaglios/rings (2nd–5th c.), not necessarily carved on-site. >300 related Mekong-delta sites; Angkor Borei (¹⁴C 4th c. BCE–7th c. CE) was abandoned with Funan; canals by 1st c. CE.

**Decline and Zhenla.** Embassies continue into the early 7th c.; Chinese believed Funan fell to former vassal **Zhenla** (Land/Water split tradition; Zhenla remained the Chinese name for Cambodia ~600 years). Miksic & Goh argue disintegration was **probably natural causes rather than external attack**: when Khmer inscriptions begin, the coastal trading orientation was collapsing while agrarian inland centers evolved—a shift from highly heterogenetic to extremely orthogenetic economy after the 8th c. Earliest dated Cambodian-territory Sanskrit inscriptions: 611 CE at Angkor Borei (perhaps a Funan capital); Jayavarman I (~650) mentions last Funan ruler Rudravarman there; Ba Phnom 667 lists one family as ministers to five kings (Rudravarman through Jayavarman I). Only four inscriptions in the putative Funan core (south Cambodia/Vietnam) vs. >15 in the Dangrek region. Isanavarman’s texts and Kdei Ang 667 suggest **gradual evolution, not violent revolution**. *History of the Sui*: Zhenla SW of Linyi, originally Funan’s vassal; mountain temple to Po-do-li (Bhaṭāra/Śiva)—speculated as Wat Phu/Liṅgaparvata (Cœdès’s Zhenla origin point). Sambor Prei Kuk = Isanapura (>20,000 families per Ma Duanlin quoting Sui); close relations with [[champa]]. Rise of Melaka-Straits ports ([[srivijaya]]) is linked to the bypass of isthmian portages and the vanishing of Protoclassic Mekong/upper-peninsula ports. Fifteenth-century Cambodia after leaving Angkor is framed as transformed toward Protoclassic Funan-like riverine trade (Phnom Penh at Mekong–Tonle Sap confluence), not total civilizational collapse.

*Source: John N. Miksic & Goh Geok Yian, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (Routledge, 2017); wiki periodization [[seasian-classic-periodization]].*
