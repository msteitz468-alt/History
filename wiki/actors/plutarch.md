# Plutarch of Chaeronea

Greek philosopher, priest of Delphi, and the most influential **biographer** of antiquity — author of
the **Parallel Lives**, the paired biographies of Greek and Roman statesmen that are a principal
source for much of classical history and shaped the Western tradition of life-writing. Analysed by
Philip A. Stadter in the Oxford World's Classics *Roman Lives* (trans. Robin Waterfield).

## Life

Plutarch (c. 45–c. 125 CE) lived through the turbulent end of the Julio-Claudians and the Flavian and
Trajanic peace, writing in "a time of recently acquired and still insecure serenity." A wealthy
aristocrat of **Chaeronea** in Boeotia (which he refused to leave, "lest it become smaller") and a
citizen of **Athens**, he taught philosophy to a small circle, served on civic embassies to Roman
governors, and was for many years a **priest of Apollo at Delphi**. Proudly and fully **Greek** in
culture, he accepted and worked within the Roman imperial system, counting among his friends and
dedicatees Roman consuls close to the emperor Trajan (Sosius Senecio). He produced an enormous body of
work, about half of which survives: the **Parallel Lives** and the miscellany of essays and dialogues
known as the **Moralia**.

## The Parallel Lives

Plutarch's masterwork (begun c. 96, continued to his death) is a series of **paired biographies** —
one Greek and one Roman statesman per book, 22 surviving pairs (plus a few single Lives like
*Artaxerxes*) — each pair usually framed by a **proem** and closed by a **synkrisis** ("comparison").
The pairing is the heart of the design: comparison "would reveal the underlying qualities of each," and
each pair forms a single work developing one overall impression (so the Greek Agesilaus prefigures the
longer, more extreme Roman Pompey). The modern split into "Greek Lives" and "Roman Lives" is an
editorial convenience.

- **Biography, not history.** "I am not writing history but biography" (*Alexander* 1): "the most
  outstanding exploits do not always… reveal the goodness or badness of the agent; often… a casual
  action, the odd phrase, or a jest reveals character better than battles." Plutarch's subjects are all
  **statesmen and generals**, but his lens is **ēthos** (character) — yet, crucially, his major sources
  were **histories** (Herodotus, Thucydides, Xenophon, Polybius, Livy, Sallust, Pollio), "massively
  rethought" in moral-philosophical terms.
- **Lives as a moral mirror.** A Platonist (with Aristotelian ethics and a sceptical streak), Plutarch
  wrote for politically active readers, that they might "treat the narrative as a kind of mirror" and
  "assimilate [their] life to the virtues of [his] subjects" (*Aemilius* 1). The heroes are **case
  studies in political behaviour**, not paragons — many "unsavoury types" — inviting the reader's own
  judgement; the *synkrisis* often reaches a verdict different from the narrative.
- **Method.** A deceptively chronological frame, rhetorically shaped, opening with family, nature and
  formative influences (Sulla's "blotched face and intimidating eyes" pointing to the later tyrant),
  built around a few peaks and closed at the subject's death, and studded with **anecdotes** clustered
  to illuminate character (not always in correct date order). He **focalizes through the protagonist**,
  abridges, conflates, personalizes group actions, and occasionally transfers an item between
  characters — adapting his sources to his portrait.

## Historical Value and Significance

The *Lives* are "extraordinarily valuable as sources" — preserving "a vast spectrum of evidence which
otherwise would have been lost" from Plutarch's "omnivorous reading," and giving us, in many cases,
our fullest portrait of a major figure. Their limits follow from their **biographical purpose**
(character over institutions, protagonist-favouring focalization, the pairing's distortions) and from
Plutarch's class, Platonism and imperfect Latin. As the inventor of sustained **moral biography**, he
became one of the most-read of all ancient authors — a model and delight for Montaigne, Shakespeare
(via North's translation), Rousseau, Emerson and the makers of the modern biographical tradition.
