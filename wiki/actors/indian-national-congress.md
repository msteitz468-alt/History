# The Indian National Congress

The **Indian National Congress** (founded 1885) was the principal vehicle of the Indian national
movement — the organisation through which the new **English-educated class** first articulated, and
ultimately won, the demand for self-government. Documented for the wiki through Percival Spear's
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965).

## Origins in the new class and the reform movements

Spear roots the Congress in the **English-educated class** that the post-[[indian-rebellion-1857|1857]]
Raj had inadvertently created (Macaulay's policy "bearing unexpected fruit"), and in the
nineteenth-century **religious-cultural renaissance** that gave it confidence: **Ram Mohan Roy** and
the **Brahmo Samaj** (1828) — reformist, rationalist, against suttee; the more militant **Arya Samaj**
(Dayananda); and **Vivekananda's** Vedantic revival. From this milieu the Congress emerged in **1885**
as "a modest body," loyal and petitioning — seeking a greater Indian share in the administration and
councils rather than independence.

## Moderates and extremists

As the Congress spread, Spear traces its defining early **split** between two leaders "alike in
ability and devotion but opposites in everything else": **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** — the constructive,
conciliatory **moderate**, a Gladstonian liberal working within constitutional channels — and **Bal
Gangadhar Tilak** — the **extremist**, orthodox Hindu, who "looked back nostalgically to the days of
Maratha and Hindu glory," made "the blood tingle with promptings to direct action," and gave
nationalism a popular, religiously-charged idiom. The dilemma, Spear notes, was that the government
"was too unbending" to satisfy even Gokhale — vindicating Tilak's school. The fissure hardened after
**Curzon's Partition of Bengal (1905)**, which provoked the **Swadeshi** (boycott of British goods)
agitation and the open moderate–extremist break (Surat, 1907).

## Toward the mass movement

Spear shows the Congress transformed after the First World War from an elite debating body into a
**mass movement** under **[[mahatma-gandhi|Gandhi]]**, who gave it a method (*satyagraha*), a popular
idiom, and a peasant base it had wholly lacked. The separate organisation of Muslim political interests
(the Muslim League, 1906; the Morley–Minto separate electorates, 1909) meanwhile planted the communal
division that would culminate in [[partition-of-india-1947|Partition]].

## Significance

The Congress is the institutional spine of modern Indian nationalism — the bridge from the petitioning
liberalism of Gokhale, through the cultural assertion of Tilak, to the Gandhian mass struggle that won
**independence in 1947** — after which it became the dominant party of the [[jawaharlal-nehru|Nehruvian]]
republic.
