# Magadha (the Saisunaga Kingdom)

**Magadha** was the kingdom of the **middle Ganga** that, in the sixth and fifth
centuries BCE, rose from one among sixteen **janapadas** to become the **paramount
power of northern India** — the political nucleus from which, two centuries later, the
[[mauryan-empire|Mauryan empire]] would impose the first unitary rule on the
subcontinent. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 3d, Bivar) treats it as the
eastern, Indian counterpart to the consolidating empires of the age.

## The sixteen janapadas

Sixth-century northern India was divided among **sixteen regional states**
(*janapadas* / *mahajanapadas*) — traditionally Anga, **Magadha**, Kasi, Kosala,
Vajji, Malla, Cedi, Vatsa, Kuru, Pancala, Matsya, Surasena, Asmaka, Avanti,
**Gandhara** and Kamboja. The four central rivals lay along the Ganga: **Kosala**
(Sravasti, Ayodhya), **Kasi** (Varanasi/Benares), the **Vajji** confederacy
(Vaisali), and **Magadha** itself, centred on **Rajagrha (Rajgir)** and later
**Pataliputra (Patna)**. After early periods of Kasi and Kosala pre-eminence, it was
Magadha that won through.

## Bimbisara and Ajatashatru

The dynasty's historical figures are **Bimbisara** and his "unfilial son"
**Ajatashatru** of the **Saisunaga** line. Their fame rests partly on their building
of a **centralized administration** in Magadha, and partly on their being
**contemporaries and patrons of the [[buddha|Buddha]] and of Mahavira**, the founder
of [[jainism|Jainism]] — the religious ferment of the Ganga's **Second Urbanization**.
Ajatashatru convened (or his reign saw) the **First Buddhist Council** at Rajagrha.
Bivar notes that several historians of India read Magadha's rise toward centralized
kingship as **reflecting the inspiration of the rising Achaemenid monarchy** on its
northwestern doorstep — Cyrus had united Medes and Persians by 550, and by 515 Darius
had carried Persian power to the Indus at [[gandhara|Gandhara]].

## Chronology and its problems

The dynasty's dates hang on the **disputed chronology of the Buddha and Mahavira**.
Reckoning back from the Buddha's *nirvana* — widely (not universally) placed **c. 483
BCE** (218 years before Ashoka's consecration) — Ajatashatru's accession falls c. 491
and Bimbisara's c. 543 or c. 519, depending on whether one follows Buddhist (52-year)
or Hindu (28-year) reign-lengths. A "degree of approximation therefore prevails," and
the whole framework shifts if the later "short chronology" for the Buddha (death c.
400 BCE) is preferred.

kingship — a possible (Bivar) but unprovable model.

## Historiography

Magadha's early history is reconstructed from **religious traditions** — Buddhist,
Jaina and Hindu — whose internal chronologies conflict, supplemented by the ambiguous
evidence of the **"single-type" punch-marked coinages** attributed (often arbitrarily)
to particular janapadas. The collection is **weak on South Asia** (CLAUDE.md coverage
note), so this page leans heavily on the single Bivar chapter and should be read as
provisional pending dedicated Indian sources.
