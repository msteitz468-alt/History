# Mikhail Speransky

**Mikhail Speransky** (1772–1839) was the preeminent Russian statesman and administrative reformer of the reigns of Alexander I and Nicholas I. Best known for his far-reaching constitutional proposal of 1809 and his monumental codification of the laws of the Russian Empire in 1830, Speransky sought to introduce the principles of legality, efficiency, and bureaucratic merit into the autocratic state.

## Biography / Description

Speransky was a self-made man who rose to the highest ranks of the imperial administration through sheer intelligence, capacity for work, and administrative talent. Unlike most of his aristocratic peers, he was born into the family of a poor village priest in the province of Vladimir. He studied at a theological seminary before entering civil service, where he rapidly attracted the attention of reform-minded officials.

In 1807, Speransky became the principal advisor to Emperor Alexander I. He worked to establish a modern, law-based state (*Rechtsstaat*) that would be free from administrative arbitrariness and corruption. However, his humble origins, his efficiency-oriented reforms (such as civil service examinations), and his perceived pro-French sympathies made him the target of intense hostility from the court aristocracy and the conservative gentry. In March 1812, on the eve of Napoleon's invasion, Alexander I yielded to political pressure, dismissed Speransky, and exiled him.

Speransky was later recalled to public service, serving as governor of Siberia before returning to St. Petersburg. Under Nicholas I, he was tasked with the codification of Russian law. He died in 1839, shortly after being created a count.

## Key Decisions and Achievements

### The Constitutional Project of 1809
At Alexander I's request, Speransky submitted a comprehensive proposal for a Russian constitution in 1809. The plan aimed to reorganize the empire on four administrative levels: the *volost* (township), the district, the province, and the country at large. On each level, he proposed:
- **Legislative Assemblies:** Culminating in a national legislative assembly, the **State Duma**, elected through a system of indirect elections based on property qualifications.
- **Judicial System:** Culminating in the Senate as the supreme court, with popular election of judges.
- **Administrative Boards:** Culminating in the central ministries.
Speransky's plan also divided Russian society into three categories (gentry, "middle condition" property owners, and working people/serfs) and defined civil and political rights. Although Alexander I shelved the plan, it served as a blueprint for future reforms: the district and provincial assemblies were realized in the zemstvo reform of 1864, the national Duma in 1905–06, and the *volost* self-government in 1917.

### Reorganization of the Ministries and the Council of State
While his constitutional project failed, Speransky succeeded in reforming the central administration. In 1810, he helped create the **Council of State**, an appointed body of experts to advise the sovereign on legislation, serving as its Secretary of State. He also reorganized the ministries, instituted a system of annual budgets, and introduced civil service exams to ensure merit-based promotion.

### Codification of Russian Law (1826–1830)
Under Nicholas I, Speransky headed the Second Section of His Majesty's Own Imperial Chancellery, charged with codifying the chaotic and contradictory mass of Russian laws dating back to the Code of 1649. Speransky successfully compiled and published:
- *The Complete Collection of Laws of the Russian Empire* (1830), consisting of forty-eight volumes of all laws in chronological order.
- *The Code of Laws of the Russian Empire* (1832), a fifteen-volume systematic compilation of active laws.
This achievement established a reliable legal framework for the administration of the empire.

## Historical Significance

Mikhail Speransky's historical significance lies in his effort to modernize the administration of the Russian autocracy from within. He recognized that the survival of the empire required a transition from arbitrary bureaucratic rule to a modern state based on legality and systematic administration. While his attempts to introduce a constitution and a legislative assembly were blocked by the conservative gentry, his legal codification and ministerial reforms provided the institutional and legal infrastructure that made the Great Reforms of the 1860s possible.
