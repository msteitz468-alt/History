# Greenland Inuit (Kalaallit)

Inuit peoples of [[greenland|Greenland]] (Kalaallit Nunaat), descendants of [[thule-culture|Thule]] populations who entered the island and developed the **Inuksuk (Inugsuk)** phase in contact with Norse materials. Self-identity as **Kalaallit** had emerged by the end of the eighteenth century and was strengthened by colonial education, a common west Greenlandic lingua franca, and modern Home Rule politics. `part_of: [[arctic-native-history-norse-to-modern]]` · `affiliated_with: [[inuit]]`

## Norse–Thule horizon

First Norse–Thule contacts usually assigned to the early thirteenth century around Disko Bay (sagas and Inuit legends; archaeology shows peaceful as well as violent contact). Inuksuk culture superimposed some Norse artifacts on classical Thule; southward ecological adjustments reduced polar bear hunting south of Disko Bay, increased harp/hooded/common seals south of Holsteinsborg, and dropped dog traction as a temperate adjustment. Inuit legends of Norse extermination have questionable historicity (late recordings, shifting locations). Polar Eskimos (“Arctic Highlanders”) of the far north remained relatively isolated into the nineteenth century (Ross 1818).

## Danish colonial regime (from 1721)

1721 landmark: Hans Egede’s Norwegian Lutheran mission—first European settlement since the Norse. Stations spread (Godthab 1728, Christianshab 1734, Jakobshavn 1741, Frederikshab 1742). Royal Greenland Trade Department monopoly after 1774; coast closed to foreign ships 1776. Deliberate **protectionism** kept hunting the principal occupation and discouraged Inuit population aggregation. Egede shifted from seeking Norse pagan descendants to Bible translation into Greenlandic; shaman power overthrown surprisingly fast. German Moravians from 1733 competed as lay crafts-teachers. Interbreeding of European men and Inuit women created an intermediate group later nucleus of West Greenland population. Instruction of 19 April 1782: two inspectorates; welfare for mixed-marriage offspring; alcohol forbidden; fair trade and famine relief.

### Nineteenth century
English whaler competition and gin excesses; isolation in Napoleonic wars; after 1813–14 Greenland solidly under Denmark; economy improved only after 1830. East coast lagged (Clavering 1823; Holm ethnography Ammassalik 1884–5). Godthab teachers’ college 1845 homogenized colonial culture and west Greenlandic dialect; *Atuagagdliutit* periodical seeded Greenlandic literature. Helge Kleivan: after missionary obliteration of traditional intellectual culture, a hybrid colonial intellectual culture formed underground. Self-identity as Kalaallit strengthened versus local *-miut* identification. Danish policy maintained hunting economy and excluded foreign trade; commercial fishing remained indifferent into the late nineteenth century.

### Early twentieth century
East Greenland mission/store 1894; Ammassalik school 1904; Scoresby Sound colonization 1925. Polar Eskimos: Knud Rasmussen’s Thule trading post after 1910; Lutheran mission 1909, conversion complete only 1934; Danish administration asserted 1937. **Act of Administration of Greenland, 18 April 1925:** districts, voting/office eligibility, compulsory education ages 7–14, municipal councils entirely of Greenlanders; Greenlandic language of instruction; alcohol officially denied. Warming extended cod/halibut ranges; commercial fishing and risky sheep raising expanded. National identity grew via Greenlandic literature freer of Christian themes. Population nearly +50% 1900–40 under superior health relative to other Arctic regions.

## WWII, G-50/G-60, and incorporation (1940–1960)

Geopolitical importance for Allied shipping and U.S. North Atlantic air control; Kauffman Treaty air bases; Northeast Greenland Sled Patrol routed German weather stations; cryolite for aluminum. Helge Kleivan: 1945 conditions were underdevelopment—poverty, unhygienic housing, alarming health, deteriorating production technology—despite unusually benevolent Danish colonial rule. G-50 plan: commercial fishing for wage labor, relocation small settlements → towns, massive housing, end of trade monopoly—but without training Greenlanders for skilled jobs → large Danish influx and occupational/pay inequality. Constitution 1953 abolished colonial status, incorporated Greenland into the Kingdom of Denmark with two parliamentary seats; legal equality did not materialize in pay/occupation.

## Home Rule and parties (1960–1990s)

G-60 plan (1960) perpetuated contradictions: greater Greenlandic participation required more Danish labor/management. 1948–69: Greenlanders +75%, Danish residents +700%. Social ills of centralization: alcoholism, suicide, homicide, VD ~100× Denmark; high illegitimacy with many single Danish men; extended-family breakdown. Emigration to Denmark ~6,000 by 1971 (~15% of home population). By 1981 nearly half the population in towns ≥3,000.

**Parties:** Siumut (“Forwards”) movement 1971 / party 1977 (moderate socialist); Atassut (“Connecting Link”) conservative-liberal; Inuit Ataqatigiit (“Human Fellowship”) Marxist. Siumut drove Home Rule Commission 1975 → **Greenland Home Rule Act** 29 Nov. 1978, implemented **1 May 1979**: Denmark retains foreign relations, defense, financial support; Greenlanders control local government, taxes, fishing, hunting, herding, vocational education, health, rent control, etc.; Greenlandic official language. Landsting entirely popularly elected. Washburn: WWII isolation had given self-government experience retained after Danish return; same autonomy class as Faeroes; “fundamental rights” to natural resources; entered EC with Denmark 1973 despite 2:1 local rejection, then voted **52–46% for EC withdrawal** (effective 1 Jan. 1985), losing ~$30M/year EC grants—attraction of circumpolar Inuit identification ([[inuit|ICC]] founded 1977) over Europe. Mid-1980s official: “Greenland survives on artificial respiration from Denmark”; Danish public-fund share fell from 80% (1979) to ~50% by end of century under transferrals.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Egede-era monopoly protectionism, West Greenland demography and language standardization would look different; without WWII and G-50 urbanization, Home Rule leadership would lack its town-based party base; without ICC identification, the 1985 EC withdrawal calculus shifts.

## Historiography

Finn Gad *History of Greenland*; Inge/Inga Kleivan on Norse Greenland; Helge Kleivan on colonial intellectual culture and Labrador comparanda; Damas ch. 14 synthesis; Washburn ch. 15 on Home Rule/EC as nativist renaissance. Debate: whether Danish “benevolent colonialism” mitigated underdevelopment or produced dependency (Kleivan’s underdevelopment diagnosis). Source quality high for west coast administration; thinner for east coast and early Polar Eskimo autonomy.
