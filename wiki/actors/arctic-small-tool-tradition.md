## Overview

The Arctic Small Tool tradition (AST) was the first widespread culture across the Arctic, constituting Arctic Period 3 (c. 2200 ±300 BCE to 1200 ±400 BCE). Defined by its distinctive miniature stone tools — tiny bifacially worked blades inserted as cutting edges into slots in larger bone or ivory composite tools — the tradition spread from its Alaskan origins eastward across the entire Arctic, reaching Greenland and representing the first human populations to occupy much of the Far North. The tradition was the direct ancestor of [[dorset-culture]] (Canadian variant Pre-Dorset) and of the various Alaskan Period 4 cultures including Norton, Choris, and Ipiutak.

## Geographic Distribution

The Arctic Small Tool tradition originated in Alaska — where it was first discovered by Alaskan Iñupiaq, the descendants of its makers — and spread eastward across the Canadian Arctic to Greenland. The Canadian variant (Pre-Dorset, c. 2500–500 BCE) eventually spread along the coast all the way to Newfoundland. Derivative remnants have been identified in Greenland as Independence I and Sarqaq. The western variant remained in Alaska, where it gave rise to multiple Period 4 cultures (Kachemak in the Kodiak region, Norton along the Bering Sea coast, Choris, and Ipiutak in northern Alaska) as descendants undertook alternative adaptive strategies after the AST's passing.

## Dating and Chronology

The Arctic Small Tool tradition spans approximately 2200 (±300) BCE to 1200 (±400) BCE, constituting Arctic Period 3. Its precursor was the Northern Archaic tradition (Period 2, 5000–2200 BCE). The tradition's ending date varies from place to place over a range of several centuries. In Canada, the Pre-Dorset variant persisted until approximately 500 BCE before giving rise to [[dorset-culture]] following a drastic contraction of Pre-Dorset populations. In Alaska, the passing of the AST was followed by the emergence of multiple derivative cultures after 1200 BCE.

Dating method: radiocarbon.

## Economy and Subsistence

AST adaptation was primarily terrestrial. Technology was "not yet sufficient to allow [people] to move permanently beyond the security of land and forest." Although seasonal seal hunting occurred along the coast during open-water periods, AST people lacked the technology for hunting seals through sea ice — a pursuit central to later Arctic cultures. Critically, most or all AST sites lacked stone oil lamps for burning sea mammal blubber, requiring camps to remain near forests for fuel wood. The absence of reliable seal-oil fuel was the primary constraint preventing permanent settlement in the treeless High Arctic.

Permanent habitations were generally located near the interior forest edge of the tundra, where caribou and migratory fish were plentiful. Architecture consisted of semisubterranean pithouses entered from the top, a form that persisted through Period 3.

## Architecture and Technology

The defining technological feature was the miniature tool — not an end in itself, but a component technology enabling efficient compound tools. A bone or ivory point needed only a slot along its edge to receive a bifacial sliver of chert to serve as a hard, sharp cutting edge. This approach minimized raw material use (important in lithic-scarce Arctic environments) while producing effective cutting implements of normal size. The small size of the stone inserts led early observers to joke about a race of little people, but the compound tool rationale is now well understood.

The pithouses of the AST were the standard Arctic residential structure of their time. In northwestern Alaska, pithouses were built in lines just above coastal beaches; as sea levels fell and new beaches formed, new house lines were built at lower elevations, creating the appearance (misleading) of planned "streets" — actually the cumulative result of sequential abandonment and rebuilding over long periods.

The AST represents the foundation from which "all later Arctic cultures were known for their many gadgets made of bone, walrus ivory, whale baleen, copper, leather, fiber, and stone." The technological elaboration of compound tools in the AST "presaged further inventiveness that would drive the efforts of northern peoples to penetrate ever deeper into the world's most hostile environment."

## Society and Identity

AST bands were likely small, egalitarian hunter-gatherers organized at the band level. Following the initial spread across the Arctic during Period 3, later generations had no knowledge of their geographical origins, having no option but to readapt in place as conditions changed. When the Arctic climate became more difficult after the AST's initial spread, descendant bands could not retreat to less hostile environments. Some survived in culturally modified form (giving rise to Pre-Dorset and [[dorset-culture]]); many bands must have simply shrunk and died out. Little serious conflict appears to have characterized this process — conflict, when it occurred, was probably interpersonal rather than inter-band.

## Decline and Legacy

The Arctic Small Tool tradition ended approximately 1200 BCE (±400 years), giving way to a variety of derivative cultures in Alaska and to the Pre-Dorset / [[dorset-culture]] sequence in Canada. In Alaska, Kachemak culture emerged in the Kodiak region; Norton culture developed along the Bering Sea coast; Choris and Ipiutak emerged in northern Alaska. The Old Bering Sea culture, the earliest example of a full maritime adaptation (with technology for hunting seals, walruses, and whales from ice and boats), dates to as early as 150 BCE and may have origins in northeast Siberia.

The AST's legacy is the template for all subsequent Arctic cultural development. The compound tool technology, the pithouse architecture, and the pattern of terrestrial-focused subsistence with coastal supplementation all fed into the derivative cultures that ultimately led, through the Pre-Dorset → Dorset → Thule sequence, to modern Inuit culture.

## Historiography

The Arctic Small Tool tradition was identified and named through comparative archaeology linking sites across the vast Arctic. The tradition's miniature tools were initially puzzling — the small size seemed impractical — until the compound tool technology was understood. The broad geographic extent of the tradition, combined with the sparse and challenging archaeological record of the Arctic (thin, acid soils in some areas; permafrost preservation in others), has made precise delineation of regional variants difficult. The relationship between the AST's various Alaskan descendants (Norton, Choris, Ipiutak, Kachemak) and the Canadian Pre-Dorset / Dorset sequence reflects genuine regional divergence rather than a simple linear succession.
