# Alexander I of Russia

**Alexander I** (1777–1825; r. 1801–1825) was Emperor of Russia during the turbulent era of the Napoleonic Wars. Known as "the enigmatic tsar," a sphinx, and "crowned Hamlet," his reign was marked by striking contradictions between early liberal aspirations and late-reign mysticism and political reaction.

## Biography / Description

Alexander was the eldest son of Grand Duke Paul (later Emperor Paul I) and was raised under the direct supervision of his grandmother, Catherine the Great. Catherine educated him in the spirit of the Enlightenment, employing the Swiss liberal philosopher Frederic-Cesar de LaHarpe as his tutor. In this environment, Alexander learned progressive political theory, but the stark contrast between these ideals and the autocratic reality of Russia shaped his contradictory nature. Furthermore, growing up in the middle of the intense hostility between Catherine and Paul, Alexander mastered the arts of flattery and dissimulation early in life.

Alexander ascended the throne in March 1801 at the age of twenty-three, following the deposition and assassination of his father, Paul I. Although Alexander had been aware of the conspiracy to depose Paul, the murder of his father came as a profound shock, instilling in him a lifelong sense of guilt that later contributed to his religious mysticism.

In his personal life, Alexander was charming and sensitive but increasingly restless, suspicious, and detached from reality as he aged. He died in 1825 in the southern town of Taganrog. His sudden death gave rise to the popular legend that he had staged his death and escaped the throne to live as the holy hermit Theodore (Fedor) Kuzmich in Siberia—a legend fueled by his frequent expressions of wanting to abdicate and a court physician's refusal to sign the death warrant.

## Key Decisions and Achievements

### Early Reforms and the Unofficial Committee (1801–1805)
Upon his accession, Alexander enacted an amnesty for twelve thousand men dismissed by Paul, relaxed censorship, reopened private printing houses, abolished torture in investigations, and restored Catherine's charters to the gentry and towns. He formed the **Unofficial Committee** (composed of his liberal friends Nicholas Novosiltsev, Count Paul Stroganov, Count Victor Kochubey, and Prince Adam Czartoryski) to draft reforms for autocracy and serfdom. 
- **Administrative Reforms:** In 1802, the old administrative colleges were replaced by modern ministries, including war, navy, foreign affairs, justice, interior, finance, commerce, and education. The Senate was also restored as the supreme judicial and administrative body.
- **Agrarian Reform:** In 1801, the right to own land was extended to non-nobles. In 1803, the **Law Concerning Free Agriculturists** allowed landlords to voluntarily emancipate serfs with land allotments. However, this resulted in the liberation of only 115,734 male serfs and their families before its suspension on the eve of the 1861 Emancipation.
- **Education:** Alexander prioritized education, establishing several new universities (adding to Moscow University), secondary schools (gymnasia), and elementary schools.

### The Speransky Reform Project (1807–1812)
During his alliance with France, Alexander relied on **Michael Speransky** to reorganize the state.
- In 1809, Speransky proposed a comprehensive constitution based on a hierarchy of legislative assemblies (*dumy*) culminating in a national **State Duma**, alongside a system of courts and administrative boards, built upon a strict concept of legality (*Rechtsstaat*). 
- Fearing gentry opposition and suspected of pro-French sympathies, Speransky was dismissed on the eve of the 1812 invasion. Only the **Council of State** (created in 1810) was implemented from his plan.
- Novosiltsev's later *Constitutional Charter of the Russian Empire* (1820) introduced a federal plan but was also ultimately shelved.

### Foreign Policy and Territorial Expansion
- **Coalition Wars:** Russia participated in the War of the Third Coalition, suffering a disastrous defeat at **Austerlitz** (1805). Following further defeats at Friedland, Alexander concluded the **Treaties of Tilsit (1807)** with Napoleon, which forced Russia into the Continental Blockade against Great Britain but established Russia as the hegemon of eastern Europe.
- **Imperial Expansion:** Alexander successfully expanded Russian territory on several fronts:
  - Annexed Georgia (eastern Georgia in 1801; western Georgia between 1803 and 1810).
  - Fought Qajar Persia in the Russo-Persian War (1804–1813), securing Daghestan and Shemakha under the Treaty of Gulistan.
  - Defeated the Ottoman Empire in the Russo-Turkish War (1806–1812), annexing Bessarabia under the Treaty of Bucharest.
  - Defeated Sweden in the Russo-Swedish War (1808–1809), annexing Finland as an autonomous Grand Duchy.
  - Established Fort Ross in California (1812) as part of Russian expansion in North America.

### The Patriotic War of 1812 and European Campaigns
- When Napoleon invaded Russia in June 1812, Alexander refused to negotiate while French troops remained on Russian soil. Despite the retreat of the Russian army under Barclay de Tolly and Bagration, the bloody standoff at **Borodino** (September 7, 1812), and the burning of Moscow, the Russian forces under **Kutuzov** drove the Grande Armee out of Russia in a devastating rout.
- Alexander pursued Napoleon into Europe, culminating in the **Battle of Leipzig (1813)** and the capture of Paris in March 1814.

### The Congress of Vienna and the Congress System
- At the **Congress of Vienna (1814–15)**, Alexander secured the annexation of the Kingdom of Poland (Congress Poland) in personal union with the Russian crown and granted it a liberal constitution.
- Driven by a mystical belief in Christian brotherhood and international peace, Alexander sponsored the **Holy Alliance** (1815) between Russia, Austria, and Prussia.
- He championed the **Congress System** (Quadruple and Quintuple Alliances), participating in congresses at Aix-la-Chapelle (1818), Troppau and Laibach (1820–21), and Verona (1822). 

## Historical Significance

Alexander's historical significance lies in his dual role as the defender of Europe against Napoleon and the preserver of autocracy inside Russia. While he defeated the French Empire and helped design the post-Napoleonic European balance of power, his internal policies failed to resolve the fundamental challenges of serfdom and political reform. 

His late-reign shift to reaction, catalyzed by the mutiny of the Semenovskii Guard Regiment in 1820 and his collaboration with Metternich to crush liberal revolutions in Spain and Italy, disillusioned the reform-minded officer corps. This directly prepared the ground for the **Decembrist Revolt** of 1825. Under Alexander, Russia became a massive European hegemon, but its internal social and political stagnation deepened, setting the stage for the crises of the mid-nineteenth century.
