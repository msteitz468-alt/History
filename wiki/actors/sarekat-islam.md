## Overview

Sarekat Islam (Islamic Union), formally constituted in September 1912 in Java, was the first mass political organization in the Netherlands East Indies and the dominant vehicle of Indonesian reformist activity through the 1910s. It claimed a membership in excess of two million at its peak and combined Islamic identity with economic advocacy and mutual aid, offering a broader mobilizing base than the elite-educated Budi Utomo (1908) that had preceded it. Its trajectory — from tolerated mass organization to radicalized vehicle of competing factions to organizational decline — illustrates the structural dilemmas facing anti-colonial movements in the Dutch colonial context.

## Origins and Initial Character

Sarekat Islam grew from a Javanese batik merchants' association formed to resist Chinese commercial competition, becoming a mass organization with extraordinary speed after 1912. Its stated aims were to promote a commercial spirit, provide mutual aid, serve the spiritual and economic interests of the Muslim population, and "combat misunderstandings concerning Islam."

Its key figure was Raden Umar Sayed Tjokroaminoto (1882–1934), an aristocratic OSVIA graduate (OSVIA was the Dutch school for training native officials) who became both the organizational leader and a charismatic popular figure. [[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v2-1992]] notes that "popular identification of him with the messianic Javanese tradition of the *ratu adil* (just king) contributed to the movement's early growth" — illustrating how the millenarian substrate (see [[millenarianism-southeast-asia]]) shaped even formally Islamic mass organizations.

The Dutch administration permitted Sarekat Islam at the local level but refused to authorize a central organization until 1916, by which time local branches had developed to such a degree that they were not amenable to central control.

## Radicalization and Internal Fragmentation (1918–1921)

Around 1918 Sarekat Islam entered a radical phase driven by pressure from the Marxist ISDV (Indische Sociaal-Demokratische Vereeniging, later PKI) whose members joined Sarekat Islam to acquire a mass base. The ISDV had been founded in 1914 by Dutch socialist Hendricus Sneevliet; its Semarang branch under Semaun and Darsono became a power centre within the organization. The Dutch government uncovered a secret "Section B" revolutionary faction within Sarekat Islam in 1918, leading to prison sentences and defections, particularly from the peasant membership.

The internal conflict between the moderate-Islamic faction (Agus Salim, Abdul Muis) and the radical ISDV faction came to a head at the sixth Sarekat Islam Congress (1921), which passed a resolution barring joint membership in Sarekat Islam and other organizations. The communists withdrew to form the Red Sarekat Islam from local branches they controlled.

## Decline and Reconstitution

Effectively stripped of its communist wing and discredited by its association with the PKI's abortive uprisings of 1926–27, Sarekat Islam reconstituted itself in 1929 as the Partai Sarekat Islam Indonesia. It never recovered its former pre-eminence. The initiative in Indonesian secular nationalism had passed to the Perserikatan Nasional Indonesia (PNI, 1927) under [[sukarno|Sukarno]], while organized Islamic political activity was represented by Muhammadiyah (modernist, non-political) and Nahdatul Ulama (traditional-conservative, formed 1926 as a counter to Muhammadiyah).

## Significance

Sarekat Islam's trajectory illustrated three key dynamics of Southeast Asian nationalism:

1. **The Islam question**: Divisions within the Muslim community between reformist (*kaum muda*) and traditional (*kaum tua*) factions, and between Islam as spiritual framework and Islam as political programme, were never resolved in the pre-war period.
2. **Communism vs. nationalism**: The Comintern's attempt to use Sarekat Islam as a mass base for PKI activity produced the fragmentation that weakened both movements.
3. **Mass mobilization vs. elite politics**: Sarekat Islam demonstrated that mass organization was possible in colonial Indonesia, but also that sustaining organizational discipline across two million members with competing ideological tendencies was beyond the capacity of any pre-war movement.

Its 1917 Action Programme — calling for transformation of the Volksraad (advisory council) into a true legislature, establishment of regional councils, and extension of the franchise — set the template for subsequent demands by Indonesian nationalist organizations.
