# Fulani

A predominantly pastoralist, Fula-speaking people dispersed across the West
African Sahel and Sudan belt from Senegal to Cameroon and Sudan, among the
largest ethnic groups in Africa and one of the largest pastoral-nomadic
populations in the world. Their historical significance lies chiefly in
their central role in the Islamization of West Africa and in a sequence of
militant reform states they founded from the seventeenth through nineteenth
centuries.

## Role and Significance

Fulani communities were established in Takrur and the western Sahel by at
least the early second millennium CE, later spreading eastward in a
centuries-long migration across the Sudanic belt. Fulani clerics were among
the earliest and most committed carriers of Islamic learning in West Africa,
and Fulani-led *jihad* movements — most consequentially Usman dan Fodio's
founding of the [[sokoto-caliphate|Sokoto Caliphate]] (1804) — reshaped the
religious and political map of the Central Sudan, establishing durable
theocratic states that outlasted the pre-colonial era.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without a mobile, religiously mobilized Fulani population straddling
multiple Sudanic states, West African Islamization would likely have
remained more confined to urban merchant and scholarly elites, and the
nineteenth-century wave of jihad states (Sokoto, Massina) would have lacked
their principal social base.

## Historiography

Fulani history is documented primarily through Arabic-language chronicles
associated with the jihad states and through oral tradition; the collection's
coverage of pre-colonial West Africa (via the General History of Africa
series) treats the Fulani mainly through their role in Takrur, the
Almoravid movement, and the Sokoto Caliphate rather than as a continuous
subject in their own right — reflecting the broader thinness of dedicated
ethnographic-historical treatment for Sub-Saharan Africa in this collection.
