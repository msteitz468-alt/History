# Navajo (Diné)

Athapaskan-speaking people of the San Juan River country and later the canyonlands of northwestern New Mexico and northeastern Arizona. Self-name **Diné** (“the people”); Spanish **Apache de Navihu** / Navajos—initially grouped with other [[apache|Apachean]] newcomers. Primary colonial and Long Walk synthesis: Lamar & Truett, [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-2|CHNPA I.2]] ch. 10; reservation-era development in later I.2 chapters.

## Overview and ethnogenesis

Spanish grouped nomadic/seminomadic Athapaskans south and east of the Pueblos as “Apache” (Pueblo word meaning enemy)—relative newcomers who migrated south from central/western Canada between **A.D. 800 and 1500** by a still-debated route. Sixteenth-century survivors include Western Apaches, Mescalero Apaches, and Navajos.

During the **early Spanish period**, Navajos were quite distinct from the sheep-herding, blanket-weaving people Americans dealt with in the nineteenth century. They lived in the San Juan River country, raided and traded with Spanish and [[pueblo-peoples|Pueblo]] Indians, and called themselves Diné.

### Post-revolt transformation

After the de Vargas reconquest of New Mexico (**1692–96**), **Pueblo refugees joined Navajo communities**. By **1754**, Ute and [[comanches|Comanche]] attacks drove them into the canyonlands of NW New Mexico and NE Arizona, carrying Pueblo skills: herding, weaving, pottery, stone hogans. **Jemez Pueblos** especially modified Navajo worldviews. Spanish contact also produced a **horse culture** for both Apache and Navajo; Navajos eventually adopted **sheep-raising and woolen blanket production**—incorporation of Spanish animals and economic practices that did **not** improve relations with the Spanish themselves.

## Mexican period warfare (1800–1846)

Between **1800 and 1850**, raiding became so massive and frequent that **war became a dominant feature of Navajo society**, though raids aimed at goods or captives to sell to Mexicans rather than killing. A fairly long peace with Mexican settlers ended in **1818**; reciprocal raiding continued to American conquest in **1846**. By **1845**, Apache and even Navajo raids reportedly reached California across the southern Sierras.

## U.S. conquest and the Long Walk (full fidelity)

U.S.–Navajo relations were almost totally hostile **1848–1868**. Col. Edwin Vose Sumner placed **Fort Defiance** in Canyon Bonito to contain Navajos; mid-1850s treaties promising reservations for Apaches, Utes, and Navajos failed Senate ratification (1856).

### Carleton, Carson, Bosque Redondo

Gen. **James H. Carleton’s** brutal policy: kill or capture hostiles until they lived on reservations, accepted Christianity, and farmed. He located Fort Wingate in the heart of Navajo country and gave **Kit Carson** orders to effect **“entire subjugation or destruction of all the men.”** Carson’s strategy was ruthless: series of **winter campaigns**; destruction of **every Navajo cornfield or food cache** found. The Army paid **$20 per Navajo horse** and **$1 per sheep**.

Carleton ordered some **~8,000 Navajos** to march eastward and settle near Fort Sumner in the **Bosque Redondo**—which he called a “reformatory”/prison. **Drought 1864** and a **freeze 1865** ruined crops. By the **1868** treaty return, Navajos had been reduced by **perhaps half their 1863 population by disease and starvation**. The emotional return of the Long Walk is preserved in testimony: *“When we saw the top of the mountain from Albuquerque we wondered if it was our mountain, and we felt like talking to the ground, we loved it so.”*

Carleton **failed** at turning Navajos into Christian farmers but **broke their military power**. Raids continued to about **1885** at waning impact. Mescalero roundups formed a concurrent Carleton campaign arm (`concurrent_with:` [[apache]]).

## Reservation rebound and nation-building

Exception to unrestrained 1880s dispossession: Navajos **rebounded from Bosque Redondo**, **doubled population 1870–1900**, and shifted toward herding and farming; no major land cessions in Arizona/New Mexico Territories though a century-long struggle with [[hopi|Hopi]] over shared grazing/hunting territory began.

Early 20th-c. sheep herding remained subsistence-level by policy (individualization/civilization goals foreclosed communal commercial herds). Handicrafts commercialized with the railroad (1881) and trading posts rising from **3 (1870) → 79 (1900) → 144 (1920)**, but licensed traders controlled marketing and limited Navajo profit.

**Stock reduction and IRA rejection (1930s):** investigations claimed Navajos grazed ~3× sustainable livestock; voluntary reduction 1933–34 failed; stock seized and destroyed in owners’ view. June **1935** IRA referendum (98% turnout) defeated by 384 votes—stock reduction crucial; factional contest between Chee Dodge (pro-Collier) and Jacob Morgan (anti–New Deal). 1938 election under imposed regulations made Morgan chairman. Postwar council that had fought stock reduction became assertive: **1950 Navajo-Hopi Rehabilitation Act** allocated mineral revenue; 1950s courts, police, power authority, newspaper—Iverson: the decade “witnessed the birth of the Navajo nation.”

Later: **Navajo Peacemaker Court (1982)** blended traditional mediation with formal court structure; 1970s–80s MacDonald vs Zah leadership conflict and 1989–90 corruption crisis (MacDonald tribal and federal convictions). Hopi–Navajo relocation/boundary dispute drew 1970s anthropologist advocacy alongside other pan-Indian causes.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Carleton’s winter destruction of food stores and forced Bosque confinement, nineteenth-century Navajo military autonomy and canyonland demography would likely have persisted far longer against thin New Mexico settlement—as Apache resistance in Arizona did into the 1880s. Without Pueblo refugee influx after 1692, the pastoral blanket-weaving Navajo of American encounter is hard to derive from early San Juan raiders alone.
