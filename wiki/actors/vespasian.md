# Vespasian (Titus Flavius Vespasianus)

The **founder of the Flavian dynasty** and the emperor who, after the chaos of 69, "made firm the
state, which was tottering and almost in ruins" (Suetonius). The first *princeps* of
**non-aristocratic, Italian municipal stock**, he restored stability, solvency, and a secure
dynastic succession to the Roman Empire. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 1.

## From obscurity to the purple

Born in **9 CE** to a Sabine family "at most equestrian on his father's side" (a tax-collector
and money-lender), Vespasian rose through the *cursus honorum*, commanded **Legio II Augusta** in
the **invasion of Britain (43)**, and ended his senatorial career as proconsul of Africa.
Tacitus, "with justice," describes his rise as **the work of fortune**: his undistinguished
background was his chief liability, but standards had dropped, and the Jewish revolt of 66
(combined with Nero's fear of well-born commanders) gave him three legions and an unexpected path
to glory. His decisive asset was **two grown sons** ([[titus|Titus]] and [[domitian|Domitian]]) —
a ready dynasty. Acclaimed by the legions of Egypt (**1 July 69**) and Judaea, he won the
[[year-of-the-four-emperors-69ce|civil war]] through his allies **Mucianus** and **Antonius
Primus** while himself **remaining in Egypt** for over a year — controlling the corn supply and,
the volume suggests, leaving the "invidia" of executions and money-raising to his deputies.

## Refounding the Principate

CAH XI stresses that Vespasian did **not devise a new system** but reasserted the Augustan one in
its "respectable" form. The **Lex de Imperio Vespasiani** (the surviving bronze law conferring
his powers) cited **Augustus, Tiberius, and Claudius** as precedents — pointedly omitting Nero,
Galba, Otho, and Vitellius — and granted the *princeps* sweeping discretionary authority,
backdated to his *dies imperii*. His **coinage** was strikingly unoriginal, advertising
**continuity** (*Pax, Libertas, Concordia, Roma Resurgens*) and reusing even Vitellian types and
dies. The adoption of the name **Caesar** by this outsider made it unmistakably "the name of an
institution," through which "the continuance of the Augustan Principate was declared."

## Ideology, finance, and the Jewish triumph

Vespasian made much of his **Sabine toughness and frugality** (wanting officers "to smell of
garlic, not perfume") and his **financial expertise** — overcoming "inhibitions about the methods
for obtaining money" to restore the treasury after Nero and the wars (the apocryphal *pecunia non
olet*). The regime's central propaganda theme was **military victory and dynastic harmony**: the
**Jewish triumph of 71** (the closing of Janus, the spoils of the Temple housed in the new
**Temple of Peace**), celebrated jointly with Titus, and the careful staging of the *concordia*
of father and two sons. He began the **Colosseum** and rebuilt the burned **Capitoline temple**
(shifting the debris himself, the plebs working *en masse*).

## Succession and significance

Vespasian's deepest achievement was a **clear, secure succession**: he sharply distinguished
**Titus** (made *particeps imperii*, censor, and uniquely **praetorian prefect** and bearer of
the praenomen *Imperator* — the military arm of the regime) from the younger **Domitian** (kept
to consulships and the title *princeps iuventutis*), expecting Titus to succeed and Domitian to
follow. He used Titus' prefecture to do the "dirty work" (eliminating the last Republican nobles
and pretenders) while preserving his own reputation for *clemency*. Dying in **79** ("*vae, puto
deus fio*"), he handed power **peacefully to his son** — the first emperor to do so by blood —
founding the dynasty that refounded the Principate and broadened it toward the provincial elites.

## Historiography

The sources are deeply shaped by **Flavian partiality** — Josephus' *Jewish War* (written in
Vespasian's old house on his pension) in an "extreme form," the elder Pliny's flattering history
left unpublished. **Tacitus** (the surviving quarter of the *Histories*) gives a "very mixed"
portrait: best for Rome that Vespasian won, "better than expected," yet grasping for money and
served by associates "no better than the discredited minions of Otho and Vitellius."
