# The Kingdom of Kongo

The **Kingdom of Kongo** was the most powerful and best-documented state of **west-central Africa** — a
centralized BaKongo kingdom that, on the eve of European contact, ruled a realm of provinces from a capital
at **Mbanza Kongo**. It is the central case of the **UNESCO [[gha-vol4-12th-16th-century|General History of
Africa, Vol. IV]]** chapter on Equatorial Africa and Angola (ch. 22, Vansina), and a pivotal actor in the
early **[[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]]**. *(Distinct from the modern
**[[congo-free-state|Congo Free State]]** of Leopold II.)*

## Formation and structure

Tradition holds that **Lukeni lua Nimi** founded Kongo (c. late 14th c.) by crossing the Congo river and
conquering the Mbundu chiefdom of **Mbanza Kongo**, blending conquerors and natives ("nobles with nobles,
commoners with commoners"). By 1483 it was a **centralized kingdom of six provinces** (Mbamba, Soyo, Nsundi,
Mpangu, Mbata, Mpemba) under the ***manikongo*** (king), who appointed provincial governors (the *mani*
lords). It already had an **aristocracy**, slave farm-labour, long-distance **trade**, and a money economy
using the **nzimbu** (cowrie-like shells from Luanda island) as currency — a genuinely complex African
state encountered by the Portuguese at its height.

## Portugal and Christianity

The Portuguese reached Kongo in **1483**, beginning one of the most remarkable early encounters between an
African and a European state. The *manikongo* **Nzinga a Nkuwu** was baptized as **João I (1491)**, and his
son **Afonso I (Mvemba a Nzinga, r. 1509–43)** made Christianity the **state religion**, corresponded as an
equal with the kings of Portugal and the Pope, sent Kongolese to study in Europe (his son Henrique became a
bishop), and tried to build a Christian, literate Kongo on his own terms.

## The slave trade and decline

Afonso's project was corroded by the very connection that enabled it: Portuguese (and São Tomé) demand for
**enslaved people** drew Kongo ever deeper into the [[atlantic-slave-trade|slave trade]], and Afonso's
famous letters protest the **depopulation and disorder** the traffic caused — even as the kingdom itself
remained a slaving power. The rise of Portuguese **Angola** (Luanda, 1575) turned an ally into a predator;
after the **Jaga** invasions and mounting Portuguese pressure, Kongo's army was destroyed and its king
killed at the **battle of Mbwila (1665)**, after which the kingdom fragmented into warring factions, though a
titular monarchy survived into the twentieth century.

## Significance

Kongo is the wiki's anchor for **west-central African statehood** and for the **African side of the early
Atlantic system** — a sophisticated kingdom that engaged Christianity and Europe diplomatically and on its
own initiative, only to be hollowed out by the slave trade. Afonso I's letters are among the most important
**African voices** in the documentary record of the period.
