# Genghis Khan (Chinggis Khan / Temüjin)

## Overview

**Temüjin** (c. 1162–1227), proclaimed **Chinggis Khan** ("universal/fierce ruler")
at a *quriltai* in 1206, was the founder of the [[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] and
one of the most consequential individuals in world history. From a fractured and
marginal position on the steppe he unified the Mongol and Turkic tribes into a single
disciplined polity and launched the conquests that would produce the largest
contiguous land empire ever assembled. [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Biran, Ch 20;
Arnason, Ch 18) presents him as the origin of an "extreme version of **dynastic
charisma**" so powerful that it outlived the unified empire, shaped the successor
khanates, and left its mark on the later history of the Inner Eurasian heartland.

## Career

- **Rise on the steppe.** Surviving a youth of exile and clan warfare (narrated in
  the *Secret History of the Mongols*), Temüjin built a following through personal
  loyalty (the *nökör* companions) rather than pure kinship, defeated his rivals, and
  was acclaimed Chinggis Khan in 1206.
  The *Secret History of the Mongols* (de Rachewiltz shorter version, ll. 1232–4000)
  details the legendary origins, birth, hardships, capture and first alliances.
  "At the beginning there was a blue-grey wolf, born with his destiny ordained by
  Heaven Above. His wife was a fallow doe. They came crossing the Tenggis." (ll.
  1239-1243). This preceded the settlement "at the source of the Onan River on
  Mount Burqan Qaldun" where the lineage began (ll. 1245-1247). Alan Qo'a bore sons
  after Dobun Mergen's death and declared: "Every night, a resplendent yellow man
  entered by the light of the smoke-hole or the door top of the tent... They are
  the sons of Heaven." (ll. 1497-1511). The arrow-bundle lesson to her sons:
  "If, like the bound arrow-shafts, you remain together and of one mind, how can
  anyone deal with you so easily?" (ll. 1527-1529) contributed_to later emphasis
  on unity. Bodončar, excluded as "a fool and a half-wit" (ll. 1541-1543), left
  saying "'If I die, I die; if I live, I live!'" (ll. 1563), enabling his founding
  of independent lineage and the Borjigin clan (ll. 1779-1781). Clan etymologies
  and feuds (Dörben split ll. 1369-1393; Ambaqai's revenge call ll. 1979-1985)
  illustrate fragmentation.
  Yisügei abducted Hö’elün from the Merkit Čiledü (ll. 1989-2103); Qutula qa'an
  fought Tatars unsuccessfully (ll. 2137-2147). At Temüjin's birth at Deli’ün
  Boldaq "he was born clutching in his right hand a clot of blood the size of a
  knucklebone" (ll. 2157-2159), named Temüjin after a captured Tatar (ll. 2161-2165).
  Betrothal to Börte (Onggirat) featured Dei Sečen's dream of "a white gerfalcon
  clasping both sun and moon" (ll. 2223-2233). Yisügei was poisoned by Tatars on
  return (ll. 2337-2343) and died urging Mönglik to "bring back my son Temü†in"
  (ll. 2385).
  After Tayiči’ut abandonment (ll. 2532-2534; "Yisügei Ba’atur is dead, and as my
  sons are not grown yet" ll. 2440-2441), Ho'elun foraged with "a pointed stick from
  a spruce" (ll. 2578-2582) and sons fished. Temüjin and Qasar killed half-brother
  Bekter over game (ll. 2708-2726); mother reviled with "Like a Qasar dog snapping
  at its own afterbirth; Like a panther assailing a cliff..." recalling the birth
  clot (ll. 2742-2770). Captured after "The little rascals have shed their down,
  The snotty ones have grown up!" (ll. 2808-2809), Temüjin hid in thicket on
  Tergüne Heights (ll. 2832-2836), twice turned back by apparent Heaven signs
  (saddle, white rock ll. 2850-2866), was seized, then escaped cangue at feast by
  striking guard and floating in Onan stream (ll. 2902-2913). Sorqan Šira of Suldus
  saw him but "I shall not tell them" (ll. 2936-2937) and passed warnings; his sons
  Čimbai and Čila’un hid him in wool cart, smashed cangue (ll. 3058-3068). Sorqan
  later sent him on a "tawny barren mare" with bow, arrows, food (ll. 3096-3106).
  This escape contributed_to his survival.
  Reunited, the eight horses were stolen; Temüjin met Bo’orču who said "Friend,
  you came to me being in great trouble... I will be your companion... my name is
  Bo’orču" (ll. 3206-3228). They recovered the geldings after pursuit and arrow
  exchange (ll. 3242-3276). Bo’orču refused share: "I became your companion because
  you, a good friend, were in trouble... I won’t take them" (ll. 3286-3296),
  emphasizing nökör loyalty over kinship or booty. Later "He jumped on his chestnut
  horse... and came with Belgütei" (ll. 3404-3410). Jelme joined when "Old ‡arči’udai...
  handed him over to Temü†in" (ll. 3492-3496).
  After wedding Börte, the sable coat was given to Ong Qan (Kereyit), sworn friend
  of father Yisügei: "In return for the black sable coat, I shall bring together for
  you Your divided people" (ll. 3448-3452). The Three Merkit raided for revenge on
  Hö’elün's abduction ("We have now seized their women to take our revenge for Hö’elün!"
  ll. 3640-3642), capturing Börte; Temüjin appealed to Ong Qan ("O Qan my father, to
  rescue my wife" ll. 3735-3736) who recalled the promise and pledged forces (ll.
  3751-3779). Jamuqa concurred, detailing campaign (ll. 3849-4000).
  These events established personal loyalty bonds and early alliances that
  `preceded_by` the 1206 quriltai.
- **Reorganization.** He broke the old tribal structure into a **decimal military
  system** (units of 10/100/1,000/10,000) cutting across clans — the [[mongol-army|Mongol
  army]] that was also the empire's administrative skeleton — established the
  *keshig* imperial guard, promoted by merit and loyalty, and (by tradition)
  promulgated the *jasaq*/*yasa* — though [[morgan-the-mongols|Morgan]] argues the idea of
  a single fixed written Mongol law code is largely a later construction (see
  [[the-mongol-yasa]]).
- **Conquests.** Campaigns against the Tangut **Xixia** and the Jurchen **Jin** in
  north China, then the devastating destruction of the **Khwarazmian Empire**
  (1219–1221) in Central Asia and Iran — the campaigns that opened the empire's
  westward expansion and earned its fearsome reputation (Nishapur, Merv, Bukhara).
- **Death (1227)** during the final Xixia campaign; succeeded by his son Ögödei, with
  the empire continuing to expand under his descendants.

The *Secret History* (de Rachewiltz, ll. 11001–16085) supplies precise detail on the 1206 quriltai and its immediate aftermath. "In the Year of the Tiger (1206) they all gathered at the source of the Onan River. They hoisted the white standard with nine tails and there they gave Činggis Qa’an the title of qan." (ll. 11254-11262) `preceded_by` the unification campaigns; `produced` the formal decimal reorganization. Činggis appointed ninety-five commanders of a thousand (Bo’orču, Muqali Gui Ong, Šigi Qutuqu, Sübe’etei, ‡ebe and many others listed ll. 11292-11350), excluding the People of the Forest. He rewarded loyal nökör with seats of honour, immunity from punishment for up to nine crimes (e.g. Šigi Qutuqu ll. 11416-11422; Bo’orču and Muqali ll. 11648-11652), and specific commands: Bo’orču the ten thousand of the right wing (ll. 11652-11654), Muqali the left (ll. 11674-11676). The ten thousand keshig (guards) were established as the main body: "The ten thousand guards attached to Us shall be strengthened and become the main body of the army." (ll. 13015-13018) Dayguards, nightguards and quiverbearers received strict rotation rules, higher status than outside commanders of a thousand (ll. 13133-13147), and specific duties over the palace, food, standards and judicial matters shared with Šigi Qutuqu (ll. 13478-13492). These measures `part_of` the Mongol army's decimal and guard institutions and `contributed_to` central control cutting across old tribal lines.

From the *Secret History* (de Rachewiltz, exclusive range ll. 7001–11000, Ch. 6–8): the 1203 betrayal plot by ‡amuqa, Altan, Qučar and Nilqa Senggüm against Činggis ("My sworn friend Temü†in has messengers sent with secret communications to Tayang Qan of the Naiman... If you do not take him by surprise and strike at him, what will become of you?" ll. 7633-7647) `contributed_to` the rift with Ong Qan; `preceded_by` the father-son declarations at the Black Forest (ll. 7527-7539). Warned by horse-herders Badai and Kišiliq, Činggis fled by night ("they have pledged to surround and capture you" ll. 7934-7935), `produced` escape from the trap at Qalaqal†it Sands. Loyal nökör Bo’orču and Boroqul saved Ögödei ("Boroqul had sucked the wound-clogging blood, letting it trickle from the corners of his mouth" ll. 8276-8286) `part_of` the personal loyalty system. Vanguard Uru’ut and Mangqut "do indeed know how to fight" (ll. 8026-8032) chosen on merit `contributed_to` victory; "four hounds" Jebe, Qubilai, ‡elme and Sübe’etei described as iron-chained elite ("Their foreheads are of hardened copper, They have chisels for snouts" ll. 10229-10235) `part_of` [[mongol-army]]. Naiman crushed at Naqu Cliff ("Tumbling down from the height of Naqu Cliff, they piled on top of each other... like heaps of rotten logs. The following morning Činggis Qa’an finished off Tayang Qan" ll. 10465-10474) `produced` conquest of the Naiman; Jamuqa's desertion ("At my words Tayang Qan has lost his head And in panic he has scrambled to the heights" ll. 10439-10441) `contributed_to` the outcome. Justice and †asaq: Naya’a detained Qulan but cleared after testimony; "made this case a matter of law" and promoted ("He is a truthful man, I shall entrust him with an important task!" ll. 10553-10625) `contributed_to` merit advancement. Sübe’etei given iron cart and standing orders for pursuit ("making this a matter of law, whoever then transgresses it shall be seized and beaten... cut them down on the spot" ll. 10846-10854; battue limits to spare mounts ll. 10834-10842) `produced` early administrative/judicial decrees. Rebellious Merkit "had the Merkit distributed here and there down to the last one" (ll. 10756-10757) `contributed_to` breaking tribal structures. These events `preceded_by` the 1206 quriltai and decimal reorganization; `enabled` the outward campaigns.

On the eve of the 1219 Sarta’ul (Khwarazm) campaign, Yisüi Qatun advised designating a successor: "Of your four sons, the heroes whom you have begotten, which one will you designate as your successor?" (ll. 15077-15079). A sharp quarrel erupted when Ča’adai called Jochi "this bastard offspring of the Merkit" (ll. 15117); Jochi seized him by the collar and challenged him to archery and wrestling (ll. 15119-15157). Kökö Čos intervened with a long reminder of Börte’s abduction during warfare and the equal upbringing of the sons (ll. 15173-15253). The brothers agreed on Ögödei as "steady and reliable" (ll. 15381-15397); Tolui pledged to "remind him of what he has forgotten, I shall wake him up when he has fallen asleep" (ll. 15497-15499). Činggis ratified: "If Ögödei speaks such words, that will do." (ll. 15485-15486). `caused_by` the need for stability before the long western campaign; `produced` the designation of Ögödei. This material `preceded_by` the actual Khwarazm operations.

## Trade Policy and the Reversal of Steppe Commerce

[[may-mongol-conquests-2012|Timothy May]] emphasizes a side of Chinggis Khan often lost
behind the conqueror: he was an active **promoter of trade** who reversed the old terms
of steppe–sedentary commerce. Where nomads had previously gone to border markets as the
weaker party, the newly wealthy Mongols made merchants **come to them** and paid fair
prices — Chinggis cultivated Muslim traders from early on (the Baljuna covenant), posted
route guards (*qaraqchin*) at the 1206 *quriltai*, and personally financed an
international caravan to the Khwarazmian Empire. Its **massacre at Otrar (1218)** —
"at bottom a pocket book issue" (Allsen), since Chinggis and his princes had invested in
it — triggered the annihilation of Khwarazm and the westward expansion. This commercial
mindset is the seed of the Pax Mongolica and the
[[chinggis-exchange|Chinggis Exchange]] that bear his name.

## Counterfactual Significance

Chinggis Khan is a paradigmatic test case for **Great Man vs. structural causation**
(a standing wiki controversy). Arnason frames his enterprise as "a new beginning
favoured by particularly unsettled conditions on a distinct but potentially strategic
periphery" — i.e. structural conditions made a steppe unifier *possible*, but the
specific scale, speed, and durability of the Mongol achievement are hard to detach
from his personal organizational genius and the charismatic legitimacy he created.
A much-cited DNA study suggests roughly **8 per cent of men** between Uzbekistan and
northeast China carry a Y-chromosome lineage plausibly traceable to Chinggis Khan and
his close male relatives — a striking, if debated, index of his demographic as well
as political reach.
