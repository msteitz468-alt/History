# Mapuche (Araucanians)

## Overview

The **Mapuche** (called *Araucanians* by the Spanish) of south-central Chile are notable in
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Volume III]] above all for their **sustained
resistance to imperial conquest**. A society of dispersed, kin-organized agro-pastoral
communities without a centralized state, the Mapuche halted [[inca-empire|Inka]] expansion at
roughly the **Maule River** and then waged the long **Arauco War** against the Spanish from the
mid-sixteenth century, preserving effective autonomy south of the **Bío-Bío** for some three
centuries — into the independent-republic era.

## Significance

The Mapuche are the volume's clearest case that **stateless societies could be more, not less,
resistant** to conquest than centralized empires: lacking a single capital or ruler to seize
(as Pizarro seized [[inca-empire|Atahualpa]]), they could not be decapitated, and their
decentralized war-making frustrated both Inka and Spanish. They feature in the conquest-era
chapter on the southern margins of Spanish rule.
