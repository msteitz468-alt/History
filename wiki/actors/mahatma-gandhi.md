# Mahatma Gandhi

**Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi** (1869–1948), the **Mahatma** ("great soul"), was the leader who
transformed the [[indian-national-congress|Indian National Congress]] from an elite debating society
into a **mass movement** and made **non-violent resistance** the method of India's struggle for
freedom. Documented for the wiki through Percival Spear's
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965).

## Ideas: satya, ahimsa, satyagraha

Spear locates Gandhi's "mystique" in a union of **original ideas, tactical flair, and an uncanny
insight into the mass/peasant mind.** A self-described but unorthodox Hindu, he wove a system at once
ethical, spiritual, and political. At its centre was **satya (truth)** — the pervasive spirit of life,
pursued through self-discipline; and its method, **ahimsa (non-violence)** — meeting the opponent with
reason and entreaty, and where met by violence, bearing **suffering cheerfully as self-purification**
("accepted suffering had healing and converting qualities"). Those who dedicated themselves to this
were **satyagrahis** ("truth-fighters"). He envisaged a simple **peasant society of self-supporting
village republics**, rejecting western mechanisation (hence hand-spinning, the *charkha*), and put
**humanity before caste** — championing the untouchables as **Harijans** ("children of God"), removing
his own sacred thread, welcoming them to his *ashram*. This fearless **communal self-criticism**
underwrote his equally fearless demand for India's political rights — and ultimately cost him his life
at the hand of a Hindu orthodox assassin.

## The campaigns

Gandhi developed *satyagraha* during two decades in **South Africa** (to 1914), returning as the known
leader of a successful passive-resistance movement. In India he repeatedly **dramatised** the issue
with "an unerring instinct": a **hartal** (religious strike) against the **Rowlatt** bills; in the
charged aftermath of the **Amritsar/Jallianwala Bagh massacre (1919)**, the **Non-cooperation
movement** (1920–22, allied with the Muslim **Khilafat** cause) — which he called off after the
**Chauri Chaura** violence (1922), insisting means matter as much as ends; and, most famously, the
**Salt March to Dandi (1930)**, walking sixty miles to make illicit salt where others merely walked
out of the Assembly. He branded the British government not a tyranny to be fought but a **"satanic"
institution** with which no conscientious person could cooperate.

## Significance

Spear ranks Gandhi as the figure in whom "the inner meaning of modern Indian history" culminates — the
leader who gave the national movement a **peasant mass base, a moral method, and a popular idiom** the
Congress had wholly lacked, getting his image across to an illiterate population "in ways which made an
immediate appeal to the ordinary man." His non-violent technique reshaped twentieth-century protest
worldwide; his vision of a decentralised, self-reliant, communally-harmonious India remained a moral
touchstone (and a reproach) for the [[jawaharlal-nehru|Nehruvian]] state that succeeded the Raj.
