## Overview

Niẓâm al-Mulk (lit. "Order of the Kingdom"; 1018–1092) was the Persian vizier who served the Seljuk sultans Alp Arslan and Malikshah for nearly thirty years, making him the effective administrator of the largest Islamic empire of the eleventh century. Born in Tus (Khurasan) into a bureaucratic family with Samanid-era roots, he rose through provincial administration to become the most powerful non-royal figure in the Seljuk domains. His two principal legacies were the *Siyasatnama* (Book of Government), the most influential Persian manual of statecraft produced in the Islamic Middle Periods, and the network of Nizamiyya madrasas, the institutional model that shaped Islamic higher education for centuries.

## Statecraft and the Seljuk Order

Nizam al-Mulk was the architect of what Hodgson calls the "amîr-a'yân system" in its mature Seljuk form — a political arrangement in which Turkic military commanders (amîrs) held iqta' land assignments in lieu of salaries while Persian bureaucratic families like his own provided the administrative and legal infrastructure. He understood this as a workable substitute for the old caliphal bureaucratic centralism, not its equal. His *Siyasatnama* is explicitly concerned with how to maintain order and legitimacy in conditions where the sultan's authority depended on managing — rather than commanding — the military élite.

He worked closely with the 'Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad, treating it as a legitimating symbol for Seljuk power: the sultan was the "sword" of the caliph's "pen." This relationship underpinned the emerging [[sunni-internationalism]] synthesis — the notion that military power and religious-legal authority were complementary rather than competing.

## The Nizamiyya Madrasas

Nizam al-Mulk's most enduring institutional contribution was the Nizamiyya madrasa network. The Baghdad Nizamiyya, founded in 1067 and formally opened in 1091, was the most famous, but he established similar colleges in Nishapur, Isfahan, Basra, Herat, Merv, and other major Seljuk cities. The network had several strategic functions:

**Staffing Seljuk administration.** Trained jurists produced by the Nizamiyya could staff qadi courts and administrative bureaus throughout the empire, binding the provinces to a common legal-scholarly culture.

**Favoring the Shafi'i madhhab.** Nizam al-Mulk himself adhered to the Shafi'i legal school and endowed his madrasas with Shafi'i curricula. This gave Shafi'ism an institutional advantage, though it did not eliminate the Hanafi, Hanbali, and Maliki schools, which retained their own institutions.

**Attracting and anchoring the 'ulama.** By offering salaries, housing, libraries, and stipends, the Nizamiyya drew the finest scholars of the age into a regularized institutional relationship with Seljuk power. The most famous beneficiary was [[al-ghazali]], who held the chair of the Baghdad Nizamiyya from 1091 until his famous withdrawal into Sufi life in 1095.

**Waqf endowment model.** Crucially, the madrasas were endowed as permanent waqf foundations — inviolable religious endowments — rather than direct royal grants. This gave them institutional stability across political discontinuities. As Hodgson notes, the waqf system meant that madrasas and khâniqâh lodges could function "almost exclusively" on endowment funds, independent of the political fate of their founders.

## Death and the Nizari Isma'ilis

Nizam al-Mulk was assassinated in 1092 by a Nizari Isma'ili agent — a killing described by later sources as coordinated with the death of Sultan Malikshah, who died weeks later under unclear circumstances. The *Siyasatnama* contains an extensive section on the Isma'ilis that functions as a policy argument for their suppression; the Isma'ilis returned the favor. His death triggered a succession crisis that accelerated the fragmentation of Seljuk central power.

## Role in the New Sunni Synthesis

Hodgson places Nizam al-Mulk at the heart of the emerging "new Jama'i-Sunni" synthesis of the Earlier Middle Period — the alignment of Shafi'i-Ash'ari scholarly culture, Seljuk military patronage, and [[ulama]] institutional autonomy that defined the social order between the collapse of the caliphate and the Mongol conquests. His madrasas were the primary institutional vehicle through which this synthesis reproduced itself across generations and territories. After his death, the Nizamiyya network continued to function and was widely imitated — the pattern of amirs and sultans founding madrasas as acts of pious patronage and political legitimation became standard practice throughout the Sunni world.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Nizam al-Mulk's systematic patronage, the [[madrasa]] would likely have remained a more localized and variable institution rather than a standardized international network. The specific alignment of Shafi'i-Ash'ari scholarship with Seljuk state power that shaped subsequent Sunni intellectual culture might have been quite different. His role in bringing [[al-ghazali]] to Baghdad was also fortuitous: it was the Nizamiyya chair that gave al-Ghazali the institutional standing from which his synthesis of Shari'ah-mindedness and Sufism could exercise its enormous influence.
