# Edward I, King of England (r. 1272–1307)

**Edward I**, son of **[[henry-iii-of-england|Henry III]]**, "laid the foundations for late
medieval monarchy." [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (D.A. Carpenter, Ch. 12) presents him
as the antithesis of his father: six foot two and a great warrior, schooled in the
[[simon-de-montfort|Montfortian]] reform crisis, who combined "the exploits of the knight
with the judgement of the general." Where Henry was a *rex pacificus*, Edward conquered
**Wales**, nearly conquered **Scotland**, and forged a state "possessed of formidable
resources."

## Reform of the Realm

Returning from crusade in 1274, Edward secured his peace with a torrent of legislation —
the great **Edwardian Statutes** (Westminster I 1275, Gloucester 1278, Mortmain 1279,
Westminster II and Winchester 1285) — extending and improving Henry II's procedures, and
meeting local grievances with knightly sheriffs and eyres open to verbal complaints
(*plaints*). The number and forms of civil actions multiplied (from nineteen writs to over
a hundred). He encouraged subjects to bring **petitions** to **[[english-parliament|parliament]]**,
opening "a clearly defined channel of communication between the localities and the centre" —
"the most important function" of parliament for ordinary people. The **quo warranto**
enquiries (from 1278) enforced the lesson that "all jurisdiction stemmed from the crown."

## The New Finances

Edward reformed crown finance without the political cost that destroyed his grandfather
[[john-of-england|John]]. He used **Italian bankers** (the **Ricciardi of Lucca**) for
credit; he established the **customs duty on wool** (1275), "a regular and permanent feature
of royal revenue" paid largely by foreign merchants; and he made **parliamentary taxation**
regular. Wardrobe receipts tell the tale of burgeoning power: c. £20,000 a year early in the
reign, a "colossal" sum by the war years after 1294. His state, "much more soundly based"
than John's, derived far more of its revenue from consensual taxation.

## The Conquest of Wales and the Scottish Wars

- **Wales**: in two wars (**1277**; **1282–1283**) Edward destroyed the infant principality
  of **Llywelyn ap Gruffudd**, who was killed in 1282; Gwynedd was "annexed and united to
  the crown of England" and secured by a great ring of **castles** costing £80,000.
- **Scotland**: the deaths of Alexander III (1286) and his heir let Edward arbitrate the
  succession; when his chosen candidate **John Balliol** proved disobedient, Edward invaded
  (1296), won at **Dunbar**, and "simply brought the Scottish kingdom to an end." Revolts
  brought a second victory at **Falkirk** (1298) and a great ordinance for Scotland's
  government (1305). It "looked like total victory" — undone only by his incompetent son.

Carpenter weighs R.R. Davies's thesis that these conquests reflected an inexorable
domination of Britain by the English state, against the role of **contingency** (the
extinction of the Scottish line; Llywelyn's blunders). What was new was less hostility than
the **power within Britain** to end Welsh and Scottish independence — power made possible by
the loss of Normandy in 1204, which freed England to concentrate its resources inward.

## The Expulsion of the Jews (1290)

The **expulsion of the Jews** (1290) showed the Edwardian regime "both at its most
oppressive and its most consensual." With Jewish wealth already destroyed by Henry III's
taxes, the crown's protective interest had lapsed; lesser men were now their main clients
and resented magnate trafficking in Jewish debts. By volunteering the expulsion Edward both
gratified the religious prejudices crystallised in the **ritual-murder libel** (first
officially sanctioned at Lincoln, 1255) and — his "main immediate aim" — secured **knightly
consent to taxation** at the 1290 parliament. "By an awful but telling paradox this most
fearful of Edward's acts was actually the one which showed him at his most flexible and
conciliatory." See [[medieval-anti-judaism]].

## Significance

Edward consummated the **thirteenth-century refashioning of the English state**: the
establishment of the **Commons** in parliament (knights and burgesses attending the
tax-granting parliaments after 1290), the customs, and government by credit and consent. It
was a "parliamentary tax-based state" — but one "based on the exploitation and the repression
of the great bulk of the population," its later war taxes falling "with disproportionate
heaviness on the peasantry."
