# Ben Chifley

Ben Chifley (1885–1951) was Prime Minister of Australia from July 1945 until December 1949. A former engine-driver, he was Curtin's indispensable treasurer and the architect of Australia's post-war reconstruction program — the most ambitious expansion of Commonwealth social and economic capacity in Australian history to that date. He articulated Labor's purpose as reaching "the light on the hill … by working for the betterment of mankind," while presiding over a government whose energies were increasingly spent by 1949.

## Overview

Chifley was "one of only four members of the new [1941 Labor] government with previous ministerial experience." As Curtin's treasurer he controlled wartime finance, expanded Commonwealth banking powers, and took over income-tax collection from the States in 1942 (a permanent shift). He was minister responsible for the **Department of Post-War Reconstruction** (established December 1942), with the young economist H.C. Coombs as director-general. The Royal Commission on Monetary and Banking Systems (1937) had already signalled the direction: Chifley was among its commissioners and the one who favoured banking nationalisation.

## Post-War Reconstruction

The Chifley government's aim was to "consolidate these advances by converting war industries to peacetime purposes, thereby maintaining full employment and lifting living standards." GDP had grown 25 per cent in real terms between 1939 and 1945. The reconstruction programme included:

- **Demobilisation**: Commonwealth Reconstruction Training Scheme, nearly 20,000 ex-servicemen and women completed university courses.
- **Migration**: 180,000 displaced persons from Europe from 1947 — "a larger number than those resettled in any other country outside Europe other than the United States." A decisive break with British-preference immigration under Minister Arthur Calwell.
- **Welfare expansion**: child endowment (1941), widows' pensions, maternity benefits for Indigenous mothers, unemployment and sickness benefits — all funded through the National Welfare Fund.
- **Industrial development**: munitions factories converted to civilian production; General Motors set up car production (first Holden 1948).

## Banking Nationalisation

The most contentious episode: after the High Court disallowed legislation compelling State and municipal authorities to bank with the Commonwealth Bank, Chifley pursued nationalisation of the private banks. This was politically damaging — mobilising doctors and bank employees against "state regimentation" and reinvigorating the new Liberal Party (formed 1945 as UAP successor under Menzies).

## Cold War and Limits

Chifley's government was "beholden to American economic supremacy" and could not withstand Cold War pressures. He joined the IMF reluctantly in 1947, reintroduced petrol rationing in 1949, and ultimately established ASIO under US pressure. He opposed communism as a political philosophy but initially refused to suppress the Communist Party as a civil liberties matter — until the 1949 miners' strike changed his assessment.

## "The Light on the Hill"

In June 1949, at the NSW ALP conference, Chifley gave the phrase its enduring resonance: Labor's purpose was not "putting an extra sixpence in somebody's pocket or making somebody prime minister," but to bring "greater happiness to the mass of the people … the light on the hill." The speech was given while the lights were literally out due to the coal shortage.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Chifley as treasurer and reconstruction minister, the institutional frameworks of post-war Australia — the welfare state, the Commonwealth Bank as effective central bank, unified income tax, mass migration — might have developed more slowly or differently. His bank nationalisation campaign, however, arguably cost Labor the 1949 election and handed Menzies the Liberal Party's founding mandate.

## Historiography

Chifley is regarded as one of Australia's most significant prime ministers. The chapter (Darian-Smith) treats his government as genuinely transformative but constitutionally constrained: "post-war reconstruction relied on agreement with the fractious States" after referendum failures in 1944 and after the High Court struck down key measures. His reputation for integrity and commitment to working-class values has made "the light on the hill" a permanent catchphrase of Australian Labor politics.
