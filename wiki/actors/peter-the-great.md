# Peter the Great (Peter I of Russia)

**Peter I "the Great"** (1672–1725), tsar from 1682 and the first **Emperor of Russia**
(1721), transformed Russia from a peripheral, inward-looking tsardom into a **European great
power** through sweeping westernizing reform and victory over Sweden. He is the pivotal figure
in "the changed relations of East and West," the subject of M. S. Anderson's chapter in
[[new-cambridge-modern-history-v6|*The New Cambridge Modern History*, Vol. VI]].

## Career

- **Rise to power.** After a contested minority (the regency of his half-sister Sophia and the
  *strel'tsy* musketeer revolts), Peter took sole power, captured the Ottoman fort of **Azov**
  (1696) to seek a Black Sea outlet, and undertook the famous **"Grand Embassy"** to western
  Europe (1697–98) — studying shipbuilding and technology incognito in the Dutch Republic and
  England. He returned to crush the **strel'tsy** revolt (1698) and launch a generation of
  reform.
- **The Great Northern War (1700–21).** Peter's central struggle, against **Charles XII of
  Sweden**, the dominant Baltic power. After early defeat at Narva, his rebuilt army won the
  decisive **Battle of Poltava (1709)**; by the Treaty of Nystad (1721) Russia took the eastern
  Baltic provinces, supplanting Sweden as the regional hegemon — a major shift in the European
  [[balance-of-power|balance of power]].
  From Bushkovitch (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II, ll. 15003–15064 exclusive range): Motives complex — "The competition for power and territory was unavoidable, and in addition the religious factor is not to be discounted"; Great Embassy 1697-8 discussions of Baltic port; Ingria/Kexholm "part of Novgorod and then of Russia since the beginning of recorded history and were lost only in the Time of Troubles"; "the population remained to a large extent Orthodox"; marched on Narva first (disturbing ally Augustus); after Narva defeat "in 1702–3 he captured Ingria... At the mouth of the Neva Peter began to build St Petersburg, precisely the naval and commercial port he had wanted"; 1706-8 offers to keep only "St Petersburg and its immediate vicinity"; post-Poltava "Peter took Viborg in 1710 to provide a better defensive perimeter"; "At Nystad in August, 1721, Peter got all he wanted: Ingria, Karelia, Viborg, Estonia and Livonia. Russia had a port, with a large defensive perimeter around it, and was now a European power, dominant in the north-east." Justifications mixed secular (Pufendorf just war recovery of unjustly taken territories, "Sweden’s failure to uphold Russian claims to Livonia") and religious ("he clearly believed that God was on his side"; objected to liturgy claiming fight "for the cross of Christ" — "Sweden was proud, and the war was not about faith, but about measure"). "Many motives made up Peter’s decision... He felt that his cause was just, even according to the latest European thinking. He believed that Russia needed a port to maintain its prosperity and power." `caused_by:` Baltic access + territorial recovery + prestige; `produced:` [[treaty-of-nystad-1721|Treaty of Nystad]] + [[saint-petersburg|Saint Petersburg]]; `part_of:` [[great-northern-war-1700|Great Northern War]]. Persian campaigns "entirely commercial in inspiration" for silk areas. 

From Fuller (imperial army, ll. 15859–15889): "Peter I initially sought to engage capable military specialists abroad, but soon ordered all males of the gentry estate into permanent service in the army, navy or bureaucracy"; "Table of Ranks in 1722, he established the principle that acquisition of an officer’s rank conferred nobiliary status even on commoners." "Peter the Great who devised expedients to extract more cash... poll (or soul) tax of 1718 that required every male peasant as well as most of the male residents... to pay to the state an annual sum of 74 (later 70) kopecks... he was able to push state income up to 8.7 million roubles by the close of his reign. Whereas military outlays had constituted roughly 60 per cent of state expenditure in old Muscovy, under Peter they may have consumed between 70 and 80 per cent of the state budget." `produced:` service state + poll tax fiscal base; `part_of:` westernization-of-russia.
- **Westernizing reforms (1699–1724).** A "series of great innovations": a reorganized,
  Western-style **standing army and navy** ([[military-revolution]]); a new administrative
  apparatus (the Senate, the colleges, the provinces); subordination of the **Church** (the
  Holy Synod, abolishing the patriarchate); the **Table of Ranks** opening service nobility to
  merit; compelled adoption of Western dress and customs (the famous beard tax); new taxation
  (the poll tax) and state-fostered industry; and the founding of a new capital, **St
  Petersburg** (1703), Russia's "window on the West."
  From Wirtschafter (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II, ll. 9331–9333): "Established in 1722 by Peter the Great, the Table of Ranks regulated promotion and ennoblement in military, state and court service. In state service, promotion to rank eight conferred hereditary nobility, whereas ranks nine to fourteen granted personal nobility." Personal nobles had privileges but children did not inherit; created service-related raznochintsy. `produced:` avenues for social mobility + raznochintsy category. 
  From Engel (ll. 10825–10827): 1701 law "mandating German clothes, hats and footwear applied to the wives and children of men of all ranks of the service nobility, as well as of leading merchants, military personnel... only clergy and peasants were exempted." Part of broader effort to transform elite women as consorts for new service men; fines for non-compliance. `part_of:` westernization-of-russia; `contributed_to:` elite cultural shift.

## Significance

Peter is the founder of imperial Russia and a turning point in European history: he added a
vast new power to the states system, shifted the [[balance-of-power|balance]] in the north and
east, and set the template for autocratic modernization that echoes through Russian history. He
helps fill the wiki's flagged **Russia/Eastern Europe** gap.

From Lieven (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II intro + ch.1, ll. 4155, 4323, 5141–5142): Peter’s proclamation as emperor (1721) and successors created “effective military and fiscal state apparatus” making Russia “core member of the European concert of great powers.” 1721 Senate ceremony in St Petersburg added “imperator vse-rossiiskii” using foreign Latin terms “imperiia and imperator” to brand Russia a European empire. Reforms (with Catherine) initiated “full-scale Westernisation of the country’s ruling elites” — one of two “great clichés” uniting the imperial era. “Until the emergence of Japan... the only example of a non-Western polity which had challenged effectively the might of the European great powers.” (ll. 4295). Baltic acquisition opened trade routes; Finland annexed for St Petersburg security (ll. 4305–4307). Peter’s St Petersburg as “window on the West” embodied shift (cf. saint-petersburg.md, Hughes ch.4).

## Additional Detail from Freeze (ed.) 2002 (Alexander ch., lines 3311–3800)
Early career: "Peter I is associated with many ‘firsts’... the first to travel incessantly by land and water and to venture abroad, the first to be titled emperor and ‘the Great, Most Wise Father of the Fatherland’" (l3327–3337). 1682 Streltsy mutiny forced co-tsarship w/ Ivan V under Sofia; 1689 coup after Streltsy 15 May massacre (Peter witnessed, "suffered a nervous shock"); Sofia to Novodevichii (l3415–3417). "Peter did not, however, immediately assume Sofia’s role in government" (l3419). Azov: 1695 siege failed ("lack of a flotilla precluded any naval blockade"; storm losses); 1696 success with Voronezh fleet; "the fortress’s capitulation with stunning ease on 19–20 July 1696"; Roman triumph with "He came, he saw, he conquered" (l3431–3439).

Grand Embassy 9 Mar 1697–25 Aug 1698: "270 persons (Peter himself incognito among many ‘volunteers’)... cost the stupendous sum of 200,000 roubles"; "the longest and grandest Muscovite embassy ever"; shipyards Holland/England; met William III, Frederick III, Leopold I, Augustus II; "diplomatic terms the Grand Embassy largely failed" (Karlowitz peace, Ryswick) but recruited experts (Farquharson, Deane, Perry); cultural exposure (Kunstkammer, Newton mint, anatomical museums) (l3479–3517). Post-return: crushed 1698 Streltsy ("ghastly tortures and massive public executions"); "divorced Evdokiia by incarceration in a monastery" (l3517–3519).

Reforms/war: "With an artisan’s eye and pragmatic mind, Peter envisioned the transformation of Russia into a great power"; manifesto Apr 1702 "good order [Polizei] and whatever else contributes to the improvement [Cultur] of a people" (l3531–3535). "By the end of the reign twenty-one general and thirty-two partial levies conscripted over 300,000 men"; navy grew to 34 ships of line + etc. by 1724 (l3579–3587). "The Table of Ranks, the system of fourteen grades... applied to all three branches"; "Military ranks enjoyed preference over civil" (l3591). Industry: iron "by 1725 twenty-four ironworks... produced more than 14,000 tons" (half Demidov Urals) (l3603). St Petersburg: "the fortress and cathedral of Peter and Paul... parallels with imperial Rome"; "By 1725 St Petersburg had a population of about 50,000" (l3785–3787).

Northern War/Poltava: "The Swedes’ decisive defeat of the Russian siege of Narva compelled the tsar to reconstitute and rearm the army almost overnight" (l3577). "flying column to intercept the Swedish relief force at Lesnaia on 28 September 1708"; Poltava 27 June 1709: "Within two hours the Swedish forces crumbled"; "some 9,000 Swedish dead on the field; 16,000 more surrendered" (l3633–3665). "Peter took the field, his hat and saddle shot through" (l3665). "placed a ‘firm stone’ in the foundation of St Petersburg" (l3675). Cultural revolution: presses increased; Vedomosti; "Renaissance elements... education, book-learning"; "The Reformation informed Petrine efforts to transform the Orthodox Church" (Holy Synod); "The Scientific Revolution had enthralled Peter" (Academy of Sciences 1724, Leibniz correspondence) (l3689–3779).

## Massie (1980) Synthesis (detailed biography spine)

Massie’s narrative restores the driving individual personality and operational texture behind the structural transformation documented in Cambridge Russia Vol. II. Formation: self-taught practical genius ignited by the 1688 boat discovery at Ismailovo and Archangel voyages (“I am a pupil and need to be taught”); scarred by 1682 Streltsy trauma witnessed at age 10. Azov campaigns: 1695 failure taught necessity of fleet; 1696 success with personal labor at Voronezh produced first Russian naval victory and Roman-style triumph. Grand Embassy (1697–98): incognito as “Peter Mikhailov”; Zaandam/Amsterdam shipyards (built frigate *Apostles Peter and Paul*); critique of Dutch empiricism led to England (Deptford, Spithead fleet review “momentous day”); William of Orange hero; Burnet’s portrait (“mechanically turned... wrought much with his own hands”). Return: personally shaved boyars’ beards, exiled Eudoxia, crushed 1698 Streltsy with systematic torture/executions (Peter often present, ivory-handled cane). 

Great Northern War: Narva disaster (left camp night before; “We must not lose our heads”); rebuilding via mass levies, bell-melt artillery, new Guards + dragoons; founding of St. Petersburg (16 May 1703) on Neva marsh despite war (“city built on bones”); Lesnaia (28 Sept 1708) interception of Lewenhaupt supplies (“mother of Poltava”); Mazepa defection + Baturin massacre; Poltava (8 July 1709) — redoubts entangled Roos; main assault broken on prepared lines + 70 guns; Peter present (hat/saddle/chest icon hit); Swedish army destroyed; “final stone in the foundation of St. Petersburg has been laid.” Pruth (1711): over-optimistic southern thrust; encirclement; recognized “he was Russia”; abandoned Black Sea ambitions permanently (“Lord God drove me out... like Adam out of paradise”). Later: Finland galley operations + Hangö (1714 “Russia’s first victory at sea”); Paris 1717 (curiosity at Mint/Academy/hospitals; “meaningless” treaty); Alexis crisis (ultimatums “transmit to a worthy stranger”; flight lured back by Tolstoy; public confession, torture, death in custody 26 June 1718 — Peter celebrated Poltava next day); Nystad (10 Sept 1721) — Livonia/Estonia/Ingria/Karelia ceded; Empire proclaimed by Senate/Synod petition at Trinity Church (“Father of the Fatherland, Peter the Great, Emperor of All Russia”).

Institutional revolution: Table of Ranks (1722) merit over birth; poll (soul) tax; Holy Synod (Patriarchate abolished); St. Petersburg assemblies (mixed-sex Western sociability). Character: tall, restless, energetic, hot-tempered, heavy drinker; deep loyalty to circle (Lefort grief: “Now I am alone”; Menshikov “Mein Brudder”; Catherine stabilizing partner at Pruth and after); capable of personal brutality when project threatened yet merciful post-victory (returned Rehnskjold sword; dinner with captured generals); exuberant in victory (danced/sang on tables); worked personally to end (Lakhta boat rescue Nov 1724 triggered final illness). Died 28 Jan 1725 (bladder/gangrene); Catherine I succeeded with Guards support. 

Massie supplies the vivid primary texture (foreign observers, Peter’s words) that makes the “revolution from above” human and contingent.
