# The Kingdom of Benin (Edo)

The **Kingdom of Benin** — the **Edo** state centred on **Benin City** (in modern southern Nigeria, *not*
the modern Republic of Benin) — was one of West Africa's oldest and most artistically renowned forest
kingdoms. Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO [[gha-vol5-16th-18th-century|General History of
Africa, Vol. V]]** (ch. 15, Alagoa).

## The Oba and the city

Benin was ruled by the **Oba**, a sacred king, from a great walled capital famous for its earthworks (the
**Benin walls**, among the largest pre-mechanical earthworks in the world). The kingdom reached an early
apogee under the warrior-king **Ewuare** (15th c.), who expanded its power across the Edo, western Igbo, and
eastern Yoruba lands. Its court is celebrated above all for its **brass and bronze art** — the commemorative
**heads of obas**, the relief **plaques** depicting court life and warfare (cast by the lost-wax method),
and carved ivory — among the supreme artistic achievements of pre-colonial Africa.

## Trade with Europe

Benin was one of the **first West African states to engage the Portuguese** (from the 1480s), trading
pepper, cloth, ivory, and — for a period — **enslaved people**, in exchange for brass (the *manillas* that
fed the bronze-casters), cloth, and firearms. Notably, Benin **restricted its sale of male slaves** for long
stretches, exercising deliberate control over its participation in the [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic
trade]] (a point used on that page to argue African states were not compelled to sell).

## Decline and the 1897 sack

Benin endured into the nineteenth century but, resisting British commercial and political demands, was
destroyed in the **British Punitive Expedition of 1897**, which sacked Benin City, exiled the Oba, and
looted thousands of the **Benin Bronzes** — now scattered through Western museums and the subject of a major
modern **restitution** debate.

## Significance

Benin is a foremost case of **enduring African statehood and high court art**, and — through the Benin
Bronzes and their looting — a central symbol in the contemporary reckoning with colonial plunder.

## Davidson on Benin: City, Court, and Bronze Art (1966)

[[davidson-african-kingdoms-1966|Davidson, *African Kingdoms* (1966)]] devotes his entire
Chapter 5 ("Forest Kingdoms") to the coastal and forest kingdoms, with Benin as the central case.
His material draws primarily on Dutch and Portuguese primary accounts and supplies the richest
eyewitness descriptions of Benin City in the existing wiki coverage.

### Ife as Artistic Predecessor

Davidson establishes the artistic genealogy that later scholarship confirms: "Two of the most
creative of the wholly African coastal societies centered at the cities of **Ife and Benin**."
By "the best archaeological guess," the sculptors of **[[ife-kingdom|Ife]]** were producing
"superbly beautiful bronze heads and figures in honor of their kings" by **c. 1200 CE** —
"sometime in the next two centuries they transmitted their bronze-casting techniques to the
people of Benin." Ife thus functions as the artistic parent of Benin's celebrated bronze
tradition. (See [[ife-kingdom]] for the separate Ife page.)

Davidson notes that according to legend the peoples of Ife came from the Western Sudan and
"may even have had some connection with the ancient metalworking land of Kush" — though he
acknowledges this is speculation.

### Ewuare the Great (r. 1440–1473)

Davidson identifies **Ewuare the Great** as the pivotal early ruler: "oral histories say
that he was a skilled magician, a doctor, a warrior, a brave and wise man. He is said to have
enclosed 201 towns and villages within his spreading power and to have made Benin a famous
city." It was Ewuare's Benin that the Portuguese first contacted in the 1470s–80s. Davidson's
dates (1440–1473) for Ewuare's reign align with GHA V material.

### Portuguese Contact and Diplomatic Relations

Davidson provides detail on the earliest Portuguese interactions:

- Benin's coast was "first discovered in the 1470s," but **Joao Affonso d'Aveiro** did not
  penetrate the interior until **1486**, when he attempted to establish diplomatic and trading
  ties; "samples of pepper from Benin were 'presently ... sent to Flanders and to other parts,
  and soon it fetched a great price.'"
- The King of Benin dispatched **Ohen-Okun** — "a man of good speech and natural wisdom," a
  priest at the shrine of the ocean god Olokun — as ambassador to Portugal in 1486 or shortly
  after; he returned safely with gifts for his king.
- "Thereafter, for more than 100 years, Portugal maintained diplomatic contacts with Benin."
- An English trader who visited in 1553 noted that the king "could speak the Portuguese tongue,
  and had learned it as a child."
- Trade goods from Benin included "striped cotton garments," "blue cloths" sold on the rivers
  of Gabon and Angola, jasper stones, leopard skins, and pepper; "they refuse to sell men"
  (male slaves) — corroborating the GHA/Ajayi-Crowder account of Benin's deliberate restriction
  of male slave sales.

### The City: Dutch Eyewitness Accounts

Davidson quotes at length from two Dutch compilations that give the fullest external
descriptions of Benin City:

**Dierick Ruyters (sojourning under King Ehengbuda, published 1602 by Pieter de Marees):**
The city was "very great when you go into it [for] you enter a great broad street, not paved,
which seems to be seven or eight times broader than the Warmoesstraat in Amsterdam; it goes
straight in and never bends." Ruyters' lodgings were "at least a quarter of an hour's going
from the gate, and yet I could still not see to the end of the street." Many additional great
streets opened from the main street, each "long: 'You cannot see to the end of them because of
their great length.'" Houses stood "in good order, one close and even beside the other as the
houses in Holland stand. Those belonging to men of quality and others have two or three steps to
go up, and along the front of them there is a kind of gallery where you may sit in the dry."
The king's court compound was enormous: "I went so far within these buildings that I passed
through four such squares, and wherever I looked I still saw gate after gate which opened into
other places."

**Olfert Dapper (drawing on Samuel Blomert, c. 1660s):** The king's palace "occupies as much
space as the town of Haarlem and is enclosed within walls. There are numerous apartments for the
Prince's ministers, and fine galleries most of which are as big as those on the Exchange at
Amsterdam. They are supported by wooden pillars encased with bronze, where their victories are
depicted, and which are carefully kept very clean." These **bronze plaques** were "used as a form
of mnemonics by the king's 'court remembrancers,' to help them reconstruct their stories of
Benin's past" — today a "pictorial record of what went on in Benin during the 16th and 17th
Centuries" including scenes of court life, royal hunting parties, ceremonial occasions, and
"some plaques even mark the coming of the Portuguese, wearing their heavy armor and unsuitable
woolen clothing."

Dapper's overall judgment: "These Negroes are much more civilized than others on this coast.
They are people who have good laws and a well-organized police; who live on good terms with the
Dutch and other foreigners who come to trade among them."

### Political and Religious Structure

Davidson describes Benin's governance:
- The **Oba** was an absolute monarch — "godlike; anyone who believed otherwise was executed as
  a heretic" — but the actual business of governing was left to **counselors** who directed
  military, economic, and agricultural affairs in the Oba's name; the Oba's time was consumed by
  ceremonies, sacrifices, and his harem "of a hundred or more wives."
- The Bini believed in "a supreme god who created and ruled the earth; they considered it useless
  to worship him, however, since he was already benevolent." They worshipped "numerous lesser
  gods, who they felt could mediate for them with the supreme god." Human sacrifices were offered
  "not to the gods, but to the devil, whom the Bini blamed for all their misfortunes."
- Trade was under the control of the king, whose agents met foreign merchants "magnificently
  dressed, wearing necklaces of jasper or fine coral." Benin's currency consisted of "cowrie
  shells and metal rings called manillas."

### Hunters as Elite Professionals

Davidson describes Benin's **professional hunters** as among the kingdom's most prestigious
citizens. Only exceptional boys could become hunters after rigorous apprenticeship — tracking,
silent forest movement, multi-day survival, and "a whole catechism of secret rituals." Elite
**elephant hunters** used blowguns and poisoned darts; their prey might crash through the forest
for days before dying. One tusk went to the Oba; the hunter received the other plus the head,
heart, and lungs as powerful talismans.

### Decline and Fall

Davidson traces Benin's trajectory through European contact: the slave trade mushroomed,
"farming and commerce were slighted and the economy—inevitably—started to collapse. The Oba,
believing his bad fortune was the work of the devil, ordered more and more human sacrifices to
turn the tide. But by 1897 the disintegration was complete; that year a British force found the
city of Benin all but deserted and littered with the bodies of sacrificial victims. After four
centuries of greatness, Benin had finally passed into history."

*Reliability note:* Davidson's 1966 popular synthesis uses the Dutch sources accurately; the
Ruyters/Dapper descriptions he quotes are genuine 17th-century eyewitness-derived accounts, also
used by GHA V. His interpretive framing (the "decline into human sacrifice" explanation) reflects
mid-20th-century popular historiography and should be treated with caution — later scholarship
situates the 1890s violence in the context of British commercial aggression and Benin's political
crisis, not simple religious decay.
