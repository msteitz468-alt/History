# The Rise of the Ottoman Empire (c. 1300–1402)

The **Ottomans** rose in the fourteenth century from "a small band of frontiersmen" in
north-western Anatolia into a "sizeable regional state" straddling Anatolia and the Balkans — the
power that would inherit Byzantium and dominate the eastern Mediterranean for centuries.
[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (I. Metin Kunt, Ch. 22) frames the century as "exactly" framing "the
first phase in the evolution of the Ottoman state": from its obscure origins to its near-destruction
by Timur in 1402.

## Origins and the Historiographical Debate

The state's origins "are obscure because, at first, it was such an insignificant entity" — beneath
the notice of the Islamic chroniclers of Tabriz, Damascus and Cairo, and unrecorded by the Ottomans
themselves until the fifteenth century. It emerged on the **Bithynian frontier** between Byzantium
and the Turkish *beyliks*, where the collapse of the Seljuk sultanate of Rum (under the Mongols) had
created a fluid borderland. Kunt surveys the long debate over Ottoman origins: **Paul Wittek's**
classic ***gazi* thesis** — the Ottomans as frontier "fighters for the glory of Islam," their
religious zeal "the life-force for large-scale conquest" — has been qualified by **Rudi Lindner** and
**Cemal Kafadar**, who stress the **inclusiveness** of early Ottoman society toward its non-Muslim
neighbours and recast the *gazis* as an open, newly formed "tribe" gathered around **Osman Bey**
rather than zealots of Muslim exclusiveness.

## The Conquest of the Balkans

From Osman (d. c. 1324) and his son **Orhan**, the Ottomans took the Byzantine cities of Bithynia
(Bursa, Nicaea), then crossed into **Europe** (Gallipoli, 1354), exploiting Byzantine civil wars to
establish themselves in **Thrace** (Adrianople/Edirne became their capital). Under **Murad I**
(1362–1389) and **Bayezid I "the Thunderbolt"** (1389–1402) they conquered the Balkans: subjugating
**Bulgaria** and the Serbian and Greek lords, defeating the Serbs at **[[battle-of-kosovo-1389|Kosovo]]
(1389)**, and crushing a western **crusade at Nicopolis (1396)**. Byzantium was reduced to
Constantinople and a few enclaves, a tributary awaiting conquest.

## The Catastrophe at Ankara (1402)

At the height of Bayezid's power, the Ottoman "enterprise almost came to a premature end" when the
Central Asian conqueror **Timur (Tamerlane)** invaded Anatolia and shattered the Ottoman army at the
**battle of Ankara (1402)**, capturing Bayezid himself. The state fragmented into a decade of civil
war among his sons (the "Ottoman Interregnum") before it was reconstituted — and resumed its advance
toward the conquest of Constantinople (1453), which belongs to the next volume.

## Significance

The rise of the Ottomans is the great new fact of the late-medieval eastern Mediterranean — the
volume's vindication of its "horizontal," wider geographical scope. A frontier *beylik* became, in a
century, the empire that absorbed the Balkans, encircled and would soon extinguish
**[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]]**, and replaced the Christian and Latin order of the Aegean and the
Balkans with a new Islamic imperial power. Timur's blow at Ankara only delayed what proved
irreversible.

## The Fifteenth Century: Recovery, Conquest and Empire (NCMH VII)

[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Elizabeth Zachariadou, Ch. 32) carries the Ottoman story from the
near-collapse at **Ankara (1402)** to the empire of Mehmed II and Bayezid II.

- **The Interregnum and recovery (1402–1413).** Timur's victory shattered the state and restored the
  Anatolian *beyliks*, but the European core (**Rumelia**), held by the *udj-bey* march-lords, recovered
  fast. After the civil war (*fetret devri*) of Bayezid's sons, **Mehmed I** (1413–21) reunited the
  state. The dynastic strife was entangled with a genuine **social-religious revolution** under the
  mystic **Sheyh Bedr ed-din**, preaching common ownership and Muslim–Christian fraternisation,
  crushed c. 1416.
- **The war-party state.** Zachariadou frames Ottoman politics as a contest between a **moderate
  party** (the vizier Halil Çandarlı — favouring vassalage, trade and Islamic-traditional order) and
  the **war-mongers** of holy war (*djihad*). **Murad II** (1421–51) inclined to peace and vassalage,
  but after defeats by the crusaders (Hunyadi) avenged himself at **Varna (1444)** and **Kosovo
  (1448)**.
- **[[mehmed-ii|Mehmed II "the Conqueror"]] (1451–81).** The **[[fall-of-constantinople-1453|fall of Constantinople
  (1453)]]** made the Ottoman Empire "the successor of the Byzantine" and gave the war party total
  power (Halil executed). Mehmed abolished the vassal states and annexed them directly — Serbia
  (1459), the Morea (1460), Trebizond (1461), Bosnia (1463) — and fought Venice (1463–79), seizing
  the Genoese Crimea (1475) and even landing at **Otranto** in Italy (1480). His was a relentless
  conquest-state, financed by debased coinage and the confiscation of *vakf* and *mülk* lands for
  *timars*.
- **The institutions.** The chapter details the mature Ottoman order: the *timar* cavalry (*sipahi*),
  the Christian-vassal and march-lord systems, and above all the **slave-soldier household** — the
  **janissaries** and the *devshirme* child-levy of Christian boys, "the highest posts of the empire"
  open to men of humble, non-Turkish origin (the *kapu kulu*). The dynasty itself practised
  **fratricide**, made law by Mehmed II.
- **Bayezid II (1481–1512): toward mercantilism.** A pious, consolidating ruler who shifted from holy
  war to organisation and trade — closing the **Black Sea** (Kilia and Akkerman, 1484) as an Ottoman
  preserve, building a navy, defeating Venice (1499–1503), and welcoming the **Jews expelled from
  Spain (1492)**. Power was passing to a **merchant** rather than imperialist milieu — just as the
  Portuguese rounding of Africa began to threaten the Levant trade.

## The structure of power and the apogee, 1512–1650 (Imber)

Colin Imber's [[imber-ottoman-empire-1300-1650|*The Ottoman Empire, 1300–1650*]] carries the
story to the mid-seventeenth century and anatomizes the **institutions** that made the empire.
He stresses that the Ottoman state was **dynastic, multinational, and sultan-centered**, not
simply "Turkish" or "Islamic": by 1650 it spanned Europe, Asia, and Africa, Christians
outnumbered Muslims in many provinces, and the political élite was largely of convert and
Balkan/Caucasian origin — "it was ultimately the person of the sultan … that held the Empire
together."

**The conquering and apogee sultans.** [[selim-i|Selim I]] (1512–20) doubled the empire,
crushing the [[safavid-empire|Safavids]] at **Çaldıran (1514)** and conquering the
[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]] lands — **Syria (1516)** and **Egypt (1517)** — and with the Holy
Cities the primacy of Sunni Islam. [[suleiman-the-magnificent|Süleyman the Magnificent]]
(1520–66) brought the empire to its **apogee**: Belgrade, Rhodes, **Mohács (1526)**, the siege
of **Vienna (1529)**, Baghdad (1534), Barbarossa's Mediterranean, and the codification of the
**[[ottoman-law-kanun|kanun]]**. The empire reached its **maximum size** in 1590.

**The machinery of empire.** Imber's institutional chapters show the empire resting on: a
distinctive **succession** practice (royal **fratricide**, made notorious by Mehmed III's
killing of 19 brothers in 1595, replaced after 1603 by **seniority** and the confinement of
princes); the **[[devshirme|slave household (kapıkulu)]]** levied from Christian boys to staff
army and government; the **[[janissaries|Janissary]]** slave-infantry corps; the
**[[timar-system|timar]]** cavalry-fief order in the provinces; and the dual
**[[ottoman-law-kanun|shari'a/kanun]]** legal system dividing society into the *askeri*
(military/servant) and tax-paying *reaya*.

## Decline, European Impact, Reforms, and Dissolution (Lapidus)

From the eighteenth century the empire experienced decentralization impairing the imperial state concept, commercial decline in the Mediterranean, and European ascendancy. European commercial and political dominance subverted Muslim states dependent on commercial revenues (see [[lapidus-a-history-of-islamic-societies-2014]]).

**Nineteenth-century reforms (Tanzimat and predecessors):** Selim III introduced the Nizam-i Jedid (modern army corps, taxation, technical schools) but was defeated by ulama and janissary opposition and deposed in 1807. Mahmud II (r. 1807–39) centralized power, destroyed the janissary corps in 1826, weakened the ulama by absorbing endowments/courts/schools into state ministries, dissolved the Bektashi order, built factories, encouraged mining/agriculture, ended monopolies (1838), introduced postal (1834), telegraph (1855), railroads (from 1866). The Tanzimat (1839–76) extended reforms to social/religious affairs: Hatt-i Sherif of Gulhane (1839) recognized rights to life, property, honor and equality of religious groups; 1856 Hatt-i Humayun promised equality for non-Muslims and army service rights; Christians appointed to councils (1867); non-Muslim communities reorganized with lay majorities; new commercial/penal codes based on French law (Commercial Code 1850, Penal 1858 etc.); Ottoman Land Code 1858 defined mulk, miri, waqf, matruka, mawat lands (privatization advanced integration into global economy); 1869 Mecelle civil code (Islamic substance but sultan authority, state courts, required registration of marriages/divorces); Family Law 1917 replaced Islamic personal law with European system. Educational reforms created rushdiye middle schools, army secondary schools, experiments in elementary education, and attempts at a university integrating professional/humanistic/religious studies. The Tanzimat "repudiated the autonomous functioning of Islamic educational and judicial institutions and challenged the very concept of Muslim supremacy" in favor of a strong integrated state.

**Young Ottomans and Young Turks:** The reforms created a new Westernized bureaucratic elite (memurs) and opposition literary intelligentsia. The Young Ottomans (1860s, Namik Kemal et al.) advocated constitutional government synthesizing Ottoman/Islamic values with reform, simplified Turkish language, and rights of citizens. In 1876 they staged a coup bringing Abd al-Hamid II to power and a constitution/parliament (suspended soon after). The Young Turks (Committee for Union and Progress, 1908 coup) were resolutely secular: aggressive secularization of schools/courts/law codes, first measures for women's emancipation (1913–18); reduced shaykh al-Islam powers, transferred Muslim courts to Ministry of Justice, colleges to Ministry of Education (1916); new family code 1917. The CUP program turned Turkish: the "Turkish idea" propagated by literary clubs (Turkish Homeland Society, Turkish Hearth); Ziya Gokalp celebrated folk culture and reformed Islam for Turkish ethos; Abdallah Jevdet presented secular basis for Turkish nationalism. The word "Turk" previously pejorative was reclaimed.

**World War I dissolution and population engineering:** Ottomans entered WWI on Central Powers side (German aid, fear of Russia, ambition to recover provinces). Allies defeated them; Britain/France partitioned Arab provinces per Sykes-Picot (1916), Balfour Declaration (1917) for Jewish national home in Palestine. Lausanne Treaty (1923) recognized independent Turkey in present boundaries (Anatolia + small European strip). The war produced "vast and virtually unimaginable rearrangement" via transfers, deportations, massacres, genocide. ... "The war had cut the Gordian knot of Ottoman loyalties by stripping away most of the non-Muslim and non-Turkish populations."

**1914 Entry (grounded in Strachan Ch. 8–9):** The empire entered through its own devices for its own interests — reversal of decline after Balkan wars and Libya, assertion of sovereignty via abrogation of capitulations (8 Sept), and consolidation of Anatolian/Turkish core. “The Ottoman empire therefore entered the First World War through its own devices, and in pursuit of its own interests. It was neither the innocent pawn in Germany’s more ambitious concepts, nor the victim of Russian and British imperialism.” Secret alliance with Germany 2 Aug; arrival of Goeben and Breslau (10 Aug) at Dardanelles “ordained” entry. Triumvirate (Enver, Talaat, Djemal) drove policy; Enver authorized Black Sea raid (29 Oct) under Souchon. Britain’s seizure of Turkish dreadnoughts (29 July) and failure to end capitulations or guarantee against Russia swung opinion. On 14 Nov Sheikh-ul-Islam proclaimed holy war calling Muslims worldwide against Britain/France/Russia. Turkey’s army was “a bewildering blend of the new and the unreformed” (Liman mission); conscription fell on Anatolian peasants; no common language; rail/logistics inadequate for grand schemes (Caucasus, Suez). Capacity nonetheless tied down Entente forces for duration. Pan-Turanian (Caucasus/Turan) and pan-Islamic (Egypt) drivers coexisted with Ottomanism. See [[events/turkey-entry-1914]], [[events/goeben-escape-1914]], [[concepts/holy-war-1914]].

## Revival and the great turning point, 1650–1700 (Finkel)

Caroline Finkel's [[finkel-osmans-dream|*Osman's Dream*]] carries the narrative past Imber's
1650 endpoint. The mid-seventeenth-century crisis was checked by a **military and
administrative revival under the [[ottoman-empire|Köprülü]] grand viziers** (from Köprülü
Mehmed Pasha, 1656) — a household dynasty of vezirs who restored order, finances, and the
offensive, conquering Crete (Iraklion, 1669) and pressing into Habsburg and Polish lands. The
revival ended at the **[[siege-of-vienna-1683|second siege of Vienna (1683)]]**: its failure
triggered the Holy League's chain of victories, the loss of Buda (1686), and the **Treaty of
Karlowitz (1699)** — the surrender of Hungary and the **turn from expansion to retreat**.
Finkel insists this was a shift in the balance of power, not the simple senescence of a "Sick
Man" (see [[ottoman-decline-thesis]]): the empire remained resilient and adaptive, but
thereafter **diplomacy rather than conquest** increasingly governed its dealings with Europe —
the world of the [[eastern-question]] and the [[tanzimat-reforms]].

## Retreat, reform, and dissolution, 1700–1922 (Finkel)

Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the empire faced a long retreat before
Habsburg Austria and Romanov Russia — the [[eastern-question|Eastern Question]] of European
diplomacy — punctuated by efforts at **[[tanzimat-reforms|reform]]**: Selim III's "New Order,"
Mahmud II's destruction of the [[janissaries]] (1826), and the **Tanzimat** "reordering"
(1839–76) that built a centralized, codified, secularizing state and a first constitution
(1876). The reforms could neither contain Balkan Christian nationalism nor escape European
financial tutelage, and after Abdülhamid II's autocracy the **Young Turk revolution (1908)**
opened the final phase. Entry into the [[first-world-war-1914|First World War]] on the German
side led to defeat, the **Armenian genocide**, the loss of the Arab provinces, and — after the
nationalist resistance under Mustafa Kemal — the **abolition of the sultanate (1922)** and the
founding of the **Turkish Republic** (see [[fall-of-the-ottoman-empire-1922]]). Finkel stresses
the **continuities** between empire and republic against the myth of a clean break.
