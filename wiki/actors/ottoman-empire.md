# The Rise of the Ottoman Empire (c. 1300–1402)

The **Ottomans** rose in the fourteenth century from "a small band of frontiersmen" in
north-western Anatolia into a "sizeable regional state" straddling Anatolia and the Balkans — the
power that would inherit Byzantium and dominate the eastern Mediterranean for centuries.
[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (I. Metin Kunt, Ch. 22) frames the century as "exactly" framing "the
first phase in the evolution of the Ottoman state": from its obscure origins to its near-destruction
by Timur in 1402.

## Origins and the Historiographical Debate

The state's origins "are obscure because, at first, it was such an insignificant entity" — beneath
the notice of the Islamic chroniclers of Tabriz, Damascus and Cairo, and unrecorded by the Ottomans
themselves until the fifteenth century. It emerged on the **Bithynian frontier** between Byzantium
and the Turkish *beyliks*, where the collapse of the Seljuk sultanate of Rum (under the Mongols) had
created a fluid borderland. Kunt surveys the long debate over Ottoman origins: **Paul Wittek's**
classic ***gazi* thesis** — the Ottomans as frontier "fighters for the glory of Islam," their
religious zeal "the life-force for large-scale conquest" — has been qualified by **Rudi Lindner** and
**Cemal Kafadar**, who stress the **inclusiveness** of early Ottoman society toward its non-Muslim
neighbours and recast the *gazis* as an open, newly formed "tribe" gathered around **Osman Bey**
rather than zealots of Muslim exclusiveness.

## The Conquest of the Balkans

From Osman (d. c. 1324) and his son **Orhan**, the Ottomans took the Byzantine cities of Bithynia
(Bursa, Nicaea), then crossed into **Europe** (Gallipoli, 1354), exploiting Byzantine civil wars to
establish themselves in **Thrace** (Adrianople/Edirne became their capital). Under **Murad I**
(1362–1389) and **Bayezid I "the Thunderbolt"** (1389–1402) they conquered the Balkans: subjugating
**Bulgaria** and the Serbian and Greek lords, defeating the Serbs at **[[battle-of-kosovo-1389|Kosovo]]
(1389)**, and crushing a western **crusade at Nicopolis (1396)**. Byzantium was reduced to
Constantinople and a few enclaves, a tributary awaiting conquest.

## The Catastrophe at Ankara (1402)

At the height of Bayezid's power, the Ottoman "enterprise almost came to a premature end" when the
Central Asian conqueror **Timur (Tamerlane)** invaded Anatolia and shattered the Ottoman army at the
**battle of Ankara (1402)**, capturing Bayezid himself. The state fragmented into a decade of civil
war among his sons (the "Ottoman Interregnum") before it was reconstituted — and resumed its advance
toward the conquest of Constantinople (1453), which belongs to the next volume.

## Significance

The rise of the Ottomans is the great new fact of the late-medieval eastern Mediterranean — the
volume's vindication of its "horizontal," wider geographical scope. A frontier *beylik* became, in a
century, the empire that absorbed the Balkans, encircled and would soon extinguish
**[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]]**, and replaced the Christian and Latin order of the Aegean and the
Balkans with a new Islamic imperial power. Timur's blow at Ankara only delayed what proved
irreversible.
