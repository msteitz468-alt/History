# Catherine the Great

Catherine II (r. 1762–1796) was Empress of Russia, presiding over a period of imperial expansion, administrative reform, and cultural Westernisation.

## Biography and Rule

Catherine II (born Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst) came to the throne via the 1762 coup against her husband Peter III. Her reign realized much of the Petrine agenda in modified form while marking new departures in territorial expansion and elite acculturation. She embodied the paradox of strong female rule in a patriarchal autocratic system.

## Major Policies and Developments (from Freeze 2002)

**Foreign Policy and Expansion**  
Russia focused on the Black Sea as vital national interest (outlet and markets) rather than any primordial “urge to the sea.” The Russo-Turkish War (1768–74), triggered by Turkish miscalculation, ended with the Treaty of Kuchuk Kainarji (10 July 1774): Turkey ceded Azov and a Black Sea coastal strip; recognized Crimean independence; granted Russian merchant ships (not warships) passage through the Dardanelles; empowered Russia to construct a Black Sea fleet; and authorized the Russian ambassador to make representations for a newly established Orthodox Church in Constantinople. Later, Catherine annexed the Crimea (1783) and spoke (with Potemkin) of “liberating” Constantinople. Russia also participated in the three partitions of Poland (1772, 1793, 1795), obtaining primarily Belarusian territories in the first and contributing to Poland’s complete erasure from the map. (Freeze 2002, ll. 4131–4215: “The old chestnut of a primeval Russian ‘urge to the sea’ has long since faded...”; “The treaty forced Turkey to cede Azov... and to recognize the independence of the Crimean peninsula...”)

**Social Order and Serfdom**  
The empire remained overwhelmingly rural (>90% peasantry). Serfs (5.6 million male souls in the 1760s, 56.2% of the peasant population) were “invisible to Russian law and justice,” subject to squires who could sell, relocate, or modify their obligations at will. By the late eighteenth century, “serfdom” bore less resemblance to Old World serfdom than to New World slavery. The poll-tax burden declined in real terms with inflation and population growth, but legal status deteriorated: serfs (unlike state peasants) lost the right to petition the emperor after 1741 and had no legal mechanisms of resistance outside the commune and household. (ll. 4289–4357)

A historic shift occurred from quitrent to corvée as landlords responded to the price revolution (fourfold rise in grain, hemp, flax, and textile prices). Nobles with large estates profited enormously; profit from corvée rose from 36 copecks per male serf in 1710 to 10 roubles in 1800. Colonization of “New Russia” (Black Sea basin) involved massive resettlement of serfs onto high-yield lands (4–5:1 seed ratios vs. 3:1 central Russia); Odessa was founded in 1794. (ll. 4793–4859)

**Nobility and State–Society Relations**  
Peter III’s 18 February 1762 manifesto freed the nobility from obligatory service. Catherine’s 1785 Charter to the Nobility granted monopoly on ownership of serfs, rights to convene provincial assemblies, appoint local judges, exemption from corporal punishment, freedom to travel, and access to the Noble Land Bank (5–6% long-term loans). Partible inheritance and low agricultural productivity kept most nobles poor (four out of five owned fewer than 100 male souls). The manifesto and charter separated service from hereditary privilege, fostering a new noble consciousness while abdicating political pretensions in exchange for corporate rights. (ll. 4390–4405)

**Enlightenment, Culture, and Limits**  
Catherine positioned herself as an enlightened absolutist. Her “Great Instruction” (Bolshoi Nakaz) for the Legislative Commission (1767–8) proclaimed Russia both European and absolutist, invoking reason, rights, tolerance, and happiness. Moscow University was founded in 1755. Secular publishing expanded dramatically. Private presses were authorized in 1783. Yet the French Revolution triggered sharp repression: Radishchev’s *Journey from St Petersburg to Moscow* (1790) led to his trial and Siberian exile; Novikov’s publications were investigated and he was imprisoned; imports were periodically banned. (ll. 5005–5073)

**Pugachev Rebellion (1773–75) and Aftermath**  
The 1762 manifesto generated rumors that serfs too would be freed. Pugachev, a fugitive Don Cossack, proclaimed himself the returning Peter III and rallied Iaik Cossacks, Turkic peoples (Bashkirs, Kalmyks, Tatars, Kazakhs), Ural factory serfs, and eventually central Russian serfs. Manifestos promised serf freedom and vengeance on masters. Over 1,500 landlords were reported killed before suppression in August 1774 (after troops returned from the Turkish war). Pugachev was betrayed, paraded in a cage in Moscow, and executed. The episode demonstrated that the myth of the “good tsar” could mobilize serfs and exposed the incompatibility of justice and serfdom once universal service ended. It prompted the 1775 Statute on Provincial Administration (expanding provinces from 8 to 35) and the 1782 Law on Provincial Police to address “under-government.” (ll. 4867–4973)

## Historiography
Freeze (ed. 2002, ch. by Gary Marker) emphasizes Catherine’s self-conscious Petrine legitimation while highlighting limits of state power, the multinational empire’s management challenges, and the unintended consequences of the 1762 noble emancipation for serfdom’s moral foundations. The volume benefits from post-1991 archives but draws on established social-history scholarship (e.g., Kabuzan demographics, Mironov on price revolution and serf dues). It rejects simplistic images of omnipotent autocracy or linear Westernization, stressing instead the “poor state/weak state” constraints and the centrality of the non-Russian periphery. Contrasts with more narrative accounts (e.g., Riasanovsky) by foregrounding social structure, estate formation, and the 1773–75 rebellion as a pivotal moral/political crisis.
