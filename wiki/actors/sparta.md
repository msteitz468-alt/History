# Sparta (Archaic)

**Sparta** (Lacedaemon) was the dominant land power of Archaic Greece — a Dorian
city of the Eurotas valley that conquered neighbouring Messenia, reduced its people to
**helot serfdom**, militarized its whole society to hold them down, and by 546 BCE made
itself, through the **Spartan Alliance**, the acknowledged leader of Greece.
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 42, N. G. L. Hammond) treats it.

## The conquest of Messenia and the Helots

Sparta first subdued its own Laconia (razing Achaean Helos) then turned on the Dorians
of Messenia in the **First Messenian War** — the "Twenty Years' War" of Tyrtaeus,
datable to **c. 740–710** — capturing Mt Ithome, reducing the Messenians to **Helots**
("suffering like donkeys under great burdens"), and dividing the rich Stenyclarus plain
into some 3,000 lots for Spartiates. The Helots were state-owned serfs, bound by oath
not to revolt, who delivered half their produce and attended royal funerals in mourning.
This conquest more than doubled Sparta's serf population and gave it the manpower to
become a great power — but at the price of permanent insecurity. After the crushing
defeat by **[[argos|Argos]] at Hysiae (669)**, a great Helot-and-Messenian revolt — the
**Second Messenian War** (c. 640–600, Tyrtaeus's "battle by the trench"), backed by
Argos, Elis, Pisatis and Arcadia — almost destroyed Sparta before it slowly won through.
Out of these wars came the choice Tyrtaeus immortalized: "to die in the front line... is
honourable; to leave one's city and live as a beggar is the depth of misery."

## The Spartan system

The crises of the Messenian Wars forged the classical Spartan constitution — which
Aristotle rightly called a **"mixed constitution"**:

- **Two kings** (the *archagetai*, hereditary), with indivisible, total power in war
  (they sacrificed, took the omens, and commanded in the field) but, at home, only
  ceremonial and judicial functions (cases of inheritance and adoption) — "a democratic
  form of constitutional monarchy," their way of life strikingly modest.
- The **Gerousia** — 28 elders over sixty, elected for life, plus the two kings — which
  **monopolized the initiation of policy** and tried capital cases; "the credit for the
  consistent direction of Spartan policy must go primarily to them."
- The **five Ephors** — elected annually, who oversaw daily life and the *agoge*,
  "declared war annually on the Helots," held powers of summary arrest, and could
  suspend even a king; their constitutional rise was traced to the Messenian crisis.
- The **Apella** — the assembly of full citizens (the **Spartiates** or **Homoioi**,
  "Equals"), who elected the leaders and voted proposals up or down (by acclamation),
  but whose decision the Gerousia could quash by adjournment under the **"Rider to the
  Rhetra"** (the Delphic injunction "if the people declare crookedly, the Elders and
  Archagetai shall be adjourners") — the conservative check appended after the First
  Messenian War.

Beneath the Spartiates stood the **perioikoi** (free "dwellers-around," who did the
crafts and trade Spartiates scorned, and provided troops) and the **Helots**; above all
loomed the imperative to **maintain the citizen birth-rate** of a numerically thin elite
(perhaps 6,000 adult males c. 700). Aristotle's verdict: "the whole system of the laws
is directed towards one part of excellence, military valour." Yet early Sparta was also a
centre of **art, music and poetry** — Tyrtaeus, Alcman, the visiting Terpander and
Stesichorus; fine Laconian pottery and bronzework; the festivals of the Gymnopaidiai and
Carneia; and the proverbial beauty and freedom of Spartan women.

## The hoplite factor

The Archaic **[[hoplite-warfare|hoplite revolution]]** — heavily armoured spearmen
fighting shoulder-to-shoulder in the **phalanx** (named from the *hoplon* shield) —
first appears in the seventh-century Peloponnese (Hysiae a prime example). It broadened
and strengthened the propertied "top layer" who could afford the equipment, shifting the
meaning of *aristoi* from birth toward wealth-and-warfare. For Sparta, with its sizeable
body of trained, equal, professional-soldier Homoioi, "hoplite warfare might have been
invented specifically to ensure their success."

## The Spartan Alliance (Peloponnesian League)

Haunted through the sixth century by the fear of a Helot revolt instigated by Argos,
Arcadia or Pisatis, Sparta built a system of allies — first **Elis** (helping it crush
Pisatis, 590–580), then, after failing to conquer **Tegea** by the sword, switching
(on Delphic advice, recovering "the bones of [[orestes|Orestes]]") to offering itself as
**protector** rather than conqueror. From c. 560 this produced the **[[peloponnesian-league|
Spartan Alliance]]** — "the Lacedaemonians and the allies." Sparta posed as **liberator
from tyranny** (Thucydides: it "put down most of the tyrants... in the rest of Greece")
and protector against aggression, drawing in Elis, Arcadia, Sicyon, Corinth and Megara.
The decisive moment was the **defeat of Argos at Thyreatis in 546** (the "Battle of the
Champions"), after which Sparta was "acknowledged as the leading power in Greece," ally
of [[lydia|Croesus]] and [[saite-egypt|Amasis]], self-appointed champion of the Greeks
against the rising power of [[achaemenid-empire|Cyrus]].

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the conquest of Messenia, which created the Helot problem; the defeat at
Hysiae and the near-disaster of the Second Messenian War, which forged the militarized
constitution.
produced: the **helot serf-economy** and the soldier-citizen society; the **Spartan
Alliance**, the dominant Greek power-bloc on the eve of the Persian Wars; and the
Archaic anti-tyranny intervention that toppled tyrants across Greece (see
[[greek-tyranny]]).
contributed_to: the bipolar Sparta–Athens rivalry that would define Classical Greece.

## The reign of Cleomenes I (CAH IV)

By the time Persia reached the Aegean, Sparta was the **hub of the [[peloponnesian-league|Peloponnesian
League]]** and "the focal point in Greece" for any land war. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol.
IV]] (Ch. 6, Jeffery) carries the story into the reign of **[[cleomenes-i|Cleomenes I]]**
(c. 521–490). His policy reveals the **structural brake on Spartan power**: fear of **Helot
revolt** made him decline distant commitments (Samos 517, the Scythians 513, the
[[ionian-revolt|Ionian Revolt]] 499). Yet he intervened decisively where he could — expelling
the Athenian [[pisistratidae|tyranny]] (510), miscalculating by backing Isagoras against
[[cleisthenes|Cleisthenes]], and annihilating [[argos|Argos]] at **[[battle-of-sepea-494bce|Sepea]]**
(494). His feud with co-king **Demaratus** (whom he deposed by bribing Delphi) and his
violent death exposed the strains of dual kingship — after which Sparta reportedly abolished
**joint royal command** on campaign. These Helot-driven hesitations would shape the whole
Greek response to Xerxes.

## Historiography

Hammond reconstructs Archaic Sparta from **Tyrtaeus** (the contemporary war-poet), the
later constitutional analyses of **Herodotus, Xenophon and Aristotle**, and a thin
archaeological record (the Artemis Orthia and Menelaion sanctuaries) that confirms the
"very simplest standard of life." The dating of the Messenian Wars rests on Tyrtaeus and
the Olympic victor-lists; the much-elaborated later "history" of the wars (Pausanias's
fourth-century sources) he judges "worthless." The *Lycurgus* tradition and the
two-stage Rhetra/Rider remain debated.
