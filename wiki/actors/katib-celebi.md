## Overview

Kātib Çelebī (Muṣṭafā ibn ʿAbd Allāh, 1609–57) was the most significant broad intellectual of the Ottoman seventeenth century. An encyclopedist, bibliographer, geographer, and philosopher, he produced a bibliographical dictionary, a major work on geography, and the treatise *Mīzān al-ḥaqq* (The Balance of Truth), his last and most philosophically interesting work — which he was reportedly completing while drinking coffee at the moment of his death. A moderate, pragmatist, and defender of the rational sciences against both anti-rationalist populism and intellectual sclerosis, he represents the Ottoman equivalent of a public intellectual committed to the compatibility of Islam and philosophy.

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Ch. 57.

## The Balance of Truth

*Mīzān al-ḥaqq* is a plea for intellectual moderation and flexibility in the face of the cultural controversies of Çelebī's day — including the Ḳāḍīzādelī movement (see [[ottoman-philosophy]]). Key arguments:

**On coffee and tobacco**: Çelebī defends the permissibility of coffee-drinking (the immediate occasion for discussion of the Ḳāḍīzādelīs, who opposed it) on pragmatic grounds — there is no point forbidding what people will do regardless. He also allows tobacco, though Adamson confesses more sympathy for the other side on this question.

**On the rational sciences**: The treatise begins by insisting on the value of logic, mathematics, and astronomy for Muslim jurists (pp. 21–8). Without logic, how is a jurist to reason? Without mathematics, how is he to adjudicate a land-measurement dispute? Without astronomy, how is he to understand Koranic references to the lunar stations? These disciplines are not foreign luxuries but essential tools.

**On Ibn ʿArabī**: Çelebī defends the controversial philosophical Sufi [[ibn-arabi]] against his critics, noting that Ibn ʿArabī's theories are so difficult to understand that it would be uncharitable not to give him the benefit of the doubt (pp. 80–3).

**On intellectual decline**: Çelebī laments that advanced philosophical topics had retreated from the standard madrasa curriculum in his day. He was forced to pursue them outside the standard curriculum — like Plato, he says, who learned in the marketplace at Socrates' feet. His intellectual heroes are al-Ghazālī, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, al-Ījī, and the Shīrāzī philosopher Dawānī.

**On knowledge**: His credo: "knowledge can never be harmful, and ignorance is never beneficial."

## Context

Çelebī occupied a moderate position in the three-way contest between the Ḳāḍīzādelīs (popular anti-innovation movement), the Sufi orders, and the *ulema* (scholarly establishment). He acknowledged problems with both the ulema's corruption and some Sufis' excesses, but opposed the Ḳāḍīzādelīs' indiscriminate hostility to innovation. He was also aware of European science — his geography draws on European cartography — and represents an early Ottoman figure open to knowledge from outside the Islamic tradition without abandoning Islamic intellectual frameworks.

---

## Legacy

Kātib Çelebī is a key figure for historians arguing against the "Ottoman decline" narrative in intellectual history. His complaints about philosophical retreat in the madrasas are real, but his own productivity demonstrates that the tradition was far from dead. Later scholars like Khaled El-Rouyaheb have drawn on his work to reconstruct the rich tradition of Ottoman rational sciences.
