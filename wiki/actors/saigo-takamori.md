## Overview

Saigō Takamori (1828–1877) was the pre-eminent warrior-statesman of the Meiji Restoration and the leader of the last great samurai rebellion against the Meiji government. Celebrated in Japan as the archetypal man of the old order — loyal, austere, incorruptible — he was simultaneously a founder of the Meiji state and its most formidable armed opponent. His death in the [[satsuma-rebellion-1877|Satsuma Rebellion]] ended the era of samurai military resistance and made him a figure of enduring national myth.

## Role and Significance

Saigō came from Satsuma domain (modern Kagoshima Prefecture), one of the two great southwestern domains (with Chōshū) that drove the Restoration. He was one of the three dominant personalities of the early Meiji government, alongside Kidō Kōin (Chōshū) and [[okubo-toshimichi|Ōkubo Toshimichi]] (Satsuma). Keene notes, writing of the post-1878 period, that "the ministers had increasing cause to regret the loss of Kido and Ōkubo, who had maintained equilibrium between Chōshū and Satsuma in the cabinet" — a balance that had existed when Saigō was present, before his resignation.

Saigō broke with the government in 1873 in the seikan-ron (subjugation-of-Korea) debate. He advocated sending a provocative embassy to Korea that he expected would lead to war — an outcome he partly desired as a way of channeling the energies of disaffected former samurai. The caretaker government provisionally agreed, but upon the return of the Iwakura Mission from Europe, the proposal was overturned. Saigō resigned and returned to Satsuma.

Back in his home domain, he founded private military academies (shigakkō) that attracted thousands of former samurai resentful of the dismantling of their class. When government agents attempted to remove arms from Satsuma in early 1877, his followers launched the rebellion. Saigō led the uprising but was defeated by the government's conscript army. He died in the final battle of the campaign, September 1877.

## The Satsuma Rebellion and Its Aftermath

Saigō's decision to lead the rebellion, and the manner of his death, became the central elements of his legend. He is remembered not as a traitor but as a man of the old virtues who could not accommodate himself to the modernizing state. His authenticity as a warrior of the old school was contrasted, favorably by many Japanese, with the calculating pragmatism of the Meiji oligarchs who survived him.

His political shadow was long. Itagaki Taisuke had been "a firm supporter of Saigō" before the rebellion; after the defeat, Itagaki redirected his energies to civilian agitation for popular rights, drawing the explicit lesson that armed resistance to the Meiji state was futile. In this way, Saigō's defeat was a necessary precondition for the [[freedom-and-popular-rights-movement|Freedom and Popular Rights Movement]].

By 1889, the government was prepared to restore his honor. On the day of the promulgation of the Meiji Constitution (February 11, 1889), "some men who had been put to death as rebels were not only pardoned but decorated: Saigō Takamori was promoted to the senior third rank, and Yoshida Shōin to the senior fourth rank." The rehabilitation reflects both the government's desire for national reconciliation and the impossibility of sustaining official condemnation of a man so revered by the Japanese public.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Saigō not resigned in 1873, or had he chosen civilian rather than military opposition to Meiji policies, the trajectory of the Freedom and Popular Rights Movement might have been very different. His presence might also have moderated samurai resentment through his enormous personal authority. Without his resignation and the 1877 rebellion, the government might have faced a more politically sophisticated opposition rather than an armed one it could suppress militarily.

## Historiography

Keene's biography of Meiji treats Saigō largely through the emperor's eyes and through the political consequences of his actions rather than through Saigō's own perspective. The text notes the emperor's discomfort with political calculations — particularly the apparent conspiracy among Satsuma councillors — but does not directly address how Meiji felt about Saigō's death or legacy.

The historiography of Saigō is extensive and contested. He has been claimed as a symbol of traditional Japanese values, as a proto-nationalist, and as a tragic hero. His rehabilitation in 1889 formalized the shift from treating him as a rebel to treating him as a misguided loyalist of genuine virtue. [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]] provides important indirect evidence about how his memory functioned in the political culture of the 1880s.
