# Charles de Gaulle (1890–1970)

French general and statesman. Junior brigadier general and undersecretary of
war in June 1940, he rejected the [[franco-german-armistice-1940|armistice]]
and broadcast the appeal of 18 June from London, founding Free France; led
the Provisional Government at the Liberation; founded the Fifth Republic
(president 1959–69). In this ingest he appears as [[vichy-france]]'s
antithesis — and its unintended beneficiary.

## 1940: nearly alone

Paxton's numbers puncture the retrospective myth of a nation-in-waiting: the
Gaullist movement counted **~7,000 by July 1940 and ~35,000 by year's end**
(holding roughly there until November 1942), mostly "automatic" recruits from
Equatorial Africa. One deputy, one admiral, one dismissed prefect (Jean
Moulin) joined in 1940; de Gaulle himself recorded bitterly that "not a
single public figure raised his voice to condemn the armistice."
Mers-el-Kébir poisoned recruitment; Dakar (September 1940) failed; the early
Left rejected him too (Communist poster, January 1941: "Ni Pétain, ni de
Gaulle… France wants neither cholera nor the plague"). Hitler investigated
and dismissed the rumor of a secret Pétain–de Gaulle "double game" by
October 1940.

## Liberation, 1944

De Gaulle's Algiers planning (Debré's commissaires de la république) shared
with Vichy an overriding concern for orderly transition — regional
super-prefects that, Paxton notes, structurally resembled Darlan's 1941
regional prefects. He refused Pétain's August 1944 Auphan mission offering to
transfer "the principle of legitimacy which I embody": his legitimacy rested
on entirely different grounds. Paxton's structural aside: "De Gaulle and his
former mentor and adversary Pétain resembled each other in many respects, but
in none so closely as when they talked contemptuously of the 'regime of
parties'" — both were, in Weber's phrase about the age, "Maurrassien without
knowing it."
