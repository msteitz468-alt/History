# Niẓāmuddīn Auliyā

Niẓāmuddīn Auliyā (ca. 1238–1325), tenderly titled *Maḥbūb-i ilāhī* ("God's Beloved") by his followers, was the pre-eminent Chishtī saint of medieval Delhi and the figure around whom the high tide of South Asian Sufism crested.

## Background and Training

Born in Badaun, Niẓāmuddīn trained as a theologian in Delhi before attaching himself to Farīduddīn Ganj-i Shakar of Pakpattan, studying under him the *ʿAwārif al-maʿārif* of Suhrawardī — "the guidebook of almost all the Indo-Muslim mystics in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]). After his third visit to Pakpattan he was appointed Farīd's *khalīfa* in Delhi.

## Role and Influence

His presence transformed Delhi: "Sufism became a mass movement in northwestern India" under his influence. The Indo-Muslim historiographer Baranī claims that Niẓāmuddīn's influence inclined "most of the Muslims in Delhi toward mysticism and prayers" and that books of Sufism — Ghazzālī's *Iḥyāʾ*, Hujwīrī's *Kashf al-maḥjūb*, Suhrawardī's *ʿAwārif*, and Qushayrī's *Risāla* — became almost fashionable to own. He outlived seven kings; his khānqāh provided moral stability through "constant intrigues, bloodshed, and rebellions."

He was "a strict follower of the Prophetic sunna, a student of and commentator on the Prophetic traditions, and, at the same time, a friend of poets and musicians." The [[chishtiyya]] permitted *samāʿ* (spiritual concert), and Niẓāmuddīn's circle was crucial to the development of Indo-Muslim musical culture.

## Circle

- **Amīr Khosrau (1254–1325)**: his closest disciple and friend, first great poet of Muslim India, founder of the Indo-Muslim musical tradition; buried beside his master in Delhi
- **Ḥasan Sijzī Dihlawī (d. 1328)**: disciple and poet who compiled the *Fawāʾid al-fuʾād* — Niẓāmuddīn's recorded conversations (1307–1322), the founding example of the [[malfuzat]] genre in India

## Legacy

His tomb in the Niẓāmuddīn dargāh complex in Delhi remains "the most frequently visited sanctuary in Delhi." The spiritual chain extends through Chirāgh-i Dehlī (d. 1356) to Muhammad Gīsūdarāz (d. 1422), who carried Chishtī Sufism south to the Deccan.

## Historiography

The most reliable medieval account of Niẓāmuddīn and the early Chishtī order is Mīr Khurd's *Siyar al-auliyāʾ*, written as expiation for Mīr Khurd's having followed Muhammad Tughluq's forced relocation to the Deccan in 1327 — leaving his master's sanctuary unattended.

**Counterfactual significance**: Without Niẓāmuddīn's long tenure (outliving seven kings), the Chishtī moral framework's diffusion through Delhi's population would likely have been far more fragile; the bhakti-Sufi overlap zone might not have stabilised as it did in 14th-century north India.
