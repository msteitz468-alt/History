# Nikola Tesla

**Nikola Tesla** (10 July 1856 – 7 January 1943) was a Serbian-American electrical and mechanical engineer and inventor, best known for designing the modern **polyphase alternating-current (AC) electricity supply system**. His inventions and theoretical work form the basis of AC power generation and distribution, the **induction motor**, the **Tesla coil** (resonant high-frequency transformer), foundational **wireless / radio** technology, and remote control. He was a central figure in the [[processes/second-industrial-revolution|Second Industrial Revolution]] and the electrification of the modern world.

**Detailed analytic biography**: [[hubs/biographies/long-19th-century/tesla-nikola|Nikola Tesla — Detailed Biography]]

## Summary

Born in Smiljan in the Military Frontier of the Austro-Hungarian Empire (modern Croatia) to a Serbian Orthodox priest, Tesla studied engineering at Graz and Prague, suffered a nervous breakdown, and conceived the principle of the **rotating magnetic field** — the key to the AC induction motor — reportedly during a walk in Budapest in 1882. After working for the Continental Edison Company in Paris and Strasbourg, he emigrated to the United States in 1884 and briefly worked for [[actors/edison-thomas|Thomas Edison]] in New York, leaving after a dispute over pay.

In 1887–88 Tesla patented a complete polyphase AC system. [[actors/westinghouse-george|George Westinghouse]] licensed the patents, and Tesla's AC system defeated Edison's direct current (DC) in the commercial and propaganda struggle known as the **[[processes/war-of-the-currents|War of the Currents]]**. The technology powered the [[events/worlds-columbian-exposition-1893|1893 World's Columbian Exposition]] and the [[events/niagara-falls-power-project-1895|Niagara Falls power project]], establishing AC as the global standard.

At his Manhattan laboratories and at [[places/colorado-springs|Colorado Springs]] (1899), Tesla pioneered high-frequency and high-voltage phenomena, demonstrated radio-controlled devices (1898), and pursued the transmission of power and information **without wires**. With financing from [[actors/morgan-john-pierpont|J. P. Morgan]], he began the [[places/wardenclyffe|Wardenclyffe]] tower on Long Island (1901) as a "World Telegraphy Center," but Morgan withdrew support — partly after [[actors/marconi-guglielmo|Guglielmo Marconi]]'s 1901 transatlantic signal made cheaper wireless telegraphy seem the better bet — and the project collapsed.

Tesla spent his later decades in genteel poverty in New York hotels, generating bold but increasingly unverifiable claims (bladeless turbines, "free energy," a particle-beam weapon or "death ray"). He died alone in the Hotel New Yorker in January 1943; the U.S. **Office of Alien Property** seized his papers. Months later the U.S. Supreme Court upheld key Tesla radio patents over Marconi's, posthumously vindicating his priority. He has since become one of the most mythologized figures in the history of technology.

## Counterfactual Significance

Tesla's polyphase AC system was, in Seifer's account, genuinely decisive: without his rotating-magnetic-field insight and the Westinghouse alliance, large-scale electrification on AC would have been delayed and the technological path of the twentieth century altered. By contrast, his visionary wireless-power program was technically and economically unrealizable on the terms he proposed, and his failure to retain financial control of his inventions — tearing up the Westinghouse royalty contract, accepting Morgan's terms — left the man who electrified the world impoverished. The gap between his foundational achievements and his marginal end is the central puzzle of his life.
