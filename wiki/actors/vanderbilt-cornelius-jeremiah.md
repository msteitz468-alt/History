# Cornelius Jeremiah Vanderbilt

Cornelius Jeremiah "Cornie" Vanderbilt (1830–1882). Son of [[actors/vanderbilt-cornelius|the Commodore]]; epileptic, chronically indebted, and labeled "the Blatherskite" by his father. Principal challenger of the 1877 will that left nearly everything to brother [[actors/vanderbilt-william-h|William H. Vanderbilt]]. Died by suicide at the Glenham Hotel, New York, on 2 April 1882.

## Narrative

Cooper & Howe open Ch. 3 on Cornie's death and work backward through the will contest. The Commodore's will concentrated the ~$100 million estate on Billy; Cornie and some sisters filed objections in March 1877 alleging undue influence and, in associated claims, conspiracy and personation schemes. Trial testimony dredged family violence, Sophia's 1846 confinement at Bloomingdale, Cornie's jail terms and asylum stays, and disputes over the Commodore's soundness of mind (including spiritualism and the Woodhull–Claflin circle). The challenge collapsed by mid-1878 and ended abruptly on 4 March 1879 when successive prior wills showed consistent treatment of Cornie as a "wayward child" under trusteeship. Billy settled for about $1 million (part remaining in trust). Cornie paid debts (including to Horace Greeley's daughters), built a thirty-room Hartford house, then ran short again; in January 1882 his former lawyer Scott Lord sued for $30,000 unpaid fees. On 2 April 1882 Cornie shot himself at the Glenham; he left no note. His will left the Hartford house to companion George Terry.

## Historiography

Cooper & Howe dramatize Cornie's desperation and treat money itself as his affliction (interpretive). Older literature often confirms the Commodore's judgment that Cornie was unfit.
