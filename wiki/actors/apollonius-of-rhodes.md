# Apollonius of Rhodes

Hellenistic scholar-poet (c. 295–c. 215 BCE), **Royal Librarian at Alexandria** and author of the
*[[apollonius-argonautica|Argonautica]]* — the only Greek epic to survive between Homer and the Roman
period, and a decisive influence on **Virgil**.

## Life

Most sources make Apollonius a native of **Alexandria** (the epithet "of Rhodes" perhaps reflecting a
period of residence on the island). He held the post of **Royal Librarian** under **Ptolemy II
Philadelphus** (c. 270–245 BCE) and was perhaps tutor to the future Ptolemy III — placing him at the
very center of the **Ptolemaic institutionalization of learning** at the great Library and Museum, to
which we owe so much of surviving Greek literature. Besides the *Argonautica*, he wrote hexameter
**foundation-poems** (*ktiseis*) of Greek and Egyptian cities and prose **scholarship** on Homer,
Hesiod and Archilochus — the twin identities of scholar-of-poetry and scholarly-poet defining his work.

## The Scholar-Poet

Apollonius exemplifies **Alexandrian poetry**: learned, allusive, self-conscious, written for a
court-and-Library readership steeped in the archaic texts it reworks. (Later tradition invented a bitter
literary feud with **Callimachus** over whether long epic was still possible; modern scholars doubt the
story but recognize the real aesthetic questions behind it.) His achievement was to write a **full-scale
Homeric-style epic** that is nonetheless utterly post-Homeric in sensibility — psychologically
inward, ironic, romantic, and erudite.

## Significance

Through the *Argonautica* Apollonius bridged Homer and Rome: his **Medea** — the young woman undone by
love (Book 3) — is the direct model for **Virgil's Dido**, and the *Argonautica* shaped the whole later
tradition of literary epic. He stands as the representative poetic voice of the **Hellenistic
[[hellenistic-world|Alexandrian world]]**.
