## Overview

Abu-Nasr **al-Farabi** (d. 950 CE), known in Latin as *Alpharabius* or *Alfarabius*, is described by Hodgson as "the man who established the classical tradition of the Muslim Faylasufs' attitude to revelation" (*Venture of Islam*, Vol. 1, p. 1776–77). He was the dominant figure of Islamic philosophy in the generation after [[al-kindi]] and the master from whom both the Iranian school (culminating in Ibn-Sina/Avicenna) and the Spanish-Maghribi school (culminating in Ibn-Rushd/Averroes) ultimately derived.

Al-Farabi was born on the Syr (Jaxartes) river in a Turkic military family — an ethnically unusual background for a philosophical figure of this stature. He studied at [[baghdad]], where he participated in the established philosophical curriculum of the School of Baghdad associated with the Syriac Christian translators. Late in life he became court musician at Aleppo under the Hamdanid Shi'i ruler Sayf al-Dawla, where he lived until his death.

## Philosophical Formation

Al-Farabi studied under a school of thought more rigorously rationalistic than al-Kindi's — specifically within the Aristotelian strand of the late Alexandrian tradition as it had been transmitted through Syriac Christianity. Unlike al-Kindi, who retained a relatively harmonistic view of the relationship between philosophy and revelation, al-Farabi brought "a powerful perceptivity of his own" (*ibid.*, p. 1849–50) to the problem.

His position on the canonical masters was that of a harmoniser: like other Faylasufs, he sought to reconcile Plato and Aristotle — interpreting Plato's logical method through Aristotle, and using neo-Platonic material (including the book mistitled "Theology of Aristotle") to reconcile Aristotle's apparently anti-immortality positions with Platonic aspirations. His major works on *The Philosophy of Plato and Aristotle* exemplify this synthetic project.

## The Political Philosophy of Revelation

Al-Farabi's most distinctive and influential contribution was his account of the relationship between philosophical truth and revealed religion, developed within a framework of political philosophy. This was his solution to the problem that al-Kindi's harmonistic approach had left unresolved.

Drawing on Plato's *Laws* and *Republic*, al-Farabi argued that **a well-ordered society requires doctrines which appear to be divinely revealed** but which need not be true in themselves. These doctrines lead people to behave in ways beneficial to society and to themselves, even if the people cannot understand the rational grounds for the required behaviour.

Applied to Islam, this meant:
- The inhabitants of a good society ought to believe in one God and in the moral obligations God imposes — but they "need not be expected to have a full rational, i.e., Philosophic, insight into that God's nature" (*ibid.*, p. 1872–73).
- The highest doctrines of revealed religion are statements of basic rational principles "put in a simple or an imaged form which ordinary people could understand" (*ibid.*, p. 1911–12).
- **Muhammad's role was that of lawgiver**: "his great mission was to bring the Shari'ah law and to surround it with sanctions which would cause it to be observed" (*ibid.*, p. 1902–04).
- The Prophet brought truth "in the form that he had the gift of bringing it in" — a lower form than philosophical truth, but "the law which the Prophet promulgated was valid and genuinely incumbent on those human beings who happened to be within the community for whom he was legislating" (*ibid.*, p. 1909–15).

The Faylasuf, therefore, was philosophically obligated to adhere to the popular religion of his community, acknowledge its doctrines (however he might tacitly understand them as philosophical parables), and support its practices. Religious learning was part of what a philosopher ought to know — "under the heading 'political philosophy'" (*ibid.*, p. 1928–29). A Faylasuf in Islamdom not only could but should be a good and believing Muslim — on these terms.

## Political Philosophy and Social Reform

Al-Farabi and his successors showed greater concern with political questions than Christian or pagan Philosophers had generally done. The political orientation of Islam suggested this concern, but so did "the general cultural fluidity of the times" (*ibid.*, p. 1855). Al-Farabi may have shared the hope — which Hodgson relates to Plato's Sicilian adventure — that a philosophically reformed society was actually possible under Islamic conditions.

The ideal ruler was a philosopher-king in the Platonic sense, fully rational and knowing, capable both of grasping philosophical truth and of instilling the necessary beliefs in the unphilosophical population. Where such a ruler was impossible, laws constructed according to rational requirements and presented in imaginatively compelling form would suffice.

Al-Farabi used language at the Hamdanid Shi'i court at Aleppo that suggested — by emphasising the need for a naturally sound *imam* as the ongoing community head — that Shi'i Islam might provide the political conditions for a more rational society. This was political philosophy in a very precise sense: not merely theorising about the ideal state but engaging with the actual political possibilities of the moment.

## Significance and Legacy

Al-Farabi's systematic reworking of the Platonic and Aristotelian heritage — and his rigorous account of revelation's place within a Philosophic framework — set the terms for subsequent Islamic philosophical discourse. His followers divided into two main streams:

- The **Iranian school** (leading to **Ibn-Sina / Avicenna**, d. 1037): emphasised the Platonic element; interpreted revelation not only politically but psychologically (the prophet as one whose faculty of imagination is perfected to receive intelligible forms directly); developed a rich internal psychology of the soul.
- The **Spanish-Maghribi school** (leading to **Ibn-Rushd / Averroes**, d. 1198): emphasised the Aristotelian element; rejected compromise with kalam; insisted on strict adherence to the Shari'ah while maintaining the radical separation of philosophical from popular religious truth.

Hodgson credits the chart of early Islamicate Philosophic schools (drawn largely from Richard Walzer's genealogy) for the lineages noted here.

## Counterfactual Significance

Al-Farabi's political solution — revelation as necessary symbolic truth for the unphilosophical masses — became the canonical Faylasuf answer to the religion-philosophy tension and shaped the entire subsequent tradition. Without his synthesis, the Faylasufs would have faced a starker choice: al-Razi's path (full philosophical autonomy, rejection of revelation) or al-Kindi's path (harmonism that could not survive the pressure of more consistent philosophical inquiry). Al-Farabi's more sophisticated elitist solution postponed the crisis, allowing Falsafah to co-exist with Islamicate society for several more centuries.

## Historiography

The interpretation of al-Farabi's political philosophy underwent a decisive reorientation through the work of Leo Strauss and Muhsin Mahdi in the mid-twentieth century. Where earlier scholarship had read al-Farabi as attempting to reconcile "religion and philosophy" as two independent paths to truth, Strauss and Mahdi argued that al-Farabi was doing something far more subversive: placing philosophy unambiguously above revelation and treating the latter as a useful political instrument rather than a source of independent truth. Hodgson accepts this reading, noting that "al-Farabi and the later Faylasufs restricted the spiritual role of popular religion very sharply and gave it very little intellectual credit in any case" (*ibid.*, p. 1941–44). Erwin Rosenthal is cited by Hodgson as displaying "consistent incomprehension" of the Faylasufs' political thinking.
