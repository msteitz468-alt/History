# Second Spanish Republic (1931–1939)

Spain's democratic republic, proclaimed 14 April 1931 after the collapse of the monarchy that followed the [[primo-de-rivera-miguel|Primo de Rivera]] dictatorship. Its trajectory — reformist biennium under Azaña (1931–33), conservative biennium (1933–35, with the Asturias insurrection of October 1934), and the Popular Front victory of February 1936 followed by escalating disorder and the assassination of Calvo Sotelo (13 July 1936) — ended in the military rebellion of 18 July 1936 and the [[spanish-civil-war-1936|Civil War]], which it lost in April 1939 to [[francoist-spain|Franco's Nationalists]].

## Significance

Payne (1987) reads the Republic's breakdown through Juan Linz's framework: polarization, the left's partial abandonment of legality (1934), and the right's turn to catastrophist conspiracy made the polity ungovernable by mid-1936 — though the rising itself, not inevitability, destroyed it. Republican wartime governance (revolution in the loyalist zone, internecine conflict) is treated in Payne only as context for the Nationalist state.

## Detail (Payne 1987)

Payne's data points: Spain in 1931 had ~23.5 million people, half the labor force in agriculture, a third illiterate. The anticlerical constitution meant "the Republic thus began on the basis of exclusivism rather than equal rights and participation"; a state of exception was in force almost continuously in the last three years. The October 1934 Asturias insurrection — "the first direct revolutionary insurrection in western Europe since the Paris Commune" — carried Payne's signature causal irony: launched "against fascism" when fascism scarcely existed, it "provoked more of the rightist polarization and movement toward a sort of fascism that it was designed to prevent," exactly mirroring how the 1936 revolt provoked the revolution it aimed to forestall. February 1936: Popular Front 34.3%, right 33.2%, center 5.4% — a narrow plurality converted into two-thirds of seats by the electoral law. Pre-war spring 1936: 269 political killings, culminating in the police murder of opposition leader Calvo Sotelo. Payne assigns "main responsibility" for the breakdown to the governing Republican Left — "more the accomplice than the victim of the revolutionary left" — while noting the wartime Republic "was not a liberal democracy" and that its mass political executions "were as extensive as those by Franco's supporters" (his contested counterfactual baseline; see [[events/francoist-repression-1936]]).

## Historiography

The causes of the breakdown remain the central controversy of modern Spanish history — Linz/Payne stressing left as well as right responsibility, Preston and others stressing rightist intransigence and the fascist threat. Positions preserved without adjudication per protocol.
