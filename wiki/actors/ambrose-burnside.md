# Ambrose Burnside

Union general remembered chiefly for the disastrous frontal assaults he
ordered as commander of the Army of the Potomac at the Battle of
Fredericksburg (December 1862), and for the failed Battle of the Crater
during the [[petersburg-campaign-1864-65|Siege of Petersburg]] (1864).

## Role and Major Decisions

After a competent early record (including the capture of coastal North
Carolina positions in 1862), Burnside was given command of the Army of the
Potomac in late 1862 against his own reservations; his insistence on
repeated frontal assaults against entrenched Confederate positions at
Fredericksburg produced heavy, futile Union casualties and led to his
removal from army command within weeks. As a corps commander during the
[[overland-campaign-1864|Overland Campaign]] and subsequent siege of
Petersburg, his IX Corps' badly mismanaged assault following the Petersburg
mine explosion (the Battle of the Crater, July 1864) ended his active field
command.

## Counterfactual Significance

Burnside's case is frequently cited in Civil War historiography as an
example of a competent subordinate commander promoted beyond his level of
strategic and tactical competence, with Fredericksburg and the Crater both
illustrating how poor senior-level execution could squander otherwise
capable troops and sound initial plans.

## Historiography

Burnside's reputation has remained consistently poor across generations of
Civil War historiography; unlike Longstreet's postwar rehabilitation, his
record at Fredericksburg and the Crater is not generally contested, though
some historians note his earlier, more successful independent commands in
coastal operations complicate a purely negative assessment.
