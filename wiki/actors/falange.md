# Falange Española (1933–1977)

Spain's genuine fascist movement, founded October 1933 by José Antonio Primo
de Rivera (son of the 1920s dictator) and merged in 1934 with Ledesma Ramos's
JONS. One of [[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|Payne's]] "four major variants"
of fascism — distinctive for its Catholic cultural matrix and its weakness:
electorally negligible (about 0.7 percent in February 1936) until the
[[spanish-civil-war-1936|Civil War]] flooded it with recruits.

## Role and Significance

José Antonio's Falange preached [[national-syndicalism|national
syndicalism]] — the label taken from the Italian syndicalist-nationalist
synthesis, carried in the JONS name itself (Juntas de Ofensiva
Nacional-Sindicalista); Sternhell notes José Antonio's founding speech
(1933) opened with an attack on Rousseau, the signature anti-Enlightenment
move of the [[sternhell-thesis|fascist synthesis]] — anti-liberalism, and
the "dialectic of fists and pistols," but the movement mattered only after
July 1936, when it became the Nationalist zone's mass militia and political
vehicle. José Antonio was executed by the Republic in November 1936; in
April 1937 [[franco-francisco|Franco]] decapitated and absorbed the movement
by decree, fusing it with the Carlists into FET y de las JONS — for Payne the
clearest case of a fascist movement captured and tamed by conservative
authoritarianism. As the regime's state party it was progressively
marginalized after 1945, surviving as bureaucratic shell until 1977.

## Under the Regime (Payne 1987)

Payne's regime history ([[sources/payne-franco-regime-1987|1987]]) fills in the arc. Origins: Ledesma's *La Conquista del Estado* circle (March 1931, ~10 members) merged with Redondo's Valladolid juntas into the JONS; the pre-1936 party never exceeded ~10,000 members, financed by Bilbao business and modest Italian subsidies (50,000 lire/month from June 1935, halved after six months — "much less than that provided by the Soviet Union to the Spanish Communist Party"). Wartime: [[primo-de-rivera-jose-antonio|José Antonio]]'s June 1936 circular warned that joining the military plot would mean the party's "total disappearance, even in case of triumph" — precisely what followed. Under the stopgap leadership of Manuel Hedilla (a former ship's mechanic who repeatedly tried to restrain Falangist participation in the [[francoist-repression-1936|repression]]), the party's April 1937 succession fight gave [[franco-francisco|Franco]] and [[serrano-suner-ramon|Serrano Suñer]] the opening for the forced FET unification; Hedilla refused his consolation seat and was court-martialed and sentenced to death (commuted). FET membership peaked at 932,000 (1942) — largely passive and automatic (all army officers ipso facto members). Thereafter the descent: the term "party" banned in favor of "movement" (1943), the fascist salute abolished (September 1945), the budget cut over 75% in 1946 (never again 0.5% of the state budget), kept alive — Franco privately told Martín Artajo — as bulwark, mass organization, and "lightning rod... blamed for the errors of the government." The 1956 Arrese draft laws, the last attempt to lock in Movement power after Franco, were killed by the other regime families; by 1965 in Lérida 85% of members were over 45; a camisa vieja shot himself in public protest in 1969; the name "Falange Española Tradicionalista y de las JONS" was formally abolished in April 1970, and post-Franco neofascist splinters together polled under 2%.

## Historiography

Payne (whose first book, *Falange*, 1961, founded the field's Anglophone
study) treats it as authentic fascism defeated *within* the winning
coalition; the "clerical fascism" literature reads Francoism itself as
fascist. Griffin ([[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]]) concurs with Payne:
the Falange was genuine fascism, but the April 1937 FET y de las JONS was a
"shot-gun marriage" imposing a [[para-fascism|para-fascist]] state party —
"its success in neutralizing fascism's revolutionary impetus by absorption
has nevertheless deceived numerous historians into mistaking Franco's regime
for the real thing, and even Hitler and Mussolini deluded themselves into
reading into it a kinship with their movements which did not exist in any
positive sense." See [[generic-fascism]].

## Payne (1980)

From [[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]] (ch. 6): Ledesma's JONS (named on the model of the Fasci di Combattimento; *La Conquista del Estado* borrowed from Malaparte) remained a student micro-sect, 1931–34; the Falange (founded Oct 1933, merged with the JONS early 1934) drew more big-business financing "prone to the radical right" and was on the Italian foreign payroll for ~9 months in 1935–36, yet won only 44,000 votes — **0.7 percent** — in 1936, "revealing fascism as weaker in Spain than in any other large continental European country." Payne's four causes: Spanish nationalism was "inverted" into Catalan/Basque regionalism; limited rural secularization made political Catholicism (CEDA) the natural cross-class alternative; the 1890 cultural-intellectual revolution had less Spanish resonance; and the left monopolized political revolutionism. From late 1934 Falangists denied being fascists, some (including José Antonio) calling Italian corporatism "too conservative and capitalistic"; the Twenty-Seven Points demanded a national-syndicalist state with nationalized banking and expropriated latifundia, though no blueprint was ever produced. Falangism's distinguishing trait vs. Italian Fascism was its Catholic (if anticlerical) identity — a "relative rather than absolute" distinction, the Iron Guard and Polish Falanga being more religiously extreme. Suppressed by the Republic in spring 1936; wartime membership swelled to several hundred thousand while combat and execution decapitated the leadership. Franco's April 1937 partido único (FET) merged in Carlists and rightists — explicitly not integral Falangism; the *camisas viejas* held "only a small minority of positions." Defascistization: by 1943 the FET was "an even more completely subservient bureaucratic instrument than the PNF"; Salvador Merino's bid for autonomous national syndicalism was suppressed in 1940 (the Spanish *sbloccamento*); after the 1956 Madrid university crisis and the 1957 technocratic turn, the Twenty-Seven Points were replaced (1958) by ten anodyne "Principles of the Movement"; the Movimiento was dissolved in April 1977, neo-Falangists drawing ~1 percent — "scarcely better than in 1936."
