# Dido (Elissa)

## Overview

**Dido** — Punic **Elissa** — is the legendary Tyrian princess credited with founding
[[carthage|Carthage]]. She is less a historical figure than a literary one: a vehicle
through which first Greek and then Roman writers projected their image of the
Carthaginians, and ultimately the deep mythic backstory of the Rome–Carthage enmity. Her
treatment is a case study in Richard Miles's argument
([[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010]]) that Carthage's "history" was written by
others. See [[punic-faith]].

## The Foundation Legend

In the fullest version (Trogus Pompeius, late 1st c. BCE): on the death of King Mattan of
Tyre (trad. 831 BCE) the kingdom was to be split between his son **Pygmalion** and his
daughter **Elissa**. Pygmalion murdered Elissa's husband **Acherbas**, the high priest of
[[carthaginian-religion|Melqart]], for his wealth. Elissa fled, tricking Pygmalion's
retainers with sham sacks of "gold," gathering nobles and (in the tale) eighty
sacred-prostitute girls via Cyprus, and founding Carthage. There she pulled off the
famous **ox-hide trick** — bargaining for as much land as an ox-hide would cover, then
cutting it into thin strips to encircle the hill later called the **Byrsa**. Threatened
with a forced marriage to the Libyan king **Hiarbus**, she immolated herself on a pyre.

## Historical Status

- **A Greek literary construct.** The earliest roots of the story reach no further back
  than a Greek source of the 3rd c. BCE; Miles suspects the hand of **Timaeus of
  Tauromenium** in crystallising it.
- **A vehicle for prejudice.** The legend encodes the Greco-Roman anti-Carthaginian
  *topoi* — treachery (against Roman *fides*), domination by women, obsession with death,
  lasciviousness, greed. Miles **rejects** the view that Carthaginians authored or embraced
  it ("rather like Thanksgiving"): a people would be unlikely to adopt a story so negative
  about themselves.
- **The Byrsa etymology.** The citadel's name most likely derives from Akkadian *birtu*,
  "fortress"; the Greek *bursa*, "ox hide," probably *generated* the hide story rather than
  the reverse.
- **The dating problem.** A gold pendant naming Pygmalion (with Astarte), once cited to
  confirm the traditional 814 BCE foundation, came from a tomb up to three centuries later,
  demolishing the historical claim. Archaeology puts Carthage's earliest occupation layers
  at only c. **760 BCE** (possibly to be pushed back).

## The Roman Dido

The romance of Dido and **Aeneas** — the Trojan founder of the Roman line callously
deserting the Carthaginian queen — was the invention of [[virgil|Virgil]] in the *Aeneid*,
long after Carthage's destruction. Miles reads Virgil's Dido as industrious, honest, and
pious — "more Roman than Aeneas" — a figure who subverts the [[punic-faith|Punic
stereotype]] even as her dying curse foretells the Punic Wars and Hannibal as her avenger.

## Counterfactual Significance

As a person, none — she is legendary. As a *symbol*, she is central: the Dido legend is
how the classical and post-classical world remembered Carthage at all, and the prism
through which its founding was moralised. Without Virgil's Dido, the Rome–Carthage
relationship would lack its enduring tragic-mythic frame.
