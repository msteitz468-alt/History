# Robert I, "the Bruce," King of Scots (r. 1306–1329)

**Robert the Bruce** was the leader who won Scottish independence from England, "the most
successful leader" of the Wars of Independence. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (the Scotland
chapter) traces his rise from beaten fugitive to victor of **[[battle-of-bannockburn-1314|Bannockburn]]**.

## The Rising

Grandson of the Bruce claimant whom Edward I had passed over for **John Balliol** (1292), Robert,
earl of Carrick, "raised the rebellion that ended English rule" in 1306. It "began inauspiciously,"
when he killed his rival **John Comyn** of Badenoch in a church and then "rebelled in self-defence,"
legitimising himself by having himself made king as **Robert I**. In Scottish terms he was a
**usurper** who had deposed King John, and the powerful **Comyn/Balliol faction** became his
"implacable enemies," siding with the English. Soon "a beaten fugitive, vainly seeking Irish help,"
Robert recovered after **Edward I's death (1307)** relaxed English pressure.

## The Reconquest and Bannockburn

In "a remarkable series of campaigns," Robert isolated and defeated his Comyn enemies in northern
Scotland, controlled most of the country by 1313, and raided across the border. When **Edward II**
marched to relieve Stirling Castle in 1314, Robert's spearmen destroyed the English host at
**[[battle-of-bannockburn-1314|Bannockburn]]** — the decisive victory of the war. He then
intensified border raids, supported his brother **Edward Bruce's** (failed) invasion of **Ireland
(1315–1318)**, and defeated English invasions by **scorched-earth** tactics.

## The Securing of Independence

Robert's cause was given its classic statement in the **Declaration of Arbroath (1320)**, the
Scottish nobles' letter to the pope asserting national freedom. The stalemate and Edward II's
deposition led to the **Treaty of Edinburgh (1328)**, by which the English government recognised
**Scotland's independence** and Robert as king. He died in 1329, having achieved what neither
Wallace nor the guardians could secure.

## Significance

Robert the Bruce is the founder of late-medieval independent Scotland — the figure whose military
genius and political resilience turned a "people's war" into a recognised kingdom. His success is the
great counter-example to the [[edward-i-plantagenet|Edwardian]] conquest of Wales: in the
[[celtic-lands-and-english-expansion|comparison of the Celtic lands]], Scotland's "developed" system
of government, its capacity to continue "even after military disaster," and its aristocratic
leadership of national resistance made it the realm that **withstood** English expansion.
