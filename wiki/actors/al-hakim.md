# al-Ḥākim bi-Amr Allāh

**al-Ḥākim** (r. 996–1021) was the sixth [[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] caliph, ruling Egypt and
the Levant from Cairo. He is remembered above all for an anomalous, erratic reign whose
persecution of non-Muslims stands out sharply against the Fatimids' general reputation as
**more tolerant** toward Jews and Christians than Sunni regimes.

## The Persecution of Dhimmīs

Beginning around **1004**, al-Ḥākim reversed Fatimid practice and imposed a harsh
enforcement — and expansion — of [[dhimmi|dhimmī]] disabilities on Jews and Christians in
Egypt and Palestine, recurring intermittently through much of his reign:

- distinctive badges and belts (*zunnār*); Cohen ([[cohen-under-crescent-cross-1994]])
  notes he went **beyond the [[pact-of-umar|Pact of ʿUmar]]**, requiring Jews to wear a
  wooden calf and Christians a cross around the neck in the public baths — an addition
  Muslim chroniclers themselves flagged as exceeding the ʿUmariyyan terms;
- destruction of synagogues and churches, most consequentially the **Church of the Holy
  Sepulchre in Jerusalem (1009)** — sometimes cited by modern historians as a long-range
  cause of the [[first-crusade-1095|First Crusade]];
- a choice, contrary to standard Islamic law, of **conversion or expulsion**; many
  dhimmīs fled or converted, though al-Ḥākim later permitted converts to revert.

Contemporary chroniclers questioned his sanity, and Cohen treats his reign as an
**exception** — "the exception that proves the rule" — against the classical Islamic norm
of relatively secure dhimmī status: even here, the persecution targeted the dhimmī
category (Christians as much as Jews), not a specifically demonized Jewish essence. His
son and successor **al-Ẓāhir** issued a reassuring decree restoring dhimmī status.

## Legacy

al-Ḥākim's disappearance in 1021 gave rise to the doctrine, among a group of his
followers, of his divinity and occultation — the origin of the **Druze** religion. In the
historiography of Jews under Islam, his reign is a standing test case: cited by the
"neo-lachrymose" [[jews-under-islam-myth-and-countermyth|countermyth]] as evidence of
Islamic persecution, and by Cohen as a genuine but exceptional episode.
