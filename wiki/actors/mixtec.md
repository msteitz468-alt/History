# The Mixtec

## Overview

The **Mixtec** (*ñusabi* / *ñuu dzavui*, “people of the rain (deity)”; Aztec *Mixteca* →
Mixteco) of the Mixteca Alta, Baja, and Coast are among Oaxaca’s major
civilization-bearing peoples. Marcus and Flannery (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 8) treat them with
the [[zapotec|Zapotec]] as joint heirs of Oaxaca’s long evolutionary sequence: shared
Otomanguean ancestry (divergence before 4000 B.C. in the Oaxaca region), parallel
Formative ranking, but a **multipolar** political structure of **cacicazgos** rather
than a single expansionist capital like [[monte-alban|Monte Albán]].

In the Postclassic they are famous for **pictorial codices** recording dynastic history
and for elite art in turquoise, gold, and polychrome ceramics (Mixteca-Puebla style).

## Geographic setting

West of the Valley of Oaxaca lie smaller valleys of Mixtec civilization:

- **Mixteca Alta** — Tilantongo, Yanhuitlán–Nochixtlán, ~2,000 m
- **Mixteca Baja** — Tequixtepec, Huajuapan, 1,000–1,500 m
- **Mixteca de la Costa** — Tututepec, Pacific tropical forest

Semi-arid conditions yield excellent archaeological preservation.

## Formative–Classic trajectory

Early Formative: Mixteca Alta valleys 3–5 villages while the Valley of Oaxaca had ~18–20
hamlets; little rank difference yet between Zapotec and Mixtec regions (1400–1150 B.C.).
Middle Formative (800–350 B.C.): rank societies; Yanhuitlán–Nochixtlán 17 sites; elite
adobe houses on stone foundations; large unexcavated earthen platform at Etlatongo.

**Urban transformation 200 B.C.–A.D. 300** (Spores: slightly later/longer than Valley of
Oaxaca). Yanhuitlán–Nochixtlán population doubled; ≥3-tier hierarchy headed by
**Yucuita** (2 km², 10+ mound complexes, megalithic walls, tunnel system, elite
residences/tombs). By A.D. 300–1000 Yucuita surpassed by planned hilltop
**Yucunudahui**. Other Classic Mixtec centers: Mogote del Cacique, Yatachio, Cerro de la
Virgen; Mixteca Baja: Cerro Caja, Nuyoo, etc. Classic Mixtec capitals had ballcourts but
temples usually lack Zapotec inner/outer room division.

**Structural contrast:** no single Mixtec city integrated as large a region as Monte
Albán; Zapotec state militaristic/expansionist; Mixteca many polities, only occasionally
>3 hierarchy tiers. *contrasts_with:* [[zapotec]] primary state · *analogous_to:*
Postclassic multipolar highland systems elsewhere in Mesoamerica.

Classic multiplex centers (Yucunudahui, Cerro Jasmín, La Palmita) may last to A.D.
900–1100 then shrink mainly in habitation while ceremonial use continues; residences
move to undefended floors, hilltops become small ritual precincts. Reduced Monte Albán
threat lessened Mixtec need for defensible mountaintop elites (Blanton’s chain-reaction
argument).

## Postclassic: codices, cacicazgos, demography

Instead of carving genealogies in stone (Zapotec genealogical registers), Mixtec painted
them on **codices** — deer-hide screenfolds with lime sizing. Both Mixtec codices and
complex Zapotec genealogical registers read in zigzag *boustrophedon*, suggesting deep
shared conceptual relationship.

**A.D. 1000–1530 balkanization:** no single Mixtec capital; numerous small hostile
polities; widespread fortification; frequent battles, ephemeral truces via royal marriage
or alliance. Mixtec nobles warred among themselves but **united with Zapotec against
Aztec expansion**. Military: noble officers with quilted cotton armor; bows, slings,
atlatl, obsidian-edged broadswords, clubs, spears, hide shields; captives often led nude
by bowstring.

**Demography:** Spores Yanhuitlán–Nochixtlán survey 159 sites (113 intensive) A.D.
1000–1520; Spanish documents estimate ~**50,000 people** in A.D. 1530 in a valley with
only 250 km² flat land — relevant to [[pre-columbian-american-population]] debates.

**Lama y bordo agriculture:** forest clearance → erosion exposing fertile Tertiary
Yanhuitlán Beds; stone/rubble dikes 1–4 m high trap good soil (*lama*) in artificial
terraces (*bordos*) for intensive maize — Yanhuitlán became the richest Mixteca Alta
kingdom.

**Cacicazgo social structure** (Spanish term from Carib *cacique*): head *yaa tnuhu*
(Mixtec hereditary lord); below, nobles *toy toho* of lesser rank by descent proximity;
class-endogamous upper stratum over commoners *ñay yucu* (“hill people”), servants,
slaves; among commoners *toy situndayu* landless farmers moved at the ruler’s command.

## Lord 8 Deer and Valley of Oaxaca presence

**Lord 8 Deer “Tiger Claw”** (codices Nuttall, Bodley, Colombino, Selden): 11th century
conquered an estimated **75–100 places**, briefly creating a large regional state from
**Tututepec** (Pacific) to **Tilantongo** (Mixteca Alta); collapsed when rivals captured
and sacrificed him at age 52; no later Mixtec ruler unified so large an area.

Some Mixtec rulers coveted Valley of Oaxaca alluvium (esp. less dense western valley Late
Postclassic). Blanton: labor shortage may have prompted Zapotec elite intermarriage with
Mixtec elites to bring agricultural labor. Ethnohistory: 13th-c. Mixtec princess from
land-poor Almoloyas marries Zaachila prince; Mixtec immigrants founded **Sa’a Yucu** near
[[monte-alban|Monte Albán]]’s base (possibly Kowalewski C-V-132). Mixteca-Puebla
polychrome by ~1400 in elite residences; gold and jadeite hair-tied figures in elite
tombs; gray wares show Zapotec continuity. Valley has 2,455 Late Postclassic sites —
more than double Early Classic count.

## Aztec wars and Spanish Conquest

Mixtec lords fought alongside Zapotec at Huitzo and opposite Tehuantepec River bank from
Guiengola during Ahuitzotl’s southern campaigns (~1486–95). Spaniards entered Oaxaca
1520s–30s with Aztec interpreters. Self-name *ñuu dzavui*; by 1520 mutually
unintelligible from Zapotec despite shared ancestry. Stereotypes: Mixtec warriors/
jewelers; highly stratified speech (different body-part vocabulary for nobles vs
commoners). Contributions: Postclassic polychrome and goldworking styles that dominated
Postclassic Mexican art. Modern: >200,000 Mixtec speakers in Mexico (Marcus & Flannery).

## Colonial–modern trajectory (Romero Frizzi, CHNPA II.2 Ch. 19)

**Mercantile primacy.** Valley Mixtecs sought to use the Spanish newcomers; by the 1530s most
Mixtec kingdoms recognized Spaniards via tribute. **Mixtec nobles** — ancient traders — used
Spanish beasts of burden and Pacific ships for cacao from Soconusco, becoming the **principal
merchants of sixteenth-century Oaxaca, more important than the Spaniards themselves**. By ~1540
Mixtecs grew wheat with plows and metal tool tips in the **Mixteca Alta**; kingdoms famous for
**silk** sold in Puebla; indigenous livestock herds sometimes larger than Spanish. ~1540–1570
silk, livestock, and cochineal funded easy tribute, nobility consumption, and Catholic festivals
recalling the prehispanic calendar — prolonging past splendor (*part_of:*
[[oaxaca-indigenous-colonial-modern]]).

**Destructuring and demography.** By ~1590 Mixteca natives refused to obey lords; preconquest
broad regional domains (e.g. **Tututepec**) yielded to peasant villages. Epidemics cut Oaxaca
natives from ~1.5 million (1520) to ~150,000 (1650); flocks expanded into vacant lands but
**accelerated erosion** in fragile Mixteca Alta. Mid-seventeenth century: Mixteca income from
small livestock sales and rents to Spanish ranchers under *repartimiento* networks.

**Cofradías and eighteenth–nineteenth centuries.** After 1725 no Mixteca/valley caciques as
rulers; cofradías commercially oriented (Mixteca flocks). Eighteenth-century Mixteca land
litigation: **three indigenous-party suits per one against a Spaniard**; Spanish livestock
fattening and late-century sugarcane/syrup/liquor on rented village/cacique land. Mixteca
imports ~47% European (esp. textiles). First half of nineteenth century: private ownership rose;
few haciendas concentrated ~**1/3** of regional livestock; ranchers, mestizos, and rich Indians
used liberal anticorporate ideas to buy poor Mixtec land. Mid-eighteenth-century Mixteca Alta
cofradías had produced ~1/4 of market products supporting the poor majority; by ~**1832**
Mixtecs producing for market down **75%** without cofradía aid — majority **pauperized**
(Pastor). **Triquis and Mixtecs in rebellion 1832–1857** defending cofradía/communal property.
Coffee largely unsuitable for Mixteca/valleys; Porfirian parcelization hit Mixteca with small
parcels (avg ~30 ha) after Tuxtepec/La Cañada. Revolution spread through Mixteca where land
was still mostly native-held; Zapata movement diffuse (few haciendas); neighboring villages
took opposite national sides for local land-quarrel reasons.

**Late twentieth century.** ~**239,000** Mixtec speakers (second-largest Oaxaca group; extend
into Puebla/Guerrero). Mixtec leaders among founders of **COCEI, MULT, SER, MICHIZA** and
related organizations (1980–96) for language/culture, forest management, and coffee
commercialization.

Deans-Smith (Gulf Ch. 18) briefly **analogizes** [[totonac|Totonac]] early colonial experience
to Maya and **Mixtec**: less intrusive early Spanish presence, major reorganization in the
eighteenth century — a comparative pointer, not a full Mixtec colonial monograph.

## Evans & Webster 2001 (Monaghan)

**Multivalent “Mixtec.”** The label is analytically multivalent (language, ethnicity,
elite, geography, art style that do not fully coincide). Recognizably Mixtec language
diverged among Otomanguean groups in the Mixteca Alta by at least ~**1000 B.C.**; by
Spanish arrival **1522** Mixtec-speaking kings and queens ruled much of the western
Valley of Oaxaca and had incorporated Chocho, Chatino, Nahuatl, Amuzgo, Trique, and
other groups (Monaghan, EW2001 “Mixtec History, Culture, and Religion”).

**Vertical ecology.** Traditional divisions: Mixteca Alta (mountainous eastern Oaxaca),
Mixteca Baja (dry NW of Alta), Mixteca de la Costa (humid Pacific strip); indigenous
subarea names stress environmental variation (Costa as “land of corn”). Proximity of
different ecological floors encouraged **vertical integration** — a major factor in
settlement, ritual exchange, and political dynamics.

**Sequence highlights.** Preagricultural Mixteca poorly known; Nochixtlán/Tamazulapan
farming villages ~**1350 B.C.** Classic (A.D. 300–800) unprecedented growth: >100
Nochixtlán sites; Yucuñudahui, Cerro Jazmín, Etlatongo >2 km²; **Río Viejo** urban
center ~3 km² with plazas, ball courts, temple mounds, mural tombs. Agricultural
intensification: irrigation, vertical microclimate integration, and *lama-bordo*
controlled-erosion terracing — “one of the most unusual agricultural technologies in the
world.”

**Postclassic *yuhuitayu*.** Best-documented period via archaeology, Mixtec manuscripts,
and colonial accounts; small kingdoms (*yuhuitayu* / cacicazgo) linked by
kinship/marriage. Major conquest-era kingdoms included Coixtlahuaca, Yanhuitlán,
Teposcolula, Tilantongo, Achiutla, Tlaxiaco, Teozacoalco, Chalcatongo, Tequixtepec,
Tecomaxtlahuaca, **Tututepec** (coast); several subordinate to the Triple Alliance;
Tututepec uniquely appointed officials in subject kingdoms.

**Strata and language.** Ruling stratum: royal lineage (*iya tnuhu* / caciques) and
hereditary nobility (*tay toho* / principales); elite origin myths (born from trees at
sacred Apoala); special *Iya* vocabulary; Dominicans translated *iya* as “saint”/“divine.”
Nonelite: barrio commoners (*tay ñuu*), rural people (*tay yucu*), landless tenants
movable with lords, slaves. Mixtec is a continuum of ≥**29** mutually low-intelligibility
dialects (related languages); Postclassic elite/merchants polyglot (Nahuatl lingua
franca; Teposcolula Mixtec as elite standard per Antonio de los Reyes 1593). Royalty
practiced cousin marriage and true **brother-sister marriage** — one of the few such
societies worldwide; kingdoms easily segmented by inheritance and ethnic/linguistic
divisions.

## Historiography

Ethnohistory and codex scholarship (Alfonso Caso’s tradition; Nuttall, Bodley, Colombino,
Selden) complement Spores and others’ archaeology. Debates continue over continuity of
Classic multipolar forms vs Postclassic reorganization; 8 Deer’s “empire” scale is
codex-based reconstruction. Marcus & Flannery emphasize structural contrast with the
single Zapotec state as a major lesson for Mesoamerican political typology.

**Evans & Webster 2001.** John Monaghan’s “Mixtec History, Culture, and Religion” is the
principal encyclopedia synthesis for multivalent identity, *yuhuitayu* structure, and
elite theology of *iya*; pairs with Smith’s [[mixteca-puebla-style]] entry for the
artistic/codex dimension.

**Colonial–modern:** Romero Frizzi (CHNPA II.2 Ch. 19) plus her own Mixteca Alta economy work
and Pastor *Campesinos y reformas*; Spores continues as archaeological/ethnohistorical
bridge. Thin colonial documentary base for Oaxaca relative to central Mexico.
