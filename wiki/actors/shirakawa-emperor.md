## Overview

Shirakawa (白河) — personal name Sadahito — reigned as emperor from 1073 to 1087 and then dominated Heian Japan as retired emperor (*jōkō*) until his death in 1129 at age seventy-six. He lived "one of the most active and involved lives of any Japanese sovereign of the premodern period" (CHJ, ch. 9). It was during his lifetime that the **insei** — political rule by an abdicated emperor — became an almost formalized political norm of the Heian court.

## Early Reign (1073–1087)

Shirakawa came to the throne at nineteen when his father **Emperor Go-Sanjō** abdicated and simultaneously appointed a Minamoto-line infant (Prince Sanehito) as crown prince — deliberately bypassing any future Fujiwara maternal connection to the succession. Go-Sanjō died soon after, leaving Shirakawa with a predetermined succession plan that would bypass his own children.

During his fourteen years on the throne, Shirakawa exercised direct personal rule in the manner of his father. Regent Morozane served but could not restrain imperial action. Shirakawa continued Go-Sanjō's estate regulation efforts, issuing an ordinance that affected at least two Tōdaiji estates in Mino, though "he was no more successful than his father" in stemming illicit estate acquisition. He also faced repeated disturbances from armed monks (*akusō*) of Mount Hiei and the Onjōji, requiring warrior escorts on imperial outings.

His central political preoccupation during the reign was succession. Crown Prince Sanehito died of smallpox in 1085. Shirakawa delayed investing Prince Sukehito (his Minamoto half-brother) as instructed by Go-Sanjō's wishes, and in 1086 resolved the issue by making his own young son Taruhito crown prince and then abdicating in his favor. Shirakawa "had chosen to shift succession to his own line."

## Early Retirement (1087–1107): Non-Intervention

There is strong documentary evidence that during Emperor Horikawa's reign (1087–1107), Shirakawa did not dominate politics. Eleven imperial edicts from Horikawa are extant; only one from Shirakawa, and no in-no-chō documents date from this period. Horikawa took his duties seriously and was regarded by contemporaries as a wise and capable ruler.

Shirakawa's great concern was that Horikawa produce an heir before he died — partly because Shirakawa had himself become a partial monk to preserve the option of reascending the throne if Horikawa died childless. When Toba was born to Horikawa and Princess Ishi in 1103, Shirakawa was "greatly relieved."

## Active Insei (1107–1129)

Horikawa died in 1107, leaving the four-year-old Toba on the throne. With no crown prince named, the threatened candidacy of Sukehito's son Arihito (Minamoto-line), and the sekkanke at a weak point, Shirakawa was effectively "forced into taking action." From this point, he became the undisputed political arbiter of the realm.

Contemporary diarist Fujiwara no Munetada (*Chūyuki*) provides the most detailed record: Shirakawa "yields to his desires, makes appointments unbound by law"; "the grandeur of the abdicated sovereign is equal to that of His Majesty, and at the present moment this abdicated sovereign is sole political master." The Heike monogatari summarized: the only things outside Shirakawa's control were the Kamo River floods, sugoroku game dice, and the unruly Mount Hiei monks.

### Character

Shirakawa "appears to have been an uncomplicated man who acted decisively on the basis of his own likes and dislikes, caring little for established custom." He lavished favors on those he liked, reacted to disfavor with enduring hostility, and had a short temper. The famous story from the *Kojidan* describes him ordering rain collected in a vessel and thrown into prison when it disrupted his temple dedication ceremony four days running. More consequentially, when Regent Tadazane refused to allow his daughter Taishi to become Emperor Toba's consort and then reversed himself, Shirakawa stripped him of the regency; Tadazane went into seclusion for the remaining twelve years of Shirakawa's life, and Shirakawa wrote in his testament that Taishi should never be made consort.

### Institutional Apparatus

Shirakawa's political power rested on:
- **In-no-chō** (retired emperor's office): expanded private bureaucracy modeled on sekkanke house organization, including household, attendants', kitchen, workshop, stable, and warrior offices
- **In-no-hokumen** ("warriors of the northern quarter"): Shirakawa's own innovation — a personally recruited elite guard. At his death, there were twenty superintendents, five scribes, four secretaries, five assistants, and over eighty warriors in this unit alone
- **Kinshin-zuryo** network: provincial governors who supported Shirakawa through "contributions" (*jōgo*) — institutionalized payments that were rewarded with appointments and reappointments to lucrative governorships. Munetada identified five ways that zuryo behavior changed in Shirakawa's era
- **Client families**: Long-term retainer relationships that extended across generations to Toba and Go-Shirakawa. Key client ancestors: Fujiwara no Tamefusa, Fujiwara no Akisue, Fujiwara no Akitaka, Takashina no Tameaki, Taira no Masamori
- **Military warriors**: Shirakawa received Minamoto no Yoshiie in audience despite earlier wariness — "tantamount to establishing a patron-client relationship" — and recruited the Ise Heishi (Taira) through Masamori's service in the hokumen

### Economic Base

Shirakawa used the zuryo "contributions" to build "four of six imperial clan temples (*rikushōji*) located in the Shirakawa area east of the Kamo River." He also accumulated a large number of imperial estates under the ex-emperor's control through confiscation and new establishment. However, "while it is undeniable that Shirakawa was responsible for acquiring vast assets and building many great temple complexes, he did not accumulate many shōen" directly — preferring court-awarded grants and contributions over direct estate acquisition (a marked contrast to his successor Toba).

### Succession Politics and the Toba Problem

Shirakawa's deepest problem in the post-1107 period was the continued candidacy of Sukehito's son Arihito. After a plot by Daigoji monks (sons of Minamoto no Toshifusa) against Emperor Toba was uncovered in 1113, Shirakawa paradoxically adopted a conciliatory attitude toward Sukehito and even adopted young Arihito — while making clear to observers that Arihito's succession was effectively impossible.

A major political consequence: Shirakawa's decision to move forcefully into politics "adversely affected the fortunes of the Murakami Genji," who had been close allies of the imperial house but now threatened it as champions of Arihito's candidacy.

## Death and Legacy

Shirakawa died in 1129. Munetada offered a complex obituary — at times calling him a "sage ruler," at other times blaming his rule for "sadly deteriorating conditions." His emergence to supreme political power resulted "in the formation of the insei, which was to remain the dominant political style of courtier rule until Go-Daigo's time in the fourteenth century."

## Counterfactual Significance

Shirakawa's success depended on two contingencies: the death of crown prince Sanehito (depriving the Minamoto succession line of its key asset) and the death of Emperor Horikawa at twenty-eight (removing a competent adult emperor who might have constrained the insei). Had Horikawa lived to normal age, Shirakawa's post-1107 political dominance might never have materialized. The entire insei system, often presented as structurally determined, was thus contingent on two premature deaths.
