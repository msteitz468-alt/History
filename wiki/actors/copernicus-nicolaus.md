## Overview

Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543) was a Polish-Prussian canon, physician, and astronomer who
proposed the heliocentric model of the solar system in his *De Revolutionibus Orbium Coelestium*
(1543), published shortly before his death. He trained in Bologna, Padua, and Ferrara — Italian
university cities at the center of Renaissance humanist and scientific exchange — before returning
to serve as a canon at Frauenburg (Frombork) Cathedral in Royal Prussia.

Copernicus's work is conventionally taken as the opening move of the [[scientific-revolution]].
However, George Saliba's analysis reveals that the core mathematical techniques deployed in his
astronomical models were not independently invented but derived, by an as-yet-untraced
transmission channel, from the [[maragha-school|Maragha school]] of Islamic astronomy.

## Mathematical Borrowings from Islamic Astronomy

The two mathematical theorems that Copernicus used to construct his planetary models — neither
of which appears in Euclid or Ptolemy — were the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī Couple]] and ʿUrḍī's Lemma,
both developed by Islamic astronomers 200–300 years before *De Revolutionibus*.

**The Ṭūsī Couple**: [[nasir-al-din-tusi|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]] (d. 1274) invented this theorem
in 1247 and formalized it in 1260–61. Copernicus uses an identical theorem in *De Revolutionibus*
III, 4, with a proof that matches Ṭūsī's letter for letter in its alphabetic labeling of geometric
points — with a single deviation where Arabic *zayn* becomes Latin F rather than Z, explicable
as a misreading of visually similar Arabic manuscript letters. See [[islamic-science-and-copernicus]].

**ʿUrḍī's Lemma**: [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]] (d. 1375) incorporated ʿUrḍī's Lemma into
his models for the upper planets. Copernicus's model for the upper planets is identical to Ibn
al-Shāṭir's except for the transposition from geocentrism to heliocentrism. Crucially, Copernicus
used the lemma implicitly, without providing its proof — suggesting he received the model as a
finished product without full understanding of its derivation. Noel Swerdlow confirmed this when
studying the *Commentariolus*: Copernicus misidentified where Mercury's epicycle would appear
largest, a misunderstanding that could only occur if he had "copied [the model] without fully
understanding what it was really about."

## The Question of Heliocentrism

Saliba stresses that none of the Islamic astronomers who preceded Copernicus entertained
heliocentrism. The Maragha tradition remained committed to Aristotelian geocentric cosmology.
The transposition of Ibn al-Shāṭir's models to a heliocentric frame — simply holding the Sun
fixed and allowing the Earth to revolve with the other planets — was Copernicus's conceptual
contribution. But this creates a puzzle: Copernicus began the *Commentariolus* with a complaint
about Ptolemaic equants, a complaint intelligible only within an Aristotelian framework, yet he
then abandoned that framework entirely. How he hoped to maintain planetary coherence without
Newtonian gravitation has not been explained by Copernican scholars. Saliba raises but does not
resolve this tension.

## Italian Education and Transmission Networks

Copernicus studied in Bologna, Padua, and Ferrara (c. 1496–1503), cities traversed by a
network of Arabists and orientalists who were contemporaries. These include:
- Andreas Alpagus (d. 1522), who spent 15 years in Damascus studying Arabic and returned to
  Padua as professor of medicine shortly before Copernicus's time there;
- Leo Africanus (d. ca. 1550), who taught Arabic at Bologna;
- Jean-Albert Widmanstadt (1506–c.1559), a student of Leo Africanus who knew Arabic astronomical
  texts;
- Guillaume Postel (1510–1581), a Frenchman who annotated in Latin the Arabic text of Ṭūsī's
  *Tadhkira* and purchased technical Islamic astronomical manuscripts in Constantinople in 1536.

Poland, where Copernicus was born, shared a border with the Ottoman Empire, and the northern
Italian corridor — Venice, Bologna, Florence — was the main route of intellectual and commercial
exchange between the Islamic world and Europe. Saliba argues that Copernicus "must suspect that
there were many people like Postel who could have advised or even tutored Copernicus on the
contents of Arabic astronomical texts."

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Ṭūsī Couple and ʿUrḍī's Lemma, Copernicus would have lacked the mathematical tools
to eliminate the Ptolemaic equant while maintaining models consistent with observation. Whether he
would have constructed equivalent theorems independently cannot be determined; what is known is
that he used these tools without apparently inventing them and without explicitly crediting any
source. The standard historiographic portrait — Copernicus as a revolutionary self-sufficiently
breaking from tradition — requires revision. Swerdlow and Neugebauer characterized him as "the last
Maragha astronomer."

His genuine contribution — the heliocentric reinterpretation of models designed for a geocentric
universe — remains historically significant but must be understood against this background of
intellectual debt to the Islamic astronomical tradition.
