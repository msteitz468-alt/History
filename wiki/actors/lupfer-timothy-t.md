# Timothy T. Lupfer

Timothy T. Lupfer is the author of Leavenworth Paper No. 4, *The Dynamics of Doctrine: The Changes in German Tactical Doctrine During the First World War* (Combat Studies Institute, 1981). As a U.S. Army officer associated with the Combat Studies Institute at Fort Leavenworth, he produced this influential short study on how the German Army adapted its tactics on the Western Front during the stalemate of 1914–1918.

## Background and Approach

Lupfer's work is practitioner-oriented military history, focused on the institutional and intellectual processes by which armies develop and disseminate new tactical doctrine under the pressure of modern industrialized warfare. He draws on German primary sources (regulations, after-action reports, memoirs of figures like Fritz von Lossberg and Ludendorff) and emphasizes the interplay between front-line experience, staff analysis, and high command direction.

The book highlights two major German doctrinal innovations:
- Elastic defense-in-depth (1916–17)
- Infiltration/stormtroop offensive tactics (1918)

Lupfer stresses that these were not the product of a single genius but of systematic collection of experience, experimentation, and rigorous training programs.

## Contribution via *The Dynamics of Doctrine*

Lupfer provides a clear model for understanding "the dynamics of doctrine" — how observation of enemy tactics and battlefield realities drives change, how principles are codified, and how new doctrine is actually implemented through training and command emphasis rather than mere publication.

Key insights:
- The German shift to elastic defense was a response to the failure of rigid forward defense against massive Allied artillery and infantry assaults (especially 1916 Somme).
- The 1918 offensive doctrine revived maneuver by using specialized assault units (stormtroops) that bypassed strongpoints, combined with precise artillery preparation and decentralized small-unit initiative.
- Success depended on intensive, realistic training at all levels and a command philosophy that encouraged initiative within the commander's intent.
- The process illustrates broader principles applicable to any army facing the need to adapt doctrine in the middle of a conflict.

From Chapter 3 "Tentative Generalizations" in Range 3 of [[sources/lupfer-dynamics-of-doctrine-1981]]: German doctrine was developed and applied inductively. "The process of deriving the doctrine was inductive, and the application was in the same inductive spirit." "Evidence from the battlefield was more respected than the doctrine." "For the Germans all tactical solutions were tentative." The process "still demands much talent and ability, and it still requires a deliberate search for evidence. Glib solutions do not replace hard work."

Allied contrast: experiences "became deductive formulas indiscriminately deemed appropriate for any situation, despite contradictory evidence or changed conditions." Germans maintained "a healthy curiosity and mental flexibility."

The effort was corporate: OHL (under [[ludendorff]] and [[hindenburg]]) selected talent efficiently (e.g., Capt. Hermann Geyer from subordinate units); "remarkable tolerance of dissent within the process" without weakening "the determination to succeed." "All large armies possess men of talent like Capt. Hermann Geyer; few armies use such talent so efficiently." Principles were "sufficiently general to apply to a variety of tactical conditions, but sufficiently precise and specific to insure common understanding and unity of effort. Their flexibility was their strength." "under the name of Ludendorff is comprised, exactly what a staff should be, a composite brain."

Rupprecht warning (Ch. 3): “There is no panacea. A formula is harmful. Everything must be applied according to the situation."
