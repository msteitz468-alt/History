# Dai Viet (Early Vietnam)

**Dai Viet** ("Great Viet") is the name of the **independent Vietnamese state** from the eleventh century
through the Ly, Tran, and Le dynasties — the one major Southeast Asian polity shaped not by
[[indianization-of-southeast-asia|Indianization]] but by **a millennium inside the Chinese empire**.
Documented for the wiki through the [[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v1-1992|Cambridge History
of Southeast Asia]] (Keith Taylor, Ch. 3), and the polity Reid treats as the great **Confucian,
anti-commercial exception** to his "age of commerce."

## Out of the Chinese empire (to 939)

The Red (Hong) River plain of northern Vietnam was **conquered by Han China in the first century CE** and
governed for roughly a thousand years as a Chinese frontier province (the Tang **Protectorate of An
Nam**), its ruling class absorbed into imperial officialdom. When Tang power collapsed, Vietnamese
leaders broke free — defeating a Southern Han fleet in **939** — and after a transitional warlord period
at **Hoa-lu** (Dinh Bo Linh, Le Hoan, who won Song recognition in 980–1), the aristocracy, **allied with
the Buddhist monkhood**, installed **Ly Cong Uan** (Ly Thai To). In **1009–10** he founded the **Ly
dynasty** and moved the capital to **Thang-long** (modern **Hanoi**), proclaiming the realm of **Dai
Viet**. The Buddhist monks were decisive — as the literate experts who could deal with China and mobilize
labour and opinion, they engineered a relatively peaceful, durable monarchy out of the warlord chaos.

## Ly, Tran, and the Mongol wars

The **Ly** (1009–1225) and **Tran** (1225–1400) dynasties built an aristocratic, Buddhist-flavoured state
that — almost uniquely in the region — institutionalized **conflict-free dynastic succession** (royal
abdication, marriage policy, rules for collateral lines) and absorbed Chinese political thought while
**fiercely defending its independence**. Its supreme achievement was repelling the **Mongols** — three
invasions (1257, 1285, 1287–88) thrown back, the last by Tran Hung Dao — when the Yuan had conquered China
itself.

## The Ming occupation, the Le, and the turn to Confucianism

After the Tran decayed, **Ming China reoccupied the country (1407–28)**, destroying much of the Ly–Tran
Buddhist-literary heritage before **Le Loi** expelled them and founded the **Le dynasty**. The trauma
produced a new ruling class of **militarized southern clans and neo-Confucian literati**, culminating in
**Le Thanh Ton (Le Thanh Tong, r. 1460–97)**, who built an examination-based **Confucian bureaucratic
state** on the Chinese model and conquered the Cham capital **Vijaya in 1471** (the decisive blow in the
long **nam tien**, the "march to the south," against [[champa|Champa]]). Taylor offers a subtle reading:
Le Thanh Ton's bureaucracy may signal **weakness, not strength** — an attempt to gain *objective* control
over a society whose older popular-cultural foundations of kingship had crumbled. His reign was followed
by sixteenth-century **civil wars** and the **Trinh–Nguyen** split that opened Vietnam's long southern
frontier (the Nguyen's southern kingdom — "Cochin-China" — being, as Reid notes, far more open to
maritime trade than the Confucian north).

## Taylor (2013): discontinuity, not one continuous nation

K. W. Taylor's [[taylor-history-of-the-vietnamese-2013|*A History of the Vietnamese*]] narrates this
sequence as a **series of discrete experiments** rather than the unfolding of a single national life
(see [[vietnamese-national-continuity]]). Several threads sharpen the picture above:

- **Naming as evidence.** Dinh Bo Linh's tenth-century realm was **Dai Co Viet** — a linguistic hybrid
  (Literary Chinese *dai* + vernacular *co*, both "great") reflecting the transition from Sino-Vietnamese
  bilingualism toward a distinct vernacular. **Ly Nhat Ton** (r. 1054–72) dropped *co*, renaming the realm
  **Dai Viet** in conscious conformity to classical imperial usage — one of many moments when the court
  mirrored Song forms (even tattooing soldiers "Son of Heaven's Army") while keeping formally correct
  vassal status.
- **A constructed southern imperial past.** Le Van Huu's 1272 court history claimed Thang Long's kings as
  heirs to a *southern* imperial tradition founded by Zhao To (2nd c. BCE), inherited through Dinh Bo Linh
  to the Ly, Tran, and Le — an ideological response to Yuan universalism, not a neutral chronicle.
- **The Kinh/Trai distinction (1256).** The court formally divided the realm into **Kinh** (the Red River
  "capital" plain) and **Trai** (the Thanh Hoa/Nghe An/Ha Tinh "outpost" plains) — the root of the modern
  ethnonym *Kinh* for the Vietnamese majority, and a marker of the internal regionalism Taylor stresses.
- **The Ho watershed.** The Tran's fall, [[ho-quy-ly|Ho Quy Ly]]'s usurpation (1400), the
  [[ming-occupation-of-dai-viet-1407-1427|Ming occupation]], and [[le-loi|Le Loi]]'s founding of the
  [[le-dynasty-vietnam|Later Le]] together form what Taylor calls a "historic watershed" — the destruction
  of the old Tran aristocracy, the triumph of Zhu Xi Confucianism over Buddhism, and a permanent shift of
  the "dragon of sovereignty" south toward Thanh Hoa.

The dynastic backbone is now covered in dedicated pages: the [[ly-dynasty-vietnam|Ly]],
[[tran-dynasty-vietnam|Tran]], [[le-dynasty-vietnam|Later Le]], and [[mac-dynasty-vietnam|Mac]] dynasties;
the [[trinh-lords|Trinh]] and [[nguyen-lords|Nguyen]] lords who partitioned the realm; and the
[[tay-son-dynasty|Tay Son]] before the [[nguyen-dynasty|Nguyen dynasty]] renamed the country Vietnam (1804).

## Significance: the China frontier

Dai Viet is the **frontier between Southeast Asia and China**. It shared a Southeast Asian substrate
(diet, [[women-in-precolonial-southeast-asia|relatively free women]], betel, cockfighting) but took its
**script, statecraft, Confucianism, and dense settlement from China** — which is why Reid treats it as
the borderline case of his region. Its distinctive, China-facing **historiographical tradition** (history
as an argument about the Sino-Vietnamese relationship) gives it a documentary richness "greatly out of
proportion" to Champa, Angkor, Pagan, Srivijaya, or Majapahit — and shaped Vietnamese identity itself.
