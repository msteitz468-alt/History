# Burma (Myanmar)

The westernmost Southeast Asian state, conquered piecemeal by Britain in three [[british-burma|Anglo-Burmese Wars]]
(1824–1885) and governed as a province of British India until 1937. Colonial rule dismantled the
monarchy, imported Indian labour and capital (producing acute plural-society tensions), and provoked
both Buddhist-led and Thakin nationalist resistance.

[[aung-san|Aung San]]'s generation won independence in 1948 after the [[japanese-occupation-southeast-asia|Japanese occupation]],
but the new state fractured along ethnic lines and fell to military rule in 1962, beginning the long
isolation that shaped modern Myanmar.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

Miksic and Goh locate Myanmar's Classic centres in an unexpected ecology: the central Dry Zone, under
twin rain shadows of the Western Yoma and Shan Plateau (500–1,000 mm rain, high evaporation, little
permanent standing water). Contrary to productivity expectations, the majority of important historic
archaeological sites lie there — a pattern they parallel with the Maya lowlands. The Ayeyarwadi
offers Southeast Asia's longest uninterrupted navigable stretch (~1,500 km) and captures ~70% of
Myanmar runoff; the Dry Zone extends ~100 km up the Chindwin, to Mandalay, and ~100 km south of the
Chindwin junction. Protoclassic populations developed surplus methods on "black cotton soil" (Chindwin,
Monywa, Shwebo, central Dry Zone) via mixed farming rather than rice monoculture; Kyaukse became a
major rice bowl only about a millennium ago without Early Classic super-centres on that tract.

**Connectivity.** The Rakhine coastal strip (300 km long, <50 km wide; 3,000–3,500 mm rain) holds some
of the oldest Protoclassic remains along the Kaladan (and later Postclassic Mrauk-U), typologically
close to Ayeyarwadi-basin material; landward isolation coexists with millennia on India–Seasia
maritime routes. The main overland Seasia–South Asia channel is the Manipur Highlands through the
Hukawng Valley; the Western Mountain range is an effective (and malarial) barrier.

**Classic to Postclassic.** Pre-Bagan Early Classic research focuses on Sri Ksetra and Tagaung as the
transition to the Middle Classic central polity of [[pagan-kingdom|Bagan]] (~eleventh century peak;
mandala lasting roughly 450 years in Miksic's framing). Bagan is grouped with Angkor among "ancient
mandalas" that faded by the Postclassic. After Bagan's late-thirteenth-century decline, Late Classic
Myanmar fragmented into four geospatial regions/mandalas (upper Myanmar under the three brothers of
Myinsaing/Sagaing/Pinya; lower coastal polities; Shan zone; western Rakhine) competing and allying in
accordion fashion. Of 251 inscriptions 1299–1368, only 24 are at Bagan versus 112 Sagaing, 46
Myinsaing, 34 Pinya. Inwa (Ava) was founded 1364; Bago (Pegu) rose as a rival from 1385. In the
Postclassic, Bago was one of three dominant mainland kingdoms (with [[ayutthaya|Ayutthaya]] and island
[[melaka|Melaka]]/Banten). Portuguese adventurers destabilized Arakan and the Ayeyarwadi Delta and
traded at Bago and Mottama (Martaban); Myanmar storage jars and celadon (possibly Twante) appear on
wrecks such as Lena Shoal and the Brunei Junk.

**Sources.** The oldest surviving Myanmar manuscript is fifteenth-century (*Rakhaing Minthami Egyin*);
genres include *yazawin* and *ayedawpon*, with a golden age of *egyin* under first Taungngu (1539–
1599) and chronicle fluorescence under Restored Taungngu at Ava (1597–1752). Nearly a hundred other
walled sites of various periods exist; Early Classic research remains nascent.
