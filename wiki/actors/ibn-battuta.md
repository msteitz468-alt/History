# Ibn Battuta

**Abu Abdallah ibn Battuta** (1304–1377) was the **most travelled person of the pre-modern world**
and the author — through his dictated **Rihla** (*Book of Travels*) — of the single most important
eyewitness portrait of the 14th-century **dar al-Islam**. A Tangier-born Maliki jurist of Berber
ancestry but Arab city culture, he left home in **1325** for the hajj and did not settle down for
nearly thirty years, crossing the equivalent of some fifty modern countries from Morocco to China.
Documented for the wiki through **[[ibn-battuta-black-africa|*Ibn Battuta in Black Africa*]]**
(Hamdun & King, trans.), the selections covering his sub-Saharan journeys.

## A life on the road

An *‘alim* of modest scholarly gifts but excellent social skills, Ibn Battuta exemplifies the
**mobility of the medieval Muslim learned class**, whose primary loyalty was to the *dar al-Islam*
rather than to state or tribe and who could find lodging, employment, and prestige anywhere Islamic
law was honoured. He performed the hajj six or seven times, dabbled in Sufism, and — in the
"rougher-edged" frontier kingdoms where Arab jurists were scarce — won lucrative posts: **eight
years as judge and official in the Delhi Sultanate (1334–41)**, a (shipwrecked) embassy toward
Mongol China, and a spell as chief judge in the Maldives. His career rode the **Pax Mongolica** and
the chain of strong, commerce-minded states (Marinid Morocco, Mamluk Egypt, Delhi, Mali) that made
the early 14th century an auspicious time to travel; it wound down just as the **Black Death** (1347,
which killed his mother) ended that halcyon age. He retired as a provincial Moroccan judge and
dictated the *Rihla* to the literary scholar **Ibn Juzayy** c. 1355.

## The African journeys

Ibn Battuta's two sub-Saharan trips occupied only a small fraction of his career but yield his most
historically precious pages — the **only eyewitness account of both the East African city-states and
the Mali empire** in the period:
- **East Africa** (c. 1329 or 1331): a monsoon voyage to **Zaila, Mogadishu, Mombasa, and Kilwa** —
  see [[swahili-coast]].
- **West Africa** (1352–54): his **last journey**, across the Sahara to the **[[mali-empire|Mali
  empire]]** of **Mansa Sulaiman**, the fullest contemporary description of the Sudanic empire's
  court, religion, and trade — see [[trans-saharan-trade]], [[griot-oral-tradition]], [[kanem-bornu]].

## Significance and reliability

The *Rihla* is a **first-rate but partial** source. Its value is unique — for medieval East Africa
and Mali there is simply nothing else written by an eyewitness. But Ibn Battuta saw the Sudan through
the lens of a **cosmopolitan Maliki jurist and a patriarchal Middle-Eastern gentleman**: he was
repeatedly scandalised by West African women's freedom and unveiling, by female nudity at the Mali
court, and by the "self-abasement" (dusting) rituals, and he relays second-hand **cannibal** tales of
the forest peoples. The book was composed years later from memory, with Ibn Juzayy's interpolations
and borrowings (e.g. the famous shared detail with an earlier traveller, Ibn Jubayr). Read against
these biases, it remains the indispensable inside view of Africa within the medieval Islamic world.
