# Ibn Battuta

**Abu Abdallah ibn Battuta** (1304–1377) was the **most travelled person of the pre-modern world**
and the author — through his dictated **Rihla** (*Book of Travels*) — of the single most important
eyewitness portrait of the 14th-century **dar al-Islam**. A Tangier-born Maliki jurist of Berber
ancestry but Arab city culture, he left home in **1325** for the hajj and did not settle down for
nearly thirty years, crossing the equivalent of some fifty modern countries from Morocco to China.
Documented for the wiki through the full **[[ibn-battuta-travels-1829|*The Travels of Ibn Battuta*]]** (Samuel Lee trans., 1829) of the *Rihla* (dictated to Ibn Juzayy c. 1355) plus the earlier specialized selections in **[[ibn-battuta-black-africa|*Ibn Battuta in Black Africa*]]** (Hamdun & King). The Lee text supplies the complete itinerary 1325–1354.

## A life on the road

An *‘alim* of modest scholarly gifts but excellent social skills, Ibn Battuta exemplifies the
**mobility of the medieval Muslim learned class**, whose primary loyalty was to the *dar al-Islam*
rather than to state or tribe and who could find lodging, employment, and prestige anywhere Islamic
law was honoured. He performed the hajj six or seven times, dabbled in Sufism, and — in the
"rougher-edged" frontier kingdoms where Arab jurists were scarce — won lucrative posts: **eight
years as judge and official in the Delhi Sultanate (1334–41)**, a (shipwrecked) embassy toward
Mongol China, and a spell as chief judge in the Maldives. His career rode the **Pax Mongolica** and
the chain of strong, commerce-minded states (Marinid Morocco, Mamluk Egypt, Delhi, Mali) that made
the early 14th century an auspicious time to travel; it wound down just as the **Black Death** (1347,
which killed his mother) ended that halcyon age. He retired as a provincial Moroccan judge and
dictated the *Rihla* to the literary scholar **[[ibn-juzayy|Ibn Juzayy]]** c. 1355.

The full *Rihla* (preserved in abridged form and translated by Samuel Lee, 1829) covers the entire
itinerary: North Africa and Egypt; Syria and the first hajj attempts; Iraq and Persia; East Africa
(Zaila to Kilwa); Hormuz and Anatolia (where he observed the Akhi brotherhoods); the Golden Horde
under Özbeg Khan; the approach through Transoxiana and the Hindu Kush; eight years at the Delhi
court of **[[muhammad-bin-tughluq|Muhammad bin Tughluq]]** (as qadi); the failed China embassy and
Malabar adventures; service in the Maldives; Ceylon; Sumatra, Java and arrival in Yuan China
(Zaytun/Quanzhou and Khansa/Hangzhou); and the return via the Indian Ocean, Arabia, Persia, and
finally the West African crossing to Mali before reaching Fez. A side journey to Andalusia is also
recorded.

## The African journeys

Ibn Battuta's two sub-Saharan trips occupied only a small fraction of his career but yield his most
historically precious pages — the **only eyewitness account of both the East African city-states and
the Mali empire** in the period:
- **East Africa** (c. 1329 or 1331): a monsoon voyage to **Zaila, Mogadishu, Mombasa, and Kilwa** —
  see [[swahili-coast]] (detailed in the specialized selections [[ibn-battuta-black-africa]]).
- **West Africa** (1352–54): his **last journey**, across the Sahara to the **[[mali-empire|Mali
  empire]]** of **Mansa Sulaiman**, the fullest contemporary description of the Sudanic empire's
  court, religion, and trade — see [[trans-saharan-trade]], [[griot-oral-tradition]], [[kanem-bornu]].

## The wider Rihla (Lee 1829)

The complete text supplies primary testimony for:
- Anatolian futuwwa (Akhi) brotherhoods
- The court and customs of the Golden Horde under Özbeg
- Eight years as qadi under **[[muhammad-bin-tughluq|Muhammad bin Tughluq]]** in Delhi, including the capital transfer experiment and the sultan's character
- Detailed descriptions of Malabar (Calicut), the Maldives (where he again served as qadi), and Ceylon
- Yuan China (Zaytun/[[quanzhou]], paper money, porcelain production, Hangzhou)
- The Indian Ocean maritime world at its 14th-century height

## Significance and reliability

The *Rihla* is a **first-rate but partial** source. Its value is unique for the regions where other
eyewitnesses are scarce (East Africa, Mali, Maldives, certain Chinese ports). But Ibn Battuta saw
the world through the lens of a **cosmopolitan Maliki jurist and a patriarchal Middle-Eastern gentleman**. He was repeatedly scandalised by frontier gender customs and "self-abasement" rituals, relayed second-hand marvels and cannibal tales, and composed the work years later from memory with Ibn Juzayy's literary interventions. The Lee translation is itself an abridgment of the epitome. Read against these biases with cross-checks to other sources (Delhi chronicles, Abu-Lughod's world-system synthesis, etc.), it remains the indispensable inside view of the 14th-century *dar al-Islam* and its connections.
