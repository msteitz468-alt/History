# The Sokoto Caliphate

The **Sokoto Caliphate** was the **largest state in nineteenth-century Africa** — a vast Islamic empire
across what is now northern Nigeria and beyond, founded by the jihad of **Usman dan Fodio** and ruling
through a federation of emirates. Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO
[[gha-vol6-19th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. VI]]** (ch. 22, Last).

## The jihad and foundation

**Usman dan Fodio** (Shehu Usman), a learned **Fulani** scholar of the **Qadiriyya** order, preached reform
against the **[[hausa-city-states|Hausa]]** king of Gobir, whom he condemned as oppressive and only
nominally Muslim. Persecuted, he declared a **jihad in 1804**, was acclaimed *amir al-mu'minin*
(Commander of the Faithful), and — with Fulani and disaffected Hausa support — **conquered the Hausa
states** within a few years (see [[west-african-jihads]]). A new capital was founded at **Sokoto (1809)**.
Usman, more scholar than administrator, divided governance between his brother **Abdullahi** and his son
**Muhammad Bello**, who became the first caliph and the empire's organizer.

## Structure: a caliphate of emirates

Sokoto was a **federation**: the caliph at Sokoto held religious and political supremacy, while some
**thirty emirates** — Kano, Katsina, Zaria, Adamawa, Ilorin, and others — governed locally under appointed
**emirs** who owed allegiance and tribute to the caliph. It was a genuinely **literate, bureaucratic
Islamic state**, with a flourishing tradition of Arabic and Hausa/Fulfulde scholarship (the writings of
Usman, Abdullahi, and Bello), sharia courts, and an elaborate administration.

## Economy and slavery

The caliphate was prosperous — the **[[hausa-city-states|Hausa]]** commercial and craft economy (textiles,
leather, Kano's dyed cloth) continued under new management, tied into the [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-
Saharan]] and Sudanic trades. It also rested heavily on **slavery**: the jihad states ran great
**slave-worked plantations** and raided "pagan" peoples to the south for captives, making Sokoto one of the
largest slaveholding societies of the nineteenth-century world.

## Conquest

For nearly a century Sokoto was the dominant power of the central Sudan. It fell to the **British** in
**1903** (under Frederick Lugard), who conquered the caliphate but **ruled through its emirs** — the model
case of British **"indirect rule"** — so that the Sokoto aristocracy and its institutions survived into
the colonial and post-colonial order of northern Nigeria.

## Significance

The Sokoto Caliphate is the **greatest of the West African jihad states** and the largest precolonial state
of nineteenth-century Africa — a literate, federal Islamic empire whose conquest and the British decision to
rule *through* it shaped modern Nigeria's enduring north–south divide.
