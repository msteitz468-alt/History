# Le Van Duyet

**Le Van Duyet** (1763–1832) was a eunuch general of southern-peasant origin who became the most
powerful figure in southern Vietnam under the early [[nguyen-dynasty|Nguyen dynasty]] — twice viceroy
of Saigon (Gia Dinh) and one of [[gia-long|Gia Long]]'s most trusted commanders. His career, and the
posthumous desecration of his tomb, sit at the center of the collision between the loose, regionally
tolerant governance of the founding reign and [[minh-mang|Minh Mang]]'s drive to centralize.

## Role and Career

Rising from humble southern origins, Le Van Duyet served Nguyen Phuc Anh (the future Gia Long) as a
general through the reunification wars against the Tay Son. Under Gia Long he governed the south as
viceroy of Gia Dinh, exercising broad autonomous authority over a diverse frontier population — Vietnamese
settlers, Chinese, Khmer, Cham, and Christians — whom he was known to protect. Gia Long's "light touch"
depended on such trusted regional viceroys (Le Van Duyet in the south, Nguyen Van Thanh in the north)
governing on the crown's behalf. Le Van Duyet also pacified frontier unrest — the Quang Ngai uplands, and
Nghe An/Thanh Hoa banditry — and, in 1820, suppressed the monk Ke's rebellion in Cambodia, leaving a
Vietnamese garrison at Phnom Penh that turned Ang Chan into a docile vassal.

## Counterfactual Significance

Le Van Duyet embodied the semi-autonomous southern viceroyalty that had governed the Mekong region since
the [[nguyen-lords|Nguyen lords']] settlement era. [[minh-mang|Minh Mang]] distrusted this arrangement; he
had already begun appointing his own men over Cambodia against Le Van Duyet's advice in 1829. After Le Van
Duyet's death in 1832 Minh Mang abolished the Gia Dinh viceroyalty, reorganized the south into the Six
Provinces (Luc Tinh), and posthumously condemned and desecrated Le Van Duyet's tomb. This humiliation was
the proximate trigger of the [[le-van-khoi-rebellion-1833-1835|Le Van Khoi rebellion]] (1833–1835) — Le
Van Khoi being his adopted son — the great southern revolt against centralization that entangled with war
against [[siam-chakri-dynasty|Siam]]. Without Le Van Duyet's disgrace, the specific southern explosion of
1833 is hard to reconstruct.

## Historiography

Taylor ([[taylor-history-of-the-vietnamese-2013|*A History of the Vietnamese*]]) presents Le Van Duyet as
the human face of a genuine structural divide — the accommodating, plural south versus Minh Mang's
uniformity — rather than as a simple loyalist or traitor. In the twentieth century Le Van Duyet's tomb at
Gia Dinh (Lang Ong) became a major popular shrine, and his rehabilitation and veneration in the south
carried an implicit regional-political charge that fits the wiki's
[[vietnamese-national-continuity|continuity-vs-discontinuity]] debate.
