# Appius Claudius Caecus

## Overview

**Appius Claudius Caecus** ("the Blind," allegedly blinded in later life) was the
dominant figure in Roman public life from approximately **312 to 279 BCE**. He was
censor (312), consul (307, 296), dictator (285?), and remained politically active
in the Roman senate until the eve of the Second Punic War. Cornell calls him
"the first living personality in Roman history" — the first Roman about whom we
can form a rounded historical picture, partly because some of his own words survived
in written form.

## Political Career

### The Censorship of 312 BCE

Appius Claudius's censorship was "truly sensational" (Cornell). The main events:

**Public works.** Appius ordered the construction of two great public works that
bore his name:
- The **Via Appia** (Appian Way): the great military highway from Rome to Capua,
  begun in 312, initially from Rome to Capua (~200 km), later extended to Brundisium.
  This transformed the strategic position of Rome in southern Italy and is perhaps
  his most enduring legacy.
- The **Aqua Appia**: Rome's first aqueduct, bringing fresh water from the Sabine
  hills into the city. Both projects were enormously expensive, and according to
  Diodorus (XX.36) Appius acted without senatorial authorization and emptied the treasury.

**The senatorial roll.** In drawing up the list of senators, Appius outraged the
establishment by passing over traditionally qualified men in favor of his own
choices, including the **sons of freedmen**. His new senatorial roll was not
recognized by the consuls of 311 BCE, who continued to use the old list — but
his reforms remained in partial effect.

**Tribal reorganization.** Appius's most politically significant reform was
redistributing the **urban poor** (the *humiles* — artisans, traders, many of them
freedmen or their descendants) across all thirty-one tribes, rather than confining
them to the four urban tribes. This dramatically increased the voting weight of the
urban proletariat in the tribal assemblies.

This reform was **reversed in 304 BCE** by the censors Q. Fabius Rullianus and
P. Decius Mus, who re-confined the *humiles* to the four urban tribes.

### The Ara Maxima Transfer and the Religious Dimension (312 BCE)

North (CAH VII.2, Ch. 12) adds a dimension not in Cornell's account: Appius's
censorship also saw the transfer of the **cult of Hercules at the Ara Maxima**
from the **gens Potitia** (a patrician family that had hereditary priestly
responsibility for the cult) to the **state** — the only known instance in Roman
history of a gentile cult being taken away from its gens and placed under public
control. Livy notes this; North regards it as "not so isolated as it now seems,"
suggesting it was part of a broader attack on the religious monopolies of the
great gentes contemporaneous with Appius's political program. If the gens Potitia
was indeed destroyed by a divine curse shortly afterward (as the tradition claims),
this was remembered as retribution for the sacrilege of the transfer. See
[[roman-republican-religion]] for the broader context of gentile religious authority.

### Cn. Flavius and the Ius Flavianum (304 BCE)

Appius's agent **Cn. Flavius** — a secretary (*scriba*) who was the son of a
freedman — was elected curule aedile for 304 BCE (the first of his class to hold
the office). Acting apparently as Appius's instrument, Flavius published:
- The **legis actiones** (the formulaic procedures for legal actions), previously
  kept secret by the pontifices — breaking the aristocratic monopoly on legal
  procedure
- A **calendar** of *dies fasti* (days on which legal business was permitted)

This publication — the *ius Flavianum* — was a direct attack on the last pillar
of patrician legal monopoly (the publication of the Twelve Tables having already
broken the monopoly on substantive law). The conservative establishment was appalled;
nobles reportedly removed their gold rings and military decorations in protest at
Flavius's election.

### Opposition to the Lex Ogulnia (300 BCE)

When the Lex Ogulnia admitted plebeians to the pontificate and augurate (300 BCE),
Appius vigorously opposed it. Cornell explains this apparent contradiction with his
democratic reputation: Appius was not opposing plebeian rights as such, but opposing
the *closed oligarchic power-sharing* that the Lex Ogulnia established — it made the
priestly colleges self-perpetuating oligarchic cliques divided between patrician and
plebeian *nobiles*, not opened to the people at large.

### The Speech Against Peace with Pyrrhus (279 BCE)

After the Roman defeat at Asculum (279 BCE), when a Roman embassy to Pyrrhus under
Fabricius Luscinus seemed to have obtained terms, Appius — now old and blind — had
himself carried to the Senate and delivered a speech arguing against acceptance of
peace. This speech was still known to Cicero (*Cato* 16; *Brutus* 61) — the first
Latin prose speech preserved in later tradition. Its argument: Rome should negotiate
only from a position of victory, not defeat.

The Senate followed his advice; peace was refused.

## Intellectual Legacy

Appius Claudius Caecus was the **father of Latin prose**. Works attributed to him:
- Political speeches (including the Pyrrhus speech)
- A work of jurisprudence (Pomponius, *Dig.* 1.2.2.36)
- A collection of moral *carmina* (sayings) that circulated in the late Republic
  and was known to the Greek philosopher Panaetius (second century BCE)

The most famous saying to survive: *"Faber est suae quisque fortunae"* — "Each man
is the architect of his own fortune."

Cornell dismisses the attempt to treat these works as late forgeries: "the traditional
picture of Appius does have some authentic touches."

## Character Assessment

Cornell's analysis of Appius Claudius is the most balanced available. He was:

**A democratic populist in methods**: He aimed to build a personal following among
the mass of the people through the tribal reorganization, Flavius's legal publications,
the enfranchisement of freedmen's sons, and massive popular public works. His politics
resembled Cleisthenes (the comparison is Mommsen's).

**An aristocratic patrician in values**: He opposed the Lex Ogulnia; he tried to
contrive all-patrician consular colleges; he was possessed of the Claudian arrogance
(the tradition is consistent). He wielded a massive *clientela*.

**Swimming against the tide**: Despite his program, the outcome of the political
struggles he participated in was the consolidation of a conservative oligarchy, not
popular government. His tribal reform was reversed within eight years; his senatorial
innovations were blocked. He succeeded only in the Via Appia, the aqueduct, and
the Flavian legal publication — institutional legacies that outlasted his political
defeats.

Cornell: "It is clear that Appius was swimming against the tide. His efforts to
democratize the assembly and to assert its sovereignty were ultimately abortive."

## Counterfactual Significance

Appius Claudius represents the **road not taken** in Roman constitutional development.
His program pointed toward a genuinely democratic republic — popular sovereignty,
open access to legal knowledge, broad citizen participation. That program was defeated
by the consolidating oligarchy of the third century. What Rome became (the senatorial
oligarchy of the Polybian constitution) was not inevitable; Appius's career shows
it was contested.

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010)

Harriet I. Flower ([[flower-roman-republics-2010|*Roman Republics*]]) marks Appius
Claudius Caecus, censor **312**, as the **first Roman whose political biography and
legislative program can be discussed in meaningful detail** — a practical starting point
if one abandons detailed fifth-century reconstruction. In her early-republics map
([[roman-republics-periodization]]), the late fourth century is the hinge into the first
*nobiles* republic (Republic 3, from ~300); the censorship gained ground partly through
Appius’s activities, alongside regular plebeian consulship after 342 and definition of
praetor and censors in Republic 2 (from 367/6). Flower’s interest is periodizing and
source-critical rather than a full career narrative: Appius anchors the earliest
reconstructible individual politics at the threshold of *nobiles* culture.
