# The Roman Kings (Regal Period)

## The Traditional Seven

Roman tradition preserved seven kings in sequence: **Romulus**, **Numa Pompilius**,
**Tullus Hostilius**, **Ancus Marcius**, **Tarquinius Priscus**, **Servius
Tullius**, and **Tarquinius Superbus**. The tradition was canonized by the late
Republic (Fabius Pictor, Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus) and is **not reliably
historical for the first four** and heavily elaborated for the last three. See
[[early-roman-source-criticism]] for the methodology.

Momigliano's key analytical verdict ([[walbank-cah-v7-2-1989|CAH VII.2]] Ch. 3):
the Roman *kings were band chiefs*, not hereditary dynasts in the Greek or Near
Eastern sense. Royalty was not hereditary — each king required inaugural rites
(*inauguratio*) and recognition by the senate and by the *comitia curiata*. The
interregnum procedure (successive *interreges* drawn from patrician senators) that
filled gaps between kings reveals the structural reality: a strong aristocratic
senate around a non-hereditary military leader.

## The "Band Chief" Thesis

The most important interpretive key for the regal period is the **band-chief
phenomenon**: leaders (*condottieri*) who acquired a following of armed clients,
operated in multiple cities, and settled in one where conditions were favorable.
Evidence includes:

- **Mastarna** (Macstrna = Latin *magister*?) and the **brothers Vibenna** (Caeles
  and Aulus), attested by the François Tomb paintings from Vulci and by Emperor
  Claudius's speech (*ILS* 212). Claudius identified Mastarna with Servius
  Tullius; Momigliano thinks it more prudent to keep them separate. The
  inscription on a bucchero vase at Veii confirms the historical existence of
  *Avile Vipiennas*.
- **Attus Clausus** (founder of the Claudii): migrated with his retinue from
  Sabine country to Rome c. 504 BCE, receiving land from the young Republic.
- **Publius Valerius**: the *Lapis Satricanus* (c. 500 BCE) attests his *suodales*
  (companions) in arms at Satricum — confirms a figure known in the Roman
  tradition as P. Valerius Poplicola as an actual band leader.
- **Appius Herdonius**: a Sabine chieftain who occupied the Capitol by surprise
  c. 460 BCE — a late survival of the same phenomenon.

The band-chief model explains the Etruscans in Rome not as "conquest" but as
aristocratic immigration into a city that was ethnically open. Rome was a
frontier city at a major Tiber crossing; it was naturally a target for ambitious
leaders from Etruria and the Sabine hills.

## The Last Three Kings and Their Context

**Tarquinius Priscus** (trad. 616–578 BCE): son of the Corinthian émigré Demaratus
of Tarquinii. His career as an outsider who became king by impressing the royal
household is plausible in the world of Archaic condottieri. His doubling of the
cavalry and construction of the Circus Maximus and early fortifications are less
reliably attributed.

**Servius Tullius** (trad. 578–535 BCE): a Latin king inserted between two Etruscan
rulers. His achievement: the reorganization of the army and assembly on the
**centuriate** system (five property classes, 193 centuries), the extension of
Roman tribal organization, the construction of the *Servian agger* (earth wall),
and the establishment of the Aventine sanctuary of Diana as a Latin federal
shrine. Whether the full five-class organization belonged to the sixth century or
is a retrojection of the fourth century is debated. The archaeologically
demonstrable step was probably simpler: a division between *classis* (hoplite
infantry, organized by wealth) and *infra classem* (auxiliary troops), the
nucleus of the later centuriate system. Momigliano sees Servius as consciously
trying to prevent hereditary patrician privilege from crystallizing — an attempt
only partially successful.

**Tarquinius Superbus** (trad. 535–509 BCE): an Etruscan tyrant, whose career
parallels the Greek tyrannies of the sixth century. His alleged murder of Servius,
his refusal of senatorial advice, and his expulsion are plausible in structural
terms. The Roman tradition that the Etruscans (under **Porsenna of Clusium**)
attempted to restore him is confirmed by the embarrassing counter-tradition that
**Porsenna actually took Rome** (Tacitus, Pliny) — a fact the main annalistic
tradition suppressed to preserve the narrative of patriotic resistance (Horatius
Codes, Mucius Scaevola, Cloelia). The ultimate liberation came from the Latins
and the Greek tyrant **Aristodemus of Cumae**, not from Roman courage — another
fact the tradition minimized.

## The Foundation Legend and Its Function

The Romulus-Aeneas synthesis (Romulus as the mythical founder of Rome in the
eighth century; Aeneas as the Trojan ancestor of the Alban kings from whom
Romulus descends) was canonical by the late third century BCE (Fabius Pictor,
Naevius). Its ideological function was clear: Rome declared itself **neither
Greek nor Etruscan** — Trojan in origin (a third option), with a civic identity
built on the admission of heterogeneous elements (Romulus's asylum, the Sabine
rape, the Claudian migration). The Roman self-image was of a **city of mixed
foundation, divinely blessed but fundamentally inclusive**.

The Romulean state was organized into **three tribes** (Ramnes, Tities, Luceres)
and **thirty curiae** — a structure that survived (as vestigial voting and
ceremonial units) through the Republic. The *lex curiata de imperio* that conferred
power on magistrates descended from the ancient confirmation of the king's
authority by the *comitia curiata*.

## Patricians, Plebeians, and the Regal Period

Momigliano argues that the **distinction between patricians and plebeians did not
originate with Romulus** (despite the tradition) but developed gradually during the
monarchy as certain *gentes* monopolized priesthoods and senate power. The
*comitia centuriata*'s property criterion did not stop this — indeed it may have
reinforced it if patricians were the main wealth-holders. The plebeian *gentes*
had the same *nomen gentile* naming system; the rhetorical claim that plebeians
had no *gentes* is a fourth-century polemic, not a fact.

The transition from monarchy to Republic was therefore not a liberation of "the
people" but a seizure of power by the **patrician senate**, aided by the
structural instability of non-hereditary kingship and the collapse of Etruscan
regional power after the battle of Aricia (c. 504 BCE) and the Greek/Latin defeat
of Porsenna.

## Historiography

The regal period is the most contested field in Roman historiography. The volume's
editors chose four scholars (Torelli: archaeological; Momigliano: literary/
comparative; Drummond: fifth-century institutional; Cornell: expansion) with
explicitly different methodological approaches, **rejecting** any single definitive
account. Key standing controversies:

- Were the *Capitoline Fasti* (the consular list) maintained from 509 BCE or are
  the early decades reconstructed? (Debated: see [[early-roman-source-criticism]])
- Was Servius Tullius's reform a sixth-century reality or a fourth-century
  retrojection? (Drummond/Cornell lean toward partial authenticity; Momigliano is
  more cautious)
- Is Mastarna = Servius Tullius? (Claudius said yes; Momigliano says probably
  not)
- Did Porsenna actually take Rome? (Strongly implied by Tacitus/Pliny; suppressed
  by the annalistic tradition)
