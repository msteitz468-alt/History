## Overview

Tōgō Heihachirō (1848–1934) was the commanding admiral of the Imperial Japanese Navy's Combined Fleet during the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]] and the victor of the [[battle-of-tsushima-1905|Battle of Tsushima]] (May 27–28, 1905), the most decisive naval engagement in modern history up to that point. He became the most celebrated Japanese military figure of the Meiji era, often compared in Japanese popular memory to Horatio Nelson.

## Role and Decisions

As Commander of the Combined Fleet from the outset of the war in February 1904, Tōgō directed both the early naval operations off Port Arthur and the climactic engagement at Tsushima. At Port Arthur on February 9, 1904, he raised signal flags with the message: "Victory or defeat will be determined by this battle. All hands, do your utmost" (Keene, ch. 54). The early Port Arthur operations — night destroyer attacks that damaged two Russian battleships and a cruiser — gave Japan control of the sea, enabling the blockade of Port Arthur and the landing of forces in Manchuria.

At Tsushima, Tōgō's fleet intercepted the Russian Baltic Fleet (Second Pacific Squadron) as it entered Japanese waters on May 26–27, 1905 after its 18,000-mile voyage from the Baltic. The two-day battle on May 27–28 resulted in the near-total annihilation of the Russian squadron. Kaiser Wilhelm II compared it to Trafalgar; Kaneko Kentarō called it "the greatest naval victory of the world's history"; President Theodore Roosevelt replied to Kaneko: "This is the greatest phenomenon the world has ever seen. Even the battle of Trafalgar could not match this" (Keene, ch. 55).

## Counterfactual Significance

Tsushima was the essential precondition for the peace terms Japan obtained at Portsmouth. Without decisive naval victory, Russia retained the strategic option of continuing the war with the expectation of rebuilding its Pacific naval position. Tōgō's victory closed that option, making Russian defeat clearly irreversible and enabling Roosevelt's mediation to succeed. A different naval outcome — even an inconclusive one — would have dramatically altered the peace terms and Japan's subsequent regional position.
