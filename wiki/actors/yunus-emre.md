## Overview

Yūnus Emre (c. 1240–c. 1321) is the founding figure of Turkish mystical folk poetry and one of the most beloved poets of the Turkish literary tradition. A near-contemporary of Jalāl al-Dīn [[rumi|Rūmī]] in Anatolia, he accomplished for Turkish what Rūmī had accomplished for Persian: he transformed a literary language into a vehicle capable of bearing the full weight of Sufi mystical experience. His poems — simple, musical, warm, and theologically rich — are still memorized by Turkish school children, still sung in religious gatherings, and still set by modern composers. Schimmel describes him as having "left Turkey a treasure of his simple, deep-felt, touchingly memorable poems, which are still known today by school children" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Life

Not much is known about Yūnus Emre's life with certainty. According to the most recent research available to Schimmel, he died in 1321 at approximately age 72, placing his birth around 1240–1249. He seems to have grown up in the circles of Turkish Sufis that developed after the Seljukid conquest of central and eastern Anatolia (after 1071), in a milieu that partly continued the tradition of Aḥmad Yasawī (d. 1166) — the first known Sufi poet in a Turkic language — and that was also probably influenced by the wave of mystics who fled eastern Iran before the Mongol advance in the early thirteenth century.

Yūnus says himself that he was a disciple of a mystic called **Tapduq Emre** for forty years (the number of perseverance in Islamic mystical convention). The "Emre" suffix probably indicates membership in a loose grouping of Anatolian mystics who had not yet fully organized into the institutional order (tariqa) system. He may have met Rūmī during the latter's last years in Konya. Numerous places in Turkey claim to house his real tomb, and the problem of authentic attribution is reflected in his texts as well as his geography.

## Textual Problems

The textual history of Yūnus Emre's poetry is complex. The different editions of his *Dīwān* produced by the Turkish scholar Abdülbâki Gölpınarlı show increasing skepticism toward authenticity: his collections were augmented over time by later poets writing in the same tradition, and many attributions are impossible to verify. Schimmel notes that "their collections have been augmented by additions from later poets, and many of them reflect rather a whole tradition — that of poems sung in the tekkes, the centers of Sufi life — than the original work of a single poet" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]). The genre of *ilāhī* (mystical poem in popular meter meant for communal singing) was so strongly associated with Yūnus that later poets wrote in his voice.

For working purposes, Schimmel accepts as Yūnus's the poems collected by Gölpınarlı in his 1943 edition, while acknowledging that many are now considered spurious. "Yet, they reveal who Yūnus might have been and how he might have felt."

## Poetic Style and Form

Yūnus used both classical Persian meters (adapted to Turkish) and native Turkish syllabic meters. His most distinctive work is in Turkish syllabic meter with the form *musammāt* — four-line verses rhyming aaaa, bbba — which approximates the form of Turkish folk songs. Schimmel notes that Rūmī also preferred meters in which each hemistich can be divided by caesura, bringing both poets close to the simple forms of Turkish folk songs: "Yūnus skillfully applied the classical meters to his own simple mother tongue, but his best poems are written in Turkish popular meters, in which syllable counting is the essential requirement (e.g., 8+7, 8+8, 6+6 syllables)" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

His imagery combined the stock of classical Sufi symbolism (the nightingale and rose, wine and intoxication, the beloved master) with images drawn directly from the Anatolian landscape: the wailing waterwheel cut from its forest and longing for its reedbed home; the stone-breasted mountain ranges as enemies separating lover from master; the cloud hanging like dark tresses from snow-covered hilltops, weeping. The waterwheel image — a Near Eastern soundscape — is shared with Rūmī's reed flute as an emblem of separation and longing.

His poem "Look, what love has made of me!" (Y 124) — with its refrain (*nakarat*) structure intended for communal singing — is among his most popular.

A significant subset of his poems repeat liturgical formulas (*al-ḥamdulillāh*, divine names) at the end of each verse: John R. Walsh argued persuasively that these were "probably meant to be recited as *dhikr* formulae in the dervish communities" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]). The integration of poem and ritual is thus structural, not incidental. See [[dhikr]].

## Themes

**The dhikr of creation**: Yūnus presents the entire natural world as engaged in continuous remembrance of God. His best-known poem on this theme (Y 552) has all mountains, stones, birds, fish, and gazelles calling upon God together:

> "With the mountains, with the stones / Will I call Thee, Lord, o Lord! / With the birds in early dawn / Will I call Thee, Lord, o Lord!"

**The beloved master**: The sheikh Tapduq Emre figures in Yūnus's poetry as the mediating presence through whom union with God becomes possible, but with the important qualification that "even the sheikh can only prepare man for union, which depends solely upon grace" (Y 516). The image "Yūnus was a falcon, perched on Tapduq's arm" (Y 179) — drawn from Rūmī's falcon symbolism — captures this dependence.

**Death and eschatology**: Yūnus meditates extensively on death and the Last Judgment, sometimes with fear ("man has to wear a shirt without collar and sleeves" when the "negro" interrogating angel comes), sometimes with the trust of the mystic who knows God's mercy.

**Cosmic consciousness**: The most ecstatic poems identify the self with all of creation and all of prophetic history — "I am the First and the Last ... I am he who gave Noah the flood" — in a manner comparable to Sufi poetry in the Indian Subcontinent. These poems push against the boundaries of Sufi convention and shade toward the "cosmic consciousness" (*wujūdī* identification) characteristic of the Ibn ʿArabī tradition.

**The *tekerleme***: Yūnus also composed poems in the *tekerleme* genre — apparently nonsensical folk-poetry with impossible images ("the fish climbed upon the poplar tree to eat there pickled pitch"). His most famous such poem (Y 131) begins "I climbed upon the plum tree / To pluck grapes there." The later mystic Niyāzī Miṣrī (d. 1697) wrote a full commentary interpreting the "prune," "grape," and "nut" as sharīʿa, ṭarīqa, and ḥaqīqa respectively. The tekerleme tradition raises questions about mystical paradox, secret languages in dervish communities, and the relationship between apparent nonsense and mystical truth.

**Prophet-love**: Yūnus is an ardent lover of the Prophet Muḥammad, and Schimmel emphasizes this against later Turkish nationalist interpretations that tried to detach him from mainstream Islamic piety: "when modern Turkish interpreters of his poetry tend to regard him as a representative of unorthodox 'Turkish national piety,' they forget how deeply rooted in the Muslim tradition is his mystical approach" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

**Rebellion and submission**: Yūnus wrote one notable poem (Y 353) in which he argues with God about the implausibility of standard eschatological imagery — why is the judgment bridge "only a hair's breadth"? why use scales to weigh sins? — before resigning himself to divine will. This genre of pious irreverence toward eschatological convention was adopted by later Bektashi poets in more extreme forms. See [[bektashi-order]].

## Influence and Legacy

Yūnus Emre's influence on Turkish literary and religious culture is immense and persistent:

- **Direct imitation**: The *ilāhī* tradition was maintained by poets who explicitly wrote in his voice and style, generating a vast body of Yūnus-like poetry.
- **Other orders**: Poets in the Qadiriyya, Khalwatiyya, and other orders drew on his conventions; Eshref-oğlu Rūmī (15th c.) and Niyāzī Miṣrī (17th c.) are notable heirs.
- **Bektashi tradition**: The Bektashis adopted and extended his antinomian strain — his pious irreverence toward eschatological convention became a template for more radical challenges.
- **Modern reception**: His image was claimed by Turkish nationalists (as a "folk poet" independent of Arabic-Persian tradition) and by secularists, both of which Schimmel regards as reductive. In 1973, the Turkish composer Adnan Saygun set his poems in the *Yunus Emre Oratoryusu*, blending traditional dervish melodies with modern orchestration.
- **Contemporary continuation**: Schimmel records a "New Yūnus Emre" — Ismail Emre (d. 1973), a blacksmith from Adana who produced mystical songs in Yūnus's tradition in states of trance, demonstrating the living continuity of the *ilhām* (inspiration) model ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Relationship to the Broader Tradition

Yūnus stands at the intersection of several traditions:
- **Aḥmad Yasawī** (d. 1166): predecessor in Turkic mystical poetry in Central Asia, whose *Ḥikam* provided the model of wisdom in the vernacular Turkic language
- **[[rumi|Rūmī]]**: near-contemporary, possibly known personally; shares imagery (falcon, soul-bird, reed flute analogue in the waterwheel), meter preferences, and the use of simple forms approximating folk song
- **Classical Persian Sufi poetry**: fully absorbed — Yūnus's symbolism, doctrines, and formal conventions come from the Persian tradition, which he then naturalizes into Turkish

See [[persian-sufi-poetry]], [[sufi-symbolism]], [[sufism]].

## Historiography

Yūnus Emre was a relatively neglected figure in Western scholarship on Sufism until the mid-twentieth century. The key Turkish scholarly editions are Gölpınarlı's (1943, revised 1965). Schimmel's own article "Yunus Emre" (*Numen*, 1961) and John R. Walsh's "Yunus Emre: A Medieval Hymnodist" (*Numen*, 1960) are the foundational Western scholarly treatments. The textual-authentication problem has not been solved and probably cannot be, given the oral/communal character of the ilāhī tradition.
