# Constantine the Great

**Flavius Valerius Constantinus** (c. 272–337, r. 306–337; sole ruler from 324) was the first
**Christian Roman emperor**, the founder of **Constantinople**, and — with [[diocletian|Diocletian]] —
the architect of the **"new empire"** of late antiquity. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]]
Ch. 4 (**Averil Cameron**).

## Rise to sole power

Son of the Caesar **Constantius Chlorus**, Constantine was hailed Augustus by his father's troops at
**York (25 July 306)** but cautiously accepted only the rank of Caesar amid the collapse of the
tetrarchic succession. He defeated **Maxentius** at the **[[battle-of-the-milvian-bridge-312|Battle
of the Milvian Bridge]]** (Oct. 312) — the battle later framed by his **vision of the cross** ("by
this conquer") — then allied with **Licinius** (the **[[edict-of-milan-313|"Edict of Milan,"]]** 313,
granting toleration). He turned on Licinius, defeating him at Cibalae (316) and finally at Adrianople
and **Chrysopolis (324)** to become **sole emperor**.

## The first Christian emperor

Cameron's chapter stresses how heavily the **tendentious sources** (Eusebius' *Vita Constantini*,
Lactantius, the panegyrics) shape — and distort — the record. From his victory of 312 Constantine
**never deviated** in his support of the church:

- He granted clergy privileges and funded church-building (the **Lateran**, St Peter's, and after
  324 the **Holy Sepulchre** in Jerusalem, where his mother **Helena** made her famous pilgrimage).
- He plunged into church politics — the **Donatist** schism in Africa (which defeated him), then the
  **Arian** controversy, summoning and presiding over the **[[council-of-nicaea-325|Council of Nicaea]]**
  (325), where the term *homoousios* was (reportedly) his own contribution.
- He styled himself **"the bishop of those outside,"** preached to his court, and was **baptized only
  on his deathbed** (337) by the pro-Arian Eusebius of Nicomedia.

Cameron cautions against both Eusebius' hagiography and the hostile pagan tradition (Zosimus): the
discrepancies in Constantine's legislation reflect the **practicalities of ruling a still
overwhelmingly pagan empire**, not a programme of "religious toleration." His Christian sincerity is
not in serious doubt, but his reign did **not** bring the abrupt revolution later ascribed to it.

## Constantinople and the "new empire"

In 330 Constantine dedicated **[[founding-of-constantinople-330|Constantinople]]** ("New Rome") on the
site of Byzantium — in essence a **tetrarchic capital** (palace, hippodrome, senate, forum), only
gradually Christianized. In administration, army, and finance he largely **continued and consolidated
Diocletian's reforms** (the provincial/diocesan structure, the civil–military split, the field army),
adding the gold **solidus** (a coin that endured for centuries) and opening the senatorial order
empire-wide. The result was the stable "new empire" (T. D. Barnes) that lasted to Justinian.

## Significance

Constantine is the hinge of late antiquity. Politically he completed the Diocletianic refounding and
secured a dynasty; religiously, his **promotion and personal adoption of Christianity** — and the
precedents he set of **imperial intervention in doctrine** (Nicaea, the exiling of bishops) — had
"even greater repercussions in future centuries," steering the Roman world toward its Christian and
Byzantine future. His late settlement of the succession failed, and his death (337) was followed by
his sons' bloody elimination of rivals.

## Freeman, *Egypt, Greece, and Rome* (2014)

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]]) reads Constantine's **arch in
Rome** (dedicated shortly after 312) as legitimation-by-quotation: it reuses spolia
specifically from "good" emperors — Trajan, Hadrian, Marcus Aurelius — to associate
Constantine with them, while contrasting the informal reused reliefs (emperor among
petitioners) with the new Constantinian panels (emperor enthroned, frontal, petitioners
shown half-size) as visual evidence of an "emperor as demigod" ideology. Freeman notes
Constantine's religious ambiguity extended well past 312 — he favored the **Sol
Invictus** cult on coinage as late as 321 — and quotes both the **Edict of Milan** (313,
with Licinius): "no one whatsoever should be denied freedom to devote himself either to
the cult of the Christians or to such religion as he deems best suited to himself," and
Constantine's own self-conception before his bishops: "You are bishops of those within
the church, but I am perhaps a bishop appointed by God over those outside." On the
**Donatist controversy** (North Africa, from 313), Freeman notes Constantine
adjudicated for the compromising church over the rigorist Donatists, persecuted the
Donatists until relenting in 321, and that the Donatist church survived as a vigorous
separate body until destroyed by fellow Christians in the early 5th century (citing
Brent Shaw, *Sacred Violence*). On **Constantinople** (formalized 330): the new capital
was modeled on Rome (seven hills, 14 districts, senate, a free grain dole from Egypt,
a Hippodrome modeled on the Circus Maximus) but Freeman stresses Constantine gave
**low priority to church-building** there — only the Church of the Holy Apostles (his
own mausoleum) was complete by his death, with the first churches dedicated to
"Wisdom" (Hagia Sophia) and "Peace" (Eirene), terminology acceptable to both pagans and
Christians; the city became genuinely Christian ("Second Jerusalem") only in the 5th
century.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

- **Conversion as defining moment (R1-013):** Constantine’s conversion (312) and the Edict of Milan (313) transformed Roman religious culture; Christianity prospered as a tolerated church and by end of century a majority professed it; in 392 it effectively became the official religion of the Roman state. Motives can be debated; the effect was clearly a defining moment in church history.
  - Quote: "While Constantine's motives can be debated, the effect of his policy transformed the religious culture of the Roman world and was clearly a defining moment in the history of the church."
  - Link labels: produced official Christian Roman Empire; contributed_to [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire]].
- **Scale at conversion (R1-011, cross-ref):** By 312 Logan estimates perhaps six or seven million Christians within the empire—about one-tenth of the population, denser in the East—so the “conversion of the empire” began from a substantial minority, not a tiny sect.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]]’s Constantine is a historiographical hinge of the [[concepts/gibbonian-decline-thesis|Gibbonian decline thesis]] — first Christian emperor and reunifier, then degenerating prince. Claims below are **Gibbon’s views**, not wiki fact.

- **Six simultaneous Augusti** after Galerius elevated Licinius and Maximin claimed the purple: West Constantine/Maxentius (+ Maximian); East Licinius/Maximin (+ Galerius) — “For the first, and indeed for the last time, the Roman world was administered by six emperors.”
- **Reunion after Licinius:** Chrysopolis and Licinius’s fall reunited the empire “thirty-seven years after Diocletian had divided his power and provinces with his associate Maximian.”
- **Consequences of his “revolution”:** “The foundation of Constantinople, and the establishment of the Christian religion, were the immediate and memorable consequences of this revolution” ([[events/founding-of-constantinople-330]], Christian establishment).
- **First public Christian emperor** (in Gibbon’s formula): from accession protector of the church; “the first emperor who publicly professed and established the Christian religion” — yet **baptized only on his deathbed** by the Arian bishop of Nicomedia.
- **Character as satire vs panegyric:** “the character of Constantine is considered, even in the present age, as an object either of satire or of panegyric”; a just portrait requires separating periods of the reign. Early career heroic (Tiber / Hadrianople); later “degenerating into a cruel and dissolute monarch, corrupted by his fortune,” with Asiatic pomp and “softness and effeminacy.”
- **Crispus (and possibly Fausta):** murder of an innocent son at Pola; Gibbon’s formula that he “atoned for the murder of an innocent son, by the execution, perhaps, of a guilty wife”; popular verses comparing his “splendid and bloody” reign to Nero’s.
- **Attributed irony on conversion and virtue:** “As he gradually advanced in the knowledge of truth, he proportionally declined in the practice of virtue”; delayed baptism, Gibbon argues, encouraged future tyrants to believe long reigns of blood “would instantly be washed away in the waters of regeneration.”
- **Helena:** innkeeper’s daughter (not British royalty); Gibbon defends the legality of her marriage; Constantine probably born at Naissus.
- **Lawgiving:** severe and even cruel in enacting laws, remiss in executing them — a symptom of weakness in prince or constitution.

Source: [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]]

## Woolf, Rome: An Empire's Story (2012)

From [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]] Chs. XV–XVI.

- **Soldier-manager pair with Diocletian.** Balkan-origin emperor; sole only after defeating Licinius (325); founded Constantinople; long foreign wars (Franks, Danube). Historiography: Persecutor vs Convert—but Woolf stresses both used religious policy for imperial unity; “difficult to identify any area of policy where Constantine’s commitment to Christ did not also serve his vision for the empire.” Also reformed military/civilian command, taxes, coinage.
- **Active patronage from ~312.** Great basilicas at Rome (Lateran, St Peter’s) by mid-320s—first monumental Christian worship places. Legal privileges and access for bishops; personal attention to Donatist petitions; neutrality not an option once unity/orthodoxy impressed advisers.
- **Nicaea and incomplete unity.** Council of Nicaea (Woolf dates 324): common creed/Easter claimed; Arianism remained central fourth-century division; successors and barbarian missions spread Arian ideas (Goths, Vandals). On death (337) Church position unassailable; all later emperors except Julian Christian—yet Christian empire came only by stages and was more divided than what preceded it.
- **Limits, not multi-faith secularism.** Edict of Milan toleration for all practice; Jewish “clergy” some privileges but severe penalties for converting Christians, owning Christian slaves, circumcising them. Banned blood sacrifice, oracles, statues to old gods—pragmatism of a soldier-emperor from civil wars, “no great vision of a multi-faith imperial society.”
- **Faustian pact.** “Constantine had made a Faustian pact with Christ”—bishops’ rhetorical power potentially enormous, but schism/heresy a structural cost.
- **Quotes:** “Much more remarkable was that around 312, Constantine began to actively patronize the Christians.” / “Constantine had made a Faustian pact with Christ.”

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Attributed claims from [[sources/mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015|Stephen Mitchell, *A History of the Later Roman Empire* (2015)]].

- **Eusebius’s *Life* — hybrid; problem is omission not lies.** The most important single source for the first Christian emperor is “an extraordinary collage of panegyric, documentary history, and hagiography.” “The major problem lies not with the veracity of what Eusebius says, but in appreciating the context of his claims and gaining a good perspective on how much he omits.”
- **Claudius Gothicus ancestry as flimsy dynastic propaganda.** The 310 panegyric first advances the claim that Constantine was grandson of Claudius Gothicus “to secure Constantine’s position as the dominant emperor” once tetrarchic non-hereditary succession was dead.
- **Milvian Bridge conversion: solar and Christian convergence.** On the eve of battle Constantine interpreted a solar apparition as Christian promise of victory; the cross (we are told) went on soldiers’ shields before the outnumbered victory (28 Oct 312). Mitchell follows Weiss: same phenomenon as the 310 Gallic panegyric’s Apollo/Victory solar vision (double solar halo) reinterpreted from Apollo to Christ by 312. “At the decisive moment of his imperial career, Constantine’s belief in a supreme solar deity and belief in the Christian God were virtually convergent.” “It is right to hail the vision as a decisive moment in late Roman history… the coincidence of the two, and above all the religious and psychological response that they triggered in Constantine transformed a remarkable event into an epochal one.” Public formulas stayed ambiguous (*mens divina*; *instinctu divinitatis*) while personal letters speak of conversion from darkness. From 312 he was Christian “in the full sense,” yet also accepted presenting the Christian God as *summus deus* without full theological baggage.
- **Palladas corroborates a strong Christian Constantinian agenda.** Against critics who dismiss Eusebius as propaganda, Mitchell highlights Kevin Wilkinson’s work on Palladas epigrams (c.325–335, Constantinople): a pagan Greek intellectual reacting to a Christian agenda after Licinius’s defeat — so “the Eusebian view of Constantine was also shared by intellectual pagan contemporaries.” (Mitchell’s text once prints “Williamson”; notes cite K. W. Wilkinson.)
- **Limits of anti-pagan policy.** Constantine closed selected offensive sanctuaries (Aphaca, Heliopolis sacred prostitution; Asclepius at Aegae; altars at Mamre) but cult often continued. Alleged complete ban on sacrifice (Eusebius VC 2.45; CTh 16.10.2 of 341) is disputed; Mitchell finds Barnes et al. place too much weight on a flimsy foundation — “If Constantine did propose such a sweeping measure, it was rapidly rescinded” for the 324 conciliatory letter; ban likely targeted magic/divination. He retained *pontifex maximus*; 337 Hispellum rescript allowed Flavian imperial-cult temple without blood sacrifice.
- **Institutional and economic acts.** Immediate post-conversion program: Ossius toward Arius–Alexander resolution; [[council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea]] June 325; decision to found [[constantinople|Constantinople]]; letters restoring Christian status/wealth and funding reconstruction. Solidus gold standard enabling later fiscal-social transformation; provision for state *annona* to feed 80,000 inhabitants with Alexandrian grain; Roman church foundations (Lateran on Maxentius’s cavalry barracks).
