# Constantine the Great

**Flavius Valerius Constantinus** (c. 272–337, r. 306–337; sole ruler from 324) was the first
**Christian Roman emperor**, the founder of **Constantinople**, and — with [[diocletian|Diocletian]] —
the architect of the **"new empire"** of late antiquity. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]]
Ch. 4 (**Averil Cameron**).

## Rise to sole power

Son of the Caesar **Constantius Chlorus**, Constantine was hailed Augustus by his father's troops at
**York (25 July 306)** but cautiously accepted only the rank of Caesar amid the collapse of the
tetrarchic succession. He defeated **Maxentius** at the **[[battle-of-the-milvian-bridge-312|Battle
of the Milvian Bridge]]** (Oct. 312) — the battle later framed by his **vision of the cross** ("by
this conquer") — then allied with **Licinius** (the **[[edict-of-milan-313|"Edict of Milan,"]]** 313,
granting toleration). He turned on Licinius, defeating him at Cibalae (316) and finally at Adrianople
and **Chrysopolis (324)** to become **sole emperor**.

## The first Christian emperor

Cameron's chapter stresses how heavily the **tendentious sources** (Eusebius' *Vita Constantini*,
Lactantius, the panegyrics) shape — and distort — the record. From his victory of 312 Constantine
**never deviated** in his support of the church:

- He granted clergy privileges and funded church-building (the **Lateran**, St Peter's, and after
  324 the **Holy Sepulchre** in Jerusalem, where his mother **Helena** made her famous pilgrimage).
- He plunged into church politics — the **Donatist** schism in Africa (which defeated him), then the
  **Arian** controversy, summoning and presiding over the **[[council-of-nicaea-325|Council of Nicaea]]**
  (325), where the term *homoousios* was (reportedly) his own contribution.
- He styled himself **"the bishop of those outside,"** preached to his court, and was **baptized only
  on his deathbed** (337) by the pro-Arian Eusebius of Nicomedia.

Cameron cautions against both Eusebius' hagiography and the hostile pagan tradition (Zosimus): the
discrepancies in Constantine's legislation reflect the **practicalities of ruling a still
overwhelmingly pagan empire**, not a programme of "religious toleration." His Christian sincerity is
not in serious doubt, but his reign did **not** bring the abrupt revolution later ascribed to it.

## Constantinople and the "new empire"

In 330 Constantine dedicated **[[founding-of-constantinople-330|Constantinople]]** ("New Rome") on the
site of Byzantium — in essence a **tetrarchic capital** (palace, hippodrome, senate, forum), only
gradually Christianized. In administration, army, and finance he largely **continued and consolidated
Diocletian's reforms** (the provincial/diocesan structure, the civil–military split, the field army),
adding the gold **solidus** (a coin that endured for centuries) and opening the senatorial order
empire-wide. The result was the stable "new empire" (T. D. Barnes) that lasted to Justinian.

## Significance

Constantine is the hinge of late antiquity. Politically he completed the Diocletianic refounding and
secured a dynasty; religiously, his **promotion and personal adoption of Christianity** — and the
precedents he set of **imperial intervention in doctrine** (Nicaea, the exiling of bishops) — had
"even greater repercussions in future centuries," steering the Roman world toward its Christian and
Byzantine future. His late settlement of the succession failed, and his death (337) was followed by
his sons' bloody elimination of rivals.
