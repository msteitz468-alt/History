# Wanli Emperor (Zhu Yijun, r. 1572–1620)

Long-reigning Ming emperor (the fourteenth ruler of the dynasty) whose reign of nearly forty-nine years encompassed the height of late-Ming institutional tensions. By 1587 (the fifteenth year of his reign, Year of the Pig), at approximately twenty-four years of age, he had already ruled for fifteen years and was exhibiting deepening disengagement from routine governance, a development central to Ray Huang's analysis of Ming structural paralysis.

## Early Life, Capping, and Accession

Born in 1563. Before his ninth year he underwent the ritual of capping, which "prematurely ushered in his manhood." The ceremony took place in a screened cubicle in the courtyard: he emerged three times in new garments and different hats, kneeling, standing, turning, holding a scepter, and drinking wine to music and chanting. The following day he received formal congratulations from the court officials while sitting stiffly.

Several months later his father, the Lung-ch'ing emperor, died. As heir designate, Wan-li met the imperial court in mourning. He was coached to decline the throne twice on grounds of grief before assenting on the third round. "From that moment on ceremonies became inseparable from his life."

He had been on the throne almost fifteen years by March 1587. Even as a child emperor he impressed courtiers with his "majestic countenance" and "deep" voice whose "pronouncements [were] clear and loud, ending in powerful tones which 'seemingly came from his diaphragm.'"

## Education, Discipline, and Relationship with Zhang Juzheng and Feng Pao

Wan-li's schooling after autumn 1572 consisted of three daily periods on Confucian classics, calligraphy, and history. Between periods, memorials arrived with rescripts drafted by the grand-secretaries; the boy emperor applied the vermilion brush, assisted by eunuchs under Feng Pao while civil officials including Tutor Chang stayed away unless summoned.

**[[zhang-juzheng|Zhang Juzheng]]** (Tutor Chang), with "wide-set eyebrows and long beard," was "always a source of authority and wisdom. Always well-groomed, he seemed to wear a new robe every day." His mind "was just as sharp and meticulous." The emperor and dowagers were grateful: after Lung-ch'ing's death, Zhang advised on the swift dismissal of the overbearing first grand-secretary Kao Kung. A yellow paper signed by the two empresses dowager and the emperor stripped Kao of rank and ordered his immediate return home under lifelong surveillance. Zhang then succeeded as first grand-secretary and took charge of the emperor's education.

Big Companion Feng Pao, Director of Ceremonies and head of the palace staff, had long cared for the young prince (who had ridden on his shoulders). Tz'u-sheng used Feng as her contact; a bad report could force even the emperor to kneel for long periods. Under this combined discipline Wan-li progressed: he learned that the ruler's primary duties were "to venerate heaven and to follow the precedents established by his ancestors."

A supernova ("Anno 1572") appeared shortly after accession; on Zhang's advice the boy examined himself for bad thoughts, speech, and conduct. Calligraphy (shared hobby with Tz'u-sheng and Feng) was later dropped from the curriculum: "Calligraphy... was after all a minor art which in itself added nothing to the empire's well-being." Chang cited historical examples of artistically accomplished but disastrous rulers (Ch'eng-ti of Han, Hui-tsung of Sung). Lanterns and fireworks after New Year were discontinued on Zhang's suggestion; redecoration orders were rescinded.

The emperor was instructed to put "good men in office and get rid of evil ones" and to follow Tutor Chang's advice because "he always knew best."

## Palace Life, Rituals, and the Weight of Ceremony

The Forbidden City covered a quarter of a square mile of glazed-tiled buildings; the surrounding Imperial City added more than three square miles. The palace was self-sufficient with supply depots, bakery, armory, printing office, and stables, all largely under twenty-four eunuch departments. In the early Wan-li years there were close to 20,000 eunuchs and 3,000 palace women. Official salaries were low (a rank 2a minister nominally 152 ounces of silver yearly; a 6a secretary only 35), necessitating customary gifts and perquisites.

Ceremonial burdens were constant. The emperor sacrificed to heaven and earth, performed ritual farming, celebrated New Year and the fifth-month festival, sacrificed at mausoleums and the family temple, observed birthdays and death dates of predecessors, received tributary missions, reviewed troops, and accepted war prisoners.

War-prisoner disposal was especially awe-inspiring: the sovereign sat on the Meridian Gate tower flanked by noble-titled generals and a battalion of gigantic imperial guards in shiny armor with red-tasseled helmets. Prisoners in chains and red cloth knelt below while the Minister of Justice read charges; the emperor's order "Take them there; be it so ordered" was repeated in expanding echoes until the entire battalion shouted it.

Daily morning audience (much simplified) required capital civil officials and army officers to assemble before dawn. Civil officials faced west, military east. Censors noted coughs, spits, stumbles. After bows and kowtows, reports were made; proceedings were to finish before dawn. Noon and evening audiences had been abandoned earlier in the dynasty. The emperor wore heavy formal regalia: a rectangular black mortarboard with twelve strings of beads dangling before the eyes and behind the neck, black jacket, yellow skirt, jade belt, red socks and boots. Less formal dragon robes were yellow or black with green borders.

Every spring the emperor performed ritual farming before the Altar of Earth. Two hundred farmers were assembled; actors played wind/cloud/thunder/rain deities. The emperor held a whip and a dragon-carved ceremonial plow but touched neither tools nor ox; he marched across the field three times flanked by elders, then retired to watch courtiers repeat the process under the Minister of Revenue. Seeding was done by the prefect of Shun-t'ien.

## The 1577 Mourning Crisis and Zhang Juzheng's Retention

In autumn 1577 Zhang's father died in Hukwang. Standard procedure required twenty-seven months of mourning and relinquishment of office. The emperor, after consulting his mothers, commanded Zhang to stay; state affairs and the emperor's own education (he had just turned fifteen) still required supervision. Zhang submitted three requests; the emperor repeated the order, finally invoking the empress dowager's wish. The correspondence was published.

Han-lin academicians, seeing the dynasty's moral character at stake, appealed in person and then remonstrated. Two initial mild memorials argued that grieving Zhang could not manage affairs wisely. Stronger papers from Ministry of Justice juniors called his retention "brazenly shameless" and demanded a stern order for immediate departure and repentance at the burial site.

Imperial rescripts ordered severe punishments. The first two memorialists received sixty strokes with whipping clubs before the Meridian Gate and removal from the Civil Service register. The other two received eighty strokes and life exile to the frontier. "The soldiers at the Silk Robe Guard... carried out [the grand-secretary's wrath] in full force. Thus the first dozen strokes had already ripped the skin... One of the victims lost consciousness; it was a miracle that he survived. Another survivor... lost one buttock." Families took the injured home; comforters were later questioned by the Eastern Depot.

Zhang remained, wearing a cloth robe and ox-horn-lined belt, salary suspended, but supplied with rice, tea, oil, firewood, and daily delicacies from the Court of Imperial Entertainments. Dozens of courtiers resigned in protest. Handbills denouncing him as traitor appeared but were destroyed by secret police.

Later, on vacation to his native Chiang-ling (mid-April to mid-July 1578), Zhang traveled in a specially designed large sedan chair dividable into bed-chamber and reception room, hauled by thirty-two bearers, with musketeer bodyguards furnished by General [[qi-jiguang|Ch'i Chi-kuang]]. He was greeted by imperial princes breaking tradition and visited his old adversary Kao Kung.

## The False Noon Audience of March 2, 1587

On an ordinary working day in Peking, trees leafless, ground frozen after snow, "hundreds of governmental functionaries, civil officials as well as army officers on duty with the capital garrison, raced toward the imperial palace" at the rumor of a noon audience. Guards at the Gate of Greater Brilliance admitted the silk-robed crowds. Inside the Imperial City they found the courtyard "too quiet," no imperial furniture displayed, no bells or drums, no extra marshals.

Inquiry of palace eunuchs confirmed the emperor had never ordered the audience. The crowd dispersed, "relieved yet still excited," spending the afternoon discussing their credulity. The emperor took "serious action": the Ministry of Rites and Court of State Ceremonial forfeited two months' salaries for failing to clarify and stop the stampede. An investigation proved inconclusive (everyone had started running simultaneously). The emperor then extended the salary suspension "to all governmental functionaries in the capital."

This group penalty was unprecedented; prior suspensions had been individual. "Because emoluments for the bureaucracy authorized by the dynasty were fixed at very low levels, many officials, especially those in the higher echelons, did not live on their regular pay." Lower ranks depended on the small salaries. The punishment caused real distress. "With all its pomp and grandeur, the imperial court was supposed to preserve its impeccable and meticulous manners as an inspiration to the nation. It was absolutely unbecoming for the entire court to run chaotically toward a wrong destination at a wrong time."

"Really, nothing of great significance happened in 1587, the Year of the Pig. China was not facing a foreign invasion, nor was the country engulfed in a civil war. ... On the whole, the Year of the Pig would go down in history as an indifferent one. Can we therefore omit that year from history books? Not quite."

## Personal Life, Consorts, and the Rise of Lady Cheng

The emperor had little private life. Even moving inside the palace he was accompanied by a large retinue with eunuchs clearing the path with whips. He changed clothes frequently for different degrees of formality.

His principal wife (Empress Hsiao-tuan), installed at the 1578 wedding when he was fourteen and she barely thirteen, "from her wedding day, [was] permanently encased in palatial pomp and comfort" yet "her marriage was only a state necessity, reducing her to an accessory to an institution." She bore a daughter and lived long but "at no time did she in any way affect the course of his life."

In winter 1581 Lady Wang (a chambermaid of Tz'u-sheng) captured his fancy and became pregnant. In July 1582 she received the title Consort Kung and in late August gave birth to the first son, Ch'ang-lo ("the eldest imperial son"). A general amnesty and tax remission were proclaimed; envoys went to Korea. Among nine concubines chosen in 1582 was fourteen-year-old Lady Cheng.

Wan-li's relationship with Lady Cheng, promoted to Consort Te in summer 1583 and to imperial consort two-and-a-half years later (above all secondary wives, inferior only to the empress), "was later said to have lasted throughout the rest of their lives and became a major cause of the 'constitutional crisis'." She was "intelligent, well-read, and strong-willed." "While other ladies distanced themselves from the emperor out of submission and fear, she teased him, laughed at him, and joked with him." Eunuchs reported they visited temples and villas together; she would shout at his indecisiveness, "You are an old lady!"

In 1580, at seventeen, after organizing drinking parties advised by eunuch Sun Hai, he sentenced two palace women to death (symbolically, by hair-tassel cutting) for failing to sing unfamiliar songs; interveners were beaten. Tz'u-sheng nearly dethroned him in favor of Prince Lu; he knelt over an hour in repentance and pledged reform in writing to Zhang, who discharged favorite eunuchs and assigned Han-lin academicians to keep him company daily from early 1581.

## The Death of Zhang Juzheng, the Purge, and Growing Disillusionment

Zhang died in 1582 at fifty-seven, shortly after receiving the unprecedented title of Grand Preceptor (last conferred on a living person over two centuries earlier). Immediately the pendulum swung. The national land survey he had ordered was declared a failure for coercing over-reporting of acreage. Officials associated with him were impeached; accusations escalated to bribery, sale of offices, and worse.

The emperor realized "his trust had been misplaced." "Just to think that during all the years when he himself, the emperor, had felt he could not afford the necessary gifts for the palace women... while Chang Chü-cheng, the saintly preacher, had all along been taking advantage of him and reaping all the benefits!" Feng Pao was stripped, banished to the dynastic founder's mausoleum in Nanking, and his property confiscated on charges of "deceiving the emperor and preying upon the nation."

Zhang's property confiscation (May 1584, after hesitation) was triggered by an imperial princess's petition. Assets assessed high; the eldest son, beaten, confessed hidden wealth then hanged himself. Household goods arrived on 110 trays, including four sheets of the emperor's own calligraphy given years earlier. In September 1584 a proclamation charged Zhang with abusing power, perverting government, silencing opinion, and gross disloyalty; his offenses were posted empire-wide. One brother and two sons were exiled perpetually. His title of Grand Preceptor was posthumously eliminated.

Kao Kung's published *Last Words on the Sick Bed* (timed for the moment) alleged that Feng and Zhang had framed an assassination attempt using Wang Ta-ch'en (a former servant seized at the gate in 1573 with concealed daggers) to permanently silence Kao. The emperor ordered then withdrew an inquiry on Shen Shih-hsing's advice; principal witnesses were dead. "Never again could the Wan-li emperor think of his deceased tutor with even mixed respect and affection."

By 1587 the emperor was "either tired or bored or both." He had chosen for the last palace examination a theme on the Taoist doctrine that good government could be maintained "while the ruler did nothing." Audiences and study sessions were suspended twice in the preceding twelve months (early autumn 1586 and spring 1587) citing blackout on rising and fire-element imbalance causing foot itch and torn skin from scratching. Eunuchs leaked that he had been horseback-riding (possibly thrown and injured his forehead) and stories circulated of excess drinking, late nights, and involvement with palace ladies. A Ministry of Rites secretary remonstrated.

## The "Living Ancestor": Ritual Life, Mausoleum, and Dehumanization of the Monarchy (from Ch. 4)

By the mid-1580s Wan-li was increasingly framed as a "living ancestor" whose primary role was ritual performance and cosmic-ancestral symbolism rather than active governance. Construction of his own mausoleum at Ta-yü-shan (ground broken summer 1584) proceeded with his enthusiastic participation despite his youth. The project, supervised jointly by the first grand-secretary and the symbolic seventh duke of Ting-kuo with army officers for manpower, featured a massive keyhole-shaped complex: ceremonial hall, stele building, and a 700-foot-diameter earth mound over a cross-shaped underground "Mysterious Palace." The burial chamber (main axis ~250 ft, side corridors 120 ft) used granite from Fang-shan/Honan and canal-transported Shantung bricks; three marble dragon chairs and a platform for the coffins of emperor, empress, and the natural mother of the succeeding emperor (who would also be honored as empress). Furnishings included gold/silver utensils and porcelain but also toy-scale wooden horses and servants; a simulated well had no water pit; lamps could not burn once sealed. Work conditions were harsh: in 1587 a state paper noted old people and children "wailing from hunger and cold" on site. Shen Shih-hsing's congratulatory notes emphasized "Eternal comfort for their spirit" and "Myriad years of good life." The arrangement made it harder for the emperor to ignore the succession question, as space in the tomb was reserved for the natural mother of the heir.

In May 1585, after drought, Wan-li personally led a four-mile foot procession (no horses/sedan chairs; ~4,000 officials and officers in simple blue robes with ox-horn belts) to the Altar of Heaven (built 1530 by his grandfather). He performed kowtow and offerings, then lectured ministerial officials that the drought reflected his lack of virtue "but in part it was also caused by the corrupt and ruthless functionaries of the government." Shen replied that the emperor's sincerity must have touched Heaven. Rain (with hail) arrived 12 June; the emperor claimed credit and ordered thanksgiving. The event produced a tax remission and stern decree against official cruelty. It was one of the last occasions the emperor derived satisfaction from ritual action before withdrawal.

Wan-li's brief interest in horses, soldiers, and palace guard drills (guard doubled in size; cavalry trotting streets pre-dawn; 1584 archery contest lasting until evening with eunuchs fainting from heat) was quietly halted by Shen Shih-hsing. Shen approached eunuch commanders with stories of past intrigues and the fate of Chiang Pin (Cheng-te's adviser, later tortured to death); "They all shuddered in horror." The emperor, "a sensible person," allowed the force to "fade into the background" and be forgotten by 1587. Shen later regretted that the succession issue had not been managed with the same indirect method.

## The Eastern Depot Crisis and Permanent Withdrawal (1587, Ch. 4)

Jurisdictional friction in 1587 over a Peking magistrate's beating of Court of Imperial Sacrifices performers led to the emperor viewing civil obstruction of Silk Robe Guard agents as contempt. Shen mediated a face-saving compromise (apology and fine). Civil officials soon forced dismissal of Eastern Depot director Chang Ching. A junior remonstrator's accusation that the emperor must have been bribed received sixty strokes. The episode "plunged the young sovereign into a deeply downcast mood for a long time" and "marked a point of no return." "Already tired of his morning audiences and study sessions, the Wan-li emperor was henceforth to suspend them permanently." He drew closer to Lady Cheng and grew cynical about "being the figurehead of a dishonest organization in which everyone said one thing and meant something else."

## Institutional Position and Limits of Ming Monarchy

Wan-li had "become a public personality" with "little private life." Ming constitution prohibited regency even for a minor: all imperial princes except the heir apparent were "permanently removed from the capital upon reaching maturity" to territorial titles and stipends but barred from politics or travel without explicit approval. Consorts were to come from humble families (Tz'u-sheng's father Li Wei had been a commoner; he later profited as intermediary on substandard tax cloth until reprimanded).

There had been only three prime ministers in the dynasty; all executed by the founder. Grand-secretaries were originally literary stylists for imperial declarations. Their influence grew; under Zhang the senior became de facto chief, with others instructed to serve under him. Power "could not be delegated and in theory never had been," yet for a boy emperor the mechanics relied on Zhang drafting and Feng's eunuch squad briefing and assisting with the vermilion brush.

Eunuchs, after self-castration, were not mere domestics: many were Inner Palace School graduates with classical education comparable to bureaucrats, serving as confidential secretaries examining lengthy memorials. The Director of Ceremonies and half-dozen top eunuchs advised which papers to read fully or scan, then the emperor approved (or rarely altered) the grand-secretaries' drafts. Their work was "anonymous; they would forever be unsung heroes within the governmental structure."

"Both the sovereign and his bureaucrats realized that to fulfill their functions they had to do well in two areas: personnel management and ceremonial procedure. ... There was a limit to the number of practical problems that could be perceived, understood, analyzed, and discharged by our literary bureaucracy that governed many millions of peasants. So discipline and decorum had to be emphasized."

Cheng-te (Wan-li's granduncle, r. early 16th c.) had asserted personal independence by separating himself as individual from the monarchy-as-institution (military campaigns, tours, disregard for kowtows and Four Books ritual), but his rebellion produced only negative results; it could not be institutionalized and ended with posthumous "martial emperor" title carrying derogatory inferences. Wan-li, having studied the records and lacking Cheng-te's physical courage and childless freedom, yielded repeatedly yet nursed grievances, using inaction as his most effective weapon. By 1587 or thereabouts the turning point was reached; the Year of the Pig, "though seemingly insignificant, may, over the long course of history, have some significance after all."

## Governance and Withdrawal by 1587 (from Ch. 1 and early Ch. 2)

By the Year of the Pig the emperor was "either tired or bored or both." He had suspended morning audiences and study sessions twice in the preceding twelve months (early autumn and again in early spring), citing blackout upon rising and "overabundance of the fire element" causing foot itch that tore the skin. Eunuchs leaked that he had been horseback-riding at the palace stable (possibly thrown, injuring his forehead, which he hid from courtiers). Stories of drinking to excess, staying out late, and involvement with palace ladies circulated. A secretary from the Ministry of Rites remonstrated against negligence of duty and failure to care for himself as Son of Heaven.

The first grand-secretary [[shen-shixing]] (age 52) bore the moral burden of maintaining ritual order. He pleaded with the sovereign for the sake of the Imperial Shrine to take care of himself, citing the Hung-wu emperor who had continued public study sessions nearly to age seventy. Shen diagnosed the empire's ailment as "a cleavage between the top and the bottom" ("the separation of the interior from the exterior") and warned that no state under such conditions "has... remained in peace and order for long." He placed his hopes in "sincerity"—a mental assertion and mobilization of will-power drawn from the opening of the Four Books—to win the emperor's heart and keep the bureaucracy functioning through shared belief in ritual and moral tone (yang over yin).

Shen understood that the bureaucracy's most pressing problem was managing itself. The Civil Service (c. 20,000 members; ~2,000 in the capital) was the only politically significant element; the military was subordinate. Capital officials assembled in embroidered robes and mandarin squares (cranes for rank 1, quail for rank 9). Yet differences between theory (compassion, propriety from the Four Books and Chu Hsi) and practice (personal networks, pecuniary temptations, yin motivations) were constant. Shen's realistic goal: "merely to provide something for the virtuous men to rely upon and the evil-minded to be afraid of."

The emperor had truly come of age after Zhang's death and the birth of his first son. He was no longer keeping company with rowdy eunuchs. Palace women (selected in groups of up to 300 from the Peking region, ages 9–14, with slim chance of favor) lived forlorn lives unless elevated; their remains were cremated unmarked. Lady Cheng had filled an emotional void the emperor's mother (who "determined to run him like an institution") could not.

## Significance within the Ming System (supported by assigned range)

"Both the sovereign and his bureaucrats realized that to fulfill their functions they had to do well in two areas: personnel management and ceremonial procedure. Of course, not many practical problems could be solved this way, but it was essential to managing an immense empire from one center. There was a limit to the number of practical problems that could be perceived, understood, analyzed, and discharged by our literary bureaucracy that governed many millions of peasants. So discipline and decorum had to be emphasized."

The 1587 false noon audience and the emperor's recent suspensions of duties were "commonplace occurrences" that nevertheless "reflected the true character of our empire." 1587 was "a year of no significance" in headline terms yet illuminated the structural rigidities.

## Historiography (from range)

Huang presents the Wan-li emperor's story not primarily as personal failing or "laziness" but as the exposure of a system whose design—extreme emphasis on ritual decorum and moral example from the center, low formal emoluments, prohibition on delegating imperial power while requiring an underage ruler to perform constant ceremonies, ambiguous but expanding role of grand-secretaries, and balance with palace eunuchs—could not sustain effective leadership once the throne withdrew. The "commonplace occurrences" of 1587 "often reflected the true character of our empire."

The emperor himself, after Zhang's fall and the revelations, could never again view his early tutor with uncomplicated gratitude. By 1587 the twenty-four-year-old sovereign who had ruled since childhood was asking trivial questions about confiscated property and choosing examination themes on ruler "doing nothing," while the first grand-secretary diagnosed the empire's ailment as "a cleavage between the top and the bottom." The construction of the mausoleum while the living emperor was "prematurely canonized and half-buried" and the rain prayer as make-believe action that "at least elicited a feeling that something could be done" illustrate how creative energies were channeled into ritual even as practical governance atrophied. Cheng-te's earlier attempt at a "free monarchy" showed that personal independence could not be institutionalized without destroying the bureaucratic logic the dynasty required.
