# Thebes

## Overview

**Thebes**, the leading city of **Boeotia**, was for a brief but consequential decade
(371–362 BCE) the leading power of Greece — the only fourth-century state to break
**[[sparta|Spartan]]** military supremacy outright, and the polis whose destruction by
**[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]]** in 335 BCE became the grim exemplar of Macedonian
ruthlessness toward Greek revolt. [[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical
Greece 500–323 BC* (2000)]] treats Thebes as a recurring great-power actor throughout the
fourth-century narrative, structurally distinct from — and, at its height, a genuine rival
to — Sparta and Athens.

## From medizing pariah to Spartan target

Thebes's Archaic-period reputation was compromised by its **"medizing"** — collaboration
with Persia — after the Persian breakthrough at Thermopylae in 480 BCE, a stain that long
made it suspect among other Greek states. In the aftermath of the Peloponnesian War,
Thebes was one of Sparta's chief rivals for control of central Greece; Spartan hostility
culminated in **382 BCE**, when the Spartan commander Phoebidas seized the Theban acropolis
(the **Cadmea**) in a "grotesque breach" of the [[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]] (see
[[corinthian-war-395bce]]). Thebes was **liberated** in **379** by a conspiracy led by
**[[pelopidas]]**, who killed the pro-Spartan junta's leaders and expelled the garrison —
the opening act of the Theban resurgence.

## The Theban hegemony (371–362)

When the renewed Common Peace of 371 required Thebes to leave the Boeotian cities
autonomous, **[[epaminondas]]** refused to swear away the **Boeotian Confederacy**,
provoking the Spartan attack that Thebes annihilated at **[[battle-of-leuctra-371bce|
Leuctra]]** (371) — the single most consequential battle of the Greek fourth century,
which "emasculated Sparta," per Osborne, as thoroughly as any defeat in Greek history.
Thebes followed up by invading **Laconia and Messenia**, permanently **liberating
Messenia** from Spartan control and founding the fortress-city of **Megalopolis** to unite
the small communities of western Arcadia — depriving Sparta of the helot-worked land that
had underwritten its power since the Archaic age.

Thebes could not, however, simply replace Sparta as hegemon. Osborne stresses the
structural limits: Boeotia was agriculturally rich but possessed **neither Athens's
silver nor Sparta's helot economy**; its formidable heavy infantry were part-time farmers
with limited capacity for sustained campaigning; and the annually-rotating **Boeotarch**
magistracy gave no stable institutional base for long-term strategy — Epaminondas and
Pelopidas, despite architecting an "unprecedented Theban predominance," were both, at
different points, prosecuted by domestic political opponents. The hegemony proved to rest
on two men rather than durable institutions: Pelopidas died at Cynoscephalae (364) and
Epaminondas at **[[battle-of-mantinea-362bce|Mantinea]]** (362), after which Thebes
retained real strength but lost the capacity to lead.

## Decline and destruction

Thebes remained a significant regional power through the mid-fourth century, joining
Athens in the doomed coalition that **[[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II]]** crushed at
**[[battle-of-chaeronea-338bce|Chaeronea]]** (338) — where the elite **Sacred Band**,
raised by Pelopidas in the 370s, was annihilated to the last man. Philip dealt with
defeated Thebes far more harshly than with Athens: a Macedonian garrison was installed on
the Cadmea and an exiled oligarchy imposed. When Thebes revolted again in 335, during
Alexander's northern campaigns, **Alexander eliminated the city entirely** — even a city
"as large and recently powerful as Thebes," in Osborne's phrase, could be destroyed
outright as a deterrent to further Greek resistance. Thebes was refounded only in 316 BCE
by Cassander.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Thebes's brief hegemony, the fourth-century contest for Greek supremacy would have
remained a straightforward Sparta–Athens rivalry; Thebes's decade of dominance — and its
demonstration that the Spartan phalanx could be broken by tactical innovation — directly
shaped the military thinking of **Philip of Macedon**, who as a young hostage at Thebes in
these years observed Epaminondas and Pelopidas at close range. The oblique order and deep
striking column pioneered at Leuctra are a direct ancestor of the Macedonian battle line
that would conquer Greece and Persia alike.
