## Role and Overview

Mikael Agricola (c.1510–1557) was Finland's principal reformer and the founder of written Finnish language and literature. He is treated in the Cambridge History of Scandinavia Vol. II as one of only two reformers — alongside Olaus Petri in Sweden — who "had studied in Wittenberg, where they personally experienced Luther's and Melanchthon's evangelical teachings" (L1160–1161). He served as bishop of Turku (Åbo) and made the Finnish liturgy the vehicle by which the royal Reformation became, as the source puts it, "a Finnish reformation" (L4301).

## Key Decisions and Consequences

**Education and Wittenberg formation.** Born around 1510 on the southern coast of Finland, Agricola attended school in Viipuri before arriving in Turku in 1528, where he became Bishop Skytte's secretary. "In 1536 he left for Wittenberg, where he attended Luther's and Melanchthon's lectures. Having received his magister artium there, he returned to Turku, where he became headmaster of the Latin school" (L4268–4271). He was one of the young men profoundly shaped by the evangelical preaching of Pietari Särkilahti in the late 1520s.

**Creation of written Finnish.** "The first work to appear in Finnish was his ABC Book, which included a Finnish translation of Luther's Catechism, published in 1543. The following year his large Biblical Prayer Book, a manual for priests, was printed. His translation of the New Testament was published in 1548, and about a quarter of the Old Testament in 1551–2. He also translated the mass and the manual from Swedish in 1549" (L4293–4297). "Only after the publication of these liturgical books did Finnish religious life take on a definitive shape all over the country" (L4298).

**Theological character.** "Agricola was not a radical reformer, but rather a Melanchthonian traditionalist. In addition to theology, he was interested in history, philosophy, law and medicine" (L4304–4306). The Finnish reformer adopted Luther's concept of authority: "Mikael Agricola, who had studied under Luther at Wittenberg, adopted his teacher's notion of authority" (L4529–4530).

**Episcopal office.** Gustav Vasa divided Finland into two bishoprics and appointed Agricola to the see of Turku; he "did not receive the full title of bishop until 1554" (L4286). He deliberately avoided political disputes — a policy not continued by his successor (L4307–4308).

**Death on diplomatic mission.** "In 1557 he was sent to Moscow as a peace negotiator and on his way back he fell ill and died on the Karelian Isthmus" (L4289–4290).

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Agricola's translations, the Finnish language would have had no written ecclesiastical basis during the critical first generation of Lutheran church formation; oral Finnish religious life would have continued in the idiom of Swedish or Latin imports, accelerating the "Swedishing" of Finland that, as the source notes, was already a growing problem even with Agricola's work in place (L4424–4428). The Reformation in Finland would likely have remained a purely Swedish-language imposition with even weaker popular roots.

## Historiography

The source (Kouri, ch. 4) presents Agricola as the decisive figure in Finnish reformation, crediting him with founding the literary Finnish tradition while characterising him theologically as a moderate Melanchthonian rather than a creative doctrinal innovator. Agricola's literary output "consists mainly of translations of Greek, Latin, German and Swedish writings, but it also reflects his cautious reforming activities and his personal thought" (L4302–4304). The Reformation's role in Finnish national formation is emphasised: "In Finland — as in Norway — the Reformation, which emphasised the national languages, was important for the formation of a nationality and culture" (L4564–4565), though the countervailing Swedishing policy is also noted as a structural tension inherent in Finnish integration into the Swedish realm.
