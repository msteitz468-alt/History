# William Morris Hughes ("Billy Hughes")

## Overview

William Morris ("Billy") Hughes was one of the most consequential and divisive Australian politicians of the early twentieth century. He served as attorney-general under Fisher (1914–15) and then as prime minister from October 1915 to February 1923 — the longest continuous prime ministership in Australian history to that point. He led Australia through the most intense years of the First World War, split the Labor Party over conscription (1916–17), formed a Nationalist coalition government, represented Australia aggressively at the Paris Peace Conference (1919), and was eventually ousted by the Country Party. Known as "the Little Digger," he embodied an intense, bellicose Australian nationalism built on the Anzac sacrifice.

## Background

Hughes was British-born, a former union official and lawyer. He entered parliament through the Labor Party in New South Wales. The source describes him as "not nearly as bright or as eloquent" — this is Fisher's comparison; by the text's own evidence Hughes was in fact the Labor Party's most effective advocate and intellectual strategist throughout the period. He was one of Labor's "two leading ideologues and propagandists" on the path toward nationalisation of monopolies. He "almost single-handedly persuaded Labor out of its stock view" on compulsory military training, arguing that a citizen army on the Swiss model "could not be used by the government against workers or for military adventures."

## Major Phases of Career

**Labor strategist (1901–14).** Hughes was the drafter of Labor's constitutional referendum proposals (1911, 1913) to nationalise monopolies: he "declared that there were 33 trusts or combines operating in Australia" and was "their most effective advocate." When war broke out during the 1914 election, "Hughes, realising Labor's disadvantage, tried first to persuade the government to postpone the election. When that failed he wrote a new election manifesto for Fisher to sign" claiming Labor had built Australia's defence forces.

**Attorney-General (1914–15).** As attorney-general under Fisher, "the ebullient patriotism of Hughes dominated parliamentary sittings." He "caught the popular mood" and in January 1916 travelled to Britain to gauge the Empire's needs, where "he was both lionised by imperial opinion makers and manipulated by power brokers."

**Prime Minister and the conscription crisis (1915–17).** Hughes succeeded Fisher in October 1915. Accepting that Australia needed to dispatch 16,500 men a month — "two-and-a-half times the number then volunteering" — he decided to seek popular endorsement by referendum rather than pass legislation directly (which was constitutionally available). In opening the campaign in September 1916 he spoke of "this great hour, when our country and all we hold dear are in deadly peril." The first referendum (October 1916) was narrowly defeated. Hughes quit the Labor Party, taking "23 of the 65 Labor members of the Commonwealth parliament with him," and joined non-Labor members to form a Nationalist Party government. The second conscription referendum (December 1917) was again narrowly defeated.

**Paris Peace Conference (1919).** Hughes attended determined to secure recognition for Australia's sacrifice. He "argued for Australian as well as imperial representation (famously speaking for 'sixty thousand dead')" and pursued Australian interests "robustly, if not always prudently." His "sardonic attacks on Woodrow Wilson delighted some but his increasingly strident table thumping aggravated the majority of delegates." He was "the most vocal opponent of the racial equality clause proposed by the Japanese for the covenant of the League of Nations" — alienating Japan. He succeeded in securing an Australian mandate over former German New Guinea.

**Post-war decline (1919–22).** Hughes won the December 1919 election capitalising on his reputation as the "Little Digger," but his position eroded. He maintained state enterprises (Commonwealth Shipping Line, wireless, oil refining) which alienated Nationalist conservatives. Rural interests hostile to the tariff cost formed the Country Party in 1920. After the 1922 election (his party's vote 35 per cent) he made way for Stanley Melbourne Bruce.

## Conscription Referendums: The Split

The conscription crisis was "the most acrimonious and enduring political schism in Australian political history." Hughes rightly represented the Empire's need for troops as dire but misrepresented Australia's domestic situation. The split divided on class, religion, and ethnicity: those for conscription were "middle-class, Protestant, from English, Scots and Welsh backgrounds"; those against were "more often from working-class, Irish Catholic backgrounds." Archbishop Mannix in Melbourne was conspicuous in the No campaign; the Easter 1916 rebellion in Dublin added fuel. Hughes' departure from Labor condemned the party to opposition until World War 2 "with the exception of a brief disastrous period during the Depression."

## Attitude Toward Soldiers

Hughes "refused to allow the 120 Australians sentenced to death for a variety of military offences, including desertion, to be executed" — a decision the source notes "is unlikely to have actually impeded [the AIF's] fighting ability." He championed returned soldiers, earning the "Little Digger" sobriquet, though an exasperated Hughes told the RSL he "couldn't work miracles" in response to incessant criticism of repatriation efforts.

## Counterfactual Significance

Hughes was the hinge figure of Australian WWI politics. Without his conscription gamble the Labor Party would have remained in government, the Nationalist Party would not have been created, and the post-war conservative dominance of federal politics might not have been established. His opposition to the racial equality clause at Paris significantly shaped Japan's grievances and the inter-war dynamics of Asia-Pacific relations.

## Historiography

The source gives Hughes a dual characterisation: brilliant strategist and dangerous bellicist. Fitzhardinge's two-volume political biography (The Little Digger, 1964; 1979) is the standard account. W.J. Hudson's study of Hughes at Paris traces the birth of Australian diplomacy. The source (Garton and Stanley) presents him as having "capitalised" on Anzac attachments politically while genuinely believing in the war's justice.
