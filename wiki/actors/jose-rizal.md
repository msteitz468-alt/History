## Overview

José Rizal (19 June 1861 – 30 December 1896) was the pre-eminent intellectual of the Filipino Propaganda Movement and the most important symbolic figure of Philippine nationalism. A polymath — novelist, poet, ophthalmologist, sculptor, and political thinker — he produced the two novels (*Noli Me Tangere*, 1887, and *El Filibusterismo*, 1891) that became the central texts of Filipino political consciousness under Spanish colonial rule. His execution by the Spanish on 30 December 1896 — framed in popular reception as a martyr's death analogous to Christ's passion — made him the founding icon of Philippine nationalism and, subsequently, a messianic figure around whom rural peasant movements organized their own anti-colonial aspirations through the early twentieth century.

## Role and Major Decisions

### Formation and the Propaganda Movement

Rizal came from a prosperous *principalia* family in Laguna province, Luzon. His education at the Ateneo de Manila and later the Universidad de Santo Tomás preceded his departure for Europe, where he studied medicine (ophthalmology) in Madrid and Paris. While in Europe he became central to the circle of Filipino students and intellectuals producing the *Propaganda Movement* — the effort, primarily through journalism and literature, to advocate for Filipino rights within the Spanish constitutional system.

The Propaganda Movement's vehicle was *La Solidaridad*, a fortnightly newspaper published 1889–95 in Barcelona. Its objectives, reflecting nineteenth-century liberalism rather than independence, included equality before the law for Filipinos and Spaniards, representation in the Spanish Cortes (parliament), and an end to the privileges of the Spanish friars (religious orders). Rizal's private letter of 1887 captures the movement's initial position: "in the present circumstances we want no separation from Spain; all we demand is more care, better instruction, better officials, one or two representatives, and more security for ourselves and our property."

### The Novels

*Noli Me Tangere* (1887, "Touch Me Not") and *El Filibusterismo* (1891, "The Subversive") were Rizal's most consequential works. Written in Spanish, they depicted with satirical force the abuses of the friar orders, the corruption of the colonial administration, and the social humiliation of Filipinos under Spanish rule. [[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v2-1992]] notes that these novels, alongside *La Solidaridad*, were "the best-known writings produced by" the Propaganda Movement. The novels galvanized a generation of educated Filipinos and remained politically explosive enough to be banned under Spanish rule.

### Intellectual Evolution and the Liga Filipina

By the late 1880s Rizal was diverging from the assimilationist position of Gregorio del Pilar and others, concluding that effective action required working within the Philippines rather than lobbying from Europe: "The error all make in thinking we can help here [in Europe], far away, is a great mistake indeed. . . . The field of battle is in the Philippines; there is where we should be."

Rizal returned to Manila on 26 June 1892. On 3 July he founded the Liga Filipina — a secret mutual-aid and reform organization — in a private home. Less than a week later he was arrested and sent into internal exile in Dapitan, Mindanao, where he remained until 1896. The Liga Filipina dissolved quickly after his arrest. One faction continued supporting the Propaganda Movement in Europe; another founded the [[katipunan-1892|Katipunan]] under Andres Bonifacio.

### The Katipunan and Refusal to Join the Armed Struggle

While Rizal was exiled in Dapitan, the Katipunan prepared a mass armed insurrection. Rizal declined to endorse it, arguing that "conditions were not yet right" for revolution. This position — which contemporaries and later historians have variously attributed to caution, tactical calculation, or genuine philosophical opposition to premature violence — meant that when the Katipunan uprising broke out in August 1896, Rizal was not involved. The Spanish nevertheless tried and executed him for supposed complicity, a verdict almost universally regarded as unjust even by the Archbishop of Manila who had refused to defrock Rizal's fellow martyrs from 1872.

### Execution and Martyrdom

Rizal was executed by firing squad at Bagumbayan field (now Luneta Park), Manila, on 30 December 1896, at age 35. He had composed a farewell poem ("Mi Último Adiós") the night before. The manner of his death — publicly executed by the colonial state for political offenses — combined with his prior articulation of Filipino national consciousness made him the natural anchor for a cult of martyrdom. The chapter on religion in [[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v2-1992]] notes that "the execution in December 1896 of the celebrated propagandist Dr Jose Rizal gave the movement a martyr to focus on. Rizal's own words and behaviour ensured that his final hours would be interpreted in terms of Christ's passion and death, a story familiar to every Christianized Filipino."

## Counterfactual Significance

The question of what changes without Rizal is analytically interesting: the Propaganda Movement would almost certainly have continued without him, since the ilustrado grievances it articulated were structural rather than depending on any one individual. The Katipunan insurrection was organized by Bonifacio and would likely have proceeded. What Rizal uniquely contributed was:

1. The literary corpus that gave elite-nationalist discourse a Filipino-authored canonical text
2. The martyrdom narrative that fused Christian and nationalist frames in popular consciousness
3. A conceptual insistence that Filipino national identity was a product of Spanish contempt directed at an entire population — "They made the race itself an object of insult" — rather than a pre-existing ethnic community, which grounded nationalism in shared colonial experience rather than cultural homogeneity

Without the martyrdom, the assimilation of nationalist politics to popular religious idiom — with Rizal's image circulating in peasant rebel chapels "from Mount Banahao in Luzon to Bucas Grande in Mindanao" through the early twentieth century — would have lacked its primary anchor.

## Historiography

Rizal's interpretation has been persistently contested. The debate about the character of the Philippine revolution — whether popular support for the Katipunan represented autonomous nationalist consciousness or elite mobilization of clientelist networks — directly involves Rizal as the revolution's intellectual precursor. Reynaldo Ileto in *Pasyon and Revolution* (1979) argued that popular participants in the Katipunan "had certain ideas about the world and their places in it, ideas quite different from those of the 'better classes' of society," suggesting that Rizal's liberal nationalist framework did not determine the meaning the mass movement gave to the struggle. John Schumacher has argued against such schematic elite-mass distinctions. Official Philippine historiography treats Rizal as the unambiguous founding hero; revisionist historians have questioned the elevation of a bourgeois intellectual above the peasant and working-class leaders who actually organized the insurrection.

Rizal himself rejected the notion that earlier Spanish-period revolts in the Philippines constituted proto-nationalism, arguing as early as 1889 "that such revolts were isolated and directed against local grievances, and that Filipinos became conscious of themselves as a nation only during the nineteenth century."
