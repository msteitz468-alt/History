# Andrew Fisher

## Overview

Andrew Fisher was the Labor prime minister of Australia on three occasions (1908–09, 1910–13, 1914–15) and the leader who oversaw Labor's greatest electoral triumph — the 1910 election in which the party won a majority in both houses for the first time, on 50 per cent of the vote. Fisher is historically significant for completing the institutional architecture of the early Commonwealth: the Commonwealth Bank, land tax, compulsory military training, the Australian navy, and the beginning of Canberra. He also led Australia into the First World War, pledging support for Britain "down to the last man and the last shilling" on 31 July 1914, four days before Britain's declaration. He resigned in October 1915 under the strain of war preparations, handing power to Billy Hughes.

## Background and Character

The source describes Fisher as "son of a Scottish coalminer and sober, autodidact leader of the Australian Labor Party." He was a former miner himself, "handsome, not nearly as bright or as eloquent as his deputy, W.M. Hughes, but absolutely honest and straightforward and a willing servant of the party." At the 1905 party conference he had "supported a complete socialisation of the economy as Labor's objective."

## Key Achievements as Prime Minister

Fisher's 1910–13 government enacted the Labor platform with unusual completeness:

- **Commonwealth Bank**: inaugurated to "compete with rather than control the private banks"
- **Land tax on large estates**: aimed at breaking up large holdings for more productive settlement
- **Maternity allowance**: payment to mothers on birth of a child, controversially available regardless of marital status; "Labor women and feminists welcomed the measure"
- **Compulsory military training and navy**: completing what Deakin had proposed; "defence expenditure doubled in Labor's first year of office and continued to grow"
- **Canberra**: began construction of the national capital; also "adding wattle to the coat of arms and putting a kangaroo rather than the King's head on the stamps"
- **Transcontinental railway**: commenced the railway across the Nullarbor to Western Australia
- **Northern Territory**: took over from South Australia

Fisher "boasted that Labor was the national party." The likely enemy in defence planning was Japan: "their racial ideals and fears — the defence of white Australia — impelled the socialists to this uncharacteristic commitment."

The government also proposed constitutional amendments to nationalise monopolies. In 1911 referendum these were defeated with only 40 per cent in favour; in 1913 they came close to success (49 per cent, three States) but Labor narrowly lost the election.

## War Leadership (1914–15)

Fisher won the August 1914 election — Labor received "the highest vote it has ever attained" — and took office in September 1914 with Hughes as attorney-general. His pledge of 31 July 1914 to support Britain "to the last man and the last shilling" became the defining statement of Australia's war commitment. The strain of war preparations took a toll on Fisher's health and he resigned in October 1915, to be replaced by Hughes.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Fisher's 1910 majority the Labor institutional program — Commonwealth Bank, land tax, naval build-up, compulsory training — would have been deferred. The constitutional referendum losses revealed the structural limits of Labor's reformist ambition, shaping the party's trajectory for decades.

## Historiography

The source (Hirst, Chapter 1) treats Fisher as a capable but secondary figure to the more intellectually distinctive Deakin and Hughes. Garton and Stanley (Chapter 2) note his 1914 pledge as the foundational statement of Australian war commitment. Marilyn Lake's work on "radical maternalism" emphasises the maternity allowance as an international innovation.
