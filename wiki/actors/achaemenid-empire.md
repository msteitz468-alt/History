# Achaemenid Persian Empire

## Overview

The **Achaemenid Empire** (550–330 BCE) was, in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]'s
words, the empire that **first united the Afro-Eurasian world** — the largest
agrarian polity yet seen, ruling perhaps a tenth of the earth's land surface from
the Aegean and Egypt to the Indus. Daryaee (Ch 10) presents it as both the
**culmination** of ~2,000 years of Near Eastern empire-building and a genuine
**innovation**: a "world-system" resting not on the conquered-land destruction of
its [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyrian]] and [[neo-babylonian-empire|Babylonian]]
predecessors but on a well-designed system of political support for production,
tolerant multiethnic administration, and a centralized but locally non-franchised
taxation.

## Founding and Expansion

Founded by **[[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus II]]** (the Great), ruler of Anšan in the
old Elamite highlands, who conquered the Medes (550), Lydia, and Babylon (539 BCE) —
freeing the exiled Jews (the [[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus Cylinder]]) and achieving a
"largely peaceful union of the ancient rival civilizations of Anatolia,
Mesopotamia, Elam, and the Iranian Plateau." His son **Cambyses** added Egypt;
**Darius I** (521–486) added the Indus and consolidated the system. The empire's
westward overreach into Greece produced the **Greco-Persian Wars** (Marathon 490;
Thermopylae, Salamis, Plataea 480–479 BCE) — its high-water mark and the formative
crucible of [[classical-greece|Greek]] identity (see [[herodotus]],
[[athens-fifth-century]]).

## Governing Structure: Darius's System

Darius "the Great" earns the title not for conquest but as **founder of a system of
state administration that, in CWH IV's striking claim, "remains the basis of state
administration in the world even today."** Its components:
- **Satrapies** — semi-autonomous provinces under appointed governors (*xšathrapaiti*),
  watched by the "King's Eyes and Ears."
- A reformed, productivity-encouraging **taxation** system tied to the center.
- Royal **garrisons** answerable only to the Great King — securing the state while
  removing the means of provincial rebellion.
- The **Royal Road** (~1,400 miles) with relay stations, inns, and caravanserais —
  a communications and trade artery (see [[trade-network-formation]]).
- Major **hydraulic works**: a Nile–Red Sea canal, and the spread of the *qanat*
  underground-water system enabling cultivation across arid zones from Egypt to Persia.

## Ideology: Justice, Order, and Tolerance

The empire's self-image rested on **arta** (cosmic order against chaos) and the
king as upholder of *data* (law) and of [[zoroastrianism|Ahuramazda's]] benevolent
creation ("good horses, good men… no enemy army, no crop failure, no Falsehood").
Famously **multicultural** (Darius's Naqš-e Rostam inscription uses *vispazananam*,
"of all kinds of people"), it adopted **Aramaic** as its administrative lingua
franca and tolerated local cults and institutions (even upholding Ionian Greek
democracies). This rehabilitated, tolerant Achaemenid — against the Greek "oriental
despotism" trope — is the achievement of the modern "Achaemenid history" school.

## Decline

Weakened by costly Greek wars, satrapal revolts, and succession struggles, the
empire fell to **[[alexander-the-great|Alexander of Macedon]]**, who defeated Darius
III at Granicus, Issus, and decisively **Gaugamela (331 BCE)**; Persepolis was
sacked (yielding ~2,500 tons of gold) and Darius III killed (330). Its institutions,
however, were inherited by the [[hellenistic-world|Seleucids]], the
[[parthian-empire|Parthians]], and the [[sasanian-empire|Sasanians]].

## Historiography

Long captive to **hostile Greek sources** ([[herodotus]], Xenophon, Ctesias),
Achaemenid history has been transformed since the 1980s by the "Achaemenid History"
school using **Persian royal inscriptions** (Behistun, Naqš-e Rostam), the
**Persepolis Fortification and Treasury tablets**, and Babylonian evidence —
recovering a sophisticated, tolerant, well-administered polity. Compare the
comparative imperiology in [[empire]].
