# Shulgi

The second and greatest king of the **[[third-dynasty-of-ur|Third Dynasty of Ur]]**
(reigned ~48 years), son of [[ur-nammu|Ur-Nammu]] and the architect of the
**bureaucratic command-state** that defines Ur III. CAH ranks him among "the
greatest kings" of Mesopotamia — though, characteristically, later tradition
"long remembered" almost nothing of him. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XXII, C. J. Gadd).

## The bureaucratic reform

For its first ~20 years Shulgi's reign was unremarkable (local religious
observances dominate the date-formulae). Then, from about his **21st year**,
everything changed: a date-formula records that he "set in order the registry of
lands" of the great god of Nippur, and from that point the famous **Ur III
book-keeping** explodes into being — minute, checked, ubiquitous accounting on
finely-made tablets, the **mobilization of resources for a policy of expansion**
("the men of Ur were sworn in as archers"). He **standardized weights** (preserved
and imitated to the end of Babylonian history), reformed the **calendar**, set up
a **bureau of standards**, and built the relay-roads and "**rest-houses**" of a
royal courier network — celebrated in a hymn boasting that he ran from Nippur to
Ur and back in a single day through a hailstorm.

## Wars in the Zagros

Shulgi's military energy was directed at the **northeastern mountains** — repeated
campaigns (places "destroyed" as often as "nine times") against Lullubi,
Simurrum, Urbilum (Irbil), Karakhar, and the unreconciled **Gutians**, holding the
Assyrian borderland (Ashur was his vassal) and subordinating **[[elam|Elam]]**
(Susa). The wars were as much defensive as acquisitive, fought "at a mean distance
of 400 miles from Ur."

## Deification and the royal hymns

From his middle years Shulgi was **deified** — writing the divine determinative
before his name, receiving cult and temple in each city. CAH connects this to the
need for a *god*-king who could legitimately appoint human governors as the
city-god's stewards (see [[divine-kingship]]). He is the protagonist of more
**royal hymns** than any other Mesopotamian king (~30 survive), in which he claims
descent from the demigod Lugalbanda and the goddess Ninsun (mother of Gilgamesh,
whom he calls "brother"), kinship with the Sun-god, and mastery of letters and of
eight musical instruments — a literate, self-mythologizing god-king.

## Significance

Shulgi is the **maker of the Ur III state** as a phenomenon: not its founder but
its organizer, the king who turned [[ur-nammu|Ur-Nammu's]] restoration into a
centralized, intensely documented command economy with standardized measures, a
courier system, a deified monarchy, and a court literature. The administrative
revolution of his 21st year produced the largest archival corpus of the ancient
world and the template of bureaucratic kingship inherited by later Mesopotamia.

## Historiography

The supreme irony of Ur III: a king buried under tens of thousands of his own
administrative tablets is almost absent from **narrative tradition** — the
chronicles and omens give only "jejune, unimportant" notices (a pious Babylonian
chronicle even condemns him for impiety toward Marduk). The hymns and royal letters
are the main qualitative sources, heavily idealized. The reading of his name (Šulgi
/ Dungi) and the attribution of the Ur III law code are debated. Dates follow the
[[mesopotamian-chronology|middle chronology]]. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
