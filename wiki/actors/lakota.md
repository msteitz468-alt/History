# Lakota

**Naming note (CHNPA / wiki convention):** Fowler’s text uses “Sioux,” “Teton Sioux/Tetons,” “Yanktons,” “Yanktonais,” “Santee Sioux.” This page is the canonical home for **Teton/western (Lakota)** expansion and power; **Dakota/Santee** and **Yankton/Yanktonai** material is retained under explicit labels here rather than collapsed into a single ethnonym. Self-designations and modern reservation nations remain plural.

The **Lakota** (Teton Sioux of Fowler’s chapter) became the dominant equestrian power of the central and northern Plains by the early nineteenth century, allied with [[actors/cheyenne|Cheyennes]] and Arapahos until the 1870s wars ended free life on the [[places/black-hills|Black Hills]]–Powder River range.

## Overview

Mid-seventeenth-century Teton, Yankton, and Yanktonai Sioux in east-central Minnesota were pushed south by better-armed Crees/Assiniboines; they pressed Iowas, Otos, Missouris, Omaha-Poncas west to the Missouri (1680s) and Cheyennes out of the Minnesota valley. By the 1740s Tetons/Yanktons reached the Missouri, attacked Mandan/Hidatsa, and bore down on Arikaras. Fully equestrian by the 1760s as “**pirates of the Missouri**,” they obtained guns via Upper Mississippi British/Santee trade rather than Missouri middlemen, harassed St. Louis and Canadian traders, overpriced guns to Arikaras, drove buffalo from villages, and raided when exchange terms were refused. After 1778–82 and 1801–2 smallpox devastated villagers (Arikaras lost four-fifths and collapsed 32 villages into two), **Lewis and Clark (1804) recognized the Sioux as the dominant power on the Missouri**.

Some Tetons moved west of the Missouri into prime buffalo country, hunting year-round and raiding for horses. They contested the Plains between Missouri and Black Hills against Kiowas, [[actors/crow-apsalooke|Crows]], Arapahos, and Cheyennes; by **1825** they drove Kiowas south of the Black Hills and Crows west to the Powder/Yellowstone. Despite long hostility, Cheyennes and Arapahos allied with Tetons and together dominated the central uplands until the 1870s. Fowler: **nineteenth-century Teton warfare aimed not merely at horses or revenge but to exterminate rival groups**. After 1838 smallpox, Pawnees lost Platte grounds; Crows hit by smallpox/cholera 1840s and pushed from Laramie, allying with Shoshones, Flatheads, and Americans.

## Role and Structure

- **Military:** multi-band Teton polity capable of coalition war with Cheyenne/Arapaho (and some Comanche/Kiowa help in the 1850s–70s).
- **Political:** many autonomous subdivisions made selecting few “intermediary chiefs” for U.S. treaties difficult; government tried to influence/dictate selection; “touching the pen” later enabled fraud accusations.
- **Economic:** buffalo hunt + robe trade (Fort Tecumseh/Pierre, Fort Laramie for Oglala with Cheyenne/Arapaho); later agency dependency when game failed.

## Key Events and Processes (Fowler)

### Dakota War 1862 (Santee/Dakota — distinct division)

Upper Santees (Sisseton/Wahpeton) ~7,000 often allied with Tetons/Yanktonais; Lower Santees (Mdewakanton/Wahpekute) ~2,500. Trader/mixed-blood betrayal of kinship ethics, 1851/1859 cessions whose money went to traders, settler contempt, withheld annuities and famine threat led warrior societies to attack; **over 500 settlers killed** in the Minnesota River valley despite many Santees protecting friends. After peace, mobs condemned 303 (including rescuers); Lincoln commuted all but **thirty-nine executed**; all Santees expelled—some to Nebraska/Dakota agencies, others joined “hostile” Tetons/Yanktonais. Sense of betrayal stiffened wider Sioux resistance while harsh reprisals may have suppressed other reservation rebellions.

### Powder River war and 1868 Great Sioux Reservation

Northern Arapahos, Cheyennes, and Sioux hunted Powder River buffalo ground; Montana gold 1862 brought illegal invasion and federal forts protecting trespassers; Sioux-led effort repelled them. **1868 peace treaty** established the Great Sioux Reservation and guaranteed Powder River hunting rights to the Sioux and allies. Four Missouri agencies plus Red Cloud and Spotted Tail agencies on North Platte/White Rivers. Some Santees made peace with [[actors/metis|Métis]] and relocated to Manitoba/Saskatchewan; Canada granted sanctuary without treaties if peaceful; seven Santee refugee reserves in Canada 1873–77.

### Black Hills war, Little Bighorn, surrender

Gold discovery 1874; 1875 purchase/lease talks failed; government ordered all Sioux on reservation or face Army attack though off-reservation hunting was needed for agency food shortfalls. Winter 1876 campaign → year of Custer’s defeat at [[events/battle-of-little-bighorn-1876|Little Bighorn]] (Sioux suffered little then dispersed); following winter Army reinforcements, Yellowstone forts, and Congress supply cutoff pressed agency Sioux (with Arapaho and some Cheyenne) to sign **fraudulent 1876 Black Hills cession**. Army seized horses/guns of alleged hostile helpers and enlisted Sioux scouts; winter **1877** forced most Tetons and Yanktonai/Santee allies to surrender; Red Cloud and Spotted Tail agencies moved north within reduced reservation. Mostly Hunkpapa Tetons under Sitting Bull fled to Canada; eventually in 1913 several hundred obtained Wood Mountain reserve; others including Sitting Bull returned to the U.S. **1881**. After Black Hills/Montana defeats Sioux resigned to reservation life if not assimilation; forced co-residence of diverse accommodation/resistance strategies shaped later reservation culture.

Pawnee demographic collapse and Like-a-Fishhook pressure (Mandan–Hidatsa–Arikara) partly **caused_by** Teton military pressure (Fowler).

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Teton expansion after the horse-and-gun revolution and post-epidemic village collapse, Upper Missouri trade geography and the map of “hostile” vs. allied nations in the 1860s–70s would have looked different. Structural U.S. settler pressure and buffalo destruction still constrained free plains life; Teton agency shaped *which* alliances and *when* the final wars erupted.

## Historiography

Fowler synthesizes ethnohistory; White on Sioux expansion is the recommended specialist anchor in her bibliographic essay. Wiki pages should not use “Sioux” as sole ethnonym for all Dakota–Lakota–Nakota without division. Full fidelity on Sand Creek aftermath swelling, Dakota War killings/executions, and fraudulent Black Hills cession is required—do not tone down.
