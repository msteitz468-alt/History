# The Hanseatic League

The **Hanseatic League** (the *Hanse*) was the confederation of north-German merchant cities that
dominated the trade of the **Baltic and North Seas** in the later Middle Ages. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH
VI]] (S.C. Rowell, Ch. 17; Peter Spufford, Ch. 7) treats it as the commercial backbone of "Baltic
Europe," the northern counterpart of the Italian maritime economy.

## From Merchant Hanses to a League of Cities

The Hansa grew out of the **merchant guilds** (*Hansen*) of the Rhineland and Westphalian towns and
the great Baltic emporium of **Lübeck** (see [[commercial-revolution]] and [[urbanization]]). Over
the thirteenth century these merged into a single guild with privileged trading posts (*Kontore*)
abroad — the **Steelyard** in London, the **Peterhof** in Novgorod, depots at Bruges and Bergen —
controlling the exchange of the **raw materials of the east and north** (Baltic grain, timber, furs,
wax, fish, Swedish iron and copper) for the **manufactures of the west** (Flemish and English cloth,
salt, wine). In the fourteenth century the loose mercantile association hardened into a **political
league of some 70–200 cities**, led by Lübeck.

## Power in the Baltic

The league's power peaked in the fourteenth century. When **Valdemar IV of Denmark** threatened its
Baltic trade, the Hansa cities formed the **Confederation of Cologne (1367)**, made war on Denmark,
and imposed the **Peace of Stralsund (1370)** — winning control of the Danish Sound tolls and a veto
over the Danish succession, the high-water mark of Hanseatic political might. The Hansa thus
exercised, as a **league of towns**, the kind of sea-power that elsewhere belonged to kings —
"the German-dominated Hanse" inheriting the Danish *dominium maris baltici* of the previous century.

## Significance

The Hanseatic League is the great late-medieval example of **urban, commercial, collective power**
on the European scale: a stateless confederation of trading cities that controlled the northern
seas, fixed the terms of Baltic and North Sea commerce, and even waged successful war on kingdoms.
It bound the [[teutonic-knights|Teutonic]] Baltic, [[grand-duchy-of-lithuania|Lithuania]], Rus′
(Novgorod), Scandinavia, England and Flanders into a single trading system — the northern wing of
the medieval [[commercial-revolution|commercial revolution]], and a counterweight to the rising
territorial monarchies.
