## Role and Significance

Gustav Stresemann (1878–1929) served as Chancellor of Germany briefly in 1923 and as Foreign Minister from 1923 until his death in October 1929 — a tenure of nearly six years that represented the most stable and internationally productive period of the [[weimar-republic]]. He is "Weimar's most famous foreign Minister" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18).

Stresemann embodied the transition from Wilhelmine nationalism to pragmatic republican statecraft. As a National Liberal during WWI, he had supported the expansionist war aims. By 1923 he had pivoted to a strategy of international integration and American-backed stabilization as the only viable path for German national recovery.

## Strategic Vision

Stresemann's foreign policy rested on a clear strategic insight: Germany, stripped of significant military capacity by Versailles and with its public credit exhausted, could use its economic importance and its indebtedness to American private investors as a lever against French and British demands. His reasoning: "Wall Street's three billion dollar investment in the Weimar Republic would make America into Germany's protector. In due course America's stake would grow so large Washington would have an interest in seeing to it that the most onerous reparations obligations were lifted" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18).

This strategy — the Dawes Plan architecture (1924) — was a substitute for military power: "The lasting ties of economic interdependence would substitute for the dangerous system of military alliances." It partially succeeded: the Young Plan (1929) reduced reparations, French troops evacuated the Rhineland, Germany joined the League of Nations (1926). But it was a "risky strategy both in political and economic terms" — it required Wall Street to maintain confidence in Germany indefinitely, which the Depression destroyed after 1929.

His vision of Volksgemeinschaft was socioeconomic rather than ethnic: he saw national community "primarily as a means of calming the atmosphere of class struggle in industrial relations and of achieving cross-party co-operation in foreign policy" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 19).

During WWI (1915), Stresemann had expressed the classic expansionist view: "Above all we must aim for a strengthening of our economic position in Central Europe (Mitteleuropa), so that we may have what American industry has, a territory of 150 million people ... to satisfying whose consumption industry can direct all its enterprise" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18). His post-1923 pivot was a pragmatic adaptation, not an ideological conversion — the methods changed, the goal of German power restoration did not.

## Key Events

- **1923**: As Chancellor, managed the hyperinflation crisis and the Ruhr occupation standoff; stabilized the Rentenmark.
- **1924 (Dawes Plan)**: Formalized the American credit architecture and reparations framework.
- **1925 (Locarno Treaties)**: Germany accepted western borders and entered a security pact with France and Belgium, ending French isolation of Germany. Received Nobel Peace Prize jointly with Briand and Chamberlain.
- **1926**: Germany admitted to League of Nations with permanent Council seat.
- **1929 (Young Plan)**: Further reduction of reparations. Stresemann died October 1929, just before the Wall Street Crash destroyed the architecture he had built.

## Counterfactual Significance

Stresemann's death in October 1929 — six days after Black Thursday on Wall Street — removed the republic's most effective foreign policy advocate at the precise moment when his skills were most needed. Whether he could have navigated the Depression crisis without resorting to the Article 48 emergency powers that Brüning used to destroy parliamentary governance is unknowable. His political base in the DVP was moving rightward; he had always been managing domestic conservatives as much as foreign adversaries.
