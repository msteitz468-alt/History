# Thucydides

## Overview

**Thucydides** (c. 460–c. 400 BCE), son of Olorus of the Athenian deme Halimous, wrote
the *History of the Peloponnesian War* — the single most important source for the Greek
fifth century and, with [[herodotus]], one of the two founders of the European
historiographical tradition. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 1, D. M. Lewis) makes
him the spine of the entire volume: the security of its narrative rises and falls with
whether Thucydides is present as a guide, and the editors warn that "it is only at our
peril that we try to reinterpret" what he reports.

## Life

He had **Thracian connexions** — his father's name and his own ownership of gold-mining
rights east of the **Strymon** (iv.105.1) — and family ties to [[cimon]] and to
Thucydides son of Melesias. Born about 460, he grew up amid the intellectual excitement
the sophists were bringing to Athens. His one recorded public act is the **generalship of
424/3**, when his failure to relieve **Amphipolis** from [[brasidas]] led to **twenty
years of exile** (v.26.5) — the enforced leisure, and access to the Peloponnesian side,
in which he watched and wrote the war. He had conceived the work from the war's outbreak
in **431** (i.1.1); how long he lived after 404 is unknown.

## The work and its method

The eight-book *History* (a later division; the manuscripts call it *Historiai*, though
Thucydides probably thought of it as his *xyngraphe*, "composition") is organized by
**war-years split into summers and winters**, a deliberate rejection of the imprecise
dating by [[archon|archons]] or Argive priestesses he criticized in others (v.20.2). Book
I is introductory and contains, "by the way," a history of early Greece (the
*Archaeology*) and a growing weight of reflection on method.

The famous methodological statement (i.22) draws a sharp line:

- **Speeches** are reconstructed as Thucydides judged "the several individuals or groups
  would have said what they had to say about the situation" — an admitted subjectivity,
  flagged by indefinite pronouns.
- **Facts** ("the events of what was done in the war") are explicitly *not* written from
  first impression or his own opinion but "by checking every detail as carefully as
  possible," because eyewitnesses disagreed under the influence of partisanship or
  faulty memory.

Lewis judges his "high competence and devotion to truth... not to be doubted," and rates
his account of **435–411** as more reliable than the materials for almost any other
period of ancient history. But the same chapter stresses the **dependence** this creates:
Thucydides has "assimilated his source material and concealed his workings," and selected
heavily — he disposes of years of Megarian invasions in a sentence, omits the ostracism of
**Hyperbolus** entirely, says nothing of the personal stories about [[pericles|Pericles']]
pre-war difficulties. His silence is therefore not evidence of absence, and CAH V is "at
times surprisingly sceptical or critical of Thucydides" while still treating departure
from what he reports as the greater danger.

## The Pentekontaetia and the change of plan

For **478–435** — the half-century ("the Fifty Years," the **Pentekontaetia**) between
the Persian and Peloponnesian Wars — Thucydides gives only a compressed, chronologically
thin survey (i.89–118), written, he says, to fill a gap left by all his predecessors
except Hellanicus and to show "how the Athenians came to power." Lewis holds that this
material, together with the **Pausanias–Themistocles** excursus (i.128–138), was composed
for other purposes and **incompletely integrated** after a late change of plan — which is
why the chronology of the period is so insecure (see [[classical-greek-historiography]]).

## The Primary Text (now ingested)

The work itself is ingested as **[[thucydides-peloponnesian-war|*The Peloponnesian War*]]** (Hammond
trans., OWC; intro/notes P. J. Rhodes). Rhodes reframes the older image of the perfectly objective
historian: Thucydides was "human, involved, and prejudiced" — pro-Periclean, anti-Cleon, writing a
"patriotically Athenian account of the causes of the war" — yet "not dishonest," and exceptionally
rigorous. The text is **unfinished** (it breaks off in 411) and built in **overlapping strata**
(Books 5.25–83 and 8 are visibly a rough draft; passages written before and after the war's end sit
side by side, with unreconciled inconsistencies — e.g. on whether the Sicilian expedition was flawed
in conception or only in execution). On the **speeches**, the most-debated feature, he professes a
compromise between "the overall intent of what was actually said" and "what [he] supposed would have
been needed" (1.22) — the language, and the obsessive analysis of *Athenian power*, being largely his
own.

## Significance

Thucydides set the standard of **critical, source-checking, secular** political history,
treating causation (his distinction between the "truest cause" — Spartan fear of growing
Athenian power — and the immediate pretexts) as the historian's central problem. His
analysis of the [[peloponnesian-war]], of empire, *stasis* (civil strife), and the gap
between power and justice (the Melian Dialogue, the Mytilenean debate) made even military
narrative, in the editors' phrase, "a commentary on the human condition."
