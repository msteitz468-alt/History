# The Xiongnu

## Overview

The **Xiongnu** were the **first great nomadic empire**, a steppe confederation that
dominated Mongolia from c. **209 BCE** and became, in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]
(Holcombe Ch 15, Liu Ch 17), premodern China's "most important foreign adversaries." Their
two-century struggle with the **[[han-dynasty|Han]]** initiated the first organized trading
networks of the **[[silk-roads|Silk Roads]]**.

## The First Steppe Empire

The Xiongnu rose precisely in reaction to Chinese unification: the **[[qin-dynasty|Qin]]**
colonization of the **Ordos** stimulated the steppe peoples to organize militarily, and
under **Modu** they formed a unified confederation (c. 209 BCE). In **200 BCE** they
surrounded and trapped the **[[han-dynasty|Han]]** founder and his army for a week. For half
a century the Han **appeased** them with tribute and marriage alliances, while the **Great
Wall** functioned less as a barrier than as a managed frontier of fairs and markets where
herders traded livestock and furs for grain and silk.

## War, Trade, and the Yuezhi

**Emperor Wu (141–87 BCE)** went on the offensive, pushing the Han far into the northwest to
**outflank** the Xiongnu, and sought an alliance with the **Yuezhi** — a people the Xiongnu
had driven west into Central Asia, where they became the **[[kushan-empire|Kushans]]**. The
envoy **Zhang Qian's** mission to find them (139 BCE) opened the Silk Roads. The Han offensives
never annihilated the Xiongnu but pushed them from the Wall; internal division and Han
pressure fragmented them by the second century CE, after which the **Xianbei** and other
peoples succeeded them on the steppe.

## Significance

The Xiongnu established the recurring pattern of **steppe–sedentary interaction** that
structured Eurasian history: nomadic confederation forming in reaction to imperial
consolidation, alternating war and trade across an ecological frontier, and the displacement
chains (Yuezhi→Kushan) that knit the Silk Roads together. They are the steppe pole of
CWH IV's "states, empires, and **networks**" thesis.

## Historiography

The Xiongnu are known almost entirely through **hostile Chinese sources** (Sima Qian, Ban
Gu) and archaeology, leaving their own language, ethnicity, and political structure debated —
including a long-argued but unproven possible connection to the later European
**[[hunnic-migrations|Huns]]**. They are the foundational case for the study of
[[pastoral-nomadism|nomadic state formation]] and steppe empires.

The principal source is **[[sima-qian|Sima Qian's]]** **"Account of the Xiongnu"** (*Shi ji*
110), now ingested as part of [[sima-qian-records-han-ii|*Records of the Grand Historian: Han
Dynasty II*]]. It supplies the foundational ethnography — a people of "horses, cows, and sheep"
with "no walled cities or fixed dwellings" and no writing, every boy raised to "ride sheep and
shoot birds" and so to "act as armed cavalry in time of war" — and frames the Xiongnu as China's
perennial northern menace "from the time of the Three Dynasties on." It is set within the long
wars of **[[han-wudi|Emperor Wu]]**, whose generals [[han-wudi|Wei Qing and Huo Qubing]] drove
the Xiongnu deep into the desert.
