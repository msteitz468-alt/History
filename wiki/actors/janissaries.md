# The Janissaries (*Yeniçeri*, "New Troops")

The **standing slave-infantry corps** of the Ottoman sultan — the first permanent professional
infantry in post-Roman Europe, recruited through the **[[devshirme|child-levy]]**, and for
centuries the disciplined core of the Ottoman army and the sultan's bodyguard. From Colin
Imber's [[imber-ottoman-empire-1300-1650|*The Ottoman Empire, 1300–1650*]].

## Origins and élite role

Founded in the fourteenth century as perhaps a few hundred infantry serving as the sultan's
bodyguard, the Janissaries were **slaves of the sultan** ([[devshirme|kapıkulu]]), salaried
from the central treasury, forbidden (originally) to marry, and famed for **discipline and
loyalty**. They proved their worth by standing firm when the rest of the army fled or
defected: at **Ankara (1402)** against Timur, and at **Varna (1444)**, where they held around
Murad II and killed the Hungarian king. Their numbers grew through the fifteenth century
(~5,000 at Mehmed II's accession in 1451, doubled in his wars with Uzun Hasan), hovering
around **10,000–12,000** for much of the sixteenth century — a small élite corps within a
cavalry-dominated army of ~40,000 horsemen.

## The "military revolution" and expansion

From the late sixteenth century the spread of **handheld firearms** and entrenched infantry
tactics shifted warfare toward foot soldiers. In the long **Austrian war (1593–1606)** the
Ottoman *timar* cavalry proved inferior to entrenched Habsburg arquebusiers, and the government
responded by **massively expanding the Janissaries** and recruiting free musket-bearing
infantry: numbers rose from **7,886 (1527) to 12,798 (1567) to ~39,282 (1609)**. This
transformed the corps from a small household élite into "one of the largest contingents in the
Ottoman army" — and, as the [[devshirme]] could no longer supply the numbers, opened it to
**free Muslims, Turks, and Janissaries' own sons**, dissolving its slave character.

## From soldiers to power-brokers

Expansion changed the corps' politics. A large, salaried, increasingly hereditary body
resident in the capital, the Janissaries became a **fiscal and political force**: their pay
strained the treasury (a crisis in 1623 after four accession-bonuses in six years), and they
repeatedly **mutinied** over arrears and policy — and could make or unmake sultans. Caroline
Finkel's [[finkel-osmans-dream|*Osman's Dream*]] shows them at the center of the recurrent
"Ottoman-style revolutions" of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries — depositions of
sultans and grand viziers (the 1648, 1687–88, and 1703 "Edirne Incident" uprisings) in which
the corps, allied with the *ulema* and the Istanbul crowd, enforced its will. Their later
history as a conservative praetorian guard, **obstructing reform** (they destroyed Selim III's
"New Order" army in 1807), ended only with their violent **abolition by Mahmud II in 1826**
(the "Auspicious Incident").

## Significance

The Janissaries were the **institutional embodiment of Ottoman centralized power** — a
slave-soldier corps that gave the early sultans a reliable standing army independent of the
provincial cavalry and the great families. Their transformation in the seventeenth century is a
key index of the Ottoman [[ottoman-empire|"Times of Trouble"]]: the same military and fiscal
pressures that expanded them also undid the slave-household system and shifted power away from
the throne.
