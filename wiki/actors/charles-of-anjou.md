# Charles of Anjou, Charles I of Sicily (1226–1285)

**Charles of Anjou**, youngest brother of **[[louis-ix-capetian|Saint Louis]]**, was the
great Capetian cadet whose ambition reshaped the thirteenth-century Mediterranean: count of
**Provence**, conqueror of the **[[kingdom-of-sicily|kingdom of Sicily]]**, and would-be
master of a Mediterranean empire, until the **Sicilian Vespers** broke his power.
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] introduces him across several chapters (Jordan on the
Capetians, Cox on Provence; his Italian kingdom is treated at length in the volume's Italian
and Mediterranean parts).

## Provence and the Conquest of Sicily

Charles gained **Provence** by his marriage (1246) to **Beatrice**, heiress of Count
Ramon-Berenguer V — a match arranged by Blanche of Castile and Louis IX to keep the county
out of hostile or heretical hands. He ruled it firmly, completing its **centralised
administration** (seneschal, *chambre des comptes*) and subduing autonomous **Marseilles**
(1257–1262), and drew it "more forcefully than ever into the world of Italian politics and
Mediterranean commerce."

When the papacy, after long seeking a prince to expel the Hohenstaufen from Sicily, offered
him the crown, Charles accepted; Louis IX permitted him to use his French apanage revenues to
raise an army. By **1266** he had displaced the Hohenstaufen, defeating and killing **Manfred**
at Benevento and (1268) executing the young **Conradin** — extinguishing the male Hohenstaufen
line (see [[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen]]).

## The Sicilian Vespers and Its Repercussions

Charles imposed an "authoritarian regime" on Sicily; its "burdensome taxation and heavy-handed
repression" provoked the rebellion of the **[[sicilian-vespers-1282|Sicilian Vespers]] (1282)**.
**Peter III of Aragon**,
whose wife had Hohenstaufen claims, intervened on the rebels' behalf, leading the pope to
proclaim a "crusade" against Aragon — into which **[[philip-iv-capetian|Philip III]] of France**
was drawn (the disastrous **Aragonese Crusade**, 1285). The settlement eventually left the
**island of Sicily** to the Aragonese and **mainland southern Italy** ("the Regno") to Charles's
Angevin heirs. The Vespers also wrecked grander Angevin designs — including the plan, backed by
the papacy, for a revived **kingdom of Arles** under Charles's grandson (see [[house-of-savoy]]).

## Significance

Charles embodies the **outward, Mediterranean thrust** of the Capetian world that David
Abulafia makes central to the volume: a French prince who became an Italian and Mediterranean
sovereign, his ambitions stretching from Provence to Sicily to claims on Constantinople. His
overreach and the Vespers turned the western Mediterranean into a long Angevin–Aragonese
contest, and his "innumerable enemies" — made by "imperiousness in any number of environments"
— illustrate the costs of a personal empire built faster than it could be consolidated. His
ambitions reached the **Balkans** (an Albanian "kingdom," Durazzo) and **Byzantium** (a planned
conquest of Constantinople, aborted by the Vespers), and he bought the crown of **Jerusalem**
(1277). His Italian kingdom is treated further under [[kingdom-of-sicily]].
