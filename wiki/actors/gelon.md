# Gelon of Syracuse

**Gelon** (*Gelōn*, c. 540–478 BCE), of the priestly **Deinomenid** family of Gela,
was the **tyrant who united most of Greek Sicily** under [[syracuse|Syracuse]] and,
with Theron of Acragas, won the **battle of [[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]] (480)**
over Carthage. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 16, Asheri) presents him as
the maker of Syracuse's greatness and the supreme example of
[[western-greek-tyranny|Sicilian tyranny]] — remembered as "benefactor, saviour,
liberator" though his rule rested on conquest and mass deportation.

## Rise: from cavalry commander to tyrant of two cities

Gelon inherited the **Geloan "empire"** built by **Hippocrates of Gela** (whose
cavalry he had commanded). On Hippocrates' death (c. 491/0) he suppressed the
Geloans' bid for freedom and **made himself tyrant of Gela**. His early position was
shaky — Anaxilas of Rhegium seized Zancle (refounding it as **Messana**) and the
north-east was lost — but he recovered through the **alliance with the Emmenid Theron
of Acragas**, cemented by intermarriage (Gelon married Theron's daughter
**Damareta**). In **485** the exiled landed oligarchs (*gamoroi*) of Syracuse, driven
out by a coalition of *demos* and Sicel **Killyrian** serfs, appealed to him; Gelon
brought them home, **took Syracuse**, and **moved his capital there**, leaving Gela
to his brother **Hieron**.

## A new kind of power: deportation and synoecism

Gelon made Syracuse "the richest city in Sicily and one of the most populous in the
whole Greek world" by a policy new to the Greeks but familiar from the **Assyrians,
Babylonians and Persians**: the wholesale **transfer of populations**. He **destroyed
or depopulated three Greek cities** — **Camarina**, **Megara Hyblaea** and **Euboea**
— deporting their wealthy (*pacheis*) to Syracuse as citizens and **selling the
common *demos* into slavery**, and moved more than half of his own Gela there too.
Asheri's pointed verdict: more than two generations before any Carthaginian army
destroyed a Greek city in Sicily, this "true panhellenic Greek tyrant" had already
annihilated three. He **restored the landed oligarchy** rather than the *demos* —
Sicilian tyranny aimed at "restoration, not revolution" (see
[[western-greek-tyranny]]).

## Himera and the height of his rule

In **481** Gelon rebuffed the mainland Greeks' plea for help against Xerxes (demanding
overall command as an impossible price) and hedged by sending Cadmus to offer Xerxes
submission should Persia win. His real war came in **480**, when Theron's seizure of
Himera provoked the Carthaginian expedition of **Hamilcar**. At
**[[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]]** Gelon's cavalry fired the Punic camp and killed
Hamilcar, and his army won the field — a victory his court at once mythologized
(Pindar linking it with Salamis and Plataea) and turned to a brilliant building and
coinage programme. He died in **478**, honoured with a **public funeral and heroic
cult**: "all his sins were expiated forever on the battlefield of Himera."

## Counterfactual significance

Gelon's analytical importance is as the man who concentrated the scattered, faction-
ridden Greek cities of Sicily into a **single dominant power centred on Syracuse** —
the template for Dionysius I and the later Sicilian hegemonies — and who showed that
**Near-Eastern statecraft** (deportation, mercenary armies, dynastic marriage) could
be wielded by a Greek tyrant. Without his unification, the divided Sicilian Greeks
might have faced Carthage as the disunited [[etruscans|Etruscans]] faced Rome.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **Geloan empire** of Hippocrates that he inherited; the **civil strife**
of oligarchs, *demos* and serfs that opened Syracuse to him.
produced: the **Deinomenid hegemony** over Greek Sicily; the demographic remaking and
enrichment of [[syracuse|Syracuse]]; the victory and moderate peace of
[[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]].

## Historiography

Gelon reaches us through **Herodotus** (VII.153–67, sharply critical of his evasion in
481), the lost **Sicilian historians** (Timaeus and his "super-patriotic" tradition),
**Diodorus/Ephorus**, and the **court poetry of Pindar and Simonides** that built the
saviour-myth. Asheri stresses the gap between the **panegyric memory** (benefactor,
liberator) and the **record of conquest, enslavement and deportation** — and warns
that the Himera-Salamis synchronism and the Perso-Punic "joint onslaught" are products
of that propaganda, not history.
