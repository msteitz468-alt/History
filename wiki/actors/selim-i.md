# Selim I ("the Grim") (r. 1512–1520)

The Ottoman sultan whose eight-year reign **doubled the size of the empire** — conqueror of
the Arab lands and the Holy Cities, and the ruler who made the Ottoman sultan the foremost
monarch of Sunni Islam. From Colin Imber's
[[imber-ottoman-empire-1300-1650|*The Ottoman Empire, 1300–1650*]] and Alan Mikhail's
[[mikhail-gods-shadow|*God's Shadow*]].

## The Safavid war and Çaldıran (1514)

Having seized the throne and (per Ottoman custom) eliminated his brothers, Selim turned on the
new **Safavid** power in Iran. He obtained a **fatwa declaring Shah Ismail and his followers
heretics** whose destruction was obligatory — "a new Ottoman claim to be defenders of Sunni
Islam" against [[safavid-empire|Safavid]] Shi'ism. In August 1514 he won an overwhelming
victory at **Çaldıran** in Azerbaijan, the Ottoman firearms and artillery decisive against the
Safavid cavalry; he briefly took Tabriz before the [[janissaries]] forced a winter retreat.
Over the next two years he expelled the Safavids from eastern Anatolia and secured the
allegiance of the **Kurdish chieftains** (through the scholar Idris of Bitlis). Çaldıran
convinced the Safavids to abandon westward expansion and shelter behind the hostile border
terrain.

## The conquest of the Mamluk lands (1516–17)

Selim then turned south against the **[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk Sultanate]]**, which had
sought a Safavid alliance against him. At **Marj Dabiq** north of Aleppo (August 1516) he
routed the Mamluks — their sultan Qansuh al-Ghawri dying in the battle — and occupied Syria
almost unopposed. In January 1517 he crossed the Sinai and destroyed the Mamluks at
**Raydaniyya** outside Cairo; after street fighting and a last stand at Giza, the public
execution of the last Mamluk sultan **Tumanbay** marked the beginning of Ottoman rule in
Egypt.

## The man and his rise (Mikhail)

Alan Mikhail's [[mikhail-gods-shadow|*God's Shadow*]] gives the fuller biography. Selim was an
**unlikely sultan** — the fourth of [[ottoman-empire|Bayezid II's]] ten sons, born in 1470 to
an enslaved concubine in a provincial Anatolian town, never expected to rule. As governor of
**Trabzon** he learned war and statecraft on the empire's volatile north-eastern edge
(against the Safavids and in the Caucasus). Monomaniacally set on power, he **forced his way to
the throne**: he was among the first non-firstborn sons to become sultan and the **first to
depose a sitting sultan** (his own father, 1512), then **killed his brothers** to secure the
throne. His cruelty earned him the name ***Yavuz*** ("the Grim"): "ferocious and cunning … a
warmonger," kicking the severed heads of the executed — the dark side of the same decisiveness
that doubled the empire.

## Significance

In eight years Selim added **eastern and south-eastern Anatolia, Syria, Lebanon, Palestine,
Egypt, and the Hejaz**. Acquiring the guardianship of the Holy Cities of **Mecca, Medina, and
Jerusalem** gave the Ottoman sultan **primacy among Islamic monarchs** and substance to the
claim to defend Islamic orthodoxy against Safavid heresy — the foundation of the later Ottoman
claim to the **caliphate**. He opened an Ottoman outlet to the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean
(setting up the long contest with the Portuguese), and bequeathed to his only son
[[suleiman-the-magnificent|Süleyman]] a peaceful succession and a doubled empire.

Mikhail presses a strong claim for Selim's **world-historical importance**: he was the first to
rule a **majority-Muslim Ottoman Empire** and the **first to hold both sultanate and
caliphate**, making Sunnism central to Ottoman identity and weaponizing it against the Safavids
(deepening the Sunni–Shia divide) and the Mamluks. For Mikhail, Selim — not even his famous son
[[suleiman-the-magnificent|Süleyman]] — was "the most influential of the line of Osman's …
sultans," "God's Shadow on Earth," whose reign spanned "the most consequential half-century in
world history." Mikhail's still bolder claims — that the Ottoman threat under Selim drove
European overseas expansion and catalyzed the Reformation — are **contested**; see
[[ottoman-impact-on-european-expansion]].
