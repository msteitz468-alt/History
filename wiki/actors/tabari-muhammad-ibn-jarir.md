## Overview

**Muhammad b. Jarir al-Tabari** (d. 923) was the pre-eminent scholar of 'Abbasid Islam — simultaneously the greatest Qur'an commentator, the most important universal historian, and a major independent legal theorist of his age. He was "a great Jama'i-Sunni legist" (Hodgson) who came from Tabaristan (the south coastland of the Caspian Sea) to Baghdad, where he taught for decades until his death. He developed his own independent legal system (*madhhab*), which later fell out of use but was in his own time widely respected.

## Scholarly Profile

Al-Tabari's ambitions were encyclopedic. His primary interest "lay in building a more perfect system of fiqh; in the course of this, he created the most substantial of all Qur'an commentaries, in which he collected the chief interpretations of every discussable verse, and gave his own point of view so soberly that it came to be very widely accepted. His history was part of the same overall effort" (Hodgson).

The three major works:

1. **Jami' al-Bayan** (Commentary on the Qur'an): A monumental collection of interpretations for each Qur'anic verse, organized by isnad-documented report; his own positions stated with careful sobriety.
2. **Ta'rikh al-Rusul wa'l-Muluk** (History of the Prophets and Kings): A universal history from creation through al-Tabari's own time, constructed on verbatim hadith reports with full isnad documentation.
3. **Kitab Ikhtilaf al-Fuqaha'** (Book of the Disagreement of the Jurists): A survey of differences among the fiqh schools, reflecting his independent legal analysis.

## The Verbatim Historical Method

Al-Tabari's history is the most sustained example of applying the [[hadith]] methodology to general historical writing. He "rarely speaks in his own voice, except in jejune frame or transition passages. What he has to say is told purely by judicious selection, arrangement, and documentation of verbatim reports which he has received" (Hodgson).

This method has both weaknesses and strengths:

**Weaknesses**: The same event may be narrated multiple times from different reporters; the overall narrative can appear confused to a reader who does not already know the outline. Al-Tabari "cannot make his own conclusions explicit."

**Strengths**: Strict isnad documentation allows accuracy of detail. The vivacity of the original reports is preserved. The writer avoids committing himself publicly on disputed questions — "to suggest two contradictory conclusions at once to two different sorts of people" (Hodgson).

Hodgson offers a detailed analysis of Tabari's treatment of the death of 'Uthman (the third caliph), showing how al-Tabari's arrangement of contradictory verbatim reports itself carried a sophisticated argument inaccessible to the naive reader but legible to the subtle one:

> "Tabari does not even pretend to give here an exact reconstruction of events. All his main sources had weaknesses of which he was himself aware... Yet at Tabari's hands the death of 'Uthman, supreme occasion for fitnah, trial, in the Muslim community, becomes a perfect occasion for showing the naivete of a conventional response and suggesting, instead, what the true Shar'i response must be." (Hodgson)

The "true Shar'i response" Tabari implies: rather than depending on the caliph to enforce justice (which 'Uthman demonstrably could not), every individual Muslim is responsible for "commanding the right and forbidding the wrong" as far as he can — while the independently developed Shari'ah, upheld by a responsible Muslim population, is what must substitute for the inadequacies of any given caliph.

## Historical Significance

Al-Tabari's history became, as Hodgson notes, the decisive source for modern historians of the early Islamic period: "it is only since the discovery of Tabari's work (as Gibb pointed out) that modern historians have begun to be able to reconstruct the periods he dealt with. Tabari meant it that way."

His political independence was tested: "his viewpoints were sufficiently free that the zealously hadith-minded faction then dominant in the city distrusted his loyalty to what they regarded as proper Sunni doctrine, and at one time he barely missed lynching" (Hodgson). He taught his own fiqh system, independent of the four canonical madhhabs, and was willing to include positions not fully acceptable to the populistic Hadith folk.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without al-Tabari's history, modern reconstruction of the High Caliphate and the early Islamic centuries would be vastly more difficult. He was the conduit through which enormous quantities of earlier material, including reports from historians of the Marwani period, were preserved with something close to their original wording.
