# Gregory I (the Great, r. 590–604)

## Overview

**Gregory I** ("the Great") was bishop of Rome from 590 to 604 and the pivotal early-medieval pope who set much of the institutional and missionary stage for the Latin church. From a senatorial Roman family, he abandoned public office for monastic life, served as papal legate in Constantinople, and was elected pope under the shadow of Lombard war, plague, and famine in Italy. Medieval tradition later numbered him among the four Latin Doctors of the Church.

## Role

- **Pastoral and administrative model.** His *Regula pastoralis* and extensive letter-register defined the duties of bishops and the practical government of the Roman church's estates and diplomacy.
- **Mission.** He launched the **[[augustine-of-canterbury|Gregorian mission]]** to the Anglo-Saxons (596–597), linking Rome to the conversion of England and, through English missionaries, to the later conversion of parts of the Continent ([[willibrord]], [[boniface]]).
- **Bridge figure.** In [[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]]'s framing, the years from Gregory's accession to Boniface's death (754) "fashion the set" on which the medieval Latin church would play: an active papacy and a Christian population across most of western Europe except Scandinavia.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Gregory's mission impulse and his articulation of papal pastoral authority, the Anglo-Saxon church's Roman orientation — and the later English mission to the Continent that reinforced the Franco-papal axis — would have taken a different shape, if it occurred at all in the same form.

## Historiography

Primary base: Gregory's *Register* of letters, *Dialogues*, *Moralia in Job*, and *Regula pastoralis*; Bede for the English mission. Modern surveys (Logan; standard papal histories) treat him as the hinge between late-antique Rome and the early medieval papacy rather than as the inventor of "Gregorian" chant (a later attribution). Collection coverage is strong via Late Antiquity / Early Middle Ages reference series.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan ranks Gregory among the strongest candidates for greatest medieval pope (while noting that “greatness” tracks historians’ values). Biographical core: distinguished Roman family (great-grandfather Felix III; collateral link to Agapitus I); almost certainly prefect of Rome in 572; after his father’s death (574) founded six Sicilian monasteries and turned the Caelian house into St Andrew’s, living as monk-founder rather than abbot; adopted *servus servorum dei*. Political setting: Justinianic wars and Pragmatic Sanction left Italy under a Ravenna exarch; Lombards held the north and Spoleto/Benevento, leaving Rome on the Via Flaminia corridor. As deacon under Pelagius II, Gregory’s Constantinopolitan mission (c.579–86) failed to secure military aid. Elected pope amid plague without waiting for imperial consent. Surviving **register of 854 letters** shows quasi-civil rule: troops against Spoleto’s duke; appointees when the exarch refused; wages and 500 pounds of gold for peace from church funds; food supply; Lombard negotiation culminating in general peace with the new exarch (598). Exigency-driven secular rule became a defining trait of the medieval papacy (tomb inscription: “Consul of God”). *Pastoral Care* became a classic (later translated by King Alfred); with the *Dialogues* it placed him among the Western Fathers—though “Gregorian chant” is a later misattribution. Mission background: the Whitby Angles/angels story is charming but probably unhistorical; behind it lies a September 595 order to buy English slave boys of 17–18 in Gaul for monastic training. Acculturation policy for England (Bede, HE 1.30): do not destroy temples wholesale; convert shrines; substitute Christian festivals—“step by step.” Classical learning should serve the faith; practical, unnuanced Latin; never learned Greek in six years at Constantinople. Further reading flags Clark’s challenge to *Dialogues* authorship and Meyvaert’s rebuttal (*JEH* 1988).

## Gibbon, *Decline and Fall* (1776–1788) — Ch. XLV

[[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776|Gibbon]]’s Gregory is the edifying pontiff whose mixed virtues “happily suited” the temper of the times.

- **Character:** “a singular mixture of simplicity and cunning, of pride and humility, of sense and superstition”; condemns “universal bishop” title at Constantinople yet limited in practice.
- **British mission:** “the conquest of Britain reflects less glory on the name of Cæsar, than on that of Gregory the First” — forty monks baptize the king of Kent and ~10,000 Anglo-Saxons within two years.
- **Context (cross-link):** Lombard-era papal governance and later iconoclastic severing of Italy from Byzantine loyalty are developed on [[papacy]] and [[byzantine-iconoclasm]].
