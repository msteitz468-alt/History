# Zoscales

**Zoscales** is named in the mid-first-century CE *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* as the king of the coastal region around [[adulis|Adulis]], described as "miserly in his ways and always striving for more, but otherwise upright and acquainted with Greek letters." He is the earliest named ruler associated with the Aksumite Red Sea zone.

## Overview

The *Periplus* (Periplus 5, Casson 1989 edition) is a practical trading handbook compiled in Greek by an anonymous Egyptian merchant, dated broadly to the mid-first century CE. It mentions Zoscales in the context of Adulis-based trade but never explicitly states that he ruled at [[aksum-city|Aksum]] itself. The conventional identification of Zoscales as "king of Aksum" — accepted widely in older scholarship — is rejected by Phillipson (*Foundations*, 2012), following Dillmann (1879), Kirwan (1979), Huntingford (1980), and Fauvelle-Aymar (2009).

Phillipson's argument: the *Periplus* text, carefully read in Greek, places Zoscales' authority in the coastal region centred on Adulis, not in the interior highland capital. The *Periplus* does mention Aksum (by name) as a market city inland, but does not identify Zoscales as its ruler. This reading is consistent with evidence for independent kingdoms in the eastern plateau and coastal region — including the inscriptions RIE 277 (from a marble throne at Adulis, attributed by Fauvelle-Aymar to a coastal ruler of the first to second century CE) and RIE 183 (Safra inscription, c. third century, mentioning a minor kingdom "dwly"). Together these suggest that, in the mid-first century CE, the coastal area around Adulis was administered by a distinct polity not yet subsumed within Aksumite hegemony.

Zoscales' good knowledge of Greek — noted by the *Periplus* — fits a coastal ruler engaged directly in Red Sea trade; it does not require identification with an inland highland king. Aksumite kings began issuing Greek-inscribed coinage only c. AD 270 under Endybis.

## Role and Major Decisions

Zoscales' political decisions are unknown. The *Periplus* treats him as the legitimate political authority with whom Red Sea merchants negotiated, capable of controlling trade access at Adulis. His name is not attested in any inscription, coin, or other corroborating source.

## Counterfactual Significance

Limited — Zoscales is documented only in this single source and cannot be traced in the archaeological or inscriptional record. His significance lies mainly as evidence for a pre-Aksumite or proto-Aksumite polity on the Red Sea coast at the time when the Aksumite state was consolidating in the highlands.

## Historiography

Scholarly treatment of Zoscales divides into two camps:

1. **Traditional view** (majority of older scholarship): Zoscales was king of Aksum and the *Periplus*' reference to him demonstrates Aksumite dominance of the Red Sea coast by the mid-first century CE.

2. **Coastal-ruler view** (Dillmann 1879, Kirwan 1979, Huntingford 1980, Fauvelle-Aymar 2009, Phillipson 2012): Zoscales ruled a coastal polity centred on Adulis, distinct from and not yet subject to the highland Aksumite state. This reading is consistent with the absence of his name from any Aksumite inscription or king-list and with evidence for other small independent kingdoms in the region before full Aksumite hegemony.

Phillipson notes that the oldest surviving manuscript of the *Periplus* (Heidelberg, early tenth century) has been described as containing a "plethora of mistakes," and that later additions or alterations to the text cannot be fully ruled out — though the mid-first century CE date for the initial compilation is broadly accepted.
