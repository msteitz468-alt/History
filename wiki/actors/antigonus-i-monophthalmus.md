## Role

Antigonus I "Monophthalmus" ("the One-Eyed," 382–301 BCE) was the most formidable of
[[alexander-the-great|Alexander's]] [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|Successors]] and the one
who came closest to reconstituting the whole empire. Installed at Babylon as satrap of
Greater Phrygia, he rose after [[perdiccas|Perdiccas']] death — when at Triparadisus he
was made *strategos of Asia* and charged with destroying [[eumenes-of-cardia|Eumenes]] —
to become, for fifteen years, the embodiment of the **unitary idea** on his own account.
Will calls him "one of the Diadochi best understood through his actions," recalling "the
tireless energy of Alexander and... the political realism and cunning of
[[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II]]."

## Decisions and Significance

- **Mastery of Asia (321–316)**: he overran Anatolia in defiance of Triparadisus,
  besieged Eumenes in Nora, and finally destroyed him (316), gaining control from the
  Aegean to Iran and driving [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus]] out of Babylon — which
  united all the others against him.
- **"Freedom of the Greeks"**: his **Tyre manifesto** (315) proclaimed the Greek cities
  free, autonomous, and ungarrisoned — the propaganda theme, henceforth recurring for
  two centuries, that Will analyses as the central political problem of the age (the
  place of the *polis* within territorial monarchy). He patronised the
  Confederation of the Nesiotes (Cycladic islanders) and, in 302, refounded the
  [[league-of-corinth|Hellenic League]] at Corinth with his son.
- **The royal title (306)**: after [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius']] naval victory
  at Salamis-in-Cyprus, Antigonus was first of the Successors to assume the title
  *basileus* — claiming Alexander's *whole* legacy, and associating his son as
  co-king to found a dynasty. This triggered the answering royal proclamations of
  Ptolemy, Lysimachus, Seleucus, and Cassander: the birth of the
  [[hellenistic-monarchy|Hellenistic monarchies]].
- **Failure and death**: the failed [[siege-of-rhodes-305|siege of Rhodes]] (305–304)
  and the costly invasions of Egypt checked him; in 301 the grand coalition destroyed
  him at [[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]], where the over-eighty king fell on the field.

## Counterfactual Significance

Antigonus is the great "what if" of the period: had he won at Ipsus, a single
Antigonid empire spanning Asia and the Aegean might have replaced the three-kingdom
system. His death is, for Will, the moment the unitary idea finally dies and "the real
history of the Hellenistic states" — territorial states with no claim to universality —
can begin. His line survived through Demetrius to rule [[antigonid-dynasty|Macedon]].
