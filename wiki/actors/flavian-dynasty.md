# The Flavian Dynasty (69–96 CE)

The dynasty of **[[vespasian|Vespasian]]** and his sons **[[titus|Titus]]** and
**[[domitian|Domitian]]** — the **Italian municipal house** that emerged from the civil war of 69
to refound the Principate, restore stability after Nero, and broaden the imperial system toward
the provincial elites. The bridge between the Julio-Claudians and the "Five Good Emperors."
Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 1.

## A new kind of dynasty

The Flavians answered the crisis the [[year-of-the-four-emperors-69ce|Year of the Four
Emperors]] had exposed: that the Julio-Claudian system "had no formal method of designating a
successor" and could not contain the army's power to make emperors. Vespasian — of undistinguished
Sabine stock — could not claim the Republican aristocracy's prestige, so the dynasty's legitimacy
rested instead on **military victory** (the Jewish War), **dynastic harmony** (the *concordia* of
a father and two grown sons), and ostentatious **continuity with the "respectable" Augustan
tradition** (Augustus, Tiberius, Claudius cited as precedents; the name **Caesar** adopted as an
institution; unoriginal coinage advertising *Pax* and *Roma Resurgens*). CAH XI's framing
question — "what was the correct image for the *princeps* who was in fact, but not in theory, a
monarch?" — runs through the whole reign.

## The three reigns

1. **[[vespasian|Vespasian]]** (69–79) — the founder; restored solvency and the frontiers, began
   the **Colosseum** and the **Temple of Peace**, and engineered a secure succession.
2. **[[titus|Titus]]** (79–81) — Vespasian's elder son and *particeps imperii*; the conqueror of
   **Jerusalem (70)**; a brief, popular reign overshadowed by the **eruption of Vesuvius (79)**
   and a fire and plague at Rome.
3. **[[domitian|Domitian]]** (81–96) — the younger son; an autocratic and able administrator whose
   conflict with the Senate made him, in the hostile tradition, the archetypal tyrant; his
   **assassination (96)** ended the dynasty and opened the way to the adoptive emperors.

## Significance

The Flavians **stabilized and institutionalized** the Principate after the Julio-Claudian
collapse. They demonstrated that the imperial office could pass to a wholly new family and **down
a bloodline by peaceful succession** (Vespasian to Titus, the first such handover); they broadened
the governing class by drawing in Italian and **western provincial elites** (the *adlectio* into
the Senate, the grant of Latin right to Spain); and they laid the administrative, fiscal, and
monumental foundations on which the **Antonine "golden age"** was built. Domitian's murder,
however, showed that the unresolved problem of the *princeps'* relationship with the Senate
remained — solved only temporarily by the **adoptive succession** of Nerva and Trajan.
