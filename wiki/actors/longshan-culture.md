# Longshan Culture

The **Longshan culture** is the late-Neolithic black-pottery horizon of the middle and
lower [[yellow-river-valley|Yellow River valley]], flourishing in the **third millennium
BC** (the book's main focus is the late Longshan, **~2600–2000 BC**). It is the analytic
heart of [[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Li Liu's *The Chinese Neolithic*]]: the "platform
for fundamental social change" on the eve of the first Chinese states, and the period in
which walled towns, mortuary hierarchy, warfare, craft specialisation, and the first
copper/bronze all appear. The Longshan is **not a single people** but a constructed
archaeological category whose meaning has shifted continuously with new ceramic finds (see
[[nationalism-and-archaeology]], [[quxi-leixing]]).

## Definition and Construction of the Category

The culture was named after the type site at **Longshan, Licheng, Shandong**, discovered by
**Wu Jinding in 1928**; its first major excavated site was Chengziyai. Identified initially
by its distinctive **black pottery**, the Longshan was at first thought to have arisen in
Shandong **independently of the painted-pottery [[yangshao-culture|Yangshao]]** and to be
the foundation of Shang civilisation (Li Chi 1934). Interpretations then changed in step
with discoveries:

- By the **early 1960s**, the sequences at **Miaodigou (Shanxian)** and **Wangwan
  (Luoyang)** proved the Longshan was **chronologically later** than the Yangshao, which
  developed into it through an intermediate phase (Miaodigou II).
- By the **1970s** it was recognised that regional Longshan cultures derived from
  **different antecedents**: the Shandong Longshan from the [[dawenkou-culture|Dawenkou]],
  the Henan/Shanxi Longshan from the Yangshao via Miaodigou II.
- In **1981, Yan** proposed treating the regional variants as **separate cultures** and
  introduced the term **"Longshan period"** for the era. Three major provincial variants
  are named: **Shandong Longshan** ("Typical Longshan"), **Henan Longshan** (Hougang II),
  and **Shaanxi Longshan** (Keshengzhuang II). Liu recognises fourteen spatial/chronological
  divisions.

## Diagnostic Traits and Social Character

Several traits mark the Longshan as a new stage: possible writing, copper/bronze for small
implements and ornaments, **rammed-earth town walls**, widespread **violence and warfare**,
**hierarchical burials**, and intensified regional interaction. Site density peaked in the
late Neolithic — over **1,000 Longshan sites in Shandong**, ~2,200 in Shaanxi, ~1,000 in
Henan. Settlement organisation acquires three new features: **compound-like house clusters,
systems of walled settlements, and very large settlements (>200 ha)** — only two exceed 200
ha: [[taosi|Taosi]] (300 ha, Shanxi) and Liangchengzhen (246 ha, Shandong).

Liu classes Longshan polities as **[[chiefdom|chiefdoms]]** (after Earle): regionally
organised, with heritable ranking, but **small and cyclical** — they rose and collapsed
without producing an internally specialised ruling apparatus. Their social range runs from
transegalitarian/rank societies (Yuchisi, Kangjia) to genuine chiefdoms (Guchengzhai,
Taosi). Mortuary practice diverges sharply by region: the eastern (Dawenkou-derived)
Longshan of Shandong (Chengzi, Zhufeng) shows **pyramidal burial hierarchies** with
ercengtai ledges, eggshell goblets, pig mandibles, and **hereditary male elites over
several generations**; the Central Plains Longshan mostly buried its dead near houses with
few goods, channelling social energy instead into **public buildings and rammed-earth
walls**. Long-distance exchange of elite ritual goods (alligator drums carried ~500 km to
Taosi; standardised jade *cong*/*yu* forms shared with the [[liangzhu-culture|Liangzhu]]
and northern Shaanxi) bound elites across regions.

## Decline and Transition

The Longshan declined around **2000 BC**, followed by the [[erlitou-culture|Erlitou
culture]] in Henan (linked to the [[xia-dynasty|Xia]]) and the Yueshi culture in Shandong
(both ~1900–1500 BC). In Shandong the Yueshi marks collapse — site numbers fall to **14%**
of Longshan levels and eggshell pottery disappears. Crucially, the first state emerged not
from the most complex Longshan chiefdom (Taosi, which collapsed) but from the **less
integrated competing polities of central Henan and the [[yiluo-basin|Yiluo basin]]** (see
[[early-china-state-formation]]).

## Historiography

The Longshan is the textbook case of how an archaeological "culture" is built and rebuilt
around ceramic typology and national-history concerns (see [[nationalism-and-archaeology]],
[[quxi-leixing]], and the controversy [[origins-chinese-civilization-monocentric-vs-multiregional]]).
Liu's contribution is to read the Longshan through **settlement and mortuary patterning**
rather than artifact checklists, foregrounding regional variability and political cycling.
Coverage in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina is now strong for early China:
[[liu-chinese-neolithic-2004|Liu (2004)]] supplies the settlement-archaeology spine, the
[[cambridge-history-of-ancient-china|Cambridge History of Ancient China]] the textual/art
backbone, and [[maisels-early-civilizations-of-the-old-world-1999|Maisels (1999)]] the
comparative framing. Note that Liu predates the full publication of Liangzhu and Shimao, so
her peripheral data are dated in places.
