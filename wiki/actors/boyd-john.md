# John Boyd

## Overview

John Boyd (1927–1997) was a U.S. Air Force fighter pilot turned strategist whose work revived serious thinking about regular warfare after the Vietnam-era retreat of the civilian strategists. "Colonel John Boyd, an American fighter pilot with experience going back to the Korean War, wrote the definitive manual on the subject" of the aerial dogfight — "one of the most elemental and iconic encounters of contemporary warfare" — and from it "hit upon an insight which he developed into a formula of considerable influence": the [[ooda-loop|OODA loop]].

## Strategic Thought

Boyd's starting point was empirical, born of the air war over Vietnam: "Boyd began with the premise that the U.S. air force had become too preoccupied with speed." Obsolete Soviet MiGs "were prevailing in dogfights because they were more maneuverable," and "after an intensive analysis of the competing aircraft, Boyd concluded that the key quality was not absolute speed but agility." The most responsive fighter "would be able to get on to the opponent's tail, ready for the kill."

He generalized this into the **OODA loop** — observation, orientation, decision, action — applicable "to any situation in which it was necessary to keep or gain the initiative." The aim "was always to disorient the opponent, who would be unable to grasp a situation developing more quickly than anticipated and in unexpected ways and thus paralyzed into indecision." Crucially, he "came to appreciate the pervasive importance of orientation." See [[ooda-loop|OODA Loop]].

The autodidactic Boyd "read widely and moved from his engineering background into mathematical theory and on into history and the social sciences," drawing on Gödel, Heisenberg, the second law of thermodynamics, cybernetics, and complexity theory. His basic conclusion was the need "to deny the adversary the possibility of uncovering or discerning patterns that match our activity, or other aspects of reality in the world." "The lasting importance of Boyd's work lay in the focus on disrupting the enemy's decision-making, encouraging uncertainty and confusion."

Boyd distinguished **attrition warfare** — "focused on the physical domain and using firepower as a destructive force" — from **maneuver warfare** — "focused on the mental domain where the aim was to generate 'surprise and shock' by using ambiguity, mobility, and deception." His key historical example was the 1940 Battle of France, which prompted his "'Blitzkrieg vs. Maginot Line mentality'": "French decision-making was paralyzed as the Germans worked out how to operate inside their OODA loops." One key to German success "was the readiness to delegate," with tactical commanders free to "realize the mission in their own way." On the essence of moral conflict, Boyd insisted the goal was to "create, exploit, and magnify menace... uncertainty... and mistrust." Yet he also conceded that "courage, confidence, and esprit" constituted "moral strength" that could counter such effects — "the imaginative physical effects designed to cause a moral breakdown would fail."

## In Freedman's argument

Boyd is, for Freedman, the figure who "set the terms" for the information-age exploitation of war: "By the time he died in 1997, the revolution in information and communication technologies was well underway. Boyd had set the terms for its military exploitation." His "prescriptions that flowed from analysis" of moral warfare were, Freedman notes pointedly, "largely derivative of the post-Napoleonic classics and of Fuller and [[liddell-hart-basil|Liddell Hart]]" — Boyd belongs to the strand "urging a return to the military classics of the modern era, but with a postmodern twist resulting from their heightened sensitivity to cognitive processes."

Boyd "never produced a definitive text of his own. His basic ideas are contained in several hundred slides entitled *Discourse on Winning & Losing*," which "formed the basis of briefings given over almost two decades to numerous audiences, including most of the senior figures in the American defense establishment." (The widely-cited brief "Patterns of Conflict" was the core of this body of slides.) His impact "was accentuated because they were spread by enthusiastic acolytes."

His influence ran directly into doctrine. William Lind picked up "Boyd's dichotomy between attritional and maneuver warfare, using the Maginot Line versus Blitzkrieg analogy," and "within five years the reformers had apparently won the argument, with the adoption of the doctrine of Air Land Battle in 1982." The U.S. Army field manual's stress on "initiative, depth, agility and synchronization" was "in line with Boyd's determination to get inside the enemy's OODA loop"; in 1989 the Marine Corps issued FMFM-1 basing its doctrine on "warfare by maneuver." Through this maneuver-warfare doctrine, Boyd shaped the U.S. way of war that produced the swift victory of the 1991 Gulf War.

Freedman is not uncritical: the doctrine was "sharpened, almost to the point of caricature," and the assumption "that just as physical blows could shatter bodies, so mental blows could shatter minds" overreached — "human minds could be capable of remarkable feats of denial, resistance, recovery, and adaption, even under extreme stress."
