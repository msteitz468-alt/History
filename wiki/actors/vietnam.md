# Vietnam

The easternmost land of mainland Southeast Asia, the core of **[[french-indochina|French Indochina]]**
(with Laos and Cambodia). French conquest in the later nineteenth century provoked a long anti-colonial
resistance that culminated in [[ho-chi-minh|Ho Chi Minh]]'s Viet Minh, the August Revolution of 1945,
and the defeat of France at Dien Bien Phu (1954).

Partition at Geneva produced a communist North and a US-backed South, and the catastrophic
[[vietnam-war|Vietnam War]], ending in reunification under Hanoi in 1975. Vietnam exemplifies the
Southeast Asian arc from colonialism through revolutionary nationalism to the modern nation-state.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

Miksic and Goh frame premodern Vietnam as a polity shaped by Chinese colonial experience and continuous
threat, producing denser provincial administration and earlier patrilineal succession than neighbouring
Seasian mandalas. By the late eleventh century [[thang-long|Hanoi/Thang Long]] was a permanent capital
with provinces and prefectures; by the fourteenth, Vietnamese elites looked on other Seasian mandalas as
"disorganized." The Red River (Songkoi) delta and coastal strip (~10,000 km², the Tonkin subregion) were
exceptionally densely settled, while the Mekong Delta remained sparse until much later; Vietnamese
culture rarely climbed above 100 m elevation and almost never above 200 m.

**Independence sequence.** The Tang Protectorate of Annam ("Pacified South," from 679) had its HQ south
of the Red River at Hanoi; eight provinces later formed the core of independent Vietnam. Walls were
repeatedly rebuilt (overrun 767 by overseas invaders, probably Indonesian; renamed Dai La, "big wall,"
perimeter 6.4 km, height 7.5 m). After Nanzhao's 862 invasion and Tang garrison withdrawal (880), the
Khuc family dominated the plain. Ngo Quyen's defeat of Southern Han at the Bach Dang River (938, iron-
tipped stakes) and Bo Linh's proclamation of Dai Co Viet (965) mark the conventional foundation of
independent Vietnam; power later reconcentrated around Dai La and the Red River core that became
[[dai-viet|Dai Viet]] under the [[ly-dynasty-vietnam|Ly]] and [[tran-dynasty-vietnam|Tran]].

**Postclassic rupture.** Postclassic narrative history is structured as the rise and fall of five
dynasties ruling part or all of the country. Ho Quy Ly (usurper of the late Tran) renamed the country
Dai Ngu (1400), moved the capital south to Vinh Loc in Thanh Hoa as Western Capital (Tay Do), and left
impressive citadel remains (900×700 m walls, 5–7 m high). Tran supporters and Chams pressured by Ho
invited Ming intervention: firearms overcame the Vietnamese elephant corps; Dong Do then Tay Do fell
within days (dynasty ~seven years). Ming occupation (1407–1427) administered Jiaozhi from Thang Long
(renamed Dong Quan), systematically removed royal archives (Ly civil code, Tran law, memoirs, poetry,
thirty volumes of the Vietnamese Annals — a principal reason for the thin archaeological and documentary
pre-Le Loi record), deported the Ho household and artisans (eunuch Nguyen An later a key architect of
Beijing's Imperial Palace), imposed Ming dress and bans on tattooing, betel, and teeth lacquering, and
levied heavy taxes that fed uprisings culminating in [[le-loi|Le Loi]] and the [[le-dynasty-vietnam|Le]].

**Maritime ceramics and dual Vietnam.** Fifteenth–sixteenth-century Hai Duong kilns in the Red River
Delta supplied export porcelain (benefiting from the Ming private-trade ban); trans-shipment shifted from
Van Don (Ha Long Bay) to Pho Hien. Under Le Thanh Tong, porcelain reached West Asia (Topkapi jar dated
1450). From 1471 until the mid-seventeenth century, Dai Viet's southern limit ran near Phan Thiet after
the sack of Cham Vijaya; relative sixteenth-century calm in Cham lands reflected the Mac vs Le–Nguyen–
Trinh struggle. Nguyen Hoang's base in Thuan Hoa (1558) and Quang Nam (1570) produced Dang Trong
("Inner," Cochin China) vs Dang Ngoai ("Outer," Tonkin). Hoi An (Faifo) rose as a commercial port from
the sixteenth century (Japanese *shuinsen*, then Chinese after 1635 *sakoku*; Portuguese firearms in the
Trinh–Nguyen wars). Urban morphology may have formed around artisan workplaces: *phuong* as both guild
and territorial ward (still a basic urban unit of 2,000–10,000 people).
