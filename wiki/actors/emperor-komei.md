# Emperor Kōmei

Emperor Kōmei (1831–1867) was the 121st emperor of Japan and the father of [[emperor-meiji]]. He reigned from 1846 until his death in early 1867 — the most turbulent decade of the Tokugawa era, spanning Perry's arrival, the unequal treaties, the Ansei Purge, and the rise of the sonnō jōi movement. Though constitutionally a figurehead under the [[tokugawa-shogunate]], Kōmei was the first emperor in at least 250 years to force the shogunate to seek imperial consultation on matters of state, and his repeated threats of abdication made him an unavoidable political actor during the [[bakumatsu]] crisis.

## Birth and Background

Kōmei was born on July 23, 1831, the fourth son of Emperor Ninkō (the 120th emperor) by a *gon no tenji* (lady of the bedchamber), daughter of the nobleman Ōgimachi Sanemitsu. As with all imperial births of the era, he was officially considered the empress's child. His elder brothers having died in infancy, he became crown prince in 1840 and acceded to the throne at fifteen upon his father's sudden death in February 1846. Keene notes the astonishing infant mortality of the imperial family: "Of Ninkō's fifteen children, only three lived past their third year; of Kōmei's six children, only one (Meiji) survived him."

## Court Life and Character

Kōmei grew up in the Gosho, the imperial palace enclosure in Kyōto, the entire world of the court. Under the [[kuge-shohatto]] regulations of 1615, emperors were enjoined to devote themselves exclusively to scholarship and the arts; Kōmei received thorough training in Confucian texts, tanka composition, calligraphy, and gagaku music, and was a skilled poet. His court companion Higashikuze Michitomi, who became his playmate at age ten, recalled: "He was able to read the Four Books and the Five Classics without difficulty and learned enough even to lecture on them… In gagaku he received instruction from Hamuro, the major counselor, and he was a skillful player of the flute."

From 1632, reigning emperors had been forbidden to leave the Gosho except in emergencies — a confinement Kōmei observed until 1863. He was effectively a prisoner of state, unknown to almost all his subjects, performing prescribed rituals in isolation. This context makes his emergence as a genuine political actor all the more striking.

Two surviving portraits capture his contradictory nature. The formal court portrait shows a composed, expressionless face indistinguishable from those of his predecessors. A second portrait reveals "a strong personality of which wrath seems to be the principal component." His surviving letters bear this out: nearly every development during his reign "infuriated him," and his response was "not merely anger but frustration over his inability to prevent the impending changes."

## Political Role: The Unequal Treaties

Before Perry's arrival in July 1853, the court had long worried about the possibility of foreign intrusion. When the shogunate finally informed the court of the American visit, Kōmei "directed the seven shrines and seven temples to pray for seventeen days for peace within the four seas." After the shogunate signed the [[opening-of-japan-1853-1858|Treaty of Kanagawa]] in March 1854 without consulting the court, Kōmei's role began shifting from ceremonial to political.

The decisive break came in 1858. When senior councillor Hotta Masayoshi arrived in Kyōto seeking imperial sanction for the proposed commercial treaty with America, Kōmei flatly refused: "Do they suppose I can be bought? If, as long as I am the ruler of this country, I allow myself to become a mere dummy and permit trading with foreign barbarians, I shall lose the confidence of the people and will leave a shameful reputation for generations to come." When tairō Ii Naosuke signed the Harris commercial treaty and similar conventions with four Western powers without imperial sanction, Kōmei twice threatened to abdicate — the only political weapon at his disposal.

His position was ideologically distinctive. He opposed the unequal treaties as a desecration of the "Divine Land" and asked the gods to send a *kamikaze* against the foreigners as they had against the Mongols in the thirteenth century. Yet he simultaneously opposed the radical *[[sonno-joi|sonnō jōi]]* faction, which wanted to use anti-foreign agitation to overthrow the shogunate. His preferred policy was **kōbu gattai** (union of court and military), cooperation between emperor and shogun to strengthen Japan and eventually expel the foreigners through legitimate means, not mob violence. "Far from seeking to profit by this new respect for the emperor and to deprive the shogun of political power, Kōmei was fiercely outspoken in his opposition to those whose 'respect for the emperor' involved the overthrow of the shogunate."

## The Ansei Purge and Its Aftermath

Ii Naosuke's [[ansei-purge-1858|Ansei Purge]] (1858–1860) was a source of personal humiliation for Kōmei. Old men who had faithfully served him — Takatsukasa Masamichi, Konoe Tadahiro (his calligraphy tutor), Sanjō Sanetsumu — were arrested, forced to shave their heads, or executed. Kōmei repeatedly interceded for them and was repeatedly rebuffed. Ii's willingness to ignore imperial feelings exposed the gap between the emperor's nominal authority and real powerlessness.

After Ii's assassination in 1860, Kōmei used the marriage of his sister Kazunomiya to shogun Tokugawa Iemochi (1861) as political leverage, securing shogunate pledges to abrogate the unequal treaties within seven to ten years — pledges never honored.

## Final Years

During 1862–1864, radical *[[sonno-joi|sonnō jōi]]* activists flooded Kyōto, committing political assassinations and pressuring the court. The Battle of the Hamaguri Gate (August 1864), in which [[boshin-war-1868|Chōshū]] domain forces attacked the palace, brought gunfire into the Gosho for the first time in over 250 years. Kōmei, in full court dress, "sat impassively, seemingly unruffled by the furious activity around him." His son Prince Mutsuhito, then twelve, was evacuated and fainted after being woken in the night.

In the coup of September 30, 1863 (Coup of the Eighth Month), the kōbu gattai faction — with Kōmei's support — expelled the radical sonnō jōi nobles and Chōshū samurai from the capital, restoring a degree of order. Kōmei's political instincts, consistently anti-foreign but also anti-revolutionary, aligned him with the shogunate's moderate wing to the end.

Kōmei died on January 30, 1867, at thirty-six — officially of smallpox. He had refused vaccination, which Keene suggests may have caused his untimely death. His only surviving child, Prince Mutsuhito, acceded to the throne as [[emperor-meiji]], inheriting a polity on the verge of revolutionary transformation that Kōmei had spent his entire adult life trying to prevent.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Kōmei lived another decade, the [[meiji-restoration-1868]] in its actual form would likely not have occurred. He was categorically opposed to overthrowing the shogunate, and his imperial authority was the essential legitimating resource the restorationists deployed in 1868. A living Kōmei might have endorsed a kōbu gattai compromise rather than imperial restoration. His death in early 1867 — whether from smallpox or, as some have suspected, deliberate poisoning — left the throne to a fifteen-year-old whose advisers could shape the Restoration without imperial obstruction.

## Historiography

Kōmei has been historically underrated, partly because the Meiji myth needed a passive predecessor to contrast with the transformative new emperor. Keene's biography, the first major treatment in English, argues for Kōmei's genuine political agency: his refusal to ratify the unequal treaties established the precedent of imperial sanction as a constitutional requirement that the Meiji genrō would later exploit. The contrast between his kōbu gattai conservatism and the radical sonnō jōi movement remains a key interpretive fault line in Bakumatsu historiography.
