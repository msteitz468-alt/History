# Philip IV of France, "the Fair" (r. 1285–1314)

**Philip IV** ("le Bel," handsome) carried the [[capetian-dynasty|Capetian monarchy]] to
the "pinnacle of its power" while straining it to breaking point.
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (William Chester Jordan, Ch. 11) shows a king who ruled
through a corps of southern **legists** — Pierre Flotte, Guillaume de Plaisians, Guillaume
de Nogaret, Enguerran de Marigny — and whose reign is defined by three great confrontations
(the papacy, England, Flanders) and three notorious fiscal-religious acts (the expulsion of
the Jews, the destruction of the Templars, the squeezing of the Lombards).

## The Wars and the Fiscal Machine

Philip's France was entering economic difficulty — recession from the 1290s, the
[[commercial-revolution|Champagne fairs]] in decline, population pressure on marginal land,
Paris swelling toward 200,000. War drove everything. The **Gascon (Aquitanian) war**
(1294–1303) against **[[edward-i-plantagenet|Edward I]]** and the two phases of the **Flemish
war** forced relentless revenue-raising: conscriptive taxes on the **Jews**, debasement and
overvaluation of the **coinage** (each coin worth three times its metal by 1306, breeding
runaway inflation), forced loans, and levies on the towns. The crown's annexation of
**French Flanders** and pressure on the **Franche-Comté**, Lyons and Montpellier rested on
the "four-river theory" (the kingdom reaching the Rhône, Saône, Meuse and Scheldt).

## The Clash with Boniface VIII

The greatest drama was the duel with **[[boniface-viii|Pope Boniface VIII]]** over the
taxation of the Church. When Philip and Edward I taxed their clergy without papal consent,
Boniface's bull ***Clericis laicos*** (1296) censured them; Philip retaliated with an
embargo on exports to Italy, and Boniface retreated (*Etsi de statu*, 1297, conceding
"urgent necessity"). The quarrel reignited over the arrest of Bishop **Bernard Saisset**
(1301); Boniface's **Ausculta fili** and then **Unam Sanctam** (November 1302) asserted that
"it is altogether necessary to salvation for every human creature to be subject to the Roman
Pontiff." Philip's councillor **Nogaret**, allied with the pope's Colonna enemies, seized
Boniface at **Anagni** (1303); the pope, rescued but broken, died weeks later (probably a
stroke). The "victory over Boniface" left the papacy "extremely correct" toward France and
opened the road to **Avignon** and the election of **Clement V** (1305).

## The Religious *auctoritas* and Its Uses

Jordan argues that Philip's attempt to charge and depose a pope underscored "the remarkable
religious claims the French crown was making" — claims rooted in the sacral kingship of his
canonised grandfather **[[louis-ix-capetian|Saint Louis]]** (canonised 1297). This
*auctoritas* "helped justify policies that might otherwise seem purely fiscal":

- **Expulsion of the Jews (1306)**: on a single July day, c. 100,000 Jews were arrested and
  expelled, their property and debts confiscated — perhaps a million livres (see
  [[medieval-anti-judaism]]).
- **Arrest of the Templars (1307)**: every Templar in the kingdom seized on one day, on
  charges of sodomy and heresy extracted under torture; the order was suppressed at the
  Council of Vienne (1312).
- **The Lombards**: the Italian bankers were repeatedly tallaged and progressively "weaned
  from positions in royal service" in a "xenophobic and moralistic" purification of the
  administration.

## The Flemish War and the End

The annexation of Flanders provoked the rising that ended in the disaster of
**[[battle-of-courtrai-1302|Courtrai]]** (1302), where Flemish footsoldiers destroyed the
flower of French chivalry, killing Pierre Flotte. French arms recovered at Mons-en-Pévèle
(1304) and the **Treaty of Athis-sur-Orge (1305)** imposed harsh terms, but the war dragged
on. Philip's last years brought scandal (the adultery of his three daughters-in-law, 1314)
and revolt: **provincial leagues of nobles** rose against "thirty years of authoritarian
rule," coinage manipulation, and the retention of war taxes after peace (the disputed
*cessante causa* principle). Philip died in November 1314, reportedly fearing he had failed
to live up to the model of Saint Louis.

## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

Logan’s ch. 13–15 refine the Boniface confrontation from the church side. Immediate issue was French (and English) taxation of clergy for war; *Clericis laicos* (24 Feb 1296) without naming kings; Boniface later allowed emergency taxation and canonized [[louis-ix-capetian|Louis IX]] (11 Aug 1297). Summer 1301: arrest of the bishop of Pamiers for secular trial; *Ausculta fili* burned and forged. Louvre (12 May 1303): Nogaret’s charges (not true pope, heresy, simony, unspeakable sins); June assembly 29-count indictment; Boniface prepared explicit excommunication of Philip for 8 September—preempted by the [[outrage-of-anagni-1303|Outrage of Anagni]] (7 Sept). Under Clement V, Philip obtained posthumous heresy proceedings against Boniface (from 1309; lapsed 1311) and absolution of Nogaret (1311) with papal praise of Philip’s “praiseworthy and honest devotion for justice.” At Vienne (1312) Philip’s armed pressure forced Templar suppression without debate of charges. Logan cautions against reading modern “nationalism” back into Philip’s power-politics, and against underestimating Flotte and Nogaret as the true administrative engines (Cromwell/Richelieu analogies).

## Significance

Philip the Fair embodies the **bureaucratic, legist-driven royal state** — the most
developed in Latin Europe — and its limits. His humbling of the papacy "spectacularly
rebuffed" its universal political claims by "kings strong in the support of their Church and
nation" (see [[papacy]]), marking a decisive shift toward the sovereign territorial
monarchy. Yet his reliance on coinage debasement, expropriation and extraordinary taxation
left a "bitter legacy" and provincial revolt — the strain underside of state-building.
