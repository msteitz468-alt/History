# Songhay Empire (Gao)

## Overview

The **Songhay (Songhai) Empire**, centered on the city of **Gao** on the eastern bend
of the Niger, was the last and largest of the great medieval
[[western-sudan-states|Western Sudanic]] empires. [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]]
(Conrad, Ch 22) traces its trajectory from the early **kingdom of Gao** — a Niger
trading polity attested from around the seventh–ninth centuries and Islamized at the
ruling level by the eleventh — through its expansion, mostly after the close of the
volume's period, into the empire that succeeded [[mali-empire|Mali]] as the dominant
power of the Sahel.

## From Kingdom to Empire

Gao long stood as a commercial rival and sometime tributary within the orbit of
Ghana and Mali. As Malian power waned in the fifteenth century, the Songhay rulers of
Gao expanded aggressively: under **Sunni Ali** (r. 1464–1492) and especially the
**Askia** dynasty founded by **Askia Muhammad** (r. 1493–1528) — whose reign falls
just past the volume's 1500 horizon — Songhay absorbed Timbuktu and Jenne, the salt
mines of Taghaza, and a vast stretch of the Niger valley, becoming the largest state
in West African history. Like its predecessors it drew "significant resources from
long-distance and trans-Saharan trade in gold, copper, iron tools and weapons,
slaves, salt, and other goods," controlling trading centers and imposing political
dominance over zones of production.

## Structure and Fall

Songhay developed a more elaborate administration than its predecessors — provincial
governors, a professional army, and (under the Askias) a strong alliance with the
Muslim scholarly establishment of Timbuktu, a renowned center of Islamic learning. The
empire's reliance on the trans-Saharan economy was also its vulnerability: in **1591**
a Saadian Moroccan expedition armed with **gunpowder firearms** crossed the Sahara and
shattered the Songhay army at **Tondibi**, ending the empire and the long era of large
Sahelian states — a vivid instance of the [[military-history-middle-millennium|gunpowder
transition]] reshaping power even at the desert's edge.

## Significance

Songhay completes the [[trans-saharan-trade|Western Sudanic]] sequence
Ghana → Mali → Songhay that Conrad's chapter reconstructs, demonstrating the long
continuity of trade-based [[state-formation|state formation]] in the West African
savanna and its integration, via the Sahara, into the
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|hemispheric economy]] — until gunpowder and the Atlantic
reorientation of trade ended its world.

## Origins and the 16th-century apogee (Ajayi & Crowder / Levtzion & Hunwick)

[[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of West Africa* Vol. I]] supplies both
ends of the Songhay story. **Levtzion (Ch. 4)** on the deep origins: the Songhay were a
*heterogeneous* river people — dominant **Sorko fishermen**, with **Gow hunters** and **Do
farmers** — whose mobility on the Niger (the war-fleet was decisive) drove both their dispersion
and their later imperial reach. Their first dynasty, the **Dya**, was founded (per the
dragon-killer myth) at **Kukiya**, with **Gao (Kawkaw)** emerging in the 8th century as the
trans-Saharan terminus; al-Mahallabi (985) describes a **Muslim king of Kawkaw**, making the
ruler of Gao arguably the **first major Sudanese ruler to adopt Islam**, even before
[[takrur|Takrur]]. After a period under Mali, **'Ali Kolon** founded the **Sonni** dynasty
(c. 1275); **Sonni 'Ali the Great** (1464–92) then took the already-independent Timbuktu (1469)
and Jenne (1473), and **Askia Muhammad** (1493–1528) expanded at Mali's expense.

**Hunwick (Ch. 6)** on the 16th-century apogee: Askia Muhammad's elaborate administration (the
*fari-mundyo*, *hi-koi* master of the fleet, *korei-farima* for Arab/Berber traders, Muslim
*qadis*), his **1496–98 pilgrimage** and investiture by the 'Abbasid caliph as **deputy over
"the lands of Takrur"** (the ideological frame for his jihads, encouraged by **al-Maghili**),
and the long golden reign of **Askia Dawud** (1549–82). The fatal weakness was the **nepotic
succession system** (key offices redistributed among the askia's brothers each reign) and the
salt-tax dispute with Morocco over **Taghaza**, which gave Ahmad al-Mansur his pretext — ending
in the [[moroccan-invasion-of-songhay-1591|Moroccan invasion and the rout at Tondibi (1591)]],
after which the askias survived only as a minor state in **Dendi**.

## GHA IV: Sonni Ali and the Askias

The **UNESCO [[gha-vol4-12th-16th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. IV]]** (ch. 8, Cissoko) gives the
fullest account of Songhay's apogee. From its base at **Gao**, the empire was forged by the warrior-king
**Sonni Ali** (r. 1464–1492), who seized **Timbuktu (1468)** and **Jenne (1473)** from Mali and Tuareg
control and built a river-and-cavalry power across the Niger Bend. His successor **Askia Muhammad Ture**
(r. 1493–1528) — founder of the **Askia dynasty** — reorganized the empire on Islamic-administrative lines,
made a famous **pilgrimage to Mecca (1496–97)** (returning as *caliph* of the western Sudan), and patronized
the **Timbuktu** scholarly renaissance centred on the **Sankore** mosque-university and the jurist
al-Maghili. Songhay was the **largest state in West African history** until its sudden destruction by a
**Moroccan** musket army at **Tondibi (1591)** — gunpowder ending the era of the Sahelian gold empires.
