# Adolphe Messimy

French politician and Minister of War (June 1911–January 1912; again briefly in 1914). Key figure in the reorganization of the French High Command on the eve of the Great War; appointed Joseph Joffre to supreme authority and ended the prewar duality of command.

## Role and Major Decisions

A former infantry officer (left active duty 1899 but remained in reserves) with professed admiration for Napoleon, Messimy entered the Ministry of War on June 30, 1911, during the Agadir crisis (German gunboat *Panther* off Morocco), amid a wave of nationalism and anti-German sentiment. He arrived "with the mandate given to me by the Chamber [of Deputies] to concentrate all my efforts on a reorganization of the High Command."

He quickly lost confidence in General Victor Michel, vice president of the Superior Council of War ("His authority... was nil"; senior generals including Dubail, Durand, and Gallieni called Michel "incapable of commanding" and "a national danger"). Michel's public criticism of the offensive à outrance (as articulated by Grandmaison) and his advocacy for a strategy relying heavily on reserves further eroded Messimy's support. Michel was given the opportunity to present his reorganization plan (including "demi-brigades" integrating active and reserve regiments to double unit size, enabling a "vigorous offensive" on the left toward Antwerp-Brussels-Namur while defending the right). The Superior Council rejected the demi-brigade concept on July 19, 1911, rendering Michel's strategic alternative impossible.

Messimy identified three candidates to replace Michel:
- First choice: General Joseph Gallieni (distinguished colonial service); Gallieni refused due to impending retirement (only 2.5 years left) and reluctance to accept after playing a key role in Michel's ouster.
- Second: General Paul Pau (army's prior choice for the post); Pau demanded the right to choose all general officers.
- Third: General Joseph Joffre, accepted after Messimy emphasized Gallieni's age to Premier Caillaux.

On July 28, 1911, a decree named Joffre chief of the General Staff and reorganized the High Command, placing it "completely and without reserve under Joffre's direction." Messimy appointed General Yvon Dubail chief of staff of the army (subordinate to Joffre except on personnel and logistical support). Joffre wanted Ferdinand Foch as first deputy; Messimy refused, so Joffre chose General de Castelnau (familiar with Plan XVI and War Ministry procedures).

These changes ended the long-standing duality (separate chief of staff of the army and vice president/"generalissimo" of the Superior Council) that had produced inefficiencies. Joffre gained unprecedented peacetime authority over training, doctrine, regulations, mobilization, and concentration.

Messimy later (postwar) expressed regret: "I have often vehemently reproached myself for not having chosen Gallieni."

## Return and Confirmation of Division of Responsibility

Messimy again served as minister of war in the first Viviani government (June–August 1914). As mobilization began, he and Joffre agreed: "the political direction of the war belongs properly to the government. The conduct of operations belongs exclusively to the commander-in-chief." This codified the framework in the October 1913 regulations on large units (presidential decree): government fixes political objectives and distributes means; commanders-in-chief conduct strategy/operations in theaters.

## Historiography / Significance

Doughty credits Messimy's short 1911–12 tenure with transformative effects on French war plans, doctrine, and the political-military relationship despite his brief time in office. The July 1911 and January 1912 decrees gave Joffre "a greater degree of control over the functioning of the French army than any general since Bonaparte" (Ralston), though constitutional limits remained (war minister stayed formal head) and bureaucratic obstacles persisted. Parliamentary support was overwhelming (338–7 confidence vote in Chamber after one legality query). The reforms addressed long-recognized weaknesses exposed in 1870–71 but also entrenched an offensive bias and centralized power that shaped the opening campaigns.
