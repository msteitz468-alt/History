## Overview

Edward Gough Whitlam (1916–2014) was Labor prime minister of Australia December 1972 – November 1975. A social-democratic lawyer representing new middle-class suburban voters, Whitlam modernised the ALP's platform and organisation after 1967, won government on the campaign slogan "It's Time," and presided over the most concentrated legislative reform program in Australian history before being dismissed by Governor-General Sir John Kerr during the 1975 constitutional crisis (see [[dismissal-1975]]). The source characterises his prime ministership as an attempt "to complete Labor's interrupted post-war social reconstruction project" with an ambition that ultimately outran economic conditions.

## Background and Rise

The son of a senior Commonwealth public servant, Whitlam joined the Labor Party during World War 2, "attracted to its commitment to expand Commonwealth powers for national reform." A tall, eloquent lawyer representing outer-suburban constituents, he became deputy to Arthur Calwell after 1960 and was identified as representing "the new middle-class voters Labor would need to attract as its traditional working-class base declined." "As tall, middle-class and well spoken as Menzies, he...was immensely able and just as impatient for office."

After yet another election defeat in 1966, Whitlam became ALP leader in 1967. He "pushed through structural reforms that expanded the role of the parliamentary leadership and branch membership in the party's organisation and established a series of policy committees to refresh and expand the party's reform agenda." Labor came close to winning in October 1969 (7 per cent swing) and won in December 1972.

## The Whitlam Government, 1972–75

See also [[whitlam-reforms]] for the full reform program.

**Formation.** Rather than wait for results to be finalised, Whitlam arranged for himself and his deputy to be sworn in as a "two-man national government" (a "duumvirate") making an "avalanche of decisions over the next fortnight": diplomatic recognition of China, withdrawal of residual Vietnam advisers, abolition of conscription and release of draft resisters, equal pay claim reopened, excise on wine and sales tax on the contraceptive pill abolished, and mining leases on Aboriginal reserves suspended.

**Major reforms.** Education occupied "pride of place in the program" — Whitlam called it "the great instrument for the promotion of equality." Commonwealth expenditure on schools trebled in two years to mid-1975; from 1 January 1974 the Commonwealth assumed full responsibility for university funding, abolished tuition fees, and introduced means-based living allowances. Medibank (national health insurance) came into operation in September 1975 after fierce Senate resistance. Welfare benefits were increased, the means test on aged pensions abolished, and a supporting mothers' allowance introduced. Attorney-General Lionel Murphy overhauled family law, abolished the federal death penalty, and introduced trade practices and racial discrimination legislation.

**Foreign policy.** Whitlam "reassured Washington that the US alliance was a central axis of his government's foreign policy" but "insisted on autonomy within that relationship." Australia downgraded ties with Britain (adopting 'Advance Australia Fair' as national anthem, replacing the imperial honours system with the Order of Australia). He stressed UN multilateralism and Asia-Pacific engagement. The government "erased the last vestiges of the white Australia policy" — Immigration Minister Al Grassby in 1973 announced: "give me a shovel and I will bury it."

**Aboriginal policy.** Whitlam "connected 'the treatment of Aborigines directly to the national image and the theme of national self-respect.'" Labor drew up legislation to give Aboriginal people title over NT reserves (the Bill was on the Senate noticeboard when the government was dismissed). In August 1975 Whitlam solemnised a land transfer to the Gurindji people by pouring earth through leader Vincent Lingiari's hands.

**Economic difficulties.** By December 1973 inflation had climbed to 13.2 per cent annually. The first OPEC oil shock (late 1973) stoked further inflation. In the second half of 1974 inflation spiralled and unemployment reached a post-war high of 3.6 per cent. Treasurer Bill Hayden's August 1975 budget announced abandonment of the full-employment objective. The government had refused to use unemployment as a weapon against inflation — "We did not use recession as an alibi against reform" (Whitlam, *The Whitlam Government*, 1985) — but this left it economically exposed.

**Constitutional crisis and dismissal.** See [[dismissal-1975]] for full account. On 11 November 1975 Governor-General Sir John Kerr terminated Whitlam's commission and installed Malcolm Fraser as caretaker prime minister. Fraser won the subsequent election in a landslide.

## Counterfactual Significance

Whitlam's government enacted in three years what the ALP had been unable to achieve across twenty-three years of opposition. Without his organisational reforms of the ALP (1967–72), the party might not have shed its structural handicap (the "thirty-six faceless men" Federal Conference) in time to win in 1972. Without the constitutional crisis of 1975, his government might have legislated Aboriginal land rights in the NT, possibly changing the trajectory of land rights nationally. Conversely, the "phantasmagoric manifesto comprising some 200 specific promises" and the government's dearth of ministerial experience contributed to the chaos that enabled Fraser's supply-blocking strategy.

## Historiography

Whitlam is among the most written-about figures in Australian political history. Key biographies: Graham Freudenberg, *A Certain Grandeur* (1977); Jenny Hocking, *Gough Whitlam: A Moment in History* (2008) and *His Time* (2012). The dismissal's constitutional legitimacy remains contested (see [[dismissal-1975]]). The reform program is assessed positively by most social historians as a belated social-democratic catch-up; economic management is assessed more critically.
