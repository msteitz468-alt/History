# Matthias Corvinus (Mátyás Hunyadi)

King of Hungary (r. 1458–1490), "the first Hungarian magnate to become king of Hungary," who built a
centralised state and a brilliant Renaissance court on the Danube. Treated by János Bak in
[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 27). The son of the great Turk-fighter **János Hunyadi** —
whose relief of the **siege of Belgrade (1456)** halted the Ottomans for seventy years — Matthias was
elected by the nobility in 1458, after his elder brother had been executed by the rival king's party.

## Role and Decisions

Matthias governed against the grain of an over-mighty baronage, leaning on the **lesser nobility** and
on humanist counsellors (the chancellor **John Vitéz**). His instruments:

- **The Black Army.** A standing, largely mercenary **professional army** (a conscious military
  reform, like the contemporary French *compagnies d'ordonnance*), funded by heavy taxation of the
  vast Hunyadi domains and the kingdom, and a chain of Danube border-castles against the Turks.
- **Northern and western wars.** Rather than press the Ottoman frontier hard, Matthias spent
  twenty-three years at war in the north and west — against the Bohemian **Hussites/Utraquists**
  (claiming the Bohemian crown against George of Poděbrady and then the Jagiellonian Vladislav) and,
  above all, against **Emperor Frederick III**, occupying Lower Austria and **capturing Vienna
  (1485)**, where he made his residence and where he died.
- **The Hungarian Renaissance.** Matthias was a great patron of Italian humanists, architecture and
  letters; his **Bibliotheca Corviniana** in Buda — among the largest libraries north of the Alps,
  perhaps 2,500 volumes — was a wonder of the age.

## Counterfactual Significance

Matthias's reign was a powerful but **personal** achievement. His centralising apparatus and Black
Army rested on his own authority; lacking a legitimate heir (he tried to secure the succession for
his illegitimate son John Corvinus), his system collapsed at his death in **1490**. The nobility
elected the weak Jagiellonian **Vladislav II**, the Black Army was disbanded, and Hungary's defences
decayed — the road that led to the catastrophe of **Mohács (1526)** and Ottoman conquest. Matthias is
thus both the high point of independent medieval Hungary and a warning of how fragile a state built on
one man's will could be.
