# Merovingian Episcopate

**The bishops of [[francia|Francia]] as a ruling institution** — the single most powerful corporate
body in the sixth- and seventh-century kingdom, drawn from the Gallo-Roman senatorial aristocracy and
controlling cities, wealth, and the cult of the saints. King Chilperic I's complaint says it best:
"There is no one with any power left except the bishops."

## Noble by birth, noble by faith

Early Merovingian bishops came overwhelmingly from the **aristocratic Gallo-Roman** stratum; seventh-
century hagiography opens again and again with the formula "he was noble by birth, but still more noble
by faith." Of 179 bishops whose social rank Martin Heinzelmann could determine across eight ecclesiastical
provinces, "only eight were… definitely not of the senatorial aristocracy." **Episcopal families** held
sees for generations — at Nantes, Châlons, Paris, Sens, Laon, Metz, Orléans, and Trier "it was normal for
sons to succeed fathers or nephews to succeed uncles." [[gregory-of-tours|Gregory of Tours]] boasted that
of the eighteen previous bishops of Tours, all but five had been his kinsmen.

## Power and its base

By the end of the sixth century the bishop **outranked the count**, who had become "an agent in
episcopal administration." Bishops defended their communities against royal fiscal demands — partly a
defence of "their own largely hereditary lordship." But the **core of episcopal power lay in control of
the sacred**, above all the management of **saints' cults**, which provided the primary source of social
unity in a violently factious society. Bishops suppressed rival, autonomous forms of holiness: the
Lombard stylite **Vulfolaic** had his pillar torn down by bishops who then required him to live under
episcopal authority; legends like Marcel of Paris taming the dragon (analysed by Le Goff) show the bishop
"as the civilizing force triumphing over the forces of nature, but not destroying them." From **511**,
on Clovis's initiative, bishops met in regular synods to legislate, discipline clergy, and keep
[[merovingian-monasticism|monasteries]] under episcopal supervision (a canon needing constant
reaffirmation).

## Succession by intrigue

Episcopal succession was often violent. The prize office *en route* was that of **archdeacon**, who
controlled diocesan wealth and was ideally placed to succeed; ambitious clerics used it to bribe king,
clergy, and people. Geary records plots to murder incumbents — at Lisieux "a priest and the archdeacon
conspired to murder Bishop Aetherius."

## Destruction under Charles Martel

The independent power of the episcopate was broken by [[charles-martel|Charles Martel]], who confiscated
diocesan wealth and installed secular loyalists regardless of religious formation — even non-ordained
"warrior-bishops" such as Hainmar of Auxerre. "Henceforth medieval bishops would be powerful lords… [but]
would never again command that particular power as monopolists of the sacred," which passed to the
monasteries. See [[pippinids]].

## Counterfactual significance

The Merovingian episcopate is Geary's prime example of how the **Gallo-Roman aristocracy survived the end
of empire by colonising the Church**, turning the bishopric into a hereditary lordship and the saint's
cult into the currency of local power. Its destruction was a precondition of Carolingian centralisation.

## Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World (2020)

Gregory Halfond's chapter in the [[sources/effros-moreira-oxford-handbook-merovingian-world-2020|Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World]] analyzes the bishops as a corporate body whose solidarity (apostolic succession, conciliar "*cum consensu omnium*", Roman norms) was repeatedly strained by **political factionalism** — "bishops… were fair game in politics if they had taken sides." The **Gundovald affair** (582–585) is the case study: of 13 bishops Gregory names, 10 came from three southern provinces, most in the former *regnum Chilperici*, acting from "necessity, not enthusiasm"; the Council of Mâcon (585) censured at least five. Royal patronage itself "bred factionalism," and kings like Chilperic I manipulated episcopal trials outright. Other chapters add the bishops' charitable (see [[early-medieval-hospitals-and-charity]]) and military (see [[merovingian-army]]) roles.
