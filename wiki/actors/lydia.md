# Lydia (the Mermnad Kingdom)

**Lydia** was the wealthy western-Anatolian kingdom centred on **[[sardis|Sardis]]** in
the Hermus valley — the second great native kingdom of Iron Age Anatolia after
[[phrygians|Phrygia]], the **inventor of coinage**, and under its last king **Croesus**
a byword for riches before it fell to [[achaemenid-empire|Cyrus the Persian]] in 546.
[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 34a, M. Mellink) treats it.

## Origins and the Mermnad dynasty

The Lydians were **Bronze Age Anatolians** of Luwian/Hittite linguistic descent (unlike
the intrusive Phrygians); their land was rich in grain, sheep, horses, and the gold of
the **Pactolus** river. A legendary "Heraclid" dynasty ended with **Kandaules**, whose
overthrow by **Gyges** founded the historic **Mermnad dynasty** (c. 685). Gyges, like
[[phrygians|Midas]] before him, faced the **[[cimmerians|Cimmerian]]** onslaught:
**around 668–665 he sent an embassy to [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]]** seeking an Assyrian
alliance (the Assyrian king at first could find no interpreter for his language), sent
captured Cimmerian chiefs in fetters as gifts, then **switched to support
[[saite-egypt|Psammetichus I of Egypt]]** against Assyria — and was killed in a Cimmerian
attack (c. 645).

## The kings and the Cimmerian wars

Gyges's successors — **Ardys, Sadyattes, Alyattes** — continued to suffer Cimmerian raids
(Sardis's lower city was sacked) until **Alyattes finally drove the Cimmerians out for
good**. The Mermnads pressed the **Ionian Greek cities** (Miletus, Smyrna, Colophon,
Ephesus) while cultivating Delphi with lavish gold and silver dedications — Gyges's
"Gygadas" stood beside Midas's throne in the Corinthian treasury.

## Alyattes: coinage and the Medes

Under **Alyattes (c. 610–560)** Lydia reached its height. He captured **Smyrna** (the
siege-mound survives at Old Smyrna–Bayrakli), and **electrum coinage began** — struck at
the Sardis mint in interaction with the East Greek cities, the **invention of [[coinage|
coinage]]** that transformed ancient economies (a hoard of 45 electrum coins reached
Phrygian Gordium). His expansion eastward brought him against the **Medes**; the
indecisive **Lydo-Median war** ended at the **battle on the Halys halted by the total solar
eclipse of 28 May 585** (predicted, says Herodotus, by Thales), mediated by Syennesis of
Cilicia and "Labynetus of Babylon," and sealed by the marriage of Alyattes's daughter to
**Astyages the Mede**.

## Croesus and the fall to Persia

**Croesus (560–547)** — son of Alyattes, "difficult to see as a truly Anatolian king" for
his Hellenism — turned the Ionian skirmishes into outright **subjugation and tribute**,
holding hegemony over western Anatolia to the Halys (only Lycia and Cilicia excepted), and
allied with [[saite-egypt|Amasis of Egypt]], Babylon, and Sparta against the rising
Persians. His clash with **[[achaemenid-empire|Cyrus]]** ended West Anatolian independence:
**Sardis fell in 547/546**, and Lydia passed to Persia — the prelude to the Greco-Persian
confrontation of CAH IV.

## The other Anatolian peoples

Mellink treats Phrygia and Lydia as the two kingdoms that achieved historicity; the
**Lycians** (Luwian descendants) and **Carians** (whose mercenaries' graffiti dot Egypt —
the "bronze men from the sea" who helped Psammetichus I) developed kingdoms only later or
under Persia. All four peoples (Phrygian, Lydian, Carian, Lycian) **borrowed the alphabet**,
marking a new Iron Age literacy independent of the neo-Hittite and cuneiform traditions.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the recovery of western Anatolia after the [[phrygians|Phrygian]] collapse under
the [[cimmerians|Cimmerians]]; the wealth of the Pactolus and the Hermus plain.
produced: **[[coinage|coinage]]** — Lydia's world-historical bequest; the Hellenization of
the Anatolian interior (Greek motifs reaching Sardis and Gordium).
contributed_to: the **Persian conquest** — Croesus's defeat opened Anatolia and the Ionian
Greeks to [[achaemenid-empire|Cyrus]], setting up the Greco-Persian wars.

## Historiography

Known from **Herodotus** (the dynastic spine, the famous Croesus logos), the **Assyrian
records** of Gyges (Ashurbanipal's annals), Greek poets (Archilochus synchronizes Gyges),
and the **archaeology of Sardis and the Bintepe tumuli** (the giant mounds of Gyges and
Alyattes). Mellink interweaves the Lydian and [[phrygians|Phrygian]] sequences via the
shared Cimmerian crisis and tumulus-burial tradition.
