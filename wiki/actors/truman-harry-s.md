# Harry S. Truman

> **Detailed biography:** [[hubs/biographies/world-wars-era/truman-harry-s|Harry S. Truman — Detailed Biography (Biography Hub)]]

Harry S. Truman (1884–1972) was the 33rd President of the United States. He succeeded Franklin D. Roosevelt in April 1945, authorized the use of atomic bombs to end World War II, launched the containment policy that defined the early Cold War (Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO), won a stunning upset victory in 1948, and led the United States into the Korean War while relieving General Douglas MacArthur of command. He is remembered as the plain-spoken "common man" who rose through machine politics yet proved decisive in the most consequential office in the world.

## Overview

Born in Lamar, Missouri, and raised on a farm near Independence, Truman never attended college. He worked as a bank clerk, farmer, and failed haberdasher before entering politics under the sponsorship of the Kansas City Pendergast machine. He served as an artillery officer in World War I (distinguished service at Meuse-Argonne), then as Jackson County judge (administrator) before winning election to the U.S. Senate in 1934.

In the Senate he gained national prominence chairing the Special Committee to Investigate the National Defense Program ("Truman Committee"), which saved billions and improved war production. Selected as Roosevelt's running mate in 1944 to replace Henry Wallace, Truman became president after FDR's death on 12 April 1945. In his first months he presided over the final defeat of Germany, the Potsdam Conference, the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and the Japanese surrender.

His presidency defined the early Cold War. The Truman Doctrine (1947) committed the United States to supporting "free peoples" resisting subjugation; the Marshall Plan rebuilt Western Europe; the Berlin Airlift broke the Soviet blockade; NATO was founded in 1949. Domestically he pursued the "Fair Deal" (extension of New Deal reforms) and took early steps on civil rights (desegregation of the armed forces). Against all predictions he won re-election in 1948 in one of the greatest upsets in American political history.

The Korean War (1950–53) dominated his second term. After North Korea's invasion, Truman committed U.S. forces under UN auspices and then expanded the war by crossing the 38th parallel. When General Douglas MacArthur publicly challenged administration policy, Truman relieved him in April 1951, provoking a constitutional crisis over civilian control of the military. The war stalemated; Truman left office in 1953 with low approval ratings that later rose dramatically.

In retirement he returned to Independence, established the Truman Library, wrote his memoirs, and became a respected elder statesman.

## Key Developments

- Pre-presidential: WWI service shaped his view of war's costs; business failure taught humility; Pendergast machine taught practical politics and the necessity of loyalty balanced with personal integrity.
- 1945 transition: Thrust into office with minimal preparation; rapid decisions on atomic weapons after being briefed on the Manhattan Project.
- Containment architect: Rejected isolationism and appeasement; used economic and military power to contain Soviet expansion without direct great-power war.
- 1948 miracle: Whistle-stop campaign, "Give 'em hell, Harry," coalition of labor, farmers, and urban voters overcame Dewey, Thurmond, and Wallace.
- Korea and MacArthur: Initial success followed by Chinese intervention; firm defense of presidential authority over theater commander.
- Domestic record: Desegregation of military and federal workforce; vetoes of Taft-Hartley; support for national health insurance (defeated); loyalty program against domestic communism (controversial).

## Causal and Consequence Notes

Truman's atomic decision was enabled by the prior investment in the Manhattan Project and the context of total war; it produced Japan's surrender without invasion but inaugurated the nuclear age and ethical debates that continue. His Cold War policies (Truman Doctrine onward) produced the institutional architecture of containment and the transatlantic alliance that shaped the next four decades; they also entrenched the national security state and military-industrial complex. The 1948 victory preserved Democratic control and the New Deal coalition for another generation. The MacArthur dismissal reinforced civilian supremacy but contributed to his political isolation late in the term.

## Historiography

Early post-presidency views were mixed or negative (high inflation, Korea stalemate, scandals). The opening of the Truman Library papers and McCullough's 1992 biography produced a major rehabilitation emphasizing character, decisiveness, and the long-term success of containment. Debates remain over the necessity and morality of the atomic bombs, the origins of the Cold War (revisionist vs. orthodox), the wisdom of crossing the 38th parallel, and the domestic loyalty program. Recent scholarship places Truman in the upper tier of presidents for crisis leadership while noting limitations on civil liberties and the long-term costs of militarized foreign policy.

McCullough's biography supplies the single most vivid, source-rich portrait in the collection of Truman the man and president: the plain-spoken Missouri "common man" who rose through Pendergast machine politics, proved unexpectedly decisive in 1945, launched the institutional architecture of the early Cold War, won the greatest upset in modern presidential politics in 1948, and enforced civilian supremacy during the Korean War.

Key grounded material:

- **Formation and character**: Farm life and WWI Battery D command ("Battle of Who Run," profanity-laced leadership under fire) forged resilience and earned loyalty. Failed haberdashery and family debts produced lifelong caution with money and empathy for ordinary people. Voracious reading of history (Hannibal, Jackson, Lee, Plutarch, Bible) shaped ideals of duty and leadership. Cincinnatus ideal of returning to the plow after crisis.

- **Rise and Senate**: Pendergast sponsorship, county offices, narrow 1934/1940 Senate wins. Truman Committee exposed waste, cost-plus profiteering, steel fakes ("F" for fake), and Canol project; saved up to $15 billion and exerted powerful deterrent effect. Civil rights positions (1940 speech on brotherhood under law) despite Missouri background.

- **1945 transition and atomic decision**: Sudden succession after FDR's death ("moon, the stars, and all the planets had fallen on me"). Potsdam briefing and Interim Committee conclusions (immediate use on populated targets without warning). Reluctant acceptance ("with reluctance he had to agree") based on military advice that no realistic alternative existed to shorten the war and avoid invasion casualties.

- **Early Cold War**: Truman Doctrine ("support free peoples resisting... subjugation"), Marshall Plan (full credit to Marshall; Soviet withdrawal cleared path), Berlin Airlift ("We stay in Berlin, period" + B-29 deployment), NATO creation, immediate de facto recognition of Israel (11 minutes after declaration; Eddie Jacobson role, May 12 confrontation with Marshall).

- **1948 miracle**: Whistle-stop campaign ("Give 'em hell, Harry"), "Turnip Day" special session exposing Republican inaction, upset victory over Dewey/Thurmond/Wallace despite unanimous polls against him. Union revenge threats after 1946 rail strike.

- **Korea and MacArthur**: Blair House decisions (air/sea then ground troops after MacArthur report), Inchon gamble despite Chiefs' misgivings, limited war choice after Chinese intervention, firing after March 24 ultimatum (JCS unanimous on military grounds; "wrong war... wrong enemy"). Hearings vindicated policy.

- **Domestic and character**: Taft-Hartley veto, Loyalty Program (preemptive yet privately doubted), Civil Rights Commission/message/executive orders (armed forces desegregation), Fair Deal. Strict refusal to commercialize the presidency; Cincinnatus-model retirement (library fundraising >$1M, $600k memoirs contract with Life, daily walks, no board jobs). "It isn't polls... It's right and wrong."

- **Legacy frame**: Containment architecture, Korea as "the supreme test" that met aggression without world war, civilian control precedent, "I am not through. I’m just starting."
