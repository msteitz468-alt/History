# Cercle Proudhon (1911–1914)

Parisian study circle uniting Sorelian syndicalists ([[berth-edouard|Édouard
Berth]], with [[valois-georges|Georges Valois]] as organizer) with
Maurrassian integral nationalists — for [[sternhell-thesis|Sternhell]] the
first institutional synthesis of the two streams that constitute
[[generic-fascism|fascist ideology]]. First meeting **16 December 1911**;
first *Cahier du Cercle Proudhon* January 1912. (Sternhell's own text also
dates the founding Sorel attended to 1912 — a minor internal variance in the
source.) It was the direct successor of the abortive national-socialist
journal *La Cité française* (1910), whose prospectus Sorel, Berth, and
Valois had signed.

The Declaration (1912): the founders — "republicans, federalists, integral
nationalists, and syndicalists... are all completely in agreement" that
"Democracy is the greatest error of the past century... it is absolutely
necessary to destroy democratic institutions." The circle venerated Proudhon
(whom Berth ranked with Marx as "father of modern socialism," and whom
*L'Action française* honored in its "Our Masters" column for his
antirepublicanism, anti-Semitism, and anti-Rousseauism). *Cahiers* 3–4 were a
"Homage to Georges Sorel," who was the circle's intellectual patron though he
never formally joined its committee. Berth's formula, endorsed by Sorel in
his January 1914 preface to *Les Méfaits des intellectuels*: from the
alliance of Action française (Apollonian, Maurras) and syndicalism
(Dionysian, Sorel) "will be able to give birth to a new grand siècle."

Sternhell stresses that anti-Semitism "became a major element in the
socialist-national ideology" here — targeting "plutocracy," "high finance,"
and "the Jewish speculator and financier" — so that "none of the classical
attributes of the most extreme forms of fascism were missing." The circle
dissolved with the war; retrospective observers dated fascism's French
prehistory to it (Pierre Andreu's 1936 essay "Fascisme 1913"; Drieu La
Rochelle on pre-1914 Action française as the "popular zest" of fascism).
Sternhell's mature-regime comparison: Mussolini's 1930s regime "was much
closer to the ideological synthesis of *La lupa* or the Cercle Proudhon than
the Stalin regime was to original Marxism."
