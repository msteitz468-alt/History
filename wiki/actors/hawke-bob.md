## Overview

Robert James Lee Hawke was Prime Minister of Australia from March 1983 to December 1991, leading four consecutive Labor election victories. He came to office having been president of the Australian Council of Trade Unions (ACTU) from 1970 to 1978 and president of the Australian Labor Party in the same period, giving him "a combination of high intelligence and a larrikin personality" that made him "one of the best known and most popular figures in public life" by the late 1970s ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). He was in parliament for only three years before winning the prime ministership, having displaced Bill Hayden on the same day Fraser called the 1983 election.

## Role and Major Decisions

Hawke's governing method was summitry and consensus: "he placed consensus building at the heart of political life, initiating a series of national summits on key policy areas." His style was "elite pluralism" — negotiating compromises between business, union, and welfare leaders — which "took policy making out of party channels and removed politics even further from 'the punters'" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7).

His central early achievement was the **Prices and Incomes Accord** with the unions, coupling wage restraint with social benefits to secure cooperation for economic liberalisation. The government then floated the dollar in December 1983, deregulated capital markets, progressively reduced tariff protection, privatised public utilities, and streamlined competition policy. Hawke and his treasurer Paul Keating "repositioned Labor as the party of market-oriented, realist government, pursuing private sector deregulation, public sector reform, and economic growth" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7).

Hawke's argument was explicit: "if the world decides it does not trust you, it can ruin you," and hence disciplined economic reform must be the overriding policy objective. The reform agenda "was broadly supported by leaders in business, finance, the unions and welfare sectors — a measure of Hawke's ability to negotiate compromise and consensus" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7).

## Key Events

- **March 1983**: Labor won office; Hawke immediately began national economic summitry.
- **December 1983**: floating of the Australian dollar — the defining first-term reform.
- **1983–1990**: four election victories on the strength of his "special relationship with the Australian people."
- **1987**: creation of the Department of Employment, Education and Training; John Dawkins' unified national higher education system, dramatically reshaping universities.
- **1988**: Australian Bicentenary; Hawke government benefited from commemorative occasions.
- **1990–1991**: Keating challenged for the leadership twice; Hawke lost the caucus vote in December 1991.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Hawke's unique capacity for consensual mobilisation, the pace and breadth of Labor's economic reform in the 1983–1990 period would almost certainly have been narrower. His ability to hold the union movement inside the reform tent via the Accord was structurally irreplaceable — no other Labor leader in the period had comparable standing with the union movement. The reforms he and Keating initiated "underwrote" Howard's subsequent success: "from the early 1990s Australia's economy began a rise that was not significantly checked until the global financial crisis (GFC) of 2007–08" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7).
