# Maria Theresa (1717–1780)

Archduchess of Austria, Queen of Hungary and Bohemia (r. 1740–1780), de facto ruler
of the [[habsburg-empire|Habsburg monarchy]] and the only woman to hold the
position. Her accession under the Pragmatic Sanction of 1713 — the legal instrument
by which Charles VI sought to secure the undivided Habsburg inheritance for his
daughter — was the disputed succession that triggered the
[[war-of-the-austrian-succession-1740|War of the Austrian Succession]] when
[[frederick-the-great|Frederick II]] invaded Silesia in December 1740.

**Role.** Maria Theresa is the exemplary defendant of eighteenth-century
[[proprietorial-warfare|property war]]: the wars of 1740–48 and 1756–63 were
fought over *legal claims* to her inheritance, argued in pamphlets and
chancelleries as much as on battlefields, and settled by treaty cessions
(Silesia lost at Breslau/Berlin 1742, confirmed 1748 and 1763). Her refusal to
accept the loss of Silesia — driving the "Diplomatic Revolution" alliance with
France and Russia and the [[seven-years-war-1756|Seven Years' War]] — shows both
the binding force and the limits of the [[verdict-of-battle|battle verdict]]
system: after Chotusitz "it was perfectly permissible under the law of victory
for Maria Theresa to bargain her way out of trouble by ceding... Silesia"
(Whitman, p. 131), yet her lifelong refusal to accept that verdict is Whitman's
own chief concession that "the hold of the law on eighteenth-century warfare was
never perfect" — by Tom Tyler's loser-acceptance test, the system fails as rule
of law. Verdicts could be relitigated, but only by staking new wagers, and the
final verdict was ultimately accepted. Domestically she rebuilt the Habsburg state
(Haugwitz's fiscal-military reforms, army reform) precisely to relitigate that
claim.

**Counterfactual significance.** Had the Pragmatic Sanction succession gone
uncontested, the Austro-Prussian dualism that structured German history to 1866
might never have crystallized.
