# D'MT Kingdom

**D'MT** (also written Di'amat, Dʿmt) is the name found in a small number of inscriptions from the northern Horn (Tigray highlands and southern Eritrea) of approximately the ninth to fourth centuries BC. It has traditionally been identified as the earliest historically attested kingdom of the region. Phillipson (*Foundations*, 2012) substantially qualifies this identification: the evidence for D'MT as a unitary kingdom is slender, the name appears in only seven inscriptions (none in primary archaeological context), and the polity — if such it was — was likely "localised, short-lived and non-inclusive."

## Overview

### Epigraphic evidence

D'MT is known exclusively from inscriptions. Seven inscriptions mention the name — three inscribed slabs found in the vicinity of Aksum, and four altar-like incense-burners from the highlands of eastern Tigray (now thought to be associated with the Almaqah temple at Maqaber Ga'ewa). Notably, **not one inscription from [[yeha|Yeha]]** — often proposed as D'MT's capital — actually mentions d'mt.

Rulers associated with D'MT include **rdm**, **rbh**, and **lmn**, each specified on occasion as both *mlk* (king) and *mkrb*. A separate ruler, **wrn hywt**, is attested with the title *mlk* at Seglamen (south of Aksum), Kaskase (Eritrea), and Yeha, but is not associated with D'MT — indicating either that multiple polities coexisted or that d'mt was not the name of the only or dominant state.

The term *mkrb* was formerly translated as "overlord of a federation," lending d'mt the appearance of a large federated kingdom with links to Saba (the formula *mkrb d'mt wsb*, "mukarrib of D'MT and Saba," appears on some inscriptions). Drewes (2001) argued that *mkrb* may simply mean "blessed," removing this political implication. If so, the question whether D'MT was a state name rather than a geographical designation remains genuinely open.

The inscriptions fall into two linguistic groups: **Group I** (true Sabaean, linguistically identical to southern Arabian inscriptions — very few, totalling only about 40 words, half personal names) and **Group II** (in an African linguistic form designated "Old Ethiopic" or "Proto-Ge'ez," with personal names not attested in southern Arabia). The Group-I inscriptions are not invariably the earliest, undermining the claim that a Sabaean-speaking colonising population introduced writing to the Horn.

### Material culture and sites

The sites most associated with D'MT's elite horizon are:
- **[[yeha|Yeha]]**: Great Temple (rectangular, 18.6 × 15.0 m, finely dressed sandstone, six-pillar portico — closely parallelling Sabaean temples at Marib and Baraqish, Yemen; dedicated to Almaqah); Grat Be'al Gebri (élite building 44 m square with square-pillar portico).
- **[[hawelti-melazo|Hawelti-Melazo]]**: stelae mound, stone baldachin/throne, female statuettes, incense-burners inscribed to Almaqah.
- **Maqaber Ga'ewa** (near Wukro): Almaqah temple discovered 2007; finely inscribed sculpture.
- **Gobochela** (Melazzo complex): small temple with Almaqah dedications.
- **Kaskase** (near Senafe, Eritrea): square-sectioned monolithic pillars, two bearing royal inscriptions.

Copper-alloy "marks of identity" — openwork objects sometimes incorporating script — are attested at Yeha, Hawelti, Sabea, and one fragment (RIE 308) from Matara. They probably served as ownership markers (seals or brands), functioning analogously for both literate and illiterate sectors of the population.

### Population and economy

The majority population of the northern Horn during the D'MT period was not South Arabian but indigenous. Ancient Ona sites near Asmara and the non-élite levels at Kidane Mehret near Aksum show "little, if any, sign of contact with southern Arabia." These communities cultivated emmer wheat, barley, and (less certainly) teff; herded cattle, sheep, and goats; lived in stone-built rectangular and circular houses; and produced hand-made pottery and flaked-stone tools continuous with earlier traditions. Long-distance exchange played "a much smaller part in the economy... than was formerly believed."

## Political Structure

The political geography of the D'MT period is uncertain. Inscriptions indicate at least one potentate (wrn hywt) who did not claim suzerainty over D'MT; no Eritrean site has yielded a D'MT inscription. Phillipson's tentative conclusion: "the earliest indications for kingdoms in the northern Horn come from the Aksum-Yeha region, extending into eastern Tigray and the southernmost Eritrean highlands. Whatever the details, it seems safe to conclude that the polities currently known were localised, short-lived and non-inclusive."

South Arabian individuals were present — inscriptions from Gobochela and Yeha mention architects or masons — but formed a small minority quickly assimilated into the indigenous population. The claim of political links with Saba itself is doubtful; it likely represents elite prestige signalling rather than genuine political union.

## Counterfactual Significance

The D'MT horizon's primary long-term contribution was the transmission of a literate, architecturally capable elite tradition to the communities that would become Aksumite: the South Semitic script (ancestor of Ge'ez), the techniques of monumental stone construction, and the Almaqah religious vocabulary. Whether South Arabian contacts were causally necessary for this — or merely accelerated an already-occurring indigenous trajectory — is the central unresolved question in pre-Aksumite studies. Without this horizon, [[aksumite-state-formation|Aksumite state formation]] would likely have followed a different path.

## Historiography

Conti Rossini (1928) first systematically argued for large-scale Sabaean colonisation as the source of northern Horn civilisation; Ullendorff (1960) codified this as orthodoxy. Epigraphic counter-evidence emerged from the 1970s (Drewes, Schneider) — most inscriptions were in an African language, not Sabaean. Archaeological counter-evidence followed: Ancient Ona sites (Schmidt, Curtis), Kidane Mehret (Phillipson). Drewes's 2001 reinterpretation of *mkrb* was a further blow to the colonisation thesis but was largely ignored until Phillipson's 2012 synthesis. The revisionist consensus — indigenous development with selective prestige borrowing — is now working orthodoxy; absolute dating and the precise political status of D'MT remain open.
