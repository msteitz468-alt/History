# Frumentius (Abba Salama)

**Frumentius** (Ethiopian church name: **Abba Salama**, "Father of Peace") was a Syrian-born traveller who became the first bishop of [[aksum|Aksum]] and is credited with introducing Christianity to the Aksumite kingdom. His consecration by Athanasius of Alexandria tied Aksumite Christianity permanently to the Alexandrian (Coptic) theological tradition.

## Overview

The account of Frumentius derives almost entirely from **Rufinus of Aquileia's** *Ecclesiastical History* (late 4th century): Frumentius and his companion Aedesius accompanied the scholar Meropius on a voyage to the Red Sea; after Meropius was killed in a conflict, the two young men were taken to the Aksumite court as servants. They rose to positions of influence — Aedesius as cupbearer to the king, Frumentius as secretary and treasurer. After the king's death, the queen-mother appointed Frumentius as regent for the young king Ezana. During this period Frumentius facilitated Christian merchants' worship and began promoting the faith at court. He then travelled to Alexandria and asked Patriarch **Athanasius** to appoint a bishop for the growing Christian community at Aksum; Athanasius consecrated Frumentius himself, who returned to Aksum and converted King Ezana.

## Significance

Frumentius's consecration by Athanasius embedded Aksumite Christianity within the Alexandrian theological orbit — a tie that persisted through centuries, making the Ethiopian church Monophysite (and later Tewahedo) in doctrine, in alignment with the Egyptian Coptic church rather than with Chalcedonian (Roman/Byzantine) Christianity. This doctrinal alignment shaped Ethiopian Christianity's distinctive character for over sixteen centuries.

## Historiography

The Rufinus account is the sole near-contemporary source, transmitted through fifth-century church historians (Socrates, Sozomen, Theodoret). A letter from **Constantius II** (preserved in Athanasius's writings), probably dating to 356/7, addressed to Ezana and demanding that Frumentius be sent to Alexandria for examination by the emperor's Arian bishop, confirms Frumentius was then active. Phillipson (*Foundations*, 2012) accepts the broad outlines of the Rufinus narrative while noting its hagiographic character.
