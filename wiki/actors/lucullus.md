# Lucius Licinius Lucullus

The brilliant, unlucky general who all but destroyed **[[mithridates-vi|Mithridates]]**
and **Tigranes**, carried Roman arms across the Euphrates for the first time, and was
robbed of the credit by mutiny and politics — proverbial afterwards for the luxury
("Lucullan") of his retirement. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 8a
(Sherwin-White).

## Role and career

A loyal **Sullan** — the only officer who stayed with Sulla on the
[[sulla|march on Rome]], and commander of his fleet in the
[[first-mithridatic-war-89-85bce|First Mithridatic War]] — Lucullus won the consulship
of **74** and the command against Mithridates in the
**[[third-mithridatic-war-73-63bce|Third War]]**.

He proved an unorthodox strategist who **shunned pitched battle**: he relieved
**Cyzicus** by starving the besiegers, overran **Pontus** (Cabira, 71), then — with no
authority from Rome — **crossed the Euphrates** and crushed Tigranes' far larger
army at **Tigranocerta** (69), razing the King of Kings' new capital. He took
**Nisibis** (68) and pressed toward Artaxata. Yet his very success undid him: his
army, led by the long-serving **Fimbrian legions** and incited by the young
**Clodius**, **mutinied**; Mithridates recovered Pontus and destroyed Triarius at
**Zela** (67); and at Rome the **publicani** he had alienated, and the friends of
Pompey, engineered his replacement by the [[pompey-pirate-command-67bce|Lex
Manilia]]. He never lost a battle, yet handed Pompey a war he had nearly won — and
was denied his triumph until 63.

## The financial reform of Asia

Lucullus' most humane achievement was civilian: he **rescued the province of Asia**
from the debt-bondage imposed by Sulla's 20,000-talent indemnity and the rapacity of
the *negotiatores* and *publicani* (indebtedness had grown sixfold by 70). He **capped
interest at 12%**, **cancelled accumulated interest exceeding the principal**,
**forbade compound interest**, and limited repayment to a quarter of annual income —
freeing the cities within four years. This made him the implacable enemy of the
**equestrian financiers**, who became a chief engine of his political destruction.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Lucullus shows how **military success without political base** was worthless in the
late Republic: his unauthorized Armenian war, his contempt for the publicani, and his
inability to manage his own legions or the Senate left his conquests to be reaped by
[[pompey|Pompey]]. Sherwin-White treats him as the real architect of Rome's eastern
expansion, executed by a successor who took the glory.

## Historiography

Plutarch's *Lucullus* (with its famous luxury), Appian, Dio, and **Memnon**. The
hostile tradition (the tribune Quinctius: he "makes one war out of another") and the
Pompeian/Ciceronian minimization of his Armenian achievements (the *De Imperio Cn.
Pompei*) are weighed by Sherwin-White against the evidence of his real skill.
