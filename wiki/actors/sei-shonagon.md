# Sei Shōnagon (c. 966 – c. 1017/1025)

The [[heian-period|Heian]]-period court lady who wrote the ***Makura no sōshi***
(*Pillow Book*, ca. 993–1001?) — one of the two supreme monuments of the age of
Heian women's letters, alongside [[murasaki-shikibu|Murasaki Shikibu's]] *Tale of
Genji*. Her sparkling wit, acute observation, and unsparing critical eye make the
*Pillow Book* the most vivid insider account of the [[heian-court-culture|Heian court]]
in its Fujiwara heyday. Source: [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian|Cambridge History
of Japan, Vol. 2]].

## Life

Sei Shōnagon (the name is a courtesy title; her personal name is unknown) was the
daughter of **Kiyohara no Motosuke** (908–990), a provincial governor and one of the
Five Poetic Geniuses. She belonged to the middling aristocracy — the same social stratum
as most of her fellow writers. Around **993** she entered the service of **Empress Teishi**
(Sadako, 976?–1001), consort of Emperor Ichijō and rival to [[murasaki-shikibu|Murasaki
Shikibu's]] mistress Empress Shōshi (Akiko). She probably finished the *Pillow Book*
shortly after Teishi's death in 1001; almost nothing is known of her life thereafter.

Her relationship with [[murasaki-shikibu|Murasaki Shikibu]] was one of famous mutual
disdain. Murasaki dismissed Sei Shōnagon in her diary as displaying "extraordinary
self-satisfaction" and producing "a patchwork of blunders" — the accusation of a
reserved, morally serious writer against a brilliant, self-pleased, and socially formidable
rival.

## The Pillow Book (Makura no sōshi)

*Makura no sōshi* is classified by Japanese scholars as a **miscellany** (*zuihitsu*) — the
sole surviving Heian example of the genre. It is "a jumble of reminiscences about the
author's days at court, random observations on people, nature, and life in general, and
innumerable lists of things." The lists are among its most celebrated features:
"Things That Make One Impatient," "Things That Give a Vulgar Impression," "Adorable
Things," "Things That Are Unpleasant to See" — the last of which includes the
observation: "a lean, hirsute man taking a nap in the daytime. Does it occur to him
what a spectacle he is making of himself?"

The book was composed because Empress Teishi "happened to make her a present of a
large quantity of paper; otherwise, presumably, she could not have afforded to do so" —
a reminder that paper was a luxury item controlled by the wealthy. This material
contingency shaped the entire [[heian-literature|literary tradition]].

The *Pillow Book* is written in a **sparkling, witty style with fastidious sensitivity and
small tolerance for human shortcomings**. It is also a valuable social document: its
detailed accounts of court ceremonies, dress, seasonal customs, and interpersonal
dynamics make it indispensable for historians of the Heian period. Sei Shōnagon and
Murasaki Shikibu together are described in the Cambridge History as "supremely
articulate spokeswomen for naturalized Chinese values" — representatives of an
aesthetic standard that had absorbed continental influences so thoroughly that they
could appear as native refinement.

## Sei Shōnagon and [[kokinshu|Kokinshū]] culture

Sei Shōnagon was steeped in the [[kokinshu|Kokinshū]] tradition. The Cambridge History
notes that she had acquired familiarity with canonical Chinese works "at second hand"
through compilations like Meng ch'iu — a primer whose material she could deploy in
the witty allusive style the court demanded. Her knowledge of Chinese learning
([[ chinese-learning-heian|kanbun]]) was genuine, if not that of a trained literatus: she
appears in the same context as women who could read Chinese by overhearing male lessons.

She brackets **romances** with go and backgammon as among the three best cures for
boredom — a casual remark that illuminates the central role of narrative prose fiction
in court women's lives. She disapproved of kemari (kickball) as indecorous for
gentlemen, a judgement she shared with [[murasaki-shikibu|Murasaki]] and that
reflects the deep Heian preference for sedentary, aesthetic, and ceremonial pursuits
over strenuous activity.

## Significance

Sei Shōnagon's *Pillow Book* complements the *Tale of Genji* as a historical document:
"We must read both if we are to capture the flavor of aristocratic society" — Murasaki's
psychological depth and Sei's "gay, high-spirited, somewhat feckless side of court life."
The *Pillow Book* is also the founding work of the Japanese **essay tradition** (*zuihitsu*),
a genre that would continue through Kenkō's *Tsurezuregusa* (ca. 1330) to the modern
period.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Sei Shōnagon the vivid, comic, critical, and unsentimental face of Heian court
life would be largely invisible — the *Genji* is psychologically penetrating but socially
selective. The *Pillow Book* preserves the texture of ordinary court days, the social
hierarchies of dress and ceremony, and the voice of a particular female intelligence
that neither official kanbun culture nor the monoawase tradition of waka would have
preserved.
