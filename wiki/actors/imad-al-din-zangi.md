# Imad al-Din Zangi

**ʿImad al-Din Zangi** (d. 1146), Turcoman warlord in Seljuk service, **atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo** (from 1127), and founder of the **Zangid** line. In the Islamic narrative of Frankish–Muslim conflict he is the first major Syrian ruler whose career produced a decisive, lasting reconquest of Frankish territory: the capture of **[[siege-of-edessa-1144|Edessa in 1144]]**, which provoked the **[[second-crusade-1147|Second Crusade]]**.

## Role

Zangi built a power base at Mosul and then Aleppo under the formal umbrella of the Great Seljuks, fighting both Frankish neighbours and rival Muslim dynasts (notably Artuqids). His son **[[nur-al-din|Nur al-Din]]** inherited and expanded the Syrian project of jihad and unification that later culminated under **[[saladin]]**.

## Career (Cobb)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] frames Zangi as the figure through whom Syria first combined **military success with ideological certainty** against the Franks: a familiar Turcoman atabeg type, but distinguished by willingness to use jihad as the basis for an independent domain—linking him to later jihad-savvy statesmen such as Saladin. The structural precondition was leadership that married Syrian military savvy with authority transcending petty factionalism; that change entered via Aleppo from outside Syria (Iraqi politics).

**Iraqi apprenticeship.** Father (former Aleppo governor) died in Zangi’s childhood; raised at Mosul. In 1122 the Seljuk sultan Mahmud named him *shihna* of Wasit and Basra; in 1127 *shihna* of Baghdad; later that year governor of Mosul and atabeg to two of the sultan’s minor sons. He clashed with Artuqids and supplemented his mamluk army with Turcoman tribes.

**Aleppo and early Syrian policy.** After Ridwan’s death (1113) Aleppo’s Seljuk connection frayed; Il-Ghazi offered a relatively stable interlude; after his death (1122) Artuqid successors mismanaged the city. Baldwin and Joscelin (with Iraqi Bedouin allies) besieged Aleppo in October 1124; the populace sought Aqsunqur al-Bursuqi of Mosul, who dispersed the siege and linked Aleppo’s fate to Mosul/Upper Mesopotamian manpower. Aqsunqur was defeated at Aʿzaz (1125) and assassinated in a Mosul mosque (1126; Nizaris claimed responsibility). In 1128 the populace turned to Mosul’s new atabeg, ʿImad al-Din Zangi.

Zangi’s first Syrian forays deliberately avoided Franks (truce with Joscelin). With a diploma over “the West,” he took Aleppo from the Artuqids (1128), garrisoned it, married one of Ridwan’s daughters, and soon returned to Mosul. He later captured Hama and failed at Homs. In spring 1130 a lightning campaign into Antioch territory took al-Atharib; against advisers he faced Frankish mobilization, and Ibn al-Athir attributes the rallying speech that gives Cobb his chapter title—“Let us make them so taste of our might that the fear of it remains in their hearts.” Franks were routed; bones of the dead were still said to litter the land fifty years later; a truce followed after Harim.

**Damascus delayed; Baʿrin.** Iraqi distractions (Artuqids, Seljuk succession, an independent-minded caliph) postponed Zangi’s primary Syrian goal: Damascus. In 1135 he besieged the city; the caliph demanded peace and the siege was lifted. In 1136 Zangid forces raided Antioch lands as far as Latakia. Spring 1137: with a Damascene treaty, he turned from defiant Homs to the Frankish fortress Baʿrin, defeated a relieving force of Raymond II and King Fulk, captured Raymond, and craftily accepted Baʿrin’s surrender for Fulk’s safe conduct before a massive Frankish relief coalition arrived—the Franks later learned they need not have surrendered. After taking Baalbek (breaking safe-conduct; flaying/crucifying the garrison), he appointed the Kurdish commander **Ayyub ibn Shadhi**—future eponym of the **[[ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubids]]**—as governor. Unur of Damascus then bought Frankish aid (Banyas offered to Fulk plus annual tribute); Banyas fell to the Franks, and Zangi returned to Mosul in June 1140 still unsatisfied.

**Edessa and death.** Edessa was the poorest Latin state, a northern outlier worn by raids; Joscelin’s absence at Tall Bashir and open break with Raymond of Antioch reduced the chance of nearby relief. Zangi cast the campaign as jihad, summoned Turcoman tribes, and took the city when sappers breached the walls (24 December 1144). After a sack he ordered a halt, promised rule of law, lightened taxes, and rebuilt fortifications. He then took Saruj and Frankish lands east of the Euphrates save al-Bira. In September 1146, while besieging an Arab rival at Qalʿat Jaʿbar on the Euphrates, he was murdered in his sleep. Sources split on the assassin (mamluks vs. a lone Frankish-origin servant); either reading, for Cobb, fits as moral coda to a man who used jihad against Franks as cover for ambitions against Muslim rivals.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Zangi’s seizure of Edessa, the Second Crusade would have lacked its immediate cause, and the later Zangid–Ayyubid consolidation of Syria might have followed a different path. He is the hinge between early twelfth-century Frankish predominance in northern Syria and the mid-century Muslim counter-offensive.

## Historiography

Latin sources cast him as a scourge of Christendom; Arabic chronicles (Ibn al-Athir and others) present a more ambivalent warlord whose primary rivals were often Muslim. [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] stresses that Edessa was taken in the context of Artuqid and local politics, not as the climax of a lifelong crusade against the Franks alone, and that Zangi is a **transitional** figure—success plus ideology—rather than a pure “counter-crusader.”
