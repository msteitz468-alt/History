# Kwakwaka'wakw

Wakashan-speaking peoples of the islands and inlets of the **middle British Columbia coast** (Fisher’s text uses older ethnonym “Kwakiutl”). Three linguistic subdivisions. Central to Northwest Coast ceremonial and anthropological literature (Boas and successors).

## Role and overview

Hierarchical coastal society within the [[pacific-northwest-coast]] culture area; potlatch prestige economy made them a primary case for debates on whether the fur trade produced a cultural “golden age.” Fisher: potlatch existed at contact among some groups and became **more frequent, elaborate, and widespread** with new wealth; Codere’s suggestion that potlatch substituted for warfare as competitive expression is noted; Benedict’s “megalomaniacal Kwakiutl culture” is rejected by most scholars.

## Key processes (Fisher)

- **Southward expansion:** After European arrival, Kwakwaka'wakw pushed [[salish]] groups as far south as **Cape Mudge on Quadra Island**—**in part made possible by fur-trade wealth**.
- **Ceremonial florescence:** With metal tools and trade surplus, leaders commissioned more art; poles and potlatches intensified (continuity with elaboration, not wholesale invention).
- **Settlement-era criminalization:** Canadian **1884** Indian Act amendment criminalized the potlatch and “Tamanawas” dance under missionary/agent pressure that saw potlatch as a formidable obstacle to “civilizing.” Enforcement ambiguous (few agents; first charges dismissed as offences unclear); responses ranged from quitting to continuing in secret or modified form. Hoxie: ceremony flourished through 1890s, driven underground by turn of century; **revival after 1951 repeal** showed survival through suppression years.
- **End-state pairing with U.S. policy:** Fisher pairs Canadian potlatch law with U.S. [[dawes-act-1887]] as parallel 1880s “reformist” assimilation assaults—different instruments, shared intent to erase distinct lifeways into a society that offered Natives no real place.

## Counterfactual significance

Fur-trade-enabled territorial expansion and potlatch intensification exemplify nondirected change under Native control; potlatch ban exemplifies directed settlement-era cultural warfare. Survival underground and post-1951 revival undercut extinction narratives.

## Historiography

Boasian ethnography made “Kwakiutl” the type-case of Northwest Coast culture—creating coast bias Fisher notes for the whole regional literature. Ethnonym correction to Kwakwaka'wakw is now standard; Fisher’s chapter retains older form in quotes.
