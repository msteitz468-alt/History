## Overview

Jamaat-i-Islami (JI) is a South Asian Islamist party founded in 1941 by [[mawdudi-abu-ala]] as a vanguard organization for establishing an Islamic state governed by divine sovereignty. It has operated across [[pakistan]], India, and Bangladesh since Partition, alternating between vanguardist opposition and pragmatic democratic alliance-building.

## Role and Key Developments

At its founding, JI opposed both the Indian National Congress's secularism and the [[muslim-league]]'s Westernized, nation-state-based demand for Pakistan, which Mawdudi deemed "un-Islamic." After Pakistan's creation, JI pushed for the 1949 Objectives Resolution's assertion of God's delegated sovereignty over the state. General Ayub Khan's 1958 military regime restricted the party as part of a broader secularizing push.

Facing restriction, JI pragmatically forged alliances with secular parties — a Muslim League faction, the Bengali ethno-nationalist Awami League, and a Marxist party — on the calculation that democratic participation, not confrontation, was the most effective path to expanding Islamist influence. JI later backed General Zia ul-Haq's 1977 coup via the Pakistan National Alliance, supporting his top-down Islamization program including the 1979 Hudud Ordinances.

## Counterfactual Significance

JI's pivot after 1958 from vanguardist rejection of democracy to pragmatic coalition politics established the template later copied by Islamist parties elsewhere in Asia (e.g., Malaysia's PAS) for advancing Islamist goals through electoral participation rather than revolution.

## Historiography

Formichi (2020) treats JI as the institutional vehicle for Mawdudi's ideas and stresses the tension between the party's revolutionary Islamist theory and its consistently pragmatic, coalition-based practice.
