# John, King of England (r. 1199–1216)

**John** ("Lackland"), youngest son of [[henry-ii-of-england|Henry II]], succeeded his
brother **Richard I** in 1199 to a realm stretching "from Dublin to the Pyrenees." Within
five years he had lost the continental heart of that empire to the Capetians; within
sixteen he had provoked the baronial rebellion that produced **[[magna-carta-1215|Magna
Carta]]**. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (D.A. Carpenter, Ch. 12) treats his reign as
the hinge on which both the political structure of western Europe and the nature of
England's polity turned.

## The Loss of Normandy (1202–1204)

John's "trail of defeat" began with his quarrel with the **Lusignans** of Poitou, who
appealed to [[philip-ii-augustus|Philip Augustus]]; in 1202 Philip, as John's feudal
overlord, declared his French fiefs forfeit. After a brilliant start (the capture of the
Lusignans and his rival nephew **Arthur of Brittany** at **Mirebeau**, 1202), John
squandered it: the murder of Arthur and his cruelty to prisoners branded him "a king both
dangerous and dishonest," and his barons deserted. Philip overran **Normandy** — its
revenues nearly England's, its capital Rouen sixty miles from Paris — and took Rouen in
June 1204. Carpenter judges the decisive factor **resources**: Capetian revenues from a
compact demesne adjoining the Norman frontier were larger and far easier to mobilise than
John's far-flung Angevin income. John "had quite simply run out of money." His 1214
campaign to recover the lost lands ended when his allies were crushed at
**[[battle-of-bouvines-1214|Bouvines]]** (July 1214).

The loss was momentous. The **Anglo-Norman elite** that had straddled Channel since 1066
ceased to exist (John required single allegiance); the Plantagenets became, for practical
purposes, an **English dynasty** — Henry III spent four and a half years of a fifty-six-year
reign overseas. This "Englishing" of the monarchy ultimately reshaped the whole political
geography of Britain (see [[edward-i-plantagenet|Edward I]]).

## The Intensification of Royal Government

Thrown back on England and facing rapid inflation, John exploited the most formidable
governmental machine in north-western Europe — exchequer, sheriffs, the general eyre, the
chancery (whose rolls begin in his reign) — "with a new vigour." His ordinary revenue from
the counties rose by over a third in real terms; total income 1207–1212 averaged over
£50,000 a year, "probably the greatest level of financial exploitation seen in England
since the Norman Conquest." He taxed the **Jews** (legally his property) ruthlessly, sold
and denied justice, and concentrated power on parvenus and foreign castellans. He also
quarrelled with **[[innocent-iii|Innocent III]]** over the election of **Stephen Langton**
to Canterbury: England was placed under interdict (1208) and John excommunicated (1209)
until, "the wisest move of his life," he made England a **papal fief** in 1213, securing
the papacy's unstinting support.

## Causal Significance

The combination of military humiliation, financial extortion, and lawless rule produced
the rebellion of 1215. "The road from Bouvines to Runnymede was direct, short and
unavoidable" (J.C. Holt). John sealed **[[magna-carta-1215|Magna Carta]]** at Runnymede in
June 1215, repudiated it within weeks (Innocent III quashed it on 24 August), and died at
Newark in October 1216 amid renewed civil war, with the French prince **Louis** (later
[[louis-ix-capetian|Louis VIII's predecessor as claimant]]) holding half the kingdom. His
nine-year-old son **[[henry-iii-of-england|Henry III]]** inherited a desperate position
but, crucially, a Charter that would take root.
