# Albert Wohlstetter

Albert Wohlstetter was a leading RAND strategist whose work on the vulnerability of US strategic forces in the 1950s was enormously influential. His famous "Delicate Balance of Terror" paper (1958/1959) argued that the nuclear balance was far more fragile than the prevailing "balance of terror" rhetoric assumed, because US bomber and (later) missile forces on the ground were highly vulnerable to a disarming first strike.

Wohlstetter (with his wife Roberta) emphasized the requirements for a secure second-strike capability: survivable basing (dispersal, hardening, airborne alert, SLBMs), warning, and command-and-control. His analysis helped drive the major US force posture and readiness improvements of the late 1950s and early 1960s.

In Freedman's narrative he stands as one of the key "formal strategists" alongside Brodie, Schelling, and Kahn, but with a more engineering/operational focus on the concrete requirements of deterrence stability rather than abstract game theory or war-fighting scenarios.

## Vulnerability Analysis and Second-Strike Requirements

From the technological arms race section: Wohlstetter presented the Gaither Committee with an analysis "similar to that later published in *Foreign Affairs* on 'The delicate balance of terror'." The anxiety about SAC vulnerability "came not so much from the development of a Soviet doctrine of and capabilities for surprise attack, but because of the lack of interest in the SAC hierarchy in doing much to reduce the vulnerability of its bombers." RAND proposed "providing each aircraft with a concrete shelter placed just below the ground as the optimum way of dealing with the problem." This was "too defence-minded for the Air Force who preferred to concentrate on pre-emption to destroy Soviet offensive systems. The only precaution they would accept was continual airborne alert."

Wohlstetter was concerned with "the fragility of the communications links" for mobile systems (submarines, rail-mobile missiles, airborne alert). He provided an example of five half-megaton weapons with two-mile inaccuracy destroying half the population of a city of 900,000; shelters or deeper protection dramatically increased the weapons required. (Minuteman arrived with roughly twice the yield and half the inaccuracy postulated.)

## 1970s Critiques of Arms-Race Legends and MAD (Grounded in Freedman 1981 Range 08)

In the 1970s Wohlstetter published an influential series in *Foreign Policy* attacking what he saw as the "legends of the arms race". He demonstrated that the costs and destructiveness of US strategic forces had been on the decline rather than accelerating; that technology could be benign as well as malign; and that there was no evidence of "invariable" overestimation of Soviet strategic forces in US intelligence estimates — if anything there had been "underestimation, especially with projections of Soviet offensive forces". (Freedman 1981, THE CONSENSUS UNDERMINED / "The Action-Inaction Phenomenon").

A better model of interaction, he argued, must incorporate "institutional factors, operational doctrines, and resource constraints". The two sides' decisions were not "unfortunate cases of reciprocal failure... to see that all their important interests were in common. They are not." "Strategic forces... are the ultimate back-up for alliance commitments." (Freedman 1981, p.347).

One underlying theme was a "deep distaste for the prescriptions of assured destruction". Wohlstetter questioned the morality of measuring "the adequacy of our weapons in terms of the number of civilians they can kill" and proposed facing up to "evasions making 'murder respectable' in such chaste phrases as 'counter-value attacks' and in all the unreflective vocabulary of the arms race". (Freedman 1981, "The Shame of Assured Destruction", p.347).

His polemic was effective in puncturing rhetoric and forcing liberals back to the evidence. It contributed to the broader undermining of the MAD consensus and the turn toward concerns with perceptions, essential equivalence, and escalation dominance in the Schlesinger era. Wohlstetter's influence extended to protégés such as Colin Gray who applied similar arguments to the late-1970s ICBM vulnerability debates. (Freedman 1981, Range 08).

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Wohlstetter's early vulnerability analyses, the US bomber and missile force posture improvements of the late 1950s–1960s would likely have been slower and less comprehensive. Without his 1970s critiques, the intellectual momentum behind pure assured-destruction stability and unilateral restraint arguments would have been harder to dislodge, delaying the doctrinal shifts represented by Schlesinger and PD59.
