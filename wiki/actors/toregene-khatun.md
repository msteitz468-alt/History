# Töregene Khatun

## Overview

**Töregene** (Turakina / Toregene; d. c. 1246), the most capable though not senior wife of [[ogedei-khan|Ögödei]], gradually received administrative power while he was “too drunk to lead the empire” for long periods (Weatherford 2004). At his death in 1241 she became official regent. Weatherford frames the next decade (to 1251) as a period when Töregene and a small group of other women—mostly married into the family from conquered steppe tribes, many Christians—controlled the largest empire in world history; treat the scale claim as **Weatherford’s thesis**, the regency itself as fact.

## Role (Weatherford 2004)

- **Yeke Khatun.** Oldest surviving record of her power: order to print Taoist texts as *Yeke Khatun* (Great Empress), under her own name and Ögödei’s seal, 10 April 1240—supporting religion, education, buildings, social structures.
- **Succession politics.** Ögödei had nominated a grandson to succeed him; Töregene pushed her “quarrelsome and arrogant” son Güyük (disliked by his father). After Ögödei’s death she summoned a *khuriltai* for Güyük but could not get a Golden Family quorum. She remained regent and spent five years building support: dismissed her late husband’s ministers and replaced them with her own, most importantly **Fatima**, a Tajik/Persian captive from the Khwarazm campaign working in [[karakorum|Karakorum]].
- **Fatima and hostile sources.** Juvaini (hostile) wrote that Fatima enjoyed constant access to Töregene’s tent, became “sharer of intimate confidences and the depository of hidden secrets,” and issued commands while older ministers were debarred—useful for historiography of gender and Persian clerical bias, not as neutral fact alone.
- **1246 installation.** By 1246 she felt ready; Güyük’s election was private to Golden Family/functionaries, but installation was a major foreign affair through summer to August. Delegates included Seljuk sultan, Baghdad caliph’s representatives, two Georgian Davids, and Grand Prince Yaroslav II of Vladimir-Suzdal (Alexander Nevsky’s father), who died suspiciously just after dining with Töregene—**suspicion flagged, not proven murder**.
- **Death.** After foreigners departed autumn 1246, Güyük attacked Fatima on a witchcraft pretext; Töregene refused to surrender her; Güyük’s men seized Fatima and Töregene was dead—records vague (illness, killed, or grief). Juzjani’s euphemism: she was “sent to join her husband.”

## Historiography

Centerpiece of Weatherford’s “Warring Queens” narrative and a key episode in the mid-century succession crisis. Core narrative also in Juvaini (hostile to Fatima/women’s power), Juzjani, and Carpini’s embassy context. Do not collapse her regency into simple “misrule”; record administrative choices (ministerial purge, Taoist patronage, Güyük’s candidacy) with source attribution. Contrast with Oghul Ghaimish, who later tried the same regency path after Güyük “but lacked the skills of her mother-in-law” (Weatherford) and was destroyed under [[sorghaghtani-beki|Sorghaghtani]]’s purge.
