# Srinivasa Ramanujan

**Summary & network:** [[hubs/biographies/world-wars-era/ramanujan-srinivasa|Detailed Biography]] · Related: [[actors/hardy-g-h]], [[actors/ramanujan-janaki]], [[actors/ramanujan-komalatammal]], [[places/kumbakonam]], [[places/cambridge]], [[events/ramanujan-letter-to-hardy-1913]]

Srinivasa Ramanujan Aiyangar (22 December 1887 – 26 April 1920) was an Indian mathematician who, with almost no formal training, produced thousands of results in number theory, infinite series, continued fractions, and elliptic functions. His notebooks and the famous 1913 letter to G. H. Hardy led to his being brought to Trinity College, Cambridge, where he collaborated with Hardy and J. E. Littlewood during World War I, publishing twenty-one papers and becoming the first Indian Fellow of the Royal Society (1918) and a Fellow of Trinity (1918). He returned to India in 1919 already gravely ill and died at thirty-two.

## Narrative

Born in Erode and raised in Kumbakonam on the banks of the Cauvery, Ramanujan grew up in a traditional Iyengar Brahmin family. His mother Komalatammal was the dominant influence; his father was a clerk. From childhood he displayed extraordinary mathematical intuition. A copy of G. S. Carr’s *Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure and Applied Mathematics* that came into his hands around 1903 became his intellectual universe. He filled three notebooks (and later a “lost notebook”) with identities and theorems, often without proofs, attributing his insights to the family goddess Namagiri of Namakkal.

He repeatedly failed college examinations because he would not study subjects other than mathematics. After an arranged marriage in 1909 to the nine-year-old Janaki, years of poverty followed while he sought recognition from Indian mathematicians. Employed as a clerk at the Madras Port Trust under Narayana Iyer, he continued his work. In 1913 he wrote to three Cambridge mathematicians. Two (Baker and Hobson) set the letter aside; G. H. Hardy recognized genius, corresponded, and arranged for Ramanujan to come to England.

He arrived in April 1914. The next five years, despite the war, cultural isolation, and increasingly poor health, produced his most famous work: the asymptotic formula for the partition function p(n), the tau function, and the beginnings of what would be called mock theta functions. He was elected FRS in 1918. In 1919 he returned to Madras a national hero. He died there on 26 April 1920.

## Causal Analysis

Ramanujan’s achievement was the product of an almost unique conjunction: an individual mind of exceptional intuitive power, formed in a devotional South Indian milieu that treated mathematics as a form of worship; a narrow but powerful textbook (Carr) that gave him both problems and a model of compressed results; the absence of formal training that left him free of certain orthodoxies; and the timely intervention of Hardy, who possessed exactly the combination of aesthetic sensibility, rigor, and institutional power needed to bring the work to the world without destroying its source.

The near-miss is central: had Hardy not replied, or had Ramanujan written a year later or earlier, or had the war prevented travel, the notebooks might have remained local curiosities or been lost.

## Consequence Analysis

Ramanujan’s papers and notebooks reshaped parts of analytic number theory and continue to influence research in partition theory, modular forms, and even applications in physics and computer science. In India he became (and remains) a symbol of native genius and of the waste of talent under colonial and post-colonial educational systems. Hardy’s advocacy and later lectures fixed his place in the Western mathematical pantheon. The question “How many Ramanujans are there?” remains a standing challenge to every educational and patronage system.

## Historiography

Kanigel’s 1991 biography remains the standard narrative life, drawing on family records, interviews, and the Hardy–Ramanujan letters. Hardy’s own 1937 lectures and *A Mathematician’s Apology* supply the closest contemporary mathematical assessment. Later notebook editions by Berndt and others have made the technical content fully available. Debates persist over the extent of Ramanujan’s formal knowledge, the precise nature of his “intuition,” the role of illness in his later work, and the degree to which Indian devotional culture shaped (or limited) his approach to proof. Nationalist and postcolonial readings emphasize the structural barriers he overcame; mathematical historians stress the intrinsic quality of the results.
