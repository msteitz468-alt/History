# Waldensians (Poor of Lyons)

## Overview

The **Waldensians** originated with **Waldès (Valdes)** of Lyons, who c.1173 renounced wealth and began a life of apostolic preaching. Unlike the dualist [[catharism|Cathars]], Waldensians were largely orthodox in creed but **rejected the monopoly of licensed clergy on preaching** and embraced radical poverty. Condemned with other groups at the Council of Verona (1184), they survived as a dissenting network into the Reformation era and beyond.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

### Origins and Alexander III

Moved by the legend of St Alexius’s renunciation of wealth, the wealthy Lyons merchant **Waldès** in **1173** abandoned business, wealth, and family (after providing for wife and daughter) for wandering poverty and preaching. He and followers preached repentance barefoot two-by-two, claiming gospel mandate **without episcopal licence**. “The problem was not what they were preaching – mostly exhortations to live a better Christian life – but that they were preaching at all.” Temporary compromise with **Alexander III** at **Third Lateran (1179)**: pope embraced Waldès, approved poverty, allowed preaching only if local priests agreed; Waldès professed orthodoxy. Compromise failed as few priests consented; a new archbishop of Lyons (1183) expelled and excommunicated them.

### Verona, schism, and survival

Though **mutually antagonistic** to Cathars (Waldensians preached and debated against them), they were condemned **with** the Cathars at **Verona 1184** “with little distinction made.” Episcopal expulsion led to expansion, especially in tolerant Lombardy. By the **1190s** some denied efficacy of sacraments by bad priests, denied purgatory, and non-priests celebrated Mass — crossing into doctrinal heresy. **Innocent III** in **1208** reconciled some French Waldensians as the “**Catholic Poor**” (poor wandering preachers under orthodoxy); two years later a Lombard group formed a Catholic community — submissions required profession of orthodox faith, not mere licensing. In **1205** Poor of Lyons and **Poor Lombards** split (personalities; Italian desire for a separate church). French Waldensians continued small into the sixteenth century; the Italian movement faded in Lombardy but stayed strong in **Piedmont** valleys (separate Waldensian church to the present). Logan cautions against pressing German/Bohemian links to Waldès without compelling evidence. The papacy’s later success with [[mendicant-orders|friars]] is framed as having learned from mishandling Cathars and Waldensians.
