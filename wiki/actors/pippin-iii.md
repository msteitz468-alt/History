# Pippin III ("the Short," r. 751–768)

> **Spelling note:** Same person as [[pepin-iii|Pepin III]] (Logan-centred page with Franco-papal alliance detail). Prefer either slug; do not treat as two men.

## Overview

**Pippin III** (Pippin the Short / Pippin the Younger; c. 714–768), son of
[[charles-martel]] and father of [[charlemagne]], was the first Carolingian king of the
Franks. In 751 he deposed the last Merovingian and took the crown with papal sanction
([[pippinid-coup-751]]), and in 754 was anointed — together with his sons — by Pope
Stephen II, sealing the **Frankish–papal alliance** that would define western European
politics for centuries. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Fouracre, Ch. 3) his reign
is the decisive transition from mayoralty to kingship.

## From Mayor to King

On Charles Martel's death (741) the mayoralty was divided between Pippin and his brother
Carloman; when Carloman withdrew to monastic life (747), Pippin ruled alone. Working with
[[boniface]] and the reforming councils of the 740s (the Council of Estinnes, 744), Pippin
burnished the Carolingians' religious credentials. In 751 — with the Frankish bishops
anointing him and the papacy endorsing the act — he set aside the powerless Childeric III and
became king. NCMH II is careful here: Pippin's elevation was *endorsed* rather than *enabled*
by Rome, and Boniface's personal role in the anointing is uncertain; the deeper driver was a
convergence of Frankish reform, Lombard pressure on Rome, and a longstanding Franco-papal
interest against shared enemies.

## The Papacy and the Lombards

In 753–754 **Pope Stephen II** crossed the Alps in person — the first pope to do so — seeking
Frankish protection against the Lombards. At the assembly of 754 the Franks reluctantly agreed
to campaign in Italy; Stephen anointed Pippin and his sons and declared that future Frankish
kings must come from this family alone. Pippin's Italian campaigns (755, 756) checked the
Lombard king Aistulf and produced the **Donation of Pippin (756)** — the grant of former
Byzantine territory in central Italy to the papacy, the territorial foundation of the later
**Papal State** (see [[the-papacy-eighth-ninth-centuries]]).

## Significance

Pippin converted Carolingian military dominance into legitimate, anointed kingship, founded the
alliance with Rome, and bequeathed to Charlemagne an undivided basis for empire (though, in
Frankish custom, he divided the realm between Charlemagne and Carloman at his death in 768).
The sacral anointing introduced a new, Old Testament-flavoured theory of Christian kingship into
the West.

## Historiography

NCMH II (Fouracre) notes that "no event of the eighth century has been more intensively
'explained'" than Pippin's 751 consecration, precisely because the sources — written under his
descendants — work hard to justify a usurpation. The interplay of Frankish, Lombard, Byzantine,
and papal interests behind 751 and 754 remains debated; Noble's *Republic of St Peter* is the
standard guide to the Franco-papal background.
