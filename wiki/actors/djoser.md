# Djoser and Imhotep

The Third-Dynasty king **Djoser** (Horus-name **Netjerykhet**, r. ~2670 BCE) and
his architect, chancellor, and physician **Imhotep** — the partnership that
produced the **Step Pyramid at Saqqara**, the first monumental building in cut
stone and the conceptual ancestor of all the pyramids. They open
[[old-kingdom-egypt|Old Kingdom Egypt]]. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH
Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XIV, W. Stevenson Smith).

## Djoser (Netjerykhet)

On his own monuments the king uses only the Horus-name **Netjerykhet**; the name
**Djoser** is attested only from later periods (the Step Pyramid temple graffiti,
the Ptolemaic Sehel "Famine Stela"). He was linked to
[[early-dynastic-egypt|Khasekhemwy]] (Dynasty II) through Queen Nymaathap, his
probable mother — though the reason for the dynastic break is unclear, and he may
have been preceded by his elder brother **Sanakhte**. His reign "presents an
extraordinarily clear picture of a civilization approaching maturity," still
"slightly barbaric" in its experimental vigour (the tomb of Hesyre, the Heliopolis
shrine reliefs of his queens Hetephernebty and Intkaes).

## Imhotep

**Imhotep** — revered for two millennia after his death as a sage, demi-god,
patron of scribes and physicians, and eventually deified as a god of medicine — was
long suspected to be legendary until his name was found on a **statue-base of
Netjerykhet** at the Step Pyramid, confirming him as a historical official. He was
"King's Sealer"/Chancellor and "Hereditary Prince," but — tellingly for the Old
Kingdom's **non-caste meritocracy** (see [[old-kingdom-egypt]]) — neither a king's
son nor a vizier; "innate ability and the favour of the king" raised him. Later
Egyptians paired him with the sage **Hordedef** as the proverbial wise men whose
words endure though "their walls are destroyed."

Michael Rice ([[sources/rice-egypts-making-2003|*Egypt's Making*]]) calls him "the
first universal genius known to history" and recites the full titulary preserved on the
statue-base: "Chancellor of the King of Lower Egypt, First after the King of Upper Egypt,
Administrator of the Great Palace, Hereditary Nobleman, **High Priest of Heliopolis**,
Builder, Sculptor and Vase-maker in Chief." Rice singles out the **Heliopolitan**
priesthood as decisive: Imhotep may have driven the rise of the **solar cult** that grows
through Netjerykhet's reign and becomes dominant in the Fourth Dynasty (the title **Son
of Ra**). At Saqqara he was himself **worshipped as a healing divinity** in later
centuries — "a benign and kindly divinity… who granted the prayers of the most humble
petitioners" — the apotheosis of a commoner-administrator. He embodies the Third
Dynasty's "new men," competent practical officials raised by merit.

## The Step Pyramid

Imhotep's achievement was twofold: **a new architectural form** — a high stepped
structure raised over the original flat mastaba — and **a new building technique**,
laying the added courses in **inward-leaning** layers for stability at
unprecedented height. He did not invent stone architecture (known since Dynasty
I) but transformed its scale. The complex translates an existing architecture of
**wood, reed, and matting into stone**: dummy gates, fluted columns, log roofs,
faience-tiled underground galleries imitating mat-hangings — an exuberant,
experimental monumentality. It became the template that Sneferu would carry to the
**true pyramid**. Barry Kemp ([[kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy of a
Civilization*]], Chs. 2–3) reads it as the architectural counterpart of the great Dynasty-1
codification of art: the Step Pyramid "represents, in architecture, a major act of
**codification of forms**," translating the portable tent-shrine archetype into permanent
stone so that "later temple architects almost invariably looked" back to it — the founding
moment of [[egyptian-formalism|Egyptian architectural formalism]].

## Significance

Djoser and Imhotep mark the threshold at which Egyptian kingship found its supreme
expression in **monumental stone** — the medium through which [[divine-kingship|
divine kingship]] was made visible for the next thousand years. Imhotep is also the
earliest individual architect/intellectual known by name in world history, and his
millennia-long afterlife as a deified sage is itself a striking datum of cultural
memory.
