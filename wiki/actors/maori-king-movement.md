The Māori King Movement (Kingitanga, "kingship") was an inter-tribal political movement that crystallised in [[new-zealand]]'s North Island between 1852 and 1858 in response to settler land pressure. Its central goal was to create a unified Māori polity capable of banning land sales and defending Māori political autonomy. The movement constituted one of the most sophisticated experiments in Indigenous political counter-organisation in nineteenth-century Oceania. See [[new-zealand-wars-1860]] for the military conflict it precipitated.

## Origins and Formation

"The idea of a unifying kingship was not entirely new." Samuel Marsden, the first missionary, had urged chiefs to make Hongi Hika king to halt inter-tribal disputes in the 1820s.

The modern movement's catalyst was the mid-1850s land crisis. Resistance to land alienation caused various tribes to "disavow inter-tribal rivalries and discuss common interests." Meetings of Māori were held across the North Island, prompting the establishment of inter-tribal councils (rūnanga) and what became known as the kotahitanga (oneness) movement. Initially, these meetings focused on placing a tapu on land sales within certain boundaries; the idea of a kingship then crystallised as a political instrument to enforce that tapu.

In 1852 the Ngāti Raukawa chief Matene Te Whiwhi led a chiefly deputation around the North Island promoting the idea, with "their principal motivation to whakakotahitanga (make unity), to create order from the chaos which followed European settlement." The kingship was offered to several paramount chiefs; most deferred to Potatau Te Wherowhero of the Waikato tribe because of his genealogical seniority, fertile lands, and control over food supplies.

Potatau declined until Wiremu Tamihana threw his weight behind the movement in 1856; several influential chiefs agreed. Potatau was finally crowned in 1858. His charter: "to hold the mana or prestige over the land; secondly, mana over man; thirdly, to stop the flow of blood [inter-tribal wars]; fourthly, the Maori King and the Queen of England to be joined in concord and God to be above them both."

## Ideology and Factions

Three segments of Māori opinion structured the movement:
- **Loyalists** (anti-Kingitanga): feared racial disharmony; wanted their own laws sanctioned by the Governor and administered by European magistrates.
- **Extremist Kingites:** opposed land sales in any circumstances.
- **Moderate Kingites:** willing to yield disputed blocks for peace while preserving Māori autonomy. Wiremu Tamihana was the moderates' voice: "he aimed to protect the mana and lands of the people, but also to live on friendly terms with the government."

Tamihana was "profoundly inspired by the Bible, and quoted the Old Testament to justify a monarchy." The British Colonial Office found this Christian constitutional argument unacceptable precisely because "all Maori were deemed to have ceded sovereignty by signing the Treaty of Waitangi."

## Structure and Limitations

The Kingitanga initially commanded the allegiance of twenty-three tribes, mainly Waikato and Taupo. Many other tribes supported the land policy without pledging allegiance. The movement suffered from structural fragmentation:
- "The coronation of Potatau and the unfurling of the flag of the Kingitanga could only reduce, rather than resolve, tribal rivalries."
- "Some chiefs refused to recognise Potatau as King in the belief that Māori social organisation did not allow for such difference in status."
- Many northern chiefs argued a Māori kingship was incompatible with the Treaty of Waitangi.
- In some areas "feuds erupted between sellers and non-sellers."

Despite disunity, "the mere existence of a king influenced inter-ethnic relations throughout New Zealand, whether or not the local tribe paid homage to Potatau. Māoridom was more centrally organised than it had been."

## Role in the Wars and Aftermath

The Kingitanga served as both the pretext and the primary target of British military action in [[new-zealand-wars-1860]]. After the Waikato invasion of 1863 and Orakau (1864), the government confiscated 3 million+ acres of Kingite tribal land under the New Zealand Settlements Act.

King Taawhiao (successor to Maatutaera) kept the movement alive during the 1870s–1890s:
- In 1884 he led a deputation to England to petition Queen Victoria for return of confiscated lands and recognition of "an independent Maori government" — he qualified this: "I am called a king, not for the purpose of separation, but in order that the natives might be united under one race, ever acknowledging the supremacy of the Queen, and claiming her protection." The petition was refused; under responsible government (from 1852), Māori affairs were the responsibility of the New Zealand government.
- In 1886 he appealed to the New Zealand government to recognise him as head of an annual Māori Council to administer the Treaty. Refused.
- In 1894 he promulgated the constitution of the Great Council — but most tribes outside those most affected by confiscations could not accept his claim.

The Māori Parliament (1892–1902), a parallel initiative by the four Māori members of the New Zealand Parliament reviving kotahitanga, competed with Taawhiao's movement and ultimately dissolved in 1902 amid internal divisions and wider Māori entry into the money economy.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Kingitanga, Māori land loss would likely have occurred faster and at worse terms, as there would have been no unified political body capable of banning land sales over a defined territory. The Kingitanga forced the colonial government to attempt indirect-rule solutions (the "new institutions"/District Rūnanga) before resorting to military force, and it generated legal and constitutional arguments that seeded later Māori rights movements. The confiscations it provoked paradoxically sustained the movement by giving Waikato tribes a focused grievance (rawpatu, "conquest") that lasted into the twentieth century.
