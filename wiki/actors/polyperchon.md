## Role

Polyperchon (c. 380–after 304 BCE), an old officer of [[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II]],
was named regent (*epimeletes* of the kings) of the empire by the dying
[[antipater]] in 319 — over Antipater's own son [[cassander]] — and so became, briefly,
the legal head of [[alexander-the-great|Alexander's]] inheritance. More soldier than
politician, he was rapidly outmatched.

## Decisions and Significance

- **The "Freedom of the Greeks" proclamation (319)**: to counter Cassander's hold on
  Greece, Polyperchon issued, in the name of Philip III, a charter restoring the
  pre-Antipatrid democratic constitutions and recalling exiles — Will analyses it not as
  a revival of the [[league-of-corinth|Corinthian League]] nor a true grant of liberty
  but as an **amnesty**, "completely original, without antecedent and without sequel,"
  and largely ineffective: Athens slipped back to Cassander and oligarchy under
  [[demetrius-of-phalerum|Demetrius of Phalerum]].
- **Alliance with [[eumenes-of-cardia|Eumenes]]**: as his power collapsed, he appointed
  Eumenes *strategos of Asia* against [[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus]] — creating
  briefly two rival regents in Europe and two rival *strategoi* in Asia, recognised by
  almost no one but each other.
- **Recall of [[olympias]]**: he brought Alexander's mother back from Epirus to lend
  prestige to his cause, precipitating the bloody dynastic killings of 317 (Philip III,
  Eurydice, then Olympias herself).
- **Decline**: pushed back into the Peloponnese, he was eventually reconciled with
  Cassander (c. 309), who bought him off with the title *strategos of the Peloponnese* —
  at the price of sacrificing the pretender Heracles, the last possible Argead.

## Significance

Polyperchon is the case study in **legitimacy without capacity**: holding the highest
legal office of the empire, he could not convert it into power, and his failure cleared
the path for Cassander in Europe and Antigonus in Asia. See [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323]].
