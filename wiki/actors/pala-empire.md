# The Pala Empire

The **Pala dynasty** (c. 765–1161 CE) was the last major Buddhist imperial house of India, ruling from **Bengal and Bihar** and at its height projecting power across eastern India into Assam, Orissa, and beyond. The Palas are historically significant as the great patrons of **Mahayana and Tantric Buddhism** at its final flourishing in India, the founders or restorers of the monastic universities that trained the Buddhist world, and one of the three powers contesting the "Tripartite Struggle" for supremacy over north India. Documented through Burjor Avari, *India: The Ancient Past* (Routledge, 2007).

## Origins and Foundation

Bengal had endured "much interference and disruption by its near and farther neighbours" in the century after Harsha's death in 647. "The respite came in 765 with the election by the people of an able leader, **Gopala**, who was neither a brahman nor a kshatriya." This election of a commoner by the people is remarkable in the Indian political context of the period, where legitimacy was ordinarily a function of brahman-kshatriya alliance. Gopala founded the Pala dynasty, which "prospered under Gopala's successors, Dharamapala and Devapala."

## The Tripartite Struggle for Kanauj

"The Palas realised the importance of Madhyadesha in the Indian political world, and fought hard to gain power and influence there." From the late eighth to the late tenth century they participated in what Avari calls the **Tripartite Struggle** — the three-way contest among the Palas, the [[gurjara-pratihara|Gurjara-Pratihara]], and the [[pallava-chalukya-rashtrakuta|Rashtrakutas]] for control of Kanauj and the Gangetic heartland. The Palas maintained marriage alliances with the Rashtrakutas even as all three dynasties competed, and "in the triangular contest over Madhyadesha and Kanauj, all three dynasties ultimately exhausted themselves." The Pala assertion of rights in this struggle meant "Bengal was no longer on the margins of the Indian polity." Over different periods the dynasty also ruled Bihar, Orissa, and Assam.

## Governing Structure and Reach

The Pala kingdom was a feudal monarchy in which the king parcelled out land grants to subordinates in exchange for military service, court attendance, and political loyalty — the standard *samanta* arrangement of the period (see [[indian-feudalism]]). Dharmapala extended Pala suzerainty into Assam, where the strategic position "lying on the main trade routes between Bengal and China and between Bengal and Burma" made it vital. The "extensive silver mines of Burma were particularly crucial." Commodities from Bengal — "aloe-wood, pottery, rice and both fine and coarse textiles" — flowed eastward, while Tantric Buddhist architectural forms and sculpture spread from Bengal into Burma.

The Bengal kingdom's reputation extended beyond India, "into Nepal and Tibet and, above all, towards Southeast Asia, in Java, Sumatra and the Malay peninsula ruled by the **Shailendra dynasty**." "Commerce and religion linked the fortunes of Bengal, Tibet and Nepal." Nepal was a bone of contention between Tibetan and Bengali interests for much of the period; Nepal broke free from Tibetan hegemony in 879, though "the general Tibetan domination was a fact of life that the Palas could not ignore." Tibetan armies occasionally invaded Bengal, and Avari notes that "the Tibetans ultimately contributed to the collapse of the Palas."

## Buddhist Patronage: Nalanda and Vikramashila

"The Palas were strongly Buddhist; and one might say that under their patronage Buddhism obtained its last chance to retain its influence in India before a newly resurgent Hinduism finally crushed it." Buddhist institutions proliferated under Dharmapala and Devapala. Buddhist scholars arrived from Sind, Kashmir, Ceylon, Nepal, Burma, Laos, the Indonesian islands, China, and Tibet. King Dharmapala "continued to support the great Buddhist monastic and educational institute at **Nalanda**, while building another centre of higher learning at **Vikramashila** on the banks of the Ganga in Bihar." Vikramashila drew many Tibetan scholars; Nalanda continued to attract monks from Southeast Asia.

The most important power in Southeast Asia during the Pala apogee was the Shailendra dynasty of the Malay peninsula and Indonesian archipelago. "The Shailendras were the followers of Mahayana Buddhism and derived their religious inspiration from Bengal, which was then the chief centre of the Mahayana and Tantric traditions." The great temple of **Borobudur** in Java stands as the most important physical monument of this Pala-Shailendra Buddhist connection.

## Decline

Pala suzerainty in Bengal and eastern India "lasted until the end of the eleventh century, when they were succeeded by the **Senas**, who, in turn, lost out to the **Turkish Khaljis** in the thirteenth century." By the middle of the eleventh century the great Pala dynasty was nearing its end; from 1080 the Sena dynasty controlled Bengal. Between 1197 and 1205 the Ghurid commander Bakhtiyar Khalji conquered Bihar and Bengal, destroying the great monastic institutions that had sustained Indian Buddhism. With the sack of Nalanda and Vikramashila, the last institutional base of Indian Buddhism was extinguished.

## Significance: The Last Buddhist Empire

The Palas represent the final chapter in the long story of royal Buddhist patronage in India that began with Ashoka and Kanishka. Their support delayed the disappearance of Buddhism from India — a process Avari describes as driven by multiple factors: the revival of Brahmanic Hinduism, the assimilating power of the [[bhakti-movement|bhakti movement]], the loss of royal patronage, the atrophy of the Buddhist laity, and finally the Turkish raids on the wealthy monasteries. By the time the Turks destroyed Nalanda, "Buddhism had been dying in India long before the advent of the Turks."

Their commercial and cultural links — with Tibet, Nepal, Burma, and the Shailendra Empire — kept Mahayana Buddhism alive across a wide pan-Asian network that outlasted the Pala dynasty itself.

## Historiography

The primary evidence for the Palas comes from inscriptions, copper-plate grants, and architectural remains. Arab geographers of the Middle Ages — particularly Al-Masudi and Ibn Khordadbih — described Bengal's commercial networks and the extent of Pala suzerainty. Tibetan Buddhist sources provide additional information on the monasteries and their international connections. The question of whether the Palas should be considered a genuinely "Buddhist dynasty" or one that balanced Buddhist patronage with the normal brahman-kshatriya legitimation apparatus is a matter of scholarly nuance; Avari's position is that they were genuinely committed to Buddhism in a way that made them anomalous among north Indian dynasts of the period. Modern historiography of the Palas has been particularly attentive to their role in the transmission of Mahayana and Tantric traditions to Tibet, Nepal, and Southeast Asia.

## Collection Coverage Note

The Pala dynasty is covered at reasonable depth in Avari's *India: The Ancient Past* (Ch. 10). The collection's South Asian coverage is moderate overall; the Palas receive more attention than many medieval Indian dynasties because of their Buddhist connections (well-covered in the collection) and their role in the Tripartite Struggle.
