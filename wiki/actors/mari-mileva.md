# Mileva Mari

**1875–1948**. Serbian physicist and mathematician; first wife of Albert Einstein; mother of his three children (including illegitimate daughter Lieserl). Only woman in Einstein’s section at the Zurich Polytechnic. Served as intellectual companion and occasional sounding board during the years leading to the 1905 papers, but the marriage deteriorated amid personal strains and Einstein’s rising career.

## Overview

Born in Novi Sad (then Hungary) to an ambitious Serbian family, Mari overcame significant obstacles as a female science student. She attended the Classical Gymnasium in Zagreb and enrolled at the Zurich Polytechnic in 1896, the same year as Einstein (she was three years older). Characterized by friends as “very smart and serious, small, delicate, brunette, ugly,” she possessed a brooding intensity and deep passion for physics and mathematics that attracted Einstein.

## Relationship with Einstein

Their relationship developed slowly. They hiked together in 1897; Mari temporarily left for Heidelberg (auditing Lenard’s lectures on kinetic theory) before returning. Their letters mix passionate intimacy (“Dollie,” “Johnnie,” “wild little rascal”) with scientific discussion: “We understand each other’s dark souls so well...” “I am more and more convinced that the electrodynamics of moving bodies as it is presented today does not correspond to reality...”

Mari helped check math in early papers and served as a partner in the bohemian Zurich and Bern years. She was present for the Olympia Academy discussions (though rarely intervening). They had a daughter, Lieserl (born ~Jan/Feb 1902 in Novi Sad; fate uncertain after 1903 scarlet fever; likely adopted or died; never publicly acknowledged by Einstein). Married January 6, 1903, in Bern (Olympia Academy friends as witnesses). Son Hans Albert born 1904; Eduard born 1910.

## Strains and Divorce

Financial pressures, Einstein’s emotional reserve, Mari’s increasing gloom and jealousy, and the demands of his scientific work strained the marriage. Einstein later described an “inner resistance” overcome only by “sense of duty.” Lieserl’s existence and presumed loss added a “shrouded” personal tragedy. By 1914 they separated; divorce finalized 1919 on terms that included Einstein promising her any future Nobel Prize money (she received it after the 1921 award for the photoelectric effect). Mari raised the sons in Zurich; her later life was marked by isolation and health issues.

## Intellectual Role

Mari’s letters show genuine engagement with physics (kinetic theory, infinity, electrodynamics). Einstein valued her mind: “How proud I will be to have a little Ph.D. for a sweetheart.” Historians debate the exact extent of her technical contributions to the 1905 papers (no surviving co-authorship; some letters suggest she checked calculations). Isaacson treats her primarily as emotional and intellectual partner whose role diminished as Einstein’s independence and fame grew.

## Historiography

Early biographies largely omitted or minimized Mari because surviving correspondence was suppressed until the 1980s–90s. Full access to Einstein-Mari letters (and family context) via the Einstein Papers Project and Isaacson 2007 revealed the depth of the early partnership and the personal costs. Mari’s own scientific promise was curtailed by gender barriers, illness, and family responsibilities. She remains a symbol in discussions of women in science and the personal price of genius.

**Grounding (Isaacson range 1)**: “Very smart and serious...”; “We understand each other’s dark souls so well...”; “I’m the same old rogue...”; detailed letters mixing romance and Helmholtz/Maxwell discussions; pregnancy announcement and Lieserl references (suppressed until late 20th c.); “I love her so much and don’t even know her yet!” (re Lieserl); post-birth domestic notes and later gloom.
