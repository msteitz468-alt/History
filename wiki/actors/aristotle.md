# Aristotle and the Lyceum

## Overview

**Aristotle** (384–322 BCE), of **Stagira** in the Chalcidice, was the most
comprehensive thinker of antiquity — pupil of **[[plato]]**, tutor of
**[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]]**, and founder of the **Lyceum** (the **Peripatos**).
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 12a, Ostwald & Lynch; Preface) singles out his
**heritage** as the part of Athens' fourth-century intellectual flowering that endured
"intellectually a great deal longer" than the 900 physical years of the schools — the
foundation of much of Western and Islamic science and philosophy.

## From the Academy to the Lyceum

Aristotle entered **Plato's Academy** about 367 and remained some twenty years, until
Plato's death (347). Passed over for the headship (which went to Speusippus), he left
Athens, spent time at the court of **Hermias of Atarneus** in the Troad and on Lesbos
(doing pioneering biological research), and was summoned by **Philip II** to tutor the
young **Alexander**. In **335**, after Macedon's victory, he returned to Athens and founded
his own school in the grove of the **Lyceum** — its members the **Peripatetics**
("walkers") — with a library and a collaborative research program. On Alexander's death
(323) the wave of anti-Macedonian feeling drove him to **Chalcis**, lest Athens "sin twice
against philosophy" (the first being Socrates); he died there in 322. His successor was
**Theophrastus**.

## The encyclopedic program

Where Plato turned the mind toward eternal **Forms**, Aristotle turned it back toward the
**observable world**, rejecting the separate existence of Forms and locating form *in*
things. His achievement was the **systematic division and investigation of all
knowledge**: he founded **formal logic** (the syllogism), wrote foundational works on
**physics, metaphysics, biology, psychology, ethics, rhetoric, poetics**, and **politics**
— the last grounded in the collection of **158 city constitutions** (the surviving
*Athenaion Politeia* among them, a key source for this very volume). His method was
**empirical and classificatory**, and his school became the first great center of
organized, cumulative research, anticipating the scholarship of Hellenistic Alexandria.

## Significance

Aristotle, with Plato, defined the intellectual inheritance of the West. The
**Peripatetic** corpus shaped Hellenistic science, was transmitted through the
**Islamic** philosophers (al-Farabi, Avicenna, Averroes) and re-entered medieval Europe as
"the Philosopher" of the schoolmen. His link to **Alexander** also symbolizes the
fourth-century convergence of **Greek thought and Macedonian power** that the Hellenistic
world would carry across three continents. He embodies CAH VI's thesis that Athens'
political eclipse coincided with its rise to enduring **cultural and intellectual
supremacy**.

## OHCW (Annas, Barnes) — method, four causes, logic, school fate

[[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]], Ch. 10 (Julia Annas): Aristotle (384–322) of Stagira entered the Academy at eighteen and stayed until [[plato|Plato’s]] death; later at Assus, tutored [[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] (**little mutual impact** per Annas), returned c.335 to found the Lyceum, fled anti-Macedonian hostility after Alexander’s death (323), died in Euboea (322). Public dialogues survive only in fragments; the Corpus is school lectures/notes later grouped (*Metaphysics*, *Physics*, *Organon*…) with uneven finish and factitious unities — hard co-operative reading, not literary coasting.

**Range and method.** Continuous explanation of nature from high theory down to meteorology and especially living things: first biologist still respected by biologists, with large observational efforts, without systematic experiment or mathematical physics. Method starts from *phainomena* (facts and common observations/explanations), maps conflicts, then rationalizes them — sometimes overturning common sense (e.g. void impossible) while explaining error. Precision must match the subject (*NE* 1094b23–5).

**Four *aitiai* (*Physics* 2).** Mutually irreducible explanations: form/defining characteristics; matter/constituents; source of movement (nearest modern “cause”); end/aim (teleology) — refusing monistic reduction that Plato’s *Phaedo* impatience toward non-Form explanations risked.

**Logic and argument.** Unique conscious innovation: *Prior Analytics* first isolates logical form via schematic letters and classifies valid argument forms, distinguishing soundness from mere persuasion; *Rhetoric* classifies persuasive sources — sorting the muddled fifth-century “art of argument” of the [[sophists|sophists]], even if later logic surpassed him.

**Ethics and politics.** Continues late-Platonic study of society via *Politics* and three ethics works; organizes research into many Greek states’ institutions (*Constitution of Athens* survives) and athletic victor lists. *NE* analyses excellence, the best life, and practical reasoning (“virtue lies in a mean”). Metaphysically: *Categories* takes concrete individuals as first substances; central *Metaphysics* books shift substance toward form; constant hostility to separate Forms/numbers; first mover’s activity is “thinking of thinking” (*Met.* 12) — impersonal mysticism without Plato’s imaginative urgency.

**School fate.** Academy continued; Lyceum’s research commitment suffered war damage and narrowing; after Theophrastus and Strato (d. c.269) “Aristotelian philosophy was a thing of the past” for most of the Hellenistic period (Barnes, Ch. 15), even as Theophrastus attracted ~2,000 students and Ai Khanoum yields an Aristotelian dialogue imprint. Griffin (Ch. 3): Aristotle dismisses Hesiodic “mythical style” once *mythos* yields to *logos*. Murray (Ch. 9): Lyceum name from the gymnasium; deliberative faculty “inoperative” in the female — philosophical underwriting of women’s subordination.

## Freeman (2014) — text transmission, method critique, and the slavery justification

[[sources/freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014|Freeman (2014)]] credits **Andronicus of
Rhodes**, editing the corpus in Rome in the 80s BCE, with the term "**metaphysics**" (*ta
meta ta physika*, "after the physics") — a cataloguing accident, not an Aristotelian
coinage. On method, Freeman cites **Geoffrey Lloyd**'s critical point that Aristotle
"often started with a hypothesis and then shaped observations to prove it" — a caution
against treating his empiricism as naive, objective data-gathering. Freeman also quotes
at length Aristotle's *Politics* justification of slavery on explicit ethnic/climatic
grounds — Europeans "full of spirit but deficient in intelligence," Asians "intelligent...
but lack spirit," Greeks uniquely combining both and therefore fit to rule — attributed
directly to Aristotle's own text, not to Freeman's voice.
