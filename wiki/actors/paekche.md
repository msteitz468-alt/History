# Paekche

**Paekche** was the southwestern kingdom of the [[korea-three-kingdoms|Korean Three Kingdoms]], in the
Han River basin and later the Kum River basin, from the early centuries AD to its fall in **660**. In
[[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes's]] account it is the great **conduit of continental culture
to Japan** — writing, Buddhism, stoneware, gold- and saddle-working all reached [[yamato-japan|Yamato]]
through the Paekche connection.

## Origins

Paekche is remembered as an elite group of refugees granted lands near present-day Seoul; its name may
derive from a Mahan polity the Chinese knew as **Bochi**. Like [[koguryo|Koguryo]], its elite claimed
**[[puyo|Puyo]] descent** and shared a pyramidal tomb form and founder myth (its early cobble-mounded
tombs copy Koguryo's Hun-River type). Texts place its appearance in the 1st century BC, but
**archaeological evidence of a stratified society appears only in the 4th century AD** — Barnes's
preferred date. Its early paired capitals were the rounded mountain fortress **Mongchon** and the
geometric walled site **Pungnap** (a 3.5-km rectangle, walls 43 m wide at the base, begun in the late
3rd century), which has yielded roof tiles, ritual ceramics, and sacrificed horses and cattle.

## The Yamato Alliance and the Wars with Koguryo

When Koguryo destroyed the commanderies (313–314) and pushed into the Han River basin, Paekche appealed
to Yamato — a tie probably forged through their **mutual procurement of [[kaya|Kaya]] iron** and sealed
by the gift of the inscribed **seven-branched iron sword (dated 369)**. The late 4th–6th centuries are a
story of shifting alliances: **Paekche + Yamato vs. Koguryo**. Under Koguryo's King Kwanggaeto, Paekche
lost 58 fortresses; Koguryo pressure forced two capital moves — to **Ungjin (474)** and **Sabi (538)**.
Paekche elite had received titles from the Eastern Jin and dispatched scribes to the Yamato court;
about 28% of a 9th-century reckoning of Japanese elite clans had continental roots, many Paekche.

## Buddhism and Fall

Buddhism reached Paekche in **384** (via an Indian monk resident in Eastern Jin) and flourished from the
early 6th century under Liang Dynasty ties; a Paekche king sent Buddhist statues and sutras to Yamato in
**538 or 552**, the event that introduced Buddhism to Japan. Paekche fell to **Silla (660)**, which had
allied with Tang; Paekche restorationists and a Yamato relief fleet were defeated, accelerating Japan's
own 7th-century reforms.
