## Overview

Lukeni lua Nimi is the traditional founder of the [[kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]], the dominant pre-colonial polity of West Central Africa. His personal name is reconstructed from the title *Ntinu Wene* ("king of the kingdom"), which appears in the coronation traditions recorded by Mateus Cardoso (1624). The "Motino Bene" of Cardoso's text is now interpreted as a probable copyist's corruption of "Motino Wene/Ñtinu Wene," with the founding king's personal name given elsewhere in the tradition as Lukeni lua Nimi.

His historicity is accepted in modern scholarship, though the details of his life and deeds derive from oral traditions embedded in seventeenth-century written sources rather than any contemporary record.

## Origins and Genealogy

According to the tradition preserved by Cavazzi (1687), Lukeni lua Nimi was the son of **Nimi a Nzima** (*Eminia-n-zima* in Cavazzi's transcription), who ruled Mpemba Kasi — a sub-domain of the Vungu confederation on the south bank of the Congo River. His mother was connected to the ruling family of **Mbata**: specifically, Nimi a Nzima had married into the family of Nsaku Lau (*Nsa-cu-clau*), the lord of Mbata. Cavazzi records his mother as "Lukeni lua Sanze" and his maternal uncle as "Mpuku a Nsuku."

This genealogy explains the structural relationship between Kongo and Mbata that Afonso I (1509–42) described in his letters: the king of Kongo was required to be the "closest relative" of the Mwene Mbata, and no one could be made king without Mbata's consent. Thornton (ch. 1 of Bostoen & Brinkman 2018) argues this genealogical connection reflects the political reality that Mbata was initially the senior partner in the founding alliance.

## Role in Founding Kongo

Thornton (2018) reconstructs Lukeni lua Nimi's path to power as follows:

- Operating from a mountain fortress in **Nsi a Kwilu** (a highland district north of modern Kimpese, through which the Kwilu River flows), Lukeni lua Nimi gathered followers and gathered tribute-paying dependants.
- He provoked a local incident — Cavazzi (1687) records that an aunt refused to pay tribute, "saying he should respect her for her relationship to him," and Lukeni stabbed her in "her pregnant womb." Far from causing disgrace, this act reportedly increased his reputation and following.
- He moved south to the unoccupied (or lightly occupied) mountain of **Mbanza Kongo**, establishing his capital there and founding the kingdom.
- He defeated and incorporated Mwene Mpangala, the existing lord at the foot of the mountain, granting his descendants hereditary rights in exchange for perpetual submission. Mpangala's heirs conducted an annual ritual challenge to the king's legitimacy, presenting a woman to court to protest the conquest, as late as Cavazzi's own time (mid-seventeenth century).
- He allied with (rather than conquered) the nearby earth-priest authority, the Mwene Kabunga, who held religious and secular rights over the region south of Mbanza Kongo to the coast.

The tradition in Cardoso (1624) describes the foundation as a relatively simple conquest: Motino Bene crosses from Vungu, invades Kongo, and assigns provinces to his supporters. Cavazzi's account, drawing on local and non-royal sources as well as court tradition, presents a more complex story with two stages (Coimba → Nsi a Kwilu → Mbanza Kongo) and multiple reigns before the capital was definitively established.

## Counterfactual Significance

Thornton argues the alliance with Mbata was essential to the success of Lukeni's enterprise — without it, a ruler of Mpemba Kasi would have lacked the political legitimacy and connections into the Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza that enabled the subsequent expansion. The western provinces of those Seven Kingdoms (Nsundi, Mpangu, Mbata itself) formed the core of the early Kongo kingdom; without the Mbata connection, access to this territory would have required military conquest of a much stronger adversary.

## Historiography

Lukeni lua Nimi appears in the historical record only through traditions recorded at least a century after his death. The key sources are:

- **Mateus Cardoso (1622/1624)**: Attended Pedro II's coronation and recorded the official royal history, normally delivered by the Mwene Mbata; in this case given by the Mwene Vunda. Published as "History of the Kingdom of Congo" (Bontinck 1972 for a fully annotated French translation).
- **Duarte Lopes / Pigafetta (1591)**: Lopes served as Kongo's ambassador to Rome 1584–9; his geographical account preserves an earlier layer of provincial tradition emphasizing Mbata's role.
- **Giovanni Cavazzi (1687)**: Capuchin missionary, visited Kongo 1664; drew on both court and local traditions from outside the capital, providing the most detailed genealogy of Lukeni and the most nuanced account of the founding.
- **António de Oliveira de Cadornega (1681)**: Soldier-chronicler based in Portuguese Angola; collected traditions c. 1640s–50s from the Okanga region of eastern Kongo; provides the otherwise-overlooked account of Kongo dia Nlaza as a point of origin.

Modern historians (Vansina 1963; Thornton 2001 and 2018; Hilton 1985) have interpreted these sources with varying degrees of skepticism. The KongoKing project's linguistic finding that the word *ngangula* (blacksmith, an important figure in Kongo traditions) derives from eastern Kikongo dialects — not from the southern dialect of the Vungu region — provides indirect linguistic corroboration for an eastern dimension to the origins narrative.
