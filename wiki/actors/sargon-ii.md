# Sargon II

**Sargon II** (reigned 721–705 BCE) was the founder of the **Sargonid dynasty**
that ruled Assyria at its zenith. Like [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]]
he was almost certainly a **usurper**: he never names his father (save one
glazed plaque), and his throne-name *Šarru-kēnu*, "legitimate king," and his
revival of interest in [[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon of Akkad]] both betray a man
asserting a legitimacy he lacked. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]]
(Ch. 22, A. K. Grayson) is the authority. He is the **Sargon** of Isaiah 20:1 —
ironically the only biblical mention of the king who completed the destruction of
[[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]].

## Accession by revolution

The **Ashur Charter** records that **Shalmaneser V** (724–722) was deposed by the
gods for imposing corvée on the privileged cities of **Ashur and Harran**; Sargon
restored their exempt status (*kidinnūtu*) and so won their backing in the coup.
He deported "6,300 Assyrian criminals" — his domestic opponents — and conducted no
foreign campaign in his first year, securing his throne first. Fearful of the old
nobility at Calah, he founded an entirely **new capital, Dur-Sharrukin ("Fort
Sargon," modern Khorsabad)** north of [[nineveh|Nineveh]], begun 717.

## The fall of Samaria and the West

The confusion of his accession triggered a Levantine revolt led by **Yau-bi'di of
Hamath** (Damascus, Simirra, Arpad, Samaria). Sargon met the allies at **Qarqar
in 720** — a century after the [[battle-of-qarqar-853bce|first battle of Qarqar]] —
won, and drove south to defeat an Egyptian army at **Raphia**. The bulk of the
deportation of **[[fall-of-samaria-722bce|Samaria]]** (the city had fallen to
Shalmaneser V in 722) was carried out in his reign, and he resettled Arabs and
others in its place. He extended Assyrian power into Philistia, crushing the
revolt of **Yamani of Ashdod** (712), the event Isaiah marks.

## Anatolia: Midas and Carchemish

On the Taurus frontier Sargon contained two powers: **[[urartu|Urartu]]** under
Rusa I, and the **Mushki (Phrygians) under Mita (the [[phrygians|Midas]] of
Greek legend)**. Midas intrigued ceaselessly with the buffer states; in response
Sargon **annexed [[neo-hittite-states|Carchemish]] in 717** (ending the last
great independent Neo-Hittite state), absorbed Tabal and Que, and after years of
war finally made **peace with Midas** — his most valued Anatolian outcome. He
also received tribute and a stela-exchange from seven kings of **[[cyprus|Cyprus
(Yadnana)]]**, the first firm Assyrian contact with the island.

## The eighth campaign (714)

His most celebrated single feat, narrated in a magnificent **"letter to the god
Ashur,"** was the **eighth campaign of 714** against [[urartu|Urartu]]: a march
through the Zagros and the Mannaean lands that broke Rusa I's power and **sacked
the temple of the god Haldi at Musasir**. It removed Urartu as a serious rival
for a generation. Babylonia, however, slipped away: the Chaldaean
**[[neo-babylonian-empire|Merodach-baladan II]]** seized Babylon at Sargon's
accession and held it until 710, when Sargon finally expelled him and took the
Babylonian crown himself.

## Death in battle

In **705** Sargon was **killed on campaign** (probably in Tabal/Anatolia) and —
uniquely and shockingly for an Assyrian king — **his body was never recovered**
for burial. The Assyrians read it as proof of a terrible hidden sin; his son
[[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] abandoned the unfinished Dur-Sharrukin, never named
his father in his inscriptions, and ordered an inquiry into Sargon's guilt. The
trauma shadowed the dynasty.

## Significance

- `produced:` the **Sargonid dynasty** ([[sennacherib]], [[esarhaddon]],
  [[ashurbanipal]]) and the empire at its territorial height — see
  [[neo-assyrian-empire]].
- `produced:` the final destruction of [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] as a people
  through the [[fall-of-samaria-722bce|deportation of Samaria]].
- `contributed_to:` the eclipse of [[urartu|Urartu]] and the annexation of the
  last [[neo-hittite-states|Neo-Hittite states]].

## Historiography

Exceptionally well documented — abundant royal inscriptions, the reliefs and
records from **Dur-Sharrukin**, chronographic texts, and a large letter-archive —
though as Grayson notes there is still **no up-to-date corpus** of his royal
inscriptions, and the chronology of his campaigns (untangled by **Tadmor**)
remains delicate. The boastful annals require correction against the more sober
Babylonian Chronicle.
