# Earl J. Hess

Prominent American military historian specializing in the **American Civil War**. Focuses on the practical details of soldier experience, logistics, and especially **infantry tactics** at the company/regimental level. Author of more than a dozen books on the war.

## Major Works and Contributions
- *Civil War Infantry Tactics: Training, Combat, and Small-Unit Effectiveness* (2015): Definitive study of linear tactics in practice. Uses intensive reading of Scott (1835), Hardee (1855), and Casey (1862) manuals plus hundreds of examples from the *Official Records*. Revises the dominant "rifle musket changed everything" interpretation (associated with McWhiney/Jamieson *Attack and Die*).
- Companion volume *The Rifle Musket in Civil War Combat: Reality and Myth* (2008).

## Key Arguments Grounded in Sources
- The linear system inherited from the Napoleonic era was **not obsolete** with the rifle musket.
- Combat almost always occurred at short ranges (well under 200 yards, often 50-100 yards), similar to smoothbore era.
- Officers and men attained high proficiency in drill and small-unit maneuvers under fire; "multiple maneuvers" in single engagements demonstrate articulation.
- Preference for **lines** for combat (firepower); **columns** for movement and supports within multiple-line formations.
- Frontal attacks and prolonged firefights were common and rational; flanking was difficult to coordinate.
- Shortcomings in Civil War combat were primarily at higher (corps/army) command and coordination, not at the level of regimental/brigade tactics.

## Counterfactual Significance
Without Hess's detailed reconstruction from primary tactical reports, the persistence and effectiveness of linear tactics in the rifle-musket era would remain misunderstood. His work anchors the American Civil War within the broader evolution of Western linear tactics (linking to Napoleonic and Enlightenment practice) rather than treating it as a revolutionary aberration.
