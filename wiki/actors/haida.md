# Haida

Indigenous people of the **Queen Charlotte Islands (Haida Gwaii)**, except the **Kaigani** group with villages on the southern tip of Prince of Wales Island (Alaska). Core [[pacific-northwest-coast]] society; matrilineal kinship; local groups divided into two exogamous clans—**Eagle** and **Raven** (contrast Tsimshian three or four clans). Haida art’s bold austerity of figures on poles is classically distinguished from crowded Tsimshian groupings.

## Role and overview

Maritime hunter-fisher-gatherers in a hierarchical ranked society; major participants in coastal trade and the sea-otter maritime fur trade. Early exuberant trade example: Dixon at Cloak Bay **1787**—300 furs in half an hour. Leaders such as **Cunneah** and **Kow** on the northern Queen Charlottes controlled regional trade access, analogous to Maquinna (Nootka) and Kotlean ([[tlingit]]).

## Key episodes (Fisher)

- **First Spanish contact in the area (July 1774):** Juan Pérez meets Haidas off northwest Langara Island; feather ceremony then trade—clothes, beads, knives for sea otter, mats, hats, plates, spoons, carved boxes, chilcat blankets; two days without Spanish landing.
- **Trade unevenness and diversification:** As Nootka Sound declined when captains preferred other ports, Haidas offset falling fur wealth with **potato cultivation and sales**, wood and **argillite** “curiosities,” and canoes for export—retaining commercial initiative longer than some neighbors. Often had to trade through Fort Simpson Tsimshians under Legaic monopolies for interior Skeena goods.
- **Ceremonial florescence:** Totem poles present at contact but carved more frequently and elaborately in the fur-trade era (late-nineteenth-century house-front forests of free-standing poles). Potlatch intensified with new wealth.
- **1862 smallpox:** Victoria outbreak drove infected northerners home; disease carried at least as far as the Queen Charlottes—within Fisher’s dispersed epidemic chronology (not continuously pervasive).
- **Late artifact trade:** Museum collectors found Haida still driving hard bargains when opportunity allowed, but poverty after poor fishing seasons forced sale of ceremonial objects they were not entitled to—undermining traditional belief systems.

## Settlement-era and later

Subject to BC reserve policy (Douglas piecemeal → Trutch minimal recognition; most of BC without treaty extinguishment). Potlatch criminalization (1884) hit coastal ranked societies including Haida. Alaska Native Brotherhood **1912** included Haida with Tlingit/Tsimshian. Twentieth-century artistic renown (e.g. Bill Reid noted in later CHNPA arts survey).

## Counterfactual significance

Haida economic diversification and art-production show Fisher’s “reciprocity / Native initiative” fur-trade model in action—adaptation along preexisting commercial and ceremonial lines rather than simple dependency collapse—until settlement and disease eroded the resource base that made that strategy possible.

## Historiography

Central case for Northwest Coast art history and potlatch studies; Fisher cites cultural-florescence literature while rejecting Barbeau’s claim that poles originated only with the fur trade. Disease impact on Haida Gwaii must be stated with **local-variation and magnitude hedges** (Fisher), not continental worst-case ratios alone.
