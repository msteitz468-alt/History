# The Arabs and the Desert Peoples

The **Arabs** of the third and fourth centuries — for the ancients, defined not by language but by **way of
life**: the man "capable of living in the desert," nomad or oasis-dweller, the people we call **Bedouin**.
Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 16 (**Maurice Sartre**), whose theme is the **emergence of
the Arabs**, for the first time, "from domination by neighbouring civilizations to make their own mark on
the population of the fertile crescent."

## Unity and diversity

From the Antitaurus to the Red Sea, the desert groups formed a "homogeneous whole," but in varying
relations to the **Aramaic** world. South of the Euphrates the Arabs were almost the only inhabitants;
north of it (Edessa, Hatra, Assur) they were a minority within a **dominantly Aramaic (Syriac) culture** —
yet the region was still called *Arbayestan* / *Beth Arbaye* / Arabia. The Arabs **adopted Aramaic
language, script and many cults** (all the Semitic texts of the region are in Aramaic, none in Arabic), but
retained their own gods (Allat, Manat, Arsu, the Ginnaye) and tribal organization. A key long-run trend
Sartre identifies: **Aramaization declined and arabization rose** — even the Nabataeans' written language
became "arabicized," culminating in the **Nemara inscription**, an Arabic text in Nabataean script.

## Sedentarization

The most important social process was the steady **sedentarization** of Arab groups between the second and
fourth centuries, "not confined to the Hauran but... the whole steppic zone of the Provincia Arabia, from
the Hauran to the Negev." Around water sources, Roman military posts and centres of rural development, Arab
clans settled into villages and small towns (Umm al-Jimal, the Bostra plain, Trachonitis; the revived Negev
cities Oboda, Mampsis, Nessana), many acquiring **autonomous institutions** by the third century and
beginning to write in Greek. The **peace guaranteed by Rome** underlay this expansion. Pastoralism (sheep,
goats, camels) remained the characteristic activity, with the camel-Arabs essential to the **caravan
trade** even as that trade declined.

## The client states and their ruin

Sartre's second half traces the **apogee and ruin of the Arab client states** that policed the desert for
Rome and Persia:

- **Edessa (Osrhoene)**: the Arab **Abgarid dynasty** over a Greek-Syriac city. Allied to Rome from 165,
  it backed Pescennius Niger and was besieged by **Septimius Severus** (195); Severus made **Osrhoene a
  province** but left a client king, later honouring Abgar VIII as "king of kings." **Caracalla** deposed
  Abgar IX and made Edessa a *colonia* (214), ending the principality (with a brief revival to 242).
- **Hatra**: an Arab principality of upper Mesopotamia at its peak in the early third century, whose rulers
  styled themselves **"kings of the Arabs."** Having aided Niger, it resisted Severus' sieges (its defence
  depicted on Severus' arch despite failure), then allied with Rome against Parthia — before the
  **Sassanians destroyed it in 240–241**.
- **[[palmyra|Palmyra]]**: south of the Euphrates, the deeply "arabized" caravan city that under
  **[[zenobia|Odenathus and Zenobia]]** became the lone guardian of the eastern frontier — until its revolt
  and **destruction by Aurelian (272)** gave the Bedouin "free range" and forced Rome to devise new ways of
  managing the desert Arabs.

The wider pattern: as both empires **absorbed or destroyed the caravan cities** (Nabataea 106, Edessa 214,
Hatra 240, Palmyra 272), commerce declined and a **"nomadization" of the lowlands** advanced — the seedbed
of the later Arab buffer kingdoms (Lakhmids, Ghassanids) and, ultimately, the seventh-century conquests.

## Tribes without cities

Sartre also surveys the **stateless** desert Arabs known from their graffiti: the **"Safaites"** of the
*harra* (southern Syria/Wadi Sirhan), pastoralists leaving thousands of south-Arabian-script graffiti,
organized around the family and long genealogies with "absence of any higher authority"; the
**Thamudaeans** of the northern Hijaz; and the **Nabataeans**, whose kingdom fell in 106 but whose people
followed the shifting caravan routes into the Egyptian desert, Sinai mines and Wadi Araba.

## Significance

Sartre's chapter is the volume's account of the **eastern desert frontier** as a social world rather than a
military line — and of a turning point: the Arabs ceasing to be a substratum dominated by Aramaic, Greek
and Iranian civilization and beginning to **make their own demographic and cultural mark** on the fertile
crescent, on the strength of unbroken links to the deep desert and Arabia. It supplies the long background
without which the third century's political upheavals — and the later rise of Islam — "would be
incomprehensible."
