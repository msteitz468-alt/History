# The Zapotec

## Overview

The **Zapotec** (*peni zaa*, “cloud people”; Aztec *Zapotecatl* → Zapoteco) of the
[[oaxaca-valley|Valley of Oaxaca]] built one of Mesoamerica’s earliest primary states,
centered from ~500 B.C. at the hilltop capital **[[monte-alban|Monte Albán]]**. Marcus
and Flannery (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 8) reconstruct a continuous sequence from Early Formative
villages through Classic urban imperialism to Postclassic balkanized kingdoms. The
Zapotec case is also central to the **“sister culture”** reading of Formative Mesoamerica:
independent highland complexity interacting with, rather than deriving from, the Gulf
Coast [[olmec|Olmec]] ([[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]]; [[olmec-mother-vs-sister-culture]]).

Mixtec and Zapotec are **Otomanguean** languages likely diverged from a common ancestor
in the Oaxaca region before 4000 B.C. Both became militaristic, highly stratified
cultures with rulers’ genealogies in hieroglyphic texts; the Zapotec developed a major
urban center at Monte Albán, while the [[mixtec|Mixtec]] developed a system of
**cacicazgos** (semi-independent polities).

## Setting and periodization (Marcus & Flannery)

Zapotec civilization began in the large, broad Valley of Oaxaca (~2,000 km² alluvium/
piedmont/slopes, ~1,550 m asl) and later spread to the northern sierras and Tehuantepec
coast. Authors’ generic periods: Early Formative 1900–850 B.C.; Middle Formative
850–350; Late Formative 350–150; Protoclassic 150 B.C.–A.D. 150; Early Classic A.D.
150–500; Late Classic 500–750; Early Postclassic 750–1000; Late Postclassic 1000–1530.

## Formative trajectory: villages to chiefdoms

After Archaic seminomadic hunting/collecting/incipient agriculture, populations became
sedentary in Early Formative hamlets. Oldest known house: **House 20 at
[[san-jose-mogote|San José Mogote]]** (~1900–1400 B.C.) with the area’s oldest ceramics.
By 1400–1150 B.C., ~18–20 valley hamlets; SJM largest (71–186 persons over 6.8 ha);
~1350 B.C. first public “Men’s House.” Between 1150–850 B.C. ranking emerges: greater
SJM ~791–1,976 persons over >70 ha — no peer; magnetite mirrors from Ward A traded to
Morelos and **[[san-lorenzo-olmec|San Lorenzo]]** (peer export supporting sister-culture
readings). Middle Formative chiefdoms: adobe architecture, irrigation, three-tier
hierarchy headed by SJM 60–70 ha. **Monument 3** at SJM — oldest carved monument with
Zapotec hieroglyphs known — shows a slain captive and the 260-day name **“1 Earthquake.”**

## Monte Albán state

~**500 B.C.** Monte Albán founded on a previously unoccupied mountain 400 m above the
plain at the junction of the valley’s three arms — defensible and central, though
waterless and far from best farmland. Blanton estimates 3,600–7,200 by 400 B.C.;
10,000–20,400 by 250 B.C. Many valley-floor villages abandoned/shrank; ceramic/
architectural continuities indicate valley-floor founders. Earliest settlement in three
discrete wards → Blanton’s **confederacy** of multi-area villages; authors compare to
Greek **synoikism**.

**Building L *danzantes*:** >300 carved slain captives (misnamed “dancers”); with
defensive walls 300–100 B.C., warfare was a founding consideration. **Protoclassic
state institutions** after 200 B.C.: kingship (governmental palace, royal tombs, funerary
urns with Cociyo/Lightning masks); state religion in standardized two-room temples;
ballcourt (Roman-numeral-I plan); defensive wall doubling as reservoir. **Building J**
conquest slabs (Caso/Marcus): identifiable provinces including Cuicatlán (Redmond &
Spencer: Zapotec military takeover ~200–100 B.C., 15 ha fortress) — subject territory
radius ~80–150 km. Four-tier settlement hierarchy: capital >1 km²; secondary centers
(SJM 60–65 ha with palace, ballcourt, temples); tertiary temple sites; >400 base villages.

**Classic peak ~A.D. 600:** ~6.5 km², Blanton ~30,000 population. Controlled-access,
fortified, religious-regal mountaintop capital — contrast [[teotihuacan]] as planned,
accessible, unfortified commercial city on an irrigated plain. Zapotec did **not**
concentrate countryside artisans in the capital (unlike Teotihuacan’s craft compounds).
Cowgill/Millon “special relationship”: Zapotec barrio on Teotihuacan’s western outskirts
(Monte Albán-style pottery, funerary urns, tomb jamb **9 Earthquake**); no reciprocal
Teotihuacan enclave at Monte Albán. Early Classic monuments include peaceful ambassadorial
scenes (South Platform corner stones; Lápida de Bazán) — possible diplomatic agreement
maintaining social distance, tribute boundaries, and special relationship. Marcus 1983
(cited in Cowgill’s Teotihuacan chapter): Monte Albán state clearly **independent** of
Teotihuacan; emissaries as rough equals, not victories of either over the other.

Detail on urban form, Danzantes counts, and Snow et al. synthesis: see
[[monte-alban|Monte Albán]].

## Breakdown A.D. 600–900 and Postclassic balkanization

As [[teotihuacan]] declines and by ~700 ceases as major urban focus, “almost as if by
chain reaction” other great cities including Monte Albán follow. Decline gradual: by 750
Main Plaza crumbling, elite relocation; still used for important Early Postclassic
burials (≥40 tombs; some Tohil Plumbate from Maya area). **Jalieza** surpassed Monte
Albán in the Early Postclassic. Former secondary centers (Zaachila, [[mitla|Mitla]],
Cuilapan, Macuilxóchitl, etc.) became regional head towns. Classic monopoly on
hieroglyphic writing broke; secondary centers produced stone **genealogical registers**.

**A.D. 1000–1530:** balkanization — no single Zapotec capital; numerous small hostile
polities; widespread fortification; frequent battles and ephemeral truces via royal
marriage. Mixtec nobles warred among themselves but united with Zapotec against Aztec
expansion. Class-endogamous upper stratum: *coqui* hereditary lord, *xoana* nobles,
powerful *coquitáo* “great king.” Political capital **Zaachila**; religious capital
**[[mitla|Mitla]]** (*uija-tào* high priest “like our pope,” Hall of the Columns).

## Aztec campaigns and Spanish Conquest

From Ahuitzotl’s accession (1486), southern campaigns aimed at a pacified route through
Oaxaca to Tehuantepec and Guatemala. Zaachila III’s “Battle of Huitzo” read as a Zapotec
diplomatic coup (Mixtec did most fighting; secret Aztec garrison). ~1495 **Cociyoeza**
(son of Zaachila III) and Mixtec allies based at fortress **Guiengola**; months of
Aztec siege stalemate (night captures of guards “butchered like deer”); truce and
marriage Cociyoeza × Aztec princess Coyolicatzin (Ahuitzotl’s daughter); son Cociyopii
later baptized by Spaniards. Spaniards entered Oaxaca 1520s–30s with Aztec interpreters.

## Cultural profile and legacies

Stereotypes in Marcus & Flannery’s closing synthesis: Zapotec as architects/diplomats;
Mixtec as warriors/jewelers. Zapotec may have been first with hieroglyphic writing, stone
masonry, and stucco; number 5 as bar (Maya-area trade contacts: Plumbate, Fine Orange).
Bilateral “Hawaiian” kinship; Zapotec do not distinguish first cousins from siblings as
Mixtec do. Genealogies in **stone** (vs Mixtec **codices**). Modern: >300,000 Zapotec
speakers in Mexico (Marcus & Flannery figures).

## Colonial–modern trajectory (Romero Frizzi, CHNPA II.2 Ch. 19)

**Contact and consolidation.** On the eve of Spanish arrival, valley and isthmus Zapotecs
lived in highly stratified societies under hereditary lords, unlike relatively egalitarian
Mixes and Chontales. The **Zapotec lord of Tehuantepec** sought alliance with Cortés before
captains landed; mountain Zapotecs (with Mixes) shut themselves off and resisted for decades;
valley Zapotecs sought to use the strangers. Spanish rule consolidated in hospitable areas by
the 1530s (elsewhere ~thirty years later). **Antequera** (~80 vecinos) sat as a Spanish island
in the [[oaxaca-valley|valleys]]. By the 1530s most valley kingdoms paid tribute continuing
Mexica patterns (*part_of:* [[oaxaca-indigenous-colonial-modern]]).

**Sixteenth-century boom and destructuring.** Valley Zapotecs (with [[mixtec|Mixtecs]]) grew
wheat with plows by ~1540 in Etla and elsewhere; silk, livestock, and cochineal ~1540–1570
brought village wealth. Far from defeated, they prolonged past splendor in a mercantile
economy. By ~1590 valley natives refused to obey lords; preconquest regional domains
(including **Zaachila**, Tehuantepec) gave way to **peasant villages** by century’s end.

**1660 Tehuantepec rebellion.** Mid-seventeenth-century *repartimientos de mercancías* under
alcaldes mayores provoked the **1660 Zapotec rising** at Tehuantepec against cotton allotments
exceeding weaving capacity — extended to Mixe and Chontal sierras, Mixteca Baja, northern
Zapotec sierra, and Chinantecs. Romero Frizzi calls 1660 the **last great colonial revolt** in
Oaxaca, ending vigorous sixteenth-century-style response.

**Communal revitalization and nineteenth–twentieth centuries.** Post-1660 cabildo/cofradía
structures filled the vacancy left by high nobility (Carmagnani). Isthmus Zapotecs retained
communal land and coastal **salt**; **1825** private monopoly order provoked multi-ethnic
discontent; risings **1827, 1834** for salt, land, and against Oaxaca merchants continued into
the later nineteenth century. Valley disentailment milder where privatization already existed
among Indians (**604** valley disentailments in **1867** alone). Revolution: isthmus Zapotecs
fought for local political control and lost salt deposits; **Sierra de Ixtlán Zapotec
(1912–20)** backed mountain caudillos who declared Oaxaca sovereign against Carranza — village
natives fighting neighbors, sometimes unsure which national faction invaders represented.
Revolutionary years delivered the **coup de grâce** to remaining indigenous dual economy
(valley/Sierra Norte cotton textiles: ~10,000 indigenous weavers in 1900 vs 600 factory
weavers). Late twentieth century: ~**342,000** Zapotec speakers (largest Oaxaca group); leaders
in **COCEI** and related organizations; 1994 traditional-authority election bill.

## Historiography

Alfonso Caso’s Monte Albán excavations (1930s) founded modern Oaxaca archaeology.
Marcus & Flannery synthesize decades of valley survey (Blanton, Kowalewski, Feinman,
Finsten, Nicholas et al.), San José Mogote excavations, Cuicatlán conquest archaeology
(Redmond & Spencer), and ethnohistory/codex work. Blanton’s confederacy/synoikism model
for Monte Albán’s founding is an interpretation, not raw fact. Snow et al. (2020) add
accessible urban-detail synthesis; CWH IV frames the sister-culture debate. Early writing
may predate earliest Maya inscriptions by centuries (Gulf/Izapa vs Oaxaca calendrics
debate; see also Tres Zapotes Stela C discussion in Gulf chapters).

**Colonial–modern:** Romero Frizzi (CHNPA II.2 Ch. 19) is the controlling narrative for
conquest-to-present; she notes thin colonial documentary collections vs central Mexico
(Burgoa as principal chronicler) and flags Taylor, Chance, Carmagnani, Zeitlin/Tutino
(Tehuantepec), Ruiz Cervantes and Garner (Revolution/sovereignty). Monte Albán is not treated
as a colonial/modern memory site in Ch. 19.
