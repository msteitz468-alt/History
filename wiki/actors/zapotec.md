# The Zapotec (Monte Albán)

## Overview

The **Zapotec** of the **Valley of Oaxaca** were, in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Begun &
Brashler, Ch 20), the key evidence that Mesoamerican complexity did **not** descend from a
single Olmec "Mother Culture" but arose in several regions through interaction and trade. Their
great hilltop capital **Monte Albán** anchored the valley for over a millennium.

## Trade and Early Statehood

During the Preclassic, the Zapotec exchanged finished goods and prized **iron-ore "mirrors"**
(produced in Oaxaca, found in elite [[olmec|Olmec]] contexts on the Gulf Coast) for Olmec
shell and feathers. CWH IV concludes the two cultures **emerged independently** but
**influenced each other** through this interregional trade — the earliest clear case of trade
driving state development in Mesoamerica.

## San José Mogote and the Danzantes

The site of **San José Mogote**, in the valley's northern arm, yields some of the best
evidence for an early **martial elite**. Its **Danzantes** — figures carved in basalt, once
thought to be ritual dancers — are now read as **dismembered, mutilated captives**, attesting
to commonplace and intensifying **warfare**. Many bear an early **Zapotec blood glyph**,
pushing back the evidence for **non-Olmec writing systems** into the Preclassic — among the
earliest writing in the Americas. Power later consolidated at **Monte Albán**, which engaged
**[[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]]** (probable marriage ties between the two dynasties) through the
Classic period.

## Significance

The Zapotec case is CWH IV's strongest argument for the **"Sister Culture"** reading of
Mesoamerican origins: independent, trade-linked centers co-creating a shared civilization. Their
early writing and calendar place them among the foundational literate societies of the
[[mesoamerican-state-formation|Mesoamerican sequence]].

## Monte Albán: Archaeological Detail

Snow, Gonlin, and Siegel (2020, Ch. 13) provide the most detailed synthesis of Monte Albán's
archaeology. The city was founded around **500 BCE** on a prominent hilltop at the confluence
of the three arms of the Valley of Oaxaca, constructed as a **defensive stronghold** — the
valley's communities formed a confederation in response to rising warfare, and Monte Albán was
their collective response. The Olmec trade network was still active at founding; Olmec influence
is visible in the earliest monuments.

Population of the mountaintop center reached **17,000**; the number of small supporting
communities around it grew from 51 to 86; surrounding landscape population topped **24,000**.
By 200 BCE, Monte Albán was unambiguously a **primate city-state**, far larger than any
surrounding community.

The city undertook a massive **landscaping project** to flatten its main plaza: outcrops were
leveled and low spots filled across an area the size of six modern football fields. Buildings were
constructed on and around the flattened plaza; a ball court was built in its northeastern corner.
The final urban configuration took form around **300 CE**. Arrow-shaped **Building J**, north of
the South Platform, bears inscriptions celebrating military conquests (Peeler and Winter 1995).

At approximately **500 CE**, urban expansion produced at least ten more monumental compounds and
dispersed powerful families across increasingly widespread city limits. The economic and power
base derived from tribute flowing from communities across Oaxaca. The city controlled an
obsidian, mica, and ceramics exchange network and maintained close diplomatic relations with
[[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] — Zapotec inscriptions depict leaders receiving visitors in
distinctive Teotihuacan tassel headdresses. The governor-rulers were also priests, identified
as supreme beings.

The **Danzantes** gallery — a series of low-relief carvings in the southwest corner of the
main plaza — numbers some **320 figures** at Monte Albán, with additional examples at smaller
valley centers. These are now understood as sacrificial victims (corpses with limbs akimbo,
closed eyes, missing genitals, and flowing blood curlicues), not dancers.

## Decline and Mixtec Succession

The decline of Monte Albán began around **850 CE**, followed by an abandonment period. By
**1400 CE**, the **Mixtecs** appeared in the Oaxacan valleys and took over some of Monte
Albán's emblematic monuments. The city had lasted some **13 centuries** — a remarkable
duration for a pre-Hispanic urban center.

## Ball Court and Calendar

Monte Albán had a **ball court** in the northeastern corner of the main plaza. The Zapotec
employed **calendrical notation and writing** from the earliest period, providing the
calendarical system upon which the Classic Maya would elaborate. For the Mesoamerican
ball game's significance at Monte Albán, see [[mesoamerican-ball-game]].

## Historiography (Snow et al. 2020 Addition)

The first scientific excavation of Monte Albán was undertaken in the **1930s** by Mexican
archaeologist **Alfonso Caso**, who initiated rigorous archaeological investigation in Oaxaca.
For Snow et al. (2020), Monte Albán exemplifies the multicentered nature of Mesoamerican
complex society: its foundation as a confederation-driven defensive capital, its 13-century
continuity, and its eventual displacement by the Mixtecs form a case study in the resilience
and ultimate limits of a highland urban state. See [[snow-gonlin-siegel-archaeology-native-north-america-2020]].
