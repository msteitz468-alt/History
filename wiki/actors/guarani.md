# Guaraní

A large family of Tupí-Guaraní-speaking peoples occupying the forests and river systems of
the Paraguay–Paraná basin (modern Paraguay, northeastern Argentina, southern Brazil, and
parts of Bolivia) at the time of European contact. The Guaraní became one of the most
consequential indigenous peoples of the colonial La Plata world: their labor and kinship
networks underwrote the founding of Asunción, their women were central to the early
mestizaje of Paraguay, and a large portion of them were drawn into the Jesuit mission
system that made the Guaraní reductions the most famous experiment in colonial mission
society in South America.

This is the concise network summary; the Guaraní recur across the colonial chapters of
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-2|CHNPA III Part 2]].

## Role and Significance

- **Demographic and labor foundation of colonial Paraguay** — the Guaraní *encomienda* and
  intermarriage produced the distinctively mestizo, Guaraní-speaking society of Paraguay.
- **The Jesuit reductions** — from the early seventeenth century the Society of Jesus
  gathered tens of thousands of Guaraní into [[reduccion|reductions]], creating a
  semi-autonomous mission province that defended itself militarily against
  [[bandeirantes|bandeirante]] slave raids and persisted until the Jesuit expulsion (1767).
- **Persistence and ethnogenesis** — Guaraní identity, language, and religious idioms
  survived conquest, mission, and nation-state formation, an example of the
  [[ethnogenesis|adaptive continuity]] Schwartz and Salomon emphasize.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Guaraní mission-and-mestizaje complex, the colonial La Plata interior would
likely have developed as a thinly held Spanish frontier rather than the bilingual
Hispano-Guaraní society that became Paraguay; the reductions also materially altered the
balance of the Spanish–Portuguese borderland.
