## Overview

Abu al-Faraj Muhammad b. Abi Ya'qub Ishaq al-Nadim, also known as al-Warraq ("the bookseller" or "paper-seller"), was a tenth-century Baghdad intellectual who compiled the *Fihrist* (completed 987–988 CE / 377 AH), a comprehensive survey of intellectual production in Islamic civilization to his time. The title means roughly "index" or "catalog."

His personal biography is obscure. The title *al-Nadim* ("boon companion") may refer to him or to his father; his birth and death dates are unknown, though his completion of the *Fihrist* in 987–988 is self-attested. His role as a bookseller or paper-seller gave him unusual access to the intellectual production of his period.

He is described by Saliba as "the foremost theoretician of the early Islamic period" whose work "is filled with nuggets that seem to have escaped the attention of modern students of Islamic intellectual history as well as the modern historians of Islamic science."

## The Fihrist: Structure and Significance

The *Fihrist* is organized into ten treatises (*maqalat*), each covering a distinct intellectual field. **Book 7** ("On the Philosophers and the Ancient Sciences") is central to Saliba's argument. In it, al-Nadim surveys opinions current in his time about how the "ancient sciences" (*'ulum al-awa'il*) entered Islamic civilization, arranging these as a series of *hikayat* (stories or anecdotes).

Al-Nadim acts in Book 7 not merely as a chronicler but as an intellectual historian: "he arranges those accounts in the form of anecdotes…but in each case he reports the transmission of science from one culture to another as if he was trying to lay down the theoretical foundation for the phenomenon of the transmission of science in general."

### The Four Accounts in Book 7

**First account (Abu Sahl b. Nawbakht, from *Kitab al-Nahmatan*)**: A legendary account tracing all sciences to Babylon, destroyed by Alexander and partially recovered by the Sasanian dynasty. Saliba reads this as professional self-justification by a court astrologer of Persian descent, reflecting *shu'ubiyya* sentiment, rather than historical narrative.

**Second account**: Traces the transmission of philosophy from Greece through Byzantium (where it was suppressed by Christian emperors, with the brief exception of Julian's reign) to the Islamic world where "philosophy was finally freed only when it reached the lands of Islam" (in al-Farabi's formulation, which al-Nadim cites). Similarly self-interested in origin.

**Third account**: Involves the Syriac-Persian transmission channel, centered on Jundishapur, and a legendary episode involving the Byzantine emperor Julian, the Sasanian king Shapur II, and the rescue of Shapur by Julian's daughter.

**Fourth account / al-Nadim's synthesis**: Connects 'Abd al-Malik's order to arabize the *diwan* to the subsequent spread of philosophical and scientific books. This is the account Saliba identifies as historically grounded and as the basis for his [[islamic-science-origins|administrative-needs thesis]].

### Al-Nadim's Interpretive Strategy

Saliba argues that al-Nadim deliberately opens with the accounts scientists themselves gave of their tradition's origins (knowing they would reflect professional biases) before moving to his own synthesis. The move from the legendary accounts of Abu Sahl and others to the *diwan*-arabization connection reflects a proto-sociological insight: that scientific traditions must be explained by social and institutional conditions, not by legendary genealogies.

"It was al-Nadim as well who saw that appropriation [of the ancient sciences] as a consequence of the reform [of the *diwan*]. One wonders if 'Abd al-Malik himself ever contemplated all the consequences that his order entailed."

## Role and Counterfactual Significance

Without the *Fihrist*, the institutional history of the early Islamic translation movement would be almost entirely opaque. The *Fihrist* is the source for:
- Documentation of which texts existed in Arabic and who owned or had read them as of 987 CE
- Attribution of translations to specific patrons (revealing the bureaucratic rather than caliphal character of patronage)
- The account of al-Hajjaj b. Matar's *Almagest* translation (c. 829 CE) and its dating
- Evidence for the Nawbakht family, the Banu Musa brothers, and other scientist-bureaucrats
- The stories of how science transmission was theorized by contemporaries

Saliba's alternative narrative is substantially dependent on al-Nadim: "I do believe that we know enough, at least in the discipline of astronomy, so that we can use it as a template through which we can build a more accurate narrative" — and that template is substantially built from *Fihrist* evidence.

## Historiography

The *Fihrist* has been used by historians of Islamic science since the nineteenth century, primarily as a bio-bibliographical catalog. Max Meyerhof's famous article "Von Alexandrien nach Baghdad" (1930) drew heavily on al-Farabi's account (which al-Nadim quotes) to argue for a Syriac-mediated transmission — an argument Saliba's re-reading of the *Fihrist* substantially dismantles by showing that al-Farabi's account is itself a motivated professional narrative.

Saliba's use of the *Fihrist* is distinctive in reading it as a work of intellectual history with an interpretive argument, rather than as a transparent catalog. This approach to al-Nadim is itself a contribution to Islamic historiography.
