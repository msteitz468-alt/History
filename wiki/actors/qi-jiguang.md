# Qi Jiguang (Ch'i Chi-kuang, 1528–1588)

Leading Ming general of the sixteenth century, best known for successful campaigns against Japanese and Chinese pirates (wokou) in the southeast and for innovative military organization and fortifications on the northern frontier. The central subject of Chapter 6 in Huang's *1587, A Year of No Significance*, presented as the "lonely general" whose talents could not be institutionalized within the Ming system.

## Background and Context of Military Decline

By the mid-sixteenth century the Ming military establishment had largely collapsed in practice, even as paper strength remained enormous. On the northern frontier the Mongol leader Altan Khan penetrated Chinese defense lines at will. Along the east coast a band of pirates "reportedly fifty or seventy men, was bold enough to maraud inland on a route that encircled the southern capital, which—unbelievably—had a garrison boasting, at least on paper, 120,000 men." The defense installations and logistical framework "had largely vanished."

The hereditary military household system, intended to shield the population from conscription, had broken down: "It was not unusual for a colony to be reduced to a skeleton, in some extreme cases a tiny fraction of its former self, such as 2 to 3 percent of its originally recorded strength." Soldiers were routinely used as construction workers, porters, and domestic servants. Logistics operated on the principle of "lateral transmission of supplies at the lowest possible level," with the Ministry of Revenue acting as "a huge accounting office" that could not adapt or guarantee deliveries. The result was a gulf between theory (centralized state with largest army in the world) and practice that left field forces ineffective.

Civil officials lacked professional interest in the army and were philosophically hostile to the concentration of force and drastic action required for military success. "In the minds of the bureaucrats strength was not power, regional interests should be minimized rather than freely championed, and there was no distress that could not be relieved by the spirit of sharing."

## Southeast Campaigns Against the Wokou

When transferred to Chekiang in 1555 amid the coastal pirate crisis, Qi Chi-kuang "had virtually to organize a new army." He personally "settled the recruiting procedure, decided the pay scale, devised general rules governing personnel assignment, standardized the organization of combat formations, selected weapons, outlined the duties of individual soldiers and their officers, designed his own banners and coordinating signals, invented his own tactics and schemes of maneuver, prescribed military etiquette, and issued his own orders of court martial, which, based on the principle of group responsibility, compelled both officers and men to guarantee one another's performance in combat on threat of the death penalty. He even handed out a recipe for making field rations!"

He recruited volunteers from inland Chekiang districts rather than hereditary families or urban misfits, using moral suasion and threats drawn from popular religion. He exhorted his soldiers that their silver pay came from taxpayers: "All they expect is that you beat off the pirates in one engagement or two. If you do not even try to kill the pirates to give those people protection, what are they feeding you for?"

His command achieved a record of attacking heavily defended positions, relieving sieges, and pursuing pirates offshore "without losing a battle." He personally cut down a retreating platoon leader during the 1562 battle to recapture a stone bridge, renewing the attack that ended in victory.

## The Mandarin Duck Formation

Qi focused on contact weapons and small-unit teamwork suited to his peasant recruits and the limitations of available firearms. The fundamental principle was a "dialectic approach": every posture had yin-yang duality (static/kinetic, guarded/unguarded, defensive/offensive), with mastery depending on "proper rhythm—or perfect timing in transforming the yin to the yang." The cardinal rule in dueling: "maneuver the opponent into a false move before delivering the fatal blow."

The most creative element was squad teamwork. Against pirates, the lance (twelve feet or longer) was most effective but required distance. Qi's "mandarin duck formation" coordinated:

> In order to provide a protective screen for the four lancers in the squad, Ch'i assigned four soldiers to walk ahead of them, one carrying an elongated pentagon shield on the right and one carrying a small round shield on the left, to be followed by two carrying bamboo trees complete with upper branches. Behind the lancers were two rearguard men with three-pronged, fork-shaped weapons, from which arrows propelled by firecrackers could also be fired. Including a corporal and a cook-porter, a squad consisted of twelve men.

The right shield-man dug in to hold the advance; the left threw javelins and lured the enemy; bamboo carriers pinned opponents at distance; fork-men guarded flanks and supplied second-line power. "Instructions were repeatedly given by Ch'i Chi-kuang that soldiers in each squad must be rewarded or punished collectively and that under no circumstances should the lancers be separated from their protective screen." The formation could split or form a continuous front line as terrain required. Rattan shields, pitchforks, and bamboo trees reflected the "peasant outlook"; the tactics accepted that "using two soldiers to perform the work of one" was necessary.

Qi used firearms (muskets and fo-lang-chi) but limited their proportion and never abandoned the contact-weapon squad structure. He recognized their limitations in loading time and reliability (muskets tended to explode; lead shot often did not fit). He authorized only two muskets per squad in later years.

## Transfer to the Northern Frontier and Chichou Command

In 1567–68, following patronage by civil official T'an Lun (a rare bureaucratic specialist in military affairs who had fought alongside troops), Qi was transferred north to take charge of troop training in the Chichou command, principal garrison defending the approaches to Beijing. He became commander-in-chief there for fifteen years—longer than the combined tenure of his ten predecessors.

Chichou faced Mongol cavalry threats (Altan Khan's confederation could field 100,000 mounts). Qi brought southern volunteers (eventually reaching 20,000) and developed an "infantryman's concept of combined arms." Essential was the battle wagon: a mule cart turned on its side with folding wooden screens forming a fifteen-foot wall. Each wagon carried two fo-lang-chi light artillery pieces (European-origin, muzzle-loaded, range up to 2,000 feet) and an assault team of ten. Wagons formed squares or circles; cavalry screened then withdrew inside; infantry counterattacked from sides and under wagons in bugle-signaled waves once the enemy charge was broken. Cavalry pursued but fought as "infantrymen on horseback" maintaining mandarin-duck principles.

He also proposed and oversaw (with Zhang Juzheng's backing) construction of castle-like watchtowers along the Great Wall. Though scaled back from 3,000 proposed to ~1,200 authorized, these became "the only lasting gift the grand-secretary left to our empire." Northern conscripts (poorly paid, partially self-supporting) were scattered among the towers while southern recruits provided the reliable core.

## Patronage, Politics, and Structural Incompatibility

Qi owed much to T'an Lun and especially Grand Secretary [[zhang-juzheng]]. Chang arranged undivided authority, extra horses and funds, and protection from routine censorial interference (censors' tours limited to once yearly). In private correspondence Chang urged humility amid rivalry between northerners/southerners and old/new officers, and even accepted other commands' credit claims to "silence the jealous rumblings."

Yet this personal patronage made Qi's position precarious. The Chichou command "appeared more and more as a petty project of his [Chang's]." After Chang's death in 1582 and posthumous disgrace, Qi's close association became "unforgivable—at least throughout Wan-li's reign." Three months before Qi's death an investigating censor's suggestion of reappointment cost the censor three months' salary; the rescript reminded that Qi "had recently been impeached."

Fundamental incompatibilities doomed sustained military professionalism. Successful commanders required "selective vision" and "extreme measures"; bureaucrats prized "restraint and moderation" and viewed drastic action as producing only "temporary and localized gains." Army officers inherited positions or passed undemanding exams emphasizing archery and Confucian writing; civil officials supervised operations. "The differences between the two branches of service were too great to allow them to coexist as equals." Qi's personal command, while effective, remained "more personal than institutional—in fact, to many stability-minded civil officials a potential threat to the dynasty rather than a safeguard of it."

## Dismissal, Death, and Complex Character

In 1585 Qi was transferred to Kwangtung command; a year later, amid the purge, he resigned under pressure and was officially "censured and dismissed." He died January 17, 1588 (twentieth day of the twelfth lunar month of the prior Year of the Pig). "The event was not taken notice of by the court." A friend wrote on his tombstone: "When the cock crowed the third time, the star of the valiant soldier fell from the sky."

Huang stresses Qi was no simple hero. He was generous yet dispatched messengers fifty miles to fetch favorite food while complaining of fuel shortages; he revived blood-wine oaths against misappropriating funds yet let his brother deliver substantial gifts to Chang and saw financial records at Chichou "vanish" after his departure. He cut ears from soldiers for offenses and (unconfirmed story) ordered execution of his second son. He incorporated popular religion—constellation flags, propitious days, astrology, lectures on karma and hell—while for literati audiences he named his residence "Hall of a Felicitous Conclusion" (Chih-chih-t'ang) and titled early essays "A Fool's Foolish Draft" to advertise humility. "Because of his social milieu, Ch'i Chi-kuang could never have been a man of single-minded virtue like Hai Jui."

He died destitute; his wife had deserted him. Wang Shih-chen (the century's greatest prose writer, with whom Qi exchanged poems) later implicated him in a controversial biography of Chang.

## Significance and Limitations

Huang presents Qi's achievement as the empire's last realistic opportunity for minimal military modernization. "Three decades later its army was to fight Nurhaci without benefit of the tactics of Ch'i's mixed brigade." Within the constraints of an agrarian empire managed by a literary bureaucracy hostile to commerce, specialization, and concentrated power, Qi "searched for viable ways to build up the best fighting forces under this restriction." He blended yin and yang, accepted peasant recruits and contact weapons alongside early firearms and wagons, and achieved tactical excellence that the system could neither institutionalize nor forgive when tied to a powerful patron.

His story illustrates the core thesis: capable individuals could achieve localized, temporary results, but structural features—ritual over substance, balance over effectiveness, agrarian uniformity over functional specialization—rendered adaptation impossible. "In the age of gunpowder, he went back to brick fortifications. Within his mixed brigade, modern artillery was deployed along with rattan shields."
