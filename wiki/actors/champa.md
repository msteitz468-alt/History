# Champa

**Champa** is the generic name for the **Austronesian-speaking (Cham) polities** strung along the
central coast of what is now **Vietnam**, from roughly the second century CE to their absorption by the
expanding Vietnamese. Documented for the wiki through the
[[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v1-1992|Cambridge History of Southeast Asia]] (Keith Taylor,
Ch. 3), whose treatment is deliberately revisionist.

## Not a kingdom but an "archipelago" of enclaves

Taylor argues that the conventional picture of Champa as a single migrating "kingdom" is a
**twentieth-century rationalization** of scraps of Chinese sources and inscriptions, and should be set
aside. Champa is better understood as an **"archipelagically-defined cultural-political space"** — the
one place (besides the Malay Peninsula) where **Malayo-Polynesian** peoples occupied continental terrain.
Its coast was a string of small, island-like **enclaves** walled off from the interior by mountains and
from each other by ridges running into the sea — a geography that suited the dispersed, mobile,
maritime-nomadic political culture of Austronesian (Cham, and upland Rhade and Jarai) peoples. Rather
than a capital moving south over time, Taylor sees **multiple, simultaneous centres of authority** rising
and falling in different river-mouth clusters. The Chinese knew the northern cluster as **Lin-yi** (a
source of frontier raids until a Sino-Vietnamese expedition destroyed its centre near **Hué in 446**).

## An Indianized, maritime, plunder-based polity

Like its neighbours, Champa was deeply [[indianization-of-southeast-asia|Indianized]] — **Shaiva
Hinduism**, Sanskrit, and brick temple-towers (the famous sanctuary of **My Son**) — and lived by
[[indian-ocean-trade|maritime trade]] supplemented, in Kenneth Hall's economic reading, by **coastal
raiding** (a "plunder-based political-economy"). It was a persistent rival of both **[[khmer-empire|Angkor]]**
(Cham fleets sacked Angkor in 1177) and the Vietnamese to its north.

## Conquest by Dai Viet

Champa's long story ends with the Vietnamese **"march to the south" (nam tien)**. As [[dai-viet|Dai
Viet]] expanded down the coast, it shattered the principal Cham polity, **sacking the capital Vijaya
(near modern Qui Nhon) in 1471** under Le Thanh Ton — the decisive blow. Remnant Cham principalities
survived under increasing Vietnamese pressure until final annexation in **1832**. The Cham survive today
as a Muslim and Hindu minority in Vietnam and Cambodia — one of the few Austronesian-Indic peoples of the
mainland.

## Taylor (2013): the long absorption

K. W. Taylor's [[taylor-history-of-the-vietnamese-2013|*A History of the Vietnamese*]] adds granular detail
on both ends of the relationship with the Vietnamese. **Early (Lin Yi phase):** the northern Cham cluster
warred with Chinese-administered Giao/An Nam from the 190s CE; a major Jin-era expedition "thoroughly
destroyed" its centre near Hue in 443–446, after which the culture recentred near modern Da Nang; the Sui
general Liu Fang sacked a Cham royal seat at Tra Kieu in 602, and resurgent Chams pressured the Tang coast
in the ninth century alongside Nan Zhao (see [[nan-zhao-war-854-866]]).

**The [[conquest-of-vijaya-1471|1471 catastrophe]] and after:** Le Thanh Tong's sack of Vijaya captured and
killed the Cham king and permanently fragmented Champa into rump polities (Phan Rang, the highlands, the
coast south of Binh Dinh). A 1472 edict pushed Vietnamization of Cham names. Thereafter the Cham decline was
demographic as much as military: seventeenth-century Hindu–Muslim factionalism, growing upland (Churu, Ede,
Jarai) influence, and a sequence of defeats — Po Ramo (d. 1651), the loss of territory north of the Phan
Rang River (1653), and the capture of Po Sot (1693) reorganizing the kingdom as the Vietnamese province of
Binh Thuan. A 1712 five-point treaty preserved a nominal Cham "aboriginal king" with limited legal autonomy
until [[minh-mang|Minh Mang]] abolished Cham kingship in 1832 — the closing act of the
[[nam-tien|southward expansion]].
