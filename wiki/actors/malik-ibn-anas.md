## Overview

Mālik b. Anas (d. 179/795) was the leading jurist of Medina in the second/eighth century and the eponymous founder of the Mālikite school (*madhhab*), the dominant tradition of the Hejaz and later of North Africa, sub-Saharan West Africa, and al-Andalus. He is the second of the four Sunnite eponyms in chronological order, younger than [[abu-hanifa|Abū Ḥanīfa]] but older than [[shafii-muhammad-ibn-idris|al-Shāfiʿī]], who was in fact Mālik's student.

## Historical Role

Mālik was the preeminent representative of the Medinese tradition, which held that the authentic Sunna of the Prophet was attested not primarily by formal hadith reports but by the *ʿamal* (practice) of the living Medinan scholarly community — a continuous, unbroken enactment of the way the Prophet had lived and judged, preserved in Medina itself. This conception of sunnaic practice as residing in the community's *living tradition* was the theoretical alternative to the traditionist movement's insistence on formal isnad-based hadith.

His principal legal compilation, the *Muwaṭṭaʾ*, is one of the earliest surviving works of Islamic law and reflects this practice-oriented methodology: it collects both Prophetic reports and the practice of Medinese scholars, presenting them as a continuous whole.

## The Medinese Consensus

Mālik's most distinctive juristic position was the claim that the consensus of the Medinan scholars (*ijmāʿ ahl al-Madīna*) constituted a binding legal authority. His reasoning: Medina was the Prophet's city, and its scholars had inherited his practice in an unbroken line of living enactment. For Mālik, "the true Sunna of the Prophet was attested by their own practice, and not by a literary narrative that had nothing to commend it except its own self-affirmation" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4 — summarizing Mālikite position).

Later Mālikite legal theory elaborated and defended this claim against attacks from jurists of other schools who argued that consensus, to be binding, must be universal (the agreement of all mujtahids of the community in a given age), not regional. The counter-argument: recognizing Medinese consensus leads to the absurd result that a mujtahid's opinion would be authoritative in Medina but not once he left the city; and no text supports the claim that geography endows opinions with validity (Hallaq, ch. 2, §6).

## Al-Shāfiʿī's Challenge

Al-Shāfiʿī was Mālik's student but became his most consequential critic. Shāfiʿī attacked sunnaic practice — including Medinese practice — as incapable of serving as authoritative Sunna, because it inevitably incorporated local custom and individual judgment under the Prophet's name without the evidentiary standards of isnad verification. For Shāfiʿī, only formally transmitted hadith with complete chains reaching to the Prophet could constitute authoritative Sunna. This attack was, in Hallaq's framing, "one of the most vocal adīth protagonists of his day" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §4), but it "did not gain immediate acceptance" — the Mālikite tradition continued to uphold a revised form of Medinese sunnaic-consensual practice for centuries.

## Authority-Construction and the School

Like the other three eponyms, Mālik's status as "absolute mujtahid" and "founder" of the Mālikite school was partly a retrospective construction. His personal school — built around the students who attended his circle and adopted his doctrine — was the nucleus from which the doctrinal Mālikite madhhab developed. His students (including al-Shāfiʿī in Egypt, Saḥnūn in North Africa, and Ibn al-Qāsim) elaborated and systematized a body of doctrine that was then attributed collectively to Mālik as its originating authority.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had the Medinese tradition prevailed in the contest with the traditionist movement, Islamic law would have retained a more community-practice-based character, less dependent on isnad verification and more amenable to local variation. Mālik's loss of the theoretical argument to al-Shāfiʿī's paradigm was not total: the Mālikite school survived and flourished precisely by finding ways to accommodate the hadith tradition while preserving a residual role for Medinese consensus and practice. Its durability in North Africa and West Africa reflects this theoretical flexibility.
