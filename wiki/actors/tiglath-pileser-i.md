# Tiglath-pileser I

Tiglath-pileser I (Akkadian *Tukultī-apil-Ešarra*, "my trust is in the heir of
Esharra"), r. **1115–1077 BCE**, was the greatest king of the
[[assyria-middle|Middle Assyrian]] period and the ruler who briefly restored
Assyrian power to the level it had reached under [[tukulti-ninurta-i|Tukulti-Ninurta
I]]. He is treated in CAH II.2 (chapter XXXI, Wiseman). He should be distinguished
from the much later **Tiglath-pileser III** (r. 745–727 BCE), founder of the
Neo-Assyrian empire.

## Campaigns

CAH II.2 reconstructs a deliberate **strategic plan** from his annals — the most
detailed Assyrian royal inscriptions yet produced — aimed at securing the trade
routes and raw-material sources around the Assyrian heartland:

- **The Mushki.** His first and most pressing campaign met the **[[phrygians|Mushki]]**
  (a people the volume tentatively links with the Phrygians, and with the Kaska who
  had helped break the [[hittites|Hittite Empire]]), who had crossed the Taurus with
  a reported 20,000 men and were pushing down the Tigris valley toward Nineveh,
  threatening the copper-mines and farmland. Tiglath-pileser routed their five chiefs
  and recovered the lost districts of Alzi and Purukuzzi.
- **The Nairi.** He drove north against the **Nairi** hill-peoples west and north of
  **Lake Van** — country he claims no predecessor had crossed — defeating a coalition
  of 23 (later 60) chiefs and setting up a victory stele at Melazgirt, imposing
  tribute in horses and cattle.
- **The march to the Mediterranean.** In a celebrated expedition he reached the
  **Lebanon and the Mediterranean coast** at Arvad, sailing along the littoral and
  hunting a *narwhal*; he received gifts from **Byblos and Sidon**, and the
  **Egyptian king sent him a crocodile** as a present — a vivid token of contact
  across the post-collapse world. He felled cedars for his temple at Ashur and laid
  tribute on **Ini-Teshub, king of [[hittites|Khatti]] (Carchemish)**.
- **The Aramaeans.** Above all he fought the **[[arameans|Aramaeans]]** (Aḫlamû),
  whom his inscriptions are the **first contemporary documents to name as a people**.
  He claims to have crossed the Euphrates after them **twenty-eight times**, twice in
  a single year, from Tadmor (Palmyra) to Anah and Rapiqu — a measure of the
  relentless, ultimately losing struggle against their migration.
- **Babylon.** Late in the reign he warred with **Marduk-nadin-ahhe** of
  [[second-isin-dynasty|Babylon]], eventually sacking the Babylonian palaces in
  reprisal for the raid on Ekallate — but, characteristically, without lasting gain.

## A King of the Age of Iron

The annals introduce several "firsts" that mark the new era: the **iron spear**
(over a hundred of his 920 claimed lions hunted on foot with bow or iron spear), the
first recorded Assyrian **botanical and zoological gardens** stocked with specimens
from his campaigns, and the most elaborate royal hunt-and-conquest ideology yet. He
restored the twin **Anu-Adad temple** and its ziggurats at Ashur and reinforced the
city walls.

## The Limits of His Success

The volume stresses the **structural weakness** beneath the glory: Tiglath-pileser
repeatedly won battles he could not consolidate. Tribute was imposed but not
regularly collected; conquered peoples were not yet systematically deported,
resettled, or organised into provinces (the machinery of the later Neo-Assyrian
empire). His successes thus "paved the way for a swift denial of Assyrian
overlordship," and after his death — under sustained **[[arameans|Aramaean]]**
pressure, famine, and lost trade — Assyria sank into one of the weakest phases of
its history for nearly two centuries.

## Causal Significance

- `produced:` a temporary Assyrian recovery and the first contemporary record of the
  [[arameans|Aramaeans]].
- `caused_by:` the migrations and power-vacuum of the
  [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] — the [[phrygians|Mushki]],
  Kaska, and Aramaean movements he spent his reign fighting.
- `enabled:` (in the long run) nothing durable; his conquests dissolved, and the
  Aramaean tide he resisted overwhelmed his successors.

## Historiography

His annals are a landmark in Assyrian royal historiography — the first to give
year-by-year campaign narratives, hunt records, and building accounts — but they are
triumphal and one-sided, and the geography of the Nairi campaigns cannot be
precisely traced. The dating follows the conventional Middle Chronology. The
**Mushki = Phrygians** identification the volume offers is debated (see
[[phrygians|Phrygians]]).
