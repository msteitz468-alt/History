# Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson

Confederate lieutenant general and one of the Confederacy's most celebrated tacticians during the American Civil War. He earned his nickname at First Bull Run (1861) for standing firm "like a stone wall," and went on to win the celebrated 1862 Shenandoah Valley Campaign against a much larger Union force through rapid marching and deception, before commanding a wing of Lee's army at Second Bull Run, Antietam, Fredericksburg, and Chancellorsville.

## Role

At Chancellorsville (May 1863), Jackson's flank march and surprise assault on the Union right was one of the most successful tactical maneuvers of the war, but he was mistakenly shot by his own pickets that night while reconnoitering; he died of complications (pneumonia following amputation) eight days later.

## Counterfactual Significance

Jackson's death is one of the most frequently invoked "Great Man" counterfactuals of the war: Lee himself lamented losing "my right arm," and historians debate whether Jackson's presence at Gettysburg two months later — notably his aggressive instincts on ground like Culp's Hill — might have altered the battle's outcome.
