# Great Moravia

## Overview

**Great Moravia** was the first major Slavic state of Central Europe, a power on the middle
Danube and Morava that flourished in the ninth century before its destruction by the Magyars.
In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Shepard, Ch. 9) it is significant above all as the
arena of the **[[cyrillo-methodian-mission|Cyrillo-Methodian mission]]** and as a Slavic power
caught between Frankish and Byzantine influence.

## Politics

Moravia lay on the eastern frontier of [[louis-the-german|Louis the German's]] East Francia, and
its princes ruled in uneasy dependence on and rebellion against Frankish overlordship. **Prince
Rastislav** — installed by Louis but increasingly independent — sought to escape the tutelage of
the Frankish (Bavarian) church by appealing to **Byzantium** for missionaries (862–863). His
nephew and supplanter **Svatopluk** expanded Moravian power to its height. The state was destroyed
in the early tenth century (c. 906) by the invading **Magyars**, whose settlement of the Hungarian
plain severed the Western and Southern Slavs.

## The Slavonic Mission

In response to Rastislav's appeal, the Byzantine emperor sent the brothers **Constantine (Cyril)**
and **Methodius**, who arrived in 863, devised the **Glagolitic** script, and translated liturgical
and scriptural texts into **Slavonic** — the origin of a written Slavic literary language. Their work
provoked the hostility of the Frankish clergy, who insisted on Latin; though the papacy briefly
sanctioned the Slavonic liturgy, after Methodius's death (885) his disciples were expelled from
Moravia and found refuge in [[first-bulgarian-empire|Bulgaria]], where Slavonic Christianity took
permanent root.

## Significance

Though politically short-lived, Great Moravia was the birthplace of Slavonic literary Christianity
and a key node in the ninth-century contest between Latin (Frankish) and Greek (Byzantine) Christendom
for the allegiance of the Slavs.

## Historiography

Moravia's history is reconstructed from Frankish annals (the *Annals of Fulda*), the Slavonic *Lives*
of Cyril and Methodius, and papal letters, supplemented by archaeology; even the location and extent of
"Great Moravia" have been debated. NCMH II treats it within the wider story of Slavic Christianisation
and Frankish–Byzantine rivalry.
