# Stuart Piggott

British archaeologist (1910–1996), Professor of Prehistoric Archaeology at Edinburgh University; Britain's leading Neolithic specialist for much of the mid-twentieth century, biographer of [[actors/stukeley-william|William Stukeley]], and the scholar most responsible for the scholarly demolition of modern Druidry's historical claims.

## Role in the Druid Controversy

Piggott was one of the new generation of professional archaeologists — sons of provincial schoolmasters who rose to prestigious chairs by their own efforts — described by Hutton as having deliberately displaced the old genteel antiquarian establishment during the 1940s–50s. He had been present at Stonehenge excavations alongside Richard Atkinson (1950–64) and encountered the [[actors/universal-bond|Universal Bond]] directly.

In earlier work (1941, 1949) Piggott had in fact allowed for the possibility that genuine religious traditions from Stonehenge's Neolithic builders might have survived into the Iron Age and been incorporated into Druidry — a position that the Universal Bond naturally welcomed. By the 1960s, however — provoked by the Ministry of Works granting the Universal Bond exclusive dawn access to Stonehenge in 1964 — he reversed field and wrote a comprehensive polemic.

His 1968 book **_The Druids_** (Thames and Hudson, commissioned by his friend Glyn Daniel) became the standard work on the subject for twenty years. It: reviewed all material remains of the Iron Age to reconstruct ancient Druidic culture; privileged hostile classical sources (Caesar, etc.) to portray ancient Druids as "bloodthirsty barbarian priests" with at best modest astronomical skill; devoted nearly half its text to tracing modern Druidry as a history of "almost unbelievably fatuous speculations and fantasies," "moonbeams from the larger lunacy," and "a sad pilgrimage through error"; and opened by asking whether the Universal Bond had any right to be at Stonehenge, and answering resoundingly in the negative. Hutton identifies Piggott's personal ideology as directly relevant: he was an atheist who believed human nature was fundamentally aggressive and adversarial, and that inequality was natural; he dismissed "eighteenth-century optimism" — the exact ideological foundation of the Universal Bond — as a dangerous myth.

Piggott had opened a correspondence with Ross Nichols (leader of the breakaway Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids) while preparing the book, asking for documentary evidence of the Universal Bond's claimed ancient history. When Nichols asserted the claims but could produce no evidence, Piggott ended the exchange and proceeded without mitigation. His book was a bestseller. He continued writing letters to national newspapers denouncing Druids far into old age whenever they claimed an ancient connection with Stonehenge.

## Significance as an Archaeological Actor

As Hutton notes, Piggott exemplifies the paradox of the new professional archaeology: trained partly through unorthodox enthusiasms (he was introduced to archaeology through Hippsley Cox's *Green Roads of England* and sponsored by the amateur Harold Peake, both associated with "wild fantasies" he later rejected), he became a fierce enforcer of narrow professional orthodoxy. His 1948 article in the first *Archaeological Newsletter* told county archaeological societies that the "days when the Bronze Age of Blankshire could be discussed chattily by the dear vicar are gone never to return" — the kind of assertion that broke the Cunnington family's grip on Wiltshire archaeology and established the professional meritocracy's monopoly.

His scholarly work on Stonehenge (with Atkinson) re-erected the fallen megaliths and established the basic construction sequence. His dating (assigning the sarsen circle to c. 1500 BCE under Bronze Age influence from the Mediterranean) was later revised, but shaped the standard account for a generation.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Piggott's *The Druids*, the Universal Bond's fabricated history would have faced no authoritative scholarly rebuttal before the 1980s. The book gave the Ministry of Works, newspapers and educated public opinion a comprehensive framework within which to dismiss the Bond's claims. It also inadvertently stimulated the next round of the debate: Glyn Daniel, partly in reaction to Piggott's rigid dismissal of any Druid–megalith link, restated a version of that connection in a 1972 lecture, and by the late 1970s Alexander Thom's archaeoastronomical work and Euan Mackie's synthesis were briefly rehabilitating a view of sophisticated prehistoric British science that fitted some ancient Druid claims.

## Historiography

Hutton's treatment in *[[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Blood and Mistletoe]]* (2009, ch. 12) is the most detailed published account of Piggott's role in the Druid controversy. Hutton is notably balanced: he acknowledges Piggott's scholarly achievement while documenting the ideological skew of his treatment of Druids both ancient and modern.
