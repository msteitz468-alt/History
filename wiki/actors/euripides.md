# Euripides

Third of the canonical Athenian tragedians; the innovator most associated with psychological realism, rhetorical debate, and the fifth-century drift toward secular, human-centred drama.

## Role

Country-born c.485; first success 441 when forty-four; died 406 aged about eighty. Seventeen complete tragedies plus one satyr play survive (*Rhesus* wrongly attributed); wrote ~ninety-two plays but won only **four** competitions in his lifetime — less often victorious than [[sophocles|Sophocles]] yet dominated later repertoires. Master of unexpected construction and set scenes; last or near-last ***Bacchae*** is bold and simple (possibly adapted from lost Aeschylus), with towering poetry — the punishment of Pentheus by Dionysus (Parker, OHCW Ch. 11, situates maenadism as temporary women’s release from domestic confinement). In **415** — year of the Syracuse expedition, high tide of Athenian power-hunger and superstitious fear — three Trojan-war tragedies culminated in ***Trojan Women***, successive “hammer blows” under fallen Troy’s walls, more like history/experience of life than formalized legendary prince story. Only complete surviving satyr play is his ***Cyclops***. [[aristophanes|Aristophanes]] stages him as a character (*Thesmophoriazusae*, *Frogs* — Aeschylus defeats him in the underworld contest of 405). Levi places him at the end of the trajectory toward more fictional and less purely mythical tragedy without ever completing that move. [[sophocles|Sophocles’]] public mourning the same year marks the close of the high classical tragic generation.

## Significance

Euripides made tragedy the theatre of contested speech, gender, and wartime morality that later antiquity and modern stages kept alive.

## Historiography

Biographical anecdotes (cave on Salamis; Macedonian exile) are often suspect; OHCW (Levi Ch. 7) is literary, not documentary biography. Griffin (Ch. 3): free retelling of myth (e.g. Helen never went to Troy — Stesichorus/Euripides *Helen*/Herodotus) shows there was no orthodox myth version.
