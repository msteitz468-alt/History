## Overview

Mullā Ṣadrā — born Muḥammad ibn Ibrāhīm al-Shīrāzī; the honorific *Ṣadr al-Dīn* means "master of religion" — is the most philosophically significant thinker produced by the Safavid period (early sixteenth to early eighteenth century) and the only Muslim philosopher from the later centuries to receive sustained attention in modern European scholarship. He was born in Shīrāz (c. 1571) after his father prayed for a pious child, moved to Qazwīn to study under the leading intellectuals of Shah ʿAbbās I's court, eventually returned to head a madrasa in Shīrāz, and died (c. 1636–40) while completing his seventh ḥajj. He is described by Peter Adamson as "the undisputed master of philosophical synthesis" in the later Islamic world, comparable to Plato, Plotinus, and Thomas Aquinas as a creative synthesizer of prior intellectual traditions.

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Chs. 54–55.

## Biography

Born in Shīrāz but finding the city's scholarly climate unfriendly after his early career, Ṣadrā traveled to Qazwīn, then the Safavid court-city, where he studied with two of the leading scholars of the age: **Shaykh Bahāʾī** (Islamic sciences — law, Koran commentary, ḥadīth) and **Mīr Dāmād** (philosophy and possibly Sufism). Mīr Dāmād was his most important philosophical teacher, and their relationship later became one of creative opposition rather than imitation: Ṣadrā eventually rejected his teacher's position on the essence/existence debate.

After a period of hostility at Shīrāz, Ṣadrā moved around considerably, undertaking the ḥajj seven times, before being appointed head of a madrasa at Shīrāz where he completed his philosophical masterwork, *The Four Journeys* (al-Asfār al-arbaʿa). He died during his final pilgrimage.

## Intellectual Background and Synthetic Project

Ṣadrā drew together four major currents of earlier Islamic thought, all of which were already in circulation in Safavid Iran but had not previously been fused into a unified system:

1. **Avicennan philosophy and kalām** — especially the essence/existence distinction and the framework of necessary vs. contingent being
2. **Neoplatonism** — including the *Theology of Aristotle* (an Arabic version of Plotinus), which was particularly fashionable in Safavid Iran; Ṣadrā cites it frequently and accepts it as Aristotle's own
3. **Illuminationism (Ishrāqism)** — the tradition inaugurated by [[suhrawardi-yahya]]; Ṣadrā adopts Suhrawardī's imagery of light and darkness and his theory of Platonic Forms (against Aristotle and Avicenna), but decisively rejects his position on existence
4. **Philosophical [[sufism]]** — especially the *waḥdat al-wujūd* ("unity of existence") tradition of [[ibn-arabi]] and al-Qūnawī

His masterwork, the *Four Journeys*, is organized around a Sufi metaphor already found in Ibn ʿArabī and the elder Dashtakī: the soul travels from creatures towards God, within God, and back. The work also functions as a sustained critical commentary on earlier Avicennan philosophy, engaging with Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, al-Ṭūsī, al-Dawānī, and others.

## Core Philosophical Doctrines

### Primacy of Existence (*aṣālat al-wujūd*)

The central move in Ṣadrā's metaphysics is his mature endorsement of the "primacy of existence" (*aṣālat al-wujūd*), a position he associated especially with Ibn ʿArabī and al-Qūnawī. Early in his career he had held the opposing view — "primacy of essence" — as had his teacher Mīr Dāmād and [[suhrawardi-yahya]]. The primacy-of-essence position holds that existence is a mere mental judgment rather than something real in the world.

Ṣadrā came to hold instead that existence — *wujūd* — is the only genuine reality. There is nothing real apart from existence. God is *pure existence* (*wujūd maḥḍ*), absolutely simple and unrestricted; all other things are existence in various degrees of limitation and admixture with non-being. The *Wisdom of the Throne* (*Ḥikmat al-ʿarsh*) states this most concisely: "God is existence itself, whereas other things have various kinds of lack or limitation mingled with their existence" (§1.1). A God who is pure existence "contains" all things within Himself before they are created — analogous to the divine names in Ibn ʿArabī's system.

Essences, for Ṣadrā, are merely mental constructs (following Suhrawardī on this point), the conceptual boundaries we impose on a continuous reality. Real things in the world do not have sharply defined natures; only our minds carve them into discrete types.

### Gradation of Being (*tashkīk al-wujūd*)

Ṣadrā's distinctive contribution to the primacy-of-existence position is the doctrine of *tashkīk* — variously translated as "gradation," "modulation," "intensification," or "systematic ambiguity." The term was already used by Avicenna and al-Ṭūsī but Ṣadrā applies it to existence itself rather than to any particular essence.

Existence comes in degrees of intensity. All of reality is a continuous, unbroken spectrum descending from God (pure, unrestricted existence) through intelligible beings, souls, and finally mere bodies. There are no firm ontological boundaries between kinds of things — only differences in the intensity of existence. This "metaphysics without any gaps" (Adamson's phrase, echoing Ṣadrā's own) allows Ṣadrā to affirm the Sufi insight that "there is nothing real apart from existence" while simultaneously avoiding a crude monism that would collapse all distinctions and identify creatures with God. The key is that variation in intensity is real, even though the variation is continuous rather than discrete.

### Substantial Motion (*al-ḥaraka al-jawhariyya*)

Ṣadrā's second major innovation is the doctrine that all things are in continuous substantial change — not just accidental change (change in qualities, location, quantity) as the Aristotelian tradition assumed, but change in substance itself. This is a deliberate revival of something like Heraclitean flux, reinterpreted through Neoplatonist procession-and-reversion.

His evidence for substantial motion: (1) the gradual transformation of water into steam admits no discrete moment of shift; (2) the development of a fetus into a baby is similarly continuous and implicates the very substance, not merely accidents, of the changing thing; (3) the continuous maturation of organisms from infancy to adulthood. He generalizes: everything is perpetually becoming something slightly different in its very substance, even when we cannot perceive the change. Mountains "fleeting just like the clouds" (Koran 27:88) is his Koranic warrant.

Substantial motion is connected to the gradation of being: since existence is continuous and intensive rather than discrete, each existent is at every moment in the process of becoming a slightly more or less intensive version of itself.

### Theory of Knowledge

Ṣadrā's epistemology flows from his metaphysics. Against Avicenna's abstractionist account of knowledge, Ṣadrā revives a Neoplatonist proposal (associated with Porphyry, known in Arabic) that genuine knowledge is *identity*: when I know a thing, my soul literally *becomes* that thing at a higher, more intensive level of existence. Knowledge is not passive reception of an image, nor abstraction of a universal, but an active unification of the knower with the known, achieved at the level of intelligible (rather than sensible) being. The "dark," material aspects of the known thing are stripped away; what remains in the soul is the pure intelligible form.

Following Suhrawardī's doctrine of "knowledge by presence" (*ʿilm ḥuḍūrī*), Ṣadrā holds that all genuine knowledge is a form of self-knowledge. There is no difference in kind between knowing an external thing and knowing oneself. This dissolves Avicenna's puzzling dualism between self-knowledge (available even to the "flying man") and ordinary propositional knowledge.

This epistemology connects back to Sufism: everything we know is in some measure a "veil" (*ḥijāb*) over God, since God *is* pure existence, and to bring anything to intelligible existence within the soul is thus to draw closer to God. The Sufi "unveiling" (*kashf*) is the limiting case, knowledge of pure existence itself.

### Koranic Exegesis and Eschatology

Ṣadrā produced extensive Koran commentaries, treating the text as a manifestation of the divine reality itself. The opening sūra (*al-Fātiḥa*) contains within it the whole Koran; the divine name *Allāh* contains all other divine names; the Koran as a whole contains all of creation. This hierarchical containment mirrors his metaphysics of intensive existence.

On the afterlife: Ṣadrā rejects the purely intellectual post-mortem existence favored by al-Kindī through Averroes, affirming bodily resurrection. But he introduces a "third realm" between sensible and intelligible — the world of imagination (*ʿālam al-mithāl*), derived from the Illuminationists and Ibn ʿArabī — in which souls project "subtle" imaginal bodies for themselves. These imaginal bodies are *more* real, not less, than gross material bodies, being one step closer to pure intelligible existence. The form of the imaginal body reflects how one lived; the morally degraded may find themselves in animal bodies.

## Legacy and Reception

During his own lifetime Ṣadrā was controversial rather than universally celebrated. But he grew in influence across subsequent generations. His works are still studied intensively in modern-day Iran, where his metaphysics of existence — the *ḥikmat al-mutaʿāliya* ("transcendent wisdom") — remains a living tradition. See [[heirs-of-mulla-sadra]] for the later Safavid and modern tradition. He is, as Adamson notes, "the most well-researched philosopher of the later eastern traditions" in European scholarship, though that tradition as a whole remains understudied.

The heirs of Ṣadrā's son-in-law produced a major commentary on the *Theology of Aristotle* in the seventeenth century (Saʿīd Qummī), and his influence extended into India through the works of Mīr Dāmād, who was widely read there.

- *Ḥikmat al-ʿarsh* (Wisdom of the Throne) — more accessible metaphysical overview
- Koranic commentaries — multiple volumes

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Ṣadrā, the traditions of Illuminationism, Avicennan philosophy, and philosophical Sufism would likely have continued as distinct streams rather than converging into a single system. The *ḥikmat al-mutaʿāliya* tradition — still alive in Iranian seminaries today — owes its coherence and its claim to synthesize all prior Islamic philosophy to his work. Whether later Iranian philosophy would have developed a similarly productive metaphysical tradition in his absence is unclear; Mīr Dāmād, his teacher, was important but did not attempt the same degree of synthesis.

## Historiography

Ṣadrā has received more scholarly attention in European languages than any other later Islamic philosopher, though this remains a thin tradition compared to coverage of Avicenna or Averroes. Key modern scholars include Henry Corbin (who championed the "School of Isfahan" framing, though Adamson notes the "school" label is contested) and Seyyed Hossein Nasr. Adamson's treatment in [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]] (Chs. 53–55) is notable for situating Ṣadrā within the broader context of Safavid intellectual history rather than treating him in isolation.

The "school of Isfahan" label: specialists disagree. The Safavid thinkers were not united by shared doctrine — Ṣadrā decisively broke with his teacher Mīr Dāmād on the central question of essence vs. existence. What they shared was a common set of sources and influences rather than a common position. See [[school-of-isfahan]].

**Collection coverage**: The Bibliotheca Alexandrina's coverage of later Islamic philosophy is thin. Adamson's survey is the primary entry point; specialist monographs on Ṣadrā by Corbin and Nasr are not confirmed present in the collection.
