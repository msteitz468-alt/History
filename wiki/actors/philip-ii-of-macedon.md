# Philip II of Macedon

## Overview

**Philip II** (382–336 BCE), king of **Macedon** from **359**, transformed a weak,
fractious frontier kingdom into the **master of Greece** and the architect of the empire
his son **[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]]** would carry to India.
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Chs. 14–15, J. R. Ellis) traces how, in a single reign,
Philip overturned the world of the autonomous **[[city-state|polis]]** — the political and
military endpoint toward which the whole exhausted fourth century had been tending (see
[[greek-federalism]], [[greek-warfare]]).

## Saving and rebuilding Macedon

Philip came to power in **359** amid catastrophe: his brother Perdiccas and 4,000
Macedonians had just been killed by the **Illyrians**, and the throne was contested by
pretenders backed by Athens, Thrace and the Paeonians. Drawing perhaps on lessons from his
years as a **hostage at Thebes** (under [[pelopidas]] and in the milieu of
[[epaminondas]]), he met the crisis with "morale-raising and diplomacy" — buying off the
Paeonians and Illyrians, neutralizing Athens, defeating the pretender Argaeus — and then
**rebuilt the army**.

## The Macedonian army

Ellis stresses the military reorganization as the foundation of everything. Philip forged a
**professional standing army** combining:
- the **phalanx** of infantry wielding the long pike, the **sarissa** (far outreaching the
  hoplite spear), drilled to manoeuvre as a unit;
- the heavy **Companion cavalry (*hetairoi*)** as the decisive shock arm;
- light troops, archers, and a **siege train** with artillery (the **catapults** pioneered
  in Sicily; see [[greek-warfare]]) able to **take walled cities** as Greek armies rarely
  could.
This combined-arms force, bound to the king by personal loyalty and enriched by the
**Pangaeum gold and silver** mines (the new mint-city of Philippi), became "the most vital
element not only of the kingdom's security but also of its social cohesion."
[[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC* (2000)]]
gives the scale of the transformation in figures: Philip **tripled Macedonian infantry**
(from roughly 10,000 to 30,000) and **increased cavalry more than sixfold** (from about
600 to 3,500), introducing differential pay, professional advancement for salaried
officers, and land grants in conquered territory as incentives. It was under Philip that
the **sarissa** pike, deployed by very deep formations, first became a genuinely
effective weapon, and that cavalry — driven in wedge formation — became for the first
time a **battle-winning force** rather than a purely reconnaissance/screening arm.

## Master of Thessaly: the Crocus Field (352)

Osborne highlights **the Battle of the Crocus Field** (352 BCE), in southern Thessaly,
during the **Third Sacred War**, as the decisive turn in Philip's rise to regional power:
a massive victory over the combined Thessalian and Phocian forces after which Philip was,
"if not entirely undisputed," master of Thessaly — the base from which his later
intervention in central Greek affairs, and his eventual clash with Athens and Thebes at
[[battle-of-chaeronea-338bce|Chaeronea]], became possible.

## The conquest of Greece

Philip expanded relentlessly: seizing **Amphipolis** and the Thracian coast, subjecting the
**[[odrysian-kingdom|Odrysian]]** Thracians and the Illyrians, and entering Greek affairs
through the **Third Sacred War** (356–346) — championing Apollo against the temple-robbing
Phocians, which made him master of **Thessaly** (elected *archon*) and a member of the
**Amphictyonic League**. He destroyed **Olynthus** and the Chalcidic League (348), imposed
the **Peace of Philocrates** on Athens (346), and — despite the furious opposition of
**[[demosthenes-orator|Demosthenes]]** — moved to dominate central Greece. The reckoning
came at **[[battle-of-chaeronea-338bce|Chaeronea]] (338)**, where Philip and the
eighteen-year-old Alexander crushed the combined armies of **Athens and Thebes**.

## The view from Athens: Demosthenes, Aeschines, Isocrates

No independent ancient history of Philip's reign survives, so Osborne stresses that the
primary contemporary window onto the conflict is the surviving corpus of
**[[demosthenes-orator|Demosthenes]]**'s speeches — which present Philip as a scheming
"barbarian" who fought by trickery and bribery rather than open Greek warfare, with
Demosthenes casting himself as champion of Greek freedom. The speeches of Demosthenes'
own opponents, chiefly **Aeschines**, present a very different Demosthenes: inconsistent,
opportunistic, and driven by personal ambition rather than principle — a reminder that
Philip is seen almost entirely through the distorting lens of Athenian domestic faction.
Not all Athenians agreed Philip should be resisted: **Isocrates**, in his pamphlet
*Philippus* (346, published in the year of the temporary Peace of Philocrates), called on
Philip to be the **champion of Greek concord** and to lead a panhellenic expedition
against Persia — the pro-Macedonian Panhellenist position that Philip's League of Corinth
would later, in its own cynical way, fulfill.

## Historiographical reassessment (Freeman)

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]]) traces Philip's shifting historical
reputation: nineteenth-century British liberal historians, following **Demosthenes**
uncritically, cast him as a tyrannical destroyer of Greek liberty, while German historians
of the same era were more sympathetic, reading him through the analogy of **Bismarck**
unifying a fragmented nation by force. Modern scholarship (Ian **Worthington**) restores
Philip as the indispensable architect of Macedonian power without whom Alexander's
conquests are unthinkable — a corrective consonant with Green's and Hornblower's
readings above. Freeman also credits Philip's marriage-alliance diplomacy (Illyrian,
Molossian [Olympias], Thracian, two Thessalian wives) as a deliberate, frontier-by-
frontier securing strategy, and treats **Chaeronea (338)** as "the decisive moment"
ending the era of the independent Greek city-state.

## The League of Corinth and the Persian plan

In **337** Philip organized the defeated Greeks into the **[[league-of-corinth|League of
Corinth]]** — a Common Peace on the [[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]] model but guaranteed
by Philip, not the Persian King, with a federal synod and himself as **hegemon**. Its first
act was to declare a **panhellenic war on Persia** to avenge Xerxes' sacrilege — the
[[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|panhellenist dream]] of Isocrates made policy. As the
advance force crossed into Asia (under the general Parmenio, spring 336), Philip was
**assassinated in autumn 336** at the Macedonian festival of the Olympia at Aegae — Osborne
notes the assassin was a young man from Philip's own royal bodyguard whose motive,
personal or political, "must remain unknown"; he was killed on the spot. Alexander, then
twenty, was "promptly presented to, and recognized by, the army as king," leaving the
throne — and the Persian invasion already under way — to him.

## The structural inheritance (Green)

[[green-alexander-of-macedon-2013|Peter Green]]'s opening chapter ("The Gardens of
Midas") supplies the fullest account in the collection of *what Philip had to overcome*
and *what, therefore, Alexander inherited*. Green stresses that Macedon was natively a
**divided realm** — a fertile lowland plain around the Thermaic Gulf (the old Argead
kingdom, seat at Aegae then Pella) ringed by semi-autonomous highland cantons (Orestis,
Lyncestis, Elimea) whose princely houses obeyed the king only when he was strong. Green's
recurring analogy is **Scotland's Lowland–Highland split**: a structurally fissile
kingdom perpetually tempted into fragmentation and dynastic murder. Philip's achievement
was to **weld these halves into one instrument** — through the new army (which doubled as
the engine of *social cohesion*), through systematic **dynastic marriage** (seven wives,
each a frontier alliance), and through a court statecraft Green presents as cynical
*realpolitik*: bribery raised to a system ("Philip's gold," the suborning of the Athenian
embassies), hostages, and calculated terror (Olynthus). On this reading the **Aristotle /
Mieza** education and the careful grooming of Alexander as heir were of a piece with the
same dynastic project — until the late marriage to Cleopatra and the repudiation of
Olympias (autumn 338) reopened the succession and supplied, in Green's analysis, the
political matrix for Philip's murder. The thesis frames Green's whole biography:
**Alexander received a finished military-fiscal machine and a panhellenic war already in
motion**, and the gulf that later opened between king and Macedonian rank-and-file is
rooted in tensions Philip had only papered over.

## Counterfactual significance

Philip is the **hinge between the Classical and Hellenistic worlds**. He solved the problem
the fourth-century polis could not — the durable combination of Greek states into a single
power — by force and federation under a Macedonian monarchy, and forged the army and the
plan that Alexander merely (if brilliantly) executed. Without Philip there is no Alexander;
"the events of the reign" that follow are the working-out of his design.

## CHGRW synthesis (Hunt, Wheeler, Billows, Sekunda, Roth, Serrati)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] treats Philip as
the synthesizer of fourth-century innovations rather than pure novelty. Hunt (I.5): although
Demosthenes claimed Philip fought in an altogether new way (Dem. 9.47–52), many features of
his army were symptomatic of growing specialization and professionalization — phalangite
replacing hoplite; coordinated multi-arm force; cavalry usually deciding; monarchical
discipline; full-time professionals funded by mines/conquest; mercenaries for garrisons and
distant ops; elite companions. Wheeler (I.6): Mantinea 362 as classical tactical ceiling
before Philip “re-thinks” the phalanx and harvests the strategic/tactical revolution as model
for subsequent Hellenistic armies. Roth (I.12): true combined-arms force, streamlined
logistics (portable mills on pack animals), up-to-date siege train, court engineers developing
torsion artillery, monarchical command able to direct the machine with a single will.

Sekunda (I.11A): Macedonian imperialism as a **dynamic feedback loop** — territorial growth
expanded demographic/financial base → larger, more efficient forces → further expansion,
ultimately necessitating the invasion of Asia. Serrati (I.14): financial base from timber
monopoly, land taxes, and minerals, dramatically expanded by Edonis (356) and Chalcidice
(348) gold/silver and Thessalian wealth; diplomatic imperialism (bribes, fifth columns,
funding coups) was scale-up of normal fourth-century Greek practice, not seditious novelty.
Billows (I.10): Macedonians brought no distinctive IR doctrines; Philip adapted Macedonian
diplomacy to advanced city-state Greek practices, with his near-absolute royal role paralleled
by Greek tyrants such as Dionysius I. League of Corinth (Hunt/Hall): “Philip and the Hellenes,”
bicameral with Philip/Alexander as *hegemon*; proportional representation in the *synedrion*
(unlike equal votes of the Delian model); 335 *synedrion* voted to raze Thebes. Despite
excellent Macedonian timber, neither Philip nor Alexander ran major early warship programs —
Philip used coastal cities’ modest forces; Demosthenes thought ten ships enough vs Philip
(Dem. 4.34). See [[hellenistic-warfare]], [[battle-of-chaeronea-338bce]].

## OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Hornblower (Ch.6), Boardman (Ch.12), Price (Ch.13)

[[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]]:

**Rise amid Greek weakness (Hornblower).** A contemporary historian called Philip “the greatest man Europe had ever produced.” He succeeded in 359 amid Social War (357–355), futile Spartan Messenia recovery, and Sacred War vs Phocis after Theban amphictyonic manoeuvre—the Sacred War’s importance “in thus bringing Philip into the heart of Greece can hardly be exaggerated.” He seized Amphipolis and Thessaly; Olynthus fell 348; Peace of Philocrates 346; Chaeronea 338 nearly lost despite [[demosthenes-orator|Demosthenes]]’s efforts — then League of Corinth entrenched possessing classes. Closing verdict: “Imperialism had after all proved incompatible with democracy.” Classical poleis failed to convert *to hellenikon* into political unity; Macedon and Rome later succeeded by force.

**Art patronage geography (Boardman).** Vergina (Philip II’s tomb) and the Mausoleum mark fourth-century shift of patronage away from classical Athenian leadership toward royal/Anatolian courts; true contemporary portraiture emerges.

**Warfare tech inheritance (Price Ch.13).** Torsion catapult development, probably by Philip, tilted siege balance decisively away from the besieged—no individual city could feel secure when faced with a king’s assault; quinquiremes and elephant diplomacy of the Diadochi build on this monarchical military package that Alexander inherited.
