# Vladimir Lenin

**Vladimir Ilyich Lenin** (1870–1924; real name Vladimir Ulyanov) was the leader of the Bolshevik faction of the Russian Social-Democratic Labour Party, principal organizer of the October Revolution, and first head of the Soviet state as Chairman of the Council of People’s Commissars (Sovnarkom). He created the basic "Soviet compound" of one-party dictatorship, ideological monopoly, state economic dominance, legal nihilism and administrative hyper-centralism that endured for seven decades. Lenin’s personal confidence, intolerance and strategic flexibility were decisive in the Bolshevik seizure and consolidation of power.

## Biography and Formative Influences

Born in Simbirsk to a family of mixed Jewish, German, Kalmyk and Russian elements, Lenin was radicalized by the 1887 execution of his elder brother Alexander for an attempt on Alexander III. Expelled from Kazan University as a troublemaker, he completed a first-class external degree at St Petersburg University (1891) and joined Marxist circles. Arrested and exiled to Siberia, he wrote *The Development of Capitalism in Russia* (1899, legal). In emigration from 1900 he founded *Iskra* and published *What Is To Be Done?* (1902), arguing for a disciplined, centralized vanguard party of professional revolutionaries loyal in doctrine. At the 1903 Second Party Congress he split the RSDLP into Bolsheviks ("majoritarians") and Mensheviks ("minoritarians").

Lenin returned to Russia in 1905, showed tactical flexibility, and proposed a "provisional revolutionary democratic dictatorship of the proletariat and the peasantry" after tsarism’s overthrow, anticipating terror. After 1907 he resumed émigré polemics in Switzerland, losing support over his 1914 call for Russia’s military defeat. By 1917 he was 47, older and more experienced than most Central Committee colleagues.

From Service (2009, lines 820–834): "Lenin was the fastest at adjusting to the change [of open politics after February]. ... His ability to rouse a crowd was such that adversaries recorded that he could make the hairs stand on the back of their necks with excitement." "The USSR would not have come into being without Lenin’s intolerant confidence."

From [[cambridge-history-of-russia-v3-2006|Cambridge History of Russia Vol. III]] (Suny lines 3011–3023): Intellectual differences between Lenin and Martov: 'Lenin also approached politics with passion, but his was a passion more of reason than of moral sensibility... Lenin repeatedly made it clear that he despised the political moralising so common to Russian socialism. For Lenin, the revolution was a matter of rationality and discipline not the romantic heroism of the struggle for justice.' In What Is To Be Done? 'left to themselves workers were unable to see beyond the economic struggle... it was necessary to create a party... of full-time revolutionaries to direct the mass movement, who embodied the full consciousness that the masses lacked and were obedient to party discipline.' (Steinberg lines 3027–3033). Smith (lines 4165–4167): Lenin 'blitzed the Central Committee with demands... “History will not forgive us if we do not assume power now.”' On peace: fought hard for Brest-Litovsk despite schism; 'bought peace with the surrender of the western borderlands to the enemy and was not forgiven for many years by patriotic Bolsheviks' (von Hagen lines 3635–3636).

## Role in 1917 and October Revolution

Lenin arrived in Petrograd 3 April 1917 and presented the April Theses: reconstruction of Bolshevism around building soviet majorities and immediate transfer of power, breaking with the "bourgeois stage" consensus. He drove the party leftward. After July Days (demonstration suppressed; Lenin and Zinoviev fled to Finland; Trotsky imprisoned), Lenin urged immediate seizure. The Central Committee initially resisted; Lenin returned disguised in October and secured approval on 10 October. The uprising was timed (via Trotsky) to coincide with the Second All-Russia Congress of Soviets (25 Oct 1917). Bolsheviks seized post/telegraph stations, railway termini and the Winter Palace. Lenin’s decrees on Peace and Land (26 Oct) won peasant and soldier support. He insisted on an all-Bolshevik Sovnarkom despite pressure for coalition; three commissars resigned. The Constituent Assembly was dispersed in January 1918 after SR plurality.

## Civil War, Brest-Litovsk and NEP

Lenin forced acceptance of the "obscene peace" at Brest-Litovsk (3 Mar 1918) despite Central Committee opposition and Left Communist revolt; the treaty cost Ukraine and western borderlands but bought time. He created the Cheka (7 Dec 1917) for sabotage and counter-revolution. During Civil War he backed Red Terror, Food-Supplies Dictatorship and grain quotas. After Kronstadt mutiny and Tambov revolt (1921) he introduced the New Economic Policy (tax-in-kind replacing requisitioning; legalization of small private trade and manufacturing). He argued NEP was a necessary retreat to preserve the political dictatorship.

From Service (lines 1140–1152, 333): "the retreat in economics was to be accompanied by an offensive in politics." The compound’s ingredients (dictatorship, centralism, etc.) "were put into the crucible by Lenin and his party within a couple of years of the October Revolution."

## Last Years, Testament and Death

Suffering severe headaches, Lenin suffered a major stroke in May 1922 and was sidelined. In late 1922–early 1923 he dictated his political testament, criticizing Stalin ("too crude"), warning of a split between Stalin and Trotsky, and advocating worker influx into central bodies to combat bureaucracy. He broke personally with Stalin over abuse of Krupskaya and the Georgian affair. A further stroke (6 Mar 1923) left him speechless and paralysed; he died 21 Jan 1924.

Lenin’s idiosyncratic ideas (dictatorship, industrialization, nationality policy) and actions shaped the Soviet state more than any other single figure. Contingency mattered: "it was not a foregone conclusion that Stalin would defeat Trotski."

## Biography / Description
Vladimir Ulianov, better known by his pseudonym N. Lenin, was born in 1870 in Simbirsk on the Volga to an intellectual family (his father was a school inspector). He became a radical early in life, influenced by the execution of his eldest brother Alexander in 1887 for participating in a plot to assassinate Alexander III. He studied law at the University of Kazan, became a Marxist, and suffered imprisonment in 1896 followed by three years of Siberian exile. In 1900, he participated in publishing the Social Democratic newspaper *The Spark* (*Iskra*) abroad, and in 1903 he led the Bolshevik faction during the split of the Russian Social Democratic Labor Party. He masterminded the Bolshevik seizure of power in the October Revolution of 1917 and led the Soviet state as Chairman of the Council of People's Commissars until his death on January 21, 1924, following a series of debilitating strokes starting in May 1922.

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Bolshevik Party Structure (1903):** Lenin insisted on a tightly knit body of dedicated professional revolutionaries with clear lines of command and military discipline, which split the Social Democrats into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks.
- **October Revolution (1917):** Lenin decided that his party could stage a successful revolution in Russia in 1917, drafting the *April Theses* to advocate transforming the bourgeois revolution into a socialist one, supported by poor peasants.
- **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (1918):** Against strong opposition within his own party (including Bukharin and Trotsky), Lenin insisted on accepting Germany's harsh peace terms to save the fledgling Soviet state, stating it was necessary to sacrifice territory to consolidate power.
- **War Communism (1918–1921):** Lenin oversaw the radical socialization of the economy, introducing grain requisitions (prodrazvyorstka), nationalization of industry, and the suppression of political opponents via the Cheka.
- **New Economic Policy (1921):** Following the Kronstadt rebellion and widespread peasant unrest, Lenin made the realistic compromise to retreat to a mixed economy, replacing food levies with a tax in kind and permitting small-scale private trade.

## Historical Significance
Lenin was the principal founder of the Soviet state and the primary theoretical architect of Marxism-Leninism, adapting classical Marxist theory to Russian conditions. He recognized the revolutionary potential of the peasantry in an underdeveloped capitalist state (the theory of the "weakest link" in the imperialist chain) and championed the role of a vanguard party. Riasanovsky notes that Lenin "combined high intelligence, an ability for acute theoretical thinking, and practical sense to become a great Marxist 'realist'" (Line 5715). Unlike Stalin, his authority within the party rested on his personality, intellect, and achievements rather than terror. In his final testament, Lenin warned the party against Stalin's rising power and rude character, though his warning went unheeded.
