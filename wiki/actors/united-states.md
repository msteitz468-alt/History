# The United States

## Overview

Ian Tyrrell's chapter in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] argues that "connections to
global history were deeply embedded from early times in American experience." The
United States emerged as "the chief beneficiary" of the imperial contest for North
America and "itself became a variety of empire"; by the late nineteenth century its
size, population, and resources made it a major force in world trade, migration, and
investment, and by the turn of the twenty-first century the world's pre-eminent
power. Tyrrell reads US history through **globalization** understood as "uneven,
contingent, reversible and not purely economic" — a "complex social formation of
economic, political and cultural relations."

## Middle Cold War Grand Strategy and Identity (from [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v2|CHCW Vol. II Range 01]])

US grand strategy shifted after early containment successes and NSC-68/Korea: from "strategy meant to retain the initiative by distinguishing vital from peripheral interests" to one that "yielded the initiative to its enemies. Wherever they chose to challenge, it would have to respond." Roosevelt had "allowed for the possibility that a 'cold war' might not happen"; post-1945 the US committed to "reviving capitalism and democracy" among Europeans to prevent despair and Communist electoral gains. Kennedy–Johnson era: robust economy base (tax cuts, investment credit); expanded defense/international affairs spending and NASA "to reestablish the United States as being on the frontiers of technology... not least in the Soviet Union." Nixon–Kissinger: "most radical shift... since NSC-68" via linkage, China opening, SALT for Vietnam exit without loss of face and to "restore presidential authority at home." Domestic identity (per Jervis): "democracy; individualism and voluntarism... opposition to concentrated power... faith that this model or 'way of life' can, should, and eventually will be adopted by others." "American identity was much less self-conscious" than Soviet, giving "flexibility... and a resilience." US saw détente as maintaining status quo amid weakness; economic tools (GATT Kennedy Round explicitly to prove "superiority of free choice" vs Sino-Soviet world; sanctions architecture) complemented military deterrence. "The Cold War was not synonymous with American culture." (Gaddis Ch. 1; Jervis Ch. 2; Cooper Ch. 3; verbatim locators from Range 01 slice)

## Founding and the Atlantic revolutions

The US was "born in an anti-colonial revolt that helped to spark a series of
revolutions in the Atlantic world of 1776–1825" (see [[atlantic-revolutions]]). The
[[long-19th-century|Seven Years' War]] removed the French–Indian check on Anglo
settler expansion and produced the taxation conflict behind the **American Revolution
(1775–1783)**. The Revolution's ideas of natural rights and equality rippled into the
French Revolution and — to the alarm of US slaveholders — the **Haitian Revolution**,
even as the 1787 Constitution entrenched slavery. From 1790 to 1815 the young
republic's fortunes hinged on the Anglo-French struggle (the War of 1812); thereafter
no European power could check US **westward expansion** at the expense of Native
peoples, backed by the **Monroe Doctrine** (itself dependent on British naval power).

## Early Cold War domestic mobilization and politics (Leffler & Westad Vol. I, Range 05, McEnaney)

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1]] (Range 05, McEnaney ch. "Cold War mobilization and domestic politics: the United States"):
- NSC 68 warned the Cold War "was not merely a clash of ideologies but rather 'a real war in which the survival of the free world [was] at stake'" requiring Americans to demonstrate "'ingenuity, sacrifice, and unity'" via "traditional democratic process" that respected values while mobilizing "home-front institutions – state and local governments, schools, businesses, labor unions, civic organizations, and media" (pp. 420–421).
- "McCarthyism" "was primarily a top-down phenomenon... there was not one, but many McCarthyisms, each with its own agenda and modus operandi... 'was, above all, a collaborative project.'" Taft-Hartley Act 1947 loyalty affidavits "wiped out an entire generation of activists"; "the labor movement turned inward and raided its own left wing." FBI "designed and ran much of the machinery" ("Hooverism"); Truman loyalty program and McCarran Act 1950; presidents Truman/Eisenhower made concessions while uneasy about excesses (pp. 424–427).
- "national security state" "a state within a state" and "disciplinary state" with powers to "spy, prosecute, and purge"; "national security" "used widely, many would say recklessly"; created "ambient militarism" (pp. 426–428).
- Contradictions: attack on government while expanding it for security; "Liberal intellectuals were willing to put the containment of communism ahead of the welfare state while conservative intellectuals were prepared to give antistatism a back seat to anticommunism" (p. 432).
- Civil rights: "segregation harmed US foreign relations"; State Dept saw it as "source of constant embarrassment"; activists advanced "counternarrative of racial oppression"; NAACP purged Communists to display credentials yet pressured Truman (1948 military desegregation) and used Justice Dept in Brown; "national security was the dominant rationale" but "made the reform impulse both urgent and limited" (pp. 433–434).
- Gender: containment fostered domestic ideal (May); but "the Cold War had no fixed association with the domestic ideal" and "offered a new vocabulary for extending democratic ideals and demanding individual freedom" (Meyerowitz); women activists in civil defense "ardent Cold Warriors" yet "maternalists" pushing representation; "Cold War national security discourse ultimately valorized masculine toughness" (pp. 435–436).
- Nuclear: "private pacifists but public militarists"; most "rejected this kind of self-help defense" (tiny minority built shelters); fears expressed in pop culture more than actual preparedness programs. "the Cold War [was] not synonymous with American culture, even at the height of its impact" (Kuznick/Gilbert); "Cold War culture... inherently unstable" (Pease) (pp. 439–441). ## State, empire, and power

- **A high-wage economy.** Like Britain and even more so, the US had high-wage labor,
  favoring the **capital-intensive [[industrialization]]** that economized on labor
  (Wong); by the turn of the twentieth century the US economy was the world's largest
  (Smil) — central to the [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]].
- **The imperial turn.** The late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw the US,
  like other powers, strengthen national borders and project power abroad — the
  **Spanish–American War (1898)**, the Platt Amendment over Cuba, and the engineered
  secession of Panama (1903) — establishing dominance over Central America and the
  Caribbean (see [[imperialism]], [[latin-america-modern]]).
- **The world wars and the post-war order.** The "finely balanced struggles between
  integration and decoupling" of the world-wars era gave way after 1945 to a tighter
  alignment of economic and political relations and an **internationalist** turn in US
  diplomacy and military affairs (see [[world-wars-era]], [[1945-postwar-order]]).
- **Superpower and "new globalization."** As one of the two [[cold-war|Cold War]]
  superpowers and then the post-1991 hegemon, the US drove (and was reshaped by) the
  1970s "new globalization" (see [[contemporary]]).

## Counterfactual Significance

Tyrrell's framing makes US development inseparable from global forces — its security,
prosperity, and expansion repeatedly turned on European wars, British power, and
global markets, undercutting any purely exceptionalist national narrative. The US is
both an outcome of the imperial and revolutionary Atlantic world and, by the
twentieth century, the chief shaper of the global order.

## Historiography

Tyrrell writes against US exceptionalism, situating American history within world
history and a critically defined "globalization" (drawing on Rosenberg, Hopkins,
O'Rourke & Williamson, and Cooper's skepticism). This complements the volume's
anti-[[eurocentrism|Eurocentric]], connective method.

## From Range 03 (Niu Jun, Guthrie-Shimizu, Stueck in Leffler & Westad Vol. I) — China, Japan, Korea

- China policy (Niu Jun): US did not want large-scale Chinese civil war; sought to maintain Jiang Jieshi’s position to concentrate Chinese forces against Japan and avoid Soviet entanglement. Sent Generals Patrick Hurley and George C. Marshall "to broker peace in China." After war, "the United States began sending troops to China" (post-Aug 1945). "The press in the Allied countries also frequently criticized the Nationalist government’s financial corruption and military shortcomings." Interactions late 1948–49 (Shenyang consulate arrest, Nanjing Stuart talks) "had no positive results" and "disabused the top CCP leaders of any notion of developing normal relations with the United States." US policy viewed by Mao as turning China into "a US colony".
- Japan occupation and alliance (Guthrie-Shimizu): Tokyo Trials 1946–48 (28 leaders including Tojo hanged; emperor spared). Reforms via existing institutions: female suffrage, Civil Code revision, Article IX pacifist constitution, peerage abolition, labor unions, education liberalization, deconcentration. "defeat acted as a catalyst for radical changes in Japan’s social and political institutions" that "perhaps would have been impossible with indigenous forces alone." Reverse course from 1947: emphasis on "political stability via economic reconstruction"; Strike Commission Report replaced punitive reparations with rehabilitation focus. MacArthur March 1947: occupation objectives "largely achieved." San Francisco Peace Treaty (midst of Korean War) + US-Japan Security Treaty gave "truncated peace". Korean War "ignited" Japanese economy via "massive US military procurements"; by April 1952 end of occupation "Japan was well on its way to economic recovery". US forced Japan into harsh PRC embargo (harsher than USSR); Japanese pursued private trade deals and "Marshall Plan for Asia" requests (rebuffed). 1960 revised treaty under Kishi provided "more equal partnership", US obligation to defend Japan, prior consultation on deployments/nuclear. "Tokyo elected to stay in its alliance of convenience with a powerful United States and chose the path to economic prosperity."
- Korea division (Stueck): US proposed 38th parallel Aug 1945 as demarcation; Stalin agreed. US zone: refused to recognize Korean People’s Republic (declared ~Sept 6 1945); discouraged people’s committees; favored colonial-era elites/conservatives (Rhee). South economic mishandling (rice distribution, delayed land reform), refugee influx, police brutality fueled Autumn Harvest Uprising 1946 (strikes Taegu etc.; mobs suppressed with US troops + new constabulary; hundreds dead; "suggest coordination rather than spontaneity").

## Cold War Resources Dimension (Leffler & Westad CHCW Vol. I Range 06)

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1]] (Painter): US "possessed the military and economic capability to look all over the world, especially to the Third World, for resources for itself and its allies." "Seven Sisters" (incl. 5 US firms) controlled >90% non-N.A./non-Communist reserves. Postwar focus on Middle East ("center of gravity ... shifting" 1943); Marshall Plan financed ~56% of US company oil to Europe 1948–51, shifting European imports to 85% Middle East by 1950. 1953 Iran coup restored Western consortium after nationalization of Anglo-Iranian. Eisenhower Doctrine (1957) and support for conservative regimes followed Suez to secure access. MOIP quotas 1959 protected domestic spare capacity. "Maintaining access to foreign oil, especially the vast reserves of the Persian Gulf, remained a key priority of US foreign policy throughout the Cold War." "US eﬀorts to maintain access to foreign oil led the United States not only to try to contain Soviet inﬂuence but also to oppose revolutionary nationalism in the Third World." (pp. 486, 491, 493, 497, 501, 507)
