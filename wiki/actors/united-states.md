# The United States

## Overview

Ian Tyrrell's chapter in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] argues that "connections to
global history were deeply embedded from early times in American experience." The
United States emerged as "the chief beneficiary" of the imperial contest for North
America and "itself became a variety of empire"; by the late nineteenth century its
size, population, and resources made it a major force in world trade, migration, and
investment, and by the turn of the twenty-first century the world's pre-eminent
power. Tyrrell reads US history through **globalization** understood as "uneven,
contingent, reversible and not purely economic" — a "complex social formation of
economic, political and cultural relations."

## Founding and the Atlantic revolutions

The US was "born in an anti-colonial revolt that helped to spark a series of
revolutions in the Atlantic world of 1776–1825" (see [[atlantic-revolutions]]). The
[[long-19th-century|Seven Years' War]] removed the French–Indian check on Anglo
settler expansion and produced the taxation conflict behind the **American Revolution
(1775–1783)**. The Revolution's ideas of natural rights and equality rippled into the
French Revolution and — to the alarm of US slaveholders — the **Haitian Revolution**,
even as the 1787 Constitution entrenched slavery. From 1790 to 1815 the young
republic's fortunes hinged on the Anglo-French struggle (the War of 1812); thereafter
no European power could check US **westward expansion** at the expense of Native
peoples, backed by the **Monroe Doctrine** (itself dependent on British naval power).

## A paradox of openness and isolation

Tyrrell's central characterization of the nineteenth-century US: an **open society in
trade, migration, and capital** — deeply integrated into the emerging global economy
and into cross-national social and cultural currents (a "Victorian/imperial phase of
globalization" tied to British economic power) — yet pursuing **political isolationism
toward Europe**. From the 1790s American merchants traded "in all oceans," tapping
British imperial routes from Massachusetts whaling ports to the China and Pacific
trade (see [[transpacific-economy]]).

## State, empire, and power

- **A high-wage economy.** Like Britain and even more so, the US had high-wage labor,
  favoring the **capital-intensive [[industrialization]]** that economized on labor
  (Wong); by the turn of the twentieth century the US economy was the world's largest
  (Smil) — central to the [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]].
- **The imperial turn.** The late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw the US,
  like other powers, strengthen national borders and project power abroad — the
  **Spanish–American War (1898)**, the Platt Amendment over Cuba, and the engineered
  secession of Panama (1903) — establishing dominance over Central America and the
  Caribbean (see [[imperialism]], [[latin-america-modern]]).
- **The world wars and the post-war order.** The "finely balanced struggles between
  integration and decoupling" of the world-wars era gave way after 1945 to a tighter
  alignment of economic and political relations and an **internationalist** turn in US
  diplomacy and military affairs (see [[world-wars-era]], [[1945-postwar-order]]).
- **Superpower and "new globalization."** As one of the two [[cold-war|Cold War]]
  superpowers and then the post-1991 hegemon, the US drove (and was reshaped by) the
  1970s "new globalization" (see [[contemporary]]).

## Counterfactual Significance

Tyrrell's framing makes US development inseparable from global forces — its security,
prosperity, and expansion repeatedly turned on European wars, British power, and
global markets, undercutting any purely exceptionalist national narrative. The US is
both an outcome of the imperial and revolutionary Atlantic world and, by the
twentieth century, the chief shaper of the global order.

## Historiography

Tyrrell writes against US exceptionalism, situating American history within world
history and a critically defined "globalization" (drawing on Rosenberg, Hopkins,
O'Rourke & Williamson, and Cooper's skepticism). This complements the volume's
anti-[[eurocentrism|Eurocentric]], connective method.
