# The Oyo Empire

The **Oyo Empire** was the most powerful **Yoruba** state — a savanna empire of northern Yorubaland that
dominated the region between the forest and the Niger from the seventeenth to the early nineteenth century.
Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO [[gha-vol5-16th-18th-century|General History of Africa, Vol.
V]]** (ch. 15, Alagoa).

## Cavalry power and constitutional balance

Unlike the forest kingdoms, Oyo sat in open savanna and built its power on **cavalry** — horses imported
from the north across the [[hausa-city-states|Hausa]]/Sudanic trade — which let it project force across a
wide area and reduce neighbours to tribute. Its government was notable for a **balance of powers**: the
divine king, the **Alafin**, was checked by the **Oyo Mesi**, a council of seven hereditary chiefs led by
the **Basorun**, who could compel a failed Alafin to commit suicide, and by the **Ogboni** religious
society — a sophisticated set of constitutional restraints on absolutism. The Yoruba revered **Ife** as
their spiritual and dynastic source.

## Apogee

At its height in the eighteenth century Oyo controlled much of Yorubaland and made the kingdom of
**[[dahomey|Dahomey]]** a **tributary**, taxing the Atlantic slave trade of the "Slave Coast" through which
it exported war captives for horses and goods. It was among the largest and best-organized West African
states of the era.

## Collapse

In the early nineteenth century Oyo disintegrated amid **constitutional crises** (struggles between Alafin
and Oyo Mesi), provincial revolts, the loss of Dahomey, and the southward pressure of the **Sokoto
(Fulani) jihad**, which sacked the capital around **1836**. Its fall plunged Yorubaland into the long
nineteenth-century wars from which new states (Ibadan) emerged.

## Significance

Oyo is the classic **savanna-cavalry empire** of the Guinea region and a notable case of **constitutional
checks on kingship** in pre-colonial Africa — the dominant Yoruba power before the nineteenth-century
upheavals.

> **GHA VI note.** The [[gha-vol6-19th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. VI]] (ch. 26, Asiwaju)
> details Oyo's **collapse** in the early nineteenth century and the **Yoruba wars** that followed — the
> jihad-linked Ilorin revolt, the sack of Old Oyo (c. 1836), and the rise of new Yoruba powers such as
> **Ibadan** amid the turmoil that fed the Atlantic slave trade right up to abolition.
