# Rosalind Franklin

**Summary & network:** [[hubs/biographies/cold-war/franklin-rosalind|Detailed analytic biography]] · **Biography hub:** [[hubs/biographies/biographies-hub|Biographies Hub]]

Rosalind Elsie Franklin (25 July 1920 – 16 April 1958) was an English chemist and X-ray crystallographer whose work was central to the understanding of the molecular structures of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), RNA (ribonucleic acid), viruses, coal, and graphite. Her structural data and X-ray diffraction images (specifically Photo 51) were critical inputs for James Watson and Francis Crick's 1953 proposal of the double-helix structure of DNA. 

Franklin was educated at Newnham College, Cambridge, and completed her PhD on the physical chemistry of coal at the British Coal Utilisation Research Association (BCURA) during the Second World War. After the war, she worked in Paris under Jacques Mering, mastering X-ray diffraction techniques. She joined King's College London in 1951, where she discovered the two forms of DNA (A and B) and resolved their hydration behavior. Following her work on DNA, she moved to Birkbeck College in 1953, where she led pioneering research on the structure of Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV) and RNA, establishing structural biology as a field. Her career was cut short by her death from ovarian cancer in 1958 at the age of 37.

## Role and Significance

Franklin stands at the center of one of the most significant scientific discoveries of the twentieth century. Her exceptionally precise experimental data on the DNA molecule provided the empirical foundation that the double-helix model required, yet she was historically marginalized in initial accounts of the discovery. Her subsequent pioneering work on viruses laid the groundwork for structural virology. Her life illustrates the challenges faced by women in mid-century academic institutions, as well as the collaborative and competitive dynamics of modern big science.

## Key Events and Processes

- Formation at St Paul's Girls' School and Newnham College, Cambridge.
- Carbon micro-structure research at BCURA (1942–1946) and Jacques Mering's lab in Paris (1947–1950).
- DNA structure work at King's College London, Raymond Gosling collaboration, and conflict with Maurice Wilkins (1951–1953).
- Creation of Photo 51 in May 1952, showing the helical structure of B-DNA.
- Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV) structural research at Birkbeck College and collaboration with Aaron Klug (1953–1958).
- Final research on polio virus, illness, and death in 1958.
