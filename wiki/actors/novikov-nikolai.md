# Nikolai Novikov

## Biography / Description
**Nikolai Novikov** was a leading Russian journalist, publisher, and social critic during the reign of Catherine the Great (L3740). As the head of the active, socially-oriented wing of Russian Freemasonry, he centered his activities around the University of Moscow, where he established a private school and organized the first large-scale independent publishing program in Russian history (L3740). 

Novikov used his periodicals to criticize the corruption of Russian society and the excesses of serfdom (L3740-3742). In 1792, amidst the conservative panic triggered by the French Revolution, Catherine the Great ordered his arrest, bringing his educational and publishing projects to an abrupt end (L3746-3748).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
* **Journalism and Satire:** Novikov published several popular satirical journals (such as *The Drone* and *The Painter*) that engaged in lively, critical debates on the state of Russian society, occasionally clashing with journals edited by Catherine the Great herself (L3683, L3733, L3740).
* **Independent Publishing:** He leased the press of Moscow University and established the Typographical Company, which printed a vast array of books, translations, and journals, significantly expanding the reading public outside of direct government control (L3740).
* **Philanthropy and Education:** Using Masonic networks, Novikov established schools, dispensaries for the poor, and relief funds during famines, demonstrating a practical commitment to social improvement (L3740).
* **Social Criticism of Serfdom:** Novikov went beyond typical gentry satire by depicting the systemic injustices and economic exploitation of serfdom, advocating for reform (L3742).

## Historical Significance
Novikov's activities represented the peak of the independent, public-spirited Russian Enlightenment of the gentry class (L3740). His arrest in 1792 and the subsequent closure of his printing houses marked the autocracy's retreat from partnership with independent intellectual circles, establishing the patterns of state censorship and suspicion of civil society that characterized much of the 19th century (L3746-3750).
