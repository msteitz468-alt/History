# Vichy France (État français), 1940–1944

The authoritarian French state established after the [[fall-of-france-1940]],
seated in the spa town of Vichy in the unoccupied southern zone and headed by
Marshal [[petain-philippe|Philippe Pétain]]. Formally sovereign over all of
France under the [[franco-german-armistice-1940|armistice]], it governed the
unoccupied zone directly until Germany occupied the whole country in November
1942 (after [[operation-torch-1942]]), then persisted as a shrinking
administration until the Liberation of summer 1944. This page follows
[[paxton-vichy-france-1972]], the foundational modern study.

## Founding, 1940

The Pétain government of 17 June 1940 was formed with full Third Republic
legality — a union nationale cabinet that even included SFIO deputies. The
armistice was reached "by modest steps, and not by conspiracy," and the
alternatives (fighting on from North Africa above all) were never formally
voted down. On 9 July 1940 the National Assembly voted 624–4 to revise the
constitutional laws; on **10 July it voted 569–80 (17 abstentions)** to grant
Pétain authority to draft an entirely new constitution. Paxton's judgment:
"The Assembly's stand of July 9, 1940, was no revolution from above. It
reflected almost unanimous French public opinion" — [[laval-pierre|Laval]]
facilitated but did not coerce the outcome. Constitutional Acts One–Three
(11 July) made Pétain "Head of the French State" with full executive and
legislative power. Vichy itself was a negative choice of capital: the most
hotel rooms, no politician's fiefdom.

## The quest for collaboration, 1940–1942

Paxton's central finding, from the captured German archives: **collaboration
was a French proposal, offered repeatedly to an indifferent or hostile
Germany** — "there is no doubt, from the German archives, who was the suitor
and who was the wooed." In July–August 1940 three channels ran simultaneously
(Pétain/Baudouin via Madrid, Huntziger via Wiesbaden, Laval via Abetz in
Paris). The Dakar crisis, not French goodwill, awakened Berlin's interest:
Vichy mattered as guardian of the empire against the Allies. The milestones:

- **Montoire (22/24 October 1940)** — Hitler met Laval, then Pétain, who
  announced on 31 October "I enter into the way of collaboration." Hitler,
  who had already promised Mussolini colonial gains at France's expense at
  the Brenner Pass (4 October), conceded nothing.
- **13 December 1940** — Pétain dismissed Laval. Paxton: this was a domestic
  and personal affair, not a policy reversal — "it is pure postwar invention
  to suggest… that Laval was removed in order to reverse the post-Montoire
  negotiations." Its chief effect was a German "cold shoulder."
- **[[darlan-francois|Darlan]]'s "Grand Design" (Feb 1941–Apr 1942)** — the
  period of maximum offered collaboration: Berchtesgaden (11 May 1941), the
  Paris Protocols (28 May 1941: Syrian airfields, Bizerte for Rommel, an
  eventual Dakar U-boat base), the loss of Syria (July 1941), and the note
  verbale of 14 July 1941 asking Germany to replace the armistice with a
  treaty of sovereignty and cooperation — dismissed by Ribbentrop as "a naive
  French blackmail attempt." Barbarossa ended German interest for good.
- **26 April 1942** — Laval returned as head of government: "a Vichy
  decision, not a German demand."

Vichy insiders' postwar image of Pétain as "Marshal Nein" finds no support in
the German records: "There Pétain appears rather as 'Marshal Bitte.'"

## Who ran Vichy

Not the 1930s fascist leagues. La Rocque was ostracized; Doriot never held
office; the Paris collaborationists got "the gaudier but less independent"
stage. Vichy was "a return of the notables, or perhaps more accurately a
persistence of the notables" — senior civil servants and the business elite
continued almost unchanged — and, at its height under Darlan, a regime of the
grands corps: "Vichy was more the creation of experts and professionals than
of any other social group, and to judge Vichy is to judge the French elite."
Its matrix was the "virtual French civil war" of 1934–37 (the 6 February 1934
riots, the Popular Front and the sit-down strikes): "antibolshevism is the
nearest thing to a Vichy common denominator," and the collaborators were "a
selection of Third Republic notables from one end of the political spectrum
to the other" — including an ex-left contingent (Belin and the CGT
"Syndicats" minority, Déat, Bergery, Lagardelle, Paul Faure) driven by
pacifism, anti-communism, and planism. See [[fascism-and-the-political-left]]
for how this evidence is used by both sides of that dispute.

## The turn to coercion and the end, 1941–1944

June–August 1941, not December 1940, was the real turning point: Communist
entry into resistance after Barbarossa brought assassinations, German hostage
massacres, and Vichy's Special Sections and Tribunal d'État — "having shed
blood, the regime now had to shed more and more of it to keep alive."
[[operation-torch-1942|Torch]] (Nov 1942) brought total occupation (11 Nov),
the dissolution of the Armistice Army (28 Nov), and the scuttling of the
fleet at Toulon (28 Nov): "the two trump cards in Vichy's hand now vanished."
The STO labor draft (Feb 1943) created the maquis; Darnand's Milice (~45,000)
fought it, climaxing at the Glières plateau (March 1944). In August 1944 the
Germans hauled Pétain and Laval east to Sigmaringen; the Liberation purge
tried 124,750 people and executed 767 under sentence, with at least 4,500
killed extrajudicially. Paxton's verdict: Pétain's followers "reaped the
divisions they sowed and the disorder they had compromised everything to
avoid."

## Legacy: breaks and continuities

"In a long view… continuities probably prevail over breaks." The grands corps
survived nearly intact (Cour des Comptes 98–99%, Inspectorate of Finance 97%;
only the deputies were destroyed — 14% survival into the 1949 Assembly). No
businessman was tried for collaboration. Vichy's planning apparatus,
regional super-prefects, old-age pensions, and family policy fed directly
into the Fourth Republic and the Monnet Plan: the occupation years moved
France away from the traditionalists' rural vision "toward the technicians'
vision: urban, efficient, productive, planned, and impersonal."

## Historiography

Paxton (1972) overturned the postwar "shield" orthodoxy (Aron 1954) — see
[[vichy-shield-thesis]] — using captured German and Italian archives while
French archives remained closed; he flags that this makes the book see Vichy
partly through German eyes. Its core findings (collaboration as French
initiative; the [[national-revolution-vichy|National Revolution]] as
indigenous reaction; the comparative demolition of the "lesser evil" claim)
became the scholarly consensus in both Anglophone and (after its 1973 French
translation) French historiography. On whether Vichy was *fascist*, Paxton
places the National Revolution "nearer the conservative than the fascist end"
of the radical-right spectrum — while adding that by the broader definition of
"hard measures by a frightened middle class… Vichy was fascist."

Payne ([[payne-history-of-fascism-1995]], 1995) concurs and is blunter:
"Vichy was not a fascist regime" but "a regime of moderate right
authoritarianism (though with increasing right radical overtones) that
identified with Franco and Salazar rather than with Hitler and Mussolini" —
in his seven-type regime schema a non-fascist satellite, unlike the genuinely
fascist Ustasha and Arrow Cross states. He adds the sting that its Jewish
policy "was much more destructive than that of Italian Fascism": by early
1943 Jews hunted by Vichy police were being protected by Italian occupation
authorities in southeastern France. Genuine fascists entered the cabinet
only in 1944 (Darnand of the Milice at security; Déat at labor). Payne cites
Paxton's modernization finding approvingly ("it was during Vichy that the
apostles of growth moved from oddity to commonplace") and records the purge
aftermath: ~163,000 prosecutions, 7,037 death sentences (791 carried out),
and an estimated 10,000–20,000 summary executions (Bourdrel).

Griffin ([[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]]) completes the three-way
convergence with his [[para-fascism]] classification: Vichy was "no slavish
imitation of Mussolini's Italy or Hitler's Germany, but a dynamic form of
home-grown neo-conservatism," born of military defeat rather than populist
revolution, with para-fascist institutions (Compagnons de France, Légion des
Anciens Combattants, the Milice) and an anti-Semitism "not biologically
grounded" that nonetheless enabled active collusion in the Nazi genocide;
Pétain is compared to Hindenburg, "not... Hitler." France's genuine fascist
ideologues (Doriot, Déat) were marginalized within it despite their
collaborationist zeal.

## Payne (1980): "distinctly rightist and authoritarian but never fascist"

*Fascism: Comparison and Definition* ([[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]], ch. 5) treats Vichy as the most important wartime satellite (it governed the most advanced country under German hegemony). "Pétain's regime was distinctly rightist and authoritarian but never fascist": it had no state party — the Parti Social Français (ex-Croix de Feu, at ~350,000 members "perhaps the largest political movement in France") was dissolved under Vichy, and the overt French fascists (Déat's RNP, Doriot's PPF) concentrated in the German-occupied northern zone. The National Revolution was "by no means merely a reactionary hoax" — its corporative regulation and limited technocracy partly survived into the Fourth and Fifth Republics. [CONTRADICTION — recorded, not adjudicated] Roger Bourderon argues Vichy *was* fascist (authoritarian rule, ideological convergence with the "new order," state economic controls, growing police power, radicalization under German pressure); Payne replies that this "seems to me to exaggerate and to blur distinctions" — no genuine state party, economic regulation "more nearly technocratic than fully statist," a "new order" blending Catholicism and progressivist republican corporatism more than Nazi/Fascist ideas — and insists early autonomous Vichy be distinguished from later German-controlled Vichy. He calls Paxton's *Vichy France* (ingested: [[paxton-vichy-france-1972]]) "the best one-volume synthesis."
