# Gaius "Caligula" (Gaius Julius Caesar Augustus Germanicus)

The **third Roman emperor** (37–41 CE) — the surviving son of the popular **Germanicus**,
acclaimed with universal joy and dead by assassination less than four years later, his name a
byword for tyrannical madness. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 5 (Wiedemann),
who mounts a sustained source-critical caution against the lurid tradition.

## Accession and the politics of beneficia

On Tiberius' death (37) the Senate acclaimed Caligula **alone**, setting aside Tiberius' will
(which had made his grandson **Gemellus** co-heir) on the pretext that the old emperor had been
insane — Gemellus soon being required to commit suicide. As Germanicus' son, Caligula
emphasised his **descent** (against Tiberius' rule by adoption), recalled exiles, honoured his
dead mother and brothers, deified his sister **Drusilla**, and lavished **donatives and games**
on guard and people after Tiberius' years of neglect. Wiedemann reads even the "extravagance"
as the rational distribution of *beneficia* required to establish a new regime.

## The "madness"

Wiedemann argues against dividing the reign into a "good" beginning and a descent into lunacy,
and against dating a "turning-point" to an illness of 37 or Drusilla's death (38). The famous
anecdotes — incest with his sisters, the horse **Incitatus** marked for the consulship, the
war on the sea (the bridge of boats at Baiae; ordering troops to gather seashells), the claims
to **divinity** — are reinterpreted as a mix of (a) political control over his kin and rivals
expressed through stories of sexual licence; (b) the standard topoi of the *tyrant*; (c)
genuine attempts to express the emperor's role as mediator between Rome and the gods (emperor-
worship being not an "oriental" aberration); and (d) misread **building works** on the
Palatine. His insistence on being honoured as a god was incomprehensible only to the monotheist
**Jews** — the origin of the crisis over his statue in the Temple.

In **39** he claimed to have uncovered a **conspiracy** centred on his brother-in-law **Marcus
Aemilius Lepidus** and the Rhine commander **Lentulus Gaetulicus**; both were executed, his
sisters Agrippina and Livilla exiled. He then conducted German expeditions and prepared (but
abandoned) an invasion of **Britain**.

## Assassination (41)

Caligula was murdered, with his wife and infant daughter, by the praetorian officer **Cassius
Chaerea** and fellow tribunes on **24 January 41** — the **first assassination of a reigning
emperor**, justified by its perpetrators as tyrannicide. Wiedemann warns against taking the
later justifications as proof of broad unpopularity; Chaerea had personal grievances. Notably,
the tradition does *not* accuse Caligula of being dominated by freedmen or prefects: "Caligula
did not shift the responsibility for his own actions onto others."

## Significance

His murder created the first **succession crisis without an heir** — the Senate briefly
debated restoring the Republic before the guard imposed his uncle **[[claudius]]**, proving
where power now lay: with the *domus Caesaris* and the praetorians, not the Senate.

## Beard (SPQR, 2015)

Mary Beard pushes the source-critical caution further: the lurid tradition — incest, the
horse Incitatus, the seashell-gathering soldiers (possibly a garbling of Latin *musculi*,
"shells" or "military huts") — is "an inextricable mixture of fact, exaggeration, wilful
misinterpretation and outright invention," largely constructed after Caligula's death "for
the benefit of the new emperor, [[claudius|Claudius]]." Her sharpest formulation: "Gaius may
have been assassinated because he was a monster, but it is equally possible that he was made
into a monster because he was assassinated." She treats his fall as illustrating a larger
point about imperial memory — see [[roman-empire]].
