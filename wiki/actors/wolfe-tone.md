## Overview

Theobald Wolfe Tone (1763–1798) was the principal founder of the Society of United Irishmen (1791) and the architect of the attempt to establish an Irish republic through French military intervention. His political achievement was ideological as much as military: he reframed Irish political identity from the sectarian and constitutional categories of Protestant patriotism to a vision of inclusive republican nationhood. His failure in 1798 — and his death in captivity after the collapse of the French alliance — made him the canonical martyr of Irish republicanism, invoked by every subsequent generation of Irish nationalists and republicans.

As Patrick Geoghegan writes in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], Ch. 25: "if tone did not, in his lifetime, achieve greatly, he started much. Without him, republicanism in ireland would virtually have no tradition" (quoting Seán Ó Faoláin, 1937).

## Career and Political Thought

Tone was a Protestant barrister of middling background, trained in law in Dublin and London. His key intellectual contribution came before the founding of the United Irishmen: his pamphlet *An Argument on Behalf of the Catholics of Ireland* (completed 1 August 1791, published three weeks later) argued that parliamentary reform in Ireland would fail, "and would deserve to fail, unless it included the Catholics of Ireland." The pamphlet sold 6,000 copies by year's end. Dr Hugh McDermot, a leading Catholic activist, declared it "should become the manual of every person who is worthy of being an irishman."

Tone's genius lay in showing "why Catholics had a capacity for liberty, and in how he popularized it" — dismantling the Protestant fear that political rights for Catholics were incompatible with constitutional stability. His goal was to "substitute the common name of Irishman in place of the denominations of Protestant, Catholic, and Dissenter." The United Irishmen was founded in Belfast on 18 October 1791, with the Dublin society following on 9 November. Lawyers formed approximately one-fifth of the original membership.

Tone's political thought was shaped by French Enlightenment ideas and by the French Revolution, which he saw as demonstrating "that the revolution seemed to contradict the Whig idea that the Catholics were incapable of appreciating liberty." He quoted from the *Northern Star* (the United Irish newspaper): "The present is an age of revolution." However, he had significant anti-Catholic prejudices that coexisted awkwardly with his political programme: he argued in *An Argument* that political freedom would lead Irish Catholics to abandon their religion. His personal faith was in secular republicanism, not Catholic identity.

## The French Alliance and Military Strategy

When the United Irishmen was declared illegal in 1795 and forced underground, Tone turned to France. His strategy was to secure French military intervention of sufficient scale to trigger a general Irish rising, capture Dublin, and use Ireland as a base for invading Britain. He persuaded the French Directory — and General Lazare Hoche, France's most capable young commander — of the feasibility of this plan, claiming a greater confidence about Irish conditions than he must have felt.

The **Bantry Bay expedition** of December 1796 came closest to success. A fleet of 17 ships of the line, 13 frigates, and 20 transports with 13,500 troops under Hoche set sail from Brest on 15 December 1796. Tone claimed it was "the nearest English escape since the Spanish Armada." French naval ineptitude — all admirals were placed on one ship, which sailed in the wrong direction — and severe storms at Bantry Bay prevented a landing. Tone was on the *Indomitable* with junior officers, not aboard Hoche's flagship. After waiting through a storm "compared to a hurricane by one English officer in Limerick who had experience of the West Indies," the mission was abandoned on 27 December. Throughout this period, no British ship had even attempted to intercept the fleet.

The failure of Bantry Bay led the French to adopt a new policy: wait until an Irish rebellion had broken out before getting involved, rather than attempt to trigger it by prior landing.

The **1798 rebellion** broke out in May without effective French coordination. A French force under Humbert landed in Connacht (August 1798) and briefly achieved local success before surrender. Before news of Humbert's surrender reached France, Tone set out with a smaller French force for Ulster. His ship, the *Hoche*, arrived three weeks late at Lough Swilly off Donegal. After "a furious six-hour battle at sea, where tone refused repeated offers to escape, he was captured." Brought to Dublin, he was tried by court martial on 10 November 1798, convicted of treason, and sentenced to hanging. Refused permission to be executed by firing squad as befitted a French army officer, he took a blade to his own throat in his prison cell and died a week later. His final words, according to tradition: "What should I wish to live for?"

## Legacy and Historiography

Tone's posthumous reputation was constructed by nationalist historiography rather than by his own limited military achievement. Seán Ó Faoláin's biography (1937) established the canonical "father of Irish republicanism" reading. The scholarly literature has complicated this: Tom Dunne's *Theobald Wolfe Tone, Colonial Outsider* (Cork, 1992) reads Tone as a marginal figure whose radicalism derived from his outsider status; Marianne Elliott's *Wolfe Tone: Prophet of Irish Independence* (London, 1989; 2nd ed. Liverpool, 2012) provides the fullest scholarly biography and treats him as a genuinely important political thinker. Thomas Bartlett's work (including an edition of *Life of Theobald Wolfe Tone*, Dublin, 1998) has placed him within the broader context of late eighteenth-century Atlantic republicanism.

The ideological tension in Tone — a Protestant republican who instrumentalized Catholic grievances he personally found somewhat distasteful, and whose vision of the Irish nation was in practice built on sectarian mobilization — recurs in the later historiography. The Taoiseach Bertie Ahern, speaking at Bodenstown on 17 October 2004, attempted to claim Tone for a "democratic republicanism" compatible with the modern Irish state and even with unionism, noting Tone's criticisms of the papacy. This is a political appropriation that the biographical and historical literature does not fully support.

The **standing controversy** over the interpretation of 1798 — nationalist versus revisionist — directly affects how Tone is read: nationalist accounts stress his republican vision, revisionist accounts emphasize the sectarian character of the rebellion he attempted to lead. See [[irish-rebellion-1798]] and [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], Ch. 25.

## Counterfactual Significance

What changes without Tone? The United Irishmen's founding pamphlet — which set the terms for inclusive republican politics — was his. Without his specific argument that Catholic inclusion was the necessary condition for any effective reform, the United Irishmen might have remained a Protestant radical society with a more limited constitutional agenda. The French alliance — secured by his personal persuasiveness in Paris — was also substantially his achievement. Whether the rebellion would have occurred without these factors is debatable; but its specifically republican and anti-British character owes more to Tone than to any other individual. After 1798, republicanism had "a tradition" in Ireland that it would not otherwise have had.
