## Overview

Eadwine (Edwin) was king of [[deira|Deira]] and subsequently the first of a succession of northern Anglian overlords, ruling as Bede's fifth overlord of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms south of the Humber. Son of [[ælle-of-deira|Ælle of Deira]], he spent his youth in prolonged exile before [[raedwald-of-east-anglia|Rædwald of East Anglia]] defeated and killed [[æthelfrith-of-bernicia|Æthelfrith]] on the River Idle in 616 or 617, enabling Eadwine to reunite Deira and Bernicia under his own kingship. He was "47 years old when he was killed," placing his birth c. 586/7. His reign represented the apogee of Deiran power, and his conversion and baptism at York in 627 or 628 made him the first non-Kentish Anglo-Saxon ruler to adopt Christianity. He was killed at [[battle-of-hatfield-chase-633|Hatfield Chase]] by Cadwallon of Gwynedd and [[penda-of-mercia|Penda of Mercia]].

## Chronology

Kirby's principal chronological argument: the Northumbrian king-list gives Eadwine a reign of seventeen years; if accession was 617, death falls in 634. Bede placed accession in 616 and death in October 633. The stronger evidence points to 617 and 634: Pope Honorius I's letter to Honorius of Canterbury, dated June 634, was written jointly in response to a request from Eadwine and Eadbald for pallia, implying that Eadwine was alive in June 634. "The balance of probability must be, therefore, that the pope would have heard of the death of Eadwine by June 634 if the king had perished eight months before. Consequently, the likelihood exists — to put it no stronger — that Eadwine was killed in October 634."

## Exile, Accession, and Early Hegemony

During [[æthelfrith-of-bernicia|Æthelfrith]]'s reign Eadwine spent years as a fugitive; while in exile he married his first wife, Cwoenburh (Cwenburh), daughter of Cearl, king of the Mercians. On Rædwald's victory over Æthelfrith, Eadwine was established as king of the northern Angles, initially possibly in a dependent capacity under Rædwald's overlordship. "The key to Eadwine's ability not only to acquire the kingship of the Deirans but to expel Aethelfrith's sons from Bernicia…and thereby recreate Aethelfrith's overkingship may have lain in the continuing support of Raedwald."

After Rædwald's death (probably before c. 624), Eadwine sought a second wife at the prestigious Kentish court of Eadbald rather than among the East Anglians — a signal of his rising independent prestige. He married Aethelburh, Eadbald's sister, and her chaplain Paulinus came north with her, subsequently consecrated bishop of York by Archbishop Justus in 625 or 626.

## Overlordship

Eadwine extended his authority systematically. He conquered the British kingdom of Elmet, driving out King Ceretic. He dominated Lindsey (where Paulinus baptized at Lincoln and Littleborough), the Mercians north and south of the Trent, and the Saxons of the upper Thames valley. Around 625–626, when Cwichelm of the Gewisse attempted his assassination, "Eadwine's retaliating attack, when he is said to have slain or subdued all those who had plotted against him (HE II, 9), confirms that he was reaching the height of his power by the mid-620s." Bede "specifically excludes Kent from the kingdoms south of the Humber which he regarded as subject to Eadwine."

He also extended power into British territory: the Welsh annals record his siege of Cadwallon on the island of Priestholm (Ynys Lannog) in 629; "Eadwine brought under Anglian rule the Mevanian Islands, by which Môn (Anglesey) and Man are meant (HE II, 5), making it clear that the northern Anglian king must have established an impressive naval and military presence in the Irish Sea."

## Conversion

Eadwine was baptized at York at Easter in 627 or 628 by Paulinus, making him the first northern Anglian ruler to convert and the first non-Kentish Anglo-Saxon king to adopt Christianity in the 620s. Bede's account of successive "conditional promises" to convert — pending marriage, victory against Cwichelm, and conciliar advice — carries "an air of literary artificiality." The Historia Brittonum's claim that he was baptized by the Briton Rhun, son of Urien, cannot be accepted without reservation. His baptism was followed by ecclesiastical missions to Lindsey and efforts to revive the Gregorian plan for a northern archbishopric at York.

## Ecclesiastical Programme

Eadwine's reign saw a deliberate effort to embed York within the Roman ecclesiastical structure. He and Eadbald jointly requested pallia from Pope Honorius I for Paulinus of York and Honorius of Canterbury. The pope dispatched the pallia in June 634, but Eadwine had already been killed, "terminated this phase in the history of the church of York and arrested its development as an archiepiscopal see for a hundred years."

## British Wars and Fall

Eadwine's aggressive campaigns in north Wales, aimed at Gwynedd, provoked a major counter-alliance. Cadwallon ap Cadfan of Gwynedd, who had been besieged on Priestholm by Eadwine and driven into exile in Ireland, formed an alliance with Penda and invaded in 633/634. On 12 October, Eadwine was "defeated and slain in battle in Hatfield Chase." His son Osfrith perished with him; another son, Eadfrith, surrendered to Penda; Queen Aethelburh and Paulinus fled to Kent with the surviving children. His severed head was reportedly brought to Aberffraw, principal residence of the kings of Gwynedd.

## Counterfactual Significance

Eadwine's death shattered the Deiran hegemony and allowed the return of Æthelfrith's exiled sons, permanently shifting political dominance back toward Bernicia. Without his baptism and his ambition for York's metropolitan status, the ecclesiastical alignment of the northern Angles with the Roman church would have been delayed; the Celtic mission that filled the vacuum under Oswald set up the tensions resolved at Whitby a generation later.

## Historiography

Kirby's key contribution is his argument for 617 (not 616) as accession year and 634 (not 633) as death year, based on the June 634 papal letter. He is cautious about Bede's dramatised account of the conversion, noting its "air of literary artificiality," and sceptical of the Historia Brittonum's attribution of the baptism to the Briton Rhun. The sources are strongly coloured by the Whitby community's veneration of Eadwine as a saint; the Whitby Life of Pope Gregory, written in Abbess Aelfflaed's community, is explicitly partisan.
