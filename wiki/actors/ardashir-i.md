# Ardashir I

**Founder of the [[sasanian-empire|Sassanid Empire]]** (r. c. 224–242) — a local ruler of **Fars**
(Persis) who overthrew the [[parthian-empire|Parthian Arsacids]] and built the centralized,
Zoroastrian Iranian state that would rival Rome for four centuries. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH
XII]] Ch. 14 (**Richard N. Frye**).

## Rise in Fars

Ardashir was the son (or adopted son) of **Papak** and grandson — by the dynasty's name — of **Sasan**;
the exact relationships are tangled in the sources (the Middle Persian *Karnamak*, Firdosi's *Shahname*,
Tabari, and Shapur's *ŠKZ* inscription give conflicting genealogies). His first capital was at **Gor**
(Firuzabad), later renamed *Ardashir-khwarreh* ("the glory of Ardashir"). Frye stresses the **geography
of Fars** — walled off by highland, desert and gulf, with a concentrated reservoir of manpower — as the
self-contained base from which an ambitious ruler could move on the rest of Iran. Ardashir spent **over a
decade** subduing the local rulers of Fars before challenging the Parthians.

## The overthrow of Parthia

Exploiting the division of Parthian power between **Artabanus IV** and **Vologaeses**, Ardashir defeated
and killed Artabanus at the battle of **Hormizdagan** (c. 224), after which most of the great Parthian
lords (the Karen, the Suren) submitted. He was proclaimed ***shahanshah* ("king of kings") of Iran** and
crowned with the kindling of his royal fire, probably at the Anahita temple in **Istakhr** near
Persepolis. Only in **[[armenia|Armenia]]** did an Arsacid line survive in opposition. Frye is sceptical
of the classical claim (Herodian, Dio) that Ardashir proclaimed a **restoration of the Achaemenid
empire** — Iranian tradition had forgotten the Achaemenids entirely; the ambition to recover "Iranian"
lands was real, but not a conscious Achaemenid revival.

## Centralization and the turn west

Ardashir's structural innovation was to **remove defeated local dynasts and appoint members of his own
family** as provincial kings-governors (Merv, Kirman, Sakastan in the *ŠKZ*) — replacing Parthian
vassalage with a centralized house. He received the submission of the western **Kushans** in the east,
then turned on the Roman frontier (from c. 230): repulsed at **Hatra** and **Nisibis** at first, he took
**Nisibis and Carrhae** (237/8) and finally **Hatra** (240), the last of the great caravan cities. He was
succeeded c. 240–242 by his son **[[shapur-i|Shapur I]]**, under whom the dynasty reached its zenith.

## Significance

Ardashir's counterfactual weight is large: without his consolidation of Fars and overthrow of a
weakening Parthia, Rome's eastern frontier might have continued to face the looser, less dangerous
Arsacid confederation rather than a centralized rival capable of sacking Antioch and capturing an emperor.
He is the structural founder both of the **Sassanian state** and of its fusion with a **Zoroastrian
church** that his successors completed.

## Wiesehöfer: legitimation and the limits of "rupture"

[[sources/wiesehofer-ancient-persia-2001|Wiesehöfer]] sharpens Frye's scepticism and
stresses **continuity**. The house of Sasan were former Parthian "petty kings" of Istakhr;
their claimed break with the [[parthian-empire|Arsacids]] was largely a *retrospective*
construction, since "internal stability was possible only through continuity in
organization as well as personnel." Ardashir's legitimation worked through Zoroastrian and
mythic, not Achaemenid, idioms: the **xwarrah** (divine Glory/Fortune) settles on the
rightful ruler — in the *Kārnāmag* romance Ardawān's own judge concedes "the divine grace
of the Kayanians has reached Ardashir, there is no longer any possible way to catch him" —
and the later [[eranshahr|Xwadāy-nāmag]]/Tabari genealogy back-dated his line to the
mythical **Kayanians** (via Sasan as a "shepherd of Pabag … from the family of Dārā"),
recasting his revolt as vengeance for Darius III. He minted the **king's fire** (*ādur*)
era and founded his circular city **Ardashīr-xwarrah** at Firuzabad. The tight Zoroastrian
"throne and altar" later ascribed to his reign Wiesehöfer judges a back-projection (see
[[kartir]], [[eranshahr]], [[zoroastrianism]]).
