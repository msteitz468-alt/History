# Edmund Barton

First Prime Minister of the Commonwealth of Australia; chief federationist leader
in New South Wales; sworn in at Centennial Park, Sydney, on 1 January 1901.

## Role and Career

Barton was a NSW barrister and colonial politician who became the central
organiser of the federation movement in the 1890s. He led the NSW delegation
at the **National Australasian Convention** (1891) and was the convention's
effective leader at the **1897–98 Constitutional Convention** (Adelaide, Sydney,
Melbourne sessions). At Adelaide in 1897, he opened with a resolution "to enlarge
the powers of self-government of the people of Australia."

He and [[henry-parkes]] took "the momentous step of agreeing" to set aside the
protectionism/free-trade conflict — Barton a Protectionist, Parkes a free trader —
to pursue federation, the decisive compromise that unblocked the 1891 convention.

## The London Delegation (1900)

Barton led the Australian delegation to London with Alfred Deakin, Charles Kingston,
Phillip Fysh, and James Dickson "to secure [the Constitution Bill's] passage without
amendment." He negotiated with Colonial Secretary Joseph Chamberlain over the
jurisdiction of the Privy Council versus Australia's proposed High Court, ultimately
accepting a compromise Deakin called "the golden bridge over which we passed to
union." The delegates "took each other's hands and did a little victory dance" on
reaching agreement.

## First Prime Minister

The first governor-general, Lord Hopetoun, initially committed what became known as
the "Hopetoun blunder" by nominating NSW Premier William Lyne (an anti-federationist).
"A transcolonial campaign against Lyne, led by Deakin," reversed the appointment.
Barton — "the tireless federationist who seemed incapable of making enemies" — was
sworn in by Hopetoun on 1 January 1901 at Centennial Park, Sydney. He subsequently
served as a Justice of the first High Court of Australia.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Barton's organisational capacity and personal diplomacy in the 1890s, the
federation movement in NSW — the colony most resistant to federation — might have
failed to generate the political leadership needed to convert Premier Reid and
mobilise the popular leagues. The 1898 referendum failure in NSW shows how contingent
the outcome was.

## Historiography

Helen Irving, *CHA* Ch 10, treats Barton as the practical fulcrum of the federation
movement — less visionary than Deakin, less symbolic than Parkes, but indispensable
as organiser and negotiator. The *Official Record of the Debates of the Australasian
Federal Convention* is the primary source for his convention role.
