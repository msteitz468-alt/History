# Ōkuma Shigenobu (1838–1922)

Meiji statesman, constitutional reformer, founder of the Kaishintō (Constitutional Progressive
Party) and of Waseda University, and a persistent advocate of British-style parliamentary
government. His forced removal from the government in 1881 crystallized the oligarchic versus
constitutionalist divide in early Meiji politics and channeled the [[freedom-and-popular-rights-movement]]
into organized party politics. Linked to [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]].

## Career

A domain official from Saga (Hizen), Ōkuma entered the Meiji government after the Restoration
and rose rapidly, serving as finance minister and playing a central role in early fiscal and
economic policy. He became associated with the British constitutional model — a rapid convening
of parliament with a cabinet responsible to a popularly elected legislature. This brought him
into direct conflict with [[ito-hirobumi]] and the Satsuma-Chōshū oligarchs, who favored the
Prussian model: a strong imperial executive with a carefully limited parliamentary role.

## Political Crisis of 1881 and the Kaishintō

In 1881, Ōkuma proposed convening a national parliament rapidly along British lines. The oligarchs
— particularly from Satsuma and Chōshū — forced him out of the government. The government
simultaneously issued an imperial rescript promising a parliament by 1890, attempting to pre-empt
the popular-rights movement by controlling the terms and timing of constitutionalization. Ōkuma
responded by founding the **Kaishintō** (Constitutional Progressive Party) in 1882, which
advocated gradual British-style parliamentary reform with a qualified franchise. The Kaishintō
drew support from urban merchants, journalists, and intellectuals, contrasting with [[itagaki-taisuke]]'s
Jiyūtō, which had stronger rural and ex-samurai support.

## The First Diet

Keene's account describes the convening of the first Imperial Diet in November 1890 as "the
realization of the dreams of many men, notably Ōkuma Shigenobu." Ōkuma had spent a decade in
opposition, organizing party politics outside the government while [[ito-hirobumi]] constructed
the constitutional machinery. When the [[first-general-election-japan-1890]] was held on July 1,
1890, and the opposition parties won a majority in the 300-seat House of Representatives,
Ōkuma's vision of parliamentary politics operating in Japan had been partially vindicated —
though the constitutional constraints [[ito-hirobumi]] had built in severely limited what the
parties could actually do.

## Later Career

Ōkuma served briefly as prime minister in 1898 at the head of Japan's first genuine party
cabinet (the Ōkuma-Itagaki coalition, known as the "Kenseito cabinet"), and again from 1914 to
1916. He founded Waseda University in 1882 as an independent center of Western learning outside
government control. He lived until 1922, witnessing the Taishō Democracy period when the
parliamentary government he had advocated gained real traction.

## Counterfactual Significance

Ōkuma represents the road not taken in Meiji constitutionalism. Had the British model prevailed
in 1881 — a parliament convened rapidly with a cabinet responsible to it — Japan's political
development would have given civilian politicians executive control from the outset. The Prussian
model that [[ito-hirobumi]] imposed instead insulated the cabinet and military from parliamentary
accountability, creating the structural conditions that [[japanese-militarism]] exploited in the
1930s. Ōkuma's expulsion from government in 1881 was thus a pivotal moment in modern Japanese
history.
