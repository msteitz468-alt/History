## Overview

Lenin summoned the world's socialists to Moscow in March 1919 to found a new
global organization, the Communist International (Comintern), or Third
International — named to distinguish it from the prewar Second International
(founded 1889), which Lenin regarded as fatally compromised by its
accommodation of parliamentarism and its failure to prevent the belligerent
powers' socialist parties from voting war credits in 1914. Lenin's timing
reflected genuine optimism: he summoned the Comintern while encouraged by the
apparently revolutionary situations in both Hungary (see [[kun-bela]]) and
Germany (see [[german-revolution-1918-1919]]).

## Role and the Division of the European Left

The Comintern's initial reception among Western European socialists was
enthusiastic — most of the Left, discredited by the Second International's
prewar failure and by many of its leaders' wartime government service, was
eager for renewed unity, and eager to defend the first socialist regime in
history from being crushed by its own governments. The Italian, Norwegian,
and Bulgarian socialist parties adhered en masse in 1919; German, French, and
British parties sent sympathetic observers.

Lenin did not want sympathizers, however; he wanted disciplined followers
prepared to seize power by coup even against a majority. At the Comintern's
second congress in July 1920 — timed amid Lenin's confidence that Soviet
success in the Russo-Polish War would soon carry revolution into Germany — he
imposed the **Twenty-One Points** on candidate parties: purge reformist
elements, restructure "in the most centralized fashion," support the "Soviet
Republics" against foreign intervention, prepare for violent seizure of
power, and combat reformist Social Democrats by all available means. Lenin
accepted no piecemeal adherence; he specified by name the reformist leaders
each party had to expel.

The result was the permanent, bitter split of the European Left between
Communists and (more numerous) Social Democrats, which Paxton dates as
lasting "until the formation of popular fronts against fascism in the
mid-1930s." Outcomes varied sharply by country: the British Labour Party and
the Austrian and Swedish Social Democratic parties lost only a fraction of
their membership; the maximalist Italian Socialist Party was the only major
Western party to go over en bloc (October 1919), though it later split again,
with only about one-third of Giacinto Serrati's maximalists finally forming
the Italian Communist Party in January 1921; the French SFIO divided on
December 25, 1920, with the majority accepting the Twenty-One Points and
taking the party apparatus and newspaper (*Humanité*); only about one-third of
Germany's Independent Social Democrats (USPD) joined the Comintern in
December 1920, the rest returning to the SPD or leaving politics in
disillusion.

Even these successes proved fragile through the 1920s, as Moscow discovered
it had recruited revolutionary enthusiasts rather than disciplined cadres:
France's new Communist Party was full of anarcho-syndicalists who dropped
away once centralized Bolshevik governance became clear; the Norwegian Labour
Party, having joined en masse in 1919, withdrew in 1923.

## Dual Diplomacy

Paxton stresses that the Comintern operated in parallel with, and in tension
against, official Soviet state diplomacy. The USSR conducted normal diplomacy
as a sovereign state — exchanging ambassadors with Weimar Germany at Rapallo
(1922), then with Britain and France (1924) — through Foreign Commissar
George Chicherin, a former tsarist diplomat and Menshevik by conviction. The
Comintern, by contrast, worked clandestinely within foreign states through
local communist parties, led through the 1920s by the Old Bolshevik Grigori
Zinoviev, to strengthen internal opposition against those same states. This
"ambiguous dual diplomacy," per Paxton, complicated non-Communist states'
relations with Moscow throughout the interwar period; alleged Comintern
interference contributed to the Labour Party's 1924 electoral defeat and
Britain's subsequent suspension of diplomatic relations with the USSR. The
Comintern's influence over actual Soviet foreign policy diminished across the
1920s as state interest and "socialism in one country" concerns prevailed
over the promotion of world revolution.

## Historiography

Paxton treats the Comintern primarily as the mechanism by which the 1917–1920
revolutionary wave's failure outside Russia hardened into permanent
organizational schism, rather than as an independent causal force. Key
citation: Jon Jacobson, *When the Soviet Union Entered World Politics* (1994),
for the Soviet international role in the 1920s. No existing wiki page treated
the Comintern as a standing entity prior to this ingest; cross-link from
[[russian-revolution-1917]] and [[german-revolution-1918-1919]] recommended.
