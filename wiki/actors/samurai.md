# The Samurai (and Japanese Feudalism)

The **warrior class** that dominated [[japan|Japan]] for seven centuries — and the
**feudal** order of lord, vassal, and loyal military service that it embodied. From
Mason & Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]] (chs 9–10).

## Origins: not a class, then a class

The future samurai began in the [[heian-period|Heian]] provinces **not as a distinct
caste** but as "men of substance" — local administrators and managers of *shōen* estates
who provided armed force for order and protection. What turned fighting men into **vassals**
was the **lord–follower bond**: warriors served a military leader (a Minamoto or Taira), and
the leader rewarded them by confirming or enlarging their rights to estate income (***shiki***).
By the twelfth century such leaders rivaled the civil aristocracy, and with
[[minamoto-no-yoritomo|Yoritomo's]] victory the warriors became the effective rulers of
the country.

## Japanese feudalism

Mason and Caiger compare Japanese feudalism to its Western counterpart — **vassalage, a
warrior ethos, and (eventually) fiefs** — but stress a key early difference: in its first
centuries it was built not on **fiefs** (compact lands held from an overlord) but on
**shiki** (divisible shares of estate income). Compact, defensible **fiefs/domains**
became central only later, with the *sengoku* **daimyo**. Crucially, feudal institutions
grew **within** the old monarchical framework and remained dependent on it (the
[[shogunate-bakufu|dyarchy]] of court and bakufu).

## The warrior ethic

The ideal vassal's first duty was **loyalty** — celebrated in the war tales (the *Heike
Monogatari*, the *Taiheiki*) where retainers ride knowingly to death beside their lord. But
loyalty was sustained by **tangible reward** (confirmation of land rights): "warriors were
loyal to military leaders because they could expect tangible rewards." The culture of the
warrior fused this martial ethos with [[zen-buddhism|Zen]] self-discipline and an
aesthetic of restraint, and with a Buddhist sense of the **impermanence of all things**.
The fully codified "way of the warrior" (*bushidō*) is largely an [[japan|Edo-period]]
elaboration (Cycle 3).

## Evolution

- **Kamakura/Muromachi:** provincial vassals of the [[shogunate-bakufu|bakufu]]; then, as
  central authority collapsed, the self-made **daimyo** and their retainers of the Warring
  States.
- **Tokugawa:** the samurai become a **bureaucratized, stipended hereditary estate**,
  separated from the land and gathered in castle-towns, increasingly administrators rather
  than fighters. *(Cycle 3.)*
- **Meiji:** their privileges (stipends, the right to bear swords) **abolished** in the
  1870s as Japan built a modern conscript army. *(Cycle 4.)*

## Significance

The samurai are the central political actors of medieval and early-modern Japan — the class
whose lord–vassal bonds built the [[shogunate-bakufu|shogunate]], whose wars and culture
defined the age, and whose disestablishment marks the birth of modern Japan.
