## Overview

Cædwalla was a West Saxon prince of the Gewissian line who burst from exile onto the political stage in the mid-680s, briefly established the most extensive overkingship in southern England, and then abdicated in 688 to seek baptism in Rome, dying within ten days of receiving it on 10 April 689. His meteoric career created and then dissolved a potential West Saxon dominance of the south before Ine could consolidate it.

## Origins and Exile

Cædwalla first appears "as a noble exile in the forests of Chiltern and Andred." He was "remembered as a son of the King Cenberht who had been a co-ruler of the western Saxons with Cenwealh before his death in the 660s." His exile may have owed as much to the impact of Wulfhere's Mercian expansion as to internal West Saxon factional strife. He was still a pagan in his mid-twenties in 685 and commanded "what was evidently a powerful fighting force."

## The Wilfrid Connection

While in Andredsweald, Cædwalla encountered Wilfrid, the exiled bishop of York, who was converting the South Saxons under Æthelwealh's patronage. Though Cædwalla remained unbaptized, Wilfrid is said to have been sought by him "as a spiritual father." Kirby is careful to note that Wilfrid exercised no control over Cædwalla's military actions and did not behave treacherously to Æthelwealh; the warfare was outside his control. But Wilfrid was quick to accommodate himself to the victor.

## Military Campaigns, 685–688

**South Saxons**: Cædwalla attacked Æthelwealh, was initially driven out by the duces Berhthun and Andhun, then attacked again and slew the king (HE IV, 15). Berhthun and Andhun governed the kingdom thereafter, possibly as kings.

**Isle of Wight**: Cædwalla attacked with the "intent to exterminate the natives and replace them with inhabitants of his own kingdom, vowing, though still a pagan, that he would give a fourth part of the island and the booty seized to the Church if he were successful." He killed King Arwald and had Arwald's two young brothers put to death after allowing them to receive baptism.

**Kent**: In 686 he ravaged Kent with his brother Mul. Mul was installed as king but "was burnt to death there and Caedwalla responded with a second invasion in 687" (ASC A, s.a. 687), perhaps ruling Kent directly for a time. The Chronicle A dates his beginning to contend for the kingship to 685, but Bede confirms a two-year reign before abdication in 688 (HE IV, 12; V, 7), placing his effective rule from 686.

## Peak of Power

By 686–688 Cædwalla held authority in Surrey (land grant at Farnham), Kent (land at Hoo in Kent to Abbot Ecgbald), and had established West Saxon influence north of the Thames for the first time, as shown by the grant of land in Battersea to Barking. Aldhelm later described him as "renowned in war and arms…a powerful occupant of the throne." Kirby judges that "not only had he transformed the political situation among the western Saxons but he had also established himself as the most powerful ruler in southern England."

## Abdication and Death

The most likely explanation for abdication in 688 is that the illness which killed him within days of baptism "was of long standing and that Caedwalla abdicated in the knowledge that his health was failing." He was the first Anglo-Saxon king known to have gone to Rome unbaptised to seek baptism — dying on 10 April 689, probably in his early thirties.

## Counterfactual Significance

"If his achievement had been consolidated, a new and formidable political and military order might have been established which could have proved resilient to subsequent Mercian attack." Kirby identifies a West Saxon dominance of southern England as one of three plausible trajectories for the late seventh century — all foreclosed by the equilibrium that actually emerged. Cædwalla's abdication created the power vacuum that neither Ine nor the Mercians immediately filled.

## Historiography

The primary sources are Bede (HE IV, 12–16; V, 7) and Stephen of Ripon's *Vita Wilfridi* (chs 41–44). Kirby notes that Stephen's account must be used with caution on the conversion of the South Saxons since it exaggerates their paganism in ways Bede's non-Wilfridian sources contradict. The dating debate (two-year vs. three-year reign) is resolved by Bede's explicit statement of two years before abdication in 688 (HE IV, 12; V, 7).
