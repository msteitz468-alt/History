# Lösener, Bernhard (1890–1952)

Lawyer and racial policy expert in the Reich Interior Ministry; one of the principal draftsmen of the Nuremberg Laws. Participated in the June 5, 1934 planning meeting. Later authored the November 1935 supplementary decrees that created the "Mischling" (first- and second-degree) and "Geltungsjude" categories — bureaucratic definitions that governed persecution, deportation, and extermination eligibility.

## Role and Significance

Lösener represented the more "moderate" or at least technically minded civil-service faction. He was concerned with workable, internally coherent legal definitions rather than the most extreme street or party demands. He and Gürtner pushed back against some radical proposals at the 1934 meeting on grounds of legal science and practicality. Nevertheless, he produced the definitional machinery (grandparent rule based on religion of grandparents rather than "blood science") that made the laws administratively usable at scale. Post-war memoirs and records show him claiming limited resistance and later disillusionment; he was not a high-profile resister.

## Key Events / Processes

- June 5, 1934 meeting and subsequent drafting: central figure (as "Judenreferent" and principal draftsman); present at the session where US models were debated. Part of the "moderate"/juristic faction that resisted the most extreme proposals on grounds of legal science, the difficulty of defining "Jew," and workability (e.g., criminalization of mixed marriage clashed with traditional civil-law doctrine that marriage was not ordinarily a criminal matter).
- November 1935 First Regulation to the Reich Citizenship Law: the key bureaucratic elaboration that turned the abstract 15 September laws into a workable (and arbitrary) classification system ("is a Jew" = 3+ Jewish grandparents; "counts as" = 2 + religion or marriage to Jew). Religion of grandparents became the operative criterion.
- Continued work on racial legislation and administration through the 1930s and into the war years.

## Counterfactual Note

Lösener's definitions locked in a system that was both expansive enough for the regime's purposes and rigid enough to survive bureaucratic implementation. Different definitional choices (or successful resistance to any statutory codification) might have left more room for individual case-by-case evasion or later reversal, though the underlying political will to persecute was not dependent on any one lawyer.

## Historiography

Lösener appears in studies of the "banality" and bureaucratic momentum of Nazi persecution as well as in accounts of intra-regime conflicts between "old" civil servants and party radicals. His post-war self-presentation has been critically examined. Whitman situates him as part of the civil-law juristic resistance to the most untrammeled radicalism at the critical 1934 moment — resistance that ultimately failed.
