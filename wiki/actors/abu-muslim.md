## Overview

Abu Muslim was the military and organizational architect of the [[abbasid-revolution-750|Abbasid Revolution]]. A freed slave of the 'Abbasid imam Ibrahim, he was dispatched to Khurasan around 745 CE to convert the 'Abbasid propaganda network into active revolt. His subsequent campaigns defeated the [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]] and installed the [[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid dynasty]]. He was then murdered by the second Abbasid caliph al-Mansur in 754 — one of the revolution's founding betrayals.

## Role and Major Decisions

**Mobilization in Khurasan (c. 745–747)**: Abu Muslim was sent by Ibrahim al-Imam to Khurasan at a moment when the province was already in ferment — a local revolt "in the name of Qur'an and sunnah" had run from 734 to 746. He organized among an existing network of 'Abbasid partisans, many of them Mawali (non-Arab converts) who resented persistent Arab ethnic privilege despite 'Umar II's decree of equality.

**The Black Banners (747)**: When Abu Muslim emerged from secret agitation into open revolt in 747, he raised black banners — the color of mourning. Hodgson notes these "seem to have been felt to symbolize mourning for [Husayn's] deaths — and indirectly, therefore, for all the injustices that either old-line Arabs or insulted Mawali felt they had received at the hands of the ruling Arab factions — personified in the Umayyad house" ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v1-1974]]). He explicitly named no caliphal candidate, canvassing support "in the name of the house of Muhammad generally" — a maximally inclusive strategy.

**Military Campaign (747–750)**: Abu Muslim "proved a talented statesman, able to mobilize excellent generals." His Khurasani forces moved west through Iran with minimal resistance. At the Euphrates they defeated a major Marwani army; the 'Abbasids were then proclaimed at Kufah (749–750). Marwan II's final defeat near the Tigris (750) and death in Egypt followed.

**Death**: Abu Muslim was on personally bad terms with al-Mansur after the latter became caliph (754). "He distrusted the new caliph but was lured by promises of reconciliation into the caliph's presence alone without his devoted soldiers, and was murdered." Al-Mansur's timing and method — ensuring Abu Muslim's troops had no warning — demonstrated the thoroughness of the 'Abbasid consolidation of power. Abu Muslim's death was one of several parallel eliminations (Abu Salamah, later Muhammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyyah) through which al-Mansur neutralized all independent power centers.

## Counterfactual Significance

Abu Muslim was not himself a claimant to the caliphate, and his own motivations remain unclear from the sources. But his strategic decisions — particularly the non-disclosure of the designated caliph's identity until victory was assured — were essential to building the broad coalition that defeated the Marwanis. Had he named an 'Alid candidate, he would have alienated the non-Shi'i components of the movement; had he named an 'Abbasid immediately, he might have triggered the kind of sectarian defection that doomed earlier Shi'i revolts. The ambiguity was architecturally essential.

His murder by al-Mansur later generated a significant cult of martyrdom in Khurasan. The *Rawandiyya* — a religious movement that attached divine or semi-divine status to the 'Abbasid caliphs — also paradoxically included veneration of Abu Muslim. Several later Khurasani revolts in the ninth century claimed to continue his cause.
