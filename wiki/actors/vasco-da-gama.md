# Vasco da Gama

Portuguese navigator and commander whose 1497–99 voyage around the Cape of Good Hope opened the sea route from Europe to India, fundamentally altering the history of the Indian Ocean.

## The 1497–1499 Voyage

Da Gama commanded a small fleet of four ships (São Gabriel, São Rafael, Berrio, and a storeship) that left Lisbon in July 1497. After a long Atlantic arc to use the prevailing winds, the expedition rounded the Cape (November 1497), made landfalls on the East African coast (including Mozambique and Mombasa), and reached Calicut (Kozhikode) on the Malabar coast of India in May 1498 with the aid of a Gujarati pilot (often identified with Ibn Majid in tradition).

The voyage was driven by the desire to bypass Muslim-controlled overland spice routes, to find Christian allies (the legendary Prester John), and to establish direct trade. Initial contacts in Calicut were tense; Arab merchants already resident there viewed the Portuguese as rivals. Da Gama returned to Portugal in 1499 with a cargo of spices, proving the route feasible despite heavy losses.

## Later Career and Impact

On his second voyage (1502) da Gama used overwhelming force, bombarding Calicut and establishing a more aggressive Portuguese posture. He helped lay the foundations for the *Estado da Índia* under later commanders such as Afonso de Albuquerque. In 1524 he was sent out again as Viceroy but died shortly after arrival in Cochin.

Da Gama’s achievement is treated in Hall as the opening shot of the “cannons of Christendom” that disrupted the ancient monsoon trading world. His use of naval artillery and willingness to resort to violence marked a qualitative change from earlier peaceful (if competitive) trading diasporas.

## Significance

The voyage ended centuries of effective Muslim/Asian control over the maritime routes between Europe and Asia and initiated the European colonial presence in the Indian Ocean. It is one of the pivotal events separating the Late Middle Ages from the Early Modern period in global history.
