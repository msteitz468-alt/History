# Henry III, King of England (r. 1216–1272)

**Henry III**, son of **[[john-of-england|King John]]**, came to the throne aged nine in
the most desperate situation of any English king, yet reigned fifty-six years — the
longest medieval English reign. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (D.A. Carpenter, Ch. 12)
presents him as a *rex pacificus*: pious, chaste, trusting and "rather lazy," "the very
opposite of his father," whose reign saw the rooting of **[[magna-carta-1215|Magna
Carta]]** and the birth of **[[english-parliament|parliament]]** — but ended in revolution
and civil war.

## The Minority and Magna Carta (1216–1227)

The regents — first **William Marshal**, then the justiciar **Hubert de Burgh**, aided by
the papal legates Guala and Pandulf — saved the throne (Marshal's victory at **Lincoln**,
1217; the naval victory off Sandwich) and, crucially, **reissued Magna Carta** (1216, 1217,
and definitively **1225**) in the king's name. Gascony was preserved; Poitou was lost to
[[louis-ix-capetian|Louis VIII]] in 1224. The Charter's reissue made the principle that the
king was bound by law a permanent feature of English politics.

## Personal Rule (1234–1258)

After Hubert de Burgh and Peter des Roches left the stage in 1234, Henry's personal rule
brought a long domestic peace, little war, and (the Charter having limited his revenues)
limited fiscal pressure on the laity — a peace that let the economy recover. Henry based it
on **appeasing the great magnates**, but at the cost of injustice to local society: minor
professional sheriffs, seigneurial officials with "virtual immunity," a remote central
government after he abolished the justiciarship and great chancellorship. His model of
kingship was not John but **Edward the Confessor**, whose abbey at **Westminster** he
rebuilt from 1245 as a shrine, his greatest achievement.

Henry never recovered the lost continental empire (his expeditions of 1230 and 1242–1243
failed, the latter when he fled at Taillebourg), and under the **Treaty of Paris (1259)**
he surrendered his claims to Normandy, Anjou and Poitou, holding **Gascony** as a fief of
the French crown — a settlement that kept peace with [[louis-ix-capetian|Louis IX]] for
thirty-five years.

## The Revolution of 1258 and Civil War

Henry's rule was undone by **factional struggle at court** (between his Savoyard in-laws,
led by Queen **Eleanor of Provence**, and his Lusignan half-brothers), the alienation of
the Church, and above all the disastrous **Sicilian affair** — his acceptance (1254) of the
papal offer of the Sicilian throne for his son Edmund, which committed him to vast papal
debts and demonstrated the "imbecillic state" of the kingdom. At the **Oxford parliament**
(June 1258) the magnates, led by **[[simon-de-montfort|Simon de Montfort]]**, vested control
of government in a **council of fifteen** (the **Provisions of Oxford**), reducing Henry to
a cipher and addressing local grievances (the **Provisions of Westminster**, 1259). Henry
overthrew the Provisions in 1261; civil war followed. Defeated and captured at **Lewes**
(May 1264), he was restored only when his son **Edward** killed Montfort at **Evesham**
(August 1265). There was no attempt to depose Henry, as there had been with John — "he might
be an incompetent politician but he was a decent, pious man."

## Significance

Henry's reign embedded two of the great structures of the English state: the **authority of
Magna Carta** and the **practice of parliamentary consent to taxation** — the latter because
Henry, dependent on extraordinary taxes, came "again and again to parliament to beg taxation"
and was refused unless he conceded reform. His weakness was thus generative: it created the
constitutional leverage that his son [[edward-i-plantagenet|Edward I]] would have to manage.
