## Life and Role

Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf was the Almohad Sultan of al-Andalus and the Maghrib during the second half of the twelfth century, and the primary court patron of Ibn Ṭufayl. He died in 1184 of wounds received at the siege of Santarém in Portugal. His son Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb succeeded him and continued his father's patronage of the elderly Ibn Ṭufayl.

## Intellectual Character

The historian ʿAbdu-l-Wāḥid of Marrakesh records that Abū Yaʿqūb "continually gathered books from all corners of Spain and North Africa and sought out knowledgeable men, especially thinkers, until he had gathered more than any previous king in the west." He is described as deeply learned in philosophy, capable of discussing Aristotle and Plato with expert knowledge: when [[ibn-rushd]] first appeared at court — introduced by [[ibn-tufayl]] — the Sultan's first question concerned whether the heavens were eternal or created, and the historian records that "he was more learned than I would have expected a full time specialist to be." The Sultan's philosophical fluency put the initially intimidated Ibn Rushd at ease.

## Relationship with Ibn Ṭufayl

Abū Yaʿqūb retained Ibn Ṭufayl as minister and chief physician for several decades. According to Marrākushī, the Sultan "loved him so well that he stayed with him in the palace, night and day, not coming out for days at a time." Ibn Ṭufayl performed both medical and cultural advisory roles, serving as an informal minister of culture: "he made it his practice to gather scholars from all over the world and saw to it that they obtained the interest and favor of the ruler."

## Commissioning of the Aristotle Commentaries

Abū Yaʿqūb's most consequential act in the history of philosophy was commissioning [[ibn-rushd]] (Averroes) to write systematic commentaries on the works of Aristotle — a commission that would shape the entire scholastic tradition in Latin Europe. The commission came through [[ibn-tufayl]], who relayed the Sultan's complaint that "Aristotle's language — or perhaps that of his translators — and the resultant difficulty in understanding his ideas" required a good interpreter. Ibn Tufayl passed the commission to the younger Ibn Rushd, citing his own age and other obligations — "the fact that I must devote myself to something that seems to me to be more important" (a reference to [[ibn-tufayl-hayy-ibn-yaqzan]] itself, or the *al-ḥikmat al-mashriqiyya* tradition more broadly).

## The Public Façade and the Private Garden

Goodman's introduction to [[ibn-tufayl-hayy-ibn-yaqzan]] draws a careful distinction between the official Almohad religious posture — the dynasty's power rested on "rigid-line religion" — and the intellectual atmosphere of the private court. "In his private garden, with his friend the philosopher, Abū Yaʿqūb was apparently quite an independent thinker." This dual register — public orthodoxy, private philosophical inquiry — shaped the conditions under which Ibn Ṭufayl could write a text with heterodox implications about mass religion while remaining at court. The "conservative Maghrib" was "an image on the official façade, rather than the reality of the inner chamber."

## Death and Succession

Abū Yaʿqūb Yūsuf died in 1184 at the siege of Santarém. His son Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb succeeded him, continued the patronage of Ibn Ṭufayl, and was present at Ibn Ṭufayl's death in 1185, when the Sultan himself officiated at the funeral.

## Significance

Abū Yaʿqūb represents a relatively rare type in medieval Islamic history: the sovereign as active philosophical interlocutor rather than merely a patron. His reign created the conditions for the last great flowering of Andalusian philosophy — the circle that produced both the [[ibn-tufayl-hayy-ibn-yaqzan]] and the Aristotle commentaries of [[ibn-rushd]] — before the intensification of Almohad orthodoxy under subsequent rulers.
