# The Vandal Kingdom of Africa

The barbarian successor state that ruled **Roman North Africa** — the richest grain- and oil-producing
provinces of the western empire — from 439 to 534. Built by **Geiseric**, the only successor king to wield
**sea-power** across the western Mediterranean, the Vandal kingdom both crippled the western empire (by
seizing Africa's revenues) and outlasted it, until [[justinian|Justinian's]] general
[[belisarius|Belisarius]] destroyed it with startling ease. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]]
Ch. 5 (**Ian Wood**).

## Origins and conquest

The Vandals were one of four peoples (with the Alans and the Sueves) who crossed the **Rhine in 406** and
entered **Spain in 409**. Shattered by the **Visigoths**, the survivors merged under the Hasding king
**Gunderic**; his half-brother **Geiseric (428–477)** led them across the straits into **Africa in 429** —
perhaps, as Procopius claims, invited by the rebel governor Boniface. They took **Hippo** after a siege
(431, during which **Augustine** died), gained coastal Numidia by treaty (435), then **broke it to seize
Carthage in 439** — the decisive blow. A new treaty (442) confirmed Vandal control of the richest African
provinces (Proconsularis, Byzacena, eastern Numidia).

## Geiseric's thalassocracy

Uniquely among the successor kings, Geiseric built a **fleet** and made the western Mediterranean a Vandal
sea. In **455** — exploiting a betrothal between his son **Huneric** and Valentinian III's daughter
**Eudocia** — he sailed on Rome and subjected it to a **second, thorough sack**, carrying off the imperial
women and (Procopius says) the Temple treasures Titus had taken from Jerusalem. The islands followed:
Sardinia, Corsica, the Balearics, and raids on Sicily. The **elimination of the Vandal kingdom became a
primary aim of imperial policy**, but both great reconquest expeditions failed catastrophically — the
western fleet captured near Cartagena (460), and **Leo I's vast eastern armada destroyed by Vandal fire-ships
off Africa (468)**, a disaster that contributed significantly to the [[fall-of-the-western-roman-empire-476|western
collapse]]. Geiseric died in 477 master of the western seas, at peace with both empires.

## Arianism and the African church

The Vandals were **Arian**, and their relations with the Catholic Roman provincials were "unusually savage."
Leading families were exiled and their lands expropriated; Catholic bishops and priests were exiled and
their churches handed to Arian clergy. The suffering was luridly recorded in **Victor of Vita's** *History
of the Persecution of the African Province* (c. 484). Persecution intensified under **Huneric (477–484)**,
eased under **Gunthamund**, resumed under **Thrasamund** (who exiled bishops to Sardinia but allowed
theological debate), and was finally ended by the Romanophile **Hilderic (523–530)** — a grandson of
Valentinian III through his mother Eudocia. Wood reads the persecution as symptomatic of **Vandal unease**:
there was probably no significant settlement of the small Germanic population on the land, and the kingdom
suffered mounting defeats by the **Berbers (Moors)** along the old desert *limes*.

## Destruction

Hilderic's rapprochement with Constantinople (reversing the earlier Vandal–Ostrogothic alliance sealed by
Thrasamund's marriage to **[[theoderic-the-great|Theoderic's]]** sister Amalafrida) led to his overthrow by
his nephew **Gelimer (Gailamir) in 530**. This was the *casus belli* for **Justinian's** expedition of
**533** (the **Vandalic War**), ostensibly to restore Hilderic. With the Vandal fleet diverted to a Sardinian revolt,
**[[belisarius|Belisarius]]** landed unopposed, defeated Gelimer at **Ad Decimum**, took **Carthage**, and
crushed the regrouped Vandals at **Tricamarum**. Gelimer surrendered in 534 and was paraded in Belisarius's
triumph at Constantinople (the recovered Temple treasures with him); the remaining Vandals were drafted into
the imperial army and **vanished as a distinct people**.

## Significance

The Vandal kingdom is the clearest case of how the loss of a single rich region — **Africa, the western
empire's fiscal heart** — could prove fatal to Rome, and of how a successor kingdom built on a small armed
élite over a hostile provincial population was brittle: it shattered at a single campaign. Its swift fall
gave Justinian the confidence and the springboard for the far harder Italian reconquest, and made **Byzantine
Africa** a province of the eastern empire until the Arab conquest of the late seventh century.
