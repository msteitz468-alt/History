# Ōkubo Toshimichi (1830–1878)

**Ōkubo Toshimichi** was the dominant statesman of early Meiji Japan — the man who, more than any other, shaped the direction of the new government between 1873 and his assassination in 1878. A native of Satsuma and a key architect of the Meiji Restoration, he survived the political crises of the 1870s to become, in Keene's description, "undoubtedly the most powerful man in the government, responsible only to the emperor" — and the single most hated man among samurai dissidents.

## Career and Role

Ōkubo rose from the lower ranks of the Satsuma samurai to become one of the three great figures of the Restoration (alongside **[[saigo-takamori|Saigō Takamori]]** and **Kido Takayoshi**). He was a member of the **[[iwakura-mission-1871|Iwakura Mission]]** to Europe and America (1871–73), an experience that transformed his political vision. Unlike Saigō, who had stayed in Japan managing the government, Ōkubo returned convinced that Japan's military and economic gap with the West was vast and that the priority must be internal development, not overseas adventure.

This conviction drove his opposition to the **[[seikanron-1873|Korea invasion proposal of 1873]]**. Working with Iwakura Tomomi, he secured the emperor's backing against Saigō and effectively forced the latter — along with Itagaki Taisuke and Etō Shimpei — out of the government. The personal cost was immense: Saigō had been his closest political ally since youth, and Satsuma samurai across the country blamed Ōkubo for destroying their hero.

After 1873, Ōkubo systematically consolidated his position. As interior minister he controlled the police, oversaw industrial promotion, and managed the suppression of samurai unrest. He traveled personally to Saga to oversee the suppression of the **[[saga-rebellion-1874|Saga Rebellion]]**, and to Beijing to negotiate the settlement of the **[[taiwan-expedition-1874|Taiwan expedition]]**. During the **[[satsuma-rebellion-1877|Satsuma Rebellion]]**, he remained the political center of the government's response while generals Yamagata Aritomo and Kuroda Kiyotaka commanded in the field.

## Vision and Methods

Ōkubo conceived of the Meiji transformation in phases. On the morning of his assassination, he told the governor of Fukushima: "it would take three periods of ten years each to achieve the work of the Restoration. Japan was about to enter the second period, which Ōkubo believed to be of critical importance, when the country would be strengthened internally and the productivity of the people enhanced. He considered that whatever his faults might be, he was the best person to guide Japan during this period. The third period would be in the hands of the next generation" (Keene, [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

This technocratic, developmental vision drove his agricultural and industrial policies. He employed foreign advisers, promoted sheep-raising and blanket manufacturing to reduce import dependency, and proposed land tax reductions to relieve peasant unrest — a proposal the emperor implemented (at a slightly higher rate) in January 1877. He was ahead of his time in understanding that internal economic development and external military restraint were complementary rather than opposed.

His methods were **often authoritarian**. He was accused of ruling through a small clique, of suppressing popular rights, of using government funds for ostentation, and of deploying the police as an instrument of political control. Many of these accusations were unfair, but his management of Etō's trial — where legal procedure was bent in the service of political deterrence — gave credence to critics who saw him as placing his own judgment above the law he claimed to be building.

## Character and Relations

Keene's portrait emphasizes Ōkubo's unpopularity alongside his effectiveness: "the most gifted, though the least popular" of the emperor's principal advisers. He lacked Saigō's charisma and popular following. Unlike **[[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]]** or **Kido Takayoshi**, he was not given to self-revelation in letters or poetry. His strength was executive: "his methods were often high-handed, and he had aroused the hatred of adherents of both the right (who blamed him for the rejection of the invasion of Korea and the defeat of Saigō Takamori) and the left (who believed that his conservatism had blocked the advance of people's rights)" (Keene, [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

He also displayed genuine public-spiritedness. When Emperor Meiji asked him about building a detached palace as a health measure, Ōkubo received the same reply as Iwakura: the emperor would not move while his people suffered from beriberi, and he commended the idea of a hospital instead. Ōkubo, "struck dumb with admiration, withdrew."

His personal life is largely invisible in the sources — he was not a man who expressed himself through poetry or personal correspondence in the way Kido did.

## Assassination

The assassination of Ōkubo Toshimichi on **May 14, 1878** — carried out by six samurai from Kanazawa (Ishikawa Prefecture), led by **Shimada Ichirō** — was one of the pivotal moments of the Meiji era. The assassins attacked his carriage on a narrow street in Tokyo, killing his coachman and dragging Ōkubo from the vehicle; "the coup de grâce was delivered to Ōkubo's throat with such savagery that the point of the sword penetrated the earth below" (Keene, [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

The assassins submitted a detailed manifesto charging five offenses: autocratic control of government, arbitrary lawmaking, depletion of public funds, neglect of loyal samurai, and loss of national prestige in dealings with foreign powers. They then walked to the nearest police station and turned themselves in. The manifesto was partly authored by Kuga Yoshinao, a populist-rights activist, producing the strange hybrid document of samurai grievances and democratic demands that characterized the political violence of the period.

Ōkubo lacked his usual pistol that day — he had left it behind because his carriage was being cleaned for a dinner reception at the Chinese legation that evening.

The emperor, informed while receiving a lecture from Motoda Nagazane, "was severely shaken by the news." He posthumously promoted Ōkubo to minister of the right and appointed **[[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]]** to succeed him as interior minister, ensuring continuity. The assassination was "deplored even in foreign newspapers"; Ōkubo's funeral was the **first state funeral in Japan**.

## Significance

Ōkubo's death left a void that no single successor filled. The **[[meiji-oligarchs|Meiji oligarchy]]** continued under Itō, Yamagata, Matsukata, and others, but the domineering first-among-equals role Ōkubo had occupied was never quite replicated. The shock of the assassination — combined with the remonstrances of the jiho and the lessons of the Satsuma Rebellion — appears to have pushed the emperor toward more personal engagement with governance, a development Keene traces through the last quarter of 1878.

Ōkubo's developmental program — industrial promotion, agricultural improvement, fiscal consolidation, managed foreign relations — became the template for Meiji governance for the remainder of the century.

## Counterfactual Significance

What changes without Ōkubo? The Korea invasion of 1873 likely proceeds, probably triggering Chinese involvement and a war Japan was not ready to fight. The Satsuma Rebellion, if it occurred, is suppressed more slowly without his administrative effectiveness as the political center of the government response. Japanese industrial policy in the crucial 1873–78 period loses its most coherent strategist. The probability of a more military-adventurist early Meiji state, with the accompanying risks, rises significantly.

## Historiography

Ōkubo has been treated harshly by early nationalist historiography, which romanticized Saigō, and more sympathetically by later developmental historians who saw in his policies the foundation of Japan's economic modernization. The tension between his authoritarian methods and his genuine developmental accomplishments is the central interpretive problem his career poses.
