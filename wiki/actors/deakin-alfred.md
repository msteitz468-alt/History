# Alfred Deakin

## Overview

Alfred Deakin was the dominant intellectual force of the early Commonwealth of Australia, serving as the first Commonwealth attorney-general and three times as prime minister (1903–04, 1905–08, 1909–10). He was the supreme exemplar of what he himself called the "independent Australian Briton" — committed simultaneously to Australian national development and British imperial loyalty. His chief legislative achievements were the New Protection tariff system (1908), the compulsory military training scheme, and the building of an Australian navy; his chief political legacy was forcing a two-party system into being through the fusion of the Free Trade and Protectionist parties in 1909.

## Rise and Path to Power

Deakin entered federal politics from Victoria, where he was a committed protectionist and advanced liberal. He had led the federal movement as a delegate to the Constitutional Convention of 1897–98, and served as the most accomplished orator and operator in Edmund Barton's first Commonwealth ministry. The source describes him as "a much more radical liberal and a more committed protectionist" than Barton, who succeeded to the prime ministership in 1903 when Barton retired to the High Court bench.

At the first Commonwealth parliament Deakin used "all the rhetorical powers of Alfred Deakin, the first Commonwealth attorney-general, and his threat of resignation before the parliament agreed in 1903 to the creation of the High Court" — the founding judges of which he ensured would be of high calibre.

## Major Phases of Career

**Attorney-General and Architect of the Settlement (1901–03).** Deakin successfully urged Barton to include a commitment to white Australia in the election platform to secure working-class and Labor support. He gave the Immigration Restriction Act its exalted public rationale — that "the desire to be 'one people and remain one people without the admixture of other races' had been the most powerful force in the making of the Federation" — though the source notes this claim was "quite misleading."

**First and Second Prime Ministerships (1903–04, 1905–08).** Deakin's great achievements were to introduce the arbitration bill in 1904 and to manoeuvre the Protectionist alliance to extract "new protection" — tariff protection conditional on manufacturers paying fair wages. The 1907 Harvester judgment, issued under his government by Justice H.B. Higgins, established that fair and reasonable wages must meet "the normal needs of an average employee regarded as a human being in a civilised country" and be sufficient to maintain a man, wife, and three children. The first fully protective tariff was carried in 1908. Deakin also in this period: (1) negotiated for an Australian naval flotilla against Admiralty resistance; (2) invited the US "Great White Fleet" to visit Australia in August 1908 in a gesture of independence from the Colonial Office; (3) proposed compulsory military training for home defence. He introduced the old age and invalid pension in 1908, paid from general revenue.

**The Fusion and Third Prime Ministership (1909–10).** Under pressure from Labor's relentless advance, Deakin amalgamated his Protectionists with Reid's Free Traders/Anti-Socialists into a new Liberal Party — a "fusion" he had long tried to avoid. He led the new grouping into parliament and governed for ten months before the 1910 election, which Labor won with a majority in both houses.

## Methods and Style

Deakin was "a much more radical liberal" than his Protectionist colleagues, combining economic nationalism with social liberalism. He "took pleasure in being unfathomable and springing surprises." His distance from day-to-day political management paradoxically made him effective. He was always "contemplating leaving politics for he thought his true mission lay elsewhere as contemplative scholar or preacher." He wanted an army without "meretricious display, or glitter of gold lace, or glamour of a separate caste" — an army in accord with "the principles of Australian social life."

He described Australians as "independent Australian Britons": his battles with the Colonial Office were "not because he was anti-British; he wanted Britain to take a more generous and inclusive approach to empire."

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Deakin: the High Court might not have been created in 1903; there would have been no New Protection formula linking tariffs to living wages; the Harvester wage standard — the foundation of Australia's wage-earners' welfare state — might not have emerged; the Australian navy and compulsory military training might not have been ready by 1914. The "lasting legacy of Deakin's outsmarting of George Reid" was the nexus of high protection and state-determined wages "not broken until the 1980s."

## Historiography

Deakin is treated in this source as the central intellectual architect of the "Australian Settlement" — the cluster of policies (tariff protection, arbitration, white Australia, imperial defence) identified by Paul Kelly as the defining framework of Australian politics to the 1980s. The source describes him as "the supreme exemplar of the mixed loyalties of Australians." Judith Brett's study emphasises his role in establishing a distinctive liberal tradition; the source engages with revisionist debates about whether the fusion was inevitable or whether preferential voting might have preserved three-party politics.
