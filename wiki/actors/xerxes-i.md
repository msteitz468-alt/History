# Xerxes I

**Xerxes I** (r. 486–465 BCE), son of [[achaemenid-empire|Darius I]] and Atossa daughter of
[[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]], was the Great King who led the **vast second invasion
of Greece** in 480 — the campaign that climaxed at Thermopylae, the sack of Athens, and the
sea-battle of **[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]**. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]]
(Chs. 10–11, Hammond & Barron) treats the expedition; the dynastic chapters that would cover
his accession are missing from the available scan (see [[achaemenid-empire]]).

## Accession and the road to war

Xerxes inherited Darius' unfinished business. In 485 he **crushed the revolt in Egypt** and
imposed a harsh, impiety-tinged regime; he also put down Babylonian risings. The preparation
of the Greek expedition was a feat of imperial logistics on a scale Hammond stresses as
unprecedented in Europe: the **canal cut through the Athos peninsula** (to spare the fleet
the cape that had wrecked Mardonius' ships in 492), permanent **road-bridges** over the
Strymon and other rivers, dumps of supplies along the Thracian coast, and above all the
**double pontoon-and-suspension bridge across the Hellespont** (the first pair destroyed by
a gale, for which Herodotus has Xerxes "lash the sea" — the type-image of oriental *hubris*).

## The invasion of 480

Mustering at Sardis and crossing in spring 480 with an army Greek contemporaries
(Simonides, Aeschylus) reckoned "huge" — Herodotus' grand total of over five million Hammond
treats as "absurd exaggeration," estimating perhaps **~220,000 fighting men on land and a
fleet of ~1,200 warships**. The army's pace was set by the need to commandeer ripening
harvests. The campaign succeeded brilliantly at first: the **forcing of Thermopylae** (where
Leonidas and his rearguard died), the parallel sea-fighting at **Artemisium**, and the
**sack and burning of Athens** (the Acropolis stormed) — the avowed first objective of his
"crusade," framed as revenge for the Greek sack of Troy.

## Salamis and after

The disaster came at **[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]**: lured by **Themistocles'**
deception into the narrows, the Persian fleet was wrecked in confined water under Xerxes'
own eyes from his throne on Mt Aegaleos. He did not flee at once (Hammond corrects the
Herodotean melodrama): he tried to **build a mole** to Salamis and probed the Isthmus, but
with the season closing and the Greek fleet intact threatening the Hellespont bridges, he
**withdrew to Asia**, leaving **Mardonius** with a picked army that was destroyed at
**[[battle-of-plataea-479bce|Plataea]]** (479). Xerxes reigned twenty more years before his
**assassination** in 465.

## Causal significance — counterfactual

- His invasion was the **structural culmination** of Achaemenid expansion: as Murray argued,
  an empire held together by mobilizing its subjects for war had to push into the
  Mediterranean once it held the Ionian and Phoenician fleets — "and must ultimately lead to
  disaster."
- His defeat at Salamis and Plataea **fixed the limit of Persian power in Europe** and
  guaranteed the survival of the independent Greek polis — the precondition for Classical
  Athens, the Delian League, and the whole subsequent course of European history.
- The **scale and failure** of his expedition made him, for the Greek tradition (Aeschylus'
  *Persae*), the archetype of the despot punished for *hubris*.

## Historiography

Known overwhelmingly from hostile Greek sources — **[[herodotus-reliability|Herodotus]]**
(Books VII–IX), **Aeschylus' Persae** (a participant's drama, 472), and Simonides — checked
against the **Persepolis tablets**, the Athos canal and Hellespont engineering, and the
Decree of Themistocles. The numbers, the king's psychology, and the melodrama of his flight
are the chief points of debate.
