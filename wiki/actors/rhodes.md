## Overview

**Rhodes** was the great merchant-republic of the [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]]
Aegean — "a distinctly Greek *polis*" amid the monarchies, the dominant trading and naval
power of the eastern Mediterranean in the late third century. CAH VII.1 (Heinen, ch. 11;
Will, ch. 2) treats both its heroic survival of the [[siege-of-rhodes-305|siege of 305]]
and its third-century commercial ascent.

## A Free Polis and Trading Power

Rhodes "emerged strengthened from the era of the Successors," having repulsed
[[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius Poliorcetes']] year-long **[[siege-of-rhodes-305|siege
(305–304)]]** — commemorated by the **Colossus of Rhodes** — and thereafter pursued a
policy of **neutrality** in the interest of its trade. Its power rested on a superb
geographic position, a powerful fleet, far-ranging trade relations, and a wealthy merchant
class. The state was led by a **financial aristocracy** that practised a "markedly
paternalistic welfare policy," sharing some of the commercial rewards with the lower
classes — a notably stable social order.

Rhodes was the chief **clearing-house of eastern Mediterranean trade** and maintained a
"special relationship" (without formal treaty) with **[[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic Egypt]]**,
its main source of grain after south Russia and the indispensable partner for access to
Ptolemaic ports. When a devastating **earthquake** struck in 227/6, rulers across the
Hellenistic world (Ptolemy III among them) showered it with aid — a measure of its
importance.

## The Rhodian Thalassocracy

As Ptolemaic naval power in the Aegean declined after mid-century, Rhodes rose to fill the
vacuum. Its leadership of the **toll-war against Byzantium (220)** — fought to keep the
Bosphorus open to trade when Byzantium imposed dues — and its assumption of the burden of
**suppressing piracy** marked the emergence of a genuine **Rhodian thalassocracy** by the
end of the third century. Tellingly, "**none of the large Hellenistic powers**" intervened
over so important a matter as the freedom of the Straits — the task fell to Rhodes.

## Significance

Rhodes is the great Hellenistic example of an **independent commercial republic** thriving
between the kingdoms — the counterpart in the islands to [[pergamon|Pergamum]] on the
mainland. By 200 the two were "the most important political factors" of the Aegean and Asia
Minor, "destined to play a leading role in the conflict between Rome and the Hellenistic
monarchies." Rhodes' role as anti-piracy maritime arbiter and its commercial law influenced
the later Mediterranean. See [[siege-of-rhodes-305]], [[ptolemaic-egypt]], [[pergamon]].

## Roman ally, then Roman victim (CAH VIII, Chs 8–9)

[[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] traces Rhodes' rise and fall under Rome. Its alarm at
Philip's Aegean aggression helped trigger the
[[second-macedonian-war-200-196bce|Second Macedonian War]]; its fleet was decisive
against [[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III]], and at the
**[[peace-of-apamea-188bce|Peace of Apamea]]** (188) it received **Lycia and Caria
south of the Maeander**. But its commercial strength and its attempt to **mediate**
in the [[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|Third Macedonian War]] (encouraged, then
turned against it, by Q. Marcius Philippus) brought Roman wrath after Pydna: Rome
nearly declared war, stripped Rhodes of its mainland territories, and made **Delos a
free port** (167) — diverting Aegean trade and crippling Rhodian revenues. The episode
shows the fate of even a loyal friend once it became too strong or too independent.
See [[peace-of-apamea-188bce]], [[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce]],
[[nature-of-roman-imperialism]].
