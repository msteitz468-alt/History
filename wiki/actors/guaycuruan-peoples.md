## Overview

The Guaycuruan-speaking peoples dominated the Gran Chaco from the sixteenth through the early nineteenth century. They comprised the Abipón, Mocoví, Toba, Pilagá, Mbaya (Mbayá), and the riverine Payaguá, speaking related Guaycuruan languages. Sources typically characterize them as a "seignorial society" comprised of "noble, warrior, and captive castes," the last referring to prisoners captured in war — a social stratification more complex than that usually found among other lowland South American indigenous societies.

Their geographic base was the flat alluvial plain of the Chaco north of the Río Salado, between the Río Paraná–Paraguay system to the east and the Andean foothills to the west. The Payaguá, a canoe-borne Guaycuruan variant, dominated the Río Paraguay from the Bermejo north to the Guasarapos.

## Role and Key Events

The adoption of the horse after 1600 transformed the Guaycuruans from pedestrian foragers and raiders into the dominant military force of the southern Chaco. Horses "extended their military reach, made their escape following a raid more secure, and broadened the area through which they could hunt." The Guaycuruans adapted European technologies — copying Spanish saddles, bridles, ropes, and hobbles from local materials; adopting iron arrow and lance points — while retaining bow, bone, and wood weapons.

At their peak power in the 1720s–1730s, the Toba, Mocoví, and Abipón threatened Santa Fe, Salta, and Córdoba. The Abipón and Mocoví "halted the commerce of Santa Fe in 1732"; the Mocoví laid siege to Salta in January 1735 "killing 100 people, capturing 200 more, and disrupting the city's public life for the next year." The Payaguá "won stunning victories in 1725, 1730, and 1739, when they annihilated large expeditions heading for the [Cuiabá] mines." These were the last great military triumphs of the southern Guaycuruans.

Several factors eroded Guaycuruan strength after 1735: loss of women to Spanish raiders, serious epidemics, Spanish ranches protecting livestock, ecological degradation of the Chaco environment from iron-tool overexploitation, and intensifying resource competition. By the 1740s Mocoví and Abipón bands accepted Jesuit and civil offers of missions, moving into San Javier and San Jerónimo reductions.

## Counterfactual Significance

Guaycuruan resistance delayed effective Spanish occupation of the Chaco by at least two centuries and forced colonial authorities to invest heavily in frontier defense, shaping the demographic and political geography of the Río de la Plata. Without Guaycuruan equestrian power, the Jesuit Guaraní mission province would have faced Chaco raiding on a larger scale and earlier. The Mbaya's protection-client relationship with the Guana (Chané) demonstrates that Guaycuruan power was not simply destructive but organized regional political hierarchies.

## Historiography

Primary sources include Jesuit accounts: Martin Dobrizhoffer's *An Account of the Abipones* (London, 1822) and Florian Paucke's diary of the Mocoví missions (1749–1767). Branislava Susnik's seven-volume *Los aborígenes del Paraguay* (1978–1985) is the foundational modern synthesis. Saeger's revisionist essays ("Another view of the mission as a frontier institution," 1985) argue that Guaycuruan mission entry was strategically rational rather than capitulation. The Wright/Carneiro da Cunha chapter contextualizes Guaycuruan resistance within broader Brazilian indigenous history, emphasizing the Payaguá-Mbaya alliance as the most effective barrier to Portuguese Mato Grosso mining traffic.
