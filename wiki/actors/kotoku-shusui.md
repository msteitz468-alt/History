## Overview

Kōtoku Shūsui (幸徳秋水, 1871–1911) was a Japanese journalist, translator, socialist, and
ultimately anarchist who became the spiritual figurehead of the [[high-treason-incident-1910]],
the largest political trial of the Meiji era. Executed by hanging on January 24, 1911, his
trajectory — from loyal emperor-praising socialist to convicted regicide conspirator — encodes
in a single life the contradictions of political dissent under the Meiji constitutional order.

## Early Career and Socialism

Born in Tosa Nakamura (Shikoku) in 1871, Kōtoku showed early scholarly promise. Family poverty
prevented him from completing his education, and discrimination against his non-samurai status
at school created lasting resentments. He arrived in Tokyo at sixteen, worked as a houseboy,
and came under the influence of Nakae Chōmin (1847–1901), a materialist philosopher and popular-
rights advocate whom Kōtoku described as his "only teacher."

His early political writings show a man who had not abandoned hope in the imperial system.
His 1901 work *Imperialism, the Monster of the Twentieth Century* praised Emperor Meiji in
terms that were effectively royalist: "The emperor of Japan, unlike the young kaiser of Germany,
is not fond of war; he places high value on peace…. He is definitely not one of the so-called
patriots or imperialists." Kōtoku even wrote to rebuke those who criticized the crown prince's
arranged marriage, calling them "insanely impious." He called for "a union of the people and
the imperial house."

He co-founded the Social-Democratic Party (Shakai Minshū-tō) in 1901 — banned by the
government within days of its formation — and from 1898 to 1903 wrote editorials for the
*Yorozu chōhō*, the most progressive major Tokyo newspaper. His 1903 book *The Essence of
Socialism* went through seven printings.

In November 1903 he and Sakai Toshihiko founded the *Heimin shimbun* (Common People's
Newspaper), explicitly anti-war during the Russo-Japanese War period. His "Memorandum to the
Russian Socialist Party" (March 1904) called Russian socialists "comrades" and blamed the
imperialistic greed of both countries for the war; it was praised by *Iskra* as "a document
of the highest historical significance."

## Radicalization

The turning point was a five-month prison term (1905) for publishing a translation of the
*Communist Manifesto*. He read Joseph-Ernest Renan's *Life of Jesus* and Peter Kropotkin's
*Conquest of Bread* in prison and emerged, by his own account, transformed from "a socialist
of the Marxist variety" to "a radical anarchist."

His period in San Francisco (November 1905–June 1906) completed the transformation. There he
encountered Russian anarchist exiles and organized the Social Revolutionary Party in America.
He came "to believe that violence was necessary in order to topple oppressive governments." The
1906 San Francisco earthquake moved him to joy rather than sympathy: "In their path, there are
no gods, no wealth, no authority of any kind."

On returning to Japan, Kōtoku abandoned parliamentary socialism for direct action and drove
a fatal split in the Japanese left between his "hard-line" (Bakuninist) faction and the
"soft-line" (Marxist, parliamentary) socialists led by Katayama Sen. The Red Flag incident of
June 1908 — a street demonstration by Kōtoku's faction — provoked severe reprisals and the
installation of the reactionary Katsura cabinet.

## Arrest, Trial, and Execution

Kōtoku had known of Miyashita Taikichi's bomb plot against the emperor from at least February
1909 but expressed skepticism about its feasibility without joining the operational conspiracy.
Police arrested him on June 1, 1910, as the arrest net spread from the Shinshū Band. At trial,
Kanno Suga testified that only four people were involved in the actual conspiracy; Kōtoku's
guilt rested entirely on his role as intellectual inspirer. Nevertheless, the police "were
determined not to let him escape."

He was sentenced to death and executed on January 24, 1911. Keene's assessment: "at this
distance from the events, one tends to sympathize with the executed men and woman, who were
motivated by ideals, not by a lust for power."

## Role and Historical Significance

Kōtoku's trajectory embodies the structural dilemma of political dissent in the Meiji system:
the same constitutional framework that allowed a limited degree of press freedom and party
politics made any challenge to the imperial institution categorically impermissible. His
intellectual path — from reformist to socialist to pacifist to anarchist — is best understood
as a sequence of positions, each tried and foreclosed by state repression, until only the
final (and most dangerous) position remained.

His early praise for the emperor makes the irony of his execution under Article 73 (the
provision against harm to the imperial family) historically striking. Keene notes that even
his adversary An Chung-gun (the Korean assassin of Itō Hirobumi) had "respected" Emperor Meiji
personally; the target of the assassination plot was not the man but the system.

The suppression following the High Treason Incident silenced Japanese socialism for a decade.
Kōtoku's intellectual legacy — his translations, his anti-imperialist writings, his *Communist
Manifesto* translation — influenced later Japanese left-wing thought despite the attempt to
erase his memory.
