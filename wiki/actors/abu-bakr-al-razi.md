## Overview

Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn Zakariyyāʾ al-Rāzī — known in the Latin West as **Rhazes** — was a physician and philosopher from Rayy in the Persian plateau (his name simply means "the man from Rayy"). His active career spanned the late ninth and early tenth centuries and was conducted primarily in Baghdad and Rayy. He is distinct from the later Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī (twelfth century), the Ashʿarite theologian-philosopher.

Al-Rāzī was one of the two most influential medical authorities of the Arabic tradition — the other being [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]]'s successor, Avicenna — and his works circulated in Latin translation throughout medieval Europe. In philosophy he was a notorious heterodox: his surviving philosophical writings are almost entirely lost, known only through hostile refutations, yet they reveal a thinker of remarkable originality willing to challenge the foundational commitments of Islamic revelation.

## Medical Career

Al-Rāzī worked as a physician at hospitals in Baghdad and Rayy, accumulating extensive clinical experience. His medical output was vast:

- **Ḥāwī (Comprehensive Book)**: an enormous compilation of clinical observations alongside learned ancient medical literature. This encyclopaedia of practical medicine became a standard reference in both Arabic and Latin (as *Continens*) traditions.
- **Introduction to Medicine**: a systematic account closely following Galenic anatomy, methodology, and therapeutics.
- **Differential diagnosis of smallpox and measles**: a pioneering work distinguishing two clinically similar diseases through careful symptom observation.
- **Doubts about Galen (Shukūk ʿalā Jalīnūs)**: a critical work in which al-Rāzī — declaring admiration for Galen — nonetheless systematically enumerates errors he had found in Galen's writings, ranging from philosophical questions about the eternity of the universe to the classification of pulse types. The work embodies his principle (itself derived from Galen) that respect for predecessors must not obstruct independent critical judgment.

## Philosophical System: The Five Eternals

Al-Rāzī's philosophical views survive mainly in refutations written by opponents, notably the Ismāʿīlī theologian Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī (no relation), who recorded their debates in *On the Signs of Prophecy*. This source must be treated with caution as a hostile witness, but it allows reconstruction of al-Rāzī's most distinctive doctrine.

Al-Rāzī posited **five eternal principles** that preceded and enabled the creation of the universe:

1. **God** — the perfectly wise and benevolent active principle.
2. **Soul** — a foolish, ignorant principle that originates the creation event by wrongly desiring to entangle itself with matter.
3. **Matter** — the passive, receptive principle out of which God fashions the world; modeled on the *receptacle* of Plato's *Timaeus*. Matter must be eternal because to create it God would need prior matter, generating an infinite regress.
4. **Absolute Time** — an infinite duration independent of any body or motion, a cosmic metronome. Distinguished from *relative time* (Aristotelian time measured by motion, e.g. the day). Absolute time must be eternal because God's creative act must occur *at* some time, implying time is already present.
5. **Absolute Place** — an infinite void into which God places the universe, described as "this where we are" (al-Rāzī reportedly gestured around him when asked). Distinguished from *relative place* (Aristotelian place defined by the surfaces of surrounding bodies). Eternal for the same regress-avoiding reason as absolute time.

This framework draws heavily on Plato's *Timaeus*, which al-Rāzī knew through Galen's commentary and summary. He understood Plato as positing an actual creation event in time, against the Neoplatonic reading. He atomised matter in a way closer to the Democritean tradition than to the geometric atoms of the *Timaeus*, and posited a void — both departures from Aristotle.

The soul-caused creation explains the imperfection and suffering of the world: God, being perfectly wise, would have no reason to create such a defective universe; the foolish Soul lurched toward matter (al-Rāzī reportedly compared this to an unwanted eruption of flatulence, scandalising his debate audience). God's benevolent intervention imposes form on matter, producing the order we observe amid the chaos. The purpose of human life is the **liberation of soul from matter** through a life of reason that ignores bodily pleasures — an ethical teaching that parallels Neoplatonic asceticism.

## Critique of Prophecy

Al-Rāzī held that God, being just, has bestowed reason (*ʿaql*) equally on all humans. It would therefore be unjust — and counter-productive — for God to single out particular individuals as prophets, withholding divine revelation from the rest of humanity. The result of multiple competing prophets is sectarian strife and war. Trust should be placed in reason, not in prophetic authority. This stance, reported by Abū Ḥātim, made al-Rāzī notorious as "the heretic" (*al-mulḥid*) in later Islamic tradition.

Scholars note that Abū Ḥātim may be misrepresenting al-Rāzī, whose critique may have targeted specifically the Ismāʿīlī doctrine of a divinely guided Imam (a form of uncritical dependence on authority, *taqlīd*) rather than Islamic revelation in general. Al-Rāzī is reported elsewhere to have emphasised harmony between the Koran and his cosmological theory. His anti-authoritarian stance was consistent with the Galenic principle of critical scrutiny — applied to philosophy as rigorously as to medicine.

## Relation to Contemporaries

Al-Rāzī was acquainted with [[mutazilism|Muʿtazilite]] theologians and debated them. His emphasis on God's justice and the universal gift of reason echoes Muʿtazilite concerns, but his five-eternal cosmology — denying God's uniqueness as the sole eternal principle — violated the core Muʿtazilite doctrine of *tawḥīd* (divine oneness). He also debated Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī on the Five Eternals and on prophecy at formal intellectual gatherings (*majlis*).

He seems largely independent of the Christian Aristotelians of [[baghdad-school-aristotelians|the Baghdad school]], though both operated in the same intellectual milieu. Unlike [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] and [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]], who sought accommodation between Greek philosophy and Islamic revelation, al-Rāzī pursued a more radically autonomous rational programme.

## Legacy

Al-Rāzī's medical legacy was enormous and enduring, shaping both Arabic and Latin European medical practice for centuries. His philosophical legacy was almost erased — his cosmological and anti-prophetic writings survive only in the texts of critics. This asymmetry between medical fame and philosophical notoriety is itself historically significant: it reflects the limits of heterodoxy within the Islamic intellectual world even during the relatively tolerant early Abbasid period. His case stands as the clearest instance in the formative period of what happened to a philosopher who pushed rational autonomy to a point that clashed with the foundations of all three Abrahamic traditions.

## Historiography

The chief problem in studying al-Rāzī philosophically is source dependency on hostile witnesses. His main philosophical works on the Five Eternals are lost; Abū Ḥātim's *On the Signs of Prophecy* is the primary surviving account of his views and the debates they provoked. Reconstruction thus resembles the situation with the Pre-Socratics or early Stoics, where hostile doxographers must be read critically. The question of whether al-Rāzī genuinely rejected Islamic prophecy or merely attacked Ismāʿīlī taqlīd remains open.
