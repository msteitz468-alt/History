# Gudea of Lagash

The most celebrated ruler of the **Second Dynasty of [[lagash|Lagash]]** ("Lagash
II"), governor (*ensi*) during or just after the [[gutian-invasion|Gutian]]
twilight, whose reign produced the **supreme masterpieces of Sumerian sculpture**
and the **classic form of the Sumerian language**. As the [[akkadian-empire|Agade
empire]] decayed and the Gutians faded, Lagash — as in the Early Dynastic age, and
again without ever entering the [[sumerian-king-list|king-list]] — rose to a
brilliant local prosperity. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]]
(Ch. XIX, §V, C. J. Gadd).

## A prince of the interlude

Gudea was "no more than one of the local princes" strong enough to hold his own
city but not to dominate his neighbours — yet he ruled with conspicuous
independence: his inscriptions make **no mention of any overlord**, he mounted his
own military foray against **Anshan and Elam** (dedicating the spoils to his city-god
**Ningirsu**), and his trading reach was vast. The political vacuum left by the
collapse of central authority gave such cities room to flourish. CAH places part
of his reign **after** the final expulsion of the Gutians by Utu-khegal, since a
warlord's free trade and foreign raid "would not have been tolerated by an
effective overlord."

## The building of E-ninnu and the trade network

The defining enterprise of his reign was the **rebuilding of Ningirsu's temple,
E-ninnu**, to which nearly all his inscriptions relate. His long account of
gathering its materials is one of the richest windows onto the
[[trade-network-formation|trade]] of the age: cedar and other timbers from east
and west, ornamental stones from Syria, **gold dust from "Armenia,"** copper, and
bitumen from near Kirkuk — drawn by caravan across an "immense area," which Gudea
credits to the god who "opened the road from the upper sea unto the lower."

## The statues and the Sumerian classic

From the ruins of Lagash (Tello) came the famous **diorite statues** of Gudea and
his son **Ur-Ningirsu** — hard-stone seated and standing figures, hands clasped,
representing "the highest achievements of Sumerian sculpture," their finest heads
carved (not inlaid) with delicately modelled features and robes. Equally important,
his lengthy temple-hymn inscriptions (the **Gudea Cylinders**) give Sumerian "in
its most developed form... the Sumerian classic, just as the Code of Hammurabi is
the Akkadian." Literary distinction was a Lagash tradition — the same city produced
the best writing of both the Early Dynastic age and the end of the Gutian
oppression.

## Significance

Gudea embodies the paradox of the post-imperial **interlude**: political
fragmentation coinciding with a cultural and artistic **peak**. His piety-centred
kingship — the *ensi* as humble temple-builder and steward of his god — is the
deliberate Sumerian antithesis of the **deified, universalist** [[naram-sin|Akkadian
monarchy]] that had just collapsed, and it points forward to the
[[mesopotamian-chronology|Sumerian revival under Ur III]].

## Historiography

Gudea is exceptionally well documented for the period precisely because his
inscriptions and statues survive in quantity — but his **chronological placement**
is genuinely difficult: his evident independence is hard to fit between Ur-Baba and
the rise of Ur-Nammu without overlapping the sovereignty of Ur, a problem CAH
flags but cannot resolve. Dates are approximate and follow the
[[mesopotamian-chronology|middle chronology]]. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
