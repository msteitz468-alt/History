# Tokugawa Yoshinobu

The fifteenth and last shogun of the [[tokugawa-shogunate]] (r. 1866–1867), and the central antagonist of the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji Restoration]] — though an antagonist who ultimately chose submission over war. Yoshinobu's voluntary return of governmental authority to the emperor (*taisei hōkan*, November 1867) was the act that made the Restoration possible; his subsequent refusal to accept its full implications, and the resulting [[boshin-war-1868]], was the act that made it necessary to fight one.

## Career

Yoshinobu was a capable administrator who recognized the shogunate's structural weakness and attempted to modernize it under French tutelage. He consulted closely with French minister Léon Roches, reorganized the shogunate's military along Western lines, and pushed through reforms that senior shogunate statesmen like Oguri Tadamasa hoped would transform it into an absolutist regime. He was also, according to contemporary foreign observers, the most impressive Japanese official they had met.

**The Return of Power (November 8, 1867)**: Under pressure from Tosa domain's Yamauchi Yōdō (whose letter was orchestrated by Gotō Shōjirō) and with Satsuma and Chōshū poised for military action, Yoshinobu assembled senior officials of forty domains at Nijō Castle and announced he would request permission to return the government to the court. On November 9 he submitted the memorial; on November 10 the court granted his request. Yoshinobu appears to have calculated that he would remain the dominant figure in any post-shogunate government — either as regent, or as the head of a ruling council in which the Tokugawa family would hold the decisive votes. Foreign observers, notably Sir Ernest Satow, suspected he planned to use an Assembly of Daimyos to ratify his own authority. Keene's account in [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]] treats this as an unresolved historical question: "Some scholars believe that he was aiming at an absolute dictatorship; others, that he sought to establish a government based on a league of the different domains."

**The Boshin War**: When the Restoration proclamation of January 4, 1868 stripped him of rank and land, and his troops were angered by news from Edo, Yoshinobu ordered an advance on Kyōto. Defeated at Toba-Fushimi (January 27–30, 1868), he slipped out of Ōsaka Castle and sailed for Edo, abandoning his army. He said later he "lost all desire to fight" once the imperial brocade pennant appeared.

In Edo, he vacillated: he met three times with Roches (who still backed him), wrote to sympathetic daimyos, and alternated between postures of submission and resistance. On March 4, 1868, he left Edo Castle for seclusion at the Daijiin subtemple of the Kan'ei-ji in Ueno, declaring he would devote himself to "submission and penance." The negotiations between Saigō Takamori and Katsu Kaishū produced the bloodless surrender of Edo Castle (May 4, 1868).

**Survival and Later Life**: Yoshinobu survived the Boshin War, was pardoned, and eventually received a title in the Meiji peerage (1902). He lived until 1913, longer than Emperor Meiji himself.

## Assessment

Yoshinobu remains one of the most ambiguous figures of the Restoration. His voluntary *taisei hōkan* is interpreted either as a statesman's realism (accepting change to preserve what he could) or a political gambit that failed. His retreat from Ōsaka and refusal to fight at Edo were acts of submission that shortened the war enormously and saved tens of thousands of lives — acknowledged even by his opponents. Katsu Kaishū, who negotiated the surrender, respected him. Iwakura, who did not, demanded at every stage that he give up not merely symbolic power but actual rank and land.

Keene's account is notable for allowing Yoshinobu's own perspective to emerge through his meetings with Roches and his letters to Matsudaira Yoshinaga and Yamauchi Yōdō: a man who genuinely did not understand why, having returned the government as requested, he was still being pursued.
