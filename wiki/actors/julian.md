# Julian ("the Apostate")

The last non-Christian Roman emperor (r. 361–363) — nephew of **[[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]**,
cousin of **[[constantius-ii|Constantius II]]**, a Neoplatonist intellectual who, after a brilliant
military apprenticeship in Gaul, seized the throne and attempted a doomed **restoration of polytheism**.
Treated in [[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 2 (**David Hunt**).

## From survivor to scholar

A child of six when his family was murdered in the **massacre of 337**, Julian and his half-brother
Gallus were raised in "glittering servitude" — confined for six years at the Cappadocian estate of
**Macellum**, given a Christian upbringing (ordained a lector), but immersing himself in the Greek
classics under the eunuch Mardonius. Drawn to the **Neoplatonist** schools of Asia Minor, he fell under
the spell of the theurgist **Maximus of Ephesus** and, about age twenty, secretly **abandoned
Christianity** ("began to follow the right path in the company of the gods") — an apostasy kept hidden
for a decade. Initiated at **Eleusis**, he embodied the fusion of philosophy, ritual and the "holy man"
that [[late-polytheism|Fowden]] sees as the heart of late polytheism.

## Caesar in Gaul

After Gallus' execution (354), the childless Constantius — needing a dynastic deputy for the war-torn
West — made Julian **Caesar (355)** and sent him to Gaul. Against expectation the bookish prince proved a
formidable general: recovering Cologne, and above all crushing a far larger Alamannic confederation
under **Chnodomarius at the battle of Strasbourg (357)** — his "finest hour." Over four years he
recovered "almost forty" towns, rebuilt the Rhine forts, slashed the Gallic tax (the *capitatio* from 25
to 7 *solidi*), and won the devotion of the army and his pagan inner circle.

## The proclamation at Paris and sole rule

When Constantius (facing Persia) ordered the transfer of much of the Gallic army east (360), the troops
at **Paris mutinied and proclaimed Julian Augustus** — raised on a shield, crowned with a standard-bearer's
torque. Julian professed reluctance and "the work of the gods," but had surely fostered the moment. As he
marched east into Illyricum for civil war, **Constantius died (November 361)**, making Julian **sole
emperor** without a battle. He then openly declared his paganism, purged the Constantian court, and at
Antioch launched his **religious programme**: reopening temples, restoring sacrifice, recalling exiled
(Christian) bishops to sow division, and — most damagingly — **barring Christians from teaching the
classics**. His austere, sacrifice-heavy zeal alienated even the Antiochenes (whom he lampooned in the
*Misopogon*).

## The Persian disaster and death

Reversing Constantius' cautious strategy, Julian led a **massive invasion of Persia (363)**, reaching
Ctesiphon but failing to take it; forced into a difficult retreat, he was **mortally wounded in a
skirmish** (near Maranga/Samarra) and died — by later Christian legend crying *"Vicisti, Galilaee."* His
successor **Jovian** bought peace by surrendering Nisibis and the trans-Tigritane lands of 298. With
Julian the **last pagan reaction ended**, and his death confirmed the wisdom of Constantius' defensive
Persian policy.

## Significance

Julian's counterfactual weight is large but his project was hollow: a single, learned, energetic ruler
could *not* reverse the [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization]] of the empire, because
(as [[late-polytheism|Fowden]] argues) public polytheism lacked the communal, ethical organization to
compete. His brief reign is the great "what-if" of late antiquity — and a demonstration that the
Christian transformation was structural, not merely the work of Constantine. As soldier and apostate he
remains the most fascinating individual of the fourth century.
