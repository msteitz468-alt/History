## Overview

Wilhelm II (Friedrich Wilhelm Viktor Albrecht von Hohenzollern, 1859–1941) was German Emperor (*Kaiser*) and King of Prussia from 1888 until his abdication in November 1918. He succeeded his father Friedrich III, who reigned only ninety-nine days. Wilhelm was thirty-two years old when he dismissed [[otto-von-bismarck]] in 1890 and took personal direction of German foreign and domestic policy. His reign defined the character of the late [[german-empire]], including its commitment to [[weltpolitik]], its naval arms race with Britain, and its catastrophic entry into World War I.

## Character and Political Style

Smith's chapter on the 1878–1914 [[german-empire]] provides the fullest portrait in this volume. Wilhelm was "a restless monarch. Narcissistic, hating to be contradicted, and in constant need of approval, he was as frenetic and modern as Bismarck seemed steady." He criss-crossed Germany by train, appearing at ceremonies and military displays across medium and large cities, in a calculated effort to cement popular loyalty to his person and to the monarchy as an institution.

His political style reflected the tensions of his era. He hoped to be "a genuinely popular Kaiser and to reach out to the working classes" and had differed with Bismarck over the anti-Socialist laws, which he wanted to allow to lapse. Yet his "constant need of approval" and inability to tolerate opposition made sustained policy coherence impossible. He played to multiple audiences simultaneously — national conservatives, colonial enthusiasts, the military — without synthesizing them.

The structural condition that surrounded him was significant: "The decades before World War I witnessed not the withering away, but the seeming permanence of monarchy, with only the United States and France lacking a regent." The late nineteenth century saw a monarchical revival across Europe that twined nation, populism, and empire. In Germany, this worked imperfectly: radical nationalists increasingly took Bismarck rather than Wilhelm as their emotional symbol.

## Role in German Policy

**Dismissal of Bismarck (1890)**: The latent issue was "the question of who rules, which Bismarck's constitution answered unambiguously in favor of the crown." Wilhelm used the manifest dispute over the anti-Socialist laws to end Bismarck's tenure. His personal rule thereafter lacked Bismarck's strategic coherence.

**Naval Expansion**: Wilhelm expressed "child-like enthusiasm about the building of a fleet." He became patron of the Navy League (founded 1898 on Tirpitz's initiative) and provided enthusiastic support for Alfred von Tirpitz's ambitious naval program — two laws (1898, 1900) authorizing a fleet of thirty-eight battleships plus cruisers to be completed by 1920. The calculation was that Germany's fleet would force Britain to concede colonial territory. Instead, it drove Britain into alliance with France.

**Colonial Appointment**: In the colonial sphere, Wilhelm's appointment of the liberal banker Bernhard Dernburg to head German colonial policy after the scandals of 1906–1907 "indicated a new commitment to a colonial civilizing mission." This reflected his awareness of public opinion and his desire to reframe German colonialism in reformist liberal terms.

**The July Crisis (1914)**: Smith's assessment is nuanced: "In the July Crisis of 1914, both Bethmann-Hollweg and the Kaiser himself attempted frantically to hold back the full consequences of their diplomacy, with Bethmann-Hollweg desperately sending urgent telegrams on the early morning of 30 July, trying to get Vienna to negotiate." The War Council of December 1912, at which Chief of Staff von Moltke insisted "the sooner the better" regarding war, notably excluded the major civilian ministers. By 1912–1914, Wilhelm was "increasingly crowded out by the Kaiser's military advisers."

## Counterfactual Significance

The most significant counterfactual is the dismissal of Bismarck and the shift to personal rule (*persönliches Regiment*) in 1890. Under Bismarck's managed balance, the [[kulturkampf]] had ended through negotiation, the anti-Socialist laws had been maintained, and German foreign policy had operated within a system of alliances (Dreikaiserbund, Reinsurance Treaty with Russia) that prevented encirclement. Wilhelm's abandonment of the Reinsurance Treaty (1890), his naval challenge to Britain, and his inconsistent handling of the Moroccan crises all contributed to the diplomatic isolation that made the July 1914 crisis unmanageable.

The question of whether a different monarch would have produced different outcomes is complicated by the structural features Smith emphasizes: the dynamic between [[weltpolitik]] and democratization, the militarization of foreign policy thinking, and the fiscal imperatives of a military budget that consumed 75–90 percent of federal spending.

## Abdication and Exile

Wilhelm abdicated on 9 November 1918 as Germany collapsed in military defeat and revolution. He fled to the Netherlands, where he lived in exile at Huis Doorn until his death in 1941. He was never extradited or tried.
