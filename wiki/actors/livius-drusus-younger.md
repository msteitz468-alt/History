# Marcus Livius Drusus (the Younger)

## Overview
Marcus Livius Drusus the Younger (c. 124–91 BCE) was the reforming **tribune of
91 BCE** whose ambitious legislative programme — and above all his bill to grant
Roman citizenship to the Italian allies — collapsed in his assassination, the
event that triggered the [[social-war-91-88bce|Social War]]. He was the son of
Marcus Livius Drusus the Elder, the tribune of 122 BCE who had been used by the
Senate to outbid [[gaius-gracchus|Gaius Gracchus]] — a neat irony, since the son
took up the very Italian cause his father had helped frustrate.

## Role and Significance
An optimate-aligned orator initially tasked (by Marcus Aemilius Scaurus and the
senatorial leadership) with restoring the criminal courts to the Senate from the
*equites*, Drusus built a broad coalition behind an interlocking package:
- enlarging the Senate from 300 to 600 by enrolling 300 *equites*;
- a subsidized grain distribution for the urban plebs;
- a Gracchan-style agrarian and colonial law;
- and, finally and fatally, a bill extending **citizenship to the Italian
  allies**, to which he was bound by a personal oath-pact with the Marsic leader
  **Quintus Poppaedius Silo**.

He carried the land, grain, and court laws, but the citizenship bill never reached
passage. The consul **Lucius Marcius Philippus**, backed by the threatened
*publicani*, secured the Senate's annulment of all Drusus's laws after the death
of his chief senatorial defender, the orator L. Licinius Crassus. In the autumn of
91 an unknown assassin stabbed Drusus in his own home; the killers were never
found. His death — "the signal for the outbreak of the revolt" — is the
conventional trigger of the Social War. [[duncan-storm-before-the-storm-2017|Duncan]]
records his reported dying words: "will my country ever have another citizen like
me?"

## Counterfactual Significance
Had Drusus's citizenship bill passed, the Social War might have been averted:
the allies' central demand would have been met peacefully. His murder converted a
legislative defeat into a war, and made Italian enfranchisement — when it finally
came in 90–89 — a concession extracted at the point of the sword rather than a
reform granted from above. His career is a case study in the [[mos-maiorum|erosion
of political norms]]: even a reformer working through the constitutional machinery
could be answered with the knife.

## Historiography
Drusus is known through Appian, Velleius Paterculus (himself of Italian notable
descent and sympathetic), Livy's *Periochae*, and Cicero (who set his dialogue
*De Oratore* at the villa of Drusus's ally Crassus in September 91). The exact
content and sequence of his laws, and the identity of his killers, are debated.
