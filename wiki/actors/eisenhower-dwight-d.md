# Dwight D. Eisenhower (1890–1969)

**Detailed biography:** [[hubs/biographies/world-wars-era/eisenhower-dwight-d|Graduate-level analytic life (formation, decision mechanics, character, legacy)]] 

Supreme Commander Allied Expeditionary Force (SHAEF) for Overlord and the campaign in Northwest Europe. Earlier commander of Torch and Mediterranean theater (AFHQ / NATOUSA). 34th President of the United States (1953–1961).

## Role in Operation Torch (from Dworak and Smith, Eisenhower in War and Peace)

As commander in chief, Eisenhower bore responsibility for the overall plan, logistics support, and the race to seize Tunisia before Axis reinforcement. Torch planning began after his July 25, 1942 decision; he approved the final supply plan October 27 (after initial convoys had departed). The plan aimed for 90-day stock levels in theater and 60-day reserves in UK, later revised to 45-day stocks in North Africa (10 for ammo) and 30-day UK reserve.

Eisenhower (with Mark Clark) made the consequential decision to reduce cargo trucks in assault convoys: "Half of the cargo trucks were to be left on the piers" due to limited merchant shipping/escorts and fear of German move into Spain or attacks from Spanish Morocco. "This assumption would prove to have serious consequences in terms of both the sustainment and mobility of the Allied force—consequences that would become apparent as soon as the assault force came ashore." He later wrote: "Our chief hope of anticipating the Axis in Tunisia lay in our acting with utmost speed. Indeed, we were involved in a race, not only against the build-up of enemy forces, but against the weather as well."

After landings (Nov 8), French resistance collapsed quickly (Darlan cease-fire), but logistic shortfalls (port/beach chaos, truck shortages, single rail line) stalled the advance. By mid-November British First Army was ~50 miles from Tunis; "An Allied attack on November 24 failed, and German counteroffensives on November 27 and December 1 forced an Allied withdrawal. Eisenhower decided to wait out the winter and provide time to build his force."

Eisenhower described the North African rail situation to Marshall: "with a rail system that could handle only nine trains per day, three of which were dedicated to nonmilitary uses, 'the logistics situation is one to make a ritualist in warfare go a bit hysterical.'"

He appointed Brig. Gen. Everett S. Hughes deputy theater commander (Feb 6, 1943) to relieve him of administrative details and designated him communications zone commander. He oversaw activation of base sections and SOS NATOUSA while managing coalition and French rearmament politics.

From Smith (this range): The invasion was the greatest amphibious operation attempted to that date—two armadas, 116,000 men, troops at sea two weeks landing on potentially hostile shore without off-loading. Object: “occupy French Morocco and Algeria with a view to the earliest possible subsequent occupation of Tunisia.” French reaction was “the single most important factor.” Murphy’s Giraud plan collapsed; Giraud demanded supreme command on Gibraltar in a four-hour “most distressing interview”; Ike held firm. Darlan’s presence produced the cease-fire via the Clark-Darlan Agreement, which preserved Vichy administration intact (no purge of fascists or repeal of anti-Semitic regulations). Ike approved and flew to Algiers to seal it. The political backlash was immediate and severe (British “Munich,” de Gaulle moral condemnation, FDR’s public “temporary expedient” and private “WE DO NOT TRUST DARLAN”). Ike accepted full responsibility: “it is easy enough for a man to be a newspaper hero one day and a bum the next.” Smith judges Ike “badly served by Robert Murphy” but also “culpable” for concentrating on the military to the exclusion of political consequences he had learned in Paris with Pershing: “It was a callow, clumsy army that had arrived in North Africa with little notion how to act as a world power.”

The race exposed the consequences of the logistics/truck trade-offs and command detachment. Anderson advanced with fewer than 12,000 men and only light tanks against rapidly arriving German veterans and air supremacy. Ike remained on Gibraltar for communications superiority and devoted “three quarters of my time, both night and day” to political explanations. He hoped to complete the occupation of Tunisia by mid-December and did not move his headquarters to Algiers until November 23; his first visit to the front was November 28 and never approached the fighting. “Eisenhower had yet to learn the art of command.” Churchill called the army “all tail and no teeth.” Patton found neither Ike nor Clark had been to the front and “showed great lack of decision.” Brooke’s diary: “Eisenhower seemed unable to grasp the urgency... I had little confidence in his having the ability to handle the military situation.” Marshall’s December 22 cable ordered him to delegate politics and focus on the battle. The stalemate held until Alexander’s arrival and massive later resupply (thousands of trucks, 1,400 tanks vs. 80 German by April) turned the tide by brute force. Tunis fell May 8; last surrender May 13.

## Role in Normandy and Liberation (from Smith, Eisenhower in War and Peace, this range; cross D'Este)

Eisenhower as Supreme Commander SHAEF (appointed post-Sicily; London Jan 15 1944). Monty revised COSSAC plan with intensity “of Christ cleansing the temple”: 5 assault divisions on 5 beaches (Utah/Omaha US First Army under Bradley; Gold/Juno/Sword British Second under Dempsey; 82nd/101st Airborne + British 6th to isolate), 9 follow-on; “the plan for the D-Day landing (NEPTUNE) reflected Montgomery’s wishes.” Planners relieved: “At least we no longer carried our dreadful burden of responsibility.” 

Landing craft shortage + ANVIL commitment forced delay to early June (DRAGOON Aug for French participation). 

Air forces: Harris/Spaatz resisted (strategic alone; Douhet/Mitchell); detested Leigh-Mallory. “When a battle needs the last ounce of available force, the commander must not be in the position of depending upon request and negotiation to get it.” Compromise Tedder “direction”; Ike ordered French rail bombing anyway. 76,000 tons; rail halved; Seine bridges out; “Normandy was sealed off”; French casualties <10k. 

De Gaulle: State Dept “no dealings” directive challenged; McCloy secured authority; “The whole matter has been thrown back in my lap...” June 4 briefing; de Gaulle approved in advance, wrote own broadcast (“orders... must be followed precisely”). “Ike became de Gaulle’s best friend in the Allied camp, and de Gaulle... spared Eisenhower the necessity of having to govern a liberated France.”

Patton: Knutsford speech (“destiny of the British and Americans to rule the world”); near relief; retained for offensive value after Marshall left decision to Ike. Emotional scene: “tears... helmet to fall off... ‘Sir, could I now go back to my headquarters?’”

D-Day decision (June 4–5): Stagg forecast window. Polls: Monty “Go”; airmen opposed initially. 24h postpone; then “gleam of hope... fair conditions” to June 6. Five-min silence. “OK, we’ll go.” Failure note: “If any blame or fault attaches to the attempt it is mine alone.” Airborne override (Leigh-Mallory 70%/50% loss est.): “I went to my tent alone... unbearable burden of a conscience justly accusing me of the stupid, blind sacrifice...” (proceeded; actual losses minimal). Eve visit to 101st: “no military pomp... ‘It’s very hard really to look a soldier in the eye when you fear you are sending him to his death.’” (Summersby). “I never realized before the loneliness and isolation of a commander...” (Bedell Smith).

Execution: 132k ashore; Omaha crisis (Gerow inexperience/frontal assault/352nd Div; redeemed by 1st Div “Two kinds... dead and those who are going to die”). German failures (Rommel away; von Rundstedt halted by Jodl/Hitler asleep; Lettau missed window; radar jammed). Post: Apollo visit June 7; letdown (“run out of steam... very much depressed”); John visit lifted. Later: Cherbourg (39k POWs); Paris (initial non-diversion; de Gaulle pressure reversed); Bulge elements; Berlin surrender (Zhukov June 5 1945).

Unity of command enforced; coalition held; visible leadership + private burden accepted. Monty pre-D-Day: “big man... Allied commander... trust him to the last gasp.”

## Presidency (from Smith, Eisenhower in War and Peace; enriched Range 06 1952–54)

Eisenhower ended the Korean War with an armistice that preserved South Korea without further US ground combat deaths for the remainder of his two terms. “I shall go to Korea” pledge (Oct 24, 1952 Detroit) “electrified the nation” and effectively ended the campaign; as president-elect visited front, rejected Clark/Van Fleet “MacArthuresque” victory plan, and as president cut ROK Class 3/5 supplies to force Rhee compliance (“Unless you are prepared... another arrangement”). Armistice July 27, 1953. He rejected repeated Joint Chiefs and NSC recommendations for US military intervention (including nuclear options) at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, telling his national security assistant “You boys must be crazy. We can’t use those awful things against Asians for the second time in less than ten years.” Public “falling domino” rhetoric masked private conditions (French independence pledge, coalition including Asians, congressional authorization) that ensured non-intervention; “the moral position of the United States was more to be guarded than the Tonkin Delta.” In 1956 he used financial and diplomatic pressure to force Britain, France, and Israel to withdraw from the Suez Canal, toppling Anthony Eden’s government and repudiating “old fashioned gunboat diplomacy.” 

Early transition and governance (1952–54): Delegated cabinet to Clay/Brownell (“committee of two”; Dulles State, Wilson Defense, Humphrey Treasury—closest personal cabinet relationship; “Eight millionaires and a plumber”). White House staff system with Sherman Adams (“The president does the most important things. I do the next...”) and sealed-envelope discipline. NSC as primary tool (presided 319/343 meetings; expanded membership). “Chance for Peace” speech (Apr 16, 1953) after Stalin’s death: “Every gun that is made... a theft from those who hunger...”—masterly overture that advanced Korean thaw. Indirect “hidden hand” vs. McCarthy (executive privilege ban; Dartmouth “Don’t join the book burners”; let Army-McCarthy hearings and Welch “Have you no sense of decency?” destroy him; Senate condemnation 67–22; “I just won’t get into a pissing contest with that skunk”). Defeated Bricker Amendment to protect presidential foreign-affairs power. Recess-appointed Earl Warren Chief Justice (Oct 1953; unanimous confirmation; launched civil-rights revolution). Approved Iran AJAX coup (initial reluctance overcome; “things we did were covert”) and Guatemala PBSUCCESS (air support added when stalled; “commit the flag... to win”). New Look: sustainable deterrent, Army reduction, “more bang for the buck,” “depend primarily upon a capacity to retaliate instantly, by means and at places of our choosing.” “Like a true professional, Eisenhower made things look easy. He was a master of the essentials... serene self-confidence... no buck passing.” Avg approval 64%; eight years peace/prosperity; Interstate Highway and St. Lawrence Seaway initiatives launched. On leaving office warned against “military-industrial complex.”

## Historiography

Dworak portrays Eisenhower as recognizing the "chief hope" lay in speed but forced by logistics limits (shipping, trucks, weather, single rail) into a winter pause that allowed Axis reinforcement and the Kasserine battle. Credit given for shifting administrative burden and building the theater sustainment base that ultimately enabled victory in Tunisia (May 13, 1943) and follow-on ops. Eisenhower's decisions on trucks and priorities are presented as understandable given 1942 information but with major operational costs.

Often portrayed as the great coalition manager rather than a battlefield general. D'Este and other operational historians credit him with holding the alliance together under extreme pressure while noting his occasional frustration with ground command arrangements. His postwar memoirs present a balanced (if sometimes sanitized) view.

Jean Edward Smith’s 2012 biography emphasizes Eisenhower’s deliberate cultivation of a folksy, effortless public persona that masked acute political judgment and a capacity for hard decisions. Smith argues that the prewar seniority system preserved Ike’s independence of thought (16 years as major) and that FDR chose him over Marshall precisely for his skill managing fractious allies. The work gives full weight to both the war years and the presidency as a period of peace, fiscal prudence, and institutional progress (Warren Court, highways, education aid) while acknowledging limitations in execution.

German side (Lettau forecast miss; Rommel absence; von Rundstedt/Jodl paralysis) reconciled with Allied accounts. Emphasizes coalition enforcement (Tedder compromise + fiat) and personal responsibility. Notes pre-D-Day mutual respect with Montgomery (“trust him to the last gasp”) vs. later tensions; Gerow assignment despite combat-vet preference contributed to Omaha difficulties.

## Late Term, 1960, Farewell, and Death

Eisenhower played a deliberately minor role in the 1960 election. He never mentioned Nixon by name in his convention speech and, when asked whether he would give the vice president greater voice in decision-making, replied that “No one can make a decision except me.” Pressed for a specific contribution Nixon had made, he famously answered: “If you give me a week, I might think of one. I don’t remember.” Relations with Nixon cooled; Eisenhower observed that the Vice President had “very few personal friends” and sometimes seemed to be “acting like a nice man rather than being one” (Ann Whitman diary). Nixon rejected Ike’s advice against debating Kennedy; the first debate was widely viewed as a disaster for the vice president.

On January 17, 1961, Eisenhower delivered his farewell address, warning of “the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex” and the risk that federal contracts would become “a substitute for intellectual curiosity” among scholars. The speech reflected his deep experience with both the necessities and dangers of permanent large-scale defense establishments.

After leaving office he lived quietly between Gettysburg and Eldorado Country Club, wrote his memoirs, and made limited interventions in politics. He suffered a second near-fatal heart attack in 1965 and a series of attacks in 1968–69. On March 27, 1969, he told his son John, “I’ve had enough. Tell them to let me go.” He died the next day. He lay in state in a simple $80 Army-issue pine coffin wearing his Ike jacket with rank insignia only and was buried in Abilene after a modest family ceremony. Mamie later reflected that she was “not sure anyone did” truly know Eisenhower.

## Postwar and Rise to the Presidency (1945–1952)

After VE Day: demobilization, victory tours (London Guildhall, Paris with de Gaulle, Abilene), only Potsdam voice against atomic use on Japan. Moscow trip bonded with Zhukov; “I see nothing in the future that would prevent Russia and the United States from being the closest possible friends.” Relieved Patton (Sept 1945) over denazification statements to enforce policy (“That man is yet going to drive me to drink”). Reluctant Chief of Staff (“nothing but straight duty”); difficult Mamie adjustment at Quarters 1.

Columbia University presidency (1948–1950): accepted after Truman encouragement; wrote *Crusade in Europe* (lump sum, Grant model, no bitterness). Defended academic freedom vs. McCarthy pressures (Marxist speaker, Jessup). Absentee president led to faculty alienation.

NATO SACEUR (1951): “Ike was NATO”; Montgomery deputy worked seamlessly.

1952 path: Clay/Dewey/Lodge maneuvering; rebuffed Truman Democratic offers; New Hampshire filing; Abilene flop then extemp success. Chicago convention: supported Fair Play Amendment; first ballot clinched by Minnesota switch. Nixon selected as VP; fund scandal handled with arm’s-length demand for disclosure then Wheeling embrace after Checkers speech. Permanent scars — no trust thereafter. Marshall public defense. Taft unity breakfast post-nomination.
