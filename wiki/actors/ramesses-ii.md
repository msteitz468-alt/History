# Ramesses II ("the Great")

The longest-reigning and most prolific builder of the Egyptian
[[new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom]] — the pharaoh of the **[[battle-of-qadesh-1274bce|
battle of Qadesh]]** and the **[[egyptian-hittite-treaty-1259bce|first recorded
international peace treaty]]**, whose 67-year reign was Egypt's last imperial summer.
Treated in [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]] (Ch. XXIII, Faulkner).
Reign 1304–1237 by the volume's chronology (conventionally ~1279–1213 BCE).

## The struggle with the Hittites

The defining theme of the reign was the long contest with **[[hittites|Hatti]]** for
Syria. After an inconclusive first campaign (Year 4, the Nahr el-Kalb stela), Year 5
brought the great **[[battle-of-qadesh-1274bce|battle of Qadesh]]** — a near-disaster
turned into a fighting draw, which Ramesses publicized as a triumph but which left
the Hittites masters of the field and of Amurru. The loss of prestige sparked
**revolts across Palestine** (he stormed Ascalon, took Dapur), and sixteen years of
intermittent war followed, until in **Year 21 (1259 BCE)** he and
[[hattusili-iii|Ḫattušili III]] concluded the **[[egyptian-hittite-treaty-1259bce|
"Eternal Treaty"]]** of parity and mutual defence. Thereafter relations were
**cordial** — diplomatic letters, a contemplated royal visit, and (Year 34) a
**Hittite marriage**, the princess becoming Queen Maetnefrure.

## Other wars

Beyond Syria, Ramesses fought punitive wars in **Moab, Edom and the Negeb**, and —
more seriously — held the **Delta against the Libyans**, building a chain of forts
along the western coast road (from the future Alexandria past El-Alamein) to check
them. The **Sherden sea-raiders** who harried the coast were beaten and then
**enrolled as a contingent in his own army** — an early sign of the Sea-Peoples
pressure that would crest under his successors.

## The builder-king

"He surely erected more fanes... than any pharaoh before or since" — though "ruthless
in dismantling older shrines and re-using the material," and usurping predecessors'
monuments. His works include the new Delta capital **Pi-Ramesse** ("House of
Ramesses," the biblical Raamses); the completion of the **Karnak hypostyle hall** and
his father's **Abydos temple**; the **Ramesseum**; the tomb of Queen **Nefertari**;
and above all the rock-cut temples of **Abu Simbel** in Nubia. He fathered **over a
hundred children**, outlived twelve heirs, and celebrated more than a dozen jubilees;
he was eventually succeeded by his thirteenth son, [[merneptah|Merneptah]].

## Shaw (2000): Piramesse, the prince-scholar, and a subordinate king

Jacobus Van Dijk's chapter adds texture to the reign. Ramesses expanded Avaris into his great
Delta residence **[[places/piramesse|Piramesse]]** — "the most important international trade
centre and military base in the country," staffed with foreign officials and, after the
treaty, **Hittite armourers** teaching shield manufacture. His usurpations deliberately
favoured statues of [[amenhotep-iii|Amenhotep III]] and 12th-Dynasty kings, signalling the
**pre-Amarna classical period** as his model. His son **Khaemwaset**, high priest of Ptah at
Memphis, was a scholar-magician who restored Old Kingdom pyramids and founded the
[[memphis|Serapeum]] galleries — arguably the first known antiquarian. Most tellingly, Van
Dijk reads Ramesses' famous **appeal to Amun at [[events/battle-of-qadesh-1274bce|Qadesh]]**
("I call to you, my father Amun") as a marker of the post-Amarna religious shift: "this shows
clearly that the king no longer represented god on earth, but was subordinate to him" (see
[[concepts/divine-kingship]]).

## Significance

Ramesses II is the **archetype of pharaonic greatness** — and the volume's
cautionary case in how a king manufactures his own legend (Qadesh as "victory"). His
reign marks the **definitive limit of Egyptian empire in Asia** (the treaty
renouncing north Syria) and the **last sustained period of New Kingdom strength**
before the decline set in under Merneptah and the chaos at the dynasty's end. His
sheer monumental output fixed his name as "the Great" and made him, for Greeks and
moderns alike, *the* pharaoh ("Ozymandias" = his praenomen Usermaatre).

## Historiography

Documented by an unmatched body of monuments — the Qadesh "Poem" and "Bulletin" and
reliefs, the treaty's two versions, the Abydos and Karnak inscriptions, Boğazköy
letters (Nefertari/"Naptera"; the marriage) — but the record is **relentlessly
self-glorifying**, and the gap between his Qadesh claims and the strategic reality is
the standard exhibit for source-critical caution. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
