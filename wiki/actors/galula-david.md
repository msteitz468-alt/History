# David Galula

David Galula (1919–1967) was a French army officer whose short book *Counterinsurgency Warfare: Theory and Practice* (1964) became the clearest and most influential distillation of population-centric counterinsurgency doctrine from the counterinsurgent's side.

Born in Tunisia to French parents and raised in Morocco, he graduated from Saint-Cyr on the eve of World War II. He saw action in North Africa, Italy, and France. As a colonial infantry officer he was posted to China, served as a UN military observer in Greece during the civil war, and later as military attaché in Hong Kong. He was serving in Algeria during the Algerian War.

While a fellow at Harvard's Center for International Affairs he wrote the book that synthesized his observations. He died in 1967.

## Core Contribution

Galula insisted that revolutionary war is qualitatively different from conventional war because of asymmetry: the insurgent's "congenital weakness" is offset by political and organizational advantages; the counterinsurgent's "congenital strength" is offset by political and organizational rigidity.

His four laws and the six-step operational sequence (clear, hold with static forces, contact and control the population, destroy the insurgent political apparatus, install new local authorities via elections, test and build a loyal political movement) provided a practical template that later influenced British, French, and especially American doctrine (explicitly referenced in FM 3-24 and by Nagl, Petraeus, etc.).

He stressed that the objective is the population, that the war is fundamentally political, that intelligence comes only from a population that feels protected, and that conventional military operations by themselves are a "fly swatter" unless paired with political work and sustained presence.
