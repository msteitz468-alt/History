# The Mughal Empire

The **Mughal Empire** (1526–1857) was the greatest of India's pre-modern empires — the Timurid-Turkish
dynasty that united most of the subcontinent under a centralised, Persianate, Indo-Islamic state, and
the immediate predecessor (and, Spear argues, the **model**) of the British Raj. Documented for the
wiki through Percival Spear's [[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965),
which deliberately reads the **Mughal and British periods as one continuous story** — "British India
was deeply indebted to Mughal India."

## Foundation: Babur and Humayun

The dynasty was founded by **Babur** (fifth in descent from Timur), a Farghana-born adventurer-king who,
having lost Samarkand and won Kabul, turned to India and shattered the Lodi Afghans at the **first
battle of Panipat (1526)** — his artillery and wheeling cavalry decisive — then broke the
[[rajputs|Rajput]] confederacy of Rana Sanga at **Khanua (1527)**, ending Rajput hopes of north-Indian
hegemony. Babur (a gifted poet whose Turki *memoirs* are a masterpiece) left a loose personal empire.
His son **Humayun** lost it to the brilliant Afghan organiser **Sher Shah Sur** (whose five-year reign
left an administrative "blue-print" — land-tax reassessment, a vigorous centre — later perfected by
Akbar), recovering Delhi only in 1555, months before his death.

## Akbar's system

The empire became a durable political fact under **[[akbar|Akbar]]** (1556–1605), whose four
achievements — territorial conquest, the **[[rajputs|Rajput]]/Hindu partnership** (with the abolition
of the *jizya*), the **mansabdar** bureaucratic service, and the **re-creation of imperial sanctity** —
turned a foreign minority's military occupation into "the accepted ruler of all Hindustan." The
**mansabdar** system (officers ranked by numerical *mansab*, paid in cash, non-hereditary, bound to
maintain troop quotas) was the "steel frame" of the state, lasting into the 18th century; ~70% foreign
under Akbar, it shared the higher direction roughly 50/50 between Indian Muslims and Hindu (mostly
Rajput) nobles. The economy rested on the **land tax** and on the trade of rich provinces (Bengal's
rice/silk/saltpetre; Gujarat's cotton and the Surat port).

## The "Great Mughals" and the seventeenth century

After Akbar came **Jahangir** (1605–27), the aesthete **Shah Jahan** (1628–58, builder of the **Taj
Mahal**, Delhi Fort, and Jama Masjid), and **[[aurangzeb|Aurangzeb]]** (1658–1707), the austere
orthodox ruler whose reign Spear divides into a settled administrative first half and a long, draining
Deccan/[[maratha-empire|Maratha]] war. Beneath the dynastic splendour, three movements pregnant for
the future took shape: the **Muslim revivalism** of Sheikh Ahmad **Sirhindi** (against Akbar's
syncretism — "the germ for a Muslim state in India"); the militarisation of the **Sikhs** (from Guru
Nanak's peace to Guru Gobind Singh's *Khalsa* after Aurangzeb executed Guru Tegh Bahadur, 1675); and
the rise of the **Marathas** under Sivaji.

## Significance and the Mughal–British continuity

The Mughals gave India its longest era of stable central government since the Mauryas, a fusion
Indo-Persian high culture (architecture, the Mughal miniature, Urdu's roots), and an administrative
template — the *mansabdar* service, cash revenue, the province (*suba*) structure — that Spear sees
the **British East India Company** inheriting and adapting. The empire's effective collapse after 1707
(see [[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|Vol. II]], Cycle 2) created the power vacuum into which the
European trading companies stepped; its **formal** end came only with the [[indian-rebellion-1857|1857
Rebellion]], a century after its real power was gone — a measure of the dynastic loyalty Akbar had
created.
