# Domitian (Titus Flavius Domitianus)

The **third and last Flavian emperor** (81–96) — Vespasian's younger son, an autocratic, able,
and centralizing ruler whose bitter conflict with the **Senate** made him, in the hostile
tradition, the archetypal tyrant; his assassination ended the dynasty and opened the way to the
adoptive emperors. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 1.

## The problem of the sources

CAH XI opens its Domitian with the historiographical problem: like Nero, he was "the last of his
dynasty," **removed, disgraced, and condemned by *damnatio memoriae***, and the emperors who
followed (Nerva, Trajan) "justified their usurpation by treating his reign as a tyrannical
aberration." The hostile tradition (Pliny, Tacitus, Dio — but also Juvenal, Martial, Suetonius)
is near-universal. The volume cautions, though, that it is **not simply senatorial bias**, and
that even invective and panegyric reveal "what was most resented" and the image Domitian wished
to project. Born **51 CE**, the younger son systematically **denied independent military glory**
by his father, and tainted as the instrument of Mucianus' unpopular measures, Domitian acceded
in 81 with neither his father's nor his brother's prestige.

## Flavian continuity and able government

The volume stresses, against the caricature, the strong **continuity** of Domitian's regime:
he confirmed his predecessors' acts, completed their buildings (the **Capitoline temple**, the
Colosseum, the Arch of Titus, the Forum Transitorium), issued the **Latin-right charters to the
Spanish municipia** (the *lex Irnitana*), and **retained his father's and brother's *amici*** in
his *consilium* (the satirized council of Juvenal's fourth satire). He was a vigorous, autocratic
administrator who took the title **censor perpetuus**, attended to provincial government and the
army's pay (raised for the first time since Augustus), and projected a **censorious, prudish
moral programme** (the discipline of the Vestals). He fought wars on the **Rhine (the Chatti, 83,
taking the title *Germanicus*)** and **Danube** (against **Decebalus** of Dacia), accumulating
23 imperial salutations and multiple triumphs.

## The descent into "tyranny"

Suetonius builds his life around a change "from clemency to cruelty" and "from generosity to
rapacity," driven (he says) **not by inborn vice but by fear and need**. CAH XI traces the
**insecurity** of a childless emperor without military credentials, surrounded by surviving
Republican nobles and dynastic kin who were potential rivals (the parallel with Nero is drawn
explicitly). The **revolt of L. Antonius Saturninus** on the Rhine (89) intensified the
suspicion; the execution of authors who eulogized the Stoic martyrs (Arulenus Rusticus,
Herennius Senecio) and the punishment of the **"philosophic opposition"** marked the "terror"
that Tacitus (*Agricola*) dates especially to the years after 93 — a wave of executions of
senators, consulars, and even his own Flavian kinsmen (Flavius Sabinus, Flavius Clemens). He was
**assassinated in his palace (96)** by a conspiracy of court officials and his own wife,
followed by *damnatio memoriae*.

## Significance

Domitian's reign exposed the **unresolved tension at the heart of the Principate** — the
*princeps'* relationship with the Senate — that the Flavians' good-emperor image had only
papered over. His **competent, centralizing administration** strengthened the imperial machine
and the army, but his autocracy and the terror made him the model of the "bad emperor." His
murder ended the Flavian dynasty and prompted the experiment that defined the next century: the
**adoptive succession** of Nerva and Trajan, by which a childless emperor chose the ablest
available successor.
