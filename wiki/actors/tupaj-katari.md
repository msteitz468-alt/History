## Overview

Julián Apaza — known by his revolutionary nom de guerre **Túpaj Katari** — was the Aymara leader who in 1781 mounted the prolonged siege of La Paz, the most sustained and lethal military action of the third and southernmost regional hearth of the Great Andean Rebellion (1780–1782). Where Túpac Amaru II embodied a relatively inclusivist neo-Inka political project, Katari represented the radical ethnic Aymara line of the insurgency: "more deadly, more of a peasant war, and more hostile to cities as such."

German Arciniegas described him as "a man of Aymara origin, 'of uncertain family, a woman-chaser, illiterate and charismatic.'" He occupied no hereditary kuraka office and lacked the education and legal experience of Pedro Vilca Apaza or the genealogical prestige of the Túpac Amaru lineage. His authority rested entirely on charisma and the mobilising force of Aymara insurgent communities.

## Rise to Power

Katari and Tomás Catari (native governor of Chayanta) had already "ignited a series of Bolivian rebellions from 1776 on — that is, well before the events in Tinta" that launched Túpac Amaru II's revolt. Their movements had an independent genesis rooted in specifically Bolivian and Potosí-region grievances against miners and *corregidores*. There is no evidence of preliminary contact between the two movements; "it was the force of events only partly under their control that pushed the Cataris into political alliance with the Amarus."

Katari adopted the title "viceroy" subordinate to the Inka king, framing his authority within the neo-Inka ideological framework that connected diverse regional insurgencies. His romantic entanglement with Gregoria Apaza, sister of Andrés Túpac Amaru who commanded rebel forces in the La Paz region, created a familial bond across the Quechua-Aymara political divide at the summit of the insurgency.

## Key Role: Siege of La Paz

The [[siege-of-la-paz-1781]] was the operational centrepiece of Katari's career. He directed Aymara forces in repeated encirclements of the city under the royalist defence of Lt. Col. Sebastián de Segurola, who left behind detailed chronicles of the siege. In January 1781 Segurola wrote to his superiors that "the spirit of rebellion is general and common in all the provinces and in the city, and that it is not confined only to the number of Indians but overreaches these limits to touch many who do not hold themselves to be such." The siege drew rebels from Umasuyo Qulla communities across the Titicaca highlands.

At the command level of the La Paz rebel forces, Andrés Túpac Amaru held symbolic supremacy as "Cusqueño Inka colonel," while Katari directed the mass Aymara mobilisation and Gregoria Apaza (his sister-in-law by romance) played a significant organisational role. The command structure combined Inka legitimacy with Aymara mass mobilisation — the most effective combination the insurgency achieved.

## Ideological Stance

Katari represented the radical pole of the rebellion's internal political spectrum. Against Túpac Amaru II's inclusivist strategy — "keep rebel rank open to mestizos and Creoles interested in forging a kingdom free of the outrageous advantages that the viceroyalty afforded peninsular Spaniards" — Katari's movement insisted on "politically and culturally indigenous stances and messianic Andeanist ideology." While the radical line still admitted participation by mestizos, mestizo-ised chiefs, or even Creoles, its mass base was Aymara peasant communities enacting ethnic retribution against cities as symbols of colonial domination. In this the Catarista phase was "the stronger in rallying revolution, especially in the later phases."

The rebels in the south did not refuse colonial material culture; documents from the assault on Sorata describe them stealing "Spanish" style garments and utensils — "rebels sought to incorporate it, to become an active part of, or even to seize and master, what their ancestors and they themselves had contributed to creating."

## Defeat and Execution

After successive defeats and the arrival of Spanish reinforcements, Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru signed an armistice at Sicuani in 1782. Katari refused the armistice and "fought on alone, even after the Cusco and southern rebellions were defeated, to die a hero's death." He was captured and executed in November 1781 — before the formal armistice, as the rebellion's third hearth was finally extinguished.

Pedro Vilca Apaza, the Azangaro kuraka who was Katari's principal highland ally and who continued fighting after Diego Cristóbal's armistice, shared this intransigent stance. Vilca Apaza's career "demonstrates the enormous difficulties that the revolution faced in combining a policy of negotiation and conciliation among classes, political interests, and lineages (the policy the Túpac Amarus favoured) with the radical ethnic line of leaders like Vilca Apaza, who insisted on politically and culturally indigenous stances."

## Counterfactual Significance

Katari's independent Aymara base — predating contact with the Cusco revolt and rooted in Bolivian grievances — demonstrates that the Great Rebellion was not a single coordinated uprising but a convergence of regionally autonomous insurgencies. Without Katari, the rebellion would have remained concentrated in Quechua-speaking Cusco territory; his movement extended the crisis into the Bolivian altiplano and nearly overthrew colonial control of La Paz. At the same time, the radical-ethnic character of his movement made it impossible to build the broad multiethnic coalition that Túpac Amaru II's project required. The tension between these two strategic visions — inclusivist Inka modernism versus radical Aymara nativism — was the decisive internal fault line of the rebellion.

## Historiography

The Catarista phase has been most thoroughly studied by María Eugenia del Valle de Siles (*Testimonios del cerco de La Paz*, 1980; *Historia de la rebelión de Túpac Catari*, 1990). Glave (Ch. 22 of [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-2]]) emphasises the independent genesis of the Catarista movements and their asymmetric relation to the Amaru leadership. The romantic dimension of the caudillo-level alliances has been explored by Teodosio Imana. Katari remains a foundational hero of modern Aymara nationalist politics in Bolivia, and the name "Túpaj Katari" was adopted by Bolivian indigenous political movements in the twentieth century.
