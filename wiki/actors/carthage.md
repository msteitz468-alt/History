# Carthage (the early city)

**Carthage** (Phoenician *Qart-ḥadašt*, "New Town") was the greatest of the
[[phoenicians|Phoenician]] colonies — founded from [[tyre|Tyre]] on the Tunisian coast,
it grew from a Tyrian outpost into the **superpower of the western Mediterranean** and
the head of the [[phoenician-colonization|Punic colonial world]]. This page covers its
**early history** to c. 500 BCE as treated by [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III,
Part 2]] (Ch. 32, W. Culican); its wars with Rome belong to later volumes.

## Foundation

Legend (via Timaeus) makes Carthage the foundation of **Elissa (Dido)**, sister of King
**Pygmalion of Tyre**, who fled her brother — a story set in **Pygmalion's seventh
year**. Both the Greek tradition (Timaeus: 48 years before the first Olympiad) and the
Tyrian king-list (via Menander/Josephus) converge on **814/813 BCE**, anchored to the
synchronism of Ba'alazor with Shalmaneser III. Culican notes the **archaeological
puzzle**: the excavated material at Carthage — the earliest graves at Douimés, Dermech,
and the Hill of Juno, and the lowest urns of the **Precinct of Tanit** — yields nothing
demonstrably earlier than **c. 750–720**, leaving a roughly century-long gap before the
record begins. The site was a defensible sea-girt peninsula with the **Byrsa** as its
acropolis, commanding a large agricultural hinterland.

## Government

Culican stresses what is *not* known — there is no evidence for the form of government in
the first centuries. By the time of the earliest inscriptions (fifth century) the leading
**Magonid** family appears as *rb* (probably "general"), and by c. 450 the civil
magistracy of the **suffetes (*šofeṭīm*)** had been instituted as a form of republican
control. The Magonids were **elected suffetes, not "kings,"** despite the classical
sources. Carthage struck **no coins before the late fifth century** (first in Sicily),
and its early external affairs are lit only by the **treaties with Rome (509, 348)**,
which show it could already dictate trade conditions in Sardinia, Punic Sicily, and the
Spanish coast.

## The rise to western hegemony

For its first centuries Carthage was one colony among many. Real **political and military
hegemony** over the western Phoenician world came only in the later sixth century:
**Malchus's campaign to consolidate the Phoenician hold on Sardinia (c. 550)** and then
the rise of the **Magonid family**. Thereafter Carthage's superiority at sea — Strabo says
its ships could intercept any foreigner sailing toward Sardinia or the Pillars — gave it
*de facto* control of the western colonies and made its Roman treaties operable. Whether
the other colonies were founded from Carthage or independently from Tyre/Cadiz, and when
they fell under Carthaginian domination, is a central unresolved problem.

## Religion and child sacrifice

Carthage was the great centre of **western Phoenician religion** — the cults of **Ba'al
Hammon** and **Tanit**, and above all the **[[phoenician-colonization|tophet]]**: the
Precinct of Tanit, a walled open-air enclosure where **incinerated children were buried**
as *molk* sacrifices. Culican reports that recent excavation shows **child sacrifice
genuinely predominated** (over animal substitution) down to the Punic Wars — Diodorus
describes the Carthaginians resorting to it at the crisis of Agathocles' invasion (310).
The Precinct of Tanit's stratified urns ("Tanit I–III") are a mainstay, if a contested
one, of Punic chronology.

## Causal Analysis

the metals trade (a way-station to Spanish silver).

leadership; and, beyond this volume, the maritime empire that fought [[roman-republic|
Rome]] in the Punic Wars.

## Historiography

Culican's account is cautious about the **gap between the legendary foundation date and
the archaeological record**, and about the **early constitution** (known only
retrospectively). The chronology leans on the Tanit Precinct stratigraphy and on
imported datable **Greek (Proto-Corinthian) pottery** in the oldest tombs. Inscriptions
and coins, both late, "throw little light" on early Carthage. The image of early
Carthaginian "kings" he corrects to elected suffetes.
