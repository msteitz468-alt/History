# Mithridates VI Eupator "the Great"

The king of **Pontus** who became Rome's most dangerous eastern enemy — "an eastern
Hannibal" — fighting three wars across forty years. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH
IX]] Ch. 5 (Hind).

## Role and career

Of a dynasty claiming **Persian (Achaemenid) descent**, Mithridates ruled Pontus on
the southern Black Sea from c. 120, building a **Black Sea empire** (Colchis, the
Cimmerian Bosporus, Crimea, Scythian and Sarmatian vassals) and a half-Greek,
half-Iranian kingship. The legend stressed his vast endurance, his many languages,
and his lifelong self-immunisation against poison. Pressing into Cappadocia and
Bithynia, he collided with Rome (the Delos-Cnidus law decade), and — exploiting Rome's
absorption in the **[[social-war-91-88bce|Social War]]** — launched the
**[[first-mithridatic-war-89-85bce|First Mithridatic War]]** (89).

He overran the **province of Asia**, posing as **liberator** — remitting taxes,
cancelling debts, courting the cities against the Roman *publicani* and money-lenders
— then ordered the **"Asiatic Vespers"** (88): the coordinated massacre of perhaps
**80,000 resident Italians**, a calculated act binding the Greek cities to him beyond
any return to Rome. He carried the war into Greece (Athens, Delos), but was crushed
on land by **Sulla** (Chaeronea and Orchomenus, 86) and, his fleet and cities slipping
away, accepted the **Treaty of Dardanus (85)**: he gave up his conquests but kept
Pontus and his Black Sea empire intact — "no king, not even Antiochus the Great, had
emerged so little scathed after a full-scale war with Rome."

### The Third War and the end (73–63)

After Murena's inconclusive raids (the **Second War**, 83–81) and eight years of
peace, the Roman annexation of **Bithynia** triggered the
**[[third-mithridatic-war-73-63bce|Third Mithridatic War]]** (73). Allied with the
**Cilician pirates** and [[sertorian-war-80-72bce|Sertorian]] officers, Mithridates
overran Bithynia but was outgeneralled by **[[lucullus|Lucullus]]** (the relief of
Cyzicus, the rout at Cabira) and driven into refuge with his son-in-law
**[[tigranes-ii|Tigranes]]**. He staged a remarkable comeback — recovering Pontus and
destroying Triarius at **Zela** (67) — but **[[pompey|Pompey]]**, armed with the
[[pompey-pirate-command-67bce|Lex Manilia]], broke him for good (66). Fleeing to his
**Crimean Bosporus**, the old king was overthrown by his son **Pharnaces** and
**died in 63** (suicide or murder by a Celtic guard) — the news reaching Pompey at
Jerusalem. He had fought Rome for over forty years across three wars.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Mithridates is the measure of how far Roman misgovernment of the East — the rapacity
of governors and *publicani* — had alienated the Greek cities: the enthusiasm for the
Vespers "displayed as much their hatred of the Roman expatriates as their fear of
Mithridates." Yet his military weakness against even small Roman forces (Sulla,
Bruttius Sura, Fimbria) exposed Pontus' limits and made future Roman provocation
likely. His career drove the eastward extension of Roman power that Pompey would
complete.

## Historiography

Heavily shaped by hostile Roman tradition (Cicero's invective) and by **Appian's
*Mithridatica*** and Memnon; the exiled **Rutilius Rufus** and Posidonius lie behind
much of the Greek narrative. Figures (army sizes, massacre totals) are inflated
multiples of 10,000. See [[first-mithridatic-war-89-85bce]].
