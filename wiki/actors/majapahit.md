# Majapahit

**Majapahit** (1293–c. 1527) was the greatest of the **Hindu-Buddhist Javanese empires** and the
classical apogee of insular Southeast Asia — the last great Indianized state of the archipelago before
the [[islamization-of-southeast-asia|turn to Islam]]. Documented for the wiki through the
[[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v1-1992|Cambridge History of Southeast Asia]] (Keith Taylor,
Ch. 3). Where [[srivijaya]] was a **maritime** thalassocracy of the Straits, Majapahit was an
**agrarian-and-maritime** power resting on the rice surplus of the **Brantas River plain** of east Java,
projecting naval power across the islands.

## From Sailendra Java to Singhasari

Javanese statehood ran deep. The eighth–ninth centuries saw two monument-building lines in central Java —
the **Saivite** kings of **Sanjaya's** line and the **Mahayana Buddhist [[sailendra|Sailendras]]** (builders
of the colossal **Borobudur**; their Saivite rivals answered with **Prambanan**) — whose rivalry Taylor
reads as an early phase of **Javanizing** Indian religious symbols into a single Hindu-Buddhist worldview.
After the royal seat shifted east to the Brantas plain (mid-10th c.) and a disaster c. 1016, **Airlangga**
(r. 1020s–1049) restored Java and tied it into the maritime world; the era of **Kadiri** followed. In
**1222 Ken Angrok** overthrew Kadiri and founded **Singhasari**, whose last and greatest king,
**Kertanagara** (r. 1268–92), first conceived an **archipelagic empire** ruled by Javanese — the idea of
**nusantara** — and practised a potent Saivite-Buddhist **Tantrism** to match his ambition.

## The empire (1293–c. 1527)

Kertanagara was murdered in 1292, but his son-in-law **Kertarajasa** seized power — famously by first
**allying with an invading Mongol fleet (1293)** and then driving it back into the sea — and founded his
capital at **Majapahit**. For a century his successors claimed hegemony over most of insular and
peninsular Southeast Asia. The claim was **largely rhetorical**: at its mid-fourteenth-century peak
(under **Hayam Wuruk**, with the famous chief minister **Gajah Mada**) Majapahit firmly dominated
**eastern Java, Bali, and Madura** and exercised punitive influence over western Java, parts of southern
Borneo, Celebes (Sulawesi), and Sumbawa, and could project power into the **Straits of Malacca** — but it
was a network of prestige and tribute, not a territorial bureaucracy. Its court culture (celebrated in
the *Nagarakertagama* and *Pararaton*) was the high classical synthesis of Javanese Hindu-Buddhism.

## Decline and the Muslim turn

Chiara Formichi ([[formichi-islam-and-asia-2020]]) adds a Sino-Muslim dimension to Majapahit's
final century: **[[zheng-he-voyages|Zheng He]]'s** voyages reversed the older Arabia–India–China maritime
route, bringing Chinese Muslims to Java's north coast, and Hanafi Chinese Muslim communities were
established at Palembang and Sambas from 1407 and spread through Java (Ancol, Cirebon, Lasem,
Tuban, Gresik) over the following decade. **Tuban**, Java's main port with Majapahit as its
hinterland, was administered from 1423 by a Chinese Muslim harbour-master ("Kapten Cina") appointed
under Majapahit's ruling queen — evidence that the pasisir ports' Islamization proceeded within,
not simply against, Majapahit's political framework before the eventual break with Demak.

From the late fourteenth century Majapahit declined as the **Muslim north-coast (pasisir) ports**
(Demak, Gresik, Tuban) — enriched by the [[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce|spice trade]] — grew at its
expense; the Hindu capital fell to **Demak** c. **1527**. Its defeated elite and Hindu-Buddhist culture
withdrew to **Bali**, which preserves a Majapahit-descended Hindu civilization to this day. Majapahit
became the **founding memory of Indonesian statehood** — invoked by modern Indonesian nationalism as the
precedent for an archipelago-wide polity (the very word *nusantara*).

## Historiography

Javanese sources (the *Nagarakertagama*, the *Pararaton*, and inscriptions) are richer than for
[[srivijaya|Srivijaya]] but the **political chronology and the empire's real extent are contested** — the
classic mid-twentieth-century quarrel between **N. J. Krom**'s reading and **C. C. Berg**'s sceptical,
"intuitive" reinterpretations (Taylor's own historiography chapter dwells on it). Majapahit's rhetorical
"empire" is now generally read as a **mandala** of graded loyalties rather than a centralized state. It
is the prime archipelagic case of [[indianization-of-southeast-asia|Indianization]] and the hinge into
the [[islamization-of-southeast-asia|Islamic era]].

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

**Foundation sequence.** After the 1292 fall of [[singhasari]] (Kertanagara killed in a Tantric ritual), Prince **Vijaya** fled to Mt Kudadu, escaped to Madura, returned 1293, allied with the Yuan/Mongol-Chinese army, defeated Jayakatwang (surrender 26 April 1293), then **drove out** the Sino-Mongol force. In **1294** he founded a new court called Majapahit (“Bitter Gourd”) under the reign name **Kertarajasa**—Miksic & Goh call it “the largest Seasian empire in history.” [[singhasari]] context: rise 1221 (Ken Angrok assassinates Kediri’s Kertajaya); renamed Singhasari 1254; Pamalayu expedition 1275 vs [[malayu]]; Amoghapāśa donation 1286 as political claim over bhumi Malayu (parallel to [[jayavarman-vii]] sending images to Thai provinces).

**Extent and military.** Administrative control limited to **east Java and Bali**, but expeditions punished subordinate mandalas that broke away. Commander-in-chief and **30,000 soldiers paid in gold**—implying a standing army. **Gajah Mada** served **Hayam Wuruk** 14 years—remembered as the apogee. By **1300 Chinese coins** were standard currency for everyday life and the official economy (Chinese mass production enabling low-value transactions). Trowulan site >**100 km²**, almost certainly the capital: dense 14th–15th-c. occupation; brick dwellings; multi-joint clay water pipes; reservoir-canal system; goods via Canggu market (~40 km upriver from Surabaya) then ~1.5 days overland; ports Tuban then Gresik; Chinese coins principal money (*Kutaramanawa* fines e.g. 160,000 coins); research 1976–88 recorded 864 Chinese coins, mostly Song. Wang Dayuan (1330s): Javanese “principal people of the Eastern Ocean”; market official received “8,000 Chinese coins every day.”

**Religion, literature, Islam.** Tralaya tombstones combine Qurʾanic Arabic with Javanese motifs and **Śaka** (not Hijra) dates 1376–1475; Puteri Champa focus stone Śaka = 1368–69 CE. By end of Hayam Wuruk’s reign (1389) some high-status Javanese had converted, but official religion remained Javanese-Indian blend; Islam a minority on the north coast until 16th-c. inland spread. Literary corpus: *Kuñjarakarṇa* (Śiva–Buddha unity); *Sutasoma* (Mpu Tantular) contains *bhinneka tunggal ika* (modern Indonesian motto); *Desavarṇana* (popularly *Nāgarakṛtāgama*, 1365; 1740 copy salvaged Lombok 1894) praises Hayam Wuruk, lists Cambodia/S. Burma/Champa/Siam as “protected,” Vietnam as “a friend,” annual royal tours, Gayatri *śrāddha* 1362. *Pararaton* (~1500 composition; oldest MS ~1600): half Ken Angrok legend, half Singhasari–Majapahit history to 1478—oral tradition, not always accurate. Inscriptions from Hayam Wuruk to the end mostly copper, not stone; texts preserved in Bali/Lombok more than Java.

**Ming crisis and decline historiography.** After Ming replaced Yuan: Hayam Wuruk two missions 1370; **1380** incident—Javanese anger at Chinese recognition of Sanfoqi as vassal; Majapahit agents killed a Chinese ambassador to Sumatra; Hongwu blamed his own foreign office, next year sent gifts (including 300 slaves), then restricted missions (every 3 years), despising commerce—major effect on Indonesia–China commerce for ~200 years and scarcity of Chinese ceramics (Vietnam, Thailand, Myanmar filled the gap). [CONTRADICTION] Legend that **Islam brought the empire down** is overstated: decline was “more complex, more protracted, and less dramatic” than *Babad Tanah Jawi* portrays; **no confirmation** Majapahit fell to Islamic forces in 1478 with nobles fleeing Bali; **no record of a clash with a Muslim kingdom**. Postclassic framing: Majapahit among ancient mandalas that “faded” alongside Angkor, Malayu, and Bagan as [[ayutthaya]], Bago, and [[melaka]] rose. Sunda enjoyed political-cultural autonomy under the Majapahit-era Sumatran domination; *Kidung Sunda* war 1357 over Gajah Mada’s concubine decision; neither Sunda nor Pajajaran appears in *Desavarṇana* (possible intentional omission). Temasik listed as vassal; Malay Annals: two Majapahit attacks on Singapura. Trowulan yields numerous 15th-c. Vietnamese sherds (Ming-gap ceramic substitution).

*Source: John N. Miksic & Goh Geok Yian, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (Routledge, 2017); [[singhasari]]; [[malayu]]; [[seasian-classic-periodization]].*
