# Adolf Eichmann (1906–1962)

SS-Obersturmbannführer and head of RSHA Section IV B4, the "Jewish desk" that organized the deportation of Jews from across Europe to the ghettos and extermination camps. Eichmann made his name managing forced emigration from Vienna in 1938, recorded the protocol of the [[wannsee-conference-1942|Wannsee Conference]], and personally directed the deportation of Hungarian Jewry to [[auschwitz|Auschwitz]] in 1944. Captured by Israeli agents in Argentina in 1960, tried in Jerusalem in 1961, and executed in 1962 — the trial that prompted Hannah Arendt's contested "banality of evil" thesis. Documented for the wiki from [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)]].

## Role and Significance

Per [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans (2008)]]: Eichmann drafted the invitations to and kept the minutes of the [[wannsee-conference-1942|Wannsee Conference]], later admitting the talk there had been of killing "in very blunt words . . . totally out of keeping with legal language"; he had already explicitly called for the shooting of all Serbia's Jews. After Wannsee he issued the stream of deportation orders that set the trains moving — 31 January 1942, then 6 March 1942 (55,000 more from the Reich, Protectorate, and Austria, in ~60 trains of up to 1,000 each), through the round-up of Berlin's last "full Jews" from 27 February 1943. He visited [[auschwitz]] to settle killing arrangements with Höss, who recalled that Eichmann "showed that he was completely obsessed with the idea of destroying every single Jew that he could lay his hands on" — drinking sessions after which Höss "almost came to regard such emotions as a betrayal of the Leader." Petitioners experienced his power directly: the writer Jochen Klepper, seeking exit permits, was told by Eichmann in December 1942 that his Jewish stepdaughter could probably leave but his wife could not; Klepper, his wife, and daughter killed themselves that night. Eichmann's postwar recollection of a verbal Führer extermination order relayed by Heydrich (autumn 1941) is treated by Evans as unreliable in its literal form (see the decision debate on [[wannsee-conference-1942]]). At his 1961 Jerusalem trial Eichmann gave the figure of six million Jewish dead — the total Evans says post-Soviet archival research has substantially confirmed.

## Historiography

Evans's Eichmann is no Arendtian cipher of banality: like Stangl and Höss he was a "hardened antisemite" whose visceral hatred translated readily into organizing murder. The Arendt "banality of evil" debate (from *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, 1963) remains the classic controversy over perpetrator motivation; Evans's portrait aligns with the post-Arendt scholarship (Cesarani, Stangneth) emphasizing ideological commitment.

## The European deportation network — Evans (2008), Part 4

Eichmann's RSHA meeting of 11 June 1942 set the Western European quotas (France 40,000, Netherlands 40,000, Belgium 10,000); he sent Alois Brunner to Drancy (summer 1943) and Salonika to accelerate lagging deportations, and turned Slovakia's own offer of Jewish "workers" into the labor force that built Auschwitz-Birkenau, assuring Bratislava that "none of the deportees would ever return" (the Slovak government paid Germany 500 RM per deportee).
