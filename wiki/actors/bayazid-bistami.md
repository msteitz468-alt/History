# Bāyezīd Bisṭāmī

Abū Yazīd (Bāyezīd) Bisṭāmī (c. 804–874) is the most dramatic figure of the [[early-sufism|formative
period]] of [[sufism|Sufism]] — the great champion of **mystical "intoxication"** (*sukr*) and the
first to describe the mystical experience in terms of the Prophet's heavenly journey (*miʿrāj*) as a
template for the mystic's own ascent. His paradoxical utterances (*shaṭḥiyyāt*) haunted Sufi
literature for centuries, cited more frequently in poetry than any other early master except
[[al-hallaj|al-Ḥallāj]].

Schimmel: "His personality looms large on the horizon of early Persian Sufism. Few mystics have
impressed and perplexed their contemporaries and successive generations as much as this ascetic
from the little place known as Bisṭām in northwestern Iran."

## Life

Almost nothing is reliably known about Bāyezīd's external biography. He lived in Bisṭām, a small
town in the Qūmis region of northwestern Iran. His mystical master is named in the sources as a
certain Abū ʿAlī as-Sindī — a figure whose identity and influence scholars have disputed. R. C.
Zaehner used this connection to argue for Indian (Vedantic) influences on Bāyezīd, but Schimmel
considers this conjecture: "not every man from Sind could be expected to know all the intricacies
of Hindu monistic philosophy." The main thrust of Bāyezīd's thought, she argues, is better
explained from Islamic roots — specifically the doctrine of *fanāʾ* (annihilation), which he
formulated as a goal distinct from the Vedantic extension of *ātman*.

His sayings were transmitted primarily by his nephew; the hagiographic sources preserve the key
*shaṭḥiyyāt* (theopathic locutions).

## The Doctrine of Fanāʾ and the Heavenly Journey

Bāyezīd is credited with being the **first to describe mystical union in terms of the *miʿrāj*** —
Muhammad's ascension through the heavens into the divine presence (Quran 17:1). For Bāyezīd, the
mystic's annihilation of self replicates that journey; the soul ascends beyond all stations until
it encounters pure Divinity.

His account of spiritual progress goes beyond ordinary asceticism:

> "Renunciation (*zuhd*) has no value. I was three days in renunciation, on the fourth day I had
> finished it. The first day I renounced this world, the second day I renounced the Otherworld, the
> third day I renounced everything save God; when the fourth day came, nothing was left to me but
> God. I reached a desperate longing. Then I heard a voice addressing me: 'O Bāyezīd, you are not
> strong enough to endure with Me alone.' I said: 'That is exactly what I want.' Then the voice
> said: 'You have found, you have found!'"

The structure is characteristic: each stage of conventional renunciation is transcended. Even the
Other world — the ascetic's goal — must be surrendered. What remains is the unbearable proximity of
God alone.

His later accounts of his ascent grow wilder and more vertiginous. He flew "three times thirty
thousand years" beyond space and time; he found nothing but "Bāyezīd" on the divine throne. And
then: "I saw that all this was a cheat."

## The Theopathic Locutions (Shaṭḥiyyāt)

Bāyezīd's most famous utterance is:

> **"Subḥānī — Praise be to Me, how great is My Majesty!"**

This saying — putting the first-person divine attribute into the mouth of the mystic — is the
paradigmatic *shaṭḥ*: a statement that, taken at face value, appears blasphemous (claiming divinity)
but is understood in Sufi commentary as expressing the complete annihilation of the human "I" in
God. The moderate interpreter Sarrāj read it as equivalent to reciting the Quranic "I am God, there
is no God besides Me" (Sūra 20:14) — the mystic's own voice temporarily replaced by the divine.

Later Persian and Turkish poets treated this *shaṭḥ* as proof of the highest unitive state:

> "Bāyezīd, who under his garment carries nothing but God" (Rūmī, *Mathnawī* 4:2102–40)

[[al-hallaj|Al-Ḥallāj]], who would later utter "anā al-Ḥaqq" ("I am the Truth/Reality"), believed
that "poor Abū Yazīd" had arrived only at the threshold of the divine — suggesting that Ḥallāj
understood himself as going further, and that the logic of *shaṭḥ* could be pushed to more extreme
consequences.

The imagery Bāyezīd deployed is consistently paradoxical and vertiginous:

> "As soon as I attained to His Unity I became a bird with a body of Oneness and wings of
> Everlastingness, and I continued flying in the air of Quality for ten years, until I reached an
> atmosphere a million times as large, and I flew on, until I found myself in the field of Eternity
> and I saw there the Tree of Oneness. ... And I looked, and I knew that all this was a cheat."

And:

> "I myself am in search of Bāyezīd." (When asked about himself by a visitor)

## The Contrast with Junayd: Intoxication vs. Sobriety

The central debate in classical Sufism about the highest mystical state is framed as a contrast
between Bāyezīd and [[junayd|Junayd]]:

- Bāyezīd (*sukr*, intoxication): the complete obliteration of human attributes in God is the
  highest state; the self is utterly annihilated (*fanāʾ*).
- Junayd (*ṣaḥw*, sobriety): the "second sobriety" — the return from ecstatic annihilation to a
  new consciousness in God, with human attributes spiritualized and restored (*baqāʾ*, permanent
  subsistence in God) — is the higher state.

Junayd admired Bāyezīd while holding that he "had not reached the final goal of the seeker." This
critique is not dismissal but rather a claim about what lies *beyond* the ecstatic annihilation
that Bāyezīd perfected.

## Later Reception and Legacy

Bāyezīd's name became an honorific: "Bāyezīd of his time" was applied to outstanding mystics in
later centuries. His utterances are cited in Persian, Turkish, and Indo-Muslim Sufi poetry more
frequently than any other early figure except Ḥallāj. Rūmī elaborated several of his stories and
paradoxes in the *Mathnawī*. Muhammad Iqbal incorporated his story in *Jāwīdnāme* (1932).

Sacred places connected with his memory survive at the extremes of the Islamic world — in the
Maghreb (Zousfana) and in Bangladesh (Chittagong) — testimony to the breadth of his symbolic
resonance.

## Scholarly Controversy

The question of Indian influence on Bāyezīd through his master Abū ʿAlī as-Sindī remains
contested. Zaehner argued for significant Vedantic parallels; Schimmel is skeptical, arguing that
his doctrine of complete annihilation of the self is formally distinct from the Vedantic extension
of *ātman* (inner self) to unity with Brahman — the negative via of Bāyezīd contrasting with the
affirmative via of Vedanta. See [[sufism-historiography]] for the broader debate on external
influences in early Sufism.
