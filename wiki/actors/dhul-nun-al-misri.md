# Dhūʾl-Nūn al-Miṣrī

Thaubān ibn Ibrāhīm, surnamed Dhūʾl-Nūn ("He with the Fish"), c. 796–859, is one of the most
attractive and theologically significant figures of the [[early-sufism|formative period]] of
[[sufism|Sufism]]. Egyptian by birth (born of Nubian parents in Ikhmīm, Upper Egypt), he is
distinguished by two major contributions: the first systematic articulation of the Sufi theory of
**maʿrifa** (intuitive gnosis of God) and an extraordinary lyrical talent that redirected the
relation between the mystic and the created world.

Hujwīrī called him "one of the most eminent of their hidden spiritualists" (H 100); the classical
sources rate him as "the unique [authority] of his time in scholarship and piety and mystical state
and culture" (Q 8).

## Life

Dhūʾl-Nūn studied religious sciences and transmitted traditions from Mālik ibn Anās, founder of
the Mālikī law school. During the Muʿtazilite persecution of the orthodox under the caliphate,
he was imprisoned because he held that the Quran was uncreated (the orthodox position against the
Muʿtazilite doctrine of a created Quran). He was released after the caliph al-Mutawakkil heard one
of his sermons and was deeply impressed. He was also accused of being a philosopher and an
alchemist, and the tenth-century bibliographer Ibn al-Nadīm's *Fihrist* listed two of his works
among alchemical writings.

Many miracles are attributed to him; he is said to have "traveled the road of blame" (*malāma*),
cultivating apparent humility while concealing his inner spiritual rank. When he died, legend says
"it was written on his forehead: 'This is the friend of God, he died in love of God, slain by
God'" (H 100).

## The Doctrine of Maʿrifa (Gnosis)

Dhūʾl-Nūn is credited with formulating for the first time a systematic theory of **maʿrifa** —
intuitive, non-discursive knowledge of God, distinct from both rational theology (*kalām*) and
legal scholarship (*fiqh*). This gnosis cannot be achieved by intellectual effort:

> "To ponder about the Essence of God is ignorance, and to point to Him is associationism (*shirk*),
> and real gnosis is bewilderment." (N 34)

The paradox is central: the very act of attempting to conceptualize God already represents a
failure of understanding. Gnosis is, for Dhūʾl-Nūn, an experience of being overwhelmed, not a
cognitive achievement.

He also formulated the dialectic of divine **Majesty** (*jalāl*) and **Beauty** (*jamāl*) — the
two poles of the divine nature that the mystic must hold in tension. The awesome, terrifying
dimension of God (*jalāl*, comparable to Rudolf Otto's *tremendum*, developed over a thousand years
later) must be balanced against the beautiful, attractive dimension (*jamāl*). Schimmel notes that
Dhūʾl-Nūn "frequently dwelt upon the quality of majesty, *jalāl*, the *tremendum*, in God."

His warning about *maʿrifa* itself illustrates this dialectic:

> "One said to Dhūʾl-Nūn: 'Show me the Greatest Name of God.' He said: 'Show me the smallest
> one!' — and scolded him." (Schimmel, citing the classical sources)

## The Hymnody of Creation

Dhūʾl-Nūn's second major contribution is his **rehabilitation of the created world** as a field of
divine praise. Earlier Basran ascetics had regarded the world as a dangerous distraction from God;
Dhūʾl-Nūn recovered the Quranic teaching that everything created worships God in its own tongue
— the voice of the bee, the growth of leaves, the scent of flowers. His extraordinary prose-poems
in *saj'* (rhymed prose) express this:

> "O God, I never hearken to the voices of the beasts or the rustle of the trees, the splashing of
> the waters or the song of the birds, the whistling of the wind or the rumble of the thunder, but
> I sense in them a testimony to Thy Unity, and a proof of Thy incomparability, that Thou art the
> All-Prevailing, the All-Knowing, the All-True." (A 9:342)

This "psalmlike" sensibility, in the words of Schimmel, "deeply impressed the mystical writers of
later centuries." The Persian poets — ʿAṭṭār above all — inherited this tradition directly. The
forty chapters of ʿAṭṭār's *Muṣībatnāma* translate Dhūʾl-Nūn's silent praise of all creatures
into elaborate Persian verse.

The love mysticism is equally striking. In his romantic hagiographic stories — a genre he helped
create — mystical problems are resolved dramatically by unexpected encounters:

> "He met a woman at the sea shore and asked her: 'What is the end of love?' And she answered:
> 'O simpleton, love has no end.' And he asked: 'Why?' She said: 'Because the Beloved has no
> end.'" (T 1:123)

## Poetic and Literary Significance

Dhūʾl-Nūn is the first Sufi credited with composing short lyrical poems — small, charming verses
in Arabic praising the Lord in hymn-like form — marking a new departure in Sufi expression beyond
prose prayers and homiletic sayings. He also popularized the mystical story (*qiṣṣa*) as a literary
vehicle for presenting problems of the Path through dramatic narrative.

## Scholarly Controversies

The exact character of Dhūʾl-Nūn's thought has been disputed. Some scholars have read him as the
first "theosophist" among the Sufis — arguing that his Egyptian location (where Neoplatonism had
deep roots) and his emphasis on gnosis suggest Neoplatonic influence. Schimmel notes that "it seems
quite natural to accept Neoplatonic influences upon Dhūʾl-Nūn" given his Egyptian milieu, though
she also observes that his descriptions of God's nature do not align straightforwardly with
Neoplatonic emanationism. Definitive proof of direct influence remains elusive.

His alleged "philosophical-gnostic character" is complicated by sayings that emphasize practical
love and the suffering that love entails rather than speculative theory — suggesting he was
simultaneously hymnodist and proto-gnostic. See [[sufism-historiography|Western study of Sufism]]
for the debate on origins.
