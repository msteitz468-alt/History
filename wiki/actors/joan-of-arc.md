# Joan of Arc (Jeanne d'Arc)

The peasant visionary from Domrémy whose brief career (1429–31) turned the moral tide of the
**[[hundred-years-war-1337|Hundred Years War]]** in favour of the Valois. Treated by Malcolm Vale in
[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 19a). Vale stresses that her significance was **symbolic and
ideological** far more than military: she gave the disinherited dauphin the one thing the Lancastrian
crown could not match — a properly crowned and anointed king.

## Narrative and Significance

Appearing as Anglo-Burgundian forces besieged **Orléans**, Joan — "a disciple and limb of the fiend
called the Pucelle, that used false enchantment and sorcery," as the duke of Bedford called her —
brought "a species of ideological warfare which was unprecedented." The **relief of Orléans (1429)**,
chiefly the work of Charles VII's captains, was outweighed by what followed: she led the dauphin
through hostile territory to be **crowned and anointed at Reims on 17 July 1429**, the time-honoured
and sacral means. This "dealt a profound body-blow to both the moral and tangible authority of the
Lancastrian crown of France" (whose child-king Henry VI was hastily, and less convincingly, crowned
in Paris in December 1431).

Her mission, as she herself acknowledged, was effectively over after Reims; the court soon abandoned
her. **Captured by the Burgundians at Compiègne (1430)**, sold to the English, and tried at **Rouen**
by a tribunal (overwhelmingly French, under Bishop Pierre Cauchon) at the request of the University of
Paris, she was condemned as a relapsed heretic and **burned on 30 May 1431**. The trial laid bare
"the extent of active collaboration between the Anglo-Burgundian regime, the University of Paris and
the clergy of northern France."

## Counterfactual Significance

Joan won no lasting military victory after Orléans, and Normandy remained English for nearly two more
decades. But by securing the **sacral legitimacy** of Charles VII she began the slow process by which
the disaffected were won back to the Valois cause — a turning point in the war's moral economy out of
all proportion to her few months in arms. (Her later cult and the witchcraft anxieties of the age are
treated under [[late-medieval-lay-religion|fifteenth-century religion]].)
