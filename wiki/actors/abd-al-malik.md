# Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan

**Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan** (646 – October 705 CE; r. 685–705 CE) was the fifth caliph of the [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]]. Often regarded by historians as the second founder of the Umayyad state, he consolidated the empire after the chaos of the [[second-fitna-680|Second Fitna]] (Civil War), centralized the government bureaucracy, and established a distinct Islamic administrative and cultural identity through his sweeping language, currency, and building reforms.

## Consolidation during the Second Fitna

When Abd al-Malik succeeded his father, Marwan I, in 685, the caliphate was highly fractured. A rival caliph, Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr, controlled Mecca, Medina, and Iraq, while Kharijite rebellions plagued Iran and Mesopotamia. 

Abd al-Malik pursued a systematic military campaign:
- He neutralized the Byzantine threat by signing a treaty with Justinian II in 688, paying a high tribute in exchange for peace.
- He defeated Ibn al-Zubayr's brother, Mus'ab, in Iraq at the Battle of Maskin (691).
- In 692, he sent his general, **al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf**, to besiege Mecca. Al-Hajjaj bombarded the city and the Ka'ba with catapults, ultimately defeating and killing Ibn al-Zubayr, ending the Second Fitna and reunifying the caliphate under Marwanid rule.

## Imperial and Administrative Reforms

Having secured military supremacy, Abd al-Malik transformed the caliphate from a confederation of Arab tribes using Roman and Persian institutions into a centralized Islamic empire:
- **Arabization of the Bureaucracy**: He decreed that Arabic replace Greek, Coptic, and Middle Persian as the sole language of government administration (*diwan*). This forced the hereditary non-Muslim administrative class to learn Arabic or face replacement.
- **Monetary Reform (696/7 CE)**: He discontinued the use of Byzantine-style and Sasanian-style coins. He minted a new gold dinar and silver dirham featuring strictly epigraphic Arabic inscriptions (asserting the unity of God and Muhammad's prophethood) and no images, establishing the economic and religious sovereignty of the Islamic state.
- **The Postal Service (*Barid*)**: He expanded and structured the imperial post, using horse relays to transmit official messages and report on provincial security.

## Monumental Architecture: The Dome of the Rock

In 691/2 CE, Abd al-Malik commissioned the construction of the **Dome of the Rock** (Qubbat al-Sakhra) on the Temple Mount (Haram al-Sharif) in Jerusalem. 
- **Interpretive Significance**: The building, located in a city predominantly populated by Christians, was designed to showcase the triumph of Islam over Judaism and Christianity. The interior inscriptions explicitly reject the Christian doctrine of the Trinity and the divinity of Jesus, asserting the absolute monotheism of Islam.
- **Historiographical Debate**: Some medieval historians (such as al-Ya'qubi) claimed that Abd al-Malik built the Dome of the Rock to divert the pilgrimage (*Hajj*) from Mecca while Ibn al-Zubayr controlled the Hijaz. However, modern scholars (such as Carole Hillenbrand) place greater emphasis on its role as a statement of imperial supremacy and religious differentiation directed at the Byzantine Empire and the local Christian population.
