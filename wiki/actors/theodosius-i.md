# Theodosius I ("the Great")

The Spanish-born general raised to the purple after **[[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople]]** —
**Augustus of the East from 379**, briefly sole ruler, and the emperor who made **Nicene Christianity the
official religion of the Roman state**, settled the Goths inside the empire, and at his death (395)
**divided the empire permanently** between his sons. The last to rule both halves. Treated in
[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 3 (**John Curran**).

## Accession and the Gothic settlement

Recalled from retirement on his family's Spanish estates after the death of his father, Theodosius was
crowned by **Gratian at Sirmium (January 379)** to retrieve the disaster of Adrianople. Unable to destroy
the Goths militarily, he rebuilt the shattered army with **harsh conscription** and unprecedented
**barbarian recruitment**, then concluded the epochal **foedus of 3 October 382**: the Goths were settled
on Roman soil (Lower Moesia, Thrace) as **autonomous allies under their own chiefs** — fighting for Rome
as *foederati* but retaining their native structure. Heather calls this perhaps "the most momentous
*foedus* in Roman history"; it set the template for barbarian settlement within the empire.

## The Nicene state church

Theodosius was a committed **Nicene** Christian. His edict ***Cunctos populos*** (380) defined orthodoxy
as the faith of Rome and Alexandria; he deposed the Homoian bishop of Constantinople and summoned the
**Council of Constantinople (381)** — which reaffirmed Nicaea, **sealed the defeat of Arianism**,
anathematized dissenters, and declared Constantinople second in honour after Rome ("the New Rome"). After
his absolution by **Ambrose of Milan** for the **massacre of Thessalonica (390)** — a landmark assertion
of ecclesiastical authority over the emperor — Theodosius issued the sweeping **anti-pagan laws of
391–392**, closing all temples and banning all sacrifice (public and private). Paganism was now illegal.

## The usurpers and the Frigidus

Theodosius twice intervened in the West against usurpers who had toppled the house of Valentinian:
**Magnus Maximus** (killer of Gratian, defeated 388) and the puppet **Eugenius**, behind whom stood the
Frankish *magister militum* **Arbogast**. The decisive **battle of the Frigidus (6 September 394)** —
where Eugenius marched under images of Jupiter and Hercules, and a providential storm broke his line —
was cast as the triumph of Christianity over a last pagan rally. Victorious and sole ruler, Theodosius
died at Milan months later (**17 January 395**).

## The division of 395

At his death the empire passed to his two young sons under guardians — **Arcadius** in the East and the
child **Honorius** in the West (under the half-Vandal generalissimo **[[stilicho|Stilicho]]**). This
**division of 395 proved permanent**: the two halves never again shared a single ruler, and their
divergent fates — the East's survival, the West's fragmentation — flow from this moment.

## Significance

Theodosius is the emperor who **fixed the religious and political shape of late antiquity**: Nicene
Christianity as the state faith, paganism outlawed, the church (in Ambrose) able to discipline the
emperor, and the empire permanently bipartite. His Gothic *foedus* — a pragmatic response to catastrophe —
embedded autonomous barbarians within the frontiers, while the division of 395 launched the divergent
histories of Byzantium and the post-Roman West. "The Great" is earned, but its legacy is double-edged.

## Freeman, *Egypt, Greece, and Rome* (2014)

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]]) quotes the **Edict of
Thessalonica** (January 380) directly: it mandated belief in the Trinity "under the
concept of equal majesty," branding dissenters "demented and insane" — the first Roman
law explicitly outlawing specific Christian beliefs. He notes the **Council of
Constantinople (381)** was attended by only 150 eastern bishops (the bishop of Rome
absent), that its presiding bishop, Gregory of Nazianzus, was forced to resign amid
the acrimony, and characterizes the settlement's "essentially political nature"
(citing his own monograph, *AD 381: Heretics, Pagans and the
Christian State*, 2008). On Theodosius and **Ambrose**: beyond Thessalonica,
Freeman details the **Callinicum synagogue affair**, in which Ambrose's pressure
forced Theodosius to retract an order requiring restitution to a synagogue burned by
Christians. Freeman also attributes the **anti-pagan legislation of the early 390s**
partly to the *magister officiorum* Flavius Rufinus's influence, and notes its
concrete effects: the ancient Olympic Games ended after roughly 1,200 years, and the
Serapeum at Alexandria was destroyed (391) by Christian monks and mobs.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]]’s Theodosius is the panegyrical second founder of Nicene empire — and the choleric author of Thessalonica — whose death makes Arcadius/Honorius look irreparable. **Gibbon’s judgments**, not wiki fact.

- **Elevation after Adrianople:** Gratian named the Spaniard Augustus; “an elevation at the same time so pure and so honorable” (no conspiracy stain) rare in world history; age ~33.
- **Eastern peace by prudence not Zama:** “The deliverance and peace of the Roman provinces was the work of prudence, rather than of valor” — Fritigern’s death, bought factions, Athanaric’s people settled as *foederati*.
- **Church benefactor rivaling Constantine:** first emperor baptized in Trinitarian orthodoxy; merit of “subduing Arianism and abolishing idol worship”; baptismal/Thessalonica edict — Catholics alone may use the church name; others “extravagant madmen.”
- **Character panegyric with two flaws:** virtues “always seemed to expand with his fortune”; yet “often relaxed by indolence” and “sometimes inflamed by passion.”
- **Massacre of Thessalonica:** circus trap; “the most moderate accounts state the number of the slain at seven thousand” (some 15,000+).
- **Public penance before Ambrose:** “one of the most honorable events in the annals of the church” (Gibbon’s phrasing) — diadem humbled; ~eight months’ exclusion; church power over emperor.
- **Death after Eugenius:** ~age fifty; “every disgraceful moment of the unfortunate reigns of Arcadius and Honorius revived the memory of their irreparable loss.”
- **Against Zosimus-style moral collapse:** Theodosian luxury read as “indolent despair” of a sinking nation under Gothic pressure, not simple vice explosion.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Attributed claims from [[sources/mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015|Stephen Mitchell, *A History of the Later Roman Empire* (2015)]].

- **Accession chronology vs the “savior from Spain” story.** The later view that Gratian called Theodosius from Spain after Adrianople and quickly made him colleague is ruled out by chronology (Valens died 9 Aug 378; Theodosius Augustus 19 Jan 379). Mitchell follows the argument that he was recalled to a middle-Danube military command in 377, making him the strongest candidate in the 378 crisis.
- **Choice of Valentinian over Maximus (387).** Support for the weaker, Arian-associated Valentinian against powerful, orthodox Spanish Maximus was a surprise; Mitchell accepts Zosimus/Gibbon’s explanation that Justina offered marriage to her daughter Galla and family reversion to orthodoxy — revealing rising influence of imperial women in the fifth-century court.
- **Nicene settlement and “militant piety” as construction.** Gratian’s Aug 379 law and Theodosius’s Feb 380 *Cunctos populos* (CTh 16.1.2) defined catholic Christians by the Damasus/Peter of Alexandria standard. Immediate context was Constantinopolitan bishopric politics, not a cosmic epoch; long-term effects were far-reaching. Council of Constantinople 381 reaffirmed Nicaea: “The settlement was imposed, not agreed by consensus.” Anti-pagan sequence: Gratian cut Rome cult subsidies (382); Cynegius Maternus’s temple destruction (384–8); 391 sacrifice/sanctuary ban; 392 Serapeum destruction under Theophilus with imperial troops. Frigidus 394 pagan-vs-Christian framing (Rufinus, Augustine) is largely Christian propaganda per Alan Cameron — real issue was western control. “Theodosius’ reputation as an emperor motivated by militant Christian piety is largely a construction of triumphalist Christian writers.” Second dynamic: Nicene orthodoxy distinguished Romans from Arian barbarians.
