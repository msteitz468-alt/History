# Seris (Comcaac)

## Overview

The **Seris** (**Comcaac**) are a hunting-gathering (later fishing) people of the **western Sonora** coast and **Tiburón Island**. Susan M. Deeds (CHNPA II.2 Ch. 13) treats them as a principal case of **continuous resistance** and ecological niche survival: with [[apache-mexico-frontier|Apaches]], they avoided permanent agricultural villages during the colonial period; unlike Apaches, they remained a small Mexican-resident people into the late twentieth century.

At contact they stood at the hunter-gatherer end of NW Mexico’s subsistence continuum (with Baja California and eastern desert bands), not the ranchería-agricultural majority.

## Colonial survival

Continuous resistance and raiding helped Seris avoid mission-style agricultural villages. **Small numbers** survived eighteenth-century **extermination campaigns**—condoned by Jesuits who despaired of further missions. Captured groups sometimes resettled with desert Pima assistance or under military roundups toward Hermosillo; many returned to coastal/island refuges.

## Nineteenth–twentieth centuries

Survivors of colonial deportation/extermination numbered only a **few hundred** by the 19th c. Combined fishing/gathering with western Sonora **hacienda raids** and highway banditry; **Tiburón Island** remained a refuge despite military roundups. Little change from civilizing efforts until the **1920s** commercial fishing push (Kino Bay, Desemboque). **1950s** INI fishing cooperative failed. Seris resisted alien education and religion; ironwood carvings and baskets entered **1960s–70s** artisan coops; **1970s** coastal *ejido* plus Tiburón as wildlife preserve.

**Key factor in persistence** (Deeds): ability to subsist in a **desert environment of little commercial attraction** for others. Approximately **500** Seris counted in **1980** retained autochthonous worldviews and rituals still poorly understood by outsiders; modest growth and some marital exogamy under peaceful coexistence.

## Counterfactual significance

If Sonoran coastal desert and Tiburón had held commercial agricultural or mining value comparable to the Yaqui Valley, Seri numbers would likely have followed Baja California’s near-extinction path under forced sedentarism. Militant resistance alone could mean disaster (Baja Pericú–Guaycura 1734) or endurance (Seris)—ecology and commercial unattractiveness tip the outcome (Deeds conclusion).
