# The Kingdom of Cyprus (Lusignan, 1192–1489)

The **Kingdom of Cyprus** was the Latin crusader monarchy established after
**[[richard-conquest-of-cyprus-1191|Richard I's conquest (1191)]]** and the transfer of the
island to **[[guy-of-lusignan|Guy of Lusignan]]** in 1192. Under the
**[[lusignan-dynasty|Lusignan dynasty]]** it became the most durable western regime of the
crusading Levant — outlasting the mainland [[crusader-states|crusader states]] (fallen 1291)
and surviving, under growing strain, until Venetian annexation in 1489.

## Foundation (1191–1205)

Richard sold Cyprus to the Templars, then to Guy (1192). Guy's settlement recruited
dispossessed Syrian Franks; feudal customs of Jerusalem were transplanted with minor changes,
while land exploitation, peasantry and commercial taxation retained much Byzantine substance.
**[[aimery-of-lusignan|Aimery]]** secured a Latin hierarchy (1196) and a royal crown from
Emperor Henry VI (coronation 1197), briefly uniting Cyprus and Jerusalem in his person
(1197–1205) while keeping separate institutions.

## Thirteenth century: Ibelins, dual monarchy, Acre

Royal minorities (32 of 62 years 1205–67) empowered the **[[ibelin-family|Ibelins]]**, who
won the **[[war-of-the-lombards-1228|War of the Lombards]]** against Frederick II (1228–33)
and monopolized high office. Cypriot resources regularly defended Latin Syria; Hugh III
(1267–84) was the first resident dual king of Cyprus and Jerusalem since the 1220s, but
failed against Angevin rivals and Mamluk might. **[[henry-ii-of-cyprus|Henry II]]** recovered
Acre in 1286 and lost it in **[[fall-of-acre-1291|1291]]**. Upper limit of forces spareable
from island garrisons: ~200–250 knights.

## Sole outpost and apogee (1291–1369)

After 1291 Cyprus was the sole major Latin outpost. Henry II's reign weathered Amaury's coup
(1306–10) and began commercial prosperity as Famagusta became the eastern entrepôt under the
papal trade ban. Hugh IV (1324–59) and **[[peter-i-of-cyprus|Peter I]]** (1359–69) mark the
apogee: anti-Turkish naval leagues, Satalia (1361), and the
**[[sack-of-alexandria-1365|sack of Alexandria (1365)]]**. Mid-century recession (route shifts
to Alexandria, Black Death) and Peter's war costs prepared the crisis of 1369–74.

## Institutions

Hybrid system: western monarchical form (coronation, *rex* title, grand sergeanties largely
ceremonial by the fourteenth century) over Byzantine public-authority substance. Kings
retained major towns, fortifications, salt, mint, and capital justice — no vassal franchises
for high justice (contrast Jerusalem and Frankish Greece). **High Court** of all royal
vassals: succession, fiefs, legislation. **Council** grew from informal counsel. **Chancery**
passed from Latin clergy to lay notaries and Roman-law judges. ***Secrète*** (name from
Byzantine *sekreton*): central finance office with Greek staff and Latin bailli. *Cour des
bourgeois* for burgess justice. Genoese and Venetian communes were the main jurisdictional
exceptions. The 1369 ordonnance after Peter's murder constrained war, recruitment and
taxation to vassal consent — no Third Estate.

## Climacteric and epilogue (1373–1489)

**[[genoese-invasion-of-cyprus-1373|Genoese invasion (1373–74)]]** seized Famagusta, imposed
tribute and a crushing indemnity, and ended Cyprus as a crusading base. The dynasty survived
Mamluk invasions (1420s) and civil war (1460s) until James II's death (1473) and Caterina
Cornaro's abdication to Venice (1489); Ottomans took the island in 1571.

Edbury's verdict: the almost 200 years before 1373 showed "remarkable… stability and
prosperity," making Cyprus "one of the most successful western regimes established by the
crusaders either in the Levant or in the former Byzantine lands around the Aegean."
