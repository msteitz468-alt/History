# Chinook

Peoples of the **lower Columbia River** from the **Dalles to the coast**. Language quite distinct from surrounding Coast [[salish]]. Before and after European contact, famous as **middlemen** in trade up and down the coast and into the interior—name almost synonymous with trade on the [[pacific-northwest-coast]].

## Role and overview

Strategic river-mouth position; control of the **Dalles** (upstream rapids)—ideal salmon fishery where interior groups came to trade salmon; the Dalles became an entrepôt for expanding fur-trade goods. Leader **Concomly** well known to Euro-Americans. Trade language **Chinook jargon** developed (origins obscure; may predate contact)—words from Chinook, Nootka, French, English; widely used in the fur-trade period and later even for treaty talk (Stevens councils used jargon for complicated provisions—itself a source of misunderstanding).

## Disease and demography (hedges preserved)

Intermittent fever that seems to have been **malaria** appeared on the southern coast in **1830** and over the next few years exacted a heavy toll. Chinooks and other lower Columbia groups were devastated in the early nineteenth century. **One student of the subject has suggested that the Native population of the lower Columbia had dropped by 80 to 90 percent by ~1840**; the disease became endemic and debilitating rather than a single pure epidemic. Fisher embeds this within a broader caution: overall early-contact demographic impact is a **“numbers game” on pliable evidence**; precontact regional totals are well-informed guesses; decline was real but **precise extent needs more caution**; type of European presence and focal-point trade exposure (Chinooks as trade hub) mattered alongside microbes. Venereal disease, smallpox (probable 1770s arrival; later discrete outbreaks), and other infections also figure. Sweat-lodge then cold-water practices may have worsened illness.

## Settlement era

As a trade focal people on the American-settled lower Columbia, Chinooks faced early settler pressure, territorial treaty-making under Lane/Stevens, and the collapse of the fur-trade mediation frontier after the **Whitman Mission** violence (1847) upstream among Cayuse. Their middleman economic niche could not survive agricultural settlement, commercial fishery competition, and demographic collapse.

## Counterfactual significance

Chinook control of the Columbia gateway made them indispensable partners in the reciprocal fur-trade phase—and uniquely exposed once disease and settlement closed that niche. Jargon’s afterlife in colonial administration is a linguistic measure of their former commercial centrality.

## Historiography

Ruby & Brown’s Chinook history is among Fisher’s cited particular-group works south of the border. Demographic claims for the lower Columbia are among the steepest in Fisher’s chapter and must not be generalized uncritically to the whole Northwest (BC late-century census stability contrasts with steeper U.S. Northwest decline).
