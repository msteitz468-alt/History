# Pyotr Stolypin

**Pyotr Stolypin** (1862–1911) was a prominent Russian statesman who served as Prime Minister of the Russian Empire from 1906 until his assassination in 1911. Stolypin pursued a dual strategy of severe political repression ("pacification") and far-reaching agricultural reform, most notably the **Stolypin Land Reform**, which aimed to stabilize the empire by replacing the peasant commune with a class of conservative peasant landowners.

## Biography / Description

Stolypin belonged to an old Russian noble family and was educated in natural sciences before entering public service. He served as governor of Grodno and later Saratov, where he earned a reputation for firmness and efficiency in suppressing peasant disorders during the Revolution of 1905. In July 1906, Nicholas II appointed him Minister of the Interior and Prime Minister.

Stolypin believed that the autocracy could survive only if it crushed the revolutionary movement and simultaneously modernized its economic and social foundations. He famously proclaimed, "First pacification, then reform." In his pacification campaign, Stolypin used emergency powers to establish military field courts-martial, which summarily executed thousands of suspected revolutionaries—a policy that earned the hangman's noose the nickname "Stolypin's necktie."

Stolypin clashed with the reform-minded First and Second Dumas. To secure a cooperative legislature, he executed the **Coup of June 1907**, changing the electoral law by imperial decree to favor the gentry and restrict the vote of peasants and national minorities. This ensured a conservative majority in the Third Duma.

As he pursued his reforms, Stolypin became increasingly isolated, attracting opposition from both the radical Left (who hated his repressive measures) and the conservative Right (who opposed his modernization plans and his efforts to expand local government). In September 1911, he was shot and mortally wounded by a revolutionary anarchist (who was also a police informant) during an opera performance in Kiev, dying a few days later.

## Key Decisions and Achievements

### Stolypin's Agrarian Reform (1906–1911)
Stolypin's most important achievement was his agricultural legislation, initiated by imperial decree in November 1906 and later confirmed by the Dumas (laws of 1910 and 1911).
- **Abolition of the Peasant Commune:** The reform allowed individual peasants to legally separate from the collective peasant commune (*obshchina*) and claim their share of communal land as private property.
- **Consolidation of Strips:** Peasants were encouraged to consolidate their scattered strips of land into unified, self-contained farms: either a *khutor* (where the peasant lived on the consolidated land) or an *otrub* (where the peasant lived in the village but farmed the consolidated land).
- **Credit and Migration:** The reform mobilized the Peasant Land Bank to help independent peasants purchase additional land from the gentry and the state. It also actively promoted and sponsored peasant migration to the fertile, undeveloped lands of Siberia, providing transportation subsidies and tax exemptions.
- **Results:** By 1916, approximately 24 per cent of communal peasant households in European Russia had legally separated from the communes, and over half of those had consolidated their holdings. 

### The Coup of June 1907
Faced with the hostile Second Duma, Stolypin dissolved it on June 16, 1907 (June 3, Old Style) and unilaterally altered the electoral law. This act, which violated the Fundamental Laws of 1906, severely curtailed the franchise of the peasants, workers, and national minorities while dramatically increasing the weight of the gentry. The resulting Third Duma was highly conservative and approved Stolypin's reforms.

## Historical Significance

Pyotr Stolypin's historical significance lies in his attempt to save the Russian autocracy through a "wager on the strong"—creating a class of independent, prosperous peasant proprietors who would have a vested interest in private property and law and order, thereby providing a stable social base for the monarchy. 

Scholars remain divided on the potential success of his reform. Optimists, such as Treadgold, argue that Stolypin was on the path to stabilizing the countryside and that, if granted "twenty years of peace" (as Stolypin requested), his reform would have prevented the Bolshevik revolution. Pessimists and Marxist critics, such as Liashchenko, point out that the reform failed to cure the basic land hunger of the peasantry (since gentry estates remained untouched) and created deep social divisions in the village between the wealthy peasant proprietors (*kulaks*) and the impoverished, communal peasants. Ultimately, the outbreak of World War I in 1914 cut the experiment short, and the peasants swept away Stolypin's proprietary farms in the agrarian upheavals of 1917, returning to the egalitarian commune.
