# Ancestral Pueblo (Anasazi)

The largest and most geographically expansive of the three major cultural traditions of the Greater Southwest. The term "Anasazi," derived from a Navajo word meaning "enemy ancestors," is neither accurate nor acceptable to modern Pueblo Indians, who are the living descendants of these people. The preferred term is "Ancestral Pueblo." The tradition emerged from Archaic hunter-gatherers of the Colorado Plateau and ultimately gave rise to the modern Pueblo peoples of Arizona, New Mexico, and adjacent states.

## Overview

Ancestral Pueblo peoples occupied the Colorado Plateau — a high-elevation landscape in what is now northern Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, and Colorado — for more than a millennium before the climatic disruptions of the thirteenth century forced widespread migration. Their characteristic solution to farming in an arid, unpredictable environment was flexible dry farming: raising maize, squash, beans, and other crops wherever rainfall, springs, and seeps made cultivation viable. A slight drop in rainfall or rise in temperature could render a village's fields unproductive, forcing relocation. This delicate balance shaped the entire developmental trajectory of the tradition.

## Geographic Distribution

At their maximum extent (by around 1150 CE), Ancestral Pueblo communities had spread across portions of five modern states. The Colorado Plateau formed the core. Branches extended westward to the edge of the Sonoran Desert, eastward toward the Rio Grande, and southward where they overlapped with Mogollon territory. After 1150 CE, environmental change forced the western margins to contract. By 1300 CE the entire San Juan River drainage had been abandoned; descendants consolidated in the northern Rio Grande region of New Mexico and at Hopi and Zuni.

## Dating and Chronology

The Pecos Classification System, still widely referenced, divides the tradition into periods based largely on ceramic and architectural development:

| Period | Approximate Dates |
|---|---|
| Basketmaker II | 0–400/500 CE |
| Basketmaker III | 400/500–725/750 CE |
| Pueblo I | 725/750–920 CE |
| Pueblo II | 920–1100 CE |
| Pueblo III | 1100–1300 CE |
| Pueblo IV | 1300–1600 CE |
| Pueblo V | 1600 CE–present |

The Pecos scheme is developmental rather than strictly chronological — periods appear earlier in the east (by 500 CE) and later in the west (by 700 CE). Dating now relies on dendrochronology and radiocarbon methods rather than ceramic cross-dating alone.

## Economy and Subsistence

The Upper Sonoran Agricultural Complex anchored subsistence: maize, bottle gourd, three varieties of beans (pinto, navy, kidney), and squash. Turkeys were domesticated after 500 CE, adding protein. Cotton arrived by 850 CE — an important introduction from Mexico — and was transformed into fine cloth for clothing, since animal hides were scarce. Hunting and gathering supplemented the diet and served as backup in crop-failure years. Pottery, derived from Mogollon prototypes, was adopted around 450 CE and became increasingly specialized after 1100 CE, with types ranging from plain corrugated gray utility wares to polychrome painted vessels traded between communities.

Seeps and springs along plateau cliffs allowed intensive cultivation of small fields in seemingly impossible locations, an adaptation described as "finely tuned." Community size doubled by 600 CE as cooler, wetter conditions improved agricultural yields.

## Architecture and Technology

Ancestral Pueblo architecture evolved through a clear sequence. The earliest settlements were clusters of round semisubterranean pithouses with side entrances and central hearths. Over time pithouses deepened until their cribwork roofs were at or near ground level; smoke holes doubled as entrances, and side passages narrowed to ventilator shafts. Aboveground masonry rooms — laid stone or adobe with contiguous square or rectangular habitation and storage units sharing common walls — appeared alongside pithouses by 800 CE. Villages grew to include great kivas, plazas, and towers.

Kivas — subterranean or semisubterranean ceremonial rooms — evolved out of earlier pithouse forms. A standard kiva contains four diagnostic features: a ventilator shaft, a deflector stone in front of it, a hearth, and a sipapu (the symbolic hole representing the emergence place of humanity in Pueblo cosmology). Great kivas, much larger structures serving whole communities, appeared in eastern Ancestral Pueblo communities by 800 CE.

The culmination of Ancestral Pueblo masonry is found at Chaco Canyon, where the local stone — splitting easily into tabular slabs — enabled the construction of multistory great houses. Masonry technique improved markedly over time, from rough stone and thick mud mortar to finely dressed stones laid with minimal mortar. Cliff dwellings (Pueblo III, 1100–1300 CE) represent a late defensive adaptation, most famously at Mesa Verde, where sites like Cliff Palace and Spruce Tree House were built in protective alcoves during the final decades of occupation before abandonment.

## Social Organization

Ancestral Pueblo communities were organized along matrilineal lines. Residences were controlled by related females; married males belonged to households of their wives' kin groups. Kivas served as meeting places for related males — exclusive ceremonial spaces modeled on older pithouse forms. There is no evidence of centralized government at any stage; the Chaco Phenomenon, the largest political formation, is best understood as ritually organized rather than state-directed.

Population aggregation was a recurring pattern: dispersed small communities clustered into larger villages during favorable periods, then dispersed again under stress. Conflict was present — recent research has overturned the older view of the Southwest as perpetually peaceful. Warfare was tied to competition over water and food resources during drought periods, though social and political factors also played roles. Fortifications and other evidence of warfare appear at some sites from the eleventh century onward.

## The Chaco Phenomenon

See [[chaco-phenomenon-900ce]] for full treatment. Between 900 and 1125 CE, Chaco Canyon in the San Juan Basin became the organizational center of a regional system linking great houses in the canyon with at least 70 outlier communities by roads and signal stations. Pueblo Bonito, the largest D-shaped great house, held hundreds of rooms over four stories. The canyon population peaked at around 5,500 by 1050 CE. Trade goods including turquoise, copper bells, macaws, and cacao residue in cylindrical vessels demonstrate connections to Mesoamerica. The system collapsed by the early twelfth century, coinciding with drought.

## Mesa Verde and the Final Phase

Mesa Verde, in southwestern Colorado, supported the region's densest populations. Basketmaker III farmers colonized it shortly before 600 CE. Population peaked in the late 1000s CE, including sites with Chacoan characteristics. By the mid-thirteenth century, famous cliff-dwelling communities such as Cliff Palace (mainly dating 1250–1280 CE) were constructed in defensible alcove positions, suggesting widespread apprehension. The inhabitants joined relatives in the northern Rio Grande region by around 1300 CE.

## Decline and Legacy

A severe drought from 1276 to 1299 CE, precisely dated by dendrochronology, caused widespread social and economic disruption. Outermost villages collapsed first; refugees retreated to surviving communities, stressing resources and triggering warfare. By around 1300 CE, Ancestral Pueblo communities had completely abandoned the San Juan River drainage. People from Mesa Verde relocated to the Rio Grande; Chacoan descendants founded new villages to the south; others merged with Mogollon communities or joined what became the Zuni and Hopi populations. Multiethnic communities formed as refugees amalgamated under stress. The Kachina Cult — a religious movement focused on more than 200 supernatural beings — spread rapidly through nearly all Pueblo systems during this period of population relocation, possibly serving to integrate diverse communities.

The Navajos and Apaches moved into the largely abandoned northern Southwest after 1500 CE, not displacing Ancestral Pueblo farmers but occupying a landscape already vacated. Modern Pueblo communities at Hopi, Zuni, Acoma, and along the Rio Grande are direct descendants of Ancestral Pueblo peoples. Navajo weaving, silver jewelry, and pottery reflect later borrowing from these Pueblo traditions.

## Historiography

The term "Anasazi" was introduced into archaeological literature in the early twentieth century and is now considered inappropriate by descendant communities. "Ancestral Pueblo" is the scholarly and community-preferred alternative. The Pecos Classification (1927) organized the sequence by ceramic and architectural stages, but this developmental framework is now supplemented by absolute dating. Major debates include the nature and organization of the Chaco Phenomenon (ritual center vs. redistributive economy vs. political chiefdom), the causes of the thirteenth-century abandonments (climate, warfare, social breakdown, or some combination), and the degree to which the region was characterized by endemic violence vs. relative peace. Research by Kohler and others has documented declining violence over time among Pueblo farmers. The collection is strong for this tradition through multiple site reports and synthetic works.
