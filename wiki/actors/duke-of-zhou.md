# The Duke of Zhou (Zhou Gong Dan)

**Dan, entitled Zhou Gong** — conventionally the "Duke of Zhou" in English — was a younger brother
of **King Wu**, founder of the [[zhou-dynasty|Zhou dynasty]], and the model regent of Chinese
political tradition. From [[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook (2017)]].

## Regent and Consolidator

When King Wu died only a few years after the [[shang-dynasty|Shang]] conquest, his heir **King
Cheng** was a minor, and Zhou Gong served as **regent**. He crushed the dangerous **eastern
rebellion** — in which other royal brothers (the "Three Guards") allied with Shang remnants under
**Wu Geng** — and then **founded the eastern administrative capital at [[luoyang|Luo]]** to control
the conquered east and house the relocated Shang aristocracy. According to the tradition praised by
**[[confucius|Confucius]]**, he could have seized the throne but **righteously surrendered power**
when King Cheng came of age — the archetype of the loyal minister.

## The Mandate Theory

In the **"Duo shi"** speech of the *Book of Documents*, Zhou Gong is portrayed addressing the
surviving Shang nobility and articulating the **[[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate of Heaven]]**: their
defeat was the inevitable consequence of the last Shang king's depravity, and Heaven had
transferred its favour to the virtuous Zhou. The doctrine was simultaneously **projected backward**
onto the Shang's own supposed overthrow of the [[xia-dynasty|Xia]].

## Source-Critical Caveats (Major & Cook)

Major & Cook flag two scholarly cautions. (1) Western Zhou **bronze inscriptions record several
"Zhou Gong" across different generations**, since it was an important governmental position — so
the title may denote an **institutional role** (uncle-protector-tutor of the royal heir) as much as
a single historical individual. (2) The authenticity of the *Book of Documents* chapters ascribed
to him is **sharply and perennially disputed**: their language is archaic, but there is no evidence
for long narrative texts in bronze inscriptions until the late Western Zhou, "over 200 years after
the original event," so some scholars treat them as later compositions.

## Significance

Whether one man or an idealised office, the Duke of Zhou became the **permanent model of virtuous
government** in Chinese political culture — the regent who serves rather than usurps, invoked from
[[confucius|Confucius]] (who revered the early Zhou order) to the end of the imperial era.
