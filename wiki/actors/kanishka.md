# Kanishka I

## Overview

**Kanishka I** (r. c. 127–153 CE) was the greatest of the [[kushan-empire|Kushan]] kings
and, in [[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin's]] account, the ruler under
whom the empire reached its zenith as the central hub of the [[silk-roads|First Silk
Roads Era]] and a decisive patron of **[[spread-of-buddhism|Buddhism]]**. A.K. Narain
called his reign "one of the great periods of world history"; Janos Harmatta, "the Golden
Age of ancient Central Asia." His empire stretched from Bactria across the Hindu Kush to
**Mathura** and the Gangetic plain, after a "punitive expedition" that took Pataliputra,
the former Mauryan capital.

## Reign and Decisions

- **A new era.** Kanishka introduced a fresh regnal dating system, dating his coins from
  "Year 1" of a new **Kanishkan Era** — possibly commemorating his conquest of the Ganges
  Basin (the *Hou Han Shu*'s "Eastern Division," *Dongli*, capital at Saketa).
- **Cosmopolitan titulature** (carried on the [[kushan-empire|actor page]]): *maharaja*,
  *rajatiraja* ("king of kings," Persian), *devaputra* ("son of god," Sino-Roman idiom),
  even *kaisara* (Caesar).
- **Coinage reform.** He abandoned Greek coin legends for **Bactrian in Greek letters**
  (following the Rabatak Inscription) and added the **deity's name** to the reverse. On
  every coin he stands sacrificing at a fire altar; reverses show mostly Zoroastrian gods
  plus Greek and Indian deities — and, uniquely, the **Buddha Sakyamuni and Bodhisattva
  Maitreya** on five surviving near-perfect gold pieces (probably ceremonial, per Cribb).
- **Buddhist patron.** He convened a **Buddhist council in Kashmir** that debated the
  competing schools and decreed key texts be translated into **Sanskrit** (transcribed on
  copper plates, stored in a stupa). He built a stupa "more than 400 feet high," reported
  by Faxian (5th c.) and still standing when Xuanzang saw it (7th c.).
- **Mathuran portrait.** The famous headless Mathuran statue shows him in **nomadic dress**
  (boots, riding trousers, long cloak) gripping a mace and great sword — feet planted to
  project power. (Mathuran sculptors destroyed the heads of all Kushan royal portraits
  after the empire fell.)

## Counterfactual / Significance

Kanishka's patronage helped transform Buddhism from an Indian renunciant teaching into a
**universal religion** carried along the Silk Roads to China, Korea, and Japan — together
with the new Gandharan/Mathuran iconography of the Buddha. Benjamin treats this diffusion
as "one of the most significant legacies of the Kushan Empire to Eurasian and world
history."

## Historiography

[CONTROVERSY] **Kushan chronology is the crux of the field.** Establishing Kanishka's
dates has been "one of the most challenging questions in ancient Central Asian history for
more than a century"; Benjamin follows **Harry Falk** (2001) in dating Year 1 to **c. 127
CE**. The 1995 **Rabatak Inscription** fixed the dynastic line (Kujula → Vima Takhtu →
Vima Kadphises → Kanishka) and refuted the older idea that Kanishka founded a new dynasty.
A Chinese Buddhist source's claim that Kanishka killed "900,000 Parthians" is unsupported
by any other evidence — Kushan–[[parthian-empire|Parthian]] relations appear to have been
cordial for two centuries. See [[kushan-empire]] for the dynastic context and
[[kujula-kadphises]] for the founder.
