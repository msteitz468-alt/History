## Overview

Abū ʿAlī Ḥusayn ibn ʿAbdallāh **Ibn Sīnā** (Latin: **Avicenna**; 980–1037 CE) was the most consequential philosopher-scientist of the [[islamic-philosophy|Falsafah]] tradition. A Persian Muslim born near Bukhārā in a family of officials with Shīʿī sympathies, he built upon the Aristotelian synthesis of [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]] to produce encyclopaedic works in medicine and metaphysics that dominated rational inquiry across Islamdom and, via Latin translation, medieval Latin Europe. Hodgson describes him as "the greatest Philosopher of the time" and the figure who set Falsafah on its enduring post-Caliphal trajectory (Hodgson, *Venture of Islam*, Vol. 2).

## Biography

Ibn Sīnā was born near Bukhārā, where his father served under the Sāmānid administration. He reports having absorbed the contents of the palace libraries by the age of eighteen — learning medicine, mathematics, metaphysics, and all available branches of philosophy. He was already practicing medicine successfully in his teens.

When the Sāmānids fell and Maḥmūd of Ghazna extended his reach eastward, Ibn Sīnā declined the invitation to Ghazna (where al-Bīrūnī was taken) and moved westward through the courts of Iran. He eventually served as **vizier** to one of the later Būyid rulers, accompanying him on military campaigns. Throughout a life of considerable political turbulence he found time to compose two massive encyclopaedic works:

- **Kitāb al-Shifāʾ** (*Book of Healing*) — encyclopaedia covering logic, natural sciences, mathematics, and metaphysics
- **Kitāb al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb** (*Canon of Medicine*) — the medical synthesis that remained a standard text in both Islamdom and Latin Europe for centuries

## Philosophical Significance

### Building on al-Fārābī — and Departing From Him

Ibn Sīnā took al-Fārābī's Aristotelian synthesis as his starting point but found it insufficient for the post-High Caliphal world, where a Sharīʿah-minded Islam had become the unavoidable social frame. He developed four interconnected departures:

**1. A more substantial account of revelation and prophethood.** Al-Fārābī had assigned prophecy to the *imaginative faculty*, a cognitive power rationalists ranked below reason. Ibn Sīnā elevated prophecy by arguing that the prophet was not merely a skilled imaginer but someone who had achieved the highest degree of intellectual intuition — the capacity for *direct contact* with the cosmic Active Intellect, bypassing discursive syllogism. Because Ṣūfī mystics demonstrated that such supra-rational intuition was real (even if at a lesser degree than prophetic awareness), Ibn Sīnā could ground prophecy in a psychologically defensible phenomenology.

**2. Individual immortality of the soul.** He insisted — against Aristotle and partly against Plotinus — that the *potential intellect* in each human was not merely a participation in the universal Active Intellect, but a **distinct, individual, immaterial substance**. This substance was indestructible; bodily resurrection was not required for personal survival. The logical instrument he deployed was the famous **"Flying Man"** thought-experiment: a person suspended in void, deprived of all sensory input, would still be aware of *themselves* as an existing subject — proving the self's independence from the body.

**3. The ontological distinction of essence and existence.** Drawing on Aristotelian logic but giving it ontological weight, Ibn Sīnā argued that existence is **not contained in essence** — it must be added to any essence from without. This generated the foundational distinction between:
   - **Necessary Being** (*wājib al-wujūd*): the being whose existence is identical with its essence — i.e., God
   - **Possible Being** (*mumkin al-wujūd*): everything else, whose existence is contingent

This framework gave God a *qualitatively different* mode of being from the rest of creation — a logical asymmetry that resonated with Islamic theological sensibilities and proved immensely generative for later both Ṣūfī and kalām thinkers.

**4. Divine particular knowledge.** Classical Aristotelianism held that God (as Intellect) could know only universal essences, not particulars. Ibn Sīnā argued that God could know particulars "in a universal way" — as knowing all celestial essences and their combinations implicitly entailed knowing any specific eclipse. This preserved divine omniscience within a rationalist framework.

### The Paradox of His Achievement

Hodgson captures a central tension: Ibn Sīnā's deepest commitment remained Philosophic — he found ultimacy in the rational harmonies of a universal nature, not in the historically revelatory events that Islam took as foundational. Muḥammad's mission remained primarily a **political event** for the philosopher-élite. He denied bodily resurrection except in works addressed to the general public, where he treated it as a matter of community *faith*. His accommodation to Islam was thus largely functional: Islam was a legitimate political and social order whose Sharīʿah had social utility and whose prophet was cognitively exceptional — but not a unique cosmic rupture.

Yet this very Philosophic orientation opened a door the Ṣūfīs walked through. His psychology of the soul — with its individual immaterial substance capable of supra-rational intuition — gave later Ṣūfī speculation an intellectually rigorous vocabulary. Hodgson quotes the apocryphal exchange: when Ibn Sīnā and a great Ṣūfī parted, Ibn Sīnā said "All that I know, he sees" and the Ṣūfī said "All that I see, he knows" — capturing how his rationalism and their mysticism addressed the same terrain from opposite approaches.

### Later Reception

Ibn Sīnā became a bone of contention. Strict Peripatetics — notably Ibn Rushd (Averroes) — preferred al-Fārābī and accused Ibn Sīnā of distorting Aristotle. But in practice, Ibn Sīnā became **the starting point of the greater part of later Islamicate rational speculation**, adopted by Ṣūfī metaphysicians and kalām theologians alike. His medical *Canon* remained an authoritative text in Latin Europe under the name "Avicenna" through the early modern period.

## Medicine

His *Canon of Medicine* organized Galenic and Hippocratic medicine into a rational systematic framework, integrating it with his natural philosophy. The emphasis on systematic classification, pharmaceutical enumeration, and the integration of theory with clinical practice made it the most comprehensive medical synthesis available in either Islamdom or Latin Europe. Together with Ibn al-Haytham's optics and al-Bīrūnī's astronomy, Ibn Sīnā's medicine represents the mature synthesis of natural science in the Earlier Middle Period.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Ibn Sīnā's synthesis, the resources available for integrating Aristotelian metaphysics with Islamic spiritual psychology would have remained thin. Al-Ghazālī's critique of Falsafah — which paradoxically presupposed mastery of it — would have been impossible without Ibn Sīnā's prior systematisation. The entire tradition of later Ṣūfī ontology (notably Ibn al-ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd*) built directly on the vocabulary Ibn Sīnā supplied.

## Historiography

The question of Ibn Sīnā's "mashriqiyyah" (eastern or illuminative) philosophy — alluded to in some writings but never fully expounded — remains debated. Did he intend a distinct mystical epistemology beyond Peripatetic demonstration, or merely regional variations in logical practice? Hodgson's note (Vol. 2, fn. 10) surveys the debate (Nallino, Gardet, Goichon, Corbin) without resolving it, suggesting the term may have carried intentional ambiguity. The degree to which Ibn Sīnā himself endorsed the mystical constructions later placed on his work is similarly unclear.

## Key Works

- *Kitāb al-Shifāʾ* (Book of Healing) — philosophical encyclopedia
- *Kitāb al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb* (Canon of Medicine)
- *Risālat Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* — allegorical narrative of the soul's journey
- Numerous short treatises on logic, psychology, theology
