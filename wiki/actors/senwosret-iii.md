# Senwosret III (Sesostris III)

The most formidable king of the Egyptian [[middle-kingdom-egypt|Middle Kingdom]]
(Twelfth Dynasty, reigned ~1878–1839 BCE) — the **principal prototype of the
legendary world-conqueror "Sesostris"** of later Greek tradition. CAH (Hayes) calls
him "the pharaoh who contributed most to the enduring glory of the Twelfth
Dynasty." Rendered *Sesostris III* in older works (including CAH), **Senwosret III**
in modern Egyptology. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch.
XX, W. C. Hayes).

## Breaking the nomarchs

His most consequential domestic act was the **suppression of the provincial
nobility**. Under his predecessors the hereditary nomarchs — above all those of the
Hare and Oryx nomes (Beni Hasan) — had rebuilt near-royal wealth and independence.
"Some time in the latter half of his reign" Senwosret III stripped them of their
rights: the series of great provincial rock-tombs **abruptly ends**, and the
country is thereafter run from the Residence through three regional departments
(*waret*) under the vizier. The change coincided with the rise of a substantial
**middle class** of officials, craftsmen, and small landholders, traceable in the
explosion of private votive stelae at **Abydos**.

## The conquest of Nubia and the Semna frontier

Senwosret III is "remembered by posterity chiefly for his consolidation of Egypt's
hold on **Nubia**." He cut a navigable **canal through the First Cataract**
("Good-are-the-Ways-of-Khakaure") to move warships south, led at least four
campaigns "to overthrow the wretched Kush" — ruthlessly destroying settlements,
fouling wells, and burning fields — and formally fixed the **southern frontier at
Semna**, above the Second Cataract. The frontier was sealed by his great chain of
**brick fortresses** (Semna, Kumma, Uronarti, Buhen), whose garrisons' reports
survive as the **Semna Dispatches**. His boundary stela at Semna forbade any
southerner to pass north except for trade.

## Western Asia and the Execration Texts

In Asia he led a rare Egyptian campaign into **Palestine, reaching Shechem
(Sekmem)** — a small operation, but the context for the more important **Execration
Texts** of his reign: hieratic curses on bowls and clay captive-figures listing
foreign princes and towns (Byblos, Jerusalem, Shechem, Ashkelon). They reveal that
the Egyptian chancellery held an "**astounding knowledge**" of Levantine
geography — though, being ritual objects, they are unreliable as a map of any
"empire."

## The "Sesostris" legend and the portraits

His name, in the Greek form **Sesostris**, fused (with Senwosret I and Ramesses II)
into a Hellenistic legend of a pharaoh who conquered the world. He is also famous
for his **portrait sculpture**: a series of statues with a grim, careworn, deeply
lined face — "fatigue and disillusionment" made visible — among the most striking
royal portraits Egypt ever produced.

## Shaw (2000): did he really "abolish" the nomarchs?

Callender's chapter in the [[sources/shaw-oxford-history-ancient-egypt-2000|Oxford History]]
qualifies the dramatic "breaking the nomarchs" narrative above. The title "great overlord"
does fade around his reign, but "the conventional view" that Senwosret III forcibly abolished
the nomarchy "is likely wrong." Detlef Franke showed the office was already being **hollowed
out gradually** — Senusret II had begun educating nomarchs' sons at the capital and posting
them elsewhere — so "the office of nomarch would eventually have been eclipsed by that of the
town mayors." What is securely his is the **administrative restructuring**: the two regional
*waret* bureaus plus dedicated treasury, labour, military and vizier's departments — "a sharp
break with past practices" (see [[concepts/egyptian-bureaucracy]]). New 1990s evidence
(Wegner) supports a long reign of up to **39 years**, against the Turin Canon's "over thirty"
and a highest contemporary date of 19 — a textbook case of king-list unreliability (see
[[concepts/egyptian-dynastic-chronology]]).

## Counterfactual / analytical significance

Senwosret III completed the transformation begun at the
[[reunification-of-egypt-2040bce|reunification]]: the conversion of Egypt from a
monarchy resting on a **hereditary feudal nobility** into a **centralized
bureaucratic state** run by appointed officials. His Nubian fortress line fixed
Egypt's southern reach for generations and turned Lower Nubia into a managed
imperial frontier — the first true colonial military system in the Nile valley.

## Historiography

Documented by monuments, boundary stelae, the Semna Dispatches, the Execration
Texts, and a vivid portrait corpus — but the *mechanism* by which he broke the
nomarchs "is not known," inferred only from the sudden end of the great tombs.
Dates are conventional and approximate. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
