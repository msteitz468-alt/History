# Pierre Laval (1883–1945)

Third Republic politician and twice head of the Vichy government. Son of a
Châteldon innkeeper-butcher; CGT labor lawyer; SFIO deputy for Aubervilliers
in 1914 and a moderate pacifist; independent after 1924; "eleven times
minister and four times prime minister" between 1925 and 1936, and one of the
wealthiest men in interwar French politics through a press and radio empire.
His signature diplomacy — Brüning/Hoover 1931, the Mussolini "free hand" over
Abyssinia and the unratified Franco-Soviet pact of 1935 — uniformly failed.
Politically destroyed by the 1936 Popular Front ("Hang Laval"), he took his
revenge in 1940, telling the Assembly's chief clerk that the parliament that
"vomited me up… now I shall vomit it up."

## Vichy, 1940

Vice-premier from 27 June 1940, Laval steered the constitutional bill through
the National Assembly on 10 July. Paxton explicitly debunks the "Laval coup"
legend as postwar scapegoating: "The Laval conspiracy theory, as legends go,
is an unusually incoherent one. While seeming to vilify the man, it elevates
him to political wizardry." The near-unanimous votes reflected opinion, not
coercion. As deputy premier he built the Paris channel to Ambassador Abetz —
offering colonial cooperation, pilots against Britain, and (after Montoire,
where he believed collaboration had finally begun) demanding German
concessions "die ins Auge fallen" ("that strike people in the eye") to bring
French opinion along. None came. On **13 December 1940** Pétain dismissed him
— for domestic and personal reasons, not to reverse collaboration — and Abetz
extracted him to Paris, where Hitler kept him as a "sword of Damocles" over
Vichy.

## Head of government, 1942–1944

Laval returned on **26 April 1942** ("a Vichy decision, not a German demand")
as head of government, displacing the technocrats with cronies. His tenure
carried the regime's darkest ledger: the relève (June 1942 — one POW freed
per three skilled workers sent to Germany, "a bad bet for the French
future"), the French enabling law of 4 September 1942 that preceded the STO
draft of February 1943, and French police participation in the Jewish
deportations from July 1942. His conduct there was a bargainer's, not a
protector's: he agreed to deliver 10,000 foreign Jews from the unoccupied
zone while telling SS General Oberg that Jews couldn't be handed over "as in
a supermarket," and in 1943 stubbornly refused the denaturalization law that
would have exposed recently naturalized French Jews — German marginalia on
his evasions read "the fox" and "where insolence becomes a method." Paxton's
comparative verdict is that none of this bargaining spared France anything
measurable (see [[vichy-shield-thesis]]). In August 1944 he attempted a
constitutional exit — fetching Herriot to reconvene the National Assembly —
before the Germans removed him east. He refused office at Sigmaringen, was
tried in October 1945, and was executed at Fresnes on 15 October 1945 after a
chaotic trial.

## Assessment

Paxton: Laval was "considerably less than the legend… neither an invincible
cajoler nor an unscrupulous turncoat" — a serial negotiator whose gambits
never once paid. His postwar defense (he had "éviter le pire," acting as a
"screen") is a variant of the shield thesis Paxton's comparative data
rejects. The High Court found his wealth prewar in origin; no graft was
proven.
