# Naram-Sin of Akkad

Grandson of [[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon]] and the **fourth and greatest king of the
[[akkadian-empire|Dynasty of Agade]]** (reigned ~37 years). With Sargon he formed
"a pair whom later history ever regarded as the greatest figures in its annals" —
so much so that tradition wrongly remembered him as Sargon's *son*. His reign was
the empire's zenith and the start of its decline. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XIX, C. J. Gadd).

## Deified king: "god of Agade"

Naram-Sin's most radical innovation was **self-deification**. He wrote the
**divine determinative** (the star-sign *dingir*) before his own name, and his
servants addressed him not merely as godlike but as **"the god of Agade."** To the
older Sumerian theology — in which the king was the city-god's humble *steward*
(see [[divine-kingship]] and [[temple-economy]]) — this was "a monstrous
usurpation," and later tradition may have read his downfall as the gods' punishment
for such presumption. He also first took the title **"king of the four regions"**
(*šar kibrāt arbaim*), the claim to universal earthly dominion later revived by
[[mesopotamian-chronology|Ur III]] and Babylon.

## The Great Revolt and the campaigns

Like every Agade king, Naram-Sin began amid a vast revolt — one account names over
twenty conspirators led by **Kish**, "thankless and mansworn to the house of
Sargon," their realms reaching "from Anatolia to Magan." Having crushed it
(claiming victory in nine battles in one year), he campaigned across the whole
imperial arc:

- **Syria and the northwest:** the conquest of **Armanum and Ebla** (capturing
  Rish-Adad), the cedar-forest of the **Amanus**, and a reach toward **Talkhatum**
  and **Purushkhanda** in Anatolia.
- **The Zagros:** his most famous victory, over **Satuni, king of the Lullubi**,
  commemorated on the **Victory Stele** (found at Susa) — the king striding up a
  mountain over his fallen enemies beneath the sun and stars, "one of the best
  works of ancient art" — echoed by the in-situ rock-relief at Darband-i Gawr.
- **The far south:** a triumph over **Manium, king of Magan**, with "booty of
  Magan" alabaster vases (Egyptian-influenced, hence the old, mistaken temptation
  to equate Manium with **Menes** — a chronological impossibility).

Material proof of his reach survives in his **palace at Tell Brak** (in the Khabur)
and his bricks and statues at Susa, where Akkadian even displaced the Elamite
language in administration.

## The shadow of decline

Even at the height, the seams were showing: **Elam** under Kutik-In-Shushinak was
growing defiant, and "the wild men of the Zagros were poised to swoop." Later
tradition — above all the Sumerian poem **The Curse of Agade** — assigned the
empire's doom to *his* reign, blaming a **sacrilegious assault on Enlil's temple
at Nippur** for the famine and the **[[gutian-invasion|Gutian invasion]]** that
Enlil loosed on the land. Historically the collapse came under his son
**Shar-kali-sharri**, but tradition fixed the guilt on the deified king. He left
the "menacing situation" to that son.

## Counterfactual / analytical significance

Naram-Sin marks the point at which Mesopotamian kingship **broke through the
Sumerian theological ceiling** — claiming divinity and universal rule rather than
local stewardship. That precedent, more than any conquest, reshaped the ideology
of Near Eastern monarchy. His posthumous role in **The Curse of Agade** is equally
significant: he became the literary archetype of the **hubristic king destroyed
for impiety**, the lens through which later Mesopotamians theorized why empires
fall — a moral, providential causation set against the [[gutian-invasion|material
and political]] one.

## Historiography

As with Sargon, the reign is undatable in detail ("no chronology for its
thirty-seven years") and known mostly through **later legend and omen-literature**,
"from which emerges nothing but a blurred picture of triumph and disaster." The
two contemporary anchors are the **original inscriptions** (and their Nippur
copies) and the **Victory Stele**. The famous *Curse of Agade* is a literary,
theological text, not a chronicle, and its dating of the collapse to Naram-Sin is
schematic. See [[mesopotamian-chronology]] and
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
