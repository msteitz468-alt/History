# Cree

Algonquian dialect continuum and political-economic actors spanning the eastern/central Subarctic into the northern Plains: Montagnais (Innu), Naskapi, East Main Cree, Swampy Cree, Western Woods Cree, and Plains Cree bands. Ray (CHNPA I.2 ch. 13) makes them the principal Subarctic middlemen then producers of the fur-trade era; Fowler’s Plains chapter supplies the 1870s–85 treaty and rebellion arc.

## Overview

At contact most central/eastern boreal Algonquians spoke Cree dialects (linguists divide whether eastern dialects are separate languages). Three eastern named clusters—Montagnais/Innu, Naskapi, East Main Cree—plus Western Cree dialects from southern James Bay (Harricanaw) to upper Churchill and Saskatchewan. Western Woods Cree certain in lower Churchill and Reindeer Lake; western margins may have reached Lesser Slave Lake (southern/western limits disputed with Assiniboine/Ojibwa pottery arguments).

Subsistence: favored moose where available, otherwise caribou; fishing more consequential than early mammal-biased archaeology suggests along much of the Algonquian Subarctic except the northern Quebec caribou zone. Social form: small consensus-led hunting groups; no hereditary chiefs.

## Fur-trade middlemen and Bay cordon

Montagnais became the first major European-era middlemen at Tadoussac/Lac St-Jean; James Bay Crees sent envoys to Quebec 1662–63 seeking missionaries and commercial-military links against the Iroquois. After HBC Bay posts (Rupert 1668, Moose 1673, Albany 1675, York 1682, Severn 1685), Swampy and Western Crees moved quickly: Governor Nixon reported within twelve years of the 1670 charter that Crees used English firearms to cordon the Bay and deny distant Indians direct Fort Albany access—except Siouan Assiniboines of the Winnipeg River/parklands (late precontact Cree–Assiniboine alliance vs Dakota; joint beaver raids).

York Factory (Hayes–Echimamish route) became the most important western Canadian fur/warehouse center before Confederation. Assiniboines and Lake Winnipeg Crees controlled inland trade for the first century; with HBC guns they intensified Dakota wars southward and pushed west between Churchill and Saskatchewan. **1694–1720:** Crees fought Chipewyans on middle/lower Churchill and Beavers toward Athabasca—Knight (1716) heard reports of thousands dead (Ray: exaggeration, but scale). Fort Churchill 1717 armed Chipewyan counter-pressure. By ~1715 Assiniboine–Cree middlemen had advanced the trading frontier to Athabasca.

On the northwestern Plains edge (Fowler): Assiniboines and Plains Crees dominated 18th-c. fur middleman trade (guns from York at 12 Made Beaver resold to Blackfeet at 36); post-1760s inland posts cut them out; by early 19th c. better-armed Assiniboines/Crees drove Gros Ventres and some Blackfeet into Montana.

**1781–82 smallpox** weakened Crees relative to Chipewyan forest expansion. Peace River Crees remained strategic provisioners (wood bison, moose, elk) for Athabasca–Mackenzie brigades under NWC/HBC rivalry. Evans Cree syllabics (Norway House 1840) later facilitated literacy networks (adapted also for Athapaskan and Inuktitut).

## Treaties, starvation politics, and 1885 (Plains Cree)

Numbered treaties 1871–77 (and later Subarctic adhesions to 1929) assigned reserves and annuities; six of the first seven involved Plains peoples including Plains Crees, Qu’Appelle/Saskatchewan Crees, Red River and Plains Ojibwas. Native parties often pressed for agricultural aid and rights recognition; Canadian motives included cheap settlement/railway and avoiding costly U.S.-style wars amid disappearing buffalo.

About half of Qu’Appelle/Saskatchewan Plains peoples initially refused—Cree/Assiniboine under **Piapot** and Crees under **Big Bear** and **Little Pine**—seeking autonomy and bans on non-Indian buffalo hunting. Piapot signed Treaty Four 1875 after agricultural-aid assurances; Big Bear/Little Pine sought nonviolent confederation pressure. Government used food as lever (deny supplies; threaten prosecution); Little Pine capitulated 1879; Big Bear signed 1880–82 after widespread starvation; promised reserve consolidation then reneged. 1883–84 leaders pressed fulfillment while restraining violence.

Hoxie: after Rupert’s Land purchase, by 1880 over 25 million acres of former Indian land ceded toward CPR; paper boundaries became enforced separation; buffalo disappeared, HBC traders moved north, starvation became familiar among Cree, Assiniboine, and Blackfeet.

**1885 Northwest Rebellion:** Métis provisional government under Louis Riel; Duck Lake; 8,000 troops. Concurrent Cut Knife Hill attack on Cree–Assiniboine–Métis camp—Crees drove troops off but leaders restrained annihilation. Government used Métis revolt and isolated killings to arrest Cree leaders and break the treaty-pressure movement. Hoxie: large-scale Cree support for Métis never materialized; six Crees and two Assiniboines hanged 27 Nov 1885 after Riel (16 Nov)—“grisly proof” the revolt defeated Plains Indians as well as Métis. Aftermath: Plains Cree and Assiniboine isolated on small reserves (`concurrent_with: [[metis]]`; `part_of: [[great-plains-native-history-1650-1885]]`).

## Subarctic industrial age and James Bay politics

Treaty annuities/ammo/twine initially subsidized continued fur participation; late-19th-c. inflation eroded value. 1873–1921 industrial competition (CPR, cash buyers, Revillon on James Bay) raised returns then crashed; 1914 HBC “black year” damaged credit trust. 1921–45 marginalization: ranch furs, beaver preserves covering nearly all James Bay lands, registered traplines, aviation displacing canoemen.

**James Bay hydro (1971–75):** Quebec’s unilateral $5.6 billion SE scheme threatened Rupert House, Nemiscau, Waswanipi, Mistassini lands. Grand Council of the Crees (Aug 1974) negotiated the **1975 James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement**—compensation, land categories, hunting priority, income security for hunters/trappers, Cree Regional Authority. Ray: under adverse conditions striking gains; more full-time hunters by early 1980s than pre-agreement; successful opposition to Great Whale and Nottaway–Broadback–Rupert; by 1995 more united and internationally assertive amid Canada–Quebec uncertainties (`part_of: [[northern-interior-subarctic-postcontact]]`; `concurrent_with: [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]]`).

## Counterfactual significance

Without Cree–Assiniboine middleman expansion and Bay firearms monopoly, Athapaskan and Plains power balances (Chipewyan advance timing; Blackfoot gun prices; HBC inland policy) shift; without James Bay Cree legal/political mobilization, Canadian modern claims settlement precedent and northern regional government models look different. Structural drivers (beaver markets, Canadian treaty policy, hydro capital) remain; Cree agency repeatedly set the cutting edge of both middleman advance and late-20th-c. resource bargaining.

## Historiography

Ray *Indians in the Fur Trade* (Western Crees/Woodland Assiniboines)—Russell and Thistle later downplay early impact; Francis & Morantz *Partners in Furs* (E James Bay); Fowler bibliographic essay flags Ray on Canadian fur trade and Canadian works on numbered treaties and 1885. Contact-location debates for Western Woods Cree remain open. Plains 1885 narratives often subordinate Cree treaty politics to Métis/Riel drama—Fowler and Hoxie both stress intertwined but non-identical agendas (large-scale Cree military support never fully materialized).
