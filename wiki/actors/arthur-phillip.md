# Arthur Phillip

**Arthur Phillip** (1738–1814) was the first Governor of [[new-south-wales-colony|New
South Wales]] and commander of the [[first-fleet-1788|First Fleet]]. A Royal Navy captain,
he was selected in part because he had previously been employed by the Portuguese to
transport convicts to Brazil (CHA vol. 1, ch. 3) — experience with penal logistics on
unfamiliar maritime routes that the Pitt administration considered directly relevant.

## Role and Major Decisions

Phillip's **Instructions** (issued 25 April 1787) envisaged a subsistence agricultural
colony that would transform convicts into farmers. Initial land grants were set at 30
acres for a man, 20 more for a wife, and 10 per child, with tools, seed grain, and
rations for two years. Women were included from the start "though as helpmeets rather
than subjects in their own right" (CHA vol. 1, ch. 4).

On arrival at [[botany-bay|Botany Bay]] in January 1788, Phillip found the bay
unsuitable — inadequate shelter and insufficient fresh water — and relocated the fleet to
**Sydney Cove** in Port Jackson, the site that would become [[sydney|Sydney]].
He simultaneously sent Lieutenant Philip Gidley King with 22 men to **Norfolk Island**
to secure it as a British possession.

Phillip was "a cautious and diligent man, and implemented his orders to the letter." He
initiated public farms at **Parramatta** (1788) and **Toongabbie**, laid out a
grid-patterned township at Parramatta, and tested individual farming with the transported
Cornish farmer James Ruse. After Ruse's success, Phillip began establishing ex-convict
families on small farms around Parramatta, issuing tools, seed, and rations.

He pursued diplomacy with the **Eora** — the coastal Aboriginal peoples of Sydney — through
a long process that involved kidnapping, retribution (Phillip himself took a spear
through the shoulder), and eventual reconciliation, culminating in Bennelong's group
"coming in" to the settlement in November 1790. Bennelong's square brick house, built
at his own request, stood on Sydney Cove's eastern headland.

Phillip's departure from the colony in late 1792 — unwell and "perhaps demoralised" by
false reports of failure reaching England — marked a turning point: military officers
began acquiring large land grants, and the assignment system took root.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Phillip's decision to relocate from Botany Bay to Port Jackson, the colony's
geography and trajectory might have been markedly different; Sydney's deep-water harbour
was fundamental to its commercial development. Without his insistence on the small-farm
vision for ex-convicts (against officer pressure for large grants), the early
agricultural decades would have been dominated by officer-grantees rather than emancipist
farmers from the outset. The emancipist social character of the colony — which Bigge's
1823 report was designed to curtail — owed much to the framework Phillip established.
