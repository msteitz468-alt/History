## Overview

Ecgfrith was the son of [[oswiu-of-northumbria|Oswiu]] and Eanflaed (daughter of [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]]) and the last of the great Northumbrian war-kings. He succeeded his father probably in early 671, ruled for fifteen years, and was killed on 20 May 685 fighting the Picts at the [[battle-of-nechtansmere-685|battle of Nechtansmere]] (Dunnichen). The defeat shattered Northumbrian overlordship north of the Forth and was understood by Bede as an overwhelming catastrophe ending the age of Northumbrian supremacy.

## Birth and Chronology

Ecgfrith was given as a ten-year-old hostage to [[penda-of-mercia|Penda]] before the battle of the Winwæd (655/656), placing his birth c. 645/646. "Ecgfrith was in his fortieth year in 685 (HE III, 15)," consistent with a birth c. 645. The Jarrow dedication inscription, recording a consecration on 23 April 685 (a Sunday) in Ecgfrith's fifteenth year, and the council of Hatfield proceedings (Ecgfrith styled rex Humbronensium, in his tenth year on 17 September 679 or 680) together show that he succeeded no earlier than 17 September 670, pointing to his accession in early 671 rather than February 670. Kirby concludes: "It certainly cannot be regarded as out of the question that Oswiu died and Ecgfrith became king in the early months of 671."

## Reign

Ecgfrith served as sub-king of Deira in Oswiu's last years. As king he suppressed a Pictish revolt c. 672 with the help of his sub-king Beornhaeth "probably between the Avon and the Carron rivers (in Manau Gododdin)" — associated with the expulsion of Drest, king of the Picts (AU s.a. 671). Building on this, he likely imposed lordship on the Dalriadic Scots after 672.

He expelled Bishop Wilfrid from York in 678, triggering a partition of the Northumbrian diocese by Archbishop Theodore. The council of Hatfield (679/680) records him as rex Humbronensium — "king of the Humbrians" — reflecting the older regional designation still current in the late seventh century.

In 684 he sent his army under dux Berht (probably Berhtred, son of Beornhaeth) to ravage Ireland, including Uí Néill territory of Meath (HE IV, 26). In 685, against the advice of Cuthbert and others, he invaded Pictland through Strathmore into the kingdom of Circinn (between the Isla and the Dee), where Bridei son of Bili defeated and killed him at Nechtansmere. The defeat reversed Northumbrian expansion north of the Forth: "the Picts recovered their land which the Angles had held and the Scots who lived in Britain and certain of the Britons (perhaps of Strathclyde) their independence (HE IV, 26)."

## Ecclesiastical Affairs

Ecgfrith quarrelled with Wilfrid and expelled him in 678 — possibly because Wilfrid's retinue of armed followers was "considered by Ecgfrith's second queen, Iurminburh, to be the equal of a king's (Vita Wilfridi, ch. 24)." His first wife was Aethelthryth, widowed daughter of Anna king of the East Angles (married c. 660); she later founded the monastery of Ely. His second queen was Iurminburh. Despite the Wilfrid dispute, Ecgfrith cooperated with Archbishop Theodore in the reorganisation of the northern church: the appointments of Bosa to York, Eata to Bernicia, and Trumwine as bishop of the Picts all suited his interests.

## Counterfactual Significance

Ecgfrith's death at Nechtansmere without a legitimate son ended the direct succession and opened a period of dynastic instability. His raids on Ireland in 684 may have contributed to the creation of a Pictish–Scottish alliance that destroyed him. His expulsion of Wilfrid introduced decades of ecclesiastical controversy into Northumbrian politics.

## Historiography

Kirby's main contributions are the chronological argument for 671 (not 670) as accession year, and the caution about Bede's "imperial" view of Northumbrian history, which tended to retrospectively attribute to earlier reigns domination that was only fully exercised under Ecgfrith. The Jarrow inscription provides a rare fixed epigraphic anchor for Northumbrian regnal chronology.
