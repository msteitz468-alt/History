# The Qing Dynasty (the Manchu Empire) (1636/1644–1912)

China's **last imperial dynasty** and one of its longest and largest — a **Manchu** (originally
Jurchen) conquest dynasty from beyond the Great Wall that ruled China for nearly three
centuries, built the empire to its greatest territorial extent, and fell in the 1911 Revolution.
From John Keay's [[keay-china-a-history|*China: A History*]].

## Manchu origins: Nurhaci and the Banners

The Qing arose among the **Jurchen** of the forests and farms of Manchuria (the north-east).
**Nurhaci** (r. 1616–26) welded the Jurchen and Mongol clans into a fighting force organized
under the **Banners** — color-coded military-social units (eventually eight) that absorbed
Jurchen, Mongol, and Han alike and became "the privileged backbone of Manchu society." He
imposed the distinctive **shaven forecrown and the queue**, took Liaodong, and in 1616 had
himself enthroned, reviving the dynastic name **Jin**. His son **Hong Taiji** (r. 1626–43)
transformed the realm into a would-be empire: campaigning into Mongolia and Korea, adopting
artillery, employing **Han bureaucrats**, claiming the title of Mongol **Great Khan** and (via
the Dalai Lama) Buddhist *cakravartin*, and in **1636 renaming the people "Manchu" and the
dynasty "Qing"** ("pure") — a universalist title transcending region and positioning the dynasty
in the legitimate all-China succession.

## The conquest of China (1644)

When the [[ming-dynasty|Ming]] fell to the rebel **Li Zicheng** in 1644, the Ming general **Wu
Sangui** opened the Great Wall to the Manchus, who seized **Beijing**. The conquest was directed
not by an emperor but by the regent **Prince Dorgon** for the boy **Shunzhi emperor** — and
later consolidated under the great **Kangxi emperor** (r. 1661–1722). Keay stresses the Manchus'
**dual policy**: conciliation (amnesties, reinstating officials, restoring the examinations and
Han bureaucracy — "We are of one family") alongside terror. The notorious **queue order (1645)**
— commanding all men to adopt the Manchu hairstyle on pain of death — provoked fierce resistance
(many chose suicide or exile), and the brutal **Yangzhou massacre** crushed the "Southern Ming"
loyalists. Kangxi made **accommodation with his Han subjects the keystone** of his long reign.

## The High Qing and the fall

Under **Kangxi** and the **Qianlong emperor** (r. 1735–95), the Qing reached its zenith: a
century of prosperity, population growth, and cultural patronage, and the **conquest of the
largest empire in Chinese history** — incorporating Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang (the "New
Territories"). As the dynasty "coeval with Europe's global expansion" (Keay), it received the
British **Macartney embassy (1793)** and rebuffed it. The nineteenth century brought
catastrophe: the **[[opium-wars|Opium Wars]]** and the "unequal treaties," the devastating
**[[taiping-rebellion|Taiping]]** and other rebellions, the loss of tributaries and territory,
and failed [[self-strengthening|self-strengthening]] — the "death throes of empire." The Qing
fell in the **[[fall-of-the-qing-1911|Revolution of 1911]]**, ending over two thousand years of
imperial rule; the boy "Last Emperor" Puyi abdicated in 1912. (The dynasty's modern
crisis and fall are treated in detail by Spence and the Cambridge volumes.)

## Significance: a conquest dynasty and the "New Qing" debate

The Qing is the bridge from imperial to modern China: it set the **borders of the modern
Chinese state**, ruled a vast multi-ethnic empire, and bequeathed the trauma of nineteenth-
century foreign domination. Keay (and recent "New Qing History") stresses its character as a
**Manchu, Inner-Asian conquest empire** — not simply a "sinicized" Chinese dynasty but one that
ruled Mongols, Tibetans, and Uighurs through distinct institutions while governing China proper
in Confucian style.
