# Iolo Morganwg (Edward Williams)

Welsh poet, antiquary, literary forger, and political radical (1747–1826), born Edward Williams in Glamorgan; "Iolo Morganwg" ("Ned of Glamorgan") was his bardic name. He was the decisive figure in making the Druids central to **Welsh** national identity. In 1792, on Primrose Hill in London, he staged the first **Gorsedd of the Bards** (Gorsedd Beirdd Ynys Prydain), presenting it as a continuous survival of an ancient Druidic-bardic order — in fact his own invention, supported by a vast body of forged manuscripts and a fabricated body of Druidic theology and lore.

Iolo enabled the Welsh to claim the Druids for themselves and place them at the heart of their cultural identity; the Gorsedd was later grafted onto the national Eisteddfod, where it survives. In Hutton's analysis he is one of three pivotal catalysts of the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]], and the archetype of the charismatic "romancer" whose forgeries proved more culturally productive than honest scholarship.

## Significance

### The Gorsedd and Welsh National Appropriation

Iolo Morganwg's decisive contribution was not merely to invent a body of pseudo-historical Druidic lore, but to embed his invention in an institution with living ceremonial force. His **Gorsedd of the Bards of the Isle of Britain** (Gorsedd Beirdd Ynys Prydain), first enacted on Primrose Hill in London in 1792, presented itself as the continuous survival of an ancient order of Druid-bards; in Hutton's analysis, it was 'in fact his own invention, supported by a vast body of forged manuscripts and a fabricated body of Druidic theology and lore.' The three grades of ancient Druidry that Iolo "recovered" — Druid, Bard, and Ovate — along with the motto *Y Gwir yn erbyn y Byd* ('The Truth Against the World'), the 'mystic sign' of three diverging rays, and the ritual of the unsheathed sword laid down in token of peace all became the constitutive symbols of the ceremony he bequeathed to Wales.

What made the invention socially operative was Iolo's insertion of the Gorsedd into regional *eisteddfodau* in south Wales from the 1810s onward. He planted ceremonies at Newbridge (later Pontypridd), and allied himself in later life with the Bishop of St David's and with the leaders of the Cambrian cultural societies — a pragmatic accommodation with institutional patronage that ensured his forms would survive him. As Hutton summarises: 'by the end of the century he had supplied some of the main concepts and expressions of modern Welsh nationhood.'

### Iolo's Theology and Invented Sources

Iolo's synthetic "Druidic" theology centred on a graduated cosmology of reincarnation through spheres of existence, a benevolent creator deity revealed through the natural world, and an ethic of peace, justice, and universal love expressed in fabricated triads attributed to the medieval bards. His legendary founding figure was **Hu Gadarn**, an invented hero whom he presented as the founder of Welsh culture and the original civiliser of humanity — an assertion later exposed as forgery by Thomas Stephens (1848) and David Nash (1858), who showed the relevant texts could not predate the twelfth century. His invented bardic alphabet, *Coelbren y Beirdd*, and his 'Iolo Manuscripts' — published posthumously by his son Taliesin Williams (1840, 1848) and later as *Barddas* (1862, edited by John Williams ab Ithel) — were long presented as editions of authentic manuscripts, with the publication of *Barddas* completing the posthumous propagation of his system to English readers.

### Propagation through Taliesin Williams (1787–1847) and the Industrial South

Iolo was succeeded, in the Gorsedd's stewardship, by his son **Taliesin Williams** (bardic name: Taliesin ab Iolo). From his base at Merthyr Tydfil — chosen shrewdly, Hutton notes, as 'the largest single concentration of Welsh people on earth' and the engine of Welsh industrialisation — Taliesin ran the principal school of the district, won the bardic chair for poetry at the Royal Eisteddfod of 1834 (with a poem expounding his father's pseudo-history), organised gorsedd ceremonies at the rocking stone above Pontypridd, and forged a crucial alliance with Augusta Hall and Lady Charlotte Guest as cultural patrons. His 'transparent honesty and sincerity, and ability to win and keep friends — all qualities in which Iolo himself had been lacking' enabled a measure of institutional acceptance Iolo had never enjoyed. At the autumn equinox following the 1834 Cardiff eisteddfod, Taliesin presided at a Pontypridd gorsedd at which Augusta Hall herself was initiated as one of the first Ovates.

### Welsh National Identity

The composite result of Iolo's programme was a powerful national legend identifying the Welsh as the people of Druidry *par excellence*. Welsh scholarly writers of the nineteenth century built on it to argue: (1) that ancient Druids had practised the one true monotheistic religion; (2) that Roman accounts of their barbarity were 'the most savage and unprincipled nation' to have 'polluted the earth' (Angharad Llwyd, 1833); (3) that medieval Welsh bards were the direct heirs of Druidic teaching (Iolo's triads providing the evidence); and (4) that an early British Church, blending Druidry with apostolic Christianity, had preceded and surpassed Roman Catholicism. As Hutton observes, 'the more that English writers ceased to regard Druidry as a valued part of their national heritage, the more incentive was provided for Wales to claim and honour it for itself.'

### Myfyr Morganwg and the Rocking Stone Circle

The most radical development of Iolo's system was undertaken by **Evan Davies** (bardic name: **Myfyr Morganwg**, 'the scholar of Glamorgan', fl. 1849–1888), a self-taught clockmaker, poet, and Independent preacher based at Pontypridd. Around 1849–50, Myfyr and his associates constructed a full-scale ritual complex around the rocking stone (*Yr Maen Chwyf*) on the eastern hillside above the town, built on Stukeley's (erroneous) plan of Avebury — 'a snake passing through a circle' — with inner and outer rings of stones and two avenues. He then claimed the title of **Archdruid of the Isle of Britain** (from 1852), declared his gorsedd to be supreme over all others, and gradually developed Iolo's framework into what Hutton characterises as 'something like an alternative religion', blending Welsh bardism, Hindu cosmology (from the *Asiatic Researches*), and the Hindu-Druid equation he derived from Myfyr's reading of Francis Wilford. Myfyr held solstice and equinox ceremonies at the stone throughout the 1850s–1880s, initiating both Welsh poets and American visitors.

### William Price (1800–1893) and the Cremation of Iesu Grist

The most famous product of the Pontypridd circle was **Dr William Price**, a radical Chartist, vegetarian, and self-styled Druid who in January 1884 attempted to cremate the body of his infant son, named 'Iesu Grist' (Jesus Christ), on a hilltop above Llantrisant. Acquitted when a sympathetic judge ruled that no law explicitly forbade cremation, Price's legal victory directly established the *de facto* legality of cremation in Britain; the first public crematorium followed within three months. Price himself was cremated, before 20,000 onlookers, on the same spot in 1893. He has become, Hutton remarks, 'one of the great Welshmen of all time'.

### The National Eisteddfod and Institutionalisation

The culminating triumph of Iolo's legacy was the [[actors/gorsedd-of-the-bards|Gorsedd of the Bards]]' integration into the **National Eisteddfod**. Ab Ithel's Llangollen eisteddfod of 1858 (despite its controversies over self-dealing and the disqualification of Thomas Stephens's prize essay on Madoc) set the precedent for a national event. A governing council was established in 1860, and the first National Eisteddfod was held at Aberdare in 1861. The archdruidship passed in 1880 to Clwydfardd (David Griffith), who brought the gorsedd into the revived National Eisteddfod association from 1881. By the 1890s, standard robes in bardic blue, Ovate green, and Druidical white — designed by Hubert Herkomer (Slade Professor at Oxford) — had been adopted; a permanent ornamental sword, breastplate, and Archdruid's crown of office were added; and permanent megalithic stone circles began to be erected for each event. At the Cardiff National Eisteddfod of 1899, the Gorsedd initiated its first Cornish member and received delegates from Brittany's newly founded Gorsedd Llydaw. By the time of Bangor 1902, the Gorsedd had been given supreme authority over the entire Eisteddfod movement: 'It had become what Iolo had designed it to be, the central institution of Welsh culture.'

## Historiography

Iolo Morganwg's forgeries were first publicly challenged by **Thomas Stephens** in *The Literature of the Kymry* (1848), which dated the key mystical poems of Taliesin and Myrddin to no earlier than the tenth century and found no genuine Druidic doctrine in them. **David Nash**'s *Taliesin* (1858) pressed the attack further, exposing 'archaeddon' (archdeacon, not 'the ark of Aeddon') and dismantling Hu Gadarn as a scholarly fiction. Their impact was partially blunted by Matthew Arnold (1867), who attacked Nash as 'too absolute' and praised the Myvyrian Archaiology (Iolo's forgeries and all), and by William Skene (1868), who allowed that some of the contested mystical poems might date from the sixth century.

The question of Iolo's character was shaped for Victorian readers by Elijah Waring's 1850 memoir, which presented him as a lovable but naïve eccentric 'incapable of any conscious duplicity' — 'completely missing the brilliant and unscrupulous deceiver' in Hutton's assessment. The systematic exposure of his forgeries was completed only by twentieth-century scholarship; Hutton's own *Blood and Mistletoe* (2009) provides the fullest modern assessment of the entire Iolo legacy and its institutional consequences.
