# Heinrich Brüning

Centre Party politician and Reich Chancellor (March 1930 – May 1932), whose
government by presidential emergency decree under Article 48 began the
de-parliamentarization of the [[weimar-republic|Weimar Republic]], and whose
deflationary response to the [[great-depression|Great Depression]] deepened the
crisis in which Nazism grew.

## Role and Decisions

Per Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]): Centre floor leader,
appointed by [[hindenburg-paul-von|Hindenburg]] after the Grand Coalition fell
(27 March 1930) to head a "cabinet of experts" resting on Article 48 decree
power rather than a Reichstag majority — the SPD excluded from the outset. A
former officer shocked by the November Revolution and "a staunch monarchist
all his life" (though Evans doubts the retrospective coherence of his
memoirs' restoration claims), his instincts were authoritarian: he planned to
combine the Chancellorship with the Prussian Minister-Presidency to expel the
SPD from Prussia, lacking only Hindenburg's backing. His March 1931 press
decree had up to 100 newspaper editions banned monthly by mid-1931.

**Deflation.** Emergency decrees (5 June, 6 October 1931; 8 December 1931
cutting wages to 1927 levels) slashed spending and unemployment support: by
late 1932 only 618,000 remained on unemployment insurance while over a million
had no support at all. He refused devaluation — partly to demonstrate to the
world that reparations caused genuine German suffering — and even after the
Danat Bank collapse (13 July 1931) forced suspension of gold convertibility,
refused reflation for fear of repeating 1923; Evans calls the inflation
trauma's grip on policy "probably the most disastrous" of its long-term
effects. Evans records (attributed) the argument of some historians that the
deflation was partly designed to weaken the unions and the SPD; Brüning hoped
to use unemployment to complete "his dismantling of the Weimar welfare state."
He was nicknamed "the Hunger Chancellor" and expected the Depression to last
until 1935.

**Fatal miscalculation.** Dissolving the Reichstag in 1930 to discipline the
SPD produced the Nazi breakthrough of [[reichstag-election-september-1930|14
September 1930]]. He banned political uniforms (7 Dec 1931, after the Boxheim
documents) and the SA outright (13 April 1932); on Hitler's legality pledge he
was acute: "If one declares that, having come to power by legal means, one
will then break the bounds of the law, that is not legality." His fall (30 May
1932) came through Hindenburg's circle: dependence on SPD toleration, the
army's resentment of the SA ban, and an eastern land-reform scheme the
landowner President read as socialism. His foreign-policy successes — the
Hoover Moratorium and groundwork for Lausanne (July 1932), plus disarmament
equality (Dec 1932) — arrived too late to help him.

**1933 epilogue** (Evans, Part 5): his doubts on the
[[enabling-act-23-march-1933|Enabling Act]] were assuaged by Hitler's
guarantees and the peace-or-war threat; he voted yes. Succeeding Kaas as
Centre leader in May 1933 he received "a pale imitation of the leadership
cult," turned the party's elected mandates into appointive ones, helped draft
Hitler's moderate 17 May foreign-policy speech — his phone tapped and mail
opened all the while — and presided over the party's self-dissolution on
5 July 1933 (see [[dissolution-of-german-parties-1933]]).

## Counterfactual Significance

Evans's material makes Brüning the pivot of the "de-parliamentarization before
Hitler" argument: presidential rule from 1930 accustomed elites and public to
government without the Reichstag, and deflation manufactured the misery on
which Nazi and Communist growth fed. Whether reflation in 1931–32 was
politically available (given the inflation trauma) remains the debated
counterfactual.

## Historiography

Evans (2003) is the primary ingested treatment; his skepticism toward
Brüning's memoirs ("lending retrospective coherence") is itself a noted
historiographical position in the Brüning literature.
