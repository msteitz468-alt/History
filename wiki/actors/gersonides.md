## Overview

Levi ben Gerson — known as Gersonides, or by the Hebrew acronym Ralbag — lived in Provence in the first half of the fourteenth century (died 1344). He is the greatest Jewish interpreter of Averroes and the central figure of the rationalist wing of medieval Jewish philosophy after [[maimonides]]. Adamson calls him "the Supercommentator" where [[ibn-rushd]] was "the Commentator": Gersonides wrote supercommentaries (commentaries on commentaries) on Averroes' paraphrases of Aristotle, focusing especially on logic and psychology ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

He also produced commentaries on books of the Bible, treatises on mathematics, significant contributions to astronomy (a crater on the moon is named "Rabbi Levi"), and his chief philosophical work, *The Wars of the Lord* (*Milḥamot ha-Shem*).

## The Wars of the Lord

The *Wars of the Lord* is one of the greatest works of medieval Jewish philosophy. Its method — exhaustively listing all positions on a topic, identifying problems with each, then declaring a reasoned conclusion — resembles both Averroes' commentaries and the Latin scholastic *disputatio* format. The title derives from Numbers 21:14, signaling Gersonides' intent to fight philosophical errors in service of truth.

The title also got him into trouble: a later critic sarcastically called it the *Wars Against the Lord*.

### Psychology: Against the Single Universal Intellect

Gersonides agrees with Averroes that there is a celestial Agent Intellect that activates human thought and gives forms to matter on earth. But he firmly rejects Averroes' notorious doctrine that all humanity shares a single intellect. He improves on Aquinas's objection: if Reuben and Simon share one intellect, then Reuben's intellect could use Simon's sensation as readily as Simon's could — making individuation of thought impossible (*Wars* §1.4).

Gersonides also fills a gap in previous philosophy: earlier thinkers focused on theoretical knowledge but never explained where practical knowledge comes from. He argues the Agent Intellect provides this too — it helps carpenters know how to build just as much as it helps biologists understand species (*Wars* §§1.4, 1.7).

### Divine Knowledge and the Future

Gersonides criticizes Maimonides' five-part account of how divine knowledge differs from human knowledge (*Guide*, discussed in *Wars* §3.2). On the question of whether God can know the infinite, Gersonides flatly denies it: to know something is to place it within a definition or limit; infinity by definition exceeds any such limit. Even humans can "know" infinity in the sense of understanding that bodies are indefinitely divisible, but this means grasping a formal principle, not embracing an actual infinite totality.

On foreknowledge, Gersonides argues that God and the Agent Intellect know the cosmic order and structure within which events occur, but do not know particular future events in full detail. To know that an individual will perform a specific act tomorrow and still leave open the possibility that they will not is incoherent — it would reduce God to making educated guesses (*Wars* §3). This is a version of Avicenna's position on divine knowledge.

This creates a tension with his own acknowledgment that astrologers, prophets, and dreamers can foretell particular events. Gersonides' resolution: astrology signifies the cosmic order; prophecy is conditional (if one thing happens, another follows); Joseph's interpretation of Pharaoh's dream is conditional — it is up to the Pharaoh to act on the forecast (*Wars* §2.2).

### Creation: Against Both Eternity and Creation from Nothing

Gersonides rejects both Averroes' (and possibly Maimonides') eternity of the universe and the standard theological creation ex nihilo. His own position: the universe has existed for a finite time but was created from a formless, utterly inert matter that pre-existed the created order (*Wars* §6.1). Creation from nothing is impossible because it presupposes a void prior to creation — and Aristotle showed void is impossible. Eternity is impossible for several reasons, including a striking original argument:

> "If an eternity of time had already elapsed, humankind would have had time to perfect itself and learn all possible knowledge, yet we see that this has not in fact occurred." (*Wars* §6.1, reconstructed)

This anticipates arguments about the implausibility of infinite elapsed time. Gersonides also argues that past events retain a kind of reality (otherwise statements about the past would be truth-valueless), which tells against treating past infinity as non-problematic.

On infinity generally, Gersonides denies that any knowledge — even God's — can truly embrace an actually infinite totality.

### Astronomy

Gersonides made genuine contributions to observational astronomy. He recognized that the standard Aristotelian–Ptolemaic cosmological picture was inconsistent: the same system could not coherently postulate both a cosmic center (Aristotle) and eccentric spheres (Ptolemy). His astronomical observations led him to propose original solutions.

## Relation to Maimonides

Gersonides represents the rationalist extension of Maimonides' project. He differs from Maimonides in:
- Rejecting creation ex nihilo in favor of creation from pre-existing matter
- Rejecting Maimonides' claim that divine knowledge extends to particulars
- Taking a more overtly Averroistic line throughout

But he shares Maimonides' conviction that philosophy and the Torah are ultimately compatible, and that the Jewish intellectual tradition demands rigorous philosophical engagement.

Samuel Ibn Tibbon (Maimonides' translator) and Gersonides represent the rationalist wing; [[hasdai-crescas]] represents a more critical response.

## Historiography

Gersonides was recognized in his own lifetime as a philosopher of the first rank; the supercommentary format he mastered was the cutting edge of fourteenth-century philosophical method. Adamson situates him as the "high-water-mark" of the Jewish reception of Averroes. His astronomical work has attracted more modern attention than his philosophy; scholars are now recovering the systematic ambition of the *Wars of the Lord*.
