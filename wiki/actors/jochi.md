# Jochi

## Overview

**Jochi** (c. 1179–c. 1227), eldest son of [[genghis-khan|Genghis Khan]] and [[borte|Börte]], was ancestor of the [[golden-horde|Jochid / Golden Horde]] line. Born after Börte’s Merkid captivity (Weatherford dates birth 1179), he was named Jochi—“visitor” or “guest.” Many scholars take the name as evidence Temüjin doubted paternity; Weatherford notes it could also mean guest of Jamuka’s band at birth. The debate “reverberated in Mongol politics for the next century.”

## Career (Weatherford 2004)

- **1203 marriage bid.** Temüjin’s proposal that Ong Khan’s daughter marry Jochi was the public test of favor over [[jamuka|Jamuka]]; refusal helped trigger the Kereyid war.
- **Post-1206 commands.** At decimal reorganization family units were smaller than loyal friends’ full *tümens*: Jochi received 9,000—more than [[chagatai-khan|Chagatai]]’s 8,000 or [[ogedei-khan|Ögödei]]/[[tolui|Tolui]]’s 5,000, but still under 10,000.
- **Sibir campaign (1207).** Age ~28, sent with his *tümen* into “Sibir” (origin of the name Siberia); returned with thousands of recruits, alliance marriages (including one with Jochi’s daughter), and tribute—black sable, hunting birds, forest products.
- **Succession *khuriltai*.** Before the [[khwarazmian-campaign-1219|Khwarazmian war]], Genghis asked Jochi to speak first on succession (signaling seniority). Chagatai exploded: “How could we allow ourselves to be ruled by this bastard son of a Merkid?” Brothers fought; Chagatai proposed compromise succession for the “mellow, good-natured, hard-drinking” Ögödei; Jochi endorsed it. Genghis ordered camps far apart: “Mother Earth is broad and her rivers and waters are numerous.”
- **Urgench.** Joint assignment with Chagatai; six-month siege; house-to-house fighting, fire, then dam and flood. Tension nearly became open fighting—Jochi fearing destruction of a city destined as his patrimony, Chagatai suspecting greed risked Mongol lives. Nothing remained for Jochi’s inheritance there.
- **Alienation and death.** After the victory hunt Jochi refused to join, claimed illness, organized rival hunts, and never met his father again. He died in the west while Genghis still lived; “leaving as much mystery surrounding his death as his birth.” Rumors that Genghis ordered his death survive without convincing evidence (Weatherford).

## Historiography

Paternity debate is both a *Secret History* narrative problem and a later political weapon among Chinggisids. Weatherford follows the traditional rescue timeline. Persian chroniclers sanitized or blanked the quarrel: Juvaini wrote it out completely; Rashid al-Din left blanks in crucial places—the *Secret History* preserved the confrontation. Jochi’s line (Batu) later strongly backed [[subedei|Sübe’etei]]’s European plan and allied with [[sorghaghtani-beki|Sorghaghtani]] against Ögödeid rivals; under [[kublai-khan|Khubilai]]’s Chinese ancestral cult Jochi was fully recognized among the four sons.
