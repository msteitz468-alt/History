# Nabonidus

**Nabonidus** (Akkadian *Nabu-na'id*, "Nabu is exalted"; reigned **555–539 BCE**) was
the **last king of the [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]]** — the pious,
antiquarian, and controversial ruler whose devotion to the moon-god **Sin**, decade-long
self-exile at the Arabian oasis of **Tema**, and estrangement from the Marduk
priesthood left Babylon divided when [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Persian]] took it in
**539** ([[fall-of-babylon-539bce]]). [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]]
(Ch. 27, D. J. Wiseman) is the authority.

## Accession

Not a member of the Chaldaean royal house, Nabonidus came to power as the head of the
**conspirators who killed [[neo-babylonian-empire|Labashi-Marduk]]** and was then
"unanimously chosen" to succeed (June 556). He may be the **Labynetus of Babylon**
who, per Herodotus, had mediated the **Medo-Lydian peace of 585** after the eclipse
on the Halys — which would make him an experienced statesman, "a good choice to
counter the rising power of Cyrus." His father was **Nabu-balatsu-iqbi**, "a learned
counsellor"; his formidable mother **Adad-guppi**, a devotee of Sin who lived past
101, claimed influence with the kings back to Nabopolassar. His son **Belshazzar**
(Bel-sarra-usur) may, through a marriage of Nabonidus to a daughter of Nebuchadnezzar,
lie behind the Daniel tradition that made Belshazzar Nebuchadnezzar's "son."

## The cult of Sin and the antiquarian king

Nabonidus is famous for **promoting the moon-god Sin** — naming him "divine king over
all the gods" — and above all for rebuilding **Ekhulkhul**, Sin's temple at **Harran**
(destroyed in the Medo-Babylonian sack of 609), his mother's special devotion.
Wiseman cautions against the standard picture of a fanatical monotheist of Sin: his
building inscriptions show he **restored the temples of Marduk, Shamash, and the
other great gods** as well, and dedicated his daughter **Ennigaldi-Nanna** as
*entum*-priestess at Ur in long Babylonian tradition. His celebrated **antiquarian
interest** — recovering the foundation inscriptions of ancient kings — "was not
unusual." Yet his administrative reforms (a royal commissioner and a "king's chest"
siphoning a fixed share of temple income) and his elevation of Sin antagonized the
**priesthood and citizens** of Babylon, Borsippa, Nippur, Ur, Uruk, and Larsa.

## The ten years at Tema

For about **ten years (his 7th–16th)** Nabonidus left Babylon and resided at the
Arabian oasis of **Tema**, controlling a 400-km chain of oasis towns as far as
**Yathrib (Medina)**, while his son **Belshazzar** ruled as regent in Babylon and
the **New Year (akītu) festival went uncelebrated**. Wiseman canvasses the competing
explanations without adjudicating: a **priest-led rebellion** over the Sin cult
(the reason given in the Harran stelae); the king's **health**; control of the rich
**Arabian trade routes** (spices, incense, gold); or a deliberate **westward
extension** of the empire. The episode is among the most debated in late Babylonian
history.

## The fall of Babylon

Returning to Babylon by his seventeenth year (following favourable astronomical
omens that "the appointed time had come"), Nabonidus restored Sin to Harran and
again celebrated the New Year festival in **539**. But [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]] —
already master of Media, Lydia, and Elam — now struck. As the gods of the outlying
cities were carried into Babylon for safety, Cyrus stormed **Opis** on the Tigris
with heavy slaughter; **Sippar fell without a battle** (Tashritu 14) and Nabonidus
fled; two days later (**13 October 539**) the Persian general **Ugbaru/Gobryas**
entered **Babylon without a battle**. **Belshazzar was slain**, and Nabonidus
surrendered. Berossus and the Dynastic Prophecy report that Cyrus **spared him** and
settled him in **Carmania**, where he died ([[fall-of-babylon-539bce]]).

proclaimed on taking the city.

## Historiography

Nabonidus is one of antiquity's most **contested** kings, because the surviving
testimony is overwhelmingly **hostile and partisan**: Cyrus's **"Verse Account"** and
the **Cyrus Cylinder** vilify him as an irreverent neglecter of Marduk to justify the
conquest; Daniel and later Jewish tradition recast his Tema exile and illness onto
Nebuchadnezzar (the "**Prayer of Nabonidus**" from Qumran preserves the original
attribution). His own **Harran stelae** and his mother's autobiography give the royal
version. Dandamaev (Ch. 28a) notes that the Babylonian texts charging famine and ruin
to his reign "demand critical evaluation," since some 3,000 economic documents attest
continuing prosperity. Wiseman's balanced reconstruction treats him as a **genuinely
religious traditionalist** whose reforms and absences, not impiety, undid him.
