# Emer de Vattel (1714–1767)

Swiss jurist and diplomat in Saxon service; author of *Le Droit des gens* (*The Law
of Nations*, 1758), the single most influential eighteenth-century treatise on
[[international-law]] and the standard legal reference of the age of
[[proprietorial-warfare|proprietary war]] — cited by statesmen from European
chancelleries to the American founders.

**Role in the law of war.** Vattel is the central publicist of what Whitman calls
the [[jus-victoriae]]. Writing within the [[natural-law]] tradition of Grotius and
Wolff but with a pragmatic, positivist bent, he treated war between sovereigns as a
formally lawful procedure: in a "regular war" between civilized states, *both*
sides are treated as lawful belligerents regardless of the justice of their cause
(the doctrine of "war in due form"), and the outcome — the
[[verdict-of-battle|verdict of battle]] — generates enforceable rights to
territory, booty, and settlement. This bracketing of just cause is precisely what
distinguished eighteenth-century practice from both medieval [[just-war]] doctrine
and the modern jus ad bellum, and what, on Whitman's argument, made wars limitable:
if neither side is a criminal, a compromise verdict is acceptable.

**Key doctrines (Whitman's reading).** Vattel coined "war in form" (*guerre en
forme*); held that every acquisition made in such a war "is valid under voluntary
international law, irrespective of the justice of the cause"; articulated the
doctrine of pretexts ("Pretexts are at least a sort of reparations that the
unjust pay to the just"); insisted war was *not* dueling ("In the frenzy of
duels, the only business is to display personal courage. But in war the object
is... by force to prosecute our rights") — hence stratagem was lawful, though
open battle-victory "procures... a greater and more permanent advantage"; solved
the sovereignty question by deferring to each polity's internal law (admitting
republics to lawful war); praised "contributions" over pillage as humane; and
treated a claim settled by victory and treaty as closed forever ("The effect of
a treaty is to put an end to the war and abolish the subject") — a finality
belied in practice by [[maria-theresa|Maria Theresa's]] relitigation of Silesia.
Kant dismissed him and Pufendorf as "peddlers of false comfort"; Whitman
partially defends him as having "more courage to dissent from eighteenth-century
orthodoxy than most."

**Counterfactual significance.** Modest as an original thinker (he popularized
Wolff), enormous as a codifier: without Vattel's synthesis, eighteenth-century
cabinet warfare would have lacked its shared legal grammar. His authority faded in
the nineteenth century as just-war moralism and positivist codification (Lieber,
Geneva, Hague) displaced the law of victory.
