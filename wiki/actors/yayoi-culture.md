# Yayoi Culture

The **Yayoi** culture of the Japanese Islands, **~1000 BC–250 AD**, brought wet-rice farming, bronze,
iron, weaving and continental contact to the archipelago — the agricultural revolution that
[[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes]] defines (an *economic*, not a ceramic, designation) and the
bridge from forager [[jomon-culture|Jomon]] to the [[kofun-culture|Kofun]] state. Ironically "Yayoi" is
a Tokyo place-name where the pottery was first found in the 1880s, though that ware formally dates to
~300 BC.

## A Package from the Peninsula

Initial Yayoi is "a 'package', derived from the southwestern Korean Peninsula" — the [[mumun-culture|
Mumun]] complex of wet-rice technology, **dolmen burial, ditched settlements, Songguk-ri pit-buildings
and pig rituals** plus polished stone reapers, grooved axes and spindle whorls (see
[[rice-agriculture-spread-east-asia]]). The earliest Japanese paddy fields date to **970 cal. BC**; the
technological transfer is now placed ~800–600 BC, with AMS dates pushing Yayoi's start as early as the
10th century BC (contested). The transfer happened by **bilateral acculturation**: at **Etsuji** and
**Shinmachi** in North Kyushu, locally-descended (Jomon-type) populations and immigrants are buried side
by side — circular Jomon-style grave arrangements beside linear "Peninsular" ones — so that "Jomon
villagers who adopted the Peninsular package became 'Yayoinised'." **Yayoi settlements were always newly
founded**; "there were no Jomon sites that became Yayoi sites."

## Expansion and Resistance

A North-Kyushu "incubator zone" generated a hybrid culture that spread through the Inland Sea to the
waist of Honshu (~400 cal. BC). The triangular reaping knife, harvesting only grain-heads, selected for
**cold-resistant** rice, slowly enabling northward spread; a few Japan-Sea-coast pioneers reached
northern Honshu but were abandoned before cold-climate japonica matured. Northeastern (Tohoku) Jomon
**resisted** wet rice, "Yayoinising" only in Middle Yayoi; Hokkaido (Epi-Jomon) and the Ryukyus stayed
outside the agricultural sphere though linked by trade (e.g. tropical **shell bracelets** moving north
from the Ryukyus). The "dual structure" hypothesis holds modern Japanese ancestry is ~65% continental,
mixing Jomon with two immigrant waves (early Yayoi and the 5th–6th century).

## Bronze, Iron and Han Contact

Bronze and iron followed rice from the Peninsula at the end of Early Yayoi (cast iron most likely from
the Zhou state of Yan; smithing — not smelting — from Middle Yayoi). Two **bronze foci** diverged: North
Kyushu (weapons interred with individuals as symbols of authority) and the Kinai/Eastern Seto (**bronze
bells** deposited in non-burial hilltop caches as community ritual objects — the Kojindani cache held 358
daggers, 16 halberds and 6 bells). From Middle Yayoi, Wa chieftains entered the [[han-dynasty|Han]]
orbit via [[lelang-commandery|Lelang]] (see [[yellow-sea-interaction-sphere]]): the *Houhanshu* records
Wa divided into 100+ *guo*, 30 in contact with the Han court; in **57 AD** the chief of **Na** received a
gold seal (unearthed near Fukuoka in 1784). When Han mirrors became inaccessible after Wang Mang's
usurpation, North Kyushu chiefly hierarchies collapsed, and Yayoi cast imitation mirrors — but the
**iron trade** with southern Korea kept the strait busy, iron swords becoming the preferred burial good
in Late Yayoi mounds. These low mounds, and the disturbances ("Wa no Hanran," 147–189) that produced
**Queen Himiko**, lead directly into the Kofun period.
