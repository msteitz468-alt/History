## Role and Significance

Walther Rathenau (1867–1924) was a German industrialist, intellectual, and statesman, head of the AEG electrical conglomerate and founder of the German War Raw Materials Office (Kriegsrohstoffabteilung, KRA) in 1914. He later served as Foreign Minister of the [[weimar-republic]] until his assassination by right-wing nationalists on 24 June 1922. Source: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18.

## War Raw Materials Office (1914–1915)

When war broke out in August 1914, Germany's decentralized Bismarckian constitution left no mechanism for centralized procurement of raw materials. Rathenau took the initiative for the private industrialists, launching "an improvised system of committees to organize the purchase and allocation of key raw materials." The resulting KRA became the most admired organizational innovation of the German war effort — though German practitioners were critical of it, comparing it unfavorably to Lloyd George's more centralized British system.

The KRA became internationally famous as an emblem of "organized capitalism": the young Bolshevik theorist Bucharin (writing from Swiss exile) described the German war economy as "a new leviathan before which the fantasy of Thomas Hobbes seems child's play." Lenin drew on the KRA as an inspiration for socialist planning in Russia. Rathenau himself, who was Jewish, wrote in one of his "Delphic essays" that "the economy was Germany's fate" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18) — a remark that summarized the shift toward national economic consciousness triggered by the war.

## Weimar Foreign Minister

As Foreign Minister of the Weimar Republic, Rathenau negotiated the Treaty of Rapallo (April 1922) with Soviet Russia — Germany's first postwar international agreement — which normalized relations and included a renunciation of mutual financial claims. He was assassinated on 24 June 1922 by members of Organisation Consul, a right-wing terrorist network, in a targeted killing that illustrated the lethal hostility of völkisch nationalists to the Republic's Jewish leadership.

## Historical Significance

Rathenau's career illustrates the paradox of German Jewish patriotism in the World War I era: a Jewish industrialist who created the organizational backbone of the German war effort, yet who remained a target of anti-Semitic agitation throughout the war (the Centralverein and Jewish community desperately hoped that service would produce acceptance, but anti-Semitic associations from the start excluded Jews from the Burgfrieden). His assassination exemplified how, after 1918, anti-Semitism shifted "from a mere 'cultural code' ... into the recurring practice of physical violent attacks against Jews on behalf of the Volksgemeinschaft" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 17).
