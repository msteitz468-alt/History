## Overview

Aḥmad Yasawī (d. 1166) is the first known poet of mystical wisdom in the Turkic language and the founding figure of the Yasawiyya Sufi order, which spread Sufi practice across the Turkish areas of Central Asia. His *Ḥikam* (Wisdom Sayings) — written in the Turkic dialect of the area of Yassi (modern Türkistan, Kazakhstan) — are the earliest documented corpus of Sufi verse in any Turkic language, and as such they stand at the origin of an immense tradition extending through [[yunus-emre|Yūnus Emre]] and the entire Turkish mystical poetry tradition.

Schimmel describes him as "the first great representative of 'Turkish' mystical literature" and notes that he was "a disciple of Yūsuf Hamadhānī, thus springing from the same spiritual root as the Naqshbandiyya, who were to become so powerful in the Turkish areas of Central Asia and in Southeast Asia" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Context and Significance

Yasawī worked in the area of Yassi (Yasi, later renamed Türkistan) in what is now southern Kazakhstan, during the period when the steppe frontiers of the Islamic world were seeing the conversion of Turkic nomadic and semi-nomadic populations. The *Ḥikam* served as a vehicle for transmitting Sufi doctrine in a vernacular accessible to Turkic-speaking audiences not literate in Arabic or Persian.

His spiritual lineage — through Yūsuf Hamadhānī — connects him to the same root tradition that produced the Naqshbandiyya order, the most important Sufi order in Central Asian and Turkish history. The Naqshbandiyya emphasized sobriety, presence with God in everyday life, and the *silent dhikr* — emphases that contrast with the ecstatic traditions but share with Yasawī a commitment to reaching non-elite audiences.

## The Yasawiyya Order

The *ṭarīqa* (Sufi order) that developed from Yasawī's disciples, the Yasawiyya, spread across Central Asia and into the Mongol steppe, surviving the Mongol conquest and providing a framework for continued Islamization of Turkic populations. It eventually gave rise to important sub-branches and merged with other orders, but maintained a distinctive tradition of Turkish-language Sufi poetry and practice.

## Relationship to Yūnus Emre

The Anatolian Turkish Sufi tradition that produced [[yunus-emre|Yūnus Emre]] partly continued Yasawī's legacy. Schimmel notes that the Turkish Sufi circles in which Yūnus grew up "partly continued the tradition of Aḥmad Yasawī" while also being stimulated by mystical emigrants from eastern Iran fleeing the Mongol conquest. The "Emre" circles of Anatolian mystics — of which Tapduq Emre (Yūnus's master) was a leading member — represent this confluence of the eastern steppe-Sufi tradition with the more sophisticated Persian-influenced Sufism reaching Anatolia.

## Historiography

Yasawī's historical figure is relatively better attested than many early Sufi poets, though the authenticity of specific poems attributed to him is debated. The primary scholarly reference is Mehmet Fuat Köprülü's foundational study *Türk edebiyatında ilk mutasavvıflar* (First Mystics in Turkish Literature, 2nd ed., Ankara, 1966), which established the historical framework for the origins of Turkish mystical literature.
