# Hatshepsut

The **female pharaoh** of the [[new-kingdom-egypt|Eighteenth Dynasty]] (ruled as king
~1473–1458 BCE) — daughter of Thutmose I, wife of Thutmose II, and stepmother and
co-regent of **[[thutmose-iii|Thutmose III]]**, whom she eclipsed for two decades
before being erased from the record after her death. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. IX, Hayes).

## From regent to king

On the death of **Thutmose II**, his only son [[thutmose-iii|Thutmose III]] — born to
a harem-girl, Isis — was a child, so the dowager queen Hatshepsut became regent. At
first she appeared merely behind her stepson with her queenly titles; but late in his
second regnal year, "stressing the purity of her royal ancestry and relying on a
group of powerful officials," she had herself **crowned king** with full pharaonic
titulary — *the Female Horus Maatkare Hatshepsut*. She justified the seizure with the
reliefs of her **Deir el-Bahri** temple: scenes of her **divine birth** (Amun
fathering her on the queen-mother) and a fictitious coronation under Thutmose I.
Thutmose III remained nominal co-regent throughout, events still dated to his reign.

## The régime and its expeditions

Hatshepsut's ~15 years were peaceful and prosperous, dominated by her great
administrator **Senenmut** (Chief Steward of Amun and of the king) and a circle
including the High Priest of Amun **Hapuseneb**. In the absence of major war her
showpiece achievements were a **trading expedition to Punt** (the incense-land on the
Red Sea — depicted vividly at Deir el-Bahri, the chief Perehu and his famously
corpulent wife Eti, ships loaded with myrrh-trees, gold, ivory, ebony, and apes) and
the quarrying and erection of two pairs of giant **granite obelisks** at Karnak. Her
reliance on **[[amun-priesthood|Amun]]** — whose oracle "commanded" the Punt voyage —
exemplifies the political weight the Karnak priesthood had acquired.

## The erasure

Around 1458 Hatshepsut disappeared (perhaps a natural death; her fate is unknown) and
[[thutmose-iii|Thutmose III]] became sole king. Late in *his* reign her **statues
were smashed, her obelisks walled around, and her name and figure chiselled out** of
monuments throughout Egypt; later king-lists ignore her, and Manetho preserves her
only as a garbled "Amensis." The erasure was once read as the personal vengeance of a
resentful stepson; CAH treats it more soberly as a **dynastic/ideological** act —
removing an irregular female interruption from the legitimate male succession — and
notes it was done years after her death, not in hot blood.

## Shaw (2000): legitimation by bloodline, and the timing of the erasure

Betsy Bryan's chapter sharpens two points. First, on **legitimation**: Hatshepsut "did not
attempt to legitimize her reign by claiming to have ruled with or for her husband Thutmose II.
Instead she emphasized her blood line" — her only clear model being the earlier female king
Sobekneferu. An Aswan inscription of [[hatshepsut|her steward Senenmut]] catches the transition
in progress, naming her still as "king's daughter, king's sister, god's wife, great royal
wife" before she took a throne name. Her **building programme** dwarfed her predecessors'
(Karnak's Eighth Pylon — the first stone pylon on the north–south axis — plus work at Luxor,
Medinet Habu, and the Mut precinct), and her [[places/thebes|Deir el-Bahri]] terraced temple,
modelled on [[actors/mentuhotpe-ii|Mentuhotep II's]] adjacent monument, was "her most complete
statement in material form about her reign."

Second, on the **erasure**: Bryan stresses it came only "around year 46 or 47" of Thutmose
III — some twenty-five years after Hatshepsut's disappearance — and was completed by
[[actors/amenhotep-ii|Amenhotep II]]. "Since Thutmose sought to destroy the memory of the
queen twenty-five years after her disappearance, it is unlikely that this was carried out as
pure vengeance against his stepmother, particularly since the king had retained a number of
Hatshepsut's officials." The likelier motive was **political**: securing the succession of
Amenhotep II by erasing a rival royal line. Her **Punt expedition** was promoted as "a major
diplomatic coup" that "stimulated interest in exotic luxury goods" (see [[places/punt]]).

## Counterfactual / analytical significance

Hatshepsut is the most fully realized of the New Kingdom's powerful royal women (the
line that runs from **Tetisheri, Ahhotep, and [[ahmose-i|Ahmose-Nefertari]]**),
the rare woman to claim the full kingship rather than rule as queen-regent. Her reign
shows both the **possibility and the limits** of female pharaonic power: she could
hold the throne for fifteen prosperous years, but the ideology of kingship could not
finally accommodate her, and the record closed over her. Whether her "weak" avoidance
of war (so framed by her impatient successor) cost Egypt ground in Asia is debated —
Thutmose III's immediate post-Hatshepsut campaign suggests the empire's military
readiness had in fact been preserved.

## Historiography

Documented above all by her own **Deir el-Bahri temple** and its propaganda reliefs,
plus the monuments of Senenmut and her partisans — all of it tendentious. The
deliberate **erasure** is itself a major source-problem, and her absence from the
king-lists long obscured her. Modern scholarship has discarded Sethe's elaborate
reconstruction of the Tuthmoside succession. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
