## Overview

The Recuay culture (c. 300–200 BCE to CE 600–700?) was the principal ERD tradition of the **north Peruvian highlands**, centred in the large intermontane basin of the **Callejón de Huaylas** — the productive valley between the Cordillera Blanca (east) and Cordillera Negra (west) in what is now Ancash, Peru — and the upper reaches of the eastern and western cordilleras. Recuay is distinguished by:

1. A tradition of **stone sculpture** in the [[chavin]] legacy, including warrior statues, lintels, stelae, and tenon-heads.
2. **Tricolour ceramics** in fine kaolin paste with resist ("negative") painting.
3. An expansive strategy competing with [[moche]] and Gallinazo for the *yunka* (inter-valley warm zone) belt between highlands and coast.

Shimada's chapter in [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]] acknowledges that Recuay "has remained quite obscure. Intermittent surveys and excavations conducted thus far have been largely unrelated to each other, and their results remain largely unpublished" — making it among the least documented of the major north Peruvian ERD cultures.

## Territory and Vertical Strategy

Unlike the [[moche]] polity's "horizontality" (lateral coastal expansion), Recuay practised a **downward thrust** into the western yunka: "the contemporaneous Recuay downward thrust emphasized the western yunka of the southern north coast (particularly between the Virú and Nepeña valleys)." This zone (typically 300–2,300 m above sea level) offered year-round sun and moisture, crops including maize, chili pepper, and potent coastal coca (*Erythroxylum novogranatense* var. *truxillense*), copper and iron oxide mines, San Pedro cactus for shamanistic use, and crucially, the apex of coastal valley irrigation canal intakes.

"Coastal populations sought to establish control over the yunka zone and keep highland populations from encroaching. It is a zone of critical resources and has long been the zone of contention, for example, between Recuay and Mochica."

## Competition with Mochica and Gallinazo

The Santa Valley provides the clearest evidence of Recuay-coastal interaction. Prior to Mochica expansion (Mochica Phase III, c. CE 350), "the local Gallinazo population focused in the lower to middle valley appears to have had a tense coexistence with Recuay settlers in the middle to upper valley, where their distinct, kaolin ceramics were abundant." When the Mochica moved south: Wilson's survey found "not only a major reduction in the number and extent of Recuay ceramics but also a new settlement pattern that emphasized the lower valley … the Mochica seems to have subjugated the local Gallinazo population and driven the Recuay settlers out of the mid-valley."

In the Nepeña Valley, "Recuay efforts to maintain a foothold in the yunka zone" also persisted. The competitive relationship is further evidenced by the "scarcity of north coast artifacts in the Recuay heartland of Callejón de Huaylas" and the depiction in Mochica painted battle scenes of "defeated warriors with distinct hair style, headdresses, and arms (e.g., star-shaped mace heads) … interpreted as being of Recuay ethnicity."

Art-style analysis confirms limited cultural exchange: "art styles of these cultures show only a few shared motifs. The popularity of the 'Moon Animal' (also called 'Recuay Dragon') in Mochica ceramic art was largely confined to Phases I and II, when the Mochica style emulated various other styles. The emulation was, however, without a clear understanding of the Recuay artistic canons."

## Stone Sculpture

Recuay stone-carving encompasses:

1. **Heads and tenon-heads** of "warriors" and "pumas."
2. **Small square tablets** (up to 50 cm per side) with animals, geometric forms, or mythical creatures in low relief.
3. **"Lintels"** up to 2.7 m long, typically showing opposing pairs of "pumas" (bodies in profile, heads frontal) or a mythical being flanked by pumas.
4. **Stelae** up to 3 m high, often depicting a naked, splayed supernatural being.
5. **Statues** up to 1.5 m high of seated men or women, hands on knees.

Male warrior statues "often stand in full military regalia, including war clubs, shields, elaborate headdresses, round ear spools, necklaces, and trophy heads." The sculptural tradition "undoubtedly derived from the preceding [[chavin]]," though "iconography on statues and ceramics is quite distinct from that of Chavin." An important site is Pashash, which featured underground chambers, galleries, and stone buildings. Most surviving sculptures have been removed from original architectural contexts, impeding functional analysis.

## Ceramics

Recuay ceramics are made in **fine kaolin paste** (shared with the Cajamarca tradition further north) and decorated with "conventionalized motifs painted in two or three colors (mostly red or brown and black)." A distinguishing technique is **resist (negative) painting**: vessels are covered first with white slip, then red slip, fired; designs are applied in organic resist paint and fired again. This technique was shared with contemporaneous Vicús and Gallinazo on the north coast, and with [[lima-culture]] on the central coast.

Vessel forms show "considerable variation," including:

- Double-chambered whistling bottles
- Complex effigy vessels depicting "warriors" with shields and elaborate headdresses, accompanied by llamas
- Jars with flange mouths, many with modelled human heads on their shoulders
- Bowls emphasising exterior surface decoration (unlike Cajamarca, which emphasised interior decoration)
- Jars with quadrangular bodies and modelled scenes of a "centrally placed 'leader' encircled by smaller figures of presumably lower rank or elaborate multistory building and occupants"

These scenic representations convey "religious or political importance" but "are not rendered in two-dimensional painting as in the case of Mochica art." The modelling "emphasizes conventionalized, static forms unlike the full sculptural, naturalistic modeling of Mochica art." A shared motif with Cajamarca and Mochica is the "Moon Animal" / "Recuay Dragon" — "a mythical, four-legged creature with a long curled projection above the snout" found in association with the crescent moon and stars in Mochica contexts.

## Vertical Economy

North Highland populations including Recuay "appear to have practiced some degree of vertical control," though the role of llama caravans "remains unclear." The Callejón de Huaylas is bounded by the snow-capped Cordillera Blanca, restricting eastern-escarpment access. Recuay downward pressure was directed primarily westward into the yunka, seeking "coca, maize and chili pepper … mines of copper, iron oxides, and silver and stands of hallucinogenic San Pedro cactus."

## Sociopolitical Organisation

Shimada does not assign a firm state or chiefdom designation to Recuay. The complexity of their stone architecture at Pashash and the territorial competition with Mochica implies organised, multi-community polity structure. The stone-sculpture tradition — mobilising significant labour for monumental carving — parallels other ERD chiefdom/early-state practices but the settlement-pattern data needed to resolve political organisation are absent.

## Decline

The Mochica expansion (Phases III–IV, c. CE 350–500) effectively expelled Recuay from the Santa and Nepeña valleys. By Phase IV, when the Southern Mochica polity "held sway over much of the north coast, corresponding Middle Cajamarca A ceramics were rare and confined to the upper reaches of coastal valleys" — a parallel reduction likely affecting Recuay access as well. After the Mochica collapse and Wari Middle Horizon expansion (c. CE 600–700), Recuay's distinct ceramic tradition fades from the archaeological record, though the terminal date is uncertain ("c. CE 600–700?").

## Historiography

Recuay scholarship is thin by comparison with the Mochica or Nasca. Shimada lists R. P. Schaedel's 1948 characterisation and D. Eisleb's 1987 photographic compilation of ceramics as the principal syntheses, alongside R. X. Reichert's doctoral thesis and the iconographic study by H. Tschopik Jr. Mochica-Recuay art-style interaction is addressed by C. B. Donnan. The key bibliographic problem is that most relevant excavations "have been largely unrelated to each other, and their results remain largely unpublished."
