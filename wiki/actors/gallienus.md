# Gallienus

**Publius Licinius Egnatius Gallienus** (c. 218–268, r. 253–268; sole ruler from 260) was the
emperor at the **nadir of the [[third-century-crisis|third-century crisis]]** — the years of his
father [[valerian]]'s capture by Persia, the breakaway **[[gallic-empire|Gallic]]** and
**[[palmyra|Palmyrene]]** empires, repeated Gothic invasions, and the collapse of the coinage. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 2 (**John Drinkwater**).

## Reign

Made co-Augustus by his father **Valerian** in 253, Gallienus took the western and northern fronts.
The catastrophe of **260** — Valerian captured alive by **Shapur I**, the loss of Egypt, the
usurpation of **Postumus** (who killed Gallienus' son Saloninus and founded the **Gallic Empire**) —
reduced Gallienus to holding a **"central empire"** of Italy, Africa, Egypt, the Danube, and Greece.
Yet he stabilized it: he repelled Alamannic and Gothic invasions (Milan, the Nestus), and survived a string of usurpers (Ingenuus,
Regalianus, the Macriani) before being murdered in **268** by his own generals during the siege of
the rebel Aureolus at Milan. He relied on **[[zenobia|Odenathus]] of Palmyra** to hold the East.

## Reforms and reputation

Drinkwater credits Gallienus with the period's most important **structural innovations**:

- The development of a permanent **mobile field-army** with a strong **cavalry** arm (based at Milan
  under Aureolus) — the seed of the later late-Roman field armies.
- The **exclusion of senators from military commands**, opening the high posts to professional
  **equestrian** career-soldiers (largely **Danubians**) — a decisive step toward the soldier-emperors
  and the divorce of senatorial rank from army command.
- **Toleration of Christianity**: he ended the Valerianic persecution (260), giving the church almost
  a generation of peace.

A patron of learning and the arts (and an initiate at Eleusis), Gallienus was vilified for "sloth" by
a hostile, senatorially-minded Latin tradition that never forgave his stripping of senatorial military
privilege.

## Significance

Gallienus is the pivot of the crisis: the ruler under whom the empire came closest to
**fragmentation**, yet who forged the **military and administrative tools** — field army, equestrian
command, "sub-capitals" — that Aurelian and Diocletian would use to put it back together. His reign
shows the crisis at once at its most dangerous and most generative.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]] makes Gallienus the nadir of the third-century crisis — “an excellent cook, and most contemptible prince.” **Gibbon’s views.**

- **Contemptible prince:** received Valerian’s captivity with secret pleasure (“I knew that my father was a mortal”); master of useless arts — cook, gardener, poet; when the state needed him, with Plotinus or mysteries; “genius without judgment” for war or government.
- **“Thirty tyrants” is idle historiography:** Augustan History’s thirty is fancy from Athens; actual ~**nineteen** pretenders, often virtuous men elected by troops, “much oftener driven into rebellion by their fears, than urged to it by their ambition” (Saturninus: “You have lost a useful commander, and you have made a very wretched emperor”).
- **Usurpations invite barbarians:** under Valerian and Gallienus, usurpers purchased barbarian neutrality with tributes and introduced “hostile and independent nations into the heart of the Roman monarchy” — the nadir against which Illyrian restorers ([[actors/aurelian]], Claudius, Probus, [[actors/diocletian]]) are measured.
