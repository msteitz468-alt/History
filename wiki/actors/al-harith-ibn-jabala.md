## Overview

**Al-Harith ibn Jabala**, Latinized as **Arethas**, was the Jafnid/[[ghassanids|Ghassanid]]
chief phylarch who, around 528/9, became the first Roman **[[super-phylarchate|super-phylarch]]**:
a chief phylarch granted authority over all of Rome's other Arab-allied phylarchs, personally
underwritten by imperial authority. Justinian created the office explicitly to counter the
Persian-allied [[al-mundhir-iii-lakhmid|al-Mundhir III]] ("Alamoundaros") of the
[[lakhmids|Lakhmid]] court at al-Hira, who by the 520s had held an equivalent position on the
Persian side for decades. Al-Harith's roughly forty-year tenure (c. 528/9–569) is the best
documented phase of the Ghassanid ascendancy and the model against which his son
[[al-mundhir-ibn-al-harith|al-Mundhir ibn al-Harith]]'s subsequent career, and downfall, are
measured.

## Formative Context and Elevation

Al-Harith was the son of "Gabalas"/Jabala, about whom almost nothing is known beyond his role
as the dynasty's figurative patriarch. Arab-Islamic tradition (Ibn Habib, al-Yaqubi) traces the
Jafnid line's establishment in Roman territory to a migration into and conquest of the
territory of the Salih tribe, followed by a Roman treaty granting self-rule under a king,
Jafna — the eponym of "Jafnid." A separate, earlier figure appears in the sources under a
similar name: Theophanes' "Arethas son of Thalabene," who concluded a treaty with the Roman
diplomat Euphrasius under Anastasius and was killed in an ambush by Alamoundaros c. 528/9 —
the same year as al-Harith's own elevation. Fisher, following Christian Robin, treats this
Thalaba/Arethas line as a related but *distinct* branch of Ghassan, not identical with Jabala's
line; the exact family relationship (possibly cemented by the marriage of Thalaba's daughter
Hind to Alamoundaros) is explicitly "murky" in the sources and should not be resolved into a
single genealogy on the wiki.

Justinian's elevation of al-Harith c. 528/9 gave him responsibility for all other Roman
phylarchs, paralleling the existing Persian arrangement for Alamoundaros. Geoffrey Greatrex
reads Justinian's motives as extending beyond simple counter-balancing of Persia's Arab client
to a broader strategic vision: a corridor of Roman influence running from Syria and Arabia
through the Red Sea into South Arabia as a counterweight to Persian power. Procopius states
al-Harith received "the dignity of king" from Justinian, though the practical meaning of the
title is unclear; a graffito from Jabal Says records "al-Harith the king [malik]" dispatching
a subordinate as a frontier guard, on a disputed date read as either 528 or 532/3. Notably,
neither al-Harith nor Alamoundaros is ever styled "king" in a Greek text or inscription — the
title seems to have been reserved for use among their own subordinates.

Al-Harith's rank climbed through the Roman senatorial grading system: initially likely
*peribleptos*, then *stratelatos* (an honorary *magister militum* title) with promotion to
*endoxotatos* among the *illustres*; he appears as *patrikios* in Malalas' Chronicle (18.148)
and Theophanes (AM 6056), both entries dated to 563. His brother Abu Karib, ranked
*endoxotatos*, was made phylarch of Palestine and administered "the Palm Groves" (the Hijazi
oases including Hegra) on Justinian's behalf.

## Career: The Persian Proxy War

Al-Harith and Alamoundaros fought an intermittent, decades-long proxy war paralleling and
sometimes outrunning the larger Roman-Persian conflict. In 529 al-Harith took part in a
successful Roman expedition against Persian fortresses, alongside "Gnouphas" and "Naaman,"
shortly before his elevation. At the Battle of Callinicum (19 April 531, Easter Saturday),
his first major engagement against Alamoundaros, accounts diverge sharply: Malalas has
al-Harith fighting on, while Procopius blames his wing for breaking formation, giving him
"the reputation of having betrayed the Romans to the Persians." Fisher judges Malalas' version
— based on the official post-battle inquiry — the more reliable, noting al-Harith kept his
rank afterward while Belisarius was temporarily removed from command.

During the 539–545 war, Alamoundaros (at Khusrau I's instigation) fabricated a border dispute
over pasturage south of Palmyra to justify a Persian incursion against al-Harith without
technically breaking the "Eternal Peace," whose terms did not cover Arab allies; al-Tabari's
Persian-derived account instead has al-Harith invading first. In 541 Belisarius sent al-Harith
on a raid with over 1,000 elite Roman troops; al-Harith ignored orders to report back, kept
his plunder, and sent Belisarius misleading intelligence — an early sign of the super-phylarch's
growing independence from Roman command. After the 545 truce the feud between al-Harith and
Alamoundaros continued on its own terms, unsanctioned by either empire; Alamoundaros captured
and allegedly sacrificed one of al-Harith's sons to the goddess "Uzzai" during this unauthorized
phase. Al-Harith finally killed Alamoundaros in battle near Qinnasrin/Chalcis in June 554,
ending the Lakhmid super-phylarch's roughly fifty-year career.

## Church Patronage and Succession

From the 1540s (chapter line numbering; i.e. mid-century) al-Harith was deeply involved in
Monophysite church politics, lobbying Empress Theodora for Monophysite bishops: Jacob Baradeus
was ordained bishop of Edessa in 542/3, and Theodore was ordained for Bostra/"hirta of the
tayyaye" at al-Harith's urging. He was drawn into the tritheist-heresy controversy (recorded
in a 569 document) and, in a 563 letter to Jacob Baradeus written from Constantinople, left
"the only piece of surviving correspondence authored by an Arab leader from this period."
Inscriptions at Nitl (near Madaba) and Qasr al-Hayr al-Gharbi (the monastery of "Heliarama,"
west of Palmyra) record him with the titles *endoxotatos*, *stratelatos*, and *patrikios*
phylarch. His authority over his own subordinates was imperfect: Cyril of Scythopolis records
a feud with a rival phylarch, Asovades, in the 550s.

In November 563 al-Harith traveled to Constantinople for an audience with Justinian to arrange
an orderly succession, securing the position for his son al-Mundhir. He died in 569/70 and was
included, alongside Khusrau I, Justinian, and Abraha, in Ps.-Dionysius's list of "kings who are
famous."

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Justinian's elevation of al-Harith, Rome's Arab frontier defense would likely have
remained the looser, less centralized web of allied phylarchs under provincial *duces* that
preceded the super-phylarchate — cheaper to Rome in the short term but far less capable of
matching a unified Persian Arab client. Al-Harith's forty-year tenure demonstrated the model's
military value (repeated containment of Alamoundaros, the eventual kill at Chalcis) but also
generated the "unintended consequence," in Fisher's phrase, of concentrating enough political
and military capital in one Arab dynasty that his son's independence would eventually alarm
Constantinople to the point of exile. Had Rome and Persia been weaker powers, Fisher argues,
a figure like al-Harith might have followed the trajectory of Clovis or Theoderic into full
independent kingship rather than remaining, however loosely, an imperial client.

## Historiography

Al-Harith is unusually well documented for a non-Roman frontier figure because his career
intersects multiple genres of surviving evidence: the classicizing historian Procopius (hostile
at points, crediting him with battlefield failure at Callinicum); the chronicler John Malalas
(more favorable, closer to the official record); ecclesiastical historians and Monophysite
church documents (the *Documenta* collection, John of Ephesus) documenting his religious
patronage; and a growing corpus of epigraphy (Jabal Says, Nitl, Qasr al-Hayr al-Gharbi) that
supplies his titulature independently of the literary tradition. The chief interpretive
difficulty is disentangling al-Harith ibn Jabala from the earlier, differently-descended
"Arethas son of Thalabene," a confusion the sources themselves invite by using overlapping
names and titles for both. Ariel Lewin's work on Roman military-administrative grading and
Geoffrey Greatrex's on Justinian's eastern strategy provide the main modern frameworks for
interpreting his rank progression and political significance respectively.
