# Marcus Aurelius (Marcus Aurelius Antoninus)

The **fifth and last of the "Five Good Emperors"** (161–180) — the **philosopher-emperor**, author
of the Stoic *Meditations*, whose conscientious reign was consumed by the wars and plague that
marked the **turning-point** from the Antonine peace toward the age of crisis. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 3.

## Accession and joint rule

Groomed from boyhood as Hadrian's chosen "Verissimus," Marcus succeeded **[[antoninus-pius|Antoninus
Pius]]** in **161** — but at once insisted that **equal powers be conferred on his adoptive
brother [[lucius-verus|Lucius Verus]]**, inaugurating the first formal **joint rule** of two
*Augusti*. A devoted Stoic (the pupil of Junius Rusticus, who introduced him to Epictetus), Marcus
governed with "painstaking thoroughness," respect for the Senate, and conscientious attention to
**justice and administration** (the *iuridici* and *curatores* of Italy). His private *Meditations*
reveal the inner discipline of a ruler who experienced the palace, in his own image, as a
"stepmother" beside the "mother" of philosophy.

## The Parthian War and the plague

Marcus' reign was almost immediately beset by crisis. The Parthian king **Vologaeses** invaded
**Armenia** and **Syria** (161), annihilating a Roman legion. **Lucius Verus** was sent to the
East, where (under able generals — Statius Priscus, Avidius Cassius, Martius Verus) the
**Parthian War (161–166)** recovered Armenia, took **Ctesiphon and Seleucia**, and "taught Parthia
a lesson" (which kept it quiet for thirty years). But the returning army brought the
**[[antonine-plague-165|Antonine Plague]]** — a devastating epidemic that ravaged the empire for a
generation, killing vast numbers and straining its manpower at the worst moment.

## The Marcomannic Wars

"While the Parthian War was being waged, the Marcomannic War was born." The denuding of the
Danube to reinforce the East, and the **pressure of migrating peoples** building between the
Danube and the Baltic (the volume connects it to the long migration of the Goths), unleashed the
**[[marcomannic-wars-166-180|Marcomannic Wars (166–180)]]** — "a war of many nations" that for the
first time since the Cimbri saw **barbarians invade Italy**. Marcus spent most of the rest of his
life on the Danube frontier, campaigning against the **Marcomanni, Quadi, and Sarmatians**, and
settling defeated peoples within the empire — the famous "rain miracle" and the scenes of the
**Column of Marcus Aurelius** belonging to these grim campaigns.

## Avidius Cassius and the succession

In **175**, on a false report of Marcus' death, his eastern commander **Avidius Cassius** revolted
and was proclaimed emperor in Syria and Egypt; the revolt collapsed when Cassius was killed by his
own officers, and Marcus showed conspicuous clemency to the rebels. Marcus' deepest break with the
**adoptive principle** was to make his own son **[[commodus|Commodus]]** his colleague and heir —
"a Caesar born to the purple" — abandoning the merit-selection that had given the empire its good
emperors. He died on the Danube in **180**, the wars unfinished.

## Significance

Marcus is remembered as the **philosopher on the throne** and the ideal of the conscientious
ruler — but CAH XI presents his reign as the **hinge** of the High Empire: the Antonine peace
gave way under the simultaneous shock of **war on two frontiers, plague, and barbarian pressure**,
and the return to **dynastic (blood) succession** in Commodus ended the run of adoptive emperors
and opened the way to the instability of the late second and third centuries. The *Meditations*
remain one of the great documents of Stoic thought.

## OHCW (Meredith; Russell)

**Anthony Meredith** (Ch. 29): born 121, emperor 161–80; twelve books of *Meditations* as private campaign notes (Book 2 among the Quadi; Book 3 at Carnuntum, c.171–73); unnoticed until ~350 then long obscure until print 1558; Stoic obedience to nature with stronger introversion/possible afterlife than Epictetus — a Platonizing Stoic tipping of the scales. **Donald Russell** (Ch. 27): native Greek speakers seldom learned Latin nuances; Latin speakers destined for office learned Greek from childhood — Marcus wrote *To Himself* in Greek, emblem of bilingual high-imperial culture.
