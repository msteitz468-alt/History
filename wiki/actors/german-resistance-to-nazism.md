# The German Resistance (1933–1945)

Umbrella page for the distinct, largely unconnected currents Evans documents in *The Third Reich at War* (2008), Part 6:

- **Working-class/left undergrounds.** SPD and KPD networks were crushed by the mid-1930s and demoralized by the Nazi–Soviet Pact; Communist cells re-emerged from 1942 (Saxony, Berlin, the Ruhr). The Herbert Baum group of young Jewish Communists bombed a Goebbels anti-Soviet exhibition (May 1942); 15 were executed and 500 Berlin Jews shot or deported in reprisal. An attempted SPD–KPD liaison meeting (22 June 1944) ended in mass arrests. In Buchenwald, Communist prisoners' capo self-management bought group survival at a price still debated (collaboration vs. resistance).
- **The Red Orchestra (Rote Kapelle).** Not the Soviet spy ring of Nazi propaganda but a home-grown network around Arvid Harnack (Economics Ministry) and Harro Schulze-Boysen (Air Ministry), with Mildred Harnack and Libertas Schulze-Boysen prominent — leaflets, help for fugitives, intelligence to both embassies (Stalin ignored their accurate Barbarossa warning). Broken by radio counter-intelligence, August–September 1942: 130+ arrested, 50+ executed, Hitler insisting on hanging.
- **The "League: Community for Socialist Living"** — Artur Jacobs's tiny Essen collective, which identified antisemitism as Nazism's core from the start and hid Jews successfully throughout the war, surviving on smallness and trust.
- **[[white-rose|The White Rose]]** — the Munich students (own page).
- **[[kreisau-circle|The Kreisau Circle]]** and the military-conservative conspiracy that produced the [[july-plot-1944|July Plot]] (own pages), with their documented mix of moral courage and prior complicity.
- **Private dissent.** Most moral opposition never left the diary — Evans quotes the Hamburg teenager "Erika S." ("it's no longer anything but one huge campaign of murder," June 1942) who nonetheless joined the Party in 1944; see the "outer shell of loyalty" problem on [[ordinary-germans-and-the-holocaust]].

Evans's structural point: terror, atomization, the oath, and the memory of 1918 kept resistance minoritarian and disconnected; its moral significance (Tresckow: "We must prove to the world and to future generations that the men of the German resistance movement dared to take the decisive step") outran its practical prospects.

## From Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century* (2011)

**Comparative structure across occupied Europe.** Resisters everywhere began as social and political outsiders. French Communists remained inactive under the Nazi-Soviet Pact — early 1941 handbills in Paris read "Neither cholera nor the plague" of the two belligerents — until catapulted into action by the German invasion of the USSR on 22 June 1941 (the poet Aragon: "My party has given me back the French flag"). Resistance gained a genuine mass base only in 1942–43, driven by forced labor drafts (the origin of the *maquis*, hideouts in the scrub), shrinking rations, and deportations — conditions that also produced the first open Catholic clerical opposition seen in occupied Western Europe. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

**German resistance's structural limits.** Within Germany itself, resistance never achieved comparable breadth. The left was crushed early and remained mutually divided; the Red Orchestra network was broken by the Gestapo in August 1942. The churches fought chiefly for institutional autonomy rather than regime change — the Confessional Church's resistance was real but narrower than a challenge to Nazi rule as such. Conservative resistance was elitist and never built a mass following: General Ludwig Beck's resignation in 1938 is the emblematic gesture, and the postwar claim by some conservative resisters that the Munich Agreement "undercut our coup" — i.e., that Western appeasement in 1938 removed the pretext for an anti-Hitler move by the army — should, Paxton and Hessler caution, be viewed with some skepticism, since it conveniently shifts responsibility onto Allied policy. The Kreisau Circle continued planning through the war; a further assassination attempt failed in March 1943, preceding [[july-plot-1944|Stauffenberg's attempt of 20 July 1944]].

**Military impact.** Resistance's military significance was greatest in the East. Soviet partisans tied down substantial German forces behind the front. In Yugoslavia, resistance was most militarily effective of all: Draža Mihailović's Chetniks increasingly pursued de facto truces with the occupiers, while Josip Broz Tito's Partisans pursued active, continuous engagement — a divergence that led the British mission to switch its support to Tito, holding down some ten German divisions and ultimately underwriting Tito's postwar political legitimacy. In the West, resistance's military contribution was more modest — chiefly intelligence-gathering and a contribution to delaying German reinforcement of Normandy after D-Day; Paxton and Hessler's summary judgment is that resistance's "real significance" in Western Europe was less military than "intellectual and spiritual renewal."

**Intellectual impact.** Resistance thought rejected the prewar European order wholesale, converging on purified democracy, egalitarian political access, and economic planning within mixed economies — providing, in Paxton and Hessler's account, a genuine seedbed of the postwar consensus that shaped reconstruction politics across Western Europe. The worker-priest movement in France had roots in both Vichy-era social Catholicism and the resistance experience. Dietrich Bonhoeffer's theology of resistance ran through his engagement with Karl Barth, framing German defeat as a form of collective penance. Jean-Paul Sartre's existentialism crystallized as an ethic of the resistance itself — "what is not possible is not to choose" — most explicitly in his 1944 essay "The Republic of Silence." Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

**Early civilian network (1937–38).** Shirer profiles the founding circle as small, elite, and largely composed of former Hitler sympathizers disillusioned in 1937–38: Carl Goerdeler (mayor of Leipzig, broke with the regime 1936 over antisemitism/rearmament); Johannes Popitz and Hjalmar Schacht (Golden Party Badge holders, awakened around 1938 — Ulrich von Hassell noted Schacht's capacity "for talking one way and acting another"); Hassell himself (purged as ambassador to Rome, February 1938, became the group's foreign-affairs adviser); the "Wednesday Club" of 16 intellectuals including Beck, Popitz, and Hassell. Younger members included Ewald von Kleist, Fabian von Schlabrendorff, trade unionists Julius Leber, Jakob Kaiser, and Wilhelm Leuschner, Gestapo insiders Artur Nebe and Hans Bernd Gisevius, Helmuth von Moltke (later Kreisau Circle), and Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who regarded Hitler as Antichrist and held it a Christian duty to "eliminate him." Nearly all were later executed or murdered. Field Marshal von Blomberg's own Nuremberg testimony dated the origin of military conspiracy specifically to the Fritsch frame-up and Case Green planning of winter 1937–38: "Before 1938–39 German generals did not oppose Hitler. There was no reason to oppose him, since he produced the results they desired." Colonel Hans Oster (Abwehr) was the crucial early bridge between the civilian and military circles.

**The "Zossen Conspiracy" (Oct–Nov 1939).** Brauchitsch and Halder, backed by Beck, Goerdeler, Oster, and General Georg Thomas, planned to depose Hitler if he insisted on attacking the West; it collapsed after Brauchitsch's disastrous 5 November 1939 confrontation with Hitler, who flew into a rage over an exaggerated report of Army defeatism. Oster alone "kept his head" after the collapse, warning the Belgian and Dutch legations in Berlin to expect the German attack — an act of direct treason, confirmed from captured records. Fourteen postponements of the Western offensive followed (Nov 1939–May 1940), each requiring frantic countermanding orders. Peace-feeler channels to Britain that winter ran via the Vatican (Dr. Josef Müller, with Oster's connivance) and via Bern (Theodor Kordt); the conspirators' own terms — Germany to retain Austria, the Sudetenland, and the 1914 Polish frontier even under a post-Hitler government — drew sharp criticism from Shirer as amounting to demanding by negotiation "the same thing handed to them... that Hitler took by force."

**August 1939 paralysis.** Even after Britain and France had firmed their guarantee to Poland — the precondition the conspirators (Oster, Canaris, Schacht, Gisevius, Beck, Hassell, Witzleben) had said they needed — the group did nothing, "gripped by utter confusion and a paralyzing sense of futility" (Shirer's phrase). On hearing of the 25 August postponement, Oster and Canaris concluded Hitler was already politically finished and dropped coup planning; Canaris: "Hitler will never survive this blow. Peace has been saved for the next twenty years." Beck's own appeal to Brauchitsch went unacknowledged. Halder, pressed at Nuremberg on why the conspirators had not acted, offered only that General von Witzleben had been transferred west — "without Witzleben the Army could not act" — which Shirer treats as evasive self-exculpation rather than a real explanation. [SHIRER-THESIS, cross-reference [[controversies/ordinary-germans-and-the-holocaust]]]

**1941–42 reactivation.** The winter 1941–42 crisis (Barbarossa's failure before Moscow) revived the Hassell–Beck–Goerdeler–Popitz–Oster circle, though the group was discouraged by the Atlantic Charter's Point 8 (Aug 1941) demanding German disarmament, which Hassell read as evidence the Allies meant to "smash Germany" rather than distinguish Nazi from anti-Nazi Germans. A plan to seize Hitler at Field Marshal von Bock's Army Group Centre HQ at Borisov (4 Aug 1941), organized by Tresckow and Schlabrendorff, foundered on Hitler's security; Bock himself refused: "I do not allow the Fuehrer to be attacked!" Beck was formally installed as head of the conspiracy in March 1942, though Hassell privately doubted him — "a man of tactics but little will power," a judgment Shirer calls prophetic of the eventual failure. Field Marshal von Witzleben, the conspirators' best hope for troops, was retired in spring 1942 (ostensibly for a hemorrhoid operation) and replaced by the non-conspiring Rundstedt, cutting off their access to a field command. Succession planning favored Louis-Ferdinand (second son of the Crown Prince, a Roosevelt acquaintance) for a restored constitutional Hohenzollern monarchy, though Popitz, Schacht, and Goerdeler each favored a different Hohenzollern prince.

**Contacts with the West (1942–44).** Goerdeler met the Swedish bankers Marcus and Jakob Wallenberg (April 1942) seeking assurance the Allies would negotiate with a post-Hitler government; none was given. Bonhoeffer and Hans Schönfeld met Bishop George Bell of Chichester in Stockholm via forged Abwehr travel papers, passing a list of conspirator names later used against them; Bell's relay to Anthony Eden (June 1942) drew no response. Hans Gisevius (Abwehr, posted to Zurich) was the chief contact with Allen Dulles (OSS Bern) from November 1942; a Beck memorandum carried to Dulles in May 1944 proposing Allied airborne/seaborne landings near Berlin, Hamburg, and Bremen while the Eastern war continued was flatly rejected — Dulles told Berlin there could be no separate peace with the West. [SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer is sharply critical of this recurring pattern: "One marvels at these German resistance leaders who were so insistent on getting a favorable peace settlement from the West and so hesitant in getting rid of Hitler until they had got it."

**Kreisau Circle.** Founded around Helmuth James von Moltke (great-great-nephew of the Franco-Prussian War field marshal) and Peter Yorck von Wartenburg (descendant of the general who signed the 1812 Convention of Tauroggen); membership crossed class and confessional lines (two Jesuits, two Lutheran pastors, socialists, conservatives, trade unionists). It was a discussion and planning group for a post-Hitler Germany — "a sort of Christian socialism" — that opposed political violence rather than plotted it directly; almost all members were later hanged. Moltke: "To us... Europe after the war is a question of how the picture of man can be re-established in the breasts of our fellow citizens." American journalist Dorothy Thompson broadcast personal short-wave appeals to Moltke (a friend) in summer 1942 urging the circle to act.

**Rommel and the widening of the conspiracy, 1944.** Field Marshal Rommel was drawn in via his friend Karl Stroelin (Oberbürgermeister of Stuttgart), moved by a Goerdeler-drafted memorandum demanding an end to persecution of Jews and the churches; Rommel opposed assassinating Hitler on practical rather than moral grounds (fearing martyrdom) and insisted on arrest and trial instead. Rommel's chief of staff Hans Speidel deepened the field marshal's commitment through spring 1944 planning for an armistice in the West while continuing the war against the USSR in the East — the conspirators' persistent, ultimately unrealistic hope of splitting the Allies. For the July 1944 assassination attempt itself and its aftermath, including named executed resistance figures and the Freisler trials, see [[events/july-plot-1944]]; for the two central military figures, see [[actors/stauffenberg-claus-von]] and [[actors/beck-ludwig]].
