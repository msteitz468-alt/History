# The German Resistance (1933–1945)

Umbrella page for the distinct, largely unconnected currents Evans documents in *The Third Reich at War* (2008), Part 6:

- **Working-class/left undergrounds.** SPD and KPD networks were crushed by the mid-1930s and demoralized by the Nazi–Soviet Pact; Communist cells re-emerged from 1942 (Saxony, Berlin, the Ruhr). The Herbert Baum group of young Jewish Communists bombed a Goebbels anti-Soviet exhibition (May 1942); 15 were executed and 500 Berlin Jews shot or deported in reprisal. An attempted SPD–KPD liaison meeting (22 June 1944) ended in mass arrests. In Buchenwald, Communist prisoners' capo self-management bought group survival at a price still debated (collaboration vs. resistance).
- **The Red Orchestra (Rote Kapelle).** Not the Soviet spy ring of Nazi propaganda but a home-grown network around Arvid Harnack (Economics Ministry) and Harro Schulze-Boysen (Air Ministry), with Mildred Harnack and Libertas Schulze-Boysen prominent — leaflets, help for fugitives, intelligence to both embassies (Stalin ignored their accurate Barbarossa warning). Broken by radio counter-intelligence, August–September 1942: 130+ arrested, 50+ executed, Hitler insisting on hanging.
- **The "League: Community for Socialist Living"** — Artur Jacobs's tiny Essen collective, which identified antisemitism as Nazism's core from the start and hid Jews successfully throughout the war, surviving on smallness and trust.
- **[[white-rose|The White Rose]]** — the Munich students (own page).
- **[[kreisau-circle|The Kreisau Circle]]** and the military-conservative conspiracy that produced the [[july-plot-1944|July Plot]] (own pages), with their documented mix of moral courage and prior complicity.
- **Private dissent.** Most moral opposition never left the diary — Evans quotes the Hamburg teenager "Erika S." ("it's no longer anything but one huge campaign of murder," June 1942) who nonetheless joined the Party in 1944; see the "outer shell of loyalty" problem on [[ordinary-germans-and-the-holocaust]].

Evans's structural point: terror, atomization, the oath, and the memory of 1918 kept resistance minoritarian and disconnected; its moral significance (Tresckow: "We must prove to the world and to future generations that the men of the German resistance movement dared to take the decisive step") outran its practical prospects.

## From Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century* (2011)

**Comparative structure across occupied Europe.** Resisters everywhere began as social and political outsiders. French Communists remained inactive under the Nazi-Soviet Pact — early 1941 handbills in Paris read "Neither cholera nor the plague" of the two belligerents — until catapulted into action by the German invasion of the USSR on 22 June 1941 (the poet Aragon: "My party has given me back the French flag"). Resistance gained a genuine mass base only in 1942–43, driven by forced labor drafts (the origin of the *maquis*, hideouts in the scrub), shrinking rations, and deportations — conditions that also produced the first open Catholic clerical opposition seen in occupied Western Europe. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

**German resistance's structural limits.** Within Germany itself, resistance never achieved comparable breadth. The left was crushed early and remained mutually divided; the Red Orchestra network was broken by the Gestapo in August 1942. The churches fought chiefly for institutional autonomy rather than regime change — the Confessional Church's resistance was real but narrower than a challenge to Nazi rule as such. Conservative resistance was elitist and never built a mass following: General Ludwig Beck's resignation in 1938 is the emblematic gesture, and the postwar claim by some conservative resisters that the Munich Agreement "undercut our coup" — i.e., that Western appeasement in 1938 removed the pretext for an anti-Hitler move by the army — should, Paxton and Hessler caution, be viewed with some skepticism, since it conveniently shifts responsibility onto Allied policy. The Kreisau Circle continued planning through the war; a further assassination attempt failed in March 1943, preceding [[july-plot-1944|Stauffenberg's attempt of 20 July 1944]].

**Military impact.** Resistance's military significance was greatest in the East. Soviet partisans tied down substantial German forces behind the front. In Yugoslavia, resistance was most militarily effective of all: Draža Mihailović's Chetniks increasingly pursued de facto truces with the occupiers, while Josip Broz Tito's Partisans pursued active, continuous engagement — a divergence that led the British mission to switch its support to Tito, holding down some ten German divisions and ultimately underwriting Tito's postwar political legitimacy. In the West, resistance's military contribution was more modest — chiefly intelligence-gathering and a contribution to delaying German reinforcement of Normandy after D-Day; Paxton and Hessler's summary judgment is that resistance's "real significance" in Western Europe was less military than "intellectual and spiritual renewal."

**Intellectual impact.** Resistance thought rejected the prewar European order wholesale, converging on purified democracy, egalitarian political access, and economic planning within mixed economies — providing, in Paxton and Hessler's account, a genuine seedbed of the postwar consensus that shaped reconstruction politics across Western Europe. The worker-priest movement in France had roots in both Vichy-era social Catholicism and the resistance experience. Dietrich Bonhoeffer's theology of resistance ran through his engagement with Karl Barth, framing German defeat as a form of collective penance. Jean-Paul Sartre's existentialism crystallized as an ethic of the resistance itself — "what is not possible is not to choose" — most explicitly in his 1944 essay "The Republic of Silence." Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].
