## Overview

Abulfazl Allami (1551–1602) of Agra was the principal ideological architect of [[akbar]]'s interconfessional civilizational program, and the author of the *Akbar-Namah* — the most important literary monument of the Mughal court. As [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974]] observes, "as Tabarî may be called the historian for the Sharî'ah-minded, and Ibn-Khaldûn the historian for the Faylasûfs, so we may say that Abûlfal was the historian for those who inclined to Sûfî metaphysics."

## Background and Intellectual Formation

Abulfazl's father, Shaykh Mubarak Nagawri (d. 1593), was "an eccentric Sufi-minded scholar of Sindhî family" who had been persecuted for sympathizing with the Mahdavi movement of Sayyid Muhammad of Jaunpur and for asserting that "every religious position might have its weak point, and any position was likely to have some truth to it." Abulfazl absorbed from his father both a principle of religious independence and an expectation of hostility from the orthodox ulama.

He had not himself advanced far along the Sufi path, but adopted the perspective of "the Sûfî metaphysicians" as his ultimate frame of reference. He retained "from his father the double concern for inward cultivation and for active social justice, but he resolved the dilemma so posed in a very different way from that of the Mahdavîs."

## Major Work: The Akbar-Namah

The *Akbar-Namah* (Book of Akbar) has two parts:

1. **The annals** — covering Akbar's ancestors (especially Babur and Humayun) and Akbar's own reign. Akbar is presented simultaneously as the ideal philosopher-king and as the Sufi "perfect man" (*qutb*). All events are interpreted as in keeping with his divine destiny; his conquests are justified by his status as *pir* and lord, even where the immediate justification is morally opaque. "As every stroke of fate is to be interpreted for the best, so is every act of the king."

2. **The Ain-e Akbari** (Akbar's Institutions) — a comprehensive survey of Mughal administration, economy, culture, and civilization, understood as an image of civilization at large "which the ideal king is to foster." It includes extensive passages on Hindu philosophy (Abulfazl's treatment of Sanskrit thought is less technically accomplished than al-Biruni's; Hodgson calls it "clumsy"), music, and the material culture of the empire.

The *sulh-e kull* ("universal conciliation") doctrine articulated in the *Akbar-Namah* is the most systematic statement of Akbar's religious policy: "to reject a human achievement because its maker was of the wrong allegiance is contrary to the good sense of the civilized man." Islam appears as "Muammad's religion" — one path among many — while *islam* in its original Quranic sense of submission to God is held available to all traditions.

## Intellectual Framework

Abulfazl operated within Sufi metaphysics but shifted its orientation in humanistic directions. Where the Sufi tradition typically stressed the inward life over historical events, Abulfazl "tried to see history and worldly civilization generally under the categories of unitive mystical thought." He presented civilization as "the fullest cultivation of all the natural possibilities offered to human beings in life" — a naturalistic and humanistic framework that Hodgson compares (across their many differences) to Ibn Khaldun's attempt to apply Falsafah categories to historical process.

The *Akbar-Namah* exhibits a double-level reading characteristic of Sufi literary convention: at one level, it describes an ideal worldly ruler; at a second level, Akbar is the type-symbol of God, as the sun is in nature. The "eulogy is undisguised" and openly acknowledged by Abulfazl himself, who notes the genre conventions while insisting on not praising what is wicked. His awareness of the tensions in his project — between honesty and eulogy, between personal spiritual anguish and political celebration — give the work a reflexive quality unusual in its genre.

## Career and Death

Abulfazl rose to high administrative responsibility, proving "a capable man of affairs" when sent to settle the newly conquered Deccan. He was Akbar's most trusted counselor in the late reign and was deliberately separated from the court by his enemies — sent to the Deccan where he could not influence the succession. "When sent (because of his enemies' desire to separate him from Akbar) to settle affairs in the newly conquered Deccan, he proved a capable man of affairs." He had promised himself retirement into spiritual seclusion after completing the *Akbar-Namah*; this was not allowed him.

In 1602, during a rebellion by Akbar's eldest son Salim (the future Jahangir), Abulfazl was assassinated. Salim "evidently held a grudge against him." The *Akbar-Namah* was left incomplete, its account of Akbar's own deeds unfinished.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Abulfazl, Akbar's religious program would have lacked its primary intellectual articulation. The *Akbar-Namah* shaped how subsequent Timurid rulers understood their dynastic tradition — providing both the vocabulary (*sulh-e kull*, *tawhid-e ilahi*) and the legitimating framework for a civilization transcending communal lines. His assassination severed the intellectual continuity at the court just as Jahangir was ascending.
