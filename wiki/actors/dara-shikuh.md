## Overview

Dārā Shikūh (1615–59) was a Mughal prince, son of Shah Jahān, and the intended heir to the Mughal throne before he was defeated and executed by his brother Aurangzeb. He was one of the most remarkable figures in the history of Islamic-Hindu intellectual encounter: a passionate student of [[sufism]] and Sanskrit literature who produced translations of the Upanishads into Persian and composed the *Majmaʿ al-baḥrayn* (Confluence of the Oceans), a systematic comparison of Islamic Sufi and Hindu philosophical concepts.

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Ch. 56.

---

## Life

Dārā Shikūh trained with a beloved Sufi master and pursued advanced degrees of spiritual enlightenment, reportedly practicing breath control so intensively that he could "pass through an entire night inhaling only twice." From a young age he was also drawn to Sanskrit learning and the Upanishads. After his father's incapacitation the succession was contested between Dārā and his brothers; Dārā lost, was charged with irreligion (charges Adamson calls "obviously politically motivated"), and was executed in 1659. The charges gave his opponents plenty to work with, but the religious heterodoxy was incidental to the political conflict.

---

## The Confluence of the Oceans

*Majmaʿ al-baḥrayn* (Confluence of the Oceans, 1655) is Dārā's most philosophically significant work — described by Adamson as "a truly remarkable document" despite a dismissive 1929 English reviewer who called it "wooden terminological comparisons" lacking "eloquence and inspiration."

The work's argument: the core ideas of Hindu teaching correspond systematically to Arabic concepts used by Muslim philosophical Sufis. Dārā's comparative identifications include:

- Sanskrit *māyā* = Sufi passionate love (*ʿishq*) (§1)
- Koranic names of God matched to Sanskrit equivalents (§11)
- Both traditions share the same cosmological theories and similar accounts of the soul
- Avicenna's theory of the five internal senses can be found in Sanskrit sources (§2)
- The Hindu theory of infinite world-cycles is reconcilable with Koranic resurrection verses; Muḥammad will recur in each cycle as the last prophet

He also argued that a Koranic verse (56:78) mentioning a "hidden book" refers to the Upanishads as the oldest revelation.

Dārā's claim was not that all religions are equal, but that their inner truth is one. He followed a well-established Islamic precedent that recognized Hindus and Buddhists as "peoples of the book" — but went further than most by elevating the Upanishads above Torah and Gospel, below only the Koran.

---

## Context

Dārā's work represents a development of Emperor Akbar's policy of religious openness. Akbar had decreed study of Hindu traditions and learned Sanskrit; married Hindu women; and engaged in inter-religious dialogue. Dārā radicalized this openness into a philosophical thesis about the ultimate unity of religious wisdom.

Structurally parallel to Dārā's project in the Islamic world was the *ḥikmat al-mutaʿāliya* (see [[heirs-of-mulla-sadra]]) in Safavid Iran — both sought synthesis across traditions. But where Ṣadrā synthesized Avicennism, Illuminationism, and Sufism, Dārā attempted to synthesize Islamic Sufism with Hindu Vedānta.

---

## Historiography

Dārā Shikūh's execution has sometimes been read as a civilizational turning point — the defeat of pluralistic, syncretic Mughal culture by orthodox Sunni intolerance under Aurangzeb. This reading probably overstates both Dārā's heterodoxy and Aurangzeb's theological motivations (both rulers had political interests that shaped the outcome). Nonetheless, the *Majmaʿ al-baḥrayn* represents a genuine philosophical achievement that has received increasing scholarly attention since the twentieth century.
