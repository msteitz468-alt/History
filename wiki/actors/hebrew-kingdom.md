# Hebrew Kingdom (Israel under Saul, David and Solomon)

The Hebrew Kingdom — the "united monarchy" of **Saul, David, and Solomon**,
c. 1020–930 BCE — was the brief Iron Age state that united the Israelite tribes of
the Palestinian highlands before splitting into the two kingdoms of **Israel** (north)
and **Judah** (south). It is treated in CAH II.2 (chapter XXXIV, Eissfeldt). This
page covers the formation, the reigns of the three kings (with their own pages for
[[david|David]] and [[solomon|Solomon]]), and the division; the source-critical
problems of the biblical account are central throughout.

## The Source Problem

Eissfeldt's chapter is built on **documentary-hypothesis source criticism**: the
books of Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings are composite, woven from the same kinds
of parallel sources (J, E, and later Deuteronomistic editing) as the Pentateuch, and
written down centuries after the events. The chapter's method is to **isolate the
older narrative strands** (e.g. Judges 1, or 1 Samuel 9–11) as closer to the truth
than the later, theologically schematised framework (e.g. the "all Israel under
Joshua" conquest, or the Deuteronomistic cycle of sin-and-deliverance in Judges).
Even so, the older strands "have all the characteristic features of legend," and
their historical content must be extracted critically. Against an earlier generation
of total scepticism, Eissfeldt affirms that David and Solomon are **historical
persons** and that the narratives preserve genuine memory — but only after careful
sifting. See [[the-exodus|the Exodus controversy]] for the related debate on the
settlement traditions.

## The Settlement and the Tribes

Eissfeldt rejects the biblical picture of a unified conquest of Canaan under Joshua.
In his reconstruction only the **"house of Joseph"** entered from Egypt and forced a
bridgehead across the Jordan (under Joshua), while other Hebrew tribes were **already
settled** in Palestine, having entered earlier by seeking pasture. The
**[[merneptah|Merneptah]] "Israel" stele** (c. 1208/1230 BCE) attests a people
called Israel already in Canaan; archaeology shows a rupture and a dense new highland
settlement around 1200 BCE. The **twelve-tribe** scheme is treated as a later, ideal
formation (some tribes — Manasseh, Benjamin — arose only after the settlement by
splitting from Joseph); the **amphictyony** hypothesis of Alt and Noth is noted but
judged to rest on weak evidence. The oldest tribal list is the **Song of Deborah**
(Judges 5, c. 1100 BCE), naming ten tribes.

## The Period of the Judges

The two centuries c. 1200–1000 BCE were the time of the **"judges"** — local
tribal saviours (Ehud, Gideon, Jephthah, Samson, Deborah and Baraq) who repelled
neighbouring enemies (Moab, Midian, Ammon, the Canaanite Jabin/Sisera) but ruled only
parts of Israel and were largely **contemporaries, not a succession**. The schematic
total of ~400 years and the "all Israel" framing are editorial. Gideon's near-kingship
and the short, disastrous autocracy of his son **Abimelech** foreshadow the move to
monarchy.

## The Choice of a King

The decisive pressure was the **[[philistines|Philistines]]** — better armed, with a
professional military class, an iron monopoly, and (after the [[merneptah|Egyptian]]
withdrawal from Asia) no outside check. Only a unified state under a single strong
leader could resist them. The tribes accordingly accepted **Saul** of Benjamin as
king "by the grace of God," acclaimed by both the northern tribes and Judah. Saul,
the volume insists against the hostile later editing, in fact **ruled strongly and
successfully**; his fall had "all the elements of a personal tragedy" rather than
being mere divine rejection. His wars were with the Philistines, Moab, Ammon, Edom,
Amalek, and the [[arameans|Aramaean]] kings of Zobah.

## David and Solomon

- **[[david|David]]** (c. 1000–960 BCE) captured **Jerusalem** from the Jebusites and
  made it his personal capital and religious centre, broke Philistine supremacy, and
  conquered Moab, Ammon, Edom, and the [[arameans|Aramaean]] states (Zobah,
  Damascus) — building a small empire whose real prize was control of the
  **caravan routes**. His kingdom was a personal union of two kingdoms (Israel and
  Judah) plus crown-city Jerusalem; the Absalom and Sheba revolts already revealed
  the north–south fault line.
- **[[solomon|Solomon]]** (c. 960–930 BCE) consolidated the dynasty, built the
  **Temple** and palace at Jerusalem (with Tyrian craftsmen under
  [[phoenicians|Hiram]]), introduced **chariotry** and an administrative division
  into twelve districts, and ran trade as a royal monopoly (the Red Sea fleet to
  Ophir, the horse-and-chariot trade) — but at the cost of heavy taxation and
  corvée that bred discontent, especially in the north.

## The Division

On Solomon's death his son **Rehoboam** refused to lighten the burdens, and the
northern tribes seceded under **Jeroboam ben-Nebat** (who had earlier fled to Egypt),
splitting the realm permanently into **[[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]]** (north) and
**[[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]]** (south, retaining the Davidic dynasty and Jerusalem).
The two states were thereafter usually hostile. [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] fell to
[[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]] in 722/721 BCE and [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]] to
[[neo-babylonian-empire|Babylon]] in 587 BCE, but the religious tradition begun here
outlasted both. The history of the two successor kingdoms from the schism (931) to the
age of Jeroboam II and Uzziah is carried forward in
[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] (Chs. 10–11) — including the
[[shoshenq-campaign-925bce|campaign of Shoshenq I (925)]], the
[[omride-dynasty|Omride dynasty]], and the [[jehu-revolt-841bce|revolt of Jehu (841)]].

## Significance

- `caused_by:` the [[philistines|Philistine]] pressure that forced the tribes into
  statehood — itself a product of the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age
  Collapse]] migrations.
- `produced:` the Davidic state and dynasty, Jerusalem as a religious capital, and
  the institutional and literary matrix of later **Israelite religion** — the seedbed
  of Judaism (and thence Christianity and Islam).
- `affiliated_with:` [[phoenicians|Tyre]] (the David–Hiram–Solomon alliance);
  `opposed_by:` [[arameans|Aram-Damascus]], the great northern rival after Solomon.

## Historiography

Eissfeldt's 1965 chapter is a classic statement of **mid-20th-century critical
biblical scholarship** — source-critical, sceptical of the schematic framework but
confident that a historical core is recoverable. Since then the debate has polarised:
"minimalists" doubt the united monarchy's scale (or existence) given thin
archaeology, while others defend a modest historical kernel; the **amphictyony** and
the grand Davidic "empire" are both now widely questioned. The volume predates the
highland-survey archaeology (Finkelstein and others) that reframed Israelite origins
as largely **indigenous** rather than invasive (see [[the-exodus|the Exodus]]). The
collection is strong on the biblical text and on Albright-school archaeology but thin
on the post-1980 revisionism.

## Dever (2017): an "early inchoate state"

[[sources/dever-beyond-the-texts-2017|Dever (2017)]] supplements the older CAH narrative with
post-1990s highland-survey archaeology. By his criteria "by the tenth century Judah, and
probably Israel, had indeed reached a state level of organization" — an **"early inchoate
state"** (Claessen–Skalník), not the biblical empire. Population of Israel west of the Jordan
rose c. 25,000 (12th c.) → 50,000 (11th c.) → ~100,000 (10th c.), with Judah ~50,000 (Ofer);
~90% rural. A **settlement hierarchy** of ~11 tier-1 cities (Hazor X, Megiddo VA/IVB, Gezer
VIII, Jerusalem 14, Shechem X, etc.), tier-2 towns, and a tier-6 fort network (Negev forts,
[[controversies/historicity-of-the-united-monarchy|Khirbet Qeiyafa]], Tell el-Ful) emerges by
the tenth century. The **[[shoshenq-campaign-925bce|Shoshenq raid]]** (c. 920) "makes no
sense if... directed merely at... highland villages. The objective must have been a
well-established polity" — a fixed terminus ante quem. Dever stresses that "united monarchy"
"never occurs in the Hebrew Bible"; the real question is whether a centralized polity existed,
to which archaeology answers yes. The earlier **[[controversies/emergence-of-early-israel|
emergence of Israel]]** he reads as indigenous Canaanite "agrarian reform," not conquest or
exodus.
