# Late Period Egypt (404–332 BCE)

## Overview

For most of the fourth century **Egypt was independent of Persia** — the last era of
native pharaonic rule, spanning the **28th, 29th and 30th Dynasties** (404–343), before a
brief Persian reconquest (343) and final absorption by **[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]]**
(332). [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 8e, Alan B. Lloyd) portrays a state of
**chronic dynastic instability**, dependent on **Greek mercenaries** and on managing two
restive internal forces — the warrior class and the priesthood — yet successful, against
the odds, in keeping the world's greatest empire at bay for sixty years.

## Independence and instability

Egypt threw off Persian rule in **404** (one of the genuine failures Hornblower allows in
the indictment of Achaemenid weakness; see [[achaemenid-empire]]). But the native dynasties
were "wracked with turmoil": the **29th Dynasty** (Nepherites, Psammuthis, Achoris) was a
succession of short reigns and usurpations — only **Achoris** held power long (13 years) —
and was deposed by the **30th Dynasty** of Sebennytus (Nectanebo I, Tachos, Nectanebo II),
itself born of military coups. The crown's survival depended on controlling two domestic
powers: the **Machimoi**, a native warrior class "perfectly prepared to play the
king-maker," who fielded the huge (if exaggerated) armies of the period; and the **wealthy
priesthood**, courted with a revival of **temple-building** (culminating in Nectanebo II's
great temple at Behbet el-Hagar).

## Greek mercenaries and the war with Persia

The decisive military fact was reliance on **Greek mercenaries and generals**. Egypt
allied with Athens, with the Cypriot **[[evagoras-of-salamis|Evagoras]]**, and with rebel
satraps (see [[revolt-of-the-satraps-366bce]]) against the common Persian enemy. The most
ambitious moment was **Tachos' expedition into Persian Asia** (c. 360), with the aged
Spartan king **[[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]]** commanding his Greek mercenaries and the
Athenian **Chabrias** his fleet. It collapsed in treachery: Tachos' nephew **Nectanebo
II** rebelled, Agesilaus (nursing a grudge) switched to him, and Tachos fled to the Great
King. Nectanebo then survived a rival Mendesian pretender only "through the excellence of
his Greek troops and the generalship of Agesilaus" — an episode that, for Lloyd, lays bare
"the precarious nature of royal power in Late Period Egypt."

## The Persian reconquest and Alexander

In **343** the energetic **Artaxerxes III Ochus** finally **reconquered Egypt**, ending
native rule and inaugurating a hated second Persian occupation. It lasted barely a decade:
in **332** Egypt welcomed **[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]]** as a liberator, and native
pharaonic Egypt passed permanently into the Greco-Macedonian world (the Ptolemies).

## Significance

Late Period Egypt is the most striking instance of the fourth-century **mercenary
economy** of war and of the limits of Achaemenid power: the empire that arbitrated Greece
could not, for two generations, hold its richest province. Its fall in 343 removed the one
chronic drain on Persian strength just years before Alexander struck — and its eager
welcome of the Macedonians in 332 set the stage for **Ptolemaic Egypt** and Alexandria.
