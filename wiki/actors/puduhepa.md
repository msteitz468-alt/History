## Overview

Puduhepa was queen of the [[hittites|Hittite]] empire and one of the most politically active and diplomatically influential women in the ancient Near East. The daughter of Pentipsharri, a priest of Ishtar at the [[kizzuwatna|Kizzuwadnan]] city of Lawazantiya, she was herself a priestess in Ishtar's service before her marriage to [[hattusili-iii]], c.1274 BCE. She served as a genuine co-ruler: named co-signatory on the [[egyptian-hittite-treaty-1259bce]], included in vassal loyalty oaths, the recipient of fifteen letters from [[ramesses-ii]] or his family, and the issuer of seals in her own right. After her husband's death she continued to exercise judicial, diplomatic, and religious authority well into the reign of her son [[tudhaliya-iv]].

## Marriage and Early Career

After the [[battle-of-qadesh-1274bce]], [[hattusili-iii]] was left in command of the land of Aba. On his homeward journey northward he stopped at Lawazantiya, the Kizzuwadnan religious centre, to perform rituals to Ishtar. There he met Puduhepa: "In Lawazantiya he met and married Puduhepa, the daughter of Pentipsharri, priest of the goddess Ishtar, and herself a priestess in the goddess's service" (Bryce). Hattusili described the union as divinely ordained, revealed to him in a dream. "The goddess gave them the love of husband and wife" (Bryce, citing Hittite text).

The marriage was immediately consequential. Returning north, Hattusili found Hakpis had revolted and Kaskans had renewed attacks during his absence. He restored order, resumed the viceregal throne of Hakpis, and "installed his bride as queen" — an eventful beginning to her public role.

## Co-Rule and Political Authority

Puduhepa's authority was not informal. She was named alongside Hattusili in the loyalty oath clause of the Ulmi-Teshub treaty with [[tarhuntassa|Kurunta]] of Tarhuntassa. The [[egyptian-hittite-treaty-1259bce]] bore both their names as co-signatories. She issued her own royal seals. Ramesses addressed four surviving letters directly to her, in terms "virtually identical to those used in his correspondence with the Hittite king himself" (Bryce). The total surviving corpus of Egyptian-Hittite correspondence to Puduhepa numbers fifteen letters from Ramesses or his family.

Her profile in international diplomacy was extensive and largely unparallelled for a Hittite queen: she corresponded with both foreign rulers (Ramesses; the Egyptian royal family) and vassal rulers, and continued to exercise these functions after Hattusili's death, at least into the early years of Tudhaliya's reign. The possibility that she was still alive during the reign of the Ugaritic king Niqmaddu III (late 13th century) would place her age at death at ninety or above, if she was fifteen when she married Hattusili.

## Diplomatic Role

Puduhepa took primary responsibility for the arrangements surrounding the Hittite-Egyptian diplomatic marriages — first and most importantly the marriage of a daughter to Ramesses (his 33rd regnal year, autumn 1246, thirteen years after the Silver Treaty). Her correspondence over the marriage settlement, dowry, and travel arrangements was extensive and not without friction. When Ramesses complained about Hittite delays, Puduhepa replied sharply, citing a fire that had allegedly destroyed the "House of Hatti" (perhaps a royal treasury or storehouse) and using the opportunity to press Ramesses about the continued presence of [[urhi-teshub]] at his court:

"At present I cannot give her to you. Do I not know, as you, my Brother, know, that the 'House of Hatti' has been destroyed by fire? What remained Urhi-Teshub gave to the Great God. Since Urhi-Teshub is there (with you), ask him whether it is so or not!" (KUB xxi 38 obv. 7'–12')

When arrangements were finalized, Ramesses wrote to her with fulsome praise: "Excellent, excellent is this decision about which my Brother has written to me . . . (our) two great countries will become as one land forever!" (KUB iii 63 obv. 12–20). Puduhepa had asked that her daughter be recognized as Ramesses' principal wife; Ramesses initially agreed, though the princess was eventually sent to the pharaoh's harem. The daughter's Hittite name is unknown; her Egyptian name was Maat-Hor-Neferure.

Puduhepa also arranged a second marriage of a Hittite princess to Ramesses (attested in the Koptos Stele), possibly after Hattusili's death. She arranged or claimed credit for the double marriage between the Hittite and [[amurru|Amurrite]] royal families (Hattusili's son Nerikkaili married Benteshina's daughter; Hattusili's daughter Gassuliyawiya was given to Benteshina). She arranged marriages with the Babylonian royal family and decided on a wife for [[urhi-teshub]]'s half-brother Kurunta. She also raised Hattusili's sons by previous marriages and, she claimed, made them army commanders.

## Religious Role

Before her marriage Puduhepa was a priestess of Ishtar at Lawazantiya; her religious activity intensified as Hittite queen. She served as chief priestess of the Hittite realm. The rock-face relief at Firaktin (approximately 100 km south of Kayseri) shows her conducting a religious ceremony alongside Hattusili as his equal: he makes a libation to a god; she makes a libation to the goddess Hepat.

She is credited with commissioning a comprehensive collection and organization of Hittite religious texts, with extensive revisions to religious ceremonies and rituals, and possibly with a rationalization of the Hittite pantheon by introducing Hittite-Hurrian syncretisms. The most important is expressed in her prayer to the Sun Goddess of Arinna: "Sun Goddess of Arinna, you are Queen of all countries! In the Land of Hatti you bear the name of the Sun Goddess of Arinna; but in the land which you made the cedar land you bear the name Hepat" (KUB xxi 27 i 1–6).

Her votive prayers for Hattusili's health survive and are among the most personal documents from the Hittite world. She sought cures for his inflammation of the feet ("A dream of the queen: Somebody said again and again to me in a dream: 'Make a vow to the goddess Ningal as follows: ''If that (disease) Fire-of-the-Feet of His Majesty will pass quickly, I shall make for Ningal ten (?) talla (oil flasks) of gold set with lapis lazuli!'' '" — KUB xv 3 i 17) and for an eye illness. Bryce describes the prayers as expressing "the love and loyalty of this queen, who always lived under the threat of losing her beloved husband."

## Judicial Activity

After Hattusili's death Puduhepa exercised judicial authority, intervening in legal disputes and making pronouncements in vassal-state cases. One surviving document in the form of a royal letter to Ammistamru II of [[ugarit]] records a dispute over deliberate damage to a ship; Puduhepa found for the aggrieved shipowner and ordered compensation. The letter was signed with her personal seal and uses the royal title "My Sun," indicating authority delegated by the Hittite king and exercised independently.

## Counterfactual Significance

Puduhepa's sustained management of Hittite-Egyptian diplomatic correspondence — fifteen letters, the marriage negotiations, the treaty — was arguably indispensable to the maintenance of the 1259 peace accord. Without her, Hattusili's increasing ill health and preoccupation with domestic succession crises might have allowed relations with Egypt to deteriorate. Her religious reorganization may also have had lasting effects on Hittite cult practice and deity syncretism. Her longevity meant that Hittite continuity across the transition from Hattusili III to Tudhaliya IV was stabilized by a figure who had been present and active from the battle of Kadesh onward.

## Historiography

Puduhepa is unusual in the ancient world in being a woman whose voice survives in multiple, self-authored documents — letters, prayers, legal judgements. The surviving record is, as Bryce notes, overwhelmingly positive, presenting her as a devoted partner, brilliant diplomatist, and pious priestess. Whether there was "a more sinister dimension to her role," as there was for Suppiluliuma's wife Tawananna, cannot be determined from surviving sources. Her influence over succession decisions — particularly whether she persuaded Hattusili to set aside the crown prince Nerikkaili in favour of her own son Tudhaliya — is speculated but undocumented. "She could not have failed to make enemies" (Bryce). The primary source base (CTH 154–176 etc.) is rich but one-sided; the Egyptian counterpart letters provide an independent witness to her correspondence.
