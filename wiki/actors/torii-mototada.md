## Role and Background

Torii Mototada was one of [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu's]] oldest and most trusted companions, described as then aged sixty-two when assigned to hold Fushimi castle in 1600. He served as lord of Fushimi and was entrusted with its defense in what both he and Ieyasu understood to be a last stand — Ieyasu "showed more emotion at parting for the last time with this companion of his early days than ever he showed before or since."

## Defense of Fushimi Castle (1600)

Fushimi was "the last castle that Hideyoshi had built, and so was a particularly strong place," comprising a main keep and five subsidiary fortresses (Nishi Maru, San-no Maru, Matsu Maru, Nagoya Maru, and Jibushoyu Maru) with a garrison of eighteen hundred men. Torii's subordinates included Naito Ienaga and his son Motonaga, Matsudaira Ietada, and Matsudaira Chikamasa.

From the eighteenth to the twenty-eighth of the seventh month, the Western army — which included Shimazu Yoshihiro, Kobayakawa Hideaki, Ukita Hideie, and Nabeshima — made no impression on the defense. When Kobayakawa and Shimazu had attempted to assist in the defense earlier, Mototada refused to admit them, rightly suspecting their loyalty. [[ishida-mitsunari|Ishida Mitsunari]] himself came from Sawayama to encourage the besiegers.

The castle's fall came through internal betrayal. A Tokugawa agent placed in charge of the Matsu Maru was blackmailed via a message shot into the castle on an arrow: Nagatsuka Masaie had seized his wife and children and threatened to crucify them if he did not betray his master. The agent set fire to the tower he was holding and, with forty men he had subverted, breached the outer wall for a hundred yards. Under cover of the flames the main gate was forced, though the defenders "resisted manfully with spear and halberd."

The subsequent fighting was desperate. Matsudaira Chikamasa and the "valiant tea-merchant Kambayashi Chikuan" fell fighting. Matsudaira Ietada, after wounding a Shimazu captain and repulsing an attack, killed himself. The Jibushoyu Maru fell to Shimazu.

Mototada's final stand: with the main keep on fire and his force reduced to approximately ten men after five counterattacks, Mototada sat down on a step to rest. One Saiga Shigetomo ran at him with a spear, whereupon "the old man called out his name, and Saiga waited respectfully while he put an end to himself." Three hundred and fifty men fell with him.

Naito Ienaga in the Nishi Maru fought alongside his sixteen-year-old son Motonaga. Unable to escape the fire, the son cut himself open and leaped onto his father's funeral pyre.

## Aftermath

Most of the eighteen-hundred-man garrison were killed; the Western army lost three thousand attackers. The castle held for almost a fortnight and inflicted such loss that Sadler terms it "a victory of doubtful value" for the West. Critically, it held Western forces in place at a key moment, and the delay (along with the similar delays imposed by Hosokawa Yusai at Tanabe and Kyogoku Takatsugi at Otsu) meant that substantial Western forces never reached Sekigahara.

Mototada's head, along with those of Matsudaira Ietada and Chikamasa, was displayed at the Kyobashi bridgehead in Osaka on a ceremonial stand of white wood — a mark of respect from the Western side for their conduct. A cloth merchant of Kyoto named Sano Shiroemon, who had received favors from Mototada, bribed guards and stole his head, bringing it to his brother, a priest, who buried it at his temple. Ishida pardoned Sano. Mototada's son Tadamasa afterward made this temple the family chantry and made the priest its abbot.

## Significance

Torii Mototada's stand at Fushimi is one of the canonical examples of loyal self-sacrifice in Japanese historical memory. Its practical military significance lay in the timing: by absorbing Western assault forces for nearly two weeks before Sekigahara, it contributed to the disruption of Western concentration that would prove decisive. Ieyasu's expression of grief at their parting — uncharacteristic of a figure known for emotional restraint — marks the episode as significant even within the unsentimental strategic narrative Sadler provides.
