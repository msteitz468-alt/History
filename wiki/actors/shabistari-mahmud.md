## Biography

Maḥmūd Shabistarī was a Persian Sufi poet and theologian who lived near Tabriz in northwestern Iran and died sometime after 1320 (traditional date c. 1340; birth c. 1288 is an estimate). Little is known of his life beyond his association with the Sufi milieu of Azerbaijan and his authorship of the *Gulshan-i Rāz* ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Principal Work: *Gulshan-i Rāz*

In 1311 Shabistarī composed the *Gulshan-i Rāz* ("Rose Garden of Mystery"), a Persian poem written as a response to eighteen questions posed by a Sufi correspondent from Khurasan named Amīr Ḥusaynī. The poem is described by Schimmel as "the handiest introduction to the thought of post-[[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] Sufism" — a concise, elegant versification of the major concepts of the theosophical school: the Perfect Man ([[al-insan-al-kamil]]), the stages of mystical development, [[wahdat-al-wujud|waḥdat al-wujūd]], and the specialized terminology of [[sufi-metaphysics]].

Shabistarī describes the divine beauty hidden within every atom, because "the Absolute is so nakedly apparent to man's sight that it is not visible" — a paradox of overwhelming nearness that blunts vision, just as unveiled light becomes invisible ("black"). The Perfect Man, in his formulation, traverses a twofold arc: descent into the world of phenomena (twenty steps from the First Intelligence to earth) and ascent toward divine unity (twenty more steps completing the return). These Neoplatonic schemas became standard teaching in many Sufi orders.

The poem attained wide circulation with the help of Lāhījī's celebrated commentary, composed in the second half of the fifteenth century (*Mafātiḥ al-Iʿjāz fī Sharḥ Gulshan-i Rāz*). For the Niʿmatullāhī dervishes of Iran, the text served (and perhaps still serves) as a traditional manual for disciples in formation.

## Western Reception

The *Gulshan-i Rāz* was one of the first major Sufi texts translated into Western languages:
- German by Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall (1838)
- English by E. H. Whinfield (1880)

Its accessibility made it a standard point of entry for European orientalists approaching Islamic mysticism.

## Influence on Iqbal

Muḥammad Iqbal composed his *Gulshan-i Rāz-i Jadīd* ("The New Rose Garden of Mystery", 1927) explicitly as a response to Shabistarī's questions, restating them in the idiom of modern philosophy and offering his own answers on the Perfect Man, divine will, and human destiny. The dialogue across six centuries illustrates the enduring vitality of Shabistarī's formulation of the core questions of [[sufi-metaphysics]].
