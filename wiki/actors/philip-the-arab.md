## Overview

Marcus Julius Philippus — "Philip the Arab" — was a native of Shahba, a small town in
the Trachonitis region of southern Syria that he later renamed Philippopolis and raised
to colony status. Ancient rumor made him the son of a bandit chief; Ball dismisses this
as a later expression of Roman anti-Arab prejudice rather than a reliable biographical
fact. Philip served as praetorian prefect to the emperor Gordian III, and was hastily
proclaimed emperor by the eastern army in AD 244 after Gordian and his army were
destroyed by [[sasanian-empire|Shapur I's]] Sasanian forces in northern Mesopotamia.
**He reigned AD 244–249** (an occasional "232–7" found in some transcriptions of the
source text is a text artifact, not Ball's intended dating). He was killed with his son,
Philip II, in a revolt led by Trajan Decius in 249.

## Peace with Shapur I

As the newly proclaimed emperor, Philip negotiated an end to the war with Shapur I,
paying a large indemnity — Shapur's own trilingual Res Gestae inscription records that
Philip "came to terms" and became tributary, paying 500,000 denarii. Shapur's victory
reliefs at Naqsh-i Rustam and at Bishapur and Darabgird depict Roman emperors in
submission before him on horseback; current scholarship, which Ball follows, identifies
the kneeling, suppliant figure in these reliefs as Philip, a standing figure (where
present) as the later-captured Valerian, and the figure trampled under Shapur's horse as
the slain Gordian III. Philip's own portrait busts, including one in the Vatican
collection, resemble the suppliant figure, supporting this identification. The relief at
Naqsh-i Rustam 4 shows Philip alone, kneeling, before the standing Valerian.

## The Millennium and Secular Games

In 248, Philip presided over Rome's Millennium celebration — the Secular Games marking
1,000 years since the city's traditional founding by Romulus and Remus. Ball emphasizes
the irony that the emperor who embodied "Roman civilisation" at this most iconic of
Roman commemorations was an Arab from a region entirely unknown to Rome's legendary
founders — for Ball, a vivid illustration of the empire's real center of gravity having
shifted east.

## The Contested Claim to Christianity

**THESIS (contested).** Ball argues that Philip, not Constantine, deserves recognition
as Rome's first Christian emperor, citing Eusebius's account of Philip attending church
services and receiving confession as the principal evidence; pagan sources are largely
silent on the question. Ball suggests that later Christian propagandists deliberately
downplayed Philip's Christianity in order to elevate Constantine's, and that Philip —
unlike Constantine, who Ball characterizes as converting for political reasons and only
formally becoming Christian on his deathbed — kept his faith deliberately private,
consistent with Roman norms treating personal belief as a private matter, and was
plausibly cautious in the aftermath of the [[elagabalus|Elagabalus]] debacle a generation
earlier. Ball concedes that modern scholarship on this question "ranges from rejection to
affirmation," with most scholars conceding at most that Philip "dabbled" in Christianity.
**This claim is disputed and should not be treated as established; it is presented here
as Ball's argument, not as settled historical fact.**

## Arab Self-Awareness

Ball argues that Philip's reign mattered as a landmark in the development of Arab
self-perception and in Roman perceptions of Arabs as more than mere "tent-dwellers,"
foreshadowing the scale and self-confidence of the 7th-century Arab conquest of the Near
East.

## Counterfactual Significance

Philip's brief reign illustrates, in Ball's broader argument, how thoroughly the Roman
imperial office had by the mid-3rd century become available to men of provincial,
non-Italian, and specifically Near Eastern origin — following directly on the precedent
set by the Syrian [[severan-dynasty|Severan dynasty]]. Had Philip not survived Gordian
III's disastrous Persian campaign to be acclaimed emperor, the particular symbolic
juxtaposition of an Arab emperor presiding over Rome's Millennium would not have
occurred, though the broader trend of provincial and eastern emperors was already well
underway.

## Historiography

Direct evidence for Philip's reign is thin and scattered — Shapur's Res Gestae
inscription is a valuable but one-sided primary source, and the question of his
Christianity rests almost entirely on Eusebius, a later and partisan Christian
historian. Ball's argument for Philip's priority over Constantine as "first Christian
emperor" is a minority, contested position; readers should treat it as part of his
larger revisionist "orientalization" thesis rather than consensus scholarship. See
[[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]] for the broader debate.
