# The Yuan Dynasty

The **Yuan dynasty** (1271–1368) was the **Mongol** dynasty that ruled all of China — the first
time the entire country was governed by a steppe conquest regime, and the East Asian heart of the
[[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]]. From the [[cambridge-history-of-china|Cambridge History of China]],
Vol. 6 (*Alien Regimes and Border States*, Franke & Twitchett).

## The "conquest dynasties" and the unification of China

CHC Vol. 6 treats the Yuan as the culmination of a series of **"conquest dynasties"** (a concept
associated with Wittfogel) that ruled parts of China from the steppe frontier — the **Liao**
(Khitan, 907–1125), **Xi Xia** (Tangut), and **Jin** (Jurchen, who took north China from the
[[song-dynasty|Song]] in 1127). [[mongol-conquests-13th-century|Mongol]] armies destroyed the Jin
(1234) and, under **[[kublai-khan|Kublai Khan]]**, completed the conquest of the **Southern Song**
in **1279** — reuniting north and south China for the first time since the Tang and ruling it as the
Yuan (proclaimed 1271 with a Chinese dynastic name).

## Mongol rule over China

The Yuan governed China as a **conquest elite** sitting atop, and deliberately apart from, the
Chinese majority:
- A **four-class hierarchy** privileged Mongols and *semu* (Central/West Asian) administrators over
  northern Chinese and, lowest, the southern Chinese — and the **examination system** was
  suspended for decades, sidelining the Confucian gentry.
- The Mongols relied on **foreign and non-Chinese personnel** (Muslims, Tibetans, even Marco Polo's
  Europe) and on Tibetan Buddhism, while keeping Chinese institutions running for revenue.
- The **Pax Mongolica** integrated China into a Eurasian network of trade and
  exchange to an unprecedented degree (the context for Marco Polo and for the disease and
  commercial flows of the 13th–14th centuries).
- Two great seaborne **invasions of Japan** (1274, 1281) failed, wrecked by storms (the *kamikaze*).

## Decline and fall

From the 1340s the Yuan was undone by **factional strife** at court, fiscal mismanagement and
hyperinflation of its paper currency, devastating **Yellow River floods** and famine, and the
**Red Turban** millenarian rebellions. In **1368** the rebel **Zhu Yuanzhang** drove the Mongols
back to the steppe and founded the [[ming-dynasty|Ming]]; the Yuan court continued in Mongolia as
the "Northern Yuan."

## Significance and historiography

The Yuan made the **reunification** of China permanent, incorporated **Yunnan** and Tibet into the
Chinese sphere, and bequeathed a larger imperial template later inherited by the Ming and Qing. The
CHC's "alien regimes" framing deliberately treats the Liao-Jin-Yuan from the **conquerors'** side,
against a Sinocentric "sinicization" thesis — debating how far the Mongols were absorbed into
Chinese civilization versus how far they ruled it as outsiders (the sinicization-vs-persistence
debate). It is the second great "conquest" of China, prefiguring the Manchu [[qing-dynasty|Qing]].
