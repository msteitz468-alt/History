# Amorites (Amurru / Martu)

The **West-Semitic peoples** — Sumerian *martu*, Akkadian *amurru*, "westerners" —
of the Syrian steppe whose long infiltration of, and intermittent thrusts into,
Mesopotamia made them the dominant political force of the
[[middle-bronze-age|Middle Bronze Age]]. They pressed on the
[[akkadian-empire|Akkadian]] and [[mesopotamian-chronology|Ur III]] states, helped
bring down the latter, and then founded the Amorite dynasties — [[mari|Mari]],
Yamhad, Assyria, and ultimately **Babylon** (Hammurabi) — that defined the early
second millennium. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch.
XXI, §IV, Goetze/Kupper on the Ur III evidence).

## Who they were

The cuneiform record gives a fairly clear picture. The Amorites were **Semites of
the western (Northwest Semitic / "Canaanite") branch** — known from their personal
names, which carry typically western theophoric elements (**Dagan, Adad, Lim,
Mer/Wer**), the suffix *-anum*, and the verbal prefix *ia-*. Many were **nomads or
semi-nomads** of the desert fringe, practising a little herding and harvesting and
"always ready for a sudden attack." Hostile Sumerian literature caricatures the
*martu* as barbarians "who know neither grain nor house, who eat raw meat, who have
no house in their lifetime and no tomb when they die." But the same sources show
**settled Amorites grouped into kingdoms** centred on Syrian cities — so the
"barbarian" image is a townsman's slander, not the whole truth.

## The long infiltration

Amorites were **not new** in the Mesopotamian record. Individuals called *martu*
appear already in the archaic tablets of **Shuruppak (Fara)**; under the
[[akkadian-empire|Dynasty of Agade]] they occur more often, some even as
land-receiving "officers" in royal service; and they multiply through the
neo-Sumerian period. The evidence suggests a **continuous, largely peaceful
immigration** stretching back perhaps before 3000 BCE, punctuated after ~2000 BCE
by **massive thrusts**.

## Pressure on the empires

- **[[akkadian-empire|Akkad]]:** Naram-Sin's son **Shar-kali-sharri** had to fight
  "the Amorite in **Basar**" (the Jebel Bishri) — one of the two converging
  pressures (with the [[gutian-invasion|Gutians]]) that destroyed the empire.
- **[[third-dynasty-of-ur|Ur III]]:** the threat was grave enough that
  **Shu-Sin** built a fortified line, the **"Wall against Amurru, the Fender of
  Tidnum"** (Muriq-Tidnim), and "chased the armed force of Amurru out of his
  country." It did not hold: under **Ibbi-Sin** the Amorite pressure (with the
  [[fall-of-ur-2004bce|Elamite sack of Ur]]) ended the last Sumerian empire.
  Ibbi-Sin called them "vagabonds who have never known what a town was."

## The Amorite age

Out of this came the political world of the early
[[middle-bronze-age|Middle Bronze Age]]: **Amorite dynasties seized the cities** of
Mesopotamia and Syria — Isin, Larsa, Eshnunna, Assyria (Shamshi-Adad I),
[[mari|Mari]], Yamhad (Aleppo), and the **First Dynasty of Babylon**, whose sixth
king **Hammurabi** would briefly reunify the land. In the Levant, the same
West-Semitic population underlies the re-urbanized **Middle Bronze Canaanite
city-states** and the "land of Amurru" of later texts. Egyptian sources of the
Middle Kingdom call the Asiatics they dealt with the *Amu* — the wretched
nomad-Asiatic of the [[first-intermediate-period|*Instruction for Merykare*]] and
the Amu chieftain Abisha painted at Beni Hasan.

## The nomads of the Mari archive

CAH Vol. II.1 (Kupper) gives, from the [[mari|Mari]] letters, the most detailed
picture in the wiki of the [[middle-bronze-age|Middle Bronze Age]] tribal world of
the middle Euphrates and Upper Mesopotamia. These were **semi-nomads, not true
nomads** — the camel was still unknown, so the herdsmen were **sheep-rearers** who
could not stray far from the rivers and returned to the valleys at seedtime and
harvest, "gradually allowing themselves to become rooted to the soil" even as new
groups formed behind them. The named groups:

- **Benjaminites** (better "Iaminites," "sons of the south") — a vast confederation
  along the Euphrates and around Harran; chronically hostile to [[zimri-lim|
  Zimri-Lim]], raiding towns and provoking insurrections (he massacred their leaders
  at Saggaratum). One of their tribes, the **Amnanu**, produced Sin-kashid, founder
  of a dynasty at Uruk.
- **Bensim'al** ("sons of the north") — the friendlier counterpart group.
- **Sutians** — the plunderers of the Syrian desert west of the Euphrates, raiding
  as far as Palmyra and Qatna, sometimes 2,000 strong.
- **Khanaeans (Hana)** — the tribe most bound up with Mari, **soldiers by
  profession** settled on palace-granted land in the Terqa district, manning
  garrisons and serving in every campaign; their name came to stand for "the
  Westerners" of the kingdom generally, and gave its name to the later land of Khana.
- **Habiru** — not an ethnic group but **bands of "free companions"** (brigands and
  mercenaries) who plundered towns or fought for the petty kings; the Mari evidence
  (a Sutian and Iamutbal men called Habiru) shows the term was **descriptive, not
  ethnic** — an important early datum in the long Habiru/Hebrew debate.

The picture is the enduring tension of **steppe and sown**: "the struggle between
nomads and settlers, having its origins in physical conditions, could never cease."

## The Mari-age state system

The settled Amorite kings — at [[mari|Mari]], [[iamkhad|Aleppo/Yamhad]], Qatna,
Eshnunna, Larsa, Assyria ([[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]]), and Babylon
([[hammurabi|Hammurabi]]) — formed a **multipolar diplomatic world** of roughly
equal powers, captured in the Mari letters' famous "no king is mighty by himself"
report (see [[interpolity-law]]). Amorite political dominance thus underlies not
just the dynasties but the whole interstate order of the early second millennium.

## Significance

The Amorites are the clearest early case in the wiki of the recurring Near Eastern
dynamic between **steppe and sown** — pastoral peoples of the margins infiltrating,
pressuring, and ultimately **ruling** the irrigation civilizations, then being
absorbed by them (their descendants "quickly lost their instability, joining the
cultivators and townspeople"). The same pattern recurs with the Aramaeans, the
Chaldeans, and later steppe conquerors. Their absorption also seeded the
**Northwest Semitic** linguistic world from which Canaanite, Phoenician, and Hebrew
descend.

## Liverani: homeland, the dimorphic zone, and the tribal map

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] revises several points. He relocates
the **Semitic "homeland"** away from the Syrian steppe to the south-western edge of the
Near East — the strip from Palestine and the Transjordan plateau to western Arabia — with
the Amorite component only *secondary* in Syria until the fall of [[ebla|Ebla]]. He reads
the expansion in phases (Palestine → Syria/Upper Mesopotamia → Lower Mesopotamia), the
violent final thrust into the south being a *necessity* against Ur III's solid resistance,
not the Amorites' nature. He sharpens Rowton's **"dimorphic zone"**: it was dimorphic not
because pastoralists coexisted with cities but because the *same* agro-pastoral population
concentrated in the irrigated lands during dry summers and dispersed to the steppe in wetter
winters/springs — **seasonal**, not structural, dimorphism. The Amorite arrival drove the
cultural shift from a **Sumerian–Akkadian** to an **Akkadian–Amorite** symbiosis (Sumerian
surviving only as an erudite language). On the Mari tribal map he follows **Durand and
Charpin**: the two real confederations were **Banu-Yamina** ("sons of the right"/south) and
**Banu-Sim'al** ("sons of the left"/north) — named by their relation to the rising sun —
and **"Hana"/"Haneans" was not a third tribe** but a general term for nomads (giving its
name to the region), a correction to the older CAH list above. The ruling
[[zimri-lim|Zimri-Lim]] dynasty was Sim'alite, which is why subduing the Yaminites was a
chronic problem. See [[ancient-near-east-environment-and-society]].

## Historiography

The Amorites are known almost entirely **from the outside** — through Sumerian and
Akkadian administrative tablets and hostile literary stereotypes, plus the onomastic
analysis of their names; they left no literature of their own in this period. Older
scholarship spoke of a single dramatic "Amorite invasion"; CAH (1971) already
prefers the more nuanced picture of **continuous infiltration punctuated by
thrusts**, which remains the mainstream view. Their role in the fall of Ur, and
the steppe-vs-town framing itself, remain debated. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
