## Biography

ʿUmar ibn ʿAlī ibn al-Fāriḍ was born in Cairo in 1181 (some sources give 1182) and died there in 1235, five years before his great contemporary [[ibn-arabi]] died in Damascus. He is the preeminent mystical poet of the Arabic language and one of the supreme masters of classical Arabic verse. His sobriquet *Ibn al-Fāriḍ* ("son of the registrar of women's inheritance") refers to his father's occupation.

Where Ibn ʿArabī poured his mystical experience into an almost inexhaustible stream of systematic prose, Ibn al-Fāriḍ was the reverse: no prose whatsoever, and a small *Dīwān* of poems of exceptional formal perfection. Reynold A. Nicholson, his most penetrating Western interpreter, described his style and diction as resembling "the choicest and finest jewel-work of a fastidious artist rather than the first fruits of divine inspiration" — remarkable because the poems were reportedly composed in states of mystical rapture.

Ibn al-Fāriḍ lived a solitary life, spending time on the Muqattam Hill outside Cairo and then fifteen years in the Hijaz before returning to his native city, where he died. He is buried in Cairo, his tomb long a site of pilgrimage and veneration ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Works

Ibn al-Fāriḍ's *Dīwān* is small by the standards of the prolific Persian mystical poets. Two poems have secured his reputation as one of the greatest mystical poets in any Islamic language.

### The *Khamriyya* (Wine Ode)

The *Khamriyya* ("Ode of Wine") is the more accessible of his major poems, describing the wine of divine love that the lovers drank before the grapes were created — that is, at the primordial Day of the Covenant (*yawm al-mīthāq*) before the material world existed. This wine intoxicates the entire world, cures the sick, makes the blind see and the deaf hear, and guides the lover toward his eternal goal like the North Star. The poem draws on the wine symbolism common to both Arabic and Persian mystical poetry, and can be understood (and translated) without the extreme difficulty posed by Ibn al-Fāriḍ's denser works.

### The *Tāʾiyyat al-Kubrā* (The Greater Poem Rhyming in T)

Also known as *Naẓm al-Sulūk* ("Order of the Progress") — a title given to it by the Prophet in a dream, according to tradition — the *Tāʾiyya* runs to 756 verses and is the centerpiece of Ibn al-Fāriḍ's achievement. Louis Massignon called it "a heavy tapestry in gold-brocade," a "*kiswa* for the spiritual pilgrimage" — evoking the gold-embroidered covering of the Kaaba that Egyptian pilgrims traditionally brought to Mecca, here woven of words and mystical symbols into a comprehensive account of the stations and stages of the mystical life, reaching what Massignon called an "*esthétique transcendantale*."

Disentangling the poem's different structural parts is no easy task; some passages are of particular interest for mystical psychology. Among the most famous is the description of synaesthetic union in *tawḥīd*:

> I knew for sure that we are really One, and the sobriety of union restored the notion of separation, / And my whole being was a tongue to speak, an eye to see, an ear to hear, and a hand to seize.

This passage — in which union with God produces an intensification and interchangeability of the senses rather than their extinction — goes back to the *ḥadīth al-nawāfil* (the tradition of supererogatory acts), in which God promises to become the eye through which His loving servant sees and the ear through which he hears.

### The Shadow Play Passage (*Tāʾiyya*, lines 680–706)

One of the most celebrated passages in all of Islamic mystical poetry:

> Lo from behind the veil mysterious / the forms of things are shown in every guise, / of manifold appearance . . . . / All thou beholdest is the act of One. / In solitude, but closely veiled is He. / Let him but lift the screen, no doubt remains: / The forms are vanished, He alone is all.

Life is here envisioned as a shadow play; the mystic's illumination consists in recognizing that behind every puppet-figure, every apparently independent form, "everything you see is the action of the One." The image of the shadow play — imported into the Islamic world from China and well-established as an entertainment form in Baghdad by the tenth century — had already been used by ʿAṭṭār (several decades before the *Tāʾiyya*) and by Ibn al-Fāriḍ's contemporary [[ibn-arabi]] in the *Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*. Ibn al-Fāriḍ's use is among the most sustained and theologically precise.

### Lyrical Love Poems

Beyond the two canonical poems, Ibn al-Fāriḍ's *Dīwān* contains shorter odes of great lyrical tenderness. Both in form and content these poems are "perfectly Arabic," in Schimmel's phrase: they deploy Arabic wordplay, puns, and diminutives, and speak of love for the Absolute using the names of heroines from pre-Islamic Arabic love poetry (Laila, Salma). The love relation is personal — the soul, shedding all self-will, reaches conformity with the divine will and becomes, as Nicholson formulates it, "the beloved of God."

## Intellectual Position

Ibn al-Fāriḍ's poetry has been read as a witness to pantheistic interpretations of Islam, but Schimmel and the scholarly consensus caution against this equation: "One must not forget that poetry is not to be equated with theoretical discussions about theological problems." When Ibn al-Fāriḍ saw the transcendent unity of all things, he was using "the language of the heart rather than that of the head." His work does not offer a systematic metaphysics comparable to [[ibn-arabi]]'s prose, and the strongest source for his imagery of the shadow play is likely his contemporary Ibn ʿArabī — making the poetic and theosophical traditions mutually reinforcing rather than identical.

Unlike the Persian mystical poets who wrote in a milieu saturated by Sufi orders and Persian literary conventions, Ibn al-Fāriḍ stands closer to the classical Arabic literary tradition. His poems are rooted in the *qaṣīda* form and in the conventions of Arabic love poetry (*ʿUdhrī* and other traditions), which he redirected toward mystical ends without abandoning their formal demands.

## Western Scholarly Reception

Ibn al-Fāriḍ attracted early and sustained attention from European orientalists:
- **1828**: Grangeret de Lagrange published some of his poems; followed by Silvestre de Sacy
- **1854**: Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall translated the *Tāʾiyya* into German (the "misfortune" of this translation, Nicholson noted, being that the meaning was only rarely captured)
- **1917**: I. di Matteo (Italian translation)
- **1919**: Nallino (Italian, provoking a literary controversy with di Matteo)
- **1921**: Reynold A. Nicholson settled the debate and wrote the most penetrating account of Ibn al-Fāriḍ's art and thought, in his *Studies in Islamic Mysticism* (chapter 3)
- **1954–56**: A. J. Arberry produced editions and translations from the Chester Beatty collection

The poem's difficulty made it a test case for whether Arabic mystical poetry could be meaningfully translated and understood in Western terms.

## Relation to Ibn ʿArabī

Though the two men were contemporaries living within the same generation of the thirteenth-century Islamic mystical efflorescence, they were very different: Ibn ʿArabī the powerful systematic prose writer from Andalusia; Ibn al-Fāriḍ the solitary Arabic poet from Cairo. Yet their work is complementary — the *Tāʾiyya*'s shadow play passage draws directly on Ibn ʿArabī's *Futūḥāt*, and both are concerned with the same fundamental vision of divine unity manifesting through created multiplicity. Scholarship has conventionally treated them together as the two summits of thirteenth-century Arabic Sufism: see [[sufi-metaphysics]].

## Historiography

The interpretation of Ibn al-Fāriḍ has been caught between two poles: those who read him as expressing pantheism through poetic means, and those who insist his poetry must be understood as mystical language that operates differently from theological or philosophical propositions. The Nicholson–Arberry tradition in Anglo-American scholarship took his poetry seriously as poetry and as mystical experience simultaneously, without reducing either dimension to the other. The French tradition (Massignon, Dermenghem) emphasized his place in the tradition of divine love.
