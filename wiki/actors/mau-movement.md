## Overview

The Mau (literally "opinion" or "stand") was Western Samoa's major anti-colonial political movement, active from 1926 to 1936, seeking self-determination under the slogan "Samoa mo Samoa" (Samoa for Samoans). It combined Samoan high chiefly leadership with merchant-class support (*'afakasi* — part-Samoan, part-European) and conducted systematic passive resistance against New Zealand mandatory authority. It was the most sustained and organised non-violent anti-colonial movement in the interwar Pacific.

## Context: New Zealand Administration and Richardson's Provocations

New Zealand's 1918–1920 military administration produced "few significant changes in law or policy." The 1918 influenza epidemic — which killed 30 per cent of adult Samoan men and 22 per cent of adult women while American Samoa (blockaded) was spared — was attributed by Samoans to New Zealand administrative negligence. New Zealand responded by blaming Samoan "moral failings." This established the adversarial frame within which the Mau developed.

Administrator Richardson (1923–1928) initially received well, then practised "social engineering on a grand scale": compulsory planting requirements, restrictions on *malaga* (travelling ceremonial parties), proposed individualization of land tenure (matai to subdivide and distribute holdings), and most provocatively the Samoan Offenders Ordinance (1922), which "gave the government the right to banish chiefs and even to revoke their titles." Richardson used this power "often and arbitrarily, to humiliate leading chiefs and punish dissent."

Organised opposition emerged when Richardson "proposed that matai should choose their own successors, replacing family selection" (1926) — attacking the central institution of Samoan social structure. "Even the Faipule baulked."

## Founding and Organisation

"O le Mau" was founded in 1926, taking as its motto Richardson's own slogan: "Samoa mo Samoa." Richardson banished two leaders, Afamasaga and Faumuina, to Apolima Island. "Samoans then began systematic passive resistance and non-compliance with government institutions and regulations."

The movement's membership was broad: "of 9300 adult Samoan men, 8000 signed the petition" summarising grievances presented to the League of Nations in 1928. Leadership included:
- **Tupua Tamasese Lealofi** — one of Samoa's highest-ranking chiefs; died from police wounds on Black Saturday
- **O. F. Nelson** — merchant, son of a European man and Samoan woman; deported repeatedly; presented petition to the League of Nations
- **'Afakasi leaders** (S. H. Meredith and others) — "bicultural" sons of European-Samoan marriages, counted as Europeans by law but Samoan in allegiance

By 1928, when Richardson was replaced by Stephen Allen, "the Mau was performing many governmental functions, such as encouraging copra production and overseeing education."

## Black Saturday (28 December 1929)

A large Mau procession marched through Apia. Police attempted to arrest some participants for non-payment of taxes. "A European policeman fell, and the other officers opened fire. From a nearby police station machine-guns joined the attack. Eleven Samoans were killed, almost all of them high-ranking matai. The three highest-ranking — Tamasese, Tuimaleali'ifano, and Faumuina — were all seriously wounded, and Tupua Tamasese Lealofi later died." Samoans "were convinced that the police had targeted them" given that the high-ranking chiefs "wore distinctive dress and were trying to quiet the crowd when they were shot."

The administration declared the Mau seditious and "called in troops to hunt down supporters. Men fled to the mountains and forests, while soldiers terrorised the villages."

## Women's Mau

"During the early 1930s women carried on the work. Notable among them were Tamasese's widow Alaisala, Losa Taisi (the wife of O. F. Nelson), and the wives of Tuimaleali'ifano and Faumuina. The 'Women's Mau' travelled around Samoa to rally supporters and continued to stage demonstrations." This was a distinctive feature: women's active leadership in public protest in a society with high-status gender norms.

## Resolution

A stand-off lasted until the 1936 New Zealand Labour government election. "Members of the Labour Party had long sympathised with the Mau, and the change resulted in an immediate policy shift. Nelson was freed, returning to a tumultuous welcome, and the Mau was recognised as a legitimate organisation. The Samoan Offenders Ordinance was repealed and a new Fono of Faipule was elected, composed largely of members of the Mau. Moreover, the new Fono had the right to select members of the Legislative Council, giving Samoans — and particularly Mau supporters — a direct role in governance."

Western Samoa became independent in 1962.

## Significance

The Mau was "surprisingly peaceful" by comparison with Melanesian primary resistance. Its combination of chiefly legitimacy, merchant class leadership, and mass passive resistance without armed insurrection made it a Pacific precedent for non-violent anti-colonial politics. The Royal Commission of 1927, despite "voluminous and heartfelt testimony," "supported Richardson's position that the Mau had been instigated by a few *'afakasi* and Europeans" — illustrating the limits of colonial inquiry in assessing movements that challenged the premise of colonial rule itself.

The Mau also illustrates the argument of [[invention-of-the-native]]: New Zealand treated "Samoans as political incompetents" and excluded the Fono from "government" entirely — yet the Mau demonstrated sophisticated political organisation, legal argumentation, and international advocacy (League of Nations petition) that directly contradicted this premise.

## Counterfactual Significance

The 1936 Labour government shift demonstrates the contingency of colonial outcomes: without a sympathetic metropolitan political change, the Mau might have remained an outlaw organisation through independence. The failure of the League of Nations Mandates Commission to hear Nelson's petition illustrates the structural limitations of international governance for colonial peoples in this period.
