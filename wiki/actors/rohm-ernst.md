# Ernst Röhm

Chief of Staff of the [[sturmabteilung-sa|SA]] from 1931 and the most dangerous
internal rival Hitler ever faced. A scarred Great War officer and freebooting
paramilitary organizer, Röhm built the SA to nearly three million men by early
1934 (4.5 million counting the absorbed Steel Helmets) and openly sought a
"second revolution" in which the SA would absorb or replace the 100,000-man
regular army — demanding the Defence Ministry held by Blomberg. Forced on
28 February 1934 to sign an agreement renouncing the militia ambition, he
privately called Hitler a "ridiculous corporal" and was placed under covert
police surveillance. Evans (2005) is emphatic that the "Röhm putsch" was a
fiction: the SS and Gestapo under [[himmler-heinrich|Himmler]] and
[[heydrich-reinhard|Heydrich]] manufactured the putsch evidence, and the
trigger was Blomberg's 21 June warning that Hindenburg would declare martial
law if the SA were not curbed. Hitler personally arrested Röhm at Bad Wiessee
on 30 June 1934 ([[night-of-the-long-knives-1934]]); after refusing a proffered
revolver, Röhm was shot in his Stadelheim cell on 1 July by Theodor Eicke and
another SS officer. His open homosexuality — long tolerated by Hitler ("the SA
is not a moral institution for the education of nice young girls") — was used
posthumously to justify the purge and triggered the regime's escalating
persecution of homosexual men.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Hindenburg's death (August 1934) arrived with the SA question unresolved,
army-regime relations — and the personal soldier's oath to Hitler — could have
developed very differently; the purge was the price of the army's acquiescence
in Hitler's absorption of the presidency.

## Historiography

Höhne (*Mordsache Röhm*) and Longerich's SA study underpin Evans's account;
the non-existence of a real putsch plan is settled scholarship.
