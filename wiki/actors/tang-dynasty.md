# The Tang Dynasty

## Overview

The **Tang** (618–907 CE) was China's cosmopolitan golden age and, with the
**[[byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]]** and the **[[caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]]**, one
of the dominant powers anchoring the **second [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] era**. In
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Holcombe Ch 15, Liu Ch 17) the early Tang was a
"self-consciously multi-ethnic empire" whose rulers posed simultaneously as Chinese
**emperors** and Turkic **qaghans**, while over three centuries it forged the stable
**late-imperial Chinese model** of bureaucratic, examination-based government.

## A Cosmopolitan Empire

Inheriting the reunification of the **[[sui-dynasty|Sui]]**, the Tang ruling house — of
Chinese descent but heavily intermarried with the **Xianbei** — built a richly cosmopolitan
order. In **630 CE** it defeated the Eastern Türks and dominated the steppe; with **Silla**
it conquered **Paekche (660)** and **Koguryŏ (668)**, ending major Japanese involvement on
the continent at the river battle of 663. The capital **Chang'an** drew merchants, monks,
and students from across Eurasia; foreign religions — [[buddhism|Buddhism]], Zoroastrianism,
Manichaeism, Nestorian Christianity — flourished under imperial patronage.

## The Examination State

The Tang turned formal **civil service examinations** into a defining institution; **Empress
Wu Zetian (r. 650–705)**, the only female sovereign in Chinese history, decisively built a
bureaucracy based on **merit rather than birth**. Written tests and the education for them
became a powerful force for cultural cohesion; **woodblock printing** spread texts, and a
**Neo-Confucian revival** (Han Yu) began in the late Tang (see [[confucianism]]). By the
tenth century China was "the world's most developed country," holding perhaps a third of
humanity.

## Decline and Legacy

The **An Lushan rebellion (755 CE)** broke Tang power; the **Battle of Talas (751 CE)**
checked Tang expansion against the **[[caliphate|Arab]]** armies in Central Asia, after which
**Islam** progressively transformed the Silk Roads. The dynasty ended in 907 — but left a
more uniformly Chinese, examination-governed state that proved highly stable, with far fewer
later dynastic changes. Around it, the "traditional" outlines of East Asia crystallized:
independent **Vietnam (939)**, a unified **Korea** under Silla, and an increasingly distinct
**Japan** (the *Tale of Genji*).

## The Tang–Song Transition (CWH V)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (von Glahn, Ch 19) carries the Chinese story past 907.
After the Tang collapse and the brief "Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms"
fragmentation, the [[song-dynasty|Song]] (960–1279) restored the **unified-empire
ideal** that the [[sui-dynasty|Sui]] had re-established in 589 — an ideal that, with
brief interruptions, became the lasting reality of Chinese history and the template
for a multi-state East Asian order. The transition is one of the great hinges of
Chinese history (the "Tang–Song transition" of the historiography): the aristocratic,
cosmopolitan Tang order gave way to the commercialized, examination-governed, and
[[neo-confucianism|Neo-Confucian]] Song world — the Chinese instance of the period's
[[cultural-crystallization|cultural crystallization]]. See [[song-dynasty]].

## Significance

The Tang created the enduring late-imperial Chinese order — meritocratic bureaucracy,
print culture, Neo-Confucianism — and stood at the western-facing hub of the second Silk
Roads era, its cosmopolitanism radiating models of statecraft, writing, and Buddhism across
the **[[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian cultural sphere]]**. Among the most thorough
borrowers was **[[japan|Japan]]**, whose [[taika-reform-645|Taika Reform]] and
[[nara-period|Nara]] state consciously modeled their law codes, bureaucracy, and capital
plan (Nara/Heian on Chang'an's grid) on the Tang — then domesticated them into a
distinctively Japanese civilization.

## Historiography

The Tang is exceptionally well documented (dynastic histories, poetry, Dunhuang
manuscripts, Japanese and Korean records). CWH IV stresses its **cosmopolitan,
multi-ethnic** character against older Sinocentric readings, and tracks the long shift from
the open early Tang to a more ethnically self-conscious late Tang. Debates concern the
social reach of the examination system (still largely aristocratic in practice) and the
economic "Tang–Song transition" toward the world's largest medieval economy.
