# The Pisistratidae (Hippias and Hipparchus)

The **Pisistratidae** were the sons of [[pisistratus|Pisistratus]] — **Hippias** (the
political head) and **Hipparchus** (the cultural patron), with the shadowy Thessalus —
who jointly ruled [[athens|Athens]] from their father's death in **528/7** until the
[[greek-tyranny|tyranny]] was overthrown by Sparta in **510**. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH
Vol. IV]] (Ch. 4, D. M. Lewis) reconstructs their reign from fragmentary and anecdotal
evidence — there is no connected narrative, and "a disproportionate amount" of the
testimony concerns a single day in 514.

## Joint rule and the question of the *tyrannos*

Whether the tyranny "changed its character" after Pisistratus is obscured by the sources'
ambiguity. Thucydides argued combatively that **Hippias** was the elder and the true
*tyrannos*; Herodotus, Aristotle and the fourth-century tradition treat the brothers as
**joint rulers** (like the Bacchiadae or Aleuadae), with Hippias the politically active
partner. Lewis suspects Thucydides "may well have been trying to prove too much," and
notes the tradition gives "no hint of disagreement between them" — unusually stable for
fraternal tyrants (contrast Sicyon, Samos).

## Conciliation and the archon-list

A fragment of the **archon-list** for these years (published 1939, M–L 6) is the great
documentary gain. It shows the tyrants using the **eponymous archonship for control and
conciliation**: Hippias himself held it in 526/5, but so did **[[cleisthenes|Cleisthenes
the Alcmaeonid]]** in 525/4 (revealing a previously unknown reconciliation), **Miltiades**
in 524/3, and the younger Pisistratus (Hippias' son) c. 522. Thucydides records that the
brothers "maintained the existing laws," taking care only that one of their own held
magistracies, and kept the 5 per cent produce-tax.

## Coinage, culture and building

To this reign belongs the change from Athens' old "armorial" coinage to the famous
**"owls"** — the tetradrachm bearing Athena's head and the owl, with the inscription
ΑΘΕ — a "national type" designed to win confidence in overseas trade, underpinned by the
**Laurium silver**. **Hipparchus** was the cultural impresario: he introduced the relay
recitation of **Homer at the Panathenaea**, brought the poets **Anacreon** (from Samos,
522) and **Simonides** to Athens, set up moralizing **herms** along the roads of Attica,
and expelled the oracle-forger Onomacritus. The dynasty's grandest project was the
colossal **temple of Zeus Olympios**, in "demonstrative competition" with the Artemisium
of Ephesus and the Heraeum of Samos — left unfinished at the tyranny's fall and not
completed until Hadrian.

## The assassination and the hardening of rule

In **514** Hipparchus was murdered at the Panathenaea by **Harmodius and Aristogeiton**
(see [[assassination-of-hipparchus-514bce|the assassination of Hipparchus]]). Whatever its
motive, all sources agree that Hippias' rule **grew far harsher afterward**: executions,
exiles, and a search abroad for security — Hippias married his daughter to the son of the
tyrant of **Lampsacus**, "perceiving that they had great influence with King Darius."

## The fall (510)

The exiled **[[cleisthenes|Alcmaeonidae]]** rebuilt the burnt temple of Apollo at Delphi
and (so Herodotus) won the oracle, which told every Spartan enquirer to **"free Athens."**
A first Spartan seaborne expedition under Anchimolius failed against Pisistratid Thessalian
cavalry; in **510** King **[[cleomenes-i|Cleomenes]]** marched by land, besieged the
tyrants on the Acropolis, and — on the chance capture of the Pisistratid children — forced
Hippias to quit Attica in five days. He withdrew to **Sigeum**, his hopes of return
"flickering for thirty years" until extinguished at [[battle-of-marathon-490bce|Marathon]].

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: inheritance of [[pisistratus|Pisistratus']] consolidated tyranny.
contributed_to: its own fall — the assassination hardened the regime, alienated opinion,
and drove the [[cleisthenes|Alcmaeonidae]] to court Delphi and Sparta.
produced: the **owl coinage**, the **Panathenaic Homer**, and a more centralized Athens
in which "it was more readily expected that Athens was the source of justice and decision"
— the ground on which [[cleisthenic-reforms-508bce|Cleisthenes' reform]] would build.

## Historiography

The reign is a textbook case of thin, contradictory, and partisan evidence: **Thucydides**
(combatively revisionist on the tyrannicides), **Herodotus**, the **Ath. Pol.**, Plato's
*Hipparchus*, scattered inscriptions (the archon-list M–L 6, the herm and altar
dedications), and the numismatic sequence of the owls. The chronology of the buildings and
the coinage, and the elder-brother question, remain unresolved.
