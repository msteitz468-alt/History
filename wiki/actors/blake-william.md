# William Blake

English poet, artist, religious mystic and prophet of national renewal (1757–1827), one of the leading figures of the Romantic movement in Britain. His prophetic epic poems gave Druids a prominent — and deeply hostile — place in his personal mythological system. Blake's engagement with Druidry was theologically original rather than archaeologically or historically driven, and it represented the most intellectually distinctive treatment of the subject in Georgian Britain.

## Significance

### Druids in Blake's Prophetic Mythology

Blake's engagement with Druids began in 1794 with *Europe: A Prophecy*, which depicted a fallen angel seeking "his ancient temple serpent-formed" — an allusion to [[actors/stukeley-william|William Stukeley]]'s representation of Avebury as a serpent-plan temple — with "oak-surrounded pillars, formed of massy stones … placed in the order of the stars," a place designed for the service of "God a tyrant crowned" ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]). The image was immediately hostile: Druids personified state religion at its worst.

He returned to Druids in *Vala* (completed by 1803), establishing as their keynote the butchery of human beings in ancient London: "groans ran along Tyburn's brook and along the river of Oxford / Among the Druid temples." In *Milton* (completed by 1810) and above all in *Jerusalem* (probably completed by 1820) the references multiplied. In *Jerusalem* Druids and their stone circles are associated with "howling, wailing, chaos, weeping, torture and bloodshed," equated with the cruel religions of Canaanites, Syrians and Philistines opposed by the Hebrew prophets. Their "rocky circles" offer "permanent remembrance of sin," and the poem's brightest moment comes when "Jesus the whole Druid law removes away from Britain." Blake supplemented these poetic treatments with an 1809 catalogue note accompanying a now lost painting, *The Ancient Britons*, produced for Iolo Morganwg's former ally William Owen.

### Original Theological Framework

What was "sensationally original" (Hutton) about Blake's reworking of existing ideas was his conviction, reached after 1800, that the corruption of true religion had begun in Britain itself. Where previous authors had held that the Druids had originally upheld the patriarchal religion of Noah or Abraham and only later degenerated, Blake reversed the sequence: the fall of true religion into Druidic corruption had happened before Abraham, not after. Abraham's special revelation was precisely a divine remedy for the threat posed by the global spread of the corrupt Druidic faith: "Abraham was called to succeed to the Druidical age, which began to call allegoric and mental signification into corporeal command, whereby human sacrifice would have depopulated the earth" (*The Ancient Britons* catalogue note, cited in [[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]). In Blake's geography, Britain was the original Holy Land; its Druids were both the original ministers of the true faith and the first to corrupt it; and the spread of their "stony Druid temples" from Lambeth across the world — "from Ireland to Peru and Japan" — gave Britain, paradoxically, both primacy and maximum guilt.

Blake equated Druidry not only with ancient priestcraft but with the Enlightenment philosophy of his own day: "Greek philosophy (which is a remnant of Druidism) teaches that man is righteous in his vegetated spectre." Where [[actors/toland-john|John Toland]] had demonized Druids as over-mighty priests, Blake demonized them as deists like Toland himself — an ironic reversal that Hutton highlights as structurally significant. Blake saw the call for reason-based religion and religious toleration as a continuation of the same satanic project that had once demanded human sacrifice under the oaks.

A.L. Owen's assessment, cited by Hutton, captures their role accurately: "without moving from the background, they are, like figures in a striking tapestry, intrusive." Owen further demonstrated that several details of Blake's Druid imagery were drawn from Stukeley, "while in general it opposed Stukeley's own characterization of Druidry" — where Stukeley made Druidry the forerunner of the established Church, Blake made it the religion of Satan.

### Relationship to Iolo Morganwg and Druid Orders

Various attempts have been made to connect Blake to [[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]] and to living Druid orders. Peter Fisher suggested Blake's "four Zoas" in *Vala* reflect Iolo's four circles of existence, though Hutton deems this speculative. Jon Mee demonstrated that Blake's publisher Joseph Johnson was a friend of Iolo, but could not prove the two men met. Blake shared with Iolo an opposition to the slave trade and to war with France, a hatred of priestcraft, and a vision of a more primitive democratic Christianity; both created luxuriant mythical histories of Britain and were fervent local patriots. Their treatments of Druids, however, "developed in precisely opposite directions" — Iolo celebrated a benevolent bardic-Druidic tradition; Blake condemned Druidry as Satanic.

Between 1785 and 1790 Blake lived at 28 Poland Street, two doors from the King's Arms Tavern where Lodge No. 1 of the Ancient Order of Druids met under Henry Hurle. Hutton's assessment is that, while it cannot be proven that Blake did not join, several factors make it unlikely: the lodge held secret meetings specifically designed to exclude outsiders; no biographer has found any reference to Blake's membership; the order never claimed him in its own early histories; the order's benevolent view of Druids was irreconcilable with Blake's own; Blake was not "particularly clubbable" and showed no exceptional enthusiasm for music, the lodge's main activity; and Hurle (if correctly identified) was a pillar of Anglican parish life.

Ross Nichols's posthumously published *Book of Druidry* (1990) claimed Blake as the fifth chief of the Universal Bond, after Toland and Stukeley, and asserted that at his 1804 Chichester trial for allegedly treasonous speech Blake refused to take the oath because he was a Druid. Hutton's investigation of the trial records (edited by G.E. Bentley Jr.) finds that Blake was never called to speak; his barrister conducted the entire defence; and Blake's only words were to cry "'tis false, 'tis false." There is no published book of Blake's in which he mentions being a Druid. Hutton concludes that Nichols "admired Blake and was anxious to claim him as an ancestor" but that to do so "was to reverse the apparent truth of history."

## Historiography

Blake's engagement with Druidry has received relatively little sustained attention in the vast critical literature on his work. Most scholarship treats it briefly, stating his use of Druids as part of his prophetic mythology without systematic analysis. Hutton's treatment in [[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]] contextualizes Blake within the full spectrum of Georgian attitudes toward Druids, arguing that his hostility was consistent with the broader pattern by which evangelical and nonconformist Protestants opposed Druidry, while also recognizing the originality of his specific theological framework. The attempted connections to Iolo Morganwg and to Druid orders are treated as unproven or false by Hutton.
