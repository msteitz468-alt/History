# Ducetius

## Overview

**Ducetius** (d. 440 BCE) was the leader of the **Sicel movement** — the attempt by Sicily's
indigenous **Sicels** to forge an autonomous state in the island's interior during the years
after the fall of the Greek tyrannies. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 7, D. Asheri)
treats him as "a remarkable personage" whose two-decade career (c. 461–440) is the most
striking episode of native agency in Greek Sicily — and, paradoxically, an engine of
**hellenization**.

## The Sicel movement

When the [[western-greek-tyranny|Greek tyrannies]] fell and the coastal cities were
paralysed by *stasis*, the autonomistic impulse spread into the **Sicel interior**. Ducetius,
first appearing in 461 helping to eject the Dorian colonists of Aitna, built a Sicel polity
in stages:

- **459/8** — the synoecism of his birthplace **Menae** into the new polis of **Menainon**
  (the one occasion he is called *basileus*), and the forcible conquest of **Morgantina**.
- **453/2** — the foundation of a sacral-administrative capital at **Palice**, beside the
  ancient sanctuary of the Sicel **Palikoi** (twin volcanic deities), as the centre of a
  Sicel **League** (*synteleia* / *koinon*) uniting "townships of the same people"
  (*homoethneis*) under a common army — its own "barbarized" coinage on Greek models.
- **451** — at its zenith, the League stormed Aitna and besieged **Motyon**, defeating a
  joint Syracusan–Acragantine relief force.

## Defeat, exile, and return

Neither Syracuse nor Acragas could tolerate an expanding Sicel state. In **450** a Syracusan
army routed Ducetius at **Nomae**; deserted by his followers, he threw himself as a suppliant
on Syracuse, which (persuaded by the moderate *chariestatoi*) spared him and **exiled him
with a pension to Corinth**, its mother-city. But in **446**, with Syracusan connivance, he
returned on the pretext of an oracle to found **Kale Akte** ("the Fair Shore") on the north
coast — once more a useful Syracusan agent. He **died of illness in 440**, after which
Syracuse destroyed the Sicel capital and resubjected the hellenized Sicel territory, building
a new land **epicracy**.

## Significance and the hellenization paradox

Asheri's verdict is that the movement was "an utter failure" judged by results — it ended by
midwifing **another Syracusan empire** — but its deepest irony is that it **accelerated Sicel
assimilation**. Ducetius' models were Gelon and Hiero, not the old Sicel kings; his cities
were Greek in plan, his institutions Greek in form, his coinage Greek in model. "Without the
full assimilation of the Greek notions of statehood and urban life, the Sicel movement would
never have seen the light." By the end of the century all non-Greek Sicilian vernaculars had
ceased to be written.
