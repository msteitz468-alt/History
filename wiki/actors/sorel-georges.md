# Georges Sorel (1847–1922)

French engineer-turned-social-theorist, a retired autodidact who became, on
[[sternhell-birth-fascist-ideology-1994|Sternhell's]] account, the pivotal
intellectual figure in the genealogy of [[generic-fascism|fascism]]: the
author of the **antimaterialist revision of Marxism** that, fused with organic
nationalism, produced the fascist synthesis (see [[sternhell-thesis]]).

## From orthodox Marxism to revision

Sorel entered socialist theory in 1893 with a letter to the *Revue
philosophique* praising Marxism as "a true economic science"; in 1894 he
called Marx's theory "the greatest innovation in philosophy for centuries."
He introduced Antonio Labriola (the "father of Italian Marxism," distinct
from the syndicalist [[labriola-arturo|Arturo Labriola]]) to France — per
Croce, Labriola's 25 April 1895 letter to Sorel marks the beginning of
theoretical Marxism in Italy. From the start Sorel stressed Marxism's
*moral* content over its economics, and this ethical emphasis became the
lever of his revisionism.

The break began with his critique of Marxist economics (1897) and was
complete by the Bernstein debate of 1900: "The Marxist theory of value...
no longer has any scientific usefulness." Crucially, Sorel took only
Bernstein's *method* (revision), not his democratic content — he revised in
the opposite, revolutionary direction. He rejected the socialization of
property, invoking Proudhon against Engels: "To reform in a bourgeois
society is to affirm private property." Sternhell emphasizes that the
Sorelians were economic "liberists" — in full agreement with extreme
free-marketeers on economics while rejecting liberalism's philosophy of
natural rights, individualism, and progress. Sorelian Marxism "necessarily
resulted in a negation of liberal democracy and democratic socialism."

## Myth, violence, pessimism

- **Theory of myths** (from the 1899 "Préface pour Colajanni," fully developed
  in *Réflexions sur la violence*, serialized 1906): myths are "systems of
  images" that "have to be accepted in their totality as historical forces" —
  immune to refutation, unlike utopias, which can be analyzed and "direct
  people toward reforms," whereas "our present myths lead people to prepare
  themselves for a battle to destroy what exists." The **general strike** is
  the paradigm myth; Sorel admitted the term "myth" let him "avoid any
  discussion with people who wish to submit the general strike to a detailed
  criticism." The theory is explicitly Bergsonian — revolutionary syndicalism
  as "the fullest practical application of Bergson's thought" — and arose
  against the paradox of the 1900s French strike wave coexisting with
  reformist electoral triumph.
- **Violence** as regenerative morality, not mere instrument: "proletarian
  violence has become a central factor of Marxism"; it "seems to be the only
  means by which the European nations, deadened by humanism, can regain their
  former energy." Sorel distinguished heroic, hate-free proletarian violence
  from bourgeois force and from the Jacobin Terror he despised; "there is no
  need for blood to be shed in torrents."
- **Pessimism** (via Eduard von Hartmann): a "metaphysics of morals";
  optimism is the root of decadence — "the optimist can lead a country to the
  worst catastrophes." His anti-Cartesianism (*Les Illusions du progrès*,
  1906; *Le Procès de Socrate*) made Descartes the founder of "bourgeois
  science" and placed "Socrates, Descartes, Voltaire, Rousseau, the Jacobins,
  and the politicians of the end of the nineteenth century" in one corrupt
  lineage. Antirationalism is, for Sternhell, "the real key to Sorelian
  thought."

## The junction with nationalism

Sorel "came round to integral nationalism during the summer of 1909" after
reading Maurras's *Enquête sur la monarchie*; his tribute to Maurrassism was
reprinted in *L'Action française* (August 1909) with praise for "the
brilliant and profound theoretician of antidemocratic socialism." The
abortive national-socialist journal *La Cité française* (1910, prospectus
signed by Sorel, [[berth-edouard|Berth]], [[valois-georges|Valois]], Variot,
Gilbert) declared: "Democracy is the greatest error of the past century...
it is absolutely necessary to destroy democratic institutions" — language
repeated in the [[cercle-proudhon|Cercle Proudhon]] Declaration (1912). His
own review *L'Indépendance* (March 1911–July 1913) drifted into the Action
française orbit and waged sustained antisemitic campaigns, including
repetition of the ritual-murder blood libel "as if it were an undisputed
historical fact"; Sternhell records flatly that "Sorel believed that the Jews
of eastern Europe ritually murdered Christian children," while noting the
unresolved contradiction of his admiration for Bergson as a "good Jew" and
his interest in Zionism. Sternhell judges the antisemitism "not a consequence
of his subservience to Maurras; a genuine community of ideas existed."

Contemporaries saw the consistency beneath the swings: "Maurras and Lenin
fulfilled the same function" — weapons against bourgeois democracy. Sorel
judged the actual World War harshly, denouncing the Union sacrée; in
September 1919 he added the famous "For Lenin" epilogue to the 4th edition of
*Réflexions*, hailing the Bolshevik Revolution; and eighteen months later
said of the Fascists: "The Fascists are not entirely wrong to invoke my
opinions, for their power very clearly demonstrates the value of triumphant
violence." He "did not disown the use that the Fascists made of his name."

## Legacy to fascism

Sternhell's summary of the inheritance: hatred of the Enlightenment heritage;
rejection of egalitarianism, majority rule, humanitarianism, and pacifism;
elite rule; Catholicism valued for its *social* virtues — "discipline,
chastity, pessimism — and not its faith"; and a revolution that "never
touched private property," laying the foundation for "a revolutionary
capitalism — a capitalism of producers, hostile to the plutocracy and high
finance" — the direct ancestor of [[national-syndicalism]] and fascist
corporatism. Via T. E. Hulme (his English translator), Sorel's ideas also
reached the Anglophone modernists (Pound, Wyndham Lewis, Eliot, Yeats) whom
Sternhell counts among fascism's "fellow travelers." Mussolini named him
first among his masters ("the Sorels, the Péguys, the Lagardelles").

## Historiography

Griffin ([[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]]) names Sorel "the symbolic
father-figure of the theory adopted here" — his account of mobilizing myth
underwrites the [[palingenetic-ultranationalism|palingenetic]] reading of
fascism — while noting Sorel was no mere analyst of myth: he "was driven by
his own palingenetic myth" and tried to catalyze the "psychic epidemics" he
theorized. Sternhell reads Sorel as fascism's chief intellectual progenitor; critics
(Jeremy Jennings, Shlomo Sand, and the De Felice school) stress Sorel's
ambiguity, his lifelong hostility to the state and to political
authoritarianism (he found the bourgeois state and the dictatorship of the
proletariat "very much alike"), and his post-1917 Leninism as evidence
against a straight Sorel→fascism line. See
[[fascism-and-the-political-left]] for the wider debate.

## Griffin (2007): Sorel in the Modernist Genealogy

*Modernism and Fascism* names *Reflections on Violence* (1908) "one of the most influential manifestos of pre-WWI social modernism," calling for movements animated by utopian myths "to regenerate a decadent, effete civilization" — and notes that August 1914 crystallized "precisely the kind of myth-driven populist regeneration movement Sorel had speculated about a decade earlier," triggered by the fatherland-in-danger rather than the general strike. Sorelian syndicalism hybridized with Maurras's integral nationalism to produce Valois's Faisceau; within the Fascist regime, Panunzio's 1933 corporativism speech invoked Sorel's sense of myth directly: corporatism's true purpose was "the myth of the nation for whom millions and millions of men sacrifice themselves." (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)
