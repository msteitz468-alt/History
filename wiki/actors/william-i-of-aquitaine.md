## Role and Background

William I, Duke of Aquitaine, known as "the Pious," ruled one of the great territorial principalities of West Francia in the decades of Carolingian political fragmentation around 900 CE.

## Foundation of Cluny

In 909 or 910, William endowed a Benedictine abbey at [[actors/cluny|Cluny]] in Burgundy with a charter of unusual design: it placed the monastery directly under papal protection rather than under his own or any bishop's lay lordship, explicitly renouncing his own and his heirs' rights of interference in the abbey's affairs and its choice of abbot. This deliberate freedom from local lay and episcopal control became the institutional foundation for Cluny's rise as the center of a vast, centrally governed monastic congregation and the leading force in tenth- and eleventh-century monastic reform.

## Significance

William's charter is one of the most consequential single acts of patronage in medieval monastic history: by insulating Cluny from the proprietary-church pressures (lay control of monastic appointments and revenues) common elsewhere in the period, it enabled Cluny's growth into Latin Christendom's most influential reform movement before the Gregorian reform.
