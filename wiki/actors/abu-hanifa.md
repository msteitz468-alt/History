## Overview

Abū Ḥanīfa (al-Nuʿmān b. Thābit, d. 150/767) was the leading Kūfan jurist of the second/eighth century and the eponymous founder of the Ḥanafite legal school (*madhhab*), the most geographically widespread of the four Sunnite schools. He is the earliest of the four eponyms in chronological order.

Hallaq's account of Abū Ḥanīfa is notable for complicating the "founder" narrative: Abū Ḥanīfa did not construct the Ḥanafite school himself. The doctrinal school bearing his name is the product of the *authority-construction* process by which later jurists — especially his student Abū Yūsuf and the subsequent generation — retroactively attributed to him a unified and originary legal methodology that was in fact a collective and cumulative achievement.

## Historical Role

Abū Ḥanīfa was the leading figure of the Iraqian tradition of *raʾy* (considered opinion) in the second/eighth century. The Iraqian approach, centered on Kūfa and Baṣra, held that the Prophet's Sunna was attested by the living legal practice of the community, transmitted through the Companions who had settled in the garrison towns of southern Iraq — not necessarily by formal hadith reports. This "sunnaic practice" was intertwined with considered opinion, and Iraqian jurists made extensive use of *istiḥsān* (juristic preference), which Abū Ḥanīfa's school systematized.

His school's relation to *raʾy* would later become a source of controversy. Al-Shāfiʿī attacked Iraqian raʾy as arbitrary human legislation, and the Ḥanafite school spent the third/ninth century accommodating the traditionalist demand for textual grounding, resulting in a theoretical remapping of istiḥsān to give it Quranic and Sunnaic justification.

## The Personal School and Its Transformation

Around the middle of the second/eighth century, Abū Ḥanīfa attracted a circle of students who adopted his doctrine: "the term madhhab meant a group of students, legists, judges and jurists who had adopted the doctrine of a particular leading jurist, such as Abū Ḥanīfa or Thawrī … those who adopted or followed a jurist's doctrine were known as aṣḥāb, or associates" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §7). Both Abū Ḥanīfa and his most distinguished student Abū Yūsuf (d. 182/798) initially had independent personal followings — personal madhhabs — that were later merged into the single doctrinal Ḥanafite school.

The Ḥanafite doctrinal school recognized three tiers of authority: (1) the *ẓāhir al-riwāya* attributed to Abū Ḥanīfa and his two students Abū Yūsuf and Shaybānī, carrying the highest authority; (2) the *nawādir*, also from these three masters but without later sanction; and (3) the *nawāzil*, the constructions of later distinguished jurists. This tiered structure reveals that "the imam's legal doctrine was one among many" in the mature doctrinal school — Abū Ḥanīfa's personal opinion enjoyed merely first-among-equals status.

## Authority-Construction

The process by which Abū Ḥanīfa became the "absolute mujtahid" and founding imam of the Ḥanafite school involved two mechanisms Hallaq identifies as general to all four eponyms:

1. **Retrospective dissociation**: Much of Abū Ḥanīfa's doctrine was transmitted from his own teachers, but his followers "claimed by his immediate and later followers to originate with the imam himself, thus severing the link — and with it abolishing the debt — to his predecessors" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §7).

2. **Prospective attribution**: The juristic achievements of his followers were attributed back to Abū Ḥanīfa, expanding his ostensible corpus.

This construction served a social function: "rallying around a single juristic doctrine was probably the only means for a personal school to gain loyal followers and thus attract political/financial support" (Hallaq, ch. 1, §7).

## Counterfactual Significance

The Iraqian tradition Abū Ḥanīfa represented was the primary target of al-Shāfiʿī's critique of raʾy. Had the Ḥanafite school not been able to accommodate the traditionalist synthesis and find textual grounding for its methods, it would likely have declined in the third/ninth century as more austere traditionalism gained ground. Its survival and subsequent geographic dominance (across Central Asia, South Asia, the Ottoman lands) depended on its theoretical flexibility.
