# Bullhē Shāh

Bullhē Shāh (1680–1752), surnamed "the Rūmī of Punjab," is considered the greatest mystical poet in the Punjabi language. He was a contemporary of Shāh ʿAbduʾl-Laṭīf in Sind and of the Delhi Naqshbandī mystics (Shāh Walīullāh, Mīr Dard, Maẓhar Jānjānān), and like them he "found peace in the inner world of love and surrender, singing his mystical songs in order to console himself and his friends in the times of external sufferings and afflictions" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Historical Context

Bullhē Shāh lived near Lahore during the political catastrophes that engulfed the northwestern Subcontinent from Aurangzeb's death (1707): Mughal collapse, Afghan invasions, Sikh expansion, and Mahratta pressure. The same crisis produced a florescence of vernacular mystical poetry across the region — Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashto — as internal spiritual consolation for external disorder.

## Poetry and Themes

Bullhē Shāh used the Punjabi *kāfī* and related vernacular forms. His signature vehicle for mystical meditation is the story of **Hīr and Ranjhā**: a historical romance (16th-century Sargodha) in which the lovers' union is continually thwarted until death — a narrative well known to every Punjabi listener. Bullhē Shāh and his contemporary Hāshim Shāh of Amritsar both used the tale for mystical purposes. Wārith Shāh's *Hīr* (completed ca. 1794) gave it its classical form and includes the key mystical allegorisation:

> "Our soul is the tragic heroine Hīr, / Our body is the lover Ranjhā, / Our spiritual preceptor is the Yogi Balmath..."

This passage identifies the soul as female (a structural feature of both Punjabi and Sindhi mystical poetry, inherited from Hindu devotional tradition), the body as the lover seeking union with the soul, and the Sufi master as the guide.

In addition to his Punjabi poetry, Bullhē Shāh "composed a number of Persian prose treatises, comparable to those of Mīr Dard."

## Relationship to the Tradition

Bullhē Shāh stood within a Punjabi Sufi lineage that traced back (at least legendarily) to Farīduddīn Ganj-i Shakar of Pakpattan (d. 1265), who is credited with early Punjabi mystical verses, and to Mādhō Lāl Ḥusayn (1539–93), who first clearly documented the insertion of folk tales into the mystical register. Sulṭān Bāhū (d. 1691) preceded him in developing the *sīḥarfī* form. Bullhē Shāh represents the mature synthesis.

## Reception and Legacy

Mohan Singh Diwāna, the foremost authority on Punjabi literature, translated and interpreted the mystical significance of the Hīr-Ranjhā narrative. After 1800 the Punjabi language was used "almost exclusively by the Sikhs as their literary medium," while Punjabi Muslims turned to Urdu for sophisticated expression. Bullhē Shāh thus marks the apogee of Muslim Punjabi mystical literature. His verses continue to be performed by *qawwāl* musicians and remain widely known in Pakistan today.
