# Urartu (Kingdom of Van)

**Urartu** — the "Kingdom of Van," centred on **Lake Van** in the eastern Anatolian /
Armenian highlands — was the great **northern rival of [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]]**
in the Early Iron Age, the first powerful state to organize the mountain zone north
of the Taurus. [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] (Ch. 8, R. D. Barnett)
traces it from obscure Hurrian origins to a kingdom that, at its height in the early
eighth century, "for a time threaten[ed] Assyria herself before sinking into
oblivion."

## Origins — Uruatri and Nairi

The name descends from **Uruatri**, a cluster of eight small kingdoms first named by
the Assyrian **Shalmaneser I** (1273 BCE) as rebels he claims to have burnt — Barnett
reads the boast as propaganda but the polities as real and long-lived. As Assyrian
pressure mounted, the Hurrian-speaking principalities of the highlands reorganized
under a new collective name, **Nairi** (the "forty kings of Nairi" defeated by
**Tukulti-Ninurta I**, who reached the "Upper Sea," probably Lake Van). For two and
a half centuries the term lapses; the region re-emerges, now **unified**, as a single
kingdom in the ninth century — a defensive **state born of the Assyrian threat**.

## The state and its kings

From a capital at **Tushpa** (Van) and a state cult of the warrior-god **Haldi**, the
Urartian kings (Sarduri I, Ishpuini, Menua, **Argishti I**, **Sarduri II**) built one
of the ancient world's most distinctive polities:

- **Fortress architecture and hydraulic engineering** — massive cyclopean citadels,
  rock-cut chambers, and irrigation/canal works (Menua's canal to Tushpa) that opened
  the highland valleys to agriculture.
- **Metalwork** — Urartian bronzes (cauldrons, shields, belts, furniture fittings)
  were prized across the Mediterranean and influenced Greek and Etruscan metalcraft.
- **Writing** — adopted the Assyrian cuneiform script for its own (Hurrian-related)
  Urartian language.

## The struggle with Assyria

Urartu and Assyria fought for supremacy over the buffer lands (the Nairi countries,
the [[neo-hittite-states|Syro-Hittite]] north, the routes to Iran) across the ninth
and eighth centuries. Urartu's **zenith coincided exactly with Assyria's "Interval"
(782–745 BCE)**: while three weak Assyrian kings struggled, **Sarduri II** expanded
Urartian power westward into Syria and claims in his own inscriptions to have
defeated **Ashur-nirari V** — the high-water mark Barnett describes. The tide turned
with **[[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]]** (from 745) and broke under
**Sargon II**, whose famous **eighth campaign (714 BCE)** — narrated as a letter to
the god Ashur — devastated the Urartian heartland and looted the Haldi temple at
Musasir. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 22, Grayson) adds the
Assyrian side: [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]] routed **Sarduri II**
personally in Kummukhu (**743**) and in **735** drove the Assyrian army clear
through Urartu to besiege **Tushpa (Van)** itself — the city held, but Urartian
power in the west was broken before Sargon delivered the blow of 714. Thereafter
Urartu (under Rusa, then Sarduri) consistently **avoided confrontation** with
Assyria, sending peaceful envoys to [[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]] and
[[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]] as both kingdoms came under [[cimmerians|Cimmerian]]
and [[scythians|Scythian]] pressure.

## Decline and end

Weakened by Assyria and then by the **Cimmerian and Scythian** incursions sweeping
out of the [[eurasian-steppe|steppe]], Urartu survived into the seventh century but
was finally extinguished around 600 BCE, its territory passing to the **Medes** and,
later, to the Armenians who succeeded it on the same ground. Barnett's verdict —
a kingdom that briefly rivalled Assyria and then "sank into oblivion."

## Liverani: a "continental archipelago" stimulated by Assyria

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] reads Urartu as a **"continental
archipelago"** — regions separated by mountains, population concentrated in valleys and
lakeshores at 1,000–1,500 m, max city size ~20 ha (Bastam), most people in unfortified
villages. Its state-building rested on a standing army (~100 chariots, a few thousand horse,
~20,000 foot — adequate locally, far below Assyria, so survival depended on inaccessible
terrain) and on **strategic resources** (metals, **horse-breeding** as a royal right, timber).
Crucially, he argues Urartu's very formation was **stimulated by Assyrian demand** for
Armenian resources: it developed partly by *imitating* Assyria (its earliest inscriptions
written in Assyrian) and partly on a different resource balance (more raw materials, less
labour). Urartu pioneered the **qanat** (before it spread to Iran), and its bronze cauldrons
and columned-hall/tower-temple architecture made it a **transmission belt** to Iran and
Greece (Median/Persian *apadana* features; cauldrons in Greek sanctuaries). Sargon II's
sack of Musasir (714) broke Rusa I's religious reform; the **Cimmerians** then caught all
three eastern-Anatolian powers (Assyria, Urartu, Phrygia) off guard. The fall (~590 BCE) is
textually undocumented, finalised by the Medes (Scythian arrowheads hint at Scythian
participation), masking a long ethnic shift from Hurrian-speaking Urartians to
Indo-European-speaking **Armenians**. See [[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]].

## Historiography

Urartian studies are comparatively young (Barnett opens with their "rise and
progress"): the kingdom was reconstructed largely from **Assyrian references** plus
Urartian royal inscriptions and the spectacular archaeology of its fortresses and
bronzes. Its Hurrian linguistic affiliation ties it to the deeper second-millennium
history of the [[mitanni|Hurrian world]]; its art is a major channel of Near
Eastern influence on the early Mediterranean.
