## Overview

Paul John Keating was Treasurer of Australia under Bob Hawke (1983–1991) and Prime Minister from December 1991 to March 1996. "An astute, self-educated, conviction politician," he regarded himself as the architect of the economic reforms of the 1980s and "grew contemptuous of the prime minister's lack of vision" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). His prime ministership attempted to supplement the economic reform agenda with a cultural and social transformation: engagement with Asia, Indigenous reconciliation, and the republic debate.

## Role and Major Decisions

As Treasurer, Keating was the enforcer of economic discipline. His 1986 "banana republic" warning — that unless performance improved Australia would "just end up being a third rate economy…a banana republic" — was designed to "soften up his audience for even more public sector reduction, wage restraint and ruthless competition" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7). He described himself as a "storyteller to the masses": "in the 1980s he had adopted the leading script of the day, economic reform, and turned it into a story of necessary change."

As Prime Minister, Keating's distinctive contributions included:

**Compulsory superannuation (1992)**: introduction of mandatory employer contributions to worker retirement savings, supplementing the non-contributory pension scheme, increasing national savings, and allowing the state to redirect pension savings to other areas of need.

**Mabo legislation (Native Title Act 1993)**: Following the High Court's *Mabo* decision of June 1992 overturning the doctrine of *terra nullius*, Keating enacted legislation to regulate native title claims. The legislation "set high thresholds for successful claims, making demonstration of traditional and unbroken links difficult, so that extinguishment of native title after long periods of settler occupation and use was the outcome in many cases" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7).

**Redfern Park Speech (December 1992)**: Keating demanded that Australians recognise "it was we who did the dispossessing…took the traditional lands and smashed the traditional ways of life…committed the murders…took the children from their mothers" — the most explicit prime-ministerial acknowledgement of colonial violence to that date.

**1993 election victory**: Hewson's "Fightback!" platform proposed even faster market reform; Keating won against expectations by forcing a debate about the role of the state in providing public goods. "For Hewson it was said to be the 'unloseable election'. Yet he lost" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 7).

**Republic debate**: Keating "allied with the republican cause explicitly to encourage 'a renewed sense of national unity'" and set up the committee led by Malcolm Turnbull that recommended a minimalist republican model. Keating proposed taking the model to the 1996 election, but lost office before he could do so.

**"Working Nation" (1994) and "Creative Nation"**: Jobs compact for the disadvantaged and arts/national identity package, representing a social-democratic revision of neoliberalism that "failed to cut through."

## Defeat and Legacy

Keating's 1996 defeat to Howard was attributed to his failure to convince electors of the significance of his "new directions" once economic trends turned sour again in 1995. He had no persuasive rejoinder to the claim that Australians had been allowed "five minutes of economic sunlight before the sky fell in again." The substantive continuity between Keating's economic management and Howard's was substantial — "neither Howard nor Keating would admit it" — but Howard successfully differentiated his approach rhetorically.

## Counterfactual Significance

Keating's compulsory superannuation reform is arguably the most durable structural change of his prime ministership — by 2013 it was accumulating trillions in managed retirement assets and had fundamentally altered Australian savings behaviour. Without the Redfern speech and subsequent reconciliation agenda, the Mabo decision would likely have been implemented in a more restrictive form; the political opening for Indigenous recognition in the 2000s required the Keating frame even as Howard rejected it.
