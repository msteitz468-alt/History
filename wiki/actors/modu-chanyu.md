# Modu Chanyu (c. 234–174 BCE)

> *Detailed biography page for the Biography Hub:* see also [[xiongnu-empire-formation-209bce]] · [[battle-of-baideng-200bce]] · [[heqin-policy]] · [[xiongnu]]

**Modu** (Chinese romanization of his name; Wade-Giles: Mo-tun; also rendered Maodun) was the founder and first great *chanyu* (ch'an-yü, supreme ruler) of the [[xiongnu|Xiongnu]] nomadic empire. He killed his father Touman around 209 BCE, seized the leadership of the Xiongnu confederation, and through a combination of military centralization, conquest, and ruthless dynastic consolidation built the first historically documented nomadic empire in world history. His humiliation of the early [[han-dynasty|Han dynasty]] at the [[battle-of-baideng-200bce|encirclement of P'ing-ch'eng]] (200 BCE) forced China into the subordinating [[heqin-policy|ho-ch'in appeasement treaty]] and inaugurated a bipolar world order in East Asia that endured for two centuries.

---

## Overview

Modu's reign transformed the political landscape of East Asia. Before him, the steppe nomads north of China — however troublesome — were qualitatively less threatening than the unified [[xiongnu|Xiongnu]] empire he created. After him, the [[han-dynasty|Han dynasty]] was forced for decades to accept tributary obligations, to send princesses as diplomatic wives, and to acknowledge the chanyu as an equal of the Chinese emperor. Di Cosmo writes that unless the qualitative difference between the pre-empire steppe nomads and the Xiongnu of the Han period is recognized, "we would have to conclude, paradoxically, that the states of Chao and Yen were more powerful and effective in their struggle against the nomads than the Han dynasty under Kao-tsu" (*Shih chi* 110).

Modu's historical significance rests on three pillars: (1) his creation of an absolutely loyal personal bodyguard that enabled a coup against the traditional tribal aristocracy; (2) his institution of a centralized, pyramidal political-military structure (the twenty-four *ta ch'en*) that replaced loose tribal confederation with a coherent state; and (3) his territorial expansion that extended Xiongnu authority from Manchuria to Central Asia, establishing a tributary system that supported the Xiongnu court and army.

## Rise to Power

### The Hostage and the Return

Modu's early life is narrated by [[sima-qian|Ssu-ma Ch'ien]] in *Shih chi* 110. His father, Chanyu T'ou-man, wished a son of another wife to succeed him. To remove Modu as a rival, T'ou-man sent him to the Wu-sun people as a hostage, then attacked the Wu-sun, hoping they would kill their hostage in retribution. Modu escaped, returned to the Xiongnu, and his father — impressed with his warrior ability — gave him command of ten thousand cavalry. This proved to be T'ou-man's fatal error.

### The Whistling Arrow: Creation of the Bodyguard

The story of Modu's coup d'état is one of the most vivid episodes in early steppe history. According to Ssu-ma Ch'ien, Modu created a bodyguard of absolutely loyal warriors through a three-stage test of obedience:

1. He ordered each man to shoot Modu's favorite horse with a "whistling arrow" (*ming-ti*), executing any who hesitated.
2. He ordered each man to shoot Modu's favorite wife; again a few hesitated, and they were summarily executed.
3. Once total obedience had been demonstrated, Modu ordered his followers to shoot his own father, T'ou-man. "Apparently this time no one failed to discharge his arrows."

Di Cosmo notes the crucial distinction between this episode and the superficially similar story of Sun-tzu training palace women: "In the case of Modun, the creation of an absolutely loyal bodyguard was part of a subversive project." The exercise was preparation for a coup against the tribal aristocracy, not merely a demonstration of military discipline. On these grounds, a derivation of Modu's story from Sun-tzu's can be discarded.

### Patricide and Accession (c. 209 BCE)

Having killed his father, Modu became chanyu and immediately proceeded to defend the Xiongnu against the aggression of other nomadic tribes — particularly the Eastern Hu (Tung Hu), who were at that moment "very powerful" (*Shih chi* 110), and the Yuezhi, who were "flourishing." The timing was critical: the crisis ignited by [[qin-northern-campaigns-215bce|Meng T'ien's Qin expedition]] into the Ordos (215 BCE) had weakened the Xiongnu, upset the balance of power in the steppe, and "made [the Xiongnu] therefore the target of other Inner Asian peoples" (Di Cosmo). Modun's rise to power occurred precisely in this interval of crisis and was itself a product of it.

## Major Decisions and Consequences

### Institution of the Twenty-Four High Dignitaries

Immediately upon seizing power, Modu reorganized Xiongnu political structure. Ssu-ma Ch'ien provides the first detailed account of an early Inner Asian nomadic ruling elite:

> "There are the Left and Right Wise Kings, Left and Right Lu-li Kings, Left and Right Generals, Left and Right Commandants, Left and Right Household Administrators and Left and Right Ku-tu Marquises. . . . Starting from the Wise Kings of the Left and Right, down to the Household Administrators, the most important ones [command] ten thousand horsemen, the least important a few thousand; altogether they are referred to as the twenty-four high dignitaries (*erh-shih-ssu ta ch'en*)." (*Shih chi* 110, 2890–2)

This was not a tribal council but a "pyramidal structure of 'kings' and military commanders." The Wise Kings of the Left and Right and the Lu-li Kings held the largest territorial appanages; the Ku-tu Marquises assisted in government; the remaining dignitaries were members of the court holding civil and military positions. Three aristocratic clans — Hu-yen, Lan, and (more recently) Hsü-pu — monopolized the highest offices. The decimal military structure (tens, hundreds, thousands) became an enduring feature of Inner Asian state organization, attested also in the later Türk empire with twenty-eight high dignitaries rather than twenty-four — a difference that, Di Cosmo argues, reflects genuine institutional continuity rather than Chinese rhetorical analogy.

### Territorial Expansion

Modu's conquests created an empire stretching across the inner Asian steppe:

- **East**: Defeated the Eastern Hu (Tung Hu) thoroughly; eastern wing extended from Shang-ku commandery to the land of the Hui-mo and Ch'ao-hsien (northern Korea).
- **North**: Subjugated the Hun-yü, Ch'ü-yi, Ting-ling, Ko-k'un, and Hsin-li, "located in northern Mongolia and western Siberia" (*Shih chi* 110, 2893).
- **South**: Recovered the land taken by Meng T'ien; pushed the border south of the Yellow River; conquered the Lou-fan and the king of Po-yang; absorbed territories in present-day Ning-hsia, northern Shansi, Shensi, and Hopei, and all of Liao-ning.
- **West**: Through his Right Wise King (in the 170s BCE), destroyed the Yuezhi, driving them westward into Central Asia (where they eventually became the Kushans).

The chanyu's personal court was located in the area of the later Tai and Yün-chung commanderies, northeast of the Yellow River's Great Bend. The site of Lung-ch'eng (probably southwest of present-day Ulan Bator) served as the location for the fifth-month sacrifices to ancestors, Heaven and Earth, and the deities.

### The Battle of Baideng and the Ho-ch'in Treaty

In 200 BCE the king of Hann (a northern Han satrapy) defected and allied with the Xiongnu. Han Kao-tsu personally led troops north; despite frigid conditions that cost twenty to thirty percent of his soldiers their fingers to frostbite, the Han army pressed on to P'ing-ch'eng. Modu then moved against Kao-tsu with cavalry said by the sources to number 400,000 — a figure Di Cosmo estimates is inflated by a factor of ten — blocked the emperor at the mountain of Pai-teng, and surrounded the Han soldiers at P'ing-ch'eng for seven days before allowing them to withdraw. The [[battle-of-baideng-200bce|encirclement]] was a total Xiongnu victory.

Modu subsequently imposed tributary conditions formalized in the [[heqin-policy|ho-ch'in treaty]] of 198 BCE: a yearly payment of silk, wine, and grain; a marriage alliance with an imperial princess; and diplomatic recognition of the chanyu as an equal of the Han emperor. The treaty represented "a fundamental change in the Chinese conception of foreign relations" — unprecedented and deeply humiliating. Modu compounded the affront by sending the empress dowager Lü Hou a marriage proposal during the reign of Emperor Hui (194–188 BCE), to which Lü Hou was forced to reply with diplomatic submission and gifts of horses rather than military retaliation.

### A Bipolar World Order

By the reign of Emperor Wen (179–157 BCE) the Xiongnu empire had reached the acme of its expansion. In a diplomatic communication to Emperor Wen, the chanyu declared: "All the people who draw the bow have now become one family and the northern region (*pei chou*) has been pacified" (*Shih chi* 110, 2896). The treaty of 162 BCE made explicit the bipolarity: the Xiongnu would rule over "the nation of the archers to the north of the Great Wall," and the settled people in the south would be ruled by the Chinese emperor. This was, Di Cosmo argues, a recognition of "the principle of great-power primacy" — mutual acknowledgment of separate areas of influence — that structured East Asian international relations until Emperor Wu's aggressive campaigns began in 141 BCE.

## Counterfactual Significance

Di Cosmo's analysis highlights that Modu's personal role was decisive in the crucial interval between Meng T'ien's expedition (215 BCE) and the collapse of the Qin (206 BCE). The crisis created by the Qin invasion was real and severe, but economic need and militarization do not automatically produce a state: "Economic need, however, did not automatically produce political unity. On the contrary, among the tribal peoples and chiefdoms of Inner Asia on the eve of the emergence of a state the more common picture was one of social disaggregation." The creation of the absolutely loyal bodyguard — a technique for which Di Cosmo cites parallels in Persian, Scythian, and later Turco-Mongol empires — was Modu's specific and contingent contribution. Without his personal genius for organization and his willingness to commit patricide, the crisis might have produced only fragmentation. The coincidence of the Qin invasion's crisis with Modu's peculiar solution to succession politics produced the Xiongnu empire; either factor alone might not have been sufficient.

## Historiography

### Sources and Their Biases

The primary source for Modu's career is **Ssu-ma Ch'ien's** *Shih chi*, chapter 110, "Account of the Xiongnu" — the foundational text and also the primary distortion. Ssu-ma Ch'ien wrote from the perspective of the Han imperial court during the reign of Emperor Wu, decades after the events he narrates. His account of Modu's rise contains "legendary and romanticized elements," as Di Cosmo notes, though "to the extent that we accept the historical existence of Modun, we cannot exclude that his rise to power was achieved through the creation of an efficient bodyguard and the slaying of his own father." The numbers given for Modu's forces (400,000 cavalry at Baideng) are demonstrably exaggerated; Di Cosmo estimates the actual figure was inflated by a factor of ten. The *Shih chi* account is echoed and supplemented by the *Han shu* (Chapter 94), which preserves the texts of diplomatic correspondence, including Modu's insulting letter to Empress Dowager Lü Hou.

### Ethnic Identity Debates

The ethnic and linguistic affiliation of Modu's Xiongnu has been debated since the eighteenth century. H. Deguignes (1756–58) identified the Xiongnu with the Huns who invaded the Roman Empire; Hirth, de Groot, and Shiratori endorsed a Turkic equation. Pulleyblank (1962) argued for a Yenisei-Siberian language group. Bailey argued for Iranian speakers; Doerfer denied any relationship with known languages. Chinese scholarship generally accepts a Mongol ethnic stock, while Mongolian scholars trace the historical Mongols to the Xiongnu. Di Cosmo concludes that "the Hsiung-nu confederation was a mixture of different ethnic and linguistic groups, albeit one whose 'kingly' language . . . is not currently identifiable."

### State Formation Debates

Whether the Xiongnu state was a genuine state, a tribe-state, or merely a tribal confederation remains debated. Masao Mori emphasized internal political process and the centralization of the chanyu's clan. Nobuo Yamada argued the Xiongnu were a loose political organization, essentially a tribal confederation. Barfield saw the Hsiung-nu imperial confederacy as a political entity organized primarily for extracting tribute from China. Di Cosmo's revisionist argument — the most fully developed in *Ancient China and Its Enemies* — places the Qin invasion of the Ordos as the decisive external catalyst, interprets Modu's coup as a response to that crisis, and reads the resulting political structure as a genuine "tribe-state" — not controlled by tribal constituencies, but structured like a tribe in terms of hierarchies and access to power.

### Relationship to Later Steppe Empires

Di Cosmo argues for institutional continuity between the Xiongnu and later Inner Asian states. The twenty-four *ta ch'en*, the appanage system, the decimal military structure, and the sacral investiture of the chanyu as "appointed by Heaven" all reappear in modified form in the Türk, Uighur, Khitan, Jurchen, and Mongol empires. The Hsiung-nu case is therefore not an isolated phenomenon but the founding instance of a distinctively Inner Asian political tradition.

## Modu and the Origins of the Silk Roads (Benjamin 2018)

[[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin]] retells the *Shiji* legend of Modu's
rise — the **whistling-arrow** drill, the patricide of Touman, the disciplined execution of
all who hesitated — and dates his reign **209–174 BCE**. Under Modu the [[xiongnu|Xiongnu]]
reached peak power (Han sources claim 300,000+ archers, probably inflated) and forced the
[[han-dynasty|Han]] into **[[han-dynasty|heqin]] tribute** of silk, grain, and princesses.
Decisively for Benjamin's thesis, Modu's raids on the [[yuezhi|Yuezhi]] from 207 BCE
culminated in their catastrophic defeat **c. 166 BCE** — the king's skull made into a
drinking cup — driving the Yuezhi migration to [[bactria|Bactria]] that ultimately produced
the [[kushan-empire|Kushan Empire]] and opened the [[silk-roads|First Silk Roads Era]].
