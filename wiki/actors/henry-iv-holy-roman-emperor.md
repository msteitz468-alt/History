# Henry IV, Holy Roman Emperor

**Henry IV** (1050–1106; king of Germany from 1056, emperor 1084–1105) was the
[[salian-dynasty|Salian]] ruler whose reign was dominated by the
**[[investiture-contest|Investiture Contest]]** with [[gregory-vii|Pope Gregory VII]]
and by rebellion within Germany. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Hanna Vollrath,
Ch. 3) makes him the figure on whom the "crisis of medieval Germany" centred — a ruler
"faced with problems that left him and most of his contemporaries without orientation"
as the revolutionary force of the [[gregorian-reform|Gregorian Reform]] combined with a
Saxon revolt to threaten the survival of his kingship.

## Career

Henry acceded as a **child of five** (1056); a contested minority and regency weakened
royal authority and alienated the **Saxon** nobility, whose revolt (from 1073) ran in
parallel with the gathering conflict with the reform papacy. His attempt to assert
traditional royal control over the church — above all **lay investiture** of bishops —
collided head-on with Gregory VII's prohibitions:

- At the **Synod of Worms (1076)** Henry and his bishops declared Gregory deposed;
  Gregory responded by **excommunicating and deposing** Henry and releasing his subjects
  from their oaths.
- Faced with a German princely revolt that exploited the excommunication, Henry made his
  dramatic penance at **Canossa (January 1077)**, obtaining absolution — but the princes
  elected an anti-king (Rudolf of Rheinfelden) regardless, and civil war followed.
- A second excommunication (1080) led Henry to install the antipope **Clement III**
  (Wibert of Ravenna), who crowned him emperor in Rome (1084), driving Gregory into
  exile.
- Henry's last years were consumed by renewed papal conflict and the **rebellion of his
  sons**: first Conrad, then **Henry V**, who deposed and imprisoned his father. Henry
  IV died in 1106, still excommunicate, and was for years denied proper burial.

## Counterfactual Significance

Henry IV's reign is the hinge of the German monarchy's medieval trajectory. By staking
royal authority on the investiture of bishops — the foundation of the
[[salian-dynasty|Salian]] system of governing through the imperial church — he made the
[[gregorian-reform|reform]] an existential threat, and his defeats (Canossa as enduring
symbol; the empowerment of the princes who exploited every crisis) contributed to the
long-run **weakening and fragmentation** of German kingship relative to its French and
English rivals. Whether a less confrontational ruler could have absorbed the reform
without this damage is the implicit counterfactual; NCMH IV stresses how far the
collision was structural rather than merely personal.

## From Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

[[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]]’s Canossa narrative: after the **1076 Lenten synod** Gregory excommunicated Henry and deprived him of Germany and Italy, releasing subjects from allegiance. Facing German opposition, Henry agreed to appear at Augsburg (February 1077); intercepting Gregory at **Canossa** (Matilda of Tuscany’s castle), he waited **three days barefoot** as penitent (**25 January**). Matilda and Abbot Hugh of Cluny counselled absolution, granted on the third day. Henry returned and civil war followed with **Rudolph of Swabia** as anti-king. Logan also notes Henry’s earlier **Milan** intervention (eliminating two claimants, consecrating a third) as the catalyst that led Gregory on **8 December 1075** to threaten excommunication and deposition, and Henry’s synod that “deposed” Gregory as invalidly elected.
