## Overview

**Kinda** was a central Arabian tribal confederation, attested from the 3rd century CE,
that served for over two centuries as the leading Arab client of [[himyar]] and, in the
6th century, briefly as a Roman-cultivated proxy of its own. Kinda's prominence rests on
a striking historiographical mismatch: Arab-Islamic literary tradition preserves a rich
narrative of "kings of Kinda," while the contemporary South Arabian epigraphic record —
which gives Kinda pride of place among Himyar's Arab allies — says almost nothing about
individual Kindite rulers.

## Origins and Himyarite Clientship

The earliest inscription naming Kinda dates to the 3rd century, found at **Qaryat al-Faw**
(300 km northeast of [[najran]]), a small city with temples, a necropolis, and
Hellenistic-influenced statuary. Inscriptions from the Great Temple at Marib record a
Sabaean punitive expedition against Kinda — the capture and replacement of a Kindite
leader — possibly triggered by interference with Mediterranean-bound trade. Kinda
subsequently came under Himyarite control and, together with the tribes Murad and
Madhhij (largely subordinate to Kinda), regularly supplied troops for the dhu-Yazan-led
Himyarite campaigns into Arabia Deserta from the 4th century onward. Kinda "usually
appears first in lists of Arab allies" in Himyarite royal inscriptions, and its prestige
grew steadily across the 4th–5th centuries.

A late-3rd-century Kindite king, **Malik son of Muawiyat**, dedicated a bronze statuette
at the Great Temple at Marib recording his submission to the Himyarite king Shammar
Yuharish — since this predates Himyar's turn to "Judeo-Monotheism" (see [[himyar]]),
this Kindite ruler was presumably still polytheist. Only two Kinda-authored inscriptions
survive in total, sharply limiting direct evidence for the confederation's later
religious affiliation; Christian Robin suggests Kindite elements closest to the Himyarite
court likely adopted Judeo-Monotheism, while Kindite leaders further north, near Najran
and on missionary routes from al-Hira, may have differed. By the Justinianic era
(527–65) political calculation, not religious alignment, appears to have driven Roman
courtship of Kinda's princes.

## "Kings of Kinda"

The 9th-century *Kitab al-Muhabbar* of Ibn Habib and al-Tabari preserve a genealogy of
"kings of Kinda," beginning with **Hujr b. Amr**, named as Himyarite deputy over Maadd
under king Abikarib Asad, with a line of successors locked in rivalry with the kings of
al-Hira (see [[lakhmids]]). Hujr is very likely the "Hujr, son of Amr, king of Kiddat
[Kinda]" of an inscription found in the Nafud al-Musamma (rediscovered in 2014 after
being "lost" to a geographic transcription error; dated mid-5th century). Arab-Islamic
genealogical tradition also links the Lakhmid queen Hind the Elder to a Kindite
great-grandfather, Hujr — an unverifiable claim from other sources but consistent with
close Kinda–Lakhmid dynastic contact.

In the 6th century Justinian's ambassadors (Julianus, then Nonnosus) attempted to enlist
Kindite military cooperation against Persia via the appointment of "Caïsus"/**Kaisos**
(Qays) of Kinda over Maadd, part of a broader diplomatic push toward Himyar and
[[aksum]]; the scheme yielded little practical result. An Aksumite inscription copied by
Cosmas Indicopleustes (the Adulis inscription) may reference Kinda under the name
"Cinaedocolpitae."

## Historiography

Kinda illustrates a recurring problem in South Arabian/Arabian historiography: the
striking gap between the epigraphic record (which names Kinda constantly but rarely
gives individual leaders) and the later Arab-Islamic literary tradition (which builds an
elaborate royal genealogy around a handful of possibly-identifiable names). Christian
Robin has been the principal modern scholar reconciling the two bodies of evidence.
