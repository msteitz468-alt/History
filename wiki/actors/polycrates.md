# Polycrates of Samos

**Polycrates** was the tyrant of **Samos** whose reign (to 522 BCE) marked the **acme
of the eastern Greeks** — the high point, for Herodotus and Thucydides, of East Greek
sea power, engineering, and courtly splendour before the shadow of Persia closed in.
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 39a, J. M. Cook) treats him.

## The thalassocrat

Having seized power by a coup (perhaps continuing the policies of a predecessor,
possibly his father Aeaces), Polycrates made [[ionia|Samos]] the dominant sea power of
the Aegean. Herodotus says "everything he turned his hand to prospered": he kept a
fleet of a **hundred penteconters** (turning to the newer **triremes**), a thousand
archers, and the first **ship-sheds** mentioned in the Greek world; he dominated the
islands, "not refraining from indiscriminate piracy" (his cynical dictum: a friend is
more grateful to have stolen goods returned than never to have been robbed), fought
Miletus, captured a Mytilenaean relief fleet, and repelled a Spartan expedition sent to
aid his enemies. He aimed, Herodotus says, at **ruling Ionia and the islands** — an
ambition pursued through alliance first with **[[saite-egypt|Amasis of Egypt]]**, then
(when Persia rose) with **Cambyses**.

## Samos as showplace

Under Polycrates Samos became "a worthy capital." Herodotus rated its engineering the
**greatest works among the Greeks**: a strong circuit wall around the three-quarter-mile
city; the kilometre-long **tunnel-aqueduct of Eupalinus of Megara**, dug from both ends
to meet under the mountain; a great mole sheltering the harbour; and the giant **Heraion
dipteron** (the largest Greek temple, though begun before his reign). He was a lavish
**patron of arts and sciences** — the poets **Anacreon of Teos** and **Ibycus of
Rhegium**, the physician **Democedes of Croton**, the engineer Eupalinus — making his
court a byword for graciousness and a touch of luxury (though Cook warns against
charging the energetic eastern Greeks with "effeminacy").

## Death and significance

Polycrates' ambition betrayed him: lured to the mainland by the Persian satrap
**Oroetes**, he was treacherously seized and killed in **522 BCE**. His fall — within a
generation of Cyrus's conquest of [[lydia|Lydia]] and the fall of Babylon — symbolizes
the moment when the **independent brilliance of the East Greeks gave way to Persian
domination**.

## Causal significance

- The **personification of the East Greek acme** — sea power, engineering, and cultural
  patronage at their Archaic height (see [[ionia]]).
- A model of the Archaic **tyrant as state-builder and patron**, comparable to the
  Cypselids of Corinth and [[pisistratus|Pisistratus]] of Athens (whose ally Lygdamis of
  Naxos Polycrates in turn helped to power).
- His death prefigures the **Persian absorption of the Aegean** that leads to the
  Ionian Revolt and the Persian Wars.
