## Overview

Cynegils, king of the Gewisse (later called the West Saxons), was baptized by the missionary bishop Birinus, probably c. 640, with [[oswald-of-northumbria|Oswald of Northumbria]] standing as godfather. His baptism is the first secure chronological anchor in early West Saxon history. He received Birinus and established the first episcopal see for the western Saxons at Dorchester-on-Thames. He is the central figure in the conversion of the Gewisse, though the process was slow and incomplete: his own son and successor [[cenwealh-of-wessex]] was still a pagan in the 640s.

## Baptism and the Oswald Connection

Bede records that Oswald, king of Northumbria, was present at the baptism of Cynegils and stood as his sponsor, and that he was "associated in the establishment of the see of Dorchester-on-Thames" (HE III, 7). Kirby notes that Oswald was still securing his position as king over the northern Angles in the mid-630s, making his presence in the Thames valley before the late 630s unlikely. The baptism is therefore probably dated to the late 630s, possibly c. 640. Oswald subsequently married Cynegils' daughter Cyneburh (HE III, 17).

The relationship between Oswald and Cynegils is characterised by Kirby as one of mutual benefit between an overlord and a dependant: "The relationship between Oswald and Cynegils ... may have been relatively cordial and an example of an arrangement between an overlord and a dependant which was mutually beneficial."

## Military and Political Context

The Chronicle A associates a Cwichelm with Cynegils in a campaign against the Britons in 614 and against Penda of Mercia at Cirencester in 628 (both dates probably too early by several years). Kirby notes that the Cirencester agreement appears to have given Penda control of the lower Severn, marking "a substantial loss of influence on the part of the western Saxon kings which was never made good." Whether Cwichelm was Cynegils' son or a separate king (or an aetheling) is uncertain. A "King Cwichelm" whose regnal length could be fitted within Cynegils' thirty-one-year span may have preceded him.

In the late 620s, Cwichelm (identified by Bede as king of the West Saxons, and possibly the same Cwichelm associated with Cynegils) sent an assassin to kill [[edwin-of-northumbria|Edwin of Northumbria]] (HE II, 9), apparently in response to Edwin's unwelcome interference in the Thames valley. Edwin retaliated militarily.

## Genealogical Uncertainty

Kirby identifies multiple princes named Cynegils in the West Saxon genealogical record, and shows that "West Saxon tradition knew of several princes of the name of Cynegils but was uncertain which was the first Christian king." Different genealogies assign the Cynegils baptized by Birinus to different branches of the royal family (son of Ceola/Ceol via Cynric, or son of Cuthwine via Ceawlin), and none can be relied upon.

The received tradition placing Cynegils as Ceawlin's grandson or great-grandson is, in Kirby's view, a product of later dynastic genealogical construction: "The received genealogical tradition ... is almost certainly the consequence of a desire to accommodate Ceawlin at an appropriate point within the royal pedigree." Kirby also raises the possibility that Cenwealh was actually Cynegils' grandson rather than son: "it could be that even here one generation has been omitted."

## Episcopal Foundation at Dorchester-on-Thames

Birinus established his see at Dorchester-on-Thames in connection with Cynegils' baptism c. 640. This first episcopal see for the Gewisse was later abandoned as Mercian power extended into the upper Thames valley. Wulfhere of Mercia had a royal residence near Dorchester by the early 670s, and in the late 670s Dorchester briefly functioned as a Mercian see. The abandonment of Dorchester is described by Kirby as "a fundamental dislocation in the history of the Gewisse."

## Counterfactual Significance

Cynegils' acceptance of baptism, facilitated by the Oswald alliance, was the formal beginning of Christian kingship among the Gewisse. Without it, the Dorchester see would not have been established and the subsequent see at Winchester (under Cenwealh) would have had no institutional predecessor. However, Christianity's hold on the western Saxons remained tenuous for decades after his reign, suggesting his baptism was more a political act — securing the northern alliance — than a cultural transformation.

## Historiography

The chief source is Bede's Historia Ecclesiastica (HE III, 7), supplemented by the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. Neither provides a reliable absolute chronology for Cynegils' reign. The Chronicle A places his accession in 611, but Kirby judges all pre-baptism dates for the West Saxons to be chronologically unacceptable. The thirty-one-year regnal figure in the West Saxon Genealogical Regnal List is regarded with suspicion. The fixed point of c. 640 for the baptism — inferred from Oswald's chronology — is the only anchor. The multiple appearances of the name Cynegils in the genealogical record make any specific genealogical claim hazardous.
