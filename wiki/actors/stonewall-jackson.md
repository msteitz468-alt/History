# Stonewall Jackson

Confederate lieutenant general, Robert E. Lee's most trusted corps
commander, renowned for his 1862 Shenandoah Valley Campaign and his flanking
attack at Chancellorsville (May 1863), where he was accidentally shot by his
own troops and died of complications days later.

## Role and Major Decisions

Jackson earned his nickname at First Bull Run (1861), standing firm "like a
stone wall" to rally Confederate lines. His 1862 Valley Campaign, using
rapid marches and interior lines to defeat several larger Union forces in
detail, became a celebrated model of operational maneuver. At
Chancellorsville, his daring flank march around the Union right delivered
one of Lee's most complete tactical victories, but Jackson was mistakenly
wounded by his own pickets while reconnoitering after dark and died on 10
May 1863, eight days later.

## Counterfactual Significance

Jackson's death is one of the most frequently cited counterfactual hinge
points of the war: Lee himself lamented losing "my right arm," and many
historians link Jackson's absence at Gettysburg (fought two months later)
to the Confederacy's failure to execute the kind of aggressive flanking
maneuver that had proven decisive under his command.

## Historiography

Jackson became a central figure of the postwar "Lost Cause" mythology,
which emphasized his piety, tactical genius, and near-mythic status
alongside Lee; more recent scholarship situates his achievements within a
more critical assessment of Confederate operational limitations, without
substantially diminishing the consensus that he was among the war's most
tactically gifted commanders.
