# Jayavarman VII

**Jayavarman VII** (r. c. 1181–c. 1218) was "arguably not only the greatest of all the Khmer kings but
also the greatest personage in Cambodian history" (Coe) — the **Mahayana Buddhist** ruler who rebuilt
the [[khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] after catastrophe and remade **[[angkor|Angkor]]** in his own
image. Documented for the wiki through Michael D. Coe's
[[coe-angkor-khmer-civilization-2018|*Angkor and the Khmer Civilization*]] (2003).

## Reconquest and the Buddhist kingship

A prince who spent years in exile (partly in the Cham capital Vijaya), Jayavarman came to the throne
at about fifty, after the **Cham sack of Angkor in 1177** had plunged the empire into anarchy. In
**1181** he turned the tables, crushing the Chams in land battle and in a great naval engagement on
the Great Lake (an inscription speaks of a "lake of blood"), and was consecrated *chakravartin*.
Deeply influenced by his devout wife **Jayarajadevi** (and later her sister Indradevi), he was a
committed **Mahayana Buddhist** — and as such **dispensed with the Brahmin *purohita* and suspended
the Hindu [[khmer-empire|devaraja]] cult**, at least for his reign, centring royal religion instead on
the compassionate bodhisattva **Avalokiteshvara**.

## The great builder

Jayavarman launched "a building programme... truly unprecedented in its scope." He raised the walled
city of **Angkor Thom** ("Great Angkor"), with its monumental face-gates and, at its centre, the
**Bayon** — its dozens of serene **face-towers** bearing the smiling visage of Avalokiteshvara (widely
thought to be the king's own face), its galleries carved with the Cham war and scenes of everyday
life. He built the monastic "cities" of **Ta Prohm** and Preah Khan, the **Elephant Terrace**, his
**Jayatataka** baray with the island-temple **Neak Pean**, and knit the empire with **roads, bridges,
rest-houses, and 102 hospitals** — a famous concern for "the health and welfare of his subjects." His
portrait statues, depicting him in **meditation**, are among the masterpieces of Khmer art.

## Significance

Jayavarman VII is the apogee of [[khmer-empire|Khmer]] power and monument-building, and the figure who
gave Angkor its most iconic face. His **Mahayana Buddhist** kingship — welfare, public works, and the
bodhisattva-king replacing the Shaiva god-king — marks a distinctive moment in the religious history
of mainland Southeast Asia, even as it strained the empire's resources; after him the realm slowly
contracted and turned to **Theravada Buddhism**.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

**Military recovery and empire maximum.** Son of Dharanindravarman; military commander who had fought in [[champa]]; after the Cham conquest of Angkor he counterattacked with army and waterborne troops **including Cham allies**; major battle on the Great Lake glorified on the Bayon and Banteay Chhmar. Standardized weaponry and introduced **Chinese ballistae**; crowned **1181**. The [[khmer-empire|Khmer Empire]] reached its **maximum size** under him: Champa ruled by Khmer appointees until **1220**; northernmost inscription on the Mekong west bank opposite Vientiane **1186**; Khmer army with Burmese and Siamese troops invaded [[dai-viet|Dai Viet]] **1207**. Projected power west to Muang Sing (Kanchanaburi) and north to [[sukhothai]] (Haripunjaya had earlier defeated a 12th-c. Khmer invasion). Wooden palace of Suryavarman I left a thick ash layer—burnt probably when the Cham conquered Angkor; Jayavarman VII’s new palace is probably illustrated in Bayon bas-reliefs. Reign lasted at least until **1218**; exact death date unknown; Sanskrit inscriptions from the first part of the reign then the epigraphic trail ends. After him, Angkor’s empire began to shrink and **no more major temple complexes** were built (start of the Late Classic in Miksic & Goh’s [[seasian-classic-periodization]]).

**Public works and Angkor Thom.** Reconstructed Angkor Thom as a mandala; appointed a Brahmin *purohita* from Narapatideśa (possibly Myanmar). Founded **102 hospitals** (c. 30 located), assigned **81,640** men and women from **838** villages to supply them; built **121 rest houses** ~15 km apart—still functioning when Zhou Daguan visited **1296**. In **1191** moved to reconstructed Angkor Thom from a temporary palace transformed into **Preah Khan**, dedicated to his father as a bodhisattva. Angkor Thom: wall **3 km** long, **8 m** high on all four sides; moat **100 m** wide; royal palace **15 ha** in the NW corner. **Bayon** at the geometrical center symbolized Mount Meru; plan revised several times; Buddharāja statue once at center; four-faced heads (possibly Lokeśvara—debate continues); almost **1,200 m** of bas-reliefs (nearly twice Angkor Wat). **Ta Prohm** (Rājavihāra) built **1186** with statue of mother Jayarājachūḍāmaṇī as Prajñāpāramitā; **12,000** people designated to support monastery rituals. Banteay Kdei may have been his first monument; 2000 discovery of a pit with **200+** Buddha statues of different styles (some broken before deposition). Preah Khan inscription (late 12th/early 13th context in source discussion): donation of **306,372 clients** and **13,500** villages—scale of unfree labour at peak. Name lists in his reign grew to include **>100,000** people (no longer listed by name); families transferred; unfree status inherited.

**Religion.** Espoused esoteric Buddhist deities known at Angkor since the 10th c.: Prajñāpāramitā, Buddha, Lokeśvara, Hevajra; incorporated mother and father into the pantheon. Only four Indian Brāhmaṇas are mentioned in Cambodian inscriptions spanning Jayavarman II to Jayavarman VII.

**Later legendary contamination.** Image-sending to subordinate Thai provinces is cited as parallel for Kertanagara’s Amoghapāśa donation to [[malayu]] as a claim of suzerainty. One Lao legend variant anachronistically has Jayavarman VII supply Fa Ngum with troops for Lan Xang founding (**12th-c. king / mid-14th-c. event**—extract as chronicle claim only, not historical fact).

*Source: John N. Miksic & Goh Geok Yian, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (Routledge, 2017); [[angkor]]; [[khmer-empire]]; [[champa]]; [[seasian-classic-periodization]].*
