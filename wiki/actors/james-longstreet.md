# James Longstreet

Robert E. Lee's senior corps commander in the Army of Northern Virginia,
nicknamed "Old War Horse," known for his preference for tactical defense and
his controversial role in the second and third days of Gettysburg (1863).

## Role and Major Decisions

Longstreet commanded the decisive counterattack at
[[battle-of-second-bull-run-1862|Second Bull Run]] (1862) that routed Union
forces, and led major commands at Antietam, Fredericksburg, Chickamauga, and
Gettysburg. His disagreement with Lee over tactics at Gettysburg — he
favored a flanking maneuver over the frontal assault that became Pickett's
Charge — became a postwar flashpoint. After the war he alienated much of the
white South by joining the Republican Party and supporting Reconstruction,
which badly damaged his reputation among "Lost Cause" memoirists, who
scapegoated him for the Gettysburg defeat.

## Counterfactual Significance

Modern historians generally regard Longstreet's tactical judgment as
sound and his postwar demonization as disproportionate to his actual
battlefield record, illustrating how postwar political alignment, not
battlefield performance alone, shaped Confederate generals' historical
reputations.

## Historiography

Longstreet's reputation underwent significant post-Reconstruction
revisionism at the hands of Jubal Early and other Lost Cause writers seeking
to protect Lee's infallibility; later twentieth-century scholarship
(notably work reassessing the Lost Cause narrative) has substantially
rehabilitated his standing as one of the war's most capable corps
commanders.
