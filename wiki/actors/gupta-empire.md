# The Gupta Empire

## Overview

The **Gupta Empire** (c. 319–550 CE) founded "the largest empire after the Mauryas" and is
celebrated as a **golden age** of South Asian science, art, and letters. In
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Kaul, Ch 18) the Guptas open the **early medieval period**
(300–900 CE) — an age of brahmanical kingship, temple religion, and (debated) socio-economic
transformation.

## Expansion

Founded by **Chandragupta I** (319/320 CE) in the middle Ganga valley, the empire expanded
"exponentially" under the great general **Samudragupta** (335–375 CE) — whose conquests across
north and central India, and deep into the south (sparing the Deccan and Sri Lankan kings
annexation), are recorded in the ornate Sanskrit panegyric on the **Allahabad pillar**. It
peaked under **Chandragupta II** (375–415 CE), conqueror of western India and patron of the
learned court of "**Vikramaditya**" at Ujjayini. Like the Mauryas, the Guptas ruled through a
**network of paramountcy** over subordinate kings (e.g. the Vakatakas via a married Gupta
princess) rather than uniform direct administration.

## A Golden Age

The Gupta and post-Gupta centuries produced extraordinary achievements:

- **Science and mathematics** (see [[ancient-science-and-technology]]): **Aryabhata** (5th
  c.) explained eclipses, argued the earth's rotation, and calculated the year's length;
  the **decimal place-value system**, an accurate value of **pi**, trigonometric sine tables,
  and algebra developed here. **Varahamihira** gave the earliest datable use of **zero as a
  number**. Translated into Arabic, these spread worldwide as the "Arabic" numerals. The
  rust-resistant **Iron Pillar of Delhi** attests Gupta metallurgy.
- **Religion and art:** great patrons of **[[hinduism|Hinduism]]** (especially Vishnu), the
  Guptas built the earliest surviving **stone/brick temples** (nagara style); **[[buddhism|Buddhism]]**
  flourished at **Nalanda**, the great monastic university drawing scholars from China,
  Korea, and Sri Lanka (the pilgrim **Faxian** visited 405–411 CE).
- **Letters:** the high watermark of **Sanskrit** poetry and drama (**Kalidasa**) and the
  final form of the Puranas and epics; the celebrated **Ajanta** cave paintings.

## Collapse and Aftermath

From the mid-sixth century the empire crumbled under regional satraps and repeated
**[[hunnic-migrations|Huna (Hephthalite)]] invasions**. The fragmented early-medieval
landscape that followed — **Harsha** of Kannauj (606–647 CE), the **Pallavas** and (later)
**Cholas** in the south, and the eighth–ninth-century **tripartite struggle** over Kannauj
among the **Gurjara-Pratiharas, Rashtrakutas, and Palas** — saw power diffuse away from the
Gangetic core into subregional states, until Turkish invasions and the **Delhi Sultanate
(1206)** opened the medieval era.

## Significance

The Gupta age gave the world foundational mathematics and astronomy (the decimal system,
zero), the mature forms of Hindu temple religion and Sanskrit high culture, and Nalanda as a
hub of pan-Asian Buddhist learning — a South Asian "classical" synthesis whose influence
radiated across Eurasia.

## Historiography

The "golden age" label, coined by early historians, is now qualified: CWH IV stresses the
Guptas' **indirect, network-based rule** and situates them within the contested
**[[indian-feudalism-debate|early-medieval transformation]]**. The collection's weakness on
South Asia (per the CLAUDE.md coverage map) makes this chapter a relatively thin single-author
overview rather than a deep reconstruction.
