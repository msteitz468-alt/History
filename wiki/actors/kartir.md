# Kartir (Kerdir)

## Overview

**Kartir** (Kirdir/Kerdir) was the **most powerful Zoroastrian priest of the early
[[sasanian-empire|Sasanian]] empire**, and one of the very few non-royal figures of
ancient Iran to leave his own monumental inscriptions. Across four reigns — those of
[[shapur-i|Shapur I]], Hormizd I, Bahram I, and Bahram II — he rose from a low-ranking
*herbed* to "*mobad* and *dadwar* ('judge') of the whole empire," and Bahram II further
raised him to the rank of *wuzurg* (magnate). His self-testimonials (at Naqsh-i Rajab,
Naqsh-i Rustam, the Ka'ba-i Zardusht, and Sar Mašhad) make him a central witness — in
[[sources/wiesehofer-ancient-persia-2001|Wiesehöfer]]'s reading, **the key figure for the
institutionalization of the Zoroastrian priesthood**, but *not* proof of a standing
Sasanian "state Church."

## Role and Key Events

- **The cursus honorum, in his own words.** His Naqsh-i Rajab inscription names the
  successive titles each king conferred: Shapur I called him "Kirdir, the Mobad and
  Herbed"; Hormizd I, "Kirdir, the Mobad of Ohrmazd"; Bahram II, "Kirdir, whose soul
  Bahram saved, the Mobad of Ohrmazd." This step-by-step ascent — *herbed* → *mobad* →
  *mobad and dadwar* of the empire — is the clearest evidence for the emergence of a
  graded priestly hierarchy.
- **Director of fires.** Bahram II made him "director and authority over the fire of
  Anāhīd–Ardakhshir and Anāhīd the Lady" at Stakhr — the dynasty's own ancestral
  sanctuary.
- **Persecution.** Kartir's inscriptions claim he promoted Zoroastrianism across the
  empire and acted against named minorities — "Jews, Buddhists, Hindus, Nazarenes,
  Christians, Baptists and Manichaeans." He was associated with the imprisonment and
  death of **[[manichaeism|Mani]]** under Bahram I.
- **The journey to the other world.** His inscriptions appear to describe a visionary
  "journey to the other world" — among the earliest such texts in Iranian religious
  literature, though its exact content is debated.

## Counterfactual Significance and Interpretation

Kartir is the linchpin of a major historiographical argument. Older scholarship read him
as the architect of a Zoroastrian "state Church" founded under Ardashir. Wiesehöfer
**rejects** this: clerical power *fluctuated* with political circumstance — strongest under
Bahram II, Hormizd II, and [[shapur-ii|Shapur II]] (reigns marked by military setbacks and
internal crisis), and contained under Ardashir I, Shapur I, Hormizd I, and Narseh.
Kartir's aggressive phase reflects a specific conjuncture (Bahram II's civil war and Roman
reverses forcing reliance on the clergy), not a permanent throne–altar fusion — which
Wiesehöfer treats as a later back-projection (see [[eranshahr]], [[zoroastrianism]]).

## Historiography

Uniquely, Kartir is known primarily from **his own inscriptions** rather than from later,
hostile or royal accounts — making him an unusually direct (if self-serving) source. The
"journey to the other world" passage and the lists of persecuted communities are central
to debates over early Sasanian religious policy.
