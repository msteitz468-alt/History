# Jewry of the Medieval Islamic World

The Jewish communities living under Islamic rule — from Iraq and Iran through the
Levant, Egypt, and North Africa to [[al-andalus|al-Andalus]] — formed, through the
classical centuries (to roughly the thirteenth), the demographic and cultural center of
gravity of world Jewry. They are the Islamic-world half of
[[cohen-under-crescent-cross-1994|Mark Cohen's]] comparison with the
[[ashkenazi-jewry|Jewry of Latin Christendom]], and the community whose daily life is
documented in unmatched detail by the **[[cairo-geniza|Cairo Geniza]]** (the basis of
S. D. Goitein's *A Mediterranean Society*).

This page is the concise, network-oriented summary; the historiographical debate over
how to characterize their experience lives at
[[jews-under-islam-myth-and-countermyth]].

## Legal and Social Position

Jews held the status of [[dhimmi|dhimmīs]] — protected, tax-paying
([[pact-of-umar|jizya]]-paying) subordinates with near-complete internal communal
autonomy under their own courts and law. Cohen stresses that, unlike in Christendom,
Jews were **one of several** tolerated minorities (alongside a numerically larger
Christian population and, in Persia, Zoroastrians): an "essential equality among
inferiors" that diffused anti-minority animus rather than concentrating it. Their status
was governed by the uniform, relatively predictable sharia of the dhimma rather than the
variable, renewal-dependent charters of the Latin West — "marginal within the hierarchy
of Islamic society, he was; excluded, hence expelled, he was not, nor did he become."

## Economic Embeddedness

The community's defining feature, on Cohen's reading, was **occupational
diversification**. Geniza evidence shows Jews in dyeing, metalwork, weaving, glass,
tailoring, tanning, sugar and cheese production, medicine, and long-distance trade
(the Radhanite merchants; the India trade) — the full range of the
surrounding economy, in an Islamic society that *valued* commerce (Muhammad himself a
merchant; the jurists "merchant-scholars"). Jewish and Muslim traders shared instruments
like the *commenda* and the *suftaja* (letter of credit) under a common "law of the
merchants." This is the sharp contrast with Ashkenazi Jewry's forced concentration into
the resented moneylending niche: under Islam, Jewish economic well-being **correlated
with** the general economy rather than inversely with it.

## Cultural and Intellectual Life

Sharing Arabic as the vernacular of high culture from the tenth century, Jews
participated fully in the "unified, secular, Hellenistic-Arab curriculum" of philosophy,
science, and medicine. The Andalusi "Golden Age" of Hebrew poetry and philosophy
(Samuel ibn Naghrela the Nagid; Judah ha-Levi; [[maimonides|Maimonides]]), the geonic
leadership of Iraq (Saadya Gaon, Hay Gaon), and the prominence of Jewish physicians and
courtiers (Ḥasday ibn Shaprut; the Tustari family; Yaʿqub ibn Killis, who converted and
became Fatimid vizier) all belong to this world. Shared popular religion — joint
veneration at the tombs of prophets (Ezekiel, Ezra), the Moroccan Mimuna festival — had
no real Christian-Jewish parallel.

## Persecution and Its Limits

Cohen is explicit that persecution was real, rebutting minimizers: the
**[[granada-massacre-1066|Granada pogrom of 1066]]** (the vizier Joseph ibn Naghrela
assassinated and the Jewish community massacred), [[al-hakim|al-Ḥakim's]] Fatimid
persecution (from 1004), and above all the **[[almohads|Almohad]] forced conversions**
from the 1140s, which shattered Andalusi and Maghribi Jewry and drove Maimonides' family
into exile. But these were **less frequent and less brutal** than in Christendom, and in
nearly every case Jews "were treated as dhimmīs, not as Jews" — the target was the
protected-minority category, not a demonized Jewish essence. There was no equivalent of
the blood libel, the Devil-Jew linkage, or the general expulsions of the Latin West.

A striking corollary Cohen documents is the **near-absence of a Jewish literature of
suffering** under classical Islam — Granada 1066 left "hardly a literary trace" — in
sharp contrast to the rich Ashkenazic "chain of persecution" chronicles and *kiddush
ha-Shem* martyrology. Where Islamic-world Jews did memorialize, the genre was
typically *miraculous deliverance* (the "Egyptian Scroll" of 1012), not martyrdom.

## Transition

The classical equilibrium was already eroding in Maimonides' lifetime (Almohad storm,
Yemen persecution, the pressure of the Crusades on Islamic self-confidence). Cohen bounds
his favorable comparison to the classical period, noting the telling saying that spread
among Almohad refugees in Christian Spain: **"Better under Edom [Christendom] than under
Ishmael [Islam]."**
