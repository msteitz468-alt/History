# Joachim von Ribbentrop

Nazi foreign-policy operator: head of the party's Dienststelle Ribbentrop,
negotiator of the Anglo-German Naval Agreement (1935), Ambassador to London
(1936–38), and Reich Foreign Minister from February 1938. A former champagne
salesman with purchased aristocratic credentials, he fed Hitler's conviction
after 1937 that Britain would not fight, and gave his name to the
[[molotov-ribbentrop-pact-1939|Nazi–Soviet Pact]]. Hanged at Nuremberg, 1946.

## Evans (2005)

A latecomer (joined the far right only in 1932 via the Herrenclub; "von" by
adoption — "von Ribbensnob" to London society), he ran a 150-strong personal
foreign-policy office outside the Foreign Ministry, negotiated the
Anglo-German Naval Agreement (18 June 1935 — Germany to 35% of British
tonnage, a triumph that broke the Stresa Front) and the Anti-Comintern Pact
(1936) behind the Foreign Office's back. His London embassy (1936–38) failed
publicly ("von Brickendrop"; the Nazi salute to George VI), and his counsel to
Hitler was contradictory — Britain would not fight, yet war with Britain was
ultimately inevitable — advice Evans calls bad but consequential. Foreign
Minister from February 1938 in the [[blomberg-fritsch-affair-1938|reshuffle]];
excluded from the Munich brokering (Goebbels: "he has a blind hatred of
England"); pushed hardest for the Soviet option and signed the
[[molotov-ribbentrop-pact-1939|Pact]] in Moscow in the early hours of
24 August 1939, then staged the midnight rejection scene with Henderson on
29 August as war came.

## Historiography

Michael Bloch (1992) and Weitz (1992) are the main biographies; universally
portrayed as vain and incompetent, though his influence on Hitler's
Britain-assessment in 1938–39 was real and consequential.

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Shirer corroborates Evans's low estimate of Ribbentrop's judgment via Göring's own postwar assessment: "When I criticized Ribbentrop's qualifications to handle British problems, the Fuehrer pointed out to me that Ribbentrop knew 'Lord So and So'... To which I replied: 'Yes, but the difficulty is that they know Ribbentrop.'" Ribbentrop signed the Anti-Comintern Pact with Japan on 25 November 1936 — Shirer, present as a correspondent, notes it was publicly framed as "defend[ing] Western civilization" but contained a secret protocol directed against Russia, with mutual consultation if attacked and a bar on political treaties with the USSR without consent (Shirer 1960).

Baron von Weizsäcker's secret memorandum of 10 November 1937 (head of the Foreign Office's Political Department, working under Ribbentrop's authority) is quoted: "From England we want colonies and freedom of action in the East... The British need for tranquillity is great. It would be profitable to find out what England would be willing to pay for such tranquillity" — early documentary evidence of the appeasement calculus later exploited at Munich (Shirer 1960).

At Salzburg on 11 August 1939, Ciano asked Ribbentrop point-blank what Germany wanted regarding Poland: "Well, Ribbentrop, what do you want? The Corridor or Danzig?" Ribbentrop replied: "Not that any more. We want war!" — one of the single most quotable, load-bearing lines in Shirer's account of the pact negotiations, confirming premeditation independent of any Danzig settlement. Ciano recorded a private wager at the same meeting — a piece of old German armor against an Italian painting — that France and Britain would remain neutral if Poland were attacked; his diary's final entry (23 December 1943, written in Verona jail awaiting execution) recalled the bet "was never paid" (Shirer 1960).

On the Nazi-Soviet Pact itself, Shirer records Ribbentrop's role dictating the fabricated "request for aid" telegram during the Anschluss crisis (see [[anschluss-1938]]) and his November 1940 Berlin talks with Molotov proposing to divide the world into four spheres of interest, suggesting a Soviet outlet toward "the Persian Gulf and the Arabian Sea" — Molotov's icy "Which sea?" marked the effective collapse of Nazi-Soviet cooperation (see [[molotov-ribbentrop-pact-1939]] for the full exchange) (Shirer 1960).

On the pretext for Barbarossa: Ribbentrop's "Very Urgent, State Secret" telegram to Ambassador Schulenburg, dated 21 June 1941, instructed destruction of all cipher material and delivery of a memorandum alleging Soviet "sabotage, terrorism and espionage" and troop concentration threatening Germany "from the rear." Per Dr. Schmidt's eyewitness account, Ribbentrop "walked up and down his room like a caged animal" before handing the declaration to Soviet Ambassador Dekanozov at 4 a.m. on 22 June, roughly thirty minutes after the invasion had already begun (Shirer 1960).

Personal touches: Shirer records that at the Pact of Steel signing (22 May 1939) Ribbentrop received the Collar of the Annunziata (denied to Göring, who "made quite a scene"); and that Ribbentrop's Nuremberg trial account of the decisive Pearl Harbor conversation with Hitler has Hitler saying "If we don't stand on the side of Japan, the Pact is politically dead. But that is not the main reason. The chief reason is that the United States already is shooting against our ships" — Shirer treats Ribbentrop's account here as probably truthful for once, since Ribbentrop otherwise claimed Pearl Harbor "came as a complete surprise to us." [EYEWITNESS] At Nuremberg, Shirer observed Ribbentrop in the dock "shorn of his arrogance," "pale, bent and beaten." He was hanged 16 October 1946, first among the ten executed that morning (Shirer 1960).
