# Celestine V (Peter Murrone)

## Overview

**Celestine V** (Peter Murrone / Pietro del Morrone; r. July–December 1294) was the hermit-monk elected *papa angelicus* (“the angel pope”) after more than two years of cardinal deadlock (1292–94). His brief pontificate, captivity after resignation, and death under [[boniface-viii|Boniface VIII]] became a touchstone of late-medieval debate over whether a pope could resign and whether Boniface’s election was valid.

## Role and Key Developments

After Clement IV’s death and a series of short pontificates and vacancies, the College of Cardinals was deadlocked over Angevin Sicilian politics. In 1294 they elected the aged hermit **Peter Murrone**—about eighty-five, not a curial insider. On **29 August 1294** he rode to consecration on a donkey. [[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] stresses that he became “the unknowing puppet” of **Charles II of Naples**, named twelve cardinals proposed by Charles, never went to Rome, and allowed church administration to fall into disarray.

Planning an Advent retreat and proposing to leave papal powers with three cardinals, Celestine was advised that this was improper. On **13 December 1294** he appeared before the cardinals, removed mitre and ring, and **resigned** the papacy—the first clearly voluntary papal resignation in centuries and a precedent that would later be invoked (and contested) in the crisis of his successor.

## Relation to Boniface VIII

On Christmas Eve 1294 the cardinals elected **Benedict Gaetani** as **[[boniface-viii|Boniface VIII]]**. Boniface had advised that resignation was possible and drafted the resignation document; he then ordered the aged hermit arrested and kept under house arrest at **Castel Fumone** near Ferentino, where Celestine died in **1296**. Colonna enemies of Boniface later claimed Celestine could not resign (so Boniface’s election was invalid) and that Boniface had murdered him—charges that fed Philip IV’s later campaign against Boniface.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Celestine’s resignation, the path to Boniface’s contested pontificate—and thus the confrontation with [[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV]] and the [[outrage-of-anagni-1303|Outrage of Anagni]]—would have been different. The resignation itself proved that papal office could be laid down, a legal fact with long afterlife.

## Historiography

Logan’s narrative profile (ch. 13) presents Celestine as a spiritual ideal miscast as monarch: *papa angelicus* imagery versus administrative collapse and Angevin capture. Debate continues over how far Boniface coerced the resignation versus merely regularizing a hermit’s incapacity, and over the justice of the Fumone detention.
