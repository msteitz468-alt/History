# Pagan (Bagan)

**Pagan** (Bagan) was the first polity to unite the **Irrawaddy basin** of **Burma** and the cradle of
Burmese Buddhist civilization — its plain of some two thousand surviving temples is one of the great
monumental landscapes of Asia. Documented for the wiki through the
[[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v1-1992|Cambridge History of Southeast Asia]] (Keith Taylor,
Ch. 3). Taylor pairs it with [[khmer-empire|Angkor]] as a sister case — both **inland agrarian polities**
that dominated coasts, both raised monumental architecture, and both fell as the **Tai peoples** entered
the lowlands.

## An agrarian power on the dry zone

Pagan sits in the arid northern dry zone, but at the point on the Irrawaddy **equidistant between the
irrigated ricelands of Minbu and Kyaukse** — the ideal site to concentrate their surplus. Its success
shows that, in early Burma, **an inland agrarian polity could mobilize people and wealth better than the
coastal entrepôts** (Pegu, Arakan, Tenasserim). It appeared as a walled city c. 849, founded by
**Burmans** — recent arrivals who entered the basin (long inhabited by **Pyus** and **Mons**) in the wake
of the Yunnan kingdom of **Nanchao** breaking Pyu power.

## Anawrahta, the Mon synthesis, and Theravada

Pagan became a major power under **Anawrahta** (r. 1044–77), whose conquest of the Mon kingdom of
**Thaton in 1057** poured **Mon culture** into Pagan and tied it into the Bay-of-Bengal maritime network
and to **Sri Lanka** (exchanges of monks and Pali texts). His successor **Kyanzittha** (r. 1084–c. 1112)
presided over the great cultural **synthesis** — Mon, Pyu, Burman, and Pali fused (symbolised by the
four-language **Myazedi inscription**) and **Theravada Buddhism** implanted at the centre of Burmese life
through "architectural pedagogy" (the Shwezigon stupa, the Ananda temple). Where Angkor's
[[devaraja|god-king]] measured legitimacy by the **amoral power of the god**, Pagan's kings claimed
**superior merit** judged by the moral standard of the Pali canon — a more ethical, competitive religious
politics.

## The land-to-the-sangha problem and collapse

Pagan's legitimacy required kings to demonstrate merit by **endowing monasteries with tax-exempt land**.
By the late twelfth century land for this was running out and the **monastic estates had eroded the
royal revenue base** — so **Narapatisithu** (r. c. 1173–1211) carried out a **monastic "purification"**
(declaring the sangha corrupt, confiscating its lands, re-ordaining monks from Sri Lanka) that
temporarily restored the throne's wealth and brought Pagan to its peak. The problem **returned** after
him; as royal authority shrank, outlying regions broke away. The **Mongol** expeditions of 1283–1301
(following their conquest of Yunnan) and the influx of **Tai/Shan** peoples finished the polity: the
kings of Pagan were reduced to ceremonial figures, and the basin fragmented into **Ava** (Burmanized
Shans), **Toungoo** (Burmans), Mon **Pegu**, and **Arakan**.

## Significance

Pagan endures as "the definitive statement of what it means to be Burmese" — though Taylor notes the
irony: its greatest moments came from a **syncretic openness to non-Burman (Mon, Pyu) peoples**, and the
later Burman attempt to **appropriate** Pagan helped alienate the Mons and lose lower Burma. Its twin
problems — **land-control** and **ethnic conflict** — frame the analysis of its fall, a counterpoint to
the [[devaraja|devaraja]]-centred collapse debate at Angkor.
