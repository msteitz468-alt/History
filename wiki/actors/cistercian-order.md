# The Cistercian Order (Cîteaux)

The **Cistercians** (the "White Monks") were the most successful and characteristic
of the new religious orders of the twelfth century, the spearhead of the
[[medieval-monastic-revival|"new monasticism"]] that reacted against the wealth and
elaborate liturgy of [[cluny|Cluny]] in favour of austerity, manual labour, and a
return to the literal observance of the Rule of St Benedict.

## Founding and Structure

The order grew from the reformed abbey of **Cîteaux** (Cistercium, Burgundy), founded
in 1098 by Robert of Molesme and consolidated by the Englishman Stephen Harding, whose
*Carta Caritatis* ("Charter of Charity") gave the order its distinctive constitution:
a federation of autonomous-but-visited abbeys bound by an annual **General Chapter**
and a system of mother-houses inspecting their daughters — an organisational model far
more centralised and uniform than the old Benedictine congregations. Cistercian houses
sought remote sites, renounced the seigneurial revenues, tithes, and serf labour that
sustained traditional monasteries, and worked their estates directly through outlying
**granges** staffed by lay brothers (*conversi*) — a regime that, paradoxically, made
the order a pioneer of large-scale, market-oriented agriculture (wool, especially in
England and Wales) within the [[medieval-agricultural-revolution|agrarian expansion]].

Their architecture matched their ideology: the "stark unadorned simplicity of early
Cistercian monastic buildings was in keeping with the reforming strictness of the new
movement" (NCMH IV, editors' Introduction) — plain, unpainted, harmonious churches
that rank among the masterpieces of the [[romanesque|Romanesque]] and early
[[gothic-architecture|Gothic]].

## Bernard of Clairvaux

The order's explosive growth was bound up with **Bernard of Clairvaux** (1090–1153),
who entered Cîteaux in 1113 and founded **Clairvaux** in 1115. The most influential
churchman of his age, Bernard preached the **Second Crusade** (1146), shaped papal
elections, opposed Abelard, and (NCMH IV, Robinson, Ch. 11) was the most important
twelfth-century writer on **papal authority** — expressing the supremacy of the Roman
Church not in the monarchical language he distrusted but through the images (mother of
churches, steersman of the ship, the pope with "no equal on earth") that would dominate
later medieval ecclesiology. Under his impulse the Cistercians spread across Latin
Europe, numbering some 350 houses by his death and over 500 by 1200.

## Significance

The Cistercians embodied the twelfth century's drive for a stricter, more authentic
religious life; pioneered a centralised, constitutional model of religious government
that influenced later orders and even secular corporations; were major economic
innovators; and, through Bernard, exerted decisive influence on the papacy, the
crusade, and the intellectual controversies of the age.

## From Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

[[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]]’s growth numbers: ~20 discontented Burgundian monks founded Cîteaux **1098**; **[[bernard-of-clairvaux|Bernard]]** arrived **1112** (~age 22) with relatives/friends; founded Clairvaux **1115**, abbot 38 years, **68 daughter houses**. Growth: **333 abbeys by 1152** (temporary halt), **525 by end of century** (Rievaulx, Fountains, Melrose, Alcobaça, etc.); at Ailred’s death (1167) Rievaulx had **140 choir monks and 500 lay brothers**.

**Lay brothers (*conversi*):** unpaid, illiterate by statute, worked fields, prayed memorized texts in the nave — thousands from the peasantry until decline after the late thirteenth century and near-vanishing after the Black Death. White monks grew rich (Yorkshire sheep; Flemish Dunes farmland/shipping). Darker side: forced clearances — Fountains possibly **23** settlements depopulated; other Yorkshire houses ~**46**; Lorraine villages destroyed. Critics: “They raze villages, destroy churches, evict parishioners… make a solitude so that they may be solitaries.”

**Cluniac–Cistercian controversy:** Bernard’s angry letter after a cousin left Clairvaux for Cluny; Peter the Venerable answered, calling Cistercian critics “a new race of pharisees” neglecting Benedictine humility. By century’s end, Logan judges, spiritual pride undercut the *Carta caritatis* ideal — the order “little different from the others in evident signs of holiness.” Nearly all new orders diverted from early intentions — “**saving always the [[carthusian-order|Carthusians]]**.” English Cistercian lands were later threatened by Henry II when Becket sheltered at **Pontigny** (see [[constitutions-of-clarendon-1164]]).
