# Ashurbanipal

**Ashurbanipal** (reigned 668–c. 631 BCE), son of [[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]], was
the **last great king of the [[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]]** —
ruler at its widest extent, victor over Egypt, Elam, and his own rebel brother,
and the **literate king** who assembled the great **Library of [[nineveh|Nineveh]]**.
Within a generation of his death the empire fell. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH
Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 24, A. K. Grayson) is the authority.

## Egypt and the sack of Thebes

Esarhaddon's death (669) left Egypt to be reconquered. Ashurbanipal's army
**recaptured Memphis (667)**, driving Taharqa back to Thebes; when Taharqa's
nephew **Tantamani** renewed the [[kush-meroe|Kushite]] challenge, the Assyrians
returned and **sacked Thebes (No-Amon) c. 663** — "the pinnacle of Assyrian
achievement in Egypt." It ended Kushite interference in the Levant; Egypt was left
to the Assyrian client **[[saite-egypt|Necho I and his son Psammetichus I]]** of
Sais, who would found the independent Saite dynasty. See
**[[sack-of-thebes-663bce]]**.

## Anatolia and Gyges of Lydia

With the **[[cimmerians|Cimmerians]]** overrunning Anatolia, hard-pressed kings
sought Assyrian friendship: Tabal, Khilakku, and — most famously —
**[[lydia|Gyges (Gugu) of Lydia]]**, who (prompted, says Ashurbanipal, by a dream)
sent an embassy to Nineveh and thereby "began to succeed" against the Cimmerians.
The alliance lapsed; **Lydia was overrun and Gyges killed (c. 645)** by the
Cimmerians under **Dugdamme (Lygdamis)**. The episode is the **first contact
between Assyria and a kingdom of the Aegean world**.

## Elam and Babylonia: the great civil war

The dual monarchy his grandfather had arranged broke down. His brother
**Shamash-shuma-ukin, king of Babylon**, built an anti-Assyrian coalition (Elam,
the Arabs, the Sealand) and rose in revolt. The **civil war of 652–648** raged
across Babylonia until Ashurbanipal **besieged and starved Babylon into surrender
(648)**; Shamash-shuma-ukin died in the flames of his palace. Ashurbanipal then
turned on **Elam**, repeatedly defeating and finally, in **647–646, sacking Susa**
— destroying its temples, ziggurat, and royal tombs, sowing the fields with salt,
and carrying off the goddess Nanaya's statue to Uruk after 1,600 years. Grayson:
the war, "although it was a victory, in the long run turned out to be a pyrrhic
victory" — and the destruction of Susa removed the buffer that had shielded
Assyria from the rising **Medes and Persians** (two Iranian kings, "one of them
none other than **Cyrus I**," sent tribute after the Elamite victory).

## The Library

Atypically among Assyrian kings, **Ashurbanipal could read** (most of his
ancestors were illiterate). He sponsored the systematic acquisition of tablets for
the **libraries at Nineveh** — the great collection of literary, religious,
omen, lexical, and scholarly texts (including the *Epic of Gilgamesh* and the
*Enūma Eliš*) recovered by 19th-century excavators, the single richest source for
Mesopotamian civilization. Grayson treats it in the civilization chapter (Ch. 26);
see [[assyrian-civilization]].

## The royal hunt and divination

His **North Palace** reliefs are dominated by the **royal lion hunt** — the
animals carved with a famous life-like naturalism — a ritual assertion of the
king's protective power. Like Esarhaddon he was "almost fanatically" devoted to
**divination**: omen reports, oracle inquiries, ecstatics of Ishtar of Arbela, and
prophetic dreams (the dream of Gyges) shaped state decisions.

## Significance

- `produced:` the sack of [[sack-of-thebes-663bce|Thebes]] and the end of Kushite
  power in Egypt; the rise of the [[saite-egypt|Saite dynasty]] as an Assyrian
  client.
- `produced:` the destruction of Elam — which `enabled:` the unobstructed rise of
  the **Medes and Persians** and so `contributed_to:` Assyria's own fall.
- `produced:` the **Library of Nineveh**, the foundation of modern Assyriology.
- His reign's end (the empire "never recovered from the Shamash-shuma-ukin
  rebellion") is the prelude to the [[fall-of-nineveh-612bce|fall of Assyria]] —
  treated in Cycle 2.

## Historiography

Paradoxically the **best-documented reign** (annals in many editions, masses of
everyday documents, the reliefs, the library itself) yet with the **most uncertain
internal chronology** — there is no Eponym Chronicle after 649, the Babylonian
Chronicles stop at 667, and the annal "campaigns" are numbered out of sequence and
differ between editions. Even the **length of his reign** (to 631 or to 627) is
disputed, a problem that bedevils the chronology of the empire's last years.
