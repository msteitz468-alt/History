# Yaghi Siyan (Governor of Antioch)

Seljuq Turkish governor (amir) of Antioch, appointed by Sultan Malik Shah in 1086–7 as part of the post-Philaretus settlement in northern Syria. He held the city during the nine-month siege by the First Crusade (October 1097 – June 1098).

## Role in the Siege of Antioch

Yaghi Siyan governed a major fortified city on the strategic route from Anatolia into Syria and the Levant. His position was complicated by the broader Seljuq succession crisis after Malik Shah's death (1092) and the rivalry between Ridwan of Aleppo and Duqaq of Damascus. He initially sought to hold and wait, relying on the city's immense walls and the expectation that a relief army would arrive. The crusader blockade was porous (the circuit too long to seal completely, especially the mountain approaches), allowing some messengers and limited supply to reach the city.

Yaghi Siyan was killed during the crusader capture of the city by treachery on the night of 2/3 June 1098. The fall reversed the strategic situation: the besiegers became the besieged inside the walls when Kerbogha of Mosul's relief army arrived shortly afterward.

## Cobb: patronage network, defence, death, and memory

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|*The Race for Paradise*]] places Yaghi-Siyan firmly inside
[[seljuk-empire|Saljuq]] Syrian appanage politics. Though a former mamluk of **Malikshah**, he
became a favorite of **Tutush** — his daughter married Tutush’s son **Ridwan of Aleppo** —
and survived the amnesty of 1095 when others were executed or imprisoned after Tutush’s fall.
**Karbuqa** of Mosul (Barkiyaruq loyalist once imprisoned by Tutush) became his neighbor and
enemy; the Frankish invasion “threw these two mutually spiteful men together with disastrous
consequences.”

**Call for aid and siege conduct.** Summer 1097, after the central-Syrian coalition dispersed
at Shayzar, Yaghi-Siyan returned to Antioch and sent one son to **Duqaq** at Damascus, another
to Karbuqa/Mosul and the eastern Turcomans, and messengers to “all the amirs of the Muslims,”
raising a large army that included jihad volunteers. Arrival of the Franks immediately inspired
scholarly debate in Damascus on whether jihad duty was local only or also for neighbors — the
latter view won. Franks planned a long siege (foraging lands, supply lines) and secured
neighboring castles partly, one chronicler claimed, due to “Yaghi-Siyan’s ugly conduct and
oppressive grip over his lands”; an English fleet took St. Simeon port and Latakia. Inside the
city he expelled able-bodied Christian men for security while vowing to protect their families;
Christian Antiochenes endured the siege encamped with the Franks.

**Death.** When alarm from the Mt. Silpius/citadel direction led him to believe the citadel
itself lost, he panicked and fled south (not toward the Muslim army north of Aleppo); men
deserted; he died of wounds or thirst; an Armenian peasant brought his head to the Franks.
Cobb: loss of Antioch was the invasion’s “first and most stinging shock,” revealing ephemeral
loyalties among Saljuq military elites; blame split between Yaghi-Siyan’s statesmanship and
Karbuqa’s contempt; the fragile unity of his initial jihad call collapsed after battle.

**Epilogue memory.** Cobb’s epilogue “Buried Horsemen” preserves a circulating tale (told by
Hamdan ibn ʿAbd al-Rahim al-Atharibi a generation or so later): workmen for Yaghi-Siyan found
brass figurines of chain-mailed horsemen during wall repairs after an earthquake; elders
recalled earlier “Turkish” figurines before the Saljuq capture of Antioch; Yaghi-Siyan scoffed
— shortly thereafter Franks encamped before Constantinople en route to Antioch. The tale
explains the city’s loss as ineluctable fate written into its fabric **and** pins blame on
Yaghi-Siyan’s hubris in ignoring omens — a genre Cobb links to broader First Crusade blame
narratives.

## Historiography / Sources

Portrayed in Latin chronicles (Gesta Francorum, Raymond of Aguilers, Albert of Aachen) primarily as the defender whose city fell to a combination of starvation pressure on the besiegers and internal betrayal. Islamic sources (e.g., Ibn al-Athir) place him in the context of the fragmented post-Malik Shah politics of Syria. France (*Victory in the East*) stresses that despite political disunity, the defence of Antioch was serious and prolonged (nine months, multiple relief attempts). Cobb adds the Tutush–Ridwan patronage web, the acid chronicler judgment on his “oppressive grip,” the ignominious flight narrative, and the buried-horsemen moral tale as Islamic memory culture rather than operational report.
