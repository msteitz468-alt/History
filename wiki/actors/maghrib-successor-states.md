# The Maghrib Successor States (Hafsids, Ziyanids, Marinids)

The collapse of the **[[almohads|Almohad]]** empire in the 1220s–1230s produced the **tripartite
division of North Africa** that would endure for centuries: the **Hafsids** of Tunis, the
**ʿAbd al-Wadids (Ziyanids)** of Tlemcen, and the **Marinids** of Morocco.
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Michael Brett, Ch. 17c) sets this against the longue durée of
the **Islamisation of North Africa** by successive tribal-religious revolutions (Fatimid,
Almoravid, Almohad).

## The Almohad Achievement and Collapse

The Almohads — drawing on the **Masmuda** Berbers of the High Atlas and the Unitarian doctrine
of **Ibn Tumart** — had, Brett argues, "completed the unification of North Africa by Islam,"
joining the lands within and beyond the old Roman pale from a base at **Marrakesh**. But the
empire had "two feet of clay": its subjects were divided by religion, region, tribe, and the
townsman/nomad split, united only by allegiance to the caliph. Defeat by Christian Spain at
**Las Navas de Tolosa (1212)** broke the military machine; succession disputes, Arab tribal
insubordination, and the repudiation of Ibn Tumart's doctrine (under al-Maʾmun, d. 1232)
shattered the regime. **Al-Andalus** fell away, **Córdoba** fell to Castile (1236), and the
caliphate fragmented.

## The Three States

- **Hafsids (Tunis / Ifriqiya).** **Abu Zakariyaʾ Yahya**, son of the Almohad viceroy of
  Ifriqiya, declared independence (c. 1229) and claimed the leadership of the Almohads for his
  dynasty, inviting recognition across the Muslim west as "the new defender of the faith." Tunis
  became the richest of the successor states, drawing the West African gold and Mediterranean
  trade of the **[[republic-of-genoa|Italian]]** and Catalan merchants.
- **ʿAbd al-Wadids / Ziyanids (Tlemcen).** Under the Berber chieftain **Yaghmurasan Ibn Ziyan**
  (from 1236), the central Maghrib around Tlemcen became a separate kingdom, caught between its
  more powerful neighbours.
- **Marinids (Morocco).** Berber **Marinid** pastoralists supplanted the Almohads in Morocco,
  taking Marrakesh by the 1260s — the heirs to the Almohad-Almoravid centre.

## Significance

The thirteenth century saw North Africa pass from **Almohad unity** to enduring **tripartite
fragmentation** — a pattern Brett reads as a new phase in the longue durée of Maghribi history,
in which "stateless" Berber tribalism, periodically mobilised by a prophet, repeatedly built and
dissolved empires. For the Mediterranean world the consequences were large: the collapse opened
**al-Andalus** to the [[reconquista|Reconquista]] (leaving only [[nasrid-granada|Nasrid Granada]]),
and the weaker successor states gave European merchants "greater access to the trade of the
Maghrib and the Sahara" — the West African gold that financed the new gold coinages of
[[republic-of-genoa|Genoa]], Florence and the Crown of Aragon.
