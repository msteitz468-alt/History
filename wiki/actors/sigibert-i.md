# Sigibert I

**Sigibert I** (c. 535–575) was a son of **[[chlothar-i|Chlothar I]]** who, in the
partition of 561, took the eastern kingdom of **[[austrasia|Austrasia]]** with his capital
at **Reims/Metz**. His marriage to the Visigothic princess **[[brunhild|Brunhild]]** and
his assassination at the instigation of his brother [[chilperic-i|Chilperic I]]'s consort
**[[fredegund|Fredegund]]** opened the long sixth-century civil wars that
**[[gregory-of-tours-history-of-the-franks-thorpe-1974|Gregory of Tours]]** chronicles.

## Career

Sigibert defended the eastern frontier against the **Avars** ("the Huns" in Gregory),
beating them after Chlothar's death and, in a later campaign, escaping capture "by bribery
and treaty." Scorning his brothers' low-born marriages — "his brothers were taking wives
who were completely unworthy of them" — he sought and married **Brunhild**, daughter of
King Athanagild of Spain, who converted from Arianism to Catholicism. The murder of
Brunhild's sister Galswintha by Chilperic poisoned relations between the brothers.

In the resulting **civil war** Sigibert beat Chilperic, captured Chilperic's son
Theudebert, and — Gregory stresses, for Sigibert is one of his more sympathetic kings —
"was a clement man" who returned the captive. As his forces besieged Chilperic in
**Tournai**, Bishop Germanus warned him with the proverb "Whoso diggeth a pit shall fall
therein." Sigibert was raised on a shield and acclaimed king at **Vitry**, then immediately
assassinated by "two young men who had been suborned by Queen Fredegund" with poisoned
*scramasaxes*. He died "in the fourteenth year of his reign, when he was forty years old,"
and was buried at Saint-Médard, Soissons; his five-year-old son **[[childebert-ii|Childebert II]]** succeeded.

## Counterfactual Significance

Sigibert's assassination at the moment of victory — he was about to depose Chilperic — is
the great contingency of the period: had he lived, Austrasia might have absorbed Neustria a
generation before the [[gundovald-affair-585|wars of the 580s]]. Instead his murder left a
boy-king under Brunhild's regency and made the Brunhild–Fredegund feud the engine of two
decades of Frankish civil war. See [[merovingian-franks]].
