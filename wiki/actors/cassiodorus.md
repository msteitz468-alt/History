# Cassiodorus

**Flavius Magnus Aurelius Cassiodorus Senator** (c.490–c.583) composed Ostrogothic king Theodoric’s *Variae*, founded the monastery of **Vivarium** in Calabria, and wrote the *Institutiones* (c.562) dividing biblical study from the **seven liberal arts**—a structure Logan credits with shaping medieval schools and, ultimately, modern universities.

## Career and works

Under Arian Theodoric, Cassiodorus served as a leading Roman administrator and letter-writer (*Variae*). After the Gothic wars and political collapse, he turned to monastic learning at Vivarium. The *Institutiones* exist in manuscripts that often preserve only one of the two parts.

> "By dividing his treatise into two parts, the first concerning biblical study and the second concerning secular study of the seven liberal arts, Cassiodorus allowed, probably not by design, the separation of religious studies from secular studies."

## Role

Logan presents the biblical/secular bifurcation as (probably unintended) enabling a durable place for **secular learning** alongside religious knowledge—foundational for medieval school culture. Together with [[boethius|Boethius]], Cassiodorus shows that Roman cultural production continued under Germanic Arian rule.

## Historiography

Whether Cassiodorus “designed” the later university arts curriculum is teleological; Logan’s claim is structural influence, not institutional continuity from Vivarium to Paris. Most specialists treat him as a crucial late-antique transmitter rather than a medieval “founder.”
