# John Aubrey

English antiquary, natural philosopher, and biographer (1626–1697), fellow of the Royal Society, best known for his manuscript collections *Monumenta Britannica* and *Brief Lives*. Aubrey was the first to argue systematically that [[places/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] and [[places/avebury|Avebury]] and similar stone circles were native British monuments built by the **Druids**, rather than works of the Romans, Danes, or Saxons as earlier antiquaries supposed. He discovered the ring of pits at Stonehenge later named the "Aubrey Holes" in his honour.

Though his *Monumenta Britannica* remained unpublished in his lifetime, Aubrey's Druid-megalith hypothesis was transmitted to and amplified by [[actors/stukeley-william|William Stukeley]], making him a foundational figure in the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]] and in British field [[concepts/antiquarianism|antiquarianism]].

## Significance

Aubrey's role in the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]] was foundational, but his path to the Druid–megalith theory was neither straightforward nor entirely original. The chain began on 7 January 1649, when Aubrey joined a royalist hunting party on the Marlborough Downs and followed the chase into the village of Avebury. He later described how he "left the hunting party to explore" the earthwork enclosing part of the village and realized he was examining "an impressive ancient monument that nobody seemed to have noticed before" — a claim overstated in retrospect, but capturing his sense of discovery ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

His serious fieldwork at [[places/avebury|Avebury]] began in the late 1650s, when his friend James Long used the village as a base for autumn hawking holidays. After the Restoration (1660) and the foundation of the Royal Society, Aubrey and Long were both made early members; the Society formally directed them in July 1663 to consider excavations at Avebury. Within weeks, Aubrey was presented to King Charles II, who visited the site on progress in September 1663 and commanded Aubrey to produce a written account. The result was a manuscript Aubrey provisionally entitled **Templa Druidum** — the title itself embodying his central argument. That argument was simple and powerful: stone circles were so widely distributed across Britain and Ireland that they could only be credited to the pre-Roman native inhabitants; as ceremonial structures, they were most plausibly associated with the Druids, the religious experts of the ancient British. Aubrey's own conclusion drawn from this was nonetheless tentative — he acknowledged in a private note "that he could be more certain of ascribing most of the monuments to the (ancient) British than to the Druids" ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

The crucial impetus for linking the circles specifically to Druids came from Scotland. Fellow Royal Society member Sir Robert Moray informed Aubrey as early as 1663 that similar stone circles existed in parts of Scotland the Romans had never reached. This fact punctured Inigo Jones's argument against Druid authorship (that nothing like Stonehenge appeared at Anglesey, the Druid stronghold). Reading Hector Boece and Archbishop Spottiswoode thereafter introduced Aubrey to the Scottish tradition — already well-developed — of associating megaliths with Druids. A correspondence with James Garden, Professor of Theology at Aberdeen (1692–3), reinforced his thinking at a critical moment: Garden dealt with Aubrey's three remaining doubts (that ancient sources did not represent Druids as worshipping in stone; that megaliths had been identified as tombs rather than temples; that Druids had used groves, not stone circles) by pointing out that stones served throughout the ancient world as altars for priests, that human remains in stone circles were probably like graves in a church, and that the groves' trees could simply have vanished.

These letters were incorporated into the expanded work, eventually titled **Monumenta Britannica**, which Aubrey struggled to complete from the early 1660s until his death in 1697. His difficulty was constitutional: "John Aubrey simply couldn't write a book; he was, rather, a natural jotter-down of notes" ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]). Edmund Gibson, editing the revised Camden's *Britannia* (1695), was "aghast and embarrassed" to find the manuscript "such a rhapsody." Aubrey delivered it to publisher Awnsham Churchill in 1695 but died in 1697 while still negotiating terms.

Despite never publishing, Aubrey disseminated his ideas through extensive conversation and correspondence. Among those he influenced directly was the young [[actors/toland-john|John Toland]], who came to Oxford in 1693 and, by Aubrey's own design, was shown the letters from Garden. Toland later acknowledged Aubrey as "the first person whom he had encountered to have the idea that prehistoric stone circles were Druidic temples." Aubrey also gave Toland access to the manuscript, though Toland judged that "neither the facts he knew, nor the reflections he made, were what I wanted" — a measure both of Toland's ambition and of the limits of Aubrey's systematisation. The argument was ultimately amplified and popularized by [[actors/stukeley-william|William Stukeley]], whose published works gave Aubrey's hypothesis its lasting cultural force.

## Historiography

Aubrey's reputation rests more on a single insight — the attribution of megaliths to a native pre-Roman culture — than on any sustained scholarly achievement. Hutton emphasises that the Druid connection in Aubrey's work remained explicitly provisional and that his evidence base depended heavily on what Scottish scholars had already developed: without Boece and Spottiswoode, the link between Druids and stone circles might not have been made at all, and without Moray's fieldwork information it would have lacked geographic force. The story of how Aubrey "discovered" Avebury in 1649 is part of the foundation epic of British archaeology, but Hutton gently qualifies it by noting that others had already noticed the site. Aubrey's tendency to "wrest almost everything to the religion and worship of the Druids" was recalled by his acquaintance John Batteley (who described him applying the label to a Roman strigil), though Hutton suggests Batteley may have exaggerated. The manuscript of *Monumenta Britannica* survives in a version dating from 1694–5, embedded in the enlarged work; the title *Templa Druidum* is securely attested by March 1673, but the precise moment when Aubrey formulated his Druid hypothesis cannot be fixed with certainty.
