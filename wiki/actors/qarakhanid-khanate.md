## Overview

The Qarakhanid Khanate (992–1212) was a Turkic dynasty of Central Asia and the first
steppe polity to convert to Islam en masse, following the conversion of its founding
khan, Satuq Bughra Khan (d. 955). The Qarakhanids' shrine to Satuq Bughra Khan still
stands at Atush, in the Tarim Basin (modern Xinjiang), a testament to his ongoing
veneration as the region's founding Islamizing figure.

## Role

The Qarakhanids are the pivotal case for the Islamization of the "far east" of the
Islamic world: their mass conversion carried Islam eastward from Transoxiana into the
Tarim Basin (Kashgar and the wider western reaches of what is today Xinjiang
province), extending the faith beyond the Ghaznavid and Samanid zones of Khorasan and
into Inner Asia proper. Chiara Formichi identifies them as the first of the
"Islamized Turkic dynasties" whose conversions, in the tenth century onward, allowed
Islam to spread among nomadic Turkic populations through the influence of a converted
ruling elite rather than by Arab or Persian conquest.

## Key Events and Processes

No single event page anchors the Qarakhanid conversion in the wiki; it is treated
here as a process within the broader eastward expansion of Islam along Central Asian
trade routes (see [[silk-roads]]).

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Qarakhanid conversion, Islam's overland advance toward the Tarim Basin
and western China would likely have depended on later, less organized channels;
the Qarakhanids provided the first ruling-dynasty vector by which an entire Turkic
steppe population converted alongside its khan, a pattern later repeated by the
Ilkhanid, Golden Horde, and Chagatai conversions.

## Historiography

Formichi cautions that the sources for Satuq Bughra Khan's conversion are thin and
were shaped by later hagiographical retelling, urging caution about taking the
traditional narrative (a miraculous individual conversion followed by mass emulation)
at face value. She also flags a broader historiographical critique: the long-dominant
narrative crediting Sufi mystics with converting Central Asia's nomadic peoples rests
on the reliability of late surviving manuscripts and on an unexamined scholarly
assumption that nomadic converts required a "simplified" message of Islam — an
assumption increasingly challenged in current scholarship.
