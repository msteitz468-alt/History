# Pueblo Peoples

Historic village-dwelling agricultural peoples of the Rio Grande and western plateaus of New Mexico and Arizona—living descendants of [[ancestral-pueblo|Ancestral Pueblo]] communities. Umbrella for independent villages speaking multiple languages, not a single polity. Primary colonial synthesis: Lamar & Truett, [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-2|CHNPA I.2]] ch. 10; precontact continuity: Cordell & Smith, CHNPA I.1 ch. 4.

## Overview

When [[coronado-expedition-1540|Coronado]] entered the Rio Grande Valley in the 1540s he found an Indian population unlike any Spanish had seen north of Mexico City: **irrigation agriculture**, permanent villages, intense religiosity, skilled pottery/weaving/basketry, cotton, deer and bison hunting, and trade with Plains groups. **Seventy or more independent villages** existed; they spoke **five different languages** and had little or no tradition of intervillage cooperation—Eastern Pueblos of the Rio Grande versus Western Pueblos (Zuni and [[hopi|Hopi]]).

Spanish demands for food, supplies, clothing, grain for horses and cattle—and later labor—quickly strained a **narrow margin between plenty and starvation**.

## Spanish colonial extraction and revolt

[[onate-new-mexico-1598|Oñate]] (1598) imposed permanent colonization. Successive governors demanded tributes of woven cloth, cattle, and labor; missions demanded tribute and labor; encomienda grantees expected Indian labor. Authorities sent captured [[apache|Apache]], [[navajo-dine|Navajo]], and Ute south as mining laborers, escalating Athapaskan raiding that forced abandonment of three southern Tiwa villages in the 1670s. Franciscan kiva raids, public burning of sacred objects, and hangings as witches precipitated the **[[pueblo-revolt-1680|Pueblo Revolt of 1680]]**—over 400 colonists and 21 Franciscans killed; Spanish expelled to El Paso; survivors slaughtered European livestock, pulled up fruit trees, and washed away baptism in the river.

## Post-reconquest bargain and demography

De Vargas reconquest (1692–94) took villages one by one after inter-Pueblo unity dissolved. It did not restore the antebellum regime: priests largely abandoned eradication of Pueblo ceremonies; **encomienda abolished**; tribute labor declined; Spanish and Pueblos cooperated against Apache, Comanche, and Navajo raids. New Mexico became a “complex island of severely defined ethnic enclaves”—early dual civil-mission rule more coercive than almost anywhere in New Spain except Cortés’s Mexico City.

**Demography (Lamar & Truett):** population estimated **60,000 in 1600 → ~15,000 in the 1700s**, largely from Old World diseases; by 1700 only **nineteen of ~sixty** original villages remained. If ~20,000 Spanish lived in NM in 1700, only ~35,000 Spanish and Pueblo held against multi-directional attacks. Eastern Pueblos were isolated by Apache raiding between Albuquerque and El Paso.

Mexican period: Franciscan numbers collapsed (as many as sixty priests → five by 1832); Pueblo population from **12,000 (1750) to under 10,000 (1800)**; missions largely abandoned so 1834 secularization could be ignored; Plains raids forced abandonment of long-established trading pueblo of **Pecos** in the early 1800s.

## U.S. territorial period

After Kearny took New Mexico (1846) and territorial status (1850), Governor Calhoun sought to treat Pueblos differently from “wild” Indians—recommending property rights and voting; federal government later **confirmed property rights but not voting**. Taos rebellion 1847 of Hispanic New Mexicans and Taos Indians killed Governor Charles Bent.

**Bursum bill / All Pueblo Council (1922):** Senator Holm O. Bursum’s bill would have validated non-Indian squatter claims to Pueblo lands. Most of the state’s Indian groups sent representatives to Santo Domingo (5 Nov 1922); the unprecedented **All Pueblo Council** appealed “to the people of the United States,” won major press coverage, lobbied Congress, and continued functioning—landmark inter-Pueblo political mobilization with Collier and Stella Atwood’s support (`part_of:` later [[pan-indian-movement]] reform coalitions; Collier’s first-year actions later settled remaining Pueblo land disputes for Indians).

Pueblo leaders also figured among those wary of the Indian Citizenship Act (1924) as a threat to land protections.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Pueblo agricultural surplus and village concentration, Spanish New Mexico as a permanent colony is hard to imagine—and without the coordinated 1680 rising, the post-reconquest bargain (ceremonial survival, abolished encomienda) that structured three centuries of Southwest ethnic politics would not exist. Independent village tradition both limited early Spanish “kingdom” fantasies and enabled de Vargas’s village-by-village reconquest.

## Historiography

Spicer *Cycles of Conquest* remains the seminal synthetic text on Spanish–Mexican–U.S. impact on Southwest Indians; *Handbook of North American Indians* vols. 9–10 (Ortiz) are primary reference starting points. Post-Bolton ethnohistory (Forbes, Jones, John, Weber) revises Spanish borderlands narrative with Indian motives and critical mission views. Bandelier’s 1880s Pecos work established unbroken ancient–modern Pueblo sequence—wiki maps precontact “Anasazi” labels to [[ancestral-pueblo]]. Disease literature (Crosby; Reff *Disease, Depopulation, and Culture Change in Northwestern New Spain*) elevates epidemics as “perhaps the most deadly element of the European conquest,” aligning with the 60k→15k collapse figures.
