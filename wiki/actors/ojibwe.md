# Ojibwe (Anishinaabe)

Anishinaabe peoples of the upper Great Lakes (colonial Chippewa/Ojibwa; includes Mississauga branches). Salisbury treats them within western refugee coalescence, French alliance, and eighteenth-century expansion.

## Overview

### Beaver Wars and refugee coalescence

Western ripple of [[beaver-wars-17th-century]]: Central Algonquians driven west; pan-Algonquian Green Bay became major pro-French, anti-Iroquois center. 1660s–70s multi-ethnic villages at Chequamegon, Sault Ste. Marie, Green Bay, Michilimackinac used intermarriage, adoption, and spread of rituals including **Ojibwas’ Midewiwin** (with Huron Feast of the Dead and Plains calumet) to facilitate unity and French trade. Late-century: well-armed Great Lakes Indians (with Abenaki and Canadian Iroquois) attacked Five Nations; Mississaugas (Ojibwa/Anishinabe branch) occupied southern Ontario hunting territories Iroquois lost.

### Eighteenth century

Foxes driven to Detroit 1712 partly by Dakota/western Ojibwa raids; Fox Wars disrupted French alliance. 1730s French posts on Ojibwa lands and among Assiniboines/Crees aimed to check HBC; eastern Dakotas resented French–rival connection, killed Vérendrye party 1736; Ojibwas with French arms drove Dakotas from Lake Superior, establishing western villages—reinforced Dakota buffalo-land movement. 1752 Ojibwa among attackers of Pickawillany. Participated in French/allied forces vs Braddock and in [[pontiacs-war-1763]] Great Lakes fort assaults. Revolution: trade disruption hardship but no invasion of Ojibwe country (Green/Salisbury).

## Subarctic and northern interior (Ray, CHNPA I.2)

Ray treats Ojibwas (Anishinabes) as the only northern Algonquians whose language differed from Cree dialects. At contact they lived along the north shore of Lake Huron and eastern upper Michigan Peninsula; abundant fishery plus moose/woodland caribou supported **twenty or more villages of 100–300 persons**—semi-sedentary relative to Cree neighbors. Possible but unproven patrilineal clans. They formed the northern arm of Ottawa Valley–north Huron trade with Hurons (Wendats) and Petuns: furs, hides, garments, Lake Superior copper for corn, tobacco, raccoon pelts, black squirrel cloaks.

**Iroquois wars mid-17th c.**: Ojibwas and Ottawas mostly fled west along the south shore of Lake Superior (some to Green Bay). After French Mohawk-country counterattack 1666 restored peace, many returned to SE Superior / N Huron shores as coureurs de bois expanded after ~1670. Eastern Montagnais/Ottawa/Ojibwa carrying trade declined as Cree suppliers gained direct Bay access and trapped more for French sales themselves.

**Rainy–Winnipeg immigration:** after ~1730, following Assiniboine withdrawal, Ojibwas moved into Rainy River–Winnipeg country. Under HBC–NWC destructive competition (1795–1821) they became major **provisioners and canoe manufacturers**—whitefish, sturgeon, wild rice, Indian corn (cultivation expanded after Huron destruction 1640s; prices rose under rivalry); both companies depended on Ojibwa builders of canots du nord on the English River–Rainy River axis. By 1821 Northern Ojibwas possessed areas vacated by Assiniboine and Western Cree from Lake Nipigon to Red River and eastern Lake Winnipeg; overtrapping left some bands short of caribou skins for moccasins; stronger trespass sense in the “Little North.”

**Treaty Three (1873):** Rainy River / Lake of the Woods Ojibwas—diversified after Dawson Road (railway/telegraph/timber/mineral lease interests)—vs Osnaburgh Lake Ojibwas still fur-trade-oriented seeking annuity enough for outfits without trader credit. Government offer ($5/capita annuity; $25 chiefs; $15 headmen; $1,500 ammunition/twine; garden help) divided Indians; southern Ojibwas then accepted. Annuities initially subsidized fur participation across the wooded treaty belt (`part_of: [[northern-interior-subarctic-postcontact]]`; `concurrent_with: [[cree]]`).

Bishop’s *Northern Ojibwa and the Fur Trade* is Ray’s key monograph for the northern branch to 1945. Contact locations of northern Ojibwas remain among Ray’s disputed maps (archaeology vs documents vs oral).

## United States reservation allotment (Hoxie, CHNPA I.2)

After the Wisconsin Central reached Ashland (1877), Ojibwa at Bad River, Lac Court Oreilles, and Lac Du Flambeau faced intensified timber/mineral demand. In the 1880s–90s reservations were surveyed into 80-acre parcels and allotted; “surplus” sold—**up to half the land within Ojibwa reservations passed to Euro-American ownership**. 1882 Interior order allowed timber sales on allotments; 1888 saw 731 contracts remove >190 million board feet—temporary cash, deeper encroachment, destruction of forest subsistence (`caused_by: [[allotment-policy]]`; `part_of: [[reservation-period-1880-1960]]`). Minnesota Ojibwas also among groups losing acreage in the 1880s imposed land-sale wave. Chippewa/Cree/Winnebago flight to unclaimed lands appears among Hoxie’s post–Wounded Knee residual resistance notes.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Ojibwa provisioning and canoe production 1774–1821, Athabasca–Mackenzie brigade logistics and NWC/HBC competition look different; without northern expansion into vacated Assiniboine/Western Cree zones, the ethno-demographic map of the Little North and Treaty Three constituencies changes. US allotment shows the contrasting southern path where agriculture/timber settlement *did* dispossess—highlighting Ray’s Subarctic exceptionalism by contrast.
