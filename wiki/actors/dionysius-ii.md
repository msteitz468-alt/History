# Dionysius II of Syracuse

## Overview

**Dionysius II** ("the Younger," c. 397–after 337 BCE) inherited from his father
**[[dionysius-i|Dionysius I]]** in **367** the greatest power in the Greek world and
squandered it — the weak heir whose reign dissolved into the **mid-fourth-century Sicilian
crisis** of tyrants, mercenaries and Carthaginian intervention that
**[[dion-of-syracuse|Dion]]** and then **[[timoleon]]** would struggle to resolve.
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 13, Westlake) recounts his career.

## Plato and Dion

The young Dionysius came under the influence of his uncle **[[dion-of-syracuse|Dion]]**, a
disciple of **[[plato]]**, who hoped to mould him into the **philosopher-king** of the
*Republic*. Plato made two visits to his court, but the experiment failed: Dionysius,
jealous and dissolute, **banished Dion** (366) and effectively held Plato a virtual
prisoner. The collapse of the Platonic project is the matter of the *Seventh Letter*.

## Fall, recovery, and final exile

In **357** Dion returned with a small force and **expelled Dionysius** from Syracuse;
Dionysius withdrew to **Locri**, where he ruled harshly, while Sicily fragmented among
adventurers after Dion's assassination (354). In **346** Dionysius treacherously
**recovered Syracuse**, but his restored tyranny was feeble and besieged. When the
Syracusan exiles appealed to **Corinth**, **[[timoleon]]** arrived (344) and Dionysius —
"closely besieged" on **Ortygia** — **surrendered and was sent into exile at Corinth**,
where the once-mighty tyrant reportedly ended his days as a schoolmaster, a stock example
in antiquity of the reversals of fortune.

## Significance

Dionysius II is the foil to his father: the same instruments of monarchy — mercenaries,
the Ortygia citadel, fortune — in incapable hands produced not a Hellenistic-style state
but anarchy. His failure prompted both the **Platonic experiment in kingship** (through
Dion) and its refutation, and created the vacuum that **Timoleon** filled by *abolishing*
tyranny and refounding Greek Sicily on republican lines.
