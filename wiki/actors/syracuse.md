# Syracuse (the Archaic and Deinomenid city)

**Syracuse** (*Syrakousai*), the **Corinthian colony** on the south-east coast of
Sicily (founded c. 733 BCE), became under the **Deinomenid tyrants** the greatest and
most populous Greek city of the west — "the prototype of the western colony."
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 16, Asheri) covers its rise to dominance
under **[[gelon|Gelon]]** and the victory of **[[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]]
(480)**; its earlier colonial history is in
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH III.3]] (see [[magna-graecia]]).

## The Archaic "empire" and its crisis

Before the tyranny, Syracuse ruled a territory "almost twice the area of Attica" —
not a centralized state but "a comprehensive amalgam": the city and *chora* on
**Ortygia** and Achradina, the military strongholds **Acrae** and **Casmenae**, the
semi-autonomous colony **Camarina**, Sicel villages reduced to **serfdom** (the
**Killyrians**/Cyllyrii), and hellenized native areas. It was governed by a rigid
**landowning oligarchy, the *gamoroi*** ("land-sharers"), whose lands were worked by
serfs. The flood of new immigrants of the seventh and sixth centuries could not be
absorbed into this "fossilized agrarian structure," producing a free urban *demos* of
traders and craftsmen and chronic *stasis*. When Hippocrates of Gela stripped away the
southern territory, the discredited *gamoroi* were **driven out by a coalition of
*demos* and Killyrians** and took refuge at Casmenae — the opening that let
[[gelon|Gelon]] in.

## The Deinomenid capital

In **485** Gelon brought the exiled oligarchs back, **took the city, and made it his
capital**, restoring the landed oligarchy over the *demos* (Sicilian tyranny "aimed at
restoration, not revolution"). By **deporting** the wealthy of Camarina, Megara
Hyblaea, Euboea and half of Gela into Syracuse as new citizens — and selling their
*demoi* into slavery — he swelled it into "the richest city in Sicily and one of the
most populous in the whole Greek world." **Ortygia** became the tyrant's fortified
preserve of palaces, barracks and docks; the newcomers built **Achradina** and the
expanding suburbs (Tyche, Neapolis, Temenites) outside the walls. The victory of
**[[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]]** financed a great building and coinage programme
(the temple of Demeter and Core at Temenites, the rebuilt Athenaeum — today the
cathedral — and the masterly "Damareteion" silver decadrachm).

## An agrarian, not a maritime, power

Asheri stresses a paradox: despite "her fleet, harbours and docks," Deinomenid
Syracuse **remained essentially an agrarian city**, favouring the *gamoroi* and
*pacheis* and regarding the *demos* as "a most unthankful fellow resident." It did
**not** evolve toward Athenian-style maritime democracy — the contrast that defines
[[western-greek-tyranny|Western Greek tyranny]]. Culturally it was a city that
"imported culture from the mother-country" (hiring the Aeginetan Glaucias and the
Milesian Bion for its dedications), yet it nurtured the original Sicilian voice of the
comic poet **Epicharmus** and hosted **Aeschylus** in its new theatre.

## Hiero, the fall of the Deinomenids, and democratic Syracuse (CAH V)

[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 7, Asheri) carries the city from the Deinomenid apogee
to the democratic republic. Under **[[hiero-i|Hiero I]]** (r. 478–467) Syracuse reached the
height of tyrannical power — the victory over the Etruscans at the
**[[battle-of-cumae-474bce|battle of Cumae]]** (474), the foundation of **Aitna**, and the
most brilliant literary court of the age (Pindar, Aeschylus, Simonides, Bacchylides). But
within five years of Hiero's death the dynasty fell: his harsh brother **Thrasybulus** was
overthrown in **466**, and Syracuse — like Acragas before it — helped break up its own
**epicracy** to win allies for its liberty.

There followed a long conflict between the **"Old" and "New" citizens** (the dispossessed
victims of tyranny versus the tyrants' mercenaries, immigrants and deportees), settled by a
"**Common Resolution**" (*koinon dogma*) and won by the Old in 461. Out of it grew the
institutions of a **republican democracy** — a General Assembly, Council, and board of
*strategoi* — though Syracuse never adopted sortition or pay for office, using instead a
short-lived variant of ostracism (**petalismos**). Democratic Syracuse rapidly became a
**major naval and imperial power**: Tyrrhenian raids (453), the destruction of
[[ducetius|Ducetius']] Sicel state and the building of a new land epicracy (440s), and a
fleet of 100 triremes — "in no way smaller than Athens" (Thucydides), a "megalopolis" of
~250,000. It also became a centre of culture: the birth of formal **rhetoric** (Corax,
Tisias, and the Leontine **Gorgias**), the **mime** of Sophron, and the first **History of
Sicily** by Antiochus, alongside the matchless decadrachm coinage of its mint-engravers.

This is the Syracuse — populous, democratic, navally powerful, and Dorian — that
**Athens would fail to take** in the **[[sicilian-expedition|Sicilian Expedition]]** of
415–413: with the Spartan Gylippus and the Corinthians, Syracuse broke the Athenian siege and
annihilated the greatest armament Athens ever sent out, the turning point of the
[[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]]. Thucydides judged the Syracusans the opponents
"closest in character to the Athenians," and so the ones who "fought best against them."

## Counterfactual significance

Gelon's Syracuse set the enduring pattern of Sicilian history: a **single dominant
power on the east coast**, fed by deportation and mercenaries, holding the Greek west
together against Carthage. It is the direct ancestor of the hegemonies of Hieron I,
Dionysius I, and Agathocles — and the prize Athens would fail to take in 415–413.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **Corinthian colonization** of Sicily; the structural **crisis of the
*gamoroi* oligarchy** under immigration pressure.
produced: the **Deinomenid hegemony** and the victory of [[battle-of-himera-480bce|
Himera]]; the demographic template of the later Sicilian tyrannies.

## Historiography

The narrative rests on **Herodotus**, the **Sicilian historians** (Antiochus, Philistus,
Timaeus) surviving mainly through **Diodorus**, and the **court poetry** of Pindar and
Simonides — a tradition Asheri reads against rich recent **archaeology** (the plans of
Ortygia, Megara Hyblaea, Camarina; the coin series). The image of Gelon's Syracuse as a
golden age must be set against its foundation in **conquest, deportation and slavery**.
