# Woodrow Wilson (1856–1924)

Twenty-eighth president of the United States (1913–1921): Progressive
reformer (New Freedom), war president (1917–18), architect of the
Fourteen Points and the League of Nations, broken by the Senate fight
over the [[treaty-of-versailles-1919|Treaty of Versailles]]. This page
currently reflects [[hartz-louis|Hartz]]'s analysis
([[hartz-liberal-tradition-1955]]); a fuller narrative treatment awaits a
dedicated source.

## Hartz's analysis: messianism as Americanism abroad

**A trapped Progressive at home.** Wilson "reveals even more vividly than
Al Smith the pathetic enslavement of the Progressive tradition to the
'Americanism' that Whiggery had uncovered" — his Algerism has "a quaint
academic touch" (illustrating "what it means to rise" with Princeton
freshmen and medieval priests), "but in essence he is as sound as a
chamber of commerce orator." Hartz dismisses Croly's New Freedom / New
Nationalism distinction (citing William Allen White's "Tweedledum and
Tweedledee"); Wilson even warned that too much regulation would bring
"big businessmen... closer to the government even than they are now."

**Messianism as isolationism's polar counterpart.** "'Americanism' has had
a dual life... a strong isolationist impulse... And yet, in the twentieth
century, 'Americanism' has also crusaded abroad in a Wilsonian way...
Its messianism is the polar counterpart of its isolationism, which is why
Harding and Wilson are both 'Americanist' thinkers." (Kennan: Americans
oscillate between fleeing the world and embracing it "with too ardent a
passion.") See [[isolationism]].

**The blindness was empirical, not just philosophic.** Beyond Lippmann's
"inner contradiction" diagnosis, Hartz argues Wilson missed Europe's
impending social upheavals because "the concept of social upheaval was
alien to the American mind." "Wilson said that he had a sample American
heart, which was true, and that America was the sample democracy of the
world, which was not." The domestic template governed abroad: "Wasn't
Wilson smashing the Austro-Hungarian Empire into bits much as he would
smash an American trust? Wasn't he depending on an automatic harmony as
clearly in the one case as he was in the other?" Alone among the
Versailles liberals — unlike Lloyd George and Clemenceau, who had accepted
social control at home — Wilson was still chasing a Horatio Alger world.

**The Burkean paradox.** A lover of Burke who analyzed modern history from
the errors of the French Revolution, Wilson nonetheless pursued the League
with "Robespierrian" rigidity ("If it won't work it must be made to
work"); Colonel House reminded him that "Anglo-Saxon civilization was
built upon compromises." Hartz's resolution: the crusading impulse toward
"alien" societies was fully in the national tradition; the refusal to
compromise was not — and Harding's isolationist backlash ("One cannot be
half American and half European") was the opposite face of the same
Americanism.

**The red scare.** Wilson denounced Bolshevism as "poison"; intervention
in Russia and the Espionage/Sedition Act prosecutions (Rose Pastor
Stokes; Judge Van Valkenburgh calling the Russian Revolution "the greatest
betrayal of the cause of democracy the world has ever seen") occurred
under his administration — ironically ended by Harding's isolationism.
Hartz treats the war-into-redscare climate as continuous with, not
opposed to, Wilsonian crusading; the century's recurring contest sets
"an A. Mitchell Palmer and a Senator McCarthy" against "a Woodrow Wilson
and a Wendell Willkie... within the single American mind."

## Historiography

Wilson scholarship divides among the heroic-internationalist tradition
(Link), realist critiques (Kennan, Lippmann, Osgood) of moralized foreign
policy — Hartz's structural version radicalizes these — and recent work
foregrounding his racism (federal resegregation) and the colonial limits
of self-determination (Manela's *Wilsonian Moment*). Collection note:
no dedicated Wilson biography ingested; this page is Hartz-derived.
