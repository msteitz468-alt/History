# The Zhou Dynasty

## Overview

The **Zhou** (c. 1045–256 BCE) was the longest-lived dynasty in Chinese history and the
foundational age of Chinese civilization. In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Holcombe,
Ch 15) the Zhou conquest of the **Shang** is "foundational": it established the
**[[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate of Heaven]]**, the name **Zhongguo** ("Central Country"),
and the political and intellectual templates — including the age of **[[confucius|Confucius]]**
and the classic schools of thought — that all later East Asian civilization revered.

## The Conquest and the Mandate of Heaven

Around **1045 BCE** King **Wu** ("the Martial") of the Zhou, a formerly semi-nomadic
people of the Wei River valley, mobilized a coalition (reputedly led by 300 war chariots)
and overthrew the **Shang**. To legitimize rule over peoples not their own, the Zhou
articulated the **[[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate of Heaven]]** (*Tianming*): Heaven (*Tian*)
confers the right to rule on a virtuous **Son of Heaven** and withdraws it from the
decadent — the doctrine that would validate every subsequent dynastic change and underpin
the Chinese ideal of a single legitimate ruler "Under Heaven."

## A Decentralized Realm

The Zhou realm was never strongly centralized: kings delegated authority to hundreds of
**semi-autonomous vassal territories** held by hereditary aristocrats — a structure often
compared (cautiously) to European [[feudalism]]. In **771 BCE** foreigners sacked the
western capital and the kings relocated east to **Luoyang** (the **Eastern Zhou**), after
which royal power decayed and the vassal states became effectively sovereign.

## The Spring and Autumn and Warring States

The Eastern Zhou divides into the **Spring and Autumn period** (722–479 BCE) — the
fragmenting world that produced [[confucius|Confucius]] — and the
**[[warring-states-period-475-bce|Warring States]]** (475–221 BCE), when seven sovereign
kingdoms waged mass infantry-and-crossbow warfare and developed the administrative
machinery (registration, taxation, conscription, **Legalism**) that the
**[[qin-dynasty|Qin]]** would use to unify China. The intellectual ferment of these
centuries — Confucianism, Daoism, Mohism, Legalism — is China's contribution to the
**[[axial-age|Axial Age]]**.

## Significance

The Zhou bequeathed to East Asia the Mandate of Heaven, the ideal of political unity, the
bronze ritual order, the Confucian classical canon, and the very name and self-conception
of "China." Its idealized early reign (the **Duke of Zhou**) became the permanent model of
virtuous government invoked from Confucius to the nineteenth century.

## Historiography

The Zhou conquest is known from later, idealizing Confucian texts and from bronze and
oracle-bone inscriptions; the precise conquest date (c. 1045 BCE) is debated and the moral
"virtue-over-depravity" narrative is a Confucian construct. Recent archaeology (Sanxingdui,
the southern bronze cultures) has dismantled the older image of a single uniform
Shang-Zhou "China," revealing multiple regional Bronze Age civilizations — part of CWH
IV's broader insistence on early China's connectedness to Central Asia and beyond.

Within the [[china|Chinese]] dynastic spine, the Zhou succeeded the **[[shang-dynasty|Shang]]**
(justifying the conquest by the [[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate of Heaven]]) and gave way, through
the Warring States, to unification under the [[qin-shi-huang|First Emperor]] (221 BCE).

## Relations with Northern Peoples (Di Cosmo 2002)

Nicola Di Cosmo's *Ancient China and Its Enemies* (2002), Ch. 3, provides the most detailed analysis of how Zhou-era states actually related to the northern peoples (Di, Rong/Jung, Ti, Yi) in the Spring and Autumn period (722–479 BCE), and its findings substantially revise the older "culturalist" reading.

**Western Zhou background.** The Western Zhou (c. 1045–770 BCE) fought against a host of northern peoples, among whom the Hsien-yün and the Jung were most prominent. The *Shih ching* (Classic of Poetry) records military engagements against the Hsien-yün, including the campaigns of Fang Shu (reputedly leading three thousand chariots) during the reign of King Hsüan (827/25–782/80 BCE). King Mu (956–918 BCE) defeated the Ch'üan Jung in the twelfth year of his reign and expanded Zhou territory westward. By the reign of King Li (857/53–842/28 BCE), however, both the Western Jung and the Hsien-yün launched invasions deep into Zhou territory. In **770 BCE** — the traditional beginning of the Spring and Autumn period — the Ch'üan Jung overran the Chou defenses, sacked the capital, killed King You, and forced the court to relocate east to Luoyang. Various Jung peoples were distributed across northern Shensi, northern Shansi, and Hopei up to the T'ai-hang Mountains — interspersed with Chou settlement, not neatly separated from it.

**The political nature of ethnic labeling.** The Chinese conceptual framework assigned non-Chou peoples to directional slots in a cosmological-moral geography: Man to the south, Yi to the east, Jung/Rong to the west, Ti to the north. In practice, however, these ethnonyms functioned as **political designations** more than ethnic ones. The binome "Yi-Ti" — used in the *Ku-liang* and *Kung-yang* commentaries as a general stigma for "foreignness" — was applied even to the major Chou states Chin, Ch'u, and Wu when those states violated accepted norms. Chin was branded Yi-Ti for employing Ti troops against the Chou political order; Ch'u for treacherously killing a Central State lord; Wu for customs (shaved heads, tattoos) that departed from Zhou norms — yet Wu was fully integrated into the Spring and Autumn diplomatic system, and Chin itself taught Wu chariot warfare. As Di Cosmo concludes, "We find the boundary between the Yi-Ti and the Chou anything but clearly defined."

**Conquest and absorption.** Throughout the Eastern Zhou, the larger Chou states — especially Ch'i, Chin, and Ch'in — systematically conquered and absorbed Jung and Ti peoples, using moral justifications as pretexts for territorial expansion. Duke Hsien of Chin (666 BCE) married two Jung women and used the half-Jung offspring to govern newly conquered frontier territories, precisely because their dual nature made them effective intermediaries. Chin progressively absorbed or destroyed the Ch'ih (Red) Ti during the sixth century BCE: in 594 BCE Chin extinguished the Lu tribe of Ch'ih Ti; in 593 BCE and 588 BCE further campaigns eliminated the last Ti confederate units. By the end of the fifth century BCE, most Ti and Jung communities in northern China had been incorporated into the Central States' territories or eliminated as independent polities. Chung-shan, a Ti-descended state, held out until conquered by Chao in 295 BCE; archaeological evidence shows it was by then fully within Chinese civilization.

**Diplomacy and alliance.** The non-Chou peoples were not excluded from the Spring and Autumn interstate system. From 721 BCE onward the Chou states maintained formal diplomatic relations with Jung and Ti — holding meetings (*hui*), swearing blood covenants (*meng*), exchanging hostages, and forming military alliances. In 640 BCE Ch'i and the Ti concluded a treaty to help Hsing against Wey. In 636 BCE the Chou king attacked the state of Cheng with Ti military assistance. In 627 BCE Chin mobilized the Chiang Jung and defeated Ch'in. The pragmatic argument for peace was made explicit in 569 BCE when Chin's adviser Wei Chiang argued against war with the Wu-chung Jung in purely political and economic terms — preserving agricultural revenues in the borderlands, avoiding military losses, gaining the respect of other states by recruiting foreign auxiliary forces: "The Jung are like wild animals: to gain them, and to lose the Hua, that cannot be!" The decision for war or peace was, as Di Cosmo argues, a matter of political calculation, not philosophical doctrine.

**Incorporation into military structures.** As the Chou states expanded, they incorporated non-Chou peoples into their armies and administrative units. In 632 BCE Chin added three new military columns specifically to fight the Ti; by 588 BCE, after absorbing Jung and Ti populations, Chin commanded six armies — a size formerly reserved only for the Chou royal house. By 541 BCE Chin was transforming its chariot army into infantry better adapted to fighting Jung and Ti foot soldiers. The new administrative unit *chou* (county) was created in the seventh century BCE precisely to govern newly absorbed non-Chou subjects. The Jung chief's speech (559 BCE) makes the integration explicit: despite acknowledged cultural differences in food, drink, clothes, and language, the Jung had served as Chin's soldiers, cleared Chin's frontier lands, and enabled Chin's military rise against Ch'in.

**Significance for Zhou historiography.** Di Cosmo's analysis challenges the older view (represented, e.g., by Cho-yun Hsu in the *Cambridge History of Ancient China*) that the Spring and Autumn period saw the consolidation of a Hua-Hsia cultural consciousness defined against a "barbarian" outside. Instead, the evidence suggests that moral-cultural rhetoric was subordinated to pragmatic political imperatives. The Zhou states' foreign relations operated on three levels — conquest, diplomacy, and incorporation — that show the Jung and Ti as **resources and competitors** rather than as culturally alien others. The hardening of the northern frontier into a demarcation between an agricultural Chinese world and a steppe nomadic world was a development of the Warring States and early imperial periods, not the Spring and Autumn. See [[sinocentrism]] for Di Cosmo's full critique of the culturalist approach. See [[rong-di-yi-ti]] for the ethnonyms and their conceptual structure.

## The Western Zhou "Ritual State" (Major & Cook 2017)

[[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook]] add depth to the **Western Zhou** (thirteen kings,
King Wen to King You; the conquest at **Muye, spring 1046 BCE**, dated by a planetary conjunction
of 1059 BCE in the founding myth). They call it a **"ritual state"**: high offices were
hereditary and personalised, and the king's legitimacy flowed from his **charisma as
Mandate-holder** and his power to authorise officials through ***ming*** (command), not from a
professionalised bureaucracy. The signature source is the **bronze inscription** — hundreds of
inscribed vessels (the longest, the ninth-century **Mao Gong ding**, just under 500 characters) —
which the authors read as a **deliberate act of recording history for the indefinite future**, a
sacred technology of memory. **Hua-Xia**, the first term reasonably translatable as "Chinese,"
emerges here.

Two distinctive positions. (1) ***De*** in the Western Zhou was **not yet Confucian moral virtue**
but a **cosmic-martial force** — health, charisma, and control over resources — transmitted from
Heaven to the founder-kings and bequeathed to their heirs, displayed externally as **"Awesome
Decorum" (*weiyi*)**; only later Confucians moralised it. (2) Major & Cook **avoid the term
"feudal"** entirely: there is no agreed definition of feudalism, no clear evidence of ritual
**subinfeudation**, and the five aristocratic titles (*gong, hou, bo, zi, nan*) show **no original
hierarchy** (*zi* and *nan* do not even appear in Western Zhou bronze inscriptions) — translating
them as duke/marquis/earl/viscount/baron imports false European connotations. The idealised
**[[mencius|Mencius]]-described "well-field" (*jingtian*)** system may never have been fully
implemented. On the **[[duke-of-zhou|Duke of Zhou]]** they caution that bronze inscriptions name
several "Zhou Gong" across generations — possibly an **institutional role**, not one man. The
dynasty fell at the western capital in **771 BCE** (see
[[spring-and-autumn-period-771-bce|Spring and Autumn]]). **Source:**
[[major-cook-ancient-china-2017]].
