## Overview

British Burma was the colonial administration of the territories of the former Konbaung kingdom of Burma, incorporated into British India between 1826 and 1885 through three wars. Unlike British Malaya, Burma was annexed outright and administered as a province of British India rather than as a collection of protectorate states. Its capital was Rangoon. Burma was separated from India as a distinct Crown colony in 1937, occupied by Japan in 1942, and became independent in 1948. See [[burma]] for the precolonial and post-colonial entity.

## Causal History

### The Problem of Anglo-Burman Relations

British contacts with Burma were shaped by a deep structural incompatibility. The rulers of British India operated within a political culture that required all neighboring states to acknowledge British paramountcy explicitly — to behave as subordinate rather than equal actors. The Konbaung dynasty of Burma, which had sacked Ayutthaya (1767), defeated the Mon revolt, and conquered Arakan, operated within an equally assertive tradition of kingship. British emissaries found Burman kings unwilling to treat on terms of equality: "the king ... will treat with no power on earth as an equal ... He will grant a boon but will not make a treaty" (report quoted by Tarling, CHSEA v.2). This incompatibility produced three wars over sixty years.

### First Anglo-Burman War, 1824–1826

Precipitated by frontier disputes in Arakan, Assam, Manipur, and Cachar as the two empires made contact along a contested border. Governor-General Amherst declared war preemptively in March 1824, framing the objective not as territorial acquisition but as "producing such an impression of the power and resources of the British Empire in India as will deter the Court of Ava from any attempt again to disturb the friendly relations." An initially disastrous campaign was eventually won. The **Treaty of Yandabo** (1826) required Burma to cede Arakan, Assam, and Tenasserim; acknowledge British rights over Manipur and Cachar; pay a large indemnity; and receive a British Resident at Ava.

The war was not an uncomplicated British victory. It exposed the limits of military power: the Burmans did not change their fundamental attitude. King Tharrawaddy (r. 1838–46) explicitly repudiated his predecessor's treaties, declaring he wished to annul "all of those acts."

### Second Anglo-Burman War, 1852

Emerged from a commercial dispute between British traders and the Burman governor at Rangoon, handled with extraordinary high-handedness by a British naval commander. Governor-General Dalhousie, though "perhaps less the aggressive empire-builder in Burma than the defender of British prestige on the subcontinent," concluded that occupation of Pegu was now "the only adequate measure for the punishment of the Burmese, for the reimbursement of expenses, and for ensuring future peace by crippling Burman power." Pegu (Lower Burma) was annexed. No formal treaty was signed; Dalhousie calculated it would provoke further quarrels. The new king, Mindon Min, simply accepted the fait accompli.

### Mindon Min's Modernization Attempt

Mindon Min (r. 1853–78) represented Burma's best chance at the kind of defensive modernization that preserved Siamese independence. He sought to develop good relations with the British: commercial treaties were made in 1861 and 1867 allowing British steamers up the Irrawaddy; missions were exchanged. But he also asserted Burma's independence by pursuing relations with France (commercial treaty 1873), the United States (1857), and Russia (overtures 1874). British officials could not accept full Burman independence and wanted formal acknowledgment of subordination. The "shoe question" — whether British envoys must remove footwear before royal reception — became a symbol of the incompatible protocols. After a confrontation in 1875 the British Resident in Mandalay effectively ceased functioning.

### Third Anglo-Burman War, 1885

The decisive trigger was diplomatic-strategic rather than commercial. French expansion in Vietnam (protectorate 1883–5) alarmed British India, whose officials saw it as putting French power on the frontier of upper Burma, the "glacis" protecting India. Thibaw Min's government (r. 1878–85) was simultaneously negotiating in Paris for arms and a French commercial convention. Lord Randolph Churchill stated explicitly: "It is French intrigue which has forced us to go to Burmah; but for that element we might have treated Theebaw with severe neglect."

The immediate pretext was a commercial dispute involving the Bombay-Burma Timber Company's teak concessions. In November 1885 British-Indian forces crossed the frontier; within two weeks they occupied Mandalay. Thibaw was exiled. Viceroy Dufferin chose annexation over protectorate: "there was a vacuum in Mandalay and it was essential to provide against European intrigue."

### Pacification, 1885–1895

The annexation proved far more difficult than the conquest. Officials who argued that the population awaited deliverance were wrong: as Brigadier-General White observed, "It is a mistake to suppose that these people were anxiously awaiting annexation ... they are very loyal, in their easy-going way, to the house of Alompra." Resistance was led by scions of the Konbaung dynasty, local leaders, and after the formal military campaign ended, by what the British called "dacoits" — a term that, like "piracy" in the archipelago, served to delegitimize organized political resistance. The Shan princes, who had paid tribute to Mandalay, accepted British authority on terms of autonomy by April 1888 ("so long as they governed well, promoted trade, and paid a moderate tribute").

The Chinese border was settled in a convention of July 1886, framing it as a relationship of equality to avoid conflict while China faced France in Vietnam. The French frontier was demarcated during the 1890s, with the Mekong River ultimately serving as the border of Laos and Burma by the Anglo-French agreement of 1896.

## Governing Structure

Burma was administered as a province of British India (Lieutenant-Governor, then Governor) until 1937, when it was separated as a distinct Crown colony. The administration was characterized by:

- **Annexation without protectorate.** Unlike Malaya, there were no residual Burman princely states. The entire structure of Konbaung monarchy was dismantled. This created a legitimacy vacuum that proved a persistent source of instability.
- **Minority reliance.** The British, as in India, tended to rely on minority peoples — Karen, Kachin, and Chin soldiers prominent in the colonial military — which deepened ethnic divisions that would complicate post-independence politics.
- **Integration of Shan states.** The Shan princes retained nominal autonomy under a "Shan States" arrangement, a partial exception to the general annexation.
- **Absence of Burmese elite collaboration.** Unlike Malaya, where Malay sultans and aristocrats were incorporated into the administration as Residents' counterparts, Burma had no surviving indigenous ruling class able to serve as colonial intermediaries.

## Counterfactual Significance

The most important counterfactual question is whether the Burman kingdom could have preserved core independence on the Siamese model. Tarling's analysis suggests the possibility was real but required Burman kings to accept a subordinate international position — not a formal protectorate, but the de facto acknowledgment of British paramountcy that Siam managed under Mongkut and Chulalongkorn. Mindon Min came closest: his defensive modernization was acceptable to British India as long as it did not involve arms acquisition from third powers or formal alliances against British interests. His successors, and particularly Thibaw's court, chose the path of diversification rather than managed subordination at the worst possible moment — when French power was advancing in Indochina. The French dimension was structurally decisive: even a more compliant Thibaw might not have averted annexation once Jules Ferry's letter offering arms was publicized.

## Historiography

British Burma is well-documented in British Indian and metropolitan sources (Dalhousie-Phayre correspondence, India Office records). Burman-language sources have received increasing scholarly attention since independence. The standard debates concern: (1) the relative weight of Indian strategic imperatives versus metropolitan commercial interests in driving annexation — the consensus favors the former for the First and Third wars; (2) the nature of Burman resistance — whether "dacoity" framing by the British obscured organized political resistance (Aung-Thwin's work argued for the latter); (3) the long-term consequences of annexation without protectorate, particularly the destruction of the monarchy as a focal point of Burman identity. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina covers this period via CHSEA and older narrative works (Hall, Desai).
