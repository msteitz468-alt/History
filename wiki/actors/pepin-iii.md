# Pepin III (Pepin the Short, r. 751–768)

> **Note on spelling/slug:** The same person is also treated at [[pippin-iii|Pippin III]] (NCMH-centred summary). Prefer cross-linking both; do not invent a second biography of a different man.

## Overview

**Pepin III** (Pepin/Pippin the Short; c. 714–768), son of [[charles-martel]] and father of [[charlemagne]], was the first Carolingian *king* of the Franks. His elevation (751), anointing by Pope Stephen II (754), and Italian interventions (755–56) forged the active [[franco-papal-alliance|Franco-papal alliance]] that Logan places among the central political-religious themes of the Middle Ages.

## Role

- **Mayor to king.** After Charles Martel’s death (741) Pepin shared the mayoralty with Carloman; after Carloman’s monastic withdrawal he ruled alone. In 751 the *Royal Frankish Annals* (written later) report that Pepin asked Pope Zachary whether title-without-power or power-without-title should be king; the pope answered that power should rule. Logan stresses **strong modern doubts**: Pepin may have become king by Frankish nobility alone; if the annalistic account were precise, a pope would have done what no earlier pope had done—create a monarch. Rosamond McKitterick’s work (further reading) challenges the traditional 751 picture.
- **Stephen II and St Denis (753–54).** Pope Stephen II crossed the Alps, met Pepin at Ponthion, and secured military aid against the Lombards. At St Denis (754) Stephen anointed Pepin (a **sacramental** royal function within the church) and conferred the title “Patrician of the Romans.”
- **Italian campaigns and “Donation.”** Pepin defeated the Lombards in spring 755; the 756 campaign sent the keys of 22 cities to Rome. Logan rejects a simple “Donation of Pepin” of conquered Lombard cities: the act restored recently seized cities of the exarchate and Roman duchy. The alliance was struck sometime 751–55.
- **Spiritual kingship.** Anointed Frankish kings took defence of the papacy as a **religious** responsibility; court ideology later cast Charlemagne as “David.”

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Pepin’s sacral kingship and Italian interventions, the later coronation of Charlemagne (800) and the territorial basis of papal temporal power would lack their eighth-century treaty framework. If 751 was a purely Frankish coup with later annalistic papal embellishment (McKitterick), the **memory** of papal king-making still shaped medieval political theology.

## Historiography

Primary narrative: *Royal Frankish Annals* (post-event, dynasty-serving). Logan follows the standard outline while flagging McKitterick, “Illusion of Power in the Carolingian Annals,” *EHR* 115 (2000), and *History and Memory* (2004). NCMH II (Fouracre) treats 751 as “endorsed rather than enabled” by Rome. Collection coverage: strong via NCMH and Logan.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan frames Pepin as the hinge of the [[franco-papal-alliance]]: Italian insecurity (Lombards; failing Ravenna exarchate) met Frankish power after Gregory III’s unanswered 739 appeal to [[charles-martel]]. The 751 query to Zachary—if authentic—was unprecedented; modern doubt does not erase later acceptance of the scenario. Stephen’s 754 anointing gave the king a place *inside* the church; the 755–56 campaigns were restoration, not pure conquest-gift. Dynasty naming: “Carolingian” derives from **Charles Martel**, not Charlemagne; Pepin’s son founded the royal line’s imperial climax. Church accomplishments outlasted the grandsons’ fratricide after 840.
