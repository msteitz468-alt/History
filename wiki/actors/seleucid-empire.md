## Overview

The **Seleucid Empire** was the largest of the [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]]
kingdoms — at its height stretching from the Aegean to the borders of India — founded
by [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus I]] and reckoned from his recovery of Babylon in 312
([[seleucid-era|the Seleucid era]]) to its final reduction by Pompey in 63 BCE. CAH
VII.1 ch. 6 (D. Musti) presents it as the successor state that **most resembled
Alexander's own empire** — similar in size, in multi-ethnic composition, in its
military character, and in political ideology.

## A Personal, Multi-Ethnic Military Monarchy

Musti's central thesis: the Seleucid kingdom was a **personal, not a national,
monarchy**. Unlike Macedonian documents (which name *basileus* + *Makedones*), official
Seleucid texts name **the king but no people** — a deliberate refusal to present the
kingdom as the rule of Macedonian conquerors over subject nations. Its three pillars
were the **king (basileus)**, his **Friends (philoi)** (the council, drawn from across
the Greek world regardless of origin), and the **army (dynameis)** — the true basis of
power. From this military character flow "the colonization, the type of relations with
the natives, the limited success of attempts at hellenization, and the sense of
precariousness pervading the whole history of the kingdom." Beneath the Greek elite lay
the mass of **royal peasants (*laoi*)** — serf-like dependents of the royal land
(*basilike chora*), "not slaves but... akin to rural serfs."

## Geography and Capitals

The compact economic and strategic **nucleus** was **Syria, Mesopotamia, and Cilicia**
— the arc from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean. The capital shifted westward:
from **[[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleuceia-on-the-Tigris]]** (c. 311–301) to
Seleuceia-in-Pieria and finally to **[[antioch|Antioch-on-the-Orontes]]**. Beyond the
nucleus lay the peripheries: **Iran** — "a sort of grandiose outpost, an extraordinary
bulwark" against the steppe, but marginal to the kingdom's Syro-Mesopotamian heart —
and **western Asia Minor**, the contested zone facing the Aegean where Seleucid power
was least stable and most often in crisis.

## Administration

- **Viceroyalties / "special commands"**: the heir typically governed the Iranian
  **"upper satrapies"** ([[antiochus-i-soter|Antiochus I]] under Seleucus I), and a
  governor at **Sardis** held western Asia Minor — a structure that solved the problem of
  a huge realm but also bred dynastic rivalry and secession (Antiochus Hierax, Achaeus).
- **Satrapies** on the Achaemenid model (~14 in the 3rd c.; Appian counts 72 later,
  reflecting subdivision), under *strategoi*; finance through *dioiketes* and *oikonomos*.

## Economy

Agriculture on the royal land worked by the *laoi* was the real fiscal base (the *phoros*
tribute), supplemented by many taxes, the great **temple-states** (which Musti argues the
Seleucids **conserved** rather than secularised — a revision of W. M. Ramsay), and the
crafts and trade of the cities. Syria became the great **reservoir of slaves** for the
western Mediterranean. On the economy and colonisation, see [[seleucid-colonization]].

## Relations with the Greek Cities

A famous historiographical debate (A. Heuss's "idealising" view of fundamentally
independent cities vs. E. Bikerman's realist picture of heavy dependence) frames the
question; Musti steers between them. The Seleucids governed their **own foundations**
directly through a royal *epistates*, while the **"old" Greek cities** of Asia Minor
enjoyed a more distant, varied relationship — courted with the propaganda of *eleutheria*,
*autonomia*, and even *demokratia* (Antiochus III "played the popular card" against Rome).
See [[seleucid-hellenization]].

## Decline

The eastern provinces seceded in the third century — **[[greco-bactrian-kingdom|Bactria]]**
under Diodotus and **[[parthian-empire|Parthia]]** under Andragoras and then the nomadic
Arsacid Parni (Parthian era 247). [[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III]] restored the
kingdom "almost to the boundaries of its founder" with his great *anabasis* (212–205),
but his defeat by Rome (Magnesia, 190; Peace of Apamea, 188) cost him Asia Minor, and the
second century brought dynastic civil war, the Maccabean revolt, and the relentless
advance of Parthia — to the kingdom's extinction in 63 BCE.

## The Terminal Decline, 187–129 (CAH VIII, Ch. 10, Habicht)

[[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] traces the kingdom from Apamea to disintegration.
The quiet, indemnity-burdened reign of **Seleucus IV** (187–175) was followed by the
forceful **[[antiochus-iv-epiphanes|Antiochus IV]]** (175–164), who broke the
post-Apamea isolation, nearly conquered Egypt (halted at the
[[day-of-eleusis-168bce|Day of Eleusis]]), provoked the
[[maccabean-revolt-167-160bce|Maccabean revolt]], and died on an eastern campaign
against a disintegrating frontier. Thereafter the dynasty consumed itself in
**civil war among rival claimants** (the boy Antiochus V and Lysias; the returning
hostage **Demetrius I**; the satrap **Timarchus** of Media, who proclaimed himself
"Great King") — while Rome cultivated the discord, keeping Demetrius a hostage
"as a weapon." Behind the dynastic chaos the empire's body fell away: **Armenia,
Persis, Elymais, and the upper satrapies** asserted independence, and above all the
**[[parthian-empire|Parthians]]** under Mithridates I advanced to absorb Media and
Mesopotamia. By 129 the Seleucids were reduced to a Syrian rump.
