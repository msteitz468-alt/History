# Srivijaya

**Srivijaya** is the generic name for the succession of **Malay thalassocracies** (maritime empires)
centred on **Palembang**, on the Musi River of southeastern **Sumatra**, that dominated the
[[indian-ocean-trade|sea-trade]] of the western archipelago from the **seventh to the fourteenth
centuries**. Documented for the wiki through the [[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v1-1992|Cambridge
History of Southeast Asia]] (Keith Taylor, Ch. 3). It is the classic case of the
[[southeast-asian-port-polity|entrepôt polity]] — power resting not on land or population but on the
**control of the Strait of Malacca**, the throat of the China–India trade route.

## A thalassocracy of the Straits

From the fourth century, Malay sailors exploited the new opportunity for **direct maritime trade**
between southern China and western Asia through the Straits (rather than the older Kra-isthmus
portages). In the **late seventh century**, rulers near Palembang achieved **paramountcy**. The Chinese
pilgrim **I Ching** (in Sumatra in the 670s–690s) and three inscriptions of the 680s reveal a prosperous
entrepôt where ships awaited the change of the monsoon, a great **Mahayana Buddhist** centre where
Chinese monks studied with Indian teachers, and a ruler sending fighting-ship expeditions to intimidate
rivals along the Straits of Malacca and Sunda. Srivijaya was less a territorial kingdom than a
**pyramidal network of loyalties** among Malay port-rulers united by a common interest in the wealth of
passing ships.

## Wealth, rivals, and the Chola raid

In the mid-ninth century a Srivijayan ruler was a prince of the central-Javanese **[[sailendra|Sailendra]]**
line (the builders of Borobudur), and financed a Buddhist monastery at **Nalanda** in Bengal. Srivijaya's
"splendid position at the throat of the trade routes" bred acquisitive rivals: the Javanese (whose
capital a Srivijayan expedition destroyed in 1016) and, most dramatically, the south-Indian
**[[chola-empire|Chola]]** dynasty, whose fleet **sacked the Srivijayan capital in 1025** and raided
other Malay centres. Srivijaya could be plundered and briefly pressed into vassalage, but not destroyed
by military force.

## Dispersal and successors

What ultimately undid Srivijaya was **commercial, not military**: the growing presence of **Chinese
shipping** (especially under the Southern Song in the twelfth century, when private Chinese ships began
visiting production sources directly) **reduced the leverage of Malay carriers** and *dispersed*
authority — individual ports could now deal directly with China, so there was no longer need for a single
Malay overlord. Palembang receded; the Srivijayan tradition passed to **Jambi** (twelfth century), then
was squeezed between expanding **Ayutthaya** and **[[majapahit|Majapahit]]** in the thirteenth–fourteenth
centuries. Its heir was **[[melaka|Melaka]]**, founded c. 1400 by the Palembang/Tumasik prince
**Parameswara** as "a new version of the Srivijayan model" under Ming Chinese protection. Keith Taylor's
striking coda: modern **Singapore** is itself "a modern version of the Srivijayan achievement" — a
favourable location concentrating commercial wealth.

## Historiography

Srivijaya is almost entirely a **twentieth-century reconstruction** (Cœdès identified it only in 1918)
from Chinese and Arabo-Persian texts, a handful of inscriptions, and — increasingly — archaeology; "the
Malays themselves have preserved virtually no memory" of it, because in a diffuse maritime culture
affirming a fixed past was never a priority (Malay collective memory effectively begins with Islamic
Melaka). Its location, extent, and even its nature as a single "empire" versus a shifting hegemony remain
debated. It is a prime instance of the [[southeast-asian-port-polity|entrepôt thesis]] and of
[[indianization-of-southeast-asia|Indianization]] (the Buddhist Sailendra link).
