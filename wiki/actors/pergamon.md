## Overview

**Pergamum**, ruled by the **Attalid dynasty**, rose in the third century BCE from a
hilltop fortress in Mysia into the most important state of western Asia Minor and a great
centre of [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]] art and culture. CAH VII.1 ch. 11 (Heinen)
traces its emergence "in the shadow of the competing Hellenistic powers."

## From Treasure-Fortress to Kingdom

The kingdom's origin was the **9,000-talent treasure** that [[lysimachus]] deposited at
Pergamum under the officer **Philetaerus**. When Lysimachus fell at
[[battle-of-corupedium-281|Corupedium]] (281), Philetaerus — who had already shifted his
allegiance to [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus I]] — kept the treasure and made himself an
independent (but cautious, still nominally Seleucid) **dynast**, his coins bearing
Seleucus' head, not his own. Successive rulers grew bolder:

- **Philetaerus** (d. 263) — founder; built Pergamum as a Greek city and royal residence,
  patron of Hellenic cities and temples.
- **Eumenes I** (263–241) — defeated [[antiochus-i-soter|Antiochus I]] at Sardis,
  expanded the territory and reached the sea (the port of Elaea), but still did not take
  the royal title.
- **Attalus I "Soter"** (241–197) — the maker of the kingdom: the first to **refuse
  tribute to the [[galatians|Galatians]]** and to defeat them in battle, taking the
  **royal title** and posing as the Greek champion against barbarism. Exploiting the
  Seleucid "War of the Brothers," he won the Seleucid lands of Asia Minor to the Taurus —
  though [[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III's]] general Achaeus drove him back to
  Pergamum (223/2) before Attalus recovered north-western Asia Minor in 218.

## Pergamene Art and Ideology

Attalus I's victories over the Galatians "led to the birth of Pergamene art." In the
sanctuary of Athena he set up sculptural monuments — the famous **Dying Gaul** and the
**Gaul killing himself and his wife** (surviving as Roman marble copies) — depicting the
defeated Celts with awe and pathos. In Attalid ideology **the Galatians took the place
that the Persians had filled for Athens**: the barbarian foil against which the half-Greek
dynasty advertised itself as the missionary champion of Hellenic civilisation — a theme
culminating in the **Great Altar of Pergamum** (Gigantomachy frieze, 2nd c.). Heinen notes
the irony that the same dynasts who posed as scourges of the Galatians also hired Galatian
mercenaries.

## Significance

Pergamum is the model of the **opportunistic new state** that the Syrian Wars and Seleucid
weakness allowed to flourish in Asia Minor — alongside [[rhodes|Rhodes]], Bithynia,
Pontus, and Cappadocia. Its later Library rivalled [[alexandria|Alexandria's]] (and gave
us "parchment," *pergamenum*). By the end of the third century Pergamum and Rhodes were
the leading powers of the region, "destined to play a leading role in the conflict between
Rome and the Hellenistic monarchies." The last Attalid bequeathed the kingdom to Rome
(133), which made it the province of Asia. See [[galatians]], [[seleucid-empire]],
[[hellenistic-monarchy]].

## The Roman alliance and Attalid apogee (CAH VIII, Chs 8–9)

[[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] shows Pergamon's strategy of riding Roman power to
greatness. **Attalus I** triggered the [[second-macedonian-war-200-196bce|Second
Macedonian War]] by appealing to Rome against Philip; his son **Eumenes II**
(r. 197–159) made Pergamon Rome's indispensable ally against
[[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III]], and at the **[[peace-of-apamea-188bce|
Peace of Apamea]]** (188) received the lion's share of Seleucid Asia Minor —
transforming a small principality into a great kingdom. Eumenes then drove the case
against [[perseus-of-macedon|Perseus]] (his prepared list of charges helped launch
the [[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|Third Macedonian War]]). But the lesson of
[[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|Pydna]] was that **kings were now at a discount**:
even Eumenes fell under Roman "baleful suspicion," Rome inviting accusations against
him (164) as it once had against Antiochus and Philip. See [[peace-of-apamea-188bce]],
[[perseus-of-macedon]], [[nature-of-roman-imperialism]].
