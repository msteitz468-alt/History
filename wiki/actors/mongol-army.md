# The Mongol Army

The **Mongol army** was the instrument of the [[mongol-conquests-13th-century|13th-century
conquests]] and the most effective military force of its age. [[morgan-the-mongols|David
Morgan]] argues its success turned on its **character, not its size**: a whole-population
cavalry of unmatched mobility, discipline, training, and organization, drawn from the
[[pastoral-nomadism|nomadic]] society [[genghis-khan|Chingiz Khan]] welded into a single
military machine.

## A nation in arms

In nomadic society there was, Morgan notes, "no such thing as a civilian": **every adult
male Mongol under 60 was liable for service**, and any Mongol who could ride — which meant
nearly all of them, trained from infancy — was a potential soldier. Juwayni described the
army as "a peasantry in the dress of an army... all, from small to great... swordsmen,
archers or spearmen." This let the Mongols **mobilize a far greater proportion of their
population** than the sedentary states they invaded, and to do so quickly.

## The great hunt as a school of war

Military training was continuous and embedded in the **great hunt** (*nerge*): a vast
ring of riders driving game across the steppe over weeks, none permitted to let an animal
escape or to kill before the khan loosed the first arrow. Juwayni and Gibbon both saw it
as a rehearsal for war — "the prompt and accurate judgment of ground, of distance, and of
time" — schooling commanders in coordinated maneuver, encirclement, and discipline.

## Decimal organization

The army's best-known feature was its **decimal structure**: units of 10 (*arban*), 100
(*jaghun*), 1,000 (*mingghan*), and 10,000 (the **tümen**). Chingiz Khan used this system
to **break up the old tribal formations**, distributing conquered and rival peoples across
units that cut across clan loyalties and answered to commanders appointed by merit and
loyalty — at once a military and a **political** instrument of unification. The system was
not original (the Liao and earlier steppe powers used it) but the Mongols applied it with
unmatched thoroughness.

## Size — and its limits

The size of Mongol armies is fiercely debated. Internal sources (the *Secret History*,
Rashid al-Din via the *Altan Debter*) suggest a core army of roughly **105,000–129,000**
at Chingiz's death; hostile outside chroniclers inflate this wildly (Juzjani's 700,000–
800,000). Morgan treats the high figures skeptically: the Mongols won "not, essentially,
by the sheer weight of numbers" — indeed their armies were probably **smaller** than the
Khwarazmian, Jin, or Song forces they beat — and exaggeration was abetted by the Mongols'
own tricks (five remounts per man, dummies on spare horses) to magnify their apparent
strength. Crucially, Morgan argues the army's reach was bounded by **pasture**: the need
to feed huge horse-herds (perhaps five per man) helps explain the repeated Mongol failure
to hold **Syria** — and, with the defeat at **[[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ain Jalut]]**
(1260), the southwestern limit of the empire.

## Significance

The Mongol army was the engine of the largest contiguous empire in history and a model of
steppe military organization. Its decimal system doubled as the **administrative skeleton**
of the empire, and the administrative decrees governing its training, communications, and
supply are, Morgan shows, the very texts later misremembered as the [[the-mongol-yasa|Great
Yasa]]. Its limits — logistics and pasture — were as historically decisive as its victories.
