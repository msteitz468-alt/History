# Francisco Pizarro

The conqueror of the [[inca-empire|Inca empire]] — the man whose
[[spanish-conquest-of-peru-1532|expedition of 1532–33]] delivered the Andean core
of [[colonial-latin-america|colonial Latin America]] and, in his contemporaries'
eyes, achieved the greatest of all the conquests.

## Role and career

The **illegitimate, unlettered son of a modest Extremaduran noble** from Trujillo,
Pizarro emigrated young to the Indies, gained experience on the Isthmus of Panama,
and became a prosperous *encomendero* and co-founder of Panama City — an obscure
frontier worthy in midlife. From 1524 he led probing voyages south with Diego de
**Almagro** and the priest **Luque**, persevering through disaster (the thirteen
men of the Isla del Gallo) until proof of a rich southern civilization let him win
a royal *capitulación* as governor of Peru. He recruited four brothers and a cousin
from Trujillo for the venture.

His genius was for **audacity and intelligence**: he gathered remarkably good
information on the Inca civil war, then copied Cortés's stratagem by seizing
**Atawallpa** at Cajamarca (November 1532) and extracting the largest ransom in
the history of the conquests. He executed Atawallpa (1533), installed puppet Incas,
took Cuzco, and founded **Lima** as his coastal capital (1535).

## Decisions and their consequences

- **The Cajamarca ambush** decapitated a centralized empire at a stroke — the
  template for the conquest's success.
- **Executing Atawallpa** removed the only authority who could have ordered a
  unified Inca counter-attack, but legitimized the suspicion and factional violence
  that followed.
- **The unequal division of spoils and titles** with Almagro bred the
  **factionalism** that erupted into Peru's civil wars and ultimately cost Pizarro
  his life: in 1541 supporters of the younger Almagro stormed his Lima palace and
  **assassinated** him.

## Counterfactual significance

Pizarro is the textbook case in the debate over whether the conquests turned on
**individual men or structural conditions**. Burkholder and Johnson stress that he
struck at a uniquely propitious moment — an empire shattered by
[[virgin-soil-epidemics|epidemic]] and the [[inca-empire|Waskar–Atawallpa civil war]],
with anti-Atawallpa peoples ready to ally. Without those conditions, his fewer-than-200
men could not have prevailed; *with* them, his audacity and cunning were decisive.
He is thus neither a superman nor a cipher — the conqueror who exploited a collapse
he did not cause.
