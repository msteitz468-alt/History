# Alexander the Great

> **Detailed analytic biography:** [[hubs/biographies/classical-antiquity/alexander-the-great|Alexander the Great — Detailed Biography]] (in progress; prioritized per Biographies Hub selection criteria and this dedicated source)

## Overview

**Alexander III of Macedon** (r. 336–323 BCE) conquered the entire
**[[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid Persian Empire]]** in a single decade, carrying
Macedonian and Greek arms and culture from the Balkans to the Indus. In
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] he is the hinge between the Persian imperial age and the
**[[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]] world** — the agent who fused the Greek
[[city-state|polis]] tradition with Near Eastern empire and stitched the western half
of Afro-Eurasia into a denser cultural and commercial web that fed the coming
[[silk-roads|Silk Roads]].

## Accession and the crushing of Greek revolt

[[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC* (2000)]]
details Alexander's brutal consolidation on taking the throne (336): revolts broke out
across the newly-won Macedonian sphere — **Thrace, Illyria, Thessaly, [[actors/thebes|
Thebes]], and Ambracia** — and were "quickly and ruthlessly crushed." Thebes rose again
in 335 while Alexander campaigned in the north, and this time he **eliminated the city
entirely** — even a city as recently powerful as Thebes was destroyed outright, a
demonstration of Macedonian ruthlessness meant to forestall further Greek revolt. Having
secured Greece, Alexander crossed the Hellespont in 334, "never to return to Europe."

## Conquest

Heir to the militarized, professionalized kingdom built by his father **Philip II**
(who had subdued the Greek city-states at Chaeronea, 338 BCE), Alexander crossed into
Asia in 334 BCE. He defeated **Darius III** at the **Granicus (334)**, **Issus (333)**,
and decisively at **Gaugamela (331 BCE)**; took the Phoenician city of **Tyre** after a
celebrated seven-month siege involving a mole from the old city, advanced torsion artillery, and personal assault (332); was acknowledged in **Egypt**, where he founded **Alexandria** (personally marking the plan); and burned the Persian capital **Persepolis**. Pressing east through Bactria and
Sogdiana (marrying Roxane; ordering 30,000 Iranian boys trained as Macedonian-style Epigonoi), he campaigned into the **Punjab**, defeating **Porus** at the Hydaspes (326) with a night crossing and elephant countermeasures, before his exhausted army refused to go further at the Hyphasis. The return featured the disastrous Gedrosian desert march and fleet coordination. He died of fever in **Babylon in 323 BCE**, aged 32, his empire undivided in name only. (Detailed campaign mechanics and source critique from [[fox-alexander-the-great-1973]].)

## Administration, the Exiles' Decree, and the Greek reaction

Osborne notes that Alexander largely **retained Persia's loose administrative
structures**, at first replacing satraps with Macedonians and Greeks and, after Darius
III's final defeat, generally reappointing natives (often reconfirming Darius's own
former officials) — alongside adopting Persian royal trappings (native sacrifice,
Persian dress, the expectation of obeisance) that many Greeks and Macedonians found
distasteful given their view of Persians as "slaves" to their king.

Near the end of his reign Alexander issued the **Exiles' Decree**, proclaimed at Olympia
in **324**: every Greek city was ordered to take back all its exiles (barring those
exiled for sacrilege) and restore property that had been publicly sold off — a universal
demand affecting whole displaced populations (notably the Samians), not just individual
political figures. Osborne treats the decree's intent as genuinely ambiguous: it may
reflect Alexander's poor attunement to Greek domestic politics, or a deliberate,
cynically calculated move to destabilize existing regimes and keep his regent
**Antipater** occupied at home. Ten months after the decree, Alexander's death (323)
triggered **another unsuccessful Greek revolt**, this time led by Athens — one that,
just as Athens itself had not joined Sparta's abortive rising of 331 (see
[[league-of-corinth]]), received no support from Sparta, Arcadia, or Boeotia. The
episode is Osborne's closing illustration of persistent Greek civic disunity even after
two decades of Macedonian domination.

## The Wars of the Successors

Alexander left no viable heir — only a retarded half-brother (Philip III Arrhidaeus)
and an unborn son (Alexander IV) — and his generals (the **Diadochi**) fought four
decades of [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|wars of succession]] (323–281) that carved the
empire into the great **[[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]] kingdoms**: the
[[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemies]] in Egypt, the [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucids]] across
Asia, the [[antigonid-dynasty|Antigonids]] in Macedon. The decisive turning points were
the death of [[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus I]] — the last to pursue
reunification — at [[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]] (301), and the murder of the boy
Alexander IV (c. 310), which **extinguished the Argead dynasty** and made spear-won
[[hellenistic-monarchy|kingship]] the basis of legitimacy. [[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|CAH
VII.1]] gives the detailed narrative (Édouard Will, chs. 2 & 4).

## Significance

Counterfactually, Alexander's conquests were extraordinarily contingent on one
short-lived individual, yet their structural consequences were durable: a belt of Greek
foundation-cities from Egypt to Bactria, a common Hellenistic Greek (koine) culture,
intensified exchange linking the Mediterranean to India and Inner Asia, and a template
of cosmopolitan, divinized kingship inherited by [[roman-empire|Rome]] and beyond. His
policy of fusion (intermarriage, recruiting Persians, adopting Persian court ritual)
remains the central interpretive puzzle.

## The fourth-century culmination (CAH VI)

[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] gives Alexander the detailed treatment of the
fourth-century narrative (Chs. 16–17, A. B. Bosworth, "The events of the reign" and "Greece
and the conquered territories") and a synthesizing **Epilogue** (Ch. 18, Simon Hornblower)
that places the conquest in context. Two themes stand out.

**Alexander as culmination, not miracle.** Hornblower presents Alexander as "**a kind of
climax to already discernible tendencies**" of the fourth century rather than a man born
with a "mission." Behind him stand the autocrats this volume has surveyed: **[[dionysius-i|
Dionysius I]]** of Syracuse (the "**prototype**" — a forceful military despot "effective
simply by knowing where he was going"), **[[mausolus]]** the semi-autonomous satrap, the
**[[bosporan-kingdom|Bosporan kings]]** — all, like Alexander, balancing rule over
"freedom-conscious Greeks" and "more absolute" mastery of non-Greeks, often through
**different titulature** for each. Hornblower warns against the slide from "the Great" (a
title first attested in Roman sources) toward an illusion of **inevitability** and a
"great-man" reading: to contemporaries he was simply "Alexander the Macedonian."

**Why Macedon invaded Asia.** The Epilogue offers a three-tiered explanation:
1. **Short-term — Philip's inheritance.** "Alexander conquered the Persian empire only
   because **[[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip]] had planned its conquest**": the army, the
   commanders, the deification precedent, and the expedition itself were Philip's, already
   begun by 336. Aborting it "would itself have required a definite decision." The campaign
   up to **[[battle-of-gaugamela-331bce|Gaugamela]]** "resembles a game of chess," dictated
   by the moves of Darius III; the one free gamble — swinging **west to Egypt** after Issus
   — was driven by economics (Egypt's wealth).
2. **Medium-term — revenge for 480.** The panhellenist motif of avenging Xerxes' impiety,
   formalized in the **[[league-of-corinth|League of Corinth]]** and dramatized by Philip's
   (and Alexander's) rebuilding of **Plataea** — though Polybius dismissed it as a pretext.
3. **Long-term** — the deep, centuries-old Greek preoccupation with the Persian Wars and
   the recurring [[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|panhellenist dream]] of Persia's
   vulnerability.

The volume thus closes by binding Alexander back into the fourth century it has narrated:
the heir of Philip, of Sicilian and satrapal monarchy, and of the long Greek argument with
Persia — the figure in whom the exhausted world of the [[city-state|polis]] gave way to the
age of the territorial kingdom.

## Army and finance (CHGRW)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|Sekunda (CHGRW I.11A)]]
gives force numbers for the inheritance and the Asian expedition: Philip’s army in 358
= 10,000 infantry + 600 horse (Diod. 16.4.3); Alexander’s 334 expedition = 32,000 foot
(Macedonian phalanx 12,000) + 5,100 horse, leaving 12,000 foot + 1,500 horse under
Antipater in Europe. Companion cavalry: originally a court title; purple long-sleeved
tunic borrowed from the Persian King’s “Friends”; under Philip 600 Companions
(Theopompus), later expanded by land grants; Alexander is alleged to have alienated
nearly all crown lands to Companions before Asia (Plut. *Alex.* 15.3–4). Cavalry
organization: *ile* of 200 in four *tetrarchiai* (allowing double-strength Royal Squadron
400); each 50-man *tetrarchia* in **wedge formation** borrowed by Philip from
Thracians/Scythians (Arr. *Tact.* 16.6) — replacing linear Greek cavalry and Thessalian
rhomboid — making cavalry the striking force of Alexander’s battle plan. Late-reign
“mixed” phalanx experiment (Arr. *Anab.* 7.23.3–4): Macedonians first three + rear of each
*dekas*; twelve middle ranks Persians with bows/javelins (~20,000 Persians / ~6,500
Macedonians ≈53 mixed *lochoi*); parallels Xenophon *Cyropaedia* 6.3.23 — Alexander may
have read Xenophon or both reflect a fourth-century Persian practice.

Serrati (CHGRW I.14): Alexander launched Asia on heavy borrowing after Philip’s
expenditures; early finances strained enough to disband the navy, then transformed by
plunder (Sardis, Issus, Damascus) and capture of Babylon, Susa, Persepolis, Ecbatana
(331–330) — “marked disregard for finances” thereafter. Much Persian movable plunder was
melted for coinage from the early 320s; he largely neglected tribute and most satrapal
treasuries — leaving resources the Successors later seized. Roth (CHGRW I.12): Alexander
became the model for Hellenistic (and Roman) generals; strategic risk-taking (including
the invasion of Persia as a gamble) remained central to Hellenistic strategy. See
[[hellenistic-warfare]].

## Historiography

No contemporary account survives intact; the narrative rests on later writers (Arrian,
Plutarch, Diodorus, Curtius, Justin) working from lost sources, leaving room for sharp
disagreement. Debates: was Alexander a visionary of human unity or a brutal
megalomaniac; how deliberate was "Hellenization"; and how much of the Hellenistic
synthesis was his design versus the work of his successors and local elites. His
legend (the Alexander Romance) circulated across Afro-Eurasia from Europe to Iran and
India — itself a marker of the connected world he helped create.

**Freeman** ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]]) sharpens the source-critical caution:
none of the five major ancient sources (Arrian, Plutarch, Diodorus, Curtius, Justin)
predates Alexander by less than 300–400 years, and all are "encrusted with later
legend" — **Bosworth**'s source-critical work is credited with correcting the hagiographic
reading of **Tarn** (1948), who held that Alexander "lifted the civilized world out of
one groove." Freeman explicitly rejects **Voltaire**'s Enlightenment thesis that Alexander
consciously brought "superior civilization" to Asia as anachronistic, and flags
**Plutarch**'s panegyric "Legacy" passages on Alexander as rhetoric rather than historical
testimony — noting it is clear from Plutarch's other writings, including his own
biography of Alexander, "that he did not himself believe in this adulation." On this
reading Alexander's true legacy was a **model of personal, divine-right monarchy**
adopted by the Hellenistic kings and later Roman emperors, not an institutional empire —
it fragmented immediately, with no orderly succession.

**CHGRW (Hanson / Hornblower / Whitby):** realist reassessment judges Alexander by ordinary
lives altered, not profession or empire size (Bosworth vs traditional Fuller/Hammond
praise). Homeric imitation layers (Arrian, Ptolemy, Callisthenes, Alexander himself)
create four non-exclusive explanations for heroic depiction. Granicus and Issus show how
encomiastic literary tradition contaminates “military” sources.

**Carol G. Thomas, *Alexander the Great in his World* (2007)**, now ingested
(**[[thomas-alexander-in-his-world]]**), approaches him through **context rather than narrative**:
arguing that the key to Alexander lies less in psychologizing the man (whom the sources cannot deliver
reliably) than in the **world that made him** — his Macedonian identity, the peculiar nature of the
Macedonian kingdom and army, the Greek intellectual inheritance, and the Persian and Near Eastern
setting he entered. It is also a useful survey of the **"many images" of Alexander** the fragmentary,
late source-tradition has generated.

**Robin Lane Fox, *Alexander the Great* (1973; 2004 reissue)** ([[fox-alexander-the-great-1973]]) supplies the richest narrative reconstruction among current sources. Explicitly "a search, not a story," it is grounded in close source criticism (especially Arrian/Ptolemy) and practical military/topographical reasoning. It adds detailed accounts of the Balkan prelude, the great set-piece battles and sieges (Granicus, Issus, Tyre, Gaugamela), the Gedrosian return, and the mechanics of fusion policy and army discipline. Its portraits of Philip's legacy and the limits Alexander faced are particularly valuable for the detailed biography.

**Peter Green, *Alexander of Macedon, 356–323 B.C.* (1974/1991/2013)** ([[green-alexander-of-macedon-2013]]) provides the most sustained psychological and Badian-influenced political reading. Green depicts Alexander as a brilliant but obsessional conqueror whose successes fed narcissism into megalomania; he is more willing than Fox to accept "vulgate" anecdotes of brutality and court intrigue as reflecting real patterns (comparing purges to later tyrannies). Green emphasizes the growing alienation between Alexander and his Macedonian core and treats fusion policies as pragmatic responses to military overstretch rather than idealistic universalism. His treatment of Philip II's structural legacy (Ch. 1) and the Granicus propaganda appendix are especially strong. Complements Fox by supplying a coherent interpretive through-line.

## OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Price (Ch.13), Annas (Ch.10)

[[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]] (Simon Price, Hellenistic History):

**Panhellenic crusade frame and lightning western campaign.** After Philip’s murder Alexander inherited Macedonian dominance of mainland Greece and launched a crusade (begun under Philip) to punish Persia for Xerxes’ invasion. Within a year he controlled the Greek cities of western Turkey, cut the Gordian knot, defeated Darius at Issus (333), took Phoenicia and Egypt, visited Zeus Ammon (greeted as “son of Ammon”), and decisively defeated Darius at Gaugamela (331).

**East, death, monarchical model.** Campaign into the eastern empire (Sogdiana resistance; marriage to Roxane; Punjab victory; troop revolt blocked further east; disastrous Baluchistan return); died at Babylon in 323 aged thirty-two. “Alexander left behind not only conquests, but also monarchy… He provided a model for the series of Hellenistic kings that followed. The diadem, the plain headband worn by Alexander, became the standard symbol of monarchy.” Twenty years of Diadochi struggles failed to produce a sole successor; by c.275 three kingdoms (Ptolemies, Seleucids, Antigonids) dominated until Rome.

**Aristotle’s tutoring (Annas Ch.10).** Aristotle (384–322), from Stagira with Macedonian court medical connections, later tutored Alexander—little mutual impact per Annas—before founding the Lyceum c.335 and fleeing anti-Macedonian hostility after Alexander’s death.

**Forrest (Ch.1) long afterlife.** Marathon-era Greek–barbarian ideology and 480–479 dual supremacy patterns are treated as the cultural background still available for Alexander’s “revenge for Xerxes” propaganda.
