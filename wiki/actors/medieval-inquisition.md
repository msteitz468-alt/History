# The Medieval Inquisition

## Founding Conditions

The medieval (papal) Inquisition emerged because earlier mechanisms for suppressing
heresy had failed. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Bernard Hamilton, Ch. 2b) explains
that by the 1220s comprehensive anti-heretical legislation was in place — canon
*Excommunicamus* of the **Fourth Lateran Council (1215)**, and death-penalty statutes from
[[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Frederick II]] (Empire 1224, Sicily 1231), Peter II of
Aragon (1197), and the Capetians — but it was **manifestly ineffective**: bishops lacked
the time and resources for inquisition, and lay officials were untrained in
interrogating heretics. **Gregory IX**, as an experiment, delegated the work to
**Dominican and Franciscan friars**, who were professional theologians qualified to
identify heresy, vowed to poverty (and so unlikely to take bribes), and free to work
full-time. The first mendicant inquisitor was appointed at Florence in 1233, and the
papal Inquisition became a regular Church court.

## Structure

- There were **seldom more than two dozen inquisitors** in office at any one time, each
  **directly answerable to the pope**, with no central coordinating body.
- Procedures were initially crude but quickly standardized: the earliest known
  inquisitor's manual was in use by 1248–49.
- An inquiry opened with a **period of grace** (light penances for voluntary confession),
  building a list of suspects summoned before a tribunal of the inquisitor, a notary, and
  sworn witnesses.
- Procedure was **weighted against the accused**: accusers were unnamed (for security),
  charges unspecified, and no lawyer would defend a suspect for fear of association with
  heresy. A suspect could appeal to the curia (expensive) or try to prove an accuser bore
  him mortal enmity.
- **Torture** was licensed by Innocent IV's bull ***Ad extirpanda* (1252)**, provided it
  shed no blood and caused no mutilation or death; it was carried out by laymen, used to
  gain information (never to force recantation, since coerced conversion was deemed
  worthless), and is recorded only rarely.

## Penances and Punishment

Most convicts received traditional penances — fasts, pilgrimages, crusade service — or
were sentenced to wear **two large yellow crosses** (greatly feared for the social
ostracism it brought). Imprisonment ranged from the *murus largus* (cells around an
exercise yard) to the *murus strictus* (chained in unlit cells). Recanting *perfecti*
were well treated, sometimes even appointed inquisitors for their expertise; unrepentant
*perfecti* were handed to the secular arm and **burned**. The dead could be tried
posthumously and their bones exhumed and burned — Hamilton notes this was partly fiscal,
since the Inquisition (funded by lay rulers) had to avoid running a deficit and the
estates of the dead could be seized without regard to Catholic heirs.

## Significance and Reputation

Hamilton argues the Inquisition's "evil reputation" is, for the thirteenth century, **not
entirely deserved**. Had the inquisitors wished to massacre suspects they could have done
so easily — that was the tradition of the [[albigensian-crusade-1209|crusade]] leaders —
but mass executions were exceptional. At the height of activity, Bernard of Caux
sentenced over 600 offenders at Toulouse (1245–46) with none burned and only 23
imprisoned. The institution faced real resistance: riots at Narbonne (1234), expulsion
from Toulouse by consular pressure, suspension (1248–55) after complaints by Raymond
VII, and Frederick II's refusal to let it operate in his Italian lands. Its lasting
historical importance is as the **first permanent, professionalized machinery of
religious policing** in the Latin West, and the chief instrument — alongside the friars'
preaching — by which [[catharism|Catharism]] was extinguished.
