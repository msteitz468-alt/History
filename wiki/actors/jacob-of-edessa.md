# Jacob of Edessa

**Jacob of Edessa** (c. 640–708) was a Miaphysite bishop, polymath, and canonist — one of the most important West Syrian intellectuals of the early Islamic period. [[tannous-making-medieval-middle-east-2018|Tannous]] uses Jacob as the central witness to the gap between learned Orthodox ideals and the lived religion of [[simple-believers|simple believers]] under early Muslim rule.

## Career and learning

- As a young man studied in Alexandria and “amassed knowledge of the sciences” (Michael the Great).
- Translated Aristotle’s *Categories* into Syriac; revised Syriac OT and Severus’s Cathedral Homilies and Hymns; wrote the earliest surviving Syriac grammar, a Chronicle, biblical commentaries, letters, and liturgical works.
- Elevated to the episcopacy by Athanasius II of Balad; associated with the [[qenneshre|Qenneshre]] scholarly network (with George of the Arabs and others).
- **Not** a simple believer — paradigmatic learned Miaphysite confronting a church that would not keep canons.

## The canon crisis (~688)

After four years as bishop of [[edessa|Edessa]], Jacob banned clergy for uncanonical acts, then confronted Patriarch Julian of Antioch (likely resident at Qenneshre). When told to go along with the times, he resigned, burned the book of ecclesiastical canons before the patriarchal monastery door, and cried that the canons were “superfluous and useless” because trampled upon. He withdrew to Mar Jacob of Kayshum and wrote treatises against pastors and against transgressors of canons.

> "he brought forth the book of ecclesiastical canons and burned it with fire before the door of the patriarchal monastery, crying out and saying: ‘It is these canons which are trampled upon by you and not observed that I am burning like things which are superfluous and useless.’"
> — *vita* via Michael the Great, *Chronicle* 11.15 (Tannous Ch. 3)

## Theology of Christianity and law

Jacob rejected the view that correct belief alone makes one Christian. Perfect Christianity required Orthodox faith **and** keeping laws/good works. He defined Christianity as God’s covenant with humans — older than paganism, Judaism, and “barbarianism” — re-established through successive covenants culminating in Christ, with a seventh covenant at the end of the world. Laxity in his age meant that final covenant was approaching “in a frightening and forceful and intolerable manner.”

## Pastoral practice and the simple

In replies to the priest Addai (dozens of questions; seventy-one published by de Lagarde/Lamy), Jacob:

- Allowed memorials/Eucharists for deceased “heretical” parents only if they had been **simple people** holding heresy by custom, not active proponents.
- Practiced pastoral accommodation and limited disclosure (“pearls before swine”; “I don’t know”) for weaker brethren.
- Told John of Litarb that canons were futile because “no one observes the canons.”
- Allowed Christian priests to give saints’ blessings or *ḥnānā* to Muslims or pagans for healing — persuasion by cure rather than dispute.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Jacob, the documentary record of West Syrian canon law, chronology, grammar, and Aristotelian translation would be far thinner. Analytically, he is Tannous’s prime illustration of **elite disciplinary ideals colliding with lived multi-confessional society** after Chalcedon and under Islam.
