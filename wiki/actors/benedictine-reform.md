# The English Benedictine Reform

The tenth-century monastic reform movement led by three bishops — **Dunstan** (Canterbury),
**Æthelwold** (Winchester), and **Oswald** (Worcester/York) — that reshaped the English
church and was "deeply entwined with royal control." Treated by Catherine Cubitt in
[[sources/stafford-companion-early-middle-ages-britain-ireland-2009|Stafford's *Companion*]].

## Overview

The reformers drew from Bede's *Ecclesiastical History* "a powerful evocation of a church
run by saintly … Benedictine monasteries" — a vision Cubitt calls "deeply unhistorical" but
energetically pursued. Æthelwold "expelled the clergy from the cathedral community, Old
Minster, Winchester," replacing secular canons with monks — "a very unusual step." King
**Edgar** promulgated the **Regularis concordia** (c.970), a monastic customary drafted by
Dunstan and Æthelwold, placing all monasteries under royal lordship and convents under the
**queen's** oversight (see [[concepts/queenship-early-medieval|queenship]]).

## Structure and Limits

The movement was sustained by **continental** networks — Oswald studied at **Fleury**,
Dunstan spent two years at **Ghent** — yet "a large part of Fleury's tenth-century library …
came from England." John Blair has stressed its "limited geographical impact and …
concentration in royal and court circles": the northernmost reformed house was
Burton-on-Trent, and **no Benedictine communities** existed in [[places/northumbria|
Northumbria]] before the Conquest. After Edgar's death (975) ealdormen and thegns "claw[ed]
back lands … made over to the Benedictine reformers." Great foundations such as **Ely**
became ecclesiastical states and "tool[s] of royal control."

## Significance

The reform tied monasticism, sanctity (Æthelwold's cult of Swithun at Winchester), and
ideology to the [[actors/kingdom-of-wessex|West-Saxon]] construction of a "divinely
ordained, highly Christian pastoral kingship." After 1066 the English episcopate was
replaced by outsiders under **Lanfranc**, who introduced continental discipline and an
unprecedented claim to Canterbury's primacy over all Britain.
