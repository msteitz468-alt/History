# The Republic of Genoa

**Genoa** was, with **[[republic-of-venice|Venice]]** and Pisa, one of the three great
Italian **maritime republics** whose commerce drove the thirteenth-century
**[[commercial-revolution|commercial revolution]]**. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (John
H. Pryor, Ch. 14a) charts how Genoa, after a century of rivalry with Pisa and Venice,
emerged from the period with the most far-flung trading network of any — even as it failed,
unlike Venice, to achieve domestic political stability.

## A Trading Empire

Genoa's wealth rested overwhelmingly on its **merchant marine and entrepreneurial trade**,
not industry: its ships carried the commodities of the whole Mediterranean. Genoese notarial
cartularies (uniquely abundant) allow Pryor to track investment flowing to the **Holy Land**
(the largest single sector before 1256), the **Maghrib** (Tunis, Bougie, Ceuta — the conduit
for West African gold), **Egypt**, **Provence/Languedoc** (the gateway to the
[[commercial-revolution|Champagne fairs]]), and increasingly **Iberia**. The accumulation of
gold permitted Genoa and Florence to mint the first new gold coins of the era — the
**genovino** and the **florin** (1252) — Venice following with the **ducat** (1284).

## The Genoese–Venetian Wars

Unlike Venice's consolidated patrician republic, Genoa "began a desperate search for political
stability that neither it nor Pisa would ever attain," lurching between factions (Doria/Spinola
vs Fieschi/Grimaldi), foreign *podestà*, and popular captains (Guglielmo Boccanegra). Yet its
"tenacity" repeatedly saw it through:

- **The Treaty of Nymphaion (1261)** — Boccanegra's masterstroke: allying with **Michael VIII
  Palaiologos** of Nicaea against Venice just before the Greeks retook **Constantinople**,
  ending the Latin empire and Venetian dominance in Romania. Overnight the upper hand in the
  **Black Sea** passed to Genoa, which gained the colony of **Pera** at Galata.
- **The War of St Sabas (1256–1270)** — sparked by a dispute over a monastery between the
  Venetian and Genoese quarters in **Acre**; Genoa lost its Acre quarter to a Venetian-Pisan
  coalition, deflecting its trade toward Egypt and the Black Sea.
- **The battle of [[battle-of-meloria-1284|Meloria]] (1284)** — Genoa's annihilation of the
  Pisan fleet, "the final disaster for Pisa," removing it as a first-rate power.
- **The battle of Curzola (1298)** — Lamba Doria crushed the Venetian fleet in the Adriatic
  (Marco Polo's chronicler Rustichello was a fellow prisoner of war); but unlike Meloria it
  "did not alter the balance of power." The **Peace of Milan (1299)**, forced by Boniface VIII,
  held for fifty years.

## The Black Sea, the Atlantic, and the Far East

From the 1260s Genoa built **Black Sea colonies** (Caffa in the Crimea) and developed the trade
in grain, furs, timber and **slaves** (sold to the Mamluk armies of Egypt and to colonial
plantations) running from the Golden Horde's Sarai to Constantinople and the west. From **Ayas**
in Cilician Armenia, **Marco Polo** set out for China (1271); Genoese merchants reached Persia,
India and beyond. Around 1277 Genoese (and Majorcan) galleys first sailed out of **Gibraltar to
Flanders and England** — opening the **Atlantic sea route** that would erode the Alpine land
routes (see [[commercial-revolution]]). In 1291 the **Vivaldi brothers** of Genoa even attempted
to reach India by sailing down the African coast.

## Significance

Genoa exemplifies the **commercial expansion** that David Abulafia makes the keynote of the
thirteenth century — and the divergence in Italian political forms: where Venice built a
**stable patrician state** and Pisa was ruined at Meloria, Genoa combined commercial supremacy
with chronic civil strife, its empire carried by private enterprise more than public order. Its
networks reached from England to China; its gold florin/genovino, its Black Sea slave trade, and
its Atlantic galleys reshaped the medieval economy.
