# Antony of Egypt (St Antony the Great)

**Antony** (c. 251–356 CE) is, by tradition, the founding figure of **anchoritic (solitary)
Christian monasticism** — "the first monk." A Coptic-speaking Egyptian of the Nile Valley, he gave
away his property and withdrew into the desert, becoming the model of the ascetic life that
[[egyptian-monasticism|Egyptian monasticism]] gave to the whole Christian world.

## Role and significance

According to the tradition reported by Bagnall and Rathbone, Antony "began in an old tomb near his
village on the Nile, where he wrestled with demons and wild animals," before withdrawing ever
deeper into the wilderness — the archetypal pattern of the holy man fleeing the secular world,
pursued by disciples and admirers. His fame spread across the empire; even
[[constantine-the-great|Constantine]] is said to have written to him asking for his prayers.

Antony's lasting influence was secured less by his own (probably limited) writings than by the
**_Life of Antony_** written shortly after his death by **[[athanasius-of-alexandria|Athanasius]]**,
bishop of Alexandria. Translated into Latin, this spiritual biography "has inspired ascetics
everywhere" and became one of the most influential texts of late-antique Christianity — a direct
spur to monasticism in the West (it features in Augustine's conversion narrative).

## Counterfactual significance

Antony is best understood as the **emblem** rather than the sole inventor of the movement —
Egyptian Christianity, drawing on indigenous traditions of withdrawal and the desert landscape on
its doorstep, was generating ascetics across the late third century (the hermit **Paul of Thebes**
is a parallel figure). Without Antony specifically, anchoritic monasticism would likely have
emerged regardless; but without Athanasius's *Life*, its diffusion into Greek and Latin
Christianity would have been slower and less iconic.
