# Urukagina of Lagash

The last ruler of the [[lagash|Lagash]] dynasty (reigned c. 2350 BCE, ~8 years)
and **the earliest social reformer whose name history preserves**. His
inscriptions recording a program of reforms are among the most remarkable
documents of the third millennium. (His name is also read **Uruinimgina**.)
Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XIII, C. J. Gadd).

## The reforms

Urukagina came to power by violence (displacing Lugalanda) and in his second year
took the title **lugal**, in apparent disregard of Kish's supremacy. He then
issued inscriptions — embedded, characteristically, in building dedications —
exposing abuses and proclaiming a new order, framed as a **restoration of the
"good old ways"** ordained by the city-god Ningirsu. The measures:
- **Curbed a swollen officialdom** — the fiscal "inspectors" who had pervaded the
  land "right down to the sea" were abolished; boatmen, fishers, farmers, and
  herdsmen were freed from supervision and from paying dues in silver.
- **Restrained the priesthood** — limited the extortionate funeral fees (cut to
  less than half), and barred priests from invading "a poor man's garden."
- **Protected the weak from the powerful** — a great man wanting a humble
  neighbour's ass or house must pay "a just price in sound money" and not spite a
  refusal — evidence (per Gadd) of substantial private property at Lagash.
- **Reinstated the gods as owners** — returned to Ningirsu, Baba, and the divine
  family the estates and houses the governor's family had appropriated.

He proclaimed that he had "joined this covenant with Ningirsu that he would not
deliver up the weak and the widow to the powerful man." His reign is also when the
Sumerian word **ama-gi** ("freedom," literally "return to the mother") first
appears in the record.

## Counterfactual significance and interpretation

Urukagina's significance is twofold. First, the reforms prove an astonishing
**political maturity** at this early date — a ruler who not only abrogates abuses
but issues a public *manifesto* exposing them. Second, the texts are a rare window
onto **social tension** in a Sumerian city: scholars dispute his motives — genuine
social justice, a populist bid to secure the military classes' support for a
usurper, or a reassertion of temple-theocracy whose chief beneficiaries were the
priests. Gadd judges the underlying tendency "cannot be said to emerge clearly,"
though the governor himself was "the principal loser."

His program was cut short: within a few years Lagash was overwhelmed and sacked by
**Lugalzaggisi of Umma**. A defiant tablet found in the ruins blames Lugalzaggisi
("may his goddess make him carry his sin upon his neck") — a curse Gadd notes was
"dramatically fulfilled" when Lugalzaggisi in turn fell to
[[naram-sin|Sargon of Akkad]].
