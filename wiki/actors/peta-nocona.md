# Peta Nocona

**Peta Nocona** (also Peta Nokona; name “He Who Travels Alone and Returns”) was a mid-nineteenth-century Comanche war chief, husband of [[actors/parker-cynthia-ann|Cynthia Ann Parker]] (Nautdah), and father of [[actors/parker-quanah|Quanah Parker]] and at least two other children (Peanuts; Prairie Flower). He led deep raids into the Texas settlements and is central to the contested narrative of the [[events/battle-of-pease-river-1860|Pease River fight (18 December 1860)]], where Texas forces claimed to have killed him—a claim Quanah and later Comanche tradition denied.

## Overview

Associated with Nokoni / middle Comanche and later more northerly free-band spheres operating from canyon country and high plains of [[places/comancheria|Comanchería]]. Gwynne treats him as the warrior-husband who completed Cynthia Ann’s incorporation into Comanche life (he gave her the name Nautdah) and as the father whose reported death marks Quanah’s emergence into orphaned young manhood.

**Possible Fort Parker link (speculative):** Gwynne’s “strongest argument” for Cynthia Ann’s relatively humane later treatment is Peta’s presence at the 1836 raid—he “may well have led the raid” and been the rider Lucy Parker placed the screaming child on. Attribute as speculation.

**1840s–50s:** With Cynthia Ann among Penateka / Tennawish circuits, then refugee moves north (Red River headwaters; Elk Creek south of the Wichita Mountains) as southern bands collapsed under disease, Lamar-era war losses, and starvation. Father of Quanah (~1848 per Gwynne) and Peanuts; blackened Cynthia Ann’s face to hide her from Comancheros.

**1860 raiding:** Through summer–early fall 1860 he led devastating night raids under a “Comanche moon” into counties between present Fort Worth and Wichita Falls (including Parker County). Middleton T. Johnson’s ~300-man Ranger regiment failed to stop him—horses stampeded, men left afoot. October camp of up to ~500 near Mule Creek / Pease River.

## Death at Pease River — both positions

### Position A — Killed on the field (Ross / Texan victory narrative)

Sul Ross’s force hit a hunting/packing party on 18 December 1860. Ross/Martinez identification and Cynthia Ann’s wailing over the dead chief support that the dead man was Peta Nocona. Ross claimed mythic stakes—“The great Comanche confederacy was forever broken… their illustrious chief slept with his fathers”—which Gwynne calls “utter nonsense” as strategic assessment even while treating the identification of Peta as sound. Participant H. B. Rogers undercut the “battle” frame: “not a battle at all, but just a killing of squaws. One or two bucks and 16 squaws were killed.”

### Position B — Not present / not killed (Quanah / reputation management)

Quanah later “forcefully denied that he was even at the Battle of Pease River or that his father had been killed there.” Comanche tradition often holds that Peta survived longer. Gwynne adjudicates both of Quanah’s assertions as **untrue**, motivated by interest in cleansing a “terrible stain on Peta Nocona’s record”: Comanches saw Pease River as a fiasco and disgrace (women and pack camp hit while warriors were elsewhere or ineffective). At the 1910 Texas State Fair Quanah still denied the death—Gwynne: lying “to save his father’s reputation.”

### Wiki handling

**Record both. Do not silently choose.** When narrative needs a working line: “reported killed at Pease River (Ross); denied by Quanah (Gwynne sides with death and reputation motive).” Full fidelity to the slaughter of women with pack loads; do not upgrade a packing-camp killing into a peer set-piece battle without attribution.

## Historiography

Classic source conflict: ranger/Texan victory literature vs. son’s oral denial vs. modern popular adjudication (Gwynne). Name variants and band labels (Nokoni vs. later Quahadi association of the family) are soft. Death date cannot be stated as exact without flagging dispute.
