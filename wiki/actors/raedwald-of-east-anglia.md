## Overview

Rædwald was king of the East Angles and the fourth overlord (in Bede's list) of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms south of the Humber. His decisive intervention on behalf of the exiled Northumbrian prince Eadwine — defeating and killing [[æthelfrith-of-bernicia|Æthelfrith]] on the banks of the River Idle — marked a pivotal reorientation of political power among the Anglian peoples, shifting the locus of hegemony from the Bernician north to an East Anglian base and then, through Eadwine's subsequent dominance, to a northern Anglian one. Rædwald is conventionally associated with the famous ship-burial at Sutton Hoo, though Kirby regards this as probable but not certain.

## Origins and Dynasty

Bede regarded Rædwald as the grandson of Wuffa, "the reputed founder of the East Anglian dynasty of the Wuffingas" (HE II, 15). His emergence as an overlord "did not entirely shatter the Kentish hegemony which evidently continued to exist side by side with Raedwald's." One of his sons, Sigeberht, possessed a Merovingian name and fled into exile in Gaul; another, Eorpwald, succeeded him and was subsequently induced by Eadwine to accept Christianity before being killed by a pagan rival.

## Overlordship and Power Base

Rædwald's power base was contested by later scholars: "it could be that trade and coastal-based power were what gave Raedwald his paramountcy," given Sutton Hoo's location near trading settlements at Ipswich; alternatively "he could have been primarily a ruler of Angles astride the Icknield Way." He "almost certainly controlled the Middle Angles by the time he fought Aethelfrith on the Idle." His ascendancy "signalled the first recorded appearance of an overlord whose power-base was in the Anglian territories north of the Thames rather than in the Saxon or Jutish lands south of it."

Bede on one occasion refers to him simply as rex Anglorum, "king of the Angles," and Kirby notes that "it is likely that Raedwald was known to his contemporaries simply as rex Anglorum."

## Partial Conversion

Rædwald is a striking figure in the history of English Christianity. "In his maintenance of two altars, one a Christian, the other a pagan, [he] was at best only a partial convert," even after a period of Christian association likely connected to Frankish commercial and diplomatic influence through Kent. His sons Eorpwald and Sigeberht were still pagans at his death, Sigeberht converting only during his Gallic exile.

## Shelter for Eadwine and Battle on the Idle

When [[æthelfrith-of-bernicia|Æthelfrith]] sought Rædwald's complicity in the murder of the exiled [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]], offering large sums of money and threatening war, "Raedwald was on the point of complying with Aethelfrith's demands when his queen dissuaded him by arguing that to do so would be dishonourable; instead, therefore, he marched against Aethelfrith and slew him in battle on the east bank of the River Idle (HE II, 12)." The battle on the Idle "reflected not only serious rivalries among the northern Angles but on a wider stage it was part of a protracted struggle to determine the military and political leadership of the Anglian peoples." Victory "must have marked an important stage in the establishment of Raedwald's imperium" and probably enabled him to impose authority over the Mercians and as far north as the Humber, with Eadwine established initially in a dependent capacity.

## Death and Sutton Hoo

Rædwald probably died before c. 624 — Kirby argues he was almost certainly dead by the time Eadwine sought his second wife, since Eadwine looked to Eadbald of Kent rather than to the East Anglian court. The Merovingian coins in the Sutton Hoo ship-burial appeared to date no later than c. 625, though some could belong to the late 620s, complicating the association. Kirby notes "earlier uncertainties about the interpretation of the regalia have been compounded by recognition of the possibility that it may be only a local 'status-seeking' chieftain" whose goods were buried there.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Rædwald yielded to Æthelfrith's blandishments and killed Eadwine, Bernician dominance of the Anglian world would have been consolidated a generation earlier. Eadwine's rescue and Æthelfrith's defeat redirected the trajectory of Anglian political development, enabling the Deiran interlude of overlordship and the subsequent ecclesiastical transformation under [[synod-of-whitby-664|Paulinus and the Roman mission]].

## Historiography

Kirby notes the tension between sources: Bede's account of Rædwald as simply king of the East Angles versus the archaic usage "king of the Angles" in both Bede (HE II, 12) and the Whitby Life of Pope Gregory, which Kirby reads as preserving an older designation. The Whitby Life, written in the interests of the community around Abbess Aelfflaed (Eadwine's granddaughter), presents Æthelfrith as a tyrant and colours the narrative accordingly. The Sutton Hoo identification, while widely accepted, rests on coincidences of date and location rather than documentary proof.
