# Al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah

**Abu Tamim Ma'add al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah** ("the One who Strengthens the Religion of God," r. 953–975) was
the **fourth [[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] Imam-caliph**, under whom the dynasty leapt from a regional North
African power to a **Mediterranean empire based on the Nile**: he ordered the **[[fatimid-conquest-of-egypt-969|conquest
of Egypt (969)]]**, the founding of **[[cairo|Cairo]]** and **al-Azhar**, and the transfer of the caliphate east.
His 23-year reign is the pivot of the whole first Fatimid century.

## Consolidation in the west

Born in 931 and named heir on the deathbed of his father [[al-mansur-fatimid|al-Mansur]], al-Mu'izz first
secured North Africa by **alliance and pardon** rather than campaign, drawing hostile tribes into the fold. He
crushed [[berbers|Umayyad]] pressure on the far Maghrib: between **958 and 960** his general
**[[jawhar-al-siqilli|Jawhar]]** reconquered the western lands to the **Atlantic**, from which he sent live
ocean fish in clay pots to al-Mansuriyya as a token of the empire's reach. He rebuffed Umayyad overtures for an
anti-Byzantine alliance, reasserting his sole claim to the title Commander of the Faithful against the rival
caliphs of **Córdoba** and **Baghdad**.

## Doctrine, law, and the da'wa

Al-Mu'izz made the reign a high point of Ismaili intellectual consolidation. His chief judge and chief da'i
(*da'i al-du'at*), **[[qadi-al-numan|Qadi al-Nu'man]]**, produced under his direction the ***Da'a'im al-Islam***
("Pillars of Islam," 958–960) — the foundational **Fatimid legal code**, built on the traditions of Ja'far
al-Sadiq, opening with ***walaya*** (devotional allegiance to the Imam) as the first pillar and incorporating
the **"Ahd of Ali,"** a governance charter stressing justice, merit-based office, and the "satisfaction of the
common people." The weekly ***majalis al-hikma*** ("sessions of wisdom") at the palace instructed the Kutama
and the wider community; al-Mu'izz insisted they remain open to all, from shepherds to scholars.

Under him the **eastern da'wa** was largely reconciled to Fatimid authority: around **960** the great Khurasani
philosopher-da'i **Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani** and his followers accepted al-Mu'izz's imamate, healing much of
the sixty-year schism that had followed al-Mahdi's claim; allegiance came from Iran, Central Asia, and — by
**958**, with a further embassy in 965 — the prince of **Multan** in **Sind**, over 8,000 km away. He staged a
month-long realm-wide **circumcision festival** in 962 that doubled as a distribution of wealth.

## Sicily and Byzantium

The reign saw a decade of naval war (955–965) with the **Byzantine Empire** over Sicily and southern Italy,
waged through the **[[sicily|Kalbid]]** governors. After early losses at sea, the Kalbid commanders won the
**Battle of the Pit** (964) and the naval **Battle of the Straits** — where divers set fire to the Byzantine
fleet — forcing **Nicephorus Phocas** to fix the frontier and inaugurating the "golden age" of Muslim Sicily.
Byzantium's 960 seizure of **Crete**, however, went unreversed. When a Byzantine envoy came in 967, al-Mu'izz
told him future negotiations would take place **in Egypt** — signalling the coming conquest.

## Egypt and Cairo

With **Ikhshidid** Egypt collapsing amid famine and factional strife, al-Mu'izz spent over 20 million dinars
over two years preparing Jawhar's expedition, which took Egypt in **969** under a famous ***aman*** guaranteeing
the lives, property, and religious practice of Sunnis, Christians, and Jews alike. Jawhar founded **al-Qahira**
(Cairo) and the mosque of **al-Azhar** that same year. In **972** al-Mu'izz migrated the entire court, treasury,
and thousands of Kutama families east, handing the Maghrib to the **Zirid** viceroy **Buluggin b. Ziri**. He
took up residence in Cairo — renaming the new city **al-Qahira al-Mu'izziya** — and died there in **975**, his
son **Nizar** (al-Aziz) succeeding as the first Imam-caliph to reign from Egypt.

## Counterfactual significance

Al-Mu'izz is the maker of the Egyptian Fatimid empire. His patient consolidation, doctrinal codification, and
the deliberate, well-financed, tolerantly-governed conquest of Egypt turned a beleaguered Ifriqiyan caliphate
into the two-century Cairo-based power that founded the greatest city and university of the medieval Islamic
Mediterranean. Without the *Da'a'im* and the reconciliation of the eastern da'wa, Ismaili Shi'ism would lack
much of its enduring legal and intellectual architecture.
