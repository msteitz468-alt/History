# Mali Empire

## Overview

The **Mali Empire** (c. 1235–c. 1600) was the largest and richest of the medieval
**[[western-sudan-states|Western Sudanic]]** states, dominating the West African
savanna and the southern termini of the [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan trade]]
at its height in the fourteenth century. [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (David C. Conrad,
Ch 22) treats it as the central case in a chapter that reconstructs West African
state formation through an innovative combination of **oral tradition, archaeology,
epigraphy, linguistics, and external Arabic written sources**. Mali drew its power, as
Wagadu/[[ghana-empire|Ghana]] had before it, from controlling the flow of **gold,
salt, copper, and enslaved people** across the Sahara, and from imposing political
dominance over the trading centers and zones of mineral and agricultural production.

## Foundation and the Sunjata Tradition

Mali's origin is preserved above all in the **epic of Sunjata** (Sundiata Keita), the
Mande hero who, in the traditional account, defeated the Sosso king Sumanguru at the
**battle of Kirina** (c. 1235) and united the Manding (Mandinka) chiefdoms into an
empire. Conrad uses Mali to make the chapter's methodological argument: West African
**oral tradition**, transmitted across centuries by professional bards (*jeliw* /
griots), "rarely reveals facts... according to strict European historiographical
standards," yet conveys genuine information about the past and is the living framework
of Mande identity — to dismiss Sunjata as "imaginary history" is "to deny a
fundamental fact of an extraordinarily vibrant culture." The reverence for ancestors
in *kuma koro* ("ancient speech") has, Conrad argues, kept aspects of the past more
alive than the written word could.

## Mansa Musa and the Apogee

Mali reached its zenith under **[[mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]]** (r. c. 1312–1337), whose
spectacular **pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324** — distributing so much gold in Cairo that
he reportedly depressed its value for years — advertised Mali's wealth across the
Islamic world and placed the empire on European maps (the Catalan Atlas of 1375
depicts him enthroned with a gold nugget). Under Musa, **[[islam]]** became firmly
established among the ruling elite and the merchant towns; Timbuktu and Jenne grew
into centers of trade and Islamic learning, and Musa patronized mosque-building (the
Djinguereber mosque at Timbuktu). Islam in Mali, as across the
[[trans-saharan-trade|Sahel]], spread chiefly **by conversion through trade** rather
than conquest, and coexisted with indigenous religious practice.

## Structure and Decline

Mali was a composite empire of provinces and tributary chiefdoms under the *mansa*
(king of kings), funded by trade tolls, tribute, and royal control of the gold trade
(though the goldfields of Bambuk, Bure, and later Akan were worked by peoples outside
direct Malian rule). From the late fourteenth century succession disputes, Tuareg
pressure (Timbuktu lost c. 1433), and the rise of [[songhay-empire|Gao/Songhay]]
eroded the empire, which contracted to a western rump before fading around 1600.

## Significance

Mali demonstrates the volume's thesis that **state formation, monumental wealth, and
long-distance exchange arose independently across the world zones**: a major
gold-based empire integrated into the [[afro-eurasian-world-system|Afro-Eurasian
economy]] via the Sahara, known to the Mediterranean world as a fabulously rich
realm, yet reconstructable for historians only through the distinctive blend of oral,
archaeological, and Arabic-textual evidence that Conrad's chapter foregrounds.
