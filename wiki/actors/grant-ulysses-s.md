# Ulysses S. Grant

> **Detailed biography:** [[hubs/biographies/long-19th-century/grant-ulysses-s|Ulysses S. Grant — Detailed Biography (Biography Hub)]]

Ulysses S. Grant (1822–1885) was the commanding general of the Union armies in the final phase of the American Civil War and the 18th President of the United States (1869–1877). He accepted Robert E. Lee's surrender at Appomattox, devised and executed the coordinated strategy that defeated the Confederacy, and as president made the most consistent and forceful effort of any 19th-century American leader to protect the civil and political rights of the four million freed slaves during Reconstruction.

## Overview

Born Hiram Ulysses Grant in Point Pleasant, Ohio, the son of a tanner and businessman, Grant graduated from West Point (1843) near the middle of his class. He served with distinction in the Mexican-American War under Zachary Taylor and Winfield Scott but resigned from the army in 1854 after postings in remote garrisons. The following decade brought repeated business and farming failures in Missouri and Illinois. At the outbreak of the Civil War he was a clerk in his father's Galena leather goods store.

Re-entering the army as a colonel of Illinois volunteers, Grant rose with startling rapidity. Victories at Forts Henry and Donelson (1862) earned him the nickname "Unconditional Surrender" Grant and national attention. After the bloody draw at Shiloh, he endured vicious criticism yet retained Lincoln's confidence. His masterpiece was the 1862–63 Vicksburg campaign: a series of bold turning movements, a daring river crossing, living off the country, and a siege that captured an entire Confederate army. Promoted to lieutenant general and given command of all Union forces in 1864, Grant imposed simultaneous pressure across every theater, accepted high casualties in the Overland Campaign to keep Lee pinned, and coordinated with Sherman, Sheridan, and Thomas to destroy Confederate capacity to wage war. He accepted Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House on 9 April 1865.

As president, Grant confronted the violent backlash against black citizenship in the former Confederacy. He created the Justice Department, used federal power to crush the Ku Klux Klan in 1870–72, signed the 15th Amendment into effect, and supported the 1875 Civil Rights Act. Scandals in his administration (notably the Whiskey Ring) and the economic panic of 1873 damaged his reputation; historians long rated his presidency a failure. Recent scholarship, including Chernow's, has restored his standing as the president who did most between Lincoln and Lyndon Johnson to make the war's emancipatory promise real.

After leaving office Grant undertook a triumphant world tour, then was ruined by a Wall Street swindle (Grant & Ward). Diagnosed with terminal throat cancer in 1884, he raced to complete his *Personal Memoirs*—a literary masterpiece published by Mark Twain's firm—to secure his family's future. He died at Mount McGregor, New York, on 23 July 1885.

## Key Developments

- Prewar "lost years": repeated failure as farmer, real-estate agent, and store clerk; the experience of poverty and social humiliation shaped his later empathy and doggedness.
- Western theater generalship 1861–63: riverine campaigns, decentralized execution by trusted subordinates, and the strategic insight that the Mississippi was the key to splitting the Confederacy.
- Command of all armies 1864–65: rejection of single-battle decisiveness in favor of simultaneous advances, attrition of Lee's army rather than capture of Richmond, and willingness to trade casualties for position and time.
- Reconstruction presidency: creation of federal enforcement machinery, successful suppression of the Klan, and consistent (if not always successful) defense of black voting and office-holding against "Redeemer" violence.
- Personal character: laconic public persona masking a vivid private raconteur; solitary binge drinking that never demonstrably impaired official duties after early war incidents; intense loyalty to friends and staff combined with occasional political blindness.
- Final campaign: the composition of the *Memoirs* under excruciating pain as an act of will and love for his family; the book's critical and commercial success cemented his place in American letters.

## Causal and Consequence Notes

Grant's rise was enabled by Lincoln's patient patronage and by the Union need for a general willing to use the North's manpower and industrial superiority without romantic illusions. His Vicksburg and 1864–65 campaigns directly produced the Confederacy's military collapse. As president his enforcement actions contributed to the temporary flourishing of black political participation in the South (1868–1876); their later rollback after 1877 produced the Jim Crow system that lasted until the mid-20th century.

## Historiography

For generations after his death Grant was remembered primarily as the blunt instrument of Union victory and as the president of Gilded Age corruption. The "Lost Cause" school and early 20th-century historians minimized his generalship (contrasted with Lee's supposed brilliance) and ignored or condemned Reconstruction. Bruce Catton and Shelby Foote began the military rehabilitation. The modern scholarly turn—exemplified by Jean Edward Smith, H. W. Brands, and especially Ron Chernow—has emphasized Grant's strategic sophistication, his consistent (if imperfect) support for black rights, and the literary quality of his *Memoirs*. The drinking question has been reframed from moral failing to chronic disease with a recognizable binge pattern. Significant remaining debates concern the degree of Grant's personal responsibility for administrative scandals and the feasibility of any Reconstruction policy given white southern resistance and Northern political fatigue.

## Chernow 2017 Contribution

(Enriched post-ingest from [[sources/chernow-grant-2017]]. Full integration of 9 subagent ranges plus main-thread synthesis.)

Chernow's biography supplies the single most detailed and psychologically acute account in the collection of Grant's formation in failure, the precise mechanics of his campaigns (especially the information environment and delegation style at Vicksburg and in 1864), the pattern and management of his alcoholism, and the causal link between his wartime defense of the Union and his presidential defense of black citizenship.

Key grounded material:

- **Formation**: Birth 27 Apr 1822 Point Pleasant OH; Jesse Root Grant domineering and opinionated (“self-assertive windbag”); Hannah Simpson Grant reserved and non-demonstrative (“I never saw my mother cry”); Grant modeled himself on her self-control; horses as outlet for suppressed emotion (“Horses seem to understand Ulysses”); West Point middling (21/39); Mexican War service under Taylor/Scott with observations of logistics and command; 1854 resignation after drinking incident on Pacific coast; Hardscrabble farm failure; Galena leather clerkship; marriage to Julia Dent 1848 (love-at-first-sight proposal during storm); early temperance pledge.

- **Drinking pattern**: “a solitary binge drinker who would not touch a drop of alcohol, then succumb at three- or four-month intervals, usually on the road.” Rawlins served as explicit watchdog (“should General Grant... become a intemperate man... I will notify you immediately”). Grant largely sober in later war and presidency through willpower and Julia’s presence.

- **Military**: Forts Henry/Donelson (“No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender”); Shiloh survival and resilience (“Lick ’em tomorrow though” with Sherman under oak); Vicksburg as masterpiece (turning movements, living off country, “When this was effected I felt a degree of relief scarcely ever equalled”); Chattanooga coordination and spontaneous Missionary Ridge charge; 1864 simultaneous advances plan (“He was to go for Lee and I was to go for Joe Johnston”); Overland to Petersburg siege shift; Appomattox generous terms defended against Johnson (“If I had told him... Lee would never have surrendered”).

- **Presidency & Reconstruction**: “The imperishable story of Grant’s presidency was his campaign to crush the Ku Klux Klan.” Created Justice Department; Akerman prosecutions (thousands of indictments); habeas suspended in SC counties; Fifteenth Amendment enforcement; 1875 Civil Rights Act signed. “Grant was the single most important figure behind Reconstruction.” Douglass: “the vigilant, firm, impartial, and wise protector of my race.” Later regret over non-intervention in Mississippi 1875.

- **Character & flaws**: Extreme modesty (“I was bitterly opposed to the measure [Mexican War]...”); aversion to boast; loyalty to staff (Babcock defense until gold speculation betrayal revealed); naiveté in business (Grant & Ward Ponzi collapse; $150k Vanderbilt loan; house transfer in contrition).

- **Final act**: Cancer (epithelioma); quit smoking; race to complete *Memoirs* with Twain firm (70% net profits); finished 16 July 1885; died 23 July at Mount McGregor. Douglass at death: “In him the Negro found a protector, the Indian a friend, a vanquished foe a brother, an imperiled nation a savior.”
