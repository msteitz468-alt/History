# Louis Franchet d’Espèrey

French general (later Marshal). Commanded I Corps (Fifth Army) in the opening battles; appointed commander of Fifth Army on 4 September 1914 in place of Lanrezac. His energy and rapid arrangements with the British were decisive in making the Battle of the Marne possible.

## Narrative

As commander of I Corps (protecting Lanrezac’s right flank along the Meuse), Franchet d’Espèrey drove back elements of Hausen’s German Third Army attempting to cross at Dinant on 22–23 August. Later, during the battle of Guise (29 August), while the left and center of Fifth Army came under heavy pressure and Lanrezac halted the move toward St.-Quentin, d’Espèrey’s I Corps on the right launched a vigorous counterattack at 1700 hours toward the north. Assisted by III and X Corps, it drove the enemy back.

Joffre lost confidence in Lanrezac and relieved him on 4 September, appointing Franchet d’Espèrey in his place. The new army commander immediately demonstrated the qualities Joffre sought. On the same day Joffre telegraphed asking if Fifth Army could launch a counterattack “tomorrow or the day after tomorrow” with the British and Sixth Army against the First and Second German armies. Before responding, d’Espèrey met the British deputy chief of staff, General Henry Wilson (not Sir John French). They agreed an offensive could occur on 6 September, with Wilson insisting Sixth Army cover the British left and launch an energetic attack north of Meaux, and that Foch’s detachment participate. D’Espèrey informed Joffre of the results and the condition of his army: it could fight on the sixth but was “not in a brilliant situation; the three reserve divisions cannot be counted on.”

Joffre was “immensely relieved.” In his memoirs he wrote that the role of Franchet d’Espèrey on 4 September 1914 “merits being underlined in history: it is he who made the battle of the Marne possible.”

During the Marne battle, Fifth Army under d’Espèrey advanced 4–5 km on 6 September. With German II and IV corps gone (shifted north by Kluck), it advanced easily on 7 September until striking the enemy rear guard. On 8 September, with III and IX corps also gone from the German side, d’Espèrey’s forces continued forward. After striking the right of Bülow’s Second Army, French infantry fought across the Petit Morin River. Once Fifth Army crossed, Bülow had no choice but to pull back his right wing, pivot on his center, and refuse his right flank. This movement opened the way for the British crossing of the Marne at Château-Thierry (seized by French cavalry at 1430 hours on 9 September, with infantry following; British had seized the bridge early). By evening the French controlled Château-Thierry. Fifth Army thus played a significant role in expanding the gap between the German First and Second armies.

## Key Decisions and Consequences

- Vigorous I Corps counterattack at Guise (29 Aug) — slowed the German pursuit despite overall failure to halt the advance.
- Rapid meeting with Wilson and agreement on 6 September offensive details (4 Sep) — provided Joffre the assurance needed to finalize the attack order; bridged British hesitation.
- Execution of Fifth Army advance into the developing gap (6–9 Sep) — pressured Bülow’s right, forced his pivot and withdrawal, enabled full exploitation by British and French forces across the Marne.

**produced:** critical arrangements enabling Marne counteroffensive date and coordination; **contributed_to:** widening of the gap that compelled German retreat 9 September; **followed_by:** command of Fifth Army in subsequent operations.

## Historiography

Doughty singles out Franchet d’Espèrey’s performance on 4 September as pivotal. The account contrasts his energy and decisive action with Lanrezac’s challenges to orders and Sir John French’s caution.
