# Cleisthenes of Athens

**Cleisthenes**, of the aristocratic **Alcmaeonid** clan and grandson of the tyrant
Cleisthenes of Sicyon, was the architect of the **[[cleisthenic-reforms-508bce|reorganization
of the Athenian state]]** (508/7 BCE) that later Athenians remembered as the foundation of
their **democracy**. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 5, Martin Ostwald) treats
him — though noting how little contemporary evidence survives, and that "we know nothing
whatever about Cleisthenes' activities after the reforms, not even when or where he died."

## Career

Cleisthenes was no consistent democrat by background. He had served as **archon under the
[[pisistratidae|Pisistratid tyranny]]** in 525/4 (the archon-list fragment revealed this
previously unknown reconciliation), then fell out with the tyrants and led the Alcmaeonidae
into exile. He **brought them back in 511/10** to help the Spartans under
[[cleomenes-i|Cleomenes]] expel Hippias.

## The turn to the people

In the power-struggle that followed the tyranny's fall, Cleisthenes lost the first round —
his rival **Isagoras** was elected archon for 508/7. Defeated by conventional aristocratic
manoeuvre, Cleisthenes took **"the unprecedented step of seeking a power base in the common
people,"** taking the demos "into partnership" (Hdt. V.66) — a step Herodotus marks as
revolutionary, "for up until that time the Athenian commons had been spurned." Isagoras
answered by calling in Cleomenes, who expelled Cleisthenes and 700 families and tried to
dissolve the Council; but **Council and people rose**, besieged Cleomenes and Isagoras on
the Acropolis for two days, and forced their surrender. Cleisthenes and the exiles were
recalled, and his reform was enacted.

## Significance — counterfactual

Ostwald reads Cleisthenes not as a self-seeking dynast (he won no obvious personal
advantage; his own genos was split across three tribes) but as a statesman whose **positive
aim was to eliminate the dynastic rivalries** that bred tyranny — by shifting the political
substructure of Attica from **kinship to territory** and giving the demos the power to
validate every major decision. The system he created "continued to function essentially
unchanged for some three hundred years." Counterfactually, the durability and "mixing"
were his deliberate design: he entrusted the lottery-combination of the tribes to chance
precisely so that no one could charge him with manipulation, "lending greater durability to
the new order." Whether he also invented **[[ostracism]]** (used only from 488/7) is
disputed, but Ostwald finds him "the only serious candidate."

## Historiography

The reconstruction rests almost wholly on **Herodotus** (V.66–73) and **Aristotle's
Constitution of Athens** (which follows Herodotus and adds the only detailed description of
the constitutional measures), supplemented by inscriptions and the topography of the demes.
The central modern debate — self-seeking politician vs. far-sighted statesman — directly
governs how scholars date and interpret his laws, especially ostracism.
