# Zhuangzi (Master Zhuang)

With Laozi, one of the two great founders of **[[daoism|Daoism]]** (Zhuang Zhou, c. 369–286 BCE) — the
most brilliant literary and philosophical voice of classical Chinese mysticism, whose teaching can be
"summed up in a single word: **freedom**." His writings are now ingested as
**[[zhuangzi-watson|The Complete Works of Zhuangzi]]** (trans. Burton Watson).

## The Man and the Text

Almost nothing certain is known of Zhuang Zhou, said to have lived in **Meng** and to have refused high
office to keep his liberty (declining to be a "sacrificial ox," preferring to "drag his tail in the
mud"). The book bearing his name has **Inner Chapters** (1–7), generally accepted as his own and the
heart of his thought, and **Outer** and **Miscellaneous Chapters** by later hands of the Zhuangzi
tradition — so "Zhuangzi" denotes both a man and a current of mind.

## Thought

- **Freedom and spontaneity.** Against the **[[warring-states-period-475-bce|Warring States]]** scramble
  for power and the Confucian and Mohist projects to reform society, Zhuangzi's answer is to **free
  oneself from the world** — to live in accord with the **Dao** (the Way) by **spontaneity (*ziran*)** and
  **non-action (*wu wei*)**, like the skilled cook Ding whose blade glides through the ox by following
  its natural structure.
- **The relativity of all distinctions.** Right and wrong, big and small, useful and useless, life and
  death are **human perspectives**, not absolutes; the sage "**makes all things equal**" and roams beyond
  fixed categories. Hence his **skepticism about language and knowledge** — words cannot capture the Dao.
- **The dream of the butterfly.** Zhuangzi dreams he is a butterfly, then wakes unsure whether he is a
  man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man — the classic image of the
  fluidity of self and reality.
- **Acceptance of transformation**, including death, as part of the endless flux of the Dao.

## Significance

Zhuangzi gave Daoism its **mystical, playful, anti-authoritarian** voice and Chinese civilization one of
its supreme literary masterpieces. Where Confucianism shaped the public, official self, the Zhuangzi
nourished the **private, contemplative, artistic** life — profoundly shaping Chinese poetry, painting and
landscape sensibility, the reception of **Chan (Zen) Buddhism**, and the perennial counter-current to
social striving across East Asia.

## Text and Death-Passage (Major & Cook 2017)

[[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook (2017)]] confirm the layered text — **Inner Chapters**
possibly by Zhuang Zhou (c. 370–300 BCE), with **"primitivist," "syncretist," and "later follower"**
strata in the outer/miscellaneous chapters — and frame his teaching as a **radical relativism**: the
*qi* of the cosmos in ceaseless transformation, with death no loss. They quote his great image:
*"I regard Heaven and Earth as a great furnace, and the Creator as a master smith."* They also
**redate the *Laozi*/[[daoism|Daodejing]]** to the **fourth century BCE** (not a contemporary of
Confucius), noting Warring States **tomb versions** place the *De* section before the *Dao* — the
order reversed by a Han editor (see [[mawangdui|Mawangdui]]). **Source:**
[[major-cook-ancient-china-2017]].
