# Avignon Papacy (1309–1377)

## Overview

The **Avignon Papacy** is the nearly seven-decade residence of the bishops of Rome at **Avignon** on the Rhône (from Clement V, 1309, until Gregory XI's return to Rome in 1377). Logan stresses that the common label "Babylonian Captivity" is polemical and misleading: the popes were not prisoners, and the period saw major **curial, fiscal, and administrative development**. The return to Rome, however, precipitated the **[[great-schism-1378|Great Schism]]** (1378–1417).

## Role and Significance

Avignon made the papacy the most systematically organised absentee episcopal court in medieval Europe, raised enduring questions of French influence, and left a palace and bureaucracy whose legitimacy crises fed [[conciliarism]].


## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

### Not intentional permanent exile

From **1309 to 1376** the popes lived at Avignon on the Rhône; seven popes until **Gregory XI** returned to Rome, after which schism followed. Popes did **not** leave Rome intending a permanent Avignon base. After [[outrage-of-anagni-1303|Anagni]] and Boniface’s death, Benedict XI ruled under eight months; pro-French cardinals elected Gascon archbishop of Bordeaux as **Clement V** (1305–14), who never reached Rome, was crowned at Lyons at Philip IV’s wish, wandered southern France, and from **1309** stayed at a Dominican friary at Avignon (cancer), taking only provisional papers.

### Absenteeism contextualized; geography

Papal absence from Rome was not new (Urban IV, John XXI, Martin IV, Celestine V never in Rome; 1100–1304 popes away ~60% of time); Avignon’s novelty was prolonged single **non-Italian** residence. The seven Avignon popes remained **bishops of Rome** (vicar general in Rome), not bishops of Avignon. Avignon was **not in the French kingdom**—east bank of the Rhône in the **Comtat-Venaissin** (papal for a century); the city belonged to the counts of Provence—convenient for northern Europe and Italy, but not Rome.

### Why they stayed

Each pope avowed desire to return; **Italian anarchy**, failure of the Papal States as insulation, and fear of repeating Anagni justified delay. **John XXII** made Avignon the normal residence (bishop’s palace); **Benedict XII** built the thick-walled palace and brought papal archives from Italy; **Clement VI** bought the town from Provence—court clearly indefinite. Hundred Years War peacemaking/crusade also delayed return.

### Historiography: bury “Babylonian Captivity”

Petrarch branded Avignon modern Babylon (gold buys heaven; Christ sold); later Gregorovius saw French enslavement; Pastor blamed decline of fervor via a “French” papacy. French School at Rome research yields a more complex, able line. Logan: the phrase “**Babylonian Captivity**” deserves burial; the seven popes from Clement V to Gregory XI were a remarkably able line; three known for piety (Urban V later beatified); no personal scandals—yet the **perception** of French puppets (especially among Italians) still shaped the post-Gregory crisis.

Petrarch (Logan’s quote): “The one hope of salvation is gold… In the end, Christ is sold for gold… What rules is lust. What runs unabated is greed…”

### Clement V

Filled the college with French cardinals (including nephews). Yielded to Philip on Lyons crowning; allowed **posthumous heresy trial** of Boniface (from Feb 1309; lapsed 1311 for political reasons); in **1311** absolved **Nogaret** and praised Philip’s handling of Boniface as “praiseworthy and honest devotion for justice,” declaring king and men not guilty of malicious charges.

### Templars at Vienne (1312)

At the Council of Vienne (1311–12), under Philip’s pressure (armed presence; French prelates feared the king), Clement on **3 April 1312** suppressed the Knights Templar **without debate** of charges—spitting/urinating on the crucifix, rejecting Christ, homosexual behaviour, idol worship—after 1307 seizures and confessions under torture. Logan: a weak pope with colon cancer could not or would not oppose a bullying king; English eyewitness: virtually all prelates favored the Templars except the French.

### John XXII (1316–34)

After a two-year vacancy (Gascon/Italian/French deadlock; mobs “Death to the Italians”), Jacques Duèse (~70), former bishop of Avignon, was elected and reigned 18 years. Centralized administration; rebuilt finances: expanded **provisions**; prelates paid **common services** (~1/3 annual income); lower appointees **annates** (~first-year income); reorganized camera, chancery, judiciary, penitentiary. Famous 1324 great-niece wedding banquet excess contrasted with personal frugality and **Pignotte** alms (67,500 loaves/week average). Detained the Franciscan minister general over absolute poverty (1328).

### Benedict XII; Clement VI; return

Cistercian **Benedict XII** (1334–42): reformed orders, purged curia, abhorred nepotism; built the **Palais des Papes** and transferred archives—“entrenched the papacy on the banks of the Rhône.” **Clement VI** (1342–52): princely court—“my predecessors did not know how to be pope”—yet during the **1348 plague** stayed at Avignon (60,000+ reported dead), hired doctors, bought a cemetery, **welcomed fleeing Jews**. **Innocent VI** sent **Gil Albornoz** to pacify the Papal States. **Urban V** entered Rome 16 Oct 1367 (first pope in 60+ years), returned to Avignon 5 Sept 1370. **Gregory XI** left 13 Sept 1376, landed Rome 17 Jan 1377; died 27 March 1378—prelude to the [[great-schism-1378|Great Schism]]. Catherine of Siena urged return; Logan says she was **not determinative**.

Duke of Anjou’s warning to Gregory (Logan quote): return risks death and Italian control of the next election—the prophecy came true.

### Independence vs French sympathy; Papal States as prison

Popes not in French territory and often independent, but desire to please the French king was often present; Clement V subservient; John XXII and Philip VI friends; revenues steered toward French interests against Flemish/English. Logan: they became prisoners of the perceived need to control the **Papal States** as the basis of independence—a policy lasting until 1870.
