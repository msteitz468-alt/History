## Overview

Béla Kun was a Hungarian journalist radicalized as a Russian prisoner of war
in Moscow during 1917, who returned to Hungary to lead the Hungarian
Communist movement. When the Allied commander General Franchet d'Esperey
ordered Hungarian troops on March 20, 1919, to withdraw behind a line
Hungarians feared would become their permanent frontier, Count Mihály
Károlyi's fragile "October Republic" — which had gambled on Allied goodwill
toward a democratic Hungary and lost — collapsed. Kun took power that same
day amid strikes and hunger demonstrations in Budapest, with no viable
alternative to his left since the Hungarian Social Democrats had already been
compromised by participation in Károlyi's government.

Kun governed Budapest and the unoccupied Hungarian countryside for 133 days,
until his flight on August 1, 1919 — the longest-lived soviet government
outside Russia, and (per Paxton) the only soviet republic to exist "between
Munich and Moscow" (i.e., alongside the briefer [[munich-revolution-1918-1919]]
soviet in Bavaria).

## Role and Major Decisions

Kun attempted to establish socialism immediately, assisted by Marxist
philosopher and culture minister Georg Lukács and by Interior Minister Tibor
Szamuelly, whose "Red Terror" killed roughly fifty people (rumors inflated
this figure considerably in the eyes of the terrified Hungarian upper
classes). Kun's decisive failure was agrarian policy: less pragmatic than
Lenin — who officially "nationalized" Russian land while tolerating peasant
seizure of it — Kun nationalized the great Hungarian estates outright rather
than allowing small farmers to divide them, and paid for requisitioned crops
in inflated paper currency. Hungarian peasants responded with hoarding and
crop destruction, denying Kun the peasant support that had insulated Lenin's
regime from counterrevolution.

Kun also gambled, as Károlyi had gambled on the Allies, that Russian aid could
preserve Hungary's historic frontiers — a hope shared by some Hungarian
patriots who backed him for nationalist rather than socialist reasons. This
gamble failed identically: Lenin was absorbed by civil war and Allied
intervention at home and could offer nothing. The Allies, meanwhile, treated
Kun's Budapest regime as an unacceptable westward extension of Bolshevism,
believing (per Paxton) that it helped inspire the Bavarian soviet and
widespread strikes in Italy and France, and encouraged the Romanian army to
capture Budapest. Kun fled on August 1, 1919; power passed to the reactionary
former Austro-Hungarian navy commander Admiral Miklós Horthy, whose "White
Terror" killed roughly twice as many people as Szamuelly's Red Terror.

## Counterfactual Significance

Paxton's comparative analysis treats the Kun regime as a control case
alongside Russia and Germany: Hungary had the land-hungry peasant mass that
(per Paxton's causal model) was a near-precondition for revolutionary
success, but Kun's orthodox collectivization — refusing Lenin's pragmatic
toleration of peasant land seizure — squandered that advantage. Without Kun's
doctrinal rigidity on the land question, Paxton's framework implies a
Hungarian soviet might have achieved something closer to Bolshevik staying
power; instead it collapsed for lack of the rural support Lenin secured by
expedience over principle. The regime's international consequence was to
harden Allied anti-Bolshevik resolve during the Paris Peace Conference and to
deliver Hungary, discredited on both the moderate and revolutionary Left, to
two decades of Horthy's authoritarian regency.
