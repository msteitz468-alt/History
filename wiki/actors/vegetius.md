# Vegetius (Publius Flavius Vegetius Renatus)

The late Roman author of the **[[vegetius-epitome-military-science|*Epitoma rei militaris*]]** (*Epitome
of Military Science*), **the most influential military manual in Western history** — the standard
textbook of war from the Roman Empire through the Middle Ages and Renaissance to the dawn of the modern
era.

## The Man

Almost nothing is known of Vegetius beyond his book. He was a **Christian** of high rank (he styles
himself *vir illustris*) and **not a soldier** but a learned administrator and compiler. He wrote under
an emperor whose identity — and thus the work's **date** — is debated: the translator N. P. Milner
favours **Theodosius I (c. 383–395)**, others a date as late as Valentinian III (mid-fifth century).
His method was to **excerpt and synthesize the lost classics of Roman military writing** — Cato, Celsus,
Frontinus, Paternus, and the regulations (*constitutiones*) of Augustus, Trajan and Hadrian — into a
single handbook.

## The Argument

Vegetius wrote with a **reforming, antiquarian purpose**: convinced that Rome's military decline could
be reversed by **restoring the discipline, selection and training of the ancient legion**, he held up
the army of the early Empire as a model against the failings of his own day. His controlling claim —
"the Romans conquered all peoples solely because of their **military training**" (*disciplina*) — frames
the whole work.

## Significance

The *Epitoma* became, after antiquity, the **most copied secular Latin text** of the Middle Ages,
studied by kings and captains from Charlemagne's circle to the Renaissance; its maxims on training,
logistics, generalship and the avoidance of unnecessary battle shaped **medieval and early-modern
warfare** and were absorbed into **Machiavelli's** *Art of War*. The proverb *"si vis pacem, para
bellum"* derives from his text. Vegetius is thus the single great channel by which **classical military
science** reached later Europe.
