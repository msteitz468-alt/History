# Ebla

The great Early Bronze Age kingdom of inner Syria (Tell Mardikh, near Aleppo), whose
**Palace G archive** (~17,000 tablets and fragments, discovered 1974) revealed an
urbanised, literate Syrian polity contemporary with [[early-dynastic-sumer|Early Dynastic
Sumer]] and the rise of [[akkadian-empire|Akkad]]. Ebla is the type-site of what
[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] calls **Proto-Syrian culture** — a
"second urbanisation" that took a structurally different path from Mesopotamia.

## Overview

At its peak Ebla covered more than 50 hectares; the city itself held perhaps 15,000–20,000
people, the kingdom (with ~100 administered villages) barely 100,000. The Palace G archive
records 11,700 employees in the central administration plus a large seasonal (corvée)
workforce notionally organised into 16 "gates" of 2,000 "houses" each — totals (130,000–
150,000) Liverani reads as administrative fictions rather than real counts. The archive
covers only about **50 years**, dominated by the last kings Igrish-Halab, Irkab-Damu, and
the long-reigning Ishar-Damu.

## Distinctive structure (Liverani)

Because Syria's first urbanisation did **not** centre on dominant temple complexes,
[[temple-economy|temples]] never held the political-economic role they had in Mesopotamia
(nor would they throughout the Bronze Age). Ebla's temples were the gods' houses and foci
of *outdoor*, redistributive festival cult, not storehouse-workshop economic engines.
Political control was correspondingly less centralised and strongly **kin-based**:

- A king titled **en** (read in Eblaite as *malikum*, West Semitic "king") and a queen
  (*maliktum*), supported by a council of elders (*abba*) representing the most powerful
  families.
- A powerful administrative vizier (*lugal sa-za*) who effectively ran trade and the army.
  The vizier **Ibrium** served ~20 years and founded a parallel dynasty; early scholars,
  finding viziers far more often than kings in the administrative texts, mistook them for
  kings.
- A **ceremonial, personal** redistributive economy: palace food-rations for ~800
  attendants; land allotments as rare personal royal gifts rather than systemic grants.

Eblaite, the dominant **West Semitic** language of central/western Syria and western Upper
Mesopotamia, points to ethno-linguistic unity across the Proto-Syrian zone rather than
Eblaite political supremacy. Scribes trained in the Mesopotamian **"Kish tradition"** (via
Mari, with teachers from Kish); the archive's key innovation was **bilingual
Sumerian–Eblaite vocabularies**. The palace's **Audience Court** opened toward the city —
a more open layout than the fortress-like Mesopotamian palace.

## Trade and the Mari conflict

Ebla's wealth came from **craft and commerce**, not agriculture (Syrian rain-fed yields of
1:3–1:5, far below Mesopotamian irrigation; a Mediterranean vine/olive/fruit diet). The
palace employed an extraordinary 140–260 woodworkers and 460–600 metalworkers. Trade ran
through a network of fixed *karum*s ("ports") along the routes, each under an Eblaite
representative — a treaty with Abarsal regulated their use. The network reached **Egypt**
(vases with the cartouches of Khefren and Pepi I, kept as antique gifts; Byblos as
intermediary) and **Afghanistan** (lapis lazuli). Liverani frames the fundamental contrast
with Mesopotamia: Mesopotamia sought to *acquire raw materials it lacked*; Ebla sought
**profit from managing and re-exporting** through the network. The structural rivalry with
[[mari|Mari]] was geographic — Ebla controlled the wide western network, Mari the narrow
Middle-Euphrates chokepoint into Mesopotamia (a "funnel").

## Causal Analysis — who destroyed Palace G?

Liverani argues Palace G was most likely destroyed by a **recovering Mari**, not by
[[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon]]: Sargon's own inscriptions say he stopped at Tuttul, and
[[naram-sin|Naram-Sin]]'s later boast that destroying Ebla was *unprecedented* would have
been impossible had Sargon done it first. Either way, the brutal Akkadian intervention in
the Middle Euphrates (especially the destruction of Mari ~10 years later) triggered the
**crisis of the Syrian commercial network**, opening the way for nomadic
([[amorites|Amorite]]) groups whose rise eventually collapsed the whole system.

## Historiography

Ebla's 1974 discovery overturned the assumption that third-millennium Syria was a cultural
backwater dependent on Mesopotamia; the "debates on Ebla" (over readings, dating, and
sensational early claims of biblical links) became a cautionary case in Assyriology.
Liverani uses Ebla precisely to argue against the Mesopotamia-centric, temple-centred model
of early urbanism — Syria urbanised on its own kin-based, commercial terms.
