# The Byzantine Empire

## Overview

The **Byzantine Empire** is the **Eastern Roman Empire** that outlived its Western half
by nearly a thousand years, ruled from **[[byzantine-empire|Constantinople]]** (founded
by Constantine, 330 CE). The Byzantines called themselves **Rhomaioi** — Romans — and
their state was the continuous, unbroken Roman polity. In
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Pazdernik, Ch 14) Constantinople is the
historiographical **anchor of Late Antiquity**: the "witness" through which the
transformation — not the simple fall — of the Roman world is best seen, and the bridge
between antiquity and the medieval Mediterranean.

## Continuity of Rome in the East

Where the Latin West fragmented into Germanic kingdoms in the fifth century, the Greek
East — richer, more urban, more populous — held. It preserved Roman administration,
taxation, law, and a professional army, while becoming progressively more Greek in
language and more thoroughly Christian in identity. Constantinople, the great walled
city on the Bosporus astride the trade routes between the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] and
the Mediterranean, was the largest Christian city in the world.

## Justinian and the Second Silk Roads Era

**Justinian (r. 527–565 CE)** marks the apogee: the **Corpus Juris Civilis** that
codified Roman law for all later European jurisprudence; **Hagia Sophia**; and a costly
**reconquest** of Italy, Africa, and southern Spain that briefly restored Roman rule
over much of the Mediterranean — overstretch that, with plague (the Plague of Justinian,
541 CE) and Persian war, left the empire exhausted. In the seventh century the
**[[sasanian-empire|Sasanian]]** wars and then the **[[caliphate|Arab/Islamic conquests]]**
stripped away Syria, Egypt, and Africa, reducing Byzantium to an Anatolian-Balkan core —
yet it survived, anchoring the **second [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] era** alongside the
[[tang-dynasty|Tang]] and [[caliphate|Abbasids]].

## Survival by Adaptation (CWH V, Cheynet)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Jean-Claude Cheynet, Ch 21) carries Byzantium through the
whole [[middle-millennium|Middle Millennium]] as the **only European or Mediterranean
state formed in antiquity to survive almost the entire period** — and explains the
survival as a capacity for **repeated self-transformation**, despite a geographic
position "on the path of every people on the move from the steppes of Central Asia to
the deserts of Arabia." Cheynet's central corrective: against the image of timeless
stability projected by official discourse (and accepted by many later historians),
"the structures of the Byzantine Empire were regularly transformed to meet new
situations."

He identifies the empire's standing advantages — the **Roman legacy** of adaptive
government, an **influential religious hierarchy** (the Patriarch of Constantinople,
Hagia Sophia, and a missionary Orthodoxy that won the Bulgars and Rus' in the
9th–10th c.), a **well-defended capital**, and a relatively **stable fiscal system** —
and its three perennial challenges: defending continental frontiers, securing
legitimate succession, and balancing central authority against the autonomy of local
elites and diverse populations. The administrative response was the **theme system**
(provincial military districts under a *strategos*, later regrouped into frontier
duchies/catepanates), reorganized repeatedly as the empire contracted and reconquered.
Byzantium fell from the primary Mediterranean power around 1000 to the catastrophe of
the **Fourth Crusade (1204)** and a dwindling rump, before the Turks — first Seljuk,
then Ottoman — finally extinguished it at the fall of **Constantinople (1453)**, the
end of the Roman state and a marker of the period's close.

## Iconoclasm and the West (NCMH II)

[[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (McCormick, Ch. 14; Brown, Ch. 13) treats eighth- and
ninth-century Byzantium chiefly through its relations with the Latin West. The defining internal
struggle of the period was **[[byzantine-iconoclasm|iconoclasm]]** (c. 726–843), whose repudiation
of religious images alienated Byzantine Italy and the papacy, contributed to the loss of the
[[exarchate-of-ravenna|Exarchate of Ravenna]] (751), and provoked a distinctive Frankish reply (the
*Libri Carolini*). Iconoclasm, the **"two emperors" problem** created by
[[coronation-of-charlemagne-800|Charlemagne's imperial coronation (800)]], and the later **Photian
schism** together mark the deepening estrangement of Greek and Latin Christendom. On the Balkan
frontier Byzantium fought a long contest with the [[first-bulgarian-empire|Bulgar khanate]] —
catastrophically under **Krum** (who killed Nicephorus I in 811) — but won a lasting cultural victory
when [[conversion-of-boris-i-864|Boris's conversion (864)]] and the
[[cyrillo-methodian-mission|Cyrillo-Methodian mission]] drew the southern Slavs into the Orthodox sphere.

## The Macedonian Apogee (886–1025)

[[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Shepard) covers the high point of medieval Byzantium under the **[[macedonian-dynasty|Macedonian dynasty]]**. The long tenth century carried the empire from a defensive **equilibrium** (Leo VI's law-books and the *Book of the Eparch*; Constantine VII's scholar-emperorship and ceremonial, palace-based "Solomonic" rule) to an aggressive **expansion** under the soldier-emperors — **Nikephoros II Phokas** (reconquest of Crete 961, Antioch 969), **John I Tzimiskes**, and above all **[[basil-ii|Basil II "the Bulgar-Slayer"]]** (976–1025), who annexed **[[conquest-of-bulgaria-1018|Bulgaria (1018)]]** and pushed the frontiers to the Danube, Euphrates, and Caucasus. The era's great internal tension was the struggle of the central state against the **military aristocracy** (the *dynatoi* — Phokas, Skleros), and its great external success the drawing of **[[kievan-rus|Rus′]]** into the Orthodox world (the [[conversion-of-vladimir-988|conversion of 988]], sealed by the Varangian alliance). For Byzantium, Reuter notes, the long tenth century was "as much a golden age as an age of iron." This apogee was followed within fifty years by the eleventh-century crisis (Manzikert, 1071).

## Crisis and the Komnenian Restoration (1025–1180) (NCMH IV)

[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Michael Angold, Ch. 8; Paul Magdalino, Ch. 20)
charts the dramatic eleventh-century reversal and twelfth-century recovery. Within fifty
years of [[basil-ii|Basil II's]] death (1025), the dominant power of the Balkans and
Near East was "struggling for its existence": the army and frontier decayed amid the
factional struggle of the civil and military aristocracies, and after the defeat and
capture of Romanos IV at **[[battle-of-manzikert-1071|Manzikert (1071)]]** — and the
civil wars that followed — the **Seljuk Turks** settled the Anatolian heartland, the
**Pechenegs** the Danube, and the **Normans** swept away Byzantine Italy (Bari, 1071).
The empire was rescued by **[[alexios-i-komnenos|Alexios I Komnenos]]** (1081–1118), who
founded the **[[komnenian-restoration|Komnenian restoration]]** — a militarised,
family-based regime that beat off the Normans and Pechenegs, recovered western Anatolia
(exploiting the [[first-crusade-1095|First Crusade]] he had partly solicited), and was
carried to a twelfth-century apogee under John II and **Manuel I** (d. 1180). But the
recovery's dependence on Latin mercenaries and Italian fleets, and Manuel's overreach,
left the empire dangerously entangled with — and resented by — the West, the prelude to
[[fourth-crusade-1204|1204]].

## 1204, Exile, and the Restoration (NCMH V)

[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (David Jacoby, Michael Angold; Chs. 16a–b) treats the
**[[fourth-crusade-1204|Fourth Crusade's]] sack of Constantinople (1204)** as the empire's
"cosmic cataclysm" — the destruction of "the myth of Byzantine invulnerability." Byzantium
fragmented into the **[[latin-empire-of-constantinople|Latin empire]]** (the crusader–Venetian
partition) and three Greek **successor states in exile**: **Trebizond**, the despotate of
**Epiros**, and the **[[empire-of-nicaea|empire of Nicaea]]**. Nicaea, under the **Laskarids**
(Theodore I, John III Vatatzes), preserved Orthodox statehood and forged a virulently anti-Latin
**ideology of exile**, before **Michael VIII Palaiologos** recovered **Constantinople (1261)**
with [[republic-of-genoa|Genoese]] help (the Treaty of Nymphaion), founding the **Palaiologan**
dynasty. But the restored empire was "a husk": power had become localised, Anatolia was
alienated (soon lost to the Turks, including the nascent **Ottomans**), and Michael's
unpopular **Union of Lyons (1274)** — a desperate hedge against [[charles-of-anjou|Charles of
Anjou]], saved only by the [[sicilian-vespers-1282|Sicilian Vespers]] — left a legacy of
"schism, poverty and rapid decline."

## Significance

Byzantium preserved Greco-Roman law, learning, and statecraft through the European
early Middle Ages; transmitted **Orthodox Christianity** and literacy to the Slavs and
Rus'; held the eastern Mediterranean frontier against Persia and Islam; and embodied a
living continuity of the Roman state into the fifteenth century. It is the decisive
correction to any account that ends "Rome" in 476.

## Historiography

Long disparaged (Gibbon's "tedious" decline; "Byzantine" as a byword for intrigue),
Byzantium has been rehabilitated as a sophisticated, resilient, and culturally central
civilization. CWH IV's choice to narrate Late Antiquity *from Constantinople* is itself
a historiographical intervention against the Western, catastrophist
[[fall-of-rome-causes|fall-of-Rome]] framing. Debates: when "Roman" becomes "Byzantine"
(a modern, not a contemporary, distinction); the impact of the
[[late-antiquity|Justinianic plague]]; and continuity versus rupture across the Arab
conquests.
