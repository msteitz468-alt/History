# Saicho (767–822)

Saicho (posthumous name **Dengyo Daishi**) was the founder of the Japanese [[tendai|Tendai school]] of Buddhism and the builder of the monastery complex on [[mount-hiei|Mount Hiei]] (Enryakuji) that would become the most powerful religious institution in Heian Japan. He was "one of the most important figures in the history of Japanese Buddhism," a reformer whose insistence on an independent Mahayanist ordination system permanently altered the structure of Japanese religious life. Source: [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]].

## Early Life and Mount Hiei

Saicho was born in 767 in Furuchi-go (part of present-day Otsu city) in Omi Province. At eleven he entered the Omi Kokubunji under his master Gyohyo, provincial bishop of Omi. His full ordination (*jukai*, accepting the 250 precepts) took place at Todaiji in 785. "Immediately thereafter Saicho moved to Mount Hiei, where he devoted himself to meditation, worship, and especially the study of scripture. His abrupt move to Hiei was in keeping with the practice of many monks of the time, who sought to purify themselves and perhaps even acquire supernatural powers by undergoing austerities in the mountains. In Saicho's case, as the five vows he made at the time suggest, the move to Hiei reflected disenchantment with the corruption that was infecting the great monasteries of Nara."

Saicho probably first heard of Tendai doctrine from Gyohyo, who stood in a lineage tracing back to the Chinese monk Tao-hsuan, familiar with both Tendai and Zen traditions. After acquiring a set of major Tendai treatises on Hiei, Saicho gathered disciples — including Gishin and Encho, destined to be major successors. Official recognition came in 797 when he was named one of the ten imperial court chaplains (*naigubu ju zenji*), a position that entitled his small Hiei temple to an Omi provincial tax subsidy.

## The China Mission (804–805)

In 802 Emperor [[kanmu-emperor|Kammu]] ordered Tendai lectures at the Wake clan temple Takaosanji, hoping to provide a unifying doctrinal basis for the quarreling Nara schools. Saicho played the leading role. Kammu then expressed a desire to establish Tendai as a full school in Japan; Saicho responded that this required a formal mission to the Chinese T'ien-t'ai patriarchate. The mission was approved.

Saicho sailed with the Japanese embassy in 804, arriving at Ming-chou (present Ningbo). He visited the T'ien-t'ai mountains, met the patriarch Tao-sui, and received Tendai doctrine at its source. Unexpectedly he also encountered the Gozu (Ox Head) lineage of Zen through a monk named Hsiao-jan, receiving its transmission. On the eve of departure (third month, 805) he received from Tao-sui the *endonkai* — perfect and immediate Tendai precepts based on the fifty-eight bodhisattva precepts of the *Bommo-kyo*. A delayed sailing allowed him to visit Yueh-chou, where he received an esoteric initiation (*kanjo*) from a master named Shun-hsiao and collected 102 Mikkyo manuscripts. In total Saicho collected 222 manuscripts in China.

Returning to Japan in the sixth month of 805, he was immediately summoned to court. Kammu, whose health was failing, was more impressed by Saicho's newly acquired esoteric (*Mikkyo*) credentials than by his Tendai learning. By imperial decree an altar for esoteric initiations was constructed at Takaosanji; in the ninth month of 805 Saicho performed the first esoteric initiation rites ever held in Japan for eight Nara monks, then conducted an esoteric palace ritual for the ailing emperor.

## Formal Recognition and the Mikkyo Problem

In 806 Saicho petitioned Kammu to formally recognize Tendai as an independent school with its own quota of annual state-ordained novices. The government accepted: the new system allocated two novices annually to Tendai — one for classical Tendai doctrine and one for Mikkyo — embedding esoteric practice within the Japanese Tendai school from its inception. "Thus from its inception the Japanese Tendai school, unlike its Chinese parent, had Mikkyo as one of its major components."

With Kammu's death in 806, Saicho lost his primary imperial patron. The new emperor Heizei curtailed religious expenditures; no Tendai ordinations took place until 810. The need to provide Mikkyo training for one annual Tendai novice drove Saicho to turn to [[kukai|Kukai]], whose mastery of Mikkyo was without equal in Japan. Beginning in 809, Saicho wrote nearly thirty letters to Kukai — often signing them "your disciple Saicho," despite being Kukai's senior. Saicho himself received a *kechien kanjo* (elementary link-establishing initiation) from Kukai in 812. The relationship collapsed when Kukai refused in 814 to lend Saicho an esoteric manuscript, sharply rebuking him for trying to master Mikkyo through texts alone: "Truth could be transmitted only 'from mind to mind.'" The break became final in 816 when Saicho's disciple Taihan, sent to study Mikkyo with Kukai, refused to return to Hiei.

## Doctrinal Controversy: The Hosso Dispute

After severing relations with Kukai, Saicho embarked on a Kanto tour, proclaiming the Tendai *Hokekyo* (Lotus Sutra) doctrine of the One Vehicle (*ichijo*) — that all three traditional vehicles of Buddhism were merely expedient devices, while in reality every sentient being was capable of Buddhahood. This directly challenged the Hosso school's doctrine that sentient beings were divided into five categories, the lowest of which (*musho*) permanently lacked the Buddha-nature. The Hosso scholar Tokuitsu, active in Kanto, denounced Saicho's teaching; the two produced eight polemical works between them over five years.

## The Ordination Reform

Saicho's most radical and enduring contribution was his campaign for an independent Tendai Mahayanist ordination system. In 818 he formally renounced the 250 Shibunritsu precepts he had taken at Todaiji, labeling them Hinayanist. He submitted three successive sets of proposed bylaws (*shiki*) to the court:

1. **818**: Proposed that Tendai ordinands' names remain in the family register with the notation "son of the Buddha" (Busshi), eliminating dependence on the Nara-controlled clerical register (*soseki*); that tokudo and full ordination occur in the same year (preventing Ritsu school preceptors from controlling ordination); and that all Tendai monks serve an uninterrupted twelve-year training period on Hiei.

2. **818 (second set)**: Eight articles on Hiei administration; again met with silence from the Office of Hierarchs.

3. **819**: Explicitly titled "Bylaws for the Conversion of Annual Ordinands from Hinayana to Mahayana," asserting that Hiei would become the only truly Mahayanist monastery in Japan and that Tendai ordinations would be based on the fifty-eight bodhisattva precepts of the *Bommo-kyo*, not the Shibunritsu.

The proposal marked a historic break: "First, Saicho's insistence that Tendai monks live in an 'exclusively Mahayanist monastery' marked the emergence in Japan of the sectarian monastery, which subsequently became one of the hallmarks of Japanese Buddhism." Saicho also created an entirely new ordination ceremony unique to Tendai, replacing the traditional three-monk Shibunritsu procedure with one in which the Buddha and two bodhisattvas serve as preceptors.

The Nara hierarchs responded with a strenuous memorial to Emperor Saga denouncing Saicho's proposals. Saicho replied with his major polemic, the *Kenkairon* (Treatise on the Precepts, 820), refuting the hierarchs in fifty-eight articles. He also submitted the *Naisho Buppo sojo kechimyaku fu*, demonstrating his legitimate transmission of four lineages: Tendai proper, Zen, Bodhisattva Precepts, and Mikkyo.

## Death and Posthumous Victory

Saicho died on Hiei on the fourth day of the sixth month of 822 — without receiving permission to build a Tendai ordination hall. Seven days after his death, Emperor Saga — without consulting the hierarchs — authorized ordinations on Hiei. The following year the monastery received the imperial name **Enryakuji** (after Kammu's Enryaku era). In 827 the ordination hall (*kaidan'in*) was completed with a grant of 90,000 sheaves of rice. Saicho was granted the posthumous name Dengyo Daishi in 866.

## Significance

Saicho's reforms permanently altered Japanese Buddhism: the sectarian monastery, the Mahayanist (as opposed to Vinaya-based) ordination, and the integration of Mikkyo with classical Chinese Tendai doctrine all trace to his decisions. His twelve-year Hiei training requirement and his vision of monks as servants of both the Buddha and the state shaped the institutional character of Japanese Buddhism for centuries. The Pure Land movement that swept Heian Japan in the eleventh century grew directly from the Tendai contemplative practices Saicho's successors cultivated on his mountain.

## Historiography

The earliest reliable biography is the *Eizan Daishi den*, compiled by his disciple Ninchu. Modern critical studies include Paul Groner, *Saicho: The Establishment of the Japanese Tendai School* (1984), the standard Western account. The source for this entry is the *Cambridge History of Japan*, vol. 2, ch. 7, authored by Stanley Weinstein, which draws on the *Dengyo Daishi zenshu* and primary ordination records.
