## Role and Overview

Christian III (r. 1534–59) was the king who completed the Lutheran Reformation in Denmark and Norway, transforming both realms into territorial Lutheran state churches under princely control. He was "openly Lutheran" from his youth, having introduced the Reformation into his duchy of Schleswig-Holstein in 1528 (L1084). His reign is treated in the Cambridge History of Scandinavia Vol. II as the pivotal moment when the Reformation in Denmark shifted from a popular urban movement to an institutionalised royal church.

## Key Decisions and Consequences

**Victory in the Count's War (Grevens Fejde, 1534–36).** Christian III could only succeed his father Frederik I after fighting a civil war lasting three years. "His victory, following the surrender of København in July 1536, presented the young king with a number of religious and political opportunities which had not been open to his father" (L2769). The war's end left him far less dependent on the nobility and the rigsråd.

**Imprisonment of Catholic bishops (1536).** Christian proceeded to imprison the Catholic bishops, accusing them of causing the civil war. "All episcopal castles and estates were sequestrated by the crown" and lay councillors were forced to sign letters promising not to hinder "the preaching of 'the pure word of God'" (L2800–2804). The bishops were publicly accused before parliament in October; the two chief targets (Joachim Rønnow and Stygge Krumpen) were singled out not chiefly for doctrinal reasons but because they had obstructed the election of a new king in 1533 and sought to preserve episcopal independence from the crown (L2817–2822).

**Church Ordinance of 1537.** The parliament of autumn 1536 decided to replace Catholic bishops with "proper 'Christian bishops and superintendents'" and to introduce an evangelical church order. This was achieved the following year: "the Lutheran Church Ordinance, produced under Johannes Bugenhagen's supervision, received royal confirmation and Bugenhagen ordained the first seven Lutheran superintendents" (L2835–2836). The ordinance created a Lutheran church firmly under princely control; the Danish preachers' suggestion for an arch-superintendent semi-independent of the crown was not included (L3008–3010).

**Unification of Norway and Denmark.** Victory provided a context "for unifying the hitherto separate kingdoms of Norway and Denmark," effectively subordinating Norway further to rule from København (L2771–2774). "Norway was formally punished by abolishing her rights of self-government" because Christian II had used Norway as his base (L5408–5412). From 1537 the Norwegian council ceased to meet; the country was governed from København by Danish governors.

**Reopening the University of København (1537).** Christian III reopened the university, remodelling it on Wittenberg, though it remained under-resourced until his son Frederik II provided adequate funds in the 1570s (L3073–3078). By his death in 1559 nearly all major Danish towns had Latin schools (L3080).

**Education and pastoral implementation.** The new church concentrated on education and catechism. Peder Palladius, the leading Wittenberg-trained superintendent for Sjælland, aimed at gradual reform through teaching: "the egg had to jump up and teach the hen" (L3093). Visitations were conducted annually, synods held at multiple levels, and clergy regularly examined.

**Norway.** Christian III never personally visited Norway and found it difficult to appoint willing superintendents. Government instructions to the royal administrator in Bergen quietly allowed Catholic priests to continue rather than risk unrest (L3264–3267). The Norwegian Reformation lagged nearly two generations behind Denmark's (L3306–3308).

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Christian III's decisive military victory in the Count's War, the Reformation in Denmark and Norway would likely have been delayed or taken a more fragmented form akin to Sweden's prolonged ambiguity. His specific decision to bar an arch-superintendent and place the church fully under the crown shaped Scandinavian Lutheranism's caesaro-papist character for centuries. Had Frederik I lived longer or Christian II regained the throne, the outcome might have been a Reformed-Catholic compromise rather than a clear Lutheran church order.

## Historiography

Earlier historiography characterised the Danish Reformation as a straightforward "princely Reformation." More recent scholarship (O. P. Grell, cited extensively in the source) complicates this: "This princely reformation, however, was not simply a reformation from above. It depended on a popular and urban magisterial base on which to engraft itself" (L2837–2839). The impulse for urban, popular evangelicalism in Jylland and Malmö from the mid-1520s preceded Christian III's decisive actions by a decade. The source also notes that the political motivations for imprisoning the bishops were as much dynastic as religious (L2810–2822). Scholarly debate continues over the degree to which Norway was "annexed" versus "confirmed in a long-evolving subordination" — Norwegian historians still dispute the status of the 1537 transformation (L5418–5423).
