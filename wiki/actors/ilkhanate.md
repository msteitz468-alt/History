# The Ilkhanate (Mongol Persia)

The **Ilkhanate** was the southwestern successor state of the [[mongol-empire|Mongol
Empire]] — the realm of **Hülegü** (grandson of [[genghis-khan|Chinggis Khan]]) and his
descendants, ruling Iran, Iraq, the Caucasus, Azerbaijan, and parts of Anatolia from 1260
to 1335. Its name (*il-khan*, "subordinate khan") records its founders' nominal deference
to the Great Khan in the east; in practice it was an independent Persian empire, the
great rival of the **[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk Sultanate]]** and, per
[[may-mongol-conquests-2012|Timothy May]], one of the two poles (with the Yuan) of the
intensive scholarly and artistic exchange that defined the [[chinggis-exchange|Chinggis
Exchange]].

## History

Hülegü's campaign was the climax of the western [[mongol-conquests-13th-century|conquests]]:
he destroyed the Ismaili "Assassin" state and **sacked [[baghdad|Baghdad]] (1258)**,
ending the [[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] — an event that scandalized his cousin
**Berke** of the [[golden-horde|Golden Horde]], a Muslim convert, and helped ignite the
**Berke–Hülegü war** over Transcaucasia. The Mamluk victory at
**[[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ain Jalut]] (1260)** fixed the Ilkhanate's southwestern
frontier; with the exception of Ghazan's brief conquest of Syria (1299–1300), the Mongols
never held Syria or Egypt. Surrounded by enemies — the Golden Horde to the north, the
Chaghatayids to the east, the Mamluks to the southwest — with its sole ally (the Yuan)
reachable only by the **sea route around India**, the Ilkhanate nonetheless built, May
notes, "a rational state": minted currency, a centralized administration, and the *iqta*/
*timar* revenue-grant system for its still-nomadic army.

From Hülegü's 1262 letter to Louis IX onward, the Ilkhans pursued the
**[[mongol-latin-crusade-alliance|crusade alliance with Latin Christendom]]** against the
Mamluks — a half-century of embassies to the popes and the Western kings (including envoys
who underwent public baptism at Lyons in 1274, and Arghun's emissary Rabban Sauma) that
[[jackson-mongols-and-the-west|Jackson]] shows never produced effective joint war.
Ghazan's three Syrian campaigns (the victory near Homs and brief seizure of Damascus in
**1299–1300**; the 1303 disaster) were the closest it came; the Mamluks took the last
Crusader outpost, Acre, in **1291**.

Under **Ghazan Khan** (r. 1295–1304) the Ilkhanate became the **first Mongol state to
convert to Islam** — a turn that raised persecution of Christians and Buddhists, alarmed
the Mamluks (who forged letters claiming the conversion was a ruse), and was institutionalized
by Ghazan's great vizier and historian **[[rashid-al-din|Rashid al-Din]]**, author of the
*Jami' al-Tawarikh* ("Compendium of Chronicles"), the first true world history. The realm
reached peace and stability under its last effective ruler, **Abu Said** (r. 1316–35) —
but his death **without an heir (1335)** dissolved it into competing successor dynasties,
opening the way, after the [[black-death|plague]], for **[[timur|Timur's]]** conquest.

## A pole of the Chinggis Exchange

The Ilkhanate is, with the Yuan, the documented heart of the [[chinggis-exchange|Chinggis
Exchange]]. The two allied Mongol courts traded scholars, physicians, and astronomers
along the sea and land routes; **Rashid al-Din's** workshops at **Tabriz** fused Persian,
Chinese, Byzantine, central-Asian Buddhist, and Italian (Siennese) styles into what art
historians call a high point of Islamic painting, while Chinese cobalt-blue motifs and
Persian cobalt dye combined to make **blue-and-white porcelain**. Tabriz became a silk
and exchange hub linking China to Italy; Persian was confirmed as the language of court
and scholarship from Anatolia to India. May also notes the Ilkhanate's experiment with
**Chinese-style paper money** (1294), a failed but suggestive transfer of the printing
press westward.

## Significance

The Ilkhanate fixed the **Mongol stamp on Iran**: it ended the Abbasid order, accelerated
the **Persianization of high culture** across Anatolia and India, made Persia a Muslim
Mongol state, and produced — in Rashid al-Din — the era's supreme historiographical
monument. Its rivalry with the Mamluks defined Near Eastern geopolitics for two
generations, and its collapse left the vacuum Timur would fill.
