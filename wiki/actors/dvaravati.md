# Dvaravati

**Dvaravati** is among the most **evanescent** Early Classic cultural entities of Southeast Asia. The name means “Possessing Gates” in Sanskrit and evokes the mythic Indian kingdom of Kṛṣṇa/Viṣṇu; the formal name of the 1351 Thai kingdom was **Dvaravati Sri Ayutthaya**. In modern scholarship the term covers a sixth–ninth/tenth-century **Mon-Buddhist archaeological horizon** of moated settlements, sculpture, and coinage in the Chao Phraya basin and related zones of what is now Thailand (with related material on the Khorat Plateau). Primary synthesis: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]] Early Classic mainland chapter.

## Overview

Within [[seasian-classic-periodization|Miksic & Goh’s Early Classic]] (from 600 CE), the Dvaravati cultural complex “arose” as Funan disappeared, new Khmer centres formed, Pyu foci shifted, Srivijaya appeared, and central Java rose. Chinese and archaeological sources do not describe a single stable territorial empire. Most scholars now agree that a polity *called* Dvaravati “may only have existed for a few years. Its geographical extent will probably never be known. It may have been one site rather than an extended area.” The name is still used—hesitantly—for an **art style** and a **mandala/peer-polity network** of radiocarbon-dated sixth–ninth-century sites sharing pottery, tools, beads, sculpture, and brick monuments.

The name occurs in two Cambodian inscriptions (952 and 1002). The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang (596–664) placed Dvaravati between Isanapura (Cambodia) and Sri Ksetra (Myanmar). Three missions went to China in **638, 640, and 649**; Yijing mentioned it in the late seventh century. These are the **only Chinese references**; no Chinese seem to have visited the kingdom. Early twentieth-century assumptions of a large Mon-ruled kingdom over central Thailand and the Khorat Plateau are not confirmed.

## Settlement Hierarchy and Major Sites

More than **40** Early Classic sites lie between Ku Bua (north end of the Siamo-Malay Peninsula) and Nakhon Sawan. Settlement hierarchy (Miksic & Goh):

| Tier | Size | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primate | 559 ha | **Nakhon Pathom** |
| Second | 300–470 ha | **Si Thep** and others |
| Third | 85–200 ha | Ku Bua, **U Thong** (96 ha), Lopburi |
| Fourth | 5–85 ha | fourteen further sites |

In central Thailand’s Dvaravati period, **moated sites** were **10 of 11** largest settlements—unlike the Phimai/Mun-Chi basin, where enclosed sites were no larger on average than open habitation sites. Routes linked the basin via the Three Pagodas Pass, the Pa Sak northward, and Dong Si Maha Pho eastward toward Cambodia, Laos, and the Khorat.

**Nakhon Pathom**: rectangular moat 3700 × 2000 m; Chedi Phra Pathom (“First Stupa”) 127 m high (earliest written references 675 CE, first stage earlier); seal depicting an outrigger sailing ship; a Dvaravati-period shipwreck nearby; habitation yields bronze ornaments and archaic iron spear points of the eighth/ninth centuries.

**U Thong**: ~70 km inland from Nakhon Pathom; oval moat/ramparts 1690 × 840 m; Thai-British Expedition radiocarbon spans ~600 BCE–600 CE; shares [[oc-eo|Oc Eo]] traits (stone/glass beads, lead/tin ear ornaments, seals); nephrite double-headed pendant links Sa Huynh networks; 1966 jar of silver conch coins; seventh-century Sanskrit copper plate naming King Harsavarman, grandson of Isanavarman, offering to *linggas*; late Tang ceramics at Khok Chang Din (1997). A mid-fourteenth-century “prince of U Thong” (born 1314 in one tradition) later founded the royal centre that became [[ayutthaya|Ayutthaya]]—temporal succession, not direct institutional continuity.

**Phanom Surin** shipwreck (Samut Sakhon): >25 m exposed hull; Tang jars suggest ninth century; “torpedo jar” possibly western Indian/Near Eastern with dammar; local earthenware; rice, betel, ivory; ship thought built in India or the Near East and **resembles the [[belitung-shipwreck|Belitung]]** wreck of the same era.

## Religion, Languages, Art

Inscriptions appear in **four languages**: Pali, Sanskrit, Old Mon, and Old Khmer. Pali “citation” inscriptions occur on ceremonial objects; Sanskrit marks elite usage (*dvarapati*, “lord of Dvaravati,” at Nakhon Ratchasima); Old Mon is common in donations as far as Vientiane; Narai Cave near Lopburi is among the oldest Mon texts (sixth-century palaeography) and mentions Anuradhapura. Geographic pattern: Pali most common in central Thailand; Sanskrit in the hills between central and northeast; Old Mon/Mon-Khmer mainly Khorat west to Nakhon Pathom; Pali/Khmer mixtures only east of Prachinburi.

Peter Skilling prefers *Śrāvakayāna/Bodhisattvayāna* vocabulary over rigid Mahayana/Hinayana institutional labels. Widespread Pali suggests a **Theravadin ordination lineage** was more common, but some monks worshipped Avalokiteśvara—a continuum rather than a binary.

**Dharmacakra** (Wheel of the Law) stone sculptures, rare in India, are numerous in Thailand and Cambodia in the Early Classic. Robert Brown analyzed **42** wheels and placed most around **650–700 CE** (some to the tenth century), with concentration at Nakhon Pathom. Indorf argues stone wheels appear **before** classic Dvaravati from Oc Eo to Yarang over a longer span, possibly symbolizing royalty (Celestial Wheel + *abhiśeka*) as well as Buddhism; an Oc Eo wheel may date **500–600 CE**. End of the tradition is linked to Khmer expansion.

Twelfth–thirteenth-century Lamphun Buddhist sculptures later reflected **Dvaravati, Bagan, and Khmer** influences—showing Dvaravati’s afterlife as a style reservoir.

## Coinage and Economy

Dvaravati-attributed sites have yielded “the most extensive and varied coinage of ancient Southeast Asia” (mostly accidental finds): silver conch/*śrīvatsa* and rising sun/*śrīvatsa* types at U Thong, Nakhon Pathom, Lopburi, and elsewhere. **At least seven** silver medallions inscribed *Srīdvāravatīsvarapuṇya* (“the meritorious act of the lord of Dvaravati”) come from Nakhon Pathom, U Thong, Ban Khu Muang, and Chainat. A Chinese source claims people who made their own coins would have their hands cut off; coins also appear in lead/tin, with variable weight (weighed, not face-value currency). After the Early Classic, **no more coins** were made in the Dvaravati zone or elsewhere in Thailand until the fifteenth century; later Thais preferred cowries, and [[sukhothai|Sukhothai]] inscriptions present the king as sanctified centre of a redistributive network.

This **contrasts with** [[funan|Funan]]/[[oc-eo|Oc Eo]], where only **foreign** coins appear and local minting is unattested.

## Continuity and Decline

Many central and northeast Thai sites show long continuity: late Preclassic → Protoclassic → Dvaravati (Early Classic) → Lopburi (Middle Classic), with burial practices continuing and long-distance trade with India and the Vietnam coast flourishing. Clay objects with incised lines common on Dvaravati sites also appear at Ban Non Wat (occupation into the late first millennium CE); Ban Takhong and Ban Prasat yield Dvaravati-period pottery and urn/jar burial systems.

The archaeological complex **declined in the ninth and tenth centuries** as [[angkor|Angkor]] expanded; “probably the culture of the region changed as a result.” On the Khorat, Early Classic changes include cremation replacing inhumation and high-fired wheel-made pottery over a wider area (possible Dvaravati horizon), while the largest sites look ceremonial without clear administrative functions—political change becomes clearer only with Middle Classic Phimai dominance.

## Causal and Network Position

- **preceded_by** Protoclassic Chao Phraya and isthmian port cultures (U Thong, Chansen parallels with Oc Eo).
- **concurrent_with** [[pyu-city-states|Pyu]] cities, early Khmer polities, [[srivijaya|Srivijaya]], and the coin/motif interaction sphere (conch, rising sun, *śrīvatsa*) from Lower Myanmar through Thailand to Vietnam and Bengal.
- **contributed_to** later Mon-Buddhist and Thai royal ideologies (name reuse in Ayutthaya’s formal title).
- **followed_by** Angkorian political expansion and, much later, Tai kingdoms of Sukhothai and Ayutthaya—not simple lineal succession.

## Historiography

Bronson designated Dvaravati the first archaeologically detectable **state** in central Thailand; Miksic and Goh reject his long-distance trade evidence as inadequate for state formation (“two Chinese artifacts, one possibly Burmese artifact, several objects which might be from Oc-èo, and no fewer than eight metallic blackware bowls…”). Their preferred model: **no state-like political organizations** clearly discernible; instead a **heterarchy of mandalas** in continuous dialogue, with common aesthetic values broader than political units—see [[mandala]].

The Phrakhon Chai hoard (Buriram 1964, looted)—majority Maitreya and Avalokiteśvara, rice-chaff radiocarbon 330–650—links Dvaravati and pre-Angkor (including Angkor Borei) styles and may connect to a Canasa mandala on the plateau. Overall, Dvaravati is a cautionary case against equating art style, coin zone, Chinese ethnonym, and territorial kingdom.
