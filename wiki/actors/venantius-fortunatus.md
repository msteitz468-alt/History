# Venantius Fortunatus

**The Italian-born Latin poet who became the literary voice of the Merovingian elite, ending as bishop
of Poitiers.** Across several chapters of the Oxford Handbook, Venantius Fortunatus emerges as the central
figure of sixth-century Gallic literary culture — patronized by kings, bishops, and the convent of
Sainte-Croix, and "our best evidence of the importance of literary exchange to social positioning in the
later sixth century" (Reimitz, Ch. 22).

## Life and Patronage

Born in Italy, Fortunatus migrated to Gaul and "achieved a permanent place in the Merovingian upper
class," with members of [[gregory-of-tours|Gregory of Tours]]'s family "among his very first
supporters." He arrived at [[brunhild|Brunhild]] and Sigibert I's wedding at Metz (566) — an event
"carefully planned… and orchestrated by people from the court" (see [[merovingian-polity]]) — and was
later closely attached to **[[radegund-of-poitiers|Radegund]]** and Abbess Agnes at Poitiers, becoming
bishop of the city late in life (after 591). He was on good terms with both Gregory and Felix of Nantes,
praising the latter's "unmatched guardianship of *romanitas*."

## Works and Significance

Fortunatus left 11 *libri* — five prose letters and "more than sixty letters in verse," plus some fifty
epistolary poems to Radegund and Agnes (see [[merovingian-epistolary-culture]]). About a third of his
verse letters address secular figures, another third his patron Gregory, who "gave permission for [the
*Histories*] to be rewritten in verse" as a personal concession to him. He composed three Merovingian royal
epitaphs (Queen Theudechild; the princes Chlodobert and Dagobert, both d. 580; see
[[merovingian-epigraphy]]) and figural cross-poems (*Carmina* 2.4–2.5) whose bi-fold mandorla framing
reappears on the Niederdollendorf stele (see [[merovingian-christology]]).

His most influential legacy is devotional: his passion hymns — *Crux benedicta* and the lines that became
*Pange lingua* and *Vexilla regis* — dwell on the bleeding, suffering body of Christ, "designed to instill
pathos in listeners," and he coined the term *tortrix* for Radegund's self-mortification. Lynda Coon (Ch.
46) reads him as the poet who made "poetry… an excellent substitution for the male body, even that of the
human savior."

## Counterfactual Significance

Fortunatus is the hinge between late-antique Latin verse and the devotional culture of the early medieval
Church: without his Poitiers years, the literary articulation of Radegund's cult of the Holy Cross, and
the hymnody that shaped Western Passion piety, would lack their principal sixth-century author. He
exemplifies the Handbook's argument that Merovingian Gaul sustained, rather than abandoned, late-antique
learned culture.
