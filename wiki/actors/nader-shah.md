# Nader Shah

**Nader Shah** (born Tahmasp Qoli Khan of the Afshar tribe) was the soldier who
rebuilt Persian power after the Safavid collapse and founded the short-lived
**Afsharid dynasty**. His relentless land campaigns "from Iraq to India" earned him
the title **"Napoleon of Asia,"** but he was, in Peter Good's phrase, "limited by
his inability to rely on naval support" — a gap he tried to close by co-opting the
[[english-east-india-company|English East India Company]] as a navy for hire in the
Persian Gulf. His career is examined in
[[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]],
ch. 8 (Good).

## Rise to Power

After the **1722 Afghan conquest** shattered the [[safavid-empire|Safavid Empire]],
Persia became "the nexus of a complex struggle" involving the Ottomans, Russia,
pro-Safavid restorationists, and the Hotaki Afghans. Nader rose as the military
commander who restored Safavid control, ruling effectively as regent through the
puppet shahs **Tahmasp II** (r. 1729–32) and the infant **Abbas III** (r. 1732–36)
before having himself **proclaimed Shah on the Moghan Plain in 1736**, ending the
Safavid line.

## The Gulf Fleet and the EIC

Wanting fleets on both the Caspian (where he hired Captain John Elton of the Muscovy
Company) and the **Persian Gulf**, from 1734 Nader Shah set out to co-opt the EIC,
"which had access to the most powerful naval technology in the region." His method was
coercive leverage: he **voided EIC privileges** — including the 1,000 toman annual
customs share at [[english-east-india-company|Bandar Abbas]] (granted 1622) and
3,000 toman owed by the late Shah Sultan Hussein — and promised reinstatement in
exchange for naval services.

The EIC obliged across three modes — borrowing ships free, chartering them for pay,
and brokering outright sales. It blockaded an Arab port with the *Britannia* (1734),
sold the privately purchased *Cowan* to Persia for 8,000 toman (renamed *Fath-i
Shah*), and delivered the *Mary* and *Pembroke* with stores in 1742 for some £23,280.
After a Persian defeat at Basra (1735) the company supplied "500 maunds of tin, 2,000
round shot and 10 barrels of powder." When the Arab crews of the Persian fleet
mutinied and killed admiral Mir Ali Khan (August 1740), Nader Shah again needed
European ships to suppress his own navy.

Good's verdict reframes the volume's central thesis: "As with the Tokugawa and the
VOC, Nader Shah controlled the terms of the relationship with the EIC." Here was "an
Asian ruler [able] to co-opt European naval technology rather than be dominated by
it" — the EIC's "carte blanche" to issue passes and fire on non-compliant shipping
evaporated under a determined land empire. By his death in **1747**, Persia had
assembled a functioning Gulf fleet from EIC-brokered sales and captures.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the EIC's willingness to act as a navy for hire, "the Persians would have had
no basis for their attempt to assert control over the Garmsirat." Nader Shah is the
volume's strongest counter-example to European maritime exceptionalism: even at sea,
where companies were supposedly "powerful," a sufficiently coercive Asian ruler could
turn company technology to his own ends (see
[[european-exceptionalism-early-modern-asia]]).

## Historiography

Standard works include Lockhart, *The Navy of Nadir Shah* (1936); Floor, *The Iranian
Navy in the Gulf* (1987); and Michael Axworthy on Nader's naval expansion. Good draws
on EIC factory records at Bandar Abbas to recover the company's role.
