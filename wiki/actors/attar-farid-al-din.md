## Overview

Farid al-Din Muhammad 'Attar (c. 1145–c. 1221) of Nishapur is "the most honoured (apart from the Sufi Jalal al-Din Rumi, who learned much from him) of those poets who wrote in a wholeheartedly Sufi vein" (Hodgson, [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v2-1974]]). His work stands at the hinge between the courtly Persian poetry of the earlier Middle Period and the fully Sufi-saturated poetry of the thirteenth century. He was a practitioner of a drug-seller's trade (attar = perfumer/druggist), not a full-time Sufi institutionally, but most of his verse glorifies Sufi saints and expounds Sufi doctrine.

## Major Works

**Mantiq al-Tayr (Conference of the Birds / Speech of the Birds)**: 'Attar's most famous mathnawi poem, an allegory in which all the birds of the world set out to find the great mythical bird the Simorgh (whose dropped feather in China had set all the painters there to futile attempts at representation). They pass through seven valleys representing the stages of the mystical way, suffering enormous losses, until only thirty birds remain. At the destination, exhausted and reduced to nothing, they discover themselves and the Simorgh simultaneously in a giant mirror—for "Simorgh" (si morgh) means "thirty birds" in Persian. The pun, simultaneously trivial and profound, captures the Sufi teaching of annihilation (fana') in the divine: the sought object is the seeker, purified.

**Tadhkirat al-Awliya (Memorial of the Saints)**: A prose collection of anecdotes about all the great Sufi masters before 'Attar's time, organized by person. Hodgson notes it is "hopelessly uncritical, filled with marvels and unrealisms; but they are all warm and lively, and teach the lessons of Sufism more effectively than any treatise could do." The collection became one of the most popular works in Persian and was translated into many Islamicate languages.

**Ilahi-Namah (Book of God)** and other mathnawis: 'Attar composed several other frame-tale mathnawis on the pattern of the Mantiq al-Tayr, though attribution is complicated by the number of works wrongly ascribed to him. **Musibat-Namah** (Book of Affliction) is another major authenticated work. He also produced an extremely popular wisdom anthology, the *Pand-Namah* (Book of Advice), which circulated across the Islamicate world and was translated into many languages.

## Structural Innovation: The Frame-Tale Mathnawi

'Attar's most important formal contribution to Persian literature is the sustained use of the frame-tale structure in the mathnawi: a single allegory or narrative that incorporates numerous embedded stories as illustrations or digressions. This structure—which has roots in older Arabic and Persian narrative—became in 'Attar's hands a vehicle of unusual flexibility and depth. The embedded stories are not decorative but functional: each interrupts and illuminates the frame narrative, so that the whole forms a mosaic rather than a linear progression.

Rumi adopted and extended this structure in his own Mathnawi, which 'Attar's work directly anticipates in form, spirit, and many specific themes.

## Relationship to Rumi

The relationship between 'Attar and Rumi is the most important intergenerational link in the Sufi poetic tradition. Rumi is said to have met 'Attar as a child when his family passed through Nishapur fleeing the Mongols; 'Attar recognized the child's spiritual gifts and gave him a copy of his Asrar-Namah. Whether literally true or legendary, the story expresses a real intellectual debt: Rumi's Mathnawi is deeply indebted to 'Attar's formal innovations, Sufi themes, and many specific stories. The relation is one of profound creative inheritance.

## Death and the Mongol Conquest

'Attar is traditionally said to have been killed during the Mongol sack of Nishapur in 1221, making him one of the great cultural casualties of the Mongol invasion of Khurasan. The date is consistent with his known chronology and the destruction of Nishapur is well attested.
