# Malayu

**Malayu** (Melayu) was a Sumatran polity associated especially with the **Batang Hari / Jambi** region—sometimes peer, subordinate, or successor-focus within the world Chinese sources called **Sānfóqí** and modern historians link to [[srivijaya|Srivijaya]]. After the [[chola-invasion-srivijaya-1025|Chola raid of 1025]], Jambi/Muara Jambi likely became the political centre of the east Sumatran coastal mandala. In the Late Classic the centre moved upriver; [[singhasari|Singhasari]]’s **Pamalayu** (1275) imposed Javanese claims; **Adityavarman** later ruled a highland Buddhist realm. Primary synthesis: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]].

## Early Mentions and Relation to Srivijaya

Yijing visited Malayu en route to and from India; on his return Malayu was “now Srivijaya.” In his description Srivijaya comprised three *zhou* (regions): Barus, Malayu, and Mukha Asin. Malayu-Jambi and Srivijaya-Palembang were the two major southeast Sumatran centres. As Srivijaya’s commercial leadership eroded, Malayu-Jambi sent its **own** ambassadors to China in **852** and **871**. The name Jambi appeared in Chinese as **Zhanbei** in 840. Later, Karimun was a toll-gathering station for the kingdom of Malayu; Arab **Zabag** into the twelfth century probably referred to a polity governed from Jambi.

## Middle Classic Apex after 1025

After the Chola raid, the Batanghari in Jambi province was probably the political centre of the Sumatran coastal region and offshore islands Chinese called Sānfóqí. **Muara Jambi** is almost certainly the capital. No Sanfoqi missions came to China between **1028 and 1077**; after 1025 Jambi **supplanted Palembang** as overlord of an east Sumatran mandala (**caused_by** the shock of 1025 and subsequent jockeying—not automatic inheritance).

Wolters inferred that in **1078–79** Palembang and Jambi jostled for overlordship and may have sent a joint mission; by **1082** the struggle resolved in Jambi’s favour. Jambi missions: 1084, 1088, 1090, 1094, 1095. Further Sanfoqi missions: 1156, 1176, 1178. The *Lingwai Daida* ranked Sānfóqí the **third most prosperous** foreign country after the Arab lands and Java—then a ~200-year break before another recorded mission. In **1157** the ruler was elevated from “chief” to “king”; in 1178 a new ruler (son) obtained similar recognition and was told future missions should go only to Quanzhou, not the capital.

Edrisi (1154) wrote that Chinese frequented Zabag because of unrest in China and tyranny in India, finding just laws in a heavily populated, foreigner-frequented land. In 1178 Sanfoqi gave **189** pieces of glassware to the Song court—evidence of greater access to Near Eastern products than China itself enjoyed.

### Muara Jambi Archaeology

- **39** *candi* (brick temples) identified over **5 km** on the left bank (many more unexcavated).
- Water facilities (3 pools, 9 canals) cover **>1,000 ha**; King’s Pool **120 × 100 m**.
- Settlement hierarchy: Muara Jambi **1,100 ha** (top); second tier 50–132 ha (Lambur, Kota Kandis, Suak Kandis); third 12–28 ha; fourth thirteen sites <1 ha.
- Probably a **ceremonial and administrative** rather than primary economic centre; foreign commercial activity was spatially separated—implications for social structure.
- Local coins in ritual deposits (not proven for everyday exchange); none at Palembang; most Sumatran coins electrum/gold (high-value only).
- **Candi Gumpung**: most complete consecration-deposit set in Seasia study (gold/silver/bronze cups, gold plates, lotus flowers, semi-precious stones, crushed lead plates—not cremation ash).
- Makara at Solok Sipin dated **1064 CE** with the word *dharmmavira*; Prajñāpāramitā at Candi Gumpung similar to thirteenth-century east Java style.
- Possible that **Atīśa** spent his twelve Sumatran years in **Jambi** rather than Palembang—Middle Classic Buddhist remains are limited at Palembang (urbanization) but rich in Jambi.

## Late Classic: Inland Shift and Java

Early Late Classic Chinese still used Sanfoqi (“Three Vijayas”) for southeast Sumatra; Yuan sources are more precise (Malayu, Palembang). The overlord of the Sanfoqi mandala lived in the Batanghari valley of Jambi. Zhao Rugua described a rich meeting-place of ships that used violence if needed to force harbour calls, with ~15 vassals including Palembang (not the centre), Lamuri, and west Java; Tambralingga is listed but a **1230** Chaiya inscription suggests non-subordination.

**1275 Pamalayu** by Kertanagara of Singhasari imposed Javanese suzerainty. A peaceful-alliance claim is unlikely given the history of violence and Javanese sources. The capital moved inland to the **Dharmasraya / Padang Roco** area (~7 km stretch Rambahan–Padang Roco); Muara Jambi lowlands seem abandoned (no fourteenth-century Chinese porcelain), while a large Chinese colony occupied the old Palembang harbour.

Malayu sent ambassadors to China in **1280, 1293, 1299, 1301**; 1281 Yuan Muslim envoys; 1293 *History of Yuan*: small countries of Malayu complied by sending brothers/sons—unlike Kertanagara—**no evidence** an anti-Mongol alliance formed. Marco Polo (1292) called Malayu “very large and noble” as a spice centre (he did not visit).

Mid-fourteenth century: Java reasserted dominance (*Pararaton*, *Desawarnana*): principal Malayu lands Jambi and Palembang “equally executing orders”; Prapanca listed extensive Sumatran highland and coastal places as claimed subjects—highlighting highland economic importance (gold, silver, copper, mercury, ivory, incense).

## Adityavarman

**Adityavarman**: born in east Java, possibly of Sumatran mother; extensive inscriptions in Java and Sumatra; possibly the envoy Seng-jia-li-yeh of **1325**; **1343** Mañjuśrī at Candi Jago; from **1347** in the West Sumatra highlands for the rest of his life—may have been a [[majapahit|Majapahit]] viceroy acceptable via matrilineal Minangkabau structures. In 1347 he inscribed on Kertanagara’s Amoghapāśa; titles include “Lord of the Land of Gold”; last inscription **1375** (Kṣetrajña / Viśeṣa Dhāraṇī). **24** inscriptions in Tanah Datar, **17** at Pagarruyung. Attempted Javanese-style court **failed after 1375**—no further esoteric Buddhist statuary.

**Padang Roco Mahākāla** statue: **4.41 m**, **4 tons**—largest ever found in Indonesia (often misidentified as Bhairava); site had moat, Song–Ming ceramics, brick sanctuaries. Adityavarman later moved the ceremonial centre ~**120 km** north to Tanahdatar; Bukit Gombak elite ceramics of the fourteenth–fifteenth centuries (Chinese/Vietnamese/Thai); the king controlled **gold transport**, not extraction.

The *Nītisārasamuccaya* legal manuscript (possibly 1347–1374 for/in Kerinci) references *dipati* audience and Paduka Sri Maharaja Drammasraya of the “land of Palimbang”; offences range from theft of eggs (face rubbed with chicken dung) to debt and sexual crimes; implies **four social levels** (slaves, commoners, officials, royalty)—a complex agrarian society.

## Historiography

Malayu is reconstructed from Chinese tributary records, Arabic geography, Old Malay/Sanskrit inscriptions, Javanese court texts, and Muara Jambi archaeology. Key debates: when and how Jambi replaced Palembang; whether Sanfoqi always meant one overlord; Pamalayu as conquest vs alliance; Atīśa’s residence; success or failure of Adityavarman’s highland experiment. Wolters’s readings of mission lists remain foundational and contested. Source base: Miksic & Goh 2017 Middle and Late Classic chapters.
