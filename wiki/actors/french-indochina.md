## Overview

French Indochina (Indochine française) was the colonial federation comprising Cochinchina (colony), Annam (central Vietnam, protectorate), Tonkin (northern Vietnam, protectorate), Cambodia (protectorate), and Laos (protectorate). It was assembled incrementally between the seizure of Saigon (1859) and the Lao settlement (1893), driven more by prestige politics and the fear of British commercial rivalry than by any premeditated design. The federation existed until 1954 when the Geneva Accords ended the First Indochina War and created independent Vietnam (north and south), Cambodia, and Laos. See [[vietnam]], [[the-philippines]] (for comparative context on other colonial outcomes).

## Causal History

### The French Interest in Vietnam: Roots

French interest in Vietnam (Cochinchina) dated to the eighteenth century, driven by rivalry with Britain in India and China. Vergennes argued in the 1770s that France should pre-empt the English in Cochinchina to "intercept in time of war the English trade with China." The mission of Pigneau de Behaine, head of the Missions Étrangères, secured a treaty from Louis XVI's government in 1787 offering military aid to the Nguyen claimant Nguyen Anh in exchange for the cession of Danang. No official aid arrived due to the reluctance of the Pondicherry authorities and the onset of the French Revolution, but Pigneau secured volunteers. Nguyen Anh reunited Vietnam and became the Emperor **Gia-long** (r. 1802–19), founding the Nguyen dynasty.

### Nguyen Dynasty Isolationism and Missionary Conflict

Gia-long's successors — **Minh-mang** (r. 1820–40), **Thieu-tri** (r. 1841–47), and **Tu-duc** (r. 1848–83) — moved progressively toward isolation and persecution of Catholic missionaries, whose activity associated Christianity with civil war and foreign subversion. The Nguyen ideological framework, rooted in Confucianism modeled on the Chinese court (Vietnam was a Chinese tributory), left little conceptual space for accommodating European commercial or religious demands.

British diplomatic missions to Vietnam — Crawford (1822), Davis (1847), Bowring's indirect approach via Wade (1855) — all failed. Unlike Siam, Vietnam did not perceive in accommodation with the predominant power a viable path to survival. Tarling's chapter quotes a French officer who recognized "the existence of a national spirit among the Annamese, whom we have always thought ready to accept and indeed worship any master who would allow them to plant and harvest their rice" — an admission that the anticipated popular welcome did not materialize.

### French Seizure of Cochinchina, 1858–1867

Napoleon III's government, pressed by missionary lobbying and anxious to demonstrate French greatness overseas after its subordination by Britain in India and China, authorized a joint Franco-Spanish expedition. In August 1858 the force seized Danang; finding overland advance on Hue impossible, it pivoted south and captured Saigon (early 1859). Britain did not oppose the venture: commercial opportunities would be opened, and the French venture could be accepted "so long as it did not trench upon the independence of Siam and Laos, and thus upon the security of their interests in Burma, India and Malaya." John Bowring wrote: "It will tend to the extension of Trade and there is perhaps no locality where less mischief will be done as regards our interests."

The Nguyen dynasty, facing internal challenges in the north, compromised rather than fought to the end. The treaty of 1862 ceded three eastern provinces of Cochinchina. Local colonial initiative — not metropolitan design — drove acquisition of the western provinces in 1867. The dynasty disavowed the partisans who continued fighting: "the Emperor does not recognise us, but it is indeed our duty to carry on our struggle for the safeguarding of our fatherland."

### Cambodia (1863) and the Pattern of Protectorate

Cochinchina base in hand, the French extended influence to Cambodia, whose king accepted French protection in 1863 as preferable to either Siamese or Vietnamese domination. This was accepted by the British India Office: "so long as France did not move into Siam itself nor into Laos." By 1867 Siam ceded its claims over Cambodia (retaining the provinces of Battambang and Siemreap), and Britain accepted this adjustment. The Cambodian protectorate illustrated a pattern that would recur: France inherited Vietnamese dynastic claims over vassal states and transformed them into colonial protectorates, using the pre-existing suzerainty framework as a legal basis for expansion.

### The Move North: Tonkin and the Sino-French War, 1882–1885

The French colonial authorities in Saigon perceived two opportunities northward: (1) the Red River might provide the commercial route into Yunnan that the Mekong had not; (2) German activity in the region made it desirable to consolidate before another power arrived. Paris initially restrained them but then "removed its curb." In 1882 a small French expedition was sent to "deal with disorder obstructing Red River traffic." Further operations in 1883 secured lower Tonkin.

The Nguyen emperor appealed to China, which regarded Vietnam as a tributary and had sent Yunnan armies and tolerated the Black Flag irregular forces (Linh Co Den) operating against the French. The result was the Sino-French War (1884–5), which France won at sea and eventually on land. The war was domestically controversial: Premier Jules Ferry fell from power after a temporary French reverse at Langson. The Peace of Tientsin (1885) secured Chinese recognition of the French protectorate over Vietnam. Emperor Ham Nghi's flight from Hue and proclamation of the Cần Vương ("Save the King") movement in 1885 initiated a prolonged guerrilla resistance, especially in the north where scholar-gentry resistance (most famously that of Hoang Hoa Tham in Bac Giang Province) continued into the early twentieth century.

### Laos and the Siamese Crisis of 1893

The French advance into Tonkin positioned them to claim Laotian territories, which the Vietnamese dynasty had claimed as tributary. French envoy Pavie in Bangkok announced in March 1893 that France intended to assert its claim to all territory east of the Mekong. A naval demonstration at Bangkok in July compelled Siamese compliance. Britain urged Siam to accept French demands so as to prevent escalation, while itself seeking to restrain France. The French did not get Battambang and Siemreap (retained by Siam), but Siam renounced its claims over Laos east of the Mekong.

The Anglo-French agreement of 1896 formalized the buffer arrangement: each power agreed not to advance into the Menam valley without the other's consent. This effectively guaranteed the survival of Siam's core, while confirming French control over Laos. In 1907, further adjustments: France returned some Luang Prabang territory it had acquired; Siam ceded Siemreap and Battambang. By 1907 the territorial configuration of French Indochina was essentially complete.

## Governing Structure

**Cochinchina** was governed as a direct colony under French civilian administration from Saigon. It was the only part of Indochina where French citizenship rights extended to the indigenous population on the same basis as European settlers.

**Annam** (central Vietnam), **Tonkin** (northern Vietnam), **Cambodia**, and **Laos** were protectorates: nominally the local dynasty (Nguyen emperors in Vietnam, the Cambodian king) retained sovereignty; in practice the Résident général / Résident supérieur held executive authority. The Nguyen emperor remained as a figurehead in Hue. The system allowed France to claim it was not annexing Vietnam while exercising full control.

The federation was governed by a Governor-General at Hanoi (from 1902). The Union indochinoise (1887) created the formal federal structure, later modified in 1899 to include Laos. Tarling notes that unlike the British Resident system in Malaya, the French protectorate over Vietnam effectively chose a "figurehead Nguyen ruler" rather than abolishing the dynasty — a pragmatic choice that could not prevent prolonged resistance but provided a nominal continuity of sovereignty.

## Economic Base

Cochinchina rice exports (via Saigon) were the economic foundation; rice cultivation expanded dramatically in the Mekong delta under French-organized irrigation and land tenure. Rubber plantations developed in Cochinchina and Cambodia from the early twentieth century; coal mining in Tonkin; tin and other minerals. The colonial economy was structured to produce raw material exports, with processing industries kept in metropolitan France. Vietnamese peasants bore the burden through heavy taxation and corvée labor requirements.

## Mechanisms of Decline

French Indochina was not simply overthrown by external force as was Dutch Java. The Japanese occupied Indochina from 1940 but initially retained the French colonial administration. Only in March 1945 did Japan dismantle the French administration and proclaim Vietnamese independence under Emperor Bao Dai. The Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh proclaimed independence in September 1945. France's attempt to restore colonial authority led to the First Indochina War (1946–54), ending in the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu and the Geneva Accords.

## Counterfactual Significance

The Vietnam case is the clearest illustration of what Tarling characterizes as the missed option: "for the Vietnamese, the best chance was to come to terms with the major power [Britain] so as better to resist any other." Had Vietnam developed the kind of relationship with Britain that Siam achieved — trading commercial and extraterritorial concessions for a British guarantee of independence — the French might have found Vietnam as difficult to penetrate as they found Siam. The Nguyen emperors' Confucian isolationism, reinforced by fear of subversion and the association of foreign contacts with civil war, made this option effectively unthinkable to the court. The outcome was the most prolonged and damaging of any in Southeast Asia: French colonial rule from 1858, Japanese occupation 1940–5, First Indochina War 1946–54, Second Indochina War (Vietnam War) 1955–75.

## Historiography

French Indochina is among the most extensively studied of all Southeast Asian colonial systems, in both French and Vietnamese scholarship. The key debates concern: (1) the metropolitan vs. local-initiative question — Ferry's role was crucial but local officers in Saigon frequently drove expansion that Paris then ratified; (2) the nature and scale of Vietnamese resistance — the Cần Vương movement and subsequent guerrilla resistance have been extensively studied, particularly since Vietnamese independence; (3) colonial economic exploitation and the peasantry (Paul Mus, Charles Robequain, and subsequent scholars); (4) the missionary dimension — the Missions Étrangères' lobbying was a genuine driver of French policy, not merely a legitimizing rationale. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina covers this period moderately well via the CHSEA and related specialist monographs; primary Vietnamese sources are less well represented in the collection.
