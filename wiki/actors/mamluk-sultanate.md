# The Mamluk Sultanate

The **Mamluk Sultanate** was the slave-soldier regime that seized Egypt in 1250, halted the
**[[mongol-empire|Mongols]]** at **[[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ain Jalut]]** (1260), and
extinguished the crusader states. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Peter Edbury / the "rise
of the Mamluks" section, Ch. 16c) traces its emergence from Ayyubid disorder and its
consolidation under Baybars and Qalawun into the dominant power of the Near East.

## Origins: From Slave Soldiers to Sultans

The **Mamluks** were slave-soldiers — chiefly **Kipchak Turks** bought as boys from the south
Russian steppe (the **Genoese** of Caffa were leading slavers), trained and manumitted into
elite regiments. The Ayyubid **as-Salih Ayyub** built an unusually large, cohesive corps, the
**Bahri Mamluks**, garrisoned on an island in the Nile. They were decisive in defeating
**[[louis-ix-capetian|Louis IX's]]** crusade at **al-Mansura** (1250); when as-Salih died and
his son **Turanshah** threatened to sideline them, they **murdered him (1250)** — ending the
Ayyubid dynasty in Egypt and inaugurating "a decade of shifting alliances, plots, purges and
small wars."

## Ain Jalut and Baybars

The decade of instability was ended by the **Mongol** invasion: **Hülegü** sacked **Baghdad**
(1258, killing the last Abbasid caliph) and took Aleppo and Damascus (1260). When Hülegü
withdrew east (on Möngke's death), the sultan **Qutuz** confronted the Mongol rearguard and
won the **[[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|battle of Ain Jalut]]** (3 September 1260) — the first
major check to Mongol expansion. Qutuz was promptly assassinated by **Baybars** (the most
prominent Bahri), who took the throne.

**Baybars (1260–1277)** built the Mamluk state: he annexed Muslim Syria (Damascus, Aleppo,
Homs), revived the **Abbasid caliphate** as a puppet in Cairo (1261), gained the protectorate
of **Mecca and Medina**, and turned the **jihad** against the Christians — taking Caesarea,
Arsuf and Safed, **Antioch (1268)**, and Crac des Chevaliers (1271), while warring on the
**Mongol Il-khanate** along the Euphrates. **Qalawun (1279–1290)** won the bloody **battle of
Homs (1281)** against the Mongols, subdued Cilician Armenia, and took **Tripoli (1289)**.

## The Mamluk System

By the late thirteenth century a **centralised military government** had taken root: Mamluk
officers monopolised high command and the senior household offices, supervised the civilian
chancery and tax bureaux, and held land as military *iqtaʿ*; armies grew three- or fourfold
over the Ayyubids. Increased agricultural productivity (soldier-supervised irrigation) and the
emirs' own role as grain and sugar merchants funded the standing army. The regime was riven by
**factional violence** between Mamluk households, yet the *system* proved durable.

## Significance

The Mamluks achieved two things of world-historical weight: they **stopped the Mongol
conquest** of the Near East (Ain Jalut, Homs, Wadi al-Khazindar), holding the Euphrates
frontier where the Il-khans were repeatedly turned back; and they **eliminated the crusader
states**, culminating in the **[[fall-of-acre-1291|fall of Acre]]** (1291) under **al-Ashraf
Khalil**. The "patchwork map" of the early-thirteenth-century Muslim world was thereby greatly
simplified into the two great blocs of Mamluk Egypt-Syria and the Mongol Il-khanate.
