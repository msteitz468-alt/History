# Aramaeans

The Aramaeans were the Northwest-Semitic-speaking people who, in the aftermath of
the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]], spread across Syria and
Mesopotamia, founded a constellation of small Iron Age kingdoms, and — most
consequentially — gave their language, **Aramaic**, to the entire Near East as its
*lingua franca* for over a thousand years. They are treated in CAH II.2 (chapter
XXXIII, Albright).

## Origins and Emergence

Aramaean origins are, in Albright's words, "elusive." The original speakers of
Aramaic appear as nomads of mixed origin settling on the fringes of the Syrian
Desert in the later second millennium. Taking advantage of the collapse of the
[[hittites|Hittite]] and Egyptian empires and the weakening of
[[assyria-middle|Assyria]] after [[tukulti-ninurta-i|Tukulti-Ninurta I]], they
**invaded the settled lands en masse**, pushing west into Syria and east into the
valleys of the Euphrates and its tributaries, combining sheep-herding with
agriculture and — crucially — **camel-borne caravan trade**, whose adoption gave
them a decisive advantage over donkey caravaneers. The descendants of the older
**Amorite** population were largely absorbed into the Aramaean world.

The first contemporary record of them as a people comes from the annals of
**[[tiglath-pileser-i|Tiglath-pileser I]]** (1115–1077 BCE), who calls them
*Aḫlamû Armāya* ("Aramaean bedawin") and claims to have crossed the Euphrates after
them twenty-eight times — vivid testimony to a migration that Assyria could harry
but not stop.

## The Aramaean States

By the early Iron Age the Aramaeans had founded numerous principalities, in many
cases taking over the older [[hittites|Neo-Hittite]] centres of north Syria, which
became "Aramaized" even while keeping Hittite-Luwian royal names:

- **Aram-Zobah** — the strongest Syrian power of the late 11th century under
  **Hadadezer**, controlling eastern Syria from the Hauran to the Euphrates.
- **Aram-Damascus** — the dominant Aramaean kingdom from the 10th century (Rezon,
  then Ben-hadad / Hadadezer), the great rival of Israel and later of Assyria.
- **Bit-Adini, Bit-Bahiani (Gozan / Tell Halaf), Sam'al (Zincirli), Hamath,**
  and others across the Khabur and middle Euphrates.

In Mesopotamia the Aramaean tribes (and the related Chaldaeans of the far south)
occupied the East-Tigris and middle-Euphrates country between
[[assyria-middle|Assyria]] and [[second-isin-dynasty|Babylonia]], reducing both to
their weakest condition; an Aramaean, **Adad-apla-iddina**, even took the Babylonian
throne (c. 1062 BCE). The climax of Aramaean political power fell between about 1100
and 900 BCE in Mesopotamia, and in the 9th century in Syria.

## David and the Aramaeans

The volume links the Aramaean expansion to the wars of **[[david|David]]**, who
between about 990 and 980 BCE defeated **Hadadezer of Aram-Zobah** (fighting at the
Euphrates), placed garrisons in Damascus, and checked the Aramaean rise in Syria —
a check that lasted until the death of [[solomon|Solomon]], after which
Aram-Damascus regained independence and became Israel's chief northern adversary.

## Significance

- `produced:` **Aramaic** — the alphabet-borne language that displaced Akkadian and
  Phoenician as the common tongue of the Near East and remained so under the
  Assyrian, Babylonian, and Persian empires.
- `caused_by:` the migrations and power-vacuum of the
  [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]].
- `produced:` the constellation of Iron Age Aramaean kingdoms and (via the camel
  caravan) a transformation of overland trade in Syria and Arabia.
- `opposed_by:` [[tiglath-pileser-i|Tiglath-pileser I]] and later Assyrian kings,
  [[second-isin-dynasty|Babylon]], and [[hebrew-kingdom|Israel under David]].

## Historiography

Albright's account weaves together Assyrian annals, the early epigraphic Aramaic
inscriptions (Bar-hadad, Hazael, Zakir, the Sefire treaties), biblical genealogy and
narrative (read with his characteristic confidence as historical memory), and
linguistic analysis placing the cradle of standard Aramaic in eastern Syria /
Damascus. His treatment is dense and assured; later scholarship is more cautious
about the biblical and genealogical material and about his frequently idiosyncratic
high chronology, but the broad picture — Aramaean emergence from the collapse, the
camel-trade engine, and the triumph of Aramaic — has held. Aramaean Iron Age Syria
is an area where the collection is moderate.
