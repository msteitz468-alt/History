## Overview

Al-Numan III was the last king of the [[lakhmids|Lakhmid]] dynasty at
[[al-hira|al-Hira]], son of [[al-mundhir-iii-lakhmid|al-Mundhir III]]. His reign,
conventionally reconstructed as running from around the 580s to his fall in 602 (or, on
an alternative chronology, as late as 606), is unusually well documented for a
pre-Islamic Arab ruler because — per Philip Wood's argument, cited by Fisher — a body of
histories was composed during his own lifetime that went on to shape later Arab-Islamic
memory of al-Hira as a whole. His fall marks the end of Sasanian Persia's own
"super-phylarchate" system on its Arab frontier, the Persian mirror of the Roman
Ghassanid arrangement that had already collapsed with the 582 exile of
[[al-mundhir-ibn-al-harith|al-Mundhir ibn al-Harith]].

## Conversion to Christianity

Al-Numan III converted to Christianity and was baptized "in the fourth year of Khusrau
[II]," i.e. 594, according to the *Chronicle of Seert*, through the agency of Simeon b.
Jabir (bishop of al-Hira), Sabrisho (bishop of Lashom, later catholicos of the Church of
the East), and the monk Isozkha. His sons were also baptized; one, Hasan, later sided
with Khusrau II in the civil war against the usurping general Bahram.

A second, alternate conversion narrative survives in the *Life of Sabrisho* (also
preserved in the *Chronicle of Seert*): al-Numan is there described as devoted to the
cult of "Zhora"/Venus/al-Uzzai, with two already-Christian sisters, Hind and Mawiya
(Mavia), pressing him toward conversion; he was tormented by competing visions — a
handsome youth offering riches for conversion, and a deformed black "Ethiopian"
symbolizing the devil — until his conversion, and relapsed once before being "exorcised"
a second time by Sabrisho and Isozkha. A much later and more hostile Arab-Islamic
retelling, the *al-Manaqib al-Mazyadiya* (written under the Mazyadid ruler Sayf al-Dawla,
1086–1108, by Abu al-Baqa al-Hilli), portrays al-Numan as a hypocrite who ultimately
apostatized — echoing the Roman historian Evagrius' description of him as "a most
abominable and totally polluted heathen." The contradictory conversion traditions
illustrate how contested and retrospectively reworked al-Numan's religious identity
became in both Christian and later Islamic memory.

## Fall and Death

Al-Numan III's downfall and death are dated inconsistently across the surviving sources,
and Fisher treats the precision here as genuinely **disputed**. Al-Tabari's detailed
(but late) narrative holds that al-Numan imprisoned the poet Adi b. Zayd, a former
secretary to Khusrau I, who died in confinement; Adi's son Zayd sought revenge, and
al-Numan was in turn jailed and died "apparently of the plague." The Persian *Tarikh* of
Balami adds the more gruesome tradition that Khusrau had elephants crush him to death.

The *Khuzistan Chronicle* offers a different account: al-Numan refused to aid Khusrau II
when the latter fled to Roman territory during the civil war against Bahram, and refused
Khusrau's request for a daughter and a prized horse. Khusrau, restored to the throne with
the military help of the Roman emperor Maurice, "kept these things in his heart" and took
revenge — al-Numan was either poisoned or left to starve outside the palace gates. Khusrau
then installed a governor, al-Nakhirajan, over a Christian bureaucrat, Iyas b. Qabisah, at
al-Hira, rather than continuing Lakhmid dynastic rule.

James Howard-Johnston offers a structural, rather than personal, explanation: Khusrau's
westward strategic pivot after Maurice's murder in 602 made a single-family Arab client
system at al-Hira obsolete regardless of al-Numan's individual conduct — "the Lakhm could
not retain a leading role, given generations of antagonism between them and the Romans'
Arab clients." Peter Edwell instead favors an early date (602) for the fall, noting that
602 also marks Khusrau's own consolidation of power and the death of his patron Maurice.
With al-Numan III's fall, in Fisher's words, "the Persian Arab super-phylarchate vanished
into eternity." The **Battle of Dhu Qar**, near Kufa (precise date unknown but after
al-Numan's fall), in which disaffected Arabs defeated Khusrau's forces, followed shortly
after — described by Fisher as "a grim preview of what was to come," foreshadowing the
Islamic conquests.

## Memorial Tradition

The geographer Yaqut (d. 1229) records the tomb of al-Numan III at the monastery of
"Hind the Younger" at al-Hira, where he reportedly prayed beneath five hundred gold and
silver lamps. His tomb and that of his father al-Mundhir III became sites of what Fisher
calls "antiquarian tourism" for later Islamic-era visitors — part of the broader Abbasid-
era romanticization of al-Hira as a lost golden-age court.

## Counterfactual Significance

Fisher argues that had Sasanian Persia remained as weak and distracted as it was in the
decades before 602, al-Numan III's line might plausibly have followed the trajectory of
Clovis or Theoderic toward genuine independent kingship rather than extinction. Instead,
his fall — whichever precise date and mechanism is preferred — removed the last hereditary
buffer on Persia's Arabian frontier at almost the exact moment the peninsula's political
landscape was being reshaped by the emergence of Islam, and the resulting vacuum (visible
already at Dhu Qar) proved unrecoverable for Sasanian frontier policy.

## Historiography

Al-Numan III is the most historiographically significant Lakhmid ruler precisely because
contemporary or near-contemporary histories were composed during his own reign, later
absorbed into the Arab-Islamic historical tradition (al-Tabari, Balami, and eventually the
polemical *al-Manaqib al-Mazyadiya*). This produces an unusually rich but also unusually
contested record: the conversion narratives conflict with each other in tone and
mechanism, and the death narratives disagree on cause (plague, execution by elephant,
starvation, poison) as well as date (602 vs. as late as 606). Modern scholars split
between structural explanations (Howard-Johnston) and more source-driven, event-focused
reconstructions (Edwell) for the timing and meaning of the dynasty's end.
