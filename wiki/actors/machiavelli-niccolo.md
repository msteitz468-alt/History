# Niccolò Machiavelli

Niccolò Machiavelli was, in Lawrence Freedman's *[[sources/freedman-strategy-a-history-2013|Strategy: A History]]*, "a Florentine bureaucrat, diplomat, political adviser, and practical philosopher." His most famous book, *The Prince*, "was written as a handbook for rulers and asserted Machiavelli's own qualifications to serve as an adviser at a time of great turbulence and danger in Italian affairs." Freedman reads the "urgency in his prose" as reflecting "the desperation of his times and a fear of the political consequences of weakness for Florence in particular and Italy in general in the face of French and Spanish strength." Like Thucydides, "Machiavelli's exclusion from actual power gave him the time to write about how power might be exercised by others" — and a detached perspective on the gap between the ideal and "the less uplifting reality."

He also wrote on war directly. Machiavelli's *Art of War* "was the only book published during his lifetime" and may have been "the inspiration for the title given to Sun Tzu's work." He sought "a more enduring form of military capability, based on conscription"; the Florentine militia he helped establish "was defeated in battle with the Spanish at Prato in 1512."

Freedman places Machiavelli on Freedman's long line of cunning: he "came to be placed on the line of cunning and thus untrustworthy operators that began with Odysseus," and "'Machiavellian' came to describe anyone with a talent for manipulation and an inclination to deceit." Shakespeare's Richard III, who vows to "Deceive more slily than Ulysses could, / And, like a Sinon, take another Troy," is Freedman's emblem of the popular caricature — the "Machiavel" as near-Satanic ("old Nick").

## Strategic Thought

Freedman insists the caricature is a distortion: Machiavelli "is considered the father of political science" because "his method was empirical." He "did not consider himself to be offering a new morality but rather a reflection on contemporary practical morality. Political survival depended on an unsentimental realism rather than the pursuit of an illusory ideal." Yet "guile and cunning could not create their own political legacy: the foundation of states still lay in good laws and good armies."

On *fortuna* and *virtù*, Freedman quotes Machiavelli anticipating game theory's "minimax": "In the nature of things you can never try to escape one danger without encountering another; but prudence consists in knowing how to recognize the nature of the different dangers and in accepting the least bad as good." Fortune "governs one half of our actions, but even so she leaves the half more or less in our power to control" — and even there, "the character would be shaped by events."

On force versus guile, Freedman gives the lion and the fox: "One needed to be a fox 'in order to recognize traps, and a lion to frighten off wolves.'" Because "men are wretched creatures who could not keep their word to you, you need not keep your word to them" — yet one must not be caught: "One must know how to color one's actions and to be a great liar and deceiver. Men are so simple, and so much creatures of circumstance, that the deceiver will always find somebody ready to be deceived." From a negative reading of human nature — men are "ungrateful, fickle, liars and deceivers" — Freedman draws the famous conclusion: "it is far better to be feared than loved if you cannot be both."

On the conduct of war Freedman notes Machiavelli's insistence on clear, total victory — "making sure an enemy was clearly defeated even after it left the field of battle, so there was no chance to regroup" — his wariness of leaving too much to Fortuna, and his demand that "excellent captains need to be orators" to persuade the multitude.

## In Freedman's argument

Machiavelli is, for Freedman, the Early Modern hinge of the cunning-versus-force theme: he "went beyond tolerance for ruses and subterfuge in war to the heart of the conduct of all the affairs of state." But Freedman's reading is corrective. "Although Machiavellian has become synonymous with strategies based on deceit and manipulation, Machiavelli's approach was actually far more balanced." Cruelty should be used "once and for all," then abandoned; benefits "conferred gradually" because "they will taste better." Crucially, "the more the prince was perceived to rely on devious methods, the less likely it would be that they succeeded" — the wise strategist builds power "on real accomplishments and general respect," for "the best power was that which had to be exercised least." This places Machiavelli at the same self-limiting boundary of cunning that Freedman finds in Sun Tzu, and on the guile side of the polarity whose force pole is later claimed by [[clausewitz-carl-von|Clausewitz]].
