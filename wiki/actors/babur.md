# Babur

**Babur** (Zahir-ud-din Muhammad, 1483–1530) was the Central Asian prince who founded the
[[mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] in India with his victory at the **first battle of Panipat
(1526)** — a soldier, poet, and one of history's great memoirists. From the
[[cambridge-history-of-india-v4|Cambridge History of India, Vol. IV]].

## From Fergana to Kabul

Babur was a **Timurid** on his father's side (a descendant of Timur) and a Chingisid on his
mother's (descended from Genghis Khan) — a double inheritance he prized. Inheriting the small
kingdom of **Fergana** at eleven, he spent his youth in the losing struggle to hold and retake
**Samarkand**, Timur's capital, against the rising **Uzbeks** under Shaybani Khan. Driven out of
Central Asia, he seized **Kabul** in 1504 and made it his base, turning his ambitions toward India.

## The conquest of Hindustan

Invited by disaffected nobles against the brittle [[delhi-sultanate|Lodi sultanate]], Babur
invaded the Punjab and then crushed **Sultan Ibrahim Lodi** at the **first battle of Panipat
(1526)** — his smaller force winning through **field artillery and matchlocks** (deployed behind a
laager of carts, the "Ottoman device") and mobile cavalry, against the Lodi war-elephants. The
next year he defeated the [[rajputs|Rajput]] confederacy of **Rana Sanga at Khanwa (1527)**,
securing the new dominion. He died in 1530, having ruled his Indian conquests only four years and
leaving an insecure realm to his son [[mughal-empire|Humayun]].

## The Baburnama

Babur wrote, in Chaghatai Turkish, the **[[baburnama|Baburnama]]** (*Memoirs of Babur*) — a
candid, vivid, self-aware autobiography unmatched in medieval Islamic literature, ranging over
warfare, politics, friendship, wine, poetry, and acute natural-historical observation of the
lands and peoples he knew. In it Babur records his own faults, drinking and "repentance," his
griefs and friendships with rare honesty, alongside precise notes on the fruits, wines, animals,
and revenues of every country he passed through. His ambivalent verdict on his new dominion is
among the most quoted lines in Indian history: *"Hindūstān is a country of few charms…
there are no good horses, no good dogs, no grapes… no Hot-baths, no Colleges, no candles"* — yet
he balanced it against India's "masses of gold and silver" and vast population, which is why he
stayed. The [[baburnama]] (read here in **Annette Beveridge's** 1921 translation from the Turki)
is a major **primary source** for the founding of the empire.

## Significance

Babur's victory founded the dynasty that would, under his grandson [[akbar|Akbar]], become the
greatest state in early-modern India. Counterfactually, his Indian empire was a near-run thing —
nearly lost by Humayun to [[sher-shah-suri|Sher Shah]] within a decade — but his combination of
gunpowder tactics, Timurid legitimacy, and Persianate court culture set the template for the
[[mughal-empire|Mughal]] order.
