## Overview

Honda Masanobu (1538–1616), styled Sado-no-kami and nicknamed "Sadda Dono" by the English traders at Edo, was Tokugawa Ieyasu's closest confidential adviser and political strategist — one of the two "secretaries of a superior order" (along with his son Honda Masazumi) through whom Ieyasu conducted most of his administration. Sadler's chapter on the Hondas treats Masanobu as the exemplary Tokugawa retainer: a man who entirely suppressed his personal ambitions in service of his lord over thirty-five years.

## Background and Service

Masanobu came from a Mikawa samurai family that had served the Matsudaira (Tokugawa) house since his grandfather's generation. In his youth he attended Ieyasu, but during the Monto rebellion of 1563 he opposed his lord and had to leave, wandering for nearly two decades and eventually settling in Kaga as a leader of the Monto sect. During this period he deepened his Chinese studies, becoming the most learned of the Mikawa samurai. Matsunaga Hisahide's assessment: "I have seen a good many of the Tokugawa retainers, and they are practically all of the simple soldier type. But Masanobu is certainly something different. He's neither too strong nor too weak, neither is he insignificant."

Shortly before Nobunaga's death Ieyasu summoned him back, and he quickly became the chief confidential adviser. He received 20,000 koku when Ieyasu became lord of the eastern provinces, and refused all increases thereafter. The foreign traders at Edo — English, Dutch, and Spanish — independently noted his refusal to accept valuable presents, contrasting it with the mint-master Goto Shozaburo who "received anything gladly, and though his property was worth some six million in gold and silver always wanted more."

## Role in the Osaka Campaigns

Honda Masanobu played a decisive role in both Osaka campaigns. Before Sekigahara his advice on the handling of Ishida Mitsunari was critical; before Osaka his suggestion for "the filling up of the moats of the latter fortress" was the key diplomatic-military stratagem of the Winter Campaign. During the Summer Campaign he rode alongside the palanquin-borne Ieyasu in civilian clothes, and personally intervened when Shogun Hidetada attempted to throw himself into the fighting: "Honda Masanobu rode up and added his weight to that of the retainer" holding Hidetada's bridle. His illness during the Winter Campaign negotiations was used to delay Osaka's complaints about the moat-filling until the work was complete.

## Character and Governance Method

Sadler's account stresses Masanobu's oblique style: "In military discussions he was very laconic, giving his opinion in a word or two, and he was clever in allegory." The Hankampu records that Ieyasu addressed him informally ("Sado! Do this!") while addressing the son Masazumi with more ceremony — a reversal of expected hierarchy that Sadler notes without fully explaining.

He was lamed by a wound in youth, so took no active military role, but was always at Ieyasu's side in the field. The planning of Edo's city layout, gates, moats, and escarpments was drawn up by Ieyasu and Masanobu in concert.

## Fall of the Honda Family

Masanobu died in 1616, reputedly disfigured by a disease (described as "the Chinese pox") that rotted one side of his face — which his enemy Okubo Hikozaemon attributed to divine punishment for his role in the downfall of Okubo Tadachika, whose father had sheltered Masanobu in his impoverished years. Masanobu's son Masazumi was later deprived of his fief and exiled to Dewa. Sadler suggests the real cause of both falls was suspicion of incomplete loyalty to the Bakufu's designs — the standard Tokugawa mechanism by which "high office has always been in Japan a dangerous responsibility, for the individual is not considered but only the system."

## Significance

Honda Masanobu represents the institutional type of the indispensable adviser who amplifies the power of a strong lord while remaining invisible in official records. His partnership with Ieyasu — described by Tokutomi as: "For thirty-five years Honda lived for and slaved for Ieyasu. They had no secrets from each other, and so closely did the secretary keep his master's confidences that the devil himself would have been put to it to find anything out" — was a template for later Tokugawa bureaucratic governance. His learning placed the Tokugawa regime in contact with Chinese classical scholarship at the moment of institutional founding, helping shape the [[tokugawa-intellectual-life|Tokugawa adoption of Neo-Confucianism]] as official philosophy.
