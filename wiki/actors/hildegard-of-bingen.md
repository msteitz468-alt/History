# Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179)

> **Disambiguation:** Not to be confused with [[hildegard|Hildegard, Queen of the Franks]] (d. 783), wife of [[charlemagne|Charlemagne]]. Logan’s twelfth-century profile is the Rhenish visionary *magistra*.

## Overview

**Hildegard of Bingen** was the most celebrated female visionary of the twelfth-century Latin church: *magistra* of a women’s community at **Rupertsberg** near Bingen, author of *Scivias*, correspondent of popes and princes, and occasional public preacher. Logan pairs her with [[peter-abelard|Abelard]] and [[thomas-becket|Becket]] as one of three non-royal, non-papal lives that give the twelfth-century church a personal dimension.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

### Status and formation

Strictly speaking she was neither **abbess** nor **canonized saint** in her lifetime: head of a community styled *magistra* (mistress); medieval canonization attempts failed obscurely, though a local German cult of “St Hildegard” survives with feast **17 September**. Born **1098** at Bermersheim near Mainz into minor nobility, she joined the anchoress **Jutta** at the male Benedictine house of **St Disibod (Disibodenberg)** around age eight, professed c.1112, and succeeded Jutta as head of the nuns in **1136**.

### *Scivias* and papal endorsement

In the preface to *Scivias* she dates a decisive vision to **1141** (age 42 years, 7 months): heavens opened, brilliant light, instantaneous grasp of the *meanings* (not grammar) of scripture; a heavenly voice ordered her to write; illness until she confided in the monk-secretary **Volmar**. Over roughly ten years she described **26 visions** in Latin; the title shortens *Scito vias* (“Know the Ways”). The abbot of St Disibod and the archbishop of Mainz approved the work; **Pope Eugenius III** read a portion of the unfinished *Scivias* aloud to the fathers of the **Council of Trier (Nov. 1147–Feb. 1148)**; **Bernard of Clairvaux** urged Eugenius to encourage her; the work was finished within three years and celebrity followed.

### Rupertsberg, Ricardis, and community practice

Claiming divine command to leave overcrowded Disibodenberg, she moved in **1150** with some twenty nuns to **Rupertsberg** overlooking the Rhine–Nahe confluence near Bingen, against abbatial opposition (shared fame; commingled endowments). Volmar became provost for spiritual care; she remained *magistra*. Favourite nun **Ricardis of Stade** (assistant on *Scivias*) was appointed abbess of Bassum; Hildegard’s failed interventions and grief-laden letter raise, Logan notes, questions of human emotion the historian cannot fully answer. She defended feast-day dress of unbound hair, white silk veils, and golden crowns as visionary authority for virgins before the High Priest, and admitted **only noble women**, analogizing class mixing to a farmer penning cattle with asses and goats.

### Public correspondence and preaching

More than **350 letters** survive (ordered by correspondent rank). She admonished **Anastasius IV**, **Hadrian IV**, **Frederick Barbarossa** (1164, for anti-pope support: “like a little boy, like one that has lost his mind”), **Henry II**, and **Eleanor of Aquitaine** (“You have not found rest”), as well as ordinary nuns and laity. Up to **four preaching expeditions** took her to monasteries and German cathedrals; at **Cologne** she blamed clerical negligence for [[catharism|Cathar]] success; at **Trier (Pentecost 1160)** she upbraided lax bishops and priests. Her self-understanding was **prophetic** (inspired speaker of God’s word), not fortune-telling: “I am but a poor little one … but these are the words I have heard.”

### Final interdict and death

In **1178** she allowed burial of an excommunicated local nobleman; Mainz canons demanded exhumation under pain of **interdict**; she refused (vision; alleged deathbed reconciliation). After Cologne’s intervention and witnesses to reconciliation the interdict was lifted. She died **17 September 1179** at Rupertsberg, aged about eighty; her body now rests at Rüdesheim opposite Bingen.

### Authorship and the migraine hypothesis

Logan treats *Scivias* and the correspondence as securely hers; the *Liber divinorum operum* raises more questions; a medical book he sets aside as questionable; seventy-plus sacred music pieces may reflect supervision rather than sole authorship. Even letters + *Scivias* alone, he holds, secure her place. To **Guibert of Gembloux** she described visions from infancy, with eyes open, fully awake, no trance, a light she named “**Shadow of the Living Light**.” Logan canvasses divine vision, fabrication (unlikely), or natural experience received as divine; classical migraine (scintillating scotomata, fortification spectra) fits, and Oliver Sacks judged the visions “indisputably migrainous.” A neuro-psychological explanation, Logan insists, **need not diminish the content** of *Scivias* as Christian cosmology from Fall to Last Judgment.
