# Philistines

The Philistines were one of the [[sea-peoples|Sea Peoples]] — the **Peleset** of
the Egyptian records — who, after their repulse from Egypt by
[[ramesses-iii|Ramesses III]] (c. 1177 BCE), settled the southern coastal plain of
the Levant in the aftermath of the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age
Collapse]]. They give their name to the region (Palestine) and are the
best-documented archaeological case of Sea Peoples settlement.

## Settlement and the Pentapolis

The Philistines occupied a confederation of five cities — the **pentapolis** of
**Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron (Tel Miqne), and Gath (Tell es-Safi)** — three on
the coast and two inland, each under a *seren* (lord; the Hebrew term may preserve
an Aegean word cognate with Greek *tyrannos*). CAH II.2 (chapter XXVIII) treats
their arrival as the southern terminus of the great migration recorded at Medinet
Habu, where Ramesses III is shown settling defeated captives in garrisons —
possibly the mechanism by which the Peleset came to hold the coast nominally as
Egyptian subjects before emerging as an independent power.

## Material Culture: the Aegean Fingerprint

The archaeology is the clearest evidence for the Aegean origin claimed in the
texts. The earliest Philistine levels yield **locally made Mycenaean IIIC:1b
pottery** — monochrome ware in Aegean shapes and decoration, succeeded by the
distinctive bichrome **"Philistine ware"** — together with Aegean-type hearths,
loom weights, and dietary shifts (notably pork consumption). The picture is of an
immigrant population transplanting Aegean and Cypriot cultural habits onto the
Levantine coast, then gradually assimilating Canaanite and Egyptian elements.

## Relations with Israel

The Philistines are the principal antagonists of early Israel in the biblical
narrative ([[hebrew-kingdom|the period of the Judges, Saul, and David]]). Their
military edge — chariotry, organised infantry, and (in the biblical tradition) a
monopoly on ironworking — drove the Israelite consolidation of monarchy. Their
ascendancy was broken by David, after which they persisted as a coastal power
until absorbed successively by Assyria, Egypt, and finally Babylon. The detailed
biblical engagement belongs to the [[hebrew-kingdom|Hebrew Kingdom]] page
(Section 5).

## Historiography

CAH II.2 reads the Philistines straightforwardly through the Egyptian texts and
the (then early) excavations as transplanted Aegean migrants. Subsequent
excavation at Ekron, Ashkelon, and Gath has richly confirmed the Aegean material
signature while complicating the "invasion" model toward a more gradual,
multi-wave settlement and rapid acculturation. The biblical portrait — uncultured
"Philistines," iron monopoly, single-combat champions like Goliath — is a hostile
Israelite source and is treated with the caution due to texts written centuries
after the events; see [[the-exodus|the Exodus controversy]] and
[[hebrew-kingdom|Hebrew Kingdom]] for the source-critical issues with the
biblical record of this period.
