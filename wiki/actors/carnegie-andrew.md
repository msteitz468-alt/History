# Andrew Carnegie

**Summary & detailed biography:** [[hubs/biographies/long-19th-century/carnegie-andrew|Detailed analytic biography]]

Scottish-American industrialist and philanthropist (1835–1919). Born in Dunfermline, Scotland, to a linen weaver's family; immigrated to Allegheny/Pittsburgh in 1848 at age 13. Rose from bobbin boy and telegraph messenger to private secretary for Pennsylvania Railroad superintendent Thomas A. Scott; used insider connections to build early fortune in oil, iron, and bridges. Pivotal figure in the rise of the American steel industry through the Carnegie Steel Company (Edgar Thomson Works, Homestead, etc.). Famous for the 1889 "Gospel of Wealth" essays arguing that the rich should administer surplus wealth for the public good. Sold his steel interests to J.P. Morgan in 1901 for ~$480 million (the largest personal transaction to date) and devoted the rest of his life to giving it away through libraries, universities, research institutions, the Carnegie Corporation, and peace endowments. Central paradox: ruthless anti-union employer whose Homestead Strike (1892) defined Gilded Age labor conflict, yet the most systematic large-scale philanthropist of his era.

## Narrative

Andrew Carnegie was born November 25, 1835, in a weaver's cottage in Dunfermline, Scotland. His father William was a handloom linen weaver whose trade collapsed with mechanization; his mother Margaret Morrison Carnegie kept a shop and took in shoemaking work. The family, supported by relatives, emigrated to the United States in 1848, settling in Allegheny City (now part of Pittsburgh). Young Andrew worked first as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill, then as a telegraph messenger boy, rapidly teaching himself Morse code and becoming a skilled operator.

His breakthrough came when he was hired as personal telegraph operator and assistant to Thomas A. Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad. Carnegie learned the railroad business, invested in early opportunities (including the Woodruff sleeping car and oil wells), and formed partnerships that gave him insider contracts for iron and bridges supplying the railroad (with Scott and J. Edgar Thomson as silent partners). By his early thirties he had accumulated a substantial fortune.

In the 1870s Carnegie concentrated on steel. He was among the first in Pittsburgh to adopt the Bessemer process at scale, building the Edgar Thomson Steel Works (named for his railroad patron). He brought in partners including his brother Tom and later Henry Clay Frick (coke) and Charles M. Schwab (operations and sales). Aggressive vertical integration (Frick Coke, Mesabi ore leases from Rockefeller), plowing profits back into plant, and relentless cost-cutting made Carnegie Steel the dominant low-cost producer. The company grew enormously through the 1880s and 1890s.

The turning point in reputation was the Homestead Strike of 1892. While Carnegie was in Scotland, Frick managed the lockout and the arrival of Pinkerton agents; the resulting battle and subsequent violence badly damaged Carnegie's image as a friend of labor (he had earlier written sympathetically about unions). In 1889 he had published "Wealth" (the "Gospel of Wealth"), arguing that millionaires should live modestly and distribute their surplus for public benefit rather than pass it to heirs or leave it to the state.

In 1901, at age 65, Carnegie sold Carnegie Steel to the new United States Steel Corporation organized by J.P. Morgan for approximately $480 million in bonds and stock. He spent the remaining 18 years of his life systematically giving the fortune away: thousands of public libraries (in the US, Britain, and elsewhere), church organs, the Carnegie Institution of Washington, the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching (pensions for professors), the Carnegie Hero Fund, the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, and ultimately the Carnegie Corporation of New York (1911) to handle the residue. He also became an active "apostle of peace," lobbying for arbitration treaties, disarmament, and a League of Peace, while opposing the Philippine-American War and the Boer War. World War I shattered his optimism; he died at Shadow Brook, Lenox, Massachusetts, on August 11, 1919.

## Causal Analysis

Carnegie's rise was enabled by the explosive growth of American railroads and heavy industry after the Civil War, combined with personal traits (restless energy, salesmanship, capacity for self-education) and strategic relationships (Scott, Thomson, later Frick and Schwab). Insider dealing and political connections common to the era provided early capital and contracts. His decision to focus on steel at the right technological moment (Bessemer) and to integrate vertically and reinvest profits scaled the enterprise. The Homestead conflict was driven by Frick's hard line on wages and union recognition amid falling prices and overcapacity; Carnegie's absence and prior public statements created the perception gap. His philanthropy was not primarily guilt-driven but flowed from a coherent (if self-serving) philosophy articulated in "Gospel of Wealth": surplus wealth belongs to the community and should be administered by the competent rich during their lifetimes.

## Consequence Analysis

Carnegie Steel demonstrated the power of integrated, high-volume, low-cost steel production and helped make the United States the world's leading steel producer by 1900. The 1901 sale created U.S. Steel, the first billion-dollar corporation and a model (and target) for Progressive Era antitrust. Carnegie's libraries and endowments materially shaped American and British public culture, higher education, and scientific research; the Carnegie Corporation became the template for large modern foundations. His "Gospel of Wealth" supplied the ideological justification for large-scale private philanthropy that Rockefeller and others adopted and expanded. Yet Homestead became an enduring symbol of Gilded Age labor repression; Carnegie's reputation never fully recovered among workers and radicals. As a transatlantic figure he helped knit elite Anglo-American cultural and political networks.

## Historiography

David Nasaw's 2006 biography is the modern standard, the first to make extensive use of new archival sources (family papers, unpublished drafts of the Autobiography, Civil War tax returns, correspondence with presidents and European leaders) and to treat Carnegie whole—ruthless capitalist and radical philanthropist—without relying primarily on the sanitized Autobiography. Earlier works (Hendrick, authorized by Louise; Wall) were constrained by family control or limited sources. Nasaw emphasizes Carnegie's deliberate construction of his own legend, the gap between his public writings and business practice (especially on labor), and the sincerity of his later giving as an expression of long-held beliefs rather than atonement. The central interpretive problem remains how to weigh the "good" philanthropy against the human costs of Homestead and the twelve-hour day. Recent scholarship situates Carnegie within the broader history of Gilded Age capitalism, transatlantic liberalism, and the invention of modern philanthropy.
