# Marcus Antonius "Mark Antony"

Caesar's chief lieutenant and the dominant triumvir of the 40s–30s, who organized the
Roman **East**, allied himself with **Cleopatra**, and lost the final civil war to
**Octavian** at [[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]]. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH
X]] Ch. 1 (Pelling); his earlier career (the *Philippics*, the war of Mutina) is in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]].

## Career

A Caesarian general and consul of 44, Antony emerged from the
**[[julius-caesar-assassination-44bce|Ides of March]]** as the strongest man in Rome,
was beaten back at **Mutina** (43, the target of Cicero's *Philippics*), then formed
the **[[second-triumvirate|Second Triumvirate]]** with Octavian and Lepidus. He won the
chief glory at **[[battle-of-philippi-42bce|Philippi]]** (42, destroying Brutus and
Cassius) and took the **organization of the East** — the richest and most prestigious
task, making him for years the **senior partner**.

**In the East** Antony proved a shrewd administrator, building a ring of able
**client kings** (Herod, Amyntas, Polemo, Archelaus) and styling himself the new
**Dionysus**. He met **Cleopatra** at Tarsus (41) and allied with her — diplomatically
and personally. His great bid for glory, the **invasion of Parthia (36)**, ended in
**disaster** (a third of his army lost), even as Octavian was crushing Sextus in the
West — the turning-point of their rivalry. He took revenge by annexing **Armenia**
(34), but his liaison with Cleopatra (the **Donations of Alexandria**, 34; the divorce
of Octavia, 32) handed Octavian a devastating propaganda weapon.

## The fall

By 32 the triumvirate had lapsed and the breach was open: the Antonian consuls fled
Rome, Octavian rallied all Italy by oath and declared war on Cleopatra. Antony, with a
huge army and fleet in Greece, was outmanoeuvred by **Agrippa** and blockaded at
**[[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]]** (31); the sea-battle was a failed break-out. He
fled to Egypt, and at the **[[death-of-antony-and-cleopatra-30bce|fall of
Alexandria]]** (30) **took his own life**.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Pelling argues Antony **still looked the likely winner** at the start of the 31
campaign — the East was as solid for him as the West for Octavian, the military odds on
his side — and that he was undone less by Cleopatra than by **Agrippa's swift opening
strokes** and by his earlier **failure to appease Italian sentiment**. Even had he won
Actium, the editor doubts a Greco-Roman world ruled by a god-king and a foreign queen
could have held together: Antony "could not have coped with success." His client-king
system, however, was largely **inherited by Augustus**.

## Historiography

The hostile tradition is overwhelming — **Octavian's victory propaganda** (the
*Autobiography*, the Augustan poets) made Antony the type of the Roman unmanned by the
East. Plutarch's *Antony* (with Shakespeare) romanticizes; Pelling reconstructs a far
abler and more rational figure beneath the legend.

## Beard (SPQR, 2015)

Mary Beard notes that the surviving image of the young Octavian as brutal and
cruel (e.g., the anecdote of a fancy-dress banquet where he appeared as Apollo)
likely originates in Antony's own wartime propaganda, per Suetonius — meaning
the "sadistic young thug" caricature of Octavian and his eventual "model
emperor" image are both partisan constructions rather than neutral fact. Her
broader point: had Antony won at [[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]], the
historical verdict on the two men could easily have been reversed — "it is not
difficult to see what the image of Octavian would have been if Antony had won at
Actium: a sadistic young thug." See [[sources/beard-spqr-2015]].

### OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Stockton / Rawson (R7 aftermath)

Rawson (Ch. 17) places Antony at the end of the overseas expansion survey (Actium and
Egypt under Augustus). Stockton (Ch. 21, R7): after Caesar’s murder, Antony controlled
Italy; Cicero’s attempt to use Octavian against him failed; autumn 43 Second Triumvirate
proscriptions killed ~300 senators and 2,000 knights including Cicero; Philippi (42)
crushed Brutus and Cassius; veteran settlement misery; eastern dependence on Cleopatra
deepened (Donations of Alexandria 34 gave Octavian propaganda that Antony was a
renegade creature of an “Egyptian she-devil”); Actium Sept 31 and suicides summer 30
ended two decades of civil war. Griffin (Ch. 19) notes Augustus avoided Caesar’s
mistakes (including clemency) but the solution was not so different once Catos and
Ciceros were gone.

Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]] (Chs. 17, 19, 21).

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010)

Harriet I. Flower ([[flower-roman-republics-2010|*Roman Republics*]]) situates Antony
within her multi-republic map ([[roman-republics-periodization]]): the **official
triumvirate of Antony, Lepidus, and Octavian (43–33)** is period 13, distinct from the
informal Pompey–Crassus–Caesar alliance of the 50s (she rejects equating them under
“First Triumvirate,” though the later three may have had the earlier antecedents in
mind). Stable third–second-century republican culture had given way to a volatile
alternation of narrow oligarchies of two or three (Pompey–Caesar–Crassus; then
Antony–Octavian–Lepidus) and one-man rule (Caesar dictator; then Augustus princeps).
Actium (31) falls in the short transition **33–28** before the principate of January 27.
