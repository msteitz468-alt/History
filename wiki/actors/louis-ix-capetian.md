# Louis IX of France, "Saint Louis" (r. 1226–1270)

**Louis IX** is the central figure of the thirteenth-century [[capetian-dynasty|Capetian
monarchy]] and the model of the sacral Christian king. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]]
(William Chester Jordan, Ch. 11) treats his reign as the apogee of Capetian government —
consolidating his father's conquests, reforming administration, crusading twice, and
investing the French crown with a moral and religious *auctoritas* that his grandson
[[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV]] would exploit.

## The Regency and Consolidation

Louis succeeded aged twelve under the regency of his mother **Blanche of Castile**, who
faced down successive baronial coalitions (Pierre Mauclerc of Brittany, Hugues de Lusignan,
Thibaut IV of Champagne) by "astute diplomacy" and the crown's material advantage. The
**Treaty of Paris-Meaux (1229)** ended the [[albigensian-crusade-1209|Albigensian Crusade]],
preserving part of the count of Toulouse's patrimony but affiancing his heiress to Louis's
brother **Alphonse of Poitiers** — promising a Capetian succession in the south. The
crown's reach grew through the **apanage** system (Louis VIII's younger sons endowed with
Artois, Poitou-Auvergne, Anjou-Maine), the new southern **sénéchaussées** of
Beaucaire-Nîmes and Carcassonne-Béziers (where Languedoc "remained for long an occupied
province"), and the **Inquisition** founded in the early 1230s to confront Cathar heresy.
A defining act of Capetian anti-Jewish policy was the **Talmud trial** at Paris (1240) and
the burning of "twenty-four cartloads" of books (1242); see [[medieval-anti-judaism]].

## Crusade and Reform

Louis took the cross in 1244 and led the **Seventh Crusade** (1248–1254): the capture of
**Damietta** (1249) was followed by catastrophe at **al-Mansura** (1250), where his army was
beaten and Louis himself captured and ransomed. He spent the crusade's estimated **1.5
million livres** — six years' revenue — and remained four years in the Holy Land. Before
departing he had launched, in 1247, the great **enquêtes**: systematic investigations
(carried out largely by [[mendicant-orders|Dominicans and Franciscans]]) into the abuses of
*baillis* and *sénéchaux*, which both purified the administration and built the king's
reputation for justice. He fixed the **coinage**, simplified the government of Paris under a
single *prévôt*, recorded the guild statutes (*Livre des métiers*), and insisted on a strict
division between the competence of Church and crown — refusing, for instance, to enforce
ecclesiastical excommunications by civil power.

A "moral vision of the kingdom" underpinned this: legislation against blasphemy and usury,
support for the Inquisition, endowment of hospitals, leprosariums, [[mendicant-orders|friars]]
and beguines, and a programme to convert the Jews. Treatises on the ruler's craft (Vincent
of Beauvais) imagined a king "whose every waking thought is about the Christian character of
his principality."

## Diplomacy and the Second Crusade

Louis sought to end "lingering disputes": the **Treaty of Corbeil (1258)** with Aragon and
the **Treaty of Paris (1259)** with [[henry-iii-of-england|Henry III]] — by which Henry
surrendered Normandy, Anjou and Poitou but held **Gascony** as a French fief. Generous and
pious, Louis was trusted enough to **arbitrate** between Henry III and his barons (the Mise
of Amiens, 1264, condemning the [[simon-de-montfort|Provisions of Oxford]] — though the
barons rejected it). He took the cross again in 1267; the **Eighth Crusade** (1270) was
diverted to **Tunis**, where disease killed him in August 1270.

## Significance

Louis combined effective government with a personal sanctity that made the French crown
"the mainstay of the crusades and the principal military supporter of the papacy." His
canonisation (1297, under [[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV]]) consecrated the dynasty: the
"sacred or semi-sacred character" of French kingship — the king's touch healing scrofula —
became a political asset. The rebels against Philip IV in 1314 rallied under the cry to
return to "the good old days of the saintly king." His reign is the high-water mark of the
medieval ideal of the just Christian monarch.

## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

Logan’s later-thirteenth-century chapter profiles Louis (d. 1270) for personal piety (daily prayer; friar’s coarse garment in private), the **Sainte-Chapelle** for the crown of thorns, an unsuccessful eastern crusade, and peace settlements with Flanders (1256), Aragon (1258), and England (1259). Dying en route east, he advised his son (Joinville):

> “My dear son, the first thing I want to teach you is to move your heart to love God, for without God no one can be saved … Deal with all your subjects in justice and equity, taking particular care for the poor … Love and respect all those who serve the church.”

Logan’s judgment: with St Louis died “the single most effective force for stability in Europe,” amid English baronial recovery, the German Great Interregnum, and a long papal vacancy. Canonized by [[boniface-viii|Boniface VIII]] (11 Aug 1297) during the early clash with [[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV]].
