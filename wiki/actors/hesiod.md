# Hesiod

**Hesiod** of Ascra in [[boeotia|Boeotia]] was, with [[homer|Homer]], one of the two
founding poets of Greek literature — and, uniquely, the **only contemporary literary
witness to the society of Late Geometric Greece** (c. 700 BCE). [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|
CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 41, i, W. G. G. Forrest) treats him.

## The poet and his works

Son of a sea-trader from Aeolic Cyme who settled on a hardscrabble farm at **Ascra** on
Mt Helicon, Hesiod composed two surviving poems. The **Theogony** — a genealogy of the
gods built on the succession-myth of Uranus, Cronus and Zeus — he sang (the tradition
held) at the funeral games of the nobleman **Amphidamas of Chalcis**, winning a tripod
he was proud of. The **Works and Days**, prompted by a bitter quarrel with his brother
**Perses** over their inheritance, is a "hard picture of the farmer's year and stern
sermon on justice" (c. 700). Like Homer, Hesiod "lived in the time of transition from
oral to written composition," probably among the first to commit his version of a long
oral tradition to writing.

## A window on Late Geometric society

Hesiod's value to the historian (Forrest stresses) is that his subject-matter — unlike
Homer's heroic past — "still applies in his own day." He shows a simple society divided
not by wealth but by the line between **noble and commoner**: Hesiod is of the *demos*,
his rulers the **basileis** ("lords" rather than "kings"). His attitude to them is
pointedly double: eloquent and just "fathers of their people" in the Theogony (sung for
a noble audience), but **"gift-devouring," corrupt and oppressive** in the Works and Days
(the fable of the hawk and the nightingale: "a stronger bird than you has got you"). He
stands "at a moment of transition... between blind acceptance of aristocratic rule and
revolt against it" — appealing over the heads of the unjust lords to **Zeus**, who
through his agents marks crooked judgements and deals out prosperity or ruin. He has no
power to enforce a norm; "there was only Zeus."

## The orientalizing of Greek myth

The **Theogony's** close parallels with Near Eastern succession-myths — especially the
Babylonian **Enuma Elish** — show the Greek cosmogony "was no spontaneous local
creation." Forrest dates the borrowing most plausibly to the **eighth-century reopening
of eastern links** (via [[al-mina|Al Mina]], perhaps through Crete and Delphi, or via
Euboea to Boeotia) — adding "a powerful religious element" to the artistic and the
possible political dimensions of Greece's [[orientalizing-revolution|debt to the East]].
Herodotus (II.53) said Hesiod and Homer "were the first to set down a theogony for the
Greeks and give the gods their functions" — needing, Forrest adds, only "a note on their
sources."

## Causal significance

- The **earliest voice of the Greek commoner** — the first articulation of a moral order
  (justice, *dikē*) standing above and judging aristocratic power, a seed of the
  political questioning that produced Solon and the reformers.
- A key witness to the **transition to literacy** and to the **orientalizing of Greek
  religion** — the Near Eastern roots of the Olympian succession-myth.
