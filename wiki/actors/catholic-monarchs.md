# The Catholic Monarchs (Isabella and Ferdinand)

**Isabella I of Castile** (r. 1474–1504) and **Ferdinand II of Aragon** (r. 1479–1516), the *Reyes
Católicos* whose marriage joined the two great Iberian crowns. Treated by Angus MacKay (Castile and
Navarre) and Mario del Treppo (Aragon) in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Chs. 24a–b). Their reign
was long seen as the moment when "chaos" gave way to "law and order"; MacKay qualifies this,
stressing how far they **built on the work of their predecessors**, and how their *annus mirabilis*
of **1492** still belonged, in spirit, to the medieval world.

## The Union of the Crowns

The marriage of **1469** — arranged by Aragonese diplomacy against the wishes of Isabella's
half-brother **Enrique IV** of Castile — united the heirs of the two **Trastámara** lines (both
descended from Fernando "de Antequera," who had won the Aragonese throne at the **Compromise of Caspe**,
1412). After Enrique's death (1474), Isabella secured Castile in a civil war against **Juana "la
Beltraneja"** and her Portuguese backers. The union was **personal and dynastic, not institutional**:
Castile and the federal **[[crown-of-aragon|crown of Aragon]]** kept their separate laws,
*cortes* and institutions (Aragon retaining its "pactist," pluralistic character down to 1714).
Castile — far larger and richer — was "the epicentre and guiding light of the new monarchy," but
foreign policy followed **Aragonese** interests in the Mediterranean and Italy (the reabsorption of
[[crown-of-aragon|Naples]], 1503, by Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba).

## Restoring Royal Authority

MacKay reads the apparent "anarchy" of fifteenth-century Castile — endemic noble *bandos*, the
factional struggles of **Álvaro de Luna** and the infantes of Aragon under Juan II, the scandalous
reign of Enrique IV — less as mindless chaos than as the nobility's manoeuvring **to extract a share
of royal fiscal revenue** (above all the *alcabala* sales tax) amid chronic debasement of the
*maravedí*. The Catholic Monarchs reasserted the crown through a mixture of force, patronage,
institutional revival (the *corregidores*, the *Santa Hermandad*, control of the military orders) and
sacral prestige — and in Aragon through **Ferdinand's reforms** (the *redreç* economic recovery, the
*consejo supremo de Aragón*, 1494).

## 1492 and the Messianic Key

The cluster of 1492 — the surrender of **[[fall-of-granada-1492|Granada]]** (1 January), the **decree
expelling the Jews** (31 March), and **Columbus's** Atlantic crossing (October) — "appear thoroughly
medieval; their full significance would emerge with time." MacKay stresses the **eschatological**
charge of the reign: Ferdinand was cast as the prophesied *Encubierto* / "Last World Emperor" who
would conquer Granada, all Islam, and finally **Jerusalem** — a messianic ideology (Joachimite, pseudo-
Isidoran) that made the regime's run of "apocalyptic success" after 1480 newly credible.

## Significance

The Catholic Monarchs are the Iberian exemplar of the **[[new-monarchy|"new monarchy"]]**: a
consolidated, militarily formidable crown that completed the **[[reconquista|Reconquista]]**, launched
Castile across the Atlantic ([[age-of-discovery|the discovery of America]] and the
[[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]]), and projected Aragonese power into Italy — the
platform for the sixteenth-century Spanish empire of their grandson Charles V. Yet "Spain" remained a
union of distinct realms; the monarchs never styled themselves king and queen *of Spain*.
