# Charlemagne (Charles the Great, r. 768–814)

> **Detailed analytic biography:** [[hubs/biographies/early-middle-ages/charlemagne|Charlemagne — detailed biography]] (Biography Hub; graduate-level, synthesised from [[sources/nelson-king-and-emperor-2019|Nelson, *King and Emperor* (2019)]]).

## Overview

**Charlemagne** (Charles I, *Karolus Magnus*; c. 748–814) was king of the Franks
from 768 and the first western emperor since the fall of Rome, crowned by Pope Leo
III at Rome on Christmas Day 800 ([[coronation-of-charlemagne-800]]). Through more
than four decades of almost continuous campaigning he assembled the largest political
unit western Europe had seen since antiquity — from the Pyrenees to the Elbe and from
the North Sea to central Italy — and lent his name to the [[carolingian-empire]] and
the [[carolingian-renaissance]]. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Fouracre, Ch. 3)
he is the culmination of three generations of Pippinid/Arnulfing consolidation begun by
his grandfather [[charles-martel]] and his father [[pippin-iii]].

## Conquests

Charlemagne inherited the Frankish kingdom jointly with his brother Carloman (d. 771),
after which he ruled alone. His wars defined the reign:

- **The Lombard kingdom (774).** Responding to Pope Hadrian I's appeal, Charlemagne
  crossed the Alps, deposed King Desiderius — his own former father-in-law — and took
  the title *king of the Lombards*, annexing northern Italy and confirming his father's
  territorial grant to the papacy.
- **The Saxon wars (772–804).** The longest and bloodiest of his campaigns, fought to
  subdue and forcibly Christianise the pagan Saxons; marked by the notorious massacre at
  Verden and the resistance of Widukind. Saxony's pacification was, in Fouracre's phrase,
  "a long-term investment" rather than a profitable conquest.
- **Bavaria (787–788).** Duke Tassilo III was deposed in an almost bloodless campaign,
  absorbing the last semi-independent Frankish duchy.
- **The Avars (791–796).** The destruction of the Avar khaganate and the plunder of the
  Avar "ring" brought a treasure of fabulous proportions into Francia, items from which
  Charlemagne sent as far as [[offa-of-mercia|Offa of Mercia]] to impress his neighbours.
- **Spain (778 ff.).** The disastrous rearguard action at Roncesvaux (778) — later
  mythologised in the *Song of Roland* — was followed by the slow establishment of the
  **Spanish March** below the Pyrenees (Barcelona taken 801).

## The Imperial Title

By the 790s Charlemagne had assumed much of the style of a Roman emperor: a new capital
palace at **Aachen**, Roman in inspiration; the commissioning of a Frankish definition of
orthodoxy on the use of images (the *Libri Carolini*); and the presidency over the
condemnation of the Adoptionist heresy — putting himself forward as the leader of western
Christendom. His coronation as emperor in 800 expressed this claim. NCMH II stresses that
Charlemagne plainly *arranged* the coronation (Einhard's tale of reluctance is a literary
convention of humility), but then "did not seem to attach anything like as much
significance to the title as later rulers and commentators would do" — the great reforming
capitulary of 802 contains little that cannot be found in earlier legislation. In 812
Byzantium grudgingly recognised him as western emperor.

## Government

Charlemagne governed through counts, bishops, and the **[[missi-dominici]]** (travelling
royal inspectors), issuing an increasing volume of **[[carolingian-capitularies|capitularies]]**.
Whether this amounted to a genuine reconstitution of Roman public authority or merely an
ideological gloss on a fundamentally personal, plunder-driven hegemony is the subject of a
[[nature-of-carolingian-government|sharp scholarly debate]] (Fouracre, Ch. 3). He sponsored
the [[carolingian-renaissance]] — the revival of Latin learning and the development of the
clear **Caroline minuscule** script — gathering scholars such as Alcuin of York at his court.

## Succession and Significance

By 811 all but one of his sons had died, so the whole empire passed undivided to
**[[louis-the-pious|Louis the Pious]]**, whom Charlemagne crowned himself at Aachen in 813
(without papal involvement). He died at Aachen in 814.

**Counterfactual significance.** The Carolingian empire depended on the person of the ruler
and on continuous conquest; its unity did not long survive him (see [[treaty-of-verdun-843]]).
But the durable achievement was structural and cultural rather than territorial: the template
of a **Latin Christian Europe** bound to the papacy, the imperial idea later revived as the
Holy Roman Empire, and the scriptural and educational infrastructure of medieval learning.

## Historiography

The image of Charlemagne is shaped by court-centred and ecclesiastical sources — Einhard's
*Vita Karoli*, the royal annals — which present a flattering, Rome-oriented portrait. NCMH II
(Fouracre) deliberately resists the old "blow-by-blow account of conquests and coronations,"
asking instead whether the Carolingian state was *objectively* different from its Merovingian
predecessor, and concludes that custom and continuity outweighed innovation: in 814 as in 714,
power on the ground still lay with counts and bishops. The depth and reality of his
administrative reforms remain the central interpretive question. See
[[nature-of-carolingian-government]].

[[sources/nelson-king-and-emperor-2019|Janet Nelson's *King and Emperor* (2019)]] is now the
wiki's source spine for the life. Its avowedly anti-teleological method — reading Charles
"forward, as he lived it," refusing both "Charlemagne" and "Father of Europe" — corrects the
birthdate to **2 April 748** (lifespan ~66, not 72), rehabilitates the queens [[hildegard]]
and [[fastrada]] as political agents, reads the [[coronation-of-charlemagne-800|coronation of
800]] as carefully co-planned (not a surprise), and rebuts Ganshof's "*l'échec de
Charlemagne*" by reconstructing a functioning late-imperial government from Aachen. Full
treatment in the [[hubs/biographies/early-middle-ages/charlemagne|detailed biography]].
