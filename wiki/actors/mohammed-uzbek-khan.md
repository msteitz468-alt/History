# Mohammed Uzbek Khan

**Mohammed Uzbek Khān** (Özbeg) ruled one of the seven great powers of the 14th-century world (alongside the Sultan of the West, Egypt/Syria, the two Irāqs, Turkistān/Māwarāʾ al-Nahr, India, and China). His territories encompassed the Kipchak/Qifjāk steppe.

## Mobile Court and Governance
The sultan’s camp (“urdū”) functioned as a moving city complete with streets, mosques, and cooking-houses whose smoke rose as the whole migrated. On Fridays he held court in a richly ornamented “golden alcove.” The four wives sat unveiled with him on the throne; sons and daughter were positioned nearby; emirs and ranked officers attended in strict order. One wife was the daughter of the Emperor of Constantinople (Takfūr). The traveler received sheep, a horse, and a bag of kimiz (mare’s milk, highly valued).

## Social and Legal Order
Theft laws were severe: convicted thieves returned the animal plus nine of equal value; lacking that, children were taken; lacking children, the thief was slaughtered. The steppe itself was green and productive (herbage served as fodder; cattle roamed without keepers). Travel was by araba carts. Women of noble and royal families enjoyed high respect, went unveiled, and engaged in religious and charitable works.

## Significance
Eyewitness testimony from Ibn Battuta (early 1330s leg) documents the functioning of a major post-Mongol successor state at the height of its power and the practical realities of steppe mobility and court culture. Links to the wider connected world (permission for a Constantinople voyage, Byzantine marriage alliance, trade with India/China).
