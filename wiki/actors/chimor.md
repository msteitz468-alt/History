# Chimor (Chimú)

## Overview

**Chimor**, the kingdom of the **Chimú**, was the largest and most powerful coastal state of
the Late Intermediate Period (c. 900–1470 CE), dominating the north coast of Peru from its
capital at [[chan-chan|Chan Chan]] in the Moche Valley. It was the principal heir to the
earlier [[moche|Moche]] tradition of the same valleys and the chief rival, on the coast, to
highland power. In Lumbreras's chapter in
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Volume III]], Chimor exemplifies coastal
**urbanism, hydraulic statecraft, and craft specialization** — vast intervalley canal systems,
monopolized metallurgy and textiles, and a stratified society organized around royal
compounds (ciudadelas).

Chimor was conquered by the [[inca-empire|Inka]] under Topa Inka around 1470 CE; the Inka
deported Chimú metalsmiths to Cuzco and absorbed north-coast craft traditions, making Chimor's
fall a key episode in the consolidation of Tawantinsuyu.

## Significance

Chimor represents the coastal pole of the Andean **coast–highland dynamic** that runs through
the volume: a wealthy, hydraulically sophisticated maritime-agrarian state ultimately
subordinated by highland militarized expansion. Its split-inheritance royal compounds at Chan
Chan offer a possible model (debated) for the Inka institution of royal estates and the
mummified ancestor-kings.

## Historiography

Chimú dynastic history survives partly through colonial oral traditions (the Anonymous History
of Trujillo) blending myth and king-list — a prime case for the **myth/history debate** that
Salomon raises in the volume's source-criticism chapter. Archaeology of Chan Chan (Moseley,
Day, and others) underpins most reconstructions of Chimú political economy.

## Split Inheritance and the Chimú–Inka Institutional Link (Fagan & Durrani 2020)

Fagan & Durrani (2020) read **Chan Chan's nine large walled royal-palace compounds** as direct
architectural evidence for the Chimú institution of **split inheritance**: each compound was a
single ruler's palace which, on his death, became his tomb and remained the property of his
descendants, so that an incoming ruler inherited none of his predecessor's estate and had to
acquire fresh lands and wealth through his own conquests. Chimor controlled at least **twelve
river valleys** (~50,600 ha of cultivated land) and practiced **forced resettlement** of
conquered populations along its road network. Fagan & Durrani treat both split inheritance and
forced resettlement as institutions later shared with — and expanded by — the [[inca-empire|Inka]]
(who conquered Chimor in the 1460s): for the Inka, [[pachacuti|Pachakuti]]'s formalization of
split inheritance and the royal-mummy cult became the structural engine of continuous imperial
expansion. This makes Chimor the clearest documented Andean case of a governing institution
passing from a conquered predecessor into the practice of its conqueror (`analogous_to` the Inka).
