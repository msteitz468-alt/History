# Fukuzawa Yukichi (1835–1901)

The foremost intellectual of [[meiji-modernization|Meiji "civilization and enlightenment"]] —
educator, author, and publicist who, more than anyone, taught Japan how to think about the
**modern West**. A low-ranking [[samurai]] who became Japan's leading apostle of Western
learning, individual independence, and national self-strengthening; his face has appeared on
the 10,000-yen note. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v5-nineteenth-century|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 5]]
(Hirakawa, ch. 7; Jansen, Introduction).

## Career

Trained in [[sakoku|Dutch learning]] (*rangaku*), Fukuzawa switched to English on realizing
after the [[opening-of-japan-1853-1858|opening of the ports]] that English, not Dutch, was the
language of the modern world. He travelled abroad on **three bakufu missions** (to the United
States in 1860 and 1867, to Europe in 1862) — the direct experience behind his enormously
popular guidebooks **Conditions in the West** (*Seiyō Jijō*). In 1868 he founded **Keiō
Gijuku** (the future Keiō University), and later the influential newspaper *Jiji Shinpō*. He
declined office, working instead as an independent educator and writer — the model of the
public intellectual.

## Thought

Fukuzawa's bestseller **An Encouragement of Learning** (*Gakumon no susume*, 1872–76) opened
with the famous democratic claim that "Heaven created no man above another," and preached
practical Western learning, self-reliance, and **individual and national independence**
("national independence through personal independence"). His major theoretical work, **An
Outline of a Theory of Civilization** (*Bunmeiron no gairyaku*, 1875), drew on **Guizot and
Buckle** to fit Japan into the universal stages of civilization. Jansen highlights its radical
**functionalism**: Fukuzawa valued imperial loyalism and the *kokutai* not as sacred in
themselves but for their *use* in advancing Japan's civilization and sovereignty — "a thing is
not to be valued for itself, but for its function," and Japan's political legitimation, like
any country's, "has frequently undergone change." He read the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji
change]] as a genuine "revolution" of public opinion against the bakufu.

## Counterfactual significance

Fukuzawa is the **intellectual architect of bunmei kaika** — the writer who gave the Meiji
"turn to the West" its popular vocabulary and its rationale, shaping how a literate nation
understood modernity, learning, and independence. His later "Datsu-A" ("leave Asia") argument
— that Japan should align with the advanced West rather than a backward Asia — has been read
(controversially) as foreshadowing the condescension of later [[japanese-imperialism|Japanese
imperialism]]. He embodies the Meiji passage from *rangaku* scholar to modern liberal
publicist.
