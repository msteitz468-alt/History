# Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala

**Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala** (c. 1535–1550 – c. 1616) was an indigenous Andean (Quechua)
nobleman, colonial functionary, and author of the **[[guaman-poma-nueva-coronica|*Nueva corónica y
buen gobierno*]]** (c. 1615) — the great illustrated chronicle-and-petition that gives the fullest
indigenous account of the early colonial Andes and a devastating critique of Spanish rule.

## Life

Born a generation or so after the [[spanish-conquest-of-peru-1532|Spanish invasion of 1532]] near
Huamanga (modern Ayacucho), Guamán Poma claimed descent from the **Yarovilca lords** of Huánuco and,
through his mother, from the **Inca house** — a genealogy modern scholars read as partly symbolic,
a claim to mediating status between Andean and colonial society. He served the colonial regime as an
**interpreter and aide to visitadores**, civil and ecclesiastical — notably the **idolatry-extirpator
Cristóbal de Albornoz** during the **Taqui Onqoy** anti-colonial religious movement of the 1560s —
and learned to denounce Andean religion as "idolatry." Later he was **dispossessed of his lands** and,
in a land suit against transplanted *mitmaq* of Chachapoyas, was declared a **falsario** (impostor)
for claiming rank he could not prove. Embittered and "desposeído," he poured his experience into the
chronicle.

## The work and its argument

The *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno* is at once a **history** of the Andean world (from a biblical
creation and pre-Inca "ages" through the Incas to the conquest) and a **reform treatise** addressed
directly to **King Philip III**. Its core arguments:

- **The catalogue of abuses.** Guamán Poma documents, case by case, the cruelty and corruption of
  *corregidores*, *encomenderos*, priests, miners, and merchants — the daily violence of the
  [[encomienda]], the *mita*, tribute, and forced labour that were destroying the Andean population
  (the colonial demographic collapse he lived through). His famous complaint to the king: that the
  Indians were treated "worse than slaves brought from Guinea."
- **"No hay remedy" vs. *buen gobierno*.** The work oscillates between despair ("*no hay remedio*")
  and concrete proposals — appealing **over the heads of the colonists to the king** to restore a
  just, Christian, but Andean-led order in which native lords would govern under the crown.
- **A Christian-Andean synthesis.** A devout Catholic, Guamán Poma nonetheless integrated Andeans
  into sacred history (as descendants of Noah) and argued the Andes had received the Gospel before
  the Spanish — turning Christian universalism into a claim for Andean dignity and self-rule.
- **The drawings.** Nearly **400 pen drawings** carry the argument visually — the Inca past, the
  ages of the world, and above all the inverted, abusive colonial present. Scholars (Rolena Adorno)
  read the work as portraying a **"world upside down" (*el mundo al revés*)** that the king is
  begged to set right.

## Significance and historiography

Lost for centuries, the manuscript resurfaced in Copenhagen in **1908** and has become a
cornerstone of Andean ethnohistory. It is prized as a rare **interior, indigenous viewpoint** on
conquest and colonization — though, as the source page stresses, a thoroughly *interested* one,
shaped by Guamán Poma's grievances, his bid for status, and his Catholic and pro-monarchical frame.
He stands beside the [[popol-vuh|Popol Vuh]] (Maya) and Inca Garcilaso de la Vega as a foundational
indigenous/mestizo voice of the early Americas — and, unlike Garcilaso, one writing from defeat and
dispossession rather than reconciliation.
