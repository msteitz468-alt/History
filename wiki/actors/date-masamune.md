# Date Masamune (伊達政宗, "the One-eyed Dragon")

Date Masamune, lord of Sendai and ruler of a large domain in northern Honshū, was one of the most
powerful tozama (outside) daimyo of the early Tokugawa period. Known as the "One-eyed Dragon"
(*dokuganryū*), he was renowned for political cunning, ambition, and a persistent search for
independent advantage. He plays a significant role in [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]] as
one of the few daimyo who sought to exploit European contact for his own strategic purposes.
Source: [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]].

---

## Role in Edo Construction

Date Masamune superintended the 1611 phase of the [[building-of-edo-1604|building of Edo]], taking
responsibility for the construction of ramparts and moats around the Nishi Maru, working under the
direction of Honda Masanobu and the personal daily inspection of Shogun Hidetada. His involvement
reflects both his subordination to the Tokugawa system and his continued prominence within it as a
northern lord of the first rank.

In 1609 Date renamed himself from his Hideyoshi-era style of Hashiba Echizen-no-kami to Matsudaira
Mutsu-no-kami, paying a hundred pieces of gold and elaborate gifts to Ieyasu for the honor.
Sadler notes drily: "There was no better weathercock in Japan than Date, and Ieyasu must have
grinned when he heard of it."

## Hasekura Mission to Europe (1613–1620)

Date Masamune's most ambitious independent initiative was the secret embassy he dispatched to Rome
and Spain in 1613. In that year Date contrived to send one of his councilors, **Hasekura
Rokuemon**, on an embassy to the Pope, accompanied by Father Luis Sotelo, a Franciscan monk who
had been condemned to death for unauthorized preaching in Edo but whose pardon Date had obtained.

**Motivation.** Date's fief in Sendai on the Pacific coast lay "directly opposite" New Spain
(Mexico). His primary aim was to open direct trade relations with New Spain, bypassing the
Tokugawa-controlled ports. Sadler adds that it is "not impossible that he thought he might thereby
get assistance to overthrow Ieyasu, or anyhow he was suspected of some such design."

**The ship.** Ieyasu's own admiral, Mukai Shogen, went to Sendai to superintend the building of the
mission ship — "of cryptomeria wood, 36 feet wide and 108 feet long, with a mast 99 feet high."
The expedition included Hasekura and five senior Sendai clan officers, five of lower rank, ten
retainers of Mukai Shogen, and forty "Southern Barbarians" (Europeans), 180 persons in all plus
merchants. Sebastian Viszcaino, a Spanish explorer who had been in Japan searching for legendary
islands of gold and silver, served as nominal captain but quarreled bitterly with the Japanese and
departed at the first Spanish port (Zakatula).

**Journey.** The mission reached Acapulco in January 1613 (by the Japanese calendar; Western
calendar likely 1614), proceeded to Mexico City where seventy-eight of Hasekura's suite were
baptized, crossed to Vera Cruz, sailed to Havana, and traveled overland through Seville, Córdoba,
Toledo, and Madrid. Philip III of Spain received them. Hasekura was baptized by the Royal Chaplain
as deputy for the Archbishop of Toledo, with the Duke of Lerma as godfather. After eight months in
Spain they went to Genoa, then Rome, where Pope Paul V received them and Hasekura was made a
Roman citizen (the Latin document of citizenship was still held in the Date family archives as of
Sadler's writing, as were originals of Date's letters in Spain).

**Date's letters to the Pope.** Date wrote that he had "great respect for Christianity, though he
is hindered in becoming a convert by an obstacle" — meaning Ieyasu's prohibition — and asked for
Franciscan missionaries to be sent: "I wish to make all my vassals Christians." He promised ships
to New Spain every year.

**Failure and suspicion.** The mission was actively undermined by the Jesuits (who wrote that it
came not from Japan's ruler but a subject), by Viszcaino (who declared its real object was trade
and that Ieyasu and Hidetada were "Protestants"), and by the Venetian ambassador in Rome (Simon
Contarini), who noted that Sotelo had told the Pope that Date was "next in power and dignity to
the Emperor (Shogun)" and aimed to supplant him and then Christianize Japan. This was explosive:
Bishop Cercyra warned the Jesuit general that if the mission succeeded and Franciscans arrived in
the Kantō, "it may exasperate the king against Masamune and cause him to suspect that there exists
some ominous alliance between him and the Spaniards."

When Hasekura returned, Christianity was suppressed more severely than ever in Japan, and his
report was that European Christianity was "nothing but a vain show." None of the embassy's
promoters gained their aims. As Sadler concludes: "only the Bakufu profited by the information it
brought back. Which was just what Ieyasu had intended."

**Connection to Tadateru affair.** Date Masamune's daughter had married Matsudaira Tadateru,
Ieyasu's sixth son. Tadateru fell under suspicion in the Okubo Nagayasu conspiracy (Nagayasu was
accused of plotting with foreign soldiers to overthrow the shogunate) and was stripped from his
fief of 620,000 koku to a small 30,000 koku allocation, surviving under strict supervision into
extreme old age.

## Assessment

Date Masamune represents the limits of tozama daimyo autonomy under the early Tokugawa system. He
was powerful, intelligent, and geographically advantaged for Pacific trade, but his every
initiative was either co-opted by Ieyasu or undermined by the structural realities of the bakufu.
The Hasekura mission is the clearest case: Ieyasu allowed and even assisted it (sending his own
admiral), then used it for information while Date gained nothing.

## Historiography

Sadler's portrait of Date draws on official records and surviving diplomatic correspondence in
Spain and Japan. The Hasekura mission is one of the best-documented episodes of early Tokugawa
foreign relations, with sources from Japanese domain archives, Spanish royal records, and
Venetian diplomatic dispatches. The mission's failure to achieve any of its stated aims — trade
with New Spain, papal appointment of a bishop for Japan, Franciscan missionaries — is
historiographically uncontested; the debate concerns Date's true intentions and whether Ieyasu
genuinely co-opted the mission or merely tolerated it. Sadler leans toward active Tokugawa
co-optation.
