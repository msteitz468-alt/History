## Role

Agathocles (361–289 BCE) was tyrant and then **king of Syracuse**, the dominant figure
of the western Greek world for some thirty-five years and the **last Sicilian ruler to
play an independent role** in Mediterranean power politics (CAH VII.1 ch. 10, K. Meister).
His career is the western counterpart of the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|Diadochi]]: while
in the East Alexander's marshals carved out kingdoms and took the royal title, in the
West Agathocles did the same — proclaiming himself **king in 304** "to demonstrate his
equality with the Diadochi."

## Rise and Seizure of Power

Born to a (probably well-off) emigrant potter's family at Thermae and given Syracusan
citizenship under [[timoleon]], Agathocles rose through military distinction and a
wealthy marriage, championing the **democratic/radical** cause against the oligarchic
"Six Hundred." After twice being exiled, he raised a private army and, appointed
*strategos* with full powers, carried out the **coup of 316/15**: a massacre of the
oligarchs (thousands killed or exiled) followed by his election as "general
plenipotentiary." Meister stresses that, despite a veneer of constitutional form, this
was a "typical tyranny" — secured by debt-cancellation, land-redistribution, public
works, and demagogic populism, but resting on murder and a mercenary militia. He
ostentatiously **dispensed with the bodyguard and diadem**, the hated symbols of
tyranny, wearing instead a priest's head-band and honouring Demeter and Kore.

## The African Campaign (310–307)

His most audacious act, "on a par with the boldest ventures of the Diadochi": besieged in
Syracuse by [[carthage|Carthage]], he **invaded North Africa** — the first European army
ever to do so — **burning his own fleet** behind him, ravaging the rich Carthaginian
hinterland to the gates of the city. He allied with, then **murdered, Ophelias
of Cyrene** (absorbing his army), and for a time controlled most of Punic Libya. But
his son Archagathus' mismanagement in his absence brought disaster, and Agathocles
eventually **abandoned his army and his sons** and slipped away (307). The expedition
failed — yet it preserved his rule in Sicily, its primary object, and (Meister notes)
"showed the way in which Carthage could be defeated": Scipio Africanus, asked the greatest
statesmen of "courage and wisdom," named "**Agathocles and Dionysius**."

## Kingship and End

Master of eastern Sicily and parts of south Italy, Agathocles took the **royal title
(304)** — like the Diadochi, "king" without a country's name — and wove himself into the
Hellenistic dynastic network (marriages to a daughter of [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]];
his daughter Lanassa to [[pyrrhus-of-epirus|Pyrrhus]], then to [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius]]).
He planned a final war on Carthage, but died (289/8) amid a succession struggle, giving
Syracuse back its "liberty." **His state collapsed instantly at his death** and Sicily
relapsed into chaos until Rome.

## Counterfactual Significance

Agathocles is the [[hellenistic-monarchy|Hellenistic-style spear-won king]] of the West —
proof that the new model of personal military monarchy was a Mediterranean-wide
phenomenon, not merely an eastern one. He stands "at the turning-point between Sicily's
Greek past and its Roman future," a model for Rome's own African strategy against
Hannibal. Hostile (Timaeus) and laudatory (Callias) ancient verdicts diverge wildly;
modern judgement, like Meister's, takes a middle course — real courage and ability, set
against an "endless series of political murders." See [[carthage]], [[syracuse]],
[[western-greek-tyranny]].

## The Invasion of Africa (Miles)

Richard Miles ([[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010]]) treats Agathocles' **310 BCE
invasion of the Carthaginian homeland** as a turning point in the
[[carthage-syracuse-rivalry|Sicilian rivalry]]. Blockaded in Syracuse by Hamilcar son of
Gisco (311), he made the audacious counterstroke of carrying the war to Africa, exploiting
the fact that "most Carthaginians had no experience of war" and that their armies were
mercenary. He funded the expedition by seizing temple offerings and women's jewellery, and
**burned his own ships on landing** (dedicating them to Demeter and Core as Sicilian-Greek
revenge propaganda). The panic in Carthage triggered, per the hostile Diodoran tradition, a
mass [[carthaginian-child-sacrifice|child sacrifice]] (200 high-born children) and Bomilcar's
attempted coup — vivid evidence of the [[carthage-syracuse-rivalry|Carthaginian fear of their
own commanders]] near the capital.

Despite controlling much of Punic Africa, Agathocles' army mutinied over pay; he abandoned
them and fled, and a peace c. 306 restored the Sicilian status quo. Miles's point: Carthage
did not press its advantage because the wars had brought it **to the brink of financial
ruin** (debased electrum, heavy bronze coinage), and the episode **exposed Carthaginian
fragility**, making Africa look like a viable target to later warlords — [[pyrrhus-of-epirus|Pyrrhus]]
and ultimately [[scipio-africanus|Scipio]]. Agathocles styled himself the "western heir to
Alexander," prefiguring the [[heracles-melqart-syncretism|Heraclean self-fashioning]] the
Barcids would adopt.
