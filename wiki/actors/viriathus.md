# Viriathus

The Lusitanian leader who for eight years (c. 147–139) inflicted repeated defeats
on Roman commanders in **Further Spain** — the most effective indigenous opponent
Rome faced in the peninsula. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 5
(Harris).

## Role and career

Viriathus emerged as leader of the **Lusitanians** about 147, after the massacre of
his people by **Ser. Sulpicius Galba** (150). Attacking Turdetania, he defeated at
least four Roman commanders within Roman territory, drawing support even from
inside supposedly pacified land south of the Guadalquivir. His successes encouraged
the Celtiberians to renew war (the [[numantine-war-153-133bce|Numantine war]]).
After Q. Fabius Maximus Aemilianus checked him (144) and Fabius Servilianus
campaigned against him, Viriathus cornered a Roman army at Erisane (141/40) and
extracted a treaty recognising him as ruler of his territory — **ratified even by
the Roman people**, a measure of Roman war-weariness. But the Senate, encouraged
by **Q. Servilius Caepio**, repudiated the treaty, drove Viriathus back, and in 139
**arranged his assassination** by three of his own envoys.

## Counterfactual significance

Viriathus demonstrated that the limit on Roman conquest in Spain was set less by
Roman resources than by Roman willingness to bear cost — service in Spain became so
unpopular that the assembly ratified his treaty. His killing by treachery, after a
Roman-recognised peace, exemplifies the bad faith Harris reads into Roman conduct
in the peninsula (see [[nature-of-roman-imperialism]]). He became a lasting symbol
of Iberian resistance.

## Historiography

**Appian** (*Iberica*) is the main source, with the Livian epitomes. The narrative
is shaped by Roman self-justification; the manner of his death (a treacherous
assassination Caepio "arranged or encouraged") is consistently attested.
