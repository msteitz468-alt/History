# Pausanias (the Regent)

## Overview

**Pausanias** (d. c. 466 BCE), the Agiad regent of Sparta for the boy-king Plistarchus,
was the victorious Greek commander at **[[battle-of-plataea-479bce|Plataea]]** (479) whose
arrogance and downfall opened the way for the **transfer of naval leadership from Sparta to
Athens** and the founding of the [[delian-league|Delian League]]. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH
Vol. V]] (Chs. 3, 5, Rhodes & Lewis) treats his career, while warning that the lurid
tradition around his end is "moving so close to romance that it is not rational to
distinguish between fact, accusation and embroidered accusation."

## Career and downfall

After commanding the allied land army at Plataea, Pausanias took command of the **fleet in
478**, overrunning Cyprus and driving the Persians from **Byzantium**. But "power went to
his head": his arrogance and severity alienated the allies, who turned to Athens — and
when Sparta sent Dorcis to succeed him, the allies refused, and the leadership passed
permanently to the Athenians. Recalled and **acquitted of medism**, Pausanias returned in a
private capacity to **Byzantium**, held it for some years until expelled by Athens, and
withdrew to **Colonae** in the Troad. Recalled again by the ephors, he was charged not only
with **medism** (collusion with Persia) but with **plotting with the Helots** — a charge
Thucydides was told enough to endorse. He took refuge in the sanctuary of **Athena of the
Brazen House** and was **starved to death** there (c. 466). The Spartans then used what they
claimed to have learned to discredit the exiled **[[themistocles]]**.

## Significance

Pausanias personifies the **first failure of Spartan overseas leadership**: his conduct at
Byzantium is the proximate reason the [[delian-league|League]] formed under Athens rather
than Sparta, and his fall — together with the troubles of King Leotychidas — marks the
**retreat of Sparta from the Aegean** and a shift of power at Sparta away from the
kingship. His alleged intriguing with the Helots also fed the chronic Spartan fear that
would erupt in the [[third-messenian-war-464bce|Great Helot Revolt]].

## Historiography

The "medism of Pausanias" grew ever more elaborate in the tradition (Thucydides' quoted
letters could not have been written in the time he was at Byzantium); Lewis and Rhodes
treat the romance critically while accepting the core — acquittal, private return,
Helot-intrigue charge, death by starvation. The date of his death (after 470, c. 466) is a
Pentekontaetia crux. See [[classical-greek-historiography]].
