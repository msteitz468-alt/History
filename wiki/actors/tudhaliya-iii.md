## Overview

Tudhaliya III was the son of Arnuwanda I and Asmunikal, and the tuhkanti (crown prince) during his father's reign. He occupies a paradoxical place in Hittite history: his reign saw the most catastrophic near-collapse of the kingdom — the burning of [[hattusha|Hattusa]] and coordinated enemy invasions from every frontier — yet he survived to conduct the campaigns of reconquest that his more famous son [[shuppiluliuma-i|Suppiluliuma I]] completed. His great-grandson Hattusili III attributed the recovery solely to Suppiluliuma, but Bryce argues this understates Tudhaliya's role: he "perhaps one of the most courageous and most determined, if one of the least known, kings of the Hittites" (Bryce, Ch. 6).

## The Concentric Attack and the Burning of Hattusa

Tudhaliya III inherited a kingdom already under severe strain from Kaska raids and the stabilizing Mitannian–Egyptian alliance in Syria. At some undetermined point in his reign, multiple enemies launched what appeared to be simultaneous invasions from all directions. Hattusili III's later decree (KBo vi 28) records the full scale of the catastrophe:

> "In the Land of Nerik, in Hursama, in the Land of Kastama … the temples which you, the gods, possessed in these lands the Kaskans sacked them … The Kaskan enemy came and sacked the Hatti lands and he made Nenassa his frontier. From the Lower Land came the Arzawan enemy … he made Tuwanuwa and Uda his frontier. From afar, the Azzian enemy came and sacked all the Upper Lands … And Hattusa, the city, was burned down."
> (KBo vi 28 (CTH 88) obv. 6–15)

Bryce interprets this not as a single coordinated assault but as a cascade: a massive Kaskan invasion from the north drew Hittite forces northward, exposing the southern and western frontiers to Arzawan and other enemies. The capital fell and burned. Even the Egyptian pharaoh Amenhotep III wrote to the Arzawan king Tarhundaradu seeking an alliance, remarking: "I have heard that everything is finished, and that the country Hattusa is paralysed" (EA 31: 26–7) — a sign that external powers regarded the Hittite kingdom as finished.

## The Recovery from Samuha

Rather than collapse entirely, Tudhaliya relocated his court to Samuha, an important cult centre on the upper Marassantiya river, which became the Hittite base of operations. From Samuha, he embarked on the reconquest of lost territories, working outward from the periphery before attempting to retake the devastated homeland. The strategy — defeat the peripheral enemies in their own territory first, eliminating their capacity for counter-attack before driving toward the core — is documented in the "Deeds of Suppiluliuma," composed by Mursili II. Crucially, Suppiluliuma served as Tudhaliya's chief military partner and adviser during these campaigns: "for many years before his accession, Suppiluliuma was his father's chief adviser, partner, and comrade-in-arms in the campaigns of reconquest" (Bryce, Ch. 6).

Key episodes of the reconquest:
- Repeated attacks from Samuha on the Kaska tribes, inflicting heavy casualties and taking prisoners.
- Joint Tudhaliya–Suppiluliuma campaign to liberate Kassiya and the Hulana River Land from Arawannan occupation.
- Joint invasion of Azzi-Hayasa, whose forces had advanced as far as Samuha; a showdown near the city of Kummaha led to the conquest of Azzi-Hayasa, which Suppiluliuma then organized as a vassal state.
- Campaign to expel Arzawan occupiers from the Lower Land; Suppiluliuma's first major clash with Arzawan forces in this region, supported by the gods: "the Sun Goddess of Arinna, the Storm God of Hatti, the Storm God of the Army, and Ishtar of the Battlefield, (so that) my father slew the Arzawan enemy" (DS frag. 14, 43'–5').

Tudhaliya reportedly took twenty years to re-establish Hittite control in Anatolia. He "almost to the end of his life, and in spite of repeated bouts of illness which confined him to his bed in Samuha, Tudhaliya led his forces in person against his enemies" (Bryce, Ch. 6).

## Succession and Legacy

Tudhaliya III was succeeded not by an older son Tudhaliya the Younger (who was the intended heir) but by Suppiluliuma I, who seized the throne after a violent conflict — details documented by Mursili II in his First Plague Prayer. Tudhaliya III's achievement was to hold the Hittite state together through its worst crisis and rebuild the military capacity that allowed Suppiluliuma to go on the offensive against [[mitanni]].

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Tudhaliya III not successfully organized the retreat to Samuha and begun the reconquest, the Hittite kingdom would likely have dissolved entirely in the mid-fourteenth century BCE — with Arzawa emerging as the dominant Anatolian power and Syria remaining under Egyptian and Mitannian control. The pharaoh Amenhotep III's diplomatic overtures to Arzawa suggest this outcome was widely anticipated. Tudhaliya's survival thus made Suppiluliuma possible; without the father's reconquest of Anatolia, the son had no platform from which to destroy Mitanni and establish the Hittite empire.

## Historiography

Tudhaliya III is poorly documented in primary sources. Most of our information comes indirectly from: the Deeds of Suppiluliuma (composed by Mursili II, focussed on his father's role); the Tapikka letters (Mashat Höyük archive) exchanged between the king and frontier officials; and the preamble of Hattusili III's decree, which records the catastrophe of the "concentric attack." Bryce notes that "Hattusili III, in recalling the devastation of the Hittite kingdom in the dark days of Tudhaliya III's reign, attributes the campaigns of reconquest which restored the kingdom's supremacy in Anatolia solely to his son Suppiluliuma" — a classic case of retrospective heroization of a later king at the expense of his father.
