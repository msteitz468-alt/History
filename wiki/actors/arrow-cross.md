# Arrow Cross Party / Hungarism (1935–1945)

Hungarian fascist movement led by Ferenc Szálasi, one of
[[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|Payne's]] "four major variants" of fascism —
notable for its genuinely proletarian mass base (the strongest working-class
support of any European fascist party, polling roughly 25 percent in 1939,
including Budapest's industrial belt) and for its ideology of "Hungarism,"
Szálasi's idiosyncratic Carpatho-Danubian imperial mysticism.

## Role and Significance

Kept from power by Admiral Horthy's conservative-authoritarian regime through
the 1930s (Szálasi was imprisoned 1938–40), the Arrow Cross was installed by
German force on 15–16 October 1944 after Horthy's attempted armistice with
the USSR. By then over half of Hungarian Jewry (~437,000
people) had already been deported to the death camps in seven weeks under
the preceding Sztójay government; under Szálasi roughly 50,000 more were
deported, and Arrow Cross militias murdered thousands of Budapest Jews in
pogroms and on the Danube embankment during the final phase of
[[the-holocaust|the Holocaust]] in Hungary (Payne 1995). The regime itself
was chaotic — Szálasi remained absorbed in ideological writing while
administering, by December 1944, about a quarter of Hungary's territory
amid the Soviet siege of Budapest. Payne notes his pre-war stance had been
"a-Semitic" (mandatory Jewish emigration, not persecution) — the movement's
terminal practice outran its doctrine. Driven into German territory by
March 1945, Szálasi was captured and executed for war crimes in 1946.

## Historiography

The Arrow Cross's worker base is standard evidence against purely
middle-class-panic theories of fascist support; its relation to the Horthy
regime is a textbook case of the conservative-right/fascist boundary —
Griffin ([[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]]) classifies Horthy's regime on
the [[para-fascism|para-fascist]] side, keeping the genuinely fascist Arrow
Cross marginalized until the German occupation made Szálasi's government "a
solely rhetorical force for change" — collaborationist, assisting Eichmann's
deportations. Griffin documents the ideology's Turanianism (Hungarians as a
"pure" Turanian race, "Jesus himself being a Turanian") and its palingenetic
core, quoting Szálasi in February 1945: "Without Good Friday there is no
resurrection... We shall drink the bitter cup to the last drop so that the
new world can arise which gives human life a new meaning." See
[[generic-fascism]] and [[fascism-and-the-political-left]].

## The Budapest terror, 1944–45 — Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

After Skorzeny kidnapped Horthy's son and forced the Regent's abdication (15–16 October 1944), Szálasi's Arrow Cross unleashed street murder on Budapest's surviving Jews: labor-battalion men shot on the Danube banks and bridges in such numbers "even the police complained"; the priest "Father Kun" reportedly commanding "In the name of Christ, fire!" (single-source anecdote, per Evans). Eichmann returned on 18 October and drove 50,000 Jews on foot toward Vienna for fortification labor, thousands dying, until Szálasi — "perhaps now fearing that he would be held to account" — halted it in mid-November. ~60,000 Jews were penned in the ghetto by January 1945 under raids, starvation, and disease; Raoul Wallenberg and fellow diplomats shielded ~40,000 with (partly forged) protective papers. Evans's summary of the whole 1944–45 phase: "Revenge for the Jews' imagined role in the imminent defeat had become their prime motivation." Source: [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008]]

## Payne (1980)

From [[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]] (ch. 5): Szálasi, a career army officer, built Hungarism into a genuine mass movement of poor workers and peasants in the late 1930s — an estimated "more than 20 percent of the popular vote" in 1939 (formally 31 Arrow Cross deputies of a 49-seat national-socialist coalition, ~13 percent of seats, with candidates blocked in some districts; strongest in Budapest worker districts), making it "the second most popular revolutionary nationalist party in Europe, second only to National Socialism." Distinctives: Hungarian racism with drastic Danubian-Carpathian expansionism, yet uniquely offering regional autonomy to areas 80–90 percent non-Magyar; theoretical eschewal of violence (Hungarism would triumph "by preaching, by conviction"); the claim to be not antisemitic but "a-Semitic," advocating that all Jews leave Hungary; "Hungarian True Christianity"; and a revolutionary corporatism against big landlords and capital. The Horthy regime's restrictiveness (not liberal democracy) blocked its path to power, compounded by "the visionary ineptitude and virtual schizophrenia of its own leaders"; it declined during the war, was increasingly Nazified, and was installed only briefly in 1944 as a German military puppet.
