# V. Gordon Childe

**Full name:** Vere Gordon Childe (often cited V. Gordon Childe). 1892–1957. Australian-born archaeologist, prehistorian, and Marxist thinker; Professor of Archaeology at Edinburgh and then London; one of the most influential synthesizers of European and Old World prehistory in the 20th century.

## Role and Significance

Childe transformed European prehistory from a collection of regional typological sequences into a coherent narrative of technological, economic, and social revolutions. His two most enduring concepts — the **Neolithic Revolution** (transition to agriculture) and the **Urban Revolution** (emergence of cities, states, writing, and specialized crafts) — provided the master framework against which virtually all subsequent work on early complex societies has been measured.

In *The Dawn of European Civilization* (1925, multiple editions) and *The Danube in Prehistory* (1929) he produced the first pan-European syntheses. He defined an archaeological "culture" as a constantly recurring assemblage of types (houses, pottery, burial rites, etc.) and treated it as the material expression of a social/ethnic group. This culture-historical approach, combined with diffusionist explanations (major innovations spreading from the Near East), dominated mid-century prehistory.

## Key Contributions (as assessed in the collection)

- Formulated the culture concept that dominated European archaeology until the radiocarbon revolution and processual critique.
- Identified and popularized the Neolithic and Urban Revolutions as the two great transformations separating foragers from complex societies.
- Synthesized vast bodies of material into explanatory models of migration, diffusion, and social evolution (often explicitly framed through Marxist lenses of base/superstructure and technological determinism in later works).
- *The Dawn of European Civilization* remained the standard reference for decades; later scholars (including the authors of *Prehistoric Europe* 1984) position their work as necessary rethinking in light of absolute dating and new questions about subsistence, settlement, and social organization.

From Champion et al. (1984): Childe is credited with laying "the groundwork for future studies"; his diffusionist model (innovations from a single Near Eastern centre) was the dominant paradigm until radiocarbon dating demonstrated that many European developments (e.g., megalithic architecture, metallurgy) were independent and earlier than supposed Eastern prototypes. The 1984 textbook treats Childe's achievement as monumental while documenting why his specific historical reconstructions required substantial revision. In Ch. 5 on early farming societies the authors explicitly revise the colonization/diffusion picture with evidence for indigenous contributions and local adaptation while retaining the transformative scale of the shift.

Specific to Ch. 7 Bronze Age prestige/hierarchies (range): "The model outlined emphasizes the importance of interregional connections in the earlier Bronze Age and has that in common with Childe's view. We have seen, however, that Childe placed the motor for the changes apparent in the archaeological record of the temperate European earlier Bronze Age in the civilizations of the eastern Mediterranean, and that such a dependency model is no longer tenable. The alternative proposed by Renfrew... of a series of autonomous local processes of change is also unsatisfactory. What we see is a convergence of local trajectories and an interaction between them, based on the influence of a widespread ideology and subsequent elite interactions which followed from it." (p. 221). This directly revises Childean diffusionism while retaining interregional interaction as explanatory.

Range-specific assessment (Ch. 6): Earlier Childean-influenced views that Corded Ware dispersed settlement and individual burial reflected "the invasion of nomadic pastoralists from the south Russian steppes (Gimbutas, 1965)" are rejected; "although there may have been an element of truth in the idea, inasmuch as it recognized the increasing importance of domestic animals, in fact the changes are much more likely to have been the result of local settlement and subsistence developments involving expansion and the adoption of new agricultural strategies" (Champion et al. 1984: 163). This exemplifies the post-radiocarbon, processual shift away from Childe's specific migration/diffusion narratives while retaining his broader problem of socio-economic transformations.

" The next major advance was the correlation of these many local studies into a pan-European framework, and the synthesis of all this information to form a coherent account of prehistoric Europe. This was very largely the achievement of the remarkable Australian prehistorian, Gordon Childe, who laid the groundwork for future studies in such books as The Dawn of European Civilization (1925) and The Danube in Prehistory (1929)." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~310-325)

The principal concept was the culture defined as "a constantly recurring assemblage of types such as houses, pottery and burial rites, and regarded it as the physical manifestation of a particular social or ethnic group." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.1 / range lines ~325-335)

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Childe's syntheses, European prehistory would likely have remained fragmented regional sequences with little comparative or explanatory power. His checklist approach to the "urban revolution" (ten criteria) directly shaped later comparative projects (Maisels 1999 testing it region-by-region; Trigger 2003 moving beyond trait lists). Even when later generations rejected his diffusionism and ethnic equations, they retained the problem-space he defined: how and why did agriculture, cities, states, and literate civilizations emerge?

His explicit use of social-evolutionary and Marxist categories opened the door for the social archaeology and settlement studies that dominate the post-1960s literature represented in this collection.

## Historiography

Childe's work is the essential starting point for any historiography of prehistoric archaeology. 

- **Culture-historical paradigm**: Defined "cultures" as proxies for peoples; change explained by migration, conquest, or diffusion. Later critiqued for reifying ethnic groups and underplaying independent invention and internal dynamics (see Hodder 1991 on the socio-political context of these critiques in Europe).
- **Radiocarbon revolution impact** (Renfrew and others): Destroyed many of the cross-datings on which Childe's chronological linkages rested and reversed the directionality of influence for key innovations. *Prehistoric Europe* (1984) is explicit that this forced "the need for rethinking the traditional prehistory of Europe."
- **Marxist influences**: Childe's later writings increasingly incorporated explicit Marxist analysis (technological determinism, relations of production). This strand was developed unevenly in European national traditions (see Hodder ed. 1991).
- **Legacy in the collection**: Treated as foundational by Maisels (testing the urban checklist), CWH volumes, and the 1984 textbook that simultaneously honors and revises him. No serious treatment of Neolithic or early state formation omits reference to his framework.

**Primary works** (selected): *The Dawn of European Civilization* (1925); *The Danube in Prehistory* (1929); *Man Makes Himself* (1936); *What Happened in History* (1942); "The Urban Revolution" (Town Planning Review, 1950).
