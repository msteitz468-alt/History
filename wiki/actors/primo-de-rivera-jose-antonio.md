# José Antonio Primo de Rivera (1903–1936)

Founder and first Jefe Nacional of the [[falange|Falange Española]] (1933), son of the dictator [[primo-de-rivera-miguel|Miguel Primo de Rivera]]. An aristocratic lawyer whose "poetic" national-syndicalist fascism never achieved mass support under the Republic (the Falange polled negligibly in 1936). Imprisoned by the Republic from March 1936, he was tried and executed in Alicante on 20 November 1936 — a death that removed the one figure who might have contested [[franco-francisco|Franco]]'s capture of the fascist movement.

## Significance

Payne (1987) treats his execution as structurally convenient for Franco: "El Ausente" (the Absent One) became the regime's official martyr-cult while the FET unification of April 1937 subordinated his party to a non-fascist caudillo — the classic mechanism of [[concepts/para-fascism|para-fascist absorption]]. The posthumous cult (his name on every church wall, reburial at El Escorial and later the Valle de los Caídos) substituted symbolism for the program.

## Detail (Payne 1987)

Payne's portrait is of an unusually ambivalent fascist leader: fastidious, ironic, morally scrupulous, doubting his own followers — telling Serrano Suñer in late 1934, "If blood had not already been shed, I would dissolve the Falange today... Many of them would have to be shipped off to the Congo." He met Mussolini just before founding the party (October 1933, with Bilbao business money), dropped the label "fascist" by end-1934 and "totalitarian" by end-1935 (once dismissing rightist conspirators as "fascist windbags"), yet, Payne insists, "never renounced the basic fascist goals in politics." The Twenty-Seven Points repudiated "the capitalist system" while protecting property from "grand capital"; the movement's first months saw nine members murdered before any fatal reprisal. From prison he negotiated only limited autonomy with Mola, warning in his 24 June 1936 circular that joining the revolt would mean the party's "total disappearance, even in case of triumph." Among his papers in Alicante was a proposal, drafted a month into the war, for an armistice and a compromise "national pacification" government of moderates — papers that impressed Prieto. Tried by a People's Court, he was executed 20 November 1936; two rescue attempts (one aided by Franco and German naval personnel) failed. The FET adopted his Twenty-Six Points as "programmatic norm" while Franco declared them revisable; the "El Ausente" cult peaked with the ten-day torchlight translation of his remains to El Escorial (November 1939).

## Payne (1980)

From [[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]]: "probably the most ambiguous of all European national fascist leaders" — fastidious aestheticism, "genuine if sometimes contradictory sense of moral scruple," intellectual irony, and "a remarkably limited spirit of sectarianism," traits that "may have disqualified him for successful leadership." Initially not averse to the fascist label (unlike Ledesma), he dropped "fascist" before end-1934 and "totalitarian" before end-1935, deriding rightist conspirators as *fascistas llenos de viento*; "of all national fascist leaders, he was probably the most repelled by the brutality and violence associated with the fascist enterprise," and there is "abundant testimony that he considered abandoning the project" but felt bound by members' deaths. Against post-fascist admirers who cite his "humanism" and Catholicism to deny he was fascist, Payne is blunt: "in the Joseantonian formulation these do not necessarily contradict fascism; fairly similar formulations might be found by some nominally leading members of the PNF."
