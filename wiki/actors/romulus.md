# Romulus

Romulus is the legendary founder and first king of Rome, who with his twin brother Remus
figures at the head of Rome's foundation story. In Mary Beard's *SPQR* he is treated not as
a historical individual but as the central figure of Rome's **myth-history** — a story the
Romans "never ceased to puzzle over" and through which they debated the nature of their own
community, its violence, and its openness. See [[sources/beard-spqr-2015]].

## The Story and Its Meanings

In the canonical version (fullest in Livy), the twins are the abandoned sons of a priestess
and (allegedly) the god Mars, suckled by a she-wolf, raised by a shepherd, and returned to
found a city. In the quarrel over which hill to found it on and after whom to name it,
**Romulus killed Remus** — so that Rome was founded, as Beard emphasises, on an act of
**fratricide**, a fact Roman writers themselves found troubling and repeatedly moralised
about. Needing inhabitants, Romulus declared Rome an **asylum** open to runaways and
outcasts, and secured wives for them by abducting the women of the neighbouring **Sabines**
(the "rape of the Sabine women"), a conflict resolved by merging Romans and Sabines into one
community under Romulus and the Sabine king Titus Tatius.

## Significance (Analytical)

Beard reads the foundation legend as Romans' own theory of Roman identity. Its recurring
motifs encode enduring Roman self-understandings: the city as an **open, composite
community** built from outsiders and absorbed enemies — an origin myth of inclusiveness that
prefigures Rome's later habit of extending citizenship (see [[concepts/roman-citizenship]]);
and the city as born in **violence and civil bloodshed**, a theme Romans invoked to make
sense of their own recurrent civil wars. The variant versions (two founders vs. one, the
several candidate founders before Romulus) show a living tradition still being argued over in
Cicero's and Livy's day.

Beard adds a philological twist: the name "Romulus" is almost certainly **backwards** from the
Roman assumption. Rather than the city being named for its founder, "Romulus" was "an
imaginative construction out of 'Roma'" — an invented archetypal "Mr Rome" retro-fitted as
founder. Romans themselves could mock their origins in "slaves and runaways"; Cicero joked to
Atticus about being lost in "the *faex* (crap) of Romulus."

## Historiography

There are no contemporary sources for the eighth century BCE; the developed Romulus narrative
is a much later literary and antiquarian construction, and Beard treats it accordingly — as
evidence for how Romans thought about themselves, not as a record of events. Archaeology
attests early Iron Age settlement on the site of Rome, but nothing that confirms the persons
or episodes of the legend. Cf. [[actors/roman-kingdom]], [[events/founding-of-rome-753bce]].
