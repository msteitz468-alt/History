# The Successors of Justinian (Justin II to Maurice)

The three emperors who reigned between **Justinian's death (565)** and the catastrophe of **602** —
**Justin II**, **Tiberius II Constantine**, and **Maurice** — across whose reigns the costs of Justinian's
overreach came due: Italy was largely lost to the **Lombards**, the Balkans to the **Avars and Slavs**, and
the empire was locked in renewed war with **Persia**. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 4
(**Averil Cameron**) and Ch. 14 (Whitby). The volume ends c. 600 — on the eve, though its authors could not
quite say so, of the seventh-century collapse.

## Justin II (565–578): retrenchment and disaster

Justinian's nephew **Justin II** repudiated the costly system of **diplomatic subsidies** by which Justinian
had bought off the empire's enemies — a proud reassertion of Roman dignity that proved ruinous. The
withdrawn payments and the dynastic upheavals of the steppe set the **Avars** loose: the **Lombards**,
fearing their former Avar allies after the destruction of the Gepids, **[[lombard-invasion-568|migrated into Italy in 568]]** under
**[[alboin|Alboin]]**, overrunning the peninsula that Justinian's **[[gothic-war-535-554|Gothic War]]** had just exhausted; the Avars seized
**Pannonia** and (582) the key fortress of **Sirmium**. Justin's defiance of **Khusro I** reopened the
**Persian war** (from 572); the fall of **Dara (573)** broke his mind, and he lapsed into insanity. His
wife **Sophia** and the *comes excubitorum* **Tiberius** governed in his stead.

## Tiberius II Constantine (578–582)

Proclaimed Caesar (574) and then Augustus, **Tiberius II** restored the subsidies and lavished money on
the army and on largesse — generosity that won popularity but strained the treasury Anastasius and Justin
had built. He prosecuted the Persian war through able generals (notably **Maurice**), whom he designated
his successor.

## Maurice (582–602): the soldier-emperor and the catastrophe

**Maurice**, an experienced general, was the ablest of the three — credited (probably rightly) with the
military manual the ***Strategikon***. He **ended the Persian war victoriously (591)** by restoring the
fugitive **Khusro II** to the Sasanid throne, gaining favourable terms and even territory in Armenia. Freed
in the east, he campaigned hard to **stabilize the Balkans** against Avars and Slavs. But chronic financial
strain forced unpopular economies: when he ordered the Danube army to **winter beyond the river (602)** to
save money, it **mutinied** under the officer **Phocas**, marched on Constantinople, and **murdered Maurice
and his sons.** The usurpation of Phocas gave Khusro II his pretext for the **great Persian war** that —
with the **Arab conquests** soon after — would end the ancient Mediterranean order.

## Significance

These reigns mark the **exhaustion of Justinian's enterprise**: an over-extended empire could not hold the
reconquered west, defend the Danube, and fight Persia at once. Maurice's murder is the hinge to the
seventh-century crisis — the point at which CAH XIV closes and "antiquity" gives way. The volume's terminus
c. 600 is chosen precisely here, before the Persian and Arab wars made the transformation irreversible.
