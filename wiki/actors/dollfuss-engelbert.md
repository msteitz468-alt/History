## Overview

Engelbert Dollfuss (4 ft. 11 in.) led the Christian Social dictatorship of Austria from
1933 until his murder in a failed Nazi coup on July 25, 1934. His regime combined
Catholic corporatist authoritarianism — explicitly modeled on Pope Pius XI's 1931
encyclical *Quadragesimo Anno* — with a foreign policy of independence from Germany,
backed by Mussolini's Italy and Gyula Gömbös's Hungary.

## Role and Major Decisions

- Dissolved Austria's deadlocked parliament in March 1933 and authorized only one party,
  the Fatherland Front.
- Restricted the Social Democratic city government of Vienna and socialist press and
  organizations; restored the death penalty (abolished 1919).
- Promulgated the Constitution of May 1934, replacing "exaggerated parliamentarianism"
  with mostly-appointed corporatist councils, formalizing the *Ständestaat* ("corporate
  state").
- Ordered military suppression of the Social Democratic Schutzbund after Heimwehr units
  provoked a general strike: artillery fire on the Karl-Marx-Hof apartment complex on
  February 12, 1934, killed 193 civilians and 128 police/soldiers, outlawing Social
  Democratic organizations.
- Forbade Nazi Party activity in Austria; negotiated an alliance with Mussolini and Gömbös
  in March 1934 explicitly to block Anschluss.
- Killed on July 25, 1934, when a Nazi band seized the Vienna chancellery and radio
  stations in a botched putsch — left to bleed to death on his office sofa. Succeeded by
  Kurt Schuschnigg, who continued the same balancing policy until the 1938 Anschluss.

## Counterfactual Significance

Paxton and Hessler judge that Dollfuss and Schuschnigg's clerical-authoritarian Austria,
backed by Mussolini's mobilization at the Brenner Pass in July 1934, "dealt Hitler a far
more striking blow in 1934 than did any liberal state through the 1930s" — the only
concrete reversal Hitler suffered before 1943. Had Mussolini not shifted alignment toward
Germany after the Ethiopian war (1935–36), Austrian independence might have persisted
longer; Dollfuss's own suppression of the Social Democrats, however, had already removed
the domestic political force most implacably opposed to any accommodation with Vienna's
Nazis, weakening Austria's internal resistance to the [[anschluss-1938]] four years after
his death.

## Historiography

Dollfuss's regime is conventionally distinguished from fascism proper: it governed
through existing elites (Church, Heimwehr, traditional Catholic notables) rather than
through a mobilizing single party, and — unlike Nazism or Italian Fascism — did not seek
mass enthusiasm. Klemens von Klemperer's *Ignaz Seipel* (1972) covers the Christian Social
tradition Dollfuss inherited; Martin Kitchen's *The Coming of Austrian Fascism* (1980) and
Francis L. Carsten's *Fascist Movements in Austria from Schönerer to Hitler* (1977) give
background; Anson Rabinbach's *The Crisis of Austrian Socialism* (1983) asks why the
Social Democrats were so easily crushed in February 1934.
