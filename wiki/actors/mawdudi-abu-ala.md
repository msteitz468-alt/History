## Overview

Abu A'la Mawdudi (1903–1979) was the most influential South Asian Islamist ideologue of the 20th century, founder of [[jamaat-i-islami]] and theorist of the Islamic state grounded in God's sovereignty (hakimiyya). Formichi (2020) treats him as a key node in the transregional history of political Islam: a South Asian thinker whose concepts flowed outward to reshape Arab Islamism, reversing the usual assumption that ideas travel from the Middle East to Asia's periphery.

## Role and Key Developments

Mawdudi opposed both the Indian National Congress's secularism and the [[muslim-league]]'s Westernized-elite, nation-state-based demand for Pakistan, which he deemed in itself an "un-Islamic" idea — his vision was a transnational Islamic order governed by divine sovereignty, not an ethno-religious nation-state. He held that women should be valued as mothers, wives, and sisters but excluded from public, political, and professional life.

After Jinnah's death, Mawdudi nonetheless promoted [[pakistan]]'s 1949 Objectives Resolution, which declared that sovereignty belongs to Allah alone but is delegated to the state — a formula bearing his theoretical stamp even though it was embedded in the nation-state framework he had originally rejected. Following Ayub Khan's 1958 restrictions on the Jamaat-i-Islami, Mawdudi's party pragmatically allied with secular parties (a Muslim League faction, the Awami League, and a Marxist party) to advance Islamist influence through democratic means.

Mawdudi's concepts — hakimiyya (God's sovereignty), jahiliyya (recast as a description of modern un-Islamic society), and jihad as legitimate political-military conflict — directly influenced Sayyid Qutb of the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood after the two met in 1951 in Ta'if, mediated by Sayyid Abu Hasan Nadwi. Qutb's later execution by Nasser's regime, and subsequent internal Muslim Brotherhood criticism of his radicalism (by al-Hudaybi), targeted Mawdudi's ideas by proxy. Takfir (declaring fellow Muslims infidels) and the political redeployment of jahiliyya were, per Formichi, devised as 20th-century political strategies specifically by Mawdudi and Qutb together.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the 1951 Mawdudi–Qutb meeting, the intellectual architecture of global political Islam — hakimiyya, jahiliyya, and takfir as tools of Islamist mobilization — would likely have developed along a different, more geographically confined trajectory; Mawdudi's South Asian theorizing was foundational to, not derivative of, Arab Islamism.

## Historiography

Formichi's account deliberately reverses the usual Mecca/Cairo-to-periphery diffusion model, positioning Mawdudi as an originator whose ideas moved from South Asia into the Arab Islamist mainstream via Qutb — a central illustration of the book's broader thesis that Asian Muslims have shaped, not merely received, global Islamic thought.
