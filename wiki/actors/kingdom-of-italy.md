# The Kingdom of Italy (Regnum Italiae), 888–1024

The **regnum Italiae** — the former Lombard-Carolingian kingdom of northern and central Italy, capital **Pavia** — passed in the long tenth century through a chaotic age of "national" kings before being absorbed into the [[ottonian-dynasty|Ottonian]] empire. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Giuseppe Sergi) reads its history not as a story of failed "order" but as the **fragmentation of a Carolingian framework into autonomous regional powers** that the great marches (Friuli, Ivrea, Tuscany, Spoleto) and the magnates contrived to keep weak.

## The "National" Kings (888–961)

After Charles the Fat's deposition, the crown was fought over by margraves drawn from the old Frankish marcher aristocracy, all loosely Carolingian-descended:

- **Berengar I** of Friuli and **Wido (Guy) of Spoleto** — rival kings from 888; Wido and his son **Lambert** won the imperial crown (891) through good relations with Rome. Berengar, based at Verona, outlasted them and was finally crowned **emperor c. 915** (after coordinating the destruction of the Arab base on the **Garigliano**), but was killed in 924 after the bloody battle of **Fiorenzuola d'Arda (923)** against **Rudolf II of Burgundy** — a battle that "decimated Italy's aristocracy."
- **Hugh of Arles (Provence)** (r. 926–947): the most effective of the national kings. Sergi shows him pursuing an **anachronistic re-centralisation** — favouring the great marches while packing them with his own kin and followers (blinding and deposing rivals), and curbing the drift of power to local families. His inflexibility provoked the aristocracy he had been invited to arbitrate.
- **Berengar II of Ivrea** (r. 950–961): Hugh's miscalculation. Exiled to Germany, he allied with **[[otto-i|Otto I]]**, returned, seized the throne, and imprisoned the dowager queen **Adelaide** — provoking Otto's intervention.

## Ottonian Conquest

Otto I conquered the kingdom (951, definitively 961), married **Adelaide**, deposed Berengar II, and was crowned **emperor in [[coronation-of-otto-i-962|962]]**. Thereafter the *regnum Italiae* became a **distinct but subordinate part of the Reich**: the Ottonians and Henry II ruled it as frequent absentees through a policy of **balancing the margraves, counts, and bishops** and granting privileges (immunities, fortification rights, *districtus*) to the episcopal cities. Regnal dues (the *fodrum*) continued to be collected at Pavia. Arduin of Ivrea's brief anti-imperial kingship (1002) was suppressed by [[henry-ii-holy-roman-emperor|Henry II]].

## Government: Carolingian Forms, Fragmenting Reality

Sergi stresses **continuity of forms with dissolving substance**. Pavia's chancery issued capitularies until 898 and thereafter only diplomata; the realm kept its Carolingian subdivision into marches, counties, and minor districts. But underneath, public authority devolved: free smallholders (the ***arimanni***, descendants of the Carolingian *liberi homines*, not an ethnic Lombard residue) commended themselves and their lands to great landowners for protection; the **curtes** grew; and the crown stopped mobilising free men in favour of vassals and lords. **Immunity** evolved from mere exemption into the positive exercise of *districtus* (justice, punishment, troop-levy), granted ever further down the hierarchy — to minor churches, a parish priest at Voghera, even twenty-nine laymen licensed to build a castle. This is the documentary face of [[encellulement|incastellamento]] and the [[feudal-revolution-debate|"feudal" fragmentation]] in its Italian form.

## The Rise of the Communes (11th–12th c.) (NCMH IV)

[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Giovanni Tabacco, Chs 4a and 15a) carries the story
into the eleventh and twelfth centuries, when the dissolution of comital and episcopal
public power and the wealth of the [[commercial-revolution|commercial revival]] gave
birth to the self-governing **[[commune-movement|city-communes]]** — Milan, Genoa, Pisa,
Cremona, Bologna, Florence and the rest. The kingdom of Italy became, in effect, a
patchwork of autonomous urban republics under a nominal and distant imperial crown. When
**[[frederick-i-barbarossa|Frederick Barbarossa]]** tried to make that crown real
(Roncaglia 1158; the destruction of Milan 1162), the cities formed the
**[[lombard-league|Lombard League]]**, defeated him at **[[battle-of-legnano-1176|Legnano
(1176)]]**, and won recognition of their self-government at the **Peace of Constance
(1183)** — confirming northern Italy's distinctive character as a land of city-republics.

## Significance

The kingdom of Italy is the laboratory of post-Carolingian fragmentation: a realm where no central power could re-form, the marcher dynasties and episcopal cities engrossed public rights, and the only durable solution proved to be incorporation into the trans-Alpine empire — fixing the **fateful German-Italian imperial link** and leaving northern Italy a patchwork of episcopal cities that would become the **communes** of the High Middle Ages.
