# Ashur-uballit I (Assyrian king)

"The restorer of the power of Assyria" — the king under whom the city-state of
**Ashur**, for three centuries a vassal squeezed between [[kassites|Kassite
Babylonia]] and [[mitanni|Hurrian Mitanni]], threw off foreign domination and
became one of the **Late Bronze great powers**. He is the effective founder of the
**[[assyria-middle|Middle Assyrian kingdom]]**. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]] (Ch. XVIII, Gadd). Reign conventionally
~1365–1330 BCE.

## Out of the shadow of Mitanni

For nearly three centuries — since Šamši-Adad I and Hammurabi — Assyria had "little
to tell": an unbroken line of thirty-six shadow-kings who left "no more than a few
records of local building," ruling as **vassals of the Hurrian kings of Mitanni**,
who controlled the country "far and wide around the city on the Tigris" (the
documents of Hurrian **Nuzi/Arrapkha**, acknowledging king Saustatar of Mitanni,
show how completely). The turning point was **external**: the
[[shuppiluliuma-i|Hittite]] destruction of Mitanni and the **murder of Tushratta**.
As Mitanni collapsed, its desperate rival kings bought support by handing Assyria
treasure, princes and territory — and Ashur-uballit "cast off allegiance" and
"scattered the hosts of the widespread Subarians."

## The new great king

His own most revealing relics are **two of the [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]]**
to Egypt. The first is modest — "to the king of Egypt from Ashur-uballit, king of
Assyria" — sending a fine chariot, horses and lapis to "make contact." The second,
written later, styles him "**the great king, your brother**," demands gold ("gold in
your land is dust, they pick it up") for a new palace, and pointedly recalls that
his predecessors had been treated as equals of "the Khanigalbatian king." This
self-promotion to **great-king status** provoked an indignant protest from the
Kassite **Burnaburiaš II** — "the Assyrians, subjects of mine... why have they come
to your country?... send them away empty" — but the pharaoh "was too well advised to
ignore reality." It is the formal birth of the **Assyria–Babylonia rivalry** that
dominates the rest of the second and first millennia.

## The intervention in Babylon

Ashur-uballit married his daughter **Muballiṭat-Šerua** to the king of Babylon; her
son **Kadashman-Harbe** took the throne. When Kassite factions murdered the young
king and raised a commoner usurper (Nazi-Bugaš), Ashur-uballit "marched forthwith
into Babylonia, defeated and slew the usurper," and installed his great-grandson
**Kurigalzu II** — making the Assyrian king a **king-maker in Babylon**. His
successors (his son **Enlil-nirari**, then **Arik-den-ili**) fought the Kassites and
the Zagros hillmen indecisively, the rivalry running on to the decisive victory of
Tukulti-Ninurta I a century later.

## Significance

Ashur-uballit converted Assyria from a buffer-vassal into a **territorial great
power**, the eastern hinge of the Amarna-age system, and set in motion the
**Assyria–Babylonia antagonism**. His reign also illustrates a structural lesson the
volume draws out: the rise of Assyria was an **unintended consequence** of
[[shuppiluliuma-i|Šuppiluliuma's]] destruction of the Mitannian buffer — "unwished
for and of little consequence at the moment." He is the personal origin-point of the
[[assyria-middle|Middle Assyrian kingdom]] that culminates in
Tiglath-pileser I.

## Historiography

Reconstructed "incidentally from the history of Mitanni" and from a handful of
sources of very different kinds: his own terse building inscriptions, the
boastful **Amarna letters**, the later **Synchronistic History** and Babylonian
**Chronicle P** (which disagree on the Kassite king-list and the order of the
Babylonian wars), and a retrospective notice by his great-grandson. The disagreement
of the two chronicles is itself a classic source-reliability problem of Mesopotamian
historiography. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
