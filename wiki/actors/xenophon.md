# Xenophon

## Overview

**Xenophon of Athens** (c. 430–c. 354 BCE) was a soldier, country gentleman, and
prolific writer whose *Hellenica* is **the only surviving continuous narrative of
Greek history for the years 411–362** — and therefore the indispensable, if
frustrating, backbone of the fourth century. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 1,
Simon Hornblower) opens with the blunt judgment that "**no guide comparable to
[[thucydides|Thucydides]] exists for the fourth century**," and the historian we have
instead is Xenophon: a writer of "glaring faults when judged as a political reporter,"
yet "a prime source for the modern historian of religion," society and Sparta.

## Life

An Athenian of the propertied class and an associate of **Socrates**, Xenophon joined
the mercenary army that **[[cyrus-the-younger|Cyrus the Younger]]** raised to seize the
Persian throne; after Cyrus fell at **Cunaxa (401)** and the Greek generals were
treacherously killed, Xenophon helped lead the **[[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|Ten
Thousand]]** back to the sea — the experience of his *Anabasis*. He then served under
the Spartan king **[[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus II]]**, his lifelong benefactor and hero,
was **exiled from Athens** (probably for fighting on the Spartan side against his own
city at Coronea in 394), and was settled by Sparta on an estate at **Scillus** near
Olympia, where he wrote. He died around 354.

## The historian and his bias

Hornblower treats Xenophon's qualities with care. With **Part Two** of the *Hellenica*
(from ii.3.11) the work becomes "parochially concentrated on Peloponnesian affairs":
Xenophon is interested in Asia "only when there is Spartan activity there," and
**systematically under-reports** what discredits Sparta — Theban penetration of
Thessaly, the battle of **Tegyra (375)**, the foundation of the
[[second-athenian-confederacy|Second Athenian Confederacy]] (which he never records),
and above all **Persian involvement** in the common peaces, to downplay Spartan
"medizing."

But "**bias**," Hornblower insists, is a slippery term. Xenophon was *overwhelmingly
interested* in Sparta, which is not the same as partisan falsification; and he could
censure the Spartans for impiety, explaining their defeat at **Leuctra (371)** as the
gods' punishment for their seizure of the Theban acropolis in 383. His real character
is that of "**an explicit moralist**" and "an impressionist with a singular gift for
vivid description" rather than a pedantically accurate reporter. Where Xenophon
falsifies — as in concealing that Spartans came to Athens as suppliants in 370/69 — it
is from a recoil at depicting Spartans "clutching the begging-bowl," a point where
[[fourth-century-greek-historiography|the rival tradition]] of **Callisthenes** and
**Ephorus/Diodorus** must be preferred.

## The range of the writings

Xenophon is the inventor or early master of several prose genres, making him a key
witness for social, military, religious and economic history:
- **Historical:** the *Hellenica* (continuing Thucydides) and the *Anabasis* (the
  closest thing to a Greek "inside source" on the Persian empire).
- **Biographical/encomiastic:** the *Agesilaus* — "the first surviving Greek essay in
  biography" — and the *Constitution of the Spartans* (*Lac. Pol.*), an institutional
  encomium that is a prime source on the Spartan system.
- **Technical and didactic:** the *Cyropaedia* ("Education of Cyrus," a precursor of
  later treatises "On Kingship"), the *Hipparchicus* on cavalry command, the *Poroi*
  (*Ways and Means*) on Athenian revenues and the **Laurium** mines, and the
  *Oeconomicus* on estate management (see [[greek-agriculture]]).

## The Primary Text (now ingested)

His masterpiece is now ingested as **[[xenophon-expedition-of-cyrus|*The Expedition of Cyrus*
(*Anabasis*)]]** (Waterfield trans., OWC; intro/notes Tim Rood) — the **first known military memoir**,
yet one that hides its author: Xenophon narrates his own deeds in the **third person** and, the
*Hellenica* implies, behind the alias **"Themistogenes of Syracuse"** (read by Plutarch as a
pseudonym to soften his self-praise). Rood surveys the debate over its purpose — **self-defence**
(apologia for his command), a riposte to a rival account, and/or **panhellenist propaganda**
advertising Persian vulnerability and Xenophon's own fitness to lead an invasion of Asia. Beneath the
"pretentiously simple" surface (T. E. Lawrence) it is a vivid study of a Greek army as a mobile
*polis* — electing generals, debating in assembly, sacrificing and reading omens — and the source of
the proverbial cry **"Thalatta! Thalatta!"**

## Significance

Without Xenophon there would be no narrative thread for the early fourth century at all.
His limitations forced the discipline that defines [[fourth-century-greek-historiography|
the modern study of the period]]: read the *Hellenica* against the inscriptions, against
**Diodorus** (drawing on Ephorus and the *Hellenica Oxyrhynchia*), and against the local
**Atthidographers** like Philochorus, and trust no single thread alone.
