# John C. Calhoun (1782–1850)

South Carolina statesman — war hawk congressman, secretary of war, vice
president (1825–1832), senator — and the antebellum South's leading
political theorist: author of the compact theory of state sovereignty,
nullification, and the "concurrent majority" (*Disquisition on
Government*, posthumous 1851). Central figure of what
[[hartz-louis|Hartz]] named the **Reactionary Enlightenment** — the
South's attempt to build a genuine European-style conservatism in defense
of slavery.

## Hartz's analysis: "a profoundly disintegrated political theorist"

Calhoun grounds government in force and "Divine ordination" (Maistre-style)
— yet builds on top of it a hyper-rationalist constitutional apparatus
(compact, nullification, concurrent majority) that is "about as far away
as you can get from the spirit of 'Divine ordination,'" pinning the
Constitution's authority to the rationalist moment of 1787 exactly as
Condorcet had, leaving "nothing to tradition, nothing to force, and
nothing to God." The concurrent majority unravels into pure Lockean
individualism once minorities-within-minorities are pressed (Legaré's
objection): "When Locke accepted majority rule... he accepted more force
in politics than Calhoun, the great theorist of force and slavery, was
ready to accept." His attempted escape in the *Disquisition* —
distinguishing "government" (force/Providence, like slavery) from
"constitution" (rational, controlling) — is "a man carefully placing a
match on top of a stick of dynamite": any contact between the categories
would validate Northern coercion of the South. Defending the South in the
tariff fight, he reached for minority-rights compact theory that is pure
Locke — the inherited liberalism he could not shed (see
[[lockean-consensus]]).

**Ironic afterlife.** The only Calhoun doctrine that survived 1865 was the
most Lockean one — minority rights / the concurrent majority, read as an
ancestor of pressure-group pluralism — "we scarcely realize that they make
a logical farce out of his whole Enlightenment structure."

## Historiography

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century admirers treated Calhoun as
America's one philosopher-statesman; Hartz's demolition (above) set the
consensus-era verdict. Later scholarship splits between rehabilitation as
a serious theorist of minority protection and readings centering the
proslavery core (his "positive good" defense of slavery, 1837). His role
as a *contributing* cause of sectional crisis is itself debated:
nullification and the concurrent majority were designed to *avert*
rupture; [[fitzhugh-george]]'s organic nationality pointed toward it. See
[[american-civil-war-1861-1865]].
