## Role

Ptolemy "Ceraunus" ("the Thunderbolt," c. 319–279 BCE) was the eldest son of
[[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]] by Eurydice, **disinherited** in favour of his
half-brother Ptolemy II when their father repudiated Eurydice for Berenice. His brief,
violent career is the link between the death of the last Diadochi and the Celtic
catastrophe in Macedon (CAH VII.1, Will, ch. 4).

## Decisions and Significance

Excluded from the Egyptian succession, Ceraunus attached himself to the court of
[[lysimachus]] (where his sister Lysandra was married to the heir Agathocles), and
helped poison relations there — part of the "vipers' knot" of Egyptian marriages
that culminated in Lysimachus' murder of his own son Agathocles. Ceraunus and Lysandra
then fled to **[[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus]]** and urged him to war; Seleucus
crushed and killed Lysimachus at **[[battle-of-corupedium-281|Corupedium]]** (281).

Then came his defining act: as Seleucus crossed into Europe to claim Macedon, Ceraunus
**assassinated his own benefactor with his own hand** (281), fled to Lysimacheia, and —
posing as the avenger of Lysimachus — had himself acclaimed **king of Macedon** by the
army. He commanded Lysimachus' fleet, defeated [[antigonus-ii-gonatas|Gonatas]], and
married (then murdered the sons of) his half-sister [[arsinoe-ii|Arsinoe II]].

## Significance

Ceraunus' reign was a disaster waiting to happen: ignorant of the frontier diplomacy by
which Lysimachus had held the northern barbarians, he failed to come to terms with the
Dardanians and so opened Macedon to the **[[celtic-invasion-of-greece-279|Gauls]]**.
Forced to fight them in the open (early 279), his army was destroyed and he was killed.
His death left Macedon in anarchy until [[antigonus-ii-gonatas|Gonatas]] secured it. He
is a vivid instance of the period's lesson that seizing a throne and holding it were
very different things. See [[battle-of-corupedium-281]], [[celtic-invasion-of-greece-279]].
