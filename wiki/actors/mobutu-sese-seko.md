# Mobutu Sese Seko

## Overview
A former army sergeant, Mobutu was promoted to colonel and army chief of staff after the July 1960 mutiny that followed Congo's independence. On September 7, 1960 he proposed a coup to CIA station chief Larry Devlin and received $5,000 the next day; he executed a bloodless coup on September 14, 1960, ousting [[lumumba-patrice|Patrice Lumumba]] while keeping Kasavubu as nominal president. With sustained CIA backing (Devlin dined with him twice weekly through the "Binza group"), Mobutu presided over the reintegration of the Katanga and Kasai secessions (completed by January 1963) and the suppression of the Simba rebellion (1964–65), which drew on white mercenaries and a CIA-recruited Cuban-exile air force of 56 aircraft.

He seized full power again in November 1965 — Devlin called it "the best possible solution" — and hanged four coup-plotting former ministers before 50,000 spectators in May 1966. He nationalized the mining company Union Minière in January 1967 and had rival Moïse Tshombe kidnapped via a hijacking operation (organized by French intelligence under Jacques Foccart) in June 1967; Tshombe died in an Algerian prison in 1969.

## Key Decisions and Consequences
- Accepting CIA backing for the September 1960 coup against Lumumba set the pattern for his entire 37-year rule as a Cold War client.
- His consolidation of power (1965) and subsequent kleptocratic rule — embezzling roughly half of state revenue and stashing an estimated $6 billion overseas (McCoy's figure), while receiving $150 million in secret CIA payments, $230 million in military aid, and $1 billion in development funds over the Cold War (McCoy's figures) — drove per capita GDP from $600 (1978) to $135 (1997).
- Deposed by Laurent Kabila's rebel coalition in the First Congo War (1996–97); died of cancer in exile.

## Counterfactual Significance
Mobutu's three-decade rule illustrates, in McCoy's framing, the long-run cost of Cold War client-state stability: a regime propped up for anti-communist reliability produced a kleptocracy whose collapse triggered the deadliest conflict since World War II (the Second Congo War, 1998–2003, an estimated 5.4 million dead per UN sources cited by McCoy). Whether that outcome is properly attributable to the original 1960 CIA intervention, or to Mobutu's own choices and later regional dynamics, is McCoy's interpretive thesis rather than settled consensus; see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]].
