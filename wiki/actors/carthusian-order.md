# Carthusian Order (La Grande Chartreuse)

The **Carthusians**, founded in **1109** and settled at **La Grande Chartreuse** after an avalanche displaced an earlier site, were the twelfth century’s most uncompromising **hermit-monks**: small intentional communities of solitaries in individual cells with gardens, gathering for communal Sunday Mass and meal and for night offices. [[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] treats them as the exception that proves the rule of later twelfth-century diversion from early ideals.

## Structure and Ideal

Unlike the large, federated [[cistercian-order|Cistercian]] system or the parish-serving [[augustinian-canons|Augustinian canons]], Carthusian life combined **eremitic solitude** with limited common liturgy. Growth was **deliberately slow**: about **200 houses by the sixteenth century**, only **nine in England**. The famous tag — ***nunquam reformata quia nunquam deformata*** (“never reformed, because never deformed”) — encapsulates their reputation for unbroken austerity.

## Significance

Logan’s twelfth-century survey concludes that nearly all new orders (Cistercians, Augustinians, Premonstratensians, Fontevrault, Sempringham) had **diverted from early intentions** by century’s end — “**saving always the Carthusians**.” They thus serve as a historiographical baseline for measuring how wealth, aristocratic capture, and institutional comfort transformed other reform orders.
