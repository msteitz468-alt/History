# The Etruscans

The **Etruscans** were the first people of Italy (outside the Greek colonies) to
build **cities and city-states**, the dominant culture of the central Tyrrhenian
peninsula from the eighth to the fifth century BCE, and the catalyst that turned
the village of [[rome|Rome]] under the Tarquins into a city.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 12, Italy from the Bronze to the Iron
Age, and Ch. 13, David Ridgway, *The Etruscans*) treats them as a civilization
that emerged **in situ** from the local Iron Age and was then transformed by
**Greek and oriental contact** — not a "nation arriving *en bloc*" from
elsewhere, the old origins theory Ridgway firmly rejects.

## Who they were: the Twelve-Peoples League, not an empire

The Etruscans were never a unified state. They formed a loose religious-political
confederation — the **League of the Twelve Peoples** that met at the *fanum
Voltumnae*, the sanctuary of Voltumna — of **independent and often rival
city-states**: chiefly **Caere** (with its port [[etruria|Pyrgi]]), **Tarquinia**
(port Graviscae), **Vulci**, **Veii**, **Orvieto** (Volsinii), **Chiusi**,
**Vetulonia**, **Populonia**, **Volterra** and **Arezzo**. Because "Etruria had
no central organization that ever felt able to concert a unified action," Ridgway
insists the term **"Etruscan Empire" is a misnomer**; Cato's claim that "almost
all Italy had once been under Etruscan sway" recognizes real expansion but no
single polity. This structural disunity is the deep cause of their eventual
absorption by a unified Rome (compare the same vulnerability in the
[[magna-graecia|Greek west]]).

## From Villanovan to Etruscan: an indigenous origin

The earliest Iron Age culture of Etruria, named **"Villanovan"** (after a cemetery
near Bologna excavated by Gozzadini from c. 1853), grew directly out of the Late
Bronze Age **Proto-Villanovan** horizon — "the Etruscans in their embryonic Final
Bronze Age stage." Ridgway's settled view, against both Herodotus' Lydian-migration
story and the "autochthonous" extreme, is that the Etruscan civilization **formed
on Italian soil** out of this Villanovan base, then was rapidly elaborated by
external contact. Its writing, art and city-form are borrowed; its **substrate
population is local**.

## The Orientalizing transformation (c. 700–580)

The decisive change came through the **Euboean Greek emporion at
[[pithecusae|Pithecusa]] (Ischia)** and **Cyme**, the conduit by which Greek and
Near Eastern goods, techniques and craftsmen reached central Italy:

- **Imported craftsmen.** "Migrations of skilled men" brought fully-developed
  oriental techniques — gold- and silver-smithing, **ivory-working** (probably
  Syrian craftsmen settling in south Etruria/Latium c. 675), monumental sculpture,
  wall-painting and architecture — that local apprentices then absorbed,
  "becoming steadily less oriental in character." The story of **Demaratus of
  Corinth**, the Bacchiad exile who settled at Tarquinia c. 656 with his
  craftsmen Eucheir, Eugrammos and Diopos, symbolizes this to an "extraordinary
  degree of accuracy."
- **Bucchero**, the glossy black ware perfected at Caere, is "the only product
  that can be described as typically and exclusively Etruscan."
- **Princely tombs** (the Regolini-Galassi at Caere, the Tomba del Duce at
  Vetulonia, and counterparts in Latium and Campania — Praeneste, Cyme,
  Pontecagnano) treated their dead as **heroes**, drawing on the imaginary Greece
  of the epics (the émigré painter Aristonothos painted the blinding of
  Polyphemus at Caere).
- **Greek loan-words** for vases (*lextum-* from *lekythos*, *eleivana* from
  *elaion*) and inscriptions like that of the Etruscanized Greek **Larth Telicles**
  attest a brisk, literate **oil-and-wine business**: by the late seventh century
  Etruria was a net **exporter** of wine (a wreck off Antibes carried 170 Etruscan
  amphoras).

The transition from flimsy huts to permanent tiled houses marks the
**village-to-city** step — reached in Italy first by Etruria, "whence it passed
first to the Rome of the Tarquins and later to native Campania and the Po Valley."

## The Archaic city-state (c. 580–480)

By the mid-sixth century a **new merchant-and-landowner class** had displaced the
old Orientalizing princes: monumental tumuli give way to **regularly planned
streets of modest single-family chambers** (the Crocefisso del Tufo cemetery at
Orvieto records ninety families, 550–500), and orthogonal **town-planning** spreads
(Marzabotto, Spina). Vast quantities of **Attic pottery** flowed in — 75% of all
provenanced Attic pottery of the late sixth century comes from Etruria; Vulci alone
yielded 1,700 vases in Beazley's indices. The flight of **Ionians** after the
Persian conquest (post-546) brought refugee East-Greek craftsmen and an "Ionian"
phase of Etruscan art (the Caeretan hydriae, the Apollo of Veii by **Vulca**, the
only Etruscan artist known by name). The port-sanctuary of **Graviscae** housed a
resident Greek trading community worshipping Aphrodite, Hera, Apollo and Demeter
(the Aeginetan **Sostratos** dedicated a stone anchor there). Tyrants like **Lars
Porsenna** of Chiusi and **Thefarie Velianas** of Caere (of the bilingual
Etrusco-Phoenician **Pyrgi gold tablets**, c. 500–480) mark the same upheavals
that the Greek and Ionian cities experienced.

## Expansion and the Tarquins of Rome

Etruscan prosperity spilled beyond Etruria in two directions:

- **South to Campania**, where Capua (Etruscan Volturnus) headed a second Twelve.
  Control of the land-route across Latium was eased by the **Etruscan dynasty at
  [[rome|Rome]]**: Lucius **Tarquinius Priscus** (son of Demaratus), **Servius
  Tullius** and Tarquinius **Superbus** brought town-planning, the great temple of
  Jupiter, and city defences. Ridgway's verdict: *"The Etruscans did not found
  Rome: the Tarquins turned it into a city."*
- **North across the Apennines to the Po Valley**, where a third twelve of cities
  (chiefly **Felsina**/Bologna) and the great Adriatic emporia **Spina** and
  **Adria** handled Greek trade with central Europe.

## Decline (from c. 509)

The losses came simultaneously on every front. At sea, the **battle of the
Sardinian Sea (Alalia), c. 540**, though a Caeretan–Carthaginian victory over the
Phocaeans, was "expensive," and the Etruscan **thalassocracy never recouped it**;
their Carthaginian allies, not they, profited. On land, the **expulsion of the
Tarquins from Rome in 509** and the fading of Etruscan influence there by 475 cut
the Campanian land-route; a bid to take **Cyme failed disastrously in 474** at the
**[[battle-of-cumae-474bce|battle of Cumae]]**, when [[hiero-i|Hiero's]] Syracusan fleet
crushed the Etruscans (the helmet dedicated at Olympia) — and democratic Syracuse pressed the
advantage with raids on **Elba and Corsica** in 453 ([[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH V]], Ch. 7).
Unlike Carthage after [[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]] (480), "the Etruscans
were in no state to fight another day." The **Samnites** took Campania (mid-5th c.),
the **Celts** drove them from the Po Valley (early 4th c.), and Rome's inexorable
advance culminated in the **destruction of [[rome|Veii]] in 396**. "There is no
'Classical period' of Etruscan art": their two identifying marks — **language and
dress** — were slowly eliminated by Roman absorption.

## Causal Analysis

origin); the **Orientalizing** contact funnelled through Euboean
[[pithecusae|Pithecusa]] and Cyme.

Campania and the Po Valley; the alphabet of much of ancient Italy (see
[[languages-of-italy]]); a major node of the Archaic
[[trade-network-formation|Mediterranean trade network]].
contributed_to: their own fall — **structural disunity** (no central organization)
against a unified Rome and pressure from Greeks, Carthaginians and Celts at once.

## Historiography

The evidence is overwhelmingly **archaeological and funerary** (tombs, cemeteries,
imported pottery) plus c. 10,000 mostly short **Etruscan inscriptions** (see
[[languages-of-italy]]) — there is no surviving Etruscan literature, and the
Greco-Roman sources (Herodotus, Livy, Dionysius) are external and often legendary.
The great twentieth-century revision, summarized by Ridgway, **rejected the
"arrival of a nation *en bloc*"** in favour of in-situ formation plus
acculturation, and reassessed **Pithecusa's** central role following its
excavation. The reliability of the Roman tradition on the Tarquins is partly
**validated archaeologically** (the François Tomb paintings; the Cale Vipiines
votive at Veii confirming the Vibenna brothers). The systematic **looting** of
Etruscan sites for the antiquities market — "the process, alas, continues" — has
deprived thousands of vases of their context.

## Language, society, and religion (Freeman)

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]], citing Sybille Haynes, *Etruscan
Civilization*, 2000) adds: the Etruscan language is **non-Indo-European**, written in a
Euboean-Greek-derived alphabet and largely deciphered, though its literary corpus is
lost. Herodotus' "eastern origin" legend now has partial **genetic** support (a
Turkey-linked variant found at Murlo), but the archaeological record still favours
continuity from a proto-Etruscan Italian population — origins remain genuinely
contested rather than resolved. Freeman also notes Etruscan **women** had markedly more
public presence than Athenian norms (attending banquets as couples rather than with
*hetairai*; inscriptions naming both parents) — a contrast Greek moralists such as
Theopompus (4th c.) condemned as licentious, which Freeman treats as biased Greek
commentary rather than accurate description. He speculatively suggests the Roman
*triumph* ritual may derive from an Etruscan chieftain's victory-celebration custom
(flagged explicitly as speculative, not established).

## OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Crawford

Michael Crawford (Ch. 16) places Etruria among Italy’s diverse peoples: from the
eighth century an advanced urban civilization arose by **internal evolution plus
largely Greek influence** (Etruscan language neither Greek nor Italic); cities
remained separate political entities. Rome’s material culture resembled Etruscan, but
Rome was **never** culturally or politically a mere Etruscan dependency. Archaic
openness included Etruscan adventurer-kings (Tarquinius Priscus and Superbus) and
horizontal elite mobility. Crawford notes Etruscan and other local histories have
almost vanished, leaving a ruthlessly Romanocentric literary tradition for early
Rome. Heavy-armed free peasantry in the conquest wars implies free peasants among
Etruscans despite literary stress on serfs. Post-Social-War Romanization saw bilingual
then disappearing Etruscan texts as Latinization advanced (see [[romanization-of-italy]]).

Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]] (Ch. 16, Crawford).
