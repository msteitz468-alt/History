# The Angevin Empire (1154–1204)

The **Angevin empire** (a modern term, not a contemporary one) was the assemblage of
lands ruled by **[[henry-ii-of-england|Henry II]]** and his sons — the kingdom of
**England** together with **Normandy, Anjou, Maine, Touraine, Brittany** (as overlord),
and the great duchy of **Aquitaine** — the most extensive dominion in twelfth-century
western Europe. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Thomas K. Keefe, Ch. 18) traces its
assembly out of the chaos of the English **Anarchy** and its collapse two generations
later.

## Composition and Character

The "empire" was a **dynastic agglomeration**, not a unified state: each territory
retained its own laws, institutions, and customs, held together only by the person of
the ruler. It was built by **inheritance and marriage** — Henry's paternal Anjou and
maternal claim to England and Normandy, plus the vast south-western duchy that came with
his marriage to **Eleanor of Aquitaine** (1152). At its height it stretched from the
Scottish border and the Irish Sea (Henry's lordship over Wales, Scotland, and, from
1171, Ireland) to the Pyrenees.

Its central tension was the relationship with the **[[capetian-dynasty|French crown]]**,
of which the Angevin kings were, for their continental lands, **vassals** — an
intolerable anomaly (a vassal more powerful than his lord) that the Capetians worked
relentlessly to exploit, allying with Henry's rebellious sons and wife.

## Collapse

The empire's cohesion depended on the ruler's personal energy and on keeping the French
king at bay. Under **Richard I** ("the Lionheart," 1189–99) it survived, though Richard
spent much of his reign on the [[crusades|Third Crusade]] and in captivity. Under
**John** (1199–1216) it disintegrated: **[[philip-ii-augustus|Philip II Augustus]]**,
using John's feudal defaults as pretext, conquered **Normandy, Anjou, Maine, and Touraine
in 1204**, leaving the English crown only Gascony in the south-west. The loss of the
continental heartland is the conventional end of the Angevin empire and a decisive step
in the rise of the [[capetian-dynasty|Capetian]] monarchy.

## Significance

The Angevin empire and its fall reshaped both England and France: it bound England's
fate to continental politics, drove the consolidation of royal government in both realms,
and set up the long Anglo-French struggle (down to the Hundred Years' War) over the
English crown's French possessions.
