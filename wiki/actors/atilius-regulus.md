# Marcus Atilius Regulus

Roman consul of the [[first-punic-war-264-241bce|First Punic War]] who led Rome's
invasion of Africa in 256–255 BCE, was defeated and captured at the
[[battle-of-the-bagradas-255bce|Bagradas]], and became — through later legend — the
Roman exemplar of *fides* (faith to one's oath).

## Role

After the great naval victory at [[battle-of-ecnomus-256bce|Ecnomus]] (256) opened the
sea route to Africa, Regulus landed on Carthaginian soil and won early successes,
overrunning the countryside and taking Tunis within sight of Carthage. Confident of
victory, he offered peace terms so harsh that Carthage chose to fight on. In 255 the
city hired the Spartan mercenary captain [[xanthippus|Xanthippus]], who reorganized
its army, used its elephants and cavalry to best effect, and destroyed Regulus' army
on the plain of the Bagradas; Regulus himself was captured.

## The legend

Goldsworthy treats the most famous Regulus story as patriotic myth that "so rapidly
became surrounded by myth that it is difficult now to know the full truth." In the
tradition (Horace, Cicero), Carthage later sent the captive Regulus to Rome to urge a
peace or prisoner exchange, binding him by oath to return if he failed. Regulus
instead advised the Senate to fight on, then honoured his oath by returning to
Carthage, where he was tortured to death. The story's historicity is doubtful — it may
have arisen to excuse or balance later Roman ill-treatment of Punic prisoners — but it
made Regulus a fixture of Roman moral education.

## Counterfactual significance

Regulus' defeat ended the only serious attempt to win the First War by a knockout blow
in Africa and forced Rome back into the long Sicilian war of attrition that lasted
another fourteen years. His over-confidence — refusing reasonable terms when he held
the advantage — is Goldsworthy's case study in how a single political miscalculation
threw away a war-winning operational position.
