Maurice of Nassau (Maurice, Prince of Orange, 1567–1625) was stadtholder of the Dutch Republic and the principal military reformer of the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. He is credited with systematizing drill, introducing platoon volleys for firearms, reducing pike depth, and creating the first modern professional standing army in Europe through continuous training, uniform pay, and permanent regiments. His "Dutch system" directly influenced [[gustavus-adolphus|Gustavus Adolphus]] and became the foundation for the linear infantry tactics of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

## Military Reforms
Maurice professionalized the mercenary forces inherited from the Dutch Revolt by imposing Roman-inspired drill (drawn from Lipsius and ancient models), small-unit articulation, and combined-arms coordination between shot and pike. He emphasized fortifications, siegecraft, and the "art of engineering" alongside field tactics. His victories and training methods made the Dutch army the model copied across Protestant Europe.

## In Delbrück
Delbrück (Vol. IV) treats Maurice as the pivotal figure who refined infantry tactics between the Swiss/Machiavellian experiments and the full realization under Gustavus Adolphus and the later standing armies. See [[sources/delbruck-dawn-of-modern-warfare-vol-4-1985]] for Delbrück's detailed analysis of the increase in marksmen, refinement of formations, and the transition from knight to cavalry.
