# Daniel E. Sickles

American politician and Union general whose career combined genuine battlefield notoriety with relentless self-promotion. As a U.S. congressman in 1859 he shot and killed his wife's lover, District Attorney Philip Barton Key II, in Lafayette Square, and was acquitted after his lawyers mounted the first successful temporary-insanity defense in American legal history. Commissioned a Union general despite no formal military training, he commanded III Corps, Army of the Potomac, and at the Battle of Gettysburg (2 July 1863) advanced his corps forward to the Peach Orchard without orders from [[actors/george-g-meade|Meade]], exposing his line to a devastating Confederate assault; the move cost him a leg to a cannonball but has been debated ever since — some historians credit it with disrupting Confederate attack timing and absorbing pressure that might otherwise have hit Cemetery Ridge, others see it as a reckless overextension that nearly broke the Union line. After the war he spent decades aggressively shaping the historical record and Gettysburg battlefield preservation in his own favor, including alleged manipulation of monument placement.

## Counterfactual Significance

Sickles's unauthorized advance is one of the most durable "great man vs. structural" debates in American Civil War historiography — whether his insubordination nearly lost the battle or inadvertently helped win it remains genuinely contested.

## Historiography

Standard Gettysburg historiography (e.g., Pfanz, *Gettysburg: The Second Day*) treats the Peach Orchard salient as tactically indefensible in isolation while acknowledging its disruptive effect on Confederate Longstreet's assault timetable; Sickles's own postwar lobbying (including before the 1880s Gettysburg battlefield commission) is treated as a case study in how battle memory can be actively shaped by participants.
