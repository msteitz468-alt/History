# The Mossi (Voltaic) States

The **Mossi complex** was a cluster of related **cavalry kingdoms** of the Volta
headwaters (modern Burkina Faso and northern Ghana) that, alone among the southern
savannah peoples, built durable centralised states and **held off the great Sahelian
empires** for centuries. The complex comprised a **southern group** — **Mamprussi,
Dagomba, Nanumba** — and a **northern group** — **Tenkodogo, Wagadugu, Yatenga, Fada
N'Gurma**. Documented for the wiki through Ivor Wilks's chapter in
[[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of West Africa* Vol. I]] (1971).

## Origins and the dynastic tradition

All the Mossi dynasties trace descent from **Na Gbewa** (Nedega) of Pusiga: the southern
kingdoms through his sons, the northern kingdoms through his daughter **Yenenga** and her
son **Widraogo** (founder of Tenkodogo), whose descendants founded Wagadugu and Yatenga.
The traditions — especially the Dagomba drum-histories of **Toha Zie, the Red Hunter** —
acknowledge a **Western Sudanese background**, plausibly that the early Mossi rulers were
**Mande (Malinke) mercenary cavalrymen** in Malian service who moved south into the Voltaic
country and imposed themselves on its peoples in the fifteenth century. The kingdoms
emerged as distinct entities only after c. 1450; by 1600 all had long histories of
consolidation.

## Cavalry power and the wars with Songhay

Mossi strength rested on **horses and cavalry** — heavy armoured horse for defence, light
horse for **long-range slave-raiding** into the stateless lands. In the 15th century the
northern Mossi contested the **Niger Bend** with [[songhay-empire|Songhay]]: they raided
Benga and Timbuktu and **sacked Walata (c. 1477–83)** before **Sonni Ali** drove them back
and entered the Mossi capital. **Askia Muhammad** declared a formal *jihad* against them
(1498–9), and **Askia Dawud** campaigned three times (1549–74) — none succeeding in
subjugating or converting them. The Mossi's long resistance to Islam (until the 19th c.)
is a defining trait, set against the Islamised empires around them.

## State and society: *nam* and the land-priests

The Mossi imposed a political order (***nam***, political authority) over indigenous
peoples (Fulse, Ninisi, Kassena, Konkomba and others) who had lacked centralised
institutions but were organised around **earth-shrine "parishes"** under land-priests
(***tengdana*** / *tengasoba*). Policy varied: in western Dagomba the land-priests were
**slaughtered and replaced**; elsewhere they were **confirmed and given political insignia**.
Mostly the autochthones were **integrated as a commoner class** (*tengabisi*), so that
Mossi society rests on a basic polarity between holders of *nam* and holders of ritual
land-authority — a polarity since blurred by intermarriage and mobility. The conquerors
adopted the **Gur (Mole-Dagbane) languages** of the conquered. Surrounding the kingdoms
were **never-subjugated stateless peoples** (Tallensi, Dogon, Konkomba) who served as a
raiding reservoir — a stable disequilibrium illustrating Horton's
[[stateless-societies|stateless-vs-state]] frontier.

## Significance

The Mossi are the great counter-example within West Africa: an indigenous,
**non-Islamic, cavalry-based** state system that proved **remarkably stable over half a
millennium**, blocked Songhay's southward reach, and shielded the forest peoples to its
south. Wilks treats them, with the [[akan-states|Akan]] and Gonja, under the unifying
theme of the **successive emergence of ever-larger state systems** organising trade and
law across the Volta basin. Gonja itself was founded (16th c.) by a Mande/Malian cavalry
expedition sent south over the collapse of the gold supply — see [[akan-states]].

## Historiography

Reconstructed from **oral/drum traditions** of the several dynasties (collected by Izard,
Tauxier, Rattray, Tamakloe and others), cross-checked against the **Timbuktu chronicles**
(*Ta'rikh al-Sudan*, *Ta'rikh al-Fattash*) for the Songhay wars and against fragmentary
Portuguese and Arabic notices (de Barros; the disputed 1410 "Awdar" manuscript).
