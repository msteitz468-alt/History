# Freisler, Roland (1893–1945)

Nazi jurist and later President of the People's Court (Volksgerichtshof). One of the most radical participants at the June 5, 1934 Justice Ministry meeting on the future race laws. Famously stated of American race jurisprudence: "This jurisprudence would suit us perfectly, with a single exception. Over there they have in mind, practically speaking, only coloreds and half-coloreds... but the Jews, who are also of interest to us, are not reckoned among the coloreds."

## Role and Significance

Freisler embodied the radical "political" wing of Nazi legal thinking that sought to liberate law from inherited "scientific" or formal constraints so that it could serve the racial and Führer-state goals directly. He was attracted to American common-law pragmatism and "realism" precisely because (in his view) it allowed politics and "life" to override rigid legal science — a stance that aligned with Nazi attacks on the "outdated type of jurist." Later notorious as the presiding judge in the show trials of the July 20, 1944 conspirators and many others; died in an Allied bombing raid on the People's Court building in February 1945.

## Key Events / Processes

- June 5, 1934 meeting (Justice Ministry planning session for the Nuremberg Laws): most vocal champion of American models. Argued US jurisprudence "would suit us perfectly" (with the noted exception on Jews); advocated "primitive" statutory language ("A state even simply says: 'colored people.' Such a procedure would be crude [roh], but it would suffice") and "primitivity" over juristic niceties. Contrasted with moderates who found US material unusable or too vague for Jews.
- Subsequent career: full alignment with the most lawless practices of the regime; President of the People's Court; institutionalization of "working toward the Führer" in judicial practice.
- Symbol in post-war memory of Nazi judicial savagery.

## Counterfactual Note

Freisler's victory in the jurisprudential struggle inside the 1934 meeting (and the broader marginalization of the Gürtner/Lösener type) helped produce a legal order in which judges and administrators enjoyed wide discretion to pursue racial persecution without being slowed by conceptual coherence requirements. A stronger survival of traditional juristic limits would likely have complicated the administrative machinery of the Holocaust.

## Historiography

Frequently appears as the extreme case in studies of Nazi justice (e.g., the "politicization" or "deformalization" of law). Whitman uses him as the clearest illustration of why certain American legal-cultural features appealed to radicals: the relative weakness of "legal science" as a brake on political will. Primary source base includes the 1934 transcript and his own later writings and court practice.
