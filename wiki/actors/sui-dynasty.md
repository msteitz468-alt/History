# The Sui Dynasty

## Overview

The **Sui** (581–618 CE) **reunified China** in **589 CE** after nearly four centuries of
division following the [[han-dynasty|Han]], ending the era of the Northern and Southern
Dynasties. Short-lived but pivotal, in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Holcombe, Ch 15) the
Sui reunification "suddenly" made China "a looming regional superpower," transforming the
balance of power across East Asia and laying the institutional foundations inherited by the
**[[tang-dynasty|Tang]]**.

## Reunification and Reach

The Sui ruling house, of Chinese descent but long intermarried with the **Xianbei** and
serving Xianbei dynasties, embodied the **north Chinese cultural synthesis** that the
centuries of "barbarian" rule had produced. Reunification rebuilt a centralized empire,
constructed the **Grand Canal** linking the agricultural south to the northern capitals, and
revived expansionist ambition — launching from **612 CE** a series of massive, repeatedly
repulsed **invasions of Koguryŏ** (in Korea). The Sui collapse came largely from the ruinous
cost of those Korean campaigns.

## Significance

The Sui restored the unity of China and bequeathed to the Tang the reunified state, the
Grand Canal, and the renewed drive into Korea that would reshape the peninsula. It is the
classic "short reunifying dynasty whose works the successor enjoys" — paired with the Tang as
the [[qin-dynasty|Qin]] is paired with the [[han-dynasty|Han]].

## Historiography

Like the Qin, the Sui is known mainly through histories written under its successor, which
emphasize the despotism and overreach (especially the Koguryŏ wars) that justified the
**[[tang-dynasty|Tang]]** succession — a moral framing to be read with caution. Its
reunification is the hinge between China's long medieval division and the cosmopolitan Tang
golden age.
