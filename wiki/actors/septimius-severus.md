# Septimius Severus

**Lucius Septimius Severus** (145–211, r. 193–211) was the African-born general who seized
power in the civil war following the murder of [[commodus|Commodus]], founding the
[[severan-dynasty|Severan dynasty]] and inaugurating what Rostovtzeff called the **"military
monarchy."** Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 1 (**Brian Campbell**).

## Origin and accession

Born at **Lepcis Magna** in Tripolitania into a rich, Romanized municipal family of Punic
extraction (a "typical product of the municipal aristocracy," not — Campbell stresses — an "alien,
un-Roman mind"), Severus rose through an unspectacular senatorial career to govern **Pannonia
Superior**. After the murder of [[pertinax|Pertinax]] and the notorious **"auction" of the empire**
to Didius Julianus (March 193), Severus was proclaimed by his Danubian legions on **9 April 193**
and marched on Rome, declaring [[clodius-albinus|Clodius Albinus]] of Britain "Caesar" to neutralize
him. He entered Rome, **disbanded the praetorian guard** (replacing it with his own legionaries),
and avenged Pertinax — whose name he took.

## Civil and foreign wars

Severus destroyed his rivals in two civil wars: **Pescennius Niger** (defeated at Issus, 194) and
**Clodius Albinus** (defeated at the bloody battle of **Lugdunum**, 19 Feb. 197). Between and after,
he waged **Parthian wars** for glory and legitimacy — sacking **Ctesiphon** (198, the centenary of
Trajan's accession) and annexing a new province of **Mesopotamia** under an equestrian prefect
(which Cassius Dio judged "expensive, unproductive and dangerous"). He died at **York (4 Feb. 211)**
during a campaign in Caledonia, leaving the empire to his sons.

## The army, the senate, and the state

Campbell's revisionist portrait resists the caricature of Severus as a brutal militarizer. He **was
not a "military emperor"**: he raised army pay (long overdue), allowed soldiers to marry, recruited
three new **Parthica legions** (one stationed in Italy — a first), and ended exclusively Italian
recruitment of the guard — but he showed no systematic preference for soldiers in administration, and
senators still **monopolized the senior posts** at his death. He governed conscientiously along
traditional lines, employed jurists (**Papinian, Ulpian**), and left a treasury surplus. Yet the
**manner of his accession** — the first capture of Rome by an army in 124 years — was the decisive
break: it bound emperor and army closer, eroded senatorial confidence, and advanced provincial
levelling. His deathbed advice to his sons distilled the new reality: **"be harmonious, enrich the
soldiers, and despise all the rest."**

## Significance

Severus refounded the Principate on a broader, more military and more provincial base, and provided
(briefly) for orderly dynastic succession. But by demonstrating again that the throne was won and
held by **securing the army's loyalty**, he entrenched the very dynamic that would unravel into the
[[third-century-crisis|crisis of the third century]] a generation after his death.
