# Pliny the Younger (Gaius Plinius Caecilius Secundus)

Roman senator, advocate and letter-writer (61/62–c. 113 CE), whose published **[[pliny-the-younger-letters|Letters]]**
are among the richest documents of social and political life in the early Empire — and whose
correspondence with the emperor Trajan preserves the earliest detailed external account of how Rome
dealt with the Christians.

## Life and Career

Born at **Comum** to an equestrian family, he was orphaned young and raised by his uncle and adoptive
father **Pliny the Elder** (the encyclopedist) and the consular Verginius Rufus. He studied rhetoric
under **Quintilian**, and rose through the *cursus honorum* under Domitian (quaestor, tribune, praetor)
and especially **Trajan**: prefect of the treasury, **suffect consul in 100** — when he delivered the
*Panegyricus* praising Trajan as the ideal *princeps* — curator of the Tiber, and finally **governor of
Bithynia-Pontus** (c. 110–113), where he died. He was a celebrated courtroom advocate (often appearing
with his close friend **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]**) and a wealthy man of letters and estates.

## The Letters

Pliny published **nine books of carefully crafted literary letters** (each polished on a single theme),
plus a tenth book of his **official correspondence with Trajan**. They are "part autobiography and part
social history," documenting the courts, the Senate, slavery, education, literary culture, patronage,
and his devotion to his young wife Calpurnia. Two sets are historically momentous:

- the **Vesuvius letters** (6.16, 6.20) to Tacitus, describing the **[[eruption-of-vesuvius-79ce|eruption
  of 79]]** and the death of **Pliny the Elder** — the only eyewitness account of the disaster;
- the **Christians correspondence** (10.96–97): Pliny's report from Bithynia asking how to try
  Christians, and **Trajan's rescript** — the foundational text on the early Roman legal posture toward
  the new religion.

## Significance

Pliny is our **fullest window onto the senatorial and administrative world of the high Empire** under
the "good emperors," the model of the cultivated Roman gentleman, and an indispensable primary source
for the **early history of Christianity**, the **Vesuvius catastrophe**, and the working relationship
between emperor and provincial governor. His career also illuminates the lives of his friends: he was
patron to **[[suetonius|Suetonius]]** and intimate of **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]**, to whom several letters
are addressed.

## Beard (SPQR, 2015)

Mary Beard, in **[[sources/beard-spqr-2015|SPQR]]**, reads Pliny's Bithynian correspondence
(c. 109–111 CE) as capturing "a new style of government in the provinces" under the
emperors — governors now directly answerable to Rome, in contrast to the looser,
more exploitative Republican-era governorship exemplified by Cicero's Cilicia. She
argues Pliny's famously exhaustive, detail-obsessed letters to **[[trajan|Trajan]]** were
not personal fussiness but a structural feature of imperial administration: "you got no
decision from the emperor unless you asked him for one." Beard singles out the
Pliny–Trajan exchange on the Christians (10.96–97) as "the earliest surviving discussion
of Christianity outside Jewish or Christian literature," showing officials genuinely
uncertain how to legally handle a new sect rather than pursuing systematic persecution.
She also notes Pliny had "little eye" for cultural difference in his province — he
never remarks that Greek, not Latin, was Bithynia's main language.
