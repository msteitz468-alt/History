# Lucretius (Titus Lucretius Carus)

Roman poet-philosopher of the late Republic, author of the six-book didactic epic ***De rerum natura*** (*On the Nature of Things*), the greatest surviving Latin statement of [[epicureanism|Epicurean]] physics and ethics.

## Role

Writing c.**60–55 BCE** amid the anxieties of [[julius-caesar|Caesar]]’s rise, Lucretius dedicated the poem to Memmius and opened with a hymn to Venus as creative force and *uoluptas*, praying for peace (Nisbet notes the political anxieties of c.60–55 when Caesar was already “subverting the Republic”). Books 1–2 expound Epicurus’ atomic theory (from Leucippus/Democritus) and the ***clinamen*** (swerve) — Epicurus’ major physical contribution, ethically intended to free volition from determinism ([[cicero]] thought a causeless swerve “disgraceful” for a scientist). Book 3 argues soul mortality with ~30 arguments (*nil igitur mors est ad nos*); attacks fear of afterlife and religion’s evils (*tantum religio potuit suadere malorum*); rationalizes underworld punishments as earthly torments (Sisyphus = ambitious politician). Despite natural-selection-like language, Nisbet stresses Lucretius has **no idea of evolution**; civilization advances by *usus et … experientia mentis*. The sixth book ends with the plague at Athens — gruesome and abrupt. Ennius’ hexameter diction (Brown, Ch. 18) underlies his high style.

## Style and Context

Nisbet (OHCW) pairs him with [[catullus]]: both Republican, candid, elegantly direct. Cicero (early 54, to Quintus) praised “many brilliances of natural genius” plus “much technique,” contrasting Sallustius’ *Empedoclea*. Lucretius stretches Latin abstract vocabulary and hexameter to carry Greek technical thought; aesthetic polish shows contact with new-poetry currents without Neoteric art-for-art’s-sake.

## Significance

Without Lucretius, Epicurean atomism lacks its Latin monument and much of its Renaissance/early-modern afterlife. He is the bridge from [[epicurus]]’s Garden to Western poetic science.

## Historiography

Biographical tradition is thin and legendary (Jerome’s suicide story usually discounted). OHCW (Nisbet; Barnes) treats the poem as faithful Epicureanism under political stress.
