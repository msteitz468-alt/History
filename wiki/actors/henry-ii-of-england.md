# Henry II of England

**Henry II** (1133–1189; king of England from 1154) was the first
[[angevin-empire|Angevin/Plantagenet]] king of England and the ruler of the largest
dominion in twelfth-century western Europe. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Thomas
K. Keefe, Ch. 18, "England and the Angevin dominions, 1137–1204") presents him as the
architect of a sprawling cross-Channel state and of a precocious system of royal justice.

## Career

Son of **Geoffrey of Anjou** and the **Empress Matilda** (daughter of Henry I), Henry
inherited the claim his mother had fought for during the English civil war ("the
**Anarchy**") against King Stephen. By 1153 he had forced the **Treaty of Winchester**,
recognising him as Stephen's heir; he became king in 1154. Through inheritance and his
marriage (1152) to **Eleanor of Aquitaine**, he assembled the **[[angevin-empire|Angevin
empire]]**: England, Normandy, Anjou, Maine, Touraine, and the vast duchy of Aquitaine —
a dominion stretching from the Scottish border to the Pyrenees.

His reign is marked by two great themes:
- **The growth of royal justice.** Henry rebuilt royal administration after the
  Anarchy and, through the **Assize of Clarendon (1166)** and related measures,
  expanded the reach of royal courts, itinerant justices, and the jury — the
  foundations of the English **[[ius-commune|common law]]** (NCMH IV stresses that the
  common law "descends from the king's law" of Henry II's reign).
- **The conflict with the Church.** His attempt to subject "criminous clerks" to royal
  courts (the **Constitutions of Clarendon**, 1164) provoked the bitter quarrel with his
  former chancellor, Archbishop **[[thomas-becket|Thomas Becket]]**, whose murder in
  Canterbury Cathedral (1170) by Henry's knights shocked Christendom and forced the king
  to public penance.

His last years were consumed by the rebellions of his sons (the "young king" Henry,
Richard, Geoffrey, John) — often allied with Eleanor and with Louis VII and Philip II of
France — and he died in 1189 at war with Richard and Philip.

## Counterfactual Significance

Henry II created the institutional framework of the English state — the common law and
royal administration — that outlived the Angevin empire itself (largely lost by his son
John in 1204). His clash with Becket became the paradigmatic medieval confrontation of
royal and ecclesiastical jurisdiction. The Angevin "empire" he assembled, and its
collapse, drove the Anglo-French rivalry that shaped both kingdoms for centuries.
