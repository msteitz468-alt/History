# Igbo People

A major West African ethnic group native to southeastern Nigeria, historically
organized into largely decentralized, village-based political systems (in
contrast to the centralized kingdoms common elsewhere in West Africa),
except for notable exceptions such as the Nri kingdom's ritual hegemony and
the Aro Confederacy's commercial-religious network.

## Role and Significance

Igbo communities were deeply enmeshed in the trans-Atlantic slave trade
through their interaction with the [[niger-delta-city-states|Niger Delta
city-states]] (such as Bonny and Old Calabar), which served as the export
points for captives drawn from the Igbo hinterland — making Igbo
populations one of the largest single ethnic sources of enslaved Africans
carried to the Americas. The Aro Confederacy, centered on the Arochukwu oracle,
built a far-reaching trade and judicial network across Igboland that
supplied much of this trade without the Igbo themselves forming a unified
centralized state.

## Counterfactual Significance

The Igbo's characteristically decentralized political structure meant that,
unlike more centralized West African kingdoms, no single Igbo polity
directed or could unilaterally halt the region's participation in the
slave trade — a structural feature historians cite in explaining both the
scale of Igbo involvement and the difficulty of any coordinated resistance.

## Historiography

Igbo history before the colonial period is reconstructed mainly through oral
tradition, archaeology (notably the Igbo-Ukwu bronzes), and the records of
European traders operating through Niger Delta intermediaries; the
collection's coverage of Igbo society specifically, as opposed to the
better-documented coastal city-states, remains thin, consistent with the
broader gap in Sub-Saharan African sources.
