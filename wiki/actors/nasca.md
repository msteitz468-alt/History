# Nasca

## Overview

**Nasca** was the principal regional culture of the arid **south coast of Peru** during the
Early Intermediate Period (c. 100 BCE–650 CE), heir to the earlier **Paracas** tradition and
contemporary with [[moche|Moche]] on the north coast. In Izumi Shimada's chapter on Andean
regional diversity in [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Volume III]], Nasca
exemplifies the era of **regional florescence** following the [[chavin|Chavín]] Early Horizon:
distinctive polychrome ceramics, fine textiles, the great ceremonial center of **Cahuachi**,
and underground filtration galleries (puquios) for water in one of the world's driest
environments.

Nasca is best known for the **geoglyphs** ("Nasca lines") etched into the desert pampa —
animals, plants, and long straight lines and trapezoids — generally interpreted as ritual
pathways and offerings connected to water and fertility cults rather than astronomical
calendars.

## Significance

Nasca demonstrates the **non-state, ceremonially integrated** end of the Andean spectrum:
a society of considerable artistic and hydraulic sophistication organized around pilgrimage
and ritual rather than centralized coercive statecraft, contrasting with the more
militarized [[moche|Moche]].

## Historiography

Interpretations of the lines have moved from Maria Reiche's astronomical hypothesis toward
ritual-procession and water-cult readings (Aveni, Silverman). Cahuachi, once thought an urban
capital, is now generally read as a vacant ceremonial center.
