# Fujiwara no Yoshifusa (804–872)

Fujiwara no Yoshifusa was the paramount figure at the Japanese imperial court
from the accession of Emperor Montoku in 850 until his own death in 872.
He was the first person outside the imperial clan ever to serve as regent (*sesshō*)
to a Japanese emperor — an appointment in 866 that institutionalized the
[[fujiwara-regency|Fujiwara regency]] as a form of government and established
a claim to that post for the Northern House of the [[fujiwara-clan|Fujiwara clan]]
that was never successfully challenged by any other clan or family.
[[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]]

## Background and Rise

Yoshifusa was the son of Fujiwara no Fuyutsugu (775–826), the Northern House
leader who served as a key minister under Emperor Saga and laid the groundwork
for his house's dominance. Fuyutsugu's premature death in 826 left the young and
relatively low-ranking Yoshifusa in a precarious position. His fortunes turned with
the **Jōwa Incident** of 842 — a political crisis in which, following the death of the
retired emperor Saga, the Northern House moved against rivals at court, removing
the Tachibana clan leader and several princes from influence. "After the Jōwa Incident
in 842 the future clearly belonged to him and his house, and he did not hesitate to
claim it."

## Regency

Yoshifusa demonstrated his power immediately upon the accession of Emperor Montoku
in 850. Montoku had already taken Yoshifusa's daughter Meishi (829–900) as consort
while still crown prince; eight months after her son's birth (the future Emperor
Seiwa, 850–881), Yoshifusa appears to have forced Montoku to bypass the elder
prince Koretaka — son of a Ki-clan woman — and name the infant as crown prince.
This positioned Yoshifusa as maternal grandfather of the next emperor.

In 857, Yoshifusa was named **Chancellor** (*daijō daijin*) — a post that had stood
vacant since the monk Dōkyō's time ninety years earlier. On Seiwa's accession in 858
at age eight (the first child-emperor in Japanese history), his control of the court
was "virtually complete." The appointment as formal regent came in 866, following
the burning of the Gate of Obedience to Heaven (*Ōtemmon*), the main palace gate.

## The Ōtemmon Affair (866)

The burning of the Ōtemmon under "mysterious circumstances" triggered a political
confrontation. Major Counselor Tomo no Yoshio (809–868) charged that the Minister
of the Left, Minamoto no Makoto (an affinal relative of Yoshifusa), had set the fire.
Yoshifusa, living in retirement at Shirakawa, intervened on Makoto's behalf. A
convenient witness then accused Yoshio and his confederates (including men from the
Ki clan) of the arson. After investigation, Yoshio, his son, and members of the Tomo
and Ki clans were sent into exile — "bringing to a decisive end the long struggle of
those ancient clans to maintain the important positions they had once occupied at
court."

The *Cambridge History* notes that "the events and motives involved in the burning
of the Gate of Obedience to Heaven are by no means clear, and even insofar as we
have a record of them, their authenticity cannot be assumed, since they are
contained in a chronicle compiled under the editorship of Yoshifusa's grandson."

Shortly after Yoshio's exile, Yoshifusa was formally appointed regent by the
sixteen-year-old Seiwa.

## Institutional Significance

Yoshifusa's regency was a political innovation, not a seizure of an existing office.
The *Cambridge History* frames it as "the confirmation of existing power through the
creation of a new office" — the regency gave institutional recognition to a dominance
already possessed in fact. The appointment had no basis in the statutory code; it was
an *ad hoc* arrangement requiring reappointment by each succeeding emperor.

By his death in 872, Yoshifusa had "achieved heights of power and prestige
unprecedented for a court noble, his position differing but little in form or
substance from that of the emperor himself." His inability to produce a natural son
was remedied by the adoption of **Mototsune** (836–891), son of his brother Nagara,
who succeeded him as the Northern House's paramount figure.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Yoshifusa's personal dominance, the formalization of the Fujiwara regency
as a hereditary institution might have been delayed or taken a different structural
form. He converted a circumstantial advantage — the infancy of Seiwa — into a
permanent institutional arrangement. His exclusion of the Tomo and Ki clans also
simplified the court landscape in ways that made subsequent Fujiwara dominance
easier to sustain.
