# John Monash

## Overview

John Monash was the most distinguished Australian field commander of the First World War, rising from divisional to corps commander and leading the Australian Corps in the Allied "Hundred Days" advance of 1918. A Melbourne civil engineer and citizen soldier before the war, he commanded the 3rd Division from 1916 and in 1918 took command of the Australian Corps — the largest corps in the British Expeditionary Force. He subsequently directed the demobilisation of the AIF. Monash's appointment as corps commander was contested on grounds of his Jewish ancestry, a fact the source records explicitly. His reputation as a military genius is treated critically in the source.

## Military Career

Monash commanded the 3rd Division, formed in Australia, when it arrived in France in 1916. His division "helped to show that advances more economical in casualties were possible" at Messines in June 1917. By late 1917, "Australian pressure resulted in the five AIF infantry divisions becoming united as the Australian Corps, the largest in the BEF. First commanded by Birdwood, early in 1918 the command went to John Monash, the citizen soldier who had proved himself as a divisional commander after a shaky start on Gallipoli."

His appointment was opposed by "a cabal of journalists and artists, including Charles Bean and Will Dyson, affronted by Monash's Jewish ancestry and 'pushy' character." The source offers a corrective to hagiography: "Though not the military genius portrayed in popular history (accounts rooted in Monash's own estimate of his abilities), he was a highly competent commander."

Under Monash the Australian Corps played "a prominent part in a series of advances" in the Hundred Days: at Amiens in August 1918 the Corps attacked alongside Canadian, British, and French formations. "Pushed hard by Monash, who was ambitious for the AIF to be seen as making a decisive contribution, the Australian Corps played a leading part in advancing up the Somme valley," culminating in the capture of Mont St Quentin and seizure of parts of the Hindenburg Line.

When enlistment collapsed Monash disbanded a dozen battalions, "orders that provoked a spate of protests and mutinies." He "handled the protests…ineptly."

## Demobilisation

After the Armistice, "John Monash was put in charge of demobilisation, devoting his superb organisational skills to the challenge of occupying men anxious to return." He established "extensive educational and training services, with lectures, lending libraries and theatre productions to fill idle moments." The priority system for return was based on length of overseas service rather than rank — seen by the official historian as a "triumph of Australian 'fairness.'"

## Counterfactual Significance

Monash's performance at the divisional and corps level demonstrated that the AIF could combine arms (artillery, aircraft, tanks, infantry) more economically than in the attritional battles of 1916–17. The source credits him as one of several British and Dominion generals to "implement hard-won lessons" rather than the sole architect of Allied victory — a correction to the "military genius" mythology.

## Historiography

The source (Garton and Stanley, Chapter 2) is explicitly revisionist about Monash's reputation, tracing the inflated mythology to Monash's own memoirs and noting the antisemitism that surrounded his appointment. The deployment of Bean against Monash during the appointment process is treated as indicating the limits of Anzac meritocracy. His administrative skill in demobilisation is presented as his most organisationally significant achievement.
