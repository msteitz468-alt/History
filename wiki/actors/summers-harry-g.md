# Harry G. Summers Jr.

**Harry G. Summers Jr.** (1932–1999) was a U.S. Army colonel, infantry officer, designated strategist, author, and one of the most influential military thinkers on the Vietnam War. A combat veteran of both the Korean War (squad leader, Combat Infantry Badge) and the Vietnam War (battalion and corps operations officer; later negotiator with the Viet Cong and North Vietnamese on the Four-Party Joint Military Team in Saigon and Hanoi), Summers combined battlefield experience with rigorous Clausewitzian analysis.

After Vietnam he served on the faculty of the U.S. Army War College (where he taught strategy), as a member of General Creighton Abrams' strategic assessment group, and in the Office of the Army Chief of Staff. His articles appeared in *Army*, *Military Review*, and *Naval Institute Proceedings*. Awards include the Silver Star, Legion of Merit, Bronze Star for Valor, and two Purple Hearts.

## On Strategy (1982)

Summers' landmark work *On Strategy: A Critical Analysis of the Vietnam War* (first circulated internally at the Army War College in 1981 as "The Vietnam War in Context," published commercially 1982) is the canonical Clausewitzian postmortem. Its central claim—captured in the 1975 Hanoi exchange with a North Vietnamese colonel ("You never defeated us on the battlefield." "That may be so, but it is also irrelevant.")—is that the United States won virtually every tactical engagement and mastered the logistics of sustaining a large expeditionary force, yet lost strategically because it misdiagnosed the nature of the war.

Key arguments (grounded in Range 01 front matter/Intro/Part I):
- The Second Indochina War was not primarily a revolutionary insurgency (people's war) but an interstate conventional war of aggression by the Democratic Republic of Vietnam (North Vietnam) using its regular forces (NVA) as the decisive arm. The Viet Cong/NLF was a supporting effort.
- U.S. strategy confused "preparation for war" (organize/train/equip, systems analysis, management) with "war proper" (the use of military force to achieve political ends). "The activities characteristic of war may be split into two main categories: those that are merely preparation for war, and war proper."
- Civilian strategists (limited-war theorists) and military abdication of the strategic function produced a failure to link means to achievable political objectives and a misapplication of the principles of war.
- National will was deliberately not invoked (Johnson: fears for Great Society; McNamara on limited war "without the necessity of arousing the public ire"); Gulf of Tonkin substituted for declaration of war; recreated 18th-c. "cabinet war" dynamic ("war was still an affair for governments alone, and the people’s role was simply that of an instrument"). The trinity (people/government/Army) was unbalanced. Friction (people + bureaucracy) produced the moral vulnerability the enemy exploited.
- Clausewitz remains "the most modern source available" for military science; Summers' purpose is to "clarify concepts and ideas that have become, as it were, confused and entangled."
- The war had to be understood through the Clausewitzian trinity (people, government, army) and the requirement for a clear political objective that the military instrument could actually secure.

The book was adopted as a teaching text across U.S. war colleges and had major influence on the post-Vietnam renaissance of strategic thinking inside the Army and on the conditions later codified in the Weinberger Doctrine.

## Legacy and Reception

Summers is credited with helping restore the professional military voice in strategy after a generation in which civilians dominated the literature. Critics from the counterinsurgency school (Krepinevich, Nagl) argue he under-weighted the southern insurgency's autonomy and the Army's institutional resistance to population-centric approaches. Summers' response framework remains the indispensable strategic-level reference for the Vietnam War.
