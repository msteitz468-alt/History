# Nikita Khrushchev
## Biography / Description
Nikita Khrushchev was born in 1894 in the village of Kalinovka in Kursk province to a peasant family. As a lad he worked as a shepherd; in adolescence he moved to the Don Basin as a miner. In the First World War he was active in the labour movement; in the Civil War he fought on the Red side and became a Bolshevik in 1918. Despite inadequate formal education he rose through the local party network in Ukraine, trained at the Industrial Academy in Moscow in 1929, and took up the cudgels against Bukharin. Kaganovich brought him to Stalin's attention. By 1935 he led the Moscow City Party Committee; in 1938 he became First Secretary of the Communist Party of Ukraine. In the Great Terror he was an unflinching purger but also a dynamic administrator. In 1941 he became the main political commissar on the Southern front. He survived Stalin's moods (sometimes carrying metal-working tools in case of sudden demotion) and in February 1944 received the joint appointment as leader of the party and government of Ukraine. Recalled to Moscow as Central Committee Secretary in December 1949, Stalin intended him as a counterweight to Malenkov. After Stalin's death in March 1953 he played a central role in the succession struggle (lines 2521–2523).

Following Stalin's death, the Presidium was reduced from twenty-five to ten members to sideline younger leaders. Malenkov became Chairman of the Council of Ministers and Party Secretary, but stepped down from the latter in March 1953. Beria, who merged MVD and MGB and pursued radical reforms (including contacts with Tito and concessions to non-Russians), was arrested on 26 June 1953 in a Presidium plot engineered by Khrushchev with Zhukov and Moskalenko; he was convicted in camera and shot in December 1953. Khrushchev's proposal placed the MVD directly under party control. In September 1953 Khrushchev was designated First Secretary (lines 2427–2443).

Khrushchev's elevation came from his daring in the Beria affair and the attractiveness of his policies to heavy-industrial ministries, armed forces and the party apparatus. He promoted the virgin lands campaign. In 1955 the  grain harvest was 21 per cent higher than the previous year. On 13 February 1956 he proposed to the Presidium a speech on "the Cult of the Individual and its Consequences" for the Twentieth Party Congress. The closed-session speech of 25 February 1956, lasting four hours, denounced Stalin's repressions of 1937–8, the failure to anticipate Hitler's 1941 invasion, ethnic deportations, the Leningrad Affair and Doctors' Plot, and the decline of internal party democracy. It exculpated the current Presidium while blaming a handful of security-police leaders. Khrushchev lied about his own role in the terror. The speech was not published in the USSR until Gorbachev's time and became known as the "secret speech". It produced enormous relief at home but gusted away legitimacy in Eastern Europe (lines 2465–2489).

In June 1957 Molotov, Malenkov and Kaganovich (with Shepilov) mounted an assault at a three-day Presidium meeting, outnumbering and defeating Khrushchev and proposing to abolish the post of Party First Secretary. Khrushchev insisted the Central Committee had the right to dismiss him. With Marshal Zhukov's assistance, CC members were flown to Moscow; some banged on Presidium doors. The 21 June plenum resulted in a resounding victory for Khrushchev. The "Anti-Party Group" was dismissed; Zhukov and others entered the Presidium. Khrushchev guaranteed that internal élite disputes would be conducted without manacles and rifles (a break with Stalin's practices). Opponents received humiliating demotions: Molotov to ambassador to Mongolia, Malenkov to a hydro-electric station in Kazakhstan, Kaganovich to a cement works (lines 2501–2505).

Khrushchev took over as Chairman of the Council of Ministers in March 1958. He pursued consumer goods (though agriculture remained more urgent in his view), broke up central ministries into 105 sovnarkhozy in 1957, and adjusted priorities toward oil, gas and chemicals while retaining capital-goods primacy. The Seven-Year Plan (1959) delivered GNP growth of 58 per cent and industrial output of 84 per cent by 1965. Spectacular successes included Sputnik (1957) and Yuri Gagarin's manned orbit (1961). He visited the USA in September 1959 and debated Nixon in the "kitchen debate". Yet impulsiveness (maize campaign against agronomists' advice, restrictions on private plots) and bossiness damaged institutions and alienated colleagues. He introduced a rule limiting officials to three periods of tenure (1961). By the early 1960s he faced problems in agriculture (virgin lands dust-bowl, 1963 harvest only 92 per cent of 1958), consumer goods shortfalls, the Sino-Soviet split, and the Cuban missile crisis humiliation (lines 2513–2557).

Khrushchev's ebullience, earthy anecdotes, gadgets, self-advertisement (appointing son-in-law Adzhubei editor of Izvestiya) and cult of personality (despite denouncing Stalin's) irked colleagues. On 14 October 1964 an emergency CC plenum deposed him after a Presidium plot involving Brezhnev, Suslov, Shelepin and Semichastny. He accepted retirement without fight. No guns or executions followed (lines 2519, 2713–2723).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Secret Speech and De-Stalinization (1956):** At the closed session of the Twentieth Party Congress Khrushchev delivered a four-hour denunciation of Stalin as capricious autocrat and blunderer responsible for mass repressions after 1934. The speech initiated mass rehabilitations (8–9 million cases within months of the Congress) and a "thaw" in culture and society while preserving the one-party state, kolkhozes and capital-goods priority. Khrushchev framed it pragmatically: "If we don't tell the truth at the Congress, we'll be forced to tell the truth some time in the future" (lines 2471–2485).
- **Defeat of the Anti-Party Group (June 1957):** Khrushchev turned a Presidium defeat into victory by appealing to the Central Committee, securing the dismissal of Molotov, Malenkov and Kaganovich. This broke with Stalinist terror in élite disputes and consolidated his personal ascendancy (lines 2501–2505).
- **Virgin Lands and Agricultural Reorganization:** He opened 36 million additional hectares in Kazakhstan and western Siberia within three years of Stalin's death. Kolkhozes were amalgamated (from 125,000 to 36,000) and machine-tractor stations abolished in 1958. Wheat output rose over 50 per cent 1950–1960; milk and meat production increased substantially. Yet over-ploughing created dust-bowls and private-plot restrictions later caused shortages (lines 2531–2533).
- **Industrial Decentralization (1957):** Central ministries were broken up and functions allocated to 105 regional economic councils (sovnarkhozy) to introduce dynamic planning. Capital-goods priority was retained but oil, gas and chemicals boosted. The system entangled party and government further when Khrushchev became Chairman of the Council of Ministers in 1958 (lines 2535, 2513).
- **Space Programme and International Presence:** Sponsored Sputnik (1957) and Gagarin (1961). Pursued "peaceful coexistence" while retaining the inevitability-of-wars thesis; visited the USA (1959), debated Nixon, hosted the American exhibition in Moscow. The U-2 incident (1960) and Vienna summit (1961) with Kennedy preceded the Cuban missile crisis (lines 2537, 2549–2551).
- **Ouster and Legacy (1964):** Removed without violence after erratic reorganizations (including the 1962 party bipartition) and policy failures alienated the élite. His mercy toward the Anti-Party Group and termination of mass terror marked a significant break with Stalin (lines 2503, 2723).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Secret Speech and De-Stalinization (1956):** At the Twentieth Party Congress, Khrushchev delivered a closed-session speech denouncing Stalin as a cruel, irrational tyrant, initiating the "thaw" and the rehabilitation of purge victims. At the Twenty-second Congress (1961), he intensified this policy, removing Stalin's body from the Red Square mausoleum.
- **Virgin Lands Project (1954):** To resolve the chronic agricultural crisis, Khrushchev opened up seventy million acres of arid lands in Siberia and Kazakhstan for cultivation.
- **Sino-Soviet Split (1956–1964):** Khrushchev's policies of de-Stalinization and "peaceful coexistence" with the West alienated Mao Zedong's China, leading to a bitter ideological and geopolitical split that fractured the communist bloc.
- **Crushing of the Hungarian Revolution (1956):** Despite his rhetoric of liberalization, Khrushchev ordered Soviet tanks to invade Budapest in November 1956 to crush Nagy's coalition government and keep Hungary within the Warsaw Pact.
- **Sputnik and Space Age (1957):** Khrushchev heavily sponsored rocket and space programs, leading to the launch of Sputnik I (October 1957) and Yuri Gagarin's historic space flight (1961), which boosted Soviet prestige.
- **Cuban Missile Crisis (1962):** Khrushchev ordered the installation of nuclear missiles in Cuba, leading to a direct thermonuclear standoff with US President John F. Kennedy. His subsequent decision to withdraw the missiles in exchange for US concessions was viewed as a capitulation by his domestic critics, contributing to his fall.

## Historical Significance
Khrushchev was a transitional leader who ended mass terror while preserving the core Soviet compound of one-party rule, ideological monopoly and state economic ownership. From unpropitious origins he rose through ambition and exuberant intelligence, becoming "the politician who denounced the 'cult of the individual'" yet zealously accumulated prestige (his picture in the press daily, mandatory eulogies, son-in-law at Izvestiya). He relished authority ("Grandad, who are you? The tsar?") and gadgets but was impulsive: the maize campaign rejected agronomist advice; private-plot restrictions threatened vegetable supplies (two-fifths grown on plots); arbitrary apartment targets and brick downgrading caused chaos (lines 2519, 2525, 2539).

His mercy toward the Anti-Party Group ("Your words yet again confirm what methods you intended...") was an important break with Stalin, guaranteeing élite disputes without manacles and rifles. Yet self-righteousness was implausible given his own role in 1937–8 terror in Moscow and Ukraine and Beria's shooting. He assumed he knew best, undermined consultation patterns he had helped establish, and convinced himself the party's problems stemmed from inherited officials (lines 2503, 2527).

By the early 1960s his quarter-reforms were probably the maximum his colleagues and élites would tolerate. He was "a Stalinist and anti-Stalinist, a communist believer and cynic, a self-publicizing poltroon and a crusty philanthropist, a trouble-maker and a peacemaker" (line 2694). Counterfactual: had Khrushchev not existed or been less bold, de-Stalinization would have been slower or absent; the Hungarian revolt might have been handled differently; but the underlying institutional rigidities (army, ministries, party apparatus) would likely have constrained any successor similarly. His ouster without bloodshed showed the system had become more institutionalized.

## Historiography
Service emphasizes Khrushchev's animal boldness at the Twentieth Congress, his pragmatic rather than moral case against Stalin, and the limited nature of reform (kolkhozes and capital-goods priority retained; Trotsky and Bukharin not rehabilitated). The "secret speech" was a turning-point that produced relief at home but destroyed legitimacy claims in Eastern Europe. Khrushchev's personality — ebullient, earthy, bossy — both powered his rise and ensured his fall when colleagues tired of reorganizations and unfulfilled consumer promises. The range highlights how his ascendancy depended on Central Committee support and fear of terror's return among opponents (lines 2475–2485, 2503, 2557). 

## From Paxton & Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century*

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton & Hessler]] add texture to the biography
and foreign-policy style already documented above. His first wife died in the 1921
famine; his formal schooling ran only four years, and his wartime responsibility for
Ukraine kept him closer to ordinary soldiers and citizens than most Soviet leaders — a
British journalist, Edward Crankshaw, called him "willing to get mud on his boots."
His September 30, 1960 shoe-pounding at the UN General Assembly accompanied a demand to
end colonial empires and to replace the UN Secretary-General with an East-West-nonaligned
triumvirate. On power consolidation: he initially sided with conservatives against
Malenkov (1953–55), then absorbed Malenkov's consumer-goods and conciliatory
foreign-policy program after Malenkov's 1955 resignation as premier; he endorsed
"polycentrism" as the basis for the new Warsaw Pact and effected rapprochement with Tito
(1955); combined the party-secretary and premier offices by August 1958.

Paxton & Hessler characterize his foreign policy as that of "an ideologically driven
gambler," in contrast to Stalin's cautious search for security — he hoped for peaceful
coexistence with the West but twice (Berlin, Cuba) provoked confrontation only to back
down; came to view the Sino-Soviet rivalry as more dangerous than the US relationship;
courted Third World nationalists in Syria, Egypt, India, Guinea, and Ghana; and famously
warned the West "we will bury you" (November 1956).

On the "Thaw" specifically: named for Ilya Ehrenburg's 1954 novel *The Thaw*; a major
Picasso exhibition ended a 25-year ban on modernism; the high point was Aleksandr
Solzhenitsyn's *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich* (1962), personally vetted by
Khrushchev. Its limits: Boris Pasternak's *Doctor Zhivago* (1957) was published only
abroad, and Pasternak was barred from traveling to accept his Nobel Prize; Khrushchev
personally denounced an exhibit of experimental Soviet art as fit "only to cover urinals
with." He also intensified anti-religious campaigns (the most vigorous since the early
1930s) and enacted the 1957 "parasite law" criminalizing "living on non-labor income" —
applied notoriously against the poet Joseph Brodsky — a reminder that his reformism
should not be confused with liberalism.

On the "virgin lands" campaign and his fall, Paxton & Hessler corroborate the account
above and add that the USSR reversed centuries of grain exports and began importing
American wheat in 1963 after drought and wind erosion ruined at least 16 million acres in
the early 1960s. His raised state purchase prices for peasant crops were arguably his
most positive agricultural reform but provoked labor unrest, notably the 1962
Novocherkassk uprising. Removed October 14, 1964; two days later *Pravda* denounced his
"hare-brained scheming, half-baked conclusions, and hasty decisions... bragging and
bluster."

## Counterfactual Significance
Without Khrushchev's secret speech, Stalin's reputation would have remained largely intact longer, slowing rehabilitations and cultural thaw. The 1956–7 crises in Poland and Hungary might have been avoided or handled with greater violence. The peaceful 1964 ouster demonstrated that the post-Stalin élite had internalized limits on intra-party violence. Yet the deeper problems of over-centralization, misinformation and low initiative that Khrushchev only fitfully addressed would have persisted under any leader drawn from the same Stalinist cohort.
