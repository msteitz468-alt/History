# Tsar Peter III

## Biography / Description
**Tsar Peter III** (born Karl Peter Ulrich, Duke of Holstein-Gottorp) was a grandson of Peter the Great (via his daughter Anne) and nephew of Empress Elizabeth, who ruled as Emperor of Russia for six months in 1762 (L3218-3220). Brought up in Germany to succeed to the Swedish throne, he was brought to Russia in 1742 and educated to succeed Elizabeth (L3218-3222). 

Mentally limited, crude, and deeply hostile to Russian culture and religion, Peter III worshiped Frederick the Great of Prussia (L3222). Shortly after Elizabeth's death, he was overthrown in a palace revolution in July 1762 led by his wife, Catherine the Great, and was subsequently killed in confinement (L3224-3226).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
* **Abolition of Compulsory Gentry Service (1762):** On March 1, 1762 (February 18, OS), Peter III signed the historic edict that abolished compulsory state service for the Russian nobility, allowing them to serve the state or not at will, and even to enter foreign service (L3236-3238).
* **Prussian Peace and Alliance:** Peter III withdrew Russia from the Seven Years' War, saving Frederick the Great from defeat (L3222-3224). He returned all Russian conquests without compensation and signed a military alliance with Prussia (L3224, L3270-3274).
* **Secular Reforms:** He signed the decree to secularize Church lands (completed by Catherine II), abolished the security police, and was prepared to force the Russian Orthodox clergy to dress like Lutheran pastors (L3222, L3338-3340).

## Historical Significance
Peter III's foreign policy reversal alienated the military and political elite, while his hostility toward the Orthodox Church eroded his legitimacy, enabling the coup of 1762 (L3222-3226). 

His edict of 1762 was of monumental social significance. It liberated the gentry from state service, which led to the development of a rich provincial gentry culture and laid the groundwork for the emergence of the independent Russian intelligentsia (L3242-3244). 

However, because the liberation of the gentry was not accompanied by the emancipation of the serfs, it was widely viewed by the peasantry as a structural injustice, fueling decades of agrarian discontent (L3240-3242). In the years following his death, Peter III's name became a powerful myth; he was represented by pretenders as the "good tsar" who had survived the plots of his wife to free the serfs, culminating in the Pugachev Rebellion of 1773–1774 (L3358-3364).
