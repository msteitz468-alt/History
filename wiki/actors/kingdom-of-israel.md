# Kingdom of Israel (the Northern Kingdom)

The **Kingdom of Israel** — the larger, richer **northern kingdom** that seceded from
the Davidic state in 931 BCE — dominated the central Palestinian highlands and the
Jezreel valley for two centuries until [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]] destroyed it
in 722/721 BCE. It is the more powerful but politically more unstable of the two
[[hebrew-kingdom|Hebrew kingdoms]], with a string of dynasties ended by coup.
[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] (Chs. 10–11, T. C. Mitchell) narrates
it from the schism to the eve of Assyrian conquest.

## The Schism (931 BCE)

On [[solomon|Solomon]]'s death the northern tribes assembled at **Shechem** and asked
his son **Rehoboam** to lighten the corvée and taxation; he refused, the labour-chief
Adoram was stoned, and the north seceded under **Jeroboam ben-Nebat**, a former
Solomonic official who had fled to [[shoshenq-campaign-925bce|Shoshenq I of Egypt]]
and now returned. The division was permanent; Israel kept ten tribes, the larger
territory, and the trade corridors, but **no settled dynasty and no equivalent of
Jerusalem's Davidic legitimacy**. Jeroboam set up rival royal sanctuaries with golden
bull-calves at **Bethel and Dan**, condemned ever after by the Judahite biblical
writers as "the sin of Jeroboam."

## Dynastic Instability

Israel's politics were violent: Jeroboam's house was exterminated by **Baasha**, whose
house was in turn destroyed by **Zimri**, who held Tirzah seven days before **Omri**,
the army commander, took power after a civil war against a rival claimant, **Tibni**
(resolved c. 880 BCE). Mitchell notes the biblical text's tendentious editing of these
struggles. Omri founded Israel's first stable and powerful dynasty — see the
[[omride-dynasty|House of Omri]] — and built a new hill-capital, **Samaria**, with
fine Phoenician-influenced ashlar masonry. His son **Ahab** allied with **[[phoenicians|Tyre]]**
through marriage to **Jezebel**, fought [[hazael|Aram-Damascus]], and led the
coalition at the [[battle-of-qarqar-853bce|battle of Qarqar (853)]] — Israel's first
externally datable appearance, in Shalmaneser III's annals.

## Jehu's Revolt and Aramaean Pressure

In **841 BCE** the army officer **[[jehu-revolt-841bce|Jehu]]** annihilated the
Omrides (and the visiting king of [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]]) in a bloody coup,
founding the longest-lived Israelite dynasty. The same year he paid tribute to
**Shalmaneser III** — depicted on the **Black Obelisk**, the only contemporary image
of an Israelite king. Jehu's purge isolated Israel diplomatically just as
**[[hazael|Hazael]] of Damascus** rose; for a generation Hazael stripped Israel of its
Transjordanian and northern territory, reducing Jehu and his son Jehoahaz to near
vassalage.

## The Indian Summer of Jeroboam II

When Assyrian and Aramaean pressure eased in the early eighth century, Israel
recovered spectacularly under **Jeroboam II** (with **[[kingdom-of-judah|Uzziah]]** in
Judah): the two kingdoms together restored borders "almost as far as David's,"
controlled the trade routes, and grew rich. Archaeology at Tirzah and Samaria shows
the new prosperity — and its underside: large mansions beside a walled-off poor
quarter, the **social polarization** that the prophets **Amos and Hosea** denounced.
This was the kingdom's golden age and its last; within a generation of Jeroboam II's
death, Tiglath-pileser III and then Shalmaneser V/Sargon II destroyed it (722/721),
deporting its population (the "lost tribes").

## The Last Kings and the Syro-Ephraimite War (CAH III.2, Ch. 29)

[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 29, Mitchell) details Israel's
violent final decades. After Jeroboam II the throne changed hands by murder: **Menahem**
(who paid tribute to "Pul" = [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]], c. 743),
**Pekahiah**, then the usurper **Pekah ben-Remaliah**. Pekah allied with **Rezin of
Aram-Damascus** to attack [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]] — the **Syro-Ephraimite War** —
trying to force Ahaz into an anti-Assyrian coalition (and to replace him with the puppet
"Ben-Tabeel"), perhaps to control the Transjordanian trade routes. Ahaz instead
**appealed to Assyria**, and Tiglath-pileser's response was devastating: in **733–732**
he stripped Israel of **Galilee, the north, and Transjordan**, organizing them into
Assyrian provinces (Dor, Megiddo, Gilead) and reducing Israel to a rump around Samaria
under the last king **Hoshea**, his own installed nominee.

## The Fall of Samaria (722 BCE)

[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Chs. 22 & 29) supplies the end.
[[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]] had already stripped Israel of Galilee
and Transjordan (732) and reduced it to a rump under the vassal **Hoshea**. When
Hoshea withheld tribute and appealed to **"So, king of Egypt,"** the Assyrians
besieged the capital: **Shalmaneser V** took **Samaria after a two-to-three-year
siege (722)**, and **[[sargon-ii|Sargon II]]** claimed the conquest and carried out
the **[[assyrian-mass-deportation|deportation]]** of the population — the
legendary **"ten lost tribes."** Foreigners were resettled in the province (the
tradition behind the Samaritans), and the northern kingdom ceased to exist, leaving
[[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]] as the sole surviving Hebrew state. See the event page:
**[[fall-of-samaria-722bce]]**.

## Significance

- `caused_by:` the secession of 931, itself `caused_by` Solomonic over-taxation of the
  north (see [[hebrew-kingdom]]).
- `produced:` the prophetic literature of **Amos and Hosea**, born of its eighth-
  century inequality — a foundational strand of biblical religion.
- `opposed_by:` [[hazael|Aram-Damascus]] (its chief regional rival) and finally
  [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]], which `produced:` its destruction and the
  deportation tradition of the "ten lost tribes."

## Liverani: a minimalist reading

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] offers a strongly revisionist
(minimalist) account that should be read alongside the more traditional narrative above.
He treats the origins of Israel as a **local Iron Age process**, not migration or conquest:
"Proto-Israelites" were tribal/pastoral groups occupying the hill country after Egyptian
withdrawal (with an etymological link from *ḫabiru* to "Hebrews"), and the **Exodus,
Joshua's conquest, and the "time of the Judges" are post-exilic constructions** projecting
later concerns backward. He regards the **Davidic–Solomonic united monarchy as
archaeologically implausible** (the Tel Dan "House of David" attests only the name of the
kingdom of Judah; "Solomon's temple/palace" descriptions match an Achaemenid-period plan) —
the **division into Israel (Samaria) and Judah (Jerusalem) being the only element of the
unified-monarchy narrative confirmed externally**. On this reading Israel was the genuinely
dominant Palestinian kingdom (first real state under the **Omrides**), Judah a small,
marginal latecomer. Crucially he explains the **uniqueness of Judaean religious survival
materially**: Assyrian mass-deportation-plus-repopulation produced syncretism in the north,
whereas Babylon deported only a small Judaean elite to a concentrated exile and left the
countryside empty — enabling the law-and-priesthood identity of the **Second Temple**.
Yahweh he reads as originally a **pastoral, possibly non-Jerusalemite** deity within a
polytheistic pantheon (a female consort is epigraphically attested), monotheism crystallising
only with **Hezekiah's and Josiah's** reforms under Assyrian pressure. His governing method:
read the Old Testament as evidence for **the period that composed it** (mostly post-exilic),
not the period it narrates — with the **prophetic books** as the most historically proximate,
politically-coded source. See [[ancient-near-east-historiography]]. *(This minimalist
position is itself contested; the [[hebrew-kingdom]] and traditional accounts above present
the maximalist alternative.)*

## Historiography

The narrative depends overwhelmingly on the **book of Kings**, a Judahite,
Deuteronomistic, retrospective source hostile to the northern dynasties — so Israel's
kings are seen through their enemies' eyes. Mitchell cross-checks it against
**Assyrian synchronisms** (Ahab 853, Jehu 841, Joash 802, Menahem 743) and the
**Moabite Stone** of Mesha, which gives Israel's chief rival's own voice. The result
is a history far better anchored than the united monarchy's, since from 853 onward
external dates fix the chronology. See [[battle-of-qarqar-853bce]] for the keystone
synchronism. The collection is strong on text and Albright-school archaeology, thinner
on later survey archaeology.
