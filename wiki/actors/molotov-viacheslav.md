# Viacheslav Molotov
## Biography / Description
Viacheslav Mikhailovich Molotov (born Skriabin) was born in 1890 in Kukarka (now Sovetsk). He joined the Bolsheviks in 1906 and was active in the underground revolutionary movement. After the October Revolution, he rose to become one of Joseph Stalin's most loyal and longest-serving lieutenants, entering the Politburo in 1926. He served as Chairman of the Council of People's Commissars (Prime Minister) from 1930 to 1941, and as People's Commissar for Foreign Affairs (Foreign Minister) from 1939 to 1949, and again from 1953 to 1956. In 1957, Molotov led the "Anti-Party Group" in an abortive attempt to depose Nikita Khrushchev. Following their defeat, Molotov was dismissed from his high offices and sent into diplomatic exile as ambassador to Mongolia, and later expelled from the Party (reinstated in 1984). He died in Moscow on November 8, 1986, at the age of 96.

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact (1939):** Replacing Maxim Litvinov as Foreign Commissar in May 1939, Molotov signed the nonaggression treaty with Nazi Germany's foreign minister Joachim von Ribbentrop on August 23, 1939. This agreement included the secret protocols that divided Eastern Europe into Soviet and German spheres of influence.
- **Wartime and Post-War Diplomacy:** Molotov represented the Soviet Union at all major wartime and post-war international conferences, including Teheran (1943), Yalta (1945), and Potsdam (1945), alongside Stalin. He was known in the West for his stubbornness and constant use of the veto power during the early United Nations negotiations.
- **Anti-Party Group (1957):** Molotov joined Malenkov, Kaganovich, and Shepilov in attempting to depose Khrushchev in the Party Presidium, objecting to Khrushchev's de-Stalinization policies and erratic economic reorganizations. When Khrushchev successfully appealed to the Central Committee, Molotov was ousted from power.

## Historical Significance
Molotov was the principal diplomat of the Stalinist era, serving as the international face of Soviet foreign policy during World War II and the onset of the Cold War. Unlike most of his peers in Lenin's Politburo, Molotov survived Stalin's purges. Riasanovsky notes that Molotov was a rare first-rank Communist leader who took charge of foreign policy, not personally committed to collective security, which enabled the sudden 1939 pact with Germany (Line 6251). His name remains immortalized in the term "Molotov cocktail," coined by Finnish soldiers during the Winter War.
