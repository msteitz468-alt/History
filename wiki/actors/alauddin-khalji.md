# Ala-ud-din Khalji

**Ala-ud-din Khalji** (r. 1296–1316) was the most powerful ruler of the [[delhi-sultanate|Delhi
Sultanate]] and the sovereign under whom Sultanate power reached its territorial and
administrative apogee — repelling the Mongols, extending Muslim arms for the first time deep into
the **Deccan**, and imposing the most centralised and interventionist regime medieval India had
seen. From the [[cambridge-history-of-india-v3|Cambridge History of India, Vol. III]] (read
against the [[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|Thapar]] account on [[delhi-sultanate]]).

## Rise to power

Ala-ud-din was a nephew and son-in-law of Sultan **Jalal-ud-din Khalji**, whom he murdered in
1296 after returning laden with plunder from a daring raid on **Devagiri (Deogir)** in the
Deccan — the first Sultanate thrust into the south. He bought the loyalty of the nobility and the
populace with the treasure and seized the throne at Delhi.

## The Mongol defence

The defining external threat of his reign was the repeated invasion of the **Mongols** of the
Chagatai khanate, who reached the gates of Delhi. Ala-ud-din met them with a large, well-supplied
**standing army** and a fortified frontier, defeating successive incursions — one of the few
medieval rulers to halt the Mongols decisively. This military requirement drove his domestic
reforms.

## The reforms

To sustain a huge army cheaply, Ala-ud-din built an unusually **centralised fiscal and economic
state** (detailed on [[delhi-sultanate]]):
- He **revoked existing land grants**, measured the land, and raised the state's share of produce
  to **one-half**, collected directly so as to break the intermediary chiefs' surplus.
- He imposed famous **market and price controls** in Delhi — fixed prices for grain, cloth,
  horses, and slaves, enforced by inspectors and state granaries — so that soldiers' low cash pay
  retained its value.
- To forestall conspiracies he **banned alcohol and unsanctioned noble marriages** and ran an
  efficient **espionage** network.

## The conquest of the Deccan

Ala-ud-din's general **Malik Kafur** (a Hindu eunuch convert) led campaigns (1307–11) that
subdued the Yadavas of Devagiri, the Kakatiyas of Warangal, and the Hoysalas and Pandyas of the
far south — exacting vast tribute rather than annexing. These intrusions destabilised the Hindu
Deccan and, within a generation, threw up the successor states of [[vijayanagara-bahmani|
Vijayanagara and the Bahmani sultanate]]. In the north he stormed the great Rajput fortresses of
**Ranthambhor** and **Chitor**.

## Significance

Ala-ud-din showed how far a medieval Indian state could be centralised, and how the Sultanate
could project power across the whole subcontinent. But his system was a personal tour de force: it
depended on his will and his espionage, and **collapsed quickly after his death** (1316) amid the
intrigues of Malik Kafur — exposing the structural fragility that the [[tughluq|Tughluqs]] would
inherit. Counterfactually, his Mongol defence preserved the Sultanate at a moment when north
India might otherwise have shared the fate of Baghdad.
