# al-Ḥajjāj ibn Maṭar

**al-Ḥajjāj ibn Maṭar** (fl. c. 786–833 CE) was one of the most important translators of the early Abbasid period, responsible for the **first Arabic translation of Ptolemy's *Almagest*** — the foundational text of Greek mathematical astronomy. He is historically significant not merely as a translator but as the first documented instance of an Islamic scientist **critically correcting a Greek astronomical text** in the act of translating it.

## The Almagest Translation

Al-Ḥajjāj produced the earliest surviving Arabic translation of the *Almagest*, likely during the reign of al-Ma'mūn (813–833 CE), though his work may have begun earlier under Hārūn al-Rashīd. The translation was a landmark event in the [[the-translation-movement|translation movement]], making the most technically demanding text of Greek astronomy available in Arabic for the first time.

## Correction of the Lunar Month

The most consequential detail of al-Ḥajjāj's translation is a correction he made to *Almagest* IV.2, where Ptolemy reports the length of the lunar month. Ptolemy states that he was following Hipparchus, who had derived the lunar month from two eclipses separated by 126,007 days and 1 hour during which the moon completed 4,267 revolutions. Ptolemy gives the result of dividing these quantities as:

**29 days, 31 minutes, 50 seconds, 8 thirds, 20 fourths** (29;31,50,8,20d)

However, if one actually carries out the division 126,007d 1h ÷ 4,267, the correct result is:

**29;31,50,8,**9**,20d**

This is precisely the number that appears in al-Ḥajjāj's Arabic translation. Al-Ḥajjāj corrected — silently — what he perceived as a scribal or computational error in the Greek text. Whether this was in fact an error in the Ptolemaic original or a textual variant has been debated in modern scholarship (Asger Aaboe addressed the question). What is historically significant is that al-Ḥajjāj *identified it as an error* and acted accordingly.

## Social Context of the Correction

George Saliba ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]], ch. 3) argues that this correction is only intelligible in the context of Abbasid social competition. Al-Ḥajjāj was working in an environment where the importers of Greek science were under constant attack from opponents who wished to discredit the "foreign sciences." Any flaw in the translated texts could be used to denounce the entire enterprise. Al-Ḥajjāj therefore "could not afford to have what looked like a mistake in the translation, and took it upon himself to correct the Greek text."

This is the key point: the correction was not incidental scholarly tidying. It reflects a **structural imperative of critical engagement** built into the social conditions of the translation movement. The translator had both the mathematical competence to identify the error (itself evidence of prior training) and the competitive motivation to fix it.

> "The classic narrative could not possibly explain such nuances in the translation process, for it did not pay any attention to the competition generated by 'Abd al-Malik's reforms, nor did it acknowledge the experience gained by the translators of the elementary sciences of the dīwān for a generation or two that could give someone like al-Ḥajjāj the necessary skill to carry out the correction." (Saliba, ch. 3)

## Significance for the Historiography of Islamic Science

Al-Ḥajjāj's correction demonstrates that the Islamic reception of Greek science was **critical from the outset** — not a generation or century after translation, but in the very act of translation itself. This undermines the classical narrative that Islamic scholars passively transmitted Greek texts before later Western scholars improved upon them.

The correction also presupposes a level of mathematical competence in al-Ḥajjāj sufficient to carry out the long division and compare the result with the Ptolemaic text — evidence that the "preliminary" generations of translators of administrative texts had, as Saliba argues, already created a baseline of mathematical training across Abbasid society.
