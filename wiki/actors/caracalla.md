# Caracalla

**Marcus Aurelius Antoninus**, nicknamed **Caracalla** (188–217, r. 198/211–217), elder son of
[[septimius-severus|Septimius Severus]] and Julia Domna, is best remembered for the
[[constitutio-antoniniana-212|Constitutio Antoniniana]] granting citizenship to (nearly) all the
free inhabitants of the empire, and for his fratricidal murder of his brother **Geta**. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 1 (**Brian Campbell**).

## Reign

Intended by his father to rule jointly with Geta, Caracalla had his brother **murdered in their
mother's arms (26 Dec. 211)** and purged his supporters. Unpopular with the senate — he openly
preferred his soldiers, raised their pay heavily, posed as **"fellow-soldier,"** and ruthlessly
taxed and requisitioned to pay for it — he nonetheless kept the machinery of administration and law
functioning (the Dmeir inscription shows his competent courtroom technique). He campaigned on the
**Rhine and Danube** (against the Alamanni, taking the name *Germanicus Maximus*), ordered a
**massacre at Alexandria** (215), and launched a Parthian expedition in imitation of Alexander
before being murdered near **Carrhae (8 April 217)** in a plot by his praetorian prefect **Macrinus**,
who briefly seized the throne.

## The Antonine Constitution

In **212** Caracalla issued the **Constitutio Antoniniana**, granting Roman citizenship to virtually
all free inhabitants of the empire. Campbell calls it "the greatest enigma of the reign." Dio's
cynical explanation (to widen the inheritance- and manumission-tax base) is doubted; Caracalla's own
words suggest an **effusive act of grand patronage** giving thanks for a great occasion. Whatever the
motive, its practical significance was muted: the value of citizenship was already declining as the
**honestiores/humiliores** status distinction increasingly governed how the state treated people.

## The end of the dynasty

After Caracalla, the [[severan-dynasty|dynasty]] limped on through the equestrian usurper **Macrinus**
(217–18), the priest-emperor **Elagabalus** (218–22, whose flaunting of the Emesene sun-god alienated
Rome), and the well-meaning but dominated **Severus Alexander** (222–35), murdered with his mother
Julia Mamaea by the mutinous Rhine army — opening the [[third-century-crisis|fifty-year crisis]].

## Significance

Caracalla embodied the Severan paradox: a cultured, articulate ruler who could quote Euripides, yet
who governed by **isolating himself among his troops** and despising the senate. His citizenship
edict — whatever its intent — capped the long process of Roman enfranchisement, formally making the
empire's free population **Romans**, even as that status lost much of its old meaning.
