# Ezana of Aksum

**Ezana** (reigned c. 320s–360s CE) was the Aksumite king whose conversion to Christianity transformed Aksum into one of the earliest Christian states in the world, and whose military campaigns — documented in his own inscriptions — extended Aksumite power across the northeastern Horn and into the Sudanese Nile valley, culminating in the destruction of the kingdom of Kush (Meroe) c. 350 CE.

## Overview

Ezana is the best-documented Aksumite king, known from: seven royal stone inscriptions at Aksum (three definitely his, two probably, one possibly, plus RIE 186 attributable to him or his predecessor Ella Amida); a series of coins; a letter addressed to him by the Roman Emperor Constantius II in 356/7; and from the fifth-century church historians Rufinus, Socrates Scholasticus, Theodoret, and Sozomen (all derived ultimately from Rufinus). His father was **Ella Amida** (also called Ousanas on the coinage). His **bisi** name — designating probably a matrilineal clan — was "Alene."

### The inscriptions and their religious arc

Ezana's stone inscriptions divide into two distinct religious phases and are preserved in three scripts: ASAM-derived Ge'ez (unvocalised, right-to-left), Ethiopic Ge'ez (with incipient vocalisation, left-to-right), and Greek. The trilingual stones stood beside the main roads into Aksum. Key inscriptions:

- **RIE 186** (Ella Amida or Ezana): king described as "son of the invincible Mahrem"; no Christian elements.
- **RIE 188** (Ezana, pre-Christian): king invokes Astar, Beher, and Meder and makes a thank-offering to Mahrem.
- **RIE 185/270 and 185bis/270bis** (trilingual, same campaign): Ezana calls himself "son of the unconquered Mahrem [Ares]"; dedicates to Mahrem, Astar, and Beher; Greek version of 270bis adds "Lord of Heaven and Earth" — possibly an attempted Greek rendering of the Ge'ez pagan deity-names rather than a Christian reference.
- **RIE 189**: No deities named; repeatedly attributes Ezana's kingship and military success to "the might of the Lord of Heaven" — monotheist but not explicitly Christian.
- **RIE 190/271** (Noba and Kasu campaign): Greek text (271) invokes "faith in God," names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, calls Ezana "servant of Christ," and designates Christ as "the god in whom I have believed" — the only Aksumite inscription referencing the Trinity explicitly. The Ge'ez version (190) lacks these named Christian references, suggesting the Greek version was amplified for a foreign audience.

The coins corroborate this trajectory: pre-Christian coins bear the crescent-and-disc over the royal portrait; Ezana's later coins replace this with the Christian cross. Some intermediate coins bear no religious symbol at all — possibly reflecting uncertainty about popular acceptance of the new religion.

### Title formula

Ezana's inscriptions followed a set formula: "king of Aksum and of [Himyar, Raydan, Saba, Salhan — territories in southern Arabia — and Seyamo, Bega, Kasu — territories in the Horn and Nile valley], king of kings." The Arabian titles were essentially anachronistic prestige-claims by the fourth century, not indicators of active control. Phillipson notes the primary title was always "king of Aksum," placed before "king of kings" and the peripheral list.

Before conversion, Aksumite royal inscriptions included "son of the unconquered Mahrem" — a claim of divine or semi-divine status. After conversion, this was discontinued and replaced by a human patronymic ("son of Ella Amida").

## Role and Major Decisions

### Adoption of Christianity (c. 337–350 CE)
The account originates with Rufinus of Aquileia's *Ecclesiastical History* (late 4th c.): a Syrian traveller Meropius and his young companions Frumentius and Aedesius were shipwrecked on the Red Sea coast; Meropius was killed; the two young men were taken to the Aksumite court. After serving in the royal household, Frumentius proceeded to Alexandria and asked Patriarch Athanasius to appoint a bishop for the growing Christian community at Aksum. Athanasius consecrated Frumentius himself, who returned to Aksum as its first bishop (known in the Ethiopian church as **Abba Salama**, "Father of Peace"). Ezana's conversion followed.

The date falls between 328 (Athanasius' initial elevation) and 356/7 (Constantius II's letter confirming Frumentius as active bishop). Phillipson favours **c. 337–350** as the best estimate; Ethiopian ecclesiastical tradition gives 333 EC ≈ AD 340. The conversion was initially more openly expressed to Greek-reading foreigners and circulating currency-users than to Ezana's Ge'ez-speaking subjects; Christianity spread slowly through the broader population over a century or more.

A letter from Emperor **Constantius II** (reigned 337–61), preserved in Athanasius' writings, was addressed to Ezana and "Sazana" (probably a military commander, not a co-ruler), urging that Frumentius be sent to Alexandria for examination by Constantius' Arian patriarch George of Cappadocia. There is no evidence the letter reached Aksum or was acted upon; Phillipson thinks it was probably intercepted in Alexandria.

### Conquest of Kush (c. 349–360 CE)
Inscriptions RIE 190 and 271 record a major campaign against the **Noba** (Nubians) and **Kasu** (Kush), departing Aksum on the 8th day of the month Magabit (mid-March). The campaigns culminated in the destruction of Meroe's last remnants. The date 349, 355, or 360 CE (years when 8 Magabit fell on a Saturday, as the inscriptions specify) is most widely accepted.

### Military campaigns
Ezana's inscriptions record multiple campaigns: against the **Bega** (Beja/Blemmyes, east of the Nile); against the **Agwezat** (whose king paid tribute); and against various peoples of the western and southwestern periphery. Captives were taken and resettled at a place called Matlia, both weakening conquered territories and augmenting Aksumite labour resources.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Ezana's politically motivated adoption of Christianity, Aksum would not have become the anchor of the Coptic-aligned Monophysite Christianity that survived the kingdom's political collapse and became the Ethiopian Orthodox Church — the central institution of all subsequent Ethiopian civilisation through the Zagwe, the restored Solomonid dynasty, and the modern state. Whether a non-Christian Aksum would have found an alternative basis for Aksumite cultural identity is unclear; the link with Alexandria provided theological, literary, and institutional continuity that outlasted Aksumite political power by centuries.

## Historiography

The primary evidence is the inscriptions (RIE) and the coinage. Rufinus' account of the conversion is the sole near-contemporary source and has been transmitted through multiple fifth-century church historians (Socrates Scholasticus, Theodoret, Sozomen), all derived from Rufinus. The Ethiopian church tradition credits the conversion to kings "Abraha and Atsbaha," apparently a confusion of Ezana's era with the sixth-century king Kaleb (whose alternative name was Ella Atsbaha). Phillipson's *Foundations* (2012) provides the most thorough synthesis of epigraphic, numismatic, and archaeological evidence for Ezana's reign. The "two Ezanas" theory (one fourth-century, one fifth-century) was once debated but is now broadly rejected on numismatic grounds. The exact date of conversion remains disputed within the range 337–356.

Key issues: the degree to which conversion was a political calculation (parallel with the kingdom of Iberia/Kartli); the extent to which Christianity was initially a foreign-elite religion; and the interpretation of the Ge'ez vs. Greek divergences in the trilingual inscriptions.
