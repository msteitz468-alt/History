# The Dutch Overseas Empire

The global commercial empire built by the **Dutch Republic** in the seventeenth and eighteenth
centuries — a **two-ocean system** spanning the Atlantic and Indian Oceans, run not chiefly by the state
but by **chartered companies**, and marked by the paradox of **religious tolerance alongside monopoly
and slavery**. Treated comparatively in **[[emmer-dutch-overseas-empire|Emmer & Gommans, *The Dutch
Overseas Empire, 1600–1800*]]**.

## Structure

The empire was largely the work of two **joint-stock chartered companies**:

- the **[[dutch-east-india-company|Dutch East India Company (VOC)]]** (founded 1602) — which seized much
  of the **Portuguese** position in maritime Asia, built a network from **Batavia** (Java) across the
  Indonesian archipelago, Ceylon, the Cape, India, and the sole European foothold in **Japan** (Deshima),
  enforcing **spice monopolies** by force;
- the **Dutch West India Company (WIC)** (founded 1621) — which warred on the Iberians in the **Atlantic**,
  briefly held **Brazil** and **New Netherland** (New York), took West African slaving posts, and built
  the plantation colonies of the **Caribbean and Guianas** on enslaved African labour.

## Character and Significance

The Dutch empire was the commercial and naval spearhead of the **Dutch Golden Age**, channeling the
wealth of global trade into the **[[merchant-republic|merchant republic]]** at home. Emmer and Gommans
stress its **paradoxes** and its place in a **comparative and indigenous** frame: a society famed for
**toleration** that nonetheless ran brutal monopolies (the Banda massacre) and a major share of the
**Atlantic slave trade**; an empire shaped as much by the **Asian and Atlantic societies** the Dutch
entered as by Dutch design. By 1800 the VOC had collapsed (1799) and the Atlantic system was in decline,
but the empire left lasting marks — Indonesia, the Cape, the Guianas, and the wider patterns of
**company colonialism** and global commerce.
