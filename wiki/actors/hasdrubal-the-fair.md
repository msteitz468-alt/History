# Hasdrubal the Fair

**Hasdrubal** (called "the Fair" or "the Splendid" to distinguish him from
[[hasdrubal-barca|Hasdrubal Barca]], Hannibal's brother) was the son-in-law and political
heir of [[hamilcar-barca|Hamilcar Barca]], and the second of the three Barcid rulers of
[[carthage|Carthage]]. He governed the Punic empire in Spain from Hamilcar's death
(229/228 BC) until his own assassination in **221 BC**, when command passed to
[[hannibal|Hannibal]]. He is the crucial connective figure in the [[barcid-dynasty|Barcid
dynasty]]: the man who consolidated Hamilcar's conquests into a durable land-empire and
made the succession from father to son workable.

> **Note.** Two Barcid-era Hasdrubals are easily confused. *Hasdrubal the Fair* (this
> page) is Hamilcar's **son-in-law**, d. 221. [[hasdrubal-barca|Hasdrubal Barca]] is
> Hamilcar's **son** and Hannibal's brother, killed at the Metaurus in 207. A third,
> Hasdrubal son of Gisco, was a later non-Barcid rival general.

## Role and Career

Hasdrubal rose as Hamilcar's lieutenant and married his daughter, cementing his place in
the family's inner circle. On Hamilcar's death campaigning in Spain (229/228), the army
and then the Carthaginian citizens elected Hasdrubal general — the dynasty's power passing
by a blend of hereditary expectation and formal election that Hoyos treats as its
defining constitutional feature.

Where the conventional picture casts Hamilcar as the conqueror and Hasdrubal as the
diplomat, Hoyos stresses that **consolidation by treaty, conciliation and marriage
alliance** was itself a deliberate imperial method. Hasdrubal:

- **Founded New Carthage** (Qart Hadasht / Cartagena, c. 227/226 BC), the great harbour-
  capital of Punic Spain, a base for its silver mines and a monument to Barcid wealth.
- Married a Spanish princess and **arranged to be acclaimed supreme leader (*strategos
  autokrator*) by the peoples of Punic Spain** — a symbolic, Hellenistic-style act judged
  worthwhile for building Spanish loyalty.
- Concluded the **Ebro accord** with Rome (c. 225 BC), fixing the River Ebro as the limit
  of Punic advance — an agreement whose interpretation became central to the outbreak of
  the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second Punic War]].

He was **assassinated in 221 BC** by an assailant avenging a personal grievance, and
Hannibal — already serving under him in Spain — was at once elected to the command.

## Counterfactual Significance

Hasdrubal the Fair is the hinge on which the Barcid dynasty turned. Without his decade of
consolidation the Spanish empire Hamilcar began might not have survived its founder's
death, and Hannibal would have inherited neither the resource base nor the Spanish
alliances that made the invasion of Italy possible. His preference for diplomacy over war
also shows the Barcid empire was not simply a military project — a point central to
Hoyos's reading of the dynasty.

## Historiography

Evidence for Hasdrubal is especially thin: Hoyos notes his doings are among the least
well documented of any Barcid, reconstructed from scattered notices in Polybius, Diodorus
and others, all Roman-filtered. The date and terms of the Ebro accord, and whether
Saguntum was already a Roman associate at that time, are debated.
