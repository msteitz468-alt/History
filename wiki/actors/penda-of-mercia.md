## Overview

Penda was the dominant figure of mid-seventh-century English politics: a pagan warlord from the Iclingas dynasty of [[mercia|Mercia]] who killed or outlasted three northern Anglian overlords — [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]], [[oswald-of-northumbria|Oswald]], and nearly [[oswiu-of-northumbria|Oswiu]] — and transformed Mercia from a peripheral midland polity into the most powerful kingdom south of the Humber. Bede described him as "a most vigorous young man of the royal race of the Mercians" (HE II, 20). He was killed at the [[battle-of-the-winwaed-655|battle of the Winwæd]] in November 655 or 656.

## Origins and Dynasty

Descent from Icel was "claimed for Penda, certainly in the course of time, through his father Pybba, also reputedly the father of Eowa and Coenwealh." The Mercian kings until at least the time of Ceolwulf (821–823) claimed descent from either Penda or one of his brothers. His father Pybba is said to have had twelve sons — a hagiographic number indicating that many princely lines traced ancestry to him. There is a possibility that Penda's family derived from eastern Anglian lands (via the place-name element Icel in eastern England), which would make the embryonic Mercian kingdom a meeting-ground of Anglian political influences from the north and the east.

Penda's brothers included Eowa, who may have ruled the northern Mercians as a subject king of the northern Angles until his death at Maserfelth. Penda gave a sister in marriage to Cenwealh, king of the Thames valley Saxons, whom he subsequently expelled when repudiated, c. 650/1.

## Rise and the Overthrow of Eadwine

Penda first appears in the record at Cirencester (c. 628 on the ASC, though Kirby suspects this is a few years too early), where he "fought Cwichelm and Cynegils" and gains secured western expansion. He allied with Cadwallon ap Cadfan, king of Gwynedd, to destroy [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]] at [[battle-of-hatfield-chase-633|Hatfield Chase]] in October 633 or 634. Kirby dates Penda's kingship from this victory — twenty-two years to his death at the Winwæd in 655 or 656 (or 634–656 if Eadwine died in 634). He carried off Eadwine's son Eadfrith to Mercia — "retaining an interest in the royal succession among the Deirans" — but killed him during Oswald's reign.

## The Defeat and Killing of Oswald

Following [[oswald-of-northumbria|Oswald]]'s re-establishment of northern hegemony, Penda invaded Bernicia at an unknown date, "storming Bamburgh and endeavouring to fire it (HE III, 16)." He killed both Sigeberht and Ecgric of East Anglia c. 640/1, ending Raedwald's dynastic line. In 642 or 643 he slew Oswald at the [[battle-of-hatfield-chase-633|battle of Maserfelth]] (Old Oswestry), with possible Welsh allies including Cynddylan of Powys, and "dismembered" his body. The death of Eowa, the Mercian co-king, in the same battle left Penda as sole king of the Mercians, "without question the most powerful Mercian ruler so far to have emerged in the midlands." The Historia Brittonum records that Penda then "separated the Southumbrians from the Northumbrians (HB ch. 65)," fracturing Oswald's Humbrian confederacy.

## Expansion and Coalition-Building

After Maserfelth, Penda attacked the eastern Angles between 649/50 and 652, temporarily expelling King Anna, then invaded again in 654 or 655 and slew Anna. He established Anna's brother Aethelhere in his place as a dependent ally. He gave his daughter Cyneburh in marriage to Oswiu's son Alhfrith and allowed his own son Peada, ruler of the Middle Angles, to be baptized by Bishop Fínán of Lindisfarne at Ad Murum c. 653/654 — while remaining pagan himself.

By November 655 or 656 Penda assembled a coalition of thirty duces against [[oswiu-of-northumbria|Oswiu]], including Cadafael of Gwynedd, Aethelhere of East Anglia, and Oethelwald king of Deira (Oswald's son), and drove Oswiu north to the fortress of Iudeu (probably Stirling). He extracted tribute and a hostage (Ecgfrith, aged 10) from Oswiu and then turned south — but Oswiu caught him on the banks of the Winwæd.

## Death and Legacy

At the [[battle-of-the-winwaed-655|battle of the Winwæd]], Cadafael of Gwynedd and Oethelwald of Deira withdrew before battle was joined; "Aethelhere, king of the eastern Angles, and the majority of Penda's duces perished in the ensuing battle in which, according to Bede, more died in the flood-waters of the river than were slain by the sword (HE III, 24)." Penda was killed — probably decapitated by Oswiu in symbolic avenging of the dismemberment of Oswald's body.

His son Peada received the southern Mercians as Oswiu's sub-king; another son, Wulfhere, led a successful coup c. 658–659 that expelled Oswiu's governors and established Mercian independence under the line Penda had founded.

## Chronology

Kirby's preferred scheme: twenty-two years from 633 (or 634) to 655 (or 656). The ASC A's attribution of a thirty-year reign starting at 626 — making Penda an octogenarian at the Winwæd — is "not credible" given his two young sons Wulfhere and Aethelred at his death. The Historia Brittonum's ten-year reign probably derives from a misreading (x for xxii). "There can be no doubt that twenty-two years is a sounder regnal length for Penda."

## Counterfactual Significance

Penda's career halted Roman Christianity's northward spread for a generation and structurally reshaped English politics by breaking the northern Anglian hegemony that might otherwise have endured under Deiran or Bernician leadership. Without him, Mercia's rise to dominance in the eighth century might have been delayed or prevented entirely.

## Historiography

Kirby's principal source-critical point concerns the regnal chronology: the ASC A's thirty-year figure and the Historia Brittonum's ten-year figure both fail against Bede's twenty-two years, which can be cross-checked against the dateable battle of the Winwæd. Penda's paganism is treated by Bede as a defining characteristic but Kirby notes that in practice it did not prevent his son's conversion or the baptism of the Middle Angles during his lifetime. The debate over Eowa's role — whether he was a subject king of Oswald or a co-ruler in the northern Mercians — remains open; Kirby leaves both possibilities open.
