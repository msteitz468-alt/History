# Aegina

**Aegina** — the small, vigorous island in the Saronic Gulf — was one of the great
**mercantile states** of Archaic Greece, "her whole flourishing economy" resting on
long trade-lines to non-Greek lands north, south and west. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH
Vol. IV]] (Ch. 6, L. H. Jeffery) sets her between Sparta's League and her bitter maritime
rival [[athens|Athens]].

## A trading power

Aegina's population were "merchant seamen" (Aristotle). She was a **founder-member of
[[naucratis]]** in Egypt early in the sixth century, and c. 519 a band of Aeginetans
seized **Cydonia** at the western end of Crete, overlooking the route to the Egyptian
Delta. Her reach ran west to Italy: the merchant **Sostratus of Aegina** — whom Herodotus
calls the richest Greek of all time — is now tracked archaeologically through the **"SO"
trademark** on Attic red-figure ware in **Etruria** and a dedication c. 500 to "Aeginetan
Apollo" at **Graviscae** (the port of Tarquinia), his wealth drawn from Umbrian slaves,
Venetian horses, timber, grain and Etruscan goldwork. Such profits paid for the superb
pedimental sculptures of the **temple of Aphaia**. Aegina also gave its name to a major
weight-and-coinage standard (the **Aeginetic standard**), and may have supplied the
technical experts who began the first Boeotian coinage.

## The "war without heralds" with Athens

In the late 490s the **"war without heralds"** (*polemos akeryktos*) broke out between
Aegina and [[athens|Athens]] — not a formal land-war but "an unhealed sore" of ship-on-sight
attacks and coastal raids across the Saronic Gulf. Enmity to "piratical Aeginetans" drew
[[corinth|Corinth]] (whose port Cenchreae shared the gulf) to Athens' side; Corinth sold
Athens twenty ships for the war. Aegina appealed to [[argos|Argos]], but post-Sepea Argos
sent no official aid — only 1,000 volunteers, who were defeated with heavy loss.

## Medism, Salamis, and after

When Darius' heralds came (491), Aegina **submitted (earth and water)** — "trade was her
life-blood," and the Persian conquest of Egypt had hit her hard. At Athens' request King
[[cleomenes-i|Cleomenes]] tried to arrest the Aeginetan **medizers**, taking ten hostages
to Athens. The rivalry drove the most consequential decision of the era: in **482**
Themistocles persuaded Athens to spend the Laurium silver windfall on a fleet **"to use
against Aegina"** — the 200 triremes that won **[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]**. In
the crisis of 480 Aegina and Athens shelved their feud to face Persia; Aegina won the
**first prize for valour (aristeia)** at Salamis. The feud nonetheless persisted until Athens
finally subdued and (431) expelled the island.

## Causal significance

- Aegina's commercial wealth made it the **maritime measure of Athens** — and the immediate
  spur to the **Themistoclean navy**, which "by accident" became the instrument of Greek
  survival against Persia.
- Her trade with Etruria and Egypt documents the **integration of the Archaic Mediterranean**
  economy linking the Aegean, the Nile and the Tyrrhenian.

## Historiography

Drawn from **Herodotus** (the unheralded war, the medism, Salamis), Aristotle's *Politics*,
and a rich body of **archaeology and epigraphy** — the Sostratus trademark, the Graviscae
dedication, the Aphaia sculptures, and the coin standard. The date and degree of Aegina's
**League membership** ("coerced," she claimed) are unclear.
