## Overview

Tudhaliya IV (r. c.1237–1209 BCE) was the penultimate Great King of [[hittites|Hatti]] and the son of [[hattusili-iii|Hattusili III]] and the formidable queen [[puduhepa]]. His reign represents a kingdom simultaneously reaching its greatest material and cultural expression while straining under mounting external pressure and internal dynastic instability. He was not the obvious heir: his elder brother Nerikkaili had held the tuhkanti office, but Hattusili removed him and installed Tudhaliya in the succession, apparently after careful planning. Hattusili had groomed Tudhaliya along his own career path — assigned to the service of Ishtar of Samuha, priest of the Storm God of Nerik, governor of Hakpis, and Chief of the Bodyguards (GAL MESHEDI) — before formally confirming the succession.

## Rise to Power and Early Reign

Tudhaliya's elevation was politically complicated from the start. His older brother Nerikkaili's wife was the daughter of a vassal king; Tudhaliya's own wife was probably the daughter of the Babylonian king Kudur-Enlil (c.1254–1246), a match Puduhepa appears to have arranged: "taking as daughter-in-law a foreigner, the daughter of a Great King" (KUB xxi 38, obv. 47–9). This superior marriage elevated Tudhaliya's status above his brother's within the court. Even so, once Tudhaliya acceded, court factions hardened — supporters and opponents of Puduhepa divided the women of the palace, with Tudhaliya's own wife apparently leading an anti-Puduhepa faction. The queen mother continued to exercise her official powers in both domestic and foreign affairs well into Tudhaliya's reign.

His throne was also under direct physical threat. A conspiracy by his half-brother Heshni — joined by high-ranking dignitaries — aimed to assassinate Tudhaliya during a royal visit to Hattina in the region of Nerik; a second attempt by poison was planned should the first fail. The plot was discovered and Heshni arrested. Tudhaliya's response was a remarkable public demand for unconditional loyalty from his dignitaries:

> "My Sun has many brothers and there are many sons of his father. The Land of Hatti is full of the royal line . . . With regard to kingship, you must acknowledge no other person (but me, Tudhaliya)." (KUB xxvi 1, i 9–29)

These are the words of a king who recognized that "his throne was far from secure, and that the greatest danger to it came from possible rival claimants within his own family." Similar language pervades his treaty with Shaushgamuwa of [[amurru]], warning explicitly against acting as the vassal Masturi had done by abandoning his overlord's heir.

## The Western Frontier and the Milawata Letter

In the west, Tudhaliya inherited a deteriorating situation. [[ahhiyawa-question|Ahhiyawa]] continued to support insurrectionist activity in Hittite vassal states. A rebellion in the Seha River Land, led by Tarhunaradu who had Ahhiyawan backing, was crushed: Tarhunaradu and his family were transported to Hatti, along with "500 (teams of) horse" and many prisoners. Tudhaliya restored the vassal throne to a descendant of Muwawalwi.

A key document for his western policy is the Milawata letter — a text pieced together when Professor Harry Hoffner discovered in 1981 that a Berlin fragment joined the original document. The letter reveals that Tudhaliya had regained overlordship of Milawata, and was establishing a new regional power-sharing arrangement: a local ruler (most likely Tarkasnawa, king of Mira, the subject of the Karabel inscription) was addressed as "my son" and granted authority as regional overlord over neighboring vassal states including [[wilusa]]. Simultaneously Tudhaliya sought the restoration of Walmu, the deposed king of Wilusa, who had remained loyal and was currently in the custody of the letter's addressee: "send Walmu to me, and I will install him as king again in Wilusa" (KUB xix 55 + KUB xlviii 90, rev. 38'–42').

With Milawata back under pro-Hittite control, the Ahhiyawan king was erased from the list of Great Kings in the Shaushgamuwa treaty: "once excluded from these regions, the Ahhiyawan king was no longer considered a Great King."

## The Assyrian Threat and the Battle of Nihriya

Assyria posed the gravest external danger. Tudhaliya initially supported the Hanigalbatean king Shattuara II against Shalmaneser I, but Shalmaneser's forces crushed Hanigalbat, blinding 14,400 prisoners, and Assyrian power reached the east bank of the Euphrates. When Shalmaneser died and was succeeded by Tukulti-Ninurta (c.1233), Tudhaliya wrote in conciliatory terms. The Assyrian king initially feigned friendship while preparing to subjugate the Subari lands north of Nihriya, which would give him control of routes into Anatolia and the strategic copper mines at Ergani Maden.

Tudhaliya reinforced Nihriya with Hittite troops and refused to withdraw when Tukulti-Ninurta demanded it. The battle that followed ended in Hittite defeat. Tudhaliya's own letter to a vassal (probably the king of Isuwa) captures the humiliation: "As (the situation) turned difficult for me, you kept yourself somewhere away from me. Beside me you were not! Have I not fled from Nihriya alone?" (KBo iv 14 ii 7). The Shaushgamuwa treaty was likely drawn up in this context, imposing a commercial embargo on [[assyria-middle]]: "No merchant of yours is to go to the Land of Assyria" (Shaushgamuwa Treaty, iv 12–20). Tukulti-Ninurta then turned to Babylon, defeating and capturing the Kassite king Kashtiliash IV, which effectively ended the Assyrian threat to Hatti.

## The Conquest of Alasiya

Tudhaliya undertook a surprise campaign against Alasiya (Cyprus), commemorated on a statue inscription preserved in a copy from Suppiluliuma II's reign:

> "I seized the king of Alasiya with his wives, his children . . . All the goods, including silver and gold, and all the captured people I removed and brought home to Hattusa. I enslaved the country of Alasiya, and made it tributary on the spot." (KUB xii 38 i, 3–8)

The probable motivation was the grain supply route. Hatti had become increasingly dependent on grain shipments from Egypt and Canaan, transhipped through [[ugarit]] to the Anatolian port of Ura. A hostile Alasiya could disrupt those routes. His victory proved temporary — his son Suppiluliuma II had to fight sea battles off Alasiya's coast.

## The Kurunta Crisis

Tudhaliya relied heavily on his cousin [[kurunta]], second son of [[muwatalli-ii]], as his most important dynastic ally, formalizing their relationship in the famous Bronze Tablet treaty (discovered 1986 beneath the pavement near the Yerkapı Gate at [[hattusha]]). He granted Kurunta additional territories, freedom to choose his successor in [[tarhuntassa]], reduced taxes and corvées, and formally acknowledged his status as equivalent to the Syrian viceroys: "second only to the Great King in Hattusa."

Seal impressions discovered at [[hattusha]] reading "Kurunta, Great King, Labarna, My Sun," and a rock relief at Hatip (17 km southwest of Konya) reading "Kurunta, the Great King, [the Hero], the son of [Mu]wattalli, the Great King, the Hero" strongly suggest that Kurunta eventually seized the Hittite throne, possibly in the aftermath of Tudhaliya's defeat at Nihriya. Archaeological evidence indicates destruction of parts of Hattusa during Tudhaliya's reign, possibly from armed conflict between Kurunta's and Tudhaliya's forces. If the coup occurred, it was short-lived: Tudhaliya apparently regained the throne and then buried the Bronze Tablet under newly laid pavement — a "de-sacralizing burial" (Neve's interpretation) intended to conceal all trace of his compact with Kurunta. All such theories, Bryce notes, remain speculative.

## Cultural and Religious Achievements

The rock sanctuary at Yazılıkaya, one kilometre northeast of [[hattusha]], reached its fullest development under Tudhaliya. He commissioned the sculptural decorations and hieroglyphic inscriptions on both natural rock chambers. Tudhaliya is represented three times in the reliefs, once "in close association with his patron deity Sharrumma." The procession of Hurrian deities in the main chamber "represents the culmination of a programme of religious reform initially undertaken by Hattusili and his Hurrian queen Puduhepa and completed by Tudhaliya." The sanctuary in its final form — the Hurrian pantheon as the national Hittite pantheon — is "the most sophisticated surviving artistic achievement of the Hittite world."

Within Hattusa, Tudhaliya oversaw an ambitious expansion of the upper city, including an extensive temple construction programme that "more than doubled the size of the original city."

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Tudhaliya not responded aggressively to the western crisis — retaking Milawata, crushing the Seha River Land rebellion, restoring Walmu to Wilusa — Ahhiyawan influence might have dominated the Aegean coast of Anatolia before the Bronze Age Collapse, altering the character of the "Sea Peoples" movements that followed. Equally, his Yazılıkaya cult programme cemented Hurrian religious vocabulary as the Hittite state religion; without this synthesis the neo-Hittite hieroglyphic tradition might have lacked a unifying idiom.

## Historiography

The reign of Tudhaliya IV was long considered a period of Hittite decline before the Bronze Age Collapse, overshadowed by the dramatic fall of Hattusa. The 1986 discovery of the Bronze Tablet radically complicated this picture, revealing the Kurunta crisis and forcing scholars to reconsider the stability of the New Kingdom. As Singer observed even before that discovery: "Without diminishing the role of the outside enemies in the fall of the Hittite Empire, I feel that more weight should be given to the symptoms of inner decline and disintegration." The inscription corpus from Yazılıkaya and the Yalburt inscription have allowed better reconstruction of his western campaigns. His military defeat at Nihriya is attested primarily through a letter from Tukulti-Ninurta to the king of Ugarit (RS 34.165) — an Assyrian source whose boastfulness must be weighed critically.
