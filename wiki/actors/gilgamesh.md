# Gilgamesh

## Overview

**Gilgamesh** (Sumerian: Bilgames; Akkadian: Gilgameš) is the legendary king of **[[uruk|Uruk]]** and the central figure of the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the oldest surviving masterpiece of world literature. Historically, he was likely a real ruler of Uruk during the Early Dynastic period (c. 2600 BCE), known from king lists and a possible reference in a contemporary inscription. In literature, he is portrayed as two-thirds divine and one-third human, a powerful but flawed hero whose adventures explore the boundaries of kingship, friendship, mortality, and civilization.

The *Epic* (in its Standard Babylonian form, "He who saw the Deep," standardized c. 1200–1000 BCE) is not strict history but a profound literary meditation rooted in older Sumerian poems and the historical memory of Uruk's golden age. It presents Gilgamesh as the builder of Uruk's great walls (still visible in the poet's time) and a tyrant who is ultimately humanized through his bond with Enkidu and his failed quest for eternal life.

## Career (from the Epic)

- **Early reign and tyranny**: As king of Uruk, Gilgamesh oppresses his people (the "young men" and "young women" suffer). The gods create Enkidu as a counterbalance — a wild man of the steppe, hairy and animal-like. "The young men of Uruk he harries without warrant, / Gilgamesh lets no son go free to his father. / By day and by night his tyranny grows harsher" (ll. 1083–1088). The people's complaint **contributed_to** the gods' decision to create a match for him (ll. 1103–1149).
- **Friendship with Enkidu**: Enkidu is civilized by the prostitute Shamhat. The two become inseparable companions ("like brothers"). They embark on heroic adventures that channel Gilgamesh's excess energy. After wrestling in the street of Uruk (ll. 1816–1848), Enkidu acknowledges Gilgamesh's kingship: "to be king of the people Enlil made it your destiny!" (ll. 1847–1848 from parallel). Their bond **produced** the expedition to the Cedar Forest.
- **Cedar Forest expedition**: Gilgamesh and Enkidu journey to the distant Cedar Forest (in Lebanon) to slay its guardian Humbaba (Huwawa in Sumerian poems) and bring back timber. Despite Humbaba's pleas and divine warnings, they kill him, felling the sacred cedars. This act brings fame but also divine displeasure. Gilgamesh proposes the journey for eternal fame: "I will conquer him in the Forest of Cedar: / let the land learn Uruk’s offshoot is mighty! / Let me start out, I will cut down the cedar, / I will establish for ever a name eternal!" (ll. 2060–2066). Enkidu warns that "Humbaba, his voice is the Deluge, / his speech is fire, and his breath is death!" (ll. 1938–1940, 1970–1972); Gilgamesh rejects caution as cowardice (ll. 1992–1994). On the journey (Tablet IV) repeated dreams are interpreted by Enkidu as favorable omens of victory with Shamash's aid (ll. 2578–2588, 2760–2762, etc.). They confront and slay Humbaba with Shamash sending thirteen winds (ll. 3303–3315); Humbaba pleads but Enkidu urges the kill (ll. 3345–3347, 3381–3383); after slaying, Humbaba curses: "May the pair of them not grow old, / besides Gilgamesh his friend, none shall bury Enkidu!" (ll. 3503–3505). They then fell cedars (ll. 3541–3585). The slaying **produced** both fame and the curse; the friendship **enabled** the joint feat (Enkidu's wild knowledge **contributed_to** navigation and urging).
- **The Bull of Heaven**: Ishtar (goddess of love and war) propositions Gilgamesh. When rejected, she sends the Bull of Heaven against Uruk. Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay the bull. Enkidu tears off its thigh and hurls it at Ishtar. The gods decree that one of the heroes must die.
- **Death of Enkidu and grief**: Enkidu falls ill and dies after a prophetic dream of the underworld. Gilgamesh is devastated — "like a lioness robbed of her cubs." He refuses to accept mortality and begins his wanderings. In Tablet VII (ll. 4355–4385), Enkidu lay sick for twelve days; his final words include the netherworld vision: he was seized by a dark man "fierce as a Lion-Bird", trussed "like the wings of a bird", and led "to the house of darkness, seat of Irkalla: to the house which none who enters ever leaves" (ll. 4291–4293). He saw past rulers, priests, Etana, Shakkan, and Ereshkigal (ll. 4311–4330). Enkidu's death **produced** Gilgamesh's inconsolable grief and the quest.
- **Quest for immortality**: Gilgamesh travels to the edge of the world, crossing the Waters of Death with the ferryman Ur-shanabi. He meets Uta-napishti (the flood survivor, "he who found life"), who recounts the story of the great deluge sent by the gods. Uta-napishti reveals that eternal life was a unique gift for him and his wife; for ordinary humans (even semi-divine kings), it is impossible. In Tablet IX–X (ll. 4799–5603), Gilgamesh laments: "My friend, whom I love, has turned to clay, / my friend Enkidu, whom I love, has turned to clay. / Shall I not be like him, and also lie down, / never to rise again, through all eternity?" (ll. 5238–5244, repeated to tavern-keeper and Uta-napishti). This grief **caused_by** Enkidu's death **contributed_to** the wanderings and crossing (preceded_by the funeral rites). Uta-napishti teaches "both Death and Life they did establish, / but the day of Death they do not disclose" (ll. 5746–5748).
- **Return and acceptance**: Gilgamesh fails the tests for immortality (sleep and the plant of rejuvenation, stolen by a snake). He returns to Uruk, reconciled to his human limits. The poem ends with praise of the city walls he built — his true, lasting achievement.
- **Funeral of Enkidu (Tablet VIII)**: Immediately after death, Gilgamesh fashions a statue, selects grave goods (obsidian, carnelian, lapis lazuli, gold, ivory; ll. 4572–4632), butchers oxen and sheep, and ritually displays offerings to netherworld deities (Ishtar, Ereshkigal, Dumuzi, Namtar, etc.) "May she welcome my friend and walk at his side!" (ll. 4658–4748). "At the very first glimmer of brightening dawn" he holds a wake with table of elammaku-wood, bowls of carnelian and lapis filled with syrup and ghee (ll. 4778–4784). The funeral **part_of** the mourning process **produced_by** grief; grave goods **contributed_to** securing goodwill of underworld rulers. The people of Uruk mourn (ll. 4560–4562). These rites **preceded_by** the wanderings.

The Sumerian poems add earlier episodes: conflict with Akka of Kish, the Huwawa adventure (in two versions), the Bull of Heaven, descent to the netherworld (related to Tablet XII), and Gilgamesh's death and funeral.

**Specifics from Old Babylonian fragments and Sumerian poem apparatus (Andrew George trans., exclusive lines 10001–12899)**:
- "AKKA King of Kish, possibly Gilgamesh’s nephew." (glossary, ll. 12449). This **part_of** the Sumerian "Gilgamesh and Akka" poem, providing an earlier political tradition of conflict with Kish that **preceded_by** the Standard Version's emphasis on heroic exploits and mortality. 
- From OB Pennsylvania tablet: "They took hold of each other, backs bent like a bull, they smashed the door-jamb, the wall did shake... Gilgamesh knelt, one foot on the ground, his anger subsided, he broke off from the fight. After he broke off from the fight, said Enkidu to him, to Gilgamesh: ‘As one unique your mother bore you, the wild cow of the fold, the goddess Ninsun! High over warriors you are exalted, to be king of the people Enlil made it your destiny!’" (ll. 10261–83). This **produced** their brotherhood in the OB tradition.
- Gilgamesh's heroic rationale: "‘If I should fall, let me make my name: “Gilgamesh joined battle with ferocious Huwawa!”’" (Yale OB tablet, ll. 10440–42). **Contributed_to** motivation for the expedition in this layer.
- Slaying in Ishchali OB: "Gilgamesh smote him in the neck, his friend Enkidu gave encouragement. He ……… he fell, the ravines did run with his blood. Huwawa the guardian he smote to the ground..." (ll. 11054–62). **Produced** the death of the guardian in this distinct fragment.
- Tavern-keeper counsel (world's end fragment): "‘O Gilgamesh, where are you wandering? ‘The life that you seek you never will find: when the gods created mankind, death they dispensed to mankind, life they kept for themselves.’" (ll. 11363–71). **Part_of** the OB quest narrative; **preceded_by** Enkidu's death.
- Sealand tablet adaptation (ll. ~11985+): substitutes Sîn for Gilgamesh, Ea for Enkidu, Ur for Uruk in dreams/civilizing scenes. "The reason for these substitutions is unknown." Shows **concurrent_with** fluidity in early OB variants.
- "There is still no evidence that the Old Babylonian version of the poem included a narrative of the Deluge..." (ll. 11485–86). **Contrasts_with** the Standard Version's Tablet XI flood (Uta-napishti). 
- Publication apparatus details the five independent Sumerian poems as the core earlier traditions (Gilgamesh and Akka; Huwawa A/B; Bull of Heaven; Netherworld; Death of Gilgamesh), with specific editions listed (ll. 12732–12780). These **preceded_by** the Standard Babylonian standardization.

**Grounded claims from Sumerian poems and appendix (exclusive range: Andrew George trans., ll. 7001–10000)**:
- After stripping Huwawa of his seven auras of terror through repeated diplomatic offers (sisters, flour, sandals, gems), "Having stripped him clean of his seven auras of terror, he drew near to his lair. Like a snake in the wine harbour, he followed behind him, and making as if to kiss him, struck him on the cheek with his fist." Then "in rage and fury Enkidu severed his head at the neck." (ll. 7215–7311). Enkidu's counsel **contributed_to** the killing; Gilgamesh's initial pity **preceded_by** the act. The head taken to Enlil, who "what Gilgamesh had done made him angry" but redistributed the auras: "his first aura he gave to the field, his second aura he gave to the river..." (ll. 7325–7355). This **produced** the distribution of Huwawa's powers into nature and institutions. **part_of** [[gilgamesh|Gilgamesh]]'s heroic tradition in Sumerian "Gilgamesh and Huwawa".
- In the Sumerian "Bull of Heaven" ("Hero in battle"): Inanna, after tantrum ("Then I shall scream until sky draws nigh earth!"), obtains the Bull from An; it ravages Uruk, "in the Engilua canal it drinks the water, though the Engilua canal extends a whole league, its thirst is not sated." (ll. 7903–7945). Gilgamesh and Enkidu slay it: Gilgamesh "with his axe of seven talents smote its crown"; he "hacked off a shoulder (to throw) at Inanna, sent her flying off like a dove, and demolished the rampart." Meat given to orphans, horns to Inanna in Eanna (ll. 8109–8142). The slaying **produced** the dedication of horns and **contributed_to** Inanna's humiliation. **part_of** Sumerian version of the exploit **concurrent_with** Standard Tablet VI.
- "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld" (Sumerian): Inanna plants a willow from the Euphrates in Uruk's garden; infested by "Snake-that-Knows-no-Charm", "Lion-Bird", "Demon-Maiden". Gilgamesh fells it for her ("in its roots the Snake-that-Knows-no-Charm he smote... To his sister, holy Inanna, he gave wood for her throne, he gave wood for her bed. For himself its root he made into his ball, its branch he made into his mallet." (ll. 8420–8462). The ball/mallet fall to Netherworld; Enkidu volunteers. Gilgamesh warns detailed instructions on proper Netherworld conduct (no clean clothes, no oil, no noise, no kisses to loved/hated) lest "the outcry of the Netherworld would seize you." (ll. 8526–8574). Enkidu ignores; "From that wicked day to the seventh day thence, from the Netherworld his servant Enkidu came not forth." (ll. 8608–8610). Netherworld **seized** him (Namtar/Asakku not). This **produced** Enkidu's permanent captivity in the Sumerian tradition.
- Gilgamesh petitions Enlil (no help), then Enki in Eridu: "O Father Enki, my ball fell into the Netherworld..." Enki helps via Utu: "when (as the Sun God) you make an opening in the Netherworld, bring his servant up to him from the Netherworld!" (ll. 8648–8672). The shade reports on Netherworld rules in detail (e.g., "‘Did you see the man with seven sons?’ ‘I saw him. Second only to the gods he sits on a throne...’"; eunuch "propped in the corner"; burnt dead "His ghost was not there, his smoke went up to the heavens."; stillborn "They play amid syrup and ghee..."; no-offerings eat "scrapings from the pot"). (ll. 8698–8800). The Q&A **produced** the canonical Mesopotamian account of differentiated afterlife fates. Ur recension adds Amorites polluting water for "sons of Sumer and Akkad" including Gilgamesh's parents; he responds with statues and 9-day mourning rites for Girsu (ll. 8820–8870). **part_of** [[enkidu|Enkidu]]'s netherworld role **concurrent_with** Standard Tablet XII appendix.
- "The Death of Gilgamesh": Gilgamesh "seized by Namtar... he is not capable of rising." Dream of gods' assembly: they review his deeds ("having fetched that unique cedar... having slain Huwawa... you reached Ziusudra in his abode!"), conclude "despite his mother we cannot show him mercy! Gilgamesh, in the form of his ghost, dead in the underworld, shall be the governor of the Netherworld, chief of the shades! He will pass judgement... as weighty as the word of Ningishzida and Dumuzi." (ll. 9199–9287, repeated). Enlil: "O Gilgamesh, I made your destiny a destiny of kingship, but I did not make it a destiny of eternal life." (ll. 9319–20). **produced** his special Netherworld status (not eternal life on earth). He diverts Euphrates, builds stone tomb in riverbed, lays down "his beloved wife, his beloved child... his beloved senior wife and junior wife, his beloved minstrel, steward..." with gifts to Ereshkigal, Namtar, Ningishzida, Dumuzi, Enki etc. (ll. 9661–9772). "They opened the river Euphrates, its waters swept over, his resting place the waters removed (from view)." People mourn: "the young men of his city scratched their faces, the young women of his city tore out their hair. The old men and women of his city rolled in the dust." (ll. 9779–9795). Names persist "how their names are pronounced will never be forgotten!" via statues and offspring (ll. 9803–9813). **part_of** [[gilgamesh|Gilgamesh]]'s legendary end in Sumerian poem **preceded_by** the epic's acceptance of mortality.

## Historiography and Value

The *Epic of Gilgamesh* is a cultural and literary primary source, not a chronicle. It preserves memory of a historical king of Uruk while transforming him into a universal everyman confronting death. Key themes:
- The costs and responsibilities of kingship.
- The redemptive power of male friendship.
- The boundary between civilization and the wild (Enkidu's arc).
- Mortality: "The gods kept life for themselves; death they allotted to humankind."
- The flood myth as one of the oldest versions, directly ancestral to later Near Eastern and Biblical flood stories.

Andrew George's translation is the scholarly standard, distinguishing the Standard Version from older Sumerian and Old Babylonian material. It highlights textual history: the poem evolved over 1,500+ years from court entertainments under King Shulgi of Ur (Ur III period) to the standardized 12-tablet form.

For the wiki, this source grounds the legendary dimension of [[uruk|Uruk]] as the first city and provides the emotional and philosophical heart of Early Bronze Age Mesopotamia. It complements archaeological evidence of Uruk's urban explosion (c. 3300 BCE), writing's invention, and temple economies with a human story of power, loss, and wisdom.
