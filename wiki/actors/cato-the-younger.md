# Marcus Porcius Cato "the Younger"

The unbending **Stoic** moralist whose rigid integrity made him the conscience — and,
Cicero ruefully thought, sometimes the liability — of the senatorial cause against the
dynasts. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 9 (Wiseman). This page covers his
career to 60 BCE; his end at Utica (46) belongs to later cycles.

## Career to 60 BCE

Great-grandson of **Cato the Censor** and famed for his ancestor's "rigid probity,"
Cato entered politics as the incorruptible enforcer of the optimate order. As
**quaestor** he forced the Sullan executioners to repay their blood-money; as
tribune-elect his **denunciation in the Catilinarian debate** (63) overbore Caesar's
plea for clemency and **carried the death penalty** for the conspirators. As tribune
(62) he shrewdly defused unrest by **more than doubling the grain dole**, and led the
furious resistance to **Metellus Nepos'** bill to recall [[pompey]] with his army.

Through 61–60 Cato was the **chief obstructor** of the three men who would form the
triumvirate: he blocked the ratification of **Pompey's eastern settlement** and his
veterans' land; opposed the **publicani's** demand to renegotiate the Asian tax
contract (against Crassus); and refused [[julius-caesar|Caesar]] the right to stand for
the consulship *in absentia* while keeping his triumph. Cicero's verdict captures him:
"he speaks in the Senate as though he were living in Plato's Republic instead of
Romulus' cesspool" — patriotic and upright, but politically inflexible.

## Civil war and Utica (49–46)

In the **[[roman-civil-war-49-45bce|civil war]]** Cato followed Pompey, holding that
any government was better than Caesar's. After Pharsalus he led an epic desert march
to **Africa**, where the Republican coalition fought on under Metellus Scipio. When
Caesar won at **Thapsus (46)**, Cato — commanding at **Utica** — organized the escape
of others and then **took his own life** rather than accept Caesar's pardon, holding
that Caesar had **no legal right "to pardon men as if he were their master."** His
suicide made him the Republic's enduring **martyr** (the *Cato* literature, against
which Caesar wrote an *Anticato*).

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Cato is the measure of how **principled intransigence** could be self-defeating: by
denying Pompey, Caesar, and Crassus each their legitimate goals, he **drove them
together** into the compact that destroyed the Republic he was defending. Wiseman:
his moral authority "almost" rehabilitated the Sullan oligarchy's image, but his
rigidity gave the dynasts no path but alliance.

## Historiography

Cato became a Stoic **martyr-icon** after his suicide (the lost *Cato* literature,
Plutarch's *Life*); the sources are correspondingly idealizing. Cicero's letters give
the most contemporary — and ambivalent — portrait.
