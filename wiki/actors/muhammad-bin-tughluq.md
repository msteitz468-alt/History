# Muhammad bin Tughluq

**Muhammad bin Tughluq** (r. 1325–1351) was the most learned and the most controversial of the
[[delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate's]] rulers — a sultan of genuine intellectual brilliance whose
boldest schemes ended in disaster, and under whom the Sultanate reached its widest extent and then
began to **disintegrate**. From the [[cambridge-history-of-india-v3|Cambridge History of India,
Vol. III]].

## The experiments

The Tughluq is remembered above all for a series of ambitious, rationally conceived, and
catastrophically executed projects:

- **The transfer of the capital to Daulatabad (1327).** To govern the newly conquered Deccan more
  effectively, Muhammad made **Devagiri — renamed Daulatabad** — a second capital, and ordered
  much of the population and administrative class of **Delhi** to relocate the ~1,500 km south.
  The forced migration caused great suffering and resentment; Delhi was partly depopulated, and
  the experiment was eventually reversed, having weakened both centres. (The contemporary traveller
  [[ibn-battuta|Ibn Battuta]], who served as a *qadi* at his court, left a vivid eyewitness
  account.)
- **The token currency (c. 1330).** Anticipating modern fiduciary money, Muhammad issued **brass
  and copper tokens** to circulate at the value of silver. Because the mint was not a state
  monopoly and forgery was rampant, the coinage collapsed; the treasury had to redeem the tokens
  in silver, draining it.
- **Over-ambitious expeditions.** A planned campaign toward **Khurasan/Iraq** was abandoned after
  a huge army had been mustered and paid; the **Qarachil (Himalayan)** expedition was destroyed by
  terrain and weather.

## Disintegration

These failures, compounded by a grievous **Deccan famine** and punitive taxation of the Doab,
provoked revolt after revolt. During his reign the Sultanate's outlying provinces broke away: the
south seceded to form the Hindu kingdom of **[[vijayanagara-bahmani|Vijayanagara]]** (1336) and
the Muslim **[[vijayanagara-bahmani|Bahmani sultanate]]** (1347), and Bengal and others followed. The
Sultanate that he inherited at its territorial peak he left **contracting and rebellious**; he
died in 1351 pursuing rebels in Sind.

## Significance and historiography

Muhammad bin Tughluq is the classic medieval Indian puzzle: contemporaries and the
[[cambridge-history-of-india-v3|CHI]] tradition cast him as a near-mad tyrant whose cruelty and
folly wrecked the Sultanate, while modern historians stress that his schemes were **intelligently
conceived** (a forward-looking fiduciary currency; a strategically central capital) and failed on
**execution, scale, and the limits of medieval administrative reach** rather than on conception.
His reign marks the **turning point** from the Sultanate's apogee under [[alauddin-khalji|Ala-ud-din
Khalji]] to its long decline, and the birth of the independent Deccan and regional sultanates.
