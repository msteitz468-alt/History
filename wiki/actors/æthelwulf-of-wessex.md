## Overview

Æthelwulf was king of the West Saxons from 839 to 858, son of [[ecgberht-of-wessex|Ecgberht]] and father of four successive West Saxon kings — Æthelbald, Æthelberht, Æthelred, and Alfred (the Great). Before his accession he served as sub-king of Kent and the south-eastern provinces from c. 825, leading the initial conquest of Kent after [[battle-of-ellendun-825|Ellendun]] in 825. His reign was defined by the escalating Viking threat, a celebrated state visit to Rome and the Frankish court (855–6), a controversial marriage to the Carolingian princess Judith, and a complex dynastic settlement whose contested terms reverberated into the reign of Alfred.

## Sub-kingship in Kent (c. 825–839)

Immediately after Ellendun, Ecgberht sent Æthelwulf "with Ealhstan, bishop of Sherborne, and Ealdorman Wulfheard with a large force into Kent" (ASC A, s.a. 823), driving out the Mercian sub-king Bealdred. Æthelwulf was entrusted with the south-eastern provinces as a sub-kingdom at least by 838 (CS 418:S 280; CS 419, 420:S 286). When Æthelwulf became king of the West Saxons in 839, he "established his son, Aethelstan, as king over Kent and the south-eastern provinces" (ASC B, s.a. 836), continuing the practice of delegating the south-east to a subordinate.

## Viking Campaigns

Æthelwulf was a military leader "who had distinguished himself against the Vikings in 851." In that year a fleet of 350 ships entered the Thames, stormed Canterbury and London, and defeated King Berhtwulf of Mercia — but "when the Viking army moved into Surrey Aethelwulf and his son, Aethelbald, crushed it at Aclea," which the Alfredian chronicler called "the greatest slaughter of a heathen army ever yet heard of" (ASC A, s.a. 851). His son Aethelstan defeated a Viking force in a naval engagement at Sandwich the same year. In 838 Æthelwulf had also defeated a combined British and Viking force at Hingston Down.

## Roman Journey and Marriage to Judith (855–6)

Æthelwulf's journey to Rome in 855–6 "did not signify abdication and a retreat from the world… It was more a display of the king's international standing and a demonstration of the prestige his dynasty enjoyed in Frankish and papal circles." He was received by Pope Benedict III and in July 856 betrothed to Judith, the 12- or 13-year-old daughter of Charles the Bald, the marriage "solemnized at Verberie on 1 October." Judith was "crowned queen by Hincmar, archbishop of Rheims… and most unusually, anointed, a charismatic sanctification which enhanced her status, blessed her womb and conferred additional throne-worthiness on her male offspring." This was contrary to West Saxon custom. Charles the Bald's consent, unusual for Carolingian princesses to marry foreign princes, "must reflect a long-standing regard for the West Saxon dynasty" and concern about shared Anglo-Frankish Viking problems.

Before departing, Æthelwulf made "generous concessions to his thegns, intended, it would seem, ultimately to benefit also the Church in his kingdom."

## Æthelwulf's Will and Dynastic Arrangements

Æthelwulf's will does not survive but Alfred's (dating to 872–8) allows a partial reconstruction. He "partitioned the kingdom between his two eldest sons" — Æthelbald in Wessex, Æthelberht in the south-eastern provinces — and "divided his 'own inheritance' between his sons, daughter and kinsmen." He was "not concerned to preserve his kingdom as a single entity." He bequeathed certain lands jointly to three sons — Æthelbald, Æthelred, and Alfred — "that whichever of us should live longest was to succeed to the whole," but this was only one category of property among several. The will became a source of dynastic conflict extending to the succession crisis of 899.

## Æthelwulf's Rebellion Problem

According to Asser, during Æthelwulf's absence in 855–6, his son Æthelbald "with the support of Ealhstan, bishop of Sherborne, and Eanwulf, ealdorman of Somerset, plotted that he would not be received back into his kingdom." Kirby argues the rebellion likely occurred after Æthelwulf's marriage to Judith rather than before it — since Charles the Bald "would hardly have sent his daughter as an anointed queen… into an overseas kingdom already known to be in revolt." To avoid civil war Æthelwulf partitioned the kingdom with Æthelbald (west Wessex to Æthelbald; east and central Wessex to Æthelwulf himself). Æthelwulf died "less than two years after his return," placing Æthelbald on the throne — who then married his father's widow Judith, contrary to Church law.

## Mercian Relations

Æthelwulf co-operated with Mercia against the Vikings: in 853 Burgred asked Æthelwulf "to help bring the Welsh… back into subjection to him. Aethelwulf responded by taking his army across Mercia and into Wales." In 853 Burgred married Æthelwulf's daughter Aethelswith at Chippenham. Alfred later married Ealhswith, a Mercian noblewoman "descended from King Coenwulf through her mother, Eadburh."

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Æthelwulf's successful military record (especially Aclea 851) and his prestigious Roman-Carolingian alliance — culminating in the Judith marriage — West Saxon legitimacy and Continental connections would have been far weaker when the great Viking army arrived in 865. The dynastic arrangement splitting the kingdom contributed to precisely the fragility the Vikings would eventually exploit, yet also helped ensure multiple royal sons survived to lead resistance.

## Historiography

Kirby critically examines the hagiographical tradition that Æthelwulf entered minor orders (dismissing it as "legend") and interrogates Asser's account of the Roman journey, questioning whether Alfred really received a royal anointing in 853 (arguing it was more likely a confirmation-anointing retrospectively reinterpreted). He argues for a generous reassessment of Æthelwulf as a capable ruler against the tendency to portray him as pious but weak. The letter purporting to be from Pope Leo IV to Æthelwulf regarding Alfred is "dismissed as an eleventh-century forgery."
