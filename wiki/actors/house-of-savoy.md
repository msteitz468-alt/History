# The House of Savoy and the Kingdom of Burgundy-Arles

The old **"middle kingdom"** of the Treaty of Verdun (843) — Lothar's Burgundy-Arles between
the Rhône-Saône and the Alps — survived in the thirteenth century only as a "geographical
expression," a part of the Holy Roman Empire that no emperor could actually rule.
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Eugene Cox, Ch. 8) tells its real history through its four
great principalities — the **county of Burgundy (Franche-Comté)**, the lands of the **house
of Savoy**, the **Dauphiné**, and the county of **Provence** — of which the Savoys were the
century's success story.

## The Phantom Kingdom of Arles

Successive Hohenstaufen and their rivals tried to make imperial authority real in the
"kingdom of Arles," and to revive it as a political entity for an ally — [[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Frederick
II]] dangled it before the Savoyards; the dowager-queens **Margaret of Provence** and Eleanor
of England schemed in the 1280s, with [[rudolf-of-habsburg|Rudolf of Habsburg]], to revive it
against [[charles-of-anjou|Charles of Anjou]]. All these projects "vanished," and after the
**Sicilian Vespers** (1282) drained Angevin resources, "the future of the kingdom of
Burgundy-Arles would be the future of the major principalities of which it was composed." The
real long-term winner was the **French monarchy**, which by the century's end had pushed its
border to the Rhône and absorbed **Lyons** (declared part of France, 1312).

## The Rise of the House of Savoy

Geographically insignificant in itself, the county of Savoy mattered because its lords
controlled the **three great transalpine routes** between France and Italy (the Great and
Little St Bernard, the Mont Cenis). Their rise to **international prominence** came through the
remarkable children of **Count Thomas I** (d. 1233): above all the four daughters of his
daughter Beatrice, who all became **queens** — Margaret (married [[louis-ix-capetian|Louis
IX]]), Eleanor ([[henry-iii-of-england|Henry III]]), Sancia (Richard of Cornwall) and Beatrice
([[charles-of-anjou|Charles of Anjou]]). Savoyard uncles became archbishop of Canterbury, earl
of Richmond, count of Flanders — connections that "helped to protect the counts from
aggressive neighbours" and advanced their domestic aims (see the Savoyard party at
[[henry-iii-of-england|Henry III's]] court).

## Building a State

Savoyard revenue came chiefly from **regalian rights over transalpine trade** — tolls,
minting, safe-conducts, the policing of the passes — and from a vigorous policy of founding
and chartering towns (at least 23 new town charters, 1232–1300). The decisive institutional
innovator was **Peter II** (d. 1268), formerly earl of Richmond, who — influenced by **English
royal government** — organised the lands into **bailliages** under salaried, transferable
*baillis*, created a **chambre des comptes** "rather like the English exchequer," and instituted
itinerant justices (*juges-mages*). Unlike the strife-torn Franche-Comté (where the house of
Chalon fragmented and the French crown eventually prevailed) and the looser Dauphiné, Savoy
"emerged from the thirteenth century with its sovereignty intact... and a solid foundation in
governing institutions" — the basis of its long future as a transalpine and ultimately Italian
royal house.

## Significance

The Savoyard story illustrates two volume themes: the **frontier principality** consolidating
itself between greater powers, and the **export of administrative techniques** (here, from
Plantagenet England to the Alps). Together with Provence under the Angevins, Savoy's survival
"offered rather a barrier than an open door to the further expansion of the French monarchy"
into the old kingdom of Arles.
