# The Rajputs

The **Rajputs** were the warrior clans who dominated the politics of **northern and western India**
in the regional-state era (c. 9th–12th centuries) — and whose martial, clan-based, feudal order
defined the world that the [[delhi-sultanate|Turkish conquest]] overran. Documented for the wiki
through Romila Thapar's [[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## A contested origin

Thapar treats Rajput origins as **uncertain and probably foreign**. The very vehemence with which
the Rajputs insisted on **kshatriya status** — and the elaborate brahman-supplied genealogies tying
them to the **solar and lunar races** — she reads as a sign that the status had to be *manufactured*.
Four leading clans (the **Pratiharas/Parihars, Chauhans, Solankis/Chaulukyas, and Paramaras/Pawars**)
claimed descent from a hero who sprang from a **sacrificial fire-pit at Mt Abu** — the **Agnikula**
("Fire Family") myth, "probably the first occasion when deliberate and conscious attempts were made
by rulers to insist on their kshatriya status" (earlier dynasties had ruled regardless of caste and
been *accepted* as high-caste simply by ruling). Most authorities, Thapar notes, derive the Rajputs
from the **Hunas** and the peoples who entered India with them, **assimilated** through the surviving
tribal-republican societies of Rajasthan — a vivid example of caste absorbing newcomers as a new
sub-caste/varna identity (cf. [[caste-varna]]).

## The clans and the feudal order

The Rajput kingdoms arose on the ruins of the **Pratihara** realm (after its fall to the Turks and
the tripartite exhaustion of Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas over **Kanauj**). The Chauhans (east
Rajasthan, founders of much around **Delhi** — Dhillika, founded by the Tomaras in 736, passed to
them), the Solankis (Kathiawar/Gujarat), the Paramaras (Malwa), the Chandellas (Khajuraho), and the
Guhilas of Mewar each took imperial titles (*maharajadhiraja*) on becoming independent. Thapar
stresses the Rajput order was the perfect expression of **[[indian-feudalism|Indian feudalism]]**:
its martial obligations, clan-held estates (the Pratihara unit of **eighty-four villages** becoming
the standard clan-chief's estate), and endless internecine war fit the *samanta* system exactly — and
fatally diffused military strength on the eve of the Turkish invasions.

## Significance

The Rajputs embody the **regional, feudal, martial** political world of early-medieval north India —
and its weakness: "minor bickerings magnified into endless campaigns" left the clans disunited and
exhausted when **Muhammad Ghuri** came. The Chauhan **Prithviraja III**, the romantic hero of the
bardic *Prithvirajaraso*, won the **first battle of Tarain (1191)** but was defeated at the
**second (1192)** — and "the kingdom of Delhi fell to Muhammad," opening the era of the
[[delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate]]. The Rajputs remained a powerful regional and military force
through the Sultanate and Mughal periods.
