# The Kingdom of Dahomey

**Dahomey** was the **Fon** kingdom of the "Slave Coast" (modern Bénin), a famously **centralized,
militarized, slave-trading absolutism** centred on its capital **Abomey**. Documented for the wiki through
the **UNESCO [[gha-vol5-16th-18th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. V]]** (ch. 15, Alagoa).

## A militarized state

Dahomey was unusually **centralized and absolutist** for the region: the king commanded a standing army
(including the celebrated corps of **women soldiers**, the so-called "Dahomey Amazons"), a developed
bureaucracy, and an annual ritual of state (the "Annual Customs"). Under **King Agaja** it conquered the
coastal kingdoms and the great slaving port of **Whydah (Ouidah) in 1727**, seizing direct access to the
Atlantic trade.

## Dahomey and the slave trade

Dahomey's economy and state were deeply bound to the **[[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]]**: it
exported large numbers of captives through Whydah in exchange for firearms and goods, and the trade
financed the monarchy. Its participation has made it a central — and debated — case in arguments about
**African agency** in the slave trade. For much of the eighteenth century Dahomey was itself a **tributary
of the [[oyo-empire|Oyo empire]]**, whose cavalry it could not match, gaining full independence only after
Oyo's decline.

## Decline and conquest

In the nineteenth century, as the Atlantic slave trade was suppressed, Dahomey shifted toward **palm-oil**
exports but remained militarily aggressive. It was conquered by **France in 1892–94** (the Franco-Dahomean
wars) and absorbed into French West Africa — its name later taken by the independent Republic of Dahomey
(renamed Bénin in 1975).

## Significance

Dahomey is the classic case of a **West African slave-trading military monarchy** — highly centralized and
state-controlled — and, through its women warriors and annual customs, one of the most studied (and
sensationalized) of pre-colonial African states.
