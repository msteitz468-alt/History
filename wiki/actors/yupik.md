# Yupik (Yuit)

Eskimo-Aleut peoples of the **Bering Sea and Alaskan Pacific coast** regions, designated **Yuit** speakers in Damas’s CHNPA I.2 framing. Linguistic and cultural relatives of [[inuit]] and Aleut speakers, but **not** correctly subsumed under the ethnonym Inuit.

## Terminology

Damas (bibliographic essay): Arctic North American inhabitants are properly designated Inuit from their word for “people.” Others in the Eska-Aleut / Eskimo-Aleut family include the **Yuit speakers of the Bering Sea and Alaskan Pacific coast regions**, as well as Aleut speakers of the Aleutians and part of the Alaskan Peninsula. The 1970s-on tendency to replace “Eskimo” by “Inuit” for all these groups (and for prehistoric Arctic populations) has two shortcomings: (1) linguistic/genetic affiliations of most archaeological cultures (except [[thule-culture|Thule]]) are uncertain; (2) **Yuit and Aleut speakers lack *Inuit* in their vocabularies**. This page follows the source’s Yuit/Yupik distinction from Inuit; older literature often folds all under “Eskimo.”

## Role in modern Alaska

In [[alaska-native-claims-settlement-1971|ANCSA]] implementation, about **half of the Bering Straits Native Corporation** shared land grants and monies between Inuit speakers and **Yuit speakers**; Arctic Slope and NANA regional corporations were Inuit-speaking. By 1960 only a handful of “Eskimos (including Inuit, Aleut, and **Yuit**)” had received post-secondary education—the text lists Yuit as a distinct category within broad Eskimo usage (Damas). WWII and postwar Alaskan boom, Anti-Discrimination Act 1945, housing and school expansion, and AFN land-claims politics therefore include Yuit communities as partners and beneficiaries alongside Inuit and other Alaska Natives, without collapsing their ethnonym into Inuit.

## Relation to Thule / Inuit continuum

Historic Inuit dialect continuum runs Greenland–northern Alaska from Thule radiation (McGhee/Damas). Yuit-speaking Pacific/Bering populations represent a related but distinct branch within Eskimo-Aleut; Damas’s chapter focuses on Inuit Arctic North America and treats Yuit primarily in terminological and ANCSA notes rather than as a full ethnographic survey. Do not force a full Thule-descendant narrative onto all Yupik groups from this source alone.

## Historiography

Thin in Damas ch. 14 relative to Central Arctic and Greenland Inuit; presence is terminological (anti-conflation rule) and institutional (ANCSA Bering Straits share; education census category). Fuller Yupik ethnography and Russian-period history require Handbook Arctic / specialist monographs beyond this extract. Reliability of the distinction claim is high (author-methodological); depth of historical narrative on this page is intentionally limited to source-supported material.
