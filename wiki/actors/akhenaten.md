# Akhenaten (Amenophis IV)

The **"heretic pharaoh"** — the Eighteenth-Dynasty king who replaced Egypt's
crowded pantheon with the exclusive worship of the **[[atenism|Aten]]** (the
sun-disk), built a new capital at **[[amarna|Akhetaten]]**, and unleashed an
artistic and religious revolution whose collapse his successors hurried to erase.
"The most remarkable king to have occupied the throne in the history of Egypt" —
though CAH is at pains to puncture the romantic image. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]] (Ch. XIX, Aldred). Reign ~1379–1362 by
the volume's high chronology; modern dating lowers it to ~1353–1336 BCE.

## The reformer reconsidered

Aldred deflates the standard portrait. Akhenaten was **not** the "international"
pacifist of legend (he appears on reliefs smiting Egypt's foes), nor a social
revolutionary raising up "new men" (his officials were largely the sons of his
father's officials), nor an artistic naturalist (the "realism" had already begun
under [[amenhotep-iii|Amenophis III]]). His one true originality, the volume argues,
was **theological**: an insistence on a "**rigid monotheism**" that "rigidly
excluded" the other gods, "as distinct from the henotheism of the sun-cult" — the
work, it suspects, of "a religious fanatic." (See [[atenism]].)

## The reign

Accepted as pharaoh by [[mitanni|Tushratta]] and
[[shuppiluliuma-i|Šuppiluliuma]] on his father's death (a long
**[[amenhotep-iii|co-regency]]** with Amenophis III is debated but not adopted
here), Amenophis IV opened his reign with a decree mustering the whole land to
build temples to "Re-Harakhte who rejoices on the horizon in his aspect of the
light which is in the Aten." Within a few years he:
- changed his name from **Amenophis** ("Amun is content") to **Akhenaten**;
- replaced the falcon-headed god with the abstract **rayed disk** whose hands held
  the sign of life only to the king and queen;
- founded **[[amarna|Akhetaten]]** ("the Horizon of the Aten") on virgin ground
  half-way between Memphis and Thebes, sworn never to be overstepped;
- and late in the reign turned passive disregard into **active persecution** — agents
  hammered out the name and image of **[[amun-priesthood|Amun]]** (and even the
  plural word "gods") across Egypt, not undone until Ramesses II.

His chief queen **[[nefertiti|Nefertiti]]** shared an unprecedented public
prominence; six daughters but no attested son cloud the succession (see
[[tutankhamun|Tutankhamun]] and Smenkhkare). His latest dated year is 17.

## The cost

The volume's central judgement is economic and political, not theological. Egypt's
revenues ran through a delicately balanced **temple-and-palace fiscal system**;
closing the local cults and pouring "the nation's resources into the coffers of the
king and his god," while building a whole new capital, "must have... overturned" it.
Over-centralized, neglectful government bred the corruption and "arbitrary
exactions" that [[horemheb|Horemheb]] later had to crush. Abroad, Egypt became "a
broken reed": as the [[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]] record, Qatna, Nukhašše,
Qadeš and above all Amurru (under Aziru) passed into
[[shuppiluliuma-i|Hittite]] vassalage while the pharaoh did nothing. "It was
doubtless the chaos caused by the economic consequences of Akhenaten's religious
reforms that brought about a complete reversal to the old order as soon as he was
dead."

## Kemp: the Aten as the only demonstrable power

Barry Kemp ([[kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy of a Civilization*]], Ch. 8) reads
the reform through the book's theme of the [[state-formation|transcendental imagination]].
Akhenaten promoted the **Aten — the visible sun-disk — "because it was the only demonstrable
manifestation of transcendental power,"** stripping away the imagined gods that had
populated Egyptian cosmology (his burial chamber bears only scenes of the family offering to
the Aten, replacing all Book-of-the-Dead imagery). The extreme body-style — elongated neck
and skull, swollen belly, transferred even to the queen and daughters — is better read "as
a bold attempt to define kingship as a force... outside the normal plane of human
experience" than as a record of disease; the *talatat* blocks show it was fully formed at
the very start, at an early Karnak Sed-festival. He ruled as a **triad** with
[[nefertiti|Nefertiti]] (her name in a cartouche) and the eldest daughter Meritaten.

Having abolished the great processional festivals, Akhenaten filled the vacuum with **chariot
processions** of himself and his family along the Royal Road — Kemp calls it "an unintended
caricature of all modern leaders who indulge in the trappings of charismatic display," a
foretaste of the presidential motorcade. He notes a telling inconsistency: at least **248
ushabti figures** (many granite) — objects bound to **Osiris**, the god he suppressed — lay
in the king's own tomb. On Kemp's account the reform's collapse "can by no means be
described as a vengeful triumph of the priesthood": the real power-base was the **army**
(hence [[horemheb|Horemheb]]'s succession), and Egyptians simply "did not like what they
saw," preferring the intellectual compromise of divine monarchy to naked personal rule.

## Significance

Akhenaten is history's most discussed early experiment in **exclusive monotheism**
and **state-driven religious revolution**, and the eponym of the
[[amarna-letters|Amarna age]]. His reign is also a key episode in the
[[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze]] story: the moment Egypt's grip on Syria-Palestine
slipped while Hatti rose. His memory was condemned (he became "that criminal of
Akhetaten"), his monuments quarried, his capital abandoned — so that the period
survives largely through what he built and what his enemies left intact.

## Historiography

Reconstructed from the reliefs and **boundary stelae** of his ruined capital, the
**Great Hymn to the Aten** (preserved in the tomb of Ay), the Amarna
sculptors' studio models (including the **Nefertiti bust**), the Amarna letters, and
the forensic re-examination of the royal mummies. Almost every biographical
question — the co-regency, the identity of Smenkhkare, Nefertiti's fate, the date of
the persecution, the paternity of Tutankhamun — remains open. The popular cult of
Akhenaten as proto-monotheist or proto-modern is itself a historiographical
phenomenon the volume warns against. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
