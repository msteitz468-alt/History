# Telipinu

The **constitutional reformer** of the [[hittites|Hittite]] Old Kingdom — the king
who, c. ~1525 BCE, broke the cycle of regicide that had consumed every one of his
predecessors since [[mursili-i|Mursili I's]] assassination, and issued the
**[[telipinu-edict-1500bce|Proclamation (Edict) of Telipinu]]**, the ancient world's
clearest attempt to bind succession law in writing and establish an institutional check
on royal violence. He was a son of Ammuna, married to Huzziya's sister Istapariya.
Treated in Trevor Bryce, *The Kingdom of the Hittites* (2005), Ch. 5.

## Accession by Counter-Coup

Telipinu came to power not by assassination but by preventing one. The usurper Huzziya
— who had seized the throne by having Ammuna's other sons Titti and Hantili murdered —
plotted against Telipinu's life. The plot was discovered in time: "the usurper had
probably not long been on the throne when his intended victim staged a counter-coup,
seizing the throne for himself and driving Huzziya and his five brothers into exile."
Crucially, Telipinu chose **exile over execution**: "There would be no more bloodshed."
This deliberate restraint was both a personal disposition and a political signal — the
first act in a reign defined by the project of substituting law for vengeance.

## Military Reconsolidation

Before issuing his constitutional reforms, Telipinu first had to recover the territories
lost under the chaotic reigns of Zidanta and Ammuna. He undertook an expedition against
Hassuwa, Zizzilippa, and Lawazantiya in the Euphrates region north of Carchemish and
near the newly emergent [[kizzuwatna|Kizzuwatna]]. By his reign's end the Marassantiya
basin was again under Hittite control, the kingdom's authority once more reached the
Mediterranean south of the Marassantiya, and in the south-east "Hittite authority once
more reached the Euphrates."

## Kizzuwatna and the First Hittite Treaty

One former territory that Telipinu did not reconquer was the newly independent kingdom
of [[kizzuwatna|Kizzuwatna]] (previously Adaniya), which had broken free during
Ammuna's reign. Rather than risk forcing it into a Hurrian alliance — and rather than
overstretch forces still consolidating the homeland — Telipinu accepted its independence
and drew up **the first known Hittite treaty**, an alliance with Isputahsu, Kizzuwatna's
first attested king. This pragmatic diplomacy established one of the most consequential
precedents in Hittite foreign policy: the parity or near-parity treaty with a bordering
state as an alternative to military conquest.

## The Edict and the Pankuš

The **Proclamation of Telipinu** — composed originally in the last quarter of the
sixteenth century BCE and surviving in nine Hittite exemplars plus a fragmentary
Akkadian version — was addressed to the **pankuš**, a general assembly of the royal
court ("palace servants, bodyguard, men of the golden spear, cup-bearers, table-men,
cooks, heralds, stableboys, captains of the thousand" etc.). It contained three
interlocking elements:

**1. Historical preamble.** A narrative review of the reigns of [[labarna|Labarna]],
[[hattusili-i|Hattusili I]], and [[mursili-i|Mursili I]] as a golden age of unity and
conquest, followed by the catastrophic succession of regicides from [[hantili-i|Hantili
I]] through Huzziya. Telipinu attributed the kingdom's disasters to divine vengeance:
bloodshed invited nemesis for the entire land, not only the perpetrator. "Henceforth
whoever becomes king and plans injury for a brother or sister, you are his pankuš, and
must speak frankly to him: 'Read this deed of bloodshed in the tablet'" (Telipinu
Procl. §30, ii 46–9).

**2. Fixed succession law.** The first formally stated rules for determining the royal
heir: "Let a prince, a son of the first rank, become king. If there is no prince of the
first rank, let him who is a son of the second rank become king. But if there is no
prince, no heir, let them take an *antiyant*-husband for her who is a daughter of the
first rank, and let him become king" (Telipinu Procl. §28, ii 36–9). This entrenched
**patrilineal succession**, restricted eligible heirs by the rank of their mother, and —
by implication — removed the king's unilateral freedom to adopt outsiders as heirs, the
source of so much conflict since [[hattusili-i|Hattusili I]].

**3. Protection of the royal family.** Even a prince who committed an offence must
"atone with his head" alone: "do no harm to his house or his son" (Telipinu Procl.
§32, ii 59–61). This removed the incentive to fabricate charges against rivals by
abolishing collective punishment of the offender's family. The death penalty — for
royal offenders — was prescribed but must be carried out openly, not "in secret, in the
manner of Zuruwa, Tahurwaili, and Taruhsu" (Telipinu Procl. §31, ii 52–3).

## Bloodshed Continues Under Telipinu

Telipinu's own reign was not free of violence. Despite his instructions, the exiled
Huzziya and his five brothers were secretly murdered by a man called Tanuwa. The
assassins were convicted and sentenced to death by the pankuš — but Telipinu commuted
the sentence to banishment. This act of mercy may itself have had unintended
consequences: Tahurwaili, one of the assassins spared, may have been the man who
"re-emerged on the political scene after Telipinu's death and seized the throne from
the legitimate heir." Telipinu's own wife Istapariya and son Ammuna were also
apparently assassinated during his reign — their deaths may have been the catalyst for
the final constitutional action that was the Proclamation.

## Death and Succession

Telipinu died without surviving male issue (his son Ammuna predeceased him). He was
survived by a daughter Harapseki and her husband Alluwamna, who — in perfect accord
with the new succession law — was the rightful heir as son-in-law. But the provisions
"gave no adequate safeguard for the security of the royal line": within a generation of
Telipinu's death, an interloper called Tahurwaili seized the throne. The constitutional
project of the Proclamation thus immediately failed in practice, though its diplomatic
legacy (Kizzuwatna alliances renewed by Telipinu's successors) proved more durable.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Telipinu, the Hittite state might have disintegrated entirely under the
pressure of the regicide cycle and the [[hurrians|Hurrian]]/[[mitanni|Mitanni]] advance.
His restoration of the Marassantiya basin and the south-eastern reaches, and above all
his articulation of a legitimate succession order, provided the structural scaffolding
that successor kings could refer back to — even when they violated it. His Proclamation
is simultaneously the best historical source for the Old Kingdom and the document that
created the very concept of constitutional limitation of the Hittite monarchy. Without
it, scholars would know almost nothing about the reigns of Hantili I, Zidanta, and
Ammuna.

## Historiography

The Proclamation of Telipinu is, with the Annals and Testament of [[hattusili-i|
Hattusili I]], the foundational document of Hittite history. It survives in nine
Hittite-language exemplars (all later copies) and a fragmentary Akkadian version,
composed c. last quarter of the sixteenth century BCE. Its primary agenda is political
and theological — justifying Telipinu's rule and the reform by narrative of prior
catastrophe — so its account of his predecessors is shaped by that argument. The
chronology of the Middle Hittite Kingdom immediately following him remains poorly
attested; the period between Telipinu's death and the Empire's founding under
[[shuppiluliuma-i|Šuppiluliuma I]] is sometimes called the "Middle Kingdom" and
remains very thinly documented.
