# Augustine of Hippo

**Aurelius Augustinus** (354–430), bishop of **[[hippo-regius|Hippo Regius]]** in Roman
North Africa, was the most influential theologian of the Latin West and — through the
*Confessions*, the *[[city-of-god|City of God]]*, and the anti-Pelagian writings — one of
the principal architects of the medieval and modern Western Christian mind. A provincial
rhetor who passed through Manichaeism and Neoplatonism to a dramatic conversion, he spent
his last thirty-four years as a working bishop, controversialist, and prodigious author,
dying as the **[[vandal-kingdom|Vandals]]** besieged his city and Roman Africa collapsed.

## Role and Career

Born in 354 at **Thagaste** (Souk Ahras, Algeria) to the modest burgess Patricius and the
devout Christian **[[monica|Monica]]**, Augustine was educated in the narrow rhetorical
curriculum of the late-Roman West and rose as a professional rhetor at Carthage, Rome, and
finally the imperial court city of Milan (autumn 384), where the pagan Prefect Symmachus
had secured him the professorship. For nine years he had been a Manichaean "Hearer"
([[manichaeism|Manichaeism]]); at Milan the preaching of **[[ambrose-of-milan|Ambrose]]**
and a reading of the Latin Neoplatonists (Plotinus, in Marius Victorinus's translation)
dissolved his materialism, culminating in the celebrated garden conversion (*tolle lege*)
of 386. He was baptized by Ambrose at Easter 387, and after Monica's death at Ostia
returned to Africa as a *servus Dei*.

Ordained priest at **Hippo Regius** in 391 and made bishop c. 395/6, he governed a small
Catholic minority in a region dominated by the schismatic **[[donatism|Donatist]]** church.
His episcopate was defined by three great controversies — against the Manichees, the
Donatists (where he came to justify state coercion of heretics), and the
**[[pelagianism|Pelagians]]** — and by the two works that outlived antiquity: the
*Confessions* (c. 397), the first true autobiography of an interior life, and the
*[[city-of-god|City of God]]* (413–426), begun in response to the pagan reaction to the
**[[sack-of-rome-410|sack of Rome]]**. His mature doctrine of
**[[original-sin-and-grace|original sin, grace, and predestination]]** — hammered out
against Pelagius and, to the end of his life, against **[[julian-of-eclanum|Julian of
Eclanum]]** — became the foundation of Western theology. He died on 28 August 430; his
friend and biographer **[[possidius|Possidius]]** recorded the end.

## Counterfactual Significance

Augustine is one of the strongest cases in Western history for individual intellectual
agency. No structural account of the Christianization of the empire predicts the specific
shape he gave to Latin theology: the doctrines of original sin, prevenient grace, and
predestination — as against the more optimistic, "classical" anthropology of Pelagius and
Julian — won not by institutional inevitability but through his particular temperament,
his Manichaean and Neoplatonic formation, and decades of relentless controversy. Had the
Pelagian view of human perfectibility prevailed, the medieval Latin church's account of
sin, sacraments, and salvation — and, through it, the Reformation debates of the sixteenth
century — would have run on radically different lines. His theory of the two cities
detached Christian hope from the fortunes of the Roman state at the moment the Western
empire failed, shaping medieval political theology for a thousand years.

## Historiography

Augustine is unusually well documented: he left the *Confessions*, the *Retractationes*
(a chronological self-catalogue of his works), some 93 works in 232 "books," and hundreds
of letters and sermons, supplemented by Possidius's contemporary *Vita*. The modern
portrait rests on **[[sources/brown-augustine-of-hippo-2000|Peter Brown's 1967
biography]]**, the founding work of the study of Late Antiquity. Two great discoveries
after 1967 reshaped that portrait: the **Divjak letters** (found 1975; Letters 1*–29*),
which document Augustine's powerlessness against the late-Roman state — abusive taxation,
an active [[slavery-in-early-christianity|slave trade]] kidnapping free African peasants —
and the **Dolbeau sermons** (found 1990), preaching to real, often disobedient crowds and
to a still-living paganism. In his 2000 Epilogue Brown retracted his own earlier image of a
"severe, aggressive," burnt-out old bishop, and much recent work (Lancel, Lepelley, McLynn
on Ambrose) has stressed Augustine's day-to-day pastoral life and his engagement with his
Christian present rather than only the classical past. The old "Janus figure" thesis — that
Augustine's turn to Pauline grace marked a sinister break between a sunnier classical
Christianity and a grace-haunted Middle Ages — is now heavily qualified, including by Brown
himself.
