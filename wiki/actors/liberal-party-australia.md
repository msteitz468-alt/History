## Overview

The Liberal Party of Australia was formed in 1944 at two conferences of representatives from non-Labor organisations, replacing the fractured United Australia Party. It was the creation principally of [[menzies-robert]], who recognised that non-Labor needed a "major reorganisation" after the UAP won only 12 seats to Labor's 49 in 1943. From its first federal government win in December 1949 through its defeat in December 1972, and again 1975–83, the Liberal–Country Party Coalition held government for all but three years of the period 1949–83.

## Founding Philosophy

The new party accepted "an expanded welfare state and greater role for government planning" in partnership with private enterprise — not the laissez-faire of the past. As Menzies had articulated in 1942: there could be "no return to the old selfish notions of laissez faire. The function of the State will be much more than merely keeping the ring in which the competitors will fight." Differentiation from Labor "centred on the role of the state, with the home, individual freedom and private enterprise pitted against Labor's socialist commitment to equality achieved through bureaucratic state planning."

## Structural Features

The party was built with a "strong national organisation, professional salaried staff at State and federal levels, an independent financial structure and enthusiastic new branches recruiting middle-class men and women." Policy direction under Menzies was essentially the leader's prerogative — "the mechanisms for party members to debate policy were little used in the 1950s and 1960s" — which meant the party was slower than Labor to adjust to changing attitudes on race and immigration, since those fights were happening in the extra-parliamentary ALP.

## Electoral Record and Coalition

The Liberal Party governed in coalition with the Country Party (later National Party). Key Country Party leaders who shaped policy: Arthur Fadden (to 1958) and John McEwen (from 1958), who — against Menzies' wishes — established the Department of Trade and negotiated a 1957 trade treaty with Japan, beginning Australia's pivot from British to Asian trade. By 1967 Japan had replaced Britain as Australia's leading trade partner.

The Democratic Labor Party (formed after the [[alp-split-1955]]) directed its preferences to the Liberal Party, ensuring Coalition victories in the close federal elections of 1961 and 1969.

## Prime Ministers

- Robert Menzies, 1949–66 (six consecutive election wins)
- Harold Holt, 1966–67 (disappeared December 1967)
- John Gorton, 1968–71 (defeated in Liberal leadership vote March 1971)
- William McMahon, 1971–72 (defeated at December 1972 election)
- Malcolm Fraser, 1975–83 (won three consecutive elections; defeated March 1983)

## Ideology and Evolution

The Liberal Party's governing philosophy remained broadly Menzian through the period: anti-communist, pro-American alliance, supportive of British connection (eroded from Holt onwards), protective of private enterprise, cautiously reformist on social questions. The source notes that after the 1975 dismissal, Fraser governed "more ideologically circumspect than he might otherwise have been," conscious of the need to bind divisions. The emergence within the Liberal Party in Fraser's final term of a free-market "dries" faction (critiquing Fraser's cautious economic management) foreshadowed the neo-liberal turn of the 1980s.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Liberal Party's formation and Menzies' leadership, non-Labor forces faced continued fragmentation. The party's structural advantages — professional organisation, independent finances, middle-class recruitment — enabled the 23-year dominance that shaped Cold War Australia. The [[dismissal-1975]] represents the Liberal Party's most constitutionally controversial moment, with Malcolm Fraser as Opposition leader blocking supply to force an election.
