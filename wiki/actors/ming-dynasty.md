# The Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)

The native Han-Chinese dynasty that **overthrew the Mongol [[mongol-empire|Yuan]]** and ruled
China for nearly three centuries — one of the "five great dynasties," celebrated for its
restoration of Chinese rule, its Confucian bureaucracy, and the spectacular maritime voyages of
**Zheng He**. From John Keay's [[keay-china-a-history|*China: A History*]].

## The founding: Zhu Yuanzhang (the Hongwu emperor)

The founder, **Zhu Yuanzhang** (the **Hongwu emperor**, r. 1368–98), was a "penniless orphan"
turned Buddhist monk turned rebel — one of the very few **self-made emperors** in Chinese
history, consciously compared to the [[han-dynasty|Han]] founder Liu Bang. Rising through the
Red Turban revolt against the failing Yuan, he took Nanjing (1356), defeated his rivals, and
proclaimed the **"Great Ming" ("Brilliant") in 1368**; Ming forces took Mongol Dadu (Beijing)
that year. For the **first time reunification welled up from the south**, and Nanjing became the
capital — the "re-sinified" empire realigned on the [[yangzi-river|Yangzi]] rather than the
Yellow River. Later nationalists hailed it as the reassertion of **Han Chinese identity** after
"alien rule" (Sun Yat-sen and Chiang Kai-shek both later made Nanjing their capital).

Keay stresses the **paradox**: despite claiming to restore Tang and Song institutions, the Ming
in practice **reconstituted much of the Yuan order** (provinces, a hereditary military caste).
And Hongwu's rule became a **reign of terror** unmatched in Chinese history — he abolished the
chancellorship to rule directly, and his purges (1380; 1393) killed tens of thousands.

## The Yongle emperor and Zheng He's voyages

Hongwu's son the **Yongle emperor** (r. 1403–24), a usurper, moved the capital back north to
**Beijing**, building the **Forbidden City** (substantially that which stands today) and
restoring the **Grand Canal**. His reign launched the most spectacular episode: the **treasure
voyages of Admiral Zheng He** (a Yunnanese Muslim eunuch), **seven expeditions (1405–33)** into
the Indian Ocean. Each fleet carried up to **27,000 men in 100–300 ships**, including ~50
**"treasure ships"** perhaps 130 m long and displacing 20,000–30,000 tonnes — "the size of small
cruise liners," five times the size of any contemporary European vessel and many times the
capacity of Columbus's. They reached Champa, Java, Malacca, Sri Lanka, Calicut, Hormuz, Aden,
and the **Somali and Kenyan coasts of Africa** — demonstrating "maritime mastery of the entire
Indian Ocean" seventy years before Vasco da Gama. Then, abruptly, the **voyages were abandoned**
— a Confucian bureaucracy resented their expense and the eunuchs who led them, and the Ming
turned inward (one of the great "what-ifs" of world history).

## Significance and fall

The Ming built the classic late-imperial order: the **Confucian examination civil service**, a
populous commercialized economy, and a vast cultural flowering (porcelain, the novel). It also
built the **Great Wall** in the form tourists see today (Keay: the visible sections are Ming
fortifications, not the First Emperor's). The dynasty declined amid fiscal crisis, factional
eunuch politics, and famine; it fell in **1644** to the peasant rebel **Li Zicheng**, after
which the **[[qing-dynasty|Manchus]]** entered through the Great Wall and seized Beijing,
founding the Qing.

## Late Ming: the Wanli era and structural paralysis (from Huang, 1587)

The reign of the **[[wanli-emperor|Wanli emperor]]** (r. 1572–1620) is the classic case study of late Ming decline in Ray Huang's microhistory *1587, A Year of No Significance*. In 1587 (15th year of the reign, Year of the Pig) "really, nothing of great significance happened": no foreign invasion, no civil war. "On the whole, the Year of the Pig would go down in history as an indifferent one. Can we therefore omit that year from history books? Not quite." It is "precisely those commonplace occurrences which historians have been inclined to overlook that often reflected the true character of our empire."

By 1587 the twenty-four-year-old emperor had ruled almost fifteen years and was exhibiting deepening disengagement. After the death of the powerful Grand Secretary **[[zhang-juzheng|Zhang Juzheng]]** (1582) and the violent purge of his followers and policies, "the empire began to slide slowly but irrevocably into an abyss." Wan-li "carried on a strike against his own bureaucrats over a long period of time" with "no close parallel." Vengeful over succession (favoring third son Ch'ang-hsun by Lady Cheng over eldest Ch'ang-lo) and cynical about the gap between professed yang and hidden yin, he refused to install the heir, left positions unfilled, ignored memorials by burying them in wastepaper, and withheld consent to routine duties that served bureaucratic interests. "For decades court rituals, if conducted at all, had to proceed without the ruler's presence."

The Grand Secretariat under **[[shen-shixing|Shen Shixing]]** (First Grand Secretary from ~1583) operated in a narrow space. Shen, who had known Chang well, adopted the self-assigned role of peacemaker. He eliminated Chang's efficiency-control system (supervising secretaries tabulating arrears and banditry with promotion tied to clearance), arguing that "Tax collection involved so many factors that stood outside an official's control" and that total-arrest demands only produced torture of innocents. In the 1587 evaluation he limited changes to an absolute minimum—only thirty-three doctoral holders demoted, none from the Ministry of Personnel, censorial staff, or Han-lin Academy. He secured imperial consent to suspend the emperor's personal army command, limit travel, and restrain the Eastern Depot eunuch. Yet he was seen as "a great compromiser" and ultimately forced from office in 1591 after the confidential-note incident during the succession crisis: accused of supporting installation publicly while maneuvering privately for favor. "If he stayed on, he would become another Chang Chü-cheng."

The result was constitutional deadlock: "the throne would not rule actively; the civil bureaucracy... could not supply coordinated leadership on its own." Shen had diagnosed "a cleavage between the top and the bottom" and warned that "no state under such conditions has... remained in peace and order for long." He placed faith in "sincerity" arrived at through ritual and classical exposition, but "his own record of four years as first grand-secretary could not convince his critics that the theory had been put into practice."

Huang centers analysis on the bureaucracy's dual character and institutional limits. The 20,000 civil officials "were jealous protectors of their own sinecures... Yet their professed adherence to the Four Books could never be totally discredited." Tax quotas "extremely uneven" and unrevised for two hundred years; customary fees bridged the gap left by low formal salaries (rank 2a minister nominally 152 oz silver). Chang's insistence on full payments bred "loss of good will"; his ostentatious living (house remodeling at 10x the emperor's gift; public-funded mansions and arches in Hukwang; postal abuses) and insults to literati (e.g., Wang Shih-chen, Wang Tao-k'un) alienated the self-perpetuating elite. "The bureaucracy as a body... was a source of power in itself and, in a sense, was master." Shen understood it had "a logic for its professed virtue; it also had an underlying urge for self-gain."

The succession issue became "a constitutional crisis because no legal apparatus existed to break it." Statutory arguments favored flexibility (no birthright until prince title; primogeniture not binding on imperial house; precedent for empress deposition), but "the realm had never been legally structured" to permit departure from Four Books moral law (impartial fathers, elder brothers lead, no sway by women). Public opinion forced denial of the true wish. After eventual installation of Ch'ang-lo the wound "did not heal." Post-1620 partisan recriminations "paralyzed the court more than the issue itself had done."

Huang uses biographical portraits to illustrate the poles of the dilemma:

- **[[hai-rui|Hai Rui]]** (1514–1587): the "eccentric model official" who died in Nanking office on 13 November 1587 (the focal year). Lacking a doctorate, he rose late to magistrate (Shun-an 1558 at age 45) and earned fame by literally enforcing frugality and confronting powerful commissioners (Hu Tsung-hsien's son; Yen Mou-ch'ing). His 1565 memorial to the Chia-ching emperor (pursuing Taoist formulas in seclusion) charged the ruler directly with being "vain, cruel, selfish, suspicious, and foolish," citing excessive taxation, corruption, palace spending, and banditry; climax: "It has already been some time since the people under Heaven started to regard Your Majesty as unworthy." Nearly executed (he bought a coffin); released after Chia-ching's death. As governor of South Chihli (Soochow, 1569) he issued a 36-article proclamation enforcing austerity (food capped at 0.2–0.3 oz silver/day; no luxuries) and intervened in land tenure on moral grounds, receiving 3–4 thousand petitions daily and ordering former grand-secretary Hsu Chieh to halve his massive holdings. Impeached after eight months ("enormous ambition but diffuse talent"); his wife and concubine died the same night (concubine by suicide). Retired fifteen years. At death <20 ounces of silver—not enough for a funeral. His verdict principles explicitly favored the weak: "better to rule against the younger brother rather than the older brother... against the rich rather than the poor... against the member of the gentry rather than the commoner." His approach was "constitutionally closer to and more logically consistent with the system originally established by the Hung-wu emperor" (rustic simplicity, self-governing villages with good/evil pavilions, compulsory banquets for public shaming, Grand Monitions as grievance passport, light tax + heavy service on wealthy, direct moral archetypes). But by the late sixteenth century the Civil Service had doubled in size, officials had become "masters," tax quotas rigid, credit limited to 20,000 pawnshops, and agrarian exploitation (by neighbors/relatives as well as large households) was "the economic basis of the bureaucracy as an institution." Hai personified "the best and the worst features of government by moral principle"; his virtue had "limited utilitarian value." "His approach could not be institutionally adopted as a standard." `produced:` legend and temporary local relief; `contrasts_with:` the pragmatic power of [[zhang-juzheng]] and [[qi-jiguang]]; `concurrent_with:` [[wanli-emperor]] and [[shen-shixing]].

- **[[qi-jiguang|Qi Jiguang]]**: capable military reformer; politically vulnerable after Zhang's fall, died in disgrace 1588.

- **[[li-zhi|Li Zhi]]**: radical thinker outside office; tonsured 1588, suicide in prison 1602.

The appendices reproduce 1590 audiences showing stilted communication. "The diligent had less reason to maintain their diligence; the corrupt, on the other hand, had a better chance to indulge their corruption." The dynasty continued "through mere inertia" because "no alternative to it existed." The Manchu conquest exposed the consequences. Shen Shih-hsing's quiet successes (recalling P'an Chi-hsun for Yellow River "self-scouring" works with fascine bundles and parallel dikes; steering away from full war with Ordos/Cürüke in 1590 while quietly handling the Nurhaci 1587 incident) and the emperor's ritual actions (1585 rain prayer procession on foot to the Altar of Heaven; construction of his own "Mysterious Palace" mausoleum) only underscored that creative energies were being directed into make-believe while the throne withdrew permanently after the 1587 Eastern Depot / Chang Ching confrontations.

### Military Institutions and the Limits of Adaptation (detailed from Huang Ch. 6)

By the mid-sixteenth century the Ming army existed largely on paper. Hereditary military colonies had withered to "2 to 3 percent of its originally recorded strength" in extreme cases; soldiers were diverted to construction, portering, and domestic service. Logistics followed "lateral transmission of supplies at the lowest possible level," with the Ministry of Revenue functioning as "a huge accounting office" supervising automatic deliveries that frequently failed. Civil officials, oriented toward stability, balance, and moral suasion drawn from the Four Books, possessed neither the technical interest nor the philosophical tolerance for concentrated force or drastic measures required in war.

Qi Jiguang (transferred to Chekiang in 1555) had to create a new army almost from scratch against the wokou (mixed Japanese-Chinese pirate bands that built inland bases and conducted sustained raids). He personally devised recruiting, pay, organization, tactics, signals, court-martial rules based on group responsibility, and even field-ration recipes. His "mandarin duck" (yuanyang) squad of twelve coordinated four lancers (12-foot weapons), shield-bearers, bamboo-tree obstructors, and fork-weapon rearguards. Discipline was collective; he personally executed a retreating platoon leader in 1562 to renew an attack. He achieved consistent tactical successes but never separated his command from its peasant base or fully modernized equipment ratios (he limited muskets and retained contact weapons).

Transferred north in 1568 to the Chichou command defending Beijing, Qi (patronized by T'an Lun and [[zhang-juzheng]]) brought southern volunteers, introduced battle-wagon combined-arms tactics with fo-lang-chi guns, and oversaw construction of watchtowers along the Great Wall sector. These represented the dynasty's most effective field forces of the era. Yet after Zhang's death and disgrace, Qi's personal command was viewed as a threat rather than an asset. He was transferred, censured, and dismissed; he died in poverty and obscurity on 17 January 1588 (Year of the Pig). His innovations were never institutionalized. "In the age of gunpowder, he went back to brick fortifications." Three decades later the army faced Nurhaci without benefit of his mixed-brigade tactics.

This outcome flowed from structural incompatibility: an agrarian empire whose bureaucracy prized uniformity and ritual could not accommodate functional specialization or the concentrated power successful generals required. Qi's story demonstrates both the possibility of localized adaptation and its inevitable containment. `produced:` temporary effective units at Chichou; `caused_by:` association with Zhang for later purge; `preceded_by:` the exposure of Ming military weakness in the early 17th century.

### Intellectual Dissent and the Divided Conscience (detailed from Huang Ch. 7)

Li Zhi (1527–1602), from a once-cosmopolitan but now thoroughly Confucianized Quanzhou merchant family, pursued a long, poorly paid civil-service career before resigning as prefect of Yao-an in 1580. Kinship obligations and clan pressures were relentless: gentry families were locked into "the obsessive acquisition of land, the compulsive choice of official careers," and mutual aid that left little room for individual deviation. After resignation Li increasingly distanced himself from family and career while living on gentry patronage.

In 1588 he tonsured himself and established the unlicensed "Hall of Buddha in Fragrant Iris" near Ma-ch'eng as a private chapel and writing base. He maintained a gentry lifestyle while writing prolifically—reinterpretations of classics and history, philosophical essays, and correspondence. He positioned himself as the literati's independent conscience, criticizing hypocrisy (including his own) and refusing to pretend selflessness when self-interest operated.

His public break with former patron Keng Ting-hsiang crystallized the conflict. Li saw Keng as maintaining a double standard; Keng saw Li as nonconformist for its own sake. Li described himself at times as "a peddler in mind and practice" cloaking himself "to deceive the world and earn for himself a false reputation" of benevolence. In 1602 he was arrested in [[beijing]] for heterodoxy. In prison he cut his throat; his last words were "What else could a man over seventy do?"

At trial he defended: "the accused has written many books, which should benefit the propagation of the teaching of the sages rather than damaging it."

Huang rejects both the "martyr" label and the "peasant spokesman" label. Li never questioned the social order sustaining him and never called for reconstruction. His drama was the internal demand for intellectual honesty within a system where "traditional statecraft could not be replaced." Without replacement of that statecraft, "China would not experience Reformation." His divided conscience was emblematic of an order that could reprint his works yet repeatedly proscribe them and ultimately imprison him—surface cultural vitality masking an inability to accommodate the very honesty it claimed to value. `contributed_to:` the historiography of late-Ming intellectual limits; `preceded_by:` the broader heterodox currents of the late 16th–early 17th century.

## Historiography Note (Late Ming from Huang)

Huang reframes traditional moralizing accounts of the Wanli emperor's "laziness" or the "decadence" of late Ming as a structural story. The Ming constitution (no regency even for minors; princes permanently exiled from the capital; grand-secretaries originally only literary stylists; low formal salaries; extreme weight on ritual decorum as the model for the empire) had no robust mechanism for sustained leadership once the throne withdrew after Zhang's fall. The 1577 mourning crisis and the 1587 false-audience episode are presented not as isolated scandals but as symptoms of a system optimized for stability and moral example that had become incapable of effective problem-solving. Shen Shih-hsing's "peacemaker" style is contrasted with Zhang's assertiveness as the only realistic posture left. Later scholarship has qualified the degree of paralysis (noting commercial vitality in some regions and the limits of Huang's institutional determinism), but the image of constitutional deadlock at the center remains highly influential.

## The Giao Chi occupation of Dai Viet (Taylor 2013)

The Yongle emperor used [[ho-quy-ly|Ho Quy Ly]]'s usurpation as a pretext to invade and re-annex Vietnam as
the imperial province of **Giao Chi** ([[ming-occupation-of-dai-viet-1407-1427|1407–1427]]) — the first
direct Chinese rule there in nearly five centuries. Huang Fu (civil) and Zhang Fu (military) governed for
two decades before Yongle's successors defunded and abandoned the province in the face of
[[le-loi|Le Loi]]'s [[lam-son-uprising-1418-1428|Lam Son uprising]]; the Ming recognized Le Loi as king in
1431. In the sixteenth century the Ming again arbitrated Vietnamese legitimacy, extracting a ritualized
surrender from the [[mac-dynasty-vietnam|Mac]] (1540) but never invading. See [[dai-viet]].
