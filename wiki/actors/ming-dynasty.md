# The Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)

The native Han-Chinese dynasty that **overthrew the Mongol [[mongol-empire|Yuan]]** and ruled
China for nearly three centuries — one of the "five great dynasties," celebrated for its
restoration of Chinese rule, its Confucian bureaucracy, and the spectacular maritime voyages of
**Zheng He**. From John Keay's [[keay-china-a-history|*China: A History*]].

## The founding: Zhu Yuanzhang (the Hongwu emperor)

The founder, **Zhu Yuanzhang** (the **Hongwu emperor**, r. 1368–98), was a "penniless orphan"
turned Buddhist monk turned rebel — one of the very few **self-made emperors** in Chinese
history, consciously compared to the [[han-dynasty|Han]] founder Liu Bang. Rising through the
Red Turban revolt against the failing Yuan, he took Nanjing (1356), defeated his rivals, and
proclaimed the **"Great Ming" ("Brilliant") in 1368**; Ming forces took Mongol Dadu (Beijing)
that year. For the **first time reunification welled up from the south**, and Nanjing became the
capital — the "re-sinified" empire realigned on the [[yangzi-river|Yangzi]] rather than the
Yellow River. Later nationalists hailed it as the reassertion of **Han Chinese identity** after
"alien rule" (Sun Yat-sen and Chiang Kai-shek both later made Nanjing their capital).

Keay stresses the **paradox**: despite claiming to restore Tang and Song institutions, the Ming
in practice **reconstituted much of the Yuan order** (provinces, a hereditary military caste).
And Hongwu's rule became a **reign of terror** unmatched in Chinese history — he abolished the
chancellorship to rule directly, and his purges (1380; 1393) killed tens of thousands.

## The Yongle emperor and Zheng He's voyages

Hongwu's son the **Yongle emperor** (r. 1403–24), a usurper, moved the capital back north to
**Beijing**, building the **Forbidden City** (substantially that which stands today) and
restoring the **Grand Canal**. His reign launched the most spectacular episode: the **treasure
voyages of Admiral Zheng He** (a Yunnanese Muslim eunuch), **seven expeditions (1405–33)** into
the Indian Ocean. Each fleet carried up to **27,000 men in 100–300 ships**, including ~50
**"treasure ships"** perhaps 130 m long and displacing 20,000–30,000 tonnes — "the size of small
cruise liners," five times the size of any contemporary European vessel and many times the
capacity of Columbus's. They reached Champa, Java, Malacca, Sri Lanka, Calicut, Hormuz, Aden,
and the **Somali and Kenyan coasts of Africa** — demonstrating "maritime mastery of the entire
Indian Ocean" seventy years before Vasco da Gama. Then, abruptly, the **voyages were abandoned**
— a Confucian bureaucracy resented their expense and the eunuchs who led them, and the Ming
turned inward (one of the great "what-ifs" of world history).

## Significance and fall

The Ming built the classic late-imperial order: the **Confucian examination civil service**, a
populous commercialized economy, and a vast cultural flowering (porcelain, the novel). It also
built the **Great Wall** in the form tourists see today (Keay: the visible sections are Ming
fortifications, not the First Emperor's). The dynasty declined amid fiscal crisis, factional
eunuch politics, and famine; it fell in **1644** to the peasant rebel **Li Zicheng**, after
which the **[[qing-dynasty|Manchus]]** entered through the Great Wall and seized Beijing,
founding the Qing.
