# John A. Nagl

U.S. Army officer (retired lieutenant colonel), scholar, and practitioner best known for *Learning to Eat Soup with a Knife: Counterinsurgency Lessons from Malaya and Vietnam* (2002; paperback with new preface 2005).

His comparative study argues that the British Army functioned as a "learning institution" during the Malayan Emergency while the U.S. Army did not during Vietnam, primarily due to differences in organizational culture (see [[learning-institution]], [[army-concept]], [[counterinsurgency]]). Constructs the institutional learning cycle (drawing on Downie), Question Set #1 (was the army a learning institution?) and Question Set #2 (did it develop successful COIN doctrine?), and grounds analysis in Clausewitz–Jomini–Mao lineage plus Thompson's principles. "the better performance of the British army in learning and implementing a successful counterinsurgency doctrine in Malaya... is best explained by the differing organizational cultures of the two armies." Explicitly extends and cross-references [[krepinevich-the-army-and-vietnam-1986]]. The British regimental system, colonial experience, and civil-military integration allowed adaptation; the American continental tradition, focus on conventional war, and personnel policies inhibited it.

Nagl deployed to Iraq in 2003-04 as Operations Officer of 1-34 Armor ("Centurions") and later contributed to the writing of FM 3-24 *Counterinsurgency* (2006). He has been a prominent voice in post-9/11 debates on adaptation, doctrine, and the limits of military force in irregular war.
