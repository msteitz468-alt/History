# Gaius Sempronius Gracchus

The younger Gracchus, tribune in **123–122 BCE**, whose flood of legislation built a
whole *popularis* programme — and whose death under the first *senatus consultum
ultimum* set the pattern for a century of civil strife. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 3 (Lintott).

## Role and career

More resourceful and a greater orator than his brother [[tiberius-gracchus|Tiberius]],
Gaius as tribune passed an interlocking body of laws that used **popular sovereignty
to create an administration in the popular interest**:
- a **corn law** selling grain to citizens at a fixed low price (6⅓ asses/modius),
  backed by new granaries — the first permanent state subsidy;
- the revival and extension of his brother's **agrarian law** and **colonies** in Italy
  and overseas (the refounded **Carthage / Iunonia**), with road-building;
- the **tax-farming of Asia** auctioned to *publican* companies at Rome — binding the
  moneyed class to the new province (see [[roman-equites]]);
- the transfer of the **extortion court** (*quaestio de repetundis*) to **non-senatorial
  (equestrian) jurors** — the *lex de repetundis* of the Tabula Bembina, a landmark in
  Roman criminal procedure (see [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Pt II);
- protection of citizens against capital trials without the assembly's sanction
  (the *lex Sempronia* behind [[senatus-consultum-ultimum|the *SCU* debate]]);
- and, fatally, a bill to extend **citizenship to Latins and voting rights to other
  Italians** (see [[roman-italian-alliance]]).

The Italian bill broke his coalition; outbid in demagogy by the Senate's tool
**M. Livius Drusus** and abandoned by the urban plebs, Gaius failed of re-election
(122). In **121**, resisting the repeal of his African law, he and **Fulvius Flaccus**
armed their followers; the Senate passed the first **[[senatus-consultum-ultimum|
*senatus consultum ultimum*]]**, and the consul **Opimius** crushed them — Gaius dying
on the Pons Sublicius, with mass executions following.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Gaius is the architect of the *popularis* method at full reach: not the destruction of
the Senate but the **breaking of its monopoly** of political influence, binding
magistrates by assembly-made rules and exposing them to non-senatorial judges. His
corn dole, the equestrian courts, and the publican tax-companies became permanent
fixtures and **political faultlines** of the late Republic. His death normalised the
**SCU** as the Senate's emergency weapon against *populares*.

## Historiography

The main friendly source-strand (Plutarch, Appian) goes back to Gaius' own writings;
Diodorus/Posidonius is hostile ("a sword threatening the Senate"). The detailed reform
of the extortion court survives **epigraphically** (the Tabula Bembina), correcting the
vaguer literary accounts. See [[optimates-populares]].
