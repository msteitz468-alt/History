# Antoine-Henri Jomini

Baron Antoine-Henri de Jomini was "born in Switzerland in 1779." Though he "started work as a banker in Paris, he joined the French army in 1797 and came under the patronage of then General and eventually Marshal Michel Ney." He wrote "a treatise on the campaigns of Frederick the Great in 1803," a work that "contained those core beliefs which sustained him until his death in 1869 at the age of 90." A "difficult egotist and a serial resigner," he held staff positions for both Napoleon and Ney; by 1813 he was Ney's chief of staff, but after being "denied promotion to general de division he offered his services to Russia, where he became a full general." During the retreat from Moscow he "lost his papers at a river crossing as the remnants of the French army were harassed by Russian partisans." His core ideas appeared in his *Art of War* (the *Summary of the Art of War*), "first published in 1830 and then in a revised form in 1838" — "described as 'the greatest military textbook of the nineteenth century.'"

Although [[clausewitz-carl-von|Clausewitz]] "is now considered to be the greater of the two and Jomini is rarely read, it was Jomini who for most of the nineteenth century was taken to be the foremost interpreter of the Napoleonic method." Napoleon "was said to have remarked that Jomini betrayed the innermost secrets of his strategy," and Jomini "certainly claimed, based on his observations of the master, to have discerned basic principles of warfare" — earning him "the 'dubious title of founder of modern strategy.'"

## Strategic Thought

**War as a science of principles.** Jomini sought, "by elucidating the enduring principles of strategy," to "make instruction easier, operational judgment sounder, and mistakes less frequent." He "assumed that military units of equivalent size were essentially equal" — so "only the quality of the commanders and their decisions really made a difference," which "was why he could conceive of [strategy] as following timeless principles." He insisted "that major material shifts, such as the use of railways, were matters of detail."

**Strategy as making war upon the map.** To Jomini, "strategy was the sphere of activity between the political, where decisions were made about who to fight, and the tactical, which was the sphere of actual combat." By calling it "the art of making war upon the map," he stressed how "the theater of operations as a whole was conceived by the commander," taking advantage of "modern cartography." His formula: "Strategy decides where to act; logistics brings the troops to this point; grand tactics decides the manner of execution and the employment of the troops."

**The decisive point and interior lines.** The aspect of war that "truly interested him," per John Shy, "concerned the supreme commander, the Frederick or Napoleon" who "by sheer intellect and will dominated the men who served him." Jomini's commanders "would show their greatness by massing force against weaker enemy forces at some decisive point" — though this was "by no means straightforward in application," requiring boldness, the weighing of risks, and accepting vulnerable flanks. Both Frederick the Great and Napoleon "had demonstrated the importance of following this core principle."

**The geometric, systematic approach.** Jomini stood in the lineage of the Enlightenment "new science of strategy" that included Bülow's "mathematical" and "geometric representations of armies." His armies "appeared as 'faceless masses, armed and fed in mysterious ways.'" He "failed to test the historical cases which did not conform to his precepts." Asked why, if the principles were timeless, "Napoleon [was] such a revelation," his answer was that "the growing maturity in military thought meant that the principles were properly appreciated." Hew Strachan notes that Jomini's "rational and managerial," "prospective and purposeful" theory of war, with its "self-contained view of battle," "appealed to generations of American generals and admirals."

## In Freedman's argument

Freedman files Jomini under the chapter heading "The False Science." The contrast with [[clausewitz-carl-von|Clausewitz]] is the organising point: Jomini offered a prescriptive set of "principles" or maxims that made him "the first port of call for any aspiring strategist and a model of lucidity and intelligibility" — "much easier to follow than Clausewitz." His "prime purpose was instruction," and "his writing showed an optimism that is lacking in Clausewitz." But that very usability was the trap. Because the *Art of War* "was published widely," "opposing armies might well have been following the same precepts, and so the advice would become self-neutralizing, unless one side dared to seek advantage by breaking Jomini's rules." Freedman closes the section with Brian Bond's question: "If strategy was a science whose principles could be learnt what was to prevent all the belligerents learning them? In that case stalemate or attrition must result."

Clausewitz's own attack on those who would "equip the conduct of war with principles, rules, or even systems" was aimed at Bülow but "might also be taken to apply to Jomini." Where Clausewitz offered a deeper, less usable theory, Jomini offered the usable but shallow one.

Both men, for Freedman, belong to the "Strategies of Force" — their "basic instinct" was that "with the 'retirement of the enemy from the field of battle,' terms could be imposed." This places them at the opposite pole from the strategies of cunning Freedman associates with [[sun-tzu]] and [[liddell-hart-basil|Liddell Hart]]: Jomini's strength was massing force at the decisive point, not guile.
