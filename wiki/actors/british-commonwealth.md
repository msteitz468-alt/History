# British Commonwealth (Commonwealth of Nations)

## Overview

The British Commonwealth — formally the Commonwealth of Nations — evolved from the British Empire's self-governing Dominions into a voluntary international organisation of mostly developing countries. The Balfour Declaration (1926) acknowledged equality among the Dominions; the Statute of Westminster (1931) made this law. The term "Commonwealth" came into use by Britain and the Dominions as an alternative to "Empire" from late in World War I and was not widely used by Australians until after World War II.

At federation in 1901, Australians used "Commonwealth" to refer to their own [[commonwealth-of-australia]]. The British Commonwealth evolved through several phases: a "white Dominions" club (1919–c.1960); an expanded multiracial body accommodating Indian and African republics (1949–65); and after 1965 "de-Britannicisation" with a Secretariat and Commonwealth Heads of Government Meetings (CHOGM). As Carl Bridge observes in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], the first CHOGM outside Britain, held in Singapore in 1971, "marked the transformation of the Commonwealth from a 'white man's club' to a 'third world' liberal democratic pressure group."

## Role and Structure

The Commonwealth has no formal constitution or enforcement mechanisms. Its main instruments are:
- **Commonwealth Heads of Government Meetings (CHOGM)**: established 1965 (replacing informal Prime Ministers' Conferences); first held outside Britain 1971
- **Commonwealth Secretary-General**: office established 1965
- **Commonwealth Eminent Persons Groups**: used for ad hoc missions, notably on South African apartheid
- **Colombo Plan** (1950–): bilateral aid framework, particularly for education and technical assistance in Asia

By the early 2000s membership comprised 53 states, including republics, monarchies with the Queen as head of state, and republics with indigenous heads of state.

## Key Events and Australia

- **1919 Paris Peace Conference**: Hughes signed the Versailles Treaty under British Empire auspices; the "Britannic experiment" in coordinated imperial policy began
- **1921 Imperial Conference**: Canada and South Africa vetoed Australian-New Zealand naval funding plans; the limits of imperial coordination revealed
- **1931 Statute of Westminster**: Australia and New Zealand refused to ratify (did so in 1942), remaining automatically committed by Britain's declaration of war in 1939
- **1944 Commonwealth Prime Ministers' Conference**: Curtin proposed a permanent imperial secretariat; rejected
- **1949**: India retained as a republic — a constitutional innovation that allowed non-monarchies to remain
- **1961**: South Africa excluded for racial policy — a development Menzies accepted with "disgruntlement"
- **1965**: Commonwealth "de-Britannicised"; Secretariat established; Crown Commonwealth concept expired
- **1971**: First CHOGM outside Britain (Singapore)
- **1980s**: Australia and New Zealand used Commonwealth forums to oppose South African apartheid and Fiji military coups, sometimes in tension with Britain under Thatcher

## Significance for Australia

For most of the twentieth century the Commonwealth was Australia's primary multilateral framework. By the late twentieth century it became "one of a number of useful platforms for dealing with global and regional issues." The five-power defence arrangement with Malaysia and Singapore (successor to ANZAM), the Colombo Plan, and various regional peacekeeping efforts maintained Commonwealth relevance for Australia even after its economic re-orientation toward Asia.

## Historiography

R.J. Moore, *Making the New Commonwealth* (1987); W. David McIntyre, *British Decolonization, 1946–1997* (1998). The key historiographical question is whether the Commonwealth represented a genuine successor to imperial solidarity or merely a convenient institutional fiction masking the dissolution of British power.
