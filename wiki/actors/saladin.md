# Saladin (Salah al-Din)

**Salah al-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub** (1137/38–1193), known in the West as **Saladin**, was
the Kurdish founder of the **[[ayyubid-dynasty|Ayyubid dynasty]]** who united Muslim Egypt and Syria and
recaptured Jerusalem from the Franks. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (R. Stephen
Humphreys, Ch. 23, "Zengids, Ayyubids and Seljuqs"; Hans Eberhard Mayer, Ch. 21) sets
his career within the wider unification of the Muslim Near East that doomed the
[[crusader-states|crusader states]]. [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]]’s prologue sketch
presents the arc as ordinary Kurdish soldier in a Syrian local potentate’s army → sultan of
Egypt and Syria; victory at **[[battle-of-hattin-1187|Hattin]]** (1187) enabled retaking
Jerusalem and much of Palestine after nearly a century of Crusader occupation.

## Rise (Egypt under Shirkuh and as Fatimid vizier)

Saladin rose in the service of **[[nur-al-din|Nur al-Din]]**, the Zangid ruler of Syria, as part of the
expeditions that intervened in the decaying **[[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] caliphate of Egypt**.
[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] details the chain: Nur al-Din chose the Kurdish amir
**Shirkuh** to lead the Zangid army restoring Shawar (Cairo captured April 1164; Dirgham
executed); Shawar then bargained with Amalric against Shirkuh; October armistice and mutual
withdrawal; 1167 cat-and-mouse without result (Frankish resident adviser left in Cairo);
late 1168 Amalric massacred Bilbays; Fatimid caliph appealed; Franks withdrew as Shirkuh
arrived; Shirkuh executed Shawar, was named vizier, and died weeks later of natural causes
(“or so we are told”).

Shirkuh’s nephew **Salah al-Din Yusuf**—former Alexandria governor and right-hand man—succeeded
as last Fatimid vizier. He faced Turcoman dissent over a “Kurdish coup”; reduced forces;
crushed 1169 Fatimid black African corps unrest over *iqtaʿ* seizures; phased out the Fatimid
army; spring 1171 replaced key administrators. Caliph al-ʿAdid died 13 September 1171; the
following Friday Egypt entered the Sunni fold, naming Abbasid al-Mustadiʾ—the first Baghdad
caliph proclaimed in Egypt in over two centuries.

Nur al-Din delighted at Levant unity Mesopotamia-to-Egypt and Sunni restoration surrounding
the Frankish states (joint Transjordan campaigns 1171, 1173 vs. al-Shawbak and al-Karak) but
grew anxious as Egypt slipped to Saladin’s kin (father Ayyub joined; Turanshah to Nubia and
1174 Yemen; small remittances). He sent tax men and mustered troops; died May 1174 before
confrontation—“Nur al-Din gave Saladin one final gift: he died.”

## Consolidation of Muslim Syria (1174–1186)

Dual succession crises followed: Amalric died ~2 months after Nur al-Din; Baldwin IV (13, leprosy)
under Raymond of Tripoli until 1176; al-Salih Ismaʿil (11) under the eunuch Gumushtakin in
Aleppo. Franks seemed to hold the upper hand initially, but Muslims had Saladin. In 1174
Saladin entered Damascus presenting as atabeg of al-Salih (not usurper) and spent twelve years
putting Muslim Syria under his sway: defeated Aleppo–Mosul at Hama (1175) and Tall al-Sultan
(1176); terms left al-Salih lord but Saladin controlled the armies; Abbasid diploma to Saladin
(not al-Salih) for Egypt, Yemen, and future conquests; married Nur al-Din’s widow; al-Salih
died 1181; Aleppo and Muslim Syria Saladin’s by 1183; Mosul submitted 4 March 1186 after
Saladin’s winter illness near Harran (wife ʿIsmat al-Din’s death withheld).

Cobb’s key thesis for this phase: in 1174 Saladin was **not** primarily planning anti-Frank
glory. He tested the Franks (routed at Mont Gisard 1177; took/razed Jacob’s Ford 1179) but
often let them be—treaty ~1175, four-year truce 1185. **Zangid Syria was a greater threat and
obstacle to ambition than “leper boy-king” Outremer.** By spring 1186 his vast sultanate
(Euphrates to Nile; northern Mesopotamia to Yemen) was divided among ambitious kinsmen; jihad
vs. Franks had been sidelined so long even advisers had misgivings. Dusting off the *mujahid*
title served to assert authority over kin and keep troops busy for plunder—jihad as a political
solution whose “time had come.” Structural contrast with the Almohads: Syrian Franks were more
isolated from European support; Saladin had Baghdad caliph backing and had subdued Muslim foes.

## Hattin and Jerusalem

In 1187 Saladin annihilated the field army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem at the
**[[battle-of-hattin-1187|Battle of Hattin]]** (4 July; army soberly estimated ~30,000,
grossly outnumbering the Franks; water battle in July heat; Reynald of Châtillon personally
executed after refusing conversion; Templars/Hospitallers executed as too dangerous) and went
on to take **[[fall-of-jerusalem-1187|Jerusalem]]** (siege from 20 September; terms settled
Friday 2 October) and most First Crusade Frankish territory by year’s end. Tyre was
reconnoitered twice and left—expediency he would regret. Generosity and carefully distributed
letters recast prior wars on Muslims as unfortunate necessity for Frankish triumph, silencing
critics; Jerusalem’s reconquest was “full in the public eye.” Post-conquest purification of
al-Aqsa and the Dome of the Rock; Nur al-Din’s carved pulpit brought from Aleppo as Nur al-Din
had planned; first Friday *khutba* framed Jerusalem as a “strayed camel” recovered after nearly
a century—as if the thirty-three months fighting fellow Muslims had never happened.

The ʿAbbasid caliph in Baghdad upbraided him for petty failings and warned him not to meddle in
Iraqi affairs; Saladin’s chancery reacted with utmost diplomacy.

## Third Crusade and last years

His capture of the Holy City provoked the **[[third-crusade-1189|Third Crusade (1189–92)]]**.
Tyre siege failed: Conrad of Montferrat rallied the defense; 30 December 1187 Conrad’s galleys
broke the blockade (Muslim watch asleep); 1 January 1188 Saladin withdrew—morale, supplies, and
funds low. Remainder of 1188: mopping up softer targets (Jabala; Yahmur; Sahyun; Tartus left
nearly in ruins; Latakia; Safad; Karak; Shawbak May 1189; Belvoir early 1189). A secretary
wrote, “We have conquered from the borders of Tripoli to those of Antioch…. Antioch remains but
cannot survive”—yet Saladin was forced to a truce with Antioch (which outlived him and his
dynasty).

At Acre (Guy’s siege from late August 1189; Philip and Richard 1191), the city fell 12 July 1191
on terms Saladin’s biographers say he did not authorize; Cobb: Acre was not a great strategic
loss (Franks already had Tyre/Tripoli harbors)—real casualties were prestige and Muslim morale.
Richard massacred some three thousand Muslim prisoners (20 August). Saladin ordered Ascalon’s
fortifications dismantled lest Richard use it against Jerusalem/Egypt. September 1192 truce
(3 years 8 months): Franks hold coast Tyre to Jaffa; Saladin retains demolished Ascalon; free
passage in Palestine. After peace he strengthened Jerusalem’s walls and founded the Salihiyya
madrasa (formerly Church of St. Anne), a hospital, and a sufi meetinghouse.

He died at Damascus on **4 March 1193** after illness (returned after years of warfare;
entertained Bohemond of Antioch at Beirut; took ill in February). Ayyubid dominions were
partitioned among kin.

## Assessment (Cobb)

Cobb’s verdict is deliberately unsentimental: Hattin was not Saladin’s finest hour (Frankish
bungling as much as genius); geopolitics usually gifted except Tyre; after Hattin the military
record was miserable—defeats and prolonged field service alienating commanders. Greatest
achievement: reconquest of Jerusalem (Muslim hands more or less until the end of WWI). The
Third Crusade was a costly stalemate—hard to claim Saladin “won.” He was stronger
diplomatically and politically as pious *mujahid* image-heir to Nur al-Din. Epilogue places
him among the three “most successfully persuasive” leaders (Zangi, Nur al-Din, Saladin) whose
sequential careers create the illusion of a continuous countercrusade.

## Reputation and modern memory

Saladin became a figure of legend on both sides — celebrated in the Islamic world as the
model *mujahid* who restored Jerusalem, and admired even in Latin Christendom (in Dante,
in chivalric romance) for his **magnanimity and chivalry**. He stands as the
counterpart and nemesis of the crusader states, and the founder of the Ayyubid order
that would govern Egypt and Syria into the thirteenth century (until the
[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]]).

Cobb opens with modern memory: a large bronze statue erected in Damascus in 1992 for the
800th anniversary of his death (horseback with companions; two kneeling Crusader prisoners;
gazing “at the West”)—Victorian heroic pose, statuary medium likely idolatrous to the
historical man, standing in a secular Arab socialist capital: Saladin as protean modern
symbol rather than transparent medieval portrait. Streets, schools, Salah al-Din province in
Iraq, statues at Kerak and Cairo’s “Saladin Citadel”; Saddam Hussein claimed Tikrit birth-link,
altered birthdate toward the 800th anniversary of Saladin’s birth, and cast himself as
“Saladin II.” First post-medieval pure biography: Namik Kemal, Ottoman Turkey, 1872; later
reception via Walter Scott’s *The Talisman* (1825) and the Arab Nahda feeding Arab nationalism;
Western popular image (e.g. *Kingdom of Heaven*, 2005) casts him as more knightly than
Christendom’s knights.
