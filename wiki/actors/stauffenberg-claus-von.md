# Claus Schenk Graf von Stauffenberg

## Overview

Colonel Claus Philipp Schenk, Count von Stauffenberg (1907–1944) was the de facto operational leader — never the nominal head — of the military conspiracy that culminated in the 20 July 1944 attempt to kill Hitler. A career General Staff officer from a South German Catholic aristocratic family, he planted and armed the bomb that exploded at Hitler's Wolf's Lair briefing and led the abortive Berlin coup ("Operation Valkyrie") that followed. He was shot by firing squad in the Bendlerstrasse courtyard hours after the plot's collapse. For the assassination attempt itself and its aftermath, see [[events/july-plot-1944]].

## Formation and Family Background

Born 1907 into a South German Catholic aristocratic family; on his mother's side a great-grandson of Gneisenau, cofounder (with Scharnhorst) of the Prussian General Staff, and also descended from Yorck von Wartenburg, the general who signed the 1812 Convention of Tauroggen. Entered the Army in 1926 (17th Bamberg Cavalry Regiment); attended the War Academy from 1936 and joined the General Staff by 1938.

## Role and Career

Served as a staff officer with the 6th Panzer Division in the Polish and French campaigns, then transferred to the Army High Command (OKH) in June 1940, spending roughly eighteen months in Soviet territory organizing Russian "volunteer" prisoner-of-war units. There he grew disillusioned by SS brutality and by Hitler's commissar-shooting order, and met Henning von Tresckow and Fabian von Schlabrendorff, becoming an active conspirator. His turn against the regime is dated by Shirer to the 1938 pogroms, which cast his first doubts, deepened by the outbreak of war in 1939.

On 7 April 1943 he was severely wounded near Kasserine Pass, Tunisia, losing his left eye, his right hand, and two fingers of his left hand, with additional injuries to his left ear and knee; he was treated by Professor Ferdinand Sauerbruch in Munich. By late September 1943 he had returned to Berlin as a lieutenant colonel and chief of staff to General Friedrich Olbricht at the Home Army command — the position that gave him direct, repeated access to Hitler's briefings and made him the plot's executor.

Politically he was more radical than the older, "stodgy" conservative conspirators, insisting on including the Socialists Julius Leber and Wilhelm Leuschner in the planned post-Hitler cabinet. He rapidly became the conspiracy's practical driving force, superseding the nominal leadership of [[actors/beck-ludwig|General Ludwig Beck]] in operational terms even though Beck remained its titular head.

## Death, Immediate Aftermath

Convinced Hitler was dead or gravely wounded — he later claimed, falsely, "I myself saw Hitler's body being carried out" — Stauffenberg persuaded Beck and Olbricht to proceed with Valkyrie regardless. The conspirators failed to seize Berlin's broadcasting headquarters, telephone/telegraph exchanges, or the Gestapo/RSHA headquarters, a failure Shirer calls inexplicable and fatal. When loyalist officers stormed the Bendlerstrasse that night, Stauffenberg was shot in the arm attempting to flee. General Fromm, having broken his parole to loyalist officers, convened a summary "court-martial" and had Stauffenberg shot by firing squad in the courtyard shortly after midnight, 20–21 July 1944, alongside Olbricht, Mertz von Quirnheim, and his adjutant Werner von Haeften. His last words, per Shirer: "Long live our sacred Germany!"

## Historiography

Shirer treats Stauffenberg at unusual biographical depth relative to other conspirators, reflecting the primary-source richness (his wife Countess Nina's testimony, Schlabrendorff's account) available by 1960; a full quote to his wife at his hospital bedside after Kasserine Pass — "I feel I must do something now to save Germany... We General Staff officers must all accept our share of the responsibility" — is treated by Shirer as a turning-point statement. Evans (2008) situates him within the broader conspiracy's structural weaknesses (a tiny base, the loyalty oath, no popular program) while affirming the moral case for the attempt regardless of its odds of success.
