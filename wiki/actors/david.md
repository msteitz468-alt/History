# David

David (r. c. **1000–960 BCE**) was the second king of the
[[hebrew-kingdom|united Hebrew monarchy]], the conqueror who broke
[[philistines|Philistine]] supremacy, captured **Jerusalem**, and built a small
Levantine empire — the founder of the **Davidic dynasty** that ruled Judah until the
Babylonian conquest. He is treated in CAH II.2 (chapter XXXIV, Eissfeldt), which
firmly affirms his historicity against earlier scepticism while reading the biblical
narrative source-critically.

## From Vassal to King of All Israel

David ruled first over **Judah from Hebron** (seven and a half years), at that stage
still effectively a [[philistines|Philistine]] vassal, and after the death of Saul's
heir Ish-baal was accepted as king by the northern tribes of **Israel** as well. His
kingship, the volume stresses, rested on a **different basis from Saul's**: where
Saul was designated "by the grace of God" and acclaimed, David's throne was a
**human recognition of power he had already won** — only later transfigured by the
theology of an "eternal covenant" guaranteeing his dynasty (2 Samuel 7).

## The Capture of Jerusalem

David's pivotal act was the capture of the **Jebusite fortress of Jerusalem**, an
independent Canaanite city on the south-eastern hill (with the Gihon spring and its
water-shaft), which he made his **personal capital** — outside both Israel and Judah
— and, by bringing in the **Ark**, the religious centre of the realm. Kathleen
Kenyon's excavations (cited in the volume) showed the Jebusite town larger than once
thought; David repaired its wall and built a palace with **Tyrian craftsmen**.
Jerusalem has remained the political and spiritual capital of Israelite/Jewish
tradition ever since.

## The Wars of Conquest

The Philistines, reading the move to Jerusalem as revolt, attacked but were heavily
defeated; thereafter they kept autonomy in their own coast but lost supremacy over
Palestine and had to grant David a share of the **caravan-route revenues**. David
then subdued **Moab, Ammon, Edom**, and the [[arameans|Aramaean]] states — above all
**Aram-Zobah under Hadadezer**, the strongest military power in Syria — placing
garrisons as far as **Damascus** and extending his reach toward Hamath. The
underlying aim, the volume argues, was control of the **trade routes** linking Egypt,
Arabia, Syria, and Mesopotamia. He maintained friendly relations with
[[phoenicians|Tyre]], whose northern coast road his trade depended on.

## State and Strains

David's enlarged realm required a new bureaucracy — a standing **mercenary force**
(Benaiah's Cherethites and Pelethites, "light-armed Cretans"), a **corvée**
superintendent, a **chancellor** (*mazkir*) and **scribe** (*sofer*) modelled on
**Egyptian** court offices. The new wealth, however, reached only court, officials,
and merchants, while the mass of Israelites bore the military levy and the burden of
his wars — discontent that fuelled the revolts of **Absalom** (who briefly deposed
him) and of **Sheba** the Benjaminite (which turned on the north–south antagonism).
Both were suppressed, and the succession passed — through court intrigue — to
[[solomon|Solomon]].

## Significance

- `produced:` the **Davidic dynasty** and the capital at **Jerusalem**; a short-lived
  Levantine empire built on control of the caravan trade.
- `caused_by:` the [[philistines|Philistine]] threat that had created the
  [[hebrew-kingdom|monarchy]] in the first place.
- `enabled:` [[solomon|Solomon's]] building and commercial programme by bequeathing a
  consolidated, secure throne; `opposed_by:` and broke the rising
  [[arameans|Aramaeans]] of Zobah (a check that lasted only until Solomon's death).

## Historiography

The David narratives (the "History of David's Rise" and the "Succession Narrative,"
2 Samuel–1 Kings 2) are among the oldest and, the volume judges, most **trustworthy**
strands in the historical books — yet still shaped by court apologetic and legend.
Modern debate ranges from acceptance of a modest historical David (supported by the
9th-century **Tel Dan "House of David" inscription**, found after this volume) to
minimalist doubt about the scale of his "empire," for which archaeology is thin. CAH
II.2 represents the confident Albright-era middle position.
