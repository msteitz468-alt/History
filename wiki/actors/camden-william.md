# William Camden

English antiquary, schoolmaster, and historian (1551–1623), best known as the author of *Britannia* (first published in Latin, 1586; enlarged editions to 1607; English translation 1610). *Britannia* was the most authoritative survey of the physical and historical antiquities of Britain and Ireland in its era, and one of the most influential works of European historical geography. Camden served for many years as second master and then headmaster of Westminster School in London.

## Role in the Druid Revival

Camden's involvement in the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]] began through his friendship with the Flemish geographer Abraham Ortelius, who visited England in 1577 and urged Camden to write a systematic survey of British antiquities for the benefit of European scholarship. Ortelius was already interested in the question of the Druid island of Mona (Anglesey), having corresponded with the Welsh historian Humphrey Llwyd on its identification. The resulting *Britannia*, first published in 1586, was originally cautious on Druids, "alluding briefly to them as practitioners of a heathen religion, relying firmly on ancient Roman sources" ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

As successive editions appeared over the next quarter-century, Camden's treatment of Druids grew more extended and more favourable. The process culminated in the 1610 English translation, which was produced for a domestic patriotic market rather than for an international Latin-reading audience. In this version, Camden quoted the classical sources at length, trimmed to highlight Druidic learning and social authority, and added what he presented as testimony from the early Christian writers Tertullian and Origen that the Druids had predisposed the British to receive Christianity by acknowledging one god.

This last claim was built on a mistranslation. Camden had misread Origen's Greek — failing to recognise that Origen was posing a rhetorical question (whether remote peoples such as the British and Berbers had believed in one deity before Christ, with the implied answer being *no*) as if it were a positive assertion. "Camden's knowledge of Greek, or that of his informant, had not been up to the understanding of the passage" ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]). Other scholars identified the error by the mid-seventeenth century, but the mistranslation was "embedded in a work of huge popularity and influence, justly respected for the generally high quality of its erudition and research," and was cited and repeated for over a century.

Camden's *Britannia* thus established for English readers a vision of ancient Druidry that, while more restrained than the eulogies produced by continental scholars, made the Druids "spiritual and ethnic ancestors worthy of admiration and celebration, firmly positioned in the nation's story." The book was foundational to British [[concepts/antiquarianism|antiquarianism]] and served as the frame around which Gibson's revised edition of 1695 — which incorporated John [[actors/aubrey-john|Aubrey]]'s unpublished manuscript on prehistoric monuments and introduced the Druid-megalith hypothesis to a wider readership — was built.

## Historiography

Hutton treats Camden as the single most important conduit for scholarly views of Druids into English culture in the period 1580–1700, both because of *Britannia*'s exceptional prestige and because its mistakes persisted so long. The Origen mistranslation is Camden's principal historiographical liability; his general scholarship was otherwise of high quality. John Selden, contributing notes to Michael Drayton's *Poly-Olbion* (1612), corrected Camden's reading of Origen, but this correction did not undo the broader effect of *Britannia*'s portrait of the Druids as proto-Christian monotheists. Camden's role in creating the institutional framework for the revised 1695 edition — via Edmund Gibson's network — also makes him indirectly responsible for the publication of Aubrey's Druid-megalith argument, the single most consequential claim in the history of the Druid Revival.
