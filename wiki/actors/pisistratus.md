# Pisistratus

**Pisistratus** was the tyrant of [[athens|Athens]] (intermittently from c. 561, firmly
from 546 to his death in 528/7) whose moderate rule — remembered as "the golden age of
Cronus" — preserved [[solon|Solon's]] constitution, fostered Athens's public cults and
arts, and set the city on the path to its Classical greatness.
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 44, A. Andrewes) treats him.

## The three factions and the road to power

After Solon, Athens was riven by aristocratic faction. Herodotus names three parties —
the **"plain"** (Lycurgus, conservative oligarchs), the **"coast"** (Megacles the
[[alcmaeonids|Alcmaeonid]], for Solon's "middle" constitution), and the **"hill"**
(*Diakrioi/Hyperakrioi*) — the last a third party that Pisistratus, fresh from glory in
the **war against Megara** (the capture of Nisaea), "created deliberately in order to
make himself tyrant." Andrewes is sceptical of seeing this as a class movement of the
poor: Pisistratus reached power not by social upheaval but, after **two failures**
(first seizing the Acropolis with a club-bearing bodyguard; then briefly restored
through a pact with Megacles and the famous charade of "Athena" — a tall girl, Phye —
escorting him back), by **mercenaries and money**. Exiled a second time for ten years,
he gathered funds from the **Pangaeum/Strymon** silver region and allies (Argos,
Eretria, Thebes, Macedon, Lygdamis of Naxos), landed at Marathon, and won the decisive
**battle of Pallene (546)** — the tyranny now "firmly based on mercenaries and money."

## The moderate tyranny

Pisistratus's rule was, by the standards of [[greek-tyranny|tyranny]], notably mild.
Crucially he **did not disturb Solon's constitution** — "the city retained its laws,
except as regards appointment to office" (which the tyrant quietly kept in friendly
hands); archons, Council and Assembly went on functioning, and Pisistratus himself once
answered a homicide summons before the Areopagus. He **reconciled the aristocracy** by
diplomacy (the Alcmaeonid Cleisthenes was archon by 525/4; Cimon's property went
unconfiscated), and helped the people with **agriculture** — loans to poor farmers,
**travelling judges (*dikastai kata demous*)** that loosened their dependence on local
nobles, and a moderate **5% (or 10%) produce tax** that funded the state without
oppression. Andrewes credits his main contribution to Athenian prosperity to this
**care for agriculture and the keeping of internal and external peace.**

## Foreign policy and the sea

Pisistratus pointed Athens toward its maritime future: he **subdued Naxos** and
installed **Lygdamis** as tyrant; **purified Delos** (a "notable assertion of Athens's
primacy among the Ionians"); held **Sigeum** as a family possession (installing his son
Hegesistratus); and approved **Miltiades the Elder's** semi-independent tyranny in the
**Thracian Chersonese** — securing the European shore of the Hellespont on the grain
route from South Russia. He maintained a wide network of foreign ties (Argos, Eretria,
Thebes, Thessaly, Macedon) "more by diplomacy than by arms."

## Cults, building, and culture

Under Pisistratus and his sons Athens's **public cults and festivals** flowered — shifting
religious life from the old aristocratic clan-cults toward "public cults in which the
ordinary man could take part with pride":

- the **Panathenaea** reorganized as a great quadrennial festival (athletic contest from
  566), advertising Athena as the city's goddess (Pisistratus claimed a "special
  relationship" with her — the Phye charade, the Heracles-and-Athena vase scenes);
- the **City Dionysia**, where **Thespis** "first acted" (c. 534) — the modest beginning
  of **Attic drama**;
- the regulation of **Homer** recitals (better attested for his son Hipparchus);
- the annexation of the **Eleusinian Mysteries** as a national cult;
- and a wave of **building** (the Acropolis temple, the Enneacrounus fountain-house, the
  agora as civic centre) and the first Athenian **coinage** (the *Wappenmünzen*, struck
  from Thracian silver) — amid the spectacular rise of **Attic black-figure** vase-
  painting that obliterated Corinth's lead.

Andrewes cautions against overstating Pisistratus's personal role in trade and building
(much was the momentum of a prospering city), but the **development of communal cults**
is a genuine, positive contribution.

## Causal significance

- The **consolidation of Athens** — ending the ruinous aristocratic faction that had
  paralysed the city, while **preserving Solon's laws**, so that the tyranny became "a
  golden age" rather than a despotism and left Athenian institutions intact for
  Cleisthenes.
- The **turn to the sea and to public civic identity** — Delos, the Chersonese, the
  Panathenaea and the City Dionysia — that prefigured the Classical Athenian polis and
  empire.

## The succession (CAH IV)

Pisistratus left three sons; the [[pisistratidae|tyranny passed to **Hippias and
Hipparchus**]], who [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 4, Lewis) shows continuing
his policy of **conciliating the nobles** (the archon-list records even the Alcmaeonid
[[cleisthenes|Cleisthenes]] and Miltiades holding office under them) and his **cultural
patronage**, while his network of foreign friendships frayed. The tyranny hardened after
Hipparchus' [[assassination-of-hipparchus-514bce|assassination]] (514) and fell to Sparta
in 510 — clearing the way for the [[cleisthenic-reforms-508bce|Cleisthenic reform]] that
his preservation of Solon's order had left intact to build on.
