# Medieval European Jewry (Ashkenazi and Sephardic, 11th–12th c.)

The Jewish communities of medieval Europe were, as the editors of
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] insist, "a key element in the civilisation of
medieval Europe" — and the volume (Robert Chazan, Ch. 17, "The Jews in Europe and the
Mediterranean basin") treats them as integral, not marginal, to the period. Two broad
worlds are distinguished.

## Sephardic Jewry (the Mediterranean / Islamic world)

In [[al-andalus|al-Andalus]] and the wider Islamic Mediterranean, long-established
Jewish communities lived in an Arabic-speaking environment (Hebrew reserved for Bible
and liturgy) and enjoyed a cultural **"Golden Age"** — poetry, philosophy (Judah
Halevi; later Maimonides), biblical scholarship, and prominence in commerce, banking,
and medicine. Their security, generally good under earlier Muslim rule, was disrupted
in the twelfth century by the rigorist [[almohads|Almohads]], whose persecution drove
many (including the family of Maimonides) into exile.

## Ashkenazi Jewry (northern Europe)

Chazan's central subject is the **new** Jewry of northern Europe (the Rhineland,
northern France, and — after 1066 — England). Jews were drawn as **immigrant traders**
into a "developing region" by its economic opportunities, settling under the protection
of rulers and towns in a **symbiotic alliance**: the authorities provided physical
safety and commercial support; the Jews stimulated the economy and (increasingly, by
the mid-twelfth century) provided **credit and moneylending**, building loans against
land in England and northern France. The greatest figure of Ashkenazi learning was
**Rashi of Troyes (1040–1105)**, whose literal biblical exegesis influenced even
Christian scholars (Andrew of St Victor).

## The Turn to Persecution

The period is, however, a turning point toward the deteriorating position of European
Jewry. The northern communities suffered an "unusually high level of anti-Jewish
sentiment" — fed by their visibility as immigrants and moneylenders, by traditional
ecclesiastical hostility, and by new fantasies (the blood libel and ritual-murder
accusations, spreading from the mid-twelfth century, which even rulers like Frederick
II and popes like Innocent IV would later denounce as baseless). The **Rhineland
massacres of 1096**, perpetrated by bands of the [[first-crusade-1095|First Crusade]] at
Speyer, Worms, and Mainz, were a watershed: the first large-scale crusading violence
against Jews and a trauma that marked Ashkenazi memory. The Augustinian guarantee of a
tolerated, subordinate Jewish place in Christian society was beginning to give way to
the harsher attitudes that would culminate in the expulsions of the later Middle Ages.
