# Maxentius

**Marcus Aurelius Valerius Maxentius** (r. in Italy 306–312, d. 28 Oct 312) was the son of [[maximian|Maximian]] who, sidelined by the 305 succession and again at Carnuntum (308), held Italy, Rome, and North Africa as Augustus without formal tetrarchic acknowledgment until his defeat by [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]].

## Regime and fall (Mitchell)

Praetorian backing and Maximian’s influence enabled about six effective years of defiance from Rome. Constantine crossed the Alps, won over most of northern Italy, defeated Maxentius’s forces outside Verona, and delivered the final blow at the **Milvian Bridge** (28 October 312); Maxentius perished in the Tiber. The battle’s religious aftermath — Constantine’s labarum tradition — is treated by Mitchell especially in his revised Constantine chapters (3 and 8).

## Counterfactual significance

Without Maxentius’s Italian regime, Constantine’s path to sole power and the religious politics of 312–313 look entirely different; Rome remains under tetrarchic rather than Constantinian control in the critical decade.

## Historiography

Constantinian propaganda (panegyric, Eusebius, Lactantius) demonizes Maxentius; Mitchell reads the military campaign as hard-edged civil war, not only providential narrative. Survey: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015|Mitchell 2015]].
