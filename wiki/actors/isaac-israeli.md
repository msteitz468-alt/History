## Overview

Isaac Israeli (Isḥāq ibn Sulaymān al-Isrāʾīlī) was a physician and Neoplatonic philosopher born in Egypt around the middle of the ninth century. His life is said to have extended for about a hundred years. He traveled from Egypt to what is now Tunisia, and possibly further east, and seems at some point to have encountered the works of [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]], from whom he drew extensively. He was the first identifiable **Jewish Neoplatonist** — a type that would recur several times in the medieval Jewish philosophical tradition, most famously in Ibn Gabirol (eleventh century).

## Medical Career

Like his later contemporary [[abu-bakr-al-razi|al-Rāzī]], Isaac Israeli was primarily a physician. He reportedly claimed that his medical writings would be a better legacy than any children. Works on medicine, diet, fevers, and urology survive and were influential in both Arabic and Latin medical traditions.

A partially preserved work **On Spirit and Soul** illustrates his integration of medical and philosophical knowledge:
- "Spirit" (*rūḥ*) is a subtle physical substance pervading the body, derived from Galenic pneumatic physiology.
- "Soul" (*nafs*) is sharply distinguished from spirit: it is an immaterial principle capable of surviving bodily death — a commitment that goes beyond Galen's deliberate agnosticism about the soul's nature and reflects Neoplatonic and Jewish theological commitments.

## Philosophical System

Isaac draws on [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] and on Greek Neoplatonic works in Arabic translation (Plotinus, Proclus) to construct a Jewish Neoplatonic emanationist metaphysics.

### Emanation and Creation

In the standard Neoplatonic scheme (Plotinus), all things emanate necessarily from the One → Intellect → Soul → matter. Isaac modifies this:
- **God** is the Creator who alone is capable of absolute origination (*ibdāʿ*, the bringing of things into existence from nothing) — a theological commitment that distinguishes him from pagan Neoplatonists, who held that emanation was necessary and eternal.
- Between God and the universal Mind, Isaac interposes **two intermediary principles** emanated directly by God: **first matter** and **first form**. These two then combine to constitute a perfectly wise universal mind that knows all things.
- From the universal Mind, further emanation produces a universal rational Soul; from the Soul, lower types of soul; finally, the physical universe — with the heavenly bodies causally primary in producing bodily substances below.

This innovation (the matter-form dyad at the highest level of the system) was taken up and developed by Ibn Gabirol in his *Fons Vitae*. It has the effect of explaining the progressive "darkening" of the emanative process: matter at the highest ontological level casts shadow rather than only reflecting light, and creation is as much a casting of shadow as a shining of light. Perfection and unity progressively fall away as God's activity becomes more mediated.

### Creation and Necessity

There is scholarly debate about whether Isaac's God creates necessarily (as in Plotinus) or freely. He describes creation both as an emanation and as an act of will, and does not resolve the tension. Adamson suggests Isaac probably thought, like Plotinus, that God necessarily gives rise to all things while remaining free in the sense that no external cause compels Him.

### Special Kind of Causation

Both Isaac and [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] use the Arabic term *ibdāʿ* ("origination") to describe the unique mode of causation by which God brings things into existence from nothing — as opposed to the natural causation by which creatures produce further creatures from pre-existing materials. This terminology appears to be shared across Jewish and Muslim Neoplatonism in the early period and reflects the Islamic theological context in which these thinkers were working.

## Significance

Isaac is notable as:
1. The **first Jewish Neoplatonist** in the medieval period.
2. An early transmitter of Kindian and Greek Neoplatonic ideas into Jewish philosophy.
3. A thinker who anticipates Ibn Gabirol's metaphysical system (the matter-form dyad at the celestial level).
4. An instance of the broader pattern by which Jewish philosophers in the Islamic world were contemporaneous with and responsive to Muslim philosophical developments, not merely derivative of them.

## Historiography

Isaac's philosophical work survives only partially, making reconstruction difficult. His *On Spirit and Soul* and a few other philosophical fragments survive; his medical works are more complete. His relationship to [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] is clear from textual parallels, though the precise channel of transmission is uncertain. He is often treated as a minor precursor to the greater Jewish Neoplatonists, but his role as the **originator** of that tradition gives him foundational importance.
