# Jan Žižka (c. 1360–1424)

Bohemian military leader and one of the most successful commanders of the Hussite revolution. Blind in one eye (later both), Žižka forged the Hussite peasant and townsman levies into a highly effective field force that repeatedly defeated larger crusading armies sent by Emperor Sigismund and the papacy.

## Role and Significance

Žižka provided the operational and tactical backbone for the radical wing of the Hussite movement (Taborites). He pioneered or perfected the use of the *wagenburg* (wagon fortress) combined with handguns and peasant infantry, turning religious fervour and social grievances into disciplined defensive-offensive power. His campaigns kept the revolution alive through five crusades (1420–1431).

## Key Events

- 1419–1420: Early defence of Prague and formation of Tabor.
- Victories at Sudoměř, Vitkov (1420), Kutná Hora (1421–2).
- 1424: Death from plague during campaign; leadership passed to Prokop the Great (Prokop Holý / the Shaven).

## Historiography

Traditional Czech national historiography celebrates Žižka as a proto-national military genius. Modern scholarship (including Housley) stresses the religious dimension: Žižka's armies fought under the conviction that their cause was divinely sanctioned. Taborite practice combined extreme claims with an "economy of violence" rather than indiscriminate massacre in many operations. Counterfactual: without Žižka's leadership the Hussite military revolution might have collapsed early, altering the religious map of Central Europe and the trajectory of the Council of Basle negotiations.
