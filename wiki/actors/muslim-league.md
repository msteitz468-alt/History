## Overview

The All-India Muslim League was the political organization that drove the demand for a separate Muslim homeland in South Asia, articulating the two-nation theory under Muhammad Ali Jinnah and culminating in the creation of [[pakistan]] at the 1947 [[partition-of-india-1947]].

## Role and Key Developments

In the mid-1930s Jinnah argued that "religion should not enter politics" and that the League was concerned only with securing minority political representation for Muslims within a united India. This position shifted decisively at the 1940 Lahore session, where Jinnah articulated the two-nation theory — that Hindus and Muslims "belong to two different civilizations which are based mainly on conflicting ideas and conceptions" — providing the ideological basis for a separate Muslim state.

The League's women's sub-committee, led by Lady Nusrat Haroon, organized from 1940 in support of Pakistan. Begum Ikramullah framed the demand for Pakistan as "an assertion of our separate, independent, religious and cultural existence" against assimilation into a Hindu-majority India. In 1947, League women strategically used purdah conventions during civil disobedience, since police could not touch their bodies — turning a restrictive social convention into a tactical advantage.

The League's demand was opposed by [[mawdudi-abu-ala]], who considered a Muslim nation-state on Western/nationalist lines an "un-Islamic" idea, preferring a transnational Islamic state governed by God's sovereignty over a bounded ethno-religious nation-state.

## Counterfactual Significance

The League's shift from minority-representation politics to the two-nation theory at Lahore (1940) was the pivotal turn that made Partition — rather than continued negotiation within a united India — the operative outcome.

## Historiography

Formichi (2020) situates the League's women's-wing activism as evidence that Pakistan's founding mobilization drew on a broader social base than elite male politics alone, while treating the two-nation theory itself as a contingent late development rather than an inevitable outgrowth of South Asian Muslim politics.
