# Hamilcar Barca

Carthaginian general, founder of the **Barcid power in Spain** and father of
[[hannibal]], Hasdrubal, and Mago. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 2
(Scullard).

## Role and career

Hamilcar commanded in Sicily in the last years of the
[[first-punic-war-264-241bce|First Punic War]], holding out undefeated until the
peace of 241, and then suppressed the **[[mercenary-war-241-237bce|Mercenary
War]]** in Africa. Overshadowing his rival **Hanno the Great**, he led an army to
**Spain** in 237 to rebuild the wealth and manpower Carthage had lost with Sicily
and Sardinia.

In nine years (237–229) he conquered or reconquered southern and south-eastern
Spain, founding **Akra Leuke**, exploiting the silver mines, and recruiting Iberian
manpower. His position was effectively **vice-regal** — a colonial governorship
accepted by the home government, financed by his own minting at Gades. He drowned in
229 while covering the escape of his sons during a reverse against the Orissi. His
son-in-law **Hasdrubal** succeeded him (229–221), founded
[[new-carthage|New Carthage]], and negotiated the **Ebro treaty** with Rome (226).

## Counterfactual significance

Hamilcar built the territorial, financial, and manpower base without which the
[[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second Punic War]] could not have been fought. CAH
VIII (Scullard) is careful, however, to **separate two questions**: that Hamilcar
created a Spanish empire is certain; that he planned it from the outset as a base
for a **war of revenge** against Rome (Polybius' "wrath of the Barcids") is the
disputed claim — see [[causes-of-second-punic-war]]. Scullard reads the Spanish
venture as primarily compensatory (replacing lost Sicily and Sardinia), with the
revenge motif a later, partly propaganda, construction.

## Historiography

The sources divide along the **pro-Barcid** (Polybius via Silenus) and
**anti-Barcid** (Fabius Pictor) lines. Polybius locates the war's deepest cause in
Hamilcar's wrath; the Fabian tradition shifted blame to Hasdrubal and Hannibal.
The Barcid **coinage** of Spain (Gades, New Carthage) is the key independent
witness to the family's growing wealth and quasi-monarchic self-presentation.
