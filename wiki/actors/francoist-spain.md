# Francoist Spain (1936–1975)

The right-authoritarian state built by [[franco-francisco|Francisco Franco]] out of the Nationalist zone of the [[spanish-civil-war-1936|Spanish Civil War]] and maintained until his death in November 1975 — one of the longest-lived right-authoritarian systems of the twentieth century. Payne (1987), the standard political history, insists the regime "was not one but several," passing through three broad phases while Franco's personal beliefs and monopoly of ultimate authority provided the continuity.

## Overview and Phases (Payne 1987)

1. **The semi-fascist New State, 1936/39–1945.** Born of the military rebellion of 18 July 1936 and Franco's elevation to Generalísimo and Chief of State (October 1936), consolidated by the forced unification of the [[falange|Falange]] and Carlists into the single party FET y de las JONS (April 1937). The most fascistized phase, peaking during the "German Phase" of 1940–42 when entry into the war beside the Axis was seriously weighed (see [[hendaye-meeting-1940]]).
2. **National-Catholic corporative dictatorship, 1945–1959.** Defascistization after the Axis defeat: Catholic ministers foregrounded, the Fuero de los Españoles (1945), survival of UN ostracism (1946), then Cold War realignment — the Concordat and the Pact of Madrid with the United States (both 1953) ended isolation. Economically the autarkic 1940s were the regime's disaster decade ("years of hunger").
3. **Developmentalist bureaucratic authoritarianism, 1959–1975.** The technocratic turn of 1957 (ministers linked to [[opus-dei|Opus Dei]]), the 1959 Stabilization Plan, and the [[spanish-economic-miracle|desarrollo]] — the fastest economic growth in Spanish history — accompanied by institutional "decay": the 1966 Organic Law, the 1969 designation of [[juan-carlos-i|Juan Carlos]] as successor, the assassination of [[carrero-blanco-luis|Carrero Blanco]] (1973), and terminal drift until Franco's death.

## Structure

A personalist dictatorship ("caudillaje" — Payne's nearest analogy: elective but absolute monarchy) balancing "political families" — army, Falange/Movimiento, Church, monarchists, Carlists, later technocrats — that Franco played against one another, sharing some ideas of each while rejecting the full program of any. Legitimation rested on victory in the Civil War (Franco's private "right of conquest"), [[national-catholicism]], and after 1959 on economic performance.

## Detail (Payne 1987)

**Wartime and 1940s state.** The name "Nationalist" (from August 1936) denoted "religion, tradition, and Spanish patriotism"; the zone underwent a "cultural counterrevolution of unprecedented proportions for any western country in the twentieth century." Post-1939 Spain was the most centralized state in Spanish history (Catalan/Basque statutes annulled, languages suppressed; only Navarre and Álava kept partial self-administration); 80% of state jobs were reserved for regime veterans and victims' kin; military men held 45.9% of ministerial posts to 1945. Autarky (proclaimed June 1939; INI founded 1941 on the IRI model) plus wartime devastation produced the "years of hunger": at least 200,000 excess deaths from malnutrition and disease in five years, rationing from May 1939 for over a decade, a black market (*estraperlo*) absorbing a third of grain output, the worst inflation in Spanish history (prices ~6× the 1936 level by 1948), living standards not surpassing 1936 until 1952, and 1950 per-capita GNP below Venezuela, Uruguay, and Argentina. The five biggest banks raised profits ~700% — ironic under a nominally anti-capitalist syndical state.

**World War II.** Non-belligerence (12 June 1940, at Ciano's suggestion) was "perfectly clear[ly]... the first step toward Axis alignment"; Tangier was occupied two days later; the Blue Division (18,694 men initially; 47,000 served, ~22,000 casualties, ~4,500 dead) fought as German Division 250 near Leningrad, its residue ending in the Waffen SS defending the Führerbunker perimeter. Counterweights: British bribery of some ten senior generals via Juan March; the November 1942 partial mobilization that helped deter German plans (Isabella/Ilona) to enter the peninsula; the May 1944 wolfram agreement. Payne's balance: Spain was "relatively neutral only during the first (1939–40) and last (1944–45) phases"; on Jewish refugees, ~30,000 received safe passage with no documented case of a Jew on Spanish soil handed back, and Spanish consuls protected 3,200+ more in the later roundups (Avni) — while Sweden and Turkey accommodated the Axis in some respects more than Spain did.

**Ostracism and rehabilitation.** The December 1946 UN recommendation to withdraw ambassadors (34–6) was answered by the Plaza de Oriente rally, Perón's Argentine lifeline (25% of imports at the 1948 peak), and Cold War reversal: WHO 1951, Concordat and Pact of Madrid 1953 ($1.69bn economic + $521m military aid in a decade), UN membership 1955 — by which point, Payne notes, the regime had fulfilled all seven points of the Calvo Sotelo/Acción Española blueprint for an authoritarian "instauration." The 1951 Barcelona strike (several hundred thousand workers) was the largest labor action of the regime; the February 1956 university crisis its first internal political crisis in fourteen years.

**The late regime.** After the [[spanish-economic-miracle|desarrollo]] transformation, institutional reform stayed cosmetic: the 1963 Tribunal of Public Order civilianized political justice only until 1968; the 1966 Press Law traded prior censorship for "voluntary" self-censorship (Franco: "I do not believe in this liberty, but it is a step required for many important reasons"); the 1966 Organic Law (95.9% referendum yes) separated the premiership from the headship of state without touching the succession; the 1962 Munich meeting of 118 oppositionists ("contubernio de Munich") drew internal exile for returnees. Late-regime crises — the MATESA scandal (1969), ETA violence (47 victims 1968–75; the 1970 Burgos trial), clergy defection after Vatican II, the Carrero assassination (1973), the Carnation Revolution next door (1974), the September 1975 executions — accumulated against a state whose economic footprint (state budget ~21% of GNP, 1973) was proportionately the smallest in Europe. Decolonization: Morocco 1956 (Ifni/Sahara raised to provinces 1958 after Moroccan attacks), Equatorial Guinea 1968 (whose ruler Macías then killed an estimated 50,000), Ifni ceded 1969; Gibraltar's frontier closed 1969 without result.

## Classification

The central classificatory problem the regime poses — fascist, semi-fascist, or authoritarian — is treated at [[concepts/para-fascism]] and [[concepts/payne-typology-of-authoritarian-nationalism]]: Payne and Griffin concur that Franco absorbed and neutralized genuine fascism ([[falange]]) rather than embodying it. Juan Linz's model of the "authoritarian regime" was built on the Spanish case — Payne quotes the 1964 definition verbatim: "political systems with limited, not responsible, pluralism: without elaborate and guiding ideology (but with distinctive mentalities); without intensive or extensive political mobilization." Payne's own formula: "from 1937 to 1945 the Franco regime was doctrinally at least a semifascist state" (Amando de Miguel's *fascismo frailuno*, friar-fascism), juridically "the most arbitrary in Europe until after the end of World War II," yet with a state party weaker than Italy's PNF; the closest comparative parallel is Antonescu's Romania, which likewise allied with and then subdued a fascist movement. Defascistization ran in three phases (1942–47, 1956–58, 1967–68 onward) until Juan Carlos dissolved the Movement in April 1977. Counterfactual, per Payne: "Had Hitler won the war, there seems little doubt that Franquism would have become less conservative and rightist and more radical and overtly fascist in form." And the transition verdict: "Never before had the formal institutional mechanisms of an authoritarian system been used peacefully but systematically to transform the whole system from the inside out."

## Historiography

Payne (1987) is the standard English political history, written with deliberate distance a decade after Franco's death; Preston's later biography of Franco (1993) is far more hostile, and Spanish historiography of the repression expanded dramatically after the 1990s (see [[francoist-repression-1936]]). Payne's periodization (following Ramírez Jiménez's totalitarian / empirico-conservative / tecno-pragmatic sequence) is widely used.

## Payne (1980): "semifascist," and the defascistization sequence

From [[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]] (ch. 6): Franco (dictator from 1 October 1936) deliberately avoided "the Primo de Rivera error" of a doctrine-less personal military dictatorship, creating the FET partido único in April 1937 with the Twenty-Six Points as official doctrine but "a point of departure" only. Payne: "it is doubtful... that one can speak of a fascist regime unless it is dominated and constructed by generic or categorical fascists, and this was hardly the case with Franquism.... That early Franquism contained a major component of fascism is undeniable, but it was so restricted within a right-wing, pretorian, Catholic, and semipluralist structure that the category of 'semifascist' would probably be more accurate." Structural parallels with Mussolini's Italy: subordinated state parties diluted with nonfascists; limited pluralism under executive dictatorship; institutionalization led "not primarily by revolutionary fascist ideologues, but more commonly by monarchist theoreticians of the radical right"; and matching suppressions of militant national syndicalism (Rossoni's sbloccamento 1928 / Salvador Merino 1940). Sequences ran parallel (coalition 1922–25 / 1936–37; institutionalization 1925–29 / 1937–42), but Franco, unlike Mussolini after 1933, "had no illusions that he need not wait on events." Counterfactual: "Had Hitler won the war, there seems little doubt that Franquism would have become less conservative and rightist and more radical and overtly fascist in form." Defascistization began as early as 1942 (antifascist opinion among rightist/Catholic sectors plus the war news); caudillaje was always "more restrained" than German leadership theory; by end-1945 Spain was transitioning to "a corporative, nonmobilized 'bureaucratic authoritarian' regime" — completed by the 1957 technocratic turn and the 1958 Principles of the Movement (see [[falange]]). In Payne's seven-type regime taxonomy: Spain 1937–45 is category 4 (syncretic dictatorship with a significant fascist component, with Antonescu's Romania); 1923–30 and post-1945 Spain category 6 (conservative/bureaucratic-nationalist, with Salazar's Portugal). Payne also notes Franco's Spain was the only "fully institutionalized modern European dictatorship" before post-Franco Spain not toppled by external military defeat.
