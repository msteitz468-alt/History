## Overview

Ahmad Sirhindi (1564–1624), known to his followers as "Mujaddid-e Alf-e Sani" (the Renovator of the Second Muslim Millennium), was the most formidable intellectual opponent of [[akbar]]'s universalist religious policy and the founder of the Mujaddidi branch of the [[tariqa-orders|Naqshbandi]] Sufi order in India. Unusually among Shari'ah-minded critics of Akbar, Sirhindi "waged his fight within the forms of Sufism itself" rather than attacking Sufism from the outside.

## Intellectual Position

Sirhindi's central theological intervention concerned the doctrine of *wahdat al-wujud* ("oneness by existence"), the monist metaphysics associated with Ibn al-Arabi and widely embraced by Indian Sufi universalists. He argued that this doctrine — which held that all existence participates in the divine — "answered to merely one stage in the long sequence of positions in the mystical quest." Having himself, he claimed, passed through and far beyond this stage, he proclaimed the superior realization of *wahdat al-shuhud* ("oneness by witness"): mystical experience reveals an ultimate distinction between God and creation, not an identity.

The practical implication was significant: by grounding the highest mystical experience in a God who remained ultimately other than creation, Sirhindi restored the paramount authority of external Shari'ah law — which governed relations between the distinct human creature and the sovereign divine — over inner spiritual experience. The Sufi path was thus subordinated to, not transcendent of, Shari'ah obligations. This allowed Sirhindi to "reconcile the very personal mystical experience, to which Sufism gave a place, with an intensely social activism such as the Islam of the Sharî'ah demanded."

## Political Role

Sirhindi became active "in the very last years of Akbar's reign" and directed his criticism at what he saw as a Muslim ruling class that had drifted toward Hindu accommodation. "He denounced Akbar's compromises with Hinduism, but he saw that the roots of Akbar's attitude lay within Islam itself, especially within Sufism." His Naqshbandi followers maintained pressure within the Mughal court throughout the seventeenth century.

[[aurangzeb]] was "friendly to the Mujaddidî Naqshbandîs, followers of Sirhindî" — a connection that reflected the ideological alignment between Sirhindi's communalist Sufism and Aurangzeb's Shari'ah-minded turn. However, Hodgson cautions that "at the time, the influence of such men as Sirhindî was slight" and that "Sirhindî's followers greatly exaggerated his influence" in later retrospective accounts.

Jahangir (Akbar's successor) imprisoned Sirhindi, "but, when he thought he had repented, showed him some honour and let him go back to his home in the Panjâb where his chief following was."

## Legacy

The Mujaddidi branch of the Naqshbandi order became a major force in Islamic revivalism far beyond India. It was introduced to Ottoman lands (by way of the Hijaz) in the eighteenth century and shaped revivalist movements there. As Hodgson notes in [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974]], "The Mujaddidî branch of the Naqshbandiyyah Tarîqah order was introduced from India (by way of the Hijâz) to the Ottoman lands." Sirhindi's philosophical restatement of the relationship between mystical experience and Shari'ah law became influential in subsequent Islamic revivalist thought from South Asia to Central Asia to the Arab world.

## Formichi: Trans-Asian Naqshbandi Lineage

Chiara Formichi ([[formichi-islam-and-asia-2020]]) situates Sirhindi within a wider trans-Asian
Naqshbandiyya network rather than a purely Mughal-court controversy: his title *mujaddid alf saani*
("renewer of the second millennium") marks him within the order's broader **tajdid** (renewal)
tradition traced back through Central Asia to Bukhara's Baha ud-Din Naqshband. Formichi traces a
later intellectual chain — via the khalifa **Ishan Niyazquli at-Turkmani** — from Sirhindi to
**'Abd al-Nasir al-Qursawi** of Bukhara (1776–1812), whose ijtihad-centred reformism was in turn
claimed by the late-19th-century Central Asian **Jadidist** movement, independent of Wahhabi
influence — evidence, per Formichi, that Sirhindi's Naqshbandi revivalism had lasting influence
across Asia well beyond the Mughal court dispute Hodgson stresses below (see
[[sufism-in-south-asia]]).

## Historiography

Hodgson is sharply critical of the hagiographic tradition around Sirhindi. "Sirhindî's followers greatly exaggerated his influence. These exaggerations have penetrated even so non-political a work as Subhan's study of Indian Sufism." The major English-language study of his thought, Burhan Ahmad Faruqi's *The Mujaddid's Conception of Tawhid* (Lahore, 1940), is dismissed by Hodgson as "exceedingly ignorant and credulous as it is bigoted." The inflation of Sirhindi's influence is linked to the Pakistan nationalist movement's need for a communalist Islamic intellectual hero to counterpose against Akbar.
