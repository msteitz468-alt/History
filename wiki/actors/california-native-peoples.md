# California Native Peoples

Umbrella actor for the diverse Native nations of Alta California under Spanish missions, Mexican ranchos, and U.S. conquest/gold rush—not a single polity. Primary synthesis: Lamar & Truett, [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-2|CHNPA I.2]] ch. 10.

## Overview: scale and diversity at contact

At first contact an estimated **275,000–300,000** Native peoples lived in what is now California—exceptionally large versus Texas, New Mexico, or Arizona, though without large towns. Population rested on coastal marine life (fish, seals, sea otter), salmon rivers, San Joaquin/Sacramento wildlife, and (for Central Valley majority) hunting plus **acorn and seed harvests** rather than agriculture. **Chumash** built seagoing plank boats. Linguistic diversity spanned **six major families**—Athapaskan, Algonquian, Yukian, Hokan, Penutian, Uto-Aztecan—and ~**118 subgroups** (Kroeber estimate for 1670).

Not all California was bountiful: Shasta, Yana, Modoc, northern Paiute faced scarce food; Paiutes of Mono Lake, Owens Valley, and east of the Sierras dug insect larvae/grubs—disdainful Americans called them **“Diggers”** and applied the name to all California Indians. SE desert Mohaves and lower Colorado Yumas practiced floodplain agriculture resembling Pima farming. Powerful territoriality and negotiation over homeland crossing; Europeans mistook general avoidance of violence for passivity or stupidity.

## Early European probes (1542–1769)

Captain Juan Rodríguez **Cabrillo** sailed north at least to San Diego Bay in **1542** (concurrent with Coronado’s end and de Soto on the Mississippi). **Vizcaíno** (1602) urged colonization without result. Sir Francis **Drake** put in around San Francisco Bay in **1579**. Cartographers long thought California an island of Amazon-like women until Father Eusebio **Kino** recognized a Pacific shell traded overland—“California is not an island!” Occupation lagged until fear of Russians in Alaska and José de Gálvez’s reorganization spurred action.

## Spanish mission frontier (1769–1830s) — demographic collapse begins

In **1769** Franciscan father **Junípero Serra** and followers joined Captain Gaspar de **Portolá**; Serra founded the first of an eventual chain of **twenty-one missions** along the coast to Sonoma. Anza opened overland routes and led 200+ colonists to San Francisco Bay. In less than a decade Spain occupied at least the coast.

California, more than any other border region, was a **mission frontier**. Serra’s aim: convert Indians and make them agriculturalists by **concentrating** Native populations in mission villages. For all their good intentions, Franciscans went a long way toward **destroying Native culture and the Indians themselves**. Indians brought by persuasion, force, or gifts entered European work routines (farming, ranching, adobe churches), monogamous marriage, and outward Catholicism. Mission fathers used force—often facilitated by Spanish soldiers—to make them work. Disease disrupted political authority limiting massive rebellion, but mission Indians fled to the hills in great numbers and staged minor rebellions.

### Mission mortality (load-bearing numbers)

Serra and successors baptized some **54,000** Indians “at a fearful price.” European diseases to which Indians were not immune, combined with **filthy sanitary conditions** in mission villages and an overwhelming sense of **psychological despair**, produced so many deaths that by the end of the mission period (**1832**) population had declined to **98,000** from an estimated **300,000**.

Indian labor linked Spanish California to colonial markets: grain, gardens, orchards, cattle; by **1800** British and American vessels traded hides (New England shoes) and tallow (Chile/Peru miners’ candles).

## Mexican secularization and peonage

After independence, large land grants and the **1834 Secularization Act** promised neophytes half of mission lands, but **rancheros acquired virtually all** mission lands—replacing church obligations with **peonage**. Indians who did not flee inland became ranch workers in rancherías. Labor scarcity led Mexican ranchers to **raid Central Valley villages** for laborers, provoking open warfare and Indian horse/cattle raids.

**John Augustus Sutter** (Fort Sutter/Sacramento, 1839; ~50,000-acre grant) used Indians for fur, wheat, weaving mills; maintained an Indian military force; held **power of life and death**; performed marriages; took young women as **concubines**; **executed** “disloyal” Indians—transition from Spanish assimilation theory to Mexican ruthless economic exploitation and **prelude to gold-rush American hostility**.

## Gold rush and genocide-scale decline (full fidelity)

California Indians still numbered **150,000 in 1845** despite missions and ranchos. The coming of **over 100,000 gold-seeking Americans between 1849 and 1851** created a major crisis. Commissioner Medill promoted small distinct reservations instead of a “Permanent Indian Frontier.” **1851** commissioners made **18 treaties** with “139 little tribes and bands” reserving **12,000 sq mi**; California senators defeated the treaties in **1852**; remaining decade efforts drowned in corruption and fraud.

### Demographic collapse

Through the combined effects of **disease, starvation, malnutrition, and simple homicide**, the Indian population plummeted from **150,000 in 1845 to 35,000 in 1860**. Decline continued into the twentieth century, dropping to about **10 percent** of original numbers at European contact.

### Militia violence and labor regimes

Gov. Peter Burnett (1850) authorized El Dorado County militia against Miwoks; Mariposa War (southern district) gained legislature approval; ~**20 militia campaigns** in the Civil War period alone often pushed Natives from subsistence bases into raiding, cycling violence. Beef contractors (including John C. Frémont) gouged ration prices; reservation Indians foraged to survive; mining-area Indians panned gold at exorbitant store prices; labor regimes (field/kitchen; Fresno wage work; sex-segregated workplaces) rarely integrated Indians into Anglo society. Anglo disdain so great that only since the **1960s** have scholars reconstructed much about leadership and survival; southern leaders (Cahuilla, Luiseño, Cupeño) negotiated persistence.

### Modoc War (1872–73)

**Kintpuash (Captain Jack)** and 60–70 Modoc families left an Oregon reservation for Lost River, northern California; Nov. 1872 Army attacked; Modocs held lava beds against ~**1,000** troops four months then killed peace commissioners including Gen. Edward R. S. Canby; leaders hanged; ~**155** followers sent to [[indian-territory|Indian Territory]]—standard removal.

## Policy failure judgment (Lamar & Truett)

First forty years of U.S. sovereignty (1848–88): attacks, mistreatment, and misunderstanding of numerous diverse, relatively peaceable peoples forced into raids/theft after the gold rush. Americans brought 250 years of frontier warfare assumptions—assimilate, remove, or exterminate. By 1850 removal was impossible, adequate reservations were not provided, and **extermination—while practiced by local militia or miners—was not acceptable** under national peace policy. Unlike single-minded military reduction in New Mexico (Carleton vs [[navajo-dine]]), California (and Arizona more than NM) suffered divided local/state/federal counsels—“a California Indian policy was the victim of too much Anglo-American democracy.”

By 1888 cultures persisted in “almost invisible pockets in California” as the southwestern Indian frontier closed (`part_of:` [[greater-southwest-california-colonial-1880s]] endpoint).

## Counterfactual significance

Ratified 1851 treaties and enforced reservations might have moderated—not prevented—the mid-century collapse, but mission-era disease and concentration had already cut the population by two-thirds before the gold rush. The gold-rush homicide/starvation phase is the sharpest demographic catastrophe in the CHNPA Greater Southwest narrative.

## Historiography

Bibliographic essay flags Cook’s population and conflict studies; Heizer *The Destruction of California Indians* (documents 1847–1865); Hurtado *Indian Survival on the California Frontier*; Rawls on changing image/policy; Phillips on resistance and central California intruders—framing gold-rush and reservation failures as documented destruction alongside persistence. *Handbook of North American Indians* vol. 8 (Heizer) is the California reference starting point. Mission “good intentions” rhetoric in older borderlands literature is explicitly undercut by Lamar & Truett’s “fearful price” and cultural destruction language—wiki preserves both the authors’ phrasing and the mortality numbers.
