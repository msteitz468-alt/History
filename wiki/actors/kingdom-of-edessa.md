## Overview

Edessa (Semitic name Orhai, modern Urfa) was founded as a military colony at the older city
of Orhai by Seleucus Nicator in 303/2 BCE and renamed for the Macedonian capital; it was
briefly called "Antioch by the Callirhoe" (163 BCE) and reverted to its Semitic name after
the Islamic conquest. David Ball (*Rome in the East*) argues (**THESIS**) that Edessa's
Hellenistic veneer — imparted by Seleucid colonization and reinforced by later kings such as
Abgar the Great — was "never more than skin deep": the city and its region remained
fundamentally Semitic throughout. This page covers the Abgarid ruling dynasty and its
political history; see [[edessa|Edessa]] for the place itself.

## The Abgarid Dynasty

With Seleucid decline, Edessa became the seat of an Arab dynasty, originally from Nisibis,
allied to the Parthians from c. 132 BCE — the **Abgarid dynasty**. Ball notes "Abgar" was
likely a dynastic title rather than a personal name, akin to "Caesar." The earliest kings
bear Armenian/Parthian-sounding names (Aryu, Fradhasht, Ezad), suggesting a possible
Armenian dynastic origin, but most later royal names are Nabataean (Ma'nu, Bakru, Gebar'u)
or Arabic (Abgar, Maz'ur, Wa'el, 'Amr); classical sources call the inhabitants "almost
invariably" Arabs. The kingdom was ruled through a council of tribal elders, like Arab
shaikhs, even after superficial Romanization.

### King Sequence (per Ball)

- **Abgar I Piqa** — killed by Romans in 69 BCE during Lucullus's war against Tigranes;
  the kingdom split from "Armenia proper."
- **Abgar II** (68–53 BCE) — preserved independence under Pompey; possibly identical with
  Plutarch's "Ariamnes," blamed by Roman sources for aiding Iran and contributing to
  Crassus's defeat at [[battle-of-carrhae-53bce|Carrhae]] (53 BCE) — Syriac sources claim
  the opposite, that Abgar backed Rome, a direct source conflict.
- **Abgar V Ukkama bar Ma'nu** (4 BCE–7 CE) — the "Christian correspondence" king (see
  below); allied with [[nabataean-kingdom|Harithath IV of Nabataea]] against Antipas
  (Herod's son).
- **Abgar VII** (109–116 CE) — backed Trajan's Mesopotamian campaign, then revolted; Rome
  destroyed Edessa and killed Abgar VII. Hadrian installed a puppet, Parthamaspat (117);
  the native dynasty was restored in 123 under Ma'nu VII (loyal to Rome, d. 139).
- During Lucius Verus's Parthian War, Parthians captured Edessa and installed a puppet,
  Wa'el bar Sharu (163); the legitimate king Ma'nu VIII fled to Rome; Avidius Cassius
  restored him in 165, at the cost of Osrhoene becoming a Roman client kingdom (166).
- **Abgar VIII "the Great"** (177–212) — backed Pescennius Niger against
  [[septimius-severus|Septimius Severus]], resulting in Osrhoene's reduction to Roman
  provincial status, leaving Abgar only the city itself. Nonetheless "considered the
  greatest of the kings of Edessa" — a cultured administrator and patron of learning who
  made Edessa a major intellectual center. Edessa was devastated by a flood in 201 (over
  2,000 dead, per the Chronicle of Edessa); Abgar rebuilt the city (a new Cathedral of St
  Sophia, the "Fish Pools of Abraham," a winter palace on higher ground). His state visit
  to Rome in 204 was the most lavish Rome had seen in 150 years. Reputed convert to
  Christianity c. 200 (see below).
- **Abgar IX "Severus"** — summoned to Rome with his son and murdered on Caracalla's
  orders (213); Caracalla made Edessa a Roman colony in 214, ending even token
  independence. Caracalla himself was later killed near Edessa/Carrhae (217), which Ball
  notes as "poetic justice."
- Later, nominal/restored kingship: Ma'nu IX (to 240); Abgar X, restored under Gordian III
  (238–244), expelled by [[philip-the-arab|Philip the Arab]] (248) after a revolt; briefly
  occupied by Sasanian [[sasanian-empire|Shapur I]] (259, after the
  [[capture-of-valerian-260|Battle of Edessa]]) then recaptured by Rome; possibly restored once
  more as a Sasanian vassal ('Amr the Abgarid, attested 293) — the last mention of the
  dynasty.

## Relations with Rome and Iran

Edessa functioned throughout its history as both meeting-place and buffer state between
Rome and the Parthian/Sasanian world, straddling the Mesopotamian plains and Anatolian
foothills. Its western boundary was presumably the Euphrates; its eastern boundary is
uncertain, with some traditions extending Edessene religious influence as far as the Church
of St Thaddeus in Iranian Azerbaijan. Edessa had strong ties to the Jewish client kingdom of
Adiabene, including a joint siege of Nisibis in 194 CE; Jewish elements were strong in both.

Ball places Edessa among Rome's small set of Near Eastern Arab client kingdoms (with Emesa,
Chalcis, and Nabataea), each described by contemporaries as "Arab," predating by centuries
the seventh-century emergence of Islamic Arab power. The Roman system of client kingdoms in
the region effectively ended with Caracalla's annexation of Edessa in 214; from the 4th–6th
centuries Rome instead relied on Arab tribal confederations (the [[tanukhids|Tanukhids]],
later the [[ghassanids|Ghassanids]]) as *foederati*, a system Ball compares to Britain's
North-West Frontier Tribal Territories.

## Religion: A Melting Pot

Edessa was a major religious crossroads: cults of Nebo/Nabu and Bel (also principal Palmyra
cults, of Mesopotamian origin, tied to Babylonian Marduk); the Sabian Moon/Sun cult of
Harran (a belief system positing an evil material world, spiritual intermediaries, and
creation renewed every 26,425 years, which survived into the Islamic era, its adherents
claiming "people of the book" status); the self-emasculation cult of Atargatis at
Mambij/Hierapolis, outlawed by Abgar the Great, whose practices Ball reads as anticipating
Christian stylitism (St Simeon Stylites' father was from Edessa); Nabataean Dushara;
Mesopotamian Ishtar/Sin; a pre-Christian virgin-mother-and-child cult common in northern
Syria; and a strong Jewish community with ties to Adiabene and Nisibis, including a Jewish
academy and the Jerusalem Temple treasury at Nisibis.

## The Abgar–Christ Legend and Edessan Christianity

King Abgar V is central to the *Doctrina Addai* (a 4th-century Edessan tradition, also
recorded by Eusebius): the legendary correspondence between Abgar V and Christ, with
originals allegedly preserved in the Edessan state archives into the 4th century and
translated into Greek by Eusebius. Abgar V reportedly also wrote to Emperor Tiberius,
"Narseh king of Assyria," and "Artashes of Persia" (probably Artaxias of Armenia) spreading
the Christian message; Tiberius purportedly replied after Senate deliberation. Per the
legend, after the Crucifixion the apostle Addai (St Thaddeus) was sent to Edessa by the
Apostle Thomas, stayed with Tobias the Jew, converted Abgar V via a miraculous cure, then
preached openly, converting many citizens; his mission extended into Adiabene, where an
Armenian monastery marks his martyrdom (66 CE).

**THESIS:** Ball treats the *Doctrina Addai*/Abgar-Christ correspondence as "extremely
doubtful," even "one of the most successful frauds of history," and notes the Syriac
tradition's Mar Addai may not be the same figure as the Greek tradition's St Thaddeus. Ball
argues the legend's veracity is beside the point — what matters is what it reveals about
Edessa's religious hybridity and its later importance as an early Christian center; the
*Doctrina Addai* was designed to assert Edessan Christianity's orthodoxy and primacy as
derived directly from Christ, implicitly rivaling Rome/St Peter's own primacy claims.

Two centuries later, under Abgar the Great, the philosopher-theologian Bardaisan (b. c. 154
in Mambij, educated by an Atargatis priest, converted to Christianity in Edessa by Bishop
Hystaspes, later expelled for nonconformity, founder of his own sect that survived to the
8th–9th century, d. 222, author of the *Book of the Laws of Countries*) reports Abgar the
Great's own conversion to Christianity. **THESIS:** Ball frames this conversion as
"controversial" but argues that whether or not literally true, Abgar recognized
Christianity's value for social stability a century before Constantine, using it to help
maintain Edessa's balance between Rome and Iran — making Abgar the Great arguably the first
Christian monarch and Edessa the first Christian state, "a major precedent... for the
conversion of Rome itself." Per Ball, the kingdom of Edessa under Abgar the Great became the
world's first state to proclaim Christianity its official religion during the Severan era,
a claim contested against Armenia's rival claim to the same distinction (Ball's account
possibly excepts Edessa as prior). This is Ball's interpretive framing, not settled
consensus; see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis|the Orientalization of Rome thesis]].

Whether or not the Abgar conversion stories are literally true, they established Edessa as
a major early Christian center: a continuous line of bishops from the early 4th century
lasting roughly 1,000 years, including St Ephraim (later 4th century); by the 5th century
Edessa was one of the main centers of Christian learning in the East.

## Counterfactual Significance

If Abgar the Great's conversion (or its later legendary elaboration) had not occurred,
Edessa's claim to primacy as an early center of Christian learning and orthodoxy — and the
propaganda value of the Abgar-Christ correspondence in later ecclesiastical disputes over
apostolic authority — would not have existed, potentially altering how Syriac Christianity
positioned itself relative to Rome and Constantinople.

## Fisher (2020): Founding, the "governor of ʿrb," and annexation

Fisher (*Rome, Persia, and Arabia*, 2020) confirms Edessa (also "Antioch Callirhoe") was
founded by Seleucus I in 303/2 BCE on an existing settlement with reliable water, well
placed on Silk Road routes near a major Euphrates crossing. It fell under the Abgarid
dynasty c. 132 BCE as Seleucid control lapsed, becoming under Parthian influence the
principal city of Osroëne, part of the "Parthian Near West" client scattering alongside
Adiabene, [[hatra|Hatra]], and [[characene|Characene]]. Old Syriac inscriptions from
Sumatar Harabesi refer to a "governor of ʿrb" ("Arab") holding office under royal
authority — named governors include Tiridates, installed by the Parthian king Vologases
IV — though Fisher stresses the term likely denoted a region, not an ethnonym, of unclear
extent (possibly reaching the Tigris).

During Crassus's Parthian campaign (54/3 BCE), later sources name the "Arab chieftain" who
lured Crassus to disaster (Plutarch's "Ariamnes") as Mazarus/Abgar II, king of Edessa —
likely the same figure as Cassius Dio's Abgar, whose Osroënean troops attacked Crassus's
rear at [[battle-of-carrhae-53bce|Carrhae]]. Fisher questions the "betrayal" framing:
Edessa was already a Parthian ally, and Crassus's own hubris and leadership failures
likely bore more responsibility — Arabs, he argues, made "better scapegoats" for Roman
authors. King Abgar VII (109–116 CE) pledged friendship to Trajan out of necessity but
later revolted with much of Mesopotamia; Edessa was sacked and burned by Roman forces in
116 CE. Vologases IV attacked Edessa again in 161 CE, deposing king Manu VIII; Avidius
Cassius recaptured the city in 165, reinstalling Manu VIII, who was styled on coinage
"King Mannus, friend of the Romans" (*philoromaios*).

Osroëne was annexed as a Roman province in 195 CE under Septimius Severus, though King
Abgar VIII retained his crown and was honored as "king of kings," styling himself "Lucius
Aelius Septimius Abgar" on coinage. The Edessan monarchy was finally dissolved by
Caracalla; a Greek epitaph from Rome refers to "the former king Abgar," perhaps Abgar IX
Severus. Edessa became a Roman colony, then passed to direct administration in 243 CE, and
remained Roman until the 7th century, when it capitulated twice — to the Persians, then
amid the Arab conquests, with a Roman restoration in between.

## Historiography

Ball's narrative draws on the Chronicle of Edessa, the *Doctrina Addai*, Eusebius, and
Bardaisan's *Book of the Laws of Countries* — sources of markedly uneven reliability,
particularly regarding the Abgar-Christ legend, which Ball treats with explicit skepticism
even while using it as evidence of Edessa's self-image. The conflicting Roman and Syriac
traditions on Abgar II's role at Carrhae (53 BCE) illustrate the broader difficulty of using
partisan client-kingdom sources for this period.
