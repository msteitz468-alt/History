## Overview

Solomon ibn Gabirol — known in the Latin philosophical tradition as Avicebron — was an Andalusian Jewish poet and philosopher of the eleventh century. Born in Málaga, he spent much of his short life in Saragossa. He is the earliest major Jewish philosopher in the Arabic-speaking world to build a systematic metaphysical system, and the figure through whom a distinctively "purer" Neoplatonism entered the Jewish tradition — purer, in Adamson's phrase, than what the Muslim thinkers al-Fārābī and Avicenna had transmitted ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

His philosophical prose work, the *Fons Vitae* (Fountain of Life / *Meqor Hayyim*), was composed in Arabic and survives only in a twelfth-century Latin translation. His Hebrew devotional poem *Keter Malkhut* (Kingly Crown) conveys the same metaphysical vision in liturgical form.

## Philosophical Position: Universal Hylomorphism

Ibn Gabirol's most distinctive and controversial claim is what modern scholars call **universal hylomorphism**: the doctrine that *everything* apart from God — including supposedly immaterial things like intellect and soul — is composed of matter and form. The argument rests on an analysis of predication: any description implies a subject–property duality; such duality is the structure of matter-and-form; since God is absolutely one, no such duality applies to Him, but it applies to all created things without exception (*Fountain of Life*, 13, 217, 334).

> "Everything apart from God Himself consists of a subject and its properties, which is to say, of matter and form." (Adamson, summarizing *Fons Vitae*)

This position made Ibn Gabirol notorious in the Latin tradition. Aquinas and other Christian scholastics used him as an opponent when arguing for the immateriality of angels, since universal hylomorphism implies that even angels have matter. Yet the position has roots in Plotinus, who spoke of the nascent intellect as a kind of matter that acquires form by turning towards the One.

### The Hierarchy of Being

Ibn Gabirol posits four levels below God, each consisting of different grades of matter receiving different grades of form:

1. Intellectual forms in spiritual matter (intellect)
2. Soul
3. The heavenly bodies
4. Terrestrial bodies (composed of the four elements)

As the levels proceed downward from God they become progressively less unified — "like water streaming forth from a fountain and getting more and more muddy" (*Fons Vitae*, 63).

### God and Divine Will

God for Ibn Gabirol is radically transcendent — not an intellect (contra [[al-farabi]] and [[ibn-sina]]) but above all thought. Positive attributes are ruled out because they imply a subject–property duality incompatible with absolute unity. Knowledge of God is primarily apophatic: a matter of denying inappropriate descriptions.

Between God and the intellectual realm Ibn Gabirol places **divine will** as an intermediary (*Fons Vitae*, 335) — an unusual move that distances him from strict emanationism while still using emanationist language ("God creates like sending forth a ray of light," *Keter Malkhut* §9).

The *Fons Vitae* avoids explicit Jewish reference — it is hard even to identify the author's religion from the text alone. But the *Keter Malkhut* makes explicit how Ibn Gabirol's Neoplatonism harmonizes with his Judaism, praising God's oneness (§§1, 8, 40) and ascending through the heavenly spheres to a realm of intellect before reaching God.

## Reception and Influence

- In the Latin West, "Avicebron" was widely read and refuted; his identity as a Jew was not known until the nineteenth century.
- Christian philosophers cited him as a useful opponent on the question of angelic immateriality.
- Later Jewish readers criticized the *Fons Vitae* for its lack of biblical content.
- In the Kabbalistic tradition, his Neoplatonic framework was absorbed into the teaching of the [[kabbalah]] — Moses of Burgos remarked that Kabbalists "planted their feet at the spot reached by the heads of the philosophers."
- [[judah-hallevi]] saw Ibn Gabirol's universalist rationalism as a potential threat within Judaism.

## Historiography

Ibn Gabirol was identified as Avicebron only in the nineteenth century (Salomon Munk, 1846), a discovery that reframed his place in both Jewish and scholastic intellectual history. The *Fons Vitae* is best studied in the Latin translation since the Arabic original is lost. Adamson situates him as introducing into Judaism the same Neoplatonic emanationism that al-Fārābī and Avicenna brought into Islam — though with a more radical transcendence of God.
