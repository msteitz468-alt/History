Baybars (r. 1260–77) was the sultan who established the [[mamluk-sultanate]] as the dominant Sunni Muslim power of the later thirteenth century, organized its military and administrative apparatus, checked the Mongol advance in Syria, and systematically eliminated the Crusader coastal presence in the Levant. His career represents the fullest early expression of the Mamluk form of the [[military-patronage-state]].

## Rise to Power

Baybars came to power by assassination, killing the sultan Quṭuz shortly after the [[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260]], the Mamluk victory over the Mongols that had established the sultanate's prestige. He was himself a Turkic Mamlûk from the Qïpchaq region north of the Black and Caspian seas — ethnically and culturally part of the world of the [[golden-horde]]. He took pains to recruit a large new supply of slaves from the same Qïpchaq region for his personal army, maintaining the Mamluk practice of each sultan building loyalty through his own corps of personally imported and trained soldier-slaves.

## Consolidation and Campaigns

Hodgson describes Baybars as organizing "the state with vigour as a military resource":

- **Military organization**: Established a rapid message service and defensive arrangements across the garrison network, admired for their efficiency.
- **Elimination of Ayyubid principalities in Syria**: Centralized the state that Saladin and his successors — loyal to family ties — had never unified.
- **Campaign against Crusader positions**: In a six-year campaign, eliminated most Crusader possessions along the coast. The remaining Christian ports were not all immediately demolished; fear of Occidental naval superiority led to channelling Syrian trade to a few strong ports while razing the rest.
- **Isma'ilis of Syria**: Subjected the Syrian Isma'ili communities (the "Assassins"), who had retained their independence when Hülegü's Mongols subdued the Iranian Isma'ilis.
- **Nubia and Red Sea**: Sent troops up the Nile and down Red Sea coasts to extend Mamluk influence.
- **Crusade against Mongols and their vassals**: Took the offensive against the [[ilkhanate]] and its Anatolian Seljuk vassals to the north.

## Legitimation Strategy

Baybars did not found a hereditary dynasty but established an oligarchic régime with a distinctive approach to legitimacy: he installed at Cairo a member of the Abbasid house who had escaped the Mongol destruction of Baghdad, as a shadow caliph. This puppet "caliph" — whom Baybars pretended to serve — gave his rule a nominal Sunni legitimacy that won occasional recognition from other Muslim states. The successor Abbasid line at Cairo was "fainéant" (powerless) but served its symbolic function.

This strategy reflects the Mamluk adaptation of the military-patronage-state model: unlike the Ilkhans, who could invoke Mongol dynastic legitimacy, the Mamluks — slave soldiers without inherited noble status — required other legitimating frameworks. The Abbasid shadow caliphate provided one; the reputation won at Ayn Jalut provided another.

## Significance

Baybars' reign set the institutional pattern for the Mamluk sultanate's subsequent century and a half. Under his model:
- Each sultan built his own personal corps of imported Mamlûk slaves
- Freeborn sons of Mamlûks were systematically excluded from the primary military bodies, ensuring each generation's soldiers were ethnically alien and personally loyal to their specific amîr
- Power was effectively held by an oligarchy of the great amîrs, from whose ranks each new sultan emerged (normally after the previous sultan's son was provisionally installed and then displaced by the strongest faction)

This system — admired for its military efficiency, criticized for its exclusion of civilian society from political life — characterized Mamluk governance until the Ottoman conquest of 1517.
