# The Chaldaeans

The **Chaldaeans (Kaldu)** were the **West Semitic tribal confederations of
southern Babylonia** whose long, stubborn resistance to [[neo-assyrian-empire|
Assyria]] runs through the eighth and seventh centuries and culminates in their
founding of the **[[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]]** under
[[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]] (626). [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part
2]] (Ch. 21, J. A. Brinkman) gives the definitive analysis of Babylonia "in the
shadow of Assyria."

## The tribal structure

Brinkman draws the crucial distinction: Babylonia's population split not by
town-vs-country but by **social organization — tribal vs. non-tribal**. Two great
tribal groups, both West Semitic, controlled the countryside:

- The **[[arameans|Aramaeans]]** — earlier arrivals (from c. 1100), **more than
  forty fragmented, largely pastoral tribes** (Gambulu, Puqudu, Ru'ua) settled
  around Nippur and the lower Tigris, resistant to assimilation; their lasting
  effect was the **Aramaization of the language** of Mesopotamia.
- The **Chaldaeans** — later (from c. 878) but **more sedentary and more unified**,
  organized in **five "Houses"** each under a single chief: the three major
  **Bit-Amukani**, **Bit-Dakkuri**, and the powerful **Bit-Yakin** (dominating Ur
  and the Gulf marshes, the "Sealand"), plus the minor Bit-Sha'alli and
  Bit-Shilani. They farmed date-palms, raised cattle, took Babylonian names, and —
  crucially — **controlled the Euphrates and the head of the Persian Gulf**, and so
  a large share of Near Eastern trade.

## The struggle for Babylonia (745–626)

This tribal geography shaped a century of war. The **weak Babylonian monarchy** (14
reigns averaging 3.2 years between 733 and 689) could not master the tribes or the
privileged cities, and Assyrian intervention from **745** began the long contest.
Brinkman's striking statistic: by official Assyrian count **almost half a million
people were deported from Babylonia (745–702), over half of them Chaldaeans** (see
[[assyrian-mass-deportation]]). Yet the Chaldaeans repeatedly regrouped. When an
exceptional leader arose — above all **[[merodach-baladan|Merodach-baladan II of
Bit-Yakin]]**, or later **Mushezib-Marduk** — the disparate tribes united with the
old cities and offered "surprisingly effective resistance to the militarily superior
Assyrians." Their disunity, not Assyrian strength, was usually what let Assyria win.

## From tribe to empire

The cities of Babylonia long preferred the **prosperity and privileged status
(*kidinnūtu*)** of Assyrian rule to the insecurity of tribal control — until the
devastation of the long wars and [[sennacherib|Sennacherib's]] destruction of
Babylon (689) turned opinion. In **626** the Chaldaean **[[nabopolassar|
Nabopolassar]]**, an Assyrian-appointed official in the Sealand, seized the
Babylonian throne and founded the dynasty that, with the **[[medes|Medes]]**,
destroyed Assyria and ruled the Near East until [[fall-of-babylon-539bce|539]]. The
Chaldaean name became so identified with Babylon that "Chaldaean" later meant
simply **astrologer/astronomer** in Greek and biblical usage.

## Liverani: a distinct people, probably of Arabian origin

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] insists the Chaldeans were **a separate
ethnic group from the [[arameans|Arameans]]** — never confused in Assyro-Babylonian sources:
they arrived later, settled a different zone (the lower Euphrates, Nippur to Ur and Uruk),
and assimilated to Babylonian culture far faster (taking Babylonian names and titles). They
were only **five tribes** (Bit Yakini, Bit Dakkuri, Bit Ammukani, Bit Sha'alli, Bit Shilani)
but far more powerful than the numerous small Aramean tribes, their leaders claiming "king of
Chaldea" or "king of the Sealand" and leading Babylonian resistance to Assyria. Distinctively,
Liverani reads their economy as based on **long-distance Arabian/Indian-Ocean trade** rather
than agriculture (their tributes to Assyria were exotic goods — gold, incense, ivory, ebony,
rosewood — partly inheriting Ur's old southern networks, partly riding the new Iron Age camel
caravans), and on that basis he proposes a homeland in the **far south of the Arabian
Peninsula** rather than among the Syrian Aramean tribes — which would explain both their
distinct settlement zone and the Assyrian insistence on their separateness. See
[[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]].

## Significance

- `produced:` the [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]] — the last
  native Mesopotamian great power.
- `opposed_by:`/`contributed_to:` the **fall of Assyria**, by absorbing a century
  of Assyrian military effort that Brinkman calls the empire's most damaging drain.
- The Aramaean component `produced:` the linguistic Aramaization that outlived
  Babylon itself.

## Historiography

Known overwhelmingly from the **Assyrian side** (royal annals, the Babylonian
Chronicle, administrative archives) with the urban bias of the surviving documents;
Brinkman's reconstruction is a model of correcting that bias against settlement
survey and tribal geography. The line between "Chaldaean" and "Babylonian" blurs
across the period as the tribes Babylonized.
