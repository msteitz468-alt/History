## Overview

Zheng Chenggong, universally known by his honorific Koxinga (a Chinese rendering of the court title *Guoxingye*, "Lord of the Imperial Surname"), was the paramount figure of seventeenth-century Chinese maritime East Asia: Ming loyalist warlord, maritime commercial overlord, and conqueror of Dutch Taiwan. Born 1624 in Hirado, Japan — "half-Chinese half-Japanese" of a Fujianese father (Zheng Zhilong, the sealord "Nicholas Iquan") and a Japanese mother (Lady Tagawa Matsu) — he died in 1662 in Taiwan, shortly after his greatest military victory, having never seen either homeland again.

Ho (Ch29 of [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]) writes that Koxinga was "a shrewd, charismatic, and autocratic man of action, Koxinga was every bit his father's son."

## Role and Significance

Koxinga's significance operates at multiple levels:

**Military:** He assembled forces "from a few hundred to more than 100,000 soldiers at his peak," launching a massive assault on Nanjing in 1658–9 before being repulsed, and subsequently besieging and capturing the VOC stronghold Castle Zeelandia in Taiwan (1661–62) after a grueling nine-month operation.

**Commercial:** He inherited and greatly expanded his father's maritime commercial network, the most important Chinese private trade organization of the century. Through the *gongsi* (Public Firm) and a pass-permit system, he "controlled some 70–80 per cent of all Chinese shipping in maritime East Asia" and mounted a Chinese embargo on Malacca, Batavia, and Manila (1656–7) "with no repercussions to his trade domination."

**Ideological:** He was the principal propagandist of Ming loyalism overseas, distributing Yongli reign-name calendars across the diaspora to maintain a common Chinese identity outside China — enabling, in Anthony Reid's words, "the legitimation, for the first time, of a Chinese identity outside China."

**Institutional:** His 1653 establishment of "his own central bureaucracy, consisting of offices that were equivalents of the Ming Six Boards" and the innovation of the *gongsi* (joint-stock commercial firm) prefigured the institutions that drove the "Chinese century" of the eighteenth century.

## Key Events

**1624:** Born in Hirado to Zheng Zhilong and Lady Tagawa Matsu.

**1646:** Father Zheng Zhilong capitulates to the Manchus and is taken to Beijing. Mother commits suicide when Qing armies invade southern Fujian. "The traumas of 1646 hardened Koxinga's crusading mentality as he wrested control of his father's assets and warfleet from rival kinsmen." At 22, he embarked on his Ming loyalist career.

**1647–1654:** Years of brutal warfare in Fujian; Qing invasion, civil war, cannibalism. Koxinga "literally whipped his men into shape at sea."

**1653:** Established his own bureaucratic state equivalent, including the Revenue Office and *gongsi* system.

**1657:** Launched a flotilla of "some 5,000 ships and 60,000 men" to probe northern coastal defenses.

**1658–9:** Koxinga's armada struck for Nanjing in "the campaigns of 1658–9." The siege failed due to "fatal miscalculations"; Koxinga retreated to Amoy in September 1659.

**1661 — Invasion of Taiwan:** The Qing's *qianjie ling* (Coastal Depopulation Law) of 1661 devastated his supply base; Koxinga decided on Taiwan, which his father had helped develop into "one of the most beautiful pearls in the crown" of the Dutch empire. He attacked on 30 April 1661 with over 20,000 men, declaring: "I have come to reclaim my land, which my father loaned to the company."

**1661–1662 — Siege of Castle Zeelandia:** Koxinga's forces easily defeated a Dutch musketeer company on the beaches but were stymied by the star-shaped European fortification of Castle Zeelandia. A Dutch relief fleet under Jacob Cauw was blown away by typhoon. The decisive factor was the defection of Hans Radis, a German mercenary who advised building a European-style demi-lune cannon battery. Frederick Coyett surrendered 1 February 1662.

**1662:** Koxinga died "a mad death in 1662 in apoplexy, from a malarial fever or perhaps syphilis (or, say some Chinese accounts, 'overwork'). He had threatened to invade the Spanish Philippines; he had conquered Taiwan but would never see his homelands of Japan or Fujian again. He died staring out to sea."

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Koxinga, Dutch Taiwan would have remained the largest VOC colony in East Asia through the later seventeenth century, and the Dutch rather than the English would likely have been best positioned to exploit the Qing reopening of China trade in 1684. Koxinga's expulsion of the Dutch created the conditions for the English East India Company — which traded with his son Zheng Jing on Taiwan — to be "uniquely positioned to exploit this opportunity." The English trade with Zheng Jing also gave them "their first taste of tea," which by 1760 constituted 40 per cent of Company imports. Koxinga thus inadvertently shaped the English commercial and eventually imperial trajectory in East Asia.

## Historiography

The source base splits between VOC records (Dutch administrative archives, Frederick Coyett's account), Chinese chronicles (Southern Ming loyalist materials, Qing records), and Japanese sources on the Zheng family's Hirado connections. Ho notes that "once, 'every Dutch schoolchild learned that in 1662 the cruel pirate Coxinga robbed his ancestors of their beautiful colony, Formosa.'" This framing has been thoroughly revised: Tonio Andrade's *Lost Colony: The Untold Story of China's First Great Victory over the West* (2011) provides the most comprehensive modern account emphasizing the military and cultural dimensions; Xing Hang's *Conflict and Commerce in Maritime East Asia: The Zheng Family and the Shaping of the Modern World, c.1620–1720* (Cambridge, 2015) provides the commercial-political analysis. Koxinga is celebrated in Taiwan (where he is credited with founding Chinese civilization on the island), venerated in Fujian, and remains contested in Chinese nationalist historiography (where his Ming loyalism complicates PRC claims).
