# The Kingdom of Kush (Napata and Meroe)

## Overview

The **Kingdom of Kush** was, in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Burstein, Ch 23), the great
indigenous power of the **middle Nile** (modern Sudan) — a Nubian state that twice rose to
rule its world: first as the **"Black Pharaohs"** who governed all Egypt, then as the
long-lived African kingdom of **Meroe**. It is the best-documented sub-Saharan polity of
antiquity and a corrective to the myth that nothing of consequence happened in Africa beyond
Egypt.

## Napata and the Black Pharaohs

After the collapse of Egypt's New Kingdom (1069 BCE) freed the central Sudan, the royal
cemetery at **el-Kurru** (near the fourth cataract) documents a regional chieftain becoming,
by the late eighth century BCE, **a king ruling the whole Upper Nile Valley in Egyptian
style** — burials shifting from Nubian tumuli to **Egyptian-style pyramids**. Allied with the
Amun priesthood of **Thebes** against Egypt's Libyan kings, the Kushite king **Shabako** was
crowned at Memphis as the first ruler of Egypt's **twenty-fifth dynasty** (c. 728 BCE),
launching a half-century of cultural and religious revival. Overreach into Syria-Palestine
brought collision with **Assyria**, which sacked Memphis and Thebes (671/667 BCE) and drove
the Kushites back into Nubia by 664 BCE — leaving Kush an **inner-African kingdom** cut off
from the Mediterranean.

## The Meroitic Kingdom

Under **Persian** and then **Ptolemaic** Egypt, Kush endured as a tributary-but-independent
state. After Alexander's conquest the kingdom's center moved south to **Meroe** (below the
fifth cataract), and a new elite replaced the old Egyptianized class: **Meroitic** replaced
Egyptian as the language of government (in a new quasi-alphabetic script), the local war god
**Apedemak** rose in the pantheon, and the ancient practice of **royal retainer burial**
returned. Meroe's economy rested on Nile agriculture and the products of the African interior —
ivory, ebony, hides, gold, and slaves. Royal succession ran through a privileged class of
**"king's sisters,"** and the kingdom is famous for its field of steep **pyramids**.

## Decline and Fall

From the second–third centuries CE the rise of **[[aksum|Aksum]]** as Rome's preferred
supplier of African goods (via the Red Sea and **[[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]]**)
diverted trade away from the Nile corridor, impoverishing the Kushite monarchy and weakening
its grip on the periphery. By late antiquity Kush had **disappeared**, conquered by Aksum (c.
350 CE) and giving way to warring successor states — later the Christian Nubian kingdoms (e.g.
**Makuria**, whose **baqt** treaty with the Arabs preserved its independence after 652 CE).

## Significance

Kush demonstrates the **two-way** cultural exchange of the Nile world: a sub-Saharan kingdom
that ruled Egypt, sustained a literate civilization in its own language for centuries, and
linked inner Africa to the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean economies. Its fall marks the
**reorientation** of Northeast African trade from the Nile to the Red Sea.

## Historiography

Kush is uniquely knowable through **archaeology** (el-Kurru, Napata, Meroe) and external
texts (Egyptian, Assyrian, Greek — Herodotus on Meroe), though **Meroitic remains only
partly deciphered**. CWH IV notes that Northeast Africa has three separate scholarly traditions
(Egypt, Nubia, Aksum), which has fragmented the study of an interconnected region.
