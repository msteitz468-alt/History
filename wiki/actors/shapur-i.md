# Shapur I

Son and successor of **[[ardashir-i|Ardashir I]]** (r. c. 240–272) and the conqueror who carried the
**[[sassanid-empire|Sassanid Empire]]** to its third-century zenith — the Persian king who defeated
three Roman emperors and achieved the **unexampled [[capture-of-valerian-260|capture of the emperor
Valerian]]**. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 14 (**Richard N. Frye**).

## The three Roman wars

Shapur's own trilingual **Res Gestae Divi Saporis** (the *ŠKZ* at Naqsh-i-Rustam) and the Roman sources
record three great campaigns:

1. **Against Gordian III (c. 243–244)**: Gordian's invasion of Mesopotamia (guided by the prefect
   Timesitheus) recovered Carrhae, Nisibis and Singara, but after Timesitheus' death Gordian was killed
   at **Misiche** (244, renamed *Perozshapur*, "Shapur's victory"). His successor **Philip the Arabian**
   bought peace for **500,000 denarii**.
2. **The Syrian war (from c. 252)**: justified (says the *ŠKZ*) by Roman interference in
   **[[armenia|Armenia]]**, Shapur destroyed a **60,000-man Roman army at Barbalissus**, then split his
   forces to raid across Syria, Cappadocia and Cilicia. **Antioch**, the Roman capital of the East, was
   sacked (in 253 and/or 256); **Dura Europus** fell in 256.
3. **The capture of Valerian (260)**: besieging Edessa and Carrhae, Shapur met the emperor **Valerian**
   (with 70,000 men) in battle near Edessa and **took him prisoner** — together with much of his army,
   resettled in Sassanian Khuzistan (Gundeshapur). The event was commemorated in **five rock reliefs** in
   Fars showing the emperor kneeling or led captive.

Crucially, Frye argues Shapur sought **booty and prisoners, not permanent annexation** — which is why his
gains evaporated, rolled back not by Rome but by **[[zenobia|Odenathus]] of [[palmyra|Palmyra]]**, who
recovered Carrhae and Nisibis. Shapur's later years were relatively peaceful.

## Patron of Mani

Religiously tolerant (in contrast to his successors the Vahrans), Shapur **favoured [[manichaeism|Mani]]**,
the Mesopotamian-born founder of the universalizing dual-faith Manichaeism, allowing him to send
missionaries throughout the empire — a tolerance reversed after Shapur's death, when Mani was martyred
(c. 276). Frye sees the rivalry of Manichaeism as a catalyst driving Zoroastrianism, Christianity and
Judaism alike toward organization and the codification of scripture.

## Significance

Shapur is the high-water mark of Sassanian power against Rome and the most vivid single symbol of the
[[third-century-crisis|third-century crisis]]: the capture of a living Roman emperor was a humiliation
without precedent. Yet his strategy of raid-and-withdraw — rather than systematic conquest — left the
frontier ultimately intact and allowed both Palmyra and a recovering Rome to claw back the East,
illustrating Frye's point that the Sassanians aimed at **prestige, plunder and the Iranian sphere**, not
the dismemberment of the Roman state.
