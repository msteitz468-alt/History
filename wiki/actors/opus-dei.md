# Opus Dei

Catholic lay institute founded in Madrid in 1928 by Josemaría Escrivá de Balaguer, emphasizing sanctification through professional work and the formation of elites. Its historical importance for this wiki lies in [[francoist-spain|Francoist Spain]]: from the government change of February 1957, ministers and senior officials associated with Opus Dei (Ullastres, Navarro Rubio, later López Rodó and others) — the "technocrats," sponsored by [[carrero-blanco-luis|Carrero Blanco]] — displaced [[falange|Falangist]] economic nationalism, engineered the 1959 Stabilization Plan, and directed the Development Plans of the [[spanish-economic-miracle|desarrollo]] years.

## Significance

Payne (1987) treats the technocratic turn as the regime's decisive reinvention: replacing autarky and Movimiento ideology with growth-based performance legitimacy, while keeping the authoritarian political structure intact. Falangist resentment of the "Opus" ministers was the chief intra-regime family feud of the 1960s (climaxing in the 1969 MATESA scandal).

## Detail (Payne 1987)

Recognized by the Church as its first secular institute (1947; personal prelature 1982, ~72,000 members worldwide); by the 1950s members reputedly held up to 20% of Spanish university chairs (probably exaggerated) and founded the University of Navarre (1952). Two distinct political faces: the *Arbor* circle of Calvo Serer and Pérez Embid, pushing authoritarian Catholic-monarchist succession (Calvo Serer fired as editor in 1953); and the economist-technocrats — Navarro Rubio, Ullastres, López Rodó, later López Bravo — who, with Carrero Blanco, delivered the 1959 Stabilization Plan and the Development Plans (Raymond Carr on López Rodó: a "symbiosis between Catholic values, an authoritarian political system, and the American way of life"). Falangist hostility was constant (Escrivá himself protested "the campaign against Opus Dei carried on so unjustly by the press of the Falange," 1966) and climaxed in the MATESA scandal (1969), which Movement leaders used against the Opus ministers — Franco answered with the "monocolor" cabinet of October 1969, the peak of Opus-associated dominance. The Arias Navarro cabinet (January 1974) dropped Opus members entirely, "putting a sudden end to years of hysteria about the supposed Opus domination."
