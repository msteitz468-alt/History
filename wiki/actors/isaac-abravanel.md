## Overview

Isaac Abravanel was a Portuguese-Spanish Jewish philosopher, biblical commentator, and royal official — the last major figure of medieval Jewish philosophy in the Andalusian tradition. Born in Lisbon in 1437 into a family of Spanish origin, he served Ferdinand and Isabella as a tax official before being forced into exile by the 1492 expulsion of Jews from Spain. He eventually settled in Venice, where he died in 1508 ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

His son Judah Abravanel (Leo Hebraeus) became a philosopher in the Italian Renaissance tradition, marking the cultural transition from the Andalusian medieval to the early modern world.

## The 1492 Expulsion

Ferdinand and Isabella's edict of 1492 — the same year they dispatched Columbus — required the remaining Jews and Muslims of Spain to convert, leave, or die. Abravanel chose exile over conversion despite his close ties to the court. The expulsion brought the long tradition of Andalusian *convivencia* — the fraught but creative coexistence of three faiths — to a violent close.

## Philosophical Position

### *Principles of the Faith*

Abravanel's contribution to the debate over the foundations of Judaism — running from [[maimonides]]' thirteen principles through [[hasdai-crescas]]' three roots and [[joseph-albo]]'s three *ʿiqqarim* — goes in a paradoxical direction: he reduces the foundational principles to zero.

He starts by narrowing more aggressively than Albo — his initial candidate for the single necessary principle is belief in creation of the world from nothing. Then he abandons even this, leaving no foundational principles at all. This is not permissiveness about belief. It is the opposite position: all the truths in the Torah should be accepted in their entirety, with nothing ranked as more or less fundamental. The Law is not structured like an Aristotelian demonstrative science; it comes from God, it is all true, and its truths stand on equal footing (*Principles of the Faith*).

> "Our attitude towards the Law should ideally be that of 'faith' (*emunah*), which here means total and unshakable certainty." — Adamson, summarizing Abravanel

### Defending Maimonides Paradoxically

Despite disagreeing sharply with Maimonides' principle-project, Abravanel presents himself as defending Maimonides against Crescas and Albo. His reading: Maimonides offered thirteen principles for *pedagogical* purposes — as a teaching strategy for those who need basic guidance — not as an analysis of the Torah's deep structure. One should not confuse a useful starting-point for learners with a claim about the Law's foundational architecture.

### On Belief as Responsive to Commands

Abravanel sides with Maimonides against Crescas on whether God can command us to believe. Crescas had argued this was incoherent (one cannot simply choose to believe something). Abravanel responds: one can take steps that lead to belief. We are under an obligation to believe in God, and can be rewarded or punished accordingly. He compares this to Pascal's later "wager" argument — one cannot flip a mental switch, but one can restructure one's life to create the conditions for genuine belief.

## Place in the Tradition

Adamson reads Abravanel as marking the genuine end of medieval Jewish philosophy in the Andalusian tradition. The cultural setting — Andalusian *convivencia*, the three faiths in creative tension — had been the condition of possibility for the whole tradition from [[ibn-gabirol]] and [[judah-hallevi]] through [[maimonides]], [[gersonides]], and [[hasdai-crescas]]. With 1492, that setting was destroyed. Abravanel's own life trajectory embodies the rupture.

His son's work belongs to Renaissance Italy, not medieval Spain.
