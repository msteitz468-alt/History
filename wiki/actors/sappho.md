# Sappho

Archaic Greek lyric poet of Mytilene on Lesbos (b. c. 620 BCE, d. c. 570 BCE), the most celebrated of the Greek lyricists and one of the earliest sustained *personal* voices in Western literature. She wrote in the Aeolic dialect for solo and choral performance to the lyre.

## Role and Significance

Sappho belongs to the seventh-/sixth-century flowering of Greek **lyric poetry** — a genre that, in Charles Freeman's framing, marks "the birth of the individual voice in western literature." Where epic (Homer) is set in the heroic past and is a "social act," the lyric is "a personal testimony... the interior of epic" (Freeman, quoting Peter Conrad): poetry of the present, of the poet's own feelings. Sappho is its supreme exponent of sensuality, longing, and loss.

Born into an aristocratic Mytilenean family — a contemporary of the political poet Alcaeus of the same city — she was at one point driven into exile to Sicily. On her return she appears to have become mentor to a circle of young women dedicated to the worship of Aphrodite, in a transitional phase of their lives before marriage. The intense, probably erotic, bonds within this group are the subject of much of her verse; a later legend (unreliable) had her leap to her death from a cliff over an unrequited love for a man, and another tradition gives her a husband and daughter.

Only **one complete poem survives** — a hymn to Aphrodite in which the poet, "in terms of easy intimacy," calls on the goddess to soothe her love for another woman — plus a wealth of fragments (including the famous "Peer of immortal gods he seems to me..." describing the physical symptoms of desire). She "comes across as a person of intense and unguarded emotions"; her vulnerability is central to her appeal.

## Counterfactual / Analytical Significance

Sappho matters historically as the **rare, unrestrained voice of a woman** in the Archaic period, a corrective to the near-total male dominance of the surviving record. Freeman uses her reception as a case study in historiographical bias: nineteenth-century scholars, committed to an image of the Greeks as both culturally and *sexually* "pure," strained to deny that she may have been a practising lesbian (one primly insisting "It is clear that Sappho was a respectable person in Lesbos"), reading or ignoring the evidence to fit their preconceptions — an instance of "the past defined by the present." The word *lesbian* itself derives from her island home.

## Historiography

Sappho survives almost entirely in fragments (papyrus finds, and quotation by later grammarians and metricians who prized her Aeolic forms); new fragments still emerge from papyrology, occasionally amid authenticity controversy. The biographical tradition is thin and heavily legendary, and must be separated from the poems themselves. Her reception history — from ancient canonization among the "nine lyric poets" through Victorian sanitization to modern feminist and queer readings — is itself a much-studied subject.
