## Overview

The Confederate Catholics of Ireland — headquartered at Kilkenny and popularly known as the Confederation of Kilkenny — was the alternative government established by Irish Catholic leaders in May–October 1642 in response to the failure of the [[irish-rebellion-1641]] to produce a rapid negotiated settlement with the Crown. It was the last major exercise of Catholic political authority in Ireland before [[protestant-ascendancy]] was consolidated after 1691.

## Formation

As early as December 1641, Old English lords had concluded an uneasy alliance with Ulster insurgents. In May 1642, the Catholic clergy and laity met at Kilkenny. The General Assembly's first meeting on 24 October 1642 constituted a supreme council of twenty-four members (six representatives from each province), twelve of whom sat permanently. Structurally, the General Assembly was unicameral (unlike the bicameral parliament it partly imitated), and the executive (Supreme Council) was subordinate to the legislature.

An "oath of association" bound members to full Catholic toleration and restoration of clergy to pre-Reformation properties and privileges. The Confederate motto — *Hiberni unanimes pro Deo, Rege, et Patria* ("Irishmen united for God, King, and Country") — expressed the attempted synthesis of Catholicism, Stuart loyalty, and Irish patriotism.

## Governing Structure

The architecture closely resembled pre-1641 structures, giving the Confederation an air of legitimate constitutionalism rather than revolutionary rupture. Provincial assemblies and armies reported to the Supreme Council; the General Assembly met periodically to ratify major decisions. Four provincial armies operated with varying degrees of coordination: the Ulster army under Owen Roe O'Neill proved the most effective; the Munster army under Garret Barry and later Richard Butler, the least.

## Internal Politics

Three loose groupings "played havoc with Kilkenny politics" (Jane Ohlmeyer in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]]):

- **War faction**: Led by Papal Nuncio Rinuccini and leading clerics; refused to compromise the demands for full Catholic restoration of church property and free worship in return for any deal with the Stuarts.
- **Peace faction**: Supporters of Ormond's terms; willing to accept guarantees of religious tolerance without full restoration; dominated by Old English lawyers and lords.
- **Neutral/moderate faction**: Sought a middle course, often acting as brokers.

These divisions reflected genuinely incompatible strategic choices: either commit fully to supporting Charles I against Parliament in the hope of a royalist victory that would deliver Catholic concessions, or pursue military independence with Continental support to make Catholic Ireland impregnable. Unable to choose decisively between these options, the Confederation "failed to achieve their principal objective and thus safeguard their own survival."

## Key Events

- **Battle of Benburb (5 June 1646)**: Owen Roe O'Neill, commanding 5,000 Confederate troops, routed the Scottish covenanting army under George Monro — the most complete Irish military victory of the seventeenth century. The Confederates failed to exploit this victory strategically.
- **First Ormond Peace (1646)**: Rejected by Rinuccini, who excommunicated those who supported it; the Supreme Council appealed to Rome against the excommunication.
- **Inchiquin Truce (May 1648)**: Rinuccini excommunicated those who accepted truce with Protestant commander Inchiquin; brief internecine Catholic warfare followed.
- **Second Ormond Peace (January 1649)**: Concluded despite Rinuccini's continued opposition; dissolved the Confederate Association and created a pan-archipelagic royalist coalition.
- **Battle of Rathmines (2 August 1649)**: Parliamentary forces under Michael Jones routed Ormond's army, enabling Cromwell's landing and ending the royalist coalition's military viability.

## Significance

The Confederation demonstrated the capacity of Irish Catholic political elites to organise a functioning alternative government, maintain armies, negotiate with European powers, and sustain a distinct constitutional position (Ireland as a kingdom in personal union with the Stuart crown, not a conquered province). Its failure was not primarily military but political: the irreconcilable demands of clerical maximalists (Rinuccini), military realists (Owen Roe O'Neill), and political moderates (Old English lords) prevented the decisive action that could have made Confederate military advantage durable.

The Confederation's constitutional claims — legislative independence, Catholic freedom of worship, security of land tenure — became the template for later Catholic political demands that would resurface, in very different circumstances, in the campaigns for Catholic Emancipation and Home Rule.

The Kilkenny archives and the 1641 Depositions together constitute the primary source base for this period. Micheál Ó Siochrú's *Confederate Ireland 1642–1649* (1999) is the standard constitutional and political analysis.
