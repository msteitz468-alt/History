# Boris Yeltsin
## Biography / Description
Boris Nikolaevich Yeltsin was born in 1931 in Butka, Sverdlovsk region, to a poor peasant family. He trained as an engineer and became a long-time party administrator, serving as first secretary of the Sverdlovsk region. In 1985 Gorbachev brought him to Moscow to head the Moscow Party organization. Yeltsin declared war on corruption and indolence, sacking hundreds of Grishin's placemen. He travelled by bus rather than limousine and harassed the metropolitan bureaucracy, winning acclaim from ordinary residents. His outspoken criticism of the slow pace of perestroika and of privileges (including those of the Gorbachevs) led to his dramatic fall. In October 1987 he resigned as candidate member of the Politburo after threatening to leave; Ligachëv ensured he could not withdraw the resignation. Pumped full of drugs while ill in hospital, he was dragged to a Moscow City Party conference, denounced for arrogance, and sacked as party secretary of the capital. Gorbachev appointed him Deputy Chairman of the State Construction Committee. Both assumed his central career had ended (lines 3109, 3182–3186).

## Key Decisions and Achievements (from range)
- **Moscow First Secretary (1985–1987):** Brought to capital by Gorbachev; purged Grishin's placemen as opponents of perestroika; harassed bureaucracy to acclaim of Muscovites. Pushed for faster pace of reform and broader glasnost than Gorbachev initially tolerated (lines 3109, 3182).
- **Fall from Grace (October 1987):** Criticized privileges of Mikhail and Raisa Gorbachev; lacked tactical finesse and tact. Forced resignation from candidate Politburo; humiliating conference appearance while ill led to sacking as Moscow secretary. Appointed to lesser post but effectively sidelined (lines 3184–3186).
- **Rehabilitation Path:** At the June 1988 Nineteenth Party Conference Yeltsin returned from oblivion, waving his party card from the foot of the platform. Gorbachev gestured him to a front-row seat. Yeltsin endorsed practically all Gorbachev's proposals and humbly asked for rehabilitation. Ligachëv led the counter-attack ("You, Boris, are not right!"); the Conference refused re-admission to supreme leadership (lines 3234–3236).

Yeltsin's later career (August Coup resistance, dissolution, presidency of Russian Federation) lies outside this range.

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Defeat of the August Coup (1991):** Yeltsin led the popular resistance against the hardline communist coup, famously rallying crowds from atop a tank outside the Russian parliament building ("the White House"), which catapulted him to supreme political prominence. On hearing of the coup he prepared a proclamation and "clambered on to one of the tanks of the Taman Division"; "Tall and bulky, he strode out... and announced his defiance" (lines 3474–3476). The State Committee blundered by failing to arrest him.
- **Dissolution of the USSR (1991):** In December 1991, Yeltsin met with the leaders of Ukraine (Kravchuk) and Belarus (Shushkevich) in Belovezhskaya Pushcha on 8 December to form the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS), "an even weaker combination" than the Novo-Ogarëvo draft. Ukraine's 1 December independence referendum made Union impossible (lines 3508–3510). This forced Gorbachev's resignation on 25 December ("I leave my post with trepidation...", line 3514). "Perhaps the Ukrainian referendum was the pretext that Yeltsin had been waiting for to break up the USSR" (line 3512).
- **Economic Shock Therapy (1992):** Under advisors like Yegor Gaidar and Anatoly Chubais, Yeltsin launched rapid market reforms, including price liberalization on 2 January 1992 ("prices rose by 245 per cent in January"), voucher privatization, and the lifting of trade barriers. "Chubais introduced a system of 'vouchers'... most citizens sold their vouchers cheap" allowing managers and speculators to dominate (lines 3539, 3565). "The methods of communism were used by Yeltsin to eradicate traces of the communist epoch" (line 3559).
- **1993 Constitutional Crisis:** Yeltsin clashed with the Russian parliament (the Supreme Soviet led by Khasbulatov and Rutskoy) over presidential authority. On 21 September 1993 (Decree No. 1400) he dissolved parliament; when they barricaded the White House and on 3 October launched attacks on Ostankino, Yeltsin ordered the army to shell the building on 4 October. "A gaping hole was blasted"; over a hundred killed. The rebels surrendered (lines 3619–3623). He then pushed further market reforms and a new Constitution giving "virtually unrestricted authority to appoint his prime minister, to prorogue parliament and rule by decree" (line 3637).
- **First Chechen War (1994–1996):** To prevent Chechnya's secession, Yeltsin ordered a military invasion. The war proved to be a disaster, exposing the appalling condition of the Russian army and resulting in massive civilian casualties (such as the Samashki massacre) before a peace pact was signed by Aleksandr Lebed in 1996. Grachëv had promised quick victory; "the Russian army proved to be in an appalling condition" (from range synthesis).
- **1996 Presidential Election:** Despite an approval rating of single digits, widespread economic misery, and poor health, Yeltsin won re-election against communist challenger Gennady Zyuganov, heavily aided by media control and financial backing from the oligarchs (secret deal with Berezovsky et al. for loans-for-shares stakes).
- **1998 Financial Crisis:** Following years of borrowing and economic stagnation, the Russian government defaulted on its debt and devalued the ruble, wiping out savings and leading to the collapse of Yeltsin's economic programs. "Russia unilaterally defaulted on its international loan repayments" (line 3684). Recovery began after devaluation stimulated domestic production.

## Historical Significance (from assigned range 2401–3300)
In the period covered by this range, Yeltsin appears as Gorbachev's radical protégé promoted to Moscow City First Secretary in 1985. He purged corrupt officials, harassed the bureaucracy, and won acclaim from ordinary Muscovites by travelling by bus and attacking privileges. His impatience for faster perestroika and glasnost, and his criticism of the Gorbachevs' privileges, led to his isolation. In October 1987 he was forced to resign as candidate Politburo member; while ill he was pumped with drugs and dragged to a conference where he was denounced for arrogance and sacked as Moscow secretary. Gorbachev showed mercy by a lesser appointment, but both assumed his central career had ended. At the 1988 Party Conference he attempted a humble return, endorsing Gorbachev's proposals, but Ligachëv's counter-attack prevailed and rehabilitation was refused (lines 3109, 3182–3186, 3234–3236).

Yeltsin's later role (August Coup, dissolution) lies outside this range. Within it, his fall illustrated the limits of reform inside the party and the personal risks of pushing too hard against the nomenklatura.

## Counterfactual Significance (from range)
Had Yeltsin not been promoted or had he been retained in high office, the pace of Moscow reform might have been faster or conflict with Ligachëv sharper earlier. His removal in 1987 temporarily isolated Gorbachev as the "solitary fore-rider" of change. The glasnost he championed as Moscow boss helped create the public space that later enabled his independent political career.

## Historiography
Service presents Yeltsin's 1985–87 Moscow tenure as an energetic drive that won popular support but exhausted Gorbachev's patience. The 1987 episode (hospital drugs, conference humiliation) is one of Gorbachev's most disgraceful personal actions. Yeltsin's Conference return showed resilience and public appetite for a bolder figure. The range emphasizes his intuitive politics and lack of coherent programme at this stage (lines 3184, 3234).
