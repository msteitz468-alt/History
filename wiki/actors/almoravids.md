# The Almoravids (al-Murabitun)

> **African-context note (GHA III).** The UNESCO [[gha-vol3-7th-11th-century|General History of Africa,
> Vol. III]] (ch. 13, Hrbek & Devisse) frames the Almoravids as a **Saharan Berber (Sanhaja) religious
> movement** that arose among the desert nomads who controlled the western [[trans-saharan-trade|trans-
> Saharan trade]] — a reforming jihad that unified the Maghrib and al-Andalus *and* projected power south
> into the Sahel, traditionally credited (though now debated) with the conquest of the [[ghana-empire|empire
> of Ghana]]'s capital c. 1076. They are the western counterpart to the [[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]]: a
> Berber empire born of the desert and the gold trade — the [[islamization-of-africa|Islamization of
> Africa]] turning back northward.

The **Almoravids** (Arabic *al-murabitun*, "those who bind themselves together" in
defence of the faith) were a Berber religious-military movement that built an empire
spanning the western Maghreb and Muslim Iberia in the late eleventh and early twelfth
centuries. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Hugh Kennedy, Ch. 16) presents them as
the force that **halted the [[reconquista|Reconquista]]** after the fall of Toledo.

## Origins and Empire

The movement arose c. 1040 among the **Sanhaja Berbers** of the western Sahara, fused
by the religious teaching of Ibn Yasin (a strict [[sunni-islam|Maliki Sunni]] reform)
into a confederation under the Banu Turgut chiefs — Abu Bakr and his cousin **Yusuf b.
Tashfin**. Kennedy stresses that the Almoravids were "a religious movement, not a
tribe," but, unlike the later [[almohads|Almohads]], their leadership remained
**secular**, dependent on the *ulama* for religious legitimacy. They founded
**Marrakesh** (c. 1070) as their capital and conquered Morocco and the western Maghreb,
also controlling the northern terminus of the trans-Saharan gold trade.

## Intervention in al-Andalus

The fall of **Toledo to Alfonso VI (1085)** so alarmed the taifa kings that they
summoned Yusuf b. Tashfin across the straits. He routed the Castilians at
**Sagrajas/Zallaqa (1086)** — then, judging the taifa rulers corrupt and feeble,
**deposed them and annexed al-Andalus** to his empire (1090s), reuniting Muslim Spain
under austere North African rule. The Almoravids checked the Christian advance for two
generations, though they failed to retake Toledo and could not prevent
[[el-cid|El Cid's]] seizure of Valencia (1094).

## Decline

Almoravid rule in al-Andalus grew unpopular (heavy taxation, the rigidity of the
jurists, the persecution of Christians and Jews that drove some — including the
philosopher families — into exile or flight), and their power in Morocco was destroyed
by the rising **[[almohads|Almohad]]** movement, which took Marrakesh in **1147**. A
second taifa period briefly followed in Spain before the Almohads crossed in their turn.

## Significance

The Almoravids reversed the momentum of the eleventh-century Reconquista, demonstrated
the decisive weight of **North African power** in the fate of al-Andalus (the Straits of
Gibraltar as a connecting sea-way within an empire, not a frontier), and entrenched a
rigorist Maliki Islam in the western Mediterranean.
