# Early Dynastic Egypt (Dynasties I–II)

The first phase of the unified Egyptian state — the eight kings of the **First
Dynasty** and the kings of the **Second Dynasty** (c. 3100–2686 BCE) — from the
unification of the Two Lands to the threshold of the [[old-kingdom-egypt|Old
Kingdom]] pyramid age. It is the period in which the institutions of pharaonic
Egypt — [[divine-kingship|divine kingship]], a centralized treasury-and-granary
administration, a royal necropolis tradition, and hieroglyphic writing — took
their lasting form. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]]
(Ch. XI, I. E. S. Edwards).

## Origins and the unified monarchy

Tradition and indirect evidence indicate that pre-unification Egypt was divided
into a **northern (Lower Egyptian) kingdom** centred on Pe/Buto in the Delta and
a **southern (Upper Egyptian) kingdom** centred on Nekhen (Hierakonpolis). The
falcon-god **Horus** was the patron deity of the southern rulers, and the
shadowy "Followers of Horus" of the king-lists (Palermo Stone, Turin Canon,
Manetho's "Spirits of the Dead, the Demigods") represent the dimly-remembered
predynastic kings. Only two predynastic southern kings are attested in
contemporary record — **Ka(?)** and **Scorpion** — the latter known from the
Hierakonpolis mace-head showing him opening an irrigation canal. The conquest of
the north was completed by [[narmer|Narmer]], commemorated on the Narmer Palette;
see [[unification-of-egypt-3100bce]].

## The kings (the sources problem)

Reconstructing the dynasty is a problem in reconciling **contemporary** royal
names (the Horus-name in the *serekh*) with the much later **king-lists** (Abydos,
Saqqara, Turin Canon, Manetho), which are riddled with scribal misreadings of
cursive hieratic. Edwards works carefully through the equations. The First
Dynasty sequence: **Narmer (≈Menes), Aha, Djer, Djet, Mer(it)neith, Den, Anedjib,
Semerkhet, Qaa.**

- **Menes** — the legendary unifier of the lists, probably to be identified with
  the Horus Narmer (an ivory label of Aha from Naqada bears the name *mn*).
- **Mer(it)neith** — most likely a **queen-regent**, ruling for the minor Den;
  her Abydos "tomb" was equipped exactly like a king's, a rare instance of a
  woman attaining sovereign status this early.
- **Den** — introduces the **Double Crown** (*sekhemty*) and the title
  *nesut-bity*, "King of Upper and Lower Egypt"; an energetic reign with an
  unusually rich archaeological record.
- **Peribsen** (Dyn. II) — anomalously took the **Seth-title** instead of Horus,
  and **Khasekhem(wy)** records bloody campaigns within Egypt itself
  ("47,209... 48,205" slain) — signs of a temporary breakdown of unity and a
  Horus–Seth religious-political conflict, ended by **Khasekhemwy**, who alone
  bore *both* the Horus and Seth titles ("the Two Lords are at peace in him").

Khasekhemwy's reign — stone temple-building, the first attested copper royal
statue, mastery of granite — was "culturally the forerunner of the Old Kingdom";
he was closely related to [[djoser|Djoser]], who opens Dynasty III.

## Government and economy

A divine absolute monarch assisted by a developing bureaucracy: the **King's
House** (*per-nesut*), the dual **Treasuries** — the White House and Red House
of Upper and Lower Egypt — and **Granaries**, supported by the biennial
"Following of Horus" royal progress and a biennial census of "gold and fields."
The titles *tjaty* (later "vizier") and "Keeper of Nekhen" (a southern viceroy)
already appear. Administration was centred on [[memphis|Memphis]] with provincial
(nome) branches; Edwards suspects Upper and Lower Egypt were governed differently
(feudal-dispersed south vs. more urban north).

## Foreign relations

- **Southern Mesopotamia** — a striking horizon of protodynastic contact:
  serpent-necked monsters on the Narmer and Ashmolean palettes, the
  Sumerian-costumed "master of animals" on the Gebel el-Araq knife-handle,
  imported [[uruk|Uruk]]–Jamdat Nasr cylinder seals, and niched-brick "palace
  façade" mastaba architecture. The movement was one-way (east→west); Edwards
  reads it as a small migration of Sumerians, not commerce. Crucially, Egyptian
  and Sumerian writing share *principles* (phonographic and determinative use)
  without sharing forms — the Sumerian contribution to
  [[writing-and-record-keeping|Egyptian writing]] was "mainly suggestive."
- **Byblos / Syria** — sea-borne trade for cedar and oils (a Khasekhemwy vase at
  Byblos); the Levantine connection that endures through Egyptian history.
- **Nubia, Libya, Sinai** — frontier campaigns (Aha, Djer, Den, Khasekhem) for
  security and for turquoise/copper; the A-Group of Lower Nubia, culturally akin
  to predynastic Egypt, fades by the end of Dynasty III.

## Religion and the royal tombs

The king was the living **Horus**; queens transmitted the succession ("She who
sees Horus and Seth"). The pantheon — Ptah of Memphis, Neith of Sais, Horus,
Seth, Anubis, the sun-cult (king Reneb, "Re is my Lord") — is largely in place.
Royal funerary architecture evolves from the niched-brick **mastaba** toward the
monumental, with a buried sand mound (the **Primeval Hill**) inside some Dynasty I
Saqqara mastabas — the conceptual seed of the pyramid.

**Retainer sacrifice.** The most arresting feature: rows of subsidiary graves of
servants and artisans around the royal tombs, evidently killed at the king's
funeral. The practice **peaked under Djer** (590+ subsidiary graves at Abydos)
and then **declined sharply** (≈26 under Qaa, ≈10–15 under Khasekhemwy) — an
early abandonment of human sacrifice with no parallel in later Egypt.

## Historiography

**The Abydos vs. Saqqara royal-tomb debate.** Both sites yielded "tombs" with
Dynasty I royal names. W. B. Emery (excavating the great North Saqqara mastabas
from 1938) argued the *real* tombs were at Saqqara and Abydos held cenotaphs; the
prevailing resolution (which Edwards leans toward) is the reverse — **Abydos
holds the royal tombs, Saqqara the mastabas of high officials.** A genuine,
largely-resolved archaeological dispute rather than a live controversy.

**The "Dynastic Race."** Edwards repeats the then-current idea of broad-skulled
"Armenoid" newcomers ("Dynastic Race") arriving from Asia to form the ruling
class. This **diffusionist racial migration theory is now discredited**; cited
here as 1971-vintage historiography, not current scholarship — the cultural
continuity from [[predynastic-egypt|Predynastic Egypt]] is now stressed instead.
See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
