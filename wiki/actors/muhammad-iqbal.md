## Overview

Muḥammad Iqbāl (1877–1938), born in the Punjab city of Sialkot, was a poet, philosopher, and political thinker who has been called the "spiritual father of Pakistan." He is one of the two most significant Muslim thinkers of the early twentieth century alongside [[muhammad-abduh]] (the other Muḥammad, as Peter Adamson notes). Iqbāl studied in Europe at several universities, returned to India to become a leading poet in Urdu (and Persian), practiced law in Lahore, and delivered a set of English-language lectures later published as *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* (1934). He drew simultaneously on the Islamic Sufi tradition, Avicennan metaphysics, and European philosophers (especially Nietzsche and Bergson) to articulate a reformist philosophy of Islam suited to the colonial condition.

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Ch. 61.

## Intellectual Formation

Iqbāl's formation was unusually broad. He was deeply rooted in the Islamic philosophical tradition — engaging with the Indian Sufi thinker Shāh Walī Allāh (see [[philosophy-in-islamic-india]]), Avicenna's epistemology, and Rūmī's poetry — while also absorbing German idealism, Nietzsche's critique of Christianity, Bergson's philosophy of time and intuition, Durkheim's sociology, and Russell's logic and mathematics. On a single page of *The Reconstruction* he cites Zeno of Elea, al-Ashʿarī, Ibn Ḥazm, Bergson, Russell, and Cantor (p. 34).

The most surprising European influence was **Nietzsche**. Iqbāl agreed with Nietzsche's critique of Christianity as a religion of weakness and self-abnegation — but argued that Islam is precisely the opposite. The Koran, for Iqbāl, is the revelatory text that celebrates *this world*, the concrete and particular, demanding marvel at physical creation as an expression of God's might. In this respect Islam is fundamentally opposed to the Greek philosophical tradition of abstract theorizing; it was therefore a mistake for early Muslim philosophers and theologians to use Hellenic frameworks to expound the Koran (pp. 13, 122–4).

## Epistemology and Sufism

Iqbāl's *Reconstruction* offers a threefold epistemology: knowledge comes through (1) experience of nature, (2) the lessons of history, and (3) intuitive union with reality. Scientists like Ibn al-Haytham mastered the first; Ibn Khaldūn mastered the second; the Sufis alone have achieved the third. But Iqbāl did not see these as incompatible. Against William James's sharp opposition between mystical consciousness and everyday consciousness, Iqbāl insisted the two are continuous: "mystical intuition sees reality all at once, whereas rational inquiry takes things bit by bit" (p. 27). He endorsed Rūmī's image of grasping the divine unity with the "heart."

He also rejected the ascetic strands of Sufism as corruptions introduced by world-denying influences (Neoplatonism, Buddhism). Genuine Sufi insight should be directed *towards* this world, not away from it.

## Political Philosophy

Iqbāl's politics evolved significantly. Early in his career he was attracted to nationalism; later he called nationalism "the greatest threat to Islam" (a remark that must be understood against the backdrop of the dissolution of the Ottoman empire). The nation-state, in Iqbāl's critique, replaces religious devotion with ethnic or geographical identity, creating "a dualism which does not exist in Islam" (*Reconstruction*, p. 148). Islam guides its adherents towards a unified grasp of reality and political harmony that transcends national boundaries; the ultimate political aim is a kind of global Islamic solidarity, a "league of nations" in which geographical and racial divisions are acknowledged "for facility of reference" but do not ground political identity.

Yet Iqbāl was practical. While rejecting nationalism as a final goal, he recognized the need for strong constituent parts within any broader whole. He supported the idea of assigning different regions of India to different communities — a position that influenced the eventual creation of Pakistan (1947), though he did not live to see it. He rejected the hard-core rationalism of Sayyid Aḥmad Khān and the secular nation-state ideal of the Young Turks, while equally opposing the conservative ulema in India.

He differed from **Mawdūdī** (who wanted explicitly Islamic states defined by religious law) by insisting on Islam's flexibility and its adaptation to historical circumstances and the character of particular communities.

## On Science and Islamic History

A recurrent theme in *The Reconstruction* is that Muslim scientists (Ibn al-Haytham, Ibn Khaldūn) pioneered the empirical approach that was later taken up in European science — Roger Bacon drew on Ibn al-Haytham's optics — and that Islam's affinity for this world accounts for these achievements. The Muslim world is therefore not inherently anti-scientific; its contemporary weakness vis-à-vis Europe is a historical aberration, not an essential feature of the tradition.

## Key Works

- *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* (1934) — English-language lectures delivered in several Indian cities; his main philosophical text
- *Asrār-i Khudī* (Secrets of the Self, 1915) — Persian poetry articulating his philosophy of selfhood
- Urdu poetry (collected in *Bāl-i Jibrīl*, *Zabūr-i ʿAjam*, and others)

## Historiography

Iqbāl is discussed in the European scholarly tradition of Islamic philosophy primarily through the lens of Islamic modernism (see [[islamic-modernism]]) and the Pakistan-formation narrative. Adamson's treatment (Ch. 61) situates him within the longer history of Islamic philosophy — connecting him to Avicenna, Rūmī, Shāh Walī Allāh, and the *ḥikmat* tradition — rather than treating him solely as a political figure. His engagement with Nietzsche has attracted particular attention as a case of creative reappropriation of European ideas within an Islamic framework.
