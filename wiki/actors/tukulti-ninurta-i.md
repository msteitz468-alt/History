# Tukulti-Ninurta I (Assyrian king)

The greatest of the **[[assyria-middle|Middle Assyrian]]** kings — the conqueror who
brought Assyria to its 13th-century peak by **defeating Kassite Babylonia and
sacking Babylon itself**, the first Assyrian to rule the whole of Mesopotamia.
Celebrated in the chauvinist **Tukulti-Ninurta Epic**. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]] (Ch. XXV, Munn-Rankin). Reign
1244–1208 (volume) / ~1233–1197 BCE.

## Building the empire

Heir to the conquests of **Adad-nirari I** (who broke [[mitanni|Khanigalbat]] and
won the title "king of the totality") and **Shalmaneser I** (who annexed Khanigalbat
after crushing Šattuara II, blinding 14,400 prisoners, and first struck the early
Urartian **Uruatri**), Tukulti-Ninurta spent his first decade securing the north and
east: the **Qutu** of the Zagros, and the **Šubari/Nairi lands** of the upper Tigris
(Katmukhi, Alzi, the Diyarbakır district), reaching toward Lake Van and gaining the
**copper of Ergani Maden** and command of the Anatolian routes. He styled himself
"king of the Nairi lands."

## The conquest of Babylon

His supreme triumph was the **defeat of [[kassites|Kassite Babylonia]]**. When
**Kaštiliaš IV** attacked Assyria (breaking his treaty), Tukulti-Ninurta read the
treaty aloud before Šamaš to fix the guilt, then **captured Kaštiliaš in battle and
took him in chains to Ashur**. He went on to **storm Babylon, demolish its walls,
sack the temple of Marduk and carry off the god's statue to Assyria** — and assumed
the full Babylonian titulary ("king of Karduniash, king of Sumer and Akkad, king of
Babylon"). For the first time an Assyrian ruled **both Mesopotamian kingdoms**. The
war is recounted in Chronicle P and the **Tukulti-Ninurta Epic**, a near-contemporary
poem of "undisguised chauvinism."

## Overreach and fall

The conquests outran Assyria's capacity to hold them. Tukulti-Ninurta built a
grandiose new capital across the Tigris, **Kar-Tukulti-Ninurta**, and his reign ended
in **disaster: he was murdered by his own son** amid revolt, and Babylonia was lost —
"its political decline was as meteoric as its rise." The carrying-off of the **Marduk
statue** became a lasting grievance poisoning Assyro-Babylonian relations for
centuries.

## Significance

Tukulti-Ninurta I is the **apex of the Middle Assyrian empire** and the man who first
made Assyria master of all Mesopotamia — a preview of the
[[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian]] world-empire to come. His sack of Babylon and
seizure of Marduk set the template for the long, bitter **Assyria–Babylonia
antagonism**. His reign also exhibits the structural lesson of the volume's Assyrian
chapters: **conquest outrunning the capacity to govern**, ending in assassination and
swift decline — the Late Bronze great-powers system buckling on the eve of the
[[unknown|collapse]].

## Historiography

Documented by his own building and campaign inscriptions, the **Synchronistic
History** and **Chronicle P** (often at odds), and the literary **Tukulti-Ninurta
Epic** — the latter a prime case of royal propaganda as historical source. His
assassination is known from later king-list tradition. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
