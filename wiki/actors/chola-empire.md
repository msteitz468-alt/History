# The Chola Empire

The **Chola Empire** (c. 850–1279) was the great medieval power of the **Tamil south** and the
most important **maritime empire** of pre-modern India — the one peninsular state, Thapar argues,
that achieved a genuinely **centralized administration** rather than a loose hegemony over
feudatories. Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## Rise and apogee

The Cholas — one of the ancient [[sangam-age-south-india|Sangam-age]] Tamil lines — re-emerged when
**Vijayalaya** seized **Tanjore** (Thanjavur) c. 850, building an independent kingdom on the decline
of the Pallavas and Pandyas. Power was consolidated under **Rajaraja I (985–1014)** and his son
**Rajendra I (1014–44)**, whose reigns of relentless campaigning carried Chola arms across the south,
into Sri Lanka, and up the east coast (Rajendra reached the Ganges, taking the title *Gangaikonda*).

## A maritime empire

What distinguishes the Cholas is **sea power**. Rajaraja conducted a **naval attack on the
Maldives**, and Rajendra launched the famous **overseas expedition against [[srivijaya|Srivijaya]]**
(c. 1025) — a campaign Thapar reads in **commercial** terms: Srivijaya controlled the Strait of
Malacca through which the rich **India–China trade** passed and could divert or tax it, threatening
Chola/South-Indian merchant interests. The Chola fleet was the instrument of a Tamil mercantile reach
that, with the great **merchant guilds**, extended across the Bay of Bengal — deepening the
[[indianization-of-southeast-asia|cultural ties]] (Pallava architecture, Tamil script) between South
India and Southeast Asia.

## The Chola state: centralization and village self-government

Thapar's key analytical point: **alone among the southern dynasties** (the Chalukyas, Rashtrakutas,
Hoysalas, Yadavas — all hobbled by their feudatories), the Cholas "were able to ignore their
feudatories to a significant extent," maintaining a centrally organized administration that "still
maintained contact with the cultivator on a wide scale." The early kings' unobtrusive titles gave way
to imperial ones (*chakravartigal*); a **cult of the god-king** developed (worship of deceased rulers'
images; great temples doubling as royal monuments — Rajaraja's Brihadishvara temple at Tanjore); the
kingdom was structured into provinces (*mandalam*) → districts (*valanadu*) → village groups. Yet
this centralism rested on a famous tradition of **village self-government**: the **sabha** (assembly,
especially of *brahmadeya* villages) managed land, irrigation, endowments, justice, and records,
its committees chosen by lot — the local autonomy Thapar treats as a defining strength of Tamil
civilization (see [[pallava-chalukya-rashtrakuta]]). The **temple** was the economic and social hub
of village life.

## Decline

After Rajendra, the Cholas were drawn into wars with the **Chalukyas of Kalyani** over the
Vengi/Krishna-Godavari frontier and, from the west, the rising **Hoysalas**; their old rivals the
**Pandyas** of Madurai revived, and by the **13th century had superseded the Cholas** as the dominant
Tamil power (Marco Polo visited the prosperous Pandya kingdom in 1288/1293) — until both fell within
reach of the [[delhi-sultanate|Delhi Sultanate's]] 14th-century intervention in the south.

## Significance

The Cholas are the wiki's prime case of **South Indian imperial statehood**: a centralized,
temple-anchored, **thalassocratic** empire that projected Tamil power and culture across the Indian
Ocean — the peninsular counterweight to the Gangetic empires, and the high point of the autonomous
[[sangam-age-south-india|Tamil/Dravidian]] tradition.

## Avari's Treatment (Chapter 11)

Avari's *India: The Ancient Past* (2007) confirms and expands the Thapar picture. The Cholas are introduced via their geography: "The eastern seaboard of southern India is known as the **Coromandel coast**, the term derived from the Cola-Mandalam." They "emerged as a substantial power only in the late ninth century when they defeated the Pallavas." They then "ruled practically the whole of the far south, excepting the Chera kingdom in Kerala" by the early tenth century, until the [[pallava-chalukya-rashtrakuta|Rashtrakutas]] "inflicted a crushing defeat on them in 949." The turn came with **Rajaraja I** (985–1012).

**Three great Chola kings**: Avari details the achievements of Rajaraja I, Rajendra I, and Kulottunga I. Rajaraja I attacked the Pandyas, Cheras, Sri Lanka, and the Chalukyas of Kalyani. He "solidified Chola identity through an elaborate royal administration and bureaucracy, along with the promotion of the **cult of god-king** at an iconic temple" — the **Brihadiswara Temple** at Thanjavur, with its 62-metre vimana carved from an 80-ton block of granite. Rajendra I (1012–44) conquered all of Sri Lanka and sent the famous expedition to Bengal's River Ganga — "ordering his commanders to bring back jars of water from the River Ganga, so that he could claim token ownership of the ancient land of Aryavarta." He also sent a **naval expedition to Sri Vijaya** not for imperial motives but "solely a matter of safeguarding the shipping lane for the Chola-sponsored merchant fleet." Under Kulottunga I (1070–1118) the kingdom "reached new heights of prosperity and maturity" and "became the symbol of southern classicism."

**Commercial context**: Avari situates Chola maritime power within two structural shifts: the displacement of the Abbasid trade network by the Fatimids of Egypt (shifting trade from the Persian Gulf to the Red Sea routes, benefiting the far south over Karnataka), and the commercial opening of China under the Song dynasty. China "was several centuries ahead of any other part of the world in the fields of steel manufacture, the armaments sector, transportation networks and techniques, the management of credits and finance." Chinese demand for Indian cotton textiles, spices, ivory, and aromatics created large trade deficits. Control of the Malacca Straits — held by **Sri Vijaya** — was the strategic chokepoint. When Sri Vijaya demanded that ships terminate journeys in the straits (routing profits through Srivijayan middlemen), "this was much too irksome for the merchant organisations in the Chola state, and Rajendra I decided to use his substantial naval force to punish Sri Vijaya."

**Institutions — the three-body model**: Avari's most important addition is the detailed treatment of how Chola centralism worked through three institutions: the **brahmadeya** (brahman-settled villages), the **nagaram** (market centre), and the **temple**. The brahmadeyas acted as agents of the king in the countryside. The nagaram hosted powerful merchant guilds such as the **Ayyavole 500** — "one of the main south Indian guilds of merchants who were the controllers of the Chola overseas trade." The temple "was more than a place of worship for the Chola kings" — "vast donations were made to the temple by rich and poor, and huge quantities of foodstuffs were moved into the temple precincts from the hinterland." The temple was "the central arena where the king, through his enormous powers of patronage, could demonstrate more than ritual sovereignty." Temples also "institutionalised the various religious cults associated with the popular [[bhakti-movement|bhakti movement]] in south India."

**Three historiographical models** are presented for the Chola state: (1) the traditional view of a bureaucratic centralised monarchy; (2) Burton Stein's **segmentary state theory** — the king held only ritual sovereignty outside his core area, with real power lying with the *nad* assemblies and local chiefs; and (3) a more recent revisionist position rejecting Stein's extremes, arguing the Cholas "made subtle use of the existing institutions to integrate their kingdom rather than allow the institutions to go their own way."

**Temples**: The Brihadiswara at Thanjavur and Gangaikondacholapuram are described in detail. Avari cites Rodin's description of the **Shiva Nataraja** bronze sculpture from Tiruvalangadu as "the most perfect representation of rhythmic movement in art."
