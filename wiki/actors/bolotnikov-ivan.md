# Ivan Bolotnikov

## Biography / Description
**Ivan Bolotnikov** was a Russian military commander, former slave, and the leader of the massive social rebellion during the Time of Troubles (1606–1607) (L2284-2286). A runaway slave who was captured by the Crimean Tatars and sold to the Ottoman Turks as a galley slave, he eventually escaped and returned to Russia through Poland-Lithuania (L2284). 

During the political chaos following the assassination of the First False Dmitry in 1606, Bolotnikov organized a peasant and Cossack war in southern Russia against Tsar Basil Shuisky, advocating for the complete destruction of the Muscovite social and economic hierarchy (L2284, L2374-2376). He was captured after the siege of Tula in 1607 and subsequently executed (L2286).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
* **March on Moscow (1606):** In late 1606, Bolotnikov led southern rebel armies to the gates of Moscow, threatening to capture the capital (L2284-2286). His forces included serfs, peasants, Cossacks, and elements of the service gentry who initially opposed Tsar Basil Shuisky (L2284-2286).
* **Social Manifestoes:** Bolotnikov issued radical manifestoes urging the lower classes to rise against their masters, kill the boyars, and seize land and property (L2284).
* **Siege of Tula (1607):** After the gentry elements of his army (led by Procopius Liapunov and Philip Pashkov) defected to Tsar Basil Shuisky, Bolotnikov retreated to Tula (L2286). A government army led by Tsar Shuisky besieged Tula for four months, finally forcing a surrender by flooding the city (L2286).

## Historical Significance
Ivan Bolotnikov's movement was the first massive class-based rebellion in Russian history, initiating the pattern of peasant wars (*krestianskie voiny*) that characterized the early modern era (L2276, L2354-2356). His radical attacks on noble property alienated the service gentry, driving them back into alliance with the autocracy (L2286, L2366). 

Soviet historians heavily studied Bolotnikov as a revolutionary hero of the class struggle, contrasting him with the "stable" elements of Muscovite society (L2354-2356).
