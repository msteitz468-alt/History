# Radegund of Poitiers

**Thuringian princess, reluctant Frankish queen, monastic founder, and the most influential holy woman of
sixth-century Gaul.** Radegund (c. 520–587) recurs across the Oxford Handbook as queen, ascetic, patron of
[[venantius-fortunatus|Venantius Fortunatus]], importer of the True Cross, and the central female figure of
Merovingian religious culture — bridging queenship, monasticism, charity, penance, visions, and Christology.

## Life

A Thuringian royal captured as a child in the Frankish conquest, Radegund was raised at the Merovingian
court and married to [[chlothar-ii|Chlothar I]]. She fled the marriage — after Chlothar killed her brother —
and established a religious community at Saix and then at **Poitiers** (the convent of Sainte-Croix),
obtaining Caesarius of Arles's *Rule for Nuns* and appointing Agnes as abbess while herself guiding the
house (see [[merovingian-queenship]], [[merovingian-monasticism]]). Her acquisition of a relic of the **Holy
Cross** from Constantinople — the occasion of Fortunatus's hymns *Vexilla regis* and *Pange lingua* — gave
the convent its name and its prestige (see [[merovingian-byzantine-relations]]). The **Poitiers nuns' revolt
of 589**, two years after her death, exposed the strains of royal women in religious life (see
[[merovingian-queenship]]).

## Holiness, Charity, and Self-Mortification

Radegund built "the first house… specifically for needy women" at Athies, washing the sick herself — "the
mistress of the palace was a servant of the poor" (see [[early-medieval-hospitals-and-charity]]). Her
asceticism was extreme: the **branding ritual** described by Fortunatus (a heated brass plate pressed *in
signo Christi*, leaving two bleeding *foramina*) made her wounds objects of Christological meditation, and
Fortunatus coined *tortrix* for her practice (see [[early-medieval-penance]], [[merovingian-christology]]).
Two hagiographies — Fortunatus's (c. 587) and Baudonivia's (c. 600) — balance body and mind, and report her
visions (see [[visions-and-the-afterlife-merovingian]]).

## Counterfactual Significance

Radegund is the hinge of Merovingian female sanctity: through her, the cult of the Holy Cross entered
Frankish Gaul, Fortunatus produced the Passion hymnody that shaped Western devotion, and the convent of
Sainte-Croix became a model of aristocratic female monasticism. Without her Poitiers years much of the
Handbook's recovery of "vital" Merovingian religion would lack its principal exemplar. She embodies the
volume's argument that the Merovingian world sustained late-antique learned and devotional culture rather
than letting it lapse.
