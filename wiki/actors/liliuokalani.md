## Overview

Lili'uokalani was the last sovereign monarch of the Kingdom of [[hawaii]], reigning from 1891 until her forced abdication on 17 January 1893. She inherited a monarchy already structurally weakened by the 1887 "Bayonet Constitution," which gave the haole missionary-planter party effective cabinet control and surrendered exclusive use of Pearl Harbor to the United States. Her attempt to restore constitutional authority was the immediate trigger of the American-backed coup that ended Hawaiian sovereignty.

## Role

Lili'uokalani succeeded her brother Kalakaua (who died in San Francisco in 1891) amid rumours — "persistent in Hawai'i" — that his death was caused by the missionary party's agents. "Soon after her accession" she received "a petition of two-thirds of the voters imploring her to do away with the Bayonet Constitution and return the powers of government to the Hawaiian people."

By 14 January 1893 she had drafted a new constitution and informed her cabinet of her intention to institute it. Her cabinet, controlled by the missionary party under the Bayonet Constitution, refused. Thurston formed the "Committee of Public Safety" and secured US Marine landing on 16 January. On 17 January, Lili'uokalani yielded under written protest to "the superior force of the United States of America, whose minister plenipotentiary . . . has caused United States troops to be landed at Honolulu." Her protest was explicit: she yielded "until such time as the Government of the United States shall, upon the facts being presented to it, undo the action of its representative and reinstate me."

## Key Decisions and Their Consequences

**Drafting the new constitution (January 1893)**: Responding to the two-thirds voter petition, she attempted to restore monarchical authority by executive action. Her cabinet (controlled by the Bayonet Constitution) blocked her, and the move gave Thurston the pretext to activate the coup plan he had been preparing since 1882. Whether restraint would have deferred or merely delayed the coup is uncertain; US Minister Stevens was already committed to annexation.

**Yielding under protest rather than resisting**: Lili'uokalani chose not to resist militarily — Blount later reported she was warned not to take up arms "lest the Marines land and forever quash the hopes of the Hawaiians." Her written protest preserved the legal record that became the basis for Cleveland's condemnation and Blount's investigation.

**Acceding to general amnesty**: When Cleveland sought her restoration on condition of granting amnesty, she initially protested that such a promise would be unconstitutional, then acceded. The Provisional Government refused to comply regardless.

**Public appeal to Americans**: As annexation was finalised, she appealed directly: "Oh, honest Americans, as Christians hear me for my downtrodden people! . . . do not covet the little vineyards of Naboth's so far from your shores . . . The people to whom your fathers told of the living God . . . are crying aloud to Him in their time of trouble." The appeal fell on "deaf American ears."

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Lili'uokalani's attempt to restore constitutional authority, the missionary party might have found a different pretext or timeline — Stevens's letters to Washington demonstrate that annexation was a personal mission pursued since 1889. However, the precise moment and character of the overthrow — and the documentary record Lili'uokalani's protest created — shaped the political controversy that led Cleveland to repudiate the coup and Blount to investigate. Her decision to yield under written protest rather than silently capitulate preserved the evidentiary basis for the 1993 US Apology Resolution (PL 103-150).

## Historiography

Laenui's section in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] treats Lili'uokalani as a sovereign head of state resisting an illegal foreign-backed coup, drawing on her own published memoir (*Hawaii's Story by Hawaii's Queen*) and the primary documentary record (Blount's Report; Cleveland's address to Congress; her protest statement published in the Honolulu Daily Bulletin, 18 January 1893). This is the mainstream Hawaiian historiographical position. The rival US-exceptionalist reading (Kuykendall's earlier volumes) emphasises inevitable modernisation, but the documentary record largely supports the Laenui/Blount interpretation of decisive US agency.
