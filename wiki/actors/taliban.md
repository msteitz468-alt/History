## Overview

The Taliban ("students," from Persian/Pashtu *talib*) is a Deobandi-influenced Afghan Islamist movement that emerged from the civil war following the 1989 Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan, took Kabul in 1996, and has ruled or contested rule of Afghanistan since. It grew directly out of the border madrasa network built up during the [[afghan-mujahideen|Afghan jihad]] against the Soviet Union.

## Origins

The movement drew its recruits from Deobandi madrasas on the Pakistan–Afghanistan border, established amid the roughly three million Afghan refugees who crossed into Pakistan during the 1980s war. Pakistani president Zia ul-Haq's Islamization policies drove the country's registered madrasas from 900 in 1971 to 8,000 by 1988, most run by Deobandi teachers of the Jamiat Ulema-i Islam (JUI, est. 1947). The Dar-ul-'Ulum Haqqania madrasa at Akhora Khattak, founded and led by Samiul Haq (b. 1937), was especially emblematic and produced key figures including advisor ties to Mullah Mohammad Omar (1960–2013), who led the movement. These border institutions were also influenced by Wahhabi "Arab volunteer" fighters present in the same camps.

## Leadership and Ideology

Mullah Omar claimed the title *amir-ul-mu'minin* ("commander of the faithful"), a title Formichi notes came closer to reviving the office of Caliph than any claimant since the Ottoman caliphate's abolition in 1924.

## Rise to Power and Alliances

The Taliban's rapid consolidation drew support from three distinct quarters: Pakistan (seeking a stabilizing client in Afghanistan), the United States (interested in securing Central Asian oil routes and limiting residual Soviet/Russian influence), and Arab jihadi networks led by [[abdallah-azzam|Abdallah 'Azzam]] and Osama bin Laden. The Taliban took Kabul in 1996 and subsequently allied with the international jihadi contingent that coalesced into [[al-qaeda]], providing it safe haven against the traditionalist mujahidin faction of Ahmad Shah Massoud (1953–2001).

## Counterfactual Significance

Absent the mass refugee flows and the state-driven Pakistani madrasa expansion of the 1980s, the social and institutional base from which the Taliban recruited would not have existed at the scale it did; the movement's alliance with al-Qaeda after 1996 subsequently made Afghanistan the base from which the September 11, 2001 attacks were organized, reshaping the post-Cold War international order.

## Historiography

This summary derives from Chiara Formichi, *Islam and Asia: A History* (2020), Ch. 7, "Islam as Resistance" — a synthetic survey account, not a specialist monograph. Formichi is attentive to the constructed, externally reinforced character of the Taliban's rise (Pakistani, Saudi, and US interests converging on the same movement for different reasons) and cautions against the post-9/11 tendency in "terrorism studies" literature to read the movement's trajectory solely through a religious-extremism lens rather than the geopolitical patronage that enabled it.
