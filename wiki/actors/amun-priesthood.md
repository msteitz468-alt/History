# The Priesthood of Amun (Karnak)

The great religious-economic institution of [[new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom Egypt]] —
the temple establishment of **Amun-Re of [[thebes|Thebes]]**, which rose with the
Theban royal house to become the wealthiest and most politically weighty body in
Egypt after the crown. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]]
(Ch. IX §III, Hayes).

## From local god to "national god"

Amun was an obscure deity of Karnak until the Theban **Twelfth Dynasty** raised him,
fusing him with the sun-god as **Amun-Re, "King of the Gods."** In the
[[second-intermediate-period|Second Intermediate Period]] he became "the divine
champion of Egyptian independence," and the [[new-kingdom-egypt|Eighteenth Dynasty]]
kings credited their victories to him above all gods. As Thebes was both his cult-home
and the imperial capital, his priests consolidated his supremacy over **Re of
Heliopolis** and **Ptah of Memphis**, until — absorbing their identities as a cosmic
creator-god — he approached the status of a true "national god." The pharaoh now
claimed the throne as **"son of Amun,"** a doctrine elaborated in the **divine-birth
reliefs** of [[hatshepsut|Hatshepsut]] at Deir el-Bahri and [[amenhotep-iii|Amenophis
III]] at Luxor.

## The temple economy

The empire's victories made Amun rich. His Karnak temple drew "a major share of the
spoils" of conquest — raw materials, prisoners, and slaves — and came to own
**hundreds of thousands of acres of farmland**, vast herds, vineyards, gold-mines,
workshops, and its own **fleets** (including seagoing ships for the Syrian and Red Sea
trade), worked by an army of serfs and managed by a great clerical bureaucracy
(overseers of the granaries, fields, cattle, gardens, and treasuries of Amun). It was,
after the crown itself, the wealthiest institution in the land — see
[[temple-economy]].

## Power, and its limits

Recent scholarship (CAH stresses) shows the priesthood's political power was **more
limited** under the strong Tuthmoside kings than older accounts claimed: the temple
"functioned as a department of the royal administration," its priests appointed and
removable by the king, its estates largely managed by **royal officials** (Senenmut
under Hatshepsut; the southern vizier Rekhmire under Thutmose III). Yet at moments of
**disputed succession** the High Priest of Amun, as interpreter of the god's will,
could swing divine sanction behind a candidate — Amun's oracle "chose" the young
[[thutmose-iii|Thutmose III]] in the temple, and a high priest backed
[[hatshepsut|Hatshepsut's]] claim. And the relentless **accumulation of wealth and
personnel** built a long-term concentration of power that, CAH judges, was in the end
"far more undermining to the nation's welfare" than any external enemy.

## Significance

The Amun priesthood is the institutional key to two of Egyptian history's great
turning points. Its swelling power under [[amenhotep-iii|Amenophis III]] is widely
read as a principal provocation of **Akhenaten's religious revolution** (the
[[unknown|Amarna]] period, beyond this volume) — the pharaoh's attempt to break the
Theban establishment by promoting the sole god **Aten**. And its eventual dominance
foreshadows the **Theban priest-kings** of the late New Kingdom, when the High Priest
of Amun would in effect rule Upper Egypt. It is the wiki's prime case of a
**religious institution as a political and economic power** rivalling the state.

## Historiography

Reconstructed from temple inscriptions and reliefs (the divine-birth and oracle
scenes), the titles and tombs of its great officials (Senenmut, Rekhmire, the high
priests Hapuseneb and Menkheperreseneb), and later economic documents (Papyrus Harris
I, the Wilbour Papyrus) projected back. The sources are the institution's own and
must be read critically; modern work has notably **downgraded** the priesthood's
independence under the strong 18th-Dynasty kings. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
