# Jefferson Davis

President of the Confederate States of America (1861–1865). Mississippi planter, U.S. Senator, Secretary of War (1853–57), and leading spokesman for Southern interests and the expansion of slavery. Davis was elected provisional (then permanent) President of the Confederacy in 1861; he led the Richmond government through the Civil War, overseeing strategy, foreign policy, and the mobilization of the slave South's resources.

## Role in the Confederacy

Davis embodied the planter elite's determination to create an independent slaveholding republic. While personally more moderate on reopening the African slave trade than fire-eaters like William Yancey or Leonidas Spratt, his administration was defined by the Cornerstone ideology articulated by his Vice President and by the practical need to secure European recognition and material support. Davis's government commissioned commerce raiders, sought loans in Europe, and dispatched diplomats (Yancey, Mason, Slidell, Benjamin) to court Britain and France.

## International Dimension (Bonner)

From the perspective of anti-Confederate opinion in Britain, France, and the wider Atlantic world, Davis's government was not a legitimate nationalist project but a "new power of a new species" whose explicit foundation on racial slavery and open threats to revive the slave trade rendered it uniquely dangerous. Davis's own public statements and those of his officials were weaponized by Sumner, Bright, and others to demonstrate that the Confederacy could never be a normal member of the family of nations.

## Historiography

Traditional military and political histories treat Davis as a flawed but dedicated wartime president (comparison with Lincoln is ubiquitous). Bonner's account foregrounds how Davis's state was defined abroad less by its battlefield performance than by its unapologetic proslavery constitutionalism and maritime depredations. The post-war Davis became a symbol both for Lost Cause veneration and for the enduring stigma the 1861–65 campaign had attached to the Confederate project.
