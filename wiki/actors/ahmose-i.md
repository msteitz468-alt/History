# Ahmose I

The Theban king (reigned ~1550–1525 BCE) who **expelled the [[hyksos|Hyksos]],
reunified Egypt, and founded the [[new-kingdom-egypt|Eighteenth Dynasty]]** — the
hinge between the [[second-intermediate-period|Second Intermediate Period]] and the
imperial New Kingdom. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]]
(Ch. VIII, James).

## The war of liberation

Younger brother and successor of **Kamose** (and son of Seqenenre Tao II, who had
died in battle), Ahmose completed the war his predecessors began. The only
contemporary account is the tomb-biography of the soldier **Ahmose son of Ibana** of
El-Kab, who served under him: a series of assaults finally took **[[avaris|Avaris]]**
(~1550), after which Ahmose pursued the Hyksos into Palestine and reduced their
fortress of **Sharuhen** in a three-year siege, securing Egypt's north-eastern
frontier. He then turned south, putting down two Nubian revolts and re-establishing
Egyptian control to **Buhen** and beyond — laying the administrative foundation for
the office of **Viceroy of Kush**.

## Reunification and reorganization

With the frontiers secure, Ahmose's "relatively peaceful" later reign (~25 years)
was devoted to **domestic reconstruction**: confirming or replacing nome officials
to secure central authority, rehabilitating the neglected irrigation and tax systems,
reopening the **Tura limestone quarries** (year 22) for a building programme,
resuming trade for silver, gold, lapis, and Lebanese cedar (via [[byblos|Byblos]]),
and lavishing gifts on **Amun of Thebes**. His monuments — and especially the
superb jewellery of his mother **Queen Ahhotep** — mark a sharp recovery in artistic
quality over the "modest" Theban work of the late 17th Dynasty.

## The royal ladies

Ahmose's reign is notable for the prominence of three royal women — his grandmother
**Tetisheri**, his mother **Ahhotep** (praised on his Karnak stela as one who
"looked after Egypt's soldiers... brought back her fugitives... and expelled her
rebels," perhaps acting as co-regent at a moment of crisis), and his wife
**Ahmose-Nefertari**, the first great **"God's Wife of Amun."** They established a
pattern of **female authority within Egyptian kingship** that runs through the whole
dynasty (down to [[hatshepsut|Hatshepsut]]).

## Shaw (2000): the campaign and its archaeology

Janine Bourriau's chapter dates the **[[events/fall-of-avaris-1528bce|capture of Avaris]]**
to Ahmose's **regnal years 18–22** (c.1532–1528 BCE) — well into the reign, not its start —
and reconstructs his **strategy**: bypass Memphis to seize Heliopolis, then take Tell
el-Habua to cut the Hyksos retreat across Sinai, then assault Avaris. Beyond the biography of
Ahmose son of Ibana, two evidence streams now illuminate it: the **Abydos temple reliefs**
discovered in 1993 (horses, chariots, Hyksos captives, a battle fleet), and the
[[places/avaris|Tell el-Dabʿa]] archaeology, where Ahmose razed the Hyksos palace and built a
new one decorated with **Minoan-style frescoes** (bull-leapers, labyrinths) that
"revolutionized ideas of the relations between Egypt and Crete." Bourriau also underscores
Ahhotep's exceptional epithets — she "pacified Upper Egypt, and expelled her rebels" — and
that Ahmose, building on [[actors/seqenenra-taa|Seqenenra]] and [[kamose|Kamose]], achieved a
reunification "not to be broken again for over 400 years."

## Counterfactual / analytical significance

Ahmose is the **founder-figure** of imperial Egypt: not himself a great conqueror
abroad (his Asian campaigning was limited), but the king who reunified the country
and built the stable administrative and economic base on which his Tuthmoside
successors raised the empire. CAH cautions against the cliché that he bequeathed a
"war-hungry army destined for conquest" — the evidence shows his priority was
**settled recovery**, not expansion. The militarized, outward-facing Egypt was the
work of his successors building on his foundation.

## Historiography

Known mainly from the **tomb-biographies** of the two El-Kab soldiers (Ahmose son of
Ibana and Ahmose-Pennekheb), his Karnak and Abydos stelae, Queen Ahhotep's burial,
and Manetho. His dates rest on the Ebers-Papyrus astronomical anchor for his son
Amenophis I. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
