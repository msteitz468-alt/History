# John Bale

English clergyman, playwright, and Protestant propagandist (1495–1563). Bale succeeded John Leland as England's foremost antiquary after Leland's permanent insanity around 1545. He was an ardent evangelist whose historical writings were explicitly in service of the English Reformation.

## Role in the Druid Revival

Bale produced the first "genuine" extended appearance of Druids in English print, in a 1546 publication that combined the pseudo-histories of Annius of Viterbo and medieval sources with the invective style of Hebrew prophets. In this account, Druids had arrived in Britain from Athens, lived as chaste hermits in the forest, won popular respect, been put in charge of sacrifices — and then proceeded to feast on the offerings while keeping jewel-bedecked whores. He cited Hector Boece's view that they had worshipped one god, but immediately turned against them by deploying Pliny's condemnation of them as evil sorcerers. The result was a lurid vilification in which Druids functioned as exemplars of everything wrong with both pagan priests and the Roman Catholic clergy Bale despised ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

Later, in exile on the Continent, Bale absorbed enough of the favourable Franco-German representations of Druids as noble predecessors of Christian culture to reverse himself. He published a recognition of their "precocious contribution to British learning" based on the genealogies propagated by Annius of Viterbo, according to which the legendary King Druiyus had founded the Druid order to propagate wisdom and monotheism. Bale never produced an extended eulogy to compensate for his earlier attack, and his inconsistency set a pattern for English writers throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries who found it impossible to agree on how to evaluate the ancient Druids.

Bale had read the unpublished manuscript of his friend John Leland, which placed Druids at the head of the tradition of British learning; Leland's insanity before publication left Bale as the most prominent disseminator of this idea in mid-Tudor England, even if Bale's own treatment was contradictory.

## Historiography

Hutton uses Bale primarily as an illustration of the incoherence of early English responses to Druids and the decisive influence of confessional identity on antiquarian interpretation. His hostility to Druids was driven by Protestant polemic (Druids as Catholic analogues); his eventual partial rehabilitation of them was driven by national and Reformationist pride. The fact that his first account was vilification followed by uncertain retraction, rather than the other way around, meant that the first English-language portrait of Druids to circulate was a deeply negative one — a contrast with the admiring traditions that continental authors had established simultaneously.
