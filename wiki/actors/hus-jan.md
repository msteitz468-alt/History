# Jan Hus

Czech priest, university master and reformer whose execution at the Council of Constance (1415)
ignited the **Hussite revolution**. Treated by John Klassen in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]]
(Ch. 18). Hus's call for the moral reform of the Church, grounded in the ideas of the Englishman
**John Wyclif**, fused with Czech national feeling to produce the most important "people's" movement
of the fifteenth century — and a century-long Bohemian challenge to the unity of Latin Christendom.

## Role and Teaching

A popular preacher (at the Bethlehem Chapel) and rector of the University of Prague, Hus set out his
programme in *De ecclesia* (1413), strongly influenced by Wyclif. He defined the **Church as the
congregation of the predestined** under the rule of Christ — so that an unworthy pope or prelate,
known "by their fruits," need not be obeyed. He preached a standard penitential piety but attacked
clerical wealth, simony and pomp, urged that **the king and nobility reform the Church and strip it
of its secular dominion** (Wyclif's idea), and held that ecclesiastical power was purely spiritual,
coercion belonging to the secular ruler alone. These positions, Klassen stresses, in effect "made
the people sovereign" — the foundation on which the Hussites would later claim the right to choose
their own king and Tabor built its republic.

Hus also advanced the **Czech vernacular**: he promoted Czech (and German) Bible translation, and
the *Orthographia Bohemica* attributed to him simplified Czech spelling (introducing diacritics like
*č*), making books cheaper and more accessible. He even "opened the door for women to participate
publicly in national life" (his tract "The Daughter").

## Major Decisions and Consequences

- **The Kutná Hora decree (1409).** In the dispute over the Council of Pisa, King Wenceslas IV gave
  control of the University of Prague to the Czech masters over their German opponents — a victory
  that sharpened the **ethnic and reforming** dimension of the movement.
- **Excommunication and exile (1411–12).** Summoned to the curia and anathematised over the
  indulgences controversy, Hus left Prague to preach and write in Czech across the south, "preparing
  broad social support for reform."
- **Constance and execution (1415).** Travelling to the Council under Sigismund's safe-conduct to
  defend his cause, he was condemned and **burned as a heretic on 6 July 1415**. (Around the same
  time Jakoubek of Stříbro introduced **communion in both kinds** — the *chalice* (Utraquism)
  becoming the symbol of the movement.)

## Counterfactual Significance

Hus's death, far more than his life, was decisive: it "transformed a localised movement for religious
renewal into a quasi-national revolution," estranging Bohemia from the Empire for a generation and
making martyrdom the rallying point of the [[hussite-wars-1419|Hussite Wars]]. A century before
Luther, Hus prefigured the Reformation; the Czechs' wounded pride at his condemnation, contemporaries
noted, "foreshadowed the devotion of Germans to Luther."
