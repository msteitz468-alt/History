# Muisca (Chibcha)

## Overview

The **Muisca** (Chibcha-speaking) peoples of the high plateau of the Colombian Eastern
Cordillera were the largest and most complex of the South American
**[[senorios-naturales|señoríos naturales]]** outside the central Andes — the principal case
in Juan and Judith Villamarín's chapter on chiefdoms in
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Volume III]]. At contact they were organized
into two large paramount confederations, the **Zipa** (Bogotá) and the **Zaque** (Tunja),
plus smaller polities, with ranked elites, intensive agriculture, salt and emerald production,
and active regional trade — but without the bureaucratic apparatus of a true state.

Muisca gold-working and ritual (the **El Dorado** ceremony at Lake Guatavita) drew Spanish
attention; Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada conquered the Muisca heartland in **1537**, founding
Bogotá.

## Significance

The Muisca exemplify Villamarín's argument that **chiefdoms were the prevalent form of complex
organization** in South America and that they **persisted** as colonial *cacicazgos*. They show
that dense, ranked, tribute-taking societies existed well beyond the Inka and Aztec imperial
cores.
