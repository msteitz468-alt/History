## Overview

The **'ulama** (sing. *'alim*; literally "the learned") were the scholars who commanded recognized expertise in the Islamic religious sciences — above all in [[hadith]], [[fiqh]], and the [[sharia|Shari'ah]] — and who therefore exercised religious authority in Muslim societies without holding formal clerical office. They are sometimes called "the heirs of the Prophet" in Islamic tradition: "The 'ulama' were the true 'heirs of the Prophet' and law was to be established by fatwa issued by a private learned man, rather than by the decree of the caliph or his agents" (Hodgson).

The 'ulama constituted a decentralized, self-organizing institution: there was no pope, no council, no hierarchy in the Christian sense. Authority within the community of scholars was established by the autonomous process of mutual recognition — each generation of 'ulama recognizing the reliability of the previous generation through the same isnad-criticism that they applied to hadith transmitters. "The recognition and acceptance took place without recourse to either hierarchical authority or councils or any other human instrument that would come between the individual human critic and his conscience" (Hodgson).

## Formation — From Piety-Minded Factions to Scholarly Class

The 'ulama crystallized from the Piety-minded (ahl al-taqwa) opposition factions of Marwani and early 'Abbasid Islam. These circles had been engaged in transmitting and elaborating hadith reports, developing local legal practice, and pushing for conformity of Muslim communal life to the Qur'anic ideal. Under the [[abbasid-caliphate|'Abbasids]], as the systematic disciplines of fiqh and isnad criticism developed, these loose circles gradually coalesced into a recognizable scholarly class with shared methods, recognized authorities, and institutional roles.

Key roles performed by 'ulama:

- **Mufti** (jurisconsultant): delivered fatwas (advisory legal opinions) to private inquirers on points of Shari'ah.
- **Qadi** (judge): presided over Shari'ah courts; appointed by the governor but expected to follow his madhhab's fiqh.
- **Muhtasib** (market inspector): oversaw public morals and commercial norms according to Shari'ah standards.
- **Imam**: led congregational prayer; as model for the congregation rather than intermediary between God and worshipper.
- **Hadith collector and critic**: evaluated transmitter chains, compiled canonical collections.
- **Legal theorist (faqih)**: elaborated fiqh from the four roots.
- **Historian**: wrote biography (sira) of the Prophet and universal histories using isnad methods.

## Structural Position: The 'Clerical Class' vs. the 'Political Class'

Hodgson identifies the crucial structural distinction that the 'ulama came to embody: a "clerical class" of men of religious learning versus a "political class" of administrators, soldiers, and tax-collectors. This was not, Hodgson emphasizes, a religious-secular distinction: both classes operated within an Islamic framework. But the 'ulama claimed jurisdiction over law, religion, and conscience; the political class wielded coercion and administered territory.

> "The clerical class led the prayer in the mosque, decided cases of law in the market, and declared the Muslim's duty on the frontier; the political class — the police, the tax-collectors, and all the various katibs, secretaries — saw to keeping the mosque in good condition, preserved order in the market, and commanded the defence of the frontier." (Hodgson)

The 'ulama vested legal authority in *all* possessors of 'ilm (learning), not in the caliph: "so far as possible, they vested legal authority in all the possessors of knowledge, 'ilm, literally the 'ulama', who were in turn distinguished as more or less sound by the autonomous working of a process of mutual recognition within any present generation" (Hodgson). This gave the 'ulama structural independence from the caliphate, a position the Sunni [[sunni-islam|Jama'i-Sunni]] 'ulama maintained even while accepting the 'Abbasid caliph's formal political legitimacy.

## 'Ulama and Hadith Criticism

The 'ulama were the practitioners of *'ilm al-rijal* (knowledge of the transmitters), the discipline of evaluating each hadith transmitter's reliability. This created a self-policing community: men with reputations for improper doctrine could be ruled out as transmitters; the isnad chains of dubious reports were challenged. The resulting canonical corpus was not historically accurate in any strict sense but was "self-consistent" — a body of doctrine that the Jama'i-Sunni scholarly community could recognize and accept as normative.

## The 'Ulama and Fiqh Schools (Madhhabs)

The [[fiqh|fiqh]] schools (madhhabs) organized the 'ulama by loyalty to a founding master and a methodological tradition: Hanafis, Malikis, Shafi'is, Hanbalis among Sunnis; Jafaris and Zaydis among Shi'is. Each Muslim was expected to follow one madhhab school unless of sufficient learning to reason independently (as did al-Tabari, who developed his own fiqh). In practice, the qadi appointed by a governor ruled by his own madhhab; the mufti gave opinions in his madhhab; personal ritual was governed by one's chosen school.

## The 'Ulama and the Limits of Caliphal Power

A significant consequence of 'ulama authority was structural restraint on caliphal power. The 'Abbasid caliph could not claim to determine the Shari'ah: he had to work through qadis who followed scholarly fiqh. "Even where his personal interventions and his appointments made a difference, the Shari'ah and the opinion of the Muslims in support of it could be expected to keep his appointees within bounds" (Hodgson). The 'ulama "gradually made a theoretical place for the court. But it was grudging and tardy."

The **Mihna** (833–848) — the 'Abbasid caliphal inquisition that compelled 'ulama to affirm the createdness of the Qur'an under al-Ma'mun and his successors — was a direct attempt to assert caliphal authority over doctrine. The resistance of **Ahmad Ibn Hanbal** (780–855), who endured imprisonment rather than recant, became a defining moment for the 'ulama's claim to doctrinal independence. After al-Mutawakkil abandoned the Mihna (848), the Hadith folk and their descendants effectively won the contest.

## Social Class and the 'Ulama

By late 'Abbasid times, the 'ulama were not a uniform class. Hodgson notes that the Hadith folk found their chief popular following among the lower and middle urban classes of Baghdad, especially artisans and common soldiers who identified with the Jama'i-Sunni communal tradition; the more sophisticated Mu'tazilis tended to appeal to a smaller intellectual elite; the Shi'i 'ulama drew disproportionately from the upper bourgeoisie and merchant class. The 'ulama were never a hereditary clerical caste but were reproduced through the informal master-pupil transmission of 'ilm — the same chain logic as the hadith isnad.

## Historiography

The 'ulama are extensively studied but often reduced to either a "conservative" foil for philosophy and mysticism or treated as a direct analog of Christian clergy. Hodgson resists both reductions. The 'ulama were not a "religious institution" in the narrow sense — they presided over what was, in their own self-understanding, the totality of Muslim communal life, not a segregated "religious" sphere. The collection's coverage is strong through Hodgson and the Cambridge Islamic History series; weak on comparative sociology of the 'ulama across regions and periods.
