# Richard Feynman

**Summary & detailed biography:** [[hubs/biographies/world-wars-era/feynman-richard|Detailed analytic biography]]

Physicist, Nobel laureate (1965), Manhattan Project contributor, inventor of Feynman diagrams, legendary teacher, and public embodiment of irreverent, pragmatic scientific genius. Born in Far Rockaway, New York; educated at MIT and Princeton; key participant at Los Alamos; long career at Caltech. His visual, calculational approach to quantum field theory resolved the postwar crisis in quantum electrodynamics and became standard tools across particle physics. Later famous for the *Feynman Lectures on Physics*, his autobiographical books, drumming, safe-cracking anecdotes, and blunt testimony on the Challenger O-ring failure (“nature cannot be fooled”).

## Narrative

Feynman’s life spans the birth of the nuclear age and the maturation of particle physics. At Los Alamos he was simultaneously a brilliant young theorist (efficiency calculations, predetonation theory), organizer of the first large-scale human + machine computing enterprise, and irrepressible safecracker / lock-picker who lightened the oppressive secrecy culture. After the war, struggling with the infinities plaguing quantum electrodynamics, he developed a radically new pictorial and path-integral formalism that made calculations tractable; the diagrams that bear his name (popularized and rigorized by Freeman Dyson) transformed the field. At Caltech he became an inspirational (if impatient) teacher whose three-volume red books reshaped how generations learned physics. In 1986 he served on the Rogers Commission investigating the Challenger disaster, using a simple glass of ice water to demonstrate the temperature dependence of the O-rings and publicly insisting on statistical honesty in engineering risk assessment.

## Causal Analysis

Feynman’s scientific style was forged in a radio-tinkering childhood that taught him to see mechanisms and diagrams as maps of function, reinforced by MIT’s practical ethos and Princeton’s theoretical intensity (especially John Wheeler’s influence on action principles). Los Alamos gave him both the highest-stakes applied problem and a model of collaborative, high-pressure science. The postwar “crisis” in QED was solved through his willingness to ignore conventional formalisms and invent new visual/procedural tools that worked. His later public role flowed from the same traits: contempt for bureaucracy and public relations when they conflicted with physical reality.

## Consequence Analysis

Feynman diagrams and the associated rules became (and remain) the universal language of particle physics calculations. The *Feynman Lectures* raised the standard for conceptual clarity in physics teaching. His Los Alamos experience helped shape the postwar model of large-scale scientific projects. His Challenger performance remains a canonical example of a scientist speaking truth to power inside a commission. Culturally, Feynman became the archetype of the “cool” physicist — playful, anti-authoritarian, deeply serious about nature — that influenced public perception of science for decades.

## Historiography

[[sources/gleick-genius-1992|Gleick, Genius (1992)]] is the standard single-volume life, strong on personality and scientific style. Feynman’s own *Surely You’re Joking, Mr. Feynman* and *What Do You Care What Other People Think?* supply primary voice (with caution for self-mythologizing). Technical histories of QED (Schweber, Dyson’s own accounts, later particle-physics surveys) provide the detailed reconstruction of the 1947–49 breakthrough. The Challenger episode is documented in the Rogers Commission report and Feynman’s appendix. Debates continue over the exact balance between Feynman’s and Schwinger’s contributions, the degree to which diagrams were heuristic versus rigorously grounded at first presentation, and how much of the “Feynman legend” was cultivated by the man himself.
