# Luis Carrero Blanco (1904–1973)

Naval officer and [[franco-francisco|Franco]]'s indispensable political deputy for over three decades — Subsecretary of the Presidency from 1941, effectively the regime's chief of staff, sponsor of the [[opus-dei|Opus Dei]] technocrats who engineered the 1957 government change and the 1959 Stabilization Plan, and architect of the "continuismo" strategy: institutionalizing the regime around the monarchy of [[juan-carlos-i|Juan Carlos]] (Law of Succession operation, 1969). Named the regime's first separate Prime Minister in June 1973, he was assassinated by an ETA bomb in Madrid on 20 December 1973.

## Significance

Payne (1987) presents Carrero as the regime's most consistent ideologue of National-Catholic authoritarian continuity and his assassination as the single event that most damaged the prospect of a Francoism-after-Franco — a genuine counterfactual hinge: with Carrero alive, the managed continuation of the regime past 1975 was at least plausible; without him, the aged Franco's system drifted under Arias Navarro toward the transition.

## Detail (Payne 1987)

An introvert convinced the world was run by the "three internationals" — Communism, Socialism, Masonry — who held it "preferable for all his descendants to die in an atomic war than survive as slaves of the Soviet Union"; ultra-Catholic and strongly anti-Semitic in his early writings (*España en el mar*, 1941, framed the Civil War as part of "the struggle between Christianity and Judaism"), co-author with Franco of pseudonymous anti-Masonic articles ("Ginés de Buitrago"). Key interventions: tipping Franco to dismiss [[serrano-suner-ramon|Serrano Suñer]] (1942); the monarchist memoranda of 1942–45, including the September 1945 insight that seven-year-old Juan Carlos "might be trained and educated as a more appropriate heir" than Don Juan; the post-1945 doctrine of "order, unity, and staying the course [aguantar]"; cabinet rank (1951), sponsorship of the technocrats (1957) — although in late 1957 he himself had urged intensified, "almost Stalinesque" autarky before conceding to the economists; vice-president (1967); the 1969 succession campaign; prime minister 8 June 1973. ETA's tunnel bomb (20 December 1973) blew his car over a Jesuit monastery roof; the assassins aimed "to break the rhythm of evolution of the Spanish state." He died with an estate of a pension, an unpaid apartment, and under 500,000 pesetas — austere to the end. At the burial ultras jeered Cardinal Tarancón ("¡Tarancón al paredón!") and Franco wept openly.
