# Erich Ludendorff

German general and de facto military dictator of Germany in the later years of World War I (with Hindenburg). Mastermind of the 1918 Spring Offensives; advocate of unrestricted submarine warfare and total economic mobilization. After defeat, a leading proponent of the "stab-in-the-back" legend, participant in the Kapp Putsch, and early associate (then rival) of Hitler.

## Significance
Emblematic of the "silent dictatorship" of the German high command, the radicalization of warfare in 1914–1918, and the toxic militarist politics of the Weimar era that helped undermine the republic.

See: [[first-world-war-1914]], [[world-war-ii-1939-1945]] (roots), [[hindenburg-paul-von]].

## Shirer (1960) Additions

- Ludendorff fled to Sweden at war's end in false whiskers and blue spectacles; he wrote his wife (per her memoir, quoted by Shirer): "It would be the greatest stupidity for the revolutionaries to allow us all to remain alive... if ever I come to power again there will be no pardon. Then with an easy conscience, I would have Ebert, Scheidemann and Co. hanged, and watch them dangle."
- Co-led the National Socialist German Freedom Movement with Gregor Strasser during Hitler's imprisonment and the Nazi Party's post-putsch ban; had broken with Hitler by 1925 and was absent from the 27 February 1925 relaunch meeting.
- At the [[beer-hall-putsch-1923|Beer Hall Putsch]] (8–9 November 1923): joined Hitler's side after being fetched by Scheubner-Richter and kept ignorant of the plot beforehand; on 9 November, during the march to the Odeonsplatz, Ludendorff alone walked upright through the police volley rather than dropping to the pavement, and was arrested on the spot, while Hitler (per contested trial testimony from Nazi physician Dr. Walther Schulz and other witnesses) fled the scene with a dislocated shoulder.
- At the Beer Hall Putsch trial (1924), Ludendorff was acquitted while Hitler and others were convicted; afterward he vowed never again to wear a German officer's uniform, out of bitterness at the Army's failure to rally to him — the definitive documentation, on Shirer's account, of Ludendorff's post-putsch estrangement from both the Army and (eventually) Hitler's own movement.
