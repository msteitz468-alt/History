# Josiah

**Josiah** (Hebrew *Yoshiyahu*; reigned **641/640–609 BCE**) was the last great king of
[[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]] and the author of its most sweeping **religious reform** —
the purge of pagan cult and the **concentration of all worship in the Jerusalem
Temple**, catalysed by the discovery of the **Book of the Law** (probably an early form
of **Deuteronomy**). Exploiting the collapse of [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]], he
briefly **reannexed the former northern kingdom**, only to die at **[[battle-of-megiddo-609bce|
Megiddo (609)]]** confronting Pharaoh Necho. He is treated by [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|
CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 30, T. C. Mitchell).

## Accession and early reform

Made king at **eight years old** by "the people of the land" after his father **Amon**
was assassinated (Amon and his father **Manasseh** had restored the pagan cults that
Hezekiah had purged), Josiah grew up among faithful Yahwists — his kinsman the prophet
**Zephaniah**, and from his thirteenth year the priest-prophet **Jeremiah**. Chronicles
dates the start of his **purge of cult platforms, pillars, Baal-altars, and idols** to
his **twelfth year (629/628)**, well before the book was found.

## The Book of the Law (623/622)

In his eighteenth year, during repairs to the Temple, the high priest **Hilkiah found
"the Book of the Law"** (*sēper hattôrâ*), taken to the king by the scribe Shaphan.
Since antiquity it has been identified with some form of **Deuteronomy** — a document
in the form of an ancient Near Eastern **covenant/treaty**, of a length readable aloud
in a day. Its reading showed Josiah that the nation had let the covenant with Yahweh
**lapse through ignorance**; the prophetess **Huldah** confirmed a coming judgement
deferred past Josiah's lifetime. Josiah summoned the people to the Temple, read the
book aloud, and led a **covenant renewal**.

## The reform and the Passover

The reform that followed was radical and centralizing: Josiah purged the Temple of all
pagan equipment, destroyed the **sun-horses and sun-chariot**, demolished the houses of
sacred prostitutes, **defiled the *tophet* fire-pits in the Valley of Hinnom**, and
abolished the cult platforms and their priests throughout Judah — and, as Assyrian power
collapsed, **in the former northern kingdom too**, destroying [[kingdom-of-israel|
Jeroboam's]] ancient sanctuary at **Bethel**. It culminated in a **Passover** unlike any
since before the monarchy. The aim — **all official worship concentrated in Jerusalem** —
is the program also set out in Deuteronomy; Hezekiah had attempted it a century earlier.
Archaeologically the reform may be marked by the **elimination of the sanctuary at Arad**,
walled over in level VI.

## Annexation of the north and death at Megiddo

The **fall of Assyria** (Ashur died 627/626; Babylonia broke away under
[[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]]) let Josiah **annex the provinces of Samaria, Megiddo, and
Gilead** — restoring something of the Davidic kingdom. Evidence includes the fortress at
Megiddo (level II) and the **Mesad Hashavyahu ostracon** (a labourer's appeal to a Judaean
*śar*, with Greek mercenaries in the garrison). The end came in **609**: when Pharaoh
**Necho II** marched north to prop up the Assyrian remnant at Harran against Babylon,
Josiah **intercepted him at Megiddo** and was killed by a bowshot, carried dying to
Jerusalem. Jeremiah lamented him. See **[[battle-of-megiddo-609bce]]**.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the apostasy of [[kingdom-of-judah|Manasseh and Amon]], which the reform
reversed; the **collapse of [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]]**, which freed Judah to act
and to expand north.
produced: the **Deuteronomic reform** and, in modern scholarship, the impetus behind the
**Deuteronomistic History** (Joshua–Kings) — the theological framing of the entire
biblical narrative of the monarchy.
contributed_to: by his death, the **Egyptian then Babylonian domination of Judah** that
led within a generation to the [[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|fall of Jerusalem]].

## Counterfactual significance

Josiah's reform is, in much modern scholarship, the **decisive moment in the formation
of biblical religion** — the centralization on Jerusalem and the elevation of a written
*tôrâ* over local cult. His sudden death at Megiddo, with the judgement Huldah had
deferred now falling on his sons, is the hinge from independent Judah to the catastrophe
of 587.

## Historiography

Known from the parallel but not identical accounts of **Kings and Chronicles** (Mitchell
argues a block of II Kings 23 is displaced and belongs earlier), the prophetic books of
**Jeremiah and Zephaniah**, and archaeology (Arad, Megiddo, Mesad Hashavyahu). The
central scholarly questions are the **date and extent of Deuteronomy** (from Mosaic to
post-exilic) and how far the "discovery" was genuine; Mitchell treats the finders as
honest men and the book as a genuinely old document, not a pious fraud planted to be
found. See [[hebrew-prophets]] for the prophetic context and [[babylonian-exile]] for the
sequel.
