Clovis is an archaeological culture — a shared material-culture tradition rather
than a single people, polity, or ethnic group — defined by a distinctive fluted
stone projectile point. It is the earliest archaeologically distinct Paleoindian
culture firmly recognized across the Americas, and for decades was treated as
the founding population of the New World (the "Clovis-first" model). It is filed
as an `actor` because in the wiki's terms it is a coherent agent whose behavior
(hunting, knapping, mobility, possible role in extinctions) is the subject of
analysis.

## Defining Features

- **Fluted points**: bifacially worked, lanceolate, typically ~10 cm long, made
  from fine-grained cryptocrystalline stone, with one or more long "flute"
  flakes removed from the base. Hafted to spear shafts and delivered with an
  atlatl (spear-thrower). Clovis and its successor **Folsom** are the only two
  cultures in all of human prehistory known to have fluted their points.
- **Outré passé (overshot) flaking**: a difficult bifacial-thinning technique
  also seen in the European Solutrean — almost certainly convergent (see the
  rejected Solutrean hypothesis on [[colonization-americas-14500bp]]).
- **Highly mobile toolkit**: bifaces, finely retouched scrapers/gravers/knives,
  cylindrical beveled bone rods, and **caches** (e.g. Wenatchee, Simon, Anzick,
  East Wenatchee) of oversized bifaces — some so large they may be ritual or
  funerary rather than utilitarian (the Anzick site is a Clovis child burial).
- **Stone sourced hundreds of km away**, and an absence of heavy grinding stones
  — signatures of a small, fast-moving foraging population processing little
  plant food.

## Chronology and Distribution

Radiocarbon dates place essentially all Clovis sites within a remarkably narrow
~600-year window (~13,500–12,800 BP), and Clovis points occur from Canada to
Mexico across the unglaciated Americas. The tight time span and vast spread are
the empirical basis for reading Clovis as a **rapid, near-continental
expansion** of a dispersed, possibly fast-growing population.

## Interpretive Significance

- **First recognized (1920s–30s)**: artifacts in undeniable association with
  extinct fauna at Folsom (1927) and Dent (1932), then the type-site at
  Blackwater Draw near Clovis, NM, proved deep human antiquity in the Americas
  and anchored the "Clovis-first" paradigm dominant from the 1930s to 1960s.
- **Status today**: Clovis is no longer regarded as the literal first culture —
  pre-Clovis sites (Monte Verde, Paisley Cave) demonstrate earlier presence —
  but it remains the "prototypical founding population of the American
  archaeological record": the first *widespread, technologically consistent*
  material culture in the unglaciated Americas. Whether it developed in situ
  from an earlier American population or was carried in remains unclear; the
  earliest Alaskan assemblages (**Nenana complex**) show no clear Clovis
  affinity.
- **Megafauna**: Clovis kill sites supply the world's clearest archaeological
  evidence of human predation on mammoths/mastodons, placing Clovis at the
  center of the [[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]] debate.

## Historiography

The Clovis record is dominated by open-air kill sites and isolated point finds,
biasing interpretation toward big-game hunting and away from plant use and
smaller prey (which preserve poorly). The "Clovis-first" debate and the
overkill debate are the two great controversies attached to this culture; see
[[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]] and [[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]].

## Additional Details (from Snow, Gonlin, and Siegel 2020)

Snow et al. (2020) date Clovis most reliably to **11,050–10,650 BCE** after calibration, with fluted points "probably com[ing] and go[ing] in less than four centuries." Clovis culture is understood to derive from Northern Paleoindian complexes in Beringia — specifically, fluting is the primary innovation distinguishing Clovis from its Northern Paleoindian predecessors in Alaska. The Dyuktai and Nenana complexes are the likely Beringian antecedents.

**Subsistence**: Clovis people were not exclusively big-game hunters. Evidence from the Gault site (Texas) shows use of birds, turtles, frogs, and antelopes. Horse remains were found with a coprolite dated to ~14,525 BP at Paisley Caves. Clovis people in the Northeast likely intercepted caribou herds. They also hunted smaller species (rabbits, hares) and gathered some plants. What is notably absent is any substantial dependence on high-cost, high-return resources (seeds requiring laborious milling, etc.) — environments had not yet stabilized enough for such adaptations.

**Labor division**: Broadly along sex and age lines, as in most historic hunter-gatherer societies. Everyone possessed a range of survival skills; the band depended on a complementary mix of individuals. Tailored skin clothing was essential for survival even in milder winters, requiring needles (bone or antler) and tanning techniques.

**Heat treatment**: Clovis knappers used heat treatment of chert to improve both workability and appearance. Heating cryptocrystalline raw material has been practiced for at least 72,000 years; the first Americans brought this knowledge with them.

**Art**: Incised stone, bone, and ivory artifacts from the Gault site and other Paleoindian sites demonstrate design and decoration. Red ochre was used. Some petroglyphs on the Colorado Plateau and in California depicting mastodons and camelids (extinct ~12,000 years) may date to the Paleoindian period.

**Spread mechanism**: Two hypotheses — (1) fluted points spread as the people making them fanned out, or (2) the technique spread independently as knappers trained others. Most archaeologists assume population spread carried Clovis points; but the brief four-century duration and widespread distribution is also consistent with technique-only diffusion.

**Controversy**: See [[clovis-first-controversy]] for the full debate about Clovis's relationship to pre-Clovis peoples, its origins, and the Solutrean hypothesis.
