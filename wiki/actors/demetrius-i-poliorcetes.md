## Role

Demetrius I "Poliorcetes" ("the Besieger," 336–283 BCE), son of
[[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus I]], was the brilliant, mercurial general whose
career bridges the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|Wars of the Successors]] and the
founding of the **[[antigonid-dynasty|Antigonid dynasty]]** of Macedon. Dashing,
extravagant, and unstable in fortune, he is the most vivid personality of the age in
the surviving tradition (largely through Plutarch's *Demetrius*, a study in the reversals
of *Tyche*).

## Decisions and Significance

- **Antigonid lieutenant**: he held Syria for his father, was defeated by
  [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy]] at Gaza (312), but recovered. In 307 he "liberated"
  **[[athens|Athens]]** from [[cassander|Cassander's]] governor
  [[demetrius-of-phalerum|Demetrius of Phalerum]], and was greeted with extravagant
  divine honours — an early, notorious instance of [[ruler-cult]].
- **Salamis and the royal title (306)**: his great naval victory over Ptolemy at
  Salamis-in-Cyprus gave the Antigonids the seas and was the occasion of
  [[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus']] (and his own) assumption of the title
  *basileus*.
- **The [[siege-of-rhodes-305|siege of Rhodes]] (305–304)**: his spectacular but failed
  siege, with the giant *helepolis* tower, won him the epithet **Poliorcetes**.
- **The 302 [[league-of-corinth|Hellenic League]]**: he refounded Philip II's league of
  Corinth as an instrument against Cassander — an Antigonid garrison held Corinth for
  sixty years.
- **After [[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]] (301)**: he survived the disaster that killed
  his father, keeping his fleet and footholds; later he **seized the throne of Macedon**
  (294–288), founding the Antigonid royal line, before being driven out by a coalition
  and finally surrendering to [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus]], in whose comfortable
  captivity he drank himself to death (283).

## Counterfactual Significance

Demetrius embodies the new model of the **spear-won, charismatic Hellenistic king**:
legitimacy through victory, spectacle, and divine honours rather than descent. Will
(CAH VII.1, ch. 4) reads his restless post-Ipsus career (301–283) as the negative
lesson of the age — the "man of the present moment ready to drop the substance for the
shadow," who failed to grasp that it was "no longer an age for vast dreams, but for
limited ambitions," and so delayed the stabilisation of Macedon by fifteen years.
Though he never held a stable kingdom in his own lifetime, his survival of Ipsus
preserved the Antigonid line, and through his son [[antigonus-ii-gonatas|Antigonus II
Gonatas]] the dynasty secured Macedon permanently (276; see [[antigonid-dynasty]]). His
Athenian cult is a key document for [[hellenistic-monarchy|Hellenistic kingship]] and
[[ruler-cult]].
