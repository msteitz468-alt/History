# Georges Clemenceau

French politician (1841–1929). "The Tiger." Prime Minister and Minister of War from 16 November 1917. Famous for "War. Nothing but war." Restored political will, quashed defeatism, and navigated high command frictions during 1917–18 crisis.

## Narrative

Assumed power 16 Nov 1917 after Painlevé government fell (first overturned by parliamentary vote during war). In ministerial declaration to Chamber (20 Nov): "Neither personal considerations, nor political passions will turn us from our duty. ... No more pacifist campaigns, no more German intrigues. Neither treason, nor half treason. War. Nothing but war." Acted swiftly: arrested Caillaux (former premier), Malvy (former interior minister); Briand under suspicion; executed spies (Bolo-Pacha, Mata Hari). Spent ~1/3 time visiting units/trenches (once 200m from enemy) to sustain soldier confidence.

Clarified political-military relations: ended closed sessions eroding ministers; invigorated War Committee (7 sessions Dec–Jan useful for strategy/coordination). Initially supported Pétain's limited offensives and defensive preparations ("I am not for an offensive... It is necessary to hold; it is necessary to endure... I support him entirely"). (Ch. 8, 402-404)

**1918 crisis leadership.** During Michael offensive, met Pétain; received pessimistic assessment and warnings of possible government move from Paris; relayed to Poincaré and Council of Ministers. Did not order "cover Paris at all costs" (Pétain acted on own priorities). At Doullens (26 Mar) and Beauvais (3 Apr) meetings supported expansion of Foch's authority (coordinator then strategic direction), despite earlier backing Pétain. "The Tiger’s eloquent and firm defense saved Foch and Pétain" from Chamber demands for relief/court-martial after Chemin des Dames (vote of confidence 377-110). Increasingly favored Foch ("Pétain, who has his faults, belongs more in the second rank than in the first"); enforced Pétain's obedience to Foch directives at War Committee (26 Jun). (Ch. 9, 455, 469-470)

Quashed defeatism while sustaining republican government at lowest point (civil war fears reported by British ambassador).

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton]]'s comparative framing: Clemenceau, "a crusty old atheist democrat, trained as a doctor," had a long parliamentary career as "a scourge of all governments" and had used the army to smash strikes as premier in 1906–09 before becoming, in 1917, the leader who most fully imposed total-war discipline on France's home front — cracking down on suspected defeatists (Malvy exiled, Caillaux imprisoned pending trial, editor Paul Bolo executed) and famously introducing his cabinet to parliament with four words instead of a policy speech: "Je fais la guerre!" ("I make war!").

At the peace conference, [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton]] adds, Clemenceau opposed Marshal Foch's push — Foch going over Clemenceau's head to lobby the conference directly — for full separation of the Rhineland from Germany, accepting instead a demilitarized (but not detached) Rhineland plus a fifteen-year Allied occupation and British and American treaties of guarantee. Those guarantees lapsed when the US Senate refused to ratify [[treaty-of-versailles-1919|Versailles]], a lapse Clemenceau's supporters regarded as a betrayal, since France's territorial concessions had been made partly in exchange for them.

## Causal Analysis

`produced_by:` political crisis (Painlevé fall amid scandals, Socialist peace efforts, German bribes) + Poincaré's choice of Clemenceau over Caillaux (suspected of negotiated peace). `contributed_to:` restoration of will after mutinies and during 1918 offensives. Support for Foch's powers `caused_by:` demonstrated crisis need for coordination + Foch's optimism vs Pétain pessimism; `preceded_by:` Supreme War Council structures.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` sustained French commitment to victory ("redoubling of France’s efforts"); enabled high command evolution (Foch's expanded role); quashed internal defeatism. `contributed_to:` Allied unity steps without collapse of republican gov. `enabled:` transition from defensive "healing" phase to 1918 counteroffensives.

## Historiography

Doughty presents Clemenceau as indispensable political will at nadir: fierce determination revived hopes; personal understanding of army (from Army Committee experience) enabled decisive command clarifications without turbulence of prior ministries. Notes his growing preference for Foch over Pétain reflected personal assessment more than pure strategic divergence. Strong on civil-military interface via archives.

**Range 7 (Ch. 10 + Conclusion, exclusive cache):** 23 Jun pressed Pershing (w/ Foch) 100 US divs by Jul 1919 (accepted goal). 5 Aug informed Foch Marshal ("Paris disengaged, Soissons and Château-Thierry reconquered by force... maneuver admirably conceived by the high command and superbly executed by incomparable leaders"). "The Tiger believed the Americans’ ineffectiveness could cost the French 'much blood.'" Blamed Pershing; questioned Foch. 1 Oct Poincaré "demand that Foch take the situation 'in hand'." 4 Oct: Wilson "Buddha"; demand Foch report Pershing shortcomings. 6 Oct Mordacq to Pétain ("If General Pershing continues along his current path, he is risking a disaster."). 7 Oct furious Foch independence + Lloyd George; "fatigue" Fr army. 8 Oct opposed new US field army. 11-12 Oct draft letter "'harsh' to Pershing... and to Foch" (Poincaré deleted "Your country commands you to command"). 21 Oct sent: "It is not necessary to be a [military] technician to understand that the immobility of our right wing is not part of your plans"; "criminal" attrition "indefinitely"; Pershing "invincible obstinacy"; Foch "the truth, the whole truth" to Wilson; "final decision" American general. Foch ignored ("Don Quixote’s charge at a windmill"). Clash: Foch peace involvement; "Your business is war, and everything that pertains to the peace... belongs to us and only us." Later "very large" contribution but "Fame is a troublesome temptress, especially when strutting reveals unattractive features." (l 464-465, 473, 490-496).

Armistice: 6 Oct Ger Wilson Fourteen Points; feared negotiated ("unthinkable to treat with the Germans as long as they occupied French or Belgian soil"). Relieved Wilson's 14 Oct military conditions. (l 496-497).

Conclusion: "War. Nothing but War" (1917) demonstrated refusal unfavorable peace/ignominious defeat. Versailles "failed to alter the fundamental strategic imbalance between France and Germany." "France’s political influence was too small to convince its allies to dismantle Germany, and its army too weak to enforce a draconian peace." (l 510-513).
