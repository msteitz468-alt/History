## Overview

The Qādiriyya is one of the oldest and most geographically widespread of all Sufi orders, founded around the death of ʿAbduʾl-Qādir al-Gīlānī (1088–1166) in Baghdad. Alongside the Suhrawardiyya, it was among "the first real Sufi fraternities" to emerge from the earlier phase of individual masters and loose circles, and it remains "influential in large areas of the Muslim world" to the present day.

## Founder

**ʿAbduʾl-Qādir al-Gīlānī** (1088–1166), from the Caspian Sea region (Gilan), was "probably the most popular saint in the Islamic world." His background was Hanbalite — he had studied Hanbalite law with Ibn ʿAqīl and received the *khirqa* from al-Mukharrimī (builder of the first Hanbalite madrasa). As a preacher in Baghdad he attracted enormous crowds through sermons and ascetic exhortation, yet his actual surviving writings contain relatively little of the lofty mystical states his later legend describes.

His honorific, **Muḥyī ad-dīn** ("reviver of religion"), derived from a legend: when he helped a destitute figure on the road and revived him, the person revealed himself to be "the religion of Islam." He is universally called **ghauth-i aʿẓam** ("the Greatest Help") and **pīr-i dast-gīr** ("the Pīr who keeps one's hand" for support). One of his most famous sayings — "My foot is on the neck of every saint" — was acknowledged even by distant masters (Abū Madyan of Tlemcen reportedly submitted upon spiritually hearing it).

ʿAbduʾl-Qādir had forty-nine sons, giving the family-based order a strong hereditary leadership structure from the outset.

## Character of the Order

Schimmel describes the Qādiriyya as a relatively broad and ecumenical order — it attracted followers across social classes and geographic regions in ways more ecstatic or class-specific orders could not. At the same time, its popular dimension incorporated significant folk practice: Gīlānī "has become the master of the jinn" in folk piety, and many haunted caves and sacred places in the Maghreb are devoted to his cult.

In the liturgical calendar, the Qādiriyya *ʿurs* (death anniversary) falls in the fourth month of the Muslim lunar year; songs and poems for this occasion are attested across the full geographic range of the order.

## Geographic Spread

- **Baghdad**: The founder's tomb in Baghdad remains a major pilgrimage site, especially for Indo-Pakistani Muslims who "often stay for weeks, silently walking around with little brooms, cleaning the sanctuary."
- **Indian Subcontinent**: Introduced in the late 14th century. By the early modern period, Sindhi popular tradition celebrated Gīlānī's spiritual realm as extending "from Istanbul to Delhi, town by town." Sir Richard Burton (ca. 1850) counted "about a hundred large trees in Sind, all called after Gīlānī" — each with a pole and flag for vow-fulfillment.
- **West Africa**: "A decisive influence in the Islamization of West Africa." The order was a primary vehicle for the spread of Islamic teaching to populations in the Sahel and Sudan regions.
- **North Africa (Maghreb)**: Numerous caves and sacred places; folk associations with control over jinn.
- **Turkey**: A Qādiriyya *tekke* (convent) inscription from 1819 in Konya attests its continued presence.

The order's broad popular appeal is reflected in naming practices: "Proper names like Ghauth Bakhsh ['gift of the help'] in Sind and Balochistan point to his popularity."

## Relation to Other Orders

The Qādiriyya was eventually superseded in some regions by the Naqshbandiyya (especially in India from ca. 1600) but never disappeared and retained strong folk roots precisely where more elite or sober orders had less traction. The beggars' order of the Heddāwa in Morocco "claims descent from the Qādiriyya and practices absolute poverty" — illustrating the range of the order's internal diversity.

## Historiography

The discrepancy between the historical Gīlānī (sober Hanbalite preacher) and the legendary saint (ghauth, master of jinn, performer of spectacular miracles) has not been adequately explained by scholarship. Schimmel notes: "A satisfactory explanation of the transition from the sober Hanbalite preacher… to the prototype of saintliness venerated all over the Muslim world is still lacking."

Walter Braune's translation of Gīlānī's main work *Futūḥ al-ghaib* (1933) provides the primary textual access in Western scholarship. J. Spencer Trimingham's *The Sufi Orders in Islam* (1971) offers institutional context.
