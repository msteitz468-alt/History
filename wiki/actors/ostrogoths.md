# Ostrogoths

**The eastern Goths**, a people whose identity crystallised between 378 and 453 in the orbit
of Attila's [[huns|Hunnic confederation]], who under [[theoderic-the-great|Theodoric]] built
the most Roman of all the barbarian kingdoms in Italy — and were then annihilated by Justinian's
reconquest. With the [[visigoths|Visigoths]], they are Geary's paired illustration that "just as
victory could be the decisive event in the creation of a people, so, too, could defeat."

## Origins: from Greuthungi to Ostrogoths

After the death of **Ermanaric** (376), the majority of the **Greuthungi** were absorbed into the
Hunnic confederation; those who served the Huns "adopted their clothing, weapons, and even the
practice of skull deformation of children." Out of this period of subordination — "between 378 and
453, when the Hunnic confederation collapsed with Attila's death — a new identity and a new name
emerged for this group: the Ostrogoths." This is [[ethnogenesis]] under Hunnic, not Roman, hegemony.

## Theodoric and the kingdom of Italy

Young **Theodoric** was sent as a hostage to Constantinople (c. 459–469, roughly ages eight to
eighteen), gaining "an intimate knowledge of the Empire and in particular of the imperial system of
government." By 485 his cooperation with the emperor **Zeno** had won him the post of *magister
militum*, the consulate, and adoption into the imperial Flavian house. In Italy he ruled through a
**dual system**: governing Romans as *Flavius Theodericus*, the emperor's representative, and his
barbarian followers through the *exercitus Gothorum*, an officially recognised Roman army.

## Failure to synthesise — and destruction

Theodoric "never synthesized Roman and Gothic societies." After his death in **526** the resulting
instability, and Italy's proximity to Constantinople, drew [[justinian|Justinian]]'s
[[gothic-war-535-554|Gothic War]]: "the ensuing twenty years of bloody conflict annihilated the
Ostrogoths and left Italy prostrate."

## Significance / Counterfactual

The Ostrogothic kingdom was the most administratively Roman of the successor states and, had
Theodoric's settlement endured, might have preserved a Romano-Gothic Italy. Its destruction — and
the exhaustion of Italy in the Gothic War — helped clear the way for the [[merovingian-franks|Frankish]]
hegemony north of the Alps and the Lombard conquest of Italy. As with the Visigoths, **Arian**
Christianity kept ruler and ruled apart, in pointed contrast to the Frankish embrace of orthodoxy.

## Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World (2020)

Jonathan Arnold's chapter in the [[sources/effros-moreira-oxford-handbook-merovingian-world-2020|Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World]] casts Theoderic as the self-styled "Roman ruler of the West" whose marriage-alliance network (his sister to [[clovis|Clovis]], a daughter to Alaric II) was meant to restrain war among the barbarian kings. His mediation failed to prevent [[battle-of-vouille-507|Vouillé]] (507), but his **counter-campaign of 508–511** — victories at Arles, Carcassonne, and Barcelona — recovered much of Gaul "for his empire," a Gothic success absent from Gregory of Tours's triumphalist narrative [REVISION]. He then governed the Visigothic kingdom as Amalaric's guardian. Ostrogothic Provence passed to the Franks in 536–537, and an Ostrogothic *and* a Frankish Italy both ended with Narses's victories by 562 (see [[merovingian-byzantine-relations]]).

## Fiscal continuity cut short (Wickham 2005)

[[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham]] treats Ostrogothic Italy as better documented than Vandal Africa and less fiscally imbalanced: even with a landed army and a shrinking capital, Italy still had to feed [[rome|Rome]] from its own territory. The *Variae* do **not** attest fiscal breakdown; Prokopios notes back-taxes under Roman reconquest (541) without need for new assessments. Pre-490 Italy had already suffered fiscal disturbance from Vandal control of African supply, but the Ostrogothic successor state might have continued long as a near-Roman regime. The hypothesis cannot be tested: [[justinian|Justinian]]’s wars (535–54) destroyed both the Ostrogoths and much of Italy; the [[lombards|Lombards]] from 568/9 inherited a **different** Italy — already war-damaged and no longer a continuous near-Roman fiscal order. Structural contrast: Vandal Africa as a more imbalanced (export-oriented) tax/land mix; Ostrogothic Italy as the most Roman fiscal successor cut short by reconquest rather than internal collapse.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

Attributed claims from [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]] (Chs. XXVI, XXXIV, XXXIX–XLIII).

- **Under the Huns.** After Hermanric’s collapse, Ostrogoths submitted; Amali later served under Attila; Gepids and Ostrogoths foremost vassals (Ardaric, Walamir).
- **Post-Adrianople remnants.** Ostrogoth remnants settled in Phrygia/Lydia under Theodosian arrangements alongside Visigoths in Thrace.
- **Theodoric (Amal).** Born near Vienna two years after Attila’s death; fourteenth in Amali descent; boy-hostage educated at Constantinople yet remained illiterate; rare Barbarian who sheathed the sword in victory’s pride; 33 years of civil government; Gothic sovereignty from Sicily to the Danube until Clovis overthrew the Western balance.
- **Military system in Italy.** Lands as military stipend; Italy as a well-regulated camp; “the Goths were instructed to spare the people, to reverence the laws… and to disclaim the barbarous license of judicial combat and private revenge.”
- **Gothic War.** Belisarius recovered Rome; Vitiges’s year-long siege; Totila revived Gothic arms then fell to Narses; “Ostrogothic nation extinguished”; Italy devastated.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

**Balkan Gothic bands and Zeno (474–91).** After Hunnic eclipse in the 450s, dominant Balkan forces were Gothic warrior bands under Theoderic the Amal and Theoderic Strabo, entangled with Zeno’s Isaurian dynasty. Although Zeno was recognized as legitimate emperor, “the story of his reign… resembled that of the Gothic chieftains as much as it did the regimes of his imperial predecessors,” until Anastasius restored a more conventional imperial order.

**Italian settlement.** ~100,000 Goths into Italy (20–25,000 fighters); settlements in the Po valley/Alpine passes, Adriatic (Ravenna, Picenum), southern Italy/Naples, Sicily, Dalmatia. Rugi joined but refused intermarriage. Beneath Cassiodoran rhetoric stood local strongmen and wartime local commanders (Pitzas, Hildebad, Sisigis). Elite bilingualism and growing intermarriage coexisted with visible Gothic distinctiveness.

**Succession crisis and Justinian’s pretext.** After Theoderic’s death (526), struggle among Amalasuntha, Athalaric, and Theodahat split Romanizing vs “barbarous” Gothic elites; both parties sought Justinian’s support without expecting direct Roman rule. Religious motive — Arian regime as affront to militant orthodoxy — joined political ones. Theodahat’s murder of Amalasuntha after Athalaric’s death gave Justinian further cause for western intervention. Mitchell’s summary judgment: “The true nature of the Ostrogothic kingdom is surely demonstrated by the events that followed Theoderic’s death… The claims that prevailed… were not dynastic but those of military prowess and authority among the Goths at large” (Vitigis, Totila).

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015|Mitchell, *A History of the Later Roman Empire* (2015)]].
