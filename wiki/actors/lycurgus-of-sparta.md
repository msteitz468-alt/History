# Lycurgus of Sparta

Legendary Spartan lawgiver to whom later tradition attributes the *eunomia* of Sparta. [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] treats Lycurgus through the lens of the [[concepts/spartan-mirage|Spartan mirage]] and the [[concepts/great-rhetra|Great Rhetra]] — as tradition, not as a recoverable Archaic biography.

## Traditional notices

By Herodotus 1.65.4 Lycurgus receives the Spartan constitution from Delphi (or, per Spartans, brings it from Crete). Diodorus and Plutarch engrain the association with the Rhetra. Plutarch’s *Life of Lycurgus* (already mirage-suffused, using Xenophon’s *Constitution of the Lacedaemonians* plus Hellenistic writers) credits him with nearly every distinctive Spartan institution: *agôgê*, common messes, iron currency, land redistribution into 9,000 equal lots, adultery regulations, etc.

## Hall’s critique

**Tyrtaeus never mentions Lycurgus** — striking given later authors’ inability to cite him despite Lycurgus’s centrality: “there are good reasons to doubt the historical existence of the legendary lawgiver.”

Fundamental Spartan constitutional elements probably date to the **early seventh century** (see Great Rhetra associated with Polydoros and Theopompus), but **attribution to Lycurgus is later invention**. New developments were cast as Lycurgan to stamp traditional authority. “The belief that Lycurgus was responsible for virtually every Spartan institution and that such institutions had persisted unchanged over several centuries is inherently unlikely.”

The 9,000 inalienable equal lots are especially suspect: Aristotle knew property inequality at Sparta; the figure of 9,000 is compromised because third-century King Agis IV proposed redistributing land into that number of lots.

## Counterfactual significance

As a **person**, largely unusable for causal history. As a **tradition**, central to how Greeks and moderns narrate Spartan exceptionalism — the principal personal face of the Spartan mirage. Without the Lycurgus package, Archaic Spartan history becomes a history of institutions, wars ([[events/messenian-wars-archaic]]), and dependency ([[concepts/helotage]]) rather than of a single founding genius.

## Historiography

Principal Hall discussion: Excursus III (mirage) and Ch.8 (Rhetra). Aristotle already distinguished lawgivers of *nomoi* from constitution-makers (Solon, Lycurgus) — a later classificatory scheme that may invent systematic lawgivers. Cross-link [[concepts/spartan-mirage]], [[concepts/great-rhetra]].

## From the Oxford History of the Classical World (1986)

**Forrest (Ch.1, [[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW 1986]])** reports the early-seventh-century tradition without reconstructing a biography: “a great lawgiver, Lycurgus, it was said, laid down the rules for a system of military training … which turned Sparta into the most efficient military power in Greece,” holding the southern Peloponnese and influencing the rest. The package Forrest attributes to that tradition includes state ownership of helots with half-produce to the Spartiate allotment-holder; perioikoi with limited domestic independence; retained dual hereditary kingship; and homoioi “equals” defined by survival of training, land, and obedience—Spartans “beginning to grope towards a definition of the citizen as a member of a society who was automatically entitled to certain rights.” Forrest thus uses Lycurgus as the **traditional name** for the system’s origin, consistent with treating the person as largely unusable for causal history while the tradition remains load-bearing for Greek self-understanding of Sparta.
