# Australian Labour Movement

The organised labour movement that emerged from colonial unionism in the 1870s–80s,
suffered decisive defeat in the Great Strikes of 1890–94, and reconstituted itself
as the world's first mass labour party movement in the early 1890s.

## Overview

The labour movement's pre-history lay in the trade unionism of the gold-rush era:
skilled tradesmen had won the eight-hour day for building workers in the 1850s.
The characteristic form of 1870s–80s unionism was narrowly focused on "the interests
of skilled workers in their own particular trade." The innovation of the late 1880s
was "new unionism" — inter-industry solidarity linking shearers, maritime workers,
carriers, and miners into coalitions capable of continent-wide industrial action.

## The 'New Unionism' and Its Defeat

The catalyst was the Jondaryan sheep-station dispute in Queensland (May 1890), when
the Queensland Shearers' Union successfully recruited maritime unionists from
Brisbane and London to boycott wool shipments, compelling the station to accept a
closed shop. This "Pyrrhic victory… galvanised employers to begin organising among
themselves": pastoral stations, shipping companies, coal mines, and commercial firms
formed employer federations, building strike funds.

**The Maritime Strike (16 August – 6 November 1890)** began when marine officers
protested their employer's refusal to allow them to join Melbourne's Trades Hall
Council. By the time it ended the strike had "spread across colonial boundaries and
industries, involving some 50,000 seamen, wharf-workers, shearers, coal and
silver-lead miners, gas-stokers and cart-drivers and at least 8,000 more in New
Zealand." Employers arranged alternative labour; colonial governments "sent police
and troops to escort non-union workers through the picket lines." The unions' funds
depleted, strikers were starved into surrender.

**The Queensland Shearers' Strike (5 January – 18 June 1891)** involved approximately
10,000 unionists. Strikers "burned paddocks and woolsheds" and some armed themselves.
The Queensland government sent soldiers to the central highlands. At Peak Downs,
pastoralist Charles Fairburn (whose family owned "40 per cent of the Queensland
wool clip") allegedly urged troops to open fire. "Some 225 unionists served gaol
terms." A second Queensland shearers' strike in 1894 and smaller actions at Broken
Hill (1892) and Newcastle (1893, 1896) all ended in defeat.

The defeats were decisive: union membership fell from "roughly one in five male
wage earners" in 1890 to "closer to one in twenty in 1896."

## Turn to Electoral Politics

"The most innovative [strategy] was their decision to participate in the formation
of political parties." Labour parties formed in the southern and eastern mainland
colonies "between November 1890 and May 1891." The NSW Labor Electoral League won
"36 out of 141 seats in the lower house to hold the balance of power after the
election of 1891." South Australian counterparts won ten lower house seats (1893);
Queensland Labor won fifteen (1893). Big employers formed National Associations in
response.

The world's first labour government — formed in Queensland in 1899 — "lacked a
parliamentary majority and lasted only a week." Western Australia and Tasmania
acquired labour parties by 1902, "by which time the Australian Labor Party (as it
became known) had also been born."

## Legislative Achievements

Working in coalition with liberal colonial governments during the late 1890s, labor
members helped pass:
- Victoria: Wages Boards (1896), setting minimum wages in sweated trades
- NSW (1896, under Reid with Labor support): *Coloured Races Restriction and
  Regulation Act*; *State Children Relief Act*; *Public Health Act*; *Coal Mining
  Regulation Act*; *Shops and Factories Act*
- NSW (1900, under Lyne): old-age pension and compulsory arbitration scheme

The goal was "civilising capitalism" — "a minimum wage, welfare payments, courts for
the arbitration of industrial disputes, ramp up protective tariffs and restrict the
entry of 'coloured' immigrants."

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the decisive employer victory in the Great Strikes, the Australian Labor
Party might never have formed as a disciplined parliamentary party — the defeats
made the parliamentary road the only viable path. Without Labor's balance-of-power
position in NSW after 1891, the colonial wage-board and arbitration reforms of the
late 1890s would likely have been weaker or absent, altering the character of the
Commonwealth's "Australian settlement."

## Historiography

The labour-history interpretation of the 1890s (Brian Fitzpatrick, Robin Gollan)
treated the strikes and party formation as the decade's defining story. Melissa
Bellanta (*CHA* Ch 9) challenges this as overly narrow. John Docker's framing
("were the nineties the cradle or graveyard of radical social change?") is cited
as capturing the ongoing scholarly debate. Bede Nairn's *Civilising Capitalism:
The Beginnings of the Australian Labor Party* (MUP, 1989) is the key institutional
history. John Rickard's *Class and Politics: NSW, Victoria and the Early
Commonwealth, 1890–1910* (ANU Press, 1976) provides the political context.
