# Douglas MacArthur

U.S. Army general. Commander of U.S. forces in the Philippines at the outbreak of war (Bataan/Corregidor defense and famous "I shall return" pledge). Led Southwest Pacific Area campaign (New Guinea, Philippines liberation). Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers during the occupation of Japan. Later commanded UN forces in the Korean War until relieved by Truman. Larger-than-life figure with strong political instincts.

See: [[world-war-ii-1939-1945]], [[battle-of-leyte-gulf-1944]].

**From Murray & Millett *A War to Be Won* (range 4 extraction - final campaigns/assessment):**
- MacArthur obsession with Manila (Luzon 1945): "As MacArthur waited for the completion of the Mindoro operation... he continued his impatient planning for the liberation of Luzon. He made clear he wanted to declare Manila liberated on his sixty-fifth birthday, 26 January 1945..." "Rational operational planning received secondary attention." "MacArthur’s intelligence experts gave him a good understanding of Yamashita’s plans, but MacArthur remained obsessed with Manila." "MacArthur personally intervened on 30 January to send a three-division charge toward the capital city." "Even as the battle for Manila raged, MacArthur diverted attention by sending the rest of the Sixth Army and part of the Eighth Army to fight the Japanese on three fronts." "MacArthur’s obsession with the city forced Krueger to fight a series of high-profile, high-risk operations around Manila Bay..." Staff underestimation (158k–195k vs actual ~267k); prohibited air strikes but accepted ~100k Filipino civilian deaths from artillery. (lines 22–24, 34–35, 117–118, 133–134, 199–201, 218–220)
- Surrender ceremony: "On 2 September, Japan’s capitulation to the Allies was officially marked by a handful of Japanese diplomats and officers on the deck of Admiral Halsey’s flagship Missouri... This final act of the Asia-Pacific war fell under the direction of the general-dramatist MacArthur, who had enlisted Halsey to stage a great naval-aerial demonstration in Tokyo Bay..." "Admiral Nimitz doing the honors for the United States. MacArthur presided with dignity and grace..." Flag from Perry’s 1853 voyage displayed. (lines 1083–1088, 1132–1133)
- Japanese war criminals (SCAP): Yamashita/Homma show trials (Manila, command responsibility for Manila atrocities/Bataan); hanged despite appeals; IMTFE: "tried and convicted 25 of 28 indicted Class A war criminals... Seven defendants died by hanging and sixteen were given life imprisonment; only two received lesser sentences. ... Tojo Hideki went to his death..." Class B/C: 5,700 tried, ~1,000 executed. Ishii Shiro (Unit 731) "bargained all their 'scientific findings' in return for amnesia"; lived free 14 years. MacArthur reforms: dissolved armed forces, women's suffrage, land reform, zaibatsu breakup. (lines 571–573)

**From West Point WWII Vol 1 (range 4 extraction - Pacific Pearl to Midway context):**
- MacArthur (Philippines defense decisions, pre-Midway, ~3155–3292): “Prewar defense plans called for the Americans to deny the use of Manila Bay to the Japanese by concentrating forces for a defensive stand on the Bataan Peninsula. However, General Douglas MacArthur, the commander of the newly established U.S. Army Forces, Far East, aimed to fight the Japanese invaders on the landing beaches on Luzon and defend the entire island. He argued successfully to the War Department that the islands could be held if more U.S. weapons and equipment were made available to the Filipino divisions then beginning to mobilize. A highly visible result of his efforts was the deployment of thirty-five B-17 heavy bombers to the islands, intended as a deterrent to Japanese aggression. Twelve of these were destroyed on the ground during the attacks of December 8, and two days later, only fourteen heavy bombers remained.” 
  - Consequence: “Over the rest of December, experienced Japanese naval pilots flying superior aircraft followed up their initial attacks and steadily whittled away at MacArthur’s surviving air force in the Philippines. When the main Japanese landings in Lingayen Gulf north of Manila began on December 22, only a handful of aircraft remained...”
- Open city and withdrawal (Dec 1941, ~3308–3310): “MacArthur declared Manila an open city and moved his headquarters to the island fortress of Corregidor at the entrance to Manila Bay. His remaining forces would withdraw to the mountainous Bataan Peninsula, fighting a phased delaying action to allow for food and supplies to move to Bataan.”
- Explicit critique of decision (Luzon, ~3570): “Contrary to prewar plans, General Douglas MacArthur attempted to repel a Japanese attack on Luzon by deploying troops to the beaches rather than concentrating on the Bataan Peninsula. The defenders were pushed back anyway, but in the retreat lost many of the supplies that had been moved out to coastal positions.”
- Later note (post-departure, ~3474, 3548–3551): “After MacArthur left for Australia... ordered MacArthur to leave the Philippines, at a time of his choosing... MacArthur departed Corregidor on March [date context].”
- Broader context from range (Midway turning point): “The twin Allied victories of Coral Sea and Midway in May and June 1942 reversed a long run of Japanese successes in the Pacific War.” “What Midway did accomplish... was to ‘put an end to Japanese offensive action’ and restore the naval balance in the Pacific. ... Midway went a long way toward redressing the balance.” “For the Allies, prewar unpreparedness, indecision, wishful thinking, and poor operational decisions added up to a series of humiliating defeats in the first half year of the Pacific War.
