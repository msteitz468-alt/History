# Pelagius

**Pelagius** (c. 350/360 – after 418), a British monk and lay ascetic whose teaching on human
nature provoked the **[[pelagianism|Pelagian controversy]]** with **[[augustine-of-hippo|Augustine]]**.
He arrived in Rome around the time Augustine reached Italy but, where Augustine returned to
Africa after four years, Pelagius remained a devout layman in Rome for over thirty, at the
center of a network of aristocratic Christian disciples.

Author of *Expositions of the Letters of St Paul* and of admired letters of moral exhortation
— above all the *Letter to Demetrias* (413), on a fourteen-year-old heiress's choice of the
veil — Pelagius taught that human nature, created good, retains full capacity for perfection:
"since perfection is possible for man, it is obligatory." He dismissed the idea that inherited
sin makes men incapable of not sinning as "quite absurd," holding that human constriction
comes from social habit, not an inherited defect, and that baptism restores full freedom of
action. He was reportedly irritated by the *Confessions*, especially Augustine's "Command
what You will, and give what You command," which seemed to him to dissolve moral effort into
divine favoritism.

Present in Rome during the **[[sack-of-rome-410|sack of 410]]**, he crossed to Africa (briefly
stopping at Hippo in Augustine's absence) and then to the East. He was examined and cleared of
heresy by the Palestinian bishops at the **Synod of Diospolis** (December 415) — partly by
disowning the more radical propositions of his disciple **Caelestius** — and circulated his
vindication widely. But the African councils of Carthage and Milevis (416) condemned his ideas
and pressed Rome; after Pope Zosimus's initial reversal, an imperial edict of Honorius (April
418) and Zosimus's *Epistula tractatoria* condemned Pelagianism empire-wide. Nothing certain is
known of Pelagius after 418. Brown's central point is that "Pelagianism as we know it... had
come into existence, but in the mind of Augustine, not of Pelagius" — the coherent heretical
system was largely Augustine's construction, linking the mild Pelagius to the combative
Caelestius and the philosophically armed **[[julian-of-eclanum|Julian of Eclanum]]**.
