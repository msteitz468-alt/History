# Thutmose I

**Thutmose I** (r. ~1504–1492 BCE) was the warrior king whose campaigns first projected
[[new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom]] power deep into both Nubia and Syria — the founder, through
his daughter [[hatshepsut|Hatshepsut]] and the Thutmoside line, of Egypt's imperial age. Not
of the Ahmosid family by blood (his mother was Seniseneb), he legitimized his rule by
claiming the kingship **directly from the gods** rather than from royal predecessors — the
first strong emphasis on the **divine-descent** ideology that the dynasty would exploit down
to Amenhotep III (see [[concepts/divine-kingship]]).

## Conqueror of Nubia and Syria

His Nubian campaign was "very likely the true death knell to Kush and its capital at
[[kerma-kingdom|Kerma]]," reaching the Fourth Cataract. His Tombos inscription is among the
most brutal of Egyptian royal texts — "the Nubian bowmen fall by the sword … their stench
floods their valleys" — and he returned with a Nubian chief "hanged head down at the front of
the boat of his Majesty." He then led an army into **Syria**, hunting elephants at Niy and
reaching the Euphrates, steering clear of direct confrontation with the **Mitanni** overlords.

**Historiographical caution:** "No document contemporary with the reign of Thutmose I mentions
this [Syrian] campaign." The accounts survive only in the tomb-biographies of the soldiers
Ahmose Pennekhbet and Ahmose son of Ibana — "built and decorated in the reign of Thutmose III
and later" — and use terminology otherwise unknown until Thutmose III's own conquests. The
conquest of the north-east in fact "did not occur until at least thirty-six years later."

## Monuments and tomb

At [[places/thebes|Karnak]] he enlarged the ambulatory and built the Fourth and Fifth Pylons.
He may have been the first king buried in the **Valley of the Kings** — his official Ineni
boasts of overseeing the excavation "in privacy; none saw, none heard" — though whether KV 20
or KV 38 was his original tomb remains disputed, and his body was later moved by Thutmose III.
