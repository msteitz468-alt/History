## Overview

Salvador Allende was the Marxist president of Chile, elected in September 1970 with a narrow plurality (36.6% to 35.3%) and inaugurated on 3 November 1970 after congressional ratification. As leader of the Popular Unity coalition, he pursued a program of nationalization and social reform, most notably the July 1971 nationalization of Chile's copper mines — a measure endorsed unanimously by the US Congress in principle but carried out by Allende without compensation to Anaconda and Kennecott, whose "excess profits" in Chile from 1955–70 the US State Department itself calculated at $744 million. His government faced sustained covert opposition from the Nixon administration from the moment of his election, including CIA funding of opposition media and civic groups and the CIA-linked assassination of army commander René Schneider in October 1970, an operation intended to prevent Allende from taking office at all. Allende died by suicide in the presidential palace, La Moneda, on 11 September 1973 as the military, led by General Augusto Pinochet, seized power in a coup years in the making. See [[chile-coup-1973]] for the coup narrative.

## Key Decisions

- Nationalized the copper industry (1971) without compensation to the US mining companies, a central irritant driving US hostility.
- Pursued Popular Unity's broader socialist economic program amid escalating CIA-funded political and economic destabilization ("make the economy scream").
- Remained in La Moneda rather than flee as the coup unfolded on 11 September 1973, dying by suicide as troops closed in.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Allende's election, the scale of US covert intervention in Chile in this period would likely not have occurred as it did; his presidency is the proximate cause of the 1970–73 Track I/Track II campaign. Absent Pinochet's 1973 coup, Chile's subsequent trajectory — including Chicago Boys economic restructuring and nearly two decades of military dictatorship — would plausibly have followed a very different, and contested, path; historians disagree on whether Popular Unity's economic program was itself unsustainable independent of US pressure.
