# Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī

## Overview

Quṭb al-Dīn Maḥmūd ibn Masʿūd al-Shīrāzī (1236–1311 CE) was a polymath who combined major contributions to Islamic theoretical astronomy with a distinguished career as a religious scholar, judge, and diplomat. He was the immediate student and collaborator of [[nasir-al-din-tusi|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]] at the [[maragha-observatory|Maragha Observatory]], and he extended and articulated the philosophical implications of his teacher's most important mathematical achievement, the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī Couple]].

Shīrāzī's career exemplifies Saliba's central argument about the relationship between science and religion in Islamic civilization (see [[science-and-religion-in-islam]]): the same man who produced original astronomical scholarship also served as a qadi (judge), issued diplomatic missions between warring Muslim states, wrote a major Quranic commentary, and became a leading hadith scholar. These were not separate careers; they were facets of a single integrated scholarly life within Islamic civilization.

## Astronomical Work

### Commentaries on Ṭūsī's Tadhkira

Shīrāzī wrote two voluminous commentaries on his teacher Ṭūsī's *al-Tadhkira fī ʿilm al-hayʾa* ("Memoir on the Science of Astronomy"), the text in which the Ṭūsī Couple was formally stated. Both commentaries contained original contributions to theoretical astronomy. Saliba notes that the first commentary was begun while Shīrāzī was still affiliated with the Maragha Observatory and simultaneously serving as judge in Sivas and Malatiya (1282).

### Mercury's Motion and the Ṭūsī Couple

Al-Ṭūsī had acknowledged in the *Tadhkira* that he had not fully solved the problem of Mercury's motion. Shīrāzī produced a solution using a double application of the Ṭūsī Couple — one of his most significant original contributions. This was "a significant step, not only because he succeeded where Ṭūsī had already declared failure" (Saliba, as cited in the tusi-couple page).

### Philosophical Implications of the Ṭūsī Couple

Shīrāzī was the first scholar to explicitly draw out the unintended philosophical implications of his teacher's theorem. The Couple demonstrated that uniform circular motion could produce oscillatory linear motion — which challenged the Aristotelian premise that linear motion and circular motion belonged to fundamentally different realms (the sublunar and the celestial, respectively). It also demonstrated that the Aristotelian requirement of a *moment of rest* between two opposing motions was not universal.

Shīrāzī articulated this through a concrete mechanical example: if a thread is passed through a hole in the bottom of a semi-spherical bowl whose circular rim varies in height above its base, and a weight is attached below while the other end of the thread is moved along the bowl's rim, the weight will oscillate up and down continuously *without pausing at the turning point*, because the driver (the hand on the rim) never stops. This variation on an earlier example by Abū al-Barakāt al-Baghdādī (d. 1152) connected the mechanical observation directly to the Couple's logic of circular motion producing linear oscillation.

> "This could be used as a proof for the absence of rest between two motions, one going up and one going down. This is obvious. And the one who asserts that there must be rest between the two motions cannot deny the possibility of such motions by the celestial bodies simply because he believes there must be rest and rest is not possible for the celestial objects." — Shīrāzī, quoted in Saliba, ch. 5

This line of argument was later taken up by Galileo — apparently through Copernicus's *De Revolutionibus* III.4, which used the same Ṭūsī Couple — to disprove the Aristotelian principle of rest between contrary motions.

## Religious and Judicial Career

Shīrāzī's religious credentials were as substantial as his astronomical ones:

- **Qadi (Judge)**: He held the position of practicing judge in Sivas and Malatiya in 1282, *while* he was still affiliated with the Maragha Observatory and *while* he was writing his first commentary on Ṭūsī's *Tadhkira*. These were simultaneous, not sequential, activities.
- **Diplomatic intermediary**: He acted as an intermediary between the Ilkhanids and the Mamluks after the Ilkhanids converted to Islam. Saliba describes this as "an exercise of his religious duty to bring peace between two warring Muslim potentates."
- **Hadith scholar**: His book *Jāmiʿ uṣūl al-ḥadīth* became a major reference work in hadith studies.
- **Quranic commentary**: His *Fatḥ al-mannān fī tafsīr al-Qurʾān* attests to wide mastery of religious disciplines.
- **Prophetic tradition**: His *Sharḥ al-sunna* (commentary on prophetic tradition) was another major religious work.

> "His religious works are as impressive as his astronomical works. Since he had become a ḥadīth scholar in his own right, his book Jāmiʿ uṣūl al-ḥadīth naturally became one of the main references for this type of religious literature at this later period." — Saliba, ch. 5

## Significance

Shīrāzī represents the deep integration of scientific and religious authority that Saliba identifies as characteristic of the hay'a tradition at its most productive. He was not a scientist who happened to have religious credentials, nor a religious figure who dabbled in astronomy; he was simultaneously a leading practitioner of both, whose dual standing was recognized by contemporaries in both domains. His articulation of the Ṭūsī Couple's philosophical consequences also makes him a link in the chain connecting Islamic astronomy to the debates that eventually contributed to the European Scientific Revolution.
