# Augustus (Gaius Octavius / Imp. Caesar Divi filius)

The founder of the **Roman Principate** — Caesar's heir, who fought his way to sole
power through the triumviral wars and then refounded the Roman state as a veiled
monarchy that endured for centuries. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 1
(Pelling, the triumviral period) and Chs. 2–3 (Crook, the reign and the system).

## The rise of Octavian (44–30 BCE)

The great-nephew and **adopted heir of [[julius-caesar|Caesar]]**, the 18-year-old
**Octavian** turned his name and Caesar's money into an army in 44, and — after the war
of Mutina — joined **[[mark-antony|Antony]]** and **Lepidus** in the
**[[second-triumvirate|Second Triumvirate]]** (43), sharing in the **proscriptions**
(the death of [[cicero|Cicero]]). At **[[battle-of-philippi-42bce|Philippi]]** (42) he
contributed little to the victory (hiding in a marsh), and drew the **hateful task of
settling veterans in Italy** — the confiscations that provoked the brutal **Perusine
War** (41–40) and Virgil's *Eclogues*.

But Pelling stresses that **control of Italy**, an embarrassment in 42, became
Octavian's "priceless asset": while Antony was committed to the East, Octavian could
pose as the **defender of Roman tradition** against eastern degeneracy. The accident of
**Calenus' death (40)** gave him Gaul and the whole West. He crushed **Sextus
Pompeius** (Naulochus, 36, with Agrippa) and stripped **Lepidus** of the triumvirate
the same year — leaving only himself and Antony.

## To sole power

Through the 30s Octavian out-politicked Antony: he won the propaganda war (over
Cleopatra, the Donations, the divorce of Octavia), battle-hardened his troops in
**Illyricum**, beautified Rome (with Agrippa), and in 32 secured the **oath of all
Italy** (*coniuratio Italiae*) and declared war on Cleopatra. **Agrippa** won
**[[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]]** (31); the **[[death-of-antony-and-cleopatra-30bce|fall
of Alexandria]]** (30) brought the deaths of Antony and Cleopatra, the annexation of
**Egypt**, and Octavian's undisputed mastery of the Roman world.

## The Principate: power, authority, achievement (30 BCE – 14 CE)

As sole ruler, Octavian constructed the **[[principate|Principate]]** — a monarchy in
Republican dress. In a **"steady return to normality"** he purged the Senate, then on
**13 January 27** "**transferred the *res publica* to the Senate and people**,"
receiving in return a vast **provincia** (the armed provinces) and the new name
**Augustus**. The near-fatal illness of **23** prompted the lasting formula: he laid
down the consulship and took **tribunicia potestas** and **imperium proconsulare
maius**. Crook (after Tacitus' *insurgere paulatim*) stresses that Augustus held
**coercive power totally from the start** — resting on a **professional, de-politicized
army** loyal to himself, **overwhelming resources** (the Ptolemaic treasure), and
universal **patronage** — and "took over the functions" of Senate, magistrates, and law
**bit by bit**. His own claim (*Res Gestae* 34) was to excel all in ***auctoritas***,
not *potestas*.

His **achievement** was to turn this into a **transmissible system**: reducing and
restocking the Senate (a property census of 1 million sesterces, 18 BCE, made it
something like a hereditary peerage), creating equestrian administrative posts and new
senatorial commissions, and emerging as **supreme judge** and head of the legal order —
"the principal formal difference between Republic and Empire." His reign (to **14 CE**)
saw the **moral/marriage legislation**, the **[[augustan-imperial-expansion|great expansion
of the empire]]** (Egypt, the Alps, the Danube, the recovery of the Parthian standards — and
the **[[battle-of-teutoburg-forest-9ce|Varian disaster]]**, 9 CE), and the anxious securing
of a **dynastic succession** — his blood
heirs dying (Marcellus, Gaius, Lucius) until he adopted his stepson **Tiberius** (4 CE)
as *collega imperii*.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Octavian's victory began, "apparent only to historical hindsight," both the **Augustan
Age** and the **Roman Empire** (Crook). Pelling's judgment on the war: his greater
**political shrewdness** and his mastery of **propaganda** "may not have won him the
war" (the military odds favoured Antony) "but it did much to win the ensuing peace."
His deeper achievement was to construct, **not from a blueprint but piecemeal**, a
**[[principate|veiled monarchy]]** stable enough to survive successors "less skilful
and less ruthless" — founding the imperial order of European history.

## Beard (SPQR, 2015)

Mary Beard frames Augustus' rise as history's most consequential personal
transformation in Roman history: from a **"brutal warlord and insurgent"**
implicated in the proscription violence to the model **"responsible elder
statesman,"** a puzzle Beard says "historians have puzzled and disagreed [over]
ever since." She argues Augustus deliberately disguised one-man rule inside
traditional republican forms — nearly all his formal powers were voted by the
Senate in conventional format, old magistracies continued to be filled, and his
preferred title **princeps** ("first citizen") and watchword **civilitas**
("we're all citizens together") underscored the camouflage. For Beard he is a
**"puzzling and contradictory revolutionary"**: radical in controlling elections
and the army, micromanaging citizens' private lives, and weaponizing literary
patronage (Virgil, Horace), yet conservative in that he "appears to have abolished
nothing," so that the regime "was no revolution in the strict sense of the word."
She reads the empire-wide flood of standardized, idealized, ageless portrait
statuary (some 250 known examples) as a deliberate political technology — an
official image that was "at best... a mask of power," bearing little resemblance
to the aging, unglamorous man of the one surviving physical description — and
treats his own *Res Gestae* as "a self-serving, partisan and often rose-tinted
piece of work" that carefully glosses over the murderous illegalities of his
early career. On the later reign, Beard notes the **Varus disaster** in Germany
(9 CE), which destroyed three legions and reportedly ended plans for further
conquest; Augustus' professionalization of the army with fixed pay and terms of
service, a military treasury (*aerarium militare*) funding veterans' pensions, and
the new **Praetorian Guard**, ending soldiers' dependence on individual generals;
the acute **succession problem** — no surviving son, and a string of dead heirs
(Marcellus, Agrippa, Gaius and Lucius) leaving Livia's son **Tiberius**; and
**Livia's** unusually prominent public role (an official image, *sacrosanctitas*).
Augustus died on 19 August 14 CE, aged 75, was deified, and the governing
framework he built "lasted for more than 200 years" — in Beard's phrase, every
later emperor "was or impersonated Augustus." See [[sources/beard-spqr-2015]].

## OHCW synthesis (Stockton; Purcell; Lyne; Wilson; Matthews)

**David Stockton** (OHCW Ch. 22) compresses the rise: born Rome Sept. 63 BCE as C. Octavius of equestrian Velitrae stock; adopted in Caesar’s will (44) as C. Julius Caesar Octavianus; Antony’s sneer that he was “a mere boy, owing everything to a name.” After Philippi, the unpopular task of settling ~100,000 veterans; Naulochus (36) and Lepidus’ fall leave West–East bipolarity; Actium (31) and Alexandria (30) end two decades of civil war. Settlements of **28/7** (*res publica* “restored,” name Augustus, charge of Gaul/Spain/Syria/Egypt) and **23** (resign consulship; lifelong tribunician power + *imperium maius*) free him from Republican equal collegiality and short tenure. Stockton rejects empty “dyarchy”: Senate rubber-stamped wishes, yet no emperor was secure without senatorial consent/acquiescence. Moral/religious programme: *pontifex maximus* (12 BCE), *divi filius*, temples to Rome and Augustus in the provinces (living Augustus never formally a god in Italy/West), Secular Games 17 BCE, adultery/marriage legislation, *curae* and 14 urban regions. Succession: Marcellus → Agrippa → forced Tiberius–Julia marriage → Gaius/Lucius dead AD 2–4 → Tiberius adopted AD 4; died AD 14 after ~45 years post-Actium. Core evaluation: army taken out of politics; mass of Italy and empire preferred peace and efficiency to aristocratic “liberty”; Elder Pliny’s “immense majesty of the Roman peace.”

**Nicholas Purcell** (Ch. 23) stresses governmental continuity under new posts: jurisdiction and exaction as the two real activities of rule; *locatio* contracting and Augustan *curae* as more hierarchy than bureaucratic novelty; ~30 legions + auxiliaries as “military rule”; *familia Caesaris* proximity without true bureaucracy; incorporation of provincial elites as Rome’s greatest “Art of Government.”

**R.O.A.M. Lyne** (Ch. 24) periodizes triumviral → first Augustan (from 27) → second Augustan (~20, heavier direct imperial hand after Maecenas fades); Maecenas’ mediation allowed [[virgil]]/[[horace]]/Propertius to decline crude epic or fulfil commissions their own way. **R.J.A. Wilson** (Ch. 32): after Actium an unprecedented building programme—eighty-two temples built/restored in one year claimed; “found Rome brick, left it marble” (Suetonius) via Luna/Carrara and Greek craftsmen; Forum of Augustus (2 BCE) and **Ara Pacis** (9 BCE) as propaganda climax (Augustus as new Aeneas). **John Matthews** (Ch. 31) notes Eusebius later reading *pax Augusta* as providential for Christian expansion after Cleopatra’s defeat.

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)

[[harris-roman-power-2016|Harris]] links Augustan expansion tightly to the internal needs of sole rule. After Actium, decision-making “changed forever”; nearly forty years of military expansion were “not simply ‘path-dependence’”: expansion “was expected of him and it added to his authority,” and “there can be little doubt that it was also intended to add to Rome’s revenues.” What was new under one-man rule was “the **big lie**… that he had conquered the Parthians… whereas in reality his representatives had made a pact with them (in 20 bc)” — freeing forces for European wars; free rein for fantasies (Horace on conquest of China). Tacitus (*Annals* 1.2) is believed: provincials were pleased Augustus eliminated the greed of republican officials; the aim was “orderly exploitation,” not saintly altruism, but financial burdens probably decreased for most communities, with more loyalty protests to “Rome and Augustus.” Organizational innovations: provincials in legions and regularized auxiliaries spreading citizenship by AD 14; *aerarium militare* (AD 6); trunk-road message system; procurators. Deathbed advice to maintain existing boundaries (*Annals* 1.11) may be structural as much as reactive to AD 6–9; many successors largely followed it. Harris does “not… minimize the tyrannous nature of the regime of Augustus,” while assigning Tiberius the decisive finishing-off of the old aristocracy and absolute monarchy (AD 16 period boundary). Empire undid republican government by recruiting impoverished soldiers and creating a capital unwilling to accept aristocratic control — “One-man rule was the logical outcome.”

## Historiography

The dominant sources are shaped by Augustus' own self-presentation — his lost
*Autobiography*, the **Res Gestae**, and the Augustan poets (Virgil, Horace) — making
him simultaneously "an end and a beginning." Modern scholarship (Syme's *Roman
Revolution*) reads behind the facade to the ruthless dynast; Pelling notes that
enthusiasm for Octavian "comes less naturally to us now." Stockton’s OHCW chapter is a mid-1980s narrative synthesis aligned with Crook’s CAH X “return to normality” reading rather than a pure Symean *Roman Revolution* frame. Harris 2016 emphasizes external–internal linkage (authority, revenue, propaganda of conquest) over pure grand-strategy or pure improvisation readings of Augustan frontiers.

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010)

Harriet I. Flower ([[flower-roman-republics-2010|*Roman Republics*]]) places the
principate on her multi-republic timeline ([[roman-republics-periodization]]): period 13
is the official triumvirate of Octavian, Lepidus, and Antony (**43–33**); after a short
transition (**33–28**) including [[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]] (31), Augustus
established the principate in **January 27**. After Sulla’s failed lawgiver project,
Romans neither debated a replacement constitution nor produced one by compromise until
**Octavian seized power by force and designed his own** — a sequence Flower contrasts
with the *nobiles* culture of deliberation. **Sulla as lawgiver/founder** is an
important precedent even for Augustus (Suetonius *Aug.* 28: wish to be called *optimi
status auctor*). Flower also notes Italian countryside insecurity lasting until the
principate despite “restoration of constitutional government,” and that golden-age Latin
literature (Virgil, Livy, Horace, Propertius) is shaped by turmoil and constitutional
change — though she argues political insecurity had already produced literary innovation
earlier (e.g. Lucilius in the 130s–120s). See [[fall-of-roman-republic]] ·
[[sullan-constitution]].

## Freeman, *Egypt, Greece, and Rome* (2014) — art and propaganda

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]]) reads the **Prima Porta statue**
(found 1863; a marble copy of a lost c. 20 BCE bronze) as a deliberate propaganda
program per **Paul Zanker's** *The Power of Images in the Age of Augustus* (1988):
it shows Augustus youthfully idealized despite his real age, breaking with the
"warts and all" republican portrait tradition in favor of idealized Greek Classical
proportions; the cuirass depicts the Parthian return of the lost legionary standards
(20 BCE); bare feet and an Eros-on-a-dolphin motif at the base signal divine descent
from Venus. Freeman similarly reads the ***Res Gestae Divi Augusti*** as "an inspired
piece of political propaganda" that became a template for later "good emperors" —
best preserved as the bilingual Monumentum Ancyranum, presenting Augustus as a
world-conqueror surpassing Alexander while claiming to have restored the Republic
(28–27 BCE). On the military settlement: legionary service was fixed at 16 years
(13 BCE, raised to 20 in 5 CE with a discharge payment), funded from 6 CE by a new
military treasury Augustus seeded himself, sustained by inheritance and sales taxes;
the Praetorian Guard was formalized as nine cohorts. On [[battle-of-teutoburg-forest-9ce|Teutoburg]]
(9 CE): Freeman notes Augustus was reportedly shaken more by this disaster than any
other event of his reign, and that his deathbed advice against further expansion held
for all but Britain (43 CE) and Dacia (105–6 CE).

## Woolf, Rome: An Empire's Story (2012)

Attributed claims from [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]].

- **Fulcrum of Roman history.** Long reign to AD 14: Republic before, only emperors after; Principate (*princeps*). Demobilized civil-war armies; regularized provincial government and military treasury; money and soldiers’ loyalty “kept firmly in Augustus’ hands.”
- **Learns from failed models.** After Actium/Alexandria (30): kills Caesarion; absorbs Egypt for soldier pay. Would not rely on Sulla’s terror/legislation alone, not dismiss legions like Pompey 62, not forgive like Caesar, not take the dictatorship and wait for daggers—“He meant to rule.”
- **Settlement 27/23 as Pompey-heir more than Caesar.** Title Augustus; vast armed province via legates; later *imperium maius* (Pompey-style); only popularis elements tribunician powers/inviolability. No formal office of emperor: accumulated determining position “through influence, persuasion, vast wealth, and the threat of overwhelming military force.”
- **What really saved the empire.** Comparative political economy: contemporaries celebrate civil peace, but Woolf: “what really saved the empire from collapse was his success in stopping expansion, and consolidating it around those institutions that were already geared to the sustainable economics of a tributary empire.” Death instruments (balance sheet of troops/taxes; *Res Gestae*) show household management of empire, coordination far beyond Gabinius’s pirate command.
- **Moral rearmament.** Civil wars explained as collective moral failure; 27 BCE shield (*virtus*, justice, mercy, *pietas*) and title Augustus frame Actium as restoring Golden Age track; *Aeneid* and authorized Roman past under monarchical family.
- **Quotes:** “The long reign of the first emperor, Augustus… is the fulcrum of Roman history.” / “He meant to rule.” / “Without ever creating a formal constitutional position of emperor, he accumulated… a determining position in the state.”
