# Augustus (Gaius Octavius / Imp. Caesar Divi filius)

The founder of the **Roman Principate** — Caesar's heir, who fought his way to sole
power through the triumviral wars and then refounded the Roman state as a veiled
monarchy. This page covers his rise as **"Octavian" to the conquest of Egypt (30
BCE)**; the **Augustan settlement and the Principate** (his power, authority, and
achievement, and his reign to 14 CE) are developed in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Chs.
2–3 (Crook) and will be expanded in the next cycle. Treated for the triumviral period
in Ch. 1 (Pelling).

## The rise of Octavian (44–30 BCE)

The great-nephew and **adopted heir of [[julius-caesar|Caesar]]**, the 18-year-old
**Octavian** turned his name and Caesar's money into an army in 44, and — after the war
of Mutina — joined **[[mark-antony|Antony]]** and **Lepidus** in the
**[[second-triumvirate|Second Triumvirate]]** (43), sharing in the **proscriptions**
(the death of [[cicero|Cicero]]). At **[[battle-of-philippi-42bce|Philippi]]** (42) he
contributed little to the victory (hiding in a marsh), and drew the **hateful task of
settling veterans in Italy** — the confiscations that provoked the brutal **Perusine
War** (41–40) and Virgil's *Eclogues*.

But Pelling stresses that **control of Italy**, an embarrassment in 42, became
Octavian's "priceless asset": while Antony was committed to the East, Octavian could
pose as the **defender of Roman tradition** against eastern degeneracy. The accident of
**Calenus' death (40)** gave him Gaul and the whole West. He crushed **Sextus
Pompeius** (Naulochus, 36, with Agrippa) and stripped **Lepidus** of the triumvirate
the same year — leaving only himself and Antony.

## To sole power

Through the 30s Octavian out-politicked Antony: he won the propaganda war (over
Cleopatra, the Donations, the divorce of Octavia), battle-hardened his troops in
**Illyricum**, beautified Rome (with Agrippa), and in 32 secured the **oath of all
Italy** (*coniuratio Italiae*) and declared war on Cleopatra. **Agrippa** won
**[[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]]** (31); the **[[death-of-antony-and-cleopatra-30bce|fall
of Alexandria]]** (30) brought the deaths of Antony and Cleopatra, the annexation of
**Egypt**, and Octavian's undisputed mastery of the Roman world.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Octavian's victory began, "apparent only to historical hindsight," both the **Augustan
Age** and the **Roman Empire** (Crook). Pelling's judgment: his greater **political
shrewdness** and his mastery of **propaganda** "may not have won him the war" (the
military odds favoured Antony) "but it did much to win the ensuing peace." His genius
lay in clothing a new monarchy in Republican forms — the work of the settlements of 27
and 23 (next cycle).

## Historiography

The dominant sources are shaped by Augustus' own self-presentation — his lost
*Autobiography*, the **Res Gestae**, and the Augustan poets (Virgil, Horace) — making
him simultaneously "an end and a beginning." Modern scholarship (Syme's *Roman
Revolution*) reads behind the facade to the ruthless dynast; Pelling notes that
enthusiasm for Octavian "comes less naturally to us now."
