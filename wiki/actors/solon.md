# Solon

**Solon** — poet, lawgiver and one of the **Seven Sages** — was the reformer who, as
"archon and mediator" of [[athens|Athens]] in 594/3 BCE, defused a revolutionary
agrarian crisis without becoming a tyrant, and laid the constitutional and legal
foundations of the Athenian state. Uniquely for an Archaic figure, **enough of his own
verse survives to reveal the man**. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]]
(Ch. 43, iv, A. Andrewes) treats him.

## The crisis and the Seisachtheia

Solon was appointed amid a "clearly revolutionary" situation, with full powers to reform
state and law. His most urgent task was **agrarian distress**: the poor were in bondage
to the rich, and the land itself "enslaved" by the **horoi** (markers of encumbrance)
set in it. At the centre were the **hektemoroi** ("sixth-parters"), cultivators bound to
pay a sixth of their produce to a noble — and liable, with their families, to be **sold
into slavery for default** (all borrowing being "on the security of personal liberty").
Andrewes argues the system originated not in money-debt (Athens had **no coinage** until
~50 years after Solon) but probably in the eighth-century reclamation of land, the noble
assigning untilled plots for a share of the crop — a low rate turned oppressive by the
later "rapacity of the rich" as silver-trade whetted greed. Solon's remedy was
**cancellation (*seisachtheia*, the "shaking-off of burdens")**: he plucked up the
horoi, **abolished the hektemoroi's dues** (freeing them on their land) and **debt-
slavery**, and even recovered Athenians sold abroad — but he **refused** the radicals'
demand to **redivide the land** (fr. 34), rejecting tyranny built on confiscation.

## The constitutional reforms

Solon's other great reform was as radical in principle: **substituting wealth for birth
as the qualification for office.** He graded citizens into four classes by **agricultural
income** — *pentakosiomedimnoi* (500 measures), *hippeis* (300–500), *zeugitai*
(200–300), and the *thetes* below — and matched offices to them (the top class held the
treasuries; the archonship the top one or two; the thetes had the **assembly and
lawcourts** only). He probably reformed weights and measures (replacing the Pheidonian
standard), and created two institutions that proved decisive:

- the **Council of Four Hundred** — a *probouleutic* council preparing business for the
  assembly, a counterweight (with the older Areopagus, his "two anchors") to keep the
  state steady;
- the **right of appeal (*ephesis*) to the Heliaea** — the assembly sitting as a court —
  from which "the great power exercised by the Athenian courts derived"; together with
  the right of a third party (*ho boulomenos*) to prosecute on a victim's behalf, this is
  the seed of popular justice.

His wide-ranging **law code** (the *axones*) — still the basis of Athenian law in the
late fifth century — covered everything from testamentary disposition (allowing a man
without sons to will his property) to the spacing of olive trees.

## The man and his choice

Solon's verse shows a moralist with "an undaunted sense of justice" — natural justice
under the protection of Zeus, concerned with "the damage a personal injury does to the
social fabric of the city as a whole." Chosen as mediator by **both sides**, he was
pressed by some to make himself **tyrant** (frs. 32–34) but **refused** on moral and
humanitarian grounds — "he would not stain his good name." Andrewes defends the choice
against critics who wished he had seized power to crush the aristocratic feuding:
repression "would not have prevented the faction breaking out again," whereas Solon's
reforms "greatly affected the character of the tyranny when it came" — [[pisistratus|
Pisistratus]] could leave the laws intact precisely because they were "those of a humane
sixth-century reformer and not those of the old aristocratic state." After legislating,
Solon travelled abroad (to Cyprus, perhaps Egypt) to escape demands for amendment.

## Causal significance

- The **defusing of the Archaic agrarian crisis** at Athens by the *seisachtheia* —
  abolishing debt-bondage and hektemorage and so removing the base for the social
  upheaval that elsewhere produced tyranny or stayed unresolved.
- The **constitutional architecture of Athens** — census classes, the Council of 400,
  the appellate Heliaea — on which Cleisthenes and the democracy would build.
- A model of **reform as an alternative to tyranny**, and (through his poems) the first
  fully individual moral-political voice in Greek history.
