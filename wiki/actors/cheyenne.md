# Cheyenne

The **Cheyennes** (Algonquian) entered the Plains in the latter half of the eighteenth century from east of the Missouri with well-documented routes; late-eighteenth-century range in western South Dakota. They became central allies of the Teton [[actors/lakota|Lakota]] and split into southern and northern bands whose destinies diverged after Sand Creek and Medicine Lodge.

## Overview

Cheyennes and Arapahos, after long hostility, allied with Tetons and together dominated the central uplands until the 1870s. In the **1820s** Cheyennes and Arapahos drove Kiowas, Kiowa-Apaches, and [[actors/comanches|Comanches]] south of the Arkansas River. Robe-trade prosperity at Fort Laramie tied Cheyenne/Arapaho/Oglala networks. After 1849 cholera, Cheyennes relaxed exogamy as a demographic response (Fowler).

Denver gold (1858) brought ~40,000 trespassers 1859–60 onto lands of ~5,000 Arapahos and Cheyennes in violation of the 1851 Fort Laramie treaty; northern bands withdrew with Cheyennes/Tetons north of the Platte. **November 1864:** Colorado volunteer militia (mostly miners determined to exterminate local Indians) attacked a camp of **peaceful Cheyennes and Arapahos ordered by the Army to camp on Sand Creek**. Aftermath: major Cheyenne–Arapaho–Comanche–Kiowa offensive into 1865, with northern bands and Teton ranks swollen by Santee refugees after the 1862 Minnesota uprising.

## Role and Structure

### Dog Soldiers after 1864–65

The 1864–65 war elevated war chiefs over intermediary peace chiefs; military societies lived apart. A new band, the **Dog Soldiers**, came into being and lived as semi-exiles from the rest of the Cheyennes, determined to retain control of the remaining buffalo ground on the southern Plains. Non-Cheyennes sometimes joined raids then returned to fort vicinity for supplies; Dog Soldiers modified Cheyenne institutions for military ends.

### Southern Cheyenne / Arapaho reservation diplomacy

Southern Arapahos sought a North Canadian River reservation in [[places/indian-territory|Indian Territory]] after 1867 treaty assignment between Arkansas and Cimarron proved unfeasible; association with hostile Cheyennes endangered them as troops did not distinguish friend/foe. **1869** Grant executive order created Cheyenne–Arapaho reservation in IT; almost all Arapahos and few Cheyennes settled and supported the agency against threatening Cheyennes. Dog Soldier raids and brutal 1868–69 Army retaliation (including against friendlies) kept turmoil; some Southern Cheyennes settled with Arapahos while **Dog Soldiers drifted north to join the Sioux** and continue the fight.

### Northern Cheyenne: Little Bighorn to Tongue River

Northern Cheyennes helped Sioux in the Powder River War 1866–67, used the Sioux Reservation between hunts after 1868, fought **1875** to keep settlers from the [[places/black-hills|Black Hills]], and were with the Sioux when they defeated General George Custer on the [[events/battle-of-little-bighorn-1876|Little Bighorn]] in 1876. That winter, Army winter-camp attacks and ~400 scouts from six nations made evasion harder; surrenders at Fort Keogh 1877 with some Cheyenne enlistment; main body of ~1,000 surrendered on the Sioux Reservation.

Main body of **1,000 Northern Cheyennes** moved to Indian Territory **1877** per the 1876 agreement; estrangement from Southern Cheyennes (separate sacred bundles; dress/habits/language shifting toward Sioux). After a year several hundred escaped north: one group reached Fort Robinson, Nebraska, seeking the Sioux Reservation—**many were massacred by the Army** before settlement and move to the Fort Keogh area late 1879; other group negotiated Fort Keogh 1879 with scout enlistment. **1884** Chester Arthur executive order created Northern Cheyenne Tongue River reservation in Montana.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Cheyenne–Lakota alliance after 1820s realignment, central-plains military balance and Sand Creek’s multi-tribal aftermath would differ; without Northern Cheyenne participation at Little Bighorn, the 1876 coalition’s scale would shrink. Structural hide-hunt and Army winter doctrine still closed free plains life.

## Historiography

Fowler’s Sand Creek and Dog Soldier passages are load-bearing ethnohistorical synthesis; full fidelity required on massacre and Fort Robinson path. Southern vs. northern split and Arapaho agency diplomacy are essential—do not treat “Cheyenne” as a single policy actor in 1867–69.
