## Overview

The Adena culture (c. 1000 BCE–400 CE) was a prehistoric Native American culture centered in the Ohio River Valley, primarily in southern Ohio and Kentucky, with outliers as far as Vermont and New Jersey. Adena people were the earliest major mound-building culture of the Eastern Woodlands, and their burial ceremonialism and regional exchange networks laid the foundation for the more elaborate [[hopewell-culture]] that succeeded and partially overlapped with them. Some conservative Adena communities persisted well into Hopewell times, continuing in northern Kentucky as late as 100 CE — two centuries after Hopewell's appearance.

## Geographic Distribution

Adena sites are concentrated in southern Ohio and parts of Kentucky and West Virginia, with outliers extending to Vermont and New Jersey. The heartland is the Ohio River Valley and adjacent interior drainages, a zone rich in hardwood forests and the native plants of the Eastern Agricultural Complex. The region was center stage for Archaic adaptations before mound-building emerged, and its ecological richness — oak, hickory, chestnut — underpinned the surplus production that supported ceremonial elaboration.

## Dating and Chronology

Prior to radiocarbon dating, Adena was assigned to the "Early Woodland" period. Radiocarbon dates now place the culture's emergence at around 1000 BCE, with the earliest settlements constructed by approximately that time. Adena overlapped chronologically with early Hopewell culture in some Ohio communities. The latest Adena burial mound activity continued to around 100 CE in Kentucky, with the broader cultural tradition fading by 400 CE. Dating precision is approximate; the relationship to Hopewell remains a subject of ongoing archaeological research.

Dating method: radiocarbon.

## Economy and Subsistence

Adena people subsisted primarily as foragers within the Eastern Woodlands, exploiting a rich array of tree nuts, game (notably white-tailed deer), freshwater mollusks, birds, and fish. By 1000 BCE, the tribal societies of the region were "enjoying unprecedented well-being, with newer technology in an environment that supported population growth and cultural elaboration." The Eastern Agricultural Complex — locally domesticated plants including squash (*Cucurbita texana*), sumpweed/marshelder (*Iva annua*), sunflower (*Helianthus annuus*), and goosefoot (*Chenopodium berlandieri*) — provided the subsistence base for the moundbuilder cultures. Hopewell subsequently made more intensive use of this complex. Adena and Hopewell people were not yet maize farmers; maize became a staple crop only after 400 CE.

## Architecture and Technology

Adena people built earthen burial mounds that evolved from simple to complex forms over the culture's lifespan. The earliest mounds were often built over the sites where circular buildings had previously stood, with post-mold patterns consistent with wigwam-style construction. Burial mounds featured two main tomb types: (1) simple clay-lined basins excavated to hold cremated remains, then covered with an earthen mound; and (2) elaborate log tombs for uncremated individuals, left accessible from the side for additional burials, eventually sealed when the log structure decayed. Mound sizes ranged enormously: Grave Creek Mound in Moundsville, West Virginia, originally stood 20 m (66 ft) high and contained 70,000 m³ of earth. Miamisburg Mound in southern Ohio was even larger. The Marietta site shows a more complex arrangement — a burial mound ("Conus") enclosed by an earthen berm and ditch, with an earthen wall defining a ceremonial precinct and a ramp providing access across the ditch.

Adena craftspeople produced pottery — early examples crude, later ones better made and decorated by simple stamping — as well as woven cloth fabrics from fiber, occasionally preserved in contact with copper artifacts whose salts protected organic materials from decay.

## Society and Identity

Adena social organization was tribal. Burial mounds served as cultural centers for a dispersed society that lacked strong land tenure or clear political boundaries. Differential burial contents reveal social stratification: the most elaborate burials, containing luxury goods of exotic imported materials, were probably reserved for local clan leaders — "big men" who had acquired status through lifelong effort. Other lavishly buried individuals were likely shamans, religious practitioners who traveled long distances to source locations for exotic materials. Burial ceremonialism was accompanied by feasting. Lavish burials signaled the prestige not only of the deceased but of the whole clan or family group.

Evidence of inequality coexists with tribal rather than chiefly organization. Many Adena individuals exhibited deliberate cranial shaping, a cosmetic alteration associated with high status or beauty in multiple prehistoric and historical cultures.

## Art and Symbolism

Adena artifact assemblages are remarkable for the range and quality of imported materials. Craft goods included: gorgets of stone, copper, and mica; freshwater pearl beads; mica cutouts; copper bracelets, rings, and beads; bird effigy atlatl weights; and engraved stone tablets. Tablets may have been used to apply dye patterns to textiles or for body decoration and tattooing. Barrel-shaped, effigy, and straight cylinder tubular pipes of fine-grained stone indicate that tobacco (*Nicotiana rustica*, not the mild modern variety) had already spread to the Eastern Woodlands, possibly via the Caribbean Antilles. Grave offerings also included red ocher, graphite, and occasionally severed trophy heads.

## Decline and Legacy

Adena culture did not abruptly end but was gradually absorbed and superseded by [[hopewell-culture]], which emerged in Illinois after 100 BCE and replaced Adena in southern Ohio perhaps a century later. Hopewell's trade network was far more geographically extensive than Adena's, and its earthwork construction was larger and more geometrically elaborate. Some Adena communities persisted alongside Hopewell for two centuries. The broader tradition of mound building that Adena pioneered shaped all subsequent Eastern Woodlands cultures, ultimately evolving through Hopewell, Fort Ancient, and into the platform mound tradition of the Mississippian period.

## Historiography

Prior to radiocarbon dating, Adena and Hopewell were classified as "Early Woodland" and "Middle Woodland" respectively, implying a simple sequential relationship. Modern research has complicated this picture: the two cultures overlapped chronologically, and Adena persisted in some areas well into Hopewell times. Scholarly debates continue about the degree to which Adena represents a single "culture" versus a shared mortuary tradition practiced by several otherwise distinct groups. The evidence for Adena is largely restricted to burial practices, creating a methodological challenge: burial ceremonialism could have been shared across culturally diverse groups. Most of what is known comes from excavated mound contexts; settlement sites and domestic contexts are far less well known. The source base is strong for the Ohio/Kentucky heartland and weak for outlier areas.
