# Jawaharlal Nehru

**Jawaharlal Nehru** (1889–1964) was [[mahatma-gandhi|Gandhi's]] chosen successor, the leader of the
[[indian-national-congress|Congress]] left, and **independent India's first Prime Minister** (1947–64)
— the architect of its secular, democratic, planned, and non-aligned republic. Documented for the wiki
through Percival Spear's [[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965).

## National leader

"Mercurial, magnetic, brilliant," Nehru emerged from the Gandhian movement as its most charismatic
modern voice — a Cambridge-educated socialist and "westerner in outlook and taste, a **secularist**" —
yet Gandhi's accepted heir. Spear shows "riper judgement" tempering his early impulsiveness by 1947,
when he broke with Gandhi to accept [[partition-of-india-1947|Partition]] as inevitable and ushered in
independence with the "**tryst with destiny**" speech.

## Building the republic

As Prime Minister, with **Sardar Patel** as his realist deputy (and, with **V. P. Menon**, the
integrator of the **362 princely states** — the velvet glove over the iron hand), Nehru oversaw:
- the **integration of the states** (the disputed cases of Junagadh, Hyderabad (1948), and **Kashmir**);
- the **Republican Constitution of 1950** — **secular**, democratic, abolishing **untouchability**,
  reviving the **panchayat** ideal, and built on the framework of the 1935 Act — which made India
  "close a chapter" and set its course;
- after Patel's death (1950), his unchallenged mastery of party and state;
- **state-led development**: the **National Planning Commission (1950)** and the **Five-Year Plans**,
  a mixed economy pursuing industrialisation and self-reliance;
- and, abroad, **non-alignment** between the Cold War blocs (a leading voice at Bandung), making India
  a champion of the decolonising world.

## Significance

Nehru gave the new nation its enduring institutional character — **parliamentary democracy, secularism,
a planned mixed economy, and non-alignment** — the "Nehruvian" settlement that defined India for a
generation. Writing in 1965 (the year after Nehru's death), Spear treats his reign as the culmination
of "the inner meaning of modern Indian history," the point at which the long encounter with the West
issued in a self-governing, modernising Indian state. (Spear's volume closes with a post-Nehru
epilogue.)
