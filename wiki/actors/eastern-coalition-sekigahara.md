## Definition and Scope

The Eastern Coalition (*Higashi-gun*) was the alliance of daimyo assembled under [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu's]] command that defeated the [[western-coalition-sekigahara|Western Coalition]] at the [[battle-of-sekigahara-1600|Battle of Sekigahara]] on October 21, 1600, establishing the foundation for the [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]].

The coalition was composed primarily of daimyo who had served together in the Korean campaigns under Hideyoshi and accumulated grievances against [[ishida-mitsunari|Ishida Mitsunari's]] style of administration. Their motivations combined personal loyalty to Ieyasu, hatred of Mitsunari, and calculation that Tokugawa dominance offered better prospects than a Toyotomi regency dominated by Ishida.

## Composition

**Commander-in-chief**: Tokugawa Ieyasu

**Vanguard and main force at Sekigahara**:
- Fukushima Masanori — veteran general, enemy of Ishida; attacked Ukita in the center
- Ii Naomasa — Ieyasu's leading general; attacked Ukita; wounded in the battle
- Honda Tadakatsu — commended after the battle for troop handling
- Hosokawa Tadaoki — attacked Ishida's wing; his defiance in the matter of his wife's death had embarrassed the Western coalition
- Kuroda Nagamasa — singled out by Ieyasu after the battle as most responsible for the victory through his intelligence work
- Todo Takatora — attacked Otani's position
- Kyogoku Takatsugu — attacked Otani
- Terazawa Hirotaka — drove in Konishi's van, breaking its formation
- Kato Yoshiaki — wounded Shima Sakon with matchlock fire early in the battle
- Togawa Michiyasu — wounded Shima Sakon
- Ikeda Terumasa — part of the vanguard force; captured Gifu before the battle
- Asano Yoshinaga, Yamanouchi Kazutoyo, Tanaka Yoshimasa, and others

**Strategic flanking forces**:
- Date Masamune and Mogami Yoshiaki — held down Uesugi Kagekatsu in the northeast, preventing him from joining the Western army
- Hideyasu (Ieyasu's son) — held Utsunomiya with 20,000 men as additional insurance
- Kuroda Josui — waged an independent parallel campaign in Kyushu against Western partisans there

**Forces that defected or stood aside (benefiting the East)**:
- Kobayakawa Hideaki — nominally Western on Matsuo Hill; attacked Otani after Ieyasu fired warning shots at him
- Kikkawa Hiroie — commanded Mori's vanguard on Nangu Hill; had privately agreed with Ieyasu to do nothing; blocked the rest of Mori's forces from engaging
- Wakizaka Yasuharu, Ogawa Suketada, Kuchigi Mototsuna — nominally Western; turned against Otani once Kobayakawa moved

## Pre-Battle Diplomatic Work

The Eastern coalition's effectiveness rested as much on Ieyasu's pre-battle intelligence and political work as on military force. Kuroda Nagamasa is identified as the key figure in negotiating with Kobayakawa and others: when asked during the battle whether Kobayakawa was coming over, Kuroda replied that he knew as little as anyone, "but if he does deceive us and throw his men against us, what of it? To cut through Ishida's men and then strike down Ukita and Kobayakawa won't take us very long." The confidence reflected prior diplomacy.

Ieyasu also worked through Hosokawa Tadaoki and Kuroda to neutralize the Maeda house, persuading Toshinaga not to support Ishida. When, at the council at Koyama, Ieyasu offered his Eastern allies — whose families were hostages in Osaka — the option of joining the West if they wished, "not unnaturally none of them did wish."

## Sekigahara Vanguard Operations

Before the main battle, the Eastern vanguard seized strategic positions on the Tokaido and Nakasendo. The most important preliminary action was the capture of Kiyosu castle (controlling the junction between the two main roads), secured by Fukushima and Ikeda before Western forces could take it. The fall of Gifu and Takegahana followed. By late September 1600 the Eastern vanguard held Akasaka, flanking Ishida at Ogaki and menacing Sawayama.

## Aftermath and Rewards

After Sekigahara, Ieyasu distributed domains in a way that strengthened the Eastern coalition's key participants while deliberately limiting the wealth of his closest *fudai* retainers (Ii, Honda, Sakai, Sakakibara) in favor of giving them strategically placed smaller fiefs. The greatest rewards went to the *tozama* allies whose loyalty had been valuable but was not fully assured:
- Kuroda Nagamasa: Chikuzen, 523,000 koku
- Fukushima Masanori: Hiroshima, 498,000 koku
- Kato Kiyomasa: increased to 520,000 at Kumamoto
- Hosokawa Tadaoki: Buzen, 369,000 koku
- Date Masamune: 605,000 (only 25,000 increase — he had been suspected of opportunism)
- Maeda Toshinaga: increased to 1,195,000 koku (the wealthiest feudal lord in Japan)

Kobayakawa was rewarded with 574,000 in Bizen for his defection, though "he did not long enjoy" it (he died in 1602). Ieyasu's key fudai retainers were deliberately kept at modest income levels but given control of administrative and communications chokepoints.
