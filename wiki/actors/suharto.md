## Overview

Suharto (1921–2008) was a general in the Indonesian army who came to power following the abortive coup of 1 October 1965 and the subsequent massacre of Indonesian Communist Party (PKI) members and supporters. He was named acting president in 1967 and president in 1968, ruling until his resignation in 1998. His regime — called the "New Order" (*Orde Baru*) — was defined by political passivity, military dominance of civilian administration (*dwifungsi*), and export-oriented economic development that transformed Indonesia's economy.

## Role and Major Decisions

**Rise to power (1965–67)**: When six generals were murdered in the coup of 1 October 1965, Suharto commanded the army's strategic reserve (Kostrad) and moved rapidly to restore order and pin responsibility on the PKI. The *Cambridge History of Southeast Asia* (Tarling, ed., Vol. 2) notes: "A relatively unknown soldier, General Suharto, was given the task of restoring security since he was in charge of Kostrad, the military's strategic reserve; he emerged as the leader opposed to Sukarno." Despite Sukarno's refusal to ban the PKI, Suharto effectively deposed him on 11 March 1966. "Meanwhile, the military allowed PKI supporters in central and east Java and Bali to be massacred by Muslims in a jihad. There were estimates of half a million to a million killed."

**New Order political structures**: Suharto's governing approach differed fundamentally from Sukarno's in its orientation toward political passivity rather than mobilization. Political parties were prohibited from organizing at the village and sub-district levels — where 80 per cent of Indonesia's population lived. The population was conceived as a "floating mass" free from party politics. To occupy the resulting political space, Suharto sponsored Golkar, a functional group organization encompassing civil servants, armed forces, intellectuals, women, youths, workers, and farmers — in effect, a state party that monopolized elections. In the 1971 elections, Golkar won 236 of 360 seats.

**Dwifungsi (dual function)**: The Indonesian military under Suharto embraced an explicit doctrine of dual function (*dwifungsi*): a defense role and a socio-political role. Military officers were systematically placed in the judiciary, executive civil administration, diplomatic corps, and major business enterprises. Provincial and local governments were opened to military officers, including village headmanships (typically filled by army sergeants or policemen). This created a patronage structure in which "benefits were allocated in exchange for control."

**Pancasila as sole principle (1987)**: In 1987, all societies including political parties were required to declare the state ideology of Pancasila as their sole guiding principle — the "de-ideologization" of Indonesian political life. Muslim parties were forced to coalesce as the Development Unity Party (PPP); the Indonesian Nationalist Party into the Indonesian Democratic Party (PDI). The result was "more internal bickering and growing ineffectiveness."

**Comparison with Sukarno**: The *Cambridge History* draws an instructive comparison. Both Sukarno and Suharto kept political parties at arm's length and both reflected a Javanese religio-cultural bias against militant Islam. But their modes differed: Sukarno sought mass mobilization for specific campaigns; Suharto sought political passivity and economic rationalization. "Suharto was deeply convinced that the New Order could be justified only by promoting orderly government, the rule of law, economic rationalization and internal consensus."

## Counterfactual Significance

Suharto's New Order delivered sustained economic growth — Indonesia became a middle-income country during his tenure — but at the cost of systematic suppression of political competition, elimination of the PKI (a mass party of several million members), and entrenchment of military patronage networks that proved difficult to dismantle after his resignation in 1998. The 1965–66 massacres, in which an estimated half-million to one million people were killed, remain among the largest mass killings of the twentieth century and are still not fully accounted for in Indonesian public life.

## Addendum: The October 1965 Countercoup and the Killings (per McCoy)

Alfred W. McCoy's *[[mccoy-cold-war-five-continents-2024|Cold War on Five Continents]]* (2024) adds detail on Suharto's countercoup and the mass killings that followed. On the night of 30 September–1 October 1965, forces under Lt. Col. Untung Syamsuri kidnapped and killed six army generals and General Nasution's five-year-old daughter, dumping the bodies in a well at Lubang Buaya. Suharto, commanding the Army Strategic Reserve and likely possessing advance warning, organized a successful countercoup by nightfall on 1 October, broadcasting a declaration that the movement "must be destroyed down to its roots." By March 1966 he had been granted sweeping "Supersemar" authority, becoming president in 1967 and ruling for the next thirty-two years.

McCoy documents that the killings of alleged PKI members and sympathizers (see [[indonesian-mass-killings-1965]]) were assisted by covert US and British involvement: the US embassy in Jakarta supplied lists of PKI leadership names to the Indonesian military (Ambassador Marshall Green cabled Washington on 20 October 1965 praising the army's "determination and organization"), while British Foreign Office official Norman Reddaway ran a black-propaganda campaign amplifying fabricated atrocity claims against the PKI-linked women's group Gerwani. Death toll estimates cited by McCoy range from roughly 500,000 to 1 million.
