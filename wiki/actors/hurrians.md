# The Hurrians

A **non-Semitic, non-Indo-European people** of the northern Fertile Crescent and
the Zagros piedmont whose long expansion across [[middle-bronze-age|Middle Bronze
Age]] Mesopotamia and Syria produced, by the 15th century, the great kingdom of
**[[mitanni|Mitanni]]** — the fourth of the Late Bronze "great powers." Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. I, Kupper).

## A second "steppe and sown" stream

Where the [[amorites|Amorites]] came out of the **Syrian steppe to the south-west**,
the Hurrians moved down from the **mountainous border to the north-east** — and the
two streams met and mingled in Upper Mesopotamia. At **Chagar Bazar** (probably
Shamshi-Adad's Shubat-Enlil), where alone the personal names can be sampled, the
population is layered: an oldest Akkadian element, then "West Semitic" (Amorite),
then an intensifying Hurrian penetration. The Hurrians were already present in the
Sargonic period; by ~1800 BCE their heaviest concentrations lay east of the Tigris,
but they controlled several principalities in Upper Mesopotamia (e.g. Burundum,
Elakhut) and had crossed the Euphrates into north Syria (Urshu, Hashshum).

## The Hurrianization of Syria

The clearest sequence comes from **Alalakh**: in the **level VII** tablets (time of
the [[babylon-first-dynasty|First Dynasty of Babylon]]) Hurrians already hold high
civil and religious office under the [[iamkhad|Amorite kings of Aleppo]], their
language is used by scribes, and their goddess **Hepat** has entered the official
cult; in **level IV** (15th c.) society is "Hurrianized in every respect," the West
Semitic element a tiny minority. The Hurrian storm-god **Teshub** (identified with
Adad) and his consort Hepat, and the divine bulls Sherri and Hurri, spread through
the religion of north Syria — statues of "the god of Aleppo and Hepat" were among
the loot **[[hittites|Hattusili I]]** carried home from Hashshum.

## Toward Mitanni

After the fall of [[mari|Mari]] "a thick veil falls over Upper Mesopotamia"; when it
lifts ~150 years later, the small Amorite principalities have been "engulfed in the
Hurrian tide" and a single Hurrian state — **[[mitanni|Mitanni]]** (Akkadian
**Khanigalbat**) — has united the whole region. It was Hurrians "from the land of
Khanigalbat" who struck **[[hittites|Hattusili I]]** in the rear and nearly destroyed
his kingdom, "the town of Hattusha alone remaining loyal." The unification was bound
up with a later, separate immigration — that of the **[[indo-european-migrations|
Indo-Aryan]]** chariot aristocracy** who supplied Mitanni's ruling class and its
horse-vocabulary, though the state and language remained fundamentally **Hurrian**
(see [[mitanni]], to be developed from the Amarna-age material).

## Significance

The Hurrians are the wiki's **second great "outsider" people** to reshape the Near
East from its northern margin — the structural counterpart to the
[[amorites|Amorites]] — and the carriers of a distinctive religion (Teshub-Hepat)
and, via Mitanni, of the **light war-chariot** into the Late Bronze power system.
Their absorption of an Indo-Aryan elite is one of the clearest ancient examples of a
small mobile aristocracy ruling a different settled population, and a key episode in
the [[indo-european-migrations|Indo-European question]].

## Historiography

Like the Amorites, the Hurrians are known largely **onomastically and from the
outside** — names in the Mari, Chagar Bazar, and Alalakh tablets, plus the Hittite
records — until the Mitannian and Nuzi archives. The **Hurrian language** (related
only to later Urartian) is imperfectly understood. CAH (1973) is careful to
distinguish the long, early **Hurrian** expansion from the later, thinner
**Indo-Aryan** element often conflated with it in the "Mitannian" label. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
