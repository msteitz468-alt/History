John Frederick Charles "Boney" Fuller (1878–1966) was a British army officer and
the foremost theorist of armored warfare to emerge from the First World War. Per
Freedman, "Fuller joined the Tank Corps in 1916, at the start of what he
immediately recognized to be a revolutionary development." Out of the stalemate he
conceived a way of winning by paralysing the enemy's command rather than destroying
its army — a "brain warfare" that made him the more original, if less accessible,
twin of [[liddell-hart-basil|Basil Liddell Hart]] and his [[indirect-approach|indirect approach]].

## Strategic Thought

Fuller's first concrete proposal was "Plan 1919," a war-winning offensive drawn up
during 1918 and "depend[ent] on a new tank coming into large-scale production the
next year." Like the airpower visionaries, "Fuller was overly optimistic about the
capabilities becoming available to support his ambition," and "the real importance
of his ideas … lay in their relevance for the conduct of future wars."

His central insight was that machines would supplant muscle. "Mechanical warfare,
he observed, was about to replace muscular warfare … The petrol engine was going
to revolutionize land warfare just as surely as steam had revolutionized naval
warfare." He envisioned "a future army of one thousand tanks, dividing for a
standard attack on enemy defensive lines and another attack directed at the enemy's
command structure."

The conceptual core was the command-paralysis idea, sharpened by the German spring
1918 offensive, which Fuller "attributed … to the paralysis of the high command."
As Freedman quotes him: "As 'the potential strength of a body of men lies in its
organization,' he concluded, 'if we can destroy this organization, we shall have
gained our object.'" Hence "Fuller became an advocate of 'brain warfare,' that is,
attacks aimed at disorganizing the enemy's mental processes and ensuring the
collapse of the enemy's will to resist. There was no need to target the enemy army;
better to target the command structure." In his organic metaphor "the headquarters
was the brain and the lines of communication the nervous system, leading into the
muscular forward forces"; the aim "was to shoot the enemy through the head rather
than force death through many wounds to the body. Literally brainless, the enemy
would be confused and its forces would turn into a rabble."

Fuller built this on idiosyncratic foundations. "Fuller had also read Le Bon
closely. His innovation was to use notions of crowd psychology to consider how not
only civilians but also armies might buckle under pressure." He "dabbled in
mysticism and the occult, had an enthusiasm for modernism and a contempt for
democracy, and eventually developed a commitment to fascism." His books *The
Reformation of War* and *The Foundations of the Science of War* dwelt on crowds and
leadership, casting the normal soldier as "a piece of machinery" for whom genius
must devise "an 'accurate system' … in 'so simple a form' that 'without thinking …
he with his hands will accomplish what our brains have devised.'"

## In Freedman's argument

Freedman sets Fuller alongside the early airpower theorists — Trenchard, Mitchell,
Douhet (see [[command-of-the-air|command of the air]] and
[[strategic-bombardment|strategic bombardment]]) — as part of a single postwar
turn: realizing "the potential of a new technology — the tank on the ground or the
airplane in the air — to break the will of the enemy," with the presumed impact
"psychological as much as physical." Fuller "had an affinity" with them; in 1923 he
wrote of an air attack turning London into "a 'vast raving Bedlam'" so that
government "would be 'swept away by an avalanche of terror.'"

Freedman's verdict is pointedly ambivalent. Fuller was a "flawed and unappealing,
arrogant and authoritarian character, whose theorizing beyond military matters was
eccentric and often barely intelligible," yet who "hit upon an original conception
of armored warfare that turned what was widely viewed as an interesting but limited
specialist tool into the basis of a new type of warfare." Crucially, "Fuller became
one of the first to focus on the possibility of disorienting the enemy's 'brain'
rather than eliminating his physical strength" — a "possible theoretical basis" for
the airpower theorists' otherwise speculative hope that the right pressure point
could bring a whole system down. Freedman also records the standing critique, via
Brian Holden Reid, that the army "was not the same as an organism because the
component parts could exist independently of each other"; "a crisis can throw up a
relatively junior officer who can provide the guidance formerly given by higher
authority."
