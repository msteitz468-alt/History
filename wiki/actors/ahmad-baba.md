# Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti

**Ahmad Baba** (1556–1627) was the most celebrated **jurist and scholar of Timbuktu** at the
height of the city's renown as a centre of Islamic learning — and, through his exile, the human
symbol of the disruption the [[moroccan-invasion-of-songhay-1591|Moroccan conquest of 1591]]
brought to the Western Sudan. Documented for the wiki through John Ralph Willis's chapter in
[[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of West Africa* Vol. I]] (1971).

## Scholar and exile

A prolific Maliki jurist of the **Sankore** scholarly milieu, Ahmad Baba was among the first
targets of the conquering Moroccans intent on silencing Timbuktu's influential *ulama*. He and his
family were **placed in chains and deported to Marrakush**, stripped of his library and
possessions. Willis is careful to read this as **political expediency, not religious persecution**:
the Saadian sultan Ahmad al-Mansur was himself an accomplished Islamic scholar and patron of
learning (his secretary al-Fishtali was a noted poet). Ahmad Baba was eventually allowed to
**return to Timbuktu (1607)**, where he resumed teaching until his death.

## Significance

Ahmad Baba personifies both the **achievement** of Sudanese Islamic scholarship — Timbuktu as a
genuine centre of jurisprudence and books, integrated into the wider learned world of the Maghrib
— and the **rupture** of 1591, after which the city never fully recovered its scholarly or
commercial pre-eminence. His writings (on law, biography, and the much-debated question of the
enslavement of Black Muslims) remain key sources, and his fate is the standard illustration of how
the [[western-sudan-arma-pashalik|Arma conquest]] dislocated the intellectual life of the Niger
cities.
