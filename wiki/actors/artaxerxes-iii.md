# Artaxerxes III Ochus

## Overview

**Artaxerxes III** (Ochus, r. 359/358–338 BCE), son of [[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes II]], is
the pivotal figure in [[sources/briant-from-cyrus-to-alexander-2002|Briant]]'s case against the
"decline" thesis. His reign **reconquered Egypt** (lost for ~60 years), crushed the **revolt of
Sidon**, and re-stabilized Cyprus and Asia Minor — demonstrating that the empire was "far from
an enfeebled organism about to collapse at the first blow" just two decades before
[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]]'s invasion.

## Role and Key Events

- **Accession.** Already proven in command (he led the army against the Egyptian pharaoh Tachos
  while his father lived), Ochus succeeded on Artaxerxes II's natural death. The hostile tradition
  (Valerius Maximus, Justin) reports a massacre of royal rivals to secure the throne — difficult
  to assess neutrally given the bias of the sources.
- **First Egyptian failure (351).** A personally-led campaign against Nectanebo II failed,
  emboldening Phoenicia and Cyprus to revolt.
- **The revolt of Sidon (c. 346).** Triggered partly by the burdens of preparing a new Egyptian
  campaign, the revolt ended when the Sidonian king Tennes betrayed the city; its citizens chose
  mass self-immolation over surrender. A Babylonian Chronicle fragment fixes Sidonian prisoners
  arriving in Babylon in October 345.
- **The reconquest of Egypt (343–342).** Where Achaemenid armies had failed for two generations,
  Artaxerxes III succeeded: a large army and fleet overcame the Sinai crossing and entered Memphis
  in 342, Nectanebo II fleeing to Nubia. Briant notes Diodorus's bias in crediting the success to
  Greek mercenaries (Mentor of Rhodes) while downplaying Persian competence — a recurring distortion.

## Counterfactual Significance

Artaxerxes III is the empirical refutation of the "rotten empire" model: a king who, on the eve of
Macedon's rise, restored the full territorial integrity of the empire and mounted sustained
large-scale offensives. That [[darius-iii|Darius III]] inherited a functioning, wealthy, militarily
capable state — not a hollow shell — is central to Briant's argument that the subsequent fall was
contingent rather than structural.

## Historiography

The reign is poorly served by the sources — Diodorus (anti-Persian, mercenary-centric), Isocrates
(propaganda urging Greek invasion), and the moralizing Justin — checked by Briant against Babylonian
and Egyptian documents. The hostile tradition culminates in Ochus's reported poisoning by the eunuch
Bagoas, who is said to have dominated the brief reign of his successor Artaxerxes IV.
