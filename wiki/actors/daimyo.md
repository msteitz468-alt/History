# Daimyo (Feudal Lords of Japan)

*Daimyo* (literally "great name") were the major feudal lords of pre-modern [[japan|Japan]]: lords
of domains (*han*) holding at least 10,000 *koku* of assessed rice yield, owing personal allegiance
to the Shogun, and commanding their own retinues of [[samurai|samurai]]. During the
[[edo-period|Edo period]] (1603–1868) they were the principal subordinate actors in the
[[bakuhan-system|bakuhan system]], governing roughly three-quarters of Japan's land through their
domains while remaining subject to the oversight of the [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]].
Their domains (*han*) were the primary unit of government experienced by the majority of the
Japanese population.

## Origins and Medieval Antecedents

The term *daimyo* emerged in the medieval period to denote lords of significant landed power. Their
direct institutional antecedents were the *shugo-daimyo* of the Muromachi period — provincial
constables (*shugo*) who converted military authority into territorial lordship. The century of the
[[sengoku-period|Sengoku period]] ("Warring States," c. 1467–1600) saw the emergence of *sengoku
daimyo*, independent domain lords who had often destroyed or absorbed the *shugo* and built
castle-based territorial states. It was this class that Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and
Tokugawa Ieyasu successively subordinated and then systematized into the Edo-period *daimyo* order.

## Classification under the Tokugawa

The [[bakuhan-system|bakuhan system]] divided daimyo into three categories, each with different
rights and political relationships:

- **Shinpan** ("related houses") — collateral Tokugawa families, typically holding major strategic
  domains (Owari, Kii, Mito — the "Three Lords" or *sanke* from whom a Shogun could be drawn).
  Ieyasu explicitly designated Yoshinao, Yorinobu, and Yorifusa as the Three Lords in his final
  instructions; he "adjured them to see that they obeyed the Shogun in everything."
- **Fudai** ("house daimyo") — lords whose ancestors had served the Tokugawa before Sekigahara; the
  only category eligible for senior bakufu office (Senior Councilor, etc.). Ieyasu distinguished
  them from *tozama* in his *Legacy* document: "Family vassals or Fudai are those bound to us by
  lineage and history, whose ancestors did loyal service to our house... Since their fidelity and
  affection exceeds that of the Outside houses, these others must not be displeased at this
  preference."
- **Tozama** ("outside lords") — lords who had submitted to Tokugawa hegemony at or after
  Sekigahara; excluded from central bakufu office; typically settled at the geographic margins of
  Japan. They were the class the Shogunate watched most carefully. Ieyasu: "The great Tozama lords
  of provinces have no part in the house laws and ancestral instructions of my family. But if they
  transgress the code of the samurai... and oppress the people, even though they do not rebel, they
  shall be deprived of their territory."

## Governance of the Han

Each daimyo was, within his domain, effectively a sovereign: he administered law, collected taxes
(assessed in *koku* under the [[kokudaka-system|kokudaka system]]), maintained his own castle and
army, managed roads, bridges, and irrigation, and provided famine relief. For the great majority of
Japanese, the *han* was "the only government they knew" (Hall). The Shogun did not administer the
domains directly; his power over daimyo operated through obligation, surveillance, and the ever-
present threat of attainder (*kaieki*).

## Obligations Under the Buke Shohatto

The [[buke-shohatto|Buke Shohatto]] (1615) defined the daimyo's obligations:

- Cultivate letters and arms; do not neglect military training in peacetime.
- No luxury or gambling that might lead to loss of a fief.
- No harboring of rebels or lawbreakers.
- No castle repair or construction without permission.
- No private marriages (political alliances require bakufu approval).
- Report innovations or factional activity in neighboring fiefs.
- Maintain prescribed limits on retinue size when attending in Edo.

Violations of any article — even the anodyne ones about economy or dress — gave the bakufu
legitimate grounds for severe measures: forced retirement, house arrest, revenue reduction, forced
domain exchange, or outright dispossession. Tokutomi called the code "a velvet bag full of
gunpowder" (quoted by Sadler).

The [[sankin-kotai|sankin-kōtai]] requirement (generalized by Iemitsu in 1635) added the obligation
of annual alternation between domain and Edo, with family permanently hostaged in the capital.

## Ieyasu's Personal Assessment of the Daimyo

Ieyasu's deathbed review of the daimyo roster with Hidetada — recorded in Sadler from the
*Tokugawa Jikki* — illustrates his approach to managing the class. On Kato Yoshiaki: "He has been
loyal and sincere... If he is well treated he will be faithful, but if he takes offence at anything
he gets discontented. So take care." Hidetada's dismissive reply ("He is only a small man") drew a
sharp rebuke: "It never does to overlook people because they are small. In a dance, for instance,
a young man may take the lead if he is good and understands rhythm... It is just the same in the
affairs of the Empire." On Fukushima Masanori (suspected of Toyotomi sympathies): Ieyasu presented
him with a tea-caddy and told him his future treatment would depend on his conduct — a combination
of reassurance and warning characteristic of Ieyasu's management style.

## Decline and Abolition

The *sankin-kōtai* suspension in 1862 freed domain resources and allowed the formation of the
domain-based military coalitions that overthrew the Shogunate in 1868. In 1871 the Meiji government
abolished the domains (*haihan chiken*), replacing them with prefectures under centrally appointed
governors. The daimyo were pensioned off with stipends and eventually transformed into a hereditary
nobility (*kazoku*); their *samurai* retainers received lump-sum commutations and were released from
feudal obligation. The *daimyo* as an institutional category ceased to exist.

## Historiography

The role of the *han* in the Edo system was neglected by Meiji-era historians (servants of
centralization) and revived only after 1945 under scholars like Itō Tasaburō and the *Hanseishi
kenkyūkai*. The Cambridge History of Japan (Hall, ch. 5, Bolitho) restored the *han* to the center
of the bakuhan analysis: the domains "covered three-quarters of Japan, held most of its wealth and
taxes, and commanded at least three-quarters of the samurai." Sadler's [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]]
treats the daimyo primarily through Ieyasu's individual management of specific lords rather than as
a systemic analysis; the systematic treatment is in the Cambridge volumes.
