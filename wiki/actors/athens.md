# Athens (Archaic)

**Athens** — the single unified state of **Attica** — followed a distinctive Archaic
path: a unitary territory unusually large for a Greek polis, an aristocratic
constitution that slowly dismantled its monarchy, and a sequence of crises (Cylon,
Dracon, Solon, Pisistratus) that, uniquely, produced neither permanent aristocracy nor
lasting tyranny but the foundations of the Classical democracy.
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 43, A. Andrewes) treats its growth
to Solon; Ch. 44 covers the tyranny.

## The unification of Attica

Unlike Boeotia or Arcadia, Attica became a **single unitary state** — its people a
homogeneous "Ionian" body, sharing dialect, tribes and festivals, with Athens as their
centre. The **synoecism** (which the Athenians credited to the hero Theseus) was, as
Thucydides understood it, a **centralization of government** — dissolving the separate
councils of Eleusis, the Marathonian Tetrapolis and other once-independent communities
into a single prytaneum, celebrated annually at the **Synoikia** — rather than a
transfer of population. Andrewes places it, on the archaeological evidence of ninth-
century prosperity, in the Dark Age (c. 900), an event lost to exact memory and so
pushed back onto the heroic Theseus. Athens also claimed the leadership of the
**Ionian migration** to the east Aegean (eleventh century).

## The aristocratic state

The monarchy was dismantled, as generally in Greece, by **putting the king's functions
in commission** among the aristocracy. By the time the record begins (the annual
archon-list from 682/1) Athens was ruled by the **nine archons** — "the" archon
(eponymous head of state), the *basileus* (heir to the king's rituals), the *polemarch*
(army commander), and six *thesmothetai* (keepers of the law) — backed by the **Council
of the Areopagus**, the venerated life-membership body of ex-archons that tried homicide
and "guarded the laws." Society was organized in four kinship **tribes** (subdivided
into *trittyes* and **phratries**), administered through the **naukrariai** (48
ship-financing/tax districts), and dominated by the **Eupatrid** nobility — a relatively
numerous aristocracy who alone held the archonship and, before Dracon, *were* the law
through their inherited expertise in custom. "Politics at this stage was a matter of
their internal rivalries."

## Cylon, Dracon, and the wars

The seventh-century age of [[greek-tyranny|tyranny]] reached Attica, but failed there.
The Olympic victor **Cylon** (c. 636), backed by his father-in-law Theagenes of Megara,
seized the Acropolis aiming at tyranny — but **the people did not support him**, and his
besieged followers were killed at the altar by the archons, bringing a hereditary
**curse on the [[alcmaeonids|Alcmaeonid]]** clan that would be invoked for two
centuries. The pressure for written law produced **Dracon's code (621/20)** — famed for
its harshness ("Draconian"), but a genuine advance in its **law on homicide**, which
distinguished premeditated from involuntary killing and laid down fixed procedure (the
only part Solon kept). Abroad, Athens fought the long war with **Megara for Salamis**
(seventh–sixth c., closed by Spartan arbitration in Athens's favour), an early naval war
with **Aegina**, and the fight with **Mytilene for Sigeum** in the Troad (c. 600, where
Alcaeus lost his shield) — securing a port near the Hellespont entrance. Athens's role
in the **[[delphi|First Sacred War]]** (led by the Alcmaeonid Alcmaeon) won it a seat on
the Amphictyony and "returns Athens to the mainstream of Greek history."

## From tyranny to isonomia (CAH IV)

The Archaic experiment closed with the **fall of the [[pisistratidae|Pisistratid
tyranny]]** (510) and the **[[cleisthenic-reforms-508bce|reform of Cleisthenes]]** (508/7),
which [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Chs. 4–5, Lewis & Ostwald) carry forward from
Andrewes' account. After Hipparchus' [[assassination-of-hipparchus-514bce|assassination]]
(514) hardened the regime, the exiled [[cleisthenes|Alcmaeonidae]] won Delphi and Sparta;
King [[cleomenes-i|Cleomenes]] expelled Hippias. In the ensuing struggle **Cleisthenes
"took the demos into partnership,"** survived a Spartan intervention through a popular
rising, and reorganized Attica from a **kinship** to a **territorial** basis —
**139 demes, 30 trittyes, ten new tribes**, and the **Council of Five Hundred** — founding
the [[isonomia]] that underlay [[athenian-democracy|Athenian democracy]]. The new energy of
the freed city showed at once in the **victory over Boeotia and Chalcis (506)**; its later
safeguard was the law of **[[ostracism]]**. This is the bridge from Archaic Athens to the
**[[athens-fifth-century|fifth-century democracy]]**.

## Fourth-century resurgence (CAH VI)

This page treats Archaic and Classical Athens; for the fifth-century empire see
[[athens-fifth-century]] and [[delian-league]]. After the catastrophe of 404
([[fall-of-athens-404bce]]) and the brief oligarchy of the **[[thirty-tyrants|Thirty]]**,
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Chs. 4, 6, Seager) traces a remarkable Athenian
recovery in the early fourth century. **[[conon]]'s** Persian-funded victory at **Cnidus
(394)** and his **rebuilding of the Long Walls (393)** restored Athens as a sea power
during the **[[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]]**; and in 378/7 Athens founded the
**[[second-athenian-confederacy|Second Athenian Confederacy]]**, proclaiming itself the
champion of the "freedom and autonomy" of the [[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]] and
explicitly renouncing the imperial abuses of the [[delian-league|fifth-century empire]].
Athens thus became the **maritime counterweight** in the multipolar world of Sparta,
Thebes and Persia — though its own backsliding toward empire would provoke the Social War
(357–355).

## The end of independence (CAH VII.1)

Athens' history as a free agent closed at the opening of the Hellenistic age.
[[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|CAH VII.1]] (Will, ch. 2) records how, on the news of
[[alexander-the-great|Alexander's]] death, Athens led the Greek revolt of the
**[[lamian-war-323|Lamian War]]** (323–322) — and lost. Her fleet was annihilated at
[[battle-of-amorgos|Amorgos]] (the battle that, for Will, ends Athenian sea-power as
Salamis had begun it); [[antipater]] abolished the **democracy**, imposed a
property-franchise oligarchy and a Macedonian garrison in the Piraeus, and
[[demosthenes]] took his own life. For a decade (317–307) the city was governed under
[[cassander]] by the philosopher-statesman [[demetrius-of-phalerum|Demetrius of
Phalerum]]. In 307 [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius Poliorcetes]] "liberated" Athens
and was showered with extravagant divine honours — a notorious early instance of
[[ruler-cult]] — restoring a democracy now under Antigonid patronage. Henceforth Athens
was a cultural capital and a pawn among the kings, no longer an independent power.

## Why Archaic Athens mattered

- The making of a **uniquely unified large polis** — and of the constitutional framework
  (archons, Areopagus, tribes, the assembly) on which Solon and Cleisthenes built.
- The setting of the great Archaic experiment in reform-without-tyranny: where Cylon
  failed, Dracon wrote, Solon mediated, and even the [[pisistratus|Pisistratid tyranny]]
  preserved the laws — the seedbed of Athenian democracy.

## Hall (2014) — late synoikism?, archaeology, and the pedion model

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] qualifies early full unification of Attica and the teleology from Solon to democracy:

**Theseus tradition and Classical anomaly.** By the fifth century Athens controlled ~2,500 km² with rural residents holding the same civic rights as city residents — anomalous vs Sparta’s larger territory via perioikoi/helots. Athenians explained this via Theseus’s synoecism (Thuc. 2.15; Synoikia festival). Hall rejects Late Bronze Age and simple Dark Age “internal colonization” models (Catalogue of Ships Attic/Salamis lines show interference; ceramic homogeneity ≠ political unity).

**Late synoikism thesis.** Full political synoecism may arrive only with [[cleisthenic-reforms-508bce|Cleisthenes]]: before the last decade of the sixth century Athens exerted weak influence beyond the pedion; long Salamis struggle; ease of Cylon/Pisistratus/Cleomenes seizing the acropolis; Tettikhos gravestone ca. 560 contrasts *astos*/*xenos*; southern kouroi and Alcmaeonid Anaphlystos base imply incomplete control. Naukrariai (48) ≈ one third of ~140 demes may underpin city trittyes only — much of Attica possibly outside the phylê system until the late sixth century. Phratries may have organized hinterland without phylê-style civic definition; paralia/mesogaia may have been perioikic-like. Great Panathenaea (560s) fosters pan-Attic affiliation (*pan-* as diversity, not already full franchise). **Final-decade choice about “perioikic” neighbors** (unlike Sparta) began fifth-century ascendancy; sixth-century Athens was not atypical until that choice.

**Physical synoecism and archaeology.** Eleventh–eighth-century burial plots south of the acropolis/agora give way from ca. 700 to outlying cemeteries (Kerameikos) — merger of clusters into conurbation. Eighth century prosperous (burials rise sevenfold 780–720; Attic sites ~15→>50) despite no colonization, like Corinth/Argos. **Seventh-century “gap”:** burial numbers collapse; Thoricus abandoned; activity mainly at remote hilltop sanctuaries; acropolis cult less visible.

**Chronography and commerce.** Agora archon list ca. 425 likely ran to 683/2, but early names (first annual archon “Kreon” = “ruler”) are hard to verify; distrust precise calendar dates before mid-sixth century. Eleusis/Salamis control for Saronic lanes; Solon frs. 1–3 show Salamis Megarian ca. 600; Pisistratus took Nisaia probably 560s; commercial revival driven more by ending Megarian threat than by Solon’s craft/export measures — second-quarter sixth century Attic ceramics drive out Corinthian overseas.

**Continuities across tyranny.** Pisistratid rule more constitutional than revolutionary (*AC* 16.2; Thuc. 6.54.6); Hippias’s fall → aristocratic factionalism first; 508/7 popular riot provoked more by Spartan troops than mass opposition to Isagoras; full democracy still decades away at century’s end.

## The imperial economy (Millett/Osborne)

Millett's economic reconstruction of classical Greece is, by his own admission, largely a
reconstruction of **Athens's** economy specifically — the surviving corpus of ~150 Attic
court speeches (late fifth century to 322 BCE) is exclusively Athenian, and Athens under
its fifth-century empire was economically atypical among Greek states. A **major silver
strike at Laurium in the late 480s** produced a windfall for the treasury that the
Athenians, on **[[themistocles|Themistocles]]'s** proposal, used to build a fleet of one
hundred triremes rather than distribute as a dividend — the fleet that proved decisive at
**[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]** and the foundation of Athenian naval power.
Poorer Athenians who could not afford hoplite armour served as rowers, a service later
cited (sardonically, by the "Old Oligarch") to justify their claim to political rights —
linking naval manpower to the broadening of Athenian democracy.

Imperial revenue was substantial: Thucydides has **[[pericles|Pericles]]** put Athens's
annual imperial income at the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War at some 600 talents,
against Xenophon's figure of roughly 1,000 talents for total revenue (internal and
external combined) — without which Athens could not have funded pay for public office, a
fleet costing roughly a talent a month per trireme in rowers' wages, major public works,
and a cash reserve on the Acropolis reported at nearly 10,000 talents, a fiscal position
Millett judges unmatched by any other polis, Sparta included. That imperial fiscal base
collapsed abruptly with the defeat of 404; the **Second Athenian Confederacy** (from 378)
failed to restore comparable income, though Athens maintained its restored democracy
(assembly pay from 403), a fleet, and intermittent public building through liturgies and
property levies on its wealthy citizens. The Spartan occupation of **Decelea** (413, during
the Peloponnesian War) cut Athens off from the Laurium mines; Thucydides records some
twenty thousand skilled slaves — likely including many mine-workers — defecting to Sparta
in consequence.

Athenian commercial life also produced the era's most striking case of social mobility:
**Pasion**, an ex-slave who bought his freedom, built up the largest and most durable
banking business in Athens, and eventually purchased Athenian citizenship, reputedly worth
almost sixty talents at his death — though his son Apollodorus faced lasting social stigma
over the perceived illegitimacy of that citizenship.

## The archaic-classical fiscal apparatus (Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World)

[[sources/scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|Scheidel, Morris &
Saller (eds.), *The Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World*]] (2007) adds
complementary detail to the Millett/Osborne account above (Osborne ch. 10; Davies, Möller,
von Reden chs. 12–14):

- **Solon's economic reforms**, in this account, tied political office to income measured
  in grain, oil and wine (census classes); banned enslavement of Athenians for debt; ended
  the *hektemoroi* ("sixth-parters") sharecropping underclass; and (per his own fr. 36)
  freed debt-slaves and returned exiles sold abroad — with the subsequent expansion of
  chattel slavery at Athens read as a 6th-century phenomenon filling the resulting demand
  for dependent labor. See [[processes/archaic-greek-economy]].
- **Piraeus administration.** Alongside the *agoranomoi* and *metronomoi*, the
  *sitophylakes* (grain officials) were expanded from 5+5 to 20+15 in the 320s BCE grain
  crisis, and emergency grain-buyers (*sitonai*, from the 350s BCE) supplemented the
  standing apparatus — Demosthenes served as one in 338/7 BCE.
- **Peak revenue figure.** Under Lycurgus (338/7–324 BCE), after the loss of empire,
  Athenian revenue reached ~1,200 talents/year from purely internal sources — the highest
  documented public revenue of any classical Greek polis, exceeding even the Periclean-era
  figure.
- **Liturgy scale.** Liturgies (choregia, gymnasiarchia, trierarchia, eisphora) drew on
  Athens's ~300–1,200 richest citizens (holding 3–4+ talents), funding at least 60, more
  likely 97 (118+ in Panathenaic years), liturgies annually.
- **Contrast with Sparta.** Per Möller (citing Thucydides' Archidamus speech, 1.80–1),
  Sparta had no common treasury and no capacity for direct taxation of citizens or allies,
  despite the largest territory, a huge helot workforce, and abundant treasure — Sparta
  went bankrupt during the Corinthian War (394–390 BCE) and permanently after Leuctra
  (371 BCE).
- **Public pay chronology.** Military pay from no later than 441 BCE (possibly the
  450s/460s); jury pay (*misthos*) introduced by Pericles (c. 450s BCE) at 2 obols, later
  3; assembly pay from 403 BCE, reaching 3 obols/session by 393 BCE — pseudo-Aristotle
  (*Ath. Pol.* 24.3) records over 20,000 Athenians supported by tribute and taxes (jurors,
  archers, cavalry, councilmen, dockyard/acropolis guards, officials).

See [[processes/classical-greek-economy]] for the fuller Davies/Möller/von Reden treatment
of Athenian production, distribution, and consumption.

## From the Oxford History of the Classical World (1986)

**Forrest (Ch.1, [[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW 1986]]).** Archaic sequence: Draco → Solon 594 (debt relief, four property classes, council as second anchor, appeal to assembly) → intermittent tyranny with [[actors/pisistratus|Pisistratus]] firm from 546 and sons after 528 (public works, Athena, Panathenaea/Dionysia, itinerant judges—arguably more for national unity than pure Solonian constitutionalism) → 510 expulsion → [[actors/cleisthenes|Cleisthenes]] deme–tribe reform. Marathon 490: ~10,000 Athenian + Plataean hoplites defeat Persian landing; Aristotle remarks victory gave the people political confidence (first ostracisms). Archon lot reform 487: long-run effect that ten elected tribal generals replaced archons as chief officers—“desire for elected efficiency overcame principle.” Laurium silver 482: Themistocles won ~200 triremes publicly framed vs Aegina, backbone of 480 naval resistance. Salamis and the dual-supremacy outcome: “Athens had won as a budding democracy, Sparta as a monarchic oligarchy.”

**Hornblower (Ch.6).** Why Athens alone was viable leader in 478: no helot/Arcadian problems; unlike Corinth not subordinated to Sparta; offered democracy and *paideia*; Pisistratid cultural patronage already a magnet; Ionian-mother-city and Marathon memorialization propaganda; she had not medized. Empire revenue (boosted by Cimon’s 460s operations) made possible 462 democratic changes—jury and other pay “paid for by the allies.” Democracy more immediate than modern (ekklesia power; ~40,000 adult male citizens, perhaps 6,000 at important debates) yet less inclusive (slaves, women, subject allies excluded); limits from deme life, boule, generals, demagogues, and assembly procedure/psychology. Culture underwritten by imperial success and the **liturgy system** (Cimon/Pericles; Pericles’ first known act financing Aeschylus’ *Persae*). Peloponnesian War: Pericles abandon Attica / Long Walls / survive = win; Archidamian “win” then Sicily 415–413 and Aegospotami/Hellespont blockade 405–404 dissolve the empire. Second Naval Confederacy 378 abjured fifth-century practices yet by 373 “contributions” and garrisons reappeared. Closing: “Imperialism had after all proved incompatible with democracy”; Athens could not impose lasting Hellenic political unity.

**Murray (Ch.9).** Athens as paradigm of latent *polis* forces—liberating the individual from tradition without loss of social identity via dense associations (deme, phratry, genos, symposium, gymnasion). Evidence concentration makes Athens untypical yet unavoidable for social history.

**Price (Hellenistic).** Democracy eroded toward rich dominance; late-third-century ephebate tiny (~40 new/year) but admitted rich foreigners; classical cultural prestige and tragic master-scrolls sustained under kings until Ptolemaic “borrowing.”

## Military and naval state (CHGRW)

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] treats classical
Athens as the prime Greek case of a **monopolistic** violence-producing state — sea-power,
hegemony, and high financial independence (Gabrielsen I.8) — and as an atypical evidence
base that can skew the picture of Greek war’s ubiquity (Hornblower I.2). Cross-links:
[[greek-naval-warfare]], [[trireme]], [[financing-greek-warfare]], [[hoplite-warfare]],
[[greek-warfare]].

**Command and discipline.** Cleisthenes’ decimal *phylai* produced the annually elected
board of ten *strategoi* — major focus of political ambition after ~460 when much else
went by lot; the ten-tribe system was **not** the organizational basis of the fleet
(except that generals were also admirals). From early fifth century overall command of
land and sea passed to the ten *strategoi*; mid-fourth century specialised portfolios
(e.g. defence of Attica); joint command by consensus/majority. Ten tribal *taxeis* under
*taxiarchoi*; cavalry two *hipparchoi* + ten phylarchs; trierarchs wealth-selected,
relying on professional *kybernetēs*. Democratic military discipline: no salutes/distinct
uniforms/separate rations; officers rarely fined; **no corporal punishment of citizens**;
no summary execution; crimes *astrateia*, *lipotaxia*, *deilia* tried by fellow soldiers
with general presiding procedurally only. From 501 the army’s ten tribal regiments
mirrored political tribes (mixed regions, ~1,000 in general levy) — politically designed,
militarily unwieldy; lowest level remained informal groups of relatives, friends,
neighbours.

**Manpower.** Full muster for first Megarid invasion 431 fielded 13,000 Athenians and
metics — “largest army of Athenians that had ever assembled” — with mass armies now
predominantly publicly paid. Metics: 3,000 of 16,000 full mobilization at war’s start
(Thuc. 2.31.2). Cavalry acme 1,000 regular plus 200 horse-archers (431); state support via
*katastasis* (horse-purchase loan), *sitos* (fodder), combat-loss reimbursement, public
reviews — more training than hoplites; fourth-century fodder cost alone ~40 talents/year.
Archers: 1,600 + 200 mounted at outbreak (Thuc. 2.13.8); four bowmen per trireme. Fifth-
century draft used deme *katalogoi* by tribe; fairness complaints prompted switch
(c. 386–366) to age-group conscription like Sparta; **navy used volunteers/slaves without
draft until mid-fourth century** — fundamental army/navy difference. Pay for office and
military service in the 450s broke leisure-class monopoly; 411 coup abolished pay and
briefly restricted rights to ~5,000 wealthiest hoplites/cavalry (~15% of citizens), though
Athens had at least twice as many equipped hoplites. By late fourth century basic public
hoplite kit plus two-year *ephebeia* at public expense — requiring exceptional revenues.

**Naval monopoly and empire.** By 478 democratic council and assembly monopolized naval
resources and armed violence at sea; the independent aristocratic raider became an outlaw
“booty-chaser” unless harnessed to communal decisions; proceeds of armed violence became
state property. Miltiades’ 489 Paros expedition used 70 ships only after assembly
authorization and a promise of public gold — unlike the elder Miltiades’ semi-private
Chersonese principality. Delian League (c.150 members 477 → c.250 by 431) funded ships via
tribute; by 431 annual revenue ~600 talents — “the equivalent of sacking a large city
every year.” Revolts (Naxos, Thasos, Samos, Lesbos) suppressed by naval expeditions and
sieges (Thasos three years; Samos nine months with rams/sheds). Pericles sent sixty ships
yearly on training exercises; maritime life made Athenians good rowers/helmsmen. Periclean
defensive strategy: refuse land battle, shelter behind Athens–Piraeus walls, supply by
sea — material/psychological cost plus plague 430 killing ~1/4–1/3 of population. Aegospotami
405 then siege forced surrender 404. Second Confederacy (378) bicameral, deliberately
avoided Delian abuses. After Chaeronea 338 hegemonic status practically vanished;
sea-power ended 323/2 as Macedon became the quintessential monopolistic state. Ambassadors
(*presbeis*) elected by assembly, unpaid except per diem, forbidden gifts on pain of
death — lacked herald-level inviolability. War dead: from the 460s annual public funeral
with ashes home (Thuc. 2.34); Marathon/Plataea buried on site.

**Evidence bias.** Athens is untypical as imperial “meddlesome” power whose literature
(Thucydides, Aristophanes) skews war’s apparent ubiquity; under-reported Argos enjoyed
thirty years of peace from 451. Geographic bias of military evidence: Athens best known,
Sparta next (Xenophon) — both atypical until Philip’s professional Macedonian army.

## Everyday Writing: Agora Graffiti and Ostracism Ballots

Classical Athens is, with Pompeii, a prime site for [[ancient-graffiti|ancient
graffiti]]. Over 3,000 inscribed sherds, lamps and vessels from the Agora
excavations carry names, alphabets, ownership and commercial marks, delivery
notes ("Boy, bring other new couches for Phalanthos") and *kalos*-inscriptions
("X is beautiful"), alongside obscene invective — evidence that ordinary
Athenians, of uncertain but often sub-elite status and including some women,
wrote. The **Dipylon Jug** (c. 740 BCE) bears one of the earliest Greek
alphabetic inscriptions; the Hymettos sanctuary of Zeus yielded ~170 votive
graffiti. The physical ballots of [[ostracism]] — over 1,100 *ostraka* from the
Agora and c. 8,500 from the Kerameikos — are the largest single body of this
evidence; a cache of ~190 sherds pre-inscribed "Themistocles" in a few hands
shows factional ballot-preparation, while Plutarch's tale of the illiterate
farmer asking Aristides himself to write "Aristides" attests participation by
the illiterate. Curse tablets (*[[defixiones]]*) from the Kerameikos targeted
legal opponents. Per [[keegan-graffiti-in-antiquity-2014|Keegan]].

## Freeman (2014) — the Delian League's early coercion and Ephialtes' reform

[[sources/freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014|Freeman (2014)]] adds that Athens's naval
dominance within the founding **Delian League** was structural from the outset, not a
later "corruption" of an originally equal alliance: Athens fielded roughly **180
triremes in 480, rising to about 300 by 431**, against Sparta's effectively nonexistent
navy. Coercive action against fellow League members predates any obvious "empire":
**Naxos was crushed in 476** for attempting to secede (Thucydides: Athens "enslaved her
contrary to what was established"), and **Thasos was besieged** over a gold-mine
dispute — both well before the 454 treasury transfer conventionally cited as the "start"
of empire. Freeman also credits **Ephialtes' reforms (461 BCE)** with stripping the
Areopagus of most of its powers (transferred to Assembly, juries, and Council),
retaining only its religious-cult vetting function.
