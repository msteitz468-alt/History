## Overview

Itagaki Taisuke (1837–1919) was the founder and leading figure of Japan's first organized popular political movement, the Freedom and Popular Rights Movement (jiyū minken undō), and the chairman of Japan's first political party, the Jiyū-tō (Party of Liberty). A former samurai from Tosa (Kōchi) domain, he transformed the energy of samurai discontent into civilian political agitation after the [[satsuma-rebellion-1877|Satsuma Rebellion]] demonstrated that armed resistance to the Meiji government was futile.

## Career and Political Strategy

Itagaki had been a major figure in the Meiji Restoration and a member of the early government. He resigned from the government in 1873 in the same political crisis that drove out [[saigo-takamori|Saigō Takamori]] — the debate over the Korean policy. Unlike Saigō, however, Itagaki turned his opposition in a civilian direction.

In 1874 he founded the Risshi-sha (Self-Help Society) in Kōchi, an organization initially more concerned with education and self-improvement for former samurai than with parliamentary agitation. In 1875 he founded the Aikoku-sha (Society of Patriots) as a specifically political body. Although Itagaki "had been a firm supporter of Saigō" before the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, the defeat of the rebellion clarified his strategic thinking: "The defeat of Saigō's forces by the government army made it clear that it was useless to attempt to oppose the government by military means; therefore, the Risshi-sha's message would be spread by speeches and newspaper articles, not swords."

This decision — to pursue political change through speech, petition, and organization rather than armed force — made Itagaki the pivotal figure in redirecting samurai political energy into constitutional agitation.

## The Jiyū-tō and the Movement's Peak

The Aikoku-sha renamed itself the League for Establishing a National Assembly in 1880. In April of that year, a convention in Ōsaka involving members from twenty-four prefectures passed a resolution calling for a national assembly. The league attempted to petition the emperor directly; while the petition was blocked, it alarmed conservatives sufficiently to accelerate discussions of constitutional government.

In December 1880 the league resolved to form a political party. The Jiyū-tō (Party of Liberty) was officially founded on December 15, 1880, and Itagaki was elected its chairman (Sōri) at the party's mass meeting in October 1881.

The Jiyū-tō's objectives became less clear after the emperor's proclamation of October 12, 1881, promising a parliament in 1890. The party's rival, Ōkuma's Rikken Kaishin-tō (Constitutional Reform Party), founded April 1882, had a more concrete objective: a British-style parliamentary democracy.

## The Assassination Attempt and European Journey

On April 6, 1882, after delivering an address in Gifu, Itagaki was attacked by a man wielding a dagger. Though the wounds were superficial, Itagaki is reported to have cried — believing himself dying — "Itagaki may die, but freedom will never die!" The incident created an enormous outpouring of sympathy and brought new members to the Jiyū-tō.

The government, however, had a more sophisticated response. Itō Hirobumi persuaded Itagaki and the co-leader Gotō Shōjirō to travel to Europe, ostensibly to study European politics and constitutions, with $20,000 secretly arranged through the Mitsui Bank. The ulterior motive was to deprive the Jiyū-tō of its two chief leaders. The plan worked: the two men spent their time largely in sightseeing, returned in June 1883 having learned little, and found the party in disarray from factional infighting during their absence. Itagaki delivered lectures urging his followers to focus on raising Japan's standard of living and strengthening the navy — "nothing was left of the crusading liberal."

The Jiyū-tō was disbanded on October 29, 1884.

## Counterfactual Significance

Itagaki's decision to pursue civilian agitation rather than armed resistance after 1877 was a structural turning point. Had he followed Saigō's path, the result would likely have been military suppression. By redirecting former-samurai discontent into petition, party organization, and press campaigns, he created the institutional foundations — however fragile — that would eventually produce the Meiji Constitution and the Diet. The tactics he pioneered survived the party's dissolution and re-emerged in subsequent decades.

## Historiography

Keene's account presents Itagaki as an earnest but not deeply intellectually rigorous figure — a man of genuine political convictions whose strategic instincts were sound but who was ultimately outmaneuvered by the Meiji oligarchs. The European journey episode is presented with notable irony: the government's manipulation of the man who cried "freedom will never die" into a costly, purposeless sightseeing tour is one of the more mordant episodes in Keene's account of 1880s politics.
