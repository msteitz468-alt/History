# Otto von Bismarck

The Prussian statesman who unified Germany and dominated European diplomacy for a generation. As
Minister-President of [[prussia|Prussia]] from 1862, he defied parliament to build the army and
practiced *Realpolitik* — "blood and iron" — engineering three wars (against Denmark 1864, Austria in
the [[austro-prussian-war-1866]], and France in the [[franco-prussian-war-1870]]) that produced the
[[german-empire|German Empire]] under Prussian leadership in 1871.

As imperial Chancellor (1871–90) he waged the [[kulturkampf]] against political Catholicism, outlawed
the Socialists while co-opting workers with the first modern welfare insurance, and built an intricate
alliance system to keep France isolated and preserve the European balance. His dismissal by the young
**[[wilhelm-ii|Wilhelm II]]** in 1890 removed the restraining hand from German foreign policy.
Bismarck's authoritarian-yet-modernizing settlement is the pivot of the [[german-sonderweg|Sonderweg
debate]]: did he found a modern nation-state, or entrench an illiberal order that led toward 1933?

## Evans (2003): Bismarck's Legacy and the Bismarck Myth

Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003|The Coming of the Third Reich]])
roots Nazism's genealogy in the Reich of 1871 — not in Luther or Herder, whom
he treats as decontextualized props of "German character" narratives. Key
attributed claims:

- **The "strong leader" myth was posthumous.** Bismarck defined politics as
  "the art of the possible" and the statesman as one who must "wait and listen
  until he hears the steps of God sounding through events; then leap up and
  grasp the hem of his garment." The ruthless-dictator image was built by "his
  acolytes after his death"; the conservative resister Ulrich von Hassell
  noted in 1944 that the "jackbooted politician of violence" image was false —
  Bismarck's "great gift was for the highest diplomacy and moderation." Evans:
  the strong-leader ideal that fed Weimar's craving for a new Bismarck was
  "a much more recent creation" than any ancient German trait. He labels
  Bismarck a "white revolutionary" — revolutionary means for conservative
  ends — alongside Disraeli, Napoleon III, and Cavour.
- **The 1871 constitution's authoritarian design**: uniquely among modern
  German constitutions it lacked any declaration of rights; the Kaiser held
  war powers and army command independent of the Reichstag; the Chancellor was
  a civil servant answerable to the Kaiser, not parliament. Marx quoted: "a
  bureaucratically constructed military despotism, dressed up with
  parliamentary forms, mixed in with an element of feudalism yet at the same
  time already influenced by the bourgeoisie." The army stayed a "state within
  a state" (seven-year budget votes; NCOs' automatic right to state jobs
  militarizing police, post, and railways).
- **Repression's long shadow.** The [[kulturkampf]] (mid-1870s: 989 vacant
  parishes, 225 priests jailed, non-nursing orders suppressed, two archbishops
  and three bishops removed, the Bishop of Trier dead after nine months'
  imprisonment) assaulted the civil liberties of ~40% of the population —
  cheered on by liberals — and created the Centre Party as a self-defense
  vehicle. The Anti-Socialist Law (1878, after assassination attempts the SPD
  had no connection to) drove the SPD toward rigid Marxism and total distrust
  of "bourgeois" parties — a gulf that persisted "well into the 1920s and
  play[ed] a vital role in the crisis that eventually brought the Nazis to
  power" (contributed_to: [[weimar-democracy-and-its-collapse]]).

**Fiscal-welfare reading (Flynn 1944, attributed)**: [[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944|Flynn]]
treats Bismarck's social insurance (1883 sickness/compensation through the 1889
old-age pension) as a defensive political maneuver to outbid socialist
agitation after anti-socialist repression, and notes the empire — debt-free in
1871 on the 4.47bn-mark French indemnity — was borrowing again by 1875, its
constitutionally starved central treasury making deficit finance "an
institution of state policy" for fifty years. He also stresses, against the
autocracy stereotype, that the Kaiser "presided over a federal government which
actually possessed less powers than the United States federal government."
