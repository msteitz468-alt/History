# Franz von Papen

Aristocratic renegade Centre politician, Reich Chancellor (June–November 1932)
— author of the coup against the Prussian government (the *Preussenschlag*) —
and Vice-Chancellor under Hitler, whose intrigue with
[[hindenburg-paul-von|Hindenburg]] delivered the chancellorship to
[[adolf-hitler|Hitler]] on 30 January 1933 in the fatal belief that the Nazis
could be "framed in" and tamed.

## Role and Decisions

Per Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]): a landed aristocrat and
obscure Centre deputy well to Brüning's right, expelled from the US in WWI as
military attaché for "activities incompatible with his status"; bought a
majority stake in the Centre paper *Germania* with his industrialist wife's
money; recommended to [[hindenburg-paul-von|Hindenburg]] by
[[schleicher-kurt-von|Schleicher]] as sympathetic to army interests. Chancellor
from 1 June 1932 at the head of the party-less "cabinet of barons," aiming at
a "New State" above parties (his Interior Minister von Gayl proposed
restricting the franchise); banned even democratic papers (*Vorwärts* twice),
and restored the Prussian hand-axe for executions.

**The bargain with the Nazis**: new elections (July 1932) plus lifting the SA
ban, hoping to tame the movement and use the SA as an auxiliary army around
Versailles limits — "another disastrous miscalculation" (Evans): street
violence surged to the [[altona-bloody-sunday-1932|Altona Bloody Sunday]]
massacre (17 July 1932).

**The Preussenschlag** (20 July 1932): using Altona as pretext, Papen deposed
the SPD-led Prussian government of Braun and Severing — the decisive blow
against the SPD in a state holding more people than France, with Ebert's 1923
Saxony/Thuringia precedent behind it. The Reichsbanner (200,000 armed members,
against ~750,000 SA and Steel Helmets) did not resist; the State Court ruled
partially for Braun in October but left Papen's Reich Commissioner control
intact. Evans: "After 20 July 1932 the only realistic alternatives were a Nazi
dictatorship or a conservative, authoritarian regime backed by the army."

**Toward 30 January**: he refused Hitler the chancellorship after July 1932
(negotiations poisoned by the Potempa affair — five SA murderers of the
Communist Konrad Pietzuch death-sentenced under Papen's own 9 August decree,
then commuted under Nazi pressure); was humiliated when Göring let a
Communist no-confidence motion pass 512–42 (September 1932); lost the army's
confidence to Schleicher (resigned 3 December 1932); then engineered the
January 1933 intrigue that brought Hitler in with a conservative-dominated
cabinet, himself as Vice-Chancellor and Reich Commissioner for Prussia.
His verdicts: "You are wrong... We've engaged him for ourselves," and "Within
two months we will have pushed Hitler so far into a corner that he'll squeak."
The French ambassador François-Poncet saw it plainly: the conservatives had
"believed themselves to be very ingenious, ridding themselves of the wolf by
introducing him into the sheepfold."

## Counterfactual Significance

Evans's account concentrates maximal contingency in Papen: the Preussenschlag
demonstrated democratic institutions could be dismantled without resistance;
the lifted SA ban legitimized paramilitary terror; and the January 1933
intrigue was a choice, not a necessity — Hitler's movement was declining and
near-bankrupt after November 1932.

## Historiography

Evans (2003) presents the "amateurish and in many ways rather ludicrous"
Papen government (his phrase) as the hinge between authoritarian and
totalitarian outcomes. His marginalization and near-murder in the
[[night-of-the-long-knives-1934|Röhm purge]] are treated in
[[evans-third-reich-in-power-2005]].
