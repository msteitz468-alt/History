# Claudius (Tiberius Claudius Caesar Augustus Germanicus)

The **fourth Roman emperor** (41–54 CE) — Germanicus' disabled, scholarly brother, kept from
public life until middle age and then made emperor **by the praetorian guard** after Caligula's
murder; an industrious if insecure ruler who conquered Britain and expanded the imperial
bureaucracy. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 5 (Wiedemann).

## Accession by the guard (41)

When Caligula was assassinated and the Senate debated reviving the Republic (or choosing its
own candidate, Marcus Vinicius), Claudius — who had survived the reigns of Tiberius and
Caligula behind a "mask of pedantry," writing histories of Carthage and the Etruscans — was
found in the palace and acclaimed *imperator* by the praetorians, who took him to their camp.
Wiedemann frames his elevation in the language of Roman **possessio**: by physically
controlling the *domus Caesaris*, Claudius held the household as a matter of *fact*, and "if
(possessio) existed, it conferred rights." He adopted the name **Caesar** to signal his
inheritance, and bought the guard's loyalty with an **unprecedented donative of 15,000
sesterces** — the moment the Principate's dependence on the soldiery became undisguised.

## An insecure reign

Claudius' early years were dominated by the need to build a network of dependants and survive
plots. His **lack of military experience** (a fatal weakness in an *imperator*) drove an
"almost obsessive" advertisement of success — 27 imperatorial acclamations, more than any
emperor. He faced the revolt of **Camillus Scribonianus**, legate of Dalmatia (42, collapsed
in days when the legions deserted), and executed many: the sources charge him with some **35
senators and 200 equestrians**, often blaming his wife **Messallina** (executed 48 for her
bigamous "marriage" to Silius). He relied heavily on able **imperial freedmen** (Narcissus,
Pallas, the secretariats *ab epistulis* and *a rationibus*) — the administrative innovation,
and the scandal, of his reign.

## Britain and public works

Claudius reserved for himself the glory of the **[[claudian-conquest-of-britain-43ce|conquest
of Britain]]** (43) — entrusting the campaign to **Aulus Plautius** but appearing in person for
the fall of Camulodunum — both as the conquest of a land "beyond the edge of the world" and to
remove surplus legions from the Rhine. He pursued serious **public works**: draining the
Fucine Lake, a new aqueduct, the harbour at **Ostia**, and a visible role as a hard-working
judge and patron of the corn supply.

## Succession and death

After Messallina's fall Claudius married his niece **Agrippina the Younger**, who secured the
adoption of her son **[[nero]]** (50) over Claudius' own younger son **Britannicus** — having
Burrus made praetorian prefect and Seneca recalled as Nero's tutor. When rumours grew that
Claudius repented and favoured a "real" Caesar, he **died (13 October 54)** after eating
mushrooms — universally believed poisoned by Agrippina, the death "most opportune" for her and
her son.

## Significance

Claudius proved that an emperor could be made **wholly outside the Julian bloodline and by the
guard alone**, deepening the institutionalization of the *domus Caesaris* and its freedman
administration. He was the third emperor in a row whose accession or death turned on the
praetorians — the pattern that would explode in 68–69.
