# William H. Vanderbilt

Son and principal heir of Cornelius Vanderbilt (1821–1885). He inherited the bulk of his father's fortune and railroad holdings and significantly expanded and professionalized the empire in the years after 1877.

## Narrative

William H. Vanderbilt had long been involved in his father's businesses but was not initially favored. After Cornelius's death in 1877 he took control of the New York Central and other lines. He proved an able consolidator, doubling the family fortune through careful management, rate wars, and strategic alliances. He is remembered for the (possibly apocryphal or misquoted) remark "The public be damned" in response to questions about railroad rates. He retired in 1883, passing management to his sons.

## Causal Analysis

Cornelius deliberately tested his sons; William was given increasing responsibility in later years. His success showed that the empire could survive the founder's death through more systematic administration, even as it retained the aggressive competitive style of the father.

## Consequence Analysis

Under William H. the Vanderbilt railroads became the core of one of the largest personal fortunes in American history. His death in 1885 led to further division among his children, but the infrastructure he helped build remained central to the American economy.
