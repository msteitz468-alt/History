# Tacitus (Publius Cornelius Tacitus)

"Ancient Rome's greatest historian" (Barrett) — the senator and orator whose *Annals* and *Histories*
are our principal narrative source for the early Roman Empire, and whose mordant, pessimistic
analysis of autocracy has shaped political thought ever since. Treated by Anthony A. Barrett in the
Oxford World's Classics *Annals* (trans. J. C. Yardley).

## Life and Career

Born c. 56 CE, probably to an equestrian family of **Narbonese Gaul or northern Italy** (his father
likely the procurator named by Pliny the Elder), Tacitus was a ***novus homo*** — the first of his
line to enter the Senate. He married the daughter of **Gnaeus Julius Agricola**, governor of Britain
(77), and rose through the *cursus honorum* under the Flavians and Trajan: quaestor, **praetor (88)**
and member of the priestly *quindecimviri*, **suffect consul (97)** — when he delivered the funeral
oration for Verginius Rufus — and **proconsul of Asia** (c. 112/13), a pinnacle of the senatorial
career. With his close friend **Pliny the Younger** (our richest source on his character) he
successfully prosecuted the extortionate governor Marius Priscus (99–100). He had been absent from
Rome during the worst of **Domitian's** terror, and the trauma of those years — the "fifteen years of
forced silence" he laments in the *Agricola* — colours his whole view of the principate. He died c.
117–130, under Hadrian.

## The Works

- **Agricola** (98) — a biography/eulogy of his father-in-law, doubling as an attack on Domitian; now
  ingested as **[[tacitus-agricola-germany|*Agricola and Germany*]]**.
- **Germania** (98) — an ethnography of the German peoples, contrasting their "simple integrity" with
  Roman decadence; ingested with the *Agricola* (see **[[tacitus-agricola-germany]]**).
- **Dialogus de Oratoribus** — on the decline of oratory.
- **Histories** (c. 106–110) — the year of the four emperors (69) to the death of Domitian (96); five
  books survive.
- **Annals** (c. 110s–120) — his masterpiece, from the death of Augustus to the death of Nero
  (14–68); now ingested as **[[tacitus-annals|*The Annals*]]**.

The *Histories* and *Annals* together comprised thirty books, of which about half survive.

## Style and Outlook

- **Tacitean Latin.** "Perhaps no other ancient writer has imposed his mark so distinctively on the
  language": following **Sallust**, Tacitus rejected Ciceronian fullness for **pointed concision,
  trenchant epigram, asymmetry, poetic and archaic vocabulary**, and a deliberately startling effect.
- **The senatorial critique of autocracy.** He was "deeply opposed to the principate as a
  constitutional form, and committed to the republican system of senatorial government." His great
  theme is the **corrupting effect of absolute power** — on the ruled above all, and often on the
  ruler. His famous promise to write ***sine ira et studio*** ("without rancour or partiality", *Ann.*
  1.1) is, Barrett warns, to be treated with care: he rarely lies outright, but "behind the 'facts'
  lurk his own prejudices" — his ascription of dark motives, his retailing of rumour and "widely held
  belief." The irony is that Tacitus himself flourished under the system he despised, even under
  Domitian — "in many ways… a company man."

## Significance

Tacitus is the indispensable source for the **Julio-Claudian principate** and the model of the
**moralizing, psychologically penetrating** historian. His portraits — the deceptive, deteriorating
**Tiberius** (his masterpiece), the manipulated **Claudius**, the monstrous **Nero** — and his
analysis of tyranny, delation, the servility of the Senate and the theatre of power have given the
Western tradition its enduring vocabulary for the pathology of absolute rule (his influence runs
through Gibbon to the modern critique of despotism).

## OHCW (Lintott Ch. 26)

**Andrew Lintott**: only about half of the major works survive (from two manuscripts). Born mid-first century CE; reached senatorial rank under the Flavians; wrote mainly under Trajan — *Historiae* (Flavian period 69–96), *Annales* (Julio-Claudians 14–68), plus *Agricola* panegyric; traditional concern with virtue and vice. Under autocracy, practical understanding of how an emperor’s mind worked made him admired by Renaissance realists (Machiavelli, Guicciardini). Reserved greatest admiration for survivors like himself and Agricola who achieved glory through compliance backed by energy, not ostentatious martyrdom. Classic [[tiberius]] portrait: hypocrisy as peeling skins of plausibility from a malevolent inner self — modern historians more often stress indecisiveness, reserve, mistrust. After Tacitus, Latin historical writing dried up for over two centuries; next great history of Rome was Greek (Cassius Dio); fourth-century Ammianus tried a Latin restart but explained little of fourth-century crises — the old framework had nothing new to say.
