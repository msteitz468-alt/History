## Overview

The Matsudaira clan was a minor warrior family of Mikawa Province (modern Aichi Prefecture, central Japan) that rose from obscurity in the fourteenth century to become the ruling house of Japan under the name Tokugawa. The family's founder in Mikawa, Matsudaira Chikauji (d. 1407?), entered the province after leaving Buddhist orders and married into the influential Matsudaira family of the village of that name. His descendants gradually extended their holdings, and by the time of Nobumitsu they controlled roughly one-third of Mikawa.

The clan traces its ultimate ancestry to Hachimantaro Yoshiie of the Seiwa Genji line — "the Tokugawa family traces its descent from Hachimantaro Yoshiie of the Seiwa Genji line" (Sadler). This pedigree, real or constructed, provided legitimating connections to the Minamoto lineage from which the Shogunate had historically been drawn.

## Rise in Mikawa

For much of the fifteenth century the Matsudaira were a semi-independent clan in the orbit of more powerful neighbors. Kiyoyasu (d. 1535), grandfather of [[tokugawa-ieyasu]], was a capable soldier whose friendship was solicited by both the Takeda and Oda houses, but he was assassinated at age twenty-five, leaving his son Hirotada to inherit at age ten under the guardianship of the loyal retainer Abe Sadayoshi.

Hirotada, father of Ieyasu, was caught between the competing pressures of the Imagawa of Suruga to the east and the Oda of Owari to the west. Unable to resist the Imagawa alone, he sought their protection, becoming effectively a vassal of Imagawa Yoshimoto. This subordination required sending the infant Takechiyo (later Ieyasu) as a hostage, though the child was intercepted by the Oda and held at their castle of Anjo. When Hirotada died young, Takechiyo remained a hostage — first of the Oda, then of the Imagawa in Suruga — until the death of Yoshimoto at [[battle-of-okehazama-1560]] opened the way for his return to Okazaki.

## Name and Identity

The name Matsudaira proliferated widely during the Sengoku period as Ieyasu's collateral branches adopted it. In 1569, Ieyasu obtained imperial permission to resume the surname Tokugawa — the older designation used by an ancestor, Minamoto Yoshisue — in order to distinguish his main line from the many collateral Matsudaira families and to emphasize his Minamoto descent. As Sadler notes, "the original name of Matsudaira is limited to cadets of his house" even into the Edo period. The Tokugawa shoguns thus presented themselves as restorers of a lineage, not founders of a new one.

## Key Retainer Families

The clan's military effectiveness in the Sengoku period rested on a core of hereditary retainer families: Ishikawa, Honda, Sakai, and Sakakibara formed "the ruling oligarchy of the clan" (Sadler). These families provided the administrative and military leadership that sustained the clan through its most dangerous decades. Figures such as Sakai Tadatsugu, Honda Tadakatsu, and Sakakibara Yasumasa appear repeatedly commanding divisions at Anegawa, Mikatagahara, and Nagashino. The "Mikawa bushi" (Mikawa warriors) acquired a reputation for ferocious loyalty that Sadler highlights throughout these campaigns.

## Historiography

Sadler's 1937 biography of Ieyasu draws on the *Mikawa Go-Fudoki* and similar Tokugawa-period chronicles, which were compiled under dynastic auspices and reflect the prestige interests of the house. The genealogical claims — especially the Minamoto lineage — must be read in the light of the political purposes they served. Post-Meiji historiography, which tended to depreciate the Tokugawa and extol Hideyoshi and Nobunaga, has been counterbalanced by more recent scholarship. The collection's Japan coverage is based primarily on narrative histories and offers a reasonably detailed picture of the early Tokugawa period, though specialist analysis of the clan's internal administration is thinner.
