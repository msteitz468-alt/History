## Overview

The Tay Son were three brothers — Nguyen Nhac, Nguyen Hue, and Nguyen Lu — from the upland village of Tay Son in Binh Dinh Province, whose 1771 uprising against the misgovernment of the Nguyen Phuc regent Truong Phuc Loan grew within a decade into a movement that destroyed both ruling houses of Vietnam: the southern [[nguyen-lords|Nguyen Lords]] (nearly annihilated by 1777) and the northern [[trinh-lords|Trinh Lords]] (extinguished 1786), briefly unifying the whole of Vietnamese-speaking territory from the Qing border to the Gulf of Siam under a single regime for the first time. Nguyen Nhac, the eldest, proclaimed himself "King of Tay Son" at Cha Ban (the old Cham capital near Qui Nhon) in 1776 and later "emperor of the center"; his younger brother [[nguyen-hue|Nguyen Hue]] — the dynasty's decisive military talent — took the north in 1786, proclaimed himself emperor at Phu Xuan in 1788 under the reign title Quang Trung, and won the regime's most celebrated victory by routing a Qing intervention force at Dong Da/Ngoc Hoi outside Ke Cho in early 1789.

The Tay Son were never a unified state. Nguyen Nhac and Nguyen Hue fell out over the north in 1787 and fought each other before partitioning the movement's territory between them; Nguyen Lu, given Gia Dinh, proved incapable and fled after an early defeat by the resurgent Nguyen Phuc Anh. Nguyen Hue's sudden death in 1792, at age 40 — while preparing an implausible invasion of the Qing provinces of Guangdong and Guangxi — left the regime to his 11-year-old son Nguyen Toan under a regency riven by factional murder among Tay Son generals (Vo Van Dung, Tran Quang Dieu, Bui Dac Tuyen). Nguyen Nhac died in 1793. Over the following decade [[gia-long|Nguyen Phuc Anh]], rebuilding from Saigon, systematically rolled back Tay Son territory, taking Qui Nhon, Phu Xuan, and finally Ke Cho by 1802, ending the Tay Son regime and founding the [[nguyen-dynasty|Nguyen dynasty]].

## Key Actors

- Nguyen Nhac — eldest brother; founder of the movement at An Khe (1771) and Qui Nhon (1773); "King of Tay Son" at Cha Ban from 1776; provincial in outlook, uninterested in ruling beyond Binh Dinh/Quang Nam; d. 1793.
- [[nguyen-hue|Nguyen Hue]] — the dynasty's military genius; conqueror of the north (1786), the Quang Trung emperor, victor over Siam (1785, Rach Gam-Xoai Mut) and over the Qing (1789); d. 1792.
- Nguyen Lu — youngest brother; commanded early expeditions against Gia Dinh; ineffective; fled and died c. 1787.
- Nguyen Huu Chinh — northern defector and Tay Son general whose advice prompted the 1786 march on Ke Cho; later executed by Vo Van Nham on Nguyen Hue's orders.
- Vo Van Nham, Ngo Van So, Vo Van Dung, Tran Quang Dieu, Bui Dac Tuyen — leading Tay Son generals/regents during and after Nguyen Hue's reign, whose feuds fatally weakened the post-1792 regime.
- Nguyen Toan — Nguyen Hue's child successor (r. 1792–1802); captured and executed after Gia Long's 1802 conquest of Ke Cho.

## Geographic Scope

Origin in Binh Dinh Province (An Khe, Qui Nhon, Cha Ban); at peak extent (1786–1787) controlled the entire Vietnamese-speaking territory from the Qing border to the Gulf of Siam, before contracting to a rump centered on Phu Xuan and then Qui Nhon/Binh Dinh under sustained pressure from Nguyen Phuc Anh after 1793.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Nguyen Hue lived beyond 1792, or had the Tay Son brothers cooperated rather than fought each other, the Tay Son might have consolidated a lasting unified Vietnamese state under a new dynasty rather than yielding, within a decade of Nguyen Hue's death, to the restored Nguyen Phuc line. K. W. Taylor treats the Tay Son as a failed experiment in unification rather than a national-liberation movement: Nguyen Nhac's explicit provincialism (indifference to ruling "distant places") reflects, in Taylor's reading, a genuine and historically contingent alternative to pan-Vietnamese unity — one many contemporaries in Binh Dinh saw as no less natural than eventual unification under Gia Long.

## Historiography

Drawn from K. W. Taylor's *A History of the Vietnamese* (Cambridge, 2013), which frames the Tay Son not as heroic founders of the modern nation (the standard nationalist historiographical treatment, especially of Nguyen Hue/Quang Trung as the victor over the Qing) but as one of three competing frontier military forces — alongside the Trinh remnant and Nguyen Phuc Anh — contesting Vietnamese unity in an era when, in Taylor's account, no unified outcome was inevitable. Taylor is explicit that this reading cuts against most Vietnamese national historiography's celebration of Nguyen Hue as national hero; readers should weigh this as an interpretive stance attributed to Taylor rather than settled consensus.
