# Dagobert I

**The last Merovingian king to wield real power across all [[francia|Francia]]** (sole rule
629–639), son of [[chlothar-ii|Chlothar II]]. Under him the dynasty reached its seventh-century
apogee — and after him began the long slide of royal authority that the [[rois-faineants|rois
fainéants]] legend would caricature.

## Reign and the centrality of Paris

After 629 Dagobert made **Paris** and the Neustrian court his centre, and "Paris became increasingly
the primary royal residence." He used the court as an instrument of rule: gathering regional
aristocrats "where they could be watched, but also educated and indoctrinated," then returning them
home as bishops and secular officials — extending royal influence while honouring the commitment to
appoint local men (see [[christianisation-of-the-frankish-aristocracy]]). He reserved to himself "the
right to appoint or approve bishops for the entire kingdom," Austrasia and Aquitaine included.

## St-Denis and royal sacrality

Dagobert richly endowed **St-Denis**, raising its patron "to a position of royal saint equal to that of
Saint Martin," establishing the *laus perennis* (perpetual chant), founding the October fair, and making
it the **royal burial site** — a role it kept until the French Revolution. The endowment served a dual
purpose: religious *stabilitas* for the kingdom and dynastic legitimation, and it incidentally helped
diffuse the bipartite-estate model beyond the Paris basin.

## Law and war

Dagobert codified the laws of the **Ripuarian Franks**, the **Alemanni**, and possibly the **Bavarians** —
"imposed laws," the first such legislative intervention by a Merovingian king. His military record was
mixed: Frankish armies were beaten by the Slavic **Wends under Samo (631–633)**, forcing him to
re-establish the Austrasian sub-kingdom under his son Sigibert III; and a Burgundian army was ambushed
and destroyed by **Basques** in the valley of the Soule — possibly a germ of the Roncevaux legend.

## Succession and significance

Dagobert died in 639 leaving **Sigibert III and Clovis II as minors** — beginning the near-continuous
royal minorities that, Geary argues (rejecting the myth of hereditary degeneracy), "more than any other
single factor, contributed to the decline of royal power." Four years before his death he had specified
that Sigibert inherit only [[austrasia|Austrasia]] while Clovis II received [[neustria|Neustria]] and
Burgundy — the structural division underlying all subsequent politics. He is thus the hinge figure: the
peak of Merovingian kingship and the start of its institutional decline.

## Historiography

Ironically, the very king Geary rates the greatest of the later Merovingians survives in French popular
memory through the mocking nursery rhyme *"Le bon roi Dagobert"* — a measure of how thoroughly
[[rois-faineants|Carolingian propaganda]] reshaped the dynasty's image. See
[[geary-before-france-germany-1988]].
