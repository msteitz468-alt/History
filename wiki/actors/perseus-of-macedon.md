# Perseus of Macedon

The **last king of Macedon** — son of Philip V, the final ruler of the
[[antigonid-dynasty|Antigonid line]], defeated by Rome at Pydna. Treated in
[[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 9 (Derow).

## Role and career

Perseus succeeded **[[philip-v|Philip V]]** in 179, having engineered the downfall
of his Romanophile brother **Demetrius** (murdered 180). He renewed the alliance
with Rome but at once set about reviving Macedon's standing in Greece — debt-relief,
amnesties for exiles and debtors, publicised at the great sanctuaries; a marriage
to the Seleucid princess Laodice; alliances and goodwill across Greece. Polybius
calls this *ellenokopein* — "to court the Greeks." It made Macedon, for the first
time since Philip's brutalities, an **attractive alternative focus** for Greek
politics, especially for the moderates and "the many" weary of Roman dictation.

This very success, Derow argues, doomed him: a Macedon strong enough to be an
alternative arbiter was **incompatible with Roman supremacy**. Fed by **[[pergamon|
Eumenes II]]'s** charges, Rome declared him *hostis* (171). After early Roman
failures and even a cavalry victory at Callicinus, Perseus — who repeatedly **sought
peace**, hoping to make Rome "more cautious about giving harsh orders" — was crushed
by **[[aemilius-paullus|Aemilius Paullus]]** at **Pydna** (168). He fled, surrendered
at Samothrace, and was paraded in Paullus' triumph; he died in Roman captivity.

## Counterfactual significance

Perseus is the test of Derow's thesis: he pursued not aggression but
**rehabilitation**, and was destroyed precisely *for succeeding* at it. His fall
ended the Antigonid kingdom, split Macedon into four republics, and triggered the
deportations and bloodbaths that cowed Greece. His tragedy is that "defeat, as ever,
rendered the Romans intransigent" — a negotiated peace was never really on offer.

## Historiography

[[polybius]] (with [[livy]] to 167) is the central source; the charges against
Perseus (Eumenes', the Delphi plot) are largely Roman/Pergamene propaganda. See
[[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce]] and [[nature-of-roman-imperialism]].
