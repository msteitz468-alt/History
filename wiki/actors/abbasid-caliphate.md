# The Abbasid Caliphate

The dynasty that overthrew the [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyads]] in the [[abbasid-revolution-750|Revolution of 750]]
and presided over the **classical age of Islamic civilization** — Hodgson's "High Caliphate." Centred on Baghdad, founded as a strategic administrative base that rapidly became the largest city outside China (~300,000–500,000 population). Lapidus notes: "Baghdad was the largest city in the history of the region; it was not a single city but a metropolitan center [...] It provided the wealth and manpower to govern a vast empire; it crystallized the culture that came to be identified as Islamic civilization." (Lapidus 2014, Ch. 9).

The ʿAbbasids "swept away Arab caste supremacy and accepted the universal equality of Muslims," recruiting widely from Persian Khurasanians, Nestorian Christians, and others into bureaucracy (diwans for chancery, kharaj taxation, army/jaysh). They developed the wazir office and hierarchical judges. Decline set in with succession crises, Turkish slave (ghilman) regiments, and the mihna under al-Maʾmun asserting caliphal religious authority (Quran "created"), which ultimately failed against the ʿulama. Provincial autonomy followed.
the new round city of [[baghdad|Baghdad]] (founded 762), the Abbasids built a cosmopolitan,
Persian-influenced absolutism that drew Arabs, Persians, and converts into a single
[[islamicate|Islamicate]] order. Their patronage of [[the-translation-movement|the translation movement]]
brought Greek, Persian, and Indian learning into Arabic, seeding [[islamic-philosophy|falsafah]],
[[kalam|theology]], and the sciences.

The classical caliphate's high tide under Harun al-Rashid and al-Ma'mun gave way to fiscal strain,
the reliance on Turkish slave-soldiers, and provincial fragmentation; from 945 the caliphs became
figureheads under the Buyids and later the Seljuks, though the line endured at Baghdad until the
Mongol sack of 1258. This is the political frame for the religious and intellectual flowering Hodgson's
volume traces.

## Eastward reach: Talas, Turkic dynasties, and maritime trade

As central Abbasid authority weakened from the ninth century, frontier Turkic ghulam (slave-soldier) commanders — recruited into the caliphal army as it expanded — repeatedly converted regional command into semi-independent dynastic power, the pattern Formichi (2020) identifies behind the rise of both the [[samanid-dynasty|Samanids]] and their successors the [[ghaznavid-dynasty|Ghaznavids]]; at Mahmud of Ghazni's death the Abbasid Caliph in Baghdad formally recognized the Punjab as part of the Abbasid Empire, a grant of prestige rather than direct rule. Earlier, in 751, an Abbasid army defeated a Tang Chinese force at the [[battle-of-talas-751|Battle of Talas]] near the Ferghana Valley — an encounter Formichi treats as marking the practical boundary between the Islamicate and Sinic spheres in Central Asia, after which Chinese influence receded from Transoxiana. Abbasid-era maritime trade also reached China directly: ninth-century Arab-Persian merchants recorded by Ibn Khordadbeh sailed as far as Luqin (Vietnam) and Qansu/Yangzhou, part of a wider Gulf-to-China trading network (see [[silk-roads]], [[islam-in-china]]).

## Fiscal centralization (Wickham 2005)

In [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham’s]] comparative fiscal frame, the early [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]] state was strongly **regionalized**: provincial *dīwān* armies expected to keep local tax, and central administration was funded largely from Syria–Palestine. **Only from 750** were the ʿAbbāsid caliphs able to force taxation out of local officials on any scale; **the first century of ʿAbbāsid rule** marks the only period of full financial centralization of the caliphate in Wickham’s 400–800 window — a structural intensification of the eastern [[tax-based-vs-land-based-states|tax-based state]], not a break into land-based politics. Source: [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005]].

## Symbolic caliphate and shadow revival (Cobb)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|*The Race for Paradise*]] uses the Abbasids as the political
baseline of the pre-Frankish Islamic world. By **1050** the Baghdad house still held the
caliphal title as “protectors of Islam’s frontiers, kin of God’s Prophet, and upholders (as
they saw it) of true Sunni Islam,” even while real authority had largely shed into symbolic
delegation. Cobb stresses institutional differences from the Latin papacy: caliphs were not
clerics/priests and **did not in practice preach jihads** as popes preached Crusades (theory:
they should authorize; practice: they tended to **retroactively sanction** jihads preached by
the *ulema*). Frankish observers often likened caliph to pope — Cobb treats that analogy as
misleading.

Against this Sunni emblem stood the Shiʿite ([[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]]) counter-caliphate
of Cairo (and, until the early eleventh century, the Umayyads of Córdoba): on the eve of
renewed Frankish assaults, the Near East was split between Fatimid Cairo and Abbasid Baghdad
while al-Andalus had fragmented into *ṭāʾifa* kingdoms. [[seljuk-empire|Saljuq]] sultans and
later Syrian consolidators continued to invoke Abbasid names for legitimacy (e.g. Ridwan of
Aleppo’s brief Fatimid flirtation, then reversion to Abbasid/Barkiyaruq pulpits).

**After 1258.** Mongol extinction of the Baghdad office did not end the title: [[baybars|Baybars]]
installed minor Abbasid aristocrats in Cairo (first caliph formally conferred the sultan title
**4 July 1261**); these “shadow caliphs” survived on and off in Egypt until the
[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]] demise in 1517 — a legitimating device for slave-soldier rule
rather than a restoration of High Caliphal power. When Saladin abolished the Fatimids (1171),
Friday prayer in Egypt named the Baghdad Abbasid for the first time in over two centuries —
closing the long Sunni–Shiʿi caliphal duel in Egypt under Abbasid symbolic umbrella before
the Mongol catastrophe relocated the title itself to Cairo.
