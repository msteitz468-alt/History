# Cyrus the Younger

## Overview

**Cyrus the Younger** (c. 423–401 BCE) was the younger son of the Persian king Darius II
and the brother of **[[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes II]]** — the prince whose **Persian gold
financed Sparta's victory** in the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]] and whose own
bid for the throne ended at **Cunaxa (401)**, launching the famous retreat of the
**[[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|Ten Thousand]]**. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]]
(Ch. 3, Hornblower) makes his anomalous position the key to one of the century's great
historical questions.

## The patron of Sparta

Sent down in **407** as *karanos* (supreme commander) of western Asia Minor, Cyrus
formed the decisive partnership with the Spartan admiral **[[lysander]]**, funding the
fleet "without reserve" — pay raised to four obols a day — that won **Aegospotami** and
starved Athens into surrender. His backing tipped the long war and ushered in the
[[spartan-hegemony|Spartan hegemony]].

## Cunaxa and the question of 401 vs. 334

On Darius II's death (404) the throne passed to his elder brother Artaxerxes II. Cyrus,
having narrowly escaped a charge of plotting against the new king, raised an army —
including some **13,000 Greek mercenaries**, recruited in Ionia, the Peloponnese and
Thessaly under cover of a "punitive campaign against the Pisidians" — and marched
upcountry. At **Cunaxa**, near Babylon, the Greeks routed the troops opposite them but
**Cyrus was killed charging his brother**, and the venture collapsed.

Hornblower uses the **near-success of Cyrus and his Greeks** to frame a central problem:
why did Cyrus *almost* topple Persia in 401, when [[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]] could not in
the 390s and only [[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] succeeded in 334? The answer lies in
Cyrus' **anomalous standing**: as satrap of Lydia, Greater Phrygia and Cappadocia rolled
into one, he could "turn Persia's first line of defence — the loyalty of the great west
Anatolian satraps — by being those satraps himself," and he got out of Anatolia before
Tissaphernes could stop him. The episode bred the **dangerous, delusory myth**, promoted
by Isocrates, that Persia was vulnerable to a swift *anabasis* from the west — a myth
that owed its origin precisely to 401.

## Significance

Cyrus is doubly consequential: his money decided the Peloponnesian War and so created the
Spartan hegemony, and his failed coup both produced **[[xenophon|Xenophon's]]
*Anabasis*** (the closest thing to an "inside source" on Persia) and seeded the
panhellenist fantasy of conquering Persia that Philip and Alexander would inherit.
