# The Sassanid Empire

The **second Persian empire** — the centralized Zoroastrian state founded by **[[ardashir-i|Ardashir I]]**
in the 220s on the ruins of [[parthian-empire|Parthia]], and Rome's great peer rival on its eastern
frontier for over four centuries. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 14 (**Richard N. Frye**).
In the third century the Sassanians transformed Rome's eastern problem: where Parthia had been a loose,
"feudal" confederation of semi-independent principalities, the Sassanian state was a centralized
monarchy with a parallel state church — a far more dangerous and enduring adversary, and one of the
prime external pressures behind the [[third-century-crisis|third-century crisis]].

## From Parthia to a centralized empire

Frye's organizing contrast: the later Parthian age was "a time of many semi-independent principalities,
varieties of religious expression and flourishing trading centres" (Petra, Palmyra, Hatra, Spasinou
Charax); the Sassanian empire by contrast had "a centralized court and bureaucracy with a state church
organization paralleling the hierarchy of the state," and the old "caravan cities" were destroyed or
absorbed. **[[ardashir-i|Ardashir]]**, a local ruler of **Fars** (ancient Persis), spent over a decade
consolidating the geographically self-contained province before challenging the Parthian king
**Artabanus IV**, whom he defeated and killed at the battle of **Hormizdagan** (c. 224). He took the
title ***shahanshah*** ("king of kings") of **Iran** (Eranshahr). Unlike the Parthians, who left
defeated local rulers on their thrones as vassals, Ardashir **removed many and installed members of
his own family** as kings-as-governors of the provinces — the structural basis of Sassanian
centralization.

## The wars with Rome (Frye's narrative)

The Sassanians inherited and intensified the Romano-Iranian struggle over **Armenia** and **northern
Mesopotamia**:

- **Ardashir** (from c. 230): besieged Hatra and Nisibis, took Nisibis and Carrhae (237/8) and finally
  **Hatra** (240, ending the last great caravan city of Mesopotamia). His campaigns provoked
  **[[severan-dynasty|Severus Alexander]]**'s indecisive three-pronged expedition of 232.
- **[[shapur-i|Shapur I]]** (240–c. 272): the dynasty's conqueror. He killed **Gordian III** at
  Misiche (244, renamed Perozshapur), extracted 500,000 denarii from **Philip the Arabian**, destroyed
  a 60,000-strong Roman army at Barbalissus (252), sacked Antioch (twice), and — in his third campaign —
  achieved the **unexampled [[capture-of-valerian-260|capture of the emperor Valerian]]** near Edessa
  (260), commemorated in the great rock reliefs of Fars and in his trilingual **Res Gestae Divi Saporis**
  (the *ŠKZ* at Naqsh-i-Rustam). But Shapur sought **booty and prisoners, not permanent conquest** — and
  his western gains were rolled back not by Rome but by **[[zenobia|Odenathus]] of [[palmyra|Palmyra]]**.
- **Narses** (293–302): after dynastic turmoil, reverted to aggression, defeating **Galerius** near
  Carrhae (296), but was then **routed east of Satala** (297/8) and forced into the humiliating
  **[[peace-of-nisibis-298|peace of 298]]** — ceding trans-Tigritane lands and accepting a 40-year peace
  that held until Shapur II reopened the wars c. 337 (beyond this volume).

## The Zoroastrian state church

Frye's second great theme is **religion as the new mark of identity**. Under Ardashir and Shapur a
**hierarchical Zoroastrian state church** crystallized, paralleling the secular hierarchy: at its head the
**mobadan mobad** (chief of priests), mirror of the *shahanshah*. The driving figure was the high priest
**Kartir (Kerdir)**, powerful under the Vahrans, who organized the church, persecuted minority faiths,
and helped codify the **Avesta**. Religious policy swung between the **tolerance of Shapur I** (who
favoured **[[manichaeism|Mani]]**) and the **intolerance of the Vahrans** (Mani martyred c. 276). The
third–fourth centuries were, for both empires, the age when "ancient beliefs were being replaced by
organized religions" — Zoroastrianism, Christianity, Talmudic Judaism, and the new universalizing rival
**[[manichaeism|Manichaeism]]**. The Sassanian state organized its minority faiths (Jews under an
**exilarch**, Christians under a patriarch) in what later became the **milet system** — communities
self-governing in taxation and law, but second-class. After **[[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]'s**
conversion, Sassanian Christians fell under suspicion as a Roman fifth column.

## Coinage and economy

Frye contrasts the **stable silver Sassanian *dram*** (drachm, ~4 g of high-fineness silver, flat and
easily stacked, kept steady throughout the dynasty) with the **debased Roman coinage** of the crisis (see
[[late-roman-coinage]]). Silver, not gold, was the Persian and central-Asian preference; far more
Sassanian silver plate reached Russia and the steppe than Roman. Sassanian coins carried **no political
propaganda** — only the ruler's distinctive crown and a pious legend — reflecting a purely economic
conception of coinage. Frye's verdict: as Rome's economic prestige declined, the Sassanians' rose among
the peoples of the north and east.

## Significance

Frye's conclusion reframes the Sassanians: **not "barbarians" but a peer state** — "the *lex* of the
Romans was matched by the *dad* of the Persians" — whose pretenders could take honourable refuge at the
rival court as equals. Where Rome had a renaissance and a revival of secular Roman law, Sassanian law
became essentially **religious law** administered by Zoroastrian priests, prefiguring the medieval church-
law of the West. The empire bequeathed its state structure and its fusion of religion and state to the
**Arab–Islamic** order that overthrew it in the seventh century, transmitting more of the ancient Near
Eastern inheritance than Rome did. For the wiki it anchors the eastern frontier of the whole late-antique
period.

## The fourth century: Shapur II and the treaty of 363 (CAH XIII)

[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Chs. 13–14 (**R. C. Blockley**; **Benjamin Isaac**) carry the eastern
frontier through the fourth century, dominated by the long reign of **Shapur II** (309–379). Renewing the
struggle over **[[armenia|Armenia]]** and **Mesopotamia** lost at the [[peace-of-nisibis-298|peace of 298]],
Shapur fought a protracted war of **sieges** against [[constantius-ii|Constantius II]], who held the
fortified towns (above all **Nisibis**, besieged three times) in a deliberately defensive strategy. The
turning point was [[julian|Julian's]] catastrophic invasion of 363: after Julian's death, **Jovian** bought
peace by the humiliating **treaty of 363**, surrendering **Nisibis**, the trans-Tigritane lands, and Roman
claims in Armenia. Renewed friction under [[valentinian-and-valens|Valens]] over Armenia ended with the
**partition of Armenia (c. 387)** between Rome and Persia — and thereafter a remarkably **stable peace**
(the embassies of Anthemius/Marutha to Yezdegerd I; the Persian Christians' synod of 410), the eastern
frontier ceasing to be Rome's chief worry while the West fragmented.

## Update — the Sasanid monarchy and Khusro I (5th–6th c., CAH XIV)

[[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] (Rubin, Ch. 21) treats the Sasanid state at its late-antique height and
revises its reputation for tidy centralization. The dynasty's own propaganda (the *Kārnāmag*, the
*Khwadāy-nāmag* tradition via al-Ṭabarī) cast Ardashir's rise as the unification of Iran against the "petty
kings," contrasting a strong Sasanid monarchy with a feeble Parthian one — but Rubin shows central control
was **never uniformly effective**: kings founded cities only in zones they directly held, never encroaching
on the lands of the **great noble houses** (many with Parthian-era roots) whose power constrained the throne.

The pivot of the chapter is the **crisis and reforms around 500–550**:

- **The Mazdakite upheaval.** The radical, possibly communalizing **Mazdak** movement won huge popularity —
  and the cynical backing of **King Kavadh I**, who used it to break the power of the nobility and clergy.
- **The reforms of Kavadh and Khusro I.** Out of the crisis came the one serious **tax reform** of Sasanid
  history (begun under Kavadh, completed by **Khusro I Anūshirwān**, r. 531–579): a fixed land-tax and
  poll-tax replacing the old proportional levy, paired with an **agricultural reform**, and a **military
  reform** creating a salaried **standing cavalry** (the *dehqāns* and the four regional *spāhbads*)
  dependent directly on the crown rather than on the great lords. Khusro's settlement made him the model of
  the **just king** in later Perso-Islamic tradition.
- **Limits.** Rubin is sceptical of the reforms' durability: the nobility Khusro "restored" remained
  dangerous (revolts dogged his successors), and the system frayed by his reign's end — though
  **Khusro II** (590–628) still drew impressive revenues from the machinery Khusro I built.

Abroad, Khusro I fought [[justinian|Justinian]] (the "**eternal peace**" of 532, then renewed war from 540 —
the sack of Antioch 540), and his grandson **Khusro II**, restored to the throne by the emperor
[[successors-of-justinian|Maurice]] (591), would launch the last great Roman–Persian war after Maurice's
murder — the mutual exhaustion that opened the way to the [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam|Arab conquests]]
that destroyed the Sasanid empire (651). Source: [[cameron-cah-v14-2000]] (Ch. 21, Zeev Rubin).
