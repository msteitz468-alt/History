# The Sangam Age (Early Tamil Kingdoms)

The **Sangam age** (c. 3rd c. BCE – 3rd c. CE) is the dawn of recorded history in the **Tamil south**
— the era of the **"three crowned kings,"** the **Cheras, Cholas, and Pandyas**, known from the
**Sangam literature**, the earliest body of Tamil poetry. It marks south India's transition "from
prehistory into history." Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## The three kingdoms

Ashoka's inscriptions already name the **Cholas, Pandyas, Satiyaputras, and Keralaputras** of the
far south as polities beyond his empire. The Sangam-age south was dominated by the **Cheras
(Keralas)** of the west coast, the **Cholas** of the Kaveri, and the **Pandyas** of Madurai —
perpetually at war, which "gave ample scope to the poets for heroic ballads." Megasthenes records a
**Pandya** kingdom "founded by the daughter of Herakles" (perhaps a memory of the **matrilineal**
society that survived in Kerala into modern times) with an army of 500 elephants. The Tamils built a
navy and raided **Ceylon** (2nd c. BCE) until expelled by the Sinhalese king Dutthagamini; the Chola
hero **Karikala** ("the man with the charred leg") won the battle of **Venni** over a Pandya-Chera
coalition. The kings were still essentially **war-leaders** protecting tribe and territory, with
village councils and the **temple** beginning the rise to the central role they would later play.

## The Sangam literature

The age's records are the **Sangam literature** — anthologies of over 2,000 poems by bards, akin to
the Vedic corpus in being collective but, unlike it, **secular in origin** (heroic and love poetry,
not religious hymns). Tradition ascribes them to **three successive academies (*Sangams*)** at
**Madurai**, the Pandya capital; the surviving Eight Anthologies and the *Tolkappiyam* (the earliest
Tamil grammar) come from the later academies. This literature is the wiki's window onto a distinct,
non-Sanskritic **Dravidian** civilization with its own gods — above all **Murugan**, god of war and
fertility, worshipped with blood-offerings and ecstatic dancing — and the cult of **hero-stones**
commemorating fallen warriors.

## Aryanisation and trade

The Tamil south did not stay at a pastoral-agrarian stage. Two forces transformed it: the spreading
**Aryan/Sanskritic pattern** (hereditary kingship, taxation, Vedic ritual — the Chola Nalangilli
performed Vedic sacrifices), carried south by Jain and Buddhist missions and the
[[satavahana-empire|Satavahana]] link; and, decisively, **integration into the Indian Ocean economy**
— the **[[indian-ocean-trade|Roman trade]]** concentrated on the Tamil coasts (the *yavana* merchants
of Kaveripattinam; the Roman station at Arikamedu), ending the south's isolation and accelerating
its move toward complex statehood.

## Significance

The Sangam age establishes the deep antiquity and autonomy of **Tamil/Dravidian civilization** — a
counterweight, in Thapar's north–south framing, to the Gangetic-centred narrative. Its three
kingdoms are the direct ancestors of the medieval [[chola-empire|Chola]], Pandya, and Chera/Kerala
states.
