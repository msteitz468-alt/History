# The Tudor Dynasty (the Foundation under Henry VII)

The English royal house founded by **Henry VII** (r. 1485–1509), whose victory at Bosworth ended the
[[wars-of-the-roses-1455|Wars of the Roses]]. Treated by Rosemary Horrox in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|
NCMH VII]] (Ch. 21b). Horrox's revisionist account dismantles the old image of Henry as the
institution-building "new monarch" who tamed an over-mighty nobility, presenting him instead as a
shrewd **consolidator and formaliser** of Yorkist practice who "succeeded because his leading
subjects wanted him to succeed."

## Founding Conditions

Henry Tudor — son of Margaret Beaufort and Edmund Tudor (half-brother of Henry VI) — had "virtually
non-existent" title; his victory at **Bosworth (1485)** owed much to French and Scottish backing and
to disenchantment with Richard III rather than enthusiasm for himself. He secured his position by
marrying **Elizabeth of York** (uniting the claims), by reconciling former Ricardians, and by
defeating the Yorkist rising at **Stoke (1487)** — the last battle of the wars. Persistent
pretenders (**Lambert Simnel**; **Perkin Warbeck**, backed in turn by France, Margaret of York's
Burgundy, and Maximilian) tested but never toppled the regime; the de la Pole claim lingered.

## Structural Features (Horrox's revision)

- **Continuity, not innovation.** "Very little of what he did was new — and this is not a criticism."
  Henry refined the **chamber finance** pioneered by Edward IV (revenues paid directly to the
  chamber, which he personally audited) and the exploitation of **prerogative rights**, raising money
  "far more effective[ly] than anything the Yorkists had managed."
- **Security through bonds.** He made near-routine use of **bonds and recognisances** to ensure the
  good behaviour of his greater subjects, and amassed treasure on Fortescue's principle that a king
  must be richer than his subjects.
- **No structural shift of power.** Horrox denies any deliberate campaign to break the nobility or to
  shift power from "periphery" to "centre": Henry still governed through noblemen as his local
  representatives and the gentry of the household, "adjusting means, not redefining ends." His
  creation of a **privy chamber** (c. 1495) reflected a more reclusive style, not a constitutional
  revolution.
- **Diplomacy.** An "unknown, and probably underestimated" force who became "a shrewd and forceful
  diplomat": the Treaties of **Medina del Campo (1489)** (the Spanish marriage alliance) and
  **Étaples (1492)** (ending French support for Warbeck and restoring the Picquigny pension) gave
  England "a very respectable appearance" of holding the European balance.

## Significance

The Tudor foundation is the English counterpart to the consolidation of the **[[new-monarchy|"new
monarchy"]]** in [[valois-dynasty|France]] and Iberia — but Horrox cautions that its achievements,
resting on **personal monarchy**, were "accordingly vulnerable to change": Henry VIII kept the
chamber machinery but spent its reserves. The dynasty's durable legacy was less institutional than
political: a polity exhausted by civil war and newly committed to effective royal authority.
