# The Mendicant Orders (Franciscans and Dominicans)

## Founding and the "Fourth Institution"

[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (André Vauchez, Ch. 2e) takes its frame from Jacques de
Vitry (c. 1220), who saw the friars as a **"fourth institution"** of religious life added
to the hermits, monks, and canons. The two original Mendicant Orders were the **Friars
Minor** (Franciscans), stemming from **St Francis of Assisi**, and the **Friars Preacher**
(Dominicans), founded by **St Dominic of Guzmán**. Their novelty was to renounce the
profane life "only in order better to turn towards" the laity — unlike the cloistered,
stability-bound monks, the friars were mobile, urban, and pastoral, living by **begging**
(hence *mendicant*) and dedicated to **preaching**.

Vauchez frames the friars' rise as the counterpart to a **decline of the older orders**:
the independent Benedictine houses and great congregations (Cluny, La Chaise-Dieu,
Saint-Victor) faced shrinking aristocratic patronage, fixed rents eroded by inflation,
strained relations with the rising cities, and the revived assertiveness of the secular
clergy and bishops. Cluny by 1300 was "a mere shadow of its former self." The friars'
success, Vauchez argues, was as much a **consequence** as a cause of this decline.

## Dominicans vs. Franciscans

Both begged and preached, but Vauchez draws a sharp contrast:

- **The Dominicans** were an order of **priests and doctors**: preaching rooted in
  learning, formed against the [[catharism|Cathars]] and Waldensians whom Dominic had
  confronted in Languedoc. For them **poverty was a weapon against heresy** — a necessary
  condition of credible preaching — but not an absolute virtue. They behaved less rigidly
  on poverty and integrated quickly into the [[medieval-university|universities]].
- **The Franciscans** under Francis **identified poverty with the evangelical life
  itself** — a refusal to own anything, individually or in common. This made the order's
  later history turbulent.

## The Franciscan Poverty Controversy

After Francis's death the order's rapid growth and clericalization strained its founding
ideal:

- The bull ***Quo elongati* (1230)** exempted the friars from observing Francis's
  *Testament* and ruled that to be a good friar one need only observe the *Rule* —
  permitting, against Francis's express wishes, settled houses and resources acquired
  through intermediaries.
- This opened a split between the **Conventuals** (accommodating practical needs, led by
  ministers like Elias of Cortona and later the great theologian-minister **Bonaventure**)
  and the **Spirituals** (insisting on absolute poverty, drawing on the apocalyptic
  expectations of [[joachim-of-fiore|Joachim of Fiore]]).
- The compromise doctrine — that the friars **used** goods of which ownership was vested
  in the papacy — was ratified by the bull ***Exiit qui seminat* (1279)**. The dispute
  nonetheless festered into the fourteenth-century crisis over evangelical poverty.

## Expansion and Conflict

Other orders were drawn into the mendicant model: the **Augustinian Hermits** (united
1256), the **Carmelites**, and short-lived groups (the Friars of the Sack, the
Apostolics of Gerard Segarelli). Their very proliferation, after 1250, generated friction
— above all the **secular–mendicant dispute at Paris**, where the secular master
**William of St Amour** attacked the friars' privileges and university chairs; the papacy
backed the friars, and William was condemned. By the later thirteenth century the
mendicants dominated preaching, confession, the universities, and the
[[medieval-inquisition|Inquisition]] — the most dynamic religious force of the age, and
the institutional embodiment of the [[urbanization|new urban Christianity]].

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan’s ch. 11 thesis: movements of dissent and mendicancy formed part of one scene in which increasing wealth failed to satisfy spiritual desire; “the voluntary poverty of Waldès and others like him was shared, in a strikingly similar way, by Francis of Assisi.” The friars “emerged from forces similar to those that gave rise to heresy, yet the papacy, perhaps having learned from the handling of the [[catharism|Cathars]] and [[waldensians|Waldensians]], was able to turn their energies to constructive use within the church.” They were “**the last great innovation of the medieval church**”: not cloistered monks or semi-cloistered canons but urban, open to neighbours’ spiritual needs, originally spontaneous and largely lay.

**Fourth Lateran** forbade new religious rules — a serious obstacle for later mendicants. [[franciscan-order|Franciscans]] avoided it by [[innocent-iii|Innocent III]]’s pre-council approval; [[dominican-order|Dominicans]] by adopting the Rule of St Augustine when approaching Innocent weeks before the council. **Carmelites**: Western hermits on Mount Carmel received a “formula of life” c.1206–14 from the Latin patriarch of Jerusalem; diaspora from ~1238–40s to the West; **Innocent IV (1247)** revised the rule allowing towns and common meals — urban mendicants (England: Hulne, Aylesford, then London, Cambridge, etc.; ~1,000 in England by 1300). **Austin Friars**: papal unions of Italian hermits (Innocent IV 1244 Tuscan union; **Alexander IV 1256** Great Union — Order of Hermit Friars of St Augustine); >500 houses within a century. **Second Lyons (1274)** forbade lesser friar orders to accept new members. Franciscan property compromises (Gregory IX 1230 agents; Innocent IV 1245 ownership vested in Holy See) belong to the same institutionalization process (detail under [[franciscan-order]]).
