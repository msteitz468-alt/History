# Li Zhi (Li Chih, 1527–1602)

Radical late-Ming thinker, historian, essayist, and self-appointed "group conscience of all the literati." The central subject of Chapter 7 in Huang's *1587, A Year of No Significance*, presented as a man of "a Divided Conscience" whose pursuit of intellectual honesty exposed the structural inability of the Ming order to accommodate or even tolerate sustained personal integrity outside conventional roles.

## Early Life, Poverty, and Official Career

Born in Ch'üan-chou (Quanzhou), Fukien, in 1527. His sixth-generation ancestor Lin Nu had been a merchant trading on the Persian Gulf who married a woman of central Asian origin; earlier cosmopolitan and Islamic family ties had "completely vanished" by Li's great-grandfather's time. From childhood he was "deeply imbued with traditional Chinese culture."

Poverty and personal stubbornness shaped him. After receiving his licentiate's degree in 1552, he entered officialdom rather than pursuing the metropolitan examinations. He endured long years of poorly paid, low-ranking appointments interrupted by mourning periods (including one twenty-month wait for a vacancy). He served as director of Confucian studies at county level (three years), instructor at the Imperial University in Nanking (two years), manager of the business office of the Ministry of Rites (five years), and vice director at the Ministry of Justice in Nanking (nearly five years) before his final post as prefect of Yao-an in Yunnan (1577).

He had four sons and three daughters; all but the eldest daughter died young (one son by drowning, two daughters from malnutrition). Li himself recalled seven days without recognizing whether food was rice or sorghum. The prefectural post provided customary fees that allowed modest savings, which he later turned over to his wife; he did not apply the rigid Hai Jui standard that anything beyond official salary was theft.

## Resignation, Kinship Pressures, and Search for Independence

In 1580, at age fifty-three, Li resigned, forfeiting possible further promotion. He and his wife became houseguests of the Keng brothers in Hukwang for three years. His wife had endured the hardships of his career and initially supported the decision, but the final break came when Li moved into a Buddhist temple. She was escorted home to Ch'üan-chou (which she had not seen for two decades) by their daughter and son-in-law. She continued to plead for his return; news of her death reached him later.

Li's wish for detachment must be understood against gentry kinship obligations. Even as a low-ranking licentiate in mourning, he had been compelled to feed up to thirty kinsfolk during pirate invasions "when 'grain could not even be bought.'" The "cult of ancestor-worship" and clan solidarity committed individuals to "the obsessive acquisition of land, the compulsive choice of official careers, the promotion of education for the sole purpose of perpetuating the system." Before his tonsure, "people of no importance from home" repeatedly pressed him to do "something against my wish." His clan compiled a Confucian-style genealogy. Even after tonsure, the family designated a nephew as his "adopted son" against his wishes; Li wrote that the unwanted heir must be enjoined not to cry at his funeral (1596).

## Tonsure, the Hall of Buddha in Fragrant Iris, and Life as Literati Conscience

In 1588 (the Year of the Pig) Li shaved his head and adopted a life "halfway between those of a Buddhist monk and a Confucian scholar." His wife died the same year; his only surviving daughter was already with her husband. "Li felt that now he could detach himself from his mundane obligations to his kin and lead his life as he wished." Yet he did not flee worldly affairs: "from this time until his death Li Chih practically appointed himself the group conscience of all the literati."

He constructed (largely through solicited contributions from official friends) the "Hall of Buddha in Fragrant Iris" near Ma-ch'eng—a private chapel on a cliff, disproportionately large, tax-free but unlicensed, with no specific sect or governing body. Li was its sole trustee and elder. He maintained a gentry lifestyle: long beard despite tonsure, meticulously laundered garments, servants constantly sweeping, sedan chair even for short distances, books read aloud by attendants. He did not take vows, participate in the monks' prayers, or adhere to vegetarianism.

For a dozen years his main occupation was writing and publishing: expositions of classics, comments on history and fiction, philosophical essays, notes, poetry, and correspondence. He kept printing plates in storage.

## The Conflict with Keng Ting-hsiang and the Creed of Individualism

Li's intellectual development was sharpened by his debate with Keng Ting-hsiang (and especially the younger brother Keng Ting-li). Keng Ting-li, brilliant and honest, concluded that "Confucian benevolence was self-negation" and wisdom a state of detachment "odorless and soundless." Li held that "ethics and morals are no more than food and clothing," or the livelihood of the common people. Ting-li kept peace between his elder brother and Li until his death in 1584.

Keng Ting-hsiang (recalled to Peking as left assistant censor-in-chief) expressed displeasure that Li had "misled his sons and nephews." This prompted Li's move to Ma-ch'eng. Li took the accusation as personal insult. He viewed Keng as maintaining a double standard; Keng saw Li as a nonconformist for its own sake who sometimes indulged in exhibitionism.

Li's own criticism of bureaucratic hypocrisy was intrapersonal. He portrayed himself at times as "a peddler in mind and practice" who cloaked himself in the robe of Buddha in order "to deceive the world and earn for himself a false reputation" of being a man of benevolence. In a letter to Vice-Minister Chou Ssu-ching he wrote with self-pity: "I am not dying this year and may not even die next year. Waiting for death year in and year out, I am growing restless. While death does not come, woes are approaching. Yet those woes are not approaching fast enough!"

## Trial, Death, and Huang's Interpretation

Li's writings were repeatedly proscribed. In 1602 he was arrested in Beijing on charges of heterodoxy and "deceiving the world." While bleeding from a self-inflicted razor wound in prison, his jailer asked why he had taken such a step. His last words were: "What else could a man over seventy do?"

At trial he defended: "the accused has written many books, which should benefit the propagation of the teaching of the sages rather than damaging it"—a position Huang compares (with important differences) to Martin Luther's.

Huang insists Li was no martyr with a noble cause and no spokesman for the peasantry. He remained supported by gentry-official friends to the end and "never did he question the impropriety of this arrangement. Nor did he even remotely suggest that the social order from which he drew his sustenance should be reconstructed in any way." His drama was "internal and intellectual: the conflict between a demand for personal honesty and the social/ideological order that made sustained honesty nearly impossible."

"The traditional statecraft could not be replaced, and as long as this was the case China would not experience Reformation. In this social climate intellectual freedom and even personal honesty could hardly be expected to thrive." Li's "divided conscience" mirrored the era's frustration.
