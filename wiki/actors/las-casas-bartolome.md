# Bartolomé de las Casas

The **Dominican friar and "Protector of the Indians"** who became the conscience —
and, to Spain's rivals, the most useful witness — of the Spanish conquest. A former
conquistador and *encomendero* turned vehement opponent of conquest and forced
conversion, Las Casas is the pivotal figure in the sixteenth-century debate over
the **justice of empire** and the source of the polemic that became the
[[black-legend|Black Legend]].

## Role and career

Las Casas first came to the Caribbean as a settler and held an *encomienda* before
a conversion of conscience (c. 1514) led him to renounce it and enter the Dominican
order. For half a century he denounced the **mistreatment of the indigenous peoples**
before the Crown and in print, most famously in the *Brevísima relación de la
destrucción de las Indias* (1552), a harrowing catalogue of conquistador atrocities.

His advocacy carried real weight: his denunciations **influenced the Crown's
promulgation of the [[spanish-conquest-of-peru-1532|New Laws of 1542]]**, which
sought to phase out the *encomienda* and protect native peoples — and which
triggered Gonzalo Pizarro's revolt in Peru and unrest in Mexico.

## The Valladolid debate (1550–51)

The climax of the controversy. Against the humanist **Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda** —
who deployed Aristotle's doctrine of **natural slavery** to argue that the natives'
inferiority, idolatry, and cannibalism justified war and forced conversion — Las
Casas maintained that the indigenous peoples possessed full **rational capacity**,
could be Christianized peacefully, and were entitled to their lands and liberty.
Charles I convened a panel of theologians and jurists at **Valladolid** and ordered
conquests suspended pending its judgment. The panel reached **no formal resolution**,
and conquest (renamed "pacification" in Philip II's 1573 ordinance) continued — but
the debate established, against [[pizarro-francisco|the men of the conquest]], that
the morality of empire was genuinely contestable within Spain itself. It built on
the bull *Sublimis Deus* (Pope Paul III, 1537), which had declared the natives
"truly men" with immortal souls who could not lawfully be enslaved.

## Significance

Las Casas is doubly consequential. He represents the **internal Iberian critique**
of conquest — evidence that the *requerimiento*, the *encomienda*, and the violence
were debated, not merely assumed. And his writings, seized on and amplified by
England, the Dutch, and Spain's other rivals, became the documentary backbone of
the **[[black-legend|Black Legend]]** — so that the most damning indictment of
Spanish cruelty came from a Spaniard. Modern historians debate whether his
atrocity-counts were exaggerated for rhetorical effect, but his role as a founder
of arguments about indigenous rights and just war (alongside
[[colonial-latin-america|Francisco de Vitoria]] and the law of nations) is secure.
