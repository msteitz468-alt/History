# Liberia

**Liberia** was, with **[[solomonic-ethiopia|Ethiopia]]**, one of only **two African states to remain
independent** through the colonial Scramble — but its independence was of a peculiar kind: a republic
founded by **freed African-American settlers**. Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO
[[gha-vol7-colonial-1880-1935|General History of Africa, Vol. VII]]** (chs. 10, 25, with Ethiopia).

## A settler republic

Liberia originated in **1822** as a settlement of the **American Colonization Society**, which resettled
**freed and freeborn African-Americans** on the West African coast (the capital, **Monrovia**, named for
US President Monroe). These **Americo-Liberian** settlers declared an independent **republic in 1847** —
the first in Africa — with a constitution modelled on the United States. But the settler minority
established a **dominance over the indigenous peoples** strikingly parallel to the colonialism around them:
the Americo-Liberian elite (the True Whig Party) monopolized power and the franchise, while indigenous
Liberians were largely excluded — a settler/indigenous divide that shaped the country into the twentieth
century (and erupted in the 1980 coup and later civil wars).

## Survival amid the Scramble

Liberia survived the Scramble through **US informal protection** and adroit diplomacy, but at a cost: it
**lost large territories** to encroaching British (Sierra Leone) and French claims, and fell into deep
**financial dependence** on European and American creditors. In **1926** it granted the American
**Firestone** company a vast rubber-plantation concession — a near-monopoly that made Liberia economically
dependent on a single foreign corporation, a classic case of **informal-empire** economics even in a
nominally sovereign state.

## Significance

Liberia is the ambiguous African "survivor" of the colonial era — formally independent yet built on a
**settler hierarchy** and bound by **economic dependence**. With Ethiopia it anchored the symbolism of
African independence and **Pan-Africanism**, even as its internal structure reproduced the inequalities of
the colonial order.
