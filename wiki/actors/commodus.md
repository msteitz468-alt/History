# Commodus (Lucius Aurelius Commodus / Marcus Aurelius Commodus Antoninus)

The **son and successor of [[marcus-aurelius|Marcus Aurelius]]** (sole emperor 180–192) — "a
Caesar born to the purple," whose accession **broke the run of adoptive emperors** and whose
autocratic, increasingly megalomaniac reign ended in assassination, closing the High Empire and
opening the way to the civil wars of 193. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 3.

## The end of the adoptive succession

Unlike the four emperors before him — each **chosen by adoption for merit** — Commodus was Marcus'
**natural son**, raised to *Caesar* (166) and co-*Augustus* (177) and inheriting the throne by
blood at his father's death on the Danube (**180**). CAH XI presents this as Marcus' great
departure from the principle that had given the empire its "good emperors": Commodus was the first
emperor "born to the purple" since Domitian, and his reign is the standard marker of the **end of
the golden age**.

## The reign

His first act was to **abandon Marcus' unfinished [[marcomannic-wars-166-180|Danube wars]]**,
buying peace with the Marcomanni and Quadi and returning to Rome — a reversal the army and Senate
resented but which was arguably prudent. Hostile to the **Senate** and uninterested in
administration, Commodus increasingly left government to a succession of powerful favourites — the
praetorian prefects **Perennis** and then the freedman **Cleander** — whose ascendancies and falls
(amid food riots and intrigue) the sources chronicle. A **conspiracy in 182** (involving his
sister Lucilla) deepened his suspicion of the aristocracy and triggered executions. In his last
years he descended into **megalomania**: identifying himself with **Hercules**, renaming Rome
*Colonia Commodiana* and the months after himself, and performing as a **gladiator** in the arena
— the conduct that fixed his image as a tyrant in the senatorial tradition.

## Assassination and aftermath

Commodus was **assassinated on the last day of 192** by a conspiracy of his own intimates (his
mistress Marcia, the chamberlain Eclectus, the praetorian prefect Laetus) — strangled by the
wrestler Narcissus — followed by *damnatio memoriae*. His murder, with no heir and no agreed
successor, **plunged the empire into the civil war of 193** (the brief reigns of Pertinax and
Didius Julianus, the auction of the empire, and the rise of Septimius Severus) — the subject of
CAH XII.

## Significance

Commodus' reign is the **hinge between the High Empire and the third-century crisis**. His blood
succession ended the adoptive system; his autocracy and contempt for the Senate previewed the
military monarchy to come; and his assassination, leaving the throne to be fought over, inaugurated
the pattern of **violent successions** that would dominate the next century. In the Roman
tradition (and in modern memory) he stands as the emperor whose folly squandered the inheritance
of the "Five Good Emperors."

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]] opens the narrative of decline with Marcus’s hereditary choice and Commodus’s reign as the end of the Antonine golden age. **Gibbon’s views.**

- **Marcus’s hereditary error:** mildness and fond partiality made him “sacrifice[] the happiness of millions to a fond partiality for a worthless boy” rather than choose a successor in the republic.
- **Character:** “Nature had formed him of a weak rather than a wicked disposition” — unprovoked cruelties unexplained by ordinary civil-war motives (he had nothing to wish and everything to enjoy).
- **Gladiatorial self-display:** ignorant of the distinction between professional fighters and imperial dignity, he exhibited himself before the Romans and “attained the summit of vice and infamy.”
- **Christians under Commodus:** hardships under “virtuous” Marcus ceased; concubine Marcia patronized Christians — “as none except themselves had experienced the injustice of Marcus, so they alone were protected by the lenity of Commodus” (~thirteen years of relative safety).
