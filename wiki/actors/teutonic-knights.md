# The Teutonic Knights and the Baltic Crusade

The **Teutonic Order** was the German military-religious order that, in the thirteenth century,
built a **theocratic conquest-state in Prussia** and made itself the spearhead of the
**Baltic Crusade**. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Michael Burleigh, Ch. 18b) traces its
origins in the Levant, its accumulation of a north-European landed base, and its redeployment to
the pagan Baltic.

## From the Levant to the Baltic

Founded as a hospital brotherhood during the [[crusades|crusades]] (the third great military
Order after the Templars and Hospitallers), the Order — under the brilliant Grand Master
**Hermann von Salza** (a former *ministerialis*) — won exemptions and the right to receive
**commutations of crusading vows**, building a landed power-base in Germany. Its brethren were
drawn largely from the **ministeriales** and lesser nobility, for whom the Order offered "a form
of corporatist social mobility." After a failed venture in **Transylvania** (invited by Andrew
II of Hungary, expelled 1225 when it sought papal protection), the Order accepted **Duke Conrad
of Masovia's** invitation to defend Poland's frontier against the pagan **Prussians**.

## The Conquest-State in Prussia

The Order's genius was **legal-political**: it secured a thicket of "mutually contradictory and
competing privileges" to make its conquests its own — **Frederick II's Golden Bull of Rimini
(1226)** (granting prospective imperial rights in Prussia), the **Treaty of Kruschwitz** with
Duke Conrad, and papal privileges asserting papal overlordship — sidelining both its Piast host
and the would-be bishop Christian. From **1230** the knights pushed up the **Vistula** from
Thorn, building timber fortresses (Kulm, Marienwerder, Elbing), campaigning in winter when the
rivers froze, "to convert or destroy" the Prussians. It absorbed the rival **Brethren of the
Sword** in Livonia (1237), but could not link the two spheres against the Samogitians and
**Lithuanians**. Prussian **rebellions** (notably 1242, in alliance with the duke of Pomerelia)
were suppressed; the **Treaty of Christburg (1249)** guaranteed converted Prussians their
liberties, conditional on continued Christianity and submission to the Order.

## Ostsiedlung and Significance

The Prussian conquest was "an integral part of the more complex process of **German settlement
in the east**" (the *Ostsiedlung*), against which Burleigh warns of nationalist myth-making.
Settlers — many from Lübeck, Brandenburg and Silesia, recruited by professional *Lokatoren* — took
land on favourable terms (large, heritable, alienable holdings under the *Hufen* measure),
distinct from the heavier obligations of the native Prussian peasantry. Yet the Order, jealous of
rivals, allowed few monasteries, and genuine **Christianisation** of the Prussian interior was
slow (largely a post-Reformation achievement).

The Teutonic Order is the volume's prime case of the **frontier crusade as state-building**: a
religious corporation that became a territorial sovereign on the pagan Baltic frontier, fusing
crusade, colonisation and German eastward expansion — the matrix of later Prussia. Its turn to
the Baltic was confirmed when the **[[fall-of-acre-1291|fall of Acre]]** (1291) ended the
Order's Levantine raison d'être.

## The Fourteenth-Century Ordensstaat and Its Crisis (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (S.C. Rowell, Ch. 17) shows the Order's **monastic state
(*Ordensstaat*)** at its height in the fourteenth century — a centralised, bureaucratic, prosperous
territory (its grand master's residence the vast brick castle of **Marienburg**), enriched by the
[[hanseatic-league|Hanseatic]] grain and amber trade and by a steady stream of crusading volunteers.
Its great enterprise was the ***reysa*** — the seasonal crusade against pagan
**[[grand-duchy-of-lithuania|Lithuania]]**, "established in the chivalric calendar throughout the
Catholic world" (Chaucer's Knight rode "in Lettow... and in Ruce"). But the Order's raison d'être
**collapsed** when Lithuania converted to Catholicism in the **Union of Krewo (1385–1386)** and
joined Poland: the crusade lost its justification, and the combined Polish–Lithuanian power crushed
the Order at **Tannenberg/Grunwald (1410)** — beginning the long decline that ended its
sovereignty in 1525.
