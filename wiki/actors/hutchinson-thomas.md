# Thomas Hutchinson

## Overview

**Thomas Hutchinson** (1711–1780), last civilian royal governor of Massachusetts,
was the foremost Loyalist intellectual and administrator in the province and the
principal local antagonist of [[adams-samuel|Samuel Adams]]. Historian of
Massachusetts, chief justice, and lieutenant governor before the governorship, he
defended parliamentary sovereignty and hierarchical order against what he saw as
demagogic disorder. [[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Stacy Schiff]] (2022)
tracks him as the indispensable foil—able, honest by his lights, deeply attached to
New England, and fatally outmaneuvered in the contest for public opinion. His own
epigram of exile (1779): “Everything in American affairs happens contrary to
probability.”

## Role

Plural office-holder at the apex of provincial power. By 1763 he was, in Schiff’s
summary, New England’s most prominent and popular citizen: lieutenant governor
(1758; again 1760), acting governor (1760), chief justice of the Superior Court
(from the early 1760s, though not a lawyer), probate judge, and Council member
simultaneously. [[adams-john|John Adams]] sniffed that most took him for “the first
man on the continent.” He began a history of Massachusetts Bay in leisure hours
(rarely more than two pages a sitting); volume 1 (to bookstores December 1764)
covered 1620–1692 and stressed colonists’ departure “with the strongest assurances
that they and their posterity should enjoy the privileges of free natural born
English subjects.”

Private temperament clashed with public skill: “‘I hate the unholy rabble!’ he
exclaimed”; in the history he confessed “I have no talent at painting or describing
characters”—Schiff’s gloss: “People remained always opaque to Thomas Hutchinson.”

## Career

**Land Bank and early stance.** Protégé of Governor Belcher; later wrote the first
published Massachusetts history of the Land Bank, immortalizing subscribers as men
“of low condition among the plebeians” whose votes groaned equal to “men of
estates.” In 1769 he judged the Land Bank “a peccadillo compared with the
combinations now afoot in so many colonies.”

**Writs, Stamp Act, offices.** Presided for the first time as chief justice when
[[otis-james|James Otis Jr.]] argued against writs of assistance (24 February 1761)—
the appointment that opened a durable Otis–Hutchinson feud (Otis had sought the post
for his father). Mildly opposed new taxes and raised “taxation without representation”
on the Sugar Act, yet as CJ would enforce the Stamp Act: “The Act will execute
itself” (June 1765). His North End mansion was sacked in the
[[stamp-act-crisis-1765|Stamp Act]] riots—“Such ruins were never seen in America.”
Adams later drove him from Council sittings as lieutenant governor (“manifest
impropriety”).

**Massacre to exile.** On 5 March 1770 he calmed a furious late-night crowd from the
Town House balcony; after Adams’s pressure he ordered both regiments to Castle
Island. The published Hutchinson–Oliver letters (1773), cherry-picked and framed by
Adams’s circle, destroyed his American credit. After the
[[boston-tea-party-1773|Tea Party]] he sailed for Britain as the port closed (June
1774), expecting a short absence under Gage; tea consignees doubted he would return.

**London, 1774–80.** July 1774: two-hour audience with George III. Corrected the king
that the greatest opposition influence belonged to Samuel Adams, not Cushing—
“pretended zeal for liberty,” first publicly to assert colonial independency. The
king left convinced Massachusetts would submit. Hutchinson never sufficiently
excoriated “the black art of Adams,” doubted Adams could raise an armed body against
the King’s troops (letter days before Lexington), then dismissed first Lexington
accounts as Adams-written fiction. Died in London of a stroke, 1780, “convinced that,
more than anyone, Adams had been responsible for the Revolution.” Property
redistributed (Warrens bought the country estate; Milton Street later Adams Street).

## Counterfactual significance

Without Hutchinson’s plural offices, published history, and rigid defense of
parliamentary supremacy, Adams’s opposition would have lacked its sharpest local
foil; without the letter publication and Tea Party crisis, the path from provincial
quarrel to open war would have been slower. Schiff’s Hutchinson is not a cartoon
tyrant but a competent constitutionalist who could not read—or move—a popular public.

## Historiography

Primary: Hutchinson’s own *History of the Province of Massachusetts-Bay*, letterbooks,
and the 1774 royal audience record; Bernard correspondence; hostile Whig press.
Schiff (2022) uses him as structural antagonist throughout the Adams biography—
office accumulation, character opacity, massacre showdown, letter catastrophe, and
exile verdict on Adams as grand incendiary. Older Loyalist and imperial
historiography treated him as martyred order; Whig tradition as corrupt placeman.
Modern work (Bailyn’s *Ordeal of Thomas Hutchinson* and successors) stresses tragedy
of a New Englander destroyed by the Revolution he tried to contain. Collection note:
this page is grounded primarily in Schiff’s Adams-centered narrative; Bailyn-scale
depth is not yet ingested.
