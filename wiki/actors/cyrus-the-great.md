# Cyrus the Great (Cyrus II)

## Role and Significance

**Cyrus II** ("the Great") was the founder of the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid
Persian Empire]] and, for [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Daryaee, Ch 10), "a great
example of what the empire embodied and how it saw and defined itself." Originally
the local ruler of **Anšan**, the ancient highland Elamite capital, he inherited
deep administrative and hierarchical traditions and, between 550 and 539 BCE,
conquered the **Medes**, **Lydia** (Croesus), and **Babylon**, assembling the
largest empire the world had yet seen.

## Major Decisions and Their Consequences

- **The peaceful union of rival civilizations.** Cyrus's distinctive achievement,
  CWH IV stresses, was *not* administrative perfection (that was Darius's work) but
  his **largely peaceful unification** of the ancient rivals — Anatolia, Mesopotamia,
  Elam, the Iranian Plateau — under a tone of legitimation and conciliation strikingly
  different from Assyrian and Babylonian conquest-narratives.
- **The release of subject peoples.** He permitted the exiled **Jews** of the
  "Babylonian captivity" to return and rebuild (the basis of his favorable Biblical
  portrayal as the Lord's "anointed"), and presented himself as restorer of Marduk's
  cult against Nabonidus — propaganda preserved on the **[[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus
  Cylinder]]**, sometimes (anachronistically) called a "first charter of human rights."
- **The satrapal seed.** Cyrus (or Darius shortly after) divided the conquered lands
  into provinces under local administrators watched by imperial agents — the germ of
  the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid system]].

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Cyrus's conciliatory model, the Near East's first universal empire might have
reproduced the extractive, terror-based [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyrian]] template
rather than the tolerant, durable Achaemenid one whose administrative DNA passed to
the [[hellenistic-world|Seleucids]], Rome, and the later Iranian and Islamic empires.

## Death

Cyrus was killed in 530 BCE campaigning in Central Asia against the **Massagetae**,
whose queen (per [[herodotus|Herodotus]]) refused his marriage overture, defeated his
army, and killed him — an early instance of the [[pastoral-nomadism|steppe-nomad]]
limit on imperial expansion.

## Briant's Revisionist Reading

[[sources/briant-from-cyrus-to-alexander-2002|Briant]] sharply qualifies the "great
conciliator" image. The conquests were not the work of a lone strategist: the Nabonidus
Chronicle shows **Astyages took the offensive**, the Median war lasted years (Cyrus losing
three of four engagements), and Babylon fell after protracted hostilities, not a sudden
masterstroke. Sparing Astyages, Croesus, and Nabonidus was **calculated policy** to win
ruling-class cooperation, not evidence of mercy; cultivating sanctuaries (Babylon's Marduk,
the Jewish "return," Anatolian Apollo) was multi-audience propaganda. The
**[[cyrus-cylinder|Cylinder]] is modelled on Assurbanipal's inscriptions**, and the Jewish
return was "a banal and typical" Near Eastern restoration, not a special relationship.
Crucially, the "I am Cyrus … an Achaemenian" inscriptions at [[pasargadae|Pasargadae]] are
almost certainly **forgeries by [[darius-i|Darius]]**, retrofitting Cyrus as dynastic
ancestor — so even the [[achaemenid-royal-ideology|Achaemenid]] name attached to Cyrus is
later construction. The satrapal, tribute, and military land-tenure systems already existed
under Cyrus and Cambyses; Darius's innovation was fixing amounts, not inventing the
[[achaemenid-tribute-economy|institutions]].

## Scale and Style of Rule (Freeman)

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014|*Egypt, Greece, and Rome*]], 2014)
gives precise figures for the empire at Cyrus's death — roughly 4,000 km east-west,
1,500 km north-south, some 6 million sq km, population estimated at ~35 million —
and stresses a deliberately **light-touch model of rule**: Cyrus kept taxes
relatively low, and his capital **Pasargadae** was "nothing like so lavish" as the
Babylonian or Assyrian palaces it succeeded, a visible contrast to the terror/
deportation model of [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]].

## Historiography

Cyrus is known through a blend of his own propaganda (the [[cyrus-cylinder|Cylinder]],
Babylonian sources), the favorable Hebrew Bible, and the Greek tradition
([[herodotus]], Xenophon's idealizing *Cyropaedia*) — sources that must be read for
their agendas. The modern "Achaemenid history" school (see [[achaemenid-empire]]) has
sharpened the line between the historical Cyrus and his many later idealizations.

### Hall (2014) — Lydia and Ionia

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]]: traditional date 547/6 for conquest of Croesus is questioned, but Sardis excavations confirm mid-sixth destruction. After Croesus’s fall ~half of Phocaea abandoned for Corsica (Hdt. 1.164–5); remaining Asiatic Greek cities entered the Persian Empire, which by the end of the sixth century stretched from Bulgaria to Afghanistan and Egypt/Libya. Persian imperial style: respect for local customs/religions if loyalty and tribute were met; reliance on local administrators benefited Ionian aristocracies; Miletus negotiated especially favorable status (Hdt. 1.141.4). This incorporation of Ionia after Lydian tributary conquest of coastal cities is the structural prelude Hall traces into the [[ionian-revolt-499bce|Ionian Revolt]] and [[greco-persian-wars-499bce|Persian Wars]].
