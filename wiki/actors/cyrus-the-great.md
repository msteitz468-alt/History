# Cyrus the Great (Cyrus II)

## Role and Significance

**Cyrus II** ("the Great") was the founder of the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid
Persian Empire]] and, for [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Daryaee, Ch 10), "a great
example of what the empire embodied and how it saw and defined itself." Originally
the local ruler of **Anšan**, the ancient highland Elamite capital, he inherited
deep administrative and hierarchical traditions and, between 550 and 539 BCE,
conquered the **Medes**, **Lydia** (Croesus), and **Babylon**, assembling the
largest empire the world had yet seen.

## Major Decisions and Their Consequences

- **The peaceful union of rival civilizations.** Cyrus's distinctive achievement,
  CWH IV stresses, was *not* administrative perfection (that was Darius's work) but
  his **largely peaceful unification** of the ancient rivals — Anatolia, Mesopotamia,
  Elam, the Iranian Plateau — under a tone of legitimation and conciliation strikingly
  different from Assyrian and Babylonian conquest-narratives.
- **The release of subject peoples.** He permitted the exiled **Jews** of the
  "Babylonian captivity" to return and rebuild (the basis of his favorable Biblical
  portrayal as the Lord's "anointed"), and presented himself as restorer of Marduk's
  cult against Nabonidus — propaganda preserved on the **[[cyrus-cylinder|Cyrus
  Cylinder]]**, sometimes (anachronistically) called a "first charter of human rights."
- **The satrapal seed.** Cyrus (or Darius shortly after) divided the conquered lands
  into provinces under local administrators watched by imperial agents — the germ of
  the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid system]].

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Cyrus's conciliatory model, the Near East's first universal empire might have
reproduced the extractive, terror-based [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyrian]] template
rather than the tolerant, durable Achaemenid one whose administrative DNA passed to
the [[hellenistic-world|Seleucids]], Rome, and the later Iranian and Islamic empires.

## Death

Cyrus was killed in 530 BCE campaigning in Central Asia against the **Massagetae**,
whose queen (per [[herodotus|Herodotus]]) refused his marriage overture, defeated his
army, and killed him — an early instance of the [[pastoral-nomadism|steppe-nomad]]
limit on imperial expansion.

## Historiography

Cyrus is known through a blend of his own propaganda (the [[cyrus-cylinder|Cylinder]],
Babylonian sources), the favorable Hebrew Bible, and the Greek tradition
([[herodotus]], Xenophon's idealizing *Cyropaedia*) — sources that must be read for
their agendas. The modern "Achaemenid history" school (see [[achaemenid-empire]]) has
sharpened the line between the historical Cyrus and his many later idealizations.
