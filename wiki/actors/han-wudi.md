# Emperor Wu of Han (Han Wudi) (r. 141–87 BCE)

The greatest of the [[han-dynasty|Han]] emperors — the martial, expansionist ruler who pushed
the empire to its widest extent, broke the [[xiongnu|Xiongnu]], opened the
[[silk-roads|Silk Road]], and made **Confucianism the state creed** — under whom
[[sima-qian|Sima Qian]] himself served and suffered. From Sima Qian's
[[sima-qian-records-han-ii|*Records of the Grand Historian: Han Dynasty II*]] (the *Shi ji*).

## The Xiongnu wars

Reversing the early Han policy of appeasement (the *heqin* marriage-and-tribute peace), Emperor
Wu launched decades of war against the nomadic **[[xiongnu|Xiongnu]]** confederation of the
steppe — whose foundational ethnography Sima Qian gives in the **Account of the Xiongnu** (a
people of horse, cow, and sheep, "no walled cities or fixed dwellings," every man a mounted
archer). Wu's great **cavalry generals Wei Qing and Huo Qubing** drove the Xiongnu deep into the
desert and won the Ordos and the Gansu corridor; the tragic veteran **Li Guang** — brave but
"unlucky," never ennobled — became, in Sima Qian's sympathetic portrait, an emblem of merit
unrewarded. The wars secured the northern frontier but at enormous cost in men, horses, and
treasure.

## The opening to Central Asia and the Silk Road

Seeking allies against the Xiongnu, Wu dispatched the envoy **Zhang Qian** on missions to the
western lands — journeys that revealed the states of Central Asia and inaugurated the
**[[silk-roads|Silk Road]]** trade. Coveting the "**heavenly horses**" (the blood-sweating
steeds) of **Dayuan (Ferghana)**, Wu sent armies across the deserts to obtain them. Han power
now reached into the Tarim oases and beyond.

## Expansion in every direction

Wu's reign was one of conquest on all frontiers (the *Shi ji*'s Accounts): the annexation of
**Southern Yue** (Guangdong/northern Vietnam), **Eastern Yue**, **Chaoxian** (northern Korea),
and the **southwestern barbarians** (Yunnan). Under Wu the Han reached its **greatest territorial
extent** — a span Sima Qian notes was matched "by no subsequent dynasty until some eight
centuries later."

## State, economy, and religion

To pay for ceaseless war, Wu's government turned to aggressive **fiscal interventionism** — the
**salt and iron monopolies**, currency manipulation, price-stabilization, and heavy taxes on
merchants, devised by officials like **Sang Hongyang** (the "Balanced Standard"; see
[[han-state-economy]]). He institutionalized **Confucianism as the state orthodoxy** (the
imperial academy, recruitment of scholar-officials), even as he ruled through **harsh Legalist
officials**. And he was obsessed with **immortality and ritual grandeur** — the **Feng and Shan
sacrifices**, alchemists and magicians, and the quest for the elixir of life.

## Counterfactual significance and the historian's verdict

Emperor Wu transformed the Han from a consolidating into a **conquering empire** and fixed the
imperial-Confucian, centrally-managed state for two millennia. But [[sima-qian|Sima Qian]] —
whom Wu had **castrated** for defending the general Li Ling — wrote his reign as an **implicit
warning**: the expansionism, ruinous projects, harsh laws, and superstitious immortality-quest
that he condemned in the [[qin-shi-huang|First Emperor]] he saw mirrored in his own master.
Later ages "habitually linked their names as fellow believers in superstitious folly." Wu is
thus both the Han's most powerful emperor and the great object of its founding historian's
veiled critique.

## Institutions, Calendar, and Cosmology (Major & Cook 2017)

[[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook]] add three points. (1) Wu's appointment of official
**Erudites** in the five Confucian texts (136 BCE), examined orally in the audience hall, **laid
the foundation of the examination system** — though they **reject** the older idea of a "Han
Confucian synthesis" engineered by **Dong Zhongshu** under Wu. (2) His **Grand Inception (Taichu)
calendar of 104 BCE** fixed the solar year at 365.2507 days and the lunar month at 29.5309 days and
set the Chinese calendrical standard for centuries; it also **reversed the early Han's Phase-Water
(black) identity** in favour of **Phase Earth (yellow)**, conquering Qin's Water. (3) His
**feng and shan** sacrifices on Mt. Tai (first performed 110 BCE) and cults to **Taiyi** were
matched, in his **waning years**, by the lethal **Li–Wei consort-clan civil war in Chang'an (91
BCE)**, which forced Empress Wei and the heir Liu Qu to suicide — the dark coda the *Shiji* leaves
implicit. **Source:** [[major-cook-ancient-china-2017]].

## The Turn from Heqin to War: Strategic Logic (Di Cosmo)

[[di-cosmo-ancient-china-enemies-2002|Di Cosmo's analysis]] gives the clearest account of *why* Emperor Wu abandoned the [[heqin-policy|heqin]] appeasement strategy and what made the subsequent campaigns militarily possible. The shift was not primarily ideological — majority court opinion in 135–134 BCE still favored accommodation — but the product of heqin's structural failure and of accumulated Han military capabilities.

**The debates of 135–134 BCE** were the pivot. Wang Hui and Han An-kuo argued before Wu whether to attack or continue to appease the Xiongnu. An-kuo's "pacifist" position won the first round (135 BCE) and the treaty was renewed. But the Ma-yi disaster of 133 BCE — when a planned ambush of the chanyu failed catastrophically — confirmed Wang Hui's fundamental argument: "Peace treaties with the Hsiung-nu did not work, and because the nomads continued to create a serious threat on the borders and limited military action of the type attempted at Ma-yi was not successful against them, there was no other solution but to confront them on their own terms, through full-scale military engagement" (Di Cosmo, citing *Han shu* 52).

**Military preconditions for the offensive.** Wu's campaigns were made possible by capabilities built during the preceding five reigns:
- By 140 BCE, thirty-six horse-breeding stations were already operating on the frontier, providing maintenance for 300,000 horses (*Han shu* 24, cited by Swann)
- The Han army had developed cavalry as "an independent arm, and finally became the most important one in the wars against the roving tribes of Central Asia" (Laufer, cited by Di Cosmo)
- Ironclad armor (replacing copper-hide) and precision crossbow triggers gave Chinese infantry an edge against Xiongnu archers even at a disadvantage in numbers
- From 130 BCE the Han army comprised volunteer professional *ping* (combat soldiers) alongside conscripted *tsu*, with the *ping* serving as the cavalry core

**Internal political prerequisites.** A "primary preoccupation of Wu-ti's predecessors had been the consolidation of central authority." Han Gaozu could not fight the Xiongnu because he was simultaneously subduing the semi-independent regional kings (*chu-hou-wang*). The major rebellion of 154 BCE and its suppression was the decisive turning point; by Wu's accession the centrifugal threat had been contained. The death of the empress dowager in 135 BCE also removed a check on Wu's freedom of action.

**The three-phase campaign (133–87 BCE).** Di Cosmo divides Wu's offensive into three phases:

*Phase One (133–119 BCE)*: Aimed at recovering lands south of the Great Bend of the Yellow River and securing vulnerable border areas. Wu dispatched four armies of 10,000 cavalry each in 129 BCE under Wei Ch'ing (Chariots and Cavalry), Kung-sun Ho (Light Chariots), Kung-sun Ao, and Li Kuang (Imperial Cavalry). Wei Ch'ing reached Lung-ch'eng — the Xiongnu sacred site of sacrifices to Heaven — killing and capturing several hundred. Three of the four commanders failed or were degraded. A pattern of Han strikes followed by Xiongnu counteroffensives continued: the Xiongnu killed the governor of Liao-hsi (128 BCE) and invaded Yen-men. Wei Ch'ing's 127 BCE campaign reached Kao-ch'üeh and pacified the region south of the Yellow River. By 123 BCE, Wei Ch'ing commanded over 100,000 cavalry in a major thrust north of Ting-hsiang. In 121 BCE General Huo Ch'ü-ping, with 10,000 cavalry, marched over 1,000 li and crossed the Yen-chih Mountains; in summer of the same year he and Kung-sun Ao led tens of thousands 2,000 li north of Lung-hsi and Pei-ti, killing or capturing over 30,000 enemies including seventy "small kings." The 119 BCE expedition was the culmination: Huo Ch'ü-ping advanced over 2,000 li from Tai, killing or capturing over 70,000 enemies; the Xiongnu withdrew their royal court north of the Gobi.

*Phase Two (119–104 BCE)*: With northern security established, Han campaigns continued with the broader agenda of **annihilating Xiongnu political power** rather than merely military defeat. The establishment of the salt and iron monopolies in 119 BCE signaled the economic rationalization required to sustain the war effort. The key strategic concept was "cutting the right arm" of the Xiongnu — severing their economic ties to the Western Regions (modern Xinjiang). The new commandery of Chiu-ch'üan was established in 110 BCE specifically to cut routes between the Xiongnu and the Ch'iang. Fourteen border commanderies were established between 112 and 108 BCE. The Han military and civil administrative structure of border regions was reorganized at three levels: commanderies inside the defensive line, Dependent States (*shu-kuo*) outside it populated by surrendered nomads, and territories beyond Han control that had accepted some form of subordination.

*Phase Three (104–87 BCE)*: The Han thrust into the Western Regions. Li Kuang-li's campaign of 104–101 BCE against Ferghana was chiefly intended to acquire superior horses. A line of forts was built from Chiu-ch'üan to the Jade Gate (Yü-men). In 99 BCE Li Kuang-li led 30,000 cavalry against the wise king of the right in the T'ien Shan — a campaign that ended in near-disaster when he was surrounded. Li Ling simultaneously led 5,000 infantry from Chiu-ch'üan but was overwhelmed and surrendered (the episode for which Sima Qian was later castrated). The heyday of Han expansion was over after 99 BCE; the Xiongnu were also beset by internal discord and natural calamities. Conditions matured for the peaceful settlement with Hu-han-yeh Chanyu in the 50s BCE.

**The Western Regions strategy.** The Han advance into Central Asia is explained by Di Cosmo not primarily as trade-motivated but as strategic: "the only reason for the Chinese expansion in Central Asia was the desire to stop the invasions of the Hsiung-nu, or, as the ancients said, to 'cut off their right arm', i.e., to deprive them of their western bases" (Hulsewé, cited approvingly by Di Cosmo). Once the Xiongnu retreated north of the Gobi, they had to rely more heavily on agricultural settlements in Central Asia and southern Siberia. The oases of Sinkiang became their main source of agricultural and craft products. Han military-agricultural colonies in Central Asia thus served three simultaneous functions: denying the Xiongnu agricultural supply, providing advanced logistic support for Chinese expeditionary armies, and protecting emerging Silk Road trade.

Chang Ch'ien's first mission to Central Asia (sent 139/138 BCE, though dating is contested) preceded military operations by six years; he was intercepted by the Xiongnu and found the Yuezhi unwilling to oppose them. But the intelligence his mission gathered about "the political composition of this region, the wealth it produced, and the hegemonic position of the Hsiung-nu over the area" was essential to the subsequent strategic decisions.

**The Pyrrhic dimension.** Di Cosmo notes that Wu's forward strategy "almost bankrupted the Han because of the rapid depletion of the state's finances." Contemporary observers including Sima Qian saw the war's ultimate victory as Pyrrhic. The wars' impact was felt "at every level of political and social life" and is reflected in the *Discourses on Salt and Iron* (held in the early first century BCE), where the "literati" faction accused Emperor Wu of pursuing expansion "to increase his power and aggrandize the state at the expense of the welfare of the people, who became bankrupt in the process." This was the debate that haunted Wu's legacy.

## Wudi and the Opening of the Silk Roads (Benjamin 2018)

For [[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin]], Wudi's reign is the **hinge** of
the [[silk-roads|First Silk Roads Era]]: "it was by seeking an alliance with the
[[yuezhi|Yuezhi]] against the [[xiongnu]] that China essentially established the first Silk
Roads exchange network." Inheriting 450,000 cavalry horses bred under Jingdi, he abandoned
defensive [[han-dynasty|heqin]] tribute, dispatched [[zhang-qian-mission-138bce|Zhang
Qian]] (139 BCE), and then sent envoys west so thickly that "the envoys were in sight of
each other on the roads." His obsession with Ferghana's **"heavenly horses"** — linked to
an *I Ching* prophecy — drove the campaigns of **104 and 102 BCE**. Benjamin also frames
Wudi's formal adoption of **[[confucianism|Confucianism]]** as state orthodoxy (with the
imperial academy at [[chang-an|Chang'an]] and Dong Zhongshu's heaven-emperor cosmology) as
the ideological complement to his westward expansion, funded by the salt, iron, and liquor
monopolies.
