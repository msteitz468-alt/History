# Miltiades

**Miltiades** (the Younger), of the great Athenian **Philaid** family, was the
**architect of the victory at [[battle-of-marathon-490bce|Marathon]]** — a career that ran
from tyrant of the Thracian Chersonese and Persian vassal to champion of Athenian freedom.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Chs. 4, 8, 9 — Lewis, Murray, Hammond) follows him
across the volume.

## Tyrant of the Chersonese and Persian vassal

The [[pisistratidae|Pisistratids]] dispatched Miltiades to rule the **Thracian Chersonese**
(c. 516) on the Hellespont — strategically vital for the Black Sea grain route — where he
took "prompt and ruthless measures." As a subject of Persia he had **no choice but to join
Darius' [[darius-scythian-expedition-513bce|Scythian expedition]]** (513); he later claimed
to have urged the Ionians to destroy the Danube bridge and abandon Darius (a story Hammond
doubts). He **captured Lemnos** and handed it to Athens, probably during the
[[ionian-revolt-499bce|Ionian Revolt]].

## Flight to Athens

When the Persian fleet swept up the Hellespont after Lade (**493**), Miltiades — who "had
clearly taken too independent a line" — **fled to Athens**, losing a ship and his son
**Metiochus** (captured and made a Persian, half-brother to the future Cimon). At Athens his
enemies at once prosecuted him on the capital charge of **"tyranny in the Chersonese"**; his
**acquittal** (and election as one of the ten generals for 490/89) marked the hardening of
Athenian hostility to Persia.

## Marathon

At [[battle-of-marathon-490bce|Marathon]] (490) Miltiades, "in a full democracy and not in
an authoritarian state," **imposed his will** on the people, his colleagues and his
citizen-soldiers: he proposed marching out at once, swung the deadlocked board of generals
(with the polemarch **Callimachus'** vote), and on his day of command **thinned the centre,
strengthened the wings, and charged** — the tactics that destroyed the Persian line. Hammond
calls him "the man of vision... the architect of victory," while crediting the win itself to
the courage of the Athenian and Plataean ranks.

## Fall — counterfactual

His triumph was brief. In **489** he led a secret expedition against **Paros**, promising the
Athenians plunder; it **failed**, and he was wounded. Prosecuted by **Xanthippus** (Pericles'
father, married into the [[cleisthenes|Alcmaeonids]]) for "deceiving the people," he was
spared the death penalty in view of Marathon but **fined fifty talents**, and **died of his
wound** soon after. His career is the type-case of the early democracy's treatment of the
over-mighty individual — the very danger [[ostracism]] was designed to manage; without him
the tactical victory at Marathon is hard to imagine, yet the city would not let him convert
it into personal power.

## Historiography

Known almost wholly from **[[herodotus-reliability|Herodotus]]** (Books IV–VI), whose
Philaid-favourable family tradition and the later glorification of Marathon both colour the
portrait (Cornelius Nepos' *Miltiades* adds little independent value). The truth of his
anti-Persian "double game" on the Danube, and of the Paros affair, is debated.
