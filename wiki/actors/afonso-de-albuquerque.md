# Afonso de Albuquerque

Second Governor and first great Viceroy of Portuguese India. The principal architect of the *Estado da Índia* — the network of fortified trading posts and naval control that allowed a small European power to dominate key nodes of the Indian Ocean trade for a century.

## Strategy and Conquests

Albuquerque understood that Portugal could not conquer the vast Asian mainland. Instead he aimed to seize and hold the “choke points” of the monsoon trade: the entrance to the Red Sea (Aden/Socotra), the Persian Gulf (Hormuz), the Strait of Malacca, and a secure base on the Indian coast (Goa). 

- **Goa (1510)**: Captured from the Sultan of Bijapur with local Hindu assistance; became the capital of Portuguese India and a major shipbuilding/repair base.
- **Malacca (1511)**: Stormed after a siege; dispersed the existing Muslim trading community but secured the gateway between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea.
- **Hormuz (1507/1515)**: Brought the key Persian Gulf emporium under Portuguese suzerainty and tribute.

He also attempted (unsuccessfully) to take Aden and probed the Red Sea as far as the Bab el-Mandeb.

## Governance and Legacy

Albuquerque combined ruthless military action with pragmatic administration. He encouraged Portuguese men to marry local women, built churches and hospitals, and tried to create a self-sustaining colonial society. His “cartaz” (pass) system required Asian ships to carry Portuguese licences or face seizure — a revolutionary claim to sovereignty over the sea lanes.

Hall presents him as the embodiment of the new European style: superior heavy cannon, disciplined infantry, and a determination to monopolise rather than merely participate in trade. His death in 1515 (shortly after the second capture of Hormuz) marked the end of the most dynamic phase of Portuguese expansion.
