# Métis

The **Métis** (French-Indian and British-Indian communities of largely Cree/Assiniboine ancestry in Fowler’s chapter) formed a distinct Plains people through fur-trade ethnogenesis, buffalo-robe hunting with Red River carts, and political resistance in 1869 and 1885.

## Overview

After the **1821** Hudson’s Bay–Northwest Company merger cut the labor force by two-thirds, French-Indian and British-Indian employees settled Red River (Manitoba) at the Assiniboine junction and Pembina, N.D. They responded to the robe trade by hunting buffalo into Saskatchewan, N.D., and Montana using **horsedrawn carts rather than travois**, plus freighter work. Red River population rose from **500 (1821) to 12,000 (1870)**. Fowler notes parallel bicultural Creole St. Louis and Texas Spanish–captive trader groups.

Some Santee refugees after 1862 made peace with Métis and relocated to Manitoba/Saskatchewan.

## Role and Structure

- **Economic:** buffalo hunt with cart logistics; freighter work; later settlement fringes after 1885.
- **Political:** provisional governments under Louis Riel (1869, 1885); Constitution Act 1982 later defined Aboriginal peoples of Canada as Indians, Inuit, and Métis (volume later chapters).
- **Social:** late 1850s–60s Euro-Canadian settlers brought racial-superiority assumptions; HBC discrimination, missionary denigration, and abandonment of Métis wives for Euro-Canadian wives fueled anxiety.

## Key Events (full fidelity)

### Red River resistance 1869

Dominion of Canada **1867** purchased HBC lands **1870** including Plains north of the 49th **without consulting Métis**; customary title and lack of territorial representation threatened lands. **Louis Riel** led the 1869 Red River resistance with a provisional government. Canada suppressed but the **1870** agreement promised land and amnesty—Canada **reneged on amnesty** (Riel fled to the U.S.) and on land grants for future generations; many Métis moved to Saskatchewan Qu’Appelle/South Saskatchewan or Montana. NWMP (**1873**) originally intended Métis hires but backlash hired only Canadian/British men.

### Northwest Rebellion 1885

Métis in Saskatchewan, unable to get treaties or title recognition, recalled Riel from Montana exile and proclaimed a provisional government **1885**. **March** clash with Mounted Police at **Duck Lake**; government sent **8,000 troops**; after two lost battles Riel surrendered. Concurrently troops attacked a Cree–Assiniboine–Métis camp on **Cut Knife Hill**—Crees drove them off but leaders restrained annihilation. Government used the Métis revolt and isolated killings to **arrest Cree leaders** to break the treaty-pressure movement. After suppression, Cree/Assiniboine/Métis individuals convicted of treason, imprisoned or executed; most Métis dispersed to settlement fringes; Plains Cree and Assiniboine isolated on small reserves—paralleling U.S. exclusion of Natives from regional development (Fowler).

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Métis cart logistics and robe-trade niche, Canadian Plains market geography would differ; without 1869/1885 resistance, the political definition of Aboriginal peoples and Manitoba/Saskatchewan land politics would lack their formative crises. Structural railroad settlement and buffalo collapse still closed free plains economies.

## Fur-trade post society and Homeguard mixed descent (Ray, ch. 13)

Ray embeds Métis/mixed-blood formation in Subarctic fur-trade social order, not only Red River nationalism. French coureurs de bois and later traders took Native wives for skills, survival, and kinship facilitation of trade; HBC officially forbade liaisons but officers/servants ignored them. By 1763 Homeguard near posts were largely mixed—French-Native “bois brulés”/“Metis”; English/Scottish-Native “mixed-bloods”/“country born.” 1774–1821 labor stratification incorporated mixed-bloods (NWC voyageurs of French-Indian descent; HBC seasonal mixed-blood lowest rung). After 1821: mixed-bloods held permanent trades/labor but were blocked from the officer class; Simpson marriage norms and Victorian/missionary morality degraded status of Native and mixed-blood wives. Key secondary works in Ray’s bibliographic essay: Van Kirk *Many Tender Ties*; Jennifer Brown *Strangers in Blood*. part_of: [[northern-interior-subarctic-postcontact]], [[eastern-north-american-fur-trade]].

## Modern claims and renaissance-era status (Ray ch. 13; Washburn ch. 15)

- **Mackenzie Valley / NWT:** During Berger Inquiry hearings (1974–76), Dene, Métis, and Inuit opposed pipeline construction until claims settled. 1988 Mackenzie Valley Dene and Métis AIP signed; 1990 annual assembly rejected the accord (partly after *Sparrow* suggested more extensive rights) but allowed regional groups to seek separate agreements on 1988 principles. Western NWT polity includes Dene, Métis, and Euro-Canadians; 1982 plebiscite path toward NWT division (western multiethnic zone vs eastern Inuit-majority Nunavut). concurrent_with: [[dene]], [[inuit]].
- **Washburn demography and constitution:** 1991 Canadian census combined single/multiple origins: **212,650 Métis** (with 783,980 North American Indian and 49,255 Inuit among Aboriginal origins totaling slightly over 1 million). Historical nadir figures for 1867 include ~10,000 Métis/half-breeds. Constitution Act 1982 defines Aboriginal peoples as Indians, Inuit, and Métis. Canadian pan-Indian landscape includes Métis National Council (paired with Assembly of First Nations and Inuit Committee on National Issues in Washburn’s typology). part_of: [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]].
- **Artistic note (Washburn):** Métis architect Douglas Cardinal designed the Canadian Museum of Civilization and planned the Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian.

## Historiography

Fowler integrates Métis into Plains regional history rather than isolating them as a Canadian specialty; full fidelity on reneged amnesty/land and 1885 executions/imprisonments. Ray (Subarctic) stresses post Homeguard ethnogenesis, racialized HBC labor hierarchy, and modern Dene–Métis claims partnership—continuity with fur-trade social order. Washburn treats Métis mainly as census category, constitutional people, and pan-Indian organizational presence within the post-1960 “renaissance.” Do not collapse French-Native “Métis” and English/Scottish “country born” labels without noting Ray’s distinction; political identity “Métis” is itself historical.
