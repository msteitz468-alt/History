## Overview

Moses ben Maimun — known in Hebrew with the honorific acronym "Rambam," and in English as Maimonides — is the central figure of medieval Jewish philosophy and arguably the most important figure of medieval Judaism as a whole. He combined the greatest Jewish rabbinic authority of his era with its greatest philosophical achievement: "from Moses to Moses, there was no one like Moses." He was an almost exact contemporary of [[ibn-rushd]] (Averroes), whom he shares both a birth city (Córdoba) and broad intellectual ambitions ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## Life

Born in Córdoba in 1138 into a family of Jewish legal scholars, Maimonides grew up in a culture of Arabic-language learning and rabbinic excellence. When the Almohads took Córdoba, the family fled — eventually to Fez in Morocco, then (after years that some sources describe as involving a period of presenting as Muslim, though this is contested) to Palestine and finally to Cairo. It was in Cairo that Maimonides spent most of his adult life, rising to prominence as the leading rabbinic authority in Egypt and producing his greatest works.

His two careers — as halakhic authority and as philosopher — were never fully separate. Both expressed the same conviction: that the truths of philosophy and the truths of revelation are ultimately one.

## Major Works

### Mishneh Torah
Written in Hebrew, this is Maimonides' greatest work of Jewish law. Its ambition is proportionate to its importance: it gathers and systematizes all of the teachings of the Mishnah and Talmud, eliminating apparent contradictions and providing the underlying principles, so that observant Jews could find clear guidance without trawling the classical texts. The title means "the second law." The first book, the *Book of Knowledge*, lays down philosophical principles — Aristotelian ethics, cosmology, and a rationalist conception of God — as the foundation for the legal teachings.

### Guide for the Perplexed (*Dalālat al-Ḥāʾirīn*)
Written in Judeo-Arabic and addressed explicitly to a student named Joseph, the *Guide* is Maimonides' greatest philosophical work. Its stated purpose is to resolve the perplexity felt by devout Jews who have studied philosophy and find certain philosophical truths (especially divine incorporeality and simplicity) apparently incompatible with biblical language describing God as having a face, sitting on a throne, getting angry, etc.

Maimonides warns in his introduction that the *Guide* may omit necessary premises or employ premises he does not accept. This has fueled centuries of debate over what Maimonides "really" believed — most notably in Leo Strauss's reading that a secret, heterodox teaching lies beneath the surface of the text.

## Philosophical Positions

### Divine Attributes and Language About God

The central problem of the *Guide* is a version of the problem of theological language familiar from the Muʿtazilites. Maimonides agrees with that tradition that God's absolute simplicity and immateriality rule out positive attributes. His threefold strategy for reading problematic biblical language:

1. **Concealed negations**: "God is powerful" means only "God is not weak" — not that God has the property of power
2. **Attributes of God's actions**: "God is providential/angry/merciful" describes the created world (its order, its hostile conditions, its favorable conditions), not God Himself
3. **Allegory**: bodily descriptions ("sits upon a throne") are symbolic renderings of God's sovereignty

Against the charge that this leaves us with an empty theology, Maimonides counters that process of elimination is genuinely informative: as negations accumulate, our understanding improves, as in a perverse game of Twenty Questions (*Guide* §1.60). Ultimate wisdom consists in "eliminating everything and being left with nothing" — a transcendent nothing superior to all things.

> "Silence is praise to Thee" (Psalms 65:2, cited in *Guide* §1.59).

### Prophecy

Maimonides holds a naturalistic account of prophecy: prophetic insight is a kind of natural emanation upon a suitably prepared intellect — one that has achieved moral and intellectual perfection. Following [[al-farabi]] and [[ibn-sina]], he holds that God would need to intervene miraculously *to prevent* a sufficiently prepared person from receiving prophetic knowledge (*Guide* §2.32). Moses is the greatest prophet; his revelation is primary.

### The Eternity Question

Maimonides argues that reason cannot decide whether the universe is eternal or was created with a first moment. Both positions have been argued without decisive proof. He rejects all available arguments for eternity — especially those that mistakenly assume God operates by the same constraints as created causes ("a tailor can't make a suit without cloth, but who says God is like a tailor?" — *Guide* §2.17). The world's appearance — including the apparently arbitrary number of stars — suggests creation by unconstrained divine will rather than by necessity (*Guide* §2.18).

His strategy in proving God's existence from the assumption of an eternal universe (chosen rather than the simpler creationist assumption) is controversial: Straussian readers see it as a signal that he secretly believed in eternity; Adamson reads it as the harder path chosen to demonstrate divine existence beyond all possible doubt.

### Ethics and Jewish Law

Maimonides draws heavily on Aristotle's ethics — especially the doctrine of the mean — in his *Eight Chapters* (the ethical introduction to his *Commentary on the Mishnah*). He also uses Galenic medicine's model of "curing the soul" of vicious character traits through practice and habituation. Where Jewish Scripture appears to valorize extreme asceticism (Abraham refusing to gaze at his wife), Maimonides reads this as deliberate overcorrection to counteract a known vice — "staying inside the line of the law" by erring on one side — not as endorsement of extreme self-denial.

His broad project is to show that the Jewish Law functions as an elaborate system of soul-conditioning designed by divine providence to bring humans to their highest end: intellectual contemplation. This aligns the Law with the Aristotelian goal of *eudaimonia*, relocating the purpose of revelation within a philosophical anthropology.

### Divine Knowledge and Providence

Against Avicenna's claim that God knows only universals, Maimonides insists God knows particulars — though in a completely different mode from human knowledge (five differences enumerated in *Guide*, discussed and criticized by [[gersonides]]). Individual human suffering is not mere chance; divine providence operates at the individual level. The Book of Job, for Maimonides, illustrates the error of identifying happiness with material goods: Job laments because he is not yet a philosopher; only through his trials does he reach "a certain knowledge of God" (*Guide* §3.23).

### Astrology

In a famous letter to the Jews of Provence, Maimonides condemns astrology as baseless and dangerous. The destruction of the Temple is attributed in part to Jewish communities that trusted in the stars rather than arming themselves. Stars bring about only general regularities of nature (species, not individuals); individual events are either chance or divine providence, neither of which can be predicted astrologically.

## Reception and Legacy

Maimonides became, in Adamson's phrase, the "apex" of medieval Jewish thought — the figure whose Aristotelianism defined the tradition for generations. His authority in rabbinic law meant that his philosophy could not be ignored: even opponents paid homage to him. His influence split into:

- **Rationalist Maimonideans** (Samuel Ibn Tibbon, Gersonides, Isaac Albalag): extending his philosophical program
- **Conservative critics**: objecting to his rationalist readings of resurrection, afterlife, and the nature of commandments
- **Kabbalists**: [[kabbalah]] absorbed his Neoplatonic language of divine ineffability while criticizing his Aristotelianism

The [[maimonidean-controversy]] of the 1230s and the Barcelona ban of 1305 demonstrate the explosive cultural significance of his work.

Hasdai [[hasdai-crescas]] used Maimonides' twenty-six physical principles as the target for a comprehensive assault on Aristotelian physics. [[joseph-albo]] and [[isaac-abravanel]] continued to wrestle with his account of the principles of the Jewish Law.

## The "Epistle to Yemen" and the Myth/Countermyth Debate

Maimonides is a central figure in the modern historiography of Jews under Islam,
chiefly through his **"Epistle to Yemen"** (*Iggeret Teman*, c. 1172), a letter of
consolation to Yemenite Jews facing forced conversion. Its bitter line —

> "on account of our sins God has cast us into the midst of this people, the nation
> of Ishmael, who persecute us severely... No nation has ever done more harm to
> Israel. None has matched it in debasing and humiliating us."

— became the favourite **prooftext of the "neo-lachrymose" countermyth** (post-1967
writers arguing Islam innately persecuted Jews). Per [[cohen-under-crescent-cross-1994|Mark
Cohen]], the statement must be read in context: the young Maimonides had himself
survived the [[almohads|Almohad]] persecution that began in the mid-1140s — which
forced conversions, killed many, and drove his family from Córdoba through Fez (where
they may have lived as nominal Muslims) to Egypt — and he was "insufficiently aware"
(H. H. Ben-Sasson) of the harsher contemporaneous conditions of Jews in Christendom.
Cohen thus treats the Epistle as a response to an exceptional twelfth-century
catastrophe, not a verdict on the classical Islamic norm. See
[[jews-under-islam-myth-and-countermyth]].

## Historiography

The Straussian reading (Maimonides as a secret Aristotelian rationalist concealing heterodox beliefs behind pious language) remains a live debate. Adamson leans against Straussianism, arguing that Maimonides should primarily be read at face value and that the "eternity assumption" in his proofs is a methodological choice, not a hidden confession. The question of whether Maimonides and his family passed as Muslims under Almohad rule is also actively debated; Muslim sources allege this, Jewish scholars defend Maimonides' integrity.
