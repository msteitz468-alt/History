## Overview

Ḥasdai Crescas was a Catalan Jewish philosopher, legal scholar, and communal leader of the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries, based in Barcelona and then Saragossa. He is the most radical critic of Aristotelian natural philosophy in the medieval Jewish tradition, and one of the most significant pre-modern critics of Aristotle anywhere. His chief work, *Light of the Lord* (*Or Hashem*), combines a devastating demolition of Maimonides' Aristotelian physical principles with a deeply traditional Jewish theology ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

He is remembered both for intellectual daring and for communal leadership during one of the worst catastrophes in medieval Jewish history.

## Life and Historical Context

Crescas rose to scholarly prominence at the Barcelona yeshiva under Christian Aragonese rule. He became the highest rabbinic legal authority for Jews in Aragon, appointed by the king and queen. In 1391, the catastrophic pogroms against Jews swept Spanish cities; Crescas's former community in Barcelona was destroyed and his own son was killed there. Thousands of Jews were massacred; an unprecedented mass conversion followed, with many becoming Christian to avoid death.

As a senior Jewish leader, Crescas worked to find safe havens for co-religionists and went on polemical offensive against Christianity in writing — a Catalan treatise criticizing ten Christian doctrines (original sin, Trinity, Incarnation, etc.), probably intended for a Jewish audience and for potential converts whom he hoped to reclaim.

## Light of the Lord: Against Aristotelian Physics

The *Or Hashem* is structured in a way that mirrors [[gersonides]]' *Wars of the Lord*: it lists arguments, identifies their problems, and reaches considered conclusions. Its occasion was Crescas's disagreement with [[maimonides]]' approach to the foundations of the Jewish Law. Maimonides had grounded the Law's first principle (belief in God's existence) in twenty-six propositions of Aristotelian physics. Crescas saw this as placing religion on shaky foundations. He set out to demolish the propositions.

### Preliminary: Belief Cannot Be Commanded

Before attacking the physics, Crescas makes an important philosophical point: belief responds to evidence and reasons, not to willful choices or commands. Maimonides had said that believing in God is a *commandment*; but one cannot command someone to believe something, any more than one can bribe someone into genuine belief. "If a servant were asked to weigh money for a king and lazily assigned this important task to someone else, he would be blameworthy" — but if one cannot choose one's beliefs, commanding them is incoherent. Crescas thus reframes the grounds for religious belief away from commandment toward rational and experiential evidence.

### Against Aristotle's Impossibility of Actual Infinity

Aristotle argued that no actually infinite body can exist. Crescas turns one of Aristotle's own devices — the comparison to indefinite divisibility — against this claim. Just as any finite body can in principle be divided indefinitely (without ever reaching a final zero), an infinitely long line extends indefinitely without any point on it being at an infinite distance from the center. At every specific point the distance is finite, yet there is no limit to how far the line extends (*Or Hashem*, 207).

He also rejects the claim — made even by al-Kindī, one of the most vigorous critics of eternity — that two infinite quantities cannot stand in a ratio. An infinite line bounded on one side is no shorter than one infinite in both directions (191), since "infinite" just means "without a limit." This anticipates modern set-theoretic thinking about the equal cardinality of different infinite sets (e.g., positive integers and even integers).

### Against the Impossibility of Void

Aristotle's most persuasive argument against void: speed of motion is inversely related to the density of the medium; void has zero density; therefore motion through void would be infinitely fast, which is absurd. Crescas, following [[ibn-bajja]] and John Philoponus, argues that a medium's density only *slows* a motion's intrinsic speed (183). In void there would be no slowing, but there is no reason for speed to become infinite, since every motion's intrinsic speed is finite.

### Cosmological Consequences

With actual infinity and void both rehabilitated:

- The cosmos need not end at the outermost heavenly sphere; infinite void can exist beyond it
- The Stoic suggestion of infinite surrounding void is coherent
- There could be other worlds in an infinite emptiness, or even an infinite series of worlds created and destroyed by God (217)
- Time need not depend on motion (contra Aristotle's definition); Crescas defines time as the mental interval between two moments, existing "only in the soul" (287–291)
- If time can stretch before our cosmos existed (as a mental possibility), an infinite past is not ruled out

### Proof of God: Via Avicenna, Not Aristotle

Having demolished Maimonides' Aristotelian proof of God's existence, Crescas does not conclude that God's existence is unprovable. He offers an alternative: even an infinite contingent universe — one that could have failed to exist — requires a Necessary Existent to explain why it was realized. This is Avicenna's argument from contingency, which Crescas thinks Maimonides used but misunderstood: the proof does not require assuming anything about whether the universe is finite, infinite, eternal, or created (*Or Hashem*).

### Principles of Judaism

Crescas reduces Maimonides' thirteen principles to three "roots" (*shorasim*): God exists, He is one, He has no body. He thinks these are shared by all reasonable religions and are necessary for Judaism. But he denies that Maimonides, Aristotle, or [[gersonides]] had managed to prove them with the Aristotelian toolkit — which has now been shown to be unreliable.

## Legacy

Crescas's demolition of Aristotelian physics attracted little immediate excitement within Judaism, but his arguments were cited by Renaissance philosophers (Pico della Mirandola) and eventually by Spinoza. Adamson credits him with a partial role in the gradual demise of Aristotelian natural philosophy, without overstating this: he was a demolition man more than an architect, and his intentions were theological rather than scientific.

His student was [[joseph-albo]], who continued the project of articulating the principles of Jewish faith, albeit in a less destructive mode.

## Historiography

Crescas is one of the most original thinkers of medieval philosophy and one of the most underappreciated outside specialist literature. His rehabilitation of infinity and void anticipates positions that would become scientifically mainstream only with Galileo and Newton. Adamson situates him alongside al-Ghazālī: both held philosophers to their own standards without rejecting philosophy entirely.
