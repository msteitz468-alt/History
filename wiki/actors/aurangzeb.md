# Aurangzeb

**Aurangzeb** (r. 1658–1707) was the last of the "Great Mughals" — the austere, orthodox emperor whose
forty-eight-year reign carried the [[mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] to its greatest territorial extent
yet exhausted it in the Deccan. Documented for the wiki through
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965), which offers a notably
**revisionist** assessment.

## Spear's revisionist view

Aurangzeb is conventionally the dark mirror of [[akbar|Akbar]] — bigotry against tolerance, failure
against the Marathas against success with the Rajputs (a "reverse to the Akbarian medal," fixed in
European eyes by Bernier's 1670 *Travels*). Spear pushes back: Aurangzeb ruled India **nearly as long
as Akbar and left the empire *larger* than he found it**; his ruthless rise (imprisoning his father
Shah Jahan, destroying his brothers including the syncretist Dara Shikoh) was "not more so than that
of others of his race"; he "succeeded not because he was more cruel but because he was more
**efficient**." A simple, austere man in private, an orthodox Sunni who saw himself as a model Muslim
ruler, he **"consciously tolerated Hindus rather than treating them as equals"** — but his "supposed
intolerance is little more than a hostile legend" built on isolated acts (a mosque on a Benares temple
site; the re-imposition of the *jizya*, 1679). In Pakistan, Spear notes, many proclaim him the greatest
Muslim ruler of India.

## The two halves of the reign

Spear divides the reign in two. The **first ~23 years** continued Shah Jahan's administration with
"an added note of austerity," keeping Marathas, Jats, and north-western tribes in order from Delhi.
From **1681** he virtually moved his capital to the **Deccan**, where he spent his last quarter-century
in camp — overthrowing the Deccan sultanates of **Bijapur and Golconda (1686–87)** but failing to crush
the **[[maratha-empire|Maratha]]** rebellion. The "assured administrator became the embattled old man";
the subtle politician became an ascetic copying the Koran and pouring out agonised letters — yet never
loosening his grip (his eldest son "never received a letter from his father without trembling").

## The reaction he embodied — and provoked

Aurangzeb represented the **orthodox reaction** against Akbar's syncretism, drawing on the thinker
Sheikh Ahmad **Sirhindi** — providing Indian Muslims an "alternative sheet-anchor" (the sovereignty of
God and the congregation of the faithful) for when the temporal power should fail: in Spear's reading,
"the germ for a Muslim state in India." His execution of the Sikh **Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675)** turned
the Sikhs into a militant anti-Mughal community under Guru Gobind Singh, and his Deccan obsession bled
the empire that **collapsed within a generation** of his death (1707).

## Significance

Aurangzeb is the hinge between the Mughal apogee and its fall — "both a most able statesman and a
subtle, highly complex character," not the cardboard "Mughal ogre" of popular history. His reign
exposes the central strain of the Mughal project: the tension between Akbar's inclusive imperial
sanctity and an orthodox Islam that, reasserted, alienated the Hindu majority on whose acceptance the
empire rested.
