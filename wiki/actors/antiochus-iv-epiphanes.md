# Antiochus IV Epiphanes

The energetic, controversial Seleucid king — conqueror of Egypt halted by Rome's
"line in the sand," and the persecutor whom Jewish tradition made the archetypal
oppressor. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 10 (Habicht).

## Role and career

A younger son of [[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III]], he had been a Roman
**hostage** (a fruit of Apamea), and seized the throne in 175 — with
[[pergamon|Eumenes II's]] backing — after the murder of his brother **Seleucus IV**.
He proved "high-spirited, capable, energetic," styling himself ***Theos Epiphanes***
("God Manifest") — the first Hellenistic king to claim divinity in his own titles —
which his enemies parodied as *Epimanes* ("the madman"). Habicht stresses that his
policy was in fact **steady and prudent**: he broke the Seleucids' post-Apamea
isolation by lavish benefactions to Greek states (above all **Athens**, where he
funded the Olympieion), paid off the last Roman indemnity (173), and rebuilt royal
finances.

His great test was the **Sixth Syrian War** with Egypt (170–168): he won decisively,
overran almost all of Egypt, and could have taken the crown — until the **[[day-of-eleusis-168bce|
Day of Eleusis]]** (168), when the Roman envoy **Popillius Laenas**, drawing a circle
in the sand around him, forced his immediate withdrawal. His compliance, Habicht
argues, "disproves the allegations that he was unbalanced."

He then turned to the **[[maccabean-revolt-167-160bce|Jewish question]]** — where his
prohibition of Judaism and desecration of the Temple (168) provoked the Maccabean
revolt — and to a great **eastern campaign** (165–164) to shore up the disintegrating
upper satrapies (Armenia, Persis, Elymais, against the rising **Parthians**), during
which he died at Tabae in Persia (164).

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Antiochus IV is the last Seleucid king who looked like a great-power monarch — and
the [[day-of-eleusis-168bce|Day of Eleusis]] is the moment Rome demonstrated it could
dictate to the Hellenistic East without an army. Habicht's revisionist reading
**shifts blame for the Jewish persecution**: the Hellenizing initiative came from
**Jews themselves** (the high priests Jason and Menelaus), and Antiochus, reading
revolt where there was factional strife, over-reacted — his one serious misjudgement.
His death opened the [[seleucid-empire|terminal dynastic decline]].

## Historiography

The sources are doubly hostile — **[[polybius]]** (friend of his rival Demetrius) and
the **Books of Maccabees** / Daniel (Jewish). Habicht systematically corrects the
"madman" caricature and the older view that Antiochus pursued a programme of forced
Hellenization. See [[maccabean-revolt-167-160bce]], [[day-of-eleusis-168bce]].
