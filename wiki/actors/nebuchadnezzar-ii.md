# Nebuchadnezzar II

**Nebuchadnezzar II** (Akkadian *Nabu-kudurri-usur*, "O Nabu, protect my lineage";
Biblical Nebuchadnezzar, classical Nabuchodonosor; reigned **605–562 BCE**) was the
greatest king of the [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]] — the conqueror
who shattered Egypt at [[battle-of-carchemish-605bce|Carchemish]], the destroyer of
[[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|Jerusalem]] who began the **Babylonian Exile**, and the
builder who made [[babylon|Babylon]] the wonder of the ancient world. Son of
**[[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]]**, he is treated by [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH
Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 27, D. J. Wiseman).

## Crown prince and the defeat of Egypt

Named crown prince at the foundation ceremonies of the rebuilt Babylon, Nebuchadrezzar
commanded his father's armies in the west against Egypt, which under **Necho II** had
seized the Assyrian succession in Syria. In **605** he marched directly on
**Carchemish**, crossed the Euphrates to cut off the Egyptian retreat, and
**annihilated** Necho's forces — "not a single man escaped to his own country"
([[battle-of-carchemish-605bce]]). The victory delivered the whole former Assyrian
west. Hearing of **Nabopolassar's death** (605), he crossed the desert with a few
companions, reached Babylon in under two weeks, and "sat on the royal throne" in his
own right.

## The reduction of the Levant

For a decade the Chronicle records him "marching about victoriously" in Khatti
(Syria), enforcing tribute. **Jehoiakim of Judah** submitted, then rebelled after
the bloody but indecisive **battle near Pelusium (601)** — fought "in the open,
favourable for chariots," in which "both sides inflicted a major defeat on each
other," forcing Babylon into a year of re-equipment. Nebuchadnezzar's reprisals
came in his seventh year: he besieged Jerusalem and **captured it on 15/16 March
597**, deporting King **Jehoiachin** (whose rations are attested on tablets from
Babylon's southern citadel) and installing **Zedekiah**. When Zedekiah too revolted,
Nebuchadnezzar returned and **destroyed Jerusalem and its temple in 587/586**
([[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce]]), blinding Zedekiah at Riblah after killing his sons.
He simultaneously pressed the famous **thirteen-year siege of Tyre** (c. 587–572).
By the close of the reign he claimed dominion "from the Upper to the Lower seas,"
an empire larger than [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal's]].

## The builder of Babylon

Wiseman stresses that Nebuchadnezzar is remembered in Mesopotamia not as a conqueror
but as the king who **rebuilt [[babylon|Babylon]]** as the dynastic capital, using
the tribute and the deported and corvée labour of the empire. He completed the
ziggurat **Etemenanki** and the temple **Esagila** of Marduk (overlaid with gold);
built the **Southern Palace** and the riverside terraces traditionally identified
with the **"hanging gardens"** said to console his Median queen **Amytis**; paved the
**Processional Way** (*Ay-ibur-shabu*) through the **Ishtar Gate**; and threw up the
massive double walls and the cross-country **"Median Wall"** against the threat from
the north-east. An estimated **164 million bricks** went into the outer northern
defence wall alone. The works drew specialist foreign labour — shipwrights from
Tyre, woodworkers from Byblos and Arvad, Ionian Greeks, Egyptians.

## Character and self-image

The royal inscriptions are striking for their **absence of military stance**: they
emphasize devotion to **Marduk** and moral qualities. A unique propaganda document
styles him **"king of justice,"** a reformer in the line of Urukagina and Hammurabi
who took the side of "the weak, poor, crippled, and widowed," suppressed bribery,
and "ceaselessly worked to please the great lord Marduk." A fragmentary epic-historical
text hints at an introspective, even troubled side — anger and illness severe enough
to leave Babylonia for a time — possibly the kernel behind the **Daniel** traditions
of the king's madness. He held all his territories firm for forty years and passed
them on intact.

## Counterfactual significance

Wiseman's analytical point is double-edged: Nebuchadnezzar's personal grip held a
fissiparous tribal-and-temple polity together, and "the suddenness of the decline
from the splendours of [his] day to the final denouement" under [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]]
testifies to how much depended on the man. Without his annihilating victory at
Carchemish, a Saite Egyptian Syria — not a Babylonian one — might have faced the
rising Persians.

## Historiography

The reign is the **best-documented** of the dynasty for events down to **594/3**,
thanks to the **Babylonian Chronicle**; thereafter Wiseman reconstructs it from
dated contracts, building inscriptions, archaeology (the destruction layers of the
City of David and Judaean towns), the **Hebrew Bible** (hostile), and later
classical writers (Berossus via Josephus). The gap between the **Biblical tyrant**
and the **Babylonian "king of justice"** is itself a historiographical lesson in
source bias. See [[neo-babylonian-civilization]] for the world his reign produced.
