# The Swiss Confederation (Eidgenossenschaft)

The federation of alpine and lowland cantons (*Orte*) that emerged "about the year 1500 as a distinct
political unit within the German Empire," treated by Roger Sablonier in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|
NCMH VII]] (Ch. 25). Beginning as "just one of the numerous systems of alliances" in the Empire, the
Confederation acquired by 1500 a "visible political cohesion" and, through its formidable infantry,
a reputation that reached across Europe.

## Structure

The Confederation was a web of mostly **bilateral treaties** among very diverse partners: free
imperial cities (**Berne, Zurich, Lucerne, Zug**), the rural valley communes of central Switzerland
(the three forest cantons of **Uri, Schwyz, Unterwalden**, allied since 1291/1315), and a penumbra of
**associated members** (*Zugewandte Orte*) and jointly-governed subject territories (*Gemeine
Herrschaften*). It "occupied no clearly defined territory" and each canton "guarded its freedom of
manoeuvre" — a federal republic of communes and cities, anomalous in a Europe of consolidating
monarchies and princely states.

## The Path to Independence

The fifteenth century saw the cantons throw off the lordship of the **Habsburgs** and refuse imperial
obligations. The threat from **[[valois-burgundian-state|Charles the Bold of Burgundy]]** drove a brief
rapprochement with the Habsburg Archduke Sigismund (the Perpetual Accord, *Ewige Richtung*, 1474) and
then the shattering Swiss victories of the **Burgundian Wars** — **Grandson** and **Morat (1476)** and
the duke's death at Nancy (1477) — which made the Confederates' pike squares the most feared infantry
in Europe (see [[military-revolution]]). The **Swabian War of 1499**, fought "almost as a civil war"
against the south German and Austrian nobility, ended in a sweeping Swiss victory that "sealed their
separation from the Empire in fact if not in law." The Swiss accepted the Empire only as a juridical
*imperium*, not as the state-like *regnum* with its fiscal and constitutional demands.

## Significance

The Swiss represent the road **not** taken by late-medieval Germany: a durable **corporative-confederal
republic** rather than a princely territorial state (the contrast Tom Scott draws for the
[[holy-roman-empire-late-medieval|Empire]]). Their pikemen also became Europe's premier mercenary
infantry — hired by France, Burgundy and Venice — until the slaughter of the dense squares by French
artillery at **Marignano (1515)** revealed their vulnerability to firearms.
