## Overview

The Republic of Ireland — constitutionally *Éire* (Ireland) — is the twenty-six-county southern Irish state. Formally a republic since 1949, it was constitutionally established by the 1937 Constitution drafted under [[eamon-de-valera]]. It succeeded the [[irish-free-state]] (1922–37) and is the political entity that emerged from the 1921 Anglo-Irish Treaty, the [[irish-civil-war-1922]], and subsequent constitutional evolution under Fianna Fáil (1932–).

## Territorial Extent and Governing Structure

The state comprises 26 of Ireland's 32 counties, with [[northern-ireland]] (6 counties) remaining part of the United Kingdom. The 1937 Constitution (Articles 2 and 3, as originally drafted) asserted a territorial claim over the whole island — 'pending the re-integration of the national territory' — though this was amended as part of the [[good-friday-agreement-1998]] process in 1998.

The state is a parliamentary democracy with a proportional representation electoral system and a bicameral legislature (Dáil and Seanad). Executive power is vested in a cabinet (government) led by the Taoiseach, with a largely ceremonial president. The judiciary has played an increasingly active role in constitutional interpretation since the 1960s.

## Constitutional Development

**1937 Constitution.** De Valera's constitution replaced the 1922 Free State constitution. It removed the oath of allegiance to the Crown, abolished the office of Governor-General, and established a broadly Catholic-framed document influenced by European (especially Weimar) constitutional thinking and by Jesuit social teaching. Article 44 recognized the 'special position' of the Catholic Church (a compromise — de Valera rejected demands for exclusive Catholic recognition). Articles on family, women's role in the home, and private property reflected Catholic social doctrine. The constitution contained scope for amendment by referendum — a mechanism later used to liberalize laws on divorce (1995), same-sex marriage (2015), and abortion (2018).

**Republic Act 1949.** The Inter-Party Government under John A. Costello (Fine Gael) declared Ireland a republic in 1949, formally leaving the Commonwealth. The Ireland Act (Westminster, 1949) guaranteed that Northern Ireland could not become part of Ireland without Stormont's consent — reflecting the 'moral capital' accumulated by Northern Ireland's wartime participation as against the Free State's neutrality.

## Economic Development

The state's economic trajectory passed through several distinct phases:

**1922–58: Protectionism and stagnation.** Cumann na nGaedheal (1922–32) pursued fiscal retrenchment and free trade, prioritizing agricultural exports to Britain. Fianna Fáil (1932–) introduced protectionism, the Economic War with Britain (1932–8, arising from withholding land annuities), and import-substitution industrialization. Neither approach achieved the self-sufficiency promised in nationalist ideology. By the 1950s, half a million people emigrated in a single decade (1951–61); 60,000 left in 1957 alone.

**1958–73: Lemass modernization.** [[sean-lemass|Seán Lemass]] and T.K. Whitaker launched the First Programme for Economic Expansion (1958), abandoning protectionism, welcoming foreign direct investment, and applying for EEC membership (1961, admitted 1973). GDP growth accelerated, emigration fell, and employment in manufacturing expanded. However, the Lemass reforms took thirty years to produce sustained prosperity; 'by 1987 Ireland, although her performance had improved considerably, was actually relatively poorer than in 1957' (Girvin, Jackson ch. 36).

**1987–2008: Social partnership and the Celtic Tiger.** Following the fiscal crisis of the mid-1980s, a social partnership framework (national wage agreements between government, employers, and trade unions) stabilized the economy. Low corporate tax rates, EU structural funds, and a surge in US foreign direct investment (particularly in pharmaceuticals and technology) produced the 'Celtic tiger' boom from the mid-1990s. Ireland generated one million new jobs in fifteen years and for the first time experienced full employment. By 2000, 'there was peace in both parts of the island, the Irish republic had a high and rising standard of living shared by most people, and public opinion was converging with European attitudes as never before' (Girvin, ch. 36).

**2008–: Crisis and recovery.** The Celtic tiger collapsed in the 2008 global financial crisis, compounded by a property bubble, under-regulated banking (Anglo-Irish Bank), and procyclical government spending. An EU/IMF/ECB bailout of €85 billion was agreed in November 2010. The severity of the Irish crisis was 'a consequence of policy errors on the part of the political elite' (Girvin, ch. 36), particularly low tax, preferential treatment for property speculation, and a poorly regulated banking system.

## Church-State Relations

The influence of the Catholic Church on matters of public morality, health, education, and welfare was pervasive and largely unchallenged until the 1970s. Key episodes include: the 1929 Censorship of Publications Act; the Mother and Child Scheme controversy (1951, when Archbishop McQuaid torpedoed Noel Browne's free maternity care proposals); the 1983 referendum inserting an absolute abortion ban into the constitution; and the 1986 divorce referendum (defeated). Revelations from the 1990s onwards of widespread institutional clerical abuse — in industrial schools, Magdalen laundries, and orphanages — shattered clerical authority and accelerated secularization.

## Northern Ireland Policy

The Republic's relationship with Northern Ireland oscillated between irredentist rhetoric and pragmatic disengagement. No southern government formulated a constructive policy on the north until the Lemass-O'Neill meetings of 1965. The Arms Crisis of 1969–70 (when ministers Haughey and Blaney were dismissed for allegedly conspiring to import arms for northern nationalists) illustrated the tension between public moderation and private mobilization. The Anglo-Irish Agreement (1985) gave the Republic a formal consultative role in Northern Irish governance. The [[good-friday-agreement-1998]] completed the transition to a constitutional approach: the Republic amended Articles 2 and 3, accepting that unification required majority northern consent.

## Political System

Fianna Fáil dominated the political system from 1932 to 2011, winning an average 45 per cent of the vote across 24 general elections — 'virtually without equal across the western democratic universe' (Peter Mair, 2004). The Civil War cleavage (pro-treaty Fine Gael vs anti-treaty Fianna Fáil) structured the party system until the 2008–11 crisis, when Fianna Fáil's vote collapsed from 41 per cent (2007) to 17 per cent (2011) — its worst result since the 1920s.
