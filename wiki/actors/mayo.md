# Mayo (Yoreme)

## Overview

The **Mayos** (often **Yoreme**) of the **Mayo River** basin (Sonora–Sinaloa borderlands) are Cahita-speaking neighbors of the [[yaqui|Yaquis]], treated with them in Susan M. Deeds’s CHNPA II.2 Ch. 13 section “Yaquis and Mayos.” Substantial Mayo-speakers persist in modern censuses but are nearly all bilingual; communities can be hard to distinguish from mestizo neighbors—yet language and a post-Revolution revitalized ceremonial tradition (with Mayo prophets/saints) continue.

## Colonial and early national patterns

With Yaquis, Opatas, and Pimas, Mayos experienced the **Sonora pattern**: later/smaller silver strikes meant less early civil interference; less hostility to mission changes and greater capacity to manipulate the process. They figure among peoples who joined the **1740** Yaqui rebellion after harvest failure and flooding—actions uncoordinated, not a concerted attempt to erase Spanish presence.

Mid-18th-c. **peasantization** trajectory (with Tepehuanes, SE Rarámuri, Opatas): non-Indian entry into coastal and valley lands; Jesuit settlement limits challenged from the second quarter of the 18th c. After independence, Mayos joined the **1828–1833** Juan Banderas pan-Indian insurrection seeking autonomous rule and collective riverine lands.

## Porfirian crisis and millenarianism

Porfirian Sonora elites promoted commercial agriculture on irrigable Yaqui **and Mayo** rivers. After the 1880s, Mayos were **unable to present unified resistance** comparable to Cajeme’s Yaqui “republic.” **1891–92** millenarian movement of **Teresa Urrea** (La Santa de Cabora)—Mayo mother, non-Indian rancher father; curing powers; Mayos, Yaquis, and poor Tarahumara mestizos anticipated an egalitarian world against Porfirian policies. Mostly channeled through Cahitan-Christian *pahkom* (purifying flood vision) rather than open Tomóchic-style insurrection; harsh **deportations to Baja California** labor followed.

## Twentieth century

Deeds notes substantial Mayo-speakers and some mountain Pimas persist but nearly all bilingual, communities hard to distinguish from mestizo; Seris, Rarámuri, Tepehuanes, Guarijíos more ethnically distinguishable—**only Yaquis** managed full separate-identity endurance in high-resource zones. Mayo language and revitalized ceremonial tradition with local prophets/saints remain the main markers of continuity. Crumrine, *The Mayo Indians of Sonora*, is the key ethnographic monographic reference Deeds flags.

## Counterfactual significance

Without riverine mission corporate inheritance shared with Yaquis, Mayo identity would likely have dissolved earlier into mestizo Sinaloa–Sonora peasantries. Fragmentation after the 1880s (vs Yaqui captain-general structure) made coordinated defense of Mayo River lands harder under Porfirian capital—illustrating Deeds’s point that **nature of accommodation and solidarity**, not resource richness alone, decides endurance.
