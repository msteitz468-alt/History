# Zagwe Dynasty

The **Zagwe** (or Zagwé) dynasty ruled the highland Christian kingdom of the northern Ethiopian interior from approximately AD 900 until 1270, when it was displaced by the "Solomonic" dynasty claiming descent from the Aksumite kings. The Zagwe represent the culminating phase of a southward political shift that began with the decline of [[aksum|Aksum]] and the relocation of the capital into eastern Tigray. Their principal monument is the [[lalibela|Lalibela]] church complex — among the greatest medieval monuments of African Christianity.

## Overview

The Zagwe were based in the mountains of **Lasta**, in what is now the eastern Amhara Region, with their political centre at Adefa near the ecclesiastical establishment of **Roha** (later renamed Lalibela after the famous king). They appear to have originated among Cushitic-speaking **Agau** peoples but adopted Christianity — if they had not done so already — and in important contexts used Ge'ez, the Semitic language of the Aksumite court and church. Their very name "Zagwe" is understood to derive from their Agau heritage; their opponents later exploited this to paint them as alien usurpers despite their evidently Christian credentials.

Most surviving historical information about the Zagwe was recorded after their displacement in 1270 and is preserved in forms acceptable to their rivals. The *gadl* (hagiography) of King Lalibela, edited by Perruchon, was compiled in the fifteenth century — over two hundred years after the events it describes — based on a nineteenth-century copy. This retrospective record-making, combined with active Solomonid propaganda, has distorted the historical picture.

## Dating and Chronology

The chronology of the Zagwe is contested. Estimates of the dynasty's duration based on royal genealogies range from 130 to 370 years. Since termination around 1270 CE is secure, these figures place the dynasty's inception between 900 and 1140. Taddesse Tamrat's widely followed "short" chronology placed the rise of the Zagwe in the early twelfth century — but this was based on the then-prevailing belief that Aksum's final decline occurred in the tenth or eleventh century. Phillipson (2012) and Tekeste (2006) independently argue that, given re-dating of Aksum's actual decline to c. 700 CE, a rise of Zagwe authority "in or even shortly before the eleventh century" is more probable and closes the otherwise unexplained three-century gap in Ethiopian political history.

Phillipson's revised model: the post-Aksumite eastern Tigray kingdom was active through the eighth to tenth centuries; the political centre shifted south into Lasta around or before the eleventh century (the same period when church-creation in Tigray diminished and the Lalibela complex's major building phases began); Zagwe authority was then firmly established until displaced in 1270.

## The Solomonid Overthrow and Retrospective Delegitimisation

Around **1270 CE** the Zagwe were replaced by a dynasty claiming direct descent from the Aksumite royal line and thus from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba — the "Solomonic restoration." The propaganda accompanying this change was, in Phillipson's words, "extraordinarily successful," presenting a seizure of power by Semitic-speaking Amhara from Agau-descended Zagwe rulers as a restoration of legitimate ancient kingship.

Phillipson's key observation: the political and ethnic dimensions of the "Solomonic restoration" should be understood as a **further stage in the continuing southward shift of the power-centre** — from Aksum (Tigray highlands) to Zagwe Lasta (Amhara Region) to Solomonid territory even further south. The dominance of Christianity continued throughout; political change had little direct impact on ecclesiastical affairs. Three Zagwe kings are regarded as saints by the Ethiopian Orthodox Church; not one succeeding Solomonid king achieved canonisation.

## Lalibela and the Church-Building Programme

The dynasty's most enduring achievement is the church complex at **[[lalibela|Lalibela]]**. Phillipson demonstrates that the complex was not created in a single reign but developed across **five successive stages** spanning perhaps five centuries:

- **Stages I–II** (possibly 7th–8th c.): Earliest features were defensive, not ecclesiastical
- **Stage III** (c. 10th c.): First Christian conversion of earlier structures; first Aksumite-style features
- **Stage IV** (c. 11th c.): The great monolithic basilican churches — Beta Madhane Alem, Beta Emmanuel, Beta Maryam — with self-consciously Aksumite architectural vocabulary
- **Stage V** (late 12th – early 13th c.): Features specifically associated with King Lalibela himself; creation of the complex's symbolic geography as an Ethiopian Jerusalem

The Zagwe programme was more than aesthetics: by deploying Aksumite architectural forms prominently at Lalibela they were asserting their status as heirs of Aksumite Christian civilisation. The largest church, Beta Madhane Alem, may have intentionally replicated the ancient Maryam Tsion Cathedral at Aksum. The fall of Jerusalem to Salah ed-Din in 1187 may have intensified this symbolic programme, stimulating development of a local counterpart to the holy city.

Other Zagwe churches include **Imrahanna Kristos** — a built (not rock-hewn) cave-church north of Lalibela, homogeneous in design with strong Aksumite features, attributed by tradition to an early twelfth-century priest-king predecessor of King Lalibela — and **Ganata Maryam**, a monolithic basilica with external colonnade and fine painted interior.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Zagwe, the Aksumite Christian tradition might have been extinguished in the gap between the seventh-century Arab encirclement and the eventual Solomonid consolidation. The Zagwe preserved, elaborated, and geographically extended Ethiopian Christianity into new territories (Lasta, Amhara Region) that had previously lacked church architecture. They handed the Solomonids a living and spatially expanded Christian tradition — the institutional and cultural basis of medieval Ethiopian statehood.

## Historiography

Historical sources are almost entirely retrospective and shaped by Solomonid interests. Modern reconstruction depends heavily on architectural and archaeological evidence. Phillipson (2012), building on earlier work by Munro-Hay (1991) and Taddesse (1972), has placed the Zagwe within a long-continuity framework grounded in church chronology. Tekeste (2006) reaches similar conclusions independently. Derat (2010) provides what Phillipson calls "a thorough and dispassionate overview of controversies surrounding the Zagwe."

The most important recent historiographical development is the re-dating of Aksum's terminal decline from c. 1000 CE (earlier consensus) to c. 700 CE (established by Munro-Hay 1989 on numismatic grounds). This revision transforms the Zagwe period: rather than being an immediately-post-Aksumite dynasty, they are the culmination of a three-century post-Aksumite arc that began with the eastern Tigray church-builders.
