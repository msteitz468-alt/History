# George Fitzhugh (1806–1881)

Virginia lawyer and the antebellum South's most radical proslavery
theorist: *Sociology for the South* (1854) and *Cannibals All! or, Slaves
Without Masters* (1857) argued that slavery was a positive social system
superior to the North's "wage slavery," and should in principle extend
beyond race. The boldest voice of the **Reactionary Enlightenment** —
[[hartz-louis|Hartz]]'s term for the South's attempt at genuine
European-style conservatism — and, for Hartz, its most revealing failure.

## Hartz's analysis

**The most consistent — and therefore most exposed.** Fitzhugh had "a
touch of the Hobbesian lucidity of mind": he saw through
[[calhoun-john-c|Calhoun]]'s contradiction and discarded compact theory
for an "organic nationality" grounded directly in slave society. On the
eve of Gettysburg he proclaimed: "We begin a great conservative
reaction... We attempt to roll back the Reformation in its political
phases." Yet Hartz's verdict on the whole movement runs through Fitzhugh:
"when we penetrate beneath the feudal and reactionary surface of Southern
thought, we do not find feudalism: we find slavery" — a "simple fraud"
masking "the impending disappearance of something very old," because "a
feudalism that has once been liberal can never be really feudal."

**Internal fractures Hartz catalogues:**
1. **Race vs. universal feudal socialism** — his logic said serfdom is
   good for whites too (the *Cannibals All!* "trump card"), colliding with
   the racial-inferiority theory the South actually needed; and the racial
   theory itself backfired, since legal limits on masters and claims of
   slave "affection" conceded the slave's humanity.
2. **Industrial promotionalism vs. anti-industrialism** — his program for
   industrializing the South "would have delighted Henry Clay," against
   his own Carlylean denunciations of Northern manufacturing (De Bow
   meanwhile feared an "upbearing of our masses").
3. **Free trade vs. paternalism** — Southern Manchesterians (Dew, Legaré)
   undercut the Disraelian pose from the other side.
4. **The forty-eight-hours admission** — pressed on "wage slavery," he
   conceded American laborers could escape "in forty-eight hours" to the
   West and become proprietors, demolishing his own Ricardo–Malthus
   indictment. His confession that Southerners "were not real Tory
   humanitarians" — America lacking Europe's feudal/clerical "natural
   friends of the poor" — is, for Hartz, the movement admitting its own
   fraudulence.

**The positivist wing.** Allied "sociologists" (George Frederick Holmes,
Henry Hughes) built a Comtean science of hierarchy ("orderers" and
"orderees") proving free society a worse, informal slavery — a genuinely
deep critique of social coercion anticipating Marx and Veblen, but
self-destroying by overstatement: "Starting out as positivists, they
ended up as metaphysicians." Fitzhugh's "domestic affection" doctrine
(only literal ownership produces genuine care) is "a weird extension of
Locke."

**Oblivion.** After 1865 the Reactionary Enlightenment "was forgotten,
and with a vengeance" — unlike defeated European conservatisms that fed
later thought (Disraeli, Carlyle, Berryer). American categories calling
Hamilton or McKinley "conservative" leave "no room whatever for the
feudalists of the ante-bellum South." Even the Southern Agrarians of the
1930s did not build on him. See [[american-civil-war-1861-1865]].

## Historiography

Rediscovered in the mid-twentieth century precisely by consensus-era
scholars (Hartz 1955; later Eugene Genovese, who took Fitzhugh far more
seriously as evidence of a genuinely paternalist master-class worldview —
*The World the Slaveholders Made*, 1969, directly contesting Hartz's
"fraud" verdict). The Hartz–Genovese disagreement over whether the Old
South was Lockean-capitalist or genuinely pre-bourgeois remains a live
axis of antebellum historiography.
