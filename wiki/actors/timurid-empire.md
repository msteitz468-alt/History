The Timurid Empire was the dynastic state founded by [[timur]] (Tamerlane) from his base at Samarqand, ruling the Syr-Oxus basin and the Iranian highlands from 1370 and reaching maximal extent (encompassing Khurâsân, Transoxiana, Iraq, Azerbaijan, and nominally Delhi and Cairo) around 1402–03 before Timur's death in 1405. After a period of succession wars among his sons and grandsons, the empire stabilized under Shâhrukh (ruled Khurâsân 1405–47), who made Herat the cultural capital of a brilliant Timurid florescence in the arts, historiography, and sciences.

## Overview

The Timurid Empire was the culminating expression of the [[military-patronage-state]] model introduced into [[islamicate|Islamicate]] civilization by the Mongols. It differed from pure Mongol successors in that its founder was a Turk claiming Chingizid legitimacy through genealogical fiction, and in that it presided over a markedly higher level of conscious cultural patronage than any of the Mongol successor states had achieved. Hodgson notes that Timur "destroyed in the grand manner, he built in the grand manner too."

## Founding and Peak (1370–1405)

Timur consolidated control over the old Chaghatay domains by 1370 and over Khwârazm by 1380. His subsequent campaigns (detailed under [[timur]]) brought virtually the entire former [[ilkhanate]] territory, much of the [[golden-horde]]'s domain, and briefly Delhi and Cairo into at least nominal submission by 1403. He retained a nominal Chingizid khan as puppet ruler and claimed only the titles *amîr* and *sultan*.

Samarqand, his capital, was transformed through the forced relocation of artisans and artists from every conquered city into one of the most architecturally splendid cities in the Islamicate world. Timur began parcelling out provinces to sons and grandsons before his death — the centrifugal tendency inherent in Mongol inheritance customs (equal division among sons) undermined any stable succession.

## Timurid Florescence under Shâhrukh (1405–47)

After succession wars among Timur's heirs, his son Shâhrukh secured control of Khurâsân and eventually of the greater part of the Syr-Oxus and Iranian domains. Shâhrukh moved the principal cultural capital to Herat, where under him and his successors the Timurid court became the leading patron of Persian-language poetry, miniature painting, historiography, and the sciences in the Islamicate world.

Hodgson notes that in Iran in the fifteenth century, especially its latter part, "we find a creative ferment in the arts and in related aspects of life which bears many striking analogies to contemporary life in Renaissance Italy." The Timurid court's openness to Indic, Chinese, and even Occidental cultural material — a legacy of the Mongol world-historical consciousness — gave its patronage an unusually broad character. The scholar al-Suyûtî and the military inventor in Shâhrukh's court (who demonstrated a new stone-throwing engine) both exemplify the period's blend of conservative spirit with genuine innovation operating within established forms.

## Governing Structure

The Timurid state embodied the three defining features of the military-patronage state as Hodgson identifies them:

1. **Dynastic law** alongside the Sharî'ah — the ruler's decrees formed a binding body of law separate from but parallel to Islamic legal tradition.
2. **The whole state as a single military force** — administration was organized as an extension of the army; civil officials were enrolled as honorary military personnel (*askerî*); the court followed the monarch on campaign.
3. **Exploitation of all economic and cultural resources as dynastic appanage** — artisans, scholars, and even poets were incorporated into the military household and exempted from taxes as patronage dependents.

## Decline and End

The Timurid state gradually contracted as Uzbek pressure from the north increased through the later fifteenth century. The empire was extinguished when the Shaybanid Uzbeks overran Transoxiana and took Samarqand in 1500 and Herat in 1507. A Timurid prince, Bâbur, was driven from Samarqand and eventually made his way to Kabul and then to India, where he founded the Mughal Empire (1526).
