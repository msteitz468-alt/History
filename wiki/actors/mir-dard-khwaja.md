# Khwāja Mīr Dard

Khwāja Mīr Dard (1721–1785) of Delhi was the first major mystical poet in Urdu and the founder of the *ṭarīqa muḥammadiyya* ("Muhammadan Path"), a Naqshbandī sub-order distinguished by its fundamentalist Prophetocentric piety and strict sobriety — combined, paradoxically, with a deep love of music and poetry.

## Family and Formation

Dard was the son of Muḥammad Nāṣir ʿAndalīb (1697–1758), a sayyid of Turkish descent and descendant of Bahāʾuddīn Naqshband, whose mother claimed descent from ʿAbduʾl-Qādir Gīlānī. Nāṣir ʿAndalīb renounced military service to become a dervish under Shāh Saʿdullāh Gulshan (a Naqshbandī master and Urdu poetry patron) and later under Pīr Muḥammad Zubayr — the fourth and final *qayyūm* from the house of [[sirhindi-ahmad]]. After Zubayr's death (1740) he dictated to his son Khwāja Mīr the *Nāla-yi ʿAndalīb* ("Lamentation of the Nightingale"), a vast allegorical theological work in which the hero "Nightingale" is ultimately revealed as the Prophet.

Dard became his father's first disciple and "spent the rest of his life propagating the doctrine of 'sincere Muhammadanism,' which was a fundamentalist interpretation of Islam, deepened by the mystical and ascetic techniques of the Naqshbandiyya" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Mystical Path

Dard's spiritual autobiography in *ʿIlm ul-kitāb* describes his ascent from "intoxication and poetic exuberance to the quiet, sober attitude of a 'sincere Muhammadan'" — a path through the *fanāʾ* in the sheikh (annihilation in the master), then in the Prophet, then in God, followed by the "descent" (*baqāʾ*) back into creation as vicegerent. Uniquely, his father-sheikh and mystical guide were the same person:

> "The higher than he who has reached this stage is he who has found baqā in his sheikh, for he has completed the whole circle. This is the terminating rank which God Almighty has kept for the pure Muhammadans." (*ʿIlm ul-kitāb* 115–16)

He received ninety-nine divine names, corresponding to the ninety-nine Beautiful Names of God, and claimed a series of ascents through the "sanctities" of all the major prophets (Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, David, Jesus, Muhammad). He distinguished his position from *waḥdat al-wujūd* (which he criticised as incomplete): true Muhammadan *tawḥīd* meant "immersion in the contemplation of God along with the preservation of the stages of servantship" (*ʿIlm ul-kitāb* 609).

## Works

- **ʿIlm ul-kitāb** (1770): ~111 chapters; spiritual autobiography and doctrinal system; commentary on 111 *wāridāt* (inspirations received ca. 1750). "A kind of spiritual autobiography, which bears an amazing similarity to that of the sixteenth-century Egyptian mystic Shaʿrānī."
- **Chahār Risāla** ("Four Treatises," 1775–85): four stylistically refined spiritual diaries, each divided into 341 sections (the numerical value of *Nāṣir*, his father's name): *Nāla-yi Dard*, *Āh-i sard*, *Dard-i dil*, *Shamʿ-i maḥfil*
- **Urdu Dīwān**: ca. 1200 verses, "most of which are of unsurpassable beauty" — the primary vehicle for his fame; composed on the principle of inspiration, never on commission
- **Ḥurmat-i ghinā**: book defending his practice of listening to music, despite strict Naqshbandī norms against *samāʿ*

## Poetry

Dard's Urdu verse is characterised by concision, mirror imagery, and a pervasive melancholy rooted in the collapsed Mughal world around him:

> "Alas, O ignorant one: at the day of death this will be proved: / A dream was what we saw, what we heard, a tale."

> "In the state of collectedness the single beings of the world are one: / all the petals of the rose together are one."

> "My turpitude is the place of manifestation for the lights of purity; / as much as I am iron, I can become a mirror."

> "Pain and happiness have the same shape in this world: / You may call the rose an open heart, or a broken heart."

He instructed Urdu poets in his home through *mushāʿiras* (poetry gatherings). His Urdu Dīwān was edited by Khalīl ur-Raḥmān Dāʾūdī (Lahore, 1962).

## Music Controversy

Despite Naqshbandī strictures against music, Dard held bimonthly *samāʿ* gatherings attended even by the ruler Shāh ʿĀlam II (pen name Aftāb). He defended himself by insisting he never called singers himself and did not treat listening as worship — "my samāʿ is from God" — while acknowledging it was "contrary to the strict Naqshbandī practice."

## Historical Context

Dard lived through the catastrophic late Mughal period: Nādir Shāh's sack of Delhi (1739), Aḥmad Shāh Durrānī's invasions, the Maratha threat, and chronic civil disorder. He never left Delhi: "Why should I go out? It means just a loss of time; for everywhere there is nothing apparent but annihilation in annihilation." His poetry of dreaming and impermanence reflects this milieu directly.

## Significance

Two of the "pillars of Urdu literature" belonged to the [[naqshbandiyya]]: Maẓhar Jānjānān (killed 1782) and Dard. Schimmel regards the Naqshbandiyya's role in Urdu literary development as an irony — "an anti-artistic order playing a decisive role in a field in which the founders of the order were not at all interested."

**Counterfactual significance**: Without Dard and the Delhi Naqshbandī milieu, the Urdu lyric ghazal would likely have lacked its first distinctly mystical register; the mystical-Urdu synthesis that later shaped Ghālib's reception might not have crystallised.
