# The Crown of Aragon (Aragon-Catalonia)

The **Crown of Aragon** — the dynastic union of the kingdom of **Aragon** and the county of
**Barcelona/Catalonia** under a single count-king — became in the thirteenth century the
great **Mediterranean** power of Christian Iberia. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (David
Abulafia, Ch. 17a, drawing on T.N. Bisson and R.I. Burns) charts its transformation under
James I from a "loose confederation" into a state with a Mediterranean empire.

## A Loose Confederation

At the century's start the count-king ruled "a super-confederation whose only real bond was
the person of the count-king himself," reflecting Catalonia's origins as "a land of
castellans." **Peter II** (1196–1213) was crowned by Innocent III and joined Castile against
the Almohads at **Las Navas de Tolosa (1212)** — but his ambitions in **Languedoc** ended at
the **battle of Muret (1213)**, where he died fighting [[albigensian-crusade-1209|Simon de
Montfort]]. Bisson's study of the **fiscal accounts** shows the crown sliding into debt under
the strain of baronial conflict (over the *Usatges* and the *bovatge* tax), pushing Peter to
rely on **Templar** financiers and **Jewish** tax-farmers.

## James I and the Conquests

Under **James I "the Conqueror" (1213–1276)** "the power and... character of the monarchy was
transformed." Recovering royal revenues (clever recoinage yielded a huge profit, per Bisson)
and pacifying Catalonia, James pursued spectacular **Reconquista** conquests:

- **Majorca and the Balearics (1229–1235)** — taken with Catalan, Provençal and Italian
  shipping; Majorca was "heavily catalanised," repopulated by Catalan, Provençal and Italian
  (and Jewish) settlers, while **Minorca** submitted on terms (1231).
- **Valencia (1238)** — the great mainland conquest, settled and organised under James;
  Muslim enclaves were gradually absorbed, their inhabitants sinking into the degraded status
  of ***mudéjares*** (Muslims under Christian rule).

The **Treaty of Corbeil (1258)** with [[louis-ix-capetian|Louis IX]] formalised the renunciation
of Aragonese claims north of the Pyrenees (with exceptions like Roussillon and Montpellier) in
exchange for the French crown's surrender of ancient claims over Catalonia — "a milestone"
turning Aragon decisively toward the Mediterranean.

## The Mediterranean Empire

Under **Peter III (1276–1285)**, who had married **Constance of Hohenstaufen**, the Crown of
Aragon turned outward: the **[[sicilian-vespers-1282|Sicilian Vespers]]** (1282) brought him
the island of **Sicily**, launching the long **Angevin–Aragonese contest** for the
Mediterranean and resisting even a French "crusade" against Aragon (1285). Catalan merchants,
"with the aggressive support of the kings of Aragon," drove into the trade of the **Maghrib**,
**Valencia** and the **Balearics**, emerging as "the second major Latin trading force in the
western Mediterranean" after [[republic-of-genoa|Genoa]] — especially after Pisa's ruin at
[[battle-of-meloria-1284|Meloria]].

## Significance

The Crown of Aragon shows the thirteenth-century fusion of **Reconquista** conquest and
**commercial expansion**: a count-king's confederation became, within two generations, a
Mediterranean empire stretching from Catalonia and Valencia to the Balearics and Sicily, its
Catalan merchants rivalling the Italians. Its **pactist**, baron-constraining political culture
(the *corts*, the *Usatges*) distinguished it from the more centralised monarchies of
[[philip-iv-capetian|France]] and [[christian-kingdoms-of-spain|Castile]].

## The Fourteenth-Century Mediterranean Empire (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (the "Crown of Aragon" chapter) follows the confederation through the
fourteenth century, when its "external policy focused" on the **Mediterranean**: beyond the Iberian
core (Aragon, Catalonia, Valencia) it held the **Balearics**, **Sicily** and **Sardinia**, and its
Catalan companies even seized the duchies of **Athens and Neopatras** in Greece. Under **Pedro IV
"the Ceremonious" (1336–1387)** the crown reincorporated Majorca, asserted royal authority against
its **pactist** baronage and *corts*, and was drawn into the **War of the Two Pedros** with
[[christian-kingdoms-of-spain|Castile]] (entangled with the Castilian civil war of Pedro I and Henry
of Trastámara). When the ruling line failed, the **Compromise of Caspe (1412)** — a negotiated
arbitration — gave the crown to the Castilian **Trastámara**, binding Aragon's future to Castile and
foreshadowing the eventual union of the Spanish crowns.

## The Fifteenth Century: Trastámara, Crisis and Union (NCMH VII)

[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Mario del Treppo, Ch. 24a) carries the crown through its
fifteenth-century transformation. The **Compromise of Caspe (1412)** installed the Castilian
**Trastámara** Fernando I, but the crown remained a **federal, "pactist" union** of distinct realms —
Aragon, Valencia and the principality of Catalonia — each with its own *corts*, *generalitat* and
liberties, the king bound by contract (*pactisme*) with his subjects.

- **Mediterranean empire.** **Alfonso V "the Magnanimous"** (1416–58) turned the crown outward,
  conquering **[[kingdom-of-sicily|Naples]]** (1442) and ruling a western-Mediterranean empire from
  there; on his death Naples passed to his bastard line (Ferrante) as an Aragonese protectorate.
- **Catalan crisis.** Catalonia, and above all **Barcelona**, sank into civil war under **Joan II**
  (the *remences* peasant revolts; the war of 1462–72), its public debt and patrician oligarchy
  paralysing government — while **Valencia** rose to economic primacy.
- **Ferdinand and the union.** **Ferdinand II** (r. 1479–1516) reformed the Catalan institutions (the
  *redreç* recovery, the *consejo supremo de Aragón*, 1494) — a "restoration of the monarchy's lost
  dignity," not a Castilian absolutist imposition. His marriage to **Isabella** (1469) and the union
  of the crowns created the monarchy of the **[[catholic-monarchs|Catholic Monarchs]]** — but a
  **personal, dynastic union only**: Aragon kept its pluralistic, federal character (and its
  Mediterranean foreign policy, reabsorbing Naples in 1503) down to 1714.
