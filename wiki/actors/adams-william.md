# William Adams (Miura Anjin, 三浦按針)

William Adams — known in Japan as **Miura Anjin** ("the Pilot of Miura") — was an English
navigator who arrived in Japan in 1600 aboard the Dutch vessel *Liefde*, became a trusted
adviser to [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu]], and served as the principal interpreter and
diplomatic intermediary for the early years of European trade relations with the
[[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa bakufu]]. He was the first Englishman to reach Japan and the first
Westerner to attain samurai status. Source: [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]].

---

## Life and Role

Adams arrived with the *Liefde* (one of five Dutch ships that departed in 1598; only the *Liefde*
reached Japan) after a catastrophic voyage. Though the Portuguese missionaries in Japan urged
Ieyasu to execute the surviving crew as pirates, Ieyasu instead had Adams brought to him for
extensive conversations on navigation, European politics, and shipbuilding.

Ieyasu found Adams so useful that he refused to let him leave Japan. Several Dutchmen from the
*Liefde* were taken into service by Matsuura Shigenobu, lord of Hirado, to make guns and
ammunition; in 1605 Captain Jan Quaeckernaeck and supercargo Melchior van Santvoort obtained
permission to return to the Netherlands (Santvoort eventually reaching Patani, from which he
sent an invitation to Dutch merchants to come to Japan). Adams also wished to leave, "to arrange
for English ships to come to Japan, but Ieyasu found him so useful for shipbuilding and
information on other matters that he would not let him."

**Shipbuilding.** Adams built Western-style vessels for Ieyasu, contributing directly to the
nascent Japanese deep-water commercial fleet.

**Status.** Adams became a *hatamoto* — a direct retainer of the shogun — an extraordinary
distinction for a foreigner. He received a village at Hemmi, near Uraga, with eighty or ninety
farmers. He had a house in Edo as well; the site was commemorated in the place name Anjin-chō,
a street in the Uogashi district of Nihon-bashi ward. His Dutch contemporary Jan Joosten
Lodensteyn similarly won Ieyasu's favor, commemorated in the place name Yaesu-chō. Adams held
"the privilege of audience with Ieyasu at all times, 'even when kings and princes are kept out.'"

**Political role.** Ieyasu used Adams as a kind of inspector (*metsuke*). Cocks's diary records
Adams being sent to interrogate Franciscan missionaries who had returned to Edo after being
banned, and carrying Ieyasu's letters to Satsuma for trade permission. When Adams told Ieyasu
that Spanish coastal surveys were considered acts of hostility in Europe, and explained the
expulsion of Catholic missionaries from England, Holland, Scandinavia, and parts of Germany, this
materially influenced Ieyasu's growing suspicion of Iberian missions. Adams also informed him of
Spanish methods of conquest in South America and the Indies.

**Marriage.** Adams married a Japanese woman, reportedly a Catholic named after her father Magome
Kageyu, superintendent of the packhorse exchange at Demma-chō. She appears in Cocks's diary as
"Migmoy," nicknamed "Machiavelli," serving as an agent for the English Company at Edo.

## Relations with the English East India Company

In 1613 John Saris arrived at Hirado aboard the *Clove*, the first English East India Company
mission to Japan. Adams went to Hirado to greet him and then escorted him to Sumpu to be
presented to Ieyasu, and to Edo to be received by Hidetada. He showed Saris his estate at Uraga.
Adams was employed by Saris at a salary of £100 per year as interpreter, though Saris "would not
allow him to handle any money" and suspected Adams of favoring Dutch and Japanese interests over
those of the Company.

Saris chose to base the English factory at Hirado rather than at Edo as Ieyasu had offered,
partly out of distrust of Adams and a desire for independence from his influence. Sadler and his
source James Murdoch both consider this a strategic error: "things were not as they were in the
old man's days" under Hidetada, who became hostile to European trade, and a presence in the
capital might have afforded better protection.

Adams's assessment of the Company's situation proved accurate: Dutch competition backed by far
greater capital, English inability to fund their share of the 1619 Anglo-Dutch Treaty of Defense
(which brought £100,000 in plunder from Manila), and the 1623 Amboyna massacre in which an
English captain was executed, all led to the English abandoning their Japan factory that year.
Adams himself died in 1620, predating the English departure.

---

## Counterfactual Significance

Adams is the clearest example of the structural role individual intermediaries played in the
formation of early Tokugawa foreign-trade policy. His navigation expertise, his ability to
explain European politics to Ieyasu in terms Ieyasu found credible, and his sustained access to
the shogun's person gave him an influence on Tokugawa policy toward Christians and toward Protestant
northern Europeans disproportionate to any formal diplomatic standing. Without Adams, it is
unlikely that Ieyasu would have been as well-informed about the distinction between Protestant and
Catholic commercial powers, and the Dutch invitation of 1609 that initiated three centuries of
Dutch trade with Japan might have been differently received.

---

## Historiography

Adams is documented in the diary of Richard Cocks, factor of the English East India Company at
Hirado, as well as in Japanese sources. Sadler's treatment draws on both. Murdoch's characterization
of Saris as "not much better than a mere dollar-grinding Philistine with a taste for pornographic
pictures" reveals the historiographical tendency to use Adams as a foil; Adams himself left letters
describing conditions in Japan that helped bring the English Company to Hirado.
