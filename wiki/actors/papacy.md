# The Papacy (the Roman See)

The **papacy** is the office and institution of the bishop of Rome, claimed since late
antiquity as the successor of St Peter and the head of the Latin Church. This page
anchors the many wiki references to the papacy and focuses on its **transformation in
the eleventh and twelfth centuries**, which [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] treats as
the decisive episode in its medieval history; for the earlier period see
[[the-papacy-eighth-ninth-centuries|the papacy in the eighth–ninth centuries]] and the
[[gregorian-reform|reform]] pages.

## From Imperial Tutelage to Independence

For much of the tenth and early eleventh centuries the papacy was dominated by the
factions of the Roman nobility and, after 1046, **reformed from above by the emperors**
(Henry III's [[salian-dynasty|Salian]] intervention at the Synod of Sutri, 1046,
installing German reforming popes such as Leo IX). The reform it thus received turned
into a movement for the papacy's **own emancipation**:

- The **Papal Election Decree of 1059** vested the election of the pope in the
  **cardinals**, freeing it from both the Roman factions and imperial control.
- The alliance with the **Normans** of southern Italy (the Synod of Melfi, 1059) gave
  the reform papacy a military protector (see
  [[norman-conquest-of-southern-italy]]).
- Under [[gregory-vii|Gregory VII]] the papacy asserted **supremacy over secular
  rulers** (the [[dictatus-papae|*Dictatus Papae*]], 1075), precipitating the
  **[[investiture-contest|Investiture Contest]]** that was settled, in the papacy's
  favour on the essentials, by the **Concordat of Worms (1122)**.

## The Papal Monarchy

The reform produced the **[[papal-monarchy|papal monarchy]]**: a centralised,
law-governed sovereignty exercised through the **College of Cardinals**, papal
**legates**, the general (Lateran) **councils**, a growing **Curia**, and the
expanding body of [[ius-commune|canon law]]. By the time of Innocent III (1198–1216) the
pope was styled *vicarius Christi* and claimed the *plenitudo potestatis* over the whole
Church — the most developed governmental institution in Latin Europe and the standing
rival of emperors and kings. The breach with the Greek East (the **Schism of 1054**,
hardened after 1204) defined the limits of the Roman claim (see [[eastern-churches]]).

## Significance and Longue Durée

The eleventh-century reform made the papacy the engine of Latin Christendom's
unification and a model of centralised governance that secular monarchies emulated. Its
high-medieval apogee was followed by the conflicts with the Hohenstaufen (Cycle 8), the
thirteenth-century zenith and then the crises of the later Middle Ages (Avignon, the
Great Schism, conciliarism) treated in [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] and later
volumes — and, far beyond, the early-modern and modern papacy. The institution's
continuity across two millennia makes it one of the longest-lived in world history.
