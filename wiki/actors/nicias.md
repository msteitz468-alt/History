# Nicias

## Overview

**Nicias** (d. 413 BCE) was the leading Athenian advocate of peace and the dominant moderate figure after the deaths of Pericles and Cleon. In Kagan's account he consistently sought a negotiated settlement on Periclean terms and was the principal architect of the Peace of Nicias (421).

## Career in the Range

- **Early offensives (426–425)**: In 426 Nicias with 60 ships and 2000 hoplites attacked Melos (only Aegean island outside alliance, Spartan colony); failed to take it but joined land army at Tanagra, ravaging Boeotia and defeating Tanagrans/Thebans in pitched battle before returning via ships to raid Locris. In summer 425 with two unnamed generals, Nicias invaded Corinthian territory (80 ships, 2000 hoplites, 200 cavalry): defeated Corinthians at Solygeia (Corinthian losses 212 vs Athenian 50), ravaged Crommyon, then at Methana “Nicias walled off the narrow neck of the peninsula and left a garrison” that raided Troezen, Halieis and Epidaurus. “In the heady days after Pylos-Sphacteria everything seemed possible.”

- **Cythera campaign (424)**: As one of a “trio of moderates — Nicias, Nicostratus, and Autocles”, seized Cythera (Spartan trade base off Laconia). “With ten ships and a small corps of hoplites Nicias swiftly seized the coastal city of Scandeia”; main force took the interior town. Offered generous terms: inhabitants keep land for 4 talents annual tribute + Athenian garrison. “The fall of Cythera struck the Spartans almost as hard as had the losses of Pylos and the men on Sphacteria.”

- **Thracian command (423)**: With Niceratus, Nicias led the expedition to recover Mende and Scione. He restored democracy in Mende and blockaded Scione. He formed an alliance with Perdiccas to block Spartan reinforcements to Brasidas.

- **Peace negotiations (421)**: After the deaths of Brasidas and Cleon at Amphipolis removed the chief obstacles to peace on both sides, Nicias led the Athenian side. The fifty-year Peace of Nicias was sworn "perhaps about March 12, 421." It provided for restoration of most territories on a status quo ante bellum basis with key exceptions (Thebes kept Plataea; Athens kept Nisaea and certain western colonies). Prisoners were exchanged. Nicias was hailed as "a man dear to the gods."

- **Spartan alliance and its failure**: Nicias supported the additional fifty-year defensive alliance with Sparta, surrendering the Sphacterian prisoners as a token of good faith. He later led the embassy to Sparta demanding return of Amphipolis and Panactum intact and abandonment of the Boeotian alliance. When Sparta refused, Nicias urged delay of the Argive alliance.

- **Opposition to the Argive policy**: Nicias argued that friendship with Sparta was more valuable than with Argos and that Athens' position was already strong. He viewed the aggressive turn as risky.

## Sicilian Expedition command and disaster (415–413 BCE)

Nicias opposed the expedition but was appointed one of three generals. After Alcibiades' recall and Lamachus' death he held sole command. His caution, delays (building southern wall and Plemmyrium forts while northern wall incomplete), failure to intercept Gylippus or Gongylus, and move of the fleet/base to vulnerable Plemmyrium reversed Athenian prospects. The counterwall completed by Gylippus ended hopes of starving Syracuse.

In fall 414 he wrote the assembly letter describing the crisis but casting no blame on himself and demanding either recall or massive reinforcements: “must either recall the force that is here or reinforce it with another just as large...” (7.15). Kagan: the letter was self-serving; responsibility lay with “Nicias’ lethargic, overconfident, and careless leadership.” The Athenians sent reinforcements under Demosthenes but kept Nicias.

After the catastrophic night attack on Epipolae (2,000–2,500 dead), Demosthenes urged immediate withdrawal. Nicias refused out of fear for reputation: returning soldiers would claim “their generals had been bribed to betray them and withdraw”; he “preferred, if he must, to take his chances and meet his own death himself at the hands of the enemy” (7.48.4). He also hoped for Syracusan financial collapse and internal betrayal.

A lunar eclipse (27 August 413) led Nicias to accept the soothsayer’s order to wait “thrice nine days.” This allowed the Syracusans to block the harbor and force the final naval defeat. The land retreat ended in the slaughter at the Assinarus river (7.84). Nicias surrendered to Gylippus; both generals were executed.

## Kagan’s judgment

Kagan holds Nicias centrally responsible: his 415 “failed rhetorical trick” (exaggerating requirements to deter) created the massive force whose destruction was catastrophic; repeated operational errors allowed the revival of Syracuse; and after clear defeat he refused withdrawal “out of fear for his reputation and safety” and “seized on the lunar eclipse as a last chance to avoid the inevitable and let slip away the Athenians’ final opportunity to escape.”

## Significance

Kagan presents Nicias as sincere, cautious, pious, and committed to Pericles' strategy of fighting with determination but restraint to achieve a secure peace. His personal desire "to hand down a name to posterity as a man who had never done harm to his city" aligned with his policy. The peace bearing his name brought joy but proved fragile; its terms did not restore the full Periclean vision and left Sparta capable of future action. Nicias' influence waned as Alcibiades rose and the quadruple alliance with Argos, Elis, and Mantinea was formed.

## OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Hornblower, Murray

[[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]]:

**Peace of Nicias as Archidamian “win” (Hornblower Ch.6).** In the Archidamian War (431–421) Sparta founded Heraclea-in-Trachis (426) and Brasidas took Amphipolis (424); Cleon’s capture of over a hundred Spartiates at Pylos cancelled Brasidas’s gains politically; the Peace of Nicias (421–415) left Athens with her empire — she had “won” the Archidamian War. Sicily 415–413 and Persian-backed Spartan naval victory then reversed that verdict.

**Laurium slave capital (Murray Ch.9).** Nicias let out 1,000 mine slaves at Laurium for 10 talents/year (33% return). Mining conditions (300-foot tunnels, child porters, chained surface workers) were a true slave economy unlike most Attic sectors; Murray condemns that Nicias, profiting from child labour of this sort, “could widely be regarded as the most moral and religious man of his generation.”

## Historiography Note (from range)

Thucydides focuses on Nicias' private motives. The peace restored some security but failed to convince Sparta of Athenian invulnerability or secure the empire's integrity on Periclean principles. The Athenians were war-weary after heavy losses at Delium, in Thrace, and from plague and invasions.

## Sicilian Expedition Command (Kagan range 17001-21000)

Nicias was chosen general “against his will, because he thought that the city had made a wrong decision” (6.8.4). In second assembly he opposed with grim evaluation of situation: Spartans only waiting “for the right moment to strike,” “We must not... reach out for another empire until we have made the one we have secure” (6.10.5). Disparaged Sicilian allies as “a barbaric people.” Rebutted Syracuse threat: “The Sicilians... would be even less dangerous than they are now if ruled by the Syracusans” (6.11.3). Personal attack on Alcibiades as dangerously ambitious younger generation seeking “to endanger the state for his own glory and profit.” When arguments failed to deter, he “thought he might change their minds if he exaggerated the size of the force it required” (6.19.2), proposing 100 triremes + 5,000 hoplites (neglected to ask for cavalry despite his own prior emphasis on Syracusan horse advantage: “The thing in which the Syracusans most surpass us is in their possession of many horses...” 6.20.4). Assembly approved vast force and full powers. Kagan: “It was only after Nicias’ speech in the second of them that the Athenians turned from a cautious and limited venture to a risky, ill-conceived and unplanned, unlimited commitment... Without his intervention there could have been no chance of their embarking on a major catastrophe.”

**Early operations and first attack (415):** At Rhegium, after Rhegians and Segestans disappointed, Nicias proposed minimal plan: demand pay from Segestans, show force, arrange Selinus peace, sail home unless “some quick and unexpected way” (6.47) — “true intention was to settle matters at Segesta... and then return immediately.” Outvoted. In first Syracuse battle (after trick landing south of Anapus), Athenians won tactically but “it was a tactical victory without strategic result” due to no cavalry to pursue (“the unopposed Syracusan cavalry was able to check the pursuit”). Nicias withdrew to Catana for winter per Thuc. 6.71.2 (lateness, supplies, money, cavalry). Contemporaries: “Nicias-delays”; Plutarch: “by calculating too carefully and delaying and being overly cautious he destroyed the opportunity for action” (Nicias 16.8). Kagan defends as tactician (“Nicias’ caution... was not unreasonable”) but faults as strategist: “Cavalry was essential... Had it been available from the first, the Syracusans would have been forced to surrender; no outside help could have saved them.” “The lack of provisions for cavalry is particularly astonishing in that Nicias himself had emphasized its importance before the expedition’s departure.”

**414 Siege and Gylippus:** With cavalry arrived, Athenians seized Epipolae, built Circle. Nicias (now ill with kidney ailment) participated in raids destroying counterwall. During southern wall construction in marsh, Lamachus died; Nicias “alone in command.” Near completing enclosure when Gylippus arrived. “Nicias became careless and overconfident, ignoring the one distant cloud... the four ships coming from the Peloponnesus.” Failed to intercept, complete northern wall, guard Euryalus, or blockade properly: “Nicias did none of these things, with disastrous results.” Gylippus seized Labdalum and began counterwall. “By now, however, Nicias had abandoned any plans of conquering Syracuse. Sick and in pain... he worried chiefly about the safety of his forces and their escape.” Chose Plemmyrium for naval base: “the crews began to deteriorate at that time” (7.4.6); divided forces dangerously. Kagan: the Plemmyrium move and incomplete northern wall squandered resources; “the chief cause of the expedition’s failure” traceable to Nicias’ initial failure to provide cavalry despite his warnings.

Nicias “never wanted to attack Sicily, and forced to take part in the campaign, intended to pursue a minimal course that would avoid any serious engagement.” “Perhaps the oversight was more a failure of purpose than of judgment.”
