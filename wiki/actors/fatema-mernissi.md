## Overview

Fatema Mernissi (1940–2015) was a Moroccan sociologist and philosopher, professor at Mohammed V University in Rabat, and one of the most influential feminist intellectuals in the Islamic world. Born in Fez, she produced several pioneering works reexamining the position of women in Islam through a combination of sociological analysis and rigorous ḥadīth-critical scholarship. Her most famous works are *Beyond the Veil: Male-Female Dynamics in Modern Muslim Society* (1973) and *Women and Islam: A Historical and Theological Enquiry* (1991).

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Ch. 60.

## Core Arguments

### Beyond the Veil (1973)

Mernissi's central thesis inverts the standard Western feminist analysis of Islamic gender oppression. Western thought (exemplified by Freud) depicts women as biologically and psychologically passive, weak, lacking. Islamic thought (exemplified by al-Ghazālī's depictions of women as sexually active and demanding) depicts women as the *stronger* sex — sexually powerful and potentially destabilizing. Hence the practice of veiling. For Mernissi, veiling exists not to protect women or hide their inferiority, but to protect men — the weaker sex — from their own uncontrollable sexual desires. The logic: "God, as al-Ghazālī describes him, created women as a source of pleasure but also a source of destabilizing fitna (strife); veiling is a social mechanism to manage men's passions, not to express women's subordination."

She connects this to the broader Islamic Platonist framework: virtue requires reason to rule desire; women are associated with the provocation of untamed desire; men must control their desires for women, hence controlling women themselves — "rather than showing them love and respect" (p. 110).

The historical argument: pre-Islamic (*jāhiliyya*) society was tribal and gave women relative self-determination. Early Islam eroded this through rules on divorce, veiling, and spatial restriction. Modernity, in breaking down traditional structures, has allowed women to reclaim public roles.

### Women and Islam (1991)

Mernissi applies classical Islamic *ḥadīth*-critical methods to challenge the canonical anti-feminist traditions. Her most detailed case concerns the saying attributed to the Prophet — "those who entrust their affairs to a woman will never know prosperity" — used across centuries to exclude women from political and economic leadership (she was quoted this saying in her local grocery store).

Mernissi traces the report to its first transmitter, **Abū Bakra**, and discovers that he was once convicted of bearing false testimony. Classical ḥadīth scholars authenticated the report despite this, she argues, because of their sexist assumptions rather than evidentiary warrant. Conclusion: this is a weak hadith that cannot bear the weight placed on it.

She applies the same method to the "verse of the ḥijāb" (Koran 33:53), arguing its specific revelatory occasion (guests overstaying at the Prophet's wedding) shows that it was a lesson in tactfulness, not a general commandment for female veiling.

Her program: recover the democratic and egalitarian core of the Islamic revelation — including a verse revealed in direct response to women's demand for equality (Koran 33:35) — from the patriarchal distortions introduced almost immediately after the Prophet's death, beginning with the first caliph ʿUmar.

## Paradoxical Structural Position

Adamson notes a paradox: Mernissi, like the conservative salafist Ibn Taymiyya (and like [[muhammad-abduh]] in his own way), urges a return to the original, authentic teaching of Islam against later distortions. Her reform program is methodologically conservative even when its conclusions are liberal: she uses classical ḥadīth-critical methods, treats the Koran as authoritative, and frames feminist conclusions as recoveries of Muḥammad's true intention.

---

## Significance

Mernissi is one of the few modern Muslim thinkers discussed by Adamson whose primary concern is gender rather than metaphysics or epistemology. Her work demonstrates that the Islamic intellectual tradition has internal resources for feminist argument — not only the import of European feminism — and that rigorous application of the tradition's own critical methods can challenge rather than reinforce patriarchal structures.

She is also notable for her bicultural position: educated in both Moroccan traditional contexts and European social science (she drew on Freud in order to argue *against* him), she exemplifies the complex negotiations of Islamic intellectuals in the postcolonial world.
