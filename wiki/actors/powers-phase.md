## Overview

The **Powers Phase** is Bruce D. Smith’s small-scale case study of a Mississippian agricultural [[chiefdom]] polity (CHNPA I.1, ch. 5) — the opposite end of the size spectrum from [[cahokia]]. Distributed across **less than 25 km²** in southeastern Missouri (western edge of the lower Mississippi Valley, along the Ozark escarpment), its mound center (**Powers Fort**), villages, and farmsteads clearly fall toward the lower end of Mississippian scale. Smith stresses that **Powers-scale polities constituted a sizable majority** of the Mississippian world; Cahokia is the outlier, not the type.

## Territory and Environment

Well-defined territory of six low, well-drained interfluve **sand-ridge “islands”** in swamp, bounded by Ozark uplands and swamps. Maize was the primary crop, plus beans, sunflower, marsh elder, chenopod, and erect knotweed; fish/waterfowl and oak-hickory fauna. Escarpment-edge placement near the Little Black River entrance sat on a NE–SW exchange route moving Mill Creek chert hoes and galena. Date focus ~**A.D. 1300**.

## Powers Fort (civic-ceremonial center)

~**5 ha**: cypress swamp on the east; fortification wall and ditch on the other three sides; central plaza with a large rectangular mound on the north and a row of three smaller mounds on the west. The large mound, excavated in the 1880s, yielded structure evidence and exotic painted/polished/engraved ceramics. Residential areas NW/W/S of the smaller mounds show superimposed rebuilds. **High-status burials have yet to be discovered** at the mound center.

## Villages

Ten Powers Phase villages identified to date — **all burned**; none on the Powers Fort ridge (villages occupy the other five ridges, 1–4 each). Villages ~40–90+ structures.

- **Turner**: ~0.6 ha; fortification ditch/palisade; 44 rectangular structures in six NE–SW rows around a west-side plaza; cemetery under plaza in orderly rows; plaza-edge corporate structures including a granary with stacked maize cobs; little clear clan-level subdivision.
- **Snodgrass**: 1.0 ha; 94 structures in seven rows; fortification; west plaza with corporate buildings; no cemetery (few scattered burials); an internal **white clay wall** separates 37 “inner” structures from >50 smaller “outer” structures with smaller artifact assemblages. Turner is comparable in size/plan to Snodgrass’s white-wall interior.

Lack of rebuilding at Turner, Snodgrass, Flurry, and Wilborn plus limited refuse suggests village occupation **≤10 years (perhaps <5)** before burning and abandonment — a “slice in time,” though contemporaneity among villages is hard to establish.

## Farmsteads

>100 farmsteads. **Gypsy Joint** fully excavated: dual warm-weather single-post and cold-weather wall-trench houses for one extended family of ~7–10 for ~1–3 years — matches ethnohistoric southeastern hot/cold house pairing.

## Socio-Political Model

Smith: ridge-based discrete social segments; village plazas integrate each ridge plus outlying farmsteads; Powers Fort integrates the full polity. Status differentiation at village level mainly **age/gender**, not ascribed rank (grave goods and placement); Powers Fort ridge likely paramount. This is midrange [[chiefdom]] organization at small absolute scale — not state-level (see [[north-american-states-debate]] for the general Mississippian debate, focused on Cahokia).

## Significance

Powers Phase shows what most Mississippian societies looked like: self-sufficient levee/ridge agriculture, short-lived fortified villages, a modest multi-mound center, limited mortuary ranking, and integration by kinship and plaza ceremony rather than imperial administration. Smith explicitly compares Cahokia’s **basic organization** to Powers-scale societies that persisted into the sixteenth century elsewhere.

## Historiography

Primary source for this page: Bruce D. Smith, “Agricultural Chiefdoms of the Eastern Woodlands,” in [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-1]]. Site-level literature on Powers Fort, Snodgrass, Turner, and Gypsy Joint underpins Smith’s synthesis; high-status burial absence at the center is an evidentiary gap he notes explicitly.
