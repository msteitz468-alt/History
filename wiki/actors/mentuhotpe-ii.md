# Nebhepetre Mentuhotpe II

The Theban king who **reunified Egypt** (~2040 BCE), ended the
[[first-intermediate-period|First Intermediate Period]], and founded the
[[middle-kingdom-egypt|Middle Kingdom]] — remembered by the Egyptians, alongside
**Menes** and **Amosis**, as one of the three great founders of their history.
Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XX, W. C. Hayes).

## The reunifier

A king of the Theban Eleventh Dynasty, Mentuhotpe II inherited a southern kingdom
and, in a long reign of 51 years, completed the conquest of the north — see
[[reunification-of-egypt-2040bce|the reunification of Egypt]]. His evolving
titulary tracks the achievement: from **Sankhibtowy** ("Making-the-Heart-of-the-
Two-Lands-Live") through **Netjeryhedjet** to the triumphant **Sematawy**, "Uniter
of the Two Lands."

## Re-centralizing the state

Mentuhotpe rebuilt central authority without (yet) abolishing the nomarchs. His
method was to **plant Thebans in every key office** — successive Theban viziers,
the new post of Chancellor, a Governor of Lower Egypt — so that "from Wawat to
Sinai the land was under the constant supervision of native-born or naturalized
Thebans." The irreconcilable nomarchs of **Asyut** (the old Heracleopolitan
champions) were removed by force; the families of **Hermopolis** and **Beni
Hasan** were conciliated and left in place. The result was a national unity
immediately reflected in renewed prosperity and a return to monumental art.

## Foreign policy: Nubia, the deserts, the Red Sea

He resumed the **Old Kingdom programme**: defensive raids against Libyans and the
Asiatic *Amu* of Sinai and the eastern desert, and a renewed push into **Lower
Nubia (Wawat)**, then an independent kingdom — levying tribute (chiefly gold),
employing Nubian (Medjay) mercenaries like the graffiti-writer **Tjehemau**, and
re-opening the desert routes. He began reopening the **Koptos–Red Sea road** toward
the incense-land of **Punt**, completed under his son.

## The Deir el-Bahri monument

His most famous work is his terraced **tomb-temple at Deir el-Bahri** in western
Thebes — a colonnaded masonry terrace surmounted (in the usual reconstruction) by a
small pyramid, set in a great bay of cliffs, with shrines for royal women and the
rock-tombs of his officials around it. It was the architectural prototype that, five
centuries later, inspired **Hatshepsut's** adjacent temple on the same site.

## Shaw (2000): the god-king and the rise of Amun

Gae Callender's chapter adds the **religious** dimension. The Deir el-Bahri complex was the
first royal monument to stress **Osirian** belief, reflecting the
[[processes/democratization-of-afterlife|religious "levelling"]] between royal and commoner
funerary cults that had taken place in the First Intermediate Period — and the evidence shows
Mentuhotpe "intended to be worshipped as a god in his House of Millions of Years, thus
pre-dating by hundreds of years ideas that became a central religious preoccupation of the
New Kingdom" (an unusually explicit early case of living-king deification; see
[[concepts/divine-kingship]]). His self-presentation drew on Hathor, Amun, and the
ithyphallic Min. Reuniting the land let him recall **Memphite craftsmen**, explaining "the
sudden reappearance of a level of expertise that had not been attested since the pyramids of
the Old Kingdom." Crucially, the **cult of Amun** at [[places/thebes|Thebes]] "began to grow
… from this time onwards" — Deir el-Bahri aligning across the river with Karnak for Amun's
annual Festival of the Valley.

## Counterfactual significance

Mentuhotpe is the man who proved that Egypt's unity could be **restored** after a
collapse — establishing the template (intermediate period → reunification under a
new dynasty) that would recur twice more. His re-centralization through a loyal
official class, rather than through the old hereditary nobility, pointed toward the
fully bureaucratic monarchy that [[senwosret-iii|Senwosret III]] would complete.

## Historiography

Known from monuments, his evolving titulary, officials' tomb biographies, and
Nubian graffiti rather than from any narrative source; the regnal-year framework of
his wars is reconstructed and the dates are conventional and approximate. Some
attributions (which courtyard tomb, which "Mentuhotpe") remain debated. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
