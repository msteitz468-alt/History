# Joseph Stalin

Soviet leader (1924–1953). Consolidated power through purges and terror; industrialized via Five-Year Plans at enormous human cost; signed pact with Hitler then led USSR through the Great Patriotic War to victory; imposed Soviet control over Eastern Europe after 1945, initiating the Cold War. One of the most consequential and brutal figures of the 20th century.

See: [[world-war-ii-1939-1945]], [[operation-barbarossa-1941]], [[battle-of-stalingrad-1942]], [[cold-war]].

## Early Life and Rise to the Party Secretaryship

Born Josef Djugashvili in Georgia, the son of a shoemaker and grandson of serfs, Stalin was — per Paxton and Hessler — "perhaps the only Old Bolshevik of truly lower-class origin." Most of his eventual rivals and colleagues among the Bolshevik leadership had spent years in Western European exile absorbing a cosmopolitan Marxist culture; Stalin's formation was markedly narrower and more provincial by comparison. He dropped out of an Orthodox theological seminary, joined the Bolshevik movement around 1900, and spent the following two decades in clandestine party work, including bank robberies used to fund party operations — activity that earned him the underground alias "man of steel" (Stalin). Unlike Trotsky, whose authority rested on revolutionary and military credentials from 1917, Stalin's rise to supremacy rested on a specific institutional position: he had held the post of Party Secretary since 1922, and used the growing administrative ascendancy of the Politburo and its Secretariat after Lenin's 1924 death to accumulate personal power through appointments and patronage rather than through revolutionary stature ([[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]]; see [[new-economic-policy]] for the industrialization-debate mechanics of his subsequent break with the Right).

## The Great Break, Collectivization, and the Path to Terror

Hessler frames the years 1928–32 as a second "revolution from above," Stalin's structural counterpart to the contemporaneous Nazi seizure of power in scale if not in ideology. Confronted with the New Economic Policy's chronic "goods famine" — peasants withholding grain from a market offering little worth trading for — Stalin broke with Bukharin and the Right in October 1928 and adopted the Left Opposition's own program of forced industrialization and agricultural extraction, even as he purged the Left's leadership from the party. He personally revised industrialization targets sharply upward through 1929 and drove collectivization's most coercive phase, including the shooting or camping of an estimated 60,000 "especially dangerous" kulaks (a group representing only 3–5% of all farmers) and the collectivization of roughly 10 million peasant families — near half of Soviet households — by March 1930. His own "Dizzy with Success" article in Pravda that spring, ostensibly rebuking overzealous local officials, only briefly slowed a campaign whose direction he never abandoned. He later told Churchill that the resulting 1932–33 famine killed some 10 million peasants, a figure Paxton and Hessler's editors note is "roughly in line with current estimates." See [[soviet-collectivization]] for full mechanics, and [[great-purge]] for the subsequent Great Terror, in which Stalin's signature appears on tens of thousands of execution lists and the Central Committee lost 98 of its 139 members.
