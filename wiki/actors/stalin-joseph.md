# Joseph Stalin
## Biography / Description
Joseph Dzhugashvili, later known as Joseph Stalin, was born in 1879 in the small Georgian town of Gori near Tiflis (Tbilisi) to a humble shoemaker's family. He attended a Church school in Gori and then a theological seminary in Tiflis before being expelled in 1899 for revolutionary activities. He joined the Social Democrats and sided with the Bolsheviks in 1903. Between 1902 and 1913, he was arrested and exiled several times, repeatedly escaping. He was appointed General Secretary of the Bolshevik Party in 1922. Following Lenin's death in 1924, Stalin consolidated control over the party machinery, defeating rivals like Trotsky, Zinoviev, and Bukharin. He established an absolute personal dictatorship from 1928, leading the Soviet Union through industrialization, World War II, and the early years of the Cold War until his death on March 5, 1953.

From [[cambridge-history-of-russia-v3-2006|Cambridge History of Russia Vol. III]] (Suny intro, lines 1567–1575): 'Once Stalin had achieved pre-eminence by the end of the 1920s, he launched a second “revolution”, this one from above, initiated by the party/state itself.' 'The ruling apparatus of Stalin loyalists nationalised totally what was left of the autonomous economy and expanded police terror to unprecedented dimensions. The new Stalinist system that metastasised out of Leninism resurrected the leather-jacket Bolshevism of the civil war and violently imposed collectivised agriculture on the peasant majority, pell-mell industrialisation on workers and a cultural straitjacket on the intelligentsia. Far more repressive than Lenin had been, Stalinist state domination of every aspect of social life transformed the Soviet continent from a backward peasant country into a poorly industrialised and urban one.' 'The Stalinist years were marked by deep contradictions: visible progress in industry accompanied by devastation and stagnation in agriculture; a police regime that saw enemies everywhere at a time when millions energetically and enthusiastically worked to build their idea of socialism; cultural revival and massive expansion of literacy and education coinciding with a cloud of censorship... and the adoption of the “most democratic constitution in the world” while real freedoms and political participation evaporated into memories.'

Stalin’s early life had been hard. Born to a Georgian couple... his real name was Iosif Dzhugashvili. His birthday was given out officially as 21 December 1879; but the parish records indicate that he entered this world a year earlier... Iosif’s father was a child-beating drunkard who died leaving the family penniless... He quickly picked up the Russian language... After being expelled from the seminary, he wandered over the Transcaucasus... he sided with the Bolsheviks... Young Dzhugashvili, whose pseudonym was first Koba and then Stalin (or ‘Man of Steel’), reacted positively to themes of dictatorship, terror, modernity, progress and leadership in Lenin’s writings (Service, lines 1557–1559).

He was crude and brutal even by Bolshevik standards... On the Southern front in 1918 he had put villages to the torch... but for Lenin’s intervention would have drowned scores of innocent former Imperial Army officers on a prison barge moored on the river Volga (line 1565). His lack of intellectual sophistication did not mean that he was unmotivated by ideas... he was conscious enough of the gaps in his education to take on Jan Sten as a private tutor in philosophy in the 1920s. He was also a voracious reader, supposedly getting through a daily quota of 500 pages (lines 1567). That this maladjusted character, whose mistrustfulness was close to paranoia, should have won the struggle to succeed Lenin boded ill for his opponents (line 1569). He also had a craving for adulation... The cult of Stalin was also a response to the underlying requirements of the regime... The tsar-like image of Stalin was useful in affirming that the state possessed a strong, determined leader (line 1573). Full regal pomp was nevertheless eschewed... he always made sure to have his photograph taken with groups of delegates. The display of ordinariness was a basic aspect of his mystique (line 1575).

## Chronology (from Freeze ed. 2002)
- 1929: Stalin calls for mass collectivization and liquidation of kulaks (27 December).
- 1930: Mass collectivization launched (5 January).
- 1932–3: Famine in Ukraine and elsewhere.
- 1934: Assassination of Sergei Kirov (December).
- 1936: Show trial of Zinoviev, Kamenev, and others (August); Ezhov appointed head of NKVD (September); promulgation of Stalin Constitution (December).
- 1937: Execution of Marshal Tukhachevskii and Red Army officers (June); height of ‘Great Terror’ (to late 1938).
- 1938: Beria succeeds Ezhov as head of NKVD (December).
- 1939: Nazi–Soviet pact (August).
- 1953: Death of Stalin (5 March).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
- **Socialism in One Country and First Five-Year Plan:** Stalin formulated the thesis... The large factories and mines... Thirty-eight per cent of industrial capital stock by the end of 1934 was located in factories built in the previous half-dozen years... Mines and factories were claimed to have doubled their production since 1928. This was exaggeration. Yet even sceptical estimates put the annual expansion in industrial output at ten per cent between 1928 and 1941 (lines 1474).
- **Forced Collectivization (1929–1933):** Stalin ordered the rapid and coercive merging... Among Stalin’s several motives... to cause maximum discomfort to Bukharin (line 1452). Cheap labour was made available by peasants fleeing the villages... Yanson recommended their transfer to the forced-labour camps... ‘Dalstroi’... Kolyma (line 1454). Kulaks... divided into three categories... Between five and seven million persons... ‘25,000-ers’... ‘sub-kulaks’... With monumental insincerity he wrote an article for Pravda in March 1930, ‘Dizzy with Success’... Probably four to five million people perished in 1932–3... peasants were killing their livestock... Ordzhonikidze was aghast... (lines 1460–1466). The kolkhoz was defined as occupying a lower level of socialist attainment than the sovkhoz (line 1478). Stalin and the Politburo had won the agrarian war (line 1464).
- **The Great Purge (1936–1938):** Stalin unleashed a massive reign of terror (Ezhovshchina)... On 1 December 1934... Leonid Nikolaev... shot [Kirov]. Stalin exploited the assassination as a pretext... (line 1668). In August 1936, Kamenev and Zinoviev... condemned to death (line 1686). Marshal Tukhachevski... shot in mid-June... Bukharin, Tomski and Rykov were guilty of espionage (line 1700). Yezhov... scheme for the NKVD to arrest 259,450 persons... torture was sanctioned... (line 1704). According to official central records, 681,692 persons were executed in 1937–8... Between one million and one and a half million... (line 1710). Stalin had succeeded in elevating himself above party, people’s commissariats, army... (line 1768).
- **Arrest and Interrogation Apparatus (primary from Solzhenitsyn range lines 400–2800):** The system was quota-driven and class/ideology-based from Lenin era onward: "We are not fighting against single individuals. We are exterminating the bourgeoisie as a class." (Latsis, Red Terror Nov 1918; line 783). 1929–30 kulak wave: "fifteen million peasants" driven out — "forced resettlement of a whole people, an ethnic catastrophe" (lines 761, 869). Article 58: "there is no step, thought, action, or lack of action under the heavens which could not be punished by the heavy hand of Article 58" (line 881). "Just give us a person—and we’ll create the case!" (line 1403). Torture: systematic (sleeplessness, standing, beatings, genital crushing, "swan dive," bedbugs, starvation, bright lights); "the principle of our interrogation consists further in depriving the accused of even a knowledge of the law" (line 1241). Bluecaps (NKVD): power/greed; "Any woman was yours!" (line 1453). Ideology enabled: "Ideology—that is what gives evildoing its long-sought justification..." (lines 1545–1547). "The line dividing good and evil cuts through the heart of every human being" (line 1501). Wartime waves: millions of POWs/returnees betrayed (lines 1843–1872). OSO: administrative penalties to 25 years, no appeal/face-to-face (lines 2117–2137). "In camp it will be … worse" (line 2755). Transport: red cattle cars, Stolypin cars with terrorized loading (searchlights, dogs, mallets; lines 2600–2750). Produced the "sewage disposal system" of the Archipelago as logical outcome of Bolshevik rule, not aberration.
- **Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact (1939) and Foreign Policy:** In the German elections of 1932 the communists were instructed to campaign mostly against the social-democrats: Hitler’s Nazis were to be ignored... ‘Don’t you think, Neumann, that if the nationalists come to power in Germany, they’ll be so completely preoccupied with the West that we’ll be able to build up socialism in peace?’ (line 1500). By the end of the working day a Nazi-Soviet Non-Aggression Treaty had been prepared for signature... secret protocols... (line 1913).
- **World War II Leadership (1941–1945):** At 3.15 a.m. on 22 June, the Wehrmacht crossed the Bug... under strict orders not to reply to ‘provocation’. This compounded the several grave mistakes... (line 1935). Zhukov alerted Stalin... The shock to Stalin was tremendous... Only at 6.30 a.m. did he sanction retaliation (line 1937). He delivered only nine substantial speeches... (line 1953). Order No. 270... forbade any Red Army soldier to allow himself to be taken captive... Order No. 227... ‘Not one step backwards!’ (line 1959). In November 1942 the Wehrmacht armies... encircled... 91,000 German soldiers... (line 1969). The Red Army... 100,000 tanks, 130,000 aircraft... (line 1967). The race was joined on 16 April [for Berlin], and Zhukov finished it just ahead of Konev. Hitler died by his own hand on 30 April... (line 2003).

## Historical Significance
Stalin was one of the most powerful and brutal dictators in world history. His regime transformed the Soviet Union from a backward agrarian society into an industrial and military superpower with nuclear capabilities. However, this was achieved at an extraordinary human cost, including millions of deaths from collectivization, famine, deportations, and political terror. He had engineered a second revolution; he had completed the groundwork of an economic transformation. But his victory was not yet totally secure (line 1510). Service emphasizes the "Soviet compound" — a durable system... that survived for seven decades not only through terror and force but also through a system of graduated rewards, recurrent "activation" campaigns, and the tacit acceptance that informal practices (corruption, clientelism, low effort, misinformation) were the necessary "lubricating oil". Stalin’s hyper-suspicious, imperious temperament... No one coming into frequent contact with him in the late 1930s had a chance to become disloyal: he had them killed before such thoughts could enter their heads (line 1730). Posthumously, his cult of personality was denounced by Nikita Khrushchev in 1956, initiating the process of de-Stalinization. His accomplices came into a disturbing inheritance... enormous problems had been bequeathed by him (lines 2396, 2398).

## The Gulag Archipelago and Destructive-Labor Camps (from Solzhenitsyn range lines 2801–5200; contributed_to [[soviet-industrialization]] and [[great-purge]]; produced by Stalin's policies)
From primary testimony in range, Stalin personally enabled the Archipelago as slave-labor engine. Met Frenkel (1929 airplane summons); approved differentiated rations and “squeeze... first three months” formula (line 2935, 3099–3101). Belomor (and metastasizing camps) built under his orders: “The canal is being built on the initiative and orders of Comrade Stalin!” (line 3167); quarter-million dead (bones in concrete); propaganda book (Gorky et al) falsely claimed “no one died” (lines 3141, 3207). 

Wartime: 58s held as “hostages”; second terms fabricated; Kolyma cannibalism of dead horse, 3.5 oz bread, -50F forced work (lines 3285–3299). “I’ll shoe you in tin cans, but you’re going to go out to work!” (line 3275). Katorga reintroduced as murder camps (12hr shifts, 200/tent; lines 5109–5121). Solzhenitsyn: camps inherent to the system Stalin perfected, not aberration. “the line separating good and evil passes... right through every human heart” (line 4845).
