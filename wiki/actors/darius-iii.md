# Darius III Codomannus

## Overview

**Darius III** (Artašata/Codomannus, r. c. 336–330 BCE) was the last [[achaemenid-empire|
Achaemenid Great King]], defeated by [[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] at Issus and Gaugamela
and murdered by his own kinsman Bessus during the retreat east. [[sources/briant-from-cyrus-to-alexander-2002|
Briant]]'s revisionist case is built squarely around him: the conventional portrait of a weak,
cowardly king ruling a rotten "colossus with feet of clay" is shown to be **Alexandrocentric
propaganda**, and the empire's fall is reframed as **contingent** — battlefield defeat followed
by the calculated defection of key aristocrats — not the inevitable collapse of a decadent system.

## Role and Key Events

- **Legitimate accession.** Darius was dynastically legitimate (son of Arsanes, grandson of
  Ostanes, brother of [[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes II]]) — Artaxerxes III's cousin, a member of
  the royal inner circle since at least the 350s, and probably the real head of the conspiracy
  that removed Artaxerxes IV, with the eunuch Bagoas as accomplice rather than ringleader. A
  Persian counter-tradition credits him with winning glory in single combat against a Cadusian
  champion. A Babylonian tablet of 331 still styles him "king of the world."
- **Systematic preparation.** He began assembling the army at Babylon months before he was
  forced into the field, fitted out a large fleet, and appointed Memnon to command "lower Asia
  and the whole fleet" — a deliberate two-track maritime-and-land strategy, not improvisation.
- **Issus and Gaugamela.** At Issus he fielded a "Darius phalanx"
  of 30,000 Greek mercenaries plus 60,000 Persian "Cardaces"; after the defeat he returned to
  Babylon and immediately raised a new army. For [[battle-of-gaugamela-331bce|Gaugamela]] he
  lengthened Persian swords and lances, leveled the battlefield near Arbela, and deployed
  scythed chariots and elephants. The decisive event was less the battle than the subsequent
  surrender of Babylon by the satrap Mazaeus — contrary to Darius's orders.
- **Death (330).** Retreating toward Ecbatana to rebuild from Bactrian resources ("preparing
  for battle rather than for flight," per Curtius), he was seized and killed by Bessus,
  Nabarzanes, and Barsaentes, who hoped to bargain him to Alexander and preserve their power
  in the east. Bessus then proclaimed himself **Artaxerxes V** — proof the Achaemenid framework
  still carried legitimating force.

## Counterfactual Significance

Briant insists the defeats "must not be considered either proof, or even a significant
indication, of military disarray." Darius was an energetic, competent commander; had a battle
gone otherwise, or had the great satraps not defected, the structural condition of the empire
gave no reason for collapse. The fall turned on personal calculation by the aristocracy, who —
exactly as their ancestors had under [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]] — negotiated with the victor to
preserve their dominance.

## Historiography

The hostile portrait derives from Arrian (channeling Xenophon's anti-Persian tropes) and the
broader Alexander historians, whom Briant nonetheless mines as an "Achaemenid source" when read
against the longue durée. The Babylonian Dynastic Prophecy (Seleucid-era) remembers Darius as a
"good king" — evidence that subject elites did not experience Achaemenid rule as oppression and
were ambivalent about its Macedonian successor.
