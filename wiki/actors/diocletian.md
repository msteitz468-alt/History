# Diocletian

**Gaius Aurelius Valerius Diocletianus** (Diocles; b. 243/245, r. 284–305, d. 311) was the
Dalmatian guards-officer whose twenty-year reign **refounded the Roman state** after the
[[third-century-crisis|crisis]], traditionally marking the transition from the "military anarchy" to
the **"dominate"** of the later empire. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 3 (**Alan
Bowman**).

## Accession and the tetrarchy

Proclaimed at Nicomedia (20 Nov. 284) after the murder of Numerian, Diocletian secured sole power by
defeating **Carinus** at the river Margus (285). He then progressively built a system of **shared
rule** (the [[roman-tetrarchy|tetrarchy]]):

- **285/6** — appointed **Maximian** co-ruler (Caesar then Augustus), taking for himself the epithet
  ***Iovius*** (of Jupiter) and giving Maximian ***Herculius*** (of Hercules).
- **293** — created two junior **Caesars**, **Constantius** (under Maximian) and **Galerius** (under
  himself) — the **first tetrarchy**: two Augusti and two Caesars, bound by marriage and adoption.

Bowman emphasizes the **debate over intention**: was this a master-plan unfolded in stages, or a
series of **ad hoc responses** to immediate need? He favors the latter, gradualist view.

## Reforms

Over two decades Diocletian and his colleagues reorganized the empire:

- **Administration**: provinces multiplied and grouped into twelve **dioceses** under *vicarii*; the
  separation of civil and military commands; an enlarged bureaucracy.
- **Army**: enlarged and reorganized for frontier defense.
- **Fiscal**: a new **taxation system** based on units of land and persons (*iuga* and *capita*),
  promulgated in Egypt in 297 (and provoking the **revolt of Domitius Domitianus**).
- **Currency**: two reforms (c. 294 and 301) of remonetization, the second paired with the famous
  **Edict on Maximum Prices** (301) — the first great attempt to regulate prices empire-wide (only
  ever enforced in the East, and largely ineffective).
- **Romanitas**: a deeply traditional, moralizing legislative tenor (the Edict on Marriages, the
  Epistle against the Manichees).

He campaigned on the Danube and against the **[[sasanian-empire|Persians]]** (Galerius' victory over
**Narses**, 297/8, won a favorable peace and a trans-Tigris zone of influence) and suppressed the
revolt in **Egypt** (the siege of Alexandria, 298).

## The Great Persecution and abdication

In **303** Diocletian launched the **[[great-persecution-303|Great Persecution]]** of Christians (a
series of edicts razing churches, burning books, and demanding sacrifice), the empire's last and
fiercest. In a unique act, he **abdicated** on **1 May 305** — compelling Maximian to do the same —
and retired to his palace at **Split** ("to cultivate cabbages"), refusing (but for the 308 conference
at Carnuntum) to re-enter politics. He died in 311 or 312.

## Significance and historiography

Bowman's revisionist conclusion: the Diocletianic transformation was effected through a **"judicious
blend of conservatism and reaction to pressing problems,"** not a prescient master-plan, and the
supposed descent into "oriental despotism" is largely a distortion of the rhetorical sources
(Lactantius, the panegyrics). There was **no foreshadowing** of the two great developments to come —
the toleration of Christianity and the foundation of Constantinople. Yet there is no doubt that the
**foundations of the later Roman empire** were securely laid in his reign. His failure to make the
**succession** work, however, plunged the empire back into civil war within a year of his
abdication — the wars from which [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]] would emerge.

## OHCW (Chadwick; Matthews)

**Henry Chadwick** (Envoi): third-century crisis (trade recession from ~200, falling birth-rate talk, defeats by Persians/Goths, civil wars, inflation from ~250) nearly disintegrated imperial government; averted by Diocletian (284–306, d.316 at Split) then Constantine. Diocletian fixed prices (drove goods off market), divided provinces (raised civil-service costs), created an inspectorate that became corrupt secret police, copied Persian court ceremonial with veils/eunuchs. **Matthews** (Ch. 31): Price Edict of 301 as key commercial evidence for the low-liquidity agrarian empire. Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]].

## Freeman, *Egypt, Greece, and Rome* (2014) — "climax of the Roman state"

Charles Freeman ([[freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014]]) cites **Peter Brown**'s
reappraisal (*The World of Late Antiquity*) directly against a decline narrative:
"Far from being a melancholy epilogue to the classical Roman empire... the first
half of the fourth century witnessed the long-prepared climax of the Roman state."
Freeman gives precise tetrarchic geography — Diocletian (east), Maximian
(Italy/Africa), Constantius (Britain/Gaul), Galerius (Danube), with new capitals at
Trier, Milan, Sirmium, and Nicomedia leaving Rome a relegated backwater — and notes
the new *iuga* tax-assessment units were based on land's production potential
(not mere extent) on a 5-year reassessment cycle, the first time a Roman "budget"
could be planned. Concrete Edict on Maximum Prices (301) examples: a sewer-cleaner
was capped at 25 denarii/day, a scribe at 25 denarii per 100 lines, a rhetoric
teacher at 250 denarii per pupil; Lactantius records hoarding and price rises
despite the death penalty for violators. Diocletian's army is estimated at
350,000–400,000, with legionary forts reduced from roughly 5,000-man to 1,000-man
garrisons, implying reorganization into smaller, more numerous units.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]] makes Diocletian the architect of the late empire’s dual/tetrarchic constitution and, later, of the Great Persecution — a statesman of “profound dissimulation,” not a heroic warrior. Claims are **Gibbon’s**.

- **Obscure origin, illustrious reign:** “As the reign of Diocletian was more illustrious than that of any of his predecessors, so was his birth more abject and obscure” (parents slaves of Anulinus); valor adequate but not heroically daring; ambition colored as public utility.
- **Maximian as instrument:** Maximian executed cruelties Diocletian “might at once suggest and disclaim”; titles Jovius/Herculius and the golden-vs-iron-age comparison after bloodshed.
- **Persian settlement of Nisibis:** moderate policy (against Galerius’s wish to annex Persia as a province) secured Mesopotamia, five trans-Tigris provinces, restored Tiridates, and ~forty years’ eastern calm — “Mesopotomia, the object of so many wars, was ceded to the empire.”
- **Frontier policy:** camps from Egypt to Persia; Rhine–Danube refortified; barbarians wasted each other so that “whosoever vanquished, they vanquished the enemies of Rome.”
- **Abdication and Salona “cabbages”:** first memorable resignation (1 May near Nicomedia; Maximian at Milan); refused recall by observing that if he showed Maximian the cabbages planted with his own hands at Salona he would not be urged back to power.
- **Eighteen years’ toleration, then persecution:** system “breathed the mildest and most liberal spirit of religious toleration” for more than eighteen years; Galerius forced the crisis; Terminalia (23 Feb) chosen “to set bounds to the progress of Christianity”; first edict demolishing churches, burning scriptures, outlawing assemblies.
- **Traditors:** many bishops/presbyters “acquired… the opprobrious epithet of Traditors” by surrendering books — seed of African discord.
- **Memory abhorred by Constantine:** Nicomedia’s strategic capital rejected because “the memory of Diocletian was justly abhorred by the protector of the church.”

## Woolf, Rome: An Empire's Story (2012)

From [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]] Chs. XIII–XVI.

- **Not a revolution.** 235–284 “Anarchy” inflicted huge institutional cost; Diocletian needed new coinage, taxation, administration, military system—yet “the late Roman Empire was not created in a revolution.” New ideal of emperor as soldier with spectacular ceremonial and ferocious justice had already emerged; changes were “cumulation of pragmatic expedients” focused on the army, building on Gallienus/Aurelian.
- **Career and tetrarchy.** Bodyguard commander → army-hailed Augustus; defeated Carinus 285; Maximian co-Augustus; 293 Galerius and Constantius as Caesars; joint abdication 305. Only real innovation—two parallel dynasties renewed by periodic adoptions—was the only component that failed. Collegiate rule reduced usurpations but risked policy incoherence.
- **Currency and prices.** Devised new currency (modified by Constantine) that “worked”; edict on maximum prices unenforceable. Net experimental package created effective fourth-century empire.
- **Great Persecution.** With Galerius (303): military purges, sacred texts destroyed, churches attacked; year before, Manichaeism condemned. Woolf: general persecution an “accident” of post-Caracalla empire-wide religious authority meeting exclusive monotheists. Edicts of Toleration Galerius 311, Milan 313.
- **Quotes:** “These changes were not the implementation of a grand plan, but the cumulation of pragmatic expedients.” / “General persecution, in other words, was an accident.”

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Attributed claims from [[sources/mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015|Stephen Mitchell, *A History of the Later Roman Empire* (2015)]].

- **Accession narrative as apologetic usurpation story.** Fourth-century Latin accounts (reluctant nominee, divine appeal, army acclamation, Aper as Numerian’s assassin) form an authorized version “almost wholly apologetic for the new regime” and “full of implausibilities.” Mitchell’s reading acquits Aper of conspiracy and identifies him as the senior officer loyal to the house of Carus, killed by the rival officer faction that promoted Diocletian; the attack on Carinus then followed usurpation logic, while blaming Carinus’s own officers diverted attention from the usurper.
- **Religious reinvention and persecution.** Diocletian’s restoration rested on “a systematic re-invention of Roman religion” around Jupiter as supreme deity, Hercules as imperial emblem, and Olympian attributes for the emperors — a structure easier to reconcile with later Christian monotheism than Aurelian’s Sol Invictus. The same spirit produced the February 303 renewal of Christian persecution (with 304/5 universal sacrifice orders) and furious anti-Manichee legislation equating [[manichaeism|Manichaeism]] with a Persian threat. Tetrarchic high-priest provincial structures were later imitated by [[julian|Julian]] without the persecution weapon. Related: [[great-persecution-303|Great Persecution]].
- **Fiscal-military systems builder.** Capita and iugera levies were “consolidated and rationalized into a single system… iugatio-capitatio” at the start of the late Roman period; fortresses and *limitanei* with *duces* lined the *limes* from Egypt to Persia (per Malalas). Building politics included Rome’s Curia rebuild, Diocletianic baths, Forum columnar monument and secular games; rare late-imperial attention to Alexandria and Upper Egypt.
