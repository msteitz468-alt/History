## Role and Background

Leo Szilard, a Hungarian-born physicist who emigrated first to Germany and then, after 1933, to Britain and the United States, conceived the idea of a nuclear chain reaction in 1933 and patented the concept in 1934, years before nuclear fission itself was discovered.

## Einstein-Szilard Letter and the Manhattan Project

Alarmed by the prospect of German nuclear weapons research after the 1938 discovery of fission, Szilard drafted the 1939 letter that he brought to [[actors/einstein-albert|Albert Einstein]] for signature, urging President Roosevelt to support American nuclear research — the [[events/einstein-szilard-letter-1939|Einstein-Szilard letter]] that helped launch what became the Manhattan Project. Szilard worked on the project himself, notably helping build the first self-sustaining chain reaction under Enrico Fermi at Chicago in 1942, but grew increasingly troubled by the weapon's implications, circulating a petition urging that the bomb not be used on Japanese cities without warning.

## Later Career

After the war Szilard became a prominent advocate against nuclear arms proliferation and shifted his own research focus to molecular biology, making significant contributions to that emerging field before his death in 1964.
