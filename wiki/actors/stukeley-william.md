# William Stukeley

English antiquary, physician, and Anglican clergyman (1687–1765), the single most influential figure in making the Druids central to the English national imagination. Through his fieldwork and publications — above all *Stonehenge, a Temple Restor'd to the British Druids* (1740) and *Abury* (1743) — Stukeley persuaded the English both that the Druids were ancestors worth embracing and that they had built Britain's most spectacular prehistoric monuments, including [[places/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] and [[places/avebury|Avebury]]. He fused careful field survey with a theological project casting the Druids as a near-Christian "patriarchal" priesthood.

In Hutton's account he is one of three pivotal catalysts of the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]] (alongside [[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]] and [[actors/reid-george-watson|George Watson MacGregor Reid]]) — a figure of genuine learning whose imaginative reconstruction shaped two centuries of belief about Druids and megaliths.

## Significance

### Fieldwork and Publications

Stukeley conducted the first systematic field survey of [[places/stonehenge|Stonehenge]] and [[places/avebury|Avebury]], publishing *Stonehenge, a Temple Restor'd to the British Druids* (1740) and *Abury* (1743). These volumes established the canonical association of British megalithic monuments with the Druids and argued for a "patriarchal" Druidic theology — a pre-Christian religion that had anticipated, and therefore legitimized, the Church of England. In the words of [[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton]], Stukeley "made [Druidry] the forerunner of the established Church." His fieldwork methodology combined measured survey with an interpretive framework that cast the Druids as learned priests holding a near-Christian monotheism derived from the patriarchs of the Old Testament. On the question of the Druids and comparative religion, his speculations in this field influenced both subsequent admirers and critics.

### Social Personality and the Druid Identity

Stukeley was exceptionally "clubbable," a member in succession of the Gentlemen's Society of Spalding, the Royal Society, the Society of Antiquaries, the Freemasons, the Society of Roman Knights, the Brazen Nose Society at Stamford, and the Egyptian Society. This sociability co-existed with a personal identification with Druidry that he found impossible to share with others: though some friends "encouraged him in his self-perceived role of Druid with banter and jollity, he could find nobody to share it with him" — a frustration that partly drove his decision to seek ordination into the Church of England ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

In 1738 he obtained a home north of London in a district he and his friends the Gale brothers nicknamed "Mount Haemus," after the Greek mythological peak home of the winds, and he used the title "Druid of Mount Haemus" for the rest of his life. In his later years he hailed women friends as "Druidesses" under nicknames like "Hebe," "Miriam" and "Phoebe," reserving the more exalted title of "Archdruidess" for noblewomen. The most elaborate instance was his courtship of Princess Augusta, the widowed Princess of Wales. When Bronze Age axe-heads were found at her Kew residence, Stukeley rode over, sent a treatise explaining their Druidic date and function, and was granted thirty minutes of audience on 5 October 1754. He hailed her as "Archdruidess of Kew" under the pseudonym "Veleda" (the name of a German prophetess in Tacitus), dedicating his 1763 collection of sermons to her under that title, and sent further treatises on the ancient Druids to her at intervals until his death. A 1761 unpublished treatise was dedicated likewise to the Countess of Pomfret as "Arethusa, Archdruidess of Rollright Temple." Neither woman appears to have had more than polite interest in the designation.

### Myth of Universal Bond Membership

Later tradition, propagated by Ross Nichols and the Circle of the Universal Bond, claimed Stukeley as second chief of a continuous Druid order founded by [[actors/toland-john|John Toland]] in 1717. The claimed "Mount Haemus Grove" of the Universal Bond was derived from the correspondence between Stukeley and the Gale brothers using that nickname for his home — a piece of private banter that was transferred to Oxford in the legend. Hutton's review of the evidence is unambiguous: "none of his many surviving treatises, notes, poems, autobiographical jottings or bodies of correspondence suggest that he was part of an actual Druid society" ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]). The Universal Bond's genealogy "was assembled by splicing together two real, and disconnected, historical events to create a mythical one."

### Influence on Subsequent Writers

Stukeley's patriarchal-Druid interpretation was the dominant framework for British engagement with Druids throughout the eighteenth century. The vicar William Cooke published a 1754 book that "wholly endorsed Stukeley's view," repeating his arguments at length "leaving out the archaeological fieldwork but adding plenty of scriptural and classical quotation" and reinforcing his equation of the Church of England with the culmination of the Druidic-patriarchal tradition. In the 1790s the curate of Avebury, Charles Lucas, produced an account of the megalithic complex "based on Stukeley" augmented by Bryant's flood-mythology. Stukeley was also a friend of Lewis Morris, one of the founders of the Honourable Society of Cymmrodorion, who listed a "Druid" as one of the society's supporters in a coat of arms he designed in 1751 — an early signal of the Welsh identification with Druidry that Stukeley's work had helped to enable.

Even critics engaged with his framework: William Blake drew several details of his Druid imagery from Stukeley (notably the "serpent-formed" plan of Avebury), while systematically inverting his theological argument — where Stukeley made Druidry the forerunner of true religion, Blake made it the religion of Satan. A.L. Owen's assessment, cited in Hutton, is that "several details of Blake's picture of Druidry were taken from Stukeley, while in general it opposed Stukeley's own characterization of Druidry."

## Historiography

Hutton treats Stukeley as one of three pivotal figures in the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]] (alongside [[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]] and [[actors/reid-george-watson|George Watson MacGregor Reid]]). The core scholarly judgment is that Stukeley was a figure of genuine fieldwork competence whose imaginative-theological interpretation of his data shaped two centuries of popular belief, and whose influence was inseparable from his personal desire to inhabit, and if possible found, a living Druidic identity. The surviving mass of manuscripts, letters, notes and jottings means that his inner life is relatively legible, and Hutton uses this transparency to debunk later claims of organizational continuity. Stukeley's work established the Druid-megalith connection so firmly that its displacement required the full development of twentieth-century archaeology. His speculations on comparative mythology contributed to a tradition developed by Jacob Bryant and others in the late eighteenth century.
