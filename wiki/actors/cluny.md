# Cluny (Abbey and Congregation)

The Benedictine abbey of **Cluny**, in Burgundy, was the most influential monastic institution of the long tenth and eleventh centuries and the emblem (though, as [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]]'s Joachim Wollasch insists, not the whole) of tenth-century monastic reform.

## Foundation and the *Libertas* Charter

Cluny was founded in **909/910** by **Duke William the Pious of Aquitaine**, whose foundation charter is its decisive feature: William surrendered all proprietary rights and placed the monastery directly under the **protection of St Peter and the papacy**, free from the lordship — spiritual or temporal — of any bishop, count, or king, with the monks holding **free election of their own abbot**. This *libertas* is what Wollasch identifies as Cluny's unique characteristic: "with the exception of Cluny," other tenth-century reformed houses were reformed *by* their proprietors (a bishop, a king, a margrave); Cluny alone reformed itself and others **on its own initiative**, free of all *dominatio*.

## The *Ecclesia Cluniacensis* — a Way of Life, Not an Order

Cluny gathered around itself a growing body of dependent and affiliated monasteries. But Wollasch and Reuter stress that this was **not a religious "order"** in the later (twelfth-century and after) legal sense. The ***ordo Cluniacensis*** was Cluny's *consuetudo* — its **way of life** (customary observance, especially an immense liturgical round and the **commemoration of the dead**) — not a constitutionally defined corporate body with a chain of command. To project the centralised Cistercian or mendicant model back onto tenth-century Cluny is an anachronism the volume warns against.

## The Abbots

Cluny's exceptional stability and influence rested on a series of unusually long-lived and able abbots:

- **Berno** (c. 910–926), first abbot, who brought the reform of Baume.
- **Odo** (926–942), under whom Cluny "became a centre of reform"; he carried reform to monasteries in Aquitaine, Burgundy, and Italy (Fleury, and houses in Rome). Odo also wrote on the dangers of the age and the imminence of Antichrist.
- **Odilo** (994–1049), who consolidated the network, instituted the feast of All Souls (commemoration of all the dead), and dealt as a peer with emperors; NCMH III shows him before Otto III seeking the *societas* of William of Saint-Bénigne.
- (Beyond the volume: Hugh the Great, 1049–1109, and the building of "Cluny III," the largest church in Christendom before St Peter's.)

## Significance

Cluny demonstrated that a monastery could be **structurally independent** of the surrounding tangle of lay and episcopal lordship — a model that fed, a century later, into the **Gregorian reform** programme of ecclesiastical *libertas* and the campaign against simony and proprietary control of the church. Cluny's vast, well-preserved archive (the *Recueil des chartes de l'abbaye de Cluny*) is, as Reuter notes, an exceptional and atypically rich documentary survival for the period.

## Caution: Reform Was Not "Spelled Cluny"

NCMH III is at pains to dismantle the older view that tenth-century reform *was* Cluny. Reform was a "dynamic and multi-faceted movement" with many independent centres — **Brogne** (Gerard of Brogne, reforming for the margraves of Flanders and the duke of Lotharingia), **[[gorze-reform|Gorze]]** near Metz (an episcopal proprietary house reformed by the bishops of Metz, the spearhead of reform in the Ottonian *Reichskirche*), **Einsiedeln**, the English **Regularis Concordia** houses, and the eremitic strand (**Romuald** of Ravenna, founder of Camaldoli). Cluny was the most famous, not the sum, of the movement.

## The Eleventh-Century Apogee (NCMH IV)

[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Giles Constable, Ch. 10) shows Cluny at its
eleventh-century **height** under Abbots Odilo and Hugh the Great: the community
quadrupled between the tenth and mid-twelfth centuries, and Cluny stood at the head of
a vast congregation — "the treasury of the entire Christian republic from which all
people draw forth." Its monks supplied the reform papacy with personnel (all popes
1073–1119 had been monks; Urban II was a Cluniac), making Cluny a seedbed of the
[[gregorian-reform|Gregorian Reform]] even as its wealth and elaborate liturgy became,
in the twelfth century, the foil against which the austere
[[cistercian-order|Cistercians]] defined themselves (the Cluniac–Cistercian polemics
of Peter the Venerable and Bernard of Clairvaux). Cluny thus stands at the hinge of
the [[medieval-monastic-revival|monastic revival]]: the culmination of the old
Benedictine model and the prompt for the new.

## From Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

[[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] dates the foundation to **909** under Duke William of Aquitaine as a reform house returning to the Rule as articulated by Benedict of Aniane; the charter provided free abbatial election and the **pope** (not local bishop or duke) as protector — underwriting exemption and Cluny’s later reforming force. Under **Mayeul/Maiolus (954–94)** priories and associates spread through France into Christian Spain and northern Italy; by the end of the tenth century Cluny was “the single most powerful spiritual force in western Europe.” Abbots **Odilo (994–1049)** and **Hugh (1049–1109)** and Cluniac popes **Urban II** and **Paschal II** mark its later height. Logan’s Cluniac–Cistercian section (via Peter the Venerable answering [[bernard-of-clairvaux|Bernard]]) frames the twelfth-century controversy over feasts vs. austerity and humility.
