# Phrygians

The Phrygians were an Indo-European people who entered west-central Anatolia from
the Balkans around the time of the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age
Collapse]], settling the plateau formerly held by the [[hittites|Hittite Empire]]
and eventually forming the Iron Age kingdom centred on Gordion. CAH II.2 (chapter
XXX) treats them as part of the broad movement of peoples that reconfigured
Anatolia after the fall of Ḫatti.

## Origins and the Mushki Question

Greek tradition (Herodotus) held that the Phrygians (*Bryges/Brygoi*) crossed into
Asia from Macedonia and Thrace. Their language, known from later inscriptions, is
Indo-European and shows affinities with Greek. CAH II.2 discusses the vexed
identification of the Phrygians with the **Mushki**, the people whose king the
Assyrian records ([[tukulti-ninurta-i|after Tukulti-Ninurta]] and especially
under Tiglath-pileser I, c. 1115 BCE) report pressing down into the upper Tigris
and Kummuh. Whether the "Mushki" of the Assyrian texts are the same as the
later Phrygians of Gordion, or a distinct eastern group, remains uncertain — the
volume records the debate without resolving it.

### The European Briges (CAH II.2, Ch. XXXVI)

Hammond's chapter on the Greek migrations supplies the **European prehistory** of the
Phrygians. He identifies the archaeological **"Lausitz" intrusion** into Macedonia and
northern Epirus (c. 1200–1080 BCE — Knobbed Ware / *Buckelkeramik*, cremation,
spiral-decorated bronzes) with the **Briges**, the people whom the *Telegony* has
Odysseus fight in Thesprotia c. 1180 BCE and whom Herodotus places below Mount Bermion
in Macedonia "until they migrated to Asia." The **Briges of Europe** are thus the same
people as the **Phryges of Asia** — confirmed by the close resemblance between
twelfth-century Lausitz pottery of Macedonia and that of **Troy VIIb** (Knobbed Ware),
and by Phrygian-derived place-names (Edessa, Scodra, Scydra). Crucially, Hammond argues
it was the **pressure of the Briges** on western Macedonia and Epirus that helped set
the [[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Dorians]] and Thessalians in motion southward — making
the Phrygian migration one driver of the whole chain of
[[greek-dark-age|Dark Age]] movements.

### The Phrygian Period in West Macedonia (CAH III.1, Ch. 15)

Hammond's later chapter on [[epirus|Illyris, Epirus and Macedonia]] traces what
happened to the **European Brygi who stayed behind**. He defines a "**Phrygian
period**" in west Macedonia, central Albania and north Epirus lasting roughly
**1150–800 BCE**, in which Brygian/Phrygian power and influence were dominant. Its
basis, Hammond argues, was **metallurgical skill** — the region is rich in metals, and
Greek tradition (Callisthenes) attributed the wealth of **Midas** to the Phrygian
Dactyli, "probably related to the Brygi of Macedonia." Phrygian prosperity peaked in
the **ninth century**; thereafter, as the **[[illyrians|Illyrian]]** tribes pressed
down and took the areas north of Ochrid and the best crossing to Italy, the Phrygian
sphere shrank — and when the Phrygians finally left, Macedonia lay open to Illyrian
expansion. This frames the Anatolian Phrygian kingdom of Gordion as the eastern,
emigrant pole of a people whose European homeland flourished and faded over the same
centuries.

## Anatolia After the Hittites

The Phrygian migration is one piece of the post-collapse Anatolian mosaic that
chapter XXX surveys:

- **Neo-Hittite ("Syro-Hittite") states**: in southeastern Anatolia and northern
  Syria — Carchemish, Malatya (Melid), Tabal, and others — Luwian-speaking
  successor principalities preserved Hittite royal iconography and the
  hieroglyphic Luwian script for centuries after the empire's fall. These are the
  political continuators of the [[hittites|Hittite]] world in miniature.
- **Tabal**: a cluster of such states on the southern plateau, later a foil to
  Assyrian expansion.
- **Urartu**: in the eastern Anatolian highlands around Lake Van, an emergent
  kingdom (drawing on the earlier Hurrian world) that would become a major rival of
  Assyria in the following centuries.
- **Phrygia proper**: consolidating around Gordion and the Sangarius valley,
  reaching its height in the 8th century under the kings remembered by the Greeks
  as Midas (the Mita of Mushki of the Assyrian records) before being shattered by
  the **Cimmerian** invasions c. 700 BCE.

## Midas, Gordium, and the Cimmerian Destruction (CAH III.2, Ch. 34a)

[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 34a, M. Mellink) gives the
**eighth-century kingdom** in archaeological detail. The Phrygian kingdom is "largely a
historical and archaeological reconstruction" centred on the one historical king, **Midas**
— the **Mita of Mushki** of the Assyrian records, who from **c. 717–709** was an aggressor
in the Taurus and a sought-after ally of the neo-Hittite kings against [[neo-assyrian-empire|
Assyria]], before submitting and sending tribute to **[[sargon-ii|Sargon II]] (709)**. The
duality of his realm — "Mita of Mushki" to the Assyrians, king of the Phrygians to the Greeks
— reflects his rule over both the **plateau Mushki** and the Phrygians of the Sangarius
valley. The **black stone of Tyana**, inscribed in **Phrygian alphabet** in neo-Hittite
Luwian territory, shows Midas asserting his own language and script at the edge of his reach.

**Gordium** (Yassıhüyük), excavated since 1950, confirms the capital: a stratified sequence
of **Phrygian citadels** with massive battered ramparts, an oblique East Gate, and parallel
**megaron** buildings (megaron 3 the likely palace of Midas), all burned and looted in the
**Cimmerian raid (Mellink dates 696/5)**. The vast **tumulus cemetery** — the colossal 53-m
mound holding a king in his mid-sixties (very probably **Midas himself**), with 170 bronze
vessels, fine inlaid **wooden furniture**, and **alphabetic graffiti** on wax-covered bowls —
defines a distinctive Phrygian burial tradition (doorless timber chambers under mounds,
contrasting with the built [[lydia|Lydian]] chambers at Sardis). Phrygian art — geometric
inlaid woodwork, engraved bronze belts and fibulae, the rock-cut façades of **Midas City**,
and the cult-image of the goddess **Matar Kubile (Cybele)** — was an original West Anatolian
"national style" that influenced its neighbours and ultimately Greece. After the
**[[cimmerians|Cimmerian]]** catastrophe Gordium was rebuilt, but Phrygia was eclipsed by
its [[lydia|Lydian]] neighbours, its later kings unknown by name.

## Causal Significance

- `caused_by:` the power vacuum of the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age
  Collapse]] and the fall of the [[hittites|Hittite Empire]]
- `concurrent_with:` the rise of the [[hittites|Neo-Hittite states]], Urartu, and
  the Aramaean polities of Syria
- `opposed_by:` [[assyria-middle|Assyria]], whose westward push under
  Tiglath-pileser I first brought it into contact with the Mushki

## Historiography

CAH II.2's treatment is a cautious 1970s synthesis of Greek legend, Assyrian
annals, and the early Gordion excavations. The Mushki–Phrygian equation and the
date of Phrygian entry remain debated; later work has tended to lower the
foundation of the Gordion kingdom and to distinguish more sharply between the
Iron Age archaeological "Phrygians" and the various groups the Assyrians lumped as
Mushki. Anatolian Iron Age archaeology is an area where the collection is moderate
rather than strong, and the volume predates most of the modern Gordion
publications.
