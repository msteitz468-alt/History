# Donald Kagan

Donald Kagan (1932–2021) was an American historian specializing in ancient Greek history, particularly the Peloponnesian War. He is widely regarded as the leading modern authority on the conflict, author of a four-volume history and this one-volume synthesis that serves as the standard narrative account.

## Background and Approach

Kagan taught at Cornell and Yale, where he was Sterling Professor of Classics and History. His work combines deep engagement with Thucydides (the primary contemporary source) with critical use of later ancient authors (Diodorus, Plutarch), inscriptions, archaeology, and modern strategic analysis. He emphasizes the role of individual leaders, specific decisions, war aims, resources, and the interaction of intelligence and chance rather than purely structural or economic determinism.

Kagan argues that the war was not inevitable and that Athenian advantages in resources, sea power, and manpower were squandered by poor leadership after Pericles, especially the disastrous Sicilian expedition. In the New Strategies range he details how post-Sphacteria Athens held the advantage (hostages as peace guarantee, empire revenue surge, no immediate Persian threat, western distractions for Peloponnesians) yet chose aggressive extension over Periclean-style negotiated security; Cleon’s supremacy produced the high tribute assessment and offensive bases (Methana, Cythera) while Nicias represented the moderate/restrained option.

## Contribution via *The Peloponnesian War* (2003)

This single-volume work provides a clear, accessible, yet detailed political and military narrative of the entire war (431–404 BCE), completing Thucydides (who ends in 411) and offering Kagan's mature interpretations:

- The rivalry between Athens (democratic, imperial, naval) and Sparta (oligarchic, land power) and the decisions that led to war.
- Pericles' strategy of attrition and its limits.
- The impact of the plague and the shift to more aggressive leaders (Cleon, Demosthenes).
- Brasidas' innovative campaigns in the north.
- The uneasy Peace of Nicias and its collapse. (Detailed in range 13001-17000: immediate Spartan failure to restore Amphipolis fully or compel allies; Boeotian demolition of Panactum; additional Spartan-Athenian alliance; Argive League counter-alliance; Alcibiades' maneuvers leading to quadruple alliance with Argos, Elis, Mantinea.)
- The Battle of Mantinea (418) and preceding campaign. (Range covers Agis' march to save Tegea, Elean withdrawal enabling Spartans to force battle, terrain of Mantinea plain (18 mi N-S, ridges at gap, sinkholes), Agis' night maneuvers and three-column approach to cut off Argives.)
- The Sicilian expedition as the great turning point and example of overreach. (Range grounds the earlier Sicilian operations 427-424: first 20 ships prevented Syracusan victory; 60-ship follow-on arrived too late after Gela congress where Hermocrates advocated "Sicily for the Sicilians" — a united island free of mainland powers. Athenians accepted but generals punished on return for alleged bribery/insufficient aggression; Kagan notes Athenian over-optimism after Pylos successes but also minimal initiative by the late-arriving generals.)
- Delium (424) and Amphipolis (422) battles with tactical detail: Pagondas' 25-deep Theban phalanx (first recorded), elite 300 Theban corps, cavalry surprise flanking from behind ridge; Brasidas' decoy gates + elite sally at Amphipolis.
- Peace of Nicias terms and critiques: 50-year duration, status quo with exceptions (Plataea to Thebes, Nisaea to Athens), prisoner exchange, Spartan betrayal of northern allies; failed to secure Periclean goals of demonstrating Athenian invulnerability.
- The Ionian War, Persian intervention, Lysander's leadership, and the final defeat of Athens.
- Reflections on the consequences for Greek civilization, democracy, and empire.

The book is explicitly positioned as a modern complement and corrective to Thucydides for contemporary readers, while honoring Thucydides' analytical power.
