# Alaric

The first king of the **Visigoths** (c. 395–410) — the Gothic leader, product of the post-Adrianople
*foederate* settlement, who spent fifteen years seeking land and office for his people from a Roman
government that repeatedly refused him, and who in **410 sacked the city of Rome**. Treated in
[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 4 (**R. C. Blockley**).

## A leader without a home

Alaric rose from the Goths settled inside the empire by [[theodosius-i|Theodosius I]] (the *foedus* of
382). After Theodosius' death (395) he led his people in revolt, ravaging Greece and the Balkans and
extracting Roman offices and subsidies, alternately fought and courted by **[[stilicho|Stilicho]]** — who
once secured him a payment of 4,000 pounds of gold. His consistent goal, Blockley stresses, was **not
conquest but a permanent settlement**: lands (in Noricum, Pannonia or Dalmatia) and a recognized command
for his followers. The fall of Stilicho (408) and the massacre of the federates' families swelled his
ranks with some 30,000 men and removed the one Roman who could deal with him.

## The march on Rome and the sack of 410

With the anti-German government of **Olympius** at Ravenna refusing both to fight and to settle, Alaric
**blockaded Rome** three times (408–410). He extracted a vast ransom, set up a puppet emperor (**Priscus
Attalus**), and moderated his demands again and again — asking finally only for grain and land in Noricum.
Honorius' intransigence (and the treachery of Sarus) drove him to a third siege: on **24 August 410** the
Visigoths entered by the Salarian Gate and **looted Rome for three days** (see [[sack-of-rome-410]]).
Marching south to cross to Africa, Alaric was thwarted by a storm and **died at Consentia (late 410)**,
buried secretly in a riverbed. His brother-in-law **Ataulf** succeeded him, leading the Visigoths into
Gaul.

## Significance

Alaric is the emblem of the **failure of the federate experiment**: a leader who wanted to be *inside* the
Roman system, not to destroy it, but whom the western government would neither accommodate nor crush. The
sack of Rome — for him "a final act of rage and despair, a confession of his failure to win a settlement"
— was the symbolic shock of the age, though strategically less decisive than its fame suggests. His
people, the **Visigoths**, would become the first of the post-Roman barbarian kingdoms (settled in
Aquitaine, 418).
