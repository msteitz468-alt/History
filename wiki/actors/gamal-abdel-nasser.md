# Gamal Abdel Nasser (1918–1970)

The Egyptian army officer who overthrew the monarchy, led Egypt from 1954 to 1970, and became
the **foremost champion of [[arab-nationalism|pan-Arab nationalism]]** — the dominant figure of
the Arab world at the height of the [[cold-war|Cold War]]. From Eugene Rogan's
[[rogan-the-arabs|*The Arabs: A History*]].

## The Free Officers and the revolution

Radicalized by the Arab defeat in the [[arab-israeli-conflict|1948 Palestine War]] and by
British coercion over the Suez Canal Zone (the "Black Saturday" crisis of January 1952),
Nasser built a secret cell of army officers, the **Free Officers**, who staged a near-bloodless
**coup on 23 July 1952**, toppling King Farouk. The figurehead General Naguib was soon
sidelined; by 1954 Nasser ruled. The regime carried out a sweeping **land reform** (breaking
the great estates of the Turco-Circassian elite) and set out to secure Egypt's economic
independence — above all through the **Aswan High Dam**.

## Suez and the peak of pan-Arabism

When the US and Britain withdrew funding for the dam, Nasser **nationalized the Suez Canal
(1956)**, provoking the **Suez Crisis** — a joint British–French–Israeli invasion that was
forced into humiliating withdrawal by US and Soviet pressure. The political victory made Nasser
the **hero of the Arab world** and the embodiment of anti-imperial [[arab-nationalism|Arab
nationalism]]; he was a leading voice of **Afro-Asianism and Non-Alignment**. Pan-Arabism
peaked in the **United Arab Republic (1958–61)**, the short-lived union of Egypt and Syria.

## The 1967 catastrophe and decline

Nasserism's credibility rested on confronting Israel — and it was broken there. The crushing
Arab defeat in the **[[arab-israeli-conflict|Six-Day War of June 1967]]** (Egypt losing Sinai
and Gaza) shattered the pan-Arab project's prestige. Nasser offered to resign; mass
demonstrations kept him in office, but the spell was broken. He died of a heart attack in
**1970**, mourned by millions. Rogan marks the defeats by Israel and Nasser's death as what
"undermined the credibility" of Arab nationalism, opening the way to the oil politics of the
1970s and the rise of **[[political-islam|political Islam]]**.

## Counterfactual significance

Nasser is the **central figure of the independent Arab world's first generation** — the symbol
of its aspiration to dignity, unity, and mastery of its own destiny, and of the limits of that
aspiration. His career embodies Rogan's theme: an Arab leader who briefly seemed to rewrite the
rules of the [[cold-war|Cold War]] in the Arabs' favour, only to be driven back by defeat. His
authoritarian, single-party, security-state model also set the template for the Arab regimes
that followed.
