# Babylonia in the Early Iron Age (c. 1000–748 BCE)

The long **eclipse and slow recovery** of Babylonia between the fall of the
[[kassites|Kassite dynasty]] and the threshold of Assyrian domination — the obscure
centuries from which the **Chaldaean** tribes rose to become, eventually, the
[[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]]. [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol.
III, Part 1]] (Ch. 7, J. A. Brinkman) reconstructs it from "tiny fragments of
information," and warns the resulting picture is sketchy.

## A vulnerable land

Brinkman stresses Babylonia's **geographical openness**: no natural frontiers, a
desert funnelling **[[arameans|Aramaean]]** semi-nomads into the north-west, southern
marshes (the **"Sealand"**) penetrable from Elam and Arabia and serving as a refuge
for anti-Assyrian tribesmen, and easy Assyrian access from the north-east. A major
**westward shift of the Euphrates** in the late second / early first millennium —
moving the main channel from Kish and Nippur toward Babylon — helped trigger the
economic decline of **Nippur** and reshaped settlement. Wealth depended wholly on
**irrigation and trade** (Babylonia astride the Gulf–Mediterranean route), both of
which faltered amid the instability.

## Eclipse and tribal infiltration

For much of the period Babylonia was weak, fragmented, and repeatedly raided —
"kingless" interludes appear in the chronicles, and Assyrian kings from
**Ashurnasirpal II** to **Adad-nirari III** intervened, the latter claiming "all the
kings of Chaldaea" as vassals while also patronizing the Babylonian cult (offerings
at Babylon, Borsippa, Cutha; the rising popularity of the god **Nabu** in Assyria
dates to his reign). Two intrusive populations dominate the story: the
**Aramaeans** (raiders and squatters on farmland) and the **Chaldaeans** — settled
tribal confederations (**Bit-Yakin, Bit-Dakkuru, Bit-Amukani**) established in the
south, "already well established" by 850.

## The rise of the Chaldaeans (810–748)

Against a decimated north and a weakened Assyria (in its post-783 "Interval"), the
Chaldaeans rose to the throne. **Marduk-apla-usur** is the first king plainly
identified as Chaldaean; **Eriba-Marduk** (of Bit-Yakin) was remembered as
"re-establisher of the foundation(s) of the land" — the first powerful Chaldaean
monarch, who expelled Aramaeans squatting on temple lands near Babylon and Borsippa
and restored the cult — though a later tradition (via Nabonidus) blames him for
disorders at Uruk. Chronic strife continued under **Nabu-shuma-ishkun** (street
fighting in Borsippa; the Nabu statue repeatedly barred from the New Year Festival).
The period closes with **Nabonassar (Nabu-nasir, 747–734)**, whose accession marks
the start of the continuous **Babylonian Chronicle Series** and the **Ptolemaic
Canon** — and the prelude to Tiglath-pileser III's intervention and the long
Assyro-Babylonian entanglement of CAH III.2.

## Babylonia in the Shadow of Assyria (CAH III.2, 745–626)

[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 21, Brinkman) carries the story
forward from Nabonassar through the century of Assyrian domination to the accession
of [[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]]. Its central analytical move is to read Babylonia
by **social organization (tribal vs. non-tribal)** rather than town-vs-country:

- **The tribal structure.** The countryside was held by two West Semitic groups —
  the fragmented, pastoral **[[arameans|Aramaeans]]** (40+ tribes: Gambulu, Puqudu,
  Ru'ua) whose lasting legacy was the **Aramaization** of the regional language, and
  the more sedentary, more unified **[[chaldeans|Chaldaeans]]**, five "Houses"
  (Bit-Amukani, **Bit-Dakkuri**, the powerful **Bit-Yakin** of the Sealand, plus
  Bit-Sha'alli and Bit-Shilani) who controlled the Euphrates and the Gulf trade.
- **A weak monarchy, privileged cities.** The Babylonian throne saw **14 reigns
  averaging 3.2 years (733–689)**; real power lay with the great cult-cities (Babylon,
  Borsippa, Sippar, Nippur, Uruk) whose citizens held tax-and-corvée exemption
  (*kidinnūtu*) and which often preferred the prosperity of Assyrian protection to
  tribal rule.
- **The century-long struggle (745–626).** [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]]
  intervened in 745; thereafter Assyria tried every method — campaigns, client kings,
  the Assyrian king holding the dual crown, and **[[assyrian-mass-deportation|mass
  deportation]]** (by official count **~500,000 people removed from Babylonia,
  745–702, over half Chaldaeans**). The Chaldaeans repeatedly regrouped under leaders
  like **[[merodach-baladan|Merodach-baladan II]]** and **Mushezib-Marduk**;
  [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] answered by **destroying Babylon (689)**, which
  [[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]] then rebuilt. The contest ended only when the Chaldaean
  **[[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]]** seized the throne in **626** and turned Babylonia
  from victim into the conqueror of Assyria — the [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-
  Babylonian Empire]] proper, treated from Ch. 27.

## Historiography

A deliberately **cautious** chapter: Brinkman repeatedly notes that the king-list,
reign-lengths, and even some royal names of these decades are uncertain, and that
the apparent obscurity is itself partly evidence of the times' disorder. The
Chaldaean tribes traced here are the matrix of the later
[[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]] of Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar
II; the Aramaean infiltration is the same process eroding Assyria from within (see
[[arameans]]).
