# Nebuchadnezzar I

Nebuchadnezzar I (Akkadian *Nabû-kudurri-uṣur*, "Nabu protect my heir/boundary";
older spelling Nebuchadrezzar), r. **1124–1103 BCE**, was the greatest king of the
**[[second-isin-dynasty|Second Dynasty of Isin]]** and the national hero of
post-Kassite Babylonia. He is treated in CAH II.2 (chapter XXXI, Wiseman). He is
**not** to be confused with the far more famous **Nebuchadnezzar II** (r. 605–562
BCE, of the Neo-Babylonian empire, the destroyer of Jerusalem), who deliberately
adopted this honoured throne-name some five centuries later.

## The Defeat of Elam

Nebuchadnezzar I's defining act was to avenge the humiliation Babylonia had
suffered when the Elamite [[elam|Shutrukid]] kings sacked its cities, ended the
[[kassites|Kassite dynasty]] (c. 1158 BCE), and carried off to Susa the **statue of
Marduk** from Esagila — together with the famous law-stele of [[hammurabi|Hammurabi]]
and the victory-stele of [[naram-sin|Naram-Sin]]. After an early counter-raid failed
(his troops struck by plague), Nebuchadnezzar launched a second campaign in high
summer — the annals dwell on the scorching heat, the dry wells, and the failing
horses — marching far to the south-east and meeting the Elamites on the banks of the
**Ulai (Eulaeus) near Susa**. In a fiercely contested battle, decided by a charge of
the Babylonian chariotry on the right wing, the Elamite king **Khutelutush-In-
Shushinak** fled and soon died, ending Elamite domination of the plain for
generations.

## The Recovery of Marduk and the Rise of a National God

The campaign's significance was more religious and psychological than territorial:
the **cult-statue of Marduk was restored to Esagila** amid great rejoicing. CAH
II.2 connects this celebrated event with the **elevation of Marduk to the supreme
national deity of Babylon** — the theological development later crystallised in the
*Enuma Elish*. Nebuchadnezzar became the subject of heroic poetry and of omen-series
(one seventh-century astrological text was titled "when Nebuchadnezzar crushed
Elam"), and his name became a throne-name of choice for later Babylonian rulers
facing eastern (Persian) and other rivals.

## Relations with Assyria

Against [[assyria-middle|Assyria]] he was less successful. He probably authored the
defiant letter to **Ashur-resha-ishi** threatening to reinstate an Assyrian
pretender, and his attacks at the border crossings of Zaqqu and Idu (Hit?) were
repulsed by Assyrian columns of mixed chariotry and infantry. The struggle for the
disputed upper Diyala frontier was inconclusive and would resume under his
successors against [[tiglath-pileser-i|Tiglath-pileser I]].

## Significance

- `produced:` the recovery of Babylonian morale and independence after the Elamite
  and Kassite catastrophes, and (per the volume) `contributed_to` the elevation of
  **Marduk** to head of the pantheon — a turning point in Mesopotamian religion.
- `caused_by:` the [[elam|Elamite]] sack of Babylon and the end of the
  [[kassites|Kassite dynasty]] — the injuries he set out to avenge.
- `concurrent_with:` the reign of [[tiglath-pileser-i|Tiglath-pileser I]] of Assyria.

## Historiography

CAH II.2 reconstructs the reign from king-lists, chronicles (the Synchronistic
History is Assyrian-biased), *kudurru* land-grant stelae, and the later heroic and
omen literature — a thin and partly legendary base. The dating follows the
conventional Middle Chronology. The link between Nebuchadnezzar's victory and
Marduk's rise to supremacy is a plausible inference rather than a documented fact,
and remains a matter of scholarly judgement.
