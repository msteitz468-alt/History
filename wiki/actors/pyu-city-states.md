# Pyu City-States

The **Pyu city-states** are the early walled urban centres of Myanmar’s dry zone and adjacent valleys—conventionally **Beikthano, Halin, Maingmaw, Sri Ksetra, and Wadi**—spanning roughly the **second century BCE to the ninth century CE**. They rank among the earliest large ramparted settlements in mainland Southeast Asia and, at Sri Ksetra, produced some of the region’s most important brick architecture, including rare **true arches and vaults**. Primary wiki synthesis: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]] (Protoclassic and Early Classic mainland chapters).

## The “Pyu” Problem

“**Pyu**” is a **problematic** modern/ethnohistoric label for peoples of the Ayeyarwady (Irrawaddy) world before and during early Burman political ascent. Chinese *Man Shu* and related sources speak of **Piao**; some evidence suggests self-reference as **Tircul**. Diagnostic artifacts often listed as “Pyu”—coins with conch, rising sun, and *śrīvatsa* motifs; finger-marked bricks; largely undeciphered inscriptions; funerary urns—are **not ethnically exclusive**. The same coin motifs appear on [[dvaravati|Dvaravati]] sites in Thailand; walls and moats are shared mainland traits. Miksic and Goh stress that people of first–ninth-century sites “probably came from a variety of ethnic, cultural, and linguistic backgrounds.” Only **stone urns with inscribed Pyu writing** (five at Sri Ksetra, tentatively a Vikrama dynasty before or during the seventh century) securely link artifacts to a Pyu **ethnolinguistic** group.

The **2013 Myittha inscription** of King Sawlu (r. 1077–1084) of the [[pagan-kingdom|Bagan]] period carries six languages including Pyu—evidence that Pyu was still a living written language in the eleventh century and that simple narratives of total Burman replacement by then are wrong. An alternative scholarship holds that Burmese speakers already lived in the plains alongside Pyu populations and that Burmans may have resided in **19 founding villages**, predecessors of Bagan.

## Major Sites and Hierarchy

| Site | Approx. enclosed area | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sri Ksetra** (Hmawza/Pyay/Prome) | **1,452 ha** (~30 km² wall circuit) | Largest; Xuanzang and Yijing mention; citadel 575×375 m; Early Classic apex |
| **Beikthano** (“Viṣṇu City”) | **859 ha** | Irregular quadrangle + citadel; ~2nd c. BCE–4th c. CE (few ¹⁴C); 12 burned gate entrances; large urn cemeteries |
| **Halin** | **629 ha** | 50 km N of Mandalay; salt fields, hot springs; Pyu inscriptions 4th–9th c.; 12 gateways; metal caltrops (also Sri Ksetra) |
| **Maingmaw** | **625 ha** | Elliptical walls |
| **Wadi** | **202 ha** | Smallest of the classic five |

Wall shapes: Beikthano and Halin are **quadrangular with rounded corners**; Maingmaw, Sri Ksetra, and Wadi are **elliptical**—possibly different periods or cultural preferences (quadrangular perhaps earlier). The five sites share at least **seven polythetic criteria**, supporting treatment as one archaeological culture with local variation. Urbanization appears abruptly with Beikthano; scholars have applied **low-density city / orthogenetic** models comparable to those used for [[angkor|Angkor]]. Myanmar’s traditional site hierarchy (*pyi / pura / myo / gon auk / ywa*) is not population-based.

### Sri Ksetra in the Early Classic

In the Early Classic Ayeyarwady, Sri Ksetra became “one of the largest ramparted enclosures built in Classic Seasia.” Architects developed—perhaps independently—the **true arch and vault**. In Southeast Asia, true arches and vaults were found only in Myanmar until introduced to [[ayutthaya|Ayutthaya]] in the fourteenth century; they are rare in India and appear in Sri Lanka only in the twelfth century. Chihara praised “fine examples of Pyu radiating arches and vaults.”

Stupas of the seventh–ninth centuries include Bawbawgyi (~46 m, bulbous cylindrical solid core on terraces), Hpaya Gyi, and Hpaya Ma; brick temples such as Bebe, Laymyethna, and Yahantha Gu are likely mostly **Bagan-period** (tenth–fourteenth centuries). Chronicle tradition (*Mahayazawingyi*) gives Sri Ksetra 32 main + 23 small gates; Chinese records describe 12 doorways; excavated gates (Shwedagar, Lulinkyaw, Nagatwaint) resemble those of Beikthano and Halin. Limited radiocarbon places much Sri Ksetra activity ~500–700 CE; dating confidence is low because sample contexts are often poorly documented. The site continues into the Bagan period.

The **Nga Kywe Nadaung** stupa at Bagan has been proposed as a late Pyu structure on stylistic grounds, but there is **no evidence of Pyu objects** (silver coins, burial urns) within the Bagan archaeological zone; Bagan itself does not clearly predate the late ninth or early tenth centuries.

## Broader Myanmar Interaction

**Tagaung** (east bank of the Ayeyarwady north of Halin) is entangled in modern nationalist origin stories (“Myanmar asa, Tagaung ka” after the 1829 *Hmannan*). Scholarship is divided: some date the site’s importance to the Bagan/Anawrahta era; 2003–04 excavations claimed a “Pyu” first capital, but context and stratigraphy problems remain. Wall layout comparisons with Bagan and iron-smelting importance link Tagaung into later dry-zone narratives without proving simple succession.

**Arakan** (Rakhine): Dhanyawadi (442 ha) and Vesali (702 ha) span Preclassic–Early Classic with central citadels; Vesali may have replaced Dhanyawadi as primate centre in the fifth century. The Anandacandra inscription of **729** lists 18 kings from the fourth century; Mahayana predecessors and royal support of Brahmins are attested. In the Middle Classic, Rakhine succumbed to Bagan attacks and joined the central Myanmar mandala.

**Lower Myanmar / Suvaṇṇabhūmi** (“Land of Gold”): Kyaikkatha at the Sittaung mouth is the largest laterite-region walled site (**375 ha**), with conch/*śrīvatsa* coins and finger-marked bricks. Thaton’s legendary conquest by Anawrahta for a Buddhist library has scant excavation behind it; art and architecture there are largely ninth–eleventh-century in style.

Coins with conch/*śrīvatsa*, *bhadrapīṭha*/*śrīvatsa*, and rising sun/*śrīvatsa* motifs distribute from Lower Myanmar through Dvaravati Thailand to Vietnam and Bengal—an **interaction sphere**, not a single culture.

## Chronological Position

- **preceded_by** and **concurrent_with** late prehistoric dry-zone settlement; ramparted sites appear in the Protoclassic if not earlier.
- **concurrent_with** [[funan|Funan]]/[[oc-eo|Oc Eo]], early Khmer centres, and [[dvaravati|Dvaravati]] in the Early Classic diplomatic and pilgrimage geography (Xuanzang’s itinerary).
- **followed_by** the rise of [[pagan-kingdom|Bagan]] from the late ninth/tenth centuries—political succession debated; cultural and linguistic strands persist.
- Early Classic inception (600 CE) in [[seasian-classic-periodization|Miksic & Goh]] is marked partly by Pyu foci shifting toward Sri Ksetra as Beikthano, Halin, and other Protoclassic centres declined.

## Historiography

Using ethnolinguistic “Pyu” for first–ninth-century sites is the central historiographical trap. Better practice: treat Sri Ksetra, Tagaung, Bagan, and the classic five as **local sequences** or a **loosely defined interaction sphere** with local variation. Chronicle ideal cities (gate counts) should not override Chinese and excavated gate evidence. Radiocarbon poverty and poor sample context mean architectural and palaeographic dating carry much of the load. Relationship to Burman ethnogenesis remains open: co-residence, gradual language shift, and elite replacement are all possible; the Myittha inscription proves late Pyu literacy under Bagan kings.

Brick structures of this horizon are among the **oldest preserved buildings in Southeast Asia**, yet little excavation has resolved dates of first large settlements with precision. Collection coverage for Myanmar is moderate in secondary synthesis; field archaeology is still catching up with Thai and Cambodian sequences.
