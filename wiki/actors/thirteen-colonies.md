## Role and Background

The Thirteen Colonies — Virginia, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Maryland, Connecticut, Rhode Island, Delaware, North Carolina, South Carolina, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Georgia — were the British settlements along the Atlantic seaboard of North America founded between 1607 (Jamestown) and 1732 (Georgia). Each possessed its own colonial assembly with substantial local self-governance, operating under royal, proprietary, or corporate charters within the broader framework of British mercantile policy.

## Development and Rupture

By the mid-eighteenth century the colonies had developed distinct regional economies (plantation agriculture in the South, mixed farming and shipping in the Middle Colonies, commerce and shipbuilding in New England) and shared in the transatlantic [[processes/consumer-revolution|consumer revolution]], importing British and Asian manufactured goods on a large scale. Escalating disputes over taxation and representation following the Seven Years' War — the Stamp Act (1765), Townshend Duties, and the Coercive Acts — drove the colonies toward coordinated resistance through the Continental Congresses, culminating in the [[events/declaration-of-independence-1776|Declaration of Independence]] in 1776 and the formation of the United States.

## Significance

The Thirteen Colonies' pattern of relatively developed local self-government, combined with resentment at metropolitan taxation without representation, produced the first successful anti-colonial revolution of the modern era and the first written federal constitutional republic.
