# Northwest Coast Cultures

The Northwest Coast cultural tradition encompasses the indigenous peoples inhabiting a 2,000 km (1,250 mi) coastal strip from Yakutat Bay in southeastern Alaska to northwestern California, running through what is now British Columbia, Washington, and Oregon. These cultures represent one of the world's most striking cases of complex hunter-gatherer societies: ranked, often stratified, and permanently settled communities achieving social and material elaboration without agriculture. Their economic base was the extraordinary productivity of the Pacific coastal environment, particularly anadromous salmon runs, which enabled food surpluses, large households, and ultimately the social institutions — potlatch, hereditary rank, and elaborate art — for which they are justifiably famous.

## Overview

Northwest Coast cultures subvert the standard anthropological expectation that complexity requires farming. The region's maritime environment — temperate rain forest dominated by western red cedar, coastal seas rich in halibut, cod, herring, and six salmon species, plus sea mammals including seals, sea lions, and whales — provided resources so abundant that logistical harvesting and long-term storage could sustain large, permanently settled communities. The result was social stratification, ranked hereditary leadership, specialist craftwork, and elaborate ceremonialism, all without a domesticated crop. As nineteenth-century anthropologists discovered, these "wealthy hunter-gatherers" fit no existing theoretical category, driving revision of evolutionary models.

## Geographic Distribution

The Northwest Coast runs from southeastern Alaska south through British Columbia (Canada), Washington, and Oregon, ending in northwestern California. The northern section (Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian territories) is where classic Northwest Coast cultural features — large cedar plank houses, totem poles, elaborate canoes, potlatches — are most strongly developed, reflecting the zone of highest seasonal marine resource abundance. The southern section (Oregon, northern California) shows less elaboration, reflecting more diverse terrestrial resources and a different adaptive emphasis.

## Dating and Chronology

The oldest human remains from the Northwest Coast come from On-Your-Knees Cave on Prince of Wales Island, southeastern Alaska, dated to 9,730–10,300 radiocarbon years BP. Distinctive cultural features began emerging in the Middle Holocene (5000–1500 BCE). Marine shell middens appeared more frequently around 4400 BCE as ocean levels and river estuaries stabilized. Simple ranked societies are inferred by 1800 BCE, becoming more complex over time. Large permanent extended households formed by around 1000 BCE or earlier. The classic suite of cultural features — the complete repertoire documented historically — was fully developed by the time of European contact.

## Economy and Subsistence

The Northwest Coast economy was built on the extraordinary seasonal abundance of anadromous fish — salmon, steelhead, smelt, and eulachon (candlefish) — supplemented by marine mammal hunting (sea otters, seals, sea lions, porpoises, and in some areas whales), halibut and cod fishing, and shellfish gathering. Eulachon, so rich in oil they could be burned like candles, were an important food source and trade commodity. Deer, elk, bear, and mountain goat supplemented coastal resources.

Critical innovations enabled the intensification of resource harvesting: an array of weirs and fish traps captured salmon in huge quantities; barbed harpoons with detachable heads on lines enabled hunting of large marine mammals; large dugout canoes (implied by heavy woodworking tools and harpoon technology, though actual canoes rarely survive archaeologically) allowed strategic movement to distant seasonal resources and transport back for storage.

Later communities shifted from residential to logistical mobility strategies — rather than moving the whole community to resources, work parties moved out while the main settlement remained fixed. This shift promoted food surplus storage and more complex division of labor. The economic base enabled large household production units, with all the competition, surplus, and ranking that such organization entails.

## Architecture and Technology

The signature architectural form of the Northwest Coast is the large cedar plank house. Unlike earlier semi-subterranean round structures, later houses were rectangular with heavy posts and beams, constructed from long, rigid cedar planks. Villages were typically organized in rows of plank houses, with the most highly ranked and imposing houses in the center. A single-row village generally represented households descended from a single ancestor; two or more rows indicated multiple extended kin groups.

Permanent communities of this type emerged after approximately 1800 BCE, evidenced by large cedar structures at various sites. House sizes grew over subsequent centuries. Large households imply large production units — lineages of dozens of related individuals — and the archaeological signature of social inequality appears in this period: ground-stone beads, pendants, and labrets likely signaling rank; evidence of cranial shaping (binding an infant's head to produce a distinctive shape, marking inherited status).

Cedar boxes — bent from a single plank, with a sewn-on bottom — represent another characteristic technology, filling the functional roles of pottery or basketry elsewhere: storage, cooking (by placing hot stones inside sealed boxes), and transport. Examples are found in many sizes, sometimes beautifully decorated.

The site of Ozette at the northwestern tip of Washington's Olympic Peninsula, buried by a 1700 CE landslide, provides an exceptional snapshot. Rescue excavations over ten years recovered more than 55,000 artifacts from three buried houses, including baskets, lines, bags, harpoons, boxes, and toys of wood and fiber normally lost to decomposition. The Makah Museum at the Makah Cultural and Research Center displays many of these objects.

## Social Organization

Historically, most Northwest Coast societies maintained two social classes: free people and slaves. Slaves were war captives and their descendants. Free people were further sorted into three ranks: chiefs and chiefly families with many titles (an elite stratum); a middle rank with few titles; and commoners with no titles. This stratification is unusually stable for a hunter-gatherer society, maintained by kin-based systems in communities small enough to enforce customary distinctions without coercive state institutions.

Household leaders controlled the production, storage, and redistribution of resources for their kin group, receiving high rank in exchange. Rank differentials between households were personified in the standings of their leaders. A strong moral injunction against hoarding complemented these arrangements: property was expected to be used and distributed, not accumulated. Strategic use and redistribution of wealth generated prestige and power.

These practices produced the quintessential Northwest Coast cultural institution: the **potlatch**. At potlatches, chiefs gave away or ostentatiously destroyed wealth to demonstrate their capacity for abundance. European missionaries and government officials condemned potlatches as wasteful; Canadian authorities banned them from 1885 to 1951. In reality, potlatches were sophisticated mechanisms for wealth redistribution, alliance formation, status display, and the public recording of social relationships.

Warfare was conducted for multiple purposes: capturing slaves, revenge, enhancing prestige, securing food resources, and taking wives. Attacks were launched from sea; warriors wore wooden-slat and hide armor tough enough to stop European musket balls. Villages were sometimes moved to defensible bluff locations; fortifications were constructed in some places. Warfare probably intensified in later prehistory, with subsistence strategies shifting from small-group sea mammal hunting toward communal salmon harvesting as security in numbers became more important.

## Art and Symbolism

The Northwest Coast is renowned for its distinctive artistic tradition. Zoomorphic designs appear on totem poles, cedar boxes, house façades, Chilkat blankets, and portable objects. The human and animal figures are often split and splayed, with identifying elements sometimes rearranged into abstract images. Three-dimensional forms serve as decoration on bowls, spoon handles, and statuettes. A strong tendency fills all available space rather than leaving empty background. Animals are sometimes skeletonized. The shape of the eye — with its recurring curves — is a central organizing motif. Anthropomorphic and zoomorphic forms combine freely. Art expressed status and communicated social relationships; the heraldic art of coppers (large shield-shaped metal plaques, one of the highest-prestige objects on the coast) and house façades encoded the ranking system understood by all Northwest Coast communities.

## Decline and Legacy

European exploration of the Northwest Coast came late — sporadic visits for three centuries after Columbus, then Bering in 1741, followed by Spanish expeditions and Cook in 1777. Fur traders arrived in the late eighteenth century, triggering competition between Russians, British, and Americans. The sea otter trade drove the animal nearly to extinction by the 1830s. Epidemic diseases accompanied the fur trade; Cathlapotle, a major Chinookan village on the lower Columbia, succumbed to malaria in the early-to-mid 1830s. Nineteenth-century American settlement along the Oregon Trail further displaced communities. Many Northwest Coast communities survive today as First Nations in Canada and federally recognized tribes in the United States.

## Historiography

Northwest Coast cultures were intensively documented in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, particularly by Franz Boas, whose ethnographic work established the anthropological study of the region. The potlatch attracted particular attention as a challenge to evolutionary schemas equating complexity with agriculture. The debate over the origins and timing of social stratification — whether rank emerged from resource intensification alone or required additional factors — remains active. The discovery and excavation of Ozette (1970–1981) transformed understanding of the material culture of the pre-contact period, demonstrating the richness of perishable technologies normally lost from the archaeological record. Key ongoing questions include the timing of warfare intensification and its relationship to resource stress, and the degree to which historic patterns project backward into earlier prehistoric periods.
