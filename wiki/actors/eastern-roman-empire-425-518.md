# The Eastern Roman Empire, 425–518

The eastern half of the Roman empire across the century when **the West fell and the East survived** — from
the sole rule of **Theodosius II** to the death of **Anastasius I**, the eve of Justinian's age. Lee's
central question in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 2 (**A. D. Lee**) is *why* the eastern state
endured comparable crises — Huns, Persians, usurpers, religious schism — yet, unlike its western
counterpart, came through them intact and even enriched.

## Theodosius II (408–450): the unmilitary emperor

Theodosius II inherited (aged seven) a state that under his father Arcadius had reeled from crisis — Alaric's
Goths, Hunnic invasions, the Gainas revolt, the deposition of **John Chrysostom**, Isaurian brigandage,
and chronic friction with the West. Yet his reign was the **longest of any Roman emperor** (formally 48
years). A scholarly, unwarlike ruler, he abandoned the old pattern of the campaigning emperor: he and his
successors **resided permanently in Constantinople**, which both empowered court figures (the prefect
Anthemius, the eunuch Chrysaphius, the empresses **Pulcheria** and Eudocia, the Alan general **Aspar**) and
**shielded the emperor from the political consequences of military defeat** — a key, if accidental,
ingredient of eastern stability. His court produced the **Theodosian Code (438)**, a reform of higher
education, and the great **land walls of Constantinople** that would protect the city for a millennium.

## Marcian and Leo I: the soldier-emperors and the Aspar problem

The dynasty's male line ended with Theodosius; the throne passed to elderly Balkan army officers legitimated
by marriage to Pulcheria. **Marcian** (450–457) repudiated the tribute to **Attila**, summoned the great
**[[council-of-chalcedon-451|Council of Chalcedon (451)]]** that defined Christ's two natures (the founding
act of "Chalcedonian" orthodoxy and the seed of the **Monophysite schism**), and left a full treasury.
**Leo I** (457–474), raised by the kingmaker Aspar, eventually broke free of his Germanic-Arian patron — in
part by recruiting the warlike **Isaurians** of the Anatolian highlands as a counterweight, and by founding
the **excubitores** guard. The murder of Aspar (471) ended the dominance of barbarian generals at the
eastern court — a fate the West never escaped.

## Zeno the Isaurian: schism and survival

Leo's Isaurian son-in-law **Zeno** (474–491) reigned through usurpations (Basiliscus), Isaurian feuding, and
the running sore of the **Monophysite controversy**. His **Henotikon (482)** — a "formula of union" papering
over Chalcedon to conciliate the Monophysite East (Egypt, much of Syria) — kept the eastern provinces
together but provoked the **Acacian Schism** with Rome (484–519). It was Zeno who, lacking the means to rule
Italy directly, dispatched **[[theoderic-the-great|Theoderic]]** westward in 488 — exporting his Gothic
problem and founding the Ostrogothic kingdom.

## Anastasius I: the financial reformer

The aged palace official **Anastasius** (491–518) crushed the Isaurian revolt, expelled the Isaurians from
the capital, and proved the **outstanding administrator** of the period. He **abolished the hated
*chrysargyron*** (the *collatio lustralis*, a tax on urban trade, 498), reformed the tax-collecting
bureaucracy (the *vindices*), and carried out a **currency reform (498)** that put the debased copper
coinage on a sound footing — yet still left a vast surplus (reputedly 320,000 lb of gold) in the treasury.
This **fiscal solidity** underwrote Justinian's wars and buildings. His Monophysite sympathies, however,
deepened the breach with Chalcedonian opinion and the West, provoking the revolt of **Vitalian**.

## Why the East survived

Lee's answer is structural, not providential. The East enjoyed a **richer and more defensible tax base**
(Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor — secure behind the sea and the Anatolian plateau, while the West bled away Africa
and the Rhine provinces); the legitimating **stability of Constantinople** and the residence pattern that
insulated emperors from defeat; the early **escape from barbarian generalissimos** after Aspar; the
**Isaurian counterweight** to Germanic troops; and **sound fiscal management** culminating in Anastasius.
Where the West's revenues collapsed into a "vicious circle," the East accumulated a surplus — the platform
for the resurgence under [[justinian|Justinian]].
