# Severan Dynasty

The **Severan dynasty** (193–235) was the line of emperors founded by [[septimius-severus|Septimius
Severus]] that ruled Rome between the [[high-empire|High Empire]] and the
[[third-century-crisis|third-century crisis]]. Rostovtzeff, following Gibbon, famously characterized
it as the **"military monarchy."** Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 1 (**Brian
Campbell**).

## Members

- **Septimius Severus** (193–211) — the African founder; civil and Parthian wars; the refounding of
  the Principate on an army base.
- **[[caracalla|Caracalla]]** (198/211–217) — co-ruler then sole emperor after murdering his brother
  **Geta**; the **Constitutio Antoniniana** (212); murdered near Carrhae.
- **Macrinus** (217–18) — the equestrian praetorian prefect who killed Caracalla and seized power; an
  interloper, not a Severan by blood, soon overthrown.
- **Elagabalus** (218–22) — Varius Avitus, the teenage priest of the Emesene sun-god El-Gabal,
  installed by **Julia Maesa** as Caracalla's supposed son; his alien cult and rumored excesses
  brought his murder.
- **Severus Alexander** (222–35) — the last Severan; personally modest and respectful of the senate
  but dominated by his grandmother **Julia Maesa** and mother **Julia Mamaea**; faced the new
  [[sassanid-empire|Sassanid]] threat and German wars; murdered with his mother by the mutinous Rhine
  army, opening the crisis.

## The Severan women

A distinctive feature of the dynasty was the prominence of its **Syrian women** — Julia Domna (wife
of Severus), her sister **Julia Maesa**, and Maesa's daughters **Julia Soaemias** and **Julia
Mamaea** — who shaped successions and, in Alexander's reign, effectively governed.

## Significance and historiography

Campbell's chapter resists the older catastrophist reading. The Severans did not consciously
militarize the state or degrade the senate; their innovations (army pay rises, the marriage of
soldiers, provincial advancement, the new *res privata* fisc) were largely **pragmatic responses** to
the manner of Severus' accession. But the cumulative effect was decisive: emperor and army were bound
ever closer, senatorial confidence eroded, and a ruler's **military capacity** became the measure of
his fitness to rule. The dynasty's fall — Alexander cut down as a "timid mother's boy" who tried to
**negotiate** rather than fight — opened the way to **Maximinus**, "the first truly soldier-emperor,"
and the [[third-century-crisis|crisis]].
