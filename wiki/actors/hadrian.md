# Hadrian (Publius Aelius Hadrianus)

The **third of the "Five Good Emperors"** (117–138) — the restless, philhellene "traveller-
emperor" who **reversed Trajan's expansion**, fixed and fortified the empire's **frontiers**
(Hadrian's Wall), toured nearly every province in person, and crushed the **Bar Kochba revolt**.
A Spanish-born intellectual, soldier, and architect. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]]
Ch. 3.

## Accession and the turn from expansion

Born **76 CE** to a senatorial family of Italica in Spain, Hadrian was the ward and kinsman of
**[[trajan|Trajan]]**, whose career he followed through long military service (his "special
concern for the army"). His **adoption** by the dying Trajan (117) was doubted even in antiquity
(arranged, the sources hint, by the empress **Plotina**), and his accession was insecure — marred
at once by the execution of **"four consulars"** on his deputy's orders, which "seriously damaged
his relations with the Senate." His first and defining act was to **abandon Trajan's new eastern
provinces** (Armenia, Mesopotamia, Assyria), citing Cato's maxim that lands which "could not be
protected" should be let go — and to crush the lingering **[[trajans-parthian-war-114-117|Jewish
diaspora revolt]]**. This marked the decisive shift from **expansion to consolidation**.

## Frontiers and the army

Hadrian's signature was the **fortified, fixed frontier**. He toured the empire almost
ceaselessly, and where there was no river boundary he marked the limit with continuous barriers:
the **palisade of the Upper German–Raetian *limes***, the stone-and-turf wall across **Numidia**,
and above all **Hadrian's Wall** in Britain (122) — eighty Roman miles "to separate barbarians
and Romans." He **drilled and disciplined the army** by personal example (the surviving Lambaesis
speeches to the Numidian legion), living on camp fare and marching in armour. CAH XI stresses
that these were less radical innovations than the rigorous application of existing practice — but
the frontier policy "symbolized a halt to further expansion" and, by ending major troop
transfers, increasingly **rooted the armies in the regions where they were stationed**.

## The traveller and philhellene

Hadrian spent over half his reign **travelling** — Gaul, Germany, Britain, Spain, the East,
Africa, Greece, Egypt — inspecting armies, founding cities, and dispensing benefactions. His
**philhellenism** was profound: initiated at **Eleusis**, titled *Olympios*, he completed the
**Temple of Olympian Zeus** at Athens and founded the **Panhellenion** (the commonwealth of the
Greeks). His grief at the drowning of his favourite **Antinous** in the Nile (130) — whom he
deified, founding **Antinoupolis** — became proverbial. He was a polymath and architect (the
**Pantheon**, the **Temple of Venus and Rome**, his **Villa at Tivoli**), and his reign marked
an important development in **jurisprudence** (the codification of the **Praetor's Edict** by
Salvius Julianus).

## The Bar Kochba War

Hadrian's philhellenism had catastrophic consequences in Judaea: his plan to refound Jerusalem
as the colony **Aelia Capitolina** with a temple to Jupiter, and his **ban on circumcision**,
provoked the **[[bar-kochba-war-132-135|Bar Kochba War]] (132–135)** — a united, prepared revolt
under **Simeon Bar Kochba** that the local legions could not contain. Hadrian summoned his best
general, **Sex. Julius Severus**, from Britain; the war was won only by a grinding war of
attrition with vast slaughter, after which Judaea was renamed **Syria Palaestina**.

## Succession and significance

Childless, Hadrian secured the succession through a chain of adoptions: first the consumptive
**L. Aelius Caesar** (who predeceased him), then **[[antoninus-pius|Antoninus Pius]]**, whom he
required in turn to adopt the young **[[marcus-aurelius|Marcus Aurelius]]** and Lucius Verus —
fixing the dynasty two generations ahead. His reign **defined the High Empire**: an empire that
had stopped expanding and turned to **defending fixed frontiers**, administering its provinces,
and cultivating a cosmopolitan Greco-Roman culture. Resented by the Senate (who deified him only
under pressure from Antoninus), he was the architect of the Antonine peace.
