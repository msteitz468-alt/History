# Pericles

## Overview

**Pericles** (c. 495–429 BCE), son of Xanthippus and the [[alcmaeonids|Alcmaeonid]]
Agariste, was the leading statesman of Athens at the height of its empire and democracy — the
man whose name the age bears. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 4, Rhodes) traces his
rise as the associate of [[ephialtes]] and architect of the mature **[[athenian-democracy]]**;
Thucydides, who admired him, called Periclean Athens "in name a democracy, but in fact rule by
the first man" (ii.65.9).

## The democratic leader

Pericles entered politics on the democratic side — *choregos* to Aeschylus' *Persae* in 472,
a prosecutor of [[cimon]], and a supporter of [[ephialtes|Ephialtes']] reform of the
Areopagus (462/1). After Ephialtes' murder and the ostracism of Cimon he became the dominant
figure, completing the democracy with two measures the *Athenaion Politeia* records:

- **Jury pay** (*misthos*), introduced to let poor citizens serve on the *dikasteria* and to
  rival Cimon's private generosity with the public purse — the first salary for civic duty,
  and the financial key to the participatory democracy.
- The **citizenship law of 451/0**, restricting Athenian citizenship to those with **both an
  Athenian father and an Athenian mother**. Variously read (a check on the citizen rolls, a
  curb on aristocratic foreign marriages, a defining of the privileges of empire), it
  tightened — did not democratize away — the boundary of the citizen body.

Thucydides son of Melesias organized the conservative opposition to Pericles' use of allied
tribute for the building program, but was **ostracized** c. 443, after which Pericles held a
"continuous and single" predominance through repeated election as **general** (*strategos*),
the one office that conferred real, re-electable leadership in the democracy.

## Empire, war, and death

Pericles directed the [[delian-league|empire]] at its zenith: the diversion of surplus
tribute to the **[[parthenon|Parthenon and the Acropolis program]]**, the suppression of the
**Samian revolt** (440), the negotiation of the **[[thirty-years-peace-446bce|Thirty Years'
Peace]]** with Sparta (446/5), and finally the strategy with which Athens entered the
**[[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]]** (431).

His **war strategy** ([[archidamian-war|Archidamian War]]) was revolutionary and defensive:
**abandon the Attic countryside**, evacuate the population behind the **Long Walls**, decline
hoplite battle, and rely on the **fleet**, the **allies**, and the imperial **revenue** —
meeting invasion with seaborne **raids**, not pitched battle, until Sparta accepted the
*status quo*. He delivered the **Funeral Oration** over the first year's dead (Thucydides'
vision of Athens as "the education of Greece"). But the strategy concentrated the population
just as the **[[plague-of-athens-430bce|plague]]** struck (430): it killed perhaps a quarter
of Athens, turned the city briefly against him (he was fined and deposed, then re-elected),
and carried off **Pericles himself in 429**. Thucydides judged Periclean Athens "in name a
democracy, but in fact rule by the first man," and thought no successor could control the
Assembly as he had — a loss that opened the way to [[cleon]] and the later disasters of the
war.

## Significance

Pericles fused **radical democracy** and **maritime empire** into a single political system
and gave it its self-image — the Funeral Oration's vision of Athens as "an education to
Greece." Whether he was a far-sighted statesman (Thucydides) or a demagogue who led Athens
into an unnecessary war (the rival tradition Plutarch could not reconcile) is the oldest
controversy about him.
