# Basil Liddell Hart

Sir Basil Liddell Hart (1895–1970) was a British military journalist, historian, and
theorist whose thought was, like that of [[fuller-jfc|J. F. C. Fuller]], "shaped... by
his experiences in the Great War (he had been gassed and wounded at the Somme) and his
determination that future wars should avoid the sort of mindless slaughter he had
witnessed." Fuller was "the more original and powerful thinker, but not always the most
accessible"; Liddell Hart "had a crisper style," and despite "some poor calls in the
run-up to the Second World War, his reputation grew after that war." His standing rose
partly because he supported a new generation of civilian strategists and academic
military historians, partly because his ideas on [[limited-war|limited war]] "gained
traction as thermonuclear weapons gave new meaning to the idea of [[total-war|total
war]]." He was also "a relentless propagandizer on his own behalf," even claiming that
the tragedy of the Second World War was that British generals neglected his ideas on
armored warfare while German generals "turned them into the blitzkrieg." After his death
in 1970 "his history was challenged and his self-promotion rebuked," but his central idea,
the **[[indirect-approach|indirect approach]]**, kept gaining adherents in business and
military circles alike.

## Strategic Thought

Freedman stresses that Liddell Hart's early work "was wholly derivative." He had hailed
Fuller's *The Reformation of War* as "the book of the century," had read T. E. Lawrence's
1920 *Army Quarterly* presentation, and "appears... to have drawn on the work of
[[corbett-julian|Julian Corbett]] as well." Following Fuller, he "adopted the analogy of
the brain controlling the body to call for attacks on the enemy's communications and
command centers." His "appeal for an 'indirect approach' as the 'most hopeful and
economic form of strategy' struck a chord with those who believed that cleverness was
preferable to brute force." Unlike Fuller, he asserted his own originality by contrasting
the indirect with a direct approach "which he claimed to be [[clausewitz-carl-von|
Clausewitz]]'s terrible legacy."

That polemic against Clausewitz defines much of his profile. Liddell Hart "blamed
Clausewitz, or at least his followers," for the conviction "that everything must be geared
to decisive battles with the sole aim of destroying the enemy army through frontal
assaults." He pinned the futile mass offensives of the Western Front on Clausewitz, "the
'evil genius of military thought,'" and wrote furiously of "the Ghost of Napoleon";
Clausewitz's "gospel deprived strategy of its laurels." Eventually he conceded "that the
differences between Clausewitz's view of war and his own were not large—they both
understood that it was an extension of politics and influenced by psychology as much as
brute force," but he held that *On War*'s density made it likely to be misread "as an
incitement to early battle." Late in his career, introducing Samuel Griffith's translation
of [[sun-tzu|Sun Tzu]]'s *The Art of War*, he set Sun Tzu's "realism and moderation"
against "Clausewitz's tendency to emphasize the logical ideal and 'absolute'" that drove
disciples to "the theory and practice of total war beyond all bounds of sense." He dated
his first awareness of Sun Tzu to a China contact in 1927, finding "many other points that
coincided with my own lines of thought, especially his constant emphasis on doing the
unexpected and pursuing the indirect approach."

Judging Clausewitz's account "too narrow, too battle-focused," Liddell Hart defined
strategy as "the art of distributing and employing military means to fulfill the ends of
policy." Policy's ends, he held, came from the level of [[grand-strategy|grand strategy]],
"where all instruments of policy were weighed, one against each other, and where it was
necessary to look beyond the war to the subsequent peace"; tactics began where "the
application of the military instrument merges into actual fighting." The aim of war was "to
subdue the enemy's will to resist, with the least possible human and economic loss to
itself," and—following Sun Tzu—"the perfection of strategy would be, therefore, to produce
a decision without any serious fighting." On this his thought converged with
[[deterrence|deterrence]]: a strategy that made defeat appear inevitable before battle
"pointed to" persuading the adversary to avoid the contest. Yet he met "very real
constraints" on the indirect approach, concluding on the eve of the Second World War that a
well-organized defense might be more potent than a maneuvering offensive, and ultimately
"that very little useful purpose could be served by war."

## In Freedman's argument

For Freedman, Liddell Hart is an exemplar—alongside [[sun-tzu|Sun Tzu]]—of the tradition
that "celebrated the subtle intelligence over brute force," prizing cunning and dislocation
over decisive battle. Freedman is pointedly skeptical of the result. Liddell Hart "claimed
that his theory developed through a careful examination of the whole of military history,"
but "his approach to history was intuitive and eclectic rather than... 'scientific.'" The
decisive critique Freedman cites is Brian Bond's: Liddell Hart "came extremely close to a
circular argument: by his definition, a 'decisive victory' was an event which is secured by
an 'indirect approach.'" Freedman also notes the tension with Fuller: though "often seen as
intellectual twins," they disagreed, for Fuller "saw no problem in taking the direct route
if that would have the desired effect"—"Where Liddell Hart was dogmatic, Fuller was
pragmatic. Liddell Hart wanted to avoid battle; for Fuller, it was the likely source of
victory." And though "celebrated as an apostle of maneuver," the campaigns Liddell Hart
admired "were often attritional, in that they required wearing down the opponent"—a
quiet concession to [[annihilation-and-exhaustion|exhaustion]] beneath the rhetoric of the
indirect approach.
