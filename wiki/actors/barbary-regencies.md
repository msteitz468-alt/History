# The Barbary Regencies (Ottoman North Africa)

The **Barbary Regencies** — **Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli** — were the semi-autonomous Ottoman provinces of
the Maghrib coast in the early-modern centuries, famous in European memory for their **corsairs**.
Documented for the wiki through the **UNESCO [[gha-vol5-16th-18th-century|General History of Africa, Vol.
V]]** (chs. 6, 9), which (with Egypt and the Funj Sudan) sets North Africa within the Ottoman world while
stressing the Maghrib's continuing **African** connections across the Sahara.

## Ottoman conquest and the regencies

After the **Ottoman conquest of Mamluk Egypt (1517)**, Ottoman power extended west along the coast. At
**Algiers (1516)** the corsair brothers **Aruj and Khayr al-Din Barbarossa** placed the city under Ottoman
protection against Spanish pressure, founding the **Regency of Algiers**; **Tunis** and **Tripoli** followed
as Ottoman regencies. In all three, real power soon passed from the distant Porte to local military
oligarchies — the **deys** of Algiers (backed by the Janissary *ojaq*), the **beys** of Tunis (the
Husaynids), and the **Karamanli** pashas of Tripoli — increasingly independent in practice.

## The corsair economy

GHA V describes the regencies' distinctive **corsair (privateering) economy**: state-licensed fleets preyed
on Mediterranean Christian shipping, taking ships, cargoes, and **captives for ransom or enslavement** — the
basis of a famous and lucrative, if precarious, political economy that reached its **golden age in the
1600s**. (The same coast was, in reverse, the northern terminus of the **[[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan
trade]]** in gold, slaves, and goods from the Sudan.) European naval power and treaties gradually curbed
the corsairs in the eighteenth century.

## Morocco apart

GHA V stresses that **Morocco remained independent** of the Ottomans — ruled by the **Saadi** sharifs and
then the **Alawi** dynasty (which still reigns). It was the Saadi sultan **Ahmad al-Mansur** who, hungry for
West African gold, sent the musket army across the Sahara that destroyed the **[[songhay-empire|Songhay
empire]] at Tondibi (1591)** — the most dramatic North African intervention in sub-Saharan history.

## Significance

The regencies show North Africa as an **Ottoman-Mediterranean** world that nonetheless stayed tied to the
Sahara and the Sudan; Morocco's independence and its conquest of Songhay mark the moment a North African
power reshaped the West African political map. The French seizure of **Algiers in 1830** would open the
colonial era in the Maghrib.
