## Overview

Honami Kōetsu (1558–1637) was the most versatile and celebrated artist-craftsman of early Tokugawa Japan. His family were hereditary sword connoisseurs and polishers — "the standard authorities on the connoisseurship of swords" — a lineage stretching back to a progenitor who died in 1352. Kōetsu inherited this role but far transcended it: he was reckoned, alongside Shokado Shōjō and Konoe Nobutsuna (or his father), as one of the "Three Pens" (finest calligraphists) of his day, while excelling equally in painting, lacquer work, pottery, landscape gardening, the tea ceremony, bronze casting, sculpture, Japanese poetry, literature, Noh masks, and sand pictures. He also published books on handmade paper, designing both illustrations and binding. Sadler describes him as "perhaps the most versatile artist in modern Japanese history" and compares him to William Morris — while noting that "it would be more correct to say that Morris was the English Kō-etsu."

## The Takagamine Art Village

In 1615, following the Osaka Summer Campaign, Ieyasu learned through the Kyoto governor Itakura that Kōetsu "is tired of living in the capital on account of people there who differ from him in their views." Ieyasu responded by presenting him with a substantial estate at Takagamine, described as lying on the Omi-Tamba road northwest of Kyoto — framed by Ieyasu as a double benefit: settling a dangerous road frequented by footpads while giving Kōetsu a suitable home.

Kōetsu laid out his house and garden on 120 yards of frontage and allotted adjacent land to family, relations, and craftsmen. The previously uninhabited area "soon became a flourishing village of craftsmen and littérateurs. And the thieves, not liking such company, ceased to haunt it, as no doubt Ieyasu had intended, wishing as always to kill two birds with one stone." He and his family built four small temples in the village, including the Jōsōji (Hokke sect), the Myōshuji (his mother's memorial), and the Taikuan (later Ko-etsuji), to which he retired in his seventies.

A contemporary plan of the Takagamine village survives, showing the land allotments — an unusual piece of early Edo urban documentation.

## Views on Politics and Governance

Though uninvolved in politics, Kōetsu expressed himself freely at a discussion recorded at the mansion of Itakura Shigemune, at which Matsudaira Izu-no-kami was present. His views were blunt:

- He admired Ieyasu and was a severe critic of Hideyoshi, arguing that "it is not seemly that a man should rise from the people to rule the Empire in one lifetime. Such a process should take several generations."
- He considered that farmers could produce good soldiers and statesmen, but "tradesmen have no capacity to engage in government. Government should not be too severe, for the people of this Empire are high-spirited, and will commit suicide."
- He expressed loyalty to the Emperor and concern that the Court nobility's tiny stipends (as low as 30 koku for some) forced them into inappropriate behavior rather than their proper life of "poetry and aesthetics."
- He opposed verse-making for warriors, warning against the influence of Chinese poets like Li Po and Tu Fu who glorified drinking.

## Circle and Contemporaries

Kōetsu's social network epitomized the early Edo cultural world: the tea-masters Furuta Oribe, Kobori Enshu, and Oda Yuraku; the Kanō painters (Sanraku, Sotan, Sosetsu, Mitsunobu, Sansetsu, Tannyu, Doun); wealthy merchants (Chaya Shirojiro, Suminokura Soan); the Confucian scholar Hayashi Razan; and great daimyos including Maeda of Kaga and the two Kyoto governors Itakura Katsushige and Shigemune.

## Significance

Kōetsu represents the distinctive synthesis of warrior patronage, Buddhist sensibility, and artisan excellence that characterized the early Tokugawa cultural settlement. His Takagamine village is an instance of Ieyasu's characteristic governance method: using a cultural appointment to simultaneously reward a valued figure, solve a practical problem (unsafe roads), and create a productive community. His political views as recorded by Sadler offer a rare contemporary critique of Hideyoshi from someone outside the political class who had lived through both regimes.
