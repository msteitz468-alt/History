# Augustinian Canons (Canons Regular of St Augustine)

The **Augustinian canons** (Canons Regular) were, with the [[cistercian-order|Cistercians]], the chief new religious form of the twelfth-century Latin West in [[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]]’s account. They were **canons, not monks**: communities of clergy living under a shortened **Rule of St Augustine** (text circulating by **c. 1118**) that offered **general principles**, a moderate life allowing meat, room for intellectual work, and — crucially — **flexibility**.

## Character and Ministry

Logan stresses that the canons “more than any of the other new orders of monks constituted a **break with past forms** of the religious life.” Flexibility was the Rule’s greatest asset. Houses were often modest: **hundreds** across Germany, France, Spain, and Italy; **England** alone had **300+ priories** (e.g. **St Bartholomew’s, Smithfield**). Typical works included **parish service**, poor relief, and (in Spain) ransom of captives. The young **[[thomas-becket|Thomas Becket]]** studied around age ten with Augustinian canons at **Merton Priory** (founded **1114**).

## Trajectory

By century’s end Logan judges Augustinians to have settled into **comfortable routine** — still valuable, but diverted from early reforming edge, like most peers except the [[carthusian-order|Carthusians]]. Related stricter variants include the [[premonstratensian-order|Premonstratensians]] and the scholarly **Victorines** of Paris.
