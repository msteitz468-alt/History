## Overview

Takeda Harunobu, known by his Buddhist name Shingen (Archbishop Shingen), was the lord of Kai and Shinano provinces and one of the most formidable military commanders of the [[sengoku-period]]. His house had held a fief in the mountain provinces of central Honshu since the twelfth century. Sadler describes him as "reckoned to be among the first two or three soldiers of the time, and a wise and progressive administrator as well."

Around age thirty he took Buddhist vows and shaved his head, "taking the name of Archbishop Shingen, for he was fond of learning and meditation, but he did not become less acquisitive on that account or any milder in his methods" (Sadler). His Buddhism was genuine but entirely compatible with aggressive territorial expansion and ruthless political calculation.

## Career and Conflicts

Shingen broke with his former ally Imagawa Ujizane by putting to death his own eldest son Yoshinobu — who had married Ujizane's daughter — and then allying with [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] to carve up Imagawa's territories: Ieyasu would take Tōtōmi, Shingen would take Suruga. He burned Ujizane's capital of Sumpu, though a subsequent clash with the Hojo forced him to retreat temporarily.

He maintained a chronic adversarial relationship with Uesugi Kenshin to his north, fighting periodic battles at Kawanakashima. This rivalry constrained his freedom to move west toward the capital. He also cultivated strategic ties with the Hongwanji Monto monks — he was brother-in-law to Lord Abbot Kennyo Kosa — in order to have them restrain Kenshin while he campaigned against Ieyasu and Nobunaga.

Shingen attempted to have Ieyasu assassinated by placing a young man in his household, but Ieyasu's habit of early morning devotions caused him to be absent from his bed when the assassin struck.

## Western Campaign and Death

In October 1572 Shingen launched his major western campaign with approximately twenty thousand men plus a Hojo contingent, marching into Tōtōmi and capturing Ieyasu's stronghold of Futamata. He defeated Ieyasu decisively at the [[battle-of-mikatagahara-1573]] in January 1573 but declined to press the assault on Hamamatsu castle, uncertain of Ieyasu's remaining strength and wary of Nobunaga moving against his rear. He then besieged the fortress of Noda in Mikawa, where he fell ill — or, in a more dramatic account, was shot in the head by a sharpshooter while listening to flute music from within the castle — and died in April 1573.

His death relieved both Nobunaga and Ieyasu of their most dangerous opponent. As Sadler observes: "A great load was lifted from the shoulders of Nobunaga and Ieyasu."

## Significance

Shingen's death left his son Katsuyori as the Takeda leader. Where Shingen combined fearlessness with shrewdness and patience, Katsuyori possessed only the fearlessness, which "unqualified did him no good" (Sadler). The contrast between the two men is central to the narrative of Takeda decline and helps explain the catastrophic defeat at [[battle-of-nagashino-1575]].

Shingen's comment on learning of Ieyasu's frugality with the peaches from Nobunaga — "Ieyasu cherishes great hopes for the future, and so he takes great care of his health" — shows that he recognized his rival's qualities even while opposing him.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had Shingen not died in 1573, he would likely have resumed his western campaign the following year with an even larger force. His coordination with the Monto monks, Matsunaga Hisahide, and potentially the Shogun Yoshiaki represented the most serious threat to the Oda-Tokugawa alliance during the Sengoku period. His removal was, as Sadler bluntly notes, "most convenient" for Ieyasu.

## Historiography

Sadler's account draws on Japanese chronicle sources compiled under Tokugawa auspices, which had an interest in portraying Shingen as formidable (to magnify the achievement of defeating him) but ultimately flawed. The romantically detailed account of his death by flute-music is flagged by Sadler himself as "picturesque though not necessarily more trustworthy." Modern Japanese scholarship on Shingen is extensive; the collection's coverage is thin beyond Sadler's narrative.
