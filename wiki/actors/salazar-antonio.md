# António de Oliveira Salazar (1889–1970)

Coimbra professor of economics and law who became Portuguese finance
minister (1928) and prime minister (1932–1968), constructing the **Estado
Novo** — at 1926/33–1974 a 48-year dictatorship, the longest-lived rightist
authoritarian system in twentieth-century western Europe, and in
[[payne-typology-of-authoritarian-nationalism|Payne's typology]] the type
case of the **conservative authoritarian right**, not fascism.

## The Estado Novo (Payne, 1980)

Salazar drew "mainly from right-wing Catholic corporative doctrine." His
1933 corporative constitution "was designed to create the first complete
authoritarian corporative state in the world, more thorough than the
contemporary Italian system, at that point still in transition from
partial national syndicalism to corporatism." His state political
organization, the **National Union**, was however like Primo de Rivera's
"formalistic Patriotic Union, not a radical new mass movement or even a
true parti unique." Payne classes the regime as "essentially of the
interwar east-European type, corporative, institutionalized, systematically
authoritarian, but not viciously repressive, and without any direct
fascist party component" — yet "one of the most fully institutionalized of
all the interwar authoritarian regimes (partly explaining its longevity)."
Notably, Payne's 1980 "three faces" chapter flags the *early* Estado Novo
as an exception even to the generalization that all three authoritarian
sectors pursued economic development.

Portuguese precursors: João Franco's "legal dictatorship" of 1907–8 (rule
by decree on "the dubious authority of the crown alone" — motto *pouca
política, muita administração*); Sidónio Pais's presidential República
Nova (1917–18); and the Integralist movement, a direct imitation of Action
Française.

## Suppression of Portuguese fascism

The one genuine fascist movement in Portugal, Rolão Preto's blue-shirted
**National Syndicalists** (Preto, like Georges Valois, an ex-Integralist
"disillusioned with the reactionary slant" of neomonarchism), was
suppressed by Salazar, who exiled Preto in 1934, denouncing the fascistic
"exaltation of youth, and the cult of force through direct action, the
principle of the superiority of state political power in social life, the
propensity for organizing masses behind a single leader." In 1935 the
National Syndicalists attempted a coup **in alliance with Portuguese
anarchosyndicalists** — Payne: "proving that the allies of fascism might be
found on the extreme left as well as the extreme right" — and were easily
crushed. See [[fascism-and-the-political-left]].

## "A fascism without a fascist movement"?

Manuel de Lucena argues the Estado Novo "more nearly resembled that of
Mussolini than did any other in Europe" — *um fascismo sem movimento
fascista* — citing six parallels: a dictator functioning as prime minister
without being head of state; a corporative economic structure culminating
in a full corporative chamber; an authoritarian police state; a
bureaucratized, nonautonomous, essentially nonrevolutionary state
political organization; semipluralist autonomy for influential social
sectors; and an imperialist foreign policy. Payne endorses this only "up
to a point": "the formal parallels with Fascist Italy do exist, though
they are obviously far from complete and are contradicted by other
factors" — above all, that the regime's goal "was depoliticization from
the very beginning," in a partially illiterate, unmobilized society.

## Fascistization and reversal

During the Spanish Civil War years (1936–39) the regime joined the general
Iberian "fascistizing" tendency: more overt authoritarianism, an expanded
National Union, a new political militia (the **National Legion**), and a
national youth movement, both using the fascist salute. The trend was
arrested by WWII and reversed after 1945, when "the Salazar regime moved
much more fully and rapidly than the Franco regime into a nonmobilized
bureaucratic authoritarianism" — though the National Legion was not fully
disbanded until the regime's overthrow in 1974. Payne's summary: the
regime "did not contain a major fascist component from the start as did
that of Franco... Yet it too was influenced by the fascist vertigo... so
that in the proper circumstance it too might have taken a more overtly
fascist form."

## Counterfactual significance

Salazar demonstrates the conservative-right pole of Payne's typology at
maximum institutional success: demobilizing Catholic corporatism proved
*more* durable than either fascism (destroyed 1945) or Franco's
semifascist hybrid (defascistized from 1942). In Payne's seven-type regime
taxonomy the Estado Novo sits in category 6 (conservative or
bureaucratic-nationalist authoritarianism) alongside Vargas's Brazil,
Piłsudski's Poland, and Horthy's Hungary.

## Historiography

Key scholarship cited by Payne (1980): Manuel de Lucena, *A evolução do
sistema corporativo português* (1976); Howard Wiarda, *Corporatism and
Development: The Portuguese Experience* (1977); Philippe Schmitter,
*Corporatism and Public Policy in Authoritarian Portugal* (1975); João
Medina, *Salazar e os fascistas* (1979). The live interpretive question is
Lucena's fascism-without-a-movement thesis versus the standard
conservative-corporatist classification; Payne splits the difference.
