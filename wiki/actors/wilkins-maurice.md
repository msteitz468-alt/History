# Maurice Wilkins

Maurice Hugh Frederick Wilkins (15 December 1916 – 5 October 2004) was a New Zealand-born British physicist and molecular biologist. He is best known for his research at King's College London on the structure of DNA, for which he was co-awarded the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, alongside James Watson and Francis Crick.

## Role and Significance

Wilkins played a pivotal, though highly controversial, role in the discovery of the double-helical structure of DNA. Working in the Medical Research Council (MRC) Biophysics Unit at King's College London, he was among the first to obtain high-quality X-ray diffraction diagrams of DNA fibres, collaborating initially with Raymond Gosling. His subsequent sharing of Photo 51 (taken by Gosling and Franklin) with James Watson in January 1953 was the critical catalyst that mobilized Watson and Crick to restart their model-building efforts.

## Key Events and Activities

### Early DNA Crystals (1950)
At a Faraday Society meeting in May 1950, Wilkins met the Swiss scientist Rudolf Signer, who provided him with a highly purified, high-molecular-weight gel of DNA. Analyzing this "Signer DNA" in conjunction with Raymond Gosling, Wilkins produced the first fiber diffraction patterns showing that DNA possessed a highly ordered, crystalline structure. Recognizing the potential of this work, he suggested to John Randall that they recruit a specialist crystallographer—Rosalind Franklin—to analyze the fibers.

### The Conflict with Rosalind Franklin (1951–1953)
Upon Franklin's arrival in January 1951, a major division of labor conflict arose. Randall had privately reassigned all DNA X-ray effort to Franklin, a letter Wilkins never saw. Wilkins expected Franklin to act as a technical collaborator in his ongoing project, whereas Franklin assumed sole charge of the DNA crystallography. The resulting temperamental clash—Franklin's fast, argumentative debate versus Wilkins's slow, non-confrontational, and quiet evasion—led to a complete breakdown in communication. In late 1951, Franklin ordered him to "go back to his microscopes" and give up X-ray work.

To resolve the impasse, Randall partitioned the research: Franklin was assigned the crystalline A-form (which she chose to solve mathematically using Patterson function maps), while Wilkins was assigned the paracrystalline B-form. Wilkins subsequently asked Bruce Fraser to build a three-chain model in late 1951, which Franklin rejected due to its lack of hydration.

### Sharing of Photo 51 and the Nobel Prize (1953–1962)
In January 1953, Raymond Gosling, preparing to complete his thesis, handed over Photo 51 to Wilkins. Wilkins showed the photograph to Watson, revealing the unmistakable helical symmetry of the B-form. Following Watson and Crick's completion of the double-helix model in March 1953, Wilkins traveled to Cambridge to view the model and agreed to separate publications in *Nature* rather than joint authorship. 

In 1962, Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Watson and Crick. Sir Lawrence Bragg had lobbied strongly for Wilkins's inclusion, noting that the Nobel rules limited the prize to three co-recipients and wishing to heal the long-standing bitterness Wilkins had felt.
