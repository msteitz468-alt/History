## Overview

Fujiwara no Mototsune (836–891) was the adopted son and political heir of [[fujiwara-yoshifusa|Fujiwara no Yoshifusa]], the founder of the Fujiwara regency. Where Yoshifusa established the office ad hoc, Mototsune consolidated it institutionally, serving as regent through four successive imperial reigns and introducing both the *kampaku* title (for regency over an adult emperor) and a clearer conceptual separation of the regental office from ordinary Council of State positions. He was head of the Northern House of the [[fujiwara-clan|Fujiwara clan]] and the dominant figure at the Heian court from Yoshifusa's death in 872 until his own death in 891.

## The Institutional Problem Mototsune Solved

Yoshifusa had demonstrated that a Fujiwara leader could exercise regental power, but the office remained undefined and dependent on ad hoc imperial recognition. Mototsune faced the structural challenge of how to define a position that "stood apart from and superior to the regular statutory organs of government but was not rendered ineffective by isolation from them" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 77). His solution was "basically the same as Yoshifusa's: a combination of the ad hoc office of regent with a statutory ministerial post."

A key innovation came from Mototsune's appointment as regent in 876 while holding the title of Minister of the Right: after this, the chancellorship (*daijō daijin*) was clearly differentiated from the regental position, allowing the two to be held by different men simultaneously. Until Mototsune's day, the Fujiwara clan chieftainship had been held by whoever had the highest statutory office, regardless of who served as regent; from Kaneie onward (following precedents Mototsune established), the regental position was fully independent.

## The Kampaku Title and the Akō Controversy

In 887, during the reign of Emperor Uda (who had no Fujiwara maternal connections and was disposed against the regency), Mototsune was appointed *kampaku* — the first formal use of this title to designate a regent over an adult emperor. The *kampaku* acted for the emperor in his dealings with the Council of State but was "not otherwise empowered to represent him" and "remained a minister to the emperor" — a formulation that contrasted with the *sesshō*, who "in effect became the emperor" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 77).

The Akō Controversy of 887 arose from a dispute over the exact wording of Mototsune's appointment document — a seemingly technical quarrel that in fact concerned the scope and nature of regental authority. The controversy remained "otherwise rather mysterious" in the CHJ's assessment; it may have been connected to the broader problem of defining the Fujiwara leader's position. Mototsune won the dispute.

## Ritual-Ceremonial Legacy

Mototsune's ritual observances became the foundation of two rival ceremonial schools (*ryū*) that structured court protocol for generations: the Ononomiya school (founded through his son Saneyori, 900–970) and the Kujo school (through his son Morosuke, 908–960). These schools were not mere formalism; knowledge of ritual-ceremonial precedent was "the sine qua non of a successful career at court" (CHJ Vol. 2, p. 100). Michinaga later synthesized elements of both into the Mido school.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Mototsune's institutional consolidation, the Fujiwara regency might have remained what it was under Yoshifusa — an ad hoc arrangement dependent on the personal authority of a single exceptional figure. Mototsune's creation of the *kampaku* title and his separation of the regental office from ordinary Council positions gave the system enough structural definition to survive the "anti-Fujiwara" interlude under Emperor Uda (891–897) and resume under Tadahira.
