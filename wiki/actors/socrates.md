# Socrates

Athenian philosopher (c. 470–399 BCE). Father of Western ethical and political philosophy. Known through the writings of [[plato|Plato]], Xenophon, and [[aristophanes|Aristophanes]]. Developed the elenchus (Socratic method of questioning) to expose ignorance and pursue definitions of virtues (justice, piety, courage, temperance). Served as hoplite; criticized Athenian democracy and [[sophists|sophists]]. Tried and executed in 399 BCE on charges of impiety and corrupting the youth (refused to escape or compromise). Central figure in the intellectual life of Classical Athens and the transition from [[presocratics|Presocratic]] to systematic philosophy.

## OHCW (Annas, Murray, Parker)

[[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]], Ch. 10 (Julia Annas): the most important facts about Socrates were that he **lived uncompromisingly for philosophy** and was put to death on anti-intellectual grounds — charges of introducing new divinities and corrupting the youth. Unspoken political motives (links to anti-democratic aristocrats) likely mixed with genuine anti-intellectual hostility. Philosophical portraits diverge (Plato, Xenophon, Aristophanes); philosophically one must follow Plato while treating Platonic Socrates as **Plato’s creation**.

**What he did.** The cliché that Socrates first turned philosophy from science to ethics is **false** (ethics/politics already thrived); what he did was make philosophy **personal again** — ignoring Protagorean social theory and Anaxagorean matter theory to ask individuals whether they understand what they are talking about, demanding a rational “account,” claiming ignorance except as gadfly. A Heraclitean strand (hostilely depicted in Plato’s *Theaetetus*) already exalted self-understanding and *sophrosyne* over conventional cosmology — context for Socratic inwardness.

**Public conflation with sophists.** Murray (Ch. 9): by the 420s travelling sophists converged on Athens; Plato antithesizes them to Socrates, but contemporary opinion ([[aristophanes|Aristophanes’]] *Clouds*) **did not distinguish** them — scepticism, relativism, “make the worse seem the better cause.” Cleisthenic official designation: “Socrates son of Sophroniscus of the deme of Alopeke.”

**Religion.** Parker (Ch. 11): late-fifth-century “religious crisis” at Athens includes Protagoras’ agnosticism, sophistic genealogies of belief, Aristophanes’ anxiety, persecution traditions, and Socrates’ charge of “**not recognizing the gods that the city recognizes**.” “Recognizing the gods” meant cult observance (orthopraxy), not creed; there were no heretics because there was no Church. Crisis was “surmounted”: explicit atheism remained rare; Plato especially conservative about the divine.

## Freeman (2014) — the three-source problem and the trial's context

[[sources/freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014|Freeman (2014)]] frames the historiographical
caution around Socrates as three fundamentally different, non-neutral witness streams:
**Aristophanes'** hostile comic caricature (*The Clouds*), **Xenophon's** *Memorabilia*,
and **Plato's** Dialogues — written decades after Socrates' death and increasingly
ventriloquizing Plato's own views in the Middle and Late Dialogues. On the 399 BCE trial,
Freeman ties the impiety/corrupting-youth charges to lingering suspicion of Socrates'
aristocratic associations (notably [[alcibiades]]) after the rule of the **Thirty
Tyrants**, and gives the guilty verdict as **280 to 220**.
