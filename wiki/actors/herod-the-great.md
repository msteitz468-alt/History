# Herod the Great (king of Judaea)

The **client king *par excellence*** of the Augustan East — a half-Jewish **Idumaean**
who ruled Judaea as a Roman vassal, whose long, brilliant, and precarious reign
[[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] (Ch. 4, Gruen) makes the prime exhibit of the relationship
between Rome and its dependent kings. The "extensive testimony of **Josephus** affords a more
intimate glimpse into the affairs of Herod than we possess for any other dependent ruler."

## From Antony to Augustus

A chief **beneficiary of [[mark-antony|Antony]]**, Herod was confirmed king by the triumvir
and stayed loyal to him down to Actium (he was absent from the battle, fighting the
Nabataeans). Like so many eastern dynasts, he turned the disaster of his patron's defeat into
opportunity: he sought out **Octavian at Rhodes in 30 BCE** and took a frank line — the same
unswerving fidelity he had shown Antony he would now give Antony's conqueror. **Octavian
reaffirmed his royal status and enlarged his holdings** (the coast, Samaria, the Decapolis,
Jericho). The bargain of 30 set the pattern of the relationship.

## The model client king

Herod **anticipated his obligations**: he supplied troops for Aelius Gallus' Arabian
campaign, refounded cities in Augustus' honour (**Sebaste**, **Caesarea**), sent his sons to
Rome to be educated, and had his subjects swear an **oath of allegiance to the emperor**. His
friendship with **Agrippa** (whom he fêted lavishly in 15 BCE) further raised his standing,
and Augustus twice more enlarged his territory. His kingdom was apparently **exempt from
tribute**, and he was even granted the privilege of **naming his own successor** and a share
in the supervision of Syria.

Yet Gruen's theme is that **the tighter the bonds, the greater the dependency**. The very
fact that the right to appoint a successor had to be *explicitly granted* exposed the reality
of subordination. Herod would not settle even **disputes within his own household** without
the emperor's direction — the sordid court intrigues that led him to **execute three of his
own sons**, and his recurrent quarrels with the Nabataeans, were repeatedly carried to Rome
for adjudication, with Herod alternately falling out of and back into Augustus' favour. "His
reign was long and memorable — but always precarious."

## Death and partition

Herod's much-rewritten **will** (4 BCE) drew Rome still deeper in: it parcelled his realm
among three sons but provided huge sums for Augustus, Livia, and the imperial household and
specified that **nothing could take effect without the *princeps'* ratification**. The
"privilege" of naming a successor had thus become a mere **recommendation**. The sons brought
rival claims to Rome, while a separate Jewish embassy asked for the **abolition of the
monarchy**. Augustus settled it even-handedly: he upheld Herod's territorial division but
**withheld the royal title** — **Archelaus** to rule Judaea, Samaria and Idumaea as
*ethnarch*, **Antipas** and **Philip** as tetrarchs. The governor of Syria, **Varus**, used
force to crush the Jewish rebellion that broke out at Herod's death.

## Counterfactual / structural significance

Herod's career, Gruen argues, traces the **logical slide from client kingdom to province**.
By tying his realm ever more closely to the emperor to bolster his own power, Herod
*increased its dependence* — so that when complaints against the misrule of **Archelaus**
reached Rome in **AD 6**, Augustus could simply **depose** him, banish him to Gaul, and
**annex Judaea** as a Roman province under an equestrian prefect, the census of **Quirinius**
marking the new fiscal order. "Taxation and direct rule only formalized a continuing process
of implementing Roman authority in the East." His tetrarch sons (Antipas, Philip) survived as
the Herodians of the Gospel narratives.

## Historiography

Almost everything known comes from **Josephus** (*Antiquities*, *Jewish War*), himself
drawing on Herod's court historian **Nicolaus of Damascus** and on hostile traditions — a
record both unusually full and deeply tendentious, oscillating between the great builder and
the murderous tyrant. The **census of Quirinius** (AD 6) is famously misdated by Luke 2 to
the reign of Herod, a notorious chronological crux. Herod's relationship with Rome is the
classic case study (Braund) of the **Roman institution of client kingship**.

## Ball's Herod: an Arab dimension (2000)

Warwick Ball's *[[ball-rome-in-the-east-2000|Rome in the East]]* stresses Herod's mixed
ancestry: his Idumaean father **Antipater** married **Cypros**, of the
[[nabataean-kingdom|Nabataean]] royal house, making Herod half-Idumaean, half-Nabataean — and,
when the Parthians installed the Hasmonaean Antigonus (40 BCE), Herod fled first to the Nabataean
king at [[petra|Petra]]. Ball offers an economic thesis for Herod's extraordinary wealth: not
Judaea's own resources but the rebuilt Temple's power to draw remissions from the **Jewish
Diaspora**, especially the large community in Parthian Iran — "a one-way flow of wealth between
the two rival empires." His account continues past Herod to the direct Roman rule that provoked
the **[[first-jewish-roman-war-66-74|Great Revolt of 66–74]]** and the later
[[bar-kochba-war-132-135|Bar Kochba war]]; Ball argues the severity of Roman suppression reflected
strategic fear of the Diaspora as a potential Parthian "fifth column" rather than mere
anti-Judaism (his interpretation — see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]]).
