# Francisco Franco (1892–1975)

Spanish general who led the Nationalist side to victory in the
[[spanish-civil-war-1936|Spanish Civil War]] (1936–39) and ruled Spain as
Caudillo of [[francoist-spain|Francoist Spain]] until his death in 1975 — the longest-lived of the right-authoritarian
rulers of the fascist era. This summary now rests chiefly on Payne, *The Franco Regime* ([[sources/payne-franco-regime-1987|1987]]), the standard political history.

## Formation and Rise (Payne 1987)

Born 4 December 1892 in El Ferrol, Galicia, to a pious mother and an agnostic, freethinking naval-paymaster father who abandoned the family — and who later mocked his son's cult as absurd, was pro-Jewish, and found the anti-Masonry mania ridiculous ("What does he know about Masonry?"). Franco rose through the colonial Army of Africa: seriously wounded 1916, major before 24, commander of the Foreign Legion (1923), leader of the Alhucemas landing (1925), brigadier at 33 — "reportedly the youngest general on active duty in any European army." Director of the Zaragoza military academy under [[primo-de-rivera-miguel|Primo de Rivera]]; technical coordinator of the bloody suppression of the 1934 Asturias rising; Chief of the General Staff (1935). Payne stresses his legalism before 1936: he twice refused to back a coup (December 1935, February 1936) and wrote to the prime minister as late as 23 June 1936 defending the Army's loyalty; the assassination of Calvo Sotelo finally decided him. His political beliefs — nationalism, unity, Catholicism, anti-party authoritarianism, politically directed development — formed in the decade 1926–36 and changed little; the program of Calvo Sotelo's radical right "most nearly anticipated the guidelines of the regime."

## Power (1936–1975)

Elevated Generalísimo and Chief of State by the Junta de Defensa (September–October 1936, the "war's-duration" limit quietly dropped from the decree), he fused Falangists and Carlists into the FET by decree (April 1937), defining himself in its statutes as "supreme Caudillo... responsible [only] before God and history." The August 1939 Ley de la Jefatura made his powers "permanently confided" — in formal theory, Payne notes, a more direct personal dictatorship than the USSR, Italy, or Germany. He presided over the [[francoist-repression-1936|repression]] (personally reviewing capital sentences from 1937, initialing *E* or *C*), aligned with the Axis as "non-belligerent" (1940–42, see [[hendaye-meeting-1940]]), defascistized after 1943 ("I shall not make the same mistake as Primo de Rivera. There will be no resignation: from here only to the cemetery"), survived UN ostracism, and reinvented the regime through the technocrats and the [[spanish-economic-miracle|desarrollo]] after 1957–59 — a Stabilization Plan in which he initially had "not the slightest confidence." Governing style: ministerial latitude with total distrust ("I must tell you that I don't really trust anyone"), marathon Friday cabinets, deliberate toleration of corruption as a binding mechanism, trivialization of dissent, obsessive anti-Masonism (pseudonymous articles as "Jakin Boor"), and — from the 1950s — mounting complacency (17 of 30 days hunting in November 1955) and Parkinson's disease (from c. 1961). He designated [[juan-carlos-i|Juan Carlos]] successor (1969: "todo está atado y bien atado"), lost [[carrero-blanco-luis|Carrero Blanco]] to ETA (1973, sobbing openly at the funeral), and died 20 November 1975 ([[death-of-franco-1975]]).

## Classification and Counterfactual Significance

The canonical case, in [[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|Payne's]] typology, of a *conservative
authoritarian* ruler subordinating and taming a genuine fascist movement
rather than embodying one; Payne (1987) calls the 1937–45 regime "doctrinally at least a semifascist state" and stresses that Franco was "tempted by some of the more radical ambitions of fascism before 1943, temptations to which he never fully succumbed." His nearest prototype, per Payne, was Napoleon Bonaparte (referendum, dyarchic monarchist formulas) — caudillaje as "elective but absolute monarchy" resting on a privately avowed "right of conquest... not altogether different from that of a sixteenth-century conquistador"; there is no evidence of Latin American influence. On WWII: Payne's verdict is that Franco was genuinely ready to enter on Hitler's side in 1940–41 at his price — the decision to keep Spain out "was initially Hitler's, not Franco's" — making the Hendaye failure, not Francoist prudence alone, the hinge for the Mediterranean war. Payne's summary: Franco's importance "lies not in his mere endurance in power for so long, but rather in the enormous changes that took place in Spain during his rule, some of them directly encouraged or even engineered by his regime and others ultimately flying in the face of all that it stood for."

## Historiography

Payne (1987, 1995) treats Francoism as
non-fascist authoritarianism; opposing scholarship (and Republican-exile
tradition) has treated it as clerical fascism. Griffin
([[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]]) classifies the regime as
[[para-fascism|para-fascist]] — a conservative order that simulated
fascism's style while neutralizing the genuine article by absorbing the
[[falange|Falange]] into FET y de las JONS (1937). Preston's biography (1993, post-dating Payne) is markedly more hostile on the repression and on Franco's WWII intentions. See
[[generic-fascism]] for the boundary debate. Contemporary pen-portraits preserved by Payne cut both ways: Hoare's "Brer Rabbit of dictators," Whitaker's "a less straightforward man I never met," Hitler's "Latin charlatan."

## Payne (1980)

*Fascism: Comparison and Definition* classes early Franquism as **semifascist** — a genuine but subordinated fascist component inside "a right-wing, pretorian, Catholic, and semipluralist structure" — with defascistization from 1942 and the full argument at [[francoist-spain]] and [[falange]]. The caudillaje leadership doctrine, Payne notes (ch. 1), "was introduced by the nationalist right in the person of Franco and imposed on the fascists" — the inverse of the Führerprinzip's origin inside the movement.
