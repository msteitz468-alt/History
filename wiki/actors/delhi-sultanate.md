# The Delhi Sultanate

The **Delhi Sultanate** (1206–1526) was the first enduring Muslim state in India — a
Turkish/Afghan-ruled power that dominated the north and, at its height, much of the subcontinent,
and the political frame within which Hindu and Islamic society first had to **accommodate** each
other. Documented for the wiki through Romila Thapar's
[[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966|*A History of India*, Vol. I]] (1966).

## Founding and the five dynasties

The Sultanate was founded after **Muhammad Ghuri's** defeat of the [[rajputs|Rajput]] Prithviraja at
the **second battle of Tarain (1192)**; on Ghuri's death (1206) his general **Qutb-ud-din Aibak**
made the Indian conquests a permanent state rather than (as with [[mahmud-of-ghazni|Mahmud]]) a
raiding-ground. Thapar's narrative runs through the five dynasties:
- **The Slave/Mamluk dynasty** (1206–90): consolidated by **Iltutmish** (who received investiture
  from the Caliph, making the Sultan nominally the Caliph's viceroy) and **Balban**.
- **The Khaljis** (1290–1320): the **apogee** under **Ala-ud-din Khalji**.
- **The Tughluqs** (1320–1414): **Muhammad bin Tughluq's** bold, failed experiments and **Firuz
  Shah's** pious reaction; shattered by **Timur's sack of Delhi (1398)**.
- **The Sayyids** (1414–51) and **Lodis** (1451–1526, "of pure Afghan" stock) — a diminished
  Sultanate of breakaway provinces, ended by Babur in 1526 (treated in Vol. II).

## The state: the iqta system

Thapar shows the Sultanate's structure was a **Turkish-Islamic overlay on the pre-existing agrarian
order**. The Sultan was absolute, the supreme judge subject only to the **Sharia** (modified for
Indian conditions with the theologians' sanction). Revenue rested chiefly on the **land tax**
(usually one-fifth, raised to one-half); the **jaziyah** (tax on non-Muslims) was a variable, secondary
revenue — and, Thapar notes pointedly, conversions *reduced* it, so Sultans had little fiscal
incentive to force mass conversion. The empire was divided into provinces under transferable
governors (**muqtis**), and land was assigned as **iqta** — *revenue* grants (not the land itself,
non-hereditary, revocable) in lieu of salary, the assignees obliged to maintain troops. Thapar
stresses the iqta was "in many respects similar to the agrarian system [of] the pre-Sultanate period"
— a reworking of [[indian-feudalism|Indian feudalism]], elastic enough to absorb existing chieftains.

## Ala-ud-din Khalji's apogee

**Ala-ud-din** (r. 1296–1316) marks "the highest point of the Sultanate's political power." Confident
of his authority, he **revoked all earlier grants**, re-assessed the land and raised the state share
to **one-half**, barred iqta-holders from levying extra cesses (channelling surplus to the treasury),
and — to forestall noble conspiracies — **banned liquor and unsanctioned marriages** among the
nobility, backed by an efficient espionage. His famous **market/price controls** supplied a large
standing army cheaply. He repelled the **Mongols** and launched the campaigns that first carried
Sultanate power into the **Deccan** — the intervention that would soon throw up
[[vijayanagara-bahmani|Vijayanagara and the Bahmani kingdom]].

## The Mughal sequel (Spear)

[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|Spear, Vol. II]] picks up where the Sultanate left off. After Timur's
1398 sack, the Sultanate shrank ("From Delhi to Palam is the realm of Shah Alam") and the **Lodi
Afghans** ruled a brittle feudal confederacy until **Babur** — invited in by a disaffected governor —
destroyed Sultan Ibrahim Lodi at **Panipat (1526)**, founding the [[mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]].
Spear stresses the **continuity**: the Mughals inherited and perfected the Sultanate's
[[indian-feudalism|land-revenue]] and provincial machinery (the Afghan **Sher Shah Sur** providing the
crucial administrative "blue-print"), so the Sultanate is best read not as a closed chapter but as the
first phase of the Indo-Muslim imperial state that the Mughals — and then the British — would carry
forward.

## Significance

The Delhi Sultanate ended India's long political insularity, installed a durable Muslim ruling class,
and set in motion the **encounter of Islam and Indian society** — the "assimilation on trial" that
produced the [[bhakti-movement|Bhakti–Sufi]] religious ferment, new languages (Urdu's roots), and
Indo-Islamic architecture. It is the bridge from Thapar's Vol. I to the **Mughal** era of Vol. II.
