# Hugo Grotius (1583–1645)

Dutch jurist, humanist, and diplomat; author of *De jure belli ac pacis* (*On the
Law of War and Peace*, 1625), conventionally counted the founding work of modern
[[international-law]]. Written amid the Thirty Years War (and after his dramatic
escape from Loevestein prison to French exile), the treatise sought a law binding
sovereigns grounded in [[natural-law]] — valid, in his notorious phrase, "even if
we should concede that there is no God."

**Role in the law of war.** Grotius stands at the hinge between medieval
[[just-war]] doctrine and the eighteenth-century [[jus-victoriae]]. He preserved
just-cause analysis in principle but developed at length the *formal* law of war:
the rights of belligerents in a "solemn war," the acquisition of territory and
booty by conquest, postliminium, and the binding force of capitulations and peace
treaties. Whitman ([[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012]]) reads this Grotian formal
apparatus — what you win by winning — as the foundation on which
[[vattel-emer-de|Vattel]] and the eighteenth-century publicists built the mature
law of victory that made [[verdict-of-battle|battle verdicts]] enforceable.

**The sors problem (Whitman's reading).** Grotius analyzed battle as a *sors* —
a decision by chance. He allowed a hopelessly weak party to resort to pure
chance (even drawing straws) to avoid annihilation, but rejected *limited*
arranged battles in general: if a cause is worth fighting, Christians must
deploy **all** their forces — anything less is "vain and impious" (tempting
God). The phrase is borrowed verbatim from Machiavelli, though the reasoning is
theological; Whitman calls the conclusion Clausewitzian three centuries early.
His own eighteenth-century commentators abandoned the rigorism, treating battle
simply as a binding *transactio* — the doctrinal step that made the
[[verdict-of-battle|wager of battle]] theologically comfortable. His three just
causes — defense, recovery of property, punishment — and his advocacy (as
counsel for the Dutch Republic) of non-monarchical polities' right to lawful war
both shaped the century that followed.

**Counterfactual significance.** Some framework for inter-sovereign law was likely
emerging from the wreck of Christendom regardless (Gentili, Suárez preceded him),
but Grotius's synthesis gave it canonical form; the "Grotian tradition" remains the
standard label for international order built on law among sovereign states.
