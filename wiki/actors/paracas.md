## Overview

The Paracas tradition was the dominant cultural and mortuary complex of the south coast of Peru (Chincha, Pisco, and Ica valleys) during roughly 800–200 BCE (Cavernas phase) and 300 BCE–CE 200 (Necropolis phase), overlapping at its close with early [[nasca]]. It is most celebrated for the most technically accomplished prehispanic textiles found anywhere in the New World, produced for elaborate funerary bundles interred in subterranean cemeteries on the hyperarid Paracas Peninsula.

Shimada (in [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]]) places Paracas as a foundational **Early Regional Development (ERD)** tradition: it was both shaped by the [[chavin]] cult's southward reach and itself the crucible from which the successor [[nasca]] polychrome ceramic and textile tradition emerged. The chapter notes, however, that "direct cultural continuity from Paracas to Nasca" is contested — the Ica-valley sequence that once served as the master template for this transition is under challenge from new fieldwork in "peripheral" Chincha, Pisco, and Acarí valleys.

## Mortuary Practice and Textile Achievement

The defining Paracas evidence comes from two tomb types:

- **Cavernas burials**: bottle-shaped tombs — circular burial chambers at the bottom of vertical shafts — used for multiple burials; painted ceramics decorated by post-fired resin application of bright mineral pigments over incised outlines.
- **Necropolis burials**: subterranean square stone chambers. Julio C. Tello and T. Mejía Xesspe excavated 429 funerary bundles (c. 300 BCE–CE 200) from the Paracas Necropolis in 1927–1928. Each bundle consisted of a mummified adult male in fetal position in a basket, surrounded by foods (maize, yuca, peanut) and wrapped in up to "over sixty layers of woven and embroidered cloth (including up to 10-m-long lengths of plain cloth) and clothing (e.g., turbans, shoulder mantles, ponchos, loincloths), as well as cloth-wrapped items of prestige and ritual value such as Spondylus shell and trophy heads." Attached to the largest bundles were headbands, sheet-metal gold masks, feather tunics, leather capes, embroidered mantles, and staffs.

The large bundles' occupants are interpreted as "deceased political-religious leaders of regional Paracas society."

## Textiles as Communication Medium

Shimada identifies decorated textiles as the south coast's **aesthetic locus** around CE 0 — the medium held to the highest standards, emulated by others. The Paracas embroidered mantles (typically 2.3–2.8 m × 1.0–1.4 m) were produced in two complementary techniques:

- **Linear style**: conservative, based on straight lines, limited colours (red, green, yellow, blue), rigid repetitive motifs — "highly stylized felines, birds, snakes, and the 'Oculate Being.'" Motifs follow warp-weft structure; associated with "his connection to the ideological tradition of the society."
- **Block Color style**: curvilinear; uses "nearly twenty colors in broad expanses"; "highly variable, innovative motifs" including "ritual impersonator" figures and "'ecstatic shamans' … that is, shamans or spiritual leaders in trance state." Each is highly individualistic, "tailor-made to match specific leaders."

Textile analyst A. Paul interprets Block Color imagery as expressing the "ritual roles or obligations that the deceased held during his life," while Linear motifs express "his connection to the ideological tradition."

Key material innovation: the introduction of **camelid wool** (probably alpaca from the altiplano) alongside indigenous cotton. Wool holds dyes far better than cotton; its arrival drove a "whole series of improvements in spinning and weaving techniques and an increase in the number of colors used." The dull plant-based red was completely replaced by the intense red of cochineal. By late Paracas Necropolis times, weaving involved "systemic consideration of many variables, including range, placement, and combined effects of colors and motifs, tactile sensation, thickness, weight, and size."

## Chavin Iconographic Influence

The Karwa cache (c. 300–200 BCE), looted from tombs about 8 km south of the Paracas Necropolis near Ica, comprised over 200 separately painted cotton textiles depicting [[chavin]]-style motifs — "the Staff God, 'cayman' or 'crocodile supernatural,' 'winged guardian,' feline head, and other classic Chavin-style motifs … depicted in Chavin conventions." Analysis by Wallace suggests the bulk were locally produced (not imports), integrating introduced Chavin iconography with local Paracas motifs and generating local sub-styles. The "Staff Goddess of Karwa" — a female deity holding staffs and associated with cotton plants — is absent at Chavín de Huántar, indicating local creative adaptation rather than passive reception.

Scholars debate the mechanism: Burger infers a Chavin branch oracle on the south coast; Cordy-Collins suggests the goddess represents the female half of the Staff God; Stone-Miller argues she symbolises agricultural fertility of cotton and the association between women and weaving.

## Sociopolitical Organisation

The chapter notes the "attribution of 'state' status to the Nasca and direct cultural continuity from Paracas to Nasca" is contested. Paracas itself is generally understood to represent chiefdom-level organisation. Monumental mound-building during late Paracas times, recently documented in the Pisco Valley, suggests political complexity, though "it seems to have lost its importance in the Nasca culture."

## Ceramics

Paracas ceramics were decorated by first outlining designs with fine incisions, firing the vessel, then applying "bright-colored mineral pigments in a viscous resin base." This distinctive post-fired resin-paint technique was gradually replaced by the Topara potters' white-slip prefired painting, which spread down the south coast around CE 0 and became the technical foundation for [[nasca]] polychrome ceramics.

## Transition to Nasca

"From late Paracas (Necropolis phase dating to c. 300–100 BCE … to early Nasca times (c. 100 BCE to CE 200), south coast textiles show a remarkable artistic and technical evolution." As mastery of polychrome slip painting emerged with the Nasca sequence, the south coast's aesthetic locus shifted from embroidered textiles to ceramics — "concomitantly, realistic representations of religious motifs in textiles seem to disappear." Nasca textiles show "stylistic, iconographic, and technical continuities" with Paracas, including discontinuous warp-and-weft weaving, but de-emphasise borders.

## Historiography

Paracas scholarship was long dominated by Tello's excavations and subsequent funerary-context analyses. The Ica Valley "master sequence" (Menzel, Rowe, and Dawson 1964) set the chronological framework for the entire south coast, but more recent fieldwork in Chincha, Pisco, and Acarí has questioned "the applicability of the Ica chronological sequence and cultural-historical reconstruction long used as a blueprint for the entire south coast." Dating of the Paracas sequence itself "is being actively debated." Shimada emphasises that the sample is overwhelmingly from looted funerary contexts: Paracas Necropolis "constitutes virtually the only large sample of complete scientifically excavated garments for the period under study."
