# Bernard Brodie

Bernard Brodie (1910–1978) was the American strategist widely credited with originating the modern theory of nuclear deterrence. A naval historian by training who had written on sea power and the political uses of military force, he encountered the atomic bomb while at the Yale Institute for International Studies. Upon learning of Hiroshima, he reportedly told his wife, "Everything that I have written is now obsolete."

## Founding Dictum

In the 1946 volume *The Absolute Weapon* (which he edited and to which he contributed the key chapter), Brodie set the terms for subsequent debate:

> "Thus far the chief purpose of our military establishment has been to win wars. From now on its chief purpose must be to avert them. It can have almost no other useful purpose."

This remains the classic statement of the deterrence revolution. Brodie recognized that the "absolute weapon" had no meaningful tactical role in stopping armies or navies on the battlefield; its value lay entirely in the threat of retaliation that would make war between nuclear powers irrational.

## Evolution on Limited Nuclear War and Graduated Deterrence

Brodie was initially open to ideas of limitation. After the 1952 thermonuclear test announcement he "became in effect a follower of yours [Liddell Hart] early in 1952". He argued the nuclear threat "will be increasingly limited to only the most outrageous kind of direct aggression" and that it was necessary to "explore ways of consciously limiting those conflicts we may be unable entirely to avoid". (Freedman 1981, "The Concept of Limited War").

By 1955, reviewing prospects for tactical nuclear war, he hinted at the nihilistic outcome: "with the unrestricted reciprocal use of nuclear weapons tactically no substantial forces will be able to live in the field at all... Thus we tend in the end to get the same kind of utterly nihilistic result in considering unrestricted tactical war in the future that we get in unrestricted strategic war." (Freedman 1981, LIMITED MEANS, p.115).

Four years later he was convinced of this. Once reciprocal use was visualized no "overwhelming advantages" could be seen. "A people saved by us through our free use of nuclear weapons over their territories would probably be the last that would ever ask us to help them." This undermined claims that graduated deterrence was more credible than massive retaliation, as it would involve sacrifice for allies within and around the combat zone. (Freedman 1981, LIMITED MEANS, pp.115-116).

## On the Blunting Mission and Importance of Being First

In a 1954 essay setting out the case for limited war, Brodie made the expected degree of success of a blunting mission the single criterion for orienting future possibilities. If either side can destroy the other's retaliatory capability by surprise attack (almost minimum definition of success), it makes sense to be trigger-happy; restraint is suicidal. But if neither can hope to eliminate the retaliatory power of the other, restraint becomes prudence. (Freedman 1981, THE IMPORTANCE OF BEING FIRST, pp.131-132).

He recognized it was virtually impossible to create a first-strike capability which could completely spare North America the full horrors of nuclear war (drifting fallout would ensure reflexive retribution). Yet the US would need the capability for a first strike "both in spirit and military power" to fulfil alliance commitments. He recoiled from offence-minded total solutions but saw preparation for war (protecting retaliatory forces, civil defence) as responsible given finite chance of war. "The danger of total war is real and finite... So long as there is a finite chance of war, we have to be interested in outcomes; and although all outcomes will be bad, some would be very much worse than others." (Freedman 1981, THE IMPORTANCE OF BEING FIRST, pp.132-133).

He reiterated the importance of security of the retaliatory force: "our ability to retaliate in great force to a direct Soviet attack is taken far too much for granted by almost everybody, including our highest national policy-makers." (Freedman 1981, THE IMPORTANCE OF BEING FIRST, pp.136-137).
