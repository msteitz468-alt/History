# Evagoras of Salamis

## Overview

**Evagoras** (king of **Salamis** in Cyprus, c. 411–374/3 BCE) was the Cypriot dynast who
nearly made himself **master of all Cyprus and of the eastern Mediterranean** — a
Hellenizing king who allied with [[athens]] and Egypt, defied the Great King in the
"**Cypriot War**," and was immortalized by **Isocrates** as the ideal monarch.
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 8d, F. G. Maier) gives a sober account behind the
encomium.

## Rise and the unification of Cyprus

Evagoras seized the throne of Salamis and, under cover of good relations with
**[[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes II]]**, set about subjecting the island's other kingdoms "by
force or by persuasion." His most consequential service to the Greek cause was sheltering
the exiled Athenian admiral **[[conon]]** after Aegospotami and helping fund the Persian
fleet that won **Cnidus (394)** — for which Athens twice honoured him. By 391 only
**Citium, Soli and Amathus** still resisted his hegemony.

## The Cypriot War

When those three cities appealed to Artaxerxes, the King — valuing Cyprus' "great naval
strength whereby he would be able to protect Asia in front" — moved to **forestall a
united Cyprus**, beginning the **Cypriot War** (c. 391–380). Maier stresses that
Evagoras' revolt was **not a planned anti-Persian "grand design"** but "arose from a wrong
assessment of Persian policy." For a time, exploiting Persia's preoccupation with its
Greek and Egyptian enemies, Evagoras flourished: allied with **Acoris of Egypt** and
disaffected dynasts, he built a fleet of 90 triremes, extended his power, and even took
**Tyre** and other Phoenician cities — "the undisputed master of the eastern
Mediterranean." But the **[[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]] (386)** stripped him of his
Athenian allies (naming Cyprus as the King's) and freed Persia to act. Defeated in the
naval battle off **Citium** and besieged in **Salamis**, he came to terms in 380/79.

## "King to king" and the meaning of the settlement

Crucially, Evagoras was allowed to keep Salamis on terms he insisted upon: he would pay
tribute and acknowledge Artaxerxes' suzerainty **not "as slave to master" but "as king to
king"** (*hos basileus basilei*). Hornblower (Ch. 3) makes this a key illustration of how
the [[achaemenid-satrapy|Achaemenid system]] pragmatically accommodated powerful clients —
and of how Greek "slave" vocabulary distorts oriental terms for dependent status. Evagoras
was murdered in a court scandal in **374/3**.

## Significance

Evagoras made Cyprus briefly almost autonomous and accelerated its **Hellenization**,
becoming the model of the wise monarch in **Isocrates' *Evagoras*** — the foremost Greek
advocate of one-man rule choosing a Cypriot king as his exemplar, a telling sign of the
fourth century's drift from the polis toward monarchy. Maier draws two lessons: Cypriot
politics were driven by the interests of individual kingdoms, not "national" feeling; and
Evagoras "impartially subdued Greek and Phoenician dynasties," fighting with or against
Persia as advantage dictated.
