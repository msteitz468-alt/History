# The Buddha (Siddhartha Gautama)

## Overview

**Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha** ("the Awakened One," c. 6th–5th century BCE), founded
**[[buddhism|Buddhism]]**, one of the two great heterodox faiths (with [[jainism|Jainism]])
born from the urban intellectual ferment of South Asia's
**[[vedic-age|Second Urbanization]]**. In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Kaul, Ch 18) his
teaching is a defining expression of the **[[axial-age|Axial Age]]** in the Ganga valley —
and the seed of a faith that "momentously travelled" across Afro-Eurasia.

## Teaching

Active in the cities and kingdoms of the middle Ganga (notably **Magadha**, whose kings
**Bimbisara** and **Ajatashatru** patronized him — see [[pataliputra|Pataliputra]]), the
Buddha rejected the sanctity of the **Veda** and the ritual authority of the **brahmanas**.
He preached the **Four Noble Truths** (*aryachatvarisatyani*): that desire is the root of
suffering, and that the **renunciation of desire** is the path to **nirvana** — release from
the endless cycle of birth and rebirth. Central to his ethic was **ahimsa** (non-violence
toward living beings). His teaching was, in CWH IV's striking phrase about its early form,
"almost atheist": the Buddha was venerated through symbols, **not** worshipped as a god.

## The Sangha

The Buddha founded the **sangha**, a monastic order that attracted large lay followings and
was sustained by **donations** from across society — especially the rising **merchant class**
of the Second Urbanization. After his death the **First Buddhist Council** (organized at
Rajagriha under Ajatashatru) compiled his preachings, beginning the textual tradition (the
Pali **Tripitaka**).

## Significance

The Buddha's teaching became, under later patrons — above all the Mauryan emperor
**[[ashoka|Ashoka]]** and the **[[kushan-empire|Kushans]]** — the first genuinely
trans-regional **[[buddhism|universal religion]]**, spreading to Sri Lanka, Central Asia,
and the whole **[[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian sphere]]**. Its rejection of Vedic
ritual and caste-bound sacrifice also reshaped the religious landscape of South Asia itself.

## Historiography

The Buddha's dates are **disputed** (traditional c. 563–483 BCE; many scholars now favor a
later death c. 400 BCE), and the earliest texts postdate him by generations, transmitted
orally. His life is heavily overlaid by later hagiography. CWH IV situates him historically —
within the urban, commercial, kingly world of the Ganga valley — rather than treating him
purely as a figure of doctrine. See [[buddhism]] for the religion's later development.

[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 3d, Bivar) reaches the same world from the
Persian side: the Buddha and Mahavira are the contemporaries whose disputed deaths
anchor the chronology of the [[magadha|Magadhan kings]] Bimbisara and Ajatashatru. The
**nirvana c. 483 BCE** (218 years before Ashoka's consecration, widely but not
universally accepted) is the peg from which Ajatashatru's accession is reckoned to c.
491 — placing the Buddha's career exactly when [[achaemenid-empire|Darius]] was
carrying Persian power to the Indus at [[gandhara|Gandhara]].
