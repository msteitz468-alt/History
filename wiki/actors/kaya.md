# Kaya (Gaya) Confederacy

**Kaya** was not a single state but a cluster of up to **seven chiefdoms** in the Nakdong River valley
and south-central coast of Korea — the iron-trading link between the [[korea-three-kingdoms|Korean
mainland]] and [[yamato-japan|Yamato]] Japan, and in [[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes's]]
words "extremely important to the development of the contemporaneous Kofun-period Yamato state."

## A Confederacy, Not a Kingdom

Kaya grew from the **Pyonhan** polities of the iron-rich Yongnam (see [[samhan|Samhan]]). Development
focused first on **Pon-Kaya** (Original / Kumgwan-Kaya, from the mid-3rd century) on the southern coast,
then shifted to **Tae-Kaya** (Great Kaya) in the upper Nakdong valley. The standard view holds that
**none reached state-level organisation**, though Tae-Kaya has recently been argued to have coalesced
into a state just before Silla absorbed it. The **Taesong-dong** royal cemetery (Kimhae) held mid-3rd-
century-onward wood-chamber tombs with Han mirrors, steppe-style iron cauldrons, stoneware, and goods
from the Japanese Islands; adjacent **Ponghwangdae** is now thought to be the Pon-Kaya capital, with
port and storage facilities for trading iron and ceramics.

## Iron, Stoneware and the Yamato Tie

Kaya's iron underwrote the **Paekche–Yamato** and later **Yamato–Tae-Kaya** alliances. Its **stoneware
technology** spread into Silla territory (producing Silla-ware) and was carried to Japan in the 5th
century by emigrating Kaya potters who built large kilns near Osaka, founding the **Sué-ware** tradition.
Tae-Kaya rulers sent a tribute mission to the **Southern Qi** (479–502), signalling ambitions for
Mainland recognition. A famous passage in the 13th-century *Samguk Yusa* has the first Kaya king, Suro,
descend from heaven and take an **Indian princess from Ayut'a (Ayodhya)** as bride — a legend Barnes
notes among the claims of foreign elite connection.

## Fall

Koguryo's 400 AD foray decimated Pon-Kaya, after which burials ceased at Taesong-dong (replaced by
Pokch'on-dong in Pusan) and Yamato shifted its alliance to Tae-Kaya. **Silla took coastal Pon-Kaya in
532 and conquered Tae-Kaya in 562**, ending the confederacy. Kaya's "Mimana" identity in the Japanese
*Nihon Shoki* — read there as a Yamato colony — became one of the modern Korea–Japan historiographical
controversies; Barnes reads the Yamato objects in Kaya tombs as **trade, not colonisation**.
