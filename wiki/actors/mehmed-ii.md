# Mehmed II, "the Conqueror" (*Fatih*) (r. 1444–46, 1451–81)

The seventh Ottoman sultan, who **captured Constantinople in 1453** and transformed the
Ottoman frontier state into an **imperial power claiming the inheritance of Rome and
Byzantium**. From Lord Kinross's [[kinross-ottoman-centuries|*The Ottoman Centuries*]] (with
the [[fall-of-constantinople-1453|conquest]] and the institutional account on
[[ottoman-empire]]).

## Heir to Rome and Byzantium

Kinross stresses that from his accession Mehmed "had seen himself as the heir to the classical
Roman Empire and its Christian successor," and the **[[fall-of-constantinople-1453|conquest of
Constantinople (1453)]]** confirmed it. He took the title **Kaisar-i-Rûm** ("Caesar of Rome,"
in succession to Augustus and Constantine) alongside *padishah* and the Islamic dream of
universal sovereignty — a deliberate fusion of Roman, Byzantine, and Islamic imperial claims.
Well-read in history and "supremely confident in his capacity to achieve," he modeled himself
on **Alexander and the Caesars** as a world-conqueror, and rebuilt the ruined city as
**Istanbul**, his imperial capital (the covered bazaar, the **Fatih mosque**, and the new
palace that became Topkapı), repopulating it with Muslims, Christians, and Jews.

## The conquest-state

Mehmed turned the Ottoman polity into a **relentless conquest-state**. He abolished the
surviving vassal principalities and annexed them directly — **Serbia (1459), the Morea (1460),
Trebizond (1461), Bosnia (1463)** — fought a long naval war with **Venice (1463–79)**, defeated
the Akkoyunlu ruler **Uzun Hasan (1473)**, seized the Genoese **Crimea (1475)**, and even
landed at **Otranto in Italy (1480)**, raising fears across Christendom that he would take "the
other Rome." His was a centralizing reign: he advanced the **slave-household ([[devshirme|kapıkulu]])**
elite, the [[timar-system|timar]] cavalry, and the legal codification of the state — the
so-called "Law-Book of Mehmed II" later notoriously sanctioning **[[ottoman-empire|fratricide]]**
"for the good order of the world."

## Tolerance and ruthlessness

Kinross draws the paradox of the man. In religion Mehmed was **tolerant and curious**: he
preserved Hagia Sophia's mosaics, re-established the **Greek Orthodox Patriarchate** under
Gennadius — giving the patriarch greater power than in late Byzantine times and making him the
benefactor-protector of Orthodoxy against the Latin pope (the seed of the Ottoman
[[ottoman-law-kanun|*millet*]] system of self-governing religious communities) — and debated
theology and Christianity with his Greek scholars. Yet in politics and on the battlefield he
was "coolly and often cruelly ruthless" (he had the Byzantine megadux Notaras and others
executed). A Renaissance prince as much as an Islamic conqueror, he sat for the Venetian
painter **Gentile Bellini**.

## Counterfactual significance

Mehmed is the **founder of the Ottoman Empire as a world power** — the sultan who ended
[[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]], gave the dynasty an imperial capital and imperial ideology, and
set the conquest-state and centralized institutions that [[selim-i|Selim I]] and
[[suleiman-the-magnificent|Süleyman]] would carry to their apogee. With the fall of
Constantinople, Kinross writes, "Byzantium [was] to be reborn in a new idiom."
