# Jomon Culture

The **Jomon** ("cord-marked") culture of the Japanese Islands, **~14,000–1,000 BC**, is the world's
classic case of an affluent, sedentary, pottery-making **foraging** society — the Insular counterpart of
the Korean [[chulmun-culture|Chulmun]] and the substrate on which the [[yayoi-culture|Yayoi]] formed. For
[[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes]] it is the keystone of the [[pen-insular|Pen/Insular]]
argument: a society with elaborate material culture and the earliest exchange networks in East Asia, yet
**no chiefly ranking** — and one that **chose not to farm**.

## Sequence and Pottery Origins

The Jomon is divided into six phases on more than 2,000 AMS dates: Incipient (15,000–12,500 BP), Initial,
Early, Middle, Late and Final. Its defining ceramics — among the **earliest pottery in the world**,
clustering ~14 cal. kya at Odai Yamamoto I and Fukui Cave — appear *before the end of the Pleistocene*;
lipid analysis shows the first pots **boiled aquatic foods**, not plants (see
[[peopling-of-east-asia]]). The "many varieties of pottery and the highly dissected Japanese landscape"
led Barnes to speak of several Jomon "cultures" and "many Jomon languages." Epi-Jomon (Zoku-Jomon)
survived in Hokkaido to ~700 AD.

## An Affluent Forager Economy

At least **58 plant species** were used; the Jomon **tended** stands of chestnut, horse-chestnut and
walnut (DNA shows deliberately propagated, size-increased chestnuts), and brought several plants to
domestication — barnyard millet (*hie*), beefsteak herb (*egoma*/shiso), burdock, soybean, azuki and the
bottle gourd (from the 9th millennium BC at waterlogged Awazu). They cultivated **lacquer** trees; a
9,000-year-old Hokkaido burial preserves lacquered-bead ornaments. **Over 4,000 shellmounds** are known
in Japan (~1,100 Jomon); shellmounds preserve bone showing ~50 fish species (sea bream a favourite),
sea mammals, and toggle-head harpoons. More than 100 dugout canoes survive. Continental grains (rice,
millets) entered from Late Jomon but stayed **under 1% of the diet** until wet rice arrived.

## The Middle Jomon Phenomenon

Middle Jomon had the highest population and site densities of the whole sequence and the **earliest
reconstructed exchange system in East Asia** — "nowhere on the China Mainland has a similar regional
economic system been reconstructed as yet." A central-Honshu network traded **jadeite** (only source:
the Itoigawa "Jade Coast"), amber, obsidian, serpentinite, shellfish and nuts in classic down-the-line
gradients. **Sannai Maruyama** (northern Tohoku, occupied 3,900–2,300 BC; up to ~500 people, six giant
chestnut pillars, 1,600 figurines) anchors it — Barnes proposes it may have been a **seasonal
congregation site** for exchange and ritual. Late Jomon **stone circles** (Oyu and ~30 others), some
solstice-aligned, served as ritual gathering-places and cemeteries. Crucially, despite all this, the
Jomon "showed no overt social inequalities" — **chiefly figures emerged only on the Mainland**.

## End

Late and Final Jomon saw population decline and the collapse of Middle Jomon nucleation and trade — the
central-Honshu mountains were depopulated around the **4.2 kya climate event** that also ended the
Mainland Late Neolithic and emptied Sannai Maruyama. The Jomon ended not by extinction but by the
arrival of the [[mumun-culture|Mumun]] **wet-rice package** in North Kyushu, beginning the Yayoi;
northeastern (Tohoku) Jomon **resisted** rice for centuries (see [[rice-agriculture-spread-east-asia]]).
