# Wari (Huari)

## Overview

**Wari** (Huari) was the first highland Andean **expansionist state** of imperial character,
centered on a large urban capital near modern Ayacucho in the central Peruvian highlands and
flourishing during the **Middle Horizon** (c. 550–1000 CE). In Luis Lumbreras's chapter on
"Andean Urbanism and Statecraft" in
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Volume III]], Wari is presented as the
precursor of [[inca-empire|Inka]] statecraft — pioneering the planned administrative center,
intensive terracing and storage, and techniques of governing dispersed highland populations.

Wari shared the **Staff God / Tiahuanaco–Huari religious iconography** with
[[tiwanaku|Tiwanaku]] to the south, and the two are often paired as the twin poles of the
Middle Horizon, though their political relationship is debated. Wari planted distinctive
orthogonal-walled administrative compounds (Pikillacta, Viracochapampa, Jincamocco) far from
its capital, an apparatus later scholars read as a forerunner of the Inka network of
provincial centers and roads.

## Significance

Wari demonstrates that **imperial-scale integration in the Andes long predated the Inka**.
Its collapse around 1000 CE inaugurated the post-Wari **regional reorganization** of Andean
peoples — the era of competing highland and coastal polities ([[chimor|Chimor]], the
[[aymara-kingdoms|Aymara kingdoms]], Quechua chiefdoms) out of which the Inka eventually
forged [[inca-empire|Tawantinsuyu]].

## Historiography

Whether Wari was a true territorial empire, a looser hegemony, or primarily a religious-
economic network remains contested. Lumbreras (a foundational figure in Peruvian archaeology)
emphasizes its statecraft and urbanism; other scholars stress the autonomy of the regions it
touched. The Wari–Tiwanaku relationship — rivals, partners, or independent expressions of a
shared cult — is unresolved.
