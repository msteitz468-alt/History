# Koguryo

**Koguryo** was the northern, horse-riding power of the [[korea-three-kingdoms|Korean Three Kingdoms]],
spanning the Manchurian Basin and the northern Peninsula from the early centuries AD to **668**. It
filled the vacuum left when it destroyed the [[lelang-commandery|Lelang]] and Daifang commanderies
(313–314), and by the late 4th century had transformed, in [[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes's]]
words, "from a resilient regional authority struggling to preserve its sovereignty to a stable
bureaucratic state."

## Origins

Koguryo derived from tribes of eastern Manchuria occupying the **Gaogouli district of the Xuantu
commandery** under Han; its early capital lay in the **Hun River** valley (a Yalu tributary), within the
Cholbon-Puyo culture. Its stone-mounded tombs yield gold earrings like those of [[puyo|Puyo's]]
Laoheshen cemetery, and its elite claimed **Puyo descent** — a Han-ally founder myth shared with
[[paekche|Paekche]]. Excellent horsemen, the Koguryo were drafted into Han armies and were chronically
rebellious; Wang Mang's decree to assassinate their king made them enemies of the commanderies. Warfare
with Puyo began after 12 AD when the weakened Xuantu commandery was relocated.

## A Two-Capital State

In the 4th century the capital moved to the **Yalu valley**, pairing a round mountain fortress —
**Hwando/Wandu** ("circular capital," ~290 ha in a natural bowl) — with a squared plains city,
**Kungnae-song** (50 ha, in modern Ji'an). Hwando was the war-refuge, Kungnae-song the peacetime seat.
A Wei reprisal razed the capital in **244**; Murong Xianbei attacks followed in 341. Koguryo then built
a new capital, **Pyongyang-song**, in **427** beside the old Lelang headquarters — the origin of modern
Pyongyang's name.

## Expansion and Fall

Under **King Kwanggaeto** Koguryo besieged 58 Paekche fortresses (369) and in **400** drove south to the
coast, decimating [[kaya|Pon-Kaya]] and pushing Wa troops out of [[silla|Silla]]; his exploits are
recorded on a stele his son raised in **414** near Kungnae-song — an 1,800-character Chinese inscription,
the **earliest preserved document of the Three Kingdoms**. Koguryo pressure forced Paekche to move its
capital twice (Ungjin 474, Sabi 538); ~50 Koguryo fortresses confirm occupation deep into the central
Peninsula. After the Sui unification (589), Koguryo's refusal of tribute drew the ruinous Sui campaigns
of 598–614 (a factor in the Sui collapse). Finally **Silla, allied with Tang, conquered Koguryo in
668**; its elite withdrew north to found **Parhae** (C. Bohai).

## Culture and the Modern Dispute

Koguryo produced corridor-chamber **mural tombs** (96 of >10,000 tombs are painted): Anak Tomb #3, the
earliest, names a Han émigré, Dong Shou (d. 357). Its layout of temples (around octagonal pagodas) and
its horse-riding armour shaped the whole Pen/Insular elite. Because the General's Tomb and Kungnae-song
now lie in China, Koguryo's national identity is bitterly disputed: a 2002–2007 PRC project claimed
Koguryo for Chinese history, and PRC and North Korea filed **competing UNESCO nominations** in 2004.
Barnes's position: Koguryo "belonged only to itself," though its **Tungusic** (non-Chinese) language
"lends weight to the Korean claims."
