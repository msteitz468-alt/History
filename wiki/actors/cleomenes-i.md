# Cleomenes I of Sparta

**Cleomenes I**, the **Agiad** (senior) king of [[sparta|Sparta]] from c. 521, was the
dominant Spartan of his generation — the leader of the [[peloponnesian-league|Peloponnesian
League]] at the moment Persia reached the Aegean, the king who expelled the Athenian
[[pisistratidae|tyranny]], crushed [[argos|Argos]] at Sepea, and died in violent disgrace.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 6, L. H. Jeffery) portrays "a powerful but
unstable character" formed by a troubled royal background and the strains of near-imperial
leadership.

## A cautious League leader

Cleomenes inherited a League built by his father **Anaxandridas** and the ephor **Chilon**,
and for most of his reign **avoided long-distance commitments** — partly from fear that a
vocal member like [[corinth|Corinth]] might balk, but "more cogently" because lengthy
campaigns or heavy losses would expose Sparta to **Helot revolt** in Messenia and Laconia.
Hence three notable refusals: aid to **Maeandrius in Samos** (517), to the **Scythians**
against the retreating Persians (c. 513, after [[darius-scythian-expedition-513bce|Darius'
expedition]]), and to the leaders of the **[[ionian-revolt|Ionian Revolt]]** (499).

## Athens: expulsion and miscalculation

His most tangled policy was at [[athens|Athens]]. Moved by the Delphic oracle (won, it was
said, by [[cleisthenes|Alcmaeonid]] money), Cleomenes **expelled Hippias in 510** — but
then made the wrong friend, forming a *xenia* with **Isagoras** rather than Cleisthenes.
When Isagoras was outmanoeuvred, Cleomenes intervened twice: first with a small force
(besieged on the Acropolis and expelled), then with a full League army aiming, says
Herodotus, to **make Isagoras tyrant** — a goal "totally against the standard propaganda of
the League." The allies, led by Corinth, and his co-king **Demaratus** disowned the
campaign and withdrew (one reason, Sparta later said, that joint royal command was
abolished). This humiliation, compounded by the simultaneous collapse of the Boeotian and
Chalcidian fronts, marked the limit of his reach over Attica.

## Sepea and the fall

On home ground he was deadly. In **494** he annihilated the Argive army at **[[battle-of-sepea-494bce|Sepea]]**
near Tiryns — reportedly 6,000 Argive dead — securing the Thyreatis and crippling Argos for
a generation. Sent at Athens' request to arrest the **medizers of [[aegina|Aegina]]**, he
went with too little force; his co-king Demaratus undermined him, so Cleomenes engineered
**Demaratus' deposition** as illegitimate, bribing the Delphic priestess Periallos. When
the manipulation came out he fled to Thessaly and **Arcadia**, where he tried to raise the
locals "to follow him wherever he led." Recalled to Sparta, he (says Herodotus) went **mad**
and died by hideous self-mutilation in the stocks — possibly, Jeffery allows, a cover for
**assassination**, or the breakdown of "an abnormal type which could not be recast to fit
the Spartan system."

## Causal significance — counterfactual

- He **brought down the Athenian tyranny** but, by backing Isagoras, inadvertently cleared
  the path for [[cleisthenic-reforms-508bce|Cleisthenes' democracy]] — the opposite of his
  intent.
- His **caution toward Persia** (the three refusals) reflected the structural constraint of
  the Helot threat: Spartan foreign policy was hostage to its internal servile base.
- His **destruction of Argos at Sepea** removed the one Peloponnesian power that might have
  balanced Sparta — and pushed Argos into the **neutrality** that weakened the Greek coalition
  of 480.

## Historiography

Entirely dependent on **Herodotus**, whose account is "vivid but sometimes inconsistent,"
coloured by the rival propaganda of the two royal houses (Agiad vs. Eurypontid) and by
hostility to Cleomenes. The madness-narrative, the Demaratus affair, and the true character
of his Persian policy are all debated.
