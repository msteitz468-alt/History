# Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1536–1598)

The second of the **three unifiers** — the **peasant's son who became master of all
Japan** — who completed [[oda-nobunaga|Nobunaga's]] reunification and set in place the
social order the [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa]] would inherit. "Not until the nineteenth
century did individuals have another opportunity to rise so far." From Mason & Caiger's
[[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]] (ch. 12).

## Career and method

After avenging Nobunaga (1582), Hideyoshi subdued the remaining daimyo by **1590** —
Shikoku and the north, then **Kyūshū** (the Shimazu, 1587, with 230,000 men) and finally
the Hōjō at **Odawara (1590)**. His characteristic method was **the convincing show of
force** followed by negotiation: he preferred to flood or besiege a castle into surrender
and then confirm a defeated lord in part of his lands, stabilizing rather than eliminating
the daimyo class. Of humble birth, he could not become shogun; he ruled instead as
imperial **regent (kampaku, 1585)** and then **taikō**.

## The settlement of society

Two great measures fixed the early-modern social order:

- **The land survey (*kenchi*, 1582–98)** — a detailed cadastral survey of every domain,
  giving the central power exact knowledge of taxable resources.
- **The "sword hunt" (1588)** — disarming the peasantry (the metal ostensibly for a great
  Buddha) and, decisively, **separating the [[samurai|samurai]] from the farmers**: men
  henceforth had to be *either* warriors *or* cultivators, freezing the class division on
  which Tokugawa society rested.

## The Korean invasions and Christianity

- **The invasions of Korea (1592, 1597)** — Hideyoshi's vast, disastrous attempt to
  conquer Ming China through Korea: initial success was undone by Chinese intervention and
  Korean naval victories; the armies withdrew on his death, having "gained absolutely
  nothing" while devastating Korea and weakening the Ming. Mason and Caiger see them as the
  overreach of a man who did not grasp the difference between war at home (settled under the
  throne) and war against whole foreign peoples.
- **Christianity** — initially tolerant, he issued an **expulsion edict (1587)** against
  the Jesuits and had **26 Christians executed (1597)** after the San Felipe affair — early
  warning shots in the suppression that the Tokugawa would complete (see [[sakoku]]).

## Counterfactual significance

Hideyoshi **completed the unification and engineered the class structure** of early-modern
Japan, but — like [[shogunate-bakufu|Taira no Kiyomori]] before him — built no lasting
dynasty: his supremacy "died with him," his infant heir Hideyori swept aside by Ieyasu at
Sekigahara (1600). His Korean adventure stands as the great strategic folly of the age.
