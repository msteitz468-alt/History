# Franciscan Order (Order of Friars Minor)

## Overview

The **Franciscan Order**, founded by [[francis-of-assisi|Francis of Assisi]] and approved in outline by [[innocent-iii|Innocent III]] c.1209–10, was a **mendicant** brotherhood dedicated to poverty, itinerant preaching, and urban ministry. Together with the [[dominican-order|Dominicans]] it transformed high-medieval pastoral care, university theology, and the church’s response to dissent.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

**Innocent III**’s 1210 approval is “shrouded in mist” (oral encouragement at least). **Honorius III**, approving the Second Rule (1223), said he was ratifying “the rule of your order approved by our predecessor of happy memory, Pope Innocent,” codifying absolute non-appropriation of property. Cardinal **Ugolino** (later Gregory IX) long supported the order. The **Portiuncula** became the center; **Clare of Assisi** founded the Second Order (Poor Clares) Palm Sunday **1212** at San Damiano.

Expansion was spectacular after early failed missions: France, Belgium, southern France (Cahors first successful southern house), Germany (Caesar of Speyer 1221 from Augsburg; four custodies), Iberia, Bohemia, Hungary, Poland. Nine friars under **Agnellus of Pisa** landed **Dover 10 September 1224**; houses at Canterbury, London, and Oxford within six weeks; by **1240** twenty-eight English houses; Eccleston counted **1,242** English friars in **1256**; Ireland a province from 1230.

Rule forbade ownership of houses/churches, yet benefactors gave them. **Gregory IX (1230)** allowed agents for financial affairs while remaining mendicants; **Innocent IV (1245)** vested ownership of buildings, furnishings, and books in the Holy See with permanent friar use — compromises that left rigorist minorities in huts and caves dissatisfied. Despite Francis’s suspicion of learning (stories of books thrown from windows), by 1219 friars recruited at Paris; **Alexander of Hales** joined 1236; Oxford theology with **Grosseteste** lecturing; Bologna school before 1236; after 1239 major offices reserved to priests; **Bonaventure** minister general 1257; 1260 chapter restricted admission to educated clerics and distinguished laymen — “Francis himself could not now have become a Franciscan.” Logan frames the open debate: whether pristine Franciscan life could survive institutionalization. Fourth Lateran’s ban on new rules was avoided by Innocent’s pre-council approval (see [[mendicant-orders]]).
