# Creighton W. Abrams (1914–1974)

U.S. Army general who succeeded William Westmoreland as COMUSMACV in 1968 and later served as Army Chief of Staff. Abrams had been associated with the 1966 PROVN study advocating pacification priority. Upon taking command he directed a "one war" plan in which "population security, not the body count, would be the criterion of success" and "the key strategic thrust is to provide meaningful, continuing security for the Vietnamese people in expanding areas of increasingly effective civil authority." Long Range Planning Task Group (Nov 1968) reported "All of our US combat accomplishments have made no significant, positive difference to the rural Vietnamese—for there is still no real security in the countryside" and "Destruction of NVA and VC units... has become an end in itself—an end that at times has become self-defeating."

Despite the rhetoric, field implementation under subordinate commanders (e.g., Ewell) continued attrition practices with extreme kill ratios; "the myth of a change in strategy is a figment of media imagination; it didn’t really change until we began withdrawing" (Komer). Abrams oversaw the beginning of Vietnamization and U.S. ground force withdrawal. Post-Tet security gains derived more from VCI losses during Tet, CORDS, and RF/PF augmentation than from shifts in U.S. main-force operations. Abrams' tenure illustrated the limits of top-down redirection when institutional incentives and the Army Concept remained intact.

## Vietnam Command

Abrams took command after Tet at a moment when U.S. strategy and domestic support were in crisis. He is associated with a reorientation (the "one war" concept) that gave greater emphasis to pacification, small-unit operations, and support for South Vietnamese forces under the Vietnamization policy of the Nixon administration. Resources and attention shifted away from large-scale search-and-destroy toward population security and improvement of ARVN/RF/PF capabilities.

Krepinevich notes that even under Abrams the underlying institutional preferences of the Army remained powerful; many changes were driven as much by declining U.S. troop levels and political necessity as by a fundamental doctrinal conversion. Nevertheless, the Abrams period saw a measurable increase in effort devoted to the "other war."

## Legacy

Abrams is generally viewed more favorably by COIN-oriented analysts than Westmoreland, although the ultimate outcome of the war (fall of Saigon 1975) occurred after U.S. withdrawal. He died in 1974 while serving as Chief of Staff.
