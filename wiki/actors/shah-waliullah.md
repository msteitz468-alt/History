# Shāh Walīullāh of Delhi

Shāh Walīullāh (1703–62) was the dominant religious intellect of 18th-century Muslim India — simultaneously a Sufi master (initiated into both the [[naqshbandiyya]] and the [[qadiriyya]]), a defender of *sharīʿa*-bound piety, a political activist, and a theological synthesiser. "His works still await serious study in the West," Schimmel wrote in 1975 ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Theological Position

Walīullāh attempted to bridge the long-standing Sufi dispute between *waḥdat al-wujūd* (unity of being — Ibn ʿArabī's ontological monism) and *waḥdat al-shuhūd* (unity of vision — Aḥmad Sirhindī's testimonial monism), treating the difference as one of experiential perspective rather than fundamental doctrine. He also explained the differences between the four Islamic law schools as "historical facts with no basic difference of importance" — a conciliatory pluralism unusual for the period.

His concept of the *qayyūm* (highest cosmic representative and sustainer of creation) is elaborated in *Lamaḥāt* and related works; he equates it variously with "the breath of the merciful," the "seal of the divine names," or the universal soul — drawing on the Naqshbandī tradition of Sirhindī while softening its exclusivist claims.

## Practical Reforms

- Translated the Quran into Persian, making it accessible to Muslim readers who lacked Arabic; his sons later translated it into Urdu
- Wrote *Ḥujjat Allāh al-bāligha* — a socially oriented Islamic theology explaining religious duties rationally
- Politically: invited Ahmad Shah Durrani Abdali to India to defend Muslims against Sikh and Mahratta expansion; the Battle of Panipat (1761) was partly a consequence of his appeal

## Legacy

His mystical chain extended to his grandson Shāh Ismāʿīl Shahīd and Sayyid Aḥmad of Bareilly (*Aḥmad Brelwī*), who fought the Sikhs and established the *ṭarīqa muḥammadiyya* (independently of but convergently with [[mir-dard-khwaja]]'s order of the same name). His political impact "helped to form the modern Indo-Muslim religious life and attitude toward the British." The Deoband school (founded 1867) perpetuated his tradition — "though without the strong mystical bias of Shāh Walīullāh's original thought."

## Relationship with Contemporaries

Walīullāh shared Delhi's 18th-century Naqshbandī intellectual world with Maẓhar Jānjānān (killed 1782) and [[mir-dard-khwaja]] (1721–85), though Schimmel notes that "relations between the great mystical leaders of Delhi during the eighteenth century" remain insufficiently studied; Walīullāh was attacked by a disciple of Maẓhar, "showing the manifold currents inside eighteenth-century Naqshbandī theology."

## Historiography

A substantial Arabic, Persian, and Urdu literature on Walīullāh exists; the Shāh Walīullāh Academy (Hyderabad, Sind) has published many of his works. Western scholarship was thin at Schimmel's writing (1975) — Bausani and Aziz Ahmad providing the principal introductions. J.M.S. Baljon's translation of *Taʾwīl al-aḥādīth* (Leiden, 1973) offered one entry point.
