# Colony of New South Wales

The **Colony of New South Wales**, founded with the arrival of the First Fleet at
[[sydney|Sydney Cove]] in 1788, was Britain's first and parent Australian colony. Begun as
a penal settlement absorbing [[convict-transportation-australia|transported convicts]], it
grew into a self-governing colony with a pastoral and commercial economy, shedding
territory as Tasmania, Victoria, Queensland, and South Australia were carved off, and
joining the other colonies in the [[australian-federation-1901|Federation of 1901]].

## From CHA Vol. 1 (Ch 3–4): The Early Autocratic Governors and Colonial Society 1788–1822

### The Five Founding Governors and Their Instructions

Governor Phillip's Instructions (25 April 1787) were "unambiguous": convicts would work
public farms, growing food from common industry; once free, men received 30-acre grants
(plus 20 for a wife, 10 per child) with tools, seed, and two years' rations. The same
agrarian vision was given to the first **five governors** — **Arthur Phillip**, **John
Hunter**, **Philip Gidley King**, **William Bligh**, and **Lachlan Macquarie** — and
continued until 1825, when free grants to ex-convicts ended.

All five operated under what was in practice military autocracy: the first four were
naval captains; Macquarie was the first military governor. Governing a colony with no
walls and a highly mobile, resourceful convict population required constant improvisation
between penal control and economic encouragement.

### The Assignment System

Arising from Acting Governor Grose's 1792 decision to grant land to fellow officers
(with ten assigned convicts each on full rations), the **assignment system** became the
dominant mechanism of convict labour. It was the "most common experience for convicts
still under sentence throughout the period of convict transportation." Assigned convicts
worked private masters' farms and businesses; skilled convicts could earn task-work wages
after completing their daily quota.

### The Emancipist Economy and the Exclusive-Emancipist Divide

The fundamental social division of the colony was between the **emancipists** (freed
convicts and their descendants) and the **exclusives** (officers, free settlers, and
civil servants who considered convict origins socially disqualifying). Governors who
favoured emancipists — most notably Macquarie — faced fierce opposition from the
exclusive interest.

Emancipists were the economic backbone of the colony. By 1821 they grew most of the
colony's grain and owned over two-thirds of cultivated land, half the cattle, and a
third of the sheep — yet free settlers (17 per cent of landholders) held 57 per cent of
alienated land. Emancipist traders like Simeon Lord built fortunes exceeding the
government's own treasury operations.

### The Rum Rebellion (1808)

Governor Bligh's attempt to reassert Crown ownership of Sydney's urban land — cancelling
leases, evicting occupants, pulling down houses — provoked a settler uprising. On
**26 January 1808**, Bligh was deposed by the officers and soldiers of the New South Wales
Corps under Major **George Johnston**, "with the apparent support of the townsfolk."
The rebels framed their action in "the passionate language of rights to liberty and
property." The event was later (mistakenly) named the "Rum Rebellion" for the supposed
motive of breaking the officers' monopoly on rum; the real driver was property rights.

### The Age of the Macquaries (1810–1822)

Governor **Lachlan Macquarie** (arrived 1810) was sent to restore order after the
Bligh overthrow. He found Sydney "in good order" despite his expectations of an
"antipodean wilderness." Key actions:
- Reorganised the Commissariat and colonial revenue; proclaimed sterling as official
  standard (1816); presided over founding of the **Bank of New South Wales**.
- Drew on the British Treasury and convict labour to build handsome classical and Gothic
  public buildings — barracks, hospital, orphanage, churches — in defiance of orders to
  desist.
- Granted over half a million acres of Aboriginal land to settlers, especially south of
  Sydney; ordered construction of **Cox's Road** over the Blue Mountains (1814–15).
- Supported emancipists, inviting them to Government House and appointing them to
  government positions — earning fierce opposition from the exclusive faction.
- Popularised the name **"Australia"** for the continent; from 1817 colonists celebrated
  26 January as Anniversary Day under vice-regal patronage.

The **Bigge Report** (1823) devastatingly critiqued Macquarie's administration and
recommended the colony's future lie with large-estate grazing worked by assigned convicts
rather than small farming by felons. Macquarie had returned to England before its
publication. Despite critics, he was "fondly remembered for decades" as the longest-
serving and most popular early governor.

### Castle Hill Uprising (1804)

King's 1803 attempt to quarantine Irish convicts at the isolated Castle Hill farm
backfired. In March 1804 **more than 300 rebels** marched from Castle Hill to Parramatta,
calling for "Death or Liberty" and intending to seize a ship for Ireland. They were
betrayed, forced to retreat, and their leaders arrested; pursuing troops shot and
bayoneted fleeing rebels. Survivors were severely flogged; leaders were hanged and their
bodies "suspended in chains to rot near the places where they had massed." The uprising
was the largest convict revolt in Australian history.

### Frontier Conflict with Aboriginal Peoples

The colony's expansion repeatedly triggered frontier war. The first **Hawkesbury frontier
wars** broke out in 1794 as settlers moved onto the most fertile river-flat country
densely occupied by Aboriginal people. Governor King solved the 1802 cycle by banishing
Aboriginal people from settled areas and demanding resistance leaders be surrendered —
**Pemulwuy** was surrendered and killed. Macquarie repeated the cycle when new lands
opened around Camden and Appin; a military raid on 17 April 1816 culminated in the
massacre of at least fourteen men, women, and children at Appin. The pattern — initial
contact, then raids and killings, then military operation, then amnesty and banishment —
was repeated each time the frontier advanced.
