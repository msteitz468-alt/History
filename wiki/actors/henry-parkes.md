# Henry Parkes

"Father of Federation"; five-times Premier of New South Wales; the most durable
politician of the colonial era.

## Role and Career

Parkes sat "in the New South Wales Legislative Assembly from 1856 to 1895,
representing nine different constituencies, surviving two bankruptcies and leading
five ministries" (*CHA* Ch 8). He exemplified the professional colonial politician:
"none was more pertinacious and durable," combining personal patronage networks
with a genuine commitment to liberal causes. He exploited the 1868 shooting of
Prince Alfred by the Irish-born O'Farrell for electoral advantage, hardening
anti-Catholic sectarianism in NSW politics.

As early as 1867 he spoke of his vision of "the footprints of Six Young Giants in
the morning dew," envisioning colonial federation. He suggested the Federal Council
in 1880 and participated in the 1883 Intercolonial Convention in the aftermath of
Queensland's failed New Guinea annexation.

## The Tenterfield Oration (1889)

Returning from Brisbane after promoting a federation conference, Parkes stopped at
Tenterfield (northern NSW) and delivered what became "a legendary 'oration.'" It
drew heavily on the Edwards defence report (warning of the colonies' military
vulnerability) and "urged Australians to accomplish, in peace, what Americans had
done through war." It was a direct call for a constitutional convention — the
trigger for the Melbourne Federal Conference of February 1890.

## Federation Leadership

Parkes presided over the **National Australasian Convention** in Sydney (March–
April 1891), which produced a fully drafted Constitution. He was described as
president of that convention, and Deakin wrote of his influence throughout. He was
robbed "by death in 1896 of the chance to complete his mission."

His formulation of Australian national identity was racial: "the crimson thread of
kinship" ran through all colonial Australians (meaning British racial kinship),
cited by Helen Irving (*CHA* Ch 10) as an "indicator of nationalism" but also of
the racial exclusivism baked into the federation project.

Parkes and Edmund Barton took "the momentous step of agreeing that the [tariff] lion's
fate could simply be left for later" — setting aside the free-trade/protection
conflict to pursue federation, the key diplomatic act that made the 1891 convention
possible.

## Historiography

A.W. Martin's *Henry Parkes: A Biography* (MUP, 1980) is the standard life. Geoffrey
Bolton's entry in *The Oxford Companion to Australian History* is cited by *CHA*
Ch 8. Parkes is presented in *CHA* as simultaneously self-serving and genuinely
visionary — the colonial politician as institution, whose personal durability
substituted for stable parties.
