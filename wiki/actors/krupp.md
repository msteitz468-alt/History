# Krupp

The Essen steel and armaments dynasty and firm — Prussia's and then
Germany's premier weapons maker across three regimes, emblem of the
entanglement of German heavy industry with state power, rearmament,
and, under [[nazi-germany]], slave labor.

## Overview

Founded on Friedrich Krupp's failed pursuit of English cast steel (he died
in debt, 1826); his son **Alfred Krupp** built the giant on railway-boom
axles and wheel-rings (the three-ring logo), employing 12,000 in Essen by
1874, and pivoted to armaments — supplying Bismarck's unification wars and
declaring "My achievement will stand and fall with Prussia's greatness and
military supremacy." His regime combined pioneering welfare paternalism
(housing for 25,000+, health and pension funds) with authoritarian
surveillance — a private police larger than Essen's, photographic worker
files, loyalty oaths — which Evans says "did indeed foreshadow the later
development of the 'Nazi model plant.'" **Friedrich Alfred Krupp**
(nickel-steel armor, the Navy League lobbying scandal, a 60% margin as
sole armor-plate supplier) died in 1902 amid a Capri homosexuality
scandal — suicide, Manchester argues; James "glides smoothly over the
controversy." Under **Gustav von Bohlen und Halbach** (with Hugenberg on
the board) the firm "had become in practice part of the German state" by
WWI (the Paris Gun); it evaded Versailles via secret tank work in the
USSR and Bofors.

**Third Reich**: Gustav declined to fund Hitler pre-1933 but signed the
November 1932 industrialists' petition to Hindenburg, welcomed the
crushing of the unions, dismissed Jewish employees, and profited
immediately from rearmament. From autumn 1941 the firm aggressively
lobbied for forced-labor allocations beyond need (Herbert); Soviet
workers were penned behind barbed wire on starvation rations ("Russian
prisoners of war must not be permitted to become accustomed to West
European food" — head office), policed by a 2,000-strong company force
that beat workers "simply because they were Eastern workers"; one
prisoner caught stealing bread was shot dead by an unpunished guard. In
1943 Hitler decreed special succession rules passing ownership to
**Alfried Krupp** (SS patron 1931, NSDAP 1938). Alfried and most
directors were convicted of slave labor and plunder at the 1947–48
Nuremberg industrialists' trial; US High Commissioner McCloy amnestied
him and restored the fortune in January 1951 as Cold War policy. Berthold
Beitz led the postwar rebuild; the firm became foundation-controlled in
1966 and later merged with Thyssen.

## Historiography

Dominated for decades by William Manchester's polemical *The Arms of
Krupp* (1968); Harold James's 2012 firm-commissioned but archivally
grounded history revised the picture of the firm as congenital
warmonger while confirming its deep complicity in Nazi slave labor.
