# Giulio Douhet

Giulio Douhet (1869–1930) was an Italian Army officer who became the most radical
and influential prophet of air power between the world wars. An artillery officer
by training, he commanded Italy's first aviation unit and agitated for an independent
air arm from before the First World War — "Twelve years ago, when the very first
airplanes began to hedge-hop between field and air, hardly what we would call flying
at all today, I began to preach the value of command of the air." During the Great
War he attacked the conduct of the Italian high command so sharply that he was
court-martialled; he was later promoted brigadier general, exonerated, and under
Benito Mussolini's regime rose to a position of authority over Italian aviation as
the air arm "assumed new importance." His central work, [[command-of-the-air|*The
Command of the Air*]], appeared in 1921 "under the auspices of the Ministry of War" —
itself "an indication of the degree to which Douhet's name had been restored since
his court-martial" — and was reworked into a far more extreme second edition in 1927.

## Role

Douhet's significance is not as a commander but as a theorist and polemicist. By his
own account he had been arguing since 1909 "that the airplane should be the third
brother of the army and navy," that "the day would come when thousands of military
planes would ply the air under an independent Ministry of the Air," and that "the
dirigible and other lighter-than-air ships would give way before the superiority of
the plane." He claimed in his 1927 preface that "the cardinal points of the program
for national defense which I proposed have been accepted" — unified command of army,
navy and air force; a Ministry of Aeronautics; and the distinction between an
Independent Air Force and auxiliary aviation. The 1927 edition "was soon translated
into various European languages" and circulated widely among the air staffs of the
interwar powers.

## Argument and Consequences

Douhet's wager was that the airplane had "broken the evolutionary continuity of the
character of war" and that future wars would be won swiftly by massive fleets of
strategic bombers striking the enemy's "vital centers" and, above all, the morale of
his civilian population (see [[strategic-bombardment]] and [[command-of-the-air]]).
"To have command of the air means to be in a position to prevent the enemy from
flying while retaining the ability to fly oneself," he wrote, and from that axiom he
deduced that the army and navy should be progressively cut back in favour of an
independent bomber force. His doctrine, transmitted through the 1927 translations,
fed directly into interwar bombing theory and shaped the doctrinal assumptions of the
air forces — including the Luftwaffe — that planned the strategic air war of 1939–45.

## Thought

Douhet insisted he reasoned "with strict adherence to logic from actual verifiable
facts" and disclaimed prophecy: "I did not prophesy then, and I do not prophesy now.
All I did then was to examine the new problem posed by the existence of the new arm
and reason from verifiable data." His logic ran: every improvement in firearms favours
the defensive, so land fronts will be permanently static; the airplane, "the offensive
weapon par excellence," can "fly to and from any point of the compass in the shortest
time" and pass over the static front to strike "the interior of the enemy's country so
devastatingly that the physical and moral resistance of the people would also collapse."
Defence was therefore futile — "Nothing man can do on the surface of the earth can
interfere with a plane in flight, moving freely in the third dimension" — and the only
defence was to "conquer the command of the air" by offensive action. He carried this
to its conclusion in the doctrine of total war, dismissing legal and moral restraint:
treaties banning "inhuman weapons" were "nothing but scraps of paper compared to the
tragedy which would follow," and "all contenders must use all means without hesitation."

## Counterfactual Significance

Douhet's importance is as a crystallizer rather than an originator of effects in the
field. Had he never written, an independent-air-force movement would still have arisen
(parallel figures pressed the case elsewhere — see [[mitchell-william]]), but the
specific, extreme, internally consistent doctrine of decisive city-bombing against
civilian morale — divorced from air defence, tactical air, and the counterforce air
battle — owes its sharpest interwar formulation to him. The doctrine's influence
mattered most where it was believed: it encouraged the interwar faith that bomber
fleets alone could win wars quickly and "bloodlessly," a faith the Second World War
would test and largely disappoint.

## Historiography

Douhet is best understood as a visionary more than a rigorous theorist. As the
editor's introduction notes, "the errors in Douhet's strategic philosophy were not to
be found in the deductions that he drew from the premises of that philosophy, but in
the premises themselves." His most fundamental premise — "that victory based on
superior airpower would be swift and complete" — rested on assumptions he never
seriously tested: he postulated "absurdly uniform and effective aerial bombardment,"
disregarding "duds, misses, overlap, and differences in target composition," with a
pseudo-mathematical "unit of bombardment" that even a trained engineer found
"baffling" (Meilinger). He grossly overestimated bombing's psychological and physical
effect on populations, identified only the crudest "vital centers," and as Brodie
asked in exasperation, having "so little idea of what it is necessary to hit," was
nonetheless "quite so sure of the tremendous results." He ignored the air battle,
dismissed pursuit interception and antiaircraft fire (despite Allied gunners downing
over a thousand German aircraft in WWI), denigrated tactical air and armour, and
brushed aside law and morality. The common indictment is that he was "rendered obsolete
by technological change." The defence — voiced in the source — is that he was theorizing
"barely a decade after the airplane's invention," in "an age of speculation about the
airplane in which prophecy was normally well ahead of technology," where "making errors
was a natural concomitant of the process."
