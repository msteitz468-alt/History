# Patriarch Nikon

## Biography / Description
**Patriarch Nikon** (born Nikita Minin) was the head of the Russian Orthodox Church from 1652 to 1666 (L2458-L2460). Born to a peasant family, he rose through the ecclesiastical hierarchy due to his intelligence and force of character, becoming the Metropolitan of Novgorod, where he won the favor of Tsar Alexis (L2458-2460). Elevated to the patriarchate in 1652, he exercised an immense personal influence over the young Tsar, who bestowed upon him the exceptional title of "Great Sovereign" (*Velikii Gosudar*), matching the honor previously given only to Patriarch Philaret (L2458-2460). 

Nikon is best known for initiating the radical corrections of Russian liturgical texts and rituals that precipitated the great Schism (*Raskol*) of the Russian Church, and for his theoretical defense of the superiority of the spiritual authority (church) over the secular power (state) (L2458-2460).

## Key Decisions and Achievements
* **Liturgical Reforms (1653–1656):** Nikon ordered the revision of Russian service books to align with contemporary Greek manuscripts, corrected the spelling of the name of Jesus, and changed the sign of the cross from two fingers to three (L2650, L2656). He forced these changes through ecclesiastical councils in 1654 and 1656, excommunicating all who clung to traditional Muscovite practices (L2650-2652).
* **Assertion of Theocracy:** Nikon actively claimed that the Church was superior to the state, adopting views that resembled Western papism (L2458-2460). When Alexis quarreled with his domineering mentor in 1658, Nikon abandoned the patriarchal seat and retired to a monastery, hoping the Tsar would beg him to return (L2460-2462, L2654).
* **Deposition (1666–1667):** At the Great Moscow Council of 1666–1667, which was attended by the patriarchs of Alexandria and Antioch, Nikon was tried for his political ambitions, defrocked, and exiled to a distant monastery, where he died in 1681 (L2462-2464, L2654-2656).

## Historical Significance
Nikon's reforms permanently ruptured the Russian Orthodox Church, giving rise to the Old Belief (*Raskol*) which drew millions of devoted Russians away from the state church (L2656-2658). Although Nikon was personally disgraced and defrocked, his liturgical corrections were fully endorsed by the 1666–1667 council and enforced by the state with violence (L2464, L2654-2656). 

Nikon's failure to assert theocratic supremacy over the Tsardom marked the final defeat of the Church's political independence in Russia. With Nikon's fall, the path was cleared for the complete subordination of the Church to the state, which was completed by Peter the Great's abolition of the patriarchate in 1721 (L2974-2978).
