# Totonac

## Overview

**Totonacapan**—centered on the Sierra de Chiconquiaco, central Veracruz—was the middle
province of the traditional tripartite Gulf Coast division by sixteenth-century language
(Huasteca–Totonacapan–Olmecapan) used by Richard A. Diehl
([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1 Ch. 4]]). Diehl cautions that
scholars tend to credit the Totonacs with more accomplishments than are their due, **including
construction of [[el-tajin|El Tajín]]**—an over-attribution. Tepehua (a closely related language)
is still spoken in the mountains and may once have been more widespread. The historic Totonac
capital was **Cempoala** (Misantla region), emerging about a century before the Conquest after
Postclassic political fragmentation.

## Aztec conquest and tribute

When Cortés arrived in 1519, the Totonacs of Cempoala dominated the region but were under Aztec
subjugation. Aztecs considered them more “humane” and “civilized” than the [[huastec|Huastecs]];
they were famed for cotton textiles, song and dance, and food, with scant respect as warriors.
Totonacapan supplied food during early-1450s Basin of Mexico famines (children sold into slavery
for maize; possible highland migration). **Moctezuma I** (the Elder, 1440–69) subjugated southern
Totonacapan; **Moctezuma II** (the Younger) conquered the northern sector early in the sixteenth
century. Tribute included cotton, warrior costumes and shields, cacao, liquidambar, feathers, and
gold/crystal/amber ornaments; maize likely moved via markets rather than formal tribute lists.

## Conquest of Mexico

Totonacs of Cempoala became Cortés’s **first Indian allies**—without whom, Diehl states, he never
could have conquered the Aztecs. Archaeology of contact-period Totonacapan is thin: restored
Cempoala temples; **Quiahuiztlan** hilltop site with 40+ miniature-temple tombs overlooking
Cortés’s landfall and Villa Rica de la Veracruz. Late Postclassic central Mexican architectural
concepts appear at Cempoala, Quiahuiztlan, and other Totonac centers (Diehl’s Postclassic Gulf
trends).

## Relation to Classic Gulf civilization

El Tajín’s Classic–Epiclassic florescence lies in what later became Totonac-associated territory,
but Diehl rejects automatic equation of Totonac ethnos with Tajín builders. Cowgill notes possible
unusually direct Teotihuacan relations with the El Tajín coastal sector without asserting
Totonac identity for the Classic city.

## Evans & Webster 2001 — Zempoala / Cempoala (Wilkerson)

S. Jeffrey K. Wilkerson (EW2001, “Zempoala”): **Zempoala (Cempoala)**, major Terminal Postclassic
coastal Totonac metropolis near the Actopan River mouth; Spaniards **1519** called it “Sevilla,”
astonished by c. **80,000** people, burnished white buildings, and irrigated fields—**most
populous Gulf Coast city of its day**; principal temple compound host to momentous first-year
Conquest events. European epidemics left the city deserted by the early **seventeenth** century
(*part_of:* [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519]] · *part_of:* [[gulf-lowlands]] South Central).
Urban design, cults, and art reflect Central Mexican highland norms reinforced by late
fifteenth-century Aztec dominance; river-cobble + cement buildings; *coatipantli* (“snake wall”)
platform merlons; rectangular temples (some round-backed); glyphic polychrome sanctuary designs;
ceramics include regional fine-paste and local Cholula-Puebla polychrome. Extensive canal
irrigation from altered river channels was crucial in the dry rain shadow. Nearby complex:
**Trapiche** (occupations from end Early Formative); **Chalahuite** (Late Formative–Protoclassic
regional major, early canals, mounds to **20 m**); **Chachalacas** (Late Formative through Classic
+ Terminal Postclassic reoccupation). Research history: Paso y Troncoso **1891**; Fewkes **1905**;
García Payón late **1930s–early 1970s** (first Gulf major site in regional context); INAH
**1979–81**. South Central Postclassic overview: Aztec Triple Alliance stationed personnel at
Zempoala and Cotaxtla; Zempoala ceramics shifted toward highland-related types (EW2001).

## Colonial–modern trajectory (Deans-Smith, CHNPA II.2 Ch. 18)

**Geography and language.** Kelly & Palerm delimit Totonacapan from Río Cazones (N) to Río de
la Antigua (S), inland to eastern Sierra Madre slopes and parts of highland Puebla. Sixteenth-
century Totonac stretched from the Sierra de Puebla to the coast (Cazones–Antigua); **Misantla**
jurisdiction was the most extensive Totonac-speech area; Nahua bilingualism is discussed by
Palerm & Kelly. Late Postclassic **El Totonacapan** densest regions: **Cempoala** (regional
~250,000; city 80,000–120,000), Jalapa (~120,000), Colipa (24,000), **Papantla** (60,000).

**Demographic collapse.** Mota y Escobar: Cempoala **30,000 tributaries** at Cortés’s arrival;
mid-seventeenth century only **8**. Wilkerson: Papantla-area population in 1610 ~**2%** of
immediately pre-Conquest levels. Major encomiendas: Cempoala (Cortés → Saavedra → Albornoz);
Papantla to Andrés de Tapia (son and grandson); Misantla to Luis de Saavedra. Most surviving
communities under Crown control by the **1560s**.

**Divergent lowland vs sierra experience.** Deans-Smith compares Totonacs to Maya and
[[mixtec|Mixtec]]: early Spanish presence less intrusive, with major social/political
reorganization in the **eighteenth century**. Lowland communities lost lands to mercedes and
cattle estancias (milpa destruction e.g. Misantla); **sierra Totonacs** remained relatively
untouched (difficult communications; few minerals). Key commercial products: **vanilla** around
Papantla and later **tobacco** under the Royal Tobacco Monopoly (Córdoba, Orizaba, Huatusco,
Zongólica as planters/fieldworkers; Papantla often excluded — Ducey).

**Weak conversion.** Ricard: Catholicism remained weak on the Gulf. 1540 idolatry trial of Don
Juan of Matlatlán; Misantla abandoned by missionaries as early as **1579**; Mota y Escobar at
Papantla 1610 on Totonac nonconformity; **Tajín** continued as a place of worship; bilingual
confessional 1752/1837. **Nahuatlization** of Totonacs as ethnic strategy (Masferrer Kan);
paucity of native-language documents hinders ethnohistory.

**Rebellion arc.** Papantla revolts **1764, 1767, 1787**; independence base under **Serafín
Olarte** (1813; ~400 Totonac rebels, Coxquihui sierra); **Mariano Olarte** insurrection
**1836–38** (vanilla/livestock conflict, electoral irregularities, religious fiestas; Plan de
Papantla). ***Condueñazgo*** mainly Indian at Papantla. **1885 Díaz Manfort** (“holy doctor”)
rebellion against land division climax of Totonac resistance — quickly repressed. Revolution
1910–18 and agrarian movement 1920–30: southern Totonacapan with **>50,000 peasants
assassinated**. Late 20th c.: ~**117,533** Totonac speakers in Veracruz; vanilla decline from
late 1950s toward oranges/livestock; Poza Rica oil displacing Papantla; Misantla Totonac
rapidly replaced by Spanish (MacKay); early-1960s Papantla-area armed insurrection repressed;
Olarte family of Tenixtepec land reclaim 1980.

## Why it mattered

Key contact-period Gulf polity whose defection from Aztec overlordship enabled the Spanish
conquest’s first secure coastal base; cotton and tropical-product tributary zone in Aztec
imperial strategy; cautionary case against projecting sixteenth-century ethnolinguistic labels
backward onto Classic urban builders. Colonial–modern: extreme demographic collapse, vanilla–
tobacco commercialization, weak spiritual conquest, and a multi-century Papantla rebellion
tradition through Porfirian land division and revolutionary agrarian violence.

## Historiography (colonial–modern)

Deans-Smith’s Ch. 18 is a bibliographical survey of secondary literature (Ducey, González de
la Lama, Meade, Masferrer Kan, Chenaut, Kelly & Palerm, Wilkerson, Chávez-Hita, etc.), not a
full ethnohistorical synthesis — post-conquest Gulf work remains thin relative to Maya/Nahua.
