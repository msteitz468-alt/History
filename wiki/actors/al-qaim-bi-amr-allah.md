# Al-Qa'im bi-Amr Allah

**Abu'l-Qasim Muhammad al-Qa'im bi-Amr Allah** ("the One who Arises by the Command of God," r. 934–946) was
the **second [[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] Imam-caliph**. Born at **Salamiyya** in 893, he had fled with his
father [[al-mahdi-billah|al-Mahdi]] as a boy on the seven-year journey to [[sijilmasa|Sijilmasa]], and stood at
the Imam's right hand at the *zuhur* of 909. His reign spanned a decade of relative peace and then the
near-fatal **[[khariji-revolt-of-abu-yazid-943|revolt of Abu Yazid]]**.

## Heir apparent

Proclaimed ***wali al-ahd*** (Guardian of the Covenant) early in al-Mahdi's reign, Abu'l-Qasim took the
regnal title al-Qa'im and acted as his father's field commander: he led the **first Fatimid expedition to
Egypt in 914**, briefly holding **Alexandria** and delivering the Id al-Fitr khutba there — the first time
Egyptians heard a Fatimid prince proclaim the duties of a righteous Imam — before Abbasid reinforcements drove
him out. He suppressed Kutama revolts that raised rival *mahdis*, and in **914** his son **Isma'il** (the
future [[al-mansur-fatimid|al-Mansur]]) was born, the first Fatimid ruler born in Ifriqiya.

## Reign and the great revolt

Al-Qa'im succeeded in 934. His first decade was largely peaceful apart from proxy conflict with the
**Umayyads of Córdoba** on the western frontier, where the loyal **Sanhaja** chieftain **Ziri b. Manad** built
the fortress-city of **Ashir** to hold the line. But from **943** the Kharijite Berber preacher
**[[abu-yazid-al-nukkari|Abu Yazid]]** — "the Man on the Donkey," backed by the Umayyads and by Qayrawan's
disaffected Sunni Maliki scholars — overran almost all of Ifriqiya, pushing Fatimid rule back to the coast and
laying **siege to [[mahdiyya|Mahdiyya]]** (January–September 945). The walls al-Mahdi had built held. Al-Qa'im,
dying, composed a sermon praising the Kutama as "like the Apostles of Jesus, and the Ansar of Muhammad," and
publicly named Isma'il his heir before dying on **18 May 946** — his death kept secret while the war still
hung in the balance.

## Counterfactual significance

Al-Qa'im's reign is the dynasty's survival test. Had Mahdiyya fallen to Abu Yazid, the Fatimid experiment would
have ended in a single generation; that it did not owed much to al-Mahdi's fortress and to the Kutama's
tenacity. His decision to withhold news of his own death so his son could finish the war as crown prince rather
than as a contested new caliph preserved the succession intact.
