# Rarámuri (Tarahumara)

## Overview

The **Rarámuri** (Spanish ethnonym **Tarahumara**) are the **largest** indigenous people of northwest Mexico’s Sierra Madre Occidental—about **60,000** at the writing of Susan M. Deeds’s CHNPA II.2 Ch. 13, largest not only of the sierra but of the whole northwest. Deeds opens her chapter with a Rarámuri text (Dolores Batista, Bocoyna, Chihuahua) stressing **memory** as preserving an incorruptible worldview and polarization of “the people” from outsiders (*chabochis*, “children of the devil”). Historians may see multiple identity-fashionings under domination; indigenous views emphasize cultural reproduction of knowledge. Colonial records of these oral societies are overwhelmingly outsider-created and must be read skeptically.

## Colonial trajectory

### Rebellions and dual settlement pattern

Uprisings involving Rarámuri and other eastern Nueva Vizcayan groups at **mid-17th century**, and Rarámuri with Pimas in the **1690s**, fall under Deeds’s **first-generation rebellion** type—within a generation or two of effective Spanish mining/mission penetration; led by former war leaders and shamans; ultimately failed against Indian allies and exemplary punishments. Midcentury rebellions **deterred further penetration** of northwestern Rarámuri territory for nearly a quarter century.

After missions and mines entered the **upper Tarahumara** in the 1670s–80s, dual 1690s rebellions joined northern Conchos, eastern Pimas, and sierra-divide groups; harsh repression after the second revolt. Some Rarámuri accepted rebuilt Jesuit missions (more tolerant of disobedience and fugitivism); others isolated in inhospitable western canyons. Eastern-foothill Rarámuri followed a Tepehuan-like mission pattern (trade corn/foodstuffs, mine labor).

### Mission attachment, flight, and autonomy

Even mission-attached Rarámuri preferred **itinerant ranchería** life, seasonal moves, sometimes raiding with Apaches. Raiding may have increased after **Jesuit expulsion 1767** (mission livestock and grains appropriated by the crown). Late 18th/early 19th c. Franciscans reestablished some missions; many communities left alone; some never under Catholic influence. Mexican independence went largely unnoticed in the Sierra Madre; de facto autonomy while silver declined and authorities focused on Apaches.

**Syncretism:** livestock, new cultigens, metal tools even among the least acculturated; elders’ sermons blended God/devil/sun/moon/Jesus/Mary; *tesgüino* fiestas linked dispersed rancherías; mission-introduced native official hierarchy persisted in pueblos; **soul concept** key and non-Christian (Merrill). Women and elders transmitted moral boundaries stigmatizing non-Indian intercourse as malevolent, decadent, shameless.

SE Rarámuri shared the mid-18th-c. **peasantizing** trajectory of Tepehuanes, Opatas, and Mayos as non-Indians flooded the Durango–southern Chihuahua heartland; Jesuits volunteered secularization of lower Tarahumara missions in the **1740s**.

## National period and Porfiriato

Chihuahua **1825** colonization law opened eastern Tarahumara crop/grasslands; Rarámuri retreated deeper; midcentury mining pushed further. **Porfiriato:** U.S. mining (esp. Batopilas), forestry, railroads; by **1890** more than **2 million acres** of SW Chihuahua controlled by the Batopilas Mining Company plus Cargill and Hearst lumber. Former eastern and upper Papigochi villages became predominantly mestizo. Few Rarámuri joined mestizo revolts such as **Tomóchic 1891**; preferred temporary capitalist jobs over permanent peonage. Jesuits allowed federal return **1900** for “civilizing.”

## Revolution and twentieth century

**Limited Revolution participation**—withdrawal and distrust of outsider alliances even when promising land restoration. Agrarian reform granted western Chihuahua forests as *ejidos*, but mestizo beneficiaries expanded faster and controlled local politics. Large-scale lumbering resumption **1950s–60s** was the most serious modern displacement; *ejidos* failed as basis for communal autonomy; Rarámuri often held the most subordinate sawmill jobs even on their own *ejidos*.

Post-Revolution state and Catholic (esp. Jesuit) efforts continued **paternalist acculturation** via 1920s–30s cultural missions, INI, proselytization—less dogmatic/racist than colonial but intolerant of obstacles to modernization. Passive resistance and withdrawal remain main weapons. Threatened by Copper Canyon tourism and outsider marijuana cultivation. Rarámuri runners compete in U.S. races for famine-relief funds.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Rarámuri midcentury and 1690s resistance, Spanish mining/mission penetration of the western sierra would likely have accelerated, compressing the “region of refuge” that later sustained demographic recovery and cultural distinctiveness. Without rigid *chabochi* proscriptions and canyon mobility, peasantization/mestizaje might have erased them as thoroughly as Acaxees/Xiximes or lowland Opatas—showing that **nature of accommodation** (flight + selective material adoption + moral boundary maintenance), not isolation alone, preserved identity (Deeds).
