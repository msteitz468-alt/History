## Overview

Ibn Torkeh ʿAlī b. Muḥammad Khojandī Iṣfahānī (d. c. 1432), known in Shīʿite scholarly circles by his title **Ṣāʾin al-Dīn**, was a Persian philosopher who played a pivotal but until recently underappreciated role in the intellectual history of Shīʿite Islam. He is now identified as one of the first thinkers systematically to harmonize Peripatetic philosophy, Illuminationist epistemology, and Shīʿite religious doctrine — paving the way for [[mulla-sadra]]'s *al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya* two centuries later.

His significance is documented in Hossein Ziai's chapter in [[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]] (ch. 19), which describes him as having "done much to recover the idea of the harmony between religion and philosophy, as well as mysticism (*ʿirfān*)." More than sixty of his works survive, though few have been published in critical editions accessible to Western scholars.

---

## Context and Career

Ibn Torkeh was educated in the clerical sciences and identified with the emerging Shīʿite scholarly (*ʿulamāʾ*) classes. He also held positions at the court of the **Gūrkānid Ilkhans** (the Timurid successors of the Mongol rulers of Iran), reflecting the pattern — common in later Iranian intellectual history — of scholastic thinkers who combined service at temporal courts with the production of rationalist philosophical and religious texts. His "manifestly political philosophical core" was "allied to a real political agenda": the legitimacy of divinely inspired rule by select members of the scholarly class.

His principal audience was the Shīʿite clerical intelligentsia, and his most important text, *Tamhīd al-qawāʿid* (Preparation of Foundations), was composed in a "language and style comprehensible by the multitude" (Āshtiyānī, cited in Ziai, ch. 19), in contrast to the more technically demanding works of later Ṣadrān philosophy.

---

## Philosophical Position

Ibn Torkeh's distinctive contribution was the systematic integration of three intellectual currents:

1. **Falsafa (Peripatetic philosophy):** The Avicennan tradition, including its technical language for ontology and epistemology.
2. **Illuminationist epistemology:** Following Suhrawardī, Ibn Torkeh drew on the notion of *al-ʿilm al-ḥuḍūrī* (knowledge by presence) — immediate, intuitive knowledge prior to predicative knowledge — to give epistemological priority to inspired and experiential cognition over syllogistic reasoning alone.
3. **Shīʿite religious philosophy:** Ibn Torkeh was "among the first to draw on the Illuminationist epistemology of knowledge by presence and use it to give priority to intuitive and inspired knowledge, especially in the case of primary principles." (Ziai, ch. 19)

The result was a philosophical foundation for the doctrine that each era has its own personification of knowledge (*aʿlam*) — the most learned Shīʿite scholar of the time — who by virtue of his heightened intuitive capacities can renew the foundations of religious knowledge. This is the philosophical grounding for the institution of the *marjaʿ al-taqlīd* ("source of imitation") that became central to Shīʿite political theology.

Ibn Torkeh also incorporated **non-polemical *kalām*** (rational theology) as a tool against Ashʿarite anti-rationalism and used Qurʾānic exegesis to support rationalist jurisprudence, producing accessible rational analyses of the five Pillars of Islam.

---

## Principal Work: *Tamhīd al-qawāʿid*

The *Tamhīd al-qawāʿid* (Preparation of Foundations) is Ibn Torkeh's most significant surviving work. Ziai describes it as "a text on phenomenology and philosophy of religion, in which the fundamental political doctrine of the legitimacy of divinely inspired rule by select members of the *ʿulamāʾ* class is upheld." (ch. 19)

The work was widely studied by subsequent generations of Shīʿite scholars. Significant twentieth-century scholars of the "intellectual sciences" who studied it include Ziyāʾ al-Dīn Dorrī (d. 1336 A.H.), Āqā Mīrzā Muḥammad Qomsheʾī (d. 1306 A.H.), and several others documented by Sadughi [258]. The text has been edited by Seyyed Jalāl al-Dīn Āshtiyānī, with a 200-page analytical introduction; previous lithograph editions were unreliable.

---

## Historical Significance

Ibn Torkeh's role in Shīʿite intellectual history is structural rather than spectacular: he legitimized the "intellectual sciences" (*ʿulūm-e ʿaqlī*) as a domain of clerical activity, made philosophy safe for the Shīʿite establishment by presenting it as compatible with — indeed supportive of — revealed religion, and thereby created the cultural conditions within which more radical thinkers (Mīr Dāmād, Mullā Ṣadrā, and their successors) could operate. Without the prior acceptance of philosophy in the Shīʿite scholarly world that figures like Ibn Torkeh helped establish, the "School of Isfahan" could not have developed as it did.

> "Thanks to figures like Ṣāʾin al-Dīn, the Shīʿite clergy came to accept the notion of the 'intellectual sciences' (*al-ʿulūm al-ʿaqliyya*), which use philosophy as philosophy, without reducing it to the role of a 'handmaiden', and which treat Greek philosophers with reverence instead of the hostility evinced by anti-rationalists like Ibn Taymiyya." (Ziai, ch. 19)

He is relatively obscure in Western scholarship — Ziai notes this explicitly and provides a detailed bibliographic note to compensate.

## Historiography

The primary account available in the collection is Ziai's chapter in [[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]]. The relevant Persian-language scholarship includes J. Naʿīnī's introduction to his Persian translation of Shahrastānī's *al-Milal wa al-niḥal*; S. A. M. Behbahani's article "Aḥvāl va Āsār-e Ṣāʾin al-Dīn Torkeh-ye Iṣfahānī" in Mohaghegh and Landolt [255]; and Āshtiyānī's edition of the *Tamhīd al-qawāʿid*. The Western scholarly tradition has largely overlooked Ibn Torkeh, whose significance has been reconstructed primarily by Iranian scholars.
