# John Toland

Irish-born freethinking philosopher (1670–1722), a leading deist and pantheist (he coined the word "pantheist"). His posthumously published *History of the Druids* (in letters to Lord Molesworth, 1720s) was an early and influential attempt to reconstruct Druidic religion and history, drawing on his knowledge of Gaelic Ireland and Scotland. Toland presented the Druids partly as cynical priestcraft — a vehicle for his anticlerical critique of organized religion — while also supplying material that later writers, including [[actors/stukeley-william|William Stukeley]], would build upon.

In Hutton's account Toland is a key early-eighteenth-century contributor to the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]], illustrating how Druids served as a screen onto which religious controversialists projected their own concerns.

## Significance

### History of the Druids and Religious Polemic

Toland was born in Gaelic-speaking Ireland and educated in Scotland and continental Europe; his first major work *Christianity Not Mysterious* (1696) scandalized orthodox opinion and led to its public burning in Dublin. His engagement with Druids was characteristic of his broader method: using an apparently historical subject as a vehicle for religious controversy. His known pronouncements on Druids were internally contradictory, "varying from the fiercely condemnatory to the warmly approving" ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]). This inconsistency reflects the double function Druids served in his writing — as examples both of dangerous priestcraft and of an original rational religion anterior to revealed Christianity.

### Pantheisticon (1720) and the Socratic-Druid Parallel

Toland's last book, *Pantheisticon* (published 1720, the year of his death, though composed earlier), was his most explicit engagement with the Druid parallel. It described a society of "Socratic companions" whose views Toland claimed to represent, and drew a direct parallel between their intellectual community and the ancient Druids: the "companions" "strenuously ruminate upon the same studies, for which the Druids and Pythagoras made themselves so illustrious." However, the work simultaneously maintained a careful distance: "the Pantheists allow not all their words and deeds; for where they depart from truth, there we also depart from them." Hutton's reading is that *Pantheisticon* "was presented as a project for a group that did not yet exist, rather than a portrait of one that did" — Toland died before being able to implement it, and the text is best understood as utopian projection rather than record of a real society ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

### Sociability Without Organization

Toland was "certainly intensely sociable, enjoying participation in various circles and salons both in Britain and on the Continent." His surviving manuscripts include the regulations for a dining club, the Knights of Jubilation, founded by French Protestant refugees at the Hague in 1710 — though his own name does not appear on the member list. More broadly, "it is actually very hard to prove that he belonged to any formal society, lodge or order at any time or place." Rumors of involvement with secret radical clubs attended him from student days onward without proof ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

### Debunking of the Universal Bond Foundation Legend

Twentieth-century Druid tradition, especially as propagated by Ross Nichols and the Circle of the Universal Bond, claimed that Toland had founded a Druid order in 1717, assembling delegates from across Britain, Ireland, and Brittany at a London tavern and proclaiming the "Mother Grove" (Gairdeachas) on Primrose Hill at the autumn equinox. Hutton's analysis shows this to be a myth assembled from spliced historical events: the Primrose Hill autumn equinox meeting was actually the Gorsedd of the Bards of Britain convened by [[actors/morganwg-iolo|Iolo Morganwg]] in 1792; the London meeting of four lodges at the Apple Tree Tavern was the founding of the Freemasons' first Grand Lodge at Midsummer 1717, organized by John Theophilus Desaguliers, not Toland. The argument that Toland's hostile remarks about Druids were a concealment of his leadership is met with the observation that it "begs the question of why his remarks on the subject were so inconsistent; a different explanation has been proposed" ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]).

### Influence on Later Writers

Toland's *History of the Druids* and his broader anticlerical framing supplied material to subsequent writers who handled it selectively. John Smith, an Argyll parish minister who published bogus Highland bardic poetry in 1780, based his account of the Druids "mainly on the work of Toland, removing the latter's prejudices and substituting those of Smith himself." Joseph Strutt, the English antiquary, was similarly "just repeating, wholesale, what he found in [Robert] Henry's book with some additions from Toland." On the other hand, William Blake's engagement with Druidry represented a deliberate counter-position: "Whereas John Toland had demonized Druids as over-mighty priests, Blake — just as radical in his attitude to political and religious norms — demonized them as deists like Toland himself" ([[sources/hutton-blood-and-mistletoe-2009|Hutton, Blood and Mistletoe (2009)]]). This reversal is structurally ironic, since Toland's deism was itself what Blake attacked through his image of Druidry.

## Historiography

Hutton positions Toland as an early-eighteenth-century contributor to the [[processes/druid-revival|Druid Revival]] whose significance lay less in the content of his Druid scholarship than in the anticlerical and pantheistic framework he brought to the subject. The argument that Toland represents an "enlightened" or proto-secular appreciation of Druidry as against Stukeley's Christian-patriarchal reading is nuanced by Hutton's observation that Toland's positions were internally inconsistent and motivated by polemical rather than scholarly aims. The Universal Bond's genealogy from Toland is treated as myth.
