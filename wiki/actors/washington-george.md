# George Washington (1732–1799)

Commander in chief of the **[[continental-army|Continental Army]]** (1775–1783) and the
indispensable figure of the **[[american-revolution|American Revolution]]**'s military
effort. A Virginia planter, militia colonel, and veteran of the
[[seven-years-war-1756|French and Indian War]] (he served under Forbes in the 1758
expedition to Fort Duquesne), Washington was appointed unanimously by Congress on 15 June
1775 — his Virginia identity, military experience, and imposing physical presence all
weighing in his selection. A delegate described him as "discreet & Virtuous, no harum
Starum ranting Swearing fellow but Sober, steady, & Calm."

## Generalship

The *West Point History* (Lengel) stresses that Washington was **not by temperament a
"Fabian"**: "Far from being a 'Fabian' leader who sought to preserve his own army while
wearing down the enemy … Washington sought to take the war to the enemy and force a quick
end to the conflict." He grasped two strategic realities — **British naval dominance** and
**American economic weakness** — and concluded the war "would have to be won quickly, or not
at all," gambling repeatedly on decisive action.

The catastrophes of 1776 forced a reluctant adjustment. After the loss of New York he wrote
that "the War should be defensive … we should on all occasions avoid a general Action … unless
compelled by a necessity into which we ought never to be drawn" — a strategy of preserving the
army (Fabian in effect, though Washington chafed at its passivity). His genius lay less in
tactics than in **holding the army — and the cause — together**: the recovery at
[[battle-of-trenton-1776|Trenton]] and [[battle-of-princeton-1777|Princeton]], the personal
leadership that carried the army through [[valley-forge-1777|Valley Forge]], and the
combination Lengel calls "firm command while demonstrating empathy for his soldiers." His
own maxim: "A people unused to restraint must be led; they will not be drove."

Conway's assessment is deliberately deflationary: Washington "lost — or at least failed to
win — most of the battles he fought," and "arguably, Washington's chief contribution was to
act as the unflappable symbol of American resistance." The 1781 march to
[[siege-of-yorktown-1781|Yorktown]] — masking his intentions from [[clinton-henry|Clinton]]
and converging with [[continental-army|French allies]] by land and sea — was his finest
operational stroke.

## Counterfactual significance

Without Washington's steadiness in 1776–78, the Continental Army might well have dissolved
or been destroyed in detail; without his scrupulous **subordination to civilian authority**
(see Newburgh, 1783), the new republic's civil-military settlement would have been far less
secure. He became the first president (1789) and personally led 10,000 militia to suppress
the **Whiskey Rebellion** (1794), demonstrating federal coercive capacity.
