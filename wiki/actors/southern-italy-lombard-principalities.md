# Southern Italy and the Lombard Principalities (10th–early 11th c.)

In the long tenth century **southern Italy** was a fragmented and contested borderland where three powers met: the **Lombard principalities**, the **Byzantine** provinces, and the **Arabs** of [[caliphate-of-cordoba|Muslim]] **Sicily**. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] treats it as a frontier zone whose very fragmentation would, a generation later, open the door to the **Normans**.

## The Three Powers

- **The Lombard principalities.** The old duchy of Benevento had splintered into the rival principalities of **Benevento, Salerno, and Capua**, ruled by competing Lombard dynasties whose endemic feuding defined the region's politics. Their warfare repeatedly drew in outside powers.
- **The Byzantine south.** Byzantium held **Apulia and Calabria**, reorganised in the later tenth century into the **katepanate of Italy** with its capital at **Bari** — the western anchor of the [[macedonian-dynasty|Macedonian]] revival, periodically reinforced and target of Ottonian ambition (Otto II's disastrous campaign ended at **Cotrone, 982**).
- **The Arabs of Sicily.** Sicily, conquered from Byzantium over the ninth century, was ruled in this period by the **Kalbid emirate** under Fatimid (then increasingly autonomous) authority. Sicilian fleets **raided the mainland** continually and exacted tribute, while Sicily itself flourished as a prosperous Islamic society.
- **The maritime duchies.** Nominally Byzantine but effectively independent, the trading cities of **Naples, Amalfi, and Gaeta** pursued their own commerce — Amalfi a notable early Mediterranean trading power — often dealing pragmatically with all sides, Muslim included.

## A Contested Frontier

No single power could master the south. The Lombard princes played Byzantium and the Western emperors against each other; Byzantium and the Ottonians disputed overlordship (resolved nominally in Otto I's favour over Benevento and Capua, sealed by the marriage of Otto II to **Theophanu**, 972); and Arab Sicily preyed on all. This unstable equilibrium of small powers — Lombard, Greek, Arab, and the autonomous trading cities — left southern Italy without a dominant state.

## Significance

The very fragmentation of tenth-century southern Italy set the stage for the **eleventh-century transformation**: the **Norman** adventurers who arrived as mercenaries in the early eleventh century exploited the rivalries of Lombards, Byzantines, and Arabs to carve out a county and then the **kingdom of Sicily** — one of the most important political creations of the central Middle Ages.
