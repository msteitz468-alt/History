# Amenophis III (Amenhotep III)

The "Magnificent" — pharaoh of the [[new-kingdom-egypt|Eighteenth Dynasty]] (reigned
~1390–1352 BCE), under whom imperial Egypt reached its peak of **wealth, peace, and
artistic splendour**. His reign closes the coverage of this volume, on the eve of the
Amarna revolution of his son Akhenaten. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. IX, Hayes).

## A reign of two phases

CAH divides his ~38 years in two. A first decade of **youthful activity** — widely
publicized big-game (lion and wild-bull) hunts and a single military expedition into
Upper Nubia — was followed by nearly three decades of **luxurious peace**, in which
Amenophis III withdrew to his vast palace at **Malkata** in western Thebes and
presided over "an unparalleled production of magnificent works of architecture,
sculpture, and fine craftsmanship." He celebrated three royal **jubilees** (*sed*-
festivals, years 30, 34, 37). Throughout, the commoner-born **Queen Tiy** remained
"the dominant influence in the king's life."

## Diplomacy and the great-powers system

Amenophis III ruled an empire so secure that he scarcely needed to fight for it. His
reign is the high noon of Late Bronze **diplomacy by royal marriage**: he took into
his harem a succession of **Asiatic princesses** — from [[mitanni|Mitanni]], Babylon,
and Arzawa — cementing alliances later vividly documented in the **Amarna
letters** (which open in his reign). The marriage alliance with **[[mitanni|
Mitanni]]**, begun by his father Thutmose IV against the rising **[[hittites|
Hittites]]**, was the keystone of his foreign policy — Egypt and Mitanni standing
together as the **[[interpolity-law|"great powers"]]** at the apex of the
international system.

## The golden age and its shadow

His monuments — the Luxor temple, the colossal "Memnon" statues of his mortuary
temple, the scarab-series broadcasting his hunts and marriages — make his reign the
artistic summit of the dynasty. Yet beneath the magnificence two strains were
building: the colossal wealth and influence of the **[[amun-priesthood|Amun
priesthood]]**, and the gathering **[[hittites|Hittite]]** power that would soon
break the Mitannian buffer. Both came to a head under his son **Akhenaten**, whose
religious revolution (the Amarna period, beyond this volume) is in part a reaction to
the over-mighty Theban priesthood his father's piety had enriched.

## Counterfactual / analytical significance

Amenophis III represents the **mature empire at rest** — proof that Thutmose III's
conquests had created a system stable enough to be governed by diplomacy and display
rather than by the sword. His reign is the documentary dawn of the **Amarna
international order**, the most fully attested moment of Bronze Age great-power
diplomacy. It also marks the **turning point** before decline: the calm before the
Hittite resurgence and the religious upheaval that together open the troubled later
Late Bronze Age.

## Historiography

Documented by monumental architecture and sculpture, the **commemorative scarab
series** (a kind of royal news-bulletin), the tomb of Kheruef, and the opening of the
**Amarna diplomatic archive**. The reign's chronology is anchored astronomically; the
debate over a possible long **co-regency with Akhenaten** affects how its end and the
Amarna transition are dated. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
