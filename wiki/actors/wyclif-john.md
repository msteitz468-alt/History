# John Wyclif

## Overview

**John Wyclif** (d. 1384), Oxford don of what [[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] calls “middling theological abilities,” held controversial views condemned at a council at **Blackfriars, London, in 1382**. He died without personal excommunication, protected by the royal family (especially the mother of young Richard II). Though himself an absentee pluralist, he attacked church abuses and developed ecclesiological and sacramental positions that, transmitted to Bohemia, helped shape the milieu of **[[hus-jan|Jan Hus]]**.

## Teaching (per Logan)

- Unrighteous churchmen lack genuine authority; obedience need not be given to the unrighteous of any dignity.
- After consecration, residual substance of bread and wine remains; he denied the physical presence of Christ in the consecrated species.
- He **denied the existence of [[purgatory|purgatory]]**.

Logan notes that all these “ventures into heterodoxy might have remained a purely English affair” had they not crossed to Bohemia.

## Transmission to Bohemia

The marriage of **Richard II** to **Anne of Bohemia** (sister of King Wenceslaus) in **1382** transported Wyclif’s teachings to the “tinder-box of Bohemia”—possibly via clerical members of Queen Anne’s household after her death (1394). Many surviving manuscript copies of his works exist only in **Czech libraries**. Reception was warm at Prague, not at Oxford. Pre-Wyclif Bohemia already saw Czech resentment of German dominance (<10% of population) over the University of Prague and higher church ranks—the milieu in which Hus came of age.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Anglo-Bohemian dynastic link, Wyclif’s heterodoxy might have stayed a limited English controversy (Lollardy) rather than feeding a continental reform that produced Hus’s martyrdom and the [[hussite-wars-1419|Hussite Wars]].

## Historiography

Logan’s brief profile subordinates Wyclif to the Constance–Hus story: an English spark, not the schism’s cause. He does not treat full Lollard social history; Blackfriars 1382 and the Bohemian manuscript trail are the load-bearing facts in his account.
