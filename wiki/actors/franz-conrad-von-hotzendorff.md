# Franz Conrad von Hötzendorff (1852–1925)

Austro-Hungarian Chief of the General Staff (1906–1911, 1912–1917). Leading advocate of preventive war as an instrument of policy; central figure in the July Crisis decision for war against Serbia.

## Role and views

Conrad was a social Darwinist who believed "the struggle for existence was 'the basic principle behind all the events on this earth'" and "the only real and rational basis for policy making." He regarded war as inevitable for Austria-Hungary to preserve great-power status and saw Serbia (and earlier Italy) as the key threat whose irridentism was both foreign and domestic. He advocated preventive war against Serbia repeatedly: 1906, 1908–9, 1912–13, October 1913, May 1914, and "between 1 January 1913 and 1 January 1914 he proposed a Serbian war twenty-five times."

In the July Crisis he pushed for decisive military action rather than sabre-rattling; a repetition of mobilizations without fighting "would be bad for the army’s morale." By summer 1914 his advocacy had shifted from calculated opportunity (Russia's weakness) to resigned fatalism: "war would be 'a hopeless struggle, but even so, it must be because such an ancient monarchy and such an ancient army cannot perish ingloriously'." He was "va banque."

## Influence on July Crisis

Conrad's earlier exchanges with Moltke (1909) secured German assurance that if Russia mobilized for Serbia, Germany would mobilize too. This gave him confidence to turn against Serbia with Germany deterring or fighting Russia. His pressure helped accustom Franz Joseph's ministers to the expectation of war. In late July he initially urged delay (harvest leave) but his broader insistence on real fighting (not just threat) reinforced Berchtold's resolve. On 30/31 July Moltke urged him to mobilize against Russia rather than Serbia.

## Limitations and context

Conrad was politically isolated after falling out with Franz Ferdinand; his personal life (long infatuation with Gina von Reininghaus) intertwined with policy calculations. Austrian army reorganization after 1912 was incomplete until 1915. He recognized Balkan League forces could outnumber Austria-Hungary. His views reflected broader militarization of diplomacy in the pre-war period but were not representative of all Habsburg opinion (Franz Ferdinand himself had been a voice for restraint).

## Eastern Front planning and 1914 limitations (Strachan Range 05)

Conrad’s army was the most under-gunned in Europe relative to its size (common-army division 42 guns vs 54 German; Landwehr/Honved 24 or fewer). Manpower training lagged (0.29% of population vs Germany 0.47%); the 1889 recruit law and Magyar obstruction meant the decade before 1912 could not be made up. Ethnic composition (25% German officers dominating a polyglot force) and the three-part structure (common army, Landwehr, Honved) created command-language chaos on mobilization. Conrad remained wedded to the offensive despite these limits: “victory would fall to him who seized the initiative, even if his forces were inferior.” His Galician plan evolved from northward thrust (1908) to a west-weighted concentration on the San (left wing 30 divisions) by March 1914 to shorten the front and mitigate Romanian unreliability and Redl treason risks; 2nd Army arrival delayed to days 21–25. He continued to labour under the illusion (fostered by 1909 Moltke exchanges) of timely German support and a joint envelopment in Poland, even after Moltke’s 10 February 1913 statement that Austria’s fate would be decided on the Seine. On 1 August 1914 the truth arrived: no German offensive in Poland; Austria-Hungary was on its own in the east. Partial mobilization against Serbia (and rail inflexibility) forfeited any initial advantage over the Russians. `produced:` the outnumbered, delayed, and poorly coordinated Austrian effort in Galicia that left the Central Powers’ eastern flank dependent on German success at Tannenberg and later operations.
