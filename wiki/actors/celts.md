# The Celts (Iron Age Europe)

## Overview

The **Celts** were the dominant peoples of Iron Age temperate Europe north and west of the
Alps — "**one of the major barbarian neighbours of the classical world**," as the Greeks
(Hecataeus, Herodotus) already called them in the sixth and fifth centuries.
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 9c, D. W. Harding) surveys them archaeologically as
part of the volume's coverage of the **North** — one of the few substantial treatments of
Celtic Europe in the wiki, a region the collection covers thinly.

Cunliffe defines a Celt as "a person who spoke the Celtic language" (a workable generalization
acknowledging that some spoke multiple languages or used Celtic as lingua franca). Koch (ed. 2006) codifies the post-Celtoscepticism position: the defining criterion is "peoples and countries that do, or once did, use Celtic languages", with greater circumspection for non-linguistic (e.g. La Tène) extensions. Vols II–III add Insular Christianity (Colum Cille/Columbanus/Iona/Easter), heroic cycles (Cú Chulainn/Dagda/dindshenchas), languages (Goidelic/Common Celtic), modern revival (eisteddfod/Conradh), Continental (Galatia/Gaul/Greek-Roman accounts). The term
has multiple historical realities: classical ethnonym (Keltoi/Celtae general; Galli/Galatae
for specific migrants), linguistic family (Continental and Insular branches, with earliest
direct evidence 8th c. BCE in SW Iberia), and archaeological material culture (Hallstatt and
especially La Tène, though these cannot be equated one-to-one with language or classical
label). "Celt" remains useful at a level of generalization for shared language, belief systems,
value systems, and connectivity, much as Iberian, Scythian, or Greek (Cunliffe 2018, Ch. 1, p. 27; Ch. 3, p. 53; [[sources/cunliffe-ancient-celts-2018|Cunliffe 2018]]).

## "When did Europe become Celtic?"

Harding frames the central question — "when did Celtic Europe become Celtic, and in what
sense?" — and warns against equating "Celtic" narrowly with **Celtic-speakers** or with a
single archaeological culture. The linguistic evidence (personal, tribal and place-names,
inscriptions across Europe) shows the peoples were indeed **linguistically Celtic**, and
that an Indo-European language had spread by direct settlement before the mid-first
millennium. But this need not align with any one "culture"; better to think of a
**progressive "cumulative Celticity"** reaching back through the Bronze Age **Urnfield**
horizon, with strong continuity into the Iron Age.

- **La Tène (from c. 450 BCE):** the culture most often identified with the historical

## The fourth-century migrations

For the period of this volume, the salient fact is the **Celtic migrations**: the great
movements of La Tène peoples out of the central European homeland into **northern Italy**
(the Po valley, the "Gauls" who sacked **Rome** c. 390/387) and down the **Danube** toward
the Balkans (culminating, beyond this volume, in the invasion of Greece and the sack-attempt
on **Delphi** in 279, and the settlement of Galatia in Asia Minor). The Celts thus became,
in the fourth century, a force impinging directly on the classical Mediterranean from the
north — the temperate-European counterweight to the events in Greece and the Aegean.

**Cunliffe 2018 range-grounded detail (migrations 450–50 BCE):** The Po valley settlement created "the continuous threat created by the Celtic settlement in the Po valley" that was "instrumental in the honing of Rome’s military competence" (Polybius framing Celts as volatile/impulsive vs. Roman steadiness). Galatian tribes (Tolistobogii, Trocmi) active 189 BCE (naked warriors noted). Egypt service from "the horde that entered Asia Minor in 278": Celts in Ptolemy II's army by 274 BCE; 4,000 rebels perished on Nile island per Pausanias; later Fayum cleruchs (shield preserved); 6,000 Gauls/Thracians at Raphia 217 BCE; identity retained into 2nd c BCE (Abydos, Hermopolis, Alexandrian stelae). Memories of "camped in its shadow waiting to pillage Delphi." Social drivers included feast/potlatch systems enabling raid leadership and followings, clientage, ritual elements (torcs, nakedness, carnyces). "close social links were maintained between the Celts who had emigrated to the south and those who remained in their northern homelands" (evidenced by 4th-c Po Valley transmission of Graeco-Italic Vegetal Style art back to Marne/north: shared helmets, motifs). See [[concepts/celtic-migrations]], [[processes/celtic-migrations]], [[sources/cunliffe-ancient-celts-2018]]. (All verbatim from assigned range.)

**Atlantic façade communities (Britain, Gaul, Iberia):** Shared La Tène warrior culture with regional traits. Britain: chariots prominent (Caesar: "combined the staying power of infantry with the mobility of cavalry"; Cassivellaunus 4,000 chariots); sling stockpiles at Maiden Castle/Danebury; leaders Cartimandua (Brigantes), Boudica (Iceni, gold torc); "insular Britons" retained older equipment. Ireland: no chariot evidence despite literature; Clonoura leather shield, Lough Crew trial pieces. Gaul: Caesar details (cavalry, classes "Druids and the knights", clientage networks); sites like Saint-Pol-de-Léon (Finistère), Tintignac (carnyces). Iberia: possible Iberian-origin brooches with naked warriors; Celtiberians. Art/warfare examples span façade (Battersea/Witham shields, Welwyn fire-dogs, Newbridge chariot Scotland, Gussage All Saints metalwork context). See [[places/ireland]], [[places/france]], [[places/italy]]. (Grounded in Cunliffe range.)

## Significance

The Celts represent the **deep hinterland of Mediterranean civilization** — a network of
warrior aristocracies, hillfort centres and long-distance trade that supplied the classical
world with metals and manpower and periodically erupted into it. Their archaeology is the
principal evidence for **Iron Age Europe beyond the literate frontier**, and the
"cumulative Celticity" model is a caution against tying ethnicity too tightly to either
language or a single material culture.

## Historiography (Cunliffe 2018, R01 range)

The image of the Celt was constructed and reconstructed across two millennia. Classical authors created a barbarian stereotype (fearless but despairing warriors, headhunters, hospitable yet unreliable) to contrast with Graeco-Roman order; visualized in Pergamon sculptures (Dying Gaul etc.). Celtomania (Stukeley, Macpherson Ossian, Iolo Morganwg, Napoleon III digs at Alesia/Bibracte) served Romantic and nationalist ends. Archaeology (Hallstatt 1846, La Tène 1857, Bologna 1871 correlation, Jacobsthal 1944 art styles) supplied data but was long subordinated to linguistic migration models, producing circular invasion narratives. The 1990s–2010s debate rejected such forcing; Cunliffe defines Celt via language while emphasizing Atlantic connectivity and Beaker-phase spread over central European homeland + mass migration. "Celt" is retained as useful generalization for shared language, values, and connectivity (verbatim grounding in source page R01 claims; [[sources/cunliffe-ancient-celts-2018|Cunliffe 2018]]).

Key from range: Tolkien "magic bag"; Hecataeus, Herodotus, Poseidonius, Caesar on names/vision; Lhuyd on languages; Atlantic lingua franca hypothesis vs traditional model.

## Eastern Fringes Communities (Cunliffe 2018, range_04)

In eastern Europe the situation differed markedly from the western and central zones. "The mountains of the Balkans formed an effective barrier between the Adriatic and the Middle Danube regions, sheltering the Celtic communities from Roman interference, apart from brief campaigns against brigands in the Upper Sava valley between 156 and 129 bc. In the Lower Danube valley the Thracians acted as a buffer against the expansion of Roman interest..." By the end of the second century bc "it is unlikely that any recognizable Celtic community existed east of the Middle Danube valley, except for the Galatians in their remote homeland in central Asia Minor."

To the east lay the Thracians (generally friendly) and Dacians; to the north Germanic groups. Dacian expansion under King Burebista (from 60/59 bc) devastated Celtic groups: he moved "first against the Scordisci and then against the Boii and Taurisci... The defeat of the Celtic tribes was so devastating that Strabo was able to write of the ‘desert of the Boii’." Displaced Boii entered Noricum and Helvetian territory, contributing to regional instability.

The kingdom of Noricum (eastern Alps, roughly modern Austria) exemplifies eastern Celtic polities: "It began as a group of loosely allied Celtic tribes but achieved a degree of unity sometime during the second century bc under the hegemony of the Nori of central eastern Carinthia." It commanded "rich supplies of iron, which were worked locally into a fine steel, well known in the Roman world as ferrum Noricum." Formal relations with Rome from 186 bc; annexed 15 bc after intensive trade and Romanization (ghetto of traders at Magdalensberg/Virunum).

Galatia in Asia Minor remained independent longer but was drawn into Roman orbit and annexed by Pompey in 67 bc.

## Warfare, Society and Hierarchy (Cunliffe 2018, range_04)

Warfare was integral to social structure. "the size of a leader’s following was seen as an indicator of his status. In earlier times... the raid provided the opportunity to demonstrate leadership and the feast was the occasion for the display of status before the assembled company." In the developed period (post ~200 bc) traditional raiding was curtailed by Roman power, Dacian expansion, and Germanic pressures. Replacement mechanisms included potlatch-style conspicuous consumption (e.g., Arvernian Louernius feasting multitudes and scattering gold/silver from his chariot) and coinage used for gift exchange and legitimizing leadership claims.

Warrior status expressed in burials: from late second century bc "warriors’ tombs, some provided with a rich array of grave goods, begin to appear" with imported wine equipment, hearth furniture, and "parts of chariots or chariot fittings." In Gaul richer graves often had "short swords with anthropoid hilts" possibly "an emblem of a particular social class." Headhunting was culturally significant: enemies' heads taken, displayed, sometimes embalmed or used ritually (e.g., Boii mounting a Roman consul's skull as a deity's cup).

Young warriors and entourages served as mercenaries or Roman auxiliaries to gain prestige and Roman ways: "an aspiring young warrior might place himself and his fighting entourage in the hands of Rome to serve as auxiliaries in the Roman army, much as his ancestors might have been engaged in a less formal way as mercenaries by the Mediterranean powers."

Sanctuary deposition of war gear was widespread (e.g., 2,000 broken weapons at Gournay-sur-Aronde).

Three broad social zones emerged by early first century bc: core (elected magistrates), intermediate (kings with oppida), periphery (elite burial rites persisting).

## La Tène Art and Material Culture of the Migration and Post-Migration Periods (Cunliffe 2018, range_04)

La Tène material culture included prestige items marking elite and warrior status: torcs (worn by chiefs and in ritual deposits), decorated mirrors and bronze bowls in female burials, short swords with anthropoid hilts, imported Roman bronze vessels and wine amphorae in tombs. Statues depict "a Gaulish chief wearing a tunic of chain mail and a torc and carrying a shield." These reflect a prestige goods economy evolving after the height of the migration period into systems of largesse, oppida production, and Roman clientage. Elite burials cluster in a peripheral zone "from Kent to Germany" in the Late La Tène, contrasting core oppida zones. (See also [[concepts/celtic-art]] and [[concepts/la-tene-culture]].)

## Retreat under Rome and Celtic Survival (Ch. 13–14)

Grounded exclusively in range_05 (Ch. 13 "The Celts in Retreat", Ch. 14 "Celtic Survival", and Retrospect synthesis; Cunliffe 2018). Additional context from earlier ranges preserved.

By the end of the first century AD most of the lands occupied by the Celtic peoples south of the Rhine–Danube line were under Roman occupation. "To the north of the Danube the Celtic territories of Bohemia and Moravia had become Germanic, while further east in the Great Hungarian Plain and Transylvania the Celtic enclaves had been absorbed either within the Dacian state or by the incoming Sarmatian tribes. It was only along the extreme north-western periphery, in north and west Scotland and in Ireland, that Celtic-speaking communities continued to exist without the dubious benefit of Romanization or other interference."

**Roman use of indigenous structures and client kings:** Roman admin reflected ethnic/tribal realities (Augustan Gaul mirroring Caesar's Aquitani/Celts/Belgae; civitates tied to pre-Roman tribes). In Britain: Iceni (Prasutagus until AD 60); Atrebates under Cogidubnus ("rex", Roman citizen); Cartimandua in north. Arrangements were temporary.

**Boudica and Agricola campaigns:** Boudican revolt caused major disruption in SE; Tacitus estimates 80,000 British died; reprisals delayed development for decades. Agricola (governor AD 78–84) completed subjugation of Scotland. "The final battle... against the army of Calgacus at Mons Graupius in the far north, on the south side of the Moray Firth. It was the last occasion when a Celtic army used chariots against a Roman force." Circumnavigation and Orkney submission followed. Roman hold tenuous; withdrawal to Tyne–Solway considered.

**Three zones of Roman Britain/Ireland:** "the Romanized core" (SE of Fosse Way; urban system, villas); "a peripheral zone" (SW peninsula, Wales, N Britain to Clyde–Forth; military control but little urbanization or Romanization; traditional patterns continued); "a free zone" (extreme N Britain, Ireland; native systems unhindered). "The degree to which Celtic culture survived was directly related to the intensity of Romanization reflected in these three zones."

**Tacitus on Romanization:** "Agricola gave private encouragement and official assistance to the building of temples, public squares, and private mansions. . . . He trained the sons of the chiefs in the liberal arts... so the Britons were gradually led on to the amenities that make vice agreeable—arcades, baths, and sumptuous banquets. They spoke of such novelties as ‘civilization’, when really they were only a feature of enslavement." (Agricola 21). Building evidence: Verulamium forum/basilica, Chichester baths, Fishbourne, Hayling temple rebuilt in masonry, Bath spring monumentalized. In Gaul west (Armorica): planned grid towns imposed on tribes (Rennes/Condate etc.); Bibracte gave way to Autun; some oppida (Alet, Le Yaudet) continued undeveloped or later refortified.

**Ireland free Celtic:** No Roman military presence. Trade (trinkets; possible Drumanagh port). Ritual centres (Tara, Emain Macha, Dún Ailinne, Cruachain). Pollen: forest regeneration suggesting 1st-millennium-BC population decline. Táin Bó Cúailnge reflects possible pan-Celtic oral epic.

**Religious systems persistence (sanctuaries, druids, language):** "elements of the Celtic belief systems remained—a reality demonstrated by the maintenance of many Celtic religious sites of great antiquity, temples such as Gournay and Hayling Island and sacred springs like those of Sulis and Coventina in Britain and Sequana in Gaul." Ausonius (4th c) notes Attius Patera (Burdigala) from Druidic family, possibly continuing pre-Conquest scholarship tradition. Consilium Galliarum fostered unity (used in 3rd-c Gallic Empire). Curse tablets at Sulis Minerva (Bath): Celtic names into 3rd/4th c AD, one full Celtic curse; parallels in Gaul (Amélie-les-Bains, Chamalières). Celtic dominant in un-Romanized zones.

**Ethnic readjustments 4th–6th c:** Germanic pressures (continuation of earlier patterns) led to raids/settlements; Latin dominant except Brittany. Irish raids (Attocotti, Picts; AD 367 Barbarian Conspiracy). Permanent Irish colonial settlements (Déisi SW Wales; Lleyn; Scotti/Dál Riata in W Scotland). British folk movement to Armorica (Brittany) from mid-5th c (from Cornwall/Devon/S Wales leadership): territories Domnonée/Dumnonia, Cornouaille, Browaroch/Broërec. Place names (Plou-/Lan-/Tré- with British names); Breton language debate (British introduction vs. indigenous Gaulish survival + strengthening). 

**Long-term survival and definition:** Celtic communities survived in Atlantic zone with varying isolation through early Middle Ages (Viking/Norman pressures); vibrant oral/law traditions. 18th-c revival as resistance symbol. "the very strong emotional appeal which the idea of sharing a common Celtic heritage has. Perhaps the only real definition of a Celt, now as in the past, is that a Celt is a person who believes him- or herself to be Celtic." Retrospect: Celts as ethnic groups speaking related Celtic dialects across W/C Europe in 1st mill BCE; 'Celticness' largely disappeared on Continent by mid-1st mill AD; survived only in extreme western fringes. Language/La Tène art not coterminous. (Cunliffe, The Ancient Celts (2018), range 05 verbatim from the celts in retreat, celtic survival, retrospect).

## Additional Evidence from Cunliffe 2018 (Ch. 3–5 range)

"When did Europe become Celtic?" roots in re-formation: Late Bronze Age Urnfield uniformity (cremation cemeteries, bronze styles) across central/western Europe from ~1300–800 BCE gave way to Hallstatt C innovations (c.800–600 BCE) with Atlantic-origin Gundlingen swords, Pontic-influenced horse gear, iron, and four-wheeled vehicle burials in a core zone Middle Rhine to Bohemia. "The long slashing sword suitable as a cavalry weapon, horses, ceremonial vehicles, the rite of inhumation, and the widespread use of iron for weapons mark a distinct break with traditional Urnfield culture." Elite absorbed influences "from the east via the mid-Danube, from the south through the east Alpine passes, and from the Atlantic zone to the west." (101–103; range ~313–365).

Hallstatt D (600–450 BCE) "princely" florescence: Massalia ~600 BCE and Rhône corridor supplied wine-drinking gear (kraters, flagons, Attic cups) to sites like Heuneburg (Greek-style mud-brick walls) and Mont Lassois/Vix (huge Greek krater burial). "prestige goods economy" in which elites monopolized exotics for feasting and redistribution; lower-value ornaments produced locally for gifting. Salt mines (Hallstatt, Dürrnberg) integral to east Alpine flows. (107–119; ~503–830).

Mid-fifth century re-formation: collapse of West Hallstatt core (rich burials and hillforts end, Heuneburg fire); rise of northern periphery warrior elites (Loire–Marne–Moselle, Bohemia) with two-wheeled chariots, fighting weapons, and "the highly distinctive Celtic art style." "Early La Tène culture differed in many significant ways from that of the old Hallstatt chiefdoms... the La Tène aristocracy favoured the two-wheeled vehicle..." "the scene was set for a new act in the story of Europe—the Celtic migrations." Atlantic tin networks persisted, now feeding Mediterranean via native middlemen. (124–129; ~910–1110). "cumulative Celticity" model: progressive integration of linguistic, material, and social elements over the Bronze–Iron transition rather than single migration event.
