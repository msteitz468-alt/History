# The Celts (Iron Age Europe)

## Overview

The **Celts** were the dominant peoples of Iron Age temperate Europe north and west of the
Alps — "**one of the major barbarian neighbours of the classical world**," as the Greeks
(Hecataeus, Herodotus) already called them in the sixth and fifth centuries.
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 9c, D. W. Harding) surveys them archaeologically as
part of the volume's coverage of the **North** — one of the few substantial treatments of
Celtic Europe in the wiki, a region the collection covers thinly.

## "When did Europe become Celtic?"

Harding frames the central question — "when did Celtic Europe become Celtic, and in what
sense?" — and warns against equating "Celtic" narrowly with **Celtic-speakers** or with a
single archaeological culture. The linguistic evidence (personal, tribal and place-names,
inscriptions across Europe) shows the peoples were indeed **linguistically Celtic**, and
that an Indo-European language had spread by direct settlement before the mid-first
millennium. But this need not align with any one "culture"; better to think of a
**progressive "cumulative Celticity"** reaching back through the Bronze Age **Urnfield**
horizon, with strong continuity into the Iron Age.

## Hallstatt and La Tène

The European Iron Age is conventionally divided into two great phases, both named from
type-sites:
- **Hallstatt (C–D, c. 800–450 BCE):** the "first Iron Age of Europe," marked by the spread
  of iron, a shift to **inhumation and tumulus burial**, and at the apex a class of
  **"princely" tombs** — four-wheeled **wagon/chariot burials** in timber chambers, richly
  furnished with Mediterranean imports, at sites like **Hochdorf**, **Vix** (the great
  Greek bronze krater), and the Magdalenenberg. These chiefly centres traded with the
  Greek (Massalia) and Etruscan worlds for wine and luxury goods.
- **La Tène (from c. 450 BCE):** the culture most often identified with the historical
  Celts, with its distinctive curvilinear **art style** — the phase of the **Celtic
  expansion**.

## The fourth-century migrations

For the period of this volume, the salient fact is the **Celtic migrations**: the great
movements of La Tène peoples out of the central European homeland into **northern Italy**
(the Po valley, the "Gauls" who sacked **Rome** c. 390/387) and down the **Danube** toward
the Balkans (culminating, beyond this volume, in the invasion of Greece and the sack-attempt
on **Delphi** in 279, and the settlement of Galatia in Asia Minor). The Celts thus became,
in the fourth century, a force impinging directly on the classical Mediterranean from the
north — the temperate-European counterweight to the events in Greece and the Aegean.

## Significance

The Celts represent the **deep hinterland of Mediterranean civilization** — a network of
warrior aristocracies, hillfort centres and long-distance trade that supplied the classical
world with metals and manpower and periodically erupted into it. Their archaeology is the
principal evidence for **Iron Age Europe beyond the literate frontier**, and the
"cumulative Celticity" model is a caution against tying ethnicity too tightly to either
language or a single material culture.
