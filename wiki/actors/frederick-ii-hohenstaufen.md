# Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor (r. 1212/1220–1250)

**Frederick II** of Hohenstaufen — "*stupor mundi*," the wonder of the world — was the
dominant secular figure of the thirteenth-century West: king of Germany, Holy Roman
Emperor, and king of **[[kingdom-of-sicily|Sicily]]**, whose lifelong struggle with the
papacy defined the era's central political drama. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Michael
Toch, Ch. 9a, on Germany; David Abulafia, editor, on Sicily) presents his reign through a
revisionist lens, sceptical of the older image of a precocious state-builder strangled by
the papacy.

## The Rise to Empire

Son of Emperor **Henry VI** and grandson of **[[frederick-i-barbarossa|Frederick
Barbarossa]]**, Frederick was a three-year-old king of Germany when his father died (1197),
left a "pawn in the Italian struggles of [[innocent-iii|Pope Innocent III]]" while Germany
fell into the long **Welf–Hohenstaufen throne struggle** between **Philip of Swabia** and
**Otto IV**. Backed by Innocent III and Philip Augustus of France, Frederick was elected
(1211/1212), crossed the Alps "with a small following," and saw his rival Otto IV destroyed
at **[[battle-of-bouvines-1214|Bouvines]]** (1214). Crowned at Aachen (1215), where he
reburied Charlemagne and took the cross, his kingship was ratified at the **Fourth Lateran
Council**.

## Germany: Concession and Absentee Rule

Toch stresses that Frederick's German policy was **not** a coherent programme of state-building
but the management, "above the level of local and regional rivalries," of the princely
consensus that secured his imperial title. The great charters — the **Confoederatio cum
principibus ecclesiasticis** (1220) and the **Statutum in favorem principum** (1231/1232) —
are now read as **codifications of already-existing princely power** rather than a surrender:
they confirmed the princes' rights to build castles, hold markets, keep mints and tolls. The
recovery of crown lands and the founding of towns in Germany were carried out by his sons and
their **ministeriales**, not by the absentee emperor, who after 1220 ruled Germany from afar
and returned only twice. His son **Henry (VII)**, made king, rebelled (allying with the
towns and Milan) and was deposed and imprisoned in 1235; his son **Conrad IV** became heir.

## The Papal Struggle and Collapse

Frederick's **personal union of the Empire and Sicily** — encircling the Papal State — made
conflict with Rome structural. Excommunicated repeatedly (over the delayed crusade; he went
anyway and recovered Jerusalem by treaty in the **Sixth Crusade**, 1228–1229), he was finally
**deposed by Innocent IV at the First Council of Lyons (1245)** — "the most drastic of all
papal political acts" (see [[papacy]]). The papal legate Albert Behaim raised anti-kings
(**Henry Raspe**, **William of Holland**), winning much of the German clergy, while the
south-east and the imperial cities remained Hohenstaufen. Frederick died in Apulia in **1250**.

## Significance

Toch argues that Frederick's death "does not appear to have been the decisive event" in the
dismantling of Hohenstaufen rule; "more important was the disintegration of the Hohenstaufen
party of princes and towns." His line was extinguished within two decades — his son **Conrad
IV** (d. 1254), his bastard **Manfred** (d. 1266) and grandson **Conradin** (d. 1268) dying
in Italy — opening the **[[german-interregnum-1250|Interregnum]]**. "It seems fitting that
the first Hohenstaufen emperor able to overcome the regional confines of his dynastic
interests was to be the last of his line, and also the one least concerned with German
affairs." His Sicilian kingdom and his Italian wars are treated further under
[[kingdom-of-sicily]] and in the volume's Italian chapters.
