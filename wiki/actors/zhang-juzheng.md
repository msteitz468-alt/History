# Zhang Juzheng (Chang Chü-cheng, 1525–1582)

Dominant Grand Secretary (1572–1582) and de facto ruler during the early Wanli reign. His drive for governmental efficiency, fiscal rigor, and personal authority, followed by posthumous disgrace, are central to Huang's account of why the Ming bureaucracy proved incapable of sustained reform. "Chang Chü-cheng was capable of remembering thousands of things in minute detail." Yet his "entire enterprise collapsed when he was dead and buried."

## Career and Methods of Control

By the early Wanli years Chang had become master of the Literary Depth Pavilion. He "wanted a strong army, and in order to have one he had to fill up granaries and treasuries with food and silver. Financial administration was his area of specialization." He reviewed fiscal accounts, checked troop strength, "personally directed the arrest and retirement of high officials who had violated the law. He even designed report forms and set up deadlines."

Tax collection had "numerous peculiarities." Quotas "extremely uneven" and "had not been revised for two hundred years." Rich counties produced "several hundred times" the proceeds of poor ones. Land surveys outdated; populations used non-standard units; many small cultivators relied on low quotas for survival. Chang "insisted that all districts make their tax payments in full," placing "undue pressure on the Civil Service at its operational level." Critics charged that "in order to fill the coffers the magistrates had had to arrest a large number of peasants and beat many of them to death." The loss of good will was real.

Because the empire's "transportation and communication could never be sufficiently improved to be systematized," Chang "had to coach his trusted governors and commissioners to submit to the throne the kind of memorial he wanted, and then, as the emperor's counsellor, he would approve the very recommendations he had worked out himself." He wrote "busily... personal letters" offering career incentives and used "grand-secretarial prerogative of appointing personnel in the name of the emperor" to create "an institution of his own to compensate for the lack of system."

The bureaucracy's dual character was ignored: the 20,000 civil officials "were jealous protectors of their own sinecures, patrons of their associates, and supporters of local landholding and usurious interests. Yet their professed adherence to the Four Books could never be totally discredited." When inspired they repaired dikes, suppressed bandits, repulsed invasions. Chang replaced "this spirit of selflessness with his improvised efficiency control, and when that was not enough, by practicing favoritism among his cohorts."

## Ostentatious Style and Alienation of the Literati

Chang's "ostentatious style of living... to his own way of thinking was no more than his due." The emperor contributed 1,000 ounces of silver toward remodeling his house for display of imperial calligraphy; actual cost ran "to ten times that amount." Officials in Hukwang duplicated the mansion using public funds and erected three stone arches. His household servants and relatives abused the Imperial Postal System for transport. Followers offered "valuable gifts, including land and cash."

He offended "so many men of letters." To Wang Shih-chen (doctoral classmate of 1547) he wrote that a fine shield and saber "would be unwise to take them out and use them; they would inevitably break." Wang later published "a most derogatory biography." To vice minister Wang Tao-k'un he wrote of dismissal: "Orchids, yes even orchids, would have to be weeded out, we regret to say, if they should ever bloom in the middle of public avenues." Chang saw individuals; "What he failed to see was that the bureaucracy as a body... was a source of power in itself and, in a sense, was master."

## Death, Purge, and Immediate Backlash

Chang died in 1582. In the years following, "anyone who had had the slightest connection with Chang Chu-cheng was under attack." His efficiency controls were dismantled by his successor. By 1585 the case against his followers was allowed to rest, but "Chang Chu-cheng had become an emotionally packed issue. Bare mention of his name could stir up antagonism."

## Place in Huang's Argument

Chang "fully exposed the weaknesses of the over-centralization of an enormous empire, whose technical problems exceeded its capacity to govern. There was a limit beyond which its administrative efficiency could not go, and to strive too hard in that direction could lead to peril." When the top administrator refused the lower efficiency scale as standard, "the bureaucrats became divided and technical problems turned into moral issues."

In the "world without Chang Chü-cheng" the empire "began to slide slowly but irrevocably into an abyss." The constitutional deadlock that followed—throne withdrawing, bureaucracy unable to lead—made long-term decline nearly inevitable. Shen Shih-hsing "had no intention of dwelling on the shortcomings of others, still less those of his own benefactor," but "knew the Chang Chü-cheng story well and had learned his lesson from it."

Zhang's northern frontier arrangements and patronage of capable generals contributed to later vulnerabilities once dismantled amid the backlash.

## Historiography

Huang credits Chang with recognizing real fiscal and military needs but argues his methods—surveillance, personal networks, pressure on an unreformed quota system—were incompatible with the Ming constitution's commitment to distributed power, ritual consensus, and the "human touch." Traditional historiography (including many contemporaries) viewed him as near-usurper whose lack of humility invited nemesis. Later scholarship has sometimes rehabilitated his reforms as necessary, but Huang's microhistory emphasizes how the purge of his memory foreclosed adaptive leadership for a generation. The account rests on Veritable Records, private correspondence, and institutional analysis of tax and personnel systems.
