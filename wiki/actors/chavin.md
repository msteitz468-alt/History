# Chavín

## Overview

**Chavín** names both the type-site of **Chavín de Huántar** (a temple complex in the
north-central highlands of Peru) and the first pan-Andean **"horizon style"** — a rapidly
expanding, homogeneous set of religious iconography that linked coast and highland
between roughly 900 and 200 BCE. In the periodization used in
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Salomon & Schwartz's]] Volume III
(Izumi Shimada's chapter on Andean diversity), Chavín marks the **Early Horizon**, the
first of the alternating eras of interregional integration that structure
[[andean-state-formation|Andean prehistory]].

Chavín is best understood not as a state or empire but as a **cult and exchange network**.
Its art — feline, raptor, and serpent imagery rendered in the carved stone of the Lanzón
and Tello Obelisk, and on textiles and ceramics — diffused widely without evident military
conquest, suggesting religious authority and pilgrimage rather than political subjugation.
It provided a shared symbolic vocabulary on which later regional cultures ([[nasca|Nasca]],
[[moche|Moche]], Paracas) built distinct local traditions.

## Significance

Chavín is the earliest clear instance of the **horizon/regional-diversity oscillation** that
recurs through Andean history — succeeded by an era of regional florescence and later by the
[[wari|Wari]]–[[tiwanaku|Tiwanaku]] Middle Horizon and finally the [[inca-empire|Inka]]
Late Horizon. Its mechanism of integration (shared religion and iconography rather than
conquest) makes it a key counter-case in debates over how Andean complexity arose.

## Historiography

Chavín de Huántar was central to **Julio C. Tello's** early-twentieth-century thesis that
Andean civilization had highland, autochthonous roots. Later work (including the maritime-
foundations argument associated with Michael Moseley) complicated the picture by stressing
coastal preceramic monumentality predating Chavín. Shimada's Cambridge chapter treats Chavín
as one regional integrative episode among several rather than the singular fountainhead Tello
imagined.

## Fagan & Durrani (2020): a late manifestation, not a mother culture

Fagan and Durrani's *World Prehistory* (10th ed., 2020) reinforces the historiographical
correction already recorded above with a direct restatement: **Julio Tello** identified the
"Chavín" art style and horizon in **1943**, and his long-held "mother culture" model (analogous
to the old Olmec model in Mesoamerica) is **now considered incorrect** — Chavín represents a
**late** manifestation of trends beginning as early as c. 2000 BCE, not their origin point. The
site of Chavín de Huántar itself dates to at least 1500 BCE. Its famous **Lanzón** carving (a
jaguar-serpent figure) anchors the temple's iconography, and the hollow pyramid contains an
**acoustic water-tunnel system** — replicated by Luis Lumbreras to demonstrate the temple's
roaring-water acoustics, tied to Richard Burger's rain/cosmology interpretation of the site.
