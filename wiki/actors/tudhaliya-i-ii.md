## Overview

Tudhaliya I/II (the conventional double designation reflects unresolved scholarly uncertainty over whether early New Kingdom Tudhaliya campaigns belong to one king or two) was the founder of what Bryce calls the Hittite "New Kingdom" — the second and imperial phase of Hittite history. He succeeded Muwattalli following a palace conspiracy in which his own father Kantuzzili was one of the assassins, meaning Tudhaliya's accession "marked the restoration of kingship to the family in whose hands it lay before it was seized by Muwattalli" (Bryce, Ch. 6). His reign saw the Hittites re-emerge as a major power after a generation of diminished influence.

## Role and Career

Tudhaliya secured his throne by defeating the counter-coup of Muwa, Muwattalli's chief of bodyguards, who had Hurrian backing: "Kantuzzili and I, the king, defeated the army of Muwa and the Hurrians. The enemy army died en masse" (KUB xxiii 16, iii 4–9). Once established, he pursued a vigorous programme of military expansion on two fronts — west and east.

**Western Campaigns.** Tudhaliya launched a series of pre-emptive campaigns into western Anatolia, targeting the [[arzawa]] lands (Arzawa Minor, Seha River Land, Hapalla) and a twenty-two-country coalition called the Assuwan Confederacy, which extended across the Aegean coast and possibly included Wilusiya and Taruisa (associated by some scholars with Wilios/Troy and Troia). After a night assault, he defeated the Confederacy and deported 10,000 infantry and 600 chariot teams to [[hattusha|Hattusa]]: "As Tudhaliya the Great King shattered the Assuwan country, he dedicated these swords to the Storm God, his lord" (Annals inscription on bronze longsword). He established the practice of mass deportation of conquered peoples — one regularly adopted by later Hittite kings. His western policy was deliberately limited: rather than establish direct control over the fractious western states, he used frontier vassals such as Madduwatta to protect the homeland's south-western borders at minimum cost.

**Kaskan Frontier.** While campaigning in Assuwa, the Kaska seized the opportunity to devastate the homeland: "while I, Tudhaliya, the Great King, was fighting in the Land of Assuwa, behind my back Kaskan troops took up arms, and they came into the Land of Hatti, and devastated the country" (Annals, rev. 9'–12'). Tudhaliya drove them out and pursued them into Kaska territory.

**Eastern Affairs and Syria.** To open the Syrian route, Tudhaliya concluded the Sunashshura Treaty with [[kizzuwatna]], recovering the kingdom that had drifted into the Mitannian orbit: "the people of the Land of Kizzuwadna are Hittite cattle and chose their stable. From the Hurrian they separated and shifted allegiance to My Sun" (KBo i 5 i 30–7). He then advanced into Syria, targeting Aleppo; a later Hittite treaty records that "when Tudhaliya, the Great King, occupied the royal throne, the king of the Land of Aleppo made peace with him" (KBo i 6, obv. 11–16). He also claimed victory over [[mitanni]] and Aleppo together, though the extent of this success has been questioned by scholars — Mitanni continued to exercise influence in northern Syria for years thereafter.

**Co-regency.** Tudhaliya made his son Arnuwanda (Arnuwanda I) co-regent and Great King. Arnuwanda had married Asmunikal, Tudhaliya's daughter — a dynastic adoption arrangement to resolve what would otherwise have been a prohibited sibling marriage.

**Madduwatta Affair.** Tudhaliya installed the western exile Madduwatta as vassal ruler in Zippasla to anchor the frontier against Arzawa, repeatedly showing "remarkable forbearance" despite Madduwatta's serial violations of his oath. The episode, documented in the Indictment of Madduwatta (written by Arnuwanda), reveals Hittite pragmatic Realpolitik: tacit approval of vassal expansionism when it served Hittite interests, combined with formal protests for the record.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Tudhaliya's western campaigns, the Assuwan Confederacy might have developed into a sustained threat to the Hittite homeland's south-western borders, tying down resources needed for the struggle with [[mitanni]] over Syria. Without the Sunashshura Treaty, the northern Syrian route would have remained blocked. Tudhaliya's restoration of Hittite credibility as a Near Eastern power created the platform on which his grandson Suppiluliuma I built the empire.

## Historiography

Tudhaliya I/II is one of the most historiographically contested rulers in Hittite studies. The central problem — whether events assigned to "Tudhaliya" in the early New Kingdom belong to one or two kings — has never been resolved. Bryce adopts the minimalist position (one king) "for the purposes of this book," while acknowledging that "the assumption may well be invalidated by new evidence." Key sources are fragmentary Annals, the Indictment of Madduwatta (reassigned from a late New Kingdom context to the early New Kingdom by modern scholarship), and references in later Hittite treaty preambles. A seal impression published by Professor Otten naming a Great King Tudhaliya as "son of Kantuzili" was important in clarifying his parentage. Dating of his reign remains approximate (middle chronology: c.1400–c.1360 BCE).
