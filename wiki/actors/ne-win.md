## Overview

General Ne Win (1911–2002) was the Burmese military officer who led the coup of 1962 that ended parliamentary democracy in Burma, and who ruled the country — through various constitutional forms — until 1988. One of the original Thirty Comrades trained by Japan who returned with Aung San in 1942, Ne Win drew on the legitimacy of the independence struggle throughout his long tenure. His political project — enshrined in the "Burmese Way to Socialism" (BWS) — aimed to create a non-partisan, non-sectarian, non-ethnic political order, but instead produced economic stagnation and ethnic insurgency.

## Role and Major Decisions

**The caretaker government (1958–60)**: Before his definitive coup, Ne Win served as prime minister of a "caretaker government" from October 1958 to February 1960, invited by U Nu to restore order after the AFPFL split threatened political stability. During this period, Ne Win stabilized the cost of living, increased exports and foreign-exchange reserves, and attempted to reassert central control over local power-holders (the *bo*), replacing them with appointed district officers. This brief interval demonstrated the military's capacity for orderly administration and established Ne Win's model for governance.

**The 1962 coup and Revolutionary Council**: Ne Win seized power on 2 March 1962 in a coup whose stated justification was the danger of disintegration posed by U Nu's proposed statehood concessions to the Arakanese and Mons, and by intensifying ethnic insurgency. The Revolutionary Council, with Ne Win as chairman, combined all state powers and ruled by decree until 1974. As the *Cambridge History of Southeast Asia* (Tarling, ed., Vol. 2) notes, Ne Win was effectively the council: "Although there was a small military oligarchy in the council, it was Ne Win who called the shots."

**Burmese Way to Socialism**: Shortly after the coup, the Revolutionary Council issued the BWS ideological statement, which specified that the state rested on the people rather than a capitalist or landlord class, rejected parliamentary democracy, and aimed at establishing a single party leading the state to socialism. Critically, the BWS was presented as non-partisan and non-sectarian — an attempt to transcend the ideological and ethnic divisions that had paralyzed U Nu's government. The *Cambridge History* interprets the BWS as shaped by Ne Win's civil-war experience: having seen the army torn apart by having to choose sides between U Nu's government and the communist rebels in 1948, Ne Win sought a synthesis that would not repeat this fragmentation.

**Burma Socialist Programme Party**: In 1962, Ne Win created the BSPP (also known as the *Lanzin*) as the single governing party. Initially conceived as a cadre party drawing from police and military, it was reconstituted as a mass party in 1971 — a move that brought in members "less committed to the party's ideology and more interested in securing favours or positions of power." This transformation created a contest between combat officers and party officers that dominated Burmese politics through the 1970s.

**Handling religion and ethnicity**: Ne Win maintained a rigorously non-sectarian position, in marked contrast to U Nu. He lifted the ban on cattle slaughter and *nat* (spirit) worship and later resisted Buddhist clerical influence on politics. On ethnicity, he moved swiftly after 1962 to dismantle the autonomous structures U Nu had permitted — arresting hereditary leaders, abolishing state councils, replacing them with centrally appointed bodies. Military campaigns against ethnic insurgents were persistent but never fully successful.

**Economic isolation**: Burma under Ne Win pursued extreme self-reliance and isolation from international capital, nationalizing industries and expelling Indian and Chinese merchant communities. The result was progressive economic deterioration that created the conditions for the 1988 student uprising.

**Fall (1988)**: Massive student demonstrations in Rangoon in 1988 forced Ne Win to resign from the BSPP chairmanship. He announced his willingness to hold a referendum and suggested multi-party elections. However, in September 1988, the military reimposed order through the State Law and Order Restoration Council (SLORC). The *Cambridge History* notes that the military of 1988 "was no longer the same as that of 1958 or even 1962" — the Thirty Comrades generation had been replaced by combat officers who had earned their experience fighting ethnic insurgents, not Japanese or British soldiers.

## Counterfactual Significance

Ne Win's 26-year rule constitutes the central missed opportunity in modern Burmese development. His combination of military authority, ideological self-reliance, and suppression of ethnic political structures created a self-reinforcing cycle of stagnation and insurgency. The *Cambridge History* is pointedly brief on his accomplishments and detailed on his failures. The comparison with Thailand — geographically and culturally adjacent, also characterized by military dominance of politics, but open to international economic integration — suggests that the isolationism of the BWS was a policy choice with massive developmental costs.
