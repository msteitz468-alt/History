## Overview

The Federal Republic of Germany (FRG), commonly known as West Germany, was founded on 23 May 1949 when the Western occupation zones — American, British, and French — coalesced into a single state under the Basic Law (Grundgesetz). The Basic Law was deliberately designed as a provisional constitution: its architects hoped for eventual reunification with the Soviet-controlled east and explicitly left open the question of national unity. The FRG ceased to exist on 3 October 1990 when the five newly reconstituted eastern states acceded to it under Article 23 of the Basic Law, absorbing what had been the German Democratic Republic.

## Governing Structure

The FRG was a parliamentary federal republic. Executive authority resided in the chancellor (Bundeskanzler), who under the Basic Law held Richtlinienkompetenz — the authority to set the overall direction of government policy. This feature, combined with a constructive vote of no-confidence that required parliament to agree on a replacement before removing a chancellor, produced what political scientists called "chancellor democracy" (Kanzlerdemokratie). In practice, strong chancellors (Adenauer, Brandt in foreign policy, Kohl in 1990) could dominate the political process; policy-making was otherwise consensual and incremental, constrained by federalism, coalition governments, and powerful parapublic institutions. The Bundestag (lower house) and Bundesrat (upper house representing the Länder) together constituted the legislature. The Constitutional Court (Bundesverfassungsgericht) served as an active guardian of the Basic Law.

## Economic Base

The FRG's postwar economy underwent the [[wirtschaftswunder]] — an economic miracle in which national income quadrupled between 1950 and 1980. The foundational model was the **social market economy** (soziale Marktwirtschaft), associated with Economics Minister Ludwig Erhard: free markets with robust social protections, an independent central bank (Bundesbank), and strong labor unions institutionalized through codetermination (Mitbestimmung). The DM currency established by the 1948 currency reform became both an economic instrument and a symbol of national pride in a country stripped of other national symbols. West Germany joined the ECSC (1951), the EEC (1957 Treaties of Rome), and was a founding member of what became the European Community and, ultimately, the European Union. By 1980, 56 percent of intra-EEC exports stayed within the Six, reflecting deep economic integration. See [[german-european-integration]].

## Military Capacity and Alliance

The FRG joined NATO on 9 May 1955, the same date it recovered sovereign status under the Deutschlandvertrag (General Treaty). Rearmament was controversial domestically and internationally, but Adenauer secured it as part of a package deal restoring sovereignty. The Bundeswehr was established as a conscript military embedded in NATO command structures, explicitly designed without independent offensive capability. The FRG formally renounced nuclear, biological, and chemical weapons. Under the 2+4 Treaty of 1990, a reunified Germany agreed to reduce combined armed forces to 370,000.

## Foreign Policy Trajectory

**Phase 1 (1949–1969): Westbindung.** Under Adenauer, the FRG's paramount foreign policy goal was Western integration — full membership in NATO and the European Communities, Franco-German reconciliation, and close alliance with the United States. This came at the cost of deferred reunification: the SPD under Schumacher argued that Westintegration hardened the division of Germany, but Adenauer maintained it was the only guarantee against Soviet aggression and Germany's own violent past. The FRG refused to recognize the GDR (Hallstein Doctrine) and maintained automatic citizenship rights for East Germans.

**Phase 2 (1969–1982): Ostpolitik.** The SPD-FDP coalition under Willy Brandt reversed course, pursuing normalization with the East. Key milestones: Moscow Treaty (August 1970), Warsaw Treaty (December 1970, including Brandt's Warsaw Ghetto kneeling), Berlin Transit Agreement (1971), Basic Treaty with the GDR (December 1972), joint UN membership (September 1973), Helsinki Accords (1975). The FRG accepted the postwar territorial status quo (including the Oder-Neisse line with Poland) as the price for reduced tensions and increased human contacts across the Iron Curtain. See [[ostpolitik]].

**Phase 3 (1982–1990): Continuation and Reunification.** Helmut Kohl's CDU/CSU-FDP coalition continued détente while maintaining Westbindung. The collapse of East Germany in 1989–1990 and Kohl's 10-point plan (28 November 1989) set the path to unification via Article 23. See [[german-reunification-1990]].

## Mechanisms of Decline / Transformation

The FRG did not decline — it expanded. East Germany acceded to it. The terms of unification extended the FRG's institutions, legal code, currency, and administrative model eastward, making the enlarged state essentially the FRG writ large. Critics noted that this "ready-made state" transfer left East Germans feeling passive recipients rather than architects of their own future, contributing to persistent Ossi/Wessi tensions.

## Historiography

West German historiography is extensive. The key historiographical controversy is the [[german-sonderweg]] debate — whether the FRG represented genuine democratic transformation or continuity with older German authoritarianism. The "chancellor democracy" concept (Katzenstein's "semisovereign state") became a major frame for understanding FRG political science. The question of how the FRG processed its Nazi past — [[vergangenheitsbewaltigung]] — generated intense historiographical debate from the Historians' Dispute (Historikerstreit, 1986) onward.
