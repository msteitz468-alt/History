# The Uí Néill

The dominant dynastic federation of early medieval [[places/ireland|Ireland]], whose
northern (Cenél nEógain, Cenél Conaill) and southern (Clann Cholmáin) branches
monopolised the prestigious **kingship of [[places/tara|Tara]]** for roughly two centuries
and projected it as a claim to the kingship of all Ireland. Their dominance — and its
construction in the sources — is a central theme of
[[sources/stafford-companion-early-middle-ages-britain-ireland-2009|Stafford's *Companion*]].

## Overview

The Uí Néill traced descent from the mythical **Niall of the Nine Hostages**; the rise of
Cenél nEógain in the eighth century was "underpinned by the creation of a fictional early
history that united them and provinces they had recently conquered as northern Uí Néill."
Having seized **Tara** from the Laigin (Leinster), they used it as the ideological vehicle
for supremacy. From 734 to 944 the kingship of Tara **alternated** between the northern
Cenél nEógain and the midland Clann Cholmáin — an arrangement held together, as Máire
Herbert showed, by systematic **marriage exchange** of royal daughters (see
[[concepts/queenship-early-medieval|queenship]]).

## Role and Limits

Holding the title "king of Tara" (increasingly *rí Érenn*, "king of Ireland") did **not**
imply continuous power: Flann Sinna (d. 916) "can only have been regarded as king of
Ireland between 905 and 908." Flann's patronage of **Clonmacnoise** (the Cross of the
Scriptures, "A prayer for the king of Ireland") epitomised the symbiosis of king and
abbot. The dynasty's ecclesiastical anchor was **[[places/armagh|Armagh]]**, whose abbot
wielded quasi-royal power and could impose Patrick's tribute across Ireland.

## Decline

The real tenth-century contest was triangular — northern Uí Néill, midland Uí Néill, and
the Munster kings — with the Connacht dynasties as rising power-brokers. The monopoly was
broken by **[[actors/brian-boru|Brian Bóruma]]** of the Dál Cais, who demonstrated that
Uí Néill descent was not necessary for the high-kingship. The death of the last great
Uí Néill king, **Máel Sechnaill mac Domnaill** (1022), was treated by annalists as the
end of the Uí Néill era and the start of the "interregnum" of kings "with opposition"
(*rí hÉrend co fressabra*).

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the Uí Néill construction of Tara as a supra-provincial kingship, the very idea of
a "king of Ireland" — which Brian and his successors fought over and which framed the
twelfth-century Norman intervention — might never have crystallised. Their ideological
work outlived their political dominance.
