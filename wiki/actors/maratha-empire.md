# The Maratha Empire

The **Marathas** were the Marathi-speaking warrior people of the western Deccan whose rise under
**Sivaji** broke open the [[mughal-empire|Mughal Empire]] and who became, for a half-century, the
greatest indigenous power in India. Documented for the wiki through Percival Spear's
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965).

## Sivaji and Maratha nationalism

A people of intelligent, exclusive Brahmins and hardy Sudra cultivators in a poor country of the
Western Ghats, the Marathas "burst forth with such brilliance in the seventeenth century" under
**Sivaji** (Shivaji) — a rise Spear calls "one of the many historical mysteries of Indian history,"
resisting the easy explanation that Muslim intolerance simply provoked a Hindu national reaction.
Sivaji outwitted the Mughals with **guerrilla tactics**, famously killing the Bijapur general **Afzal
Khan with concealed steel claws (1659)**, sacked the great Mughal port of **Surat (1664)** (only the
English merchants holding out in their warehouse), escaped from Aurangzeb's court, and by his death
in 1680 was "master of a compact and well-organized kingdom." His genius, Spear stresses, was to
**weld caste-conscious Brahmin and independent Sudra into a harmonious whole** under the warcry of
cow, country, religion, and homeland — "both Maratha nationalism and Maratha Hinduism, an embryonic
regional national state."

## From nationalism to imperialism

The long **Mughal–Maratha war** that Aurangzeb waged in the Deccan from 1681 to 1707 is usually said
to have broken the empire; Spear qualifies this but draws a darker lesson: in the struggle Sivaji's
disciplined kingdom **broke up**, and the moderate patriots became "toughened raiders notorious for
their rapacity and ruthlessness," dreaded across India by Hindus and Muslims alike. What emerged was a
**Maratha imperialism** — a confederacy (under the Peshwas) that treated all India as its sphere — but
one **defeated before it began**: the Marathas had made themselves too hated to be accepted as
national leaders. "The iron had entered into their souls in the Mughal struggle." Their bid for
all-India empire collapsed at the catastrophic **Third Battle of Panipat (1761)** against the Afghan
Ahmad Shah Abdali, after which they remained a powerful but fragmented confederacy until the British
broke them (1818).

## Significance

The Marathas were the chief **indigenous successor power** to the Mughals and the great
might-have-been of pre-British India — a Hindu, Deccan-based force that filled the vacuum Aurangzeb
left but could neither unite the country nor withstand, ultimately, the
[[english-east-india-company|Company]]. Spear treats their rise-and-souring as a case study in how
**national feeling can be deformed by the very struggle that forges it**.
