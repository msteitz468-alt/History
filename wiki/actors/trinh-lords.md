## Overview

The Trinh were a warlord lineage that ruled northern Vietnam (Dong Kinh / Ke Cho, the Red River plain, Thanh Hoa, and Nghe An) as hereditary military "lords" (*chua*) in the name of a powerless, restored [[le-dynasty-vietnam|Le dynasty]] king, from the mid sixteenth century until 1787. Trinh Kiem and his descendants built their power on soldiers recruited from Thanh Hoa and Nghe An — the same base that had restored the Le against the [[mac-dynasty-vietnam|Mac]] — and used that military establishment first to complete the destruction of Mac authority in the Red River plain and then to wage generations of war against the southern [[nguyen-lords|Nguyen Lords]] in an unsuccessful effort to reunify the country under Le/Trinh rule.

Trinh Trang (r. from 1623) launched the opening campaigns of the [[trinh-nguyen-war-1627-1672|Trinh-Nguyen War]] against Nguyen Phuc Nguyen in 1627 and repeatedly thereafter; his son and eventual successor Trinh Tac led the war's final phases before formally abandoning the war policy around 1673–1674. Trinh Tac's death in 1682 marked the first peaceful succession in Trinh history. Subsequent lords — Trinh Can, Trinh Cuong, Trinh Giang, Trinh Doanh, Trinh Sam — presided over cycles of reform rhetoric (the Vinh Tho and Canh Tri eras, 1658–1671, modeled on the fifteenth-century Hong Duc government of Le Tu Thanh) that repeatedly foundered on the same structural problem: a court dependent on Thanh Hoa/Nghe An soldiers to dominate a resentful, impoverished Red River peasantry, unable either to dispense with the Le fiction or to translate reform edicts into real rural administration. Trinh Giang's reign (1729–1740) collapsed into eunuch misrule and a nervous breakdown amid a decade-long wave of popular rebellions (1737–1751) that his brother Trinh Doanh eventually suppressed. Trinh Sam (r. 1767–1782) restored a measure of order but bred renewed factional corruption (Le Quy Don) and left a disputed succession. The house was destroyed when Nguyen Hue of the [[tay-son-dynasty|Tay Son]] took Ke Cho in 1786, ending Trinh rule; the last Le king was extinguished by 1789.

## Key Events

- 1627–1672: [[trinh-nguyen-war-1627-1672|Trinh-Nguyen War (Fifty Years War)]] against the Nguyen Lords — repeated Trinh invasions of the south, stalemated at the Nguyen wall system near Dong Hoi.
- 1658–1671: Vinh Tho and Canh Tri reform eras under Trinh Tac and the scholar-official Pham Cong Tru, modeled on Le Tu Thanh's Hong Duc government.
- 1737–1751: Widespread rebellions across the Red River plain and northern border provinces (Nguyen Huu Cau, Nguyen Danh Phuong, Hoang Cong Chat, Le Duy Mat) suppressed under Trinh Doanh.
- 1774–1775: Trinh occupation of Thuan Hoa (Hue) under the general Hoang Ngu Phuc, exploiting the outbreak of the [[tay-son-rebellion-1771-1802|Tay Son rebellion]] in the south — the northerners' last territorial gain, abandoned after an epidemic in 1775.
- 1786: Nguyen Hue's Tay Son army takes Ke Cho; Trinh Khai commits suicide; Trinh rule ends.

## Key Actors

- Trinh Trang — opened the Fifty Years War against the south.
- Trinh Tac — presided over the war's end and the Canh Tri reform era; first peaceful Trinh succession (d. 1682).
- Trinh Can, Trinh Cuong, Trinh Giang, Trinh Doanh, Trinh Sam, Trinh Khai — successive lords; see individual mentions on the war and rebellion event pages.
- [[nguyen-lords|Nguyen Lords]] — the rival southern seigneury against whom the Trinh fought for fifty years and with whom they reached a lasting stalemate after 1673.
- [[nguyen-hue|Nguyen Hue]] — Tay Son commander who destroyed Trinh rule in 1786.

## Geographic Scope

The Red River plain (Dong Kinh/Ke Cho and environs), Thanh Hoa, Nghe An, and the northern border provinces (Cao Bang, Lang Son) bordering Qing China, south to the Gianh River / Dong Hoi wall system that marked the border with Nguyen territory after 1673.

## Counterfactual Significance

Had the Trinh broken through the Nguyen wall system in the 1630s–1670s, a reunified Le-Trinh Vietnam under northern control would likely have emerged two centuries earlier, forestalling the entire southern *nam tien* expansion into Champa, Cambodia, and the Mekong delta that instead proceeded under independent Nguyen rule. Historian K. W. Taylor treats the durability of this stalemate as the decisive evidence that Vietnam's north and south had become, functionally, two separate countries by the later seventeenth century — a discontinuity thesis running against narratives of essential Vietnamese national unity.

## Historiography

The main narrative source used here is K. W. Taylor's *A History of the Vietnamese* (Cambridge, 2013), drawing on Vietnamese court annals (continuations of the *Complete Book of the History of Great Viet*, the Chinh Hoa Edition of 1697) alongside Dutch, Jesuit, and other European merchant/missionary accounts of Ke Cho (Alexandre de Rhodes, Samuel Baron, Jesuit and Paris Foreign Missions correspondence). Taylor is explicit that the annalistic record was produced by and for the literati class that staffed Trinh government, and reads its recurring rhetoric of reform ("the same old" edicts reissued generation after generation) as evidence of chronic administrative failure rather than achievement — a stance some more institutionally focused historians of Vietnamese bureaucracy might weight differently. Taylor's broader interpretive framework treats the Trinh north and Nguyen south as increasingly distinct polities after the 1670s, part of his larger argument (flagged elsewhere in this wiki, see [[vietnamese-national-continuity|Vietnamese National Continuity]]) against reading Vietnamese history as a single continuous national trajectory.
