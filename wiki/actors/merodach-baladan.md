# Merodach-baladan II

**Merodach-baladan II** (Marduk-apla-iddina), chief of the Chaldaean tribe
**[[chaldeans|Bit-Yakin]]**, was the **great antagonist of Sargonid Assyria in
Babylonia** — twice king of Babylon, and the most effective leader the
[[chaldeans|Chaldaeans]] ever produced. He is the biblical **Merodach-baladan** of
Isaiah 39 / II Kings 20. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Chs.
21–23, Brinkman & Grayson) covers him.

## Career

He first appears submitting to [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]] in 729.
When [[sargon-ii|Sargon II]] seized the Assyrian throne amid confusion (721),
Merodach-baladan **took Babylon** and held it for **a decade (721–710)**, backed by
Elam — the high point of Chaldaean independence. Sargon finally expelled him in
710 and took the Babylonian crown himself. On Sargon's death (705) Merodach-baladan
**briefly retook Babylon (703)**, but [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] crushed the
revolt; Merodach-baladan fled to the marshes and ultimately **died in exile in
Elam**. His sons (notably Nabu-bel-shumati) continued the resistance into
[[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal's]] reign.

## The embassy to Hezekiah

His best-remembered act outside Mesopotamia: an **embassy to Hezekiah of
[[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]]** (II Kings 20:12–19; Isaiah 39), almost certainly to
recruit Judah into the anti-Assyrian coalition that [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]]
would smash in [[sennacherib-siege-jerusalem-701bce|701]]. In the biblical
narrative Isaiah uses the visit to prophesy the future **Babylonian exile** — a
striking link between the Chaldaean rebel and the [[neo-babylonian-empire|empire]]
his people would later found.

## Significance

- He showed that the [[chaldeans|Chaldaean tribes]], united under a capable leader
  and allied to Elam, could **hold Babylon against Assyria for years** — the model
  for [[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar's]] eventual success.
- His resistance `contributed_to:` the cycle of revolt that drove
  [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]] to the catastrophic [[neo-assyrian-empire|destruction
  of Babylon (689)]].
