## Overview

The Konbaung (or Alaungpaya) dynasty was the last ruling house of independent Burma, founded by Alaungpaya (r. 1752–60), who defeated the Mon revolt, suppressed both British and French presence in lower Burma, and reunited the kingdom. The dynasty reached its greatest territorial extent under his successors, sacking Ayutthaya in 1767 and conquering Arakan in 1784 — the latter bringing the Burman empire into geographic contact with British India and setting in motion the chain of frontier disputes that led to three Anglo-Burman Wars. The dynasty was extinguished when the British exiled the last king, Thibaw Min, to India in 1885 following the Third Anglo-Burman War.

## Major Kings and Key Decisions

**Alaungpaya (r. 1752–1760).** Founded the dynasty by defeating the Mon revolt and reconstituting the Burman kingdom. Expelled both the French (from Syriam) and the British (from Negrais). His successful assertion of Burman independence against European interference established the dynasty's assertive political culture. "The opposition of Alaungpaya ... to both [British and French], and the destruction of French power in India, virtually ended the British contacts" (Tarling, CHSEA v.2).

**Hsinbyushin (r. 1763–1776).** Sacked Ayutthaya in 1767 — the foundational trauma of Thai-Burman relations that shaped the hostility between the two kingdoms for decades. Defeated four Chinese Qing invasions (1765–69).

**Bodawpaya (r. 1782–1819).** Conquered Arakan (1784), creating the direct land contact with British Bengal that generated the refugee crisis and frontier disputes leading to the First Anglo-Burman War. Received Michael Symes's British mission; told Symes that Burma could not deal with "a subordinate government like Bengal" — the first clear articulation of the incompatible sovereignty conceptions that would lead to war.

**Bagyidaw (r. 1819–1837).** Reigned during the First Anglo-Burman War (1824–6). Accepted the Treaty of Yandabo under military duress but regarded the Resident's presence as "a proof of our supremacy and a badge of his servility and vassalage." His mental breakdown later in the reign reduced governmental coherence.

**Tharrawaddy (r. 1838–1846).** Explicitly repudiated his predecessor's treaties: "I am determined to place the relations between the two countries on precisely the same footing as they were previous to the reign of the late King who committed a blunder in going to war with you." The British chose not to enforce the treaties by force at this juncture (Afghanistan preoccupied the governor-general), a decision that deferred but did not resolve the structural conflict.

**Mindon Min (r. 1853–1878).** The dynasty's most sophisticated response to the British challenge. He accepted the consequences of the Second Anglo-Burman War without a formal treaty, pursued commercial treaties with the British, engaged in "defensive westernisation," and maintained generally good relations for two decades. He also sought to establish Burma as a recognized international actor — missions to the United States (1857), commercial treaty with France (1873), overtures to Russia (1874). This diversification strategy proved fatal: it alarmed British India and, though Mindon himself died before the crisis matured, set the pattern his successor continued to worse effect.

**Thibaw Min (r. 1878–1885).** Succeeded through court intrigue; his reign began with the massacre of rival princes. His government ratified the French commercial convention (1873), negotiated a supplementary convention with Jules Ferry, and sought arms from France. The publication of Ferry's secret letter offering arms triggered the British ultimatum and the Third Anglo-Burman War. Thibaw was exiled to Ratnagiri in India, where he died in 1916. He was the last king of Burma.

## Role of the Dynasty in Colonial Outcomes

Tarling's chapter treats the Konbaung dynasty as a study in the failure of a viable option. Independence for Burma was not impossible: Siam preserved it under comparable pressure by accepting managed subordination to Britain. The Konbaung kings, shaped by a tradition of assertive sovereignty and genuinely proud of their military successes against the Thais, the Chinese, and initially the British, could not accept the implicit subordination that British paramountcy required. Mindon Min came closest but miscalculated in the diplomatic diversification phase. Earlier writers (Crawfurd, Symes) recognized the problem: the Burmans had "an extravagant opinion of their own power," respecting British power less than the powers of the subcontinent itself.

The dynasty's failure to mobilize popular resistance in the Third War — and the prolonged popular resistance that followed despite the dynasty's absence — is itself significant. Popular loyalty to "the house of Alompra" was real, as British officers acknowledged, but it operated without and indeed despite the dynasty's own political failures.

## Counterfactual Significance

The most consequential counterfactual is whether a more politically flexible dynasty could have preserved Burman independence on the Siamese model. Tarling's analysis suggests that the structural differences (Burma's geographic proximity to India, the strength of Indian prestige requirements, the French dimension from the 1870s onward) made a Siamese-style solution increasingly difficult but not impossible before 1878. Mindon Min's last decade, had it been managed without diplomatic overreach, might have led to a protectorate relationship analogous to those the British established with Malay sultans — degrading sovereignty without abolishing it.

## Historiography

The Konbaung dynasty is well-documented in Burmese-language chronicles (the Hmannan Yazawin and related court histories) and in British Indian records. Hall's *Europe and Burma* and the Dalhousie-Phayre correspondence (edited by Hall) are foundational English-language sources for the nineteenth-century period. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina covers this period via CHSEA v.2 and older monographs; specialist Burmese-language scholarship is less well represented.
