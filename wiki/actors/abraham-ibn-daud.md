## Overview

Abraham Ibn Daud was a Spanish Jewish philosopher of the twelfth century, a generation before [[maimonides]], who paved the way for the Aristotelian–Jewish synthesis that Maimonides would bring to completion. He is also probably the same man as "Avendauth," the Jewish scholar who collaborated in Toledo with the Christian translator Dominicus Gundissalinus to render Arabic philosophical texts into Latin — making him a significant conduit for the transmission of Islamic philosophy to medieval Christendom ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## The Exalted Faith (*Ha-Emunah ha-Ramah*)

Written originally in Arabic (now lost), surviving only in later Hebrew translations, the *Exalted Faith* is Ibn Daud's major philosophical work. Its goal is closely related to Maimonides' later *Guide for the Perplexed*: to resolve tensions between Aristotelian philosophy and Jewish Scripture.

The work's distinguishing focus is **human freedom and divine providence**. Ibn Daud identifies puzzlement about free will as the occasion for the entire treatise:

> "He identifies such puzzlement over this issue as the occasion for the whole work, and returns to solve it at the end, seeking to vindicate both human freedom and divine providence." — Adamson

### Aristotelianism as Method

Following the lead of Saadia Gaon, Ibn Daud establishes philosophical doctrines by rational argument and then quotes Scripture in confirmation. He identifies angels with the Aristotelian heavenly intellects; uses the Aristotelian framework of matter, form, and the Agent Intellect throughout; and offers a critical treatment of his predecessors, especially [[ibn-gabirol]].

### Astrology, Stars, and Free Will

Ibn Daud occupies a middle position in the debate over astrology and freedom. He accepts that heavenly bodies cause events in the sublunar world through physical means (stars cause heating and cooling, which contribute to natural events and illnesses). He also invokes the Agent Intellect, the "giver of forms" from [[al-farabi]] and [[ibn-sina]], as a complementary cause: physical processes prepare matter; the Intellect gives form to suitably prepared matter. This dual causation — physical/material causes plus formal/intellectual causes — is compared to building a ship: one needs both physical labor and the guiding idea in the shipbuilder's mind (145b).

The heavenly motions serve as "instruments of divine providence" and "servants of God's decree" (91b). This might seem to entail determinism. But Ibn Daud inverts the argument: it is precisely the presence of intermediary causes that creates space for freedom and contingency. God, being absolutely simple, can give rise only to a simple effect; He cannot directly produce opposed contraries. Evils and privations cannot be caused by God — they must arise from other, intermediary causes. Following Plotinus, Ibn Daud treats evil as a privation rather than a positive thing requiring divine causation: "God does not need to create the absence of an elephant in Spain" (202b).

Human actions, unlike natural events, are voluntary (207a): "Like all good managers, the God of Ibn Daud's philosophy is willing to delegate. He oversees a providential order by appointing the heavens as His deputies, and then gives humans the capacity to act of their own accord."

### Critique of Astrology

Unlike Abraham Ibn Ezra (a near-contemporary who elaborated astrology in great detail), Ibn Daud invokes stellar causation only in broad terms. He does not go into detailed astrological specifics. His framework accommodates the stars as part of a providential order without endorsing the astrologer's project of predicting individual fates.

## Role in Transmission

If Ibn Daud is indeed the same as Avendauth — a probability accepted by most modern scholars — his role in the Arabic-to-Latin translation movement in Toledo was considerable. The translations he produced with Gundissalinus helped bring [[al-farabi]]'s, [[ibn-sina]]'s, and others' philosophy into Latin Christian culture, contributing to the twelfth-century "translation movement" that transformed medieval European thought.

## Place in the Tradition

Adamson sees Ibn Daud as preparatory for Maimonides: less powerful philosophically, but demonstrating that the Aristotelian synthesis was workable before Maimonides attempted it at full scale. His critical treatment of [[ibn-gabirol]] already established Aristotelianism (rather than Neoplatonism) as the preferred framework for Jewish philosophy. Where Ibn Gabirol had introduced a purer Neoplatonism, Ibn Daud opened the door that Maimonides would stride through.
