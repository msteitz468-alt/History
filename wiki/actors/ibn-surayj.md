# Ibn Surayj

Aḥmad b. ʿUmar b. Surayj al-Baghdādī (d. 306/918) was a Shāfiʿī jurist in Baghdad and the head of the circle that Wael Hallaq identifies as the actual originator of *uṣūl al-fiqh* (Islamic legal theory) as a systematic discipline. Despite the retrospective attribution of uṣūl al-fiqh's founding to [[shafii-muhammad-ibn-idris|al-Shāfiʿī]], Hallaq argues that "it was Ibn Surayj who must be credited with paving the way for his students, who would discourse on this synthesis and elaborate it in greater detail."

## Role in the Great Synthesis

Ibn Surayj's scholarly position combined three attributes that had been rare in earlier generations but were becoming normative: he was simultaneously a tradition(al)ist, a jurist, and a speculative theologian. This combination was characteristic of the "Great Synthesis" — the centrist position between extreme traditionalism and extreme rationalism that emerged in the period ca. 250–350 H (870–960 CE). See [[rise-of-legal-theory|Rise of Legal Theory]].

His intellectual achievement was to conceptualize legal theory as "a synthesis between rationality and the textual tradition, that is, between reason and revelation." He did not produce finished works of uṣūl al-fiqh himself but shaped the intellectual environment in which his students did.

## Students and Legacy

His students produced the earliest extant systematic works on uṣūl al-fiqh:
- **Abū Bakr al-Fārisī** (fl. ca. 350/960)
- **Ibn al-Qāṣṣ** (d. 336/947)
- **Abū Bakr al-Ṣayrafī** (d. 330/942)
- **al-Qaffāl al-Shāshī** (d. 336/947)

These works were "not the product of an ongoing process of elaboration based on an established tradition" — they were, rather, "the product of the specific historical process that had begun a century or more earlier," culminating under the influence of the synthesis at the close of the third/ninth century. They owed "little more to Shāfiʿī than nominal affiliation."

## The Retrospective Attribution to Al-Shāfiʿī

The authority of al-Shāfiʿī as "founder" of the Shāfiʿī school and "master architect" of uṣūl al-fiqh was, in Hallaq's account, "both constructed and augmented" by Ibn Surayj's generation and the generations following. Al-Shāfiʿī had advocated a rudimentary form of the synthesis — defending ḥadīth supremacy over sunnaic practice and insisting on text-controlled qiyās — making it "convenient for Ibn Surayj and his students to impute the achievement of uṣūl al-fiqh to him." By the time al-Shāfiʿī was "rediscovered," his theory "had not only come to be taken for granted, but must have also been seen as rudimentary and basic."

This pattern of retrospective construction of a school founder's authority is a recurring feature of Islamic intellectual history.

## Counterfactual Significance

Without Ibn Surayj's circle, the systematic articulation of uṣūl al-fiqh might have occurred later or in a different school context. The Ḥanafī tradition, through figures like al-Shāshī (d. ca. 344/955), was simultaneously producing systematic legal theory, suggesting that the synthesis was structurally inevitable by the fourth/tenth century — the product of a broad intellectual process, not of any single school or jurist. Ibn Surayj's significance is therefore as the first *named* crystallization point of the process, not as its sole cause.
