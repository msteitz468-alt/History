# Nerva (Marcus Cocceius Nerva)

The **first of the "Five Good Emperors"** (96–98) — the elderly, childless senator raised to the
purple by the Senate after the assassination of Domitian, whose chief and lasting act was the
**adoption of Trajan**, inaugurating the **adoptive succession** that defined the Antonine age.
Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 2.

## A senatorial emperor

When [[domitian|Domitian]] was murdered (Sept. 96), there were fears history would repeat 69 —
"an extravagant young aesthete... replaced by a childless patrician sexagenarian." **Nerva** —
of high birth, descended from a triumviral consul and imperial jurists, but **unrelated to the
Flavians** — was chosen by the conspirators precisely because he was uncontroversial. Crucially,
he did **not** come to power by armed revolt (unlike Galba): the **army had remained loyal to
Domitian** (for the pay rise), and the initiative lay with neither army nor Senate but with the
palace assassins. This left Nerva weak. The Senate's first act of restored *libertas* was to
**condemn Domitian's memory** (*damnatio memoriae*), and senators rushed to prosecute their
enemies — a vendetta Nerva, by nature inclined to **compromise and conciliation**, worked to
restrain.

## Clemency and frugality

Nerva's reign was framed as the restoration of liberty after tyranny (the coin legends *Libertas
Publica*, *Roma Renascens*). He confirmed his predecessors' *beneficia* (protecting even
Domitian's beneficiaries), swore **not to put senators to death**, banned the *maiestas* charges
of defamation and "adopting Jewish ways," recalled the exiles and the philosophers, and ended the
abuses of the ***fiscus Iudaicus*** (celebrated on coins). He financed his liberality to plebs
and soldiers through **ostentatious frugality** (selling imperial estates and treasures),
introduced the **alimenta** scheme (loans to support poor children in Italy, fully developed
under Trajan), and relied on a circle of **elderly, cautious statesmen** (Frontinus, Iulius
Ursus, Verginius Rufus) whose careers predated Domitian's terror.

## The praetorian mutiny and the adoption of Trajan

Nerva's fatal weakness was his **age and childlessness** and his **lack of control of the army**.
In autumn **97** the **praetorian guard mutinied** under Casperius Aelianus, seizing the palace
and forcing Nerva to surrender Domitian's assassins and even to **thank the soldiers** for their
murder — a humiliating revelation of his impotence. To restore his authority, Nerva turned to
**adoption** — "making a virtue of necessity by describing it as a free choice of the best." On
the occasion of a Danubian victory (late Oct. 97) he adopted **[[trajan|Marcus Ulpius
Trajanus]]**, the trusted commander of the legions of Upper Germany (the nearest large consular
army), as his son and Caesar. Nerva died of fever three months later (**Jan. 98**).

## Significance

Nerva's reign was brief and, in itself, fragile, but his **adoption of Trajan** was epochal: it
established the **principle (and, for the next eighty years, the practice) of the adoptive
succession** — a childless emperor choosing the ablest available man rather than a blood heir —
that gave the empire the stable, capable rulers Gibbon called the period "during which the
condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous." It also showed, in the praetorian
mutiny, that **military backing remained the precondition of imperial power**, which Trajan (a
great army commander) supplied.
