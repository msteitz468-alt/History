# The Phoenicians

## Overview

The **Phoenicians** (self-named **Canaanites**; "Phoenician" likely from the Greek
for the purple murex dye they traded) were a Semitic-speaking people of the Levantine
coast whose city-states — **Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Beirut** — dominated Mediterranean
trade for four centuries (c. 1200–800 BCE) after the
[[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]]. In
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Benjamin & Wiesner-Hanks, Ch 12) they are the
archetype of a world-historical phenomenon: the **dynamism of small commercial
city-states** that, "geographically tiny," could out-innovate and even challenge the
"vast but sluggish tributary empires."

## The Canaanite Revival out of the Collapse (CAH II.2)

CAH II.2 (chapter XXXIII, Albright) supplies the **origin story** that CWH IV's
world-historical overview presumes — "the Canaanite revival in Phoenicia." The
[[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] drastically reduced the
[[canaanite-civilization|Canaanite]] world: the [[hebrew-kingdom|Israelites]] took
the Palestinian hills, the [[sea-peoples|Sea Peoples]] / [[philistines|Philistines]]
seized the southern coast, and the [[arameans|Aramaeans]] overran the Syrian
interior — leaving the Canaanites only the narrow, mountain-backed coast of Lebanon.
Contraction became opportunity because the same upheaval cleared the field: the
destruction of the [[hittites|Hittite Empire]] removed the Anatolian threat, Egypt
and Assyria were too weak to interfere, and the **collapse of
[[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] sea power** emptied the western Mediterranean
of rivals. The spread of **iron** (after the Hittite monopoly broke) and of
**watertight cisterns** then gave better ships and a larger coastal population to
crew them.

Tyre and Sidon were both destroyed in the crisis; **Tyre was rebuilt under Sidonian
auspices** (native Tyrian date c. 1191 BCE), and the two formed a single
"**Sidonian**" state (the general term for the southern Phoenicians in the Bible and
Homer). The **Wenamun report** (c. 1075 BCE, under Ramesses XI) captures the moment:
**Zakarbaal of Byblos** treats the Egyptian envoy with contempt, reminding him Egypt
no longer rules Asia, and describes Phoenician shipping organised into **trading
syndicates (ḫubūr)** under foreign princely protection — pooled capital and convoys
against piracy. Byblos then led; the [[philistines|Tjekker]] of Dor were feared
pirates.

The **10th century** was the golden age. Once **[[david|David]]** broke the
[[philistines|Philistine]] sea-empire (c. 975 BCE), Tyre under **Hiram I**
(c. 969–936 BCE), in close alliance with **[[solomon|Solomon]]**, launched the great
expansion: joint Red Sea expeditions from **Ezion-geber to Ophir** for gold and
ivory, the "**Tarshish ships**" working Sardinian and Spanish metals, and the western
colonies (**Citium, Utica, Carthage, Gades**) — which Albright dates, on epigraphic
and art-historical grounds, no later than the 10th–9th centuries, rejecting the still
higher legendary foundation dates. Like the [[philistines|Philistines]], Phoenician
kings were checked by a **council of elders** (*moʿed*).

## A Commercial Thalassocracy

Lacking the agrarian base or armies of the great empires, the Phoenicians built
wealth through **seagoing trade** — importing food and resources, exporting prized
manufactures (above all murex-dyed purple/red textiles, glass, and metalwork).
Their fleets ranged for tin, copper, ivory, and silver as far as the Atlantic coasts
of Iberia, France, and Africa, and reputedly the British Isles, quickening
commercial and cultural exchange across the [[trade-network-formation|middle sea]].
Trade was organized through **emporia** (trading colonies), and (debated) likely run
by the city aristocracies rather than independent merchants.

## Two World-Historical Legacies

1. **The [[alphabet|alphabet]].** They adopted and spread a **consonantal phonetic
   alphabet** (probably invented in the Sinai) — far easier to learn than logographic
   or syllabic scripts. The Greeks adapted it (adding vowels), passed it to the
   Romans, and it underlies most of the world's alphabets.
2. **[[carthage|Carthage]].** Their North African colony (trad. founded 814 BCE) grew
   into a major maritime empire that fought [[roman-republic|Rome]] for control of the
   western Mediterranean in the **Punic Wars** (264–146 BCE).

## Significance

The Phoenicians prefigure the recurring power of commercial city-states — the Greek
[[city-state|polis]], the Indian Ocean ports, the medieval Italian republics. Their
political history is "relatively insignificant," but their commercial and cultural
impact, CWH IV stresses, is "anything but." Subordinated successively to
[[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]], [[neo-babylonian-empire|Babylon]], and Persia, the
homeland cities ended with [[alexander-the-great|Alexander's]] sack of Tyre (332 BCE).

## Historiography

The Phoenicians left little literature of their own; they are known largely through
the hostile or incidental testimony of others (Greek, Roman, Biblical) plus
archaeology. Recent scholarship (Aubet) has rehabilitated Phoenician agency in the
Mediterranean against a long Greco-Roman-centered narrative. Compare the
[[trade-network-formation|commercial network]] framing and the [[coinage|monetary]]
analysis (the Phoenician/Carthaginian trade preceded coinage).

Note that the CAH II.2 (Albright) material above rests on his **idiosyncratic high
chronology** (e.g. raising [[ramesses-ii|Ramesses II]]'s accession to 1304 BCE) and
on confident use of late and legendary sources; later scholarship lowers his dates
but confirms the central narrative of a Canaanite maritime revival out of the
collapse.
