# Northumbria

The northernmost Anglo-Saxon realm, formed by the union of **Bernicia** (north of the
Tees, centred on the citadel of **Bamburgh**) and **Deira** (Yorkshire, centred on
**[[places/york|York]]**). In the seventh century it was the dominant power of the
archipelago — its kings **Oswald, Oswiu, and Ecgfrith** (634–85) holding overlordship over
the southern English, the Picts, the British, and Dál Riata — and the seedbed of the
Insular golden age (the [[events/synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]], 664; Bede; the
Lindisfarne Gospels). William Aird's chapter in
[[sources/stafford-companion-early-middle-ages-britain-ireland-2009|Stafford's *Companion*]]
treats its later, more fragmented history.

## A Tripartite Political World

Aird rejects the West-Saxon "liberation" narrative: Northumbria "should not be seen as
predestined for incorporation into England," and Scots and Scandinavians were "net
contributors to [its] history." After the Great Army took **York** (867), the region's
parts diverged sharply: **Deiran** Northumbria (York) became deeply Scandinavian and
commercially vibrant, its **archbishops** acting as quasi-political "prince-bishops"
(Wulfstan I received Óláf Guthfrithson in 939 and backed Eric Bloodaxe in 952); **Bernicia**
(the earls of Bamburgh) faced the Scots and, against York-based Vikings, "allied with those
closest at hand … namely the Scots."

## The Community of St Cuthbert

The [[actors/cuthbert-of-lindisfarne|community of St Cuthbert]] left Lindisfarne in 875,
settling at Chester-le-Street and then **Durham** (995); King Guthred granted it the land
between Tyne and Wear, the core of the "liberty of St Cuthbert." By 1000 it claimed estates
"from Lothian … to Yorkshire," so that "anyone wishing to extend his lordship into the
region had to reach an accommodation" with it. Cnut made a barefoot pilgrimage to Cuthbert's
shrine; Bishop William of Saint-Calais introduced the **Benedictine rule** to Durham in
1083, breaking the local families' hold on the cult.

## Between Wessex and Scotland

Royal power from the south was "always exceptionally limited," especially north of the Tees.
Edgar's **Chester ceremony (973)** — under-kings rowing his boat — and his recognition of
Scots possession of **Lothian** (confirmed after Carham, 1018) registered the limits of
English reach. The rebellion of 1065 against Earl Tostig (defending the "Laws of Cnut" and
northern custom) and William I's ferocious **harrying of the north** (1069–70) both expose
the structural difficulty of ruling the north from the south.
