## Overview

Mbata was the most constitutionally significant province of the [[kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]], occupying a position described in the earliest sources as virtual co-regency. Its ruler, the **Mwene Mbata**, held the title "first voice of Congo": no king could be made without his consent, and Kongo's kings were required to be his "closest relative." The province lay in the Inkisi Valley on the eastern flank of Kongo's nuclear territory, along the route toward the [[kongo-dia-nlaza|Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza]].

## Constitutional Role

The centrality of Mbata to Kongo's constitution is documented across multiple independent sources spanning a century:

**Afonso I's letters (1509–1535)**: Writing on 5 October 1514 about his rise to power in 1509, Afonso I noted that his enemies had appealed to the Mwene Mbata (named Dom Jorge) to unseat him. He described the Mwene Mbata as "the head of the kingdom" and "he who would be king should be his closest relative." In a letter of the late 1520s he further stated that the Mwene Mbata was the "first voice of Congo, and no one can make a king without him, according to the custom of the country" (Brásio 1952:521).

**Duarte Lopes / Pigafetta (1591)**: Lopes, serving as Kongo's ambassador to Rome, gave the fullest account of Mbata's privileges. He described Mbata as "great and strong in ancient times" and having "come to join the kingdom of Kongo spontaneously of its own free will, without war, as there were dissensions among the great men." In exchange, it was "esteemed above the other provinces of the kingdom in privileges and liberty." The king of Kongo appointed Mbata's ruler from its own royal family. Should Kongo's royal line fail, the Mwene Mbata, as "second person," would succeed to the throne. He sat next to the king, ate at a table nearly as high, maintained his own musketeers, commanded an army that "spearheaded warfare among neighbouring regions," and could make his own decrees which no one could alter (Pigafetta 1591:37).

**Mateus Cardoso (1622/1624)**: At Pedro II's coronation in 1622, it was normally the Mwene Mbata who delivered the official history of Kongo. On that occasion the Mwene Vunda substituted. Cardoso's "History of the Kingdom of Congo" treats Mbata's special status as an afterthought — reflecting the relative political decline of Mbata by the seventeenth century — noting that Mbata was not conquered but later came to be regarded as merely tributary.

**Title of "grandfather"**: Cardoso records that the rulers of Mbata were addressed by Kongo kings with the honorific *enkak'andi a Mwene Kongo* ("Grandfathers of the King"), while the kings called themselves the Mwene Mbata's *nkotekolo* ("grandchildren"). Da Montesarchio, visiting in the 1650s, found this same "grandfather of Kongo" title extended across the northeast of the kingdom, suggesting it applied throughout the [[kongo-dia-nlaza|Seven Kingdoms]] region of which Mbata had formerly been part.

## Origins: Part of the Seven Kingdoms

Thornton (ch. 1 of Bostoen & Brinkman 2018) argues that Mbata was originally a member polity of the [[kongo-dia-nlaza|Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza]] — the large eastern empire from which Kongo progressively detached territory. When the Seven Kingdoms began to weaken (c. early 1300s), its western vassals in the Inkisi Valley broke away. Mbata, perhaps the strongest of these newly-independent entities, sought an alliance with the ruling family of Mpemba Kasi (associated with Vungu on the Congo's north bank). This alliance — between Nimi a Nzima (Mpemba Kasi) and Nsaku Lau (Mbata) — produced [[lukeni-lua-nimi|Lukeni lua Nimi]], the founder of Kongo.

In this reconstruction, Mbata was the **senior partner** in the founding alliance: "at least in the deepest layers of tradition, Mbata was a senior partner in the alliance which Lukeni lua Nimi would upset as he built his own expansive domain to the south and then west, along the coast." The constitutional privileges retained by Mbata into the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries represent this original seniority, gradually eroded by Kongo's expansion.

## Relationship to Nsundi and Mpangu

Lopes recorded that Nsundi (on the north bank) and Mpangu were among the early conquests of the Kongo kings, made after the alliance with Mbata. Cardoso's 1621 report on a revolt placed these three provinces — Nsundi, Mpangu, and Mbata — within the geographic region called "Momboares" (*Mumbwadi*, "the Seveners"), the remnant territory of the Seven Kingdoms, suggesting they had all been detached from Kongo dia Nlaza in the founding period.

Nsundi had its own special constitutional role: the heir apparent's seat. Afonso I had ruled Nsundi before his kingship; Álvaro II had served as Mwene Nsundi before becoming king.

## Decline

By the seventeenth century, Mbata's power had been substantially reduced. The narrative shift between Lopes's account (c. 1580s) — which gives Mbata near-co-regency status — and Cardoso's account (1624) — which treats Mbata's special position as a merely historical courtesy — reflects the centralizing ambitions of seventeenth-century Kongo kings. Cardoso explains Mbata's subjection by recording that "what was once agreed upon has become tribute, so that today the lords of Bata are subjects to that kingdom." This trajectory prefigured the broader [[kongo-decentralization|decentralization]] that would shake the kingdom after the [[battle-of-mbwila-1665|Battle of Mbwila (1665)]].
