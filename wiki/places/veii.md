# Veii

## Overview

**Veii** was one of the most powerful cities of the **Etruscan dodecapolis** and,
at its height, probably comparable in population and wealth to **Rome** itself. It
occupied a naturally defended plateau approximately 16 km north of Rome on the right
bank of the Tiber, carved out by two streams (the Cremera and a lesser tributary).
Its violent destruction by Rome in **396 BCE** — the most consequential military
event in early Republican history before the Punic Wars — transformed the political
geography of central Italy.

## Geography and Natural Defenses

The plateau of Veii (modern Piazza d'Armi and the surrounding area) was naturally
fortified on three sides by steep stream gorges. The only easy approaches were from
the south (toward Rome) and the north. The city occupied an area of approximately
190 hectares — smaller than Rome's c. 285-hectare area within the Servian Wall but
comparable in effective urban density. It was connected to Rome by the Via Veientana
running along the right bank of the Tiber.

The territory around Veii (**ager Veientanus**) was extensive and agriculturally
rich — wider and more productive than Rome's cramped territory on the left bank.
Control of this territory was the economic prize that motivated the Roman conquest.

## The City

Veii was a fully urbanized Etruscan city with:

- **Monumental temples** at the Portonaccio sanctuary and elsewhere, decorated with
  high-quality terracotta sculpture. The Portonaccio sanctuary has produced some of
  the finest Etruscan terracotta work known — including fragments of the famous
  **Apollo of Veii** (c. 510–500 BCE), a nearly life-size terracotta group
  attributed to the sculptor **Vulca of Veii**
- **Vulca** himself is the only Etruscan artist known by name: he was commissioned
  by the Tarquins to create the terracotta cult statue for Rome's great temple of
  Jupiter Optimus Maximus on the Capitoline — evidence of Veii's artistic and
  cultural prestige even as a rival of Rome
- **Planned street grid** with evidence of urban organization
- **Water management**: the plateau's streams and the surrounding territory required
  extensive **cuniculi** (drainage tunnels) to maintain agricultural productivity —
  the most intensive hydraulic engineering in pre-Roman central Italy
- An extensive **necropolis** revealing a wealthy aristocratic culture with strong
  Greek and Near Eastern imports in the seventh–sixth centuries (orientalizing phase)

## Relationship with Rome

The **two cities were structural rivals**: both occupied defensible hill sites,
both commanded sections of the lower Tiber, both competed for the **salt routes**
running from the Tiber mouth (at the site of Ostia) into the Apennine interior.
The Tiber was navigable for shallow-draft vessels as far as Rome; the left-bank
salt depots (the Via Salaria route) and the right-bank approaches (the Veii side)
were both essential to the central Italian economy.

Rome controlled the left bank and the Tiber island crossing; Veii controlled the
right bank and the northern approaches. A Romano-Veientane border dispute over the
salt pans at the Tiber mouth was an ancient source of conflict.

The **Fidenae** dispute (Fidenae was a satellite town between Rome and Veii, on the
left bank of the Tiber) was a recurring flashpoint: Rome and Veii each tried to
control it, and it changed hands several times in the fifth century before finally
passing to Rome.

The most dramatic episode of the conflict was the **private war of the Fabii
(c. 479 BCE)**: the Fabian clan established a fortified post on the Cremera
(the stream on Veii's southwestern edge) and conducted what was effectively a
private vendetta against Veii, until the entire Fabian contingent was ambushed and
killed at the **Battle of the Cremera (477 BCE)**. This episode — possibly legendary
in its details — reflects the reality of ongoing low-intensity raiding and
counter-raiding in the Tiber valley.

## Destruction and Aftermath

The capture of Veii in **396 BCE** was total and deliberate. The city was **sacked,
its population enslaved or dispersed**, and its political organization destroyed.
The **Juno Regina evocatio** transferred Veii's patron goddess to Rome. The
**ager Veientanus** was distributed in 7-iugera lots to Roman citizens, nearly
doubling Rome's agricultural territory.

The site was not immediately abandoned — some votive activity continued at the
Portonaccio sanctuary — but the city ceased to exist as a political entity. No
successor community arose on the site for centuries. Under Augustus, a Roman
**municipium** was established at Veii, but the ancient city was not rebuilt; the
new settlement was elsewhere on the plateau and significantly smaller.

## Archaeological Significance

Veii is exceptionally well-excavated relative to most Etruscan sites. The British
School at Rome conducted major investigations in the twentieth century. Key findings:

- Clear **destruction horizon** datable to c. 400–380 BCE — confirming the
  historicity and approximate date of the Roman capture, in contrast to the
  **absence** of any destruction horizon at Rome for the supposed Gallic Sack of 390
- The **Portonaccio sanctuary** (outside the city proper, on a spur overlooking
  the gorge) was a major religious complex with four building phases and extensive
  terracotta votives; the famous Apollo group was found here
- The **cuniculi** drainage network is among the most studied examples of pre-Roman
  hydraulic engineering in Italy
- The **Campetti area** within the plateau has produced evidence of continuous
  occupation from the Final Bronze Age through the destruction

## Why This Place Mattered

**Strategically**: Veii controlled the northern approach to Rome and the right-bank
Tiber. As long as Veii existed, Rome could not expand northward or control the
full river corridor.

**Economically**: The ager Veientanus was the largest block of good agricultural
land available to Rome in central Lazio. Its distribution transformed the Roman
citizen body from a land-poor city-state to a territorially substantial power.

**Symbolically**: Veii was, in cultural and demographic terms, essentially a second
Rome. Its complete destruction — and the absorption of its gods, its land, and
eventually its people — was Rome's most dramatic demonstration (before Carthage)
that it was willing to annihilate as well as incorporate.

**Methodologically**: The contrast between the clear archaeological destruction at
Veii and the absence of archaeological destruction at Rome from the "Gallic Sack"
is one of the most revealing data points in the study of the reliability of early
Roman historical tradition.

## Significance Changed Over Time

| Period | Significance |
|---|---|
| 8th–6th c. BCE | Major Etruscan city; artistic and cultural peer of Rome; controls right-bank Tiber and salt routes |
| 5th c. BCE | Rome's primary rival in Latium; repeated conflicts; the Fabian private war |
| 396 BCE | Destroyed; territory annexed; population dispersed; becomes the model for total Roman conquest |
| 4th–1st c. BCE | Site sporadically occupied; votive activity at sanctuaries continues |
| Augustan era | New municipium established on site; ancient city not rebuilt |
