# Malabar

Coastal region of southwestern India stretching roughly two months’ journey from Sindābūr to Kawlam. A critical node in 14th-century Indian Ocean pepper and spice trade.

## Geographic and Economic Features
The entire route lay under shade trees. Every half mile stood wooden rest-houses with chambers for travelers (Muslim or infidel), wells, and appointed attendants who served drinks (into the hand for Muslims to avoid ritual contamination; vessels for others). The land was densely cultivated with virtually no uncultivated span. Primary wealth came from black pepper (trees resembling dark grapevines trained on supports; harvested in autumn, sun-dried without boiling, sold in quantity). Aloes (a type of seed-aloe) were abundant and cheap on the shore.

## Political and Social Order
Twelve kings of varying strength (greatest fielding ~50,000 troops). Districts were demarcated by wooden “gates of safety” belonging to each ruler; a criminal fleeing into another king’s territory was untouchable. Succession was matrilineal: the king’s sister’s son inherited, not his own son. Strict justice prevailed: death for stealing even a single nut or fruit seed; theft was effectively unknown. Nobles traveled in wooden boxes (dūla) carried on the shoulders of hirelings or slaves. No beasts of burden were used for ordinary travel or goods; everything moved on human carriers (one or two hundred per merchant party) who used iron-shod hooked staffs as rests.

## Muslim Presence
Muslim merchants were present in most districts and maintained their own houses. Even in infidel towns they received full hospitality and cooked on banana leaves (leftovers given to dogs). At Hinaur (a key port in the region) the population was Shafiʿi Muslim; the king (Jamāl Oddīn) was noted for naval activity and relative independence from the Delhi Sultan.

## Significance
Ibn Battuta’s detailed eyewitness account (c. 1340s, Lee 1829 translation) provides primary texture for the functioning of a major pepper-exporting coastal society, traveler infrastructure, matrilineal politics, and the integration of Muslim merchants into non-Muslim polities along the Indian Ocean circuit.
