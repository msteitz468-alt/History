# Caffa

**Caffa** (modern Feodosia) was the principal [[republic-of-genoa|Genoese]]
colony in the Crimea and the great Genoese entrepôt of the Black Sea from the
early 1270s until its conquest by the Ottomans in 1475. Founded on an open bay on
the eastern Crimean coast, sheltered by a mountain chain, it grew from a trading
post into a walled multi-ethnic city of perhaps 20,000 inhabitants — a colony
that Michel Balard, in *[[abulafia-berend-medieval-frontiers-2002|Medieval
Frontiers]]*, treats as the model of a **"trading frontier": a living membrane**
rather than a barrier, porous to demographic, economic, religious and cultural
exchange even as it was fortified against attack.

## Foundation and Function

The Genoese established Caffa in the early 1270s, in the commercial space opened
to Western merchants by the Treaty of Nymphaion (1261) between Genoa and the
restored Byzantine Empire. Caffa became the redistribution hub for the goods of
the Pontic and trans-Asian trade: grain (wheat), furs, salt, dried fish, and —
carried overland from Central Asia via Tana and Trebizond — silk and spices,
funnelled onward to Constantinople and the West. In 1390–91 Caffa supplied 14%
and 10% of Genoa's grain in successive years. It was also a major node of the
Black Sea slave trade.

## Government

Caffa was administered through the Genoese **Officium Gazarie**. After the fall
of Constantinople to the Ottomans in 1453 cut the colony's lifeline, the
administration of Genoa's Pontic possessions passed to the **[[republic-of-genoa|
Banco di San Giorgio]]**, whose reluctance to fund defence out of shareholder
revenue left Caffa's walls unrepaired. Unlike some frontier towns, Caffa did not
generate its own body of frontier law: it followed the Genoese legal model, with
statutes issued from the metropole.

## Population and Coexistence

The colony was strikingly plural. At the end of the 13th century roughly 74% of
identifiable inhabitants were Ligurian/Genoese; a c. 1400 register lists 1,516
people, 85% of the Westerners Ligurian. Among the "Oriental" communities, Greeks
were the largest group into the late 14th century (listed first in the *ordo de
Caffa* of 1316), but in the 15th century **Armenians** became the most numerous —
possibly refugees from Cilicia, Ani, Saraï, or the wars of [[timur|Timur]]. A
third community of Turks and Tatars paid a special 5% tax and was judged by a
*tudun* appointed by the neighbouring Tatar Lord of Solgat. Intermarriage
occurred in one direction only: a 1381 document records that 14% of sailors'
wives were unions of a Latin man with an Armenian, Greek or Tatar woman. Church
lands of Greeks, Armenians and other Eastern Christians were protected by
statute; Pope Clement VI called Caffa a *refugium singulare* for Oriental
Christians and funded a defensive tower that still bears his name. The
multilingual *Codex Cumanicus* interpreter's manual was likely produced for this
frontier society.

## Fortification and Fall

Caffa's defences grew from a ditch and wooden palisade — breached by the Tatars
in 1308 — to a stone *castrum* and city wall (ordinance of 1316, completed 1352),
which survived Tatar sieges under Khan Janibek of the Golden Horde in 1344 and
1346, and finally a 5.5-km outer wall (1382–85). Yet the garrison shrank even as
the city grew: from 236 men-at-arms during the Solgat war of 1386–87 to only 154
in 1459 — far too few for a 6-km circuit. Balard argues that the condition of the
walls and garrison tracked the fiscal commitment of the metropole directly; the
withdrawal of investment after 1453 opened the way for **Mehmed II's** fleet,
which took Caffa in 1475 and ended Genoese rule in the Crimea.

## Significance

Caffa is a laboratory case for the [[medieval-frontier|medieval frontier]] as a
zone of osmosis rather than a line: here the "enemy" (the Tatars) lived inside
the city itself, and the colony's life depended on exchange across, not closure
of, the frontier. Its fall exemplifies Balard's "breaking of equilibrium" — the
loosening of ties to the homeland, declining finance and recruitment, and the
demographic shift that dissolved the balance sustaining the colony.
