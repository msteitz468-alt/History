# Puebla-Tlaxcala Region

## Geographic description

The **Puebla-Tlaxcala** region occupies the southeastern corner of the central Mexican highlands astride routes to the [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of Mexico]], the Gulf Coast, the Mixteca, Guerrero, and [[morelos-region|Morelos]] (Patricia Plunket Nagoda and Gabriela Uruñuela, “Puebla-Tlaxcala Region,” in Evans & Webster 2001). Core elevations are mostly **2,000–2,600 m**, bounded west by the Sierra Nevada (**Popocatépetl** 5,452 m; **Iztaccíhuatl** 5,286 m) and centered on **Malinche** (4,461 m). Climate is temperate subhumid (**14–22°C**; **800–1,200 mm** rain). Domesticates include maize, beans, squash, amaranth, chile, nopal, maguey, and others; the only pre-Hispanic animal domesticates were dogs and turkeys. The Puebla-Tlaxcala Valley is a cool highland basin defined by major Balsas tributaries (Atoyac, Nexapa). Western areas were repeatedly affected by Popocatépetl activity (Tetimpa, Atlixco).

Core kingdoms produced monumental architecture, murals, and polychrome ceramics. Historical ethnic mosaic included **Pinome (Popoloca)**, **Otomí**, **Olmeca-Xicalanca**, and **Nahua** (Tolteca-Chichimeca) groups.

## Paleoindian and Archaic

The **Valsequillo Depression** has produced some of the earliest claimed American occupations (Caulapan radiocarbon dates prior to **21,000** years ago; Hueyatlaco), but the data are much debated; mammoth remains are common but never with stone tools. A Clovis point is reported at Chaucingo, Tlaxcala. Archaic remains are poorly known; El Texcal cave (**7000–2500 B.C.**) has Tehuacán-like lithics but no plant remains documenting agriculture’s start in the region (Plunket & Uruñuela).

## Formative sequence

| Phase | Approx. dates | Developments (Plunket & Uruñuela) |
|---|---|---|
| Tzompantepec | to ~1200 B.C. | Earliest settled hamlets; Ajalpan-related ceramics |
| Tlatempa | 1200–800 B.C. | Settlement growth; Olmec/Olmecoid materials in west (Moyotzingo, Colotzingo; Las Bocas baby-face figurines); first [[cholula|Cholula]] occupation late in phase |
| Texoloc | 800–400 B.C. | Hierarchical settlement; ceremonial centers/chiefdoms (Tlalancaleca, Coapa, Colotzingo, etc.); irrigation/terracing; double-line-break whitewares |
| Tezoquipan | 400 B.C.–A.D. 100 | Continued growth; Proto-Cholula monumental architecture; canals, raised fields/chinampas; early **talud-tablero** at Tlalancaleca and Tetimpa; Capulac Concepción ball court; Cantona multi-ball-court urbanism east of core |

One of the most important architectural developments — **talud-tablero** house and temple platforms — dates to the end of Texoloc or very beginning of Tezoquipan at Tlalancaleca and Tetimpa. At **Amalucan** (east of Puebla city), a Middle Formative irrigation/drainage canal system in a fossil Pleistocene lake basin was abandoned in the late Formative/Early Classic; Cerro Amalucan slopes later held Early Classic residential terraces (Melvin Fowler).

## Classic: Cholula and Cantona

**[[cholula|Cholula]]** was the major Early Classic city of the region; by **A.D. 500** it covered about **10 km²**, when most major construction of the Great Pyramid was undertaken (Plunket & Uruñuela; Geoffrey G. McCafferty’s dedicated entry). Tlaxcala’s Classic saw population decline or stagnation under Cholula’s rise and Teotihuacan intrusion near Calpulalpan. Cholula declined from ~**A.D. 550**; building ceased ~**600**; population may have fled to Cerro Zapotecas.

East of the core, **Cantona** (no dedicated place page yet) was a fortified Classic city of ~**12.5 km²** — second in size only to Teotihuacan — competing in the obsidian trade (Angel García Cook). Occupied **A.D. 100–900**, peaking **600–900** with an estimated **80,000–90,000** people on basalt badlands at **2,500–2,600 m**; economy combined agriculture with Oyameles-Zaragoza obsidian export to southern Puebla, southern Veracruz, and the Maya area; **24** ball courts; dense walled patio groups and constructed streets; stone-on-stone construction without mortar/plaster; strong fortification and ceremonialism including decapitation and human-bone offerings. Contemporaneous with Teotihuacan and Cholula but shared few cultural elements with them; at peak/decline it resembles Teotenango, [[xochicalco|Xochicalco]], and [[cacaxtla|Cacaxtla]] in fortification.

## Epiclassic: Cacaxtla and Olmeca-Xicalanca

**A.D. 650–850** (Early Texcalac): defensive hilltops; **Olmeca-Xicalanca** capitals at **[[cacaxtla|Cacaxtla]]** and **Xochitécatl** as Teotihuacan power eroded. Cacaxtla murals — Maya references, warfare, sacrifice — are central to understanding post-Teotihuacan Central Mexico (Plunket & Uruñuela; García Cook). The postulated Olmeca-Xicalanca highland intrusion (A.D. **700–900**) is evidenced by “international” painting and architectural styles mixing Lowland Maya and Central Mexican elements; murals depict physically distinct Central Mexican and Lowland Maya individuals (Edward Schortman and Patricia Urban).

## Postclassic to Conquest

Early Postclassic saw Cholula’s resurgence under Olmeca-Xicalanca (Cholulteca I–II, ~**850–1200**). Late Postclassic: Tolteca-Chichimeca/Chichimec arrivals at Cholula, Tlaxcala, Huejotzingo, and related centers; osteology supports population change; the *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* emphasizes alliances and warfare. Cholula became a pilgrimage and market center dedicated to **Quetzalcoatl**; Mixteca-Puebla style polychromes and the **Codex Borgia** tradition are linked to Cholula/Tlaxcala (Plunket & Uruñuela; McCafferty).

Mid–late fifteenth century: the [[aztec-empire|Triple Alliance]] conquered parts of the south and east (garrison at Cuauhquechollan) and prosecuted **flowery wars (*xochiyaoyotl*)** with Huejotzingo, Tlaxcala, and Cholula — never fully absorbing the Tlaxcalan confederacy. At the Conquest, Cortés allied with Tlaxcalan rulers **Xicotencatl** and **Maxixcatzin**; Cholula was massacred as an Aztec ally (see [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519]]).

## Why it mattered

Puebla-Tlaxcala was the **eastern hinge** of central Mexico: early talud-tablero and Formative irrigation; the Great Pyramid and pilgrimage economy of Cholula; Epiclassic international murals at Cacaxtla; Cantona’s fortified obsidian urbanism; and the unconquered Tlaxcalan polities whose alliance with Cortés helped end the Aztec empire. Routes through the region tied the Basin to the Gulf, Mixteca, and Morelos for three millennia.
