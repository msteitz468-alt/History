# Pskov
## Description
**Pskov** is an ancient city located in northwestern European Russia, situated at the confluence of the Pskova and Velikaya rivers, just east of Lake Peipus (Chud). Historically subject to Novgorod, Pskov became an independent city-state republic in 1348 CE and served as Russia's westernmost military bulwark against Sweden, Lithuania, and the Teutonic Knights.

## Historical Significance
During the Kievan period, Pskov was the "little brother" of Novgorod, sharing its commercial and democratic traditions but maintaining a separate administrative identity.

In **1348 CE**, Pskov secured formal independence from Novgorod under the Treaty of Bolotovo, establishing a highly democratic republic. Its political structure featured:
* A **veche** (town assembly) that held supreme authority, electing two posadniki and acting as the high court for serious crimes (l. 1369).
* A prince with strictly circumscribed military and executive powers.
* Subdivisions into streets and quarters (*kontsy*), overseen by a council of elders.

Because Pskov was smaller and more compact than Novgorod, it experienced less social differentiation and class conflict. It was renowned for its peaceful internal life and its brilliant school of stone architecture. Pskov is also famous for the **Pskov Sudebnik (1397 CE)**, a highly developed legal code issued by the veche that served as a major source for later Russian national law.

Unlike Novgorod, Pskov was not a political rival of Moscow; instead, it relied on Muscovite military assistance to defend itself from western invasions. However, in **1511 CE**, Grand Prince Basil III of Moscow formally annexed Pskov, abolishing its special republican institutions, carting away its veche bell, and executing deportations to integrate the city into the Muscovite state (l. 1373).

In the seventeenth century, Pskov remained a key commercial city of the north-west. "Pskov and Novgorod were the two largest commercial cities of the north-west. In both cases in 1650 the townspeople as a whole, together with the musketeers from the garrison, rose up against the city governors and rich merchants who were implicated in the sale of grain to Sweden." (lines 17144–17146). "In Pskov, where the uprising continued for six months, sharp divisions developed between the richer merchants and hereditary servicemen, on the one hand, and the poorer townspeople and strel’tsy, on the other" (lines 17146–17149). Peasants from neighbouring villages joined insurgent raiding parties. The revolt was suppressed after siege by Prince I. N. Khovanskii; settlement negotiated in August 1650.
