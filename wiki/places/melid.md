## Overview

Melid (Neo-Assyrian *Milidia*, Urartian *Meliteia*, Classical *Melitene*) was one of the most significant Neo-Hittite kingdoms of the Euphrates region, centred on the mound now called Arslantepe, 6 km north-east of modern Malatya in eastern Anatolia. Settlement on the site began in the late fifth millennium (Chalcolithic period) and continued through successive ages until the mid first century CE. Its most significant phase, however, belongs to the Neo-Hittite Iron Age. Melid guarded the upper Euphrates corridor between central Anatolia and northern Mesopotamia, and its fate was shaped by the competing pressures of [[carchemish|Carchemish]], [[urartu]], and [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]] over five centuries.

## Geographic Description

The territory of the kingdom was, as Hawkins notes (quoted in Bryce), comparatively well defined: it centred "on the plain of Malatya on the west bank of the upper Euphrates below the junction of the Kara Su and Murat Su branches, and extending westwards along the routes to Anatolia and into the plain of Elbistan." This position placed Melid at the junction of the Anatolian highlands with the Euphrates valley, giving it control over key east-west routes connecting inner Anatolia with Syria and Mesopotamia.

## History

**Late Bronze Age background.** The city is first attested, in the form *Maldiya* or *Malitiya*, in an early fourteenth-century Hittite text (the Indictment of Mita). During this period it was Hittite subject territory, probably within the jurisdiction of the viceroy of [[carchemish]].

**Neo-Hittite foundation.** After the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]], authority over Malatya was assumed by the most prominent Hittite survivor, Kuzi-Teshub, who almost certainly installed a branch of his family there as a local dynasty under Carchemish's overlordship. By the time Kuzi-Teshub's grandsons Runtiya and Arnuwanti occupied the Malatyan throne—both identified in their inscriptions as grandsons of Kuzi-Teshub—the kingdom had very likely already become independent of Carchemish. Their dynastic title was "Country-Lord of the city Malatya."

**The Kuzi-Teshub dynasty.** Two genealogical lines are attested in hieroglyphic inscriptions from the Malatya region. The first, descending from Kuzi-Teshub, includes:
- (PUGNUS-mili I): son of Kuzi-Teshub; no royal title attested; possibly an administrator rather than king
- **Runtiya** (later 12th cent.): first clearly attested ruler; inscriptions record city-restoration, road-building, and population resettlement following Bronze Age disruptions
- **Arnuwanti I** (later 12th cent.): brother and probable successor of Runtiya; titles "King, Hero, Country-Lord"; set up a stele honouring his grandfather Kuzi-Teshub
- (PUGNUS-mili II) (late 12th–early 11th cent.): son of Arnuwanti I; no explicit royal title; possibly equated with the "Allumari" who paid tribute to Tiglath-pileser I, though this remains uncertain
- **Arnuwanti II** (late 12th–early 11th cent.): son of PUGNUS-mili II; title "Country-Lord"; authored the DARENDE inscription
- **PUGNUS-mili III?** (probably 11th or early 10th cent.): possible third member of this name; title "Potent(?) King" (Hawkins's uncertain translation)

**The Taras dynasty.** A second dynasty, beginning with CRUS+RA/I-sa (read by Hawkins as "Taras"), is attested in inscriptions from Elbistan and Arslantepe. Taras claimed substantial frontier extensions and a city-building programme. His successors were Wasu(?)runtiya (his son) and Halpasulupi ("Hero, Lord of Malizi, Potent(?) King"). Whether this dynasty immediately followed or overlapped with the Kuzi-Teshub line remains unclear; an inscription of Taras mentions an "Arnuwanti," possibly indicating continuity between the dynasties.

**Later rulers (9th–early 8th centuries):** After a gap of over a century in written records, the next attested Malatyan king is **Lalli**, a tributary of Shalmaneser III documented in 853 and 844 BCE and still on the throne in 835. He cannot be linked to any name in the hieroglyphic record.

**Shahu/Sahwi** (early 8th cent.): known as father of Hilaruada in Urartian inscriptions; possibly the "Shahu" of the Şırzı hieroglyphic inscription bearing the title "Hero, Country-Lord of Malizi."

**Hilaruada** (c.784–760): his reign extended through several decades of the first half of the eighth century. Argishti I of Urartu attacked Malatya in his fourth regnal year (c.784); Hilaruada was still reigning when Sarduri II conquered the kingdom c.760. Malatya then remained subject to Urartu until 743.

**Sulumal** (743–732): led the coalition that joined Sarduri II against Tiglath-pileser III; after the coalition's defeat, Tiglath-pileser allowed him to retain his throne as a tributary, attested for 738 and 732.

**Gunzinanu** (deposed c.719): deposed by Sargon II after participating in anti-Assyrian uprisings in the Hatti lands.

**Tarhunazi** (c.719–712): appointed by Sargon in Gunzinanu's place. He too broke his oath to Sargon, withholding tribute. Sargon invaded, destroyed the royal capital and surrounding cities, and deported Tarhunazi and much of his population to Assyria. Part of the kingdom (the land of Kammanu with Til-garimmu) was assigned to an Assyrian provincial governor; the city of Malatya itself was given to Muwatalli of [[kummukh|Kummukh]].

A final king, Mugallu, reigned in the seventh century and falls outside Bryce's chapter coverage here.

## Why It Mattered

Melid's strategic value lay in its position astride the upper Euphrates corridor and the routes connecting the Anatolian plateau with the Euphrates valley. It was the northernmost major Neo-Hittite kingdom and the link between Carchemish's sphere and the Anatolian interior. Its ruling dynasty's descent from Kuzi-Teshub gave it the strongest genealogical claim of any Neo-Hittite kingdom to the Hittite imperial legacy. The kingdom's agricultural plain and its control of the Elbistan routes made it a prize contested repeatedly by [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]] and [[urartu]].

## Relationship to Assyria and Urartu

Melid paid tribute to Tiglath-pileser I (1114–1076) as early as c.1100 BCE, establishing a pattern of Assyrian tributary status. Shalmaneser III (858–824) campaigned against it repeatedly. In the ninth and early eighth centuries, three Urartian kings — Minua, Argishti I, and Sarduri II — imposed Urartian sovereignty over Melid, making it a contested buffer zone between the two great powers of the age. Tiglath-pileser III's decisive victory over the Urartian–Arpad coalition in 743 ended Urartian overlordship and reimposed Assyrian tributary status. Sargon II finally terminated the kingdom's independence c.712.

## Historiography

Several chronological disputes attach to Melid's history. First, Bryce notes a disagreement over Tiglath-pileser I's references to "Milidia": one view holds there was only one such city (Malatyan); the other posits a second Milidia in northern Mesopotamia (Hanigalbat), though the latter is otherwise unattested and Bryce considers the balance of evidence to favour a single, Malatyan site. Second, the identity of "Allumari" — the man who paid tribute to Tiglath-pileser I from Malatya — cannot be firmly established; he may be hidden behind the hieroglyphic PUGNUS symbol, but no confirmed connection to the known dynastic sequence exists. Third, Hawkins and Bryce disagree on whether the Urartian-attested *Hilaruada* can be equated with the hieroglyphically-attested *Sa(?)tiruntiya*: Hawkins regards the names as too dissimilar; Bryce argues the discrepancy is not unique in Neo-Hittite naming and does not entirely dismiss the equation. Fourth, the relationship between the Kuzi-Teshub dynasty and the Taras dynasty at Malatya — whether one directly succeeded the other or there was overlap — remains unresolved.
