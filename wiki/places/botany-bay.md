# Botany Bay

**Botany Bay** was the site named in the Pitt administration's August 1786 decision to
send British convicts to [[australia|New Holland]], and the landfall of [[first-fleet-1788|the
First Fleet]] in January 1788. It lay on the country of the **Gweagal and Kamaygal**
peoples. The name became so synonymous with penal exile that convicts spoke of being
"bound to Botany Bay" and caricatures of the "first parliament of the 'Thief Colony' were
offered for sale" in London within weeks of the scheme's announcement (CHA vol. 1, ch. 3).

## Geographic Description

Botany Bay is a broad, shallow embayment on the New South Wales coast, roughly 30 km south
of Port Jackson. Cook's 1770 visit gave it its name from the botanical specimens collected
by Joseph Banks. The bay offered no adequate anchorage, and a shortage of fresh water
in the first El Niño summer of 1788 led Governor [[arthur-phillip|Arthur Phillip]] to seek
a better site immediately on arrival. He found **Sydney Cove** at Port Jackson to the
north — "a snug cove" with fresh water and no warriors on the shore — and moved his ships
and people there within days.

## History of Control and Significance

Botany Bay was a marginal location for sustained settlement, but its name carried
enormous symbolic weight. It had been first proposed as a convict destination by
**Sir Joseph Banks** before a House of Commons select committee in **1779**, on the
strength of his 1768–71 voyage with Cook. Banks recommended dispatching 200–300 convicts
with seeds, fishing equipment, and arms to fend for themselves. The idea was mooted again
in 1783, and gained decisive traction only after the survey vessel *Nautilus* returned in
July 1786 declaring the Das Voltas Bay (Namibia) alternative "doomed to everlasting
sterility." Just two weeks later the Pitt administration announced its intention to send
convicts to Botany Bay.

The announcement unleashed intense public debate — see [[botany-bay-decision|The Botany
Bay Decision controversy]]. What is clear from the chapter's evidence is that Botany Bay
was selected as the least-bad surviving option rather than as the product of a coherent
strategic plan, though strategic arguments were quickly attached to the choice.

## Strategic Significance Claimed and Actual

Contemporary newspaper correspondents argued Botany Bay was "roughly equidistant from
the Cape of Good Hope, Madras, Canton, the Moluccas and Batavia" and strategically
placed for Pacific and Indian Ocean commerce. Supporters cited potential naval stores
(flax and timber on nearby Norfolk Island), the China tea trade (transports could
proceed to Canton after unloading), and access to Pacific fur trade. When the Norfolk
Island pines "proved rotten at the core" and the flax impossible to work with European
methods, the naval stores rationale quietly collapsed. What the settlement did achieve
by the 1790s was a harbour where ships could be careened and resupplied — fulfilling at
least the logistical argument.

## Relationship to Aboriginal Land

Unlike the earlier Gambia and Das Voltas Bay schemes, the Botany Bay proposal included
no plans to purchase territory from indigenous inhabitants. Stuart Banner's work (cited
in CHA ch. 3) notes that this omission was foundational to the legal doctrine of
[[terra-nullius]] as applied in Australia.
