# Moscow
From Evtuhov (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II, ll. 9657–9717): Nizhnii Novgorod "follows a strictly Petersburgian layout" but "streets remained lined not with single buildings as in a European or American city, but with whole manors" (cf. Belinsky on mid-c. Moscow: "much as Belinsky had described Moscow at mid-century"). Nizhnii fair goods reached Moscow; provincial contrast to capitals. From Wirtschafter: Moscow University (1755) expanded raznochintsy to include non-noble students/gymnasia; centre of educated service classes and early "society". `analogous_to:` Nizhnii Novgorod provincial urban form; `produced:` early raznochintsy/intelligentsia milieus. See [[russian-empire]], [[westernization-of-russia]].

Moscow is the historic capital of Russia, emerging as the political center of the Russian lands during the Appanage period and serving as the capital of the Grand Duchy of Moscow, Tsardom of Russia, Soviet Union, and modern Russian Federation.

**Rise from NE Russia under Golden Horde (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. I, Perrie ed. 2006, Martin ch. ll. 3709–4698):** Attacked 1237–8 ("Rostov, another of the main cities of the region, as well as Tver’, Moscow and a series of other towns, were also listed among those subjected to direct attack"); depopulated by invasion + conscription for Sarai (ll. 3770–3783). Recovery made Moscow "dynamic political and economic centre" of north-eastern Russia 14th c.; masonry churches erected early 14th c. (Dormition, Archangel Michael + three others within decade); continued projects under Dmitrii/Vasilii (ll. 3823–3846, 4515–4518). Daniil’s sons tripled domain (Serpukhov, Kolomna, Mozhaisk: full Moskva River control + Oka Kolomna–Serpukhov); gained Vladimir throne via khan favour over traditional heirs; Ivan I used tribute collection + marriages for influence (ll. 4062–4065, 4223). Dmitrii built stone fortifications 1367; broad coalitions vs Tver’ (1375) and Mamai (Kulikovo 1380); expelled rivals; ended other branches’ claims to Vladimir; named heir (ll. 4472–4503). Vasilii I added Nizhnii Novgorod etc.; wealth from tribute fees (customs/transport kept) (ll. 4505–4511). Civil war resolution and Kasim khanate dependency (ll. 4678–4698). Church ties (Petr Dormition 1325 collaboration/burial/cult) (ll. 4177–4182). See [[golden-horde]], [[rurikid-dynasty]], [[novgorod]], [[vladimir]], [[dmitry-donskoy]], [[ivan-i-kalita]].

## Events and Significance (from Freeze (ed.) 2002, lines 600–3800)
Centre of Muscovite expansion and administration in 16th c.: Kremlin ensemble (Dormition Cathedral copied from Vladimir model, replicated elsewhere) symbolized power and conquests; "the centre-piece of the ensemble was the Dormition ( Uspenskii) Cathedral" (l2149–2151). Ivan III reconstructed churches/palaces post-1470s. Oprichnina targeted lucrative areas around Moscow (l1479). Time of Troubles: Polish occupation under Seven Boyars 1610–12; liberated Oct 1612 by Minin/Pozharskii second levy + Cossacks; "the ‘third levy’... liberated Moscow in October 1612" (l2535). **From Cambridge History of Russia Vol. I range 11001-15000:** Kremlin centre of Romanov gov (Dormition coronation of Dmitrii 1605). Tushino rival camp NW drew boyars 1608-9 ("migratory birds"). National militia mid-Aug 1612 routed Chodkiewicz; Kremlin surrender late Oct. Chancelleries in Kremlin/Kitai-gorod gave clerks power access. "On 20 June 1605 the pretender made a triumphal entry into Moscow... crowned in the Dormition cathedral in the Kremlin." "Pozharskii’s army arrived outside Moscow in mid-August, just in time to play a major part in the rout of Chodkiewicz’s Polish forces ... the occupiers of the Kremlin surrendered." (lines 11034, 11506-11512) "the prikazy were located in the Kremlin and Kitai gorod and their employees lived in the environs. This proximity gave them access to power that was unimaginable for the typical Russian." (lines 12278) `part_of:` [[romanov-dynasty]] administration. 1648 uprising: "major uprising in June 1648, which together with the fires that swept through Moscow, cost approximately two thousand lives" (l2715); led to Ulozhenie. "The initial impulse for the revolt in Moscow in 1648 was provided by the ordinary townspeople" (lines 17105–17106). "On 2 and 3 June the crowds, now joined by many of the strel’tsy... began to attack the homes and property of the most unpopular members of the ruling elite... Nazarii Chistyi... was lynched by the mob" (lines ~16903–16906). Copper uprising 1662: mobs to Kolomenskoe summer residence (l2736–2741). 1682 Streltsy massacre in Kremlin (l3155); Peter fled 1689 to Trinity-Sergius (l3265). Peter’s early play regiments at Preobrazhenskoe (near Moscow); 1698 Streltsy executions post-Grand Embassy (l3517). "Moscow remained the old capital and largest city, but after 1710 Peter visited it sparingly"; capital transferred to St Petersburg ~1713 (l3785–3787). 

**From Cambridge History of Russia Vol. I (Perrie ed. 2006, exclusive range 7501-11000, Shaw ch. on towns and commerce):** Richard Chancellor’s 1553 description: "It remaineth that a larger discourse be made of Moscow, the principal city of that country. – Our men say that in bigness it is as great as the city of London with the suburbs thereof. There are many and great buildings in it, but for beauty and fairness nothing comparable to ours. There are many towns and villages also, but built out of order and with no handsomeness: their streets and ways are not paved with stone as ours are, the walls of their houses are of wood, the roofs for the most part are covered with shingle boards." (l7699-7703). 

The urban network: "the Russian term for ‘town’ (gorod) meant little more than a fortified settlement." French estimates "at least 130 towns in the Russian network at the beginning of the sixteenth century"; Chechulin ~218 by end. "By far the most significant town founding in the period occurred as a consequence of the Russian occupation of the Volga valley... Samara (1586), Tsaritsyn (1588) and Saratov (1590). ... Ufa, and also Tiumen’ in western Siberia, followed by Tobol’sk a year later. Verkhotur’e was founded in the Urals in 1598... Tomsk nearby in 1604." (l7762-7809). 

Moscow size and role: "Moscow was, of course, the seat of the tsar and government with all the activities which these implied. It was also a major commercial and trading centre..." Compared to other cities "Novgorod... retained a significant role at least down to its sacking by Ivan IV’s oprichniki in 1570." Kazan' up to 15,000. Decline: "Eaton has estimated that the average number of urban taxpaying households per town declined from 231 to 151, or by 35 per cent, between about 1550 and the 1580s; in towns for which household data are available for both periods he calculates an overall decline of 61 per cent." Examples: Kolomna, Serpukhov, Murom. (l7819-7892).

Morphology and population: "The typical sixteenth-century Russian town had a fortified core, usually called the kremlin (kreml’) or gorod... Outside this was the commercial suburb or posad... Beyond the posad... other suburbs (fortified or not, and sometimes referred to by the term slobody)." "Members of the posad, and the land that they occupied, were designated ‘black’, meaning that such persons were liable to the tiaglo. But not all traders and craftspeople... were designated ‘black’. Others were ‘white’..." "Moscow had a large element of service people in its population." (l7915-7958).

Commerce: "the trading square (torg), usually located at a central and accessible point. In Moscow’s case this was to the east of the Kremlin by the Moscow River on the site of the present-day Red Square..." Specialised rows (riady): Surozhskii, shoe, ironmongery, cloth, etc. "Crafts and manufactures generally took place in the urban suburbs in the homes of the various artisans." (l8078-8100). Long-distance: English Muscovy Company arrival 1553 via White Sea; Dutch etc. "At first the trade involved a rather difficult transhipment and transit of goods to Kholmogory..." Northern route via Vologda and Iaroslavl’ to Moscow. Trade with Persia via Volga/Astrakhan flourished later 16th c. (l8200-8227).

Conclusion in source: "Sixteenth-century Russia and its towns underwent many vicissitudes. From apparent buoyancy in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries the towns, and commercial life in general, seem to have entered a more problematic phase" due to Livonian war, oprichnina and other calamities. (l8234-8239). 

From range also: Multifunctional nodes: "administrative centres, points of control... military and defensive nodes... commercial foci... handicraft and manufacturing activities... religious role. And not a few had intensive gardening and even agrarian functions." (l7903-7913).

Previous Cambridge references in page remain for 17th c. (Note different line ranges in volume.)

## Processes Here
- Rise of service landholding and enserfment policies centred on Moscow élite (pomest'e grants, forbidden years legislation).
- Church and state rituals (pilgrimages, processions) projecting unity from Kremlin.
- Westernizing experiments (theatre at Preobrazhenskoe) and backlash (Streltsy, Old Believers).

## Historiography Note
Kollmann stresses Moscow as minimalist centre tolerating regional autonomies while mobilizing resources; Torke/Alexander highlight its role in crises and Peter's pivot away from it. Collection coverage strong for Muscovite social/political history.
