# Senj

Dalmatian coastal town (modern Croatia) that became the base of the Uskoks, a community of refugees, soldiers, and raiders who conducted maritime and land warfare against the Ottomans in the name of Christendom. A key case study for Housley of religious warfare sustained by the *antemurale Christianitatis* ideology even in the absence of formal crusade institutions and privileges.

## Geographic and Strategic Significance

Situated on the Adriatic, Senj controlled narrow sea lanes and was difficult for Venice or the Ottomans to suppress. Its hinterland (the "Morlach" or Vlach frontier) supplied fighters. The town's economy depended heavily on booty from raids ("the need for booty to sustain Senj’s economy").

## Religious Warfare Dimension

Although the crusade was not formally preached for Senj's activities in the same way as major expeditions, the uskoks and their clerical supporters framed their struggle as holy war. Priests participated in campaigns; blood-brotherhood pacts and demonisation of the "Turk" reinforced the religious character. The *antemurale* image justified their position vis-à-vis both Ottoman enemies and Venetian commercial hostility.

## Historiography

C. W. Bracewell's study is foundational. Housley uses Senj to show how crusading ideas persisted in "unexpected ways" and could shape communities without papal bulls or indulgences. Highlights the pragmatic/ideological mix on the frontier.
