## Overview

The Newark Earthworks are the largest connected series of geometric earthworks in North America, built by [[hopewell-culture]] approximately 2,000 years ago (c. 100 BCE–400 CE). They originally spread across more than 12 km² (4.5 mi²) of a broad terrace along Raccoon Creek in the modern communities of Newark and Heath, Ohio.

## Description

Surviving remnants include:

- **The Great Circle**: a circular enclosure 366 m (1,200 ft) in diameter with an interior ditch
- **The Octagon Earthworks**: a circular enclosure 321 m (1,054 ft) in diameter connected by a short segment of parallel walls to an octagonal enclosure with walls approximately 168 m (550 ft) in length
- **Destroyed features** (documented before demolition): a perfectly square enclosure 284 m (931 ft) on a side; an oval embankment with a maximum diameter of 549 m (1,800 ft) enclosing 11 burial mounds; a network of parallel-walled avenues connecting all major enclosures to one another and to three surrounding streams

The 11 burial mounds within the oval embankment were leveled without archaeological investigation, though early accounts suggest they contained offerings similar to those recovered from Hopewell burial mounds in the Scioto Valley.

## Geometric and Astronomical Knowledge

Careful surveys have revealed sophisticated geometric relationships embedded in the design: the circumference of the Great Circle equals the perimeter of the large square enclosure; the area of the square enclosure equals the area of the large circle connected to the octagon. The Octagon Earthworks incorporate alignments to the 18.6-year cycle of maximum and minimum moonrises and moonsets. The parallel-walled avenues likely channeled pilgrims' movements through the earthworks in a prescribed ceremonial sequence.

## Significance

The Newark Earthworks exemplify the highest expression of Hopewell architectural ambition. Their construction required no special engineering beyond "sticks, cordage, and an understanding of basic geometry," according to Snow et al. (2020), but the geometric precision and astronomical alignment embedded across 12 km² of earthworks reveal sophisticated planning. The complex served as a ceremonial center — not a residence — for the dispersed Hopewell communities of central Ohio.
