## Geographic Description

Medina (before Islam called Yathrib) is an agricultural oasis "about two hundred miles north of Mecca" in the Hijaz. Unlike Mecca it had sufficient water to support date palm cultivation and settled agriculture. Its population in the early seventh century was ethnically mixed: several Arab pagan clans (including the Aws and the Khazraj) and a substantial community of Jewish clans (Banu Qaynuqa', Banu Nadir, Banu Qurayza, and others). The name *al-Madina* ("the city," par excellence) reflects its post-Hijra significance.

## Pre-Islamic Medina

Before Muhammad's arrival, Medina was torn by "bitter strife among its leading clans" — primarily the rivalry between the Arab Aws and Khazraj. The Jewish clans, economically significant as craftsmen and traders, navigated this conflict without controlling it. The oasis had extensive contact with monotheistic religion through the Jewish community; "Medina had come to recognize and no doubt to respect monotheistic religion" before Muhammad's arrival.

This internal dysfunction created the conditions for the Hijra: the Medinan factions sought "an external arbiter," someone unconnected to local rivalries who could impose peace. Delegations at the Meccan pilgrimage season contacted Muhammad; "in 622 six times as many were at hand to reinforce the pledge, promising Muhammad and his Meccan followers protection if they came to Medina."

## Medina as the First Islamic Polity (622–632)

After the [[the-hijra-622|Hijra]], Muhammad transformed Medina into the center of the first Muslim polity. The "constitution of Medina" established Muhammad as commander and arbiter over a diverse community including Meccan emigrants (Muhajirun), Medinan converts (Ansar), and initially the Jewish clans. The [[early-muslim-community]] took its institutional form here.

### The Jewish Clans and Their Expulsion

The Jewish population of Medina posed the most significant internal challenge to Muhammad's authority. Their presence had prepared the soil for monotheism but their rejection of Muhammad's specific prophetic claim was both theologically and politically threatening. Muhammad "had always hoped for the Jews to accept him" as the prophet anticipated in their own scriptures. When they did not:

- The **Banu Qaynuqa'** were expelled following Badr (624) — "in part an admission of defeat and a countermeasure" to the battle's consequences for Muslim prestige
- The **Banu Nadir** were expelled c. 625 after an alleged assassination plot
- The **Banu Qurayza** were massacred in 627 following the Battle of the Trench, having been accused of negotiating with the besieging Meccan forces

These expulsions concentrated power, eliminated internal opposition, and freed Medinan agriculture (partly held by Jewish landowners) for the Muslim community. They also produced Medina's shift from cosmopolitan oasis to exclusively Muslim city.

### Key Institutions Developed at Medina

- **Collective prayer (*salat*)**: formalized at Medina, "three times a day in unison, normally at the prayer ground (mosque)"
- ***Zakat***: formalized "as the financial basis of the community" — a wealth tax collected centrally and redistributed
- **Family law**: "perhaps at the heart of any social structure" — Medinan legislation regulated marriage, divorce, inheritance, the status of women
- ***Hajj***: Muhammad's Farewell Pilgrimage in 632 "establishing the forms of the pilgrimage which were to hold"
- **The *Friday sermon* (*khutba*)**: the preaching of the recognized Muslim ruler became the political act par excellence of caliphal authority

## Medina as Capital of the Rashidun Caliphate (632–656)

After Muhammad's death (632), the Saqifa assembly at Medina produced the acclamation of **Abu Bakr** as first caliph. Medina served as the administrative capital for the Rashidun caliphate through the reigns of Abu Bakr (632–634), 'Umar (634–644), and 'Uthman (644–656). The Companions of Muhammad (*Sahaba*) resident in Medina constituted the social core of the early Islamic elite; their consensus (*ijma'*) and example (*sunna*) gave Medina permanent religious prestige.

'Uthman's murder in Medina in 656 triggered the [[first-fitna-656]]. 'Ali initially ruled from Medina but moved his capital to Kufa, marking the effective end of Medina's political centrality. The Umayyad caliphate that emerged from the First Fitna was centered at Damascus.

## The Sack of Medina (683)

During the [[second-fitna-680]], Umayyad forces under Muslim ibn 'Uqbah took Medina by assault at the **Battle of the Harrah** (683), "taking Medina (under Muslim b. 'Uqbah, at Battle of the Harrah), besiege[ing] Mecca." This event — the violation of the Prophet's city by an Umayyad army — became a permanent grievance in Islamic historical memory, reinforcing the religious opposition to Umayyad rule.

## Why Medina Mattered

**Foundational:** The Medinan period (622–632) was the crucible in which Islamic law, community structure, ritual practice, and political institutions were formed. All subsequent Islamic jurisprudence looked to Medinan practice (*sunna*) as its primary normative standard after the Quran itself.

**Prestige:** As the city of the Prophet, of his Companions, and of the first caliphs, Medina had a claim to religious authority that no other city could rival. "The 'Umayyad' caliphate" operated on "the basis of the prestige of Muhammad's city and the consensus of Muhammad's companions," even from Damascus.

**Political:** The shift of the caliphal capital from Medina to Kufa (under 'Ali) and then to Damascus (under Mu'awiya) traced the political shift of power from the community of Companions to the military-tribal power of the conquest armies. The contrast between Medina (community legitimacy) and Damascus (imperial power) runs through early Islamic political thought.
