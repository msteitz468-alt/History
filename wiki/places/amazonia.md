# Amazonia

## Overview

**Amazonia** — the vast lowland forest and floodplain of the Amazon and Orinoco basins —
is, in [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Volume III]], the principal arena for a
major historiographical revision led by **Anna C. Roosevelt's** chapter on the "Maritime,
Highland, Forest Dynamic." Against the older "**counterfeit paradise**" view (Betty Meggers)
that tropical-forest soils could support only small, mobile, egalitarian bands, Roosevelt and
others marshal evidence — early pottery (Taperinha), large floodplain populations, **terra
preta (Amazonian Dark Earths)**, and ranked chiefdoms such as those of **Marajó Island** — for
substantial, sedentary, complex Amazonian societies.

The volume treats Amazonia across deep time (the forest pole of the continental "dynamic"),
in the [[senorios-naturales|chiefdoms]] chapter, and in the conquest-era chapters on the
western Amazon margins and on lowland Brazil, where the [[tupinamba|Tupinambá]] and others
faced Portuguese slaving and missionization.

## Significance

Amazonia is the key **counter-environment** to Andean-centric narratives: how much complexity
the tropical lowlands sustained, and how environmental determinism should be weighted, is one
of the central debates the volume stages. The post-contact demographic collapse of dense
floodplain populations also helps explain why early chroniclers (Carvajal on the Orellana
expedition) described populous riverbanks that later observers did not find.
