# Valley of Oaxaca

## Overview

The **Valley of Oaxaca** is a Y-shaped (three-pointed star) highland valley system —
~**2,000 km²** of alluvium, piedmont, and slopes at ~**1,550 m** asl, ~**550 mm** summer
rain — where the sierras madres converge south of Mexico City. Marcus and Flannery
(CHNPA II.1 Ch. 8): it seems to have been in this large, broad valley that
[[zapotec|Zapotec]] civilization began, later spreading to the northern sierras and
Tehuantepec coast. To the west lie the smaller valleys of [[mixtec|Mixtec]] civilization
(Mixteca Alta, Baja, Costa). Semi-arid conditions yield excellent archaeological
preservation — one of Mesoamerica’s best-documented sequences from Archaic foraging
through state-level urbanism and Postclassic kingdoms.

## Archaic foundations

Radiocarbon dates from stratified valley sites establish the beginning of the
Mesoamerican Archaic at **8750 b.c.** (Zeitlin & Zeitlin / Flannery tradition;
[[paleoindian-and-archaic-mesoamerica]]). **Guilá Naquitz** (near Mitla): Naquitz phase
8900–6700 b.c.; largest single Mesoamerican Archaic macrobotanical sample; early
domesticated *Cucurbita pepo* confirmed by AMS. **Gheo-Shih**: possible “dance ground”
macroband aggregation (5000–4000 b.c. cross-dated). Flannery’s broad-spectrum
readaptation and scheduling models were built largely on Oaxaca–Tehuacán data.

## Formative: San José Mogote to Monte Albán

Early Formative sedentary hamlets: oldest house **House 20 at
[[san-jose-mogote|San José Mogote]]** (~1900–1400 B.C.). Growth to ~40 hamlets/villages
by 1150–850 B.C. with SJM unmatched primacy; Middle Formative three-tier hierarchy and
irrigation (Neely terraced spring system; Xoxocotlán boulder dam). ~**500 B.C.**
founding of [[monte-alban|Monte Albán]] by valley-floor synoikism; demographic center of
gravity shifts to the mountaintop junction of the three arms.

Protoclassic four-tier hierarchy: Monte Albán capital; secondary centers (SJM 60–65 ha);
tertiary temple sites; **>400** base villages 1–3 ha. Subject territory outside the
valley (Cuicatlán and other Building J provinces) at 80–150 km radius.

## Classic and Postclassic

Classic peak: Monte Albán ~30,000; special relationship with [[teotihuacan]]; Zapotec
barrio at Teotihuacan. Breakdown A.D. 600–900; Jalieza surpasses Monte Albán; secondary
centers (Zaachila, [[mitla|Mitla]], Cuilapan, Macuilxóchitl, etc.) become regional head
towns. Late Postclassic: **balkanization**, Mixtec immigration and intermarriage in the
western valley, 2,455 sites (more than double Early Classic), Aztec campaigns
(Huitzo, Guiengola), Spanish entry 1520s–30s.

## Colonial–modern (Romero Frizzi, CHNPA II.2 Ch. 19)

The central valleys remain one of three principal flatland cores of Oaxaca (with the
Pacific Mixtec coast and the Isthmus of Tehuantepec) in a state that is only ~**9%**
arable. **Antequera** (Oaxaca City) was founded as a tiny Spanish island (~**80 vecinos**)
amid thousands of indigenous inhabitants — the clearest early sign of Spanish presence.
Valley [[mixtec|Mixtecs]] and [[zapotec|Zapotecs]] sought to use the newcomers; Spanish
rule consolidated here by the **1530s**. Cortés held valley villages; estate near Antequera
introduced wheat, silkworms, hydraulic mills. By **1540** Mixtecs and valley Zapotecs grew
wheat with plows and metal tips in the Valley of **Etla** (and Mixteca Alta); ~1540–1570
silk, livestock, and cochineal brought village wealth (*part_of:*
[[oaxaca-indigenous-colonial-modern]]).

Epidemic collapse (Oaxaca ~1.5 million → ~150,000 by 1650) hit tenant/serf populations of
even expanding valley lords. By ~1590 valley natives refused to obey lords; peasant villages
replaced kingdoms. Mid-eighteenth-century valley lords of **Etla** and **Cuilapan** still
held vast lands and tenant villages (Taylor); after **1725** no Mixteca/valley caciques as
rulers. Spanish bought valley land in the demographic nadir; by end of eighteenth century
**half** of commercial agricultural produce of the Oaxaca bishopric came from Spanish
properties — yet commerce (blankets, tallow, wool, cochineal) remained more important than
hacienda agriculture relative to central/northern New Spain. Spanish economy in Oaxaca was
weaker than those regions: indigenous lords still held much land; Indian herds often larger;
Antequera depended on native production and Central American cacao/indigo transit.

**Independence–Porfiriato:** 1832 agricultural/livestock production half of 1780 — worst in
Mixteca and valleys most integrated into the colonial market. Liberal disentailment milder
in valleys where privatization tendency already existed among Indians, but renters could buy
remaining parcels after distribution, aiding large valley haciendas. **1867 alone: 604 valley
disentailments.** Coffee unsuitable for valleys; colonial valley haciendas expanded under
Porfirian privatization. Revolution and aftermath: landlords formed armed units in valleys
(among other zones), murdering agrarian reformers; Cárdenas redistributions statewide (see
process page) still left ~90% of rural properties <5 ha.

Romero Frizzi does **not** treat [[monte-alban|Monte Albán]] as a colonial or modern
memory/ritual subject; the archaeological capital’s postconquest afterlife is outside Ch. 19’s
scope (link for prehispanic civilizational context only).

## Why it mattered

Dense settlement archaeology (Marcus, Flannery, Blanton, Kowalewski, Feinman, and
others) made Oaxaca the principal case for **independent** highland complexity
interacting with, rather than deriving from, the Gulf Coast [[olmec|Olmec]] — a
cornerstone of the “sister culture” reading of Formative Mesoamerica
([[olmec-mother-vs-sister-culture]]). It is also a laboratory for primary state
formation, early writing, and the contrast between centralized (Zapotec) and multipolar
(Mixtec) political evolution. Colonial–modern: Antequera as Spanish island within a still
indigenous mercantile landscape; valley wheat/silk boom; milder but real liberal
disentailment; and the shift from cacique domains to village/cofradía autonomy under weak
haciendas.

## Evans & Webster 2001 (Zeitlin, Flannery, others)

**Setting (Zeitlin, “Oaxaca and Tehuantepec Region”).** Valley ~1,500 m elevation,
Y-shaped, ~**1,200 km²** farmland (figures vary by alluvium vs full catchment
definition; CHNPA uses ~2,000 km² including piedmont/slopes); Atoyac and Salado rivers;
[[monte-alban|Monte Albán]] on peaks where arms intersect; irrigation for >1,500 years
expands farming beyond riverine zone under 300–1000 mm rainfall. Tehuantepec became a
Zapotec seat after late-15th-c. migration from the Valley under Mixtec pressure.

**Archaic and early ceramics.** Late Pleistocene occupation by ~10,000 B.C. at **Cueva
Blanca** near Mitla; valley sparsely populated (<150 people). **Guilá Naquitz** Naquitz
phase ~8900–6700 B.C. begins Archaic transition (possible early squash/bottle-gourd
experiments). By **Blanca** phase (~3300–2800 B.C.) domesticated maize widely cultivated;
Archaic ends ~2200 B.C. (Martínez). **Gheo-Shih** (Flannery): 1.5-ha open-air Archaic
site, 20 × 7 m cleared boulder-lined “dance ground,” probable 5000–3000 B.C. — ancestral
open-air encampment type for earliest villages. **Esperidión** ceramic complex at Tierras
Largas and [[san-jose-mogote|San José Mogote]] (1900–1400 B.C.) rivals Tehuacán’s Purrón
as among Mesoamerica’s earliest ceramics (Zeitlin).

**Cuicatlán and secondary centers.** ~200 B.C. Cañada de Cuicatlán fell to the
expansionist Zapotec state: La Coyotera burned and abandoned; Lomas phase ridge
resettlement with canal irrigation and skull rack; Quiotepec 45-ha fortified frontier
(Spencer & Redmond). **Dainzú** founded Rosario 600–400 B.C., flourished Monte Albán II
with ballplayer reliefs; ~5,000 in IIIa. **Fábrica San José** Middle Formative piedmont
village (Guadalupe–Rosario c. 850–450 B.C.) with ascribed status, salt production,
dependence on San José Mogote. **Etlatongo** (Mixteca Alta) major Middle Cruz growth to
26.2 ha with Olmec and Valley of Mexico exchange (Blomster). Isthmus: **Laguna Zope**
longest-lived Formative center (>90 ha), shell/export node; never a victim of Monte
Albán II imperialism though a goods source; abandoned c. A.D. 300, replaced by Saltillo;
Aguadas phase (A.D. 900–1300) highest pre-Hispanic density (Saltillo ~267 ha, ~6,000);
Ulam phase from ~1300: Zapotec invasion, Tehuantepec town ~**25,000** under Zaachila
royalty with fortified **Guiengola** (7-month Aztec siege failed per Burgoa).

**Late Postclassic valley.** Monte Albán V from ~A.D. 1300: ~**20** petty states
(Zaachila most important), population record **160,000**; Mixtec expansion; Mixteca-
Puebla polychrome alongside Zapotec graywares; Tomb 7 Mixtec reuse at Monte Albán;
Mixtec might blocked Aztec control of the Oaxaca–Tehuantepec–Soconusco route until
partial tributary status on the eve of conquest.
