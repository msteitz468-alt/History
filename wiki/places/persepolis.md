## Overview

Persepolis was the great ceremonial and administrative capital of the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid
Persian empire]], located in the homeland province of **Fars (Parsa)** in southwestern Iran,
on the eastern edge of the Marv Dasht plain ~47 km northeast of modern Shiraz. "Persepolis"
("city of Persis") is the Greek name; the Persians called it **Parsa**, the same name as the
homeland itself. It was founded by **[[darius-i|Darius I]]** (522–486) as a new royal seat to
replace [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus's]] **Pasargadae** 40 km to the north, and remained the empire's
pre-eminent ceremonial centre — the place of coronations, royal burials, and the great festivals
— for the rest of the empire's life (Bryce 2009: 538–42).

## The site

Excavated by the University of Chicago (1931–39), then by the Iranian Archaeological Service
and an Italian restoration team (1964–78), Persepolis centres on a vast stone **terrace** (the
*Takht*, "throne"), some 455 × 300 m, carrying a series of monumental royal buildings raised by
Darius and his successors **Xerxes, Artaxerxes I, and Artaxerxes III**. Entry was through the
**All Nations Gate** (built/completed by Xerxes), guarded by colossal stone bulls and winged,
human-headed bulls and reached by a monumental double staircase. The complex's signature
structures are the columned **Apadana** (the great Audience Hall), the **Hall of 100 Columns**
(probably the throne-room), and the palaces of Darius and Xerxes. Reliefs on the Apadana's
north and east stairways depict **files of Median and Persian nobles and tribute-bearers from
twenty-three regions** of the empire — the most famous visual statement of Achaemenid imperial
ideology. Craftsmen pressed into service "from as far afield as [[lydia|Lydia]]" worked the
stone.

## The Fortification Tablets

Persepolis's greatest documentary treasure is the **Persepolis Fortification Tablets** —
some 15,000–20,000 administrative tablets found in the city's fortifications, dating from the
13th to 28th years of Darius (509–494). They are **the earliest administrative documents of
the Persian empire**, recording food distributions from the imperial stores around the capital
to the royal family, priests, travellers, and workers, and donations to the gods. A smaller
**Treasury** archive (139 tablets) records payments to workers. Together they make Persepolis a
key source for the empire's economy and administration, complementing the royal inscriptions.

## Destruction

Persepolis was **looted and burned by [[alexander-the-great|Alexander the Great]] in 330 BCE**.
Diodorus (17.70–71) — reporting Alexander's description of it as "the most hateful of the cities
of Asia" — claims 120,000 talents of gold and silver were carried off from the Treasury, hauled
to [[susa|Susa]] by 3,000 camels and a vast train of mules. The surviving finds (military
equipment, seals, gold jewellery, silver and lapis statuettes) represent only a fraction of the
city's wealth at the moment of its destruction.
