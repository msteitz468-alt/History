# Casas Grandes (Paquimé)

## Overview

**Paquimé** (Casas Grandes) in northwestern Chihuahua was the great center of a
Mediaeval-period interaction system linking [[greater-mesoamerica|Greater Mesoamerica]]
to the U.S. Southwest — multi-story adobe architecture, long-distance exchange (macaws,
shell, copper, turquoise), specialized craft production, and a hierarchical hinterland
of irrigation communities, fortresses, and communication towers. Gorenstein (CHNPA II.1
Ch. 7) summarizes Charles Di Peso’s periodization and the dual research threads on
material/ideological distribution vs formal trade models for the Southwest–Mesoamerican
long-distance role.

## Viejo and Medio periods (Di Peso)

**Viejo** (Classic into start of Postclassic): early houses-in-pits → pit houses →
semisubterranean; northern ceramics (Anasazi, Mogollon, Ootam) appear; start of
Postclassic brings contiguous rectangular surface rooms around plazas and southern
material culture (ceramics, copper, pyrite plaques).

**Medio:** construction of the great settlement of **Paquimé** — multi-storied
buildings, revamped water system, effigy mounds, ballcourts, marketplace, plazas.
Production center for a hinterland of hundreds or even thousands of smaller settlements
in valleys and mountains, perhaps covering an area of **~85,000 square miles**, with
irrigation, fortresses, and communication towers (hierarchical organization).

## Collapse

After a short heyday, public decline (residences in public areas, water-system decay)
while artisanship and market activity continued; then **burning and destruction**.
Ultimately not only the center was abandoned but also many villages in its sphere;
artisans scatter to villages or migrate north into the U.S. Southwest.

## Regional debates

In Sonora/northern Sinaloa, the **Rio Sonora** tradition’s Late-phase “statelets” are
disputed: indigenous development of 3–4 tier settlement hierarchies with economic
regional centers (Doolittle) vs products of the Casas Grandes economic system (Pailes).
By Protohistoric times, active Southwest–northern Mexico trade (Riley). **Trincheras**
(NW Sonora) shows little interchange with Rio Sonora.

## Trade routes

Paquimé sat at the northern end of the **inland corridor**: La Quemada →
[[chalchihuites|Chalchihuites]] → Schroeder → Zape → Casas Grandes → Southwest. A
**coastal route** (Central Mexico via Lerma–Chapala → Ixtlán → Amapa → Chametla–
Culiacán–Guasave) could also turn east across the Sierra Madre to Casas Grandes. Late
Postclassic [[purepecha|Tarascan]] takeover of routes reoriented some of this traffic;
Sonoran statelets continued parrot, macaw, feather, coral, shell, and metal exchange.

## Why it mattered

Paquimé is the northwestern proof that complex, hierarchical, multi-story urbanism and
long-distance production economies flourished well outside Central Mexico — a core
empirical plank of Gorenstein’s Greater Mesoamerica thesis.

## Cordell & Smith (CHNPA I.1 ch. 4) — borderland view from the Southwest

Through most of its history the Casas Grandes region **resembled Mogollon**. In the
**fourteenth century** much of the population was organized into one very large site —
the “City of Paquimé”: planned multistory adobe great houses, ball courts,
animal-effigy/stone-faced platform mounds, plazas with macaw breeding, wells, drainage,
workshops for copper ornaments/axes/bells, marine shell, turquoise. Reevaluation suggests
an important regional center but influence differs from Di Peso’s original description;
craft/parrot production largely **local or southward**. Large northern AZ/NM
communities/alliances **1270–1350** (occasionally to 1400) with trade in White Mountain red
wares, Jeddito yellow ware, obsidians, turquoise, shell — whether hierarchical elites is
**debated**. Paquimé was **burned and abandoned** sometime in the late 1400s.
*part_of:* earlier [[mogollon]] resemblance; *concurrent_with:* [[ancestral-pueblo]]
northern alliances; *part_of:* late [[southwest-agricultural-development]].

Fourteenth–fifteenth centuries (pre-European contact) witnessed the regionally organized
system around Paquimé alongside complex Mississippian chiefdoms in the East (ch. 5) and
some form of alliance network among large Anasazi/Mogollon villages.
