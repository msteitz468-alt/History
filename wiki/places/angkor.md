# Angkor and Southeast Asian Urbanism (Angkor Borei to Angkor Thom)

## Overview

This page covers **early Southeast Asian urbanism** through the long Khmer
trajectory of the lower Mekong Basin — from the early moated city of **Angkor
Borei** (~500 BCE–500 CE) to the imperial capital of **Angkor / Angkor Thom**
(802–1431 CE) — following [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] Ch. 4 (Miriam
Stark), "From early city to classical state." Southeast Asia is the volume's case
of urbanism "from early city to classical state," in which polity, power, and
city were fused through **ritual performance**.

## The moated-city form

The region's first cities (Iron Age / Early Historic, ~500 BCE–500 CE) were
**nucleated, walled, and moated** settlements, the moats linked to drainage and
giving dry-season water and defense:
- **Angkor Borei** (southern Cambodia, ~300 ha) — moat-and-wall complex, brick
  ritual monuments, a central elite core, linked via canals to **Oc Eo** and the
  South China Sea trade network. The polity the Chinese called **Funan**.
- **Co Loa** (northern Vietnam, ~600 ha) — ~200× the area of preceding Bronze Age
  settlements.

A recurring **concentric / modular** plan (Kulke): a ritual epicenter with
hydraulic features, an inner elite core (the ruler's residence), a residential
halo, and surrounding agrarian lands dotted with shrines — blending Indic
("Sanskrit cosmopolitan") cosmology with an indigenous animist substrate.

## From Chenla to Angkor

Power moved northward (Chenla, "temple-cities" like Sambor Prei Kuk) to the Tonle
Sap, where the Angkorian capital was founded in **802 CE**. Each new Khmer ruler
built a **new capital** anchored by a central temple-mountain memorializing a
royal ancestor. **Jayavarman VII** (late 12th–early 13th c.) built **Angkor
Thom** — a 9 km² gridded city (the Bayon at its center) following Indian
cosmological precepts — and knit the empire together with roads, hospitals,
rest-houses, and bridges. Angkor was a vast low-density **"conurbation,"** the
largest preindustrial settlement complex known.

## Performance and power

Khmer rulership ran on **public performance**: consecrations, annual festivals,
and grand processions (described by the Chinese envoy Zhou Daguan in 1296),
using long causeways and "avenues of approach." Stark argues — *against*
Clifford Geertz's "theater state" (*Negara*, on 19th-c. Bali), which minimized
royal power — that in the Khmer case performance carried **genuine political
power** alongside economic and military acumen. See [[ritual-and-urban-performance]].

## The abandonment of Angkor (CWH V)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Radkau, Ch 2) treats Angkor — abandoned as a capital
c. 1431–1440 — as a leading case in the
[[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism|collapse-vs-resilience debate]].
The environmental reading holds that Angkor's vast hydraulic network was
intrinsically **fragile** under local conditions (clay-rich soils, low gradient,
tropical monsoon), so that a weakening of the political authority maintaining it
triggered a chain reaction — silting, river-course shifts, system failure. Radkau,
however, cautions that the collapse "was not tantamount to a decline of nature": the
forest that swallowed the ruins suggests a city undone by an *overpowering* nature
reclaiming it rather than a destroyed one, and the hinterland population may have
"led a more pleasant life" once freed from the centralized apparatus (James C.
Scott, *The Art of Not Being Governed*). The fourteenth/fifteenth-century shift of
Khmer power toward the maritime trade-oriented south (Phnom Penh) is also part of
the story. See [[environmental-history-middle-millennium]].

## The monuments and the hydraulic city (Coe)

Michael D. Coe's [[coe-angkor-khmer-civilization-2018|*Angkor and the Khmer Civilization*]] (2003)
fills in the monumental and dynastic detail. Recent **LiDAR** survey (Damian Evans) confirms Greater
Angkor as a low-density urban sprawl of ~**1,000 km²**, organised around colossal **barays** (the
East, West, and Jayatataka reservoirs) fed by the monsoon and the Tonle Sap — the hydraulic basis of
its wet-rice economy. The supreme monument, **Angkor Wat**, was built by **Suryavarman II** (early
12th c.) for **Vishnu**: a five-towered temple-mountain in concentric galleries and moat, **facing
west** (which led Cœdès to read it as a royal **funerary temple**), its Third Gallery carrying the
masterpiece bas-reliefs — the **Churning of the Sea of Milk**, the epics' battles, and the procession
of Suryavarman II — plus ~1,900 *apsaras*; Eleanor Mannikka has argued (controversially) that its
dimensions encode a **solar-cosmological** scheme. After the **Cham sack of 1177**,
**[[jayavarman-vii|Jayavarman VII]]** rebuilt the capital as **Angkor Thom**, its state temple the
**Bayon** crowned with serene **face-towers** of the bodhisattva **Avalokiteshvara** (likely the
king's own face), and added Ta Prohm, the Elephant Terrace, and Neak Pean. The earlier jewel
**Banteay Srei** and the eyewitness account of the Chinese envoy **Zhou Daguan** (1296–97) round out
Coe's portrait of the living city. Angkor Wat, a working Buddhist temple since the 15th century, is
today the **national symbol of Cambodia**.

## Why this place mattered

Southeast Asian urbanism was deep-rooted and indigenous (not a mere Indian or
Chinese import), sustaining cities of 50,000–100,000 into the 16th century before
European colonialism reversed the trend. Angkor shows the city as the centripetal
ritual-political core of the state. Insular Southeast Asia (Srivijaya, Majapahit)
urbanized later and in trade-based maritime forms; mainland Classical capitals
(Pagan, Sukhothai, Ayutthaya) shared the modular ceremonial template.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

**Scale and uniqueness.** Angkor’s core area (temples, fields, water features, artifact scatters) covers nearly **4,000 km²**. The Angkor Empire was unique in Seasian history for keeping its center in one place **>500 years**, except Jayavarman IV’s 10th-c. move to **Koh Ker** (922–944; back 945). “Angkor” is the Khmer pronunciation of *nagara* (“capital/palace”). Yasovarman (from 889) built capital **Yasodharapura** ~20 km west of Roluos around Phnom Bakheng (with Phnom Krom and Phnom Bok in a straight line; largest linga in Cambodia on Phnom Bok); eastern baray with Nagari Sanskrit corner steles similar to Java a century earlier. Preclassic/Protoclassic occupation: grave under West Baray; Lovea and other ramparted mounds north of Angkor (Protoclassic burials under Angkor-period Chinese porcelain); Ko Ta Meas circular site ~2870 BP. Ak Yom inscriptions 600–1001, partly buried when West Baray built (central shaft 12 m).

**Monuments and dynastic sequence (selected).** Harsavarman I (~900–922): Baksei Chamkrong, Prasat Kravanh. Jayavarman IV at Koh Ker: closer to eastern iron; may be earliest SEA ruler claiming to **be** a god (*Tribhuvaneśvara*) rather than merely divinely inspired. Rajendravarman returned to Yasodharapura; East Mebon 952; **Banteay Srei** 967 (Yajnavaraha)—“most beautiful building in Classic Southeast Asia”; first Cambodian lintels to depict Hindu legends. West Baray (~8 × 2 km) finished early 11th c. (Suryavarman I or Udayadityavarman II); West Mebon probably Udayadityavarman II; **Baphuon** last Śaiva terraced temple. Angkor Wat (Nagaravatta) built **1122–1150** under Suryavarman II, probably inspired by Phimai; [CONTRADICTION] still often called his funerary monument—authors reject funerary-ash theory (ashes in deposit boxes are consecration deposits). [[jayavarman-vii]]: Angkor Thom wall 3 km × 8 m high, moat 100 m; palace 15 ha NW; Bayon at geometric center as Meru (~1,200 m bas-reliefs, nearly twice Angkor Wat); Preah Khan from temporary palace 1191; Ta Prohm 1186. Lidar 2012: hundreds of house mounds in grid pattern across Angkor Thom, Angkor Wat, Preah Khan interiors—highly centralized/regimented society. Zhao Rugua (1225): no “city,” only one stone building for the king; commoners and nobles in thatch/bamboo. Zhou Daguan: Phimeanakas “tower of gold”; king must sleep with naga queen (legitimacy trope also in Malay/Javanese legend).

**Hydraulics and population.** Simple irrigation-reservoir theory for baray **discarded** (too shallow; no downhill outlets); purely symbolic theory also without evidence; transport/canal theory for Kulen stone; Greater Angkor Project consensus: complex **flood/drought management** stabilizing water table with some irrigation without sluices. Field systems: small fields 0.4–0.5 ha, square grids aligned with temples/ponds—centralized land management. Population estimates for the “extended boundary city” **350,000–1,000,000**; low-density-city inclusion would add 600,000–700,000 to Middle Classic Seasian urban totals. Chinese porcelain at Angkor mainly elite areas (huge nobility–commoner gulf vs wide insular distribution). First glazed ceramics in SEA beyond Chinese control under Indravarman and Yasovarman (880s–940s). No money per Zhou; barter in cattle, cloth, rice, even spittoons.

**After the capital.** By 1420s weakened by [[ayutthaya]] and [[champa]]; conquered 1432. Activity continued (e.g. Kok Phnouv 15th-c. Thai/Vietnamese/Chinese ceramics). Fifteenth-century Cambodia transformed toward Protoclassic [[funan]] riverine trade at Phnom Penh rather than total collapse. [[borobudur]] terraced-pyramid model adapted at Roluos and Angkor; Prambanan Rāmāyaṇa reliefs inspired Angkor Wat and Bayon 250–300 years later.

*Source: John N. Miksic & Goh Geok Yian, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (Routledge, 2017); [[khmer-empire]]; [[jayavarman-vii]]; [[seasian-classic-periodization]].*

## Fagan & Durrani (2020): dynastic milestones and the climate-whiplash collapse

Fagan & Durrani (2020) supply a compact dynastic spine consistent with Coe and
Miksic & Goh above: **Jayavarman II** founds the unified Angkor state in **802
CE**; **Suryavarman II** builds **Angkor Wat** from **1113 CE**; **Jayavarman
VII** builds **Angkor Thom** from **1181 CE**, an inscription recording
**306,372 people serving one temple** from 13,500 villages, consuming 38,000
tons of rice annually (matching the Preah Khan donation figure already cited
on [[khmer-empire]]).

They date the Angkor **collapse to c. 1430–1431 CE**, attributing it to a
combination of **Thai military pressure** and a **climate "whiplash"**: a
prolonged 1300s drought followed by catastrophic monsoon flooding that
overwhelmed and eroded the hydraulic system — a sequence supported by
**tree-ring evidence** from Thailand and Vietnam. This adds a specific
dendrochronological data point to the environmental reading already discussed
above (CWH V/Radkau; the Greater Angkor Project consensus via Miksic & Goh).

Source: Fagan & Durrani (2020), *World Prehistory: A Brief Introduction* (10th ed.).
