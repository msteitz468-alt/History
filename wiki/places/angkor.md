# Angkor and Southeast Asian Urbanism (Angkor Borei to Angkor Thom)

## Overview

This page covers **early Southeast Asian urbanism** through the long Khmer
trajectory of the lower Mekong Basin — from the early moated city of **Angkor
Borei** (~500 BCE–500 CE) to the imperial capital of **Angkor / Angkor Thom**
(802–1431 CE) — following [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] Ch. 4 (Miriam
Stark), "From early city to classical state." Southeast Asia is the volume's case
of urbanism "from early city to classical state," in which polity, power, and
city were fused through **ritual performance**.

## The moated-city form

The region's first cities (Iron Age / Early Historic, ~500 BCE–500 CE) were
**nucleated, walled, and moated** settlements, the moats linked to drainage and
giving dry-season water and defense:
- **Angkor Borei** (southern Cambodia, ~300 ha) — moat-and-wall complex, brick
  ritual monuments, a central elite core, linked via canals to **Oc Eo** and the
  South China Sea trade network. The polity the Chinese called **Funan**.
- **Co Loa** (northern Vietnam, ~600 ha) — ~200× the area of preceding Bronze Age
  settlements.

A recurring **concentric / modular** plan (Kulke): a ritual epicenter with
hydraulic features, an inner elite core (the ruler's residence), a residential
halo, and surrounding agrarian lands dotted with shrines — blending Indic
("Sanskrit cosmopolitan") cosmology with an indigenous animist substrate.

## From Chenla to Angkor

Power moved northward (Chenla, "temple-cities" like Sambor Prei Kuk) to the Tonle
Sap, where the Angkorian capital was founded in **802 CE**. Each new Khmer ruler
built a **new capital** anchored by a central temple-mountain memorializing a
royal ancestor. **Jayavarman VII** (late 12th–early 13th c.) built **Angkor
Thom** — a 9 km² gridded city (the Bayon at its center) following Indian
cosmological precepts — and knit the empire together with roads, hospitals,
rest-houses, and bridges. Angkor was a vast low-density **"conurbation,"** the
largest preindustrial settlement complex known.

## Performance and power

Khmer rulership ran on **public performance**: consecrations, annual festivals,
and grand processions (described by the Chinese envoy Zhou Daguan in 1296),
using long causeways and "avenues of approach." Stark argues — *against*
Clifford Geertz's "theater state" (*Negara*, on 19th-c. Bali), which minimized
royal power — that in the Khmer case performance carried **genuine political
power** alongside economic and military acumen. See [[ritual-and-urban-performance]].

## The abandonment of Angkor (CWH V)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Radkau, Ch 2) treats Angkor — abandoned as a capital
c. 1431–1440 — as a leading case in the
[[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism|collapse-vs-resilience debate]].
The environmental reading holds that Angkor's vast hydraulic network was
intrinsically **fragile** under local conditions (clay-rich soils, low gradient,
tropical monsoon), so that a weakening of the political authority maintaining it
triggered a chain reaction — silting, river-course shifts, system failure. Radkau,
however, cautions that the collapse "was not tantamount to a decline of nature": the
forest that swallowed the ruins suggests a city undone by an *overpowering* nature
reclaiming it rather than a destroyed one, and the hinterland population may have
"led a more pleasant life" once freed from the centralized apparatus (James C.
Scott, *The Art of Not Being Governed*). The fourteenth/fifteenth-century shift of
Khmer power toward the maritime trade-oriented south (Phnom Penh) is also part of
the story. See [[environmental-history-middle-millennium]].

## Why this place mattered

Southeast Asian urbanism was deep-rooted and indigenous (not a mere Indian or
Chinese import), sustaining cities of 50,000–100,000 into the 16th century before
European colonialism reversed the trend. Angkor shows the city as the centripetal
ritual-political core of the state. Insular Southeast Asia (Srivijaya, Majapahit)
urbanized later and in trade-based maritime forms; mainland Classical capitals
(Pagan, Sukhothai, Ayutthaya) shared the modular ceremonial template.
