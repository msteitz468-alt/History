# Ternate and the Northern Moluccas

In the early modern period "the Moluccas" (Maluku) denoted what is now the
**Northern Moluccas** — the island of Halmahera and its surrounding islands, home
to four sultanates: **Ternate, Tidore, Gilolo, and Bacan**. Ternate and Tidore,
"two states in constant rivalry," projected power far beyond their tiny home
islands; large areas of the Moluccas and parts of Sulawesi were at some point their
vassals. Ternate was "the original habitat of the clove tree," and control of the
clove trade is what drew successive European powers — and made Ternate, with
[[makassar]], a central arena of the seventeenth-century spice wars described in
[[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]].

## The Portuguese Century (1512–1575)

A group of Portuguese shipwrecked near the Moluccas in **1512**; the sultan of
Ternate invited them, "hoping that the Portuguese would be an asset in Ternate's
conflicts with Tidore." A Portuguese fort was completed on Ternate in **1523**;
Ternatan-Portuguese forces sacked the Tidoran capital Mareku in 1524, and the clove
trade "accelerated the state-formation process" (Leonard Andaya). Relations soured
across the sixteenth century as Portuguese governors took the sultan hostage and
executed officials. After the Portuguese killed **Sultan Hairun** in 1570, his son
**Babullah** united a Muslim coalition, starved the fort into surrender, and in
**1575** evicted the Portuguese to Ambon — ending their presence on Ternate and
establishing Ternate as the most powerful of the Moluccan sultanates.

Francis Drake visited in **1579** at Ternatan invitation, finding a cosmopolitan
court but only two unserviceable cannon. By the time of the **1585** Spanish attack
from Manila, the fort had been heavily reinforced and "some 20 Turkish gunners
participated in the defence, using bombs and grenades," forcing the Spanish to break
off — a vivid instance of Asian military adaptation (see
[[european-exceptionalism-early-modern-asia]]).

## Spanish Conquest and the VOC Alliance (1606–1607)

In **1606** Pedro de Acuña brought a fleet of five large ships, dozens of smaller
vessels, and over 1,400 Iberian troops from the Philippines, conquered the old
Portuguese fort, and left a 600-man garrison; Tidore allied with Spain. The
following year the [[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] admiral **Cornelis Matelief de
Jonge** could not take the Spanish fort but built **Fort Oranje at Melayu** on
Ternate's east coast and concluded a **treaty (1607)**: Sultan Muzaffar recognised
the Dutch as his "protector" in exchange for a VOC monopoly on Ternatan cloves. The
royal family rebuilt its capital around the Dutch fort.

## Ternate as VOC Proxy

The alliance pulled the VOC into Ternate's pre-existing web of vassalages and
rivalries (Hoamoal on Seram, Buru, Buton, areas of Sulawesi, Selayar), enforced
through Ternatan stewards (*kimelaha*). It also let the company, a republic lacking
royal standing, conduct **"diplomacy by proxy"** — pressing claims and treaties in
the sultan's name, including its abortive 1616 overture to Makassar. A 1613 VOC
treaty with Buton named "the king of Makassar" as the main threat the company would
protect against, marking the collision of the Ternatan and Gowa-Tallo spheres.
