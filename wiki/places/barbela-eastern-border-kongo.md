# Barbela (Hydronym) and the Eastern Border of the Kongo Kingdom

**Barbela** (also written *Berbela*) is a hydronym appearing in numerous sixteenth- and seventeenth-century maps and chronicles of the [[kingdom-of-kongo]] and used to designate what was described as the kingdom's eastern border. Its correct identification has significant consequences for understanding the spatial extent and internal organization of the Kongo kingdom. Igor Matonda (ch. 5 in [[bostoen-brinkman-kongo-kingdom-2018]]) argues that Barbela represents the present-day **Kwango River**, not the **Inkisi River** as most twentieth-century scholarship has assumed.

## The Historiographical Problem

The dominant twentieth-century interpretation, established by Simar (1919) and reinforced by Van Wing (1921), Plancquaert (1932), Hilton (1985), and others, identified the Barbela with the Inkisi River — one of the most important left-bank tributaries of the lower Congo River, originating in Angola's Uíge province. On this reading, the Inkisi formed the kingdom's eastern boundary at the time of first European contact (late fifteenth century), with territories east of it representing later annexations from around 1600 onward.

Hilton (1985:34) captured the consensus: "the eastern capitals, Mbanzas Nsundi, Mpangu, and Mbata were all located in the fertile Nkisi valley near the eastern frontier of the kingdom." Vansina (1999) placed the eastern border slightly east of the Inkisi. Thornton (1982:335; 2001:112, 116) proposed that the addition of the "Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza" to royal titles under Álvaro I (1583) marked the incorporation of the entire zone between the Inkisi and Kwango Rivers — implying these territories were *not* part of the original kingdom.

This interpretation materially influenced fieldwork: the KongoKing project's archaeological strategy initially focused excavations west of the Inkisi, expecting all significant *mbanza* (political capitals) to lie in that zone.

## Matonda's Relocation Argument

Matonda (ch. 5) advances two primary arguments for relocating Barbela to the Kwango:

### Argument 1: Later Cartographic Traditions Replace "Barbela" with "Coango/Cuango"

The key source introducing the Barbela is Filippo Pigafetta (1591), who never visited the Kongo kingdom; his entire account derives from the testimony of Portuguese trader Duarte Lopes. Pigafetta's map (produced by the brothers Theodore and Israël de Bry, 1591) initiated what Matonda calls the "Pigafetta cartographic tradition," which continued until 1792.

In 1640, Johan Johannes Blaeu's map of Africa initiated a third cartographic tradition that departed from Pigafetta's by renaming the easternmost river: Barbela is replaced by **Coango/Cuango** (the Kwango). In some maps of this tradition the upper course is still called Barbela while the lower is Cuango; in others Barbela appears as a tributary flowing into Cuango from the west. This partial overlap in naming across the two traditions directly corroborates the identification: the river that Pigafetta called Barbela is the river that later cartographers correctly named the Kwango.

### Argument 2: The Town of Cundi

On most maps of the Pigafetta tradition, a small settlement appears between the towns of Sunde (= Mbanza Nsundi) and the Barbela River. On seventeenth-century maps — particularly Joannes Janssonius (1650) and Jacob van Meurs (1668) — this town is named **Cundi**. According to Jadin (1968:433), Cundi was governed by the princes of Mbata. Crucially, Cundi was historically known as a major market for raffia cloths (*panos Cundi*) on the **left bank of the Kwango River**, attested as late as 1885 when visited by German explorer Büttner. A settlement named Kundi exists today on the Kwango's right bank.

The presence of Cundi on maps between Sunde/Nsundi and the Barbela — with Cundi historically situated on the Kwango — indicates that the Barbela depicted on those maps is the Kwango, not the Inkisi. On stripped-down versions of Pigafetta's map (e.g., Mercator's *Congi Regnu*, c. 1630; Sanson maps of 1677 and 1695), the anonymous town of Cundi was deleted, leaving Sunde apparently adjacent to the Barbela and misleading later scholars into conflating the Barbela with the nearest river to Mbanza Nsundi (the Inkisi).

### Supporting Evidence

Matonda marshals additional evidence:

**Pigafetta's own text:** Pigafetta (1591:17) locates the eastern border junction at the confluence of the Vumba River with the Zaire, with Barbela lying further east. The Vumba has been identified as the present-day **Wamba River**, a tributary of the Kwango. It is geographically impossible for the eastern boundary to have been simultaneously at the confluence of the Wamba/Kwango and along the Inkisi, which lies much further west.

**Lake Aquilunda:** Pigafetta states that the Barbela "issues out of the first lake" (Aquilunda/Achelunda). Miller (1973:140) identifies this lake as a small lake in present-day Songo territory in Angola, near the sources of the Kwango — not near the Inkisi's sources in the Zombo area (where South Kikongo varieties are spoken, not the Umbundu-influenced language of the southern highlands).

**The Inkisi's actual names:** Missionaries living in the Kongo kingdom who crossed the Inkisi repeatedly never called it Barbela. They used: *Nzari a luelo*, *Zaire de Bata*, *Zaire piccolo/pequeño*, *Singa*, and *Singuam* (various sources cited in Matonda 2018:133). The Inkisi had no shortage of names; Barbela was simply not among them.

**The Mbata problem:** A second passage in Pigafetta (1591:36) describes the province of Mbata as bounded such that "on the east it crosses the River Barbela." If Barbela were the Inkisi, this would place Mbata extending east of its supposed eastern frontier — contradicting the border-province interpretation. If Barbela is the Kwango, this simply means Mbata extended toward the Kwango, consistent with Kongo heartland geography.

**Kongo dia Nlaza disambiguation:** Thornton (2001:112) conflated "Kongo dia Nlaza" with the "Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza" (*Momboares* in Cardoso's spelling, = Kikongo *mumbwadi* 'people of the seven'). Matonda (2017:183–237) argues these are distinct entities: the Seven Kingdoms region (*Momboares*) comprised Lula, Kongo dia Nlaza, Kundi, Okanga, Nsundi, Mbata, and Mpangu — straddling the Inkisi, which ran through its centre. The Inkisi was never this region's boundary; it was its spine. The titles of Álvaro I did not mark new annexation but described an existing constituent region.

## Consequence for Understanding Kongo's Spatial Organization

Relocating the Barbela to the Kwango has significant implications:

1. **The Inkisi valley as heartland, not periphery.** The northern provinces of Nsundi, Mbata, and Mpangu — whose *mbanza* lie in the Inkisi basin — were in the kingdom's interior, not at its edge. Their location was a function of the desire to control the valley's resources and trade routes, not evidence of peripheral status.

2. **The Inkisi–Kwango zone as the kingdom's eastern extension.** The region between these two rivers — the *Momboares* or Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza — was an integral part of Kongo since the earliest European contacts, long before the sixteenth century. This zone "deserves more attention than it has received until present" (Matonda 2018:139) for understanding the kingdom's genesis.

3. **Archaeological implications.** The KongoKing project's initial decision not to excavate east of the Inkisi was premised on the erroneous eastern-border hypothesis. Searching for historical Kongo settlements and archaeological traces beyond the Inkisi "should not be a vain enterprise" (Matonda 2018:139).

## The Concept of Border in Pre-Colonial Africa

Matonda frames the geographic argument within a broader historiographical discussion of pre-colonial African borders. Against the view that borders were a colonial invention or entirely absent in pre-colonial Africa, he assembles three types of evidence:

- **Linguistic:** The Proto-Bantu root ***-dìdò* 'boundary' (reconstructed) has reflexes across all major Bantu branches, attested in all subgroups of the Kikongo Language Cluster including in Van Gheel's 1652 Kikongo dictionary (*múrilú* 'limitatio; margo; terminus'). The concept of border is at minimum 4,000–5,000 years old in Bantu-speaking Central Africa.
- **Documentary:** Missionaries in Kongo reported border awareness explicitly — a 1585 account shows Kongo king Álvaro I ordering prevention of missionary border-crossing at the Congo River; Marcellino d'Atri (1697) identified the village of Matuntolo as the last in Nsundi province, "functioning as a border with the province of Mpangu."
- **Theoretical:** Herbst (2000) and Coquery-Vidrovitch (2005) model pre-colonial African authority as concentric circles of diminishing power from the political centre — a model that fits Kongo well (Randles 1968). Borders were real but reflected diminishing-authority gradients rather than sharp lines.

## Historiography

The Barbela identification problem is a case study in how cartographic source criticism can overturn long-standing historical assumptions. Simar (1919) established the Barbela = Inkisi equation; it went largely unchallenged until Miller (1973) questioned it and proposed the Kwango as an alternative. Matonda (2017, 2018) provides the first systematic cartographic and documentary argument for the Kwango identification. The error's persistence reflects the influence of Belgian missionary historians (Van Wing, Cuvelier, Plancquaert) who worked from colonial-era administrative borders — the Inkisi did indeed become an important colonial boundary in 1947 — and projected those boundaries anachronistically onto pre-colonial geography.
