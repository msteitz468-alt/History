# Palmyra

**Palmyra** (Aramaic **Tadmor**) was the great **caravan city** of the Syrian desert that, under
[[zenobia|Zenobia]], briefly ruled an eastern empire during the [[third-century-crisis|third-century
crisis]]. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 2 (**John Drinkwater**).

## Geography and trade

Set at an **oasis** midway across the Syrian steppe between the Roman Mediterranean and the
Euphrates–Mesopotamia routes, Palmyra grew rich as a hub of the **caravan trade** linking Rome to the
East. Incorporated into the Roman province of Syria, it retained a distinctive culture — Aramaic
language, hybrid Graeco-Roman and Iranian art, and a powerful local aristocracy of merchant-princes —
and fielded its own desert cavalry and archers.

## The Palmyrene empire

In the catastrophe of the 250s–260s, Palmyra's nobleman **Odenathus** held the eastern frontier
against Sassanid Persia for [[gallienus|Gallienus]], who made him *Corrector Totius Orientis*. After
his murder (267), his widow **[[zenobia|Zenobia]]**, regent for their son **Vaballathus**, expanded
Palmyrene control over **Syria, Egypt, and much of Asia Minor**, edging from a nominal Roman
condominium toward open empire. The emperor **[[aurelian|Aurelian]]** crushed this realm in 272,
captured Zenobia, and — after a second revolt in 273 — **destroyed the city**, from which it never
fully recovered as a power.

## Significance

Palmyra exemplifies two themes of the crisis: the importance of **local self-help** when central
authority failed in the East, and — in its self-presentation as **"Roman"** even at the height of
Zenobia's ambition — the fundamentally **centripetal** character of the period (Drinkwater), in which
even the great breakaway regimes aspired to Rome rather than to secession. Its ruins remain among the
most spectacular of the Roman East.
