# Yehud — the Persian Province of Judah

**Yehud** (Aramaic *yhd*, the name on its coins and seal-impressions) was the small
Achaemenid province of **Judah**, centred on **Jerusalem**, within the satrapy of
**[[eber-nari|Beyond the River]]**. It is the best-documented corner of the Persian
Levant — the setting of the **Restoration** that created **Second Temple Judaism** —
because the Hebrew Bible preserves its story where the rest of Syria is silent.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 3b, Eph'al) treats it as the type-case of
Achaemenid provincial government; the fuller internal history is reserved for CAH VI.

## The Restoration

After **[[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]]** took Babylon (539), his **edict** (Ezra 1; 6)
permitted the exiled Jews to **return** and rebuild the **Temple of Jerusalem**,
returning the vessels Nebuchadnezzar had looted — part of a Persian **repatriation
policy** that ran for some eighty years and was extended to other deported peoples
(e.g. the Neirabaeans). **Darius I** and **Artaxerxes I** went further, ordering the
Temple's building costs and cult met "from the resources of the king derived from the
taxes of Beyond the River," and exempting its personnel from tribute — the same favour
Persia showed other central sanctuaries (the Apollo temple at Magnesia). Early
messianic hopes attached to **Zerubbabel** of the House of David (Haggai 2), but he
**vanished from the record after 521** — perhaps deposed, Eph'al suggests, lest a local
dynast inspire revolt.

## Government: autonomy under a local governor

Yehud was ruled by a **governor (*peḥah*)** who — like his counterpart the **House of
Sanballat in neighbouring Samaria** — came from the **local people**, not from an
imposed Persian official: Sheshbazzar and Zerubbabel at the start, then **Nehemiah** and
**Yehizkiyah**, and the Jewish (despite his Persian name) **Bagohi**, to whom the
Elephantine Jews appealed in 408. **Nehemiah's memoir** (Neh. 1–13) is the richest
portrait of an Achaemenid provincial governor anywhere: appointed by the king, escorted
by cavalry, authorized to draw timber from the royal "paradise," he organized **task
forces (*pelek*)** to rebuild Jerusalem's **wall** (445), force-settled a tenth of the
population into the city (a *synoikismos* with Greek parallels), and enacted sweeping
social-religious regulations (Sabbath trade, debt remission, foreign wives) — by
persuasion and force, clashing with the Temple establishment and the satrapal hierarchy.
Building the wall had earlier been **halted by royal order** after the "adversaries of
Judah" (the army of Samaria) denounced it as seditious (Ezra 4).

## Coins and the rise of the High Priest

Yehud struck tiny silver coins inscribed *yhd*, bearing a falcon and lily — and,
revealingly, naming **local figures, not satraps**: "Yehizkiyah the governor"
(*yhzqyh hpḥh*) and, at the very end of the period, **"Yohanan the priest"** (*ywḥnn
hkwhn*). That a **High Priest** wielded secular, coin-issuing authority signals the
late-Persian **eclipse of the governor by the priesthood** — the shape of Jewish
self-government that the Hellenistic age would inherit, with the High Priest as the
people's political head.

## Why Yehud mattered

- **The cradle of Second Temple Judaism.** The Temple's restoration and Nehemiah's and
  Ezra's reforms (Sabbath, intermarriage, the Law) gave post-exilic Judaism its
  institutional and textual shape.
- **A model of Achaemenid rule** — local governor, internal religious autonomy, royal
  patronage of the cult, taxation through the satrapy — which Eph'al argues was typical
  of how Persia handled its **ethnic-national units** ([[eber-nari|see Beyond the River]]).

## Historiography

Known overwhelmingly from **Ezra–Nehemiah and Haggai**, supplemented by the **Elephantine
papyri**, the **Wadi Daliyeh** finds (the Sanballat dynasty of Samaria), **bullae and
seal-impressions** naming the province, and the **Yehud coinage**. The documentary bias
toward Judah means we cannot assume its treatment was typical, though Eph'al thinks it
probably was. Whether Judah was a province from the start of Persian rule or only a
sub-district of Samaria until Nehemiah (445) is debated; Eph'al rejects the latter view
on the evidence of the early "governor of Judah" title and sixth-century *yhd* seals.
