# Jericho (Tell es-Sultan)

The deeply stratified mound of Tell es-Sultan in the lower Jordan valley, one of
the earliest continuously occupied settlements in the world and the type-site for
the Pre-Pottery Neolithic of the Levant. Its sequence — from a
[[natufian-culture|Natufian]] camp through fortified Neolithic town to Bronze Age
tell — makes it a keystone for the transition from foraging to farming and the
deep prehistory of [[urbanization]]. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. VII, Mellaart), drawing on
Kathleen Kenyon's excavations.

## Geographic Setting

A perennial spring (Ain es-Sultan) in the otherwise arid Jordan rift made the site
a permanent oasis — the kind of [[postglacial-environment-near-east|water-defined
locale]] on which early Near Eastern settlement concentrated. Its low elevation
(below sea level) and warmth gave a long growing season.

## The Pre-Pottery Neolithic Sequence

- **Natufian / PPNA**: a Natufian occupation underlies the earliest Neolithic. The
  PPNA town is famous for monumental communal works — a stone **wall and tower** —
  exceptional for so early a date and central to debates over pre-agricultural
  social complexity. Mellaart's CAH account notes early radiocarbon dates placing
  the aceramic occupation across the **9th–7th millennia BCE**, with more than
  twenty building levels.
- **PPNB**: marks "a distinct break" with PPNA. Well-built **rectangular houses**
  with a main room and ante-rooms around courtyards; walls and floors finished in
  burnished lime **plaster** stained red, buff and pink. Mellaart suspects the
  PPNB culture's origins lie farther north in Syria (Ras Shamra, Tell Ramad).

## Plastered Skulls and Intramural Burial

Jericho's most distinctive practice: the dead were buried **intramurally** (beneath
house floors), and human **skulls were plastered and painted, with cowrie shells
set into the eye sockets** — interpreted by Mellaart as venerated **ancestors**
rather than head-hunting trophies. This is among the earliest clear evidence of an
ancestor cult and ritual treatment of the body in the Near East.

## Trade and Connections

Jericho sat on long-distance exchange routes: **obsidian from central Anatolia**
reached the site already in the late Natufian (perhaps after ~8300 BCE), evidence
that the networks later carrying [[catalhoyuk|Çatal Hüyük]]'s products were open
very early. This obsidian trade is one of the oldest documented long-distance
exchange systems and a thread in [[trade-network-formation]].

## The Early Bronze Age city (CAH v1p2)

Millennia after its Neolithic fame, Jericho was again a walled town in the
[[early-bronze-age-levant|Early Bronze Age]]. [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I,
Part 2]] (Ch. XV, de Vaux) reports an EB rampart of brick on stone footings
**repaired or reconstructed seventeen times** on the west face, with projecting
semicircular and rectangular towers, a ramp and ditch — the defences of a typical
EB Canaanite city. Jericho was among the cities **abandoned in the ~2200 BCE
collapse** that ended the Levantine Early Bronze Age, not reoccupied as a town
until the Middle Bronze Age. (This EB town is distinct from the much later one of
the Biblical "walls of Jericho" tradition.)

## The EB–MB intermediate and the second urbanization

Kenyon's Jericho is the type-site for the **non-urban interlude** that CAH (Ch.
XXI) places between the Early and Middle Bronze Ages — what she named the
**"Intermediate EB–MB period."** After the seventeen-times-rebuilt EB wall was
**violently burned**, the walled town was not replaced: the newcomers were
**semi-nomadic pastoralists** with no interest in towns, leaving no fortifications
and no solid houses, only **single-burial shaft tombs** with distinctive
copper daggers — a sharp stratigraphic and cultural break (associated with the
incoming [[amorites|Amorite / West-Semitic]] population). Only in the
[[middle-bronze-age|Middle Bronze Age]] proper was Jericho **re-urbanized**, as one
of the revived fortified Canaanite city-states — the "**second urbanization**" of
the Levant. A Middle Kingdom seal-impression of a "**Scribe of the Vizier**" found
at the site marks the renewed Egyptian reach of the [[middle-kingdom-egypt|Twelfth
Dynasty]].

## Communal Labor and Ancestor Cult (Fagan & Durrani 2020)

Fagan & Durrani (2020) corroborate and date the walled settlement's key features:
a camp at the spring existed by ≥10,500 BCE, and by ~9000 BCE the community had
built a **walled settlement** — stone walls, a tower, and a rock-cut ditch 2.7 m
deep — constructed through organized **communal labor**. They also cite Kathleen
Kenyon's 1953 discovery of **seven plastered ancestor skulls** as evidence of an
ancestor cult, consistent with this page's account of intramural burial and
skull veneration above. (Fagan & Durrani 2020)

## Significance

Jericho is the paradigm case in the argument that **monumental construction and
town life began before, or alongside, rather than after, full agriculture** —
sharpening [[why-did-agriculture-begin]] and the deep prehistory of
[[urban-revolution|urbanism]]. Whether its wall-and-tower imply centralized
authority or communal labour without hierarchy remains debated
(cf. [[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]]). Its later sequence — EB walled town,
[[middle-bronze-age|EB–MB]] nomadic interlude, MB re-urbanization — also makes it
the type-site for the Levantine cycle of urbanization, collapse, and second
urbanization (see [[amorites]]).

## Historiography

CAH I.1's account is Kenyon-era and pre-calibration; absolute dates here are
superseded, and later work (the social reading of the PPNA tower, Abu Hureyra,
Göbekli Tepe) has reframed the picture. Treat the *stratigraphic sequence and the
material culture* as durable and the *absolute dating* as middle-20th-century. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
