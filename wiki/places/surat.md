# Surat

**Surat**, on the Tapti River in Gujarat, was the premier port of Mughal India and
the first major foothold of both European companies in the subcontinent — "that
famous and rich port of the Indies" (François Bernier). In
[[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]],
Martha Chaiklin and Ghulam Nadri use Surat to overturn two received narratives: that
Surat "fell" as Bombay rose, and that the companies dominated the Indian merchants
they dealt with.

## A Mughal Port

Insignificant until the sixteenth century, Surat surpassed the silted-up Cambay to
become the great emporium of the Arabian Sea. As a **neutral Mughal port** its
status shielded it from attack, and [[mughal-empire|Mughal]] authorities encouraged
European traders because "the primary revenue of the city was through customs
duties." Both the [[english-east-india-company|EIC]] (from the 1600s) and the
[[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] (from the 1610s) arrived early but established
stable factories only in the 1620s, seeking to bypass Portuguese Goa. Surat was "the
first foothold for the Company in India" for the English and, for the Dutch, "a port
city second in importance only to Batavia." In the seventeenth century the VOC
outperformed the EIC there, thanks to its better intra-Asian networks; its later
decline owed "less to greater English effectiveness than the breakdown of the VOC
through a decline of intra-Asian trade and supplies of Japanese copper."

## Surat's Resilience and the Ivory Trade

Chaiklin argues Surat "remained an important commercial entrepôt well into the
nineteenth century because indigenous patterns of trade and consumption of ivory
were tenacious." The structural engine was the ancient **Gujarat–East Africa
textile-for-ivory circuit**: Gujarati cotton was exchanged for African ivory, whose
demand came from **ivory bangles** — wedding bangles mandatory for Hindu, Muslim,
and Parsee women across Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Sindh, destroyed on death or
widowhood and thus generating "a continuous demand that was not subject to the
vagaries of fashion." The market required large African "bangle tusks" (Asian tusks
were too small); cotton and ivory formed a circular economy.

Crucially, the **ivory craftsmen stayed in Surat**, so even ivory landed at Bombay
"still had to be brought to Surat, because that is where the craftsmen resided." The
fall of Ahmedabad to the Marathas (1757) and turmoil in Sindh drove yet more
craftsmen to Surat even as its political power waned. This trade was largely
invisible to European records "because it was one that Europeans did not control or
directly profit from" — a case study in the "congealed power" of the European
archive (Subrahmanyam). Bombay supplanted Surat as an ivory centre only in the late
nineteenth century, and then to serve **European industrial demand** (billiard
balls, piano keys), not indigenous bangle consumption.

## The English Bridgehead (Roy)

[[roy-east-india-company-2016|Roy (2016)]] makes Surat the stage of the EIC's early,
deferential Mughal phase. **William Hawkins** reached Surat in 1608, travelled to
Agra disguised as an Afghan nobleman, and obtained from **Jahangir** a licence to
build a factory plus the title of **400-horse mansabdar** (the two men conversing in
Arabic) — though Portuguese intrigue saw him expelled by 1611. Naval victories
secured the foothold: **Thomas Best** (1611) and **Nicholas Downton** (1614, against
the viceroy of Goa's fleet — Portuguese losses of 500 men against five English)
"more or less marked the end of Portuguese power on the western seaboard." Until
about 1640 "all of the Company's operations in India were placed under the
administration of Surat," whose first president, **William Methwold**, was the first
officer to propose building a harbour at [[bombay]]. The relationship's turning point
came during **Shivaji's raid of 1664**: EIC employees fought off the
[[maratha-empire|Maratha]] forces and saved the Company warehouse, so that "for the
first time in the Company's history the Mughals were dependent on the English" — a
role-reversal Roy reads as the seed of later ascendancy.

## Merchants and Brokers

Surat is the setting for the [[surat-merchant-brokerage|brokerage system]] that
bound the companies to Indian capital. Merchant-princes such as **Virji Vora** and
broker families such as the **Parekhs** (EIC) and **Kishandas** (VOC) often held
near-monopsonistic control over company sales and were the companies' creditors —
"Virge Vora, by reason of our continuall mighty ingagements, must not bee displeased
in any case" (EIC, 1643). Even the **EIC takeover of Surat castle in 1759** did not
end company dependence on these intermediaries.
