# Armenia (the Arsacid Kingdom)

The mountainous kingdom of **eastern Anatolia** — for most of the third and fourth centuries **not a strong
independent state but a contested buffer**, its survival owed to the rivalry of its powerful neighbours,
**Rome** and **[[parthian-empire|Parthia]] / [[sassanid-empire|Sassanian Persia]]**. The first kingdom
to adopt **Christianity** as a state religion. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 15 (**C. S.
Lightfoot**).

## A land of strategic geography

Eastern Anatolia — high ranges, isolated valleys, bleak *yailas*, a severe climate — supported only a
small, scattered population and "was never fashioned into an effective and sustained political unit." But
standing at the **crossroads** of invasion and trade routes (to the Anatolian plateau west, the Caucasus
passes north, the Caspian and Atropatene east, Mesopotamia south), it had lasting **strategic importance**
and was repeatedly the stage of Romano-Iranian war.

## The Arsacid buffer kingdom

After Nero's Armenian wars, the **Arsacid dynasty** (a branch of the Parthian royal house) was established
on the throne — **Tiridates I** crowned by Nero at Rome in 66, a compromise that set the *status quo* until
the monarchy's dissolution in the fifth century. Both empires "paid court" to the Armenian king with gifts
and subsidies, exacted tribute, hostages and military service (Armenian archers served Maximinus Thrax),
and could depose or kill kings who displeased them. After the Sassanians overthrew the Parthian Arsacids in
the 220s, the **Armenian Arsacids leaned increasingly toward Rome** — a tilt later reinforced by shared
Christianity — since the new Persian dynasty sought to **uproot them** as a nucleus of Arsacid opposition.

## Nakharar feudalism — the source of weakness

Lightfoot stresses that **Armenia was a feudal society**, and therein lay its internal weakness. It was
divided into districts ruled by hereditary noble families, the ***nakharars*** and ***azats***, who held
imposing strongholds, parcelled their lands as **military fiefs**, and commanded contingents loyal first to
themselves. The king depended on their goodwill, granting them hereditary court offices; they were
"obsessed with their own status," prone to withdraw allegiance or revolt, and locked in mutual rivalry.
There was little central administration, **no native script** before the fifth century (Greek and Aramaic
were used), and **no native coinage** — money transactions used Roman and Sassanian coin. The Armenian
sources' picture of a unified centralized monarchy is, Lightfoot insists, "completely false."

## Conversion to Christianity

Armenia's adoption of Christianity under **Tiridates IV (the Great)** — evangelized by **Gregory the
Illuminator**, with strong currents from Cappadocia (and earlier Syriac influence) — is the kingdom's
epochal event. The traditional date is **301**, but Lightfoot favours **c. 314**: Tiridates at first
**persecuted** Christians on Diocletian's orders, and his conversion was probably a consequence of
**[[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]'s** own and the **[[edict-of-milan-313|Edict of Milan]]**. The
conversion was contested — conservative *nakharars* saw an imposed uniform royal religion as a threat to
local cults and their own autonomy, and much of the population stayed pagan at heart for generations.

## The marches and the 298 settlement

Armenia proper was flanked by minor states bound to it in varying degrees — **Iberia** and **Albania**
(guarding the Caucasus passes), and the principalities of the Syrian and Arabian **marches** (Sophene,
Corduene, Arzanene), ruled by *bdeakhsh*/*vitaxae*. The decisive turn came with the **[[peace-of-nisibis-298|peace
of 298]]**: after Galerius routed **Narses**, Armenia gained eastern territory and the restored
**Tiridates IV**, and Rome took the five *regiones Transtigritanae* and suzerainty over Iberia (whose king
**Meribanes III** also converted, c. 335). The frontier and the contest over Armenia remained unresolved at
**Constantine's death (337)**, bequeathed to Constantius II.

## Significance

Armenia is the textbook **buffer state**: too weak and too feudally fragmented to control its own fate, yet
too strategically placed to be left alone, "subject to both conquest and conciliation" by Rome and Persia.
Its early Christianization gave it a lasting western and religious orientation that outlived the kingdom
itself, while its *nakharar* nobility — entrenched by the very terrain that isolated it — survived the
monarchy's fall.

## The fourth century: contest and partition (CAH XIII)

[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] (Chs. 1, 13–14) carries Armenia through the fourth century as the
perennial bone of contention between Rome and **Shapur II's [[sassanid-empire|Persia]]**.
[[constantius-ii|Constantius II]] secured the kingdom by diplomacy (restoring king **Arsaces**, 338) while
fighting his defensive Mesopotamian war; but after [[julian|Julian's]] disaster and **Jovian's treaty of
363** (which abandoned Roman claims), Persia pressed its advantage. Renewed war under
[[valentinian-and-valens|Valens]] led at last to the **partition of Armenia (c. 387)**: the larger part
fell under Persian suzerainty, the smaller under Rome, which formally installed the satraps of six
trans-Euphrates districts. The kingdom's Christianization gave it a lasting western/religious orientation,
but its **division between the two empires** — a direct consequence of the fourth-century contest — fixed
the shape of the Armenian question for centuries.

## Update — Armenia in the fifth and sixth century (CAH XIV)

[[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] (Thomson, Ch. 21) carries Armenia through the period after the partition.
The deep tension was that the **Armenian nobility (the *nakharars*)** were socially and culturally closer to
**Iran** than to Rome, making Armenia "an uncertain ally for the Romans," even as **Christianity** (since
Tiridates' conversion) pulled it westward — a contradiction at the heart of Armenian history.

- **The struggle for Christianity.** When the Sasanids tried to enforce **Zoroastrianism**, Armenian
  resistance culminated in the great revolt under **Vardan Mamikonean** and the battle of **Avarayr (451)** —
  a military defeat remembered as a spiritual victory that preserved Armenian Christianity, and later (under
  Vahan Mamikonean) won a measure of religious autonomy from Persia.
- **The invention of a literary culture.** The fifth century saw the creation of the **Armenian alphabet**
  (Mashtots) and a flowering of Christian historiography (Agathangelos, the *Buzandaran*, Lazar, Eḷishē) —
  the **"classical age"** of Armenian letters, partly idealized in retrospect, that gave Armenia an enduring
  national-religious identity.
- **The rejection of Chalcedon.** Theologically Armenia drew apart from Constantinople: the Armenian church
  **rejected the [[council-of-chalcedon-451|Council of Chalcedon]]** (definitively in the early sixth century,
  aligning with the Monophysites), even excommunicating the Georgians over the issue — a confessional breach
  that permanently distinguished the Armenian church.
- **Between the empires.** The emperor **[[successors-of-justinian|Maurice]]** sought to integrate Roman
  Armenia more firmly (deporting nobles, planting an anti-patriarch), but Armenia "as a whole was never
  integrated into the Greek-speaking empire," remaining a contested march between Rome and a
  [[sassanid-empire|Sasanid]] Iran to which much of its nobility inclined.

Source: [[cameron-cah-v14-2000]] (Ch. 21, Robert Thomson).
