# Naqada

**Naqada** (ancient **Nubt**, "the town of gold," for the gold of the adjacent eastern
desert) is the Upper Egyptian site, on the west bank of the Nile north of Thebes, whose
predynastic cemeteries gave their name to the **Naqada culture** — the archaeological
sequence through which [[predynastic-egypt|Predynastic Egypt]] moved toward the
[[unification-of-egypt-3100bce|unified state]]. Excavated by
**W. M. Flinders Petrie** in 1894–95, its huge grave-fields supplied the
material on which the whole relative chronology of late predynastic Egypt was built.

## The type-site and its sequence

Petrie's seriation of Naqada's graves produced the framework still used (with revision)
today. Michael Rice ([[sources/rice-egypts-making-2003|*Egypt's Making*]]) sets out the
three horizons, each named for the find-site where it was first defined but anchored to
Naqada:

- **Naqada I** (Amratian, after El-Amra; c.4000–3500 BC) — the direct successor of the
  earlier [[predynastic-egypt|Badarian]] culture; red-polished and white-cross-lined
  pottery, reed boats, the first river traffic, ivory combs and figurines, cold-worked
  copper. Rice reads it as "a more advanced phase of the Badarian."
- **Naqada II** (Gerzean, after El-Gerza; c.3500–3200 BC) — the pivotal phase: towns at
  Naqada, [[hierakonpolis|Hierakonpolis]] and This/[[abydos|Abydos]] become effectively
  cities; buff "decorated ware" with boats and animals; stone-vessel and gold working
  industries; and the clearest evidence of contact with south-west Asia
  (Uruk-style filter-spouted pots, the pear-shaped mace, cylinder seals, high-prowed
  boats). See [[egypt-mesopotamia-predynastic-contact]].
- **Naqada III** (c.3200–3100 BC) — the "Dynasty 0" horizon immediately before the
  First Dynasty, the era of [[narmer|Scorpion and Narmer]].

## A cult capital that yielded power

Naqada was the great centre of the cult of the god **Set**, as
[[hierakonpolis|Hierakonpolis]] ("the city of the Falcon," ancient Nekhen) was of
**Horus** — the two poles whose mythic opposition the Egyptians later read back into the
duality of the [[divine-kingship|Two Lands]]. Rice notes that *at* the unification both
Naqada and Hierakonpolis appear to **decline as royal capitals** even as they retain
their prestige as the residences of the two great gods: the centre of gravity moves
north to [[memphis|Memphis]] and the royal necropolis to Abydos. Naqada thus marks the
transition from a landscape of competing Upper Egyptian "courts" to a single kingship.
