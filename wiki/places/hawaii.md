## Geographic Description

An archipelago of volcanic and coral islands in the north-central Pacific Ocean, at approximately 20°N, 157°W. The main islands are O'ahu (location of Honolulu and Pearl Harbor), Maui, Hawai'i (the Big Island), Kaua'i, and Moloka'i. Strategic location made Pearl Harbor a prime US naval objective in the Pacific.

## History of Control and Settlement

### Kingdom of Hawai'i (to 1893)

From the reign of Kamehameha I (unified the islands c.1810), Hawai'i was recognised as a sovereign nation with international standing. By 1887 it had "treaties and conventions with a score of nations, at least five treaties or conventions with the United States, and about a hundred diplomatic and consular posts." In 1843 Britain and France jointly recognised Hawaiian independence. By 1892 it was "a multi-racial, multi-cultural nation . . . Literacy rates were among the highest of any nation." Iolani Palace had "telephones and electricity . . . before the American White House."

### The Plantation Era and Haole Power (1830s–1893)

Sugar planters required privatised land, imported labour, and US market access. The Mahele (1840s–1850s) privatised land; the 1875 Reciprocity Treaty opened the US market. The "'missionary party' — an alliance of missionary descendants and businessmen" dominated public policy by mid-century. The 1887 "Bayonet Constitution" stripped the monarchy of executive authority and gave the US exclusive rights to Pearl Harbor.

By the 1890s the indigenous population had "withered"; Hawaiians owned "about 14 per cent of the land, by no means the best; most had quit — or been driven from — the countryside, to survive on plantations or in town." Women "were favoured in land inheritance during the period of the Mahele" — an anomalous pattern arising from Hawaiian gender ideology and the disruption of normal inheritance chains by depopulation.

See [[overthrow-of-hawaiian-monarchy-1893]] for the 1893 coup. "Thurston, Dole and their associates founded the haole oligarchy that controlled the politics and economy of Hawai'i until the 1950s."

### US Territory and State (1898–present)

Annexation via the Newlands Resolution (1898); Organic Act (1900) made all citizens of the Republic US citizens. Vast Crown and government lands ("Ceded Lands") transferred to US control. Statehood in 1959; federal government "retains substantial holdings, especially on O'ahu, as military reservations." The Hawaiian people "were given no voice in these changes."

## Strategic and Economic Significance

Pearl Harbor was the primary US strategic objective: American interest was "primarily strategic, focused on Pearl Harbor." Hawaii's sugar economy depended on US market access (Reciprocity Treaty); the haole planters saw annexation as locking in that access permanently. By the interwar period Hawai'i was fully incorporated into the US economy.

## Demographic Context

Post-contact depopulation of native Hawaiians was among the most severe in the Pacific — described as "the most important 'fact' in Hawaiian history." Hawaiian women urbanised rapidly (almost half urban by 1920) and, paradoxically, "by 1940 they enjoyed a higher life expectancy (over 50 years) than any other Islanders" — the result of public health investment that came with US territorial status, even if unevenly distributed.
