# Behistun (Bisotun)

## Overview
The cliff-face at Behistun (Old Persian *Bagastāna*, "place of the gods") in the Zagros mountains is the site of the most important surviving Achaemenid royal monument: the trilingual (Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian) inscription and relief of Darius I, carved c. 520 BCE shortly after he consolidated power.

## The Monument
The relief shows Darius triumphant over the "liar-kings" (Gaumata the Magian and eight other rebels), with the figure of Ahuramazda above. The Old Persian text (with Elamite and Babylonian parallels) is Darius's own first-person record: genealogy ("I am Darius the king, son of Hystaspes, an Achaemenid, a Persian, son of a Persian, an Aryan, of Aryan seed"), the Magian Gaumata's usurpation (impersonating Bardiya/Smerdis after Cambyses' death), restoration of the ayadana (places of worship) and observances the usurper destroyed, and suppression of nine "liar-kings" in rebellions (Elam, Babylon, Media, Sagartia, Margiana/Thatagush, etc.) via 19 battles in one year "by the favor of Ahuramazda." All opposition is drauga ("the Lie"); Darius upholds arta (truth/order). The 19th-c. vocabulary edition (see [[behistun-inscription]]) supplies etymologies, Median/Babylonian variants, and historical commentary keyed to columns/lines.

A copy of the Old Persian text was sent to Elephantine in Egypt, showing the inscription's role in imperial communication. The monument itself is royal sculpture (tachakam); Darius records his building/labour (hamatakhshiya).

## Significance
Behistun is the key primary source for the events of 522–521 BCE that defined the early Achaemenid empire. It is also the foundation stone for the modern decipherment of cuneiform (Rawlinson's work). The site itself (a mountain pass) had long been a place of power and visibility.
