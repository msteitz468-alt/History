# Lefkandi

Key Early Iron Age site on Euboea (Xeropolis settlement and the Toumba cemetery/heroon), central to [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]]’s arguments about the so-called Dark Age, elite “big-man” authority, and the misuse of archaeology in [[events/lelantine-war-c700bce|Lelantine War]] reconstructions.

## Geographic description

Coastal Euboea between [[places/chalcis|Chalcis]] and [[places/eretria|Eretria]], controlling access toward the Lelantine plain. Settlement focus at Xeropolis; elite burial complex at Toumba.

## History of settlement and elite display

**Toumba “Heroon” (post-1000).** Monumental apsidal building (45×10 m, earliest known peripteral structure in Greece) with horse burials and a warrior’s cremation in a twelfth-century Cypriot bronze amphora beside a richly adorned female inhumation (possible sacrifice). Interpretations:

- House of a “big man” whose authority died with him (building dismantled and buried under a tumulus) — personal/charismatic leadership model ([[concepts/basileus-and-big-man]]).
- Purpose-built mausoleum later buried after subsidence fear.

Mid–late tenth century is Lefkandi’s heyday of gold and eastern imports; cemeteries abandoned ca. 825; traditional view holds settlement largely abandoned ca. 700.

**Dark Age significance.** Wealth, labor for the tumulus (500–2,000 person-days), and long-distance contacts are unmatched on this scale elsewhere; possible ranked society (differential metal wealth between cemeteries) rather than pure egalitarianism. Exceptionality shows Dark Age wealth was lower and less evenly distributed than in Mycenaean or post-eighth-century Greece — Lefkandi “hardly serves to refute the concept of a Dark Age.” Hall also cites Lefkandi for complex political/social hierarchies sometimes used to prioritize “state” before urbanism, and for apsidal house plans among Dark Age detached residences.

**LHIIIC context.** New post-palatial sites including Lefkandi are sometimes read as “refugee” communities matching expulsion traditions; Hall resists equating them with a simple [[controversies/dorian-migration-historicity|Dorian migration]] story.

## Lelantine War and “destruction” ca. 700

Traditional reconstructions that Eretria fared worse in the Lelantine War cite Lefkandi’s apparent destruction around 700 and identification with Strabo’s “Old Eretria.” Hall challenges both:

- Only a tiny fraction of the settlement is excavated; sixth-century pottery is reported; 2003 Xeropolis work suggests continued occupation.
- The so-called “destruction” may be “merely a local conflagration” with other parts continuing into the seventh century.
- Strabo elsewhere may mean Old Eretria was merely a quarter of Modern Eretria.

History does not record the war’s outcome; Lefkandi cannot securely supply it. See [[controversies/lelantine-war-historicity]].

## Why it mattered

Lefkandi is the flagship site for Early Iron Age elite connectivity and for testing models of personal authority before the Archaic polis. Its significance changed from exceptional Dark Age node to (in modern scholarship) over-used correlate for late Geometric war narratives.

## From the Oxford History of the Classical World (1986)

**Forrest (Ch.1, [[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW 1986]]).** British excavations “dramatically changed our view of the so-called Dark Ages of Greece, in the tenth and ninth centuries B.C.” Lefkandi anchors a central Greek common-culture zone (southern Thessaly, Boeotia, Euboea, nearby islands): “stunningly prosperous (by contemporary standards) throughout the Dark Age, say 1100 to 750,” peaking in the late ninth century, with more than a century earlier “the tomb of a hero, buried with his consort and with his horses, of unparalleled grandeur and wealth.” Overseas, Euboeans acting in concert sought metals and settled (N Syrian coast before 800; Italy later). About **730** Chalcis and Eretria quarreled in the Lelantine War, which Thucydides said ranged “the rest of the Greek world in alliance with either side”; Eretrian defeat, Lefkandi (probably early Eretria’s site) abandoned, “the community had crumbled”—strains of war producing readjustments elsewhere and “something more like the city-state structure of later centuries.” Forrest’s abandonment thesis sits alongside Hall’s caution that “destruction” may be local and occupation may continue (**`concurrent_with:`** [[controversies/lelantine-war-historicity]]).
