# Uruk

## Overview

**Uruk**, in southern Mesopotamia, is the type-site of the world's **first
cities** and the place where **writing** was invented. It is the anchor case of
[[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] Ch. 6 (Hans Nissen) and the empirical core of
V. Gordon Childe's [[urban-revolution]]. By the Late Uruk period (~3300 BCE,
Level IVa) it covered at least **2.5 km² (~250 ha) with perhaps 50,000
inhabitants** — an order of magnitude larger than anything before it — and it was
"the economic, social, and intellectual hotbed for the emergence of writing."

## Geographic description and form

Sited on the Euphrates alluvium (the river later shifted around the city). Two
monumental precincts dominate: the **Anu** precinct (a high terrace with the
White Temple) and **Eanna** (the precinct of the city goddess Inanna), where all
the earliest tablets were found. The city was probably walled (the Gilgamesh epic
later describes Uruk's wall). Domestic quarters are barely excavated.

## The first cities and the Uruk Expansion

- **Explosive growth.** Through the Ubaid (~5000–4000 BCE) the alluvium was
  sparsely settled. In the Late Uruk a slightly drier climate opened the
  floodplain; immigration (possibly Sumerian-speaking) drove a jump from ~11 to
  >100 sites in Uruk's hinterland, organized as a three-tier **central-place
  system** with Uruk at the apex (the countryside reorganized as hinterland —
  [[ruralization]]).
- **The Uruk Expansion.** Uruk founded distant outposts/"colonies" (Algaze) —
  Habuba Kabira, Jebel Aruda, Tell Brak, Nineveh, Godin Tepe — to funnel metals
  and stone to the resource-poor south. Mesopotamian cultural items reached
  Egypt (cylinder seals, niched façades), supporting the case that the *idea* of
  writing diffused from Mesopotamia to Egypt (see [[memphis|Egypt]]).

## Writing as the climax of administration

Writing did **not** appear first: for centuries Uruk managed complexity with
clay **tokens**, then tokens sealed in **bullae**, then numerical tablets, plus
**stamp and cylinder seals** and mass-produced **beveled-rim bowls** (standardized
ration containers). Proto-cuneiform (~3300 BCE) emerged in temple/economic
"schools" as an *extension of accounting* — initially aides-mémoire that did not
record spoken language. The earliest texts are economic records and **lexical
lists** (e.g. the List of Professions, headed by a "king" figure), implying a
ranked society. See [[writing-and-record-keeping]].

## The archaeological sequence (CAH / Mallowan)

[[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. VIII, Mallowan) supplies the
stratigraphic scaffolding behind the socioeconomic story above. At Warka the
**Eanna** precinct yields a deep temple sequence (E-anna levels), and the period
labels used across Mesopotamia derive from its soundings: the **Uruk period**
covers strata **Uruk 12–4**, after which the **"Protoliterate"** (Uruk
4–3 / Jemdet Nasr) is defined precisely by the appearance of writing. The
**Uruk 4** tablets are the older, more pictographic series; **Uruk 3** tablets are
written in identifiably **Sumerian**. This ties the [[chalcolithic|Ubaid]]→Uruk
ceramic and architectural sequence — anchored at [[eridu|Eridu]]'s eighteen-temple
series — directly to the birth of the written record. See
[[ancient-near-eastern-chronology]] for how these phases are dated.

## The Protoliterate temples and art (CAH XII, Frankfort)

[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XII) details the artistic
flowering of the Protoliterate. The **Eanna** precinct held concurrent tripartite
temples decorated with **cone-mosaic** — thousands of coloured baked-clay cones
pressed into mud plaster forming geometric friezes (the **Pillar Hall** columns,
9 ft thick, are the masterpiece) — paralleled by the painted **"Painted Temple"
at Tell 'Uqair**. On the Anu terrace stood the whitewashed **White Temple**.
Frankfort reads the **Riemchengebäude** as a building made expressly to receive
the ritually-buried furniture of a superseded shrine. The **Uruk (Warka) Vase**,
3 ft of carved alabaster, shows a procession bringing offerings to **Inanna** as
fertility goddess — the earliest narrative relief. Inanna's Eanna is where the
[[writing-and-record-keeping|first tablets]] were found.

## Early Dynastic Uruk and the road to empire (CAH XIII)

In the [[early-dynastic-sumer|Early Dynastic]] king-list, Uruk holds the
supremacy after Kish. Its kings are largely legendary — **Enmerkar** (whose
"Aratta" stories concern long-distance trade), **Lugalbanda**, **Dumuzi**, and
above all **Gilgamesh**, builder of Uruk's city wall (partly excavated, of
plano-convex brick) and hero of the great epic. His war with **Agga of Kish** is
the first glimpse of the inter-city struggle for the *nam-lugal*. Uruk's last
independent figure, **Lugalzaggisi**, briefly unified "the land" before falling
to [[naram-sin|Sargon of Akkad]].

## Power and society

Seals and the Uruk Vase depict a dominant **ruler** figure (worshipping Inanna,
beating bound prisoners, hunting) — but the [[sumerian-king-list|tradition]] of
Gilgamesh and Agga recalls a senate of elders and an assembly of fighting men
overruling them, hinting at a more complex, partly assembly-based politics, not
pure despotism (the "primitive democracy" debate; see
[[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]], [[heterarchy]]).

## Why this place mattered

Uruk is the clearest case in the volume of the link **urbanization → writing**,
and the demographic and institutional template against which all other early
cities are compared. It anchors the [[city-state|Sumerian city-state]] world of
the [[early-bronze-age|Early Bronze Age]].
