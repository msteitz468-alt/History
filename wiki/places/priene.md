# Priene

**Priene** is a small Greek *polis* on the north flank of the Maeander river valley in western Asia Minor, founded in the mid-fourth century BCE on the terraces below the rock of **Teloneia** (Mycale range). [[thonemann-hellenistic-age-2016|Peter Thonemann]] treats it as the best-preserved ordinary Hellenistic town — a "Pompeii of the Hellenistic world" — and uses it as a microhistory of civic life under kings.

## Geography and Relocation

Teloneia is a natural stronghold with steep cliffs to south and west; at its foot, terraces between about 130 and 30 metres above the Maeander plain offered a buildable site. The Maeander carried exceptional silt loads, advancing its delta faster than any other Mediterranean river. Before the fourth century the Prieneans lived further up-valley (perhaps near modern Söke); around **350 BCE** they relocated 5–10 miles south-west to chase the delta-front and restore sea access. The new city flourished for roughly 250 years before silt again sealed its harbours. Today the ruins lie about **seven miles inland**.

## Urban Plan

The available building area was about **15 hectares** (37 acres). Town-planners laid a rectangular grid of blocks **120 × 160 local feet**, usually with **eight houses** per block of about **207 m²** each — roughly **480 housing units**, implying an absolute maximum urban population around **5,000** (at ≤10 per household). North–south streets often took the form of stairways. Public buildings were slotted into the grid: the **theatre** occupies 1½ blocks; the sanctuary of **Athena Polias** three full blocks.

Whether uniform house-sizes express democratic ideology is debated. Some rural lots were also standardised (50 *schoinoi*, ~5 ha). Uniform housing, however, appears in oligarchic as well as democratic cities; many citizens lived in the countryside; the rich may have preferred non-urban residences; and "democracy" excluded the serf-like non-Greek **Pedieis** ("Plain-dwellers"), native Carian villagers who worked the territory, occasionally revolted, and were not citizens.

## Kings and Autonomy

A monumental archive on the north anta of Athena Polias (excavated 1868/9 by the Society of Dilettanti; much now in the British Museum) recorded royal and later Roman dealings. Its first text: **"King Alexander dedicated the temple to Athena Polias."** An Alexander edict of **334 BCE** (inscribed in the mid-280s to defend privileges against a successor, probably [[lysimachus|Lysimachus]]) exempted Priene and Greek Naulochon from *syntaxis*, confirmed autonomy and freedom, and defined tax-free territory "between the sea and the hill of Sandeis," while outer villages remained tribute-paying as under the Persians.

In **287 BCE** [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius the Besieger]] invaded; Magnesia defected and Pedieis rebelled, but Priene stayed loyal to Lysimachus and was rewarded (cult/statue of Lysimachus). After [[battle-of-corupedium-281|Corupedium]] (281) the city switched display to Seleucus and Antiochus. Mid-270s [[antiochus-i-soter|Antiochus I]] settled his officer Larichus on a large slave-worked border estate; the city shifted from sanctuary bronze to an equestrian agora statue and tax privileges for his livestock and slaves — dependence on royal agents without formal provincial annexation.

Thonemann's broader reading: for Asia Minor Greeks the Hellenistic centuries were something of a **golden age** relative to Persian/Athenian Classical subjection and post-133 Roman taxation. At Priene kings did not run internal politics: the city struck its own coinage, passed democratic decrees, paid **no tax or tribute** to any monarch, and retained considerable local foreign-policy independence.

## Crisis: Galatians and Sotas

In the mid-270s Galatian Celts who had crossed into Asia in **278 BCE** ravaged Prienean territory: rural sanctuaries sacked, Maeander farms burned, Greeks killed or enslaved. A long decree for **Sotas** describes a scratch citizen militia — paid infantry, "horse-breeders" (a residual cavalry aristocracy), and willing non-Greek rural allies — that seized strategic points and recovered captives. The city could not assume Pedieis loyalty.

## Civic Life and Institutions

Government was democratic in form: adult male assembly supreme; annual boards; some priesthoods sold for life (Dionysus Phleus, Phrygian Mother, Poseidon Heliconius). Of 25 dated decrees, 18 fall in Metageitnion (Aug/Sep), often on the 5th day (*archairesiai* elections). No direct income tax; the rich carried liturgies. Comparative assembly turnouts from neighbours (Magnesia 2,113–4,678; Colophon 903–1,342) place Priene in a middle size-band.

Age-classes (*paides* ~12–18, *epheboi* ~18–20, *neoi* ~20–30) trained in two gymnasia. Early first-century decrees for Zosimus prescribe oral exams for boys and, for ephebes, punching-bags, knuckle-dusters, boxing-gloves, weapons, plus a *grammatikos* — training of body and soul. Classroom graffiti survive in the lower gymnasium ("Seat of Autocrates… Hook-Nose"). Formidable walls with about two dozen towers and a permanent garrison of recent graduates held Teloneia; a late third-century decree for commander **Helicon** — daily rounds, equal treatment, "nothing more important for Greek men than freedom" — was voted by the garrison, who sent "ambassadors" to the town as if to a foreign city: a city-within-the-city.

## Oligarchization and Decline

Over the third–second centuries super-rich **career benefactors** replaced office-holding as the path to honour. Early model: Apellis, elected secretary for twenty years, paid liturgies himself. Later model: Moschion, praised for hereditary virtue, private loans, famine grain, banquets, and gymnasium funding after Attalus III's line failed — what Philippe Gauthier called becoming "patrons" of the city. Benefactor statues choked the agora (two of Apollodorus forced detours at the Athena stairs). In the first century BCE the first woman *stephanephoros* funded an aqueduct — not liberal progress, Thonemann argues, but aristocratic capture, with even female relatives of great houses parachuted into office.

**Orophernes** of Cappadocia, raised in exile at Priene, seized the Cappadocian throne in **158 BCE** and deposited **400 talents** of silver with the city; Ariarathes V overthrew him the next year and ravaged Priene when it refused return; Rome mediated; funds eventually returned to Orophernes in Seleucid Syria. The Sacred Stoa (~130 BCE) was paid with Cappadocian money. Six fresh Orophernes tetradrachms found under the Athena base in 1870 may be a royal ritual deposit or skimmed city reserve.

Late second century: western residential district destroyed by fire and never rebuilt. Documentary record largely ends mid-first century BCE (of 397 Greek inscriptions only 63, mostly graffiti, postdate 50 BCE; only two or three of ~200 public documents postdate Augustus). Modest Augustan revival, then Imperial backwater.

## Historiography

Priene is load-bearing for modern images of the Hellenistic *polis* because fire, silt, and demographic decline froze a mid-Hellenistic urban fabric with an exceptional epigraphic dossier. Thonemann (*The Hellenistic Age*, 2016, ch. 6) uses it deliberately as one vantage among many (Athens, Jerusalem, Philadelphia, Pergamon could equally have served) while stressing what it shows best: ordinary civic scale, king–city negotiation without annexation, and the long slide from participatory democracy toward euergetic oligarchy.
