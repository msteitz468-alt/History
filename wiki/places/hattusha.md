# Hattusha (Boğazköy)

The **capital of the [[hittites|Hittite kingdom]]** — a mountain stronghold within
the great bend of the **Halys (Kızılırmak)** river in north-central Anatolia, and
the find-spot of the **Hittite royal archives** (tens of thousands of cuneiform
tablets) on which almost all Hittite history depends. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Chs. VI & XV, Gurney).

## Site and setting

The earliest settlement sat on the towering rocky citadel now called **Büyükkale**,
protected on the east by the precipitous gorge of the Budak Özü torrent and
fortified on its gentler western approaches. As the city grew it spread west and
north down the slope, and later south behind the famous **cyclopean walls** still
visible today. In the Old Assyrian age this had been the site of a *kārum* (trading
colony, level IV) beside the native town; both were violently destroyed — the event
remembered in tradition as **Anitta's sack and cursing of Hattusha** — and the
mound lay deserted until the new Hittite dynasty reoccupied it.

## Why a king chose a cursed, ill-placed capital

Hattusha was, on the face of it, a **bad choice**: peripheral, far in the north,
and increasingly remote once the kingdom expanded south beyond the Taurus. That
**[[hattusili-i|Hattusili I]]** deliberately moved his residence here from
Kushshar — flouting Anitta's curse, and taking the surname "man of Hattusha" to
commemorate it — is best explained by **strategy**: a defensible mountain fastness
sited at the **point of danger**, facing the turbulent northern hill-tribes. Those
tribes, the **Kaska (Gasga)**, would indeed become the kingdom's chronic menace,
sacking the nearby holy city of Nerik in the Middle Kingdom (it lay deserted ~500
years). On Anitta's old palace at Kanesh and here alike, the Hittites built in the
**megaron form** that ran through the whole life of the kingdom.

## The archives

Hattusha's supreme importance is documentary. The German excavations (from 1906)
recovered the **Hittite state archives** — treaties, edicts, annals, ritual and
mythological texts, king-lists and the Laws — the great majority inscribed in the
14th–13th centuries but including **late copies of Old Kingdom originals** (the
death-bed testament of Hattusili I, the **[[telipinu-edict-1500bce|edict of
Telipinu]]**). It was these tablets that yielded Hrozný's 1915 demonstration that
Hittite is an **Indo-European language**, and they remain the richest single
source for Bronze Age kingship, law and international diplomacy. The cuneiform the
Hittites used was a distinct script imported (probably via north Syria, akin to
Alalakh VII) and taught to scribes at Hattusha in the time of Hattusili I.

## Why this place mattered

Hattusha is the **command-centre and memory-bank of the first Indo-European
empire** — the seat from which Hatti rose to topple [[iamkhad|Aleppo]] and
[[fall-of-babylon-1595bce|Babylon]] and to rival Egypt and [[mitanni|Mitanni]], and
the archive without which the Hittites would be almost as silent as their neighbours
in the Anatolian highlands. Its abandonment ~1200 BCE (its destruction marked by
intrusive "Phrygian" pottery) is one of the markers of the
[[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze Age]] collapse.
