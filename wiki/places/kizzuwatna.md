# Kizzuwatna

The **semi-independent kingdom of south-eastern Anatolia** — modern Cilicia — that
occupied the crucial corridor between the [[hittites|Hittite]] heartland and northern
Syria from approximately the reign of [[hittites|Ammuna]] through the Hittite Empire
period. Its strategic value derived from its position astride the main mountain passes
linking Anatolia to Syria: whoever controlled Kizzuwatna controlled the road to Aleppo
and the Euphrates. Treated in Trevor Bryce, *The Kingdom of the Hittites* (2005), Ch. 5.

## Origins and Identity

The name Kizzuwatna "surfaces for the first time in our records" in the reign of
[[telipinu|Telipinu]]. But at least part of its territory had previously been known as
**Adaniya** — a region that had apparently been incorporated into the Hittite kingdom
during or before [[hattusili-i|Hattusili I's]] reign, and which "had clearly broken its
ties with Hatti by the reign of Ammuna, when it was listed as one of the hostile
countries against which Ammuna campaigned without success." The independent kingdom of
Kizzuwatna was probably established at that juncture, c. mid-16th century BCE.

The leader of the country's rebellion against the Hittites may have been a man called
**Pariyawatri**. A seal impression discovered at **Tarsus** (placing the kingdom's
reach in the Cilician plain) bears the inscription "Isputahsu, Great King, Son of
Pariyawatri" — confirming both the royal title (*Great King*) claimed by Kizzuwatnan
rulers and the dynastic succession from the probable rebel founder.

## The First Hittite Treaty

[[telipinu|Telipinu]] accepted Kizzuwatna's independence and drew up with Isputahsu
**the first known Hittite treaty** — a treaty of alliance whose surviving fragments
suggest its main purposes were to formalize an agreement on **territorial limits** in
the border region and perhaps to confirm Hittite possession of certain towns (Hassuwa,
Lawazantiya, Zizzilippa) in the wake of Telipinu's own military expeditions there. The
treaty established the diplomatic template for all subsequent Hittite-Kizzuwatna
relations: negotiated border accords managing the chronic problem of semi-nomadic
communities crossing territorial boundaries with their livestock.

Telipinu's restraint — not attempting to conquer the whole of Kizzuwatna — reflected
a careful balance of considerations: the danger of forcing Kizzuwatna into a Hurrian
alliance; the cost of stretched resources at a time when homeland security was the
priority; and the calculation that "a diplomatic alliance with Kizzuwatna might achieve
this objective more effectively than military force, and at far less cost."

## Oscillation Between Hatti and Mitanni

Kizzuwatna's history is defined by its repeated switching of allegiance between the
[[hittites|Hittite]] and [[mitanni|Mitannian]] great powers — determined by which posed
the more immediate threat to its own kingdom at any given moment. The sequence of
alliances recorded by Bryce:

- **Telipinu** (Hittite king) — treaty with **Isputahsu** (Kizzuwatna). First Hittite treaty; alliance or near-parity.
- **Tahurwaili** (Hittite king) — renewed alliance with **Eheya** (Isputahsu's successor). Parity treaty.
- **[Hantili?]** (Hittite king) — possible treaty with **Paddatissu** (Eheya's successor). The treaty addressed cross-border movements of semi-nomadic communities: "if a community of the Great King, with its women, its belongings, its cattle, its sheep and goats, moves and enters into Kizzuwadna, Paddatissu shall seize and give them back" (KUB xxxiv 1, CTH 26, 17–20).
- **Zidanta** (Hittite king) — treaty with **Pilliya** (Pelliya). Following prior conflict between the two kingdoms over towns in the border zone.
- **Pilliya switches to Mitanni**: Under pressure from the Mitannian vassal king **Idrimi** of Alalakh, who campaigned near Kizzuwatna's frontiers at the order of the Mitannian king Parrattarna, Pilliya concluded a treaty with Idrimi "under the authority of Parrattarna." Both Pilliya and Idrimi thus became Mitannian tributaries simultaneously. As Bryce notes, this was "a question of weighing up who posed the greater and more immediate threat" — a hostile Mitanni being more dangerous than an alienated Hatti.

## Strategic Significance

Kizzuwatna mattered because of **the passes**. The loss of Adaniya/Kizzuwatna to
the Hittites "very likely deprived the Hittites of their access to Syria via the Taurus
mountain passes" (during Ammuna's reign). Every Hittite campaign into Syria — from
Hattusili I's first incursion through Mursili I's march to Babylon — depended on
controlling or at least passing through the routes that ran through this region. The
**Cilician Gates** and **Syrian Gates** (modern Beilan Pass) converged on precisely this
corridor. When Kizzuwatna was under friendly control, the Hittites had a secure southern
flank and access to Syria; when it was hostile or under Mitannian suzerainty, it became
a strategic liability.

The region also had **Hurrian cultural influences**: Bryce notes a suggestion (which he
regards as tenuous) that Kizzuwatna was "created under Hurrian influence." Regardless
of its origins, by the Late Bronze Age the kingdom became increasingly Hurrian in
culture and religion, and served as one of the chief conduits through which Hurrian
religious and ritual traditions entered the Hittite cultural sphere.

## Subsequent History

After the period covered by Bryce's Ch. 5, Kizzuwatna's oscillation between Hatti and
Mitanni continued until the [[hittites|Hittite Empire]] period, when it was permanently
incorporated into the Hittite state — providing the Empire with direct access to Syria
and contributing heavily to the Hittite adoption of Hurrian deities and rituals in the
imperial synthesis.

## Historiography

Kizzuwatna is known primarily through the treaty texts preserved at [[hattusha|Hattusa]]
— the sequence of parity and near-parity treaties from Telipinu onward being the main
evidence for its history and borders. No substantial archive from Kizzuwatna itself has
been discovered. The Tarsus seal impression provides the single piece of direct
archaeological evidence for the kingdom's royal titulature. The relationship between
Kizzuwatna and the later Cilician kingdoms of the Iron Age and classical periods is a
matter of ongoing scholarly interest.
