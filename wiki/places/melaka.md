# Melaka (Malacca)

**Melaka** was the paradigmatic [[southeast-asian-port-polity|entrepôt]] of the
[[southeast-asian-age-of-commerce|age of commerce]] — "a city that was made for merchandise, fitter than
any other in the world," in the famous judgment of the Portuguese apothecary **Tomé Pires** (1515),
"the end of monsoons and the beginning of others... the trade and commerce between the different nations
for a thousand leagues on every hand must come to Melaka." Founded **c. 1400** by **Parameswara**, a
prince of the old [[funan|Srivijayan]]-Palembang line, on the narrowest, most strategic point of the
**Strait of Malacca**, it rose within a century to be the greatest market in maritime Asia and the
political and cultural centre of the **Malay** world.

## The great entrepôt

Melaka's position at the **pivot of the monsoon trading sectors** — where Indian Ocean shipping (Gujarati,
Tamil, Arab, Persian) met the China–Java–Maluku trade — made it the natural place to await the change of
winds and exchange cargoes. It dealt above all in the spices funnelled up from **Maluku** and **Banda**
and the **pepper** of Sumatra, against **Indian cloth** and **Chinese** goods. Its rulers grew rich on
tolls and the **shahbandar** (harbour-master) system, by which each major foreign merchant community was
assigned its own quarter and headman. The court's prestige standardised **Malay** as the trade language
of the whole region and codified maritime custom in the **Undang-undang Melaka** (the Melaka law code)
and the **Malay Maritime Code** — texts Reid reads as evidence of an emergent mercantile ethos. The
*Sejarah Melayu* (Malay Annals) preserves the sultanate's self-image.

## Islam and the Malay sultanate

Melaka's third ruler adopted **Islam** in the early fifteenth century, and the city became the great
springboard for the [[islamization-of-southeast-asia|conversion of the archipelago]]: as Melaka's
merchants and its Malay successor-states (Johor, Patani, Aceh, Brunei, Banten) carried the faith along
the trade routes, "becoming Malay" and "becoming Muslim" fused into a single cultural identity. The
sultanate also accepted **Ming Chinese** suzerainty (the voyages of [[zheng-he-voyages|Zheng He]] called there),
balancing Siam to the north against the protection of the Chinese tributary system.

Chiara Formichi ([[formichi-islam-and-asia-2020]]) calls Melaka "the first port of entry for Islam
to Southeast Asia," the hub where jawi Muslim scholars studied and from which conversion radiated
along the spice route — a 16th-century Sultan Mahmud is said to have styled it "the right Mecca."
Formichi's four-factor model of maritime Islamization (Perso-Arab traders settling and marrying
locally; co-religionist trade preference; conversion for advancement within the new Muslim polity;
and genuine spiritual conversion) describes the mechanism behind Melaka's own conversion as much as
the communities it later seeded. As elsewhere in the archipelago, Melaka's rulers took the title
**Sultan** while retaining Hindu-Buddhist court ceremonial — an instance of what Formichi terms
**"contextual orthodoxy"** rather than incomplete conversion (see
[[syncretism-vs-authenticity-islam-in-asia]]).

## 1511 and after

In **1511** **Afonso de Albuquerque** stormed and seized Melaka for **Portugal** — the single most
momentous European blow of the age of commerce. But Portuguese control of the city did **not** give them
control of the trade: Muslim and Asian shippers simply diverted to rival entrepôts (**Aceh**, **Banten**,
**Patani**, **Johor**, **Ayutthaya**) or to the overland portage across the Peninsula, so the Portuguese
capture *dispersed* rather than captured the commercial world. The exiled sultan's line founded **Johor**
and carried on the struggle. The **Dutch VOC**, allied with Johor, finally took Melaka from Portugal in
**1641**, but by then had already made **Batavia** (founded 1619) its capital, and Melaka declined into a
secondary post. It passed to **Britain** in 1824 and became one of the Straits Settlements.

## Why Melaka mattered

Melaka is the clearest single case of the [[southeast-asian-port-polity|port-polity]]: a cosmopolitan
city-state whose wealth and power fused in a single ruler, knitting the
[[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]]–China trade together and seeding both the **Malay-Muslim** cultural
world and the political map of the modern Malay states. Its fall in 1511 is the conventional opening of
the era of European intrusion into Southeast Asian commerce — though, as Reid stresses, the boom
continued for over a century *despite* it.

## From Miksic & Goh, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (2017)

**Foundation and succession myths.** Temasik (Singapura) attacked ~**1396**; its ruler—a refugee from Palembang—fled to the Malay Peninsula and ~**1400** founded Melaka as the new southern Straits mandala center. First ruler probably **Parameswara**; the *Sejarah Melayu* (Malay Annals) omit him and name the founder **Iskandar Shah**—Annals likely composed ~1435 to legitimize the ruling group after Islam conversion, possibly anti-Parameswara/[[malayu]] (Jambi) faction. Parameswara mission to China **1405**; [[zheng-he-voyages|Zheng He]] visited **1409** (stone inscription acknowledging Melaka as vassal—implications for [[ayutthaya]] relations); Parameswara visited China **1411**. Third ruler bore title **Sri Maharaja** (non-Muslim title identical to [[srivijaya]] usage) to 1444; evidence he converted as Muhammad Shah (missions 1424, 1431). *Ying-ya Sheng-lan* states king and people are all Muslims, but Pelliot believed this was added after 1433; 1445 ruler still had non-Muslim title Sri Parameswara Dewa Shah; **1446** assassination by a clear Muslim who took title Sultan **Muzaffar Shah** and minted Arabic coins—by his reign Melaka was officially a Muslim sultanate and foremost Seasian trading port.

**Mandala geography and urban form.** Postclassic: ancient mandalas (Angkor, Malayu, Majapahit, Bagan) faded; Melaka became center of the largest mandala in the southern Melaka Straits ~1400. Mandala included the Siamo-Malay Peninsula as far north as Kedah and east Sumatra from Rokan/Rupat to Jambi; after Portuguese conquest the Malay mandala fragmented and was not reconstituted. City plan resembled Palembang and Singapore: ruler on a hill symbolizing Mount Meru overlooking estuary trade; population estimates include lower figure **40,000–50,000**; Pires said **84 languages**; merchant community ~4,000 with four harbormasters (Gujaratis most numerous ~4,000; India/Bago/Pasai; Seasian islands; Ryukyu/Champa/China). Oldest Malaysian funerary inscription 1442–43 (Kedah); Melaka Legal Code largely maritime law; under Mansur Shah (1459–77) Old Malay inscriptions at Pengkalan Kempas first in Kawi then Arabic-script Malay five years later.

**China, Ayutthaya, and conquest.** Portuguese proverb: “Whoever is Lord of Melaka has his hand on the throat of Venice.” Melaka handled much China–Seasia trade; Pearl River docking points designated for Melaka and Siam ships. Melaka missions asked Chinese help vs Ayutthaya, but a 15th-c. Ayutthaya text cites Melaka as vassal; ~1490 per Pires Melaka had thrown off allegiance. **1413** Melaka requested authority over Old Port (Palembang)—not granted; emperor reassured Vikramavardhana Palembang still technically part of [[majapahit]]. Zheng He’s “jewel-ships” sometimes rendezvoused at Melaka’s fortified stockade. Portuguese conquest **1511** initiated European colonization but had **little long-term systemic influence**—integrated into pre-existing system; systemic change foreshadowed only with Dutch/English arrival 1596; Muslim traders shifted principally to **Banten**. Chinese not listed among permanent Melaka merchants 1515—likely small transient traders. [Historiography] **No archaeological study of Melaka yet published** (as of authors’ writing)—little historical archaeology. Hybrid colonial-city type (with Manila, Batavia, Syriam).

*Source: John N. Miksic & Goh Geok Yian, *Ancient Southeast Asia* (Routledge, 2017); [[srivijaya]]; [[malayu]]; [[seasian-classic-periodization]].*
