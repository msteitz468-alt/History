# Sub-Saharan Africa

**Sub-Saharan Africa** — the vast portion of the continent lying south of the Sahara desert — is the
**birthplace of humanity** and the setting for some of the world's deepest and least-written histories.
This page is the regional anchor for the wiki's **Gap 2** ingest, built first from John Reader's
**[[reader-africa-biography|*Africa: A Biography of the Continent*]]** and to be deepened by the UNESCO
**General History of Africa** volumes and the primary sources.

## Reader's environmental argument

Reader's "biography" frames the whole continent's history around an **environmental** thesis, which the
wiki adopts as a working frame (and flags as an argument, not a neutral baseline):

- **An ancient, eroded continent.** Africa is a high, geologically old landmass of **thin, fragile,
  heavily-leached soils** — far less forgiving than the young alluvial soils of Eurasia's river valleys.
  Agriculture was correspondingly harder to intensify.
- **Capricious climate.** Rainfall across the Sahel and savanna is highly **unpredictable** year to year;
  subsistence strategies had to be built around variability, not around a dependable surplus.
- **A uniquely heavy disease burden.** **Malaria** and, for livestock, **trypanosomiasis** (spread by the
  tsetse fly) made much of the continent lethal to people and impossible for cattle — a constraint on
  population density, the keeping of draught animals, and the concentration of people in cities.
- **Indigenous innovation.** Against the older **Eurocentric** "Hamitic"/diffusionist assumption that
  African civilization must have been imported (down the Nile, across the Sahara, or from Arabia), Reader
  insists African societies were **innovators in their own right** — and that the distinctive forms they
  took were responses to this environment, not signs of any deficiency.

## Major Developments (continent-wide arc)

- **Human origins** — Africa is where hominins evolved and where anatomically modern humans arose before
  the [[out-of-africa-migration|Out-of-Africa]] dispersals (see [[human-origins]]).
- **Food production and iron** — independent West African domestications (sorghum, millets, African rice,
  yams), early African food production, and the spread of ironworking, carried south and east by the
  **[[bantu-expansion|Bantu expansion]]** (c. 1000 BCE onward).
- **The first states** — Nile-corridor **[[kush-meroe|Kush and Meroë]]**, the Horn's
  **[[aksum|Aksum]]** (Sub-Saharan Africa's first literate, coin-issuing state), and the indigenous
  non-coercive urbanism of **[[jenne-jeno|Jenne-jeno]]** on the inland Niger delta.
- **Trade empires** — the West African Sahel gold-and-salt states ([[ghana-empire|Ghana]],
  [[mali-empire|Mali]], Songhai) fed by the **[[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan trade]]**, and the
  **[[swahili-coast|Swahili city-states]]** and **[[great-zimbabwe|Great Zimbabwe]]** fed by the Indian
  Ocean gold trade.
- **Foreign intrusion** — Islam across the Sahara and the Swahili coast; the Portuguese on the Atlantic
  and Indian Ocean coasts; the catastrophic **[[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]]**.
- **Conquest and freedom** — the late-19th-century **[[scramble-for-africa|Scramble for Africa]]** and
  colonial rule, then **[[decolonization|decolonization]]** and independence after 1945.

## Why this region mattered

Sub-Saharan Africa supplied the **gold** that underpinned both the medieval Mediterranean economy (via the
trans-Saharan trade) and the Indian Ocean world; the **enslaved people** whose forced transport built the
Atlantic plantation economies; and, in the colonial and post-colonial eras, the minerals and commodities
of a continent incorporated into the world economy on subordinate terms (see [[imperialism]],
[[late-victorian-famines-1876|colonial famine]]).

## Historiography

The field has been transformed since the mid-twentieth century by the **rejection of the racist
"Hamitic hypothesis"** (which credited every African achievement to lighter-skinned outside "civilizers"),
by **archaeology** (Jenne-jeno overturning the "Arab stimulus" model of West African urbanism), by
**oral-tradition** scholarship ([[niane-sundiata|the Sundiata epic]] and the griot tradition), by
**historical linguistics** (the Bantu expansion), and by Arabic and European travellers' accounts
([[ibn-battuta-black-africa|Ibn Battuta]]). The authoritative synthesis is the eight-volume UNESCO
*General History of Africa*, written largely by African scholars; Reader supplies the accessible
continent-wide narrative.
