## Overview

Unqi / Pat(t)in (Assyrian *Unqi*; Luwian *Patin* or *Pattina*) was a Neo-Hittite kingdom on the Amuq plain of northern Syria, occupying the territory formerly held by the Late Bronze Age kingdom of Alalah. Its capital, Kinalua (Kunulua), is almost certainly to be identified with the extensively excavated site of Tell Ta'yinat. The kingdom is documented primarily through Assyrian records spanning from Ashurnasirpal II (883–859 BCE) to Tiglath-pileser III (745–727 BCE), when it was converted into the Assyrian province of Kullanni(a). Its history illustrates the complex dynamics of Neo-Hittite identity, Aramaean infiltration, and Assyrian tributary politics in the western Syrian corridor.

## Geographic Description

Patin occupied the Amuq plain, a broad alluvial basin formed by the Orontes river in what is now the Hatay region of southern Turkey. The plain had been agriculturally productive since the Neolithic and was one of the most densely settled zones in the ancient Near East. Alalah, the Late Bronze Age kingdom centred on Tell Atchana, dominated the Amuq until its destruction in the twelfth century BCE; the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] left a vacuum that renewed settlement at Tell Ta'yinat (Kinalua) eventually filled. Patin's northern territory bordered [[gurgum|Gurgum]] and Sam'al; to the south lay the kingdom of [[hamath|Hamath]] and its northern province Luash. The capital Kinalua controlled trade routes linking Anatolia, the northern Levantine coast, and the Syrian interior.

## History

**Possible early predecessor: Taita and P/WaDAsatini.** Before the rulers attested in Assyrian records, Bryce discusses a king named Taita, who bore the title "King of the land P/WaDAsatini" in four hieroglyphic Luwian inscriptions (from Tell Tayinat, Aleppo, Meharde, and Sheizar). Hawkins identifies P/WaDAsatini with the Amuq Plain (following Meriggi) and concludes that Taita ruled a substantial kingdom incorporating the Amuq, extending east towards Aleppo and south along the Orontes to the environs of modern Hama. Dating Taita's reign is contested: Hawkins proposes the eleventh century based on Building Period I at Tell Tayinat (Amuq Phase O); others suggest the late tenth century. Hawkins raises but does not endorse a philological connection between P/WaDAsatini and *Palastin* — a possible link to the Philistines or another Sea People group who settled in northern Syria after the Bronze Age Collapse. Bryce regards Taita as "a predecessor of the rulers, attested primarily in Assyrian sources, who held sway over the kingdom of Patin."

**The Iron Age palace at Tell Ta'yinat.** During the Iron Age, Tell Ta'yinat was dominated by a citadel with two main building phases. The first, dating to the tenth century or earlier, contained what appears to have been a palace complex of the *bit hilani* type (Building XIII), presumably the royal residence of the kings of Patin.

**Attested rulers:**

- **Lubarna I** (attested c.870–858?): earliest known ruler. When Ashurnasirpal II marched westward to the Mediterranean coast c.870, he advanced upon Kinalua and received Lubarna's submission without resistance (RIMA 2: 217). His reign probably ended in the first regnal year of Shalmaneser III (858), who reports attacking and destroying Lubarna's fortified city Urimu during his first western campaign.
- **Suppiluliuma** (Assyrian *Sapalulme*, attested 858/857): bore a traditional Hittite royal name. He led the anti-Assyrian alliance of northern Syrian and Mesopotamian states that fought Shalmaneser III in 858 near Lutibu on Sam'al's frontier. Defeated once, the coalition regrouped under Suppiluliuma and confronted Shalmaneser again near his fortified city Alimush (Alishir) — and was defeated decisively a second time. Patin may have been divided between Lubarna I and Suppiluliuma before the Assyrian campaigns.
- **Halparuntiya** (Assyrian *Qalparunda*, 858/857–853+): successor to Suppiluliuma following the second defeat. He submitted to Assyrian authority, paying tribute recorded in 857 and 853. He can probably be identified with the man called Halparuntiya in a Luwian hieroglyphic inscription found beneath a palace floor at Tell Tayinat (TELL TAYINAT 1). The shared name *Halparuntiya* with at least three kings of [[gurgum|Gurgum]] may indicate family connections between the two royal dynasties.
- **Lubarna II** (attested 831): the kingdom appears to have remained submissive to Assyrian overlordship for more than two decades before matters came to a head when, in 831, the Patinites rose up against their king — "perhaps because of his loyalty to Assyria" — and assassinated him, appointing in his place a commoner called Surri.
- **Surri** (831): Shalmaneser III referred to him as "a non-lord of the throne" (transl. Grayson). He was not a member of the royal dynasty and had presumably been installed by a populace hostile to Assyria. Shalmaneser dispatched an army under Dayyan-Ashur; before a confrontation occurred, Surri died, probably by his own hand. Dayyan-Ashur contented himself with substantial tribute rather than military action.
- **Sasi** (appointed 831): Dayyan-Ashur appointed a man called Sasi, from the land of Kurussa (otherwise unknown), as Patin's new ruler.
- **[Gap, c.831–738]**: a gap of almost a century follows in the Assyrian record. The kingdom may have remained submissive.
- **Tutammu** (attested 738): last attested king of Patin. In 739, he broke the oath of allegiance binding Patin to Assyria. Tiglath-pileser III deposed the rebel, executed him, and converted the kingdom into the Assyrian province of Kullanni(a) (Tigl. III 56–9).

## Why It Mattered

Patin's strategic importance lay in its control of the Amuq plain — the principal corridor between the Anatolian plateau and the northern Levantine coast — and its command of the Orontes river routes leading south toward Hamath and Damascus. Its capital Kinalua (Tell Ta'yinat) sat at the intersection of long-distance trade networks linking metals from Anatolia with Levantine luxury goods and Mesopotamian markets. The *bit hilani* palace type attested at the site reflects the kingdom's role as a cultural intermediary between Hittite, Syrian, and Aramaean traditions. The kingdom's four centuries of existence bridged the world of the Late Bronze Age Alalah kingdom and the Assyrian provincial system, making it one of the most important transitional polities in the northern Levant.

## Relationship to Assyria

From Ashurnasirpal II's campaign c.870 onward, Patin occupied a strategically central position in Assyrian western campaigns. Lubarna I's peaceful submission set the pattern for a long tributary relationship, periodically disrupted by anti-Assyrian coalitions (858, 831) and ultimately terminated by outright annexation in 738. The internal assassination of Lubarna II (831) and the subsequent Assyrian appointment of Sasi illustrate the degree to which Assyrian power intervened directly in Patinite succession by the ninth century.

## Historiography

**Dating Taita.** The most significant scholarly dispute concerns the date and identity of Taita, the possible early king of a kingdom called P/WaDAsatini overlapping with later Patin's territory. Hawkins proposes an eleventh-century date and suggests Taita may have resided at Tell Tayinat; others assign him to the late tenth century. The proposed equation of P/WaDAsatini with *Palastin* — and a possible connection to Sea Peoples — is regarded by both Hawkins and Bryce as speculative, with Bryce noting "the dating remains questionable." The site of Tell Tayinat has yielded significant archaeological evidence (including the temple complex and palace) but its full stratigraphic relationship to the textual record remains an area of active research.

**Division of Patin before 858.** Bryce entertains the possibility that Lubarna I and Suppiluliuma were contemporaneous kings who divided Patin between them before Shalmaneser III's campaigns consolidated it under single rule — a hypothesis that fits the rapid succession of rulers at the kingdom's first appearance in Assyrian records but cannot be confirmed.
