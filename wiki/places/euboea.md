# Euboea (and Lefkandi)

The long island of **Euboea**, running like "a scabbard along the eastern flank of
central Greece," was the **most dynamic region of the Greek Dark Age** — the home of
**Lefkandi**, the richest excavated site of the age, and of the towns **Chalcis** and
**Eretria**, whose ships took the first serious steps of Greek overseas trade and
colonization in both directions across the Mediterranean. [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH
Vol. III, Part 1]] (Ch. 18, J. Boardman) makes it the pivot of the whole Aegean
recovery.

## Lefkandi: the Richest Dark Age Site

Occupying a broad peninsula on the Euboean Gulf between Chalcis and Eretria,
**Lefkandi** was an important place from the Bronze Age and, after a destruction
around 1200 BCE, came through the Dark Age with **near-continuous occupation** while
most of Greece languished — "a site which had much to offer still in a Greece" that
was otherwise impoverished. Its key feature is **early and exceptional contact with the
east**: before 900 BCE it was **casting bronze tripod-stands**, and through the ninth
century its graves yield a wealth of **gold** and a striking range of **eastern
imports** — Phoenician and Cypriot pottery and metalwork, scale armour, Egyptian
amulets and a bronze vase, a Cilician seal. Lefkandi (whose Classical successor may be
Eretria) shows that even in an "introspective" Dark Age Greece, the **sea routes from
the east to Euboean waters were open**.

*(Note: the spectacular Toumba "Heroön" and its warrior-and-lady burial were excavated
just as this 1982 volume appeared and so lie at the edge of Boardman's account; they
have since made Lefkandi still more central to Dark Age studies.)*

## The Euboean Lead in the Orientalizing Trade

Boardman's central argument is that **the Euboeans, not easterners, took the first
serious step in the orientalizing of Greek culture**:
- In the East, by the end of the ninth century, a trading post was established at
  **Al Mina**, at the mouth of the Orontes in Syria — "the first clear evidence for a
  serious Greek trading enterprise overseas in the Iron Age," and from its pottery the
  Greeks there were **mainly Euboeans**. Al Mina was no ordinary colony (*apoikia*) but
  a port-of-trade, sought out for the **metal** (copper, tin, iron) it could procure —
  it was a **scarcity of metal, not of land**, that drew the Euboeans east, to
  [[cyprus|Cyprus]] and beyond.
- In the West, **Chalcis and Eretria** jointly founded **Pithecusae** on the island of
  **Ischia** (off the Bay of Naples) around 770 BCE — a true Greek colony, again
  driven by the search for metals (Etruscan iron and copper). Pithecusae is among the
  earliest Greek colonies in the west and a findspot of one of the oldest Greek
  alphabetic inscriptions (the "Nestor's Cup").

Through these ventures the small Euboean states became the **bridge between the
Levant and the Aegean**, channelling eastern goods, artistic motifs, and — critically
— the [[alphabet|Phoenician alphabet]] into the Greek world on the eve of the Archaic
revival.

## Why This Place Mattered

- **First mover of Greek overseas enterprise** — east (Al Mina) and west (Pithecusae)
  alike, decades before the great colonizing movement.
- **Conduit of the orientalizing revolution** — the channel through which the
  [[phoenicians|Phoenician]]-borne alphabet and eastern art reached Greece.
- **Metal-seeking, not land-hungry** — its earliest expansion was commercial, a key
  qualification to the standard "colonization = land hunger" model.

## Archaic Euboea after the Lelantine War (CAH III.3)

[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 39d, W. G. G. Forrest) carries the
island past the Lelantine War into the Archaic period — where, Forrest laments, peace
"brought none of any kind of light." The cities slip out of the mainstream: **Chalcis**
stays archaeologically mute (its aristocracy the **Hippobotae**, "horse-breeders," still
on the land when an Athenian victory of c. 506 planted 4,000 cleruchs on it);
**Eretria**, refounded after the move from Lefkandi, shows its growing wealth in a
striking **building programme from c. 700** — the walled city with its unparalleled
**West Gate**, a temple of Dionysus, and a fine sculpture-rich **temple of Apollo
Daphnephorus** — broken only by the **Persian sack of 490**. Brief tyrannies (Tynnondas
over "the Euboeans"; Antileon and Phoxus at Chalcis; Diagoras at Eretria) flit past as
little more than names. Euboea's enduring importance, Forrest stresses, is as the
**"scabbard lying along the flank of Central Greece"** — the link between Attica/Boeotia
and the outer world (Thrace, Chalcidice, the Black Sea). Eretria's ties to eastern
Attica show in [[pisistratus|Pisistratus]]'s using it as the base for his final return
to Athens (c. 546), and in Eretria's five ships sent to the Ionian Revolt (499) — the
choice to fight Persia being also a choice against the pro-Persian Pisistratids.

## The islands

Forrest's chapter also surveys the **Cyclades and Aegean islands** — isolated yet
sea-linked communities, plagued by piracy. Among them: **Paros**, home of the poet
**Archilochus** (early-mid 7th c., "a perfect example of post-Homeric man," whose verse
voices the new individualism of the colonizing, mercenary age and can even "envisage
wanting a tyranny"); **Thera**, whose drought drove the conscripted colonization of
[[cyrene|Cyrene]]; **Delos**, the [[ionia|Ionian]] cult-centre "with nothing to sell but
religion," courted by Naxos, Pisistratus and Polycrates and later the seat of the Delian
League; and **Naxos**, "largest and richest of the islands," whose tyrant **Lygdamis**
(allied with [[pisistratus|Pisistratus]] and [[polycrates|Polycrates]]) gave it brief
Cycladic dominance.

## Historiography

Boardman builds the case from **ceramic distribution** — Euboean and "Euboeanizing"
pottery at Al Mina, in the Cyclades, and at Pithecusae — at a time (1982) when Lefkandi
excavation was still in progress. The attribution of the Al Mina Greeks to Euboea, and
the very identification of "Euboean" wares (Chalcis vs. Lefkandi vs. Eretria cannot yet
be distinguished), have been debated since, but the broad thesis of a **Euboean-led
Iron Age commercial expansion** has held and been strengthened by the later Lefkandi
finds. Euboea is a strength of the collection's Aegean coverage.
