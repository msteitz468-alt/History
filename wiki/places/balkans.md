# The Balkans (Prehistoric and Ancient)

The **Balkan Peninsula** — the Carpatho-Danubian and southeastern-European zone
between the middle Danube, the Black Sea, the Adriatic and the Aegean — is one of
the longest continuous archaeological sequences in Europe. [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH
Vol. III, Part 1]] (1982) gave the region its **first dedicated synthesis in the
Cambridge Ancient History**: an admission, the editors explain, that postwar
excavation in Romania, Yugoslavia, Bulgaria and Albania had finally made an
overall survey possible, and that Balkan developments bore directly on Aegean and
Anatolian cultures at the close of the Bronze Age. This page is the regional anchor
for that sequence; the Aegean (Greek) part of the peninsula is treated separately
under [[greek-dark-age]] and the Greek regional pages.

## Geographic description

A mosaic of mountain, plateau and river plain. The **Carpathians** enclose the
Transylvanian plateau (a metal-rich "central stronghold"), drained by rivers that
nearly all feed the **Danube** and so the **Black Sea**. To the south the central
Balkan massifs give onto Thrace, the Vardar–Morava corridor (the natural land
route from the middle Danube to the Aegean), and the Adriatic coastlands of
Illyria and Epirus. The Carpathians, as Dumitrescu stresses, "have never been an
ethnic and cultural barrier" — the region is a contact zone, not a wall.

## The prehistoric cultural sequence

CAH III.1's Balkan chapters (Dumitrescu on Romania; Garašanin on the central
Balkans; Prendi on Albania) lay out a culture-by-culture succession:

- **Palaeolithic–Mesolithic.** A full Old Stone Age record (the disputed
  Villafranchian "pre-Palaeolithic" bone assemblage at Bugiulești; Mousterian
  cave-bear-hunter sites; Aurignacian and an eastern **Gravettian** of Russian-plain
  affinity). In the **Iron Gates** gorge of the Danube the late-Mesolithic
  **Schela Cladovei** culture developed an antler hoe-and-mattock technology and
  pollen-attested incipient plant cultivation — a move toward farming that was
  **arrested by the arrival of the Early Neolithic Starčevo–Criș culture**.
- **Neolithic.** The **Starčevo–Criș** and then **Vinča** traditions of the
  central Balkans, and the painted-pottery cultures of the lower Danube — village
  farming, tells, and elaborate figurine art. (See [[neolithic]].)
- **Chalcolithic / "Eneolithic."** The florescence of **"Old Europe"**: the
  graphite-painted **Gumelnița–Karanovo VI**, **Marica**, **Sava** and
  **Cucuteni** complexes, large tells, social stratification, and the **earliest
  copper and gold metallurgy** at the **Rudna Glava** and **Ai Bunar** mines and
  the **[[varna|Varna]]** cemetery. This is the subject of
  [[old-europe-copper-metallurgy]]. (See [[chalcolithic]].)
- **The steppe intrusions and the Bronze Age.** From the later Eneolithic, waves
  of **Kurgan / ochre-grave / tumulus** populations entered from the North Pontic
  steppe (Cernavoda, Usatovo–Foltești, Globular Amphorae, Yamnaya barrows),
  gradually assimilating the Old European farmers and — in the volume's reading —
  introducing **Indo-European speech** (see [[indo-european-migrations]] and the
  controversy at [[indo-european-origins]]). On this base the regional Bronze Age
  cultures crystallized (**Monteoru, Otomani, Wietenberg, Tei, Verbicioara, Noua**).
- **The Late Bronze Age metal boom and Hallstatt A.** Transylvania's gold and
  copper made it "an exceptional centre of bronze-working": of ~25,000 catalogued
  metal hoard-objects, **over 20,000 date to Hallstatt A** (12th–11th c. BCE), the
  transition into the [[early-iron-age|Early Iron Age]].
- **The Early Iron Age, c. 1200–700 BCE (CAH III.1, Chs 14–15).** Garašanin treats
  the period c. 1200–700 as a "**closed unit**" of uninterrupted cultural development
  in the Balkan interior, opening with the turning-point of the
  [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] (the Dark Age in Greece, the Sea
  Peoples, the fall of the Hittites). While reading the Dorian and Sea-Peoples
  movements more cautiously than older scholarship, he still affirms real **migrations
  within the Balkans** — from the Morava valley into Macedonia, and from the
  Carpatho-Balkan area through Thrace into Asia Minor. In the mountainous south-west
  (Hammond's Ch. 15), the period belongs to **transhumant tumulus-building peoples**:
  the [[illyrians|Illyrians]] expanding southward, the Greek-speaking Epirotes of
  [[epirus|Epirus]], and the [[phrygians|Brygi/Phrygians]] dominant in west Macedonia
  until c. 800. These are the Palaeo-Balkan peoples — Thracians, Illyrians,
  Macedonians — who form the "barbarian" backdrop to Archaic Greece.

## Why this place mattered

The Balkans were a **two-way hinge**: a corridor through which steppe peoples,
metallurgy and (on one model) the Indo-European languages passed into Europe and
toward the Aegean, and a metal-rich production zone in its own right. The
peninsula's Eneolithic produced some of the **earliest worked gold and copper
anywhere on Earth**, and its Early Iron Age peoples — Thracians, Illyrians,
Daco-Moesians, and the Macedonians on its southern edge — form the immediate
"barbarian" backdrop to Archaic and Classical Greek history.

## A social reading of the Neolithic–Copper Age (Bailey 2000)

[[bailey-balkan-prehistory-2000|Douglass Bailey's *Balkan Prehistory*]] (2000)
supplies the social-theoretical interpretation that the older CAH culture-history
lacks. Bailey treats the period **6500–2500 BC** as a single problem and argues
that its transformation was neither an economic revolution (Childe) nor a
population replacement (Gimbutas) but a change in **how people arranged
themselves, their places, and their things** — a shift from mobile, flexible,
verbally negotiated forager life to the **anchored, durable, inflexible** world
of houses, villages, tells, and cemeteries. His three regions for 6500–5500 BC
are **northern Greece** (south), the **lower Danube / Serbia / east Hungarian
Plain** (north and west), and **south-central Bulgaria** (between), with
north-west Anatolia and the [[danube-gorges|Danube Gorges]] as special cases.

Two claims of his are filed in detail elsewhere: the interpretive triad of
[[exclusion-incorporation-projection|exclusion, incorporation and projection]],
and the argument that the **house, household, and village** were the era's key
social institutions, with **tells**, the deliberate **burning of houses**, and
under-floor infant burial all expressing an emergent *ideology of the
household*. Against the older picture of Neolithic communalism (cf.
[[neolithic-communities]]), Bailey holds that the Balkans were "riven with
tension and conflict, where hoarding and the exclusion of people was just as
frequent as sharing and helping," and that the brilliant displays at
[[varna|Varna]] were a projected **illusion** masking a more mobile,
conflict-prone reality. On the **post-4000 BC dissolution of the tell world** he
follows Whittle in reading gradual, local, indigenous change rather than steppe
invasion (see [[indo-european-origins]]).

## Historiography

The Balkan chapters were written by **Eastern-bloc scholars during the Cold War**
and translated for the volume; they work firmly within a **culture-historical**
framework in which named "archaeological cultures" are equated with peoples and
migrations are read directly from changes in pottery and burial rite — an approach
much qualified by later processual and (since the 2010s) **ancient-DNA** work,
which has both **confirmed** the reality of a massive steppe genetic influx and
**complicated** the simple "pots = peoples" equation. A second, technical caveat
stated in the volume's own Preface: **radiocarbon dates in Part 1 are uncalibrated**
(old 5570-year half-life) and therefore run several centuries too young against
modern calibrated chronologies — any absolute date taken from these chapters must
be flagged accordingly. Garašanin himself notes he trusts typological cross-dating
to the Aegean over "data obtained by the radiocarbon method."
