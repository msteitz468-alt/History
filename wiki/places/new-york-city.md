# New York City

Major global city and the central setting for the career of Robert Moses. Between the 1920s and 1960s the physical form of the five boroughs and their immediate suburbs was transformed by an unprecedented program of parks, parkways, bridges, tunnels, expressways, beaches, and large-scale housing and "renewal" projects directed largely by one unelected official.

## Geographic and Historical Significance

New York in the early 20th century was already the largest and most economically dominant city in the United States, but its infrastructure for recreation, cross-borough movement, and automobile access was inadequate for its growing population and for the emerging suburban pattern. The East River and Hudson created natural barriers; Long Island's beaches were largely inaccessible to most residents.

Moses' projects (detailed in [[sources/caro-the-power-broker-1974]]) changed the shoreline in multiple places, created or vastly expanded the city's park system (including the great ocean beaches), built the Triborough Bridge complex and other major spans, and laid down much of the expressway network that still structures daily movement in the region. They also cleared large areas for housing and "renewal," often destroying stable working-class and middle-class neighborhoods in the process.

## Control and Key Actors

Formally governed by elected mayors and (for state functions) the governor. In practice, from the mid-1930s through the mid-1960s, Robert Moses held a unique portfolio of city and state titles that gave him de facto control over the capital budget and physical development decisions. Mayors (La Guardia, Wagner, Lindsay) and governors (Smith, Roosevelt, Rockefeller) worked with, accommodated, or eventually confronted him.

## Major Developments Linked to This Place

- Creation of Jones Beach and the Long Island parkway system (state-level, serving the city).
- Triborough Bridge and related East River crossings.
- West Side and East River drives / parkways.
- Cross-Bronx and other urban expressways.
- Multiple Title I urban renewal projects and public housing.
- Planning and construction of the 1964 New York World's Fair at Flushing Meadows.

The consequences — both the addition of major public amenities and the social costs of displacement and car-centered planning — remain central to the history of the city and to national debates about urban policy.
