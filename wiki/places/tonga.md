## Overview

Tonga (the Friendly Islands) is an archipelago in western Polynesia, comprising roughly 170 islands centred on the main island of Tongatapu. It is historically significant as the only Polynesian polity that retained formal independence through the era of European imperial expansion, a survival owed principally to the political genius of King George Tupou I (Taufa'ahau) and the constitutional settlement he engineered between 1839 and 1875.

## History of Control and Settlement

The Tu'i Kanokupolu lineage was dominant at the time of sustained European contact. Internal civil conflicts between chiefly factions marked the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. The decisive figure was Taufa'ahau, a chief of the Tu'i Kanokupolu family who converted to Christianity in the late 1820s, combined the essential dynastic titles through warfare and negotiation, and ruled a united Tonga as King George Tupou I.

The LMS attempted missions from 1797 but failed twice (withdrawing 1800) due to civil war and language barriers. A decisive turn came in 1826–1827 when two Tahitian teachers built a congregation at Nukualofa before British Methodist missionaries joined them in 1827.

Taufa'ahau's conversion was shaped by dynastic calculation: "He had become sceptical of the Tongan gods and the traditional religious practices. He had reason to doubt his family gods, for they had failed to come to his father's aid... Increasing contact with Europeans and the superiority of their weapons also helped to undermine his beliefs in the traditional gods." After this conversion, Methodism became "almost the state religion" — but the King's control was total: "the mission depended so massively on the King's patronage that he, rather than any missionary, was both the temporal ruler and the real head of the church."

## Strategic and Economic Significance

Tonga sat at the intersection of trans-Pacific trade routes and was a significant supplier of food and labour to European vessels from the late eighteenth century. Tongan copra production evolved into "something like peasant production" with German traders purchasing the crop, operating through a chiefly system that retained communal control over land and labour while monetising exports.

The Tongan adventurer Enele Ma'afu (Ma'afu'otu'itonga) dominated the Lau group of Fiji in mid-century, buttressed by Tongan Methodists, extending Tongan influence into Melanesia. Ma'afu was a credible rival to Cakobau for control of Fiji and ultimately became Cakobau's Viceroy in the short-lived Fijian Kingdom (1871–1874).

## Constitutional and Legal Significance

Tonga's constitutional history is the most coherent in the Pacific:
- **Vava'u Code (1839):** First written law; committed Tonga to Christianity; prohibited murder, theft, adultery, fornication, and alcohol retail.
- **1850 extension:** Explicitly forbade the sale of land to foreigners (Clause XXIX); mandated labour for family and community; extended the code's economic provisions.
- **1862 revision / proto-constitution:** Abolished "serfdom" and "vassalage"; restated the ban on land sales; introduced individual land leasehold based on King George's observations in Sydney, where he "was determined that such an appalling situation should never be allowed to arise in Tonga." "The prohibition of the sale of land... was only a legislation of customary land tenure, but the notion of individual ownership of land by leasehold was something quite new."
- **1875 constitution:** Drafted by missionary Shirley Baker under the King's instruction (consulting the Premier of New South Wales and the Hawaiian Consul-General for precedents); secured recognition from Britain, Germany, and the United States. King George's own words on proposing it: "my rule was absolute, and that my wish was law... But that, it appears to me, was a sign of darkness and now a new era has come to Tonga—an era of light." The constitution, with few modifications, "still serves the kingdom."

Access to land continued to depend on kinship, clientage to particular chiefs, and relations with the church, including misinale (vakamisioneri-style) contributions to overseas missions. With land securely in Tongan hands, "the economy evolved through something like peasant production and larger-scale production on nobles' estates."

## Comparison with Hawai'i

[[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] repeatedly contrasts Tonga's success with Hawai'i's dispossession as a paradigmatic paired case:
- In Hawai'i, the Mahele (1848–1850) distributed less than 30,000 acres to the mass of the people while 1.5 million went to chiefs and millions more were sold to haole planters. Tonga's prohibition of foreign land sale produced the opposite outcome.
- "Cakobau and Thurston in Fiji understood [the Mahele] as a scenario to avoid, whereas entrepreneurs were inspired by the prospect."

## Why It Mattered

Tonga's survival as an independent polity provides the clearest Pacific case study of what a combination of decisive royal leadership, constitutional state-building, and effective land protection could achieve against imperial pressure. It demonstrates that the "fatal impact" model was not inevitable — choices made by Tongan chiefs (especially Taufa'ahau) and by advisers loyal to the crown rather than to foreign commercial interests produced structural outcomes very different from those of Hawai'i, Fiji, or Samoa. By 1900 all major Polynesian societies except Tonga were formal colonies or territories.
