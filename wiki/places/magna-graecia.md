# Magna Graecia (the Western Greeks)

**Magna Graecia** ("Great Greece," *Megalē Hellas*) — the Greek cities of **Sicily and
southern Italy** — was the richest and most populous theatre of the
[[greek-colonization|Archaic colonization movement]], a region where the Greeks "could
transplant their way of life, and especially their agriculture, virtually unchanged…
only the scale was different." [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 38,
A. J. Graham, in homage to Dunbabin's *The Western Greeks*) treats it.

## The foundations

After the earliest colonies (pre-700: [[pithecusae|Pithecusa]], Cyme, Naxos,
[[syracuse|Syracuse]], Zancle, Rhegium, Sybaris, Croton, Taras), a second wave filled
out the map between 700 and 500:

- **Sicily.** **Gela** (688, Rhodians and Cretans under Antiphemus and Entimus),
  **Selinus** (628, from Megara Hyblaea — the westernmost Greek city, friendly with the
  Elymians of Segesta), **Himera** (648, Chalcidians of Zancle and exiled Syracusan
  Myletidae — the only north-coast colony besides Mylae), and **Acragas** (580, from
  Gela and Rhodes), which "as planned and built became one of the greatest and richest
  Greek cities."
- **Southern Italy.** **Locri Epizephyrii** (c. 679, from central-Greek Locris — its
  colonists swore a treacherous oath to the natives, then expelled them), **Siris**
  (Ionians from Colophon fleeing Lydia, c. 660s) and **Metapontum** (Achaean), the two
  cereal-rich cities of the Tarentine gulf whose rivalry the sources entangle.

## The colonies and their territories

The general pattern was that each colony established **firm control of an immediate
farmed territory**, beyond which (usually in higher country) native peoples lived under
growing Greek cultural penetration. [[syracuse|Syracuse]] is the model: it seized the
coastal plain, planted the subordinate military colonies **Acrae (663) and Casmenae
(643)** and the daughter-polis **Camarina (598)** up the Anapo valley, and reduced
Sicels to **serfs (the Cyllyrii)** — building a territory of some 4,000 km². The
contrast often drawn — **Dorians subjugating natives by force, Ionians (Chalcidians)
keeping peaceful commercial relations** — fits much of the evidence (the friendly,
progressively Hellenized Sicel sites of the Heraean hills — Licodia, Grammichele,
Morgantina — writing their language in Greek letters by the mid-sixth century) but
cannot be applied universally. **Metapontum's** territory, mapped by air photography
into long parallel land-divisions and scattered farms (mid-sixth century), is "the
best-known example of the organization of its territory by an Archaic Greek colony."

## Greeks and Phoenicians

In western Sicily the [[phoenicians|Phoenicians]], as the Greeks arrived in force,
"withdrew to the western end of the island" to three settlements — **Motya, Soloeis
and Panormus** (Palermo, tellingly with a Greek name) — trusting in their alliance with
the **Elymians** and their short sea-route to [[carthage|Carthage]]. Early relations
were peaceful (unwalled Motya, plentiful Greek pottery), but the foundations of Himera
and Selinus, and Greek interest in the Spanish silver trade (worked from c. 640), made
the Greeks a threat: Motya built its first wall c. 600. Two Greek attempts to colonize
the Phoenician/Elymian far west were crushed — **Pentathlus** (c. 580, Cnidians and
Rhodians at Lilybaeum; survivors settled the Lipari Islands) and **Dorieus** (c. 510,
at Drepanon/Eryx, killed by Phoenicians and Segestans) — opening the long Greco-
Phoenician wars of the fifth–third centuries.

## Tyranny, lawgivers, and wealth

The western constitutions were **oligarchies** (Syracuse's landowning *Gamoroi*;
Locri's "Hundred Houses" and council of a thousand; Taras's Spartan-style kings and
ephors) "interrupted by tyranny" — the first western tyrant being **Panaetius of
Leontini** (c. 608, risen by demagogy), the most notorious **Phalaris of Acragas**
(from 571, proverbial for cruelty). The west also produced the **earliest historical
Greek lawgivers**: **Zaleucus of Locri** (fl. 663 — the earliest whose date seems
trustworthy, author of a severe, deliberately unchangeable code) and **Charondas of
Catane** (whose laws served the Chalcidian colonies). The cities' **prosperity** —
overwhelmingly agricultural (Metapontum's barley-ear and Sybaris's bull on their
coins), but with vigorous trade and lavish **temple-building** (the earliest Doric
stone temples and the first **orthogonal town-plans** in the Greek world) — became
proverbial in the luxury of **Sybaris**.

## Sybaris and the end of an epoch

**Sybaris**, the Achaean city of the instep, was the richest of all — controlling "four
tribes and twenty-five cities" and the great colony of **Posidonia (Paestum)** — its
luxury a byword (Smindyrides, Herodotus's most luxurious man). Around **510 BCE** a
typically Greek quarrel — the tyrant **Telys**, backed by the *demos*, expelled the
leading men, who fled to **Croton** — ended in war and the **total destruction of
Sybaris**, its site reportedly drowned by diverting the river Crathis. Graham calls the
fall of so great a city "the end of an epoch": Miletus, which lost a rich textile
market, went into public mourning.

## Cultural osmosis and the Deinomenid hegemony (CAH IV)

[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 16, David Asheri) carries Sicily into the
early fifth century and **revises the old picture** of "a Greek sea-coast fringing
barbarian lands." Asheri sees sixth-century Sicily instead as a country of "manifold
ethnic elements, all at some stage of levelling, uniforming **hellenization**" — a
broad coastal belt of Greek culture 30–90 km deep, with Sicel townships turning into
Greek-type cities, Sicels and Elymians adopting the **Greek alphabet** for their own
[[languages-of-italy|languages]], and constant intermarriage and cohabitation. The
sharp Greek/barbarian line gives way to **cultural osmosis**; even the Greco-Punic
wars were "political or commercial, not cultural."

Politically the age belongs to the **tyrants** (see [[western-greek-tyranny]]):
**Hippocrates of Gela** built the first Geloan "empire," and **[[gelon|Gelon]]** then
fused most of Greek Sicily under **[[syracuse|Syracuse]]**, allied with **Theron of
Acragas**. Gelon enriched Syracuse by **deporting and enslaving** whole cities
(Camarina, Megara Hyblaea, Euboea) — Asheri's reminder that a Greek tyrant
annihilated three Sicilian Greek cities before any Carthaginian army did. The bloc's
victory at **[[battle-of-himera-480bce|Himera]] (480)** over Carthage, won the same
year as Salamis, opened the spectacular florescence of **Acragas** (its vast temple
line, the colossal Olympieum) and **Syracuse**, celebrated by **Pindar**.

## Why Magna Graecia mattered

- The **largest and wealthiest colonial world** of Archaic Greece — agricultural
  powerhouses that ranked among the leading Greek cities, won Olympic victories, and
  (with East Greek refugees) hosted the first western **philosophical schools** (Elea,
  Pythagorean Croton).
- The crucible of **Greek–Phoenician/Carthaginian conflict** that would dominate
  central-Mediterranean history for centuries.
- An innovator in **town-planning** (orthogonal layouts) and a showcase of the
  Hellenization of native Sicily.
