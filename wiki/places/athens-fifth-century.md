# Fifth-Century Athens

## Overview

**Fifth-century Athens** is the **regional case study** for the Mediterranean in
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Ch 13) — the volume's chosen "close-up" beneath the
global and interregional scales. Between the Persian Wars (480/479 BCE) and defeat in
the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]] (404 BCE), Athens combined the most radical
**direct democracy** of the ancient world with an exploitative naval **empire**, and in
the same decades produced an unmatched concentration of drama, philosophy, history,
art, and architecture. It is the paradigm of the small [[city-state|polis]] whose
cultural output vastly exceeded its size — and of the moral tension between democracy
at home and domination abroad.

## Democracy

The reforms of **Cleisthenes (508/7 BCE)** reorganized citizens into ten artificial
tribes cutting across kin and region, breaking aristocratic blocs. Sovereignty lay
with the **Assembly (ekklesia)** of all adult male citizens; the **Council of 500
(boule)**, chosen by lot, set the agenda; magistracies and the large popular juries
were filled by lot and paid (under Pericles), so that even the poor could serve.
This was, CWH IV notes, "the most democratic form of government thus far seen in
world history" — yet it rested on the exclusion of women, resident foreigners
(**metics**), and a vast enslaved population (see [[slavery]]), and Athenian women were
among the most secluded in Greece.

## Empire and the Peloponnesian War

The anti-Persian **Delian League** (478 BCE) became an Athenian empire: allied tribute
funded the fleet, the poor rowers' wages, and the **Periclean building program** (the
Parthenon and the Acropolis). Athenian expansion frightened Sparta into the
**[[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]] (431–404 BCE)**. Pericles' strategy of
withdrawing behind the walls turned catastrophic when **plague (430 BCE)** killed
perhaps a quarter of the population, Pericles among them. After Sicily (415–413) and
decades of attrition, Athens surrendered in 404 BCE.

## The Cultural Apex

In these same decades Athens hosted the tragedians **Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides**;
the comedian **Aristophanes**; the historians **[[herodotus|Herodotus]]** and
**[[thucydides|Thucydides]]**; and **Socrates** — whose trial and execution (399 BCE) by
the restored democracy crystallized the conflict between the philosopher and the city,
and seeded **Plato** and **Aristotle** (see [[axial-age]]).

## Significance

Athens demonstrates both the creative power of participatory citizenship and its
compatibility with empire and slavery — a permanent ambiguity at the root of the
Western political tradition. Its art and thought were carried across Afro-Eurasia by
the [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]] world and revived in the [[byzantine-empire]],
Islamic, and Renaissance traditions.

## Historiography

The sources are extraordinary but Athenocentric and elite-male: **[[thucydides]]**
shaped the master narrative of the war; comedy and oratory supply social texture.
Debates: how "democratic" the democracy truly was given slavery and the exclusion of
women; whether the empire was predatory or stabilizing; and how far Athens can stand
for "Greece" at all. The volume's choice of Athens as case study is itself a
historiographical statement about the disproportion between a polity's size and its
world-historical footprint.
