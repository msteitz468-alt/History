# Denmark

The earliest-unified and, for much of the Middle Ages, the wealthiest and most
populous of the three Scandinavian kingdoms. Centred on Jutland and the Danish
islands (Zealand, Funen), medieval Denmark also controlled the Scanian provinces
(Skåne, Halland, Blekinge — now southern Sweden) and the contested southern
border zone of Schleswig.

## Significance

Denmark's fertile lowlands and command of the Danish Straits (the Baltic–North
Sea passage) gave it demographic and commercial weight. The
[[harald-bluetooth|Jelling kings]] forged a unified kingdom and adopted
Christianity in the 10th century ([[jelling|Jelling]] monuments); under
[[sweyn-forkbeard|Sweyn Forkbeard]] and [[cnut-the-great|Cnut the Great]] Denmark
briefly headed a [[north-sea-empire|North Sea empire]] including England. In the
late Middle Ages [[margaret-i-of-denmark|Margaret I]] made Denmark the dominant
partner in the [[kalmar-union-1397|Kalmar Union]].

## Medieval Denmark in Detail (Cambridge History)

The largest and most feudalised kingdom: within present-day borders, population
reached a medieval maximum of **600,000–700,000 around 1300** (the whole realm perhaps
over a million), and Denmark **exported grain** throughout the Middle Ages. Early
state-building is visible archaeologically — the **Danevirke** (dendro-dated to 737),
the **Kanhave canal** (726), Godfred's 808 frontier works — before the
[[harald-bluetooth|Jelling dynasty]] consolidated the kingdom c. 940–986. The
**Valdemarian age** (1157–1241) made Denmark a European-type kingdom; **Valdemar II**
conquered Estonia (1219) but lost his German conquests at **Bornhøved (1227)**. The
**Jutland Law (1241)** ("*Meth logh schal land byggæs*") was the key code, though
Denmark uniquely never received a national law. After 1241 came a century of unrest —
the murder of **Erik Klipping (1286)**, the **handfæstning of 1282** (called Denmark's
first constitution), and Erik Menved's ruinous mortgaging of the realm.

Landholding was the most seigneurial in Scandinavia: by the late Middle Ages nobles
and Church held c. **75% of farms**; manorial demesnes ran 15–65 ha while tenants
worked 5–6 ha. The peasantry's legal position deteriorated toward near-serfdom in
Sjælland by the 1490s, and the nobility's **"right to hand and neck"** was ratified in
1523. See [[scandinavian-agrarian-crisis]], [[valdemar-dynasty]],
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003]].

## Denmark, 1520–1870: Reformation, Absolutism, Nation State (CHS Vol. II)

The pre-Reformation church held roughly a third of cultivated land; the
[[reformation-in-scandinavia|Lutheran Reformation]] under
[[christian-iii-of-denmark|Christian III]] (1536) transferred it to the crown, and
**1536 is regarded as "the birth of the modern Danish state"** (L1220). The
"aristocratic regime" of king-and-council governed until the catastrophe of the
[[struggle-for-baltic-supremacy|Baltic wars]] — Roskilde (1658) cost the eastern
provinces — precipitated the 1660 coup and [[scandinavian-absolutism|absolutism]],
constitutionalised in the [[kongelov|Kongelov]] (1665). Sound-Due revenue at times
supplied up to two-thirds of state income (L6140). The eighteenth century brought
[[scandinavian-colonial-empires|colonial trade]] and Caribbean sugar, the *stavnsbånd*
(1733) and its abolition amid the **agricultural reforms** (enclosure 1781,
two-thirds of farms freehold by c. 1810).

The two defining nineteenth-century ruptures were the loss of [[norway]] by the
[[treaty-of-kiel-1814|Treaty of Kiel]] (1814) — reducing Denmark to "a little state
of the second rank" — and the loss of Schleswig-Holstein in **1864**: "Denmark
consisted of 1.8 million people on 43,000 square kilometres. But it was a nation
state" (L40566–70). Between them came compulsory schooling (1814), the advisory
estates (1834–36), and the liberal **June Constitution of 1849** under Frederik VII
(Folketing and Landsting, freedoms of association, speech and belief). The Schleswig
question — the king ruling a Danish duchy and a German one — drove the national
crisis from the 1840 language edict through the First Schleswig War (1848–50) and the
November Constitution (1863) that gave Bismarck his *casus belli*. See
[[scandinavian-nation-state-formation]], [[denmark-norway]],
[[kouri-olesen-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v2-2016]].
