# Jenne-jeno

## Overview

**Jenne-jeno** ("Old Jenné"), on the Inland Niger Delta of Mali, is the wiki's
key example of **heterarchical urbanism** — a true city that arose and flourished
(~250 BCE–1400 CE) **without** kings, citadels, monuments, or despotic
surplus-extraction. It is treated in both [[barker-cwh-v2-2015|CWH Vol. II]]
(Ch. 7, Gallagher & McIntosh) and [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] (Ch. 17,
Roderick McIntosh), and is the anchor case for [[heterarchy]] and the
[[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]] controversy. Pointedly, it is drawn from
sub-Saharan Africa — a region the older "archaic state" models ignored.

## Form: the clustered city

Jenne-jeno is a tell rising ~7 m over the floodplain; with ~70 contemporaneous
satellite tells within a 4 km radius, the urban complex housed tens of thousands.
Crucially it shows **nucleation, scale, density, and internal differentiation**
but **lacks** the classic "archaic urbanism" signatures: no subsistence
intensification under elite control, no clear vertical stratification, no royal
monuments or palace — "cities without citadels" (S. & R. McIntosh).

## Heterarchical organization

The satellite mounds housed **spatially discrete specialist corporate groups** —
distinct fishing styles, aquatic-mammal hunters, weavers, and especially
**iron-smelters** (a dangerous, occult-associated craft pushed out to the
satellites). The urban complex was built on **reciprocal relations among these
horizontally related groups**, not on a single elite extracting agricultural
surplus, and it served a region far larger than its own farming hinterland.
Power was **distributed**, not concentrated — the paradigm of [[heterarchy]].

## Why this place mattered

Jenne-jeno is the volume's proof that the city is not necessarily a creature of
kingship or the state: complex urbanism could be **self-organizing and
non-hierarchical**. It directly refutes the assumption that urbanism requires
despotic agricultural surplus-extraction, and de-centers the Mesopotamian/
[[uruk|Uruk]] template in defining [[what-defines-a-city|what a city is]]. Its
legacy connects to the later Soninke and the wider West African urban tradition
(Ghana/Mali; see [[early-african-food-production]]).

## Jenne-jeno as a trade node (CWH IV)

[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Burstein, Ch 23; Austen, Ch 24) emphasizes Jenne-jeno's
**commercial** role. Founded c. 200 BCE in the exceptionally fertile Inland Niger Delta, it grew
into a **walled city of ~33 hectares** ringed by craft-specialist satellite villages. Its
prosperity lay in its **location**, giving access to the **salt and copper of the Sahara** to
the north and the gold, plant, and animal products of the **forest zone** to the south — making
it the hub of a regional trade network across the Niger basin. Heir to the proto-urban
**Tichitt Tradition** of Mauritania, Jenne-jeno (and its peer Delta cities) supplied the
**urban and commercial foundation** on which the later Sahelian empire of
**[[ghana-empire|Ghana]]** built its wealth, and a southern terminus of the
[[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan trade]].

## The ecological reading (Reader)

**[[reader-africa-biography|Reader]]** grounds the heterarchy in the **ecology of the inland Niger delta**
— a Belgium-sized floodplain of extreme, unpredictable annual flooding. Survival demanded deep
**specialization** (rice-farmers, herders, fishermen, each with secret local knowledge), yet specialization
in an unpredictable environment is fatally fragile unless risk is spread across groups. The paradox —
specialization *vs.* diversification — was resolved socially: distinct ethnic/occupational communities
(Marka, Bambara, Fulani, Bozo) lived intermixed, bound by shared myths and reciprocal obligation into one
economy with **no group ruling the others**. Reader stresses how the site **"unshackled West African
history from the Arab-stimulus paradigm"**: urbanism here was indigenous and a **millennium older** than
the trans-Saharan Muslim trade once credited with creating it (see [[sub-saharan-africa]]).
