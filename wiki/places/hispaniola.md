## Geographic Description

Hispaniola is the second-largest island in the Caribbean, with a total area of approximately 75,940–76,484 km² (shared today by Haiti at 27,560 km² and the Dominican Republic at 48,380 km²). It forms part of the Greater Antilles archipelago, which comprises roughly 89% of the insular land area of the Caribbean.

The island has complex terrain formed by multiple mountain ranges. The southern range includes the Sierra de Baharuco. The dominant northern range — the Cordillera Central — is an extension of mountain ranges originating in northern Central America, running through the Dominican Republic and continuing into the central range of Puerto Rico. The highest mountain in the Antilles, **Pico Duarte** (3,175 meters), is a gold-bearing batholith uplifted into the Cordillera Central of the Dominican Republic. A secondary northern range, the Cordillera Septentrional, runs along the northern coast of the Dominican Republic. Despite volcanic foundations, the island's surfaces are mostly weathered limestone, sedimentary, and metamorphic rock. Hispaniola has a coastline of approximately 3,059 km (combined Haiti and Dominican Republic figures: 1,771 km and 1,288 km respectively).

The island was seen by its indigenous inhabitants as a "giant female beast" oriented with its head to the east — reflecting a cosmological orientation toward the rising sun rather than European north-facing cartography (reported by Spanish friar Ramón Pané).

## Settlement History

Hispaniola was among the first Caribbean islands colonized by humans. The oldest radiocarbon dates from the island come from the **Vignier III site** in Haiti (4510–4350 BCE), making it one of the three earliest-occupied islands alongside Cuba and Puerto Rico. Jamaica, by contrast, shows no evidence of human occupation before approximately AD 500.

The earliest inhabitants used flaked-stone macroblades struck from fine-grained cherts. Under Rouse's taxonomy, these assemblages belong to the Casimiroid series, with Hispaniola-specific assemblages classified as Courian Casimiroid (dated 2000 BCE to AD 145, though dates in the taxonomy were selected to fill empty time slots rather than derived from robust chronology). The **Barrera-Mordán sites** in the south-central Dominican Republic — a cluster of four named sites (Barrera, Mordán, Casimira, Las Alejandrinas) within 100–175 meters of each other — are the most widely recognized and studied flaked-stone assemblage in the Greater Antilles, often used as "type" examples for the Lithic/Archaic Age. These are primarily lithic workshops or quarries.

Ground-stone tools appear in the Archaic Age assemblages of Hispaniola. The **Courian Casimiroid** material culture on the island includes macroblade technology showing substantial continuity with the earlier Lithic Age, along with single- and double-bitted basalt axes (likely used as hoes rather than cutting tools), conical pestles, stone and coral balls, mortars, and complex ornaments. Red ochre was used as body paint and funerary treatment. Veloz Maggiolo (1991) identified three traditions within Hispaniola's Archaic Age: a flaked-stone tradition along the dry south and west coasts; a ground-stone tradition in the southeast showing Banwari Trace affinities; and a widespread hybrid "El Porvenir culture" (typical Courian Casimiroid).

Archaic Age pottery has been documented at several Hispaniolan sites: the Couri 1 site (potsherds noted by Rainey 1941 and Rouse 1941), the Source Pascade II site in Haiti (1090 BCE), the Île à Rat site west of Cap Haïtien, and in the Dominican Republic at El Curro in the Barahona area (1450 BCE) and the El Caimito and Musiépedro sites (as early as the first century BCE). Rouse interpreted El Caimito pottery as evidence of transculturation between the Archaic Age El Porvenir culture and early Ceramic Age Hacienda Grande colonists from Puerto Rico — assigning the makers to the Courian Casimiroid subseries despite their pottery use.

During the pre-contact Ceramic Age, Hispaniola became a major center of cultural development. Three pottery series — Saladoid (which, unlike in Puerto Rico, never fully materialized on Hispaniola as an established tradition), **Meillacoid**, and **Chicoid** — characterized the island's Ceramic Age. Meillacoid communities expanded their influence across Hispaniola and into Cuba, Jamaica, and the Bahama archipelago. Chicoid traditions are associated with the political and cultural florescence linked to **Classic Taíno** communities in eastern Cuba, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico. Las Casas reported that three mutually unintelligible languages were spoken on Hispaniola at contact — "Taíno" (the general lingua franca), "Macorix de Arriba," and "Macorix de Abajo" — indicating a culturally and linguistically diverse population, not the homogeneous Taíno implied by singular naming conventions.

Columbus arrived at Hispaniola on his first voyage (1492), establishing the first permanent European settlement in the Americas at La Navidad. Hispaniola became the administrative center of early Spanish colonial enterprise in the Caribbean.

## Significance

Hispaniola's central importance to Caribbean prehistory derives from multiple factors: its size (second only to Cuba in the Greater Antilles), its early occupation (among the three first-colonized islands), its role as the site of the type-assemblages for Lithic Age flaked-stone complexes (Barrera-Mordán), and its development as the locus of Meillacoid and Chicoid ceramic traditions that expanded across much of the Greater Antilles and Bahamas. The island's linguistic diversity at contact challenges the standard application of "Taíno" as a monolithic cultural label.

Hispaniola's mountain ranges — particularly the gold-bearing Cordillera Central — were the target of early Spanish resource extraction. Its indigenous population was catastrophically reduced in the decades following 1492 through disease, violence, and forced labor.

## Historiography

Hispaniolan sites have been studied primarily by Dominican and Haitian archaeologists, many working within a historical-materialist framework; this work, mostly published in Spanish and French, has been underrepresented in North American syntheses. The Barrera-Mordán sites, despite being the type-assemblage for Lithic Age/Casimiroid culture, have yielded ambiguous dates — the first radiocarbon dates from a single charcoal sample returned three different results from three laboratories, and subsequent dates from the same stratum showed pottery, complicating the clean sequence the site is supposed to represent.

The pre-Columbian population of Hispaniola, and its subsequent collapse, remain major historiographical controversies that will require sources beyond this volume to address adequately.
