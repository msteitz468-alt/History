# Latin America in the Modern Era

## Overview

Julie Charlip's chapter in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] frames Latin America — "a
product of the Iberian conquest of the Americas" — as a region defined by **constant
negotiation between foreign interests and domestic concerns**, complicated by internal
"tensions between democracy and development, equity and efficiency." Its nation
states, "characterized by conflicts of class, race, and ethnicity, have struggled for
identity and sovereignty in a world dominated by foreign powers" — but Latin
Americans have also been "important actors on the world stage," especially as
experimenters with social change (the Mexican Revolution, populism, later socialism).

## History of Control and Settlement

- **The death of the colonies.** Within generations of conquest, American-born
  *criollos* ("Americanos") came to resent peninsular Spaniards; the **Bourbon
  reforms** (the intendancy system, Jesuit expulsion) and mercantilist restraints
  deepened the rift, while Enlightenment ideas and the North American and French
  revolutions supplied a model — tempered by elite fear after the **Haitian
  Revolution** showed slaves overthrowing masters.
- **Independence (1808–1824).** Napoleon's conquest of Iberia triggered the wars;
  after a failed first round (defeated by 1814), a second succeeded, so that by the
  **Battle of Ayacucho (1824)** all of Spanish America was independent except Cuba and
  Puerto Rico (held until 1898). Brazil became an independent monarchy. *This arc now
  has its own dedicated page — see [[spanish-american-independence|The Wars of
  Spanish American Independence]] — built from the colonial side in Burkholder &
  Johnson; the deeper colonial roots are at [[colonial-latin-america]] and
  [[bourbon-reforms]].*
- **The liberal export economy (later 19th c.).** Integration into global trade as a
  primary-products exporter produced new urban working and middle classes — and the
  **US ascendancy**: the **Spanish–American War (1898)** and **Platt Amendment**
  (1903) over Cuba, and the engineered secession of **Panama (1903)** for the canal,
  marked US dominance over Central America and the Caribbean (see [[imperialism]],
  [[united-states]]).
- **The Mexican Revolution (1910–1917).** The most extreme reaction to region-wide
  grievances (middle-class exclusion, *campesino* land hunger, worker conditions):
  Díaz fell (1911); Madero, Carranza, Villa, and Zapata ("Land and Liberty") contended;
  the **1917 Constitution** — predating the Bolshevik consolidation — became "the
  world's most radical charter" (labor rights, land reform). It drew global
  revolutionaries (Mariátegui, Haya de la Torre; refuge for Trotsky) and launched the
  Mexican mural movement (Rivera, Orozco, Siqueiros).
- **The fall of export economies (1914–1945).** The First World War, the **Great
  Depression** (exports fell ~50% in value 1928–32), and the Second World War
  repeatedly shattered Latin America's export markets, exposing the cost of economic
  openness and toppling liberal regimes in favor of **dictators** and then **populists**.
- **Populism and ISI.** Liberalism was eclipsed by **populism** — urban, hierarchical,
  charismatic (Perón and Evita in Argentina, Vargas in Brazil, Cárdenas in Mexico,
  who nationalized oil as PEMEX in 1938) — and by **import-substitution
  industrialization (ISI)**, theorized by Raúl Prebisch and the UN Economic Commission
  for Latin America (see [[industrialization]]).

## Why This Region Mattered

Charlip's Latin America is a laboratory of the **international/domestic matrix**: a
region whose silver once financed Iberian empire, whose primary exports tied it to
volatile global markets, and whose people pioneered influential experiments in
revolution, social rights (the 1917 constitution), economic nationalism, and populist
politics that resonated worldwide.

## How Its Significance Changed

From the silver heartland of the early-modern world economy, through nineteenth-century
independence and dependence on a liberal export model, to twentieth-century
experiments in revolution, populism, and state-led [[industrialization]] — always
under the lengthening shadow of US power.

## Historiography

Charlip writes against both a purely external ("foreign domination") and a purely
internal account, stressing the **two-way** negotiation of global and domestic forces
and Latin American agency. The framework resonates with dependency theory (the
unequal exchange between manufactured-goods and primary-products exporters) while
emphasizing Latin Americans as world-historical actors. The pure-external pole is
stated most forcefully by Eduardo Galeano's [[galeano-open-veins|*Open Veins of Latin
America*]] (1971), the popular manifesto of [[dependency-theory|dependency]]: five
centuries of "open veins" draining the region's wealth to Europe and the US. Charlip's
two-way model is precisely a corrective to that mono-causal externalism — the spectrum
mapped at [[causes-of-latin-american-underdevelopment]]. A distinct
mid-century comparative reading is Louis Hartz's [[fragment-thesis]]
([[hartz-founding-new-societies-1964]]): Latin America as a **feudal
fragment** of pre-Enlightenment Iberia — no Whiggery, hence no
Jacobinism, hence no "normal" socialism; post-Independence caudillos as
projections of a preliberal tradition rather than Bonapartes; Comte
looming larger than Marx as the giveaway. Specialists have long
contested the "feudal" characterization of colonial Latin America, and
the framework competes with (rather than complements) dependency theory
by locating the region's trajectory in imported ideology rather than
external economic extraction.
