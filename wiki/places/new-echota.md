# New Echota

Capital of the [[cherokee-nation]] in the early nineteenth century (northern Georgia); site of the unauthorized **Treaty of New Echota** (December 1835) that furnished the U.S. legal instrument for the [[trail-of-tears-1838]]. Michael D. Green, CHNPA I.1 ch. 8.

## Geographic and political description

New Echota was the seat of Cherokee republican government after the **1827 constitution** and **1828** institutions (judiciary, bicameral legislature, elected Principal Chief). It embodied the public-relations and legal strategy of southern Indian nationalism: prove “civilization” and proclaimed sovereignty to stave off Removal under the [[us-civilization-program]]. The bilingual *Cherokee Phoenix* (from 1828) was published in this political center. Georgia’s extension of state jurisdiction into the Cherokee Nation—and the missionary registration laws that produced **[[worcester-v-georgia-1832]]**—targeted this same territorial/political space.

## Treaty of New Echota (Dec 1835)

At **Elias Boudinot’s home** in New Echota, Boudinot, **John Ridge**, **Major Ridge**, and about **100** like-minded Cherokees signed a removal treaty despite the John Ross government and national policy opposing Removal. Treaty Party rationale (Green): American racism and economic ambition; political order committed to removal; only safety in flight; essence of nation is people not land. Ross petition showed virtually the entire population opposed; signers lacked official standing or authority—**Senate ratified anyway**. Terms: Cherokees out of the eastern nation by spring 1838.

## Why it mattered

New Echota concentrates the paradox of Removal: the capital of the most successfully “civilized” Native republic became the place-name of a treaty that the nation as a whole rejected. After Removal the physical capital was abandoned as the Cherokee political center shifted west to [[indian-territory]]; the treaty’s name remained a wound in Cherokee memory (Treaty Party executions of Boudinot and the Ridges, summer 1839).
