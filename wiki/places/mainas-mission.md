## Overview

The Mainas mission (also spelled Maynas) was the most extensive Jesuit mission enterprise in the western [[amazonia|Amazon]] Basin, and arguably the most consequential colonial institution for the indigenous peoples of the upper Amazon during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. At its height it nominally encompassed some forty reductions and "stretched from the Andes to the Rio Negro and from the upper Napo to the middle Ucayali" — a territory comparable to modern France (Taylor, *CHNPA* Vol. III, Part 2, Ch. 17).

## Foundation and Geography

The Jesuits first established themselves at Borja, on the Marañón River in what is now northern Peru, in 1634, when they were invited by local settlers hoping the missionaries would help control an increasingly rebellious indigenous population. The mission took its name from the Mayna people, one of the first groups the Jesuits attempted to reduce. Over the following century it expanded northeast into the Curaray-Tigre sector, south toward the Ucayali and middle Amazon (the mision baja), and west toward the upper Napo and Pastaza basins.

The mission operated in an environment far more logistically challenging than the famous Jesuit reductions of Paraguay or the Bolivian Mojos. There were never more than a score of Jesuit missionaries at any one time to supervise what Taylor estimates was a theoretical population of up to 200,000 people at the mission's height.

## Internal Organization

Taylor's account draws on the diary of Jesuit M. Uriarte (1750–1768), who administered the Omagua reduction San Joaquín (population ~600). Daily life in the reduction was governed by strict schedules: obligatory 5 am mass, formal salute to the missionary, communal work assignments by sex and age group until 3 pm, evening mass, and strict curfew after dark. The political structure combined indigenous parcialidades (ethnic quarters) — each with a theoretically hereditary capitan — with a Spanish-model hierarchy of governors, alferez, alcaldes, varayos, and fiscales. "Their duties were to report to the missionary morning and evening, to make sure that nobody would fall back into 'antique ways'...to provide manioc beer in moderate quantities to visiting Indians" (Taylor).

The economic life was organized on a principle of specialized local production: each reduction produced a specific trading commodity (curare, salt, particular crafts) monopolized and distributed by the Jesuits. This greatly expanded but also redirected indigenous trade in salt (convoyed by Napo and Bobonaza Quichua) and curare (manufactured by Tikuna and Lamista). The Jesuits also imposed Quichua as the mission lingua franca, "in elaborating a syncretic native culture transmitted by the Quichua spoken in the mission" while retaining some indigenous ornamental and ritual elements for their "decorative or dramatic value."

## Demographic Impact

The Mainas mission produced what Taylor calls "an appalling demographic scar." The primary mechanisms of death were:

1. Forced congregation on major riverside sites, maximizing epidemic exposure
2. Endemic malaria and other diseases linked to unhealthy living conditions
3. Low birth rates and high mortality linked to psychological stress
4. Armed Portuguese slave raids from the east (from c. 1730)

"The population in the mission region fell from some 200,000 in 1550 to 20,000 or 30,000 in 1730" — a 90% reduction in approximately 180 years (Taylor). Many survivors fled continuously, "almost invariably rebelled after a few years of Jesuit regime."

## Ethnic and Cultural Legacy

Despite (and because of) its catastrophic demographic record, the Mainas mission left deep structural traces on surviving upper Amazon societies:

- **Linguistic**: Quichua became the common language of groups along the Napo, Marañón, lower Huallaga, and Ucayali — even among groups that had not originally spoken it.
- **Residential**: the alternation between nucleated habitats and dispersed family hunting lodges (purina) found among modern Quichua-speaking selva societies reflects mission-era adaptation.
- **Kinship**: abandonment of polygyny and adoption of genealogically distant marriage patterns in many groups.
- **Ethnic**: creation of new colonial "tribes" — Lamista, Canelos, Andoas, and post-eighteenth-century Quijos — that served as brokers between the autonomous forest populations and the colonial world.

## Relation to Indigenous Agency

Taylor stresses that indigenous peoples were not passive victims of the mission system. Groups employed multiple strategies: outright flight and resistance; temporary acceptance of reduction to access iron tools, then absconding; adoption of "Zaparo" identity (safer than Candoan identity, which was associated with enslavement risk); and the formation of composite "fugitives' societies" in inaccessible areas. The Waorani (formerly Abijira) exemplify the strategy of "absolute isolation and independence from colonial control at all costs, even if it meant drastic cultural impoverishment." The Lamista and Canelos exemplify the creation of new identities that allowed negotiation with the colonial world on less unfavorable terms.

## End and Aftermath

The Jesuit expulsion from Spanish America in 1768 transferred the Mainas mission to the Franciscans, who proved less capable of maintaining its organization. The eastern expansion had already been stopped by Portuguese pressure. By 1820, the mission structure had effectively dissolved, leaving behind the linguistic, kinship, and settlement patterns described above as lasting legacies of the Jesuit presence. The rubber boom of the late nineteenth century would subject the same populations to a new and even more violent round of exploitation, building on the labor-extraction patterns first developed in the encomienda and mission frameworks.
