# Roman Spain (Hispania)

The Iberian peninsula under Rome — the **first overseas territory** Rome acquired (218 BCE) and,
by the end of the Julio-Claudian period, one of the most thoroughly Romanized and important
regions of the empire. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 13c (**Géza Alföldy**).

## Conquest and the three provinces

After nearly two centuries of intermittent war, only the **north-west** (the Cantabri and
Astures in their mountains) still resisted. **[[augustus|Augustus]]** completed the conquest in
person: residing at **Tarraco** (the new capital of Hispania Citerior), he led the
**[[augustan-imperial-expansion|Cantabrian campaign]]** of 26 BCE, fell ill in 25, and left his
legates and finally **Agrippa** (19 BCE) to break the last resistance. His two years' presence
and his return (16–13 BCE) underline Spain's importance; many Spanish communities enjoyed the
**patronage of leading senators** and the imperial house.

Augustus reorganized Spain (definitively c. 13 BCE) into the **three provinces** that lasted
three centuries: **Baetica** (Andalusia; a senatorial/"public" province under a proconsul, at
Corduba), **Lusitania** (an imperial province under a legate, at Emerita Augusta/Mérida), and
the vast **Hispania Citerior / Tarraconensis** (the rest, the only province with a **legionary
garrison**, under a senior consular legate at Tarraco). The provinces were subdivided into
**conventus iuridici** for jurisdiction and imperial cult.

## The army and roads

At the conquest's height **six or more legions** held Spain; afterwards Augustus left **three**,
deployed in a "bow-shaped formation" facing the Cantabrian mountains — their task to control the
newly subdued tribes. As pacification took hold (roughly the third generation), the garrison was
run down: **IV Macedonica** left for the Rhine (39/43 CE), **X Gemina** for Pannonia (63),
leaving only **VI Victrix**. The army also built the road network — the **Via Augusta** along
the east coast and through the south, and the western *Camino de la Plata* — knitting the
peninsula together.

## Urbanization

Alföldy stresses **urbanization** — the founding of *coloniae* and the granting of municipal
status — as "one of the most effective elements" of Augustan policy, with enormous impact on
society, economy, and culture. From a thin late-Republican base, Caesar and Augustus founded a
dense network of **colonies** (Emerita Augusta, Caesaraugusta, Barcino, Astigi…) and
**municipia** (with Roman or Latin right), concentrated in Baetica, the eastern coast, and
southern Lusitania — but reaching even the Celtiberian interior (Segobriga). The Julio-Claudians
added little; it was the **Flavians** who completed the work with the general grant of the *ius
Latii*.

## Economy, society, and the "colonial elites"

Urban territories embraced the richest farmland (the Baetis/Guadalquivir valley); Spain exported
**corn, wine, oil, garum, and metals** (Strabo: no country rivalled its gold, silver, copper,
and iron). Wealth bred a **local urban elite** (the *ordo decurionum*) — landowners also engaged
in trade and mining — and deepening social differentiation (*ordo* vs. *plebs*; the spread of
slavery and manumission in the towns). Alföldy's central theme is the rise of the **"colonial
elites"** (Syme): the richest provincials entered the **equestrian and senatorial orders**.
Spain produced the first non-Italian senator (Q. Varius, 90 BCE), the first non-Italian consul
(Cornelius Balbus of Gades, 40 BCE), and ultimately the first provincial emperor (**Trajan**);
the **Annaei of Corduba** — Seneca the Elder, **Seneca** the philosopher, and the poet **Lucan**
— and writers like **Columella** and Pomponius Mela exemplify the intellectual flowering of
Baetica. Gades alone had 500 *equites* under Augustus, matched only by Patavium.

## The impact of Romanization — and its limits

Romanization was **profoundly uneven**: the south and east were fully integrated, while the
**interior and north-west** preserved indigenous names, cults, and clan organization
(*gentilitates*, *castella*) well into the Empire. Rome Romanized the backward areas chiefly by
**making the native upper classes see their interest as Rome's** — auxiliary recruitment
(teaching Latin and Roman *mores*), citizenship grants, the first interior *municipia*, and
above all the **imperial cult** (the *concilium provinciae* under the *flamen*, integrating
disparate local elites into a homogeneous provincial aristocracy; the temple of Augustus at
Tarraco, begun AD 15, set the model "at the request of the *Hispani*").

## Significance

Alföldy's conclusion: a century after the conquest, Spain was "mature enough to become the
centre of political power … by virtue of its own efforts." The **revolt of [[year-of-the-four-emperors-69ce|Galba]]** (governor of Hispania Citerior for ten years) in 68 — proclaimed
emperor at Clunia — first demonstrated the ***arcanum imperii***, "that it was possible to
create an emperor outside Rome." That Spain was the country where this truth was first shown was
the measure of its development.
