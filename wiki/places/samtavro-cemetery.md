# Samtavro Cemetery

A large (~20-hectare) multi-period cemetery at **Mtskheta, Georgia**, at the confluence of
the Aragvi and Kura rivers, in the historic heart of the Caucasian **Iberian kingdom**.
It holds roughly **4,400 documented tombs**, in use from the Bronze Age through Late
Antiquity, and has been excavated in four campaigns since 1871 (most recently the
Georgian–Australian GAIA project, 2008–2010). It is the type-site for a study by Antonio
Sagona, Claudia Sagona and Aleksandra Michalewicz (Kim et al. 2017, ch. 9) asking whether
its Late Antique burials record the southward movement of the [[alans|Alans]].

## Why It Mattered

Mtskheta sat where long-distance trade routes intersected: turquoise from Iranian workshops
and lapis lazuli from Afghanistan reached the graves, alongside pearl, coral, garnet and
amber — evidence of sustained commerce passing through Iberia. The cemetery is a dense
archive of the region's population, ritual and belief across the transition to Christianity.

## The Evidence

The study rests on **1,075 tombs** of the Late Roman/Late Antique periods (1st–6th c. CE),
with detailed data for 923 tombs (839 holding human remains, 1,249 individuals) and 5,842+
artefacts, in a relational database. Chronology is reinforced by **33 radiocarbon/AMS
dates** (calibrated to 95.4% probability, OxCal v4.2.3): **tile-lined tombs** dominate from
the 1st to late 4th c. CE, giving way to **stone-cist tombs** (576, the best-represented
type) from the late 4th to mid-6th c.

Distinctive features:
- **Collective burial and secondary ritual** in stone cists — capstones removed, bones
  rearranged (long bones clustered/crossed), sometimes removed as possible relics, then
  resealed; distinguished by the authors from grave-robbing.
- **Deliberate cranial modification** (cone-shaped or flattened skulls) in both males and
  females — unlike Europe, where the practice skews female, arguing against a marriage/
  exogamy transmission model in the southern Caucasus.
- **Ritual breaking of bronze mirrors** before burial — identified as an "enduring Hunnic
  tradition" and the one clear practice shared with the northern Caucasus.
- **Religious pluralism**: Christian *ichthys* rings beside Graeco-Roman intaglios, solar/
  astral imagery, and a possible Zoroastrian fire-room (Area 118).

Only 42.9% of tombs held grave goods; weaponry, horse-trappings and Hunnic metal cauldrons
— common in northern-Caucasus Alanic graves — are **minimal or absent** here, while pins
and glass bottles are abundant. The mixed picture underlies the authors' cautious
conclusion for a "trickling" of Alanic people, not merely fashion, into the region.

## Controversy

Whether the Late Antique population was primarily a **Jewish diaspora** cemetery
(Mgalobishvili and Gagoshidze) or reflects general **religious/ethnic pluralism**
(Sagona et al.) is a live scholarly dispute — see
[[samtavro-cemetery-population|the controversy page]].
