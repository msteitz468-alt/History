# Jēme (Western Thebes)

**Jēme** (Western Thebes / Madīnat Habū) was a large agricultural *kastron* of Upper Egypt in the eighth century—[[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Chris Wickham]]’s second major Egyptian village case, complementing [[aphrodito|Aphroditō]]. Its documentation is essentially **Coptic** and, Wickham notes, still under-analysed relative to tax-focused Aphroditō work despite its richness. Part of: [[roman-egypt]].

## Village government

Unlike Aphroditō, Jēme lacked fiscal autonomy of the autopract type. The major figure was the annual village headman or ***lashane*** (often also called *dioikētēs*; often two–three in office; over thirty named), frequently styled *lamprotatos*. Headmen dated documents, collected tax, judged disputes, and controlled a **village prison**. A permanent elite of ***noc nrōme*** (“big men” / elders) heard cases with them. Outside landowners are unrecorded; alternative patrons were chiefly local abbots. Headmen both represented and policed the community—“they essentially controlled it.”

## Exchange, land, and social rigidity

Jēme shows a lively **house market**, loans, wage labour, and leases—capillary exchange—yet agricultural land sales and disputes are rare, suggesting strong family control over the main wealth base. Society was **less flexible than Aphroditō**: a stable *noc nrōme* hegemony, few external political patrons from Ermont, and relative isolation in Upper Egypt. Smaller owners may have had to accept local hegemony controlled by richer neighbours.

## Abandonment

The settlement appears abandoned by the **end of the eighth century**—possibly a casualty of tax revolts and their repression (no documentation after that terminus). Wickham uses the Aphroditō–Jēme contrast to show that “Egyptian villages” were not a single type: both were active communities with powerful leaders (against Bagnall/Bonneau’s weak-community thesis), but internal structures varied sharply.

## Why it mattered

Jēme is a type-site for coercive village elite control in early Islamic Upper Egypt and for the limits of peasant flexibility under *noc nrōme* hegemony. Related: [[aphrodito]] · [[roman-egypt]] · [[umayyad-caliphate]] · [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005]].
