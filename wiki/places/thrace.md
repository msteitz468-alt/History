# Thrace and the Satrapy of Skudra

**Thrace** — the populous land between the Danube, the Aegean and the Black Sea — became,
after **[[darius-scythian-expedition-513bce|Darius' expedition of c. 513]]**, the
**European satrapy of the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid empire]]**, known from Persian
inscriptions as **Skudra**. It is the only sustained Persian foothold on European soil and
the **land bridge** by which the invasions of Greece were mounted.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 3f, Fol & Hammond) is the source — the two authors
openly disagreeing over the satrapy's extent.

## The making of the satrapy

After Darius withdrew from Scythia, **Megabazus** as "general in Europe" reduced the
**Hellespontine cities** and **Perinthus**, then "every city and tribe" of Thrace, and
**dispossessed the Paeonians** of the Strymon basin (deporting them and giving their lands
to Thracian tribes — a deliberate reshaping of the balance of power). In **492** Mardonius
re-affirmed control of the coast, and the offshore island **Thasos** surrendered. The
satrapy's centre lay in the **Hebrus plain** (perhaps the fortress-rock of Plovdiv /
Philippopolis), with the Aegean garrison at **Doriscus** (still held in the late 460s).

**The two views.** Fol & Hammond present **both** readings of Skudra's extent: Hammond
holds it included **central Thrace** (the rich Hebrus interior with its road-system); the
other view confines it to the **coastal fringe**. The satrapy's three named peoples were
the **Saka paradraya** ("Scythians beyond the sea," probably the Getae), the **Skudra**
(Thracians), and the **"Yauna with the shield-hat"** (the Macedonians).

## Macedon's submission

The advance west of the **Axios** brought **Macedon** into the Persian orbit: c. 510
Megabazus secured the submission of **Amyntas I**, who married his daughter to the Persian
**Bubares**. **Alexander I** (from c. 495) continued the loyal policy and was rewarded with
**Upper Macedonia** — so that, in a striking judgment, "the foundations of the greater
Macedonian state were laid under Persian suzerainty." When the Persians fled in 479
Alexander seized a silver mine in Bisaltia and began his fine coinage.

## Wealth, coinage and Persian influence

Skudra was **rich in silver and gold** (Mt Pangaeum, Bisaltia, Thasos), exceptionally pure
and in demand in Asia and Egypt. Under the Persian peace the region entered a **stable
trade community** linking the Danube, the north Aegean, the Black Sea and Asia Minor;
**"Thraco-Macedonian" silver coinage** (large, mostly letterless denominations, struck by
Thracian and Paeonian tribes from c. 550/530) is found more in **Anatolian and Egyptian
hoards** than in Europe — minted for the market the Persian empire opened, and partly to
pay tribute. The rich princely burials of **Sindos** and **Trebenishte** (gold death-masks,
silver phialai, Illyrian helmets) show the prosperity; and a "flood of **Persian motifs**"
entered **Thracian art** (the Achaemenid-inspired vessels of Kukuva Magila c. 500–475).
Culturally Thrace looked "not to Greece but to Scythia, Asia Minor and Persia."

## Why Thrace mattered

- **Persia's European bridgehead** — the base from which Mardonius (492) and Xerxes (480)
  invaded Greece, and the limit (the Danube, the Peneus and Mt Olympus) of Achaemenid
  Europe.
- **An economic catalyst** — Persian peace and markets quickened a silver-and-trade boom
  whose **decline came with the Persian withdrawal**; the **Odrysians** and the **Argead
  Macedonians** both rose on foundations laid under Persian suzerainty.

## Historiography

Almost wholly dependent on **Herodotus** (who "knew little and cared less" about inland
Thrace, focusing on the Greek cities), checked against **Persian inscriptions** (the Skudra
satrapy lists), **coin hoards**, and the **archaeology** of Sindos and Trebenishte. The
chapter is unusual in airing an **unresolved disagreement between its two authors** over
whether the satrapy held central Thrace or only the coast.
