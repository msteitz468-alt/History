# Roman Syria

The **major military province of the Roman East** — Pompey's creation, holding the largest army
on the empire's most sensitive frontier (facing **Parthia**) and supervising the arc of Near
Eastern client kings. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 14c (**David Kennedy**).

## A Semitic land under a Greco-Roman veneer

Pompey's annexation of the rump of Seleucid Syria in **64 BCE** introduced a Roman rule that
endured **seven centuries**. Kennedy's framing theme: beneath the **Greco-Roman veneer** the
**Semitic** substrate (Aramaic — the "Syrian language" — Phoenician, Arab, and Jewish) "retained
its vitality," to re-emerge under Islam (old place-names resurfacing: Beroea → Halab, Epiphania
→ Hama). The province was a **modest territorial core** — the Greek cities of the northern
**Tetrapolis** (Antioch, Apamea, Seleucia, Laodicea), the southern **Decapolis**, and the
Phoenician coast — set within a far larger geography of desert, oasis (Palmyra), and the trade
routes linking Iran, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and the Mediterranean.

## From triumviral chaos to Augustan order

Pompey's settlement brought "no lasting peace": the disaster of **Crassus** (53 BCE) exposed
Syria to Parthia, and the civil wars brought the rebellion of Caecilius Bassus, Cassius'
exactions, the **Parthian occupation (40–39 BCE)**, and the systematic stripping of the
province's wealth, capped by **Antony's gifts to Cleopatra** (Phoenicia, Chalcis) and the
Donations of Alexandria. Octavian's victory nullified the gifts and opened the way for
**Augustus' reorganization** — keeping Pompey's basic formula (a directly governed core plus a
**network of client states**) but reforming it.

## The great military province

In **27 BCE** Syria's status was "enhanced and fixed" as the most vital eastern province
assigned to Augustus, endowed with the **largest army (3–4 legions, ~40–50,000 men with
auxilia)** and an invariably **consular legate** — "the most powerful and prestigious" command
in Rome's Asiatic provinces. The army answered both **internal security** (banditry, the policing
of turbulent Antioch, the religious festivals, and recurrent intervention in **Judaea**) and the
**external** confrontation with Parthia. Kennedy stresses that, after Crassus, the **Parthian
menace was controlled by diplomacy** — the colourful Euphrates meetings (Gaius Caesar and
Phraates V, AD 1; Vitellius and Artabanus, 37; later Titus) restoring the local perception of
Roman power, with no direct Parthian conflict until Nero's reign.

## Administration and the imperial princes

The governor (resident at **Antioch**, now "effectively capital of the Asiatic East," second
only to Alexandria) was checked by a powerful imperial **procurator** (Josephus sometimes pairs
the two as "governors of Syria"), and by procurators of imperial estates and annexed districts
(Commagene, the Decapolis prefecture, the Roman "resident" perhaps directing Palmyra). Because
Syria's **remoteness** delayed decisions and Rome was wary of over-mighty governors, the East was
repeatedly entrusted to **members of the imperial house** — Agrippa, then Varus and **Gaius
Caesar** (after the deaths of Herod and Obodas), and **Germanicus** (who oversaw the annexation
of Cappadocia and Commagene, dealt with Arabia and Palmyra, and conducted the Parthian diplomacy
— his Mesene contact tied to the stimulation of the **Far Eastern caravan trade**, the first
attested Palmyrene caravans dating to AD 19 and 24).

## Significance

Kennedy's Syria is the **strategic pivot of the Roman East**: the army that faced Parthia, the
governorship that ranked among the empire's greatest prizes, and the framework within which the
client kingdoms (Nabataea, Commagene, Emesa, and Herodian **[[roman-judaea|Judaea]]**) were
supervised and one by one **absorbed**. Beneath the imperial peace, the prosperous, "more
unified culture, essentially Semitic in character but with a Greco-Roman influence" took shape —
the world that would later send Syrian families (the Emesene dynasty) to the heart of the empire.
