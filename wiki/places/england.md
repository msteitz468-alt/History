# England (Anglo-Saxon)

The lowland south and east of [[british-isles|Britain]], the most populous and richly
documented part of the early medieval archipelago. "England" is a teleological term for
this period: it names a political entity that existed only after c.920 and an identity
deliberately constructed by the West-Saxon dynasty. This page covers the Anglo-Saxon
centuries; for the late kingdom see
[[actors/kingdom-of-england-late-anglo-saxon|the kingdom of the English]].

## From Many Kingdoms to One

After the end of Roman Britain, Germanic settlement produced — over generations, not a
single *adventus* — a patchwork of kingdoms; by the seventh century Bede and the charters
record **thirteen** with royal houses (Bernicia, Deira, Mercia, the East Angles, Kent,
the West Saxons, the Hwicce, and more). Hegemony passed from [[places/northumbria|
Northumbria]] (Oswald, Oswiu, Ecgfrith) to [[actors/mercia|Mercia]] (Æthelbald, Offa) to
[[actors/kingdom-of-wessex|Wessex]], which alone survived the Viking Great Army and
absorbed Mercia (920) and the Danelaw to make the **kingdom of the English** — under
Æthelstan (Brunanburh, 937) and, at its apogee, Edgar (the 973 coronation and coinage
reform).

## Government and Localities

Late Anglo-Saxon England was governed at a level of administrative reach unusual in
contemporary Europe: **shires** (subdivided into **hundreds**; in the Danelaw,
**wapentakes** and **ridings** of Scandinavian origin), the **burh** network of mints and
fortified centres, and a standard silver coinage with periodic recoinage. The shire court
remained "a folk court," presided over by ealdorman and bishop, but by the late tenth
century its administration was passing to the **sheriff** (*scirgerefa*). The
**blood-feud** persisted but was increasingly regulated — King Edmund's code let a killer's
kindred opt out of liability — and wergild was paid in graduated instalments to family,
lord, and king.

## Church, Settlement, Society

The [[processes/conversion-of-britain-and-ireland|conversion]] left a network of **minster**
mother-churches, later fragmented as thegns built proprietary churches (a church and
graveyard among the assets that made a *ceorl* a thegn). The tenth-century
[[actors/benedictine-reform|Benedictine reform]] (Dunstan, Æthelwold, Oswald) was
"deeply entwined with royal control." Settlement saw a shift from loose early layouts to
planned thegnly enclosures (Goltho, Raunds) and the tenth-century "village moment" of
manor-and-church nucleation; by 1066 about a tenth of the population lived in towns.

## Rupture and Continuity

Cnut's conquest (1016) was, in Charles Insley's reading, an **ideological** rather than
merely tenurial blow — "the shared consensus … about what the English kingdom was … was
gone" — making the multiple-claimant crisis of **[[norman-conquest-of-england-1066|1066]]**
possible. Yet the kingdom William I passed on "was in many respects still an Anglo-Saxon
kingdom," its administrative structures little changed, the Normans ruling "in a framework
of ideas … established in the tenth century."
