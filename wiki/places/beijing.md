# Beijing (Peking)

Northern capital of the Ming and Qing empires and one of the great planned imperial cities of East Asia. Under the Ming it became the primary political and ritual center after the Yongle emperor relocated the capital from Nanjing.

## Ming Period Role

Under the Ming (after Yongle relocated the capital from Nanjing), Beijing (then Peking) served as the ritual, political, and administrative center of the empire. The Forbidden City (roughly a quarter square mile of glazed-tile buildings) and surrounding Imperial City (over three square miles) housed the emperor, ~20,000 eunuchs, 3,000 palace women, and the central bureaucracy in the early Wan-li years. It was largely self-sufficient under twenty-four eunuch departments with its own supply depots, bakery, armory, printing office, and stables.

The Meridian Gate (Wu-men) was the site of major ceremonies, including war-prisoner disposal (emperor on the tower with noble-titled generals and imperial guards; Minister of Justice reading charges; orders echoed by the battalion) and public beatings of remonstrators (60 or 80 strokes with whipping clubs before crowds). Capital officials (civil on west, military on east) assembled before dawn for morning audiences in embroidered robes and mandarin squares; censors noted any cough, spit, or stumble. The Eastern Depot (with Silk Robe Guard) conducted surveillance and semijudicial operations from within the palace complex, reporting directly to the throne on market prices, city-gate traffic, fires, and overheard conversations—functions that overlapped with and created friction with the censorial-supervising branch of the Civil Service.

Key events from the late 1580s illustrate the city's role in the structural crisis:
- 2 March 1587 false-noon-audience stampede of hundreds of officials toward the palace (no preparations; unprecedented group salary forfeiture imposed on the entire capital bureaucracy).
- 16 May 1585 imperial rain-prayer procession on foot from the palace through the city to the southern Altar of Heaven (~4,000 participants in simple blue robes; residents of Peking witnessed the Son of Heaven in person for what was often a once-in-a-lifetime event; rain followed with hail on 12 June).
- 1587 jurisdictional confrontation over Eastern Depot agents barred from a metropolitan court trial of a Peking magistrate (led to mediated apology but contributed to permanent suspension of audiences).
- Ongoing construction oversight and rituals tied to the emperor's future mausoleum (Ta-yü-shan project involved massive logistics through the capital's networks; Shen Shih-hsing made multiple trips).

The city was placed under curfew and reinforced guards whenever the emperor traveled even short distances to the imperial mausoleums in the suburbs; Prince Lu slept in a gate tower during such absences. Beijing's residents experienced the weight of the "pyramid of the empire" losing balance when its "pinnacle was removed." All claims drawn from [[huang-1587-a-year-of-no-significance-1981]] (Ch. 4 in particular).

The **Forbidden City** (substantially the complex visible today) was constructed under Yongle. It housed the emperor, the inner court, eunuchs, and the immediate apparatus of ritual and decision-making. The Meridian Gate (Wu Men) was the main ceremonial entrance where officials assembled for audiences.

In the late Ming, particularly under the Wanli emperor, the physical and ritual routines of the capital (noon audiences that had not been held for 160 years, the elaborate protocol of the Court of State Ceremonial and Ministry of Rites) became central symbols of the tension between perfect form and effective governance. Much of the action in Huang's *1587* takes place in and around this court setting: the stampede of officials on a false rumor of an audience, the stilted communications between the emperor and Grand Secretary Shen Shixing, and the broader atmosphere of seclusion and ritual.

## Strategic and Symbolic Importance

Beijing was both the northern anchor of the empire (facing the Mongol/Manchu frontier) and the ritual heart of the Ming state. Control of Beijing conferred legitimacy; its loss in 1644 marked the effective end of Ming rule even before the last claimant was eliminated.
