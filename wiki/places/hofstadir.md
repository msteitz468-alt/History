# Hofstaðir

## Overview

Hofstaðir is a Viking Age site in northern Iceland on the upper Laxá River near Lake Mývatn. The name translates as "temple farm." It features an exceptionally large long hall (four times the floor space of the average Viking Age dwelling) and associated structures, with rich midden deposits. The site rose to temporary prominence c. A.D. 950–1000 and was abandoned around A.D. 1000 with evidence of ritual closure.

## History of Settlement and Control

Excavations indicate Hofstaðir was not among the very first farms settled in the area (soon after A.D. 871). Its peak reflects the dynamic and competitive nature of chieftain politics during the Settlement Age (landnám). It functioned as a full-scale working farm and chieftain's seat. After abandonment the farm was relocated 150 meters across the home field, where a medium-sized turf farm and small Christian chapel continued through the medieval period. The Viking Age ruins were never reoccupied.

## Major Features and Evidence

- Large hall with outbuildings, including an early timber-framed structure later used as hay store, and a well-built outbuilding interpreted as a communal privy.
- Exceptionally large and deep pit house (area G) filled with stratified animal bone, artifacts, smithing slag, charcoal, and fire-cracked stones. Evidence of iron smelting from local bog ore.
- Floor deposits sampled for soil micromorphology, botanical, and insect remains.
- Postholes with deliberately placed bones and artifacts at bases.
- Artifacts modest: small silver jewelry fragments, bronze ring pin, glass beads, worn knife blades, composite bone combs; iron nails common.
- Tephra under walls and radiocarbon dates support the chronology.

## Ritual and Abandonment

When the hall was abandoned c. A.D. 1000, two sheep were beheaded and bodies thrown onto the floor (heads landing nearby). Skulls of cattle, sheep, goat, and pigs (previously displayed on the roof) were thrown into wall collapse or a pit. A sheep skull was placed in each doorway. This suggests ritual associations alongside its function as a chiefly farm.

## Environmental and Economic Context

The site contributes to understanding rapid landscape change and economic adaptation in the first generations of landnám. Zooarchaeological and other data from the region (including Hofstaðir) show shifts from diverse domestic stock (cattle, pigs prominent early) toward sheep-dominated strategies in response to deforestation and soil erosion.

## Historiography

Nineteenth-century antiquarians and early excavators (Bruun and Jónsson 1908) interpreted the large hall as a pagan temple with sacred chamber. Olaf Olsen (1960s) argued against specialized pagan temples, favoring multifunctional "temple farms." Modern interdisciplinary project (from 1992, directors Adolf Friðriksson and Orri Vésteinsson) combines zooarchaeology, archaeobotany, tephrochronology, geoarchaeology, and micromorphology, confirming a chiefly farm with ritual elements. The site is no longer seen as a dedicated temple but as a high-status farm with complex social and ritual functions.
