# Ona Nagast (Beta Giyorgis)

**Beta Giyorgis** is a hill immediately adjacent to the later city of [[aksum-city|Aksum]] in west-central Tigray. Its summit and slopes preserve the principal excavated evidence for the so-called "Proto-Aksumite" phase of northern Horn development (last four centuries BC) and for earlier first-millennium-BC occupation. The site of **Ona Nagast** on Beta Giyorgis is the focus of intensive archaeological research directed by Rodolfo Fattovich and Kathryn Bard.

## Archaeological Sequence

### First-millennium-BC non-élite occupation (Kidane Mehret / Beta Giyorgis lower levels)

A large farming settlement investigated in the deeper levels at nearby **Kidane Mehret** (Phillipson 2000b) and on Beta Giyorgis hill (Bard et al. 1997, 2000) was broadly contemporaneous with the Ancient Ona sites near Asmara and, like them, lacks evidence for significant southern Arabian connections. Pottery, flaked-stone tools, and subsistence remains (cattle, sheep/goats, emmer wheat, barley) reflect continuity with earlier indigenous traditions.

### Proto-Aksumite phase (last four centuries BC)

The most intensively studied phase at Beta Giyorgis is what Fattovich and Bard designate "Proto-Aksumite," characterised by:

- **Funerary architecture**: a massive built platform in which rough-hewn stelae marked shaft graves; later graves were more elaborate, stelae larger and more neatly shaped. The funerary area extended over some 10 hectares, with over one hundred stelae visible on the surface.
- **Pottery**: a homogeneous assemblage displaying "limited continuity" with earlier periods, contrasting with the more continuous sequence at other sites.
- **Lithics**: continued and refined the earlier trend, with finely produced crescentic microliths apparently deposited as grave goods.
- **Imports**: glass beads (comparanda dating after mid-2nd c. BC); possibly a copper-alloy seal; a pottery beaker fragment of Meroitic affinity; an iron arrowhead and two model axes. Wine amphorae fragments appear to have originated in the western Mediterranean, possibly via a Nile Valley route.
- **Subsistence**: barley, emmer wheat, a little free-threshing wheat, possibly teff; cattle and ovicaprids; dogs.

Phillipson's assessment: "There can be little doubt that the occupation of Beta Giyorgis at this time belongs firmly within a local tradition, traits suggesting foreign connections being few... the affinities of imported artefacts — and, it is suggested, other features — are predominantly with the Nile Valley rather than with southern Arabia."

### Continuity into the Aksumite period

Beta Giyorgis saw continued occupation into the first millennium AD (Aksumite period), discussed in Phillipson's later chapters. The "Proto-Aksumite" community here was "directly ancestral to those who were subsequently responsible for the establishment and expansion of the Aksumite kingdom."

## Significance

Beta Giyorgis is the single most important site for understanding the transition from pre-Aksumite to Aksumite civilisation. It demonstrates that:
1. The Aksumite state had local, indigenous roots rather than being imposed by external colonisers.
2. The formative community's external contacts were with the Nile Valley, not southern Arabia.
3. The transition to Aksum proper involved a geographic shift (to the adjacent valley) rather than a population replacement.

## Terminology

"Ona Nagast" (*ona* = Tigrinya for "ruin"; *nagast* = "of kings") is one of several named locations on the hill. "Beta Giyorgis" (Church of St George) is the modern toponym. The site is sometimes cited as "Beta Giyorgis hill" to distinguish the archaeological focus from the town of Aksum itself.
