# Makassar (Gowa-Tallo Sultanate)

Makassar, on the southwest coast of **South Sulawesi**, was the great free port and
political capital of the **Gowa-Tallo dual kingdom** — the dominant power of the
eastern Indonesian archipelago in the early seventeenth century and the principal
non-Dutch outlet for Moluccan spices until its defeat in the Makassar War of
1666–69.

## A Dual Kingdom, Not Merely an Entrepôt

Tristan Mostert's chapter in [[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]]
argues explicitly against the dominant **"bandar" framing** (associated with
Anthony Reid's *Age of Commerce*) that reduces Makassar to a cosmopolitan open
port defined chiefly by its contrast with VOC monopoly. Makassar "was not merely a
trade entrepôt; it was also the main political centre of South Sulawesi" — the seat
of an **expanding regional empire** whose rivalry with [[ternate|Ternate]] preceded
and structured the later VOC conflict.

Gowa began as a wet-rice agricultural principality. In the 1530s it defeated and
then **united with Tallo** — not as overlord and vassal but as a dual kingdom:
sources spoke of "only one people but two rulers." **Karaeng Tunipalangga**
(r. 1547–65) subjugated most west-coast South Sulawesi polities, institutionalised
the office of *shahbandar*, and gave the Malay trading community written guarantees
of their rights. **Karaeng Tunijalloq** (r. 1565–90) extended diplomatic ties
across Mataram, Timor, Banjarmasin, and Johor. By the early 1600s the senior
**Karaeng Matoaya of Tallo** (r. 1593–1623) governed as tutor to the young Gowan
sultan **Ala'uddin**.

## Islamisation and Expansion

The rulers of Gowa and Tallo **converted to Islam c. 1605**. Jacobus Noorduyn read
this as genuine theological inquiry rather than political opportunism. Conversion
triggered the **"Wars of Islamisation"** — Gowa-Tallo militarily converted the Bugis
states of the east coast, completing the campaign by about 1610 — and opened new
Islamic diplomatic networks reaching to Mecca. Its diplomatic connections "reached
from the Moluccas to Mecca, including ties with the English and Danish East India
Companies and the Portuguese." Backed by a formidable army, extensive coastal
fortifications, and an "impressive navy," Gowa-Tallo expanded toward Sumbawa,
Buton, and the Manado region — colliding directly with the Ternatan sphere.

## Rice Port, Then Spice Port

In its early dealings with Europeans (c. 1601–1610s) Makassar was "mainly a rice
port, where various traders, including Europeans, would buy rice before going to the
Spice Islands to buy spices there directly." The Dutch kept a lodge from c. 1605
(closed 1615); the [[english-east-india-company|EIC]] opened a factory in **July
1613**, using it the same way. Portuguese private traders from Melaka came annually
in junks but held no fortification.

Mostert's central revisionist claim inverts the conventional decline narrative:
**VOC exclusion policy *caused* Makassar's rise as a spice market**, "as they
inadvertently caused all the forces opposing the Dutch monopolies to concentrate
there" — at least into the early 1640s.

## The Break with the VOC (1615–1616)

The VOC's [[dutch-east-india-company|Governor-General]] Reynst had already decided
to close the Makassar lodge and demand the sultan cease trading with the Moluccas —
driven by monopoly policy, not Spanish aggression — when Sultan Ala'uddin gave his
famous reply asserting the freedom of the seas: *"God made the land and the sea,
divided the land among the people, and gave the sea in common… If you would do it,
you would take the bread out of the mouths of people. I am a poor king."* (This is
a striking indigenous statement of *mare liberum* against monopoly; compare
[[interpolity-law]].)

- **Enkhuizen incident (April 1615):** A Spanish flotilla attacked the VOC ship
  *Enkhuizen* off Makassar; as the Dutch abandoned their lodge the crew tried to
  seize local dignitaries who had come aboard. Nine died, including a son of the
  sultan; two royal kinsmen were captured.
- **Eendracht massacre (December 1616):** The VOC ship *Eendracht* — earlier the
  first European ship to touch the west coast of Australia — arrived unaware of the
  rupture. The sultan let the first landing party leave but warned them off; when a
  second launch was sent ashore, "the Makasars shot on sight and then stormed the
  boat, killing all its sixteen crew members." A "cold war" followed.

Open war came in 1633–37 and the 1650s–60s, ending in the **Makassar War of
1666–69**, when the VOC allied with the Bugis nobleman **Arung Palakka** to break
Gowan political power over the entrepôt.

## Historiography

Mostert corrects Reid's open-port model and engages Jennifer Gaynor's
*Intertidal History* (2016), which re-centres the Makassar-Ternate rivalry; he
agrees in substance but argues Gaynor "overstates it" by separating European and
local interests that "had become inextricably intertwined." Leonard Andaya's
*The Heritage of Arung Palakka* (1981) supplies the South Sulawesi dimension.
Geoffrey Parker's *Military Revolution* uses Makassar's fortifications as a case
study of Asian military adaptation. The chapter rests on VOC and EIC archives,
Gowa court chronicles (Cummings, *A Chain of Kings*), and Drake's account.
