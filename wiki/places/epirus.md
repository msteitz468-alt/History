# Epirus and the South-West Balkans

**Epirus** — the mountainous north-western corner of the Greek world, spanning modern
NW Greece and southern Albania — and the wider South-West Balkans were, in the Early
Iron Age, a world of **transhumant shepherd peoples** whose tumulus-burying tribes
Hammond identifies as the **reservoir from which the Greek-speaking migrants flowed
south** into the Aegean. [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] (Ch. 15, N. G.
L. Hammond) is its fullest treatment in the collection.

## A Land Shaped by Mountains and Flocks

Hammond opens with an exacting geography: two great watersheds divide the South-West
Balkans, crossed only by a handful of difficult passes (Kačanik, Preševo, the Zygos
pass over North Pindus) that snow blocks for months. The terrain dictated the
economy — **stock-raising and large-scale transhumance**, flocks moving between
coastal winter pastures (the plains of Albania, Epirus, Thessaly, Macedonia) and the
high summer pastures of the lakeland and Pindus. There the scattered shepherd peoples
**congregated and formed their centres**; there, too, their raids on the rich southern
plains originated (Hammond cites Benjamin of Tudela's 12th-century CE account of
"Wallachian" mountaineers descending on Thessaly as a timeless pattern). Politically
the watershed kept west (Epirus) and east (Macedonia) apart — when powers arose they
expanded outward, into Italy or Asia, not across the divide.

## The Peoples

Three groups share the region in Hammond's account:
- the **Epirotes** proper — the Greek-speaking tribes of the west (**Molossians,
  Thesprotians, Chaonians**), in whose land stood the ancient oracle of **Dodona**;
- the **[[illyrians|Illyrians]]** — non-Greek peoples pressing down from the north and
  west, leaving Glasinac-type bronzes and "typically Illyrian" dedications even at
  Dodona;
- the **[[phrygians|Phrygians/Brygi]]** — whose "Phrygian period" dominated west
  Macedonia c. 1150–800 BCE on the strength of metallurgical skill before receding.

The whole zone is archaeologically defined by **tumulus burial** (the double tumuli of
Vodhine and Pazhok, the cemeteries of the upper Drin), which Hammond reads as the
signature of these northern peoples and connects to the heroic burials of Homer.

## Cradle of the Greek Migrations

Epirus is, in Hammond's long-argued thesis, the **springboard of the
[[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Dorian and north-west Greek migrations]]**. The Dorians
learned the seamanship for their crossing of the Gulf of Corinth "in the Gulf of
Ambracia and Thesprotia"; the **Thessalians** crossed into Thessaly **from
Thesprotia**; and the pressure that set the chain in motion came from
[[phrygians|Brygian/Lausitz]] peoples moving through Macedonia and Epirus around
1200–1150 BCE. The tumulus-burial culture and dialect geography together, for Hammond,
locate the homeland of the West-Greek dialects in this region — though this is exactly
the link that the **absence of a clear archaeological "Dorian horizon"** in the south
leaves disputed (see [[dorian-invasion-1120bce]]).

## Why This Place Mattered

- A **demographic reservoir**: the pastoral over-spill of the South-West Balkans
  repeatedly fed migrations and raids into the richer south.
- A **religious centre**: **Dodona**, the oldest Greek oracle, drew dedications from
  Greek and Illyrian alike.
- A **frontier of Greek and non-Greek**: the meeting zone of Epirote Greeks,
  Illyrians, and Macedonians, whose later kingdoms (Molossian Epirus under Pyrrhus;
  Macedon) grew from these Iron Age roots.

## Historiography

Hammond's chapter is the mature statement of a scholar who spent his career on this
landscape, and it is **migration- and ethnicity-centred**, correlating tumulus types
and pottery with named peoples and tying them to the literary tradition of the Greek
*Völkerwanderung*. The method is powerful but contested: the "pots-and-peoples"
inference and the firm ethnic labels are the very things later prehistory questions
(compare [[indo-european-origins]]). The factual core — a pastoral, tumulus-building
South-West Balkan world in lively contact with the Aegean — is secure. The region is
moderately well covered in the collection through the Albanian and Greek excavations
Hammond synthesizes.
