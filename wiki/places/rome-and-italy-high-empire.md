# Rome and Italy under the High Empire

The **imperial heartland** in the age of the Flavians to the Severans (AD 70–192): the **city of
Rome** as the wonder-capital of the world, and **Italy** as its privileged but increasingly
"provincialized" backdrop. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 12 (**Nicholas
Purcell**), whose theme is the **ambiguous interdependence** of empire and heartland — and the
"national vacuum" the imperial institution maintained at the centre. Continues the city analysed in
[[augustan-art-and-the-city-of-rome]] and the [[romanization-of-italy|Romanization of Italy]].

## Rome the city of wonders

Purcell reads Rome through the *thaumata* — the "wonders" (curiosities, art, exotic beasts,
heirlooms of conquest) that visitors like **Pausanias** and **Galen** came to see. Rome's prestige
rested on the **blessings of Nature** (the *laudes Italiae* of Virgil, the elder Pliny: Italy as
holy, therapeutic, a "spa for the whole of humanity") fused with the **booty of the world**. But
visitors came not as tourists: they came for **power** — petition, patronage, influence — so the
"world of wonders" was an expression of the whole complex of **imperial exactions** (tribute, tax,
the *annona*). In the world of the **Second Sophistic**, Rome was also the cultural centre, "the
norm and exemplar of Antonine cities."

## The emperor's stage — and his absence

The emperors made Rome and Italy **spectacular to approach** (Trajan's harbours at Ostia/Portus,
Centumcellae, Ancona; the road investments along the Via Appia and Via Domitiana) as a worthy
stage for the ceremonial ***adventus* and *profectio***, institutionalized under the Flavians. The
city's skyline filled with **dynastic monuments** — the Colosseum, the Forum and Column of Trajan,
the Pantheon, the temples of the imperial cult, the Palatine *domus Augustana* rivalling the
Capitol — as religion shifted toward an **all-embracing inclusiveness** centred on the
ubiquitously-worshipped emperor (Marcus' summoning of "priests from all quarters"; the inclusion of
Serapis, Sol, even Christianity, in a city "big enough for them too"). Yet the actual imperial
**presence grew transient** (Hadrian, Severus residing only briefly, often in the suburban *horti*
and Campagna villas): the saturation of the **imperial image** ("portraits of you at every
counter") and the all-pervading ideology mattered more than the body.

## The city populace and its violence

Rome's vast, mobile, "over-peopled" population — fed by the *annona* and the **ideology of the
*commoda*** (the perks of the world-capital: grain, games, baths, the rebuilt Circus Maximus) —
made the city "one of its most volatile and violent places." The **Circus** was the chief arena of
the informal politics of emperor and people; debt, rents, the property market, gambling, and the
**Antonine Plague** (worst at Rome, 165–180) fed unrest. As emperors absented themselves, the
**prefects** (praetorian, urban, *vigiles*) gained power — and risk (the falls of Cleander and
others). Purcell insists the golden age "was not golden for all," recovering the view "from
beneath" in Justin Martyr's account of arbitrary city prefects.

## Italy: privilege, recruitment, and "provincialization"

Italy kept its **ideological value as Roman homeland** and its privileges (no direct tribute, the
*ius Italicum*) even as the spread of citizenship made the model "obsolete" — by now "far more
*cives Romani* outside Italy than within." Its specialness showed in:

- **Calibrated military recruitment**: Italian youth served as a mark of favour in the **Rome
  garrison** (*vigiles*, urban cohorts, praetorians, later the *equites singulares* and II
  Parthica at Albanum), a "carefully calibrated indicator of hierarchy" rather than a military need
  — and a reminder that Italy's peace was a **privilege and fragile** (when the praetorians were
  abolished, "the youth of Italy took to banditry"; Bulla Felix roamed the countryside).
- **The "provincialization of Italy"**: the *curatores rei publicae* (the Caere dossier), Hadrian's
  four ***legati* to govern Italy** (abolished by Pius, *restitutor Italiae*; revived by Marcus as
  *iuridici*; Severan *correctores*). Purcell's key argument: these were **not "administration" in
  the modern sense** but instruments of **status politics** — regulating the promotion and
  competition of the senatorial/equestrian elite (*quo latius senatorias tenderet dignitates*) — as
  the old Italian municipal aristocracy congealed into a landed **"squirearchy"** of intermarried
  senatorial, equestrian, and decurional families that could even produce emperors.
- **The *alimenta***: child-support schemes funded by mortgaging local land (the tables of Veleia
  and Ligures Baebiani), expressing the ideology of *Italia restituta* and the maintenance of the
  free population — but, Purcell argues, **better read as identity-management** (reassuring an Italy
  now ruled by a "citizen of Italica") than as proof of demographic or economic crisis.

## The economy and the "decline of Italy" debate

Purcell engages the major historiographical controversy. The Flavian–Trajanic countryside shows a
**burgeoning villa economy** (cash crops, fine wheat and wine, high-quality meat — Settefinestre
the type-site), yet by the end of the Antonine age **many villas are dead** (Settefinestre among
them). The standard reading — a **second-century crisis of Italy** (slavery's "comeuppance,"
provincial competition, the plague, soil exhaustion, decadence) — Purcell treats sceptically: the
disappearance of *conspicuous* luxury villas reflects **elite whim and the mobility of capital**
more than terminal indigence; the *alimenta* and debt-cancellations addressed people "of moderate
status," not the destitute; the plague's role as a "turning-point" is **not yet demonstrable**.
Italy was a **heavily monetized** land tied to the all-consuming Roman market (the later *annona*
division into *Italia annonaria* and *suburbicaria* enshrining an old historical geography).

## Significance: an empire without a nation

Purcell's conclusion (via Coleridge): "Rome under Trajan was an empire without a nation." The
period's reciprocal imitation of capital and provinces **levelled the difference** — Rome's
streets, like Ostia's, came to resemble "a thousand others"; the idea of Rome (Dea Roma worshipped
*at* Rome in Hadrian's temple) became **abstracted and detachable** from the place. The cultural
fluidity of figures like the bishop Hippolytus or Iulius Africanus shows a heartland whose
imperial character subsisted **without ethnic foundations** — the "national vacuum" that the vast
power of the imperial institution maintained at the centre, making national identity "redundant —
for a time."
