# Guerrero Region

## Overview

**Pre-Hispanic Guerrero** belongs to the [[mesoamerica-culture-area|Mesoamerican culture area]] through village maize–squash–bean agriculture, interregional interaction networks, periods of socio-political complexity, and shared ideology — though not developed to Basin of Mexico or Maya Lowlands intensity (Paradis, EW2001 “Guerrero Region”). The modern state (created 1843) does **not** match pre-Hispanic boundaries; frontiers show linguistic ties to Tarascan, Matlatzinca, and Mixtec spheres. There was **no internal state-level centralization** of Basin/Michoacán/Oaxaca type, but **Teopantecuanitlán** (Middle Formative) and **La Organera–Xochipala** (Classic) show complex hierarchies; the Postclassic saw [[aztec-empire|Mexica]] and [[purepecha|Purépecha]] penetration.

## Environment and resources

Mountain-dominated: Neovolcanic Axis (N), Río Balsas Depression, Sierra Madre del Sur, narrow Pacific coast. Balsas basin ~**105,900 km²**; mean altitude ~500 m; generally <800 mm rain (June–Oct); semiarid. Agriculture scarce and conflict-prone; **cotton and cacao** thrived in the hot dry climate, making Guerrero an important Aztec tribute source. Resources: gold, copper, serpentine, quartz, amethyst, possibly jade, salt, marine shell, forest products. Mountains caused isolation; the Balsas dendritic system provided the main NE–SW circulation axis (Paradis, EW2001).

## Early occupation and Olmec horizon

Once thought a candidate for very early agriculture; **Puerto Marqués** shell midden c. 3000 B.C. has **Pox pottery** among Mesoamerica’s oldest, but first well-documented settlement is ~**1500 B.C.** Most regions then had agricultural hamlets/villages with pottery showing local individualism plus pan-Mesoamerican **Olmec style** (except Lower Balsas). Olmec-style objects appear **earlier in some Guerrero contexts than in Veracruz–Tabasco lowlands**: Amuco Abelino figurines 1530±230 to 1220±110 B.C. (Paradis).

**Teopantecuanitlán** (Mezcala region; Martínez Donjuán): complex civic-religious center with Olmec style, residential zones, and irrigation canals; occupation from Early Formative (14C ~1423–1393 B.C.) continuously toward Spanish conquest; Olmec style in first two construction stages. Related Formative Guerrero claims (encyclopedia minors): earliest known Mesoamerican corbel vault, ball court, irrigation canals, Olmec-style sculptures — 1400–500 B.C. range.

### Olmec-Guerrero painted caves

The **earliest sophisticated painted art** known in Mesoamerica occurs in **Juxtlahuaca** and **Oxtotitlán** caves (Grove, EW2001). They share motifs with Middle Formative Gulf Olmec monuments/portable art (stylistic date to that horizon). Juxtlahuaca deep-cave murals: multicolored-huipil personage and jaguar/serpent; Oxtotitlán (~30 km N): polychrome personage with owl headdress on earth-monster face above south grotto; black human-jaguar “copulation” scene in north grotto. Other Guerrero caves (Cacahuaziqui, Texayac) have simpler rock art.

**Olmec-Guerrero style** (EW2001): geographically specific Olmec-style variant best known for polychrome cave/rockshelter murals; flourished Middle Formative (900–400 B.C.) around middle Balsas in north-central Guerrero, possibly originating ~600 years earlier. Heartland forms influenced it, but vitality and craftsmanship were local; art for chiefly rulers; contributed corn-curl headdress and other elements to the broader Olmec-style corpus.

## Mezcala florescence and Classic

After the Olmec Horizon, regional florescence **500 B.C.–A.D. 200** preceded Classic expression. Mezcala region peaked: denser settlements, agricultural intensification, craft production; urban agglomerations **Ahuinahuac, Apantipan, Tehuacaltipa** with temple mounds, ball courts, residential complexes; alluvial terraces held villages, hamlets, and workshops producing **Mezcala polished stone objects** (raw material, pre-forms, waste — finished products often not in situ) (Paradis).

**Mezcala style** (Covarrubias identification): small polished-stone human/animal masks, figurines, ornaments, miniature temples. Tradition likely started in the Olmec Horizon (~1200 B.C.), bloomed Late Formative–Classic; heirlooms later dedicated in Mexica **Templo Mayor**. First in-context Guerrero finds (1989+) at Ahuinahuac (seven objects under residential floors, **500–200 B.C.** radiocarbon); also Cuetlajuchitlán, El Mirador, La Organera-Mezcala, La Organera-Xochipala (Middle/Late Formative to A.D. 900) (Paradis; Reilly, EW2001 “Mezcala Lapidary Style”).

**Teotihuacan** influence: intrusive/late in Medio Balsas and Costa Grande; pervasive and reciprocal in Mezcala (annular bases, figurines, talud-tablero; Mezcala objects and Granular ware at Teotihuacan — possibly resident Mezcala artisans). Middle Balsas became a strategic buffer only when Postclassic Aztec and Tarascan frontiers closed (Paradis).

## Postclassic: Coixca, Tarascans, Mexica

From ~A.D. 900: new linguistic groups and fifteenth-century imperial conquest. **Coixca** (Nahua) settled northern Guerrero/Mezcala ~A.D. **1250**. **Tarascans** conquered Tierra Caliente ~**1370** (Middle Balsas dividing Tarascans from Cuitlatecos). Under **Itzcoatl** (1427–1440) Mexica conquered northern Guerrero (Chontales west, Coixca east); Motecuhzoma Ilhuicamina put down rebellions and completed Tlahuica/Coixca/Chontal conquests; expansion continued under Tizoc (Tetela del Río, Tlapan) and **Ahuitzotl** (Zacatula on Costa Grande). Tribute provinces **Tlachco, Tepecoacuilco, Tlalcozauhtitlán, Quiauteopan, Tlapan, Cihuatlan** yielded beans, corn, cotton, cacao, honey, copal, gold, copper ornaments, varnished gourds, green-stone necklaces. Political motive included defending the SW border against Tarascans (Paradis).

**Barnard Site** (coastal Guerrero): large Postclassic site known for abundant metalwork — bells, gold sheets, bronze celt, fishhook, silver-alloy plaques; deliberate bronze alloying and cold working; slag suggests local manufacture; obsidian lip plugs/ear spools link Michoacán Postclassic; polychrome/Yestla-Naranjo-like pottery; some silver purity and bronze techniques suggest possible Peruvian influences (Brush, EW2001).

## Why it mattered

Guerrero is a **precocious Olmec-horizon highland/coastal zone**, a Classic producer of Mezcala lapidary art later re-sacralized by the Mexica, a cotton–cacao–metal tribute frontier, and the geographic hinge between [[purepecha|Purépecha]] and Aztec imperial systems along the Balsas. It shows that Mesoamerican complexity was multi-centric long before the Aztec provinces were drawn on maps.

## Historiography

Louise I. Paradis’s EW2001 “Guerrero Region” is the controlling synthesis; David C. Grove on caves; F. Kent Reilly on Mezcala style; Ellen Brush on Barnard metallurgy. Research remains thinner than Oaxaca or the Basin of Mexico; looting of Mezcala stonework for the art market and sparse excavation coverage remain structural limits. Debate continues over how “Olmec” early Guerrero material is (local vitality vs heartland diffusion) and over the scale of Teotihuacan presence outside Mezcala.
