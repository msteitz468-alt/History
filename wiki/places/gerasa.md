## Overview

Gerasa (modern Jerash, Jordan) was a member of the [[decapolis|Decapolis]] with a
Bronze/Iron Age precursor settlement, refounded at the end of the 4th century BC as a
Macedonian-veteran colony under the dynastic name "Antioch-of-the-Chrysoroas," reputedly
by Perdiccas, one of Alexander the Great's generals. Its great building boom — the
Cardo, theatre, propylaea, Temple of Artemis, hippodrome, a second theatre, and Hadrian's
Arch — came mainly after the mid-2nd century AD. The city never finished expanding to
meet its own monumental ambitions: the Temple of Artemis was left unfinished, and the
city's built-up area never reached as far as the Arch of Hadrian.

## Ball's Paradigm Case: "Veneer Over Native Form" (THESIS)

Gerasa is, in Ball's reading, the paradigm case for his broader argument about the Roman
Near East. He argues Jerash's overwhelming outward "Roman-ness" is "merely a veneer that
hides an essentially oriental city built with familiar oriental architectural forms," and
stresses that despite its monumental appearance Jerash was in reality "nothing more than
… a minor provincial town" — smaller than Scythopolis and bypassed by the region's main
highway, the Via Trajana between Bosra and Aqaba.

Ball explains the city's outsized monumental ambition as a conscious, ultimately
unfulfilled attempt to live up to its dynastic name "Antioch(-on-the-Chrysoroas)," in
imitation of the real [[antioch|Antioch-on-the-Orontes]] — reproducing its colonnaded
streets, tetrapylons, a nymphaeum, and a processional way to Birkatayn mirroring
Antioch's sanctuary suburb of Daphne. Ball's summary line: Jerash ended up "not so much
Semitic 'Gerasa' and even less Hellenistic 'Antioch-on-the-Chrysoroas,' but merely
'Antioch-not-on-the-Orontes.'"

This is Ball's interpretive position; mainstream scholarship (e.g. Fergus Millar, *The
Roman Near East*, 1993) reads the Roman Near East, including cities like Jerash, as more
substantively Graeco-Roman in character — see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]] for the
debate.

## Architecture

The Artemis Propylaeum at Jerash is called by Ball "surely one of the most elaborate in
Classical architecture," comparable to the great propylaeum at [[baalbek|Baalbek]]. Ball
cites Jerash, alongside [[palmyra|Palmyra]] and [[apamea|Apamea]], as evidence that the
Roman East's colonnaded streets functioned as oriental market bazaars (Arabic *suq*)
rather than as monumentalized fora, pointing to excavated shop-rows along Jerash's
colonnades. Jerash's Severan-era circular plaza, at the head of a monumental quarter, is
cited by Ball as the direct architectural parallel invoked in his reading of
[[lepcis-magna|Lepcis Magna]]'s Severan building program — a form he says occurs nowhere
in the Roman West outside that Lepcis instance.
