# Gundeshapur (Beth Lapat)

A great **Sasanian city of Khuzistan** (~10 km south of Dizful) founded or refounded by
**[[shapur-i|Shapur I]]** (r. 240–272 CE) — the Sasanian afterlife of the old
[[elam|Elamite]] lowland, and a key node in the transmission of Greek learning into the
Iranian and later Islamic world. Its Middle Persian name means roughly **"(the) better
(is the) Antioch of Shapur"** (*Weh-Andiyok-Shabuhr*); its Syriac name was **Beth/Be
Lapat**, which Theodore bar Koni glosses as the **"city of the Elamites"** — a direct
verbal link to Elam's deep past. [[potts-archaeology-of-elam-2016|Potts]] uses it to close
the long arc of his book: Elam as a name and place persists into Late Antiquity.

## Roman captives and a planned city

After Shapur I's victories over Rome — defeating Gordian III (244 CE) and **capturing the
emperor Valerian (260 CE)** — he settled **deported Roman prisoners and the population of
captured Antioch** in Khuzistan. Gundeshapur was laid out on a **Hippodamian grid**
("almost certainly the work of Antiochan planners") and spread over 3 km; **captive Roman
engineers built** the great dam/weir at Shushtar on the Karun and the weir on the Diz.
Deportees and Greek physicians from Antioch established a **medical academy** that endured
into the 9th century; the captured Valerian is said to have died here, and **Mani** met his
death here in 276 CE. Gundeshapur served as a Sasanian **royal winter residence** from
Shapur I onward and, at the synod of 410 CE, became the Nestorian **metropolitan see of
Beth Huzaye** — "likely the second-largest city in the Sasanian empire."

## Why it mattered

Gundeshapur embodies the Sasanian policy of **forced resettlement as state-building**:
mixing deported captives with local families "so that through intermarriage the latter
should become tied down." As a hub of Greek-Syriac-Persian medicine and learning, it
became a celebrated transmitter of classical knowledge — a reputation later magnified
(sometimes anachronistically) in Islamic-era memory. Its survival contrasts with nearby
**[[susa|Susa]]**, destroyed by Shapur II ~339 CE and re-christened *Shush i er-kar*
("Susa made Iranian"); the regional administrative capital shifted to **Eran-xwarrah-
Shapur** (Ivan-e Kerkha).

## Ball: a "two-way" city (2000)

Warwick Ball's *[[ball-rome-in-the-east-2000|Rome in the East]]* uses Gundeshapur as an emblem
of his [[orientalization-of-rome|orientalization]] thesis working in reverse — a Sasanian city
built by deported Roman captives (after the [[capture-of-valerian-260|capture of Valerian, 260]])
that became a great Christian and academic centre, refuge for the philosophers of Athens after
Justinian closed the Academy (529), and a channel for Greek learning into the Islamic world. He
stresses (as Potts does above) the Roman engineering — the surviving **Band-i Qaysar** at
Shushtar — and the relative religious and intellectual tolerance he finds "not known in the Roman
world at the time."
