# Tiwanaku

## Overview

**Tiwanaku**, in the high Andean altiplano near Lake Titicaca (~3,850 m), was the
great city of the southern Andes during the **Middle Horizon (~500–1000 CE)**.
It is the case study of [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] Ch. 11 (John Janusek)
and the volume's paradigm of **"distributed urbanism" and animate landscapes** —
a city best understood not as a bounded product but as a long-term regional
*process* of cyclical gathering. At its height (~700–900 CE) it covered 4–6 km²
with ~10,000–20,000 permanent residents, but its population "pulsated":
people came periodically from across the basin for ritual events.

## Form

Two monumental campuses gave the city a NE–SW ritual axis: the
**Kalasasaya–Akapana** complex (with the early Sunken Temple and the
terraced-mountain Akapana pyramid) and **Pumapunku**. Stone **portals** (the
"Gateway of the Sun"/Solar Portal) and carved **monoliths** — understood not as
images but as *embodied ancestors* — structured movement and ritual. A
Kalasasaya "balcony wall" of pillars functioned as a solar **observatory**
marking solstices.

## Distributed urbanism and animate landscapes

Janusek's two key arguments:
- **Distributed urbanism.** Tiwanaku grew out of a Late Formative (200 BCE–500 CE)
  network of ritual centers — especially **Khonkho Wankane**, its paired center
  across the Kimsachata range, sharing longitude, monolith style, and spring/stone
  sources. Most affiliates did *not* live at the centers; they gathered cyclically.
  The city was "a center of social, political, and ceremonial convergence."
- **Animate landscapes.** Andean urbanism sanctified natural forces (mountains,
  water, celestial cycles) and built them into the city. Authority crystallized
  as the mediation of these relations — a "pragmatic, if profoundly ritualized,
  animistic ecology," *not* epiphenomenal religion.

## Blurring urban and rural

Within and around the city, **raised fields** and sunken basins (**qochas**)
produced food; a perimeter canal made the core a "sacred island" that was also
productive. The Western urban–rural and urban–nature dichotomies did not hold —
the city "showcased production and celebrated water." ~40–50% of the periphery at
any time was abandoned/secondary deposition (ritual-meal "ash pits"), reflecting
the pulsating, ceremonial character. See [[ruralization]].

## Why this place mattered

Tiwanaku shows urbanism as a **regional, processual, ceremonial** phenomenon, a
direct counter to static trait-list definitions of the city ([[what-defines-a-city]]).
Janusek explicitly **sidesteps** minimum-size/monument criteria. It is the
Andean comparand to gridded [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] and Mesopotamian
[[uruk|Uruk]].

## Fagan & Durrani (2020): scale, the staff god, and the drought collapse

Fagan and Durrani's *World Prehistory* (10th ed., 2020) dates Tiwanaku's Titicaca Basin
florescence to c. 600–1000 CE (with growth from c. 450 CE), reaching a population of roughly
**20,000** at its height. Central to the site's iconography is the **Kalasasaya** enclosure and
the "staff god"/**Viracocha** doorway carving. They attribute the city's collapse **after 1200
CE** possibly to a prolonged drought, evidenced in regional ice-core records — consistent with
the drought-driven collapse pattern documented elsewhere in the Andes for this period (compare
[[moche|Moche]] and [[wari|Wari]]). See [[andean-state-formation]] for the wider Middle
Horizon integration these two centers represent.
