# Potosí and Colonial Silver

**Potosí** — the *Cerro Rico* ("rich mountain") of Upper Peru (modern Bolivia),
discovered in **1545** — was the **richest silver strike in the Americas** and the
beating heart of the colonial economy. With the Mexican mines, it turned
[[colonial-latin-america|colonial Latin America]] into the engine of a
[[global-silver-trade|global bullion economy]], and it stands as the supreme
symbol of both colonial wealth and colonial human cost. By 1620 the city had
~120,000 people — one of the largest in the world.

## The mountain and the metal

Silver demanded far more processing than gold. Early miners used **smelting**, but
Potosí sat above 15,000 feet, beyond the timberline, and fuel was scarce. The
transformative technology was **mercury amalgamation** — the *patio* process in New
Spain (from the 1550s), and a tank variant in Peru (from **1571**) — which mixed
finely ground ore with salt/copper pyrite and **mercury** to extract silver from
even poor ores. This made mercury supply the master variable of production. Peru's
needs were met by the Crown's **mercury mine at Huancavelica** (discovered early
1560s, ~220 km from Lima) — itself worked by a deadly mita — supplemented by
Almadén in Spain. Because the Crown set mercury's price for fiscal, not market,
reasons, **mercury policy effectively governed silver output**: high prices
depressed it; the late-eighteenth-century price cuts let it boom.

## The mita

The labor came from the **[[mita-and-andean-reciprocity|mita]]**, the Andean
rotational labor draft that Viceroy **Francisco de Toledo** reorganized in **1572**
to deliver ~**13,000–13,500 Indian men a year** to Potosí (plus ~2,000 to
Huancavelica), drawn from sixteen highland provinces — one-seventh of the adult
males serving one year in seven. The work — hauling 100-pound ore loads up rickety
ladders by candlelight, later amid blasting, breathing mercury vapor — killed and
maimed. Its social effect was demographic upheaval: to escape it, families fled
their *ayllus*, swelling the ranks of *forasteros* and *yanaconas*; by the 1680s the
Indian population of Upper Peru had **halved** since 1570. What began as Andean
reciprocity had become, in the authors' words, "the forced transfer of wealth from
poor Indians to rich Spaniards." Yet mitayos worked **alongside free wage laborers
and slaves** — by the early seventeenth century just over half of Potosí's ~9,900
workers were wage laborers, the skilled *barreteros* among them increasingly
*castas*.

## Production and the wider economy

Registered silver output rose to a peak in the early seventeenth century, then
declined in Peru while Mexico — which by the eighteenth century far outproduced
Peru — boomed (Mexican registered output 1801–10 topped 200 million pesos). Silver
revenue (taxes, the mercury monopoly, the *quinto* royal fifth) was so central that
the Crown bent the whole economy to sustain mining — guaranteeing its cheap labor
at the expense of other sectors. Potosí's silver flowed out through the
[[global-silver-trade|fleet system]] (via Lima → Panama → the Atlantic) and, as
contraband, down to Buenos Aires; vast amounts also crossed the Pacific on the
**Manila galleon** to China, where the silver **peso** became a near-currency.

## Environmental and human legacy

Potosí scarred its landscape — pits, tunnels, tailings, deforestation, and the
**poisonous mercury** that leached into soil and water and sickened the refinery
workers; thirty-plus dams powered seventy mills by the early seventeenth century.
"*Vale un Potosí*" ("worth a Potosí") entered Spanish as a synonym for fabulous
riches; the mountain remains, for historians, the emblem of the silver economy's
double character — global wealth built on Andean death.
