# Babylon

**Babylon** (Akkadian *Bābilim*, "Gate of the God") was the great city of southern
Mesopotamia, on the **Euphrates** in the heart of Babylonia, and the capital of the
[[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]]. Under **[[nebuchadnezzar-ii|
Nebuchadnezzar II]]** it became the largest and most splendid city of its age — the
"wonder-city" of double walls, the Ishtar Gate, the Processional Way, the ziggurat
**Etemenanki**, and the legendary **hanging gardens**. It is the centre of
[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] Ch. 27 (Wiseman) and the
civilization chapters 28a–c.

## Geographic description

Babylon straddled the Euphrates in the alluvial plain of southern Iraq, dependent
wholly on **irrigation** and commanding the river artery that linked the Persian
Gulf to the Mediterranean overland routes. The city's vulnerability — and Babylonia's
in general — lay in its **lack of natural frontiers** ([[babylonia-iron-age|see
Brinkman on Babylonia's geographical openness]]); its strength lay in agricultural
fertility (barley and dates) and its position on long-distance trade. A westward
shift of the Euphrates over the first millennium reshaped settlement in the region.

## History of control

A minor town in the third millennium, Babylon rose under **Hammurabi** (18th c. BCE)
to head the first Babylonian empire, then passed through Kassite, Assyrian, and
Chaldaean control. Throughout the [[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian]] century it was
the great prize and problem of Mesopotamian politics — a holy city the Assyrian kings
alternately patronized and brutalized, climaxing in **[[sennacherib|Sennacherib's]]
sacrilegious destruction of the city in 689**, which [[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]] then
rebuilt. With [[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar's]] accession in **626** it became the
capital of an independent empire and "the uncontested successor of Nineveh."

## Nebuchadnezzar's city

Wiseman's Ch. 27 devotes a full section to the **rebuilding of Babylon**, which the
building inscriptions describe in lavish detail. Nebuchadnezzar:

- repaired the **Euphrates quay and river wall** to receive building supplies and
  protect the city from the annual inundation;
- built his **Southern Palace** ("a building for the admiration of my people, a place
  of union for the land, and the seat of my royal authority"), roofed in Lebanese
  cedar and adorned with enamelled brick, gold, silver, bronze, and ivory;
- raised on the riverside terraces the structure traditionally identified as the
  **"hanging gardens,"** said to remind his **Median queen** of her mountains;
- paved the sacred **Processional Street (*Ay-ibur-shabu*, "May the arrogant not
  flourish")** from the akītu house through the **Ishtar Gate** to Etemenanki;
- completed the ziggurat **Etemenanki** and gilded the **Esagila** temple of Marduk
  and the shrines of Zarpanitu and Nabu;
- girdled the city with the **double walls** Imgur-Enlil and Nemet-Enlil, an outer
  eastern enclosure, and the cross-country **"Median Wall"** (Xenophon's) between
  Euphrates and Tigris against Media and Elam.

The scale was staggering — an estimated **164 million bricks for the outer northern
wall alone**. Similar works went up in **Borsippa** (then almost a suburb), Sippar,
Ur, Uruk, and other cult-cities. Babylon under the Chaldaeans was a **cosmopolitan
metropolis** of deported and immigrant communities — Egyptians, Jews, Phoenician
shipwrights, Ionian Greeks, "fugitives from Media" — settled in ethnic quarters
around the city and at Nippur and Uruk (see [[neo-babylonian-civilization]]).

## Why Babylon mattered

- **Religious capital** — seat of **Marduk** (Bel), whose **akītu (New Year) festival**
  annually re-legitimated kingship; the city's *kidinnūtu* privileges (tax-and-corvée
  exemption) gave its citizens real political weight, which kings ignored at their
  peril (the lesson of [[nabonidus|Nabonidus]]).
- **Centre of learning** — home to the astronomical archive whose **Diaries** ran
  from c. 750 BCE to 75 CE, and the cradle of the mathematical astronomy that
  descended, via Hipparchus and Ptolemy, into all later Western exact science
  ([[neo-babylonian-civilization]]).
- **Imperial prize** — its fall to [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]] in **539**
  ([[fall-of-babylon-539bce]]) handed the entire Near East to Persia; **Alexander**
  later died here (323), and intended it as his capital, before Seleucia drained it
  and it decayed into the "great desert" of Strabo and Pliny.

## Babylon and the Successors (CAH VII.1)

Babylon was a strategic prize in the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|wars of the Successors]].
[[walbank-cah-v7-1-1984|CAH VII.1]] (Will, ch. 2) records that
[[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus I]] received Babylonia at Triparadisus (321), was driven
out by [[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus]] (315), and **recovered the city in 312** —
the event from which the **[[seleucid-era]]**, the first continuous year-count in history,
was reckoned. A Babylonian cuneiform chronicle records the see-saw of these years.
Seleucus then made Babylonia the springboard for his reconquest of the Iranian upper
satrapies. But his foundation of **Seleuceia-on-the-Tigris** as a new royal city
progressively drained Babylon's population and commerce, beginning the long decline that
left it the deserted ruin of Strabo and Pliny — even as its priest-astronomers kept
writing cuneiform for three more centuries.

## Changing significance

Babylon's significance shifted from **Bronze Age royal capital** to **Iron Age holy
city and imperial bone of contention** to **Chaldaean world-capital** to **provincial
seat under Persia and the Seleucids**, finally abandoned in the early centuries CE —
yet its astronomers kept writing cuneiform in its ruins for three centuries after
its civil death.
