# San Lorenzo (Olmec)

## Overview

**San Lorenzo** (San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán) was the first major political center of the
[[olmec|Olmec]] Gulf Coast heartland. In Richard A. Diehl’s Lowe-based scheme
([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1 Ch. 4]]), **Initial Olmec**
(c. 1200–900 BCE) coincides with San Lorenzo’s growth, its emergence as an important
cultural power in southern Mesoamerica, and its decline at the beginning of the first
millennium BCE—after which [[la-venta|La Venta]] replaced it as Intermediate Olmec center.

## Pre-Olmec occupation

Diehl places San Lorenzo **Ojochi-phase** remains at 1500–1350 BCE among the region’s early
sedentary ceramic villages (parallel to Santa Luisa Raudal/Almería, 1700–1350 BCE). Pottery
resembles Pacific Coast Chiapas/Guatemala assemblages; early forms include jars, shallow
bowls, and tecomates. Pre-Olmec Gulf societies are inferred as egalitarian tribes; inequalities
of wealth and status appear with Olmec culture after **1500 BCE** at San Lorenzo, La Venta,
and elsewhere in southern Veracruz–Tabasco.

## Elite town traits

Among elite Olmec towns, only San Lorenzo, La Venta, Laguna de los Cerros, and
[[tres-zapotes|Tres Zapotes]] have been studied archaeologically. Such towns were larger than
subordinate villages; they contained stone sculptures; temples and elite houses on earth
mounds; and buried caches and offerings of discarded monuments, greenstone celts, figurines,
and other rare objects. Commoners continued in small villages much as before.

## Sculpture, labor, and exchange

Magnificently carved basalt sculptures are the Olmec hallmark. Stone from the **Tuxtla
Mountains**, some weighing many tons, was transported over distances of more than 150 km by
water and land. The corpus includes colossal human heads that may be portraits of rulers,
huge flat-topped “altars” or thrones, and realistically depicted life-size human figures. Few
monuments have been found in original placement.

Olmec elites mobilized large work parties—perhaps numbering in the thousands—to construct
earthen mounds, transport basalt, and carry out other community-scale projects. They
controlled long-distance exchange supplying obsidian, serpentine, jadeite, pottery, feathers,
iron-ore ornaments, and other luxuries from central Mexico, Morelos, Guerrero, Oaxaca, coastal
Chiapas, and Guatemala. Trace-element analysis shows San Lorenzo importing central-highland
obsidian by ~1200 BCE (Cobean et al. 1971; Adams intro, CHNPA II.1). Ixtepeque (highland
Guatemala/El Salvador) obsidian also reaches San Lorenzo at this horizon via Pacific–Tehuantepec
routes (Sheets, CHNPA II.1 Ch. 9).

Nearby **El Manatí** wet-spring offerings (after ~1000 BCE) preserve wooden busts with faces
stylistically identical to Olmec stone sculpture, wooden scepters, greenstone celts, and rubber
balls probably used on “Palangana”-style ballcourts enclosed by four earth mounds—like the
mound complex identified as a ballcourt at San Lorenzo itself (Diehl).

## Decline and succession

Initial Olmec San Lorenzo declined as the first millennium BCE began; La Venta replaced it in
the Intermediate Olmec phase. Earlier syntheses (Cyphers via Snow et al.; Mann) place peak
population near 12,000, colossal heads as ruler portraits, and multi-causal decline (competition,
environment, labor, ideology) with population exodus as La Venta rose—details complementary to
Diehl’s political-succession framework.

## Historiography

Coe and Diehl’s *In the Land of the Olmec* (1980) remains foundational excavation publication;
Adams (CHNPA intro) notes that Type:variety ceramic description was less consistently applied
in the west than in the Maya area, citing older ambiguous ware descriptions for relatively
recent San Lorenzo work. Laguna de los Cerros is named as a peer elite town still poorly
excavated relative to San Lorenzo and La Venta.

## Why it mattered

Type site for early hereditary rulership, monumental basalt display, and long-distance
prestige-goods exchange in Mesoamerica; its decline precedes La Venta’s florescence. Central
to debates over Olmec “mother culture” primacy versus multi-regional Formative interaction
([[olmec-mother-vs-sister-culture]]).
