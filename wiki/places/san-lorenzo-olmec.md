# San Lorenzo (Olmec)

## Overview

**San Lorenzo** (San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán) was the first major political center of the
[[olmec|Olmec]] Gulf Coast heartland. In Richard A. Diehl’s Lowe-based scheme
([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1 Ch. 4]]), **Initial Olmec**
(c. 1200–900 BCE) coincides with San Lorenzo’s growth, its emergence as an important
cultural power in southern Mesoamerica, and its decline at the beginning of the first
millennium BCE—after which [[la-venta|La Venta]] replaced it as Intermediate Olmec center.

## Pre-Olmec occupation

Diehl places San Lorenzo **Ojochi-phase** remains at 1500–1350 BCE among the region’s early
sedentary ceramic villages (parallel to Santa Luisa Raudal/Almería, 1700–1350 BCE). Pottery
resembles Pacific Coast Chiapas/Guatemala assemblages; early forms include jars, shallow
bowls, and tecomates. Pre-Olmec Gulf societies are inferred as egalitarian tribes; inequalities
of wealth and status appear with Olmec culture after **1500 BCE** at San Lorenzo, La Venta,
and elsewhere in southern Veracruz–Tabasco.

## Elite town traits

Among elite Olmec towns, only San Lorenzo, La Venta, Laguna de los Cerros, and
[[tres-zapotes|Tres Zapotes]] have been studied archaeologically. Such towns were larger than
subordinate villages; they contained stone sculptures; temples and elite houses on earth
mounds; and buried caches and offerings of discarded monuments, greenstone celts, figurines,
and other rare objects. Commoners continued in small villages much as before.

## Sculpture, labor, and exchange

Magnificently carved basalt sculptures are the Olmec hallmark. Stone from the **Tuxtla
Mountains**, some weighing many tons, was transported over distances of more than 150 km by
water and land. The corpus includes colossal human heads that may be portraits of rulers,
huge flat-topped “altars” or thrones, and realistically depicted life-size human figures. Few
monuments have been found in original placement.

Olmec elites mobilized large work parties—perhaps numbering in the thousands—to construct
earthen mounds, transport basalt, and carry out other community-scale projects. They
controlled long-distance exchange supplying obsidian, serpentine, jadeite, pottery, feathers,
iron-ore ornaments, and other luxuries from central Mexico, Morelos, Guerrero, Oaxaca, coastal
Chiapas, and Guatemala. Trace-element analysis shows San Lorenzo importing central-highland
obsidian by ~1200 BCE (Cobean et al. 1971; Adams intro, CHNPA II.1). Ixtepeque (highland
Guatemala/El Salvador) obsidian also reaches San Lorenzo at this horizon via Pacific–Tehuantepec
routes (Sheets, CHNPA II.1 Ch. 9).

Nearby **El Manatí** wet-spring offerings (after ~1000 BCE) preserve wooden busts with faces
stylistically identical to Olmec stone sculpture, wooden scepters, greenstone celts, and rubber
balls probably used on “Palangana”-style ballcourts enclosed by four earth mounds—like the
mound complex identified as a ballcourt at San Lorenzo itself (Diehl).

## Decline and succession

Initial Olmec San Lorenzo declined as the first millennium BCE began; La Venta replaced it in
the Intermediate Olmec phase. Earlier syntheses (Cyphers via Snow et al.; Mann) place peak
population near 12,000, colossal heads as ruler portraits, and multi-causal decline (competition,
environment, labor, ideology) with population exodus as La Venta rose—details complementary to
Diehl’s political-succession framework.

## Historiography

Coe and Diehl’s *In the Land of the Olmec* (1980) remains foundational excavation publication;
Adams (CHNPA intro) notes that Type:variety ceramic description was less consistently applied
in the west than in the Maya area, citing older ambiguous ware descriptions for relatively
recent San Lorenzo work. Laguna de los Cerros is named as a peer elite town still poorly
excavated relative to San Lorenzo and La Venta.

## Evans & Webster 2001 (Grove; Cyphers; Clark)

Encyclopedia entries supply the densest site-level synthesis now on this page. **Name:** Stirling’s
**San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán** covers three sites—**San Lorenzo** (major Olmec), **Tenochtitlán**
(Olmec under Late Classic–Early Postclassic cover), and **Potrero Nuevo** lands—focusing on San
Lorenzo. This is an **Olmec** place name only; it must not be confused with Aztec **Tenochtitlan**
in the Basin of Mexico.

### Scale, chronology, and setting (Ann Cyphers)

San Lorenzo is the **earliest Olmec regional center** in the Gulf Coast heartland; over 3–4 centuries
it attained complexity greater than any contemporary Mesoamerican site, **possibly achieving
incipient statehood**. Occupation before **1300 B.C.** to ~**A.D. 1100**; apogee Early Formative
**1200–900 B.C.**; decline Middle–Late Formative **900–100 B.C.**; no Early Classic; small Late
Classic from ~**A.D. 900** through Early Postclassic to **A.D. 1100**.

**Setting:** Coatzacoalcos drainage, southern Veracruz; Formative San Lorenzo was an “island”
between two now-inactive river branches, strategically controlling confluences and trade.
Subsistence: maize–beans–squash plus fish, turtle, deer, peccary; no ceramic griddles → maize
possibly as tamales; domestic dog eaten. Architecture: monumental modification of a natural hill
(terraces, fill, retaining walls)—not a giant effigy mound but among the largest Early Formative
earthen monuments, possibly the first “sacred mountain”; low platforms and causeways. Early
Formative surface remains to the 30 m contour ≈ **690 ha (7 km²)**; deep burial of Early Formative
habitation means surface mounds cannot estimate population. Once considered a fairly small
**53-hectare** site, San Lorenzo actually occupies **>690 ha** with significant internal
differentiation—largest and most complex Early Formative site in Mesoamerica. Elite lived on the
heights with basalt steps, columns, and aqueducts from distant Tuxtlas.

**Domestic:** terrace dwellings of wattle-and-daub or mud brick, **>100 m²** household clusters,
tamped dirt floors with stone, palm thatch, outdoor hearths. **Craft:** elite-controlled monument
recycling/recarving; workshop with **>6 metric tons** of multi-drilled ilmenite blocks;
greenstone/obsidian at household level. Obsidian sources: Guadalupe Victoria (Puebla), Otumba
(Central Mexico), El Chayal (Guatemala). Elite trade: magnetite mirrors (Oaxaca), Motagua jade,
Tuxtlas basalt, Chiapas/Oaxaca ilmenite, mica, serpentine. Hinterland hierarchical settlement with
San Lorenzo as regional center regulating import/export. Stones up to **25 metric tons** moved from
the Tuxtlas. **Ten colossal heads** portray rulers. Florescence to **900 B.C.** then decline;
[[la-venta|La Venta]] rose afterward; proposed causes include internal revolt, invasion, internal
polity failure, or Tuxtlas tectonic/environmental disruption of trade and crops (Cyphers, EW2001).

### Grove and Clark regional framing

David C. Grove (EW2001, “Olmec Culture”): Olmec monuments occur at nearly two dozen sites from
Papaloapan (S Veracruz) to Chontalpa (W Tabasco); **>80% of ~200 known monuments** at La Venta,
San Lorenzo, and Laguna de los Cerros—major centers spaced across different ecological settings
(basalt/Tuxtlas access; river trade; coastal salt/tar/estuaries). Coe & Diehl 1966–68 phases:
Ojochi **1500–1350**, Bajío **1350–1250**, Chicharras **1250–1150**, San Lorenzo **1150–900**,
Nacaste **900–700**, Palangana **600–400 B.C.**—first stratigraphic pre-Olmec to Olmec evolution.
Greatest importance San Lorenzo phase (Early Formative); public architecture earlier than Middle
Formative La Venta; Cyphers: houselike buildings on low platforms; basalt U-shaped “drain” lines
reinterpreted as aqueducts (possibly sacred). Nearby [[el-manati|El Manatí]] spring deposits
preserve wooden busts, jade, and rubber balls (Olmec ball game). Rulership is the principal focus
of monumental art: colossal heads (individual ruler portraits by headgear motifs); monolithic
“altars”/thrones (ruler in niche = cave to underworld); most monuments mutilated—now usually
ritual/sacred destruction (not invasion/revolution); some recycled by recarving.

John E. Clark (EW2001 Formative; South Region): Olmec civilization arose by **1150 B.C.** with San
Lorenzo as most spectacular capital—massive earth-moving, multi-ton basalt sculpture, possibly
**>10,000** inhabitants. By **1000 B.C.** San Lorenzo was “Mesoamerica’s first city,” ~**690 ha**
at apogee c. **1100–950 B.C.**; four- or five-tier settlement hierarchy (colossal heads only at San
Lorenzo) plus Soconusco influence suggest **state-level society**. Occupied by sedentary
agriculturalists centuries before becoming a major center; ceramic connections to Soconusco.
**Cessation** of San Lorenzo as regional center and of its elite as cultural brokers at about
**900 B.C.** marks end of Early Formative and start of Middle Formative (**900–400 B.C.**);
significant Olmec developments continued at La Venta and Laguna de los Cerros, but their
pan-Mesoamerican influence is less apparent than San Lorenzo’s (*followed_by:* La Venta succession).
Credible (but unproven) model: Olmec as Mixe-Zoquean speakers related to Chiapas coast/interior.

## Why it mattered

Type site for early hereditary rulership, monumental basalt display, and long-distance
prestige-goods exchange in Mesoamerica; its decline precedes La Venta’s florescence. Central
to debates over Olmec “mother culture” primacy versus multi-regional Formative interaction
([[olmec-mother-vs-sister-culture]]). EW2001 Cyphers entry fixes the **>690 ha** Early Formative
footprint and “incipient statehood” claim as current specialist framing.
