# Avaris (Tell el-Dab'a)

The **capital of the [[hyksos|Hyksos]]** in the eastern Nile Delta — Egyptian
**Hatwaret**, modern Tell el-Dab'a — the great Asiatic stronghold of the
[[second-intermediate-period|Second Intermediate Period]]. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. II, Hayes).

## The Hyksos capital

About **1720 BCE** Asiatic settlers in the Delta elevated the local god **Seth** of
Avaris to the head of a new principality — the event dated by the "400-Year Stela"
of Ramesses II, set up on the site. **Salitis**, founder of the
[[hyksos|Fifteenth Dynasty]], "rebuilt and fortified it with massive walls" and made
it his summer base, mustering and paying his troops there. Strategically placed on
the **Bubastite branch of the Nile**, controlling the routes into Sinai and Palestine,
Avaris was the hinge of the Hyksos realm — the node through which Asiatic peoples,
goods, and the new military technologies (the chariot, the composite bow) entered
Egypt.

## The fall

Avaris was the final objective of the Theban war of liberation. **Kamose** raided to
its walls; his brother **[[ahmose-i|Ahmose I]]** took it after repeated assaults
(~1550 BCE) — an event recorded with laconic brevity by the soldier **Ahmose son of
Ibana**: "They sacked Avaris; I brought plunder from there: one man and three women;
His Majesty gave them to me as slaves." Its capture ended the
[[second-intermediate-period|Second Intermediate Period]]. Under the
[[new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom]] the site continued in use, and Ramesside Egypt
later built its Delta capital **Pi-Ramesse** nearby.

## Why this place mattered

Avaris is the geographic expression of Egypt's [[second-intermediate-period|turn
toward Asia]]: the point at which the [[middle-bronze-age|Middle Bronze Age]]
Levantine world reached into the Nile valley, the seat from which Asiatics ruled
Egypt, and the prize whose fall launched the imperial New Kingdom. Modern
excavation (Bietak) has revealed a thoroughly **Canaanite/Levantine material
culture** there — Middle Bronze pottery, Asiatic burials with donkeys and weapons,
and Minoan-style frescoes — confirming the contemporary, non-propagandistic picture
of the Hyksos as a Levantine immigrant population.
