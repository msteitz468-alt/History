# Rome (The Archetypal Imperial City)

## Overview

**Rome** is the volume's capstone case — "the archetypal imperial city" (Nicola
Terrenato, Ch. 25 of [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]]), center of a
territorial empire for over five centuries and, at its peak, a city of perhaps a
million people. The chapter is as much **historiographic critique** as urban
history: Rome's outsized place in Eurocentric culture (the "Eternal City,"
endlessly "pressed into the service of the dominant discourse, from Charlemagne
to Mussolini") has distorted and **isolated** it from comparison — the very thing
this comparative volume sets out to correct.

## From city-state to empire

Rome was first settled in the late Bronze Age and became a [[city-state]] in the
early first millennium BCE, one of a **tight cluster of central Italian
peer-polities** (50–150 ha, neighbors 20–40 km apart) on the cultural frontier
between Etruscan, Latin, and Greek worlds. Its unusual hill-and-floodplain site
forced massive **land reclamation** (the drained Forum); its walls (~285 ha,
mid-6th c. BCE) enclosed the largest area in central Italy. A **republican**
system of annually elected, faction-driven magistracies emerged by the late 5th
century.

## How Rome actually expanded

Terrenato dismantles the "mighty war machine" myth as a Romantic fantasy. After
the unprecedented destruction of neighboring **Veii**, Rome expanded across Italy
chiefly through a **vast mosaic of one-to-one treaties** with other cities (kept
as "allies"), via negotiation, protection, threat, and selective war — harsh
treatment (Veii, later Carthage, Corinth) was the exception. Rome's decisive
**advantage was inclusivity**: it admitted foreigners into its citizen body
(relocated Veientes soon got equal rights), fueling growth "exceeding the
potential of pre-modern fertility." Expansion fed expansion through economies of
scale (taxation, tribute, the draft) — a snowball, not an oil slick.

## Urbanism and the burdens of empire

Rome both **prioritized existing cities** (reaching along the urbanized west
coast first, leaving the rural Apennines for later) and **spread urbanism** by
founding colonies — each new colony a member-state of the alliance, tied to a
reorganized, cadastrally surveyed landscape ([[ruralization]]). Imperial
expansion rested on prior **[[agriculture-and-urbanism|agricultural
intensification]]** that produced the necessary surplus. The "burdens of empire"
— provisioning a vast population, the costs of scale, dependence on the provinces
— define the [[imperial-city]].

## Why this place mattered

Beyond its real historical weight, Rome's importance here is methodological:
restoring it "within the broader fold of the history of complex societies" — set
on the same dissecting table as [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] and
[[nineveh|Assyria]] — is the comparative project of the whole volume.
