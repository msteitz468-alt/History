# The Forbidden City

The imperial palace complex at the heart of [[places/beijing|Beijing]], built between 1406 and 1420 under the [[actors/ming-dynasty|Ming]] Yongle Emperor after he moved the capital from Nanjing to Beijing, and serving as the residence and ceremonial-administrative center of Chinese emperors for nearly five centuries, through the remainder of the Ming dynasty and the entire [[actors/qing-dynasty|Qing dynasty]] (until the last emperor, Puyi, was expelled in 1924).

Enclosed by a moat and high walls covering roughly 180 acres and containing nearly a thousand surviving buildings, the complex was called "forbidden" because entry was restricted to the emperor, his household, and authorized officials and servants — ordinary subjects, and even most officials, could not enter without explicit permission. Architecturally it followed strict cosmological and hierarchical principles (north-south axial symmetry, a progression from outer ceremonial halls to inner private residential quarters, imperial yellow roof tiles reserved for the palace) intended to express the emperor's position as the terrestrial pivot of cosmic and political order. The complex is often used metonymically in historical writing to stand for the imperial court and central government itself, distinct from its narrower architectural meaning. It survived the fall of the imperial system in 1912 and, following the 1924 expulsion of the last Qing emperor, was converted into the Palace Museum, opened to the public in 1925.
