# Ordos Region

## Geographic Description

The Ordos is a plateau enclosed within the great bend (the "Ordos Loop") of the Yellow River in what is now Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region and the northern fringes of Shaanxi province. The plateau is bounded on the east, south, and west by the Yellow River itself, and on the north by the Yin-shan mountain range. The region encompasses a mosaic of grassland, semi-arid steppe, and desert (the Mu Us Desert forms the southeastern portion). Elevations range from roughly 1,000 to 1,500 meters, and the climate is continental with cold, dry winters and warm summers. The rivers that cut through the southern edge — including the Fen River and tributaries flowing toward the Wei River Valley — provided routes of communication between the Ordos core and the Chinese agricultural states to the south.

This geographic configuration gave the Ordos exceptional strategic importance in the pre-imperial and imperial periods. Because the Yellow River wrapped around three sides, the Ordos was simultaneously adjacent to the Chinese agricultural world (reachable from the Wei River Valley and central Shaanxi) and connected northward and westward to the Mongolian grasslands and beyond. Control of the Ordos meant control of the principal horse-breeding grounds immediately accessible to Chinese states, and also control of the most direct corridor by which steppe peoples could approach the agricultural heartland.

## Bronze Age Occupation and the Chu-k'ai-kou Culture

The earliest archaeologically attested cultural occupation of the Ordos core dates from the Chu-k'ai-kou culture, whose territory "extended out to northern and central Inner Mongolia, northern Shensi, and northern Shansi, with the Ordos region at its center" (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 48). The Chu-k'ai-kou people (c. mid-third to mid-second millennium BCE) were primarily millet agriculturalists who also raised sheep, pigs, and cattle. By the late third millennium certain pottery motifs — the snake pattern and the flower-shaped edge of the li vessel — appeared in their assemblages; these motifs later archaeologists identify as characteristic of the area's subsequent nomadic peoples. The Chu-k'ai-kou culture practiced oracle bone divination — a practice closely associated with Shang statecraft — suggesting complex ritual and communication links with the Chinese core even in this early period.

In the culture's final phase (Chu-k'ai-kou V, c. 1500 BCE), the Ordos area produced what may be the earliest bronze daggers of the Northern Zone type, alongside Shang-type ritual vessels and hybrid objects combining Shang and northern features. This makes the Ordos the probable birthplace of the distinctive Northern Zone bronze tradition.

## The Ordos as Center of Northern Zone Bronze Culture

Through the late Shang and Western Zhou periods (c. 1300–800 BCE), the Ordos emerged as one of the principal foci of the [[northern-zone]] bronze complex. The region gave its name to the so-called "Ordos bronzes" — a body of material characterized by daggers with integral animal-head pommels, tubular-socketed axes, and ornaments in the animal style. The Ordos bronze type sites (especially Hsi-kou-p'an, T'ao-hung-pa-la, and A-lu-ch'ai-teng) define the standard typological reference for the entire Northern Zone complex.

The Ordos was particularly significant as the zone where the animal style — the decorative tradition shared across Inner Eurasia's Bronze and Iron Age cultures and expressing pastoral identities through stylized depictions of deer, horses, rams, eagles, and predatory animals — reached its most exuberant expression. Bronze belt plaques, harness ornaments, and dagger hilts from Ordos sites constitute the core of what the archaeological literature identifies as "Ordos bronzes," a term used as roughly synonymous with early nomadic metalwork from the Northern Zone.

A characteristic bronze object — the "bow-shaped" object, a slightly bent decorated bar with curved lateral ends — was probably invented in the Ordos-Northern Zone region and thereafter transmitted both south into Shang culture and north and west to South Siberia (Minusinsk region). This bi-directional diffusion underscores the Ordos's role not as a peripheral receiver of Chinese culture but as an independent center of cultural production and export.

## Foci of Early Nomadic Culture (c. 9th–3rd Century BCE)

The Ordos region is extraordinarily rich in early nomadic archaeological sites spanning from the late Western Zhou through the Warring States period. These sites provide the clearest evidence for the three-phase evolution of nomadic pastoral culture identified by Di Cosmo.

### Key Sites

**T'ao-hung-pa-la** (late Spring and Autumn period, c. 6th–5th century BCE) is considered by some investigators to be one of the progenitors of the Xiongnu culture. Its pottery — single-ear kuan pots, hand-made and low-fired — is regarded as a typological antecedent of the gray, refined pottery found in Warring States period "Xiongnu" sites. The metal inventory includes gold earrings of the same type found at Nan-shan-ken (northeast) and Pei-hsin-pao (Hebei), demonstrating the broad network of contacts maintained by Ordos elites. The site also yields bronze daggers in the "antennae style" (ch'u-chiao shih) widely distributed across the Northern Zone, and animal-style plaques similar to those found at northeastern sites.

**Mao-ch'ing-kou** (Ordos, four phases spanning late Spring and Autumn through Warring States) is the most studied early nomadic site in the Ordos region, providing a rare stratigraphic sequence that allows diachronic analysis. Phase I (late Spring and Autumn) contains pottery and bronze items with analogies to Kuo-hsien-yao-tzu in Inner Mongolia; horse fittings include a bronze bit and belthooks, and a dagger with a double bird-head pommel (the bird-head motif connects this site to Saka culture in northern Xinjiang). Phases II–IV show a decisive increase in iron use — including ornamental iron plates — and the investigators attribute the early phase to the Ti (a pre-nomadic northern people) and later phases to the Lou-fan (horse-riding steppe nomads culturally related to the Xiongnu). Notably, a settlement, kilns, and pottery found next to the cemetery indicate that farmers lived alongside the pastoral warriors, a coexistence Di Cosmo identifies as typical rather than exceptional in the Ordos region.

**Hu-lu-ssu-t'ai** (early Warring States, c. 5th–4th century BCE) represents a transitional site between the early T'ao-hung-pa-la assemblage and later Warring States sites. Its bronze tools and weapons preserve archaic characteristics, but also include items found in later sites (especially ornamental waist belts and standardized wing-shaped dagger guards), suggesting the site bridges the developmental gap between earlier nomadic foci and the "Xiongnu culture" horizon.

**Hsi-kou-p'an** (Ordos, late Warring States) is among the richest nomadic sites in the Northern Zone. Gold and silver ornaments abound in its most elaborate burials. Yet at the same site, iron agricultural implements — hoes, adzes, and pickaxes — have been found alongside a settlement. This combination of extraordinary nomadic wealth and evidence for settled agriculture in close proximity exemplifies the symbiotic economic structure of the late Ordos nomadic polities: a pastoral-nomadic aristocracy ruling over tribute-paying farming communities.

**A-lu-ch'ai-teng** (north of T'ao-hung-pa-la, late Warring States) yielded, from two tombs, 218 gold objects and 5 silver objects. The most remarkable piece is a unique four-part gold headdress set (skullcap and three headbands) in the Scythian animal style, interpreted by excavators as a royal burial of the Lou-fan people. This assemblage places the Ordos region firmly within the continuum of Inner Asian steppe aristocratic display culture extending from the Pontic steppe westward.

**Yü-lung-t'ai** (Ordos, possibly 3rd century BCE) preserved seven animal-shaped bronze finials representing lambs, antelopes, deer, and horses, along with chariot axle ends, indicating that the chariot remained in use among late Ordos nomads — whether for military or ceremonial purposes is unclear.

### Shift to Precious-Metal Wealth

The most striking development in Ordos sites from the fourth to third centuries BCE is the dramatic increase in gold and silver objects in aristocratic burials, accompanied by a relative decline in the proportion of weapons. Some of these objects bear Chinese characters — a gilt silver dagger handle at Na-lin-kao-t'u (northern Shaanxi, attributed to the Xiongnu) or golden plates and silver ornaments at Hsi-kou-p'an bearing Chinese characters — and some silver rein rings carry marks interpreted as workshop marks from the Chinese state of Zhao.

Di Cosmo interprets this pattern as evidence of a commercialization of the Ordos aristocracy's relations with China: rather than purely raiding or extracting tribute, Ordos nomadic elites were increasingly obtaining wealth through structured trade in horses, cattle, and furs, receiving precious metalwork (in Chinese and hybrid styles) as payment. This shift reflects both the growing economic integration of the steppe-China frontier and the transformation of nomadic leadership from predominantly military to partly diplomatic-commercial functions.

## Ecological Significance: Horse Breeding

The Ordos's most strategically vital resource for both nomadic peoples and Chinese states was its capacity for horse production. The region's grasslands supported large horse herds, and the archaeological record from Ordos sites consistently shows horse sacrifices, elaborate horse trappings, and — from the Warring States period — iron horse bits and chamfrons indicating sophisticated cavalry equipment. Chinese states recognized this fact: the state of Zhao's adoption of cavalry (by King Wu-ling, late 4th century BCE) was partly driven by the need to compete with Ordos-based pastoral peoples who had mastered mounted warfare.

The importance of the horse — not only economically and militarily but symbolically and ritually — is visible throughout the Ordos archaeological record. Horse gear evolves from relatively simple cheek pieces and bits in the earliest nomadic phases to highly elaborate bronze and iron harness sets by the late Warring States. Horse sacrifice in burials is universal across Ordos nomadic sites, from the early Spring and Autumn through the Xiongnu period.

## Mixed Economy and Symbiosis

A key insight from the Ordos archaeological record is the consistent coexistence of pastoral nomads and agriculturalists — not as enemies or rivals, but as economically integrated and socially hierarchical communities. Sites like Hsi-kou-p'an (with iron farming tools alongside nomadic gold) and Mao-ch'ing-kou (with a farming settlement adjacent to the warrior cemetery) demonstrate what Di Cosmo argues was likely a typical arrangement: a nomadic aristocracy extracting surplus from tributary farming communities, with each party dependent on the other's specialized production.

This challenges the classical Chinese historiographical framing of the north-south frontier as a sharp boundary between incompatible ways of life. The Ordos in particular shows that before the Qin militarization of the frontier (215 BCE), the zone operated as a space of economic integration and cultural exchange rather than simple confrontation.

## Change of Control and Imperial Significance

The pre-imperial Ordos was controlled by various northern pastoral peoples — including groups Chinese sources identify as the Rong, Di, Lou-fan, and Lin Hu — whose archaeological remains are recoverable through the Ordos bronze sites described above. The Qin empire's seizure of the Ordos in 215 BCE under General Meng Tian (see [[qin-northern-campaigns-215bce]]) constituted one of the most consequential military operations of the early imperial period. Di Cosmo argues that this was an offensive move to control horse-breeding resources rather than a defensive reaction to nomadic pressure — and that it likely catalyzed the formation of the Xiongnu empire (209 BCE) by displacing steppe peoples and forcing political consolidation under Modu Chanyu (see [[xiongnu-empire-formation-209bce]]).

The Han empire recovered the Ordos following the humiliation of Baideng (200 BCE) but faced ongoing pressure from Xiongnu forces who regarded the region as ancestral territory. The region oscillated between Chinese and Xiongnu control through the Former Han period, finally secured by Han Wudi's northern campaigns in the 120s–110s BCE.
