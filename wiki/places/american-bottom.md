## Geographic Description

The **American Bottom** is the Mississippi River floodplain immediately east of the river opposite modern St. Louis (Illinois). Bruce D. Smith (CHNPA I.1, ch. 5) describes ~**350 km²** of coalesced levees, oxbow lakes, and marshes — archetypal fertile floodplain soils and fish/waterfowl habitats that form the environmental core of the largest Mississippian polity complex. Seasonal floods shaped meander belts of natural levees (sandy, well-drained, easily hoe-tilled, flood-renewed — suited to corn), oxbow lakes, and backswamps; flood recession trapped fish; the Mississippi flyway supported massive waterfowl migrations.

## Settlement Landscape

[[cahokia]] stood central throughout a developmental cycle of growth, florescence, and decline from ~A.D. 900 to 1400. Smaller town/mound centers in the Bottom include **East St. Louis**, **Mitchell**, **Lohmann**, **Grossmann/GrossLake**, **Lunsford-Pulcher**, and **Mathews** — ranging from ~20 km north to ~65 km south of Cahokia — with diverse and unequal occupational histories.

Many inhabitants lived in short-lived single-family **farmsteads** dispersed on natural levees. Favorable levee loci also show specialized corporate structures integrating dispersed communities: small circular sweatlodges and large rectangular dual-hearth structures. Nodal sites such as **BBB Motor** and **Sponemann** yield elaborate mortuaries and fertility figurines of nonlocal bauxitic clay, evidence of more intense elite integration into the Cahokia system.

## Political and Economic Pattern

Smith’s landscape model: generally equivalent **mound-centered societal groups** with Cahokia at the center; **politically independent to varying degrees**; **economically self-sufficient**; interlocking cycles of florescence and decline — “rather than forming a rigid, centralized, and highly organized system.” Cahokia’s elite directly controlled adjacent floodplain and exercised varying indirect administrative/tributary oversight of other Bottom communities, drawing tribute labor for Monks Mound, central-precinct fortifications, woodhenges, and public structures. Classification of this arrangement as complex [[chiefdom]] vs. state is contested; see [[north-american-states-debate]] and [[cahokia]].

## History of Control and Significance

- **~A.D. 950–1000**: Cahokia rose to paramountcy among roughly equal American Bottom mound societies; large-scale mound construction began.
- **~1050–1150**: peak florescence; households increasingly dispersed into farmsteads as central authority expanded.
- **~1150–1200**: florescence largely fragmented; large-scale depopulation of the American Bottom begins (suggested factors: rising groundwater reducing farmland; firewood depletion).
- **A.D. 1200–1300**: not a return to equal floodplain polities but **large-scale abandonment of the river floodplain** and smaller-scale polities in upland stream-valley settings.
- **After ~A.D. 1400**: the Bottom falls within the interior **Vacant Quarter** — dramatic decline and abandonment well before European explorers or introduced diseases; post-1400 groups small, without large villages or mound centers. Late-seventeenth-century European travelers found the once-dense valley nearly empty.

Strategic and economic significance rested on floodplain agricultural productivity, aquatic protein, and Cahokia’s role as a prestige-goods and ceremonial node in [[mississippian-culture]] and [[southeastern-ceremonial-complex]] networks — not on bulk long-distance food extraction from distant regions (Smith).
