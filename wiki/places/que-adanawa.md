# Que / Adanawa (Hiyawa)

**Adanawa** (Luwian hieroglyphic name), **Que** (Assyrian name), and **Hiyawa** (an alternative Luwian designation) are three names for a single Iron Age kingdom occupying the Cilician plain and parts of the mountainous territory to its north-east. The kingdom lay "in a Luwian as well as a Hurrian cultural zone, and its population was largely an admixture of Luwian and Hurrian elements" (Bryce 2012). Its western neighbour, **Hilakku** (rough Cilicia / Cilicia Tracheia), gives Classical Cilicia its name. To the north and east lay the kingdoms of the [[tabal|Tabal]] complex.

## Geographic Description

The kingdom of Adanawa occupied the region known in Classical sources as **Cilicia Pedias** or **Cilicia Campestris** — the rich, well-watered plain drained by the Ceyhan (Pyramos) and Seyhan (Saros) rivers, with Adana at its centre. It extended also into mountainous territory to the north-east of the plain, bordering the Taurus foothills. The frontier fortress of **Azatiwataya** (modern Karatepe), 100 km north-east of Adana, above the Ceyhan valley, marks the north-eastern boundary zone.

To the west lay **Hilakku** (Assyrian name), covering the rugged western half of Cilicia (Cilicia Tracheia / Aspera). "The name Cilicia is clearly derived from Hilakku" (Bryce 2012). Hilakku's Iron Age local name is unknown — no Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions have been found there — but it was almost certainly Luwian-speaking, as attested by Luwian names persisting in the region into the Roman period. In the Late Bronze Age Hilakku had formed part of Hittite Tarhuntassa.

## Names and Their Significance

The multiplicity of names for this kingdom is itself historically significant:

- **Adanawa** (Luwian): used in the Karatepe bilingual (Luwian version) and in Hittite records as *Adaniya* or *Adana*; "probably the name of both the country and its capital" (Bryce 2012); retained from the Bronze Age.
- **Que** (Assyrian): the standard term in Neo-Assyrian annals.
- **Hiyawa** (Luwian): used in the Çineköy bilingual (Luwian version). Bryce identifies this as "an aphaeresized form of Ahhiyawa," the Late Bronze Age Hittite name generally understood to designate the Achaian/Mycenaean Greek world. Its appearance in the Iron Age may "reflect a migration of Greek populations from western Anatolia or the Aegean to Cilicia at the beginning of the Iron Age."
- **Danunians** (Phoenician): the ethnic designation used in both bilinguals' Phoenician versions.
- **Hume** (Neo-Babylonian): a later variant.

The fact that the same Luwian version of Awariku's own inscription (Çineköy) calls the country **Hiyawa**, while the parallel Karatepe inscription (authored by his subordinate Azatiwata) calls it **Adanawa**, remains unexplained and is a live historiographical question (see below).

## Bronze Age Background

Adana (as Adaniya) is first attested in a Hittite text of the mid 15th century listing countries that rebelled against Hittite king Ammuna. It subsequently became part of **Kizzuwadna**, an independent kingdom created from former Hittite territory in the second half of the 15th century, before being restored to Hittite control when Tudhaliya I/II annexed Kizzuwadna early in the 14th century. The name Kizzuwadna survived into the Iron Age as **Kisuatnu**, one of the cities of Adanawa captured by Shalmaneser III in 839 BCE. A possible connection between the "Denyen" (Dnyn) among the Sea Peoples and "the people of Adana" is raised by Bryce but treated as one of four competing identifications — "the most plausible one" among them, but not established.

## Kings of Adanawa / Que

| Ruler | Dates (approx.) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Kate | — 858–831 | Joined anti-Assyrian coalition 858; deposed by Shalmaneser III 831 |
| Kirri | 831 — | Brother of Kate; installed by Shalmaneser III |
| **Awariku** (Assyrian: Urikki) | c. 738–709 | The central figure; author of the Çineköy bilingual; overlord of Azatiwata |

**Kate** is first attested in 858, when he joined Hilakku's ruler Pihirim in a military coalition of northern Syrian states that confronted Shalmaneser III in his first regnal year. Shalmaneser conducted four subsequent campaigns against Adanawa (839–831) and deposed Kate, replacing him with his brother **Kirri**.

**Awariku** (also spelled Awarikku, Warika; Assyrian: Urikki) is first attested as a tributary of Tiglath-pileser III c. 738, and last attested c. 709–710, when his secret delegation to Urartu was intercepted by the Phrygian king Mita and reported to Ashur-sharru-usur, Sargon II's governor of Adanawa. Awariku declared in the Çineköy bilingual that "the king and royal house of Ashur became a 'mother and father to him', and that the people of Adanawa and Assyria became one house" — the strongest statement of Assyrian client identity in the Neo-Hittite corpus. He built fifteen fortresses east and west of his kingdom.

Awariku claimed descent from the line of **Muk(a)sas** — corresponding to **Mopsus** in the Phoenician version — connecting the Adanawa royal house to Greek legendary traditions of Mopsus as founder of settlements along the Anatolian coast in the early Iron Age. This reinforces the Hiyawa/Ahhiyawa connection: the dynasty may have been founded by "the leader of a Greek colonizing group" (Bryce 2012).

## The Karatepe Bilingual (Azatiwada)

The most important epigraphic monument from Adanawa is the **Karatepe bilingual** inscription, discovered at a Neo-Hittite site above the Ceyhan valley, 100 km north-east of Adana. The site's ancient name was **Azatiwataya**, named after its founder **Azatiwata**, a subordinate of Awariku who built it as a frontier fortress. The bilingual comprises five inscriptions in Luwian hieroglyphs and Phoenician, all parallel versions of a single dedication text. It "played a substantial role in the decipherment of the Luwian hieroglyphic script" (Bryce 2012).

Azatiwata's inscription records extensive achievements: building the city and a system of frontier fortresses, subduing hostile peoples, extending Adanawa's frontiers east and west, filling granaries, increasing military forces, establishing peace with every king, and "bringing about the succession of his lord's son to the throne." This last claim is historically significant: it suggests Awariku's family had lost royal status and Azatiwata re-established them — consistent with the period of instability following Sargon's death in 705 BCE and the likely abolition of the local monarchy under Ashur-sharru-usur.

Bryce reconstructs Azatiwata's activities as belonging to the years immediately after Sargon's death (705), when Assyrian control loosened, Adanawa fell into disorder, and Azatiwata — operating possibly as an Assyrian client — restored order, expelled "bands of robbers" (possibly Cimmerians or Phrygians), and reinstated Awariku's dynasty. The Karatepe inscription was probably carved after Awariku's execution (c. 709–710).

## The Çineköy Bilingual

The **Çineköy bilingual**, carved on a statue of the Storm God Tarhunza (site 30 km south of Adana, not the original location), was authored by Awariku himself. It establishes the equation Urikki (Assyrian) = Warika (Luwian Çineköy) = Awariku (Luwian Karatepe), confirming the identity of Adanawa's ruler across all source types. It also provides the name Hiyawa for the kingdom (in the Luwian text) and the Mopsus dynastic claim.

## Hilakku (Rough Cilicia)

The western neighbour **Hilakku** (rough Cilicia) remained largely independent and elusive throughout the Neo-Assyrian period. It was incorporated into the Assyrian provincial system probably under Shalmaneser V and then formally assigned to Ambaris of Bit-Burutash as a dowry kingdom by Sargon II. "This rugged country, populated by a fiercely independent people, remained largely free of Assyria throughout the Neo-Assyrian period, and at the best of times, the Assyrians could claim no more than token sovereignty over it" (Bryce 2012). When Ambaris was deposed in 713, Hilakku and Bit-Burutash were both placed under an Assyrian governor. The last reference to Hilakku in this period is Esarhaddon's claim to have conquered its people after defeating Cimmerians at Hupishna (679 BCE).

## Relationship to Assyria and Urartu

Adanawa's relationship with Assyria moved through three phases:
1. **Hostility and tributary status** (858–831): Kate's anti-Assyrian coalition, Shalmaneser's four campaigns, deposition of Kate.
2. **Client kingdom** (c. 738–713): Awariku's long tenure as tributary, paralleling Warpalawa of Tuwana; deep integration into Assyrian suzerainty, as expressed in the Çineköy bilingual.
3. **Direct Assyrian province** (713 onwards): Ashur-sharru-usur appointed governor; the 710–709 interception of Awariku's delegation to Urartu brought his career and probably his life to an end; Assyrian control restored under Esarhaddon by 675.

## Historiography

### The Hiyawa / Ahhiyawa Debate

The identification of Iron Age Hiyawa with Late Bronze Age Ahhiyawa (Mycenaean Greeks) is widely accepted but not universally so. The question — whether "Hiyawa" reflects genuine ethnic Greek continuity in Cilicia from the Late Bronze Age into the Iron Age, or is simply a geographic archaism — is a significant open issue in Aegean–Anatolian studies. Bryce treats the identification as plausible and the Mopsus connection as supporting evidence, while noting the difficulty of reconciling the dual Luwian names Hiyawa (Çineköy) and Adanawa (Karatepe) for the same kingdom authored by associated figures.

### The Dating of the Karatepe Monument

The date of the Karatepe monument has "fluctuated between 9th- and 8th-century alternatives on the basis of the style and iconography of the sculptures." Hawkins's palaeographic analysis of the Phoenician script supports a date at the end of the 8th century (end of Sargon's reign or early Sennacherib), while acknowledging that "the monument's chronology still remains problematic." The excavator's archaeological evidence does not straightforwardly support either date. Bryce accepts the late 8th-century date and the identification of the Karatepe Awariku with Tiglath-pileser's and Sargon's Urikki, but explicitly concedes this is an assumption.

### The Yahan / Bit-Agusi Distinction

Separate from Adanawa's own sources, the historiography of the "Danunians" and the House of Mopsus connects Adanawa to broader debates about Greek migration into Cilicia and the role of the Sea Peoples in the Bronze Age collapse — making this kingdom one of the most contested intersections of Aegean and Near Eastern Iron Age studies.
