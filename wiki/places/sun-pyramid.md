## Overview

The Pyramid of the Sun is the largest pyramid at [[teotihuacan]] and one of the largest structures in the pre-Columbian Americas. It sits to the east of the Avenue of the Dead, roughly midway between the Moon Pyramid to the north and the Ciudadela to the south. The pyramid likely reached its ultimate height in the Tzacualli phase (ca. 1–100 CE) or early Miccaotli phase (ca. 100–170 CE), making it the civic-ceremonial epicenter of the early city. Its fore-platform and surrounding plaza were substantially developed in the Miccaotli and Early Tlamimilolpa phases. Unlike the Moon Pyramid and the Feathered Serpent Pyramid, systematic sacrificial tomb excavations have not been conducted inside the Sun Pyramid; it remains less archaeologically dissected than its counterparts.

## Construction History

The Sun Pyramid was already immense before the Miccaotli phase (ca. 100–170 CE), when it "reached its ultimate height early in this period" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). Its fore-platform was "greatly enlarged in at least two stages that had cut stone facings and two or more rooms." Unpublished 1990s excavations by Eduardo Matos revealed that "the earlier stage included white and orange-red murals with geometric motifs, including a checkerboard pattern" — a color scheme with transverse-cut conch-shell motifs that also appears in early Ciudadela palace murals and in the "Ward Temple" of the La Ventilla district (Cabrera and Gómez 2008).

Leopardo Batres, who directed early twentieth-century excavations, incorrectly reconstructed the fore-platform at an angle (~23 degrees north of true west) to the main pyramid; its actual orientation is identical to that of the main pyramid — ca. 15.5 degrees east of true north, the canonical Teotihuacan orientation.

The fore-platform served a functional purpose: because "the top of the pyramid itself is so high that anything carried out at that level would have been hard to see from the plaza," the fore-platform provided a stage for "activities easily visible from the sizable plaza in front of the pyramid" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). The plaza was separated from the Avenue of the Dead by a long platform several meters high, with stairways on both sides.

## The Sun Plaza

The Sun Plaza contains several smaller pyramids and room complexes, including the "Palace of the Sun." Numerous fallen stone sculptures of uncertain date have been found there, including felines, human skulls, and other motifs — "some probably representing period-ending fire ceremonies" (Fash et al. 2009, cited in Cowgill 2015). Most pertain to the fore-platform, which used cut stone "to an extent second only to the FSP" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

The Sun Pyramid's location east of the Avenue of the Dead permits an "uninterrupted visual sweep of over two kilometers along the Avenue, and a distant view of the Moon Pyramid" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). The six low transverse platforms that cross the Avenue do not seriously interrupt this sightline.

## Significance

The Sun Pyramid complex was probably "the civic-ceremonial heart of the Tzacualli phase city" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). Its subsequent position along the axis — flanked by the enlarged Moon Pyramid complex to the north and the Ciudadela to the south — created an elongated north-south principal axis for the city's ceremonial core, covering approximately 2.15 km from the Moon Pyramid to the southern edge of the Ciudadela.

Surviving stone carvings from the plaza and murals emphasize jaguars, human skulls, and diving figures with macaw attributes, "which likely relate to the sun." Some jaguars may represent "the night jaguar of the Underworld," while others "entwined with vegetation" seem more related to fertility. The evidence suggests the "Sun Pyramid was dedicated to the Sun God as well as the Storm God" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6) — a conclusion consistent with Storm God jars found in associated burials.

The burials at the Moon Pyramid, Sun Pyramid, and Feathered Serpent Pyramid share emphasis on the Feathered Serpent and Storm God, though "symbolism at the Sun Pyramid perhaps did not emphasize the Feathered Serpent" as prominently (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

## Archaeological Evidence

Unpublished excavations by Eduardo Matos in the 1990s clarified the fore-platform's phasing and revealed its early mural program. The structure 6:N5W1 adjacent to the Moon Pyramid, radiocarbon-dated to the 400s CE, illustrates the density of ancillary structures throughout the civic core. The Sun Pyramid's interior remains largely unexcavated relative to the Moon Pyramid and FSP.

## Historiography

Early twentieth-century reconstruction by Batres imposed significant errors on the fore-platform that were later corrected by more careful survey. The Sun Pyramid's dedicatory program — whom or what it honored — remains less well understood than that of the Moon Pyramid and FSP because its interior has not been systematically tunneled for tombs. The astronomical hypothesis linking the Sun Pyramid, Moon Pyramid, and FSP to a triad of Sun, Moon, and Venus (cited in Cowgill 2015) is plausible but unconfirmed. Cowgill (2015) considers it likely that long-established priestly organizations "endowed with rich resources, persisted at the Sun and Moon Pyramids" even after the Ciudadela was built, suggesting an institutional continuity predating the political innovations of the Miccaotli phase.
