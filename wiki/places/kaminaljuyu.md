# Kaminaljuyú

## Overview

**Kaminaljuyú**, in the Valley of Guatemala (~1,500 m, Pacific–Caribbean divide), was the principal Preclassic–Classic center of the southern Maya highlands and a classic case of long-distance interaction with [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]]. Much of the site lies under modern Guatemala City. Robert Sharer (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 10) treats it as the **most powerful Late Preclassic center in the southern Maya Area**, though no single site dominated the whole south. Richard Adams (CHNPA Ch. 1) stresses that Sanders’s 1960s salvage/survey showed the site an **order of magnitude larger** than coastal Abaj Takalik and [[izapa|Izapa]] — a plausible early regional capital.

## Scale and form (Sharer)

Originally ~**5 km²**, with **>200 earthen mounds** up to 20 m high (adobe-plastered platforms once supporting wood/plaster/thatch buildings); volcanic stone for drains, steps, monuments, grinding stones; platforms often in N–S groups around a modified lagoon/reservoir; surrounding valley full of satellite sites. About half known platforms are Preclassic, including many of the largest. Settlement origins Early Preclassic; monumental architecture and stratification by Middle Preclassic; **peak Late Preclassic** (size, constructions, vast carved monuments including hieroglyphic texts, external contacts). Control of the **El Chayal** obsidian quarry (~20 km NE) made Kaminaljuyú the hub distributing obsidian through the southern area into the lowlands. Power and wealth of Late Preclassic rulers are visible in two spectacular tombs in **Structure E-III-3** (ancestral shrines): hundreds of pottery offerings, stingray spines (autosacrifice); Tomb 2 elite male with jade mask/headdress and three unadorned facedown companions (two children, young adult).

## Writing and sculpture

Kaminaljuyú’s Preclassic sculptured corpus **far outnumbers** that of any other site; rulers with deity masks, headdresses, weapons, trophy heads. Among the best Late Preclassic text+image monuments are **Stela 10** (with Chalchuapa Monument 1): portraits and extensive calendrical texts on themes of warfare/sacrifice or office succession (Sharer). Pot-bellied monuments of Middle–terminal Preclassic include examples at Kaminaljuyú. Related early writing: El Portón Monument 1 ~400 B.C. (glyphs ancestral to later Maya forms).

## Classic period and Teotihuacan debate

**Older view** (including Adams citing Sanders & Michels 1977): Teotihuacan episode as **genuine intrusion**, probably military, becoming administrators of the region including the chocolate-producing south coast.

**Sharer’s current view:** Maya state-level societies originated in the **Late Preclassic**, long before Teotihuacan involvement; “Teotihuacan-style” platforms at Kaminaljuyú are **funerary shrines** over elaborate tombs in long-standing Maya tradition, for local rulers with mostly local/Maya-area status goods plus some central Mexican imports — foreign traits combined with local traditions by **local elites to reinforce authority**, not conquest/colonial enclaves. Teotihuacan contacts far less evident at most highland/coast sites than at Kaminaljuyú.

**Cowgill** (CHNPA Ch. 6): stronger Teotihuacan-related manifestations in highland Guatemala than expected for ~1,100 km distance (talud-tablero pyramids, rich burials with elite imports and local Teotihuacan-style elites; local utility wares persist). He **rejects political control from Teotihuacan**, preferring well-armed adventurers’ takeover creating an autonomous or quickly autonomous local state replicating metropolitan elite culture (Kidder et al. 1946). Lowland interactions (Tikal, Copán): elite exchange and political roles for Teotihuacan-connected persons, not direct control or high-volume commerce.

Valley of Guatemala surveys: **population maximum Late Classic**; Kaminaljuyú largest highland polity of that period but **not rivalling the greatest lowland powers** (Sharer). Much of the site destroyed by Guatemala City expansion.
