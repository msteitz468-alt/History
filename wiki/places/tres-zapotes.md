# Tres Zapotes

## Overview

**Tres Zapotes** sits on the northern edge of the Tuxtla Mountains in south-central Veracruz.
In Richard A. Diehl’s Gulf Coast chapter ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA
II.1 Ch. 4]]), after ~600 BCE complex societies spread west of the Olmec heartland between the
Tuxtlas and the Pánuco, but Late Formative occupations are poorly known **except Tres Zapotes**.
Lowe’s scheme places Tres Zapotes and other sites northwest of the Tuxtlas in power during
**Epi-Olmec** times (c. 300 BCE–1 CE), under strong Pacific-coast Chiapas/Guatemala stylistic
influence.

## Occupation and monuments

Diehl describes a long, poorly defined occupation. Scattered Early Formative ceramics indicate an
**Initial Olmec** population, but the **majority of occupation** and virtually all of the known
**twenty-four stone monuments** belong to the Late Formative **Epi-Olmec** phase. Three colossal
heads are in the Olmec genre yet distinct from those of [[san-lorenzo-olmec|San Lorenzo]] and
[[la-venta|La Venta]].

Many carvings reflect the **[[izapa|Izapa]] sculptural tradition** native to southern
Mesoamerica’s Pacific Coast and piedmont—suggesting a **reversal in the directionality of
cultural influences** relative to earlier centuries, when Olmec heartland societies left a strong
imprint on their southern neighbors.

## Stela C and early calendrics

Tres Zapotes **Stela C** bears an Izapa-style mask and a Long Count date
**7.16.6.16.18 6 Eznab = 31 BCE**—one of Mesoamerica’s oldest calendrical inscriptions.
Stirling’s 1939 reading sparked unresolved debate on the origins of writing and calendrics (Maya
homeland vs. Gulf Coast / Tuxtla statuette). Michael Coe (1957) argued that Mesoamerica’s
earliest known inscriptions occur on Late Formative Izapa-style monuments in Chiapas and
Guatemala rather than in the Maya lowlands—correct for Maya systems, though Zapotec writing and
calendrics in Oaxaca may predate the earliest Maya by centuries (Diehl). Earlier wiki notes often
cite ~32 BCE for Stela C (September 2, 32 BCE in some conversions); Diehl’s chapter uses 31 BCE
for the 7.16.6.16.18 reading—both belong to the same early Long Count horizon.

## Epi-Olmec script context

Diehl links Tres Zapotes’s Late Formative horizon to the broader Epi-Olmec phenomenon best
documented at **La Mojarra** Stela 1 (Acula River / Papaloapan drainage; found 1986 submerged):
an elaborately dressed man in profile with >500 glyphs—one of the longest Mesoamerican
inscriptions—and Long Count dates A.D. 143 and 156. Justeson & Kaufman (1993) proposed a widely
accepted decipherment as **Epi-Olmec** script in Zoquean languages still spoken in the greater
Isthmian region; the text documents a ruler’s rise, warfare, ritual, and astronomy. Only 4–5
known Epi-Olmec texts exist; open questions include origin, possible Olmec-script roots, relation
to Classic Maya, and why this tradition alone died out precolumbianly. One authority suggests the
La Mojarra stela may have been en route to [[cerro-de-las-mesas|Cerro de las Mesas]] when lost in
the river.

## Why it mattered

Primary Gulf Coast window on the **Epi-Olmec** transformation after La Venta’s decline: survival
and local reinvention of Olmec-genre sculpture under reverse Pacific (Izapa) influence, plus early
Long Count monumental writing. Bridges Formative heartland civilization and Classic-period Gulf
centers (Cerro de las Mesas, Matacapan, El Tajín).
