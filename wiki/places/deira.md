## Geography and Extent

Deira was the Anglian kingdom between the Humber and the Tees, forming the southern core of what later became the [[kingdom-of-northumbria|kingdom of Northumbria]]. Its heartland lay in the Vale of York; the royal sanctuary at Godmunddingaham (possibly Goodmanham, near York) anchored its religious geography, and [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]] was baptised at York. Unlike the militarised frontier zone of [[bernicia|Bernicia]] to the north, Deira was "the more settled and prosperous" of the two Anglian northern kingdoms.

## Origins and Settlement

The Deirans "included communities whose ancestors were established in Britain in the earliest days of Germanic settlement." After Soemil — a dimly remembered figure — they re-emerge into recorded history only under [[ælle-of-deira|Ælle]], who founded or re-founded the ruling dynasty in the late sixth century. Their communities, like the men of Lindsey and the eastern Angles, represented the deepest stratum of Anglian settlement in Britain.

## Political History

The fundamental tension of Northumbrian history was the eclipse of the "more settled and prosperous Deira by the Bernician war-zone," which "created long-enduring tensions within the Anglian polity north of the Humber." [[æthelfrith-of-bernicia|Æthelfrith]] extended his kingship over Deira in 604 or 605, expelling Ælle's son Eadwine. After Eadwine's own reign (617–634), which used Deira as its essential power-base, Bernician domination reasserted itself under [[oswald-of-northumbria|Oswald]] and [[oswiu-of-northumbria|Oswiu]]. The Deiran royal family was repeatedly decimated by violence: Eadwine's sons, the sub-king Oswine (killed by Oswiu 651/652), and the princes scattered to Gaul.

Deira's importance as a sub-kingdom persisted throughout the seventh century. Oswiu placed his son Alhfrith there after the Winwæd; Ecgfrith's brother Aelfwine followed; after Aelfwine's death in the battle of the Trent (679/680) Deira was "ruled from then on directly by the Bernicians."

## Ecclesiastical Significance

York was the natural ecclesiastical centre for Deira and the ambition of successive Northumbrian kings: Eadwine and Paulinus established an episcopal see there, aimed at metropolitan status; Oswald ignored it in favour of Lindisfarne; Oswiu restored a bishop to York after Whitby, in the person of Chad. The struggle over whether the northern see should be at York or Lindisfarne, and whether it should be Ionan or Roman in character, was a persistent thread of seventh-century Northumbrian ecclesiastical politics.
