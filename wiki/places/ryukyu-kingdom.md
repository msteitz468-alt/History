# The Ryukyu Kingdom (and the Hokkaido/Ainu Periphery)

The **Ryukyu kingdom** (modern **Okinawa**) — an independent maritime kingdom of the
[[japan|Japanese]] archipelago's southern islands that was simultaneously a **tributary of
China** and, after 1609, a **vassal of the Satsuma domain** — and, in parallel, the northern
**Hokkaido/Ainu** frontier. The two "peripheries" are the subject of David L. Howell's chapter
in the [[howell-early-modern-japan-asia-world|New Cambridge History of Japan Vol. II]] (ch. 18),
which uses them to ask **where "Japan" began and ended** in the early-modern centuries.

## The Ryukyu kingdom

The Ryukyu archipelago — a chain of fifty-plus islands stretching from Kyūshū toward Taiwan —
developed complex society in the **Gusuku period (c. 1050–1429)**, named for its stone
fortifications, as trade with Japan and China grew (sulfur for Chinese gunpowder was its great
export). Three Okinawan kingdoms emerged, all entering **tribute relations with the Ming in
1372**; in **1429** the **Chūzan** kingdom unified the island, founding the **Ryukyu kingdom**
centered on **Shuri** (Naha). The kingdom flourished as a great **entrepôt of East Asian
maritime trade**, brokering goods among China, Japan, Korea, and Southeast Asia.

In **1609** the **Satsuma domain** invaded and subjugated the kingdom — but, tellingly, **left
it nominally independent**. This served both Satsuma and the shogunate: a formally sovereign
Ryukyu could continue its lucrative **tribute trade with China** (now Ming, then Qing) while
funneling profits and goods to Satsuma. Ryukyu thus became one of the **"[[sakoku|four gates]]"**
of Tokugawa foreign relations (the Satsuma–Ryukyu–China channel), an early-modern entity of
**layered, ambiguous sovereignty** — at once an independent kingdom, a Chinese tributary, and a
Japanese vassal.

## The Hokkaido / Ainu periphery

Howell pairs Ryukyu with the northern frontier of **Hokkaido** (then **Ezo**), home of the
indigenous **Ainu**. Most of Hokkaido lay "beyond the boundaries of the realm inhabited by
Japanese." The **Matsumae domain** monopolized trade with the Ainu (the northern "gate"),
exploiting and increasingly subordinating them through the contract-fishery system. Like
Ryukyu, Hokkaido was a zone of **contact and asymmetric exchange** rather than full
incorporation — until the modern state absorbed both.

## Incorporation into the modern nation-state

Both peripheries were **annexed outright by Meiji Japan**: **Hokkaido** was formally colonized
and developed from 1869 (with the Ainu reduced to a marginalized indigenous minority), and the
**Ryukyu kingdom was abolished and made Okinawa Prefecture in 1879**. Howell uses their
incorporation to show how the **boundaries of "Japan"** — fluid and layered in the early-modern
period — were hardened into the fixed territory of a modern nation-state (cf.
[[japanese-colonial-empire]]). Okinawa's later history (the 1945 battle, US occupation to 1972)
and the question of Ainu and Okinawan **indigeneity** remain live issues.

## Why it mattered

The peripheries are central to the New CHJ's **"Japan in Asia and the world"** framing: they
show early-modern Japan not as a sealed island but as a realm of **graduated sovereignty and
trans-regional connection**, and they complicate any simple equation of the archipelago with
the nation.
