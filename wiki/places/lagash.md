# Lagash (and Girsu)

The best-documented city of Early Dynastic Sumer — paradoxically, one the
[[sumerian-king-list|Sumerian King List]] omits entirely. Its own dynasty's
inscriptions and the temple archives excavated at **Tello** (ancient **Girsu**,
one component of the larger Lagash complex centred on al-Hiba) make it the window
through which the whole [[early-dynastic-sumer|Sumerian city-state world]] is
read. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XIII, C. J.
Gadd).

## Why Lagash dominates the record

"Always an individualist," Lagash kept its own king-list and apparently withheld
its record from the Isin scribes who compiled the standard list. Excavated since
the 1870s, Tello yielded the dynasty's royal inscriptions and the **Baba temple
accounts** — the richest single body of administrative tablets from the period,
and the empirical basis for everything we know about the
[[temple-economy|Sumerian temple economy]]. Gadd cautions that Lagash was "by no
means the greatest" Sumerian city; its prominence in our histories is an accident
of preservation.

## The dynasty (Ur-Nanshe line)

The line was founded by **Ur-Nanshe** (whose archaic "family reliefs" name his
relatives and servants), and reached its height under his grandson **Eannatum**,
who claimed victories over Umma, Kish, Akshak, Elam, Mari, and Subar — a dominance
that would have justified a claim to the *nam-lugal* (supremacy) the king-list
never grants Lagash. His nephew **Entemena** dug a great Tigris–Euphrates canal
and made a treaty with Lugalkinishedudu of Uruk. The line ran through
**Enentarzi** and **Lugalanda** to the reformer **[[urukagina|Urukagina]]**,
under whom Lagash was sacked by **Lugalzaggisi of Umma** — ending the dynasty and
ushering in the [[akkadian-empire|age of empire]] (Lugalzaggisi himself soon fell
to [[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon]]). Lagash revived spectacularly a century and a half
later under **[[gudea|Gudea]]**, "Lagash II" (see below).

## The border war with Umma

Lagash's defining external story is the multi-generational
[[lagash-umma-border-war|boundary war with Umma]] over fields claimed for the god
**Ningirsu** — the conflict commemorated on Eannatum's **Stele of the Vultures**,
the earliest historical war monument. Control of the boundary **canal** was the
material stake (see [[temple-economy]]).

## Religion

Lagash's gods — **Ningirsu** (the city-god), his consort **Baba**, and **Nanshe**
of Nina (Zerghul), an oracular goddess praised as upholder of justice, who
"protects widows and orphans" — frame both its wars (fought as the god's vengeance)
and its one moment of social reform.

## Lagash II: the Gudea revival

After the [[akkadian-empire|Akkadian]] and [[gutian-invasion|Gutian]] periods,
Lagash rose again — as in the Early Dynastic age, and again without entering the
[[sumerian-king-list|king-list]]. The governors **Ur-Baba** and his son-in-law
**[[gudea|Gudea]]** ruled with near-total independence amid the political vacuum,
patronizing the **finest school of hard-stone sculpture** in Babylonian history
(the famous diorite statues) and, in Gudea's temple-hymn cylinders, the **classic
form of the Sumerian language**. The dynasty ended when **Nammakhni** fell to
**Ur-Nammu**, founder of the [[mesopotamian-chronology|Third Dynasty of Ur]].

## Why this place mattered

Lagash is the documentary keystone of third-millennium Sumer: through it we see
the temple economy, the mechanics of inter-city war, the ensi–priest tension, and
the first recorded program of [[urukagina|social reform]] — none of which survives
in comparable detail from any contemporary city.
