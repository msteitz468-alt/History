# Francia

**The kingdom of the Franks** — the *regnum Francorum* created by [[clovis|Clovis]] and ruled by
the [[merovingian-franks|Merovingians]] and then the [[pippinids|Pippinids]] — covering the old
Roman provinces of Gaul and the Rhineland. Though repeatedly partitioned among heirs, "there was
only one *regnum Francorum*"; the divisions were of a single kingdom, not separate states.

## Geography and the three sub-regions

By the later sixth century Francia had crystallised into three named sub-regions whose boundaries
shifted with each royal division:

- **[[neustria|Neustria]]** — the "New West Lands," richest in fiscal land, Roman cities (Paris, Tours,
  Rouen), and productive population; Merovingian power centred here, with capitals at Soissons and
  increasingly **Paris**.
- **[[austrasia|Austrasia]]** — the "East Land," reaching from Metz and Trier west to Reims, Châlons,
  and Laon; in the sixth century still a centre of Roman culture, later the Pippinid power base.
- **Burgundy** — the absorbed Burgundian kingdom (conquered by 534), governed for a time from Chalon.

Beyond these lay the peripheral territories — [[aquitaine|Aquitaine]], [[provence|Provence]],
[[bavaria|Bavaria]], Alemannia, Thuringia — which would harden into the duchies and principalities of
later medieval Europe.

## Settlement and cities

Roman isolated villas in northern Gaul were largely abandoned in the third and fourth centuries for
more concentrated habitations near forests and waterways, becoming Merovingian villages. Settlement
expanded rapidly: around Trier, new sites grew from twenty (450–525) to twenty-eight (525–600) to
sixty-seven (600–700). Cities survived chiefly as episcopal seats — "ancient cities which did not
become the sees of bishops tended by and large to disappear in the early Middle Ages." Paris perhaps
held ~20,000 people and Bordeaux ~15,000 (figures some historians halve).

## The fisc and the economy

The **imperial fisc confiscated by Clovis** in the north of Gaul "had always formed the heart of
Merovingian wealth" — estates clustered around Paris (Chelles, Rueil, Clichy) and Soissons (Bonneuil,
Compiègne, Nogent). Because fiscal estates escaped private inheritance and division, they preserved
record-keeping and the late-Roman tax system longer than private holdings. On them, during the reigns
of [[chlothar-ii|Chlothar II]] and [[dagobert-i|Dagobert I]], a **bipartite manor** took shape:
peasant *manses* paying fixed rent, plus a directly managed reserve worked by gang slaves and labour
obligations.

Commerce was structurally plural — gold coinage, gift exchange, plunder, and tribute all operated at
once. Long-distance luxury trade (jewellery, silks, papyrus, spices) was in the hands of **Greek,
Syrian, and Jewish** merchants resident in the cities; import duties on them were a major royal
revenue. The trade was largely one-way: Gaul exported little beyond timber, weapons, and slaves, and
gold won as booty or Byzantine subsidy flowed east to pay for imports. As booty declined across the
later sixth and seventh centuries, the gold drain contracted trade and the foreign merchant
communities disappeared, gold giving way to **local silver** coinage.

## Why it mattered

Francia was the most durable of the Roman successor states and the matrix of medieval Europe. Its
strength lay in the **fusion** of a Germanic-speaking warrior elite with Gallo-Roman provincial
administration and the orthodox Church — the amalgamation Geary's book exists to describe. See
[[frankish-ethnogenesis]], [[merovingian-franks]], [[roman-gaul]].

## Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World (2020)

Paul Fouracre's chapter in the [[sources/effros-moreira-oxford-handbook-merovingian-world-2020|Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World]] reframes the transition "from Gaul to Francia" as a 270-year amalgamation — not a conquest — in which "a degree of cultural solidarity coalesced around a landholding elite drawn from a variety of backgrounds," knitted together by intermarriage and the Columbanian monastic network (see [[merovingian-monasticism]]). Frankish identity "rested ultimately on success in warfare" yet was "sufficiently flexible to accommodate local custom." The economic chapters add that Francia's river network sustained intense exchange (Wickham: "second only to the intensity of exchange in the Nile Valley"), with garnets reaching it from Sri Lanka and India (see [[merovingian-economy]], [[garnet-and-bead-trade]], [[merovingian-coinage]]).
