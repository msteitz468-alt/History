# Buchenwald

**Buchenwald**, opened July 1937 on the Ettersberg near Weimar, was one of the largest camps in the pre-war Reich and, by the end, the single biggest KL complex inside Germany. It sat pointedly beside a protected oak associated with Goethe — a desecration prisoners felt keenly. Documented from [[wachsmann-kl-concentration-camps-2015|Wachsmann, *KL* (2015)]].

## Pre-war and the First Executions

Under the corrupt commandant Karl Otto Koch, Buchenwald became a collection camp for "professional criminals" (38% of the population by January 1938) and Jews. After prisoners killed the SS guard Albert Kallweit during a May 1938 escape, the reprisal produced the **first official KL execution** (Emil Bargatzky hanged on the roll-call square, 4 June 1938, with Hitler's approval). Designated a central camp for Jewish prisoners in September 1938, it received ~10,000 "November Jews" after [[kristallnacht-1938|Kristallnacht]], recording 297 of the ~469 Jewish KL deaths of November–December 1938 — the highest of any camp. A late-1938 typhoid epidemic made it the deadliest KL of the period in absolute numbers.

## War: Soviet POWs, Slave Labour, and the "Little Camp"

Buchenwald built a neck-shooting facility modelled on Sachsenhausen's for the 1941 murder of Soviet POWs, and hosted the T-4 selections that sent 384 Jewish men to gas at Bernburg in March 1942. Its **typhus experimental station** (Ding-Schuler and Hoven) ran 24 trials on 1,500+ subjects. As the armaments-satellite system exploded from 1943 (its Gustloff works, and the vast subcamp network), Buchenwald swelled to 106,421 prisoners by March 1945 across 87 satellites. Its notorious **"little camp"** — a quarantine zone in windowless horse stables — held 18,000 by April 1945; ~6,000 died there Jan–Apr 1945, among them Shlomo Wiesel, father of Elie Wiesel.

## Liberation

Ordered fully evacuated by Himmler in April 1945, Buchenwald was partly cleared (28,000 marched out) but 21,000 remained when, on **11 April 1945**, prisoners took control and an international prisoners' committee under the German Communist Hans Eiden raised a white flag as U.S. troops arrived — one of the few camps liberated with large numbers still inside.
