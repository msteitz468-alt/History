# Al-Andalus (Muslim Iberia)

**Al-Andalus** is the name for the Muslim-ruled portion of the Iberian peninsula from
the conquest of 711 to the fall of Granada in 1492. This page covers its
**eleventh- and twelfth-century history** as treated by [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH
IV]] (Hugh Kennedy, Ch. 16); for its tenth-century apogee see
[[caliphate-of-cordoba|the Caliphate of Córdoba]].

## From Caliphate to Taifas

In the year 1000 the [[caliphate-of-cordoba|Caliphate of Córdoba]] was, Kennedy judges,
"almost certainly the richest and most powerful polity in western Europe" — only
Constantinople was comparable — with a prosperous agriculture, a cash economy, a
professional army, and a literate bureaucracy. Yet within a quarter-century the
political unity of al-Andalus was "broken beyond repair." The civil war (*fitna*) that
destroyed the caliphate (1009–1031) left a patchwork of **taifa kingdoms** (*muluk
al-tawa'if*, "party kings") — Seville, Toledo, Saragossa, Granada, Badajoz, and others
— often along the lines of "submerged political structures" (local Arab, Berber, and
*saqaliba* lords) that the caliphate had masked. Crucially, the Andalusi population had
become **demilitarised**, the armies recruited from northern slaves (*saqaliba*) and
North African Berbers; the taifas were wealthy and culturally brilliant but militarily
weak.

## Subjection, Conquest, and the Berber Empires

This weakness exposed al-Andalus to the northern [[christian-kingdoms-of-spain|Christian
kingdoms]], which extracted **parias** (tribute) and, in 1085, took **Toledo**. The
resulting crisis brought in the [[almoravids|Almoravids]] (after 1086) and then the
[[almohads|Almohads]] (after 1147) — North African Berber empires that successively
reunited and defended al-Andalus but ruled it as a province from Marrakesh. Their
defeats — decisively at **Las Navas de Tolosa (1212)** — opened the great
thirteenth-century [[reconquista|reconquests]], reducing al-Andalus by 1248 to the
Nasrid emirate of **Granada**, the last Muslim state in Iberia until 1492.

## Significance

Al-Andalus was the most urbanised, commercialised, and learned society of the western
Mediterranean for much of this period, a centre of philosophy, science, poetry, and
[[ashkenazi-jewry|Sephardic Jewish]] culture (the "Golden Age"), and the principal
conduit (with Sicily) of Greco-Arabic learning into the Latin
[[twelfth-century-renaissance|West]]. Its eleventh-century fragmentation, and the
North African interventions it provoked, are central to the history of the
[[reconquista|Reconquista]] and the medieval Mediterranean.
