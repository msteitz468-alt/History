## Overview

Lepcis Magna, in present-day Al-Khums, Libya, was the birthplace of the emperor
[[septimius-severus|Septimius Severus]] (b. 145). Originally a Phoenician colony, likely
a sub-colony of [[palmyra|Carthage]], it retained bilingual Punic/Latin inscriptions and
a predominantly Phoenician aristocracy for centuries after becoming a Roman colony in
112 BC, and still nominally regarded Tyre as its "mother city" as late as the mid-2nd
century AD.

## Ball's Core Thesis: "A Conscious Oriental Transplant in the West" (THESIS)

Lepcis Magna anchors what Ball presents as the central claim of his chapter on the
Severan dynasty: that Septimius Severus's monumental rebuilding of his native city after
becoming emperor was "almost wholly eastern in character" — in Ball's words, "a conscious
oriental transplant in the West." Ball cites as evidence: the four-way Severan Arch (an
eastern tetrapylon form, depicting Julia Domna and a siege of an "oriental city," carved
probably by eastern artists from Aphrodisias); a new monumental quarter built around a
circular plaza, a form Ball says is otherwise paralleled only at [[gerasa|Jerash]], not
anywhere in the West; a monumental nymphaeum built in the Syrian "kalybe" style, found
nowhere else in the western Roman world; a 400-meter colonnaded street leading to the
circular plaza, which Ball calls "an entirely eastern urban feature … with no occurrences
in the Roman West" and the sole example of the form outside the Near East; and the
Severan Forum, whose walled, single-temple-dominated plan Ball compares directly to
[[baalbek|Baalbek]]'s Temple of Jupiter Heliopolitanus and [[palmyra|Palmyra]]'s Temple of
Bel, rather than to conventional Roman forum plans such as the Forum of Trajan.

Ball describes the colonnaded street in particular as representing, "more than any other
monument at Lepcis … a deliberate orientalism." He attributes the Severan Forum's temple
dedication (probably carried out by Caracalla in 216) to the Severan family itself,
rather than to the Capitoline triad or the cult of Rome and Augustus, and credits the
entire Severan building program at Lepcis to a single unnamed master architect "whose
eastern origins cannot be in doubt."

Ball further argues (THESIS) that behind Septimius Severus and his architect stood
[[julia-domna|Julia Domna]], funded by the "prodigious wealth of the Emesene temple," and
frames the whole ensemble as embodying a "Severan Revolution": oriental cults
anticipating Christianity, Phoenician/Syrian syncretism anticipating monotheism,
Caracalla and Geta's proposed East-West division anticipating Diocletian's later
reforms, [[caracalla|Elagabalus]]'s priest-emperorship anticipating both the Papacy and
the Byzantine Christian emperors, and dynastic succession upheld chiefly by the Severan
women anticipating hereditary monarchy generally. Ball suggests Septimius's massive
investment in Lepcis over Rome itself, together with Caracalla's later choice of
Byzantium as a headquarters, anticipated Constantine's move of the imperial capital: "In
the stones of Lepcis Magna the long march to Constantinople had begun."

This reading is Ball's interpretive thesis, not a settled consensus; mainstream
scholarship treats the Severan building program at Lepcis primarily within a Roman
provincial-architecture framework — see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]] for the
broader debate over Ball's "orientalization" argument.

## An Etymological Aside

Ball notes, as a coincidence he calls "particularly appropriate," that both
[[emesa|Emesa]] (Julia Domna's home city, modern Homs, Syria) and Lepcis Magna today
share the same modern Arabic town name, Homs/Al-Khums — an etymological point about the
modern place-name near the ancient site, not a claim that the two ancient cities were the
same place or otherwise historically identical.
