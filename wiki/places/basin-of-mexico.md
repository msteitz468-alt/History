# Basin of Mexico

## Overview

The **Basin of Mexico** (also called the Valley of Mexico) is a highland enclosed basin in
the Central Mexican highlands, covering roughly 8,000 km² at a floor elevation of
approximately 2,240 m (7,350 feet) above sea level. Ringed on the west, south, and east
by mountains rising above 3,000 m — including the volcanoes Ixtaccíhuatl (5,286 m) and
Popocatépetl (5,452 m) in the southeast — it was the heartland of successive Mesoamerican
hegemonies: [[cuicuilco]], [[teotihuacan]], the Toltec state at Tula, and the Aztec Empire.
Its fertile soils, lake-system agriculture, perennial springs, and proximity to major
obsidian sources made it the most powerful agricultural zone in the Central Mexican
highlands. The Mexico City metropolitan area now covers most of the former lake bed and
houses more than 21 million people.

## Geographic and Environmental Context

The Basin is "a broad plain that slopes gently upward from a floor about 2,240 m above
sea level," limited by mountains on the west, south, and east (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 2). The
northwestern margin is poorly defined, leading by a low divide to the Tula drainage region
whose streams flow into the Gulf of Mexico. The **northeastern Teotihuacan Valley** opens
via a gentle gradient onto the plains of Tlaxcala and central Puebla. Defining the Basin
strictly by its mountain ring gives dimensions of roughly 110 km north-south by 80 km
east-west.

**Lakes**: Interconnected shallow lakes once occupied the central Basin — Xaltocan and
Zumpango in the north, Xochimilco and Chalco in the south, with the moderately saline
Lake Texcoco in the middle (saline because it lacked exterior drainage). In the Aztec
period these were major sources of food and transport and supported intensive **chinampa**
(wetland raised-field) agriculture, especially in the southern lakes. In Teotihuacan times
the Basin's population was concentrated in the northeast; only about 100 ha of the
Teotihuacan Valley were suitable for wetland agriculture.

**Rainfall**: Seasonal, mostly June–October; averaging around 600 mm/year in the
Teotihuacan Valley, over 1,000 mm in parts of Morelos and the southern Basin, and only
400–600 mm near Tula (Sanders et al. 1979). Precipitation varies greatly year to year and
over short distances, with violent localized storms. The Storm God was therefore one of
Teotihuacan's principal deities.

**Irrigation**: No large streams are available. Irrigation from perennial springs — most
notably the springs near the parish church of San Juan Teotihuacán, which in 1954
supplied some 3,650 ha of irrigated land at an average flow of about 590 liters per second
(Sanders et al. 1979: 258) — was the foundation of intensive agriculture in the
northeastern Basin. Outside the Teotihuacan Valley, perennial springs are few and
scattered. All major types of water management (wetland farming, floodwater irrigation,
spring-fed canals) appear to have been present by Teotihuacan times, and were likely
"well developed several centuries before the beginning of the city" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 2).

## The Teotihuacan Valley

The Teotihuacan Valley is the "broad and gently sloping northeastern part of the Basin of
Mexico, framed on the north by hills and an extinct volcano (Cerro Gordo), the Patlachique
range on the south, and more distant mountains on the east" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 2). It is
about 35 km long, covering roughly 500 km². Most ruins of the ancient city of Teotihuacan
lie in the middle section, at altitudes ranging from 2,265 m near the springs to 2,350 m
in its most uphill parts. The nearly flat terrain contrasts with many other major
Mesoamerican sites (e.g., hilltop Monte Albán), and this flatness facilitated the
extraordinarily regular layout of Teotihuacan.

Subsoil in most of the valley is **tepetate** — agriculturally sterile compacted volcanic
ash, hard and yellowish-brown — beneath the dark fertile topsoil. In Teotihuacan times the
water table was not far below the surface; recent decades have seen dramatic drops
following deep-drilled wells.

## Resources

**Obsidian**: Two key sources: a gray obsidian deposit about 18 km east of Teotihuacan
near Otumba (used especially for bifacial points and knives); and the **Sierra de las
Navajas** near Pachuca, about 55 km north, source of distinctive high-quality **greenish
(Pachuca) obsidian** prized for prismatic blades. The Pachuca obsidian became a
cornerstone of Teotihuacan's long-distance trade. Obsidian from more distant sources (e.g.,
Ucareo in Michoacán) was also used.

**Limestone**: No significant local deposits. The nearest sources were 30–40 km northwest
(Zumpango region), with additional sources near Tula (~55 km) and over 100 km southeast.
Obtaining limestone for the lime plaster that coated Teotihuacan's surfaces was therefore
a major logistical challenge; control of limestone source regions may have been an early
incentive for Teotihuacan's territorial expansion.

**Clay, basalt, andesite**: Ceramics were made from clay deposits within the Teotihuacan
Valley. Grinding implements came from local basalt exposures or more distant sources.
Light-colored andesite (cantera) from nearby sources including the Texcoco area (~20 km
south) was used for cut stone at the Feathered Serpent Pyramid and elsewhere.

**Lapidary imports**: Greenstone (fuchsite, serpentine), jadeite, mica, iron pyrites,
slate, cinnabar, and Pacific and Gulf coast shells were all imported from greater distances.

## History of Settlement and Political Control

The earliest pottery-using settlements appear in the Basin by 2000 BCE or earlier. In the
Teotihuacan Valley there were "only a few small settlements" through about 1000 BCE.

During what Mesoamericanists call the Late Preclassic (c. 500–100 BCE), the Basin became
a major player in Mesoamerican developments. [[Cuicuilco]], in the southwest, grew to at
least 20,000 people and emerged as the dominant polity, with ceramic influence throughout
the Basin. The Basin may have had a four-tier settlement hierarchy by this time.
Simultaneously, in the northeastern Teotihuacan Valley, small Cuanalán-tradition villages
clustered near the springs and wetlands.

Around 100 BCE, Teotihuacan began explosive growth in the Patlachique phase, rising to
rival and then surpass Cuicuilco. By the Tzacualli phase (1–100 CE), Teotihuacan
contained perhaps 60,000–80,000 people and dominated the Basin politically and
demographically, with most of the remainder of the Basin's population sharply declining.
At its peak (Xolalpan phase, c. 350–550 CE), Teotihuacan's population approached 100,000
and the city covered about 20 km², making it "the largest city in the western hemisphere"
for much of this time (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 1).

Following Teotihuacan's collapse (c. 550–650 CE), political dominance in the Basin shifted
repeatedly. Later hegemons — the Toltec state at Tula and the Aztec Empire at Tenochtitlan
— built on Teotihuacan's legacy, as shown by Aztec reverence for and ritual reinterpretation
of Teotihuacan monuments.

## Why the Basin Mattered

The Basin was the pivot of Mesoamerican history for nearly two millennia because it combined:
(1) a productive agricultural system (springs, wetlands, chinampas) capable of sustaining
very large populations; (2) proximity to critical raw materials, especially green obsidian;
(3) topographic defensibility (mountain barriers) combined with good routes of access to
neighboring regions (especially the northeastern valley into Tlaxcala-Puebla and beyond to
the Gulf Lowlands); and (4) a lacustrine core enabling bulk transport by water (important
for the Aztecs). The settlement-pattern surveys of Sanders et al. (1979) and Parsons (1971,
2008) — the most extensive archaeological settlement surveys ever conducted in Mesoamerica —
provide the foundational dataset for Basin history.

## Historiography

The Basin of Mexico has one of the densest archaeological records in the Americas. The
**Teotihuacan Mapping Project** (Millon 1962–73) mapped ~30 km² of the ancient city.
Sanders et al.'s (1979) settlement survey covers most of the Basin from earliest pottery-
using societies through the 1970s; for many sites destroyed by modern development, this
survey is now the only record. Cowgill (2015) notes that Teotihuacan studies are in "an
early stage" and many questions remain open. Geological work by Siebe (2000) clarified the
dates and sequence of eruptions (Popocatépetl ~200–1 BCE; Xitle ~200–400 CE), overturning
earlier assumptions about volcanic destruction of Cuicuilco. Collection coverage of the
Basin is strongest for Teotihuacan period archaeology, moderate for the Aztec period, and
thin for post-Teotihuacan intermediate periods.
