# Scandinavia (Early Medieval)

## Overview

Early medieval **Scandinavia** — the Scandinavian homeland of the Vikings — was the seedbed
of the [[viking-age]] and the site of the first northern experiments in state formation. In
[[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Lund, Ch. 8) the region is reconstructed largely from
archaeology and from the later, king-less society of Iceland.

## Early State Formation in Denmark

The first concentration of power emerged in **Denmark** in the early eighth century, attested by
three monumental works requiring organised resources: the planned trading centre at **Ribe** in
southwest Jutland (c. 704–710), with coinage by c. 720; the **Kanhave canal** cut across the island
of Samsø (726) to control sea traffic in the Belts; and the first **Danevirke** (737), a 10-km
earthwork guarding the southern Danish frontier. The only named Danish king of the period, **Ongendus**
(encountered by the missionary Willibrord), is plausibly behind this early central power, which may
have inherited the North Sea maritime hegemony of the early Merovingians.

## Society: The Evidence of Iceland

Because **Iceland** was settled from Scandinavia but had no king, NCMH II treats it as the clearest
window onto pre-state Scandinavian society. Authority there lay with chieftains (***goðar***) whose
power was personal rather than territorial, exercised over freemen who commended themselves to a
particular *goði* and met in assemblies called ***thing***. Over time, rivalries consolidated these
into larger territorial lordships (*ríki*). Recent scholarship has revised the older picture of a
society of independent free peasants: holdings were often large, worked by numerous dependants and
slaves, and much land was held by magnates and worked by tenants.

## Significance

Scandinavia's combination of seafaring skill, commercial wealth (Ribe, later Hedeby and Birka), and
incipient royal organisation generated both the Viking expansion outward and, in the longer run, the
medieval kingdoms of Denmark, Norway, and Sweden.

**Additional evidence from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004 (range 06):** Birka (A.D. 750–970, island in Lake Mälaren west of Stockholm) yielded abundant local and imported textiles (linen, wool, Byzantine silk) preserved by metallic salts, plus evidence of craft workshops. Oseberg ship burial (southeastern Norway, A.D. 834 wealthy female grave) contained textile production tools (weaving tablets with unfinished braid). Jewelry workshops documented at Helgö, Birka, Ribe, and Hedeby. Clinker boatbuilding tradition and dugout expansion techniques supported trade and colonization (see early-medieval-boatbuilding concept). North Atlantic *landnám* (Iceland c. 874, Greenland c. 985) originated from these Scandinavian homelands and emporia, with rapid landscape impacts documented archaeologically.

## Historiography

The early Scandinavian record is overwhelmingly **archaeological** (settlements, burials, earthworks,
coin finds), supplemented by external observers (Frankish missionary lives and annals) and by much
later Icelandic saga and law material that must be used with great caution for the eighth and ninth
centuries. See [[viking-age]].
