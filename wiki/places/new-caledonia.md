## Overview

New Caledonia (Grande Terre and surrounding islands) is the southernmost major Melanesian island group, located approximately 1,200 km east of Queensland, Australia. It was formally annexed by France in 1853 — the only French Pacific dependency that offered genuine settlement prospects — and became both a penal colony modelled on convict-built Australia and the site of the Pacific's most significant nickel-mining industry. Its Melanesian population (Kanak) was subjected to a cantonnement (reserve) system, unique among French dependencies in the Pacific, to make room for settlers.

## History of Control and Settlement

### Pre-European and Early Contact
New Caledonia was part of the Melanesian sandalwood-trading zone; Sydney-based traders were active here from the early nineteenth century. Sandalwood was "also the focus of trade in Fiji, New Caledonia and the New Hebrides." Islander knowledge of comparative values grew rapidly: "Islanders soon acquired better knowledge of comparative values. As knowledge grew, so did the quantity, quality and variety of trade goods." By the 1840s, sandalwood expeditions (perhaps 150 in ten years across Vanuatu/New Caledonia) were a dominant economic presence.

### French Annexation (1853)
France annexed New Caledonia in 1853. The text notes the contextual irony: "precisely when the transportation of convicts to eastern Australia was ending — a penal colony modelled on convict-built Australia." New Caledonia was "the only French dependency which confined indigenous people to cantonnements (reserves), to make room for settlers." This made it structurally more similar to Australian settler colonialism than to other French Pacific possessions (Tahiti, Wallis and Futuna).

### Mining Economy
John Higginson — born in Britain, settled in New Caledonia in 1859 — was the central figure in the colony's economic development. He began as a government contractor, cornered Australian supply, and became the conduit for mining capital:
- Gold at Fern Hill (discovered 1870) — Higginson acquired the mine outright.
- Copper: floated a copper-mining company with William Morgan; when that capital was spent, "persuaded the Rothschilds to invest."
- Nickel: "In 1880 with further support from Rothschilds, Higginson launched Société le Nickel. The Rothschilds took over Le Nickel in 1890 and nursed it through depressed prices until a cartel raised price levels in 1895."

New Caledonia's nickel thus became integrated into global finance capital through the Rothschild network — a qualitatively different economic trajectory from copra-based Polynesia.

### Caledonian Company of the New Hebrides and Land Grab
Recruiting labour for the nickel mines, Higginson moved into the New Hebrides (Vanuatu). In 1882 he "set out with piles of trade goods and the heroic vision of buying the islands piecemeal." By 1886 his Caledonian Company of the New Hebrides had "paper claims to 780,000 hectares — about half the entire land area" of the New Hebrides. Much was "bought" from Melanesians at fraudulent prices: MacLeod (a European planter) sold at 263 francs per hectare; Melanesians in eastern Efate "sold" for 0.07 francs. When Higginson died in 1904, "even the Caledonian Company had failed, along with much else. More investors than Islanders had been fleeced."

### Indentured Labour
New Caledonia drew indentured workers from multiple sources:
- Japanese workers until World War I (when they were redirected to Micronesia)
- Vietnamese workers, carried by the Ballande company from their wine-shipping base in Bordeaux: "their interests led them, by the same business logic, to recruit labourers (and carry their rations) from Vietnam to New Caledonia"
- Indian workers: "a few Indians were shipped to New Caledonia in the 1860s"
- New Hebrideans (Vanuatu): "thousands of New Hebrideans continued to sign on for New Caledonia, where crass treatment and working conditions continued, and even deteriorated" after 1900

The ethnic mix of the labour force reflected the powerlessness of each group in proportion to its distance from political influence.

### Missionary Activity
Marist missionaries from New Caledonia were despatched into the New Hebrides at Higginson's instigation "to redeem the archipelago from Protestantism and to forestall British annexation." The venture was a failure: a veteran Marist missionary wrote resignedly that "we're going there accompanied by the military and at the behest of political agitators." Protestant missionaries — Presbyterian and Anglican — were already entrenched, and "inspired by the Protestants' bigotry, Islanders were contemptuous of the poverty of the priests (who travelled by canoe while Protestants sailed their own vessels)."

## Strategic Significance

New Caledonia was strategically important to France as its sole Pacific colony with genuine settlement potential; it justified the naval deployments and diplomatic manoeuvres (including annexation of the Marquesas in 1841 and the Tahiti protectorate in 1842) that France used to establish a Pacific presence against British dominance. Unlike Australia or New Zealand, however, French colonisation was "markedly less successful" in building a regional maritime and commercial empire.

The island's significance changed dramatically in the twentieth century as nickel reserves proved among the largest in the world. The Société le Nickel (SLN) established by Higginson remained the dominant economic force. Indigenous Kanak land rights and political autonomy became the central political conflict of the late twentieth century, culminating in the Matignon Accords (1988) and Nouméa Accord (1998) — events beyond this source's scope.
