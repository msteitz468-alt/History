# The Western Mediterranean (Bronze & Early Iron Age)

The lands of the central and western Mediterranean — Italy, the great islands
(Sicily, Malta, Sardinia, Corsica, the Balearics), Iberia, southern France, and the
North African Maghreb — in the long prehistory **before the historical peoples
(Phoenicians, Greeks, Etruscans, Romans)** emerge. [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol.
II.2]] (Ch. XXXVII — Trump, Daniel and others) surveys the whole region from the
Palaeolithic to the early Iron Age; this page captures the **Late Bronze and Early
Iron Age** strata most relevant to the volume's c. 1380–1000 BCE horizon, and the
threshold at which these cultures meet the literate Mediterranean. (The deep
prehistory overlaps wiki coverage of the [[neolithic|Neolithic]] and
[[early-bronze-age|Bronze Age]].)

## Italy

A long sequence of indigenous cultures, with the **Po valley and Alpine region**
consistently the most advanced (in close contact with Central Europe across the
"amber route"), and the **Apennine peninsula** more pastoral and Aegean-facing:

- **The Terramare** — Bronze Age timber-framed villages of the Po valley, rich in
  Central-European bronze types (and the Mycenaean-influenced "Peschiera" dagger),
  some raised on piles against flooding.
- **The Apennine culture** — a fairly uniform peninsular culture of transhumant
  **stock-breeders** (cheese-strainers, milk-boilers; sites to 6,000 ft), whose
  pottery reached the Lipari Islands and Ischia in **Mycenaean** (LH I–III) contexts —
  the western edge of the [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] trade network.
- **The Urnfield incursions (~1100 BCE)** — small, fast-moving **cremating** peoples
  from Central Europe who swept the peninsula, ushering in the final Bronze Age and
  the prelude to the Iron Age.
- **The Iron Age (from ~950 BCE)** — strongly regional: the **Golasecca** culture of
  the Alpine zone; above all the **Villanovan** culture (Bologna and Tuscany/Latium),
  whose cemeteries lie beneath the later Etruscan ones and which "obviously provided
  the basic population which went to the making of the **Etruscan** civilization";
  the Latial hut-urn culture (ancestral to **Rome**); and the southern "fossa grave"
  and Apulian groups that soon imitated **Greek geometric** pottery as colonization
  approached.

## Sicily, Malta and the Aeolian Islands

A dense sequence registering successive Aegean contacts: the spectacular **megalithic
temple culture of Malta** (Ġgantija–Tarxien, late third millennium, ended
mysteriously); the **Castelluccio**, **Thapsos**, and **Pantalica** cultures of
Sicily; and the Lipari sequence (Capo Graziano → Milazzese → the **Ausonian** culture
brought by mainland Apennine invaders, linked to the legend of Liparos). **Mycenaean
pottery** is abundant at Thapsos and Milazzese (LH IIIA–B), marking the height of
Aegean trade ~1400–1200 BCE; with the collapse, Sicilian contacts shifted toward
**Cyprus and the Levant** — "the rise of [[phoenicians|Phoenician]] mercantile
enterprise after 1000 BCE."

## Sardinia and the Sherden

Sardinia is dominated by the **Nuraghic civilization** — the builders of the ~7,000
stone tower-fortresses (*nuraghi*), the "giants' tombs" (*tombe di giganti*), and the
famous votive **bronzes** (warriors, archers, model ships). It began c. 1400 BCE and
ran (Archaic → Middle → Late Nuraghic) down into the Iron Age, the indigenous society
the **[[phoenicians|Phoenicians]]** met when they founded Sulcis, Nora, Caralis and
Tharros in the eighth century. Daniel connects it to the **Sherden** of the
[[sea-peoples|Sea Peoples]]: of the two views — that the Sherden came *from* Sardinia,
or *from* Sardis in Asia Minor and later settled the island that took their name — he
prefers the **Asia Minor (Sardis) origin**, seeing the Sherden as warlike eastern
chiefs who, after raiding Egypt, helped found or reinforce the Nuraghic order. **Corsica**
runs parallel with its **Torrean** (*torri*) culture and its remarkable statue-menhirs.

## Iberia, the Balearics and the Maghreb

Iberia's Bronze Age is anchored by the **El Argar** culture of the southeast (fortified
hilltop towns, intramural cist/jar burial, strong eastern-Mediterranean affinities),
preceded by the "colonial" metallurgical settlements of **Los Millares** and the
**Bell Beaker** horizon. From the ninth–eighth centuries, **Urnfield** and then
**Hallstatt (Celtic)** peoples infiltrated from southern France into the centre and
west, while the southern and eastern coasts developed the orientalizing **Iberian**
culture under Punic and Greek influence. Here too lies the legendary kingdom of
**Tartessus** (Andalucía) — perhaps the biblical **Tarshish**, source of the silver
and tin of the West — and the tradition of the Phoenician colony of **Gades (Cadiz)**,
"founded in 1100 BCE," though the earliest firm archaeological evidence for
Phoenicians in Spain is eighth-century. The **Balearics** evolved the fortified
**Talayotic** culture (the *talayot* towers, the Minorcan *taulas*, the *naveta*
tombs). The **Maghreb** remained, by contrast, a land of Neolithic pastoralists into
the first millennium — the desiccation of the Sahara having cut it off from the
flourishing of the northern shores.

## Why this place mattered

The western Mediterranean is the **far end of the Bronze Age world-system**: the
terminus of the Mycenaean and then Cypriot/Levantine trade routes, the source of
western **metals** (Iberian and Sardinian copper, silver, and the tin that the
"ships of Tarshish" carried east), and the home of the indigenous societies —
**Villanovan/Etruscan, Latial/Roman, Nuraghic, Iberian, Tartessian** — onto which the
**[[phoenicians|Phoenician]]** and Greek colonizations of the early first millennium
would be grafted. It is where the prehistoric Mediterranean meets recorded history.

## Historiography

Ch. XXXVII is a sweeping, radiocarbon-aware survey (the chapter stresses how C-14
dating was then **overturning** the old three-age and "ex oriente lux" diffusionist
schemes, pushing key western developments — Maltese temples, Iberian metallurgy —
earlier and making them more independent of the Aegean). Its weaknesses are those of
its date: pre-1975 chronologies, and a region where the collection is otherwise thin.
Per CLAUDE.md the Americas and sub-Saharan Africa are critical gaps; the western
Mediterranean Bronze/Iron Age is **moderately** served, and this chapter is the wiki's
main anchor for it pending later, dedicated Etruscan, Roman, Phoenician-colonial, and
Iberian sources.
