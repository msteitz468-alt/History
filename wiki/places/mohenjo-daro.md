# Mohenjo-daro and the Indus Cities

## Overview

**Mohenjo-daro** (>200 ha, the largest and most iconic) anchors this page on
**Indus (Harappan) urbanism** of the Mature Indus phase (**~2600–1900 BCE**),
drawn from [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] Ch. 15 (Carla Sinopoli). It
extends the South Asian story begun in [[early-agriculture-south-asia]]
(Mehrgarh) into the urban era. The other Indus cities — **Harappa, Dholavira,
Ganweriwala, Rakhigarhi** — are folded in.

## "Splendid isolation"

Sinopoli's central point: across the Mature Indus distribution (1,000+ sites over
**>1 million km²**), only **five sites are urban**, separated by enormous
distances (Harappa to Mohenjo-daro: 600 km) — "islands of urbanism in a vast sea
of villages." This makes the [[city-state]] model strained: if each city
controlled its surroundings, individual territories would span 100,000–170,000
km². The Indus political order was almost certainly more varied than any single
model allows.

## Urban form

Indus cities share a distinctive plan: a high western **"citadel" mound** with
monumental non-residential buildings (at Mohenjo-daro: the **Great Bath**,
"Granary," "College") on massive mudbrick platforms, and a lower **eastern town**
of dense residential blocks along gridded streets and lanes, with sophisticated
**hydraulics** — wells, drains, and (at Dholavira) reservoirs and canals.
Standardized **weights**, baked-brick construction, and the undeciphered **Indus
script** (seals) point to shared norms across the civilization.

## Power without obvious kings

Notably, the Indus cities lack unambiguous **palaces or royal tombs** and produce
little ruler iconography (the so-called "priest-king" sculpture is a lone, much-
overinterpreted figure). Some craft production (e.g. stoneware bangles) appears
administratively controlled, but the distribution of power is unclear — another
case feeding the [[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]] debate.

## Colonial baggage and decline

Sinopoli flags the persistent colonial trope of **"foreign inspiration"**
(Mesopotamian) for Indus cities — a bias the 1924 discovery (via shared seals at
Ur/Susa) inadvertently reinforced. The urban phase ended gradually (~1900 BCE,
not an abrupt collapse). A **second, independent South Asian urbanization** — the
Early Historic "landscape of cities" of the Ganges — followed more than a
millennium later.

## Why this place mattered

The Indus is a major independent hearth of [[urbanization]] whose **dispersed**
urban geography and **muted hierarchy** make it a critical outlier in the
comparative study of early cities.

## Avari (2007): Detailed Description

Avari's treatment (Chapter 3) adds the following specifics:

**The bricks:** The kiln-baked bricks at Mohenjo Daro were all of standard size, measuring 7 × 14 × 28 cm, with the ratio of thickness to width to length being 1:2:4. The city contained "massive" stocks of these bricks.

**City layout:** In the west was a high citadel mound with strong fortifications; in the east was the lower city with smaller mounds on a grid pattern. Major north–south and east–west streets intersected each other, with numerous smaller streets and alleyways criss-crossing. Houses did not open onto the main streets. The city had approximately 700 wells. Each house had its own well, with cisterns and reservoirs ensuring citizens "had sufficient water for drinking, cooking, bathing and cleaning."

**The Great Bath:** Described by its excavator John Marshall as "the most imposing of all the remains unearthed at Mohenjo Daro." It measures 180 ft (N–S) × 108 ft (E–W) overall, with the swimming bath 39 ft long × 23 ft broad and sunk 8 ft below the paving, with steps at each end. The tank lining was finely dressed brick in gypsum mortar, 3–4 ft thick, backed by an inch-thick bitumen waterproofing course, then another thin wall of burnt brick, then packing of crude brick, then another solid rectangle of burnt brick. Marshall's own description: "A more effective method of construction with the materials then available would hardly have been possible, and how well it has stood the test of time is sufficiently apparent from the present state of the tank which, after 5000 years, is still astonishingly well preserved." The water was most probably considered holy, reserved for ritual washing by groups of priests.

**Power without palaces:** No palaces or rich tombs have been found. The most famous sculpture retrieved from the site — the "priest-king" figure now in the National Museum in Karachi — is only 17.8 cm high. The so-called Dancing Girl, a bronze product made by the lost-wax process, is "quite nude except for her bangles and a necklace."

**Discovery history:** D.R. Bhandarkar visited the site in 1911 and dismissed it as "only 200 years old." R.D. Banerji began excavating in 1919 and correctly identified the site as very ancient. John Marshall worked the site intensively from 1925 to 1927, and Mortimer Wheeler brought new methods of controlled stratigraphic excavation from 1944.

**The granary question:** A building on the citadel mound "looks like a granary, although archaeologists are not entirely sure." There is "disagreement among the archaeologists as to the identification of the actual granaries," but storage depots and food warehouses certainly existed.

**Collapse:** By 1700 BCE the city had become a ghost town. The silt layers in its upper levels — "many feet above the river course" — attest to the great floods that followed river course changes. The drains and sewers "kept in good and clean working order for centuries, clogged up with waste and excreta, resulting in disease and pestilence."

## Fagan & Durrani (2020): pace of growth, trade debate, and a source-flagged inconsistency

Fagan & Durrani (2020) sharpen the comparative point already made above (Sinopoli):
the Indus civilization underwent **"a short period of explosive growth over one or
two centuries ending about 2500 bc"** — in explicit contrast to "the long phase of
increasing... complexity in Egypt and Mesopotamia." Mohenjo-daro's population is
estimated at **35,000–40,000** (Harappa c. 23,500), and the city was **rebuilt at
least nine times**. The undeciphered Indus script (~400 pictographic symbols) and
the absence of known ruler names or images remain, as elsewhere noted, a striking
contrast to Egyptian and Mesopotamian royal iconography.

**Meluhha trade debate.** After 2600 BCE the Sumerians reorganized Gulf maritime
trade via **Dilmun, Magan, and Meluhha** (Meluhha probably referring to the Indus
region); Sargon of Agade (c. 2350 BCE) boasted of ships from these lands moored at
his capital. This is **contested**: Fagan & Durrani note "many scholars believe
overseas trade was less important than sometimes claimed and that Indus
civilization was an entirely indigenous development" — a caution against reading
Mesopotamian contact as formative for Indus urbanism.

**Source-flagged inconsistency.** Fagan & Durrani give the Indus civilization's
area as both "just under 1.3 million square kilometers" and, elsewhere in the same
discussion, "more than 777,000 square kilometers (3,000,000 square miles)" — the
two figures are mutually inconsistent (777,000 km² ≠ 3,000,000 sq mi; 3,000,000 sq
mi ≈ 7.77 million km²). This is flagged here as an apparent error internal to the
source itself, not adjudicated by the wiki.

Source: Fagan & Durrani (2020), *World Prehistory: A Brief Introduction* (10th ed.).
