## Geographic Description

Tahiti is the largest of the Society Islands, a volcanic archipelago in French Polynesia, roughly 4,000 km south of Hawai'i and 5,600 km east of Australia. The island is divided into two roughly circular sections (Tahiti Nui and Tahiti Iti) joined by an isthmus. High central ridges create their own rainfall, supporting dense vegetation and multiple river valleys. Each valley system includes "a coastal strip at the valley mouth, fronting lagoon, reef and offshore waters, all backed by a wedge-shaped segment containing the island's major resources" (Ward, cited in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]]). Land tenure followed this valley structure: "it appears all Tahitians had access to the valley, coastal and marine resource zones contained in the particular valley system in which they lived."

## Pre-Contact Society and Land Tenure

Tahitian society was a ranked chiefdom system. Land was divided into valley-based wedge-shaped territories giving communities cross-section access to all ecological zones. Political units formed around these valley systems — "each system, and its inhabitants formed a single political unit within large tribal groups" (Finney, *Polynesian Peasants and Proletarians*). The Society Islands were part of the broader eastern Polynesian exchange community, with chiefs deriving authority partly from genealogical connections to divine ancestors and partly from accumulation and redistribution of bark cloth (tapa), feather valuables, and food.

The arioi — a special class of servants of the god 'Oro — played important ceremonial and redistributive roles. Feather girdles were "the currency of authority. They conferred title and rank." Tahitian chiefs commanded mana (spiritual power); common people "belonged to the land" while chiefs were associated with divine origins.

## Significance in Pacific Contact History

Tahiti became the most consequential site of early European-Islander encounter in the Pacific, principally because:

1. **Bougainville's romantic account** (1768) transformed European perception of the South Seas from a place of "ferocious savages" to an island paradise. His "rhapsodic depiction" was "quickly taken up by philosophers, influencing European ideas about the condition of humankind in a 'state of nature'." Bougainville likened Tahitians to Greek gods. This triggered the "Pacific Edens" mythology analysed in [[primitive-affluence]].

2. **Matavai Bay** on Tahiti's north coast served as the primary anchorage for European ships: Wallis (1767), Bougainville (1768), Cook (three visits 1769–77), and Bligh (1788–89, 1791–92).

3. **The Bounty episode** (1788–89) and its aftermath brought continuous Tahitian contact with multiple European factions over a generation — Bligh, the mutineers under Christian, and the Royal Navy's pursuit — giving Tahitians unparalleled experience of European internal divisions and motivations.

## Key Events in Contact History

### Wallis, 1767
Samuel Wallis (English) was first to anchor off Tahiti's east coast — greeted by "a hundred canoes filled with men waving banana-leaves and orating." Tahitians boarded and helped themselves freely, consistent with Islander protocols for receiving castaways (visitors surrendering possessions as acknowledgment of hosts' authority). Wallis's crew expelled them with gunfire. After repeated cannon fire causing "many deaths," Tahitian chiefs "had discovered an effective way of placating the strangers … some women of low birth were ordered to prostitute themselves as a political strategy." This tactical deployment of sexual access established a pattern that fed the South Seas paradise myth.

### Bougainville, 1768
His ship was "immediately surrounded by canoe-loads of young women, most of them naked." French understood from male gestures that "the women were being offered for sex." His subsequent published account created the "Nouvelle Cythère" (New Cythera) image of Tahiti as a libertarian paradise.

### Cook's Visits (1769, 1773–74, 1777)
Cook's successive visits gave Tahitians extended experience of a single European figure and enabled sophisticated perceptions to develop. Tahitians had to navigate being simultaneously observed scientifically (Banks and Solander collecting specimens) and politically managed by Cook.

### The Bounty and Dening's Analysis
Captain Bligh arrived in 1788 to collect breadfruit plants. Tahitian cosmology "made provision for receiving and domesticating powerful strangers from the sea. From Wallis's arrival onwards, Tahitians tried with varying success to make the strangers conform to these rituals, whereby their alarming power was rendered understandable, predictable — and to an extent manageable."

When Bligh returned pursuing the mutineers, he observed Pomare's feather girdle into which Tahitians had sewn: (a) a lock of auburn hair from Skinner (the Bounty's red-haired barber, whose ability to "touch tapu places" made him powerful in Tahitian eyes); and (b) the British red pennant that Wallis had erected on June 26, 1767 when he took possession of Tahiti for King George III. "The Tahitians had taken down the symbol of English sovereignty and incorporated it into a symbol of sovereignty of their own." Greg Dening's analysis of this episode illustrates how Tahitians selectively incorporated foreign mana into existing frameworks rather than displacing their own.

### LMS Mission, 1797
The Duff brought four ordained ministers and twenty-five other men to Tahiti, the Marquesas, and Tonga. Pomare received them "auspiciously" for material and political reasons — access to goods and firearms. After the Duff departed, "missionaries failed to provide Pomare with goods and firearms, or to lend moral support for his war and political ambitions" — eleven missionaries left for Sydney. Relations gradually repaired; by 1808 Pomare promised to "banish 'Oro, and to 'cast off evil customs'." By 1820s the Society Islands became "a Christian epicentre from which missions evangelised the Cook Islands, Samoa and Niue."

## Why Tahiti Mattered

**For Europeans**: Tahiti became the conceptual anchor of the "South Seas paradise" myth — reproduced through "songs, novels, poetry, films, advertising and tourist promotions" and "permeated the Western cultural subconscious." This myth was "manifestly remote from realities" — Tahitian motives for sexual exchanges in the early contact period were religious, diplomatic, and economic, not libertarian.

**For Pacific history**: Tahitian experience with multiple European visitors over a generation from 1767 made it the site of the most sophisticated Islander adaptation to European contact in the eighteenth century. The ritual incorporation of Wallis's pennant into Pomare's girdle is the exemplary case of how Islander agency operated in the contact period — not passive reception but active cosmological domestication.

**Demographically**: Like most Polynesian islands, Tahiti suffered severe population decline through introduced disease. Bougainville's estimate of 200,000 inhabitants and other estimates range from 8,000 to 200,000 for the pre-contact period — the uncertainty itself reflects the historiographical problem of reconstructing pre-contact populations from post-contact records. A fair estimate of 66,000 is offered in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]].

## Historiography

The key works on Tahitian-European contact are Dening, *Islands and Beaches* and *Mr Bligh's Bad Language*; Oliver, *Ancient Tahitian Society* (3 vols); Thomas, "The Force of Ethnology"; Howe, *Where the Waves Fall*. Pearson's thesis that Polynesian greeting protocols explain the "theft" accusations in early contact encounters is critiqued by Campbell. Finney, *Polynesian Peasants and Proletarians*, covers the pre-contact land tenure structure.
