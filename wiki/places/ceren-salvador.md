# Cerén (Joyas de Cerén)

## Overview

**Cerén** (Joyas de Cerén) in El Salvador’s **Zapotitán Valley** is the best-preserved **mid-Classic domestic settlement** in southeastern Mesoamerica—a “Pompeii of the New World” buried by the **Laguna Caldera** eruption about **A.D. 600** (Sheets, CHNPA II.1 Ch. 9; Sharer Ch. 10 gives ~A.D. 600/650). Richard Adams highlights Payson Sheets’s excavations for the volume of solid data yielded by outstanding preservation plus superlative field technique. The village sits in the zone recovering from the earlier **Ilopango** disaster (~A.D. 200–250).

## Classic reoccupation context

After Ilopango, central El Salvador was reoccupied by **thoroughly Mesoamerican** peoples with social differentiation, core-blade technology, polychromes, elaborate earthen residences, and long-distance trade—unlike northeast Honduras’s largely self-sufficient, minimally differentiated reoccupation. Primary centers such as **Campana-San Andrés** had formal pyramid-plaza groups and centralized economic control. Fowler & Earnest see Paraiso Basin reoccupation first on the Lempa floodplain then secondary drainages; reoccupants were likely **Ch’orti’ Maya or related**, partly from ceramic similarities with [[copan|Copán]]. (A supposed “codex” at Cerén was later identified as a collapsed polychrome painted gourd—Sheets’s own note.)

## Domestic architecture and household life

- All structures oriented **~30° east of magnetic north**, indicating **village planning** above the household.
- Households built **3–4 specialized buildings**; communal structures most prominent, with elevated benches for sleeping and daytime activity.
- Household 2 niche held a polychrome serving vessel with **finger-swipes of food** still preserved. Niche count may mark status (Structure 3: 4 niches; intermediate: 1; humble: 0).
- Separate **bodegas** for storage/processing; food in large capped ceramic vessels (also ants and fieldmouse intrusion).
- Buildings on fired clay platforms; wattle-and-daub or puddled adobe walls; palm/grass thatch roofs; **unused obsidian blades stored in thatch** (safety for children).

## Regional settlement hierarchy

Survey of 15% of **546 km²** of the Zapotitán Valley found an elaborate Late Classic hierarchy from farmsteads to primary/secondary regional centers (centralized political, economic, and religious components). Black (1983) estimates valley population **≥40,000 Late Classic**, 38,000 Early Postclassic, 27,000 Late Postclassic (~70, 70, and 50 persons/km²). No single “typical” site—complex interrelated settlement types. Sharer places Cerén adjacent to **San Andrés**, buried ~A.D. 650, as a unique everyday-life window during Chalchuapa/Tazumal’s Late Classic prosperity and commercial ties to Copán.

## Public archaeology

Sheets cites Cerén as the 1990s exemplar of **public archaeology**: multidisciplinary research, conservation, community guides, living museum, UNESCO World Heritage management—contrasting earlier elite-focused humanistic work at [[copan|Copán]].
