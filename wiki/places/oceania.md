# Oceania

**Oceania** is the island Pacific — the vast oceanic region conventionally divided into
[[polynesia|Polynesia]], [[melanesia|Melanesia]] and [[micronesia|Micronesia]], together with
the closely linked Maori world of [[new-zealand|New Zealand]]. It is less a landmass than a
"sea of islands" (Epeli Hau'ofa): scattered high volcanic islands, coral atolls and the large
continental islands of New Guinea spread across some 12,600 km of ocean from Palau to Rapa Nui
(Easter Island). [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997|The Cambridge History of the Pacific
Islanders]] treats the region as a single, if internally diverse, field of human history.

## Geographic Description

The threefold division — Polynesia ("many islands"), Melanesia ("black islands") and
Micronesia ("small islands") — is a nineteenth-century European scheme (Dumont d'Urville, 1832)
imposed on the region, and the volume treats it as a colonial-era construct as much as a
description: the boundaries it draws cut across deeper distinctions between **Near Oceania**
(the Bismarck/Solomon region settled in the Pleistocene) and **Remote Oceania** (everything
beyond, settled only after the [[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement|Lapita expansion]] from
c. 1500 BCE). Archaeologists, the volume argues, articulate the strongest sense of Oceania as
an integrated entity, transcending the cultural-complex boundaries that anthropology and
linguistics tend to reinforce.

## History of Control and Settlement

- **Settlement** — Pleistocene colonization of Near Oceania (Australia–New Guinea / Sahul) by
  c. 40,000+ BP; the [[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement|Austronesian/Lapita expansion]] into
  Remote Oceania from c. 1500 BCE; New Zealand settled last, c. 1300 CE.
- **First contact** — sustained European encounters from the sixteenth century, intensifying
  with eighteenth-century exploratory voyages.
- **Colonial partition** — informal nineteenth-century imperialism gave way to formal colonial
  rule under Britain, France, Germany, the United States and (after WWI) Japan, Australia and
  New Zealand; the era produced the [[invention-of-the-native|colonial "native"]].
- **The Pacific War** swept the region 1941–1945; [[pacific-nuclear-testing|nuclear testing]]
  followed in Micronesia, Kiribati and French Polynesia.
- **Decolonisation** from the 1960s left a mosaic of independent states, self-governing polities
  in free association, and persistent dependencies (French Polynesia, New Caledonia, Guam, West
  Papua).

## Significance

Oceania is the **last major region of the earth settled by humans** and, in the modern era, a
theatre where small island societies negotiated whaling, plantations, missions, colonial rule,
total war, nuclear weapons and globalisation. Its history is a standing test case for whether
"island isolation" was a real condition or — as the volume argues — partly an invention of
scholars and administrators.

## Fischer (2002): regional framing and the limits of the three-region scheme

[[fischer-history-pacific-islands-2002|Fischer (2002)]] stresses that by the sixteenth century "most inter-insular and inter-archipelagic networking had collapsed, perhaps because of the Little Ice Age" (L3011–3012); on the eve of European contact active voyaging survived only in three pockets — the Society Islands/NW Tuamotus, Micronesia (where it was essential to atoll survival), and Tongan-dominated Western Polynesia (L3003–3009). He questions the validity of "Melanesia", "Micronesia" and "Polynesia" as *prehistoric* categories, arguing the Polynesian Outliers "witness what most of Pacific Islands might once have resembled" (L1914–1922). Fischer is also pointedly anti-romantic about ecology: "one should toss out any notion of 'happy Pacific Islanders' living in harmony with nature" — Hawai‘i alone lost "at least 1000 species of animals" to Polynesian and later European arrival (L2968–2998). On the colonial encounter he frames the nineteenth century as a mutual absorption rather than one-way assimilation.
