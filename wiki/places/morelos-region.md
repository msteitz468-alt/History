# Morelos Region

## Geographic description

**Morelos** (modern Mexican state) lies immediately south of the [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of Mexico]] across the Ajusco range. In Evans & Webster’s encyclopedia synthesis (Michael E. Smith and Kenneth G. Hirth, “Morelos Region”), the territory is mostly below **1,500 m**, subtropical, with **800–1,100 mm** annual rainfall and mean temperatures about **20–24°C** — almost frost-free below Ajusco. That climate permitted warm-country crops, especially **cotton** and various fruits, that could not grow in the adjacent high Basin. Cotton textiles and **bark paper (*amate*)** were major Postclassic exports into the Basin and beyond.

Of roughly **4,900 km²**, only about **27%** is level enough for rainfall agriculture; some **~500 km²** of alluvial land is irrigable. Pre-Hispanic settlement concentrated in river valleys (Cuernavaca/Cuauhnahuac, Yautepec, Amatzinac), where irrigation is ethnohistorically attested for the Late Postclassic. The region thus functioned as a **symbiotic southern counterpart** to the Basin within Sanders’s Central Mexican Symbiotic Region — cotton, fruit, and paper flowing north against Basin staples, craft goods, and later imperial tribute demand (see [[aztec-economy]]).

## Formative period

Early Formative maize villages of **100–200** persons show ceramic and burial links to [[basin-of-mexico|Basin]] traditions (Río Cuautla-style red-on-brown bottles/stirrup vessels; Tlatilco-related burial customs). At **Gualupita** (near Cuernavaca), the Vaillants’ 1934 excavations defined Gualupita I burials with Early Formative (c. **1100–900 B.C.**) pottery and figurines — the first important Early Formative discovery in Central Mexico, virtually identical to Tlatilco goods (temporal position recognized only decades later); Gualupita II is Late Formative (c. **400–200 B.C.**); Gualupita III is Morelos Late Postclassic (c. **1350–1521**) (David C. Grove, “Gualupita”).

In the Middle Formative, **[[chalcatzingo|Chalcatzingo]]** in the Amatzinac Valley of eastern Morelos emerged as the dominant regional center (population roughly **500–1,000**) until about **500 B.C.**, with Olmec-style rock carvings, civic-ceremonial architecture, an elite cemetery, and exchange with Gulf Olmec, Oaxaca, Guerrero, and Puebla (Smith & Hirth; Grove on Chalcatzingo). By **1100 B.C.** the site was already an important regional trade center, peaking in the Cantera phase (**700–500 B.C.**) before decline around **500 B.C.** (Grove).

## Classic and Epiclassic

Classic-period Morelos lay under the shadow of [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] influence; detailed settlement numbers for the Classic are thinner in the encyclopedia’s regional entry than for Formative and Postclassic poles. In the **Epiclassic** (c. A.D. **650–900**), populations drop relative to the Teotihuacan horizon, Teotihuacan influence ends, and warfare rises. **[[xochicalco|Xochicalco]]** — about 25 km southwest of Cuernavaca, on five hills with Cerro Xochicalco at the center — grew rapidly from c. **A.D. 650** into one of the largest and most influential Central Mexican Epiclassic centers, contemporaneous with [[cacaxtla|Cacaxtla]], Teotenango, and declining Coyotlatelco Teotihuacan (Kenneth G. Hirth, “Xochicalco”; Smith & Hirth).

At its ninth-century height Xochicalco held an estimated **10,000–15,000** persons (residential densities **80–120/ha**; **12–14** wards of 100–300 persons mixing elite and non-elite). Nested defenses (ramparts, ditches, dry moats, terraces, guard platforms) and five excavated obsidian workshops (one in a marketplace plaza) signal a militarized trade hub controlling the Balsas corridor. The **Pyramid of the Plumed Serpents** combines talud-tablero with Maya-style seated figures; its tablero lists conquered towns paying tribute (**~30 names**); three stelae name the earliest known Central Mexican kings (birth, death, coronation, conquests) — a stela medium borrowed from Zapotec or Maya, with art rejecting Teotihuacan canons. Around **A.D. 900** the center was **catastrophically destroyed and rapidly abandoned** by local revolt or military conquest (public buildings burned/sacked). Other fortified Epiclassic hilltops in Morelos include Cerro Jumil and Cerro Tenayo (Smith & Hirth).

## Postclassic: Tlahuica city-states and Aztec provinces

**Middle Postclassic** immigrants — **Tlahuica** and Xochimilca associated with the Aztlan migration tradition — founded city-states and the Tlahuica Polychrome ceramic tradition. Morelos peoples are often termed “Aztecs” for linguistic and cultural similarity to Basin Nahuas (Smith & Hirth). **Cuauhnahuac** (Cuernavaca) was the Tlahuica capital: Middle Postclassic **Teopanzolco** carries a twin-stair pyramid (style later of the Templo Mayor at [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]) with a mass sacrificial burial; the Late Postclassic center moved across a ravine. Cuauhnahuac itself conquered more than **20** Tlahuica city-states until Mexica conquest in **1438** as a tributary province (Michael E. Smith; Feinman economic notes).

**Late Postclassic** intensification included hillside terraces and canal irrigation of alluvium. Powerful polities — Cuauhnahuac, Yautepec, Huaxtepec — were conquered by Mexica kings **Itzcoatl** and **Motecuhzoma I** and organized as the **Cuauhnahuac** and **Huaxtepec** tributary provinces of the [[aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] (Smith & Hirth). Rural villages **Cuexcomate** and **Capilco** show architectural (more than artifactual) elite/commoner distinctions, with sumptuary items also in non-elite contexts; households produced cotton textiles and amate paper and imported Central Mexican ceramics/obsidian and Tarascan copper-bronze tools. Royal gardens and nurseries at **Huaxtepec** (and Acatetelco) figure among Aztec horticultural estates (Susan Toby Evans, “Gardens”).

## Why it mattered

Morelos was the **warm-country production belt** of the central highlands: cotton and paper for Basin markets and tribute; an early Formative–Middle Formative corridor linking Tlatilco-style communities to Olmec-symbol elite centers at Chalcatzingo; the Epiclassic laboratory of fortified multi-ethnic statecraft at Xochicalco; and, after 1438, a tightly integrated imperial province whose household archaeology (Cuexcomate/Capilco) is among the best windows on commoner life under the Triple Alliance.
