## Geographic Description

Puerto Rico is the fourth-largest island in the Caribbean at approximately 8,897 km², with a coastline of 501 km. It forms the eastern anchor of the Greater Antilles, positioned between Hispaniola to the west and the Virgin Islands to the east. The island is geologically an eastward extension of the same mountain ranges that form the Cordillera Central of Hispaniola. The highest point, Cerro de Punta, reaches 1,065 meters.

Puerto Rico is described by Keegan and Hofman as "a microcosm of the natural diversity found in the Caribbean": it has a high central mountain chain covered by dense rainforest; a narrow, well-watered north coastal plain; a rain-shadowed, xerophytic, broad south-coastal plain; and narrow, deeply dissected river valleys extending from the central cordillera. The Virgin Islands archipelago begins as an eastward geological extension of Puerto Rico's central mountain range, creating a geographic continuity between the two archipelagoes.

## Settlement History

Puerto Rico was among the three earliest-occupied Caribbean islands. The Angostura and Maruca sites in Puerto Rico date to approximately 4000 BCE, among the oldest radiocarbon-dated sites in the Caribbean. All of the earliest sites in the region are confined to Cuba, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico; Jamaica shows no evidence of human occupation before AD 500.

The Archaic Age assemblages of Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands have been classified as belonging to Rouse's **Ortoiroid series**, within which three complexes are recognized:
1. **Coroso complex** (Vieques Island) — similar to northern Lesser Antilles assemblages; pebble tools used as percussors and grinders, flaked-stone tools, *Lobatus* columella tips, quartz, and red ochre.
2. **Cayo Cofresí complex** (eastern Puerto Rico) — distinguished by well-formed ground-stone pestles, reminiscent of the Banwari Trace assemblage in Trinidad.
3. **Krum Bay complex** (St. Thomas) — flaked igneous stone and pebble hammer stones and grinders, crude bifacially worked tools, shell beads and discs, *Lobatus* columella tips, coral files, quartz, and red ochre.

The differences between these complexes may reflect separate aspects of a single subsistence-settlement system rather than distinct cultural traditions.

**Archaic Age pottery** is well documented in Puerto Rico. The Angostura site yielded dates of 2700–1800 BCE in association with pottery. At Paso del Indio, a deeply buried stratum below 2–2.5 meters of sediment was dated to 2630 BCE. The Coroso complex at Playa Blanca and Jobos sites is among the earliest documented instances. On St. Thomas, the Gramobokola site and Chanel Hill site both yielded pottery in contexts dated to 870 BCE.

Starch-grain analysis at Puerto Rican Archaic Age sites has produced some of the most significant revisions to the standard model: "the Antillean botanical trinity of manioc, sweet potatoes, and maize … has all been documented during Archaic Age times in Puerto Rico at least since 1300 BCE" (Pagán Jiménez 2013; Rodríguez Ramos 2010). Yellow sapote and sapodilla, obtained from Central America, were also present. This evidence collapses the clean distinction between an aceramic, non-agricultural Archaic Age and a ceramic-using, horticultural Ceramic Age.

Sediment cores from Laguna Tortuguero show a marked increase in charcoal beginning approximately 5,300 years ago, attributed to anthropogenic burning despite the wetter climate at that time. Similar evidence comes from the Vega Baja sediment record.

More recent Archaic Age investigations at the **Maruca site** and **Angostura site** have demonstrated that at least some Archaic Age communities on Puerto Rico had a more sedentary existence than the mobile-band model assumed, with the organizational requirements of settled life producing more complex social arrangements (Rivera Collazo 2011; Rodríguez Ramos 2010).

Puerto Rico was subsequently promoted within Caribbean archaeology as the "heart" (*corazón*) of precolonial cultural development — a status shaped by the intensive systematic archaeological investigation there compared to other islands. The Saladoid was the foundation on which post-Saladoid Puerto Rican cultural developments were built; the island was used to model the emergence of Classic Taíno. The consolidation of local polities in post-Saladoid Puerto Rico (from approximately AD 600 onward through European contact in 1493) is treated as a key sequence in Ceramic Age Caribbean prehistory. This Puerto Rico–centric model has been criticized by Keegan and Hofman as partly an artifact of research intensity rather than historical centrality.

## Significance

Puerto Rico's dual significance in Caribbean prehistory is as (1) one of the three earliest-occupied islands, with Archaic Age occupation from c. 4000 BCE; and (2) the central site of North American Caribbean archaeological synthesis, whose well-excavated Ceramic Age sequence formed the backbone of Rouse's time–space model. The challenge for interpretation is distinguishing Puerto Rico's genuine historical centrality from the distortion created by its disproportionate research attention.

The island's position at the eastern end of the Greater Antilles, adjacent to the Anegada Passage (one of the four major water gaps where the next landmass is not visible on the horizon, at 65 km), gave it a particular strategic significance in inter-island movement and exchange.

## Historiography

Puerto Rico has been the subject of far more systematic archaeological investigation than any other Caribbean island, giving it outsized influence on theoretical models of the entire region. Rodríguez Ramos (2010) and colleagues have led recent revisionism, particularly in recalibrating radiocarbon dates and documenting pre-Ceramic Age pottery and horticulture. The island's well-stratified sites, including Paso del Indio and Angostura, provide some of the most secure chronological anchors for the Caribbean sequence.

The assumption that Puerto Rico was the origin point for all subsequent Ceramic Age development westward and northward is challenged by the simultaneous appearance of Ostionoid redware in Jamaica and Puerto Rico, suggesting multiple origins or more complex interaction patterns than the linear model accommodates.
