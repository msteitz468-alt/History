# West Mexico

## Overview

**West Mexico** (traditionally Jalisco, Nayarit, and Colima — somewhat artificial modern-state boundaries, but standard for archaeological discussion) is the western highland and Pacific fringe of [[mesoamerica-culture-area|Mesoamerica]]. Until recently its archaeology received less professional attention than Central Mexico or the Maya area, often left to looters and art historians and framed in negative terms (“peripheral,” “non-urban”). Settlement and architecture research overturned that presumption: by the time of Christ the Tequila–Teuchitlán valleys already hosted a regional civilization with unique circular public architecture, monumental shaft tombs, drained-field agriculture, and massive obsidian production (Weigand, EW2001 “West Mexico”).

The region is also the principal corridor for **maritime Pacific interaction** that introduced copper–bronze metallurgy (South American technical affinities) and for Late Postclassic **[[purepecha|Purépecha]]** expansion from the [[michoacan-region|Michoacán]] lake basins. *part_of:* [[west-and-northwest-mexico-prehistory]] · *contrasts_with:* Central Mexican urban trajectories centered on [[teotihuacan]] and the [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of Mexico]].

## Geographic setting

Volcanic lake basins and barranca-cut plateaus (Atemajac/Guadalajara; Tequila valleys; Río Grande de Santiago); Sierra La Primavera and related obsidian sources; Pacific coastal plain and river mouths (Amapa, Banderas, Tomatlán); Colima volcanic slopes and Laguna de Cuyutlán salt works. Adjacent [[michoacan-region|Michoacán]] (Pátzcuaro, Cuitzeo, Balsas) is sometimes treated within a broader West Mexico culture area when discussing Tarascan empire and metallurgy.

## Archaic and Formative foundations

- **Paleoindian–Archaic:** Sparse Clovis points (N Jalisco, Zacoalco); Archaic sites at Autlán–Grullo and Etzatlán (Lerma points c. 5000 B.C.); Nayarit shell middens (Matanchén/San Blas c. 2000 B.C.).
- **Early Formative:** Three Jalisco sites (Citala, Teuchitlán, San Juanito) have **El Opeño–variety shaft tombs** (stair access, oval chambers, small solid figurines) dated c. **1500 B.C.** — first clear sociocultural complexity in the region (Weigand, EW2001). **Capacha** ceramics (often 1400–1000 B.C.; some early 14C poorly supported) with gourd-prototype composite, stirrup, and effigy vessels; San Blas Capacha-related 1200–1000 B.C.; El Infiernillo Balsas villages link to Colima coast.
- **Middle Formative San Felipe:** Circular platforms avg. ~28 m diameter × 2 m high with burials (mostly pits; few elegant shaft tombs) at regular intervals on upper shores of W Jalisco lake basins; flat figurines with some Tlatilco relationships.

## Shaft-tomb tradition (c. 500 B.C.–A.D. 300)

The famous mortuary tradition of Middle–Late Formative and Early Classic West Mexico is concentrated in Colima/Jalisco/Nayarit; four general tomb styles range from simple pits through shallow and deep shafts to monumental shafts 18–22 m with multiple chambers. Types 3–4 occur only in the **Teuchitlán–Etzatlán** region — nucleus of an emerging regional civilization (Weigand). Greatest offerings: jade, turquoise, shell, pseudo-cloisonné, hollow portrait figurines, obsidian. Only one great tomb professionally excavated with perishable goods in situ: **Huitzilapa** (2-chamber, 8 m). **El Opeño** (Michoacán) is often cited as a possible antecedent. Colombian parallels exist, but Vallarta-area work and Weigand’s morphology/chronology critique find **no significant evidence for direct South American derivation** of the shaft-tomb complex — “so seriously flawed… that it should probably be put to rest” (Weigand on Colima; Foster, EW2001 “Shaft Tombs”). Associated small circular compounds (altar + patio + banquette with 4 or 8 platforms) are vernacular models for Classic monumental circular buildings.

## Classic Teuchitlán civilization (A.D. 200–700)

Circular architecture based on **concentric circles planned from a radial center** — found nowhere else in Mesoamerica or the world (Weigand). Largest circles diameters >120 m or circumferences ≥400 m; precincts of 6–8 structures plus monumental ball courts (largest ~135 m) and elite residential compounds. Best precincts: Teuchitlán, Loma Alta, Mesa Alta, Santa Quiteria (others badly damaged). Habitation zones cover thousands of hectares of terraced countryside with hilltop fortifications at regular intervals/passes. Wetland drained-field (chinampa-type) features ~**32 km²** documented. Obsidian mines: 1,265 at La Joya, >800 Primavera, ~150 San Juan de los Arcos; plus malachite/azurite and crystal quarries. Pseudo-cloisonné vessels may indicate early glyph-based recording. Colima’s Comala phase (c. 400 B.C.–A.D. 500) participates with smaller Teuchitlán-type circles plus rectangular sunken-patio buildings. Atemajac Valley (Guadalajara) was peripheral Tabachines-phase territory relative to the Tequila valleys core.

## Epiclassic–Postclassic and Aztatlán

Epiclassic–early Postclassic (A.D. 700–900; 900–1250): the first Jalisco civilization experiment **collapsed**; square/rectangular platforms and pyramids replaced circular formats. Principal Teuchitlán-zone sites become Santa Cruz de Bárcenas, Tabaquero, Portezuelo; polychrome molcajetes; poorly understood **Aztatlán** complex spreads especially along the coast. No Postclassic site matches Classic Teuchitlán–Etzatlán elaboration.

**Aztatlán** (coastal onset consensus ~A.D. 900; radiocarbon coast A.D. 883–1400; some valleys later, e.g. Banderas ~1130): floodplain agriculture, highland obsidian prismatic blades, copper/bronze metallurgy (ultimate origin likely coastal Ecuador/N Peru but local smelting), shell jewelry, Mixteca-Puebla-related polychrome iconography, Mazapan figurines, Plumbate imports; earth-and-stone platforms, stelae, ball courts, community cemeteries (EW2001 Aztatlán). **Amapa** (Nayarit): large Postclassic floodplain site; earth mounds up to 200 m long; ball court; dominant Aztatlán into 13th–14th c.; late polychromes resemble Mixteca-Puebla and Guasave; copper tools. Amapa–Peñitas copper metallurgy sequence is among the **best described in all Mesoamerica**, with analytical work suggesting South American origin of much technology into a prepared local medium (Weigand). **Etzatlán** (~650 ha under modern town): capital of a small Postclassic state (peak A.D. 1250–1520), ~10,000 population, controlled La Joya obsidian, exported metal and stone goods, **resisted Tarascan conquest ~50 years** before Spanish arrival (Weigand).

Early Postclassic maritime Pacific trade introduced South American copper–bronze techniques; artisans then experimented with tin and arsenic alloys; technology remained West-Mexico-centered until later (Smith, EW2001 “Postclassic”). South American contact arguments for **metallurgy** have a “good foundation” (Weigand on Colima); Colima is a possible entry point. Salt export from Laguna de Cuyutlán.

## Purépecha pressure and Spanish contact

From c. **1400** the [[purepecha|Purépecha (Tarascans)]] conducted predatory expansion into large segments of southern and central Jalisco. Late Postclassic contact sites include Ocomo/Oconahua (125 m square palace block compared to Códice Quinatzin Texcoco palace plans), Guaxacate/Magdalena, Etzatlán. Spaniards in the mid-1520s–1530s found small-scale states as a military march between Tarascans to the south and Caxcanes to the north. Nayarit epidemics left <10% of population by end of 16th c. (Weigand).

## Why it mattered

West Mexico supplies (1) an independent Classic experiment in complex society with unique circular urban form; (2) the Formative shaft-tomb figurine corpus of world art-historical fame, now re-anchored in settlement archaeology; (3) the principal Mesoamerican theater of Pacific-linked metallurgy; (4) the western theater of Late Postclassic dual-empire politics ([[purepecha]] vs [[aztec-empire]]). Marginalizing historiographies that treat the region only as a delayed Aztec copy are explicitly rejected in regional syntheses (Pollard for Michoacán; Weigand for Jalisco–Nayarit–Colima).

## Historiography (EW2001)

Phil C. Weigand’s “West Mexico” entry and sub-entries for Jalisco, Nayarit, and Colima are the controlling regional synthesis here; Michael Foster on shaft tombs; Dorothy Hosler / Michael E. Smith on metallurgy chronology; Helen Perlstein Pollard bridges into the Tarascan state. Heavy looting of tombs and figurines still warps the sample; Huitzilapa is a rare professional deep-tomb excavation. Debates: South American origins of shaft tombs (largely rejected) vs metallurgy (still supported); Aztatlán as mercantile “pochteca-like” impulse linking SW U.S. to Central Mexico vs diffusion into existing polychrome traditions; degree of Purépecha incorporation of Colima/Jalisco.
