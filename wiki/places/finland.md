# Finland

The eastern, Finnic-speaking land across the Gulf of Bothnia, incorporated into
the kingdom of [[sweden|Sweden]] through a combination of settlement, mission,
and the "Swedish crusades" of the 12th–13th centuries, with the church centre at
Turku (Åbo). Medieval Finland formed Sweden's eastern half and the frontier with
Novgorod and Orthodox Christianity. The [[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003|Cambridge History]]
treats Finland as an integral part of the Swedish realm and of the Scandinavian
demographic and settlement story.

## Finland, 1520–1870: From Swedish Province to Grand Duchy (CHS Vol. II)

Through the early modern era Finland was "an organic part of the Swedish realm"
(L1141), sharing its [[reformation-in-scandinavia|Reformation]] — carried by
Wittenberg-trained clergy, above all [[mikael-agricola|Mikael Agricola]], founder of
written Finnish — and its wars (the Club War, *nuijasota*, 1596–97, killed ~3,000
peasants). It was a frontier of perpetual conflict with Russia. The decisive break
came in **1809**, when Sweden lost Finland to Russia: the **Diet of Porvoo (Borgå)**
made it an autonomous **Grand Duchy** under Alexander I, who "raised Finland to the
rank of nations," retaining Lutheran faith, Swedish-era law, and the Gustavian
constitution. Helsinki became the capital (1812, designed by Engel) and the
university moved there (1828). National identity grew through the **Fennoman
movement**, the Finnish Literary Society (1831), Lönnrot's *Kalevala* (1835),
Runeberg, Topelius, and the Hegelian statesman Snellman — encapsulated in "We are not
Swedes, we are not Russians, so let us be Finns." The peaceful transition is
attributed to a clear territorial definition, an own bureaucracy and senate, the
integrating Lutheran church, and railway-building from the 1860s. See
[[scandinavian-nation-state-formation]], [[scandinavianism]],
[[kouri-olesen-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v2-2016]].
