## Overview

Poverty Point is a residential and ceremonial site of massive scale located in northeast Louisiana on a bluff overlooking the Mississippi River floodplain, built by hunter-fisher-gatherers approximately 1700–1100 BCE. It was one of the largest earthwork complexes in pre-Columbian North America, exceeded in scale only by the later Cahokia site (built by agriculturalists). Poverty Point is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and is managed by the Louisiana Office of State Parks.

## Description

Poverty Point's builders moved as many as 100 million 22.7 kg (50 lb) basketloads of soil to construct five mounds, six concentric C-shaped ridges, and a large plaza. A later culture added a sixth mound around 700 CE. The site's scale and unique design are globally significant — it was built not by farmers but by hunter-fisher-gatherers, a fact that required archaeologists to fundamentally revise their assumptions about what such societies could accomplish.

The people of Poverty Point did not grow crops; they subsisted on wild foods including fish, deer, nuts, and fruits. Stone was the only critical raw material not found locally; many tons of rock were imported from hundreds of kilometers away for tools (spear points, plummets, celts), decorative items (beads, gorgets), and fired clay cooking balls called "Poverty Point Objects." Earth was molded and fired to create cooking balls, human figurines, pipes, and a small amount of pottery.

## Historical Context

Poverty Point was built later than the nearby Watson Brake site (c. 3400 BCE), which itself featured 11 earthen mounds connected by a 280 m diameter oval earthwork ridge. These Louisiana sites demonstrate that large-scale earthwork construction in North America began with Archaic hunter-gatherers in the southwestern corner of the Eastern Woodlands, predating the more famous [[adena-culture]] and [[hopewell-culture]] mound-building traditions by centuries to over a millennium.

The construction of earthworks at both Watson Brake and Poverty Point did not necessarily require enormous long-term labor forces; some mounds were built in single stages, others grew through brief construction episodes over centuries. These sites were planned and constructed by members of tribal or perhaps chiefly societies.

Poverty Point's legacy in archaeology is its role in forcing a reassessment of hunter-gatherer social complexity: "For decades, archaeologists did not believe that hunter-gatherers were capable of long-distance trade and public construction projects on the scale seen at Poverty Point."

## CHNPA I.1 — Dean R. Snow (ch. 3)

Snow places Archaic sedentism at “impressive scale” **between 1500 and 700 B.C.** at Poverty Point, Louisiana:

- Houses of wood, bark, and mud on long low ridges arranged around a central plaza; ridges in **concentric rows, six deep**; outermost octagon diameter ~**1,300 m**; incomplete on the river side.
- Away from the river: large artificial earthen mound **20 m high**, **200 m long** E–W, with ramp; equinox sunrise over the plaza from the mound top.
- Related mounds and villages nearby.

**Dating and interpretation.** Before radiocarbon dating, some archaeologists suspected Poverty Point was later and related to [[adena-culture|Adena]] and [[hopewell-culture|Hopewell]] of the Ohio Valley. Distant Olmec or other Mexican influence is possible but **unnecessary** — “It can just as easily be interpreted as a local outgrowth of long-term Archaic trends in the Eastern Woodlands.”

**Technology detail.** Mississippi Valley alluvial soils provided few pebbles; archaeologists found **thousands of fired clay balls** used for stone boiling (*part_of* Poverty Point technology; concurrent with broader Eastern Archaic ground-stone and cooking innovations on [[paleoindian-and-archaic-north-america]]).
