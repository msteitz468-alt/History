## Overview

The Feathered Serpent Pyramid (FSP), also called the Temple of Quetzalcoatl, is the third-largest pyramid at [[teotihuacan]] and the central focal structure of the [[ciudadela-teotihuacan]] enclosure. Built in a single construction episode — probably shortly after the enclosing Ciudadela platforms were completed, in the Miccaotli phase (ca. 100–170 CE) — it is architecturally unique among Teotihuacan pyramids: its [[talud-tablero]] facades are composed not of the rubble-and-concrete fill used elsewhere, but of precisely fitted andesite stone blocks weighing several hundred kilograms each, densely adorned with three-dimensional sculptural heads. The FSP housed by far the largest documented program of sacrificial burials at Teotihuacan, with an estimated ~200 victims interred in a highly structured arrangement during construction.

## Construction

The FSP measures approximately 65 × 65 m at its base and was originally about 20 m high. Sugiyama's (2005) careful reconstruction established seven superposed bodies rather than the six previously proposed by Marquina (1951). The facades are in [[talud-tablero]] form but are distinguished by their stonework: "huge cut stone blocks carefully fitted together, each weighing several hundred kilograms," of andesitic raw materials probably obtained from the Teotihuacan Valley and the vicinity of Texcoco (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). A thin grayish sandy layer below the surrounding surface "suggests that the blocks were roughly shaped at quarry sites and then finished and fitted together when set in place at the pyramid." Postholes observed by Martin Dudek in the 1988 INAH-Brandeis season may indicate construction scaffolding.

"Sugiyama estimates that in spite of its much smaller size, the total labor involved in constructing the FSP may have been comparable to that at the Sun Pyramid" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

The pyramid's fill consists of cells of unworked stones set in mud mortar, packed with earth, stones, and debris — including fragments of worn metates, potsherds, and forty-six large conical stone objects apparently derived from an earlier civic-ceremonial structure.

## Sculptural Program

The sloping taludes are covered with undulating relief profiles of the Feathered Serpent amid seashells. The vertical tableros display paired three-dimensional projecting heads. One member of each pair is unambiguously a feathered serpent (a rattlesnake body shown in low relief). The identity of the second image is disputed:

- Armillas (1945, 1947): the feathered serpent as Tlaloc; the image represents lightning or thunder clouds
- Drucker (1974): the second image belongs to Cipactli (the crocodilian first-day creature)
- Caso and Bernal (1952): equivalent to the Oaxacan "dios con el moño," = Aztec Xiuhcoatl or fire/year/turquoise serpent
- López Austin et al. (1991): the headdress of Cipactli on the Feathered Serpent's body, at the moment of creation of time; pyramid dedicated to origin of calendar

The total number of heads is uncertain because the pyramid's upper portions are destroyed. Sugiyama (2005) estimates 361–404; no likely total carries obvious calendrical significance. Cowgill suggests the "Teotihuacano love of multiple repetitions of figural elements" and the technical constraints of projecting stone sculpture (structural instability of larger figures) may together explain the quantity (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). The FSP was "most likely... a place for worship and ritual connected with both the Storm God and the Feathered Serpent."

## The Plataforma Adosada

The FSP's original facade was later covered by a large stepped fore-platform (Plataforma Adosada). Partial exploration in the 1988 season found hints of an earlier, lower free-standing platform preceding it. The fill of the later fore-platform contains "fragments of clay motifs that apparently came from a destroyed structure that once stood atop the FSP" — evidence that a superstructure existed atop the pyramid before it was demolished.

## Sacrificial Burial Program

See also: [[teotihuacan-human-sacrifice-fsp]]

The burial program was detected first by Cabrera's 1980–82 project and greatly expanded by the Cabrera-Sugiyama-Cowgill project of 1988–89 and further operations in 1992–94. "Remains of 119 individuals were found in the 1988–89 operations, bringing the total to 137, mostly sacrificial victims arranged in a highly structured pattern of mass graves" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). More victims were found in 1992–94. Symmetry considerations "imply that a total of around 200 victims had been buried at ground level during construction." The burials are organized by status level, with numerical regularities (sets of 8, 9, 18, and 20) probably intentional.

## Archaeological History

The FSP was first substantially excavated by Gamio and Marquina (1917–1922). Marquina (1979 [1922]) reported disturbed remains atop the pyramid with marine shells, earspools, greenstone and jade figurine heads, a Storm God jar, and serpent nose pendants. Pedro Dozal (1925) found four additional individuals in deep pits at the pyramid's four corners. The 1980–82 Cabrera project cleared the north and south palaces, the Enclosure of the Artisans, and further areas. A full report of the burial program is cited as in preparation by Sugiyama (2005). Sugiyama (2005) provides extensive data and interpretive analysis.

## Historiography

Scholarly debate on the FSP centers on three issues: the identity of the sculptural program's iconography (see above); the interpretation of the sacrificial burial program (cosmological dedication vs. political/military statement; see [[teotihuacan-human-sacrifice-fsp]]); and the question of whether the Ciudadela generally — and the FSP specifically — represents the seat of secular political authority or primarily a religious complex. Sugiyama (2005) doubts the Ciudadela was intended as a ruler's seat and argues victims were dedicated to gods; Cowgill (2015) continues to suspect "the Ciudadela was probably built as a new political center for a changing Teotihuacan state, occupied by persons whose activities intertwined the sacred and the secular."

The FSP's fore-platform (Plataforma Adosada) was added later and may signal a significant political change: Cowgill (1983, 2007) has argued that the Avenue of the Dead Complex, probably built after the FSP was desecrated, represents a "shift from more autocratic rule to a system in which a larger number of the elites had a significant voice."
