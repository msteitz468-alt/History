# Boeotia (Archaic)

**Boeotia** — the large, fertile central-Greek plain around Lake Copais — was the
agrarian, inward-looking heartland that produced [[hesiod|Hesiod]] yet "translated its
grumbling into no action": an oligarchic world that slowly coalesced, under Thebes, into
the federal state of Classical times. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]]
(Ch. 41, ii, W. G. G. Forrest) treats it.

## A stale agrarian society

Boeotia was "large enough and fertile enough to keep Boeotians happy with its vegetables
and grain, the fowls of Tanagra and the eels of Copais" — and for the rich, their
horses — so there was "no great urge to colonize or exploit the new economic
opportunities" of the age. "A stale agrarian economy does not breed social, political or
even much cultural excitement": the cities stayed **oligarchic from 700 to Xerxes**
(only one lawgiver is named, the Corinthian Bacchiad **Philolaus**, c. 700, who
regulated adoption to keep up the number of property-owners). Boeotian art "plodded
along behind its Corinthian and Attic models" — its one original line the colourful
**"bird-cups"** — though the country that produced Hesiod, Pindar and Corinna, and the
*aulos*-players of Copais's reeds, "is scarcely backward."

## The slow road to federation

The deeper theme is **Boeotian unity**. Homer listed thirty-one Boeotian contingents;
around 700 there were still a dozen independent cities — **Thebes, Orchomenus** (a
proud old rival), Thespiae, Haliartus, Coronea, Tanagra, Plataea — divided by pride,
proximity and religious rivalry (Thebes coveted Acraephia's Ptoion sanctuary). A sense
of ethnic unity was celebrated at the **Pamboeotia** festival of Athena Itonia, "more
akin to the Panionia than the Panathenaea" — cultural, not yet political. From the early
sixth century **Thebes expanded** (encroaching on the Ptoion), and by c. 500 controlled
**all Boeotia south of Copais** — a development legible in the **federal coinage** (the
Boeotian shield, struck by Thebes, Tanagra, Haliartus and others from c. 550), with
Orchomenus asserting its independence with a rival corn-ear coinage. The one lasting
"thorn in the flank of the Boeotian pig" was little **Plataea**, which (c. 519) appealed
to **Athens** against Theban pressure and kept its autonomy — repaying Athens at Marathon
and in 479.

## Why Boeotia mattered

- A paradigm of the **conservative agrarian oligarchy** that, lacking the pressures that
  drove colonization and reform elsewhere, changed little across the Archaic period.
- The slow genesis of the **Boeotian federal state** under Thebes — and the Theban–
  Plataean–Athenian antagonism that runs through Classical history.
- The homeland of [[hesiod|Hesiod]], and so of the earliest literary picture of Late
  Geometric Greek society.
