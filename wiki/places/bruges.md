# Bruges

Bruges was the **first city in the Low Countries to become a major international
market** and the first term in the Bruges → [[antwerp|Antwerp]] →
[[amsterdam|Amsterdam]] sequence of commercial primacy analyzed in
[[gelderblom-cities-of-commerce-2013|Gelderblom's *Cities of Commerce*]].

## Geographic Setting

Bruges sat in the [[county-of-flanders|County of Flanders]] and gained sea access
around 1134 when a flood created the **Zwyn**, an arm of the North Sea reached
through the outports of Damme and Sluis. This let its merchants sail to England
for wool and to Gascony for wine, and connected the city to the Flemish urban
network (Ypres, Lille, Messines, Torhout) and, from c. 1180, to the
[[commercial-revolution|Champagne fairs]].

## Rise and Function

Granted the right to an annual fair in 1200 and designated the staple for all
Flemish cloth in 1322, Bruges became the linchpin of Flanders' textile economy.
As the [[medieval-europe-asia-trade|Champagne fairs]] declined in the late
thirteenth century, foreign merchants needed a permanent Northwestern hub, and
Bruges transformed its seasonal fairs into a **year-round exchange** — the
institutional adaptation at the heart of Gelderblom's
[[urban-competition-commercial-institutions|urban-competition thesis]]. Italian
houses (Venice, Genoa, Florence, Lucca) sent galleys with silk, alum, dyes, and
spices; the [[hanseatic-league|German Hanse]] carried Flemish cloth to the Baltic
and brought back grain, timber, iron, and hides; English, Iberian, Portuguese,
and Scottish merchants completed the mix.

## Institutional Profile

Bruges organized international trade through **separate foreign "nations"**
(*vreemde naties*) — by the mid-fifteenth century more than a dozen, the German
Hanse the largest (100–200 merchants in season). Each had privileges, safe-
conducts, and — from the fourteenth century — **consular jurisdiction** over
internal disputes, while accommodation and brokerage were dominated by the city's
100-plus **hostellers**. This **particularistic** regime (contrast the later
[[open-access-institutions|open-access]] model of Amsterdam) suited a market of
seasonal visitors; the local magistrate of traders and artisans tolerated the
foreigners' legal autonomy because the city's middling groups lived off their
presence.

## Decline

Bruges's commercial hegemony was ended by the **Flemish Revolt (1483–92)**, during
which the future Emperor Maximilian I came down hard on the city knowing its
merchants would simply relocate to [[antwerp|Antwerp]] within his territories — a
vivid illustration of how a city's position in the urban hierarchy, not its
military weakness alone, governed its fate. The silting of the Zwyn compounded the
shift. Commercial leadership passed to Antwerp by the early sixteenth century.

## Significance

Bruges shows the **early, particularistic** phase of the institutional sequence:
generalized open-access institutions had not yet displaced the patchwork of
national privileges, yet the city already pioneered the year-round permanent
market that later entrepôts inherited and refined.
