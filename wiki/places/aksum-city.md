# Aksum (city)

The highland city of **Aksum** (modern Axum, Tigray, Ethiopia; c. 2,130 m elevation) was the capital of the [[aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] from its emergence in the late first century BCE until its abandonment as royal capital around AD 700. It remains the most archaeologically investigated site in the northern Horn.

## Geography

Aksum sits on the plateau of the northern Ethiopian highlands, approximately 70 km from the Red Sea port of [[adulis|Adulis]] via caravan routes through the Tigrayan escarpment. Its elevation provided defence, cool climate, and proximity to the agricultural hinterland. The Aksum area includes the Beta Giyorgis hill and the Stelae Park — the latter the most visible monument of Aksumite civilisation.

## History

The city rose to prominence as the political centre of the Aksumite state in the late first century BCE or early first century CE. By the first–second centuries CE it was the capital of a state controlling trade between the Red Sea and the interior. Its monumental phase — giant stelae, elite tombs, throne-bases, palace complexes — peaked in the third–sixth centuries CE. King Ezana's conversion to Christianity in the fourth century made Aksum a Christian royal capital, with the cathedral of Mary (Maryam Tsion) as its central institution.

Around AD 700 the royal capital relocated to an unidentified site called Kubar in eastern Tigray, probably in response to the Arab disruption of Red Sea trade and the exhaustion of local resources. Aksum itself did not vanish but was eclipsed: the cathedral and ecclesiastical establishments survived; secular monuments were no longer maintained.

## Key Monuments

- **Stelae Park**: Giant carved granite obelisks (monolithic stelae) marking elite tombs. Six are elaborately carved to represent multi-storey building facades in the Aksumite architectural vocabulary (projecting beam-ends, recessed windows, false doors at the base). The largest (Stela 1, now fallen) is estimated at c. 520+ tonnes — the largest monolith ever erected in the ancient world. Stela 2 (33 m, nine storeys) was removed to Rome in 1937 and returned in 2008. Stela 3 (24 m, seven storeys) still stands. See `[[aksumite-stelae]]` and `[[aksumite-stelae-erection-3c-ce]]`.
- **Royal tombs**: Elaborate multi-chambered underground stone-lined tombs beneath the great stelae. The **Mausoleum** (below Stela 2) and the **Tomb of the Brick Arches** (below Stela 3) are the best documented. The **Tomb of the False Door** is a further elaborate example. All use fired-brick vaulting and iron clamps (Graeco-Roman type) to join dressed stone. All were robbed in antiquity; the Tomb of the Brick Arches nonetheless yielded ivory and metalwork.
- **Throne-bases**: Stone throne platforms lining the approach roads, bearing royal inscriptions in Greek and Ge'ez.
- **Elite structures (palaces)**: Multi-storey stone complexes built on high stepped plinths. **Ta'akha Maryam** (the largest, covering over one hectare) lies on the southern edge of the main built-up area. **Dungur** (c. 700 m west of the Stelae Park) is smaller. Both feature the standardised Aksumite plan: a central pavilion with projecting corner towers set in an open court surrounded by ranges of rooms. See `[[aksumite-architecture]]`.

## Urban Character

Aksumite texts and the Greek *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* (c. mid-first century CE) call the city a **metropolis** — a political and commercial capital — rather than a "city" in the usual sense. Aksum lacked the walls or formal defensible perimeter characteristic of contemporaneous urban centres elsewhere. Settlement was loosely packed: a mixture of elite structures, craft-working areas, domestic buildings, and extensive funerary and ceremonial zones with no sharp demarcation between them. The roughly triangular core delimited by Beta Giyorgis and Mai Qoho hills to the northwest and northeast and by Ta'akha Maryam to the south covers approximately **110 ha** (a minimum; the full extent was probably considerably greater). By the sixth century, population density estimates suggest a total of ~45,000 persons within approximately 5 km of Mai Qoho, representing 90 per cent of the population of a 714 sq km survey area (Michels 2005, with subsequent upward revision by Fattovich and L. Phillipson).

Craft production areas (metalworking, stone-working) were interspersed with elite and domestic buildings. Sources of heavy timber became depleted by the late fifth or early sixth century, limiting the use of the elaborate beams-and-monkey-heads construction to the most prestigious buildings thereafter.

## Significance

Aksum's significance is geographic (node between Red Sea trade and Ethiopian interior), political (capital of the first sub-Saharan African state to issue its own coinage), religious (one of the earliest Christian royal capitals in the world), and architectural (home to the largest monoliths erected in ancient Africa).

## Historiography

Phillipson's *Foundations of an African Civilisation* (2012) provides the most comprehensive archaeological account. Earlier work by Munro-Hay (1991) and Anfray remains foundational. The Aksum Archaeological Research Project (AARP) directed by Phillipson from the 1990s produced key excavation data.
