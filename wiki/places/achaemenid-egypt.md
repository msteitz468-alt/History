# Achaemenid Egypt (the First Persian Period)

The **First Persian Period** (the **Twenty-Seventh Dynasty** of Manetho, 525–404 BCE)
was the century in which Egypt was a **satrapy of the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid
empire]]** — the sixth and one of the richest, assessed at **700 talents** of tribute.
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 3g, J. D. Ray) treats it as the imposition of
"an alien system of government upon a country that was already highly developed," and as
the wound through which, Ray argues, Persia "began to bleed to death."

## Conquest and the two faces of Cambyses

**[[cambyses|Cambyses]]** conquered Egypt in **525** (see
[[persian-conquest-of-egypt-525bce]]), ending **[[saite-egypt|Saite]]** independence at
the battle of **Pelusium**. Greek tradition (Herodotus) made his name "synonymous with
madness and atrocities" — the wounding of the **Apis bull**, temple sacrilege, the army
lost in the sands toward Siwa, the failed Nubian campaign against Meroe. Ray returns a
verdict of **"not proven, even not guilty"**: the Apis that died in 525 was buried
normally, and the hostile tradition is folklore. The counter-witness is **[[udjahorresne]]**,
the Saite collaborator who has Cambyses **restore the temple of Neith at Sais** and take a
full pharaonic **titulary** (Cambyses is the only Persian king with a complete one) — the
psychological "denial" by which Egypt assimilated its conqueror as **Mesuti-Re**, offspring
of the sun-god. A genuine **decree cutting temple revenues**, however, shows the real
fiscal squeeze the conquest imposed.

## Darius the ideal pharaoh

**Darius I** (522–486) became, in Egyptian eyes, the model good king. He **codified
Egyptian law** (a commission of warriors, priests and scribes assembled the law as it
stood in Amasis's last year, copied to Susa in Aramaic and demotic); completed the
**Nile–Red Sea canal** from Bubastis to the Gulf of Suez (lined with trilingual stelae —
"ships sailed from Egypt through this canal to Persia, as was my wish"); built the great
**temple of Hibis** at Kharga oasis (Darius as universal high priest sacrificing to Amun);
and was commemorated by a **statue with hieroglyphic inscriptions** (found at Susa in
1972). The Egyptian legend of "Sesostris" in Herodotus is a "thinly disguised portrait" of
Darius. He visited Egypt three times.

## Administration

Over Egypt stood the king as an **absentee pharaoh**; on the ground, a **satrap**
(*ḫšaçapāvan*) — an aristocrat or royal kinsman (Aryandes, Achaemenes brother of Xerxes,
the long-serving **Arsames**), so powerful he was effectively a viceroy, repeatedly
recalled and watched by dispatch-riders and a divided bureaucracy. Beneath him the old
**nomes** survived under a *frataraka* (nomarch) and the powerful **royal scribe** of
land and taxes; a **dual legal system** served Egyptians (demotic) and foreigners
(Aramaic). Tribute was 700 talents plus grain, fisheries and symbolic Nile water; the
**Persian *artaba*** measure was grafted onto the old Egyptian system, and the first
**coinage** (Athenian "owls," some restruck with a demotic "Artaxerxes Pharaoh" legend)
made a hesitant arrival in a still-distributive economy.

## A cosmopolitan land

Achaemenid Egypt was "a richly cosmopolitan state." Alongside the Egyptians lived
**Persians** (the Arsames estate-correspondence), **[[elephantine|Jews]]** (the Elephantine
garrison and its temple of Yahu), **[[ionia|Ionians]]** and **Carians** (mercenaries at
Memphis and [[naucratis|Naucratis]] since Saite times, their Saqqara stelae), **Phoenicians**
(the *Phoinikaigyptioi* of Memphis), and Aramaeans (the Hermopolis letters). Two-way
**acculturation** ran deep: Persian magnates took Egyptian names (the brothers Atiyawahi and
Ariyawrata/Djeho in the Wadi Hammamat), foreigners made dedications to Osiris and the Apis,
and Egyptian wives were "inevitable." Egypt in turn **absorbed Mesopotamian astrology and
mathematics** and exported its art and craftsmen to Susa and Persepolis (whose palaces show
Egyptian cavetto cornices).

## Revolt and the recovery of independence

The later period is marked by **native revolts** — the rising of 486 (a change-of-ruler
opportunity), and above all **Inaros son of Psammetichus** (460s), who with an **Athenian
fleet** diverted from Cyprus took Memphis and killed the satrap Achaemenes at Papremis,
before Megabyxus crushed the revolt and annihilated the Athenian relief force (454).
These rebels were largely **disaffected warriors (*machimoi*) isolated from the mass**,
but their **alliance with Athens** ("one of the quickest routes to Persepolis lay through
Memphis") made Egypt a chronic Persian wound. From 411 Persian control dissolved, and on
the death of Darius II (404) **Amyrtaeus of Sais** was recognized as pharaoh — "the first
to come after the Medes" — ending the First Persian Period and opening the last era of
native rule.

## Historiography

Built on **Herodotus** (Books II–III) and **Ctesias** for narrative; **hieroglyphic**
monuments (Udjahorresne's statue, the canal stelae, the Apis epitaphs); a rich and growing
body of **demotic** documents (Papyrus Rylands IX — the petition of Petiese — the most
informative single text from ancient Egypt); and the **Aramaic** archives (the Elephantine
papyri, the Arsames "diplomatic bag," the Hermopolis letters). Ray stresses the sources
"scarcely allow" an economic balance-sheet, but that **culturally Egypt gained** from the
conquest while Persia took a fatal strategic burden.
