# Dachau

**Dachau**, on the grounds of a derelict munitions factory near Munich, was the **first SS concentration camp** and the only one to operate for the entire Nazi period (March 1933 – April 1945). It became the organizational and disciplinary **template for the whole KL system** — its barrack layout, five-department Commandant Staff, and punishment regulations were exported system-wide — and the training ground for the Camp SS careerists ([[hoss-rudolf|Höss]], Koch, Loritz) who ran the camps. Documented from [[wachsmann-kl-concentration-camps-2015|Wachsmann, *KL* (2015)]].

## Founding and the Early Camp (1933)

[[himmler-heinrich|Himmler]], newly Munich police president, announced "the first concentration camp" to the press on 20 March 1933; the first ~100–120 prisoners, mostly Munich Communists, arrived on 22 March. The initial guards were regular Bavarian police who treated prisoners tolerably. The SS took over the prisoner compound on 11 April 1933 under the brutal first commandant Hilmar Wäckerle. On **12 April 1933** SS men shot four prisoners — all identified as Jewish (Rudolf Benario, Ernst Goldmann, Arthur Kahn, and Erwin Kahn) — in nearby woods; at least 8 of the 12 prisoners murdered before late May were Jews, making Dachau the deadliest early camp for Jews. Munich prosecutor Josef Hartinger's murder investigation forced Himmler to dismiss Wäckerle (2 June 1933), but the case files were "lost." Still, terror remained comparatively limited: of 4,821 men held in 1933, no more than 25 died.

## Eicke and the Dachau Model

On 26 June 1933 [[eicke-theodor|Theodor Eicke]] became commandant. His **camp regulations of 1 October 1933** codified flogging ("twenty-five blows"), the bunker, and execution for "mutiny" or agitation — a self-serving bureaucracy of official torture later rolled out to every KL. Eicke made Dachau his base for the [[night-of-the-long-knives-1934|Night of the Long Knives]] (June–July 1934), personally shooting [[rohm-ernst|Ernst Röhm]] and leading a killing spree that claimed 20+ lives on the grounds. Made concentration-camp inspector days later, Eicke universalized the "Dachau model": the rectangular barrack layout, the punishment catalogue, and the five-department Commandant Staff structure.

## Later History

From Himmler's February 1937 order Dachau was the **central camp for male Jewish prisoners**; the lawyer Hans Litten, who had once cross-examined Hitler in court, died there by suicide (Feb 1938) after a punitive mail embargo. It received the first Austrian transports after the 1938 Anschluss (7,861 men by year's end, ~80% Jewish) and thousands of "November Jews" after [[kristallnacht-1938|Kristallnacht]]. During the war Dachau was a slave-labor and medical-experiment centre (Rascher's high-altitude and freezing experiments; Schilling's malaria station of ~1,100 infected prisoners) and, in 1940–42, a **collection point for dying "Muselmänner"** from across the system, then again from October 1942 explicitly for extermination. In all, almost **40,000 prisoners** died at Dachau. U.S. troops liberated it on 29 April 1945, finding a siding train of over 2,000 corpses and ~32,000 survivors; its liberation symbolized the destruction of the Nazi camp system — "Dachau, Germany's most dreaded extermination camp, has been captured" (*New York Times*, 1 May 1945).
