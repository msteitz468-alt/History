## Geographic Description

Jamaica is the third-largest island in the Caribbean, situated south of Cuba and west of Hispaniola. Its terrain ranges from the Blue Mountains in the east to broader alluvial plains and coastal lowlands. The island's diverse microenvironments — mangrove swamps, morasses (freshwater swamps), karst hills, and coastal dunes — shaped distinctly different settlement patterns across the island. The Liguanea Plain near Kingston is surrounded by an arc of hills that became a preferred zone for Meillacoid hilltop settlements.

## Settlement History

### No Archaic Age Settlement

Jamaica exhibits the complete absence of Archaic Age archaeological evidence — the only major Caribbean island for which this is true. Despite decades of avocational and professional survey (Keegan and Atkinson 2006), no Archaic Age artifacts have been identified. The island was not settled until pottery-using Ceramic Age communities arrived from the wider Greater Antilles.

### First Settlement: Ostionoid (Redware) Communities (c. AD 700–900)

The earliest settlers arrived c. AD 700, associated with the Little River pottery style — redware classified by Howard (1956, 1965) within Rouse's Ostionoid series. Sixteen open-air and three cave sites with redware pottery have been documented; only three have been excavated. Sites exhibit:
- Strongly coastal settings
- Heavy reliance on marine fish, mollusks, and sea turtles
- Very shallow deposits suggesting short-term base camps for logistic foraging
- Expedient chert tool use (possibly reflecting protoagrícola Archaic Age practices)
- No evidence of agriculture

The Paradise site at Paradise Park, Westmoreland (cal. AD 850 ± 60), is the best-excavated example. It contained only Ostionoid pottery (redware, red-paint above shoulder, tabular lugs, loop handles, black smudging), a single large post stain (centre post of a large structure), abundant sea turtle bones, olive shell beads and pendants, an agate ear spool, and a greenstone dog-spirit pendant. Faunal evidence indicates exploitation of Bluefields Bay seagrass habitat; mangrove resources were largely ignored at this time.

### Second Settlement: Meillacoid Communities (c. AD 800–1492)

Meillacoid pottery rapidly established itself across Jamaica. There is no stratified evidence for a transition from redware to Meillacoid — the two pottery traditions appear in fully separate, single-component sites with no co-occurrence or style-mixing, in contrast to the Dominican Republic. Whether the Ostionoid communities assimilated into Meillacoid populations or abandoned the island is unknown.

Three local Meillacoid styles are defined:
- **White Marl style**: the most widespread, found island-wide
- **Montego Bay style** (west): distinctive for a wider fillet or separate band of clay at the rim, decorated with deeply incised parallel lines
- **Port Morant style** (east)

All styles share Meillacoid characteristics: boat-shaped or hemispherical vessels with in-turned shoulder (casuela), filleted rims, incised and appliqué decorations between shoulder and rim. Jamaican pottery is distinctive — the shoulder-to-rim distance is shorter than in Haiti, and the shapes and specific motifs are unique to the island. 186 open-air midden sites, 61 cave sites, and numerous rock art sites have been attributed to the Meillacoid, with 30 radiocarbon dates ranging from the mid-8th century to the European encounter.

Meillacoid settlement favoured hilltops overlooking the coast — the same pattern as Cuba, Haiti, and the northwestern Dominican Republic — indicating a stronger reliance on agricultural production than their Ostionoid predecessors. Sites have deep midden deposits (up to 100 cm) indicative of permanent occupation. An emphasis on the *hutia* (*Geocapromys brownii*, Jamaican coney) in faunal assemblages is a Jamaican peculiarity — it may have been domesticated on the island (Scudder 2006). Inland sites show a higher proportion of terrestrial fauna than coastal and near-coastal sites, reflecting the integration of coastal fishing villages supplying processed marine protein to inland agricultural communities.

Jamaican Meillacoid communities notably did **not** adopt Chicoid influences that mark "Taíno" cultural spread elsewhere in the Greater Antilles. There is no evidence for Chicoid pottery, earthworks (calzedas), monticulos, or stone-lined plazas on Jamaica.

## Key Sites

### Paradise Park Sites, Westmoreland (c. AD 850 and AD 1430)

Two distinct sites on the same beach dune in southwestern Jamaica, excavated by William Keegan 1998–2002:

**Paradise site** (c. AD 850): Ostionoid; 400 × 60–100 m; abundant sea turtles; greenstone dog-spirit pendant; agate ear spool; olive-shell bead manufacture. Shallow deposits (max 60 cm).

**Sweetwater site** (c. AD 1430): Meillacoid (Montego Bay style); 220 × 60 m; 82 m² excavated, deposits 20–100 cm. Located unusually in a hardwood forest on a dune in a swamp rather than on a hilltop. Dominated by bivalve scrapers (*Lucina pectinata*, *Codakia orbicularis*), suggesting intensive root-crop processing; exploitation of mangrove habitats (mud conchs); small fish, birds, saltwater crocodiles, hutia, and one dog tooth. Near absence of conch (*Lobatus*) tools contrasts with the Paradise site. Laterally drilled human incisor ornament.

The two sites document changes in marine environment: wetter conditions and increased land clearance after AD 900 produced more turbid bay conditions, reducing seagrass habitat (Veneridae clams at Paradise) while expanding mangrove habitats (Lucinidae and Melongena at Sweetwater).

### White Marl Site

The type-site for the widespread White Marl pottery style. Location near Kingston on the arc of hills above the Liguanea Plain. Meillacoid hilltop settlement pattern; deep deposits indicating permanent occupation. Neutron activation analysis confirms locally made pottery.

### Retreat Property, St. Ann (north coast)

Excavated by de Booy (1913b). Located on a 400-m-elevation hill 10 km from the sea with middens positioned around the hilltop edge — a configuration common to Meillacoid sites across the Greater Antilles. White Marl pottery with the large number of handles typical of the unique Jamaican canteen form. Land snails (*Pleurodonte acuta*) dominated the midden with secondary marine taxa.

## Significance

Jamaica illustrates the distinctiveness of island archaeology within the broader Greater Antillean pattern. Though sharing many characteristics — Meillacoid pottery tradition, hilltop settlement, pan-Caribbean ceremonial object types (stone/shell beads, pendants, wooden sculptures, petroglyphs) — Jamaica developed a fully distinct pottery tradition unaffected by Chicoid expansion. The island's pre-Columbian absence of Archaic Age settlement and absence of Chicoid influences are the two most archaeologically distinctive features.

## Historiography

Jamaican archaeology has been treated as a "black hole" in broader Caribbean syntheses (Rouse 1992; Wilson 2007), despite a rich tradition of local avocational investigation dating to the 19th century (Duerden 1897) and systematic scholarship (Robert Howard's 1950 dissertation; James Lee's island-wide collections curated and published by Allsworth-Jones 2008). The primary emphasis has been on artifact description and Rousean typological classification. Systematic large-scale excavation began only recently (Keegan et al. 2003; Allsworth-Jones and Wesler 2012). Faunal analysis is the best-developed analytical domain (Scudder 2006). The meaning of pan-Caribbean "ceremonial" objects found on Jamaica — typically labelled "Taíno" without further analysis — requires detailed independent study of origins, distributions, and exchange contexts (Keegan and Hofman 2017).
