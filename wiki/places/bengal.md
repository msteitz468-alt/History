# Bengal (Mughal Province)

The Mughal *subah* (province) of **Bengal** — encompassing Bengal proper, often
Bihar, and at times Orissa — was "one of the empire's richest provinces," a
deltaic frontier whose customs revenue and textile wealth made it a magnet for the
European companies. In [[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]],
Guido van Meersbergen uses seventeenth-century Bengal and Orissa as the setting for
his concept of **"provincial diplomacy."**

## Mughal Frontier Province

The [[mughal-empire|Mughals]] conquered the Sultanate of Bengal in **1575–76** and
annexed Orissa in 1593, though "imperial authority in the region remained hotly
contested until the 1610s"; Orissa was made a separate subah in 1607 but in practice
fell under Bengal's governor. Bengal retained "the character of a frontier region,"
with continuing conflicts against Assam and Arakan and recurrent tension with
Portuguese freemen and slave-raiders. In **1632** subahdar Qasim Khan stormed
**Hugli**, the principal Portuguese settlement, asserting Mughal control over the
Ganges delta — and inadvertently "expedit[ing] the establishment of English and
Dutch factories in the region from 1633 onwards."

Because Bengal was entrusted only to the highest-ranking nobility and imperial
princes — **Shah Shuja** (1639–1660), **Mir Jumla** (1660–1663), **Shaista Khan**
(1664–1678, 1679–1688, Aurangzeb's maternal uncle) — its governors often "ruled as
if they were kings themselves," exercising the autonomy that made provincial, not
imperial, decrees the real basis of company trade.

## Provincial Diplomacy

Van Meersbergen argues against the embassy-centric historiography (the "steady
stream of publications focused on the embassy of Sir Thomas Roe"). Farhat Hasan had
shown EIC privileges in Bengal "relied not on imperial farmans but on decrees issued
by a series of provincial governors," who sometimes "consciously contravened
imperial edicts to encourage English investment." Port towns and provincial courts
"were the sites where most of the diplomatic action happened."

- **Cartwright's mission to Cuttack (1633):** EIC agent Ralph Cartwright reached the
  Mahanadi delta on 21 April 1633 and held six audiences (1–8 May) with the nawab of
  Orissa (probably Mu'taqad Khan), securing a customs exemption and factory licence
  and receiving a *khil'at* (robe of honour). Van Meersbergen reads the encounter as
  "characterised by mutuality and apparently unhindered by any serious form of
  cultural barrier" — against the older "incommensurability thesis" (Cohn).

- **The succession war (1657–1659):** With Mughal authority split, both sides
  extorted the [[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]] for money and weaponry. Shah Shuja's
  forces recovered Hugli (2 July 1659) and arrested VOC merchant Dirck Essinghs;
  Buland Akhtar attacked the Dhaka factory (26 August 1659), seizing 51,000 guilders
  and imprisoning Dutch merchants. The VOC supplied "at least eleven pieces of iron
  cannon and six bronze cannons" to the war and owed Mir Jumla over 500,000 guilders.

- **Chittagong campaign (1665–66):** Shaista Khan sent envoy Khwaja Ahmad to Batavia
  Castle (3 March 1665) demanding ships and cannon; Batavia consented only in July
  1666 after a second envoy delivered a farman from Aurangzeb — a calculated
  deference, since defying Shaista Khan would bring consequences "in Bengal."

This co-optation of European naval power, paralleled in
[[safavid-empire|Safavid Iran]], [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Japan]], and Qing
China, matured into Aurangzeb's **convoying duties** on the Dutch, English, and
French in the 1690s–1700s.
