# Aphrodisias

A Greek city of Caria in south-west Asia Minor (modern Geyre, Turkey), named for Aphrodite and famed both for its marble-carving industry and for the **Sebasteion**, one of the best-preserved monuments of the [[roman-imperial-cult]]. Aphrodisias was one of the "far-seeing Greek cities" (Charles Freeman) that early recognized the advantage of winning Roman patronage, and it enjoyed privileged status under the Julio-Claudians.

## Geographic and Historical Significance

Aphrodisias sat amid rich marble quarries that supported a celebrated school of sculptors whose work was exported across the empire. Its loyalty to Rome — cultivated from Augustus onward — earned it tax privileges and a favoured position in the province of Asia. The city persisted into Late Antiquity as a regional centre before decline.

## The Sebasteion

The Sebasteion (from *Sebastos*, the Greek rendering of *Augustus*) is a temple-and-processional complex built by two local families across the reigns of **Tiberius to Nero** (c. AD 20s–50s). A roughly 90-metre colonnaded way, three storeys high on each side, led to a temple of the deified emperors, integrating Roman imperial-cult architecture with Greek form. Some **180 relief panels survive** — many preserved because they fell face-down when the structure collapsed in an earthquake — and constitute a primary visual source for how a provincial Greek city imagined the Roman empire:

- **Heroized emperors**: Claudius depicted nude, conquering a personified Britannia; Nero over a personified Armenia — both idealized far beyond the historical reality of those conquests.
- **The Nero-and-Agrippina relief**: a technically outstanding panel of Nero being crowned by his mother Agrippina, later defaced and buried following Nero's *damnatio memoriae*.
- **Greek myth**: Heracles, Dionysus, Apollo, Aeneas fleeing Troy, Romulus and Remus, Aphrodite and Eros — weaving the imperial family into the Greek mythological order.
- **The *ethne* series**: thirteen personified conquered peoples plus three islands, a geographic roster of Augustan conquests running from the Callaeci of Spain to the Judaei, Arabi, and Aegyptii.

The Sebasteion is thus the finest surviving example of the provincial Greek visual *negotiation* of empire — emic devotion and political self-positioning fused — and a cornerstone document for the study of the imperial cult (Simon Price, *Rituals and Power: The Roman Imperial Cult in Asia Minor*, 1984).

## Why It Mattered

Aphrodisias illustrates how eastern Greek cities converted early loyalty into lasting privilege, and how the imperial cult was as much a locally-driven idiom of civic prestige as a mechanism imposed from Rome. Its reliefs give the imperial cult a face that no literary source supplies.
