# Oxyrhynchus

**Oxyrhynchus** (modern el-Bahnasa; Coptic *Pemje*) was a nome capital on the western bank of the
Bahr Yusuf in Middle Egypt, about 180 km south of Cairo. It is, above all, the site of **the
single largest find of papyri of the Roman and Byzantine periods** — the discovery that, more
than any other, made [[papyrology|papyrology]] a discipline and Greco-Roman Egypt the
best-documented society of antiquity.

## History of the site

First attested as a nome capital in the seventh century BCE, Oxyrhynchus took its Greek name from
its sacred animal, the "sharp-nosed" elephant-snout fish. It grew steadily through to Late
Antiquity, when it was the **seat of a bishop and capital of the province of Arcadia** (Middle
Egypt). By the sixth century it was dominated by the estates of the **Apion family**, great
landowners and imperial officials whose archive documents late-antique estate management. After
the Arab conquest it remained a town noted for textiles before dwindling to a village.

Its standing monuments are almost entirely gone — the stones were burned for lime or reused — but
papyri record a large theatre (estimated at c. 11,200 seats, perhaps the largest in Roman North
Africa), colonnaded streets, a hippodrome, baths, and many churches.

## The Oxyrhynchus Papyri

The Oxford scholars **B. P. Grenfell and A. S. Hunt** excavated the site from **1897 to 1907**,
recognising that the papyri lay not in buildings or tombs but in the **rubbish mounds outside the
ancient city**. The finds were overwhelming. The first volume of *The Oxyrhynchus Papyri* appeared
in 1898; the series is **still being published** more than a century later.

- The texts are **overwhelmingly in Greek** (with some Latin, Coptic, Arabic, and Persian; almost
  no demotic), dating mostly from the **second and third centuries CE**, with a sixth-century
  recovery driven by the Apion archive.
- They are overwhelmingly **documentary** — administration, legal documents, leases, loans,
  accounts, and private letters — but include literary and sub-literary texts: famously, **half
  the published papyrus texts of Herodotus come from Oxyrhynchus**, along with lost works of
  Greek poetry and drama, early Christian writings, magical papyri, horoscopes, and music.

## Why it mattered

Oxyrhynchus is the type-site for the **urban society of Roman and Byzantine Egypt**: its documents
let historians reconstruct municipal administration, the liturgical class of town councillors, the
economy of a provincial *metropolis*, and the rhythms of ordinary life in unmatched detail (see
[[papyrology]]). The continuing publication of the texts means the site still actively reshapes the
study of the ancient world. Its very richness, however, exemplifies the **place-skew** of the
evidence: what we "know" of Roman Egypt is disproportionately what the rubbish of Oxyrhynchus and
the [[the-fayyum|Fayyum]] happened to preserve.

## Wickham on the Apion estates (*Framing*, 2005)

In Wickham’s comparative estate analysis, the sixth-century **Apion family of Oxyrhynchos** (~300 papyri) is emblematic of highly organized, profit-oriented great landowning: detailed accounts, hierarchical managers (*antigeouchos*), irrigation maintenance, wage labour plus leasing, and *autopragia* (self-collection of tax). They remained attached to city society (pagarchs, *patēr tēs poleōs*, racing stables) and owned mainly within c.90 km—not empire-wide. They are the richest known sixth-century eastern aristocrats yet **local**, and disappear after 621 mid-Persian occupation. Wickham stresses that Oxyrhynchos is **atypical in scale of large-landowning dominance**; [[aphrodito|Aphroditō]] and other centres show prosperous medium owners without such dominance. Best guide to **how estates worked**, not best guide to Egypt as a whole. See [[bipartite-manor]], [[politics-of-land]], [[roman-egypt]].
