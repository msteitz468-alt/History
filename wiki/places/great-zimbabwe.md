# Great Zimbabwe

**Great Zimbabwe** — the **largest pre-colonial stone-built structure in sub-Saharan Africa** — was the
capital of a Shona state that flourished on the southern African plateau between roughly **1275 and 1450
CE**, grown rich on **cattle and gold**. Its drystone **Great Enclosure** (an outer wall 244 m long, 10 m
high, 5 m thick, enclosing the enigmatic solid-stone **Conical Tower**) gave its name to the modern nation.
Documented for the wiki through **[[reader-africa-biography|Reader]]**.

## Cattle and gold (Reader's thesis)

Reader roots Great Zimbabwe in **carnivorous pastoralism**. On the southern African high grasslands —
temperate, derived from ancient granite cratons, and crucially **tsetse-free at altitude** — cattle could
be raised for **meat** (unlike the milch pastoralism of the hotter Sahel and Rift). Cattle "converted
otherwise worthless grass into wealth that could be owned, controlled and inherited," producing **social
stratification and a ruling elite** — the people who lived inside the *zimbabwe* walls and, the bone
middens show, ate prime young beef while their neighbours did not. The same ancient cratons that grew the
grass also bore the **gold**: control of the gold trade was, in Reader's phrase, "a corollary of success in
the meat trade."

## Sequence: Mapungubwe → Great Zimbabwe → Mutapa

- **Mapungubwe** (Limpopo valley, c. 1075–1250) was the first state of the tradition — its hilltop elite
  burials and the famous **gold rhinoceros** (c. 1200) mark the earliest indigenous use of gold in
  southern Africa, tied to the new Indian Ocean demand reaching the coast at **Sofala**.
- As a warm, moist climatic phase ended c. 1250, power shifted north to the gold-bearing plateau and
  **Great Zimbabwe** rose, peaking in the 15th century with **11,000–18,000 inhabitants**. Its elite were
  **middlemen**, buying gold from inland miners and selling it on to the Swahili coast — the Renders Ruin
  hoard (Persian and Chinese ceramics, a Kilwa coin, tens of thousands of glass beads) proves direct
  contact with the [[swahili-coast|coastal trade]].
- Like [[aksum|Aksum]] and [[kush-meroe|Meroë]], Great Zimbabwe **outgrew its environment**: dense
  population stripped the land of wood, water, and pasture, and it was **abandoned in the late 15th
  century** (the ecological damage was such that certain trees still will not grow there). Its successors
  **Torwa** (Khami) and **Mutapa** carried the cattle-and-gold pattern into the era of Portuguese contact —
  Vasco da Gama found Arab dhows "laden with gold dust" off the Zambezi in 1497.

## Why it mattered — and a historiographical scandal

Great Zimbabwe is the **proof of indigenous southern African state-building**, and the single most abused
site in the Eurocentric historiography of Africa: colonial-era writers, unwilling to credit Africans with
the ruins, fancifully attributed them to Phoenicians, the Queen of Sheba, or "lost" outsiders. The
archaeology is unequivocal — Great Zimbabwe was built **by the ancestors of the local Shona**, contemporary
with the great medieval cathedrals of Europe (see [[sub-saharan-africa]], on the rejection of the "Hamitic"
diffusionism).

## GHA IV note

The **UNESCO [[gha-vol4-12th-16th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. IV]]** (ch. 21, Fagan, "The
Zambezi and Limpopo basins 1100–1500") sets Great Zimbabwe within the full sequence of the gold-trading
plateau states — **Mapungubwe → Great Zimbabwe → Mutapa and Torwa/Khami** — and stresses (against the
old racist denialism) the **indigenous Shona** authorship of the stone architecture and the **Indian Ocean
gold trade**, via the [[swahili-coast|Swahili]] port of Sofala, that underwrote their wealth.
