# Sparta

Sparta (Lacedaemon) was the dominant land power of Classical Greece: a militarized oligarchy in the Eurotas valley whose unique constitution, hoplite army, and control of the Peloponnesian League made it the chief counterweight to Athenian naval empire. Its society was built on the subjugation of the Helot population and the rigorous agoge training of citizen males. Sparta's stand at Thermopylae and victory in the Peloponnesian War defined its image for posterity, even as its power waned after the 4th century BCE.

## Geographic Description

Sparta occupied the fertile Eurotas valley in Laconia (southeastern Peloponnese), surrounded by mountains (Taygetus to the west, Parnon to the east) that provided natural defenses and limited arable land. The city itself was unwalled for most of its history (relying on its army and the "shield of the Peloponnese"), with villages rather than a dense urban core. The port at Gytheion gave access to the sea. Control of Messenia (west of Taygetus) after the Messenian Wars supplied the Helot labor that freed Spartiates for full-time military training. The region was agriculturally rich (grain, olives) but strategically isolated.

## History of Control and Settlement

- **Bronze Age**: Part of Mycenaean world (possible references in Linear B, Homeric tradition).
- **Dark Age / Early Archaic**: Dorian settlement traditions; conquest of Laconia and (c. 8th–7th c. BCE) Messenia in the Messenian Wars, reducing the population to Helot status.
- **Archaic Constitution** (traditionally Lycurgan, c. 8th–7th c.): The "Great Rhetra" (oracle-sanctioned constitution) established dual kingship (Agiad and Eurypontid lines), Gerousia (council of 28 elders + kings), ephors (five annual magistrates with veto power), and Apella (assembly of adult male citizens). "Homoioi" (Equals) — full citizens trained in the agoge from age 7 — lived on state land worked by Helots. Perioeci (free non-citizens in surrounding areas) handled trade and crafts.
- **Classical** (500–323 BCE): Leader of the Peloponnesian League (alliance of Dorian states). Stand at Thermopylae (480 BCE) under Leonidas. Hegemony after Peloponnesian War victory (404 BCE). Defeat at Leuctra (371 BCE) by Thebes ended supremacy; loss of Messenia.
- **Hellenistic / Roman**: Reduced to second-rank power; Cleomenes III's revolution (227 BCE) attempted social reform but crushed at Sellasia (222 BCE). Roman conquest (146 BCE); later favored by emperors as cultural/museum piece.
- **Later**: Byzantine, then Ottoman; modern town near ancient site.

## Why Sparta Mattered

**Strategic / Military**: The finest hoplite army in Greece — professional, disciplined, laconic. The Peloponnesian League gave Sparta hegemony over the southern mainland. Thermopylae became the archetype of sacrificial defense ("Go tell the Spartans...").

**Economic / Social**: Helot system (state-owned serfs, especially Messenians) allowed Spartiates to focus exclusively on warfare and civic duty. Austerity ("laconic" speech and lifestyle) was both ideology and necessity. Iron currency discouraged wealth display.

**Political**: Unique mixed constitution (kings + elders + ephors + assembly) admired by later theorists (Plato, Aristotle, Polybius, Machiavelli) as a model of stability and balance — though in practice often oligarchic and conservative. Fear of Helot revolt shaped foreign policy (reluctance to campaign far from home).

**Cultural / Symbolic**: Embodiment of militarism, discipline, and communalism in contrast to Athenian individualism and culture. "Spartan mirage" influenced Western ideas of virtue, education, and the state from antiquity to the modern era (e.g., Prussian, Nazi, and even some American military ideals).

## How Its Significance Changed Over Time

- **Peak (c. 550–404 BCE)**: Dominant land power, leader of anti-Persian and then anti-Athenian coalitions.
- **Decline (4th–3rd c. BCE)**: Demographic crisis (oliganthropia — too few full citizens), loss of Messenia, Theban and Macedonian defeats. Attempts at reform (Agis IV, Cleomenes III) failed.
- **Hellenistic/Roman**: Symbol and tourist attraction more than power; Roman emperors (Augustus onward) patronized "traditional" Spartan festivals and training displays.
- **Modern**: Mythologized in literature, film (*300*), and military culture. The actual archaeological/historical Sparta was less monolithic than the legend.

## Historiography and Source Base

The wiki draws on the (comparatively thin) ancient literary sources (Herodotus, Thucydides, Xenophon, Plutarch's *Lycurgus*, Aristotle's *Politics*) cross-checked against archaeology and the detailed modern reconstructions in the ingested CAH, Kagan (political/military narrative), Hanson (tactical/social analysis), and Kennell. Debates: historicity of "Lycurgan" reforms (likely gradual); severity and numbers of Helots; extent of Spartan "austerity" vs. reality; why demographic decline occurred.

Collection coverage is **strong** for Sparta's role in the Classical wars (via the major Peloponnesian War ingests and CAH); more moderate for earlier Archaic constitution and later Hellenistic decline.

### Hall (2014) — villages, Rhetra, mirage

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]]: Sparta remained “settled in villages (*kômai*) according to the old custom” (Thuc. 1.10.2) — Limnai, Pitana, Mesoa, Kynosoura (~300 ha) plus Amyclae; dyarchy may reflect shared basileus authority at village coalescence. Great Rhetra among earliest Greek constitutional evidence (early 7th c. with Polydoros/Theopompus); [[concepts/spartan-mirage|Spartan mirage]] means Xenophon/Plutarch have little value for early institutions. Messenian conquest progressive, not single total annexation; perioikic Laconia as many autonomous small poleis. Full actor-side treatment: [[actors/sparta]].

### From the Oxford History of the Classical World (1986)

**Forrest (Ch.1, [[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW 1986]]).** Sparta as the notable **exception** to the urban *polis* form: a community of citizens, dependents, and non-citizens with a focal religious-political centre, but without the usual fortified city growth. Lycurgan military system, helotage, and dual kingship structure the early Archaic place-history; Chilon’s alliances and 510 intervention against Athens project Spartan power beyond Laconia without annexation.

**Murray (Ch.9).** Place as counter-ideal: state upbringing, syssitia, allotment land farmed by helots; described by Athenian writers as Athens’ opposite (**[[concepts/spartan-mirage]]**), so ordinary economic/social detail (e.g. arms manufacture) falls silent.

**Hornblower (Ch.6).** Domestic geography of power—Messenian helots of one nationality—explains reluctant Aegean imperialism after 478; loss of Messenia after Leuctra (369) collapses the place’s economic-military base.
