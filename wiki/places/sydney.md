# Sydney

**Sydney**, founded at Sydney Cove on **Port Jackson** in 1788, was the landing place of
the First Fleet and the capital of [[new-south-wales-colony|New South Wales]] — the first
and largest British settlement in [[australia|Australia]], on the country of the Eora
people. From a penal beachhead it became the principal port, commercial hub, and gateway
of [[convict-transportation-australia|convict transportation]] and free migration to the
continent.

## From CHA Vol. 1 (Ch 4): Sydney's Physical and Social Development 1788–1822

### The Choice of Sydney Cove

Governor [[arthur-phillip|Arthur Phillip]] found [[botany-bay|Botany Bay]] unsuitable
(insufficient fresh water, poor shelter) and relocated to **Sydney Cove** on Port Jackson
within days of the January 1788 arrival. The cove offered a reliable stream of fresh water
and — critically — no armed warriors on the shore. Port Jackson is the drowned valley
of the Parramatta River, draining the flat Cumberland Plain to the west; the plain,
girdled by the Hawkesbury-Nepean and enclosed by sandstone plateaux, was "the cradle of
modern Australia and the cauldron of the first frontier wars."

### Growth as a Polyglot Port Town

Despite Phillip's instructions to deflect arriving ships to the north shore and ban
shipping, trade, and ship-building, "Sydney quickly became a thriving, polyglot port town,
alive with people, ships and goods from all over the globe." By the first decade of the
nineteenth century, **23 to 33 ships a year** called from the United States, the Cape of
Good Hope, India, China, and southern fisheries. In 1802 the visiting Frenchman François
Péron marvelled at "this assemblage of grand operations, this constant movement of shipping."

Sydney's urban growth was organic — "on these tides of ambitions, hopes and freedoms"
— not planned. Convicts ordered to build government huts appropriated them and fenced
yards. By 1810 some had occupied allotments for fifteen or twenty years; by 1808 around
**85 per cent of the population** occupied their property by "naked possession" (permissive
occupancy without lease or grant). Governor Bligh's 1806–08 attempt to reassert Crown
ownership by cancelling leases and pulling down houses triggered the **Rum Rebellion**
(26 January 1808) and was the colony's most serious political crisis.

### The Eora in Sydney

The coastal Eora were a "notable presence" in Sydney after November 1790, when Bennelong's
group "came in" to the settlement after a long process of kidnapping, retribution
(Phillip took a spear in the shoulder), diplomacy, and reconciliation. Aboriginal people
in Sydney sold fish and shellfish, guided hunting parties, worked in homes and pubs, and
continued to practise traditional law through "great contests" — public trials by spear
— in Sydney's streets intermittently until at least 1824. **Bennelong's** brick house,
built at his own request, stood on Sydney Cove's eastern headland (later Bennelong Point).
Two Eora men, Bennelong and Yemmerrawanie, sailed to London in 1792 with Phillip.
**Bungaree** circumnavigated the continent with Matthew Flinders on HMS *Investigator*
in 1802 — the first Australian to do so.

From the mid-1810s, the Macquaries attempted to shift Aboriginal people out of Sydney,
and newcomers from the 1820s onward coveted the waterfront lands Aboriginal families
still occupied. Dispossession in the Sydney region was "not a single event…but a cycle
repeated continually as the city expanded into its hinterland."

### Commerce and the Emancipist Economy

The first four governors were naval captains who organised the colony like a ship's crew —
weekly rations, communal mess, naval punishments. Officers, as the only colonists with
access to sterling, quickly became importers and wholesalers; emancipists like **James
Underwood**, **Simeon Lord**, and **Mary Reibey** built trading fortunes by purchasing
goods directly from ships. A sophisticated monetary system arose from promissory notes
and merchant's notes without any government money supply. Sydney's "vigorous private
economy far outstripped the government economy" by the 1800s.

The first export industries were maritime: sealing at Bass Strait (from 1798) and whaling
(from 1791) preceded pastoral exports. By 1820 Sydney had the "most extensive and complex
port regulations in the British Empire" — driven by the escalating need to prevent convict
escape on departing vessels.

### The Age of Macquarie

Governor Lachlan Macquarie (1810–22) found Sydney in "good order" and spent the next
decade building it as a proper town: streets renamed, straightened, widened, and levelled;
a grand hospital, convict and soldiers' barracks, orphanage, churches, and ornamental
structures in classical or Gothic brick and stone. He and his wife Elizabeth shared "a
passion for fashionable architecture, art and landscapes, and considered themselves agents
of taste and civilisation in what they expected would be an antipodean wilderness."
Macquarie was responsible for popularising the name **"Australia"** and inaugurating
26 January as **Anniversary Day** (1817).

By 1820, Sydney housed only a **quarter of the colony's population** — most had moved to
rural farming and pastoral communities — yet it remained the commercial, administrative,
and symbolic heart of [[new-south-wales-colony|New South Wales]].
