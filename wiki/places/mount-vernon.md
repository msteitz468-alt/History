# Mount Vernon

## Overview

Mount Vernon was the plantation estate of George Washington on the Potomac River in Virginia. It served as his home, working farm, and a microcosm of the contradictions of the American founding: a site of Enlightenment republican ideals coexisting with chattel slavery.

## History of Control and Settlement

Originally patented in the 17th century, the estate passed to the Washington family. George Washington inherited and expanded it, making it one of the largest and most prominent plantations in the Chesapeake. After Washington's death in 1799, it remained in family hands until sold; in 1858 the Mount Vernon Ladies' Association purchased and preserved it as a historic site.

## Why It Mattered

Mount Vernon was both a working slave plantation and the domestic seat of the first US president. Washington's management of enslaved labor, his evolving (but limited) views on slavery, and his 1799 will providing for the eventual manumission of the Mount Vernon enslaved people make it central to understanding the "tragic side" of the founding. The estate also became a key site of American civil religion and selective memory about the founders.

## Historiography

Scholarship (e.g. Wiencek, Thompson) has used Mount Vernon's records, Washington's papers, and archaeology to document the scale of enslavement there (~300+ people over time), living conditions, and Washington's private expressions of regret alongside public silence and political compromises. The preservation by the Ladies' Association shaped a sanitized public image for generations.
