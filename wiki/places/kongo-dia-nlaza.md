## Overview

**Kongo dia Nlaza** ("the Seven Kingdoms") was a large polity — described variously as a kingdom and an empire — that existed east of the nuclear Kongo territory in the Inkisi and Kwilu river valleys, straddling the area west of the Kwango River. It is a significant but underappreciated element in the origins of the [[kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]]: Thornton (ch. 1 of Bostoen & Brinkman 2018) argues that Kongo dia Nlaza was *the senior political entity* in the region before Kongo's founding, and that several of Kongo's core provinces (including [[mbata-province-kongo|Mbata]], Nsundi, and Mpangu) were originally its western vassals.

The polity is identified by Thornton through a philological correction of a problematic passage in the soldier-chronicler António de Oliveira de Cadornega (writing 1681). Cadornega referred to a polity called "Congo de Amulaca" as Kongo's place of origin; Thornton argues that with a missing cedilla the name becomes "Amulaza" = Kongo dia Nlaza (confirmed when Cadornega refers to the same place a few pages later as "Congo Amulaza").

## Geography

Cardoso (1621 revolt report, published Brásio 1988:533–4) provides the most detailed description of the territory, which he called "Momboares" (*Mumbwadi* = "the Seveners"):

> "It begins 10 leagues east of the City of Salvador [Mbanza Kongo], and ends on the border of the Kingdom of Ocanga, having as a frontier and boundary the Oamba River, and is 70 leagues long and more than 40 wide; it borders the River Zaire on the north."

The territory encompassed:
- The duchies of **Sundi** (Nsundi) and **Batta** (Mbata)
- The Marquisate of **Pango** (Mpangu)
- A core territory to the east and southeast called "Kingdom of Congo Reamolaza" (presumably the ancient core)

Richard Büttner crossed "Kongo dialase" in 1886 and mapped it west of the Kwango River, just south of the modern Congo–Angola border.

## The Seven Kingdoms

The name "Seven Kingdoms" (*Mumbwadi* or *Momboares*) appears in Álvaro II's 1583 letter to the Pope, in which he claimed "the seven kingdoms of Congoria Mulazza" among his titles, apparently indicating recent conquest. A first documentary reference to a ruler of Kongo dia Nlaza as an "Emperor" occurs in a text of c. 1561 (Sebastião de Souto; Brásio 1953a:480). Cavazzi (1687) noted that "the kingdom of Congo Riaucanga" (*Kongo dia Ukanga*, or Okanga) was outside the Seven Kingdoms though eventually integrated into Kongo via [[mbata-province-kongo|Mbata]].

The title "king of kings" = emperor explains why the ruler was styled an emperor in 1561: he was sovereign over seven subordinate kings.

## Role in Kongo's Origins

Thornton's revised thesis assigns Kongo dia Nlaza a central but previously underweighted role in Kongo's founding:

1. **Mbata's origins**: Mbata, the polity that formed the founding alliance with Lukeni lua Nimi's family, was originally a vassal of the Seven Kingdoms whose western provinces had broken away as the empire weakened (c. early fourteenth century). Mbata sought the alliance with the Mpemba Kasi ruling family in part to secure its independence from its former sovereign.

2. **"Grandfather of Kongo"**: The honorific by which Kongo kings addressed Mbata rulers (*enkak'andi a Mwene Kongo*, "Grandfathers of the King") may extend throughout the Seven Kingdoms region. Da Montesarchio in the 1650s found the same "grandfather of Kongo" title in the northeastern district of Lembe (near the Malebo Pool), suggesting it applied to the entire Kongo dia Nlaza territory rather than to Mbata alone.

3. **Senior partnership**: Thornton argues that initially, "Kongo dia Nlaza, represented by Mbata, was the senior partner in the alliance." The Kongo kings' calling themselves "grandchildren" of the Mbata rulers reflects this original seniority, which Lukeni lua Nimi's southward expansion progressively reversed.

4. **Gradual absorption**: Kongo "took bite after bite" of the Seven Kingdoms over two centuries: first Mbata (voluntary alliance, c. 1390), then Nsundi and Mpangu (by conquest, recorded by Lopes as early annexations), and finally the core territories, absorbed by Álvaro I who put them in his titles in 1583.

## The Jagas Invasion

Thornton (2018) raises the possibility that the Jagas invasion of Kongo c. 1568–70 — which nearly destroyed the kingdom and forced Álvaro I onto river islands — may have been organized by or in defense of Kongo dia Nlaza, whose core territories had not yet been absorbed. Lopes reported that the Jagas entered Kongo through Mbata, on its eastern frontier, and a Portuguese priest in 1584 called them "rebels." Thornton suggests they may have been allies of Kongo dia Nlaza resisting Kongo's eastward expansion.

## Historiography

The polity of Kongo dia Nlaza was known to early twentieth-century scholars through the Cadornega passage (published 1877), but the Jesuit missionary Joseph Van Wing's interpretation of it as "Kongo dia Muyaka" and his connection to the Jagas was methodologically flawed. Jean Cuvelier's rewrting of origin traditions (1941) focused on Lukeni lua Nimi's crossing from Vungu and largely set aside the eastern origin question. Vansina (1963) consolidated Cuvelier's account.

The KongoKing project (2012–16) revived interest in the eastern dimension of Kongo origins via linguistic evidence: Bostoen et al. (2013) showed that the word *ngangula* (blacksmith, an important figure in Kongo traditions) derives from eastern Kikongo dialects, providing indirect support for an eastern origin dimension. Thornton's 2018 chapter synthesizes this linguistic finding with a reanalysis of the Cadornega passage and the 1621 revolt report to present the most complete account of Kongo dia Nlaza to date.
