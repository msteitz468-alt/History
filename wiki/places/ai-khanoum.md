# Ai Khanum

**Ai Khanum** (the modern name; the ancient one is uncertain — possibly Alexandria- or
Eucratideia-on-the-Oxus) is the best-excavated Greek city of the
[[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]] Far East, at the confluence of the Oxus (Amu Darya)
and the Kokcha in northern Afghanistan. Uncovered by French excavations in the 1960s–70s,
it is the archaeological showpiece of [[greco-bactrian-kingdom|Greek Bactria]] and a key
piece of evidence in CAH VII.1 ch. 6 (Musti) for the reach of Hellenistic urbanism.

## A Greek City on the Oxus

The site revealed a full Greek civic ensemble thousands of kilometres from the Aegean: a
monumental **palace**, a **theatre**, a **gymnasium**, temples, and a fortified upper
town. **Greek inscriptions** were found — including a famous set of Delphic maxims copied
by one Clearchus (associated with Clearchus of Soli) — and traces of **papyri** (a Greek
philosophical text and an accounting document), proof of a literate Greek high culture
living on the edge of the Iranian and Indian worlds. The surrounding territory was fed by
an irrigation network of Achaemenid (and earlier) origin, now worked by Greek landowners.

## Significance

Ai Khanum is the physical demonstration that [[seleucid-colonization|Hellenistic
colonisation]] planted genuine Greek cities — not mere garrisons — as far as the Oxus, and
that a Graeco-Macedonian urban culture persisted there for generations after the
[[greco-bactrian-kingdom|Bactrian]] secession from the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]]. It
also illustrates the "interpenetration" of Greek and Iranian forms characteristic of the
Hellenistic East. The city was destroyed by nomadic invaders around the mid-second
century BCE; the modern looting of its successor sites underlies many of the famous coins
of the Greco-Bactrian kings.
