# Bonampak

## Geographic description

**Bonampak** (“painted walls,” name coined by Sylvanus Morley) lies in the Lacandon forest of Chiapas, roughly **30 km southwest of Yaxchilan**, in the western Usumacinta lowland world that also includes [[palenque|Palenque]], Piedras Negras, and Yaxchilan. Brought to modern attention in **1946** by Giles Healey.

## Historical significance

### Murals and politics (Evans & Webster 2001)

Bonampak is best known for **Structure 1** murals — the most complete Classic southern lowland Maya painting program surviving. Painted in the **last decade of the eighth century** under king **Chan Muwan**, with Yaxchilan connections, the program depicts more Maya lords together than in any other Maya visual work. The paintings were **unfinished** and represent final work at the site.

- **Room 1:** heir installation; cacao tribute (**5 pi kakaw**); dancers; full musical band.
- **Room 2:** battle and captive presentation with finger mutilation and a dead captive.
- **Room 3:** autosacrifice and captive slaughter.

**Stelae 1–3** celebrate Chan Muwan. An emblem glyph appears on a looted Early Classic monument. Politically, Bonampak was linked to larger **Lacanha** and paid fealty to **Piedras Negras** in the early seventh century before entering the **Yaxchilan** sphere after Shield Jaguar’s accession in **681**.

## Why it mattered

The murals overturn romantic “peaceful priest” stereotypes with documentary force: war, tribute, bloodletting, and court spectacle as simultaneous elite practices. As terminal-eighth-century unfinished art, they also freeze a polity at the edge of the [[maya-classic-collapse-800ce|Classic collapse]].
