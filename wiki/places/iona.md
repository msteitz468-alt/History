# Iona

The small Hebridean island whose **monastery, founded by St Columba (Colum Cille) in 563**, became the
spiritual and cultural powerhouse of the Gaelic world — mother-house of a far-flung monastic *familia*,
the cradle of the **conversion of northern Britain**, and the early home of the **[[charles-edwards-chronicle-of-ireland|Chronicle
of Ireland]]**.

## History and Significance

The Irish prince and monk **Columba** crossed from Ireland to found Iona among the Gaels of **Dál Riata**
in **563**. From this base the **Columban familia** evangelized the Picts and, through the mission of
**Aidan** to **Lindisfarne** (635), much of northern **Northumbria** — making Iona, with Canterbury, one
of the two founts of English Christianity. Its abbots, drawn largely from Columba's kindred (the Cenél
Conaill), governed a network of churches across Ireland and Scotland.

Iona was a centre of **learning and manuscript art** — **Adomnán**, abbot 679–704, wrote the great *Life
of Columba* and the *Law of Innocents* (*Cáin Adomnáin*), and the **Book of Kells** is associated with
the community. It was also a centre of **annal-keeping**: the early layers of the reconstructed
**[[charles-edwards-chronicle-of-ireland|Chronicle of Ireland]]** (to c. 740) appear to have been
compiled here before the record passed to the Irish midlands.

Iona stood at the heart of the **Easter and tonsure controversy** between the Ionan (Irish) and Roman
customs that came to a head at the **[[synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby (664)]]**; the Columban
churches eventually conformed to the Roman Easter (Iona itself in 716). From **795** the monastery was
repeatedly **raided by Vikings** (the massacre of Martyrs' Bay, 806), prompting the removal of Columba's
relics and the community's centre of gravity to Kells in Ireland and Dunkeld in Scotland, though Iona
remained a revered burial-place of kings.
