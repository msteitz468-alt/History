# Cilicia

**Cilicia** is the coastal lowland and mountain hinterland of the **north-eastern corner of
the Mediterranean**, where the Anatolian plateau drops to the sea between the **Taurus**
mountains to the west and the **Amanus** range to the east. Geographers since antiquity
divided it into two zones: **Cilicia Pedias** ("Smooth Cilicia"), the fertile alluvial
plain of the Cydnus, Sarus, and Pyramus rivers around **Tarsus, Adana, and Mopsuestia**;
and **Cilicia Tracheia** ("Rough Cilicia"), the rugged limestone uplands of the western
Taurus. Two passes made the region strategically pivotal: the **Cilician Gates** through
the Taurus (the gateway from central Anatolia to the plain) and the **Syrian Gates** /
Belen Pass through the Amanus (the gateway to Antioch and Syria). Whoever held Cilicia
controlled the land bridge between Asia Minor and the Levant — which is why it mattered so
much to the **[[crusades|Crusaders]]** marching toward [[antioch|Antioch]] and Jerusalem.

It is on this ground that displaced Armenians, pushed out of the [[armenia|Bagratid
homeland]] after the Byzantine annexations and Seljuk incursions of the eleventh century,
built the **[[cilician-armenian-kingdom|Cilician Armenian Kingdom]]** — the only Armenian
state ever to sit on the Mediterranean. Its power rested on a chain of mountain fortresses
commanding the passes and the plain: **Vahka** (the early Roupenid stronghold in the
Taurus), **Anazarbus**, **Sis** (the eventual royal capital), **Lampron** and **Babaron**
(the Hetumid lordships guarding the Cilician Gates), **Tarsus**, the river-island
catholicosate of **Hromkla** on the Euphrates, and the coastal port-castle of **Korikos**.

The strategic value that made Cilicia a kingdom also made it a target. After three
centuries as a Christian polity entangled with Byzantium, the Latins, the Mongols, and the
Seljuks, it fell to the **[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]]** with the capture of Sis in **1375**,
ending the last medieval Armenian state. (Geography and control history above are filled out
from [[ghazarian-armenian-kingdom-cilicia-2000|Ghazarian]] during reconciliation.)

## History of Control and Settlement

In antiquity Cilicia passed through Hittite, Assyrian, Achaemenid, and Seleucid hands before
Rome annexed it in **27 BC**, dividing it administratively into Cilicia Pedias (metropolis
**Tarsus**) and Cilicia Tracheia; its churches fell under the patriarchate of Antioch. Lost
to the Arabs and recovered for Byzantium by **Nicephorus II Phocas in 965**, it was ruled
through a network of regional fortresses. From the eleventh century, Armenians displaced from
[[armenia|Greater Armenia]] settled in growing numbers — a trickle from the sixth century
that became a major migration after the fall of Ani (1045) and [[battle-of-manzikert-1071|Manzikert
(1071)]]. Two rival Armenian houses took root: the pro-Byzantine **Hetumids** around Lampron
and Barberon, west of the Cilician Gates, and the traditionalist **Roupenids** in the eastern
Taurus, who declared independence under Roupen I (1080) and built the
**[[cilician-armenian-kingdom|Cilician Armenian Kingdom]]**. After 1375 the region was held by
the [[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluks]] and later the Ottomans.

## Why It Mattered

Cilicia's significance was overwhelmingly **strategic and commercial**: it was the **land
bridge** between Anatolia and Syria, controlling the only practical routes — the Cilician and
Syrian Gates — for any army moving between the plateau and the Levant. This made it
indispensable to the [[crusades|Crusaders]] marching on [[antioch|Antioch]], and gave the
Armenian polity there leverage far beyond its size. Its Mediterranean port of **Ayas** became
a principal terminus of the overland trade from Persia, India, and China to the Italian
maritime republics, the economic foundation of the Cilician kingdom. (Detail from
