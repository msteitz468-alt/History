## Description

The Rokumeikan (鹿鳴館, "Deer-Cry Pavilion") was a two-story Western-style building in central Tokyo, completed November 28, 1883, and operated as a venue for diplomatic entertainment and the display of Japanese adoption of Western social customs. It was designed by the English architect Josiah Conder in "French Renaissance style" with a mansard roof, arched portico with "a vaguely Moorish look," and columns showing "an Indian influence." Only the garden, with its pines, pond, and stone lanterns, indicated the building's Japanese location.

The name derived from a "deer crying" (rokumei) song in the ancient Chinese book of poetry the Shih Ching that describes welcoming honored guests — appropriate for a building whose primary function was entertaining foreign dignitaries.

It was built on the site of a former armory of the Satsuma domain at a total cost of 180,000 yen — compared to 40,000 yen for the Foreign Ministry building — and the irony was not lost on contemporaries: "surely nothing could be further from the spartan discipline characteristic of Satsuma samurai than the frivolity to which the new building was dedicated."

## Historical Significance

The Rokumeikan was Foreign Minister Inoue Kaoru's primary instrument for demonstrating to Western powers that Japan had achieved sufficient cultural parity to be treated as an equal — and thus to secure revision of the unequal treaties. Inoue's theory was that "by cultivating congenial relations with foreign dignitaries, he might persuade them that the Japanese had attained so high a degree of European culture that they had to be treated as equals."

The venue hosted elaborate dinners (menus listed in French), ballroom dancing by Japanese in London-tailored evening clothes to European tunes played by the army and navy bands, and — famously — a costume ball staged at the prime minister's residence in 1885 by Itō Hirobumi at which 400 members of the nobility and high government officials dressed in European fancy dress. "Itō and his wife, Umeko, were attired as members of the Venetian nobility, and their daughter as an Italian peasant girl."

## Reception and Failure

Western observers were not impressed. European guests found the spectacle comic rather than convincing. The French artist Georges Bigot produced a famous cartoon showing a Japanese couple admiring themselves in a mirror — their elegant European fashions reflected as a pair of monkeys. The French writer Pierre Loti, attending the emperor's birthday ball in November 1886, described it as "quite simply a monkey show" and published his impressions as "Un bal à Yeddo." The one figure who earned his genuine admiration was Inoue's wife, Takeko — who, having lived in Europe, displayed "complete ease."

The Rokumeikan era "did not contribute significantly to ending the unequal treaties." Inoue resigned as foreign minister in 1887, and his successor Ōkuma Shigenobu pursued a different strategy. The unequal treaties (specifically extraterritoriality) were not terminated until 1899; tariff autonomy was not achieved until 1911.

## Cultural Significance

Despite its failure as diplomatic strategy, the Rokumeikan became a defining symbol of the early Meiji era's ambivalent relationship to Western modernity. The building represented both the genuine energy with which Japanese elites engaged Western forms and the gap between surface adoption and deep equality that foreigners exploited. Keene notes that "writers of more recent times have experienced nostalgia for the brief flowering of the Rokumeikan, a time when some Japanese boldly stepped from the shadows of upper-class society of the past into a brightly lit ballroom evoking the Paris of Napoleon III."

The Rokumeikan also marked the emergence of Japanese women into public ceremonial life. The opening ceremony, presided over by Inoue and his wife Takeko, was itself significant as a joint public appearance by a senior official and his spouse. The empress began wearing Western dress in public in 1886 partly in the context of the social changes the Rokumeikan represented.
