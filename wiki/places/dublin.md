# Dublin

## Hiberno-Norse Dublin (9th–11th c.)

Dublin began as a Viking **longphort** (naval base) founded in 841 at the Liffey/Poddle
confluence — part of the [[processes/scandinavian-settlement-of-britain|Scandinavian
settlement]] of [[places/ireland|Ireland]], which was "largely restricted to urban
locations." After expulsion (902) and re-occupation (914) it grew into a true town: by 989
"an informal, yet recognizably urban streetscape existed," and the first Dublin coin was
struck by Sitric son of Amlaíb in **997**. Its kings (notably **Amlaíb Cúarán**, also king of
York) oriented Dublin toward both the [[places/york|York]]–Irish-Sea world and Irish
overkingship. Sacked by **[[actors/brian-boru|Brian Bóruma]]** on New Year's Day 1000, Dublin
became the strategic key to the Irish high-kingship: **Diarmait mac Máel na mBó** seized it in
1052, and "control of Dublin provided a key with the potential to unlock" the Isle of Man, the
Hebrides, and maritime Wales. Its city walls are archaeologically dated to c.1100. See
[[periods/early-middle-ages-britain-ireland]].

## Modern Dublin (Joyce's city)

Capital of Ireland and the city that James Joyce made the center of modern literature. In Joyce's lifetime (1882–1941) it was a provincial capital under British rule, marked by political ferment (Parnell, Home Rule, 1916 Rising), economic stagnation, and a rich, self-contained cultural and linguistic world. Joyce left in 1904 but reconstructed Dublin from memory and imagination with obsessive fidelity in *Dubliners*, *A Portrait*, *Ulysses*, and *Finnegans Wake*.

The city supplied Joyce with his primary subject: the paralysis ("hemiplegia of the will") of Irish life and the possibility of redeeming it through art. 16 June 1904 (the day he first walked out with Nora Barnacle) became Bloomsday, the single day of *Ulysses*. The National Library, Sandymount Strand, the pubs, the streets, the churches, and the domestic interiors of Dublin are rendered with encyclopedic precision.
