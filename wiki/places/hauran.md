## Overview

The Hauran is the volcanic basalt plateau of southern Syria and north-eastern Jordan,
home to [[bosra|Bosra]] (the Nabataean and later Roman provincial capital) as well as the
temple/shrine sites of Qanawat (Roman Canatha) and Si' (Seia). It is architecturally and
geologically distinct from the two neighboring building traditions Ball surveys in the
same region: the limestone [[dead-cities|Dead Cities]] massif to the north, and the
mud-brick building tradition of the Euphrates region to the east.

## Basalt "Slab-and-Lintel" Architecture

Ball identifies a distinct regional building tradition centered on the Hauran and
north-eastern Jordan: the local black volcanic basalt gave rise to a distinctive
cantilevered "slab and lintel" architectural style, strong enough to support multi-storey
domestic buildings — three, even four storeys survive in places, notably at Umm
al-Jimal — a structural capacity absent in the limestone Dead Cities region to the north
or the mud-brick architecture of the Euphrates to the east.

## Temple and Tomb Decoration

Hauran temple and tomb decoration exhibits the eastern "baroque"/hybrid-order tendency
Ball documents throughout the Roman Near East: the pilaster capitals of the temple at
Slim combine Ionic and Corinthian elements in hybrid form, and a 2nd-century AD tomb at
Shaqqa has a baroque plan with deep exterior exedrae comparable to the "Temple of Venus"
at [[baalbek|Baalbek]].

## Nabataean Influence at Si' and Qanawat

At Qanawat (Roman Canatha) and the associated sacred site of Si', Nabataean cultural
influence was, per Ball, "uppermost" and Hellenistic influence "least" of all
[[decapolis|Decapolis]] cities. Si' was sacred to the Semitic gods Baal-Shamin
(worshipped there before the Roman period) and Dushara (the chief Nabataean deity, whose
cult arrived with the Nabataean conquest in the early 1st century BC). The Si' temple
complex mixes Semitic religious content, Nabataean decorative richness, Hellenistic
"classical order" ornamental vocabulary, and — per Ball — an "Iranian tradition" evident
in its "square-in-square" sanctuary plan.

Ball also notes that rock-cut tomb façades, so characteristic of [[petra|Petra]] and
Medain Saleh (Hegra), are not typical of Nabataean architecture generally: they occur
only at those two sites and nowhere else in Nabataean territory, including the Hauran,
Jordan, or the Negev.
