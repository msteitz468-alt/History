# Madras

Madras (now Chennai) began as the [[english-east-india-company|EIC]]'s first
**territorial** foothold in India and became the colonial administrative and
educational capital of South India.

## Fort St George — the EIC's first landlordship (Roy)

[[roy-east-india-company-2016|Tirthankar Roy (2016)]] traces the English arrival on
the Coromandel coast through a string of failures: an attempt to settle at Pulicat
(1611) was blocked by the Dutch, and the fallback at Masulipatnam, ruled by
Golconda, became freely tradeable only in **1632** — by which time "the weavers and
dyers who had earlier supplied them cloth had all died in the 1630 famine." In
**1639** the factor **Francis Day** (1605–70) chose a site between the villages of
**Madrasapatnam and Chinnapatnam**, purchased from the local ruler **Damarla
Venkatadri**, who had promised to build a fort in exchange for Persian horses. When
Day arrived in 1640 the promised structure proved to be "a fort made of palmyra
leaves," so Day built **Fort St George** with his own money, "in defiance of orders
from London" — possible only "because Surat and Masulipatnam joined Day in quietly
ignoring the Company headquarters."

Madras was an immediate commercial success: within months weaver families settled
there and merchants relocated from the Portuguese colony of San Thomé, so that by
the 1670s the combined settlement held some **40,000** people (Thomas Bowrey).
Crucially, "unlike Surat or Masulipatnam, where the English merely had the right to
reside and conduct trade, in the Fort St George they were landlords" over roughly
six square miles — making the Company a territorial power on a small scale decades
before [[battle-of-plassey-1757|Bengal]]. The Coromandel head office shifted from
Masulipatnam to Madras in 1642, and in **1657** Fort St George was elevated from
subordination to Bantam into an independent presidency. A century later, surviving
the **First Carnatic War** (1746–49 — Madras lost to the French and restored by the
European peace) convinced its leaders "that their own well-being did not depend on
following orders from London," but on becoming "a stronger military force."

## Colonial and educational capital

By the nineteenth century Madras was the administrative and university capital of
South India — and the place where [[ramanujan-srinivasa|Ramanujan]] spent his most
difficult patron-seeking years (1909–1914) and died in 1920. The Madras Port Trust,
the University of Madras (which eventually gave him a research scholarship), the
Connemara Library, and the houses in Triplicane and later Chetput are all part of
the story. It was in Madras that the letter to Hardy was posted and where the hero's
welcome and funeral took place.
