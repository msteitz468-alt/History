# Varna (Chalcolithic Cemetery)

**Varna**, on the Bulgarian Black Sea coast, is the type-site of the richest variant
of the Balkan Eneolithic (Chalcolithic) — a cemetery of the later fifth millennium
BCE that yielded **the oldest large assemblage of worked gold known anywhere in the
world**. In [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] (Ch. 3, Garašanin)
Varna is the climax of the **East Balkan–Lower Danubian graphite-pottery complex**:
the coastal **Varna group** corresponds to Phase III of the
**Gumelnița–Kodzadermen** culture and is "in fact the richest variant" of it.
(*The place-type field is a schema limitation — Varna is a cemetery/settlement
cluster, not a battle-site.*)

## Description and significance

The Varna cemetery (and neighbouring sites such as Goljamo Delčevo and Devnja)
belongs to the world of fortified tells and, near Varna, **pile-dwelling
settlements** in the Luda Kamčija valley. Goljamo Delčevo shows **seventeen
Eneolithic horizons**, rectangular houses in regular streets, and triple
palisades — evidence of dense, organized, defended communities.

Varna's importance is **mortuary and social**, not architectural. Its graves
divide sharply between the lavishly furnished and the poor, with a class of
**symbolic ("cenotaph") burials** containing clay masks and gold but no body. The
concentration of gold — ornaments, appliqués, sceptre- and regalia-fittings — in a
few graves is the **earliest unambiguous archaeological signature of institutional
social rank** in Europe: a society in which prestige metal marked out particular
individuals. It is the showpiece for the volume's argument that **metallurgy drove
social stratification** (Garašanin: the working of metal "inaugurated the beginning
of social stratification").

## Place in the metallurgy story

Varna's gold is the prestige end of a production system whose extraction end is
visible at the **Rudna Glava** copper mine (eastern Serbia, worked in the
Vinča–Pločnik phase) and the **Ai Bunar** mine near Stara Zagora in Thrace — shaft
mines worked with stone mallets and fire-setting. Together they anchor the case for
an **autochthonous Balkan metallurgy** independent of (or only loosely stimulated
by) the Near East. The full argument and its historiography are on
[[old-europe-copper-metallurgy]].

## Dating

CAH III.1 places the East Balkan Eneolithic "mainly in the fourth and third
millennia," cautioning that "new methods of dating have produced even higher
dates, which can be accepted only with reservations" — the volume's standing
**uncalibrated-radiocarbon** caveat. Modern **calibrated** dates put the Varna
cemetery at roughly **4600–4200 BCE**, i.e. several centuries earlier than the
volume's uncalibrated figures imply; the wiki uses the calibrated range and flags
the volume's dates as systematically too young.

## Historiography

Written within the Cold-War-era **culture-historical** tradition (Varna "culture"
= Varna "group" = a people), the CAH account predates the site's full
international fame; the spectacular grave inventories have since made Varna the
standard textbook case for **emergent inequality** in prehistoric Europe — though
whether the gold marks hereditary rank, achieved status, or ritual office remains
debated. The contrast between Varna's wealth and the **collapse of the Old European
tell world** soon after (under steppe pressure — see
[[indo-european-migrations]]) is one of prehistory's sharper reversals.
