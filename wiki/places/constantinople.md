# Constantinople

**Constantinople** — refounded by [[actors/constantine-the-great|Constantine the Great]]
on the site of Greek Byzantium and dedicated in **330 CE** — was the capital of the
eastern Roman, then [[actors/byzantine-empire|Byzantine]], empire for more than eleven
centuries. Sited on the high road linking west and east, on a defensible promontory
commanding the Bosporus between the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds, it became the
greatest city of late-antique and medieval Christendom.

## Geographic and Strategic Position

Constantine chose the location for its commanding access to "the lands of the march"
and its natural defenses. By the fourth century the city's population had grown
**ten-fold in under a century, from c. 30,000 to c. 300,000**, sustained by grain
fleets redirected from [[places/alexandria|Alexandria]] — in Justinian's early reign,
8,000,000 *artabai* (some 310 million liters) of Egyptian wheat arrived annually. A bread
dole established under Constantine fed 80,000 citizens, and the threat of urban riot made
the smooth arrival of grain a permanent imperative of statecraft. The city was a
**multilingual hub**: in its streets one heard Syriac and Aramaic, Coptic and Ethiopic,
Gothic and Hunnic, Persian and Arabic, alongside Latin and Greek.

## Monumental Building

[[actors/justinian|Justinian]]'s reign (527–565) saw "the grandest building spree in the
annals of Roman history," crowned by the rebuilt **Hagia Sophia** after the Nika riots —
its dome the largest of the ancient world. [[actors/procopius|Procopius]] catalogued these
works in his *Buildings*.

## Disease, Siege, and the Late-Antique Crisis

Constantinople's dense connectivity made it a magnet for catastrophe as well as wealth:

- In **447** an earthquake felled 57 towers of the land walls and left the capital
  exposed to **Attila's Huns**; according to Harper it was "the ramparts of the local
  disease pool" — endemic infection striking the besiegers — that repelled them.
- In **542** the **[[events/justinianic-plague-541|Justinianic Plague]]** reached the city
  from Pelusium via Alexandria, killing on a scale that Procopius compared to the
  emptying of the world; [[actors/justinian|Justinian]] himself contracted and survived
  bubonic plague.
- In **618**, during the Persian wars, the centuries-old state grain shipments from Egypt
  **ended forever** — "the spine of imperial connectivity was snapped" — a structural
  turning point in the contraction of the empire into a Byzantine rump state.

See [[sources/harper-fate-of-rome-2017|Harper, *The Fate of Rome*]] and
[[concepts/roman-climate-optimum|the Roman environmental and disease regime]].

## The City as Economic Actor (Laiou & Morrisson)

Constantinople was a **megalopolis** without medieval Western parallel and the pivot of the
[[byzantine-economy|Byzantine economy]]. In the sixth century it was the largest city in the
world (≥400,000; C. Zuckerman argues ~700,000), provisioned by the annual Egyptian grain
*embole* (~234,000 tons via ~500 ships) until public wheat distribution ceased permanently in
**618**; after the crisis its provisioning base shrank to Thrace, Bithynia and the Pontic
region, and its population fell to perhaps 40,000–70,000 by c.700. It recovered toward
~400,000 at its twelfth-century apogee. Its huge demand generated "industries of art" —
silk, goldsmithing, glass — regulated through the **Book of the Eparch** (issued 912), which
organised the guilds (*systemata*) of craftsmen and traders under the eparch, set maximum
profits on some trades, and structured the silk industry's supply chain; foreign traders
(Rus, Muslims) faced residency limits and quarters.

The city's controlled "port-of-trade" character eroded once
[[alexios-i-komnenos|Alexios I]]'s privileges (1082/1126) opened it to Venetian and Pisan
merchants. After **1204** its population and industries collapsed (~50,000 by 1453); the
Genoese colony of **Pera/Galata** across the Golden Horn, charging only 2% customs against
the city's 10% *kommerkion*, captured the Black Sea trade — a fiscal self-defeat the empire
corrected only in 1347/8, far too late. See [[byzantine-monetary-system|the monetary
system]] and [[medieval-europe-asia-trade|Italian-dominated trade]].

## Wickham, *Framing the Early Middle Ages* (2005)

[[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham]] argues that the continued strength of the Byzantine state even in crisis **helped weaken provincial cities**: unlike Umayyad Syria-Palestine, where non-converting city elites had mainly the local stage, Byzantium remained rich enough that elites could abandon informal urban patronage for thematic military, Constantinopolitan administrative, or ecclesiastical hierarchies. The roughly **80% deurbanization** of the heartland can thus be read as reconfiguration around fewer state-driven provincial centres (Antalya, Izmir, Trabzon, Nicaea, Thessaloniki) plus Constantinople’s enduring demographic/economic dominance despite size decline. Public processional space kept Constantinian/Justinianic spatial rationale far longer than in most former-empire cities (tenth-century city “not very different from that of the sixth” in this respect). Contrasts with localized city aristocracies of [[roman-syria]].

## Islamic views and late medieval transformations (Cobb 2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb, *The Race for Paradise* (2014)]] situates Constantinople in the Muslim mental map of the *dar al-harb* and in the long Frankish–Ottoman endgame.

**Scale and itinerary.** On the eve of the Crusades Cobb places only Constantinople among Christian cities near great Islamic urban scale—**~half a million**—versus Baghdad’s late-ninth-century peak (maybe ~**800,000**), Cairo ~half that, Córdoba at least ~**100,000**. Late-ninth-century captive **Harun ibn Yahya**, taken by the Byzantine navy off Palestine, was brought to Constantinople; after release he traveled west through the Balkans to Thessalonica, past “a village, called Venice,” to Rome, mapping movement between *dar al-islam* and *dar al-harb*.

**Crusading corridor.** People’s Crusade survivors of the **1096** Kibotos disaster were rescued by Byzantine ships to Constantinople; the Crusade of **1101** staged from the city; Louis VII’s core after Anatolian attrition was shipped to Antioch by March **1148**, Conrad to Constantinople January **1148**. Ibn al-Qalanisi recorded **1097** anxiety at “armies of the Franks… from the direction of the sea of Constantinople with forces not to be reckoned for multitude.”

**Fourth Crusade (1204).** Preached **1198** with Ayyubid Egypt as principal target; diverted to Constantinople—toppled the Byzantine emperor and established a Latin kingdom on Byzantine soil. Arabic sources are sparse but informed (sack/burn; relics and objets sold, including to the Near East); Abu Shama: Franks looted treasures and marbles—“Damascus has seen plenty of that marble.” Al-ʿAdil had clear advance warning; a **1204** naval raid on Rosetta (probably connected) met impotent Ayyubid response (no fleet). Greeks retook the city from the Franks in **1261**.

**Ottoman approach and 1453.** **1359**: Prince Murad, still acting ostensibly for ousted Cantacuzenus, threatened Constantinople—the first Ottoman attack on the city—then spent years subduing Thrace along the Via Egnatia. Beyazid besieged the city around the **1396** Nicopolis campaign. Mehmed II from the outset aimed at final conquest: **1452** Rumeli Hisar completed opposite Anadolu Hisar, cutting Black Sea supplies; Constantine XI likened neighborliness to “the falcon and the crow.” By early April **1453** the city was surrounded; surrender offer refused; Genoese Pera pledged neutrality while supplying both sides; Venetians sided with Byzantium. Late April: overland portage of ~**70** ships on greased rollers into the Golden Horn overstretched defenses. Fall before dawn **29 May** after a fifty-four-day spring siege; ~**4,000** said killed including Constantine XI (hedge chronicler figure). Traditional three days’ pillage; Hagia Sophia adapted as mosque; first Friday prayers invoked Sultan Mehmed. Successors adorned Istanbul with palaces, mosques, madrasas and repopulated with Christian, Muslim, and Jewish subjects; by **1500** it was the largest city in Europe—Cobb’s “final ingredient” transforming the Ottoman house from a ghazi confederation into a world empire.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

Attributed claims from [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]] (Chs. XVII, XXVI, XLI, LIII, LXVIII, etc.).

- **Foundation.** Constantine left “a new capital, a new policy, and a new religion”; site “formed by nature for the centre and capital of a great monarchy”; “cities of Greece and Asia were despoiled of their most valuable ornaments”; “artificial colony… raised at the expense of the ancient cities”; Egyptian corn fed “a lazy and insolent populace”; titled Second/New Rome, name Constantinople prevailed; “never profaned by the worship of idols.”
- **Extent.** Constantine’s walls five of seven hills; extreme length ~three Roman miles; Golden Horn “attracted the commerce of the ancient world.”
- **Preservation thesis.** “The foundation of Constantinople more essentially contributed to the preservation of the East, than to the ruin of the West.”
- **Sieges and survival.** Reduced nearly to its walls under Phocas/early Heraclius; two Arab sieges saved by Greek fire and walls; under Amurath I first encircled in Asia and Europe by one hostile monarchy—“decline… almost coeval with her foundation.”
- **Latin conquest era.** Crusaders astonished by riches—“sovereign of all other cities” (Villehardouin via Gibbon); antiques melted for pay; Venetian horses to St Mark’s.
- **1453.** After fifty-three days “irretrievably subdued by the arms of Mahomet the Second”; Timur’s victory over Bajazet had accidentally delayed the fall ~fifty years.
- **East–West politics.** Byzantine court beheld “with indifference, perhaps with pleasure, the disgrace of Rome… and the loss of the West” (General Observations coloring).

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

**Foundation, annona, water, walls.** Building from late 324; dedication 11 May 330 exploited a near-ideal maritime/strategic peninsula; Constantine modeled it on tetrarchic imperial cities rather than as an immediate replacement for Rome. Egyptian tax grain from the start (Constantine: annona for 80,000; by Justinian possibly up to 600,000 recipients); Notitia (~425) lists multiple public granaries; water supply expanded (Valens aqueduct >180 km; successive cisterns 421–609); Theodosian land walls (413, rebuilt after 447 earthquake) plus mid-fifth-century sea walls made the city “virtually impregnable” until 1204.

**Hippodrome as ruler–people encounter.** Base of Theodosius I’s hippodrome obelisk (victory over Magnus Maximus) depicts emperors in the loggia with family (Valentinian II, Arcadius, Honorius), civilian/military dignitaries, bodyguards, and packed spectators — “the first monument to depict the crucial encounter of the east Roman ruler with his people in the hippodrome.” After the fourth century such contact was largely ritualized.

**Central government concentrated in the capital.** Fifth–sixth-century eastern civilian government concentrated in Constantinople (cabinet/*consistorium*, *magister officiorum*, *quaestor sacri palatii*, *comites* of largesses and *res privata*, chamberlain, scholae, *agentes in rebus*, scrinia). By Justinian’s time central archives sat under hippodrome seats beside the palace. Mitchell rejects “Byzantine” as synonym for tortuous government: “Roman government in the sixth century ad was more rationally organized than it had ever been,” grounded in codified law.
