# Aphroditō (Kōm Ishkāw)

**Aphroditō** (modern Kōm Ishkāw), a Middle Egyptian *kōmē*, is one of [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Chris Wickham]]’s core documentary windows on late Roman and early Islamic Egyptian society—paired with Oxyrhynchos for great landowning, Aphroditō for **small and medium properties**, and later with [[jeme|Jēme]] for eighth-century Coptic village life. Part of: [[roman-egypt]].

## Documentary profile

In the latest Roman period the two main documentary foci are Oxyrhynchos and Aphroditō. A second Aphroditō set from the **700s–710s** is trilingual (Greek, Coptic, Arabic); later eighth-century collections from Bala’īza and especially Jēme are essentially Coptic. Eighth-century Aphroditō has attracted good work because its documentation is largely about the **tax system**; later Coptic collections remain under-analysed relative to their richness.

## Autopract village and fiscal protagonism

Sixth-century Aphroditō was an **autopract** village: it held a grant of fiscal autonomy from the pagarchy of Antaiopolis, dating from Leo I (457–74). Successive pagarchs attacked that status (including violence under Mēnas: imprisonment, rape of nuns, canal-blocking, confiscations). Villagers petitioned Theodora and Justinian, hired high officials to enforce judgments, and by the early eighth century Aphroditō was independent enough to have become a **pagarchy in its own right**.

## Society: medium owners and strong community

Despite some urban-style crafts and markets, Aphroditō’s society was run by **medium owners and rich peasants** (e.g. the Psimanobet/Dioskoros family)—it “belongs in a chapter on villages.” Mid-520s cadaster shows extreme field-by-field fragmentation (plots often under an aroura; none over 27 arourai in the surviving section); monasteries and Count Ammonios appear among larger owners, but petitions could even style the village as made of *leptoktētores* (small owners). Individual fates were unstable and violent-edged, yet social structure and elite continuity endured (Psimanobet family and major monastery still on eighth-century tax lists). Multiple competing patronage systems cancelled each other out; a **strong village community** ran taxes and irrigation, structuring interaction top to bottom—unlike the weak villages of the Lucchesia ([[lucca]]).

## Umayyad micro-management (Qurra–Basilios)

Umayyad Aphroditō (especially 709–11 under governor Qurra ibn Sharīk writing to pagarch Basilios) models **hands-on fiscal government**: poll tax, money land tax, grain *embolē*, and ad hoc *dapanē*, due thrice yearly as under Rome but often late; unlimited ad hoc requisitions (men, sailors, ships, building materials for Fusṭāṭ and the Damascus mosque, even iron for nails) over 350 km of Nile waterways; fugitive hunting; orders against disabling torture; insistence on fair assessment. Structure: governor apportions by pagarchy → village; village leaders (*lashane*/*meizōn*) collect. Continuities with Roman main taxes and register forms, but denser central-to-local instruction than late Roman decentralized city/landowner mediation—fewer levels, governor speaking directly to pagarchs and sometimes villages. See [[umayyad-caliphate]], [[tax-based-vs-land-based-states]].

## Why it mattered

Aphroditō shows Wickham’s Egypt in microcosm: corrupt but stable fiscal social relations; fragmented landowning; active peasant/village agency; continuity of tax practice across the Arab conquest. Contrasts with great-estate Oxyrhynchos and with the more closed *noc nrōme* hegemony at [[jeme|Jēme]]. Source: [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005]].
