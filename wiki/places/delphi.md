# Delphi and the Oracle of Apollo

**Delphi** — the sanctuary and oracle of **Apollo** on the slopes of Parnassus — was the
most authoritative religious centre of Archaic Greece, "the impartial interpreter of
Apollo's will" whose blessing colonists, lawgivers, tyrants and kings sought before any
major undertaking. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 41, v, W. G. G.
Forrest) gives a sharply argued political history of the oracle.

## The oracle and its workings

After a Mycenaean cult (to the Earth-goddess Ga) and a Dark-Age lull, Delphi
**blossomed about 750 BCE** and by 700 was "known throughout the Greek world for its
wealth and its wisdom" (already in the *Iliad*). The client, ritually purified at the
spring of Castalia and having sacrificed and paid, put his question to the **Pythia**,
Apollo's priestess, who "became possessed of the god" and answered — by lot or in
verse, the latter most probably rendered intelligible by a priest (*prophetes*).
Forrest deflates the later Christian caricature of "frenzied ravings": the Greeks, "with
keen noses for corruption," found Delphi no more fraudulent than any successful
religious organization, and clients and priests alike **genuinely believed**. The
standard question was not "what will happen if I do X?" but "**would it be a good thing
if I did X?**" — leaving the unchosen alternative safely hypothetical, and (with a few
prudent refusals and a safety-net of "the priests misrepresented the god") making
general success almost assured.

## Delphi and colonization

Delphi's rise was "intimately connected with the success of Greek
[[greek-colonization|colonization]]": from c. 735 (Sicilian Naxos) states "turned above
all to Delphi for divine sanction" before founding a colony — Cicero's question, "what
colony did Greece send out without a prophecy from Pytho?" The oracle gave colonists a
**moral title to other men's land**, and every foundation story duly acquired its (often
forged) oracle. Forrest argues the pattern is partisan, not neutral: in the
[[lelantine-war-c700bce|Lelantine War]], **every** colony of Chalcis and her allies (Corinth,
Thessaly, Sparta) had a Delphic foundation-legend, while Eretria's side had none —
Delphi "had been adopted by the winning side."

## Delphi and the Archaic revolution

Through the seventh century Delphi backed the **new against the old** — giving oracles
to the tyrant **Cypselus** of Corinth (proclaiming him "king" and liberator), to
**Pheidon** of Argos, to **Cylon** at Athens (unsuccessfully) — siding with the wave of
[[greek-tyranny|tyranny]] and lawgiving that overthrew the old aristocracies (it also
guided the Spartan and other constitutional settlements). Forrest weighs whether this
reflected **expediency** (backing likely winners) or **principle** (a genuine sympathy
with reform, perhaps owed to the Cretan priests who, tradition said, interpreted the
god). The oracle's authority drew even non-Greeks: **[[lydia|Gyges]]** of Lydia paid for
the Pythia's verdict in his coup, and his descendant **Croesus** became "one of Apollo's
most ardent admirers," lavishing gold before the fatal advice to "cross the Halys and
destroy an empire."

## The First Sacred War and the Amphictyony

The neighbouring city of **Crisa/Cirrha**, grown rich on the pilgrim traffic, "harassed
the pilgrims" — and was crushed in the **First Sacred War (c. 595–590)** by a holy
alliance of **Thessaly, Sicyon (Cleisthenes) and Athens (the Alcmaeonid Alcmaeon)**;
Crisa was destroyed and its plain dedicated to Apollo, never again to be cultivated.
The war was at heart "a struggle for possession of the oracle": thereafter (from c. 550
at latest) Delphi was governed by the **Amphictyony** — the ancient association of a
dozen central-Greek "tribes," bifocal between Delphi and Demeter's shrine at Thermopylae,
weighted toward **Thessaly** (whose dependent *perioikoi* padded its council votes). The
Alcmaeonids of Athens, enriched through Delphi by their Lydian contacts, rebuilt the
temple after its **fire of 548**.

## The fatal medism

Forrest's verdict: Delphi made "four great moments of decision" — adoption by the winning
side in the Lelantine War; the triumphant backing of the seventh-century revolution; an
error (over-mighty interference) corrected by the calculated piety of the Sacred War's
victors; and a final, unredeemable error — when the **Persians came, Apollo "together
with most of his amphictiony, medized,"** counselling caution and submission. "It is no
accident that thereafter Delphi ceased to be an active power in Greek politics," still
revered privately but no longer able to make governments or wars.

## Why Delphi mattered

- The **panhellenic clearing-house of legitimacy** — sanctioning colonies, reforms,
  tyrannies and alliances across the Greek world, a rare unifying institution among the
  fractious poleis.
- A barometer of Archaic politics: its shifting clientele (Dorian then increasingly
  Ionian after 600) tracks the rise of tyranny, the Alcmaeonids, and the coming Persian
  crisis.

## Hall (2014) — amphictyony, First Sacred War skepticism, Serpent Column

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]]: against eighth-century panhellenic consensus, monumental temples/treasuries and stephanitic games at Delphi mainly late seventh–sixth (Pythian games reorganized 582). Early sanctuary phases show local dining then broader dedications; some Bronze Age cult sites (perhaps Delphi) have Dark Age blanks. **Amphictyony** originally at Anthela (Demeter); early members Dorians, East Locrians, Ainianes, Malians; equal votes of later-subordinate Magnesians/Perrhaebi/Phthiotid Achaeans imply enrollment before Thessalian hegemony; control of Delphi tied to Thessalian interest in the “Great Isthmus Corridor.”

**First Sacred War:** virtually no literary evidence predates the fourth century; fullest clusters in 340s–330s; Herodotus’ silence on Solon/Alcmaeon/Cleisthenes of Sicyon is troubling; chronological squeeze 594–585; Hall prefers agnosticism — inventing a Sacred War from nothing was risky, but famous names accreted; kernel is amphictyonic (not local) administration and ban on cultivating sacred land already before 480, likely after multi-year hostilities against locals.

**Serpent Column (ML 27):** after Plataea, gold tripod on bronze serpents listed **31 allies** — Hall’s standing document that only a minority of poleis resisted Persia. Delphi also open to Lydian kings as aristocratic partners before “barbarian” ideology hardened post-480.
