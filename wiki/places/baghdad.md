# Baghdad (Abbasid Foundation)

## Overview

**Baghdad** was founded in **762 CE** by the Abbasid Caliph **al-Mansur** as a
deliberate imperial capital — the paradigm of a **city as a creation**, the theme
of Part V of [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] (Ch. 19, Françoise Micheau).
After 410 CE three cities dominated the Old World: Constantinople, Tang Xi'an, and
Abbasid Baghdad — "world cities" as much of the imagination as of politics.
Medieval Arabic geographers placed Baghdad at the **center of the world**:
"the man who has not seen Baghdad has not seen the world."

## A new capital for a new dynasty

The Abbasid Revolution (746–750) overthrew the Umayyads; the Abbasids, claiming
descent from Muhammad's clan (Hashim), could not rule from Umayyad Damascus. They
shifted to **Mesopotamia** — central between Iran, Syria, and Egypt, fed by the
Tigris–Euphrates canal system. Al-Mansur chose the Tigris-side site of a small
town called Baghdad in 762.

## The Round City

Al-Mansur built **the Round City** — a circular **palatial city**, said to be the
only round city in the world. Double walls with a moat and **four equidistant
gates** (Kufa, Basra, Khurasan, Syria) facing the empire's four quarters; at the
exact center stood the **Caliph's palace** (the Green Dome, 35 m high) and the
**Great Mosque**. The plan is a **cosmogram** — the ruler at the center of the
world (Persian "Mesopotamia at the center"; Greek *isonomia*; an "empty center"
of sovereignty, like Beijing's Forbidden City). The Round City housed only the
Caliph, his administration, guards, and trusted followers; the population lived in
planned quarters outside.

## A city known only through texts

Strikingly, **no material remains** of early Baghdad survive — unfired brick,
floods, fires, and the Mongol sack of 1258 erased it. Knowledge rests entirely on
**later written sources** (especially al-Ya'qubi's *Book of Countries*, 891),
written in the imperial ideology that made Baghdad a "city-world." The **foundation
account** is mythic: a site destined by God, a recovered prophecy, a cast
**horoscope** (analyzed to July 30, 762), and the appropriation of Sassanian
(Ctesiphon) and Solomonic (Wasit's gates) symbols — Baghdad as heir to past empires.

## Why this place mattered

Baghdad is the volume's clearest case of a city **willed into being** as an
instrument and image of empire — and a caution that some great early cities are
known only as **imagined and remembered** places, not excavated ones. It became
the center of the medieval Islamic world until 1258. See [[founded-cities]].
