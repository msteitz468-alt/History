# Monte Albán

## Overview

**Monte Albán** is the great hilltop capital of the [[zapotec|Zapotec]] in the
[[oaxaca-valley|Valley of Oaxaca]] — founded around **500 B.C.**, peaking in the Classic
(~A.D. 600; Blanton ~30,000 over 6.5 km²), and declining as a major urban center by about
**A.D. 750–900**. It is one of the primary anchors of
[[mesoamerican-state-formation|Mesoamerican state formation]], the type site for early
non-Maya writing and martial state ideology in highland Mexico, and the longest-lived
primate center of the southern highlands (~13 centuries of significance per Snow et al.).

## Founding (~500 B.C.): synoikism on a defensible mountain

Marcus and Flannery (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 8): a previously unoccupied mountain rising **400 m**
above the plain at the point where all three arms of the valley meet was chosen —
defensible and central for subjugation, though **waterless** and far from the best
farmland. Rapid urbanization: Blanton estimates **3,600–7,200** by 400 B.C.;
**10,000–20,400** by 250 B.C. Many valley-floor villages abandoned or shrank
([[san-jose-mogote|San José Mogote]], Huitzo, Tierras Largas, Fábrica San José); ceramic
and architectural continuities indicate valley-floor founders; demographic center of
gravity shifted south to Monte Albán.

No single prior community was large enough to stock early Monte Albán; earliest
settlement in **three discrete wards** → Blanton’s proposal of a **confederacy** of
villages from several areas of the valley; authors compare this to Greek **synoikism**.
Parallel (Mixteca Alta): **Monte Negro** (Tilantongo valley) as contemporary mountaintop
civic-ceremonial center — later abandoned while Yucuita urbanized.

Snow, Gonlin & Siegel (2020): confederation in response to rising warfare; Olmec trade
network still active at founding; landscaping of Main Plaza (outcrops leveled, low spots
filled — area of six modern football fields); final urban configuration ~300 CE; by 200
BCE unambiguously a primate city-state.

## Militarism: Danzantes and walls

**Building L** originally held a gallery of **>300 carved stones** of slain captives like
SJM Monument 3 — naked, awkward death postures, blood scrolls; early observers misnamed
them *danzantes* (“dancers”). Some set as staircase risers for treading. Combined with
mountaintop location and defensive walls **300–100 B.C.**, warfare was a founding
consideration; Monte Albán also became major religious capital with most temples in the
valley. Snow et al. cite ~**320** Danzantes figures at Monte Albán plus examples at
smaller valley centers (closed eyes, missing genitals, blood curlicues).

## Protoclassic state institutions (after ~200 B.C.)

Main Plaza (**300 × 150 m**) superimposed on earlier Late Formative structures; Blanton
population **10,000–19,000** (200 B.C.–A.D. 100). New institutions mark anthropological
“state” level:

- **Kingship** — governmental palace (habitation + audience halls, corvée labor); royal
  tombs (later multi-ton lintels, polychrome murals); royal ancestor worship via funerary
  urns (Cociyo/Lightning masks; “Lord 1 Tiger” on commemorative beakers)
- **State religion** — standardized **two-room temples** (often E–W paired), outer less
  sacred / inner for *bigana* priests; columns from baldcypress to stone; Spanish sources
  later list sacrifices of quail, turkeys, infants, adult slaves, combat prisoners
- **Ballcourt** — Roman-numeral-I plan, ~41 m N–S, no Maya-style stone rings (see
  [[mesoamerican-ball-game]])
- **Defensive 3 km wall** N/NW (300–100 B.C.) also dammed a reservoir ~2.25 ha / up to
  **67,500 m³** water — authors read preparedness as **offensive**

## Building J conquests and early imperialism

Building J yielded **>40 “conquest slabs”** (Caso): “hill” place sign + place glyphs +
often upside-down “conquered” head with regional headdress. Marcus proposes six
identifiable provinces: Ocelotepec, Tututepec, **Cuicatlán**, Miahuatlán, Sosola,
Chiltepec — subject territory radius **~80–150 km** from Monte Albán. Best documented:
**Cuicatlán** — Redmond & Spencer show Zapotec military takeover ~**200–100 B.C.**,
15 ha fortress with defensive walls and Monte Albán-style tombs; canyon 1,000 m lower
grows tropical crops by irrigation; invaders increased production with aqueducts.

## Settlement hierarchy (Protoclassic)

Surveys (Kowalewski, Blanton, Feinman, Finsten, Nicholas et al.): capital Monte Albán
**>1 km²** with greatest public-building diversity; **4–5 secondary centers** (SJM
60–65 ha with palace, ballcourt, up to ~10 temples); tertiary centers (Fábrica San José,
Tomaltepec) 5–8 ha with one temple, no ballcourt/palace; base of **>400 sites** 1–3 ha
without public buildings. *contrasts_with:* multipolar Mixtec urbanism (no single city
integrated as large a region).

## Classic peak vs Teotihuacan

Peak ~**A.D. 600**: largest southern highland urban center — **6.5 km²**, Blanton
~**30,000**; [[teotihuacan]] ~350 km north — 20 km², Millon/Cowgill 125,000–200,000;
Millon’s “**special relationship**.” Monte Albán founded earlier but never as large;
**not** on a grid — unplanned growth over hills; Main Plaza least accessible in elastic
terms vs Teotihuacan cruciform plan. Craft: Teotihuacan drew rural artisans into
compounds; Classic Zapotec **did not** concentrate countryside artisans in the capital —
many rural villages retained kilns/craft evidence.

**Summary contrast (Marcus & Flannery):** Monte Albán = controlled-access, fortified,
religious and regal administrative capital on a mountaintop; Teotihuacan = planned,
accessible, unfortified, administrative, religious, and **commercial** city on an
irrigated plain.

### Zapotec barrio and diplomatic monuments

1–2 ha residential ward on Teotihuacan’s western outskirts: Zapotec colony — adobe
compound follows Teotihuacan wall orientation but Monte Albán-style pottery; Zapotec
funerary urns; masonry tomb jamb with calendric name **9 Earthquake**. Intermediate-
status group (perhaps trade middlemen). **No reciprocal Teotihuacan enclave** found at
Monte Albán.

Early Classic monuments: militaristic themes continue, but five show peaceful
ambassadorial relations. **South Platform** corner stones: eight persons in Teotihuacan
headdresses leave Teotihuacan-style temples, arrive at “Hill of 1 Jaguar,” met by
Zapotec-headdressed lord; carry copal bags; C. Millon-type high-ranking diplomat
headdresses — no weapons. **Lápida de Bazán** (Mound X): Teotihuacan visitor meets
jaguar-suited Zapotec lord; narrative historical-political text; possible diplomatic
agreement maintaining social distance, tribute boundaries, and special relationship.

Cowgill (CHNPA Teotihuacan chapter, citing Marcus 1983): Monte Albán state clearly
**independent** of Teotihuacan; surviving monuments represent interactions between
emissaries who seem roughly equals, rather than celebrating victories of either over the
other. Oaxaca-related ceramics in Teotihuacan’s western enclave by end of A.D. 150–300
period (Cowgill ceramics discussion).

Snow et al.: ~500 CE urban expansion — at least ten more monumental compounds; tribute
from communities across Oaxaca; obsidian, mica, ceramics exchange; governor-rulers also
priests.

## Decline (A.D. 600–900) and afterlife

A.D. 600–900: Teotihuacan declines; “almost as if by chain reaction” Monte Albán and
other great cities follow. Decline **gradual**: by **750** Main Plaza buildings
crumbling, terraces abandoned, elite relocation to kin elsewhere; still used for
important burials (Early Postclassic **≥40 tombs**; some Tohil Plumbate from Maya area).
Blanton: while Teotihuacan was expansionist, Oaxaca autonomy preserved by large Monte
Albán; as threat declined, rivals rose — **Jalieza** surpassed Monte Albán in the Early
Postclassic, siphoning tribute/manpower. Former secondary centers (Zaachila,
[[mitla|Mitla]], Cuilapan, Macuilxóchitl, Teotitlán, Yegüih, etc.) became regional head
towns lasting to Spanish contact. Classic monopoly on hieroglyphic writing broke;
secondary centers produced small **genealogical registers** for tomb antechambers.

Late Postclassic: some [[mixtec|Mixtec]] presence near the base of the mountain (Sa’a
Yucu / possibly Kowalewski C-V-132); Mixteca-Puebla style materials in elite contexts;
valley has 2,455 Late Postclassic sites — more than double Early Classic count.

## Why it mattered

Commanding the junction of the valley’s three arms, Monte Albán concentrated elite
residence, public architecture, early writing, and carved conquest ideology. It is the
clearest highland case of **primary state formation** with early imperialism (Cuicatlán),
peer diplomacy with Teotihuacan, and a millennium-scale urban arc — foundational for
[[mesoamerican-state-formation]] and the sister-culture debate.

## Evans & Webster 2001 (Blanton, Zeitlin, others)

**Disembedded capital and phase demography (Blanton, “Monte Albán”).** Political and
cultural center of the Zapotec state from origin c. **500 B.C.** to A.D. **700**; among
the most thoroughly studied Mesoamerican sites. Founded suddenly in Period I on
previously unoccupied hilltops (highest **400 m** above valley floor) central to the
Y-shaped valley’s three arms — a **“disembedded capital”** in a political neutral zone
integrating prior Rosario-phase (600–500 B.C.) autonomous chiefdoms. Early I (500–350
B.C.) population ~**5,000**; Danzantes gallery of >300 near-life-size carved captives;
Late I ~**16,000**; 20-km hinterland intensification (piedmont irrigation, mud-brick
housing, specialized production, market exchange, first *comales*/tortillas).

- **Period II (200 B.C.–A.D. 300):** local autonomy of valley centers rises; capital
  population falls to ~**14,500**; massive defensive wall **>2 km** on N/W slopes;
  rulers extend control ~**100 km** into Cañada de Cuicatlán — possibly compensating for
  weakened home-region control; Main Plaza leveled and expanded with new military
  gallery.
- **IIIA (A.D. 300–500):** capital ~**16,500**; southern arm reaches highest densities
  ever; Main Plaza formalized (South Platform etc.); stelae show rulers’ accessions,
  victories, and Teotihuacan diplomatic visitors; more centralized state involvement in
  regional economy.
- **IIIB (A.D. 500–700):** southern valley collapses; capital hinterland shrinks but
  city peaks near **25,000** on >2,000 terraces with barrio market plazas; Main Plaza
  reaches final monumental form (North Platform as rulers’ living/working area); craft
  specialization by barrio but not a major basic-commodity center; elite goods
  (shell-working) elevated vs valley.
- **End IIIB / Period IV:** Main Plaza construction ceases and buildings decay; Early
  Postclassic reduced, scattered occupation, new defensive walls — no longer major
  political center.
- **Period V Late Postclassic:** again demographically major (~**14,000**) as
  Mixtec-named **Sa’a Yucu** on lower slopes; Main Plaza deserted; Zapotec royal
  palaces at Zaachila south of the old city.

Clark (Formative entry): planned defensible city for a confederation government formed
by alliance of leaders of various chiefdoms; factors prompting the alliance remain
unknown; early state activity included conquest of neighboring chiefdoms as tributaries.
Santley (Classic): conquest state dominating most of the southern highlands; craft-
production center (ceramics, shell, stone) with regional distribution. **Atzompa**:
important distinct barrio with its own political and administrative functions.

**Zeitlin regional synthesis.** Within a century of founding Monte Albán replaced San
José Mogote as principal settlement (~5,000). By end of Monte Albán I (200–100 B.C.)
valley population ~**40,000**, nearly half at densely urban Monte Albán, with multitiered
subsidiary hierarchy. Monte Albán II: stucco-paved Main Plaza, first of seven I-shaped
ball courts, defensive wall complete; ~**19%** valley population decline attributed to
outward colonization; ~50 Building J “conquest slabs.” **Debate:** some archaeologists
deny an imperialistic Zapotec state at 200 B.C.–A.D. 300, placing state formation only
in the Classic and reading Monte Albán II conflict as localized chiefdom strife. Early
Classic: wary Monte Albán–Teotihuacan relationship (Zapotec barrio at Teotihuacan;
Teotihuacan dignitaries on Monte Albán monuments). By end of Monte Albán III ~**25,000**
of valley’s **80,000** at the capital; Main Plaza access restricted to upper class. From
A.D. 750 (IV) capital deteriorates — population <4,000; Jalieza largest at 16,000;
collapse partly linked to concurrent Teotihuacan disintegration. **Tomb 7**: reused
Classic Zapotec vault for a Mixtec ruler with sacrificed servants and immense gold,
silver, amber, coral, pearl, turquoise, and carved jaguar-bone goods.

**Mortuary (Urcid).** At the A.D. 600–800 height, under-house tombs for household-head
generations with continuous ancestor rituals — core of Zapotec notions of death (detail
on [[zapotec]]).

## Historiography

Alfonso Caso’s 1930s excavations; Building J decipherment tradition (Caso; Marcus place-
glyph proposals); Blanton Mapping Project and valley surveys; Marcus & Flannery cultural-
evolutionary synthesis; Snow et al. (2020) accessible overview; independence from
Teotihuacan stressed by Marcus 1983 and Cowgill. Founding-as-synoikism and “offensive”
reading of the reservoir wall are interpretive positions.

**Evans & Webster 2001.** Blanton’s signed Monte Albán entry is the densest single
phase-by-phase demographic and institutional synthesis; Zeitlin’s Oaxaca–Tehuantepec
article supplies the valley-wide population frame and the explicit early-imperialism
debate; Urcid, Webster, and Clark fill mortuary, warfare, and confederation angles.
