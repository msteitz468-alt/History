# Thessalonike

**Thessalonike** (modern Thessaloniki) was the **second city of the
[[byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]]** throughout the medieval period, a great port and
economic hub on the Thermaic Gulf at the Aegean terminus of the **Via Egnatia** (the Roman
road from Dyrrachium). This page records its role in the
[[laiou-morrisson-byzantine-economy-2007|economic history]] of Byzantium; its wider
political and ecclesiastical history is only sketched here.

## Economic Role

- **A surviving city through the crisis.** With a population of ~100,000 in the early sixth
  century, Thessalonike was among the empire's largest cities and one of the few that
  survived the seventh–eighth-century contraction intact, continuing to trade and to
  interact (even in barter) with the surrounding Slavic settlements — a relationship
  documented in the *Miracles of Saint Demetrios*. Salt was produced at scale in the nearby
  Thermaic bay.
- **The fair of Saint Demetrios.** Its great annual *panegyris* (26 October) drew merchants
  from Boeotia and the Peloponnese (textiles, yarns), Italy, Egypt, Syria, Spain and the
  Black Sea, and doubled as a cattle fair — vividly satirised in the twelfth-century
  *Timarion*.
- **A provincial mint** was active in the ninth century (resumed under Theophilos).
- **Late-medieval hub.** With ~40,000 inhabitants in 1423, it remained a major
  redistribution centre for Western woollen cloth and a site of pottery and cloth
  production. During the great civil war of **1341–54** its seamen, merchants and bankers
  contested aristocratic control of the city (the "Zealot" episode), which Laiou and
  Morrisson compare to contemporary Western urban revolts (Genoa, Florence, the Flemish
  cities).

## Significance

Thessalonike exemplifies the resilience of the Byzantine provincial city: administrative
centre, fortified strongpoint, ecclesiastical metropolis, and — through its fair, its port
and its industries — a genuine node of production and exchange, second only to
[[constantinople|Constantinople]] itself. Its passing under Venetian and then Ottoman rule
(1423–1430) marked the near-end of Byzantine urban economic life outside the capital.
