# East Asia in the Modern Era

## Overview

Mark Selden's chapter in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] gives "**pride of place to
regional over national and local dynamics**," framing **East Asia as a world region**
(China at its core, embracing Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and reaching into Inner and
Southeast Asia) whose post-1970 resurgence is "a phenomenon without precedent in
world history in its scale, speed of advance, and regional character." Following Bruce
Cumings and Takeshi Hamashita, Selden argues that country-by-country analysis "misses,
through a fallacy of disaggregation, the fundamental unity and integrity of the
regional effort." This is the empirical heartland of the
[[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] — and of its **partial reversal**.

## Three Epochs (Selden's frame)

1. **c. 1750 – early 19th century — parity or primacy.** With the **China-centered
   tributary-trade system** as its institutional expression and a shared written
   language, Confucianism, and political institutions across China, Japan, Korea, and
   Vietnam, East Asia "rivaled, and in certain respects surpassed, Europe as a center
   of power, population, and perhaps prosperity," enjoying protracted peace (even as
   the Qing expanded deep into Inner Asia). The crucial eighteenth-century trajectory
   "was not from tribute to treaties but **from tribute to trade**."
2. **Early 19th century – 1970 — subordination and decline.** Western (then American
   and Japanese) imperial expansion, the Opium Wars, the collapse of the Qing and the
   tributary order, civil and international wars, and successive Chinese revolutions
   subordinated much of the region — the period in which, per Pomeranz, a single
   hegemonic European "core" and an East Asian periphery emerged (the
   [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]]). Yet new regional bonds also formed
   (overseas Chinese capital, Japan's Asia-centered empire) that seeded later
   resurgence.
3. **1970 – present — resurgence.** Between 1970 and 1975 the divisions of the "era of
   war" and the capitalist/socialist rift closed; **US–China accommodation** (early
   1970s) and China's turn toward state capitalism and the market ushered in the
   rapid interpenetration of regional economies and the **shift of world
   manufacturing from Europe and North America to East Asia** (Japan, the "tiger"
   economies, then China). By the new millennium the most dynamic bonds were
   intra-regional. See [[great-acceleration]], [[contemporary]].

## Why This Region Mattered

East Asia's modern arc is the strongest single case for the volume's central
revisionist claim: the [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] was **late and
contingent**, not the product of European exceptionalism, "given East Asia's earlier
robust political economy" (Pomeranz, Wong). Its post-1970 resurgence — the labor-
intensive [[industrialization]] path Sugihara describes — is the leading edge of the
divergence's reversal and the recentering of the global economy.

## How Its Significance Changed

From the demographic and economic center of the early-modern world, through a century
and a half of subordination, to the most dynamic region of the contemporary world
economy — Selden's point is that this is **one regional process**, not the sum of
national stories, and that contemporary borders "for the most part have served as hot
zones facilitating intensified exchange" rather than divides.

## Historiography

Selden's world-regional approach explicitly breaks with three schools: the
**Fairbank** "China's response to the West" paradigm (and Landes/Rostow modernization
theory); **essentialist** narratives of inherent Western superiority; and **Marxist**
"sprouts of capitalism" framings of feudal Asia. It aligns with the Hamashita–Wong–
Pomeranz–Sugihara revisionism. Selden also "deconstructs mythologies" accompanying the
growth story and flags unresolved conflicts (the Korean War's legacy, Japanese-empire
memory disputes, the South and East China Sea tensions) that threaten regional order.
