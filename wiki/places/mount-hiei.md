# Mount Hiei (Enryakuji)

Mount Hiei (Hieizan) is a mountain approximately 848 meters high on the border of Omi Province and Yamashiro Province (present-day Shiga and Kyoto prefectures), northeast of the capital Heian-kyo (Kyoto). It is the site of **Enryakuji**, the great Tendai monastic complex founded by [[saicho|Saicho]] (Dengyo Daishi) in 785 and the most powerful religious institution in Heian and medieval Japan. Throughout the [[heian-period|Heian period]], Hiei functioned simultaneously as a center of Buddhist learning, an esoteric ritual powerhouse serving the imperial court, a launching site for the Pure Land movement, and — ultimately — a heavily armed military establishment capable of terrorizing the capital. Source: [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]].

## Foundation

Saicho moved to Hiei immediately after his full ordination at Todaiji in 785, "where he devoted himself to meditation, worship, and especially the study of scripture. His abrupt move to Hiei was in keeping with the practice of many monks of the time, who sought to purify themselves and perhaps even acquire supernatural powers by undergoing austerities in the mountains." The choice of Hiei also reflected "disenchantment with the corruption that was infecting the great monasteries of Nara." From this small hermitage — which received a provincial tax subsidy after Saicho's appointment as imperial court chaplain in 797 — grew the most extensive monastic complex in Japan.

The monastery was named **Enryakuji** by imperial decree in 823, the year after Saicho's death, in memory of Emperor Kammu whose reign era (Enryaku) had been its sponsoring period. The long-sought ordination hall (*kaidan'in*) was completed in 827 with a grant of 90,000 sheaves of rice for construction costs.

## Physical Organization: The Santo (Three Pagodas)

Hiei is traditionally divided into three geographic areas known as the **Santo** (Three Pagodas):

- **Toto (Eastern Pagoda)**: The original area developed by Saicho. Contains **Kompon Chudo** (Fundamental Central Hall), the main hall of Enryakuji; the Lecture Hall (*Kodo*); the Ordination Hall (*kaidan'in*); and the first Jogyodo (Pure Land meditation hall, built by Ennin in 848). The name "Toto" refers to the Great Pagoda in this precinct.

- **Saito (Western Pagoda)**: Centers around **Shakado**, built by Saicho's disciple Encho. The second Jogyodo was built here with imperial sponsorship in 893.

- **Yokawa**: Lies to the north of Toto and Saito. First settled by Ennin, who withdrew here in 831 to undertake spiritual exercises. Ennin established **Shuryogon'in** as the central Yokawa cloister. Yokawa fell into decline during Enchin's incumbency but was revived by Ryogen (912–985), who made it his initial residence on arriving at Hiei in 949, and whose Fujiwara patron Morosuke sponsored construction there. The third Jogyodo was built at Yokawa by Ryogen in 968. The Eshin'in at Yokawa was the seclusion retreat of **Genshin** (942–1017), author of the *Ojo yoshu*.

## Strategic and Political Significance

Hiei's location was not accidental. Situated to the northeast of the Heian-kyo palace — the direction from which, according to Chinese cosmology, evil forces entered — the mountain was regarded as a protective barrier for the capital. Seven major shrines on Hiei were believed to protect the capital from natural disasters and epidemics. This gave Hiei monks an extraordinary instrument of political intimidation: when their demands went unmet, monks could carry the *mikoshi* (sacred palanquins) from these shrines down into Kyoto, striking terror into citizens who feared incurring divine wrath.

The famous saying of Fujiwara no Yorimichi — that of the things he could not control, three were unstoppable: the flooding of the Kamo River, the gambling of sugoroku dice, and the monks of Hiei (yama-koshi) — captures Hiei's power in the later Heian period. The monastery's fighting men (*akuso*, wicked monks, precursors to the *sohei* monk warriors) descended repeatedly on the capital carrying weapons and harassing officials.

## Court Patronage and Imperial Cloisters

From the mid-ninth century, successive emperors founded personal cloisters on Hiei:
- Emperor Montoku (r. 850–858): built Sojiin and Shioin
- Emperor Suzaku (r. 930–946): built Emmyoin
- Emperor Kazan (r. 984–986): built Joryoin
- Emperor Go-Reizei (r. 1045–1068): built Jissoin

Gifts of land by ex-emperors and princes for specific annual rituals became standard after the 860s. By 972 Hiei controlled estates in Omi, Wakasa, Yamashiro, and Izumi. Hiei also extended influence across the provinces by persuading the government to designate provincial recognized temples (*jogakuji*) as "Tendai betsuin" (branch temples), administered only by Enryakuji-ordained monks. By the end of the ninth century Tendai betsuin existed in Mutsu, Kozuke, Harima, Shinano, and Kaga.

## Ryogen's Restoration

Enryakuji was devastated by fires in 935 (which destroyed Kompon Chudo) and 966 (which destroyed thirty more buildings including the Sojiin, Lecture Hall, Shioin, Emmyoin, and Jogyodo). Abbot Ryogen (966–984), backed by the Fujiwara — his patron Morosuke had become devoted after Ryogen's prayers were credited with the birth of the future Emperor Reizei (950) — orchestrated a comprehensive reconstruction:

- 967: Hokkedo and Jogyodo completed
- 972: Lecture Hall and four other buildings reconstructed
- 979: Shakado, Sutra Repository, Jeweled Pagoda (*Hoto*) finished
- 980: Kompon Chudo formally consecrated in a ceremony attended by Emperor En'yu, Regent Fujiwara no Yoritada, and the Ministers of Left and Right
- 984: Ornate Hodoin completed with gold fittings supplied by the governor of Mutsu

Ryogen also instituted formal doctrinal debates (*kogaku ryugi*, "erudite disputations") on the *Hokekyo* at the annual Saicho memorial services, awarding the academic rank of *tokugô* (scholar) to successful disputants. His twenty-six-article disciplinary code (970) sought to curb clerical abuses — but also reveals how militarized the community had become, prohibiting monks from carrying weapons, concealing faces, inflicting corporal punishment, and violently disrupting services.

## Hiei and the Sammon-Jimon Conflict

After monks of the Enchin lineage were expelled from Hiei in 993 and established themselves permanently at Onjoji (Miidera), Hiei became the permanent stronghold of the **Sammonha** (Mountain Branch) of the [[tendai|Tendai school]] — the Ennin lineage controlling the mountain. The two Tendai monasteries clashed violently throughout the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Hiei burned Onjoji four times (1074, 1081, 1121, 1140). In one incident (1081), "a force of several thousand armed monks and laymen assembled by Hiei from its subordinate temples and shrines" totally devastated Onjoji's fifteen imperially sponsored temples, seventy-nine halls, fifteen sutra repositories, and many other structures.

Hiei also waged ongoing conflict with the Kofukuji (Nara), which competed for provincial temple affiliates and at one point proposed that Enryakuji be made a subsidiary temple of Kofukuji. Hiei's response was to burn Kiyomizudera (1165), provoking a Kofukuji rampage through Kyoto trying to burn all Hiei-affiliated temples.

## Legacy

Enryakuji remained the dominant religious institution in Japan through the medieval period — until Oda Nobunaga's comprehensive destruction of the complex in 1571 (one of the most violent acts of the Sengoku period). The monastery was later rebuilt by Toyotomi Hideyoshi and Tokugawa Ieyasu. Hiei continues today as an active Tendai center; Enryakuji was designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1994 as part of the "Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto."

The *Cambridge History of Japan* characterizes Hiei's significance not only in religious terms but as a political actor capable of projecting armed force into the capital, a landlord controlling estates across central Japan, and a training ground that produced the founders of the major Kamakura Buddhist schools — Honen, Shinran, Eisai, Dogen, and Nichiren all trained on Hiei before breaking away.
