# Memphis (Ancient Egypt)

## Overview

**Memphis** was ancient Egypt's principal city and royal residence from the
beginning of the Dynastic period (~3000 BCE). It is the anchor case for
[[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]]'s treatment of Egyptian urbanism (Ch. 2,
John Baines), which argues that early Egyptian "cities" were unusually
**dispersed and ceremonial** — better understood as a *region* of monuments,
residences, and necropoleis bound together by movement along the Nile than as a
single dense nucleated settlement. Memphis is therefore the volume's prime
example of a city defined by **monumentality and the performance of power**
rather than by Childe's demographic criteria (see [[urbanism]]).

## Geographic description

Sited south of the apex of the Nile Delta near modern Cairo, on the floodplain
beside the river with its necropoleis (Saqqara, Giza, Abusir, Dahshur, Helwan,
Abu Rawash) strung for ~80 km along the low desert escarpment. The Nile
floodplain was a difficult location — the shifting main channel destroyed
settlement traces and the annual inundation submerged the plain — so the
earliest urban core was modest (under ~1 km²) and the river was the connective
artery and stage.

## History of control and settlement

- **Predynastic forerunners** — Naqada, Abydos, and Hierakonpolis in the
  southern Nile Valley show nucleation and social differentiation from ~3700 BCE.
  Memphis was their urban successor, expanded under the Abydos rulers who became
  kings of all Egypt at unification (~3100 BCE).
- **Foundation (CAH XI).** Herodotus credits **Menes** ([[narmer|Narmer]]) with
  founding the city — diverting the Nile by a dyke to reclaim the site — together
  with its temple of Ptah. Its original name was the **"White Wall"** (*Inebu-hedj*),
  either commemorating the southern conqueror's white crown or describing its
  white-plastered mudbrick. Sited at the junction of the Two Lands, it was from
  the first the **capital and coronation stage** of [[early-dynastic-egypt|Early
  Dynastic Egypt]]: the coronation rites "Union of the Two Lands" and "Procession
  around the Wall" were performed within it. The name "Memphis" itself derives
  later from the pyramid-town of Pepi I (*Men-nefer*) at South Saqqara.
- **[[old-kingdom-egypt|Old Kingdom]] (~2686–2160 BCE)** — the dispersed
  ceremonial mode at its height: colossal royal mortuary monuments
  ([[djoser|Djoser's]] Step Pyramid; the Giza pyramids of Khufu, Khafre,
  Menkaure) dominate, while the inhabited core remains small and elites display
  *rural* values. Power is enacted through movement, festival processions,
  funerals, and the controlled interplay of display and concealment. Memphis
  remained the administrative capital until the
  [[fall-of-old-kingdom-2160bce|collapse into the First Intermediate Period]].
- **Middle Kingdom** — the nearby residence Itjtawy; regional elites (e.g.
  Djehutihotep at Deir el-Bersha) adopt royal modes of display.
- **New Kingdom (~1540–1070 BCE)** — Memphis and Thebes become genuinely large
  floodplain cities focused on temple complexes; ceremonial avenues, harbor
  basins, and processional ways are laid out. Egypt becomes an imperial power.

## Related Egyptian urban centers (folded in)

- **Thebes / Karnak / Luxor** — the religious capital of the second millennium;
  the Opet festival processions between Karnak and Luxor reaffirmed kingship.
- **Akhetaten / el-Amarna** — Akhenaten's short-lived (~20-year) purpose-built
  capital (~1350 BCE), laid out along the river with broad roads for chariot
  processions; abandoned when traditional religion was restored.
- **Piramesse and Tanis** — Ramesside and Third Intermediate Period delta
  capitals; the line culminates in Alexandria (332 BCE).

## Memphis as the mythic place of unification (Kemp)

Barry Kemp ([[kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy of a Civilization*]], Ch. 2) notes
that the **Memphite Theology** of the Shabaka Stone made Memphis the *theological* locus of
[[unification-of-egypt-3100bce|unification]]: Horus "united the Two Lands in the Nome of the
Wall," and Horus and Seth were "pacified and united... in the House of Ptah, the 'Balance of
the Two Lands.'" Kemp argues this composition is **relatively late** (perhaps of Shabaka's
own time, c. 721–707 BCE) in deliberately archaic language, not an Old Kingdom original — a
piece of the [[egyptian-creation-myth-and-cosmogony|invented founding myth]]. He also
deflates Menes: Herodotus has him found the city, but Manetho says only that he "made a
foreign expedition and won renown, but was carried off by a hippopotamus" — little legendary
tradition attached to the supposed unifier beyond his place at the head of the king-lists.

## Why this place mattered

Memphis shows that urban form is culturally specific: Egypt's early ruling
"city" was a landscape of dispersed monuments and a mobile court, not a dense
grid. Its significance was as the **stage for the performance of royal and
divine power** — a counter-example to the Mesopotamian template that
[[urbanism|defines]] the city demographically. By the New Kingdom Egyptian
cities converged toward Near Eastern forms.
