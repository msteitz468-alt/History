## Geographic Description

Montserrat is a small volcanic island in the northern Lesser Antilles, approximately 16 km long and 11 km wide. Its landscape is dominated by active and dormant volcanic peaks; one volcano (the Soufrière Hills) underwent major eruptions beginning in 1995, burying the southern part of the island — including the area around the main archaeological site at Trants — under metres of volcanic ash and forcing the permanent evacuation of the south. The island's windward (eastern) coast, where the Trants site was located, receives strong easterly trade winds and under 1,000 mm of rainfall annually, supporting predominantly low scrub vegetation. The Farm River on the eastern coast provided a freshwater source adjacent to the site.

## Settlement History

### Trants Site (c. 400 BCE–AD 500)

The Trants site is one of the earliest and most important Ceramic Age sites in the entire Caribbean. Located on the eastern windward shore adjacent to what was the island's airport, it was investigated in the 1990s by David Watters and James B. Petersen before being buried by volcanic ash from recent eruptions.

The site was a large oval village approximately 275 m (north–south) × 225 m, with houses and middens arranged around a central plaza. Soil analyses confirmed this layout, and the community plan was maintained with remarkable continuity for nearly a millennium of continuous occupation (Petersen 1996). Primary historic use of the land as pasture contributed to the excellent stratigraphic integrity and preservation prior to the volcanic ash burial.

**Pottery:** Classified as Saladoid, with some Huecoid elements. Principal decorative modes include white-on-red (WOR), black-on-red, and white-and-black-on-red painting, along with zoned incision and zoned-incised crosshatching (ZIC). Only 7.5% of the excavated pottery sample was decorated (Watters and Petersen 1999).

**Lapidary industry:** A well-developed lapidary industry utilised non-local exotic stones including diorite, carnelian, rock crystal, amethyst, aventurine, turquoise, jade-group minerals, and serpentine. The distribution of finished vs. unfinished beads reveals an active inter-island exchange system: carnelian bead production waste is abundant at Trants (65% of debitage, 88% of blanks) but finished carnelian beads are rare (1.1%); conversely, finished amethyst beads are common at Trants while amethyst debitage is rare. At Pearls (Grenada), exactly the opposite pattern appears — demonstrating that Trants and Pearls were exchange partners, with each site completing the other's production process (Watters and Scaglion 1994).

**Subsistence:** Faunal samples were split roughly equally between land animals (primarily oryzomyine rodents) and reef fish, typical of early Ceramic Age sites in the northern Lesser Antilles.

The site is characteristic of Early Ceramic Age settlements in the northern Lesser Antilles alongside Hope Estate (St. Martin), Indian Creek (Antigua), and Tecla (Puerto Rico).

## Significance

Montserrat's Trants site is archaeologically significant for several reasons:

1. **Chronological anchor:** Its early dates (before 400 BCE) were among the evidence that overturned the stepping-stone migration model by demonstrating that northern Lesser Antillean islands were settled centuries before the Windward Islands closer to the mainland.

2. **Exchange network evidence:** The complementary bead production/finishing distribution between Trants and Pearls (Grenada) provides the clearest material evidence for pan-Caribbean inter-island exchange networks during the Early Ceramic Age.

3. **Community plan continuity:** Nearly 900 years of continuous occupation with a stable oval village plan demonstrates the persistence and stability of Saladoid community organisation, including the central plaza as the locus of communal activity.

4. **Irreplaceability:** The site is now buried under volcanic ash from the Soufrière Hills eruptions of 1995–present, making excavated data from the Watters-Petersen investigations the primary record. Future investigation will require removal of metres of tephra.

## Historiography

Archaeological investigation of Trants by Watters and Petersen in the 1990s was prompted by planned airport expansion and conducted under time constraints. The eruptions of Soufrière Hills beginning 1995 subsequently buried the site, destroying most surface-accessible material and transforming Montserrat into a partly depopulated island with restricted access to its southern half. Pre-eruption data therefore represent an irreplaceable baseline.
