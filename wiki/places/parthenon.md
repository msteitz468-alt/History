# The Parthenon and the Periclean Acropolis

## Overview

The **Parthenon** — the great Doric temple of Athena built on the **Athenian Acropolis**
between **447 and 432 BCE** — is the central monument of the Periclean building program and
the emblem of High Classical Greek art. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Chs. 6 and 8b–8c,
D. M. Lewis and R. E. Wycherley) treats the program both as a religious dedication and as
"a visible symbol of the achievements and the power of the city" — funded, as the building
accounts confirm, from the appropriated tribute of the [[delian-league|empire]].

## After the Persian sack

When the Persians withdrew in 479 they left Athens "mostly in ruins." Rebuilding came in
phases: first **housing and defence** (the hasty Themistoclean circuit, incorporating
gravestones; the fortification of Piraeus; the mid-century **Long Walls**), then modest
public buildings in the Agora (the **Tholos**, the **Stoa Poikile** with its Marathon
mural). Major temple-building waited until after the **[[peace-of-callias-449bce|Peace of
Callias]]** (449), when the end of the Persian war freed both the political justification and
the surplus tribute. Damaged temple-members built into the Acropolis north wall were left as
a deliberate **memorial of the destruction**.

## The building program

Under **[[pericles]]** and the overseer **Phidias**, with the architects **Ictinus and
Callicrates**, the Acropolis was crowned by three great structures:

- **The Parthenon** (447–432). Octastyle Doric in **Pentelic marble**, reusing the
  foundations of an earlier, Persian-interrupted temple. Famous for its **refinements** (the
  upward curve of horizontal lines, the inward tilt and *entasis* of columns, thickened
  corner columns) and its **9:4 *symmetria***. Its **sculptural program** — metopes, the
  Ionic **frieze** (the Panathenaic procession, bestowing "divine attributes on the ordinary
  Athenian citizen"), and pediments — bore the stamp of Phidias and embodied the recurring
  theme of **civilization over barbarism** (Lapiths/Centaurs, Greeks/Amazons, Gods/Giants).
- **Phidias' chryselephantine Athena Parthenos** (dedicated 438/7), of gold and ivory,
  costing 700–1,000 talents; major parts of Athena's cult were boldly transferred to the new
  statue, so that, in Lewis's phrase, "to say the Athenians built the Parthenon to worship
  themselves would be an exaggeration, but not a great one."
- **The Propylaea** (Mnesicles, 430s) — the monumental gateway, never finished.
- **The Erechtheum** (the last two decades) — the highly irregular Ionic shrine housing the
  ancient olive-wood Athena Polias and a reliquary of holy tokens (the sacred olive, the
  salt spring, the tombs of early kings), with the **Caryatid porch**. Pollitt reads it as
  "a shrine to the same irrational forces that haunted Euripides in the *Bacchae*."

Other works of the program and its circle include the **Hephaesteum** overlooking the Agora,
the temple of **Athena Nike**, and temples at Sunium (Poseidon), Rhamnous (Nemesis), and
elsewhere in Attica.

## Tribute, politics, and meaning

The program was the focus of a **political struggle**. Pericles' critics, led by **Thucydides
son of Melesias** (ostracized 443), charged that he was "gilding and embellishing the city
like a wanton woman" with funds "compulsorily contributed to the war"; Pericles answered that
no account was owed the allies so long as they got the defence they paid for, and the city
might use the surplus for "an eternal glory." Lewis confirms the charge factually: the funds
came from the **Treasurers of Athena**, into whom the [[delian-league|League]] reserves
transferred from Delos had been merged. The Parthenon was thus from the first **part victory
dedication, part imperial statement, part civic self-worship** — Athens as, in Pericles'
phrase, "the education of Greece." Still under construction at the Erechtheum, the program
outlasted the peace and reached into the years of the [[peloponnesian-war|war]].
