## Geographic Description

Archipelago in central Polynesia divided politically between Western Samoa (independent since 1962, renamed Samoa 1997) and American Samoa (US territory). The main islands are Upolu (where Apia, the capital, is located) and Savai'i in Western Samoa; Tutuila and the Manu'a group in American Samoa. The fa'amatai system — chiefly title (matai) governance with elaborate ceremonial protocols — was the durable political institution that outlasted German and New Zealand colonial rule.

## History of Control

### German Commercial Dominance (1860s–1900)

Samoa was "the focal point of German enterprise." The Deutsche Handels- und Plantagen-Gesellschaft (DHPG) "had land, but could not mobilise enough labour to dispense with Samoan-grown copra. Samoans would not work on plantations and Gilbertese (I-Kiribati, who recruited readily) were too few." German consul in Apia "regulated affairs to suit DHPG." Formal annexation of Western Samoa by Germany in 1900 made "little difference to DHPG dominance in framing and interpreting land and labour policies." Governor Solf supported corporations over small settlers. The first DHPG dividend was paid five years after operations began; a 1875 commission of inquiry into Gilbertese workers' petition found abuses but attributed the strike "to agitators."

### New Zealand Military and Civilian Rule (1914–1936)

Australian troops captured German Samoa in 1914. Civilian government took over in 1920. New Zealand "was less experienced and much less competent than their German predecessors." German plantations were retained as "Reparation Estates" (nationalisation) rather than sold to settlers, but this generated no development push for Samoans.

The **1918 influenza pandemic** was the defining trauma of modern Samoan history: "90 per cent of the people were infected, bringing life to a standstill. When it burned out, 30 per cent of adult men were dead, 22 per cent of adult women and 10 per cent of the children." American Samoa, blockaded by its commander, was spared. The contrast fed Samoan resentment of New Zealand administration.

General George Richardson's arrival (1923) initiated social engineering: compulsory tree-planting, restrictions on cricket and *malaga* (travelling-party ceremonies), proposed individualization of land tenure, and use of the Samoan Offenders Ordinance (1922) to revoke chiefly titles. This triggered the [[mau-movement]]. Richardson "banished two leaders, Afamasaga and Faumuina, to Apolima Island."

"Black Saturday" (28 December 1929): police opened fire on a Mau procession in Apia, killing eleven Samoans — "almost all of them high-ranking matai." Tupua Tamasese Lealofi died of wounds. The administration declared the Mau seditious; "soldiers terrorised the villages."

A New Zealand Labour government (elected 1936) shifted policy: the Mau was recognised as legitimate, the Samoan Offenders Ordinance repealed, and a new Fono of Faipule elected composed largely of Mau supporters.

### American Samoa

The US blockade commander excluded influenza in 1918. American Samoa did not develop a significant plantation economy and remained smaller and more isolated.

## Samoan Political Culture and the Fa'aSamoa

Samoans "drew strength from the conviction that fa'aSamoa — Samoan tradition — reflected their superiority, and that their system of government was better than that imposed by New Zealanders." The Fono of Faipule survived colonialism as a recognised body; the elaborate matai title system "successfully competed with" New Zealand governance. Ceremonial exchange (*malaga*, *'ie toga*) persisted despite colonial discouragement: "The Samoans' elaborate ceremonies for distributing *'ie toga* reaffirmed social relationships of central importance."

Of 9,300 adult Samoan men, 8,000 signed the 1928 Mau petition to the League of Nations. The demographic resilience was also notable: "numbers increased at 2.5 per cent per annum in the five years following the [1918] epidemic, and in the following ten years it averaged 3.3 per cent."

## Economic Significance

Copra was the primary export for most of the colonial period. Samoan village growers produced three-quarters of Western Samoa's copra, contrasting with the plantation model elsewhere. The Reparation Estates (former German properties managed by New Zealand) produced most plantation copra interwar, with Chinese indentured labourers on six-year contracts. The economic dependency on single-commodity export and foreign corporate management was a persistent structural problem.
