# New Zealand

**New Zealand (Aotearoa)** — two large temperate islands in the southwest Pacific — was the last
major landmass settled by humans and, for [[crosby-ecological-imperialism-1986|Alfred Crosby]], the
clearest single demonstration of the biological mechanics of European expansion.

## Settlement and significance

New Zealand was reached around **1250–1300 CE** by Polynesian voyagers — the southern terminus of
the great [[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement|Pacific voyaging]] expansion out of
[[polynesia|tropical Polynesia]] — whose descendants became the **Māori**. Isolated for roughly
five centuries, Māori built a dense, competitive society of fortified *pā* and (in the warmer
north) intensive horticulture, while the cold far south reverted toward foraging — the contrast
[[geographic-determinism|Diamond]] uses (Chathams Moriori vs. North Island Māori) as a natural
experiment in environment shaping society.

## A textbook "Neo-Europe"

Sustained European contact began with **Cook's voyages from 1769**; British annexation followed
the **Treaty of Waitangi (1840)**. New Zealand then ran through Crosby's full sequence in
compressed form: the arrival of the **[[portmanteau-biota|portmanteau biota]]** — European weeds,
grasses, livestock, rats, and rabbits that overran the islands' endemic biota — alongside
**[[virgin-soil-epidemics|virgin-soil epidemics]]** that drove a steep Māori population decline,
clearing the temperate grasslands for settler pastoralism. By the late nineteenth century the
islands had become one of the [[neo-europes|"Neo-Europes"]], an offshore temperate farm exporting
wool, meat, and dairy to Britain. The transformation was, in Crosby's reading, "more a matter of
biology than of military conquest."
