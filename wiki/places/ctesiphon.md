# Ctesiphon

## Geography and Control

A great city on the **Tigris** in central Mesopotamia, paired with neighbouring Seleucia.
It was a royal residence of the [[parthian-empire|Parthian]] king **Mithridates I** (r. c.
171–138 BCE) — alongside Seleucia and Ecbatana — and served as a principal Arsacid capital
throughout the period. Under the [[sasanian-empire|Sasanians]] (from 224 CE) it became one
of the largest and most cosmopolitan cities of late antiquity; **Khusro I** (r. 531–579)
built nearby the **Arch of Khusro**, reportedly the tallest structure in western Eurasia of
its time. It fell to Muslim forces after a long siege in **637 CE**.

## Why It Mattered

As the Parthian (and then Sasanian) seat of power on the Tigris, Ctesiphon anchored the
**western terminus zone** of the overland [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] in Mesopotamia. Its
fate tracks the great-power contest of the era: it was captured by Rome **at least three
times** — by **Trajan** (115–116 CE), by **Marcus Aurelius'** generals (163 CE, after which
the army withdrew carrying the [[antonine-plague-165|Antonine Plague]]), and by **Septimius
Severus** (197 CE). [[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin]] uses Ardashir I's
**coronation at Ctesiphon in 224 CE** to mark the Parthian–Sasanian transition.

## Significance

Ctesiphon's repeated sacking and rebuilding embodies the **Roman–Parthian/Sasanian
frontier struggle** along the Euphrates–Tigris that periodically disrupted the western land
routes — even as commerce remained a shared interest of both powers.
