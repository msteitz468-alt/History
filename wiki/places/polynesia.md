# Polynesia

**Polynesia** ("many islands") is the great triangle of the central and eastern Pacific bounded
by Hawai'i, [[new-zealand|New Zealand]] (Aotearoa) and Rapa Nui (Easter Island), with Tonga,
Samoa, Tahiti and the Society Islands, the Cook Islands and Tuvalu at its heart. A sub-region of
[[oceania|Oceania]], it is the most culturally and linguistically homogeneous part of the Pacific
— the product of a relatively rapid late dispersal out of the West Polynesian
(Tonga–Samoa–Fiji) homeland during the [[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement|voyaging expansion]].

In [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997|the Cambridge History of the Pacific Islanders]],
Polynesia carries much of the volume's narrative weight: its scholarship is the region's richest,
and Hawai'i, Maori New Zealand, Samoa and Tonga supplied many of the era's defining episodes —
chiefly state-formation, missionary conversion, the [[overthrow-of-hawaiian-monarchy-1893|overthrow
of the Hawaiian monarchy]], the Samoan Mau, and the late-twentieth-century cultural
"renaissance." West Polynesia (Tonga and Samoa) produced the region's most enduring indigenous
monarchies and chiefly (*matai*) systems.

## Significance

Polynesia is where the volume's central tensions are sharpest: between island agency and colonial
power, between "custom" and the modern state, and between the isolation thesis and the reality of
a deeply networked, voyaging "sea of islands."
