# Africa in the Modern Era

## Overview

Frederick Cooper opens his chapter in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] with the line
"**World history began in Africa**" — the cradle of the species, and (via Du Bois,
Cheikh Anta Diop) a continent whose deep past became "an argument for its liberation
in the twentieth century." Cooper's governing theme for the modern era is the
**"ambiguities of connections"**: Africa's history is one of engagement — not
isolation — but the new connections of the modern age were profoundly **uneven**,
mixing integration with marginalization. He insists on seeing Africans as agents
making their own lives, against two European "conceits": Africans as the "enslaveable
other" and Africans as passive victims (the latter, paradoxically, used to justify
the "civilizing" conquest).

## History of Control and Settlement

- **The slave trade after 1750.** Cooper grounds the era in the
  [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]], at its height around 1800. He stresses
  its origins in the **intersection of African and European networks** (Mandinka,
  "Atlantic creole" societies) and the political logic by which some rulers (Asante,
  the Aro) raided and sold captives *externally* rather than exploiting "their own"
  people in situ. At least 10 million Africans were shipped across the Atlantic, with
  large further mortality and a redistribution of population within the continent.
  Parallel **East African** (Indian Ocean, Zanzibar clove) and **trans-Saharan** trades
  expanded from the late eighteenth century, producing even higher insecurity.
- **Abolition and "legitimate" commerce.** Britain banned the trade in 1807 and
  patrolled the coasts, but Cuban and Brazilian demand kept it alive to mid-century;
  meanwhile **falling slave prices within Africa and rising European demand for
  tropical products (palm oil, cloves) increased agricultural slavery inside the
  continent** — abolition did not end African slavery.
- **Nineteenth-century state-building.** New polities arose: the **Zulu kingdom**
  under Shaka (the stabbing spear and age-regiments, c. 1818), whose shock waves (the
  *mfecane*) reached Tanzania; and a series of **Sahelian jihads** transforming West
  African politics. Cooper reads these as local innovation interacting with
  coastal/colonial pressures.
- **Colonial conquest and the partition.** Late-nineteenth-century
  [[imperialism|New Imperialism]] brought the scramble for Africa — technologically
  one-sided conquest, the Belgian Congo's ~5 million deaths, the Herero genocide (see
  [[genocide]]).
- **Decolonization.** The mid-twentieth-century wave (Bandung 1955; independence
  c. 1957–1975) made Africa central to global [[decolonization]] and Third World
  [[nationalism]] — though Cooper stresses forced labor and violence did not vanish
  with the colonizers' professed aims.

## Why This Region Mattered

Cooper's Africa is neither isolated nor merely acted-upon: it was bound into the world
through the slave trade, "legitimate" commerce, colonial extraction, and
decolonization, while Africans continually reshaped these connections. The
**unevenness** of integration — some kingdoms empowered, vast areas marginalized — is
the modern continent's defining structural feature.

## How Its Significance Changed

From a continent of diverse kingdoms and networks deeply (and traumatically) tied to
the Atlantic and Indian Ocean economies, through colonial partition, to a decolonized
world of nation states — Cooper's "ambiguities of connection" frame resists both the
isolationist and the victimhood narratives.

## Historiography

Cooper (a leading skeptic of "globalization" as an analytic — "What is the concept of
globalization good for?") foregrounds African agency and the construction of the
"African" past as itself a political act (Du Bois, Diop). The chapter is a corrective
to [[eurocentrism|Eurocentric]] framings and connects to the wiki's deep-time African
coverage ([[bantu-expansion]], [[early-african-food-production]], [[jenne-jeno]]).
