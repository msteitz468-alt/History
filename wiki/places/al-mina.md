# Al Mina

**Al Mina** ("the harbour" — its ancient Greek name is unknown) was the **trading
depot at the mouth of the Orontes** that was the single most important point of
contact between the Greek world and the Near East in the early Iron Age — the channel
through which eastern metals, ivories, textiles and, above all, the **alphabet** and
the **orientalizing** impulse reached the Aegean. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol.
III, Part 3]] (Ch. 36a, T. F. R. G. Braun) treats it.

## A trading station, not a colony

Al Mina lay in the neo-Hittite kingdom of **Pa(t)tin / Unqi** (Aramaic ʿAmq), at the
Mediterranean end of the shortest caravan route from Mesopotamia — whoever held the
lower Orontes also commanded the land traffic between Anatolia and the south.
Woolley's excavation (1936–9) found **ten levels of occupation, c. 825–301 BCE**.
From the start it had a **Greek element**, but it was not a *colony* (an
[[greek-colonization|apoikia]]): in the East the Greeks "settled only if they were
allowed to settle," unable to drive out the natives as they did in Sicily. Al Mina was
an **ἐνοίκισμα** — a settlement of Greeks *among* natives, comparable to the later
Greek quarter at Etruscan Gravisca — a warehouse-town of single-storey magazines round
courtyards, with gravel lanes and drains, no proper houses (the merchants probably
lived inland on Sabouni hill), and **no graves and no Greek inscriptions**.

## The Euboean phase and the orientalizing trade

The earliest Greek pottery at Al Mina is **Euboean (and Cycladic)** — pendent-
semicircle and concentric-circle cups — and its presence here *precedes* the
Euboeans' own western colony at [[pithecusae|Pithecusa/Ischia]]. This makes Al Mina
the eastern pole of the [[euboea|Euboean]] commercial reach: the metals of the
interior (Urartian, Tabalian and Assyrian bronze cauldrons, Cypriot copper, Levantine
silver and gold plate, purple-dyed cloth, ivory and trinkets) flowed out through it
to Greece, in exchange — partly — for **slaves** got by Greek raiding and piracy.
After a destruction **c. 700 BCE** (perhaps linked to the Cilician revolt Sennacherib
crushed) the site was rebuilt with a different Greek element — **Euboean pottery
disappears**, replaced by Rhodian, East Greek "Wild Goat," and much **Corinthian**
ware (probably carried by Aeginetan middlemen). A sixth-century hiatus may reflect
Babylonian disruption; from c. 520 the Persians re-planned it in regular blocks and
Athenian imports surged, until **Seleucia/Antioch's port replaced it in 301**.

## Why Al Mina mattered

- **The orientalizing channel.** It is the best-documented node of the eighth-century
  Greek–Near Eastern contact that transformed Greek art from Geometric to
  [[orientalizing-revolution|orientalizing]] and drove the great expansion of Greek
  trade.
- **A candidate for the alphabet's transmission.** As the prime point of sustained
  Greek–Phoenician contact it is a natural setting for the Greek adoption of the
  [[alphabet|alphabet]] — though, frustratingly, no early writing has been found on
  the site itself, so Rhodes and Crete remain rival candidates.
- **The Euboean reach.** Together with [[pithecusae|Pithecusa]] in the west, Al Mina
  defines the trans-Mediterranean span of [[euboea|Euboean]] enterprise before the
  great colonization.

## Emporion, not colony

In the colonization chapter (Ch. 37) **A. J. Graham** stresses the negative point that
Al Mina was emphatically **not a Greek colony** (*apoikia*): in its early levels there
are "no Greek graves, no Greek writing, no Greek cults and no Greek architecture," and
the abundant Greek pottery is "equalled in quantity by the non-Greek." Pottery alone
cannot prove who lived there — Greek ware turns up even at Tyre, where Greek settlers
are most improbable. Al Mina belongs to the **common Cypro-Levantine culture** of the
early Iron Age, significant for the history of colonization not as a settlement but as
the clearest evidence of the **commercial contact between Greeks and Phoenicians** that
gave Greece the alphabet — the *emporion* as against the independent
[[greek-colonization|colonial polis]].
