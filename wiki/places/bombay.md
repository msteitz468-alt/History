# Bombay

**Bombay** — seven malarial islands on India's west coast — became the
[[english-east-india-company|EIC]]'s western headquarters but, contrary to the
familiar story of its rapid rise displacing [[surat|Surat]], remained commercially
marginal for most of the eighteenth century. Martha Chaiklin's chapter in
[[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]]
treats Bombay as "a colonial centre rather than a commercial hub underpinned by
indigenous demand or royal patronage."

## A Slow and Contested Rise

The English acquired the seven islands from Portugal in **1668**, as part of the
dowry of **Catherine of Braganza** to Charles II, and made Bombay the official EIC
headquarters in **1687**. Yet for decades it was a "noxious climate, rife with
malarial mosquitos." A land-reclamation project to join the islands began only in the
mid-eighteenth century. Governor Sir Robert Cowan (1729–34) "complained he could not
make inroads into the Surat trade because of fierce local competition," and the
Maratha admiral **Kanhoji Angre** (1669–1729) disrupted shipping into the 1730s.

Merchants resisted relocation: in 1669 the EIC broker **Bhimji Parekh** led some
8,000 merchants from Surat to Bombay in protest at heavier non-Muslim taxation, but
the company itself declined to support the move (fearing Mughal reprisal) and they
returned. In 1749 Surati goldsmiths refused to mint coins at Bombay, fearing it would
"damage their reputation as elite craftsmen." As Chaiklin puts it, "It was not until
Mughal power declined that Bombay finally emerged as a viable alternative."

## The Industrial Pivot

Bombay's true ascendancy was a nineteenth-century, industrial-imperial phenomenon.
The Bombay Spinning and Weaving company was founded in **1854**, with industrial
weaving from c. 1860; the **American Civil War**'s disruption of US cotton finally
let Bombay build its own cotton-and-ivory circuit. From the 1870s to about 1915,
Bombay was "the third largest ivory market in the world" (after London and Antwerp) —
but serving European industrial demand, not the indigenous bangle trade. "Bombay only
supplanted Surat when technology and imperialism altered the composition of these
networks."
