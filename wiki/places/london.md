# London

England's capital and largest city; the political, commercial, legal, and cultural centre of the kingdom. In the late 16th and early 17th centuries London (including suburbs and Southwark) had a population of roughly 150,000–200,000 and was growing rapidly. It was the site of the royal court (Whitehall, Greenwich), Parliament, the law courts, the great trading companies, and — crucially for theatre — the concentration of audiences, capital, and regulatory oversight that made the professional playing companies viable.

The public amphitheatres (The Theatre 1576, Curtain, Rose, Swan, Globe 1599, Fortune, Red Bull, Hope) were mostly built outside the City proper in the liberties or on the Bankside, evading direct City control while remaining accessible. The indoor Blackfriars (from 1608 used by the King's Men) catered to a more elite and expensive audience. The Master of the Revels exercised pre-performance censorship. Plague orders repeatedly closed the theatres for months or years, forcing companies into provincial touring.

Shakespeare's adult career was embedded in this world: the move from Stratford, the successive company affiliations, the building and operation of the Globe, court performances, and the daily pressures of repertory, collaboration, and audience demand all took place in and around London. The city supplied the language, the crowds, the wealth, the power, and the danger that animate the plays.
