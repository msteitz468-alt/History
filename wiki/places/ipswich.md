# Ipswich

**Ipswich** (Anglo-Saxon *Gipeswic*) was one of the four major craft-production and trading settlements (wics or emporia) of seventh- to ninth-century England. It lay at the tidal reach of the Orwell estuary in southeastern Suffolk, on the shortest North Sea crossing to the mouth of the Rhine, and served as the principal economic capital of the East Anglian kingdom.

## Geographic Description and Setting

Ipswich is located on the north bank of the Orwell estuary. The earliest settlement (seventh century) covered up to 15 hectares centered on the river crossing that later became Stoke Bridge. Field boundaries containing cereal remains were excavated at Fore Street, about 200 meters east of the settlement, indicating an agricultural aspect of the local economy. To the north lay an extensive cemetery. A hollowed-out tree trunk well at Turret Lane, at the northern limit of the early area, gave a dendrochronological date of A.D. 670 (plus or minus ninety years).

> "Ipswich lies at the tidal reach of the Orwell estuary, in southeastern Suffolk, on the shortest crossing of the North Sea to the mouth of the Rhine. Extensive archaeological excavations between 1974 and 1990 have shown that the town is one of the four major craft production and trading settlements of seventh- to ninth-century England (the so-called wics, or emporia)." (Bogucki & Crabtree 2004, vol. 2, p. 330; range_05_31757_39695.txt)

## History of Settlement and Development

The earliest occupation dates to the seventh century. Excavations in 1986 west of St. Peter’s Street revealed the first structures and rubbish pits associated with local handmade pottery and Merovingian (Frankish) black wares, indicating a trading function. Burials of seventh-century date were excavated at Elm Street (1975), Foundation Street (1985), and the largest group (77 graves) on the Butter Market site immediately north of the early settlement (1988). Radiocarbon dates indicate burial was restricted to the seventh century. All burials were inhumations; nearly half were accompanied by objects, though most were poorly furnished, often with only a knife. Three richly furnished graves dating A.D. 610–670 included Continental grave assemblages; the richest was a male in a coffin with sword, shield, two spears, and two glass palm cups.

In the early eighth century Ipswich expanded to a massive 50 hectares by the creation of a virtual new town north of the original settlement and expansion south of the river into Stoke. New streets were laid out on a gridiron pattern, and buildings were constructed on their frontages.

> "In the early eighth century Ipswich was expanded to a massive 50 hectares by the creation of a virtual new town, to the north of the original settlement, and by expansion south of the river, into Stoke. New streets were laid out on a gridiron pattern, and buildings were constructed on their frontages." (Bogucki & Crabtree 2004, vol. 2, p. 331; range_05)

## Craft Production and Economy

Craft activities including spinning and weaving, antler and bone working, and metalworking occurred on most sites. Leatherworking is represented on the waterlogged riverfront site at Bridge Street. The major industry of the town in both the eighth and ninth centuries was pottery production. Evidence stretches for about 200 meters on the south side of Carr Street.

> "There can be little doubt, however, that the major industry of the town in both the eighth and ninth centuries was pottery production. ... Ipswich ware was the only wheel-made and kiln-fired pottery produced in England between the seventh and ninth centuries. The industry supplied the entire East Anglian Kingdom with pottery, and it was exported to aristocratic and ecclesiastical sites as far away as Yorkshire and Kent." (Bogucki & Crabtree 2004, vol. 2, p. 332; range_05)

On the margins of settlement, environmental evidence indicates agricultural activities, but overall the animal bone evidence suggests that meat was imported into the town from the rural hinterland and that Ipswich was a consumer, rather than a producer, of food.

The first Christian churches appear to be associated with the “new town” of the early eighth century (St. Peter, St. Augustine, and St. Mildred on the basis of dedications). The Anglo-Saxon waterfronts were simple timber revetments, no more than 1 meter high.

## International Trade

International trade was important throughout the eighth and ninth centuries. Imported Norwegian hone stones, Rhenish lava millstones, and Frankish pottery are found on all sites throughout the 50 hectares of occupation and in quantities far in excess of finds from rural sites. The dominant trade link was with the Rhine and Dorestad, but there are also links with Belgium and northern France.

> "International trade was important to the Ipswich economy throughout the eighth and ninth centuries. Imported Norwegian hone stones, Rhenish lava millstones, and Frankish pottery are found on all sites throughout the 50 hectares of occupation and in quantities far in excess of finds from rural sites. The dominant trade link is, not surprisingly, with the Rhine and Dorestad, but there are also links with Belgium and northern France. It is assumed that wool or cloth was exported in return." (Bogucki & Crabtree 2004, vol. 2, p. 332; range_05)

Rhenish imports undoubtedly included wine for consumption by the local aristocracy and early church. Wooden barrels reused as lining for well shafts have been dated by dendrochronology to shortly after A.D. 871 and match the tree ring pattern of the Mainz area of Germany.

## Political Context and Later Development

Ipswich was founded by the East Anglian royal house, the Wuffingas, whose burial ground at Sutton Hoo and palace at Rendlesham lie less than 10 miles northeast of Ipswich. During the ninth century other towns were founded in the region (Norwich, Thetford, Bury St. Edmunds), and Ipswich gradually lost its role as the East Anglian capital, though it remained a significant international port and became a market town serving southeastern Suffolk.

## Historiography and Sources

The detailed picture derives from extensive excavations 1974–1990. The site illustrates the planned, royal-controlled nature of wics and the transition from seventh-century small-scale trading to eighth-century large-scale craft production and monetized exchange. Ipswich ware pottery provides a key chronological and economic marker for East Anglian supremacy in the period.

**Grounded claim (Bogucki & Crabtree range 05):** "Gipeswic (Ipswich) served East Anglia and certainly was founded by the East Anglian royal house, the Wuffingas..." (vol. 2, p. 332).
