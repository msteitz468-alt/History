# La Venta

## Overview

**La Venta** was the principal Middle Formative [[olmec|Olmec]] center on the Tabasco
coastal plain. In Diehl’s presentation of Lowe’s scheme
([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1 Ch. 4]]), La Venta **replaced
[[san-lorenzo-olmec|San Lorenzo]]** during the Intermediate Olmec phase (c. 900–600 BCE) and
**climaxed in Terminal Olmec times** (within the Late Olmec span c. 600–300 BCE). Diehl notes
that recent La Venta research may require substantial chronological revision, but the
succession framework remains a useful working model.

## Pre-Olmec Bari-phase villages

The oldest known remains of permanent villages occupied by maize-farming farmers in Diehl’s
Gulf survey are Early and Middle **Bari-phase** sites (2250–1400 BCE) **near the later Olmec
center of La Venta**. Houses were round or rectangular wattle-and-daub structures on river
levees. Trash deposits indicate a varied diet: maize (*Zea mays*), palm nuts, mollusks, fish,
turtles, crocodilians, deer, armadillos, raccoons, and domesticated dog; grit-tempered brushed
pottery; basalt grinding tools; and obsidian. These settlements establish a deep agricultural
baseline in the La Venta landscape centuries before monumental Olmec construction.

## Elite center and heartland role

La Venta belongs to the small set of archaeologically studied elite Olmec towns (with San
Lorenzo, Laguna de los Cerros, and [[tres-zapotes|Tres Zapotes]]): larger than subordinate
villages, with stone sculpture, temples and elite houses on earth mounds, and buried caches of
discarded monuments, greenstone celts, figurines, and other prestige objects. Heartland elites
mobilized massive labor for earthworks and for transporting Tuxtla basalt over 150+ km, and
controlled long-distance exchange in jadeite, serpentine, obsidian, iron-ore ornaments, and
tropical exportables (cacao, pelts, feathers, stingray spines).

Earlier wiki detail (Cyphers/Snow; Mann) places the site on a salt dome, Complex A with massive
offerings and three large serpentine mosaics as geometric masks, a planned central axis, and a
~103-foot fluted clay mound—still the best architectural image of La Venta’s ceremonial core.
Diehl’s chapter body does not expand full architecture/caches beyond general elite-town traits;
he points readers to Drucker/Heizer/Squier, Rust & Sharer (1988), and González Lauck.

## Research history

Adams (CHNPA intro) cites Rebecca González Lauck’s 1988 revision of the La Venta map as an
example of how remapping “well-known” sites can yield surprising insights. Rust and Sharer
worked smaller Bari River villages near La Venta; riverine landscape dynamics (levees,
meanders, coastal outbuilding) complexly interact with early village placement.

Middle Formative southern Mesoamerican centers (e.g. Chalchuapa’s conical pyramid) may have
modeled architecture partly on La Venta (Sheets, CHNPA Ch. 9)—a position about **emulation and
prestige**, not proven political control.

## Why it mattered

Second pole of Olmec monumentality after San Lorenzo and a primary dataset for Formative Gulf
Coast religion, elite ideology, and interregional exchange. Anchors Intermediate–Terminal
Olmec chronology and the Bari-phase agricultural deep history of the Tabasco plain. Central to
[[olmec-mother-vs-sister-culture|mother vs. sister culture]] arguments about whether La Venta’s
complexity was unique or peer to contemporary highland and Pacific centers.
