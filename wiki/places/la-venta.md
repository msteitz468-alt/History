# La Venta

## Overview

**La Venta** was the principal Middle Formative [[olmec|Olmec]] center on the Tabasco
coastal plain. In Diehl’s presentation of Lowe’s scheme
([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1 Ch. 4]]), La Venta **replaced
[[san-lorenzo-olmec|San Lorenzo]]** during the Intermediate Olmec phase (c. 900–600 BCE) and
**climaxed in Terminal Olmec times** (within the Late Olmec span c. 600–300 BCE). Diehl notes
that recent La Venta research may require substantial chronological revision, but the
succession framework remains a useful working model.

## Pre-Olmec Bari-phase villages

The oldest known remains of permanent villages occupied by maize-farming farmers in Diehl’s
Gulf survey are Early and Middle **Bari-phase** sites (2250–1400 BCE) **near the later Olmec
center of La Venta**. Houses were round or rectangular wattle-and-daub structures on river
levees. Trash deposits indicate a varied diet: maize (*Zea mays*), palm nuts, mollusks, fish,
turtles, crocodilians, deer, armadillos, raccoons, and domesticated dog; grit-tempered brushed
pottery; basalt grinding tools; and obsidian. These settlements establish a deep agricultural
baseline in the La Venta landscape centuries before monumental Olmec construction.

## Elite center and heartland role

La Venta belongs to the small set of archaeologically studied elite Olmec towns (with San
Lorenzo, Laguna de los Cerros, and [[tres-zapotes|Tres Zapotes]]): larger than subordinate
villages, with stone sculpture, temples and elite houses on earth mounds, and buried caches of
discarded monuments, greenstone celts, figurines, and other prestige objects. Heartland elites
mobilized massive labor for earthworks and for transporting Tuxtla basalt over 150+ km, and
controlled long-distance exchange in jadeite, serpentine, obsidian, iron-ore ornaments, and
tropical exportables (cacao, pelts, feathers, stingray spines).

Earlier wiki detail (Cyphers/Snow; Mann) places the site on a salt dome, Complex A with massive
offerings and three large serpentine mosaics as geometric masks, a planned central axis, and a
~103-foot fluted clay mound—still the best architectural image of La Venta’s ceremonial core.
Diehl’s chapter body does not expand full architecture/caches beyond general elite-town traits;
he points readers to Drucker/Heizer/Squier, Rust & Sharer (1988), and González Lauck.

## Research history

Adams (CHNPA intro) cites Rebecca González Lauck’s 1988 revision of the La Venta map as an
example of how remapping “well-known” sites can yield surprising insights. Rust and Sharer
worked smaller Bari River villages near La Venta; riverine landscape dynamics (levees,
meanders, coastal outbuilding) complexly interact with early village placement.

Middle Formative southern Mesoamerican centers (e.g. Chalchuapa’s conical pyramid) may have
modeled architecture partly on La Venta (Sheets, CHNPA Ch. 9)—a position about **emulation and
prestige**, not proven political control.

## Evans & Webster 2001 (González Lauck; Grove; Clark; Tate)

### Site extent, architecture, and offerings (Rebecca González Lauck)

Key Olmec city in NW Tabasco (Huimanguillo municipality) on a Miocene elevation ~**20 m** asl amid
alluvial coastal plain; climate **>2,000 mm** rain, mean **27°C**. Original extent ~**200 ha**
(half destroyed by modern urban/industrial blight). Earthen architecture on a **N–S planned axis**;
**Complex A** restricted ceremonial precinct with **~30 m** pyramid and basalt-column fence;
**~4 ha** public plaza south of Complex C principal pyramid. Stirling “Acropolis”: stone trough
drainage with tar joints and basins; possible stone-carving workshops. Domestic architecture in
Complexes E, I and modern Villa la Venta.

Complex A excavations (Smithsonian **1942–43**, **1955**): basalt-column tomb with two young
individuals + rich jade under cinnabar; sandstone coffer burial-like offering; **“Massive
Offerings”**—**4 m** deep pits with serpentine platforms and mosaics of **>400** shaped blocks
buried under clay/sand and adobe mounds; **>50** offerings (ceramics, magnetite mirrors, **>100**
votive celts/axes; famous **16-figurine** semicircle scene). Four-phase architectural sequence
proposed **1000–600 B.C.** (controversial; ¹⁴C mostly construction-fill charcoal). Largest
thematically diverse Olmec monumental sculpture corpus; **no written language**—sculpture and
architecture as large-scale ideological communication. Stone sources **60–>400 km** (Veracruz,
Hidalgo, Chiapas, Oaxaca, Guatemala); blocks **>30 tons**. Elite + specialists + labor force;
elite-level contact with distant neighbors ([[chalcatzingo|Chalcatzingo]], Chalchuapa style
carvings). Culmination of long Olmec cultural manifestation (González Lauck, EW2001).

**Settlement/subsistence context:** dynamic riverine system; ancient Palma River course ~1 km N
with moundless and central-mound settlements mostly contemporary with La Venta occupation
**1200–400 B.C.** (some from **1730 B.C.** to present). Excavations recover corn, beans, palm
nuts, deer, crocodile, turtle, dog, fish, mollusks; alluvial soils potentially three crops/year.

### Research history and succession (Grove; Clark)

David C. Grove (EW2001): research began at La Venta (Stirling & Drucker **1942–43**; Drucker,
Heizer, Squier **1953**): Complex A caches of jade celts, royal burials, **32 m** earthen pyramid
(among earliest in Mesoamerica); first Olmec radiocarbon dates **1000–600 B.C.** Long treated as
Olmec archetype despite lacking earlier periods. González renewed work found pre-Olmec
(**1750 B.C.**) levee settlements with maize; maize remained the basic Olmec food crop. Ten
colossal heads at San Lorenzo and **four at La Venta**; four La Venta and two San Lorenzo
monolithic “altars”/thrones with ruler-in-niche underworld iconography.

John E. Clark (EW2001 South Region): La Venta **succeeded** San Lorenzo rather than competing as
contemporary antagonist (early-style sculptures at La Venta/Tres Zapotes likely reused).
Innovations: tall pyramids; N–S axial basal platforms for temples/elite residences with
offerings/sculptures; abundant jade celts/figurines in offerings and elite burials; shift to
multi-figure low-relief sculpture of rulers-as-gods; god monuments on main pyramid façade
anticipating Maya stucco temple façades. La Venta-style low-relief influence reached Guerrero to
El Salvador—**surpassing San Lorenzo’s reach**. Abandoned ~**400 B.C.** = conventional end of
Olmec culture; region nearly uninhabited **>six centuries** until Middle Classic repopulation
(Lowland Maya from E; Zoques from S; Mixes/Popolucas from S/W; later Nahua waves linked to
Toltec/Aztec commerce) (*followed_by:* post-Olmec demographic trough). Stela cult originated
Middle Formative at La Venta (Newsome/Houston, EW2001).

Carolyn Tate (EW2001 Art): **Massive Offering 2** of three consisted of about **1,000 tons** of
rough imported serpentine slabs; associated caches of celts and stone mirrors; cruciform
**37-celt** offering; basalt-column sarcophagus and tombs with jade figurines, beads, marine
items—interpreted as underworld-water effigies with world-tree celts; tombs at symbolic sea edge;
entire complex buried under Mound A-2 and not seen after deposition. Olmec art chronological
priority confirmed by La Venta radiocarbon **1957**.

## Why it mattered

Second pole of Olmec monumentality after San Lorenzo and a primary dataset for Formative Gulf
Coast religion, elite ideology, and interregional exchange. Anchors Intermediate–Terminal
Olmec chronology and the Bari-phase agricultural deep history of the Tabasco plain. Central to
[[olmec-mother-vs-sister-culture|mother vs. sister culture]] arguments about whether La Venta’s
complexity was unique or peer to contemporary highland and Pacific centers. EW2001 fixes ~**200 ha**
original extent, Massive Offering logistics, and the successor-not-antagonist relation to San
Lorenzo.
