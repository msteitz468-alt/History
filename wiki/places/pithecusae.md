# Pithecusa (Ischia)

**Pithecusa**, on the island of Ischia in the Bay of Naples, was the **earliest Greek
settlement in the west** — the [[euboea|Euboean]] (Chalcidian and Eretrian) foundation,
established around 770 BCE, that opened the whole western [[greek-colonization|
colonization]] movement and the trans-Mediterranean reach of Euboean enterprise.
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Chs. 36–38) treats it.

## The Euboean western pole

Pithecusa is the western counterpart of [[al-mina|Al Mina]]: the same **Euboeans**
whose pendent-semicircle cups mark the eastern emporion planted, slightly later, this
offshore island in the Tyrrhenian — "the Euboeans' interest in Al Mina preceded their
mid-eighth-century colony at Pithecusa." An offshore island was a classic safe colonial
site. The settlers worked **iron**, using ore brought from **Elba**, and the place is
rich in **eastern objects** — Cilician seals and Egyptian scarabs (including a cartouche
of the pharaoh Bakenrenef/Bocchoris) — testifying to the **East–West links** the
Euboeans maintained between their trading posts in both directions: Pithecusa is a key
node of the [[orientalizing-revolution|orientalizing trade]].

## Society and the alphabet

The excavated graves show **definite economic differences** — a stratified society with
a nobility (and slaves) within a generation or two of foundation — bearing on the debate
over whether early colonists shared land equally (see [[greek-colonization]]). The
houses are mostly small rectangular structures, with rare apsidal and oval examples.
Pithecusa has produced some of the **earliest Greek alphabetic inscriptions** —
including the celebrated **"Cup of Nestor,"** a Late Geometric skyphos scratched with a
playful three-line hexameter verse — placing the island, with Euboea and Attica, at the
documented birth of the Greek [[alphabet]] around the mid-eighth century.

## Why Pithecusa mattered

- The **opening of the Greek west** — the springboard from which the Euboeans founded
  Cyme on the mainland and the colonization of Sicily and Italy followed.
- A prime witness to the **fusion of colonization, East–West trade, and the alphabet**
  that defines the eighth-century Greek expansion.

## Hall (2014)

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]]: settlement ca. 770 (Lacco Ameno), contemporary with Al Mina. Literary founders: Livy Chalcis; Strabo Eretrians and Chalcidians (Chalcidians later leave after quarrel). Early Monte de Vico dump has some Euboean imports but origins more diverse — Corinthian dominance typical of western Greek sites; also Cretan, East Greek, and credible Phoenician presence. **Not a pure *emporion***: other Ischia settlements and extensive island exploitation imply serious agriculture; estimated population 5,000–10,000 large for a trading post. Valle di San Montano cemetery: hierarchical family clusters; ~40% of adults inhumed without goods — if social-inclusion reading is right, a large adult class lacked full sociopolitical recognition. Bokkhoris scarab (718–712) with EPC aryballoi is a dating fixed point. Alphabet transmission candidates include Pithecusae/Italy among others; earliest Greek graffiti generally mid-eighth century.

## Graffiti and Trade Marks

Keegan reads Pithecusa's informal inscriptions as some of the earliest and most
telling [[ancient-graffiti|graffiti]] evidence for the Greek west. The **"Cup of
Nestor"** (Tomb 168, c. 735–720 BCE) is a retrograde hexameter in the Euboean
alphabet functioning as a playful conditional incantation ("Whoever drinks from
this cup, desire for Aphrodite will seize him"). A Late Geometric amphora from
Grave 575 bears three Semitic (mostly Aramaic) inscriptions recording capacity
("200, double the standard quantity," ~54.8 litres) — in effect an ancient
customs declaration pointing to a direct [[al-mina|Al-Mīnā]]–Pithecusae shipping
link, and reused for an infant burial with a Phoenician funerary sign. A potter's
signature graffito ("[…]inos made me") and the locally-made "Shipwreck krater"
(depicting a capsized ship and drowning sailors) attest export production and the
perils of the trade that carried Euboeans west. Per
[[keegan-graffiti-in-antiquity-2014|Keegan]].
