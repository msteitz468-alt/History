# Italy

The Italian peninsula and associated islands, core of the Roman world and later center of the Renaissance. Geographic position made it a crossroads of Mediterranean trade, invasion corridor between northern Europe and the south, and cradle of multiple civilizations (Etruscan, Greek colonial, Roman, and the independent city-states of the medieval and early modern periods).

## Geographic Description
Boot-shaped peninsula extending into the central Mediterranean, divided by the Apennines, with fertile Po valley in the north, volcanic regions (Vesuvius, Etna), and extensive coastline. Natural harbors (Genoa, Venice, Naples) supported maritime power.

## Why It Mattered
- Heartland of Roman Empire: political, legal, cultural unification of the Mediterranean.
- Medieval and Renaissance fragmentation produced competitive city-states (Florence, Venice, Genoa, Milan) that drove banking, trade, art, and political theory.
- Papacy in Rome shaped European religion and politics for centuries.
- 19th-20th c. unification and modern nation-state role in European wars and EU.

**Iron Age Celtic migrations (Po valley/Cisalpine Gaul):** The Celtic settlement in the Po valley created "the continuous threat created by the Celtic settlement in the Po valley" instrumental in early Roman military development (Polybius). 4th-c movements brought La Tène groups (Boii, Senones); continued social links with northern homelands transmitted Graeco-Italic artistic ideas back (Vegetal Style helmets/motifs shared with Marne). ## Wickham, *Framing the Early Middle Ages* (2005)

[[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham]]’s Italy is the region ruined less by “barbarian” conquest per se than by **war and incomplete reconquest**. Justinianic reconquest (536–54; Verona/Brescia to 561) devastated governmental infrastructure; Lombard invasion from 568–9 never completed conquest. Italy’s coherence fragmented definitively—**never again under a single ruler between 569 and 1870**. By 605 the Lombard polity was the Po/Tuscany kingdom plus autonomous Spoleto and Benevento; Byzantine Italy comprised seven or eight tenuously connected coastal zones including [[rome|Rome]], Naples, Ravenna, and Sicily.

### Political and aristocratic structure
- The **Po plain** was agriculturally rich but imperfectly part of late Roman Mediterranean trade; Lombards made it their kingdom (capital Pavia), reversing northern political marginality. The *political* shift north occurred in Wickham’s period; the *economic* shift to northern dominance did not.
- Italian aristocracies saw **greater discontinuity than Gaul’s**: Mediterranean break-up, Gothic war, and incomplete Lombard conquest made more-than-local landowning rare by 750–800 (city-territory scale predominating). Late Roman southern Italy (Rome–Naples lowlands, Sicily, Lucania/Bruttii) had been the peninsula’s richest senatorial focus; after Carthage 439 and Gothic-war damage the private economic networks of the “senatorial region” fell into disarray.
- In Byzantine Italy, senators **changed identity rather than simply dying out** (Christian names; senate untraceable past c.580; no senatorial families past early seventh century). Between 600 and 750 aristocratic activity contracted further (late-eighth Rome almost no Sicily links); the pan-Mediterranean senatorial region ceased to exist. Ravenna leases show lay elite landowning **at city level only**—dramatically unlike Gaul, matched by Lombard Italy.
- Lombard political fragmentation had origins in **economic regionalization already acute before 568** (Marazzi; Modena *pozzi-deposito* c.600: local market survived, wider exchange gone). Active eighth-century land market (24% of northern private charters to 774 are sales) remained localized in single city-territories.
- Benevento slightly more sub-regionally integrated than the northern kingdom but not richer; S. Sofia, S. Vincenzo, Montecassino emerged c.800 as really serious landowners. No certain Italian **slave plantation** after ~300. Italian *sistema curtense* is early and better documented than mid-century Francia but demesnes were small/fragmented—labour as symbolic subjection, not St-Germain-style intensification. Bobbio and S. Giulia polyptychs are atypical late profit manors on a Frankish model; southern boundary of the *sistema curtense* did not extend much beyond Farfa and Amiata (papal *domuscultae* unusual/controversial).
- North of Rome, really rich aristocrats/monasteries appear mainly under **Carolingian rule** (ninth century); peasant-mode microregions could survive into the eighth–ninth centuries. Post-sixth-century aristocracies were **overwhelmingly urban** (best-attested eighth-century urban aristocracy outside Syria-Palestine and Egypt). Type-case: [[lucca]].

### Cities and exchange
- Urbanism debate uniquely intense (documents vs archaeological poverty). Italian cities kept classical spatial kit (forum as open space/market, cathedral, *curtis regia/ducalis*, walls, dense churches—e.g. 39 eighth-century churches in Lucca) with less plan disruption than most regions. After c.550 (Gothic war) cities were universally poorer: subdivided houses, wood, abandonment of paved streets/sewers. **Brescia** is the fullest excavated model. High-quality church building continued without transforming ordinary residential housing.
- Fora demonumentalized early (Africa-like) but often remained markets; curiae sometimes survived past 550 while practical power shifted to bishops and notables. Successful political centres kept structural coherence despite poverty—greater settlement density than Africa, poorer buildings. No Byzantine-style forced urban rationalization: local elites stayed put, seeding later autonomous city polities. Praise-poems for Milan (739) and Verona (c.800) preserve classical civic ideals through the peninsula’s greatest economic crisis.
- Late Roman Italy was already a set of ceramic sub-regions linked mainly by ARS and Rome’s demand. Vandal Africa and Gothic/Lombard wars made Italy a loose set of unrelated economic sub-regions. Northern ceramic involution c.650 left only highly localized common/coarse wares until late medieval fine-ware revival. After African/eastern bulk exchange ended c.700, **Rome and the Tyrrhenian south** remained the most complex Italian exchange zone (Forum ware; commercial amphora networks)—by 800 the most complex maritime exchange zone in the Mediterranean. **Venice** is the only real Mediterranean parallel to Dorestad (take-off late eighth/early ninth on Carolingian aristocratic demand).
- Italy was hit by all four of Wickham’s parameters (fiscal involution, reduced aristocratic wealth, disruptive war, loss of interregional links); only continued elite city living prevented sharper change. The south, less specialized as export producer than Africa, kept richer (if localized) elites and internal bulk routes feeding Rome into the eighth century.
