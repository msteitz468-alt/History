# Cyrenaica under Rome

The old Greek **Pentapolis** of north-east Libya — **Cyrene**, Apollonia, Ptolemais, Teuchira,
and Berenice — governed (with Crete) as the senatorial province of **Crete and Cyrene**. A
prosperous backwater of "modestly prosperous cities" whose history in the High Empire is dominated
by one catastrophe: the **Jewish Diaspora Revolt of AD 115–117**. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 17 (**Joyce Reynolds**), a deliberately "provisional" account
built from epigraphy and archaeology.

## A modestly prosperous Greek world (AD 70–115)

Cyrenaica's economy rested on **agriculture and pastoralism** (the once-lucrative **silphium**
trade never recovered its old volume), yielding "satisfactory incomes" rather than great fortunes —
enough to revive the residential quarter of Sidi Khrebeish at Berenice and import marble and goods.
The Greek cities were **gently Romanizing**: Italian immigrant families intermarrying with the
Greek elite, Roman citizenship spreading among leading Greeks (the one known Cyrenaican senator, P.
Sestius Pollio), Latin names and Roman-style funerary portraits replacing the old "faceless
goddess" busts — while **Libyan names and influences** grew more visible. Roman administration was
**fiscal**: Vespasian's resumed reclamation of *ager publicus* (boundary stones of his legate
Paconius Agrippinus), with survey and re-letting to tenants, "the emperor's aim surely fiscal," plus
attention to civic finances (sale of public land to Apollonia) and gifts of Romanizing public
buildings (Domitian's record-office annexe, Trajan's baths).

## The Jewish communities and the Diaspora Revolt (115–117)

Cyrenaica held **substantial Jewish communities**, always in touch with Judaea. A sinister
precursor was the affair of **Jonathan the Weaver** (c. AD 73), a refugee from Masada who led
~2,000 poor Jews into the desert; the governor **Catullus** crushed them and used the episode to
destroy and despoil richer Cyrenaican Jews — leaving "festering resentment."

The great **Jewish Diaspora Revolt** (probably AD 115–117) — part of a coordinated outbreak across
**Egypt, Cyprus, and Mesopotamia** ("the Kitos War"), rooted in bitterness after the destruction of
the Jerusalem Temple in 70 and exploiting **Trajan's absence on the Parthian campaign** — struck
Cyrenaica with "remarkable force." The literary sources stress huge casualties (Dio: 220,000) and
rural atrocities; **excavation confirms extensive destruction** of temples and administrative
buildings at Cyrene (the temple of Zeus' fallen peristyle, never re-erected; the Sanctuary of
Apollo; the road to Apollonia cut). The revolt was suppressed by **Q. Marcius Turbo** under Trajan;
the **Cyrenaican Jewish communities disappear entirely** from the inscriptions thereafter.

## Recovery and resettlement

Reynolds rejects the older view that the revolt sent Cyrenaica into "irreversible decline." Trajan,
then Hadrian, took **active relief measures**: emergency food (Hadrian called "nourisher" at
Cyrene), troops rebuilding the Cyrene–Apollonia road, the Caesareum/basilica and baths, and above
all **colonization** — Trajan dispatched **3,000 veteran legionaries** (under the camp prefect of
*legio XV Apollinaris*), and a new coastal city, **Hadrianopolis**, made the region a **Hexapolis**.
The eastern frontier was pulled back from Catabathmos to Darnis, probably a consequence of the
revolt. By the second half of the century the cities were again "modestly prosperous, even
vigorous": an ambitious building programme at Cyrene (the colonnaded Valley Street, new temples,
cisterns), reviving benefactions, and the inter-city rivalry typical of the Greek world (Berenice
and Ptolemais jockeying against Cyrene for assize status and Panhellenic precedence).

## A three-fold culture

Cyrenaica's distinctive feature is its **synthesis of Greek, Roman, and Libyan** elements. The
veteran colonists (recruited and serving in the Greek East) integrated into the Greek cities,
swelling the Roman-citizen middle range and hastening the decline of the Cyrenaean dialect. Yet
**Hadrian fostered Cyrene's Hellenism** — revising her law-code, stressing her Delphic foundation
and **Spartan kinship**, and drawing the cities into his **Panhellenion** (Cyrene a member from
AD 134), which bound Cyrenaica more closely to mainland Greece (Cyrenaean notables holding office at
Athens, Sparta, and Olympia, perhaps preferring this to Roman public service). Meanwhile **Libyan**
religious self-expression grew bolder — the second-century rock-cut **chthonic shrine at Slonta**
adapting Graeco-Roman techniques to native cult. By the end of the century Cyrenaica showed "a
vitality consonant with her natural resources, and a culture a striking combination of Greek, Roman
and Libyan elements."

## Significance

Cyrenaica is the High Empire's clearest case of a **Greek provincial world** Romanizing on its own
terms while keeping its Hellenic identity (and Panhellenic ties) — and the chief theatre, with
Egypt and Cyprus, of the **Jewish Diaspora Revolt**, one of the most destructive and least
documented convulsions of the Trajanic age, which annihilated a major Jewish community and reshaped
the province's population through veteran colonization.
