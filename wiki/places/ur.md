# Ur

One of the great cities of southern Mesopotamia, excavated by **Leonard Woolley**
(1922–34), and the site of the **Royal Cemetery** — the most spectacular single
discovery of Early Dynastic Sumer. Famous later as the seat of the
[[mesopotamian-chronology|Third Dynasty of Ur]] (the Sumerian imperial revival,
treated in this volume's Ch. XXII). First introduced here through its Early
Dynastic phase in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Chs. XII–XIII).

## The First Dynasty of Ur

When the [[sumerian-king-list|king-list]] passes the supremacy to Ur, its
reign-lengths suddenly shrink to human scale, and contemporary inscriptions of
**Mesannepada** and his son **A'annepada** confirm the list at this point (the
"80 years" of Mesannepada probably pool father and son). A'annepada built the
temple of Ninhursag at al-Ubaid. Ur was held at times by the rulers of
[[lagash|Lagash]] (inscriptions of Ur-Nanshe and Enannatum found there).

## The Royal Cemetery

Beneath strata predating the First Dynasty, Woolley found the **Royal Tombs**:
richly furnished graves whose occupants — including **Meskalamdug**,
**Akalamdug** (titled "king of Ur" on a seal), and a lady **nin** of the
First-Dynasty house — were accompanied in death by **dozens of retainers of both
sexes, with animals and full equipment**, evidently killed to continue their
service in the afterlife. The deposits (the gold "helmet" of Meskalamdug, the
inlaid **Standard of Ur**, lyres, jewelry of lapis and carnelian) display "a
command and mastery of foreign resources which was never again to be matched."

This **retainer sacrifice** is a Sumerian counterpart to the contemporary
practice at [[early-dynastic-egypt|Early Dynastic Egypt's]] royal tombs — yet
Gadd stresses it is otherwise unknown in later Babylonian history, and its meaning
"remains for the present uncomprehended." The tombs' occupants are mostly absent
from the king-list, so their exact status is debated.

## Trade and wealth

The Royal Tombs are physical proof of Sumer's long-distance
[[trade-network-formation|trade]]: gold (perhaps from Meluhha/Armenia), copper
(Oman), lapis lazuli (Badakhshan), carnelian (etched beads from the Indus, e.g.
Harappa). Gadd notes that around the mid-third millennium "the whole ancient world
seems to have reached together the zenith of its prosperity" — Sumer's heroic age,
Egypt's Old Kingdom pyramids, and the treasures of [[troy|Troy II]] alike.

## Ur under the empire: Enkheduanna and the *entu*-priestesses

When [[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon]] conquered Sumer, he bound Ur's premier cult to the
Akkadian house by installing his daughter **[[enkheduanna|Enkheduanna]]** as
*entu* (high-priestess) of the Moon-god **Nanna** — the first holder of an office
that royal women would fill for nearly two thousand years, and the earliest author
known by name. The **Second Dynasty of Ur** (its last king, **Kaku**) led the
Sumerian revolt against Sargon's son [[unknown|Rimush]], who took the city and
dismantled its wall; Enkheduanna's lament records the disaster. Ur's greatest age
still lay ahead, as capital of the [[mesopotamian-chronology|Third Dynasty of Ur]]
after the [[gutian-invasion|fall of Akkad]].

## Imperial capital: the Third Dynasty of Ur

Ur's greatest age was as capital of the **[[third-dynasty-of-ur|Third Dynasty of
Ur]]** (~2112–2004 BCE), the Neo-Sumerian empire founded by
**[[ur-nammu|Ur-Nammu]]** and perfected by **[[shulgi|Shulgi]]**. Ur-Nammu raised
the city's enduring landmark — the **Great Ziggurat of Nanna**, the best-preserved
temple-tower in Mesopotamia — and from Ur the dynasty ran its famously
[[temple-economy|hyper-bureaucratic command economy]]. The age ended in catastrophe:
the [[fall-of-ur-2004bce|Elamite and Subarian sack of Ur]] (~2004 BCE) under
Ibbi-Sin, mourned in the Sumerian **Lamentations over the Destruction of Ur** —
after which "never again was Ur to play an important part in history."

## Why this place mattered

Ur is the showcase of Early Dynastic wealth, art, and funerary religion, and
later the **imperial capital of the last great Sumerian state** ([[third-dynasty-of-ur|
Ur III]]). Its Royal Cemetery is the single richest source for Sumerian material
culture and one of the two great early instances of royal human sacrifice; its
ziggurat is the architectural emblem of Sumerian religion.
