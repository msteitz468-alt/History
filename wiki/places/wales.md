# Wales

The mountainous western promontory of [[british-isles|Britain]], whose "highland centre
separating coastal strips" shaped a distinctive political geography. Welsh medieval
kingdoms evolved broadly from Roman *civitates* (Dyfed from the Demetae); their rulers
traced themselves to the Roman commander **Magnus Maximus**. John Reuben Davies's chapter
in [[sources/stafford-companion-early-middle-ages-britain-ireland-2009|Stafford's
*Companion*]] is the wiki's anchor for early medieval Wales.

## Kingdoms and Overkingship

In 825 **Merfyn Frych** founded a new line in **Gwynedd**; his son **Rhodri Mawr** (d. 878)
added Ceredigion and Powys, and every Welsh ruler down to the Edwardian conquest of 1282–3
traced descent from him. **Hywel Dda** (d. 950) — entering Dyfed by marriage, annexing
Gwynedd (942), consolidating the south as **Deheubarth** — achieved a near-pan-Welsh
hegemony that "lasted just eight years." Wendy Davies reads this as a new but fragile
**overkingship**: great kings ruling lesser kings' territories with "no basis of
administrative machinery on which to build a secure and enduring regime." Only
**Gruffudd ap Llywelyn** (d. 1063) ever ruled "the whole of Wales," holding the former
Mercian burh of Rhuddlan and collecting renders from English manors, before Harold and
Tostig's 1063 pincer drove his own retinue to kill him.

## English and Norman Pressure

Mercian and West-Saxon aggression was constant: large areas of north Wales fell to Mercia
in the ninth century, **Cornwall** (Dumnonia) ceased to exist as a kingdom under West-Saxon
control around 800, and from 927 to 956 Welsh rulers were styled **subreguli** ("little
under-kings") in English charters, Hywel's subscription always taking precedence. After
1066 the Conqueror established the border earldoms of **Chester, Shrewsbury, and Hereford**;
in 1081 William received the submission of **Rhys ap Tewdwr** at St Davids, and on Rhys's
death in 1093 a chronicler wrote "And then fell the kingdom of the Britons."

## Society, Church, and Economy

Welsh bishoprics were **coterminous with kingdoms** (Elfoddw, "archbishop of the Venedotian
kingdom," d. 809), reduced to three principal sees — **Bangor, St Davids, Llandaf** — by
the consolidation of kingdoms. The economy was agrarian, organised through the *maenor*
estate (with *llys*, *llan*, and *tref*); Wales "lacked the urbanized settlements that had
grown up in England" — the only town was the English burh at **Rhuddlan** — and coinage
barely circulated. Welsh law was attributed to Hywel Dda but survives only in twelfth-
century compilations (a legitimating device). On "Celtic solidarity," Davies cautions that
Welsh, Cornish, and northern British rulers freely allied with English kings against each
other: any shared feeling "represented more a shared sense of having been oppressed … than
any appeal to racial or cultural affinity."
