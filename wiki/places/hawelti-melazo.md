# Hawelti-Melazo

The **Melazzo sites** beside the Mai Agazen river, approximately 10 km southeast of [[aksum-city|Aksum]], comprise a cluster of localities discovered and first excavated in the late 1950s by Jean Leclant and Henri de Contenson, and revisited in 1974 by Joseph Michels. They are cited in the literature under various individual names — **Gobochela**, **Hawelti**, **Hawelti-Malazzo**, **Enda Tcherqos**, **Mazaber** — but their spatial and stratigraphical relationships remain imperfectly understood; many artefacts were not recovered from primary contexts and may have been moved in antiquity.

## Significance

The complex is one of the principal elite/monumental sites of the first-millennium-BC northern Horn, alongside [[yeha|Yeha]] and Maqaber Ga'ewa. Its importance lies in the quantity and quality of South Arabian-affiliated art and epigraphy recovered, and in the religious dedications to Almaqah (the southern Arabian moon god).

### Hawelti mound

A low but extensive mound preserving traces of at least twenty square-sectioned monolithic stelae, broadly analogous to those at Kaskase in Eritrea. Most are no longer in their original positions, and they do not appear to derive from temple porticos. On the mound's southern slope were found the disturbed remains of two rough-stone buildings (each approximately 10 m square), between which a cache of fine stone-carving — clearly collected and deposited there rather than manufactured in situ — was recovered.

The centrepiece of this cache was a remarkable **stone baldachin (canopied throne)**, 1.4 m high, with relief carving: a frieze of stylised ibex on the front edges, and large bearded male figures carrying paddle-shaped objects on each side. Two seated female stone statuettes (one 0.8 m high) were also recovered. Phillipson notes that "most commentators have attributed these objects or their inspiration to southern Arabia, but in fact neither the statuettes nor the baldachin show clear affinities with works of demonstrable provenance in that region. Their affinities are far wider and more generalised; the only element with clear parallels in southern Arabia is the ibex decoration on the baldachin." The date of these sculptures — plausibly in the second quarter of the first millennium BC — rests solely on stylistic comparisons; renewed excavations began in 2010.

### Gobochela

A small building of undressed masonry (interpreted as a temple) associated with stone altars and incense-burners bearing dedicatory inscriptions to *lmq* (Almaqah). Three inscriptions (RIE 3–5) found re-used in the nearby Enda Tcherqos church are thought to have originated at Gobochela.

## Chronology and Uncertainty

Dating of the Melazzo complex is problematic: artefacts span a wide range (some of Aksumite age), stratigraphic documentation is incomplete, and the most spectacular pieces were recovered in disturbed or redeposited contexts. The chronological uncertainty is likely to be remedied through ongoing excavations.

## Historiography

The site achieved early prominence in the literature partly because of its accessible location and the dramatic quality of its art objects. Leclant, de Contenson, and Pirenne were the principal early interpreters; their focus on art objects, with limited attention to stratigraphy and subsistence evidence, shaped a generation of reconstructions. Phillipson's 2012 synthesis re-situates the Melazzo complex within a picture of numerically exceptional elite sites surrounded by a majority population of indigenous farmers with no southern Arabian connections.
