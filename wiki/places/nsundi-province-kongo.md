## Overview

Nsundi was one of Kongo's northern provinces, situated in the Inkisi Valley on the north bank of the Congo River. It held a special constitutional role as the **seat of the heir apparent**: the king's designated successor ruled Nsundi before acceding to the throne. The province was also among the earliest conquests of the Kongo kings after the founding of [[mbanza-kongo|Mbanza Kongo]], and its pre-Kongo origins lay within the [[kongo-dia-nlaza|Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza]].

## Constitutional Role

Duarte Lopes / Pigafetta (1591:35) noted that Nsundi had "a special place" in the kingdom's structure: "the king made it the seat of his heir apparent." He gave as examples [[afonso-i-kongo|Afonso I]], who had ruled Nsundi in the late fifteenth century before his contested succession in 1509, and Álvaro II, who had served as Mwene Nsundi before becoming king. Afonso I himself described Nsundi as the "head of the kingdom" (*cabo de reino*) in his early correspondence (Brásio 1952:460–1). However, Thornton notes (ch. 1) that subsequent kings ruled other provinces, so holding Nsundi was "hardly a guarantee of succession to the throne."

## Early Conquest and Origins

Lopes recorded that Nsundi, Mpangu, and [[mbata-province-kongo|Mbata]] together made up the provinces in the Inkisi Valley, and that Nsundi was among "the earlier conquests that the kings of Kongo made" — conquered, then Mpangu, both therefore by force rather than the voluntary alliance by which Mbata joined (Pigafetta 1591:36). Thornton (2018) places these conquests in the generation of [[lukeni-lua-nimi|Lukeni lua Nimi]] or his immediate successors (c. 1390–1420), with Nsundi serving as "the spearhead for an advance, southward down the Inkisi River to take over Mpangu and eastward along the south bank of the Congo River."

All three provinces — Nsundi, Mpangu, and Mbata — appear in Cardoso's 1621 revolt report within the geographic region called "Momboares" (*Mumbwadi* = "the Seveners"), confirming their prior membership in the [[kongo-dia-nlaza|Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza]] before Kongo's annexation.

## Archaeological Evidence

The KongoKing project (2012–16) excavated the site of **Kindoki**, which it identified with reasonable certainty as Mbanza Nsundi, the provincial capital. Radiocarbon dating of two samples found several hundred metres apart within the same site showed settlement by at least 1350 — before the founding of Kongo (c. 1390 in Thornton's genealogical calculation). The site's extent suggests more than a simple village. This dating pushes Nsundi's pre-Kongo occupation back to the period of the Seven Kingdoms' hegemony, confirming Thornton's argument that the Inkisi Valley was already politically organized before Kongo's expansion (Clist et al. 2015c; Matonda et al. 2015; Clist et al. 2018a).

## The KongoKing Project's Focus

The KongoKing project (2012–16) deliberately focused its archaeological fieldwork on Kongo's northern provinces including Nsundi, Mpangu, and Mbata — all in present-day Congo-Kinshasa — in part because excavation permits in Angola (the location of Mbanza Kongo and Soyo) were never obtained. This "geographical reorientation" proved fruitful, allowing a "decentring" of Kongo history away from its Portuguese-documented heartland toward the regions most relevant to its pre-contact origins.
