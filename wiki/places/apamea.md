## Geographic Description

Apamea (Apamaea) sits on the Orontes river in western Syria, with occupation at the site
traced back to the Chalcolithic. Ball notes it lay within the region that also housed
[[emesa|Emesa]] to the south and [[dura-europos|Dura-Europos]] and other Euphrates-facing
settlements to the east, forming part of the dense network of Orontes-valley and northern
Syrian cities the Seleucids refounded on much older settlement layers.

## History of Control and Settlement

The site was the Achaemenid-era town of Parnakka (Pharnake), renamed Pella by the
Macedonians after Philip II's Macedonian capital, then renamed Apamaea by Seleucus Nicator
in honor of his wife Apama, daughter of the Bactrian prince Spitamenes. Under the
Seleucids it became army headquarters and a major stud farm, with 30,000 mares, 300
stallions, and around 500 elephants recorded there. Population is recorded at 117,000 free
inhabitants shortly after Pompey's conquest of the region in 64 BC, during which Pompey
destroyed the city's citadel.

Apamaea was devastated by an earthquake on 3 December AD 115 — the same quake that damaged
[[antioch|Antioch]], forcing Trajan to camp in the hippodrome there — after which the city
was completely rebuilt, including an enormous 2-kilometer colonnaded Cardo, one of the
grandest colonnaded streets in the Roman world, financed partly by the local philanthropist
Julius Agrippa. The rebuilt city had the largest theatre in the East and a famous
Temple/oracle of Zeus Belos, which later housed the Neoplatonic school of Iamblichus before
being destroyed by Bishop Marcellus in the late 4th century. Apamaea served as winter
quarters of the Second Parthian Legion in the early 3rd century, and was finally destroyed
by [[sasanian-empire|Khusrau I]] in AD 540, having surrendered peacefully to his advancing
army — Ball notes archaeological evidence from the Syrian countryside suggests the
Sasanian advance was in places accompanied by support for the local economy and protection
of villages, contrary to the destruction implied by Western literary sources for this
campaign.

## Why It Mattered

Ball links Apamaea's outsized wealth and monumentality — remarkable for a city that was
neither a provincial capital nor a client-kingdom seat — to its close ties with the
[[severan-dynasty|Severan dynasty]]. The [[emesene-dynasty|Emesene family]]'s early
history connects to the Orontes Valley/Apamaea region; [[elagabalus|Elagabalus]]'s father
was from Apamaea; and young Septimius Severus reportedly first received intimations of his
imperial destiny at the Apamaea oracle of Zeus Belos. A dedicatory Corinthian column dated
263, honoring [[sasanian-empire|Shapur I]], stood at a crossroads in Bishapur closely
paralleling one at Apamaea, evidence for Ball of continued cross-frontier architectural
exchange even amid Roman-Sasanian conflict.

Apamaea's rebuilt colonnaded Cardo is one of Ball's key examples in his broader argument
about the function of eastern colonnaded streets: he argues these were not monumentalized
stoas or forum-substitutes in the Western sense but literally oriental bazaars (Arabic
*suq*, Persian *bazar*) — market streets lined with shops, evidenced by surviving shop
names and trades inscribed at [[palmyra|Palmyra]] and by excavated shop-rows at Apamaea and
Jerash. He argues the eastern colonnaded street is the functional counterpart of the
Western forum/agora, both being fundamentally markets in origin, and notes that true
Western-style forums are rare in the East, confined to a handful of deliberately "Roman" or
Herodian foundations (Antioch, Beirut, Sebaste, Caesarea, Philadelphia/Amman) rather than
typical of eastern cities generally.

Apamaea also lay within the broader "Tetrapolis" of Seleucid foundations (Seleucia,
Antioch, Laodicaea, Apamaea); Ball's wider thesis holds that none of these were true new
foundations but refoundations of much older native settlements — a claim he applies to
Apamaea as part of his larger argument that "the Romans did not found a single new city in
the East. All were refoundations of existing cities."

## Historiography

The specific causal claim linking Apamaea's wealth to Severan/Emesene patronage is part of
Ball's broader interpretive framework and is presented here as his argued position rather
than uncontested fact; see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]] for the fuller debate and the
note that mainstream scholarship (e.g. Fergus Millar, *The Roman Near East*, 1993) is more
cautious about reading such patronage networks into the archaeological record.
