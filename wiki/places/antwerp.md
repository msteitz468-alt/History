# Antwerp

Antwerp was the **second great entrepôt of the Low Countries**, succeeding
[[bruges|Bruges]] in commercial primacy across the sixteenth century and itself
displaced by [[amsterdam|Amsterdam]] after 1585 — the central sequence of
[[gelderblom-cities-of-commerce-2013|Gelderblom's *Cities of Commerce*]].

## Setting and Rise

Antwerp sits on the river **Scheldt** in Brabant, with deep-water access superior
to the silting Zwyn that served Bruges. Its rise rested on the **fairs of Brabant**
(Antwerp and nearby Bergen-op-Zoom), held four times a year, which by the
fifteenth century supplied English and South German textiles and a wide range of
Low Countries manufactures that Bruges-based merchants increasingly traveled to
obtain. When the **Flemish Revolt (1483–92)** and Maximilian I's pressure broke
Bruges, merchants relocated to Antwerp — within the same sovereign's territory,
exactly as the [[urban-competition-commercial-institutions|urban-competition]]
logic predicts.

## Commercial and Institutional Profile

Sixteenth-century Antwerp became the hub of a genuinely
[[early-modern-commerce|global early-modern trade]]: Portuguese spices, South
German copper and silver, English broadcloth, and an increasingly sophisticated
**money market**. Charles V intervened in the organization of credit operations on
the Antwerp market for his own fiscal benefit — one of Gelderblom's examples of a
sovereign adapting an institution out of self-interest rather than out of
commitment to merchants. Notably, **active international traders did not govern
Antwerp** (Gelderblom uses it, with Venice, to refute the claim that merchant
political control was the source of pro-trade institutions). The city continued
to refine the permanent-market and contracting institutions pioneered at Bruges,
moving toward more generalized arrangements.

## Decline

Antwerp's fall was political-military: the **Dutch Revolt** and the Spanish
recapture of the city in **August 1585**, combined with the Dutch blockade of the
Scheldt, drove its merchants — Flemish, German, Portuguese, English, Italian — to
emigrate. Most ultimately chose [[amsterdam|Amsterdam]], transferring capital,
skills, and commercial networks northward and shifting the economic center of
gravity of the Low Countries. Philip II's enormous military effort to retain
Europe's principal market within his territory could not reverse the relocation.

## Significance

Antwerp is the **transitional** term in the sequence: a global sixteenth-century
market governed by a narrow non-merchant elite, refining the inclusive
institutions Bruges began and Amsterdam would generalize, and demonstrating that
a city's commercial fate could be decided by its place in the international urban
hierarchy and by war rather than by institutional quality alone.
