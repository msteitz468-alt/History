## Overview

The Ciudadela is the most architecturally distinctive complex at [[teotihuacan]] and arguably the most debated in terms of its political and religious function. Situated at the southern end of the civic-ceremonial core, on the east side of the Avenue of the Dead roughly 2.15 km from the Moon Pyramid, it consists of a great walled enclosure approximately 400 m on a side, enclosing a large open plaza, the [[feathered-serpent-pyramid]] (FSP), and two residential palace complexes flanking it. The enclosing platforms represent a construction innovation without clear Mesoamerican precedent. The Ciudadela was built primarily in the Miccaotli phase (ca. 100–170 CE), "probably in this period, as were a pair of huge low platforms (the Great Compound) enclosing a large plaza that was possibly a marketplace, just across the Avenue of the Dead from the Ciudadela" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

## Construction and Layout

Pre-Ciudadela structures were "thoroughly razed and a wholly new complex was built," suggesting "a sharp break with what went before" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). This architectural break is one basis for inferring new religious and political institutions.

**Outer platforms**: The north, east, and south platforms are approximately 7–8 m high and 80 m wide. Their solid fill of adobe bricks was constructed in the Miccaotli phase, probably early in that phase. A series of concrete floors atop the platforms span the Early Tlamimilolpa through Late Xolalpan or Metepec phases, adding approximately a meter to their height over time. Four stepped pyramids sit atop the north platform; four more on the south platform; three on the east platform. The stairways of all these pyramids face inward — toward the great plaza. Walls originally connecting the pyramids on the north, east, and south platforms were about 1.4 m thick and painted red; they would have restricted views into the enclosure's interior.

The total volume of all four surrounding platforms (not counting their associated pyramids) is approximately 627,000 cubic meters — "greater than the volume of the Moon Pyramid and nearly two-thirds the volume of the Sun Pyramid" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**West side (entrance)**: The west platform is notably different: only 3–4 m high and 35 m wide, with four stepped pyramids whose stairways face outward toward the Avenue of the Dead. A broad stairway 32 m wide between the middle two pyramids gives easy access from the Avenue to the great plaza. An earlier lower stage of the west platform (confirmed by 1980–82 excavations) means "the Ciudadela was originally quite open on the west" (Drucker 1974; Cabrera 1991, cited in Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**Great plaza**: Approximately 250 m north-south by 200 m east-west; effective area about 4.4 hectares — "enough to hold up to a hundred thousand people without much crowding." A concrete floor, probably coeval with the FSP, covers the entire plaza and was renewed twice. Near the center is a large low square platform (20 m on a side) with stairways on all sides, analogous to the central altars in residential compound courtyards and the Moon Plaza. The enigmatic structure 1B-prime:N1E1 in the southern plaza violates Teotihuacan's usual bilateral symmetry; it appears to be a pre-Ciudadela structure that was preserved in place through the city's history, perhaps regarded as the cosmological center of the universe (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

## The Feathered Serpent Pyramid Within the Ciudadela

The [[feathered-serpent-pyramid]] stands at the eastern end of the great plaza, originally facing directly onto it before its front was covered by the later Plataforma Adosada. North-south transverse platforms separate the plaza from the FSP and from the residential complexes on either side.

## Residential Palace Complexes

East-west corridors separate the north and south sides of the FSP from two large room complexes, each approximately 80 m north-south by 60 m east-west (about 4,800 square meters each). The later stages of both complexes consist of five apartments arranged around a large central courtyard "in a far more orderly fashion than in most other Teotihuacan residential compounds" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). Courtyards lack altars. Evidence for murals in the later stages is "curiously lacking" despite surviving plaster, though murals existed in earlier building stages.

An early stage of both complexes predates the FSP but post-dates the pre-Ciudadela; the North Palace shows early orange-red motifs on white backgrounds reminiscent of the Sun Pyramid's fore-platform murals and the "Ward Temple" in La Ventilla. The later stages were probably coeval with the FSP, and their paired arrangement suggests "dual political or religious offices" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

Interpretations divide between Sugiyama (2005), who suggests the complexes merely "housed priests and others in the service of the FSP," and Cowgill (2015), who continues to suspect they were built for "persons who held unprecedented kinds of political office."

## The Enclosure of the Artisans

On the northern exterior of the Ciudadela is the Enclosure of the Artisans: a great walled area approximately 380 m long and 80 m wide (2:N1E1), bounded on three sides by a freestanding wall 3–4 m high and on the south by the Ciudadela itself. It was most actively used in the next period (Tlamimilolpa–Xolalpan), when artisans made coarse censers and fine clay censer ornaments using molds, but two early stairways connecting it to the Ciudadela platforms confirm it existed in the Miccaotli–Early Tlamimilolpa interval.

## The Great Compound

Directly across the Avenue of the Dead from the Ciudadela, the Great Compound is a complementary structure with very low platforms (only about a meter high) enclosing a large central plaza approximately 254 m east-west by 214 m north-south (total area ~20 ha). Its very existence was not recognized until 1962 (Millon 1973). The central plaza covers about 5.4 ha. René Millon (1973) suggested that, together with the Ciudadela, the Great Compound may have been "the city's bureaucratic, religious, and commercial center"; its central plaza may have served as the city's principal marketplace. Earliest layers in TMP Excavation 17 date to the Miccaotli phase, suggesting it was begun around the same time as the FSP or "soon thereafter" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

## Significance and Interpretation

"Architecturally, the Ciudadela complex was without precedent, and it was never copied except at Teotihuacan outposts, such as Chingú in the Tula region" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). This uniqueness is a primary indicator that it represented genuinely new religious and political institutions.

The Ciudadela's relationship to the Sun and Moon Pyramids is a central interpretive problem. Cowgill's preferred reading is that "long-established priestly organizations, endowed with rich resources, persisted at the Sun and Moon Pyramids, while the Ciudadela complex was created to be the seat of a new line of potentates" — possibly analogous to the differentiation of palaces from temples in early Mesopotamia (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). However, given the FSP's prominent place within it, the Ciudadela was never purely secular. Authority at Teotihuacan "may have been divided among two or more hierarchies" — one centered on the Sun Pyramid retaining older authority, with new institutions at the Moon Pyramid and Ciudadela (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

## Archaeological History

The first large-scale project after Gamio and Marquina (1917–1922) was directed by Rubén Cabrera (1980–82), covering the north and south palaces, the Enclosure of the Artisans, the FSP area, and portions of the Avenue of the Dead. Further operations in 1988–89 (Cabrera-Sugiyama-Cowgill) and 1992–94 (Cabrera) focused on the FSP's sacrificial burial program. The TMP (Teotihuacan Mapping Project) documented the Ciudadela's plan and surface finds.

## Historiography

Armillas (1964) first proposed the Ciudadela as the seat of Teotihuacan rulers; Millon (1973) and Cowgill (1983) agreed. Sugiyama (2005) has argued against this interpretation, proposing instead a primarily religious function dedicated to divine (not ruler) commemoration. The question remains unresolved. The later desecration of the FSP — probably deliberately stripped of its superstructure and associated with a political transition — is interpreted by Cowgill (1983, 2007) as evidence of regime change, possibly from more autocratic rule toward oligarchic governance centered on the Avenue of the Dead Complex.
