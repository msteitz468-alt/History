## Overview

Gurgum was a Neo-Hittite kingdom in the plain of modern Maraş (Kahramanmaraş) in south-eastern Anatolia, bordered by Sam'al to the south and [[kummukh|Kummukh]] to the east. Its Luwian name is unknown; "Gurgum" is the Assyrian designation. The capital was located at Maraş, called *Marqas* in the inscriptions of Sargon II. The kingdom is notable for possessing a relatively complete, nine-generation dynastic genealogy reconstructed from Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions found at and near Maraş — one of the most detailed dynastic records surviving for any Neo-Hittite state. Gurgum was annexed by Sargon II c.711 BCE and made an Assyrian province named Marqas.

## Geographic Description

Gurgum occupied the fertile Maraş plain in the Anti-Taurus foothills, a region controlling routes between the Anatolian plateau and northern Syria. Its southern border with Sam'al and eastern border with Kummukh are partially documented by boundary inscriptions. Bryce notes that the site of Maraş has never been systematically excavated: "the numerous sculptured and inscribed monuments which have been appearing from the site for more than a century have all been found by irregular digging and have no accurately recorded provenance" (Hawkins, cited by Bryce). The kingdom generally pursued a policy of agricultural development rather than military expansion.

## History

**Origins and dynastic foundation.** The foundation of Gurgum may date to the late eleventh century or earlier; its first attested ruler, Astuwaramanza, is named as great-great-great-great-grandfather in a later genealogical inscription, implying the dynasty was already established by c.1000 BCE at the latest. Bryce suggests the preservation of the name Muwatalli in the dynasty possibly indicates "that the Gurgumean dynasty was founded by a branch of the central Hittite dynasty after the fall of the Hittite empire" — specifically, perhaps, descendants of the Hittite king Muwatalli II's line through his son Urhi-Teshub, who had been exiled to Syria. Alternatively, the Maraş region may originally have lain under the control of [[carchemish|Carchemish]], with a branch of the Hittite royal family established there under Carchemish's overlordship.

**The Gurgumean dynasty** (reconstructed from three key hieroglyphic inscriptions, principally MARAŞ 1):

Astuwaramanza → Muwatalli I → Larama I → Muwizi → Halparuntiya I → Muwatalli II → Halparuntiya II → Larama II → Halparuntiya III

The succession passed from father to son through all nine known generations.

- **Astuwaramanza** (late 11th cent.): earliest known king; may have founded the dynasty.
- **Muwatalli I** (early 10th cent.): known only as Larama I's father.
- **Larama I** (c.950): author of MARAŞ 8; his inscription records a reconstruction programme including replanting crops and vineyards following some devastation of his land.
- **Muwizi** (later 10th cent.): known only from later genealogical inscriptions.
- **Halparuntiya I** (earlier 9th cent.): known only from genealogical inscriptions.
- **Muwatalli II** (Assyrian *Mutallu*, attested 858 BCE): first Gurgumean king attested in Assyrian records; became a tributary of Shalmaneser III in his first western campaign in 858 (RIMA 3: 16).
- **Halparuntiya II** (Assyrian *Qalparunda*, attested 853): son of Muwatalli II; his own hieroglyphic inscription on a colossal statue fragment at Maraş records military exploits including an attack on the city Hirika (possibly Hilakku/Cilicia Tracheia, though Hawkins prefers a border territory near Elbistan) and the capture of Iluwasi. He appears among Shalmaneser III's tributaries in 853.
- **Larama II** (Assyrian *Palalam*, later 9th cent.): known from genealogical inscriptions and from the Pazarcık boundary stone of 805 BCE (RIMA 3: 205), where his name appears as "Palalam."
- **Halparuntiya III** (Assyrian *Qalparunda*, attested 805–c.800): son of Larama II; author of MARAŞ 1, the most comprehensive genealogical inscription. The 805 boundary stone set up by Adad-nirari III between Kummukh and Gurgum identifies him as king of Gurgum. He may also have led a Gurgumean contingent in the alliance that c.800 besieged Hamath's northern city Hatarikka.

**Gap (c.800 to 743).** More than fifty years pass without an attested Gurgumean king. The gap was filled by rulers who may or may not have continued the original dynastic line.

- **Tarhulara** (attested 743–c.711): re-appears in records as a member of the Urartian–Arpad led anti-Assyrian coalition confronted by Tiglath-pileser III in 743 (Tigl. III 100–1, 132–3). Tiglath-pileser invaded and allegedly destroyed one hundred of Tarhulara's cities but, after Tarhulara submitted and requested his capital be spared, allowed him to retain his throne as a tributary. Gurgum lost further territory: Tarhulara was forced to cede cities to Panamuwa II, king of Sam'al (TSSI II: 14, 15). He is attested as tributary again in 732 and held his throne until c.711.
- **Muwatalli III** (c.711): son of Tarhulara; assassinated his father and seized the throne. Sargon II responded by deposing him and deporting him to Assyria. That ended Gurgum's existence as an independent state. Sargon annexed the kingdom and made it an Assyrian province named Marqas after its former capital.

## Why It Mattered

Gurgum's significance was primarily agricultural and strategic: its Maraş plain controlled routes from the Anatolian interior into northern Syria. The kingdom generally avoided direct military conflict with Assyria until 743, maintaining a long period of stability. Its extensive dynastic record — nine consecutive generations attested in hieroglyphic inscriptions — makes it one of the best-documented Neo-Hittite royal lines and a key reference point for reconstructing chronologies across the Neo-Hittite world. The shared name *Halparuntiya* with the contemporaneous [[unqi-pattin|Patin]] royal house may indicate family links between the two dynasties.

## Relationship to Assyria

Gurgum's first attested Assyrian contact (858 BCE) was tributary submission to Shalmaneser III. Bryce notes the dynastic members "appear to have had no dealings with" Assyria during the gap period of c.800–743, implying a long interval of independence. The 743 campaign brought Gurgum firmly back into Assyrian orbit. The final act — patricide by Muwatalli III — gave Sargon the pretext for annexation.

## Historiography

The reconstruction of the nine-generation dynasty depends heavily on cross-referencing three Luwian inscriptions with Assyrian tributary lists, a method that produces reliable absolute anchors at 858 BCE (Muwatalli II) and 853 BCE (Halparuntiya II) but leaves the earlier generations (before c.950 BCE) without independent dating. The possibility of dynastic descent from the Hittite Muwatalli II's line through Urhi-Teshub is speculative but noted by both Hawkins and Bryce. The identification of the city Hirika attacked by Halparuntiya II — whether in Cilicia or near Elbistan — remains disputed between Hawkins's and alternative views.
