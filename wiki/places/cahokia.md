# Cahokia

## Overview

**Cahokia**, on the Mississippi floodplain opposite modern St. Louis, was the
largest city of pre-Columbian North America — built in a deliberate "**big bang**"
around **1050 CE**, depopulated by ~1350, and forgotten by European contact. It is
the case study of [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] Ch. 21 (Pauketat, Alt &
Kruchten) and, with [[baghdad|Baghdad]] and [[jerusalem|Jerusalem]], an exemplar
of the **city as a creation** (Part V). Yoffee pairs it provocatively with
Jerusalem; nothing like it existed in North America before 1050, "nor would exist
again until the expansion of New York and Philadelphia after 1785."

## Form: a city of earth and wood

A tripartite "**capital zone**" (Cahokia + East St. Louis + St. Louis) sprawling
across ~20 km², with **191 earthen pyramids** (120 at Cahokia), the largest being
**Monks Mound**. Laid out on a deliberate **orthogonal grid** (offset 5° east of
north) anchored on a 20-hectare **Grand Plaza**, with planned neighborhoods and
astronomical alignments (some buildings to the 18.6-year lunar cycle). Population
~10,000–16,000 at Cahokia proper; perhaps **25,000–50,000** regionally. Up to the
mid-12th century the city was **open** — no walls.

## A planned creation

Pre-1050 "Old Cahokia" was a large village geared to small corporate-group
gatherings. At ~1050 it was rapidly **rebuilt** as "New Cahokia" — a single,
designed city. Contributing circumstances: a convergence of **immigrant** peoples
(identifiable from non-local pottery), a warm/wet climate ideal for bumper maize
crops, and a burst of **celestial activity** (the **1054 supernova**, comets).
The Grand Plaza alone took >10,000 person-days to raise; great **feasts** (Mound
51 — thousands of deer, pumpkin soup, tobacco) marked its founding.

## Religion, performance, and ruralization

New Cahokia was "fundamentally about proffering the cosmos to its citizens" — an
**axis mundi** of cosmic order, heir to a long eastern-North-American tradition of
ceremonial centers (Poverty Point, Hopewell). Its design was extended into the
**hinterland** ([[ruralization]]): reorganized farmlands, upland religious
complexes, lunar temples, and ritual outposts (Grossmann), knit by procession and
pilgrimage. Recurring **theatrical mortuary sacrifice** (Mound 72 — dozens of
mostly immigrant or captive young women) staged cosmic legends. See
[[ritual-and-urban-performance]].

## Why it was abandoned

A **palisade** went up ~1150; East St. Louis was **burned** (likely ritually)
~1160s; ritual architecture vanished region-wide after 1200; population fell to a
few thousand; by 1350 Cahokia was empty. Causes remain debated — loss of faith,
leadership failure, war, faction, drought, climate shift. The page notes the
[[pre-columbian-population-debate]] context for North American demography.

## Why this place mattered

Cahokia proves that **planned, populous, monumental urbanism arose independently
in North America** — a city consciously created, extended into a ruralized region,
and then deliberately unmade and forgotten.

## The deep background: a thousand years of mound-building

[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Begun & Brashler, Ch 20) supplies the long lead-up the
"big bang" emerged from. Cahokia was the climax of the eastern North American
[[north-american-mound-building|mound-building]] tradition stretching back through the
**Middle Woodland Hopewell** exchange/ceremonial complex (200 BCE–400 CE) to Late Archaic
**Poverty Point** (c. 3600 BCE). The immediate precursor was the **Late Woodland** period
(c. 400–950 CE): once dismissed as the dull "gray cultures," it is now seen as the era when
**maize agriculture became dominant** and **platform mounds** spread (common after 800 CE at
sites like Troyville and Toltec), signalling the shift from kin-and-ritual integration toward
the hierarchical, warring, urban **Mississippian** order that crystallized in the **American
Bottom around 950 CE** — and produced Cahokia a century later.
