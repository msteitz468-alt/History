## Description

Thang Long ("ascending dragon") was the royal capital of the Ly and Tran dynasties, founded on the site of Dai La, itself built by the Tang general Gao Pian in the 860s. It sits roughly 85 km almost due north of the earlier Hoa Lu capital, in the center of the riverine communication network of the Red River plain, surrounded by rice fields. This location — centrally placed amid an abundant plain, at the confluence of the plain's transport routes — was explicitly contrasted by [[ly-thai-to|Ly Thai To]] in his 1010 relocation edict with the cramped, defensible but marginal terrain of Hoa Lu, a limestone antechamber at the plain's southern edge.

Thang Long is the direct historical predecessor of modern [[hanoi|Hanoi]]; the modern city's name and administrative identity postdate this period (renamed in the nineteenth century), but continuous urban settlement runs from Dai La/Thang Long forward. Sites still identifiable in modern Hanoi trace to this era: the "One Pillar Pagoda" (Chua Mot Cot), built for **Ly Phat Ma** following a 1049 dream of Avalokitesvara; the site of the modern Hanoi cathedral, which occupies the location of a stupa built in **Ly Nhat Ton**'s reign; and the Van Mieu (Temple of Literature), whose 1070 predecessor shrine — built for the Duke of Zhou, Confucius, and his disciples — was retrospectively recast by fifteenth-century historians as an eleventh-century Confucian temple, though Taylor notes this is implausible since the first Van Mieu in China was not built until 1410.

## History of Control and Settlement

Founded 1010 when [[ly-thai-to|Ly Thai To]] moved the court from Hoa Lu within a year of taking the throne, citing the precedent of Shang and Zhou kings relocating capitals. A dragon reportedly rose above the king's boat on arrival, giving the city its name. The Ly royal family and Buddhist temple estates dominated the surrounding heartland of Giao, and Thang Long remained the political center through the Ly dynasty's rise and slow twelfth-century disintegration, including its sack and burning during the Tran Tu Khanh–Nguyen Non wars of the 1210s. Under the Tran dynasty from 1225 it remained the primary capital, though the Tran developed a dual-capital pattern with a secondary palace complex for the "senior king" near the Tran home estates (modern Nam Dinh area), some seventy kilometers downriver.

Thang Long was besieged and occupied twice by Mongol/Yuan forces during the three Mongol wars (1258, 1285) — each time evacuated in advance by the Tran court, who withdrew downriver rather than defend the city directly, then counterattacked to retake it (see [[mongol-invasions-of-dai-viet-1258-1288]]).

## Significance

Strategically, Thang Long's location gave the Ly and Tran direct control of the agricultural core of the Red River plain and its transport network, in contrast to Hoa Lu's more defensible but peripheral position oriented toward the southern provinces. Politically, it was the seat from which the Ly and Tran kings projected power outward — into the northern borderlands against Song/Yuan, and south against [[champa|Champa]]. Religiously, it hosted the dense concentration of Buddhist temples that underwrote both dynasties' legitimacy and administrative capacity.
