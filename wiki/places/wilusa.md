## Geographic Description

Wilusa was a kingdom in northwestern Anatolia that served as one of the westernmost Hittite vassal states. Its identification with the site of classical Troy — and with the Homeric (W)ilios — is now the dominant scholarly position, though absolute certainty is impossible given the fragmentary documentation. The name correspondence (Wilusiya/Wilusa ~ Ilios/Ilium) and its location in the northwest Anatolian sphere make the equation compelling. Its king Alaksandu, who concluded a treaty with [[hattusili-i|an earlier Hittite king]], has been compared with Homer's Alexandros (Paris). The Troad's strategic position at the entrance to the Hellespont gave it significance for controlling maritime traffic between the Aegean and the Black Sea.

## History and Control

Wilusa entered the Hittite orbit through the treaty with Alaksandu (attested in earlier chapters of Bryce's source), and remained a Hittite vassal state for at least a century. Relations were generally stable but periodically troubled. The clearest documentation of Wilusa's late history comes from the so-called Milawata letter, a document authored by [[tudhaliya-iv]] and partially reconstructed when Professor Harry Hoffner of the Oriental Institute discovered in 1981 that a Berlin fragment joined the original tablet.

During the later years of [[tudhaliya-iv]]'s reign, Wilusa's king Walmu was deposed and fled the country. He ended up in the custody of the addressee of the Milawata letter — most plausibly Tarkasnawa, king of Mira (also identified as the subject of the Karabel inscription and relief in the pass south of Smyrna). Since Walmu had apparently remained loyal to his Hittite overlord — possibly deposed because of that loyalty — Tudhaliya sought his restoration. He wrote to the addressee:

> "Now, my son, send Walmu to me, and I will install him as king again in Wilusa. And just as previously he was the king of Wilusa, now let him be so again!" (KUB xix 55 + KUB xlviii 90, rev. 38'–42', trans. Hoffner)

An envoy named Kuwalanaziti was sent with "wooden tablets" — documents confirming the legitimacy of Walmu's claim to the throne.

## Strategic and Administrative Significance

Wilusa's political significance in the Milawata letter lies partly in what it reveals about Hittite administrative innovation. Tudhaliya addressed Tarkasnawa (or whoever the correspondent was) as "my son" — indicating a marriage alliance and quasi-adoption — and apparently granted him the status of regional overlord over Wilusa and other western kingdoms. Walmu was described as a "kulawanis vassal" of this intermediary, as well as of Tudhaliya directly: "As Walmu was previously our kulawanis vassal, so let him (again) be a kulawanis vassal!" This represented "a distinct divergence from previous Hittite policy which gave no local ruler precedence over any other, and insisted that each deal directly with and be answerable exclusively to the Hittite king." The arrangement reflects Hatti's difficulty in projecting authority into the far northwest and its need to delegate control to a trusted regional power (Mira).

Wilusa was also connected to the broader Ahhiyawa question. The Seha River Land rebellion — backed by Ahhiyawan support — and the recovery of [[millawanda]] from Ahhiyawan control all formed part of the same western Anatolian crisis in which Wilusa's domestic troubles were embedded. The reestablishment of a pro-Hittite regime at Milawata likely improved conditions for restoring Walmu to his throne.

## Subsequent History

The fate of Wilusa after Walmu's presumed restoration is unknown. The kingdom disappears from the documentary record before the Bronze Age Collapse. Bryce notes that "a southward shift of some of the peoples" of the western Anatolian region probably occurred in the unsettled centuries following the Bronze Age Collapse, though major centers like Milawata survived and were absorbed into the Ionian Greek world. Whether Wilusa/Troy persisted as a settlement through the collapse is not addressed in this source.

## The Homeric Connection

The identification of Wilusa with Homeric Troy is not merely a name correspondence. The Alaksandu treaty (not analyzed in this source range) establishes that a king of Wilusa bore a name resembling Homer's Alexandros. The Hittite kingdom's documented difficulties in controlling the Troad, and the Ahhiyawan involvement in western Anatolian affairs, form a historical backdrop against which the Trojan War tradition may be assessed — though Bryce is appropriately cautious about any direct correlation.

## Historiography

Wilusa's identification with Troy was contested for much of the twentieth century. The text-join enabling fuller reading of the Milawata letter (Hoffner, 1981) significantly advanced knowledge of Wilusa's late history. The Karabel inscription — a hieroglyphic monument in a mountain pass — was identified by Hawkins as pertaining to Tarkasnawa of Mira, the probable addressee of the Milawata letter, providing a physical anchor for the administrative geography of late Hittite western Anatolia. Scholarly debate continues about whether the Walmu restoration was actually accomplished, and about the relationship between Hittite Wilusa and the city destroyed (in Troy VIIa) c.1180 BCE.
