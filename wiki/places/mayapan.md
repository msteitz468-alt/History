# Mayapan

## Geographic description

**Mayapan** (Spanish *Mayapán*) was the last major pre-Columbian political capital of northwestern **Yucatán**. It is a densely settled, **walled** city of >4,000 structures on about **4.2 km²**, with an estimated **~12,000** inhabitants (density ~**2,850/km²**). Architecture is often described as ramshackle scaled copies of **[[chichen-itza|Chichén Itzá]]** (including an El Castillo mimic); entries note **no ball courts, sweat baths, or corbel arches** of Classic type. The Carnegie Institution mapped and excavated the site **1949–1955**.

## Historical significance

### Political succession after Chichén Itzá (Evans & Webster 2001)

After the collapse of Chichén Itzá’s regional hegemony (variously placed ~**12th–mid-13th century** in encyclopedia entries), Mayapan rose as the dominant Yucatán city and assumed the political role formerly held by Chichén while Chichén continued as a major **religious pilgrimage** center. Founding is dated **c. A.D. 1200–1230** (small local center from the late eleventh century in some accounts; political capital from mid-thirteenth). Late ceramics of the **Hocaba** and **Tases** complexes relate to the period when the capital had moved here.

Tradition assigns **Itzá-Maya** and **Cocom** lineage rule with Tabasco “Mexican” mercenaries. The political economy was **tribute-based**, housing other polities’ ruling families as **hostages** within a confederated *multepal* (joint-rule) government of northwestern Yucatán ruling families. Economy was also strongly linked to **maritime trade**; **Cozumel** flourished **A.D. 1250–early sixteenth century** as a seaborne-trade way station closely linked with Mayapan, continuing after Mayapan’s fall until Cortés’s **1519** landing and disease/trade-route destruction.

### Collapse and fragmentation

Mayapan collapsed from **internecine warfare** among ruling groups. Dates in encyclopedia entries cluster **~1430–1450**: one entry places collapse **around 1430**; others give **~A.D. 1450** factional strife, military defeat, and slaughter of the Cocoms with destruction of the city. After defeat, Yucatecan society **broke apart into numerous small warring polities** loosely organized in provinces — the political landscape the Spanish first encountered roughly **half a century** after the fall.

## Why it mattered

Mayapan is the keystone of Late Postclassic northern lowland political order: successor to Chichén’s hegemony, model of *multepal* and hostage-tribute confederation, and the immediate pre-contact baseline for [[conquest-of-yucatan-1527]] and [[lowland-maya-colonial-modern]]. Its fall, not the Classic collapse, produced the fragmented *kuchkab’alob* the Montejos faced.
