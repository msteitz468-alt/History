# Durrington Walls

**Durrington Walls** is one of the major henge monuments of southern England, part of the remarkable group of third-millennium BCE ceremonial enclosures that includes Stonehenge, Avebury, and Mount Pleasant. It illustrates the scale of collective labor investment and the nature of late Neolithic / Chalcolithic ritual and social organization in Britain.

## Description

Durrington Walls is a large enclosure "bounded by a very large ditch and bank, with the ditch generally on the inside rather than the outside." Excavations revealed wooden circular structures within the enclosure. A plan (after Royal Commission on Historical Monuments, 1979) shows the layout with magnetic survey confirming features.

Together with Avebury and the great mound of Silbury Hill, these monuments represent "enterprises on a vastly greater scale" than earlier Neolithic enclosures across north-west Europe.

## Labor and Social Implications

The scale of construction has been used to infer forms of centralized organization:

> "Renfrew (1973) used calculations of the man-hours of labour involved in the construction of the monuments of the British neolithic to argue that those of the earlier neolithic were relatively modest undertakings which could have been accomplished by small-scale egalitarian groups, but that the monuments of the late neolithic, and the final phase of Stonehenge in the early Bronze Age, when the trilithons were erected, were several orders of magnitude greater in scale and must have involved some kind of centralized planning and control as well as a call on the manpower resources of a relatively wide area."

Subsequent work by Startin and Bradley (1981) produced lower total man-hour estimates but preserved the evidence of a major increase in scale. These projects required coordination beyond small egalitarian groups.

## Context in Settlement, Expansion and Socio-economic Change (3200–2300 BCE)

Durrington Walls and the southern English henges belong to the period of:

- Continued or intensified monument building in Britain (in contrast to the dispersal and disappearance of central places in Corded Ware zones further east).
- The later appearance of Bell Beaker-associated individual burial and prestige differentiation in western areas.
- The broader European pattern of agricultural expansion onto more marginal soils, increased reliance on domestic animals, and changing inter-community relations (exogamy, social storage, status display).

In Britain, the building of monuments "of various kinds continued at this time" west of the main Corded Ware distribution. The shift after the Beaker appearance saw "a drastic decline in the scale of monument building since the new form of social system operated in a very different fashion from the one which preceded it. Society became re-oriented around the control and consumption of prestige goods."

## Significance

Durrington Walls is a key exemplar of the "henge monuments" whose construction and use track the development and transformation of hierarchical organization in prehistoric Britain. It anchors discussions of centralized planning, ritual landscape, and the transition from collective monumentality to individual prestige-good burial (Wessex culture) in the early Bronze Age.
