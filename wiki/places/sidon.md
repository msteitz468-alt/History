# Sidon

**Sidon** (Phoenician *Ṣidon*) was, with **[[tyre|Tyre]]**, one of the two great
**[[phoenicians|Phoenician]]** cities — and in the **Achaemenid period the pre-eminent
one**, whose fleet led the Persian navy and whose kings enjoyed the widest autonomy of
any subject of the empire. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 3b, Eph'al) treats
its Persian-period role within the satrapy of [[eber-nari|Beyond the River]]; for the
earlier city see [[phoenicians]].

## Sidon under Persia

Among the four Phoenician city-states (Sidon, Tyre, Byblos, Aradus), Sidon was the
**foremost naval power** of the Persian empire in the west. Because the land-bound
Persians had "never adapted for naval warfare," the Phoenician fleets — the Sidonian
above all — were **indispensable and irreplaceable**, and Sidon's kings accordingly held
a status "involving a considerable degree of independence": they ruled as **hereditary
kings**, were not demonstrably answerable to Persian officials, and from the mid-fifth
century **struck their own silver coinage** (Sidon leading her sister cities by a few
years).

- **The Persian wars.** The **Sidonian contingent was pre-eminent** in Xerxes' fleet of
  480 (Herodotus repeatedly gives its king pride of place), and Phoenician ships fought
  on through the later struggles with Athens (Lade, the Eurymedon, the Egyptian
  expedition, Cyprus).
- **Eshmunazar's reward.** The **sarcophagus inscription of Eshmunazar II**, king of
  Sidon, records the grant to Sidon of **"Dor and Joppa, the great corn lands in the
  field of Sharon"** from "the Lord of Kings" (the Persian king), as a reward for
  "important deeds" — possibly the Sidonian fleet's service in Xerxes' campaign.
- **The Tennes revolt.** In the mid-fourth century, under **king Tennes**, Sidon led the
  great **Phoenician rising** (provoked by Persian officials' arrogance at the council
  of Tripolis and backed by Egypt). When Artaxerxes III took the field, **Tennes betrayed
  the cause**; the Sidonians sealed off and **burned their own city with themselves and
  their families** inside (Diodorus' exaggerated 40,000 dead). Even so Sidon was again a
  city "of some importance" when Alexander reached Phoenicia in 332, welcoming him with
  open arms.

## Why Sidon mattered

- **The naval key to the western empire.** Sidon's warships were the instrument with
  which Persia projected power across the Mediterranean and contested the Aegean against
  the Greeks — the maritime arm a continental empire could not build for itself.
- **An autonomous kingship.** Sidon exemplifies Eph'al's point that Persia ruled through
  **existing local structures**, leaving useful vassals their kings, cults and coinage.

## Historiography

Sidon's Persian-period history rests on **Greek narrative** (Herodotus, Diodorus, the
Alexander-historians), **Phoenician royal inscriptions** (the Eshmunazar and Tabnit
sarcophagi), and **coinage**. The dating of Eshmunazar II's reign — and hence whether his
"important deeds" mean Xerxes' campaign (480) or the mid-fifth-century wars — is disputed.
The scale of the Tennes catastrophe is likely overstated in Diodorus.
