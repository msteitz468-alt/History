# Etruria

**Etruria** is the homeland of the [[etruscans|Etruscans]] — the metal-rich,
volcanic country between the **Tiber and the Arno** on the Tyrrhenian side of the
central Italian peninsula (modern Tuscany, western Umbria and northern Lazio).
[[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Chs. 12–13, Ridgway) treats it as the seedbed
of the first cities in Italy outside the Greek colonies.

## Geography and resources

Etruria was, in Ridgway's words, "not a poor country." Its wealth rested on
**metals** — the iron of **Populonia** and the island of Elba, and the copper of
the *Colline Metallifere* ("ore-bearing hills") — and, increasingly through the
seventh and sixth centuries, on **agriculture**: large rural estates producing the
**wine and olive oil** that made Etruria a Mediterranean exporter. Its coast,
facing Sardinia, Corsica and Massalia, placed it at the heart of Tyrrhenian trade;
its rivers and the Apennine passes opened routes south to Latium and Campania and
north to the Po Valley.

## A landscape of cities and ports

Etruria was not a state but a **network of independent city-states**, each
typically paired with a coastal **port-emporion** that handled the Greek and
oriental trade while keeping the foreigners at arm's length:

- **Caere** (Cerveteri) with its ports **Pyrgi** (of the bilingual gold tablets)
  and Punicum;
- **Tarquinia** with **Graviscae**, where a resident Greek community worshipped
  Aphrodite, Hera, Apollo and Demeter;
- **Vulci**, a great centre of bronze and vase production (1,700 Attic vases in
  Beazley's indices);
- **Veii**, nearest to Rome, home of the sculptor Vulca and the Apollo of Veii;
- inland **Orvieto** (Volsinii), **Chiusi** (Clusium), **Volterra** and **Arezzo**;
- the northern metal towns **Vetulonia** and **Populonia**.

## Etruscan Italy (detailed synthesis from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004 Range 04, Rae Ostman entry; cache lines ~1719–2430)

**Iron Age origins (900–700 B.C.):** "The traditional Etruscan territory in central Italy is delineated by the Tyrrhenian Sea in the west, the Apennines in the east, and the Arno and Tiber Rivers to the north and south... During the Iron Age, the roots of Etruscan cities, economy, religion, and language were established." Villages on volcanic tufa plateaus (Veio, Cerveteri etc.) or hilltops; huts oval/rectangular 13x7 m or 5x4 m with central hearth, wattle-and-daub walls, thatched roofs. Cemeteries outside on hillsides; cremations in decorated urns with modest goods; 8th c. B.C. rich burials signal emerging aristocracy. Etruscan non-Indo-European language learned alphabet from Greeks ~700 B.C.; religion in sacred groves/caves/springs.

**Orientalizing period (700–575 B.C.):** Transition to town life; streets, drainage, reservoirs, fortifications, sanctuaries. Volterra small fortified settlement; circuit of walls. Princely residences e.g. Poggio Civitate (Murlo): rubble foundations, lime-plastered earthen walls, tiled roof with terra-cotta sculpture (seated "cowboy" figure), U-shaped around courtyard with workshops. Elite chamber tombs under large tumuli (30–40 m diameter); Tomb of the Chariots (Populonia). Earliest temple ~600 B.C. at Veio. Specialized agriculture, craft (bucchero pottery, granulation/filigree jewelry, bronze exports). New classes: merchants, manufacturers.

**Archaic and Classical periods (575–300 B.C.):** "The Etruscan civilization reached its greatest political and economic signiﬁcance... powerful Etruscan city-states developed and allied themselves in the League of Twelve Cities" (Cerveteri, Tarquinia, Vulci, Roselle, Vetulonia, Populonium, Veio, Bolsena, Chiusi, Perugia, Cortona, Arezzo, Fiesole, Volterra, Pisa). Oligarchic, patron-client. Expansion to Campania, Po valley, Adriatic. Roman Tarquin kings 616–509 B.C. Urbanization visible (Volterra 7 km walls enclosing 116 ha). Domestic: Acquarossa rectangular mud-brick houses on stone foundations, terra-cotta tile roofs, courtyard focus; Marzabotto regular grid plan (15 m main streets). Cemeteries grid-planned (Banditaccia Cerveteri, Crocefisso del Tufo Orvieto); Tomb of the Reliefs (Cerveteri, late 4th c.) stucco house interior with magistrate's ivory chair. Religion: Etruscan gods assimilated to Greek Olympians; monumental temples e.g. Portonaccio Veio mid-6th c. (square 18.5 m side, Tuscan columns, three cellae, terra-cotta Apollo statue). Sculpture: Cerveteri sarcophagus of reclining couple ~525 B.C.; Chimera of Arezzo (wounded lion/goat/snake, late 5th/early 4th c.). Painting: Tarquinia Monterozzi tombs (Tomb of the Leopards, early 5th c.). Economy intensifies (red figure local, black-slip tableware, bronze chariots/weapons). New classes erode traditional aristocracy.

**Classical and Hellenistic decline (470–31 B.C.):** Veio falls to Rome 396 B.C. after 10-yr siege; Battle of Sentinum 295 B.C. gives Rome supremacy. Peaceful relations after 270 B.C.; colonization, roads (via Aurelia etc.) 3rd–2nd c. 89 B.C. full Roman citizenship; Etruria Latin-speaking by 1st c. B.C. Northern cities like Volterra thrive with public works, lavish tombs (Inghirami Tomb). Legacy: "The symbols of Roman ofﬁce—the fasces... and the curule... are derived from Etruscan examples. The Romans adopted rituals of military triumph... The Roman toga originated as the Etruscan mantle. And many of the most famous architectural and engineering feats of the Romans... were ﬁrst achieved in Italy by the Etruscans."

Etruria is where the **city and the city-state first took root in Italy** beyond
Magna Graecia, and from which — via the Tarquins — they passed to **[[rome|Rome]]**.
Its ports were a principal western node of the Archaic
[[trade-network-formation|Mediterranean trade network]]; its mines made it a prize
that drew Greeks, Carthaginians and ultimately Rome. The progressive Roman conquest
— sealed by the destruction of **Veii in 396** — extinguished its political
independence, and with it the Etruscan language and dress.
