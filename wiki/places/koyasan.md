# Koyasan (Mount Koya)

Koyasan (Mount Koya) is a mountain plateau approximately 900 meters above sea level in Kii Province (present-day Wakayama Prefecture), southern Japan. It is the site of **Kongobuji**, the training monastery of the Japanese [[shingon|Shingon]] school, established by [[kukai|Kukai]] (Kobo Daishi) as a meditation retreat in 816 and the site of his tomb. After a period of decline in the late ninth and early tenth centuries, Koyasan was restored as Japan's most important Shingon pilgrimage center in the eleventh century, attracting Fujiwara regents and retired emperors in a pattern that confirmed its status as a sacred mountain of the first order. Source: [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]].

## Foundation

In 816 Emperor Saga approved Kukai's request for exclusive proprietary rights to the uninhabited mountain plateau of Koya in Kii Province, "on which he sought to establish a retreat for meditation and a monastery for the training of Shingon monks." Two disciples were already laying foundations when Kukai moved to Koya in 818, but he was recalled to Kyoto the following year, his presence at court deemed indispensable. The Kongobuji monastery complex grew from his disciples' work; it was accorded formal *jogakuji* (recognized temple) status in 835, the same year the Shingon school was admitted to the annual state ordination system. Kukai died at Koya in the third month of 835 and was interred there.

## The Toji-Koya Rivalry and the 919 Settlement

In 853 the Toji abbot Shinzei persuaded the court to shift the annual ordination examinations for Shingon novices from Koya to Toji, arguing that Koya's remoteness made it impractical. Koya had this decision reversed in 885 through the protests of Shinzen (Kukai's nephew, abbot of Kongobuji), but Toji regained priority in 897 when its abbot Yakushin argued that "Koya, 'a branch temple located in the mountains,' had usurped the prerogatives of Toji." A compromise around 907 distributed ten annual novices: four to Toji, three to Koya, three to Takaosanji.

The institutional resolution came in 919 when the court decreed that the Toji abbot (*choja*, "elder") would henceforth concurrently serve as overseer (*kengyo*) of Kongobuji, combining authority over both major Shingon centers in a single office. This prevented schism. However, since the combined abbot resided permanently in Kyoto, Koya's day-to-day administration fell to a resident "executor" (*shigyo*, appointed 928), later renamed "overseer" (*kengyo*, 983). Koya's abbot title *zasu* was not reinstated until 1875.

A major crisis occurred in 994 when lightning struck the main pagoda, causing a fire that almost completely destroyed Kongobuji. When the governor of Kii seized Koya's estates under the pretext of funding reconstruction, the few remaining monks were "compelled to quit the mountain completely."

## Restoration: Kishin, Ningai, and the Pilgrimage Tradition

Koya's restoration was led by two monks. **Kishin** (also known as Joyo) moved to Koya in 1016 following a vision in which "his spiritual eye was suddenly opened, enabling him to see that Kongobuji was none other than the Inner Palace of Tosotsu Heaven" — the celestial realm where the Future Buddha Miroku resides. After finding his parents transformed into bodhisattvas at Kukai's overgrown tomb, Kishin vowed to devote his remaining life to restoration. By his death in 1047, Kongobuji had achieved greater prosperity than ever before.

The prestigious **Ningai** (the "Rain Bishop," 951–1046, founder of the Ono branch of Shingon) promoted the perception of Koya as sacred mountain. In 1023 he persuaded former regent Fujiwara no Michinaga (then fifty-seven) to make the arduous journey to Koya, convincing him "that by making a pilgrimage to Koya, he would not only be sure to escape an unpleasant rebirth but would also be present when Miroku makes his descent into this world from Tosotsu Heaven." Michinaga spent five days in travel before reaching the **Okunoin** (Inner Sanctum) where Kukai was entombed, pledging the rebuilding of Kukai's mausoleum and donating land for Koya's upkeep.

This established a pattern of aristocratic and imperial pilgrimage that became a regular feature of Heian religious life:
- Fujiwara regents: Yorimichi (1048), Morozane (1081), Moromichi (1108), Tadazane (1144), Yorinaga (1148)
- Retired Emperor Shirakawa: three visits (1088, 1091, 1127)
- Retired Emperor Toba: three visits (1124, 1127, 1132)

Each aristocratic or imperial pilgrim contributed generously to reconstruction: Shirakawa formally ordered the rebuilding of the Great Pagoda (*Daito*) on his first visit and donated an estate in Bingo for its permanent upkeep; Toba sponsored the Western Pagoda (consecrated after his 1124 visit) and donated seven estates for training monks at Daidemboin (built 1132).

## The Deification of Kukai

By the end of the tenth century it was widely believed that Kukai had not died in 835 but had entered a deep trance (*nyujo*) from which he would emerge when Miroku descends from Tosotsu Heaven. When the abbot Kangen opened Kukai's tomb in 921 to read the court decree granting the posthumous name Kobo Daishi, he reportedly found the body intact, the skin warm, and the hair grown longer. Kukai, already a devotee of Miroku, was identified with him; Koya was seen as a terrestrial manifestation of Tosotsu Heaven. By the late eleventh century the equation advanced further: Kukai was being identified with the cosmic Buddha Dainichi himself, and Koya was described as the eternal Pure Land of Dainichi Nyorai (*Mitsugon Jodo*). When Retired Emperor Shirakawa in 1088 expressed a wish to visit Vultures' Peak in India, the court scholar Oe no Masafusa told him to go to Koya instead, "since Koya was none other than the eternal Pure Land of Dainichi Nyorai and hence vastly superior to Vultures' Peak in India."

The first lay burial on Koya — that of Mifukumon-in, consort of Emperor Toba, in 1160 — initiated what became a vast necropolis. (Women were barred from visiting the mountain during their lifetimes; Mifukumon-in worshiped daily toward Kukai's tomb from a hermitage about twenty kilometers away.) Koya now has more than 100,000 graves.

## Institutional Structure

By the end of the Heian period, Koya's institutional arrangements had crystallized. The resident overseer (*kengyo*) managed the monastery complex; the formal authority of the Toji choja was largely nominal. Daidemboin (1132), built by Retired Emperor Toba's sponsorship, became a major center for Shingon learning and training. Koya's independence from the Toji lineage proper was secured by the principle (established 1018 at the parallel Daigoji) that only monks actually trained at the specific complex could hold its top offices.

## Significance

Koyasan developed into the paradigmatic "sacred mountain" of Japanese religion — a model of the mountain retreat as spiritual center complementing but distinct from the city temple. Its remoteness, which had been a liability in the ninth-century Toji rivalry, became an asset as it was interpreted through the lens of Kukai's deification: distance from the corrupt world made it purer. The Okunoin, Kukai's burial site, remains today the most visited Buddhist pilgrimage site in Japan.

The Cambridge History's treatment emphasizes the interplay between Koya's claimed sanctity and its material dependence on aristocratic patronage — a patronage secured not through doctrine or institutional power but through the compelling narrative of Kukai's continued presence within his mountain.
