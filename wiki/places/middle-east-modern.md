# The Middle East in the Modern Era

## Overview

John Obert Voll's regional chapter in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] frames the
Middle East since 1750 as "a focal point of international relations... an area that
**emanates** international issues, not an area where they are merely played out."
Voll's analytical key is **"glocalism"** — the synthesis of the global and the local,
in which "outside forces" and "regional actors" are complementary rather than
opposed: European imperial policy was shaped by local responses, and local
developments (oil, religious movements, the Arab Spring) in turn shaped global
trends. He concentrates on the region's two most globally consequential dimensions:
the **strategic-political** (its central location and oil) and the
**religious-ideological** (its role in the global resurgence of religion).

## History of Control and Settlement

- **Eighteenth century:** Voll questions the "decline" narrative. The **Ottoman
  Empire** entered two decades of peace after 1750 and reformed under Selim III
  (r. 1789–1807); **Alawi Morocco** (whose dynasty still rules) became, in 1777, the
  first state to recognize the United States; in **Iran** the Safavids collapsed and
  the **Qajars** reunified the country, avoiding colonial conquest. These were not
  passive victims but allied, trading states.
- **Long nineteenth century — the Eastern Question:** the **Tanzimat** reforms
  (1839–1876) and Muhammad Ali's Egypt exemplify [[self-strengthening|self-strengthening]],
  pursued amid mounting European pressure and territorial loss (see [[imperialism]]).
- **World Wars era — mandates and secular republics:** the Ottoman collapse produced
  the British and French **mandates** (redrawing Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Palestine,
  Iraq); **Atatürk's** secular Turkish republic abolished the sultanate and caliphate;
  **Reza Shah** modernized Iran. Across the region, the post-WWI liberal
  constitutional revolutions gave way to authoritarian modernizers — a global pattern.
- **Cold War — independence and radicalism:** virtually all European-controlled areas
  gained independence after WWII (part of the global [[decolonization]] wave); the
  state of **Israel** was created (1948); old-style nationalists (the Wafd, Allal
  al-Fasi) gave way to revolutionary leaders, above all **Nasser** (1952 coup).
- **Contemporary:** authoritarian regimes, religious resurgence, and the **2011 Arab
  Spring** — national uprisings amplified by global social media (see [[contemporary]]).

## Why This Region Mattered

- **Oil.** The first Middle Eastern field opened in southwest Iran (Anglo-Persian Oil
  Company, 1908); the region supplied ~20% of world production by 1950. The era of
  Western company consortia (Aramco, 1948) gave way to **profit-sharing** (Saudi
  50-50, 1950) and, decisively, **Mossadegh's nationalization** of Anglo-Iranian oil
  (1951) — reversed by the 1953 US-backed coup but a signal that "local initiatives
  were reshaping global relationships." This prefigures OPEC.
- **Religion and identity.** Voll stresses that the **nation** was never the only
  political identity: Saudi Arabia rested on a religious identity, Israel on a Jewish
  homeland, Iraq and Transjordan on Hashemite monarchy. The region is central to the
  **global resurgence of religion** in "globalizing modernity," with Islamism becoming
  intertwined with anti-imperialism (see [[islam]], [[comparative-religion]]).
- **Strategic location** at the bridge of Asia, Africa, and Europe — the "Eastern
  Question," big-power competition, and the supposed "clash of civilizations."

## How Its Significance Changed

Voll narrates a shift from a region of reforming Islamic empires (Ottoman, Qajar) to
a zone of European mandates, to independent nation states, to a contemporary arena
where oil wealth and religious-ideological currents give it disproportionate global
weight — always through the **glocal** interplay of regional agency and global force.

## Historiography

Voll explicitly contests both the "decline" thesis and the view of the Middle East as
a passive object of "outside forces," insisting on its agency in shaping global
trends (the "glocal" frame, after Roland Robertson). This complements the
[[great-divergence|Great Divergence]] revisionism and the volume's broader
anti-[[eurocentrism|Eurocentrism]].

## The Arab world: living by others' rules (Rogan)

Eugene Rogan's [[rogan-the-arabs|*The Arabs: A History*]] supplies the Arab-centered narrative
the CWH chapter only sketches. Rogan dates **modern Arab history from the Ottoman conquest of
the Arab lands (c. 1500)** — when the Arabs first came to be ruled by an external power — and
argues that ever since they have negotiated the modern age **"by the rules set by the dominant
powers of the day,"** through four eras: the **Ottoman**, the **European colonial**, the **Cold
War**, and the present age of **US domination and globalization**. The Arabs were "most
empowered when there was more than one dominant power" to play off; each transition opened
opportunity but deepened foreign intrusion. Against this runs the **"Arab malaise"** (Samir
Kassir) — a sense of powerlessness — measured against two eras of greatness: the first five
Islamic centuries and the nineteenth-century cultural renaissance, the ***nahda***. Rogan
insists on Arab **agency** (responsible for "successes and failures alike") and on the recurring
gap between Western promises of "liberation" and the reality of occupation (Napoleon 1798;
Maude in Baghdad 1917; Bush 2003). The wiki's discrete Arab-world pages —
[[arab-nationalism]], [[colonial-partition-of-the-arab-world]], [[arab-israeli-conflict]],
[[gamal-abdel-nasser]], [[political-islam]] — rest on this account.
