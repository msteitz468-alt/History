## Geographic Description

New Guinea is the world's second-largest island, divided between its eastern half (now Papua New Guinea) and western half (now the Indonesian province of Papua). The eastern half was subdivided under colonial administration into:
- **German New Guinea** (north-east quarter of the mainland + Bismarck Archipelago + northern Solomons, 1884–1914)
- **Papua** (south-east quarter, British protectorate 1884; Australian Commonwealth territory 1906)
- The **Highlands** (interior; population ~1 million, unknown to Europeans until the 1930s Leahy expeditions)

The terrain — "one of the most rugged in the world" — severely constrained colonial penetration. "Most New Guineans did not notice a colonial state until the 1930s."

## History of Control

### German New Guinea and the New Guinea Company (1884–1914)

In 1884 the New Guinea Company was "commissioned to govern New Guinea — the north-eastern quarter, and the Bismarck Archipelago." Capitalised by Hamburg banker Adolph Hansemann, it "studied the Dutch East Indies, which inspired an elaborate scheme of plantations." The results were catastrophic: "malaria, which cut down company officers and forced the evacuation of one settlement," crop failures from tropical micro-organisms, and "fevers and dysentery which struck down Melanesian and Chinese labourers." "Only in the Gazelle Peninsula and the Duke of Yorks, where planters were already established, did plantations flourish."

In 1899 the company surrendered its mandate. Governor Hahl then "struggled to promote the collective interests of planters, to restrain their appetites for New Guinean labour, and to bring down the mortality rates of Chinese workers." By 1914 "a fairly prosperous plantation colony had been created in the Bismarck Archipelago."

### Papua under Australia (1906–1942)

Lieutenant-Governor Hubert Murray governed from 1906 through World War II. He "came to see himself as a governor in the British mould with primary responsibilities to Papuans." Under the White Australia policy: Asian labour barred, high shipping costs (Navigation Act required copra for Europe/Asia to route via Sydney, "nearly 2000 miles in the wrong direction"). European plantation acreage grew from 1,500 to 44,447 (1906–1914) then stagnated. Murray's Native Plantations Ordinance (1918) compelled village coconut planting; it did not transform Papua into a village export economy.

### German New Guinea to Australian Mandate (1914–1942)

Australian troops captured German New Guinea in 1914. Germans continued managing estates under military oversight, then were expropriated and replaced by Australian ex-servicemen. "Despite some war-time rhetoric about brutish German administration, the League of Nations Mandated Territory of New Guinea remained a plantation colony." "No More um-Kaiser . . . God Save um-King. The distinction was minimal."

Not until the 1930s did administrative control extend beyond the German occupation footprint. Gold discovery at Mount Kaindi (1926) transformed the economy: hundreds of Australian prospectors, thousands of New Guinean labourers. Bulolo Gold Dredging Ltd made Lae "the busiest airport in the world."

### The Highlands

The Leahy brothers' 1932–1933 expeditions, with government officer Jim Taylor and sixty carriers, crossed from Bena Bena into the Wahgi valley and west to Mount Hagen: "They found hundreds of thousands of people whose existence was previously known only to themselves, and whose lives revolved around intensive agriculture and exchanges." The administration used its Uncontrolled Areas Ordinance to limit prospector and missionary influx.

## Economic Significance

New Guinea was "the largest block of land in the Pacific, with the richest resources." Copra was the staple export until gold supplanted it. Gold from Edie Creek (1926) and Bulolo produced "more than 110,000 ounces" in 1927 alone. Yet the economy was "lethargic" by Pacific standards until the 1930s, constrained by White Australia policies (barring Asian labour), Australian Navigation Act shipping costs, and limited capital. Bulolo was "a classic enclave development . . . remitting most earnings abroad, and employing New Guineans who saved little or foreigners who repatriated their savings."

## Labour and Colonial Administration

The "internal labour trade" within New Guinea signed "an estimated 280,000 indentures . . . between the world wars." Head tax (10 shillings/year) was designed to force labour supply. *Kiaps* (patrol officers) spent much of their time supervising labour indentures. The decisive authority on patrol was often the senior New Guinean policeman; single policemen at remote posts "necessarily involved themselves in local politics, usually forming an alliance by marrying into a leading clan."

The Rabaul Strike (1929): the single most significant collective labour action, organised by Sumsuma (a ship's captain) and senior policemen. Strikers demanded a minimum wage of £12 (nearly fifty times the prevailing rate). "Charges were discovered or invented, prison terms imposed, and prisoners drafted to carry cargo on the goldfield track, or to labour there, and most were savagely beaten while awaiting trial or during their sentences."

## Depopulation

Governor Hahl's 1907 report: "the population is sparse, of inferior quality and diminishing: malaria and dysentery are present everywhere; high freight rates have to be paid." New Guinea was less severely depopulated than Polynesia: Melanesia generally received fewer European whalers and explorers, conditions were less favourable for the initial spread of venereal disease, and frontier contact with the Highlands was delayed until the 1930s — by which time tuberculosis was controllable. The Papua New Guinea Labour Scheme "did not produce a catastrophe, because the Public Health Department enforced immunisation before recruitment."
