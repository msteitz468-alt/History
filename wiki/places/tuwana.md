# Tuwana

**Tuwana** (Assyrian: Tuhana; Classical: Tyana) was "the largest and most important kingdom of the southern Tabal region" (Bryce 2012), occupying the territory of the Classical Tyanitis in south-central Anatolia. Its strategic value derived above all from its proximity to the **Cilician Gates** — the principal mountain pass linking the Anatolian plateau to Cilicia and the Levant — making it a critical hinge point for movement between Anatolia and the rest of the Near East.

## Geographic Description

Tuwana occupied the southern portion of the [[tabal|Tabal]] complex, corresponding to the Classical Tyanitis. Its capital was probably at Classical Tyana (modern Kemerhisar), 20 km south-west of Niğde, though at an earlier period the royal seat may have been at **Nahitiya** on the site of Niğde itself. The kingdom's territory extended south towards the Taurus range; an inscription found in situ on an outcrop of rock in the Taurus near the Cilician Gates (BULGARMADEN) marks its southern frontier zone. The sub-kingdom of Tarhunaza, attested as a vassal of Warpalawa, was located on "Mt Muti," probably near the site of Porsuk, c. 10 km north-west of the Bulgarmaden inscription.

The kingdom derives its name from Late Bronze Age **Tuwanuwa**, one of the cities of the Hittite Lower Land. "Conceivably, the kingdom arose in the wake of the Hittite empire's fall, with a population perhaps largely made up of Luwian elements from Tuwanuwa" (Bryce 2012).

## Dynasty and Rulers

Tuwana's dynastic history is partially recoverable from Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions, cross-referenced with Assyrian records. The royal line may extend back before the earliest attested figures:

### Warpalawa (II)

Warpalawa is the pivotal figure of Tuwana. He first appears among Tiglath-pileser III's five tributary kings of the Tabal region (738/732 BCE) and is last attested c. 709 in a letter from Sargon II's governor Ashur-sharru-usur to the Assyrian king. His long reign — perhaps 35 years — was "probably due to a policy of ostensible cooperation with the Assyrians, and notes the strikingly Assyrianizing style of sculpture on his surviving monuments" (Hawkins, cited in Bryce 2012).

The most celebrated monument of his reign is the **İvriz rock relief**, 17 km south of Ereğli, depicting Warpalawa offering prayers to the Luwian Storm God Tarhunza. "The king himself is endowed with distinctly Assyrian features," yet wears a robe and fibula considered by some scholars to show Phrygian characteristics — though this interpretation has been disputed. The monument illustrates the cultural triangulation of Tuwana between Luwian tradition, Assyrian suzerainty, and Phrygian influence.

Warpalawa also appears in the TOPADA inscription of Wasusarma of Tabal "Proper" as one of three allied kings who supported Wasusarma against eight enemy rulers near the city of Parzuta. His kingdom included at least one sub-kingdom: Tarhunaza, who held the title "Ruler" (IUDEX) and acknowledged Warpalawa as overlord, controlled the territory around the Cilician Gates (BULGARMADEN inscription, CHLI I: X.45).

Warpalawa is known to have met with an envoy from Phrygia in the presence of the Assyrian governor — raising Ashur-sharru-usur's suspicions about his loyalty — but "none of the texts relating to his reign give any indication that he ever broke his allegiance to Assyria" (Bryce 2012).

### Old Phrygian Inscriptions in Tuwana

Old Phrygian inscriptions on basalt slabs ("Black Stones") have been found at Tuwana/Tyana, among the earliest known Old Phrygian texts (generally dated late 8th to 3rd century BCE). One may contain the name Midas, which some scholars take as evidence that the Phrygian king Midas physically penetrated Neo-Hittite territory and set up a monument in the city "of a Luwian friend and ally" (Mellink, cited in Bryce). Bryce is cautious: there is no hard evidence Warpalawa was ever an ally of Midas, and any Phrygian inscriptions at Tuwana may belong to the post-705 BCE vacuum following Sargon's death in the Tabal region.

### Muwaharani (II) and End of Independence

Muwaharani II, son of Warpalawa, is known from a single inscribed stele at Niğde, dedicated to the Storm God Tarhunza — described as "the latest securely datable Neo-Hittite inscription and sculpture apart from the Karatepe examples" (Hawkins, cited in Bryce 2012). By his reign, Tuwana was probably already under direct Assyrian rule, formalized through Sargon's administrative arrangements following his removal of Ambaris from Bit-Burutash in 713. Ashur-sharru-usur, appointed as governor of Adanawa (Que), had authority extending to Tuwana and other southern Anatolian regions.

## Strategic Importance

Control of Tuwana meant control of the southern approaches to the Cilician Gates. This gave the kingdom leverage over trade and military movement between Anatolia and the Levant, making it one of the most strategically situated of all the Tabal kingdoms. Sargon II died in 705 campaigning against Cimmerians in the Tabal region; Tuwana would have been among the territories exposed to subsequent instability.

## Relationship to Assyria and Urartu

Tuwana was a client-state of Assyria through the reigns of Tiglath-pileser III, Shalmaneser V, Sargon II, and possibly Sennacherib. The communications preserved between Sargon and Ashur-sharru-usur about Warpalawa's kingdom (SAA I: no. 1) reveal an increasingly direct Assyrian administrative hand in Tuwana's affairs. Urartu posed a background threat, and Bit-Burutash's conspiracy with Urartu and Phrygia (which brought Sargon's crackdown in 713) immediately affected the regional balance around Tuwana.

## Historiography

Tuwana's history depends on the Luwian hieroglyphic corpus (CHLI I, ed. Hawkins 2000), cross-referenced with Assyrian annals (Tigl. III; SAA I). The question of whether there was a "Warpalawa I" earlier in the dynasty is explicitly flagged by Bryce as "highly conjectural." The identity of the bearded figure in the Kızıldağ relief — attributed by Hawkins to Wasusarma but by Bryce to Ambaris — is unresolved. The significance of Old Phrygian inscriptions at Tuwana, and specifically the alleged name "Midas," remains debated: Bryce concludes the evidence for a Phrygian political presence under Warpalawa II is insufficient.
