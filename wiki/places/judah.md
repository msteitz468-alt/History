# Judah (the Persian Province of Yehud)

## Overview

**Judah** — the small Persian province of **Yehud** around Jerusalem — was, under
Achaemenid rule (539–332 BCE), the crucible in which **Second Temple Judaism** took
shape. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 8c, Hayim Tadmor) treats its **internal
development** (the regional-survey companion to [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH IV]]'s account
of Judah within the empire), drawing on Ezra–Nehemiah, the **Elephantine papyri**, the
**Samaria (Wadi Daliyeh) papyri**, coins and seals.

## The Return and the Temple

After Cyrus conquered Babylon (539), his **edict** (538) permitted the Judaean exiles to
**rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem** from royal funds — an act consistent with his wider
policy of restoring local cults (see [[achaemenid-empire]]). Successive waves of returnees
came under **Sheshbazzar** "the prince of Judah" and then **Zerubbabel** (both
Davidic-line governors, *peha*), and the **Second Temple** was completed under **Darius
I** (520–515), urged on by the prophets **Haggai** and **Zechariah**. Many exiles chose to
remain in a prosperous Babylonian diaspora. Judah was governed not as a vassal kingdom but
as a **province under a governor**: "just as Cyrus was king of Media, Elam and Babylonia,
he was also king of Judah."

## Ezra and Nehemiah: the constitution of Second Temple Judaism

The transformative figures are the priest-scribe **Ezra** and the governor **Nehemiah**
(mid-fifth century). Tadmor presents their work as the foundation of post-exilic Judaism:

- **Nehemiah** rebuilt the **walls of Jerusalem** (with imperial authorization), restoring
  the city as a defensible cult-centre, and as governor enforced social and religious
  reform.
- **Ezra's public reading of the Law** before the assembled people — with Levites giving
  an **Aramaic translation and explanation**, the people responding "Amen, Amen" — is "the
  first record in Jewish history of a ritual revolving around the **Law** rather than
  around sacrifices," the model for synagogue worship throughout the Second Temple period
  (later tradition makes Ezra a second Moses).
- The **covenant** (Nehemiah 10) extended the ban on **mixed marriages**, codified strict
  **Sabbath** observance (a diaspora badge of identity), and fixed Temple dues (the
  one-third—later half—shekel tax, the wood-offering) — measures derived by interpretation
  beyond the literal Pentateuch, "the beginning of... the Oral Law."

## The Samaritan schism

Judah's defining rupture was with **Samaria** to the north — a province whose population
(partly the descendants of peoples the Assyrians settled after 720, partly the old
Israelite remnant) also worshipped the God of Israel. Despite cooperation between the
Samarian governor **Sanballat** and the Jerusalem priesthood (sealed by intermarriage),
the **separatist policies of Ezra and Nehemiah** — Nehemiah evicting Sanballat's
son-in-law, a son of the High Priest, from the Temple — drove the two apart. The final
**rupture came in the fourth century**: a later **Sanballat (III)**, with permission said
to be Alexander's, built a **rival temple on Mount Gerizim**, where his son-in-law
**Manasseh** became High Priest. The **Samaritans**, claiming to be the true Israel,
became a permanently distinct national-religious community.

## Significance

In the Persian province of Yehud, a small, politically negligible community produced
something of world-historical consequence: a **scripture-centred, exclusivist monotheism**
robust enough to survive without statehood. The shift from Temple sacrifice toward **the
Law and its interpretation**, the hardening of communal boundaries (mixed marriage,
Sabbath), and the Samaritan schism set the framework for **Judaism, Samaritanism**, and —
in the longer run — Christianity and Islam.
