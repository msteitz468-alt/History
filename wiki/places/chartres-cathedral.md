# Chartres Cathedral

## Geographic and cultic setting

**Chartres Cathedral** (Notre-Dame de Chartres) stands in the town of Chartres, southwest of Paris in the royal Île-de-France sphere. Its fame rested on the relic of the **Virgin’s tunic** (legendarily the gift of Charles the Bald), kept in the crypt; after the fire of 1194 the survival of the tunic made reconstruction a **national pilgrimage undertaking**.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

On the night of **9–10 July 1194** fire destroyed the Romanesque cathedral. The mid-twelfth-century **west façade and Royal Portal windows** largely escaped; a new **Gothic** church was built behind that façade and was essentially complete by **c.1220**. Logan stresses the social scale of the rebuild: townsmen hauled stone carts to the towers; stained glass was funded by royal and guild donors (including **Queen Blanche** and the duke of Brittany). The crypt relic’s survival made Chartres the **principal French pilgrimage site** of its day.

Logan situates Chartres within a building boom: between **1180 and 1270**, France alone raised about **80 cathedrals and 500 abbey churches**. Chartres thus stands not as an isolated masterpiece but as a flagship of a civilizational construction wave that carried [[gothic-architecture|Gothic]] from the Île-de-France across Latin Europe. He lists Chartres with Amiens, Beauvais, Bourges, Laon, Paris Notre-Dame, and Rheims among representative French Gothic churches in an international style that stretched “from Dublin … to Gdansk and Cracow … from Trondheim and Uppsala … to Seville and Milan.”
