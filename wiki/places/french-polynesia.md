## Geographic Description

French Polynesia comprises five island groups—the Austral, Gambier, Marquesas, Society, and Tuamotu archipelagos—administered from Pape'ete on Tahiti. The territory spans a vast area of the South Pacific, with a total land area small relative to its enormous exclusive economic zone. The Tuamotu Archipelago—low coral atolls stretching across hundreds of kilometres—contains the nuclear test sites of Moruroa and Fangataufa. The Society Islands, dominated by Tahiti, form the demographic and political centre of the territory. Tahiti's sister island Moorea lies nearby; together they held 74 per cent of the population by 1967.

## History of Control and Settlement

The Maohi (Polynesian) people had inhabited the islands for centuries before European contact. Tahiti became famous in Europe through the voyages of Cook and Bougainville. France completed annexation in 1880, and the territory became the *Établissements français d'Océanie* (EFO), a colonial backwater of little significance to metropolitan France until the 1960s.

After World War II, France, like other imperial powers, initially moved toward self-determination: the 1946 constitution recognised overseas peoples' right to develop their civilisation, granted French citizenship, and allowed political parties. French Polynesia made "slow but steady moves towards greater autonomy," culminating in the Defferre Law of 1957 granting partial autonomy. Independence seemed a distinct possibility.

This trajectory was reversed by France's decision to test nuclear weapons in the Tuamotu Archipelago. When President de Gaulle announced the creation of the Centre d'Expérimentation du Pacifique (CEP) in July 1962, "there had been no consultation with territorial representatives." France combined the strategic decision to test with massive economic transfers designed to buy acquiescence. The EFO became "French Polynesia" under the 1958 Fifth Republic constitution, an overseas territory rather than a colony.

## Political Significance

French Polynesia's post-war political history was dominated by the interplay of nuclear dependency, nationalist aspiration, and French patronage. The key figure was **Pouvanaa a Oopa** of Huahine, a Polynesian nationalist who had fought in both World Wars and whose party, the *Rassemblement Démocratique des Populations Tahitiennes* (RDPT), won consistent electoral majorities from 1949 to 1957, campaigning for expanded autonomy and economic reform for the Maohi people. The RDPT's programme included expanded access to education, public-sector jobs, land reform, and social security—"all in favour of the Maohi people."

In 1958, as de Gaulle returned to power and France moved toward testing in the Tuamotu Archipelago, Pouvanaa campaigned for independence in the September referendum, earning 36 per cent (against the required simple majority for Yes). Within months he was arrested on dubious arson charges, jailed for eight years, and exiled for fifteen—a political imprisonment. When the RDPT continued to threaten France's strategic plans, de Gaulle dissolved it in 1963 under an archaic law protecting "national integrity."

The CEP's arrival in 1966 created an "economy of transfer": French military and civil spending peaked at 76 per cent of GDP in the construction period of the mid-1960s. By 1990 France supplied 75 per cent of French Polynesia's external income (35 per cent from the military). The territorial economy became the most financially dependent in the Pacific: exports covered 90 per cent of imports in the 1950s but only 9 per cent by 1965. By the early 1990s the territory produced merely one quarter of its food.

Pouvanaa returned to a hero's welcome in November 1968 after a presidential pardon, won the National Senate election in 1971 with two-thirds of the vote, became president of the Territorial Assembly in 1972, and delivered his inaugural speech in Tahitian—unprecedented. At 77 he was "still setting the nationalist agenda." The autonomist parties maintained electoral pressure through the 1970s, eventually forcing France to grant internal autonomy in 1977 (limited) and again in 1984 (statute of Internal Autonomy), while France retained control of foreign relations, the 200-mile exclusive economic zone, immigration, law and order, and defence.

French testing ran from 1966 to 1996: atmospherically until 1975, then underground, then offshore beneath the Moruroa lagoon from the 1980s. A series of accidents—including a 1979 blast that produced a subterranean landslide and tidal wave—led the Territorial Assembly unanimously to demand suspension and independent inquiry; France refused. When President Chirac announced a final test series in 1995, Pape'ete saw its largest riots since 1973: demonstrators stormed the international airport terminal and rampaged through the city. France completed eight tests and then signed the South Pacific Nuclear-Free Zone Treaty in 1996.

## Why French Polynesia Mattered

**Strategic**: France valued the territory primarily as a nuclear test site once Algeria's independence removed the Sahara option. The CEP was "France's primary strategic interest" in the Pacific.

**Economic dependence as control**: French transfers were explicitly designed to "buy acquiescence"—to prevent the emergence of an effective independence movement that would cripple nuclear testing. *"Who pays, controls,"* Prime Minister Rocard stated in 1989.

**Assimilation**: A French-only language policy from 1860 to 1971 in state schools, combined with French television reaching 90 per cent of households by the 1990s (81 per cent with sets), and a rapid urban migration driven by CEP employment, accelerated the disintegration of Maohi language and culture. A linguist estimated in 1992 that in Tahiti "only the older generation had competency in Tahitian while those under thirty had a fragile knowledge and teenagers were hard pressed to speak Tahitian without substantial additions of French."

**Legacy**: The years of nuclear testing left "not only contaminated atolls but, more significantly, economic, political and cultural absorption of the territory into France itself." Independence remained a minority cause even after 1996: Gaston Flosse's pro-French Tahoeraa party easily won the 1996 territorial election. Yet the independence movement was "far from defunct"—the September 1995 riots had shown that. Oscar Temaru's Tavini Huiraatira party, anti-nuclear and pro-independence, held Faa'a municipality and won its first Territorial Assembly seats in 1986.

## Historiography

Coverage of French Polynesia in this source draws primarily on work by von Strokirch (*Tahitian Autonomy: Illusion or Reality?* and *The Impact of Nuclear Testing on Politics in French Polynesia*), Grand (on Pouvanaa), Danielsson and Danielsson (*Poisoned Reign*), and Finney (*French Polynesia: A Nuclear Dependency*). The tight French security around the test program means contamination data remains incomplete. Internal territorial politics are well documented through electoral records and the writings of political participants; the health and environmental consequences of testing are inadequately documented owing to French restrictions on independent monitoring.
