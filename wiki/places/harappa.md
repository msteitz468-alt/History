# Harappa (Indus Valley)

## Evolutionary Position (Yoffee 2005)
"the Indus Valley civilization is an example of archaic sociocultural complexity, but without the state." (Possehl 2002 via Yoffee)

Reasons: no consensus on "the definition of the archaic state"; no kings/palaces/bureaucracies/"other trappings of ‘stateness’." Yet cities, differentiation, large public ceremonial areas, record-keeping, and countryside restructuring around "politically independent cities that shared a common ideology."

Yoffee: "Possehl rightly rejects Mesopotamia as the model for the archaic state."

"Indus Valley city-states look different from Mesopotamian city-states. They were ruled differently and seem to have had different rules about how power was to be exhibited in them and, presumably, about how power was to be contested. Their development and collapse were also different from what they were in Mesopotamia."

## Avari (2007): Harappa Specifically

Harappa is located in western Punjab (modern Pakistan). It was one of the five first-tier Harappan settlements, covering approximately 100 hectares (250 acres). It gave its name to the entire Harappan Civilisation — the term "Harappan," Avari notes, "referring to the great city-settlement of Harappa in the Punjab, is frequently used to describe collectively the styles and features of monuments and artefacts from the entire Harappan/Indus Civilisation."

**Discovery history:** The first Western visitor was Charles Masson (real name James Lewis), an English soldier who had deserted from the East India Company's army. In the 1820s he described the site as "a ruinous brick castle" with high walls and towers, noting a large number of fine, standard-size bricks. Lieutenant Alexander Burnes subsequently visited and calculated the site at 3 miles in circumference. Sir Alexander Cunningham (first director general of the Archaeological Survey of India) made three expeditions, two in the 1850s and a third in 1873. By 1873 he found that the British engineers building the railway line between Lahore and Multan had been using the ancient bricks as ballast, significantly depleting the site. Cunningham's survey produced the first seal: "a seal made of unpolished but smooth black stone, on which was deeply engraved a bull, without hump, with two stars under the neck, along with an inscription in six characters" — now in the British Museum. Systematic excavation began in 1920–25 under Dahya Ram Sahni, one of John Marshall's deputies.

**Distinguishing features (per Avari):** Unlike Mohenjo Daro, Harappa does not have a citadel mound but possesses a unique set of circular brick platforms, "supposedly for threshing grain." Harappa and Mohenjo Daro together account for approximately 85 per cent of all Harappan inscribed objects (~4,200 total). Measuring scales with finely graduated divisions for standardised weights and measures have been found at Harappa as well as Lothal and Mohenjo Daro.

**Post-Harappan:** After the urban collapse (c. 1900–1700 BCE), the Cemetery H Culture developed in the Punjab, continuing some Harappan traditions at the village level.
