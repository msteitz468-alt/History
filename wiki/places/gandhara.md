# Gandhara and the Achaemenid Indus Satrapies

**Gandhara** (Old Persian *Gandara*) and its neighbours formed the **easternmost
provinces of the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid empire]]** — the furthest extent of
Persian rule, reached down the **Indus** in the reigns of Cyrus and Darius. This is
the world-historical fact the page records: the Persian empire was an **Indian power**
as well as a Mediterranean one, its frontier the Indus a full two centuries before
[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] crossed it. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]]
(Ch. 3d, A. D. H. Bivar) is the source.

## The Indus borderlands under Persia

The Achaemenid east comprised a cluster of provinces strung along the Indus and its
western approaches:

- **Gandara (Gandhara)** — the Peshawar valley around **Puskalavati** (west of the
  Indus) and, east of it, **Taxila** and Kashmir; capital region of the satrapy.
- **Thatagush (Sattagydia)** — west of the middle Indus, perhaps around Bannu (Akra
  Dheri), "having hundreds of cattle."
- **Hindus (Hi<sup>n</sup>dush, "India")** — the province Darius organized **c. 515
  BCE** after Scylax's reconnaissance; located either in lower **Sind** (the Indus
  delta) or at **Taxila / West Punjab**. It rendered the empire's most fabulous
  tribute: **360 talents of gold dust** (Herodotus III.94) — a sum Bivar argues must
  mean gold *to the value of* 360 silver talents, since the eastern treasuries kept
  accounts in bulk silver. Herodotus's tale of giant gold-digging "ants" attaches to
  it; gold was indeed panned from the upper Indus and its tributaries.
- **Pactyica** — the tribal Kabul-river highland (Bivar links the name, against some
  philologists, to the modern **Pakhtuns/Pathans**); its **Aparytae** recall the
  Afridis, its **Dadicae** the Daradas.

The whole eastern group paid only **170 talents** as a tribal bloc (Gandarans,
Sattagydians, Dadicae, Aparytae), a low assessment marking their largely tribal
character — except for the rich Hindus province.

## The voyage of Scylax and the conquest of the Indus

Cyrus the Great had earlier campaigned through **Arachosia** (capturing Capisa /
Begram, Pliny *HN* VI.92) on his way to the Jaxartes and his death (530), attaching
the trans-Hindu-Kush lands to the empire. It was **[[scylax-of-caryanda|Scylax of
Caryanda]]**, a Carian Greek in Darius's service, who in **c. 517 BCE** sailed the
Indus from near **Peshawar** (Herodotus's "Caspatyrus," better *Paskapyrus*) down
through the Attock gorge to the sea and round to Egypt (Hdt. IV.44) — and "after the
transit, Darius subjugated the Indians." The **Persepolis Fortification Tablets**
confirm a lively two-way traffic c. 500–498 BCE between Susa and Gandara, Arachosia
and Hindus: named satraps (Irdubama of Hindus, Megabazus of Arachosia), elite guides,
and high-ranking Indian travellers such as **Abbatema** moving on the royal road with
sealed authority.

## Why these lands mattered

- **The empire's true eastern limit.** Gandara, Sattagydia and Hindus mark the Indus
  as the Achaemenid frontier — the eastern counterpart of the empire's collision with
  the Greeks in the west; Gandaran and Indian troops, and Indian war-dogs, marched in
  **Xerxes' army of 480** (the Gandarans led by a royal cousin, Artyphius).
- **Early Indian money.** The region's **bent-bar coinage** (a double Achaemenid
  *siglos* standard) and the **punch-marked silver** of the [[magadha|janapadas]] show
  monetized exchange spreading along the Iran–India axis; Indian numismatists have
  even claimed priority over the [[coinage|Lydian invention of coinage]], though the
  dating is insecure.
- **A cultural hinge.** Persian, Indian and (later) Greek worlds met here; the
  **Kambojas** of the borderland spoke an Iranian dialect, and some link their name to
  **Cambyses** and a possible Persian colonization.

## Historiography

A reconstruction built from **fragmentary and derivative sources** — the
Alexander-historians (Arrian, Curtius), Pliny, **Herodotus** (whose eastern
geography and tribute-list are corrupt and dislocated), the **Bisitun inscription**,
the **Persepolis Fortification Tablets**, and scattered **numismatic finds** (the Mir
Zakah and Chaman Huzuri hoards). Bivar stresses how thin and "all but unknown" the
archaeology of Sind and the trans-Indus remains: the exact location of the Hindus
satrapy, the meaning of the early coinages, and the degree of real (versus nominal)
Achaemenid control toward 500 BCE all stay open. The chronology of the contemporary
Indian kings ([[magadha|Bimbisara, Ajatashatru]]) is itself entangled with the
disputed dates of the [[buddha|Buddha]].

## Gandharan Art and the Buddha Image (Benjamin 2018)

Under the [[kushan-empire|Kushans]] (1st–3rd c. CE) Gandhara became one of the two great
centers — with Mathura — of a new Buddhist sculpture that, in
[[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin's]] account, transformed Buddhism into a
world religion. Working in hard **gray schist**, Gandharan sculptors fused Hellenistic
naturalism (Apollo statues, Roman imperial portraits like [[augustus|Augustus]]) with
Buddhist subjects to create the **first anthropomorphic images of the Buddha** — before
this the Buddha had been shown only by symbols (footprints, umbrella). The new iconography
spread along the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] as far as Sri Lanka, Korea, and Japan (see
[[spread-of-buddhism]]). The style is so distinctive that Aurel Stein could trace Buddhism's
diffusion archaeologically — recognizing Gandharan drapery in Buddha sculptures at **Miran**
near Lop Nor. Gandharan stair-riser reliefs (Kushan aristocrats, musicians, dancers in a
mix of Indian, Romano-Hellenistic, and steppe-nomadic dress) are a vivid record of Silk
Roads cultural synthesis. Source: [[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018]].
