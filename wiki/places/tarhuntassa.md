## Overview

Tarhuntassa was a city in southern Anatolia — located in the region the classical world would later call Cilicia — that achieved sudden and dramatic political prominence when [[muwatalli-ii|Muwattalli II]] transferred the entire seat of Hittite royal power there from [[hattusha|Hattusa]], probably during the middle years of his reign (c.1295–1272 BCE). Its prior history as a settlement is obscure: Bryce notes that "we have no certain references to Tarhuntassa before the royal seat was established there," marking it as an unlikely candidate — apparently insignificant and largely unknown — before Muwattalli's radical decision.

## History of Control and Settlement

### Muwattalli's Transfer of the Capital

The transfer of the Hittite capital from Hattusa to Tarhuntassa was, in Bryce's terms, "a plan of astonishing boldness — one totally without precedent in the history of the Hittite kingdom." It was no mere temporary relocation. Muwattalli removed the state deities and ancestral spirits of [[hittites|Hatti]] wholesale, signalling permanence:

> "When, however, my brother Muwattalli at the command of his (patron) deity went down to the Lower Land, leaving the city of Hattusa, he took the gods and the ancestral spirits of Hatti . . . and he brought them down to the city of Tarhuntassa and made it his place of residence."
> (Apology of Hattusili, CTH 81, §6, i 75–ii 1–2, §8, ii 52–3)

Muwattalli appears to have assumed the title "Great King of Tarhuntassa" after the transfer. Hattusa did not cease to exist — a functionary called Mittannamuwa (later his son Purandamuwa) was left to administer the old capital — but its status was drastically reduced.

### Motivations

Bryce identifies two principal motives, both rooted in strategic calculation:

1. **Proximity to Syria.** With Egypt's resurgent nineteenth dynasty posing a direct military challenge to Hittite holdings in Syria, Tarhuntassa — located in the Lower Land, far south of the plateau heartland — provided a geographically far more convenient base for launching Syrian campaigns than the old capital on the north Anatolian plateau.

2. **Security of the northern frontier.** Diverting the kingdom's military resources massively southward toward Syria would leave Hattusa dangerously exposed to the [[kaska|Kaska]] enemy in the north. Rather than risk the sacred capital, Muwattalli chose to establish the permanent centre of Hittite power at a site "far removed from the menace of the northern enemies."

The effect was to virtually partition the kingdom: the north, including much of the homeland, was placed under the authority of Muwattalli's brother [[hattusili-iii|Hattusili]], ruling from Hakpis, while Muwattalli directed affairs from Tarhuntassa.

### Opposition and Legacy

The transfer provoked strong disquiet. Hattusili III — who would eventually restore the capital to Hattusa — later distanced himself from his brother's decision, insisting it had not been his wish:

> "Whether [the transfer of the gods] was in accordance with your wishes or whether it was not in accordance with your wishes, you, My Lady, are the one who knew that in your divine soul. But I was not involved in the order to transfer the gods. For me it was a matter of coercion."
> (KUB xiv 7 (CTH 383) i 3'–15')

After Muwattalli's death, Tarhuntassa became the appanage kingdom granted to his younger son [[kurunta|Kurunta]], who had been placed in Hattusili's care prior to the Kadesh campaign. The city and its southern territory thus remained a significant political unit well into the post-Kadesh era and into the reign of [[tudhaliya-iv|Tudhaliya IV]].

## Strategic and Religious Significance

Tarhuntassa's significance was simultaneously religious and military. The removal of the gods — the entire cultic apparatus of the Hittite state — to Tarhuntassa made it, however briefly, the spiritual as well as administrative capital of the empire. Hattusa had been "the spiritual as well as the material symbol of the might of the Hittite kingdom, the location of the great Temple of the Storm God." To transfer those cults southward was an act of radical religious reorganisation with no precedent.

Strategically, the city commanded access to the Syrian theatre and shielded the Hittite core from the logistical impossibility of defending both a northern Kaskan frontier and a southern Egyptian one simultaneously.

## Historiography

Our knowledge of Tarhuntassa depends almost entirely on Hittite textual sources — above all Hattusili III's Apology, treaty documents naming Tarhuntassa as a political unit, and references in the Muwattalli-era Alaksandu treaty. No archaeological site has been definitively identified with ancient Tarhuntassa, and the city's precise location within the broad region of later Cilicia remains debated. The testimony about Muwattalli's motives comes chiefly from his brother Hattusili, whose perspective is self-interested and retrospective; modern historians must account for his tendency to cast the transfer as religiously dubious and politically imprudent.
