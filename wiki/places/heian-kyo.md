# Heian-kyō

Heian-kyō ("Capital of Peace and Tranquility") was the imperial capital of Japan from
794 CE until the Meiji Restoration of 1869, when its functions were transferred to Tokyo.
Known today as Kyoto, it was the political, ceremonial, and cultural center of the
[[heian-period|Heian period]] (794–1185) and remained the formal imperial seat for over
a millennium. [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]]

## Foundation

The capital was established by [[kanmu-emperor|Emperor Kammu]] (r. 781–806) in the tenth
month of 794, after two failed attempts to build a new seat of government — one at Nara
([[nara-period|Heijō-kyō]]) and a ten-year interlude at Nagaoka (784–794). Kammu moved
his court from Nara partly to escape the political entanglements of the old capital,
where established Buddhist institutions and noble clans with roots in the line of Emperor
Temmu had complicated imperial authority. His longtime confidant Wake no Kiyomaro
(733–799) reportedly urged the choice of the Kadono site northeast of Nagaoka, and
Kammu accepted it in early 793.

The move to Heian closed, in the source's words, "a temporally long peregrination
that had taken Japanese rulers and their courts from one site to another ever since
the inception of the statutory (*ritsuryō*) system of government in the seventh century."

## Layout and Physical Form

Heian-kyō was laid out on a Chinese grid plan modeled on the Tang capital Chang'an,
with the Greater Imperial Palace (*Daidairi*) complex at its northern center. The palace
enclosure contained the Court of Government (*Chōdōin*), where the statutory
morning councils (*chōsei*) were originally held, and the Gate of the Vermilion Sparrow
(*Suzakumon*) as its main southern entrance. The residential palace compound, distinct
from the Court of Government, housed the Chamberlains' Office (*Kurōdodokoro*)
established by Emperor Saga in or about 810, which became a pivot of court administration.

A broad central avenue, Suzaku Avenue, ran south from the palace gate to the city's
southern perimeter. The western half of the city (*Ukyō*) proved less favorable for
settlement — it was marshy and lower-lying — while the eastern half (*Sakyō*) developed
more fully, eventually expanding beyond the original grid into what became the city's
enduring residential and commercial core.

## Strategic Significance

The site offered several advantages over Nara and Nagaoka:
- Superior land and water communication routes connecting to the Yodo River network
  and the Inland Sea, facilitating tax shipments from the provinces
- Distance from the Buddhist monastery complexes that had grown powerful at Nara
- Association with Kammu's matrilineal kin (the Korean-descended Yamato and Haji
  lineages had roots in the Kadono/Kyoto basin area)

The enormous personal and economic cost the capital moves imposed on the nobility
was, according to the *Cambridge History of Japan*, probably also a deliberate
instrument of Kammu's rule: "the great personal cost of the moves" increased noble
dependence on the imperial government.

## Capital Functions and Government

At the beginning of the Heian period, the major business of the [[ritsuryo-state|ritsuryō
government]] was conducted at the Court of Government in formal ministerial councils
each morning. By the early ninth century those councils had atrophied; from Saga's
reign onward, governmental business shifted into the emperor's residential palace
compound, where the Chamberlains' Office and later the "Guard-Post Judgments"
(*jinnosadame*) — ministerial meetings held at the Imperial Guards' posts in the
residential compound — became the actual machinery of governance.

By the late ninth century, real political power in the capital resided not at the
palace but at the private mansions of the Fujiwara regents. Under [[fujiwara-clan|
Fujiwara no Michinaga]] (966–1028), an eleventh-century diarist could write that
"the regent's house had become the imperial court."

## Physical Condition and Hazards

The capital suffered recurring fires, which were a chronic source of political crisis.
The burning of the Gate of Obedience to Heaven (*Ōtemmon*), the main gate to the
Court of Government, in 866 triggered the downfall of the Tomo and Ki clans and
the formal establishment of the Fujiwara regency under Yoshifusa. Violence and
banditry within the city were persistent problems from the early ninth century, and
the *kebiishi* (Offenses Investigation Agents), established probably in Saga's reign,
became the chief instrument of public order in Kyoto through the eleventh century.

## Long-Term Significance

Heian-kyō remained Japan's imperial capital in name for over a millennium. The
court culture that developed there — poetry in the *waka* form, the aesthetics of
*mono no aware*, the prose of women writers like Murasaki Shikibu — defined
"classical Japanese civilization" in later national memory. The physical city also
became the chief locus of Buddhist temple construction, eventually hosting hundreds
of major religious institutions despite Kammu's original intent to limit Buddhist
institutional power at his new capital.
