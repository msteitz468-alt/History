## Overview

Osaka Castle was the greatest fortress in early modern Japan, built by Toyotomi Hideyoshi as the centerpiece of his power. It passed to his son Toyotomi Hideyori and became the seat of the Toyotomi house during Hideyori's minority and through the campaigns of 1614–1615.

## Description and Defenses

At the time of the Winter Campaign (1614) the castle's outer defensive perimeter was "about eight and three-quarter miles round, and everywhere it bristled with guns of all sizes, from the two great cannon called Taro and Jiro, that were mounted in the tower before the Sakura Gate in the Ni-no-maru, and are described as the biggest in the Empire, to the 'great and small ordnance' that fired through the embrasures cut everywhere in the many walls and towers, while every hundred yards or so there was a fire-projecting mangonel" (*Sadler*). The fortification system comprised three nested enclosures: the Ni-no-maru (second bailey), San-no-maru (third bailey), and the Honmaru (inner keep). A celebrated forward defense work, the *Sanada no Dejiro* (Sanada Barbican), was added during the Winter Campaign by [[sanada-yukimura|Sanada Yukimura]].

Ieyasu's Winter Campaign headquarters were established at Chausuyama ("Tea-mill Hill"), a small steep-sided hillock facing the castle's front, close enough to be in range of the castle guns.

## Strategic Significance

Osaka Castle's strategic importance derived from its combination of natural defensibility (surrounded by moats, river approaches, marshland) and economic base. Hideyori held the castle with a 650,000-koku income — an enormous endowment that allowed him to maintain and attract tens of thousands of fighters. Hosokawa Tadaoki compared it favorably to the same castle's resistance against Oda Nobunaga, when "under the Ikko monks it withstood Nobunaga for seven years" — while acknowledging that the changed political situation (daimyo families held as hostages at Edo) made continued resistance unlikely.

## Fall and Aftermath

The Winter Campaign of 1614 deprived the castle of its outer defenses through diplomatic deception: Ieyasu concentrated workers to fill the moats "night and day" until all but the inner works were levelled. The Summer Campaign of 1615 then overwhelmed even this reduced stronghold. During the final assault the castle was set on fire — first in the kitchen through treachery, then by Tokugawa forces entering the enclosure. Hideyori, Yodogimi, and their staff retreated to the keep and committed suicide there. Ieyasu recovered the remnants of Hideyoshi's bullion stored in the castle, estimated at roughly half a million sterling in gold and silver.
