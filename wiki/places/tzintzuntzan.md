# Tzintzuntzan

## Overview

**Tzintzuntzan** (“place of hummingbirds”) on the shores of **Lake Pátzcuaro** was
the capital of the Late Postclassic [[purepecha|Purépecha (Tarascan)]] state — the
administrative and royal seat from which an empire of ~75,000 km² was governed until
Spanish conquest (c. 1530). Gorenstein (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 7) treats it as the type site
for a **centralized absorption state**: exclusive power in one capital, not a
confederation of coordinated peers.

## Formation and political geography

Middle Postclassic migrations into the Lake Pátzcuaro Basin brought Nahuatl speakers
and successive Chichimec groups (earlier on lake islands; later from Zacapu via
Uayameo into basin settlements including Tzintzuntzan). Environmental redistribution of
lake water altered irrigable land and marsh resources unequally. Tzintzuntzan gained
control of Uayameo’s irrigable land; high elite + richest resource base → control of
the entire basin.

Strategy: **absorption** of defeated polities and creation of new ones so that power
was exclusive in Tzintzuntzan, not shared among peer rulers. This contrasts with the
[[aztec-empire|Aztec]] **coordinated** mode (one polity dominates but continues
negotiating with defeated peers). *produced:* hierarchical tribute network under the
royal family (maize, cotton cloth/clothing, slaves, sacrificial victims, fruit, cacao,
gourds, skins, feathers, metals).

## Urban scale and centralization

Even in its heyday Tzintzuntzan was a comparatively small urban center:
Protohistoric population only ~**25,000–35,000**. Growth was primarily
political/administrative rather than commercial. An extraordinarily high **~37% of the
basin’s population** lived in the capital — Gorenstein’s strongest archaeological-
demographic signal of administrative centralization. Distinctive **yácata** platform
architecture, specialized lapidary, and metallurgy mark elite and state identity.

## Imperial role

From Tzintzuntzan the royal family managed a centralized tribute network spanning the
empire (Lerma north, Balsas south, Sayula west, Michoacán–México border east). Eastern
frontier conflict with the Aztecs involved mutual penetration without empire-breaking
victory and a borderland of local groups serving both powers. At the conquest of
Mexico the Tarascans, based here, refused Aztec requests for help.

Late Postclassic takeover of long-distance West Mexican trade routes (cutting the
eastern/Central Mexican branch) was directed from this political center — see
[[west-and-northwest-mexico-prehistory]].

## Why it mattered

Tzintzuntzan anchors the West Mexican alternative to Central Mexican imperial form:
a compact, highly centralized capital securing a large empire through administrative
proximity (ultimate authority one or two levels away in the basin) rather than through
vast commercial reorganization of the Basin of Mexico type. Principal ethnohistorical
window: *Relación de Michoacán* (1540–41).

## Conquest and early colonial capital (Van Young, CHNPA II.2)

Van Young restates the imperial demographic peak — capital **25,000–35,000**; Pátzcuaro
Basin up to ~**200,000** — and places Tzintzuntzan at the hinge of Spanish entry into
the Center-West. **Cristóbal de Olid** (1522) reached the city unopposed after no
resistance at Taximaroa. Fray Martín de la Coruña arrived **1525**, destroyed
temples/icons, and founded the Franciscan house **1526**; the *cazonci* had been
baptized that year in Mexico City under Cortés’s sponsorship. In February **1530**
Nuño de Guzmán’s trial and brutal execution of Cazonci **Tzintzicha Tangaxoan** ended
independent Tarascan kingship at this capital.

Cortés had reserved the densely settled Tzintzuntzan heartland for himself, but it
failed to join the Marquesado del Valle; by ~1530 his Michoacán influence was nearly
gone, while [[encomienda]] remained longer-lived and economically more important in the
Tarascan zone than in wilder Nueva Galicia. Nearby on Lake Pátzcuaro, the hospital-
village **Santa Fé de la Laguna** (1533), founded by future bishop **Vasco de Quiroga**
on the model of More’s *Utopia*, became the most famous node of the Franciscan/
diocesan hospital regime — dwellings for hundreds of families, church, school,
workshops, wards, gardens; artisan crafts (pottery styles still associated). *part_of*
[[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church]] and [[western-mexico-indigenous-colonial-modern]].

Basin demography under [[virgin-soil-epidemics]]: ~**100,000–200,000 (1520) → <10,000
natives by 1650**. The *Relación de Michoacán* (1540–41), compiled at this Franciscan
orbit from Tarascan elite testimony, is the principal ethnohistorical window on the
fallen court and conversion-era rumor and belief.

## Colonial–modern place: language shift and tourism

As the old imperial capital, Tzintzuntzan became a microcosm of Purépecha cultural
contraction. Van Young: Tarascan speakers constituted about **50% of the population in
1850** but only about **10% a century later** — reflecting the wider restriction of
Tarascan speech to NW Michoacán heartland (Pátzcuaro shores/islands, western sierra,
Cañada). The town remained inside the embattled communal-holding districts that
sustained Tarascan culture through liberal desamortización, Porfirian timber and rail
penetration (Pátzcuaro–Morelia 1886), Revolution/Cristero catastrophe, and Cárdenas-era
reform (see [[purepecha]]).

Late twentieth-century Lake Pátzcuaro tourism and artisanal commercialization (including
ceramics associated with the Santa Fé tradition) made the former capital a showcase of
what remains vital in Tarascan culture for outside markets — Van Young’s irony that
survival is negotiated daily, not preserved like a fly in amber.
