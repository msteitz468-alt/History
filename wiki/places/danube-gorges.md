# The Danube Gorges (Iron Gates Mesolithic–Neolithic)

The **Danube Gorges** — the Iron Gates / Đerdap defile where the Danube cuts
between the Carpathians and the Balkan range, today the Serbian–Romanian border
— hold one of Europe's most remarkable prehistoric site clusters: a string of
riverside settlements occupied between roughly the **end of the eighth
millennium and the sixth millennium BC**, spanning the local Mesolithic and the
transition to farming. The type-sites are **Lepenski Vir**, **Vlasac**,
**Padina**, **Hajdučka Vodenica**, **Ostrovul Corbului**, **Ostrovul Banului**,
and **Kula**. The composite chronology used here follows Ivana Radovanović's
*The Iron Gates Mesolithic* (1996), as synthesised by
[[bailey-balkan-prehistory-2000|Bailey (2000)]].

## Why the Gorges matter

The sites are anomalous against the wider [[balkans|Balkan]] pattern in two
ways that have drawn outsized attention (Srejović 1969; Radovanović 1996;
Whittle 1996):

- **Very early durable architecture.** From the end of the eighth millennium BC
  people built **stone-based structures and hearths** on the narrow strips of
  land, terraces, and islets between river and slope — at Padina, Vlasac,
  Icoana, Veterani Terrace, Razvrata, Ostrovul Corbului — long before this was
  common elsewhere in the region.
- **Settled life *without* the usual Neolithic package.** For the first
  millennium and a half of occupation the sites lack **pottery** and
  **domesticated foodstuffs** — the two traditional markers of "Neolithic"
  life. People lived in substantial, repeatedly rebuilt structures while
  remaining hunter-fisher-foragers, drawing heavily on the Danube's fish
  (notably migratory sturgeon).

## The Lepenski Vir sequence

Lepenski Vir is the showpiece. Founded in the early seventh millennium BC
(Gorges phase 2) as eight oval semi-subterranean structures cut into hillside
niches, with stone-slab rectangular hearths set on the river side and dense
material accumulating in the rear, it developed (phase I/1) the Gorges'
signature **trapezoidal-floored buildings** — distinctive plastered floors laid
out in **up-stream and down-stream aggregations**, the downstream cluster
marked by denser burials, "altars," and figurative material. Many buildings
were **repeatedly reconstructed in superimposed sequences** (e.g. the
17–8–7–9 and 41–38–37 sequences), implying a continuity of use approaching, if
not reaching, permanent occupation. From the mid-seventh millennium BC (Gorges
phase 4) **pottery and new blade-based lithic technology** appear — yet
beneath them run deep continuities in building technique, with new structures
placed directly over earlier hearths at Padina and Vlasac.

## Interpretation in this wiki

[[bailey-balkan-prehistory-2000|Bailey]] reads the Gorges not as a freakish
exception but as a **local variant of the wider trend toward building social
environments** — the marking-out of particular places, the separation of
intra- from extra-mural space (substantial **stone thresholds** dividing
interior from exterior), and the patterning of activity by hearth location and
by the up/down-stream division. The trapezoidal buildings, in his scheme, are
an intermediate architectural form between flimsy round huts and durable
rectilinear houses. The Gorges thus illustrate
[[exclusion-incorporation-projection|exclusion, incorporation and projection]]
operating among foragers, well before the tell-village world of the fifth
millennium BC — and they complicate any clean equation of "Neolithic" with
farming, pottery, and a single migratory origin.
