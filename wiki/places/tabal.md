# Tabal

**Tabal** was the Iron Age designation for a large plateau region of south-central Anatolia, extending southwards from the southern curve of the Halys river (Kızılırmak) towards the Taurus mountains, westwards towards the Konya Plain, and eastwards towards the anti-Taurus range. The population was "very likely a predominantly Luwian one, as it had been throughout the Late Bronze Age and perhaps already in the early second millennium" (Bryce 2012). In the Assyrian sources the name sometimes denotes a specific northern kingdom (Tabal "Proper"), and sometimes the entire cluster of polities on the plateau.

## Geographic Description

The Tabal region was not a single unified territory but a fragmented cluster of small kingdoms sharing a common cultural and linguistic heritage. The northern core — Tabal "Proper" — corresponded roughly to the modern provinces of Kayseri and Niğde; its capital was probably at modern Kululu (30 km north-east of Kayseri), with another city at Sultanhan to its west. The southern kingdoms occupied the Classical Tyanitis (Tuwana/Tyana), the area around modern Niğde and Ereğli, and the upper valleys approaching the Cilician Gates. The Konya Plain formed the south-western margin.

## History of the Kingdoms

### Origins

Tabal emerged from the wreckage of the Hittite Empire following the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]]. The region had been part of the Hittite Lower Land, and several kingdom names survive from Late Bronze Age predecessors (notably Tuwanuwa → Tuwana). The population continuity is strong: Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions persist here into the late 8th century, and royal onomastics are predominantly Luwian. The [[neo-hittite-states|Neo-Hittite]] cultural framework thus extended onto the Anatolian plateau, though more fragmented than in north Syria.

### The Twenty-Four Kingdoms (837 BCE)

The Annals of Shalmaneser III record a western campaign in 837 BCE in which twenty-four kings held sway in Tabal. The first attested ruler of the northern line was **Tuwati I**, forced to take refuge in his royal city Artulu during Shalmaneser's invasion; his son **Kikki** then surrendered and paid tribute. This fragmentation — two dozen polities — reflects the decentralised character of Tabalian political life in the early Iron Age.

### Consolidation under Tiglath-pileser III (c. 740s)

By the reign of Tiglath-pileser III, the number of kingdoms had contracted sharply. Five are listed as his tributaries:

| Kingdom | Ruler (Assyrian name) | Modern location approx. |
|---|---|---|
| Tabal "Proper" | Wasusarma (Wassurme) | Kayseri–Niğde region |
| Atuna | Ushhitti | North-west Tabal, near Aksaray |
| Tuwana (Luwian Tuhana) | Warpalawa (Urballa) | Classical Tyanitis, south |
| Ishtuanda | Tuhamme | Near Atuna |
| Hupishna (Cybistra) | Uirimme | Southern Anatolia, Lower Land |

A sixth, **Shinuhtu** (capital probably at modern Aksaray), is attested in the reign of Sargon II, ruled by Kiyakiya (Assyrian Kiakki).

### Tabal "Proper" and the Wasusarma Crisis (c. 740–730)

**Wasusarma** (c. 740–730), son of Tuwati II, bore the title "Great King" — a deliberate archaism evoking Hittite imperial prestige. The TOPADA inscription, carved on a cliff-face near modern Acıgöl, records a battle he won against a coalition of eight enemy rulers near the city of Parzuta, with the support of Warpalawa of Tuwana, Kiyakiya of Shinuhtu, and a third king called Ruwata. Tiglath-pileser III, furious at Wasusarma for styling himself the Assyrian king's equal, deposed him around 730 and installed a commoner, **Hulli**, in his place.

Hulli was in turn deposed by Shalmaneser V (726–722), deported to Assyria, then restored by Sargon II. His son **Ambaris** (c. 721–713) received Sargon's daughter in marriage, along with the country of Hilakku (rough Cilicia) as a dowry — giving him domain over a significantly enlarged territory, renamed **Bit-Burutash** (Bit-Paruta). This new kingdom probably extended south-westwards to the Konya region (Kızıldağ). Ambaris subsequently plotted with Phrygia and Urartu, was deposed by Sargon in 713, and deported to Assyria with his family and courtiers. Bit-Burutash and Hilakku were placed under an Assyrian governor.

### Atuna

Atuna lay north-west of Tabal "Proper", in the vicinity of modern Aksaray. Under **Kurti** (attested c. 718), it remained loyal to Assyria through the reigns of Tiglath-pileser III, Shalmaneser V, and the early years of Sargon II. As a reward, Sargon ceded to Kurti the territory of the deposed Kiyakiya of Shinuhtu. Kurti briefly defected to the Phrygian king Mita but returned to the Assyrian fold; around 710 Atuna and Ishtuanda jointly attacked cities of Bit-Burutash.

### Hilakku

To the south and west of Tabal proper, covering rough Cilicia (Cilicia Tracheia), lay **Hilakku** — the region from which the Classical name "Cilicia" is derived. It had been part of Hittite Tarhuntassa. Unlike Adanawa, no Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions from Hilakku are known. Its population was predominantly Luwian. Hilakku was incorporated into the Assyrian provincial system probably in the reign of Shalmaneser V; Sargon assigned it to Ambaris of Bit-Burutash as a dowry kingdom, then placed it under an Assyrian governor in 713. It remained "largely free of Assyria throughout the Neo-Assyrian period" in practice, with the Assyrians able to claim "no more than token sovereignty over it" (Bryce 2012).

## Strategic Importance

Tabal occupied the strategic gateway between the Anatolian plateau and Cilicia. Control of the passes through the Taurus — especially the Cilician Gates — gave whoever held the southern Tabalian kingdoms (principally Tuwana and Tuhana) enormous leverage over movement between Anatolia and the Levant. The Assyrians repeatedly sought client rulers here because Tabal was both a buffer against [[urartu|Urartu]] and a zone vulnerable to [[phrygians|Phrygian]] encroachment. Sargon II died in 705 fighting Cimmerians in the Tabal region, reflecting its persistent volatility.

## Relationship to Assyria and Urartu

Tabal's kings oscillated between tributary submission and defiance. Urartian interest in the plateau is attested: Argishti I of Urartu refers to "the land of the sons of Tuate" (descendants of Tuwati I), and Sarduri II of Urartu supported Mati'ilu of Bit-Agusi against Assyria c. 743 in a coalition that included Tabalian elements. The region also faced Phrygian pressure from the west: the Phrygian king Mita operated near Tuwana in the final years of the 8th century, and Ambaris of Bit-Burutash conspired with both Phrygia and Urartu — prompting Sargon's decisive intervention in 713.

## How Significance Changed

After 713, the independent political life of Tabal effectively ended. The territory was absorbed into the Assyrian provincial system. Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions cease by the end of the 8th century (Muwaharani II of Tuwana's Niğde stele is described as among the latest securely datable Neo-Hittite inscriptions). In 679 the Assyrian king Esarhaddon fought Cimmerians at Hupishna and claimed to have conquered the people of Hilakku — the last flash of historical visibility for the region in Neo-Assyrian records.

## Historiography

The history of Tabal is reconstructed almost entirely from Assyrian annals, supplemented by Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions discovered at Kululu, Sultanhan, Topada, Ivriz, Bor, Bulgarmaden, and Aksaray. The corpus was assembled and translated by J. D. Hawkins (CHLI I, 2000). The Kızıldağ relief figure — a seated bearded man in a peaked cap — has been variously attributed to Wasusarma or Ambaris; Bryce prefers an identification with Ambaris on territorial grounds, though the question is not resolved. The relationship between Tabal "Proper" and the broader Tabal region (a political entity vs. a geographic designation) is explicitly noted by Bryce as a source of terminological ambiguity in Assyrian records. Collection coverage is moderate: Assyrian sources are well represented; local hieroglyphic inscriptions require specialist knowledge of CHLI and are covered here via Bryce (2012).
