# Nunavut

Eastern Canadian Arctic territory created for a largely [[inuit]] population under the **Nunavut Final Land Claim Agreement** (ratified **25 May 1993**) and territorial legislation aimed at inauguration by **1999**. Deep archaeological roots in [[thule-culture|Thule]] occupation of the Canadian Arctic; modern political product of [[arctic-native-history-norse-to-modern]] and the Canadian land-claims era of the [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]].

## Geographic description

Eastern Northwest Territories division: High and Central Arctic islands and mainland coasts historically occupied by Central Arctic Inuit groups (Iglulik, Baffin, etc.) with Thule-ancestry continuity. Place-name politics of the claim era included renaming (e.g., Frobisher Bay village → **Iqaluit**). Washburn’s settlement figures: ~**17,500 Inuit** comprising ~**80%** of the designated area’s population; Inuit ownership of **350,000 km²** of land (Damas/Washburn: roughly 10% of the broader region in claim provisions; Washburn notes ≈36,000 km² with mineral rights in one summary).

## Path to the claim and territory

NWT centralization accelerated after 1960; 1967 Eskimo Camp Study Committee: only about one-fifth of Inuit still in year-round hunting-trapping camps. Administrative philosophy: southern Canadian hamlet ideal (housing, compulsory education, medical care, sanitation, recreation, communication, transport) only via wholesale welfare-state provision from the south. Mechanized hunting costly under inflation; commercial art, service, and construction helpful but heavily subsidized. Health advances raised populations; alcohol-linked marital strife, crime, and violence; some communities went “dry.” Education lagged (few beyond grade 6) until 1970s educated youth led politics.

Political build-up: 1975 Territories two federal ridings (Western Arctic, Nunatsiak); Inuit members held 7 of 10 Arctic NWT Assembly seats by 1979. **Inuit Tapirisat of Canada (ITC)** formed 1971 as umbrella for regional organizations (LIA, COPE, others) and **initiated the Nunavut proposal** dividing the NWT so one section would be largely Inuit country. Boundary problems with the Dene Nation and Métis Association; **15 January 1987** agreement with Inuit representatives; federal ratification timetable toward 1 October 1991.

Before territorial finalization, a comprehensive land claim in spring 1990 between the Tungavik Federation and federal (and related) governments—described as the “last major northern land claim”: **$580 million over 14 years**; title to **350,000 km²** including subsurface mineral rights among provisions putting large control in Inuit hands; aims included recognition of Inuit as a distinct people, protection of traditional cultures/lifestyles/aboriginal rights, self-government rights, lands, and economic resources. **Final ratification of the Nunavut Agreement: 25 May 1993** (Washburn: signed by Mulroney, Nellie Cournoyea, James Eetoolook and others; legislation to create Nunavut Territory by 1999). Rest of NWT moved toward greater self-government/provincial status in parallel discourse.

Related western Arctic settlement (not Nunavut but same process family): **Inuvialuit / Western Arctic Claim**, 5 June 1984—11,000 km² surface+subsurface and broader surface rights without gas/oil on additional lands (Washburn: 11,000 km² surface+subsurface and 78,000 km² without gas/oil rights; Damas: 91,000 of 435,000 km² traditionally used/occupied).

## Why it mattered

Nunavut is the largest modern Inuit jurisdictional achievement in Canada: a public government territory with Inuit demographic majority plus a comprehensive land claim package, contrasting with corporate Alaska ([[alaska-native-claims-settlement-1971]]) and Home Rule Greenland ([[greenland-inuit]]). It crystallized ITC umbrella politics, resolved (imperfectly) Dene–Métis boundary tensions, and symbolized the shift Damas tracks from passive contact-traditional images to assertive, organized Inuit self-determination—still under heavy fiscal dependency.

## Significance over time

Precontact/Thule homeland → contact-traditional fox-trade and RCMP/mission map → postwar welfare towns (Frobisher Bay etc.) → 1970s–90s land-claim and division politics → 1993 agreement and 1999 territory. Continuity of Inuit presence is archaeological and demographic; the *place as a polity* is late-twentieth-century.
