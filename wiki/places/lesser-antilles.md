The Lesser Antilles form the eastern and southern arc of the Caribbean island chain, comprising fifteen major islands and countless smaller ones extending from the Virgin Islands south to Trinidad and Tobago. They represent the front line of human settlement in the Caribbean from the earliest Archaic Age colonizers through the Kalinago period and European contact.

## Geographic Description

The Lesser Antilles divide geologically into two parallel arcs. The inner (western) arc consists of volcanic islands with pronounced relief, rich soils, and higher rainfall — Dominica, Martinique, St. Lucia, St. Vincent, and Grenada. The outer (eastern) arc consists of older limestone islands, lower and flatter — Anguilla, Antigua, Barbuda, and Grande-Terre (Guadeloupe). This geological diversity created an uneven distribution of natural resources that may have stimulated craft specialization and inter-island exchange networks throughout prehistory.

The archipelago is characterized by rich marine resources in contrast to a depauperate endemic terrestrial fauna. Before human colonization, the absence of major predatory species made terrestrial and marine fauna relatively easy to harvest from forest, estuary, mangrove, littoral, and coral reef environments. The islands are fragile environments subject to volcanic eruptions, hurricanes, tsunamis, sea-level changes, and periodic drought cycles, all of which shaped settlement patterns and cultural responses throughout the pre-Columbian period.

The continental islands of Trinidad and Tobago, though geographically adjacent, relate more closely to mainland South America in their biota and archaeological sequences and were not subject to true island colonization in the same sense as the other Lesser Antilles.

Paleoenvironmental data from St. Martin and Guadeloupe document significant climatic variation: drought with frequent hurricanes during the Archaic Age (from c. 4300 BCE); a less dry period with diminished hurricane activity during the Ceramic Age (c. 500 BCE–AD 800); another major drought episode between AD 800 and 1000; and wetter conditions after AD 950 associated with the Late Saladoid occupation.

## Settlement History

### Archaic Age (c. 3300 BCE–AD 100)

The earliest human presence in the Lesser Antilles dates to the Archaic Age, though the distribution of sites is uneven. All confirmed Archaic Age sites lie in the **northern Lesser Antilles**; none are known in the Windward Islands south of Guadeloupe despite repeated surveys. The most densely occupied islands during this period were Antigua and St. Martin, with over forty Archaic Age sites identified on Antigua alone.

Keegan and Hofman propose three phases for the northern Lesser Antilles:
- **Early Archaic Age** (3300–2600 BCE): Sites include Jolly Beach (Antigua) and Etang Rouge (St. Martin)
- **Middle Archaic Age** (2600–800 BCE): Sites include Norman Estate, Bay Longue 2, and multiple St. Martin sites; Plum Piece (Saba); River Site (Barbuda); Whitehead's Bluff (Anguilla)
- **Late Archaic Age** (800 BCE–AD 100): Sites include Fort Bay (Saba), Corre Corre Bay and Smith Gut (St. Eustatius), Krum Bay (St. Thomas), and the latest phase at Baie Orientale (St. Martin, to AD 100)

Archaic Age communities practised an annual mobility cycle, moving between coastal and interior sites to exploit seasonally available resources. Sites were typically small temporary habitations abandoned and reoccupied over centuries. Flint from Long Island (Antigua) circulated across the northern Lesser Antilles, demonstrating broad social networks. The wide distribution of Antiguan flint across sites 150 km distant indicates the extent of these networks.

**Plum Piece, Saba (c. 1875–1520 BCE):** A key Archaic Age campsite located at 400 meters elevation in mountainous tropical forest on the leeward side of Saba, demonstrating interior occupation by Archaic peoples. Radiocarbon-dated to 1875–1520 cal. BCE. The toolkit of grinding stones, shell adzes, and imported Antiguan flint suggests seasonal forest exploitation for land crabs, birds (especially Audubon's shearwater), and reef fish. Evidence for plant cultivation includes starch grains of guáyiga (zamia, possibly imported from Puerto Rico or Dominican Republic), maize, sweet potato, and annatto. The near-total absence of mollusks distinguishes it from coastal Archaic sites. Flint cortex patterns indicate pre-worked cores arrived from Antigua and were transported further after tool manufacture on-site.

### Early Ceramic Age / Saladoid (c. 400 BCE–AD 600/850)

The Saladoid ceramic tradition entered the northern Lesser Antilles between 400 and 200 BCE, possibly beginning as early as 800–400 BCE on some islands. The Hope Estate site (St. Martin) and Trants (Montserrat) are among the earliest Ceramic Age sites in the region. Both Huecoid and Saladoid pottery appear together in the earliest Ceramic Age assemblages of the northern Lesser Antilles and Puerto Rico.

The Windward Islands (southern Lesser Antilles) received permanent ceramic communities beginning around AD 200–400: Carriacou (Grand Bay), Union Island (Chatham Bay), St. Vincent (Arnos Vale Field and Brighton sites), St. Lucia (Grande Anse), Martinique (Diamant II), and Dominica (Vieille Case).

Saladoid settlement introduced agriculture (manioc, maize, sweet potato, and diverse crops), horticulture, and landscape modification that permanently altered island environments. Exchange networks circulated semiprecious stones, animal tooth pendants, carnelian beads (from Antigua flint sources), nephrite and jadeite (possibly from Guatemala or Dominican Republic), greenstone celts (St. Martin), serpentinite (Puerto Rico), and amethyst (Grenada) — what Rodríguez Ramos calls the "Iridescent Period."  Dog burials, often alongside humans, are common at Saladoid sites until AD 600.

Saladoid society has been characterized as tribal and egalitarian, though James Petersen proposed lineage-based hierarchical societies. The large round maloca structure (19 m diameter) at Golden Rock (St. Eustatius) may indicate a successful shaman/leader figure. Saladoid pottery was remarkably homogeneous across the region — what Keegan calls the Saladoid "veneer."

### Post-Saladoid / Late Ceramic Age (c. AD 600–1350)

After AD 600–850, Troumassoid series pottery replaced Saladoid throughout the Lesser Antilles, though Saladoid persisted in the north until AD 850–900. Settlement patterns shifted toward greater site diversity, the emergence of clear site hierarchies after AD 600–850, and the development of localized micro-styles in pottery reflecting increasing autonomy of specific islands and sites. The northern Lesser Antilles distinguished Mamoran Troumassoid sub-styles; the southern islands showed Troumassan and Suazan subseries with stronger South American affiliations.

**Anse à la Gourde, Guadeloupe (c. AD 450–1350):** The best-documented post-Saladoid site in the Lesser Antilles. Located at the northeastern end of the limestone island of Grande-Terre, excavated 1995–2000 over 1,424 square meters by Hofman, Hoogland, and Delpuech. Four successive occupation phases dated AD 450–1350, with the major occupation AD 1000–1350. The site features a doughnut-shaped midden (1.1 hectares, 200 × 130 m oval) encircling a habitation area with round houses (5–12 m diameter), a cleared plaza (70 × 35 m), and 83 excavated burials comprising 101 individuals. Complex mortuary practices include primary, secondary, partially secondary, and composite burials, with skulls and long bones selectively removed — likely related to ancestor veneration and lineage ideology. Exotic lithic materials came from Antigua (flint), St. Martin (greenstone axes), and Anguilla (calcirudite). Non-local females identified via strontium-isotope analysis were more often interred with non-local objects than males, suggesting female mobility in exchange networks.

**Giraudy Site, St. Lucia (c. AD 900–1500):** Located in the Beane Field area of Vieux Fort in southern St. Lucia, first excavated by Ripley and Adelaide Bullen in the early 1970s and re-investigated by Leiden University in 2004. Two occupations representing modified Saladoid and Suazoid components. Subsistence shows greater use of sea turtle in the earlier period and guinea pig identification in the Saladoid layer; the Suazoid layer shows increased small fish and dominated mollusk assemblage of Carib Pointed-Venus shells (Anomalocardia brasiliana). Located only 500 meters from Point Sable beach.

**Kelbey's Ridge 2, Saba (c. AD 1350):** Located at 140 m elevation in northeastern Saba, radiocarbon-dated to AD 1350. Evidence for connections with Greater Antillean (Chicoid / Boca Chica style) culture — the only such site in the Lesser Antilles. Produced ceremonial paraphernalia including a snuff-inhaler made from manatee bone. Five round or oval houses (6–8 m diameter) around post holes and pits; seven burials with ten individuals, all under house floors in strongly flexed positions. Interpreted as an outpost of a Greater Antillean polity, exploiting the Saba Bank fishing grounds and serving as a gateway community controlling trade along the route between the Greater Antilles and the South American mainland.

### Demographic Collapse and Late Sites (after AD 1300)

After AD 1300, settlements decreased drastically in number and size and relocated to strategic defensive positions — elevated ridges, remote plateaus. Kelbey's Ridge 2 (Saba), Morne Cybèle, and Morne Souffleur (La Désirade) all show this pattern.

**Morne Cybèle and Morne Souffleur, La Désirade (c. AD 1440–1460):** Located at 205 m elevation on the plateau of La Désirade. Radiocarbon-dated to AD 1440–1460 — the latest pre-European sites in the northern Lesser Antilles. Both sites produced shell masks (guaíza) reminiscent of Greater Antillean examples. Pottery shows affiliations to Cayo and Suazoid styles of the southern Lesser Antilles and possibly mainland Venezuela.

Keegan and Hofman interpret this post-AD 1300 demographic decline as reflecting the absorption of northern Lesser Antillean communities into the expanding sociopolitical structure of Greater Antillean polities, disrupting independent local development and transforming the islands from permanent villages into ports of trade and lookout posts at strategic intervals.

### Kalinago Period (c. AD 1250–European contact)

**Cayo pottery** — associated with the Kalinago (Island Carib) — appears in the southern Lesser Antilles, dated by Boomert to c. AD 1000–1500 and more precisely c. AD 1250. More than twenty archaeological sites with Cayo pottery have been identified across Grenada, the Grenadines, St. Vincent, Dominica, and (more rarely) Guadeloupe, St. Lucia, and Martinique. Cayo pottery is characterized by incisions on flat rims, cone-shaped collars, appliqué clay-ball adornos perforated with hollow straws, multi-convex vessels with anthropomorphic faces, and caraipe temper (Amazonian tree bark). The pottery reflects mainland Koriabo and Greater Antillean Meillacoid and Chicoid affiliations.

Cayo sites on St. Vincent and Grenada occupy strategic ridge positions overlooking the Atlantic Ocean, near rivers on the windward sides of islands. Early European materials (iron, lead, Spanish olive jars, glass beads, French faïence) appear in direct association with Cayo pottery, confirming the sites date to the 16th–early 17th centuries and documenting indigenous–European trade before formal colonial establishment.

**Argyle Site, St. Vincent:** The first complete early colonial Kalinago settlement excavated in the Lesser Antilles. Located on a ridge overlooking the Atlantic at the mouth of the Yambou River in southeastern St. Vincent. Rescue-excavated 2009–2010 by Leiden University over 2,800 square meters. Two plaza phases (10 × 15 m and 15 × 25 m), eleven structures (nine round, two oval), and European trade goods including late 16th- to early 17th-century Spanish olive jars, Portuguese and Spanish majolica, glass bottles, seed and chevron beads, and French tin-glazed earthenware. Human teeth from grave pits under house floors included one local and one non-local individual (strontium-isotope analysis). The site structure matches historical chronicle descriptions of Kalinago settlement.

## Significance

The Lesser Antilles functioned as a network of interaction rather than isolated islands. Exchange of resources (flint from Antigua, greenstone from St. Martin, carnelian, amethyst, jadeite), people, and ideas linked communities across hundreds of kilometers throughout the pre-Columbian period. The archipelago served as the critical stepping-stone between South America and the Greater Antilles — allowing both northward population movement during the Saladoid migration and southward influence from Greater Antillean complex societies in the post-Saladoid period.

The diversity of geological environments (volcanic vs. limestone) produced different resource bases that incentivized specialization and exchange, sustaining multi-island social networks throughout the Ceramic Age. After AD 1200, the Lesser Antilles were increasingly integrated into the Greater Antillean sphere, which disrupted local autonomous development and produced the demographic and settlement collapse visible archaeologically after AD 1300.

## Historiography

Caribbean archaeology long treated the Lesser Antilles as a secondary region — a stepping stone between the South American mainland and the "culturally advanced" Greater Antilles — rather than a dynamic zone in its own right. The unilinear stepping-stone migration model, associated with Irving Rouse's taxonomic framework (see [[rouse-archaeological-framework]]), dominated interpretations from the 1940s through the 1980s.

The major historiographical revision since the 1990s, strongly advanced by Corinne Hofman and Menno Hoogland at Leiden University and by Keegan at the Florida Museum of Natural History, reframes the Lesser Antilles as exhibiting continuous dynamic exchange, local diversification, and autonomous development rather than passive reception of migration waves. Key contributions include:
- Recognition of the co-existence of Huecoid and Saladoid pottery traditions (not sequential migrations)
- The concept of "archipelagic resource mobility" as an Archaic Age strategy
- Identification of post-Saladoid micro-style areas reflecting local identity formation
- The first excavation of confirmed Kalinago (Cayo culture) sites in stratigraphic context (Argyle, AD 1990s–2010s)

Major unresolved issues include the absence of Archaic Age sites south of Guadeloupe (despite surveys), the relationship between Suazoid and Cayo ceramic traditions, and the degree to which the post-AD 1300 demographic collapse reflects external Greater Antillean absorption versus internal social transformation.

The source base for Lesser Antilles prehistory is relatively strong in the archaeological record but almost entirely lacking in primary documentary sources for the pre-contact period. European descriptions begin only with Columbus's second voyage (1493) and the French missionary accounts of the 17th century.
