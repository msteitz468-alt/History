## Overview

The Decapolis was a loose grouping of cities under indirect Roman rule spanning the
Jordan/Israel/Syria borderland, roughly from Amman to Damascus, active from the 1st
century BC to the early 2nd century AD. The name means "ten cities," but the actual
membership fluctuated — 12 to 15 cities are attested by the end of the 1st century AD —
and, per Ball, the discrepancy simply did not matter to contemporaries. The core member
list included [[gerasa|Philadelphia (Amman)]], [[gerasa|Gerasa (Jerash)]], Pella,
Capitolias, Gadara, Abila, Raphana, possibly Dion, Scythopolis (the only member west of
the Jordan), Hippos, Adra, and Canatha; Damascus was briefly included as well.

## History of Formation and Control

The grouping emerged from Pompey's 64 BC annexation of Syria, which detached these
formerly Seleucid-held or newly liberated cities from Jewish and Nabataean control and
placed them under loose, indirect Roman oversight. The Decapolis effectively disappeared
as a distinct entity once Judaea (AD 94) and Nabataea (AD 106) came under direct Roman
rule and were absorbed into the provinces of Syria, Arabia, and Palestine.

Population was mixed — Graeco-Macedonian, Arab, and Jewish — with Arabs "probably"
forming the numerical bulk while Hellenized elites staffed the administration. Old
Semitic city names persisted or resurfaced whenever direct Roman rule slackened.

## Ball's Interpretation (THESIS)

Ball argues it would be "a mistake to view the Decapolis as somehow Hellenistic" — in his
reading it was a creation of Pompey's Roman annexation of Syria, not a Seleucid league or
Hellenistic institution, and the term itself may have been "simply propagandistic,"
asserting a Roman presence in an otherwise "almost wholly Arab" area. Ball further notes
the Nabataeans probably never used the term themselves. He explicitly rejects framing the
Decapolis as a "Hellenic alliance" against Judaea and Nabataea, observing that all three
were under indirect Roman rule, their territories were not even contiguous (Judaea and
Nabataea extended into Decapolis territory, cities moved between administrations), and
the Nabataean capital [[bosra|Bosra]] itself sat within the Decapolis "enclave." Ball
explicitly rejects calling the Decapolis an "island of Hellenism" or "unambiguously
Greek" in public character. This is Ball's interpretive position; mainstream scholarship
(e.g. Fergus Millar, *The Roman Near East*, 1993) reads the Roman Near East as more
substantively Graeco-Roman — see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]] for the debate.

## Individual Cities

At Qanawat (Roman Canatha) and the associated sacred site of Si' (Seia), Nabataean
cultural influence was, per Ball, "uppermost" and Hellenistic influence "least" of all
Decapolis cities. Capitolias (Bayt Ra's) was founded AD 97/8 under Nerva/Trajan, minted
its own coins, and used mixed Greek/Nabataean inscriptions. Scythopolis (Beth Shean),
per Josephus, was the largest Decapolis city and probably had a Jewish-majority
population — the only Decapolis city to do so; Ball disputes a claim that Scythopolis
"describes itself" as Greek, noting only a single inscription (not "the city") made that
claim. Pella (Tabaqat Fahl) shows near-continuous occupation since the 4th millennium BC.
Gadara (Umm Qays) was a noted center of Hellenistic learning, though its name is
pre-Macedonian/Semitic and Nabataean influence there was strong. Abila (Qweilbeh) had an
estimated Roman-period population of 8,000–10,000.
