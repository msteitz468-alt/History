## Overview

**Hatra**, in the Iraqi desert northwest of the Tigris, was a Parthian client city with
an attested "Arab" population, known chiefly from over 500 local Aramaic-dialect
inscriptions and modern archaeology rather than surviving narrative sources. It withstood
two major Roman sieges before falling to the Sasanian Persians c. 240 CE, and in the
2010s suffered severe deliberate vandalism at the hands of ISIS.

## Origins and Site

Strabo, discussing the Euphrates–Tigris route, never mentions Hatra, suggesting it began
as a modest settlement around a religious sanctuary visited by nomads, perhaps in the
1st century CE. The name derives from Aramaic *hadr*, "enclosure." The site drew on the
seasonal Wadi Tharthar and internal wells for water. Hatra functioned as a trade
way-station rather than a central node like [[palmyra]]; the Peutinger Table shows it
linking Singara/Nisibis with Seleucia-Ctesiphon and onward routes to [[characene]] and
the Gulf, and CORONA satellite imagery has identified long-distance and local road traces
connecting Hatra to its neighbors.

## Fortifications and the Roman Sieges

Hatra's major building phase followed a failed siege by **Trajan** in 116 CE, during a
wider revolt of Mesopotamia and [[kingdom-of-edessa|Edessa]] against Rome; the siege was
abandoned owing to heat and insect swarms. Lord **Nasru**, a major ruler (titled "great
priest of the god Shamash"), built the eastern (and perhaps northern) gate by c. 138 CE.

**Septimius Severus** besieged Hatra twice, probably in 193/4 and 197/8 CE, in part
because its king had backed his rival Pescennius Niger, and reportedly for its wealth —
Cassius Dio describes "a vast number of offerings to the Sun-God [Shamash] as well as
vast sums of money." Both sieges failed. Herodian describes clay pots of stinging
insects catapulted into the Roman ranks, causing mass casualties from stings and heat
exhaustion; Dio describes long-range Hatran archery, siege engines, and bituminous
naphtha used to destroy Roman siege equipment and soldiers. Severus's troops reportedly
mutinied, and the second siege was abandoned after twenty days. Despite the failures,
Severus took the title "Arabicus," and the siege is depicted on the fourth panel of the
Arch of Severus in Rome.

After Severus's failed siege, Hatra's defenders raised ten-metre defensive walls
supplemented with bastions up to fifteen metres, where an intact ballista (siege
catapult) was later found, now in the Baghdad Museum. Modern archaeology rates Hatra's
siege-works among "the most extensive of any from Near Eastern antiquity" — some 24 km
of circumvallation, contravallation, and a walled Roman camp are still traceable.

## Religion

Hatra has been called a "pre-Islamic Mecca" for the density of its shrines. Its central
sacred enclosure was accessed via three large eastern gates and contained the Temple of
Maren (the sun god, also identified with Shamash, depicted on city coinage with an eagle
symbol), a "Hellenistic Temple," the Temple of Allat (equated by Romans with al-Uzzai/
Venus), the Temple of the Triad (Maren, Barmaren, Marten), a temple to Shahiru, the
Square Temple (Shamash), and Parthian-style domed-arch *iwans*. At least fourteen further
temples stood outside the central enclosure but within the walls, dedicated to deities
including Nabu, Nanaia, Nergal (linked with Heracles), and Atargatis — a mix of
Mesopotamian, Greek, Syrian, and Arabian religious influences, alongside a likely Persian
fire temple. Hatra's Aramaic inscriptions refer to kings of *ʿrb* ("Arab," used as a
place-name) and *rby* ("Arabs," inhabitants); two inscriptions distinguish "Arabs" and
"Hatrans" as separate population groups, though the precise relationship is unclear. The
title used by Hatra's rulers was "Lord" until the reign of **Sanatruq I** (c. 162/3–176/7
CE), after which "king" was adopted.

## Fall to the Sasanians

By its final decades Hatra had become a Parthian client with kings titled outright
"king" (a change likely sanctioned from Ctesiphon amid the Marcus Aurelius-era wars).
Under its last king, **Sanatruq II**, Hatra shifted toward rapprochement with Rome,
receiving a Roman garrison (IX Cohors Maurorum Gordianae) after 235, attested by a Latin
dedication to Sol Invictus/Shamash by the tribune Quintus Petronius Quintianus. Ardashir
I, after an abortive early siege, returned with his son Shapur and sacked the city in
**240**, deporting its population to Persia; Cassius Dio says Ardashir intended Hatra as
a "base for attacking the Romans." Stefan Hauser dates the fall precisely to between 12
April 240 and 1 April 241. A legend preserved by al-Tabari — that Sanatruq's daughter
Nadira betrayed the city for love of Shapur — is treated with appropriate skepticism by
modern scholarship. The SKZ inscription lists the surrounding district, called "Arabia,"
among Shapur's conquered territories; Sasanian attention then turned south toward the
Gulf and eastern Arabia. Following its fall Hatra was permanently abandoned: Ammianus
Marcellinus, retreating with Julian's army from Persia in 363, described it as "an old
city lying in the midst of a desert and long since abandoned."

## Modern Destruction

Hatra suffered severe vandalism after the 2003 Iraq invasion and the rise of ISIS.
Statues at the Mosul Museum, including one of King Sanatruq II, were smashed with
sledgehammers, and a famous Nergal/Cerberus-like relief was stolen between 2014 and 2017.
Scholars (notably Philip Wood) have framed ISIS's iconoclasm at Hatra and nearby Nineveh
as a deliberate re-enactment of Muhammad's destruction of the Kaaba idols, paralleling the
Taliban's 1999 destruction of the Bamiyan Buddhas.

## Historiography

Hatra's history is poorly attested in classical narrative sources, which barely mention
the city outside the Trajan and Severus sieges; the modern picture rests overwhelmingly
on its Aramaic epigraphic corpus and site archaeology. The extent and character of the
Hatran "Arab" population remains debated — Segal's judgment that "there can be no doubt
of the Arab character of a significant, almost certainly the dominant, section of its
population" represents one influential reading, but the precise relationship between
groups labeled "Arab" and "Hatran" in the inscriptions is unresolved.
