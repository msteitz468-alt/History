## Geographic Description

**Moundville** lies just south of modern Tuscaloosa, Alabama, on the Black Warrior River — among the best-preserved regional Mississippian civic-ceremonial centers (Bruce D. Smith, CHNPA I.1, ch. 5). It anchors a Black Warrior valley polity landscape within the broader [[mississippian-culture]] mosaic of the Eastern Woodlands.

## Developmental Trajectory

Smith’s complex-chiefdom exemplar:

- **~A.D. 1000**: small **single-level** mound center established.
- **By ~A.D. 1200**: extended authority over a number of neighboring polities; grew to **over 100 hectares**, with **twenty mounds** constructed around a plaza of **thirty-two hectares** in a relatively short period of time.
- Growth is accompanied by increased variety and quality of **prestige goods**, elite burials, and more complex mortuary and corporate ceremonies — material record likely of **tribute and alliance** (exact nature hard to discern archaeologically).

Secondary surveys (Snow et al. and regional literature) also cite ~29 mounds across ~75 ha and residential-to-necropolis transitions in later phases; reconcile counts as source-dependent. Peebles’s work on Moundville decline (“Paradise Lost, Strayed, and Stolen: Prehistoric Social Devolution in the Southeast,” 1986) is Smith’s bibliographic pointer for the chiefdom’s later devolution — complementary to the chapter’s use of Moundville at peak as a multi-mound complex-chiefdom type site.

## Why It Mattered

Archaeologically, larger multi-mound centers with rich elite burials and complex mortuary programs are the primary means of recognizing regional-scale or “complex” [[chiefdom]]s that dominated and integrated other polities to varying degrees (Smith). Moundville is the textbook illustration of that scale-up from a single-level center. Prestige-goods exchange and [[southeastern-ceremonial-complex]] iconography (including the well-known Rattlesnake Disk / hand-and-eye palette in secondary literature) link Moundville into pan-southeastern elite networks without requiring state-level organization — classification remains within the [[north-american-states-debate]] frame applied to Mississippian polities generally.

## Historiography

Smith uses Moundville briefly as a peak complex-chiefdom exemplar rather than a full developmental monograph. Decline narrative: Christopher Peebles 1986. Broader Moundville research tradition (Knight, Steponaitis, and others) elaborates ceramic chronology, mortuary ranking, and political cycling beyond Smith’s chapter.
