## Overview

**Najran** was a South Arabian oasis and caravan stop that became the region's most
important Christian community — and the site of one of late antiquity's most notorious
religious massacres, in 523 CE (see [[najran-massacre-523]]).

## Early History

Najran was targeted by the Roman prefect **Aelius Gallus**'s expedition of 26–24 BCE (see
[[aelius-gallus-expedition-26bce]]), which captured the city in 24 BCE. Roman casualties
from the campaign left at least one cavalry epitaph in the region. Centuries later, in
the al-Namara inscription (328 CE), Najran — called "the city of Shammar," after the
Himyarite king Shammar Yuharish, under whose control it then lay — was the target of a
campaign by the frontier ruler Mara al-Qays (see [[lakhmids]]).

## Christianization

Two differing traditions describe Najran's conversion to Christianity, both plausibly
linked to [[al-hira]]: (1) a merchant, **Hannan**, traveled to Constantinople, was
baptized at al-Hira, and introduced Christianity to Najran around 450; (2) a Syrian
captive named **Fimiyyun**, enslaved at Najran, converted his owner and the wider
community through devout prayer — a story with a related miracle-legend involving one
Abdallah b. Thamir, and a further variant in which a Himyarite king converts in secret.
Fisher treats a connection to al-Hira as "highly plausible" regardless of which narrative
is taken literally, and cites Christian Robin's argument that by the 5th century Najran
was al-Hira's principal trading partner. The first bishops of Najran were consecrated by
Philoxenus of Mabbug at the start of the 6th century.

Christology at Najran was mixed: Nestorian Christians were present, but many Najranites
adopted Monophysite Christianity, perhaps as a counterweight to Nestorian dominance
further north in the Gulf; other "heretical" groups also settled there, including
Phantasiasts and exiled Julianist Monophysites from al-Hira. New Christian inscriptions
discovered at nearby **Hima** (c. 90 km from Najran) reinforce these Najran–al-Hira
religious and trade links, and show the Najrani Christian community using early Arabic
script — distinct from the South Arabian script used by Jewish Himyar — and the dating
era of the Roman province of Arabia, suggesting a deliberate self-alignment with northern
Arab Christians over Himyarite identity, possibly mediated by [[kinda]].

## Persecution and Massacre

Religious tension at Najran predates the great massacre: in the 470s a Christian priest,
Azqir, was executed there with a rabbi's approval. By the late 5th century Himyarite
kings had begun a broader persecution of Najran's Christians. This culminated in **523**,
when the Himyarite king Joseph (Dhu Nuwas) besieged and massacred the Christian
population — see [[najran-massacre-523]] for the event itself, its casualty figures, and
its aftermath.

## Historiography

Najran's early Christian history rests on a genuinely mixed and partly legendary
tradition (the Chronicle of Seert and Arab-Islamic sources give differing conversion
narratives), cross-checked against a small but growing corpus of Sabaic and Arabic
inscriptions from Najran and Hima. The 523 massacre itself is comparatively
well-documented and is treated on its own page.
