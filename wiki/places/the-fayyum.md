# The Fayyum (Arsinoite Nome)

The **Fayyum** is a large triangular depression west of the Nile, about 100 km south of Cairo,
watered from the river by the **Bahr Yusuf** ("Joseph's Canal") through the natural gap in the
hills at Lahun. Drained and reclaimed by the early Ptolemies, it became one of the most
intensively documented agricultural regions of the ancient world — the heartland of Greek
military settlement in Egypt and, through the survival of papyri in its abandoned edge-villages,
the cradle of [[papyrology|papyrology]].

## Geographic description

The depression drains north into the **Birket Qarun**, a lake some 45 m below sea level. Its
Egyptian name was simply "The Lake" (New Kingdom *P3jm*), the origin — through Coptic *Phiom* — of
the name "Fayyum." Inhabited since the fifth millennium BCE, it had by the Old Kingdom a capital,
**Shedet** (Medinet el-Fayyum), and a cult of the crocodile god **Sobek** (Souchos). Dynasty 12
pharaohs, especially Amenemhet III, built pyramids and temples at Lahun and Hawara (the
"Labyrinth" Herodotus claimed to have seen).

## Reclamation and the Arsinoite nome

Under [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]] and [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Ptolemy II]] (c. 310–250 BCE),
Macedonian engineers modified the Lahun dyke, cut high-level canals along the desert edges, and
**lowered the Lake of Moiris**, roughly **tripling the cultivable area to about 1,500 km²**. New
villages were founded by settling Greeks and transferring Egyptians from other nomes; around
267 BCE the region became the **Arsinoite nome**, named for **Arsinoe II**, sister-wife of
Ptolemy II. This reclamation was the showpiece of Ptolemaic [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic]]
land policy: it created the estates of cleruchs (military settlers) — like the 10,000-aroura plot
of the finance minister Apollonios, managed by **Zenon** (see [[ptolemaic-papyri]]) — and the
waterwheel and Archimedean screw made perennial irrigation possible for vineyards and orchards.

## History of settlement

Prosperity was sustained, more or less, into the late second century CE, when several villages
show severe depopulation from the **Antonine plague** (the smallpox-like epidemic of 167 CE; see [[late-antiquity]]). The third century saw
recovery as large estates took over vacant land for wine. Then, in the **early fourth century, for
reasons still unclear, many edge-villages lost adequate water and were partly or wholly
abandoned** — the event that sealed their papyri in the sand. By the fifth century the Fayyum was
predominantly Christian, dense with churches and monasteries (e.g. Naqlun). The **Arab conquest
of the 640s**, though strongly resisted, did not greatly change settlement; the real break to the
medieval order came with the Fatimid conquest (969) and the eleventh-century shocks.

## Why it mattered

- **Greek settlement and the cleruchy.** The reclaimed Fayyum was where the Ptolemaic state
  planted its Greek soldier-settlers; its archives document the making of a Greco-Egyptian rural
  society.
- **Documentary exceptionalism.** Renowned for three classes of find: the Roman-period
  **[[fayyum-mummy-portraits|"Fayyum" mummy portraits]]**; Ptolemaic and Roman terracotta
  figurines; and "thousands of papyri" in eight languages spanning the fourth century BCE to the
  eleventh CE. Its best-preserved town, **[[karanis|Karanis]]**, plus Tebtunis, Soknopaiou Nesos,
  Dionysias, and Narmouthis, are the type-sites of village archaeology in Egypt.
- **A cautionary archaeology.** Early excavators (Petrie in the 1880s; then Grenfell and Hunt,
  Jouguet, Rubensohn) dug for papyri with little care for stratigraphy, while farmers stripped the
  mounds for *sebakh* fertiliser — a destruction only checked by the careful American and Italian
  excavations of the 1920s–30s.
