# Oc Eo

**Oc Eo** (Oc-èo) is the largest known **Protoclassic** settlement in Southeast Asia and the principal archaeological complex of the [[funan|Funan]] maritime culture in the Mekong delta. It lies at the village of Oc Eo, about **25 km from the sea**, at the foot of the granitic **Ba Thê** hills. A canal leads seaward; **11 km** toward the shore another canal intersects it at **Ta Kèo**, perhaps the outer harbour. Primary synthesis: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]] Ch. 4; also referenced in broader Angkor/Funan literature.

## Geographic Description

The site covers **450 hectares**, enclosed by **five** exterior rectangular moats and **four** ramparts measuring **1.5 km wide by 3 km long**, plus canal infrastructure. Related sites lie within a ~20 km radius. **Thirty-five mounds** with brick on granite foundations may be among the **first brick structures** built in Southeast Asia, possibly from the fifth century. The delta setting is metal-poor; metal and other exotics arrived through maritime and riverine exchange. Pottery at Oc Eo and Angkor Borei is closer to later Cambodian ceramics than to Sa Huynh Austronesian assemblages—supporting a Mon-Khmer rather than Austronesian affiliation for the Funan cultural zone.

## History of Control and Settlement

Oc Eo flourished within the **Funan** network known from Chinese sources (third–seventh centuries). Chinese reports describe walled villages, palaces, books and archives, Indic script, and taxes in gold, silver, and perfume; Funan ships carried up to 100 paddlers; cloves from eastern Indonesia and iron from Philippine “Dan-lan” islanders moved through Funan’s South China Sea links. Extensive canals suggest territorial organization over a large proportion of the Mekong delta—but **not beyond it**. Historians no longer accept 1960s maximal maps stretching Funan across the Menam Valley and Malay Peninsula as a Chinese-style centralized empire; Funan was “probably a cluster of polities in a deltaic arena.”

**Angkor Borei** (Takeo River) offers a complementary inland centre: radiocarbon from the fourth century BCE to the seventh century CE; abandoned at the end of the Protoclassic as Funan crumbled; hypothesized as Naravarnagara (Vickery places that capital ~60 km northeast). Inscriptions K.557 and K.600 (both **611 CE**) imply that the last recorded Funan king, Rudravarman, had his capital there; the *Liang Shu* distance of 500 *li* (~200 km) from the sea fits Angkor Borei better than coastal Oc Eo.

**Oc-èo archaeological culture and Chinese references to Funan both end in the seventh century.** The Mekong Delta was “never an important center of civilization again”; a new society coalesced upstream toward Zhenla and eventually [[angkor|Angkor]]. Many authorities link Oc Eo’s disappearance to the rise of Melaka Straits routes that bypassed peninsula portages and Mekong ports—as Straits ports prospered in the Early Classic, Protoclassic upper-peninsula and delta ports vanished.

## Craft, Trade, and Writing

Specialized crafts: glass beads, pottery (kiln supports, wheel, paddle), tin pendants, gold ornaments, **23+** types of precious stones. Oc Eo has yielded the **largest glass-bead assemblage** of any Seasian site. Indo-Pacific / *mutisalah* beads were mostly imported from India; suggestions that Oc Eo manufactured and exported them remain unproven for the majority.

Oldest known **writing** in Seasia appears here: Brāhmī on seals, intaglios, and rings of the **second–fifth centuries CE**, apparently **imported** carved objects rather than written on-site. **No local coinage** is attested—only foreign coins—contrasting with the extensive Early Classic coinage of [[dvaravati|Dvaravati]]. A stone **dharmacakra** from Oc Eo may date **500–600 CE** (Indorf), part of a broader wheel tradition from the delta to Yarang before classic Dvaravati.

Pale green hexagonal glass beads of types found at Oc Eo also appear at Ban Don Ta Phet (early fourth century BCE) and Pasemah (South Sumatra)—deep Preclassic bead networks. Funan/Oc Eo pottery occurs at Kuala Selinsing (Perak) with a fourth–fifth-century Sanskrit carnelian seal; a Pontian (Pahang) boat was “covered with heaped up sherds” of Oc Eo–style jars, with rice grains on the hull. U Thong and other Chao Phraya sites share beads, lead/tin ear ornaments, and seals with Oc Eo. Disc-like *gacuk* objects known from Oc Eo (fifth century) have a pan-Seasian distribution into Java and Singapore centuries later.

## Why It Mattered

Oc Eo mattered as:

1. the **primate Protoclassic port-settlement** of the Mekong delta mandala world;
2. a **craft and import hub** linking Indian Ocean, South China Sea, and inland Mon-Khmer societies;
3. a source of the earliest writing objects and some of the earliest brick architecture in the region;
4. a negative case for local minting and for Chinese over-centralization of Funan.

Its decline is a hinge in [[seasian-classic-periodization|periodization]]: end of Protoclassic coastal primacy, rise of Early Classic Straits and upstream Khmer centres.

## Historiography and Source Notes

Louis Malleret’s mid-twentieth-century excavations established Oc Eo in world archaeology; subsequent work refined canal systems, dating, and the Funan–Zhenla transition. Debates: unified state vs cluster of polities; location of capital (Oc Eo vs Angkor Borei vs others); degree of “Indianization” vs local agency; cause of seventh-century end (Straits bypass, political conquest narratives of Zhenla, environmental change). Chinese sources over-read centralization; archaeology cannot yet decide unity. Collection coverage for Funan/Oc Eo is moderate-to-strong in secondary synthesis (Miksic & Goh, Coe, Higham traditions).
