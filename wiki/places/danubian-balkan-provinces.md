# The Danubian and Balkan Provinces

The lands Rome conquered between the Adriatic and the **Danube** — Noricum, Pannonia, Dalmatia,
Moesia, and Thrace — securing the **overland route between Italy and the East** that became "for
nearly four centuries the principal military axis of the Roman empire." Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 13 (the Danubian and Balkan provinces, **J. J. Wilkes**).

## Strategic significance

Wilkes opens with the Younger Pliny's "*Magnum est stare in Danubii ripa*" — and with
[[augustus|Augustus']] boast in the *Res Gestae* of advancing the boundary of **Illyricum to the
Danube** through his stepson **[[tiberius|Tiberius]]**. The key was the **middle Danube basin**
(Pannonia, above all the Drava–Sava region around Mursa and Sirmium): "not until the middle
course of the Danube had been secured could Rome hold and exploit the overland route between
Italy and her eastern territories." This Pannonia was "the keystone of the empire's defensive
arch against the northern peoples"; the highway it guarded (Zagreb–Belgrade–Niš–Sofia, and south
to Thessalonica) carried every later civil war. Wilkes' sharp contrast: in the imperial
reckoning **Germany beyond the Rhine was expendable** (discarded AD 15/16) — **Illyricum and the
Danube were not**.

## The advance to the Danube (43 BCE – AD 6)

The Balkans saw "the death-agony of the Roman Republic" (Brutus' command, the pact of Brundisium
of 40 BCE dividing Octavian's West from Antony's East at Scodra). Octavian campaigned **beyond
the Adriatic in 35–33 BCE** (the *Illyrian War*, the garrison at Siscia, the subjection of the
Delmatae and recovery of Gabinius' lost standards) — operations launched largely to **build his
military reputation against Antony**, furnishing the first instalment of his triple triumph (29
BCE). In Macedonia, **M. Crassus** defeated the Bastarnae and Getae (29–28, killing King Deldo,
controversially denied the *spolia opima*).

The decisive conquest was the **Bellum Pannonicum**: building on Vinicius and Agrippa (14–13
BCE), **Tiberius** overcame the Breuci of the Sava (with Scordiscan help, 12 BCE) and in four
more campaigns (to 8 BCE) **completed the conquest south of the Drava and carried the boundary of
Illyricum to the Danube** — the defeated Pannonians disarmed and their young men sold into
slavery. **Noricum** (the Alpine kingdom) was annexed about the same time.

## The Pannonian–Dalmatian revolt (AD 6–9)

The strategic value of the new conquests "was amply demonstrated when it held firm, though only
just," during the **great revolt of AD 6–9** — the most dangerous war of Augustus' reign, which
tied down up to ten legions under Tiberius and ended only with the brutal suppression of the two
Batos. Its near-success forced the cancellation of the planned assault on the Marcomanni; its
collapse cleared the way for the [[battle-of-teutoburg-forest-9ce|Varian disaster]] (the German
catastrophe followed days after the Pannonian victory).

## Organization

Out of these wars Augustus created the provinces of **Pannonia** and **Dalmatia** (from the old
*Illyricum*) and **Moesia** on the lower Danube. **Thrace** remained a **client kingdom** under
the Sapaean dynasty (Rhoemetalces I and successors), repeatedly drawing Roman armies into its
quarrels (the Bessi vs. Odrysae; the three-year Thracian war under L. Piso, 12–10 BCE) before
its annexation as a province under Claudius (46 CE). Beyond the river lay the recurring menace of
the **Dacians** (united briefly under Burebista) and the first collisions with the Iranian
**Sarmatians**.

## Significance

Wilkes' Danube is the empire's true strategic spine. The conquest of the middle Danube under
Augustus and Tiberius — far more than the abandoned German adventure — was the **enduring
achievement** of the northern wars, fixing the **Rhine–Danube frontier** and the land bridge
between the empire's halves whose loss, centuries later, would mark the divergence of West and
East. The archaeological record, Wilkes notes, shows the imported, military character of early
Roman presence — soldiers and settlers, with "little or nothing" owed to indigenous Danubian
traditions.
