## Geographic Description

The Calchaquí Valleys are formed by two rivers, the Calchaquí and the Santa María, which meet at Cafayate in modern Salta province. The system encompasses three distinct subregions: the Pulares valley (south), the Calchaquí valley proper (centre), and the Yocavil valley (north, extending into modern Tucumán province). Elevations range from roughly 1,500 to over 3,000 metres above sea level; the landscape combines dry mountain terrain (average precipitation c. 250 mm), irrigable river bottoms, and high-altitude pastures for camelids. Dense archaeological terracing and village fortifications attest to intensive prehispanic land use.

## History of Control and Settlement

**Prehispanic era.** The Santamaría culture dominated the Yocavil and Calchaquí valleys in the Late Period (C.E. 850–c. 1480), practising intensive irrigation agriculture with terraces and dams, herding llamas, and living in fortified hilltop villages some housing more than 1,500 inhabitants. Federations formed among Santamaría communities, linked by shared Cacan-Diaguita language and culture. The Quebrada de Humahuaca (further north) was a distinct corridor with its own multiethnic dynamic (Omaguacas, Chichas, Atacamas).

**Inka occupation (c. 1480–1535).** During the reign of Tupaq Inka, northwestern Argentina was partially incorporated into Tawantinsuyu, but "the Inka did not find it any easier to penetrate the valleys than the subsequent European invaders did." Imperial administrative installations (*tambos*, *pukará* fortresses such as Andalgalá) controlled communication corridors rather than achieving deep social integration. The Inka used *mitmaq* transplants to buffer the valleys against Chaco groups. Relations "were always tense, as was usual in Tawantinsuyu's frontier zones."

**Spanish conquest attempts (1553–1585).** Spanish incursions into the Calchaquí valleys met "total and completely successful" indigenous resistance that prevented permanent settlement in the high valleys throughout the sixteenth century. Spaniards were forced to site their first cities (Santiago del Estero 1553, San Miguel del Tucumán 1565, Esteco 1567) on the low plains, attacking valley groups from a Santiago base. The first great Calchaquí uprising (1560–1563) was led by the kuraka Juan Calchaquí of Tolombón, who "effectively deterred further Spanish settlement in the valleys until 1585."

**Limited encomienda compliance (1585–1630).** From around 1585 to 1630 some valley groups reluctantly fulfilled encomienda obligations. The ethnic mosaic was complex: the Pulares "tended toward silent acceptance and mute reticence" while the Quilmes and Tolombones fought openly; "almost every gradation" of accommodation and resistance existed between these poles. The absence of centralized leadership complicated both Spanish negotiations and indigenous coordination — confederations formed "sporadically . . . as circumstances demanded."

**Great Calchaquí Rebellion (1630–1643).** In 1630 a pan-valley rebellion mobilized most indigenous groups. It lasted thirteen years, forcing Spanish withdrawal. Even after suppression the Spaniards "left the region as soon as the repressive campaign was over. Clearly they had realized that short of uprooting the whole population there was no prospect of controlling this region."

**Bohorquez episode and final war (1656–1664).** An Andalusian adventurer, Pedro Chamijo (alias Bohorquez), appointed himself the Calchaquí "Inka" and led the last major uprising from 1656 to 1664. Governor Mercado y Villacorta suppressed it and ordered the populations dispersed: the Quilmes were transplanted over 1,200 kilometres to a reducción near Buenos Aires, where their number fell to 455 by 1680. "The resistance had been overcome. But it had endured over 130 years since the beginning of the conquest, and it was stamped out only at the cost of practically depopulating the valleys."

## Why It Mattered

The Calchaquí Valleys constituted the most durable zone of indigenous resistance in the entire La Plata Basin colonial project. Three features account for this:

1. **Terrain.** High-altitude fortified settlements and limited entry corridors made sustained Spanish military campaigns logistically difficult.
2. **Social structure.** Dispersed kuraka authority and inter-ethnic variability meant there were no high-value leadership targets whose capture would end resistance; "lack of centralized native leadership, and thus of leaders, with whom to negotiate" frustrated Spanish negotiating strategies.
3. **Economic peripherality.** The valleys were not on the main Potosí supply road and lacked the easily exploitable surplus the mita required, reducing Spanish incentive to invest the force needed for full conquest.

The 130-year resistance fundamentally shaped the colonial geography of Tucumán: the province's major Spanish cities remained on the plains, Potosí road traffic bypassed the most resistant valleys, and Spanish settlement patterns were distorted for the entire colonial era. The violent dispersal of surviving Calchaquí peoples after 1664 destroyed their communities and scattered their populations as yanaconas across Salta, Tucumán, and La Rioja — an act of colonial ethnicide whose consequences are visible in the region's archaeology and oral tradition.
