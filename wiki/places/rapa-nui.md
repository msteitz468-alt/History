# Rapa Nui (Easter Island)

## Overview

**Rapa Nui** (Easter Island), the most isolated inhabited island on Earth, is in
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (McNiven, Ch 22) both an emblem of the reach of
**[[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement|Polynesian voyaging]]** and a contested case study in
**human–environment relations**. Settled around **800–700 BP** in the "near-instantaneous"
colonization of East Polynesia, it lay near the eastern limit of the Polynesian world — and
perhaps not the limit, since sweet potato and Polynesian chickens hint at contact with South
America 3,000 km further east.

## The Moai and Chiefly Society

Between ~800 and 500 BP, Rapa Nui chiefs held sway over hundreds of coastal territorial
districts (**kainga**), each a village of commoners' houses, chiefly and priestly residences,
and a monumental stone plaza (**ahu**) bearing the island's famous **moai** — huge
anthropomorphic carvings weighing up to **80 tonnes**. Of the 500+ recorded moai, about **200
remain unfinished** in the **Rano Raraku** quarry, a frozen snapshot of an interrupted carving
tradition.

## The "Ecocide" Debate

By European contact (1722) some **twenty tree species had disappeared** in the wake of
Polynesian settlement. Rapa Nui became the textbook case of self-inflicted **"ecocide"** — a
society felling its forests (in part to move statues) into collapse, popularized as a parable
of environmental overshoot. CWH IV explicitly **qualifies** this: following **Hunt and Lipo**,
McNiven holds that deforestation's "causal association with societal collapse has been
**overstated**" while the **devastating impacts of European contact** (disease, slaving) have
been **underestimated**. Per the wiki's practice the position is recorded, not adjudicated; it
connects to the standing debate over [[geographic-determinism-diamond|geographic and
environmental determinism]].

## Significance

Rapa Nui demonstrates both the **monumental capacity** of a small, isolated Polynesian
chiefdom and the danger of reading island ecological change as a simple morality tale of
collapse. It anchors the revisionist turn in Pacific environmental history.
