## Geographic Description

Canterbury lies in east Kent, the most economically and culturally advanced territory of early Anglo-Saxon England. It derived its name from Cantium, a probably pre-Roman designation of the region which became the Roman civitas Cantiacorum (civitas of the Cantiaci). The first kings of Kent may have been military commanders who took control of this Romano-British civitas, with Canterbury as its centre.

## Roman and Sub-Roman Period

Canterbury was a major centre of the Romano-British civitas system. As a former Roman urban centre, it retained physical infrastructure — including the church of St Martin — that outlasted the collapse of the Western Empire. When Augustine and his missionaries arrived in 597, Bertha, the Frankish Christian queen of [[æthelberht-of-kent]], was already practising her faith at the church of St Martin (HE I, 25–26).

## The Gregorian Mission and the Metropolitan See

In the spring of 597, Augustine established himself and his monks at Canterbury by agreement with Æthelberht (HE I, 25). Pope Gregory I's original plan had envisaged London and York as twin metropolitan sees for Britain, each with twelve suffragans (HE I, 29). London never became an archbishopric. Canterbury, however, emerged as the first archiepiscopal see. Bede styles Augustine "archbishop of Britain" (HE II, 2). The expectation from an early stage was that Canterbury's authority would embrace the whole of Britain.

The prestige accruing to Æthelberht from hosting the metropolitan see was immense. "The potential for prestige to accrue to Aethelberht must have been immense." Successive archbishops — Laurentius, Mellitus, Justus, and then Theodore (668–90) and Beorhtwold (692–731) — cultivated the concept of an archbishop or primate of the whole island of Britain.

## Canterbury and Anglo-Saxon Nomenclature

Kirby argues that it was Canterbury's ecclesiastical organisation which imposed the territorial designations "East," "West," "South," and "Middle" on the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. The records of the Councils of Hertford (672) and Hatfield (679/80) — both Canterbury records — are the earliest evidence for such designations in use. The symmetry of the directional names (East Saxon, West Saxon, South Saxon, etc.) "indicates a common Canterbury viewpoint." Canterbury's perspective from the east made those Saxons to the west "West Saxons," those to the north of the Thames "East Saxons," and so on.

## The Pagan Reaction

During Eadbald's paganism (c. 616–c. 624), the Gregorian mission was threatened with extinction. Mellitus and Justus fled to Gaul; Laurentius contemplated following. Canterbury itself, however, was never abandoned. The mission's survival in "its principal remaining seat at Canterbury in the heart of his [Eadbald's] territory" was the precondition for the eventual recovery. The legend of Laurentius' scourging by St Peter is located at the church of St Peter and St Paul (St Augustine's monastery, Canterbury), identifying it as early Canterbury hagiography.

## Cultural Significance

Kent and Canterbury were in advance of the Anglo-Saxon hinterland culturally and economically throughout the early medieval period. Canterbury was engaged in luxury trade with the Franks. The Kentish court hosted visiting ecclesiastics including Wilfrid and Benedict Biscop. Canterbury spearheaded the adoption of the silver sceatta coinage in Anglo-Saxon territories in the 670s, in response to monetary changes in Gaul. The kings of Kent maintained a personal involvement in the appointment of successive archbishops.
