# Aachen

**Aachen** (Latin *Aquisgranum*) was the fixed capital and palace complex of
[[charlemagne|Charles the Great]] in the imperial years, and the symbolic heart of the
[[carolingian-empire]]. Charles already issued his very first charter there
(13 January 769), but from the mid-790s it became his settled *sedes regni* — "the
capital seat of the realm." Between 807 and 813, of his late imperial charters all but
one were issued at Aachen.

## Why It Mattered

Per [[sources/nelson-king-and-emperor-2019|Janet Nelson]], Aachen's significance was that
it was *new*. It "had never been among the seats of the ancient Caesars but was, instead,
a new place of power, of *nova antiquitas* and *antiqua novitas*. On balance, there was
more new than old." Charles built an empire's centre from scratch in the heart of Francia
rather than inheriting Rome's or Constantinople's.

Aachen was simultaneously:

- **A seat of government** — "at once pulling power centripetally into its orbit, and
  radiating power out from itself, primarily by the issuing of capitularies and the
  deployment of [[missi-dominici|missi]]." Archbishop Hildebald of Cologne was fixed there
  permanently (Frankfurt 794, c.55) as head of the royal chapel.
- **An intellectual centre** — site of "the recovering of Aristotelian dialectic and its
  application to theology," a phenomenon Nelson says "would not really recur outside
  episcopal schools before the high Middle Ages." A "textual community … also an oral one":
  Theodulf, Moduin, Fridugis, Dungal worked there; Moduin sang of "Golden Rome's renewal."
- **A social centre** — the famous thermal baths, where "over a hundred men might be in
  the water together," combined bathing with scriptural discussion.

The **palace church** — dedicated below to the Virgin and in its upper gallery to the
Saviour — was legally the town's parish/baptismal church, "neither a monastic shrine nor a
cathedral." In it Charles crowned [[louis-the-pious|Louis the Pious]] co-emperor on
11 September 813; received the Byzantine envoys who acclaimed him *basileus* in 812; and was
buried on the day of his death, 28 January 814.

## Significance Over Time

Aachen's status as coronation church of the German kings endured for centuries after the
Carolingians, fixing Charles's "new place of power" as a permanent symbol of imperial
legitimacy in the medieval West.
