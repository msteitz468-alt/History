## Overview

The Pyramid of the Moon (Pirámide de la Luna) is the second-largest pyramid at [[teotihuacan]], situated at the northern terminus of the Avenue of the Dead. Its stepped mass closes the northern end of the city's monumental axis and faces directly onto the Moon Plaza, the great open space that served as the ceremonial climax of the avenue. Construction unfolded in at least seven stages, from roughly 100 BCE through the Xolalpan phase (ca. 350–550 CE), with the most dramatic enlargements occurring in the Miccaotli and Early Tlamimilolpa ceramic phases (ca. 80–250 CE). The pyramid's sacrificial tomb series offers the most systematically excavated evidence for ritual violence at Teotihuacan.

## Construction History

Construction stages are numbered earliest to latest; tombs are numbered in order of discovery, which can be confusing. The following represents Cowgill's (2015) ceramic-phase attributions, drawing on tabulations by Pedro Baños, Zeferino Ortega, and obsidian studies by William Parry and Shigeru Kabata (2004), and differs somewhat from Sugiyama and López Luján (2006).

| Stage | Likely Phase | Approximate Date | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Patlachique | 100–1 BCE | Before the Sun Pyramid |
| Second | Tzacualli | 1–100 CE | — |
| Third | Tzacualli | 1–100 CE | — |
| Fourth | Terminal Tzacualli / Early Miccaotli | 80–150 CE | After Sun Pyramid; Tombs 2 & 6 |
| Fifth | Miccaotli | 100–200 CE | Roughly coeval with FSP; Tomb 3 |
| Sixth | Early Tlamimilolpa | 170–250 CE | Tombs 4 & 5; Maya connections |
| Seventh | ca. 400 CE | — | Minor enlargement; rear platform in Xolalpan phase |

**Stage Four** was the transformative construction event. It enlarged the pyramid to approximately 89 × 89 m at its base, "covered with plaster-coated concrete," and made the Moon Pyramid the second largest pyramid at Teotihuacan — though the Ciudadela as a whole contains a greater total volume of construction material (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). Stage Four probably post-dates the Sun Pyramid's completion by only a short interval.

**Stage Five** extended the north face so the pyramid measured more than 104 m north-south, while the front face was cut back to provide a large projecting fore-platform — an arrangement analogous to the Sun Pyramid's fore-platform. "Both the Moon and Sun Pyramids face sizable plazas," implying "an interest in producing spectacles that could readily be viewed by large audiences, in addition to whatever more esoteric rites may have been carried out atop the pyramids" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**Stage Six** brought the structure close to its present size and contains Tomb 5, which shows significant Maya connections including jade pectorals in Maya style. Ceramics date Stage Six to the Early Tlamimilolpa phase; it is probably a little later than the Feathered Serpent Pyramid but unlikely to post-date 250 CE.

**Stage Seven** is a minor enlargement. A low platform added to its rear dates to the Xolalpan phase (ca. 450 CE). A trench excavated by Sugiyama and Cabrera at the pyramid's rear included approximately 6 percent Aztec ceramics, confirming some later Aztec activity at the site.

## Sacrificial Tomb Program

The Moon Pyramid's tombs are the best-documented series of dedicatory burials at Teotihuacan. Across all excavated tombs (excluding Tomb 4), they share: one to twelve human sacrificial victims (37 total); rich offerings in obsidian, greenstone, and perishable materials; sacrificed animals of fierce species associated with military orders (pumas, jaguars, wolves, eagles, rattlesnakes); scarcity of ceramics except Storm God jars; and "the absence of any individual likely to have been a Teotihuacan ruler" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**Tomb 2** (Stage Four, ground level): One bound human victim, two pumas, one wolf, nine eagles, three rattlesnakes, eight Storm God jars. A distinctive greenstone tableau shows an upright female statuette (31 cm high) standing on a rosette of bipointed obsidian knives, with a smaller recumbent male figure nearby. The female's display of breasts and genitalia is "unusual for Teotihuacan," where imagery "rarely shows or suggests genitalia." The tableau "was perhaps a dominant female figure standing over a male, whose subordination is indicated by his recumbent position and smaller size" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). The identities of these figures are unresolved.

**Tomb 6** (Stage Four, mid-level pit): Twelve human victims. Ten were bound and decapitated, their skulls and cervical vertebrae missing, "apparently... killed on the spot, then tossed haphazardly into the north side of the pit." Two others were richly attired and wore shell imitation-maxilla collars like those found on victims at the Feathered Serpent Pyramid. The unique greenstone mosaic figure and nine pairs of very large obsidian objects (34–53 cm long) are significant: one member of each pair represents a feathered serpent, the other an undulating bipointed form probably representing the Storm God's lightning staff. This pairing "is tempting to conjecture" represents "the Storm God... and the Feathered Serpent... as joint heads of the Teotihuacan pantheon" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**Tomb 3** (Stage Five): Four sacrificed men with rich offerings including heads of 18 animals and small greenstone figurines in cross-legged "tailor" position, wearing tau-shaped headdresses similar to those found in FSP offerings.

**Tomb 5** (Stage Six): Three high-status individuals seated in cross-legged tailor position — a posture also observed at Kaminaljuyú Pyramids A and B — with two Maya-style jade pectorals and obsidian assemblage linking to a cache at Altun Ha in Belize. This "indicates significant interaction between the Maya and Teotihuacan long before the events recorded on stelae at Tikal and elsewhere" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**Tomb 4** (Stage Six, rear at ground level): Seventeen human skulls with no offerings. The "disrespectful treatment of these victims suggests something quite different" from the other tombs, recalling the ten decapitated victims in Tomb 6.

## Moon Plaza and Setting

The Moon Pyramid faces south onto the Moon Plaza, approximately 135 m east-west by 175 m north-south. The plaza "forms the northern climax of the Avenue of the Dead" and "opens onto the Avenue without any barrier, in contrast to the platforms separating the Sun Plaza and the Ciudadela Plaza from the Avenue" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). The Avenue of the Dead "was not a thoroughfare connecting different districts of the city. Instead it is fitted for processions culminating in the Moon Plaza."

The plaza is flanked by eight stepped pyramids in [[talud-tablero]] style, with two more slightly to the sides. At its center is a low square platform (~22 m on a side) with stairways on all four sides. Just north of this, in front of the pyramid's fore-platform, stands the Building of the Altars, whose masonry features are set at 45-degree angles to the canonical Teotihuacan orientation — recalling intercardinal cosmogram motifs in sixteenth-century codices and in offerings at Tombs 2 and 6. The Quetzalpapalotl "Palace" adjoins the southwest corner of the Moon Plaza, with polychrome murals and carved stone pillar reliefs; its "principal occupants must have been of very high status" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

## Deity Associations

The great monolith known as the "Diosa de Agua" (Water Goddess, 3.19 m high, ~24 tons) was found face-down near a pyramid on the west side of the Moon Plaza. Its attire is "unmistakably womanly." Whether it represents a moon goddess, a maize goddess (Paulinyi 2013), or some other deity is unresolved. A second large stone was demolished at some unknown time, probably to destroy it as a pagan idol. Cowgill notes there is "no good evidence for" a pervasive "Great Goddess" at Teotihuacan (citing Paulinyi 2006). The Storm God jars and obsidian feathered-serpent figures in the tombs suggest strong associations with both the Feathered Serpent and the Storm God. The Aztec name "Moon Pyramid" may or may not reflect accurate memory of the structure's original dedication. The possibility that "the Moon Pyramid, the Sun Pyramid, and the FSP represented the astronomical triad of Moon, Sun, and Venus" remains speculative (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

A "high-status priesthood was surely associated with the Moon Pyramid" and was "probably powerful enough to be largely independent of civic units associated with other major structures such as the Sun Pyramid and the Ciudadela" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

## Archaeological Evidence

The Moon Pyramid project was directed by Saburo Sugiyama and Rubén Cabrera (Sugiyama and Cabrera 2007; Sugiyama and López Luján 2006, 2007). Obsidian analysis was conducted by William Parry and Shigeru Kabata (2004). The structure 6:N5W1, attached to Stages 6 and 7, yielded great quantities of obsidian debris and dates its last major construction stage to the 400s CE.

## Historiography

The Moon Pyramid's construction stages and their ceramic correlates remain somewhat contested. Sugiyama argues Stage Four was contemporary with the Feathered Serpent Pyramid, but Cowgill (2015) finds the ceramics in Stage Four fill suggest it was "somewhat earlier." The absolute dates adopted here follow Cowgill's ceramic-phase attribution. Interpretations of the tomb offerings — whether as cosmograms, dynastic dedications, or representations of the Teotihuacan pantheon — remain open. The identity of the "Diosa de Agua" monolith is unresolved; the "Great Goddess" hypothesis (Pasztory 1973) is now widely questioned. Maya connections visible in Tomb 5 have strengthened the case for early Teotihuacan-Maya interaction predating the Tikal stela records.
