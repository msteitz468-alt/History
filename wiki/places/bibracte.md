# Bibracte

## Geographic and Historical Description

Bibracte (Mont Beuvray) was the principal oppidum and capital of the Aedui, a major Gallic polity in central Gaul. It lies on a prominent height in the Morvan region of Burgundy, controlling routes between the Loire and Saône basins. In the first century BCE it was a major nucleated settlement of urban character with extensive fortifications, craft zones, and elite functions.

## Role in Classical and Historical Narrative

Classical sources (notably Caesar) identify Bibracte as the Aeduan capital. It features in accounts of Gallic politics and the Roman conquest.

## Significance in the Construction of the Modern Vision of the Celts

Napoleon III, as part of his scholarly programme supporting the *Histoire de Jules César* (1865–6), initiated large-scale excavation at Bibracte (entrusted to J.-G. Bulliot; published 1899). This was explicitly intended to provide archaeological background to the campaigns of Caesar and to foster national awareness of Gallic (Celtic) heritage and heroic resistance (Cunliffe 2018, Ch. 1, pp. 18–20, cache ~790–800, ~1720).

In the late twentieth century, President Mitterrand provided state funding for new multinational excavations at the site, echoing Napoleon III but in the service of a pan-European vision of Celtic roots alongside Roman and Christian heritage (Cunliffe 2018, Ch. 1, pp. 25–26, cache ~980–990).

The site thus exemplifies how archaeological investigation of oppida has been harnessed to successive political and identity projects while simultaneously supplying primary data for understanding Late Iron Age social complexity, production, and urbanization.

## Relation to Broader Systems

As one of the best-documented oppida, Bibracte illustrates the culmination of trends visible in earlier Hallstatt princely centres and La Tène developments: centralized settlement, craft specialization, coinage, and integration into wider exchange networks.
