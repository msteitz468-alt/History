# Institute for Advanced Study (Princeton)

**Founded 1930, Princeton, New Jersey.** Premier independent research institution designed as a haven for scholars free from teaching and administrative burdens. Einstein's primary base from his 1933 settlement in America until his death in 1955.

## Geographic and Institutional Description

Located in Princeton, New Jersey (adjacent to but independent of Princeton University). Designed by Abraham Flexner (first director) as an "intellectual hotel" or "paradise" for luminaries. Original faculty included Einstein, Oswald Veblen, Hermann Weyl, John von Neumann, and others. Einstein's office in Fuld Hall (later buildings added); he walked daily from 112 Mercer Street.

The Institute provided Einstein "freedom to pursue your great scientific work" (per 1952 offer language) with minimal duties. It enabled his late unified field efforts, political writings, and role as elder statesman of science.

## History of Control and Settlement

Recruited Einstein in 1932–33 amid Nazi rise (Flexner offer: $10,000 salary plus position for his stepdaughter). Einstein resigned Prussian Academy and renounced German citizenship; arrived US October 1933 (initial visits earlier). "Today I resolved to give up my Berlin position and shall be a bird of passage for the rest of my life." Full settlement 1934 at 112 Mercer Street; Institute as daily workplace.

Oppenheimer became director postwar; relationship with Einstein cordial but not intimate. Gödel (also refugee) became close walking companion. Kurt Gödel's US citizenship test chaperoned by Einstein (1947).

## Why This Place Mattered

- **Refuge and continuity**: Allowed Einstein to continue research and public role after forced exile. "After settling in Princeton in 1933, he never once in the remaining twenty-two years of his life left the United States, except for the brief cruise to Bermuda..."
- **Scientific work**: Site of final unified field theory attempts (non-symmetric metrics, correspondence with Schrödinger, Pauli visits). Daily blackboard work with assistants (Straus, Kaufman, Hoffmann). "Our equations are much more important to me. Politics is for the present, while our equations are for eternity."
- **Political and moral voice**: Drafted world-government statements, arms-control appeals, civil-liberties letters (Frauenglass, Rosenberg, Du Bois support) from Princeton home and Institute. Hosted or received visitors (Eban for Israel offer, Stevenson).
- **Relationships in exile**: Daily walks with Gödel (time, incompleteness, relativity implications); interactions with Oppenheimer (1935 first impression: “madhouse”... “Einstein is completely cuckoo”; later “landmark but not a beacon”); loyalty of Dukas; family visits.

## Significance Over Time

Transformed from temporary refuge to permanent home and symbol of Einstein's American phase. Enabled the "lion in winter" scientific persistence despite declining health and the public role as atomic-age conscience and defender of nonconformity. Post-1945: site of H-bomb skepticism, Red Scare resistance, final equations written April 1955.

`produced:` late statements on world government and civil liberties. `enabled:` integration of refugee scientists (Einstein, Gödel, others) into American intellectual life. `contrasts_with:` Berlin Kaiser Wilhelm Institute (pre-1933, lost to politics).

## Historiography and Collection Coverage Note

Isaacson draws on Princeton routines, Fantova journal (1953–55 conversations), Pais recollections, Oppenheimer anecdotes, and letters to document daily life and final quests. Debates center on whether Einstein's Princeton years were scientifically "wasted" on unified fields or a principled stand; and the effectiveness of his political interventions from this base. Collection (via Isaacson) strong on personal texture and Institute anecdotes; gaps in full institutional archives or comparative refugee experiences at other sites.
