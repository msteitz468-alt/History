# Yangzi River

The longest river in Asia, flowing roughly 6,300 kilometers from the Tibetan Plateau to the East China Sea, and historically China's central artery for rice agriculture, internal trade, and — repeatedly — political division. The fertile Yangzi delta and middle basin became China's economic and demographic center of gravity over the medieval and early modern periods, eventually surpassing the Yellow River (Huang He) heartland of early Chinese civilization in population and agricultural output.

## Historical Significance

The river has recurrently marked a north–south political frontier during periods of Chinese division — most notably the Southern Song, which retreated south of the Yangzi and Huai rivers after losing north China to the Jin, and the early [[ming-dynasty|Ming]] state, whose founding regime realigned the empire's political and economic center on the Yangzi (with an early capital at Nanjing) after a period in which northern-based conquest dynasties had dominated China. The river's connection to the Grand Canal, extended repeatedly from the Sui dynasty onward, tied southern agricultural surplus to northern political capitals, underwriting the fiscal and logistical basis of successive Chinese states.
