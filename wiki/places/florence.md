# Florence

In the thirteenth century **Florence** emerged as "the leading commercial and banking centre of
western Europe," and developed a distinctive **guild republic** that let a mercantile aristocracy
rule with the collaboration of artisans and lesser merchants. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]]
(Louis Green, Ch. 15d) traces the intertwining of economic growth, factional violence, and the
papal–Angevin alliance that shaped the city by 1300 — the matrix of the early Renaissance.

## The Banking Capital

Florentine ascendancy rested on its **wool and cloth trade** (the Arte della Calimala, then the
Arte della Lana) and, above all, on **international finance**. The minting of the silver *grosso*
(1235) and the gold **florin** (1252) gave it a trusted currency; its companies — the **Mozzi,
Spini, Bardi, Peruzzi, Frescobaldi, Cerchi** — became **papal bankers** and tax-collectors and
financiers to the kingdoms of **England, France and Naples**. The collapse of the Sienese
Buonsignori and Lucchese Ricciardi in the 1290s left the Florentines supreme. This wealth was
fused to politics: backing **[[charles-of-anjou|Charles of Anjou's]]** conquest of Sicily (1266)
won the bankers monopolies and tax-farms in his kingdom, binding the **papacy, the Angevins, the
Florentine Guelf party and the merchant companies** into a durable alliance.

## Faction and the Popolo

Florence was riven by the **[[guelphs-and-ghibellines|Guelf–Ghibelline]]** feud, traced by the
chroniclers to the murder of **Buondelmonte dei Buondelmonti** (1216). The Ghibelline **Uberti**
(under Farinata) and the Guelf families fought a half-century of expulsions, with the
**Ghibelline victory at Montaperti** (1260, with Manfred's cavalry)
and the **Guelf return** after Benevento (1266). Meanwhile the **popolo** — master-craftsmen,
shopkeepers and lesser merchants, organised by guild and militia company — rose as a political
force: the *primo popolo* (1250) created the *anziani*; the **priorate** (1282), drawn from the
guilds, became the republic's central executive.

## The Ordinances of Justice (1293)

The defining constitutional act was the **Ordinances of Justice (1293)**, championed by
**Giano della Bella**: they imposed severe penalties on **"magnates"** (great families) who
harmed *popolani*, excluded magnates from the priorate, and made **guild membership** the
prerequisite for political office (the guilds raised to twenty-one). Historians debate their
meaning — Salvemini saw a middle-class-versus-aristocracy struggle; Ottokar a transient popular
explosion that left a homogeneous ruling group intact. After Giano's fall (1295) and the
**Blacks vs Whites** feud (which exiled **Dante** in 1302, engineered by Boniface VIII and
Charles of Valois), a compromise preserved the Ordinances while confirming the dominance of the
wealthy *popolo grasso*.

## Significance

By 1300 Florence had reached "the end of a significant stage of its development": pre-eminent in
European finance, governed by a guild-based republic that held faction in check, and generating
the culture — Brunetto Latini, the *dolce stil nuovo*, Cavalcanti, **Dante**, and soon Giotto and
the chroniclers Compagni and Villani — that would flower in the next century. Florence is the
volume's great case of **economic power translated into political form**, the antithesis of the
[[signoria|signorial]] cities around it.
