# British Isles

The archipelago off the north-west coast of continental Europe comprising two
large islands — Great Britain and Ireland — and several thousand smaller ones,
including the Isle of Man, the Hebrides, the Orkney and Shetland Islands, and
(geographically associated but politically distinct) the Channel Islands. It is
the principal stage for the history this wiki files under the **british-isles**
region tag.

## The Naming Problem

The archipelago has no neutral, universally accepted collective name — a fact
that is itself historically significant. "The British Isles," the most common
English term, is rejected in the Republic of Ireland as implying a British
political claim, and has been avoided in Irish official usage since the 1949
declaration of the Republic; "Britain and Ireland," "These Islands," "the
Anglo-Celtic Archipelago," and "the Atlantic Archipelago" are among the proposed
alternatives. [[davies-the-isles-1999|Norman Davies]] settled on simply *The
Isles* for his 1999 history precisely because the namelessness reflects the
absence of any single owning nation — see
[[four-nations-british-history|four-nations British history]].

The constituent political labels are likewise unstable and non-equivalent:
"England" is not "Great Britain" (the larger island, and as a state only from
[[union-with-scotland-1707|1707]]); "Great Britain" is not the "United Kingdom"
(of Great Britain and Ireland from [[act-of-union-1801|1801]], of Great Britain
and Northern Ireland after 1922). Conflating them is the master error the
four-nations historians attack.

## Geography and Its Consequences

The islands were joined to the Continent until the early Holocene; rising
post-glacial sea levels severed the land bridge (the "Doggerland" plain beneath
the southern North Sea) by c. 6000 BCE, making the Isles islands. Davies stresses
that insularity never meant isolation: the surrounding seas — the Channel, the
Irish Sea, the North Sea — functioned as highways as much as barriers,
channelling successive contacts and settlements (Continental Celtic-speakers,
[[roman-britain|Roman]] administration, post-Roman Germanic and Irish movements,
Scandinavian Vikings, the cross-Channel Norman and Angevin realms). The Irish Sea
in particular bound western Britain and Ireland into a single cultural zone for
long stretches of prehistory and the early Middle Ages.

## A Place No Single Polity Long Held

Across recorded history no single state has durably controlled the whole
archipelago. Roman Britannia stopped at the Highland line and never crossed to
Hibernia; medieval English lordship over Wales, Scotland, and Ireland was partial,
contested, and repeatedly reversed; the regnal union of 1603 and the
parliamentary unions of [[union-with-scotland-1707|1707]] and
[[act-of-union-1801|1801]] each created new and temporary configurations; and the
1922 secession of most of Ireland ended even the brief all-archipelago United
Kingdom. The present political map — the United Kingdom, the Republic of Ireland,
and the Crown Dependencies of the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands — is only
the latest in a long series.

## Significance

For two centuries (roughly 1707–1922) the Isles were the metropolitan core of the
largest empire in history and the birthplace of the
[[industrial-revolution|Industrial Revolution]]; the global reach of the English
language, English common law, and parliamentary government radiates from this
small archipelago. Its history is therefore disproportionately consequential at
[[empire|imperial]] and global scale despite its modest size — and, as the
four-nations historians insist, that consequence was the achievement of a
composite, multinational state, not of a single nation.
