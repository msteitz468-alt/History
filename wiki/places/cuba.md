## Geographic Description

Cuba is the largest island in the Caribbean, stretching over 1,200 kilometres east–west. Its diverse environments include the Sierra Maestra mountains in the southeast, the broad Vega Real plains, and the arid Imias desert east of Santiago Bay lying in the mountains' rain shadow (600–800 mm annual rainfall), contrasting with wetter zones to the west (1,200–1,400 mm). The island sits roughly 120 kilometres from Haiti to the east and 170 kilometres from the Yucatán Peninsula to the west. Its geographical position made it central to Caribbean-wide interaction networks.

## Pre-Columbian Settlement History

### Phase 1 — Preagroalfarera (Archaic Age, c. 5000 BC–AD 800)

Cuba was first settled c. 5000 BC by migrants from coastal Central America (Nicaragua, Belize, Honduras). The oldest reliable sites come from the Mayarí and Levisa zones in the east, identified by lithic macroblades of the Seboruco-Mordán tradition. Over 2,012 preagroalfarera sites have been catalogued. The Caminar Abajo rock shelter contains Banwaroid-tradition artifacts (c. 2500 BC), evidence of domestic activities, burials, and starch grain residues of maize, sweet potato, and legumes, suggesting early plant management alongside hunting and gathering.

Cuban archaeologists distinguish two sub-phases within the Archaic Age — Lithic (flaked macrolithic blades) and Archaic proper (ground-stone tools) — though Keegan and Hofman (2017) argue there is insufficient evidence for these to represent separate migrations. Pottery use began early in Cuba; AMS dates document small quantities of pottery as early as 2600 BC (Jouravleva 2002), contemporaneous with similar incipient pottery use in Hispaniola and Puerto Rico.

### Phase 2 — Protoagrícola (Incipient Agriculture, c. AD 700–1000)

The protoagrícola stage is transitional between the Archaic Age and the full Ceramic Age, characterised by pottery appearing in association with Archaic Age artifact assemblages. Sites such as Arroyo del Palo and Mejias contain simple pottery alongside fishing and gathering implements. Red-painted pottery appears on the southeastern coast in the early 9th century AD, possibly Ostionoid influences from Hispaniola, though many Cuban archaeologists resist Rouse's Ostionoid classification for this redware.

An important ecological divide bisects the southeast at Santiago Bay. The arid zone east of the bay (eastern Imias desert) supported only temporary settlements; western zones with better rainfall and river-renewed soils supported permanent agricultural communities. Three site types reflect this contrast: *conchales* (shell heaps, procurement sites); *paraderos* (small temporary farmsteads); and *sitios de habitación* (permanent villages, 30–180 m diameter, on hilltops near coast and rivers).

If starch grain identifications of maize in Cuba at early dates are correct (Pajón et al. 2007), Cuba may have been the source for the earliest introduction of maize into the southeastern United States (c. 100 BC–AD 200), transmitted through a route distinct from the Mexican-to-southwestern US pathway.

### Phase 3 — Agricultores Ceramistas (Ceramic Age, c. AD 800–1492+)

Meillacoid pottery arrived in eastern Cuba in the 9th century AD, contemporaneous with its emergence on Hispaniola. Haiti remains the most probable source (Rouse 1992; Ulloa Hung and Valcárcel Rojas 2013), though some researchers propose a separate migration from the Guyana coast following the south-coast ocean current. The earliest Ceramic Age sites occur in the area the Spanish called the province of Bayaquitirí. Calibrated radiocarbon dates for the Banes region place Ceramic Age commencement at c. AD 1100.

The adoption of Meillacoid produced a recognisable shift: settlement moved from coastal-mangrove settings to hilltops overlooking the coast (hilltop village sites average 2,500 m², with 5–10 mounds; distinct from smaller coastal fishing villages). Sites are paired on adjacent hilltops — the same pattern documented for the Bahamas and Hispaniola, interpreted as reflecting matrilineal/avunculocal social organisation. Exchange between coastal fishing villages and inland agricultural settlements is documented by marine resources at inland sites.

Irving Rouse's (1942) study in the Maniabon Hills area documented 74 village sites on hilltops averaging c. 4 km inland, all adjacent to good agricultural land and freshwater, with deep midden deposits (up to 3 m). Coastal shell-heap sites were interpreted as fishing villages complementing the inland agricultural villages.

Chicoid influences arrived in northeastern Cuba from the 11th or 12th centuries AD, most clearly after the 13th century. In the Banes region they are manifested as a limited adoption of curvilinear designs onto a predominantly Meillacoid base. Cuban Chicoid pottery is of lower quality than that of the eastern Dominican Republic, executed with a pointed stylus in a more Meillacoid manner. Four plaza sites in eastern Cuba with earthwork enclosures (Laguna de Limones, Pueblo Viejo, Monte Cristo, Big Wall) resemble Chicoid plaza construction in the Dominican Republic and Haiti. However, Cuba has no stone-lined plazas comparable to Tibes, Jacanás, or Caguana in Puerto Rico.

## Key Sites

### El Chorro de Maíta, Banes (c. AD 1200 to post-1550)

Located 4 km from the coast at 160 m elevation in the Banes region. A large settlement covering c. 22,000 m², with a central burial area (~2,000 m²) functioning as a probable plaza. Noteworthy for its size, an exceptional cemetery of at least 111 burials, and a high density of ceremonial objects (quartzite beads, metal objects). Strontium-isotope analysis identified 22 individuals (25%) as non-local; at least one was probably African and another probably Mesoamerican (cranial modification not observed in the Antilles), consistent with the Spanish encomienda system of forced labour relocation. Extended burials (a Christian practice) and European lacetags (brass clothing fasteners) in six of the extended burials confirm post-1492 occupation and possible Spanish-organised burial practices. Roberto Valcárcel Rojas (2016) argues the site in its final phase was a Spanish encomienda.

The most decorated burial is a female aged 26–35 interred with gold, quartzite, coral, guanín pendants, and a Colombian Tairona bird's-head pendant, documenting high-status women and Colombian exchange connections. A collection of systematic metal objects from the site traces the pre-contact use of gold (associated with female gender) and guanín — a gold-copper alloy imported from South America, particularly Zenú and Tairona Colombia, associated with male gender and the ancestors.

### Los Buchillones (c. AD 1220–1640)

Located on the north-central coast in Ciego de Avila province. Twenty-three AMS dates document continuous occupation from before AD 1220 to AD 1640 or later. The site originally occupied the Punta Alegre Lagoon; coastal geomorphological change has since moved most of it underwater in the Bahia de Buena Vista. Underwater excavations (1996–2001) preserved organic materials including hundreds of wooden artifacts (duhos, effigies, vessels) and structural elements (mahogany and lignum vitae posts, rafters, stringers, thatch) from at least 40 structures (9–12 m diameter round; rectangular; 8.5 × 6.2 m oval). The faunal assemblage is strongly marine (fish 58%, marine mammals 18%, sea turtles 4%, terrestrial only 13%). Basket-impressed sherds from the site show communication with the Bahama Islands (where matt-impressed sherds are more common). The nearest pre-Columbian parallel for structures built over water is Lake Maracaibo, Venezuela. The site's longevity into the colonial period is striking; it is unlike any other Caribbean site discovered.

## Regional Integration

The Banes archaeological area contains 76 agricultural communities showing evidence for regional centres. GIS analysis (Persons 2013) identified the earliest Ceramic Age sites in pairs (Aguas Gordas and El Mango); subsequent phases show four evenly spaced primate sites (Aguas Gordas, El Mango/Loma de Baní, El Chorro de Maíta, Esterito) each surrounded by satellite communities within 5 km. This two-tier hierarchy was not imposed by Chicoid influences; regional integration was organic, reflecting long internal development (Valcárcel Rojas 2002). A high frequency of ritual objects at primate centres relative to satellites is consistent with craft specialisation and elite-controlled exchange.

The picture that emerges is "variantes culturales" (Guarch Delmonte 1990) — numerous communities with local identities expressing differential selections from a general Meillacoid decorative grammar, integrated through exchange and not representing a linear progression toward any single cultural endpoint.

## Significance

Cuba's archaeological sequence is the most complete in the Caribbean for documenting Archaic Age diversity and the emergence of pottery-using agricultural societies. Cuban archaeologists' emphasis on ecological diversity and transculturation (following Fernando Ortiz 1983) offers a corrective to unilinear models. Cuba's geographical position — closest Greater Antillean island to the Bahamas and Florida — made it the probable source of Bahamian colonisation (Archaic Age protoagrícola communities from northern Cuba) and a possible early conduit for maize into North America.

## Historiography

Cuban archaeology has been marginalised in Caribbean-wide scholarship due to modern political barriers limiting North American access to Cuban scholarship (Curet et al. 2005; Berman et al. 2005). After the Castro revolution, Cuban archaeology adopted historical materialism as its theoretical framework (Montane M. 1981), producing classification schemes emphasising economic stages (preagroalfarera, protoagrícola, agricultores ceramistas) rather than fine-grained cultural sequences. Internal critics now argue this produced a static, stage-bound model (Torres Etayo 2010; Ulloa Hung and Valcárcel Rojas 2013). Roberto Valcárcel Rojas and José Guarch Delmonte are the central scholars for the Banes and eastern Cuba archaeology respectively; Brooke Persons (2013) has produced the most recent systematic pottery seriation and GIS site-distribution study for Banes.
