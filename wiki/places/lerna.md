# Lerna

A low mound beside the **Lernaean spring** south of Argos, on the western shore of
the Argolic Gulf — the **type-site of the Early Bronze Age mainland**, whose
stratified excavation in the 1950s rewrote the prehistory of Greece. Its great
**"House of the Tiles"** is the finest known building of Early Helladic Greece, and
its burnt destruction defined the catastrophe that ended the EH II florescence.
Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XXVI(a), Caskey, who
directed the excavation).

## The sequence

Lerna's deep, well-preserved strata gave the mainland its clearest Bronze Age
sequence:

- **Lerna I–II** — Neolithic.
- **Lerna III** (EH II) — a **fortified citadel**, levelled and encircled by a
  **double wall with towers and interior compartments**, holding large buildings
  rebuilt many times.
- **Lerna IV** (EH III) — the post-catastrophe town of **apsidal houses**, new
  pottery, the potter's wheel, and the first grey ware.
- **Lerna V** (Middle Helladic) — a typical MH town. **[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH
  Vol. II.1]]** (Caskey) makes it the **type-sequence of the Middle Helladic
  period**: succeeding Lerna IV *without violence*, it carries on the apsidal houses
  and grey **Minyan** ware while adding Matt-painted and lustrous wares, **intramural
  burials**, the first true **horse** bones (*equus caballus*), and a run of dated
  **Minoan imports** (M.M. Ia → III) that peg the whole MH chronology (a Lerna V
  radiocarbon date of 1948 ± 117 BCE).
- **Lerna VI** (the Shaft Grave period) — two large **royal shaft graves**,
  contemporary with Grave Circle B at **[[mycenae|Mycenae]]**, mark the
  [[mycenaean-civilization|Late Helladic]] transition; one was cut into ground that
  had been **taboo** all through the MH period — a hint of a new régime.

## The House of the Tiles

The last and finest building of Lerna III was a rectangular **palace or "corridor
house," ~12 × 25 m**, of mud-brick on stone socles, plastered, with stairways to an
upper storey and a **gabled roof covered in flat terra-cotta tiles** (which gave it
its name). Valuables were stored in jars and boxes sealed with clay **stamped by
hundreds of small seals** — among the earliest administrative sealing systems in
Europe, evidence of a ranked society with controlled storage. It was destroyed,
apparently while being remodelled, by a **great fire ~2200–2100 BCE**.

After the fire the ruin-mound was deliberately **flattened into a low circular
tumulus ringed with stones**, which the new inhabitants left untouched for
decades — a striking act of memory or taboo over the fallen great house.

## The end-EH II catastrophe

The burning of the House of the Tiles was not local: **Tiryns, Asine, Zygouries**
burnt at the same horizon, **Corinth was abandoned**, and the destruction reached
Attica. Caskey read this as a **foreign invasion** that defeated the EH II
population. Because Lerna's EH III layer survived intact where erosion had stripped
it elsewhere, Lerna is the site that **proved** the catastrophe fell at the end of
EH II — overturning the older view of a peaceful EBA ended only by a later Middle
Helladic incursion. The identity of the newcomers is left open; the horizon feeds
the [[coming-of-the-greeks-1900bce|coming of the Greeks]] question.

## Significance

Lerna is the **keystone of mainland Aegean prehistory**: it fixes the EBA pottery
sequence, gives the period its grandest architecture and earliest sealing
administration, and dates the destruction horizon that the
[[indo-european-origins|Indo-European migration debate]] turns on. In myth Lerna
was the home of the **Hydra** slain by Heracles — a classical memory attached to a
place that was already ancient ruins.

## Historiography

The 1952–58 American excavations (Caskey) are a model of stratigraphic control, and
their result — the EH II destruction — is the empirical anchor of the chapter. The
absolute date (~2200–2100 BCE) rests on **early radiocarbon** with wide latitude.
Whether the destroyers were Greek-speakers, an earlier Indo-European wave, or a
non-Indo-European people is **not** settled by the archaeology. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
