# Naucratis

**Naucratis** was the **single chartered Greek trading-city in Egypt** — the
emporion on a western branch of the Nile, ten miles from the Saite capital of Sais,
through which all Greek (and much non-Greek) commerce with Egypt was funnelled. It is
the prime example of the Greek *emporion* as opposed to the colony, and the meeting-
point at which "essentially conservative" Egypt opened to the Greek world.
[[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 3]] (Ch. 36b, T. F. R. G. Braun) treats
it.

## From Milesian fort to chartered emporion

Strabo's foundation story (drawn from local Naucratite tradition) has the **Milesians
come with thirty ships and found "the Milesian fort"** under **[[saite-egypt|
Psammetichus I]]**, then move upstream — defeating an Egyptian "Inaros" in a sea-
battle — to found Naucratis. The first Corinthian pottery there dates c. 630–620,
the East Greek perhaps earlier; the foundation falls somewhere between Psammetichus
I's accession (664) and c. 620. **[[saite-egypt|Amasis]]** later "gave Naucratis to
the Greeks as a city to live in" (Herodotus) — a re-chartering that confirmed its
privileged monopoly: any ship bound for Egypt had to put in at Naucratis, or, if blown
to another Nile mouth, swear it had come unintentionally and carry its cargo round to
the Canopic mouth. This protected the Saite revenue (a 10% import tithe is attested
under Nectanebo I) while preventing any rival Delta port — and it made Naucratis
the **port of Sais**.

## A panhellenic trading city

Uniquely, Naucratis was a city of **Greeks of all races living side by side** —
Aeolians, Ionians and Dorians — "nothing like it in the Greek world until the
panhellenic foundations of Periclean Athens." Petrie's and Hogarth's excavations
identified its temples from votive pottery: the **temple of Apollo** (built by the
Milesians), the **Hera** sanctuary (Samian) standing beside it "like the embassies of
two perennial enemies," temples of Aphrodite, the Dioscuri and Zeus (Aeginetan), and
above all the **Hellenium**, built jointly under Amasis by **nine East Greek cities**
(Ionian Chios, Teos, Phocaea, Clazomenae; Aeolian Mytilene; Dorian Rhodes, Cnidus,
Halicarnassus, Phaselis). The Hellenium's joint magistrates (*prostatai*, and locally
chosen *timouchoi*) look like an attempt by these cities to balance the influence of
the founding city Miletus and the great carriers Samos and Aegina. The city's own
laws (in Hadrian's day) forbade intermarriage with Egyptians.

## What was traded

Naucratis is a window on the Archaic economy. Greeks brought **wine** (Sappho's
brother **Charaxus** carried Lesbian wine and famously spent a fortune freeing the
courtesan **Doricha**), **olive oil**, and — decisively — **silver**, often in a
three-cornered trade: Aeginetan and East Greek carriers sold woollens for the silver
of Thrace and Macedon, then took the silver to Egypt where its purchasing power was
highest (hoards of gashed coins and ingots show it was valued as bullion). In return
they took **Egyptian grain** (Bacchylides' "corn-carrying ships"), **papyrus** (the
word *byblos* gave us "Bible"), **linen, alum** and faience trinkets — a Naucratite
scarab-factory operated in the sixth century. Its rise c. 620 was synchronous with
the opening of the Black Sea grain trade ([[greek-colonization|Olbia]]) and the
reach to [[gades-tartessos|Tartessus]]: "the last third of the seventh century brought
a prodigious acceleration of Greek trade" — new wealth that destabilized the old
agrarian order and helped drive the [[solon|Athenian crisis of 594]].

## Why Naucratis mattered

- **The emporion model** — a tightly regulated, chartered trading post rather than a
  self-governing colony, the form Greek commerce took where it met a strong state.
- **The Egyptian opening of Archaic Greece** — the conduit for the
  [[orientalizing-revolution|Egyptian debt]] of Greek monumental sculpture (the
  *kouros*) and architecture, and for the silver-and-grain trade that monetized the
  Aegean economy.
