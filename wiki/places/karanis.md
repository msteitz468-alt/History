# Karanis

**Karanis** (modern Kom Aushim) was a village on the north-eastern edge of the
[[the-fayyum|Fayyum]], one of the **best-preserved settlement sites of Greco-Roman Egypt** and the
type-site for the archaeology of an ordinary Egyptian village.

## The site

Founded in the **third century BCE** during the Ptolemaic reclamation of the Fayyum and named
after **Karanos**, the mythical Macedonian founder-king, Karanis was occupied through to the sixth
century CE or later. The site covers some **60 hectares** — slightly smaller than Pompeii — and at
its peak probably held a population of around **4,000**. The remains visible today are mostly of
the **early Roman period** (first–second centuries CE), a particularly prosperous phase, partly
because later layers were destroyed (stripped for *sebakh* fertiliser) and earlier layers often
not reached.

The main modern excavations were carried out by the **University of Michigan in 1925–35**; many of
the artefacts and papyri are now in the Kelsey Museum, Ann Arbor. Excavated features include two
Egyptian-style limestone **temples** (the south temple dedicated under Nero to the crocodile gods
Petesouchos and Pnepheros; a later Graeco-Roman porticoed courtyard added in the late second
century), Roman-style **baths**, granaries, and dense mudbrick **housing** preserved to upper
storeys.

## Why it matters

Karanis is where the **archaeology and the papyrology of Roman Egypt meet on the same ground**.
Unlike [[oxyrhynchus|Oxyrhynchus]], whose papyri came from rubbish mounds detached from their
buildings, Karanis preserved houses, their contents, and documents together — allowing
reconstruction of household economy, occupancy and the legal subdivision of houses into "shares,"
agricultural life, and the texture of a Fayyum village across the Ptolemaic, Roman, and
Late Antique centuries. It is a central case-study for the social history built on
[[papyrology|papyrology]] and the demographic work of Bagnall and Frier on Roman Egypt.

Its excavation history is also a cautionary tale: even the careful Michigan work was driven partly
by the search for papyri, and the site "continues to decay," like much of the Fayyum, under
modern agricultural re-expansion.
