# Heuneburg

## Geographic and Site Description

The Heuneburg is a promontory site overlooking the upper Danube in southern Germany. It encloses about 3 hectares and was one of the wealthiest and most extensively excavated early Iron Age defended sites in central Europe. Occupation climax in 6th–early 5th centuries BCE, with earlier and later use.

## Key Evidence and Features

It was defended by a circuit rebuilt many times; standard local timber-framed with stone facing, except one phase (Period IV) using "plastered mud-brick on a limestone base for the walls" with "bastions on the north-west side, sharp angles and a tangential gateway" — "Both plan and technique are of Mediterranean inspiration" (Ch. 9 box, ~p. 287, cache ~730s-740s). Destroyed by fire and rebuilt in local style.

Interior: thick deposits, multiple phases; dwellings, granaries, workshops (bronzesmiths producing brooches; mould for copy of Italian jug handle); iron, lignite, antler, coral (Mediterranean import), amber (Baltic) worked for prestige ornaments. Imports: "fine pottery from Athens and amphorae, presumably with their contents of wine, from the south of France"; "silk and the domestic chicken are also known from here, the first occurrence of these Oriental items in temperate Europe" (Ch. 9, ~p. 288, cache ~750s-790s).

Associated burials: groups of mounds including Hohmichele (80m diameter, 14m high; robbed central vehicle burial in wooden chamber with horse harness, sheet bronze cauldron, textiles, bow/quiver; secondary vehicle burial). Other rich graves (Grafenbühl, Eberdingen-Hochdorf) nearby with wagons, gold, imported ivory, Etruscan/Italian couch, etc.

## Significance

"The Heuneburg was clearly the residence of an elite who were able to mobilize local resources to connect themselves to very far-reaching exchange networks" (Ch. 9, ~p. 288, cache ~782). Exemplar of Hallstatt-period hierarchy, defended site + rich burial pairing, and deliberate imitation of Mediterranean forms for status display. Climax of Bronze Age/Iron Age competitive social organization before shifts around 500 BCE.

**Cross-reference from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004 (range 03, Hillforts entry):** The mud-brick phase is "an obvious imitation of a Mediterranean town wall, emphasizes once again that functional considerations alone were not always paramount concerns in defensive construction." Contrasted with Late La Tène murus Gallicus oppida ramparts. (range 03, cache lines ~725–735). Strengthens prestige/display interpretation. All links to pre-established.

## Additional Detail from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004 Range 04 (Iron Age Germany / The Heuneburg entry, Bettina Arnold)

**From range cache lines ~784–1000 (exact source lines ~23818+ offset):** "The Early Iron Age (600–450 B.C.) Heuneburg hillfort... occupies a roughly triangular natural spur about 60 meters above the Upper Danube River some 600 meters above sea level. The 3.3-hectare fortiﬁed promontory settlement was associated with a much larger outer settlement, or suburbium..."
"Beginning in 1950, twenty-nine years of systematic ﬁeldwork... uncovered a fortiﬁcation system of air-dried, whitewashed mud bricks on a limestone foundation. This arid-climate construction technique is not found on any other temperate European Iron Age site... Though relatively ﬁre-resistant, the mud-brick wall was ultimately leveled following a major ﬁre around 540 B.C.... Additional evidence for contact with the Mediterranean world of the sixth century B.C. was recovered in the form of distinctive Greek imported pottery known as black ﬁgure ware, as well as trade amphorae..."
"Population estimates for the Early Iron Age site complex... appear to have housed several thousand people at its peak during the Late Hallstatt–Early La Tène period (seventh to ﬁfth centuries B.C.)... The Heuneburg is one of a small number of such sites in the so-called West Hallstatt Zone (southwest Germany, eastern France, Switzerland north of the Alps)."
"Following Paulus’s excavations... Gustav Riek’s partial excavation in 1937–1938 of the Hohmichele—at 13.5 meters high and with a diameter of 85 meters, the second-largest known Early Iron Age burial mound in Europe... seven inhumations... including an intact chamber grave (Grave VI) containing the inhumations of a man and a woman buried with a four-wheeled wagon, bronze drinking vessels, personal ornaments... and weapons (a dagger, a quiver full of iron-tipped arrows, and a bow..."
"Beginning in 1999, excavations... in two smaller mounds (Tumulus 17 and Tumulus 18)... produced twenty-three new burials. Tumulus 17 Grave 1 contained a bronze cauldron, an iron short sword... Tumulus 18... produced two burials with bronze neckrings, a costume element that was a marker of elite status in Iron Age Europe..."
"See also Hillforts (vol. 2, part 6); Greek Colonies in the West (vol. 2, part 6); Iron Age Germany (vol. 2, part 6)."

## Additional Evidence from Cunliffe 2018 (Ch. 5 range)

The site was "built on a plateau overlooking the Danube. The earliest Hallstatt rampart was constructed in the middle of the seventh century, and the site continued in use for some 200 years. During Hallstatt D1 the defences of the plateau enclosure were completely remodelled in a style unique in Transalpine Europe: stone wall footings were laid with hollow, forward-projecting bastions regularly spaced along its west side. Above the foundations the wall was built of carefully squared mud-bricks of regular size and shape. This alien structure, so clearly the concept of a Greek mind, and presumably overseen by a Greek architect, is a striking example of the willingness (and ability) of the local Hallstatt elite to adopt totally foreign modes of expression in the interests of displaying status and power." The defended site overlooked a lower town "tightly packed with buildings and... defended by a five-metre-high rampart fronted by a massive ditch." Beyond lay "as many as fifty" farmsteads; "in all, the early sixth-century settlement extended over about 100 hectares and probably housed about 5,000 people." (west central europe and the mediterranean 109–110; range ~549–580).
