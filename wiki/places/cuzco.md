# Cuzco (Qosqo)

**Cuzco** — Quechua *Qosqo*, "the navel" — was the sacred capital of the [[inca-empire|Inca empire]] and
the ritual centre of the Andean world: the point from which the **four *suyus*** of **Tawantinsuyu**
("the four parts together") radiated. Documented for the wiki through Terence D'Altroy's
[[daltroy-incas-2004|*The Incas*]]. More a city of temples, palaces, and royalty than a populous town,
Cuzco was, in D'Altroy's phrase, "a spatial metaphor for Inca society and their world."

## The city as cosmogram

The urban core (~40 hectares), reportedly designed by **[[pachacuti|Pachacuti]]**, was famously laid out
in the shape of a **puma**: the fortress-temple complex of **Saqsawaman** formed the head, and the body
held **Upper and Lower Cuzco** (*Hanan* and *Hurin*) — the dual halves into which Inca royalty was
divided. Around the centre, a dozen neighbourhoods settled with **ethnic lords and colonists** echoed the
layout of the empire, making the capital a deliberate **microcosm**. Counting the heartland's service
population — domestic staff, temple attendants, artisans, **khipu** accountants, farmers — greater Cuzco
held perhaps **100,000** people.

## Sacred architecture

- The **Qorikancha** ("Golden Enclosure," the Temple of the Sun) was the focal point of Inca sacred
  geography, housing **Punchao**, the image of the Sun, and a famous garden of **maize and animals worked
  in gold and silver**.
- **Saqsawaman**, the grandest complex in the empire, crowned the ridge above the city — at once
  ceremonial centre, fortress, and magazine, its colossal **zig-zag megalithic walls** among the finest
  stonework in the Americas.
- Royal palaces (Hatunkancha, Amarukancha, Q'asana) faced the twin central plazas, **Awkaypata** and
  **Kusipata**; Awkaypata, strewn with sacred sand and buried offerings, hosted the **royal mummies** at
  ceremonies of state (see [[split-inheritance-and-royal-mummies]]).

## The ceque system

Cuzco was tied to landscape and cosmos by the **ceque system**: at least **332 (probably 400+) shrines
(*waq'a*)** — springs, stones, peaks — organised along radial lines (*ceques*) emanating from the
Qorikancha, each with its own name, custodian kin-group, and place in the ritual calendar. It is one of
the most remarkable known integrations of **city, sacred geography, kinship, and calendar**.

## Conquest

The Spanish took Cuzco in **1533**; in **1536** **Manco Inca** besieged the occupied city in a great but
ultimately failed revolt (the assault on Saqsawaman). Spanish Cuzco was built directly on Inca
foundations — the Qorikancha became the convent of Santo Domingo — and the city's Inca masonry still
underpins the colonial and modern town.
