# Troy (Hisarlik)

The fortified citadel-mound of **Hisarlik**, commanding the Asiatic shore of the
**Dardanelles (Hellespont)** at the gateway between the Aegean and the Black Sea.
Identified by **Heinrich Schliemann** (excavations from 1870) with the Troy of
Homer, it is one of the most famous and most contested sites in archaeology. Its
deep stratification — nine principal settlement layers (Troy I–IX) spanning the
Early Bronze Age to the Roman period — makes it a keystone for the prehistory of
the northeast Aegean. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]]
(Ch. XVIII, with the stratigraphy from **Carl Blegen's** Cincinnati expedition,
1932–38, refining Schliemann and Dörpfeld).

This page covers **Early Bronze Age Troy (Troy I–V)**; the later citadel of the
Trojan War tradition (Troy VI–VIIa) belongs to the [[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze
Age]] and will be treated from the Hittite and Mycenaean sources.

## Geographic Setting

Troy sits on a low ridge above the Trojan plain, near the mouth of the Scamander,
in sight of the strait. Its position let it **command the Dardanelles** — the
maritime choke-point through which all sea traffic between the Aegean and the
Black Sea (and the overland route between Asia Minor and the Balkans) had to pass.
This strategic location is the standard explanation for the wealth of a settlement
otherwise on the agricultural margin: Troy lived on **exchange and the control of
movement**, not on a rich hinterland.

## Troy I (c. 3000–2500 BCE)

The earliest citadel: a small but strongly walled fortress of stone, entered
through gateways flanked by towers. Its diagnostic building is the **megaron** —
a long rectangular hall with a porch and a central hearth, entered on the short
end. The megaron plan, which recurs at Troy and across the Anatolian/Aegean
world, is a forerunner of the later Mycenaean palace hall. Troy I already shows a
**maritime** orientation and produced some of the region's **earliest tin-bronze**,
marking it as a participant in the metal-based prosperity of
[[early-bronze-age-anatolia|Early Bronze Anatolia]].

## Troy II and the "Treasures" (c. 2500–2300 BCE)

Troy II is the wealthy, fully urban citadel: a larger fortified enclosure with a
monumental ramped gateway and a central **megaron palace**. The **potter's wheel**
appears, and with it the distinctive **depas amphikypellon** (a tall two-handled
drinking cup). It is to the destruction-level of Troy II that the celebrated gold
hoards belong — the gold diadems, vessels, and jewellery Schliemann excavated and
named **"Priam's Treasure."** Schliemann misdated and mislabelled these: they are
the wealth of an Anatolian prince of the later third millennium, **a thousand
years before** any historical Trojan War. The treasures place Troy II in the same
**mid-third-millennium zenith** of Anatolian metal-wealth as the royal tombs of
[[early-bronze-age-anatolia|Alaca Höyük and (disputedly) Dorak]], and link it by
trade to the Cycladic islands and the Greek mainland (Early Helladic).

Troy II ended in a **great fire** (c. 2300 BCE) — part of the broad
**end-EB2 catastrophe** that burned settlements across western and central
Anatolia (see [[early-bronze-age-anatolia]]). The depas cup, sauceboats and seal
designs tie Troy II tightly to the [[early-bronze-age-aegean|Early Bronze Age
Aegean]] across the Hellespont — the islands and the Greek mainland of Lerna's day.

## Troy III–V (c. 2300–1900 BCE)

After the fire, Troy was rebuilt as a poorer, more crowded settlement (Troy
III–V), continuing through the end of the Early Bronze Age into the Middle Bronze
Age but never recovering the splendour of Troy II. The site's next great phase —
the broad, well-built citadel of **Troy VI** — belongs to the
[[middle-bronze-age|Middle]] and [[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze Age]] and the world
of the Hittites and Mycenaeans. The grey **"Minyan"-type wares** of the Troy V–VI
horizon stand at the centre of a debate over whether such pottery (and the
**[[coming-of-the-greeks-1900bce|first Greek-speakers]]**) spread *from*
north-western Anatolia *into* Greece (Mellaart) or the reverse — see
[[indo-european-migrations]].

## Troy VI and Hittite "Wilusa" (the Middle/Late Bronze citadel)

The broad, finely-built citadel of **Troy VI** is the settlement of the
[[middle-bronze-age|Middle]] and [[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze Age]] — the one that
underlies the Homeric tradition. **[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II.1]]** adds two
threads. From the **[[hittites|Hittite]]** side, a later treaty records that
"Labarna had conquered Arzawa and **Wilusa**" — and **Wilusa** (≈ Greek *(W)ilios*,
Ilion) is widely, if not universally, identified with **Troy**, placing it on the
fringe of the Hittite west alongside the rival kingdom of **Arzawa**. From the
**[[early-bronze-age-aegean|Aegean]]** side, Mycenaean pottery shows Greece was in
contact with Troy VI from **Late Helladic II**, and the **earthquake destruction**
of Troy VI falls early in Late Helladic IIIb — a synchronism the CAH cautiously
links to the Greek legend of Heracles' sack of "Laomedon's Troy" (accomplished, in
the myth, with the aid of Poseidon the earthquake-god). The fuller Aegean treatment
belongs to the Mycenaean chapters (this volume's Section 5).

## Troy VIIa — the "Troy of Priam" (CAH II.2)

**[[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II.2]]** (Ch. XXI(c), Blegen) gives the layer
that best fits the **Trojan War**. **Troy VIIa** is a direct continuation of Troy VI
after the earthquake — the walls repaired, but inside the citadel the open spaces
**crammed with small houses** packed wall-to-wall, almost every one with **large
storage jars (pithoi) sunk beneath the floors**. Blegen reads this crowding and
stockpiling as **preparations for a siege**. The settlement of "one, or at the most
two, generations" ended in a **devastating fire that swept the whole citadel**, with
human skeletons in the streets — "the appearance of the handiwork of man," "the usual
accompaniment of the capture, sacking and burning of an ancient town." Dated by
Mycenaean **IIIb** pottery to about **1250 BCE** (Blegen's estimate, coinciding with
Herodotus; Eratosthenes' canonical date was 1184): "if a Troy of Priam, besieged and
taken by an Agamemnon, ever actually existed in fact, it must be identified with...
VIIa." This is the volume's careful, evidence-based statement of the historicity
question.

**Troy VIIb** followed: VIIb1 carried on the local culture, but VIIb2 brought a
**new people** marked by handmade **Knobbed Ware (*Buckelkeramik*)** — paralleled in
Late Bronze Age **Hungary**, and thought to mark a migration via Thrace into Anatolia,
part of the wider disturbances of the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] / transition to
the Iron Age.

## Significance

Troy is doubly important: as the **archaeological type-site** for the prehistory
of the northeast Aegean and the Dardanelles trade, and as the focus of the
**Homeric tradition** that made it, for the Greeks and for all later European
literature, the archetypal besieged city. The two must be kept distinct — the EBA
"treasure" citadel of Troy II is not the Troy of the *Iliad* — but it was the
spectacular wealth of Troy II that first persuaded Schliemann he had found Homer's
city.

## Historiography

The site is a cautionary tale in the history of archaeology. **Schliemann's**
methods were destructive and his identifications romantic and self-serving (the
"Treasure of Priam," "Mask of Agamemnon" at Mycenae); **Dörpfeld** and then
**Blegen** disentangled the stratigraphy and showed that Schliemann's "Homeric"
treasures belonged to the third millennium, far too early. CAH's account rests on
Blegen's published sequence. The **provenance of the Dorak treasure** reported by
Mellaart (used to date Troy IIc–g via an Egyptian Fifth-Dynasty synchronism) is
itself doubtful — the objects vanished and were never verified — and that
synchronism should be treated with caution. Absolute dates here are pre-calibration
and approximate. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].

[[sources/bryce-kingdom-of-the-hittites-2005|Bryce (2005)]] approaches Troy from the
**Hittite documents** rather than the spade. He treats the equation of Hittite
**[[wilusa|Wilusa]] with (W)ilios/Troy** as effectively settled (the Manapa-Tarhunda
letter fixes Wilusa beyond the Seha River Land, in the north-west Troad), notes the
suggestive Wilusan king **Alaksandu** (≈ Alexandros/Paris) of Muwattalli's treaty, and
accepts that thirteenth-century clashes involving Mycenaean **[[ahhiyawa-question|Ahhiyawa]]**
and Wilusa supplied a **historical kernel** for the Trojan War tradition — while concluding
firmly that the *Iliad* is a centuries-long literary conflation with no single documented
Greek assault on Troy in the Hittite record.
