# Muziris

## Geography and Identity

A major **South Indian port on the Kerala (Malabar) coast**, the premier western terminus
of Roman–Indian [[indian-ocean-trade|maritime trade]] in the first two centuries CE. The
*Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* describes it as abounding "in ships sent there with
cargoes from Arabia and by the Greeks"; a Tamil bardic poem evokes Yavana (Western) vessels
arriving "with gold and departing with pepper... brimming with prosperity."

[CONTROVERSY] Its precise location was lost for centuries; excavations at **Pattanam** near
Cranganore have yielded first–third-century Roman coins, amphora fragments, glass, and
gaming counters alongside Arabian and Mesopotamian material — but whether Pattanam *is*
Muziris remains "hotly debated."

## The Muziris Papyrus

The port gives its name to the **Muziris Papyrus** (2nd c. CE), a contract between an
Alexandria importer and a financier covering a single cargo on the ship ***Hermapollon***:
ivory, cloth, and nard worth **almost 7 million sesterces** and weighing ~3.18 tonnes. In
[[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin's]] account it is decisive evidence that
**[[roman-imperial-trade|Pliny]] was not exaggerating** his 100-million-sesterce figure for
Rome's annual luxury-import drain — one cargo from one port equals **7%** of that total.
Peter Sidebotham combined the cargo value with Strabo's "120 ships a year from Myos Hormos"
to extrapolate South Asian imports of perhaps **17.64 billion sesterces** annually.

## Significance

Muziris is the clearest single window onto the scale, value, and financing of the
**maritime** [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] — black pepper and spices outward, Roman gold and
silver coin inward — and the integration of the Roman fiscal economy with South Indian
production.
