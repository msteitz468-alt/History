# Palenque

## Overview

**Palenque** (Classic *Lakam-ha'*, “place of abundant water”) on the western edge of the southern lowlands (Chiapas foothills) was a medium major Classic Maya capital—**slightly smaller than [[copan|Copán]]** in Hammond’s ranking, yet among the best-documented dynastic centers through architecture and long hieroglyphic texts. Snow et al. (2020) emphasize its sophisticated **hydraulic engineering** (channels, aqueducts, stone bridges on five streams) and the jade-bedecked burial of **K’inich Janaab Pakal I** (603–683 CE) in the Temple of the Inscriptions.

## Palace and Temple of the Inscriptions (Hammond, CHNPA II.1 Ch. 5)

The **palace** courtyards and ranges were built up over the reigns of **three rulers beginning with Hanab Pakal (A.D. 615–683)**; each accession was often marked by a new throne room. **Hanab Pakal’s funerary monument**, the **Temple of the Inscriptions**—with subterranean vault and massive carved sarcophagus—epitomizes elite commitment of material and intellectual resources. The long temple text, linked to the **Cross Group** tablets, gives an account of **mythic and historic dynastic ancestry** that remains a model of Maya rulership documentation.

Adams (CHNPA Ch. 1) notes that Palenque was long **poorly published archaeologically** except Ruz’s Temple of the Inscriptions work and Rands’s ceramics, while dynastic and iconographic publication was strong—an imbalance later field programs partly corrected.

## Regional style

Hammond places Palenque at the center of a **western Usumacinta style**: **mansard roofs, openwork roofcombs, and polychrome stucco dynastic themes** restricted to the lower Usumacinta region (with an outpost at **Comalcalco**). This contrasts with Tikal’s nine-tier temple-pyramids (NE Petén) and the Río Bec–Chenes–Puuc sequence farther north.

## Society and succession

Hammond’s social model notes **patrilineal elite inheritance with exceptions**, including **matrilineal transmission in unusual circumstances at Palenque**—important for reconstructing how royal power could pass when the male line failed. Palenque appears among the Pasion–Usumacinta centers (with Piedras Negras, Yaxchilan, Bonampak, Dos Pilas) whose **Late Classic epigraphy** is especially rich.

## Collapse

Like other western lowland capitals, Palenque **stopped erecting dated monuments** in the early ninth century and was **rapidly abandoned after ~800 CE** (Snow et al.; see [[maya-classic-collapse-800ce]]).

## From Evans & Webster 2001

Direct Palenque encyclopedia coverage in the extracted claims is thin; one load-bearing cross-link: sixteenth [[copan|Copán]] ruler **Yax Pac** may have been something of an outsider because his mother was a **royal woman from Palenque** and his father was not the preceding king (Webster, Copán entry). This documents elite marriage diplomacy between western Usumacinta and Motagua courts in the late eighth–early ninth centuries and supports Hammond’s note on matrilineal transmission exceptions at Palenque. Palenque remains listed among few Classic centers that may have dominated larger territories or influenced neighbors (with Tikal, Calakmul, Sayil, Copán) — though political domination was generally short-lived.
