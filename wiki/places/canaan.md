# Canaan

**Canaan** is the ancient name for the eastern shore of the Mediterranean and its
hinterland — roughly the modern territories of Lebanon, Syria, Israel, Jordan, and the
Palestinian West Bank and Gaza. In [[sources/noll-canaan-israel-antiquity-2013|K. L.
Noll's *Canaan and Israel in Antiquity* (2013)]] it is the organising category of the whole
history: not a nation or an ethnic group but a **single, relatively homogeneous "Canaanite
cultural sphere"** of which Israel was one part.

## A geographic label, not an ethnicity

In the ancient texts "Canaanite" was **not** an ethnic designation or a means of personal
identity. Canaan was a **geographic** term, and many documents define a Canaanite simply as
anyone who lived in the land of Canaan, regardless of background. The clearest case is the
[[merneptah|Merneptah stela]] (c. 1207 BCE), where an Egyptian king lists
[[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] among the peoples he defeated *in Canaan* — i.e., from the
Egyptian point of view, the people called Israel were Canaanite. The Bible itself preserves
this usage: Ezekiel 16.3 tells Jerusalem "your origin and your birth are of the land of the
Canaanites."

- The word was used for many centuries and shifted meaning. Sometimes it was **political**
  — during the [[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze Age]], Canaan was a province of the
  [[new-kingdom-egypt|Egyptian Empire]]. Its root may derive from a word for
  **purple-dyed cloth** (an expensive luxury), so "Canaan" could also designate a luxury
  **merchant** (Prov. 31.24).
- Ancient communities rarely called *themselves* Canaanite; each preferred its own local
  label — Israelites, [[philistines|Philistines]], Arameans, Judahites, Jebusites, and many
  more. The cultural lifestyles of all these groups were so similar that "neither the Bible
  nor any other ancient source enables a modern archaeologist to distinguish between these
  groups in the material remains of Canaan," except in rare and debated instances.
- Noll divides Canaan into a **northern half (Syria)** and a **southern half (Palestine)**.
  "Palestine" itself derives from "Philistine," the coastal people who lived in southern
  Canaan while Israelites lived in the southern highlands.

## Bronze Age Canaan

In the Late Bronze Age (1550–1150 BCE) Canaan was a patchwork of small city-states under
Egyptian hegemony, their mayors negotiating the demands of empire (the Amarna correspondence
is the classic window onto this world). The agrarian majority lived under a
[[divine-patronage-ancient-near-east|patron–client]] order in which a city's surplus flowed
upward to its lord and ultimately to Pharaoh. The
[[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze Age]] system fractured in the
[[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] (~1200 BCE), after which Egyptian control
receded, the Philistines settled the coast, and a wave of new
[[emergence-of-early-israel|highland villages]] appeared in the hill country — the matrix
from which Israel emerged.

## Why it mattered

Canaan was the **land bridge** between Egypt and Mesopotamia, repeatedly fought over by the
great powers (Egypt, [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]], Babylon, Persia) precisely because it
controlled the coastal road and the approaches to the Nile. Its enduring historical
significance, on Noll's account, is less imperial than cultural: it was the cradle of the
alphabet, of the [[ugaritic-religion|Ugaritic]] and broader West Semitic pantheon
([[yahwism-and-israelite-religion|El, Asherah, Baal, and eventually Yahweh]]), and of the
literary tradition that became the Hebrew Bible.
