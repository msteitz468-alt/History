# Northern Arid Zone

## Geographic description

The **Northern Arid Zone** does not form a coherent environmental, cultural, or research area: archaeologists have defined it largely in terms of what it is **not**—[[mesoamerica-culture-area|Mesoamerica]] (Randall H. McGuire, EW2001). The traditional northern Mesoamerican frontier was defined economically (farmers south / hunter-gatherers north; Kirchhoff 1943); Di Peso’s **“Gran Chichimeca”** and Braniff’s **“Mesoamerica Septentrional”** (southernmost Northern Arid Zone) are related frames. Armillas noted Formative northward farmer expansion past the Río Lerma peaking Early Postclassic then collapsing back to the Lerma, attributed to moist then desiccating climate.

The one unifying trait is **aridity** (evaporation > precipitation). Three physiographic provinces: **Sonoran Desert** (hot subtropical cactus desert—Baja, S Arizona, W Sonora); **Sierra Madre/Rockies** backbone (to 4000 m, pine-oak); **Chihuahuan Desert** (high temperate grassland/scrub with peyote in lower latitudes). Four major regions used for discussion: Baja California; Northeast Mexico; Mesoamerica Septentrional; Southwest US & Northwest Mexico. The key research issue is **Southwest–Mesoamerica interaction**.

## Historical significance

### Mesoamerica Septentrional (southern NAZ)

Earliest ceramics north of the Río Lerma are **Chupícuaro** (Terminal Formative 300 B.C.–A.D. 300), style ranging Guanajuato to Cuernavaca. Early Classic A.D. 300–600: farming expands into San Luis Potosí with Tunal Grande and Bajío ceramic provinces and multi-mound centers (Ibarrilla, Cañada de Alfaro). Early Postclassic A.D. 900–1200: decline north of the Lerma; Toltec types (Plumbate); possible Tula military domination/forts. **Tula’s collapse c. A.D. 1200** coincided with the abandonment of Mesoamerica Septentrional by Mesoamerican farmers (McGuire, EW2001).

**Balcón de Montezuma** (Tamaulipas): ~7 ha defensible hilltop; >90 circular stone foundations; Late Classic–Postclassic; may mark northern edge of Huastec/Mesoamerican civilization or Chichimec settlement with Huastec influence—frontier concept still poorly defined (Mary Pye).

### U.S. Southwest sequence (McGuire summary)

- First **maize** ~1500 B.C. in Archaic foraging; sedentary life and ceramics not before A.D. 200–300, seen as northern extension of Chupícuaro-linked Mesoamerican pattern.
- From A.D. 300: **Anasazi** (Colorado Plateau Black-on-White, pit houses then pueblos/kivas), **Mogollon** (mountain brownware, later pueblos), **Hohokam** (Sonoran Desert; after A.D. 700–900 distinguished by ball courts, platform mounds, canal irrigation, cremation, buffware). Early Mesoamerican contact markers: copper bells, macaws.
- A.D. 900–1200 regional centers: Hohokam (Phoenix Basin; Snaketown; max irrigation by 1100; ≥165 sites/≥206 ball courts), **Chaco** (~73,000 km² road network, great houses; rare Mesoamerican elements), **Mimbres** (~36,000 km²). All three collapse mid-12th century.
- A.D. 1200–1450: Salado (Phoenix Basin–Casas Grandes; elite goods including copper bells, macaws), Little Colorado, O’odham; late 13th-c. Pueblo reorganizations and Katsina religion with many Mesoamerican-origin symbols; 15th-c. Salado/Little Colorado collapse and Casas Grandes razing.

### Casas Grandes / Paquimé

**Casas Grandes (Paquimé)**, Chihuahua—largest prehistoric pueblo of U.S. Southwest/northern Mexico (>2000 rooms); revised occupation c. A.D. 1150–1450; combines pueblo residential architecture with Mesoamerican-style ball courts and platform mounds; imported shell, copper, turquoise; parrot cages for plumes. Original trading-outpost and highly centralized polity models now challenged in favor of prestige-goods competition among rising elites; ball courts and parrot cages within ~30 km but not at 70 km, suggesting limited polity scale (Whalen & Minnis, EW2001). See also [[casas-grandes-paquime]] and [[greater-mesoamerica]].

### Southwest–Mesoamerica interaction debate

Much Southwest culture derives from Mesoamerica (corn, beans, squash, cotton, pottery, multi-tiered universe, cyclic creation, Twins, Feathered Serpent, masked dancers, New Fire) but reworked in uniquely Southwestern social “deep structure.” ≤~1,500 Mesoamerican objects (pseudo-cloisonné mirrors, copper bells, ceramics, macaws) are known from all Southwest sites, mostly from the NW Mesoamerican frontier/West Mexico; SW turquoise is found there and in the Central Highlands.

Debate polarized as **“imperialist”** (Di Peso, Kelley: SW extension of Mesoamerica via pochteca-like agents) vs **“isolationist.”** Recent work favors significant regional interactions that did **not** drive overall Southwest cultural development (McGuire, EW2001). This northern frontier continuum is a core plank of [[greater-mesoamerica|Greater Mesoamerica]] revisionism.

Source: Randall H. McGuire, Michael E. Whalen & Paul E. Minnis, Mary Pye in [[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001|Evans & Webster (eds.) 2001]].
