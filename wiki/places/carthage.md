# Carthage

**Carthage** — Phoenician *Qart-ḥadašt*, "New City," traditionally founded from Tyre
c. 814 BCE on the Gulf of Tunis — was the dominant maritime and commercial power of the
western Mediterranean before Rome. "On nearer shores, the Punic peoples of Carthage built
an ambitious empire, before the Romans knew how to rig a sail." After its destruction by
[[roman-republic|Rome]] in 146 BCE at the close of the Punic Wars, it was refounded as a
Roman colony and became the second city of the Latin West — the great metropolis of
Roman Africa, the empire's chief grain and olive-oil hinterland.

## Punic Carthage (the city before 146 BCE)

Per Richard Miles ([[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010]]), the name *Qart-Hadasht*
("New City") marks Carthage as a **colonial settlement**, not a mere trading post,
strategically sited at the junction of the east–west (Levant–Spain) and north–south
(Tyrrhenian) trade routes. Archaeology dates the earliest occupation layers to c. **760
BCE** — later than the legendary 814 (see [[dido-elissa|Dido/Elissa]]). The settlement grew
explosively: within roughly a century it held some **30,000 people** (ringed by three large
cemeteries), packed with sun-dried-brick housing on a regular plan parallel to the shore,
walled by a 3-metre casbah-style casement wall in the early 7th c., and dependent at first
on heavy food imports before becoming a major manufacturing exporter.

- **The Byrsa** hill was the city's centre throughout its life; its religious heart was
  completely demolished in 146 BCE, leaving a ~60-cm black burn layer, though outlying
  districts survived (the intact "Hannibal Quarter" on the Byrsa preserves a snapshot of the
  late, ~700,000-population city).
- **Harbours and religion:** the rectangular commercial harbour adjoined the
  [[carthaginian-child-sacrifice|tophet]], one of the oldest sacred areas; the chief deities
  were [[carthaginian-religion|Baal Hammon and Tanit]], with Melqart a senior god whose
  Tyrian *egersis* was still practised. A circular military harbour (170 berths) was added
  in the city's prosperous final phase.
- **African hinterland:** from the 6th c. Carthage expanded over the fertile Medjerda valley
  and Cap Bon, later the Sahel and Syrtis, becoming a celebrated agricultural producer (Mago's
  agronomic treatise; Punic farming innovations such as the *tribulum* threshing cart and the
  pomegranate, Roman *malum Punicum*).

The city's destruction in 146 BCE — and Rome's dispersal of its libraries to the Numidian
princes — is the central event of [[third-punic-war-149-146bce|the Third Punic War]] and the
hinge of [[punic-faith|the Roman construction of "Carthage"]]. The Punic struggle for the
western Mediterranean ran through [[carthage-syracuse-rivalry|Sicily]] and the
[[first-punic-war-264-241bce|three Punic Wars]].

Mary Beard ([[sources/beard-spqr-2015]]) records the tradition — via Polybius as
eyewitness — that Scipio Aemilianus wept as Carthage burned in 146 BCE while
quoting from memory a line on the fall of Troy, reportedly reflecting that Rome
itself might one day meet the same fate. Beard treats the anecdote as telling
regardless of its sincerity: "crocodile tears or not, they made their point."

## Roman and Late-Antique Carthage

Under the empire Carthage flourished as "a hub connecting the fertile hinterland to the
wider world." Its prosperity rested on the agrarian wealth of North Africa, the breadbasket
that helped feed [[places/rome|Rome]] itself. The emperor **Hadrian**, arriving during a
five-year drought, built a great **aqueduct over 120 km long** — among the longest Roman
water-supply works ever constructed — to secure the city's water.

The city was central to the religious and demographic crises of the third century:

- It was the see of **[[actors/cyprian|Cyprian]]**, bishop from 248, whose sermons during
  the **[[events/plague-of-cyprian-249|Plague of Cyprian]]** are a primary witness to that
  pandemic.
- North Africa around Carthage was **parched by severe drought in the 240s**, an aridity
  that framed Cyprian's defense of Christianity against pagans who blamed the disasters on
  the new religion.

Carthage remained a flourishing metropolis through the sixth century, recovered for the
empire by [[actors/belisarius|Belisarius]] from the Vandals in 533. But "from the later
sixth century, there was manifest recession all across the African heartland," as the
seaborne networks and population that sustained it entered the long late-antique
contraction described in [[concepts/roman-climate-optimum|Harper's environmental history]].
The city fell to the Arab conquest in 698. See
[[sources/harper-fate-of-rome-2017|Harper, *The Fate of Rome*]].

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

Attributed claims from [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]] (Chs. XV–XVI, XXXIII–XXXVI, XLI, XLVI).

- **Christian metropolis.** Cyprian of Carthage opposed Roman pontifical monarchy “like Hannibal”; cathedral later maintained hundreds of ministers; considerable estates on great churches by late 3rd c.
- **Donatist origin.** Double election at Carthage (Caecilian vs Majorinus/Donatus) produced the African schism lasting until Christianity’s extinction in Africa.
- **Vandal capture (439).** “Carthage was at length surprised by the Vandals, five hundred and eighty-five years after… the younger Scipio”; granary of Rome lost; fleets again claimed Mediterranean empire from Carthage’s port.
- **455 spoils.** Temple ornaments, Jewish Temple spoils, Christian treasure, and Imperial captives carried to Carthage after Genseric’s sack of Rome.
- **Belisarius (533).** Ad Decimum; Carthage opened joyfully; Tricameron; end of Vandal kingdom.
- **Heraclius.** Exarch’s son sailed from Carthage to seize the capital and overthrow Phocas.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Carthage’s wealth rested on Africa Proconsularis/Byzacena grain and olives and the shortest Europe–Africa traverse. Few Numidian/Proconsular cities had walls in the 3rd–4th centuries; Carthage’s own defenses were only built in **425** — “ironically just fourteen years before the city fell to the Vandals” (439). Gaiseric redistributed the best Proconsular land to warriors as a new hereditary class. After reconquest (533) Justinian restored territory respectively to private landowners, the state, and the church, and rebuilt defenses/churches; occupied area shrank before the Arab fall (698).

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015|Mitchell, *A History of the Later Roman Empire* (2015)]].
