# Roman Judaea (Herodian Kingdom and Province)

The Jewish homeland under Rome — ruled first as the **client kingdom of [[herod-the-great|Herod]]
and his dynasty**, then (from 6 CE) as a **directly administered province** whose tensions
culminated in the great revolt of 66. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 14d
(**Martin Goodman**), drawing above all on **Josephus**.

## The Herodian dynasty

Herod's rule "was inaugurated in 40 BCE and preserved until c. 4 BCE almost entirely at the
behest of Rome." His **Idumaean** father **Antipater** had risen by exploiting Hasmonaean
faction and cultivating Roman officials; Herod, granted the throne by the triumvirs and Senate
(40 BCE) and installed by Sosius' storming of Jerusalem (37), proved a competent and ruthless
client king (see [[herod-the-great]]). His reign rested on a **complete break with the
[[hasmonean-dynasty|Hasmonaean]] past** — the killing of Antigonus' associates and his own
Hasmonaean wife **Mariamme** and her kin, the **breaking of the high priesthood** (shortening
tenures, holding the high-priestly vestments, appointing diaspora Jews of undisputed but
powerless lineage), and the replacement of the old elite with his own largely Gentile court.
Goodman stresses Herod's **"double book-keeping"** — Jewish to Jews (no portrait on his coins,
the lavish rebuilding of the **Temple**), Greek to Gentiles — though he judges this more
characteristic of Herod's grandson **Agrippa I**.

At Herod's death (**4 BCE**) the disputed succession and the procurator Sabinus' exactions
sparked **widespread revolt** (Judas in Galilee, Simon in Peraea, Athronges in Judaea), crushed
by **Varus**, legate of Syria, with two legions. Augustus upheld Herod's will, dividing the
realm: **Archelaus** (ethnarch of Judaea/Samaria), **Antipas** (tetrarch of Galilee/Peraea), and
**Philip** (the north-east).

## From kingdom to province (6 CE)

In **6 CE** complaints of misrule led Augustus to **depose and banish Archelaus** and **annex
Judaea, Samaria, and Idumaea** as a Roman province under an **equestrian prefect** (later
procurator), under the general supervision of the **legate of Syria** — the census of Quirinius
marking the new fiscal order. Goodman's theme: Roman favour to the Herods was "remarkably
constant," each ruler judged by **efficiency** at keeping the peace and easily deposed when
incompetent (Archelaus; **Antipas**, exiled 39). The dynasty flickered back to power once more
under **Agrippa I**, who — by court intrigue and the friendship of Gaius, then his central role
in the accession of [[claudius|Claudius]] (41) — briefly held **the whole kingdom of Herod**
(41–44), before his sudden death returned Judaea to direct rule. **Agrippa II** held lesser
territories and the right to oversee the **Temple**, but no longer governed the province.

## Structural tensions

Goodman locates the roots of conflict in the structure Herod created and Rome inherited: the
delegitimized **high priesthood**; the resented **taxation** (and the question of paying any tax
to rulers of doubted legitimacy); the enduring **Hasmonaean myth of national liberation**; and
the friction of direct Roman rule with Jewish religious sensibility (the procurators' provocations,
the affair of Gaius' statue). The province yielded little revenue (a **twelfth** of Egypt's, per
[[roman-egypt|Bowman]]) but was, as Goodman notes, "not easy to hold in subjection."

## The road to revolt

Under [[nero|Nero]], the exactions of the procurator **Gessius Florus** — seizing 17 talents
from the **Temple treasury** (66) — sparked an uprising the Jewish elite "were unable to
control." The defeat of the Syrian legate **Cestius Gallus** (Nov. 66) forced a full-scale war;
Nero appointed **Vespasian** to its conduct (67), whose pacification of Galilee (and the capture
of the future historian **Josephus**) was interrupted only by Nero's fall and Vespasian's own
bid for the throne — the **Jewish Revolt** running on to the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 (the
subject of CAH XI).

## The High Empire: catastrophe and reconstitution (CAH XI, Goodman)

[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 22 (also **Goodman**) covers the period when "the fortunes of
the Jews sank to their lowest ebb" — three disastrous wars and the loss of the homeland:

- **The destruction of 70 and its aftermath.** **Titus** stormed Jerusalem and **burned the
  Temple** (Aug. 70); the city was razed and *legio X Fretensis* permanently garrisoned. Flavian
  power was justified by **propaganda of the Jewish victory** (the *Iudaea Capta* coinage, the
  Temple of Pax). The land was confiscated; the **fiscus Iudaicus** redirected the Temple tax to
  Capitoline Jupiter — a "demeaning" annual poll-tax on all Jews (rigorously exacted by Domitian,
  its *calumnia* eased by Nerva). Masada's defenders, per Josephus, chose **suicide** (73/74).
- **A society without the Temple.** The destruction shattered the **pillars of Judaean society** —
  Temple, priesthood, Sanhedrin, the old ruling class all gone, Agrippa II given no authority over
  Jews. Authority became **localized**, and into the vacuum stepped the **scribes**, above all the
  **rabbis**: the academy at **Yavneh (Jamnia)** (the tradition of Yohanan b. Zakkai; then Rabban
  Gamaliel II of the house of Hillel). Goodman stresses that rabbinic authority spread **gradually**
  and was probably "not far advanced by 132"; Rome entrusted local administration not to the rabbis
  but to **gentile cities** founded nearby (Flavia Neapolis, Flavia Joppa, the *colonia* Caesarea).
- **The Diaspora Revolt (115–117).** A coordinated, ferocious uprising of diaspora Jews in **Egypt,
  Cyrene, Cyprus, and Mesopotamia** — exploiting **Trajan's absence on the Parthian campaign** —
  with messianic leaders (Lukuas/Andreas) and huge gentile casualties, suppressed by **Q. Marcius
  Turbo** and **Lusius Quietus**. Its effect was **cataclysmic**: the great Jewish communities of
  Egypt, Cyrene, and Cyprus were effectively annihilated (Jews banned from Cyprus). The Jews of
  **Palestine and northern Syria did not join**, suggesting no central organization (see
  [[cyrenaica]]).
- **The Bar Kochba War (132–135).** The greatest revolt: led by **Simon bar Kosiba (Bar Kochba)**,
  whose letters from the Judaean desert reveal a fierce insistence on **discipline, an organized
  state, and religious observance**. Goodman weighs the causes — Hadrian's plan to refound Jerusalem
  as the pagan **Aelia Capitolina** (Dio) and/or the **ban on circumcision** (*Historia Augusta*) —
  and judges it one of Rome's costliest second-century campaigns (detachments drawn from many
  legions; **C. Iulius Severus** summoned from Britain). The aftermath was **thorough Roman
  reprisal** (the fall of Bethar, mass enslavement, the bones in the desert caves). See
  [[bar-kochba-war-132-135]].

## Significance

Judaea is Goodman's exemplar of the **client kingdom-to-province trajectory** and of the
peculiar difficulty of governing a society defined by an exclusive monotheism. The Herodian
experiment — a Gentile-staffed monarchy ruling a Jewish nation for Rome — bought stability at the
cost of a delegitimized native leadership, leaving the province, once under direct Roman rule, to
oscillate between fragile order and the explosive revolt that would end the Second Temple.
