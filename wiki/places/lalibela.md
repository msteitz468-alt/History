# Lalibela

**Lalibela** (historically known as **Roha** or **Adefa**) is a highland town in the Lasta district of what is now the Amhara Region of Ethiopia, at approximately 2,500 m elevation. It is famous for its complex of eleven **rock-hewn churches**, among the most remarkable monuments of medieval African Christianity, and served as the political and ecclesiastical centre of the [[zagwe-dynasty|Zagwe dynasty]] from approximately the eleventh through early thirteenth centuries.

## Historical Development

The site was significant before the Zagwe. Phillipson's structural analysis (*Foundations*, 2012) identifies **five successive developmental stages** in the rock-hewn complex:

**Stage I–II (possibly 7th–8th c. CE)**: The earliest rock-hewn features at Lalibela were **defensive, not ecclesiastical** — massive above-ground masonry walls, elaborately protected entrances, and an underground tunnel connecting features. No Aksumite-style architectural elements are present. These pre-date the Zagwe and may represent the non-ecclesiastical importance of the site before Zagwe authority.

**Stage III (c. 10th c. CE)**: First demonstrably Christian innovations: conversion of earlier defensive structures to ecclesiastical use, including the excavation of sanctuaries in Aksumite style. Beta Danagel was probably created at this stage. This is the period when the political centre of gravity was shifting from eastern Tigray into Lasta.

**Stage IV (c. 11th c. CE)**: The great **monolithic basilican churches** for which Lalibela is famous. Aksumite architectural features predominate — but, unlike Tigray practice, door- and window-frames were carved integrally from the volcanic tuff (not wooden inserts). The churches are freestanding within great excavated pits, carved externally and internally with remarkable precision. This is the stage of Beta Emmanuel, Beta Madhane Alem (the largest, with external colonnade possibly replicating the ancient cathedral at Aksum), and Beta Maryam. Their explicit self-consciousness about Aksumite heritage strongly suggests deliberate political messaging — the Zagwe stressing continuity with Christian Aksum.

**Stage V (late 12th – early 13th c. CE)**: Features associated with King **Lalibela** himself, the Zagwe king whose reign coincided with the fall of Jerusalem to Salah ed-Din in 1187. Stage V includes Beta Golgotha (traditional burial place of the king), Beta Mika'el, Beta Abba Libanos, and Beta Giyorgis — churches that complete the symbolic recreation of Jerusalem and its environs at the site. The Jordan River equivalent, mounts Tabor and Olives, the Tomb of Adam, and Beta Golgotha all receive their Jerusalem toponym at this final stage. The **toponym "Lalibela" for the place is not attested until the sixteenth or seventeenth century**; the Ethiopian Orthodox Church still formally calls it "Debra Roha."

The church of **Imrahanna Kristos** — a homogeneous built (not rock-hewn) cave church north of Lalibela, with strong Aksumite architectural features — is attributed by tradition to one of King Lalibela's predecessors and probably dates to the early twelfth century.

## Geology and Architecture

Lasta rock-hewn churches differ from their Tigray predecessors in their medium: **soft volcanic tuff** rather than hard sandstone. Tuff allowed finer carving of wooden architectural details integrally from the rock, but has proved more susceptible to weathering. Wooden features, structural screens, and decorative elements in Tigray were insertions into sandstone; at Lalibela they are carved monolithically. The two groups are geographically separated by c. 100 km with no intermediate churches recorded, and differ significantly in age — Tigray churches peak in the 7th–10th centuries; Lalibela's great florescence is the 11th–12th centuries.

## Symbolism

The complex as a whole came to symbolise Jerusalem and the sacred landscape of the Holy Land — a function most explicitly realised in Stage V. The suggestion that Beta Madhane Alem intentionally replicated the ancient Maryam Tsion Cathedral at Aksum (Dabbert 1938; Buxton 1947) remains plausible but unproven. If so, Lalibela simultaneously imitated Jerusalem (the universal Christian holy city) and Aksum (the Aksumite ancestral capital) — a double legitimation strategy of the Zagwe.

The **Ark of the Covenant** may have been at Lalibela during the late twelfth century, according to Abu al-Makarim (c. 1200).

## Historiography

Ecclesiastical tradition attributes all eleven churches to the reign of King Lalibela. Phillipson argues this should be understood as indicating when the most recent stage was added and the complex received its overall symbolism — not when construction began. Lepage (2002) proposed attribution of the earliest defensive features to the activity of Mika'el, an early thirteenth-century Egyptian metropolitan; Phillipson refutes this, arguing Stage I predates King Lalibela by at least three centuries. Dating remains contested: Buxton proposed 11th–12th century for most; Phillipson argues Stage IV centres on the eleventh century; Stage V on late twelfth–early thirteenth.
