# Tyre

**Tyre** (Phoenician *Ṣur*, "rock") was the foremost [[phoenicians|Phoenician]]
city-state of the Iron Age — the **mother-city of [[carthage|Carthage]] and the western
colonies**, the seat of the god **Melqart**, and the commercial leader of Phoenicia
from c. 800 to 600 BCE. Its island fortress withstood siege after siege until Alexander.
[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 32, W. Culican) treats it.

## Geographic description

Tyre's old city stood on a **rocky island just off the coast**, with natural defences,
deep wells, and (according to Ezekiel's imagery) fresh-water springs even under the sea
— making it formidably hard to besiege. A mainland settlement (**Ushu**, the
"Palaetyrus") faced it, supplying water and farmland. The island's security underlay
Tyre's commercial confidence and its survival through the imperial centuries.

## Commerce, purple, and Melqart

Tyre commanded the central Phoenician trades: **murex purple dye** (the "Tyrian purple"
monopoly), **Lebanese cedar timber**, **bronze and silver work** (the Bible makes the
Tyrians expert bronzeworkers; Homer calls Sidon "rich in bronze"), and the long-range
metals trade — the "**Tarshish ships**" working Sardinian and Spanish silver. Its
tutelary god **Melqart** ("king of the city"), a Heracles-like sea-god, was carried west
by the colonists; his temple's iconography — the **twin pillars** (the "Pillars of
Hercules"), the perpetual flame, the god's tomb — was **duplicated across the
[[phoenician-colonization|western colonies]] to Cadiz**.

## Under Assyria and Babylon

Tyre paid tribute to Assyria from the ninth century and was handled with relative
**leniency** — officials installed, the cedar trade taxed, but the kings left in place,
since a prosperous Tyre served Assyrian interests. The hard moments came with revolt:
**[[sennacherib|Sennacherib]]** drove King **Luli (Elulaios)** to flee to Cyprus (the
scene is on his Nineveh reliefs); **[[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon]]** bound **Ba'al I** by
treaty (and limited Tyre's trade north of Byblos); **[[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]]** kept
it a vassal, returning a hostage prince. Then came the great trial: **[[nebuchadnezzar-ii|
Nebuchadnezzar II]] besieged Tyre for thirteen years (c. 587–574)** — a blockade rather
than a storm, ending in Tyre's submission while the Saite Egyptian navy raided back.
Ezekiel 27 preserves a "fulsome profile of Tyre's trading network" composed around this
event.

## Why Tyre mattered

- **Metropolis of the colonial world** — founder of [[carthage|Carthage]] (trad. 814) and,
  by tradition, of the western network; the religious and commercial template the colonies
  reproduced.
- **Commercial leader of Phoenicia** — the dominant city c. 800–600, hub of the purple,
  timber, and metals trades.
- **The unconquerable island** — its sieges (Shalmaneser V, Sennacherib, Nebuchadnezzar)
  became proverbial; it fell only to **Alexander (332)**, who built a mole to reach it.

## Changing significance

Tyre's significance shifted from **Bronze Age trading town** to **Iron Age commercial
capital and colonial metropolis** to **imperial vassal** (Assyrian, Babylonian, Persian),
its independence finally ended by Alexander — after which it remained an important
Hellenistic and Roman port.

## Crusades-era Tyre (Cobb 2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb, *The Race for Paradise* (2014)]] covers Tyre as the Levantine coast’s last major Muslim holdout of the early twelfth century and as Saladin’s great missed opportunity after Hattin.

**Coastal mop-up and 1112 hold.** After Genoese-assisted Frankish captures of Jaffa/Haifa/Arsuf/Caesarea (**1100**), Jubayl and Acre (**1104**), Beirut and Sidon (**1110**), Franks concentrated on Tyre from **February 1112**. Arab sailor defenses (winch/rams); Tyrians offered the city to Tughtakin of Damascus; he harassed the Franks, raided forts, and blockaded Sidon until the Franks lifted the siege to Acre. Tughtakin took control but wrote the Fatimids he would hold only until they claimed it—Tyre remained Muslim for the moment. Franks finally took Tyre in **1124** after nearly twenty-five years of pressure, completing near-total Mediterranean coast control in the kingdom’s orbit.

**1187–88: Conrad’s defense.** After [[battle-of-hattin-1187|Hattin]] and the [[fall-of-jerusalem-1187|fall of Jerusalem]], Saladin reconnoitered Tyre twice but left it untouched—an “act of expediency he would live to regret.” He relocated to besiege Tyre even before Jerusalem’s affairs were fully settled. Ibn Jubayr (passed through **1184**) called Tyre proverbially impregnable, prepared as a Frankish refuge; harbor key—walls on three sides, mole, chain between seaward towers. One chronicler suggests the city, thronged with Frankish refugees but leaderless, was about to surrender before **Conrad of Montferrat** arrived and rallied the defense. Saladin blockaded by land and sea with five galleys; on **30 December 1187** Conrad’s dozen-plus galleys broke the blockade because Muslim watch sailors had fallen asleep; Muslim ships fled, beached, hulls stove in. **1 January 1188**: Saladin withdrew; armies dispersed; morale, supplies, and funds low despite a final massive assault. The September **1192** truce left Franks holding the coast from Tyre to Jaffa.

**End.** After Acre fell (**18 May 1291**), Tyre, Sidon, Beirut, and Haifa soon surrendered with little or no resistance (Tyre: few dozen old men and women per Cobb’s summary of Muslim accounts).
