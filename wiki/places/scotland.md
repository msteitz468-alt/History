# Scotland

The northern third of the island of [[british-isles|Britain]]. In the early Middle Ages
"Scotland" was not a meaningful entity: around 500 the area was divided among the
**Picts** (north of the Mounth, the highland barrier), the trans-marine kingdom of
**[[actors/dal-riata|Dál Riata]]** (Argyll and the southern Hebrides, with strong Irish
links), and the British kingdoms of the "Old North" between the Roman walls — the
**Gododdin** (from the Votadini, centred on Edinburgh), **Alt Clut / Strathclyde**
(Dumbarton, surviving into the tenth century), and **Rheged** (the Solway, under Urien
c.600). The Picts were a British-cultural people speaking **P-Celtic**, not the
"mysterious" race of legend; their name was a Roman coinage of c.300, and their carved
symbol-stones are the main non-textual evidence for Pictish elites.

## The Making of Alba

Over the ninth and tenth centuries these polities resolved into the
**[[actors/kingdom-of-alba|kingdom of Alba]]** — the Picts vanishing from the record
c.875–900, a linguistic shift toward Gaelic, and the mac Alpín dynasty consolidating its
ascendancy partly by replacing Pictish imagery on picture-stones with Christian imagery
(Driscoll). The decisive reign was that of **Constantín son of Aed** (900–943); the
inauguration site became **Scone**. The conflicts of Donnchad, **Macbethad** (Macbeth),
and **Máel Coluim III** were the closing episodes of an eleventh-century dynastic crisis,
after which Máel Coluim's marriage to [[actors/margaret-of-scotland|Margaret]] opened the
Anglo-Norman era.

## The Norse and the North

[[processes/scandinavian-settlement-of-britain|Scandinavian settlement]] was heavier here
than in England: place-names are "ubiquitous" in **Orkney, Shetland, and Caithness**, the
**Norn** dialect survived into the late Middle Ages, and boat-burials and female/juvenile
graves abound — "one of the clearest examples of population movement into previously
occupied territory known to archaeology" (Barrett). By the mid-tenth century Alba was
"surrounded on three sides by polities of Scandinavian origin."

## Historiography

Scottish early-medieval scholarship has recently been "Gaelicized" — shifting from a model
of parallel institutional development with England toward an Irish-Sea, Gaelic context
(Broun, Woolf). The central source, the **Chronicle of the Kings of Alba**, survives only
in a late copy: "without CKA we would be lost; with it … we are scarcely on firm ground."
Large areas (north of the Mounth, the Western Isles, the south-west) are "effectively
without history" for the tenth century. See [[concepts/four-nations-british-history]].
