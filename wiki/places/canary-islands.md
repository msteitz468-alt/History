# Canary Islands

The **Canary Islands** — the *Insulae Fortunatae* ("Fortunate Isles") of
classical and medieval geography — were the site of Europe's late-medieval
encounter with a "Neolithic" people, an encounter David Abulafia (in
*[[abulafia-berend-medieval-frontiers-2002|Medieval Frontiers]]*) reads as the
direct **rehearsal for the European encounter with the Americas after 1492**. The
archipelago is the frontier where "Neolithic meets Medieval," and where the
ethnographic methods, missionary-crusading justifications, papal claims to
dispose of pagan land, and the "noble savage"/"wild man" duality that would
reappear in the New World were all worked out first.

## The Islands and Their Peoples

Of up to twelve islands, seven were inhabited by Berber-speaking populations that
were culturally distinct island to island — the term **Guanche** properly
applies only to Tenerife, though it is now loosely used for all. The peoples were
technologically Stone Age: no metalworking, no weaving (clothing was sewn animal
skins), and by the late Middle Ages no inter-island navigation (the art having
apparently been lost). They practised animal husbandry throughout and agriculture
on several islands, with toasted barley (ancestor of modern *gofio*) a staple.
Tenerife was organised into nine tribes under petty kings called *menceys*; Gran
Canaria's inhabitants showed the most advanced building and irrigation; some
islands preserved a pictographic/alphabetic script akin to Saharan writing. The
classical name derives from Pliny's *Natural History* (the "discovery" ascribed
to King Juba of Mauretania), "Canaria" reputedly from the dogs (*canes*) he
recorded there.

## The Medieval Encounter

- **1341** — a Portuguese-service expedition under the Genoese captain **Niccoloso
  da Recco**, with Castilians, Catalans and Florentines aboard, reached the
  islands; its account survives reworked by **Boccaccio** as *De Canaria*. The
  crew found naked islanders, an independent agriculture of wheat and figs, and a
  temple statue they carried back to Lisbon, along with four young Canarians.
- **1340s** — Majorcan missionary and commercial voyages founded a bishopric at
  **Telde** on Gran Canaria that lasted several decades; the Catalan **Jaume
  Ferrer** (1346) sailed on beyond the islands seeking the West African "River of
  Gold" linked to [[mansa-musa|Mansa Musa]] of Mali.
- **1344** — Pope **Clement VI** granted the Castilian **Luis de la Cerda** the
  Fortunate Isles as a *feudum perpetuum*, asserting papal authority to dispose of
  unconquered pagan islands "to eliminate their wicked pagan error."
- **1402** — **Jean de Béthencourt's** Norman-French expedition (documented in
  *Le Canarien*), whose leaders were veterans of the 1390 crusade against
  al-Mahdiyya, began the conquest of the eastern islands.
- **1490s** — **Alonso de Lugo** completed the conquest of Tenerife for the Crown
  of Castile.

## Two Images: Noble Savage and Wild Man

Already in the 1340s two competing European representations emerged that would
outlast the Middle Ages: **Boccaccio's** intelligent, sociable islanders,
innocent in their nakedness and ripe for civilizing — the "noble savage" — and
**Petrarch's** (*De Vita Solitaria*) islanders "so little unlike brute beasts,"
the "wild man." Abulafia argues both strands persist into the language that
justified conquest and, ultimately, the slave trade, and cautions that the
romanticized 16th-century chronicles (Espinosa, Abreu Galindo, Torriani) impose a
chivalric frame; the earliest sources and archaeology are more reliable.

## Rights of Infidels

The Canaries fed a live canon-law debate on **the political rights of pagans**.
Pope Innocent IV (Sinibaldo Fieschi) had argued that pagans could not be deprived
of lordship merely for being pagan; Aquinas engaged the same question; King
Duarte of Portugal (1436) argued to Eugenius IV that the "nearly wild" islanders
lacked religion, law and social order and so were fair objects of conversion. This
debate (preserved in Vatican Register 62) directly prefigures the 16th-century
argument over the rights of American Indians — one of the clearest lines Abulafia
draws from the medieval frontier to the Atlantic world.

## The 1492 Link

**Columbus** stopped at **La Gomera** in September 1492 on his way west, and on
reaching the Caribbean explicitly compared the skin colour of the Arawaks to that
of Canary Islanders, "since this is in the same latitude as the island of Hierro."
The Canaries were literally the last European-held ground before the Atlantic
crossing, and conceptually the template for reading the peoples on its far side.

## Significance

The archipelago is the volume's bridge from the [[medieval-frontier|medieval
frontier]] to the prehistory of overseas expansion: a frontier of radical
technological and cultural difference, colonised through Mediterranean methods and
papal-crusading legitimation, that furnished the direct precedent for
[[columbian-exchange-1492|1492 and the Columbian Exchange]].
