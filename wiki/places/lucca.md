# Lucca

**Lucca** and its territory (the Lucchesia) are [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Chris Wickham]]’s best-documented Italian case for eighth-century **urban aristocracy, fragmented landowning, and weak village communities**—a foil both to northern Francia’s denser aristocratic landscapes and to Egyptian village strength ([[aphrodito]]). Part of: [[italy]].

## City-based Lombard aristocracy

The eighth-century Lombard aristocracy seems above all **city-based and city-orientated**: called *cives*, stating *casa habitationis* in city or suburbs, privileging urban churches in gifts. Of thirteen richest documented Lucchese families in the late Lombard period, only two or three seem to have lived in the countryside. Each city, with local government, tribunals, episcopal structures, and local military identity (e.g. *exercitus Senensium civitatis*, 730), focused the political activity of most of its territory’s landed elite. Cities were dominated by **many families, not one**—facilitating checks and balances analogous to Merovingian Clermont’s factions. Lucca is repeatedly cited for surviving classical spatial kit and dense church networks (Wickham notes **39 eighth-century churches** in Lucca as an example of Italian urban church density).

## Fragmented tenure

Large- and small-scale landowning was **highly fragmented**; there is no record of large single-owner land blocks. Few villages had lands of only one external urban or ecclesiastical owner, so no single outsider could easily dominate village society from outside. This contrasts with Île-de-France estate blocks (*villae* as single estates) and with middle Rhine aristocrats richer than even major Lucchese owners (e.g. Rupertiner vs Peredeus of Lucca). Italian bipartite estates and *angaria* appear early in Tuscany (Lucca, Monte Amiata) from the 730s–740s, but demesnes were small and fragmented—labour as status/subjection more than intensifying production ([[bipartite-manor]]).

## Weak villages, strong patronage choice

Eighth-century Lucchese villages were **socially weak**: settlement dispersed (extreme in the plain), village boundaries sometimes uncertain, economic co-operation limited under Mediterranean dry farming. Free peasant owners could choose among local/urban, lay/ecclesiastical patrons; weak horizontal village solidarity made some vertical patronage almost unavoidable, embedding peasants in an aristocratic-dominated world despite tenurial independence. Free–unfree boundary was sharp (a Pisa court-case of 796 shows status could not be read from agricultural work alone). Wickham contrasts this with Aphroditō, where the village community structured interaction “from top to bottom,” and with middle Rhine villages acting collectively.

## Why it mattered

Lucca is Wickham’s type-case for the **post-Roman world as more like Lucca and Mainz than like Paris**: fragmented ownership, urban elites, limited manor intensification, peasant owners still present but patronage-embedded.
