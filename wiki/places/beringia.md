## Overview

Beringia was the broad land isthmus that connected northeastern Siberia (Asia) and Alaska (North America) during periods of lowered sea levels caused by Pleistocene glaciation. During the last Ice Age, sea levels fell 100–150 meters below current levels as vast quantities of water were locked up in glacial ice, exposing a continental shelf that today lies under the Bering Sea and Chukchi Sea.

The term "land bridge" is misleading — Beringia was not a narrow crossing. At its maximum extent it was approximately **1,600 km (1,000 miles) across**, a landmass roughly the size of Australia. It was, for the most part, **unglaciated**: small ice caps existed in places, but the regions of northeastern Siberia and western Alaska that comprised Beringia formed a single continuous landmass.

## Geological History

Beringia formed and submerged repeatedly during Pleistocene glacial cycles, each time the ice sheets expanded (locking water on land and lowering sea levels) and then retreated (releasing water and raising them). The last great exposure occurred during the Last Glacial Maximum (approximately 26,500–19,000 BP), when sea levels were near their minimum. The isthmus began to flood again as global temperatures rose after ~18,000 BP. The Bering Strait re-formed around 11,000 BP as rising seas finally separated Alaska from Siberia.

## Ecology and Environment

During the last glacial maximum, Beringia was part of the **mammoth steppe** biome — a cold, relatively dry grassland that extended from western Europe through Siberia into Beringia. This biome supported mammoths, woolly rhinoceros, bison, horses, reindeer, cave lions, and other large mammals, but was harsh and lacked edible plants for humans. The ecological zones were organized roughly in east-west bands:

| Zone | Type |
|---|---|
| Tundra (Arctic) | Tundra |
| Taiga | Boreal Forest |
| Forest-Steppe | Park Grassland |
| Steppe | Grassland |

Despite these conditions, Beringia was largely **ice-free** while most of Canada lay under kilometer-thick glaciers. Eastern Beringia (modern Alaska) remained habitable even during the glacial maximum when the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets merged and sealed off the interior of North America.

## Role in Human Prehistory

Beringia was the sole land route by which humans could reach the Americas from the Old World before the development of advanced ocean-going vessels. Its significance in human prehistory is unique: it was both the staging ground for the colonization of the Americas and a potential refuge where populations were isolated for extended periods.

**Sequence of human occupation:**
- Humans reached the Yana RHS site in northeastern Siberia ~30,000 years ago.
- By ~21,000 BCE, genetic, dental, and linguistic evidence indicates humans had entered western Beringia.
- Evidence from Bluefish Caves (Yukon) suggests human presence in eastern Beringia by ~24,000 years ago (though contested).
- The Beringian population may have grown to **8,000–10,000 people** over the following 5,000 years (Mulligan and Kitchen 2014).
- By at least ~14,500 BCE (~16,500 BP), ancestors of American Indians were living in eastern Beringia (modern Alaska), unknowingly poised to expand southward.
- Domesticated dogs joined these human bands by at least ~10,500 BCE.

**Why the southward expansion was blocked:**
The Laurentide ice sheet (centered over north-central Canada) and the Cordilleran ice sheet (centered over the Rocky Mountains) merged at glacial maximum, creating a solid wall of ice south of Beringia. The ice-free corridor between these two sheets did not open to human passage until approximately **12,600 years ago (10,650 BCE)** — by which time [[clovis-culture|Clovis culture]] had already appeared south of the glaciers, suggesting Clovis may have derived from an earlier coastal entry rather than through the corridor. The corridor was open after ~10,650 BCE and likely provided a route for later Northern Paleoindian groups.

**Beringian "standstill" hypothesis:**
Genetic evidence is consistent with a period during which the founding population of American Indians was isolated in Beringia — possibly for several thousand years — before expanding southward. This isolation allowed the genetic distinctiveness of American Indian populations to develop. The upper and lower bounds on this period are understood: Beringia was occupied no more than ~23,000 years ago (upper limit) and ancestors of American Indians were in eastern Beringia by at least ~14,500 BCE (lower limit for southward expansion).

## Key Archaeological Sites in Beringia

- **Yana RHS** (northeastern Siberia): evidence of humans ~30,000 BP, at 71°N latitude
- **Bluefish Caves** (Yukon, Canada): bone tool marks suggesting human presence ~24,000 BP
- **Swan Point** (Tanana River valley, Alaska): derived from Dyuktai culture, dates to ~12,000 BCE
- **Mead and Broken Mammoth** (Tanana River valley, Alaska): among the earliest tool assemblages in eastern Beringia
- **Dry Creek** (Alaska): among the earliest Northern Paleoindian assemblages, predating Clovis south of the glaciers by a few centuries
- **Ushki Lake** (Kamchatka Peninsula, Siberia): stemmed bifacial points and evidence of salmon exploitation; some archaeologists see similarities with early Alaskan Paleoindian assemblages
- **Dyuktai Cave** (Siberia): late Pleistocene complex relevant to peopling; emphasized mobile strategy and small temporary camps

## Significance for Understanding the Peopling of America

Beringia is the keystone geographic feature in any account of the [[colonization-americas-14500bp|colonization of the Americas]]. Two major debates turn on Beringian geography:

1. **The ice-free corridor vs. coastal route debate**: Did the first Americans move south through the gap between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets, or did they follow the Pacific coast? The timing of corridor viability (~10,650 BCE) versus the Monte Verde site date (~10,500 BCE, south of the corridor by 15,000 km) strongly favors the coastal route for the earliest arrivals. See [[clovis-first-controversy]].

2. **The Beringian isolation model**: Whether the founding population of American Indians spent an extended period isolated in Beringia — accumulating genetic distinctiveness — before expanding into the Americas. Genetic and linguistic evidence points to such a period; its duration and continuity are debated.

## Historiography

Beringia was first proposed as a migration route in the early 20th century. The geological evidence for its existence (sea-level records, submerged shorelines, continental shelf bathymetry) is secure. What remains debated is the exact timing and nature of human occupation — specifically whether occupation was continuous through the Last Glacial Maximum or whether populations fell back to Siberia during the harshest conditions. Archaeological evidence from Bluefish Caves supports presence by ~24,000 BP, but the sparse record makes interpretation difficult.

**Dating methods**: Radiocarbon, stratigraphy, genetic molecular clocks, glottochronology, and dental morphology all contribute lines of evidence. Radiocarbon dates require calibration; for this period calibration curves are well-established.
