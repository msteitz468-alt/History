# Gades and Tartessos (the Phoenician far west)

The **far west of the [[phoenician-colonization|Phoenician world]]** — the colony of
**Gades (Cadiz)** at the Atlantic gateway, and the native silver kingdom of
**Tartessos** in southern Spain — was the ultimate goal of the Phoenician westward
drive, the source of the **silver** that drew Tyre across the whole Mediterranean.
[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 32, W. Culican) treats it.

## Gades — the gateway colony

**Gades** (Phoenician *Gadir*, "the walled place"; modern Cadiz), on an island at the
Atlantic mouth of the Mediterranean, was the westernmost major Phoenician colony — a
foundation traditionally older even than Carthage, though the archaeology (as at
Carthage) does not confirm the highest dates. Its great **temple of Melqart**, with the
twin pillars, the perpetual flame, and the god's tomb, reproduced at the edge of the
known world the iconography of [[tyre|Tyre's]] mother-shrine — so that the **"Pillars of
Hercules"** (the straits of Gibraltar) were at once a geographic strait and a cosmic
boundary between the known sea and the ocean beyond. From Gades the Phoenicians
controlled the Spanish silver trade and probed the Atlantic for tin (Himilco's voyage to
the "tin islands," perhaps Brittany or Cornwall).

## Tartessos — the native silver kingdom

**Tartessos** was the wealthy native kingdom of the lower Guadalquivir and Huelva region,
ruled (per Greek tradition) by the long-lived **Arganthonios**, whose silver so impressed
the Samian explorer **Colaeus** (c. 630 BCE). Culican shows it was **not a Phoenician
foundation but a local culture** — built on a pre-Phoenician base (the "pattern-burnished"
pottery and the Cáceres warrior **stelae**, depicting eastern-style fibulae, mirrors, and
shields) that was **already mining silver before the Phoenicians arrived**. Phoenician
(and some Ionian) contact then transformed it into the orientalizing "**Tartessian**"
culture — red-slip and polychrome pottery, gold and ivory work, the rich tombs of **La
Joya (Huelva)** — a "very expansive culture" centred on the Guadalquivir valley and Jaén.

## The biblical "Tarshish"

Culican discusses the much-debated equation of Tartessos with the biblical **Tarshish**
(and with Tarsus). He favours reading *Tarshish* as deriving from a **function** —
"refinery/smelting place" — a name that **migrated westward** with Phoenician trade: at
one stage it may have meant ports near Cilicia, but by the time of Isaiah and Ezekiel
"something further west is implied," and "ships of Tarshish" meant simply "refinery
ships." An Esarhaddon text naming "Yaman and Tarsisi" among the kings "of the middle of
the sea" points beyond Cilicia to the far west.

## The Málaga colonies and the mines

Between Gades and the open Mediterranean, the **Málaga coast** held the earliest firmly
dated Phoenician settlements in Spain — **Toscanos, Morro de Mezquitilla, Chorreras,
Trayamar, Almuñécar** (some founded before 700 BCE) — storehouse-and-export "factory"
towns. At **Huelva** (Cabezo de San Pedro, Cerro Salomón) the evidence shows a **native
populace working silver for over a century before the Phoenicians arrived**, with
Phoenician management appearing in the seventh–sixth-century levels.

## Why it mattered

- **The metals engine of colonization** — Spanish **silver** (and Atlantic **tin**) was
  the prize that justified the whole [[phoenician-colonization|westward enterprise]] and
  filled the tombs of Carthage, Motya, and Tharros with silver.
- **The edge of the world** — Gades and the Pillars of Hercules marked the limit of the
  Phoenician (and the classical) known world, sanctified by the Melqart cult.
- **The orientalizing of Iberia** — Phoenician contact reshaped native Tartessian culture
  and carried eastern art and the alphabet to the far west.
