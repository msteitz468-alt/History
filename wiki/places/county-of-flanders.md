# The County of Flanders

**Flanders** was, "after northern Italy, the earliest and most densely urbanised area of
medieval Europe" — a great cloth-manufacturing principality whose towns made it both rich
and ungovernable. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Wim Blockmans, Ch. 9b) treats it as the
classic case of a thirteenth-century **urban society** caught between an overbearing French
crown, a weak comital dynasty, and explosive social conflict — culminating in the
[[battle-of-courtrai-1302|battle of Courtrai]].

## A Weak Dynasty Under Pressure

Though a fief of the king of France (with eastern parts held of the Empire), Flanders had
under Count Philip of Alsace (d. 1191) been "one of the mightiest and most progressive
principalities of western Europe." But across the thirteenth century the **crown constantly
undermined the counts' power**: the loss of **Artois** (Arras, Hesdin) by 1212, then
Saint-Omer and Aire, and (a century later) Lille, Douai and Orchies. The comital dynasty was
itself "a weakening factor" — marked by the absence of crusading counts (Baldwin IX became the
first **Latin emperor of Constantinople**, 1204, and died in Bulgar captivity), long stretches
of **female government** (Joan and Margaret of Constantinople, 1205–1278), captivity, and the
century-long **Dampierre–Avesnes feud** over the succession, which King **[[philip-iv-capetian|Philip
IV]]** "cynically played off" to tighten his grasp.

## The Cloth Economy

Flanders' density rested on its **textile industry**: up to 60% of workers in cities like
Douai, Ypres and Ghent lived off cloth, exported across Europe since the eleventh century. A
far-reaching division of labour produced everything from the costly coloured **scarlets**
(woven of the finest wools, often English) to cheap whites. Flemish, Artesian, Norman and
Champenois cities organised as the **"Hansa of the seventeen towns,"** concentrating their
trade on the **[[commercial-revolution|Champagne fairs]]**. By the late thirteenth century,
rival producers (Brabant, Tuscany, Languedoc) buying English wool directly, plus mechanisation
(spinning wheel, fulling mill) and political disputes, eroded the Flemish price advantage.

## Towns, Patricians and Revolt

The great cities — **Ghent** (perhaps the largest town in northern Europe after Paris),
**Bruges**, **Ypres**, **Lille**, **Douai** — won wide autonomy from the weak counts, but
their governments became "strongly exclusive and plutocratic," dominated by a closed
**patriciate** (in Ghent, thirty-nine *schepenen* rotating power for life). Social tension
between patricians and the exploited **textile workers** mounted through the century (strikes
at Douai 1245, Ghent 1252; revolts of 1274–1280). When the antagonism between Philip IV and
**Count Guy de Dampierre** climaxed, the patricians sided with the **king** and the count
with the **craftsmen**, producing the explosion of 1302: the **Bruges Matins** (a massacre of
French occupants, May 1302) and then **[[battle-of-courtrai-1302|Courtrai]]** (July 1302).

- "The primary factors underlying the decline of Bruges and Ghent were natural, epidemic, political, and economic... England could withhold the supplies at will" (ll. 275-301). taxonomy: caused_by: external dependency for [[county-of-flanders]].
- Plague "arrived in Flanders from Asia Minor by way of Genoa" (ll. 236-237); population of Bruges "declined precipitously at mid-century" (ll. 259-260). taxonomy: contributed_to: contraction of [[county-of-flanders]].

The Courtrai victory had two lasting effects: it **secured the continuity of Flanders as a
largely independent fief** under the French crown (it could be occupied but not absorbed, as
Champagne had been — its high urbanisation the "main explanatory factor"), and it ended the
predominance of the patricians, giving Flanders "by 1302/1304 arguably the most open and
broadly based political system of Europe." Flanders is the volume's prime example of how
**urbanisation** could blunt royal state-building and generate a genuinely popular politics.

## The Fourteenth-Century Low Countries and the Coming of Burgundy (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Walter Prevenier, Ch. 14c) sets Flanders within the **Low
Countries** — "a group of provinces which... formed a strong socio-economic and cultural unity"
(Brabant, Holland, Hainault, Liège, Flanders) but no political unity, divided in allegiance between
the German emperor and the French king. The most urbanised region of northern Europe, its cities saw
**power broadened** from the closed patriciate to "a broad range of social groups," and its
provincial **estates** wrote their right to consent to taxation into constitutions — a precocious
representative politics. Flanders' great cloth towns (Ghent, Bruges, Ypres) remained turbulent: the
revolt of **Jacob van Artevelde** (1338–1345) aligned Flanders with [[edward-iii-of-england|Edward
III]] for its English wool, and the **Ghent revolt under Philip van Artevelde** ended in defeat at
**Roosebeke (1382)**. The decisive long-term development was dynastic: through the marriage of
**Margaret of Flanders to Philip the Bold of Burgundy (1369)**, Flanders and much of the Low
Countries passed to the **[[valois-dynasty|Valois dukes of Burgundy]]**, who would assemble them into
the great late-medieval Burgundian state straddling the Franco-Imperial frontier.
