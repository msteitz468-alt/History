# Amarna (Akhetaten)

The purpose-built capital of [[akhenaten|Akhenaten]], **Akhetaten** ("the Horizon
of the Aten"), founded on virgin ground midway between Memphis and Thebes and
occupied for barely a generation. Because it was **built, inhabited, and abandoned
within a single reign**, then never reoccupied, it is the best-preserved city plan
of pharaonic Egypt and the type-site of the Late Bronze [[amarna-letters|Amarna
age]]. Modern **Tell el-Amarna**. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II,
Part 2]] (Ch. XIX, Aldred).

## Foundation

In his 4th–6th regnal years Akhenaten chose "a natural amphitheatre about eight
miles in diameter" on the Nile's east bank, declared (on the great **boundary
stelae** cut into the surrounding cliffs) that the Aten himself had revealed it as
virgin ground "which belonged to no god, goddess, prince, princess," and swore never
to extend the city beyond its bounds. The choice was theological, not economic — the
strip of cultivation was so thin that even today the local villages are "among the
poorest in Egypt," and the city had to be fed from the opposite bank and the rest of
Egypt.

## The city

A long ribbon along the river, hastily built and still growing when abandoned:
- the **Central City** — the **Great Palace** (over 750 m long), the **Great
  Temple** ("House of the Aten") and the smaller "Mansion of the Aten," both
  elaborations of the open-air Heliopolitan sun-temple, focused on altars before a
  stela rather than a cult-image;
- the **"Bureau of the Correspondence of Pharaoh,"** the office where the
  **[[amarna-letters|Amarna letters]]** were found;
- official quarters, the spacious estates of the wealthy fronting the main avenues,
  and the "hovels of the poor... squeezed in wherever there was space," with no
  drainage;
- a **South City** with the *Maru-Aten* pleasure-palace and "sunshade temples";
- a **workmen's village** whose finds (household gods Bes, Taweret, Isis, even Amun)
  show how shallowly [[atenism|Atenism]] penetrated ordinary belief.
Stone was reserved for temples and palaces; all domestic building was mud-brick,
distinctively decorated with inlays of coloured stone, glass and faience.

## Abandonment

When [[tutankhamun|Tutankhamun]] (then Tutankhaten) moved the court back to
**Memphis** early in his reign, the artificial city — sustainable only as a
royal residence — could no longer support itself and "dwindled to the status of a
mere village," deserted by Ramesside times in favour of Hermopolis across the river.
The royal burials were transferred to Thebes (the disputed **Tomb 55** in the Valley
of the Kings may have received Tiy, Akhenaten and Smenkhkare); the cumbersome
cuneiform tablets and the sculptors' studio models were simply left behind — which
is why they survive.

## Significance

Amarna is doubly important: as the physical record of the [[atenism|Aten cult]] and
[[akhenaten|Akhenaten's]] revolution, and as the **find-spot of the Amarna
letters**, the diplomatic archive that opened the Late Bronze "great-powers system"
to modern history. Its single-period occupation makes it an unrivalled archaeological
window on a 14th-century Egyptian city.
