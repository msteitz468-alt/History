# Soconusco

## Geographic description

The **Soconusco** is the Pacific coastal plain and piedmont of Chiapas and Guatemala—rich forests, swamps, rivers, and estuaries southwest of the core Maya area. Natives were culturally akin to and interacted with the Maya but generally spoke **Mixe-Zoquean** languages. The region was a setting for early ranked societies and for **cacao** production in late prehistoric and colonial times (Barbara Voorhies, EW2001).

**Mazatan** lies in the heart of the Chiapas coastal plain and is key for understanding origins and evolution of early village life in Mesoamerica (Michael Blake, EW2001).

## Historical significance

### Late Archaic: Chantuto (c. 4000–1600 BCE)

Earliest known occupation is the **Chantuto** complex—complex hunter-gatherers with six high-profile shell mounds (Islona Chantuto, Campon, Tlacuachero, Zapotillo, El Chorro, Cerro de las Conchas) and inland Vuelta Limón; shellfish, fish, and shrimp sun-dried for inland consumers (Voorhies, EW2001).

### Early Formative: Mokaya Tradition (c. 1600–800 B.C.)

Sedentary **Mokaya**—the first pottery producers—replaced Chantuto; identified by Coe at La Victoria and by Coe and Flannery at Salinas La Blanca. Mokaya combined fish/game with early domesticates (corn, beans); early pottery jars may mark elite-hosted feasts. Isotopic C/N analysis indicates maize was **not** a large dietary share until after ~900 B.C. (Blake).

**Barra phase** c. 1550–1400 B.C.: permanent Mazatan settlement begins (15 sites; Altamira, San Carlos, Paso de la Amada) with elegant pottery. **Locona** 1400–1250 B.C.: >30 sites and two- or three-tier hierarchy (centers 20–75 ha vs 1–5 ha hamlets)—simple chiefdoms. 1995 excavations at **Paso de la Amada** found Mesoamerica’s **earliest ball court**, built 1400–1300 B.C. Leaders built large residences and possibly sponsored ritual ball games.

Mazatan lost Early Formative primacy by **Cuadros** (1000–900 B.C.); during **Conchas** (850–600 B.C.) **La Blanca** (Guatemala) grew huge as many Mazatan villages were abandoned. Thereafter Mazatan may have been a province of larger centers such as **Izapa** and never recovered its Early Formative lead (Blake).

### Middle–Late Formative centers

Middle Formative Pacific coastal peoples contacted Gulf Coast Olmec society across the Isthmus of Tehuantepec (Olmec-style figurines, head deformation, pottery motifs, portable stone sculpture, bas-relief boulders); direction of influence is debated. Major centers:

| Center | Notes |
|---|---|
| Tzutzuculi | ≥35 ha, ≥25 platform mounds, Olmec motifs |
| La Blanca | ~100 ha, ≥43 residential platforms; largest mound once 25 m high |
| Abaj Takalik | sculpture focus |
| **Izapa** | Late Formative dominant center (~80 large platforms, >250 carved monuments with Olmec and Maya resemblances) |
| Others | La Perseverancia, Tonalá/Iglesia Vieja, Horcones, Sin Cabezas (327 ha, ~90 platforms), El Bálsamo, Monte Alto (pot-belly sculptures) |

**El Bálsamo** (Escuintla, Guatemala): large Pacific Lowlands center c. 500–200 B.C.; Late Formative platform mounds up to 10 m; highland obsidian; possible exports cotton, cacao, feathers, jaguar pelts (Stark).

### Classic: Cotzumalhuapa

**Cotzumalhuapa** culture on the Pacific piedmont of Guatemala (Escuintla) is known mainly for Mexican-influenced Middle–Late Classic sculpture; political core of Bilbao, El Baúl, and El Castillo near Santa Lucía Cotzumalguapa at ~400 m altitude, ~50 km inland—a rich cacao zone. Hierarchical system with district capitals at Palo Gordo and Los Cerritos Norte; influence band ~150 km along piedmont and southern highlands, as far as Tonalá, Chiapas, and Cara Sucia, El Salvador; >140 sculptures (Bove; Chinchilla Mazariegos, EW2001).

### Postclassic, Aztec, and colonial cacao

Soconusco was the rich cacao terminus of the Oaxaca Valley–Tehuantepec trade route contested by Mixtec, Aztec, and Zapotec; Huave may have aided Aztec merchants along waterways. High-fired **Plumbate** ceramics with glazelike finish were made on this Pacific coast. The region was controlled by the Quiché of highland Guatemala, then taken by the expanding Aztec Empire for feathers, skins, amber, and especially cacao—used as Aztec small-purchase money via imperial tribute and independent merchant trade (Zeitlin; Michael E. Smith, EW2001).

Late Postclassic sites are less abundant than ethnohistory implies for heavy population. **Acapetahua** was conquered by the Aztecs when they took Soconusco (perhaps **1486 CE**)—head town of a small independent polity in a series along the coast; after conquest, former rulers paid tribute and entered Aztec bureaucracy until Spanish arrival. Colonial epidemics cut Soconusco population to maybe **5–10%** of contact size by 1570; SE cacao growers were taxed more heavily than under the Aztecs but retained orchards (Voorhies).
