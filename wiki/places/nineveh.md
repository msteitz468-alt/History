# Nineveh and the Neo-Assyrian Capitals

## Overview

This page covers the **Neo-Assyrian capital cities** (Otto, Ch. 23 of
[[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]]), the volume's Near Eastern case of the
**early imperial city** (Part VI). The Assyrian Empire (~911–612 BCE) dominated
the Near East from a succession of heartland capitals: the eternal religious
center **Aššur**, then the founded military capitals **Kalhu/Nimrud**
(Ashurnasirpal II), **Dur-Sharrukin/Khorsabad** (Sargon II), and finally
**Nineveh** (Sennacherib) — the largest, sacked by Medes and Babylonians in
612 BCE.

## Aššur, the eternal capital

Aššur — the name of the god, the city, *and* the land — was the ideological core:
the king was the earthly representative of the god Aššur, and his legitimacy was
bound to the Temple of Aššur, **fed daily by a different province** so that "the
whole empire literally fed the god." Even after kings moved their administration
elsewhere, Aššur kept the priesthood, the royal tombs ("Palace of the Fathers"),
and the religious center. Notably, at Aššur palaces and temples were **not**
segregated from residential quarters — contradicting the cliché of rigid
royal/urban separation — which may be exactly why kings sought new capitals.

## The founded imperial capitals

To house an empire's swollen bureaucracy and army, kings built vast new
capitals on virgin or enlarged sites: Kar-Tukulti-Ninurta (a failed attempt),
**Kalhu** (360 ha, the military headquarters), **Khorsabad**, and **Nineveh**.
These were deliberate [[founded-cities|creations]] with segregated royal citadels.

## Empire, deportation, and dependence

Neo-Assyrian cities grew chiefly through **mass deportation** — up to ~4 million
people deported between ~850 and 614 BCE — making the capitals **cosmopolitan,
multiethnic** places (Egyptians, Medes, Luwians, Arameans as attested house-
owners). But the heartland capitals sat so close together that their hinterlands
could not feed them: they depended on **tribute, taxes, and long-distance
transport** from the provinces. This structural dependence — concentration of
people and power outrunning the local agrarian base — is the signature
vulnerability of the [[imperial-city]], and the empire collapsed quickly when the
provinces' coerced loyalty failed (614–612 BCE).

## Why this place mattered

The Assyrian capitals show the imperial city as **headquarters and population-
attractor** — sustained by force, tribute, and forced migration, monumentalizing
royal power while remaining hostage to the empire that fed it.
