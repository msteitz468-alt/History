## Overview

Eshnunna (modern **Tell Asmar**) was an Early and Middle Bronze Age city, and in the early
second millennium an independent kingdom, in the **Diyala river basin** of central
Mesopotamia, about 81 km northeast of Baghdad. Excavated in the 1930s by the Oriental
Institute of Chicago under **Henri Frankfort**, the site is important both as a major regional
power and as a chronological yardstick: a sequence of superimposed temples (possibly to the
god **Abu**) provided the basis for dividing the Early Dynastic period into the three phases
**ED I, II, and III** — though Eshnunna's "provincial" character has made scholars cautious
about applying these divisions to the Sumerian core further south. The eleven gypsum
**Square Temple statues** (ED II), among the best-known works of Early Dynastic art, are
emblematic of this debated provincialism (Bryce 2009: 235–6).

## History

Founded in late M4 / early M3, Eshnunna flourished through the [[akkadian-empire|Akkadian
empire]] and reached its peak under the [[third-dynasty-of-ur|Ur III]] kings (C21) and the
following Isin–Larsa period. Under Ur III it was administered by an *ensi* (local governor);
its ruler **Ituriya** built the only known Mesopotamian temple **dedicated to a living king**
(his deified Ur III overlord **Shu-Sin**), adjacent to which the "Palace of the Rulers"
served as an administrative centre yielding large numbers of texts.

After the fall of Ur III, Eshnunna became an independent kingdom under local rulers of the
land of **Warum**. In the 19th century its king **Ipiq-Adad II** (1862–1818) extended control
over the Diyala valley; his son **Dadusha** (d. 1780) conquered (reconquered) the middle-
Euphrates land of **Suhum**, bringing Eshnunna into direct collision with the Assyrian
viceregal kingdom of [[mari|Mari]]. Hostilities with [[shamshi-adad-i|Shamshi-Adad I]]'s
[[amorites|Amorite]] "kingdom of Upper Mesopotamia" alternated with alliance: Dadusha made
peace in 1781 and supplied troops for Assyrian campaigns. After Shamshi-Adad's death in 1775,
Eshnunna under **Ibal-pi-El II** seized the initiative, occupying Shubat-Enlil and the former
Assyrian cities of [[ashur|Ashur]] and Ekallatum.

Eshnunna's power was broken from the east and west in quick succession: in **1765 it fell to
the [[elam|Elamites]]**, who ended Ibal-pi-El's reign, and in **1762 its successor Silli-Sin
was defeated by [[hammurabi|Hammurabi]] of Babylon**. A final bid for independence — a revolt
against Hammurabi's son **Samsu-iluna** in his tenth year (c. 1738) — was crushed, and the
rebel king Iluni killed (*RIME 4: 387). Eshnunna thereafter drops from the historical record,
surfacing once more only when [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus II]], after conquering Babylonia in 539,
claimed to have restored the gods to their long-abandoned shrines "on the other side of the
Tigris," including the land of Eshnunna.

Eshnunna is also remembered for the **Laws of Eshnunna**, an Akkadian law collection
predating Hammurabi's, a key witness to early Mesopotamian legal tradition.
