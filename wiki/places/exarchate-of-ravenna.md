# The Exarchate of Ravenna (Byzantine Italy)

## Overview

The **Exarchate of Ravenna** was the administrative core of **Byzantine Italy** — the territory
governed from Ravenna by the imperial **exarch** after Justinian's reconquest, comprising the city
of Ravenna, the Pentapolis, the maritime lagoons (Venetiae), and (loosely) the duchies of Rome,
Naples, and Calabria. In [[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Brown, Ch. 13) its long decline frames
the transformation of Italy into a patchwork of Frankish, papal, Lombard, and Byzantine zones.

## Decline and Fall

By the eighth century Byzantine authority in Italy was eroding under pressure from the
[[lombards|Lombard kingdom]] and strained by the **iconoclast controversy**: the imperial iconoclast
decrees were widely resented in Italy (though the exarch made little effort to enforce them), and
disputes over taxation and religion alienated the local population and the papacy. In **751** the
Lombard king Aistulf captured **Ravenna**, ending the Exarchate. Its fall was decisive: it severed
the papacy from its imperial protector and drove [[the-papacy-eighth-ninth-centuries|the popes]] into
the arms of the Franks, leading directly to [[pippin-iii|Pippin's]] intervention and the
[[carolingian-conquest-of-lombard-italy-774|Carolingian conquest]].

## The Survival of Byzantine Italy

Byzantine power did not vanish from Italy: it persisted in the **south** (Calabria, Apulia, and after
the 870s a revived presence around Bari) and at sea. Crucially, the lagoon settlements of **Venetiae**
evolved out of the old Byzantine province into the autonomous **[[republic-of-venice|duchy of Venice]]**,
which exploited its position between the eastern and western empires (recognised in the Frankish-Byzantine
peace of 812) to build a commercial future.

## Significance

The end of the Exarchate (751) is one of the hinges of early medieval Italian history: it removed the
imperial framework from central Italy, made possible the temporal **papal state**, and opened the
peninsula to Frankish intervention.

## Historiography

NCMH II (Brown, Ch. 13) reconstructs Byzantine Italy from the *Liber Pontificalis*, the Ravenna papyri and
local sources, and archaeology, tracing the slow "provincialisation" of a Byzantine elite into an
Italian-Lombard one. The relative weight of iconoclasm, taxation, and Lombard pressure in alienating
Italy from Byzantium is debated.
