# Hispania (Roman Spain)

The Iberian peninsula as the arena of one of Rome's longest and most revealing
conquests (218–133 BCE) — and, after silver, one of its most profitable provinces.
Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 5 (Harris).

## Geography and peoples

Harris cautions against the stereotype of a "barbarian Spain conquered by civilized
Romans": pre-Roman Iberia resembled **Oscan Italy in the Samnite period** —
Punic/Greek coastal cities (Gades, Emporion), and inland the **Celtiberians** and
**Lusitanians** and some twenty other peoples in hill-top *poblados*, with their own
alphabets, coinages, and notably skilled metallurgy (Rome adopted the Spanish
sword). Few large cities existed inland; **Numantia** was the most impressive.

## From the Hannibalic War to two provinces

Spain entered Roman history as a theatre of the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second
Punic War]] — the Barcid empire built from [[new-carthage|New Carthage]], the
Scipios' seven-year campaign, and [[scipio-africanus|Scipio's]] victory at **Ilipa**
(206). Rome **annexed** the territory from 206/205 (not 197), sending a regular
series of governors, and organised two provinces — **Hispania Citerior** (Nearer)
and **Ulterior** (Further) — formally divided by 197, each under a praetor after the
praetorships were raised from four to six.

## The conquest and its character

The conquest proceeded relentlessly but at a moderate pace, the **main pressure
coming from the Roman side** (Cato's command 195–194; the Celtiberian and Lusitanian
wars from 193; Gracchus' settlement of Celtiberia 178/7). It resumed savagely in
153–133 — the [[numantine-war-153-133bce|Celtiberian wars]] and [[viriathus|the war
of Viriathus]] — ending with **Numantia (133)**. Roman conduct featured pretextual
*casus belli*, governors' *avaritia* (provoking the *lex Calpurnia* repetundae court
of 149), and massacres (Lucullus at Cauca, Galba in Lusitania).

## Why it mattered

**Silver.** The mines near New Carthage and in the Sierra Morena were, in Gibbon's
phrase, "the Peru and Mexico of the old world": Polybius reports 40,000 slaves
working the New Carthage mines, yielding the Roman state 25,000 drachmas a day.
Revenue came also from the *stipendium* (a fixed cash tax, paid via the Iberian
"denarii" minted on the Roman standard from c. 197) and a grain levy. Unlike
Cisalpine Gaul, Rome did **not** seize Spanish farmland wholesale; immigration
concentrated in the mining districts and ports (with Latin colonies at Carteia 171,
Corduba, Valentia 138). Romanization in the second century was thin outside the
coast and mines.
