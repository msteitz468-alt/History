# Aquitaine

**The southwestern region of [[francia|Francia]]**, between the Loire and the Pyrenees, which
preserved more of late-Roman civilisation than anywhere else in the Frankish world — and which
slid into near-independence before being violently reabsorbed by the early Carolingians.

## Roman continuity

Aquitaine "had the greatest continuity of Roman culture in Francia." Its great *fundus* estates —
"some almost the size of a modern French department" — worked by slaves and *coloni*, "continued
without major interruption" from the fifth century. After [[battle-of-vouille-507|Vouillé (507)]]
ended [[visigoths|Visigothic]] rule, vulgar Roman law, Latin letters, the road network, towns, and
fiscal structures all survived into the Frankish period (see [[roman-gaul]]). It was also richly
endowed — "salt, wood, furs, marble, lead, iron, and silver mines" — so that every royal division
gave each king a share.

## From border kingdom to independent duchy

[[chlothar-ii|Chlothar II]] set his son **Charibert** at the head of a border kingdom in Aquitaine
against the Gascons (Basques). After Charibert's death (632) renewed Basque pressure drew
[[dagobert-i|Dagobert I]]'s armies south; a Burgundian contingent under Duke Arnebert was ambushed and
destroyed in the valley of the Soule — a defeat Geary suggests may lie behind the later legend of
**Roncevaux**. The mayor Ebroin created a border principality (c. 650) under the patrician **Felix of
Toulouse**, "over all the cities as far as the Pyrenees."

During the Pippinid succession crisis after 714, **Duke Eudo** ("prince of the Aquitainians") built an
effectively independent principality; in 721 he checked a Muslim army besieging Toulouse, winning
recognition from Pope Gregory II.

## Carolingian conquest

[[charles-martel|Charles Martel]] invaded in 731; the next year the Spanish governor 'Abd al-Raḥmān
destroyed Eudo's army, forcing Eudo to seek Martel's help. The resulting Frankish victory between
Poitiers and Tours checked the Muslim advance and "meant the beginning of the end of Aquitainian
independence." Martel's and [[pippin-iii|Pippin III]]'s pacification wars devastated Aquitaine: it
"ceased to be a center of learning," the tradition of literate laymen died out, and writing became a
clerical monopoly.

## Why it mattered

Aquitaine is Geary's prime example both of deep Roman continuity in the Merovingian world and of the
**violence of the Carolingian "reunification,"** which extinguished one of late antiquity's last
literate lay cultures.
