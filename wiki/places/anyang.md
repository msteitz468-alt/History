# Anyang (Yinxu — Shang Capital)

## Overview

**Anyang** in north China was the last capital of the **Shang dynasty** and the
site of the earliest substantial corpus of Chinese writing — the **oracle-bone
inscriptions**. It is the case study of [[yoffee-cwh-v3-2015|CWH Vol. III]] Ch. 7
(Wang Haicheng), "Writing and the city in early China." Anyang comprised two
successive cities astride the Huan River: the walled **Huanbei** city (~1350–1250
BCE) and, after Huanbei burned, **Yinxu** ("the ruins of Yin," ~1250–1050 BCE) at
Xiaotun. Yoffee notes the largest Chinese cities were the largest of all ancient
cities (Anyang ~30 km²).

## Form

Yinxu broke with the Neolithic-to-Bronze tradition of orthogonal walled cities:
it had **no city walls**, bounded only by the river — a deliberate royal decision
(attributed to King **Wu Ding**). The city was an **agglomeration of lineage
settlements** and cemeteries growing outward from the royal precinct (~70 ha) in
the northeast. Each lineage (with its own name/emblem, cast on ritual bronzes)
clustered courtyard compounds around a lineage head's compound — a form
comparable to the patio-group organization seen among the [[tikal|Maya]].
Population estimates range ~15,000–120,000. Intersecting thoroughfares with clear
**wheel ruts**, waterways, bronze foundries, and pottery workshops are attested.

## Writing as administrative control

Wang's central argument parallels Nissen on [[uruk|Uruk]]: although the surviving
inscriptions are **divination records** (oracle bones — questions to royal
ancestors about the coming week, sacrifices, harvests, war), writing was **not**
invented to communicate with spirits. Most divination bones are *uninscribed*;
inscriptions were added afterward as display. The real motive was
**administrative**: inventory and accountability. Even the ritual texts are full
of book-keeping — armies of 5,000 or 13,000 men, 1,570 head-trophies, 1,000
shells, field surveys, granary inspections, ration tools (caches of hundreds of
stone sickles for distribution). Everyday administration was written on perishable
**bamboo and wood slips**, which do not survive before ~5th century BCE. See
[[writing-and-record-keeping]].

## Why this place mattered

Anyang shows the same urbanization→writing linkage as Uruk, independently: the
Shang state's scale (agriculture, bronze industry, colonial expansion eastward,
mass human sacrifice) required record-keeping, and Chinese writing's "uninterrupted
transmission" from here underlies East Asian literacy. The Erlitou→Erligang
(Zhengzhou, ~400 ha)→Huanbei→Yinxu sequence places Anyang within China's
[[early-bronze-age|Bronze Age]] urban tradition.

## Additional claims from Maisels (1999) Range 05 (China Ch. 5)

**Capital district extent and structure (exclusive to this source range):**
"Anyang is not a single site but the capital district extending over about 24 square kilometres in which seventeen sites are known so far." Core "inner capital" ~elliptical <200 km long axis; "outer domain" buffer; "lordships" beyond. Ruling centre at Xiaotun: Section A (ceremonial altar + 15 parallel houses), Section B (21 houses in 3 rows, royal temple sector, horse-chariot burials), Section C (17 foundations). Servitors in ~4m semi-subterranean dwellings with storage; bronze foundries, bone/stone/pottery workshops. "Experiment has conclusively demonstrated that bronze casting was by the piece-mould method... and not by cire perdue". (lines ~1990-2040)

**Royal cemetery at Xibeigang:**
"North of the river at Xibeigang is the royal cemetery of eleven large graves, accompanied by over 1,000 small graves". "The eleven large tombs are grouped into a western cluster of seven and an eastern cluster of four, happily coinciding in number with the eleven kings from P’an Geng to Ti Yi who ruled from An-Yang". Tombs with long N-S approach ramps + E-W; "at least 7,000 working days would be required just to excavate the pits". Abundant goods + sacrificial victims (heads often separated). Fu Hao tomb (nearby) inventory detailed: wooden chamber/coffin, 16 victims, 6 dogs, ~7000 cowries, 200+ bronze ritual vessels, 130+ bronze weapons, 590+ jade objects, etc. (lines ~2045-2060, ~1335-1365)

**Continuity with Erlitou and pre-urban Longshan:**
Anyang part of sequence from Erlitou (possible Xia, ~1.5 x 2.5 km site, two large palace compounds with stamped-earth platforms 36x25m hall foundations, elite tombs with "signs of ritual slaughter", early bronzes but no scapular inscriptions). Longshan towns (e.g. Chengziyai max ~20 ha) were "still only towns rather than cities". Anyang "cities" dispersed clusters. (lines ~1820-1840, ~1435-1440, ~2385-2395)

**Shamanistic political instruments:**
"In this sense, the first Chinese civilizations were shamanistic; their art was in fact shamanistic paraphernalia, which were not only the trappings but also the very instruments of political power." Oracle bone example from Wen Ding (134 characters). (lines ~2065-2070)
