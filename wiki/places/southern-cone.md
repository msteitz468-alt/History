# Southern Cone (Prehistoric)

## Overview

The **Southern Cone** — southern Chile and Argentina, the **Pampas**, **Patagonia**,
**Tierra del Fuego**, and the **Gran Chaco** — is treated in **Mario A. Rivera's** chapter on
the "Prehistory of the Southern Cone" in
[[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Volume III]]. It encompasses some of the
**earliest securely dated human presence** in the Americas (the far-southern sites tied to the
Monte Verde horizon debate), and a mosaic of adaptations: maritime foragers of the Fuegian
channels, equestrian and pedestrian hunters of the Pampas and Patagonian plains, Chaco
foragers and horticulturalists, and the agro-pastoral societies of the **south-central
Andes** (Atacama, northwest Argentina) linked northward to [[tiwanaku|Tiwanaku]].

Unlike the central Andes, most of the Southern Cone never developed states; its peoples — above
all the [[mapuche|Mapuche (Araucanians)]] — are notable in the conquest chapters for their
**long resistance** to both Inka and Spanish expansion.

## Significance

The Southern Cone anchors the volume's treatment of the **non-state, non-agricultural** end of
the South American spectrum and bears directly on the **peopling-of-the-Americas** chronology,
since its southernmost early sites constrain how fast and how early humans reached the
continent's tip.
