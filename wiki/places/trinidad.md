## Geographic Description

Trinidad is the sixth-largest of the Caribbean Islands at 4,828 km², with a coastline of approximately 362 km. Together with Tobago (300 km²), it forms the Republic of Trinidad and Tobago. Unlike the volcanic island arc of the Lesser Antilles, Trinidad is geologically an extension of the South American mainland. Its formation reflects the "Andean folding and faulting" that creates the rugged northern coast (a Miocene-age formation), while most of the relief consists of low hills with poorly drained lowlands.

Because of its continental geological origin, Trinidad has a significantly more continental flora and fauna than other Caribbean islands. Its Pleistocene land bridge to the South American mainland (submerged by rising sea levels around 7000 BCE) facilitated the movement of numerous animal species typically absent from the Antilles. This gives the island's ecological history a distinctively transitional character between mainland and insular environments.

Trinidad is situated approximately 10 kilometers off the northeastern coast of Venezuela. The Gulf of Paría separates it from the South American mainland. Tobago (300 km²) and Barbados (440 km²) share the same Andean geological structures as Trinidad, but being farther from the mainland, they have a more insular flora and fauna and are classified with the Lesser Antilles.

## Settlement History

Trinidad has been characterized as the "gateway" to the Caribbean (Boomert 2013; Rouse 1992), and the island's Archaic Age occupation is the oldest in the Caribbean region.

### The Banwari Trace Site (c. 5000 BCE)

The **Banwari Trace site** is one of the two oldest Archaic Age sites excavated in the Caribbean Islands (alongside the St. John site on Trinidad), dated to approximately 5000 BCE. Located on a hill above the wetlands between the Oropuche and Coora rivers in southwestern Trinidad, the site is approximately 10 kilometers from the coast. It consists of a shell midden about 30 meters in diameter and over 2 meters tall.

Excavations were directed by Peter O'Brian Harris in the late 1960s and early 1970s. UNESCO has recognized the site's significance and it has been nominated as a World Heritage Site. Unfortunately, in the intervening decades, most of the site was removed for use as construction fill. Harris and Emily Lundberg conducted additional excavations in 2005 using new analytical methods.

The tool assemblage is distinctive. Ground-stone tools — mostly shaped by use from originally water-rounded flat stones — include large and small pestles, mortars (metates), manos, anvils, and grooved axes. For the first time in the archaeological record, some tools were purposely produced by grinding prior to use (rather than merely worn through use). Bone tools include sharpened tips (2–75 cm in length) for use as spear points, needles, and gorgets for fishing. Small flakes and fragments of bipolar-flaked chert from local sources are also present.

Two small potsherds were recovered — one on the surface and one in the upper levels of the 2005 excavations — foreshadowing the "Pre-Arawak Pottery Horizon" documented more extensively in the Greater Antilles.

The site shows clear changes in subsistence practices through time: the earliest strata emphasize terrestrial animals and freshwater shellfish; later strata shift toward marine resources. This shift partly reflects the opening of the Gulf of Paría as Trinidad separated from the mainland during the Holocene.

Starch-grain analysis at the **St. John site** on Trinidad revealed evidence for maize as early as 7,700 BP — among the earliest evidence for cultivated plants in the Caribbean region.

Rouse (1992) named the Archaic Age series for the **Ortoire sites** (three shell middens near the village of Ortoire in southeastern Trinidad), rather than for Banwari Trace, although the Ortoire sites date to between 1000 and 700 BCE — several millennia younger. This naming choice has caused confusion in the literature. Veloz Maggiolo, by contrast, named the complex "Banwaroides" for the most characteristic site.

### Trinidad's Role as Gateway

The conventional model — proposed by Rouse (1992) and others — held that Ortoiroid peoples from Trinidad migrated north through the Lesser Antilles, carrying ground-stone technology, eventually reaching Puerto Rico where they encountered and assimilated the Casimiroid flaked-stone tradition. Trinidad's land connection to the mainland until approximately 7000 BCE, and the early date of Banwari Trace, made it the obvious staging point.

This model has been substantially revised. The critical problem is the **absence of Archaic Age sites south of Guadeloupe** — the entire Windward Islands chain through which the migration was supposed to pass shows no evidence of Ortoiroid presence, despite systematic surveys. Callaghan (2010) concluded that neither navigational limitations nor volcanic post-depositional destruction can explain this gap. This points instead toward a direct crossing of the Caribbean Sea from South or Central America into the central Caribbean, consistent with Callaghan's (2001, 2013) voyaging simulations showing that such crossings are not only feasible but easier than navigating between Lesser Antilles islands.

The alternative interpretation is that ground-stone tools spread through the islands via exchange networks rather than population replacement — consistent with evidence that material goods from the Isthmo-Colombian region circulated throughout the Caribbean during the Archaic Age.

Regardless of the mechanism, Trinidad's role as the closest large Caribbean island to South America, with the earliest documented ground-stone assemblage in the region, makes it central to understanding Caribbean prehistory. Grenada, the southernmost island of the Lesser Antilles, was likely "visible" from Tobago — meaning cloud formations, horizon reflections, and bird migrations could indicate the presence of islands to the north, encouraging northward voyaging.

## Significance

Trinidad's significance in Caribbean prehistory is threefold. First, it provides the oldest well-documented Archaic Age site in the Caribbean region (Banwari Trace, c. 5000 BCE). Second, its land connection to South America until approximately 7000 BCE made it a natural migration corridor. Third, its continental flora and fauna profile, combined with the Archaic Age sequence, provides the clearest evidence of continuity between mainland and island material cultures in the southern Caribbean. The island is also a terminus point for Kalinago-affiliated communities who retained links to mainland South America into the historical period; self-identified Kalinago still live on Trinidad today.

## Historiography

Trinidad occupies an outsized position in Caribbean archaeological theory because Banwari Trace provided the cornerstone evidence for Rouse's Ortoiroid migration model. The site's dating and assemblage characteristics drove the "Trinidad as gateway" framework for decades. Recent revision of that framework — particularly the absence of Ortoiroid sites in the Windward Islands — has shifted emphasis toward exchange networks and direct trans-Caribbean crossings as mechanisms of cultural transmission, demoting Trinidad from the central migration hub of the standard model while preserving its significance as an early occupation site.

The partial destruction of Banwari Trace for construction fill before modern analytical methods could be applied is a significant archaeological loss. Harris's original excavations (1970s) remain the primary data source, supplemented by the 2005 work.
