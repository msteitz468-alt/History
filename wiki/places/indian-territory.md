# Indian Territory

**Indian Territory** (in the 19th-century U.S. usage) designated lands west of the Mississippi set aside for Native nations removed from the eastern United States, most famously the "Five Civilized Tribes" after the Indian Removal Act of 1830. In the framing of Blaakman et al., *The Early Imperial Republic* (2023), it was envisioned and administered as a key site for the expansion of slavery's empire on lands cleared of prior Native occupants.

## Geographic and Political Description

Originally a broad "Indian country" concept formalized in legislation and treaties, the Territory became a zone of "fragmented sovereignty" under U.S. superintendency. Native nations exercised internal self-government on paper while subject to U.S. trade regulation, boundary enforcement, and eventual further incursions. The region was not a blank space; it was already occupied by other Indigenous groups, creating layered conflicts.

## Significance in the Source

Nakia D. Parker (Ch. 6) analyzes Indian Territory as "a slave state in embryo": U.S. policy and white Southern interests projected the plantation system onto removed Native lands. Enslaved people of African descent were brought or purchased by some removed nations and by white interlopers. The jurisdictional and sovereignty ambiguities facilitated slavery's extension even after the 1808 Atlantic trade ban. The volume ties this to broader patterns of using prior imperial tools for racialized territorial control.

## Transition and Later History

Over time the Territory was repeatedly reduced by treaties, the Civil War, and eventual allotment and Oklahoma statehood (1907). It remains central to Native sovereignty claims, the history of slavery in Indian nations, and the historiography of U.S. empire and removal.

## Green, CHNPA I.1 ch. 8 — Removal destination and second dispossession

Michael D. Green’s century frame: eastern nations forced west of the Mississippi into Indian Territory rebuilt lives even as numbers continued to decline; by **1880** Civil War plus corporate economic penetration left them surrounded and in imminent danger of further dispossession and fragmentation—part of [[indian-removal-eastern-dispossession-1780-1880]].

**Name and 1834 bill.** In **1834** Congress debated organizing Indian Territory (Indian legislature, presidential governor, future statehood)—bill **did not pass** (removed nations relieved) but the **name stuck**. Army posts expanded but underinvested. Until **Kansas-Nebraska 1854**, the idea stretched Red River to Platte; 1820s southern portion assigned to southern nations; Kansas north became homes for northern removals, then was cut away—renewed pressure by late 1850s.

**Arrival paths (full fidelity).** [[choctaw-nation|Choctaws]] 1831–34 (~2,500 dead on 550-mile trek); [[chickasaw-nation|Chickasaws]] 1837 among Choctaws (separate 1855); [[creek-muscogee|Creeks]] 1836 forced winter march after Alabama terror; [[seminole|Seminoles]] ~4,000 via New Orleans/rivers after [[second-seminole-war-1835]] (Fort Gibson / Creek country; separate tract 1856); [[cherokee-nation|Cherokees]] Treaty Party/Old Settlers ~6,000 then National Party ~15,000 on [[trail-of-tears-1838]]; northern nations (Sauk/Fox, etc.) via Iowa/Kansas—Sauks to Indian Territory 1860 on Creek-sold lands.

**1840s–50s rebuilding.** Unexpectedly fertile land; large corn surpluses sold on American markets—**Creeks contributed 100,000 bushels** to Irish potato famine relief **1846**; large horse/cattle herds; slaveholding planters shipped cotton to Little Rock/New Orleans; Cherokee Joseph Vann purchased a steamboat; a Choctaw planter owned a river steamer fleet. Missionaries rebuilt schools/congregations. Fort Gibson mid-1830s conferences with Pawnees, Kiowas, Comanches, etc.; mid-1840s Creek then 1843 Cherokee councils firmed tolerant amicability with Plains groups for decades. Cherokee post-removal civil strife 1839–46 before reunification.

**Civil War and 1866.** Confederate agent Albert Pike summer **1861** treaties with each of the five nations: Choctaw and Chickasaw virtually unanimous southern; Creek, Seminole, Cherokee treaties obscured deep division → bloody north/south factional conflict (Cherokee Nation a crossroads; Creek Nation devastated; Unionists to badly managed Kansas camps; Confederates to Choctaw Nation and Texas). U.S. reconstruction treated nations as defeated Confederate enemies. **1866** dictated treaties: Creeks sell western half; Seminoles lose all, receive small Creek parcel; Choctaws/Chickasaws give western lands; Cherokees sell Kansas holdings; abolish slavery; full citizenship to freedmen; railroad rights of way. Railroads brought corporate America so fast that by the **1880s** Natives were on the verge of becoming minorities in their own nations. Treaty system ended federally in **1871** ([[treaty-sovereignty-native-north-america]]).

## Fowler, CHNPA I.2 ch. 9 — Plains removals into Indian Territory

Loretta Fowler’s Great Plains chapter treats IT as the terminal dumping ground for **already-Plains and mid-Missouri** peoples crushed by settler pressure, game collapse, and Teton military expansion—not only for eastern removals.

**Creation and excision.** Eastern removals (including after the [[events/indian-removal-act-1830|Indian Removal Act 1830]]) pushed thousands into Osage/Kansa country. In **1825** Congress created an “Indian Territory” bounded on the north by the Platte River, on the east by Arkansas/Missouri Territories, on the south by the Red, and on the west by Spanish territory—**already occupied** by Osages, Kansas, Otos, Missouris, Pawnees, Poncas, and others. The **1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act** excised land for territories already settled by trespassing Americans; the U.S. disregarded insulation guarantees under settler pressure.

**Pawnees.** Faced constricting buffalo, eastern immigrant hunters (+35% Native hunters), and Teton attacks (alliance ~25,000 vs Pawnees outnumbered ~8:1); population ~20,000 mid-eighteenth century → ~6,200 (1850) → 2,400 (1869). Treaties 1833/1857 failed to deliver promised guns/protection; Quaker agents opposed military resistance; ~200 men scouted for the Army vs Tetons/Cheyennes and guarded the UP Railroad. Grasshoppers, drought, and settler theft forced sale of the Nebraska reservation and **full relocation to Indian Territory by 1876**, taking crop and medicinal seeds.

**Osage, Kansa, Oto-Missouri, Iowa, Ponca.** Osages on a southern Kansas reservation since 1825/1839 ceded half **1865** then all **1870** under settler arson/theft and Cheyenne competition, relocating to IT **1871–74** (under 3,000). Kansas forced successive cessions 1825–1872 then IT from **1873**. Iowas and Oto-Missouris sold reservations and moved ~**1874–83**. Poncas ceded 1858/1865; Congress ordered IT removal **1876** (forced march **1877**); some returned **1879**, arrested, then freed after eastern outcry and received Nebraska homesteads; remainder got a small IT reservation **1884**. Omahas kept a portion of the 1854 Nebraska reservation.

**Texas Caddo–Wichita flight.** After Texas annexation (1845) the state controlled public lands; Texas placed Wichitas, Caddos, and remnants on a Brazos River reservation **1855**; settler hostility blaming them for Comanche raids forced hurried U.S. removal (**1859**) to an IT reservation on former Chickasaw lands near the Wichita Mountains, leaving most property behind—**“to save them from annihilation”** (Fowler). Civil War split loyalties then return to the reservation.

**Southern Arapaho / Cheyenne reservation.** After [[events/medicine-lodge-treaty-1867|1867]] terms proved unfeasible, **1869** Grant executive order created the Cheyenne–Arapaho reservation in IT; almost all Arapahos and few Cheyennes settled. Main body of ~1,000 Northern Cheyennes moved to IT **1877** per the 1876 Black Hills agreement—then several hundred escaped north (Fort Robinson massacre path)—see [[actors/cheyenne]].
