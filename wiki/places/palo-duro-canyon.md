# Palo Duro Canyon

**Palo Duro Canyon**, on the eastern edge of the [[places/llano-estacado|Llano Estacado]] in the Texas Panhandle, was a major Comanche wintering and refuge area — one of the largest canyon systems in the American West. Gwynne calls it the second-largest canyon in North America after the Grand Canyon. Free [[actors/comanches|Quahadis]] and allied bands liked to camp in its depths, within a roughly hundred-mile ambit of present-day Amarillo that also included headwaters of the Pease River, McClellan's Creek, and Blanco Canyon.

## Strategic geography

- **Refuge:** Caprock Escarpment and canyon bottoms formed a near-impregnable fortress for free bands of Comanchería; permanent water and winter shelter.
- **Operations base:** In the terminal phase of free plains power (1874), northern Texas Panhandle canyon lands (Palo Duro and Red River forks) were the last hideout of ~3,000 hostiles / ≤800 warriors still off-reservation.
- **Post-conquest:** Rancher Charles Goodnight acquired much of the canyon country. By a last unsupervised Comanche-Kiowa hunt (1878, Gwynne), living buffalo were gone even in Palo Duro — only carcasses and bones; "the white man owned the sacred canyons." Quanah and Goodnight parleyed over cattle killed in lieu of buffalo (two beeves every other day until buffalo found — none were).

## Red River War strike, 28 September 1874

In the [[events/red-river-war-1874|Red River War]], [[actors/mackenzie-ranald-s|Mackenzie]]'s Fourth Cavalry struck Palo Duro at dawn 28 September 1874 after a Tule Canyon feint and a night march (Comanchero tortured on a wagon wheel for the camp location). Seven companies at the rim: five villages, ~200 lodges over ~3 miles along the stream — O-ha-ma-tai Comanches (majority), Maman-ti Kiowas, Iron Shirt Cheyennes. Maman-ti's medicine said no bluecoat attack → no sentries. Descent by precipitous ~900-ft goat trail (~1 hour for companies). Running fight ~4 miles; 4 Comanches killed. Villages burned (buffalo robes/meat plus white goods: blankets, china, breechloaders, flour, sugar). **1,424 horses** captured, marched to Tule Canyon; best to scouts; **more than 1,000 shot** until ~3 p.m. — "as a military tactic, a way to take away the Indians' means of survival." Rotting pile became a landmark, then fertilizer; legend of a phantom herd. Survivors afoot without shelter/food/clothing facing winter; most straggled to [[places/fort-sill|Fort Sill]] never to roam free again.

Earlier in the same campaign Miles (30 August) fought Cheyennes near Palo Duro — claimed exaggerated numbers, killed 25, burned a large village — but the Mackenzie strike and horse destruction were the operational hinge.

## Why it mattered

Palo Duro mattered as **mobility infrastructure**: water, winter range, and horse pasture. Destroying lodges and herds there broke free-band logistics more effectively than killing a few warriors in the canyon bottom. After 1874 the place passed from Comanche imperial geography into ranching and later state-park heritage tourism — the same landscape rewritten as scenic rather than strategic.
