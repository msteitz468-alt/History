# Norte Chico (Caral-Supe)

The **Norte Chico** — four narrow river valleys (Huaura, Supe, Pativilca, Fortaleza) on the arid
north-central coast of Peru — held the **oldest urban complex in the Americas** and one of the world's
six independent cradles of civilization. Documented for the wiki through Charles Mann's
[[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]] (Ch. 6, "Cotton (or Anchovies) and Maize"), reporting the work of Ruth Shady
Solís (at **Caral**) and Jonathan Haas, Winifred Creamer, and Álvaro Ruiz. Its dates astonished
archaeologists: large-scale monumental architecture rose here between roughly **3200 and 1800 BCE** —
making it **contemporary with Sumer**, and the only urban society then on earth besides Mesopotamia.
"Most of the Andes is covered with granules of sand," Haas and Creamer wrote; "then in the Norte Chico,
there is a volcano."

## An improbable cradle

The Norte Chico overturns the textbook prerequisite that **intensive grain agriculture** must precede
complex society. Unlike the fertile Nile, Indus, Tigris-Euphrates, and Yellow River breadbaskets, the
Peruvian littoral is "an agronomical no-go zone" — walled off from rain by both the Andes and the cold
**Humboldt Current**, receiving ~2 inches of precipitation a year. Yet here arose at least **25 cities**
with platform mounds, sunken circular plazas, and residential complexes — **Aspero** at the coast,
**Caral** (a 150-acre array with a 60-foot mound) fourteen miles inland, and others on the Pativilca and
Fortaleza, some older still. Strikingly, they had monumental architecture but **almost no pottery and
little maize** — a puzzle that misled the first investigators (Willey and Corbett in 1941 took Aspero's
seventeen artificial mounds for "natural eminences of sand": "an excellent, if embarrassing, example of
not being able to find what you are not looking for").

## "Cotton or anchovies": the maritime-foundations thesis

How did cities arise without staple agriculture? Mann lays out the debate. Michael Moseley's
**"maritime foundations"** hypothesis holds that the rich fisheries of the Humboldt Current — anchovies
and sardines, among the most productive waters on earth — fed the cities, with **irrigated cotton**
grown along the rivers not for food but for the **nets and lines** that caught the fish (and for the
textiles that were the culture's signature craft). The settlements thus rested on a **fishing-and-cotton
economy** rather than a grain economy — a genuinely novel route to urbanism. The earlier **Chinchorro**
of the Atacama (history's first artificial mummies, before 5000 BCE) prefigured the enduring Andean
**ancestor cult** — "the belief that the venerated, preserved dead could exert a powerful impact on the
living" — that would run all the way to the [[inca-empire|Inca]].

## Significance

Norte Chico is the **deep root of Andean civilization** — the lineage that runs through Chavín, the
[[moche|Moche]], [[tiwanaku|Tiwanaku]] and Wari, to the Inca. Crucially, it was **fully independent of
Mesoamerica**: Mann stresses that the two American cradles had almost **no contact** (there is still no
road across the Darién), so the Andes did *not* learn civilization from the [[olmec|Olmec]], as an older
diffusionist view held — it got there **first**, and on its own. It is a centerpiece of the
[[pre-columbian-americas|revisionist picture]]: the Americas were not only more populous and older than
believed, but home to **autonomous experiments in urbanism** as ancient as any in the Old World.
