# Vix

## Overview

Vix (the famous "princess" or elite burial) is the richest known early Iron Age burial in western Europe, located near the defended site of Mont Lassois in eastern France. It exemplifies the pairing of fortified elite residence with rich tumulus burials and the peak of Mediterranean prestige exchange in the Hallstatt period (6th–5th c BCE).

## Key Finds and Context

The burial contained "a large bronze crater, or wine-mixing [vessel], and a pottery drinking cup, both imported from Greece, together with the remains of a vehicle and many other wealthy items" (Ch. 9, ~p. 283, cache ~580s). "Among the many rich graves around the Hohenasperg, two are of particular note... The recently discovered burial at Eberdingen-Hochdorf, however, was intact... a large cauldron containing the remains of a drink, drinking horns, a wheeled bronze couch of Italian or Etruscan manufacture" (parallel; Ch. 9, ~p. 283, cache ~590s). Vix itself is the standout Greek import example.

Mont Lassois defended site shows similar elite control of long-distance networks.

## Significance

Demonstrates "the effects of this trade with the classical world... in the imported goods and other wealth found in fortified sites and rich burials, such as... Vix in eastern France" (Ch. 9, ~p. 293, cache ~1420s). Part of the "climax of the whole phase of hierarchical development that had characterized Bronze Age social organization" (Ch. 9, ~p. 292, cache ~1290s). Mediterranean goods (wine service) used to enhance status; proximity to Mediterranean made control difficult as volume grew, contributing to later hierarchy shifts.

**Grounded claims from Champion et al. 1984 range lines 18501-22500 (Ch. 9):** Quotes above. "The Vix burial, for example... near the defended site of Mont Lassois, contained a large bronze crater..." (Ch. 9, ~p. 283, cache ~579s). "As the intensity of these interactions increased, there was a tendency for the affected areas to be incorporated more fully into the economic and social sphere of the classical world" (Ch. 9, ~p. 293, cache ~1430s); temperate Europe resisted full incorporation.

## Additional Detail from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004 (Range 03, dedicated Vix entry)

"At the small settlement of Vix near Châtillon on the upper Seine River in eastern France, an unusually richly outfitted grave was excavated in 1952 and 1953. Numerous burial mounds are still visible around the fortified hilltop site of Mont Lassois, but the mound above the Vix grave had eroded and was no longer apparent on the surface. Excavations revealed the remains of a mound 42 meters in diameter and probably about 5 meters high, within which was a wooden chamber 3.1 by 2.75 meters in size, covered by a layer of stones. Inside was an undisturbed burial that included the skeletal remains of a woman about thirty-five years of age, buried c. 480 B.C., at the end of the Early Iron Age."

"The woman’s body was laid on the box of a wagon in the center of the grave, with her head toward the north. The wagon’s detached four wheels had been arranged along the east wall of the chamber. On the western side was an extraordinary assemblage of ceramic, bronze, and silver vessels. Around her neck the woman wore a uniquely ornamented gold ring of exceptionally fine workmanship, weighing 480 grams. At the two terminals were lion paws, tiny winged horses, and intricately incised ornamentation." "On each wrist she wore three bracelets of schist and one of thin bronze. A necklace was made of amber, diorite, and serpentine beads. On each ankle was a hollow bronze ring. With her were eight fibulae... Two were of iron, the other six of bronze, and some were ornamented with gold, amber, and coral."

Feasting equipment: "eight vessels, at least six of them imports from the Greek and Etruscan worlds. Two wine cups were products of the luxury ceramic industry in Athens. One was painted in the black-figure style about 525 B.C., and the other was a plain black cup made about 515 B.C. A bronze jug and three basins all may have come from Etruscan workshops in Italy. A silver bowl with a central omphalos, or knob, of sheet gold was 23 centimeters in diameter. The most unusual object in the grave was an enormous bronze krater, a kind of vessel used in the Greek world for mixing wine and water at feasts, made by Greek bronzesmiths. The Vix krater is 1.64 meters tall and weighs 208 kilograms—the largest metal krater known."

## Additional Evidence from Cunliffe 2018 (Ch. 5 range)

Vix and the hillfort of Mont Lassois formed a paired princely centre in the West Hallstatt zone during Hallstatt D2–3. "The rich female burial at Vix neatly exemplifies the range of imports with its huge krater for wine-mixing, jugs for pouring, basins, and Attic cups for drinking." "the discovery of numerous fragments of Attic cups in the nearby hillfort of Mont Lassois is an indication of a princely residence. A geophysical survey has shown that the area within the fortifications was systematically laid out and heavily occupied. One dominant enclosure contained a large timber building with an apsidal end. The scale of the structure and the associated Greek pottery suggests that it may have been a palace." 

The burial "was contained in a wood-lined chamber. The female burial was accompanied by personal ornaments, a set of imported Mediterranean wine-drinking gear, and a dismantled funerary vehicle. The burial dates to the end of the sixth century bc." This reflects the adoption of the symposion complex: "it was the idea of the symposion that was accepted and adopted by the elite, as an extension, no doubt, of their long-established feasting rites." Part of the concentration of power: "in the West Hallstatt zone this can best be characterized as a concentration of power in fewer centres... the increasing exclusivity of the elite burial rite." The site contributed to the prestige goods economy in which paramounts controlled exotics for status maintenance and redistribution.
