## Geographic Description

**Spiro** lies on the Arkansas River valley in eastern Oklahoma at the Eastern Woodlands–Prairie Grasslands border. It was the civic-ceremonial mound center for a society largely dispersed in farmsteads growing maize and local seed crops and hunting deer, raccoon, and turkey (Bruce D. Smith, CHNPA I.1, ch. 5). Occupied ~**A.D. 800–1350**. Often labeled Caddoan Mississippian in secondary literature (southwestern fringe of Smith’s projected language mosaic).

## Site Plan

Smith: two flat-topped platform mounds (**Brown Mound** and **Copple Mound**) and four smaller dome-shaped mounds encircling a vacant oval plaza roughly **200 × 300 m** with a large central post; **no encircling fortification**. Habitation extends onto a lower terrace with three additional mounds including **Craig Mound** (~100 × 30 m). The surface plan is modest relative to multi-hectare complex chiefdoms; Spiro’s significance is **iconographic and mortuary richness**, not sheer mound bulk.

## Craig Mound and the Great Mortuary

**Craig Mound** consists of four linearly conjoined conical mounds; the northernmost is twice as large — location of a series of **charnel structures** for preparation and display of deceased notables before interment in adjacent accretional burial mounds (the three smaller cones). The norm was periodic closing and dismantling of charnel houses and interment of often-broken contents. One mortuary structure was left intact under a clay mound, then the charnel sequence resumed on the summit. That intact facility — commercially mined by 1930s looters (“**the great mortuary**”), ~18 × 12 m, dated **A.D. 1200–1350** political expansion — contained objects **more varied, rare, and numerous than the contents of any other single mortuary so far found in eastern North America**, now dispersed among museums and private owners.

Throughout occupation the Craig locality housed ritually/socially significant dead (most people were buried in outlying village/farmstead cemeteries). After ~A.D. 1200 habitation areas largely predate that date and Spiro became a specialized **mortuary-temple town**.

### Great Mortuary Burial Tiers (full archaeological record)

Smith’s three top elite categories, distinguished spatially and by mortuary treatment from lower-ranking regional elite of outlying centers and from the general populace in scattered cemeteries:

1. **Highest tier — cedar-pole litter burials** (0.6 × 0.6 m to 2 × 2 m): few skeletal elements of one individual plus rich engraved marine shell cups/gorgets, shell and pearl beads, fabric robes, wood/metal/stone objects.
2. **Partially disarticulated extended burials** with few artifacts among floor caches.
3. **Disarticulated remains in basketry chests** with goods including copper plates.

Artifacts almost exclusively high-status, ritual, and military — very few utilitarian objects: shell beads, arrowhead masses, headdresses, earspools, copper plumes/plates, gorgets, nonfunctional mace/knife warclubs and monolithic axes, figurines, effigy pipes, bird staffs, face masks, engraved marine-shell cups (possibly associated with black drink / *Ilex vomitoria*), and intact polychrome textiles and feather-fur costumes for **ritual impersonation of mythical beings**.

## Iconography and the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex

>500 engraved marine-shell cups from the great mortuary depict ritual performances, costumes, and real/mythical creatures; detailed meanings partly beyond recovery and may have been restricted to Craig elite. Smith interprets via historically documented southeastern **tripartite cosmos**:

- **Upper world**: raptorial birds; structure/stability; sun as central deity linked to sacred fire.
- **This world**: human social order.
- **Underworld**: snakes, lizards, frogs; disorder; composite monsters (e.g. Cherokee ethnohistoric **Uktena** — serpent body + deer antlers + bird wings).

Spiro cups show Uktena-like and Piasa/underwater-panther monsters (mountain lion + snake tail + raptor feet/wings), often with human elements suggesting ritual impersonation by cosmic mediators; falcon/peregrine-dancer themes impersonate upper-world hawk enemies of underworld beings (Tlanuwa-like). Mississippian polities lived between opposing upper/lower forces requiring constant ceremonial balance.

**Two schools of shell art** (Smith / Brown revisionism):

| School | Themes |
|---|---|
| **Craig** | Cosmic mediation: underworld monsters, ritual impersonation of upper/lower creatures, vertical pole/panel/sun–sacred fire scenes |
| **Braden** | Warfare and death: warriors with arrows/warclubs; arrows, maces, skulls, scalps, corpses, broken bones, trophy heads; arrow sacrifice; snakes with arrow heads; double-headed “tie-snake” knots |

Craig maintains cosmic order; Braden ensures survival by force against external enemies; spheres interconnect. Ritual art also maintained the socio-political status quo and privileges of higher-ranking kinship units — the constant struggle of ascribed-status elite to hold power.

Red bauxite figurines link Spiro to Shiloh, [[cahokia]], and other centers within prestige-goods exchange. Secondary literature also reports exotic long-distance materials (including claimed Pachuca obsidian and Pacific/Gulf shell) — treat Mesoamerican connections as source-attributed and debated relative to Smith’s broader independence thesis for Mississippian emergence.

## Why It Mattered

Spiro demonstrates that **iconographic and mortuary elaboration** can far exceed surface settlement scale: a modest plaza-and-mound plan concentrated an unparalleled elite ritual assemblage. It is a primary empirical anchor for the [[southeastern-ceremonial-complex]] and for reconstructing Mississippian cosmology through art.

## Historiography

Looting of the Craig great mortuary by the so-called Pocola Mining Company (1930s) destroyed primary context for an exceptional concentration of objects — a major evidentiary loss; later WPA and professional work recovered residual deposits. Landmark motif description: Phillips and Brown, *Pre-Columbian Shell Engravings from the Craig Mound at Spiro*. Style divisions: Brown, “On Style Divisions of the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex: A Revisionist Perspective,” in Galloway ed. 1989. Later reinterpretations (e.g. Brown 2012 in secondary literature) stress cosmologically organized collective ossuary use over purely individual elite wealth display.
