## Overview

Bosra saw continuous Near Eastern settlement since the Bronze Age. The Nabataean king
Rabbel II moved the Nabataean capital there from [[petra|Petra]] in the later 1st
century AD (93 CE), likely reflecting a shift in trade routes from the Red Sea toward the
Wadi Sirhan. After Trajan's annexation of the Nabataean kingdom in AD 106/107, Bosra
became capital of the Roman province of Arabia and garrison of the Third Cyrenaica
Legion (renamed *Nova Trajana Bostra*), inheriting much of Petra's former trade role and
heading the new Via Nova Traiana to the Gulf of Aqaba. Under Ghassanid ascendancy in the
6th century, Bosra was a major [[ghassanids|Ghassanid]] center with one of the greatest
cathedrals of the East, and it remained important through the Middle Ages as a stop on
the Mecca pilgrimage route.

## Ball's Reading: Roman Veneer Over Nabataean Substance (THESIS)

Ball treats Bosra as the clearest case of his central argument that Roman rule in the
Near East was superficial. He argues that "most of the layout of the city, and many of
the monuments, are in fact Nabataean rather than Roman": the eastern quarter's grid, the
great temple enclosure and its propylaeum (the "Nabataean Gate," dated to the late 1st
century AD by its Nabataean-style capitals), and the city's palace — probably Rabbel
II's residence rather than a Roman governor's. Citing recent scholarship, Ball argues
even the main "Roman" Decumanus was actually a pre-existing Nabataean processional way to
the temple, paralleling the arrangement at Petra, with Ionic-order colonnades on the
transverse streets consistent with a pre-Roman date (replaced by Corinthian order only in
the 2nd century, a pattern also attested at [[gerasa|Jerash]]). Ball concludes the actual
"Roman contribution" was limited to a single colonnaded street (Theatre Street), the
insertion of a tetrapylon, a central arch, a nymphaeum/kalybe at street junctions, and
possibly the Oval Plaza.

Ball draws a structural comparison to British Raj-era Indian cities: a large
Nabataean/native "native quarter" comprising the bulk of the city, a "civil lines"
administrative quarter, and a "military lines"/cantonment (the Third Cyrenaica Legion's
headquarters) for the army.

This is Ball's interpretive framing; mainstream scholarship (e.g. Fergus Millar, *The
Roman Near East*, 1993) reads the Roman Near East, including provincial capitals like
Bosra, as more substantively Graeco-Roman — see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]] for the
debate.

## Ghassanid Christian Center

Bosra's Cathedral of SS Sergius, Bacchus and Leontius (built 512), a circular domed
structure, is regarded as a prototype for the great domed churches of Ravenna and
Constantinople, and was also the model for the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem. Ball argues
Bosra's strong early Christian building program reflects "its prevailing Arab character
and continuity from the past" rather than Greek influence, citing onomastic evidence that
more than half the personal names on Bosra's Greek/Latin inscriptions are Semitic, and
arguing that the silent majority who left no inscriptions would have been
"overwhelmingly Semitic" — noting that even Greek personal names imply no more Greek
ethnic character than Greek-derived names like "Peter" do in modern Britain (THESIS).

Islamic tradition places the monk Bahira at Bosra, reputedly conversing with the young
Muhammad while accompanying his uncle Abu Talib on trade missions — a tradition Ball
describes as factually uncertain but historiographically significant as evidence the
Arabs had become a major Near Eastern political and religious force by the Ghassanid
era. Ball quotes Philip Hitti's judgment that the Ghassanids "served as a pre-view of the
gigantic show to come," concluding: "The clients of Byzantium had become its rivals."
