## Geographic Description

The Kantō is Japan's largest plain, bounded by the Japanese Alps to the west and north and the Pacific Ocean to the east and south. The plain is drained by the Tone, Arakawa, and Sumida rivers. In the late sixteenth century — when Ieyasu received it from Hideyoshi — it was a landscape of coastal swamp (much of present-day Tokyo's downtown sat under tidal water), flat agricultural plains in Musashi, and wooded highlands toward the mountains. Eight provinces comprised the Kantō: Sagami, Izu, Musashi, Awa, Kazusa, Shimōsa, Kōzuke, and Shimotsuke.

## History of Control

The Kantō was the heartland of Japanese feudalism: the first shogunate was founded at Kamakura (1185) in Sagami province. The Later Hōjō clan (Go-Hōjō) established control over the region from their base at Odawara castle (Sagami) beginning in 1493, building a century of stable, administratively sophisticated rule over the eight provinces. Their capital Odawara was "a strong and populous city, a ri long, and well furnished with every kind of shop and trade" by the late sixteenth century (Sadler, chapter 20).

When the Hōjō fell to Hideyoshi's coalition in the Odawara campaign of 1590, the entire Kantō was confiscated. Hideyoshi offered the eight provinces to [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] in exchange for his five ancestral provinces (Mikawa, Tōtōmi, Suruga, Kai, Shinano). The transfer was formalized on the fourteenth of the seventh month of 1590; Ieyasu entered his new capital of Edo on the first of the eighth month.

## Strategic Significance

The Kantō's strategic importance derives from:
1. **Agricultural capacity**: the flat plains of Musashi and adjacent provinces were among the most productive agricultural land in Japan.
2. **Defensibility**: the Hakone mountain barrier on the west limited access from the capital region; control of Odawara or Hakone essentially closed the Kantō to western attack.
3. **Population base**: the eight provinces represented the largest concentration of agricultural population in Japan, providing the labor and tax base for large armies.
4. **Distance from political center**: the Kantō's remoteness from Kyoto and Osaka was both a weakness (distance from court politics) and, ultimately, a strength — when the Tokugawa made Edo the seat of the shogunate (1603), the capital shifted east.

Hideyoshi's calculation in transferring Ieyasu to the Kantō was that the unfamiliar territory, residual Hōjō loyalties, and distance from power would keep Ieyasu busy and might even weaken him. He filled the Tōkaidō corridor with his own retainers to close off Kantō from the west. The opposite occurred: Ieyasu used the Kantō as a base for the construction of the Tokugawa order.

## Edo and Urban Development

When Ieyasu arrived, Edo was little more than "a straggling town that was hardly more than a village" around a dilapidated castle. The town of Hirakawa encompassed perhaps a hundred houses. "To the north of the castle was Kanda Hill, while on the east was a reedy swamp completely covered by water at high tide." Ieyasu's immediate priorities were: assigning retainer fiefs, relocating temples outside the city center, and beginning drainage and construction of the castle grounds. Within a century, Edo would become one of the largest cities in the world.

## Historiography

The Kantō's role as the birthplace of Japanese feudalism and the eventual seat of the Tokugawa shogunate makes it central to virtually all histories of premodern Japan. The later Hōjō's administration of the region has been reassessed by modern scholars as more sophisticated and innovative than earlier Tokugawa-centric narratives admitted. The demographic and agricultural development of the Kantō under Tokugawa rule remains an active research area. The collection is moderate in coverage of this region: the Sadler volume and the Cambridge histories provide the political framework, but detailed local history and agricultural history are gaps.
