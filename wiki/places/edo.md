## Geographic Description

Edo occupied the southwestern edge of the Kantō plain at the point where the Musashino upland meets the coast of Edo Bay. At the time of Ieyasu's entry in 1590, the castle stood on a promontory; to its east lay a "reedy swamp completely covered by water at high tide" extending to what is now the Hibiya district. The Kanda River bounded the northern approaches. The western approach by the Tōkaidō highway provided the main access.

## History to 1590

Ōta Dōkan built the original Edo castle in 1457 during the Muromachi period. The Hōjō clan took it from the Ōgigayatsu-Uesugi around 1524 and used it as an eastern outpost of their Sagami-based domain. By 1590 the castle had stood for about 130 years without significant repair since Hōjō's siege. It was "extensive but dilapidated," roofed with boards and thatched, with an entrance made of three planks from an old ship. The surrounding settlement amounted to "the village of Hirakawa," covering a stretch from present-day Otemachi and Eiraku-chō to Yūraku-chō — "some hundred houses or so."

Clustered around this hamlet were fifteen or sixteen temples and two or three shrines, including the Kanda Myōjin shrine (where the National Treasury building now stands in Nagata-chō) and the Nichirinji. The Kanda Myōjin festival on the fifteenth day of the eighth month was, as Sadler notes, "then as now, the most important event in the year."

## Ieyasu's Entry and First Transformations (1590)

[[tokugawa-ieyasu]] made his official entry on the first day of the eighth month of 1590, arriving by the western highway. He paused at the gate of the Kōmyōji temple, spoke with its abbot Son-ō (recognizing him as a disciple of the Tokugawa family temple at Daijuji in Mikawa), drank tea, and returned the next morning to arrange the temple's conversion to Jodo Buddhism and its role as his chantry in Edo. His headquarters were initially at the Hō-ōnji temple.

Inspection of the castle with Honda Masanobu revealed rain-damaged mats and the rudimentary entrance. Ieyasu refused substantial renovation: "The stipends of our retainers must be seen to before anything else." Minimal repairs were made. He then immediately began reorganizing the city: temples and shrines in the Tsubonezawa district were ordered to relocate to sites outside the city center, provided with new plots at state expense but required to move swiftly. The Kanda Myōjin shrine alone moved twice as the city grew.

His retainers were given fiefs proportional to merit, with those of small stipend placed close to Edo to minimize travel costs. The city proper would grow into its retainer quarters, temple districts, merchant quarters, and eventually the great urban fabric of Tokugawa Edo.

## Significance

Edo's choice as Ieyasu's capital in 1590 — over Odawara (prosperous, fortified) and Kamakura (historically prestigious) — was contested by his own retainers. Ieyasu's reasons are not fully documented; Sadler suggests he "no doubt saw the possibilities of this now small city." Whatever the motivation, the choice determined the future of Japan: Edo became the seat of the Tokugawa shogunate (1603) and the de facto capital of Japan, growing over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries into one of the largest cities in the world, eventually renamed Tokyo (1868) and today the capital of Japan with a metropolitan population exceeding 35 million.

## Historiography

The contrast between Edo's appearance in 1590 and its later greatness is a central set piece in Tokugawa origin mythology. The specific detail preserved by Sadler — the tidal swamp to the east, the planks-from-a-ship entrance, the hundred-odd houses, the temple inventory — is valuable because it anchors the urban history of Tokyo to a specific documentary moment. The choice of Edo over Odawara and the rapidity of Ieyasu's arrival (only fourteen days after the formal transfer) are consistent across the main Tokugawa chronicles.
