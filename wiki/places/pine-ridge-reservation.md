# Pine Ridge Reservation

Oglala Lakota homeland in southwestern South Dakota; site of the **1890 Wounded Knee Creek massacre** and later of the **1973 Wounded Knee occupation**—a primary geographic node of the [[reservation-period-1880-1960]] and of twentieth-century [[pan-indian-movement]] memory politics.

## Geography and formation

Modern Pine Ridge Agency territory descends from the Great Sioux Reservation complex created after Fort Laramie treaty politics and subsequent reductions. Fowler (CHNPA I.2 ch. 9) describes Red Cloud’s and Spotted Tail’s Teton agencies on the North Platte/White Rivers and Missouri agencies without using the modern name “Pine Ridge”; Hoxie (ch. 12) uses **Pine Ridge Sioux Reservation** for the 1890 crisis in the **newly admitted state of South Dakota**. Sister agency **Rosebud** and northern **Standing Rock** / Cheyenne River form the multi-reservation Lakota geography of the Ghost Dance crisis.

## Why it mattered, 1880–1890

- **Scale:** Pine Ridge and Rosebud communities were many times the size of Apache and Crow groups previously intimidated by troop massing.
- **Leadership memory:** Leaders were battle-hardened veterans of campaigns against Americans in eastern Montana in the 1860s and of Custer’s defeat at the Little Bighorn (1876)—not men who trusted U.S. Army officers.
- **Land pressure:** 1889 Crook commission opened millions of acres west of the Missouri as Dakotas prepared for statehood, after Teton Sioux had defeated two earlier reduction attempts.
- **Ghost Dance 1890:** Growing Lakota performance of Wovoka’s round dance; Indian Office massed troops around Sioux camps (events_here: [[ghost-dance-1890]]).
- **Wounded Knee, 29 Dec 1890:** After Sitting Bull’s killing at Standing Rock (15 Dec) and Big Foot’s flight, Seventh Cavalry engagement at Wounded Knee Creek on Pine Ridge left **146 Sioux men, women, and children** and **25 soldiers** dead (Hoxie; first shot disputed) (events_here: [[wounded-knee-1890]]).

## Later reservation-period politics

- Rosebud (sister Sioux agency) IRA vote pitted boarding-school “progressives” and landless supporters of Indian corporations against Black Hills Treaty Council advocates who feared new tribal organization would undermine treaty-based dealings with the United States—illustrating subsumption of New Deal policy into local Native politics (related Sioux agencies including Pine Ridge).
- Black Hills claim litigation linked nine Sioux communities from 1911 Black Hills Council onward ([[black-hills]]).
- Large non-Indian ranch leases on adjacent Standing Rock (e.g. Ed Lemmon 865,000 acres fenced 1902) exemplify economic enclosure of Lakota reservation space.

## Twentieth-century symbolic site

Washburn (ch. 15): AIM siege of the Oglala Sioux village of Wounded Knee, South Dakota, in **1973**—radical pan-Indian challenge to existing tribal government and federal order (events_here: [[wounded-knee-occupation-1973]]; part_of: [[pan-indian-movement]]). The place thus anchors both the assault-phase deterrent massacre and later renaissance-era protest memory.
