## Geographic Description

Monte Verde is an open-air archaeological site on a minor tributary creek in the cool, humid lake-district of south-central Chile, roughly 900 km south of Santiago. Jared Diamond named it "1989's favourite" site in the debate over the timing of the peopling of the Americas. The site lies in a "damp and cool environment, at a rather poor camping place along a minor creek," in Thomas Lynch's characterisation — an observation that formed part of his sceptical evaluation of the pre-Paleoindian claims.

## History of Occupation and Discovery

Excavations were led by Tom Dillehay and Michael Collins, who proposed two occupation horizons:

- **MV-II (~13,000 BP):** Claimed to be an Archaic-like village occupied year-round before the Paleoindian horizon elsewhere in the Americas. Dillehay and Collins identified hearths, wooden structures, wooden artefacts, and mastodon (*Haplomastodon*) remains — the latter illustrated in Lynch's list of Late Pleistocene mammals found in the area.
- **MV-I (~33,000 BP):** A much earlier alleged occupation based on a limited scatter of material; widely rejected even by most proponents of pre-Clovis occupation.

The site's significance rests primarily on the MV-II dates, which would make it contemporaneous with or earlier than the Clovis horizon in North America (~11,500–11,200 BP).

## Why Monte Verde Matters

If the ~13,000 BP dates are valid and the cultural associations genuine, Monte Verde would constitute the earliest well-documented human site in South America and would require humans to have been present in the Americas well before the Clovis florescence — implying either an earlier entry or a coastal route that bypassed the glaciated corridor.

The site directly challenges the "short chronology" dominant among North and South American archaeologists, which places the Paleoindian colonisation of South America between ~12,000 and 10,000 BP.

## Scholarly Debate and Scepticism

Lynch devotes considerable space to Monte Verde in [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]], placing it alongside Taima-Taima, Pedra Furada, and Alice Boer as the most-discussed "pre-Paleoindian" candidates. He summarises the main issues:

> "The main issues are the radiocarbon dates (possibly misinterpreted or even systematically contaminated), the interpretation of the site's stratigraphy, the recognition of artifacts and other signs of human culture, the possible conflation of remains of different ages, and the possible conflation of cultural with noncultural remains."

Critics include Gary Haynes, Dena Dincauze, Lautaro Nunez, Calogero Santoro, and Lynch himself. Lynch's specific objections:

1. **Contamination of radiocarbon dates:** The site lies along a minor creek whose fluctuating water table could have moved dissolved or suspended carbon through the deposits, potentially giving anomalously old dates to later material. Lynch states: "it is likely that dissolved or suspended carbon has moved with the groundwater to contaminate the samples."
2. **Stratigraphy and mixture:** The site is an open-air context along a creek, not a sealed cave deposit. Lynch suggests "the settlement and extraction pattern proposed by the excavators might belong instead to the more recent Archaic pattern."
3. **Artefact identification:** "Many archaeologists are skeptical of most of the Monte Verde artifacts, including hearths, structures, and most of the wooden artifacts. The stone artifacts, which would fit later Archaic industries, may have been mixed with remains of a paleontological site."
4. **Ecological implausibility:** A permanent pre-Paleoindian village in a poor, damp creek-side environment while no comparable sites are known elsewhere in South America is "an improbable coincidence in a continent where verifiable glacial-age sites have otherwise been as scarce as hen's teeth."

Despite Lynch's scepticism (written before the 1997 site review by a multi-disciplinary committee), the broader scientific community moved in the 1990s toward provisional acceptance of the MV-II occupation as genuine after Dillehay's monograph and an on-site peer review. The status of Monte Verde remains contested but influential.

## Relation to Peopling Debate

Monte Verde sits at the intersection of two competing models for the peopling of the Americas:

- **Clovis-first (short chronology):** Lynch, Turner, and the majority hold that humans arrived in the Americas no earlier than ~12,000–11,500 BP via the inland Beringian corridor, with the Clovis horizon marking the archaeological threshold.
- **Pre-Clovis:** Advocates (Dillehay, Collins, Bryan, Gruhn) cite Monte Verde as evidence of an earlier human presence, possibly via a coastal route that has been submerged by postglacial sea-level rise.
