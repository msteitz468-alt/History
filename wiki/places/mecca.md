## Geographic Description

Mecca lies in a narrow valley in the Hijaz, the mountainous western strip of the Arabian peninsula. It is "relatively unpromising" as an oasis site — "unlike Ta'if, it had no great oasis, that is, sufficient underground water to support much vegetation." Its strategic significance was commercial and religious rather than agricultural: it sat "at the junction of two major routes" — the north-south Red Sea coastal corridor linking Syria and Yemen, and routes running east toward the Persian Gulf and central Arabia. This position made it "approximately equidistant from the three spheres of power around Bedouin Arabia": the Byzantine-controlled Syria, the Sasanian-controlled Iraq, and the Aksumite-influenced Yemen.

## History of Control

### Pre-Islamic Period

Mecca was controlled by the **Quraysh**, a confederation of Arab clans who "took over the springs at Mecca and the shrine from other Bedouin." The Quraysh were organized "on Bedouin principles, without a king or any other municipal institutions beyond the clan councils." Their power rested on a combination of commercial acumen, management of the annual pilgrimage (*hajj*) to the Ka'ba, and diplomatic skill in maintaining neutrality among Arabia's competing tribal and imperial interests.

The **Ka'ba** — a rectangular building housing the sacred tokens of the Meccan clans and those of allied tribes — was the center of Mecca's religious significance. It was "presided over by Allah," understood as guarantor of the sanctuary's inviolability. Three goddesses widely worshipped in the region — Allat, al-'Uzza, and Manat — had associated shrines. "Even Christian Arabs made pilgrimage to the Ka'bah, honouring Allah there as God the Creator," indicating its trans-sectarian sanctity.

The Quraysh maintained "sacred months" during which "fighting was taboo even in" the hallowed area surrounding Mecca, enabling commerce across otherwise hostile tribal territories. This system of commercial peace, pilgrim management, and religious neutrality was unique in Arabia — "the only effective Bedouin-based alternative to assimilation to the settled cultures."

### Muhammad's Preaching and Departure (c. 610–622)

Muhammad ibn 'Abd-Allah, "a substantial and respected merchant in Mecca" of the Banu Hashim clan, began receiving revelations c. 610. His proclamation of strict monotheism directly threatened the Meccan system, which required the Ka'ba's value as a multi-cult shrine. The Quraysh persecuted the early Muslim community. After the death of Muhammad's protective uncle Abu Talib removed his tribal shelter, and after negotiations with Yathrib produced an invitation, Muhammad led his community in the [[the-hijra-622|Hijra]] of 622.

### Conquest and Re-consecration (628–630)

After years of conflict — the battles of Badr (624), Uhud (625), the Trench (627) — Muhammad negotiated the Treaty of Hudaybiyya (628) with Mecca, which was widely seen as a Muslim concession but proved a strategic step. In 630, "Muhammad marched with perhaps a thousand or more" on Mecca; the city submitted with minimal resistance. The Ka'ba was purified of its idols and re-consecrated to Allah alone. The Qurayshi elite, including Abu Sufyan (father of the future caliph Mu'awiya), converted and were integrated into the Muslim leadership.

### Post-Conquest Role

After Muhammad's death (632), Mecca's political centrality diminished relative to Medina (the administrative capital) and the new garrison cities of Iraq and Egypt. But its religious centrality was permanent: as the site of the Ka'ba and the annual *hajj*, it remained the symbolic heart of the Islamic world.

During the [[second-fitna-680]], Ibn al-Zubayr made Mecca his caliphal capital (681–692) after being besieged and forced there by Umayyad forces who "took Medina (under Muslim b. 'Uqbah, at Battle of the Harrah)" in 683 and then besieged Mecca. The Ka'ba was damaged. Ibn al-Zubayr was defeated and killed by the Umayyad general Hajjaj in 692, ending the Second Fitna.

## Why Mecca Mattered

**Commercial:** The pilgrim fairs that accompanied the *hajj* created a protected commercial space in which trade across tribal lines could occur. The Quraysh's management of this space was the basis of their wealth and influence.

**Religious:** The Ka'ba was the only effective pan-tribal Arabian sanctuary, neutral to all confessional allegiances and yet linked to all major cult groups in the region. Its re-consecration as an exclusively Islamic site in 630 was a decisive act of symbolic appropriation.

**Political:** Mecca's position as home of the Quraysh — the tribe of Muhammad, the early caliphs, and the Umayyad dynasty — gave it permanent prestige claims in Islamic political culture, even after Damascus became the Umayyad capital.
