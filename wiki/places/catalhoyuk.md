# Çatal Hüyük (Çatalhöyük)

A large Neolithic settlement on the Konya plain of south-central Anatolia, one of
the most spectacular early agglomerations in the world and a centrepiece of the
debate over what counts as a "town" before cities. Its discovery transformed the
picture of Neolithic origins — the editors of [[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I,
Part 1]] single it out in the Preface as a **7th-millennium "city" of at least
thirty-two acres** — and it is treated at length in Ch. VII by its excavator,
**James Mellaart**.

## Form and Scale

Çatal Hüyük was a dense honeycomb of mudbrick houses sharing party walls, entered
from the **roof** (there were no streets), so that the rooftops formed the
community's circulation surface. Its size — far larger than contemporary villages —
made it exceptional, though whether its scale and ritual elaboration make it a
genuine **town/proto-urban** centre or simply a very large village is exactly the
question posed by [[what-defines-a-city]] and
[[hierarchy-vs-heterarchy-early-cities]]. (Later excavation under Ian Hodder has
stressed its striking *lack* of evident hierarchy.)

## Shrines and Wall-Paintings

Mellaart's excavations revealed numerous rooms he interpreted as **shrines**,
elaborately decorated with **wall-paintings**, modelled **bulls' heads (bucrania)**,
plaster reliefs, and figurines including the famous seated female figure. The
imagery — hunting scenes, vultures, leopards, bulls — is among the earliest
substantial corpus of Near Eastern religious and symbolic art. Mellaart read it as
evidence of a developed cult; the interpretation of these rooms as dedicated
shrines (versus ordinary houses with ritual features) has since been debated.

## The Earliest Pottery in the Near East

CAH identifies the Anatolian plateau, and Çatal Hüyük specifically, as **"the
earliest known centre of pottery production in the Near East."** A heavy straw- and
grit-tempered burnished **cream ware** appears from level XIII (~6750 BCE); by level
VII (~6200 BCE) fine dark burnished wares mature and are exported — reaching north
Syria via Mersin in Cilicia and Byblos by ~6000 BCE. The site thus stands near the
origin of Near Eastern ceramic technology.

## Trade

Çatal Hüyük lay near the **obsidian** sources of central Anatolia and was a hub in
the volcanic-glass trade that reached [[jericho|Jericho]] and the Levant; imported
**Red Sea cowries** appear in level VII (~6200 BCE), showing connections reaching to
the southern seas. See [[trade-network-formation]].

## Significance

With [[jericho|Jericho]], Çatal Hüyük anchors the case that **large, ritually
elaborate, economically specialized communities existed within the
[[neolithic|Neolithic]]**, before metallurgy and writing — pushing the deep
prehistory of [[urbanization]] back into the 7th millennium and complicating any
neat sequence from village to city.

## Historiography

The CAH account is Mellaart's original (1960s) excavation, pre-Hodder; his "shrine"
and cult interpretations, and the absolute (uncalibrated) dates, are now qualified
by decades of later fieldwork emphasizing domestic continuity and egalitarian
social organization. Treat the *material richness and scale* as durable, the
*interpretation and dating* as of their period. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
