# Pompeii

A coastal town of Campania in southern Italy, on the Bay of Naples in the
shadow of Mount Vesuvius. Of Oscan origin and later Samnite, it became a Roman
colony (*Colonia Cornelia Veneria Pompeianorum*) after 80 BCE. Its destruction
and burial under volcanic ash and lapilli in the [[eruption-of-vesuvius-79ce|eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE]]
preserved the fabric of an ordinary Roman town to a degree unmatched anywhere
in the ancient world.

## Why It Mattered

Pompeii is less important for its role in political events than for what its
sudden preservation reveals: streets, houses, shops, bakeries, fulleries,
brothels, baths, theatres and tombs frozen in the mid-first century CE. It is
the single most important archaeological witness to the material and social
texture of Roman urban life.

## Graffiti Capital of the Ancient World

Pompeii yields the richest corpus of [[ancient-graffiti|ancient graffiti]]
known — nearly 11,000 texts from the Vesuvian region catalogued in *CIL* IV:
roughly 3,348 painted notices (including electoral posters, *programmata*),
4,664 incised or ink/charcoal inscriptions, and 2,242 marks on pottery. At
least half were made on the interior and exterior surfaces of lived-in
spaces — houses, shops, bakeries, brothels and gladiator quarters — evidence
that marking surfaces was tolerated and commonplace rather than illicit. The
graffiti record grief, insult, desire, commerce, sport and politics in the
voices of ordinary and often sub-elite Pompeians, including men and women of
mixed Greek and Italian heritage (e.g. the Basilica graffiti of Pyrrhus and
Chius, *CIL* IV.1852 and IV.1820). The corpus anchors much of the evidence in
[[keegan-graffiti-in-antiquity-2014|Keegan's *Graffiti in Antiquity*]].

## What the Graffiti Record

- **Oscan and the Social War.** Painted Oscan *eituns* notices at street corners
  gave mobilization/defence instructions during the Social War (91–88 BCE), when
  Pompeii joined the Italian revolt and fell to Sulla; it was refounded as the
  veteran *Colonia Cornelia Veneria Pompeianorum*, Oscan magistrates replaced by
  Latin *duoviri*. A painted "Spartacus" (Oscan *Spartaks*) combat scene in a
  house entrance may reference the revolt of 73 BCE (Spartacus reportedly camped
  on Vesuvius) or an earlier gladiatorial spectacle.
- **Electoral *programmata*.** Thousands of painted notices endorse candidates
  for *aedile* and *duovir*, using the formula name + office + *rogo*/*OVF* +
  endorsement ("worthy of the state"). Endorsers include trade guilds
  (mule-drivers, onion-sellers, fruit-vendors, worshippers of Isis), women
  (over 50 named, though women could not vote), and satirical mock-groups
  ("the sleepers," "the petty thieves," "the late-drinkers") mocking Marcus
  Cerrinius Vatia — early "dirty" campaigning. Imperial favour surfaces too: the
  *iudicia* graffiti acclaiming **Nero** and **Poppaea** (63–68 CE), and
  Suedius Clemens's sponsorship "in accordance with the authority of Vespasian"
  after 69 CE. See [[nero]].
- **Gladiators and games.** The House of the Gladiators (V.5.3) preserves 140+
  graffiti in which fighters recorded their own win-tallies (Celadus the Thracian
  "girls' sigh"; Crescens the *retiarius*); *edicta munerum* announced games (the
  Lucretii Valentes' twenty-pair shows, painted by the sign-writer Aemilius
  Celer). The 59 CE amphitheatre riot with Nuceria (Tacitus, *Annals* 14.17)
  brought a ten-year senatorial ban on shows.
- **Commerce.** Amphora labels advertise the town's famous *garum* (fish sauce)
  of Aulus Umbricius Scaurus, wines from Falernum, Sorrento, Sicily and Crete,
  and a market-day (*nundinae*) calendar keyed to regional towns. See
  [[ancient-graffiti]].
- **Religion, magic and sexuality.** Isis-cult electoral blocs, Pompeian Venus
  love-graffiti, apotropaic phallus (*fascinum*) marks, "*cacator cave malum*"
  latrine curses (see [[defixiones]]), and ~500 erotic graffiti concentrated in
  the lupanar, Basilica and gladiator quarters (see [[history-of-sexuality]]).

## Significance Over Time

An unremarkable provincial town in life, Pompeii became, through catastrophe,
the benchmark site for the study of Roman daily life, epigraphy, wall painting,
and urban form from its rediscovery in the 18th century onward.
