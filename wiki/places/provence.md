# Provence

**The lower-Rhône and Mediterranean region of [[francia|Francia]]**, ceded to the Franks by the
[[ostrogoths|Ostrogoths]] in 536 and, like [[aquitaine|Aquitaine]], a zone of deep Roman survival
that drifted into autonomous lordship before Carolingian reabsorption.

## Roman survival and the Rhône Church

Provence preserved the Mediterranean, urban, Romanised face of Gaul. Its coast and the Rhône corridor
were the seat of the aristocratic **Rhône monastic tradition** — Lérins, St-Victor at Marseille — that
ran in parallel to, and largely ignored, the [[gregory-of-tours|Martinian]] tradition of the north
(see [[merovingian-monasticism]]). Marseille remained a port through which eastern luxuries entered
Francia.

## Autonomous patricians

As central authority disintegrated after Dagobert, Provençal **patricians with both local and Frankish
ties — Antenor and Maurontus —** built autonomous lordships "along the same model as that of the
[[pippinids|Pippinids]] themselves," pledging nominal loyalty to the Merovingian king while acting
independently. Antenor confiscated the estates of **St-Victor of Marseille** and had its land records
burned on the high altar to bar any reassertion of property rights — a tactic [[charles-martel|Charles
Martel]] would later mirror. In the 720s–730s Maurontus allied with the Muslims of Septimania against
the Pippinid partisan Abba; Martel exploited this to cast himself as **champion of Christianity**,
expelling the Muslims and installing Abba as *patricius*. Abba died heirless, bequeathing his estates
to his family monastery **Novalesa**, which then passed under direct Carolingian control.

## Why it mattered

Provence shows the same pattern as Aquitaine — late-Roman continuity, fragmentation into private
lordship, and forcible Carolingian reintegration dressed as a Christian crusade. Its monastic
geography (Lérins, St-Victor) also makes it central to the history of Gallic monasticism.
