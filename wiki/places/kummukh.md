## Overview

Kummukh (Urartian *Qumaha*; Neo-Babylonian *Kimuhu*?; Classical *Commagene*) was a Neo-Hittite kingdom occupying roughly the area of modern Adıyaman province in south-eastern Anatolia, west of the Euphrates between [[melid|Melid]] (Malatya) to the north and [[carchemish]] to the south. Its capital, also called Kummukh, was probably the predecessor of Classical Samosata (modern Samsat Höyük). The kingdom is attested in sources from c.866 to 708 BCE, when it was annexed by [[neo-assyrian-empire|Sargon II]]; its territorial and cultural successor in later centuries was the Hellenistic and Roman-period kingdom of Commagene.

## Geographic Description

Kummukh sat astride the middle Euphrates corridor, wedged between Melid to the north and Carchemish to the south. Its location between former Hittite and Mitannian territory in the Late Bronze Age gave it strategic importance in conflicts between those kingdoms. In the Iron Age its position made it a pivot point in relations between the Neo-Hittite world, Urartu to the north, and Assyrian power to the east. The Pazarcık stele (805 BCE) attests a boundary between Kummukh and [[gurgum|Gurgum]] to its west, indicating a well-defined territorial edge on that side.

## History

**Origins.** No textual or archaeological evidence specifies when the kingdom was established. Almost certainly it began as a sub-kingdom of [[carchemish]] under Kuzi-Teshub's overlordship. The region may already have been under Carchemishean authority before the fall of the Hittite empire. Bryce notes that a king of Kummukh may be the "Tudhaliya" mentioned in the Kelekli inscription of Carchemish's king Suhi II, as the intended husband of Suhi's daughter — which would imply a diplomatic marriage alliance between the two kingdoms, perhaps at the moment of Kummukh's emergence as an independent entity.

**Ruling dynasty.** Four of the six known kings of Kummukh bore names of Late Bronze Age Hittite kings (Hattusili, Suppiluliuma, Hattusili, Muwatalli), strongly suggesting that the ruling family traced its origins to a branch of the Hittite royal dynasty. Bryce suggests "the ruling family at Kummuh was from beginning to end a continuation of a branch of the Late Bronze Age Hittite royal dynasty." The two outliers — Kundashpu and Kushtashpi — bear non-Hittite names and may represent successions through female lines or adoption.

**The attested rulers, in sequence:**

- **Hattusili I** (Assyrian *Qatazilu*, c.866–857): earliest known ruler; first attested as a tributary of Ashurnasirpal II c.866 (RIMA 2: 219), then of Shalmaneser III in 858 and 857.
- **Kundashpu** (c.854 —): probably Hattusili I's successor; attested among Shalmaneser III's tributaries in 854. His non-Hittite name stands apart from the dynastic norm.
- **[Gap]**: at least one unattested ruler between Kundashpu and Suppiluliuma must be assumed given the chronological distance.
- **Suppiluliuma** (Assyrian *Ushpilulume*, attested 805–773): the best-documented Kummuhite king. He appears on the Pazarcık boundary-stone (RIMA 3: 205), erected by Adad-nirari III of Assyria in 805 to demarcate the border between Kummukh and Gurgum; the stone had been seized by Damascus' Bar-Hadad II and was restored to its position by the Assyrian commander Shamshi-ilu in 773, confirming Suppiluliuma was still on the throne then. His wife Panamuwati authored a hieroglyphic inscription (BOYBEYPINARI) recording her dedication of a throne and table to the goddess Kubaba.
- **Hattusili II** (mid 8th cent.): son of Suppiluliuma; named in three Ancoz inscriptions as his father's designated successor. An Ancoz curse formula pairs the names: "(Whoever) erases the name of Suppiluliuma and Hattusili, of the father and son, may the gods be the prosecutors against him!" (transl. Hawkins). No Assyrian records of his reign survive.
- **Kushtashpi** (c.750 —): next attested king; his non-Hittite name, like Kundashpu's, may reflect a succession irregularity. He was forced to become a tributary of Sarduri II of Urartu c.750 but was treated by Tiglath-pileser III as "a loyal vassal, not a rebel" (Tigl. III 168, transl. Tadmor) after Tiglath-pileser's defeat of the Urartian–Arpad coalition in 743 restored Assyrian hegemony.
- **Muwatalli** (Assyrian *Mutallu*, to 708): the last ruler of independent Kummukh. Initially loyal to Assyria — rewarded by Sargon II with the city of Malatya after the break-up of [[melid|Melid]]'s troublesome kingdom. But he subsequently fell foul of Sargon, who accused him of plotting with the Urartian king Argishti II. Sargon invaded, plundered, and deported much of the population to Babylonia. Muwatalli escaped, but Kummukh was annexed and made an Assyrian province.

**General posture.** Kummukh was unusual among the Neo-Hittite kingdoms for generally maintaining Assyrian loyalty. It received Assyrian diplomatic support in its disputes with neighbours. Bryce notes that "generally, Kummuh's rulers remained loyal to Assyria, and received some support from it in their disputes and conflicts with other states west of the Euphrates."

## Why It Mattered

Kummukh occupied the critical middle reach of the Euphrates between the two most prominent Neo-Hittite centres (Carchemish and Melid). Its stability as a loyal Assyrian tributary served Assyrian interests in keeping the upper Euphrates corridor open. Its possible dynastic continuity with the Hittite royal house gave it a legitimating prestige that outlasted its political independence: the Commagenian kingdom of the Hellenistic and Roman periods, which occupied the same territory and cultivated Persian and Greek royal ancestry, may have also drawn on older Hittite memory.

## Relationship to Assyria and Urartu

Kummukh was an Assyrian tributary from its first appearance in the record (c.866) and remained so, with one interruption, until its annexation in 708. The interruption came when Sarduri II of Urartu forced Kushtashpi into tributary status c.750 — a subjugation ended by Tiglath-pileser III's victory in 743. Thereafter Kummukh resumed its Assyrian tributary role, enjoying special favour under Sargon II until Muwatalli's suspected plot with Urartu triggered annexation.
