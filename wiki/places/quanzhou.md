# Quanzhou

Quanzhou (Ch'uan-chou / Zaytun) was the premier port of China and, according to contemporary observers Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta, the greatest port in the world during the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. Located on the Fujian coast facing the Taiwan Strait, it served as the eastern terminus of the maritime Indian Ocean circuits within the larger Afro-Eurasian world system.

## Geographic Description and Strategic Importance

Built on the shores of an enormous artificial lake (West Lake) connected by the Che River to the sea in the case of its sister city Hangzhou, Quanzhou's own harbor supported massive maritime traffic. It linked the northern overland Silk Road routes with the dominant Indian Ocean sea route. The port connected directly to the Malacca Strait entrepôts, Indian ports (Gujarat/Cambay, Calicut), and onward to the Persian Gulf and Red Sea.

## Role in the 13th-Century World System

Quanzhou exported silk and porcelain—China's most demanded industrial goods—and absorbed silver and other bullion. Chinese advances in paper manufacture, printing, siderurgy (iron/steel using coal), and state-managed paper money supported large-scale production and exchange. The city exemplified the "agro-urban" settlements tied to rural silk production organized through peasant family units and guilds.

Italians and Arabs knew it as Zaytun. Both Polo and Battuta ranked it above all others. It anchored the Far Eastern subsystem and, through relay networks via Malacca and Indian intermediaries, supplied the Mamluk and European circuits with high-value manufactures.

## Control and Historical Trajectory

Under the Southern Song and especially the Yuan (Mongol) dynasty, Quanzhou thrived as foreign merchants (Arab, Persian, Italian) were welcomed. After the Mongol period, Ming policy shifts, combined with the broader post-1350 shocks (Black Death, loss of Mongol security on overland complements, internal economic strains), led to withdrawal from large-scale maritime projection. The "Fall of the East" devolution in the system hit Chinese port activity hard; Quanzhou's peak role was not sustained into the era of European expansion.

## Historiography and Collection Note

Primary testimony comes from travelers (Polo, Battuta) whose descriptions of scale and activity are cross-referenced in economic histories. Abu-Lughod (1989) synthesizes these with Chinese sources to place Quanzhou as the eastern "core" of the polycentric 13th-c system rather than a periphery. Later archaeology and Ming records nuance the scale and the reasons for Ming naval retrenchment. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina holds strong complementary material via traveler translations and Cambridge World History volumes on exchange networks.
