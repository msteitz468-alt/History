# Alexandria

**Alexandria** ("Alexandria by Egypt"), founded by [[alexander-the-great|Alexander]] in
331 BCE on the Mediterranean coast west of the Nile Delta, became the capital of
[[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic Egypt]] and the greatest city of the
[[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic world]] — a cosmopolitan port and the supreme centre of
Greek learning. CAH VII.1 ch. 5 (E. G. Turner) treats its rise under the first three
Ptolemies.

## Geographic Description and Plan

Alexander "read the military lessons right": the site gave Egypt **a door to the west** —
a secure naval base and a two-way conduit of goods and ideas — while removing the
hiding-places that had sheltered earlier rebels against foreign rule. Laid out on a
**Hippodamian grid**, the city was divided into five districts (lettered Alpha to
Epsilon), with full citizenship restricted to Greeks and Macedonians (organised in
demes) and a large, mixed population including the most important **Jewish** community
of the diaspora. Throughout its history Alexandria remained legally **set apart from
Egypt** — a Greek *polis* with its own dependent territory, not part of the Egyptian
*chora*.

## The Wonders of the City

Under [[ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Ptolemy II]] and [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]],
Alexandria acquired the monuments that made it "a wonder of the world":

- **The Pharos** — the great fenestrated lighthouse, surmounted by a fire-basket and a
  statue of Zeus Soter, guiding ships into the artificial harbours of a dangerous coast;
  one of the Seven Wonders.
- **The tomb of Alexander** — the dynasty's talisman, whose body Ptolemy I had diverted
  to Egypt; later enshrined in a magnificent mausoleum.
- **The Serapeum** — the great temple of [[sarapis|Sarapis]], the dynasty's
  Graeco-Egyptian god, completed by Ptolemy III Euergetes.

## The Museum and the Library

Alexandria's "crowning glory" (Turner) was its scholarly establishment: the **Museum**
(*Mouseion*, "shrine of the Muses") — modelled on Aristotle's Lyceum but going further, a
community of scholars **funded entirely by the Ptolemies** and devoted to **research rather
than teaching** (CAH VII.1 ch. 9, Lloyd) — and the great **Library** (with a "secondary"
library, traditionally at the Serapeum). Here the "worship of the Muses with the head rather
than the heart" produced both **definitive advances in [[hellenistic-science|science]]**
(Euclid's geometry; Eratosthenes' measurement of the earth; the anatomists Herophilus and
Erasistratus, who under royal patronage practised human dissection) and the **invention of
methodical scholarship** — the line-by-line commentary (*hypomnema*), textual criticism, and
bibliography. Its scholar-librarians ran from Zenodotus through
Eratosthenes and Aristophanes of Byzantium to Aristarchus; **Callimachus** of Cyrene,
though probably never titular librarian, catalogued the collection in his monumental
*Pinakes*. The poets **Theocritus** (the *Idylls*), Callimachus, and **Apollonius of
Rhodes** (the *Argonautica*) defined "Alexandrianism" — learned, allusive, miniature.
Here too the **Septuagint** — the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures — was made
for the city's Hellenised Jews.

## Why Alexandria Mattered

- **The hinge of Ptolemaic power**: royal capital, chief port, naval arsenal, and mint —
  the engine of the dynasty's Mediterranean reach.
- **The intellectual capital of antiquity**: the Museum and Library set the model of
  state-patronised research and the methods of philology and exact science that descend,
  via Rome and Byzantium and the Arabic translators, into the modern world.
- **A cosmopolitan crucible**: Greek, Egyptian, and Jewish communities in close (if
  legally separated) proximity — and the birthplace of the [[sarapis|Sarapis]] cult that
  spread across the Mediterranean.

## Changing Significance

From Ptolemaic capital, Alexandria passed under Rome (30 BCE) as the second city of the
empire and remained a great centre of learning, Hellenistic Judaism, and later
Christian theology into Late Antiquity. See [[ptolemaic-egypt]], [[hellenistic-science]],
[[hellenistic-world]].
