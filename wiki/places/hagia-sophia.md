# Hagia Sophia

The **Great Church of Holy Wisdom** at Constantinople — the supreme monument of the **age of
[[justinian|Justinian]]** and the masterpiece of late-antique architecture, dedicated in **537** and for
nearly a thousand years the **largest church in Christendom**. Its vast dome on pendentives became the model
of Byzantine sacred building and the architectural emblem of the eastern empire's "spectacular prosperity
and splendour." Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 31 (building and architecture).

## Building the Great Church

The first churches on the site (Constantinian, then Theodosian) were destroyed in the **Nika revolt of
532**; from the ashes Justinian commissioned an entirely new and unprecedented structure. Its architects —
exceptionally, **named** and celebrated, unlike most ancient builders — were **Anthemius of Tralles**, "a
prominent mathematician," and **Isidore of Miletus**, also a man of science. The church rose with
extraordinary speed and was **dedicated in 537**. Its daringly shallow, broad **dome** partially collapsed
after earthquakes and was **rebuilt, higher and stronger, by Isidore the Younger** (the nephew) in **558**.
**Procopius**, in his *Buildings*, gave the classic description of its light-filled interior, the dome
seeming "suspended from heaven by a golden chain."

## Architecture and meaning

Hagia Sophia fused the Roman engineering of **vault and dome** (developed in the bath-chamber and basilica)
with a new monumental scale, carrying a circular dome over a square nave by means of **pendentives** — a
solution of lasting influence. Sheathed in coloured marbles and gold mosaic, it embodied the late-antique
theology of the church as an image of heaven (contemporaries "imagined heaven as a palace," and the Great
Church as its earthly counterpart). It stood at the ceremonial heart of the capital, beside the **Imperial
Palace**, the **Augustaion**, the law courts and the patriarchate — the meeting point of **imperial and
ecclesiastical power** that defined the Justinianic state.

## Context: building in the age of Justinian

CAH XIV sets the church within two broad trends: a **decline of monumental building in the West** (Rome's
mid-fifth-century church "boom" — Santa Sabina, Santa Maria Maggiore — gave way to little after; Ravenna's
building ended with the Ostrogothic kingdom) against a **surge in the East**, where Justinian's programme
(recorded by Procopius over forty years) covered the empire with churches, fortifications and new cities
(Dara, Justiniana Prima). Hagia Sophia is the apex of this eastern efflorescence — proof that late-antique
society under "effective imperial control" still built on a fully ancient scale.

## Significance

Hagia Sophia is the **architectural statement of Justinian's reign and of Byzantium itself** — the visible
counterpart of the [[codification-of-roman-law|codification of Roman law]] and the reconquest, the claim
that the eastern empire was the true heir of Rome and the seat of orthodox Christianity. It shaped Orthodox
church design for a millennium, was converted to a mosque after the Ottoman conquest of 1453 (its dome the
model for Ottoman imperial mosques), and remains the defining image of the late-antique East.
