# Norway

A mountainous, fjord-cut kingdom whose population clustered along the western and
southern coasts and the Trøndelag. Poorer in arable land than Denmark or Sweden,
medieval Norway drew strength from fisheries, the [[bergen|Bergen]] stockfish
trade, and command of the North Atlantic island communities (the *skattlönd* /
tributary lands: Iceland, Greenland, Faroes, Orkney, Shetland).

## Significance

Tradition credits [[harald-fairhair|Harald Fairhair]] with the first unification
(c. 900); the kingdom was consolidated and Christianized under
[[olaf-tryggvason|Olaf Tryggvason]] and [[olaf-haraldsson|St Olaf Haraldsson]],
whose cult at Nidaros (Trondheim) became the kingdom's spiritual anchor. The 13th
century under Hákon Hákonarson and Magnus the Lawmender brought a unified law and
the incorporation of [[iceland|Iceland]] and Greenland. Norway entered the
[[kalmar-union-1397|Kalmar Union]] as the weakest of the three crowns.

## Medieval Norway in Detail (Cambridge History)

The most geographically distinctive and, by the late Middle Ages, demographically
weakest kingdom. Population rose from c. **185,000** at the end of the Viking Age to
c. **345,000** by c. 1330, with settlement pushed to 800 m altitude in the south.
**No manorial demesne system** developed; the Church owned c. 40% of land by value,
the nobility c. 20%, the crown c. 7%, freeholders c. 33%. From the 12th century the
**Lofoten/Vesterålen cod fishery** and the [[bergen|Bergen]] **stockfish** trade
anchored the economy, but western Norway became dependent on Hanseatic-imported Baltic
grain from the later 13th century.

Politically, the customary right of any king's son to claim the throne produced the
**Norwegian Civil Wars (1130–1240)**, resolved by [[sverre-sigurdsson|Sverre]] and the
Birkibeinar and ended under his descendants. [[hakon-hakonarson|Hákon Hákonarson]]
secured papal coronation (1247), hereditary succession (1260), and incorporated
[[iceland|Iceland]] and Greenland; his son **Magnus Lagabøte** gave Norway the
**Landlaw (1274)** and **Townlaw (1276)** and renamed the *hirð* ranks barons and
knights (1277). Royal revenue was small and rested on unpaid popular services and the
retained [[leidang|naval levy]] — which left the Norwegian peasantry comparatively
strong. The [[black-death-scandinavia-1349|Black Death]] then devastated Norway
(c. 60–65% mortality), disintegrating the service nobility and making Norway the weak
partner in the [[kalmar-union-1397|union]]. See
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003]].
