## Geographic Description

Petra sits in a narrow sandstone basin in southern Jordan, reached through the defile of
the Siq, on the trade corridor linking the south Arabian incense-producing regions to the
Mediterranean and the Nile. The site's rose-red sandstone cliffs were carved into rock-cut
tomb façades and shrines, and the surrounding terrain is dotted with dozens of open-air
"high places" — rock-cut altars reached by rooftop staircases, a tradition Ball traces to
Mesopotamian ziggurat precedent and finds especially concentrated in Nabataean religious
architecture (Petra's high places and the comparable site of Khirbet Tannur).

## History of Control and Settlement

According to Ball, the [[nabataean-kingdom|Nabataeans]] gradually and largely peacefully
displaced or absorbed the earlier Edomite population of southern Jordan between the 6th and
4th centuries BC, establishing a base at Petra by the late 4th century BC. The city
prospered chiefly from the south Arabian incense trade, with Dead Sea asphalt as a
secondary resource, and repelled a siege by the Macedonian general Demetrius Poliorcetes
("the Besieger") in 312 BC. Petra became sedentary from the 1st century BC.

Petra was the Nabataean capital through the kingdom's rise, reaching its greatest
prosperity and territorial extent under Harithath (Aretas) IV "lover of his people" (8
BC–AD 40), whose realm extended into the Sinai, southern Negev, Jordan, the Hauran, the
Wadi Sirhan to Jawf, and the Red Sea coast to Hegra. Ball attributes Petra's major
monuments — the theatre, the Temple of Dushara (Qasr al-Bint), and probably the Khazneh —
to Harithath IV's reign, and argues Petra's architecture, though it blends Hellenistic,
Roman, Egyptian, Assyrian, and Persian elements, should properly be classified as
"Nabataean" rather than "Roman," since virtually all major building there predates the AD
106 Roman annexation.

Rabbel II moved the Nabataean capital from Petra to [[bosra|Bosra]] in 93 CE, and after
Rabbel's death in 106 Trajan annexed the kingdom to form the Roman Province of Arabia.
Building and embellishment at Petra continued after annexation without any discernible
break in continuity, even though Bosra (not Petra) held provincial-capital status.
Decline was gradual thereafter: a major 4th-century earthquake caused significant damage,
followed by slow contraction through the 4th–6th centuries as southern Jordan became a
Roman-Byzantine backwater.

## Why It Mattered

Petra's wealth rested on control of the overland incense trade from south Arabia; Ball
notes this land-based trade declined over the 1st century AD as Roman-controlled sea
routes via Egypt became competitive, and south Arabian trading kingdoms correspondingly
shifted orientation toward the Red Sea (highland Himyar over the older inland desert
kingdoms of Ma'in, Saba, and Qataban). Despite this shift, Nabataean hydrological and
agricultural expertise allowed Petra to keep flourishing for a time; Ball observes it was
ultimately [[palmyra|Palmyra]], not Nabataea, that captured the main benefit of the
resulting shift of trade routes northward.

Religiously, Petra was a major center of Nabataean worship of the god Dushara, represented
abstractly as a plain square block of stone — one of many "god-blocks" and cuboid
sculptural motifs at the site. Ball connects this cuboid tradition to an ancient
pan-Semitic pattern of aniconic deity representation running from Bronze Age Near Eastern
baetyls through Phoenician cube-tophets at Carthage and South Arabian temple architecture
(Marib, Sirwah) to the Ka'ba at Mecca, describing this only as an analogy, not a claim of
direct historical descent.

## Monumental Architecture and Its Reinterpretation

Ball argues the famous rock-cut tomb façades (the Khazneh, the Deir, the Corinthian Tomb,
the Palace Tomb, the Urn Tomb) have been systematically misread as building fronts implying
substantial interiors, when for several — notably the Khazneh and the Deir — there is
little or nothing carved behind the façade. Citing the Turkmaniyah Tomb inscription (which
describes a courtyard, benches, a triclinium, water wells, and "acts of consecration"
watched over by "Dushara and his throne and all the gods"), Ball argues the façades
functioned as theatrical backdrops, akin to a Classical scaenae frons, for ritual performed
in the courtyard before them — with tomb function secondary to this stage-setting role.

Petra's baroque, Classical-influenced façades are, in Ball's account, only one of two
architectural categories at the site; a larger group of simpler façades shows
predominantly native Semitic/Arabian features (Assyrian-style crow-step merlons,
Egyptian-style flaring cornices, Nabataean pilasters), found also at Medain Saleh (Hegra).
Ball further notes that rock-cut tomb façades are not typical of Nabataean architecture
generally — occurring only at Petra and Medain Saleh, nowhere else in Nabataean territory
(Jordan, the Hauran, the Negev).

## Fisher (2020): Metropolis, defensibility, and later continuity

Fisher (*Rome, Persia, and Arabia*, 2020) calls Petra, per Strabo, "metropolis of the
Nabataeans" and confirms recent archaeology places the site's occupation to the 4th–3rd
century BCE. He emphasizes Petra's "combination of inaccessibility and defensibility": the
Macedonian commanders Antigonus the One-Eyed and his son Demetrius "the Besieger" both
campaigned against the Nabataeans there without success in the late 4th century BCE,
Demetrius reportedly abandoning the siege after Nabataean envoys argued that subjugating a
people so committed to mobility and desert self-sufficiency was pointless (Diodorus
19.94–98). The main entrance, the siq — a narrow vertical rock cleft — physically embodies
this defensibility.

Petra was multicultural religiously (Isis and the Egyptian pantheon alongside Dushara and
al-Uzza) and remained economically and politically significant well past the 106 CE Roman
annexation into the 6th century CE, by which point it was used as a place of exile for
troublesome bishops (attested in the Petra Church papyri); one Roman governor of Provincia
Arabia, Sextius Florentinus, chose Petra as his burial place. Fisher also flags that
current research locates part of the development of the Arabic script — significant for
the later Quranic text — within former Nabataean territory centered on the Petra region.

## Historiography

Scholars disagree over the function of Petra's high places. Per Ball, the traditional
interpretation reads them as sites of blood sacrifice, citing a Mada'in Saleh inscription
referring to a young man "immolated" to a deity and channels near altars interpreted as
blood-drainage; Ball characterizes this reading as over-argued from thin evidence. An
alternative interpretation, first proposed in the 1960s and, per Ball, generally poorly
received since, holds that some high places and monumental tomb façades functioned as
exposure platforms (dakhmas) and ossuaries for secondary burial, by analogy with
Zoroastrian/Sasanian Iranian practice (astadans) — citing Strabo's report that Nabataean
kings' bodies were "left on refuse heaps after death" and possible links to King Maliku I's
pro-Parthian leanings. Ball treats this exposure hypothesis as speculative but
underappreciated, noting that actual burial practice at Petra likely varied (ordinary
burial, burial with lime, possible exposure, partial cremation, cremation). This is a
live scholarly disagreement rather than a settled fact, and the wiki records both readings
without adjudicating between them; see [[orientalization-of-rome-thesis]] for Ball's wider
interpretive framework treating Petra as evidence of Near Eastern cultural continuity
under a thin Graeco-Roman veneer, a position mainstream scholarship (e.g. Fergus Millar,
*The Roman Near East*, 1993) reads more conservatively.
