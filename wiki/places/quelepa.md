# Quelepa

## Overview

**Quelepa** in eastern El Salvador is Payson Sheets’s (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 9) key case for how the **southeastern Mesoamerican frontier** shifted after the **Ilopango** disaster and during Classic reintegration with Gulf Coast–Maya networks. Founded **~400 B.C. or shortly after** (Andrews 1976), it shows continuous Mesoamerican **core-blade obsidian technology** alongside strong local and Intermediate Area ties.

## Late Formative foundation

- Mud-plaster/pumice platforms refurbished multiple times; abundant manos, metates, and comales indicate an agricultural base.
- Massive monolithic **“Jaguar Altar”** (~3 × 3 m) with Cara Sucia–style face shows strong stylistic links to **[[izapa|Izapan]]** and **[[kaminaljuyu|Kaminaljuyú]]** monuments.
- **Usulután** multiple-applicator resist pottery dominates; external links to western El Salvador, highland Guatemala, and Honduras.
- Andrews proposed a Late Formative **Uapala ceramic sphere** for eastern El Salvador and southern/western Honduras (Quelepa, [[copan|Copán]], Los Naranjos, Lo de Vaca II, Yarumela, Santa Rita)—possibly reflecting **Lenca** separation from Maya/Macro-Maya ~two millennia ago. Weeks, Black & Speaker (with Campbell) treat Quelepa and Usulután makers as likely prehistoric Lenca. Protohistoric Lenca lived in competitive chiefdoms (settlements of 2,000–4,000 with nearby fortified hilltop retreats; no shared azimuth/grid).

## Classic isolation and Shila phase (A.D. 150–625)

Eastern El Salvador was largely **spared direct Ilopango tephra** (winds to west/north) but **cut off from western El Salvador and Guatemala**, shifting affiliation toward Honduras and the Intermediate Area—making eastern El Salvador a **Mesoamerican frontier in the Early and Middle Classic**. Quelepa’s **Shila phase** saw large terraces, paved ramps, specialized structures, cut- and undressed-stone masonry, and construction apex; ceramics closer to Los Naranjos and Copán than to western El Salvador; more Intermediate Area traits (carved-legged metates, stone ball sets of three, small jadeite beads possibly Costa Rican).

## Lepa phase (A.D. 625–1000): site-unit intrusion

The prior ritual zone was abandoned for smaller structures around a rectangular plaza. Andrews sees **Mesoamerican (especially Gulf Coast) influence** linked to Travesía and Terminal Classic Seibal: staircases replace ramps; masonry **ballcourt**; Usulután replaced by white-slipped polychrome; cache of **ballgame paraphernalia** (yokes, palmas, hacha); flutes with rolling pellets and wheeled toys = Gulf Coast connections. Andrews argues a **site-unit intrusion**—sudden arrival of a package with no local antecedents. Contemporary **Los Llanitos** also has an I-shaped ballcourt. By Late Classic the frontier shifted farther east toward the **Choluteca River** as Quelepa and peers resumed fuller Mesoamerican participation.
