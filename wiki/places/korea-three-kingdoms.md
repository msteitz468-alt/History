# Korea — The Three Kingdoms

## Overview

The **Three Kingdoms** — **Koguryŏ**, **Paekche**, and **Silla** — are the formative polities
of Korean history, emerging from the fourth century CE and culminating in **Silla's
unification of the peninsula in 676 CE**. In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Holcombe, Ch 15)
Korea is a key case of **secondary state formation** within the
**[[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian cultural sphere]]**: shaped by Chinese contact yet
remaining culturally distinct.

## From Chinese Commanderies to Native Kingdoms

After the **[[han-dynasty|Han]]** conquered northern Korea in **109 BCE**, four centuries of
Chinese **commandery** rule (notably **Lelang**, near modern Pyongyang) created a regional
"interaction sphere" that catalyzed state formation. As Chinese administration disintegrated
in the early fourth century CE, native kingdoms crystallized:

- **Koguryŏ** — the northern, partly **Manchurian** power; source of the English name
  "Korea" (via *Koryŏ*); learned cavalry warfare from the Xianbei.
- **Paekche** — the southwestern kingdom; closely tied to the Chinese **Southern dynasties**
  and to **Japan**, and the conduit through which **[[buddhism|Buddhism]]** passed to Japan
  (538/552 CE).
- **Silla** — the southeastern kingdom; latest to Sinicize but ultimately victorious.

## Unification

Allying with the **[[tang-dynasty|Tang]]**, Silla destroyed **Paekche (660)** — annihilating
a relieving **Japanese** fleet in 663 and ending major Japanese involvement on the
continent — and **Koguryŏ (668)**, then **expelled its Tang allies by 676**, unifying most of
the peninsula under native rule for the first time. The northern third was long held by
**Parhae (713–926)**, a successor to Koguryŏ.

## A Distinct Culture

Though adopting Chinese law codes, reign periods, Buddhism, and (increasingly) Confucianism,
the Korean kingdoms remained distinct: **bilateral kinship** (descent traced through both
parents), a powerful aristocracy graded by **"bone ranks,"** and distinctive royal tombs and
gold crowns. Korea remained an **autonomous "tributary"** of the Chinese empire into the
nineteenth century.

## Significance

The Three Kingdoms exemplify CWH IV's theme of **secondary state formation** on an imperial
periphery and the selective, adaptive incorporation of Chinese culture that built the East
Asian sphere while preserving a separate Korean identity.

## The archaeological deep background (Barnes)

[[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015|Barnes (2015)]] supplies the archaeological underpinning the
chronicle-based account lacks. She traces the kingdoms back through the [[mumun-culture|Mumun]] and
[[samhan|Samhan]] (Three Han) substrate and the iron-trading [[yellow-sea-interaction-sphere|Yellow Sea
sphere]] centred on the [[lelang-commandery|Lelang commandery]], and dates statehood by the **debut of
monumental tomb-building (mid-to-late 3rd century AD)** rather than the native foundation dates
(57/37/18 BC). Detailed analyses now exist for each polity: [[koguryo|Koguryo]] (two-capital system,
mural tombs, Kwanggaeto stele), [[paekche|Paekche]] (Pungnap/Mongchon, the Yamato alliance and
seven-branched sword), [[silla|Silla]] (un-lootable gold tombs, bone-rank system, Tang-backed
unification), the [[kaya|Kaya]] confederacy (iron and Sué-ware to Japan), and ancestral
[[puyo|Puyo]]. She frames all of these as **secondary states** (see
[[pen-insular-state-formation]]) and addresses the modern Koguryo and keyhole-tomb
controversies directly. **Source:** [[barnes-archaeology-east-asia-2015]].

## Historiography

The period is reconstructed from later Korean chronicles (the *Samguk Sagi*), Chinese
sources, and archaeology (the differing tomb styles of the three kingdoms; the Dong Shou
tomb's hybrid Chinese-Xianbei-Koguryŏ culture). CWH IV warns against reading modern
nationalist "Chinese" or "Korean" categories back into this cosmopolitan, border-blurring
age.
