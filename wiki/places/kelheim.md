# Kelheim

Kelheim is the site of a major Late Iron Age **oppidum** (large fortified settlement of the final prehistoric Iron Age) in Lower Bavaria, Germany, at the confluence of the Altmühl River and the Danube. The enclosed area is about 600 hectares on the Michelsberg limestone plateau and the Mitterfeld valley below; the western rampart is 3.28 km long. Occupation dates from about the middle of the second century B.C. until the middle of the first century B.C.

## Fortifications and Construction

The western wall, an inner wall, and a Danube-bank wall were built with tree trunks ~60 cm in diameter sunk at intervals of 2 m or less; the front was faced with limestone slabs 5–6 m high, backed by an earth rampart. Construction estimates: more than 8,000 trees felled, 25,000 cubic meters of limestone quarried, and 400,000 cubic meters of earth moved.

On the plateau, ~6,000 mining pits (many Late Iron Age) reached limonite iron ore; smelting furnaces were associated.

## Settlement and Economy

Settlement evidence is densest on the Mitterfeld below the plateau: postholes, storage pits, wells, wall plaster. It is a typical Late La Tène settlement, comparable to Manching. Abundant iron slag, furnace bottoms, tools (axes, anvils, chisels, awls, nails, brooches, spearheads), bronze ornaments (including a finely crafted vulture head), wheel-made painted pottery, graphite-clay cooking pots, storage vessels, spindle whorls, glass manufacture debris, bronze/silver coins and casting mold attest to specialized production.

Subsistence: barley, spelt wheat, millet, peas; pigs and cattle. The site was actively engaged in Late Iron Age commercial systems; copper, tin, raw glass, and graphite-clay were imported. Roman imports include a bronze wine jug, sieve, and dolphin attachment.

Population estimate (educated guess, lacking burial evidence): 500–2,000 people. Landscape survey indicates surrounding farm and village populations moved into the oppidum in the second century B.C. The oppidum was abandoned around the mid-first century B.C., like many east of the Rhine.

## Interpretation

Kelheim exemplifies the oppida of the Late La Tène period—large population centers with craft specialization, coinage, writing use, and involvement in interregional trade networks, protected by massive fortifications, supported by hinterlands, before the Roman occupation and Germanic pressures.

**Grounded claims from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004, Range 04 (cache lines ~618–780):**
"From about the middle of the second century until the middle of the first century B.C., Kelheim was the site of an oppidum... The area enclosed by this wall and the two rivers is about 600 hectares..." (verbatim: "Kelheim, a city with a population of about fifteen thousand, is situated at the confluence...") 
"Tree trunks about 60 centimeters in diameter were sunk into the ground... Estimates suggest that more than eight thousand trees were felled..." 
"On the Michelsberg plateau... some six thousand pits have been identified... iron-mining pits..."
"Pieces of ore, slag, and furnace bottoms occur over much of the settlement... bronze was cast, and glass ornaments made."
"The pottery assemblage is typical of the major oppidum settlements... A number of bronze and silver coins have been recovered..."
"Like all of the major oppida, the community at Kelheim was actively involved in the commercial systems of Late Iron Age Europe."
"As at most of the oppida in Late Iron Age Europe, few graves have been found at Kelheim... an educated guess might put the size of Late Iron Age Kelheim at between five hundred and two thousand people."
"Around the middle of the first century B.C., the oppidum was abandoned, like many others east of the Rhine..."
