# Eridu (Tell Abu Shahrain)

The southernmost of the Sumerian cities and, in Sumerian tradition, **the first
city of all** — where "kingship descended from heaven." Its excavated **sequence of
eighteen superimposed temples** is the single most important demonstration of the
continuity of cult and the temple-centred origins of the Mesopotamian city, and
the type-site that gives the **Ubaid period its earliest pottery phase ("Eridu
ware")**. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. VIII,
Mallowan).

## Geographic Setting

Eridu lies at the desert edge of the southern alluvium, near the ancient head of
the Persian Gulf marshlands. Mallowan notes that wind-blown **sand** plagued the
site from its very first settlement — the earliest building stood on a sand dune —
to the Third Dynasty of Ur, when the unfinished ziggurat was abandoned to the
advancing dunes. The environment is the [[postglacial-environment-near-east|young
deltaic landscape]] in which Sumerian urbanism arose.

## The Ubaid Pottery Sequence

Eridu is the type-site for the start of the Ubaid:
- **Eridu ware = Ubaid 1** (the deepest decorated pottery),
- followed by **Hajji Muhammad ware = Ubaid 2** (named from a site near Warka),
- then **Ubaid 3–4**, leading into the Uruk period.
CAH adopts the convention of applying "Ubaid" to the whole of this long sequence.

## The Eighteen Temples

Beneath the ziggurat built by **Amar-Sin** of Ur III (c. 2047–2039 BCE), Mallowan's
excavators found **eighteen building levels** in a 12-metre sounding, separated by
clearly marked strata of wind-blown sand that mark the lapse of time between each
temple's abandonment and the next rebuilding — "additional proof of the continuity
of life in the centre of the city from the earliest 'Ubaid until the Uruk period."

- **Levels 18–15**: the earliest shrines, beginning as a small rectangular room
  with internal piers (level 17) — the first temple plan known to southern
  Mesopotamia.
- **Levels 14–12**: fragmentary (the main temple site shifted).
- **Levels 11–9**, then **8–6**: progressively elaborated temples with offering
  tables and side chambers; level 6/7 temples (end of the Ubaid proper) already
  show the **niched-and-buttressed façade, central cella with altar and offering
  table** that define the canonical Mesopotamian temple. Quantities of **fish
  offerings** were found — fitting Eridu's later identity as the seat of **Enki/Ea**,
  god of the freshwater Apsu.
- **Levels 5–1**: Uruk and Jemdet Nasr periods.

The sequence shows the temple growing *in place*, by continuous rebuilding on
consecrated ground, across many centuries — the archaeological backbone of the
argument that the Sumerian city crystallized around its temple.

**From Sumerian Gilgamesh poems (ll. 7001–10000)**: In "Gilgamesh and the Netherworld", when the ball falls, Gilgamesh goes "alone to Eridu, the house of Ea" and weeps: "O Father Enki, my ball fell into the Netherworld..."; Enki helps, instructing Utu to open Netherworld for Enkidu's shade (ll. 9018–8672). This **part_of** Eridu's role as seat of Enki/Ea in ED legendary tradition.

## Significance

Eridu supplies the **deep-time, single-site** demonstration of what
[[uruk|Uruk]] shows at full urban scale: that Mesopotamian cities grew out of
long-lived **cult centres**. Together the two sites frame the
[[urban-revolution|urban revolution]] and the Ubaid→Uruk trajectory of the
[[chalcolithic|Chalcolithic]].

## Historiography

Mallowan's account rests on the Iraqi/British excavations of the late 1940s; dates
are uncalibrated and the "temple" identification of the earliest levels is an
inference from plan continuity. The *stratigraphic sequence* is the durable
contribution. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]] and the dating caveats
in [[ancient-near-eastern-chronology]].
