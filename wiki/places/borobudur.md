# Borobudur

**Borobudur** is the great Early Classic **Mahayana Buddhist** monument of central Java—a stepped mandala-mountain of narrative reliefs and stupas built under [[sailendra|Sailendra]] patronage within the [[mataram-kingdom|Mataram]] polity (late eighth–early ninth century CE). It stands on a low hill in the **Kedu plain** near Gunung Wukir, on the opposite side of Merapi from most other central Javanese monuments. Primary synthesis: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]].

## Geographic Description

The monument occupies a natural hilltop levelled for construction. In the mid-eighth century two terraces incorporating perspective effects typical of **Hindu** architecture were begun, then abandoned; work resumed that converted the structure to a **Buddhist** monument of three square terraces, two round terraces, and a central stupa. The original design framework is Javanese. The terraced-pyramid model was later adapted in Cambodia at **Roluos** and [[angkor|Angkor]]—an architectural analogy across the Early–Middle Classic mainland, not proof of direct political control.

## History of Construction and Control

No record of the founding, the sponsor, or even the monument’s **original name** has been found. An inscription dated **824 CE** mentions that King **Samaratuṅga** built a “place for the conqueror Buddhas” divided into ten parts. An inscription of **842** says Queen Śrī Kahulunan granted *sima* status to a village to support a sanctuary named **Bhūmisambhāra** (“mountain of the accumulation of the ten merits/perfections”)—the name most scholars associate with Borobudur.

Construction thus falls under Sailendra-period Mataram kingship, with possible revisions as Mahayana ideology evolved and access was restricted. Later east Javanese and Sumatran Buddhists remembered the relief tradition: Candi Jago (~1280) revived narrative reliefs “like those carved at Borobudur and Prambanan in the ninth century,” including torments of the damned already depicted at Early Classic Borobudur. Similarities between Borobudur’s Buddha-life reliefs and Tibetan monastery paintings have been attributed to **Atīśa**’s transmission—interpretive, not proven site-to-site copying.

## Why It Mattered

Miksic and Goh summarize four simultaneous identities:

1. a multi-tiered terraced structure like prehistoric Indonesian religious sites;
2. a **mandala** with concentric rows of sculptures representing Buddhist deities;
3. a symbol of royal might composed of **more than two million** large stone blocks;
4. a device for inculcating wisdom through **1,350 narrative reliefs**.

Originally, visitors could see **160** base reliefs of the *Mahākarmavibhaṅga* (“Great Exposition of the Law of Karma”); these had to be covered early because the structure was too narrow. Galleries present Jātakas, Avadānas, the *Lalitavistara*, the *Gaṇḍavyūha* (**460** panels), and the *Bhadracarī* (a unique series). Completing all 1,350 reliefs in order requires **ten circumambulations**, analogizing the ten bodhisattva stages. On the three upper terraces, **62** hollow stupas each contain a Buddha in *dharmacakra mudrā*.

In the *Gaṇḍavyūha* cycle—central to Borobudur—the hero Sudhana goes to meet Mahādeva (Śiva) at **Dvāravatī**, a textual link to the mythic name also used for the [[dvaravati|Dvaravati]] culture of Thailand.

## Design Debates

Some scholars reject the theory that Borobudur was designed as a mandala, citing lack of *vajra* symbolism and a distribution of Buddhas that does not match any known pattern. Counter-arguments: lost literature may have held the key; the *Sang Hyang Kamahāyānikan* refers to the six types of Buddha images found at Borobudur. Miksic and Goh conclude Borobudur was “probably not designed only as a mandala; it had several themes.”

## Control History and Afterlife

After the early tenth-century collapse of central Java Mataram, the plain lost political primacy; the monument endured as a landscape feature, later buried in volcanic and vegetal cover until modern clearance and conservation. Its reliefs were the **first** of three great Early Classic narrative series (followed by [[prambanan|Prambanan]] and, in east Java, Jalatunda as the first east Javanese series—still three-dimensional, not yet Late Classic flat style).

## Historiography and Source Notes

Evidence is archaeological-architectural plus two key ninth-century inscriptions; no foundation charter survives. Controversies include original name, degree of mandala intentionality, sequence of Hindu-then-Buddhist phases, and identification of Samaratuṅga/Kahulunan passages with this specific hill. Comparative arguments (Angkor terraced pyramids, Atīśa, later Jago) are structural or diffusionist hypotheses.
