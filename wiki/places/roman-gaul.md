# Roman Gaul

The vast territory conquered by Julius Caesar — "some 30 per cent of the empire's land area
apart from Italy" — whose acquisition "fundamentally shifted the balance of the Roman world"
from the Mediterranean toward central Europe and the Rhine. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]]
Ch. 13d (**C. Goudineau**).

## "Gaul" or the Gauls?

Goudineau's framing argument is that there was **no "Gaul"** except as ancient conceptual
geography: the sources always speak of the **Gauls (*Galliae*)**, and sharply distinguish
**Narbonensis** (the old Transalpina, a Mediterranean land long Roman) from the **Tres Galliae**
/ *Gallia Comata* (Aquitania, Lugdunensis, Belgica — the "new world" of the harsh north). Even
*Comata* was no real unity: its peoples identified with their **civitas** (Remi, Aedui,
Pictones…), not with Gaul or province, and in the crisis of 68–69 "could not agree on a common
policy" through old rivalries. What unity existed was political, supplied by the **imperial
cult** at the **altar of Condate** (founded by Drusus, 12 BCE, at the confluence of Saône and
Rhône near Lugdunum), where delegates of the sixty peoples met annually each 1 August.

## Caesar's legacy and Julio-Claudian loyalty

Caesar's death (44 BCE) brought short-lived disturbances (the Allobroges' expulsion of the
Vienne colonists, leading to the founding of **Lugdunum/Lyons**, 43), contained only through the
**personal clientelae** Caesar had built and his lieutenants and heir inherited. Goudineau's
theme: because **Caesar's direct descendants ruled for over a century**, continuing loyalties,
the Rhine campaigns (in which the imperial family took part), imperial visits, and even imperial
births in Gaul **neutralized the old senatorial "anti-Gallic" hostility** (still strong under
Claudius). The relationship was two-way: the Gallic elites stayed faithful to the house of
Caesar, which kept faith with them. Goudineau cautions that "Romanization" is the wrong main
theme — the Gallic provinces *are* Roman; the task is to study the **transformations** and their
rhythm.

## Narbonensis: "Italia verius quam provincia"

The transformation is captured by contrasting Cicero's *pro Fonteio* (c. 70 BCE, a province of
wild tribes) with **Pliny's** verdict (c. AD 70) that Narbonensis was "**more truly Italy than a
province**." Its **juridical integration** ran fast: Caesar granted **Latin status** to the
whole of southern Gaul (58–44 BCE) and a colonial programme (refounding Narbo, founding Arles,
the Latin colonies), continued by Octavian (Béziers, Orange, Fréjus). The province was "returned
to the Roman people" (c. 22 BCE), and in **AD 14** Augustus and Tiberius granted its Roman
citizens the **right to stand for magistracies at Rome** — opening the senatorial order. The
result: Narbonensis **produced more equestrians and senators, with more brilliant careers, than
any region but peninsular Italy** (Burrus, Valerius Asiaticus, Pompeius Paulinus, the prefect
Vestinus).

## The organization of territory

Augustus' settlement reorganized Narbonensis around about **twenty-two civitates**: he reduced
some seventy-five *oppida latina* by nearly 60% (forty-three losing autonomy, twenty-four
attached to **Nîmes**), concentrating elites in larger centres — a policy promoting **urbanism**
and entrenching the existing inequalities of power (the great peoples — Allobroges of Vienne,
Arecomici of Nîmes — whose *principes* could raise thousands of armed followers, outpaced even
the veteran colonies in producing senators). Italian forms spread only slowly (the title
*praetor* yielding to *IIviri*/*IIIIviri*; the Vocontii kept *praetores* to the 3rd century).

## The Three Gauls

The *Comata* Caesar conquered was organized by Augustus (the census and settlement at the
**conventus of Narbonne**, 27 BCE; the work of 16–13 BCE) into **Aquitania, Lugdunensis, and
Belgica** — at first a single command. Its great integrative moment came under **Claudius**: the
emperor's speech (the surviving **Tabula Lugdunensis**, AD 48) won the Roman citizens of *Gallia
Comata* the right to hold magistracies and enter the Senate at Rome — extending to the north the
privilege Narbonensis already enjoyed. The economy (Italian land-accumulation, colonial land-
divisions, mining) Goudineau largely reserves for CAH XI.

## The High Empire: the silent, prosperous provinces (CAH XI, Goudineau)

[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 14 (also **Goudineau**) covers AD 70–192, when the Gallic
provinces fall almost entirely **out of the historical record** — Jullian's "silence... a good
omen" — mentioned only for the **persecution of the Christians of Lyons (177)** and the **banditry
of Maternus** under Commodus. Domitian's creation of the **Germanies** placed the Tres Galliae
safely *behind* the frontier zone. With no narrative texts, the chapter is built from **epigraphy
and archaeology**, and its themes are the depth of integration and the scale of rural prosperity:

- **Thorough Romanization.** By the second century Gaul was "thoroughly romanized": the **Latin
  right** (granted to Narbonensis by Caesar, extended to the Tres Galliae perhaps only under the
  Flavians) and the spread of **Latin** had effectively displaced Celtic — Goudineau argues even
  the "barbarous speech" Irenaeus preached to was bad Latin, not Gaulish. "Resistance" and
  "survivals" are the wrong frame; Old French descends from Latin.
- **A revised countryside.** Aerial photography and rescue archaeology (the motorway/TGV transects)
  reveal a **density of rural settlement** six or seven times higher than once thought (Picardy: a
  great site every 2–3 km), prompting an upward revision of Gaul's **population to well over 10
  million**. The misnamed "*villae*" were varied **farmhouses** evolving from Iron Age forms, the
  basic unit ~25–30 ha, with no thousand-hectare *latifundia*.
- **Small towns.** The ***agglomérations secondaires*** ("small towns") — 75 in the Aeduan/
  Lingones/Sequani region alone, many with baths, sanctuaries, even theatres — flourished under the
  Flavians and second century *alongside* (not in competition with) the growing *civitas* capitals,
  serving agriculture, crafts, mining, and cult.
- **Agriculture and export.** Above all a land of **stock-raising** (the Italian *bos
  brachycephalos* enlarging native breeds) and **cereals** (the Gallic reaping machine, the
  *vallus*), with booming **viticulture** — Domitian's vine edict (AD 92) plainly **not enforced**,
  since Gaulish wine in its own "Gaulish amphorae" now reached the Rhine camps, Britain, and Ostia.

## Significance

Goudineau's Gaul is the test case of the empire's **heterogeneous, composite character**: not a
uniform "Romanitas" but a spectrum from thoroughly Mediterranean Narbonensis to the
civitas-patriotism of the north — held together by the house of Caesar, the imperial cult, and
the steady **juridical integration of provincial elites** into the Roman governing class.
