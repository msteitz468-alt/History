# Ionia and the Eastern Greeks

**Ionia** — and the wider **East Greek** world of the Anatolian coast and the offshore
islands — was the most commercially and intellectually advanced region of Archaic
Greece: the home of the giant temples, the Wild Goat pottery, the colonization of the
Black Sea and the far west, the invention of coined money's spread, and the **Ionian
"enlightenment"** of Thales and Anaximander. [[boardman-cah-v3p3-1982|CAH Vol. III,
Part 3]] (Ch. 39a, J. M. Cook) treats the eastern Greeks from c. 700 to Polycrates.

## Between Lydia and Persia

The defining external fact was the rise of [[lydia|Lydia]] on the landward horizon.
After the [[cimmerians|Cimmerians]] shattered Phrygia, **Gyges** (acc. c. 680) and his
Mermnad successors pressed the coastal cities — attacking Smyrna, Miletus, Colophon,
Priene — though never aiming at permanent conquest of the seaboard, only blocking Greek
penetration of the interior. **Croesus** (from c. 560) finally **subjected all the
Greeks of Asia to the Halys**, becoming both conqueror and **philhellene patron**:
Sardis became "the metropolis of the Greek East," with intermarriage in the upper
class and a two-way cultural traffic. Then **Cyrus the Persian** overthrew Croesus
(c. 546) and his general **Harpagus** reduced the cities (c. 540) — the Phocaeans
sailing west and the Teians to Abdera rather than submit. Only **Miletus** kept its
treaty rights; the **islands** (Samos, Chios, Lesbos, Rhodes) stayed effectively free
until Darius, since Cyrus had no fleet.

## City politics: tyrants and tribal reform

"Tyrants were common in East Greece from the later seventh century" — arising, as
Aristotle saw, from the great power vested in a city's chief magistrate in a time of
rapid economic change, not as a fixed stage toward democracy. **Miletus** suffered
endemic strife (the tyrants Thoas and Damasenor; the famous Thrasybulus who advised
Periander to "lop the tallest ears of corn"); **Mytilene's** turmoil is lit by
**Alcaeus's** poetry (Melanchrus, Myrsilus, and the moderate lawgiver **Pittacus**, who
ruled ten years and resigned). Several cities **reorganized the old four Ionic tribes**:
at **Ephesus** and (via the famous "constitution" stele) at **Chios** new tribal
structures and a popular council broadened citizenship — probably the tyrants'
incorporation of metics, immigrants and natives into the body politic as the trading
economy dissolved the old land-based order.

## The artistic koine and the giant temples

The eastern Greeks formed a recognizable **artistic *koine*** — a shared style with a
territorial spread unmatched elsewhere in Archaic Greece (it embraced Rhodes and the
Aeolis too). Its pottery ran from the universal **Wild Goat style** (c. 650 on,
decorative files of goats, lions, sphinxes) to the later Fikellura and "Clazomenian"
black-figure and the painted terracotta sarcophagi. Its crowning achievement was the
**giant marble temple**: the first **Samian dipteron** (the Heraion, c. 560, 105 m
long), the **Artemision at Ephesus** (with Croesus's dedicated columns), and the
**Didymaion** at Miletus — vast "forests of columns" in the slowly-forming **Ionic
order**, demanding the engineering of **Theodorus of Samos** (credited with the square,
level and lathe). Cook stresses that East Greek art was "more decorative and less
intellectually exacting" than Athenian — content with harmony of form rather than
narrative.

## Overseas expansion

The eastern Greeks were latecomers to colonization but flung themselves into it from
the late seventh century in **two great fields**, each led by a single city:

- **Miletus and the Pontus.** Miletus, "mother of 75 or 90 colonies," studded the
  shores of the **Hellespont, Propontis and Black Sea** with predominantly Milesian
  settlements (Sinope, Olbia, Panticapaeum), opening grain, fish, hides and slaves —
  commodities that *complemented* rather than duplicated Greek produce, unlike the
  western colonies.
- **Phocaea and the far west.** [[greek-colonization|Phocaea]] founded **Massalia**
  (Marseille, c. 600) and a chain of settlements to the Spanish coast, monopolizing the
  **Tartessus** silver trade (Colaeus of Samos had pioneered the route c. 638), trading
  in war-galleys until the "Cadmean victory" off Alalia (c. 540) checked them.

Alongside, the eastern Greeks dominated the trade with **[[saite-egypt|Egypt]]**
through the nine-city Hellenium at [[naucratis|Naucratis]] — a corporate venture
revealing the same **sense of East Greek unity** as the artistic koine (though Cook
deflates the idea of a political "Panionian league": Panionium shows no Archaic
buildings, and Thales proposed Teos, not Panionium, as a federal centre).

## The East Greek acme and Ionian rationalism

The seventh-century burst of trade produced, by the sixth, a wealthy professional
trading class, monetization (the spread of **[[coinage|coinage]]** from neighbouring
Sardis), and economic **specialization** (Chian wine and slaves, Milesian wool and
furniture, Coan silk, Rhodian sponges) — Chios even abandoning self-sufficiency to
exploit export markets. The high point was the tyranny of **[[polycrates|Polycrates of
Samos]]** (d. 522), whose sea power, engineering marvels and patronage made Samos the
showpiece of the age. Above all, the expansion made the Aegean "fully
intercommunicating" and gave Ionia its most lasting bequest: the **Ionian
enlightenment** — **Thales** and his disciple **Anaximander** of Miletus setting "the
pattern of Ionian rationalism" by explaining the world through observation and reasoned
theory rather than inherited myth; **Hecataeus** writing systematic geography and
drawing a world map; and the shift to **prose** that marks "the emergence of a literate
culture." Cook is careful: this owed something to the stimulus of older Near Eastern
wisdom, but the Greeks "were not content to be mere recipients."

## Why Ionia mattered

- The **commercial and intellectual engine of Archaic Greece** — the colonizing,
  coining, temple-building, philosophizing leading edge of the Greek world.
- The **birthplace of Greek rational inquiry** (the Milesian natural philosophers) and
  of systematic prose, geography and history.
- The **frontier of Greek and Near Eastern empire** — its subjection by Lydia and
  Persia set up the Ionian Revolt and the Persian Wars (CAH IV).
