# Chaco Canyon

## Overview

**Chaco Canyon**, in the arid San Juan Basin of northwestern New Mexico, is the **regional
case study** for North America in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Lekson, Ch 21). Its
monumental ninth–twelfth-century **"great houses"** anchor Lekson's argument that Native North
America was "awash with states and empires" — that Chaco was a **secondary state**, a local
version of a Mesoamerican polity, and not the simple egalitarian pueblo that orthodoxy long
assumed.

## The Bonito Phase

Chaco's apogee, the **Bonito phase (c. 850–1150 CE)**, is named for **Pueblo Bonito**, the
archetypal great house: built up over almost three centuries into a **D-shaped, four-to-five
story** masonry complex of nearly **700 rooms**, of which only a few dozen elite families
actually lived — the rest were vast **storage** and ceremonial space, with elite founders
buried in wealth beneath the oldest core. Great houses ("palaces") stand in stark, **Minoan**
contrast to the small single-family **"unit pueblos"** (commoner farmsteads) — one of
archaeology's clearest cases of **stratified housing**. Chaco was a planned **city** and a
**capital** of ~2,700, laid out on a precise north–south axis (the "Chaco Meridian") with
celestial alignments.

## A Regional System

Beyond the canyon, Chaco was the hub of an **80,000 km² regional system** (the size of
Ireland) of ~150 smaller "outlier" great houses, linked by **engineered roads** (wide,
straight, often symbolic rather than utilitarian) and a **line-of-sight signalling** network.
The canyon was a poor place to farm; **maize, timber, and pottery** were imported from up to
150 km away. Chaco concentrated extraordinary **exotica** — turquoise (100,000+ pieces, worked
from the Cerrillos mines and **exported to Mesoamerica**), and imports from western Mexico:
**copper bells, scarlet macaws, and cacao** detected in cylinder vases.

## Chaco as *Altepetl* (Lekson's Thesis)

Lekson argues that a century of interpretation (farming village → chiefdom → "rituality" →
pilgrimage center → "mystery") failed because it stayed trapped in **"Pueblo Space"** —
projecting modern egalitarian Pueblo ethnography onto a past that was profoundly different.
Read instead in its **actual context — Mesoamerica** — Chaco fits the **altepetl**, the
ubiquitous Postclassic Mesoamerican **city-state** of several noble families, each with their
commoners, under a weak first-among-equals king. Chaco's **seven major great houses** map onto
the altepetl's ideal of eight noble families; its radial road-divided hinterland mirrors the
altepetl's "pie-slice" subdivisions. The chief difference is **scale**: Chaco stretched the
altepetl form across a vast, bleak territory a thousand times larger than the Mesoamerican
norm — which may explain why it **failed**. Construction ceased ~1125; a successor capital at
**Aztec Ruins** was crippled by the 1130–1180 drought; and by 1300 CE, **Pueblo peoples
consciously rejected** Chaco's Mesoamerican-style hierarchy to build the egalitarian, communal
societies seen today. The Southwest's last great city, fourteenth-century **Paquimé**
(Chihuahua), may have been an heir of Chaco's noble line.

## Significance

Chaco is CWH IV's keystone for re-situating North America "on a continent awash with states
and empires," densely connected to Mesoamerica along routes like the Chaco Meridian. It is the
crux of the [[north-american-states-debate]] and a vivid case of CWH IV's **networks** thesis
applied to the New World.

## Historiography

Chaco is among the **best-dated** prehistoric sites on earth (tens of thousands of precise
tree-ring dates) in a setting of superb arid preservation. Yet interpretations remain
"staggering" in their range and unreconciled; Lekson's altepetl model is itself a contested
intervention against a long indigenist, anti-Mesoamerican orthodoxy in Southwestern
archaeology. CWH IV notes Chaco falls slightly **after** the volume's 900 CE horizon but uses
it to illustrate ancient North American world history.
