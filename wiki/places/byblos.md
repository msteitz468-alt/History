# Byblos (Jubeil)

The principal **Phoenician port** of the Lebanese coast and Egypt's chief gateway
to Asia — "one of the longest continually inhabited cities in the Near East." Its
Egyptian connection, established by sea from the earliest dynasties, makes it the
clearest index of Egypt's reach into the Levant across the third millennium.
Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XVII, Drower &
Bottéro; and Chs. XI, XIV on the Egyptian side).

## Setting and long history

Byblos sat on a low rocky promontory above a fertile plain, backed by
**thickly forested mountains** (the cedar slopes) with a spring and a sheltered
winter beach for ships. A Neolithic village (5th millennium) grew through a
Chalcolithic phase (apsidal cottages, stamp-sealed jar burials recalling Uruk
designs) into a town by the Jamdat Nasr epoch — wheel-made pottery, multi-room
houses, drained streets wide enough for carts, the first temples. The Kesrwan
mountains behind the city contained **tin and copper together** by rare chance,
making Byblos an early centre of **bronze**-working.

## The Egyptian connection

By the Old Kingdom, Egyptian influence was paramount. The reason was **cedar**:
Egyptian fleets came for Lebanese timber (and oils/resins), depositing royal-name
vessels at the temple of the "Mistress of Byblos." The connection runs continuously
through the wiki's Egyptian pages — a Khasekhemwy vase (Dynasty II), and inscribed
stone vessels of [[old-kingdom-egypt|Khufu, Menkaure, Teti, Pepi I, and Pepi II]].
Byblos lies in the region the Egyptians called the **Land of Negau**. Sea-borne
trade with Byblos was, in effect, Egypt's lifeline for large timber.

## Destruction and the wider collapse

Byblos was **destroyed by fire under Pepi II** (Sixth Dynasty), and the sage
Ipuwer laments that "there are no more expeditions by sea to Byblos." Its
destruction is part of the same horizon as the abandonment of the
[[early-bronze-age-levant|Early Bronze Levantine cities]] ('Ai, Megiddo, Khirbet
Karak, Jericho) and the [[fall-of-old-kingdom-2160bce|collapse of the Egyptian Old
Kingdom]] — the synchronized ~2200 BCE crisis associated with the
[[unknown|Amorite]] "thrust from the desert."

## Revival under the Middle Kingdom

After the [[first-intermediate-period|First Intermediate Period]] interruption,
[[middle-kingdom-egypt|Twelfth-Dynasty]] Egypt restored the Byblos connection more
intensely than ever — the touchstone of the debate over whether Egypt held an
"empire" in Asia (see [[middle-bronze-age]]). The princely tombs of Byblos yielded
lavishly Egyptian or Egyptianizing treasure: an **Amenemhat III** pectoral and a
gold-inlaid obsidian vase, an **Amenemhat IV** obsidian-and-gold casket. Most
telling is the political idiom of the local rulers: they bore **Semitic names** but
wrote them in **hieroglyphs**, took the Egyptian title **"governor" (*ḥꜣty-ꜥ*)**,
and even enclosed their names in royal **cartouches** — "at one and the same time
sovereigns and Egyptian officials." Whether the pharaohs' gifts went to a friendly
power or a vassal is exactly what the sources will not say; the Execration Texts
proscribe the *region* of Byblos but pointedly **not its princes**.

## Why this place mattered

Byblos is the eastern Mediterranean's premier emporium and the fixed point of the
Egypt–Levant relationship for three millennia; its name gave Greek *byblos*
(papyrus) → "Bible/biblio-". Its archaeology is also a cautionary case in method:
the excavators' rigid horizontal "total excavation" stripped the site in uniform
layers, badly blurring its stratigraphic history.
