# Iceland

A North Atlantic island settled from Norway and the Norse British Isles from
c. 870. After the [[settlement-of-iceland-874|landnám]] it developed the
[[icelandic-free-state|Icelandic Free State]], a stateless commonwealth governed
by chieftains (*goðar*) and the [[thing-assembly|Althing]] without a king.
Iceland adopted Christianity by collective decision at the Althing c. 1000,
submitted to the Norwegian crown in 1262–64, and became the great preserver and
producer of Old Norse [[saga-literature|saga and Eddic literature]].

## Medieval Iceland in Detail (Cambridge History)

The [[settlement-of-iceland-874|landnám]] (c. 870, confirmed by Greenland ice-core
tephra dated 871±2) filled most farmable land within c. 60 years; one-third of the
island, then birch-forested, was rapidly deforested by sheep-raising. The
[[icelandic-free-state|Free State]] governed through the [[thing-assembly|Althing]]
(c. 930), divided into quarters c. 965, with no executive power. Christianity was
accepted at the Althing in 999/1000; the tithe came in 1096–7; the first native
bishop, Ísleifr (1056–80), founded the see of Skálholt. Population peaked early
(c. 4,560 households in the 11th century, perhaps 32,500–39,000 people). From the
mid-11th century power concentrated into territorial *ríki* held by *stórgoðar* such
as the Sturlungar; the Sturlung Age ended with submission to Norway (1262–64,
*Gamli sáttmáli*), after which Norwegian law (Járnsíða, then **Jónsbók 1281**, parts
still in force) and royal sýslumenn replaced the goðar.

Iceland exported homespun cloth (*vaðmál*, to c. 1320) then dried fish; in the 15th
century English traders fetching fish directly broke [[bergen|Bergen]]'s monopoly —
the "English Age" (*enska öldin*). By the end of the Middle Ages only 5% of farms were
owned by those who worked them. Plague struck late (1402–4, 1494–5). See
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003]], [[saga-literature]],
[[settlement-of-iceland-874]], [[hofstadir]].
