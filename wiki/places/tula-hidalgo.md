# Tula (Tollán, Hidalgo)

## Overview

**Tula** (Tollán) in Hidalgo was the capital of the [[toltec|Toltec]] Early Postclassic
state and the second of central Mexico's great "macroregional" systems after
[[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] (and before the [[aztec-empire|Aztec]] Triple Alliance).
Its fall (c. 1150–1200 CE, dating disputed) opens the fragmented city-state landscape from
which the Mexica eventually built empire. See [[fall-of-tula-1150]].

## Identification: Tollan, Toltec, and Teotihuacan confusion

Archaeological Tula (north of Tula de Allende) is the Tollan of many sixteenth-century
sources and the Toltec capital per tradition — once confused with Teotihuacan. Jiménez
Moreno ethnohistory and Acosta excavations corrected this. “Tollan” can also mean
Teotihuacan or other sites, or purely mythical abstractions; many references are so abstract
that relating them to any concrete place is impossible. “Toltec” is likewise polysemic and
often denotes urban, urbane, skilled craftsmen rather than a single ethnicity (Cowgill,
CHNPA II.1 Ch. 6).

## Teotihuacan-period precursor and rise of the city

There is a strong Teotihuacan presence in the Tula region, almost certainly administered
through a major center at **Chingú** (Cowgill 2000). The city of Tula itself rose after
Teotihuacan’s decline and declined well before Mexica power. Two major precincts: earlier
**Tula Chico** (Coyotlatelco ceramics); later **Tula Grande** (~Mazapan-era associations,
though most local ceramics are not Mazapan). Coverage ~**12–13 km²**; rough population guess
~**60,000** — denser urban settlement; civic scale and quality below Teotihuacan but not a
“small town.” Continuities with the past (e.g., feline/coyote frieze echoing Atetelco) without
a complete break.

**Tollan ceramic phase** — traditionally ~A.D. 950–1150/1200 but more likely ~**800–1000**
from recent Basin dates and Tohil Plumbate association (Cowgill 2000). Mazapan
(multiple-brush wavy lines), supposed Tula hallmark, is rare and southern-concentrated at
Tula — possibly a Basin-affiliated enclave; more abundant in the Basin, may have developed
there.

Northwest ceramic affinities from the Prado phase (~A.D. 700–800 or earlier; hilltop sites
possibly defensive) precede Tula’s importance; Corral-phase Coyotlatelco (~800–900 or as
early as 600–800 by Basin 14C) supports substantial NW migration into the highlands as
Teotihuacan declined (Cowgill 2000).

## Scale, wealth, and Chichén Itzá links

Specific architectural and iconographic links with **Chichén Itzá** (northern Yucatán,
~1,150 km) plus varied evidence for central Mexican–connected people. Kubler’s
reverse-priority argument (Chichén first, Tula imitation) lacks good reason — Chichén’s
larger/finer monuments reflect better masons, limestone, and labor, not temporal primacy;
contact is more complex than simple Toltec replacement of Maya traditions (Cowgill 2000).

The Tula state was never very wealthy or extensive; the region is environmentally harsh
(possible soil degradation). Other important sites coexisted in central Mexico. Aztec
Golden-Age myth and pre–Late Aztec comparison inflate Tollan’s wonders relative to modest
remains. Diehl argues for a Toltec Horizon spread by merchants; Calnek notes physical
evidence for distant imperial control is not obvious; Weaver argues limited integrated
territory with extended sphere of influence (Charlton, CHNPA Ch. 11). Charlton treats Tula
as the first crystallization of a macroregional secondary state in the continuum from
Teotihuacan to Tenochtitlan — grandeur below both.

## Decline and Aztec afterlife

Decline is poorly understood: residential excavations show trash dumping in abandoned rooms,
shrine offerings torn out, and dismembered adults/children consistent with massacre
(Mandeville & Healan cautious; Cowgill finds the massacre case strong). Documentary
tradition and Charlton’s synthesis place the end around **A.D. 1150** (range ~1100–1200);
see [[fall-of-tula-1150]].

Aztecs excavated extensively at Tula (damaging buildings) not as anachronistic “looting” but
because Tollan was central to Aztec mythical charter — objects from Tula (and to a lesser
extent Teotihuacan, Xochicalco, etc.) heavily meaningful; Late Aztec products often hark to
Tula and earlier styles (Umberger 1987; Cowgill 2000).

## Why it mattered

Architectural and sculptural forms (warrior columns, *coatepantli*, chacmools) became
templates for later Central Mexican and northern Maya discourse about “Toltec” legitimacy —
a mix of archaeology and later Nahua historical memory. The city’s fall starts the migration
traditions of nearly every political unit in central Mexico at Spanish contact (Carrasco,
cited in Charlton).
