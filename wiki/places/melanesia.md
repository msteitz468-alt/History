# Melanesia

**Melanesia** ("black islands") comprises the large continental island of New Guinea
([[new-guinea|Papua New Guinea]] and West Papua/Irian Jaya), the Solomon Islands,
[[vanuatu|Vanuatu]], [[new-caledonia|New Caledonia]] and [[fiji|Fiji]]. A sub-region of
[[oceania|Oceania]], it holds **Near Oceania** — the Bismarck and Solomon archipelagos settled
in the Pleistocene, the deepest-rooted human occupation in the Pacific — and is by far the
region's most linguistically diverse area (over a thousand languages, including the non-Austronesian
"Papuan" families).

In [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997|the Cambridge History of the Pacific Islanders]],
Melanesia is the region the volume frankly admits it covers least well — the scholarship is
thin and "almost entirely the work of outsiders," especially for West Papua. Yet it supplied
some of the modern era's most consequential episodes: the New Guinea mining frontier, the
[[bougainville-civil-war-1988|Bougainville civil war]], the [[fiji-coups-1987|Fiji coups]],
the Kanak independence struggle in New Caledonia, and the *kastom* (custom) movements of Vanuatu.

## Significance

Melanesia exposes the limits of the regional frame: its diversity, its Pleistocene depth, and
its colonial and post-colonial conflicts resist the homogenising "Pacific Islander" category.
It is where the volume's questions about custom, resource extraction and the post-colonial state
press hardest.
