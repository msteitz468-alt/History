# Pacific Ocean

The largest body of water on Earth — roughly one third of the planet's surface —
and, in the framing of *The Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean*, a historical
actor in its own right rather than a mere stage for human action. Its currents,
gyres, winds, marine ecosystems, volcanic and coral islands, and deep trenches
shaped the possibilities of every society that lived in, around and across it.

## Geographic Description

A "blue hemisphere" stretching from the Asian and Australian margins to the
Americas and from the Arctic to the Southern Ocean. It is studded with the island
worlds of [[oceania|Oceania]] — [[polynesia|Polynesia]], [[melanesia|Melanesia]]
and [[micronesia|Micronesia]] — and bounded by the dynamic Pacific Rim: the
Chinese and Southeast Asian seaboards, the New Guinea and Australian coasts, and
the maritime cultures of the Americas' Northwest Coast and Andean/Mesoamerican
shores. Its tectonic "Ring of Fire" makes islands and natural hazards (volcanism,
tsunami, cyclones) constitutive of Pacific history.

## History of Human Engagement

Human entanglement with the Pacific runs from Pleistocene coastal and island
voyaging (see [[pacific-voyaging-and-settlement]]) through the
[[austronesian-expansion|Austronesian]] colonization of the remote islands and
the development of sophisticated Indigenous navigation, to the maritime political
economies of China and Southeast Asia and the late, transformative arrival of
European seapower after 1521.

## Why It Mattered

The ocean was simultaneously barrier and highway: its scale long isolated island
societies, yet its winds and currents, once mastered, knit together an
ocean-spanning web of migration, exchange and knowledge. Control of its sea lanes,
fisheries and island bases became a central object of both Indigenous and
imperial power.

## Deep Time: Geological and Biological History

David Christian's "little big history" of the Pacific (ch. 2) situates the ocean in
planetary time. The Pacific originated between 1,000 and 900 million years ago as
the ocean *Panthalassa* formed from the break-up of the supercontinent Rodinia.
At roughly 250 million years ago, Pangea — surrounded by Panthalassa on all sides
— began to crack; by about 200 million years ago, Pacific-age crust was forming
as Pangea's fragments diverged. Today the Pacific covers about 45 per cent of
Earth's water surface and contains twice as much water as the Atlantic; the Pacific
Plate moves unusually fast, at more than 185 millimetres per year.

Key geological features:
- **Ring of Fire**: Subduction zones around the Pacific rim generate most of the
  world's earthquakes, tsunamis, and volcanoes (running from Macquarie Island
  through New Zealand, Tonga, the Marianas, Japan, Kamchatka, the Aleutians, and
  down the Americas).
- **High islands**: Formed as the Pacific Plate slid over mantle hotspots; the
  Hawaiian chain is the canonical example (most volcanic at its southeast end, oldest
  and most eroded to the northwest).
- **Coral islands**: Low atolls built on eroding volcanic peaks; reefs grow upward as
  volcanoes subside. Mururoa atoll, where France tested nuclear weapons, sits atop
  a volcanic peak now one kilometre below the surface.
- **ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation)**: The Pacific is the largest single store
  of heat energy on Earth's surface. Its easterly trade winds normally drive warm
  water westward, raising the western Pacific ~60 cm above the eastern. In El Niño
  phases (irregular cycles of 2–7 years) these easterlies weaken, disrupting fisheries
  and rainfall across the entire Pacific Rim. Christian notes these patterns "are
  ancient, and may help explain the timing of events such as some Polynesian voyages."

Biological colonization of Pacific islands proceeded almost entirely from west to east
(from Southeast Asia and New Guinea), because that is where most islands cluster.
By Bougainville in the Solomons a new island might have several thousand species;
Easter Island in the far east had only thirty before human arrival.

## Humans in the Pacific: Three World Zones

By the end of the last Ice Age (~11,700 BP), Pacific-Rim peoples lived in three
effectively separate "world zones" — Afro-Eurasia, Sahul/Australasia, and the
Americas:

- **Sahul**: Humans crossed to the Sahul continent (Australia, Papua New Guinea,
  Tasmania joined by ice-age sea levels) at least 65,000 years ago in planned
  migrations of "perhaps hundreds of people, possibly in bamboo seacraft." By
  40,000–30,000 BP, small groups had made forays into the Bismarck Archipelago
  (Near Oceania). Japan and Okinawa were settled at about the same time.
- **Americas**: Humans entered via the Beringia land bridge, perhaps as early as
  20,000 years ago. About 90 per cent of Native Americans descend from a single
  early migration.
- **Remote Oceania**: Settled in two pulses. The first, linked to the Lapita cultural
  complex, began ~4,000 BP from agricultural populations of Taiwan or south China
  (Austronesian speakers); Lapita ware reached Tonga and Sāmoa by 700 BCE.
  The second pulse, within the last 1,500 years, settled most remaining Pacific islands.
  By ~1200 CE, navigators from both sides of the Pacific had met in eastern Polynesia,
  "completing the human circling of the globe."

Pre-contact environmental impacts were substantial: the Pacific rat drove as many as
2,000 Pacific bird species to extinction; in Aotearoa nearly half of ancient forests
were cleared and the moa was extinguished before Europeans arrived.

## The Anthropocene Turn

Christian frames the entire modern era as an Anthropocene transition: humans have
become "the most significant driver of change in the Pacific" — transforming the
biosphere as profoundly as geological and biological drivers that dominated for
billions of years.

> "Vast, deep and fluid as they are, the oceans are sensitive indicators of the impact
> of modern histories of production and consumption, that moment — our moment —
> when human and geological time scales increasingly interfere and intersect."
> (editors of *Oceanic Histories*, quoted in Christian ch. 2)

By the early 21st century, the region around the Pacific produced roughly 60 per cent
of global GDP; nearly 50 per cent of world trade crossed the ocean. Yet
industrial-scale fishing has reduced marine mammal biomass by ~80 per cent, the
Great Barrier Reef is endangered, and rising sea levels threaten to submerge nations
such as Tuvalu and parts of Kiribati.

Indigenous frameworks — especially [[concepts/te-moana-nui-a-kiwa]] — provide
alternative temporal and ethical orientations: the ocean as genealogical homeland
(*whakapapa*) whose future is "entrusted in the future" of its human communities.

## Significance Over Time

Conceived by Europeans as a "Spanish Lake" and then an arena of naval rivalry, the
Pacific has more recently been reclaimed by Islander scholars (Epeli Hau'ofa's
"sea of islands") as a connective Indigenous homeland — a shift this volume
embodies.
