# Cuicuilco

## Overview

**Cuicuilco** was the first major urban center in the [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of Mexico]]
and the direct predecessor of [[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] as the Basin's dominant polity.
Located in what are now the southwestern reaches of Mexico City — about 50 km south of
Teotihuacan — it occupied what was, before the eruption of the volcano Xitle, "one of the
most lush and fertile zones in the entire Basin" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3). At its height
(c. 400–100 BCE) it covered at least 400 ha with an estimated population of at least
20,000, making it for a time the largest settlement in the Basin of Mexico, comparable in
scale to contemporaneous Monte Albán in Oaxaca. It was buried by lava and ash from
Xitle, probably sometime between 200 and 400 CE, though the city had already been
abandoned long before the eruption.

## Geographic and Environmental Context

Cuicuilco lay in the southwestern Basin at an altitude similar to the rest of the highland
basin floor (~2,240 m). Before Xitle's eruption, the area was among the best-watered and
most agriculturally productive zones in the Basin, superior in this respect to the northeast
where Teotihuacan would later rise. Evidence of irrigation canals predating the eruption —
from small local streams diverted for agriculture — was recorded by Palerm and Wolf in
1956 (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3), suggesting sophisticated water management was already in place
by the city's height. The eruption of Xitle buried approximately 70 km² of land under
"barren and nearly lifeless rock… in twisted and tormented shapes" — the Pedregal lava
field that still covers much of the area today.

## History of Settlement and Control

The earliest evidence of occupation is a ceramic phase called **Tlalpan**, with
uncalibrated radiocarbon dates around 2000–1800 BCE (Heizer and Bennyhoff 1972), making
it among the earliest sedentary communities documented anywhere in the Basin of Mexico.
The Tlalpan ceramics — deep open thin-walled bowls with red slip and channeling — show
little clear ancestry from later Basin traditions and remain incompletely studied.

After Tlalpan, a long series of post-Tlalpan ceramic phases saw the site grow steadily.
The great **circular pyramid** (Cuicuilco A), about 130 m in diameter and 16–20 m high,
was enlarged in five stages from a modest beginning to its final monumental form (Heizer
and Bennyhoff 1972). Even today it rises above the lava that engulfed it. An additional
complex of eleven mounds was partially explored at **Cuicuilco B**, about 500 m to the
west.

During its height (c. 400–100 BCE), ceramics at Cuicuilco closely resemble the **Ticomán
I–III tradition** (named after a site on the northwestern shore of Lake Texcoco), which
Bennyhoff (1967) proposed should more properly be called the **Cuicuilco tradition** since
Cuicuilco was its major center. The local Teotihuacan Valley variant of this tradition is
called **Cuanalán**. Serving vessels tend toward simple red-slip decoration over natural
surfaces, "often remarkably graceful in form" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3); bowls with medial or
shoulder breaks in profile, often with large hollow supports, are characteristic.

## Political Status and Competition with Teotihuacan

At its peak Cuicuilco "must have politically dominated at least a sizable part of the
southwestern Basin of Mexico, and ceramic uniformity throughout the Basin suggests that its
influence was strong everywhere in the Basin" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3). Sanders et al. (1979)
estimate that by 400–100 BCE there was a four-tier settlement hierarchy in the Basin,
consisting of hamlets, small villages, large villages, and 13 small regional centers,
all dominated by two much larger centers — Cuicuilco and the rising Teotihuacan —
"competing to expand their spheres of influence." Whether the Cuicuilco polity qualifies
as a state or a complex chiefdom remains contested: Cowgill is skeptical of settlement-
tier counts as reliable indexes of political complexity.

A critical question is the relationship between Cuicuilco's decline and Teotihuacan's
rise. Ceramics of the **Patlachique phase** (c. 100–1 BCE) of Teotihuacan also appear
below the Xitle lava, indicating Cuicuilco was still occupied during Teotihuacan's initial
explosive growth. However, there are no middens with Tzacualli (1–100 CE) or later
ceramics below the lava — only a few Tzacualli trade sherds — indicating Cuicuilco was
abandoned before Teotihuacan reached its full dominance, and before Xitle erupted.
"We can no longer look to Xitle for an easy explanation for the decline of Cuicuilco and
the rise of Teotihuacan" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3).

The more likely cause of Cuicuilco's decline was a combination of: (1) competition and
possibly warfare with Teotihuacan; (2) ash fall from a major eruption of **Popocatépetl**
dated by Siebe (2000) to c. 200–1 BCE, which devastated the southeastern Basin (well
documented at Tetimpa in Puebla) and probably also affected Cuicuilco, farther to the
west; (3) Teotihuacan's superior location on the trade route toward Tlaxcala, Puebla, the
Gulf Lowlands, and south-central Mexico. Teotihuacan, in the northeastern extreme of the
Basin, would have been "far less affected" by Popocatépetl ash than Cuicuilco.

Importantly, "Cuicuilco's urban experiences were available as models for Teotihuacan's
creators. Teotihuacan was the continuation and elaboration of an urban tradition already
developed at Cuicuilco" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3). This helps explain why Teotihuacan
developed its urban character so rapidly.

## Why Cuicuilco Mattered

Cuicuilco is the founding case of [[urbanism]] in the Basin of Mexico. Its scale, civic
pyramid, and political complexity demonstrate that the urbanization of the Basin was not a
late development beginning with Teotihuacan, but rather went back to 400 BCE or earlier
— "not long after the beginning of Monte Albán in Oaxaca, currently estimated at around
500 BCE" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 3). Cuicuilco's settlement on relatively flat ground (unlike
the hilltop Monte Albán) foreshadows Teotihuacan's distinctive choice of flat terrain and
may have influenced the regularized layout that made Teotihuacan's grid possible.

## Historiography

Knowledge of Cuicuilco is severely limited because the Pedregal lava is "virtually
impenetrable solid rock," unlike the softer volcanic ash that preserved Pompeii. Byron
Cummings excavated the main pyramid in the 1920s using pre-modern methods. Heizer and
Bennyhoff (1958, 1972) provide the principal ceramic and architectural sequence. Further
salvage work was carried out by INAH in 1967 at Cuicuilco B; Florencia Müller (1990)
published ceramics and architectural features. Pastrana and Ramírez (2012) summarize more
recent excavations. Compositional (NAA) study of ceramics is needed but has barely begun.
The geological debate about the date and nature of the Xitle eruption was substantially
clarified by Claus Siebe (2000), who argues for a single explosive event between 200 and
400 CE — much later than the site's abandonment, making Xitle irrelevant to Cuicuilco's
fall. The collection is weakest precisely where Cuicuilco is most important: knowledge of
the city's internal layout and social organization is "desperately little" even by
Cowgill's own assessment.

## Grove CHNPA Preclassic framing (Ch. 3)

David C. Grove ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1]]) places Cuicuilco
on the southern Basin lakeshore best agricultural zone as the central highlands’ **first urban
center**. Early history is clouded by lava from eruptions beginning ~100 BCE. Possible Early
Preclassic occupation under lava; 1957 platforms attributed to Middle Preclassic—“already very
large,” unique in the Basin if correct. By **300 BCE** Grove’s account makes Cuicuilco the largest
Basin settlement: **27 m high** circular stone-faced truncated-cone pyramid (~**80 m diameter**)
plus ≥11 rectangular pyramids within ~1 km; may have covered ~**400 ha** with ~**20,000** people.
Large irrigation canals under lava support intensive agriculture for what may have been
“Mesoamerica’s first city-state.”

Concurrently, the Teotihuacan Valley—marginal in Early/Middle Preclassic—grew in the early Late
Preclassic; by 300 BCE community and public architecture covered 600–800 ha with 20,000–40,000
inhabitants, rivalling Cuicuilco. Grove dates a ~**100 BCE** eruption that devastated Cuicuilco’s
agricultural land and displaced population, with a second eruption centuries later finishing the
city, after which highland urbanization centered on the Teotihuacan Valley. (Cowgill 2015 / Siebe
refine Xitle to ~200–400 CE and stress Cuicuilco’s abandonment before full Teotihuacan dominance—
preserve both chronological frameworks as source-attributed positions.)

## Cowgill CHNPA Ch. 6 phase notes

In Ticoman (~500–150 BCE; Cuanalan in the Teotihuacan Valley), southern Basin towns had
respectable ceremonial centers; Cuicuilco may have had **5,000–10,000** people while the northern
Basin including the Teotihuacan Valley was relatively marginal. In Patlachique–Tzacualli
(~150 BCE–A.D. 150), Cuicuilco reached maximum early with pyramids up to 80 m diameter and
~**20,000** people (Sanders et al.), then was seriously damaged by lava mid-period, possibly
surviving as a small regional center to ~A.D. 200. Cowgill frames probable multi-polity warfare
and tribute extraction, with Cuicuilco and Teotihuacan as sole Basin rivals until Cuicuilco was
crippled by lava—speculative in his own wording.

## Evans & Webster 2001

[[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001|Evans & Webster (eds.) 2001]]
adds signed-entry consensus that must be held next to Cowgill/Siebe and Grove CHNPA
(**EW2001**).

**Susan Toby Evans (Cuicuilco entry):** Late Formative (300–1 B.C.) Cuicuilco was the largest
and most impressive Basin of Mexico site — pyramid largest of its time, population **perhaps
as great as 20,000**. In **50 B.C.** the Xitle eruption covered the site with lava flows some
**10 m deep** over ~**80 km²**. Circular stepped pyramid nearly **20 m high** and **>110 m
across**, recognized as a built feature only in 1922 by Gamio and Cummings. Founded c. **1100
B.C.** (with Coapexco), early irrigation, first pyramid version by **400 B.C.**; late Late
Formative shared power with growing Teotihuacan; Teotihuacan’s great growth spurt “almost
certainly” included Cuicuilcan refugees who built Teotihuacan’s monuments for that city’s
rulers.

**Jeffrey R. Parsons (Basin of Mexico):** Middle Formative lakeshore center **40–50 ha**; by
**Patlachique** a capital of ~**40,000** competing with Teotihuacan for regional hegemony;
destroyed with its heartland by volcanic eruptions in the **first century B.C.**; massive
circular platform and pyramid anticipate later round temples (Cholula, Tlatelolco,
Tenochtitlan) and may suggest early Quetzalcoatl-cult presence; incense burners and fire-god
effigies present.

**Lorraine Williams (Architecture):** Middle Formative Basin lakeshore sites Cuicuilco,
Tenayuca, Tlapacoyan display formal monumental elements; pyramid is large packed-earth-and-
stone series of truncated conical platforms with base burials indicating ritual functions.

**John E. Clark (Formative Period):** major Middle and Late Formative center covered by lava;
**“the relationship of this major community to the development of Teotihuacan remains to be
determined”** — flag of genuine uncertainty against Evans’s refugee-labor certainty.

**Population tension (attributed):** Evans ~20,000 peak vs Parsons ~40,000 at Patlachique vs
Grove CHNPA ~20,000 / Cowgill CHNPA max ~20,000 (Sanders et al.) — report ranges by author;
do not collapse. **Xitle dating tension:** Evans places the sealing eruption at **50 B.C.**;
Cowgill 2015 / Siebe place Xitle ~**200–400 CE** after political abandonment — preserve both
as source-attributed chronological frameworks (see Historiography above).
