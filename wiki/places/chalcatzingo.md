# Chalcatzingo

## Overview

**Chalcatzingo** (eastern Morelos) was the principal Middle Preclassic monumental center on the
eastern limit of the “Tlatilco” ceramic assemblage of the western Basin of Mexico–Morelos
interaction sphere. David C. Grove’s Preclassic central-highlands chapter
([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1 Ch. 3]]) is the primary synthesis
below. The site is famous for bas-relief monumental art unique in Morelos/Basin and for limited
elite associations with Gulf Coast [[olmec|Olmec]] chiefdoms—executed in a distinctive
**“frontier” style**, not as proof of Olmec domination of highland villages (Grove position).

## Early Preclassic public architecture

Very few central-highland Early Preclassic sites have raised platforms classifiable as public
architecture. Chalcatzingo had a 2 m earthen platform (earliest stage of a mound that by 500 BCE
was 4 m tall × 70 m long) and a stone-faced 1 m platform—already exceptional for the Early
Preclassic highlands.

## Middle Preclassic community (c. 900–500 BCE)

Hill slopes were remodeled into broad terraces. A **70 m earthen platform** dominated the
uppermost terrace opposite an elite house compound (chiefly lineage). A lower terrace held a
stone-faced sunken patio with bas-relief earth-supernatural altar. Non-elite houses were large
(>60 m² floor), wattle-and-daub front with adobe/tepetate side walls; cooking on ceramic
braziers; diet maize/beans/squash plus dog, deer, rabbit, birds, iguana. Elite upper-terrace
burials more often included jadeite and stone-lined graves than lower-terrace commoners—a **rank
continuum**, not rigid classes (Grove).

After ~900 BCE, ceramic griddles and processed lime appear highland-wide (tortilla technology);
Chalcatzingo households participate in that labor reorganization.

### Figurines

Anthropomorphic figurines concentrate in household (especially kitchen) contexts; heads were
purposely broken off to terminate ritual importance. Nearly half of Chalcatzingo’s identifiable
figurines are type **C.8 “portrait”** faces representing at least twenty individuals—possibly
chiefs, lineage heads, or elite ancestors. Most wear ear ornaments (jade earspools = high rank).
Some bodies show pregnancy stages (Cyphers Guillén). Zoomorphic figurines are mostly
whistles/ocarinas, also “decapitated.”

### Monumental art themes

Chiefs displayed carved stone monuments unique in Morelos/Basin:

1. **Cliffside bas-reliefs** of ritual/mythico-religious content (e.g., Monument 2 masked ritual
   scene; Monument 1 personage in earth-supernatural U-niche/cave under rain imagery—possibly a
   deceased ancestral chief as rain/fertility mediator).
2. **Political stelae/statues** on plaza and terraces as chiefly portraits, often beside low
   platforms, frequently “decapitated” analogously to clay figurines—one monument head interred
   with an elite burial.

## Alliances: Teopantecuanitlan and the Gulf Coast

**Teopantecuanitlan** (Guerrero, Amacuzac–Mezcala junction) shared with Chalcatzingo stone-faced
patios, bas-relief monumental art, and distinctive construction techniques—suggesting alliance;
Teopantecuanitlan’s patio antiquity (Early Preclassic origins; cut stone by 800 BCE with four
inverted-T monolithic supernatural faces) may have inspired Chalcatzingo’s patio via Guerrero
contact. Monument 21 at Chalcatzingo (only identified woman in Middle Preclassic monumental art)
may commemorate a marriage alliance (Cyphers Guillén). Adams (intro) likewise notes large
regional centers such as Chalcatzingo and Teopantecuanitlan linked by elite-class marriage ties
and definite Teopantecuanitlan–Gulf Olmec–central Mexico ties c. 900 BCE.

**Grove on Olmec links:** stone monumental art was atypical of Preclassic highland political/
ideological systems and characteristic of Gulf Coast Olmec (~300 mountainous km SE). Presence of
carved monuments indicates significant associations with one or more Olmec chiefdoms—likely
motivated by Gulf need for central Mexican exotics (serpentine/greenstone from Guerrero).
Chalcatzingo chiefs displayed certain Olmec political-ideological symbols in frontier style;
Teopantecuanitlan patio faces show even fewer direct Gulf similarities. Display of “foreign”
symbols enhanced local chiefly prestige. This is **limited elite alliance/symbol adoption**, not
blanket Olmec cultural domination—consistent with Grove’s deliberate refusal to narrate highland
Preclassic lifeways as “Olmec influences” (see [[olmec-mother-vs-sister-culture]]).

## Decline (c. 500 BCE)

By or soon after 500 BCE, settlement realignment: Chalcatzingo and many Middle Preclassic
villages abandoned; population nucleated around large “central place” villages with tall
pyramidal public architecture. Cultural focus shifted for at least the next millennium to the
previously marginal [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of Mexico]] rather than southern river valleys.
Grove footnotes that Chalcatzingo’s decline may also relate to Gulf Coast Olmec decline and a
diminished highland exchange role.

## Why it mattered

Best-documented highland case of Middle Preclassic chiefly monumentality and selective Olmec
symbol adoption; key site for separating **elite long-distance alliance** from **mass cultural
diffusion**. Anchors Morelos in the Preclassic arc that culminates in [[cuicuilco|Cuicuilco]] and
[[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] urbanization.
