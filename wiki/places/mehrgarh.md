# Mehrgarh

## Overview

Mehrgarh is the most important Neolithic farming site in the Indian subcontinent, located at the foot of the Bolan Pass on the River Bolan (a tributary of the Indus) in the Kachi plain of Baluchistan, Pakistan. Archaeological work since the 1970s has established it as the earliest farming settlement in South Asia, with continuous occupation extending from approximately 7000 BCE — the starting point that Avari uses to define the beginning of "ancient India." As Avari writes, "Nowhere in the Indian sub-continent does a site exist which allows us this unique glimpse into the previous 4,500 years before 2500 bc when Mohenjo Daro was at its zenith."

## Geographic Position and Strategic Significance

Mehrgarh's location is historically crucial. It sits at the foot of the Bolan Pass, one of the key access points between the Indian subcontinent and Afghanistan, on a route linking the Indus valley with the Iranian plateau and onward to Central and West Asia. The alluvium and floodwaters of the Bolan river and Indus basin made the site suitable for farming. Mehrgarh is located approximately 250 kilometres northwest of Mohenjo Daro, placing it within the Indus drainage system. Its position on a major trade route is confirmed by the discovery of lapis lazuli beadware from its earliest occupation layers, indicating that the Mehrgarhians were trading even at the very beginning of their history.

## Excavation History

The significance of Mehrgarh was only revealed after major excavations begun in 1974, directed by the French archaeologist Jean-François Jarrige leading a joint Franco-Pakistani team. These excavations, described by Avari as "the most critical excavation of the post-war period," demonstrated a thread of archaeological continuity from the earliest foundations of Mehrgarh in the seventh millennium BCE all the way to the great cities of the Indus — definitively disproving the earlier thesis that Indus urbanism must have had Mesopotamian origins.

The site consists of six mounds spread over approximately 500 acres. The oldest mound, designated MR3 by archaeologists, shows evidence of continuous human occupation from about 7000 to 4700 BCE. After Period I there were six additional periods, bringing the chronology down to approximately 2300 BCE — giving Mehrgarh a total span of occupation of nearly five millennia.

## Phases of Occupation and Material Culture

**Period I (c. 7000–4700 BCE, the Baluchistan Phase):** The earliest stratum at MR3 contains remains of mud-built structures, chert blades, barley and wheat seeds, and bones of sheep, goats and cattle — the clearest evidence of a farming and pastoral community. This is the earliest such evidence in South Asia. By contrast, the next major proto-urban site in the region, Amri, does not begin until c. 3500 BCE, illustrating the extraordinary antiquity of Mehrgarh's Period I.

**Periods II–VII (c. 4700–2300 BCE):** The later periods show Mehrgarhians progressively improving their skills and arts toward a level eventually matching that of Mature Harappan society. Their pottery, copper ware, figurines and various other ornaments reflect a people "striving towards a state of advancement and perfection, by experimenting, innovating and improvising rather than by imitating foreign models" (Avari). Some of the oldest cotton seeds recovered anywhere have been found in Mehrgarh's mounds, raising the possibility that cotton cultivation and manufacturing first occurred in the Indus zone. A study from the Human Biology Department of the University of Rome has credited the people of Mehrgarh with the skills of dentistry.

Avari suggests a periodisation for the broader northwest zone: the years 7000 to 4500 BCE should be called the Baluchistan Phase, given the importance of Mehrgarh; 4500 to 3500 BCE an age of transition; and 3500 to 2600 BCE the Early Harappan phase (or, in Jim Shaffer's terminology, an Era of Regionalisation).

## Significance for Understanding South Asian Prehistory

Mehrgarh occupies a pivotal position in our understanding of the origins of South Asian civilisation. It definitively demonstrates that:

1. The Indus Valley Civilisation had **indigenous** Neolithic roots in Baluchistan, not foreign (Mesopotamian) origins.
2. Farming in South Asia began no later than 7000 BCE, making India's agricultural tradition among the oldest in the world.
3. The transition from village farming to urban civilisation was a slow, endogenous process across several millennia.

Mehrgarh is the single site in the entire subcontinent that allows archaeologists to trace the continuity from Neolithic village life through proto-urban phases to the great cities of the Mature Harappan phase.

## Relationship to the Harappan Civilisation

The continuity between Mehrgarh and the Mature Harappan phase is now well-established. Archaeological research since the 1970s, particularly Jarrige's work, has "disproved the thesis" (Avari) that Indus urbanism must have had foreign origins. The roots of the Indus cities "lie not outside the sub-continent but within." The Mehrgarh sequence is the most important single piece of evidence for this indigenous development.

## Historiography

**Source quality:** Mehrgarh is known almost entirely through archaeology, primarily from the 1974–75 and subsequent excavations by Jarrige and the Franco-Pakistani team. Earlier scholarship was largely unaware of the site. Jarrige's publications (1980, 1982) are the foundational primary archaeological reports.

**Dating methods:** Radiocarbon dating. The 7000 BCE date for the earliest occupational level has been arrived at through systematic archaeological research and is not based on ancient texts or mythology. Avari notes this explicitly: "The date of 7000 bc has been arrived at through systematic archaeological research. It is not based on an interpretation drawn from an ancient text or mythology."

**Scholarly debates:** Some earlier scholars believed the very first farmers of South Asia, in Baluchistan, might have come from Mesopotamia and the Fertile Crescent (Diamond 1998 is cited by Avari on crop origins). The indigenous vs. Fertile Crescent origin of agricultural crops remains debated. There is also continuing debate about rice cultivation — whether it was indigenous or arrived from East and Southeast Asia during the second millennium BCE.

**Collection coverage:** This source (Avari 2007) gives substantive treatment of Mehrgarh in Chapters 2 and 3. The Cambridge World History Vol. III also treats South Asian prehistory (Sinopoli) but focuses more on the Mature Harappan phase.
