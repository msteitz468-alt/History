# Boston

Principal port and political capital of provincial Massachusetts; the “great town” of [[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Schiff]]’s Samuel Adams biography and the hinge city of early American resistance. Town meeting culture, newspapers, wharves, and crowded streets made it the laboratory of [[adams-samuel|Samuel Adams]]’s public-opinion politics—from Stamp Act crowds through the Massacre, Tea Party, and the siege of 1775–76.

## Geography and mid-century character

Nearly an island, ~**16,000** people in fewer than four square miles, modeled on England; closest major colonial port to Great Britain. Superb harbor—“majestic crescent bristling with masts”; longest wharf half a mile; forest of steeples; seventeen churches; Sabbath silence (barber fined for a Sunday shave). Literacy “more universally diffused… than in any other considerable town in the world” (visitor). Hierarchical trades (cabinetmaker above shoemaker); **Caucus Club** (“Corcass Club”) pre-settled town appointments. One in five families owned enslaved people.

**Purchase Street** side (Adams neighborhood): less fashionable—pastures, fields, orchards; wharves at the back; ropewalks west/north; New South Church a few blocks north; causeway to the mainland left; **Griffin’s Wharf** right (later tea landing). **Town House** at center on a small green with booksellers; North End chic among warehouses and shipyards. **Liberty Tree**: 120-year-old elm at Essex/Orange, copper plate as Tree of Liberty by October 1765—focus of 14 August and 1 November Stamp Act rites and later agent-resignation summonses.

## Economic and civic strain

Trade fell by half in the decade after 1735; prices soared, currency plunged; by 1760 New York and Philadelphia overtook Boston in population. Acute currency shortage and **Land Bank** (1740–41) mutated economic crisis into political challenge to Parliament—template for later resistance. Tax system: all men over 16 (exemptions: governor, LG, clergy, teachers, Harvard connections); collectors personally liable. Hailed as best-governed colonial town (fence-viewers, overseers of the poor, measurers of boards, surveyor of hemp). Produced **one-fifth** of all colonial newspapers; Harbottle Dorr from January 1765 preserved and annotated the Boston press as first draft of history. Catastrophes ~1760: wharf blast, great fire Griffin’s Wharf to arsenal, smallpox, drought, storm, earthquake—Adams’s ward hard hit.

## Crowd calendar and occupation

**Pope’s Day** (5 Nov) North vs South End battles; 1764 bloodshed then 1765 peaceful union under Sons pressure. **1747 Knowles** impressment riot—militia and mob one and the same; Knowles wished he could bombard the town.

**Townshend customs board** of five sited in Boston despite its distance from the colonial center; commissioners docked 4 November (Pope’s Day). Occupation quartering fight: law required no town lodging if barracks available at Castle (3 miles); troops nonetheless in Faneuil Hall, Town House, tents on Common, then warehouses at Crown expense—“morally in the wrong.” Desertions; private shot for desertion on the Common; sentries at town gates made civilians identify themselves—garrison-town insult. ~**2,000** soldiers = potential targets for every family; barracks blocks from Adams’s door. Civilian harassment: “bloody-back” epithets, stones, mud, spittle, snowballs, brick; magistrates dismissed soldiers’ complaints. **Christopher Seider** killing (late Feb 1770) and grand funeral under Liberty Tree immediately preceded the [[boston-massacre-1770|Massacre]].

**1771** quiet: merchandise floods; only mob drives prostitutes; warships in harbor; resistance largely on *Gazette* pages. Record blizzard (~7-foot snow) before an annual Massacre oration. Intelligence culture: confidential morning insinuation became evening common knowledge—yet 342 tea crates immersed and “no one had seen a thing”; military stores vanished before Gage; cannon under coal/manure; powder under beds.

## Tea crisis and Port Act cityscape

**Old South** as largest civic chamber for tea days; Green Dragon secrecy oaths; Liberty Tree agent summons failed. After 16 December destruction: “still as on a Sabbath evening,” then exhilaration; coffee prices rose. **Port Act** (1 June 1774 noon, tolling bells, mourning dress): deserted wharves, empty warehouses, soaring grain and wood; fish thirty miles overland; donations—CT sheep/cheese, VA grain, SC rice, Caribbean, Canada, Martha’s Vineyard Indians >£200. Common as armed camp—“not room for a cow to graze”; only warships in harbor. Government seat to **Salem**. During the [[siege-of-boston-1775|siege]]: sealed town, rats as dinner, Old South riding school in dung, Liberty Tree fourteen cords of firewood; evacuation left ruins, furniture in the harbor, grassy refuse-filled streets.
