# Karakorum (Qaraqorum)

Imperial capital of the united [[mongol-empire|Mongol Empire]] under
[[ogedei-khan|Ögödei]] and [[mongke-khan|Möngke]], on the Orkhon steppe near
older Uighur/Orkhon power centers. A polyglot court city (William of Rubruck’s
description is classic): multiple religious buildings, workshops, and the famed
Silver Tree fountain. After [[kublai-khan|Khubilai]] shifted the political center
to China, Karakorum declined; it remained symbolic of the steppe khanate and was
fought over in the Toluid civil war.

## History of settlement and control

[[weatherford-genghis-khan-making-modern-world-2004|Weatherford]] (2004) gives the
place-name as *Karakorum*, “black stones” or “black walls,” from the first stone
enclosure. [[ogedei-khan|Ögödei]] abandoned mobile steppe rule and built a fixed
capital on the Orkhon in former Kereyid/Turkic lands (his own territorial allotment;
the Onon–Kherlen homeland had passed to [[tolui|Tolui]]). Weatherford stresses the
site’s paradox: excellent as a nomad camp, poor as a permanent city—food had to
come from south of the Gobi, and winter winds were bitter.

Chinese architects designed the buildings; a Muslim-style private palace at
Kerchagan stood a day’s ride away. Functionally the city was less showpiece than
**warehouse and workshop**: roughly one-third reserved for multi-language clerks
and translators. Juvaini (via Weatherford) describes a garden compound with gates
to the four cardinal directions, a Chinese-built castle, a throne with three
stair flights, lakes for waterfowl, and enormous gold and silver drink vats
lifted by camels or elephants at feasts. Rashid al-Din has Ögödei sitting daily
after meals outside court, heaping world merchandise and giving it away to
Mongols and Muslims.

Ögödei erected houses of worship for Buddhist, Muslim, Taoist, and Christian
followers. Weatherford calls small Karakorum “probably the most religiously open
and tolerant city in the world at that time.” Christian wives of the four
brothers raised Christian influence at court (favorite grandson Shiremun =
Solomon).

Under [[mongke-khan|Möngke]], the city shifted from Ögödei-line family seat to
true **imperial capital**. Möngke seized Karakorum and surrounding territory from
Ögödei’s descendants during the post-Güyük purges; survivors had to present
loyalty there. He employed Guillaume Boucher—a Parisian goldsmith captured at
Belgrade—with fifty assistants for European-style palace art, including the
**Silver Tree**: a silver tree with four golden serpents, a pneumatic angel
trumpet, and pipes discharging four drinks (wine, black airak, rice wine, mead)
symbolizing the four directions. Census registers of people, animals, orchards,
and farms were centralized at Karakorum. William of Rubruck’s late-1253 Christmas
mass description and the 1254 inter-religious debate (Christian, Muslim, Buddhist
judges; “no one shall dare to speak words of contention”) are set at this court.

## Decline and destruction

The capital depended on continual food shipments from farmlands controlled by
[[kublai-khan|Khubilai]]. When Khubilai cut that flow in the Toluid civil war,
Karakorum faced evacuation or starvation; his army took the city; Arik Boke
retaken it temporarily in 1261 before surrendering at Shangdu (1264). Weatherford:
“For a mere three decades, Karakorum had served as capital of the Mongol Empire
before the Mongols themselves, under the command of Khubilai, looted and virtually
destroyed it”—the Silver Tree disassembled and carted away. The political center
moved to Shangdu/Xanadu and Khanbalik (Beijing).

**Weatherford thesis (not asserted as wiki fact):** “The dismounting of Ogodei at
Karakorum, and the building of stone walls so hated by his father, marked a major
step away from the policies of Genghis Khan”—opening a four-decade co-optation
from mounted warriors toward sedentary court culture.

## Why it mattered

Administrative and ceremonial heart of the [[pax-mongolica|Pax Mongolica]] at its
widest coordination; showcase of Mongol religious pluralism and dependent artisan
labor. Weatherford uses it as the hinge between conquest empire and governing
empire. Goods and prisoners from European and Middle Eastern campaigns were
routed here; simultaneous war-planning (Sung + Europe, 1230s) was framed at
khuriltais near the capital.
