# Roman Britain

The empire's remote north-western island province (Britannia), whose history from Vespasian to
Severus was "dominated by military affairs" — the Flavian conquest, the abandonment of Scotland,
and the two great frontier walls. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 18 (**Martin
Millett**), built largely from **archaeology** (the written record, dominated by Tacitus' *Agricola*
and the **Vindolanda tablets**, being thin and hard to correlate with the dig evidence). Tacitus's
*Agricola* is now ingested directly — see **[[tacitus-agricola-germany]]** — as the eyewitness (if
partial) source for the governorship of his father-in-law **[[agricola|Agricola]]**, the campaign to
Mons Graupius, and the Calgacus speech that gives the conquest its bitterest epitaph ("they make a
desert and call it peace").

## The Flavian conquest and retreat

At AD 70 three legions held a province still recovering from **Boudica**. Vespasian's governors
drove the conquest north and west: **Petillius Cerialis** (with a restored fourth legion) into
**Brigantia** to the Solway–Tyne line; **Iulius Frontinus** subduing the **Silures** of south
Wales (the new fortress at **Caerleon**); and **Gnaeus Iulius Agricola** (78–84) completing the
conquest of Wales and the north, culminating in the victory of **Mons Graupius** and the legionary
fortress at **Inchtuthil** on the Tay. The triumph was short-lived: the **Dacian wars** drained
troops (*legio II Adiutrix* withdrawn by c. 92, then *IX Hispana*), forcing the **abandonment of
Inchtuthil** and a pull-back to the **Stanegate** (Carlisle–Corbridge) line under **Trajan**, who
treated the loss of Scotland as no major strategic concern and consolidated (rebuilding the
fortresses in stone).

Millett raises the striking question of **why Britain absorbed such disproportionate resources**:
native populations were apparently low, and the linear frontiers seem "out of all proportion to
their likely defensive function." His suggestion follows the lesson of 69 — diverting the best
generals and the energies of major armies into campaigns "safely beyond the continental shores"
was itself a motive of imperial policy.

## The two walls

- **Hadrian's Wall** (begun AD 122): the ~80-Roman-mile stone-and-turf barrier from Tyne to
  Solway, with milecastles, turrets, and forts, built by all three legions (the newly arrived *VI
  Victrix*) and the **Classis Britannica** — "a prestigious undertaking; a strong symbol of the
  power of Rome," reinforcing the decision not to conquer further north.
- **The Antonine Wall** (begun c. 142): Antoninus Pius' shorter turf wall between Clyde and Forth,
  with outpost forts beyond — built, Millett argues, less for any new threat than because the new
  emperor needed **rapid military prestige**. Its occupation was complex (reinforcements from
  Germany under Iulius Verus in the mid-150s; coins of "Britannia subdued"), and it was finally
  **abandoned** in the early reign of Marcus Aurelius, probably as resources shifted to the
  **Marcomannic** front, the frontier reverting to Hadrian's Wall.

Under Commodus, **Dio reports "the greatest war" of the reign was fought in Britain** (c. 184) —
evidence of a **shift in the balance of power toward the northern tribes** (the emerging
**Maeatae** and **Caledonii** confederacies), the prelude to Septimius Severus' northern campaign
of 208.

## The slow, uneven Romanization of the civil province

Away from the frontier the record is almost wholly archaeological, and Millett stresses
**Romanization was gradual, patchy, and driven more by native-aristocratic aspiration than imperial
policy** (the Tacitean ascription of urban encouragement to Agricola cannot be sustained — the
Verulamium forum he "inspired" was begun before his governorship).

- **Cities** recovered slowly from Boudica (a decade's delay at Colchester, London, Verulamium);
  city life was at first confined to the demilitarized **south-east** (Cogidubnus' kingdom around
  Chichester). New *civitas* capitals (Exeter, Cirencester, Leicester) and the *coloniae* of
  **Gloucester and Lincoln** (founded in old fortresses, with marked economies) spread as the army
  moved; the network filled out into the second century, but unevenly and never beyond the early-
  second-century foundations. Town **defences** (earthen ramparts) appeared near-universally at the
  end of the second century — a "common policy" perhaps prompted by the northern wars of Commodus
  or Clodius Albinus' departure (197).
- **The countryside**: villa development concentrated in the **south-east** (the gradual Verulamium
  group; the exceptional **Fishbourne "palace,"** built ~AD 75 in Italian style), while the
  peripheral and northern *civitates* show "little evidence of Romanization before the third
  century." Rural **Romano-Celtic temples** and the spa-temple of **Bath (Aquae Sulis)** mark the
  same south-eastern, élite-led pattern — the romanizing native élite being "difficult to identify"
  over much of the province.

## The economy and the military magnet

Agriculture remained the pivot, supplemented by **mineral extraction** (iron in the Weald and
Forest of Dean; lead in the Mendips and Pennines; some gold, tin, copper) — but the **army was the
dominant economic force**. The military, the town- and road-building programme, and the mines were
the great consumers; supply systems show an "economic irrationality" (remote central-Gaulish
*sigillata* and Baetican oil outcompeting nearer sources) and a **northward distortion** by which,
by the second century, the whole urbanized south was drawn on to supply the northern frontier —
**London** the central node of a centralized supply organization, the **Classis Britannica**
servicing the coastal routes. Britain may have **imported basic grain** in the first two centuries,
the province's surplus struggling to meet army and infrastructure demand.

## Significance

By the elevation of Severus the **military and civil framework of Roman Britain was firmly set**:
the Hadrianic frontier fixed, the *civitas* system complete, and the great expansion of private
wealth (town houses, villas) poised to unfold in the third and fourth centuries. Millett's Britain
is the empire's clearest case of a province where **disproportionate military investment** and
**slow, élite-driven, geographically uneven Romanization** ran side by side — a frontier society
whose "civil developments cannot be isolated from the framework imposed by the progress of
conquest."

## Wickham, *Framing the Early Middle Ages* (2005)

[[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Wickham]] presents post-Roman Britain as the extreme western case of **nearly immediate systemic collapse and de-Romanization**. Romans abandoned the province c.410; archaeology shows nearly complete loss of Roman social forms. Anglo-Saxon incomers from c.450 found structures more like their own than Roman ancestors’; in unconquered Wales Latin survived only as an ecclesiastical language. Lowland Zone’s main visible Roman inheritance was settled-landscape structure; Highland Zone could add Christianity (returning to Lowlands only in the seventh century).

- Late Roman lowland Britain was a fairly ordinary provincial part of the empire (civitates, towns, hundreds of villas)—comparable to northern Gaul but **less militarized** up to 400; highland Britain was a military fringe.
- By 400 villas and towns were in recession; by **450** recognizably Roman villas and urban economies are virtually untraceable; industrial ceramic production and the potter’s wheel ended for centuries; market exchange beyond the simplest ceased. Recession turned into **catastrophe** by the third quarter of the fifth century—unparalleled speed/generality of indigenous pottery failure anywhere in the post-Roman world. Involution not reversed except on a small scale until the eighth century.
- Dramatic scale-change from late Roman c.400 was accomplished **before the Anglo-Saxons arrived**: indigenous British polities that lost ground were already micro-scale tribal groups. Wickham’s reconstruction (explicitly guesswork): a lowland aristocrat after state collapse became a chieftain whose control rested on loyalty not property rights, with material concessions that collapsed market infrastructure—Romanitas abandoned by Gildas’s mid-sixth century.
- Contrasts with relatively contained northern Gaul crisis and survival of landowning military aristocracy in [[francia]].
