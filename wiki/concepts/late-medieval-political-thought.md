# Late-Medieval Political Thought and the Sovereign State

In the fourteenth century, political thought registered a decisive shift: the **universal claims
of empire and papacy** gave way to the theory of the **sovereign territorial state**.
[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Albert Rigaudière, Ch. 1) treats this as the era "when feudalism
died" and "absolute monarchy everywhere took its first steps," guided by jurists, theologians and
propagandists who occupied "a position of prime importance in the life of these young states."

## From Learned Law to Political Science

The foundation was **learned law** — the Roman law of the glossators and the **Postglossators**,
above all **Bartolus of Sassoferrato** (1314–1357) and his pupil **Baldus** (1327–1400), for whom
"law constituted the cardinal axis of political reflection." Canon law contributed maxims that
became templates for secular government — the *sanior et maior pars* (the wiser and greater part)
and ***quod omnes tangit ab omnibus approbari debet*** ("what touches all must be approved by
all"), a "wholesale transfer of structures and effective juridical skills from the Church... to
the state." But law was increasingly challenged by a self-conscious **political science**: Nicole
Oresme presented politics as "the most worthy and the most profitable," an "architectonic" science
"queen over all," while Giles of Rome dismissed the lawyers as mere "political idiots."

## The Decline of *Dominium Mundi*

The great theme is the **collapse of the universal claims**. With the fall of the
[[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Hohenstaufen]] and the [[german-interregnum-1250|Interregnum]], the
imperial ***dominium mundi*** (world supremacy) decayed; civil and canon lawyers asserted the
sovereignty of each realm in the victorious formula ***rex in regno suo imperator est*** ("the
king is emperor in his own kingdom"). The key thinkers radicalised the secular claim:

- **Dante**, in *De Monarchia* (c. 1317), argued that temporal authority comes **directly from
  God**, not through the pope — releasing kings and emperor from papal allegiance.
- **Marsilius of Padua**, in *Defensor Pacis* (1324), denied the Church all coercive power, located
  sovereignty in **the people** and its legislating "general assembly of citizens," and made the
  state the supreme human authority — a system Rigaudière calls radically secular, even
  proto-"totalitarian."
- **William of Ockham**, the Franciscan, attacked papal claims over the temporal sphere while
  holding that imperial dignity came ***immediate a solo Deo*** (directly from God alone). Both
  Marsilius and Ockham served **Lewis of Bavaria** against [[papacy|Pope John XXII]].

The princes' assertion of independence found institutional form in the declaration of the
prince-electors at **Rhens (1338)** and Lewis of Bavaria's constitutions: the king of the Romans,
once elected, needed **no papal confirmation** — a milestone toward the [[holy-roman-empire-late-medieval|Golden
Bull of 1356]].

## The *Ministerium Regale*

In place of universal empire, theory now centred on **princely power** as a *ministerium regale* —
royal office as a divinely entrusted *service*, bound to justice and the common good. Three
political systems emerged from the "remodelling of the states": the **fragmenting Empire**, the
**triumphant national monarchies** ([[capetian-dynasty|France]], England, [[crown-of-aragon|Aragon]],
Castile), and the **Italian city-states** and [[signoria|signorie]]. Rigaudière's governing caution
is that "western Europe in the fourteenth century was as diverse as the states of which it was
composed."

## Significance

This is the intellectual charter of the **late-medieval and early-modern sovereign state**: the
decoupling of secular authority from the universal Church, the doctrine that the king is supreme
within his realm, and the elaboration of law and counsel as the architecture of government. It
extends the thirteenth-century [[state-formation|state-building]] of [[philip-iv-capetian|Philip
IV]] and [[edward-i-plantagenet|Edward I]] into explicit theory, and supplies the vocabulary
(sovereignty, consent, the common good) of later European political thought.
