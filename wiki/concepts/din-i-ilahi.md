## Definition and Scope

Din-i Ilahi (literally "Divine Religion") is the label by which historians have come to designate a spiritual movement instituted by the Mughal emperor [[akbar|Akbar]] at his court, probably from around 1582. As [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974]] cautions, "the term dîn-e ilâhî, 'divine religion,' by which the cult is now generally known, does not seem to have been used seriously in Akbar's time." The contemporary phrase was *tawhid-e ilahi* — divine monotheism — which better captures Akbar's insistence that he was extending and deepening Islamic monotheism, not replacing it.

The movement was structurally organized as a *tariqa* (Sufi brotherhood), with Akbar as its *pir* (spiritual master). Initiates wore a medallion bearing Akbar's portrait and committed themselves to "a universalist outlook (tawhid-e ilahi, 'divine monotheism'), to moral purity, and to personal devotion to himself as leader and ruler." Membership was restricted to a small circle: Hodgson notes that "a limited number of disciples" were admitted and that "all but one of the disciples were Muslims." There was no preferential treatment of disciples in matters of imperial appointment.

## Intellectual Foundations

Akbar's universalism drew on several convergent traditions:

1. **Sufi metaphysics**: The conceptual framework for the movement was provided above all by Sufi thought, especially through the influence of Abulfazl Allami and his father Shaykh Mubarak, who held that "every religious position might have its weak point, and any position was likely to have some truth to it."

2. **Falsafah (Islamic Philosophy)**: The Philosopher tradition, as received from Ibn Sina and the Illuminist school, grounded the ideal ruler as a philosopher-king. Akbar was cast by his court intellectual Abulfazl as standing in a double role: the ideal worldly ruler and the Sufi "perfect man" (*qutb*), around whom the world revolves spiritually.

3. **Indian universalist devotionalism**: The Bhakti and Kabiri movements, which stressed inward love for the one Deity beyond ritual or social condition, provided a practical model. Kabir (1440–1518), whom Hodgson identifies as moving in Sufi circles while using both Muslim and Hindu symbolism, had already demonstrated a tradition of "formal religious allegiance [being] felt to be secondary to spiritual attitude." The Sikhs, founded by Guru Nanak (b. 1469), offered a further example.

4. **Philosophical humanism**: The concept of *sulh-e kull* ("universal peace" or "conciliation") articulated by Abulfazl meant that "to reject a human achievement because its maker was of the wrong allegiance is contrary to the good sense of the civilized man." This is distinguished from mere tolerance — it is a positive civilizational claim about the equal dignity of human achievements across traditions.

## Practice and Ritual

Akbar organized regular sessions in a "house of worship" (*ibadat-khana*) from 1575 onward, gathering scholars representing first various Muslim viewpoints and later "all known religious traditions" in debate. He introduced ritual touches from multiple traditions into daily court routine, "notably regarding the sun as an emblem of the divine Light." He allowed Hindu Rajput wives to maintain their worship in the palace and contributed financially to temple-building for various faiths.

At the same time, Akbar remained within Islam — "asserting a radical monotheism of the Islamic type" — while insisting that his spiritual insights transcended communal boundaries. The Jesuit missionaries invited to the house of worship found no success in converting him, but their presence was welcomed as part of the comparative inquiry.

## Controversy and Criticism

The movement generated immediate and sustained criticism on three fronts:

- **Shari'ah-minded Muslims** accused Akbar of apostasy. The courtier and scholar Badaoni "in his history willingly distorted facts in order to blacken Akbar and his friends." Contemporary reports circulated that Akbar had abandoned Islam altogether; Hodgson judges these as "grossly distorted."

- **Ahmad Sirhindi** (d. 1624), the most formidable intellectual opponent, attacked not from outside Sufism but within it. He argued that the monist doctrine of Ibn al-Arabi (*wahdat al-wujud*, "oneness by existence") on which Sufi universalism rested represented only one stage in the mystical path, not its summit. His counter-doctrine of *wahdat al-shuhud* ("oneness by witness") restored the absolute distinction between God and creation and grounded an activist Shari'ah-minded communalism. Sirhindi founded the Mujaddidi branch of the Naqshbandi order, which became the principal organized opposition to Akbari universalism.

- **Modern scholarly controversy**: Historians have debated whether the movement constituted a "new religion" intended to supersede Islam or merely the practices of a Sufi pir extended to a wider court circle. Hodgson sides with the latter interpretation, noting that "some of Akbar's special practices [were continued] by his successors, and finally from Awrangzêb's explicit assimilation of them to the practices of a Sûfî pîr."

## Distinction from Emic and Etic Usage

The concept is primarily etic — imposed retrospectively by historians — while the emic vocabulary of Akbar's court used *tawhid-e ilahi* and framed the movement within existing Sufi categories of *pir*, *murid* (disciple), and *tariqa*. This distinction matters: calling it a "new religion" (as some modern scholars have done) projects a confessional-boundary logic back onto a context where Akbar precisely denied that such boundaries were absolute.

## Legacy

After Akbar, successors maintained varying degrees of the universalist court orientation: Jahangir cautiously, Shah Jahan with Shari'ah-minded leanings coexisting, until Aurangzeb definitively reversed course after 1679. The Mujaddidi Naqshbandis spread from India to the Ottoman lands and shaped later Islamic revivalism. The *sulh-e kull* ideal of civilizational conciliation, articulated by Abulfazl, influenced later debates about the character of Mughal rule in both subcontinental and colonial-era historiography.

## Historiography

The concept is heavily freighted by the Hindu-Muslim communalist debates of the twentieth century, particularly the Pakistani national movement, in which both Akbar (as tolerant hero or Muslim apostate) and Aurangzeb (as faithful Muslim or imperial bigot) served as symbolic stakes. Hodgson's treatment in [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974]] is one of the most carefully balanced, insisting on reading Akbar's religious initiatives within Islamicate (specifically Sufi and Falsafah) frameworks rather than as either a proto-secular pluralism or an Islamic betrayal. He notes that even the term *din-i ilahi* was an anachronistic imposition.
