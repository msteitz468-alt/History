# The Sumerian King List

The single most important native source for the political history of early
Mesopotamia — and a textbook case of how an ancient document can be both
indispensable and deeply misleading. Called by its scribes "Kingship" (from its
first word), it survives in several copies and was given final form, by its own
dating, at the end of the **Dynasty of Isin (1794 BCE)**. Discussed in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XIII, C. J. Gadd).

## Structure

The list enumerates dynasties city by city in a rigid formula: a city is named,
"the kingship resided there," king A reigned x years, B reigned y years (with
filiation if known), and at the dynasty's end "(this city) was smitten with arms,
the kingship was taken to" its successor. It counts roughly twenty post-diluvian
dynasties and ~140 kings. Behind the historical section lies a **mythic
prologue**: "kingship descended from heaven" and rested first at Eridu, then
other cities; a line of **antediluvian kings** with reigns of tens of thousands of
years; and the **Flood** that "swept over" the land (the survivor Ziusudra is the
Mesopotamian Noah of the Gilgamesh tradition).

## The two governing assumptions — and why they mislead

Gadd identifies the list's two structural premises:
1. **That "the land" was a single entity** made up of principal cities. *Partly
   true:* despite endless inter-city war there was a real sentiment of the
   solidarity of "the land" (Gadd compares the Hellenes).
2. **That only one city held the kingship at a time, in succession.** *Badly
   false:* it is demonstrable from other sources that many of these "successive"
   dynasties **reigned simultaneously** in their different cities. This single
   error "has vitiated the whole document as history": it both **exaggerates the
   total time-span** (compounding the legendary reign-lengths) and **omits**
   cities of the first importance — most strikingly **[[lagash|Lagash]]**, which
   appears nowhere in the list yet is the best-documented city of the age. (Lagash
   kept its *own* king-list and apparently "refused its record to the scribes of
   Isin.")

## Use and value

Despite these defects the list is "the outstanding authority for the oldest
history of Babylonia," reaching behind even the earliest royal monuments. The
first king attested **both** in the list and by his own inscription is
**Enmebaragisi of Kish**; from the **First Dynasty of [[ur|Ur]]** onward the
reign-lengths shrink to human proportions and agree with contemporary evidence,
giving the historian firm ground. Synchronisms (e.g. Rimush of Akkad vs. "Kaku
king of Ur") allow whole blocks of nominally successive dynasties to be collapsed.
The Epic prologue (ll. 1039–1041) credits Gilgamesh (fifth king of Uruk's dynasty after the Flood per the list tradition) with restoring "the cult-centres destroyed by the Deluge, / and set in place for the people the rites of the cosmos"; this **concurrent_with** the post-diluvian re-establishment of kingship "in the city of Kish" followed by Uruk in the king-list scheme (as discussed in the source's introduction, ll. 832–870). From Sumerian poems apparatus in range (ll. 10001–12899): "AKKA King of Kish, possibly Gilgamesh’s nephew" (glossary, ll. 12449); the "Gilgamesh and Akka" poem is among the five earlier independent Sumerian traditions (publication notes, ll. 12732–35) that supplement the list's schematic ED dynasties.

## Contested points

- **Political distortion.** Some argue the Isin scribes deliberately reshaped the
  past — projecting the *one-kingship-at-a-time* ideology — to legitimize their own
  dynasty's claim to a unique, heaven-sanctioned supremacy.
- **The Flood stratum.** Woolley identified a thick silt layer at Ur as the
  Sumerian Deluge; Gadd judges the identification "hardly acceptable" on
  stratigraphic grounds (it lies in the Ubaid period, four prehistoric "periods"
  below the earliest royal inscriptions).
- It bears directly on Mesopotamian [[mesopotamian-chronology|chronology]], since
  naïve summation of its reigns is one source of inflated early dates.

## Contrast: the Assyrian method

The Sumerian King List embodies the **opposite** of the chronological apparatus the
Assyrians later devised. Where this list *stacks* the dynasties of rival
city-states **end-to-end as if successive** — a theological scheme of a single,
divinely-rotated "kingship" — the [[assyrian-king-list|Assyrian King List and eponym
(limmu) canon]] tie a **sequential annual count** to the royal sequence, producing a
genuinely usable relative chronology. The difference is not merely technical: it
reflects a distinctively **Assyrian interest in history** against a Sumerian-Babylonian
preference for theological pattern over sequence — the reason Assyrian dates are
far more securely reconstructible than Babylonian ones.
