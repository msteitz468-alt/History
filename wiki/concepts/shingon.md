# Shingon (Japanese Esoteric Buddhism)

Shingon (*Shingon-shu*, "True Word School") is the Japanese school of esoteric Buddhism (*Mikkyo*) founded by [[kukai|Kukai]] (Kobo Daishi) on the basis of the teachings he received from the Chinese master Hui-kuo in Ch'ang-an in 805. From its establishment in the early ninth century through the end of the [[heian-period|Heian period]] and beyond, Shingon was the dominant religious tradition of the imperial court and aristocracy, providing the ritual services — exorcisms, rain prayers, healing rites, imperial protection ceremonies — that were the practical daily demand of Japan's ruling class. Source: [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]].

## Definition and Doctrinal Core

Shingon takes its name from the Japanese translation of *mantra* (Sanskrit: "true word"), reflecting its centrality of incantatory formula in practice. The supreme figure in Shingon is **Dainichi Nyorai** (Sanskrit: Mahavairocana, the Great Illuminator), the cosmic Buddha who encompasses all reality. Dainichi differs from the historical Buddha Sakyamuni: rather than a being who attained enlightenment through long practice, Dainichi is enlightenment itself — timeless, omnipresent, identical with the phenomenal universe when properly apprehended.

Shingon doctrine holds that the universe is the body, speech, and mind of Dainichi, expressed through the **Three Mysteries** (*sanmitsu*): mudra (hand gestures), mantra (verbal formulas), and mandala/visualization (mental concentration on sacred diagrams). By aligning one's own three activities — physical gesture, verbal utterance, mental image — with those of Dainichi, the practitioner does not merely approach enlightenment but achieves identity with it in this body and this lifetime (*sokushin jobutsu*, "attaining Buddhahood in this very body"). Kukai argued this was unattainable through exoteric Buddhist practice, where enlightenment is a distant goal requiring multiple lifetimes.

The two fundamental graphic maps of Shingon cosmology are the **kongokai mandala** (diamond realm, *vajradhatu*) and the **taizokai mandala** (embryo realm, *garbhadhatu*), which together represent the wisdom and compassion aspects of Dainichi. Initiation into these mandalas (*kanjo*, "sprinkling"), administered through increasingly high levels — kechien kanjo (linking), intermediate, and the highest *dembo kanjo* (transmitting the Dharma) — constituted the heart of Shingon transmission.

Shingon scriptures center on the *Dainichi-kyo* (Mahavairocana Sutra) and the *Kongochokyo* (Vajrasekhara Sutra). More than 130 such esoteric texts had already reached Japan before Kukai's birth — esotericism was not his invention but his systematic organization and legitimization of what had been scattered practice.

## The Claim to Superiority

Kukai's *Jujushin ron* (Treatise on the Ten Stages of Mind, 830) presented a ranked classification of all Buddhist doctrines and secular philosophies from lowest to highest. Shingon occupied the tenth and highest stage. All other schools — Confucianism, Taoism, Theravada, Hosso, Sanron, Tendai, and [[zen-buddhism|Zen]] — occupied lower stages. Submitted to the throne, the treatise "not a word of protest was heard from Nara," indicating how thoroughly Kukai had won the goodwill of the Buddhist establishment. Kukai had also argued to Saicho that esoteric knowledge "could be transmitted only 'from mind to mind'" and that teaching Mikkyo without proper transmission was "stealing the doctrine" — a claim that established Shingon's exclusive control over authentic esoteric transmission.

## Institutional History: Toji, Koya, and the Post-Kukai Schools

### Toji and Court Service

Kukai's major Kyoto institution was **Toji** (Eastern Temple), the chief state-supported temple of the capital, placed under his exclusive Shingon control in 823 by Emperor Saga and confirmed by his successor Junna with a permanent limit of fifty Shingon monks in residence. The annual *go-shichinichi mi-shiho* (eighth to fourteenth of the first month) performed by Shingon monks from Toji in the palace's Shingon Chapel became the most important annual religious rite of the court. Shingon monks from Toji also conducted the *taigenho* rite in the Jonei Hall — a complex ceremony using weapons laid before an elaborate altar to protect the emperor from enemies.

Kukai's disciples divided the administration of Shingon's four major centers: Jitsue at Toji, Shinzei at Takaosanji, Shinga (Kukai's younger brother) at the Shingon'in in Todaiji, and Shinzen (Kukai's nephew) at Kongobuji on Koya. The divided leadership sometimes placed disciples on opposing sides of aristocratic succession disputes. In 850 Shinzei prayed for Prince Koretaka's succession while Shinga prayed for Prince Korehito (the future Emperor Seiwa); Fujiwara no Yoshifusa backed Korehito and Shinga, whose prayers succeeded. Shinga was rewarded with the Toji abbotship in 860 — the first monk permitted to enter the palace grounds in a carriage.

### Koyasan and the Toji-Koya Rivalry

[[koyasan|Mount Koya]] (Koyasan) in Kii Province was Kukai's chosen retreat and place of interment. Kongobuji, the primary monastery complex there, served the Shingon school's training function, while Toji served its court ritual function. The two centers fell into decades of rivalry over the right to conduct ordination examinations and the status of their abbots. A critical 853 decree shifted ordination examinations from Koya to Toji (and Takaosanji); Koya had this reversed in 885 but Toji regained control in 897. A final compromise increased annual ordinands to ten, distributed among Toji, Koya, and Takaosanji.

The rivalry was resolved in 919 when the court decreed that the Toji abbot (*choja*, "elder") would concurrently serve as overseer of Kongobuji — formally subordinating Koya to Toji while preventing a schism. This administrative solution contrasted with Tendai's failure to prevent the Sammon-Jimon split.

Koya was restored to importance from 1016 by the monk Kishin (also known as Joyo), who had a vision identifying Kongobuji as the earthly manifestation of Tosotsu (Tusita) Heaven, the realm of the Future Buddha Miroku. The prestigious monk Ningai (the "Rain Bishop," founder of the Ono branch) persuaded former regent Fujiwara no Michinaga to make a pilgrimage to Koya in 1023 — beginning a tradition of aristocratic and imperial pilgrimages (Fujiwara regents Yorimichi, Morozane, Moromichi, Tadazane, Yorinaga; Retired Emperors Shirakawa and Toba each visited three times) that transformed Koya into Japan's most important sacred mountain.

By the late eleventh century Kukai was believed not to have died but to have entered a deep trance (*nyujo*) from which he would emerge when Miroku descends. Reports that Kangen found Kukai's body intact when opening the tomb in 921 circulated widely. Kukai was identified successively with Miroku and then with Dainichi himself; Koya was described as the eternal Pure Land of Dainichi Nyorai (*Mitsugon Jodo*).

### Ninnaji and Daigoji

The Shingon school developed particularly close ties with the imperial family through two Kyoto-area temples. **Ninnaji**, established in 888 west of the palace, became a Shingon temple in 900 when Retired Emperor Uda — himself tonsured by the Toji abbot Yakushin — became its first *monzeki* (abbot of imperial lineage). No fewer than twenty-two of Ninnaji's abbots were ordained imperial princes (*hoshinno*), eight of them in the Heian period. **Daigoji** (southeast Kyoto, built 874 by Shobo) received lavish support from Emperor Daigo, who sponsored the construction of its three major halls (904–907), decreed that only monks of Shobo's lineage could serve as abbot, and was buried there along with his sons Suzaku and Murakami. By the mid-twelfth century the Daigoji complex encompassed 42 main halls, 4 pagodas, 3 imperial villas, 4 sutra repositories, and 183 dormitories.

### The Ono and Hirosawa Branches

By the twelfth century Shingon had diversified into twelve subschools (*ryu*), divided into two main groups:

- **Ono ryu**: tracing lineage to Shobo through the fourth-generation disciple Ningai, who established Zuishin'in in 991 in the Ono district of Kyoto. Split into six subschools centered on Daigoji and Kanjuji complexes.
- **Hirosawa ryu**: tracing lineage to Yakushin (seventh abbot of Toji) through Retired Emperor Uda. Named after Hirosawa Pond (northwest Kyoto) where Kancho, Yakushin's third-generation disciple, founded Henjoji in 989. Split into six subschools, four centered on Ninnaji subtemples.

The subschools differed not in doctrine but in the minutiae of ritual — reflecting the character of Shingon as a tradition where correct performance was as essential as correct belief.

## Shingon and Tendai: Parallel and Competition

Shingon and [[tendai|Tendai]] dominated Heian Buddhism together but represented different orientations. Where Tendai built a comprehensive scholastic synthesis taking the Lotus Sutra as its summit, Shingon asserted that the esoteric scriptures of Dainichi stood above even the Lotus and could not be approached through exoteric study. Where Tendai under Saicho sought institutional independence through a confrontational ordination reform, Kukai built Shingon's position through personal alliances and conciliatory institutional strategy. Where Tendai splintered into Sammon and Jimon factions in violent conflict from 993, Shingon achieved the formal unification of Toji and Koya under a single choja in 919.

The rivalry was not without intellectual dimension. Tendai's Ennin and Enchin acquired Mikkyo training specifically to challenge Shingon's esoteric monopoly; the resulting Taimitsu (Tendai Mikkyo) gradually rivaled Tomitsu (Shingon Mikkyo) in court prestige. By the time Ennin died in 864, Tendai had effectively broken Shingon's exclusive access to imperial patronage.

## Historiography

The Cambridge History entry (ch. 7, Stanley Weinstein) draws on the *Kobo Daishi zenshu* (eight volumes, Mikkyo bunka kenkyujo edition, 1965–68), the *Go-shorai mokuroku*, the *Toji choja bunin*, the *Koya shunju hennen shuroku*, and modern studies by Shimode Sekiyo, Sonoda Koyu, and Katsuno Ryushin. Key primary sources include Kukai's own *Jujushin ron*, the three early biographies of Kukai (none of which accounts for his 806–809 whereabouts), and the Heian chronicle series. Yoshito Hakeda's *Kukai: Major Works* (Columbia, 1972) is the standard Western introduction to Kukai's thought; Taiko Yamasaki's *Shingon: Japanese Esoteric Buddhism* (1988) provides accessible doctrinal survey.
