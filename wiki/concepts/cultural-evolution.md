# Cultural Evolution (Group Selection of Moral Traditions)

The process by which rules of conduct, moral traditions, and institutions are selected through the differential survival and growth of the groups that practice them. Successful rules spread because the groups following them increase in numbers and power; unsuccessful rules lead to the relative decline or disappearance of the groups that adhere to them.

## Definition and Scope

Unlike genetic evolution, cultural evolution operates on learned, transmitted rules and practices. Transmission is not automatic: it requires mechanisms (including religion, taboo, “symbolic truths,” education, and prestige) that preserve rules across generations even when individuals do not understand their function or when the rules conflict with innate instincts.

The key selective criterion in Hayek’s account is the capacity of the rule-set to generate and sustain an extended order of cooperation that supports larger populations and greater wealth than tribal or centrally directed alternatives.

## Relation to Instinct and Reason

Rules that survive are often older than reason and lie “between instinct and reason.” They are not the product of conscious design and frequently cannot meet constructivist tests of justification. Yet they are the product of evolutionary selection at the group level.

Hayek emphasises that we did not adopt these rules because we foresaw their benefits; groups that happened to follow them survived and multiplied.

## Role of Religion and “Symbolic Truths”

Non-factual or mythical beliefs frequently served as the transmission and enforcement mechanism that allowed beneficial but counter-intuitive rules (honesty toward strangers, respect for several property, restraint on violence) to persist long enough for selection to operate at scale. Even an agnostic can recognise the historical service rendered by such beliefs.

## Earlier Statement in *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960)

The 1988 formulation was already substantially present in
[[hayek-constitution-of-liberty-1960|*The Constitution of Liberty*]] chs. 2–4
and 10:
- Traditions and institutions are "tools" of cumulative growth "without ever
  having been designed by any one mind"; drawing on Adam Ferguson, institutions
  are "the result of human action but not the execution of any human design."
- Priority claim: the theory was not borrowed from biology — "it was from the
  theories of social evolution that Darwin and his contemporaries derived the
  suggestion for their theories" (with James Hutton as one channel); and the
  mechanism is distinct: "the selection by imitation of successful institutions
  and habits," not inheritance of physical traits.
- Group selection of values already stated: "the ultimate decision about what
  is good or bad will be made not by individual human wisdom but by the decline
  of the groups that have adhered to the 'wrong' beliefs" — with Hayek's
  caution against reading this as a naturalistic justification.
- Law itself as an evolved institution: rules of conduct develop from habit
  into abstract law "as little invented by any one mind as language or money"
  (see [[rule-of-law]]); opinion change filters "slowly downward from the top
  of a pyramid" of abstraction (citing Dicey), so legislative opinion is
  typically a prior generation's.
- The paradox of liberal traditionalism: "a successful free society will
  always in a large measure be a tradition-bound society"; freedom of action —
  practical know-how, not just thought — is a chief generator of advance.
- Antiquity of the anti-design view: Cato (via Cicero) praised the Roman
  constitution as founded "not in one generation, but in a long period of
  several centuries" — against Sparta, the rationalists' model (Rousseau,
  Robespierre, Saint-Just). Hayek also credits reactionary thinkers (Coleridge,
  Möser, Bonald, De Maistre) with anticipating the theory of grown
  institutions while admiring free growth "only in the past."

## The Trilogy Statement: *Law, Legislation and Liberty* (1973–79)

Vol. 1 ch. 1 gives the mid-career formulation: societal orderliness is due to a process "in which practices which had first been adopted for other reasons, or even purely accidentally, were preserved because they enabled the group in which they had arisen to prevail over others." "Man is as much a rule-following animal as a purpose-seeking one"; "Man acted before he thought and did not understand before he acted"; learning from experience "is a process not primarily of reasoning but of the observance, spreading, transmission and development of practices which have prevailed because they were successful." The lineage claim is spelled out (Mandeville, Hume, Ferguson, Smith, Burke, Humboldt, Savigny, Maine, Menger's 1883 restatement), including the Darwin-priority argument — Darwin applied "a concept which he had largely learned from the social sciences" — and the double rejection of Social Darwinism (which "concentrated on the selection of individuals rather than on that of institutions and practices") and of historicist stage-theories (Comte, Hegel, Marx). Ethology is enlisted: individuals "learned to observe (and enforce) rules of conduct long before such rules could be expressed in words," down to the territorial "property" of crayfish and robins.

The Vol. 3 Epilogue, "The Three Sources of Human Values" (1979), is the fullest pre-*Fatal Conceit* statement. Against Pugh's sociobiology (values either genetic or rational): "Culture is neither natural nor artificial, neither genetically transmitted nor rationally designed." Modern man carries **three superimposed layers of rules** — genetically inherited instinctive drives from small-band life, unchosen traditions spread by group success, and "the thin layer of rules, deliberately adopted." The signature reversals: "Man did not adopt new rules of conduct because he was intelligent. He became intelligent by submitting to new rules of conduct"; "Cultural selection is not a rational process; it is not guided by but it creates reason." Historically, the load-bearing rules "were all initially infringements of customary rules — so many falls from grace": private property in land, contract enforcement, competition with fellow craftsmen, lending at interest. The prophets "from Moses to Plato and St Augustine, from Rousseau to Marx and Freud" condemned the very practices that made their civilizations possible; Freud, "through his profound effects on education, … has probably become the greatest destroyer of culture" (balanced against Donald Campbell's 1975 APA address conceding psychology's anti-tradition assumption "may now be regarded as scientifically wrong"). The closing verdict: the twentieth century as "an outstanding age of superstition," chiefly connected with Marx and Freud.

## Controversy and Relation to Social Darwinism

Hayek explicitly distinguishes the argument from “social Darwinism” in the crude sense and from the misuse of evolutionary ideas to justify inequality within a society. The selection is between groups and their rule-packages over time; within a successful order, the rules themselves are abstract and egalitarian in application.
