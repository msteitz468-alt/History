## Definition and Scope

"Pacific Bodies and Personal Space Redefined" is an analytical framework developed by Jacqueline Leckie (in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]) to describe how colonialism in the Pacific, from approximately 1850 to 1960, extended beyond political and economic reorganization to restructure the most intimate dimensions of life: bodies, health, sexuality, reproduction, domestic space, and personal identity.

The framework draws on Talal Asad's formulation that colonial violence involved "monumental destruction at every level, precisely because it attempted to reorganize not only the polity, but social relations, economic structures, and values. It was not just the violence of pillage, but the violence of self-conscious and self-righteous transformation of social life." Leckie adds that this restructuring operated "not just a question of conquest but of law: an attempt to redefine norms as well as practices."

The central theoretical tool is Michel Foucault's concept of **biopower**: state hegemony exercised through control of bodies and minds, not necessarily via "raw coercion but through the power of discourse and the inculcation, internalization, and performance of — in the Pacific — colonized and racialized bodies."

**What the concept includes:**
- Medical and sanitary regimes (mapping, classification, inspection, quarantine, confinement)
- Reproductive and sexual regulation (maternity, childbirth, venereal disease, prostitution)
- Institutional confinement (leprosaria, lunatic asylums)
- Hygiene and sanitation legislation
- Dress, comportment, domestic architecture
- Education as embodied transformation
- Indigenous medical systems and their partial survival

**What the concept distinguishes:**
Leckie is careful to separate this from a top-down hegemony model. The chapter "emphasizes the entanglement between colonial administrations, missions, philanthropic bodies, migrant communities, and indigenous cultures." Pacific peoples "were hardly passive victims": they evaded, resisted, negotiated, incorporated, and sometimes actively cooperated with health regimes. "Even the fear of handing over one's faeces to a stranger could be overcome, when exchanged for useful goods such as fishhooks."

## Analytical Dimensions

### Biopower and Governmentality

Foucault's biopower framework structures the chapter: populations are governed by counting, classifying, and regulating bodies rather than (or in addition to) direct coercion. Key Pacific manifestations:

- **Censuses.** Population and medical censuses "counted, classified, and reordered bodies into new definitions and categories." In New Guinea during the 1920s, health patrols gathered wide-ranging data providing "a perfect graphic record of the health state of the whole area." In the Trobriand Islands, Dr Rayner Bellamy's census represented "a hallmark of modern governmentality: an individual relationship between each person in a population and the state."
- **Racial categories.** "Races were defined and measured, and sociobiological difference was assigned into categories within colonial engagements." Fiji's censuses classified bodies into racial categories (Fijians, Indians, Europeans, Half-castes, Polynesians).
- **Bodily inspection.** Interwar surveys by Rockefeller Foundation director Sylvester Lambert counted and treated entire populations: in Rotuma in 1928, "2,020 out of 2,040 bodies were inspected." Lambert's census required villagers to strip to the waist for inspection of head, trunk, limbs, and genitalia. European officials "inspected the genitalia of Trobriand Islanders aged over eight years on the grounds of treating venereal disease."
- **Mind census.** Fiji conducted a "census of minds" in its general censuses 1911–46, asking respondents to identify any "Lunatic, imbecile, or feeble-minded" person in their household. The 1884 Lunacy Ordinance constituted madness as a category of the state.

### Medicine as "Tool of Empire"

Medicine functioned as "a 'tool' of empire — a sometimes subtle but powerful means to convince indigenous peoples of the wisdom of pacification, colonial rule, and the 'magic of modernity.'" Key figures:
- **Dr Raphael Cilento** (Australia/Pacific): pioneered colonial and tropical medicine; transferred public health concepts from Papua New Guinea to Australia.
- **Dr Sylvester Lambert** (Rockefeller Foundation): conducted Pacific-wide health surveys; advocate of yaws campaigns and the Central Medical School in Suva.
- **Medically trained missionaries**: provided much early health care; their role "persisted into the twentieth century in weak or divided colonies such as the New Hebrides."

### Indigenous Health Ontologies in Tension

Pacific medical systems shared common assumptions: "illness could lie within a disturbance among ancestral spirits that had been caused by failure to fulfil social obligations or conform to cultural norms. The roots of illness and healing lay within the community, with the supernatural, or in sorcery." Specific examples:
- In Hawai'i, disease emanated from losing *mana* or offending an *amakua* (ancestral god); "only the restoration of mana would restore wellness."
- Among Samoans, "'doing sickness' aimed to re-establish spiritual wholeness, for the sick person and the social group, through the processes of care and cure."
- In Kanak society, "disease and disability were rarely isolated or separated from social life."

Western medicine sought to replace these systems, but "there was extensive persistence of traditional healing rituals and medical remedies, as well as resistance to coercion and the negotiation of Western medical systems. Pacific peoples frequently shunned medical centres or hid when villages were surveyed."

### Reproductive Bodies and Sexual Regulation

Imperial projects "tried to reshape reproduction and sexuality." The dominant discourse blamed depopulation on mothers:

- Fiji's 1893–96 Commission Decrease Report attributed population decline to "General insouciance of the native mind, heedlessness of mothers, and weakness of maternal instinct." Practices considered harmful included arduous pregnancy work and "even fishing by child-bearing women."
- Women's committees across the Pacific (Au Vaine in the Cook Islands, Langa Fonua in Tonga, Komiti Tumana in Samoa) were "bolstered by the state and the church to play a key role in the redefinition of gendered roles."
- In Fiji (1927), women convicted of offences could be ordered to make mats or *masi* (cloth) as punishment.
- Hawaiian regulations from the 1840s–1850s banned adultery, prostitution, and *moe kolohe* ("mischievous sleeping"), pushed by missionaries and supported by ruling chiefs.
- During the Pacific War, "women's bodies, mobility, and above all their sexuality came under even stricter control when foreign troops were stationed on the islands."

Leckie connects this to a broader metropolitan pattern: "working-class mothers in late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Britain were blamed for 'population problems, racial degeneration and imperial decline'" — Pacific mothers entered the same colonial discourse.

### Segregated Spaces: Leprosaria and Asylums

The "most feared spaces" were sites of separation and confinement:

**Leprosaria.** From 1890–1925, Cook Islands leprosy sufferers were isolated on motu (small reef islets); the facility "became known as Molokai or Morokai — after Hawai'i's leprosarium." Between 1865 and 1900, approximately 5,000 Hawaiians were diagnosed with leprosy and separated to the "natural prison" on Kalaupapa peninsula. The pan-Pacific Central Lepers' Hospital at Makogai (Fiji) received patients from Cook Islands, Tonga, Niue, and Samoa 1911–1969. In New Caledonia, a leprosy colony established on a former penitentiary site eventually transferred to Ducos, a former high-security prison; "no one was discharged from Ducos until 1976."

**Lunatic asylums.** In Fiji, the asylum founded in 1884 was "built next to Suva's gaol and cemetery, and known within the community as a 'prison' rather than as a hospital" — locals called it *vale ni mate* (place of death). "On islands that lacked mental health facilities, mentally ill people were often confined in gaol." In Papua, people certified insane were confined at Bomana gaol until 1959.

The pattern across both institutions: "policies towards both groups were underlaid with negative connotations of danger, pollution, and contagion, but also overlaid with humane and civilizing discourses of care, cure, and compassion."

### Sanitation as Cultural Imperialism

Sanitation reform was pervasive and invasive. Its drivers included miasmatic theory (later superseded by germ theory), Rockefeller Foundation hookworm campaigns, and explicit concerns about economic productivity. Its effects went far beyond hygiene:

- Villages were relocated or amalgamated "ostensibly for hygienic reasons" but often for colonial control.
- Pigs had to be enclosed 100 metres from villages in Fiji.
- Burial of the dead within domestic spaces was prohibited (Fiji Cemeteries Act, 1871).
- In Samoa, new regulations prohibited refuse from polluting drinking water, latrines, and bathing pools; "even rotten breadfruit came under scrutiny."
- In Guam, "practices such as washing hands and faces before eating, and wearing shoes, became emblems of progress and evolution, rather than neutral indicators of better sanitary practices."
- Papuans were forbidden from wearing clothes on their upper bodies because this was "considered a danger to health."
- The British "insisted that Fijians abandon hairstyles considered to be evidence of heathenism."

As Leckie summarizes: "Sanitation may have denoted 'progress' and saved lives, but it could also be a form of cultural imperialism and assimilation."

### Drugs and Medical Technology

Before sulfa drugs and antibiotics (1930s–40s), medical interventions had "limited efficacy in curing disease." Yaws treatment was the exception: salvarsan, then improved arsenicals, produced visibly dramatic results. Lambert observed that yaws treatment "in Pacific islanders is probably the finest demonstration to them of the value of Western Medicine." Between 1923 and 1926, 75,000 yaws injections were administered in Western Samoa (population c. 38,003).

Post-World War II, experimental psychiatric technologies arrived: Cardiazol (Metrazol) shock therapy in Fiji (1947), then insulin coma therapy and ECT. Largactil (chlorpromazine) reached Fiji in 1955. Pacific hospitals became sites of "relatively open field for experimentation."

### Cooperative Bodies

Leckie insists the framework is not simply top-down. Colonial health projects succeeded only through Indigenous cooperation:
- In Cook Islands, Chief Medical Officers Dr Pohau Ellison (Māori) and Dr Tom Davis (Cook Islands Māori) bridged indigenous and colonial medicine, working with *ta'unga* (indigenous healers).
- Fiji's Native Medical School (from 1885) and Suva Colonial Hospital (from 1908) trained Native Medical Practitioners and Native Obstetric Nurses who became "significant intermediaries" merging traditional and colonial medicine.
- Women's Committees, island councils, and village health committees actively enforced and promoted sanitation.
- Annual village inspection events (Cook Islands *tutaka*; Western Samoa *malaga*) became important communal occasions enrolling local leaders and showcasing "healthy bodies."

## Emic / Etic Distinction

The concept is primarily **etic** — an analytical framework applied retrospectively by historians. However, it captures emic realities: Pacific communities developed their own linguistic responses to colonial medical institutions (*vale ni mate*; *ma'i ho'oka'awale 'ohana* — "the disease that separates families"; *bakava* — "tin shack" / crazy person). These terms encode Indigenous experience of bodily and spatial restructuring.

## Limitations and Critiques

**Uneven colonial reach.** The chapter explicitly acknowledges that "vast regions, as in Papua New Guinea, [were] barely touched by public health." The framework risks over-generalizing from better-documented colonies (Fiji, Hawai'i, Guam, Cook Islands) to the entire Pacific.

**Colonial archives.** Most source material is colonial: health survey reports, government censuses, mission records, administrative correspondence. Indigenous voices are mediated and underrepresented.

**Agency without structure.** Emphasizing Pacific agency risks under-weighting the coercive conditions within which that agency operated — fines, imprisonment, exile, enforced inspection.

**Temporal periodization.** The 1850–1960 frame is somewhat arbitrary. Many processes (missionary medical work, legal regulation of sexuality) began earlier; many consequences (stigma around psychiatric institutions, disrupted traditional medicine) extend into the present.
