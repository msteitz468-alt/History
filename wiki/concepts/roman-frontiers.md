# Roman Frontiers (the Frontier as Zone, not Line)

The nature of the Roman imperial **frontier** — what the *fines imperii* were, how Romans
conceived of imperial space, and why the empire stopped where it did. The interpretation is
**C. R. Whittaker's**, the major thematic chapter of [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] (Ch. 8): a
sustained argument that Roman frontiers were **zones of transition, not defensive lines**, and
that "grand strategy" is an anachronism. Underpins the [[roman-grand-strategy|grand-strategy
controversy]].

## Roman concepts of space

Roman frontier thinking was inherited from Greeks (frontiers as **cultural**, not physical — the
divide between measured and unmeasurable space) and Etruscans (the ritual quartering of terrestrial
space mirroring the celestial *mundus*; the cult of **Terminus** beside Jupiter). The surveyors'
(*agrimensores*) art distinguished **organized, centuriated land** (*limitatio*) from the
unallocated but controlled land beyond it (*arcifinius*, "protecting" the organized land) — a key
distinction: **administered land was not the limit of Roman ownership or control**. The word
***limes*** originally meant a **road or path** between allotments, an *administered boundary*,
and only came to mean "military frontier" in the third century. Maps were rare and "always
associated with conquest" (Caesar's world map; **Agrippa's map**, displayed by Augustus in 7 BCE,
*orbis urbi spectandus*).

## The ideology of *imperium sine fine*

Whittaker insists the **ideology of limitless empire** remained central throughout the period.
Augustus' proudest *Res Gestae* boast was *imperio nostro fines auxi* ("I extended the boundaries
of our rule"); Strabo lists every physical feature of the empire **except frontiers**, and notes
Augustus took the "barbarian sector, adjoining the tribes **not yet** subdued." The deathbed
*consilium* "that the empire should be confined within limits" (which Tiberius obeyed) was at most
**temporary prudence** after the [[battle-of-teutoburg-forest-9ce|Varus disaster]], not a
permanent renunciation of conquest. *Imperium sine fine* stayed the mark of a "good emperor" —
proclaimed by Trajan (Dacia, Mesopotamia, "his desire for glory"), even by the unwarlike Antoninus
Pius (the Antonine Wall advance), and by Marcus' projected trans-Danubian provinces. The
"defensive imperialism" of Appian and Aelius Aristides was, Whittaker argues, a **Greek** courtly
gloss on the quiescence of Hadrian and Pius, not the Roman view (Tacitus, Suetonius, Florus all
still spoke the language of expansion).

## Walls were not the edge of empire

The visible works — **Hadrian's Wall**, the **Antonine Wall**, the African ***fossatum*** and
*clausurae*, the German **outer *limes*** — are the most misread evidence. Whittaker shows:

- They were **not uniform** (Hadrian's Wall manned by thousands of auxiliaries; the Tripolitanian
  *clausurae* by small late-second-century detachments; Mauretania Tingitana with no linear
  frontier at all; Arabia's "frontier" merely the trunk road, the **Via Nova Traiana**).
- Roman control demonstrably extended **far beyond** them: census-taking north of the Solway as
  Trajan staked it; Roman buildings and tiles across the middle Danube in Slovakia/Moravia;
  military inscriptions **beyond** the Euphrates and Arabian roads outnumbering those on the
  "frontier" itself; Palmyrene militia operating from still-Parthian Dura Europus.
- In Tunisia the *clausurae* walls lay **north of** Trajanic tribal allotments — vivid proof the
  wall was not the outer *fines*. The *Historia Augusta*'s claim that Hadrian's Wall "divided
  barbarians and Romans" is dismissed as a fourth-century anachronism.

## East versus West — and why frontiers stopped where they did

A single "grand strategy" is *a priori* implausible given the contrast between the poor, little-
urbanized *gentes* of the West (conquered for **glory**) and the hellenized, centralized states of
the East (drawn by **wealth**, and dominated by the one true rival, **Parthia**, with which Rome
negotiated borders as between equals — though even there the Euphrates was never formally accepted
as *the* frontier, and Rome launched twice as many attacks as it received). Frontiers were
**culturally mixed zones** ("marches"): La Tène and Germanic cultures straddled the Rhine and
Danube; the Brigantes and Carvetii straddled Hadrian's Wall.

Whittaker's positive thesis, drawing on **Owen Lattimore's** study of China, is **ecological and
economic**: frontiers fell along the **margin of profitable agriculture** — the Wall zone where
crop culture gives way to cattle, the African *limes* at "the natural limit of profitable cereal
farming," the Syrian road on the 200–250 mm isohyet between sown land and steppe. The stopping-
point was a **"zone of choice"** set by the *economy of conquest* (Appian on the unprofitability
of ruling poor barbarians), not a strategic line — which explains the otherwise puzzling
fluctuations (Hadrian's Wall vs. the Antonine Wall; the German *limes* advance under Pius). Rivers
(Rhine, Euphrates) were **arterial supply lines behind** the forward zone, not defensive barriers.

Finally, this explains the frontiers' eventual **collapse**: by attracting supply and bringing
order, prosperity, and social advance to the peoples beyond them, the frontiers contained a
"built-in obsolescence" — those peoples grew strong enough to turn on Rome.

## Significance and contest

Whittaker (with A. R. Birley, **J. C. Mann**, and **Benjamin Isaac**) overturns the older picture
of scientific, defensive, exclusionary frontiers — above all **Edward Luttwak's** *Grand Strategy
of the Roman Empire* — replacing it with **open frontiers, designed for movement and surveillance,
through which Roman power reached far beyond the administered line**. The reading remains contested
(see [[roman-grand-strategy]]); it also rests heavily on archaeology that is far more advanced in
the West (Britain especially) than in the strategically weightier East.

## The frontiers in the crisis and after (CAH XII)

[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 8 (**John Wilkes**) carries the frontiers through the
[[third-century-crisis|crisis]]. Under the **Severans** the limits *advanced* — to the **Tigris**
(the new province of Mesopotamia), to the **northern Sahara fringe** (the African oasis-forts and
the *Nova Praetentura* of Mauretania), and in Britain — the high-water mark of direct occupation.
The crisis then concentrated its damage on **four invasion zones** (the lower Rhine/northern Gaul,
the upper Rhine/upper Danube, the lower Danube/Black Sea, and the eastern provinces), with much of
the empire (Britain, Africa, Spain) little touched; the "epigraphic habit" collapsed around 250.
Wilkes (with Mann and Isaac, against Luttwak) again **rejects empire-wide "grand strategy."** The
response was reactive: subsidies, the evacuation of **Dacia** and the **Agri Decumates** for shorter
lines, and — under [[diocletian|Diocletian]] and [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]] — a vast,
**centrally directed** programme of fortification: the densely-forted **Strata Diocletiana**
(Arabia–Palmyra–Euphrates), the **Saxon Shore** coastal forts, the **claustra Alpium Iuliarum**
barring Italy, walled cities and hill-refuges, and bridgehead forts. Many Severan units still appear
in the **Notitia Dignitatum**. The chapter also tracks the rise of the **imperial "sub-capitals"**
(Trier, Milan, Sirmium, Nicomedia, Antioch, Thessalonica, and finally
[[founding-of-constantinople-330|Constantinople]]) as the centre of gravity shifted from Rome to the
frontiers.
