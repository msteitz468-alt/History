# Athenian Drama (Tragedy and Comedy)

## A civic and religious institution

For the fifth-century Athenian, [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 8f, B. M. W. Knox)
argues, "the dominant literary phenomenon was the drama" — and it was something modern
categories cannot quite capture, since the chief medium of literature was **public
performance**, not the written text, and the performances were simultaneously an **act of
worship**. Tragedy and comedy were competitive events mounted **at the city's expense** at
the two festivals of **Dionysus** — the **City (Great) Dionysia** in March–April, which (as
the sailing season opened) drew visitors from across the Greek world, and the **Lenaea** in
December. Crucially, Greece had **no holy book, no priestly hierarchy, no fixed sacred
history**: theology was the business of the **poets** (Homer and Hesiod, then the
tragedians), so that the relation between "religion" and "literature" was, Knox writes, "a
phenomenon unique in the history of the West."

## The festival and the liturgy

The City Dionysia was a four-day religious holiday (cut to three in the war), preceded by a
procession (*pompe*) with phallic and satiric elements recalling the god's fertility cult.
Before the plays the audience (the theatre held ~14,000) saw the **tribute of the
[[delian-league|empire]]** displayed, the war-orphans paraded in arms, and honorific decrees
proclaimed — "a demonstration to the Greek world of Athenian imperial power." The contests:
twenty **dithyrambs** (tribal choruses of fifty), three sets of **three tragedies each
followed by a satyr play**, and five (later three) **comedies**. The chorus was trained and
financed by a wealthy **choregos** as a *leitourgia* — the same liturgy-system that funded
warships — so that, like the lawcourts and Assembly, "the theatre of Dionysus reflected the
egalitarian spirit of democratic Athens."

## Tragedy

Drawn from the central **myths of the Greek past**, tragedy reflected civic and political
concerns (the fate of a *polis* often hangs in the balance) while avoiding direct
contemporary allusion. Knox traces the three masters:

- **Aeschylus** (d. 456) — the *Persians* (472, uniquely set in contemporary reality), the
  *Seven*, the *Suppliants*, and the **Oresteia** (458), whose trilogic form works "the
  mystery of the will of Zeus through human crime and suffering" to civic reconciliation —
  the founding myth of the [[areopagus|Areopagus]], staged the very years of
  [[ephialtes-reform-462bce|Ephialtes' reform]] (Aeschylus "spurs the audience to think... he
  does not tell them what to think").
- **Sophocles** (first victory 468) — grown up in the imperial city, his tragedy sees the
  *polis* "as a problem rather than a solution"; the lonely, intransigent hero (Antigone,
  Ajax, Philoctetes, Oedipus) defies the community, often on **religious** grounds, amid an
  archaic awe of **pollution** and **hero-cult**. Dodds called him "the last great exponent
  of the archaic world view."
- **Euripides** (debut 455) — exposed to the [[sophists|sophistic]] questioning of the age,
  he stages **scepticism about prophecy and pollution** and lets his characters debate the
  gods' justice "with a freedom and sophistication owed to the philosophical speculation of
  the age" — yet his gods (the Aphrodite of *Hippolytus*, the Dionysus of the *Bacchae*) are
  solid, terrifying stage realities indifferent to human morality, demanding only submission.

## Comedy (Old Comedy)

The five comic poets offered one play apiece. **Old Comedy** — its great surviving exponent
**Aristophanes** — mixed fantasy, obscenity, and direct, named political and personal
**satire** (of Cleon, Socrates, Euripides, the war, the demagogues, the professional
oracle-mongers). Where tragedy veiled the contemporary in myth, comedy attacked it openly,
making it, like the oratory of the lawcourts, a contemporary source the historian can use
once "a joke or a piece of abuse" is distinguished from "the fact which makes it meaningful"
(see [[classical-greek-historiography]]).

## Significance

Athenian drama is the literary face of the radical [[athenian-democracy|democracy]] and the
[[delian-league|empire]] at once — a mass civic institution, financed by the liturgy system,
that made theology, politics, and the human condition the business of the whole assembled
*polis*. Its three tragedians and Aristophanes shaped the entire Western dramatic tradition.
