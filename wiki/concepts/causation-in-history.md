# Causation in History

How historians identify, weigh, and order causes — the analytical framework behind
this wiki's own link taxonomy (`caused_by` vs `contributed_to` vs `preceded_by`).
Primary source for this page: Richard J. Evans, *In Defence of History* (1997), ch. 5.

## Carr's Framework

E. H. Carr: "the study of history is a study of causes." Historians must enumerate
multiple causes and "arrange them in some kind of hierarchy of importance." Against
Isaiah Berlin's chance-and-free-will emphasis, Carr insisted causation and moral
responsibility are "two distinct categories which should not be confused."

- **Accident**: Carr conceded contingency is real (Lenin's premature death at 54
  affected Russian history) but held accidental causes "cannot be generalized... they
  teach no lessons." The **Cleopatra's nose** trope (Bury, from Pascal): a plainer
  Cleopatra → no infatuated Antony → no Actium → no Augustan empire. Carr: true but
  barren as a general proposition.
- **The Jones/Robinson road accident**: legitimate causes (blind corner, bad brakes,
  drink) are those society can act on; Robinson's cigarette errand is "dead and
  barren" chance. Evans's objection: this smuggles present policy-relevance into
  causal selection — the historian's purpose is understanding the past, and "if the
  sources suggest that an accidental cause was at work, it would not be right to rule
  it out just because it could not be made to serve our purposes in the present."

## Formal Typology (Evans)

Necessary causes (without A, no B) vs. sufficient causes (A alone produces B); within
necessary causes, absolute (B definitely impossible without A) vs. relative (B
probably impossible without A). Historians avoid monocausal explanation and often
treat events as **overdetermined**. Against refusing hierarchy altogether: it "can
lead to... a mere description of 'free-floating', relatively valid and equally
significant forces."

## Counterfactuals as Causal Tests

Evans's worked example — **Hitler dies in 1928**: most historians would still expect a
European war (Weimar's post-1929 survival chances were slim; a Papen-style far-right
dictatorship or Hohenzollern restoration would likely have pursued rearmament,
Versailles revision, Anschluss, and eastern conquest continuous with 1914–18 war
aims), and conservative antisemitism would have produced persecution — "but it is far
from certain that they would have launched the systematic programme of extermination":
the Final Solution was tied more closely to Hitler's specific worldview, though on the
functionalist "genesis from below" account large-scale massacres might still have
occurred. Moral: "Accident, chance and contingency certainly have a real effect, but
that effect is almost always rather limited in practice" — contingency operates inside
structural corridors. Compare [[great-man-vs-structural-causation]] and the wiki's
counterfactual query pages. A. J. P. Taylor's provocation that WWI was "caused by
railway timetables" (mobilization schedules as lock-in) belongs here as a
small-cause/large-effect claim — see [[causes-of-the-first-world-war]].

## Temporal Structure: Braudel's Three Speeds

Fernand Braudel (*The Mediterranean*): fast political events ("surface disturbances,
crests of foam"), slower social/economic conjunctures, and the near-static *longue
durée* of geography and environment. Evans's caveat: the Annales model exaggerated
stasis (Braudel treated the Mediterranean environment as given rather than transformed
by, e.g., deforestation) and "was never very good at accounting for historical change
at any level."

## The Anti-Causal Turn

- Barraclough (1967): abandon causal speculation for the study of "results";
  Vincent: "Cause is a constriction on historical thought"; Zeldin: a "pointilliste"
  history of unconnected dots — though Evans shows Zeldin's own *France 1848–1945*
  smuggles in cogent explanations throughout.
- Postmodernist versions: Hayden White (1966) — causal "nets" rob the present of
  agency; Jenkins — causation as an arbitrary discursive convention; Ankersmit —
  historical time itself "a recent and highly artificial invention." Evans's reply:
  "post-modern" presupposes the sequential time it denies; and (Zagorin, quoted
  approvingly) "It is an illusion... to assume that historiography can dispense with
  the concept of causality."
- The cultural/linguistic turn more moderately undermined the *layered* causal model
  common to Marxism, Annales, and neo-Weberian history (economic → social → political/
  cultural), replacing it with cultural determination of no greater universality.

## Practice Note: Narrative and Causation

Evans's own Hamburg cholera study (1892 epidemic) as exemplar: twelve parallel
causal strands (administrative amateurism, water supply, laissez-faire medical dogma,
poverty, etc.) each narrated 1830→1892, converging on the outbreak — demonstrating
that narrative arrangement of causes is an interpretive and aesthetic choice layered
onto the evidence ("We start with a rough-hewn block of stone, and chisel away at it
until we have a statue... it would have been perfectly possible... to have made a
different statue").

## Consequences vs. Causes

Evans: explanation is not history's only serious task — description, categorization,
and recovery are legitimate, and "Consequences are often more important than causes."
(The wiki's mandatory `consequences` frontmatter reflects the same judgment.)
