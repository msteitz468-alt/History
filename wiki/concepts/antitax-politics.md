# Antitax Politics

A political ideology and movement that frames taxation as inherently illegitimate or tyrannical, equates high taxes with loss of freedom, and treats "taxpayers" as a distinct and virtuous class whose interests are opposed to those of "tax eaters" or government dependents. In the Williamson account, antitax politics functions as a mechanism for limiting the capacity and accountability of democratic government.

## Definition and Scope

Antitax politics is not merely low-tax preference or fiscal conservatism. It is a worldview in which:
- Taxes are portrayed as theft or coercion rather than the price of collective goods.
- "Taxpayers" are constructed as a moral and political identity (often racialized or class-inflected).
- Reducing taxes is treated as an end in itself, even when it starves public capacity or increases deficits.
- Democratic majorities that support higher taxes for public goods are portrayed as illegitimate or captured by special interests.

## Key Phases in the United States

- **Post-Reconstruction / Jim Crow**: "Taxpayer leagues" and "white leagues" used antitax rhetoric alongside violence to roll back Reconstruction tax policies that had taxed planters for Black schools and services.
- **Gilded Age / Progressive Era**: Legal and constitutional resistance (Pollock decision) to income and wealth taxation.
- **1970s–1980s**: Proposition 13, Reagan tax cuts, supply-side economics, and the popularization of "tax eaters" language.
- **Contemporary**: Persistent opposition to tax increases even for popular programs, use of tax cuts to create fiscal crises ("starve the beast"), and framing of social spending as transfers from "makers" to "takers."

## Interaction with Democracy

Williamson argues that antitax politics is not a neutral preference but a strategy for restricting the scope of democratic decision-making. Because effective democracy requires the capacity to raise and spend revenue in response to citizen demands, sustained antitax success limits what government can do even when majorities support action. The movement has often overlapped with efforts to restrict the electorate (poll taxes, felon disenfranchisement, voter ID) under the banner of protecting "taxpayers."

## Historiography and Contestation

Defenders present antitax politics as a defense of liberty and limited government rooted in the American founding. Critics (including this source) see it as a post-1970s innovation that inverts the historical American demand for taxation *with* representation and that has been deployed to erode the fiscal foundations of the mid-century social contract. The racial and class dimensions of "taxpayer" identity are central to the critical account and disputed by proponents.
