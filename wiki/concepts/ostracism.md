# Ostracism

**Ostracism** (*ostrakismos*) was the Athenian procedure for the **ten-year banishment of a
prominent citizen** by popular vote — named for the **ostraka**, the potsherds on which
voters scratched their candidate's name. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 5,
Martin Ostwald) analyses it as an expression of **[[isonomia]]**: a way of letting "the
people as a whole, rich and poor alike," resolve a deadlock between rival policies without
bloodshed or permanent exile.

## Procedure

Once a year the Assembly voted (in the sixth prytany) **whether** to hold an ostracism; if
yes, the *ostrakophoria* followed (eighth prytany) in the Agora under the archons and
Council. A quorum of **6,000** votes was required; a simple majority sent the leading
name into **exile for ten years** — but he **kept his property and revenues** and resumed
full citizenship on return. It was **not a punishment** for a crime (treason was tried in
the lawcourts), and **only one person** could be ostracized in any year.

## Purpose — emic and etic

Aristotle gives two rationales: **suspicion of over-mighty men** (the memory that
Pisistratus rose "as popular leader and general"), and the safeguarding of **equality** by
removing those who "exceed in power by reason of their wealth, popularity, or some other
kind of political strength." Ostwald argues the deeper function was to **decide between
mutually exclusive policies**: when Athens could pursue only one line — after
[[battle-of-marathon-490bce|Marathon]], whether to **resist or accommodate Persia**; after
483/2, whether to **build Themistocles' navy** — ostracism let the demos choose which
policy would have a clear field, by removing the chief spokesman of the losing side for a
decade. The safeguards (6,000 quorum, the risk to the proposer himself) make it "hard to
believe the procedure was initially devised for party-political purposes."

## The early ostracisms (487–480)

First used in **488/7** against **Hipparchus son of Charmus** ("leader of the friends of
the tyrants"), then **Megacles** (485) and others, then — explicitly "for his great
influence alone" — **Xanthippus** (484, Pericles' father) and **Aristides** (482, over
Themistocles' naval bill). The discovery of **>11,000 ostraka** (9,000 from the Kerameikos
in 1966) names 130+ targets and shows **Themistocles** was already a favourite candidate in
the 480s, with many "scatter votes" for men unknown to literature.

## The fifth-century ostracisms (CAH V)

[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Chs. 3–4, Rhodes) follows the institution into the era of
empire, where it became the device for **deciding contests of political leadership**: the
ostracism of **[[themistocles]]** (c. 472/1, who went to Argos and ultimately to Persia); of
**[[cimon]]** (461) as *philolakon* and *misodemos* when he tried to reverse
[[ephialtes-reform-462bce|Ephialtes' reform]]; and of **Thucydides son of Melesias** (c. 443),
the leader of the opposition to [[pericles|Pericles']] building program, after which Pericles
held an unbroken predominance. Other states imitated the practice (Argos, Megara, Miletus;
Syracuse's short-lived *petalismos*). The series ended with the collusive removal of
**Hyperbolus** (see below).

## Limitations and critique

Ostracism could **not** suppress an opponent's faction (his adherents remained), and any
proposer **risked becoming its own victim**. Its one clearly factional use — the collusive
removal of **Hyperbolus** — was also the **last** ostracism ever held, "for once it had
been shown that it could be used to remove a mere political rival, it was never used again."
The institution's **date** (Cleisthenic enactment vs. enactment c. 488) and its **purpose**
remain contested, the two questions being interdependent.
