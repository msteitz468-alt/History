## Definition

Kokuga (国衙) designates the provincial headquarters as both a physical installation and an administrative institution in Heian Japan. In the early ritsuryo period, the kokuga was a bureaucratic branch of the central state — a scaled-down replica of the capital grid, staffed by capital-appointed officers, subdivided into functional departments (tokoro), and operating within strict hierarchical rules. By the 10th century, it had evolved into something qualitatively different: a semi-autonomous fiscal entity with its own domain, its own resident officialdom, its own customary law (kokurei), and its own discretionary taxing authority — in short, a kind of estate management agency.

This transformation is central to understanding the Heian fiscal system. The [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]], chapter 4, analyzes it in detail.

---

## Physical Structure

Provincial capitals were constructed on the symmetrical grid typical of ritsuryo planning, replicating the Heian capital on a miniature scale with provincial headquarters standing in for the imperial palace. They included official residences, stables, post horses, and official granaries. Gun headquarters (gunke or miyake) were similarly walled or moated. In theory, all persons present were "on temporary duty" — no subject household took the provincial capital as its official residence.

By the early 10th century, the tokoro (functional offices) within the kokuga had become permanent institutions, possessing their own degree of autonomy while subordinate to the governor. The reception ceremony for a new governor, preserved in a 10th-century document, enumerates the offices: the tax-grain office (zeisho), the major-report office (daichōsho), the court-report office (chōshusho), the militia office (kondeishō), the provincial management office (kokushosho), and the administrative office (mandokoro). The concentration of local elites as irregular officials in these tokoro "show a degree of corporate autonomy quite antithetical to the original ritsuryo order" (CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4).

## The Kokuga Domain (Kokugaryō)

By the early 11th century, the kokuga had become the administrative center of a distinct domain — the kokugaryō — comprising all taxable land not formally exempt through shōen certificates. As the allotment system was abandoned and period redistribution ceased, all cultivated rice paddy was categorized for kokuga purposes as either:
- **Kōden** ("public fields"): taxable land within the kokuga's domain, whether leased to tato on a year-by-year basis or held as reclaimed property under the reclamation rules.
- **Exempt shōen land**: noted on the permanent provincial land maps by grid-square location.

The Iga Province formula of the early 11th century illustrates the mature system: a standardized per-tan levy on all kōden, specifying exactly what the tato should pay, including how much rice should be threshed and where delivered. Special rates were provided for betsumyo ("special-name" holdings), which the CHJ identifies as embryonic forms of the medieval shōen.

## Discretionary Powers

The kokuga's most important resource was its discretionary taxing authority. Rinji zōyaku (extraordinary irregular levies), which had no basis in ritsuryo law and originated as provincial precedent from the Jōgan era (859–877), were imposed by the incumbent governor on tato and other capable farmers. The authority to impose or exempt from rinji zōyaku was entirely the governor's prerogative. This made each zuryō appointment a de facto renegotiation of the fiscal compact with local gentry.

The Exchange (koeki) system — the forced procurement of luxury commodities (silk, oil, bark, etc.) for the capital using stored tax-grain at prices set by the kokuga — was another major discretionary lever. By the 10th century, koeki had become a major source of tax commodities, replacing the collapsing tribute (chō) system.

---

## Resident Officialdom

The transformation of the kokuga into a domain was inseparable from the growth of its resident irregular officialdom — the zōshiki, who staffed the tokoro and provided the permanent administrative capacity the annually rotating governor lacked. These resident officials were typically members of the local gentry elite: gun-magistrate families, established migrants, and their dependents. By the mid-10th century, direct references to "the people" had disappeared from official documents in favor of phrases like "gun magistrates and farmers (hyakusho)" — unambiguous references to this gentry class.

The Owari Province petition of 988 shows the resident officialdom was capable of organized political action: gun magistrates and tato farmers compiled a 31-article impeachment of the incumbent governor, demonstrating both the sophistication of provincial institutional culture and the limits of gubernatorial authority.

---

## Relation to Shōen

The kokuga and [[shoen]] were complementary and competing institutions of the mature Heian fiscal order. Kokugaryō (the kokuga domain) and shōen were the two categories into which all rice land fell by the early 11th century. Their boundary was contested and subject to negotiation: new reclamation within shōen was subject to annual survey by the land office (tadokoro) and required kokuga certification to be treated as exempt. Governors could and did confiscate uncertified reclamation even on established shōen (as in the case of Oyama-no-shō in Tamba, where the Tamba authorities confiscated reclaimed fields from Tōji Temple and returned them to public-field status). Conversely, betsumyo within the kokuga domain evolved toward partial shōen immunity over time.
