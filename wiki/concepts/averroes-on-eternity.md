## Definition and Scope

This page documents [[ibn-rushd|Averroes']] defense of the Aristotelian doctrine of the world's
eternity (*qidam al-ʿālam*) in the First Discussion of the
[[tahafut-al-tahafut|Tahāfut al-Tahāfut]] — the longest and most philosophically demanding section
of that work. The First Discussion occupies the overwhelming bulk of the opening pages and engages
systematically with all four of [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī's]] proofs against the philosophers'
eternity thesis as set out in the [[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]].

Averroes' central charge against al-Ghazālī is methodological rather than merely substantive: that
al-Ghazālī argues dialectically (*jadalīyan*) rather than demonstratively (*burhānan*), deploying
arguments based on common notions, equivocal terms, and rhetorical pressure rather than the
genuinely necessary premisses that apodeictic proof requires. The defense of eternity and the
critique of al-Ghazālī's method are therefore inseparable in the First Discussion — see also
[[averroes-demonstrative-vs-dialectical]].

The world's eternity as defended by Averroes following Aristotle holds that the universe has
coexisted with God without beginning or end, as an effect coexists with its eternal cause. This
is distinct from the Avicennan emanationist version, which Averroes regards as a distortion; see
[[ibn-sina]] and [[emanationism-avicennan]]. For al-Ghazālī's counterarguments, see
[[eternity-of-the-world]] and [[islamic-theology-of-creation]].

## The Methodological Complaint: Dialectic Masquerading as Demonstration

Before engaging al-Ghazālī's arguments individually, Averroes establishes a global objection to
the entire project of the *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*: its arguments do not reach the standard of
demonstrative proof.

Averroes writes of al-Ghazālī's First Proof:

> "This argument is in the highest degree dialectical and does not reach the pitch of demonstrative
> proof. For its premisses are common notions, and common notions approach the equivocal, whereas
> demonstrative premisses are concerned with things proper to the same genus."

The specific charge is that al-Ghazālī's argument turns on the term *possible* (*mumkin*), which is
equivocal: it covers (a) what happens more often than not, (b) what happens less often than not,
and (c) what has equal chances either way. These three types of possibility do not have the same
logical relationship to the need for a new determining principle. Moreover, "possible" applies both
to the agent (the capacity to act) and to the patient (the capacity to receive), and the logic of
each differs. Al-Ghazālī treats all of these as one problem — which Averroes identifies as "one of
the well known seven sophisms."

This methodological attack recurs throughout the First Discussion. At every turn Averroes
distinguishes between:

- **Demonstrative premisses**: derived from the nature of the thing in question (*propria*), capable
  of producing necessary conviction.
- **Dialectical premisses**: derived from common notions, accepted opinions, or analogies from
  unrelated domains; produce only probability or persuasion.
- **Sophistical premisses**: deployed to produce the appearance of a refutation by creating doubt
  and perplexity, without genuinely touching the truth.

Al-Ghazālī's counter-arguments shift between these three modes without distinguishing them. Averroes
regards this as intellectually dishonest:

> "Most of the arguments with which this man Ghazali opposes the philosophers are doubts which arise
> when certain parts of the doctrine of the philosophers come into conflict with others, and when
> those differences are compared with each other; but this is an imperfect refutation. A perfect
> refutation would be one that succeeded in showing the futility of their system according to the
> facts themselves."

## Averroes on al-Ghazālī's First Proof: The Eternal Will and Temporal Determination

### Al-Ghazālī's Argument

The First Proof runs: if an eternal God creates a temporal world, something must give *preponderance*
(*murājjiḥ*) to the world's existing at one moment rather than another. Either this determining
factor is itself eternal — in which case it already existed and should have produced the world at
all prior moments — or it is new, in which case the same question applies to it. The argument
terminates in an infinite regress or in the conclusion that temporal events cannot proceed from a
purely eternal cause without change.

### Averroes' Response: The Equivocity of "Possible"

Averroes does not deny the logic of the argument when its premisses are granted. Instead he attacks
the premisses as equivocal. The phrase "pure possibility" (used to describe the world's state before
creation) conflates multiple senses of "possible," each with a different relationship to external
determination. Further, the type of agent matters: a voluntary agent and a natural agent actualize
possibilities differently, and it is not established that God resembles either the natural agent or
the voluntary agent of human experience. Demonstrative proof would require settling these questions
first; al-Ghazālī skips them.

### The "Eternal Will" Problem and the Ash'arite Dilemma

When al-Ghazālī responds on behalf of the [[asharism|Ash'arites]] that the world was created by
"an eternal will which has decreed its existence in the time in which it exists," Averroes presses
a dilemma:

Either (1) the act of the agent does not imply any change in the agent, or (2) there are changes
which arise by themselves without an agent who causes them. The Ash'arites need one or the other
position, but both are hard to defend on their own terms. Averroes notes that an "eternal will" in
the Ash'arite sense is a contradiction in terms:

> "In Ghazali's objection there is a confusion. For our expressions 'eternal will' and 'temporal
> will' are equivocal, indeed contrary. For the empirical will is a faculty which possesses the
> possibility of doing equally one of two contraries and then of receiving equally one of the two
> contraries willed... But when one says: 'There is a Wilier who wills eternally one of two
> contraries in Himself', the definition of the will is abandoned, for we have transferred its
> nature from the possible to the necessary."

The Ash'arites are forced, Averroes argues, to posit either a first agent or a first act, but they
cannot admit that the disposition of the agent at the moment of acting is the same as before the
act. This implies a new disposition — which itself needs an agent — producing an infinite regress.

### Divorce Analogy and the Limits of Convention

Al-Ghazālī introduces an analogy from Islamic law: a conditional divorce formula (dependent on a
future event, such as someone's entering the house) produces an effect delayed after its formulation.
This parallels, he claims, an eternal will producing a delayed temporal effect. Averroes replies
that this analogy **weakens** rather than strengthens al-Ghazālī's case:

> "This example of divorce based on convention seems to strengthen the argument of the philosophers,
> but in reality it weakens it. For it enables the Ash'arites to say: In the same way as the actual
> divorce is delayed after the formula of divorce till the moment when the condition... is fulfilled,
> so the realization of the world can be delayed after God's act of creation until the condition is
> fulfilled on which this realization depends, i.e. the moment when God willed it. But conventional
> things do not behave like rational."

The Literalist (Zahirite) retort — that such a conditioned divorce is not binding because it lacks
connection to the actual act of the divorcer — shows that even by conventional standards the
analogy is contested.

### The Argument from Universal Assent

Al-Ghazālī challenges the philosophers: if the connection between an eternal will and a temporal
creation is truly impossible by rational necessity, why do not all rational persons recognize it?
Vast numbers affirm temporal creation without logical discomfort. Averroes dismisses this:

> "This argument is mistaken, for it is not a condition of objective truth that it should be known
> to all. That anything should be held by all does not imply anything more than its being a common
> notion, just as the existence of a common notion does not imply objective truth."

The criterion of truth is not consensus but "the sound understanding which does not base itself on
prejudice and passion, when it probes according to the signs and rules by which truth and mere
opinion are logically distinguished."

## Averroes on the Infinity of Past Revolutions

Al-Ghazālī presses a mathematical argument against eternity: if the world is eternal, the
celestial revolutions are actually infinite in number. The sphere of Saturn revolves once every
thirty years, the sun once a year. The total revolutions of each would be infinite — yet they
would stand in a ratio of 30:1 to each other. But two infinities cannot stand in a ratio (since
both are infinite). Therefore both being infinite is absurd.

Averroes' response is philosophically important:

> "If, however, there is no proportion between two movements in their totality, because they are
> both potential, i.e. they have neither beginning nor end but there exists a proportion between
> the parts, because they are both actual, then the proportion between the wholes is not necessarily
> the same as the proportion between the parts."

The key distinction is between **actually infinite** and **potentially infinite** quantities:

- An actual infinite would be a completed totality with definite cardinality — and Averroes agrees
  with Aristotle that an actual infinite is impossible.
- A potential infinite has neither beginning nor end in reality; it exists as an indefinitely
  extensible series, not as a completed whole. Past celestial revolutions, considered as a
  potentially infinite series, are neither even nor odd, neither greater nor lesser than one another
  in the aggregate — because they do not form an aggregate.

> "For of the number which exists only potentially, i.e. which has neither beginning nor end, it
> cannot truly be said that it is even or uneven, or that it begins or ends; it happens neither in
> the past nor in the future, for what exists potentially falls under the law of non-existence."

This dissolves al-Ghazālī's argument: the ratio holds for any finite segment of the revolutions
(the *actual* parts), but there is no totality of all past revolutions to which the ratio could
be applied.

### Essential vs. Accidental Infinite Series

A subsidiary but important argument concerns the apparent paradox that an infinite past would
require an infinite number of prior causes to have been traversed before the present moment.
Averroes distinguishes two types of infinite series:

- **Essential infinite** (*infinite per se*): each term is the efficient cause of the next as such.
  Philosophers deny this is possible — it would produce an effect without cause.
- **Accidental infinite** (*infinite per accidens*): the temporal order between terms is accidental;
  neither term is an agent of the other by its nature. The series of man generating man is of this
  type — each individual man is generated by another, but the succession is accidental relative to
  the eternal first mover who underlies all generation.

> "The acts of an agent who has no beginning have a beginning as little as his existence, and
> therefore it follows necessarily that no preceding act of his is the condition for the existence
> of a later, for neither of them is an agent by itself and their sequence is accidental. An
> accidental infinite, not an essential infinite, is admitted by the philosophers; nay, this type
> of infinite is in fact a necessary consequence of the existence of an eternal first principle."

## Averroes on al-Ghazālī's Second Proof: The Time Argument

### The Philosophers' Argument

The Second Proof holds that God's alleged priority to the world implies a temporal "before." Either
God is prior in essence (simultaneous cause and effect, hence both eternal) or prior in time (a
duration of God without the world) — but time is the measure of motion, so pre-world time implies
pre-world motion, i.e., an eternal world.

### Averroes' Assessment

Averroes accepts the main line of the philosophers' argument on this proof but notes one flaw: the
argument holds within the frame of temporal reasoning but does not as such prove the *relation*
of God to the world before the world's existence. He assesses al-Ghazālī's spatial analogy (the
limit of the world's sphere is not followed by empty space, just as the first moment of time is not
preceded by empty duration) as philosophically interesting but ultimately "malicious" — a
sophistical substitution:

> "He [Ghazali] treats the quantity which has no position and does not form a totality, i.e. time
> and motion, as the quantity which possesses position and totality, i.e. body... This is a manifest
> error. For to imagine an increase in actual spatial magnitude... is to imagine something which does
> not exist in the essence and definition of spatial magnitude, but to imagine priority and
> posteriority in a movement that occurs is to imagine something that belongs to its essence."

The instant (*ān*) necessarily has both a past and a future — that is part of the definition of the
instant. A point on a line, by contrast, can be a beginning without being the end of another line,
since lines are at rest. Al-Ghazālī's analogy confuses the point with the instant and thus fails.

Furthermore, Averroes charges, the analogy that al-Ghazālī draws between spatial and temporal
limits collapses: if anything, the argument shows that the theologians' supposition of a temporal
beginning is *less* coherent than the philosophers' eternity, not more:

> "In relation to the temporal creation of the world — for him who believes in it — before and after
> cannot even be imagined, for before and after in time can only be imagined in relation to the
> present moment, and as, according to the theologians, there was before the creation of the world
> no time, how could there be imagined something preceding the moment when the world was created? A
> definite moment cannot be assigned for the creation of the world."

## Averroes on the Third and Fourth Proofs: Possibility and Matter

Averroes engages al-Ghazālī's Third Proof (possibility of existence is eternal, therefore the world
is eternal) and Fourth Proof (every temporal event requires pre-existing matter as the seat of
possibility, hence prime matter is eternal) more briefly, treating them as extensions of the same
methodological failure.

On the Fourth Proof, he criticizes [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]'s claim that individual immaterial souls
are numerically distinct after death (a claim al-Ghazālī uses to press the philosophers). Averroes
regards this as non-Aristotelian and indefensible:

> "The impossibility of an actual infinite is an acknowledged axiom in philosophical theory, equally
> valid for material and immaterial things. We do not know of any one who makes a distinction here
> between the spatial and the non-spatial, with the single exception of Avicenna. I do not know of
> any other philosopher who affirms this... Perhaps Avicenna wanted only to satisfy the masses,
> telling them what they were accustomed to hear about the soul."

On the soul question raised by Plato's unity-of-soul doctrine (pressed by al-Ghazālī), Averroes
responds that "different" is equivocal and that souls are one in form while many through their
material substrates. After bodily death, if the soul is immortal, it must form a numerical unity
precisely because matter — the principle of numerical individuation — no longer differentiates it.

## Al-Ghazālī's Concession and Averroes' Final Verdict

Near the end of the First Discussion, al-Ghazālī explicitly concedes that his book is not a
constructive work — it aims only to destroy the philosophers' claim to *knowledge* of eternity, not
to establish temporal creation. He promises a future work, *The Golden Mean in Dogmatic Beliefs*,
that will build the positive doctrine.

Averroes treats this as a serious intellectual failure:

> "To oppose difficulty with difficulty does not bring about destruction, but only perplexity and
> doubts in him who acts in this way... It would have been necessary for him to begin by establishing
> the truth, before starting to perplex and confuse his readers, for they might die before they could
> get hold of that book, or he might have died himself before writing it. But this book has not yet
> come into my hands and perhaps he never composed it."

Averroes concludes that al-Ghazālī's actual philosophical allegiances lie with the Neoplatonist
tradition — pointing to the *Mishkāt al-Anwār* (*The Niche for Lights*) as evidence:

> "It appears from the books ascribed to him that in metaphysics he recurs to the philosophers. And
> of all his books this is most clearly shown and most truly proved in his book called The Niche for
> Lights."

The First Discussion's overall verdict: neither the theologians' proofs for temporal creation nor
the philosophers' proofs for eternity reach the standard of **demonstrative proof** (*burhān*). The
best answer on God's relationship to time is:

> "The starting-point of His acts is at the starting-point of His existence; for neither of them has
> a beginning."

## Significance

The First Discussion is the longest and philosophically densest section of the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*.
Its arguments were taken up by Latin scholastics — Averroes' distinction between actual and
potential infinity influenced medieval debates on whether an eternal world is logically coherent,
a question Aquinas addresses explicitly in the *Summa Theologiae* and *De aeternitate mundi*. The
charge that [[asharism|Ash'arite]] theology deploys dialectical rather than demonstrative methods
became a standard criticism of *kalām* in the Averroist tradition.
