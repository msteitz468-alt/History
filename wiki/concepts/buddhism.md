# Buddhism

## Overview

**Buddhism**, originating in the teaching of the **[[buddha|Buddha]]** in northern India
around the fifth century BCE, became in [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Kaul Ch 18, Liu Ch 17,
Holcombe Ch 15, Yao Ch 16) the great **universalizing religion of the Silk Roads** — the most
consequential cultural transmission of the first and second [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] eras,
reshaping the civilizations of **Central and East Asia**.

## South Asian Origins

Buddhism arose in the **middle Ganga valley** during the ferment of the **Second Urbanization**
(see [[vedic-age]]), one of the heterodox *shramana* (renouncer) movements — alongside
**[[jainism|Jainism]]** — that challenged Vedic brahmanical orthodoxy and sacrifice. The
**[[buddha|Buddha]]** (Siddhartha Gautama, c. 5th c. BCE), a Shakya prince of Kapilavastu,
taught the **Four Noble Truths** — that existence is suffering (*dukkha*), caused by craving,
and ended by following the **Eightfold Path** to *nirvana*, release from the cycle of
**rebirth** (*samsara*). His early teaching was, in CWH IV's words, "almost atheist": it
posited **no creator god and no enduring self** (*anatta*), and for centuries the Buddha was
venerated only through **symbols** (the wheel, the empty throne, the *stupa*, the Bodhi tree)
rather than images.

The faith's decisive turn from a regional teaching toward a **world religion** came with royal
patronage under the **[[mauryan-empire|Mauryas]]**, above all **[[ashoka|Ashoka]]** (r.
272–232 BCE): his edicts propagated a Buddhist-inflected ethic of **dhamma**, he endowed the
**sangha** lavishly, and he dispatched **missions abroad** — notably to **Sri Lanka**, where
the early **Theravada** ("Doctrine of the Elders") tradition took lasting root.

## The Mahayana Transformation

Under the **[[kushan-empire|Kushan]]** Empire (1st–3rd c. CE) Buddhism underwent a profound
"theological and institutional transformation." The new **Mahayana** doctrine worshipped a
**divine Buddha** and numerous semi-divine **bodhisattvas**, departing from the "almost
atheist teaching of the Buddha during his lifetime." Mendicant monks gave way to wealthy,
landed **monasteries**; a **commercial ethos** encouraged merit-making donations from
traders and rulers; and the Greco-Persian-Indian fusion of **[[bactria|Gandharan]] art**
produced the first **Buddha images**, the visual models that spread along the trade routes.

## The Religion of the Silk Roads

Buddhist institutions "provided the infrastructure on the trade routes": monasteries served
as way-stations, civic centers, and banks across the Tarim oases (Khotan, Kucha, Dunhuang),
**[[bactria|Bactria]]** (the giant **Bamiyan** Buddhas), and into China (the **Yungang** and
**Longmen** cave-temples). **[[sogdians|Sogdian]]** traders were among its first missionaries
to China. Mahayana texts (the *Lotus Sutra*, *Pure Land Sutra*) sanctified Silk Roads
commodities — silk and the "Seven Treasures" — binding faith and trade together.

## Buddhism in East Asia

Introduced to China via the Silk Roads during the **[[han-dynasty|Han]]**, Buddhism truly
flourished from the fourth century CE, its scriptures translated and supplemented into an
**East Asian Buddhist canon**. It spread to **Korea** (Koguryŏ and Paekche, 4th c.) and from
Paekche to **Japan** (538/552 CE), becoming a pillar of the
**[[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian cultural sphere]]**. Under the
**[[tang-dynasty|Tang]]** it flourished alongside Daoism, Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, and
Nestorian Christianity, transforming Chinese **eschatology** (rebirth, merit) even as it
coexisted with state [[confucianism|Confucianism]].

## The Spread of Buddhism in the Middle Millennium (CWH V)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Tansen Sen, Ch 17) treats Buddhism's diffusion as one of
the three "world religion" ecumenes of Part IV, and stresses that it was a **complex,
multi-directional process** carrying far more than doctrine — "art forms, literary
genres, ritual items, geographical knowledge, and technologies" moved with it. Sen
identifies **two key turning points**:

- **The fifth century**, after which new regions were incorporated into the Buddhist
  realm and locally adapted forms emerged. In the fifth–sixth centuries Buddhism
  spread to **maritime Southeast Asia** (a fifth-century Sanskrit inscription of the
  sea-captain Buddhagupta at Kedah; Gupta-influenced and locally made images along the
  Malay peninsula), often adopted by new polities (after the decline of Funan, and by
  Śrīvijaya in Sumatra) **to legitimize political power** — consciously paralleling
  the Chinese dynasties' use of Buddhism. In the same centuries it was established in
  **Korea** (Koguryŏ 372, Paekche 384, Silla 528) and thence **Japan**, via diplomatic
  and commercial ties to China.
- **The tenth century**, after which there were **multiple centers of Buddhism**, each
  with its own sphere of influence and connections, rather than a single
  India-centered diffusion.

Sen's conclusion: the spread of Buddhism "triggered vibrant commercial interactions
and cross-cultural exchanges that integrated various regions of Asia from Iran to
Japan," transforming worldviews, political identities, sacred landscapes, and social
lives across most of Asia — the religious counterpart to the
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|world system's]] economic integration. (Buddhism's
decline in its South Asian homeland, displaced by a resurgent
[[hinduism|Hinduism]] and the advance of [[islam|Islam]] in the north, falls within
this same period.)

## Buddhism in Early Modern East Asia (CWH VI)

[[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]] (Menegon & Cogan) overturns the old view of early
modern (1400–1850) East Asian religion as static. In **Ming China**, the founding
**Hongwu Emperor** (r. 1368–98) — a former Buddhist acolyte — built the first true
**"regulatory state"** in religious matters: he restructured the monastic hierarchy,
restricted ordinations and monks' movement (1394), taxed monasteries, and opposed
the Tibetan tradition tainted by Mongol-Yuan association. Buddhist monasticism fell
into a century-long crisis, deepened by the Daoist **Jiajing Emperor's** disrobing
edict (1544) and coastal destruction by pirates — before a vigorous **revival under
the Wanli Emperor** (r. 1572–1620): canon reprinting, doctrinal flourishing, a
**rebirth of Chan (Zen)**, surging lay and literati involvement (encouraged by Wang
Yangming's [[neo-confucianism|School of the Mind]]), and new vernacular fiction
(*Journey to the West*). The **Qing** emperors patronized Buddhism instrumentally —
deploying the **cakravartin** ("wheel-ruler") image to legitimize Manchu rule over
Mongols and Tibetans (see [[intercultural-relations-early-modern]]) — even as
Yongzheng and Qianlong's interventions (Qianlong **abolished the official ordination
system in 1754**) weakened Chan. This is the religious counterpart to the dynamism
the volume finds across the early modern world.

## Significance

Buddhism is CWH IV's paradigm of a **network-borne universal religion**: carried not by
conquest but by merchants, monks, and monasteries along the Silk Roads, it became the first
genuinely trans-Eurasian faith, knitting India, Central Asia, and East Asia into a shared
religious world. Its westward Silk Roads dominance only receded with the spread of
**Islam** across Central Asia from the eighth century.

## Historiography

CWH IV stresses Buddhism's **economic and institutional** dimensions — monasteries as
commercial actors — over purely doctrinal history, and its role as connective tissue in the
"networks" thesis. The Mahayana shift, the Gandharan art synthesis, and the dating of
transmission to China are reconstructed from texts, inscriptions, cave-temple art, and
travelers' records. See also [[universal-religions]].
