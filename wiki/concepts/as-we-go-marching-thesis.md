# The *As We Go Marching* Thesis

[[flynn-john-t|John T. Flynn]]'s claim ([[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944]]) that
fascism is best defined not by its rhetoric or racial doctrine but as a
recurring **policy syndrome** produced when parliamentary states, unable to tax
enough to fund the welfare and patronage demands of mass politics, slide
through deficit finance into permanent crisis economics.

## The Eight-Point Definition (Part One, ch. X, verbatim)

> "As we survey the whole scene in Italy, therefore, we may now name all the
> essential ingredients of fascism. It is a form of social organization
> 1. In which the government acknowledges no restraint upon its powers —
>    totalitarianism.
> 2. In which this unrestrained government is managed by a dictator — the
>    leadership principle.
> 3. In which the government is organized to operate the capitalist system and
>    enable it to function — under an immense bureaucracy.
> 4. In which the economic society is organized on the syndicalist model, that
>    is by producing groups formed into craft and professional categories under
>    supervision of the state.
> 5. In which the government and the syndicalist organizations operate the
>    capitalist society on the planned, autarchial principle.
> 6. In which the government holds itself responsible to provide the nation
>    with adequate purchasing power by public spending and borrowing.
> 7. In which militarism is used as a conscious mechanism of government
>    spending, and
> 8. In which imperialism is included as a policy inevitably flowing from
>    militarism as well as other elements of fascism.
>
> Wherever you find a nation using all of these devices you will know that
> this is a fascist nation. In proportion as any nation uses most of them you
> may assume it is tending in the direction of fascism."

Flynn immediately adds that dictatorship alone is not fascism — Stalin's USSR
is "very far from being a fascist dictatorship" because it lacks the
capitalist-defense economic structure (points 3–5); and that public attention
fixates on the dramatic dictatorship/violence elements while remaining "only
very dimly aware" of the economic ingredients, which is precisely how fascism
could creep into a society like America's unrecognized. In Part Two he
restates the list in a five-element compression: debt-supported spending,
the planned/corporativist economy, militarism, imperialism, and the
totalitarian state.

The polemical payoff is the book's three-part structure: Italy ("the soil of
fascism"), Germany ("the bad fascism"), America ("the good fascism") — the
argument (Part Three, "The Last Mile") that the [[new-deal|New Deal]] plus
wartime planning, militarism, and [[imperialism|imperial]] commitments had
assembled four of the five elements in the United States, and that the fifth —
the totalitarian state — is not optional but structurally necessary, "because
it is impossible to operate a public-debt-supported autarchy save by means of
a totalitarian government." Two further signature arguments: the **"good
fascists / bad fascists"** critique (Part Two, ch. X) — Americans were learning
to excuse structurally fascist allies (Dollfuss, Schuschnigg, Salazar, Metaxas,
Kemal) as they once distinguished "good trusts" from "bad trusts"; and the
**crisis-dependence mechanism** of the Final Note: the system "is born in
crisis, lives on crises, and cannot survive the era of crisis," so it must
manufacture fresh crises indefinitely.

## Emic vs. Etic

Etic in form (an analyst's definition applied across cases) but written as an
emic intervention in the 1944 American debate. It is a contemporary polemic,
not retrospective scholarship — its evidentiary value is highest for Italian
and German fiscal history (Flynn's professional competence) and lowest as a
neutral characterization of the New Deal.

## Relation to Other Frameworks

- Converges with the contemporaneous [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]] (Hayek, 1944):
  both argue liberal democracies drift toward totalitarian economics via
  planning; Flynn's mechanism is fiscal-political, Hayek's epistemic.
- Sharply [[generic-fascism|contrasts with post-1990 consensus definitions]]
  (Paxton, Griffin) that center palingenetic ultranationalism and political
  violence, treating economics as secondary — the mainstream ground for
  rejecting Flynn's American application.
- Feeds directly into [[fascism-and-the-political-left]] (Flynn is a primary
  exhibit for the "fascism as statism" position) and
  [[roosevelt-and-new-deal-revisionism]].
- Contrast [[communism-and-fascism]] on whether fascism is anti-capitalist.

## Limitations and Critiques

Standard critiques: the definition is broad enough to capture most wartime
states; it minimizes racism, paramilitary violence, and the counterrevolutionary
alliance with conservative elites that Paxton's five stages make central; and
its 1944 prediction of a permanent postwar American fascism did not materialize
in the predicted dictatorial form. Defenders (Radosh 1973; later libertarians)
answer that Flynn predicted the permanent military-Keynesian, interventionist
state with real accuracy, whatever the label.
