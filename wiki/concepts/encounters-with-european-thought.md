## Overview

From the seventeenth century onward, scientific and philosophical ideas moved from Europe into the Islamic world, creating a mirror-image of the movement of ideas from Arabic into Latin in the twelfth century. The reception was not uniform: responses ranged from enthusiastic embrace to selective appropriation to defensive rejection, and the political context of colonial domination gave urgency to every position. As Khaled El-Rouyaheb has noted, to criticize Muslim intellectuals for failing to embrace European ideas is as odd as criticizing seventeenth-century Europeans for failing to embrace ideas from the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires (cited in Adamson, Ch. 59).

## 17th-Century Beginnings: Copernicus, Paracelsus, Aristotle

Three distinct lines of transmission brought European ideas into the Ottoman empire in the seventeenth century:

**Copernican Astronomy**: In 1660 the Ottoman scholar **Ibrāhīm Efendi** translated a book on Copernican astronomy from French into Arabic. He did not endorse heliocentrism but saw the translation as potentially useful for rectifying problems in Ptolemaic astronomy. Responses ranged from dismissal to cautious interest to acceptance. One scholar argued that heliocentrism was plausible by analogy: "it makes more sense to turn meat over a fire than turning the fire around the meat." Earlier, in the 1570s, the Istanbul astronomer Taqī al-Dīn had made observations of Halley's comet contemporaneous with Tycho Brahe in Europe (1577). The destruction of his observatory in 1580 has been misread as anti-rationalist; Adamson argues it was likely a political consequence of failed military advice.

**Paracelsian Medicine**: In the seventeenth century Arabic translations introduced the chemical theories and pharmacology of Paracelsus to Ottoman doctors. These challenged Galenic medicine, which had been central to the Islamic medical tradition since al-Kindī's era. The result was partial engagement: some Paracelsian ideas were accepted, but Galen was not displaced.

**Aristotle in Greek Commentary**: The scholar **Yanyalı Esad Efendi** (fl. early 18th c., "Tulip Age" under Grand Vizier Ibrahim Paşa) translated portions of Aristotle's *Organon* and a Greek commentary on the *Physics* into Arabic. Notably, to find Averroes' commentary on the *Physics* — standard in the Latin West — he had to use a Latin translation, since the Arabic original was apparently unavailable to him. This illustrates that Averroes had been largely forgotten in the Islamic East even as he remained foundational in European scholasticism.

## 19th Century: Tanzimat and Its Aftermath

The **tanzimat** reforms (inaugurated 1839) restructured the Ottoman state along European lines: free-market economics, a new bureaucracy, a reformed educational system. The reforms transformed Ottoman political ideology. A group of reforming intellectuals called the **Young Ottomans** argued the tanzimat had not gone far enough and pushed for constitutional government — notably, they still drew legitimacy from Islamic texts and did not challenge the *ulema*'s status. The more radical **Young Turks** (late 19th–early 20th c.) went further, embracing European materialism and positivism while increasingly displacing religion from public life.

Key Young Turk thinkers in relation to European philosophy:

- **Ziya Gökalp** (influenced by Durkheim): saw religion as "culture" (serving a social-solidarity function) and science as "civilization" (a rational universal achievement). This separation allowed him to value Islamic identity as *culture* while embracing European science as *civilization*.
- **Abdullah Cevdet** (influenced by Büchner and Comte): "religion is the science of the masses whereas science is the religion of the elite." Like al-Fārābī, he saw philosophy as knowledge for the rational few and religion as its popularized version. Aggressively materialist — rejected the soul as distinct from the body; skeptical of God's existence. Tried for blasphemy.
- **İzmirli İsmail Hakkı** (moderate): wanted to fuse modernity with tradition. Proposed renewing Islamic theology by incorporating Comte's positivism, as Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī had renewed theology by incorporating Avicenna. This made him a bridge figure between radicals and conservatives, attacked by both.
- **Said Nursi** (traditionalist reformer): insisted Islam is a fundamentally rational religion and predicted its triumph over other belief systems precisely *because* of the European Enlightenment — since Islam, unlike other traditions, is not inextricably bound up with taqlīd. Preached pan-Islamic unity through love rather than violence. His Damascus Sermon (1911) was delivered on the eve of the First World War; his *Risale-i Nur* (Light Epistles) fused Sunni theology with Sufi ideas and gave rise to the modern Nūr movement.
- **Ahmed Hilmi** (Young Turk with Sufi sympathies): rejected crude materialism but drew on racist French social theories to support Turkish nationalism. Represents the darker possible intersection of European ideas with Islamic reform.

## Indian Parallels

In India, **Sayyid Aḥmad Khān** (d. 1898) founded Aligarh University to replace the traditional *dars-i niẓāmī* with Westernizing education. He argued that early Muslims had done with Greek science what his contemporaries should do with European science: take the best and discard what is erroneous. He called Islam a "natural religion" in harmony with whatever science discovers. His interpretation of polygamy as effectively prohibited (because equal treatment of all wives is impossible in practice) — taken over by [[muhammad-abduh]] in Egypt — illustrates how European norms could be imported by way of *ijtihād* rather than straightforward adoption.

[[muhammad-iqbal]] in the early twentieth century drew most broadly on European philosophy (Nietzsche, Bergson, Russell, Durkheim, Cantor) while arguing that Islam's affinities for this world and for concrete empirical investigation meant that European science was in some sense already anticipated by the Islamic revelation.

## Pattern Analysis

Several patterns characterize the reception:

1. **Selectivity**: No Islamic intellectual embraced all European ideas indiscriminately. Selection was typically driven by what could be harmonized with Islam, what might reverse military and political weakness, and what served specific political programs.
2. **Historical precedent**: The Greek–Arabic translation movement (9th c.) provided a model and a legitimating precedent. Reformers who wanted to embrace European ideas regularly invoked this parallel.
3. **Authenticity claims**: Both those who embraced European ideas and those who resisted them typically framed their positions as representing the authentic Islam — i.e., a return to original sources rather than acceptance of foreign norms.
4. **Colonial urgency**: The political context of colonial domination gave every intellectual choice a stakes that purely academic debates lack. Whether to engage with European philosophy was never merely a philosophical question.

## Historiography

The encounter with European thought has often been narrated as a "failure of modernization" — Islamic societies were unable to absorb European rationalism because of religious conservatism. Adamson's treatment rejects this framing, showing that engagement was sophisticated, varied, and often philosophically acute. El-Rouyaheb's work on the Ottoman encounter is particularly important. The intellectual history of the 19th–20th century Islamic world remains understudied relative to the classical period.
