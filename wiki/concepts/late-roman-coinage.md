# Coinage, Debasement and Taxation in the Crisis (193–337)

The monetary and fiscal history of the [[third-century-crisis|third-century crisis]] — the
**debasement of the silver coinage**, the great reforms of [[aurelian|Aurelian]],
[[diocletian|Diocletian]] and [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]], and the survival and overhaul
of the **Augustan tax system**. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 11 (**Mireille
Corbier**, "Coinage and taxation: the state's point of view"), a chapter whose central argument is
a **caution against the easy causal chain** debasement → inflation → economic collapse.

## The tri-metallic system and its accounting unit

The early-imperial coinage was **tri-metallic**: the gold **aureus**, the silver **denarius**, and
the base-metal small change (sestertius, dupondius, as). The **denarius remained the unit of
account** throughout, even as the physical coin decayed — prices, pay and taxes continued to be
*reckoned* in denarii long after good silver denarii had ceased to circulate. The state's monetary
interventions are best read not as economic policy in the modern sense but from **"the state's point
of view"**: the need to pay the army and meet expenditure from a finite stock of precious metal.

## The chronology of debasement

- **Caracalla** introduced the **antoninianus** (the "radiate," tariffed at two denarii but
  containing less than two denarii's silver) in **215** — a profitable retariffing that effectively
  devalued.
- The silver content then fell steadily and, in the central crisis decades (**c. 253–270**, under
  [[gallienus|Gallienus]] and his contemporaries), **collapsed**: the antoninianus became a
  copper coin with a wash of silver, its fineness reduced to a few per cent.
- **[[aurelian|Aurelian]]'s reform (274)** restored a heavier, marked silver-washed coin (the
  *XXI* / *KA* marking, signalling a 20:1 controlled ratio) — a partial stabilization, not a return
  to good silver.
- **[[diocletian|Diocletian]]'s reforms (286–296)**: a comprehensive recoinage introducing the gold
  **aureus** at a fixed standard, a good silver coin (the **argenteus**), and a large silvered
  bronze (the **nummus / follis**), supported by a network of newly organized **imperial mints**;
  the closure of remaining local civic coinages (e.g. the Alexandrian tetradrachm, 296).
- The **Currency Edict and the Edict on Maximum Prices (301)** — the famous attempt to *re-tariff*
  the coinage and then cap thousands of prices and wages across the empire. Corbier reads the Price
  Edict less as naïve folly than as a response to the inflation that Diocletian's own monetary
  manipulations had helped unleash; it was unenforceable and soon abandoned.
- **[[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]'s solidus (introduced 309 at Trier, generalized from 324)**:
  a lighter, reliable **gold coin at 72 to the pound**, which became the **stable monetary anchor of
  the late-Roman and Byzantine world** for centuries. The decisive late-antique shift is thus from a
  silver to a **gold** standard, with base metal for daily use and silver marginalized.

## The revisionist caution: debasement is not inflation

Corbier's central methodological point — the heart of the chapter — is that the causal link from
**monetary manipulation to proportional inflation "still has to be proved."** Debasement was real
and continuous, but:

- **Serious price rises came late** (essentially from the 270s), not in step with the debasement of
  the 250s–260s; much of the visible price movement was a **remonetization effect** (the public
  re-pricing goods in the new, openly devalued coin) rather than a monetarist spiral.
- Prices and wages are **thinly documented** (the Egyptian papyri aside), and the surviving figures
  cannot sustain the old picture of runaway hyperinflation across the empire.
- The state debased not from ignorance but to **stretch a fixed metal stock** across rising military
  costs; hoarding (the "metal stock" withdrawn from circulation in the insecure decades) compounded
  the shortage of good coin.

This dovetails with the wider volume's deflation of "crisis" (see [[roman-egypt]], [[late-roman-cities]]).

## The survival and overhaul of the tax system

The other half of Corbier's chapter is **taxation**, and here the story is **continuity then
reform**:

- The **Augustan tax system endured essentially intact until Diocletian** — the land tax (*tributum
  soli*) and poll tax (*tributum capitis*) assessed on provincial censuses, with Italy and Roman
  citizens long privileged.
- The [[constitutio-antoniniana-212|Constitutio Antoniniana]] (212), which made almost all free
  inhabitants citizens, **did not lighten the tax burden** — the constitution's own clause
  *"salvis tributis"* (the public taxes being preserved) shows taxation was explicitly maintained;
  Caracalla simultaneously doubled the inheritance tax (which now fell on all citizens).
- **Diocletian universalized taxation**: the new **iugatio–capitatio** system assessed land in fiscal
  units (*iuga*) and persons/labour (*capita*) on a regular **census** (the 15-year *indiction*
  cycle), turning the annona (requisitions in kind for the army) into a **regular, budgeted tax**.
  Crucially, **Italy lost its immunity** and was brought into the taxed provinces — the symbolic end
  of Italy's privileged exemption and of the old citizen/subject fiscal distinction.

## Significance

Corbier's chapter reframes the monetary "catastrophe" of the third century as a **managed (if
damaging) response by a cash-strapped state**, and insists the economic consequences cannot simply be
read off the silver content of the coins. The lasting outcomes — Diocletian's reformed,
fully-taxed, regionally-minted fiscal state and Constantine's gold solidus — are the **institutional
foundations of the late-Roman and Byzantine economy**, achieved *through* the crisis rather than
swept away by it.
