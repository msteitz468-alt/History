# Eigenkirche (Proprietary Church)

**Eigenkirche** (German: "own church" or "proprietary church"; plural *Eigenkirchen*) is the modern historiographical term—coined by the German legal historian **Ulrich Stutz** in 1895—for a church founded, owned, and controlled by a private lay lord. In the Eigenkirche system, which was the dominant form of ecclesiastical organization in the early medieval Frankish, Lombard, and Anglo-Saxon worlds, the church and its revenues were treated as private property of the founding family, who exercised rights over the appointment of clergy, the use of tithes, and the disposition of the church's endowment.

---

## Definition: Emic vs. Etic

*   **Emic Perspective**: Early medieval contemporaries did not use the term *Eigenkirche*. Instead, they referred to the proprietor's church using terms indicating ownership: *ecclesia propria* ("own church"), *ecclesia de iure nostro* ("church of our legal right"), *basilica dominica* ("lord's basilica"). The *ius patronatus* (right of patronage) was the formal legal bundle of rights that encapsulated the lord's claim over "his" church.
*   **Etic Perspective (Stutz's Framework)**: Ulrich Stutz introduced *Eigenkirche* as an analytical category to describe a phenomenon he argued was categorically different from the later Gregorian canonical theory of church organization. Stutz showed that early medieval churches were legally embedded in the Germanic system of private property law—treated by their founders not as sacred communal institutions but as family assets with revenue-generating potential.

## Origins and Development

### The Late Roman Foundation
The roots of the Eigenkirche lie in the late Roman practice of *oratories* (private chapels on great estates). As the villa economy spread Christianity into the countryside in the fourth and fifth centuries, wealthy landowners began to build chapels for their dependents. The bishop of the *civitas* technically supervised all churches in his diocese, but the physical remoteness of rural settlements and the collapse of the Roman state progressively undermined episcopal authority over these private foundations.

### The Early Medieval Proliferation (6th–8th Centuries)
The Eigenkirche system became the dominant reality of rural Christianity in the post-Roman West for several reasons:

1.  **Privatization of public functions**: As the Roman state withdrew, its administrative functions (including the support of religious infrastructure) devolved onto local magnates. The great landowner who built and endowed a local church was performing a service previously provided by the imperial government.
2.  **Revenue rights**: The founder and his heirs held the right to collect the **tithe** (a tenth of agricultural produce) owed to the church. They also received offerings, burial fees, and benefited from the church's endowment of land (*dos ecclesiae*). A large estate with several dependent churches could generate substantial income from these ecclesiastical revenues.
3.  **Clerical appointment (right of *Investiture*)**: The lord could appoint, install, and dismiss the priest of his church. This priest was effectively a dependent of the lord in the same way as any other estate official. The lord might appoint a younger son, a loyal freedman, or an educated cleric from another lord's household—with minimal involvement from the bishop.
4.  **Expansion into monasteries**: The Eigenkirche principle extended to monasteries. Great magnates founded and endowed monasteries, which they treated as family assets—using them as retirement homes for elderly family members, as repositories for family treasure, and as sources of prayer for the family's souls. The abbot was appointed by the founding family, not elected by the monks or approved by the bishop.

## The Eigenkirche and the Structure of the Early Medieval Church

**Georg Scheibelreiter** (*NCMH*, Ch. 24) argues that the Eigenkirche system fundamentally shaped the character of early medieval Christianity:

*   **Parallel ecclesiastical hierarchies**: The theoretical hierarchy ran Pope → Archbishop (Metropolitan) → Bishop → Priest. The actual structure of rural Christianity ran Lord → Dependent Priest, with the bishop often struggling to exercise any meaningful supervision over dozens or hundreds of private churches in his diocese.
*   **The fiscal bishop**: The most important function of the early medieval bishop was fiscal and administrative—managing the bishop's *familia* (household), supervising construction, distributing alms, and adjudicating disputes—rather than primarily pastoral or theological. Bishops were typically drawn from the great noble families of the region, appointed by the king, and were as likely to be warriors and administrators as preachers.
*   **Royal synods as the key institution**: In place of the Roman canonical system (provincial councils presided over by metropolitan archbishops), the effective instrument of ecclesiastical governance in the Frankish and Visigothic kingdoms was the **royal church council** (*synod*). These councils—chaired by the king or his representative, attended by bishops and abbots—enacted church law, distributed church property, and disciplined clergy, all under royal direction. The Visigothic Councils of Toledo (from 589 CE) are the most systematic example.
*   **The Carolingian reform context**: The Eigenkirche system was a primary target of the **Carolingian church reforms** of the late eighth and ninth centuries (under Charlemagne and Louis the Pious) and, more fundamentally, of the **Gregorian Reform** of the eleventh century. The reformers—led by Pope Gregory VII's conflict with Henry IV—sought to end lay investiture and assert canonical episcopal control over all churches, in direct contradiction to the *Eigenkirche* principle. The *Investiture Controversy* (1076–1122) was, in essence, a conflict over whether the Eigenkirche model was legally valid.

## Limitations of the Concept

Historians have qualified Stutz's original, rigidly proprietorial interpretation:

*   **Variable in practice**: The actual relationship between a lord and his church varied enormously. Some lords exercised their rights assertively; many others left their churches largely autonomous in practice if not in law.
*   **Not purely secular**: The spiritual dimension was real. Founders believed that endowing a church bound the priests to pray perpetually for their souls and those of their ancestors. The church-as-property-asset and the church-as-intercessory-institution were not contradictory in the early medieval mind.
*   **Episcopal resistance was ongoing**: Bishops continuously asserted theoretical rights over private churches through synodal legislation, even when unable to enforce them in practice.
