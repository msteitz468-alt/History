# Merovingian Coinage

**Coins as an "invisible Roman empire": Frankish minting that perpetuated late-Roman imperial ideology
without claiming the imperial title.** Jürgen Strothmann's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 35) reads
Merovingian numismatics as both an economic system and an ideological statement — hundreds of decentralized
mints producing gold coins in the Roman tradition, positioning Frankish kings within a Roman framework of
legitimacy.

## Definition and Scope

The standard coin was the gold **tremissis** (*triens*), one-third of a solidus, inherited from the late
Roman/Byzantine system and dominant through much of the sixth and early seventh centuries. Coins "retained
Roman-style busts, Victory figures, crosses, and Latin legends long after any connection with Rome or
Byzantium" — for Strothmann, "not… mere inertia but active ideological claim-making."

## The Decentralized System of Moneyers

Merovingian coinage was strikingly **decentralized**. It was produced by semi-private **moneyers**
(*monetarii*) whose names appear on the coins alongside the mint place-name: "hundreds of named moneyers
operated across hundreds of mint sites" — "potentially over 800 different mint place-names" — "contrast[ing]
sharply with centralized Byzantine mint organization and reflect[ing] the fragmented political geography of
the Merovingian kingdoms." The goldsmith-turned-courtier Eligius began at the mint under Chlothar II (see
[[merovingian-polity]]).

## The Gold-to-Silver Transition

A crucial shift occurred across **c. 650–700**: the gold tremissis gave way to silver *denarii*, the basis
of the later Carolingian monetary reform. Strothmann situates this within "reduced Mediterranean gold
supply and changing trade patterns" — the same horizon at which Mediterranean ceramic imports ceased (see
[[merovingian-ceramics-and-mediterranean-trade]]) and Byzantine subsidies ended (see
[[merovingian-byzantine-relations]]).

## Debate and Historiography

Whether the coins served primarily **prestige** functions (tribute, gifts, ransom) or **commercial**
circulation remains contested: the high number of mints and coins in rural cemeteries argue for some
commercial use, but the scarcity of small silver suggests incomplete monetization of the early rural
economy. German-language scholarship (Strothmann, Kluge) emphasizes the ideological dimension; English-
language scholarship (McCormick, Wickham) the economic functions. The "invisible Roman empire" thesis is
Strothmann's own interpretive contribution, a numismatic parallel to the Romanizing claims visible in
[[merovingian-coinage|coin]] iconography, [[merovingian-epigraphy|royal epitaphs]], and the spolia of
[[merovingian-religious-architecture]].
