# The Roman Constitution and Public Criminal Law

How the late Republic understood "constitutional" right, the sanctity of the
citizen's person, and — its central legal innovation — the **standing criminal courts
(*quaestiones perpetuae*)**. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 13 (Duncan
Cloud).

## There was no "constitution"

Latin had **no word for "constitution."** Cloud stresses that Romans argued
"constitutional" questions by appeal to an *assemblage* of **laws (*leges*)**,
**ancestral custom (*mos maiorum*)**, **institutions**, **right (*ius*)**, and
**precedent (*exemplum*)** — never to a single document. Because custom ruled,
**innovation was inherently suspect**: legitimate change had to be dressed as a
**return to an ancestral norm**, so the two great innovations of the age (the *Lex
Sempronia de capite civis* and the *[[senatus-consultum-ultimum|SCU]]*) were both
given **fictitious early-Republican pedigrees**.

## The citizen's *caput*

What raised the emotional temperature was **treating a citizen as a slave**. A
magistrate's summary power (*coercitio*) could flog or execute a slave, but not a
citizen without due process — the principle of ***provocatio ad populum*** (appeal to
the people). Cloud (against Mommsen) reads *provocatio* not as a formal "appeal to a
higher court" but as a **challenge** reminding the magistrate that summary violence
against a citizen affronted the nature of citizenship, backed by the **threat of the
crowd's sympathy**. A determined governor (Verres killing Gavius) could ignore it.

## The SCU and the dictatorship

Two devices let the community override the citizen's protections:

- The **[[senatus-consultum-ultimum|*senatus consultum ultimum*]]** (first 121, last
  43): the Senate urging the magistrates to defend the state. It conferred **no new
  legal powers** but lent overwhelming moral-political pressure to act; from 88 it was
  usually paired with declaring named men ***hostes*** (public enemies) — assimilating
  a citizen to a foreign enemy and so making his killing tolerable (the logic of the
  **[[sullan-proscriptions-82bce|Sullan]] and triumviral proscriptions**).
- The **dictatorship**, revived by **[[sulla|Sulla]]** (open-ended but for a stated
  purpose — to make laws and set the state in order) and abused by
  **[[julius-caesar|Caesar]]** (whose final, *perpetuo* and general-purpose
  dictatorship "gave him the powers of a pre-Republican king" and made
  **[[julius-caesar-assassination-44bce|tyrannicide]]** a persuasive option).

Cloud's theme: the Republic could adapt city-state institutions to empire (the
extraordinary commands of Antonius and [[pompey]]), but **never solved the problem of
the over-mighty magistrate** — that was left to Augustus.

## The standing criminal courts (*quaestiones perpetuae*)

The chapter's core is the rise of **permanent jury-courts**, which by the end of the
Republic had **displaced the popular assemblies** as the venue for serious offences
(*ius publicum*). Before them, crime was handled by five overlapping means: the
*paterfamilias*' domestic *consilium*; the minor magistrates (*IIIviri capitales*,
for slaves and humble citizens); **assembly trials** (*iudicia populi*, mostly for
*perduellio*/treason — slow and cumbersome); the archaic **private criminal action**;
and **ad hoc special courts** (*quaestiones extraordinariae*).

- The **first** standing court was the ***quaestio de pecuniis repetundis*** (the
  **extortion court**), set up by the tribune **L. Calpurnius Piso in 149** — the
  revolutionary feature being a court with a **defined field, always available**. It
  offered mere restitution and proved feeble (scandalous acquittals).
- The **Gracchan extortion law** (123/2) was far tougher — **double damages**,
  non-citizens suing in their own right, simplified procedure (*nominis delatio*) —
  and, decisively, **transferred the juries from senators to equites**.
- **[[sulla|Sulla]] (81)** systematized a whole range of standing courts (murder,
  *vis*, forgery, *maiestas*, etc.) — "the most durable aspect of his work" — and
  **restored senatorial juries**; the **Lex Aurelia (70)** then split juries among
  **senators, equites, and *tribuni aerarii***.

## Contested — control of the courts

Cloud rejects the "radical" thesis (that **Gaius Gracchus** meant the equestrian
juries to be an **alternative power-centre** to the Senate): the equestrian order was
**not a homogeneous pressure group**, and the *publicani*-vs-governor clash that made
juries a burning issue was largely confined to the **90s** (the
[[roman-equites|equites]] and senators otherwise shared propertied interests). The
control-of-the-courts controversy was real only between **106 and 70**.
