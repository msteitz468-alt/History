# Trial by Ordeal

A category of medieval legal proof in which guilt or innocence was determined by subjecting the accused to a physical test believed to reveal divine judgment — such as carrying a red-hot iron a set distance, plunging an arm into boiling water, or being bound and submerged in water (sinking taken as innocence, floating as guilt, on the theory that "pure" water would reject a perjurer). Alongside judicial combat (trial by battle), the ordeal functioned as an "irrational" mode of proof, relying on divine intervention rather than testimony, documentary evidence, or reasoned argument, and was typically administered with ecclesiastical participation (priestly blessing of the ordeal instruments).

The ordeal was gradually displaced across the twelfth and early thirteenth centuries by the rise of Romano-canonical procedure (*ius commune*) — written, reasoned law drawing on rediscovered Roman law and systematized canon law, administered by professionally trained judges relying on witness testimony, documentary proof, and formal rules of evidence. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) formally prohibited clerical participation in ordeals, effectively ending the practice's legal legitimacy across most of Latin Christendom, though local variations and later revivals (such as witch-trial "swimming" tests) persisted informally for centuries.

## Significance

The decline of the ordeal marks one of the clearest institutional turning points in medieval European legal history: the shift from divinely-mediated, irrational proof to professionally administered, evidence-based rational procedure that underlies the modern civil-law tradition.
