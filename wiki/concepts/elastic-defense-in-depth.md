# Elastic Defense-in-Depth

The German defensive tactical doctrine adopted on the Western Front in late 1916–1917 in response to the stalemate and the increasing power of Allied artillery and infantry assaults (particularly the British Somme offensive of 1916). It replaced the earlier rigid, forward-held trench lines with a flexible, layered system designed to absorb and defeat attacks through depth, reserves, and timely counterattacks rather than holding every inch of ground.

## Core Principles (per Lupfer and German regulations)

- **Defense in depth**: Multiple positions (outpost zone, main battle zone, rear zone) rather than a single continuous line. The goal is not to stop the attacker at the first trench but to canalize him into kill zones where he can be engaged by artillery and counterattack forces.
- **Economy of force in the forward positions**: Lightly held forward zones (outposts and advanced positions) designed to delay and disrupt rather than decisively defeat the attacker. The bulk of the infantry and reserves are held further back.
- **Counterattack as the decisive element**: Local and larger-scale counterattacks (Gegenstoß and Gegenangriff) launched from depth to restore the situation and inflict losses on the attacker while he is disorganized.
- **Artillery and combined arms integration**: Heavy use of artillery for counter-preparation and support of counterattacks; coordination between infantry, artillery, and machine guns.
- **Initiative at lower levels within the commander's intent**: Commanders at battalion and company level are expected to act aggressively with counterattacks when opportunities arise.

This doctrine was a direct reaction to the failure of "holding the forward line at all costs" against overwhelming firepower. It conserved German manpower and made Allied offensives far more costly for limited gains.

## Relation to Other Concepts

- Predecessor to modern "active defense" and "defense in depth" ideas.
- Complementary to the 1918 German offensive infiltration tactics (the elastic defense's virtues were incorporated into the attack): per [[sources/lupfer-dynamics-of-doctrine-1981]] (Range 3 / Ch. 3), the 1918 offensive doctrine "incorporated many of the virtues of their elastic defense-in-depth".
- Illustrates principles of war: economy of force, maneuver (counterattacks), security, and surprise (through depth and reserves).
- Contrasts with Allied "bite and hold" or massed artillery tactics of 1916–17.
- See also [[concepts/infiltration-tactics-1918]] and [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/]].

## Implementation and Training

The doctrine was not merely published; it was disseminated through intensive training programs and reinforced by high command (OHL) emphasis. Success depended on the timely commitment of reserves and the willingness of forward commanders to yield ground temporarily in order to set up successful counterattacks.
