# Constructivist Rationalism

The epistemological and moral stance that holds that human institutions, morals, law, and economic arrangements are, or ought to be, the product of deliberate rational design; that only those rules or practices that can be fully justified, consciously understood, given an explicit purpose, or scientifically proven are legitimate or “reasonable.”

## Definition and Scope

Hayek also calls it “scientism” (after the French). It treats the social order on the model of a physical or engineering problem: someone who possesses (or claims to possess) all the relevant facts can and should arrange the parts into an optimal whole. It is “constructivist” because it sees order as something that must be constructed by reason rather than as something that emerges from following abstract rules whose effects no one fully knows.

It stands in opposition to the evolutionary view that most serviceable rules and institutions arose through cultural selection without anyone designing them or fully understanding why they work.

## Four Requirements That Traditional Morals Fail

From the constructivist standpoint, whatever is not scientifically proven, not fully understood, lacks a fully specified purpose, or has unknown effects is “unreasonable” or “unscientific.” Traditional morals, several property, and the extended order fail these tests — and are therefore condemned by socialists and constructivists — even though they demonstrably produce the order that sustains civilisation.

## Intellectual History

- Foundational statement in [[hayek-constitution-of-liberty-1960|*The Constitution of Liberty*]] (1960), ch. 2: the conception of man deliberately building his civilization "stems from an erroneous intellectualism that regards human reason as something standing outside nature and possessed of knowledge and reasoning capacity independent of experience" — the mind is itself a product of the civilization it inhabits.
- Canon of the rationalist tradition (ch. 4): "deeply imbued with Cartesian rationalism" — the Encyclopedists, Rousseau, the Physiocrats, Condorcet; paradigmatically Sieyès urging the revolutionary assembly to act like men emerging from the state of nature to sign a social contract. The division crosses nationality: Hobbes, the English Jacobins (Godwin, Priestley, Price, Paine), and Jefferson after his French stay belong here; Montesquieu, Constant, Tocqueville do not. Hayek even assigns *homo oeconomicus* to this lineage, "explicitly introduced... only by the younger Mill." See [[classical-liberalism]] for the full two-traditions thesis.
- The rationalist's dilemma (ch. 2): "The use of reason aims at control and predictability. But the process of the advance of reason rests on freedom and the unpredictability of human action" — a fully planned society could halt the growth of the knowledge planning presupposes; progress structurally consists in "the few convincing the many," so majority opinion cannot be its standard. The argument for liberty targets "all exclusive, privileged, monopolistic organization," not organization as such.
- Applied to law (ch. 10): case-by-case "extreme" utilitarianism denies the very function of rules — "Only a society of omniscient individuals could give each person complete liberty to weigh every particular action on general utilitarian grounds."
- Historical-political variant (ch. 16): Hayek attributes Germany's rapid turn from liberalism to state direction partly to unification achieved "by the artifice of statesmanship rather than by gradual evolution," strengthening the belief that deliberate design should remodel society.
- Roots in Cartesian rationalism and the Enlightenment demand for transparency and justification.
- Reinforced by the prestige of the natural sciences and the misapplication of their methods to society.
- Expressed politically in socialism’s project to redesign morals, law, and language on rational lines.
- Critiqued by Hayek as continuous with earlier attacks on commerce (Aristotle through Rousseau) and with the atavistic demand to return to the solidarity and visible purposes of the small group.

## The Canonical Definition: *Law, Legislation and Liberty* I (1973)

The trilogy's opening chapter gives the term its formal definition: "a conception which assumes that all social institutions are, and ought to be, the product of deliberate design" — rooted in "a deeply ingrained propensity of primitive thought to interpret all regularity to be found in phenomena anthropomorphically, as the result of the design of a thinking mind," revived by Descartes ("radical doubt" delegitimizing all rules not derivable from clear and distinct premises) and given social content by Hobbes and Rousseau (social-contract intentionalism). Hayek pairs it against "evolutionary (or, as Sir Karl Popper calls it, 'critical') rationalism," and names its offshoots: legal positivism ("driven to the fiction that all law is the product of somebody's will"), the doctrine of unlimited sovereignty, act-utilitarianism, and much of sociology. Two further arguments: ordinary language itself is anthropomorphic (society "acts," "rewards," "is just") and so "regularly lead[s] to false conclusions"; and over-claiming reason breeds its own negation — "the constructivist rationalism which knows no bounds to the applications of conscious reason has historically again and again given birth to a revolt against reason" (his exhibits: Hegel, quoted conceding that liberalism "clings to abstraction"; Rousseau's "no law except law willed by living men"; Keynes's 1938 "My Early Beliefs" — "We entirely repudiated a personal liability on us to obey general rules … we recognized no moral obligation"). In the 1982 preface Hayek notes he adopted the uglier "constructivistic" to shed the commendatory ring of "constructive." The mirror-image page is [[spontaneous-order]]; the "synoptic delusion" — "the fiction that all the relevant facts are known to some one mind" — links it to [[knowledge-problem]].

## Consequences

When acted upon, constructivist rationalism destroys or weakens the abstract rules on which the extended order depends. The result is not a rationally superior order but the atavistic morality of the tribe writ large — or the totalitarian planning state.

## Relation to Other Hayek Concepts

Directly produces the fatal conceit (see [[fatal-conceit]]). Incompatible with the knowledge problem and the spontaneous formation of the extended order ([[extended-order]]). Contrasted with true individualism and with the evolutionary account of tradition.
