# The Third-Century Crisis

The **"crisis of the third century"** is the conventional label for the half-century of acute
disruption in the Roman Empire between the murder of [[severan-dynasty|Severus Alexander]] (235) and
the accession of [[diocletian|Diocletian]] (284) — an age of incessant civil war, foreign invasion,
plague, and monetary collapse. It is the organizing theme of [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]], whose
narrative (Ch. 2, **John Drinkwater**) and discussion subject the very concept to **critical
revision**.

## The phenomena

The period's defining feature was **war, civil and foreign**. At least **fifty-one** men claimed the
imperial title; emperors rose and fell with the loyalty of their armies, almost all dying violently.
The pressures were twofold and simultaneous: **Germanic invasion** from the north (Goths, Franks,
Alamanni; the Gothic sea-raids that sacked Athens, Corinth, and Sparta) and the aggression of the new
**[[sassanid-empire|Sassanid Persia]]** in the east. The low point came around **260**: the emperor
**[[valerian]] captured alive** by Shapur I, and the empire fracturing into three — a central rump
under [[gallienus|Gallienus]], the breakaway **[[gallic-empire|Gallic Empire]]** (Postumus), and the
**[[palmyra|Palmyrene]]** realm of [[zenobia|Zenobia]]. Recovery came through the "Illyrian"
soldier-emperors — **Claudius II** (Gothicus, victor at Naïssus 269) and above all **[[aurelian|Aurelian]]**,
the *Restitutor Orbis*, who reunited the empire (272–274).

## Causes

Drinkwater (following the consensus) sees a **new problem exacerbating old weaknesses**:

- **Military**: an army too small and dispersed, dependent on piecemeal field-armies and
  inexperienced senatorial commanders, lost the initiative against large, well-led forces attacking on
  two fronts.
- **Fiscal**: continual war was expensive, taxation traditionally low and already committed to the
  army; the favored expedient — **debasement of the silver coinage** — disrupted the economy.
- **Political**: emperors were **military dictators** personally responsible for defense; failure
  invited usurpation, and dynastic succession repeatedly failed.

These fed a **vicious circle**: weakness invited invasion, invasion bred usurpation, civil war
invited further invasion.

## Revisionism: "crisis," "change," or neither?

Drinkwater's **discussion** is the chapter's analytical core, and it qualifies the catastrophist
picture sharply:

- The "crisis proper" (continual civil war driven by two-front invasion) really ran only **c. 248–270**,
  not the full 235–284. The years 235–248 were "the **final phase of the Severan world**," and
  Rostovtzeff's image of Maximinus as a peasant tyrant leading the poor against the bourgeoisie is
  rejected.
- The empire **never came close to collapse**. The breakaway empires advertised themselves as
  **"Roman"**; the prevailing tendency was **centripetal**, with strikingly little genuine separatism —
  the provincial elites were too Romanized to lead local resistance.
- Suffering was **uneven**: border provinces were hit hard, but Britain and Africa little; coin
  debasement mattered less to farmers; only **one** legitimate emperor (Philip) fell in pitched battle
  against Roman troops.
- Many historians therefore prefer to speak of **change** rather than crisis — an acceleration of
  developments (begun under the Severans) producing a new style of empire: experiments in **shared
  rule**, the permanent **mobile field-army** (cavalry-heavy), the rise of administrative
  **"sub-capitals"** (Trier, Milan, Sirmium, Antioch), the **eclipse of Rome and the senate**, and the
  **equestrian takeover** of high military and civil command.

## Significance

Whether labelled "crisis," "transformation," or both, the period is the **crucible of late antiquity**:
the institutions improvised under pressure — field army, equestrian command, multiple capitals, solar
henotheism — were precisely the tools [[aurelian|Aurelian]] and then [[diocletian|Diocletian]] used to
refound the state. That the empire could **produce a Diocletian** is, for Drinkwater, the proof that it
"was not in decline."

## Historiography

The period is notoriously **ill-documented**: no comprehensive contemporary narrative survives. The
Latin tradition rests on the brief fourth-century epitomes (Aurelius Victor, Eutropius, the
"Epitomator") derived from a lost *Kaisergeschichte*; the **Historia Augusta** is a single, unreliable
late-fourth-century author to be used only when corroborated. Historians depend heavily on
**numismatics, papyrology, and epigraphy** (the *Res Gestae Divi Saporis* of Shapur I; the Augsburg
victory-altar) — which is why the basic **chronology** remains one of the most disputed aspects of the
age. The "crisis vs. continuity" debate (Alföldy, MacMullen, Strobel) is itself unresolved.
