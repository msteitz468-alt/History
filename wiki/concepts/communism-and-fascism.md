# Communism and Fascism

## Definition and Scope

Robert Strayer, in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], treats **communism** and
**fascism** together as the twentieth century's two great revolutionary alternatives
to liberal democracy — phenomena of "the greatest world historical significance."
Both sought "an unprecedented degree of state control over individuals and society,"
giving rise to the category **"totalitarianism."** Both produced human catastrophe on
an immense scale (the Holocaust; the Soviet terror; the Great Leap Forward and
Cultural Revolution; Cambodia's killing fields — see [[genocide]]), and each spawned
one of the century's great conflicts: fascism the [[world-wars-era|Second World War]],
communism the [[cold-war|Cold War]]. By the 1970s communism governed roughly **a
third of the world's population.** Strayer analyzes both along three axes — as
**ideologies, as movements, and as regimes**.

## Common origins, shared structure

Strayer's central comparative claim: though derived from "very distinct intellectual
genealogies," both arose as viable projects from the **fragmented, bitterly divided
Europe of the First World War**, as **revolutionary responses to the discontents of
industrial modernity and political liberalism** — its class conflict, economic
instability, atomizing individualism, and pervasive materialism. Both:

- exalted the **collective** over the liberal individual (for communists, the
  international working class; for fascists, the organic nation);
- looked to **overthrow** existing orders and forge a "**New Man**" and a new
  consciousness;
- despised liberal democracy, party politics, and bourgeois compromise;
- embraced modern science, technology, industry, and mass media.

Hitler in 1934: "There is more that binds us to Bolshevism than separates us from
it... above all, genuine revolutionary feeling." Mussolini was a socialist before he
was a fascist; the 1939 Nazi–Soviet Pact showed the relationship was not pure enmity.

## Communism as ideology

Rooted in **Marx** (history as class conflict; capitalism's fatal flaws; an inevitable
proletarian revolution yielding a classless, stateless abundance), communism was
transformed as it took root outside the industrial West:

- **Lenin:** revolution remained necessary even in backward Russia (the "weak link");
  a socialist revolution there could trigger world revolution — privileging **will**
  over economic stage — led by a **small, disciplined vanguard party**.
- **Stalin:** "**socialism in one country**" — the state mobilizing resources for
  forced industrialization and "building socialism."
- **Mao:** the **peasantry** (not the proletariat) as revolutionary class; "**permanent
  revolution**" against the new elites of bureaucratic state socialism, behind the
  Great Leap Forward (1958–61) and Cultural Revolution (1966–76).

Beneath the variants, communism was thoroughly **modernist and future-oriented**,
with a utopian, voluntarist faith in remaking society and the self ("engineers of the
human soul"), certain of victory because Marxism claimed the "scientific" laws of
history.

## Fascism as ideology

Far more **amorphous** — "many parents, no core text": radical nationalism, racial
and anti-Semitic theory, the "revolt against positivism," Social Darwinism, futurism.
Fascism's revolutionary task was the **purification and rebirth of the organic
nation**, corrupted by capitalism, party politics, feminism, and socialism — a
cultural/moral rather than social revolution producing a "New Man" ("hard like Krupp
steel"). It spoke "the language of emotion, myth, will, and action," exalting a
leader mystically bound to the people and valorizing **struggle, violence, and war**
as ennobling, with an extreme masculinism. Its modernity was **selective** — an
"alternative, disciplined modernity in service to the nation." The **Nazi** variant
was distinctive: *völkisch* mysticism plus pseudo-scientific **racism** that defined
the nation by **blood**, casting the Jew as the source of racial pollution — the
ideological engine of the [[genocide|Holocaust]].

## As regimes

Both built one-party states with mass mobilization, secret police, propaganda
monopolies, and the "totalitarian" ambition to control society — and both, Josephson
notes (see [[technology-and-innovation]]), ran the same exploitative large-scale
industrial systems as capitalist states.

## Historiography: the contested comparison

Strayer foregrounds that **comparing communism and fascism has long been highly
controversial.** Soviet communists, victorious over fascism, "strenuously resisted"
any equation, resenting Cold War "totalitarian" analyses (and later *The Black Book of
Communism*'s claim that communism was "even worse"). The fading of communism after
1991 opened space for more "dispassionate" comparison, aided by recognition of wide
variation within each camp. The wiki records this comparison without adjudicating it
(per the Contradiction Protocol). Key thinkers: Hannah Arendt, François Furet, Ernst
Nolte, Robert Paxton, Roger Griffin.
