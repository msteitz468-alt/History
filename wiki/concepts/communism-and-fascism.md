# Communism and Fascism

## Definition and Scope

Robert Strayer, in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], treats **communism** and
**fascism** together as the twentieth century's two great revolutionary alternatives
to liberal democracy — phenomena of "the greatest world historical significance."
Both sought "an unprecedented degree of state control over individuals and society,"
giving rise to the category **"totalitarianism."** Both produced human catastrophe on
an immense scale (the Holocaust; the Soviet terror; the Great Leap Forward and
Cultural Revolution; Cambodia's killing fields — see [[genocide]]), and each spawned
one of the century's great conflicts: fascism the [[world-wars-era|Second World War]],
communism the [[cold-war|Cold War]]. By the 1970s communism governed roughly **a
third of the world's population.** Strayer analyzes both along three axes — as
**ideologies, as movements, and as regimes**.

## Common origins, shared structure

Strayer's central comparative claim: though derived from "very distinct intellectual
genealogies," both arose as viable projects from the **fragmented, bitterly divided
Europe of the First World War**, as **revolutionary responses to the discontents of
industrial modernity and political liberalism** — its class conflict, economic
instability, atomizing individualism, and pervasive materialism. Both:

- exalted the **collective** over the liberal individual (for communists, the
  international working class; for fascists, the organic nation);
- looked to **overthrow** existing orders and forge a "**New Man**" and a new
  consciousness;
- despised liberal democracy, party politics, and bourgeois compromise;
- embraced modern science, technology, industry, and mass media.

Hitler in 1934: "There is more that binds us to Bolshevism than separates us from
it... above all, genuine revolutionary feeling." Mussolini was a socialist before he
was a fascist; the 1939 Nazi–Soviet Pact showed the relationship was not pure enmity.

## Communism as ideology

Rooted in **Marx** (history as class conflict; capitalism's fatal flaws; an inevitable
proletarian revolution yielding a classless, stateless abundance), communism was
transformed as it took root outside the industrial West:

- **Lenin:** revolution remained necessary even in backward Russia (the "weak link");
  a socialist revolution there could trigger world revolution — privileging **will**
  over economic stage — led by a **small, disciplined vanguard party**.
- **Stalin:** "**socialism in one country**" — the state mobilizing resources for
  forced industrialization and "building socialism."
- **Mao:** the **peasantry** (not the proletariat) as revolutionary class; "**permanent
  revolution**" against the new elites of bureaucratic state socialism, behind the
  Great Leap Forward (1958–61) and Cultural Revolution (1966–76).

Beneath the variants, communism was thoroughly **modernist and future-oriented**,
with a utopian, voluntarist faith in remaking society and the self ("engineers of the
human soul"), certain of victory because Marxism claimed the "scientific" laws of
history.

## Fascism as ideology

Far more **amorphous** — "many parents, no core text": radical nationalism, racial
and anti-Semitic theory, the "revolt against positivism," Social Darwinism, futurism.
Fascism's revolutionary task was the **purification and rebirth of the organic
nation**, corrupted by capitalism, party politics, feminism, and socialism — a
cultural/moral rather than social revolution producing a "New Man" ("hard like Krupp
steel"). It spoke "the language of emotion, myth, will, and action," exalting a
leader mystically bound to the people and valorizing **struggle, violence, and war**
as ennobling, with an extreme masculinism. Its modernity was **selective** — an
"alternative, disciplined modernity in service to the nation." The **Nazi** variant
was distinctive: *völkisch* mysticism plus pseudo-scientific **racism** that defined
the nation by **blood**, casting the Jew as the source of racial pollution — the
ideological engine of the [[genocide|Holocaust]].

## As regimes

Both built one-party states with mass mobilization, secret police, propaganda
monopolies, and the "totalitarian" ambition to control society — and both, Josephson
notes (see [[technology-and-innovation]]), ran the same exploitative large-scale
industrial systems as capitalist states.

## The wartime origin of the comparison: Hayek (1944)

The comparison predates the Cold War analytic. [[hayek-friedrich|Hayek's]]
[[hayek-road-to-serfdom-1944|*Road to Serfdom*]] (1944) — written while the
USSR was an ally — made the structural equation its central move: communism
and fascism "differ among themselves in the nature of the goal toward which
they want to direct the efforts of society. But they all differ from
liberalism and individualism in wanting to organize the whole of society…
they are totalitarian in the true sense of this new word." His evidence was
deliberately drawn from ex-left witnesses (Eastman: Stalinism as
"superfascist"; Voigt; Drucker) and from parallel Nazi/Soviet abuses of
science and language (ch. 11 juxtaposes "Nordic physics" with physics "in
conflict with dialectical materialism"). By 1956 Hayek could claim that what
had "seemed to many almost sacrilege… has become almost a commonplace." His
further genetic claim — that fascism grew *out of* the socialist tradition —
remains far more contested: see [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]] and
[[fascism-and-the-political-left]].

Hayek's late restatement (*Law, Legislation and Liberty* II, 1976) adds the
moral-psychology claim: "the greatest crimes of our time have been committed
by governments that had the enthusiastic support of millions of people who
were guided by moral impulses. It is simply not true that Hitler or Mussolini,
Lenin or Stalin, appealed only to the worst instincts of their people" — the
appeal, on his atavism thesis, was to genuine tribal-inherited cravings for
visible common purpose that both movements satisfied (see
[[social-justice]]).

## Flynn's ownership distinction (1944)

Writing the same year as Hayek, [[flynn-john-t|Flynn]]
([[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944]]) drew the structural line the other way
around — insisting the two systems are *not* one genus: "in the communist
state the government plans for the industries of the nation which it owns and
in the fascist state the government plans for industries which are owned by
private persons." Dictatorship alone does not make fascism ("a mistake... just
as it would be a mistake to define an automobile as a vehicle with four
wheels"); Stalin's USSR, for all its terror, is "very far from being a fascist
dictatorship" because fascism is definitionally a defense of capitalism. He
named Hitler, Mussolini, and Stalin together only as history's three "great
exemplars of autarchy." Flynn also explicitly rebutted the charge that the New
Deal was crypto-communist: its architects were "ex-socialists or academic or
parlor pinks" building corporativist capitalism — "a very genteel and dainty
and pleasant form of fascism which cannot be called fascism at all because it
will be so virtuous and polite." See [[as-we-go-marching-thesis]].

## Payne's comparison (1995): parallels without equation

[[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|Payne]] gives the fullest mainstream
treatment between the two poles. On technique: "Lenin did not create the
political doctrines of fascism, but his Communist totalitarianism based on
systematic mass violence initiated most of the new practices and
institutions of fascist-type regimes" — mass propaganda, coup seizure,
one-party dictatorship, personality cult, terror with camps (Hitler's March
1921 remark about confining "instigators" to concentration camps, when "the
only existing model lay in the new Communist system"). He offers a hedged
**ten-point list of Nazi–Communist parallels** (mutual recognition as the
only real revolutionary counterparts, action theory, elitist
leader-follower logic, party control of the military, autarky and
militarization), concluding that "Hitlerian National Socialism more nearly
paralleled Russian communism than has any other non-Communist system" —
while stating explicitly the list "is not presented to propound a theory of
'Red fascism.'" Himmler himself told the SS in 1943 that Fascism and
National Socialism were "two fundamentally different things," and Goebbels
judged "Mussolini is not a revolutionary like the Führer or Stalin."
Contemporary theorist Manoilescu's tripartite formula: the Soviet system a
state run by a party, Fascist Italy a state to which the party was
subordinate, Nazi Germany a dual party-state system. Payne treats the
Comintern's shifting definitions (landowners' tool 1922 → "social fascism"
1924 → Dimitrov's finance-capital formula 1935) as polemic, the **agent
theory as definitively refuted** (citing Turner: big business "consistently
advised against" Hitler governing; the NSDAP was member-financed), and
documents that Comintern strategy in Germany "was aimed not at preventing a
Nazi triumph but at weakening the Socialists." His atrocity comparison cuts
both ways: the Nazi wartime record "exceeded that of any equivalent phase
even of Stalinism," yet in occupied Poland 1939–41, before the mass
liquidations, the Soviet kill rate ran roughly 600 percent of the German
(Gross's figures), and Mussolini's Italy (9 political executions to 1940)
was "brutal and repressive, but not murderous and bloodthirsty."

## Paxton's critique of the totalitarian equation (2004)

[[paxton-anatomy-of-fascism-2004|*The Anatomy of Fascism*]] accepts the
similarity of techniques ("Awaiting the knock in the night and rotting in a
camp must have felt very similar to both systems' sufferers") while
rejecting the genus: "Stalin ruled a civil society that had been radically
simplified by the Bolshevik Revolution… Hitler (totally unlike Stalin) came
into power with the assent and even assistance of traditional elites, and
governed in strained but effective association with them. Totalitarian
theory is blind to this fundamental character of the Nazi governing
system." Declared aims differed — master race vs. universal equality —
"though Stalin's egregious and barbarous perversions tended to make his
regime converge with Hitler's in its murderous instruments"; and the Soviet
system "lacked Nazism's ingrained competition between party parallel
organizations and established elites for the leader's favor." On the moral
comparison Paxton is explicit: Stalin killed "class enemies" ("a condition
one can change," striking mostly adult males), Hitler "race enemies" ("an
irremediable condition that condemns even newborns") — "This book
acknowledges the repugnance of both terrors, but condemns even more
strongly Nazi biologically racialist extermination because it admitted no
salvation even for women and children." He also traces the concept's
politics: Amendola coined *totalitaria* (May 1923) as a denunciation,
Mussolini gloried in it, the 1960s attacked the theory as Cold War
instrument, and after 1991 it revived "along with its corollary that Nazism
and communism represented a common evil." See [[generic-fascism]].

## Sternhell: common origin, opposite revisions (1994)

[[sternhell-birth-fascist-ideology-1994|Sternhell]] adds a genealogical
dimension to the comparison: fascism arose from a *revision* of the same
Marxism that communism claimed to fulfill — Sorel revised out the
rationalism and materialism and kept the violence; Lenin kept the
materialist doctrine and revised the agency (vanguard for proletariat).
The fork was visible in one room: [[mussolini-benito|Mussolini]]'s review
*Utopia* (1913–14) counted both future Communists (Bordiga, Tasca) and
future Fascists (Panunzio, Lanzillo, Rocca) among its contributors, and
[[sorel-georges|Sorel]] himself ended by praising Lenin ("For Lenin,"
1919) *and* accepting the Fascists' invocation of his name — contemporaries
said "Maurras and Lenin fulfilled the same function" for him. Sternhell's
regime-level aside: "the Mussolini regime of the 1930s was much closer to
the ideological synthesis of *La lupa* or the [[cercle-proudhon|Cercle
Proudhon]] than the Stalin regime was to original Marxism." At the same
time his fascism/Nazism firewall (biological determinism as Nazism's
defining criterion) warns against letting the communism–fascism comparison
collapse into a communism–Nazism one. See [[sternhell-thesis]].

## Griffin: mythic kinship, causal independence (1991)

[[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991|Griffin]] adds two comparative points. On
kinship, he quotes Koestler: "Both the Fascist and the Soviet myths were not
syncretic constructions, but revivals of archetypes... both provided
emotional saturation" — and groups Nazism with Jacobinism, Stalinism, Pol
Pot, Ceaușescu, and Saddam as sharing a "synergy of palingenesis and
destruction," while insisting fascism's totalitarian aspirations, legitimated
by the myth of the reborn *nation*, remain "quite distinct" from regimes
built on a different political myth (his standing critique of the
Friedrich–Brzezinski model). On causation, he explicitly rejects Nolte's
*Historikerstreit*-era thesis (1988) that Nazism was "essentially
anti-Marxist and symbiotically related in its goals and tactics to
Stalinism": Nazi imagery came from pre-war völkisch nationalism, and
Bolshevism's real causal role was **mythic and catalytic** — the more
effective the Bolsheviks' strategy, the more powerful the ultra-right
forces that arose in reaction — not the pivot Nolte makes it. His 1991
postscript adds that the greater post-war danger comes from "palingenetic
movements based on combinations of nationalism and socialism but which lack
a genuine populist dimension" — Khmer Rouge Cambodia and Ba'athist Iraq.
See [[palingenetic-ultranationalism]].

## Historiography: the contested comparison

Strayer foregrounds that **comparing communism and fascism has long been highly
controversial.** Soviet communists, victorious over fascism, "strenuously resisted"
any equation, resenting Cold War "totalitarian" analyses (and later *The Black Book of
Communism*'s claim that communism was "even worse"). The fading of communism after
1991 opened space for more "dispassionate" comparison, aided by recognition of wide
variation within each camp. The wiki records this comparison without adjudicating it
(per the Contradiction Protocol). Key thinkers: Hannah Arendt, François Furet, Ernst
Nolte, Robert Paxton, Roger Griffin.

## The KPD in the Weimar Endgame (Evans 2003)

Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]) on the German Communist Party as
fascism's enemy-twin in the street war: membership grew from 117,000 (1929) to
360,000 (1932), overwhelmingly unemployed (fewer than 10% of members in the
north-west littoral had jobs by 1932) with >50% annual turnover around a
disciplined hardcore. The Comintern's "Third Period" line from 1928 branded
the SPD "social fascists" — a split rooted in the
[[freikorps|Free Corps]] murders of Liebknecht and Luxemburg — foreclosing any
united front. Leader Ernst Thälmann (Hamburg docker background, "instinctive
revolutionary," weak on theory, loyal to Stalin's line) ran for president in
1925 and 1932. Evans's verdict (attributed): KPD power was "an illusion"; its
"fascist" labeling of every Weimar government "blinded it completely to the
threat posed by Nazism," and its confidence in imminent capitalist collapse
was "in retrospect... completely unfounded" — the party met the Nazi seizure
of power expecting a "German October" (see [[reichstag-fire-1933]],
[[nazi-seizure-of-power-1933]]). The milieu war with the SA was
"quasi-guerrilla warfare" over neighborhoods and pubs; "Blood May" 1929 in
Berlin-Wedding (31 dead, mostly by police fire) marks the SPD-police/KPD
front of the triangle. The memoirist Richard Krebs supplies Evans's inside
voice ("Policemen were enemies. God was a lie, invented by the rich"), though
Evans cautions the memoir "read like a thriller" and that Red Front violence
"owed more to the temper of the 'Red Front-Fighters' on the ground than to any
plans laid by Moscow."

## Griffin (2007): Both as Political Modernism

*Modernism and Fascism* places Bolshevism and fascism inside one genus, [[political-modernism]], while preserving the differentia. The October Revolution is political modernism's "first state": Bolshevism as the mazeway of a communitas "led by a new propheta, Vladimir Lenin," with God-building (Gorky/Lunacharsky; Lenin elected "first socialist deity," 1924), Proletkult, the New Soviet Man, and May Day liturgy keying "into primordial associations of spring with cyclic renewal... no less energetically than the Third Reich." Bernice Rosenthal's thesis is endorsed: Nietzschean active nihilism fused with the Bolsheviks' "ruthlessly voluntarist, mercilessly cruel, and radically future-oriented interpretation of Marxism" — extending to Kulak liquidation, the Five Year Plans, and the purges. Bauman's gardening-state frame names "Hitler and Stalin" alike "legitimate offspring of the modern spirit." The key structural difference Griffin retains: "Unlike Bolsheviks who theoretically could look forward to the eventual withering away of the state, inter-war fascist movements had no exit strategy" — fascism's liminality is perpetual by design. (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)

## Payne (1980): ten similarities between National Socialism and communism

*Fascism: Comparison and Definition* ([[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]], ch. 4) offers an explicit ten-point list — presented as *not* propounding "red fascism," but noting Nazism paralleled Russian communism more closely than Fascist Italy ever did: (1) Hitler and Mussolini recognized their "only revolutionary and ideological counterparts were to be found in Soviet Russia"; (2) both rested on a "revolutionary action theory" in which practical success validated ideological innovation; (3) doctrines of constant struggle; (4) rigid elitism and the leadership principle — "a National Socialist was someone who followed Hitler; a Bolshevik was not necessarily a Marxist but someone who followed Lenin"; (5) the "have-not, proletarian-nation theory," which Lenin adopted only after its Italian introduction; (6) one-party dictatorship independent of any particular class; (7) party-army emphasis with the regular army under party control (the 1943 NS Guidance Officers as commissar-equivalents); (8) autarchy, militarization, and revolutionary war as alternative to balanced internal development; (9) an "NEP phase" of partial pluralism en route to fuller dictatorship; (10) international projection of a new ideological myth — Fascism and Nazism being "the last notable ideological innovations in the modern world after Marxism." He quotes Hilferding: the USSR "is neither [capitalist nor socialist]. It represents a totalitarian state economy... to which the economies of Germany and Italy are drawing closer and closer."

The limiting counter-thesis: on Payne's strict definition of totalitarianism, "only a socialist or communist system can achieve full totalitarianism" — Nazism's mixed economy kept it short of the complete model before 1945, and Fascist Italy never approached it. And against Soviet-era equations of Maoism with fascism, all communist regimes except Yugoslavia's remained committed to "complete state bureaucracy, almost total state collectivism... and philosophical materialism" — "cardinal principles absolutely opposed to fascism." His footnote typology of revolutionary mass movements since the 1790s places Leninism (1903–) and Fascism (1919–45) as parallel, distinct genera among six (Jacobin, Anarchist, Socialist, Leninist, Fascist, Populist — with mass counterrevolution, "most notably the Spanish Carlists," a possible seventh).
