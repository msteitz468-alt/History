## Definition and Scope

Ijmāʿ — "consensus" — is the third formal source of Islamic law ([[usul-al-fiqh]]) after the Qurʾān and the Prophetic Sunna. In its fully theorized form it is defined as the agreement of all recognized mujtahids (qualified independent jurists) living in a given age on a particular legal ruling. Once established, a ruling subject to ijmāʿ acquires certain (qaṭʿī) rather than merely probable (ẓannī) epistemic status, and cannot be re-opened by fresh ijtihād.

Hallaq distinguishes ijmāʿ's two functions: (1) as a *macro-instrument*, it defines Sunnism itself — the community that upholds the Prophet's Sunna and the institution of consensus — versus other Muslim communities; and (2) as a *micro-instrument*, it renders authoritative particular fiqh rulings that would otherwise remain merely probable.

## Historical Development

### First/seventh century: consensus as sunnaic practice

In the earliest Islamic century, "consensus" was not yet a distinct legal category but was intertwined with what Hallaq calls *sunnaic practice* — the living legal tradition of a garrison town or city, understood as the continuous inheritance of Prophetic and Companion conduct. The unanimous opinion of a local group of scholars constituted consensus in this looser, practice-based sense: "consensual opinion of a group (*ijtamaʿa raʾyuhum ʿalā …*) provided an authoritative basis not only for action but also for the creation of sunna" (Hallaq, ch. 1). In this early sense, consensus nearly always entailed the notion that what the scholars agreed upon must have been what the Companions — and therefore the Prophet — had sanctioned.

### Second/eighth century: regional consensus

Medinan scholars held that *their* consensus, rooted in the living community's practice, was the authoritative expression of Prophetic Sunna. The [[malik-ibn-anas|Mālikite]] school's legal theory elevated Medinese consensus to a binding authority on the grounds that Medina, as the Prophet's own city, preserved the most authentic continuous record of his practice. Later theorists of other schools objected: if consensus is binding, it must be that of the entire Muslim community, not of one city. A mujtahid whose opinion partook of Medinese consensus would be authoritative in Medina but not elsewhere — an absurd result.

### Third/ninth century: the textual foundation problem

For consensus to count as a *certain* source of law it had to be grounded in revealed texts, since revelation alone yields certainty. Early Quranic arguments for this proved insufficient. By the end of the third/ninth century, jurists turned to Prophetic hadith reports thematically asserting that the Muslim community as a whole cannot agree on an error: "My community shall never agree on a falsehood." These reports were individually *solitary* (āḥād) and therefore only probable — but in combination they formed *tawātur maʿnawī* (thematic recurrence), collectively sufficient for certain knowledge: "a dozen or so hadiths to the effect that the community of Muslims will never agree on an error, providing in the process the authority for consensus" (Hallaq, ch. 2, §5).

### Fourth/tenth century onward: mature theory

By the middle of the fourth/tenth century the theory had settled: ijmāʿ is the unanimous agreement of all mujtahids in a given age, grounded ultimately in revelation, and its conclusions enjoy certainty. Consensus can ratify as epistemically certain any ruling originally based on probable textual evidence — if the community cannot err, its agreement on a probable inference upgrades that inference to certainty: "if consensus is reached on what appears to be probable evidence, then the fact that consensus was possible makes it necessary to believe that this evidence was certain after all" (Hallaq, ch. 2, §6).

A practical consequence: cases subject to consensus are numerically few relative to cases subject to *khilāf* (juristic disagreement). Reopening a case on which ijmāʿ had been declared would amount to challenging certainty itself — equivalent to challenging the Qurʾān.

## Epistemological Framework

The authority of ijmāʿ is not self-grounding. It is a *derivative* source: the Qurʾān and Sunna are material sources, while ijmāʿ and [[qiyas-analogy|qiyās]] are formal/derivative ones. This asymmetry determines a hierarchy: Qurʾān → Sunna → ijmāʿ → qiyās. A ruling derived by qiyās that subsequently gains ijmāʿ becomes certain; a ruling entrenched by ijmāʿ cannot be overturned by fresh qiyās.

## Twelver-Shīʿite Divergence

Sunnite ijmāʿ requires the agreement of all mujtahids. Twelver-Shīʿite theory modifies this fundamentally: consensus is authoritative only if it *includes the opinion of the (hidden) Imam*, since "it is his inclusion that guarantees its certitude, not the collective weight of the jurists" (al-Shahīd al-Thānī, cited by Hallaq, ch. 2, §9). For Akhbārists, who reject ijtihād altogether, only the infallible Imamic reports provide certainty, making juristic consensus irrelevant. The Uṣūlist camp ultimately prevailed in Shīʿite jurisprudence by the late eighteenth century.

## Historiography

Western scholarship long underestimated the practical role of ijmāʿ, viewing it as an abstract theological fiction. Hallaq argues against this: the function of ijmāʿ was not primarily to freeze law but to provide the schools with a tool for reducing the plurality of opinions (*ikhtilāf*) to a single authoritative ruling through *taṣḥīḥ* (validation), and thereby to allow the schools to exercise the legislative function that political sovereigns failed to perform. "The schools' strategy of taṣḥīḥ were heavily dependent on the tools of legal theory" (Hallaq, ch. 2, §1). The Mālikite insistence on Medinese consensus is treated by Hallaq not as provincial stubbornness but as a historically coherent extension of the pre-theoretical, practice-based consensus of the second/eighth century.
