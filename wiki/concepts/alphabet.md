# The Alphabet

The **alphabet** — a writing system in which a small set of signs each denote a single
sound — is one of the most consequential technologies in history, replacing the
hundreds of signs of logographic and syllabic scripts with a few dozen letters that
ordinary people could learn. Its two decisive stages were the **West Semitic
(Phoenician) consonantal alphabet** and its **Greek adaptation with vowels**, the
latter narrated in [[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] (Ch. 20: B. S. J.
Isserlin on the Semitic background, L. H. Jeffery on the Greek script).

## The Semitic Consonantal Alphabet

The alphabet was invented in the **Levant** in the second millennium BCE (its remote
ancestors the Proto-Sinaitic/Proto-Canaanite scripts), maturing into the **22-letter
West Semitic alphabet** used by the [[phoenicians|Phoenicians]], Aramaeans, and
Hebrews. It was **consonantal** — only consonants were written, vowels being supplied
by the reader (an *abjad*). Isserlin distinguishes three Iron Age Levantine
provinces — **Syria, Phoenicia, and Palestine** — whose scripts differ, and notes
that **Syria/Aramaic was apparently where vowel-letters (*matres lectionis*) were
first used**, a regional feature that may have prepared the way for the Greek
innovation. The script spread because it suited **commercial and administrative**
writing; the Greeks most likely learned it from everyday traders' hands rather than
formal monuments (their letters match a conservative formal ductus of straight
downstrokes, not the developed Semitic cursives).

## The Greek Innovation: Vowels

The Greeks "learnt the alphabet from Asia" — classical tradition called the letters
*phoinikeia* ("Phoenician things"), and the **shapes, names, and order** of the
letters confirm it. But they made a transforming change. Jeffery shows that all the
local Greek scripts share the same **systematic re-use of unneeded Semitic consonant-
signs as pure vowels**:
- the three Semitic letters *aleph, he, ʿayin* (consonants Greek did not need) became
  the vowels **α, ε, ο**;
- the Semitic *waw* was **split** into a semivowel (digamma, Ϝ) and a vowel (**Υ**,
  added at the end of the row);
- and a tell-tale **shared confusion of the four sibilant names** (zeta, xi, san,
  sigma) appears in every local script.

Because these same "errors" recur everywhere, Jeffery argues for a **single limited
area and moment of borrowing**, with the divergent local scripts arising as writing
spread along trade-routes — not independent inventions. The result was the **first
full alphabet** notating both consonants and vowels, the basis (via Etruscan and
Latin) of most of the world's alphabets.

## When and Where

- **Date.** The earliest Greek inscriptions belong, on archaeological grounds, to the
  **Geometric period, around 750 BCE**. Jeffery cautions against positing a ninth-
  century or earlier Greek alphabet "on purely general grounds," more than a generation
  or two before the surviving examples. (The Semitic letter-forms borrowed match ninth–
  eighth-century inscriptions.)
- **Place.** Candidates cluster around the documented points of Greek–Levantine
  contact: **[[euboea|Al Mina and Euboea]]** (the Euboean trading post in Syria, and
  the island that has produced the earliest datable Greek inscriptions); **Crete,
  Rhodes, Cyprus**; and the **north-Syrian / Aramaic** zone (whence [[phrygians|Phrygia]]
  too apparently drew its alphabet). The **Euboean orientalizing trade** is the leading
  context.

## Significance

- `produced:` mass-accessible literacy — a script learnable in days, not years,
  loosening the scribal monopoly of the old palace and temple bureaucracies.
- `enabled:` the recording of [[homer|Homeric]] epic, early Greek law, and the
  literate culture of the [[archaic-period|Archaic]] and Classical
  [[city-state|polis]]; the **Ionic** version became standard (officially in Athens by
  403/2 BCE).
- `caused_by:` the renewed Greek contact with the [[phoenicians|Phoenician]] / Levantine
  world in the eighth century — the same [[euboea|Euboean]]-led orientalizing
  expansion that brought eastern art and goods into Greece.
- `contrasts_with:` the lost Bronze Age [[aegean-scripts|Linear B syllabary]], whose
  disappearance left Greece illiterate for centuries (only Cyprus kept a syllabary).

## Historiography

The transfer is dated and located by **comparative letter-forms** (Greek vs. dated
Semitic inscriptions) and by the **archaeology of the earliest Greek graffiti** — both
imprecise, so the date (eighth century vs. a possible earlier "experimental" phase) and
the place remain debated. Jeffery's "single area, then trade-route divergence" model is
the classic synthesis; some scholars argue for multiple points of adoption. The find of
ever-earlier datable inscriptions could lower the date. This is an area where the
collection (with Jeffery's own *Local Scripts of Archaic Greece* behind the chapter) is
strong.
