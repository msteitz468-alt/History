# Adaptive Behavior (in Archaeology)

**Adaptive behaviour** is the analytical framework that treats material culture and social organization as the means by which human groups maintain themselves and reproduce in specific environmental and social conditions. Change is explained as adjustment to altered circumstances rather than as the automatic product of increasing intelligence or ethnic replacement.

## Definition and Rationale

In the study of the Palaeolithic, "what we are studying is adaptive behaviour. Material culture is the means by which people adapt to their environment — the latter includes both the physical environment, from which food and other resources are obtained, and the social environment, which ensures biological reproduction." Societies aim to fulfil "two goals: the maintenance of the present population and its continued existence into another generation." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.2 / range lines ~1200-1215)

Lower Palaeolithic societies were "as successful in attaining these goals as those in the upper palaeolithic." Simple technology was "both sufficient and efficient" under the conditions of the time. (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.2 / range lines ~1215-1225)

## Relation to Earlier Frameworks

This view replaces two older explanatory models:
- Increasing intelligence as the driver of cultural evolution (biological determinism).
- Social traditions / ethnic groups as the source of patterned variation (Childean culture-history).

"These two concepts—increasing intelligence and social traditions—do not, however, provide an adequate explanation of either changes or differences amongst palaeolithic data." Ethnographic examples (e.g., Australian Aboriginal seasonal toolkits) show that the same group can produce archaeologically distinct assemblages without constituting "two distinct peoples." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.2 / range lines ~1180-1200)

Instead, "the explanation of change would not... be dependent upon a single factor such as intelligence but rather the result of the interplay between many variables, among which environment, subsistence, population pressure on resources, biological development and social organization would all be relevant." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.2 / range lines ~1225-1235)

## Application in the Source

Champion et al. use adaptive behaviour as the unifying lens for the entire volume: settlement patterns, subsistence scheduling, exchange, and social organization are all interpreted as responses that allow populations to persist and reproduce. "As Kent Flannery has described it, we are not only looking for the Indian behind the artifact but also for the system behind the Indian." (Champion et al. 1984, Ch.2 / range lines ~1240-1255)

## Collection Note

All interpretations of European Palaeolithic and Mesolithic material in the wiki must be framed in terms of adaptive success or failure rather than unexamined assumptions of "progress" or ethnic succession. This concept page supplies the explicit methodological grounding from the post-radiocarbon European textbook tradition.
