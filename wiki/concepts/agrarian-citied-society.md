# Agrarian-Citied Society (Agrarianate)

A conceptual category developed by Marshall Hodgson to describe the dominant form of human social organization from roughly the invention of writing (~3000 BCE) through the late eighteenth century CE — the entire era commonly called "civilized history" prior to industrial modernity. Hodgson prefers the term **agrarianate** to competing terms like "traditional," "pre-modern," or "medieval" because those alternatives carry Eurocentric periodization baggage or misleadingly imply stasis.

## Definition and Scope

"Citied society" (Hodgson's preferred phrase for civilized life) means a society **containing cities** — marked by urban centers, craft specialization, literacy, and long-distance trade — as distinct from "urban" which refers only to cities themselves. The "agrarian" element specifies that the economic surplus sustaining citied life flows primarily from agricultural land — peasant farming, tribute extraction, land revenues — rather than from industrial production. Hence "agrarian-citied": urban in cultural form, agrarian in economic base.

The **agrarianate** level of social organization is explicitly distinct from:
- **Pre-agricultural** (foraging, pastoral nomadic) societies — earlier in structural terms, though often contemporary
- **Technical Age** (technicalistic/industrial) society — the post-1789 era in which calculatively rationalized technical production becomes dominant

Hodgson articulates this contrast sharply: the single antithesis "traditional-modern" "not only oversimplifies historically what is chosen as a contrast to 'Modern' but underplays the dynamic nature of what is commonly thought of as tradition, and it definitely ignores the active role of 'tradition' in the most Modern society."

## The Oikoumene

Hodgson uses the term **Oikoumene** (borrowed from Alfred Kroeber but modified) to name the Afro-Eurasian historical complex of agrarian-citied societies — "the Afro-Eurasian agrarian historical complex as having a distinctive interregional articulation in an ever growing area." This is not just a geographical designation but a recognition that the agrarian-citied societies of Eurasia and North Africa were in sustained contact and mutual influence, forming a zone whose internal dynamics differed decisively from the more isolated developments of the Americas, sub-Saharan Africa, and Oceania.

## The Agrarianate Age: Pre-Axial, Axial, Post-Axial

Hodgson subdivides the agrarianate era into three phases defined by the **Axial Age** framework:
- **Pre-Axial**: before the great philosophical and religious transformations of roughly 800–200 BCE
- **Axial**: the period of the great prophets, philosophers, and lawgivers (Hebrew prophets, Greek philosophy, Zoroastrianism, Buddhism, Confucianism)
- **Post-Axial**: the long period of the confessional faiths and high civilizations, from roughly 200 BCE to 1789 CE

This three-part division allows Hodgson to avoid the misleading question of whether to call the post-1500 world "Medieval" or "Early Modern" — from a world-historical perspective, it is still post-Axial agrarianate. Only in the Occident does the 1500–1789 period initiate modernity; globally, it does not.

## Mercantile vs. Agrarian Bases for High Culture

A key analytical distinction within agrarianate societies is whether high culture (the arts, sciences, religion, philosophy) is supported primarily by land revenues and agrarian aristocracies, or primarily by mercantile wealth and urban commercial networks. Hodgson argues this distinction tracks the difference between the more agrarian-anchored civilizations of medieval Europe and China and the [[irano-semitic-tradition|Irano-Semitic tradition]], in which high culture increasingly shifted toward a mercantile base:

> "The Irano-Semitic cultural traditions showed a long-term tendency, within what remained overall an agrarian-based cultural context, to shift from a more agrarian base for high culture toward a more mercantile base for it."

This shift does not abolish the agrarianate structure — land revenues remain the primary economic base — but it determines which social class sets the cultural tone and which values dominate high culture. Islamicate civilization, in Hodgson's reading, represents the most fully realized mercantile-inflected agrarianate high culture.

## Why "Agrarianate" Rather Than "Traditional" or "Pre-Modern"

Hodgson objects to the vocabulary of "traditional" for several reasons:
1. It implies stasis, obscuring the dynamic, creative nature of pre-modern societies ("Too readily an answer to the question of why 'reform' efforts so often failed: the 'tradition-bound' lands were ruled by blind conservatives. Some are thus spared the trouble of discovering what very practical and alert statesmen those 'tradition-bound' men often were.")
2. It treats everything non-modern as a single undifferentiated category, obscuring crucial differences between, say, post-Axial agrarianate and pre-agricultural societies
3. In Islamic contexts specifically, "traditional" confusingly merges "hadith-minded textualism" (which was often anti-customary and innovative) with genuine folk tradition

The term "agrarianate" is **Hodgson's neologism** and has not gained wide independent use outside his work, though the conceptual distinction it marks — between agrarian-anchored civilization and technical-industrial civilization — is widely made using other vocabulary (e.g., "pre-modern," "pre-industrial," "the Agrarian Age").

## Historiography

Hodgson's insistence on "agrarianate" reflects his broader project of constructing a genuinely world-historical framework not distorted by Eurocentrism. The conventional "Ancient / Medieval / Modern" periodization encodes a European narrative; "agrarianate" is intended to apply uniformly across Eurasia and Africa. His critique of Max Weber is relevant here: Weber's contrast between "rational" Occident and other societies depended on not knowing those other societies well enough, and on mistaking "certain sorts of formalism for rationality." A properly agrarianate-level analysis, Hodgson argues, would have dissolved many of Weber's apparent contrasts.

The concept also frames the stakes of the "why Europe first?" question about industrialization: the question is about why the Transmutation (Hodgson's term for modernity) happened where and when it did within the agrarianate world, not about some essential European trait versus an essentially static non-European tradition.
