# The Achaemenid Satrapy and the Powers of Satraps

## Definition

A **satrapy** was a province of the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid Persian empire]]
governed by a **satrap** (Old Persian *xšaçapāvan*, "protector of the realm"). The
office is usually described as the empire's administrative backbone — but
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 3, Simon Hornblower) argues that to understand how
Persia held together for two centuries, the satrapy must be seen **not as a bureaucratic
post but as a quasi-feudal relationship**.

## The Greek picture: institutional controls

Greek theoretical writers — above all **[[xenophon]]** in the *Cyropaedia* and
*Oeconomicus*, and **Isocrates** — imagined satraps held in check by a dense system of
**institutional controls**: a standing royal army, deliberately **divided commands** to
encourage spying, royal **garrisons** answerable only to the King, touring **inspectors**
with police powers, royal **scribes** at satrapal courts, and the famous "**King's Eye**"
and "**King's Ear**." Hornblower notes that of these last two, only the *Ear* (Aramaic
*guskaye*, "listeners") finds even doubtful corroboration in the oriental evidence. The
whole apparatus, he warns, is largely a **Greek construct**, shaped by Greeks' own
experience of archaic tyranny and of [[western-greek-tyranny|Dionysius I]] — the instinct
to "associate one-man rule with close and oppressive control."

## The reality: a feudal relationship

Starting instead "**with attested satrapal actions and areas of inaction**," Hornblower
concludes that "**the relationship which emerges is a feudal one**" — allowing **much
satrapal freedom of action in return for military service**, and resting "ultimately not
on formal controls but on **loyalty to a system of allegiance, protection, and territorial
and other kinds of gift-giving**" that was foreign to Greek civic categories. The Greek
habit of describing Persian subjects (and even satraps, addressed as the King's *doulos*
or *bandaka*) as "slaves" mistranslates oriental terms for dependent status that ranged
from household servant to high officer; **Evagoras of Cyprus** could insist on being
"subject as king to king," a nuance the Persians readily granted.

## Why it matters: explaining Persian durability

This reframing carries the chapter's central thesis. The **loyalty of the western satraps
at the Granicus (334)** — fighting Alexander "extempore" in what Arrian called a "satraps'
battle" — is "striking and important": "by explaining it we shall have explained the
secret of Persian success over so long a period." The fiercest anti-Macedonian resistance
came from places with "a long tradition of clientship to Persia" (Halicarnassus, Tyre,
Gaza), who "had nothing to gain from seeing Persia overthrown." The lesson is that the
fall of the empire is "to be laid at the door of **Darius III personally**," not at the
structure of the satrapal system. A little **flexibility at the margins** — even the
satraps' revolts — "can be seen as a sign of Persian strength, not weakness" (Hornblower
borrows Paul Veyne's needling question about feudalism: "'Weak and far-off power.' What
power is not?").

## Limitations and critique

The reconstruction is necessarily built "out of the often Greek evidence we happen to
have," and Hornblower concedes the **poverty of inside sources**: the Persepolis
Fortification Tablets relate to the fifth century, and there is no surviving Persian
narrative. The "feudal" analogy is itself a borrowed Western category to be used with the
same caution Hornblower urges against the Greek "slave" vocabulary. The page therefore
pairs with the standing rehabilitation of Persia in [[achaemenid-empire]] and the
[[fourth-century-greek-historiography|source-critical method]] of the volume.

## Briant's account

[[sources/briant-from-cyrus-to-alexander-2002|Briant]] supplies the administrative substance the
Greek-derived picture lacks. Satraps held authority over both civil and military domains but were
checked by **garrison commanders appointed by and answerable to the king**, by royal inspectors
(*hoi ephodoi*) making annual circuits, and by **family members kept at court as implicit hostages**.
A satrap was a [[achaemenid-kings-men|*bandaka*]] bound by the gift-and-service relationship; royal
letters carried near-royal authority, received with *proskynesis*. Satrapal courts **replicated the
royal court in every detail** — audience ritual, banquet, paradises, the education of provincial
nobles' sons at the satrap's "Gate" — diffusing court culture across the empire. On the
[[achaemenid-tribute-economy|fiscal]] side, the satrap was responsible to the king for delivering his
district's tribute but delegated internal apportionment to local kings, dynasts, and cities,
intervening only when they evaded; assessment rested on cadastres and sealed land-registers
(*basilikai graphai* at Sardis). Briant stresses that the fourth century saw **more** territorialization,
not less: Caria, Cilicia, and Lycia were "satrapized" more thoroughly than before (the Xanthus
trilingual shows Lycia folded into Caria), and Artaxerxes III installed the first Persian satrap of
Caria, Orontobates — evidence of tightening, not loosening, control.
