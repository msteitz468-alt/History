# High Medieval Armies (1000–1300)

Analytical concept describing the characteristic form, composition, size, recruitment, organisation, and sustainment of Western European armies in the High Middle Ages. Armies were ad hoc, retinue-based collections shaped by landowning society; they lacked permanent infrastructure, exhibited extreme size fluctuation, and were dominated by a core of the commander's personal troops. This page is grounded exclusively in John France's analysis.

## Definition and Scope

High medieval armies (c. 1000–1300) were occasional bodies without continuous existence. "The face of war changed across the period 1000–1300. By the latter date, armies usually had more and better iron weapons. Many had siege-trains with specialized equipment and an apparatus of supply. But armies remained ad hoc bodies, whose structures were dominated by the gradations of landowning society and its preoccupations. It was always difficult to sustain them in the field and even the greatest of forces could melt away." (Ch. 10)

**Emic perspective**: Contemporaries viewed armies as the following of a lord or king summoned for a specific campaign or feud; service was a tenurial or political obligation or voluntary act for reward/influence. Terms like *hostis*, *exercitus*, *batailles* referred to the assembled force rather than a standing institution.

**Etic perspective**: France's analysis emphasises that these were not proto-modern armies. Their structure reflected proprietorial imperatives: land as wealth, limited government, and the absence of state capacity to maintain forces in being. Professionalism grew at the margins but "made little impact on command".

## Composition and Sizes

Armies varied enormously even for the same leader. A "great event such as the Conquest of England in 1066" could produce exceptional mobilisation: "in 1066, the Normans mobilized 14,000, amongst them 6,000–7,000 fighting men." Robert Guiscard gathered 15,000 for the 1081–5 Byzantine campaign.

Imperial efforts under Frederick Barbarossa illustrate limits: claims of 15,000 knights at Milan 1158 are exaggerated; realistic estimates ~15,000 total in peak circumstances. After fall of Crema the army "went home", leaving only ~400 cavalry and 2,500–3,000 foot at Carcano (1160); reinforced to ~2,100 German knights for the 1161 Milan siege. Legnano (1176) ~3,000–4,000 knights. "Overall, the campaigns of Frederick Barbarossa suggest that in very favourable circumstances a major effort could raise 4,000–5,000 cavalry and about double that number of infantry, but that most of the time he had to rely on much smaller forces."

Angevin forces small: Henry II "operated with about 600 mercenary foot plus cavalry"; 5,000 strong in 1173–4 rebellions. "At Bouvines, there were less than 20,000 men in all."

Edward I could raise large numbers sequentially (15,500 max Wales 1277; ~20,000 1282–3; 31,000 1294–5; 26,000 Falkirk 1298) but "these are overall numbers: they were not raised at one time and were never deployed in one place." Frederick II Cortenova (1237) core of ~2,000 German knights + allies + 7,000–10,000 Saracen archers; total force perhaps over 20,000 but never fully concentrated. Charles of Anjou 1265 ~4,000 cavalry + 10,000–12,000 infantry (after adjusting inflated claims).

**Key point**: "The fluctuations in the size of armies, even those gathered by the same leader, have fairly obvious explanations. Commanders tailored their effort to the demands of the situation that they faced."

## Recruitment and the Social Basis

"A king or great lord could levy as many troops as he could afford from his demesne, and these formed the core of his army." More important vassals owed service "but the basis of this was not always clear, and the German nobles resisted any fixing of quotas under Barbarossa. Even where service was admitted in principle, in practice norms were not fixed."

English barons saw service "in a sense, freely given in return for political influence. To accept money was to accept subordination". Roger Bigod example: accepted pay in 1297–8 under necessity but served at own cost 1298 "as a demonstration of his independence".

In England/France royal wealth enabled hiring: scutage as tax; Philip Augustus 1202–3 maintained "a standing force of 250 knights and about ten times that number of archers, crossbowmen and foot". Edward I tried wholly paid force for 1282 Welsh war. "formal contractual relationships for the provision of troops only began to appear in England and France at the very end of the period."

Italian cities relied heavily on mercenaries (foreign knights, condotte companies) due to continuous war and small contadi; "the distinction between Italy and northern Europe was not, however, truly very great, because fighting for money was everywhere a norm."

Mass infantry from communal obligation or array (Statute of Winchester 1285 England; French constabularies 50–150 men; Flemish urban forces). "These mass infantry contingents were a European-wide phenomenon." But "not an unmixed blessing" (desertion, impediment).

Cavalry drawn from upper ranks; "very large numbers must have served at some time in their lives". Light forces and mixed quality common.

## Organisation and Cohesion

"the retinues of kings and nobles had to be incorporated within these great divisions." Armies ordered into *acies* (battle-lines or "battles"/"batailles"/schiera). Within: knights in *conroi* "much smaller units of between 10 and 40".

"In English armies of the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries, the knight–banneret seems to have been a minor commander with a force upwards of 13 knights, and was usually distinguished by carrying a square banner rather than a pennant."

"These were not standardized units like modern sections, platoons and companies, and 'battles' varied enormously in size, being tactical units rather than organizational entities." "even within the smallest units there was little continuity of service in most cases." "considerations of rank disrupted these units as elements in a command structure."

Cohesion rare and circumstantial: William's Dives sojourn; First Crusade march; Acre under Richard; Philip Augustus 1202–3; Charles of Anjou's French settler nucleus in Italy. "there was no linear progression, no gradual and sustained improvement of armies, and in this respect those of the thirteenth were very little different from those of the twelfth century."

Core always the commander's household troops: "For the most part, the largely ad hoc armies of this period were rather like onions, in that there was always a core of loyal and fairly dependable troops around which the force was built. ... But the heart of a medieval army was the commander’s own troops and they were always its most crucial element." (Ch. 10)

## Logistics, Sustainment, and Attrition

"Maintaining armies also remained a difficult business." Edward I paid attention to supply "but his armies in Wales and Scotland sometimes suffered from desperate shortages of food." "Hardship was indeed a norm of war, and the rigours of the weather were especially severe in northern climes."

Disease "a terrible scourge": Barbarossa's army "virtually destroyed by disease" at Rome 1167; plague after Las Navas 1212. Wounds described in graphic detail by Joinville at Mansourah; "The wounded had to care for themselves or perish".

Treatment of prisoners: infantry routinely slaughtered ("little alternative"; "worthless in the literal sense of the word" as no economic slavery in west); knights ransomed for profit. Ransom "so profitable that it could be regulated and controlled only by the strongest of commanders."

## Historiography and Limitations

The ad hoc character explains slow evolution despite growing numbers and pay: "the persistent association of rank, political power and military authority meant that there could be no clear chain of command. Medieval armies were not monolithic. ... they depended on the personal valour of those who could afford to equip and train themselves. They belonged to a privileged caste whose habit was not to submerge themselves in the anonymity of mass... This whole ethos was bound up with the nobility’s sense of social position and identity as a ruling elite. In these circumstances, armies changed only slowly."

**Emic/etic note**: Contemporaries did not articulate a unified "army doctrine"; the structure was experienced as the natural expression of lordship and status. The modern analytical category highlights the contrast with later bureaucratic/contract armies and the constraints this imposed on strategy, command, and technological adoption.

Limitations of the model: Italy and the Latin East showed variations (more explicit mercenarism; greater siege infrastructure in some crusader contexts); ideological mobilisation (crusades) could temporarily override ad hoc limits; individual personality and circumstance mattered enormously.
