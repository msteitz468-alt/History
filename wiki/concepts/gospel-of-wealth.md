# Gospel of Wealth

Ideological framework articulated most famously by Andrew Carnegie in his 1889 essay "Wealth" (often called the "Gospel of Wealth"). The core argument: in an industrial society the concentration of wealth in the hands of a few is inevitable and on balance beneficial because it enables the accumulation of capital for productive enterprise. The rich, however, have a duty to administer their surplus wealth during their own lifetimes for the public good rather than pass it intact to heirs or leave it to be dissipated by the state.

Key principles (from Carnegie):
- The millionaire should live modestly and without extravagance.
- Surplus wealth should not be given as indiscriminate charity (which Carnegie viewed as pauperizing).
- It should be used to create institutions that help "those who will help themselves" — libraries, universities, hospitals, parks, organs for churches, research institutions, etc.
- The best philanthropy is that which attacks root causes and provides ladders of opportunity (education, knowledge, health).
- "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced."

The essay was widely reprinted and debated. John D. Rockefeller and others adopted similar language of stewardship, though Rockefeller's practice (via Frederick T. Gates) emphasized "scientific" wholesale giving through permanent foundations even more systematically than Carnegie.

## Historiography and Critique

Critics (contemporary and later) charged hypocrisy: Carnegie preached the gospel while his mills imposed twelve-hour days, broke unions at Homestead, and accumulated the fortune through practices many regarded as exploitative. Defenders note that Carnegie had long held redistributionist views (influenced by his radical Scottish background and readings of Spencer et al.) and that the philosophy was sincere even if self-justifying. The "Gospel" supplied the intellectual and moral justification for the modern philanthropic foundation and remains a touchstone in debates over wealth, inequality, and private vs. public responsibility for the common good.
