# Cairo Geniza

A **geniza** is a storeroom where Jews deposit worn-out writings bearing the name of God,
which may not be destroyed. The geniza of the **Ben Ezra Synagogue in Fustat (Old
Cairo)** preserved, instead of discarding, an extraordinary mass of everyday documents —
letters, contracts, court records, accounts, marriage deeds, and communal correspondence
— accumulated across roughly the tenth to nineteenth centuries. Because Jews in the
Islamic world wrote their vernacular Arabic in Hebrew characters (Judaeo-Arabic), and
because the pious reluctance to destroy writing extended to secular paper, the Geniza
became a near-continuous archive of ordinary life.

## Significance as a Source

Dispersed to Cambridge and other Western libraries from the 1890s, the Geniza was
transformed into a historical source above all by **S. D. Goitein**, whose multi-volume
*A Mediterranean Society* reconstructed the economic, family, and communal life of the
medieval Islamic Mediterranean from its documents. It is the single richest window onto:

- the **commercial world** of the [[jewry-islamic-world|Jews of the Islamic world]] — the
  India trade, partnerships (*commenda*), letters of credit (*suftaja*), and the "law of
  the merchants" shared by Jewish and Muslim traders;
- the **lived practice of [[dhimmi|dhimmī]] status** — showing, for instance, that the
  Pact of ʿUmar's dress rules were only sporadically enforced and that Jews, Christians,
  and Muslims often shared residential compounds;
- family life, marriage, charity, education, and communal governance.

## Historiographical Role

The Geniza is central to the wiki's coverage of medieval Jewish and Islamic social
history. It is the evidentiary backbone of [[cohen-under-crescent-cross-1994|Mark
Cohen's]] comparison of Jews under Islam and Christendom (Cohen is himself an editor of
the Geniza documentary corpus), grounding claims about economic embeddedness and the
predictability of dhimma protection that no narrative chronicle could supply. Its
limitation is scope: it richly documents the Egyptian and Mediterranean Jewish world of
the classical centuries, and comparatively little of Latin Christendom — one reason the
Islamic side of Cohen's comparison rests on firmer documentary ground than the Christian
side.
