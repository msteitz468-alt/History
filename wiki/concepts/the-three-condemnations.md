## Definition and Scope

"The Three Condemnations" refers to the formal verdict (*ḥukm*) delivered by [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]] in the concluding section ("The Book's Conclusion") of the [[ghazali-incoherence-of-philosophers|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]. Having devoted twenty discussions to refuting the philosophers' arguments across metaphysics, natural science, and psychology, al-Ghazālī concludes by specifying which of their doctrines constitute *kufr* (unbelief, apostasy) — thereby placing their holders outside the pale of Islam and, in principle, subject to the legal consequences attached to apostasy in classical Islamic jurisprudence.

The verdict is historically decisive. It is not simply a theological opinion: it is a formal takfīr declaration against a named intellectual tradition (the falāsifa, principally al-Fārābī and Avicenna), delivered by the most authoritative Islamic scholar of his generation at the height of his influence. The Tahāfut's claim to fame in the history of Islamic intellectual life rests substantially on this conclusion, which shaped the reception of philosophy in Sunni Islam for centuries.

## The Three Doctrines Condemned as Unbelief

Al-Ghazālī's Conclusion states (§§2–3):

> "Pronouncing them infidels is necessary in three questions. One of them is the question of the world's pre-eternity and their statement that all substances are pre-eternal. The second is their statement that God's knowledge does not encompass the temporal particulars among individual [existents]. The third is their denial of the resurrection of bodies and their assembly at the day of judgment."

### First Condemnation: Pre-Eternity of the World

The philosophers hold that the world is eternal — without beginning or end — proceeding from God necessarily, as an effect from its cause, through a hierarchy of emanated intellects and spheres. Al-Ghazālī contested this through Discussions 1–5, arguing that the world was temporally created by a free divine will and that world-eternity makes divine will unintelligible.

The theological ground for condemnation: this doctrine implies that God did not create the world *ex nihilo* at a beginning point. It contradicts explicit Quranic statements about God's creation and implicitly denies the sovereignty of the divine will. Moreover, if all substances are pre-eternal, then God's act of creation becomes meaningless — "the world would not have originated through His will" (Discussion 11). This is not a peripheral interpretive question; it strikes at the foundation of Islamic theism.

Reference: [[eternity-of-the-world]]

### Second Condemnation: God's Knowledge of Particulars

The philosophers hold that God knows only universals — general essences and species — not the particular, individual, temporal things that occur in the world (this man, this event, at this time). This follows from their doctrine that God knows only through self-knowledge and that knowledge of changing particulars would require change in the knower, but God is immutable.

Al-Ghazālī contested this in Discussions 11–13, arguing that the doctrine makes God ignorant of the created world and contradicts the Quranic teaching that "not the weight of an atom escapes your Lord in the heavens or on earth" (10:61). If God does not know particulars, He cannot know whether a specific person prays or murders, which makes divine judgment (the Day of Reckoning) incoherent.

Reference: [[god-knowledge-of-particulars]]

### Third Condemnation: Denial of Bodily Resurrection

The philosophers hold that the resurrection is a spiritual event — the soul's union with the intelligible world — and that Quranic descriptions of physical paradise, fire, houris, and bodily pleasures are parables for the unsophisticated. Al-Ghazālī contested this in Discussion 20, the Tahāfut's longest and most detailed chapter.

The ground for condemnation: this denial "does not agree with Islam in any respect. The one who believes them believes that prophets utter falsehoods and that they said whatever they have said by way of [promoting common] utility, to give examples and explanation to the multitudes." This is not allegoresis of a difficult verse; it is the attribution of deliberate deception to the Prophet — "manifest infidelity which none of the Islamic sects have believed."

## The Scope of the Verdict

Al-Ghazālī is careful to limit the takfīr to these three specific doctrines. His Conclusion explicitly states (§4):

> "As regards questions other than these three, such as their treatment of the divine attributes and their belief in divine unity entailed therein, their doctrine is close to that of the Muʿtazila. Their doctrine of the necessary [connection] of natural causes [and their effects] is the one which the Muʿtazila have explicitly expressed in [their doctrine of] generation. Similarly, all of what we have reported [of their doctrines] has been held by one or another of the Islamic sects, with the exception of these three principles."

This is a significant qualification. The philosophers' occasionalist-adjacent causal doctrines, their positions on divine attributes, their logical and mathematical work — none of this is condemned as unbelief. The Muʿtazila held parallel positions on causality and have always been recognized as Muslims, however heterodox. The condemnation is surgical: only what directly denies world-creation, divine omniscience, and bodily resurrection falls under kufr.

Al-Ghazālī further declines to rule on whether heretical innovators (*mubtadiʿa*) within Islam should also be declared unbelievers for overlapping doctrines: "We, however, prefer not to plunge into [the questions] of pronouncing those who uphold heretical innovation to be infidels . . . lest the discourse should stray from the objective of this book."

## Historical Significance and Reception

The Three Condemnations are the reason the Tahāfut became a landmark rather than simply a philosophical polemic. The work had already demonstrated al-Ghazālī's thorough command of Avicennan philosophy; the Conclusion deployed that mastery in service of a formal legal-theological judgment.

**Immediate impact**: The Tahāfut contributed to the effective marginalization of the Avicennan falsafa tradition in the eastern Islamic world (Khurasan, Iraq, Persia) after the 11th century. The synthesis of Avicennan philosophy with Islamic theology would continue, but increasingly under [[asharism|Ashʿarite]] constraints and in the Illuminationist (*ishrāqī*) tradition associated with al-Suhrawardī, which reframed the philosophical project in ways that avoided the condemned doctrines.

**Averroes' counter**: The Andalusian philosopher [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] (Ibn Rushd, 1126–1198) wrote the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* (Incoherence of the Incoherence) specifically to refute al-Ghazālī's arguments and defend the philosophical tradition. The exchange became the defining controversy of medieval Islamic philosophy and was transmitted to Latin scholasticism through the Averroist tradition.

**Later elaboration**: Al-Ghazālī himself returned to the question of takfīr in his later work *Fayṣal al-Tafriqa* (The Criterion of Distinction), where he offered a more systematic account of when allegorical interpretation of revealed texts constitutes heresy versus legitimate interpretation. The Conclusion of the Tahāfut anticipates this later work's concerns.

**Historiographical controversy**: Modern scholars disagree about whether al-Ghazālī's polemical conclusion is consistent with his later philosophical writings. The *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn* and the mystical works appear to draw on Avicennan psychology and cosmology in ways that seem in tension with the Tahāfut's condemnations. Marmura argues the condemnations are repeated in works written after the *Iḥyāʾ* (including the *Fayṣal* and the *Munqidh min al-Ḍalāl*), suggesting al-Ghazālī saw no inconsistency — he could use philosophical concepts while condemning specific metaphysical doctrines as incompatible with Islam.

## The Logic of the Verdict

The three condemned doctrines share a common structure: each directly contradicts what al-Ghazālī understands as *necessary Islamic belief* — belief without which the prophet's message becomes incoherent or the prophet himself a deceiver. This is the test he applies:

- World-eternity → the prophets' proclamation of divine creation is false
- God's ignorance of particulars → the prophets' proclamation of divine judgment is false  
- Denial of bodily resurrection → the prophets' detailed descriptions of the afterlife are deliberate lies to manipulate the masses

In each case the conclusion is not mere heterodoxy but the attribution of deception to the prophetic office. This is why these three, and not others, cross the line from innovation (*bidʿa*) to unbelief (*kufr*).
