# Durability and Docility of the Roman Empire

Harris's label for a **double puzzle** of the high Roman Empire (especially first–early fourth centuries AD): how a system of harsh, exploitative power — slavery, steep hierarchy, frequent dynastic instability — could remain **durable** (the imperial structure survived coups, plague, and the third-century crisis) and relatively **docile** (large-scale rebellion by slaves and free subjects was less frequent or less successful than the severity of domination might predict). Developed in [[harris-roman-power-2016|Harris, *Roman Power*]] Chapter 5 ("from empire to nation?").

## The Puzzle

- Emperors and dynasties were often short-lived, yet the **central political structure** persisted.
- Slavery and class oppression were pervasive (Harris opens with the Pedanius Secundus massacre of 400 household slaves under Nero, Tacitus *Annals* 14.42–5), yet systemic slave war after the late Republic was rare.
- Provincial elites were co-opted; many modest provincials served in the army; economic interests under the [[pax-romana|peace]] aligned substantial groups with the status quo.
- Alongside co-optation, Harris stresses a **diffused loyalty to the idea of Rome** — from bare acceptance of power to belief in eternity and near-universality of Roman rule — strongest among the growing body of citizens.

## Limitations

"Docility" is a loaded framing (implies subjects *should* have rebelled more); other historians stress fragmented resistance, everyday non-compliance, banditry, and local violence poorly captured in elite sources. Harris himself notes many subjects were alienated and inclined to rebellion. Treat as a **problem-statement**, not a finding that the empire was consensual.

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016) — mechanisms of durability

**Framing (Ch. 5).** “What in fact needs to be explained is a double mystery: the durability of the Roman Empire and its high degree of docility; and both… seem all the more puzzling when we consider that by the standards of other monarchies both Roman emperors and Roman dynasties were relatively short lived.” Opening vignette: AD 61 murder of city prefect L. Pedanius Secundus by a slave; law and custom required execution of all slaves “under the same roof” — **~400 persons**; popular protest, senatorial majority approval, Nero’s soldiers enforce (Tacitus, *Annals* 14.42–5); the law lived on (*Digest* 29.5). Slavery is “the water in which everything else floats,” conditioning economic life and psychology of virtually the whole population; collective slave rebellion remained rare because slaves were too weak.

**Holding together.** In major divisive crises (Second Triumvirate; AD 69; 193–6; 235–284) the empire always held: new rulers and armies, “imbued with a deep sense of the majesty of the Roman Empire as well as of their own interests, almost always took it for granted that there should be a single central government ruling in the name of Rome.” Provincial rebellions always repressed (SW Germany and Dacia abandoned under *external* pressure, not indigenous rebellion). Mystery of light garrisons: Josephus notes Romans control Gaul with **1,200 soldiers** despite many cities; vast tracts (Gaul, much of Spain/N Africa, Asia Minor, Greece, Dalmatia, Alps) were “very lightly garrisoned most of the time.” Tax compliance is not fully explained by fear; Harris rejects the “current myth” of a general low-tax regime — burden varied, often oppressive.

**Consensus doctrine.** Main explanation: assimilation of significant local populations especially elites; patronage linking centre to Italy/provinces; capital “bread and circuses,” provinces left games/food to local leaders; local elites got imperial backing and *honestiores* status while ordinary people became *humiliores* under the Antonines; army faced outsiders, administered immature provinces, recruited non-citizens and made them Roman soldiers. Doctrine is “broadly convincing” but needs more historical and sociological depth. Bedrock: **property-owner alliance** — Aelius Aristides (*To Rome* 64): no garrisons needed for acropolises in unarmed provinces because “the greatest and most powerful men in every city guard their own fatherlands for you.” Josephus on Judaea 66: men of property wanted peace. Since Italian conquest Rome favoured landowners with political privileges; oligarchic city government by Flavian times.

**Emperor insecurity vs structural survival.** Scheidel: Roman imperial reigns shortest among 31 monarchies compared; of 72 Augusti 14–337, **50 died from Roman military actions**. Yet Ch. 8: durability depended on co-opting large proportions of provincial elites and enough modest provincials for the army, because empire served economic interests of great numbers while keeping peace, **plus** “widely diffused loyalty to the idea of Rome” — strongest among the growing citizen minority. Factors carried the empire through the third-century crisis; many other subjects remained alienated. Ch. 4 close: by 337 frontiers always vulnerable when imperial power was not intimidating, “but the empire itself was not vulnerable – not yet”; manpower and money still sufficed for the bulk of territory; army under Diocletian still large, reasonably equipped and trained.

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012) — complementary mechanisms

Woolf does not use Harris's "durability and docility" label, but supplies structural answers that Stage-2 integration should **juxtapose**, not erase:

- **Elite class solidarity from the Italian phase:** Roman hegemony "always stressed class solidarity among the elites. The seeds of an aristocratic empire had been sown"; for propertied allies, rule could seem "more like a movement that benefited those drawn into it" ([[romanization-of-italy]], [[roman-expansion-italy]]).
- **Conquest-state → tributary empire:** Ideal types coexisted from the second century BC, but Rome moved toward sustainable taxation; booty remained (including Trajan's Dacian funding for Rome's buildings), yet "the long-term trend was towards the sustainable income that only taxation could provide." Stopping expansion and fiscal consolidation "really saved" the empire more than subject psychology alone ([[roman-imperial-taxation-and-fiscal-system]], [[pax-romana]]).
- **Provincial preference for monarchy:** Woolf stresses that many provincials preferred the principate to late-Republican predation — a political-economy "welcome" rather than pure docility ([[principate]], [[roman-power]]).
- **Late weakening of symbioses:** From the third century each successive imperial version was less successful as ties to classical city and propertied classes weakened; Christianity was "a less effective imperial ideology than had been the traditional state cults" (schism; episcopal independence).

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

Attributed claims from [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]] (Ch. I army chapters); source of the concept’s Gibbonian vocabulary.

- **Docility thesis.** As freedom was lost in extent of conquest, war became art and trade; mercenary servants of a despotic prince needed honor and religion; the good soldier should dread officers far more than the enemy; valor gained “a degree of firmness and docility unattainable by the impetuous and irregular passions of barbarians.”
- **Recruitment decline.** In the purer commonwealth arms reserved for ranks with country, property, and share in laws; under emperors common soldiers “drawn from the meanest, and very frequently from the most profligate, of mankind,” though officers often of liberal birth.
- **Discipline and Severus.** Later (Ch. V–VI), Severus’s indulgence (gold rings, wives in quarters, raised pay, expected donatives) is presented as relaxing the nerves of that docility—primary cause in the commander-in-chief’s pernicious indulgence.
