# Art of Commitment

The **art of commitment** comprises the tactics by which a party in a bargaining or coercive relationship makes its threats or promises credible by deliberately reducing its own freedom of action. Because a rational, self-interested actor will not carry out a costly threat if it can avoid doing so, credibility often requires visible, costly, or irreversible steps that tie the threatener's hands or change the payoffs.

## Core Mechanisms

- **Irreversible actions**: Burning bridges, sinking the transport fleet, or otherwise making retreat physically or politically impossible.
- **Delegation and pre-commitment**: Handing authority to agents who cannot or will not back down (e.g., forward-deployed commanders with limited communications, or domestic political constraints that punish retreat).
- **Reputation and "face"**: Treating past commitments as investments in future credibility. "Face is merely the interdependence of a country's commitments; it is a country's reputation for action, the expectation other countries have about its behaviour."
- **Visibility and audience**: Making the commitment observable to the adversary (and sometimes to one's own domestic audience).
- **Focal points and saliency**: Using conspicuous, natural, or precedent-based lines that make compliance or violation unambiguous and therefore easier to commit to.

Schelling notes that commitment can be to a *threat* ("I will hurt you if you do X") or to a *promise* ("I will reward you if you do Y"). Both are often required for successful coercion: the threat supplies the stick, the promise the carrot of relief.

## Rationality of Apparent Irrationality

A powerful theme is that it can be rational to appear (or become) irrational, or to create a situation in which one no longer fully controls the outcome. By limiting one's options, or by creating a risk that the situation will spiral out of control, a player can make an otherwise incredible threat believable.

This connects directly to the "threat that leaves something to chance" developed in the following chapter.

## Examples and Limits

Historical examples range from the Greek commander who burned his ships to the modern practice of "trip-wire" forces whose presence guarantees involvement because retreat would be intolerably humiliating. In the nuclear context, forward-deployed nuclear weapons or declared launch-on-warning policies can be understood as commitment devices (with obvious dangers).

Limits: over-commitment can produce disaster if the opponent calls the bluff or if circumstances change; commitments that are too rigid foreclose mutually beneficial bargains; domestic commitments can become hostages to internal politics.
