## Definition and Scope

**Islamic occasionalism** is the doctrine, developed within [[asharism|Ashʿarite]] *[[kalam|kalām]]*, that God is the **sole genuine cause** of every event and attribute in the world. Secondary causes — fire burning, a blow causing pain, a seed producing a plant — are not real causes but mere occasions (*munāsabāt*): God creates the expected effect alongside the apparent cause, but the cause itself does nothing to produce the effect. Crucially, this means God re-creates every attribute of every atom at every moment, and that what we perceive as stable causal laws are simply God's regular habits in creation, which He can vary at will (miracles being instances of such variation).

The doctrine is named "occasionalism" in the etic scholarly tradition after the similar view independently developed by the French philosopher Nicolas Malebranche in the seventeenth century. Al-Ashʿarī had, however, articulated the position in full philosophical detail already in the tenth century. Source: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]].

**Emic usage**: Ashʿarite thinkers did not use "occasionalism" as a term; they described God as the creator of all atoms and accidents (*aʿrāḍ*), and used the concept of *kasb* (acquisition) to assign derivative moral responsibility to human agents.

**Etic usage**: Scholars apply "occasionalism" both to medieval Islamic thought and to Malebranche, treating the Islamic version as the earlier and philosophically developed precedent.

## Theological Context

Occasionalism emerged from the **Ashʿarite atomic theory** of the physical world. Both the Muʿtazilites and the Ashʿarites accepted a physics of atoms (*jawāhir*, roughly substances) and accidents (*aʿrāḍ*, properties). Key features:

- Atoms cannot exist without accidents (an atom must be either in motion or at rest, for instance)
- Accidents have only a momentary existence — they do not persist from one instant to the next
- Therefore atoms, which depend on accidents, are also momentary

From this the Ashʿarites drew a radical conclusion: **at each instant, God re-creates every atom and every one of its properties from scratch**. The fire's heat, the cotton's combustibility, the burning itself — all these are created anew at each moment by God. The apparent persistence and continuity of things is God's regular habit in creation, not any intrinsic stability of the created world.

As Adamson describes al-Ghazālī's formulation: "Rather, God simply creates these two things 'side by side', with the result that we infer a necessary connection between them. Yet God could always create things differently." (*Philosophy in the Islamic World*, ch. 21)

## The Problem of Moral Responsibility

The deepest philosophical challenge to occasionalism concerns human **moral responsibility**. If God creates every event, including every human action, then in what sense are humans responsible for what they do?

### The Muʿtazilite Alternative

The [[mutazilism|Muʿtazilites]] had insisted that humans are genuine causes of their own actions. They spoke of humans "engendering" effects: if I pull a trigger, I cause the bullet's motion, the target's wound, the death. The whole causal chain from intention to consequence is mine. This preserved moral responsibility at the cost of limiting God's causal sovereignty.

### The Ashʿarite Doctrine of Acquisition (*Kasb / Iktisāb*)

Al-Ashʿarī's response introduced the concept of **acquisition** (*kasb* or *iktisāb*). Even though God creates every action, the human agent "acquires" or "performs" the action — and thereby bears moral responsibility for it — because:

1. God creates in the human agent the **power** to perform the action
2. The human intentionally exercises that power (as opposed to involuntary motions like shivering)
3. The derivative moral features of the action (its badness, the responsibility it generates) belong to the human agent, not to God

The analogy Adamson uses: God creates the color blue of a giraffe's eyes, but He does not become colored, visible, or aesthetically striking by doing so. Similarly, God creates an evil action without acquiring its derivative moral character.

Al-Ghazālī acknowledged the tension: the human has no real choice about whether to use the power God gives them. If God determines that someone will hit another person, He gives them the power to do so, and they will use it. This makes the Ashʿarite position a form of **compatibilism**: moral responsibility is compatible with the human's actions being fully determined by God.

### The Argument Against Unrealized Powers

The Ashʿarites also produced an argument to show that **unrealized powers are incoherent**. The Muʿtazilites had posited a "capacity" that humans could choose to use or not. Against this:

> Suppose I have the power to hit you. I am either going to use it or not. My power to hit you is not by itself sufficient for my hitting you (otherwise I would have already done it). I therefore need a further power: a power to use the power. But then I need another power to use *that* power, generating an infinite regress. (Adamson, paraphrasing al-Ghazālī, ch. 15)

If there are no unrealized powers, then at any given moment I have only the power that I am actually exercising — which is the power God has given me to carry out what God has determined I will do. I cannot simultaneously have the power to hit you and the power to refrain from hitting you.

## God's Justice and the Euthyphro Problem

The occasionalist system created a sharp engagement with what philosophers now call the **Euthyphro dilemma**: does God determine what is morally right, or does He command things because they are right?

The Muʿtazilites held the second position: there are moral truths accessible to human reason, and God is bound by them. The Ashʿarites held the first: justice simply means conformity to God's will. Al-Ashʿarī explicitly accepted the most counterintuitive implications:

> "Al-Ashʿarī has no hesitation in embracing the most counterintuitive aspects of this theory, saying that God could torment innocent children in the afterlife, if He so chose. He could also punish those who believe in Him and reward the unbelievers. Indeed, not only could He do these things; if He did them, His actions would be just." (Adamson, *Philosophy in the Islamic World*, ch. 15)

Al-Juwaynī added that even if humans cannot help but do what God determines, it is not unjust for God to punish them — because whatever God does is just by definition.

This position reflects the Ashʿarite priority: to preserve God's **untrammeled power** (*qadr*) above all other considerations, even at the cost of making God's goodness opaque to human understanding.

## Occasionalism and Miracles

The most concrete expression of occasionalism is its account of **miracles**. Because fire does not cause burning (God creates burning alongside fire as His usual habit), miracles require no suspension of "laws of nature" — there are no such laws. When Abraham was thrown into fire and survived unscathed (Quran 21:69), God simply declined to create burning on that occasion, while still creating fire. This preserves both God's omnipotence and the Quranic testimony to miracles, without the philosophical embarrassment of God "interfering" in a lawlike natural order He has established.

## The Occasionalism Debate in al-Ghazālī

Al-Ghazālī's treatment of causation in the [[tahafut-al-falasifa|*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*]] has generated a long-running scholarly controversy about whether he was a full occasionalist or held a weaker position. He clearly denies that fire *necessarily* causes burning, but it is disputed whether he also denies that fire causes burning at all (full occasionalism) or merely denies the necessity while preserving secondary causation (moderate position where God's tacit permission is required for causes to operate).

Adamson suggests al-Ghazālī may have deliberately left both options open: either is sufficient to defeat Avicennan necessitarianism, which was his primary target.

## Relationship to Avicennan Necessitarianism

Occasionalism stands in explicit opposition to the necessitarianism of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]], for whom:
- God necessarily causes the universe (because everything God does He does necessarily)
- The universe contains a determinate causal order that flows from God's nature
- Miracles in the sense of violations of this order are therefore impossible

Al-Ghazālī saw Avicennan necessitarianism as theologically catastrophic: it eliminated genuine divine agency, bound God to a causal order He cannot override, and made the Creator into something more like a natural law than a personal God.

## Comparison with Malebranche

The parallel between Islamic occasionalism (10th–11th c.) and Nicolas Malebranche's occasionalism (17th c.) is noted by historians of philosophy but the relationship is one of independent development, not direct influence. The structural similarity arises because both traditions sought to explain the relationship between God's omnipotence and created causation within a monotheistic framework. Malebranche's occasionalism is more explicitly Cartesian in its context (addressing body-mind interaction) but makes the same core claim: finite things cannot truly act on one another; God is the only genuine cause.

## Legacy

Ashʿarite occasionalism remained the dominant theological account of causation across much of Sunni Islam. It shaped:
- The form of the Ashʿarite-Avicennan debate that dominated eastern Islamic intellectual life from the 11th century onward
- Later [[kalam|kalām]] methodology, which had to engage with philosophical accounts of causation
- The reception of Avicennan natural philosophy, which was accepted selectively (natural science) while its necessitarian metaphysics was rejected
