# Mos Maiorum

## Definition
*Mos maiorum* — literally "the custom of the ancestors" — is the body of
unwritten norms, precedents, and expectations that governed public life in the
Roman Republic. Rome had no written constitution; the *mos maiorum* did the work
one performed, regulating how magistrates competed for office, how the Senate and
the popular assemblies interacted, how far ambition could be pursued before it
became tyranny, and what forms of political conflict were legitimate. It was
emic — the Romans invoked it constantly — and it functioned precisely because
elite Romans internalized it and policed one another's adherence to it through
reputation, shame, and mutual restraint.

## Emic vs. Etic
- **Emic**: Roman orators, especially Cicero, appealed to the *mos maiorum* as a
  living authority — a reason to accept or reject a course of action because "it
  is not how our ancestors did things."
- **Etic**: Modern historians (and popularizers such as [[duncan-storm-before-the-storm-2017|Mike Duncan]])
  use "the erosion of the mos maiorum" as an analytic frame for the fall of the
  Republic: the argument that the Republic's institutions survived not on legal
  rules but on norms of restraint, and that once those norms were breached —
  once violence, private armies, and the disregard of custom became normalized —
  the written machinery of the Republic could not hold.

## The Norm-Erosion Thesis
The narrative frame associated with this concept holds that the late Republic
fell through a ratchet of precedent-breaking, each transgression making the next
easier:
- the political murder of [[tiberius-gracchus|Tiberius Gracchus]] (133 BCE),
  the first lethal factional violence in the city in centuries;
- the recruitment of landless volunteers into the legions under [[marius|Marius]],
  creating soldiers loyal to a general rather than the state;
- [[sulla|Sulla's]] march on Rome at the head of his own army (88 BCE), the first
  time a Roman commander turned his legions on the city;
- the [[sullan-proscriptions-82bce|proscriptions]], state-sanctioned murder of
  political enemies for profit.
Each act broke a norm that had held for generations; none could be undone.

## Limitations and Critiques
The frame is powerful but contested. Critics note that:
- it can romanticize an earlier "healthy" Republic that was itself built on
  conquest, slavery, and elite domination;
- "norms" is doing analytic work that structural explanations (the social and
  economic dislocations of empire, the [[optimates-populares|land question]], the
  militarization of politics) may do better;
- treating the *mos maiorum* as a stable object obscures how much the "ancestral
  custom" was continually reinvented and invoked selectively by whichever side
  found it convenient.
The concept is best used as a lens on how the Republic's *informal* constitution
worked and failed, not as a full causal account of its collapse.
