# Mos Maiorum

## Definition
*Mos maiorum* — literally "the custom of the ancestors" — is the body of
unwritten norms, precedents, and expectations that governed public life in the
Roman Republic. Rome had no written constitution; the *mos maiorum* did the work
one performed, regulating how magistrates competed for office, how the Senate and
the popular assemblies interacted, how far ambition could be pursued before it
became tyranny, and what forms of political conflict were legitimate. It was
emic — the Romans invoked it constantly — and it functioned precisely because
elite Romans internalized it and policed one another's adherence to it through
reputation, shame, and mutual restraint.

## Emic vs. Etic
- **Emic**: Roman orators, especially Cicero, appealed to the *mos maiorum* as a
  living authority — a reason to accept or reject a course of action because "it
  is not how our ancestors did things."
- **Etic**: Modern historians (and popularizers such as [[duncan-storm-before-the-storm-2017|Mike Duncan]])
  use "the erosion of the mos maiorum" as an analytic frame for the fall of the
  Republic: the argument that the Republic's institutions survived not on legal
  rules but on norms of restraint, and that once those norms were breached —
  once violence, private armies, and the disregard of custom became normalized —
  the written machinery of the Republic could not hold.

## The Norm-Erosion Thesis
The narrative frame associated with this concept holds that the late Republic
fell through a ratchet of precedent-breaking, each transgression making the next
easier:
- the political murder of [[tiberius-gracchus|Tiberius Gracchus]] (133 BCE),
  the first lethal factional violence in the city in centuries;
- the recruitment of landless volunteers into the legions under [[marius|Marius]],
  creating soldiers loyal to a general rather than the state;
- [[sulla|Sulla's]] march on Rome at the head of his own army (88 BCE), the first
  time a Roman commander turned his legions on the city;
- the [[sullan-proscriptions-82bce|proscriptions]], state-sanctioned murder of
  political enemies for profit.
Each act broke a norm that had held for generations; none could be undone.

## Limitations and Critiques
The frame is powerful but contested. Critics note that:
- it can romanticize an earlier "healthy" Republic that was itself built on
  conquest, slavery, and elite domination;
- "norms" is doing analytic work that structural explanations (the social and
  economic dislocations of empire, the [[optimates-populares|land question]], the
  militarization of politics) may do better;
- treating the *mos maiorum* as a stable object obscures how much the "ancestral
  custom" was continually reinvented and invoked selectively by whichever side
  found it convenient.
The concept is best used as a lens on how the Republic's *informal* constitution
worked and failed, not as a full causal account of its collapse.

## OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986)

Miriam Griffin (Ch. 19) frames late-Republican politics as the **legacy of Sulla**:
the dictator fixed the *cursus honorum* so top commanders had ~20 years’ senatorial
socialization, absorbing traditions and learning to value oligarchic cohesiveness —
institutionalizing *mos* as career design. Yet Sallust dated the moral turning-point
to Sulla’s Eastern return and vindictive victory; Cicero’s generation remembered
proscriptions, and Cicero as consul opposed restoring rights to sons of the proscribed
lest Sulla’s laws — and state cohesion — dissolve. Griffin’s structural diagnosis of
the Republic’s fall stresses that laws “bent and ignored” too often by 49 to command
obedience, and that rising success standards (Marius, Sulla, Pompey, Caesar) plus the
claim that great deeds exempt one from law made the Republic incompatible with one-
or two-man ascendancy — the practical death of ancestral restraint.

P. G. McC. Brown (Ch. 18) notes Plautine comedy freely mixed Greek settings with
Roman institutions (triumph, *clientela*, edicts) while still performing traditional
moral commonplaces — literature as both solvent and vehicle of ancestral norms.
Ennius’ *Annals* (Fabius Cunctator fragment) glorified traditional morality in the new
hexameter medium.

Robin Nisbet (Ch. 20, Range 7) treats Lucretius and Catullus as sharing Republican
candour that still assumes an elite world of *mores*, even when Epicurean physics or
Neoteric private emotion undercut civic piety.

Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]] (esp. Griffin Ch. 19; Brown;
Nisbet).

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010)

Harriet Flower treats *mos maiorum* as a **political and cultural artifact under rapid change**, not as a stable ancestral code that modern historians should take at face value. Dramatic social transformation, she argues, produced a *discourse of tradition* and an insistent claim to timeless heritage precisely *because* rules of the political game were continually fine-tuned (e.g. the gradual development of the *cursus honorum* before the *lex Villia annalis* of 180). Via Gotter, she notes the tendency to *define* ancestral custom as itself a symptom of **increasing loss of consensus**.

The contrast with Sulla is load-bearing for her multi-republic map ([[roman-republics-periodization]]): pre-Sullan *nobiles* republics rested on deliberation, debate, compromise, and lively elections, with *mos* strong even as change continued; Sulla’s New Republic ([[sullan-constitution]]) tried to replace that foundation with *lex* enforced by permanent courts — “lex was to replace mos maiorum.” Romans, Flower holds, had never had a system principally based on law rather than custom and refused that revolutionary shift. After Sulla, increasingly assertive **ancestor rhetoric and iconography** was innovative rather than traditional: a symptom of estrangement from a republic that “no longer belonged to the *nobiles*.” Violence itself counted as a basic breach of republican principle — an admission that the accepted system had failed to resolve conflict. Source: [[flower-roman-republics-2010]].

## Harris, *Roman Power* (2016)

Harris lists *mos maiorum* among **abstractions that exercise real power** at Rome alongside *libertas*, Stoicism, and rhetoric: “Traditions can have power, as *mos maiorum* (‘ancestral custom’) did at Rome.” Republican legitimacy derived from established convention—the *mores maiorum*—plus limited “mass approval” from better-off male citizens, not the whole population; Weber’s pure types fit poorly (magistrates combined legal and traditional authority; consuls some charisma). Mid-republican ideology was expressed in temples of abstract deities (Concordia, Salus, Victory, Fides, Hope, Honos/Virtus, Mens, Pietas) vowed by magistrates from booty when non-military state spending was minimal. *Virtus* was expected of all ranks yet a special quality of great leaders and a major basis of the aristocracy’s claim to rule (Marcellus’s Virtus/Honos temple, 222). Source: [[harris-roman-power-2016]].
