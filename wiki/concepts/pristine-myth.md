# The Pristine Myth (the Americas as a Managed Landscape)

The **"pristine myth"** — a term from geographer **William Denevan** (1992) — is the belief that
pre-Columbian America was a **pristine, untouched wilderness** lightly inhabited by people who left no
mark on it. It is the environmental face of [[holmbergs-mistake|Holmberg's Mistake]], and the third of
the great revisions Charles Mann's [[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]] (Part Three, "Landscape with Figures")
marshals: the Americas were not only **more populous** and settled **earlier** than believed, but far
more **environmentally engineered**. Native Americans, on this view, were not passive dwellers in nature
but **active managers** of continental landscapes — and the "wilderness" Europeans found was largely the
**regrowth over a depopulated, managed continent**.

## Humanized landscapes

Across the hemisphere, Mann assembles cases of large-scale environmental engineering:
- **Amazonia** — the strongest case: [[terra-preta|terra preta]] soils and orchard-forests, "a cultural
  artifact" (see [[terra-preta]]).
- The **Bolivian Beni** — tens of thousands of square miles of mounds, causeways, canals, raised fields,
  and fish-weirs, the savanna itself maintained by deliberate **annual burning** ([[holmbergs-mistake]]).
- **Eastern North America** — Indians used **fire** to clear underbrush, create park-like forests and
  grassland edges, and drive game; the open, "natural" woodlands and prairies the colonists admired were
  in part **anthropogenic fire-regimes**. The vast **bison** herds of the Plains may themselves have been
  partly a post-collapse phenomenon — populations that exploded once the Indians who had controlled them
  died.
- The **Maya** lowlands and **Mississippian** [[cahokia|Cahokia]] — engineered agricultural landscapes of
  reservoirs, raised fields, and earthworks supporting dense populations.

## "The virgin forest was invented"

Mann's central paradox: the apparently primeval wilderness of the European imagination was a **recent
historical product**. The [[virgin-soil-epidemics|Great Dying]] removed the managers; their fields,
orchards, and fire-cleared parklands then **grew wild**. As the colonists pushed west they were "preceded
by a wave of disease and then a wave of ecological disturbance" — game and forests rebounding into the
human vacuum. In the historian **Stephen Pyne's** epigram (quoted by Mann): *"The virgin forest was not
encountered in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; it was invented in the late eighteenth and early
nineteenth centuries."* Far from finding pristine wilderness, **"Europeans bloodily created it."**

## The debate and its stakes

The thesis is contested. Critics — the geographer **Thomas Vale** most pointedly — warn that scholars
have "simply replaced the old myth with a new one, the myth of the humanized landscape," over-reading
sparse evidence; **Betty Meggers** resists the high-population, heavy-management claims for Amazonia.
The dispute carries unusual **political weight**, which Mann foregrounds: it bears on **conservation**
(is "wilderness" without people the authentic baseline, or a colonial artifact?), on **Indigenous land
rights** (e.g., who may set the Beni afire?), and on the moral accounting of conquest. Like the
[[pre-columbian-american-population|population debate]] it is entangled with, the *direction* of the
revision is widely accepted; the *magnitude* remains open.
