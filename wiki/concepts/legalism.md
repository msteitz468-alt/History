# Legalism (*Fajia*)

The Chinese political philosophy of **government through impersonal law, administrative technique, and
the concentrated power of the ruler** — the great statist rival to Confucianism in the Warring States,
and the ideology that armed the **[[qin-dynasty|Qin]]** to unify China.

## Core Ideas (emic)

Legalism rejects the Confucian faith in moral cultivation and rule by virtuous example. Holding that
**human beings are self-interested** and that order must be engineered, it relies on three elements,
synthesized by **[[han-feizi|Han Feizi]]**:

- ***fa* (law)** — clear, codified, publicly known rules, **applied uniformly** regardless of rank,
  enforced through **rewards and punishments** (the "two handles");
- ***shu* (technique/statecraft)** — the ruler's hidden methods for **selecting, testing and controlling
  officials**, holding them to their job-titles (the "performance and title" check) and concealing his
  own mind;
- ***shi* (positional power)** — authority residing in the **office** of ruler, not in his character.

Earlier exponents emphasized different elements: **Shang Yang** (the reformer of Qin) stressed law and
agricultural-military mobilization; **Shen Buhai** stressed administrative technique; **Shen Dao**
stressed positional power. Legalists were **anti-traditionalist** — rejecting the Confucian golden age,
they held that institutions must change with the times.

## Historical Role

Legalist methods, pioneered in **Qin** by **Shang Yang** (4th c. BCE) and given their fullest theory by
Han Feizi, made Qin a ruthlessly efficient war-state and enabled the **First Emperor** and his minister
**Li Si** to **unify China in 221 BCE** — abolishing feudal states for centralized provinces, codifying
law, standardizing script, weights and measures, and (notoriously) the "burning of the books." The
**brutality** of Qin's application of Legalism was blamed for the dynasty's collapse after only fifteen
years.

## Afterlife and Critique (etic)

Publicly **repudiated** under the Han in favour of Confucianism, Legalism nonetheless supplied the
**enduring institutional skeleton** of the Chinese imperial state — centralized bureaucracy, codified
law, the merit-tested official — so that the empire is often described as "**Confucian in
appearance, Legalist in substance**." Modern scholars caution that "Legalism" (*fajia*) is a **later
classificatory label** grouping diverse thinkers, not a self-conscious school; and its amoral statecraft
has been variously read as proto-totalitarian or as hard-headed political realism (compared to
Machiavelli and to modern theories of bureaucracy and law).
