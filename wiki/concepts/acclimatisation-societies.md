# Acclimatisation Societies

## Definition and Scope

Acclimatisation societies were voluntary organisations, active in the British Empire and other European empires from the 1850s, that sought to improve colonial environments by importing, exporting, and releasing into the wild the most "useful" and "beautiful" species from around the world. In [[australia]], these bodies "imported and exported various species and transferred them within and between colonies, aiming to create a garden landscape that was abundant, profitable and morally uplifting" (Gaynor, in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]]).

The concept is *etic* from the perspective of modern ecology (the movement did not understand ecosystem dynamics) but *emic* in its own terms: acclimatisers believed they were rationally improving a deficient nature, guided by natural history — "a form of scientific inquiry concerned with classifying and naming the natural world" rather than understanding species interactions or what would later be called ecosystems.

## Intellectual Origins and Context

The movement grew out of Enlightenment notions of "improvement" — the idea that antipodean nature was raw material awaiting transformation into a congenial, civilised landscape. It was supported by the same cultural logic that drove European settlers to recreate English gardens, import European seeds and animals, and map colonial land through the aesthetic categories of the "noble park." The acclimatisers' failure to understand ecosystem interactions — "dealing with nature's pieces rather than systems" — meant that "few counselled caution."

The movement operated alongside game-law legislation: most Australian colonies passed game laws in the 1860s, over working-class objections to similarities with "the evil of the game laws in the mother country." These laws generally aimed to protect *imported* fauna, though some also protected native birds.

## Key Activities in Colonial Australia

**Introduced species releases.** Acclimatisation societies introduced a wide range of birds and mammals. In South Australia, only seven of the sixteen bird species introduced between 1863 and 1885 became established in the wild. The most consequential introductions were not always society-organised: Thomas Austin's private 1859 release of 24 wild rabbits near Geelong — for sporting hunting — became catastrophic. Foxes were first released for sport (probably the Chirnside family at Werribee Park, mid-1870s) and spread across south-eastern Australia once rabbit populations exploded in the 1860s–70s. Cats arrived as domestic pets and spread independently through the bush.

**Fisheries protection.** Acclimatisation societies were behind the first colonial fisheries protection Acts, intended to protect both imported species and iconic natives such as Murray Cod.

**Not all introductions succeeded.** The rabbit spread "faster than any other introduced mammal anywhere in the world" with "plentiful food and shelter, relatively few predators and climates similar to its Iberian homeland." The fox followed, using rabbit population growth to establish itself. Cats reached 90 per cent of the mainland by 1890. Government responses — barrier fences, bounties, poisons, mongoose releases — were largely ineffective.

## Limitations and Critique

The acclimatisation movement's understanding of nature "dealt with nature's pieces rather than systems," leaving colonists unable to anticipate ecological cascades. The rabbit became an emblem of the movement's failures: by 1883–85, nearly £1 million was paid in New South Wales alone in rabbit-control bounties and wages. W.K. Hancock by 1930 judged the rabbit had "made new deserts."

The movement also illustrates the selective nature of colonial conservation sentiment: advocates for preserving imported fauna did not extend their concern to the destruction of Aboriginal societies or native species declared "pests." In Queensland and New South Wales, landholders were legally required to destroy wallabies, kangaroos, and dingoes; in 1892 alone, 27,736 pademelon scalps were bounty-claimed in the Lismore district.

"In the wake of the rabbit, enthusiasm for acclimatisation waned" — the movement faded in the 1880s–1890s as its failures accumulated, though introduced species remained a permanent feature of the Australian environment.

## Historiography

The concept is treated in [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]] as an example of how Enlightenment natural history — classificatory, species-focused — left colonists "poorly equipped to understand interactions between species." The movement is not controversial in its characterisation (harmful ecological consequences are well established) but the relative weight of acclimatisation vs. pastoral clearing vs. Aboriginal dispossession in causing ecological change remains an active area of environmental history research.
