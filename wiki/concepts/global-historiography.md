# Early Modern Historiography (the Global Writing of History)

## Definition and Argument

This concept covers the **writing of history (historiography) as a global
practice**, as surveyed by Sanjay Subrahmanyam in CWH VI. His principal argument
is twofold. First, **against the prejudice** of nineteenth-century European
thinkers (Hegel above all), recognizable historiography existed "over very long
periods of time and in many diverse societies" — not only Europe, the
Mediterranean, and China. Second, the early modern period (1400–1800) saw the
**rise of new forms of large-scale history writing** that derived from, but
clearly differentiated themselves from, earlier traditions of "universal
history" — and these changes can be tied to **new material circumstances**:
long-distance travel, imperial conquest, and intensified contact between
previously isolated societies, along with parallel changes in cartography and
ethnography.

## "Universal History" from Polybius to Tabari

Subrahmanyam traces a long genealogy. Rather than starting with Herodotus, he
pairs the roughly contemporary **Polybius** (2nd c. BCE Mediterranean) and **Sima
Qian** (Han China) — both soldier-historians who wrote "universal" histories. He
reads "universal" not as *total* coverage but as **symmetry between Self and
Other** (egoistical and xenological): a synopsis that embraces one's own tradition
and a "complementary opposite" (Polybius on Rome's rise; Sima Qian on the Han and
the Xiongnu). The two had divergent afterlives — Sima Qian canonical across the
Sinic world, Polybius forgotten until the Renaissance — but the *idea* of universal
history persisted in the Mediterranean (Isidore of Seville) and passed to the
**Arabic/Persian *tarikh*** tradition (al-Tabari's *History of Prophets and Kings*,
with its concern for the reliability of oral transmission; al-Biruni's *Book of
India*; the Persianate historiography consolidated at Ghazna and, after the
Mongols, across the Iran–China corridor).

## The Early Modern Proliferation

Subrahmanyam's vivid exhibit is the **Mughal and Indo-Persian** historiographical
field. He opens with the *Tuzak-i Walajahi*, a 1780s Persian dynastic history from
the southern Indian kingdom of Arcot by an *ordinary* intellectual (Burhan Khan
Handi) — whose very ordinariness shows how broadly such wide-scope history writing
was within reach. The text incorporates a potted history of **Europe** (a garbled
"Columbus" as inventor of the compass; the East India Companies as lowly merchants
turned political powers; the papacy in "Rum"), demonstrating that Mughal historians
were neither isolated nor ignorant of other historiographies. Indo-Persian history
ranged across genres — imperial chronicles (Abu'l Fazl's *Akbar Nama*), regional
syntheses (Firishta's *Gulshan-i Ibrahimi*), sub-dynastic biographies,
autobiographies, and local "chorographies" of single towns — with authors moving
flexibly between forms as patronage shifted.

## Significance

The chapter is a corrective to **Eurocentric** accounts of the origins of modern
history writing (see [[eurocentrism]], [[world-history-as-field]]): the comparative
and large-scale impulse was global, with roots in many literate traditions, and
the early modern transformation was driven by the same connective processes —
travel, conquest, cross-cultural contact — that the rest of [[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|
CWH VI]] traces. It exemplifies Subrahmanyam's own method of **"connected
histories."**

## Contestation

The thesis cuts against the standard narrative that scientific, critical
historiography is a European invention of the Renaissance and Enlightenment. It
also raises the question — as with the volume's other "discovery"/"world" claims —
of how far diverse traditions of "history" share enough to be treated as one
object.
