# The Bakuhan System (*bakuhan taisei*)

The structure of government that defined **early-modern [[japan|Japan]]** (c. 1600–1868):
a national [[shogunate-bakufu|shogunate]] (*bakufu*) ruling the country **through a
subordinate coalition of largely autonomous daimyo domains** (*han*), rather than through
a centralized bureaucracy of its own officers. The term — a compound of **baku**(fu) +
**han** — was coined by postwar Japanese historians (Itō Tasaburō, Nakamura Kichiji) and
is the organizing concept of [[cambridge-history-japan-v4-early-modern|CHJ Vol. 4]]. It
replaced an older historiography that had treated the Tokugawa regime as the bakufu alone —
"the willful creation of the Tokugawa house" — and now gives the **[[shugo-daimyo|han]] a
position of equal standing** with the bakufu.

## Definition and Scope

The present scholarly consensus (Hall) is that Edo government "was the consequence of the
evolution of two political systems: the shogunal system of national control and the daimyo
system of local control. Both of these aspects of government were necessary and mutually
dependent." The shogun's hegemonic power **rested significantly on the ability of the
daimyo** who made up his vassal band to command their own provinces — domains that
accounted for **roughly three-quarters of the national land base**. The shogun held the
remaining quarter (the *tenryō*) but was "not prepared to abolish the daimyo and take on
the administration of the entire country through a private officer corps." As Hall puts it,
"the shogun's government was essentially an expanded version of *han* government" — many
features of Tokugawa administration derived from precedents set when [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]]
was himself merely the daimyo of Mikawa.

The system rested on the **[[kokudaka-system]]** of land measurement and taxation (assessed
rice yield in *koku*), which gave both the hegemon and each daimyo a quantified knowledge
of land, manpower, and the military service (*gun'yaku*) owed.

## The structure of power

CHJ Vol. 4 (Hall, ch. 4) describes a deliberate **balance of power** among classes of
daimyo, all the shogun's sworn vassals holding their domains as personal grants certified
by the shogun's seal:

- **Shinpan** (collateral Tokugawa houses) — ~23 by the eighteenth century.
- **Fudai** ("house"/hereditary daimyo) — ~145; the only daimyo who staffed bakufu office.
- **Tozama** ("outside" lords) — ~98; the remnants of daimyo created by [[oda-nobunaga|Nobunaga]]
  or [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Hideyoshi]], kept from central office and often planted at the
  geographic margins.

The Tokugawa house itself was the single largest power bloc, holding ~4.2 million *koku* of
granary land (*tenryō*) plus the lands of its **bannermen** (*hatamoto*, ~5,200 enfeoffed
men in 1722) and **housemen** (*gokenin*, ~17,400 stipendiaries). At the close of the first
Tokugawa century the taxable land divided roughly evenly between the shogunal bloc
(*tenryō* + hatamoto + fudai/shinpan ≈ 16 million *koku*) and the tozama (~9.8 million).

## Mechanisms of control

The bakufu governed the daimyo not by destroying them but by **keeping the relationship
precarious**:

- **The Buke shohatto** (Laws for the Military Houses, first issued 1615, expanded by Iemitsu
  to 21 clauses): daimyo could not repair or build castles without permission, contract
  marriages without approval, harbor antibakufu criminals, or build large ships; they must
  "cultivate letters and military arts." Read aloud before the assembled daimyo at each new
  shogun's accession.
- **Attainder and transfer** (*kaieki*, *tenpō*): using the Buke shohatto, the shogun could
  reduce, relocate, or abolish any daimyo — even for failing to produce an heir. Under the
  first five shoguns some **13.2 million *koku* (≈ half the taxable land base) changed
  hands**, ~200 daimyo were destroyed and 172 created; the rate of attainder then fell off
  sharply as the balance settled in the shogun's favor.
- **Alternate attendance** (*sankin-kōtai*): made universal by Iemitsu in 1642, it required
  every daimyo to maintain a residence in Edo, attend the shogun in alternate years, and
  leave **wife and children as permanent hostages** in the capital. Hall calls it "the most
  effective mechanism developed by the Tokugawa shoguns" — affirming Edo's centrality,
  enabling constant personal communication, and (as a side effect) draining daimyo finances
  on dual residences and travel. It generalized to the whole country a hostage practice the
  unifiers had used within their own vassal bands.

## The han as a unit of life

Harold Bolitho's chapter (ch. 5) restores the **han** to the center of the picture. The
domains covered three-quarters of Japan, held most of its wealth and taxes, and commanded
at least three-quarters of the [[samurai|samurai]]. For the great majority of Japanese the
*han* was **the only government they knew** — it gave them their roads, bridges, laws, and
order, separated villages quarreling over water, and provided famine relief. The shogun and
emperor were, for the common people, "little more than abstractions, dimly perceived." Han
studies were neglected for seventy years after the domains' abolition in 1871 (Meiji
scholars, "servants of strong central government," looked only at the bakufu); they revived
only after 1945 under Itō Tasaburō and the *Hanseishi kenkyūkai*.

## Interpretation and debate

- **Feudal or early modern?** The bakuhan/kokudaka systems are *sui generis* — "so
  distinctive, so difficult to compare with other historical models, particularly European,
  that the use of feudalism as an explanatory device has become less attractive" (Wakita).
  Japanese historians tend to treat the Edo order as *kinsei* — "late" or "centralized
  feudalism," still more feudal than modern; Western historians since the 1960s prefer
  "early modern," more modern than feudal. The label is unresolved (see
  [[tokugawa-intellectual-life]]).
- **Military absolutism vs. balance of accommodation.** Older readings (Sansom; the
  "centralized feudalism" of Reischauer and E. H. Norman) saw the bakufu as a military
  absolutism resting on the lord–vassal bond. Hall and the volume stress instead a
  **network of checks and balances** and the conversion of military into civil government —
  legitimacy through proximity to the *tennō* and the claim to benevolent public authority
  (*kōgi*), not force alone.
