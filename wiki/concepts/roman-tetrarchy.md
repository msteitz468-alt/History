# The Roman Tetrarchy

The **tetrarchy** ("rule of four") was the system of **collegiate imperial government** devised by
[[diocletian|Diocletian]] and brought to its developed form in **293**: two senior emperors
(**Augusti**) and two junior emperors (**Caesars**), sharing the defense and administration of the
empire. It is the institutional heart of the Diocletianic refounding treated in
[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 3 (**Alan Bowman**).

## Structure

- **285/6** — Diocletian appointed **Maximian** as co-Augustus, the two taking the divine epithets
  ***Iovius*** (Diocletian, of Jupiter — the planner) and ***Herculius*** (Maximian, of Hercules — the
  executive).
- **293** — two **Caesars** were added: **Constantius** (under Maximian) and **Galerius** (under
  Diocletian), bound to their Augusti by **marriage and adoption**. (The symmetry was imperfect:
  Constantius, the senior Caesar, was Herculius; Galerius, the junior, Iovius.)

The four rulers were **not** assigned four fixed kingdoms; Bowman stresses there were only **two
praetorian prefects**, and emperors "went where they were needed" with staff and armies not
necessarily their own — though preferred residences emerged (Diocletian at **Nicomedia/Sirmium**,
Galerius at **Antioch/Thessalonica**, Maximian at **Milan**, Constantius at **Trier**). **Rome
remained the single nominal capital.**

## Ideology

The tetrarchy projected an **austere, massively authoritarian collegiality** — brothers under divine
patronage, expressed in distinctive porphyry sculpture, coinage, and the fulsome **Latin panegyrics**.
Bowman cautions against overstating the change: court ceremonial (*adoratio*) and divine patronage
were not new, the rulers were **not gods**, and the supposed turn to "oriental despotism" owes much to
modern interpretation. The system's purpose was **shared burden** and, in principle, an **orderly
succession** — the Caesars rising to replace the Augusti.

## Working and failure

The tetrarchy succeeded in its immediate tasks: it defeated the **Carausian** secession in Britain
(296), the Persians under **Narses** (297/8), revolts in **Egypt**, and frontier threats on the Rhine
and Danube. It oversaw the great administrative, fiscal, and monetary reforms (dioceses, the *iuga
et capita* tax, the **Price Edict**). In a unique act, Diocletian and Maximian **abdicated together**
on 1 May 305, the Caesars Constantius and Galerius becoming Augusti and two new Caesars (Severus,
Maximinus) appointed.

But the **succession mechanism failed at once**: the claims of **Constantine** (son of Constantius)
and **Maxentius** (son of Maximian) were ignored, and within a year the system dissolved into renewed
**civil war** (306–324), from which [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]] emerged as sole ruler,
reverting to **dynastic** monarchy.

## Significance and debate

The central historiographical question — **master-plan or ad hoc?** — divides scholars (Kolb vs.
Seston). Bowman favors the **gradualist** view: the tetrarchy and its reforms evolved over two decades
as responses to need, not a blueprint imposed at a stroke. Either way, the system laid the
**administrative foundations of the later Roman empire**, even though its defining innovation — power-
sharing without dynastic succession — proved its most fragile feature and did not outlive its
creator.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]] treats the tetrarchy as Diocletian’s **fundamental constitution**, not a temporary expedient. **Gibbon’s views.**

- **Fundamental law of four:** Diocletian associated three colleagues and “considered the joint administration of four princes not as a temporary expedient, but as a fundamental law of the constitution” — two Augusti with diadem, two Caesars rising in turn.
- **Geographic partition:** Constantius — Gaul, Spain, Britain; Galerius — Danube/Illyricum; Maximian — Italy and Africa; Diocletian — Thrace, Egypt, and Asia; each sovereign in his jurisdiction, united authority over the whole.
- **Harmony unique to the founder:** the balance required “two emperors without jealousy, two Cæsars without ambition, and the same general interest invariably pursued by four independent princes”; abdication was followed by eighteen years of discord, five civil wars, and the armed rise of Constantine.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **War-burden division as main objective.** Burdens of warfare were the main reason Diocletian created an imperial college (Maximian Augustus 286; Caesars Constantius and Galerius 293)—manageable division of powers producing unofficial territorial division (Constantius beyond Gallic Alps; Maximian Italy/Africa; Galerius Illyricum to Pontic strait; Diocletian the remainder). Each ruler had a praetorian prefect tied to person not region. Lactantius’s claim that tetrarchy quadrupled the army (and tax burden) is rejected as not literally true. Tetrarchy systematically organized civil law and (with censuses) taxation/*iugatio-capitatio*; Latin for major inscribed decisions including Prices Edict.
- **Portraiture.** Cropped hair, stubble, stern gaze; porphyry group embraces (R. R. R. Smith): collegiality of the four and moral fundamentalism/severity, while still acknowledging Diocletian’s dominance.
- **Dynasty defeated the system.** Artificial tetrarchic transfer proved weaker than dynastic claims; Diocletian himself used marriage to bind Caesars; father–son combinations (Constantius–Constantine; Maximian–Maxentius) were resilient; by 310 the aim of divorcing imperial power from family succession was dead.
