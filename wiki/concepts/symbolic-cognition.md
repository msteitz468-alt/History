## Definition

Symbolic cognition is the distinctively human capacity to break the experienced
world into discrete **mental symbols** and recombine them to re-create and
interrogate that world — to ask "what if?", to imagine, and (virtually
synonymously) to use [[syntactic-language-origins|language]]. In
[[tattersall-world-from-beginnings-2008|Tattersall's]] account this marks a
difference *in kind*, not merely degree, between *Homo sapiens* and every other
organism, including such complex beings as the [[neanderthals]] and chimpanzees,
whose impressive cognition is held to be essentially intuitive and non-symbolic.

## The Core Argument

Three linked claims define the concept as used in this wiki:

1. **Anatomy precedes behavior.** Anatomically modern humans existed well before
   symbolic behavior appeared. Early moderns at Herto (~160 kya) and Jebel Qafzeh
   (~92 kya) left an archaic, essentially non-symbolic archaeological record —
   Qafzeh's stone tools are indistinguishable from those of Neanderthals. The
   modern mind therefore cannot simply be read off modern bones.

2. **Exaptation and emergence, not gradual selection.** The neural substrate for
   symbolic thought arose for reasons unrelated to its eventual use (an
   *exaptation*, like feathers before flight), and consciousness/symbolism is an
   *emergent* property — more than the sum of its parts, like the properties of
   water relative to hydrogen and oxygen. A small genetic/developmental change in
   an already-large brain sufficed to create an entirely new potential. This
   revives Alfred Russel Wallace's perception of a genuine discontinuity — while
   rejecting his supernatural explanation — against Darwin's view that ordinary
   natural selection gradually built the human intellect.

3. **A cultural "release."** Because the capacity was present but unexpressed
   ("lying fallow"), its activation required a *cultural* trigger rather than a
   further biological change — most plausibly the invention of
   [[syntactic-language-origins|language]], itself drawing on a vocal tract that
   had evolved earlier for other reasons. Thereafter the *pattern* of innovation
   changed: behavioral novelty accelerated, and the uses of the new capacity were
   discovered sequentially (an ongoing process).

## Archaeological Signature

- **Africa, first.** Blombos Cave engraved ochre (~75 kya) — widely accepted as
  the earliest symbolic objects — plus pierced shell beads; early blade tools
  (~250 kya), pigment grinding, and long-distance exchange (>100 kya) as scattered
  precursors.
- **The Cro-Magnon "explosion."** From ~40 kya in Europe the [[cro-magnons]] leave
  cave painting (Chauvet >32 kya), figurines (Vogelherd, Hohlenstein-Stadel
  lion-man), notation, bone flutes, sewn beadwork and stratified burial (Sungir
  ~28 kya) — the full panoply of the modern mind, in sharp contrast to the
  symbol-free record of the contemporary Neanderthals. See
  [[paleolithic-art-symbolic-culture]].

## Limitations and Critiques

Strongly **contested**. (1) The sharp *kind*-not-degree boundary and the late,
"sudden" appearance of modern behavior are challenged by gradualist/"accretional"
models that point to the deep and piecemeal African record (pigments, beads, blades
across hundreds of thousands of years) as evidence of slow assembly rather than an
abrupt switch. (2) Attributing the trigger specifically to language is one
hypothesis among several (theory of mind, working memory, demographic/population-
size effects). (3) The strong denial of Neanderthal symbolism has eroded since
2008 as claims for Neanderthal pigment use, ornaments, and cave markings have
accumulated (though each remains debated). (4) The framework is bound to
Tattersall's [[punctuated-equilibria]] and splitter commitments. Use it as a
sharply-drawn position, not a settled consensus.
