# Führerprinzip (The Leader Principle)

The Nazi doctrine that absolute authority should flow downward from a single supreme leader (*Führer*) rather than through elected representation, majority vote, or collective deliberation, with each subordinate leader in turn exercising unquestioned authority over those below while owing total obedience upward. Rooted in the early Nazi Party's organizational practice under Adolf Hitler during the 1920s — a period in which figures such as [[actors/drexler-anton|Anton Drexler]], the party's founder, were progressively marginalized as Hitler consolidated singular authority over the movement — the principle was elevated after the 1933 seizure of power into a formal governing doctrine applied throughout the German state, party, and military hierarchy.

In practice, the *Führerprinzip* dismantled institutional checks and collective decision-making bodies in favor of direct, personal chains of command terminating in Hitler, and was used to justify the concentration of overlapping, competing authorities that historians (following the "working towards the Führer" analysis) argue characterized Nazi governance — subordinates anticipating and enacting what they believed Hitler wanted, often without explicit orders, in a system that rewarded radical initiative over bureaucratic regularity. The principle extended beyond the state into party organizations, the Hitler Youth, and eventually into occupied territories' administration.
