## Definition

The "decline narrative" is the historiographic claim that Islamic science entered a terminal
period of decline beginning in the 11th–13th centuries, variously attributed to:
1. Al-Ghazālī's (d. 1111) critique of philosophy (*Tahāfut al-falāsifa*), taken as inaugurating
   the triumph of religious over rational thought.
2. The Mongol devastation of Baghdad (1258), taken as physically destroying the infrastructure of
   Islamic learning.

The critique of this narrative — associated with George Saliba, and foreshadowed by historians of
Islamic science since the mid-20th century — argues that these explanations are contradicted by
the extant scientific texts, which show a period of *increasing* creativity and sophistication in
astronomy, medicine, optics, and mechanical engineering from the 13th through at least the 16th
centuries.

## The Classical Narrative and Its Two Groups

Saliba identifies two distinct intellectual lineages that converged on the decline thesis:

**Group 1 — Religion-vs.-Science Framework**: Interpreters who read Islamic intellectual history
through the lens of European Enlightenment conflict between reason and faith projected this model
onto Islam. Al-Ghazālī's *Incoherence of the Philosophers* became the pivotal event: his
apparent victory over philosophy (in the Muʿtazilite-traditionalist conflict) was taken as the
death of rational science. This reading was reinforced by the 19th-century orientalists, who
"summarily dismissed the scientific texts that were written after the Ghazālī period" and
investigated them poorly or not at all.

**Group 2 — Dynastic/Political History Framework**: Those who read Islamic history as a sequence
of dynasties and military events blamed the Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258) under Hülegü Khan for
ending the tradition of Islamic learning. Historical sources (mainly written in Mamluk areas
outside the Mongol zone) described manuscripts thrown into the Tigris until the river ran black
with ink — an anecdote that passed into collective memory as the definitive rupture.

Both groups converged on the 11th and 13th centuries as the terminal phase of Islamic science,
producing a "self-fulfilling prophecy": scholars not expecting originality in these texts did not
find it, because they were not looking for it.

> "In a sense, the way this lemma functioned in ʿUrḍī's model for the upper planets is very
> similar to the way Apollonius's theorem functioned in the solar model of Ptolemy." The
> sophistication of these post-Ghazālī achievements was systematically missed by those who had
> "pre-judged this period as a period of decline." — Saliba, ch. 7

## The Evidential Case Against the Narrative

Saliba's critique proceeds discipline by discipline:

### Astronomy

The post-Ghazālī period (after 1111) produced the most sophisticated theoretical astronomy in
the Islamic tradition. A continuous chain of astronomers — [[al-urdi|Muʿayyad al-Dīn
al-ʿUrḍī]] (d. 1266), [[nasir-al-din-tusi|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]] (d. 1274), [[qutb-al-din-al-shirazi|
Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī]] (d. 1311), Niẓām al-Dīn al-Nīsābūrī (d. 1328), [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn
al-Shāṭir]] (d. 1375), Ṣadr al-Sharīʿa al-Bukhārī (c. 1350), ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn al-Qushjī (d. 1474),
Mullā Fatḥallāh al-Shirwānī (c. 1450), and Shams al-Dīn al-Khafrī (d. 1550) — each engaged in
a cumulative critical project of constructing *alternative* planetary models, not merely
commentary. The key invention — the Ṭūsī Couple (1247/1260–61) — was born in this supposedly
declining period, as was ʿUrḍī's Lemma, and together they supplied the only non-Euclidean,
non-Ptolemaic mathematical tools that Copernicus used in *De Revolutionibus*.

Saliba had earlier characterized this period, in a separate book on Arabic planetary theories,
as "the golden age of Islamic astronomy":

> "In a separate book devoted to the study of one aspect of Arabic astronomy: the aspect of
> planetary theories, I went so far as to call that same age of decline the golden age of Islamic
> astronomy... That book, and the various articles that have appeared since then, dealing mainly
> with the work of the sixteenth-century astronomer Shams al-Dīn al-Khafrī, described an
> unparalleled originality during that period that would be difficult if not impossible to
> dismiss." — Saliba, ch. 7

### Medicine

Ibn al-Nafīs, writing a commentary on Avicenna's *Canon*, refuted Galenic doctrine on the basis
of observation and in so doing laid the conceptual groundwork for the eventual discovery of
pulmonary blood circulation. His characteristic formulation — "this is the common opinion, but
according to us, it is false" (*hadhā huwa al-raʾy al-mashhūr. wa-huwa ʿindanā bāṭil*) — echoes
the adversarial stance toward Greek authority characteristic of the best astronomers of the period.

### Optics

Kamāl al-Dīn al-Fārisī (d. 1320), guided by his teacher Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī, studied Ibn
al-Haytham's optics and surpassed it: he provided the first correct explanation of the rainbow,
through experimental instrumentation. This constituted an original scientific advance in the
supposed age of decline.

### Mechanical Engineering

Ismāʿīl al-Jazarī (c. 1206) — writing roughly a century after al-Ghazālī — exhibited a
sophistication far beyond the 9th-century Banū Mūsā. Where the Banū Mūsā designed machines to
entertain patrons, al-Jazarī explicitly theorized his devices as demonstrations of physical
principles, using Aristotelian concepts of actuality and potentiality to justify mechanical
design. His patron expected devices to instantiate (*istanbata*) — physical principles, not merely
entertain.

## The Commentary Medium

A subsidiary argument addresses the form of late Islamic scientific writing. Critics of the period
frequently condemned the prevalence of commentaries as a sign of intellectual stagnation — what
Professor Ibrāhīm Madkour (president of the Academy of the Arabic Language of Cairo) called
"sterile repetition and imitation." Saliba inverts this judgment:

Medieval scientific commentaries functioned as an equivalent of the modern specialist journal.
A new idea inserted into a commentary on a canonical text was automatically contextualized,
immediately reaching the relevant scholarly audience. The Ṭūsī Couple itself was first articulated
in a commentary (*Taḥrīr al-majisṭī*, 1247). Al-Khafrī produced two major commentaries before
distilling his innovations into an independent treatise titled *Ḥall mā lā yanḥall* ("Resolving
that which could not be resolved"). "One can say that it was the commentaries that gave rise to
originality rather than the other way around."

## The Mongol Paradox

The political narrative's strongest case — the sack of Baghdad 1258 — is undermined by the
most famous fact of that event: Hülegü Khan's vizier was Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī, the Maragha
astronomer. Ṭūsī arranged the preservation of approximately 400,000 manuscripts before the
assault, and subsequently persuaded the Ilkhanids to fund the [[maragha-observatory]]
— "one of the most elaborate observatories the Islamic world had ever known." The Ilkhanids
shortly converted to Islam. Far from terminating Islamic astronomy, the Mongol period created a
new institutional center for it. The diversification of centers — Diyār Bakr, Isfahan, Damascus,
Cairo — after the fall of the caliphate may have *widened* rather than contracted the productive
base of Islamic science.

## The Religion-Science Model Does Not Apply

Saliba notes that virtually every astronomer in the post-Ghazālī tradition cited held official
religious positions — judge, timekeeper, jurist — and many were more famous for religious writings
than scientific ones. ʿUrḍī (whose religious credentials are uncertain) is the sole possible
exception. The science-religion conflict model imported from European historiography is inapplicable:

> "The model of conflict between science and religion, which may have worked in Europe somehow,
> and I am not sure it did for it sounds too simplistic to contain the truth, this model does not
> seem to apply at least as far as the Islamic civilization is concerned." — Saliba, ch. 7

## Historiographic Consequences

The decline narrative was generated by two prior commitments:
- A European-centric teleology in which "progress" required the defeat of religious authority.
- A political history methodology that equated dynastic rupture with intellectual rupture.

Neither commitment was tested against the scientific texts themselves. The narrative's persistence
into the modern period — including among Arab intellectuals — reproduced the self-fulfilling
prophecy. The corrective requires reading the texts: "Any one who takes the time to read the
scientific production in the post-Ghazālī period would have to characterize this period as the
most fecund, and in the field of astronomy in particular completely unparalleled."
