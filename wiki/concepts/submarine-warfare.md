# Submarine Warfare

Submarine warfare here denotes the interwar (1919–1939) doctrinal problem of how navies should use — and defend against — the submersible warship, as analysed by Holger H. Herwig for Germany, Britain, and the United States. The submarine had divided opinion since its inception: "To its advocates, it constitutes a cheap but effective counterthreat to superior surface forces, the weapon of the poorer against the richer power"; to adversaries it was "an unfair and unsportsmanlike weapon, a weapon of piracy and barbarity." Herwig adopts the framing of [[military-innovation|military innovation]] in the interwar period and reaches a deliberately negative verdict on the submarine: it is the cautionary counter-case to the success stories of [[armored-warfare|armored warfare]], [[carrier-aviation|carrier aviation]], and [[amphibious-warfare|amphibious warfare]].

## "Innovation Ignored"

Herwig titles his study "Innovation Ignored" because the near-decisive U-boat campaign of 1917 — a clear, recent lesson — was nonetheless not acted upon. As he puts it, "in none of these conflicts did navies prepare for antisubmarine warfare; in all cases they preferred offensive strategies based on large surface units." The lesson cut against service culture, treaty politics, and the dominant Mahanian battle-fleet paradigm, and so it was set aside. All three navies "quickly forgot, or in the case of the Royal Navy literally destroyed, the 'lessons' of World War I with regard to antisubmarine warfare," and "the 'lessons' of 1917–1918 all had to be relearned at great cost during World War II." The convoy system, which had cut shipping losses to "about one in 120 (ninety-nine out of 12,008), or 0.83 percent" by mid-1918, was the central forgotten lesson.

## Germany

Germany was "the first power to conduct unrestricted submarine warfare and the only major power in the twentieth century to resort twice to this form of warfare." Yet "German naval doctrine emphasized construction of a Mahanian surface fleet and not U-boats." Article 191 of the Treaty of Versailles forbade German submarines, and in 1919 "Germany possessed no U-boats." Development went clandestine: through the Dutch front company I.v.S., secret "Special" and "Black" funds, and Arno Spindler's archive bureau, the navy stayed "abreast of foreign technological developments" while building boats abroad for Finland, Spain, Sweden, Turkey, and Japan. The Anglo-German Naval Agreement of 18 June 1935 gave Berlin "carte blanche from London to proceed with U-boat development."

Doctrine, however, lagged. Spindler "was a traditionalist who ignored the possible use of U-boats for commerce raiding in favor of support for the battle fleet." Raeder confirmed "his belief that the U-boats had failed to alter the last war's outcome" and held they could succeed "only if they operated in conjunction with the battle fleet" — the surface-fleet priority embodied in the Z-Plan. Dönitz, drilling crews in "night surface attacks, 'wolf pack' tactics against convoys," and wanting "a fleet of at least 300 boats," was an outlier: "not a single high-ranking officer shared Dönitz's concentration on a U-boat navy." Crucially, "Dönitz's 'fixes' were only relevant to the U-boat war as it had existed in 1918"; the Germans "failed to innovate in the larger sense," lacking "the operational endurance and intelligence base to attack Allied commerce in the open expanses of the Atlantic." Raeder conceded in the war diary on 3 September 1939 that Germany had already lost the Battle of the Atlantic.

## Britain

The Royal Navy "adopted a two-pronged policy" — publicly seeking abolition of submarines, privately retaining them and pressing for binding rules. Its fatal error was misplaced faith in ASDIC (the Anti-Submarine Detection Investigation Committee device), which officers "widely viewed as the critical deterrent to another U-boat campaign"; Churchill called it "the sacred treasure of the Admiralty." Yet secrecy "prevented realistic testing of the technology," and a "small band of antisubmarine officers at Portland" knew that "nine times out of ten, submarines were able to avoid detection by asdic." Hotham had warned it was "a 'gigantic bluff.'"

The deeper failure was forgetting the convoy. "The British basically forgot that convoys alone had played the crucial role in blunting the U-boat offensive in 1917." Convoys fell away because "naval professionals despised the dull and monotonous work of escorting convoys" and judged success by submarine "kills" rather than "the number of merchantmen that arrived safely in port." Service culture — an obsession with battleships and "refighting Jutland," a "sterile anti-intellectualism," and the Ten-Year Rule's fiscal "wet blanket" — meant commerce protection was neglected. The navy entered the war "with only 100 destroyers and thirty-eight submarines in service."

## United States

The U.S. Navy fought in both an antisubmarine campaign (the Atlantic) and a submarine campaign (the Pacific). Doctrine since 1911 split submarines between "coastal protection (defensive) and fleet operations (offensive)," and "the navy steadfastly clung to the notion that the 'primary task of the submarines is to attack enemy heavy ships'" — fleet-scouting and warship-killing, not commerce raiding. There was a sharp contradiction between interwar legal and moral opposition to unrestricted submarine warfare — "the national conscience" would never permit it — and later practice: on 7 December 1941 Stark ordered forces to "execute against Japan unrestricted air and submarine warfare," a campaign that sank "54.6 percent of all Japanese tonnage." Herwig shows "unrestricted submarine warfare was the de facto if not the de jure American naval strategy well before the attack on Pearl Harbor." America built "the best submarine of the war" (the Tambor class), but suffered "the real scandal" of the torpedo: the Bureau of Ordnance's magnetic and contact exploders were flawed, ran "eleven feet deeper than they were supposed to," and BuOrd "refused to test its torpedoes" — "not until summer 1943 would the U.S. submarines have torpedoes that worked."

## The Lesson for Military Innovation

The submarine is Herwig's cautionary counter-case for [[military-innovation|military innovation]]: a clear past lesson — the 1917 convoy/U-boat war — can still be *ignored* when it cuts against service culture, treaty politics, and the dominant battle-fleet paradigm. All three navies "were united in the belief that submarines could never constitute true sea power": "Mahan remained the prophet and *The Influence of Sea Power upon History* the Bible." That orthodoxy, plus fiscal parsimony and the conviction that "no civilized country would again resort to unrestricted submarine warfare," "worked to block innovation." When change came after 1939 and 1941, it was driven "less to their warnings ... than to the reality that submarines were all that remained to hurl against the enemy." As Herwig concludes, "war was truly the engine of innovation."

## Limitations and Critiques

This is a single-source analytical frame, and Herwig is explicit that postwar accounts distort the record: Dönitz's claim of a "lonely effort against the 'big ship' fanatics" must "be considered with a grain of salt," since "Dönitz shared much of Raeder's vision" in 1935–1936. The chapter is weighted toward doctrine and institutional culture rather than technology, which Herwig calls "never a determining factor." Its three-navy comparison excludes France, Japan, and Italy except in passing, and treats the U.S. Atlantic antisubmarine effort only briefly ("the less said about the American preparations the better"). The "innovation ignored" thesis is itself a judgment about counterfactual preparedness — how much better the war might have gone with earlier convoy and antisubmarine investment — and so shares the contestability of all such verdicts.
