## Definition and Scope

Islamic theology of creation addresses the unique relationship between the one God and everything else that exists. The qur'anic "creator paradigm," as formulated by Ian Netton and invoked throughout Muslim reflection, embraces a God who: (1) creates ex nihilo; (2) acts definitively in historical time; (3) guides His people in such time; and (4) can in some way be known indirectly by His creation (source: [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 7, David B. Burrell).

The central difficulty is the "ontological divide" which creation introduces: if God is not one of the things He creates, "what sort of a thing is God?" No sort of thing — yet this distinction resists conceptualization. Every attempt to articulate the creator-creature relationship using human categories risks reducing it to something comprehensible, thereby undermining tawhid. As Burrell notes, "any pretension to have articulated the founding relation adequately will have reduced that relation to one comprehensible to us, and so undermine and nullify the distinction expressed by tawhid."

The plurality of Islamic accounts of creation is thus not a failure of Muslim thought but a fidelity to the irreducible difficulty of the subject.

---

## Key Conceptual Problems

Four interlocking conundra define the field:

1. **Eternity of the world**: If God is eternal and everything else temporal, how does the act of creating bridge that chasm? Did the universe always exist (as falsafa implied) or was there an initial moment of time?
2. **Divine freedom vs. necessity**: Can an eternal God act freely in time without implying change in the divine essence?
3. **Causation and divine sovereignty**: If God creates all things, what room remains for creaturely causation? If creatures truly cause effects, does this limit divine sovereignty?
4. **Human agency and moral responsibility**: If God creates all things including human actions, how can creatures be held responsible? How can divine action be imputed to human agents?

The last problem is the "crux because it directly affects human lives, and also seems to prove that any metaphysical account which tries to be faithful to the original revelation will end up undermining the point of that very revelation."

## Kalam Schools

### Mutazilite Atomism

Early Mutazilite reflection (emanating from Basra, probably an indigenous Islamic development rather than a Hellenic import) proposed a distinctive ontology: the cosmos is composed of primitive atoms (jawahir farda), indivisible particles whose configurations constitute the apparent substances of the world. The creator creates the atoms directly; the configurations of atoms display God's ongoing conserving agency.

This atomist ontology served the Mutazilite theological agenda in two ways:

1. It prevented the cosmos from having inherent consistency or internal dynamic that might suggest independence from God. Unlike Aristotelian substances with formal and final causes, atoms are bare primitives dependent entirely on divine arrangement.
2. It allowed the Mutazilites to locate authentic agency exclusively in uncaused origination. In Richard Frank's formulation: "the programmed sequence of sufficient causes and fulfilled conditions represented in the causation of the 'illa and asbab does not offer an adequate model for an explanation of the grounding of the possible that exists."

Crucially, the Mutazilites equated **authentic action with creating**: any genuine agent must be an originator (mukhtar'). This identification gave them a way to preserve human moral responsibility — human beings are genuine agents of their own acts because they truly originate them. But it also logically excluded God from causing evil (since God originates only what is good) and created a theology of human independence that the Asharites found incompatible with qur'anic divine sovereignty.

### Asharite Occasionalism and Kasb

[[al-ashari]]'s school made two decisive shifts from Mutazilism. First, it radicalized divine sovereignty: God creates all things including human actions. Nothing obligates God; divine rewards are pure favor (thawab), not entitlements. Second, it invented a doctrine to reconcile divine creation of actions with human moral responsibility: **kasb** (acquisition) or **iktisab**.

In the Asharite scheme:
- God creates each human action by creating in the human agent a "power" (qudra) specific to that act.
- The human being "acquires" (yaksib) the act that God creates — not as an originator, but as the locus (mahall) in which power is created.
- The act is "created as belonging to another, not by God as His own act" (Richard Frank), so it can be imputed to the human agent.

This concept is widely acknowledged as philosophically awkward. Burrell describes it as attempting "to formulate the relation between creating agent and created agent, using the crude instrument of a created power to perform this act." Modern commentators (including Muhammad 'Abduh and Rashid Rida) affirmed its intent: "By their acquisition [kasb], human beings are indeed autonomous agents, yet hardly independent; they are only agents because God wills it and creates them as free agents."

The connection to [[occasionalism-islamic]] is direct: the Asharite position tends toward a view in which there are no genuine creaturely causes at all, only God's direct action at each moment.

### Maturidi Parallel

The [[al-maturidi|Maturidi school]] (Samarkand; traceable to Abu Mansur al-Maturidi's Kitab al-Tawhid) reaffirmed "the twin assertions that human beings are truly the agents of their actions, while these actions are at the same time created by God." The Maturidis insisted that the divine act of takwin (bringing into existence) is eternal and distinct from the existing things it produces — a point of controversy with Asharism — and avoided the ambiguous kasb/iktisab language to account for human free action.

## Falsafa on Origination

### Farabian Emanation

Al-Farabi adapted Plotinus' Neoplatonic emanation scheme to articulate creation: all things flow necessarily from the One as from a unitary and immensely fruitful grounding principle. The governing dictum "from one only one can come" (ex uno non fit nisi unum) dictated a step-wise emanation through nine celestial intelligences following the actual cosmological pattern. The world did not begin in time; it has always been proceeding from the One.

This elegant model served the "philosophers' intent" of moving thought beyond a God who makes individual things to the very "cause of being." But it entailed a necessary, eternal cosmos incompatible with the qur'anic creator paradigm's insistence on creation in time and divine freedom.

### Avicennan Necessary Being

Avicenna systematized Farabi's scheme. His ontological distinction of **necessary** from **contingent** being:

- **Contingent being** (mumkin bi-dhatihi): has potentiality to exist or not; "false in itself" and "true due to something other than itself"
- **Necessary being** (wajib al-wujud bi-dhatihi): "true in itself," source of its own being, has no quiddity/essence (mahiyya) other than existence (wujud), is One because any differentia would require a cause beyond itself

The Necessary Existent necessarily implies the existence of an emanated world. "It is not 'logically' plausible that God exists and that the world does not exist." This necessary emanation was the chief target of Ghazali's critique: it eliminates divine freedom, reduces God to an impersonal metaphysical principle, and makes salvation dependent on intellectual achievement rather than grace.

Avicenna also held that God knows particulars only "in a universal way" to preserve divine immutability — a view Ghazali identified as tantamount to unbelief, since "there does not escape from Him the weight of an atom in the heavens or the earth" (Qur'an 34:3).

## Later Kalam Theologians

### Ghazali's Constructive Response

[[al-ghazali]] attacked the falasifa in his Incoherence of the Philosophers (see [[tahafut-al-falasifa]]) on three counts warranting the charge of unbelief (kufr): (1) denial of bodily resurrection; (2) claim that God knows only universals; (3) **maintaining the eternity of the world, past and future**.

But the destructive Ghazali was not the only Ghazali. His constructive account of creation, developed in his Revival of the Religious Sciences (Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din), absorbed considerable Avicennan ontology while deploying it differently — grounded in the sunnat Allah (God's "custom" or "way" with creation) rather than logical necessity:

> "There is no agent but God Most High ... a human being is an agent in one sense, and God is an agent in another. The sense in which God Most High is agent is that He is the originator [mukhtar'] of existing things, while the sense in which a human being is an agent is that he is the locus [mahall] in which power is created after will has been created after knowledge has been created."

Ghazali's solution to the double-agency problem uses an analogy: "the emir killed someone, and also said that the executioner killed him; in one sense, the emir is the killer and in another sense, the executioner." The term "agent" is irreducibly analogous; there is no univocal sense covering both divine and human agency. Divine "determining" by the "divine decree" (qadar) remains inexpressible and cannot be assimilated to human determining.

Ghazali held that the universe is "the best possible" — a claim whose reception was examined by Eric Ormsby in Theodicy in Islamic Thought (Princeton, 1984). He resisted both Asharite created-volition and Avicennan necessitarian scheme, deploying the latter only as metaphor for divine ordering whose ultimate character remains beyond conceptualization.

### Fakhr al-Din al-Razi

[[fakhr-al-din-al-razi]] (d. 1209), the most philosophically sophisticated later kalam theologian, pushed the double-agency analysis to its logical conclusion. In his commentary on Qur'an 6:102 ("Creator of all things"):

> "Action depends on motivation which is created by God. And when power and motivation are joined, the action necessarily occurs. Now this requires that God be the Creator of the creatures' acts."

Gimaret summarizes: Razi "does not hesitate to declare himself a Jabrite" — affirming that because acts can occur only if God creates the power and motivation, the combination necessarily brings about the act. Razi acknowledged this created severe difficulties for theodicy and moral responsibility, concluding: "It is as though this question is located in a field of contradiction ... Let us ask God Most High to bring us to the truth of it."

## Mystical and Illuminationist Traditions

### Suhrawardi and Illuminationism (Ishraq)

Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi (d. 1191) replaced Aristotelian substance with light as the fundamental ontological category. The metaphor of light allowed him to account for emanation from the One while "finessing the necessitarian implications of Avicenna's scheme" — light emanates freely, not by logical compulsion. Spiritual exercises were integral to this philosophical inquiry (under the influence of Ghazali's path and the ancient tradition Pierre Hadot analyzed as "philosophy as a way of life").

### Ibn 'Arabi and Mulla Sadra

[[ibn-arabi]] provided the mystical foundation for the most sophisticated late Islamic account of creation. His cosmogony held that the divine word (kalimat) on its descent manifests as "throne," "footstool," then "ruling" (hukm) and "report" (khabar) — each pair further splitting into the categories of divine law. "The law as a particular manifestation of the divine Word constitutes [humans] by way of word." The creator-creature relationship is a relationship of divine Names to themselves in the locus of created beings: "one divine name prescribes the Law for another divine name, addressing it within the locus of an engendered servant."

[[mulla-sadra]] (1572–1640) reoriented Suhrawardi's legacy by giving primacy to existence (wujud) over essence (mahiyya), drawing on both Ibn 'Arabi and Avicenna's essence-existence distinction:

> "Contingent beings need something proper to them constituting what they are in themselves [huwiyyat], for should one consider them apart from the One who originates them by that very fact they must be considered to be empty and impossible."

In Mulla Sadra's system, creation is the bestowal of existing: God alone exists in Himself, while everything else exists "from God." Existence plays the role light played for Suhrawardi. The creator-creature relation is a "non-reciprocal relation of dependence" — non-duality — that need not be necessitarian (as in Avicenna) but can be thoroughly intentional. This represents the most developed Islamic metaphysical account of creation, connecting Shia philosophical reflection to Sunni kalam and falsafa traditions.

## The Eternity-of-the-World Dispute

The clearest fault line between falsafa and kalam was the question of whether the cosmos had a beginning in time:

- **Falsafa** (Farabi, Avicenna): The world is coeternal with God; it has always been proceeding from the Necessary Existent. Time itself had no beginning.
- **Kalam** (Mutazilite, Asharite, Maturidi): The world was created in time; there was an initial moment. This follows from both qur'anic revelation and the kalam proof that an actual infinite temporal regress is impossible (huduth al-'alam argument).

Ghazali identified "maintaining the eternity of the world, past and future" as one of the three positions warranting the charge of kufr. Averroes defended the falasifa and attacked Ghazali in his Incoherence of the Incoherence (Tahafut al-Tahafut) — which, by preserving Ghazali's text in full while excoriating it, may ironically have reinforced Ghazali's influence.

## Atomism and Occasionalism

The Mutazilite and Asharite atom doctrines connect directly to the occasionalist account of causation: see [[occasionalism-islamic]]. The key move: if atoms are primitive metaphysical particles without inherent formal or final causes, then the regular sequences we observe in nature are not produced by intrinsic natures of things but by God's habitual arrangement of atoms at each moment. "Nature does not act of itself but is used as an instrument by its Creator" (Ghazali). Regular causal sequences are God's "custom" (sunna, or sunnat Allah), not natural necessity.

This occasionalist reading was not inevitable from the atom doctrine but was the direction Asharism took it. The Mutazilites retained a stronger notion of creaturely causation (consistent with their doctrine of human agency as genuine origination).

---

## Resolution Status

No single account achieved consensus. The dominant Sunni position became Asharite — affirming creation ex nihilo, divine freedom, a temporal cosmos, and the kasb doctrine for human agency — but the kasb doctrine was always contested as philosophically inadequate. Mulla Sadra's synthesis (17th century) represents the most philosophically sophisticated resolution, but remained within a specifically Shia philosophical tradition. The tension between divine sovereignty and human responsibility is acknowledged in the tradition as an irreducible mystery: "the secret of the divine decree which confuses the many."

## Historiography

The falsafa/kalam divide on creation has been extensively studied: Richard Frank's Creation and the Cosmic System: Al-Ghazali and Avicenna (Heidelberg, 1992) is the definitive treatment of Ghazali's complex debt to Avicenna. Eric Ormsby's Theodicy in Islamic Thought (Princeton, 1984) examines Ghazali's "best possible world" claim. Gimaret's Théories de l'acte humain (Paris, 1980) covers the full range of kalam positions on human agency. The chapter by Burrell in Winter's Cambridge Companion provides the most concise English-language map of the field.

A persistent historiographical debate concerns whether Ghazali's Incoherence effectively "closed" falsafa in Islam: the consensus view (that it did) has been substantially revised; the "constructive Ghazali" who absorbed Avicennan ontology into kalam was at least as influential as the polemical one.

The collection is strong for the kalam period (8th–13th century) through the Cambridge Companion and specialist monographs. Mulla Sadra (17th century) and later Shia philosophy are less well covered; this represents a gap in the existing wiki pages.
