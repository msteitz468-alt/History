# Augustan Religion and the Restructuring of Roman Cult

The transformation of Roman state religion under **[[augustus|Augustus]]** — presented at the
time as a **"restoration"** of neglected ancestral cult, but in reality a **restructuring** of
the whole religious system around the person of the emperor. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]]
Ch. 16 (**S. R. F. Price**).

## Religion of place

Price's organising theme is that Roman religion was bound to **place** — above all the city of
Rome and its sacred boundary, the **pomerium**. Roman myth was "myth of place," recounting the
history of the *site* of Rome in an unbroken sequence (Hercules and the Ara Maxima; the Parilia
and the founding of the city; Romulus, Remus, and the killing that the violation of the boundary
"justified"). The late-Republican and Augustan age **privileged the historicizing
interpretation** that tied each festival to the founding of Rome (Livy, Dionysius, Ovid's
*Fasti*), against the background of a fear (raised by Caesar, revived by Antony and Cleopatra)
that the seat of empire might move East — which Augustus answered by promoting **Rome as the
eternal capital**. The pomerium remained the central dividing line (civil vs. military
authority, the ban on burial within it), redefined to accommodate the emperor's combined
*imperium*, and explicitly tied to the empire's frontiers — Claudius and Vespasian extending it
"having increased the boundaries of the Roman people."

## "Restoration" as restructuring

Price attacks two old orthodoxies as **false dichotomies**:

1. **Decline and revival.** The notion that Republican religion was in *decline* and Augustus
   artificially *revived* it (meaningful energy supposedly lying in "oriental cults" or
   Christianity) "now seems very fragile." Republican religion suffered **disruption, not
   decline**, and the talk of "revival" obscures the extent of genuine **change**. Augustan
   stress on restoration was not mere obfuscation: the age was "fundamentally concerned to relate
   the present to the past."
2. **Restoration vs. innovation (the imperial cult).** The conventional split — traditional
   cults restored *plus* a novel ruler-cult — also "disappears." The ancestral cults were not
   simply restored but **restructured**; and ruler-cult in Rome was, in many aspects (apotheosis,
   the place of the founder), **deeply traditional**. Both belong to one process of restructuring
   around the *princeps*, "sometimes seen as the new Romulus."

## The instruments of restructuring

- The **rebuilding of temples** and revival of vacant **priesthoods** (Augustus held all the
  major colleges; *pontifex maximus* from 12 BCE), and the famous claim to have restored 82
  temples.
- The reorganization of the **ward cults** (the *compita*, with the *Lares Augusti* and the
  *Genius* of Augustus) — bringing the system among the **poorer citizens** and freedmen
  (Price stresses the "official/popular" opposition is a continuum, not two separate religions).
- The revival of the **Secular Games** (17 BCE) as a marker of a new age.
- The **codification of sacred law** — a neglected achievement: jurists and antiquarians
  (Antistius Labeo, Ateius Capito, Veranius) wrote systematic treatises on pontifical and augural
  law in the Augustan–Tiberian period, after which "subsequent work was unnecessary."

## The emperor at the centre

The poets (Horace, Virgil, c. 28 BCE) framed the recent civil wars as the fruit of **religious
neglect** whose remedy "lay in the hands of one man." The proposal to rename Octavian
**"Romulus"** (rejected as too regal and tainted by fratricide, in favour of **"Augustus,"** of
august augury) shows the religious recasting of the founder. Price extends the analysis to the
empire: Roman cults were **replicated** across Italy and the provinces (in colonies and the
army), so the relation of empire to Rome is not only "the imperial cult" but the whole range of
**Roman cult** carried outward.

## Contested

Price's account is itself revisionist, overturning the older Warde Fowler / Latte model of
decline-and-artificial-revival. Debates persist over whether Rome ever had a native
**mythology** (Dumézil's Indo-European thesis vs. the "no myths" orthodoxy), and over how far the
restructured system penetrated the **lower classes** and the non-citizen ethnic cults of the
metropolis.

## OHCW (Stockton; Wilson; Lyne)

**Stockton** (Ch. 22): elected *pontifex maximus* 12 BCE; *divi filius*; cult of genius/*numen* and temples to Rome and Augustus in the provinces; living Augustus never formally a god in Italy/West; rebuilt temples; Secular Games 17 BCE; legislation on adultery, marriage incentives, sumptuary restraint — religious renaissance paired with moral programme claiming restoration of ancestral seriousness.

**Wilson** (Ch. 32): eighty-two temples built/restored in one year claimed; Ara Pacis Augustae (dedicated 9 BCE) and Forum of Augustus (2 BCE) as propaganda climax — processional friezes of 13 BCE thanksgiving; values *gravitas*, *humanitas*, *pax*; Augustus as new Aeneas; Greek craftsmen essential for marble expertise.

**Lyne** (Ch. 24): second Augustan period (~from 20 BCE) more assertive monarchy; *Carmen saeculare* (17) as ritual lyric of the restored order. Source: [[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986]].
