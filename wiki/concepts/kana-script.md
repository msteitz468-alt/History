# Kana (Japanese Syllabary)

The phonetic syllabary — or rather two syllabaries, **hiragana** and **katakana** — by which
the Japanese language was finally freed from total dependence on Chinese logographic
writing. Kana is the technical precondition for the entire [[heian-literature|vernacular
literature]] of the [[heian-period|Heian period]], including the [[kokinshu|Kokinshū]],
the [[tale-of-genji|Tale of Genji]], and the *Pillow Book*. Documented in the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian|Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 2]].

## Development

The process that produced kana unfolded over three centuries:

1. **Man'yōgana** (8th century): Chinese characters were used purely phonetically —
   for their sound value, ignoring their meaning — to write Japanese proper nouns, poems,
   and grammatical particles. Named for their prominence in the *Man'yōshū* (Collection of
   Ten Thousand Generations, mid-8th century). These characters were still written in
   their full, complex forms.

2. **Sogana** (early Heian): Man'yōgana characters written in the flowing **grass style**
   (sōsho) of Chinese calligraphy, producing simplified, cursive forms. Early Heian writers
   used these alongside the older full forms.

3. **Hiragana** (mature form by early 11th century): Further streamlining of sogana
   produced a relatively small set of standardized syllabic signs — "a syllabary (*kana*)
   similar to the modern one except for the survival of numerous alternative forms for
   most sounds." This syllabary was used primarily for **poetry, romances, and women's
   diaries**, and was also known as the **woman's hand** (*onnade*), since women, for whom
   formal Chinese study was considered unsuitable, drove its development and made it
   the medium of literary expression.

4. **Katakana**: A parallel utilitarian syllabary, derived by extracting abbreviated elements
   from Chinese characters rather than by cursive simplification. It "dates from the same
   general period" but "developed as a utilitarian system of notation outside the mainstream
   of Heian culture" — used chiefly for glossing Chinese texts and religious documents,
   not for literary composition.

## Social and gender dimensions

Most scholars assume that women led the development of hiragana as a practical writing
system, since they needed a script for poetry and letters but were excluded from formal
Chinese education. Men had strong incentives to write officially in kanbun
([[chinese-learning-heian|Chinese]] literary prose); women, excluded from that prestige
track, created the alternative. The result was a remarkable **gender inversion**: men
held official, public, Chinese-language authority; women, writing in the new vernacular
script, produced the enduring literary masterpieces. As the *Cambridge History* notes,
"There is evidence that kana were used to some extent by men during the period of
gestation," but the public prestige and creative leadership belonged to women.

## Kana calligraphy

The new script generated its own calligraphic art. The canonical masters are the
**Three Calligraphers** (*sanseki*) of the **wayo** (Japanese) style:
- **Ono no Michikaze** (Tōfu, 894–966): pioneered a graceful, simplified approach to
  Chinese characters that diverged from the orthodox vigor of the Three Brushes tradition
  (Kūkai, Emperor Saga, Tachibana no Hayanari).
- **Fujiwara no Sukemasa** (944–998): carried naturalization further.
- **Fujiwara no Yukinari** (972–1027): the most influential; his "gentle, smooth brushwork"
  became the classic model transmitted by the **Sesonji school** for generations. Yukinari
  also pioneered the classic kana style, "linking individual kana in a long series of graceful
  loops and curves."

The supreme examples of kana calligraphy in its golden age include:
- **Koyagire** ("Koya fragments," ca. 1100?): scrolls containing portions of the
  [[kokinshu|Kokinshū]], written in classic hiragana cursive.
- **Genji monogatari emaki** (12th-century Genji scroll): combined kana calligraphy with
  yamato-e painting.
- **Sanjūrokunin shū** ("Collection of Thirty-Six Poets," ca. 1110–1120?): celebrated for
  its combination of calligraphy, literature, painting, and decorated paper — an integration
  of the arts that is the hallmark of late Heian aesthetic culture.

## Significance

The Cambridge History of Japan identifies kana as "the pivotal event" of the late ninth
century, enabling:
- The social rise of **waka** poetry as the dominant aristocratic form.
- The development of **narrative prose fiction** (*monogatari*) and the literary diary (*nikki*).
- A "truly Chinese-style society" in a paradoxical way: by making poetic expression
  accessible in Japanese, kana integrated literary culture into daily aristocratic life as
  deeply as Chinese poetry was integrated into Chinese court life — but in the native tongue.

The deeper importance of kana is that it made the [[heian-literature|literature]] of the
Heian period possible as we know it: the [[kokinshu|Kokinshū]] was compiled in kana and
Chinese together; the [[tale-of-genji|Tale of Genji]] was written entirely in hiragana; and
the genre of the literary diary (*nikki*) depended on kana for its intimate, first-person
voice.

## Historiography

The CHJ treatment emphasizes the paradox that a cultural move apparently "away from
China" (toward a native script) actually advanced Sinification in a deeper sense, by
making literary culture as pervasive and as integral to social life as it was in China.
The gender dimension is treated as historically significant: women's exclusion from
kanbun culture created the social niche in which the new script — and the great
vernacular literature — flourished.
