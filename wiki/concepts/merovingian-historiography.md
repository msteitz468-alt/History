# Merovingian Historiography (History-Writing)

**How the post-Roman Franks wrote history — and why no single royal master narrative emerged until the
Carolingians.** Helmut Reimitz's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 22) analyzes the three great Merovingian
histories — [[gregory-of-tours|Gregory of Tours]]'s *Decem Libri Historiarum*, the Chronicle of
[[fredegar|Fredegar]], and the *Liber Historiae Francorum* — as competing experiments in writing a
distinctly post-Roman, Christian, and increasingly Frankish past. This is the practice of history-
writing itself; for the longue durée of how the period was later interpreted, see
[[merovingian-studies-historiography]].

## Definition and Scope

The Merovingian world inherited "a rich and varied historiographical legacy from the Roman world" in
which history sat "within an imperial framework" — Eusebius arranging peoples as columns (*fila
regnorum*) that "collapsed into one single column of a Christian Roman world." Merovingian scribes copied
Jerome's chronicle but **none continued it**, showing "a growing understanding that it was necessary to
write distinct histories for a distinct time — a time that was not 'Roman time.'"

## Gregory of Tours: The First Post-Roman History

Gregory wrote "the first comprehensive post-Roman history written in Gaul." His treatment of Rome was
radical: instead of listing emperors, he counted "the succession of persecutors of the church," so that
"the history of Roman rule and rulers appeared only as a pale background to the history of Christian
saints, martyrs, and bishops." He emphasized continuity "not with Rome but… with the long past of
Christian Gaul," producing "a completely novel historical narrative." A lifelong project (finished after
594), the *Histories* ran from Adam to his own day, with Gregory himself "an acting and speaking
character." He called his own work *mixteconfusequae* — "a messy and confused order" — and famously
conjured future copyists, "by Judgment Day," never to abridge or rewrite it. They did anyway: a **six-book
abridgment** (omitting Books VII–X) circulated within a generation, and a Carolingian nine-book version
added Fredegar's Fourth Book as a tenth — though the complete text survived to check the reworkings.

## The Chronicle of Fredegar

Compiled c. 660 in the eastern kingdoms "in a milieu with strong connections to the… [[pippinids|
Pippinid]] family," the Chronicle is a "chain of chronicles" — Hippolytus, Isidore, a reworked Jerome and
Hydatius, Gregory's six-book version, then the compilers' own "Fourth Book" to the 640s. Its key
innovation reversed Eusebius: Jerome's synoptic columns became "a linear text," so "every story about a
community or a people could be considered as a discrete history or 'file'… world history as a series of
peoples and kingdoms which Roman imperial history never absorbed." The **Trojan origin myth** of the
Franks "most clearly illustrate[s] this historiographical tactic." See [[fredegar]].

## The Liber Historiae Francorum

Finished 726/727 by an anonymous author "probably a monk or a nun in Soissons," the LHF "rewrote
[biblical *historia*] as a history of the Franks… present[ing] the Franks as the new chosen people,"
their heroes "avatars of a militant Israel." It gave an uninterrupted Trojan-to-present king list
including the early king Faramundus, and openly rewrote Gregory, dismissing "contradictory and incoherent
reports."

## The Structural Argument

Reimitz's central claim: all three historians "positioned themselves as talking *to*, not *from*, the
centers of royal power." Merovingian kings stood "equidistant from the different groups," "interested
neither in imposing a historical master narrative of their own… nor in supporting stronger historical
profiles of any other social or political group." Only with the Carolingians did "the kings and their
historians offer… a shared past and a common future" — the rearranged, continued Fredegar chronicle
helping "legitimate the usurpation of the Frankish throne." This frames history-writing as a key arena of
[[ethnogenesis|Frankish identity-formation]].
