# Medieval Castle

## Definition and Scope

The medieval castle (Latin *castrum*, *castellum*, or vernacular equivalents; in England often "castle" vs. simple "fortified house") was the characteristic fortified residence of the landed elite in Western Europe and the Latin East during the High Middle Ages. It was not primarily a purpose-built military fortress in the modern or Roman sense but a proprietorial centre whose form and location were dictated by the needs of estate administration, social domination, and personal security in an age of limited central government.

**Emic perspective**: Contemporaries described a wide variety of structures with overlapping terms; fortifications were often "strong houses" or ringworks that served domestic functions. The castle was the "stately home of its age" for its proprietor.

**Etic perspective** (modern historiography): Historians have sometimes over-emphasised strategic siting on routes or passes. In reality, as France demonstrates, "Castles were built where proprietors had land."

## Core Characteristics

### Home and Estate Centre First

Castles originated from the requirement to store renders in kind and travel to consume them on scattered estates. Insecurity made fortification necessary.

> "Castles were many things at different times, but at heart they were the homes of the great and centres of their estate administration and government. The European elite had estates which, because they were acquired by death, inheritance, marriage, gift and conquest, were scattered. Rents were largely in kind and transport poor, so it was simpler for kings and lords to store renders in kind where they were collected, and to travel around eating them. In an age of insecurity, such residences needed to be fortified. It was these domestic needs that determined the location, and to a very large extent the form, of the castle: it was the stately home of its age." (Ch. 7, France)

They functioned as local government centres:

> "Castles were centres of estate management and, therefore, local government where lords or their deputies held court." (Ch. 7, France)

In the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem, studies emphasise "their many functions, especially as centres of seigniorial administration." Castle-towns often developed around them: "the castle-town 'is almost the classic form of the medieval town in western and central Europe'."

### Military Potential: Defensive and Offensive

While domestic in origin, castles had real military value because of the technical limits of contemporary warfare: attackers had to come face-to-face.

> "The castle was necessary because of the limited competence of medieval government. It was effective because of the technical limitations of the means of war, which meant that men had to come face-to-face before they could fight. ... Moreover, a fortification was a secure base for attack and a garrison, especially if mounted, could threaten the country round about. In fact, it is a mistake to see the castle as simply a defensive structure, because it could menace enemies as well as protect friends." (Ch. 7, France)

The motte-and-bailey exemplified the social structure:

> "The bailey could serve as a protective enclosure for the people, livestock and goods of the area in time of war: this was the public function of the castle derived from the past. But in the end the really strong protection was afforded to the owner, the lord and his family and close dependants in the tower, which could usually be cut off from the rest. This was a compact fortification, economic of manpower for its defence, and capable of being built and supported by local resources." (Ch. 7, France)

### Siting by Landholding, Not Strategy

Location followed proprietorial patterns, not abstract geography:

> "Modern visitors often wonder why a castle is built in a particular location which does not dominate routes, passes or rivercrossings: the answer is that it was built where the owner had land important enough to defend in this elaborate way, and it was sometimes influenced by claims that he was pursuing against others, or enmities that he had incurred." (Ch. 7, France)

Examples: Kerak and Showbak in fertile enclaves; counts of Champagne guarding holdings along Seine, Marne, Aube with specific castles; Clare family centres at Tonbridge and Clare.

Controlling castles was essential to power:

> "Everywhere, the stability of power depended on controlling castles. To remain dominant, a king or lord needed castles to protect his demesnes, to keep the loyalty of followers and to overawe enemies." (Ch. 7, France)

Princes attempted to regulate building (e.g., counts of Champagne limiting vassals to two castles; counts of Anjou controlling castellans), but complete prevention was impossible, especially during successions or minorities.

### Evolution of Form

Early structures were earth and timber (ringworks, mottes). Stone became more common from mid-twelfth century, driven by cost and status as much as military need. Experimentation with round towers (inspired partly by Roman models and Armenian practice) and integrated concentric defences (pioneered in the Latin East at Belvoir, later at Crac, Marqab, and by Edward I in Wales) marked advanced designs by the thirteenth century.

Multiple lines of defence, machicolations, carefully sited arrow-loops, and oblique approaches became standard in the most sophisticated examples. However, variety remained the norm because "they represented the wishes and needs of diverse proprietors who were normally restrained only by cost and the limitations imposed by the site."

### Cost and Exclusivity

Building and maintaining castles tied them to local resources. Castleguard obligations were common. Only the greatest could own multiple castles. In England, baronial incomes often made elaborate stone castles unaffordable for all but the richest; royal control of building was tighter than in France or the Empire.

## Role in War

The spread of castles shaped the character of war:

> "The spread of castles intensified the basic character of war. The fact that a defender could take refuge in a castle decreased the attractions of battle and increased the need to ravage as an act of war. But castles were much more than merely defensive places of refuge. A garrison of mounted knights could harass enemies." (Ch. 7, France)

In "Castles and war" (Ch. 8), France shows that at the start of the period "war was primarily a matter of plunder, destruction and skirmishing. The difficulties inherent in an attack on even minor fortifications simply intensified this."

The 1184–85 Hainaut campaign under Baldwin V illustrates the castle strategy in action: no major battles or sieges occurred; defence relied on garrisoning and strengthening castles, scorched-earth tactics, and political factors. The "castle strategy was the rock of Baldwin’s defence."

> "The great value of the castle was that it held down the land; without taking it there could be no secure mastery of the land that it dominated. No castle could hold out indefinitely, but its strength could pin down an attacker and make him vulnerable to the attentions of a friendly field-army." (Ch. 8, France)

Networks mattered more than isolated strongpoints: "It was the combination of a network of castles and cities with a field-army which was the great strength of the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem."

By the late twelfth century, "it was only rarely that a single fortress could exert decisive influence." The real strength lay in "the garrison within it and their political connections beyond."

## Limitations and Critiques

Not all fortifications were "castles" in the elite sense; simple wooden structures or town walls performed similar roles. Fashion, status display (lofty halls, large windows), and comfort often competed with pure military efficiency. In the thirteenth century, some structures (e.g., Frederick II’s Castel del Monte) were primarily residential or symbolic.

The model is strongest for Western Europe and the Latin East; it explains the conservatism of change and the dominance of raid/siege over battle. It does not claim that castles were impregnable or that strategy was absent—only that proprietorial realities constrained and shaped what was built and how war was waged.

## Historiography

France's analysis privileges the social and economic logic of landholding over purely technological or "great man" accounts of castle development. It builds on earlier work on *incastellamento* (Italy) and motte-and-bailey studies but stresses the domestic/administrative primacy and offensive potential against the older "defensive only" or "strategic network" views.

Primary sources rarely distinguish clearly between "strong house" and castle; archaeological evidence (Goltho, Doué-la-Fontaine, Hen Domen) shows continuous adaptation rather than clean typological progression. Modern study benefits from excavation but must account for the fact that most castles were modest and short-lived.

Collection coverage for this topic is strong in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina via specialist monographs and Cambridge series on medieval military institutions.

## City Fortifications and the Attack on Castles/Cities (Ch. 9 evidence)

City walls followed the same principles as castle defences and were not, "in principle, very different from those of castles." Double walls inherited or imitated at Crema, Antioch, Jerusalem, Acre (deep ditch between); concentric patterns at Carcassonne (1228–39) and shortly after at Oxford. Advanced features (machicolations, angled arrow-slits, sally-gates, steeply raked talus, stone-lined ditches) emulated in St Louis's Aigues-Mortes and the walls of Conway/Caernarfon. Barbicans common additions at weak points. "Such complex structures were probably as rare as concentric castles, although the strengthening of city defences with stone walls was a feature of the thirteenth century."

Citadels frequently formed distinct entities within urban defences (Tower of David on Jerusalem's west perimeter; Château de Narbonne at Toulouse, later isolated by demolitions; Templar castle cut off Montmusard suburb at Acre). At Antioch the remote citadel "had no influence whatsoever" during the crusader siege but held out and later admitted Kerbogah. "Citadels on the perimeter sometimes strengthened the defences of a city, although Antioch was an exception. ... existence, like those of keeps in castles, must have given confidence to those of the defenders who might find an ultimate refuge in them. However, citadels often represented the menace of some outside ruling power and were intended to hold down the city rather than defend it, notably in early Norman England."

"In the end it was the garrison, not the walls, that mattered." Surprise and internal dissension were decisive (Frederick II's seizure of Vicenza 1236 via sympathizers and absent troops; Moissac 1212 where citizens lacked will; Parma 1247 via exiles). When surprise failed, mining proved "the most consistently successful tactic used against fortifications" (Rochester 1215 keep undermined with pig-fat props burned; Bedford 1224; Crac des Chevaliers 1271; Marqab 1285). Countermeasures existed (batters, cisterns) but required skilled labour and favourable soil.

No engine transformed the balance: "No machine in this period actually altered the balance of advantage between attack and defence." The decisive requirement remained isolating the target with overwhelming numbers plus sustained political will and supplies.
