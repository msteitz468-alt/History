## Definition and Scope

African music history, as a scholarly field, analyses the development of musical forms and practices across Africa during the modern period (c.1800–present) and their relationship to colonialism, nationalism, urbanization, state power, globalization, and religious change. As Veit Erlmann argues in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], the history of African music is "one of displaced correlation in which the relationship between historical sequence, social structure, cultural practice, and expressive forms cannot be subsumed under an overarching logic."

The field is distinct from ethnomusicology's earlier focus on mapping "traditional" African music as timeless and primordial. It treats "the modern" not as something imposed from outside, but as a dynamic category generated from within, characterized by fluid genres and blurred boundaries — between musical styles, national and ethnic markers, historical periods, "art" and "popular" music, and rural and urban performance.

## Causal Drivers

**Colonial missions**: Christian missions suppressed indigenous performance genres — particularly those involving drums deemed offensive — while introducing four-part choral singing (kwaya), keyboards, and brass bands. These introduced forms were often reinterpreted by African performers as assertions of equality and communal identity, not submission. The Church of Nazareth (founded 1900, South Africa) combined Zulu ancestral music with Western elements.

**Urbanization**: Cities were historically the primary nodes of musical innovation. Port cities of North and East Africa (Cairo, Tunis, Mombasa, Lamu) had vibrant pre-colonial music cultures. West African coastal cities (Freetown, Accra, Lagos) produced hybrid styles from the 1920s — most importantly "highlife" on the Gold Coast, blending Christian choral singing, brass bands, parlour music, and indigenous traditions. Mining regions of the Katanga, Northern Rhodesian Copperbelt, and South Africa were "powerhouses of musical innovation in the colonial period." The kalela dance performed by Copperbelt migrants in the 1940s–50s prompted Max Gluckman's observation that "an African townsman is a townsman."

**Nationalism**: Nationalist movements from the 1940s incorporated music as cultural politics. Some drew on precolonial tradition — Zimbabwe's mbira music of the First Chimurenga (1896–7) was revived in the Second Chimurenga guerrilla war (1966–79) as the basis for chimurenga electric guitar style. Others pursued a "modernizing" synthesis, producing a dilemma: music should express immutable national "spirit" but also demonstrate modern progress. National dance troupes (Ballets Africains of Guinea, National Ballet of Senegal) served as touring showcases for postcolonial statehood. FESTAC (1977, Nigeria) deployed oil wealth to stage pan-African cultural spectacle.

**State patronage and censorship**: Postcolonial states developed systematic intervention in music through national broadcasting monopolies, patronage networks, censorship, and outright repression. The Mobutu regime's authenticité policy in Zaïre used state-sponsored animation politique (singing and dancing) to legitimize autocratic rule. Fela Kuti in Nigeria was repeatedly jailed and his "Kalakuta Republic" raided. Malawian reggae artist Evison Matafale died in police custody (2001). Simon Bikindi's role in broadcasting genocide incitement during Rwanda 1994 was prosecuted as a crime against humanity by the UN International Criminal Tribunal.

**The recording industry**: The Gramophone Company first recorded in Cairo (1903); records were on sale in South Africa by 1907. By 1939, HMV/Zonophone had sold over 200,000 records in East Africa alone. Léopoldville's three Greek-owned labels (Ngoma, Opika, Loningisa) captured rumba's emergence in the late 1940s. The arrival of cheap cassettes in the 1970s began the implosion of the African music industry; by 1999, Africa's share of global music retail value was 0.5 per cent, of which 95 per cent was South Africa alone. Intellectual property law remains poorly enforced across most of the continent — with the notable exception of Ghana (1985 folklore legislation) and South Africa (where Solomon Linda's heirs won against Walt Disney in 2006 over "The Lion Sleeps Tonight").

**Globalization and worldbeat**: Paul Gilroy's The Black Atlantic (1993) and the "worldbeat" boom reoriented African music history by tracing the connections between Africa, the Americas, and Europe over the longue durée. Trans-Atlantic slave trade carried African music to the Americas; it returned in new forms. Paris became the capital of African worldbeat in the 1970s–80s through performers including Francis Bebey, Manu Dibango, Salif Keïta, and Youssou N'Dour.

## Major Phases and Forms

**Precolonial urban music (to c.1880)**: North African cities had sophisticated music cultures. Arab-influenced taarab (Swahili vocals with Egyptian-style light classical accompaniment) emerged in East African port cities. Timbuktu blended North African Arab and West African traditions. Griots of Mande-speaking West Africa occupied a hereditary caste position linking music to power, praise, and memory.

**Early hybrid forms (c.1880–1940)**: The most important early modern hybrid musics appeared in Luanda (Luso-African musseque culture), Cape Town (Cape "Coloured" community fusions), and West African coastal cities. Gold Coast highlife emerged c.1900 as an amalgamation of "palm wine" guitar music and Kru sailor guitar styles. Missionary-trained choral competition became a vehicle for asserting African identity under colonial rule. Military brass bands inspired East African beni associations. South Africa's isicathamiya choral tradition used Western-derived four-part harmony to re-enact pre-urban social relationships.

**Recording era and transnational styles (1940–1970)**: Congolese rumba — strongly influenced by recordings of Cuban music — became the first truly transnational African style, spreading under the soukous/kwasa kwasa labels to eastern and southern Africa after independence. Juju emerged among urban Yoruba in Lagos as a syncretic blend. In South Africa, jazz developed in tension with Apartheid repression; Hugh Masekela, Miriam Makeba, and Abdullah Ibrahim all went into exile.

**Worldbeat and global Africa (1980–2000)**: The 1980s worldbeat boom brought African music international visibility. Youssou N'Dour's mbalax, Salif Keïta, Mory Kanté's "Yéké Yéké" (1987), and Ali Farka Touré's blues-griot fusions reached global audiences through Paris labels and Peter Gabriel's WOMAD festival. Hip-hop arrived from the late 1980s and became the dominant popular style by the 2000s — adapted into local forms (bongo flava in Tanzania, genge in Kenya's Sheng slang) while maintaining ambiguous relationships with global African American hip-hop templates.

**Contemporary religious and counter-public music (2000–present)**: Gospel music is the second most commercially significant genre after hip-hop across much of sub-Saharan Africa. Bandiri Islamic chant in northern Nigeria became a site of contestation between Sufi practitioners and Wahhabi-influenced clergy who objected to its Bollywood melodic borrowings. The deregulation of media from the 1990s opened space for community radio and local FM stations; simultaneously, global music industry concentration reduced the leverage of African artists in international markets.

## Analytical Concepts

**Tradition vs. modern**: The early ethnomusicological category of "tradition" — mapped against modernity and the West — was revealed from the 1980s as itself historically constructed. Hobsbawm and Ranger's The Invention of Tradition (1983) showed that African "traditional" musics were reformulated or invented through colonial and postcolonial agency. Precolonial mbira music was revived and electrified for guerrilla war; griot forms were enrolled into postcolonial autocratic praise. "Tradition" is a political resource, not an ethnographic datum.

**The politics of the belly**: Jean-François Bayart's concept, applied to music, describes the system of patronage through which autocratic rulers co-opt musicians — particularly griots — as praise-singers, while musicians extract material support and occasionally transgress the limits of acceptable flattery.

**Worldbeat and the Black Atlantic**: Gilroy's framework traces the circular movement of African musical forms — to the Americas through the slave trade, back to Africa through jazz, blues, soul, and gospel — as constitutive of an Atlantic "counter-culture of modernity" rather than a derivation from it.

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Recordings from the early twentieth century survive unevenly; pre-1930 African music is largely undocumented. Newspaper accounts, colonial administrative files, and mission records supplement recordings. Oral history is essential. The field is methodologically eclectic, drawing on ethnomusicology, social history, cultural studies, and postcolonial theory.

**Scholarly debates**: (1) How much colonial impact reshaped African music vs. how much African agency drove innovation. (2) Whether worldbeat represented genuine cultural exchange or Northern commodification of African artists. (3) The relationship between African hip-hop and African American hip-hop — derivative or independent? (4) The role of Islamic piety movements in reshaping African soundscapes.

**Recent revisionism**: The shift from salvage ethnomusicology to urban music history; the reinterpretation of "tradition" as political construction; the attention to musicians' economic precarity rather than their iconic status; and the study of copyright, piracy, and global music industry political economy are all recent orientations.

**Collection coverage**: The Bibliotheca Alexandrina is weak on post-1750 Africa and on the specialized ethnomusicology literature. The Parker–Reid Oxford Handbook chapter by Erlmann provides the framework for this entry. Deep engagement with the field would require Veit Erlmann's own monographs (African Stars, Nightsong), David Coplan's work on South Africa, and Kelly Askew on Tanzania.
