# Power to Hurt

The **power to hurt** — the capacity to inflict pain, shock, loss, grief, privation, or horror — is, in Schelling's analysis, among the most impressive attributes of military force, yet traditionally received less systematic attention than the capacity to seize, disarm, or occupy.

Unlike brute force, which overcomes the enemy's strength directly, the power to hurt works by influencing the enemy's choices. "Hurting, unlike forcible seizure or self-defense, is not unconcerned with the interest of others. It is measured in the suffering it can cause and the victims' motivation to avoid it." Its value lies in its *potential* (latent violence) more than in its actual exercise. "The power to hurt is bargaining power. To exploit it is diplomacy — vicious diplomacy, but diplomacy."

## Latent vs. Applied

The threat of damage, or of more damage to come, can make an adversary yield or comply. Actual hurting is often required only to demonstrate credibility or to communicate that the capacity is real and contingent on behavior. Once demonstrated, the expectation of future hurt does the work.

This reframes much of what is called "limited war," strategic bombing, naval blockade, or sanctions: not as attempts to destroy capacity but as efforts to make continued resistance more painful than the demanded concession.

## Limits and Conditions

The power to hurt is effective only when:
- The victim has something to lose or can feel pain (not applicable against "weeds or floods").
- The suffering can be made contingent on the victim's behavior.
- There exists (or can be created) a bargain in which compliance leaves the victim better off, all things considered, than non-compliance.
- Common interest exists at least in the avoidance of unlimited mutual damage.

When interests are absolutely opposed, pure hurting without bargaining possibility simply produces reciprocal destruction.

## Enduring Relevance

Schelling argues that in the nuclear age the power to hurt became central because brute-force conquest between major powers became either impossible or suicidal. The insight, however, is general: any actor with a comparative advantage in inflicting pain (terrorists, insurgents, states with long-range precision weapons) operates in the domain of the diplomacy of violence and the power to hurt rather than classical battlefield decision.
