## Definition

Meillacoid and Chicoid are ceramic traditions — Rouse's "series" — that archaeologists use to track cultural change across the Greater Antilles and Bahamas during the Late Ceramic Age (c. AD 700–1500). Both were defined by Rouse (1939, 1992) as diagnostic assemblages of vessel form, surface treatment, and decorative mode.

**Meillacoid** (c. AD 800–1500) is the dominant tradition of Hispaniola, especially northwest Hispaniola, Haiti, and the Cibao Valley. Its hallmarks are relatively thin-walled (3–7 mm) vessels with a hard but unpolished surface, rounded rims with folded-fillet construction, casuela (in-turned shoulder) and boat shapes, and a distinctive fine-line incised decoration: narrow (1–2 mm) incised lines that leave a clay ridge, sigmoid and other appliqué (C-shapes, hand-paw motif), crosshatching, oblique parallels, and strap handles. Anthropomorphic appliqué faces (not modelled lugs) appear on some adornos. Meillacoid includes named regional styles: Meillac and Finca in Haiti; Baní in Cuba; White Marl, Montego Bay, and Port Morant in Jamaica.

**Chicoid** (c. AD 900–1500) first emerges in the eastern Dominican Republic, centred on the Boca Chica heartland southeast of the Yuma River. It is thicker (7–9 mm), softer, and highly polished. Vessel shapes are more varied — effigies, white-slipped bottles (mammiform), potizas (jars). Decoration uses broad-line incision (4–5 mm) with smoothed and rounded edges in circular, oval, and rectangular panels; curvilinear designs; circles with a dot in the centre; the characteristic "Chicoid eye"; and large modelled lugs that resemble sculpture. There is no appliqué. Chicoid styles include Boca Chica (Dominican Republic), Carrier (Haiti), Pueblo Viejo (Cuba), and Capá and Esperanza (Puerto Rico). Keegan and Hofman (2017) question whether Capá and Esperanza should be classified as Meillacoid rather than Chicoid.

The two traditions are distinguished not only by technique but by vessel function and cuisine. Meillacoid in-turned vessels (casuela) were well suited for boiling liquids such as fish stew. Chicoid flared-rim vessels, constricted at the shoulder but open at the top, were better suited for thicker pepper-pot stews requiring stirring. This change in vessel form is consistent with dental-pathology evidence showing more highly processed, carbohydrate-rich foods in the Late Ceramic Age (Mickleburgh 2013).

## Key Sites

- **El Cabo, southeastern Dominican Republic** (c. AD 600–1504): Ostionoid and Chicoid occupation across multiple phases. Six house clusters on limestone bedrock, each with 3–5 circular houses (6.5–10 m diameter, modelled on the South American maloca). European materials (Nueva Cádiz beads, metal, majolica) appear in a tightly bounded area of the final occupation phase, distributed through pre-existing indigenous exchange networks. Abandoned c. 1504 after the Higüey wars.

- **El Flaco, northwestern Dominican Republic** (c. AD 900–1500): Meillacoid site at the foot of the Cordillera Septentrional, c. 12 km from the coast. Platforms and mounds encircle a cultural landscape; house platforms (8.5–9 m diameter) with double post rows. Intermingling of Meillacoid, Chicoid, and minor Ostionoid pottery. Eighteen burials in three mounds, with evidence of open-grave desiccation and secondary manipulation of crania.

- **Île à Rat, Haiti** (c. AD 900–1500): Tiny island (<0.5 ha) at the mouth of the Baie de l'Acul providing a stratigraphic sequence of Archaic Age → Ostionoid → Meillacoid → Chicoid → Spanish. Radiocarbon dates: charcoal at 40 cm = cal. AD 1295 ± 70; charcoal at 57 cm = cal. AD 905–950 ± 50. Functioned as a specialist fishing station supplying inland Hispaniolan villages with processed marine protein (juvenile *Lobatus gigas*, processed fish heads). Net-sinker technology shifted from stationary seine nets (Meillacoid) to cast nets (Chicoid), paralleling broader economic changes.

- **En Bas Saline, Haiti** (c. AD 1300 to contact): Large Chicoid village identified as the probable site of Guacanagarí's town and the first Spanish settlement of La Navidad (December 25, 1492). Site features include a C-shaped earthwork (20 m wide, 80 cm tall, 350 × 270 m), a central plaza with a large oval wattle-and-daub structure (≥15 m diameter, burned at ~1400° C), ceremonial feast trenches, and a deep pit reused as a burial cache containing cohoba ritual equipment. Evidence for an indigenous road connecting coastal villages every 28 km.

- **En Bas Saline plaza sites** (Dominican Republic, AD 1300+): Three enormous plaza complexes — Coral de los Indios (691 m diameter, 43,000 m²); Plaza de Chacuey (250 × 145 m, 29,000 m², with astronomical alignments); Plaza La Cacique (132 × 77 m). All feature cobbled causeways leading to rivers with petroglyphs. These are the strongest archaeological evidence for paramount caciques (cacicazgos) in the Caribbean.

## Meillacoid Revolution

Keegan and Hofman identify the spread of Meillacoid as a "revolution" — not merely a pottery change but a shift in modo de vida. Meillacoid spread rapidly from Hispaniola to Jamaica, Cuba, and the southern Bahamas (c. AD 800–1000). The rapid expansion reflects the introduction of a suite of tropical forest cultigens and associated cultivation practices: intensified swidden horticulture (slash-and-burn), a shift from coastal-mangrove to hilltop-overlooking-coast settlement locations, and the increasing importance of clay griddles. Agriculture employed manioc, sweet potato, maize, and guáyiga (*Zamia*) in different proportions across ecological zones. Maize is attested in the Dominican Republic interior by AD 1060.

## Chicoid Expansion

A new social formation emerged in the eastern Dominican Republic after AD 950, at the interface between Hispaniolan Meillacoid and Puerto Rican Ostionoid cultures on the Mona Passage. After AD 1200 communities in the eastern Dominican Republic expanded territorially through a combination of warfare, exchange, marriage, and religious conversion. This expansion was social and ideological, not technological.

Chicoid spread differently to different islands and regions:
- **Western Hispaniola**: infiltration through marriage and establishment of neighboring paired settlements; survival of Macorix communities near the "Taíno" heartland suggests a relatively peaceful transfer under a Chicoid cultural umbrella.
- **Cuba**: colony establishment and conversion of local ceramicists to Chicoid decorative modes; local interpretations produced lower-quality Chicoid pottery executed with Meillacoid techniques.
- **Puerto Rico**: primarily religious conversion; Ostiones and Santa Elena styles transformed to Capá and Esperanza; settlement remained dispersed, houses small, no earthworks or monticulos adopted.
- **Saba (Lesser Antilles)**: clear Chicoid colony.
- **Anguilla**: local allegiance rather than full colonization.

## The "Taíno" Problem and Historiography

The concept of "Taíno" as a unified culture has been heavily criticised. Keegan and Hofman (2017) argue the name reflects a misuse of Spanish chronicles by modern historians. Spanish documents distinguished primarily between "good and noble" Indios and "fierce cannibals," a dichotomy most archaeologists now reject but that still structures interpretive frameworks. Rouse (1992) attempted to accommodate both the distinction and his unilinear evolutionary model by subsuming Ostionoid, Meillacoid, and Chicoid as "Ostionan," "Meillacan," and "Chican" subseries of a single Ostionoid taxon — a move Keegan and Hofman reject as forcing evidence into a predetermined model.

The key revisionist insight from Ulloa Hung (2013, 2014) is that Meillacoid and Chicoid coexisted contemporaneously and mixed in the northwestern Dominican Republic — they were not sequential replacements. Sites show mixing of styles, Meillacoid motifs on Chicoid vessels, and vice versa. The region was, at every period, a multicultural mosaic. Moreover, the sheer number of Meillacoid sites (437 in Haiti alone) compared to Chicoid (128) makes it implausible that Chicoid "replaced" Meillacoid as Rouse argued.

The pan-Antillean distribution of ceremonial objects (Chama sarda beads, shell masks/guaízas, three-pointed stones/trigonoliticos, stone collars, wooden duhos, guanín, gold-copper alloy) has been explained through "Taínoness," but Keegan and Hofman argue this concept is unwarranted. The distribution pattern more closely resembles the Hopewell Interaction Sphere (100 BC–AD 500) in eastern North America — autonomous residential communities exchanging symbolic objects through social networks without hierarchical political control — than the stratified Southeastern Ceremonial Complex model applied by scholars who see the Caribbean as developing "chiefdoms" (cacicazgos).

## Contested Aspects

1. **Origins of Meillacoid**: Whether Meillacoid pottery originated from South American/Colombian/Venezuelan contact (Veloz Maggiolo 1972; Zucchi 1991) or from internal Hispaniolan development remains debated. Keegan and Hofman (2017) suggest any contacts were likely initiated from Hispaniola rather than being a migration to it.

2. **Chiefdom / cacicazgo model**: Whether the large Chicoid plaza sites evidence true hierarchical chiefdoms or more loosely integrated polities (analogous_to: Hopewell Interaction Sphere) is actively debated. Curet (2002, 2003) challenges the matrilineal-descent model proposed by Keegan and Maclachlan (1989), though Curet himself concedes that Hispaniolan and Puerto Rican polities developed from distinct ancestral societies through different historical processes.

3. **Meillacoid versus Chicoid as distinct cultures or social identities**: The contemporaneous segregation of styles (east bank = Chicoid Carrier; west bank = Meillacoid at Bois Neuf, Haiti) may reflect social duality rather than sequential replacement — each tradition may have needed the other. The Rouse model of replacement is no longer accepted.

4. **What Chicoid pottery represents**: Whether its spread reflects religious conversion, hereditary chiefly expansion, economic hegemony, or some combination remains unresolved. Keegan and Hofman (2017) caution against attributing all Late Ceramic Age ceremonial objects and beliefs to a single "Taíno" religious system derived from Friar Ramón Pané's 1497–1498 account of the Macorix.
