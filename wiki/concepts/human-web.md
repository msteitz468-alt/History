# The Human Web

A framework for world history advanced by **J.R. McNeill and William H. McNeill** in
*The Human Web: A Bird's-Eye View of World History* (2003), which reconceptualizes the
human past as a history of **webs of connection** — networks of communication and
exchange linking people who cooperate and compete. The unit of analysis is the
connection itself rather than the polity or civilization.

## Definition and Scope

The McNeills argue that from the very beginning of human history individuals and
communities have formed connecting webs "large and small, rigid and ﬂexible." Studying
these webs reveals the patterns of interaction, cooperation, and competition that "have
driven history relentlessly into the modern age" ([[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin 2018]]).

The model is **developmental and cumulative**, distinguishing webs by density and reach:
- the **"thin, localized" webs** of the Early Agrarian Era;
- the **"denser, more interactive, metropolitan webs"** of the great agrarian
  civilizations — among which the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]] is the paradigm case; and
- the **"electriﬁed global web"** of the modern world.

William McNeill's earlier conviction — that intercultural contact has been "the main
drive wheel of history" (*The Rise of the West*, 1963) — is the seed of the web concept.

## What It Is Not

The human web is **less structured than a [[world-systems-theory|world-system]]**: it
does not require a core/periphery hierarchy or unequal exchange, and it makes no strong
claim about economic integration. It is a heuristic for *connectivity over time*, not a
formal economic-sociological model. This looseness is both its strength (it applies from
the Paleolithic to the present) and the reason it generates fewer testable predictions.

## Relationship to Other Frameworks

`analogous_to: [[world-systems-theory]]` — shares the connections-first premise.
`contributed_to: [[big-history]]` — the McNeills are central figures in the world-history
tradition feeding big history; the web's notion of thickening connectivity dovetails with
big history's concept of **collective learning** (humanity's unique capacity to store and
exchange information, which exchange networks like the Silk Roads dramatically magnified).
`part_of:` the broader connective turn in world history alongside Janet Abu-Lughod and
Frank & Gills (see [[world-systems-theory]]).

Benjamin states that his analysis of the Silk Roads "borrows much from world systems
theory, and also from the idea of an eternal web of connections that has bound societies
together from the very beginning of human history, but that intensiﬁed during certain
periods such as the First Silk Roads Era."

## Limitations and Critiques

Because the web metaphor is so capacious, critics note it can describe almost any
historical relationship without explaining *why* particular connections form, thicken, or
break. It is strongest as an organizing image and weakest as a causal theory — which is
why Benjamin pairs it with world-systems theory and ancient-globalization analysis
([[proto-globalization]]) rather than relying on it alone.
