## Definition and Scope

The **dhimmī** (pl. dhimmiyyūn) is a non-Muslim subject living permanently under Islamic rule under a covenant of protection (dhimma). The dhimma is a bilateral contractual relationship: the Islamic state grants protection of life, property, and religious practice; the dhimmī accepts Islamic political sovereignty and pays the **jizya** (poll-tax) as the material expression of that submission.

The protected communities historically recognized as dhimmīs are the **People of the Book** (Ahl al-Kitāb) — primarily Christians, Jews, and Magians (Zoroastrians). Pagans were generally not eligible for dhimmī status in classical juristic theory (being required to convert or fight), but historical practice diverged substantially: Hindus, Jains, Buddhists, and other non-monotheists in the Indian subcontinent were in practice accorded protected status over centuries.

A distinct category is the **mustamān** (pl. mustaʾminūn): an individual from the Abode of War granted temporary safe-conduct (amān) to visit, trade, or reside in the Abode of Islam. Unlike dhimmīs, mustamins are not permanent residents and owe no poll-tax.

## The Jizya (Poll-Tax)

The jizya is the defining fiscal obligation of dhimmī status. It is incumbent upon all adult dhimmī males. Exempt from the jizya are: women, children, freedmen, slaves, the insane, the chronically ill, and the poor. Payment is due annually as a lump sum scaled by wealth:

| Prosperity level | Rate |
|---|---|
| Rich | 48 dirhams (or 4 dīnārs) per year |
| Middle income | 24 dirhams (2 dīnārs) |
| Modest means | 12 dirhams (1 dīnār) |
| Poor | Exempt |

Conversion to Islam at any point during the year — even in the final week — cancels the year's jizya obligation entirely.

## Legal Status: Rights and Obligations

The dhimmī's legal status in Islamic law is carefully differentiated rather than uniformly subordinate. Key features:

**Under most ḥudūd rules**: Dhimmīs are subject to the same ḥudūd penalties as Muslims for theft and highway robbery. For zinā, the rules vary: the Ḥanafites and Mālikites include membership in the Islamic faith as part of the definition of **muḥṣan** (the status triggering the stoning penalty), meaning that no dhimmī is subject to stoning for adultery. The Shāfiʿites and Ḥanbalites do not share this exemption.

**Property law**: Dhimmī property is legally protected. Wine and pork — considered lawless property under Shāfiʿite and Ḥanbalite doctrine — are considered valid property (mutaqawwim) in Ḥanafite doctrine when owned by dhimmīs (since those communities regard them as property), entitling dhimmīs to full compensation if destroyed. The Mālikites similarly uphold dhimmī compensatory rights on the grounds that dhimmīs themselves regard such items as property.

**In qiṣāṣ (retaliation for homicide)**: The Ḥanafites alone accept the equality of Muslims and non-Muslims in qiṣāṣ liability — meaning a Muslim is subject to the death penalty for intentionally killing a dhimmī. The other schools do not hold this position.

**In testimony**: Dhimmī testimony is generally not admissible against Muslims in Islamic courts, though the precise rules vary by school.

**In warfare**: Under the law of jihād, the People of the Book who accept dhimmī status after a jihād war are entitled to protection and maintenance of their religious practices under Islamic rule. The imam who strikes such an agreement is bound to honor it.

## The Amān Contract (Safe-Conduct for Temporary Visitors)

Distinct from the permanent dhimmī status, the **amān** is a temporary protection contract for **mustamins** — visitors from the Abode of War. Any private Muslim — male, female, or slave — may grant amān to up to ten individuals; the imam may grant it to any number including entire populations. The amān may be concluded orally, in writing, or by course of conduct (the customary commercial practice of merchants entering Muslim territory constitutes implied amān). Foreign envoys receive automatic amān.

Hallaq notes the "considerable tolerance in the law of non-Muslim denizens in Muslim lands" as remarkably contrasting with modern nation-state immigration regimes.

## Historiography

The dhimmī institution has been interpreted across a wide spectrum in modern scholarship. Orientalist and Islamophobic readings emphasized restrictions and subordination; apologist Muslim readings emphasized protection and tolerance. Scholarly consensus now focuses on the contractual nature of the dhimma as a form of legal pluralism within an imperial framework, distinct from both modern religious tolerance (which assumes equality) and persecution (which aims at elimination).

The practical divergence between juristic theory and historical application — particularly the de facto extension of protected status to Hindus and Buddhists in South Asia, contrary to classical doctrine — is a significant area of research.
