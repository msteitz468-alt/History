# The Senate under the Principate

How the Roman **Senate** — and the senatorial and equestrian **orders** — were reshaped by
[[augustus|Augustus]] and his successors: still busy, dignified, and indispensable, yet
stripped of its old control over war, finance, and the provinces. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 9 (**Richard J. A. Talbert**).

## Augustus' reform of membership

The "restoration of the Republic" required reforming the Senate, swollen to 1,000+ by Caesar's
adlections and post-Ides bribery. Augustus **purged the roll** repeatedly (190 expelled in 29
BCE; a drastic review in 18 BCE) and settled it at a notional **~600** — Sulla's size. He
**raised the census** for membership from the equestrian 400,000 to **one million sesterces**
(18 BCE), helping worthy members meet it, and turned the senators and their descendants (to the
third generation) into a defined, hereditary **"senatorial class"** — first appearing in the
marriage legislation of 18 BCE, marked by the *latus clavus*, front seats, and a battery of
restrictions (bans on marrying the low-born, on the stage) to guard its dignity.

The **cursus honorum** was streamlined: the *vigintivirate*, then the **quaestorship** (now at
25, the normal door into the Senate via twenty annual places), the aedileship/tribunate, the
**praetorship** (now at 30; Augustus kept praetors as few as 10–12, so reaching it was a real
achievement), and the **consulship**, whose numbers the emperor expanded from 5 BCE by adding
**suffect** consuls (up to six men a year), easing competition and supplying more men for the
senior posts.

## Augustus' regulation of business

The **lex Iulia de senatu habendo (9 BCE)** codified procedure for the first time: quorums for
every kind of business, **fixed meeting days** (Kalends and Ides), a public annual roll, and a
"retirement age" (60 or 65). Fines for absence proved futile and were dropped. Crucially,
Augustus introduced a preparatory **senatorial *consilium*** to vet business before the full
house — and in 13 CE gave its decisions authority equal to the Senate's, deepening members'
sense that the "real" debate happened elsewhere.

## The shrinking of prerogative

Tacitus' verdict — Augustus "drew to himself the functions of the Senate, the magistrates and
the laws" — Talbert finds largely true. The Senate kept much (it **legislated** actively, its
*senatus consulta* now law without popular ratification; it bestowed honours; it retained
authority in religion). But the emperor reserved **the army, foreign affairs, finance, and the
provinces** (his own and all new ones): the Senate "lost for ever" the prerogative of
disposing the state's military forces. New curators (roads, aqueducts, the corn supply) further
encroached. Augustus' scrupulous **consultation** could not dispel the central problem: members
could never be sure where frank speech became dangerous, so "the majority preferred to take no
risk at all, and the Senate as a deliberative body suffered" — the servility Tiberius scorned
("*O homines ad servitutem paratos!*").

## Tiberius and the rise of senatorial jurisdiction

Talbert stresses Tiberius initially **strengthened** the Senate: he abolished the Augustan
*consilium* (restoring the full house's primacy) and **transferred magistrate elections** from
the assemblies to the Senate (14 CE). Most consequentially, he let the Senate develop into a
**high court** — taking *maiestas* (treason) and *repetundae* (extortion) cases (the trial of
Piso in 20 a turning-point), eclipsing the *quaestiones*. But this was double-edged: in
**treason** cases, which touched the emperor's own safety, he intervened, and his failure to
restrain the *delatores* — and his withdrawal to Capri (26) — turned senatorial jurisdiction
into "a major tragedy of his reign."

## Claudius and provincial entry

Hostile emperors (Gaius, Nero) did only short-term damage; by the late reign of Tiberius the
Senate's reformed membership and redefined functions were settled. **Claudius** made the most
lasting change — admitting **provincials** (his famous speech on the *Tabula Lugdunensis*
arguing for the "flower of the colonies," opening the Senate to Gallic notables), though the
newcomers were overwhelmingly **Western**; easterners came only after the Julio-Claudians.

## Significance and contest

Talbert's balanced conclusion: the Senate retained great vitality (day-long meetings, real
debate, fierce competition for office, deep prestige) and its members "individually and
collectively still exerted a decisive influence." Its well-being remained "painfully dependent
upon the emperor's pleasure," yet the standard set by Augustus and Tiberius became one
**senatorial opinion could afterward demand each successor maintain** — respect for the Senate
becoming a chief criterion of the "good emperor." The degree of the Senate's real power, and
how far its jurisdiction was a gift or a trap, remain debated (the role of the *acta senatus*
in Tacitus; whether the elections transfer was Augustus' or Tiberius' design).
