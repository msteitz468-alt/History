# The Roman Equites

The **equestrian order** — the wealthy non-senatorial class whose conflict with the
Senate, especially over the law-courts and the tax-farming of the provinces, became a
structural faultline of the late Republic. Analysed in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]]
Ch. 3 (Lintott).

## Definition and ambiguity

Strictly (so Nicolet), the *equites Romani* were the men enrolled by the censors in
the **eighteen centuries** of the *comitia centuriata*, given the public horse, voting
first with the wealthiest class. But by the late Republic the term blurred to cover a
much wider body of **wealthy non-senators** (property qualification c. 400,000
sesterces, *de facto* hereditary) — many of them **tax-contractors (*publicani*)** and
**jurors**. When the sources speak of a conflict of "Senate and *equites*," they mean
this looser group, not the cavalry-centuries (and not the young *equites* who were
senators' sons destined for the Senate).

## The political faultline

[[gaius-gracchus|Gaius Gracchus]] forged the order into a political force by:
- transferring the **extortion court** (*quaestio de repetundis*) to **equestrian
  jurors** — "a sword threatening the Senate," giving non-senators judicial power over
  senatorial defendants; and
- auctioning the **direct taxes of Asia** to *publican* companies at Rome — making the
  tax-companies "centres of important political pressure-groups."

Thereafter control of the courts oscillated (Caepio's mixed panels 106, Glaucia's
restoration to the *equites*) and the order's interests clashed repeatedly with
provincial governors — the condemnation of **Rutilius Rufus** (c. 92), an honest legate
ruined by the Asian tax-collectors, became the symbol of "venal and vindictive"
equestrian juries (a charge Lintott qualifies: the real senatorial grievance was that
the juries *condemned*). The **societas publicanorum** was, uniquely in Roman law, a
business association with something like a corporate legal personality.

## Significance

The *equites* are the "third force" whose rise the aristocracy "failed to assimilate"
(Meier) — neither governing nor excluded, courted by *populares* and feared by the
Senate, their grievances and their financial reach (especially in the provinces) a
recurring engine of late-Republican politics down to the courts-question that fed the
Social War.
