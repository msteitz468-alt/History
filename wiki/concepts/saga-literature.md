# Saga Literature

The corpus of Old Norse–Icelandic prose narratives written down chiefly in
[[iceland|Iceland]] from the 12th to 14th centuries. Major genres include the
*Íslendingasögur* (sagas of Icelanders / family sagas), *konungasögur* (kings'
sagas, e.g. Snorri Sturluson's *Heimskringla*), *fornaldarsögur* (legendary
sagas), and contemporary *samtíðarsögur* (e.g. *Sturlunga saga*). Alongside Eddic
and skaldic poetry, the sagas are the richest vernacular literature of medieval
Europe and the principal narrative source for early Scandinavian history.

## Emic vs. Etic / Historiographical Problem

Sagas were prized as historical record by earlier scholars but are now read
critically: most were composed centuries after the events they describe, shaped by
oral tradition, literary convention, and the politics of the writers' own day. The
**"bookprose vs. freeprose"** debate (how much derives from fixed oral tradition
vs. authorial composition) frames their reliability. They remain invaluable for
mentalities, law, social structure, and the self-understanding of medieval
Scandinavians, even where unreliable for events.

## Centres, Genres, and Transmission (Cambridge History detail)

Written prose began at the lawspeaker's farm (laws recorded 1117–18) and with **Ari
Thorgilsson**'s *Íslendingabók* (early 12th c.). Production centred on episcopal sees
and monasteries — Skálholt, Hólar, [[snorri-sturluson|Snorri's]] Reykholt in the west;
Lund, Roskilde, Uppsala, Linköping in the east, where Latin and East Norse traditions
([[saxo-grammaticus|Saxo's]] *Gesta Danorum*; the Swedish *Erikskrönikan*) dominated.
The **kings' sagas** (*konungasögur*) were the first written; the c. 40 **sagas of
Icelanders** (*Íslendingasögur*) were mostly 13th-century; the **fornaldarsögur**
(legendary sagas) and the contemporary *Sturlunga saga* round out the corpus. The
Bergen court of [[hakon-hakonarson|Hákon Hákonarson]] commissioned Norse translations
of French chivalric romance (Tristan, Chrétien) — a key conduit of courtly culture —
and Queen Eufemia commissioned the Swedish *Eufemiavisor*. **Eddic poetry** (the Codex
Regius, c. 1270) and **skaldic verse** (a west-Nordic monopoly built on the *kenning*)
are the two poetic traditions; the **bookprose/freeprose** debate has settled toward a
consensus that most sagas combine a historical core, oral tradition, and individual
authorial composition.
