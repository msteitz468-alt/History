# Renaissance

The Renaissance denotes the cultural, artistic, and intellectual movement, centered in Italy from the 14th through 16th centuries and subsequently spreading across Western Europe, characterized by renewed engagement with classical Greek and Roman texts, art, and models of civic life, and by a humanist educational program (*studia humanitatis*) emphasizing rhetoric, history, moral philosophy, and philology alongside — and in growing tension with — the scholastic university curriculum inherited from the [[concepts/twelfth-century-renaissance|medieval "renaissances"]] that preceded it.

Its Italian core unfolded through interlocking urban centers, above all Florence (Petrarch, Boccaccio, Brunelleschi, Botticelli, and the political-cultural patronage of the Medici), Venice, Rome (papal patronage of art and architecture), and Milan, closely bound up with the distinctive competitive diplomacy of the [[processes/italian-renaissance-state-system|Italian city-state system]] whose rival courts drove much of the era's artistic and intellectual patronage. Beyond the visual arts (Leonardo, Michelangelo, Raphael) and architecture, the Renaissance transformed political thought (Machiavelli), historical and philological method (recovery and critical study of ancient texts), and, through printing, the diffusion of these ideas across Europe.

Historian Jacob Burckhardt's influential 1860 account cast the Renaissance as the birth of modern individualism and secular political rationality against a supposedly static medieval backdrop — a sharp period break since substantially revised and complicated by later scholarship, which emphasizes continuities with the [[concepts/twelfth-century-renaissance|Twelfth-Century Renaissance]] and other earlier revivals of classical learning, and treats "Renaissance" less as a clean rupture than as an intensification and popularization of currents already present in late medieval intellectual life.
