# Gender in Merovingian Gaul

**Gender as a performed, life-cycle construction read through grave goods and law.** Guy Halsall's
synthesis (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 8) treats Merovingian gender not as a fixed binary anchored in
anatomy but as something **enacted** — through dress, adornment, weapons, and ritual — and running
on different timetables for men and women across the life cycle. The furnished inhumations of
sixth-century Gaul, with their two sharply distinct artifact clusters, are the richest evidence
for how a post-Roman society staged masculinity and femininity.

## Definition and Scope

Halsall grounds the chapter in Judith Butler's **performativity**: "Gender is constituted in its
performance; behind the mask is nothing or — rather — only another mask." Identification is "an
enacted fantasy." He concedes the field is comparatively underdeveloped: thirty years after Joan
Scott (1986), "early medieval historians remain… comparatively unsubtle investigators of the
topic." The evidence is overwhelmingly mortuary, which records the *social* body the family chose
to display — not biological sex directly.

- **The disjunction made visible.** At Ennery (Grave 32) an anatomically male skeleton was buried
  with a necklace otherwise found only with biologically female skeletons; the public burial ritual
  shows the family "were well enough aware of the disjunction between the physical body and its
  social skin and were prepared to represent the latter in public display." Gregory of Tours
  (*Histories* 10.15) records a cross-dressing Poitevin of Sainte-Croix — a story "undecidable at so
  many levels."

## The Roman-to-Merovingian Shift

Classical Roman gender centred on the **civic male** — moderation and self-control — against which
the female, the barbarian, and the animal were all defined; in Roman thought "the default,
pre-social gender was feminine." From the late fourth–fifth century a **martial masculinity**
emerged in the late imperial army, stressing the opposite of the civic ideal. Halsall argues the
oscillation between civic and martial masculinities let "more actively idealized feminine traits…
emerge, based more strongly on sex, the body, and reproduction" — producing the two-cluster
furnished burials.

- **Feminine cluster**: brooches, earrings, hairpins, dress-pins, bracelets, necklaces — costume
  and adornment. Most early Merovingian decorative metalwork "was invested in items of female
  adornment." Law penalised touching a woman's hair, arm, or breast (*Pactus Legis Salicae*
  20.1–4): "the unavailable female body is not concealed but highlighted." See [[salic-law]].
- **Masculine cluster**: weapons, tools, flints, strike-a-lights, knives — "things that men did to,
  or with, things or other people." The decorated belt was the principal masculine symbol.

## Gender and the Life Cycle

Halsall's central finding is that masculine and feminine identities were constructed on **different
timetables**. Early childhood burials made little recognition of sex. At menarche a girl was abruptly
interred with the full range of feminine objects; a woman of childbearing age carried a wergeld
**three times** that of an ordinary free Frank (PLS 24.8, 41.1) — "the interpellation of a child
into a fully sexed/gendered female role was sudden and brutal." Boys transitioned at twelve (first
haircut, PLS 24.2), but full masculine socialization stretched over two decades; before marriage
males were *pueri* regardless of age. Men dying between thirty and sixty — heads of household with
incomplete succession — received "the most lavish interments." The old of both sexes reverted to
gender-"neutral" graves like children's.

## Change around 600 and the Age of Abbesses

After c. 600 feminine grave goods declined while the share of male burials with at least a token
weapon rose; great decorated **plate-buckles** dominate the seventh-century record. Originally
masculine, plate-buckles were adopted by women, then redefined by men — possibly a return toward a
more "monopolar" gender in which "the good woman" demonstrated virtue partly through masculine
artifacts. In the religious sphere, a central role "could be occupied effectively by a woman as well
as a man. It is small surprise that the seventh century was an age of great abbesses" — see
[[merovingian-queenship]] and [[merovingian-monasticism]].

## Historiography

Halsall's chapter is heavily theoretical (Butler, Derrida, Lacan), and he stresses that early
medieval gender history has been slow to absorb post-structuralist theory relative to other fields.
The reading of sex from grave goods is itself contested (see [[merovingian-archaeology]] and
[[merovingian-bioarchaeology]] for the bioarchaeological check on artifact-based sexing). Émilie
Perez's quantitative work on children (see [[merovingian-childhood]]) confirms that gendered
artifact associations were broadly stable from childhood into adulthood.
