# Harappan Script

## Definition and Scope

The Harappan script is the undeciphered writing system used by the people of the Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilisation during its Mature phase (c. 2600–1900 BCE). Its continued illegibility is the central epistemological problem of South Asian prehistory: as Avari states, "The problem of the script is particularly frustrating, because our understanding of the Harappans would be so much more enhanced if it could be deciphered." Because the script cannot be read, the Harappan Civilisation "must, therefore, be classed as pre-historic" in the sense that it cannot speak for itself in linguistic terms.

This is an **etic** analytical category imposed by modern scholars to describe a writing system of unknown name and function whose practitioners left no readable account of themselves.

## Physical Characteristics

Nearly **4,200 inscribed objects** have been recovered from Harappan sites, with Mohenjo Daro and Harappa together accounting for approximately 85 per cent. The objects inscribed include soapstone (steatite) seals, impressible materials such as clay or soft metal, stone and copper tablets, bracelets, marble, ivory, shells, and bangles.

The script contains **419 signs**, of which:
- 113 occur only once
- 47 occur twice
- 59 occur fewer than five times
- 200 occur more frequently

This is "far too many to constitute an alphabetic or syllabic script; they are mostly logo-syllabic characters." Only eight texts longer than fifteen signs have been found. The average inscription length is five signs; the longest known inscription consists of twenty-six signs.

Directionality: the script was written from **right to left**, but reversed direction (left to right) when it continued onto a second line — the boustrophedon pattern (as the ox turns in ploughing).

The most striking single inscription was found at **Dholavira**: nine signs, each 37 cm high and 25–27 cm wide, made by joining crystalline pieces and engraved on a wooden board. If this was a signboard, it would imply a degree of public literacy among the inhabitants.

## Context of Use

The most common medium for the script is the soapstone seal, of which approximately 2,000 inscribed examples have been recovered from Mohenjo Daro and Harappa alone. Since seals were important symbols of authority and exchange mechanisms in trade, "it is likely that the script must have something useful to say in terms of administration, trade or perhaps spirituality." The seals also typically bear animal images (unicorn, zebu, elephant, rhinoceros, water buffalo, short-horned humpless bull, goat, antelope, crocodile, hare), and both the script and the animal imagery were probably intertwined with systems of authority, trade identification, or religious symbolism.

## Decipherment Attempts and Obstacles

Three eminent scholars who spent years attempting to crack the script — Professor Mahadevan of the Archaeological Survey of India, Professor Asko Parpola of the University of Helsinki, and Professor Walter Fairservis Jr. of Vassar College, New York — have all been unable to decipher it. 

The key obstacles are:
1. **No bilingual text.** As Avari notes, if "a bilingual text, one using the Harappan script against another, already understood, were to be found on a single seal or some object, then the mystery might unravel, in the same way as the decryption of the Rosetta Stone in Egypt, containing the same text in three languages, solved the puzzle of the hieroglyphs." No such object has been found.
2. **Unknown language family.** "Nor are we able to state definitively whether their language belonged to the Indo-Aryan or the Dravidian family of languages." This is crucial: knowing the underlying language would narrow the decipherment options dramatically.
3. **Short inscriptions.** With an average of five signs per inscription, there is insufficient statistical data for pattern analysis using the methods that worked for hieroglyphics or Linear B.
4. **No known cognate system.** Unlike Egyptian hieroglyphics (which had Coptic as a descendant) or Linear B (which was an early form of Greek), the Harappan script has no established surviving descendant.

Some scholars (Witzel and Farmer 2000; Witzel 2006) have questioned whether the Harappan inscription system should even be called a "script" in the full sense — suggesting it may be proto-writing or a sign system rather than a complete phonological writing system. This is noted by Avari but not endorsed.

## Relationship to Indian Scripts

The question of whether the Harappan script is ancestrally related to later Indian scripts, particularly Brahmi (the ancestor of most modern South and Southeast Asian scripts), remains open. Avari notes that non-scriptural graffiti found on some Kalibangan pottery "may be seen to anticipate the Mature Harappan script," suggesting the script itself developed progressively. However, no demonstrated genetic connection between the Harappan script and Brahmi has been established.

## Limitations and Critiques

The inability to read the Harappan script means that:
- All statements about Harappan religion, governance, political organisation, social structure, and self-identity are based on inference from material remains alone.
- The Harappan Civilisation's own name for itself is unknown.
- Relationships between Harappan sites (whether they constituted an empire, a federation, or independent city-states) cannot be confirmed through documentary evidence.

## Historiography

**Source quality:** All knowledge of the Harappan script is archaeological. There are no external decipherments and no surviving oral traditions that illuminate the script's content. The 4,200 inscribed objects constitute the entire corpus.

**Scholarly debates:** Whether the script represents a full writing system or a proto-writing/sign system; which language family underlies it; whether it is related to Brahmi; what the inscriptions on seals actually signified (commercial, administrative, religious, or all three). The Dravidian hypothesis for the underlying language (advanced by Parpola) is the most developed scholarly proposal but remains unproven.

**Collection coverage:** Avari (2007) gives a detailed treatment in Chapter 3. The Cambridge series and specialist Harappan scholarship (Possehl, Kenoyer, Mahadevan) hold the bulk of technical work on decipherment. The wiki's coverage of the script is adequate at the introductory level; deeper treatment would require specialist sources.
