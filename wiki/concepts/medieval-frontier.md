# Medieval Frontier

The **medieval frontier** is a historiographical category for the edges — and
internal seams — of medieval political and cultural systems, above all the zones
where Latin Christendom, Byzantium and Islam met one another and met pagan and
"Stone Age" societies. Its central modern claim, sharpened in Abulafia &
Berend's *[[abulafia-berend-medieval-frontiers-2002|Medieval Frontiers: Concepts
and Practices]]* (2002), is that the medieval frontier was **"not so much an
identifiable phenomenon, a hard fact, as ... a conceptual tool used by
historians"** — typically a *zone* of ambiguity, overlapping lordship and
negotiated control rather than a *line* on a map.

## Emic vs Etic

The category is largely **etic** — an analyst's construct. Medieval people had no
single word matching the modern "frontier"; the English word derives from the
military *front*, and cognate terms (Latin *marca*/"march", the Byzantine
*thema* of frontier provinces, Arabic *thughūr*) denoted militarised border
provinces, not sovereign boundary-lines. Yet frontier *consciousness* was
genuinely **emic** in places: contemporaries spoke of "marcher" lands in Wales,
Hungary and eastern Germany with a distinctive military and ethnic character, and
frontier ideologies (see below) were consciously articulated. The historian's
first task, Abulafia argues, is to recover the "political language of the time"
rather than impose modern jargon — he explicitly rejects "liminality" and
postmodern framing as obfuscation.

## The "Seven Types of Ambiguity" — Abulafia's Typology

Abulafia's introduction (its title borrowed from William Empson's 1930 work of
literary criticism) approaches the frontier from several distinct angles rather
than defining it once. The principal approaches:

1. **Historiographical frontiers** — the difficulty of describing a political
   world built on medieval assumptions (overlordship, divided tenure, contested
   sovereignty) in the vocabulary of the modern sovereign state.
2. **Economic frontiers (core and periphery)** — unequal exchange between
   "advanced" cores and lightly-populated peripheries, drawing on Wallerstein's
   world-systems idea but resisting its determinism; e.g. the "two Italies"
   (north vs Sicily/Sardinia), the Baltic rye lands supplying Hanseatic towns,
   and **internal** economic frontiers (marginal land colonised inside "old
   Europe," like the East Anglian Brecklands).
3. **Political frontiers ("a world without edges")** — the absence of hard lines
   under the theory of a single Christendom headed by pope and emperor; kings as
   "mere *reguli*"; papal and imperial claims to universal dominion (including
   over unclaimed islands, feeding into the Treaty of Tordesillas, 1494);
   customs stations and boundary markers as the real, patchy expressions of
   authority.
4. **Miniature frontiers (enclaves and anomalies)** — surviving Muslim enclaves
   in Christian Iberia (Crevillente, Minorca, Granada's "necklace" of forts),
   combined jurisdictions (Andorra), merchant colonies (*fonduks*, the Hanseatic
   Steelyards, the Genoese/Venetian overseas quarters), and pirate/mountain
   micro-states — revealing the underlying assumptions of medieval power.
5. **Cultural / linguistic frontiers** — language and identity as markers:
   linguistic conservatism (Mozarabic, Maltese as Siculo-Arabic, Yiddish,
   Ladino), the survival of enclave tongues, and the naming of neighbours
   ("Welsh"/*w-l-s* = "foreigners" from Wales to Wallachia).
6. **Mental frontiers ("distant horizons")** — how cross-cultural encounters
   altered mental worlds, and the methodological problem of reading descriptions
   of illiterate "others" (Canary Islanders, Arawak, Tupinambá) available only
   from the European side.

The recurring theme is **ambiguity**: no two frontier "experiences" were alike,
and any attempt to reduce them to a single formula "does violence to the
evidence."

## Frontier as Zone, Not Line

The volume's strongest shared finding, argued independently across chapters, is
that medieval frontiers were commonly **zones defined by strong points and
influence, not linear borders**:

- [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium's]] eastern frontier around Antioch and Aleppo
  was porous and negotiable, integrating Muslim lords rather than sealing them
  out (Holmes).
- In the [[crusader-states|Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem]], the space between
  Christian Acre and Muslim Damascus was a borderland of castles and shared
  pastures, not a mapped line (Ellenblum).
- In the western Mediterranean, [[reconquista|Reconquista]] Iberia was a
  patchwork of surrender treaties, Muslim "bubbles with a Christian skin"
  (Valencia), and tribute arrangements that concealed "a lack of communication
  between two incompatible worlds."

Where frontiers *did* sharpen into something like a line, it was usually because
of **natural defences** (Nasrid Granada's sierra), **treaty definition** (Louis
IX's treaties of Corbeil 1258 and Paris 1259), or a **sea** functioning as
border (the Danish "blue Baltic border").

## Frontier as Ideological Construct

A second major strand treats frontier identity as a **construct serving power**.
[[hungary-arpad|Hungary]] as "the Gate of Christendom" was an ideology built to
legitimise royal authority against nomads and, later, Mongols (Berend); the
Danish crusade against the pagan Wends was framed through Saxo Grammaticus's
chronicle (Jensen); crusade chroniclers "granted power to enemy gods" to make
Baltic paganism legible and defeatable (Mažeika). Such ideologies were not
neutral geography but tools of state- and church-building.

## Relation to the Turner Thesis

The comparative medieval-frontier field grew partly by transposing **Frederick
Jackson Turner's** American "frontier thesis" (1893) onto the Middle Ages — most
directly by Robert I. Burns for Valencia. Abulafia and his contributors keep
Turner at arm's length: the medieval frontier was rarely a moving line of
settlement into "empty" land (though internal colonisation of the German East and
marginal lands offers partial analogues), and far more often a durable zone of
coexistence, exchange and negotiated overlordship. See [[roman-frontiers]] for
the antique precedent (*limes* as zone rather than wall), and
[[american-exceptionalism]] / [[fragment-thesis]] for the Turnerian afterlife in
American historiography.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Over-generalisation risk.** Because "no two cases are identical," the concept
  resists a single definition and can dissolve into a list of case studies; the
  editors concede the volume has "something of the character of a miscellany."
- **Mediterranean bias corrected, others introduced.** The 2002 volume was a
  deliberate corrective to the Anglo-Celtic/German-East emphasis of Bartlett &
  Mackay (1989); but its own centre of gravity is the Mediterranean and
  Byzantium.
- **Anti-theory stance is itself contested.** Abulafia's rejection of
  "liminality" and world-systems jargon is a methodological position other
  frontier scholars (e.g. those in Power & Standen's *Frontiers in Question*,
  1999, more interested in "frontier control") do not share.
