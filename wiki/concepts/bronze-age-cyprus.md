# Bronze Age Cyprus

**Bronze Age Cyprus** (c. 2500/2300–1100 B.C.) saw the island transform from a village-oriented Chalcolithic society to an international, urban-centered producer and exporter of copper ("Alashiya" in ancient texts). Key drivers were the Secondary Products Revolution (plow, wool sheep, dairy), copper metallurgy from abundant ores, and integration into eastern Mediterranean trade networks involving the Levant, Egypt, Aegean, and later Sardinia/Italy.

## Cultural Sequence

**c. 2500–1700 B.C. (Early–Middle Cypriot):** Introduction of plow and reappearance of cattle mark Secondary Products Revolution. Ground stone tools increase; dependence on domestic animals rises. Copper use evident; expertise shown in north coast tombs (Lapithos, Bellapais Vounos, Vasilia). Foreign imports (pottery, metals, faience) from Levant/Egypt/Aegean recovered there. Earliest cuneiform reference to "Alashiya" copper in 19th c. B.C. Mari texts. Settlements increase in size/number/spread; specialization in wool/textiles, stone figurines, metal tools. Sites: Kissonerga-Mosphilia, Sotira-Kaminoudhia, Marki-Alonia, Alambra-Mouttes. Indigenous elites manage copper industry and trade.

**c. 1700–1100 B.C. (Middle–Late Cypriot):** Urban centers with public/ceremonial architecture ("temples") appear island-wide. Social status distinctions in burials (e.g. Tomb 11 at Ayios Dhimitrios with gold). Cypro-Minoan writing appears on tablets. Copper production/export intensifies with regional/long-distance trade; fortifications and weapons increase. Port cities (Hala Sultan Tekke, Maroni, Kition) prosper with inner harbors (now silted). Centralized storage (Ayios Dhimitrios: ~50 pithoi for 50,000 kg olive oil). Cuneiform letters from "Alashiya" to pharaoh show royal authority over copper; Ugarit documents diplomatic trade. Mixed farming (sheep/goats emphasis, some cattle); organic goods (coriander etc.) in Uluburun wreck illustrate invisible trade component.

**End of Bronze Age (1250–1150 B.C.):** Site destructions and "Sea Peoples" movements affect region. Cyprus less disrupted than mainland states; coastal sites (Enkomi, Kition, Palaepaphos) survive and revitalize copper production/trade. Early iron technology develops. New maritime routes (Crete, Sardinia). By 1100 B.C. Late Bronze Age settlement patterns and political organization give way to Iron Age kingdoms.

## Significance

Cyprus's circumscribed island environment + copper wealth + Secondary Products innovations enabled elite centralization and international role without full palatial economies of Levant/Aegean. "The name Cyprus... is directly related to the Latin word for copper—cuprum." Trade transformed social groups and economies across the Mediterranean.
