# Terra Nullius

**Terra nullius** ("land belonging to no one") names the doctrine by which the British
treated [[australia|Australia]] as legally vacant at the moment of colonisation, acquiring
sovereignty by **settlement** rather than by conquest or treaty with the
[[aboriginal-australians|Aboriginal inhabitants]]. Because the land was deemed unoccupied
in law, no recognition of Indigenous title, sovereignty, or prior possession followed —
distinguishing Australia from settler colonies (e.g. New Zealand, North America) where
treaties were at least nominally struck.

The doctrine is **emic** in its eighteenth- and nineteenth-century operation (an assumption
embedded in colonial law and land grants) and **etic** as later analysed and named by
historians and jurists. It is deeply **contested**: revisionist scholarship and the
Australian courts (culminating in *Mabo* 1992, outside this volume's period) rejected it.
Per [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013|CHA Vol. 1]], the assumption underwrote
[[australian-frontier-conflict|frontier dispossession]] and the legal architecture of the
settler colonies.

## Limitations and Critiques

The label flattens a more complicated legal history: contemporaries debated Aboriginal
land use, and the doctrine coexisted uneasily with humanitarian and protectionist
impulses. Critics argue "terra nullius" as a fixed doctrine is partly a back-projection;
its analytical value is in capturing the *effect* — dispossession without consent — rather
than a single codified rule.
