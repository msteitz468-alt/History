## Definition and Scope

The **muftī** (Arabic: muftī, pl. muftūn) is the jurisconsult of Islamic law — a qualified jurist whose authoritative opinion on a point of law (the **fatwā**, pl. fatāwā) was the primary vehicle through which [[sharia|Sharīʿa]] doctrine was applied to novel situations and through which legal change occurred in the pre-modern Islamic world. Distinct from the [[qadi-judiciary|qāḍī]] (the court judge), the muftī did not issue binding decisions but rendered authoritative legal opinions that functioned, in practice, as the normative basis of judicial rulings. Together, the muftī and the author-jurist (*muṣannif*) constitute what Hallaq identifies as the engine of legal development in classical Islamic law, the two roles often combined in a single scholar.

## The Muftī's Role: Formal and Informal

The muftī occupied two complementary positions in the Islamic legal system:

### 1. Grassroots Legal Authority
"The muftī was instrumental in propounding legal norms and legal knowledge at grassroots level." Ordinary Muslims — men and women, merchants, litigants, scholars, and rulers — could approach a muftī with a factual situation (a *wāqiʿa*) and receive an authoritative statement of what the law required. This opinion, while formally non-binding, "settled many disputes 'on the spot'" because "a disputant who failed to receive a fatwā in his favor was not likely to proceed to court, and would instead abandon his claim altogether or opt for informal mediation." The fatwā thus functioned as a pre-litigation filter that resolved a large share of disputes before they reached the qāḍī's court.

### 2. Court-Embedded Legal Advisor
From early Islamic history, "the Islamic legal tradition has insisted on the presence of muftīs, at times described as 'the people of knowledge' (ahl al-ʿilm), in the courts of law, both as advisors for the qāḍī on difficult points of law and as overseers-cum-witnesses of court proceedings." In the [[maliki-school|Mālikī]] courts of al-Andalus, the presence of court-sitting experts (mushāwirūn) was a formal legal requirement. In the eastern lands of Islam, the formal presence was less consistent, but the functional dependence was universal: "difficult cases were routinely referred to muftīs, local or distant. The bulk of fatwā literature at our disposal attests to the now well-established fact that fatāwā were requested by judges from muftīs who, at times, lived hundreds of miles away."

### The Fatwā's Paradoxical Authority
The fatwā's formal non-binding status is paradoxical in practice. Hallaq describes the paradox precisely: "While it is true that the fatwā is formally non-binding, because of the obvious reason that it does not qualify as a qāḍī's decision (ḥukm), it was nonetheless commonly accepted as the basis of court rulings, and rarely, if ever, ignored. When on occasion a fatwā was disregarded, it was usually because another fatwā constituted a more convincing and authoritative opinion." A judge who dismissed a fatwā in favor of his own opinion would only do so if "he happened to be of a juristic caliber higher than that enjoyed by the muftī from whom the fatwā was solicited."

This explains a structural feature of Islamic law that distinguishes it sharply from common-law systems: "Law was to be found not in precedent, or in a doctrine of Stare Decisis, but rather in the juristic corpus of the school, a corpus elaborated by the author-jurist and extracted for difficult and complex cases by the muftī." Court decisions were not binding precedent; fatāwā were the authoritative statement of law.

## The Author-Jurist (Muṣannif)

The muftī and the author-jurist (muṣannif, one who "compiles" law works) are closely linked roles, frequently held by the same person. Where the muftī provided authoritative opinions in response to specific factual situations, the author-jurist produced the comprehensive [[fiqh]] compendia, commentaries, and abridgments (*mukhtaṣar*s) that systematized the law:

> "The author-jurist's activity extended from writing the short but specialized risāla to compiling longer works, be it the mabsūṭ (lit. extended) or the sharḥ (commentary). It was mainly these two types of discourse that afforded the author-jurist the framework (and full opportunity) to articulate a modified body of law, one that reflected both the evolving social conditions and the state of the art in the law as a technical discipline."

The author-jurist incorporated fatāwā into the school's operative doctrine, weeding out opinions that had "fallen out of circulation" while "retaining opinions that had newly arisen or those that continued to be relevant to legal practice." The law developed through this "dialectical wheel": social disputes → court → muftī consultation → fatwā integration into fiqh compendium → updated doctrine guiding future courts.

## How Fatāwā Drove Legal Change

A crucial argument in Hallaq's analysis: Islamic law was not static between the "closing of the gates of ijtihad" and colonial modernization (as the older Western consensus held), but continuously adaptive, driven by the fatwā mechanism. Key features of this change process:

- **Organic and imperceptible**: "No sudden mutability was required, no ruptures, violent or otherwise, but rather a piecemeal modification of particular aspects of the law, and only when general and wide-ranging circumstances demanded such modifications."
- **Cases of 'widespread occurrence'**: Author-jurists specifically included "cases that were deemed relevant and necessary to the age in which they were writing" — explicitly citing "much needed cases" and those of "widespread occurrence" (*mā taʿummu bihi al-balwā*), while excluding cases that had "become irrelevant to the community and its needs."
- **Later opinions over founding masters**: "Reflecting the 'changing conditions of people and of the age', the author-jurists opted for later opinions that were at variance from the doctrines of the early masters." New fatāwā that "answered contemporary needs and had at once gained currency in practice" were absorbed; those that "ceased to be of use in litigation were excluded as weak or even irregular."

The famous legal maxim — "the fatwā changes with the changing of times and places" — was thus not a modern reformist slogan but a structural working principle of the pre-modern legal system.

## Fatwā Collections and Legal Literature

Fatāwā "emanating from the world of legal practice were collected and published, particularly those among them that contained new law or represented new legal elaborations on older problems that continued to be of recurrent relevance." The collections underwent editorial transformation: "legally irrelevant facts and personal details (e.g., proper names, names of places, dates, etc.) were omitted" and abstracted into "strictly legal formulas, usually of the hypothetical type: 'If X does Y under a set of conditions, then L (legal norm) follows.'" Whether abstracted or not, these fatwā collections became part of the authoritative legal literature of each [[madhhab]].

In [[hanafi-school|Ḥanafī]] law, the fatwā literature constituted the third (and quantitatively dominant) tier of authoritative doctrine, layered above the positions of the founding masters. In the [[maliki-school|Mālikī]] school, no formal tiered hierarchy existed, but absorption of fatāwā into author-jurist compilations was equally systematic.

## The Official Muftī: Şeyhülislam and State Fatāwā

Beyond the private muftī issuing opinions to individual clients, Islamic history developed the office of an official, state-appointed muftī, of which the Ottoman Şeyhülislam (Shaykh al-Islām) was the most powerful example. The Şeyhülislam headed the entire Ottoman judiciary, appointed qāḍīs across the Empire, and issued fatāwā with the weight of imperial sanction on matters of public policy, including — notably — the conduct of the sultan himself. Hallaq notes that "several Şeyhülislams often militated against the qānūn" — the sultanic law — "particularly against" its provisions permitting torture, legalized usury, and extra-judicial taxation, using the fatwā as the instrument of resistance.

In the [[maliki-school|Mālikī]] courts of al-Andalus, the mushāwirūn "often issued fatāwā bearing on the policies and conduct of the sovereign, who appointed them to various jurisdictions after consultation with judges" — a function somewhat parallel to the Ottoman Şeyhülislam but "politically less powerful."

In the Mamlūk Dār al-ʿAdl, a "distinguished muftī (or muftīs) whose erudition and legal knowledge earned him (or them) what might be termed epistemic charisma" was always present at the highest court hearings. See [[circle-of-justice]].

Under the Mughals, the imperial administration appointed "some muftīs in an official capacity" as legal advisors for both the government and their judge-colleagues, administered by the chief judicial officer (the ṣadr).

## The Muftī and the Moral Community

The muftī's authority was ultimately grounded not in political appointment but in scholarly reputation and the moral community of the [[ulama]]. This meant that even unofficial, privately practicing muftīs commanded authority: "as he constituted an accessible center of legal and moral authority, his opinion, though non-binding, settled many disputes 'on the spot.'" The muftī's social role as the articulate representative of the Sharīʿa in daily life made him, alongside the author-jurist, the institutional backbone of Islamic legal culture at the level below the ruler and his administrative machinery.

## Relationship to Other Concepts

- [[madhhab]]: The muftī always operated within a specific school, applying its doctrinal tradition; his fatwā carried the authority of the school's corpus.
- [[taqlid-and-ijtihad]]: The muftī exercised a form of ijtihād within the school tradition (extending doctrine to new cases), not the radical founding-level ijtihād of the early masters.
- [[madrasa]]: The institutional context in which muftīs were trained; professors of law in madrasas were typically also practicing muftīs.
- [[qadi-judiciary]]: The institutional partner of the muftī; the court depended on the muftī for difficult cases, while the muftī depended on the court for enforcement.
- [[ulama]]: The muftī is the most visible and active member of the scholarly class in direct engagement with social and legal problems.
- [[circle-of-justice]]: The muftī's participation in provincial dīwāns and the Dār al-ʿAdl made him part of the Circle's practical institutional machinery.
- [[waqf]]: Muftī opinions on waqf administration were among the most frequently solicited categories of fatwā in the pre-modern period.

## Historiography

The muftī and fatwā institution has become a major focus of Islamic legal history in recent decades, partly through the monumental study of Ottoman court records. Hallaq's own contributions (his studies "From Fatāwā to Furūʿ" and others cited in the source) are central to the revisionist case that the fatwā mechanism sustained continuous legal adaptation rather than stagnation. The Ottoman fatwā literature is extensively preserved; earlier Mamlūk and ʿAbbāsid collections are patchier. The collection is strong on the Ottoman muftī through legal history monographs; weaker on the Safavid and Qajar traditions. See [[hallaq-sharia-2009]].
