Islamic natural philosophy (*al-tabi'iyyat*, "the natural sciences") is the tradition of systematic reflection on the physical world — matter, motion, space, time, the continuum, and causation — developed by thinkers in the Islamic world from the 9th through the 13th centuries CE. Despite the multiplicity of schools (kalam atomism, Avicennian neo-Aristotelianism, Averroist Aristotelianism, mathematical infinitesimalism), the tradition was unified by shared engagement with two fundamental problems inherited from Aristotle: the status of the minima (the question of whether matter is infinitely divisible or made of irreducible parts), and the distinction between actuality and potentiality (especially regarding motion and the infinite).

## Definition and Scope

*Tabi'iyyat* corresponds roughly to Aristotle's "physics" in the broad sense — the study of nature (*physis*), including the works now grouped as Physics, De Caelo, De Generatione et Corruptione, and Meteorologica. In practice, the Arabic tradition concentrated most intensely on problems of motion (local, quantitative, and qualitative), the ontology of matter and form, the status of the void, and causation. It was not an isolated discipline: it intersected with [[kalam]] (Islamic theology), which had developed its own atomistic physics, and with mathematics (especially geometry and the theory of infinity).

The tradition is also distinct from [[islamic-science-medieval]], which covers the practical and empirical sciences (astronomy, optics, medicine); natural philosophy proper was concerned with the metaphysical foundations underlying those sciences.

## The Kalam Atomism of the Mutakallimun (ca. 830 CE)

The earliest systematic physics in the Islamic world was developed by the Basrian Mu'tazilite theologians, whose controversy "may be taken as marking the birth of a whole tradition of physics in Islam."

**Abu al-Hudhayl** (fl. ca. 820s) posited "indivisible parts" (*al-ajza' allati la tatajazza'*) that are: (1) non-corporeal (constituents of bodies, not bodies themselves); (2) without extension; and (3) indiscernible from one another. Motion consists in a body (assemblage of indivisibles) occupying one position and then another; indivisibles are separated by void. This is an "abstract" or "geometricizing" atomism quite different from ancient Greek corpuscular theories — the indivisible part is analogous to the Euclidean point.

**Al-Nazzam** accepted Abu al-Hudhayl's rejection of Aristotelian continua but rejected finitism. If atoms have no extension, then any finite distance contains an infinite number of positions; since nothing can traverse infinitely many positions in finite time, motion must involve "leaps" (*tafra*) — God annihilating and recreating the moving thing at each new position, skipping over intermediate ones. This makes every local motion a kind of perpetual miracle.

Al-Nazzam's leap theory had long echoes: it was known to Leibniz, who formulated a similar doctrine of "transcreation" (*transcreatio*) in the context of infinitesimal calculus.

**Abu Hashim al-Jubbai** (d. 933) and Abu al-Qasim al-Balkhi (d. 931) refined Abu al-Hudhayl's geometricizing atomism. Abu al-Qasim explicitly cited the Euclidean definition of the point as authority for the existence of minimal parts — "the first time in the long history of atomism, an atomist appeals to the Euclidean definition of the point as his authority." The implication is radical: mathematical realities are not abstracta from the sensible, but directly constitutive of it. The geometrical point and the material indivisible are identical.

## Thabit ibn Qurra and Mathematical Infinitesimalism

Thabit ibn Qurra (d. 901) combined al-Nazzam's infinitist approach with mathematical rigor in his treatise on actual infinity, without appealing to divine intervention to explain motion. For Thabit, a moving point (e.g., the soul leaving the body at death) traverses an actually infinite number of positions in a finite time — a genuinely mathematical, not theological, solution. This represents "a decisive event in the history of physics: the conscious combination of a 'philosophical' and a 'mathematical' theory of actual infinity."

## Avicenna's Dynamics

[[ibn-sina]] (Avicenna, d. 1037) produced "the single most important authority of preclassical physics" in the Islamic world — a reformulation of Aristotelian dynamics that influenced both Islamic and Latin scholars for generations. His approach addressed two connected problems:

**The law of motion.** John Philoponus (6th c. CE) had challenged Aristotle's law of motion (speed = force/resistance), proposing instead that speed is directly proportional to weight plus a medium-dependent increment. Some mutakallimun had gone further, arguing that in a void, all bodies fall at the same speed (since atoms are identical in constitution; differences in speed in a medium are explained by differences in atomic density cutting through the medium). Avicenna had to navigate between the Aristotelian inheritance and these challenges.

**Impetus and infinity.** Avicenna introduced a concept of *mayl* (inclination, impetus) to explain how a projectile continues to move after it leaves the hand. His most significant innovation was distinguishing types of impetus — particularly the "violent" impetus of sublunar bodies vs. the "natural" impetus of celestial bodies — and showing that a coherent doctrine of rectilinear (sublunar) impetus required admitting some form of actual infinity. This led him to reformulate Aristotle's discussion of infinity in the Physics.

## Averroes: Orthodox Aristotelianism

[[ibn-rushd]] (Averroes, d. 1198) represents a reactionary movement in natural philosophy — a return to "orthodox" Aristotelianism in contrast to Avicenna's neo-Aristotelian innovations. Averroes rejected Avicenna's modifications to physics as departures from the true Peripatetic doctrine, though his own position evolved through his three commentaries on the Physics (short, middle, and long). In the Latin West, Averroes' commentaries on Aristotle's natural books became the authoritative interpretive tradition; his nickname "the Commentator" reflects this.

## The Void Question

One of the most contested issues in Arabic natural philosophy was the existence of the void. Aristotle had denied the void on the grounds that motion requires a medium; the mutakallimun's atomism required void between atoms; Avicenna maintained the Aristotelian rejection of the void while revising other aspects of Aristotelian physics; and Averroes defended the void-rejection more consistently. The cornicular-angle argument used by Abu al-Qasim al-Balkhi against continuists was in fact a sophisticated mathematical critique that remains philosophically interesting.

## Relation to Kalam

A persistent tension runs through the entire tradition: the Aristotelian philosophers (falasifa) criticized the mutakallimun's physics as dependent on imagination rather than reason — the kalam atomists gave "excessive role to imagination" in their ontology. Yet the falasifa could not ignore kalam; Avicenna in particular was deeply engaged with its arguments. The question of whether the world is eternal (Aristotelian) or created in time (Islamic orthodoxy) was simultaneously a physical and a theological question that forced Arabic natural philosophers to engage with kalam positions on causation, actuality, and potentiality.

## Influence on Latin West

Arabic natural philosophy was transmitted to Latin Christendom primarily through Averroes' commentaries and translations of Avicenna's *Shifa'* (Sufficientia). Avicenna's doctrine of *mayl* was the antecedent of the Latin *impetus* theory developed by Jean Buridan and others in the 14th century. The kalam atomism of the mutakallimun had no direct Latin reception but parallels later medieval debates over minima naturalia.

## Historiography

Marwan Rashed's chapter in Adamson and Taylor's *Cambridge Companion to Arabic Philosophy* (2005) provides the most recent systematic account, emphasizing the geometricizing character of kalam atomism and Avicenna's dynamics as the central axis of the tradition. Shlomo Pines' earlier work on kalam physics (*Beiträge zur islamischen Atomenlehre*, 1936) remains the foundational study of the mutakallimun. A. I. Sabra and F. Jamil Ragep have contributed to the study of the mathematical dimensions. The interaction between kalam physics and Greek atomism vs. Aristotelianism is a live research question.

## Collection Coverage Note

The *Cambridge Companion to Arabic Philosophy* covers natural philosophy well for the formative and classical periods. The collection is weaker on the post-Averroes transmission of Arabic natural philosophy into Latin scholasticism and on the later commentarial tradition in the Islamic East.
