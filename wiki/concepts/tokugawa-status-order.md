# The Tokugawa Status Order (*Mibun*)

The system of **social status** (***mibun***) that structured early-modern [[japan|Japanese]]
society — the lived reality behind the textbook "four-class order" of [[samurai]], peasant,
artisan, and merchant. From Maren Ehlers's chapter in the
[[howell-early-modern-japan-asia-world|New Cambridge History of Japan Vol. II]] (ch. 17).

## Beyond the four classes

The familiar **four-status scheme** (*shi-nō-kō-shō*) is, current scholarship insists, a
simplification. Ehlers presents the [[edo-period|Tokugawa]] order as a society organized into
**many self-governing status groups**, each defined less by wealth or even occupation than by
**role and membership**: the warrior [[samurai]]; the **peasants** (*hyakushō*) of the
self-administering village; the **townspeople** (*chōnin*) of the urban ward; and, beyond these
principal groups, the **court nobility**, the **Buddhist and Shintō clergy**, doctors, and a
range of others — down to the **outcaste** groups (*eta* and *hinin*) burdened with
ritually "polluting" work (leatherwork, executions, mendicancy). This is the social face of
Bitō's *yaku* (function) and *mibun* concept already noted on
[[tokugawa-intellectual-life]].

## How status worked

Status was **collective and corporate**, not merely individual. Each status group held
**recognized privileges and obligations** — self-governance, monopoly rights over certain
livelihoods, internal dispute resolution — in exchange for performing its assigned **role and
duties** (*yaku*) to the [[bakuhan-system|bakuhan]] authorities. Ehlers's larger scholarship
(*Give and Take*) stresses the **reciprocity** in this: even the poorest and most marginal
groups — outcastes, mendicants — were bound into the order by mutual obligations of relief and
service, so that status membership offered "collective guarantees" as well as constraints. The
order was thus a framework of **negotiated interdependence**, not simply a top-down hierarchy
of oppression.

## Dissolution at the Meiji Restoration

The status order was legally abolished after the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji Restoration]] —
the *samurai* [[samurai|disestablished]], the formal categories swept away, and (in 1871) the
outcaste statuses legally emancipated. Ehlers stresses the **mixed and uneven** consequences:
for educated elites the removal of status was "liberating"; but millions of humble
townspeople, villagers, and mendicants lost the **collective guarantees** of their status
groups; **outcastes** gained legal freedom but faced "renewed discrimination" (the roots of
the modern *burakumin* problem); and **women**, while some embraced new equality discourse,
were explicitly **subordinated to men** in the new household registers. The end of the status
order "marked the beginning of a long struggle to gain a voice under a new system."

## Significance

The status order is the **social constitution** of early-modern Japan — the everyday structure
through which the [[bakuhan-system|bakuhan]] state actually reached people, and the thing that
the [[meiji-modernization|Meiji transformation]] had to dismantle to create a "homogenized
nation of commoners." Recovering its complexity (beyond the four classes) and its reciprocity
is central to the New CHJ's project of seeing Japanese [[kinsei-early-modernity|early
modernity]] on its own terms.
