# The "New Monarchies" of the Fifteenth Century

The **"new monarchies"** is the term for the strengthened, more centralised royal states that
emerged across Europe in the later fifteenth century, recovering and extending royal authority
after the disorders of the [[late-medieval-crisis|fourteenth-century crisis]], the
[[hundred-years-war-1337|Hundred Years War]], and the civil wars. [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]]
(Jean-Philippe Genet, Ch. 1) treats the fifteenth century not as "decay and decline" but as an age
of "dynamic achievements" in government — the consolidation that bridges the medieval and
early-modern state.

## The Phenomenon

After the mid-century low point, rulers across Europe rebuilt and extended their power on broadly
parallel lines: **standing armies** funded by regular **taxation**, professional **bureaucracies**
and councils, the curbing of the over-mighty nobility, and an increasingly **sacral and
propagandist** royal image. The classic cases are:

- **France** under **Charles VII** and **Louis XI** (1461–1483) — the recovery from the Hundred
  Years War, a permanent army (the *compagnies d'ordonnance*) and the *taille*, and the breaking of
  the great princes (the destruction of [[valois-dynasty|Burgundy]], 1477).
- **England** under the early **Tudors** (Henry VII, from 1485) — the end of the Wars of the Roses
  and the restoration of crown finance and order.
- **Spain** under the **Catholic Monarchs**, Ferdinand and Isabella, whose union of Castile and
  Aragon (1469/1479), conquest of [[nasrid-granada|Granada]] (1492), and use of the Inquisition
  forged the most powerful of the new monarchies.

## Political Culture

Genet stresses that this was also a transformation of **political culture**. The written word and
**propaganda** became "a well-established practice" — proclamations, pamphlets, political prophecy,
the speech-sermons opening the English parliament, the French estates general, and the German
*Reichstag*. Political ideas circulated widely (Genet quotes Philippe Pot: "In the first place the
sovereign people created kings by its vote"), and **representative estates** remained part of the
machinery of the strengthened state even as royal power grew — the tension between
[[english-parliament|representation]] and centralisation being a defining theme.

## Significance and Critique

The "new monarchy" is a useful but **contested** label: critics note that the fifteenth-century
states built on thirteenth- and fourteenth-century [[state-formation|foundations]] rather than
inventing royal centralisation, that the "newness" was uneven, and that the [[holy-roman-empire-late-medieval|Empire]]
and Italy followed quite different (federal, city-state) paths. Still, it captures a real
convergence: by 1500 the strongest European realms were more centralised, better financed, and more
militarily formidable than their fourteenth-century predecessors — the platform for the
early-modern sovereign state and for overseas expansion.
