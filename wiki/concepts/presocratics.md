# The Presocratics (the Birth of Greek Philosophy)

The **Presocratics** were the first Greek thinkers to attempt a **reasoned, secular
account of nature** in place of the mythical cosmologies of Homer and Hesiod — the
intellectual breakthrough that [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 7b, G. S. Kirk)
sets within the broader Archaic "development of ideas, 750–500 BCE." It is the Greek
contribution to what is elsewhere called the **[[axial-age|Axial Age]]**.

## Why Miletus, and not the mainland

Kirk's central question is **why philosophy began at Miletus** rather than at Corinth or
even Athens, "among the most intellectually advanced places in Greece." His answer is
sociological: Miletus enjoyed the "**simpler constitution of an originally colonial
settlement**," a primarily **mercantile economy** relatively free of the town-vs-country
class war that consumed Solonian Athens, **cosmopolitan contact** with Lydia, Sardis and
beyond (Babylonian astronomy, Egyptian and Near Eastern myth), and **political stability**.
Athens, by contrast, channelled its energy into a "totalitarian reinterpretation" of myth
and ritual in service of the city (the cult of Theseus, the reorganized Panathenaea) —
"hardly conducive to a passionate interest in explaining the world at large."

## The Milesian school

- **Thales** (fl. early 6th c.) — engineer and adviser as much as theorist: he predicted
  an eclipse (drawing on Babylonian records), diverted a river, urged an Ionian federation,
  and posited **water** as the originative substance (a synthesis of Near Eastern
  water-myths — Oceanus, Apsu/Tiamat, Nun).
- **Anaximander** — made a world-map, introduced the *gnomon*, studied fossils, and
  preferred an **indeterminate** originative substance (the *apeiron*).
- **Anaximenes** — took **air/mist**, condensing and rarefying, as a source that combined
  an *apeiron*'s open-endedness with a continuing presence in the world.

Kirk stresses their **pragmatic** character: "engineers and inventors as much as
theoreticians," and 'philosophers' only in a secondary sense. They largely dropped mythical
language, treated the cosmos as a **developing organism** (the genealogical model of
differentiation lay ready to hand), and were "obsessed by the idea of an *archē* (beginning)."

## Heraclitus and Pythagoras

- **Heraclitus of Ephesus** (c. 500) — "arguably the most important of all the Presocratics"
  — rejected cosmogony altogether for a model of **cosmic permanence under a regular
  dispensation**: an ever-living **Fire** "kindling in measures and extinguishing in
  measures," governed by **Logos/Measure** (resembling the Diké of Zeus). By making his
  directive Fire operate alike in nature and in the soul, he joined **psychology and
  epistemology** to cosmology — "a genuine, if primitive, kind of philosophy in itself."
- **Pythagoras of Samos** (b. c. 570; emigrated to **Croton** to escape
  [[polycrates|Polycrates]]) — combined mathematics and superstition (the bean-taboo, the
  worship of the *decad*) with the profound intuition of the **mathematical structure of
  the universe**. Kirk warns against the old east–west cliché (pragmatic Ionia vs.
  metaphysical West): the "shamanistic element" broke out sporadically across Greece, and
  both Heraclitus and Pythagoras were Ionians by birth.

## Significance and limits

The Presocratics mark the **emancipation of the human and natural order from theology** —
the slow shift Kirk and [[polis-religion|J. K. Davies]] both trace toward secularization.
But Kirk is careful: in its early stages philosophy "had no popular following and small
social effect"; Homer remained a moral force, organized religion gained fresh support
(Eleusis, Delphi), and "many Greeks remained reactionary, pious and irrational." The
movement was "complex and discontinuous," its excesses "reserved for the sophists and
demagogues of the century that followed."

## Philosophy reaches Athens (CAH V)

[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 8h, Ostwald) records the moment Ionian natural
philosophy **reached Athens**, which "had not yet produced any philosophers of its own." It
was **Anaxagoras of Clazomenae** — the associate of [[pericles]], with his doctrine of *Nous*
(Mind) as the cause of motion — who "brought philosophy into the city"; by the century's end
his books sold for a drachma and his ideas saturated Euripides. Others passed through:
**Parmenides** and **Zeno** of Elea (the latter lecturing to Pericles), the atomist
**Democritus** of Abdera, and **Diogenes** of Apollonia (whose air-cosmology was familiar
enough to be parodied in Aristophanes' *Clouds*). The first **native Athenian philosopher**,
**Archelaus**, studied with Anaxagoras and was said to be **Socrates'** teacher. Pollitt (Ch.
8a) sees the **two traditions** — Pythagorean mathematical **idealism** and the relativism the
[[sophists]] derived from Protagoras — held in tension in the [[classical-greek-art|art of the
Parthenon]]. The reception, however, opened a rift between intellectuals and the people: the
impiety trials of Anaxagoras and Protagoras prefigure that of [[athenian-drama|Socrates]].

## Historiography

The Presocratics survive only in **fragments and later testimonia** (Aristotle, the
doxographers, Diels–Kranz), so reconstruction "is a special and difficult field" where
"serious mistakes of emphasis" are easy. Kirk's own sociological explanation of why
philosophy arose at Miletus, and his reading of Heraclitus' Fire as a transformed Zeus-Diké
pattern, are interpretive positions, not settled fact.
