# The Amarna Letters

The **cuneiform diplomatic archive** of the Egyptian court of [[amenhotep-iii|
Amenophis III]] and [[akhenaten|Akhenaten]] — some 350+ clay tablets, mostly
**letters in Akkadian** (the diplomatic lingua franca), found at
[[amarna|Akhetaten]] in 1887. They are the single richest source for the
[[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze]] **"great-powers system"** and for the political
geography of Syria-Palestine, and they give the whole age its name. The archive
underpins the [[interpolity-law|Late Bronze international system]] and is treated
across [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]] (Chs. XVII–XX) — Ch. XX
(Albright) covering the ~150 letters from Palestine.

## Two kinds of correspondence

1. **The "great kings."** Letters between the pharaoh and his royal peers —
   [[mitanni|Tushratta of Mitanni]], [[shuppiluliuma-i|Šuppiluliuma of Hatti]],
   [[kassites|Burnaburiaš II of Babylon]], [[ashur-uballit-i|Ashur-uballit of
   Assyria]], the king of Alašiya ([[cyprus|Cyprus]]). These address one another as
   "brother," exchange dynastic marriages and lavish gifts (Egyptian **gold**,
   "which in your land is dust," for Asiatic lapis, horses and chariots), and haggle
   like merchants — a form of "**state trading**" that documents the diplomacy,
   gift-economy and marriage-alliance machinery of the period.
2. **The vassals.** Letters from the petty princes of Syria-Palestine — Rib-Adda of
   [[byblos|Byblos]], Aziru of [[unknown|Amurru]], Labaya of Shechem,
   ʿAbdi-Heba of Jerusalem, Biridiya of Megiddo and dozens more — grovelling before
   "my Sun, my god," denouncing one another as traitors, and begging endlessly for
   troops that rarely came. They expose the **slow collapse of Egyptian control** in
   Asia under Akhenaten (see [[akhenaten]]).

## The language

The Palestinian letters are written in a **scholastic, hybrid jargon** — Akkadian as
learned and mis-learned by Canaanite scribes in local schools, "abounding with
Canaanitisms in vocabulary, syntax... and a formalizing of mistakes." To read them
one must know not only Akkadian but Hebrew and Ugaritic. They thus also document the
**[[unknown|Canaanite]] language substratum** two centuries before the Hebrew Bible,
and the spread of cuneiform literacy (a fragment of the **Gilgamesh epic** turned up
at Megiddo).

## What they reveal

- the **four-power balance** in Syria (Egypt, Mitanni, Hatti, with Babylon and
  Assyria pressing in) and its tipping toward Hatti;
- the **feudal vassal order** of Canaan — "kings" (*šarru*) who were really mayors
  (*ḫazannu*), many with **Indo-Aryan names**, the *maryannu* aristocracy over a
  Hurrian/Canaanite mass;
- the **demography of Palestine** (Albright's estimate: a sparse total population of
  perhaps a quarter-million), corvée, tribute, and rampant official **corruption**;
- the **[[habiru|ʿApiru/Habiru]]** — the stateless, raiding outsiders who appear all
  over the correspondence and whose name became one of the great problems of Near
  Eastern history;
- the careers of figures like **Labaya of Shechem**, an ʿApiru-allied dynast whose
  seizure of Shechem some have linked (cautiously) to Genesis 34 and the later
  Hebrew hold on the city.

## Significance

The Amarna letters are the **foundation document of Late Bronze international
history** — the reason the period's diplomacy, unlike that of most ancient ages, can
be read almost contemporaneously. They are equally central to the historical
geography and demography of Canaan on the eve of Israel, and to the
[[habiru|ʿApiru/Hebrew]] question. Their chronology (anchored on Egyptian regnal
years, ~1389–1358 BCE by this volume's reckoning) is a key node in the
[[mesopotamian-chronology|Late Bronze synchronisms]].
