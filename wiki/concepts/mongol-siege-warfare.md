# Mongol Siege Warfare

Mongol **siege warfare** evolved from early weakness against fortifications to a standardized
operating procedure under [[genghis-khan|Chinggis Khan]] and his successors: reduce outlying
places first, levy captives as labour and arrow-fodder, **circumvallation** walls, concentrated
catapult fire, tunneling, damming, and night assault after breach. Muslim and Chinese **engineers**
were indispensable throughout the empire's existence. [[may-mongol-art-of-war-2016|May]] treats the
Khwarazmian campaign (Bukhara 1220, Otrar) and later Russian sieges (Vladimir 1238) as
exemplars of the mature system.

## Early weakness and engineer incorporation

"The army's inability to conduct sieges effectively during the early days of the Mongol conquests
was a weakness that Chinggis Khan and his generals knew would have to be overcome if they were to
hold territory." As successes grew, "the Mongols incorporated engineers into their armies. These
were either conscripted or came over to the Mongols voluntarily." Nonetheless they "remained
dependent on Muslim and Chinese engineers – who both manned and manufactured their artillery and
other siege equipment – throughout the entire existence of their empire." (L577–579)

## Engineers and military-industrial supply

Beyond field armies, Mongol rulers did not leave armament to individual soldiers alone. Rulers
"moved hundreds of craftsmen and artisans from Central Asia, Persia, and China" to military-industrial
colonies (Chinqai Balasghun, Besh Baliq, Xinmalin, Hongzhou) "purely so that they could benefit
from their skills." (L80–81) These centers "were industrial and agricultural centers whose raison
d'être was to supply the Mongol armies and the court," continuing Khitan garrison-town smithy
traditions on an expanded scale. (L82–83)

"The Mongols were quick to adapt to siege warfare once they understood it" and, like regular
taxation, "recognized the value of possessing a regular supply of arms and armor." Ögödei purchased
"bone arrowheads, bows, and even more mundane items such as leather thongs and bags for the
military" from merchants — plunder supplemented but did not replace systematic procurement. (L84–85)
Individual soldiers still made arrows and leather armor, but sedentary and nomadic artisans both
supplied higher-quality equipment. (L86–87)

At Nishapur the Mongols "amassed enough weaponry to overawe its defenders, who were allegedly
defended by 300 ballistae and catapults along with 3,000 crossbows" — indicating a large engineer
corps even if figures are exaggerated. (L483–484)

## Campaign sequencing

The Mongols "delayed beginning major sieges until the later part of a campaign, starting instead
with the reduction of smaller outlying places." (L580–581) This conserved manpower for major
targets. Inaccessible cities faced **blockade** to starve defenders; strongholds might be
**bypassed** and isolated until hunger or diplomacy ended resistance; if reduction failed, a
**counter fortress** blockaded the target. (L582–583)

## Circumvallation

After the Khwarazmian War, **circumvallation** became standard. Nasawi recorded that against a
heavily fortified citadel "the Mongols constructed around Hal a wall pierced with openings that
they close at night and open during the day." (L584–588) Russian sources likewise note cities
"surrounded with walls and then built catapults, ladders, and other siege weapons." (L600–601)

At **Vladimir (1238)** the Mongols "isolated the city by surrounding it with a wall before
bombarding it with catapults, arrows, and fire arrows, and attacking it with battering rams operated
by levies." After breach they mounted swift assault before moving to the next stronghold. (L601)

## Levies and siege standard operating procedure

Prior to siege the Mongols "collected numerous captives and conscripts from conquered cities and
villages to serve as corvée labor and arrow fodder." Populations were divided into units of ten;
"each Mongol soldier received a unit." Captives gathered "grass, wood, earth and stone"; stragglers
were executed. (L592–593)

At the objective, levies "quickly filled the moat or defensive trench with stones and other
materials – bundles of straw, wood, and debris – so that the Mongols could reach the walls." Prisoners
built siege engines under Chinese and Persian engineers and maintained "a constant barrage on the
city in order to prevent the enemy from resting," using naphtha and possibly Greek Fire; Carpini
reported fat from killed people thrown as nearly inextinguishable incendiary. (L593–594)

Levies dug trenches, erected defenses, and worked battering rams under canopies; flight meant death
by Mongols, while defenders' fire meant probable death from the other side. (L594–595) Tunnels
undermined walls; nearby rivers could be dammed to flood streets (as at Xi-Xia). (L596)

During bombardment Mongols stayed out of range; after breach they donned armor and attacked, often
at night. "The conscripted levies did most of the dangerous work and the Mongols only exposed
themselves when they were required to engage in combat" — conserving Mongol troops. (L597–598)
"These tactics were standard operating procedure for the Mongols throughout their conquests."
(L599)

## Case study: Bukhara and Otrar (1220)

The Khwarazmian campaign illustrates the full sequence. At **Otrar** (autumn 1220) Chinggis Khan
"laid siege" until the city fell; Governor Inal Khan was executed "by having his ears and eyes
filled with molten silver." (L393–395)

At **Bukhara**, Chinggis Khan's army "fanned out and began a wide encircling movement which cut
communications between the city and Muhammad Khwarazmshah," while refugees from outlying districts
strained food and water. (L397–400) Commander Kok-Khan's sunrise sally surged through "a gap in
the Mongol lines, which had perhaps been intentionally left open"; when the Khwarazmians did not
return, Mongols pursued from rear and flanks. (L401–402)

After the city's submission, the citadel held out. Chinggis Khan ordered Bukhara razed; Mongols
"organized the population into a workforce and set up their siege engines, and when the mangonels,
ballistae, ladders, and battering rams were ready the Mongols assailed the citadel. The population
of Bukhara now served as arrow fodder, manning the rams and carrying debris to fill the moat."
(L409–410) Moat filling used "stones, faggots, rubble, and the bodies of the Bukharan populace";
defenders hurled stones and naphtha but conscripts continued under threat of execution. (L411)

Survivors were then driven out; "young and strong men" were selected "to serve as laborers and
arrow-fodder" for the march on Samarqand. (L415–416) — the levy pipeline that fed later sieges.

## Weatherford (2004) — learning curve and population as weapon

[[weatherford-genghis-khan-making-modern-world-2004|Weatherford]] stresses the **Tangut
(Xi Xia) campaigns of 1207–09** as the dress rehearsal for Jurched siege warfare: Tangut
forces ~150,000 (nearly twice Genghis's army); first Yellow River diversion flooded the
**Mongol** camp rather than the capital — "in the future, the Mongols would use this method
again, but each time they would be more adept." From Chinese engineers they acquired
catapult, trebuchet, and ballista, and "acquired the engineering intelligence needed to
create them"; the **firelance** (bamboo gunpowder tube) was encountered among the Jurched
and later adapted. Siege engines, he argues, fascinated Mongols because they kept attackers
"well outside of the city and away from the danger of the person-to-person combat that they
so abhorred."

On population management after Khwarazm: empty each hostile city of people and animals
before looting (to avoid Zhongdu-style chaotic plunder); kill soldiers; sort literate
professionals and craftsmen for Mongol use; use the unskilled as labor, human shields, and
moat fill; slaughter those of no use; kill aristocrats to "decapitate" society (contrasted
with European ransom chivalry). Each warrior was expected to round up ~ten locals; refugees
were stampeded into cities to clog roads and strain supplies. Cities that surrendered then
revolted after thin Mongol occupation were destroyed utterly on return — "An annihilated
city could not revolt again." At Riazan (1237–38) Weatherford describes full **nerge**
stockade + multi-day bombardment of rocks, naphtha, gunpowder smoke/smell bombs, and
firelances/rockets/grenades before ladders and rams — late mature siege combining Chinese-
derived gunpowder with steppe encirclement (detail that enriches May's Vladimir case study).
