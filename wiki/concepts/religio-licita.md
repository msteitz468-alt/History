# Religio Licita

**Religio licita** (Latin for "permitted or approved religion") is an analytical and historical concept describing the legal status of Judaism within the Roman Empire and its subsequent transformation in the post-Roman successor kingdoms of the early Middle Ages. In the Roman framework, it permitted Jews to practice their ancestral religion, exempting them from the imperial cult, a privilege that was not extended to early Christians.

---

## Emic vs. Etic Perspectives

*   **Emic Perspective (Historical Use)**: The specific term *religio licita* was not a formal category of Roman statute law. Instead, it was coined by the Christian apologist **Tertullian** in his *Apologeticum* (c. 197 CE) as an rhetorical contrast to describe the imperial toleration of Judaism. In the minds of Roman administrators and Jewish subjects, the relationship was framed not by a single abstract concept, but by a series of specific imperial privileges (*privilegia*), charters, and exemptions granted by emperors and Julius Caesar.
*   **Etic Perspective (Modern Historiography)**: Modern historians use *religio licita* as a shorthand for the late Roman legal system's treatment of Jews as a distinct, legal religious group with protected assemblies, synagogues, and court jurisdiction. In early medieval studies, the term highlights the transition from this Roman legal inheritance to the exclusionary Christian state policies of the post-Roman West.

## Historical Development and Successor Kingdoms

### The Roman Inheritance
Under Roman law, ancestral custom (*mos maiorum*) was highly valued. Because Judaism was ancient and tied to a specific nation, Roman authorities tolerated its practice. Jews were exempt from sacrificing to the state gods and the emperor, contributing instead a special tax (the *fiscus Judaicus*) after the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE. The *Theodosian Code* (438 CE) consolidated these laws, declaring that the Jewish sect was not outlawed (*non esse illicitam*) but restricting new synagogue construction and proselytization.

### Ostrogothic Italy: Continuation of Roman Law
In the Ostrogothic Kingdom (c. 493–553 CE), **Theoderic the Great** strictly maintained the Roman legal framework. Following his principle of *civilitas*, Theoderic upheld the privileges of the Jews under Roman civil law. When Christian mobs burned synagogues in Rome, Milan, and Ravenna, Theoderic ordered the local municipal authorities to rebuild them at the perpetrators' expense. His minister **Cassiodorus** famously recorded Theoderic's declaration to the Jews: *"We cannot command religion, because no one is forced to believe against his will"* (*Variae* II.27).

### Merovingian Francia: Pragmatic Tolerance
In Merovingian Gaul (c. 500–750 CE), the Roman legal status of Jews was modified but broadly preserved. Jews participated in local commerce, agriculture, and municipal life. While Church councils (such as the Council of Orléans in 538 and Mâcon in 581) repeatedly legislated to separate Christians and Jews—forbidding Jews from holding public office, owning Christian slaves, or appearing in public during Easter—these canons were often pragmatically ignored by secular authorities. King **Chilperic I** (r. 561–584) attempted forced baptisms of Parisian Jews, but this was an isolated event rather than a systematic state program, and Gregory of Tours records that many converts practiced in secret or relapsed.

### Visigothic Iberia: Dismantling the Roman Framework
The most radical departure from the *religio licita* framework occurred in Visigothic Spain, especially after the conversion of King **Reccared** from Arianism to Catholicism at the **[[third-council-of-toledo-589|Third Council of Toledo (589)]]**:
1.  **Sisebut's Persecutions (r. 612–621)**: In 616 CE, King Sisebut issued a royal decree ordering the forced baptism of all Jews in the kingdom under pain of exile and property confiscation. This was the first systematic attempt by a Christian state to eradicate Judaism.
2.  **Isidorean Ambivalence**: **Isidore of Seville** criticized Sisebut's forced conversions as lacking theological justification ("not according to knowledge"), but he and successive councils of Toledo nevertheless ruled that forced baptisms were legally binding and that the children of converts must be raised as Christians.
3.  **Total Legal Exclusion**: The **[[liber-iudiciorum|Liber Iudiciorum]]** (654 CE) under Chindasuinth and Reccesuinth eliminated Roman law and established a unified territorial code that banned all Jewish rites (Sabbath, circumcision, dietary laws).
4.  **Egica and the 17th Council (694 CE)**: Under King Egica, the 17th Council of Toledo accused Jews of conspiring with Muslim powers across the straits. The council declared all Jews in Spain to be state slaves, ordered their children to be removed and raised by Christian families, and confiscated their entire property. This marked the absolute destruction of the Roman legal framework.

## Limitations and Critiques

While *religio licita* is a useful term for contrasting Christian illegalization with Roman toleration, historians like **Leonard Rutgers** and **Tessa Rajak** caution that using it can create an anachronistic view of Roman law. It implies a modern, bureaucratic system of licensed vs. unlicensed religions that did not exist in antiquity. Instead, Roman policy was reactive, local, and based on custom rather than a coherent statute of religious toleration.
