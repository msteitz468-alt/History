## Definition and Scope

Hawaiian historiography and nation refers to the intellectual, cultural, and political movement that emerged from Native Hawaiian (Kānaka Maoli) scholars, activists, and language practitioners from the 1970s onward, culminating in a distinctive body of scholarship that centres Hawaiian-language sources, asserts political sovereignty, and integrates environmental, cultural, and linguistic revitalisation as inseparable from historical understanding. Jonathan Osorio, writing from inside this movement, traces three lineages — **Kūʻē and Kūʻokoʻa** (resistance and sovereignty), **ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi** (Hawaiian language), and **Aloha ʻĀina** (love of the land/environmental stewardship) — as "distinct and at the same time significantly connected." No serious study of modern Hawaiʻi can proceed "without some understanding of all of these intellectual lines." [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]]

## Intellectual Genesis (1960s–1980s)

The modern Hawaiian scholarly movement grew out of Pacific Islands History as taught at the University of Hawaiʻi by scholars such as David Hanlon and Brij Lal, who taught that "perspective changed one's whole understanding of history. The mere publication of a study did not ensure a necessarily accurate portrayal of a person, people, or community." For post-World War II American-schooled Native Hawaiians, these lessons "were an intellectual liberation."

The 1970s saw the Pacific Islands academy wrestling with the "legitimacy of oral testimonies, and how they were to be regarded in comparison to written documents produced by drifters, explorers, traders, missionaries, and colonial administrators." Language revitalisation became the immediate focus. Crucially, the 1978 Hawaiʻi Constitutional Convention — to which Kānaka Maoli delegates were elected — created the Office of Hawaiian Affairs, mandated the teaching of Hawaiian language and Hawaiian Studies in public schools, and opened the pathway for Native land claims.

## Kūʻē and Kūʻokoʻa (Resistance and Sovereignty)

The early 1980s produced "a rise in the critical analysis of American and European colonialism in Hawaiʻi through the works of Indigenous scholars such as Haunani-Kay and Mililani Trask, Lilikalā Kameʻeleihiwa, Pōkā Laenui, and Kekuni Blaisdell." Following Memmi, Fanon, Ngũgĩ, and Foucault, Trask and others "laid a foundation for later writers like Kanalu Young, Jon Osorio, and Noenoe Silva to develop a deepening critique of Euro-American racism and exploitation in Hawaiʻi and in Oceania."

Key works in this lineage:

- **Haunani-Kay Trask**, *From a Native Daughter* (1993) — "has had the greatest influence on political and historical scholarship in Hawaiʻi to the present day." Trask and Mililani Trask formed Ka Lāhui Hawaiʻi in 1987, the first Native Hawaiian governing initiative, registering more than 20,000 native citizens within three years.
- **Lilikalā Kameʻeleihiwa**, *Native Land and Foreign Desires* (1992) — historical account of the 1847 Māhele land transformation that created property rights to assist the sugar industry.
- **George Terry Kanalu Young**, *Rethinking the Native Hawaiian Past* (1998) — focused on *hana lawelawe* (serving chiefs), allowing "a whole generation of Native Hawaiian undergraduate and graduate students to identify with historical figures."
- **Jonathan Osorio**, *Dismembering Lāhui* (2002) — studied the Kingdom legislatures and four constitutions (1840–1887) to describe "the Kānaka Maoli reaction and strategies to the slow and insinuative process of colonization."
- **Noenoe Silva**, *Aloha Betrayed* (2004) — analysed Hawaiian-language newspapers (*nupepa*) of the 1860s–1870s to reveal "strategies that Kānaka adopted to record their way of life and to register their political values and criticism of the growing American presence."
- **Keanu Sai**, *Ua Mau ke Ea: Sovereignty Endures* (2011) — portrayed American control as "a prolonged occupation," influencing the current generation and "the rise of several Hawaiian Kingdom entities."
- **Kamana Beamer**, *No Mākou ka Mana: Liberating the Nation* (2014) — affirmed that "the creation and operation of the constitutional monarchy in Hawaiʻi was a proactive and successful attempt to protect the Islands' political sovereignty during the critical 1840s."
- **Noelani Goodyear-Kaʻōpua**, *The Seeds We Planted* (2013) and *A Nation Rising* (2014) — examined the Hawaiian charter school movement and protest movements from the 1970s onward.
- **Noelani Arista**, *The Kingdom and the Republic* (2019) — contends with historians who do not work with Hawaiian-language texts.

"For all of these teacher/author/activists since Trask, the recovery of political power for Hawaiians was the most important goal, and for all of us it was a power independent of the American state." Hawaiian political aspirations are fundamentally distinct from electoral power-sharing: "Genuine political leaders in the Hawaiian community may favour full independence from the USA, or a nation-within-a-nation relationship with the Americans, but what they seem to be in agreement about is that a Hawaiian government would be separated, if not wholly from the USA, at least from the State of Hawaiʻi."

## ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi (Hawaiian Language)

The creation of **Pūnana Leo** (Hawaiian-language preschools) in 1984, modelled on the Māori Kohanga Reo, opposed an 1896 law requiring English-only education. Founders Kauanoe Kimura, Pila Wilson, and Kauanoe Kamanā secured legislation in 1987 creating **Kula Kaiapuni** (Hawaiian language immersion schools within public elementary schools). These efforts were simultaneously political protest and cultural recovery, challenging "the legitimacy and morality of the American takeover."

Two institutional centres now anchor the language movement:
- **Ka Haka ʻUla o Keʻelikōlani College of Hawaiian Language** (University of Hawaiʻi at Hilo): created by Pūnana Leo founders, offering a language immersion and instructional model from preschool to PhD.
- **Kawaihuelani Center for Hawaiian Language** (University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa): scholarship plumbing "the huge literary archive of the Kingdom's Hawaiian-language newspapers" to understand ancient Hawaiian culture through "nineteenth-century Hawaiian observers, composers, writers, and political leaders."

**Puakea Nogelmeier**, *Mai Paʻa I Ka Leo: Historical Voices in Hawaiian Primary Materials* (2010) — provides foundations for publications using Hawaiian-language sources; introduced research into *nupepa*, "an archive of quarter of a million newspaper pages recorded on microfilm and microfiche." His students include Kapā Oliveira, kuʻualoha hoʻomanawanui, Bryan Kuwada, Tiffany Ing, and Alohalani Brown.

**Noenoe Silva**, *The Power of the Steel Tipped Pen* (2017) — intellectual history of Hawaiian literature through the lives of nineteenth-century writers Joseph Kanepuʻu and Joseph Poepoe.

"Increasingly, academic studies of Hawaiian history and culture by researchers who have no knowledge of our language simply do not enjoy a great deal of credibility or relevance for Native scholars."

## Aloha ʻĀina (Stewardship of the Land)

Environmental advocacy is "the clearest and most consistent political advocacy that unites Hawaiians across the political spectrum." From the early struggles against urbanisation on Oʻahu and the successful 1970s protest halting US military operations on Kahoʻolawe, to geothermal opposition (1989–92) and the Mauna Kea Thirty Meter Telescope protest (1998–2019), these movements are unified: "Kānaka Maoli are *pilina* (connected) to the ʻāina and every story of our people is, in one way or another, the story of that relationship."

Key works and figures:
- **Davianna McGregor**, *Nā Kuaʻāina: Living Hawaiian Culture* (2007) — stories of "back country" people who maintained subsistence practices "despite enormous pressures from urbanisation and tourism."
- **Pua Kanahele** — taught *Papakū Makawalu* (eight eyes), a method of categorising knowledge linking the physical world and human existence to ancestral names for phenomena; "her influence on Hawaiian scholars is at least as significant as Keanu Sai's influence on political advocacy."
- **Kapua Sproat** and **Malia Akutagawa** (UH Law/Hawaiian Studies) — work on Hawaiian land rights grounded in Kingdom laws and traditional and customary practices.
- **Noʻeau Peralto** — dissertation on the ahupuaʻa of Koholālele and Kukaʻiau using nineteenth-century mele and newspaper sources.
- **Jamaica Osorio** — retelling of the Hiʻiaka saga through Aloha ʻĀina values.
- **Kahikina DeSilva** — 2018 dissertation discovering continuous affirmation of Aloha ʻĀina in *mele* (songs) across Hawaiian history.

Hawaiian music and performance have been inseparable from political protest. *ʻĪlioʻulaokalani* (Red Dog of the Heavens), formed by hula schools in 1998 to oppose Senate Bill 8 (restricting Native Hawaiians' gathering rights), drummed at the State Capitol every hour on the hour and succeeded in defeating the bill. The Mauna Kea protest of 2019 — coordinated by social media, with protesters invoking *Kapu Aloha* (a loving devotion to the sacred) — blocked construction of the Thirty Meter Telescope.

Osorio reads these protests as constituting a "paradigmatic change in how we create and organize knowledge," suggesting "the beginning of a real revolution in thinking and education that more directly challenges philosophies grounded in white supremacy."

## The Canon and Its Tensions

Osorio's chapter is notable for what it excludes or minimises. American authors (Sarah Vowell, James Haley) "do not rise on anyone's horizons, and with very few exceptions Native writers simply aren't taking the time to contest the assumptions and methods of foreign writers." The cleavage between Indigenous and Western scholars is real: "Ignoring the influence that haole have on the larger academy and in the global culture seems naively dangerous." The challenge for Hawaiian studies is whether its increasingly particular, place-specific, and Hawaiian-language-grounded scholarship can be recognised by the broader academy without being domesticated by it. "We are certainly in need of rebuilding trust globally in the 'academy.'"

## Counterfactual Significance

Without the 1978 constitutional reforms (themselves the product of Kānaka Maoli delegates), the Office of Hawaiian Affairs, Hawaiian Studies, and the language mandate would not exist, and the subsequent flowering of Native scholarship almost certainly would not have occurred on the same timeline. The Māori Kohanga Reo model was the enabling template for Pūnana Leo — a cross-Pacific transfer of institutional knowledge whose absence might have delayed Hawaiian-language recovery by a generation.

## Historiography

This chapter is an insider's survey by a major participant. Osorio's bibliographic reconstruction is selective and advocacy-oriented rather than comprehensive — he explicitly says of American writers, "I don't believe we are reading their work." The chapter should be read alongside [[pacific-islands-historiography]] for the broader regional framing, and against the grain of outside assessments such as Thomas's *Islanders* (2010) and McClintock-style colonial analysis, which Osorio regards as "only tangentially important to Kānaka Maoli scholarship."
