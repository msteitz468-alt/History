# Chivalry and Knighthood (Milites, Dubbing, and the Chivalric Ideal)

## Definition

**Chivalry** (from French *chevalier*, "horseman") is the social status,
professional ethic, and ideology of the mounted warrior — the **knight** (Latin
*miles*, pl. *milites*) — as it crystallised in the eleventh and twelfth centuries.
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Jean Flori, Ch. 6) traces how a functional term
for an armed mounted fighter (*miles*) hardened over this period into a mark of
**social rank and noble quality**, increasingly conferred by the ritual of
**dubbing** and freighted with moral and religious meaning — until "the myth of
chivalry left its stamp on the entire middle ages."

## The Two Pillars: Castle and Knight

Flori identifies the **castle** and the **knight** as the twin symbols of
contemporary power, both transformed across the period:

- **Castles** multiplied during the eleventh century — at first modest wooden towers
  on mottes behind palisades, increasingly rebuilt in stone (*donjons*) and becoming
  general in the later twelfth century. The castle was simultaneously a military
  strongpoint, the residence of (now often autonomous) holders of local public
  power, and the centre of economic exploitation of its **castellany**. Its prime
  function, Flori argues, was "domination and protection."
- **Knights** specialised. Castle garrisons (*milites castri*) mixed paid fighting
  men with dependants owing castle-guard; a "court life" developed around the
  castellan. The spread of heavy cavalry, expensive horses and armour, and the
  possession of castles and local power fused the *milites* into an aristocratic
  elite.

## From Function to Order

The decisive change was the conversion of a **function** (fighting on horseback)
into an **order** with its own status, rituals, and ethic:

- **Dubbing** (the *adoubement*) emerged as the ceremony of entry, conferring "an
  additional quality, a sort of decoration" — the social, moral, and charismatic
  virtues of an idealised chivalry. Flori connects early knighting ceremonies to
  rituals of vassalic and ecclesiastical investiture (the handing-over of arms or
  banner with blessings of originally royal flavour).
- **The Church's role.** Churches recruited *milites ecclesiae*; great lords and
  even the emperor bore the title *miles sancti Petri* ("knight of St Peter"). The
  eleventh century saw "a veritable ideological promotion of the warriors fighting on
  behalf of the church," sanctifying knightly violence and channelling it — a current
  flowing through the [[peace-of-god|Peace of God]] and the [[just-war|just-war]]
  tradition into the [[crusades|Crusade]] (see also [[three-orders]]).
- **Literature.** The cult of chivalry developed alongside the writing of romance —
  the *[[chanson-de-geste|chansons de geste]]* (the *Chanson de Roland*) and, from the
  twelfth century, the romances of Chrétien de Troyes — which both reflected and
  idealised the knightly world.

## Significance

Chivalry gave the medieval warrior aristocracy a **self-conscious identity and code**
that outlasted its military rationale, shaping aristocratic culture, courtly love,
heraldry, and tournament into the late Middle Ages and beyond. It is also a key link
in the transformation of violence: by attaching honour, religious duty, and a defined
status to fighting, it helped redirect aristocratic aggression outward — into Spain,
Sicily, the Baltic, and the Holy Land.
