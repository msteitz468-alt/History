# The Westernizers

**The Westernizers** were an intellectual movement in Russian educated society in the 1830s and 1840s. Operating in constant debate with the **Slavophiles**, they argued that Russia did not possess a unique, superior civilization but belonged to the Western world, and that its future progress required following the European path of science, rationalism, and political liberty.

## Definition

The Westernizers shared the Slavophiles' training in German idealistic philosophy (especially Hegel) and their opposition to serfdom and the police state of Nicholas I. However, they reached opposite conclusions:
- **Universal Historical Path:** They believed that history followed a single, universal path of development, of which Western Europe was the leading model. Russia had to adopt Western science, education, and political institutions to modernize.
- **Praise of Peter the Great:** Contrary to the Slavophiles, the Westernizers praised Peter the Great for forcing Russia's integration with the West, though they wanted to continue Westernization beyond his military-autocratic reforms to include political freedom.
- **Rationalism and Science:** They assigned little importance to Orthodoxy or religion, advocating instead for secular science, rationalism, and the dignity of the individual.
- **Split into Moderates and Radicals:** As the movement developed in the 1840s, it split into two wings:
  - **Moderate Westernizers:** Kept their faith in religion and advocated gradual reform, constitutional monarchy, and popular education (represented by the historian Timothy Granovsky and Nicholas Stankevich).
  - **Radical Westernizers:** Turned to Left Hegelianism, atheism, and socialism, calling for a revolutionary overthrow of the autocracy and gentry society (represented by the literary critic **Vissarion Belinsky**, the publicist **Alexander Herzen**, and the anarchist **Michael Bakunin**).

## Context and Origin

The movement arose in the intellectual circles of the 1830s (such as the Stankevich circle) and developed in the salons of Moscow and the journals of St. Petersburg. The early Westernizers were motivated by Pyotr Chaadaev's famous *Philosophical Letter* (published in 1836), which outrageously claimed that Russia had contributed nothing to world culture and had developed in isolation from the European family of nations.

The radical Westernizers faced severe government repression. Belinsky died young in 1848, escaping arrest only because of his illness; Herzen left Russia permanently in 1847 to publish radical journals abroad; and Bakunin was arrested in Europe, handed over to the Russian government, and exiled to Siberia.

## Limitations and Critiques

The Westernizers' movement had several internal and external tensions:
- **Class Isolation:** Like the Slavophiles, the early Westernizers were primarily wealthy gentry intellectuals whose advanced Western ideas had no contact with or understanding of the illiterate Russian peasant masses.
- **The Romanticism of the West:** The radical Westernizers, particularly Herzen, suffered a profound crisis of faith when they witnessed the failure of the European revolutions of 1848 in Paris, which they saw as the triumph of a selfish, materialistic bourgeoisie. This led Herzen to romanticize the Russian peasant commune as a shortcut to socialism, merging with the populist movement.
- **Divisions over Tactics:** The split between the reformist liberals and the revolutionary socialists weakened the movement's political impact during the Great Reforms of Alexander II.
