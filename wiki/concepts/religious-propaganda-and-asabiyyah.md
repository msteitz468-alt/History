# Religious Propaganda and Asabiyyah (Da'wa as Political Multiplier)

## Definition

In [[the-muqaddimah|the Muqaddimah]], [[ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]] develops a precise theory of the relationship between religious calling (*da'wa*, often rendered "propaganda" in the Rosenthal translation) and [[asabiyyah|group feeling]]. The relationship is multiplicative, not substitutive: religion amplifies existing group feeling but cannot replace it. A religious call without group feeling behind it will fail; a group feeling augmented by a genuine religious call becomes nearly unstoppable.

This analysis is one of the most philosophically daring elements of the *Muqaddimah* because it integrates prophetic and religious phenomena within a naturalistic causal framework — placing revelation itself within the theory of [[group-feeling-and-power|the asabiyyah–mulk dynamic]].

## The Core Argument

Ibn Khaldun states the relationship in three propositions (Ch. III, §§4–6):

**Proposition 1**: Dynasties of wide power have their origin in religion based on prophecy or "truthful propaganda."

> "Royal authority results from superiority. Superiority results from group feeling. Only by God's help in establishing His religion do individual desires come together in agreement to press their claims, and hearts become united." (*Muqaddimah*, Ch. III, §4)

Religious belief eliminates mutual jealousy within the group and produces unified purpose. Without it, the members of a group pursue different ends, fear death, and collapse under pressure from a smaller but religiously unified opponent.

**Proposition 2**: Religious propaganda doubles the effective strength of group feeling.

> "Religious propaganda gives a dynasty at its beginning another power in addition to that of the group feeling it possessed as the result of the number of its supporters... Religious coloring does away with mutual jealousy and envy among people who share in a group feeling, and causes concentration upon the truth. When people who have a religious coloring come to have the right insight into their affairs, nothing can withstand them, because their outlook is one and their object one of common accord. They are willing to die for their objectives." (*Muqaddimah*, Ch. III, §5)

The mechanism is psychological and sociological: religious conviction converts members from calculating individuals who weigh the cost of dying against potential gain, into men who actively *seek* death for their cause. This removes the principal constraint on group military effectiveness.

**Proposition 3**: Religious propaganda cannot materialize without group feeling.

> "This is indicated in the afore-mentioned tradition: 'God sent no prophet who did not enjoy the protection of his people.' If this was the case with the prophets, who are among human beings those most likely to perform wonders, one would expect it to apply all the more so to others." (*Muqaddimah*, Ch. III, §6)

This is the limiting condition: religion does not create political power from nothing. It requires an existing social base with solidarity. Reformers who attempt to establish truth without group feeling behind them "risk being killed, and most of them actually do perish in consequence of their activities as sinners and unrewarded, because God had not destined them for such activities... He commands such activities to be undertaken only where there exists the power to bring them to a successful conclusion."

## Cases of Failed Religious Reform Without Group Feeling

Ibn Khaldun catalogs several failures to illustrate Proposition 3:

- **Khalid ad-Daryush** (Baghdad, 817): rose briefly to suppress criminal elements in Baghdad during the civil war between al-Amin and al-Ma'mun, but was defeated and captured by Ibrahim b. al-Mahdi's army — "his power quickly dissolved."
- **Abu Hatim Sahl b. Salamah al-Ansari** (Baghdad, 817): hung a Quran around his neck, appealed to the law, briefly governed Baghdad — also defeated and captured.
- **Ibn Qasi** (Sufi shaykh in Spain): revolted in Spain with genuine religious calling, had early success only because the Almoravids were distracted. "But there were no groups and tribes there to defend him." Once the Almohads took over the Maghrib, Ibn Qasi submitted to them.
- **At-Tuwayziri** (as-Sus, early 9th/15th century): claimed to be the Expected Fatimid, attracted Berber followers, was killed in his bed by an agent of the Masmudah leader.
- **Al-Abbas** (among the Ghumarah): made messianic claims, entered a city by force, was killed forty days later.

These failures establish the theoretical point empirically: charisma and genuine religious calling are insufficient without the structural backing of a tribe or group with real solidarity.

## The Paradigm Cases of Success

The inverse is demonstrated by the great founding moments of Muslim dynasties:

**Early Islamic Conquests**: The early Muslims combined Arab tribal group feeling (the strongest group feeling in Arabia) with the religious calling of Islam. The result was conquest of Persia (120,000 troops at Qadisiyyah) and Byzantium (400,000 troops at the Yarmuk) by forces of 30,000 in each case. The numerical disproportion is explained entirely by the multiplication of effective group feeling through religious solidarity.

**The Almohads**: The Masmudah Berbers already had strong group feeling. The Mahdist mission of Ibn Tumart added the religious dimension, multiplying their effective strength. They defeated the Zanatah — who had *stronger* desert group feeling than the Masmudah in isolation — because the religious amplification more than compensated for the raw solidarity differential. When the Almohads later lost their religious coloring, the Zanatah "rose up against them from every side and took their power away from them."

**The Almoravids (Lamtunah)**: The same pattern — strong Lamtunah group feeling combined with the Maliki reformist call produced a dynasty capable of conquering the Maghrib and Spain.

## Integration with the Natural Order

Ibn Khaldun's theory requires him to integrate prophetic activity within the causal order of group feeling. He does this not by denying the reality of prophecy or divine assistance but by asserting that God "permitted matters to take their customary course" — meaning that even genuinely divinely-assisted prophets operated through the natural mechanism of group feeling.

This creates a theologically delicate position: divine causation and natural causation operate simultaneously. Ibn Khaldun does not present them as alternatives. Prophets who relied on group feeling were using the natural mechanism that God established; their divine support was expressed *through* that mechanism.

The implication is that when the religious coloring of a dynasty fades, it loses the supernatural amplification and reverts to raw group feeling — and is then vulnerable to competitors with stronger group feeling or fresh religious energy.

## When Religious Coloring Fades

The decay of the religious calling follows the same structural logic as the decay of group feeling:

> "This can also be illustrated by the situation existing at the time when the religious coloring changes and is destroyed. The power of the ruling dynasty is then wiped out. Superiority exists then merely in proportion to the existing group feeling, without the additional power of religion." (*Muqaddimah*, Ch. III, §5)

The example: the Masmudah (Almohads), having lost their religious coloring, were overcome by the Zanatah — who had stronger basic group feeling and could now prevail without the need to overcome religious multipliers.

## Emic vs. Etic Status

Ibn Khaldun's framework is a sophisticated etic (analytical) theory that takes emic religious experience seriously on its own terms while also subjecting it to structural analysis. He does not reduce religion to ideology or false consciousness; he treats religious conviction as a genuine social force with real causal efficacy. What he denies is that religious conviction alone, absent structural (group feeling) conditions, can produce durable political power.

This places him in a long tradition of Islamic political thought that distinguishes between spiritual truth and political capacity — but his innovation is to make the relationship between them a subject of systematic empirical investigation rather than legal or theological assertion.
