# European Civil War (1905–1949)

Interpretive concept referring to the unusually intense and widespread period of civil wars, revolutionary insurrections, and internal political violence across Europe from the 1905 Russian Revolution to the end of the Greek Civil War in 1949. Not a single "European" civil war but a cluster of national conflicts sharing common drivers and often linked by foreign intervention and ideological contagion.

## Definition and Scope

Payne defines genuine civil war as "an extended contest of arms to win state power" (as opposed to mere political violence or insurrections). The era featured three main types: dynastic/succession, secession/national liberation, and ideological/revolutionary (the last dominant in the 20th c.).

The "European" framing highlights the transnational dimensions: shared causes (rapid modernization, competing radical ideologies, trauma of world war and defeat, rising expectations), mutual influence (contagion from Russia to Finland/Spain/Germany, Spanish CW as "dress rehearsal" for WWII), and the role of foreign intervention (Allies in Russia, Axis/Comintern in Spain, etc.).

## Major Phases (per Payne)

- Pre-1914: 1905 Russian Revolution and early confrontations.
- WWI and immediate aftermath: Russian Civil War (archetype), Finnish Civil War, German 1918-23, Hungarian, Italian squadrismo, etc.
- 1930s: Spanish Civil War as the most complex case (revolution + counter-revolution + internationalized).
- WWII era: Yugoslav and Greek civil wars as "wars within the war."

## Causal Drivers

1. Rapid pace of modernization and democratization pressures.
2. Interaction of extreme modern ideologies (communism, fascism, anarchism) espousing violence and authoritarian solutions.
3. Social transformation, education expansion, psychological revolution of rising expectations.
4. Frequent regime changes and demands for more.
5. Growth of nationalism and dissolution of empires.
6. Traumatic consequences of foreign war (defeat, occupation, demobilization).

## End of the Pattern

After 1945/47: exhaustion of revolutionary ideologies; economic stabilization and social democracy in West; totalitarianism's monopoly on violence in East; rapid growth "without precedent"; US leadership in reconstruction; nuclear/Cold War environment discouraging old-style internal explosions. The "classical modernity" factors (intense nationalism, empire rivalry, vitalist ideologies, autarky) declined.

## Historiography

The concept is contested. Some historians (e.g., Nolte) use "European Civil War" more broadly for the 1917-1945 ideological struggle between communism and fascism/Nazism as a whole (including international wars). Payne insists on distinguishing internal civil wars while acknowledging the blurring during WWII. Useful for highlighting that the "world wars" era was also an era of unprecedented domestic conflict, with Spain as the most "classic" and complex case. Challenges both Marxist class-war narratives and liberal downplaying of internal violence.
