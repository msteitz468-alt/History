## Definition

**Asymmetrical reciprocity** in the Andes refers to a hierarchically organized system of labor prestation, gift exchange, and feasting in which the state (or any superior party) incurred obligations to workers and subordinates by providing food, drink, textiles, and ceremony, while subordinates provided labor, military service, and political allegiance. It was "a socioeconomic organizational system that regulates labor service at several levels. It served as the lever that propelled the production and distribution of goods. It was a way of ordering relationships among members of a society whose economy was a nonmonetary one."

**Redistribution** refers to the centralized accumulation of produce (from state lands, herds, and labor obligations) in storehouses (qollqa), which was then disbursed by the state to fulfill reciprocal obligations, support feasts, equip armies, and subsidize administration. The combination meant the Inka state could operate without currency or markets (in the highland core), "because money in the sense of currency did not exist in Tawantinsuyu."

---

## Emic vs. Etic

**Emic**: Andean actors understood reciprocity as obligatory mutual exchange across all social levels — between households (ayni), between communities (mit'a), between lords and subjects, and between the Inka and the kurakas. The Inka framed their overtures ("entreaty") as gifts requiring a gift in return; acceptance acknowledged submission. The term "entreaty" (Quechua: ask, petition) denotes the formal mechanism by which the Inka solicited labor and allegiance.

**Etic**: Modern scholars (Murra, Polanyi's substantivism, Morris) analyze the system as a **political economy without markets** in the highland core, where "the value added by ceremony, warehousing, and the cachet of power gave the state the control of reciprocity, key to Andean organizational systems." Redistribution is explicitly political: "production and distribution of goods are conducted on behalf of a chief, a lord, a temple, or a despot." The humanitarian reading (Baudin's "socialist empire") is rejected; the real function was political maintenance and expansion.

## How It Worked: The Mechanism of Inka Expansion

The clearest account comes from Betanzos's description of Pacha Kutiq's (Inca Yupanqui's) early reign, quoted by Rostworowski and Morris:

> "Inca Yupanque [Pacha Kutiq] ordered in the city of Cuzco that all the important cacique lords, [kurakas] who had pledged obedience to him and were living in the provinces and neighboring lands around the city of Cuzco, attend a meeting on a certain designated day... And thus all together, having received this great favor that he did for them by giving them lands that would be recognized in perpetuity for each one of them, all together they gave him many thanks."

The exchange sequence:
1. Inka builds storehouses in [[cuzco]] "for all the foods such as maize, agi [chili peppers], beans, choclo [tender maize], chuno [a variety of freeze-dried potato], quinua [a grain], dried meat, and all the other dried provisions and foods."
2. Inka invites kurakas to Cusco, entertains them "with feasts, public banquets, gifts, and the exchange of women."
3. Pacha Kutiq gave "jewels of gold and silver... two sets of garments he wore, and to each one he also gave a lady born in Cuzco and of Inca lineage. Because of these family ties, they would never rebel."
4. Then — only then — Pacha Kutiq delivers "the entreaty — the request to carry out the necessary public works before embarking on his conquests."

This sequence was repeated at every expansion frontier: "In this way, and with other good methods they employed, [the Inka] entered many lands without war." When an entreaty was refused, military conquest followed; but even then, administration was restored through reciprocal mechanisms at the new center.

## The Absence of Markets in the Highland Core

The Andean highland economy rested on **labor** as the primary taxable good, not monetary commodities. No highland market system existed comparable to the Aztec tianguis. Wealth consisted of "tallies of commodities" — textile bales, food stores, camelid herds. The key passage:

> "Inka wealth rested on access to three sources of revenue: labor, land, and herds. Together these ultimately were turned into goods accumulated in state warehouses and redistributed for state purposes."

On the **coast**, a different regime prevailed: barter (based on established equivalencies, not negotiated prices) coexisted with redistribution, and specialist long-distance traders (mercaderes) operated — the Chincha case documents 6,000 merchants sailing balsa rafts between Chincha and Ecuador to obtain mullu shell (Spondylus) for ritual offerings. The state allowed politically authorized coastal trade while restricting plebeian tributaries from independent travel, "because such relative free-lancing offered an undesired alternative to dependence on state largesse."

## The Vertical Archipelago

John Murra identified the **vertical archipelago** as the ecological logic behind Andean redistribution. Highland communities established multiethnic colonies (enclaves) in distant eco-zones — coastal valleys for fish, cotton, and coca; eastern lowlands for tropical fruits, feathers, and honey — enabling access to resources unavailable at the altiplano. "These enclaves were the first contact between Inka and the coast."

The model varied by region:
- **Southern Andes**: distant enclaves "at distances of more than a day's walk from each other," sometimes weeks away, reflecting the sparse and distinct micro-climates of the south
- **Central Andes**: more compact, where closely spaced microclimates allowed communities like Canta to access maize, coca, and camelid pastures without distant colonization

The Inka state incorporated and expanded this system: it helped allies "encroach on valleys and widen their 'archipelagoes'" while also requiring them to supply labor for state-controlled outlier islands that filled imperial warehouses.

## Feasting as Political Infrastructure

Reciprocal feasting was not incidental but structurally central. Huanuco Pampa — the largest Inka administrative center outside Cusco, set at 3,800 meters above sea level on a barren plateau — illustrates this. "The center's emphasis was neither military nor bureaucratic... Instead the emphasis was on the rites and feasting related to reciprocal administration." Its 500 warehouses held food and supplies to support feasts; a compound of fifty structures housed weavers and brewers producing cloth and maize beer. "Thousands of beer jars and serving plates found in buildings around smaller plazas... suggest that public feasts took place there."

The mamakuna (chosen women of the Aqlla Wasi) were the institutional labor force of reciprocity: they brewed chicha (maize beer) in industrial quantities, wove fine cumbi cloth, and served rituals — all goods that the state deployed in reciprocal exchanges. "These women made large amounts of high-quality chicha which was for offerings to the gods and for the priests to drink."

## Complementarity in the South-Central Andes

Rivera's account of the South-Central Andes documents the early operation of reciprocity/complementarity **before** Inka incorporation. At Alto Ramirez (Early Intermediate, 600 BCE–CE 500): "a new sociopolitical system, with an economy based on reciprocity and complementarity. Rationally managed surplus and the adoption of new agricultural and livestock techniques allowed the population to grow." Exchange nodes — coast, oasis, highland, lowland — were ideologically unified through shared iconography centered on the Lake Titicaca sphere. Post-Tiwanaku balkanization (CE 900–1350) saw "demand for renewed ideological frameworks capable of organizing exchange among heterogeneous and mobile groups with limited economic resources."

This demonstrates that reciprocity and complementarity were pan-Andean institutional forms, not Inka inventions. The Inka massively scaled and politicized what already existed. See [[andean-state-formation]].

## Political Religion as Reciprocal System

The Inka managed provincial wak'as (sacred places and objects) through the same reciprocal logic. Cobo reports that "the Inca would have offerings of his gold and silver given according to his quipo account, to all the huacas." Provincial cult objects were brought to Cusco in the Qhapaq Qucha (Capacocha) ceremony, consulted in "a congress of oracular deities," and returned with allocated sacrificial goods. The Huarochirí manuscript preserves a rare Inka political speech in which the Inka tonguelashes wak'as for insufficient military reciprocity — threatening to burn them if they do not "come to my aid."

The story of Llocllay Huancupa (Huarochirí) shows local cults being coopted via reciprocal investment: the Inka positioned itself to "leverage a major increment in state-supervised infrastructure (temple), productive goods (herds), and labor (herders)" from an initial small outlay of reciprocity. This was the Inka state's standard playbook for absorbing local sacred authority.

## Limits of the Reciprocal System

1. **Accumulation imperative**: "If, for any reason, the state lacked accumulated goods, it could not meet the requisites for administration or the constant gifts and 'donations' required by the institution of reciprocity." Storage depletion = political collapse.
2. **Split inheritance pressure**: Each new Inka needed to build his own wealth from scratch, requiring perpetual conquest and labor extraction. This escalated demands across the realm.
3. **Resentment**: The system operated by extracting labor against promises of state hospitality; but "the labor demands of the state increased with each successive reign," and "resentment among the provincial ruling classes did contribute to the startlingly rapid collapse of the Inka empire as local lords mistakenly sought a better deal from the Spaniards."
4. **Coastal exception**: Barter and market-like exchange operated on the coast, where the highland reciprocal model was adapted rather than directly applied.

## Historiography

**Scholarly debates**:
- **Baudin/socialist reading vs. political-economic reading**: Baudin's *L'Empire Socialiste des Incas* (1928) argued for humanitarian redistribution as the system's purpose; John Murra, Morris, and Rostworowski have replaced this with a model emphasizing political leverage and power maintenance. The shift is now well established.
- **Extent of reciprocity beyond the highland core**: Whether coastal barter and northern (Ecuadorian) market-like exchange represent exceptions or integral parts of a unified system is debated.
- **Mit'a vs. tribute**: Whether Inka labor demands constituted "tribute" in a comparative sense or were conceptually distinct as "reciprocal obligation" is contested; current scholarship tends to see it as a politically framed labor tax dressed in reciprocal idiom.

**Key scholars**: John Murra (vertical archipelago, cloth-wealth, labor-tax thesis); María Rostworowski (kurakas, women rulers, coastal trade, Chincha); Craig Morris (Huanuco Pampa archaeological excavations); R. Tom Zuidema (ceque system, calendar).

**Source limitations**: All evidence is either post-conquest Spanish documentation (with linguistic and cultural filters) or archaeology (subject to recovery bias and interpretation). No Inka writing system decoded to date; khipu records remain only partially interpretable.
