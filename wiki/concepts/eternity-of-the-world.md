## Definition and Scope

The **eternity of the world** (*qidam al-ʿālam*) is the doctrine, held by the majority of the
Aristotelian and Neoplatonist philosophers (*falāsifa*) working in the Islamic world, that the
universe is without beginning in time — that it has coexisted with God eternally, as an effect
coexists with its eternal cause. Its opposite is **temporal origination** (*ḥudūth*), the doctrine
that God brought the universe into existence at a definite first moment.

This page documents both the philosophers' doctrine and the four proofs for it, and al-Ghazālī's
systematic refutation in **Discussion 1** of the [[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]
(c. 1095 CE) — the longest and most philosophically developed discussion in that work.

Al-Ghazālī regarded the eternity doctrine as not merely philosophically mistaken but one of the
three positions that constitute outright unbelief (*kufr*) — denying, as it does, God's free
creative act and making the world a necessary emanation rather than a willed creation.

Source: [[ghazali-incoherence-of-philosophers]] (Marmura translation, Discussion 1, lines 1280–3599
of the raw text).

## The Philosophers' Doctrine: The World's Pre-Eternity

[[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] and [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]], following Aristotle, held that the universe
has never ceased to exist alongside God:

> "The view of their multitudes, both ancient and modern, has settled on upholding its past
> eternity: that it has never ceased to exist with God, exalted be He, to be an effect of His, to
> exist along with Him, not being posterior to Him in time, in the way the effect coexists along
> with the cause and light along with the sun; that the Creator's priority to [the world] is like
> the priority of the cause to the effect, which is a priority in essence and rank, not in time."

The key claim: God's causal priority over the universe is **ontological** (cause is prior to
effect), not **temporal** (earlier in time). Just as sunlight coexists with the sun as its
cause — there was never a moment of sun without light — the universe coexists with God as its
necessary emanation. To suppose a temporal first moment would require a change in God or in the
conditions for creation, which is impossible if God is immutable.

A dissenting voice: Galen, near the end of his life, professed agnosticism about the question.
Plato was sometimes read as affirming temporal creation, but the majority of the Islamic
Neoplatonists interpreted Plato's language as metaphorical.

## Proof One: The Preponderance Argument (Al-Ghazālī's Formulation)

The first and strongest proof runs as follows:

**Step 1**: Before the world existed, the Eternal had all the conditions for creation present.
If He then creates the world at a specific moment, something must have given *preponderance*
(*murājjiḥ*) to existence over non-existence at that moment.

**Step 2**: Either this "giver of preponderance" came into being anew at that moment, or it
did not. If it did not come into being anew, the situation at the moment of creation is identical
to all prior moments — and the world should not have existed in those prior moments either (or
should have existed in them just as much).

**Step 3**: If a new preponderating factor came into being, the same question applies to it:
why did *it* arise at that moment and not earlier? This either regresses infinitely or terminates
in an eternal first cause — from which a first temporal event must proceed without any change in
the cause, which was the original problem.

**Conclusion**: "The proceeding of the temporal from the Eternal without a change of state of
affairs in the Eternal by way of power, instrument, time, purpose, or nature is impossible."
Therefore the world must be eternal.

### Al-Ghazālī's First Objection: Prosecutorial Burden-Reversal

Al-Ghazālī's primary counter is not to offer an alternative explanation but to demand
**demonstrative proof** of the impossibility the philosophers claim:

> "Do you know the impossibility of an eternal will related to the temporal creation of something
> through the necessity of reason or its theoretical reflection? According to your language in
> logic, is the connection between the two terms known to you with or without a middle term? If you
> claim a middle term, which is the reflective theoretical method, then you must show it. If you
> claim to know this through the necessity of reason, how is it, then, that those who oppose you
> do not share this knowledge, when the party believing in the world's temporal creation by an
> eternal will is [such] that no one land can contain it?"

The argument: if the impossibility of an eternal will producing a temporal event were truly known
by rational necessity (as self-evident as 2+2=4), it would be known universally. Since vast
numbers of rational people affirm temporal creation without self-contradiction, the impossibility
is not rationally necessary. It may feel impossible to the philosophers, but that feeling is
produced by *wahm* (estimation), not by demonstrative proof.

### Al-Ghazālī's Second Objection: The Will as Specifier

Separately, al-Ghazālī argues that the philosophers' own system cannot escape the problem of
one similar being differentiated from another. The **Will** (*irāda*) is precisely the divine
attribute whose function is to differentiate between two equally possible options:

> "The world came to existence whence it did, having the description with which it came to exist,
> and in the place in which it came to exist, through will, will being an attribute whose function
> is to differentiate a thing from its similar. If this were not its function, then power would be
> sufficient. But since the relation of power to two contraries is the same and there was an
> inescapable need for a specifying [agent] that would specify one thing from its similar, it was
> said: 'The Eternal has, beyond power, an attribute that has as its function the specifying of
> one thing from its similar.'"

The philosophers object that differentiation between **genuine similars** is incoherent — a thirsty
man with two truly equal glasses of water could not choose between them. Al-Ghazālī replies with
the date analogy: suppose a hungry man facing two genuinely equal dates who can only take one.
He will inevitably take one through an act of will. "It is, hence, inescapable for anyone engaged
in theoretical reflection on the true nature of the voluntary act... but to affirm the existence
of an attribute whose function is to render one thing specifically distinct from its similar."

### The Pole Problem: Forcing the Philosophers' Hand

Al-Ghazālī forces a parallel problem onto the philosophers. The outermost heavenly sphere, they
maintain, is a simple body of similar parts — it has no inherent differentiation. Yet it rotates
around two specific poles. Why these points and not any other pair of opposite points on the
perfectly homogeneous sphere?

The philosophers cannot appeal to a difference in the parts themselves (since they insist the
sphere is perfectly homogeneous) nor can they coherently appeal to a further specifying attribute
without the same question recurring. "Assigning [a particular place] with a specific characteristic
is either arbitrary or else [realized] through an attribute whose very function is to render one
thing [more] specific than its exact similar." Whatever accounts for the pole's position serves as
an analogue of the Will — and the philosophers have admitted it.

## Proof Two: The Meaning of "Prior" (The Time Argument)

The second proof concerns what is meant by saying God is *prior* to the world.

**Dilemma**: There are only two coherent meanings of God's priority:
1. **Essential/ontological priority** (cause over effect, simultaneous) — this entails that God and
   the world are both co-eternal, since cause and simultaneous effect must share the same temporal
   status.
2. **Temporal priority** — this requires that before the world existed, there was a duration of
   God without the world: a "before" filled with time. But time is the measure of motion, and if
   time is pre-eternal, motion and the moved thing (the world) are pre-eternal.

Either way, the world is eternal.

### Al-Ghazālī's Objection: Time is Created

Al-Ghazālī's response becomes the most analytically developed part of Discussion 1:

> "Time is originated and created, and before it there was no time at all. We mean by our
> statement that God is prior to the world and time: that He was and there was no world, and that
> then He was and with Him was the world. The meaning of our statement, 'He was and there was no
> world,' is only [the affirmation of] the existence of the Creator's essence and nonexistence of
> the world's essence."

The word "was" (*kāna*) does not commit one to a third thing — a pre-existing time — any more
than it does when we say "God was and there was no Jesus, and then He was and Jesus with Him."
The intuition that "was" requires a pre-existing temporal duration is the product of *wahm*.

The philosophers press: the difference between "was" and "will be" implies something about the
past beyond mere existence and non-existence — namely, elapsed time. Al-Ghazālī responds that this
difference is **relational to us**: it is how a temporally situated observer carves up reality,
not a feature of reality independent of observers. The "before" of the world's beginning is like
the "outside" of the world's spherical surface — a limit, not a thing beyond the limit.

**The spatial analogy**: Just as the philosophers themselves affirm that beyond the finite sphere
of the world there is neither void nor filled space (the "above" terminates at the world's surface),
so al-Ghazālī affirms that before the first moment of time there is no temporal extension. The
*wahm* insists on an "above" beyond the world just as it insists on a "before" before time — but
in both cases reason can establish limits that the imagination refuses to accept.

### The "Hypothetical Possibilities" Argument (A Sub-Proof)

A subsidiary argument for the pre-eternity of time runs: God could have created the world earlier —
by a year, a century, a thousand years. These "possible earlier creations" have different
magnitudes; what has magnitude requires something quantified — namely time. Hence before the world
there was time.

Al-Ghazālī's parity response: similarly, God could have created the outermost heaven larger or
smaller by one cubit. These hypothetical sizes have different magnitudes. By the philosophers'
own argument, this would require a quantified space beyond the world — but they deny that. The
same epistemological move that blocks the spatial version blocks the temporal version. Both are
*wahm*-generated illusions: "What one must admit is that God, exalted be He, is eternal and
powerful. Action is never impossible for Him, if He wills it. In all this there is nothing that
necessitates affirming [a limitless] extended time, unless the estimative faculty, in its
confusion, adds to time some other thing."

## Proof Three: The Possibility Argument

The third proof is the most concise:

**Argument**: The world's existence is possible (*mumkin*) — not impossible. This possibility has
no beginning: there is no temporal state in which the world's existence was impossible (that would
be absurd, since the impossible never becomes possible). Therefore possibility is eternal.
But "possible existence can be [eternal] in conformity with [ceaseless] possibility" — if something
is eternally possible, there is no reason to deny that it exists eternally.

### Al-Ghazālī's Objection: Possibility of Temporal Origination

Al-Ghazālī argues that the possible in question is not "existence at any moment" but specifically
"temporal origination" — coming to be at a beginning. This is consistent with having an infinite
future (post-eternity) while having a definite first moment. The possibility of temporal origination
does not require that the world have always existed, only that it could have been created at any
moment of time (once time exists). He uses the analogy of spatial magnitudes: God could make the
world larger or smaller by indefinitely many increments, yet the world has a finite actual size.
The infinity of possible sizes does not entail an actually infinite world.

## Proof Four: The Matter Argument

The most technical of the four proofs:

**Argument**: Every temporal event requires matter (*māda*) preceding it in which the event's
possibility resides. For before a temporal event occurs, its existence is possible — neither
impossible nor necessary. This "possibility of existence" is a **relative description** (*wasf
iḍāfī*): it cannot subsist on its own; it must inhere in something. The only candidate is matter.
Therefore matter must precede every temporal event. Primary matter (*hayūlā*) itself cannot be
temporally created — if it were, *its* possibility would need to inhere in a prior matter, and so
on ad infinitum. Hence prime matter is eternal — and therefore the world (which requires matter)
is eternal.

### Al-Ghazālī's Objection: Possibility as Rational Judgment

Al-Ghazālī argues that "possibility," "necessity," and "impossibility" are **judgments of the mind**,
not attributes requiring a material substrate:

> "The possibility which they have mentioned reverts to a judgment of the mind. Anything whose
> existence the mind supposes, [nothing] preventing its supposing it, we call 'possible'; and, if
> [it is] prevented, we call [it] 'impossible'; and, if it is unable to suppose its nonexistence,
> we name it 'necessary.' For these are rational propositions that do not require an existent so as
> to be rendered a description thereof."

He offers three proofs that modality is mind-dependent rather than materially grounded:

1. **The impossibility argument**: If possibility requires a material substrate, so does
   impossibility. But the impossible in itself has no existence — there is no matter for
   "impossibility" to inhere in. Therefore modality cannot require a material basis.

2. **The blackness argument**: The mind judges "blackness is possible" in abstraction, before
   specifying which body might become black. If possibility were relative only to the body, then
   "blackness is in itself possible" would be false — only "this body is possibly black" would
   be possible. But we plainly do make the former judgment.

3. **The soul argument**: Avicenna himself holds that human souls are temporally created but are
   not imprinted in matter — they are immaterial substances. Yet before their creation, their
   existence is possible. If their possibility cannot be grounded in material substance, then
   material grounding is not a universal requirement for possibility.

The philosophers attempt various responses (souls are possible relative to the bodies they will
govern; impossibility is always relative to some qualified subject). Al-Ghazālī treats these as
ad hoc. He concludes by invoking the universal scope of his enterprise: "In this book we have
undertaken only to muddy their doctrine and throw dust in the face of their proofs with that which
would reveal their incoherence."

## The Second Objection to Proof One: Temporal Events from an Eternal Cause

Al-Ghazālī offers a second, more compressed objection to the first proof. The philosophers'
own causal chain requires that an eternal cause ultimately produce temporal events. They admit this:
the soul of the outermost heavenly sphere is eternal, and it produces the eternal circular motion
of the sphere; this motion in turn produces the successive configurations that are the proximate
causes of all sublunary events. But al-Ghazālī presses:

> "Is the circular motion, which is the foundation [of all temporal events], temporally originated
> or eternal? If eternal, how does [this foundation] become a principle for the first temporal
> event? If temporal, it would require another temporal event, [and so on,] regressing [ad
> infinitum]."

The philosophers' attempt to resolve this — saying circular motion is both eternal (as a continuous
process) and temporal (each of its parts comes into being and passes away) — al-Ghazālī treats
as equivocation: "Is it the principle of temporal events inasmuch as it is permanent or inasmuch
as it is being renewed? If inasmuch as it is permanent, how is it, then, that there would proceed
from something permanent whose states are similar something that occurs at [certain] times but not
others?"

## Theological Stakes and the Classification as Heresy

Al-Ghazālī classifies the eternity doctrine as one of the three positions constituting **kufr**
(unbelief), not merely philosophical error, because:

1. It denies God's **free creative act**: if the world exists necessarily as God's effect, God
   did not choose to create it — creation is an attribute of God in the same way as burning is
   an attribute of fire. This collapses the distinction between God and natural necessity.

2. It makes God's will **subordinate to necessity**: the philosophers' God necessarily produces the
   world. For al-Ghazālī (following [[asharism|Ashʿarite]] voluntarism), this eliminates genuine
   divine agency. A God who cannot refrain from creating is not the God of the Quran.

3. It contradicts the **explicit quranic teaching** of creation (*khalq*) — God as active,
   choosing Maker of the heavens and the earth.

The connection to al-Ghazālī's broader anti-necessitarianism is direct: the same philosophical
assumption that makes the world eternal (God necessarily produces effects) later makes fire
necessarily burn (natural causes necessarily produce effects). Both are targets of the *Tahāfut*;
both are denied in the name of divine freedom.
