# Sceatta

**Sceatta** (plural *sceattas*; Old English *sceatt*, meaning "treasure" or "money") is the modern numismatists' name for the small, thick silver coins produced in England, Frisia, and adjacent parts of the Frankish kingdom from roughly the 680s to the 750s CE. They are the primary monetary instrument of the North Sea **[[wic|wic/emporium]]** trading zone and represent the most significant numismatic shift of the post-Roman West: the replacement of the inherited late Roman *tremissis* (gold) coinage system with a silver-based, low-denomination currency suited to everyday commercial transactions.

---

## Physical Description

Sceattas are immediately recognizable by their distinctive form:

*   **Metal**: Fine silver, typically 90%+ pure in the earliest issues, declining somewhat in later series.
*   **Weight**: Approximately 0.8–1.3 grams—considerably lighter than the Roman *siliqua* and the Carolingian *denier* that would replace them.
*   **Diameter**: 10–15 mm, smaller than a modern shirt button.
*   **Shape**: Thick and dumpy (a high-relief "dump" flan) rather than the thin, broad flan of Roman or later Carolingian coins.
*   **Iconography**: Iconographically diverse and often obscure, ranging from degenerate Roman imperial portraits (the "porcupine" type) to interlaced animal ornamentation in the Insular style, runic inscriptions, and Christian crosses.

**Grounded from Bogucki & Crabtree 2004 (range 05, vol. 2 pp. 357–358):** In the seventh century gold gave way to silver; "the silver coins of Frisia and England in the period, known as sceattas, also were small, thick, and lacking in legends." "A brief issue of sceattas minted at Ribe on the west coast of Jutland c. A.D. 720 can lay claim to being the earliest European coinage minted beyond the ancient Roman borders." Carolingian/English reforms produced the broader penny ~A.D. 793. Viking hoards include "Islamic silver dirhams, chiefly of the tenth century; Byzantine silver coins from the same period; and German and English pennies of the late tenth century and the eleventh century." Minting initiated in Bohemia A.D. 960s, Kiev ~A.D. 990. (range_05).

## The Monetary Transition: Gold to Silver

**Mark Blackburn** (*NCMH*, Ch. 23) places the sceatta within a broader monetary transition that reshaped early medieval economics:

### The Late Roman and Merovingian Gold Phase (c. 400–670 CE)
The late Roman monetary system was anchored in **gold**. After the fall of the West, the Merovingian kingdoms and the Visigoths continued to mint gold *tremisses* (one-third of a *solidus*), closely imitating the imperial prototype. This system was **high-value**, suited to elite exchange, tax collection, and long-distance luxury trade, but not to everyday market transactions. A single gold *tremissis* was worth roughly one ox or several days' agricultural labor—far too large for routine commerce.

### The Late Seventh-Century Silver Revolution (c. 660–700 CE)
From roughly the 660s, Merovingian mints began debasing the gold coinage, mixing in increasing amounts of silver and then copper. By the 670s–680s, what had been gold had effectively become silver. This debasement process, which occurred on both sides of the Channel (in England the shift to silver was clean rather than debasement), produced a new, low-denomination silver coinage perfectly suited to the commercial economy of the emerging **[[wic|wics]]**:

*   In **Frisia and northern Francia**, the silver coinage (*sceattas* or their continental equivalents, sometimes called *denarii*) was minted by multiple authorities—kings, bishops, and apparently independent moneyers operating from the major wics.
*   In **England**, the *sceatta* was produced at the key trading sites—primarily Hamwic, Lundenwic, and Ipswich—and circulated throughout the English kingdoms.
*   In the **Frankish heartland**, the Carolingian reforms under Pippin III (c. 755 CE) and Charlemagne (c. 793 CE) rationalized the silver coinage into the standardized, broad-flan *denier* (*denarius*), which became the standard coin of medieval Europe.

## Numismatic Significance

Sceattas are the most abundant and geographically widespread early medieval coin type in northwestern Europe:

*   **Volume**: Thousands of individual sceattas survive, recovered from archaeological excavation, particularly at wic sites, and from hoard deposits.
*   **Series classification**: Modern numismatists (led by **Rigold** and **Metcalf**) have classified sceattas into approximately 70–80 distinct *series* (labeled A–Z and continuing) based on iconographic type, find distribution, and metallurgical analysis. The geographical distribution of individual series maps closely onto regional political boundaries and trade networks.
*   **The North Sea trade zone**: The interlocking find-distributions of English, Frisian, and Frankish sceatta series document a genuinely **integrated commercial zone** spanning the Channel and the southern North Sea coast—a **North Sea common market** of the early eighth century.

## Royal Control and Ideological Function

Like all early medieval coinage, sceattas were a prerogative of political authority:

*   **Minting rights**: In England, the right to mint coin was reserved to the king, with licensed moneyers operating under royal supervision. Minting in unauthorized locations or with underweight coins was a serious offense under the law codes.
*   **Propaganda**: Although sceatta iconography is often obscure, the use of Christian symbols (crosses, birds interpreted as the Holy Spirit or Woden's ravens in a syncretist idiom) and royal names or monograms placed the coin within the emerging Christian royal ideology of the period.
*   **Toll revenues**: The dense concentration of sceatta finds at wic sites, combined with documentary evidence for royal toll-collectors, shows that royal income from taxing trade was a significant component of the early medieval fiscal system.

---

## End of the Sceatta Era (c. 750 CE)

The classic sceatta was replaced in England by the broader, thinner **penny** (*denarius*) under King Offa of Mercia (r. 757–796 CE), who adopted the Carolingian reformed coinage standard and introduced a heavier, broader flan with a bold portrait type that remained the standard English coin format until the 13th century. This reform brought the English monetary system into alignment with the continental Carolingian standard.
