# The City of Rome and the *Plebs Urbana*

The population of the imperial capital — the largest city in the Mediterranean — and
the **urban plebs** as a constitutional, social, and demographic phenomenon. Treated
in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 17 (Nicholas Purcell).

## Three collectivities

Purcell distinguishes: the ***populus Romanus*** (all citizens everywhere); the
***plebs urbana*** (citizens resident in the city who were **not** of senatorial or
equestrian rank — excluding slaves and *peregrini*); and the wider **city population**
(including the huge numbers of slaves and foreigners). Once nearly co-extensive, the
*populus* and the *plebs urbana* split apart as the citizenship spread across Italy;
because the *populus* could vote **only at Rome**, its constitutional role **devolved
on the plebs urbana**, giving it a strong corporate self-awareness — registered,
docketed, and assessed by the **census**, organized into *tribus* and *centuriae*, and
expressing its will through the formal assemblies and the **informal politics** of the
*contiones* and the "mob."

## "Rome's problems were the problems of success"

Purcell's central model: the **immeasurable success of the Roman elite** drove the
continuous growth of the city's social organisms, "within which the tensions of
readjustment and incorporation, the insecurities about belonging, and the struggle of
people for social locations" generated the recurrent crises of the record. The city's
turmoil — political impotence, overcrowding, food crises, elite manipulation — were all
**aspects of one underlying phenomenon**: the problems of a metropolis growing on an
unprecedented scale.

## A city sustained by immigration

Quantification is "doomed" (the dole figures — 320,000 in 46, 150,000 after — measure
**eligibility, not population**). Purcell's key argument: a great pre-industrial city
was a **net consumer of population** — a disease-pool with appalling mortality (the
plague of 175–174) — so Rome's vast size **entailed constant large-scale immigration**.
The "population of Rome" is best understood as **the people at Rome on any given day** —
mobile, transient, fluid — not a stable huddle of lifelong urbanites. The inflow came
from:

- **Free-born Latins and Italians** (12,000 from Latium complained of as early as 187;
  the flood after the Social War, "lured by the hand-outs," preferring *urbanum otium*
  to rural toil) — provoking periodic **expulsions of aliens** (Pennus 126, the Lex
  Papia 65) that failed;
- **Slaves**, deployed in the *familiae urbanae* — and, through frequent **manumission**
  (thousands of *libertini* a year), feeding citizens of servile, often Greek-named,
  origin into the plebs. The incorporation of the **freedman** — "the essential but
  unwelcome new arrival" — is the recurring problem of the city's social history.

## The grain dole and "drainage"

The ***frumentationes*** (state grain distributions) — begun by **C. Gracchus in 123**
— were, Purcell stresses, **not a means-test or a measure of real population** but the
creation of a **privileged status** at the heart of the citizen body, the symbol of
belonging to the imperial capital. The counter-mechanism was **resettlement**: the
planned "draining-off" of the city's surplus into **colonies** (Caesar's foundations
at Corinth, Carthage, and in Africa pointedly included **freedmen**) — generous but
prudent management of a population the elite found "despised but useful."

## Contested

Purcell writes against **static, bounded models** of the city (no "Aurelianic Wall" on
the Republican city dividing a teeming core from an agrarian desert) and against the
**ancient elite's vocabulary of disdain** (the *demos*, the mob) that "processed almost
all the information we possess." The balance of free-born to freed in the plebs, the
ethnic meaning of Greek *cognomina*, and the city's true size remain debated — the
evidence "elusive, fragmentary, and defying synthesis."
