# The Aztec Economy (Commodity Money, Markets, and the Pochteca)

Unlike the [[inca-empire|Inca]]'s [[mita-and-andean-reciprocity|moneyless, market-less]] economy, the
[[aztec-empire|Aztec]] world ran on a **vibrant commercial economy** of marketplaces, commodity monies,
and professional merchants — layered over the imperial **tribute** system. Documented for the wiki
through Frances Berdan's [[berdan-aztecs-2021|*The Aztecs*]] (Berdan is a leading scholar of the Aztec
economy). It is a striking contrast: two great American empires, two opposite economic solutions.

## Money that grew on trees

The Aztecs used several **commodity monies** — objects that were both useful goods and media of exchange:
- **Cacao beans** — the everyday "small change," grown in the humid lowlands and used to price small
  goods (a tamale = 1 bean; a turkey hen = 100). Common enough that **counterfeiters** hollowed and
  refilled the shells.
- **Quachtli** — large white cotton cloaks, the **high-value** currency and standard of wealth (a
  commoner could live a year on ~20; a *quachtli* was worth 65–300 cacao beans). Paid in tribute by the
  tens of thousands.
- **Thin copper axes** (and, regionally, copper bells, shells, stone beads) — lower-value currency,
  concentrated in the south.

These were **flexible commodity monies**, their rates set by bargaining and fluctuating by season and
supply — not standardised state coinage.

## The marketplace (tianquiztli)

Every city-state had its **marketplace** — the hub of goods, services, and news. The greatest,
**Tlatelolco** (beside Tenochtitlan), drew **20,000–25,000 people daily and 40,000–50,000** on the
five-day "big" market — a scale that astonished the conquistadors (Bernal Díaz "shall never finish if I
tell it all in detail"). Markets were **highly ordered**: goods sold in designated zones, prices set by
**haggling**, a central shrine-stone (*momoztli*), and **market judges** who policed cheating, false
measures, and theft on the spot. Most trade was **barter** supplemented by the commodity monies; many
markets specialised (Acolman for dogs, Azcapotzalco for slaves) or rotated across a five-town weekly
cycle, and frontier markets resembled European fairs.

## The pochteca

The **pochteca** were hereditary **long-distance professional merchants**, living in their own
*calpolli* (wards) in Tenochtitlan and a dozen Basin cities, with their own courts, schools, patron god
(**Yacatecuhtli**), and rituals. They braved hunger, robbery, and assassination to carry high-value
goods — feathers, jade, gold, cacao, cotton, slaves — across ecological zones and political borders to
**international trading centres** on the empire's fringes (Xicalanco, Xoconochco, Tochtepec). Crucially,
they doubled as **state agents**: trading the ruler's goods abroad, serving as **diplomatic envoys**,
spying disguised as locals in enemy territory, and judging the Tlatelolco market — so that attacks on
*pochteca* became standing **pretexts for imperial war**. Though commoners by birth, they could grow
**richer than nobles**, an "ambiguous and uneasy" new wealth they prudently hid, and they bought social
standing by sponsoring lavish feasts and offering sacrificial slaves.

## Significance

The Aztec economy shows the Americas hosting a **fully commercialised, money-using market system** —
making the Inca's reciprocity-and-redistribution model not an "American" necessity but **one of two
distinct American solutions**. It also reveals the empire as a **planned commercial enterprise**: the
Triple Alliance did not merely extract tribute but sponsored the *pochteca*, manipulated key markets, and
protected the trade arteries — commerce and conquest entwined. Columbus's 1502 encounter with a great
Maya trading canoe off Honduras (obsidian, cacao, copper, textiles) captures the reach of this
Mesoamerican commercial world on the eve of contact.

## Multi-scalar integration and imperial restructuring (Charlton, CHNPA II.1)

Thomas H. Charlton ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1]] Ch. 11) embeds
the market–tribute system in the **Central Mexican Symbiotic Region (CMSR)** — Sanders's
elevation-driven zone of crop complementarity (Basin, Toluca, Mezquital, Tulancingo,
Puebla-Tlaxcala, Morelos) with five obsidian source clusters — already underwriting economic
symbiosis at contact in 1519.

### City-state as economic module

[[Altepetl|City-states]] were the **basic economic units** of the CMSR in 1519: commoners
produced basic foods and crafts supporting themselves and the nobility; some products moved as
tribute to [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]; other goods entered the market as tribute redistributions
or *pochteca* items. Economic variability among polities reflected history, location, demography,
and relations with the Aztecs. Nevertheless an **integrated system** developed with local, regional,
and centralized dimensions; urban and rural settlements linked through interdependent political and
economic relationships, **both tribute- and market-based**.

**Early Aztec (pre-1428) pattern.** Despite political fragmentation after Tula, regional integration
persisted: widespread Pachuca and Otumba obsidian; Aztec I/II and Chalco polychromes; cotton spindle
whorls documenting cotton movement from Morelos into the Basin (Tetla, Chalco, Huexotla, Xico) —
possibly tied to more rural elites. The institutional form was the **solar market** focused on small
city-states (Smith; Hassig); local production/distribution within each *altepetl* was often the rule.
Part-time nonagricultural specialization is attested at Huexotla; Otumba shows more complex craft
linkages. Northern Basin (obsidian, weaker ag potential) emphasized nonagricultural production;
central/southern Basin emphasized agriculture, including early [[chinampa-agriculture|chinampa]]
construction that helped feed growth from ~92–120k to ~250k people.

### Indirect rule and calpixque

Political integration rested on Tenochtitlan's dominance with allied Tlacopan and Texcoco.
Administrative integration of conquered areas was weak: **indirect control**, local dynasties left
as administrators, **calpixque** (tribute collectors) coordinating provincial deliveries — maximum
benefits at minimum costs. Further integration was unusual.

### Core–periphery after 1428

With agricultural products and luxury goods flowing into the Basin from tribute and long-distance
trade after [[triple-alliance-formation-1428|1428]], the Basin system changed significantly. Nichols
& Charlton: archaeological data suggest a **core–periphery dichotomy focused on Tenochtitlan**.

| Zone | Approx. extent | Economic emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Core | ~30–35 km radius (central/southern lake system) | Intensified agriculture at expense of craft; nonag focus on salt; utilitarian and elite goods made at Tenochtitlan and entered its market; tribute and *pochteca* goods also entered capital markets; higher volume of regional goods (e.g. obsidian) at core sites |
| Periphery | Outer Basin (e.g. Otumba) | Accentuated noncomestibles: maguey fibers, obsidian cores/blades, figurines, ground stone, lapidary elite products; solar markets persist locally; specialized products enter market, tribute, or long-distance trade; some new city-states (e.g. Tepeapulco) founded to control resources and crafts |

Late Aztec Basin population **800,000–1,200,000** — a threshold for expansion, not a sufficient cause.

### Historiographical debate: craft centralization vs. markets

Some scholars (especially Brumfiel) argue the Triple Alliance, with large goods from state-administered
foreign trade and tribute, **manipulated Basin production and marketing** to reduce full-time craft
production outside Tenochtitlan and increase agricultural production directed to the capital. Smith
stresses the **importance of markets** and the need for archaeology to correct ethnohistoric biases.
Otumba craft-production research (Charlton et al.) addresses these issues directly. Provincial
signatures: Morelos materials show a post-conquest **trade→tribute** shift (urban cotton spinning
emphasis, reduced rural living standards, fewer trade items — Smith & Heath-Smith). Eastern independent
states retained distinctive ceramics with only casual trading contact with the Basin (Snow).

Together with Berdan's commodity-money and *pochteca* ethnography above, Charlton supplies the
**spatial-political architecture**: modular *altepetl* economies, solar markets, calpixque tribute,
and post-1428 core–periphery restructuring under a hegemonic rather than territorially deep empire.

## Evans & Webster 2001 (Berdan Tribute; Clark; Feinman; Coe & Coe)

Frances F. Berdan’s encyclopedia entries (Aztec Culture and History; Tribute) and related craft/market notes in [[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001|Evans & Webster 2001]] refine the tribute ledger and market toolkit:

### Tribute architecture

- **Tribute** = goods/services by conquered populations to overlords (vs taxes by citizens). Ethnohistory is richest for the late Postclassic Aztec: *Matrícula de Tributos* and Codex Mendoza Part 2 (related pictorials with Nahuatl/Spanish annotations); a 1534 Spanish document on nearly the same provinces; Sahagún, Durán, Tezozomoc, Ixtlilxochitl.
- Empirewide: **38 provinces** of 1–26 [[altepetl]] conquered by Mexica (often with Texcoco/Tlacopan); payments shared (Tenochtitlan ≈ Texcoco > Tlacopan). Multiple overlapping tribute layers (local lord + conquest state + imperial).
- **Calpixque** collectors; *petlacalcatl* warehoused goods in [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]. Schedules every **80 days**, biannually, or annually.
- Goods: maize/beans/chia/amaranth bins; chiles, cacao, maguey/bees honey, salt; ~**665** warrior costume ensembles; nearly **300,000 textile items annually** from all but two Mendoza provinces; distant provinces paid greenstone, turquoise, gold, copper, amber, jaguar skins, feathers. Manufactured goods dominate; little armament. Border/strategic city-states often not on Matrícula rolls — tribute as reciprocal “gifts.”
- Tribute alone was insufficient: local production, long-distance trade, and markets also moved goods; tribute most closely tied to imperial political/military goals.
- Conquered areas as **tributary provinces** (existing *tlatoani* usually retained with tribute collectors, sometimes governor/garrison) vs diplomatic **“strategic provinces”** on borders/routes exchanging “gifts” and kinship with more reciprocity. Imperial growth shifted control toward jadeite, gold, tropical feathers matching expanding noble demand; tribute stimulated provincial specialized production of manufactured goods.

### Markets, blades, cloth, food

- **Tlatelolco market**: Cortés claimed **60,000** daily (higher than some conquistador-scale estimates elsewhere on this page); *pochteca* oversaw layout/judging; markets daily, 5-day weekly, or 20-day; cacao beans and standardized cotton cloth as money; cacao from Soconusco via tribute and merchants. Late Postclassic markets relatively open to commoners (luxury goods in commoner house excavations) — contrasting earlier elite-restricted luxuries (Michael E. Smith; John E. Clark).
- Prismatic blades: artisan could produce **>100** nearly identical blades per core with a ~1.5 m lever pressure tool; blades sold cheap in Aztec markets; inset in wooden weapons (*macuahuitl* etc.) giving razor edges capable of decapitating a horse but needing frequent replacement (Clark).
- Cotton cloth production was women’s work; cotton clothing restricted to nobility while maguey/yucca was commoner wear (with exceptions); *quachtli* plain white cotton cloaks as exchange media — one source equates cloak grades to **65–100** cacao beans, another **240–300**; *Matrícula* lists **65,600** *quachtli* among *tilmahtli* (Berdan; Feinman; Blanton).
- Aztec craft specialization largely **domestic/household**, not industrial workshops; land and labor distributed through the political system rather than the market, the market distributing commodities and elite goods. Money-like goods: cotton cloaks, cacao beans, gold-dust quills, copper beads/axes, jade (Feinman; Blanton).
- Cuisine (Sophie Coe and Michael Coe): Aztec staples from tribute/Sahagún — maize, beans (12 varieties), *chian* (*Salvia*), *huauhtli* (Amaranthus/Chenopodium — ritual dough images of gods, esp. Huitzilopochtli), squash seeds, many chiles; “fasting” = without chile and salt. Domesticates: turkey, Muscovy duck, dog (principal animal protein; raised by tens of thousands). Lake foods: *Spirulina* cakes, shrimp, small fish, axolotl. On cannibalism, most scholars agree never massive scale; selected war captives, ~**15 g** per person, eaten without chile/salt as a religious act.
