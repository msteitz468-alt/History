# The Aztec Economy (Commodity Money, Markets, and the Pochteca)

Unlike the [[inca-empire|Inca]]'s [[mita-and-andean-reciprocity|moneyless, market-less]] economy, the
[[aztec-empire|Aztec]] world ran on a **vibrant commercial economy** of marketplaces, commodity monies,
and professional merchants — layered over the imperial **tribute** system. Documented for the wiki
through Frances Berdan's [[berdan-aztecs-2021|*The Aztecs*]] (Berdan is a leading scholar of the Aztec
economy). It is a striking contrast: two great American empires, two opposite economic solutions.

## Money that grew on trees

The Aztecs used several **commodity monies** — objects that were both useful goods and media of exchange:
- **Cacao beans** — the everyday "small change," grown in the humid lowlands and used to price small
  goods (a tamale = 1 bean; a turkey hen = 100). Common enough that **counterfeiters** hollowed and
  refilled the shells.
- **Quachtli** — large white cotton cloaks, the **high-value** currency and standard of wealth (a
  commoner could live a year on ~20; a *quachtli* was worth 65–300 cacao beans). Paid in tribute by the
  tens of thousands.
- **Thin copper axes** (and, regionally, copper bells, shells, stone beads) — lower-value currency,
  concentrated in the south.

These were **flexible commodity monies**, their rates set by bargaining and fluctuating by season and
supply — not standardised state coinage.

## The marketplace (tianquiztli)

Every city-state had its **marketplace** — the hub of goods, services, and news. The greatest,
**Tlatelolco** (beside Tenochtitlan), drew **20,000–25,000 people daily and 40,000–50,000** on the
five-day "big" market — a scale that astonished the conquistadors (Bernal Díaz "shall never finish if I
tell it all in detail"). Markets were **highly ordered**: goods sold in designated zones, prices set by
**haggling**, a central shrine-stone (*momoztli*), and **market judges** who policed cheating, false
measures, and theft on the spot. Most trade was **barter** supplemented by the commodity monies; many
markets specialised (Acolman for dogs, Azcapotzalco for slaves) or rotated across a five-town weekly
cycle, and frontier markets resembled European fairs.

## The pochteca

The **pochteca** were hereditary **long-distance professional merchants**, living in their own
*calpolli* (wards) in Tenochtitlan and a dozen Basin cities, with their own courts, schools, patron god
(**Yacatecuhtli**), and rituals. They braved hunger, robbery, and assassination to carry high-value
goods — feathers, jade, gold, cacao, cotton, slaves — across ecological zones and political borders to
**international trading centres** on the empire's fringes (Xicalanco, Xoconochco, Tochtepec). Crucially,
they doubled as **state agents**: trading the ruler's goods abroad, serving as **diplomatic envoys**,
spying disguised as locals in enemy territory, and judging the Tlatelolco market — so that attacks on
*pochteca* became standing **pretexts for imperial war**. Though commoners by birth, they could grow
**richer than nobles**, an "ambiguous and uneasy" new wealth they prudently hid, and they bought social
standing by sponsoring lavish feasts and offering sacrificial slaves.

## Significance

The Aztec economy shows the Americas hosting a **fully commercialised, money-using market system** —
making the Inca's reciprocity-and-redistribution model not an "American" necessity but **one of two
distinct American solutions**. It also reveals the empire as a **planned commercial enterprise**: the
Triple Alliance did not merely extract tribute but sponsored the *pochteca*, manipulated key markets, and
protected the trade arteries — commerce and conquest entwined. Columbus's 1502 encounter with a great
Maya trading canoe off Honduras (obsidian, cacao, copper, textiles) captures the reach of this
Mesoamerican commercial world on the eve of contact.
