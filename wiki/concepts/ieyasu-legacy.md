# The Legacy of Tokugawa Ieyasu — Statecraft, Deification, and the Edo Settlement

[[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu]] (1543–1616) founded the political order that governed
[[japan|Japan]] for 260 years (1603–1868). His legacy operates on three levels examined in
[[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]]: a **document** purporting to record his private
instructions to successors; a **cult** through which he was transformed into the deity Tosho
Daigongen; and the **institutional settlement** — the [[bakuhan-system|bakuhan system]],
[[buke-shohatto|Buke Shohatto]], [[kuge-shohatto|Kuge Shohatto]], and [[sankin-kotai|sankin-kōtai]]
— that gave the [[edo-period|Edo period]] its extraordinary stability.

## The Legacy Document

The *Legacy of Tokugawa Ieyasu* (*Tōshō-gū Goikun*) is a document of private instructions to
Ieyasu's successors in the Shogunate. Sadler notes it "exists in several recensions, and has
obviously been supplemented at a later period to include developments up to the middle of the
seventeenth century or beyond, since it mentions institutions that had not come into being at the
death of the Divine Lord, but only developed in the days of Iemitsu and Ietsuna." This discredits
it as a verbatim record of Ieyasu's words, but it is nonetheless "a large part of it quite
succinctly represents the principles and intentions of the founder." Tenkai and Kasuga no Tsubone,
who lived into the Iemitsu period, were capable of redacting the legacy to serve dynastic interests
— Tenkai even claimed direct communication with Ieyasu through "inspired dreams."

The document thus functions less as a historical source for Ieyasu's actual views than as a
normative statement of the principles on which the Tokugawa house grounded its claims to rule —
analogous to the house laws of other clan chiefs, and "in many cases contains identical material,
the product of experience in administering clans and military rule that had been gathered in the
course of the preceding centuries since Yoritomo."

## Core Principles Attributed to Ieyasu

### Benevolence (*jin*) as the Foundation of Rule

The *Legacy* insists that "the civil and military principles both proceed from Benevolence. However
many books and plans there may be the principle is the same. Know therefore that herein lies the
way of ruling and administering the Empire." This is not sentimentality but political theory: a
ruler who lacks benevolence toward his people will face rebellion; a ruler who embodies it will
have "no enemies in the Empire." "The Empire does not belong to the Empire, neither does it belong
to one man. The thing to be studied most deeply is how to act with Benevolence."

This formulation — the empire belonging to itself, entrusted to the Shogun under Heaven —
simultaneously legitimated Tokugawa rule and imposed on the ruling house the obligation of
beneficent governance. Ieyasu described his own political awakening in these terms: "Since I
discovered the teaching of Yuyo that helping the people and thus tranquillizing the country is the
Law of Nature, I have undeviatingly followed it until now. Let my descendants continue my policy.
If they reject it they are no posterity of mine. For be very certain that the people are the
foundation of the country."

### Patience (*nintai*) as the Personal Virtue of the Ruler

"The strong manly ones in life are those who understand the meaning of the word Patience. Patience
means restraining one's inclinations. There are seven emotions, joy, anger, anxiety, love, grief,
fear, and hate, and if a man does not give way to these he can be called patient." Ieyasu presented
patience as his defining quality, the key to his success after decades of subordination to Imagawa,
Nobunaga, and Hideyoshi. His famous maxim "Asu wa ame, hito wa dorobo to omoe" ("All men are
thieves and it will rain tomorrow") encapsulates the same disposition: anticipate every contingency,
trust nothing, but act steadily rather than rashly.

### Law Above Reason

A consistent theme in Ieyasu's legislation and the *Legacy* is the subordination of individual
reason to the law: "The law may upset reason, but reason may never upset the law... the sage first
studies the people's way of reasoning, and then establishes the law and determines the method of
government." This was not arbitrary authoritarianism but a systemic principle: published law,
consistently enforced, produces social order; law that bends to individual reasonableness produces
uncertainty and disorder. Even conscience — illustrated by his exile of the Fuju-fuse sect of
Nichiren for refusing on conscientious grounds to perform a religious function — was no defense
against the law: "Not even reason, much less conscience, if it be so called, was any excuse for
transgressing the laws of the Bakufu."

### Continuity with Precedent

Ieyasu consistently presented his legislation as restoration rather than innovation: "With a view
to transmitting and not to creating, for they are no new laws decreed at my will... I have drawn
them up in this form as an exemplar." He consulted Hosokawa Fujitaka on Ashikaga precedents,
retained the legal codes of Takeda Shingen when he acquired Kai (lightening only the taxation),
and maintained Hojo customs in the Kanto. He blamed Ashikaga Yoshimasa, Takeda Katsuyori, and
Saito Yoshitatsu for losing their domains through departing from ancestral custom. Precedent
legitimated; innovation destabilized.

### The Fudai–Tozama Distinction as Natural Order

The *Legacy* treats the preferential trust given to *fudai* (hereditary) vassals over *tozama*
(outside) lords as a matter of natural justice, not favoritism: "Since their fidelity and affection
exceeds that of the Outside houses, these others must not be displeased at this preference, resting
as it does on such a basis." The institutional expression — *fudai* alone staffing senior bakufu
offices, *tozama* excluded — was simultaneously a security measure and a moral statement about the
meaning of loyalty.

### Military Readiness in Peacetime

"When the Empire is at peace do not forget the possibility of war, and take counsel with the Fudai
vassals that the military arts be not allowed to deteriorate." The same principle appears in
article 1 of the [[buke-shohatto|Buke Shohatto]]: "In times of order we cannot forget disorder;
how, then, can we relax our military training?" The [[sankin-kotai|sankin-kōtai]] had a related
rationale: the processions and Edo residency kept daimyo and retinues in condition, visible, and
monitored even in peacetime.

## Personal Qualities: Ieyasu as Ruler

Chapters 42 of Sadler synthesize anecdotal material about Ieyasu's habits and character:

- **Hawking** was his only true recreation, pursued from youth to old age and justified as
  practical training: "When you go out into the country hawking you learn to understand the
  military spirit and also the hard life of the lower classes. You exercise your muscles and train
  your limbs."
- **Frugality and physical stamina** — a simple diet, avoidance of excess, an outdoor life, the
  ability to sleep anywhere and at any time, "a complete absence of nerves" — preserved health
  through decades of campaigning. His only recorded illnesses before his final one were a cold and
  a carbuncle.
- **Judgment over personal prowess** — "a general does not need to fight hand to hand. He must
  know how to avoid taking risks." He claimed never to have killed anyone with his own hand in all
  his battles, and discouraged Hidetada from excessive fencing practice. The key quality was
  "the capacity to judge the capacity of the people he uses."
- **Precision in management of subordinates** — his deathbed review of the daimyo roster (discussed
  in [[death-of-tokugawa-ieyasu-1616]]) shows the same granular attention to individual character
  that marked his career: "It never does to overlook people because they are small."

## The Deification and Its Function

The transformation of Ieyasu into the deity [[tosho-daigongen-deification-1617|Tosho Daigongen]]
in 1617 gave the Tokugawa settlement a religious dimension that reinforced its political one. For
two centuries, the Nikko shrine was the tutelary cult of the ruling house and, through it, of all
Japan. Tenkai's victory over Suden in the posthumous-title dispute — choosing *gongen* over
*daimyojin* — ensured that Ieyasu's cult was explicitly distinct from (and implicitly superior to)
the Toyotomi cult of Toyokuni Daimyojin. The Nikko shrine, positioned northeast of Edo (the
cosmologically dangerous direction), served simultaneously as guardian of the capital and as a
permanent symbol of Tokugawa divine sanction.

Sadler notes the strategic positioning: "the center of this influence being situated east of Edo
was intended to counteract any insidious and softening and disintegrating tendencies that might...
emanate from Kyoto." The deification thus completed the institutional work of the Buke and Kuge
Shohatto: where the laws subordinated the court politically, the cult placed Tokugawa authority on
a plane that transcended the court's religious legitimating function.

## Durability and Collapse

The settlement Ieyasu built lasted 260 years without major internal war — a record without parallel
in Japanese history and remarkable by any world-historical standard. Its durability rested on:

1. The elimination of all rival power centers capable of sustained military opposition.
2. The [[buke-shohatto|legal code]] that gave the bakufu legitimate grounds to discipline any
   daimyo at will.
3. The [[sankin-kotai|sankin-kōtai]] that drained daimyo resources and maintained family hostages.
4. The [[kuge-shohatto|subordination of the court]] to ceremonial functions.
5. The [[tosho-daigongen-deification-1617|religious sanction]] that wrapped the political order in
   divine authority.
6. The bakuhan system's **decentralization of local governance** — domains administered themselves,
   reducing the administrative burden on the bakufu and giving local populations stable government.

Its collapse came from external shock (the forced opening by Perry in 1853) interacting with
internal weaknesses: the fiscal impoverishment of the samurai class, the intellectual challenge of
*kokugaku* nationalism, and the emperor-centered ideology that the Kuge Shohatto had paradoxically
preserved by keeping the court alive. The *sankin-kōtai* suspension in 1862 released domain
resources that enabled the Meiji coalitions. The Meiji Restoration did not destroy Ieyasu's legacy
entirely — it channeled the organizational habits and hierarchical discipline of the Edo order into
the structures of the modern Japanese state.

## Contested Aspects

- **Authenticity of the *Legacy* document** — Sadler accepts the document as substantially
  representing Ieyasu's views while acknowledging later interpolation. Scholars have disagreed on
  how much is genuine and how much is Tenkai-era confection.
- **Benevolence vs. control** — The Confucian language of benevolence in the *Legacy* and the Buke
  Shohatto coexists with the explicitly coercive machinery of the bakuhan system. Whether Ieyasu's
  "benevolence" was a sincere political philosophy or ideological cover for dynastic consolidation
  is an unresolved interpretive question.
- **Innovation vs. restoration** — Ieyasu consistently presented himself as conserving precedent,
  but the *sankin-kōtai*, the Kuge Shohatto, and the deification were all genuinely novel. The
  restoration framing was itself a political choice.
