## Definition and Scope

Waḥdat al-wujūd — conventionally translated "Unity of Being" or "Oneness of Existence" — is the central metaphysical doctrine associated with the Spanish-born Sufi theosopher [[ibn-arabi|Ibn ʿArabī]] (d. 1240). It holds that ultimate reality is one, that God alone truly "is," and that the multiplicity of created things are manifestations, theophanies, or reflections of the single divine reality rather than independently existing substances.

The translation of *wujūd* as mere "existence" or "being" obscures important nuance. Schimmel, following Marijan Molé, notes that *wujūd* derives from the Arabic root meaning "finding" or "to be found," making the doctrine more dynamic than static Western translations suggest: "At the end of the Path only God is present, is 'found.'" Waḥdat al-wujūd thus also carries connotations of the act of existentialization itself, and the perception of that act — sometimes rendering it nearly synonymous with *waḥdat al-shuhūd* ("unity of witnessing"), though the two terms became the subject of intense later debate, particularly in India ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## Core Doctrine

### The Divine Reality and Its Self-Manifestation

Ibn ʿArabī begins from the premise that God's pure essence (*dhāt*) is absolutely unknowable and transcends all categories: "By Himself He sees Himself. None sees Him other than He." Yet God, described via the prophetic *ḥadīth* as a "hidden treasure" who "wanted to be known," brings creation into being as a mirror for His self-manifestation (*tajallī*, pl. *tajalliyāt*).

Creation is understood not as a substantial event but as an "effusion of Being upon the heavenly archetypes" — comparable to articulation or breath. The Koranic concept of the *nafas al-Raḥmān* ("breath of the Merciful") becomes for Ibn ʿArabī the image of creation itself: the divine essence held its breath, and the world appeared. As breath is inhaled and exhaled, the universe is perpetually created and annihilated, drawn out from and returned to transcendent unity. The two movements are encoded in the *shahāda* itself: *lā ilāh* points to the emanation of "things other than He," while *illā Allāh* indicates their return.

### Names, Archetypes, and Creatures

A central pillar of the system is the correlation between the divine names (*asmāʾ*) and the things named. Each of the ninety-nine divine names is the *rabb* ("Lord") of a particular created being, which is in turn its *marbūb*. The names function as archetypes or molds through which creative energy is channeled to produce particular beings. The name remains Lord; the creature remains servant: "God becomes the mirror in which the spiritual man contemplates his own reality and man in turn becomes the mirror in which God contemplates His Names and Qualities."

Each creature's existence consists only in its "face turned toward God" — that face is real, while the rest is "pure not-Being." This formulation is crucial to contested interpretations: creatures in their actual existence are not *identical* with God, but are *reflections* of His attributes. Transcendence across all categories, including substance, is maintained in Ibn ʿArabī's careful formulation, even as popular condensations of the doctrine — expressed in Persian as *hama ūst* ("everything is He") — often collapse this distinction.

### Cosmic Orders

Ibn ʿArabī maps reality onto a hierarchy of cosmic planes:
- **Hāhūt** — the divine essence itself (named from the Arabic letter *h*, symbol of essence)
- **Lāhūt** — the divine nature in self-revelation
- **Jabarūt** — spiritual existence beyond form; site of divine decrees
- **ʿĀlam al-mithāl** — the "world of images" or "imaginal world," inserted between jabarūt and malakūt, where existentialization occurs; the plane reached by the *himma* (spiritual ambition) and prayers of saints
- **Malakūt** — the angelic world
- **Nāsūt** — the plane of humanity and material creation

### The Pantheism Controversy

The question of whether waḥdat al-wujūd constitutes pantheism, panentheism, or a sui generis Islamic metaphysics has been debated since the thirteenth century and remains open. Western scholarship has tended toward a pantheist reading (A. A. Affifi: "Ibn ʿArabī is a pure pantheist"). Louis Massignon proposed the term "existential monism" (*monisme existentiel*). Henry Corbin and Seyyed H. Nasr have argued against the pantheist label, stressing the decisive role of theophanies and "creative imagination" and insisting that substantial continuity between God and creation is not asserted.

Orthodox Muslim critics, from the Hanbalite theologian Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) to the modern thinker Fazlur Rahman, have seen the system as inevitably corrosive of moral seriousness: "A thoroughly monistic system, no matter how pious and conscientious it may claim to be, cannot, by its very nature, take seriously the objective validity of moral standards." Hans Heinrich Schaeder (1925) contrasted Ḥallāj's activism with Ibn ʿArabī's contemplative system, arguing that active loving surrender to God was replaced by a *Weltdeutung* (world-explanation) no longer concerned with moral agency.

## Waḥdat al-Wujūd vs. Waḥdat al-Shuhūd

The most significant internal Islamic debate arose between partisans of *waḥdat al-wujūd* (unity of being) and *waḥdat al-shuhūd* (unity of witnessing/contemplation). The latter position — associated most prominently with the Indian Naqshbandī reformer Aḥmad Sirhindī (d. 1624) — holds that the mystic's experience of unity is an epistemic state, a witnessing in which only God seems present, rather than an ontological fact about the structure of reality. The two terms were, as Schimmel notes following Molé, sometimes even interchangeable in earlier usage, making the later sharp distinction partly a product of polemical clarification in South Asian Sufism.

## Reception and Influence

After the thirteenth century, Ibn ʿArabī's terminology became virtually inescapable even for mystics who did not accept his positions. The doctrine spread through the Persian- and Turkish-speaking worlds with particular force in the aftermath of the Mongol conquests, which seem paradoxically to have catalyzed an upsurge of mystical activity and the need for a comprehensive metaphysical framework.

Key vehicles of transmission included:
- Ṣadruddīn Qōnawī (d. 1274), Ibn ʿArabī's principal interpreter in Anatolia
- [[shabistari-mahmud|Maḥmūd Shabistarī]]'s *Gulshan-i Rāz* (1311), the most accessible Persian poetic introduction to post-Ibn ʿArabī Sufism
- ʿAbd al-Karīm Jīlī's *Al-Insān al-Kāmil* ("The Perfect Man"), which systematized the doctrine's implications for anthropology

The popular Persian condensation *hama ūst* — "everything is He" — preceded Ibn ʿArabī himself (found in ʿAṭṭār's poetry) but became identified with his school. Jāmī (d. 1492), the great Naqshbandī poet and admirer of Ibn ʿArabī, expressed this in verse while also maintaining a distinction: "We and Thou are not separate from each other, but we need Thee, whereas Thou dost not need us."

In India, the doctrine took root among Sufi orders and provoked the most sustained theological counter-argument in the form of Sirhindī's *waḥdat al-shuhūd*. The debate shaped South Asian Islamic intellectual culture for centuries.

## Imagery and Poetic Expression

The concept generated a rich repertoire of images in Islamic mystical poetry:
- **Ocean and drop**: the individual soul as a drop of water returning to the divine ocean (Rūmī, Jāmī, and countless others); alternatively, the drop becomes a pearl — changed by its journey, unable to live without the ocean yet distinct from it
- **Mirror**: God and creation as mutual mirrors; the heart of the saint as a polished surface reflecting divine names
- **Shadow play**: the mystic as puppet of the divine playwright (used by Ibn al-Fāriḍ, ʿAṭṭār, Rūmī; ultimately from Chinese shadow-theatre adopted into Islamic entertainment by the tenth century)
- **Breath** (*nafas al-Raḥmān*): creation as divine exhalation, annihilation as inhalation
- **Chess**: God as the great chess player, creatures as figures carved from one piece of wood who nevertheless move against each other

## Historiography

The doctrine's interpretation has been shaped by the disciplinary commitments of scholars. Orientalists of the late nineteenth and early twentieth century tended toward pan-pantheism readings, often importing Western philosophical categories ill-suited to the Arabic metaphysical vocabulary. The "existential" reading of *wujūd* (following Western being-language) was complicated by Massignon's linguistic work and decisively reoriented by Corbin's phenomenological approach, which emphasized the "imaginal world" and creative imagination. Contemporary scholarship (William Chittick, James Morris) has largely followed Corbin and Nasr in rejecting the pantheist label while acknowledging the genuine difficulty of the texts.
