# African Slave Trades in Comparative and Global Perspective

## Definition and Scope

The **comparative global framework** for African slave trades is the analytical approach that treats Africa's four distinct slave trading systems — the **Atlantic**, **trans-Saharan**, **Indian Ocean**, and **internal sub-Saharan** trades — as components of a single integrated slaving system centred on the African continent, rather than as separate oceanic or regional histories. The framework was systematically articulated by Pier M. Larson in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]] and represents a significant methodological departure from the dominant Atlantic-focused historiography.

The central claim: **analysis based solely on any one of Africa's external slave trades leads to partial and erroneous conclusions** about the scale, demography, geography, and long-term impact of African slaving.

## The Aggregate Picture

### Four trades, one system

| Trade | Approximate period | Estimated volume (1401–1900) |
|---|---|---|
| Atlantic | 1501–c.1870 | 12.6 million (68% of external trades) |
| Trans-Saharan | ongoing from c. 650 CE | ~3 million (1401–1900) |
| Indian Ocean | ongoing from c. 650 CE | ~3 million (1401–1900) |
| **External total** | 1401–1900 | **~18.5 million** |
| Internal sub-Saharan | concurrent | ~18+ million (rough estimate = external total) |
| **Combined all trades** | 1401–1900 | **~37 million+** |

Taking a longer view beginning c. 650 CE: the combined volume of the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trades across their full duration equals that of the entire Atlantic trade. The Atlantic dominated in *rate* (faster economic growth in the Americas drove higher annual demand), but not in cumulative *duration*. See [[atlantic-slave-trade]], [[trans-saharan-trade]], and [[indian-ocean-trade]] for individual treatments.

### The internal trades: the invisible majority

The most significant revision the comparative framework introduces: **more captives remained in sub-Saharan Africa than departed it**. Paul Lovejoy and David Richardson found that in the central Sudan between 1780 and 1850, "a majority, perhaps a large majority, of newly enslaved individuals and their slave descendants stayed within West Africa." The Sokoto Caliphate by mid-nineteenth century held approximately **2 million enslaved persons**.

Interior zones of heavy slave concentration included: the Mande-speaking areas and middle Niger River bend; the Senegal River valley and Futa Jalon highlands; the Asante kingdom; the Nile River south of the Sahara; highland Ethiopia; the African Great Lakes region (Buganda, Bunyoro, Busoga, Buhaya); both banks of Lake Tanganyika and the Lualaba River; the Nyamwezi highland interior of Tanzania (Tabora region); the Rufiji River corridor; the Lake Malawi–inland Zambezi region; and the Angolan highlands.

The Segu Bambara state of the Middle Niger (c. 1712–1861) exemplifies the intersecting pattern: "Slaves captured by Segu warriors were sent to the Atlantic, the desert, and interior markets. Some were retained locally" (Richard Roberts). Interior intersections — not coastal entrepôts — were the centres of African slave production.

## Why Africa? The Unresolved Question

Why sub-Saharan Africa became and remained a net labour provider to the globe over two millennia is, as Larson notes, a question that "has not received adequate attention." Proposed explanations:

- **Racial ideology**: Slave traders justified operations through racial theories. But race cannot explain why Africans enslaved *other Africans* internally, or why non-European agents (Arab, Indian, Persian merchants) also enslaved Africans in the Indian Ocean.
- **Tropical disease immunity**: Certain African captives carried immunities to tropical diseases, making them desirable labourers in Caribbean and Brazilian plantation zones. Partial explanation at best — not all captives went to disease-endemic environments.
- **Labour productivity differential**: A labour theory of value argument suggests that African workers were "relatively unproductive" in situ, making their transfer to higher-productivity environments (the Atlantic plantation complex) economically rational. Contested.
- **Cultural reluctance to enslave Europeans** (Eltis): Europeans in the post-medieval era were reluctant to enslave other Europeans but had no such inhibition regarding Africans. Explains Atlantic trade but not the trans-Saharan or Indian Ocean trades, which predate European participation.

Larson's conclusion: "Answers will not be singular and may require working with complex, interactive models and taking multiple cultural and economic influences into consideration."

## The Demography of the Four Trades

Comparative demography reveals sharp divergences between trading systems:

### Atlantic: male-majority external trade, female surplus at African coasts

The Atlantic trade carried approximately 64% males and 36% females among captives. A significant minority of males were boys; thus fewer than half of all Atlantic captives were adult men (49%). This gender imbalance was matched by a compensating **female accumulation at African coastal zones**: contemporary accounts and eighteenth-century censuses show abnormally large proportions of women in urban and rural coastal settlements. Fertility rates in these zones remained normal or high even as the overall population suffered depopulation, because the female surplus sustained reproduction.

The **supplying regions** — the African interior — suffered the greatest depopulation, while coastal areas maintained or grew their populations through the influx of retained female captives.

### Trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean: female-majority trades

In sharp contrast, the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trades carried varying assortments, but **females and children tended to predominate** — particularly in trades to the Arabian Peninsula and Persian Gulf, which absorbed women primarily as domestic servants and concubines. From the mid-nineteenth century, male proportions in East African Indian Ocean exports increased. European vessels operating in the Indian Ocean (post-1500) typically carried more males.

### Internal trades: female preference

A "strong preference developed for female captives" in the internal sub-Saharan trades, with exceptions in areas supplying the trans-Saharan/Red Sea trades or heavily involved in warfare-based capture.

The global demographic pattern: **only the Atlantic trade was consistently male-majority at the point of export from Africa**. All other trades showed female majorities or parity. These patterns were connected through the logic of African interior markets, where females were preferred for domestic and productive labour, and the differential external demands determined which captives left the continent.

## The Circulation of Goods and People Across Oceanic Boundaries

A key argument of the comparative framework is that oceanic histories (Atlantic history, Indian Ocean history, Mediterranean history) artificially segment what was a continuous system. Examples:

- West African gold crossed the Sahara with captives, transited Egypt, and reached India and China.
- Captives who crossed the Sahara were often re-exported into the Indian Ocean (Persian Gulf, Red Sea).
- American silver flowed into the Indian Ocean as payment for captives.
- Slaves from Kilwa, Mozambique, and Madagascar were disembarked in Virginia, Barbados, Brazil, and Cuba.
- South Asian textiles were "nearly everywhere key to the Atlantic slave trade."
- New England cottons purchased slaves and ivory in East Africa.
- Cowrie shells from the Maldive Islands circulated counter to the flow of captives across West Africa, arriving via sea and overland.
- Ships' crews and captives were "not altogether different categories" in terms of the blurring of labour coercion across oceanic systems.

## Methodological Implications

1. **Centring Africa**: "Recentring analysis to Africa in global histories of slaving allows scholars better to assess the continent's simultaneous and entangled external trades and internal slave systems" — including the interior, which "is typically invisible in externally oriented histories."

2. **Against ocean-bounded history**: Oceanic frameworks (Atlantic history, Indian Ocean world) were originally designed to challenge area-studies models based on continents; they have inadvertently created new area studies "bounded by land [i.e., seabed] rather than by water." Neither oceanic nor continental histories are intrinsically superior; both are needed.

3. **Segment-by-period analysis**: Demographic patterns in African slaving are "best studied segment-by-segment and period-by-period rather than in aggregate by ocean" because the patterns shift significantly over time and between sub-regional systems.

4. **Interior as research frontier**: "The African interior is where future research should yield important results" — the internal slave trades have received far less systematic attention than the external.

## Destinations of External Captives

- **Trans-Saharan trade**: Primarily Egypt, Libya, Morocco (largest southern Mediterranean economies); some onward to the Ottoman Empire, Persian Gulf, western India; most absorbed as household servants, elite administrators, and slave soldiers. A minority in productive enterprises (date farming, pearl diving, opium and cotton production) — less than previously recognized.
- **Indian Ocean trade**: Arabian Peninsula and Persian Gulf consumed most East African captives; the Mascarene Islands (French colonial), inland Mesopotamia, and parts of South Asia (Gujarat, Bombay, the Deccan, Sri Lanka) were important in specific periods. Some Atlantic re-export (New England to Rio de la Plata).
- **Zanj revolts**: East African slaves (*Zanj*) on agricultural estates in southern Mesopotamia (ninth century CE) joined a major revolt against the Abbasid Caliphate, severely weakening Baghdad — one of the largest slave revolts in pre-modern history.
