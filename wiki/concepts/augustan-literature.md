# Augustan Literature and Society

The literary culture of the **"Golden Age" of Latin** — Virgil, Horace, Livy, the elegists,
Ovid — and its complex, never simply subservient, relationship to **patronage** and the
[[principate|Augustan regime]]. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 19 (**Gavin
Townend**).

## A conservative, imitative culture

Townend stresses the **conservatism** of Roman literary culture, governed by ***imitatio***:
first the transfer of Greek **genres** into Latin, then the recognition of a Roman master
(Cicero, Virgil) to be adapted to new themes — "avoidance of innovation and failure to welcome
change," paralleling Roman politics, where Augustus' revolution was "concealed under the cover of
'restoration of the Republic'." Only **satire** had no Greek model. The masters (Cicero, Virgil,
Horace) became paragons whose dominance even the Silver Age reactions (Seneca, Lucan) "never
escaped." The period boundary itself is "particularly arbitrary" — the 30s a transition in
literature as in politics.

## Patronage and its limits

After Actium the social position of literature shifted: **oratory lost its pre-eminence** with
its "divorce from a genuine political function," and literature became "more and more the
property of an elite." Most writers came **from outside Rome** (the Italian towns, Cisalpine and
southern Gaul, Spain) but were assimilated into upper-class society. The great poets entered the
circles of **Maecenas** (Virgil, Horace) and **Messalla** (Tibullus) — Horace, son of a freedman
and a Philippi veteran, becoming a country gentleman; Virgil restored after losing his Mantuan
estate. But Townend's central argument is that **patronage did not produce subservience**:
Maecenas did not commission works "for quasi-political reasons" (apart from occasional pieces like
the *Carmen Saeculare*), and **Livy** remained a notorious republican sympathizer and admirer of
Pompey without losing Augustus' friendship. The poets stayed conspicuously **out of the
Antony–Octavian propaganda war**.

## "Propaganda" doesn't fit

Townend insists the term **"propaganda" fits awkwardly**:

- **Virgil's *Aeneid*** is not the national epic Augustus might have wanted: it ends not in
  triumph but in the "savage killing" of Turnus "in the madness of rage and revenge," and its
  pageant of Roman glory closes on the death of **Marcellus** "in a minor key" — what matters to
  Virgil "in the end is the price paid for military and political triumph."
- **Horace's** Roman Odes "hardly add up to direct propaganda": overt praise of Augustus "drifts
  off into the poet's private reactions and his addiction to wine and girls," the private themes
  predominating.

## The poets against the moral programme

Most strikingly, the poets propounded **values at variance with Augustus' moral reform**. The
[[augustan-social-legislation|Julian marriage laws]] sought Roman men marrying Roman women and
producing children for Rome's wars — yet **Virgil never married**, **Horace remained celibate**,
the elegists (Tibullus, Propertius) wrote of **extramarital and bisexual love** ("no sons to
fight Rome's wars," Propertius), and **Ovid** flaunted a world of "philandering and promiscuity,"
declaring (*Ars Amatoria*) "I congratulate myself on being born now" — that the *pax Augusta* had
produced the conditions for **unworried self-indulgence**. This defiance, more than his alleged
complicity in the younger Julia's intrigues, Townend judges "largely responsible" for Ovid's
**banishment** (AD 8) — "the clearest example of a decisive punishment visited by Augustus on an
offensive writer."

## Flattery and *libertas* under the Julio-Claudians

As the Julio-Claudian age advanced, **flattery** grew (Velleius on Tiberius; Seneca's
*Apocolocyntosis* mocking the dead Claudius to exalt Nero) — often building a new emperor by
blackening his predecessor, the very mechanism of the historical tradition. Against it ran the
senatorial ideal of ***libertas*** — the right to speak and act by conscience, harking back to
Cato, Brutus, and Cassius — given voice in the independent figures Tacitus chose to dramatize
(Cremutius Cordus defending the historian's rights; Thrasea Paetus upholding the old values),
though this ideal "never proposed any genuine reform of the imperial system."

## Contested

The old reading of Augustan poetry as **officially inspired propaganda** (the *Georgics* as an
agricultural revival programme; the *Aeneid* as panegyric) is what Townend overturns — but the
**"poetry or propaganda?"** dichotomy, and the **sincerity** of the poets (the possibility of
ironic flattery), remain genuinely debated, the more so because "we can never confidently assess
the poet's sincerity."
