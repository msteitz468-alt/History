## Definition and Scope

Sufi prophetology designates the cluster of doctrines, practices, and literary genres through which Sufi thinkers and communities engaged with the person of the Prophet Muhammad — moving beyond legal imitation (following the *sunna*) toward mystical identification, cosmological speculation, and devotional veneration. Schimmel's treatment traces a trajectory from simple loving imitation to elaborate cosmic theories, while noting that popular piety and learned theosophy remained in productive dialogue throughout.

The concept is both emic (Sufis themselves developed the framework of *fanā fīʾr-rasūl* and *nūr muḥammadī* as spiritual categories) and analytical (prophetology as a scholarly label for this complex).

## Foundations: Muhammad as Model and Limit

From earliest times, Muhammad served as the behavioral ideal for the faithful — imitated even in dress, beard style, ablutions, and food preference. But the Sufis added dimensions beyond imitation:

1. **Muhammad as divine test**: Rūmī (M 3:801) says the Prophet is "put in between" as a divine test for man — the acknowledgment of Muhammad's special rank distinguishes genuine Islam from pantheism or the "Iblisian tauḥīd" that bows only before God directly. "Mystics who used the first half of the profession of faith exclusively, without acknowledging the special rank of Muhammad, were prone to fall into a sweeping pantheistic interpretation of Islam."

2. **Muhammad as intercessor**: In popular veneration, the dominant image is Muhammad as *shafīʿ* (intercessor) at Doomsday — "sent as mercy for the worlds, *raḥmatan lilʿālamīn*" (Sūra 21:107). This trust in intercession colored the simplest folk piety from Indonesia to West Africa.

## The Nūr Muḥammadī (Muhammadan Light)

The speculative core of Sufi prophetology is the **nūr muḥammadī** — the Muhammadan Light. Schimmel traces its development:

- **Muqātil (8th c.)**: First interpreted the "Light Verse" (Sūra 24:35) as pertaining to Muhammad, "whose light shines through the other prophets" (W 93–94).
- **Sahl at-Tustarī (d. 896)**: "When He wanted to create Muhammad He showed a light from His light which illuminated the whole kingdom."
- **Ḥallāj (d. 922)**: In his *Kitāb aṭ-ṭawāsīn*, Muhammad is "the first created being, his light precedes everything and is part of God's light" — "both the cause and goal of creation."
- **ʿAṭṭār**: "The origin of the soul is the absolute light, nothing else, / That means it was the light of Muhammad, nothing else." (*Ushturnāme* 358)

The doctrinal formula is a *ḥadīth qudsī*: *laulāka mā khalaqtuʾl-aflāka* — "if thou hadst not been, I would not have created the heavens." This extra-canonical tradition, apparently first formulated around the 10th century, became the cornerstone of later prophetology.

## The Miʿrāj as Mystical Paradigm

Muhammad's night journey and heavenly ascent (*miʿrāj*) — barely touched upon in the Koran (Sūra 17:1) but elaborately embellished in later legend — became the primary object of mystical meditation and the paradigm for mystical experience generally.

- Bāyezīd Bisṭāmī was the first Sufi to use miʿrāj imagery for his own mystical flight through the heavens.
- The mystic's experience of *waqt* — the eternal now, breaking through creaturely time — was understood through the lens of Muhammad's "I have a time with God in which no created being has access, not even Gabriel."
- Even Gabriel cannot accompany Muhammad beyond *as-sidrat al-muntahā*, the ultimate lotus tree: "If I would go one step further, my wings would get burned" — interpreted as the limit of discursive reason before divine love: "Reason speaks, like Gabriel: O Aḥmad, / If I advance one step, He will burn me." (M 1:1066)
- Crucially, even in this moment of supreme proximity, Muhammad is called *ʿabduhu*, "His servant" — the highest possible name for a human being, one who speaks to God without being extinguished.

Enrico Cerulli demonstrated that the literary genre of the *Libro della Scala* — the "Book of Miʿrāj" — was sufficiently well known in the medieval Mediterranean that possible influences on Dante's *Divina Commedia* cannot be excluded.

## Fanā fīʾr-Rasūl: Annihilation in the Prophet

Mature Sufi soteriology added a Muhammadan station to the classical stages of annihilation: the mystic no longer goes straight toward God, but must first achieve:

1. **Fanā fīʾsh-shaykh** — annihilation in the spiritual master
2. **Fanā fīʾr-rasūl** — annihilation in the Prophet
3. **Fanā fī Allāh** — annihilation in God (if ever reached)

The sheikh, at his highest, is "united with the *ḥaqīqa muḥammadiyya*" and thus becomes the Perfect Man, leading disciples "with a guidance granted directly by God." This structure made the sheikh not merely a teacher but a sacramental mediator — a development with enormous institutional consequences for the ṭarīqa system.

## The Maulid (Birthday Celebration)

From the late 11th century, and universally from the 12th, the veneration of the Prophet assumed visible public form in the **maulid** — celebration of his birthday on 12 Rabīʿ ul-awwal. 

The most beloved example: Süleyman Çelebi of Bursa (d. 1419) composed the *Mevlȗd-i şerif* in simple Turkish — "still recited on special occasions in many Turkish families: at the anniversaries, or at the fortieth day, of a bereavement or in fulfilment of a vow." Similar welcome-poems for the newborn Prophet were composed in Arabic, Persian, Turkish, Urdu, Swahili, Sindhi, Panjabi, and other languages.

The *naʿt* (praise poetry for the Prophet) became one of the most important literary genres across the Persianate world. Major figures: Sanāʾī, Khāqānī, ʿUrfī, Rūmī (whose *Mathnawī* is permeated with Muhammadan imagery), Ghālib. Even Hindu poets in India composed *naʿt* in Urdu and Persian.

The **Burda** of al-Būṣīrī (13th c. Egypt) — composed when the Prophet miraculously healed the poet by casting his mantle (*burda*) upon him — became the single most widely circulated text of this genre, translated into Persian, Turkish, Urdu, and Swahili.

## Letter Mysticism and the Name

Later Sufis elaborated Muhammad's name through letter mysticism (*ḥurūfī* tendencies):

- The *ḥadīth qudsī* *anā Aḥmad bilā mīm* ("I am Aḥmad without the letter m" = *Aḥad*, "One"): only the letter *m* — associated with creatureliness, death, and limitation — separates the Prophet from divine Unity. The numerical value of *m* is forty, corresponding to the forty stages of human descent into and ascent from creaturely existence.
- Adam's bodily form was held to spell "M-Ḥ-M-D" in old Arabic script: "The form of Adam, and therefore of every human being, bears in itself the name of Muhammad."
- Aḥmad Sirhindī (d. 1624) built his entire program for Islamic renewal around speculation on the two *m*'s of Muhammad.

## Muhammad as Limit of Islam's Identity

Schimmel emphasizes a sociological-theological function: "It is Muhammad who makes Islam a distinct religion." When political Islam was defeated by Western colonialism, reformers "called them *ṭarīqa Muḥammadiyya*, 'the Muhammadan path'" — the figure of Muhammad became a center of resistance and renewal for movements in India (Mīr Dard, Aḥmad Brelwī) and North Africa (Sanūsiyya). Iqbal's formulation: "You can deny God, but you cannot deny the Prophet."

## The Ummī Doctrine

Muhammad's Koranic designation as *ummī* (usually "illiterate") received a distinctly Sufi interpretation: not mere illiteracy but the pure receptivity of a heart unspoiled by outward intellectual achievement — "as pure a vessel as Mary's virgin body." Later mystics who boasted of alleged illiteracy were invoking this Muhammadan model of pure love over cold reasoning.

## Historiography

Tor Andrae's *Die person Muhammads in lehre und glauben seiner gemeinde* (Stockholm, 1918) remains the foundational study of Muhammad veneration's development across the first six centuries of Islam. Schimmel's own *And Mohammed Is His Messenger* (Chapel Hill, 1985) extends this analysis. Constance Padwick's *Muslim Devotions* (1960) documents popular piety.

The collection is strong for Persian and Turkish *naʿt* literature and for classical Sufi theoretical treatments; the Swahili, West African, and Indonesian popular dimensions are thinly covered.

## Contested Dimensions

- Orthodox critics (Ibn al-Jauzī and others) accused Sufis of fabricating *ḥadīth* to support Muhammadan mysticism; indeed, major traditions (including *laulāka*) have no basis in 9th-century canonical collections.
- The equation *muṭāʿ = Muhammad* in Ghazzālī raised questions about the boundary between prophetic mediation and divine function.
- Reformist Islam from the 18th century onward has often targeted Muhammadan popular veneration as bidʿa (innovation) — particularly the maulid celebration and saint-tomb visitation.
