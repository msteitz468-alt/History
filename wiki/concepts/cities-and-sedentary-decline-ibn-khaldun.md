## Definition

The concept that urban sedentary civilization is not self-sustaining but is instead a dependent product of royal authority ([[asabiyyah]]-backed dynastic power), that it passes through a predictable life-cycle, and that the very luxury it generates becomes the agent of its decline. Articulated by [[ibn-khaldun]] in Chapter IV of [[the-muqaddimah]], this is the urban dimension of his broader theory of [[ilm-al-umran]] (the science of human civilization).

The core claim: "Dynasties are prior to towns and cities. Towns and cities are secondary (products) of royal authority." Cities do not arise organically from human preference; they require coercive collective effort only possible when a dynasty imposes and funds it. The life of any given city is therefore tied to the life of the dynasty that founded or protects it.

## The Urban Life-Cycle

Ibn Khaldun identifies five stages through which cities pass, though he does not number them explicitly:

### Stage 1 — Foundation by Dynasty

Buildings and city planning are features of sedentary culture brought about by luxury and tranquility. They presuppose a prior Bedouin phase: nomadic group-solidarity ([[asabiyyah]]) produces royal authority; royal authority then creates the conditions for sedentary life. "The stick of royal authority is what compels [people to build cities], or they may be stimulated by promise of reward and compensation. Such reward amounts to so large a sum that only royal authority and a dynasty can pay for it."

Founding a city requires not only coercive direction but also engineering skill, machinery (pulleys, hoists), and sustained collective labor. Ibn Khaldun explicitly rejects the folk belief that ancient monumental cities (Baghdad with 65,000 baths, the pyramids of Egypt, the arches at Carthage) were built by giants of superhuman physical stature. They were built by coordinated human effort — itself the product of strong dynastic organization. Very large monuments are typically the work of successive dynasties, each adding to what the previous generation began; the Dam of Ma'rib and the great mosques are his examples.

### Stage 2 — Growth and Elaboration

As long as the dynasty is vigorous, the city grows: "If the dynasty is of long duration and lasts a long time, new constructions will always go up in the town, the number of large mansions will increase, and the walls of the town will extend farther and farther." Baghdad under the Abbasids exemplifies peak growth.

Population size drives prosperity through a labor-surplus mechanism (see [[urban-economics-ibn-khaldun]]): the more inhabitants, the more cooperative labor, the larger the surplus above bare necessities, and therefore the more luxury, craft specialization, and commercial activity. A large city (Ibn Khaldun uses Fez, Cairo, Tlemcen) will have higher-quality goods, lower food prices relative to necessities, and even better-off beggars than a smaller city.

### Stage 3 — Sedentary Luxury Takes Hold

Luxury is the pivot of the entire theory. As wealth increases, inhabitants habituate to comfort: finer houses, richer clothes, more elaborate food, servants, fine mounts. "The more numerous and the more abundant the civilization (population) in a city, the more luxurious is the life of its inhabitants." This is not morally condemned in a simple way — luxury produces the crafts, the arts, the markets — but it has a structural consequence: it undermines the martial virtues that produced [[asabiyyah]] in the first place.

Urban populations become dependent (on imported food, on hired soldiers, on the dynastic umbrella) and lose the self-reliance of the desert. Luxury becomes "customary" and then "necessary," raising the cost of urban life to the point where Bedouins cannot afford to settle there: "When a city has a highly developed, abundant civilization and is full of luxuries, there is a very large demand for those conveniences... they become customary, and thus come to be necessities."

### Stage 4 — Dynastic Contraction and Urban Decay

When the dynasty weakens or collapses — whether from internal luxury-induced enervation, from conquest, or from the exhaustion of asabiyyah described in Chapters II–III — the city loses its protector. "The life of the dynasty is the life of the town." Two scenarios follow:

1. **Geographic reprieve**: if the city sits in or near productive territory with a surrounding rural population capable of replenishing it, urban life may persist even after the founding dynasty falls — Fez and Bougie survive because highland populations continuously migrate in.
2. **Structural collapse**: if no such hinterland exists, the city decays rapidly — al-Kufah, al-Basrah, al-Qayrawan, and the original Baghdad are examples of cities whose populations dispersed when dynastic protection ended.

A new dynasty may occupy an existing city, giving it a "second life" — Cairo under the Fatimids supplanting the earlier Tulunids is Ibn Khaldun's example.

### Stage 5 — Physical Ruin and Bedouinization

The physical fabric of a city follows its population downward. "When a city is first founded, [buildings] are built in Bedouin style, and the materials used for them are perishable." As civilization grows, stone, marble, and lime replace mud-brick. As it recedes, the process reverses: skilled craftsmen leave or die; building materials become unavailable; existing masonry is cannibalized for new (smaller) structures; adobe replaces stone; ornament disappears. "The architecture of the city reverts to that of villages and hamlets. The mark of the desert shows in it." The city returns to the same material conditions from which royal authority once lifted it.

Ibn Khaldun's empirical illustration of irreversible physical decay: the aqueduct arches at Carthage (which he witnessed being dismantled stone by stone by builders from Tunis who could barely break them) show that even destructive efforts against great monuments are enormously laborious — construction was a dynastic achievement that individual effort cannot replicate.

## Site Planning and Natural Requirements

Chapter IV also contains a sophisticated urban geography argument. A well-founded city must satisfy:

- **Defensibility**: rugged terrain, hilltop or island situation, or river-crossing approach
- **Air quality**: stagnant putrid air (near swamps or corrupt water) causes disease; population density itself improves air circulation (Ibn Khaldun's explanation for why depopulated Gabes became plague-ridden)
- **Fresh water**: river or good springs nearby
- **Pasturage** for livestock; **arable fields** for grain
- **Timber** for construction and fuel
- **Sea access** for trade (desirable but secondary)

Arab cities founded at the beginning of Islam — al-Qayrawan, al-Kufah, al-Basrah, Sijilmasah — failed on most of these criteria because their founders were Bedouins who cared only for camel pasture and proximity to desert caravan routes. This structural defect made them peculiarly vulnerable when dynastic protection weakened: they had no natural resource base to sustain a resident population independently.

The contrast is with non-Arab (Persian, Byzantine, Roman, Coptic) urban traditions, which had millennia to develop naturally suited sites and to embed the crafts deeply in the population. "The Persians had had a period of thousands of years. The same was the case with the Copts, the Nabataeans, and the Romans. They had a great deal of time, and the crafts became firmly established among them."

## Luxury, Prices, and Economic Analysis

Ibn Khaldun's urban economics (developed in sections 11–13 of Chapter IV) anticipates several early-modern insights:

**Labor surplus theory of prosperity**: Large populations produce more cooperative labor than their own subsistence requires. The surplus supports luxury goods, specialized crafts, and trade with other cities. Profit = value of labor; more labor = more profit. Prosperity thus scales with population in a self-reinforcing spiral.

**Price structure in cities**: In large cities, necessary foodstuffs are *cheap* (everyone grows or imports grain; surplus is large) while luxury goods and skilled labor are *expensive* (high demand from many wealthy buyers, competing for limited artisans). In small cities, the reverse: grain is relatively expensive (small producers, fear of shortage) while conveniences are cheap (no demand). This analysis is empirically grounded: Ibn Khaldun compares Fez, Tlemcen, Oran, and Algiers in a descending hierarchy, and notes that even beggars in Fez beg for luxuries (meat, butter, utensils) that would get them chased away in Tlemcen.

**Tax and cost**: Government levies on markets and at city gates raise the cost of food in cities above desert prices — a burden that falls harder in the "later years of a dynasty" as fiscal pressure increases.

**Bedouin exclusion**: Bedouins cannot afford to live in large cities because their incomes (generated in labor-poor desert environments) are structurally insufficient to pay large-city prices. Only a Bedouin who has already accumulated surplus property can make the transition to urban life.

## The Arab and Berber Cases

Ibn Khaldun applies the general theory to specific regional patterns. The Berbers (of Ifriqiyah and the Maghrib) had been rooted in nomadic life for millennia before Islam. European Christian and Arab dynasties ruled them too briefly to implant sedentary culture deeply. Therefore the Maghrib has few cities compared to Syria, Egypt, the non-Arab Iraq, and Spain — regions where sedentary culture had millennia to crystallize.

Arab builders after Islam were also hampered by: (a) Bedouin unfamiliarity with crafts; (b) early Islamic restraint on ostentatious building (citing 'Umar's injunction to build no more than three houses and to avoid vying in construction); (c) short dynastic tenure before decline set in. By contrast, Persian, Coptic, Nabataean, and Roman dynasties had thousands of years during which crafts became embedded, producing monuments that survive to Ibn Khaldun's own day.

## Relation to the Broader Muqaddimah Framework

This concept is the urban instantiation of the cyclical theory developed in Chapters II–III:

- [[asabiyyah]] → royal authority → dynastic power (Chapters II–III)
- Royal authority → cities and sedentary civilization → luxury → loss of asabiyyah → dynastic decline (Chapter IV)
- Dynastic decline → urban decay → return toward Bedouin conditions → new cycle

The mechanism connecting the political and the urban is luxury (*naim*, *taraf*). Luxury is not simply moral corruption; it is a structural consequence of successful sedentary life that removes the selective pressure for group solidarity. The city is simultaneously the highest expression of human civilization and the mechanism by which that civilization destroys the military-political basis on which it depends.

## Historiography

Ibn Khaldun's urban theory was largely unknown to European political economy until the 19th century. Modern scholars have noted its anticipation of: (a) Malthusian population dynamics; (b) Adam Smith's labor theory of value and the pin-factory division of labor; (c) Simmel's sociology of the metropolis; (d) central-place theory (Christaller). The key scholarly debate is whether Ibn Khaldun's model is cyclical-determinist (cities always decay) or conditional (decay depends on geographic and demographic factors he identifies). The text supports the conditional reading: cities with good sites and fertile hinterlands can survive dynastic collapse; those without cannot.

The collection coverage for Ibn Khaldun's urban theory is strong through the Rosenthal translation; secondary scholarship in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina is thinner for this specific chapter than for Chapters II–III (asabiyyah and political theory).
