# Basileus and Big-Man Authority

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] Ch.6 charts Early Iron Age and early Archaic leadership using Homeric *basileis*, Linear B antecedents, and anthropological “big-man” / chieftain models — then the shift toward aristocratic collectives, institutional offices, and the return of one-man power as tyranny.

## Classical and Linear B baselines

Later tradition (Pausanias, Parian Marble, Hellanicus; Ephorus/Diodorus on Corinth; Aristotle) cast early rulers as hereditary kings (*basileis*). In the Classical period *basileus* meant (1) an annual magistrate (e.g. Athenian archon basileus), (2) non-Greek monarchs (esp. the Persian king), or (3) mythical Greek rulers.

*Basileus* descends from Linear B *qa-si-re-u*/*pa-si-re-u*, a fairly low-ranking local official (Pylos implies ~9–12 basileis), while the supreme monarch was *wa-na-ka*/(w)anax. Accepting early Archaic hereditary kingship would require *basileus* to climb from local administrator to hereditary monarch then shrink again to limited-tenure magistrate — “a rather unlikely sequence of events.”

## Homeric basileis are not modern kings

Hall’s three reasons:

1. **Relative term:** *basileutatos* / *basileuteros* — personal authority with gradations rather than formal absolute office.
2. **Plurality:** Ithaca’s many basileis; Scheria’s twelve plus Alcinous as thirteenth.
3. **Heredity not guaranteed:** Laertes alive; Telemachus powerless; suitors Antinous and Eurymakhos also basileis.

## Big-men and chieftains

Some historians liken Homeric basileis to Marshall Sahlins’ Melanesian **big-men**: authority personal/charismatic, achieved by persuasion, martial skill, rhetoric, and amassing/redistributing goods; support starts from household/kin then obligated “strays”; systems often collapse with the big-man’s death — contrasted with Polynesian **chiefs** whose power attaches to pre-existing office.

Hall’s synthesis: Homeric heroes achieve status by prowess, feasts, wealth, calculated generosity (*therapontes*); enjoy community-awarded *temenos*; lack full coercive power — yet scepter genealogy, Menelaus on god-reared basileis, and Hesiod’s Zeus-fostered basileis imply hereditary/chieftain features. Epics may show a ranked society transferring big-man authority into traditional office, or regional/typological variation ([[places/lefkandi|Lefkandi]] vs Athens burial display). Little evidence for a fully stratified society.

## Archaeology of “unstable” sites

Possible big-man residences: Lefkandi Toumba building as feasting-hall/residence demolished and buried under a tumulus on the big-man’s death (first half of 10th c.); Nikhoria Unit IV–1; Koukounaries apsidal building. Lefkandi identification is controversial; Koukounaries/Nikhoria may show longer continuity, so authority could have been more continuous even at “unstable” sites. Lefkandi’s wealth “hardly serves to refute the concept of a Dark Age” — it is exceptional, not typical.

## Not yet a true aristocracy (eighth century)

Politically, aristocracy requires wealth/labor divisions and formalized offices. Homeric basileis may resemble an aristocracy collectively but are too few within home communities. Archaeology: Eretria West Gate’s sixteen wealthy burials over a generation suit a powerful family not a class; Argos Tomb 45 is a unique basileus-like warrior grave among ~100 eighth-century graves — no aristocratic cemeteries. Eighth-century Greece is **leaders and followers**, not aristocrats and commoners (Hesiod juxtaposes *basileis* and *laoi*).

## Seventh-century shift to ascribed office

Early inscribed laws and named magistracies mark the shift: basileis still appear in Dracon and Chios law, but as appointed limited-tenure officials far outnumbered by *arkhontes*, *ephetai*, *prytaneis*, *dêmarkhoi*, *damiourgoi*, etc. “There is… now a clear emphasis on ascribed offices rather than achieved authority.” Rise of the state is more a **seventh-** than eighth-century feature. Competitive elite ethos can coexist with collective aristocratic consciousness (Panhellenic games; rotation; *esthloi*/*kakoi* language). Tyranny is the later return of one-man power from within aristocratic competition, not simple survival of Homeric kingship ([[concepts/greek-tyranny]]; [[actors/pheidon-of-argos]]).

## Related Greek social organization note

Hall finds little Dark Age kinship organization; Homeric *phylon*/*phratra* structured around locality; Hesiod counsels neighbors over kin — yet Classical *phylai*/*phratrai* use descent suffixes and Cleisthenic *phylai* are artificial late-sixth-century creations, so Greece shows a shift from residence to **imaginary kinship**, against evolutionist kinship→territory models.

## Emic / etic

- **Emic:** *basileus*, *wanax* (epic memory), *agathos*/*esthlos* status language.
- **Etic:** “big-man,” “chief,” “ranked vs stratified,” “achieved vs ascribed” — anthropological tools Hall applies with caveats.
