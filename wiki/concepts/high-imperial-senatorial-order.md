# The Senatorial Order under the High Empire (Provincialization and the *Cursus*)

How the **Senate** changed — and how it stayed the same — between Vespasian and Commodus: its
membership was **radically reprovincialized** while its forms were preserved, and the senatorial
**career** (*cursus honorum*) became the most regular it ever was, even as the body lost real
political initiative. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 5 (**Werner Eck**); the
developed form of the order analysed for the Julio-Claudians in [[senate-under-the-principate]].

## Forms preserved, reality acknowledged

Eck opens from Pliny's praise of Trajan as *unus ex nobis* ("one of us") set against his own
admission that "everything depends on the will of one man." The **official position of the Senate
did not change at all** after the Julio-Claudians: every contender of AD 69 had to win the Senate
as well as the army (the *Lex de Imperio Vespasiani*, surviving on bronze, was cast as a *senatus
consultum*), because each was himself a senator of decades' standing and none could conceive of a
political model in which the Senate was superfluous. Yet **Vespasian's censorship (AD 73/4)** —
the emperor himself, with Titus, as censor — showed power concentrating: the census had become
"solely a concern of the emperor," and Domitian's assumption of the title *censor perpetuus* made
the old censorship "no more than a sham," so that henceforth **all emperors raised *homines novi*
to the Senate whenever they wished**.

## Provincialization: a body remade in four or five generations

Eck's central theme is the **radical change in the Senate's regional composition** under cover of
unchanging appearance. Through *adlectio* (direct promotion into a rank) and ordinary recruitment,
the emperors restructured the order:

- Under **Nero** the Senate was still predominantly **Roman–Italian** (with southern Spain and
  Narbonensis emerging). Nero's destructive purges, the deaths of AD 69, and Vespasian's eastern
  base accelerated the influx of provincials: by Vespasian perhaps **a third of senators came
  from the provinces**.
- The novelty was the rise of families from the **Greek East** (Asia above all, then Achaea,
  Pontus-Bithynia), already Romanized, wealthy, and educated (the Second Sophistic). Patrons such
  as **Iulius Quadratus** of Pergamum drew up further easterners; by the end of the second century
  about **half of all non-Italian senators came from the Greek East**.
- **African** families (esp. Africa Proconsularis) broke through somewhat later, behind a patron
  like **Cornelius Fronto** of Cirta (Marcus' teacher). By Marcus and Commodus **most provinces
  had at least one senatorial family**, the conspicuous absentees being Egypt and (on ethnic/
  religious grounds) some marginal frontier provinces.

The total of active senatorial families never exceeded **about four hundred**. To bind these men
to the centre, **Trajan required new senators to invest a third of their fortune in Italian land**
(Marcus reduced it to a quarter) — a sign that for many provincials Rome was not attractive enough
in itself to eclipse their home towns, where their wealth and patronage remained rooted.

## The career: the most regular it ever was

The basic *cursus honorum* fixed by Augustus persisted, the new imperial posts slotted into the
republican magistracies. Eck stresses how **hierarchical and predictable** the senatorial career
became in the calm second century:

- After the praetorship a man held a **legionary legateship**, then a praetorian provincial
  governorship or the prefecture of the *aerarium Saturni*, then the consulship; consular
  governorships were **ranked by the number of legions** (Britain, Syria, the Pannonias and Moesia
  Inferior with three), capped by the prestige proconsulates of **Africa and Asia**.
- Variation was driven above all by **social origin**: patricians and consuls' sons reached the
  consulship years faster (patricians 2–3 years after the praetorship), but for that reason rarely
  commanded the great military provinces.
- **Patronage** (the *amici*, praetorian prefects, *principes officiorum*, even imperial women and
  freedmen, and the recommendations Pliny published) was effective chiefly at the start of a career
  and never overrode the de facto rules — which is why the careers are so regular. The number of
  senators in office rose from c. 100 under Vespasian to over 160 under Marcus; "nearly a third of
  all active senators" were always on official business, **paid** from the *aerarium Saturni* or by
  the emperor, and therefore felt **dependent on him**.
- Military posts of real power — legionary commands up to consular governorships — remained almost
  entirely **senatorial** (Egypt the exception); the number of imperial provinces with senatorial
  legates rose sharply (Judaea, Cappadocia, Dacia, Arabia, Thrace, Noricum, Raetia upgraded), while
  procuratorial governorships were halved. Even as the equestrian administration grew beside it,
  "there is no indication of any real reduction in senatorial competence."

## The Senate as part of the emperor's system

The Senate still legislated (the jurists increasingly cite the emperor's **orationes** rather than
the resulting *senatus consulta*, especially from Hadrian's long absences), tried its own erring
provincial governors, and conducted real if routine business — occasionally even acting without
the emperor's visible involvement (market grants, points of civil law bearing proposers' names).
But Eck's verdict is that "**towards the end of the second century there was no longer any real
political activity in the Senate as a whole**." The deep trauma of Domitian's tyranny, the de facto
superiority of imperial power, the emperors' frequent absences (Trajan, Hadrian, Marcus away for
years), and the entrusting of so much power to men outside the Senate and answerable only to the
emperor, meant that — whatever the high *esteem* it retained — the Senate "was as much a part of
the whole system organized and run by the emperor" as the *ordo equester* and the Palatine
officials. The theoretical possibility of the Senate as the *consilium principis par excellence*
was never realized.

## Significance and contest

Eck replaces the picture of a Senate either crushed or autonomous with one of a body whose **forms
were scrupulously preserved while its membership was transformed and its political will atrophied**
— bound to the emperor by appointment, pay, and the regularity of the career he controlled. The
exact share of provincial senators in each reign, the degree of competition for places, and how
far the second-century *cursus* was genuinely "regular" versus patronage-driven remain matters of
prosopographic debate.
