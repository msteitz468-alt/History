## Definition and Scope

Al-insān al-kāmil, "the Perfect Man," is the central anthropological and cosmological concept of mature Sufi theosophy. It designates the human being who has realized the full range of divine qualities in themselves, becoming the microcosmic mirror of God. The concept answers the question of why God created the world and what role humanity occupies within it.

The concept is both emic (actively used by Sufi thinkers as a spiritual category) and etic (applied by scholars to organize Sufi anthropology). Its meaning shifts between three registers: (1) a cosmological archetype — the primordial spirit through which God becomes conscious of Himself; (2) a Christological-style figure — the Prophet Muhammad as the historical instantiation of this archetype; and (3) a spiritual ideal — the goal toward which every Sufi mystic strives on the Path.

## Koranic and Hadith Foundations

Schimmel grounds the concept firmly in the Koran and Prophetic tradition. Key textual pillars cited in her treatment:

- **"He created Adam in His image" (ʿalā ṣūratihi)**: Adam is the prototype of the Perfect Man, who uniquely reflects divine qualities.
- **"He taught Adam the names" (Sūra 2:31)**: Knowledge of the divine names grants Adam — and potentially every human — mastery over creation and proximity to God.
- **"And we will show them Our signs in the horizons and in themselves" (Sūra 41:53)**: The Sufis read this as a command to seek God within the heart — the human self as locus of divine self-disclosure.
- **"Man ʿarafa nafsahu faqad ʿarafa rabbahu" (ḥadīth)**: "Who knows himself knows his Lord" — the mystical inward turn grounding the Perfect Man doctrine.
- **ḥadīth qudsī "kuntu kanzan makhfiyan"**: "I was a hidden treasure and wanted to be known, so I created the world." God's self-disclosure finds its peak expression in the Perfect Man.

## The Muhammadan Light (Nūr Muḥammadī)

The key development linking Perfect Man doctrine to prophetology is the concept of the **nūr muḥammadī**, the Muhammadan Light. Schimmel traces this to Muqātil (8th c.) and Sahl at-Tustarī (d. 896), but it reaches systematic form with Ḥallāj and then Ibn ʿArabī:

> "The first thing God created was my spirit." / "I was a prophet when Adam was still between clay and water." (traditions attributed to Muhammad)

The Prophet is both cause and goal of creation. A ḥadīth qudsī states: *laulāka mā khalaqtuʾl-aflāka* — "if thou hadst not been, I would not have created the heavens." The Muhammadan Light is the primordial archetype from which all creation proceeds:

> "The origin of the soul is the absolute light, nothing else, / That means it was the light of Muhammad, nothing else." (ʿAṭṭār, Ushturnāme)

In Ibn ʿArabī's systematization, this archetype is expressed as the **ḥaqīqa muḥammadiyya**, the "Muhammadan Reality" — a mode of the uncreated divine spirit through which God becomes conscious of Himself in creation.

## Ibn ʿArabī's Systematic Formulation

Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240) gave the concept its most influential formulation. In his schema:

- God created a microcosmic being — the Perfect Man — through whom His consciousness is manifested to Himself.
- The Perfect Man is "the spirit in which all things have their origin."
- Muhammad's spirit corresponds to the heavenly Pen; his soul to the Well-preserved Tablet; Isrāfīl is created from his heart; Gabriel corresponds to the First Intelligence.
- The **amr** (divine command), roughly equivalent to the Logos in Greek philosophy, is equated with the ḥaqīqa muḥammadiyya.

Schimmel notes: "God indeed remains the creator of the world, but He is no longer in any direct sense its ruler. He is absolutely transcendent… That function is assigned to One by whose command the spheres are moved, i.e., to the muṭāʿ [the obeyed one]… the archetypal Spirit of Muhammad, the Heavenly Man created in the image of God and regarded as a Cosmic Power on whom depends the order and preservation of the Universe." (citing Nicholson)

## Jīlī's Development

ʿAbd al-Karīm al-Jīlī (d. after 1408) systematized the doctrine into a comprehensive theosophy. In his work, the Perfect Man becomes the locus of all divine self-manifestation across every prophetic figure. The sheikh who has been annihilated in the Prophet — achieving **fanā fīʾr-rasūl** — becomes, united with the ḥaqīqa muḥammadiyya, the Perfect Man himself and thus a direct channel of divine guidance for his disciples.

## Man as Microcosm

A closely related strand is the microcosm doctrine. Najmuddīn Kubrā (d. 1220) explicitly holds that man "contains everything that exists in the macrocosmos" and that "man can be qualified with God's qualities." Rūmī calls man "the astrolabe of the qualities of highness" (M 6:3138–43). A Bektashi poet encapsulates it:

> "This universe is a tree — man became its fruit. / That which was intended is the fruit — do not think it is the tree!" (BO 118)

## The Numerical-Alphabetic Dimension

Later Sufis elaborated the connection between Adam's form and Muhammad's name through letter mysticism. One 13th-century Egyptian mystic proposed that Adam's bodily form — head, hands, middle, lower part — spells the consonants M-Ḥ-M-D in old Arabic script. The ḥadīth qudsī *anā Aḥmad bilā mīm* ("I am Aḥmad without the letter m," i.e., Aḥad, "One") condenses the idea: only the letter *m* — associated with creatureliness, limitation, and death — separates the Prophet from divine Unity.

## The Quṭb as Living Instantiation

The **quṭb** ("axis, pole") — the highest ranking saint in the Sufi saintly hierarchy — is understood as the nearest living approximation to the Perfect Man in any given era. Rūmī said: "He who does not know the true sheikh — i.e., the Perfect Man and quṭb of his time — is a kāfir" (M 2:3325). The quṭb rests in tranquillity at the center of the spiritual cosmos; all other saints revolve around him.

## Spiritual Path Implications

The Perfect Man concept has direct consequences for the mystical path:

- The mystic must first achieve **fanā fīʾsh-shaykh** (annihilation in the master), then **fanā fīʾr-rasūl** (annihilation in the Prophet), before approaching **fanā fī Allāh**.
- The highest a saint can reach corresponds spiritually to what the Prophet performed bodily in the miʿrāj.
- The sheikh, in his highest manifestation, becomes a mirror of the Prophet — "so completely purified that all the virtues of the Prophet are visible in him as in a mirror."

## Contested Dimensions

The doctrine generated significant controversy:

- Orthodox critics feared that inflating the human to cosmic status crossed into shirk (associating partners with God) or pantheism.
- Aḥmad Sirhindī (d. 1624), the great Naqshbandī reformer, critiqued Ibn ʿArabī's waḥdat al-wujūd on grounds related to this doctrine.
- ʿAlāʾuddaula Simnānī (d. 1336) questioned the truth of Ibn ʿArabī's concept while remaining within the broader framework.
- Modern Muslim reformers (including Iqbal) engaged the concept critically, seeing it as both resource and danger for Islamic renewal.

## Historiography

Schimmel treats the Perfect Man doctrine as central to the mature phase of Sufism from the 13th century onward, distinguishing it carefully from earlier, simpler Sufi anthropology. The doctrine's relationship to Neoplatonism and Hellenistic philosophy (active intellect, Logos) is acknowledged. Western scholarship has debated whether the concept represents an Islamicization of Gnostic/Hermetic materials or an organically Islamic development from Koranic anthropology.

Key scholars: Reynold A. Nicholson (*The Idea of Personality in Sufism*, 1921), Tor Andrae (*Die person Muhammads*, 1918), Henry Corbin (on celestial anthropology), Toshihiko Izutsu (on metaphysical structure).
