# The Senatus Consultum Ultimum

The Senate's emergency decree authorising magistrates to "see that the Republic
takes no harm" (*videant consules ne quid res publica detrimenti capiat*) — the
constitutional instrument by which the *optimates* met *popularis* threats with
force. Analysed in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 3 (Lintott).

## Origin and meaning

First passed in **121 BCE** against **[[gaius-gracchus|Gaius Gracchus]]** and Fulvius
Flaccus, when the consul **L. Opimius** used it to justify raising an armed force,
killing them, and executing their captured supporters without trial. The decree did
not in itself confer legal power — constitutionally the **magistrate** remained
responsible for any illegal act — but **Opimius' acquittal** (prosecuted by the
tribune Decius for violating Gaius' own *lex Sempronia* protecting citizens from
trial-less execution) established the *SCU* as an **institution**: the Senate's
trust became a magistrate's defence.

## Function and critique

The *SCU* sat in deep tension with ***provocatio*** — the citizen's protection against
summary execution. It could not be reconciled with that right; in practice it was a
**carte blanche** whose victims were, almost without exception, those who had
challenged the Senate's authority through the assemblies. Lintott stresses it was thus
an **instrument of class politics**: invoked against Gaius Gracchus (121), Saturninus
and Glaucia (100), the Catilinarians (63), and Caesar's opponents' targets — always
against *populares*. Its legality was never settled; Caesar's bitter phrase gives it
its modern name, and Cicero's execution of the Catilinarians under its cover became
the great test case (CAH IX Ch. 9).
