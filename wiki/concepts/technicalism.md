## Definition

**Technicalism** (also: **technicalization**, the **technicalistic spirit**) is Hodgson's term for the core dynamic of the [[great-western-transmutation|Great Western Transmutation]]. He defines it as "a condition of calculative (and hence innovative) technical specialization, in which the several specialties are interdependent on a large enough scale to determine patterns of expectation in the key sectors of a society."

Hodgson deliberately prefers "technicalism" over "industrialization" because:

- Industrialization names only the economic facet (power-mechanized industry), whereas technicalization encompassed equally decisive transformations in science, social organization, government, and moral culture.
- A country like Denmark, primarily agricultural but thoroughly modernized, illustrates the failure of "industrialization" as a marker: its agriculture was technicalized even if it lacked heavy industry.
- The technicalistic process in science and social organization was "equally decisive" with that in economic production.

## The Technicalistic Spirit

Central to the concept is a psychological dimension: "the expectation of impersonal efficiency through technical precision." Under technicalism, all other considerations — "aesthetic, traditional, interpersonal" — were increasingly subordinated to this standard. This produced specific institutional correlates:

- **Institutionalized innovation**: patent offices replacing family trade secrets; scientific journals aimed at discovering new knowledge rather than preserving old; legislatures conceived explicitly as law-changing bodies (a conception Hodgson notes had no real equivalent in agrarianate political theory, including the Ottoman *qânûn* system).
- **Mass participant society**: technicalism required a broad base of skilled labour and literate consumers; the lower classes gradually had to share in the refinements of urban life, producing political mass movements as a structural byproduct.
- **Extended state power**: the state in technicalized societies intervened in daily life "in unprecedented detail and with inescapable efficiency," replacing the intermediate corporate bodies of agrarianate society with direct state-to-individual relationships.
- **Individual isolation and cultivation**: technicalism required individuals independent of guild rules, tribal loyalties, and communal religious convention — producing simultaneously an ethic of personal integrity and teamwork, and a tendency toward anonymity and "impersonalization."

## Technicalism vs. Rationality

Hodgson warns explicitly against identifying technicalism with human rationality in any broad sense: "To subordinate all considerations of ethics or beauty or human commitments to maximizing technical efficiency, however successfully, is quite likely to prove an irrational nightmare." The technicalistic spirit is a selective cultivation of calculative efficiency for specialized material ends — not a general emancipation of human reason.

He is equally critical of the standard "traditional/rational" dichotomy in modernization theory (Weber, Parsons). All societies are traditional in operating through cultural traditions; all are rational in that their institutions survive only by being pragmatically functional. What the Transmutation introduced was not rationality as such but a particular form of calculative investment in specialized technical ends.

## Scale and Irreversibility

The decisive feature of technicalism was not isolated technical innovation (which had precedents in many agrarian societies) but the achievement of "critical mass" — a point at which "the technical efficiency of any particular activity was increased by its use of the fruits of other specialties, and it in turn served to increase their efficiency." Once this nexus of interdependent specializations reached sufficient breadth, it became socially irreversible: "no social process or historical event originating outside the process could reverse it or seriously slow it down."

Hodgson dates this irreversibility to c. 1600 in northwest Europe, distinguishing it from the Renaissance florescence (which remained within agrarianate presuppositions) and from its culmination in the Industrial Revolution (c. 1785). He compares the process to geometric progression: "each new round of inventions, once exploited, cleared the way for yet another."

## Moral Dimensions

Hodgson notes that technicalism carried a "moral discipline of its own." The bourgeois morality associated with the British Industrial Revolution — going "far towards doing away with not only the grosser forms of dissipation and display in personal life, but even with graft and bribery in politics" — was structurally related to the technicalistic demand for impersonal, predictable procedure. The same spirit underlay the Enlightenment's "gentling of manners" and the mitigation of public cruelty.

He is honest about the dehumanizing implications: "there was... something of a dehumanizing implication in technicalism." By the time of Goethe — "the last of the universal men" — no individual could follow in detail the whole technical process of which his work formed a fragment. Ibn Khaldun or Leonardo, Hodgson suggests, could still have done the equivalent in their time.

## Relations to Other Concepts

- **[[great-western-transmutation]]**: Technicalism is the core dynamic of the Transmutation, not identical with it. The Transmutation also included moral and aesthetic changes less directly tied to technicalistic specialization.
- **[[industrialization]]**: A subset of technicalism; the culmination of the economic strand, achieved c. 1785 with the joining of specialized machines to steam power. Hodgson treats it as a token of technicalization, not its essence.
- **[[gunpowder-empires]]**: The Islamic gunpowder empires' difficulties in the Technical Age stem partly from structural features of technicalism's irruptive force on non-technicalized societies.
- **[[complementary-dependence]]**: The typical form of integration of non-technicalized economies into the world order created by technicalism.
