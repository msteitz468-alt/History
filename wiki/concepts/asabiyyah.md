## Definition

*Asabiyyah* (Arabic: عصبية) is the central concept in [[ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun's]] science of history and civilization — denoting the group solidarity, party spirit, or esprit de corps that binds a social group and enables collective political action. The Glossary in Hodgson's *Venture of Islam* Vol. 2 defines it as "the spirit of tribal solidarity that enables a relatively small number of hardened pastoralists to conquer a larger number of city dwellers who have grown effete."

Ibn Khaldun developed the concept in his *Muqaddimah* (c. 1377) as the key variable explaining why relatively small pastoral groups repeatedly overthrew larger, wealthier, more culturally sophisticated urban states — and why those same groups then built urban states of their own, only to see their asabiyyah erode and their states succumb in turn to fresh pastoral challengers.

## The Cyclical Theory

Ibn Khaldun's theory of historical cycles is built on the rise and fall of asabiyyah:

1. **Formation**: A pastoral or rural group develops strong mutual solidarity through shared hardship, danger, and material conditions. This asabiyyah enables them to organize and fight as a unit against numerically superior settled populations.

2. **Conquest**: A group with strong asabiyyah conquers an urban or agrarian area. The resources of the many can now be concentrated by the dominant group, generating economic specialization and the arts of civilization.

3. **Erosion**: In prosperity, asabiyyah weakens. Members of the ruling group "learn to command by force of money more than by force of personal vigour, and in prosperity... become more jealous of each other than mutually bound in alertness against their disarmed subjects." The ruler relies increasingly on mercenaries rather than on the now-luxurious group members.

4. **Fiscal collapse**: Increased taxes to support mercenaries sap the productive arts; the tax base declines; military capacity diminishes.

5. **Conquest**: The weakened state becomes "easy prey to the next fresh rural, especially pastoral, group with strong solidarity."

Hodgson notes that Ibn Khaldun himself recognized regional variation: "the effects of such a cycle were far more severe in lands like the Maghrib than in a long and deeply cultural land like Egypt."

## Emic vs. Etic Status

Asabiyyah is an emic concept — coined by Ibn Khaldun from his observation of Maghribi political history — that has been taken up by historians as an etic analytical tool. Its status is unusual: it was developed within the Falsafah (philosophical) tradition as a contribution to a science of civilization, not as popular vocabulary.

Within Ibn Khaldun's system, asabiyyah is not a mystical ethnic property but a product of "quite intelligible facts of common social interests." He demonstrates this by showing how Ibn Tumart — the Almohad founder — built his movement on Berber tribal solidarity even while projecting a claim of 'Alid genealogical legitimacy: the solidarity was with the tribe because "his stock became involved with theirs and... assumed their ties of kinship."

## Relationship to Prophetic Power

Ibn Khaldun's treatment of the prophetically founded state fits within the asabiyyah framework. Prophetic imagination can add to the imaginative appeal of a group's solidarity; it is an adjunct to natural state formation, not a replacement for it. A prophet or reformer can only succeed when the times — meaning the current phase of the asabiyyah cycle — are favorable. "Not only the Philosophical but even the would-be Shari reformer must await the right times and seasons and depend on ecological and historical conditions to favour him."

This naturalistic integration of prophecy within the cycle was Ibn Khaldun's most philosophically daring move — transforming revelation itself into a historical phenomenon subject to natural law.

## Translation Problems

The standard English translation by Franz Rosenthal (1958) is, according to Hodgson, "quite unusable" for the general philosophical framework of the *Muqaddimah*. Hodgson does not specifically comment on the translation of *asabiyyah* itself in the passages available, but notes that the Rosenthal translation's general tendency to "distort sentence after sentence through incomprehension" of technical terms means that any reader relying solely on it will miss the precision of Ibn Khaldun's usage.

Hodgson uses "group solidarity or party spirit" as his rendering, emphasizing the social-structural rather than the affective or ethnic dimensions.

## Analytical Value for Islamic History

Hodgson commends asabiyyah as illuminating "very effectively the political state of Islamdom in the Middle Periods, which is the time he [Ibn Khaldun] knew best." However, he notes a limitation: Ibn Khaldun "was unable to appreciate the role that civil political forces could play in better circumstances, and did play in both earlier and later Islamic times." The concept is most powerful for explaining the dynamics of the military-patronage states of the Later Middle Period and least applicable to periods of more stable civil administration.
