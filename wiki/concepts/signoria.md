# The Signoria (the Rise of the Signori)

The **signoria** was the personal, hereditary **lordship** that displaced collective communal
government across the city-states of northern Italy from the mid-thirteenth century — the
political form from which the principalities of Renaissance Italy (Visconti, Este, Gonzaga,
Scaligeri) would grow. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Trevor Dean, Ch. 15c) reframes the
*signori* against the traditional image of "despots" and "tyrants."

## The "Despot" Problem

Dean argues that the English habit of calling the signori "despots" — set by the Victorian
J.A. Symonds — "is really displaced." The signori called themselves **lords, governors, rectors,
captains**; "tyrant" was their critics' word, drawn from Aristotle's distinction between rulers
who govern for the common good and those who govern for themselves. But, as the jurist
**Bartolus of Sassoferrato** admitted, "rarely may any government be found... in which there is
not something of tyranny" — the difference being only one of degree. The communal past should
**not** be idealised: most communes in the first half of the century were "weak, divided and
unable to command adequate political consent," their structures "rudimentary, semi-private and
provisional."

## From Commune to Lordship

As **[[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Frederick II's]]** attempt to reimpose imperial authority
failed, "monarchical power was recreated at the local level" — first in his lieutenants, then in
local faction-chiefs. The most notorious early figure was **Ezzelino da Romano** (lord of Verona,
Vicenza and Padua, d. 1259), demonised by the papal crusade against him as a monster of cruelty —
though Dean finds "more sober evidence" of constitutional respect and popular support. Later came
Uberto da Pallavicino, and then the dynastic lords: the **della Torre** and **Visconti** in Milan,
the **Este** in Ferrara, the **della Scala** (Scaligeri) in Verona, the **Bonacolsi** in Mantua,
the **da Camino** in Treviso.

## The Popolo Debate

Dean rejects the older thesis that the signori arose from a **class struggle** in which the
**popolo** (artisans and merchants organised in guilds and neighbourhoods, with their own captain
and statutes) overthrew the aristocracy. Class tensions were real, but "almost everywhere the
popolo was divided, almost everywhere led by aristocrats." Magnates led and joined the popolo to
mobilise it against rival factions; anti-magnate legislation was often "the work of magnate
partisans... directed against their rivals." The signori arose from the **logic of faction**, not
a popular revolution.

## What the Signori Were

Most signori, Dean insists, were **barons** — their power "legitimised not so much by delegation
or constitutional transfer, but by possession and long use," resting on castles, vassals, and a
shared **chivalric culture** of courts, tournaments, liberality and (its reverse) **vendetta**.
They drew on many sources of authority: pacifying factions, holding communal office, building
castles and palaces, patronising the [[mendicant-orders|friars]] (and exploiting the Church),
maintaining armed guards, projecting an image of justice and liberality — and, above all,
inspiring **fear**, "silencing enemies by whatever means necessary." Their formal grants of
*arbitrium* (free discretionary power) "undid the constitution of the commune": a witness said
the captain "ruled as a man who could do what he wanted." Yet, as Bartolus and Machiavelli both
implied, fear alone could not sustain rule; the durable lords (Matteo Visconti) also won "love."

## Significance

The rise of the signori is the central political transformation of thirteenth-century communal
Italy — the replacement of fragile collective republics by personal dynastic lordship. Dean's
revision warns against reading it "as a degradation from an ideal, free and democratic past to a
period of dark and bloody oppression": given the communes' chronic disorder, sharp instruments
were needed — *gatta inguantata mal piglia sorci*, "a cat with gloves on can hardly catch mice."
The signoria is the matrix of the Renaissance principality.

## The Fourteenth-Century Consolidation (NCMH VI)

[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (John Law, Ch. 13a) shows the *signorie* hardening into **territorial
states** in the fourteenth century. **Henry VII of Luxemburg's** Italian expedition (1310–1313)
revealed that northern Italy "had no recent tradition of centralised, let alone imperial rule" — "a
mosaic of lordships (*signorie*) and communes" still split by the [[guelphs-and-ghibellines|Guelf–Ghibelline]]
rivalry — and his failure cleared the way for the dynastic lords to expand. The greatest was the
**[[visconti-of-milan|Visconti of Milan]]**, who built the largest signorial state in Lombardy
(briefly seeming poised to dominate the whole north under Giangaleazzo, d. 1402); the **della Scala**
(Verona), **Este** (Ferrara), **Gonzaga** (Mantua), and **Carrara** (Padua) ruled comparable
principalities. By 1400 the personal, often informal lordships of Ezzelino's generation had become
**hereditary regional states** with courts, bureaucracies, and (in the Visconti case) imperial
titles — the political form from which the Renaissance principalities directly grew.
