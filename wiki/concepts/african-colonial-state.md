# The African Colonial State

## Definition and Scope

The African colonial state refers to the administrative, military, legal, and economic apparatus erected by seven European powers — Britain, France, Germany, Belgium (via King Leopold's private initiative), Portugal, Spain, and Italy — in African territories from the late nineteenth century through decolonization in the 1950s–1960s. It is simultaneously a historical institution and a contested analytical concept.

As a Weberian entity, colonial states claimed monopolies of legitimate violence within bounded territories. They were economic as well as political enterprises committed to the extraction of natural resources, the development of trade, and the control of labour. Crawford Young's formulation of the colonial state as *bula matari* ("breaker of rocks") — the brute force of the Congo Free State — remains influential, though historians now see the exertion of colonial power as "somewhat more erratic and uneven."

## Creation of Colonial States

The so-called Scramble for Africa was precipitated by France's occupation of Tunisia (1881) and Britain's occupation of Egypt (1882). The Berlin Conference of 1884–5 regulated the free-for-all by requiring "effective occupation" — the development of administrative infrastructures — to confirm territorial claims. This provision transformed European imperialism: henceforth, powers insisted on centralised rule over colonies and the right to extract taxes.

European technologies enabled this new imperialism: quinine (allowing European survival in the tropics), rapid-firing rifles and machine guns, steamships, the Suez Canal, railways, and cameras and typewriters for surveillance and record-keeping. Italy introduced aeroplanes as a colonial weapon in Libya in 1911.

**Anomalous cases**: Liberia (colonised by African Americans from the 1820s, independent 1847); Ethiopia (retained independence through diplomatic balancing, though conquered by Italy 1935–41); the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan (a "condominium" of Britain and Egypt from 1898); South Africa (a tense union of British- and Afrikaner-dominated regions from 1910).

**Arbitrary borders**: the borders that emerged from the Scramble often reflected prior claims and the trading of diplomatic favours rather than physical or cultural topographies. Germany exchanged two pieces of Cameroon for France's recognition of Morocco (1911). Britain secured parts of northern Nigeria from France in return for recognising French fishing rights off Newfoundland.

## Administrative Philosophies

### British Indirect Rule

Frederick Lugard (1858–1945) developed "indirect rule" — identifying and cultivating local chiefs and hereditary rulers as intermediaries in colonial governance — in Nigeria and theorised it in *The Dual Mandate in British Tropical Africa* (1922). Indirect rule rested on three premises: Britain had a "dual mandate" to extract wealth and help "backward peoples" to progress; it was possible to preserve "authentic" and "traditional" local cultures; and "detribalized" Africans were dangerous. Lugard described Africans as "primitives" and "child races of the world."

Indirect rule became British pan-African policy, though applied differently region to region. In practice, the "traditional" authorities it preserved were often invented or assembled from parts: "British rulers did not merely preserve African chiefdoms and customs; in some cases they invented them."

### French Mission Civilisatrice

France's colonial project rested on the *mission civilisatrice* — propagating French culture and the Enlightenment values of rationality and liberty. French officials were less sentimental about "tradition" and more willing to eliminate chiefs who got in their way. They promoted French as a consistent lingua franca and recognised a tiny number of educated Africans (*évolués*) as quasi-citizens. In 1936, only 2,000 out of some 14 million French West Africans held évolué status. French colonial law in practice amounted to "rule by decree, enacted in often arbitrary and sometimes spectacular punishments."

### Other Powers

Germany hoped that colonial economic success would serve as a "release valve for rising social pressures" at home (Hans-Ulrich Wehler). German colonialism subcontracted colonial rule to companies and, at least in East Africa before 1897, military officers fought dozens of campaigns without reporting them to Berlin — achieving "the militarization of colonial policy."

Belgium via Leopold II operated the Congo Free State as a personal fiefdom from 1885–1908, with concessionary companies inflicting "widespread and systematic violence" in the pursuit of rubber extraction. International exposure of Congo atrocities produced the Congo Reform Association and the handing of the territory to the Belgian state in 1908.

## Theory vs. Practice

A central theme in the historiography: colonial states rarely made the profits hoped for, their administrations were far less coordinated than official discourse suggested, and their power was more erratic and contingent than the "breaker of rocks" image implies.

**The thin white line**: colonial officials were extraordinarily sparse on the ground. A. H. M. Kirk-Greene noted that British rule amounted to "a great confidence trick, a huge game of white man's bluff." Power was "filtered through" multiple centres — not only London but Cape Town, Cairo, Algiers, Brazzaville, and Dakar.

**African agency**: many more kinds of people shaped colonial states than was once recognised — African translators, schoolteachers, tax collectors, clerks, lawyers, and court assessors exercised considerable influence. Ousmane Fall, officially a district interpreter in French Guinea around 1900, had "designed and supervised an elaborate colonial 'justice' system that employed four other Africans who traveled through the district, hearing cases, and passing down judgments."

**Collaborators and resisters**: Ronald Robinson's 1972 "sketch for a theory of collaboration" argued that British colonial rule functioned because local collaborators were willing to work with and help maintain colonial orders. But the simple resistance/collaboration binary has been abandoned: Africans "pursued their own agendas" within "autonomous spaces" created by the fragmented nature of colonial power (Parker and Rathbone, 2007).

## Control of Labour

Collecting taxes and controlling labour were paramount colonial concerns. Colonial states imposed compulsory labour ranging from twelve days a year in French colonies to forty hours a week in the Congo Free State (1903–8). Most British colonies ended forced labour in the 1920s; Portuguese and Liberian colonies retained it until the early 1960s. Cash-tax demands compelled Africans to seek wage employment, driving large-scale labour migrations and urbanisation.

The mobilisation of labour required coercion: "recruits on their way to the mines were at times linked with ropes around their necks" (Belgian Congo, post-1905). Labour bureaus, private militias, and pass laws extended coercive control. In Southern Rhodesia, South Africa, and Kenya, laws regulating labour favoured employers and imposed imprisonment for violations.

## Late Colonialism and the Developmental State

From the 1930s–1960s, colonial regimes faced a "crisis of confidence" driven by the Great Depression, the Second World War, accelerating urbanisation, labour unrest, and rising African expectations. Late colonialism's response was the "developmental state" — investing in economic growth, social welfare, and constitutional concessions.

Examples like the "Zande Scheme" (1946, south-western Sudan) — resettling 60,000 Zande-speaking people in elongated villages to facilitate palm oil and cotton production — illustrate the rushed and haphazard nature of late colonial development projects. Colonial bureaucracies also expanded their recruitment of African petty employees, making them increasingly important — and ambitious — actors.

Between 1945 and 1958, France extended the electoral franchise through "a dizzying series" of votes in "four referenda, two constitutions, three National Assemblies, and three territorial assemblies."

## Postcolonial Legacy

Central debate: to what extent did independence merely disguise a process of continuity from autocratic colony to autocratic "postcolony" (Mahmood Mamdani, Achille Mbembe)? Colonial states bequeathed their successors an apparatus of government departments, military structures, and bureaucratic methods — and, critics argue, a governing culture of ruthless tyranny and unchecked power.

Colonial states (along with Christian missions) also passed the baton of development and social welfare to multinational and international philanthropic agencies, anticipating the roles NGOs would play in late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century Africa.

## Historiography

Early debate: Adu Boahen (1990) vs. Roland Oliver and J. D. Fage (1990) on "ruthless exploitation" versus "moral obligation." Current scholarship rejects both poles in favour of a more granular account emphasising the diffuse, contradictory, and locally contingent nature of colonial power.

Key frameworks:
- Lugard's own writings as both policy document and ideological text
- Mamdani's *Citizen and Subject* (1996) on the bifurcated state (citizens vs. subjects) and decentralised despotism
- Frederick Cooper's colonial labour studies
- Crawford Young's comparative *The African Colonial State* (1994)
- Postcolonial and gender approaches expanding the range of actors recognised as shaping colonial states
