# The History of Sexuality (and the Constructionist Debate)

The **history of sexuality** is the field that studies how human societies have organized,
understood, regulated, and experienced sex, desire, and the body across time and place. Its
defining premise — and central controversy — is that **"sexuality" is not a timeless natural
given but a historically and culturally constructed category**, so that the modern Western
framework (with its identities such as "heterosexual" and "homosexual") cannot be projected
unchanged onto the past. From [[cambridge-world-history-of-sexualities-v1|*The Cambridge
World History of Sexualities*, Vol. 1]].

## Emergence of the field

The field grew in the 1970s–80s out of three currents: **women's history** (and feminism —
Beauvoir's *The Second Sex*, 1949; Joan Scott's "Gender: A Useful Category of Historical
Analysis," 1986), **gay and lesbian (later LGBTQ+) history**, and the **psychoanalytic**
tradition of **Sigmund Freud**, who made sexuality central to the self. But its decisive
catalyst was **Michel Foucault**.

## Foucault's intervention

Foucault's *History of Sexuality, Vol. 1: An Introduction* (1976) reframed the whole subject:

- **Against the "repressive hypothesis."** Where Marxists and Freudians stressed how
  modern society *repressed* sex, Foucault stressed the opposite — the modern West's
  obsessive *incitement to discourse* about sex (in confession, medicine, psychoanalysis,
  law). Silence and the compulsion to speak are "two sides of the same coin."
- **Power is productive, not just prohibitive.** Power does not merely forbid; it *produces*
  knowledge, identities, and desires. Sexuality is a domain where power operates by
  constituting categories of people.
- **Sexuality as *scientia sexualis*.** Since the 17th century the West has treated sex as
  a privileged **pathway to truth** about the self, generating a "science of sexuality"
  that multiplied "perversions" as objects of knowledge.
- **"Sexuality" is a modern deployment.** Foucault inverts common sense: "sex" is not the
  natural bedrock on which "sexuality" is built; rather **"sexuality" is the historical
  apparatus that produces the very idea of "sex."** Famously, he dated the **"homosexual"
  as a *species*/type of person** (as opposed to sodomy as an *act* anyone might commit) to
  c. 1870 — a key instance of the **act-vs-identity** distinction.

## Constructionism vs. essentialism

The field's organizing debate is between **essentialism** (sexual orientations and desires
are stable, natural, trans-historical realities) and **social constructionism** (sexual
categories and identities are produced by particular cultures and periods). The
constructionist position — dominant since Foucault — holds that applying modern labels
("gay," "lesbian," "heterosexual") to historical figures distorts their experience: classicists
(Halperin, Winkler, Zeitlin, *Before Sexuality*) argue that ancient Greek erotic life was
organized by **active/passive and status**, not by the gender of one's partner. **Queer
theory** (Teresa de Lauretis, Judith Butler) radicalized this: Butler's **performativity**
treats all gender and sexual identities as enacted, "scripted, learned, ad-libbed, and
renewed with each staging," rather than expressing a prior essence. Critics counter that
strong constructionism risks erasing real continuities and the lived reality of same-sex
desire across time.

## Methodological stakes

The constructionist insight reshapes practice: historians read past figures — e.g. Hatshepsut,
the cross-dressing conquistador Catalina de Erauso, or the 14th-century London sex worker
Eleanor Rykener (born John) — without assuming modern categories, attentive to how each
society drew its own lines around bodies, acts, marriage, and identity. It makes the
**history of sexuality** a test case for the general problem of **anachronism** in history,
and ties it closely to [[gender-history]].
