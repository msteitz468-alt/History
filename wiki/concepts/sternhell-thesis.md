# The Sternhell Thesis

Zeev Sternhell's interpretation of [[generic-fascism|fascism]], stated
programmatically in [[sternhell-birth-fascist-ideology-1994|*The Birth of
Fascist Ideology*]] (with Mario Sznajder and Maia Asheri).

## Core claims

1. **Fascism was a cultural phenomenon before it was a political force.**
   "The cultural revolt preceded the political": fascism grew out of the
   revolt against the Enlightenment and the French Revolution that swept
   Europe at the fin de siècle. "The crystallization of ideology preceded
   the buildup of political power."
2. **Fascism is a coherent ideology**, "not less intellectually
   self-sufficient than socialism or liberalism" — against Croce's
   "parenthesis"/moral-infection reading, Nolte's "shadow of Marxism,"
   Linz's anti-liberalism reduction, and A. James Gregor's
   "variety of Marxism" claim.
3. **The synthesis**: "the Fascist ideology represented a synthesis of
   organic nationalism with the antimaterialist revision of Marxism" — a
   *revision* of Marxism (via [[sorel-georges|Sorel]]), emphatically "not a
   variety of Marxism or a consequence of Marxism." Its two pillars are
   organic/tribal nationalism (Barrès, Maurras, [[corradini-enrico|Corradini]])
   and Sorelian antimaterialism.
4. **Pre-1914 maturity**: "All the main elements of Fascist thought came to
   fruition before the explosion of August 1914." The war supplied the
   sociological conditions of victory, not the ideas. Mussolini "came to
   power equipped with a coherent ideological system"; fascism's 1920s
   evolution pursued objectives fixed "ten years before the march on Rome."
   Indeed "the Mussolini regime of the 1930s was much closer to the
   ideological synthesis of *La lupa* or the [[cercle-proudhon|Cercle
   Proudhon]] than the Stalin regime was to original Marxism."
5. **France as laboratory, Italy as realization**: "The France of integral
   nationalism, of the revolutionary Right, was the real birthplace of
   fascism"; in 1914 France the convergence "did not go beyond the stage of
   an intellectual synthesis, but on the other side of the Alps... this
   synthesis became the great revolutionary force of the time."
6. **Fascism ≠ Nazism**: "Fascism can in no way be identified with
   Nazism... the criterion of German national socialism was biological
   determinism." Racism "was not a necessary condition for the existence of
   fascism," so "a general theory that seeks to combine fascism and Nazism...
   is not possible." (Valois's Faisceau had no antisemitism; Italian racial
   laws came only in 1938.)
7. **Fascism kept the market**: the fascist revolution "was supported by an
   economy determined by the laws of the market" — it adopted liberalism's
   economics while rejecting its philosophy, and kept Marxism's belief that
   violence drives history while rejecting historical materialism: "a third
   revolutionary option between the two great systems."

The book's formal definition: "Fascism becomes a conceptual system when the
rejection of materialism and rationalism becomes the essence of a total
political outlook... The emergence of fascism became inevitable when this
comprehensive rejection of the heritage of the Enlightenment was accompanied
by a strong cultural pessimism... and a cult of elitism and violence."
The Epilogue closes with the authors' own normative verdict: "to this day no
better basis has been found for a human order worthy of the name than the
universalism and humanism of the Enlightenment."

## Limitations and critiques

- **The Italian school** (Renzo De Felice, Emilio Gentile) treats Italian
  fascism as primarily a product of the war and of Italian conditions, and
  finds Sternhell's France-centered genealogy overdrawn; De Felice's dual
  typology (fascism-movement vs. fascism-regime) is the main rival framework
  acknowledged in the book itself.
- Critics argue Sternhell over-reads intellectual continuity: many
  syndicalists he tracks (Arturo Labriola, Leone, De Ambris) ended as
  anti-Fascists, which strains the claim of a single trajectory (Sternhell's
  reply distinguishes "economists" from "ethical revolutionaries" — see
  [[national-syndicalism]]).
- The thesis intersects the [[fascism-and-the-political-left]] controversy
  but should not be flattened into "fascism was left-wing": Sternhell's
  fascism is a *synthesis* that is "neither left nor right," born of the
  revolutionary Left's revision *and* of integral nationalism.
- [[payne-typology-of-authoritarian-nationalism|Payne]] and
  [[paxton-anatomy-of-fascism-2004|Paxton]] both treat ideology as less
  causally primary than Sternhell does; Paxton in particular inverts the
  emphasis (fascism as political practice, ideas as secondary).
- **Griffin** ([[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]], now ingested) engages
  the thesis directly and cuts both ways. He credits Sternhell with
  recognizing "the centrality of what we have called 'palingenetic myth'"
  (the "revolt against decadence" as fascism's thrust, the fin de siècle
  as fascism's "incubation years") and quotes his fascist-minimum method
  approvingly. But he rejects the Nazism exclusion outright — Nazism
  "proves to be an outstanding specimen of generic fascism once analysed
  in terms of this core myth"; treating biological racism as "a rule unto
  itself... tend[s] merely to confuse the issue" — and argues Sternhell
  "excessively homogenize[s] and intellectualize[s]" fascist ideology by
  seeking doctrinal coherence where the real unity is mythic. He also
  records the standing objections that the France-centered account imposes
  a rigid democratic/fascist dichotomy (ignoring, e.g., political
  Catholicism) and neglects sociological preconditions. See
  [[palingenetic-ultranationalism]].

## Payne's 1980 endorsement (avant la lettre)

Writing before *The Birth of Fascist Ideology*, Payne
([[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]], ch. 5) already endorsed the
core priority claim from Sternhell's *La Droite révolutionnaire* (1978):
"nearly all the ideas found in fascism and nazism first appeared in
France" — while drawing the anti-determinist moral that ideas alone do not
make a movement: France's structural stability as an old, victorious,
imperial nation left "little need or room for revolutionary nationalism,"
and no French fascist party ever broke the electoral "2 percent rule."
