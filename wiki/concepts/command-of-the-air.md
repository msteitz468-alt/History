# Command of the Air

**Command of the air** is the central concept of [[douhet-giulio|Giulio Douhet]]'s
*The Command of the Air* (1921, revised 1927), the foundational doctrine of strategic
air power. Douhet gave it a deliberately spare definition: **"To have command of the
air means to be in a position to prevent the enemy from flying while retaining the
ability to fly oneself."** From this single axiom he built an entire theory of future
war in which the airplane displaces the army and navy as the decisive instrument.

## The New Form of War

Douhet held that the airplane had ruptured all prior military experience — that "the
World War was only a point on the graph curve showing the evolution of the character
of war; at that point the graph curve makes a sharp swerve showing the influence of
entirely new factors." Because the plane "has complete freedom of action and
direction" and moves "freely in the third dimension," it overflies the static land
front entirely: "the repercussions of war are no longer limited by the farthest
artillery range of surface guns, but can be directly felt for hundreds and hundreds of
miles." The consequence is that "the battlefield will be limited only by the boundaries
of the nations at war, and all of their citizens will become combatants… There will be
no distinction any longer between soldiers and civilians." The army and navy are
reduced to a defensive, secondary role; "the air force is destined to predominate over
both land and sea forces." Future wars, Douhet argued, would be won by massive fleets
of strategic bombers carrying "explosive, incendiary, and poison gas" bombs — "the
explosives will demolish the target, the incendiaries set fire to it, and the
poison-gas bombs prevent fire fighters from extinguishing the fires" — directed at the
enemy's industry, transport, communications, government, and population (see
[[strategic-bombardment]]).

## The Civilian-Morale Target

The decisive target, for Douhet, is the will of the people. Because air power "will
strike against entities less well-organized and disciplined, less able to resist," the
collapse of morale would be swift: **"A body of troops will stand fast under intensive
bombings, even after losing half or two-thirds of its men; but the workers in shop,
factory, or harbor will melt away after the first losses."** Aerial offensives could be
aimed "not only against objectives of least physical resistance, but against those of
least moral resistance as well." Douhet even offered a grim moral justification: "the
decision will be quick in this kind of war, since the decisive blows will be directed
at civilians… These future wars may yet prove to be more humane than wars in the past
in spite of all, because they may in the long run shed less blood."

## Offense, Defenselessness, and Preemption

A second pillar is the supremacy of the offensive in the air. **"Viewed in its true
light, aerial warfare admits of no defense, only offense."** Defence is dismissed as
arithmetically hopeless: to guard twenty targets against an attacking force one must
station "twenty times as many planes as the enemy has," a "solution… which partakes of
the absurd." Antiaircraft fire is "a useless dispersion of enormous quantities of our
national resources," like "trying to catch a homing pigeon by following him on a
bicycle." The only defence is offence — to "destroy his air power before he has a
chance to strike at us," as "coastlines are defended from naval attacks… by conquering
the command of the seas." This yields a destabilizing logic of the **air fleet in being**
and **preemption**: "wars will begin in the air, and… large-scale aerial actions will
be carried out even before the declaration of war, because everyone will be trying to
get the advantage of surprise." "The war in the air will be decided by those aerial
forces which are in being and ready when hostilities break out."

## The Battleplane and the Independent Air Force

In the 1927 revision Douhet collapsed his earlier separate bombing and combat units
into a single multipurpose aircraft he called the **battleplane** — a plane "suitable
for both combat and bombing," with "radius of action, speed, and armor protection" plus
"armament sufficient both for aerial combat and for offensives against the surface,"
heavy, multimotored, ideally amphibious. With an all-battleplane force "all the planes
could take part in the engagement, with full freedom of action," fighting the air
battle in the first phase and striking the surface in the second. All such assets were
to be concentrated in a single **Independent Air Force**, "organically self-sufficient
and independent of land and sea forces," with "auxiliary aviation" stripped from the
army and navy.

## Total War and the Rejection of Limits

Command of the air belongs to a vision of war as [[total-war]] in which legal and
moral restraint is swept aside. Treaties banning "inhuman weapons" are "nothing but
scraps of paper compared to the tragedy which would follow"; "all contenders must use
all means without hesitation, whether or not they are forbidden by treaties." War "has
to be regarded unemotionally as a science, regardless of how terrible a science." This
sharply contrasts with the naval theorists' acceptance of restraint and gradation — see
[[limited-war]] — and with [[mahan-alfred-thayer|Mahan]] and [[corbett-julian|Corbett]]'s
focus on [[sea-power]] and [[maritime-strategy]], where command of the sea served
limited political ends rather than the annihilation of populations.

## Emic and Etic

In Douhet's own usage (emic), "command of the air" is a maximal, war-winning end state
— total exclusion of the enemy from the sky, achieved by destroying his aircraft "in
the air, at his bases of operation, or at his production centers." In later analytical
usage (etic), the term survives in diluted form as **air superiority** and **air
supremacy** — control of the air not as a stand-alone war-winner but as a *precondition*
enabling the operations of all forces. The phrase outlived Douhet's specific claims;
the doctrine of [[air-power]] retained the goal of controlling the air while abandoning
his insistence that bombing populations into collapse would by itself decide the war.

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept's central wager — that *independently decisive* bombing of civilian morale
would produce swift, complete victory — is also its most contested claim, and the one
the Second World War tested and largely disappointed. Douhet "hardly explored" the
premise of bombing efficiency, "postulating absurdly uniform and effective aerial
bombardment" and ignoring "duds, misses, overlap." He "virtually ignored the counterforce
air battle as a prerequisite for command of the air" and assumed defence "useless"
despite the WWI record of antiaircraft success. By dismissing pursuit and air defence,
he diverged from [[mitchell-william|William Mitchell]], who — within the broader theory
of [[air-power]] — retained pursuit aviation and a defensive role rather than betting
everything on the unescorted bomber. Critics from Brodie onward noted the gap between
his certainty about results and his vagueness about what to hit; the common verdict that
he was "rendered obsolete by technological change" is tempered by the recognition that
he theorized barely a decade after powered flight, in an age when "prophecy was normally
well ahead of technology."
