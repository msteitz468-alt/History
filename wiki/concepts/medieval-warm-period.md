# Medieval Warm Period

A climatic phase, roughly c. 950–1250 CE, during which parts of the North Atlantic region — especially Europe — experienced somewhat warmer average temperatures than in the preceding and following centuries, reconstructed from proxy evidence such as tree rings, ice cores, and lake sediments rather than direct instrumental measurement. It is conventionally associated with agricultural expansion (including the Norse settlement of Greenland and expanded viticulture in England), population growth, and the clearing of new land (assarting) across Western Europe.

The concept is genuinely contested among climate historians and paleoclimatologists: while regional warming in the North Atlantic during this period is reasonably well attested, whether it constituted a coherent, globally synchronous warm period comparable in scale to modern anthropogenic warming — as opposed to a regionally variable set of climate anomalies — remains debated. The term has also become politically charged in modern climate-change debates, where it is sometimes invoked (controversially) to argue that pre-industrial climate variability was comparably large to modern warming.

## Significance

The Medieval Warm Period is frequently cited as a contributing environmental factor in the High Middle Ages' agricultural expansion and demographic growth, though historians increasingly treat it as one input among many (technological, social, institutional) rather than a sufficient explanation on its own.
