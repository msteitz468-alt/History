# Gupta Classical Culture

## Definition and Scope

The term **'Classical'** as applied to the Gupta period 'implies unsurpassed intellectual and aesthetic excellence — a norm against which all other similar achievements can be judged.' Historians use it because 'the Brahmanic–Buddhist–Jainist civilisation that had been maturing for many centuries reached a particularly splendid height under the Guptas.' As Avari argues, 'Undoubtedly Gupta India was a far more desirable and creative place in which to live than the other great civilisations of that era, whether European, Chinese or Persian; but we must be careful that we do not succumb to the exaggerated notion that it was an age of well-being for all northern India's people. We need to ask critically who benefited most and who did not.'

The beneficiaries were primarily 'the elites of the upper and upper middle classes of the caste pyramid or creative and gifted people. As far as the ordinary people were concerned, the reality was somewhat different. The vast majority of them lived a short life, economically precarious, and disadvantaged by social, cultural and gender inequalities.'

## Mathematics and Astronomy: Aryabhata

The most iconic figure of Gupta classical culture in the sciences is **Aryabhata I** (born 476 CE). He worked at **Kusumpura and Nalanda**, and his treatise **Aryabhatiya** (celebrated at an international seminar in Trivandrum in January 2000, its 1,500th anniversary) is the foundational text of ancient Indian mathematics and astronomy.

The *Aryabhatiya* consists of four chapters (Padas): Gitika Pada, **Ganita Pada** (the 33-verse mathematical core), Kalakriya Pada, and Gola Pada. Its contributions include:
- Calculation of squares, cubes, square-roots, cube-roots, areas of triangles, volumes of prisms and spheres, elementary trigonometry, and the theory of pulveriser
- Methods to solve simple quadratic equations — earning Aryabhata 'the credit of founding the science of Algebra'
- The alphabet-numeral system of notation for conveying mathematical information in verse form
- The belief that **the earth was a sphere rotating on its axis** and that **eclipses were caused by the shadow of the earth falling on the moon**
- Calculations of the **pi ratio** and **length of the solar year** 'remarkably close to recent computations'

These achievements were built on earlier foundations: 'Long before the Guptas came to power there had been steady progress made in Indian mathematics' including problems in arithmetic, geometry of altars, algebraic equations, and number theory in the Satapatha Brahmana, Sulvasutras, Jain mathematics, and the Bakhshali Manuscript. The Indian number system with ten symbols (1–9 and zero) and the decimal place-value system were also already in use, though 'the earliest documented symbol for zero dates from the Gwalior inscription of the ninth century.'

After the Guptas, 'the works of Aryabhata and his illustrious successor, Brahmagupta, would firstly be closely scrutinised and then approvingly appropriated for development within the Islamic mathematical tradition, which in turn eventually influenced European developments in mathematics.' The numerals known to the world as 'Arabic' numerals originate in this tradition.

## Technology and Metallurgy

The **Iron Pillar of Delhi**, 'a shaft 24 feet high and weighing more than 6 tons, showing no signs of erosion or rust since about AD 400,' is the iconic symbol of Gupta metallurgical achievement. In copper, a seventh-century bronze statue of the Buddha (1 ton, found in 1864 at a Buddhist monastery at Sultanganj, Bihar, now in Birmingham Museum) demonstrates the cire perdue technique described in contemporary texts. 'For many centuries, excepting China, India led the world in metal technology.'

## Sanskrit Literature

The Gupta period was the high watermark of **Sanskrit literature**:

- **Kalidasa**: one of the 'Nine Gems' (Navaratna) at [[chandra-gupta-ii|Chandragupta II]]'s court at Ujjain, wrote three plays but is best known for *Shakuntala*. A critic is quoted: 'Of the arts the best is the drama; of dramas, Shakuntala; of Shakuntala, the fourth act; of that act, the verses in which Kanva bids farewell to his adopted daughter.' Sir William Jones translated *Shakuntala* into English in 1789; its 'real impact… was felt most on a long line of German Romantics, great geniuses such as Schiller, Goethe and Herder.'
- **Kama Sutra** by Vatsyayana: 'an account of the untainted and sublime beauty of erotic pleasure' and a sophisticated guide to urban culture; seriously distorted by Sir Richard Burton's 19th-century translation.
- **Panchatantra** and **Hitopadesha** (Book of Wise Counsels): collections of animal fables that 'subtly influenced medieval European literature' through increased contacts with the Middle East and Levant.
- Brahmanic religious literature (epics, shastras) was embellished and augmented by learned commentaries; Buddhist literature was systematised; Jainist knowledge was codified. The **eighteen major and eighteen minor Puranas** were 'essentially a product of the Gupta age' and became 'the key agencies by which the Shaivites and the Vaishnavites drew the people towards them.'

## Medicine: Ayurveda

Ancient Indian medicine (Ayurveda) drew on the works of **Charaka** and **Susruta**, who 'belonged to this period' (the post-Mauryan and Gupta centuries). Their works 'describe and explain fully eight branches of medical knowledge and practice: therapeutics, surgery, diseases of the sensory organs, mental illness, infant disorders, toxicology, tonics/drugs and sexual virility.' The materia medica of Ayurveda was extensive. Avari judges that 'there is a greater likelihood of fairly strong Indian influences' on Greek and Hellenistic medicine, based on the testimony from Alexander's contemporaries, Megasthenes's accounts, the export of Indian medicinal plants, and the generally more articulate Indian medical texts.

## Buddhist Art: Ajanta

The greatest artistic legacy of the Gupta–Vakataka age is the **Ajanta caves** of Maharashtra — twenty-nine man-made caves cut through the living rock 'at the crossing of merchants' and travellers' routes.' The caves contain both **Chaitya** (preaching halls with horseshoe-shaped windows) and **Vihara** (monastic cell) structures.

Ajanta's history spans about 700 years (c. 200 BCE – 500 CE) in two main phases: a first phase of ~400 years, and 'the most spectacularly creative portions' completed in 'less than two decades in the second half of the fifth century AD.' This later, shorter phase coincided with the reign of the **Vakataka king Harishena** (460–78 CE), 'the last great patron of Ajanta,' who ordered the most beautiful frescos depicting Vakataka court scenes and Bodhisattva manifestations. The site was 'rediscovered' by a British hunting party in 1819. 'The architecture, paintings and sculpture that emerged and evolved in Ajanta (in the Gupta–Vakataka Age) were to leave an impression on all subsequent Indian, Central Asian and Far Eastern Art.'

## Nalanda

The **Buddhist college at Nalanda** — 'the greatest of all the educational institutions of India during the second half of the first millennium' — gained its status during the reign of **Kumaragupta I** (415–55 CE), with 425 CE as 'the most approximate date' of its establishment. The college received a large land grant with rent drawn from 'nearly 100 villages' from Kumaragupta. Great philosophers including **Dignaga, Asanga, and Vasubandhu** taught complex ideas there. 'Long before the Western world came to embrace the idea of a college as an institution… everything that a college was ideally capable of was being realised at Nalanda.' It lasted until about 1205.

## "Classical" as a Label — Critiques

The 'golden age' or 'classical' label is contested on several grounds:

1. **Class and regional restriction**: The flowering was real but 'restricted — true mainly for the upper classes of northern India,' while the peninsular south's own classical flowering came in the *post*-Gupta centuries.
2. **Nationalist nostalgia**: 'Those searching for an idyllic Hindu past of India have tended to glorify the Gupta times as the "Golden Age" of Indian history.' The early-20th-century nationalist context shaped and inflated the label.
3. **Broad scope**: 'Indian creativity was not confined to the peoples and territories controlled by the Guptas' — the Vakataka achievement at Ajanta being the clearest example, carried out by an ally-dynasty rather than the Guptas themselves.
4. **Social shadow**: The same period saw the growth of **Untouchable** (Chandala) communities assigned the most demeaning occupations, the tightening of **land grants** creating feudatory intermediaries at the expense of cultivators, and increasing social rigidities offsetting the mobility of some groups.

## Historiography

The 'classical' designation for the Gupta period was coined by early Indian historiography and given wide currency in nationalist and colonial scholarship alike. Thapar's qualification — that it was 'restricted' and that the 'Golden Age' label was shaped by 20th-century nationalist nostalgia — is now standard in academic treatments. The Gupta period's scientific achievements (particularly Aryabhata and the transmission of Indian mathematics to the Islamic world) have received fresh scholarly attention through the history of mathematics. The Ajanta caves have been subject to intensive art-historical study, with the Vakataka contribution increasingly foregrounded. Nalanda's role as a trans-Asian hub of Buddhist learning is increasingly analysed in the context of the Silk Road intellectual network.
