# Several Property

The institution of private (separate, several) ownership of the means of production and of goods more generally. For Hayek it is not merely a legal form but the indispensable foundation of the extended order: the device that disperses knowledge, creates incentives aligned with dispersed information, enables trade and specialisation, and permits the growth of population and civilisation.

## Definition and Scope

“Several” property means property held by distinct individuals or small associations, not by “society” as a whole or by a central authority. It includes the right to use, to exclude, and to exchange. It is the precondition for the price system to function as a discovery procedure.

Hayek repeatedly cites Henry Sumner Maine (“Nobody is at liberty to attack several property and to say that he values civilisation”) and Carl Menger.

## Advantages in the Extended Order

- Disperses information and decision rights to those who possess local knowledge.
- Allows widely dispersed benefits to non-owners (the “widely dispersed benefits” passage).
- Makes possible the complex web of trade and contract that no one designs or surveys.
- Precondition for the growth of numbers and of wealth that only the extended order can sustain.

## Historical Development

Appears with long-distance trade in antiquity; unknown or weakly developed in “savage” (small-group) society; increasingly suspect after Rousseau; supported by surviving religions; still developing as a legal and moral notion.

## In *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960)

[[hayek-constitution-of-liberty-1960|*The Constitution of Liberty*]] ch. 9
grounds the institution in the theory of coercion: "The recognition of private
or several property is thus an essential condition for the prevention of
coercion, though by no means the only one" — with the modern qualification
that what matters is not that each individual own property but that ownership
be sufficiently dispersed that no one depends on a single controlling agent
(see [[rule-of-law]]). Ch. 8 adds the defense of inheritance as "probably the
best means of selection known to us" for sustaining an independent class able
to fund causes the market and majority will not yet support.

## Relation to Socialism and the Fatal Conceit

Socialist thought (and much modern “social justice” discourse) treats several property as morally suspect or as the root of alienation and injustice. Hayek argues that the demand to abolish or override it is the practical expression of the fatal conceit: the belief that we can reorganise production and distribution according to a visible common plan without destroying the order that feeds us.
