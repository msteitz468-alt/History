# Sothic Cycle

The **Sothic cycle** is the ~1,460-year period over which the Egyptian civil
calendar drifts a full year against the solar year and re-synchronizes with the
**heliacal rising of Sirius** (Egyptian *Sopdet*, Greek *Sothis*). It is the
principal astronomical anchor for absolute
[[concepts/egyptian-dynastic-chronology|Egyptian chronology]].

## Mechanism

The Egyptian civil ("wandering") year had a fixed 365 days with no leap day, so it
fell behind the true solar year by roughly one day every four years. The heliacal
rising of Sirius — its first pre-dawn reappearance, which anciently coincided with
the onset of the Nile inundation and the start of the year — therefore aligned with
the calendrical New Year only once every 1,460 years (more precisely ~1,456). The
last such synchronization is securely dated to **AD 139** under Antoninus Pius by a
commemorative Alexandrian coin; earlier risings fell in 1321–1317 BCE and
2781–2777 BCE.

## Chronological anchors

Two records of dated Sothic risings fix the Middle and New Kingdom chronologies by
extrapolation:

1. A 12th-Dynasty letter from Lahun dating a rising to year 7 of **Senusret III**
   (≈1872 BCE).
2. The **Papyrus Ebers** dating a rising to year 9 of **Amenhotep I** (≈1541 BCE).

Awareness of Sirius's calendrical role is far older: the earliest depiction of the
goddess Sopdet, on an ivory tablet of the 1st-Dynasty king **Djer** (~3000 BCE) from
[[places/abydos|Abydos]], "appears to show her as a seated cow with a plant between
her horns."

## Uncertainty

Sothic dating is precise only if the **place of observation** is known, because the
heliacal rising occurs on different days at different latitudes. Scholars dispute
whether the ancient readings were taken at Memphis, Thebes, or Elephantine — leaving
"our astronomical linchpins … in reality somewhat floating," though the resulting
"high" vs. "low" chronologies usually differ by only a few decades.
