## Definition

The *aldeia* (pl. *aldeias*) was the Jesuit mission-village system established in sixteenth-century Brazil, designed to congregate converted indigenous peoples into permanent, disciplined settlements under clerical supervision. Physically modeled on European village layouts featuring a church and central plaza, the aldeias replaced the mobile, fragmented settlement patterns of precolonial Tupi society with fixed communities administered by the Society of Jesus.

John Monteiro's analysis (Ch. 13, *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas*, Vol. III, Part 1) provides the essential historical account of the aldeias as simultaneously a missionary project, a labor-recruitment mechanism, a crown security asset, and an unwitting agent of epidemiological catastrophe.

## Emic vs. Etic

**Emic (Jesuit) understanding:** The aldeias embodied the Jesuit ideal of *reduction* — the congregation of dispersed "heathen" peoples into an ordered Christian polity where they could be catechized, baptized, and made into loyal, productive Catholic subjects. Jesuits saw themselves as protecting indigenous peoples from enslavement while rescuing their souls. Manuel da Nobrega's correspondence frames the aldeias as "a peaceful, alternative solution to the problems of domination and labor recruitment."

**Etic (analytical) understanding:** The aldeias functioned as a mechanism of colonial control over indigenous land and labor, substituting Jesuit authority for indigenous autonomy. As Monteiro states bluntly, the aldeias "transferred the control of coastal Indian land and labor into European hands." Their legal foundation, the *sesmaria* granted to Indian villages, was a collective land title that could not be alienated — superficially protective, but in practice it confined indigenous peoples to spaces "conveniently located on the outskirts of zones most suited to sugar production."

Stuart Schwartz (*Sugar Plantations in the Formation of Brazilian Society*, 1985) characterizes part of the Jesuit strategy as attempting "to mold an independent indigenous peasantry out of the detribalized peoples thrown together in the aldeias" — a "paper peasantry" that never materialized because aldeia residents rarely produced enough to maintain themselves.

## Establishment and Scale

The Jesuits began itinerant mission activities among Tupi villages from their arrival in 1549. By the late 1550s, with active support from Governor Mem de Sa, they shifted to establishing permanent mission villages. In Bahia, "at least twelve aldeias were created in the late 1550s, concentrating over 40,000 Indians in the immediate area of Salvador by the early 1560s." Governor Mem de Sa reported to the crown in 1560 that 437 Indians had been baptized in a single day at the aldeia of Espirito Santo, near Salvador. Similar networks were established in the captaincies of Espirito Santo and Sao Vicente among the Tupinikin.

## Social Engineering and Cultural Disruption

The Jesuit program systematically dismantled core structures of Tupi social organization:

- **Sedentarization**: Fixed, permanent villages with restricted land rights stood "in stark contrast with the irregular patterns of fragmentation and regeneration which characterized precolonial village formation." Tupi villages had typically relocated every few years as horticultural productivity declined, game was depleted, or factional disputes drove fission.
- **Spatial reorganization**: The European church-and-plaza model "departed somewhat from the organization of non-Christian Indian villages."
- **Kinship and domestic structure**: "The replacement of extended-family living units with nuclear households and the prohibition of polygamy affected some groups greatly." Polygamy among headmen had been a key mechanism for building political followings through marriage alliance.
- **Ceremonial suppression**: "The suppression of most ceremonial activities and their substitution with Christian rituals sought to restructure the basic contours of Indian existence."
- **Work discipline**: "The Jesuits attempted to inculcate a totally new concept of time and work discipline into their Indian subordinates, where a new sexual division of labor and a rigid schedule of productive activities clashed with preconquest patterns."

The Jesuit program centered on three targets: conversion of headmen, indoctrination of youths, and elimination of the *pajes* (shamans). At each level it met resistance. Headman conversion could be a social catastrophe — one early Bahian headman "came to be ostracized by all of his relatives because of his adherence to Christianity." Youth instruction was undermined when boys reached adolescence and "would take up the customs of their elders." The *pajes* were described by Anchieta (1557) as the "last line of resistance," preaching that baptismal water caused disease and organizing *santidade* cults as syncretic counter-movements.

The Jesuits also adopted indigenous rhetorical forms strategically. It was "common, for example, for Jesuits to preach before sunup, as was the practice of headmen or pajes who harangued their fellow tribesmen." Anchieta preached "in a loud voice throughout their houses, as is their custom," offering that Christianity would "give them abundant crops, health and victory against their enemies."

## Epidemiological Catastrophe

The most devastating unintended consequence of the aldeia system was epidemic amplification. By concentrating previously mobile populations — "some individual aldeias had over 5,000 residents" — and "restricting its customary mobility, the Jesuits unwittingly contributed to the conditions favoring the spread of epidemics." Luis Felipe Baeta Neves identified specific Jesuit practices that further accelerated disease spread: the use of clothing and the burial of contaminated persons within the village perimeter.

The aldeias also survived only through constant replenishment with newly contacted groups, who were "far more susceptible to the imported diseases." The first major smallpox epidemic struck in 1559; measles followed in 1562–1563. Between 1560 and 1580, the population of the Bahia aldeias fell from over 40,000 to fewer than 10,000. Anchieta's assessment of the early 1580s: "The people that have been wasted in Bahia over the last twenty years is something incredible; because no one considered that so many people could ever be expended, especially in so short a time."

## Conflict with Colonists over Indian Labor

The aldeia system created permanent structural tension with private colonists:

**Crown and Jesuit rationale:** The crown saw healthy aldeias as a way of protecting the sugar economy from external threats (French interlopers, Tapuia raids, runaway African slaves), providing military auxiliaries, and supplying "a reserve of free labor for colonial enterprises" through a system of contract labor — a cheaper and legally cleaner alternative to chattel slavery.

**Colonist perspective:** Private settlers initially supported the plan as an alternative to the logistical difficulties of slave-raiding, but the aldeias "failed to produce the structures necessary to sustain and reproduce a reserve labor force sufficiently large to meet settler demands." Contract labor from aldeias was insufficient; colonists pushed for unrestricted access to aldeia residents, culminating in direct raids (as during the Caete Just War of 1562, when colonists enslaved Caete living in Jesuit missions alongside declared enemies).

**Outcome:** "Unable to meet the labor needs of the settlers and frustrated by their incapacity to transform the recalcitrant tribal Indians into loyal Catholic subjects, the Jesuits and their aldeia program quickly lost ground to alternative forms of labor recruitment." The aldeia system "succeeded only in creating marginal communities of sickly, morose, and unproductive residents, hardly able to provide for their own survival."

## Native Resistance to the Aldeias

Indigenous peoples resisted the aldeias through multiple strategies:

- **Refusal of baptism**: Old women in one aldeia near Salvador refused baptism "because they are certain that baptism will bring them death" — a well-founded fear, since the Jesuits tended to reserve baptism for the dying, and epidemics were directly associated with Jesuit presence.
- **Active insubordination**: Chief Cururupeba of Bahia "openly defied the governor's prohibition of cannibalism." Residents of the Rio Vermelho village rebelled in 1557 when Jesuits pressured their headman to abandon polygamy.
- **Flight**: "The most common form of resistance was flight from the aldeias." In 1560 "many of the residents of the Sao Joao aldeia followed headman Mirangoaba into the interior." Nobrega admitted "many flee the subjection of our doctrine and live like their ancestors, eating human flesh as before."
- **Santidade cults**: Syncretic movements blending indigenous shaman traditions with Christian symbolism emerged as organized spiritual resistance, particularly in the backlands of Bahia in the late sixteenth century.

## Limitations and Critiques

The aldeia concept has been analyzed from several critical angles:

1. **Colonial function over stated purpose**: Whatever the Jesuits' intentions, the aldeias functioned as an apparatus of colonial control, stripping indigenous peoples of territorial mobility, kinship structures, and ceremonial life.
2. **Epidemiological perversity**: The congregating function actively worsened demographic conditions for the people the aldeias were nominally designed to protect.
3. **Labor competition vs. protection**: The Jesuit claim to protect Indians from enslavement was partially undermined by their own use of contract and coerced labor, and by the crown's simultaneous endorsement of Just War enslavement.
4. **Cultural destruction as method**: The systematic suppression of *pajes*, polygamy, ritual sacrifice, and village mobility destroyed the social structures that gave Tupi life its coherence, producing what Monteiro calls "marginal communities" rather than Christian peasants.
