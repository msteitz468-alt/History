# Pre-Christian Aksumite Religion

## Definition and Scope

Pre-Christian Aksumite religion was a form of polytheism prevalent in the northern Horn of Africa for centuries before the adoption of Christianity under [[ezana-of-aksum|Ezana]] (c. 337–350 CE). The pantheon centred on three principal deities attested in Ezana's early royal inscriptions: **Mahrem** (war-god), **Astar** (celestial/astral), **Beher** (sea/coast), and **Meder** (earth). The crescent-and-disc symbol — widely used on pre-Christian Aksumite coins, stelae, and altars — was the principal visual marker of this religious tradition. The system was analogous to, but not identical with, the polytheism of southern Arabia (where the deities had different names, with one exception — Astar appears in both).

## The Deities

| Deity | Domain | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Mahrem** | War | The pre-eminent royal deity; Aksumite kings before Ezana's conversion called themselves "son of the unconquered/invincible Mahrem"; equated with Ares in Greek versions of the inscriptions; not attested in earlier inscriptions in the northern Horn or in southern Arabia — apparently specifically Aksumite |
| **Astar** | Celestial/astral | Shared name with a southern Arabian deity (making it the only overlap); named in Ezana's trilingual inscriptions |
| **Beher** | Sea/coast | Named alongside Meder in RIE 188; Beher and Meder may have been distinct in early inscriptions but show signs of conflation in later ones |
| **Meder** | Earth | Named in the Ethiopic-script version of RIE 185bis (where the ASAM-derived script uses Beher); the distinction may have been eroding |

## Material Evidence: The Crescent-and-Disc Symbol

The crescent-and-disc (the disc typically above the crescent) was the primary visual symbol of pre-Christian Aksumite religion. It appears on:
- Pre-Christian Aksumite coins (above the royal portrait)
- The Anza stela (RIE 218) and the Matara stela, both tentatively dated to the third century on palaeographic grounds
- Earlier altars and incense-burners from the pre-Aksumite D'MT horizon in both the northern Horn and southern Arabia

The coinage sequence documents the religious transition: pre-Christian coins bear the crescent-and-disc; Ezana's later coins replace it with the Christian cross; some intermediate coins bear no religious symbol at all — Phillipson interprets these as reflecting uncertainty about the popular acceptability of the new religion.

## The Divine Kingship Claim

Before Christianisation, Aksumite royal inscriptions included the formula "son of the unconquered [invincible] Mahrem" — a claim to divine or semi-divine descent that legitimated royal authority. This appears in inscriptions attributed to Ezana's predecessor Ella Amida (RIE 186) and in early inscriptions of Ezana himself. The claim was discontinued after conversion, replaced by a human patronymic.

The crescent-and-disc appears to have been used as a generic astral symbol rather than necessarily representing a single specific deity; the inscriptions name the deities separately.

## The Transition to Monotheism

The transition from polytheism to Christianity under Ezana was gradual and strategically managed:

1. **Early inscriptions**: Mahrem invoked as divine father; dedications to Astar, Beher, Meder.
2. **Trilingual inscriptions (RIE 185/270, 185bis/270bis)**: Still invoke Mahrem as divine father and dedicate to the pagan triad; but the Greek version of 270bis adds "Lord of Heaven and Earth" — possibly an attempt to gloss the Ge'ez deity-names for a Greek readership rather than a Christian reference.
3. **RIE 189**: No deities named; "Lord of Heaven" credited for Ezana's power — a monotheist formulation also common in contemporary southern Arabian inscriptions, not exclusively Christian.
4. **RIE 190/271**: Explicitly Christian, naming the Trinity and Christ; but the Ge'ez version is less overtly Christian than the Greek — Christianity was more openly publicised to Greek-readers (foreigners and international trade partners) than to the Ge'ez-speaking population.

This pattern suggests Christianity was first adopted as an elite/court religion and the language of international engagement, while indigenous populations retained older practices longer. Cross-designs on domestic pottery do not become common until the late fifth and early sixth centuries.

## Judaic Elements in Aksumite Christianity

Phillipson discusses the persistence of "Judaic" elements in Aksumite and later Ethiopian Christianity, including dietary practices and Old Testament observances. He situates this within the broader South Semitic monotheist milieu: the fourth-century trend toward monotheism in both Aksum and southern Arabia (Himyar) drew on shared Judaic traditions from which Judaism, Christianity, and Islam all emerged. By the sixth century, Aksumite kings adopted Old Testament names (Kaleb, Israel, Gersem), coinciding with the Bible's translation into Ge'ez. Phillipson cautions that "Jewish" was used by fifth-century writers to mean any non-Christian or non-Islamic monotheism — the Himyaritic monotheism was *judaïsant mais pas explicitement juif* (Gajda's formulation).

## Historiography

Pre-Christian Aksumite religion is known almost entirely from the stone inscriptions and coins; no pre-Christian Aksumite religious buildings have been published. The crescent-and-disc symbol is the main archaeological marker, but it was widespread across the South Arabian world and cannot always be precisely assigned. The relationship between the Aksumite deities and their South Arabian analogues is debated (Sima 2003, 2007); Mahrem's absence from earlier inscriptions in either region is notable. Phillipson warns against assuming all post-mid-fourth-century structures at Aksumite sites were necessarily Christian — the transition was gradual and the evidence sparse for its early phases.
