# Romanesque Art and Architecture

## Definition

**Romanesque** is the art and architecture of Latin Europe at its eleventh- and
twelfth-century height — massive, round-arched stone churches with thick walls, barrel
and groin vaults, and richly carved portals and capitals. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH
IV]] (Peter Kidson, Ch. 19, "Architecture and the visual arts") treats it as the
artistic counterpart of the period's general expansion: the great age of monastic and
pilgrimage building, when "Romanesque art and architecture reached their zenith in all
parts of western Europe."

## Character

The style (the nineteenth-century name registers its debt to Roman round-arched
construction) is marked by:
- **Monumental masonry churches** — the abbey and pilgrimage churches of Cluny ("Cluny
  III," the largest church in Christendom before St Peter's), Santiago de Compostela,
  Vézelay, Speyer, Durham — with the structural ambition (stone vaulting over wide
  spans) that the period's engineering increasingly achieved.
- **Architectural sculpture** — the tympana, portals, and historiated capitals that
  made the Romanesque church a "sermon in stone."
- **Regional diversity** — Norman/Anglo-Norman, Burgundian, Lombard, Rhenish, and
  Spanish variants, with the Mediterranean traditions of [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]]
  remaining powerful in Spain, Sicily, and Venice.

NCMH IV emphasises that building proclaimed meaning: "all castles and all churches
proclaim respectively lordship and faith." The austere, unadorned simplicity of early
[[cistercian-order|Cistercian]] churches expressed the reforming strictness of the new
monasticism, while the sumptuous Byzantine-style mosaics of Norman Sicily proclaimed
the rulers' imperial ambitions.

## Significance and Transition

Romanesque is the visual expression of the [[high-middle-ages|High Middle Ages]] in its
formative phase — the [[medieval-monastic-revival|monastic revival]], the
[[crusades|pilgrimage]] networks, and the wealth of the [[medieval-agricultural-revolution|
agrarian]] and [[commercial-revolution|commercial]] expansion. From the mid-twelfth
century it gave way in northern France to the [[gothic-architecture|Gothic]], the
"transition from rounded to pointed forms" that NCMH IV identifies as the most
noticeable feature of the change.
