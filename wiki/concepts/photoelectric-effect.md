# Photoelectric Effect / Light Quanta

**1905**. Einstein’s hypothesis that light consists of discrete energy packets (“light quanta,” later photons) explained the photoelectric effect observed by Philipp Lenard and launched quantum theory. Einstein regarded it as his most revolutionary 1905 contribution.

## Definition and Scope

When light shines on a metal surface, electrons are emitted (photoelectric effect). Classical wave theory predicted that brighter (more intense) light should impart more energy to emitted electrons, causing them to leave faster. Lenard’s experiments showed otherwise: higher frequency (shorter wavelength, e.g., ultraviolet vs. red) increased electron energy/speed; increased intensity only increased the *number* of electrons emitted, not their individual energy. There was a frequency threshold below which no electrons were emitted, regardless of intensity.

Einstein’s explanation (March 1905 paper “On a Heuristic Point of View...”): light propagates as localized quanta of energy *E = hν* (Planck’s constant *h* times frequency *ν*). A single quantum transfers its entire energy to one electron. Higher frequency → higher quantum energy → faster electrons. More intense light → more quanta → more electrons, but each carries the same energy set by frequency.

## Genesis in Isaacson Range

Einstein pondered Planck’s 1900 blackbody formula (energy emitted/absorbed in discrete packets) and Lenard’s 1902 paper for years. In his 1904 heat paper he found statistical agreement between radiation and molecular systems. He interpreted Planck’s constant literally as a feature of light itself, not merely a calculational device for emission/absorption at surfaces.

Paper opens with contrast between particle (kinetic gas) and continuous (Maxwell field) descriptions. Concludes radiation “behaves thermodynamically as if it consisted of mutually independent energy quanta.” Applies to Lenard: “As far as I can see, our conception does not conflict with the properties of the photoelectric effect observed by Mr. Lenard.”

Einstein called the paper “very revolutionary.” Planck and others initially accepted only a limited version (quanta at matter interaction, not in free radiation). Millikan later verified the law experimentally but rejected the underlying particle picture. Einstein won the 1921 Nobel specifically “for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect.”

## Distinctions and Controversies

- **Heuristic**: Einstein presented it provisionally (“heuristic point of view”); he spent decades uneasy about its implications for causality and completeness of description.
- Wave-particle duality: light exhibits both continuous wave behavior (interference, diffraction) and particle behavior (photoelectric, Compton scattering). Einstein’s 1909 Salzburg talk explicitly advocated fusion of wave and emission theories.
- Undermined classical determinism: quanta and later quantum mechanics introduced probability and discontinuity.

## Historiography (per Isaacson)

The most radical of the 1905 papers; slower to be accepted than relativity. Isaacson stresses Einstein’s deduction from entropy/statistical arguments rather than pure induction from Lenard’s data. “Einstein... figured out the physical significance of what Planck had discovered.” Planck warned Einstein he had “overshot.” Millikan: theory “untenable.” Yet the law proved correct; quanta became fundamental.

**Grounding quotes**: “The first deals with radiation and the energy properties of light and is very revolutionary...” (Habicht letter). “According to the assumption... the energy is not continuously distributed... but consists of a finite number of energy quanta...” “I believe that this agreement must not be ascribed to chance” (1904 precursor). Later: “All these fifty years of pondering have not brought me any closer to answering the question, What are light quanta?”
