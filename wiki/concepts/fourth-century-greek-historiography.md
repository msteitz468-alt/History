# Fourth-Century Greek Historiography

## The governing problem: no Thucydides

[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] opens (Ch. 1, Simon Hornblower) with the defining
fact of the century's study: "**No guide comparable to [[thucydides|Thucydides]] exists
for the fourth century.**" There is no contemporary narrative that imposes a firm
framework of political and military events, and this lack "is a serious obstacle to one
sort of knowledge." Yet the loss is partly compensated: the very kinds of history
Thucydides treated only selectively — **social, economic, and religious** — can
actually be studied *better* for the fourth century, because the literary sources are
less obsessed with the Athens–Sparta polarity and because the **epigraphic record is far
richer**, especially for places other than Athens. This is the historiographical
companion to [[classical-greek-historiography|the fifth-century source page]].

## The thin narrative spine

For the years **403–362** there is **only one surviving primary account**: Books
ii.3–vii of **[[xenophon|Xenophon's]]** *Hellenica*. Its limitations are severe and
patterned (see [[xenophon]]): a Peloponnesian, pro-Spartan, often pro-Agesilaus
viewpoint that omits or downplays Theban successes, the
[[second-athenian-confederacy|Second Athenian Confederacy]], the satraps' revolts, and
Persian involvement in the common peaces. Reconstructing the period therefore means
**triangulating Xenophon against three other traditions**.

## The four traditions to triangulate

1. **Diodorus Siculus → Ephorus.** Books xiv–xv (and parts of xvi) of Diodorus'
   universal history rest on the lost **Ephorus of Cyme**, who "used one main source at
   a time." Behind Ephorus lie still better sources, so Diodorus often **corrects
   Xenophon** — for instance preferring Diodorus' (and the Oxyrhynchus Historian's)
   ambush account of Agesilaus' 395 campaign over Xenophon's glamorous open battle.
   Diodorus' archon-year datings, forced to equate Athenian and Roman years, remain
   mechanically unreliable.
2. **The *Hellenica Oxyrhynchia*.** A sober, intelligent continuator of Thucydides
   (terminal date uncertain, ending in the 380s), important above all for the **Boeotian
   federal constitution** (its ch. xvi) and as proof that "a sober historian lies behind
   Diodorus/Ephorus." It represents a tradition "independent of and preferable to
   Xenophon."
3. **Callisthenes of Olynthus** (the nephew of Aristotle). Ephorus turned to him for
   events after 386 when the *Hell. Oxy.* ended. Much **less pro-Spartan than
   Xenophon**, Callisthenes gave "proper space and significance to Thebes" and to
   [[epaminondas]], and lies behind some of Plutarch's *Pelopidas*. His F 8 — Spartans
   appealing to Athens for help in 370/69 — exposes Xenophon's near-"falsification" of
   the same scene.
4. **The Atthidographers and the orators.** The local chroniclers of Attica —
   especially **Philochorus** (*FGrH* 328) and Androtion — supply decisive points (it is
   Philochorus who reveals that the 375 peace was "sent from Persia"). The great
   fourth-century **orators** (Demosthenes, Aeschines, Isocrates, Lysias) and the
   technical writers (**Aeneas Tacticus** on siegecraft) add contemporary, if
   tendentious, testimony.

## Persia and the West: special source-problems

For **Persia**, Ephorus drew on the *Persica* of writers like **Dinon of Colophon** and
the gossipy **Ctesias of Cnidus**, plus Xenophon's *Anabasis* — so even Diodorus
preserves, at one remove, a Xenophontic strand. For **Sicily and the West**, Diodorus
exceptionally drew on *two* writers, Ephorus *and* **Timaeus of Tauromenium** (the first
great historian of western Hellenism), behind both of whom stands the shadowy
**Philistus**; Timaeus is also Plutarch's source for the Lives of **Dion** and
**Timoleon**. **Plutarch's** loss of interest in Athenian subjects after 400 — no
Athenian *Life* between the *Alcibiades* and the *Demosthenes/Phocion* — "correctly
reflects the changed historical reality" of a multi-centred Greek world.

## Why it matters

The fourth-century source problem is not merely an obstacle; it shapes interpretation.
The editors of CAH VI use the richer epigraphy and the rival anti-Spartan tradition to
**deliberately shift the narrative focus from Athens** (which still dominates the
surviving material) **toward Sparta and Thebes** — recovering a period "full of ideas
and innovation" that older scholarship had read merely as the confused prelude to Philip
of Macedon.
