# Medieval European Images of the Mongols

How **Latin Christendom imagined the Mongols** — the "Tartars" — as it tried to fit a
wholly unfamiliar people into its inherited scriptural, classical, and ethnographic
categories. [[jackson-mongols-and-the-west|Peter Jackson]] devotes a central chapter
("Images of the Enemy") to the West's swing from messianic hope to apocalyptic terror to
ethnographic curiosity, a case study in how a culture comprehends a radical newcomer. The
image mattered practically: it shaped whether Europe saw the Mongols as a providential
ally, the forces of Antichrist, or a power to be converted, allied with, or traded with.

## From "King David" to the scourge of God

The first reports (via the Fifth Crusade in Egypt, 1221) cast the Mongols hopefully as the
Christian army of **"King David"** or **Prester John**, come to crush Islam from the rear —
a notion interpolated into the Prester John legend and surviving in rumours that the
Mongols meant to retrieve the bones of the Three Magi from Cologne (hence "Tarsenses").
The **devastation of Poland, Moravia, and Hungary in 1241–2** shattered this optimism. The
Mongols now seemed "barbarians... hostile and bloodthirsty," a people who "had taken a mere
two decades to burst upon the West's horizons from nowhere." Matthew Paris asked: "Where
had such a people lain hidden?"

## The apocalyptic frame: Gog and Magog, the Ten Lost Tribes, Antichrist

Lacking any place for the Mongols in scripture or encyclopaedia, writers reached for the
**eschatological tradition**:

- The very name **"Tartar"** was assimilated to **Tartarus**, the Hell of classical
  antiquity — the Mongols as fiends "from Hell."
- They were identified with **Gog and Magog**, the apocalyptic nations whom **Alexander
  the Great** (in the widely-read *Alexander Romance*) had enclosed behind iron gates in
  the Caucasus, destined to break out at the end of days (the same identity earlier pinned
  on the pagan Hungarians).
- Some made them the **Ten Lost Tribes of Israel** — feeding anti-Jewish panic that
  European Jews were in league with the invaders (a charge that recurs at the
  [[black-death|Black Death]]).
- Many read the invasion as a sign of the **Last Days** and the coming of **Antichrist**,
  with the year **1260** a focus of dread.

## The ethnographic image: barbarian, unclean, godless

Alongside the apocalyptic image ran a more empirical (if hostile) **ethnography**, supplied
above all by the friar-envoys **Carpini** and **Rubruck** (and Simon of Saint-Quentin). The
Mongols were nomads who "nowhere have any lasting city" (Rubruck, echoing Hebrews 13:14) —
Jackson notes the medieval link between rootless pastoralism and the lack of a "salvific"
faith, since Christians made the *city* the image of both earthly and heavenly community.
They were **unclean**: eating raw and "unclean" meat, dogs, mice, marmots, even (in the
lurid accusations of Simon of Saint-Quentin and Matthew Paris's famous illustration)
**human flesh**; ignorant of bread, wine, and fish — the three staples of the Western diet
*and* the Christian liturgy. And they were **godless** in Latin eyes: acknowledging one
God but worshipping him with no prayer or ceremony, knowing nothing of salvation or
damnation, and blasphemously calling the qaghan "son of God" (Carpini and Rubruck mistook
the ancestral *ongghod* effigies for idols).

## The Mongols' image of the Franks

Jackson notes the mirror: the Mongols, lacking any narrative source on Europe, conceived of
Latin Christendom as a single large power, initially taking the **Pope** for its emperor.
They held the **mendicant friars in contempt** — poor, barefoot, bearing meagre gifts and
urging conversion ("is not your Pope a dog, and are not all you Christians dogs?") — even
while reportedly respecting the **Frankish warrior**; but their sense of their own
superiority was unshaken, since they regarded all Westerners as future subjects.

## Significance

These images are a primary case in the history of **cross-cultural perception**. They
governed European responses to the Mongols — the messianic hope of an anti-Muslim ally
(see the abortive [[mongol-latin-crusade-alliance|crusade alliance]]), the apocalyptic
terror behind the calls for crusade against the Tartars, and the curiosity that drove the
[[medieval-latin-missions-to-asia|missions]] and the travel literature (Carpini, Rubruck,
later Marco Polo). They also illustrate Jackson's larger theme: that the medieval
"discovery" of Asia was filtered through, and often distorted by, inherited categories.
