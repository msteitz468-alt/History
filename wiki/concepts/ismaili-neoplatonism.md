## Definition and Scope

**Ismaili Neoplatonism** denotes the distinctive philosophical theology developed by a series of Ismaili missionary-intellectuals (*dāʿīs*) in the tenth and eleventh centuries, who drew on the Neoplatonic system introduced to Arabic culture by the [[the-translation-movement|Kindī circle]] to provide a systematic philosophical framework for [[shia-islam|Ismaili Shiite]] religious claims. It is distinct from both mainstream [[islamic-philosophy|Falsafah]] (which was not explicitly missionary in intent) and from [[kalam|kalām]] (which operated within Sunni methodological constraints). Its central ambitions were: (1) to articulate the utter transcendence of God in more radical terms than either Muʿtazilite or Neoplatonic thought had achieved; and (2) to integrate the Ismaili doctrine of the necessary Imam into a philosophical cosmology. Source: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]].

**Emic usage**: Participants would have described their project as uncovering the *bāṭin* (inner, esoteric) meaning of Islamic revelation through rational inquiry guided by the Imam's teaching.

**Etic usage**: Modern scholars use "Ismaili Neoplatonism" or "Ismaili philosophy" to designate the school; Hodgson treats it as part of the broader Islamicate philosophical tradition.

## Key Thinkers (Chronological)

- **Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī** (d. 933): The earliest major Ismaili philosopher, known for his debate with [[abu-bakr-al-razi|Abū Bakr al-Rāzī]] over the necessity of prophecy. His double-negation theology of divine transcendence: God is "not perfect" — but also "not not-perfect."
- **Muḥammad al-Nasafī** (d. 943): Active in Khorasan; early systematizer
- **Abū Yaʿqūb al-Sijistānī** (fl. c. 971): The most philosophically sophisticated of the early group; developed the radical negative theology of *lā wa-lā lā* ("not and not-not")
- **Ḥamīd al-Dīn al-Kirmānī** (d. after 1020): Shifted the tradition toward al-Fārābī's celestial intellect schema; integrated Farābian cosmology into Ismaili theology
- **Nāṣir Khusraw** (1004–c. 1088): First to compose philosophical works in Persian; synthesized the tradition and linked it to his own extensive travels and missionary work

## Core Doctrines

### Radical Divine Transcendence

All the Ismaili philosophers shared an intense commitment to God's transcendence, going beyond the [[mutazilism|Muʿtazilite]] negative theology. The Muʿtazilites had denied positive attributes of God to protect divine unity — saying God is "not ignorant" rather than "knowing." Al-Sijistānī argued that even this single negation was insufficient:

> "Al-Sijistānī spoke of worshipping God through the expression *lā wa-lā lā*, 'not and not-not'." (Adamson, *Philosophy in the Islamic World*, ch. 14)

The rationale: when we say God is "not perfect," we imply that perfection *might* have applied to God but happens not to. This is like saying blue is "not heavy" — the predicate of heaviness does not even apply to colors in the first place. Similarly, human concepts do not merely fail to fit God; they are *categorically inapplicable* to Him. The double negation (*lā lā*) conveys this categorical inapplicability better than single negation alone.

This doctrine is sometimes compared (by scholars) to the radical apophatic theology of the late Greek Neoplatonist Damascius, whom the Ismailis presumably did not read but whose logic they independently reproduced.

### The Cosmic Hierarchy

The Ismailis accepted the general Neoplatonic hierarchy from the Kindī-circle texts:

God → Intellect → Soul → World

But they introduced a further level between God and the Intellect: God's **Word** or **Command** (*amr*), drawn from Quranic passages describing God creating by saying "Be!" (*kun*). This additional level served the dual purpose of protecting divine transcendence (the Intellect is now separated from God by an additional intermediary) and giving the cosmological scheme an explicitly Islamic theological grounding.

Like [[al-amiri|al-ʿĀmirī]], the Ismailis applied Quranic epithets to the Neoplatonic entities — calling the Intellect the "Pen" and the World-Soul the "Tablet," in reference to Quranic cosmological imagery.

### Prophecy and the Imamate

The Ismaili thinkers had a specific theological agenda that distinguished them from non-Ismaili philosophers: they needed to explain not only why prophecy was necessary (a problem all Islamic philosophers engaged with) but also why the Prophet's revelation required **ongoing interpretation by an Imam**.

Their solution integrated the Neoplatonic hierarchy with Ismaili religious claims:
- The prophets were legislators whose teachings were grounded in the truths of the universal Intellect
- But prophetic law required esoteric interpretation: this was the Imam's role
- The Imam's authority derived from his unique access to the *bāṭin* (inner meaning) of revelation

This was attacked by Sunni critics (and later by [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]]) as demoting the Prophet by making his revelation incomplete without subsequent Imamic guidance.

### Exoteric vs. Esoteric

A signature Ismaili theme was the contrast between *ẓāhir* (outer, exoteric) and *bāṭin* (inner, esoteric) dimensions of religion:
- Philosophers had attained the *ẓāhir* through human reason
- The Imams possessed the *bāṭin* through divine inspiration

This schema became both a defense against charges of antinomianism (the Ismailis insisted on full observance of the *ẓāhir*, including prayer and ritual law) and a claim to spiritual superiority over both ordinary Muslims and non-Ismaili philosophers.

Al-Sijistānī compared the relation of inner and outer to the relation of a conceptual meaning to its verbal expression — an analogy that echoed the contemporary Baghdad debate about logic vs. grammar.

## Development and Divergence

**Early phase** (al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥātim, al-Sijistānī): Plotinian Neoplatonism with the added "Command" level; radical apophatic theology

**Kirmānī turn**: Al-Kirmānī shifted toward [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]]'s system of ten celestial intellects, integrating Farābian cosmology into the Ismaili framework. He retained the radical transcendence theology but adopted a more systematic astronomy-based cosmology.

**Nasir Khusraw**: Working in the eleventh century after the Ismaili [[fatimid-caliphate|Fāṭimid caliphate]] had established itself in Egypt, Khusraw synthesized the tradition and composed the first systematic philosophical works in Persian. His best-known book is a travel account (*Safarnāma*); his philosophical works argued for harmony between Ismaili teachings and Hellenistic philosophy across a range of issues: creation, divine unity, physics, logic.

## Political Context

The Ismaili philosophical project was inseparable from the political success of the [[fatimid-caliphate|Fāṭimid caliphate]], which controlled Egypt from the early tenth to the later twelfth century and used it as a base for missionary work (*daʿwa*) throughout the Islamic world. The *dāʿīs* (proselytizers) who carried this mission were often the same figures who produced the philosophical texts. This gave Ismaili philosophy a missionary urgency absent from the more court-oriented [[miskawayh|Miskawayh]] or the Baghdad Peripatetics.

The Ismaili project ultimately failed: the lands of the [[buyid-dynasty|Būyids]] and Sāmānids were not converted to Ismaili belief, and the "Sunni revival" under the [[seljuk-empire|Seljuqs]] reversed Shiite political power across the eastern Islamic lands. Nāṣir Khusraw lived long enough to see this reversal.

## Historiography

Ismaili philosophy long suffered from inaccessibility of sources, since the Ismaili communities kept many texts secret. Modern scholarship has benefited from the Institute of Ismaili Studies (London), which has edited and translated many texts. There is ongoing debate about the relationship between the philosophical and the purely religious dimensions of the tradition, and about whether the Brethren of Purity were Ismailis.
