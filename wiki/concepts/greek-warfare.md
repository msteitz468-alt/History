# Greek Warfare

**Greek warfare** here covers Archaic and Classical land and sea conflict among the *poleis*
and their neighbours — not only the set-piece [[hoplite-warfare|hoplite]] clash, but
diplomacy, force composition, campaign logistics, naval power, finance, and the
fourth-century professionalization that bridges to Macedon. Detail on equipment and
phalanx mechanics lives at [[hoplite-warfare]] and [[phalanx]]; fleets at
[[greek-naval-warfare]] and [[trireme]]; pay and booty at [[financing-greek-warfare]];
hired troops at [[mercenaries-in-greek-classical]]. The principal multi-author synthesis
is [[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] Part I.

## Men are the city: army and *polis*

Hornblower (CHGRW I.2) centres the principle *andres gar polis* (“men are the city,”
Nicias in Sicily 413): the civic community defined as the totality of heavy-armed
citizens. Political and military categories mapped closely — *phylai* and phratries as
brigading units already in Homer (*Il.* 2.363) and in Thucydides’ Sicilian narrative for
both Athenians (Cleisthenic ten) and Syracusans (three Dorian tribes). Greek armies, more
than Roman ones, functioned as **miniature political entities** (“army as *polis*”),
electing commanders, voting, and calling leaders to account — evidence, Hornblower
argues, for the paramountcy of civic institutions and against the claim that war and
fighting were simply “basic” to Greek society. Continuity of that political culture
into Alexander’s Successors (Eumenes’ daily council “like some city ruling itself on
democratic principles,” Diod. 19.15) cuts against modern “ignoble warfare after 400”
narratives that underplay pre-400 mercenaries and post-400 citizen-mercenaries.

Greek armies showed a **marked lack of physical discipline** compared with Persian or
Roman harshness. At Athens desertion was a civil prosecution; corporal punishment of
citizens was demeaning (left to Scythian slave police); Spartan stick-discipline was
resented as treating free men like helots. Cohesion without Roman-style discipline
rested on a **hoplite ethos** solemnized in soldierly oaths and Tyrtaeus-style poetry —
shame at abandoning the man next in line (see [[hoplite-warfare]]).

## Interstate norms and the “agonistic” world

Hall (CHGRW I.4) rejects a pure “billiard-ball” model of fully formed *poleis* colliding
as autonomous units. Interstate protocols **predated** full *polis* formation: Linear B
from Mycenaean Thebes mentions a Lacedaemonian guest; Dark Age Aegean traffic required
regulatory frameworks; Achaea lacked *polis* governance until the fifth century after
earliest attested interstate transactions. Cross-border elite networks (intermarriage,
*xenia*) and free movement of professionals linked Greek and non-Greek peers — East
Greek mercenaries carved signatures into Rameses II’s statue at Abu Simbel (ML 7) in the
early sixth century.

Hall prefers to view war as the extreme of Burckhardt’s **“agonistic spirit”** — a
zero-sum prestige contest more demanding than bare reciprocity (“help friends, harm
enemies”). Neutrality was suspect (Solon allegedly criminalized civil-war neutrality;
Corcyreans and Hermocrates argue against it in Thucydides). Diplomacy included:
**proxenia** institutionalizing elite *xenia* at *polis* level (earliest known *proxenos*
Menecrates of Oeanthea, end seventh century); *symbolai* treaties for commercial legal
recourse; **heralds** (*keryx*) under Zeus/Hermes inviolability; third-party
**arbitration** for zero-sum disputes (Periander for Athens–Mytilene over Sigeum; Delphi
and amphictyonies as religious arbiters). Hall argues Greeks adopted both coinage and
diplomatic concepts from Lydians (heirs of Hittite diplomacy); archaic treaties
(*spondai*/*horkoi*) stood under divine tutelage.

Supracivic leagues (Panionion; Delphic Amphictyony with oath forbidding destruction of
fellow members’ *poleis*, starvation, or cutting water) **regulated rather than
prevented** conflict. Festival *hieromenia* and *ekecheiria* protected competitors and
host region without a total war ban. Major sanctuaries hosted competitive dedicatory
monuments and war trophies. Amity required special sworn effort and limited duration —
“amity and harmony were not the defining characteristics of international relations in
the archaic Greek world.”

Wheeler (CHGRW I.7) distinguishes **agonal** war “by the rules” from *polemos akēryktos*
or *aspondos* (“war without herald/truce”) allowing free rein to trickery and ferocity
(early-fifth-century Phocian–Thessalian and Athenian–Aeginetan wars as unlimited
examples). Homer introduced twin warrior models — Achilles (open force) vs Odysseus
(trickery) — a tension not uniquely Greek. Among major mainland *poleis*, limited
agonal practice channelled violence into a single clash and often guaranteed both sides’
political survival — war as *agōn* — but Wheeler rejects equating that ideal with full
military reality; the Peloponnesian War did not invent stratagem.

Gabrielsen (CHGRW I.8) rejects Weberian monopoly-of-legitimate-violence as a necessary
criterion of Greek statehood: archaic and classical Greece hosted multiple coexisting
**“violence-producing agencies”** — private raids and community-wide campaigns side by
side. Custom of reprisals (*sylē*, *rhysia agein*) meant private predation often
escalated into full communal war. Classical [[athens]] became the prime **monopolistic**
example (sea-power, hegemony, financial independence); most other *poleis* remained more
oligopolistic. See [[financing-greek-warfare]].

## Force types beyond the hoplite

Hoplites remained the dominant plains force, but CHGRW stresses a fuller spectrum
(Hunt I.5; Wheeler I.7):

- **Cavalry** history is contested (true cavalry outside Thessaly only from the sixth
  century vs continuous Dark Age use); classical Athenian cavalry peaked at ~1,000
  regulars plus 200 horse-archers (431). Cavalry served recon, harassment, flank
  protection, and pursuit — not shock against formed hoplites until Philip/Alexander.
- **Peltasts** (*peltē* crescent shield, javelins with throwing strap) excelled at
  ambush, recon, rough terrain; Iphicrates’ destruction of a Spartan *mora* near Corinth
  (390) showed decisive light-armed potential. Light infantry became a regular army
  component in the second half of the fifth century; unescorted hoplites were vulnerable
  (Spartolus 429; Aetolia 427; Sphacteria 425).
- **Archers**: composite bows effective vs individuals ~60 m, formations >150 m; fully
  armed hoplites mostly protected (est. ~1/10 arrows hitting a formation cause injury
  without shields). Contempt for “distant cowards” was real but not universal — “barbarian
  archers” appear on Athenian casualty lists; pre-artillery archers held off besiegers.
- **Slingers**: effective range ~200 m (could outdistance archers); low status yet
  specialist sources (Rhodes, Acarnania); Athens hired 700 Rhodian slingers for Sicily.
- **Naval and amphibious**: almost one-third of known archaic wars involved
  ship-transported troops; most Peloponnesian War land battles were against seaborne
  invaders; fleets carried substantial embarked infantry. See [[greek-naval-warfare]].

Force composition should be analysed by capacities × terrain × objectives × full social
costs (equipment, training time, professional maintenance) — not weapon lethality alone;
tradition and inertia often outweigh rational recalculation (Hunt, CHGRW I.5).

## Campaigning: logistics, season, battle and aftermath

Krentz (CHGRW I.6) dismantles the pure Mardonius/Herodotus agonal model of annual farmer
contests for status: campaigns were sequential decision processes by both sides. Daily
ration ~1 Attic *choinix* grain (~0.84 kg, ~2,800 cal) plus supplements to ~3,000 cal;
attested carry-days commonly three. Typical hoplite had a slave porter; pack animals and
wagons followed; merchants trailed armies. March rates: reasonable army maximum with
baggage ~26 km/day. Under threat, hoplites marched in a square with light troops and
baggage inside (Brasidas 423; Syracuse retreat 413). Camps prioritized natural security,
wood, water; sanctuaries ideal; fortification optional. Defenders’ “pass paradox”: all
southern Greek passes turnable by alternate routes or seaborne hoplites.

Campaign season was **not** limited to summer — grain, vines, and olives were vulnerable
in different seasons; winter operations are attested in the Archidamian War. Festival
truces protected pilgrims and host state, not all warfare. Greeks normally refused battle
when outnumbered beyond ~3:2; manliness and shame were at stake mainly in evenly matched
contests (Thuc. 5.101). Systematic permanent agricultural destruction was rare (Hanson),
but temporary harvest losses crippled; longest Attica invasion forty days.

By mid-fifth century, post-battle ritual crystallized: **tropaion** at the turn-point;
losers concede by requesting burial truce; victors strip corpses. Prisoner fates (Ducrey):
battles ~20% killed, ~24% enslaved; captured cities higher enslavement rates. Booty was
collected and redistributed (*dekate* to gods; *aristeiai*; Spartan *laphyropolai*).
Mantises were highly valued; unsolicited omens could stop campaigns. See also
[[hoplite-warfare]] on *machē ex homologou* and casualty estimates.

## Imperialism and the end of limited aims

From the Persian conflicts onward, Wheeler argues, **imperialism erased limited strategic
aims**: Greek cities could annihilate other Greeks; single battles were less decisive;
set-piece battles relatively rare 479–362; generalship shifted toward battle management
and stratagem. Fourth-century IR discourse proliferated *philia* and kinship diplomacy
(*syngeneia*), foreshadowing Hellenistic interpersonal-style interstate relations. Appeals
to **autonomy** became more vocal precisely when most *poleis* were dependent (King’s
Peace; Second Confederacy; League of Corinth). Decline of religious/consensual *nomoi*
as IR guarantee reflected sophistic challenge to convention (Melian Dialogue; Corcyra
*stasis*), not simply “less religion.”

Sicily was often the tactical “cutting edge”: Gelon of Syracuse (480) offered a
combined-arms force as possible precursor of Philip II’s army, yet democratic Syracuse
later struggled to fight in phalanx. **Mantinea 362** culminated classical tactics —
Epaminondas combined stratagem, massed left, coordinated cavalry/*hamippoi* — before
Philip II would “re-think” the phalanx (see [[phalanx]]).

## Phase: the fourth-century transformation (CAH VI / Garlan)

The fourth century transformed Greek warfare from the ritualized clash of amateur
**citizen [[hoplite-warfare|hoplites]]** into something more **professional, technical and
ruthless** — the military half of the changes that made the city-state obsolete and
prepared the way for Macedon. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 12e, Y. Garlan, with
the narrative chapters) traces the shift across institutions, tactics and technology.
Hornblower (CHGRW I.2) stresses **continuity** against “ignoble warfare after 400”
rhetoric; the material below remains the classic CAH framing of what *did* intensify.

### Professionalization and mercenaries

The defining development was the rise of the **mercenary (*misthophoros*)**. Cities and
satraps alike "were only too ready to entrust their overseas campaigns to mercenaries,"
who by mid-century "could be counted in tens of thousands." The glut had social roots —
poverty, exile, the [[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|Ten Thousand]] — and ideological
sanction: reforming philosophers (Hippodamus, the **Plato** of the *Republic*) commended a
**professional military caste**. Warfare spawned a **technical literature** (Xenophon's
cavalry manuals, the *Poliorcetica* of **Aeneas Tacticus** on siegecraft and treason) and
**professional instructors** for hire. The great commanders — Athens' **[[conon]],
Timotheus, Iphicrates, Chabrias, Chares** — became **specialist generals** who served their
own city or foreign "barbarian" paymasters by turns, occupying "an ambiguous position...
belonging at the same time inside and outside" the polis. See
[[mercenaries-in-greek-classical]].

### Combined arms and new tactics

Pitched hoplite battle remained important — and was itself revolutionized by
**[[epaminondas]]'s** deep oblique column at **[[battle-of-leuctra-371bce|Leuctra]]** —
but it became "only one element in a more complex strategy." Campaigns now aimed at
controlling **walled cities and fortified frontier zones**, demanding **combined-arms
forces** "integrated on the model of the human body": heavy hoplites, cavalry, archers, and
above all the mobile **light-armed peltasts** whose javelins, under **Iphicrates**,
destroyed a Spartan hoplite *mora* at **Lechaeum (390)** (see
[[corinthian-war-395bce]]). These are the components Philip of Macedon would weld into the
combined-arms army that conquered Greece and Persia.

### Siege technology: the catapult

The most consequential technical leap was in **siegecraft**. The **catapult** (the
arrow-shooting *katapeltikon*) "was invented at this time in **Syracuse**," among the
armaments assembled by **[[dionysius-i|Dionysius I]]** for his wars against
[[carthage|Carthage]] (c. 399) — "a new machine, unknown" before. Together with improved
siege towers and rams, artillery shifted the balance toward the **attacker of fortified
places**: the long-standing strategic problem (that Greek armies could ravage but rarely
*take* a walled city) began to dissolve. Hornblower notes that **Alexander's** later
ability to storm cities — where [[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]] could not — owed much to
"the largely fortuitous result of technological advance in places like **Sicily** and
Thessaly."

### Significance of the fourth-century phase

Fourth-century warfare is the bridge between the citizen phalanx and the professional
Macedonian war-machine. Mercenary professionalism, combined-arms tactics, the oblique
order, and siege artillery — developed piecemeal across Greece, Sicily and the Persian
service — were the toolkit that **Philip and Alexander** inherited and perfected. The
military transformation runs in parallel with the political one analysed in
[[greek-federalism]]: in both, the autonomous citizen polis gave way to larger, more
professionalized, ultimately **monarchical** structures.

## Historiography

CHGRW Part I is now the wiki’s primary multi-author frame for archaic–classical Greek
war as a whole (Hornblower, Hall, Hunt, Krentz, Wheeler, Strauss, Gabrielsen, van Wees,
Sekunda). Older CAH narrative volumes remain essential for chronology. Recurring debates
— agonal vs unlimited war, “hoplite revolution,” *othismos*, citizen decline after 400 —
are tracked at [[hoplite-revolution-debate]] and in the historiography sections of
[[hoplite-warfare]] and [[phalanx]]. Collection bias: Greco-Roman military history is
strong in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina; cross-check political-economic claims against
economic histories (Scheidel/Morris/Saller CEHGRW).
