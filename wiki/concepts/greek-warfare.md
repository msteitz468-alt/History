# Greek Warfare in the Fourth Century

## The eclipse of the amateur citizen-soldier

The fourth century transformed Greek warfare from the ritualized clash of amateur
**citizen [[hoplite-warfare|hoplites]]** into something more **professional, technical and
ruthless** — the military half of the changes that made the city-state obsolete and
prepared the way for Macedon. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 12e, Y. Garlan, with
the narrative chapters) traces the shift across institutions, tactics and technology.

## Professionalization and mercenaries

The defining development was the rise of the **mercenary (*misthophoros*)**. Cities and
satraps alike "were only too ready to entrust their overseas campaigns to mercenaries,"
who by mid-century "could be counted in tens of thousands." The glut had social roots —
poverty, exile, the [[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|Ten Thousand]] — and ideological
sanction: reforming philosophers (Hippodamus, the **Plato** of the *Republic*) commended a
**professional military caste**. Warfare spawned a **technical literature** (Xenophon's
cavalry manuals, the *Poliorcetica* of **Aeneas Tacticus** on siegecraft and treason) and
**professional instructors** for hire. The great commanders — Athens' **[[conon]],
Timotheus, Iphicrates, Chabrias, Chares** — became **specialist generals** who served their
own city or foreign "barbarian" paymasters by turns, occupying "an ambiguous position...
belonging at the same time inside and outside" the polis.

## Combined arms and new tactics

Pitched hoplite battle remained important — and was itself revolutionized by
**[[epaminondas]]'s** deep oblique column at **[[battle-of-leuctra-371bce|Leuctra]]** —
but it became "only one element in a more complex strategy." Campaigns now aimed at
controlling **walled cities and fortified frontier zones**, demanding **combined-arms
forces** "integrated on the model of the human body": heavy hoplites, cavalry, archers, and
above all the mobile **light-armed peltasts** whose javelins, under **Iphicrates**,
destroyed a Spartan hoplite *mora* at **Lechaeum (390)** (see
[[corinthian-war-395bce]]). These are the components Philip of Macedon would weld into the
combined-arms army that conquered Greece and Persia.

## Siege technology: the catapult

The most consequential technical leap was in **siegecraft**. The **catapult** (the
arrow-shooting *katapeltikon*) "was invented at this time in **Syracuse**," among the
armaments assembled by **[[dionysius-i|Dionysius I]]** for his wars against
[[carthage|Carthage]] (c. 399) — "a new machine, unknown" before. Together with improved
siege towers and rams, artillery shifted the balance toward the **attacker of fortified
places**: the long-standing strategic problem (that Greek armies could ravage but rarely
*take* a walled city) began to dissolve. Hornblower (Ch. 3) notes that **Alexander's**
later ability to storm cities — where [[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]] could not — owed much to
"the largely fortuitous result of technological advance in places like **Sicily** and
Thessaly."

## Significance

Fourth-century warfare is the bridge between the citizen phalanx and the professional
Macedonian war-machine. Mercenary professionalism, combined-arms tactics, the oblique
order, and siege artillery — developed piecemeal across Greece, Sicily and the Persian
service — were the toolkit that **Philip and Alexander** inherited and perfected. The
military transformation runs in parallel with the political one analysed in
[[greek-federalism]]: in both, the autonomous citizen polis gave way to larger, more
professionalized, ultimately **monarchical** structures.
