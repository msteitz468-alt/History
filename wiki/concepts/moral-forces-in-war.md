# Moral Forces in War (Clausewitz)

## Definition: What Clausewitz Means by "Moral"

When [[clausewitz-carl-von|Carl von Clausewitz]] uses the term "moral" (*moralische*) in [[sources/clausewitz-on-war-1832|*On War*]], he does not mean ethics or morality in the ordinary sense. He means psychological, emotional, and spiritual forces — the interior states of commanders, soldiers, and peoples that shape the conduct and outcome of war. As he puts it in Book Three, Chapter Three, "the moral elements are among the most important in war. They constitute the spirit that permeates war as a whole, and at an early stage they establish a close affinity with the will that moves and leads the whole mass of force, practically merging with it, since the will is itself a moral quantity."

This is a deliberate expansion of the domain of war theory beyond the material. Clausewitz explicitly attacks those who "in the old-fashioned way one lays down rules and principles in total disregard of moral values" — which he calls "paltry philosophy." He insists that moral factors "cannot be classified or counted. They have to be seen or felt." Yet because they resist academic taxonomy, earlier theorists had excluded them, and Clausewitz judged this a fundamental error.

## The Organic Unity of Physical and Moral

Clausewitz is emphatic that physical and moral factors cannot be cleanly separated: "The effects of physical and psychological factors form an organic whole which, unlike a metal alloy, is inseparable by chemical processes." In a celebrated image he writes that "the physical seem little more than the wooden hilt, while the moral factors are the precious metal, the real weapon, the finely-honed blade." Theory therefore cannot treat physical factors in isolation: "In formulating any rule concerning physical factors, the theorist must bear in mind the part that moral factors may play in it; otherwise he may be misled into making categorical statements that will be too timid and restricted, or else too sweeping and dogmatic."

History, he argues, is the primary evidence: it "provides the strongest proof of the importance of moral factors and their often incredible effect: this is the noblest and most solid nourishment that the mind of a general may draw from a study of the past."

## The Four Principal Moral Elements (Book Three, Chapter Four)

Clausewitz names "the principal moral elements" in Chapter Four with characteristic directness. **They are: the skill of the commander, the experience and courage of the troops, and their patriotic spirit.** He adds that "the relative value of each cannot be universally established; it is hard enough to discuss their potential, and even more difficult to weigh them against each other. The wisest course is not to underrate any of them — a temptation to which human judgment, being fickle, often succumbs."

He notes that different elements flourish in different terrain and operational conditions. Troops' national feeling — "enthusiasm, fanatical zeal, faith, and general temper" — is most apparent in mountain warfare "where every man, down to the individual soldier, is on his own." Efficiency, skill, and "tempered courage that welds the body of troops into a single mold will have their greatest scope in operations in open country." The commander's talents are given "greatest scope in rough hilly country."

## Military Virtue as a Distinct Moral Factor (Book Three, Chapter Five)

Chapter Five distinguishes "military virtue" (*Kriegertugend*) from simple bravery or patriotic enthusiasm. "Military virtues should not be confused with simple bravery, and still less with enthusiasm for a cause. Bravery is obviously a necessary component. But just as bravery, which is part of the natural make-up of a man's character, can be developed in a soldier — a member of an organization — it must develop differently in him than in other men."

Clausewitz defines the army that possesses true military spirit: "An army that maintains its cohesion under the most murderous fire; that cannot be shaken by imaginary fears and resists well-founded ones with all its might; that, proud of its victories, will not lose the strength to obey orders and its respect and trust for its officers even in defeat; whose physical power, like the muscles of an athlete, has been steeled by training in privation and effort… a force that regards such efforts as a means to victory rather than a curse on its cause… such an army is imbued with the true military spirit."

Professional pride (*esprit de corps*) binds the components of military virtue together: "Professional pride is the bond between the various natural forces that activate the military virtues; in the context of this professional pride they crystallize more readily." Yet the spirit cannot be manufactured by discipline alone: "grim severity and iron discipline may be able to preserve the military virtues of a unit, but it cannot create them." Only "a series of victorious wars" and "frequent exertions of the army to the utmost limits of its strength" can plant the seed that grows into true military virtue.

## Why Moral Factors Resist Positive Theory

Clausewitz offers a methodological argument as well as a substantive one. Theory cannot reduce moral phenomena to rules because they "will not yield to academic wisdom." He rejects the approach of cataloguing moral elements in abstract: "This method, however, all too easily leads to platitudes, while the genuine spirit of inquiry soon evaporates." Rather, the theorist should point toward these factors, demonstrate their importance, and trust the reader's judgment and experience to do the rest. The correct role of theory is to "expand its horizon, and simply by establishing this point of view would condemn in advance anyone who sought to base an analysis on material factors alone."

## Connection to the Trinity

The moral forces map directly onto the [[remarkable-trinity]] that Clausewitz develops elsewhere in *On War*. The trinity's three poles — primordial passion/violence, the play of chance/probability, and rational policy — correspond to the people, the army and commander, and the government respectively. The moral factors discussed in Book Three are the psychological and spiritual content of the first two poles: the passion of the people and the courage, skill, and spirit of the army. [[friction-in-war]] also belongs to this framework, since friction operates precisely on the moral layer — wearing down courage, eroding confidence, magnifying fear.

[[military-genius]] (Book One, Chapter Three) is Clausewitz's treatment of the supreme moral quality at the command level: the capacity to function coherently when all moral forces are under maximum stress.
