# Empire of Liberty

The **empire of liberty** was Thomas Jefferson's influential phrase for an expansive American republic that would spread liberty, self-government, and enlightenment across the continent (and potentially beyond), in explicit contrast to European monarchical empires of domination. In the framing of the 2023 volume *The Early Imperial Republic*, it functioned as a legitimating ideology for what was in practice a racialized, removalist, and slavery-expanding imperial project.

## Definition, Emic Use, and Contradictions

Jefferson and other founders described the United States as a "rising empire" (Washington) or "potent empire" that would outstrip Persia and Rome (Hutchins 1784), yet one founded on liberty rather than conquest. Plans for "federative empire" envisioned dependent or associated polities for Native and Black populations that would ostensibly preserve equality rhetoric while maintaining white supremacy at the core. The concept thus enabled square of revolutionary ideals with dispossession and enslavement.

## Treatment in the Source Volume

The editors and contributors (especially Guyatt Ch. 11) trace how fluid late-18th-century notions of empire allowed white Americans to embrace the term more readily than later generations. Federative empire offered a "liberal imperialism" solution to the problem of race: removal or colonization as the price of "liberty" for the republic proper. Black critics of the colonization movement are positioned as "among the first genuinely anti-imperial thinkers in American history."

The volume shows the phrase's deployment alongside concrete imperial practices (boundary lines, land claims, slave empire projection) that belied the liberty claim for those displaced or enslaved.

## Critiques and Limitations

The "empire of liberty" has been celebrated in exceptionalist historiography and critiqued as ideological cover (e.g., in studies of Indian removal, slavery expansion, and Manifest Destiny). The 2023 volume supplies a synthetic early-republic focus that reconnects the slogan to inherited imperial repertoires rather than treating it as purely aspirational or exceptional.
