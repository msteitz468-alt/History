# Slavery in Early Christianity

A focused treatment of [[slavery|ancient slavery]] as it operated within the world of early
Christianity — the early Roman Empire of the first through fifth centuries CE — and of how
the new movement reproduced, used, and occasionally questioned the institution. The central
scholarly finding, associated above all with **Jennifer Glancy**, is that the rise of
Christianity did **not** soften the ancient view of the slave as a body: "Christian authors
nonetheless employ conventions and clichés that construct an image of the slave body as
vulnerable to invasion and abuse."

## Scope: What the Institution Was

- **Scale**: enslaved persons made up perhaps 15–25% of the population of Roman Italy and
  10–20% across the empire (Scheidel); slavery was ubiquitous and economically pervasive —
  "no occupation in Roman society was closed to slaves" (Bradley).
- **Status as body, not person**: slaves were legally property, presumed to lack *logos*
  (reason); the Greek *doulos* was interchanged with *sōma* (body), and slaves of any age
  were called *pais* ("child"). Orlando Patterson's "**social death**" captures their
  alienation from birth, kin, and legitimate social order.
- **Sexual vulnerability**: both male and female slaves were sexually available to owners as
  a "commonplace in Greco-Roman literature from Homer on" (Finley); this is inseparable from
  the analysis of [[gender-history|gender]].
- **Truth and torture**: a slave's testimony was held credible only under torture (*basanos*),
  while a free person could lie even under torture — duBois's *Torture and Truth* ties the
  slave body to a "site of truth" extracted by violence. This is the backdrop against which
  the [[enslaved-truth-teller|uncoerced enslaved truth-teller]] is striking.

## The Christian Question (contested)

- **Continuity**: the early church absorbed slaveholding wholesale; enslaved people belonged
  to Christian households and the institution persisted unbroken into late antiquity.
- **Tension**: the baptismal formula of Galatians 3:28 ("no longer slave or free … all one
  in Christ") led some communities and some scholars to see a subversive,
  emancipatory potential — performed "new Christian identities within an egalitarian
  community" — though it never abolished the institution.
- **Source problem**: almost no texts were written by the enslaved; literary and even
  archaeological evidence (e.g. slaves on funerary monuments, included to signal an owner's
  wealth) "advance arguments that reinforce certain social hierarchies" (Shaner) rather than
  offering windows onto enslaved reality.

## Limitations and Critique

The category risks reading later (especially modern Atlantic) slavery into the ancient
world; ancient slavery was not racialized in the modern sense, manumission was common, and
the slave/free line was permeable. Yet, as [[cobb-slavery-gender-luke-acts-2019|Cobb]]
stresses, the dehumanizing logic — slave-as-body, truth-by-torture — was real and shaped
the texts of early Christianity, including [[luke-acts|Luke-Acts]].
