# Elite Women and Queenship in Merovingian Gaul

**The exceptional, property-based power of Merovingian queens and aristocratic women — and the
hostile male sources that record it.** Edward James's chapter (Oxford Handbook, Ch. 11) examines elite
women as regents, property-holders, and convent founders, in a society where reigning royal widows
were, in the words of the *Life of Balthild*, "sublime in power." James cautions that "the proper
history of elite women in the Merovingian world can probably never be written" — the evidence is
thin and male-authored (Gregory's *Histories* name 1,346 people, only 14.6% women).

## Definition and Scope

The historiographical debate (Wemple, Schulenburg, McNamara) holds that Merovingian elite women
enjoyed more power than their Carolingian successors or Roman predecessors, the key being **control of
property**. Burgundofara's will (c. late 630s) shows the sources of female wealth — paternal
portions, testamentary half-shares, exchanged vineyards — nearly all donated to Faremoûtiers, freeing
her slaves "by tablet or by letter." Marculf's Formulary (2.12) records a father making his daughter
"equal and legitimate heir," calling the exclusion of daughters "an ancient but impious custom"; James
argues [[salic-law|Salic Law]]'s inheritance restriction applied only to *terra Salica*, not allodial
land. A woman of childbearing age carried triple wergeld (see [[gender-in-merovingian-gaul]]).

## Queens as Regents

The defining Merovingian phenomenon was the **reigning royal widow as regent** — a function James
attributes to Frankish dynastic loyalty, which accepted child kings rather than an adult from a new
line.

- **[[brunhild|Brunhild]]**: widowed in 575, exiled, briefly married to Chilperic's son Merovech,
  she fought back across four decades — regent for Childebert II, then for Theudebert II and Theuderic
  II — before Chlothar II had her killed in 613. Hostile sources (Fredegar, Jonas, the *Life of
  Desiderius*) were written after her death; in 581 she was rebuffed with: "Stand back, woman! It
  should be enough that you held regal power when your husband was alive."
- **[[fredegund|Fredegund]]**: after Chilperic's murder (584) she fled "with treasure into the
  cathedral of Paris… to negotiate for her future," gained Guntram's protection, and (per Gregory)
  may have had Bishop Praetextatus of Rouen stabbed during Mass (586). She declared her resources her
  own: "Everything you see belongs to me… None of it has been taken from the public treasury."
- **Balthild**: possibly Anglo-Saxon (the *Liber Historiae Francorum* calls her "a girl from the
  Saxon nobility"), married Clovis II, and as regent (658–c. 664) founded Chelles and Corbie,
  reformed churches, and forbade the Christian slave trade, before being "forced into the monastery
  by the ambitious Ebroin." The Treaty of Andelot (587) shows how queens held cities and revenues:
  Galswinth's *Morgengabe* (Bordeaux, Limoges, Cahors) passed to Brunhild "by the decree of King
  Guntram and with the agreement of the Franks."

## Convents as Power Bases — and Their Limits

Convents let women control property (Fara) and lead men (abbesses of double monasteries). **Radegund**
fled [[chlothar-ii|Chlothar I]], founded a community at Poitiers, secured Caesarius's *Rule for Nuns*
and a relic of the Holy Cross from Constantinople (see [[radegund-of-poitiers]]). But the **Poitiers
nuns' revolt (589)** — forty nuns, led by the royal Chlothild ("I am a queen, the daughter of one king
and the niece of another!"), leaving over poor food and broken Rule — exposed the limits: the dispute
turned partly on "the unwritten rule that royal nuns should retain their superior status" (Harrison).
Gertrude of Nivelles refused Dagobert's marriage match (her mother Ida cutting a tonsure into her
hair); Sadalberga was forced to marry, bore five children, then founded a monastery at Laon — showing
that entering religion also "engaged women in family property strategies beyond their control."

## Historiography

James's chapter sits between the optimistic "women's power" thesis and the constraints of patriarchal,
hagiographically-mediated evidence. It connects to [[merovingian-polity]] (queens' limited treasury
access), [[merovingian-monasticism]] (convents and the cult of saints), and [[gender-in-merovingian-gaul]]
(the seventh-century "age of great abbesses").
