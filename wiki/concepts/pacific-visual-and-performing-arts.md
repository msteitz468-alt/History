## Definition and Scope

Pacific visual and performing arts encompasses the material, architectural, musical,
choreographic, and oral artistic traditions of Oceania from prehistory to the present.
The framework is developed by Adrienne Kaeppler, who addresses a foundational
problem before surveying content: most Pacific Island cultures lack an Indigenous word
or concept for "art" as a separate category, because traditional Pacific arts are "not
separated from each other or from daily or ceremonial life."

Kaeppler's working definition is:

> "Paciﬁc arts encompass cultural forms that result from creative processes that use or
> handle with skill words, sounds, movements, materials, scents, or spaces in such a way
> that they formalize the non-formal."

Aesthetics is correspondingly defined as "culturally speciﬁc, evaluative ways of thinking
about such cultural forms." Two key operative concepts are **skill** (without which the
formalization cannot occur) and **indirectness** (hidden or veiled meanings "unravelled
layer by layer until the metaphors on which they are based can be understood"). An
object, ceremony, or performance cannot be understood in isolation; it must be read
within its social and cosmological context.

Kaeppler organises Pacific arts into five overlapping categories: (1) organisation of
space and architecture; (2) sculpture and carving traditions; (3) fibre and textile arts;
(4) body ornamentation and scent; (5) oral, musical, and movement arts.

## The Collection of Pacific Art: History and Bias

### Contact-Era Collections (18th–19th centuries)
The earliest datable Pacific objects are the approximately 2,000 pieces collected during
James Cook's three voyages (1768–1780), received into Europe as "artificial curiosities"
(objects made by people) distinct from "natural curiosities" (things made by God):
"The carving was 'singular', the cloth made of bark 'curious', the incising 'ingenious'."
Subsequent eighteenth-century collections survive from Henry Wilson and George Keate
(Palau, 1781; now British Museum), George Vancouver (1791–5), Alessandro Malaspina
(1789–94; Museo de América, Madrid), and Bougainville (1766–9).

Russian expeditions (Krusenstern, Kotzebue, Bellingshausen, 1803–21) deposited
collections in St Petersburg. Whaling voyages (from 1801) brought material to US
maritime museums. The US Exploring Expedition under Charles Wilkes (1838–42)
assembled a large collection now primarily in the Smithsonian Institution. London
Missionary Society collections are in the British Museum; American Board of
Commissioners for Foreign Missions collections are in the Bishop Museum, Honolulu.

### Late 19th–20th Century: Museums and Anthropology
German expeditions and trading firms (Godeffroy, Hamburgische Wissenschaftliche
Stiftung 1908–10 aboard the *Peiho*) produced at least thirty-one published volumes
and 15,000 objects, concentrating knowledge of Micronesia and Melanesia/New Guinea.
The Hamburg-based Godeffroy Museum focused on Fiji and Samoa (most material now
in Leipzig); Krämer's Samoa collections are in Berlin.

European armchair collectors (Ratton, Oldman, Fuller, Beasley, Hooper) traded Pacific
objects without ever visiting the region; their acquisitions are now scattered across
British, European, Australasian, American, and Japanese museum collections.

In their new contexts, eighteenth- and nineteenth-century objects "ﬂoundered without
theoretical frames, ﬁnding their ways to cabinets of curiosities, natural history
collections, and curio shops, waiting to be 'discovered' another time by those who
might regard them with more dignity and honour."

During the twentieth century, Bishop Museum expeditions (Bayard Dominick
Expeditions; Mangareva Expedition) generated important scholarly monographs alongside
collections. AB Lewis collected some 30,000 Melanesian objects (1909–13), now in the
Field Museum, Chicago.

### Exhibitions and Repatriation
The first major international exhibition of Pacific art was "Arts of the South Seas,"
Museum of Modern Art, New York (1946). Large-scale comprehensive shows followed:
National Gallery of Art, Washington DC (1979); "Oceania," Royal Academy, London and
Quai Branly, Paris (2018–19). Repatriation efforts have achieved success in Hawai'i,
Aotearoa New Zealand, and French Polynesia, where objects "are treated as historical
valuables to be admired by visitors and venerated by their makers' descendants."

## Arts by Category

### Ceremonial Sites, Monuments, and Architecture

Pacific architecture and spatial organisation embed social, cosmological, and political
meaning. Three broad regional patterns can be distinguished.

**Micronesia.** The Mariana Islands contain latte sites (from c. 1000 CE): double rows
of megalithic columns with hemispherical capstones as bases for large wooden
structures. The most dramatic complex is **Nan Madol** on Pohnpei: ninety-two natural
and artificial islets in a shallow lagoon, constructed of basalt boulders and prismatic
basalt columns quarried inland and ferried by sea, laid in "headers and stretchers"
stacking. At its zenith (1000–1500 CE) it housed approximately 1,000 people and served
as the seat of the Sau Deleur dynasty — an 81-hectare political and religious centre.
Its most impressive islet, Nan Dauwas, holds a royal tomb with retaining walls 7.5
metres high and a seawall 4.5 metres high and 10.5 metres thick, oriented east–west for
navigation. Nan Madol is now a UNESCO heritage site. A related complex, Lelu
(c. 1200–1400 CE), lies 550 kilometres east on Kosrae.

**New Guinea / Melanesia.** Large ceremonial men's houses dominated the landscape,
incorporating the geography of mountains and rivers. Huge masks and ritual objects —
made to spectacular effect and then discarded — characterised the Papuan Gulf area
(documented by F.E. Williams in the early twentieth century; collections in the Field
Museum, Chicago, and German museums). The A-frame architectural form persists in
community buildings including the Papua New Guinea Parliament building.

**Polynesia.** Spatial ordering encodes social hierarchy and cosmology. In Fiji, villages
divide between chiefly seaward areas and common landward areas, with houses
replicating this orientation. In Tonga, elevated rectangular stone tombs mark historic
gravesites of the Tu'i Tonga, while chiefs' oval houses feature elaborate rafter lashings
using coconut-fibre sennit in two colours; high-ranking lineages lived inland, lesser
ones near the sea. In Samoa, villages are oriented around the malae (village green) with
chief's houses raised on earth and stone platforms; mounds with multiple rays relate to
gods including Tagaloa and the octopus god Fe'e. In Hawai'i, religious sites ranged from
simple fishing shrines to elaborate high-walled heiau, often built at hilltops and oriented
parallel or perpendicular to the shoreline, with differentiated structures for offerings
(lele), oracle ceremonies (anu'u), drumming (hale pahu), and sacred images.

**Maori meeting houses (wharenui), Aotearoa New Zealand.** The Māori meeting house
is the most elaborated example of architecture as cosmological statement. The house is
simultaneously a model of the cosmos, an embodiment of an ancestor, and a genealogical
map. Its ridgepole (kaho) represents the ancestor's spine; painted rafters (heke) represent
ribs; bargeboards (maihi) represent arms and fingers; the apex figure (tekoteko) represents
the ancestor's head. Carved wall panels (poupou) represent more recent ancestors; plaited
panels (tukutuku) alternate with them. The right side of the house is tapu, associated with
death and visitors; the left side is noa, associated with life and local people.

> "Upon crossing this sacred boundary into the house, marked by the doorway lintel, one
> enters into a horizontally and vertically ordered space conceptualized as a model of the
> cosmos and a metaphor of the history and embodiment of the ancestors of the group."

### Sculpture and Carving

Human sculpture was most important in Central and East Polynesia, largely absent
elsewhere. Stone sculptures include the Easter Island moai and medium-sized Marquesan
temple figures. Wood sculptures ranged in scale from large Marquesan and Hawaiian
temple images to medium-sized figures in Tonga, Fiji, Aotearoa New Zealand, Cook
Islands, and Austral Islands, and small figures in Easter Island, Hawai'i, and New Guinea.

Sculptures were dynamically posed (bent knees, ready to spring), sometimes
individualized with incised designs representing tattoo or body paint, and activated by
attaching feathers and directing sung prayers toward them. "Others could be deactivated
by removing their eyes."

Rapa Nui produced sculptures of barkcloth-covered plant structures; only five examples
survive, two in the Peabody Museum at Harvard. In the Society Islands, coconut-fibre
images (to'o) outranked wooden ones (ti'i) because they were activated by red feathers;
in Hawai'i, the most important images were basketry figures covered in red feathers.

Canoes were in some areas considered the epitome of artfulness: Māori war canoes
carried intricately carved prow and stern pieces with relief-carved washstrakes; Society
Island canoes bore elevated endpieces with mounted human sculpture. Large
double-hulled oceangoing vessels enabled long-distance voyaging and settlement.

### Fibre and Textile Arts

**Barkcloth (tapa).** Made from the beaten inner bark of paper mulberry (*Broussonetia
papyrifera*), breadfruit, banyan, or in Hawai'i endemic *Pipturus*, barkcloth was among
the most significant Pacific media. Its uses reached high points in Melanesia and
Polynesia. In New Britain, huge barkcloth masks and sculptures appeared in spectacular
nocturnal ritual performances. In Polynesia, barkcloth was categorised as a "valuable"
with specific terms in each island group (iyau in Fiji, koloa in Tonga, tōga in Samoa)
distinguishing it from food and men's products; its fabrication was women's work but
the product was sacred to men, women, and gods alike.

Barkcloth is intimately associated with presentation aesthetics that transform
two-dimensional cloth into three-dimensional performance:

> "In Fiji, a chief presented himself to a higher chief clothed in hundreds of feet of narrow
> barkcloth and disrobed, either by spinning to unravel wrapped barkcloth or by dropping
> a huge looped barkcloth dress as an aesthetic gesture in honour of the receiving chief."

Tongan barkcloth (*ngatu*) could be as large as 5 by 50 metres, used as pathways,
presentation pieces, and to surround royal tombs as three-dimensional enclosures.
The Cook Islands staff god from Rarotonga (British Museum) — roughly 3.5 metres long —
is wrapped in a large bale of barkcloth embellished with red feathers and pearlshell;
it was the barkcloth wrapping, not the carved wood, that made it sacred.

**Fine mats.** In West Polynesia especially, named and mana-imbued fine mats plaited
from pandanus — sometimes thirty strips per inch — functioned as the highest-prestige
valuables, evaluated by fineness, colour, age, and the occasions and persons associated
with them, and inherited across generations. In Samoa the tradition continues into the
twenty-first century.

**Loom weaving (Micronesia).** Backstrap and bench looms were used in Kosrae,
Pohnpei, and the Marshall Islands. Fine banana-fibre belts from Pohnpei and Kosrae
sometimes numbered thirty warps per centimetre. Marshall Islands women produced
pandanus-leaf dress mats worn in pairs. In Kiribati, intricately woven coconut-fibre
armour and pufferfish helmets were made.

**Featherwork (Hawai'i).** Hawaiian feathered cloaks, capes, and helmets were "visual
objectiﬁcations of social inequality," worn by male chiefs in dangerous or sacred
situations. Primary red feathers came from the honeycreeper ('i'iwi); rare yellow feathers
were plucked from birds (which were then released). Feathered god figures and the
feathered kahili (standards) signalled the presence of ranked individuals. A feathered
sash carried the right to rule.

**Coconut-fibre sennit.** Made by men and manipulated by religious specialists through
braiding, twisting, and knotting, sennit carried aesthetic and sacred weight across
Polynesia: in Tonga it formed the complex rafter lashings of chiefs' houses; in the Society
Islands it produced the most important divine images (to'o).

### Body Ornamentation and Scent

Tattooing and body painting were most elaborated in the Marquesas, Aotearoa New
Zealand, Rapa Nui, Samoa, and Hawai'i; cicatrization was found in the Solomons.
Permanent body decoration was associated with rank, status, and genealogy, most
extensively applied to men but also used by women.

Ornaments were made of whale ivory, turtleshell, greenstone, dog teeth, boar tusks,
land shells, sea shells, and porpoise teeth, worn on necks, arms, legs, and ears.
Feathered headdresses were characteristic chiefly markers in Hawai'i, the Society
Islands, the Australs, Samoa, Tonga, and the Marquesas.

### Oral, Musical, and Movement Arts

Oral art — poetry, oratory, genealogical recitation, prayer, historical accounts — was
"a most important universal art in the Paciﬁc." Poetic texts were traditionally intoned
within a narrow melodic range; polyphony occurred, often as drone-plus-melody, and
occasionally in as many as six parts. Musical instruments (log idiophones, drums, rattles)
were works of art in themselves.

The introduction of 'ukulele and guitar transformed musical conceptualisation and
generated pan-Pacific pop; reggae from Jamaica added further dimensions in the final
decades of the twentieth century.

In Polynesia, dance is a "stylized visual accompaniment to poetry: performers usually
face an audience and tell a story by alluding to selected words of the text with movements
of the hands and arms, while the legs and body serve mainly for rhythm and keeping time."
In Melanesia/New Guinea, performances are more ritually based, often associated with
male initiation, performed in inward-facing circles or across ritual spaces.

Hawaiian hula demonstrates the depth of continuous transmission: Mary Kawena Pukui,
"totally immersed in hula traditions evolved from the eighteenth century, composed a
hula in the 1950s in honour of the snow goddess Poli'ahu, which has passed to her
descendants."

## Prehistoric Foundation: The Lapita Cultural Complex

The earliest datable artistic tradition across much of the Pacific is the **Lapita cultural
complex**, an archaeologically defined tradition characterised by earthenware ceramics
with a distinctive decorative system. Originating in the Bismarck Archipelago (New
Ireland, New Britain), Lapita spread along the coast of New Guinea, through much of
Melanesia, and into Polynesia. It "is the most important prehistoric cultural tradition
for understanding the prehistory of art in much of the Paciﬁc." Over time, ancestral
Lapita cultures diversified into the historic complexes now grouped as Melanesia,
Micronesia, and Polynesia.

## Change, Continuity, and Contemporary Practice

Kaeppler resists any simple narrative of decline or replacement. Some Pacific creations
were meant to endure (Polynesian heirloom sculptures and fine mats); others were made
to be used once and discarded (New Guinea ritual masks). Where practices have lain
dormant — barkcloth production on some islands — revival has occurred widely in the
contemporary Pacific.

Contemporary artists "have continued and recycled many artistic traditions and have
added new ones." Belau (Palau) meeting-house aesthetics find expression in tourist
carvings; contemporary Hawaiian abstract sculptures combine Hawaiian religious
concepts with Western aesthetic frameworks; contemporary Māori carving transforms
traditional genealogical themes into new forms. In all cases: "These new works depend
on knowledge of the traditional aesthetic systems in which the artists have immersed
themselves to create new forms based on their own backgrounds and experiences."

Dance, once inseparable from specialist knowledge of poetic metaphor, has in many
communities shifted toward pantomime as performers address tourist or diaspora
audiences who do not share the language. This shift is itself a historical process, not
a simple loss.

## Historiography

**Problem of the concept "art."** Kaeppler's opening move — confronting the
inapplicability of the Western "art" category to Pacific cultures that embed creative
practice in daily and ceremonial life — is itself a historiographical position. It situates
the chapter within a broader critique of the imposition of Western aesthetic taxonomies
on non-Western material culture.

**Source base.** Europeanised recorded history of Pacific art begins with voyage journals
and illustrations (Cook, Bougainville, Malaspina). The earliest identifiable corpus of
material art objects is the Cook voyage collection (c. 2,000 pieces). Archaeological
evidence provides the longer chronology, especially for the Lapita tradition. Ethnographic
fieldwork from the late nineteenth century onward supplies contextual knowledge.

**Museum bias.** Collections have been assembled primarily in Britain, France, Germany,
and the United States. The repatriation movement beginning in the late twentieth century
has begun to address maldistribution, with successes in Hawai'i, New Zealand, and
French Polynesia. The shift from "cabinet of curiosities" to "art object" to "cultural
patrimony" tracks the changing disciplinary and political frameworks applied to these
objects.

**Key synthetic works.** Kaeppler, Kaufmann, and Newton, *L'art océanien* (1993/1994/
1997, in three languages); Kaeppler and Love, *Australia and the Pacific Islands* (1998);
Kaeppler, *The Pacific Arts of Polynesia and Micronesia* (Oxford UP, 2008); Roger Neich,
*Painted Histories* (1994) and *Carved Histories* (2001) on Māori visual art.
