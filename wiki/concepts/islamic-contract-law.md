Islamic contract law (the contractual branch of muʿāmalāt, meaning dealings between persons) is the body of [[fiqh]] governing all forms of binding and non-binding legal transactions: sale, lease and hire, partnership, agency, deposit, loan, pledge, guaranty, assignment of debt, and gift. Unlike Western legal systems, [[sharia]] does not treat "contract" as a separate doctrinal category; instead, contractual principles emerge implicitly from juristic discussions of specific transaction types. Source: [[hallaq-sharia-2009]], chapter 7.

## Constitutive Elements of Contracts

Every contract (ʿaqd) is formed by three essential elements (arkān; sing. rukn): (a) the **parties**, (b) the **form** (ṣīgha) of offer and acceptance, and (c) the **object** (subject-matter). The Ḥanafites held form alone to be the essential element since it presupposes the other two.

### Parties (Contracting Capacity)

A valid contracting party must be:
- Of major age (bāligh) — having reached physical and mental maturity
- Possessed of rushd — the capacity to behave responsibly in one's own interest
- Not subject to interdiction (ḥajr) for insanity, minority, or bankruptcy

Minors and the insane require a guardian except for discerning minors (mumayyiz), who may receive gifts and benefit from a waqf.

### Offer and Acceptance (Ījāb and Qabūl)

Central to all contracts is **riḍā** — "wholehearted consent without any trace of coercion whatsoever." Offer and acceptance may be expressed through spoken or written language, signs (including those of the mute), or silent interaction (muʿāṭāt — action without verbal exchange). The Mālikites accept silent interaction even when parties are neither mute nor deaf. Exception: the marriage contract, which requires explicit contractual language (zawwaja / nakaḥa).

The majority doctrine requires offer and acceptance in the same session (majlis). The Mālikite Ibn Rushd (the Grandfather) uniquely held that once an offer is made it cannot be withdrawn unilaterally — only non-acceptance renders it void.

Intent (niyya) is the primary criterion of a contract's meaning and effect. The Ḥanbalites give intent the broadest scope, treating language as the conduit of meaning rather than its determinant.

### Object (Locus of Contract)

The object must generally: (a) be in existence at conclusion (except salam); (b) be lawful (wine, pork, insects are excluded); (c) be owned by the seller; (d) be capable of immediate delivery; and (e) be known with reasonable specificity to both parties.

Two doctrinal hazards invalidate contracts:
- **Gharar**: ontological uncertainty about the very existence of the object ("buying pearls still in the sea")
- **Jahāla**: epistemic lack of knowledge of the object's characteristics

Minor uncertainty (gharar yasīr) is permissible; major uncertainty (gharar fāḥish) voids the contract. The benchmark is whether uncertainty "has the potential to produce discord and dispute (mufḍī ilā al-nizāʿ)."

## Typology of Contracts

**By bindingness:**
- **Lāzim** (binding): neither party may annul without the other's consent — sale, salam, rent/hire (ijāra), agricultural leases
- **Jāʾiz** (non-binding): may be annulled unilaterally — partnership (sharika), agency (wakāla), loan (qarḍ), deposit (wadīʿa)
- **Mixed**: binding on one party but not the other — guaranty (kafāla)

**By monetary character:**
- **Mālī** (pecuniary): gifts, sleeping partnerships (muḍāraba), all types of sale
- **Ghayr mālī** (non-pecuniary): agency, suretyship
- **Quasi-pecuniary**: marriage (involves a consideration from one party)

**By exchange structure:**
- **ʿUqūd muʿāwaḍa** (commutative/exchange contracts): consideration required from both parties
- **ʿUqūd tabarruʿāt** (donative contracts): no reciprocal consideration; lesser degree of certainty required

## The Prohibition of Ribā (Usury/Interest)

The ribā prohibition is one of the foundational principles of Islamic commercial law. Any contractual condition that entails an added benefit in a loan transaction "amounts to ribā." The juristic rule is stated through the qar (loan) doctrine: "it is of the essence that the contract not be predicated upon garnering an added benefit — this amounting to ribā. Yet, since qar represents a commended pious activity involving charitable works, the borrower may voluntarily elect to return the borrowed value plus an additional sum."

Ribā extends to sales: the exchange of the same genus in unequal amounts or weights — e.g., "selling one gold coin for two gold coins" — is prohibited. Stipulations involving usury in a contract of sale invalidate the entire contract.

The ribā prohibition directly shaped Islamic financial instruments: the muḍāraba (sleeping partnership) and musharaka (equity partnership) emerged as licit alternatives to interest-bearing lending, since profit-sharing involves genuine risk and does not guarantee a fixed return to the capital provider.

## Key Contract Types

### Sales (Buyūʿ)

Together with marriage contracts, commutative contracts are regarded as "the 'pillars' on which the social order rests." Standard sale requires:
- Immediate delivery capability (object in seller's possession, free of encumbrances)
- Object must be known with specificity (genus, species, type, quantity)
- Price may be anything that legally qualifies as an object of sale
- Form concluded within one contractual session (majlis al-bayʿ)

**Options (Khiyārāt):** Several rescission rights exist — khiyār al-majlis (option during the session), khiyār al-shart (stipulated option period), khiyār al-ʿayb (option for defects), khiyār al-ghaban (option for excessive price deviation), khiyār al-tadlīs (option for fraud).

**Salam contracts:** Price paid immediately for future delivery of a non-existent object. Must specify exact characteristics, delivery time, and be for commonly available goods. Permitted as an exception to the existence requirement.

### Partnerships (Sharikāt)

Two broad categories: **sharikat milk** (joint ownership, not contractual — arising e.g. from co-inheritance) and **sharikat ʿaqd** (contractual partnership, always jāʾiz).

Key contractual partnership types:
- **Sharikat mufāwaḍa**: fully equal partnership in capital, labor, profits, and mutual agency/suretyship (Ḥanafites unique in requiring kafāla between partners by operation of law)
- **Sharikat ʿInān**: unequal partnership; all schools accept
- **Muḍāraba** (sleeping partnership): capital from one party (rabb al-māl), labor from another (ʿāmil); profit divided by agreed ratio; loss falls on capital alone unless agent is negligent. Permitted by istiḥsān despite violating qiyās (hired labor must have a determined fee).
- **Sharikat amwāl**: financial partnership; capital must be naqd (gold/silver coin in majority view)
- **Sharikat abdān**: labor partnership without capital
- **Sharikat wujūh**: partnership for purchase with delayed payment, repaid from profits

Profits in all partnerships must be stipulated as a percentage, not an absolute sum; vagueness invalidates the contract.

### Hire and Lease (Ijāra)

A lāzim commutative contract covering both rent of objects and hire of labor/animals. Locus is the usufruct (manfaʿa), not the substance (ʿayn). The fee must be specified in advance; customary usage defines the scope of services. Termination: by exhaustion of time-limit or completion of work, perishing of object, or mutual agreement (iqāla). The Ḥanafites uniquely deem ijāra terminated by either party's death.

**Exclusive (khāṣṣ) vs. common (mushtarak) hired persons:** exclusive employees work for one employer (duration specifies their commitment); common workers serve multiple hirers (work must be precisely specified).

### Guaranty and Suretyship (Kafāla/Ḍamān)

Kafāla is "the joining of the guarantor's liability (dhimma) to that of the principal." Most schools treat it as unilateral obligation; some Ḥanafites and Shāfiʿites treat it as a bilateral contract. Kafāla may cover pecuniary debt, bodily harm compensation, mahr, wifely support, and merchants' trade credit — but never capital or physical punishment.

### Assignment of Debt (Ḥawāla)

Transfer of debt liability from debtor (muḥīl) to a third party (muḥāl ʿalayh), freeing the original debtor from all liability upon conclusion. The transferred amount must be precisely known. The Ḥanafites uniquely do not require the muḥāl ʿalayh to be indebted to the muḥīl.

### Agency (Wakāla)

A jāʾiz contract by which a principal (muwakkil) endows an agent (wakīl) with capacity to act on his behalf during his lifetime. The agent bears a fiduciary duty (amāna) and is liable for damages caused by negligence (taqṣīr) or transgression (taʿaddī). Subject-matter must be fully owned by principal at conclusion. Agency terminates by mutual cancellation, completion, death or insanity of either party, or interdiction.

### Deposit (Wadīʿa)

Deposit is a fiduciary relationship and a type of restricted agency for safe-keeping. Being jāʾiz, it may be cancelled by the depositor; termination by the depositary requires immediate return. The depositary is not liable for perishment or diminution unless caused by negligence or transgression; the depositary's oath suffices as vindication.

### Loans (ʿĀriya and Qarḍ)

- **ʿĀriya** (free loan of non-fungible): revocable, non-contractual; amounts to a gift of usufruct. The object itself must be returned.
- **Qarḍ** (loan of fungibles with consideration): a contract; the borrower must return equivalent value. Any stipulated excess constitutes ribā.

### Pledge / Security (Rahn)

Property pledged as security for a debt obligation; the pledgor retains ownership but surrenders possession. Risk of damage falls on the pledgee if caused by negligence.

## Historiography

Islamic contract law has attracted substantial comparative legal scholarship, particularly regarding its functional equivalents for interest prohibition (muḍāraba, bay al-wafāʾ) and for risk management (gharar doctrine). Schacht's foundational work (Introduction to Islamic Law, 1964) shaped a generation of Western scholarship but is increasingly critiqued for over-emphasizing Roman law antecedents and for treating the doctrine as rigid rather than adaptive. The Ottoman Mecelle (1876), a codification of Ḥanafite contract law, has been closely studied as the first modern attempt to systematize the muʿāmalāt. Hallaq's contribution is to situate contract law within the broader social and moral function of [[sharia]] rather than treating it as an isolated doctrinal corpus.

**Inter-school variation** is pronounced on specific points (Ḥanafite mufāwaḍa incorporating kafāla; Mālikite acceptance of muʿāṭāt in all contexts; Ḥanbalite maximization of intentionality) but the underlying architecture of offer-acceptance-object, the ribā prohibition, and the lāzim/jāʾiz typology are common across all schools.
