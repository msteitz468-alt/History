# The "Freedom of the Greeks"

The slogan — and policy instrument — by which Rome justified and managed its
intervention in the Greek world: *eleutheria* (freedom, autonomy, ungarrisoned and
tribute-free self-government). Analysed across [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Chs 8–9
(Errington, Derow).

## Emic origin, etic use

"Freedom of the Greeks" was an old **Hellenistic propaganda formula**, claimed in
turn by the Successors, the Antigonids, and the Ptolemies to legitimate
intervention. Rome appropriated it: **[[flamininus|Flamininus]]'** herald proclaimed
at the **Isthmian Games of 196** that the Greeks defeated from Philip were "free,
ungarrisoned, tribute-free, and living by their own laws" — pointedly including the
**Greeks of Asia Minor** (a warning to [[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III]]).

## Function: benefaction as control

Errington's reading: the slogan was an **instrument of indirect control**, not
disinterested philhellenism. During the [[second-macedonian-war-200-196bce|war with
Philip]] it served two aims — forcing Philip to obey, and **winning Greek "hearts and
minds."** The Roman demands on Philip and Antiochus were framed in its terms
precisely *so as to be rejected*, while reaping a propaganda advantage. Its deeper
logic was [[flamininus|Flamininus' doctrine]] that **Greek gratitude is a cheaper,
more durable substitute for legions** than occupation. It was applied again in the
[[peace-of-apamea-188bce|settlement of Asia Minor]] (188): freedom for the cities
that had sided with Rome, the rest parcelled to Pergamon and Rhodes.

## The slide into "obedience"

Derow traces how the benefaction-logic hardened into **imperium as the demand for
obedience**. The reception of appeals from individual cities against their own
leagues (Sparta vs. Achaea), the **Callicrates turning-point (180)** — when the
Senate openly resolved to back those who "promised obedience" — and the manipulation
of class divisions (Rome favouring the wealthy and reliable, "the many" favouring
the nationalists) turned "freedom" into a screen for control. The endpoint was the
[[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|post-Pydna]] deportations and the
[[achaean-war-146bce|destruction of Corinth]] (146): the Greeks who had been
"freed" had merely, as the Aetolians warned in 196, changed masters — to a master
"stronger and more deleterious" than Philip had ever been.

## Contested

The dispute is over Roman **sincerity**: a genuine (if paternalist) philhellenism
(an older, more charitable reading), or an instrumental slogan masking the steady
extension of Roman power (Errington, Derow; see [[nature-of-roman-imperialism]]).
The trajectory from 196 to 146 is the strongest evidence for the instrumental view.
