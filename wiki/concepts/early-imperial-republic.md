# Early Imperial Republic

The **early imperial republic** is the historiographical framing advanced in Blaakman, Conroy-Krutz, and Arista (eds.), *The Early Imperial Republic* (2023) for the United States from the American Revolution through the U.S.–Mexican War. It holds that the young republic was "always imperial" in practice and often in self-description, inheriting, adapting, and extending imperial tools and logics rather than representing a clean break from empire into republican exceptionalism.

## Definition and Scope

The concept emphasizes continuities across the 1776/1783 divide: U.S. officials reused British late-colonial devices (e.g., the Indian boundary line system from the Royal Proclamation of 1763 via the Trade and Intercourse Acts) for centralized control over land, trade, and diplomacy with Native nations. Expansion proceeded by accretion of imperial practices—fragmented sovereignty, clientage, legal pluralism, public-private partnerships—rather than invention ex nihilo. The republic simultaneously proclaimed an "empire of liberty" while building structures of racial hierarchy, removal, and slave expansion.

## Key Applications in the Source

- **Part I (Empires, Nations, and States)**: Reconstruction of the "imperialization" of U.S.–Indian affairs (Robert Lee); sutler merchants as agents of frontier authority (Stearns); Native survival and adaptation using prior imperial experience (DuVal); gendered land encroachment (Bauer); Louisiana private land claims (Lewandoski); projection of slavery's empire into Indian Territory (Parker).
- **Part II**: Global "corridors and enclaves" — Hawaiian sovereignty paradoxes under missionary influence (Smith); Liberian colonization governance struggles (Burin); illegal slave trade and Texas borderlands as vectors of U.S. slave empire (Heerman); African American colonizationists' own imperial visions (Power-Greene).
- **Part III**: Ideological work — comfort with "empire" language among founders (Guyatt on federative empire); qualified acceptance of empire by peace reformers (Minardi); military violence and ambivalence in wars of empire (Greenberg).

## Relation to Other Concepts

Analogous to but distinct from [[settler-colonialism]] (which it overlaps); contrasts with later "New Imperialism"; builds on Jefferson's "empire of liberty" and Washington's "rising empire" while highlighting their racialized and contradictory implementation. Challenges narratives that treat the Revolution as a decisive anti-imperial rupture.

## Historiography and Limitations

The volume positions itself against mid-20th-century reluctance (e.g., Prucha) to apply "imperial" to the early U.S. and against siloed subfields. It draws on imperial history, Indigenous history, and transnational methods. Critics of the frame may argue it overstates continuities or underplays the genuinely revolutionary/republican elements and anti-imperial strands. The 2023 volume supplies a synthetic intervention rather than exhaustive new archives.
