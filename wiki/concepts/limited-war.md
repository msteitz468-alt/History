# Limited War (the dynastic age)

The dominant character of European warfare between the Peace of Westphalia (1648) and the French
Revolution (1789): wars fought by **small, expensive professional standing armies** for **limited
dynastic objectives** (a province, a fortress, a succession), constrained by **logistics
(magazine-based supply), cost, and a shared aristocratic-cabinet political culture** that did not
seek the enemy's annihilation. Campaigns emphasized **maneuver, sieges, and the avoidance of risky
decisive battle**; the populations were largely spectators.

Doughty & Gruber use the category as the **baseline against which the [[napoleonic-warfare|Napoleonic
revolution]] is measured**: the [[nation-in-arms|nation in arms]] would shatter these limits.
Commanders such as [[marlborough-john-churchill|Marlborough]] and [[frederick-the-great|Frederick the
Great]] are read as men who **sought decisive battle *within* the limited-war frame** — Marlborough at
[[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/war-of-the-spanish-succession/battle-of-blenheim|Blenheim]], Frederick at
[[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/seven-years-war/battle-of-leuthen|Leuthen]].

## Limitations and critique

The "limited war" model is **contested**: revisionists note that the period's wars could be long,
costly, and socially disruptive (the [[seven-years-war-1756|Seven Years' War]] was a world war), and
that the contrast with later "total" war can be overdrawn. Use it as an **ideal type**, not a strict
description.

## The Whitman Explanation

Whitman ([[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012|The Verdict of Battle]], 2012) accepts
the golden-age characterization (the era of "war in form," Vattel's term, praised
by the Red Cross's own journal in 1931 as "the last and most beautiful creation
of the old civilization") but rejects both standard explanations for it —
chivalric/aristocratic self-restraint (Howard, Best, Bell, Schmitt) and the
abandonment of moralizing just-war doctrine (Neff). His alternatives: (1) the
**monarchical monopolization of military violence** — disciplined standing armies
replaced pillage with "contributions" and the "siege in form"; (2) the
[[jus-victoriae]] wager doctrine, which made battle outcomes contractually
binding and thereby self-limiting; (3) **limited stakes** — wars legally confined
to the quasi-private law of dynastic succession ("wars with limited aims tend by
their nature to be limited wars"); and (4) uncontested political legitimacy,
which removed the need to demonstrate power through devastation (contrast Edward
III's chevauchées and Louis XIV's burning of the Palatinate, 1689). A caveat he
concedes: Malplaquet cost a quarter of the allied force in one day — limited war
was not small war. See [[verdict-of-battle]] and [[death-of-pitched-battle]].
