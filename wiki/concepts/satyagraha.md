# Satyagraha

Satyagraha is the method of non-violent resistance developed by Mohandas K. Gandhi in South Africa (1893–1914) and later deployed on a mass scale in India. It is not merely a tactic but a philosophy and way of life that fuses ethical, spiritual, and political claims.

## Definition and Scope

- **Literal meaning**: "satya" (truth) + "agraha" (insistence, holding fast). Gandhi glossed it as "truth-force," "soul-force," or "the Force which is born of Truth and Love."
- **Core principles** (from Gandhi's own articulation and practice):
  - Truth is the end and the means; one must be willing to suffer for it and to correct one's own errors.
  - Ahimsa (non-violence) is active, not passive: refusal to harm the opponent while refusing to accept or cooperate with injustice.
  - Suffering (self-suffering) has purifying power for the actor and potentially converting power for the opponent and onlookers.
  - Means and ends are inseparable: violent or immoral means cannot produce a just or moral outcome.
- **What it is not**: Mere passive resistance, civil disobedience for its own sake, or a cover for cowardice. Gandhi repeatedly insisted that a satyagrahi must be willing to die but never to kill, and must have no hatred for the opponent.

A. M. Cachalia (1908): "the passive resister is higher in the moral scale, and in that of human development than the active resister … Passive resistance is a matter of heart, of conscience, of trained understanding." Henry Polak (1910): "our programme will remain, as it has always been, not one of violence or attempts to disturb, but one of suffering on the part of our people, who intend to go on enduring these hardships until they make the authorities ashamed of themselves."

It differs from other non-violent traditions (e.g., some Western pacifisms) in its explicit political aim of compelling change through moral witness and mass disciplined action rather than only personal witness.

## Causal Drivers

- **Contextual**: Racial legislation in Natal and the Transvaal (disenfranchisement, compulsory registration/"Black Act," £3 tax, invalidation of non-Christian marriages) that affected a diverse Indian diaspora (Gujarati merchants, Tamil indentured labourers and ex-indentured, Chinese).
- **Intellectual**: Gandhi's reading of Tolstoy (*The Kingdom of God Is Within You*), Ruskin (*Unto This Last*), the Gita, the Sermon on the Mount, and Jain traditions of ahimsa.
- **Personal/organizational**: The need for a method that could unite a divided community, sustain long struggle without arms, and appeal to both local and imperial audiences. The coining of the term itself (during a 1906–07 *Indian Opinion* competition) gave the movement a distinctive cultural and moral identity.
- **Experimental**: Phoenix Settlement (1904) and Tolstoy Farm (1910) served as laboratories for communal living, discipline, and the training of satyagrahis.

## Major Phases (in this source)

- 1906–1908: Transvaal registration campaign — first mass use. 11 Sep 1906 Empire Theatre vow of imprisonment (drawing on hartal/risaamanu traditions); term "satyagraha" coined 1906–07 by Maganlal Gandhi; 1908 Johannesburg certificate burnings (Fordsburg Mosque, 3,000+); first prosecutions (Ram Sundar Pandit); Gandhi's own two-month imprisonment in Fort (native section).
- 1913–1914: Climactic phase — revival after Searle judgment (£3 tax + marriage invalidation). Kasturba independently volunteers for jail; women cross borders; indentured strikes in Natal mines/sugar estates; Gandhi leads epic 11-day march of ~2,000 (including women/children) from Newcastle toward Tolstoy Farm; police shootings; Enquiry Commission; 1914 Indians’ Relief Bill (repeal of £3 tax; religious marriages recognized). Gandhi adopts indentured-labourer dress after the shootings.
- Post-1915 (foreshadowed): Transfer to India (Champaran, Kheda, Ahmedabad, Rowlatt, Non-cooperation, Salt March, Quit India).

In Guha's synthesis: "potent, however, though passive resistance was as an instrument for winning reforms – perhaps the mightiest instrument on earth"; "the struggle of the Indians in the Transvaal is the greatest of modern times, inasmuch as it has been idealised both as to the goal as also the methods adopted to reach the goal." A South African friend: "You gave us a lawyer; we gave you back a Mahatma." Hofmeyr (1931): "India had given to South Africa one of the most difficult of its problems; South Africa in turn gave to India the idea of civil disobedience."

## Interaction with Other Concepts and Processes

- Closely linked to **ahimsa**, **brahmacharya** (as self-purification), and Gandhi's vision of village republics.
- Contrasts with revolutionary/terrorist strands in Indian nationalism (e.g. Tilak, later Bhagat Singh) and with constitutional petitioning (early Congress moderates).
- Enabled the [[indian-independence-movement]] to become a mass peasant movement rather than an elite affair.
- Exported and adapted globally (Martin Luther King Jr., anti-apartheid movement, various 20th/21st c. non-violent struggles).

## End Conditions / Limits

Satyagraha requires:
- A minimum of moral discipline and fearlessness among participants.
- An opponent capable of shame or responsive to public opinion (Gandhi was explicit that it would not work against absolute Nazis in the same way).
- Willingness to call off action if violence or loss of discipline occurs (e.g. Chauri Chaura 1922).

It does not guarantee victory on every demand; its success is measured in moral and political awakening as well as concrete concessions.

## Historiography

Guha's *Gandhi Before India* supplies the most detailed reconstruction from contemporary records of how the method was invented, tested, and theorized in South Africa. It corrects later romanticization by showing the improvised, experimental, and sometimes ad hoc character of early campaigns while documenting Gandhi's own growing clarity about its universal applicability.

Key notes from Guha: the 1906 Empire Theatre vow drew on Indian precedents (hartal, risaamanu) rather than Thoreau (whom Gandhi had not yet read); popular accounts over-emphasize the 1893 Pietermaritzburg ejection while under-playing the 1897 Durban mob and 1913 mass participation of indentured and women; "Haste and teleology do injustice to both man and place." Counterfactual: earlier Milner/Smuts compromise might have prevented the full development and testing of satyagraha at scale. The method's success in 1913 required the "mass base" of indentured workers and women; "when the women could show such courage … the men dared not prove themselves weaker than the women."
