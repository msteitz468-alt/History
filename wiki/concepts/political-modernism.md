# Political Modernism

Etic analytical concept: the political pole of [[modernism]] in Griffin's maximalist sense — "the attempt to create a new sacred canopy through the comprehensive political restructuring of modern society" ([[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007|Griffin 2007]], ch. 6). It applies to "vanguard or (would-be) mass movements of radicalism which work towards creating an entirely new form of society, envisaged metaphorically as 'a new world', by replacing a system of government experienced as spiritually bankrupt."

## Rise, 1848–1945

Currents of political modernism — anarchism, revolutionary syndicalism, Marxism — run from the mid-nineteenth century, but "it was only in the October Revolution that it formed its first state." Griffin reads the Bolshevik Revolution as a modernist [[revitalization-movement]]: Bolshevism as the "mazeway" of a communitas of communists "led by a new propheta, Vladimir Lenin," complete with God-building (Gorky/Lunacharsky; Lenin elected "first socialist deity" in 1924), the New Soviet Man, and leader cults. Peter Osborne is the key theoretical authority: the *Communist Manifesto* is "the founding text of an internationalist political modernism," and modernism generally is "the affirmative cultural self-consciousness of the temporality of the new." Bernice Rosenthal's Nietzschean-Bolshevism thesis (active nihilism fused with a "ruthlessly voluntarist, mercilessly cruel, and radically future-oriented interpretation of Marxism") extends the category to the Five Year Plans and purges. See [[communism-and-fascism]].

On the right, the same temporality appears in organic nationalism (Barrès's "la Terre et les Morts"; Maurras's integral nationalism hybridized with [[sorel-georges|Sorelian]] syndicalism to produce Valois's Faisceau), in völkisch nationalism, Zionism (Mosse's framework extended), and in the German "Conservative Revolution" (Moeller van den Bruck, Jünger, Spengler, Schmitt, Heidegger). Osborne's decisive reframing, adopted by Griffin: "Reactionary modernism is not a hybrid form (modernism + reaction). Rather it draws attention to the modernist temporality of reaction per se" — its "image of the future may derive from the mythology of some lost origin... but its temporal dynamic is rigorously futural." The contest between socialism and fascism is therefore not revolution vs reaction but a contest between rival revolutionary temporalities.

## Relation to Fascism

Osborne's inference, which Griffin's whole book elaborates: fascism is "a particularly radical form of conservative revolution," "neither a relic nor an archaism" but "a form of political modernism." Griffin's culminating definition (ch. 6): "FASCISM is a revolutionary species of political modernism originating in the early twentieth century whose mission is to combat the allegedly degenerative forces of contemporary history (decadence) by bringing about an alternative modernity and temporality (a 'new order' and a 'new era') based on the rebirth, or palingenesis, of the nation" — recorded in full at [[generic-fascism]] and [[palingenetic-ultranationalism]]. The *Aufbruch* mood ("the sense of a beginning" — Kracauer's word for early Weimar) is the phenomenology of political modernism; Kermode's line on Yeats names its literary correlate: "totalitarian theories of form matched or reflected by totalitarian politics." *Mein Kampf*, "though it has nothing aesthetically modernist in style or form... has claims to be one of the most important manifestos of political modernism of the twentieth century."

Griffin's Postscript extends the category forward: post-1945 religious extremism (Lifton's analysis of Aum Shinrikyo and al-Qaeda) as ritualized "palingenetic war on Modernity" structurally paralleling interwar Bolshevism and fascism; the European New Right (Bergfleth's recoding of "Blood and soil" as "earth and homeland") as demonstrating "the fascist pedigree of the European New Right's brand of political modernism."

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept inherits every objection to the maximalist ideal type of [[modernism]] (see that page). Specific to the political extension: critics of the "political religion"/redemptive-politics family of concepts (Kershaw calls it a "voguish revamping of an age-old notion," while conceding Nazism's "politics of national salvation"); and the risk, which Griffin polices explicitly, of collapsing into direct-lineage claims from avant-garde art to atrocity (Virilio, Lukács) — the connection is "multi-causal and 'rhizomic' rather than 'arborial'."
