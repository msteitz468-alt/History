# The New Imperialism (1870–1914)

The **New Imperialism** is the term for the **intense, accelerating burst of overseas
empire-building** by the European powers (joined by the United States and Japan) between c.
1870 and 1914 — above all the [[scramble-for-africa|partition of Africa]] and the carving of
"spheres of influence" in Asia and the Pacific. It is distinguished from earlier
[[imperialism|imperialism]] by its speed, its global scope, its competitive great-power
rivalry, and its entanglement with industrial capitalism and mass nationalism. From
[[new-cambridge-modern-history-v11|*The New Cambridge Modern History*, Vol. XI]].

## Emic and etic

"Imperialism" became a charged **emic** word in exactly this period (J. A. Hobson's
*Imperialism: A Study*, 1902; later Lenin), and historians use it **etically** for the era.
NCMH XI frames it structurally: "the imperialism that characterised the new epoch was simply
the outlet for internal changes and transformations, **the political aspect… of the process by
which the industrial revolution which had started in Europe went on to embrace the whole
world.**"

## The phenomenon

- **Scale and speed.** Within a generation, Europe seized most of **Africa** (the
  [[scramble-for-africa|Scramble]], 1880s–90s), tightened its grip on **South and Southeast
  Asia**, and forced "unequal" concessions and spheres on **China**, while the US and **Japan**
  joined the ranks of empire.
- **Drivers (debated).** The volume ties it to the **[[second-industrial-revolution|industrial
  economy]]** and surging **European population** (more than doubling, 1800–1900) seeking
  markets, raw materials, and outlets — alongside **strategic/great-power rivalry** (once one
  power moved, others followed to preserve the [[balance-of-power|balance]]), **prestige and
  nationalism**, and on-the-spot "men on the spot" and technological enablers (steamships,
  quinine, the machine gun).
- **Methods.** Formal annexation, protectorates, chartered companies, and "informal empire"
  of trade and finance.

## The debate over causes

The causes are the classic controversy:

- **Economic** (Hobson, Lenin): surplus capital seeking investment outlets — imperialism as
  "the highest stage of capitalism."
- **Strategic/political** (Robinson & Gallagher, *Africa and the Victorians*): the partition as
  a reactive, peripheral, crisis-driven scramble (the Egyptian crisis of 1882) more than a
  planned economic grab — "the official mind."
- **Cultural/ideological**: nationalism, social Darwinism, the "civilizing mission," and mass
  jingoism.
- NCMH XI's reading leans toward imperialism as the **outward political face of industrial-
  demographic expansion**, not a single-cause economic plot.

## Significance

The New Imperialism brought nearly the whole non-European world under European political or
economic control by 1914 — the high-water mark of Western global dominance and the matrix of
modern **[[decolonization]]** and anti-colonial nationalism. The scramble for colonies and
spheres also sharpened the great-power rivalries (Anglo-German, Franco-German, in Africa and
Asia) that fed the **[[first-world-war-1914|First World War]]**.

## Scale and Historiography (Paxton addendum)

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton and Hessler]] (ch. 1) supply the quantitative scale of the New Imperialism by 1914: Britain's empire was some 140 times the size of Britain itself, Belgium's 80 times, Holland's 60 times, France's 20 times. Germany, entering the imperial competition late (1885), compensated for a comparatively restricted colonial empire (East and Southwest Africa, the China coast) with investment in Eastern and Southern Europe and rapid naval construction after 1900, while Russia established itself as a Pacific power via the Trans-Siberian Railroad (1891–1903). Only **Japan** resisted the tide, revoking in 1894 the capitulations (extraterritorial privileges) granted to foreign traders in 1858, having adopted European industrial and military techniques successfully enough to defeat Russia in 1905 (see [[russo-japanese-war-1904]]). Europe's near-monopoly of modern military force — colonial armies with European officers and modern weapons — let the empires be held with only occasional reverses: the 1879 Zulu defeat of a British force in South Africa, an 1885 British defeat in Sudan, and the Italian defeat by Ethiopians at Adowa (1896) were striking exceptions that did not reverse the overall balance.

Paxton surveys the same causal debate covered above with additional texture. The *cultural/religious* strand stresses missionary zeal (Catholic missionaries calling in the French navy in Indochina from the 1850s–80s), engineers, and military glory-seeking. The *economic* strand runs from John A. Hobson's *Imperialism: A Study* (1902, provoked by the Boer War) — low wages and maldistributed wealth in Europe left workers with insufficient purchasing power, forcing capitalists abroad for markets and investment returns — through Lenin's 1917 extension, which Paxton judges "underestimated the extent to which capital continued to be invested in the advanced countries" even at the height of the imperial rush (1885–1914), while still concluding that "no interpretation of imperialism can fail to assign some role to economic aims." Concrete cases: the French occupation of Tunisia (1881, protecting French bondholders), the British occupation of Egypt (1882, protecting investors owed interest by the Khedive Ismail), Sir George Goldie's United African Company (1879, Nigeria) as a nearly pure commercial venture, and the Italian conquest of Libya (1911) combining land-hunger and glory. A third, *strategic self-sustaining momentum* strand (Robinson and Gallagher, *Africa and the Victorians*, 1967) argues Britain took Egypt in 1882 to protect its existing stake in India — "the very existence of a colony... created the strategic necessity for controlling access to it" — though critics respond that Britain's original presence in India was itself substantially economic.

Europe supplied roughly 83% of the world's foreign investment by 1914 — see [[gold-standard]] for the underlying financial-system infrastructure — flowing into Canadian mines, American railroads, South American electric utilities, Senegalese groundnut plantations, Egyptian cotton, South African gold, and Shanghai trading houses; only Chile, among Latin American states, owned its own railroads in 1914.
