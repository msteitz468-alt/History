## Definition

*Iconophobia* is a term coined by Hodgson to designate a broader and more deeply rooted mistrust of figural imagery than the specifically Christian "iconoclasm" (image-breaking). Where iconoclasm refers specifically to the destruction of cult images in devotional contexts, iconophobia extends to a generalized avoidance of figural imagery — human and animal forms — even in non-cult contexts, rooted in theological and moral convictions about the appropriate relation between the human imagination and the divine.

Hodgson introduces the distinction to explain a key feature of Islamicate visual culture:

> "Such a generalized mistrust of figural images, going beyond merely the elimination of them from cult use, can better be designated 'iconophobia'." (*Venture of Islam* Vol. 2)

## Origins in the Prophetic Traditions

Iconophobia developed gradually across the Irano-Semitic prophetic traditions before Islam:

**Jewish tradition**: Beginning perhaps in the pre-Christian period, "hostility to the image went still further; all figural imagery — that in human or animal form — was ruled out in places of worship, even when there was no question of directing the cult to the images; and the pious went on to rule out all such imagery even in ordinary places, that the whole of life might be dedicated only to God."

**Christian tradition**: Under pressure from Christianization, a movement to restore cult images in Christianized form generated the counter-movement of iconoclasm (image-breaking). This was, in Hodgson's reading, the more specifically "prophetic-minded" Christian response to the demand that the divine not be represented materially.

**Islamic tradition**: Islam inherited and intensified these tendencies through multiple converging channels:

1. **Theological**: The Quran and Muhammad's message continued the Irano-Semitic prophetic tradition hostile to nature cults. The divine is invisible and moral; images of the natural world cannot symbolize it. The Quran itself — as a text — took the place of images as the sole acceptable medium of divine presence.

2. **Sharia-minded egalitarianism**: The legist tradition condemned luxury (associating it with oppression of the poor) and condemned figural imagery both within the cult (as potentially idolatrous) and outside it. Painters of animate figures were threatened with eschatological punishment: doomed to try to breathe life into their figures at Judgment, and destined to fail.

3. **Devotional monotheism**: The pure monotheistic devotional experience was "intensely exclusivist: the moral challenge of the divine Transcendent demanded total and unified attention." Any visual symbolism that competed with Quranic presence risked "dissipating the soul's attention."

4. **Mistrust of magic**: The Sharia-minded were "intensely mistrustful of anything that smacked of magic, which by definition escaped the limits on human ambition imposed by a matter-of-fact, egalitarian propriety."

## Legal Codification

Sharia law codified iconophobia differently across the schools:

- Hanafi and Usuli Shii law books banned images "apropos of avoiding what might distract from the salat worship"
- Shafii and Maliki schools linked the issue to luxury, discussing it "apropos of accepting an invitation to a wedding feast"
- All came to "like conclusions": the painter of animate figures "must try to breathe life into his figures at the Judgment, and would fail"; made images "must not be used save in ways that clearly degraded them, as to step on on the floor"

## Effects on Islamicate Art

Iconophobia's effects on Islamicate art were real but indirect — less through explicit prohibition than through the way it constrained the imaginative horizon available to artists:

**Within the mosque**: The Sharia ban was effectively enforced in religious architecture. Inside mosques, no figural imagery appeared. This meant the major public art form — monumental religious architecture — developed entirely without figural symbolism.

**In secular contexts**: The ban "did not prevent widespread use of two-dimensional figural imagery" in private and courtly contexts. Manuscript illustrations, murals in palaces, ceramics — all continued to use figural art. But the attitude underlying the ban did leave its impress on this secular figural art.

**The positive outcome**: By ruling out the art of myth and objective natural symbolism at its highest, iconophobia "set the artists a problem at the heart of their inspiration." The solutions they developed — the arabesque, geometric composition, calligraphic design, and ultimately the graphic autonomy of [[timurid-miniature-painting]] — constitute Islamicate art's most distinctive contribution to world visual culture. See [[islamic-visual-arts]] for the full analysis.

**Popular superstition**: Iconophobia was reinforced by "popular superstitious fears of the magic implicit in symbolic art. Marks of such fears have been common in Islamdom, where if a peasant came upon ancient paintings or statues he was likely to destroy them at once, and even a scholar (with the sanction of fiqh law) might ritually draw a line across the throat of a painted figure to show that it was not alive."

## Emic vs. Etic

"Iconophobia" is an etic term coined by Hodgson; the historical actors worked within the categories of Sharia legal rulings, theological arguments about divine transcendence, and social condemnation of luxury. The term is analytical, not a description used by Muslims themselves.

"Iconoclasm" is partially emic in the Christian context (referring to the specific Byzantine controversy), partially etic as an analytical category.
