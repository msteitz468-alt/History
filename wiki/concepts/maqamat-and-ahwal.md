## Definition and Scope

The maqāmāt (singular: maqām, "station") and aḥwāl (singular: ḥāl, "state") constitute the foundational taxonomic framework of the Sufi path. They describe the inner landscape through which the wayfarer (sālik) passes on the journey toward God — but with a crucial distinction: stations are earned through sustained human effort, while states are gifts of divine grace that cannot be held or summoned at will.

Hujwīrī (d. c. 1077) captures the essential contrast: "State is something that descends from God into a man's heart, without his being able to repel it when it comes, or to attract it when it goes, by his own effort." Rūmī (d. 1273) provides the most poetic formulation: "The ḥāl is like the unveiling of the beauteous bride, while the maqām is the [king's] being alone with the bride" (Mathnawī 1:1435). The station is the lasting dwelling; the state is the moment of visitation.

The maqām is thus an achieved moral and spiritual discipline — the wayfarer must not move on from a station before having fulfilled all its obligations, and must not act as if he were in a station he has not yet reached. The states that descend upon him will vary in character according to which station he currently inhabits: the qabḍ ("contraction") of one in the station of poverty is qualitatively different from the qabḍ of one in the station of longing.

## The Major Stations (Maqāmāt)

The Sufi manuals do not agree on a single fixed sequence, but the core stations recur across the tradition. Three early 9th-century classifications illustrate the variation:

- **Dhūʾn-Nūn** (Egypt, d. 859): faith, fear, reverence, obedience, hope, love, suffering, intimacy; then confusion, poverty, union.
- **Yaḥyā ibn Muʿādh** (Iran, d. 872): repentance, asceticism, peace in God's will, fear, longing, love, gnosis.
- **Sahl at-Tustarī** (Iraq, d. 896): response to God's call, turning toward Him, repentance, forgiveness of sins, loneliness, steadfastness, meditation, gnosis, discourse, election, friendship.

The canonical sequence, while variable, consistently foregrounds:

1. **Tawba** ("repentance") — the threshold of the path; turning away from sin and worldly concern. Awakened by any number of outward triggers — a Koranic verse, a chance encounter with a saint, a dream. Junayd defined true repentance as "the forgetting of one's sins," while Sahl at-Tustarī insisted sins must be remembered perpetually as a guard against spiritual pride. Hujwīrī distinguishes tawba (return from great sins to obedience), ināba (return from minor sins to love), and auba (return from one's self to God).

2. **Waraʿ and Zuhd** ("abstinence" and "renunciation") — giving up what is ritually permitted and eventually renouncing the thought of renunciation itself; tark at-tark, "quitting the quitting."

3. **Ṣabr** ("patience") — "to remain unmoved before the arrows of the divine decrees" (Muḥāsibī). Three ranks: the mutaṣabbir (one who attempts patience), the ṣābir (patient under affliction), and the ṣabūr (perfectly patient under any condition).

4. **Shukr** ("gratitude") — superior to patience; includes thanking God even when a wish is withheld, and ultimately gratitude for the capacity to be grateful. Related to riḍā ("contentment") — not passive endurance but joy in the bitter decree: "Riḍā is the joy of the heart in the bitterness of the divine decree" (Dhūʾn-Nūn).

5. **[[tawakkul-trust|Tawakkul]]** ("trust in God") — complete reliance upon the divine will; a central station covered in its own page.

6. **Faqr** ("poverty") — the central attitude of Sufi life; covered under its treatment in the **[[fana-and-baqa]]** page and partially in the maqāmāt context here.

7. **Maḥabba** and **Maʿrifa** ("love" and "gnosis") — the final and highest stations; see **[[sufi-love-mysticism]]**.

## The Major States (Aḥwāl)

States descend and pass; they cannot be willed. The theoreticians debated whether certain experiences (fear/hope, contentment) belong to stations or states. Key aḥwāl include:

- **Khauf and Rajāʾ** ("fear and hope") — the two wings of action without which flight is impossible (Sarrāj). Early ascetics emphasized fear; in later Sufism hope predominated, but advanced mystics knew the fear of God's makr ("ruses") — the danger that even spiritual elation might be a divine snare.

- **Qabḍ and Basṭ** ("contraction and expansion") — the states that correspond to fear and hope at the experiential level. Basṭ is an extension of being, "to leave off the two worlds in one rapture" (ʿAṭṭār); qabḍ is a compression of the soul into a needle's eye, a darkness sometimes months long. Junayd held qabḍ superior: "when He presses me through fear he makes me disappear from myself."

- **Sukr and Ṣaḥw** ("intoxication and sobriety") — Hujwīrī: "Intoxication is the playground of children, but sobriety is the death-field of men." True sobriety means reaching the goal while retaining the capacity to act in the world; sobriety after annihilation is the mark of the complete mystic.

- **Uns and Waḥshat** ("intimacy and estrangement from creation") — Uns is linked to divine jamāl (beauty and loving qualities); waḥshat to loneliness in the divine wilderness. Contrasted with hayba ("reverence/awe"), which awakens at divine jalāl (grandeur).

- **Shawq** ("longing") — debated: some mystics denied that genuine longing is possible, since God is never absent; others (like Bāyezīd Bisṭāmī) made it the supreme state: "I would not give [all the paradises] for that single sigh which rises at morning-time from the depth of my soul in remembering my longing for Him."

- **Waqt** ("the present moment") — a special category: the moment in which a given state is granted. "Time is a cutting sword" that leaves man in absolute nakedness before God. The Sufi is ibn al-waqt, "the son of the present moment." At the highest level he transcends even this and becomes a ṣāfī, "pure," submerged in divine light and free from "times and states" (Rūmī, Mathnawī 3:1426–34).

- **Jamʿ and Tafriqa** ("collectedness and separation") — unification and the subsequent return to distinctness after union; the pair that most directly precedes [[fana-and-baqa|fanāʾ and baqāʾ]].

## The Wayfarer and the Guide

No traversal of the stations is possible without a guide. The sheikh (Arabic) or pīr (Persian) holds the murīd ("he who has willed to enter the Path") as a physician holds a patient; he interprets dreams, prescribes seclusion (the arbaʿīn, forty-day retreat), and diagnoses the spiritual illnesses that masquerade as virtues. A common saying: "When someone has no sheikh, Satan becomes his sheikh." The murīd receives the patched frock (khirqa), instruction in the formula of [[dhikr]], and the company of the master. Three years of service are required before formal acceptance.

## Scholarly Debates

The classification and ordering of stations was contested from the beginning. Key unresolved issues include:

1. Whether certain experiences (fear/hope, riḍā) belong to stations or states — Sarrāj places fear and hope among states; later theoreticians treat them as stations.
2. Whether states can be "appropriated" and held for a time, or whether they are always entirely transient.
3. The relationship between the station of faqr (poverty) and the terminal experience of fanāʾ — many authorities treated them as nearly identical (see [[fana-and-baqa]]).
4. Whether gnosis (maʿrifa) or love (maḥabba) constitutes the highest maqām: Ghazzālī argues love without gnosis is impossible; others invert the hierarchy.

The entire framework presupposes that the path is traversable by effort in cooperation with grace — a position that later philosophical Sufism, particularly in Ibn ʿArabī's school, complicated substantially by emphasizing the primacy of divine disclosure (kashf) over human striving.

## Historiography

The maqāmāt/aḥwāl schema is among the best-documented structural features of classical Sufism, preserved in the major manuals (Sarrāj's Lumaʿ, Kalābādhī's Taʿarruf, Hujwīrī's Kashf al-Maḥjūb, Qushayrī's Risāla, Ghazzālī's Iḥyāʾ). These sources are the primary analytical tool. Schimmel's treatment (chapter 3 of *Mystical Dimensions of Islam*, 1975) remains the most accessible English synthesis. The Swiss scholar Benedikt Reinert's monograph on tawakkul is noted as a model for concept-specific research. Debates persist about how much the schema reflects a lived psychological sequence versus a post-hoc theoretical ordering by the compilers.
