# Feudalism (Fiefs, Vassalage, and the "Feudal Society" Construct)

## Definition

**"Feudalism"** is the etic model, built by early-modern jurists and refined by
nineteenth- and twentieth-century historians, that describes medieval European
society as organised around the **fief** (*feudum*) and the personal bond of
**vassalage**: a free or noble man (the vassal) commends himself to a lord, performs
homage and fealty, and receives in return a fief (typically land) in exchange for
military and counsel service; lordship over peasants rests on proprietary rights
over their holdings. In its broadest ("feudal society") sense — Marc Bloch's
*La société féodale* (1939) — it names an entire social type: a world of
fragmented, personalised, contractual political power, of castles and knights, in
which "government" had dissolved into private **lordship**.

## Emic vs. Etic

The term is **strictly etic**. Medieval people spoke of *feuda*, *homagium*,
*fidelitas*, *milites* and *seigneurie*, but never of "feudalism" or a "feudal
system" — abstractions invented by sixteenth-century lawyers reading the
twelfth-century Lombard *[[ius-commune|Libri Feudorum]]* and generalised by later
scholars. This gap between the actors' vocabulary and the historians' system is at
the heart of the modern critique.

## The Reynolds Critique

[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Susan Reynolds, Ch. 4, "Government and
community"; building on her *Fiefs and Vassals*, 1994) argues that the "feudal
society" model is **inadequate and largely a construct**:

- **Government was more than personal lordship.** The belief that the eleventh
  century lacked any sense of public welfare "seems to rest on nothing but old
  stereotypes of feudal society." Once a lord had exercised control over an area for
  some time, his power was legitimised by prevailing ideas of custom and lawful
  subjection: it became **public authority**, carrying obligations to the common
  welfare, irrespective of its violent origins.
- **Community was pervasive, not the achievement of a twelfth-century "communal
  movement."** Government at every level — kingdom to village — depended on a great
  deal of collective activity: rulers took counsel, raised armies and taxes, and
  judged disputes through panels of unpaid subjects, because no medieval ruler could
  afford enough servants to coerce. Units of government that endured came to be seen
  as communities of culture and descent (*gentes*, *nationes*, *populi*).
- **The fief–vassal nexus** that earlier scholars treated as the structuring
  principle of society was, on Reynolds's reading, neither as uniform, as early, nor
  as politically central as the model claims; much of its apparent coherence was read
  back into the sources by post-medieval jurists.

The verdict is not that lords, fiefs and homage did not exist, but that
**"feudalism" as a system is a misleading abstraction** that obscures the reality of
public obligation, community, and law in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. See the
[[feudalism-construct-debate|controversy page]].

## Limitations and Critiques

- The word has been used in incompatible senses (a narrow legal sense — the law of
  fiefs; a broad social sense — Bloch's society type; and a Marxist sense — a mode of
  production based on serf labour), so that scholars often talk past one another.
- It fits western Francia and Norman England far better than [[ottonian-dynasty|
  Ottonian/Salian Germany]], León, or the Italian cities, where public structures
  persisted — the same regional unevenness that dogs the [[feudal-revolution-debate|
  *mutation de l'an mil*]] thesis.
- Many specialists (following Brown and Reynolds) now avoid "feudalism" as an
  analytic term altogether, preferring to describe specific relations of lordship,
  tenure, and obligation.

## "Bastard Feudalism" (Late-Medieval Coda)

A separate modern coinage, **"bastard feudalism,"** names the system that *replaced* classic
fief-and-vassal feudalism in the later Middle Ages (Philippe Contamine, [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|
NCMH VII]] Ch. 4): the man-to-man bond now rested not on enfeoffment with land for military service
but on **fees, annuities and livery** — the English magnate's **indenture** with his retainers,
the Hungarian *familiaritates*, the Spanish *criados*, the French household clientele. These
clienteles could harden into political "bands" and factions, and were notorious for interfering
violently with justice. The term is treated more fully under the
[[european-nobility-late-medieval|fifteenth-century European nobility]].
