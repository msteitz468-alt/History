# Atlantic History (as analytic construct and field)

Atlantic history is an **analytic construct** and explicit category of historical analysis devised to organize study of key early modern developments: the emergence (from the fifteenth century) and growth of the Atlantic basin as a site of intensive demographic, economic, social, cultural, and other exchanges among and within the four continents (Europe, Africa, North America, South America) and adjacent islands.

As John Elliott put it, it studies “the creation, destruction, and re-creation of communities as a result of the movement, across and around the Atlantic basin, of people, commodities, cultural practices, and values.”

## Emergence and Institutionalization

- **Pre-history**: Earlier transoceanic perspectives existed (Herbert Baxter Adams; C. M. Andrews; C. H. Haring; Perry Miller; Wesley Frank Craven; Bernard Bailyn on merchants; Philip Curtin on the slave trade; D. W. Meinig’s *Atlantic America*; Jacques Godechot’s *Histoire de l’Atlantique*).
- **Institutional start**: Late 1960s, Johns Hopkins Program in Atlantic History and Culture.
- **Maturation**: 1990s onward. Harvard International Seminar on the History of the Atlantic World (from 1996) generated hundreds of papers. Programs, courses, H-Net list (H-Atlantic), special journal issues/forums, book series, and the first textbook appeared. By the 2000s it was challenging national/imperial frames in U.S. academia.

It began primarily as a **perspective** to broaden horizons and encourage transnational comparison, not necessarily a new encompassing field.

## Core Definition and Promises

The Atlantic is treated as a zone of interaction where events in one place had repercussions elsewhere. Key agents: people, pathogens, plants, commodities, ideas, values. It facilitates:
- Breaking out of national/imperial containers.
- Transnational and comparative work.
- Attention to border-crossing, diasporas (especially the African diaspora), commodity chains, entangled histories.
- Study of creolization, middle grounds, and leading-edge sectors (maritime, islands, frontiers).

## Major Criticisms (the “five substantive objections”)

Critics have raised concerns that the volume editors (Greene & Morgan, 2009) take seriously:

1. **Lack of coherence/unity**: The Atlantic comprised many distinct seas, North vs. South, national spheres, “color-coded” Atlantics (white, black, green, etc.), tropical vs. temperate. Not a single system or civilization like Braudel’s Mediterranean.

2. **Not self-contained/bounded**: Always linked to the Indian Ocean, Pacific, Asia (silver flows, textiles, cowries, tea). Focusing inward risks parochialism. Global or conjunctural perspectives may be superior.

3. **Neo-imperial or politically correct rebranding**: Can appear as imperial history with “other bits” added deferentially; privileges east–west over north–south; Latin America sometimes marginalized.

4. **Neglect of interiors and non-Atlantic zones**: Privileges coastal and Europeanized zones; deflects attention from inland indigenous populations and continental interiors (advocates for “continental” approaches, especially for North America and later U.S. history).

5. **Over-emphasis on connections at expense of internal histories and comparison**: Much work has traced linkages and border zones but has not sufficiently advanced knowledge of developments *within* discrete areas or systematic comparison of commonalities and peculiarities.

## Propositions for Doing Atlantic History Well (Greene & Morgan synthesis)

The editors offer guarded defense plus practical guidelines:

- Do not exaggerate either connections or contrasts; balance them (Elliott’s Spanish–British comparison as model).
- Pursue interactive, border-crossing studies (commodities, ideas/religious networks, scientific knowledge; Catholic Atlantic).
- Focus sustained attention on leading-edge sectors: the maritime world itself, island hubs/stepping-stones (Azores, Madeira, Canaries, Cape Verdes, Caribbean), and borderlands/transfrontier regions (“middle ground”).
- Give greater weight to the exchange of values, ideas, and cultural/religious/scientific circulation alongside people and goods.
- Revisit traditional imperial topics (ideology, law, governance) from broader perspectives (legal pluralism, negotiated authorities, Mancke, Benton, Pagden).
- Pay close attention to chronology and phases of development (various tripartite schemas: contested marchlands/occupation → integration/consolidation → creole triumphalism/emancipation/revolution). Note that uniform chronology does not apply everywhere; pre-1492/1415 Atlantic prehistory and post-1825 vitality also matter in places.

Atlantic history is best treated as a **framework or angle of vision**, not an imperial field that must subsume everything. For some questions it is too limiting; for others (especially connective, diasporic, or comparative work in the early modern era) it remains highly fruitful.

## Relation to Other Frameworks

- **Continental**: Emphasizes inland and indigenous dynamics (Peter H. Wood).
- **Hemispheric** (for the Americas): Promotes comparison across the two continents and appreciation of internal variation (Jack P. Greene).
- **Global**: Stresses connections beyond the basin (silver, rice, Indian Ocean circuits; Nicholas Canny, Peter Coclanis); “Conjuncto-Atlantic history.”
- **Imperial / national**: Atlantic crosses and complicates but does not replace them; boundaries were permeable yet contemporaries often operated within national spheres.
- **Area studies / internal histories**: Atlantic work should ultimately deepen, not replace, understanding of specific societies.

## Part III: Competing and Complementary Perspectives (Continental, Hemispheric, Global, and Beyond Atlantic)

The volume's Part III (chs. 10–13) systematically examines alternative or complementary spatial frameworks to the Atlantic construct itself. These perspectives arose both from internal critiques of Atlantic history's limits and from longstanding traditions in U.S., Latin American, and global historiography. The index of the volume registers dedicated treatment of the "continental approach" at pp. 6, 280–81, 300, 308 (with "antecedents, eighteenth-century, 290" and "continental defined, 291n4"); the "hemispheric perspective" at pp. 212, 301, 307–12 (with "approaches to, 308–10" and "research, possible areas for, 311"); "global history, and place for Atlantic history, 321"; "comparative history, 149–50, 280–81, 330–31"; "New Philology approach, 303"; "California School, 345–47"; and "world systems, 346–48". These entries document the volume's engagement with the question of where Atlantic history fits—or whether it is best subordinated to—larger, alternative, or intersecting scales.

### The Continental Approach

Advocated especially in North American contexts (Peter H. Wood and others), the continental perspective prioritizes the internal dynamics of the landmass, particularly indigenous societies, borderlands, and later national (U.S.) development, over transoceanic connections. It draws eighteenth-century antecedents (p. 290) and is explicitly defined in the volume (p. 291n4). Key indexed themes include "facing east" (pp. 281–83, associated with Daniel Richter), which inverts the traditional westward narrative of European expansion by centering Native American perspectives and continental interactions; references to Frederick Jackson Turner (p. 281–82) on the frontier; and the "prenational history of United States" (pp. 300–301, 312, linked to Elizabeth Mancke's contribution at pp. 17–18, 312).

From the index, continental emphases appear in discussions of Native American autonomy "within imperial structures" (p. 200 etc.), "shatter zone" (p. 200, 204), "Middle Ground" (p. 122–23, 143, 200; Richard White), mourning wars, fur trade significance for Native communities, missions and reductions as zones of interaction, and vast interior regions (e.g., Ohio Valley, pays d’en haut, New Mexico borderlands, Texas, California missions) that remained only lightly touched by direct Atlantic institutions for long periods. Population decline debates, environmental changes (ungulate irruption, sheep pastoralism emphasis in New Spain), and Pan-Indian movements (Pontiac’s War, Túpac Amaru) are framed as primarily continental processes with Atlantic ramifications rather than the reverse. Proponents argue that privileging the Atlantic risks marginalizing these interior histories and that "U.S. colonial history" has a "paradox" of exceptionalism that continental framing helps resolve (p. 279–80).

The volume presents this as a legitimate corrective rather than outright rejection of Atlantic history: the two can be complementary when Atlantic connections are not allowed to eclipse endogenous continental developments.

### The Hemispheric Perspective

Associated with Jack P. Greene among others, the hemispheric approach treats the Americas as a comparative unit, highlighting both shared patterns and sharp internal contrasts across the hemisphere (indexed at pp. 212, 301, 307–12). "Approaches to" it occupy pp. 308–10 with suggested "research, possible areas for" at p. 311. It engages Latin American historiography directly ("Latin America, colonial, 300–303"; "historians of, 72–73"; "contrasts with, 302–304").

Indexed markers of hemispheric analysis include "colonial heterogeneity" (p. 124, 126) vs. "colonial homogeneity" (p. 126, 128), "inclusionary colonies" (p. 304–305) versus "exclusionary colonies" (p. 305), "New Philology approach" (p. 303; James Lockhart pp. 62, 301–303 and Stuart Schwartz pp. 11, 301–302), and attention to the diversity of Spanish American viceroyalties, borderlands (New Mexico, Florida, Texas, Río de la Plata), and social structures (creoles, free people of color, mestizaje).

Hemispheric work often contrasts the densely bureaucratic, silver-centered, inclusionary Iberian systems (with extensive indigenous incorporation via encomienda, missions, and later Bourbon reforms) against the more settler-driven, plantation-oriented, and (in some accounts) exclusionary British and French models. It also surfaces in demographic comparisons (North American vs. South American population estimates pp. 283–86, 290–91n1) and in studies of missions, legal pluralism, and creolization across the hemisphere. Greene and others position it as bridging Atlantic and area-studies traditions while avoiding over-emphasis on any single imperial or oceanic frame.

### Global and "Beyond Atlantic" Perspectives

Several contributions and indexed topics push Atlantic history outward or subordinate it to global or world-historical frames. "Global history, and place for Atlantic history" is treated at p. 321 (Dirk Hoerder pp. 321–22, 326–27). "World systems" receive coverage at pp. 346–48 (Immanuel Wallerstein, André Gunder Frank, Eric Hobsbawm). The "California School" (pp. 345–47) is highlighted for its comparative global-economic approach that recenters Asia (especially China) and challenges narratives of inevitable Western divergence. "Integration and endogenous growth, theory of" appears at pp. 350–51 (Luis Rivera-Batiz and Paul M. Romer).

Specific connective threads indexed include:

- Silver flows to China and the Manila Galleon (pp. 344, 349; "China, and American silver, 346"; "silver... and China, 342, 346").
- Rice trade (p. 343) and Indian Ocean emporia/circuits (pp. 345–46, 319, 349).
- "Atlantic as pathway to Pacific" and European exploration of the Pacific (Cook, Vancouver, Mackenzie, Vizcaíno; pp. 287, 289).
- "Industrious revolution" (Jan de Vries, pp. 319, 325) and debates on links between Atlantic trade and European industrialization or proto-industrialization (pp. 267, 270, 319, 342).
- Broader commodity circuits, re-exports, and intra-European trade that embedded the Atlantic in larger Eurasian and global systems.

Peter Coclanis and Nicholas Canny (indexed references in the volume) exemplify the argument that Atlantic history is most powerful when treated as one circuit among many in a "conjunctural" or global framework rather than a self-contained arena. The index also notes "Atlantic as subsection of world history, 130." "Production modes, articulation of" (p. 348–49) and "Marxist approach" / "Neo-Marxist approach" (pp. 347–48) further signal engagement with structural global analyses.

These perspectives do not dismiss Atlantic work but insist on its limits: the basin was never closed; key drivers (silver prices, textile demand, cowrie currencies, knowledge circulation) often originated or terminated outside it; and some of the most consequential transformations (e.g., integration of the Pacific or shifts in Asian economies) require scales larger than the Atlantic.

### Synthesis and Implications

Part III underscores the volume editors' "guarded enthusiasm." Atlantic history excels for certain connective, diasporic, and comparative questions in the early modern period but is incomplete or distorting when applied in isolation. Continental, hemispheric, and global frames are presented as complementary tools rather than rivals; best practice involves explicit attention to which scale best illuminates a given problem and to the interplay among them. The indexed debates on "comparative history" (multiple clusters) and on "colonial heterogeneity" reinforce the call for precision about what any single frame includes or occludes. These discussions directly inform the six propositions for Atlantic history outlined in the introduction and reinforce the need for chronology, leading-sector focus, and balanced attention to connections versus internal developments.

## Old Worlds and the Atlantic: Impacts on Indigenous America, Africa, and Europe (Part II)

The volume's Part II examines how Atlantic integration affected the three "Old Worlds" — with emphasis on agency, limits of penetration, and reciprocal (not one-way) effects. The central finding across contributions is that Atlantic influences were powerful but uneven, often more consequential for the Atlantic system itself than transformative for the sending societies' deep interiors or core structures.

### Indigenous America
Atlantic contact triggered crises and transformations of invaded societies across the Americas, from coastal Brazil and the Caribbean to North American frontiers and the Spanish borderlands. Demographic collapse from disease and violence combined with mission systems, environmental transformations (e.g., introduction of livestock leading to "plague of sheep"), resistance movements, and shifting alliances produced new hybrid or reconfigured polities. Coastal and mission zones experienced the most intense restructuring; interior and "barbarian" frontiers (Guaraní reductions, Chaco, Sonora, California missions, Great Plains horse cultures) showed both destruction and adaptation, with indigenous groups exercising significant agency through flight, revolt, and selective adoption. The Atlantic lens reveals these not as isolated "frontier" stories but as integrated into broader flows of pathogens, goods, and people — yet also underscores that vast continental interiors remained little touched by direct Atlantic institutions for centuries.

### Africa and the Atlantic (Philip D. Morgan)
**Pre-contact baseline**: "Before the arrival of Europeans, the Atlantic Ocean played little role in most Africans’ lives... a combination of prevailing winds and currents, lack of sheltered seas, few natural harbors, treacherous offshore bars, and heavy surf inhibited an indigenous seafaring tradition... Africans used waterways, but generally not the sea." Dugout canoes existed but without sails or advanced navigation; population pressures were absent. 

**Transformation by ~1820**: ~10 million Africans transported across the ocean (largest forced movement in history); prior to 1820, "about four Africans arrived in the Americas for every European." Atlantic trade value rose sixfold 1680s–1780s, reaching ~£47 million sterling at peak; by 1780s slaves comprised "over 90 percent of the value of all African exports." New ports (Luanda, Ouidah, Bonny, Old Calabar, Anomabu) and maritime expertise (Kru canoemen, Gold Coast sailors) developed.

**Agency**: Despite European initiation of sea routes, "a voluntary partnership best captures the relationship between African traders and rulers and European merchants and ship captains. Africans called the tune in many aspects of this relationship." Trade conducted on African terms (offshore or littoral only; Europeans "perched precariously on the edge of the shore"); Africans landlords, Europeans tenants. "The term 'subordinate symbiosis' has been coined to describe European dependence." Africans were "eager, discriminating consumers" prioritizing textiles (half import value), followed by metals, spirits, tobacco, firearms. Terms of trade shifted "inexorably in their favor. By 1800, for each slave they sold, African traders generally received at least three or four times as much as a century earlier." "the Atlantic slave trade was largely a sellers’ market."

**Regionalism**: Enormous variation along 5,000 km coast. Seven regions per contemporary traders (Senegambia to West-Central Africa). Preferences dictated distinct cargoes; age/sex ratios, mortality, and shipboard revolt rates varied sharply by African region (not buyer or destination). "West-Central Africa was the opposite of Upper Guinea. This region exported the most people—almost half of all Africans—across the Atlantic." Upper Guinea least integrated despite proximity (political fragmentation, Islam, internal demand, resistance, delivery costs).

**Impact**: "The impact of growing Atlantic integration on African societies was undoubtedly significant, but its effects were mixed." Economic effects modest: "the ratio of overseas trade to domestic economic activity was far lower for the majority of Africans than for the typical inhabitant of Europe or the Americas." Imports supplementary; local industries resilient. Firearms "a component of a process of military transformation that was already underway"; not the primary driver of enslavement. New states (Asante, Dahomey) arose amid centralization, but "political fragmentation was still the dominant feature." Population of Atlantic Africa "appears to have remained fairly stable" (~22–23 million late 17th–late 18th c., perhaps ~20 million 1820), aided by New World crops (maize, manioc) offsetting losses; retained more captives internally than exported. Social effects more marked: enlargement and depersonalization of internal slavery (mostly women/children); intensified patriarchy, gerontocracy, stratification ("the rich became richer; the poor, poorer"). "For them [the uprooted], there could be no mitigation of their loss."

**Diaspora**: Debate polarized between continuity and creolization, but "there is considerable middle ground." African ethnic identities "flexible and inchoate" even at home; "Wolof rarely thought of themselves as Wolof." Middle Passage trauma profound yet "shipmate bond" formative; labels ("Coromantee," "Angola," "Mina") crude approximations. Specific retentions salient in places/times (Kongo practices in Brazil; Stono Rebellion rebels likely Kongo using homeland tactics/Catholicism; Akwamu captives in Danish St. John). Broad shared principles (aesthetics, religion) facilitated exchanges everywhere. "It was not just one-way; there was always a trickle... of Africans or people of African descent who moved east as well as west." Brazilian (Bahian) diaspora on Slave Coast fed ethnic terms back (e.g., "Nago").

**Conclusion**: "Africans played an important role... Not passive victims or unwitting dupes, they were active agents, voluntary partners, major shapers..." Yet "Atlantic influences were unequally diffused throughout Africa; much of the continent had hardly been penetrated by them." "Africans were more important to the Atlantic world than the Atlantic world was to Africans." Internal and other external diasporas (Sahara, Indian Ocean) remained significant.

### Europe and the Atlantic (Carla Rahn Phillips)
European expansion shaped and was shaped by Atlantic rivalries and trade from the outset. Iberian competition (Portugal vs. Castile) produced the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) and early island/ African/ American claims; Asian goals drove initial voyages but Atlantic profits sustained them. Rivalries propelled English (Cabot, Drake, privateering) and French (Brazilwood, Canada fisheries, Caribbean) challenges; Habsburg power rested partly on American revenues. Seventeenth-century European upheavals (Thirty Years' War, English Civil War, Portuguese revolt 1640) opened space for rivals to expand colonies and trade. Eighteenth-century wars (Seven Years' War, American Revolution) were transatlantic in stakes and repercussions (Britain gains Canada/Florida; Spain regains Florida temporarily; French financial strain precipitates Revolution).

Economically, transatlantic trade was central to Europe's three great early-modern phases. Spanish fleets (galeones system) and House of Trade funneled silver (Potosí peak ~280,000 kg/year late 1580s), hides, sugar, cochineal, indigo to Europe, fueling Price Revolution inflation and funding Habsburg policies. "American bullion undoubtedly added to the European money supply." Portuguese Brazil shifted from brazilwood to sugar (multiplier effects on mills, slaves, re-exports) then 18th-c. gold/diamonds (~14,600 kg/year average peak). Dutch interloping and later northern plantation colonies added volume. "the creation of the Atlantic world undoubtedly provided new venues for trade and colonization for Europe’s rising population." Debates persist on profits' role in industrialization (Hobsbawm/Frank/Wallerstein extractive thesis vs. O’Brien/Pomeranz complexity/minor role), but "it seems fair to say that many of the changes in European political and economic development... were linked to overseas exploration, colonization, trade, and the international rivalries they fomented."

Migration and cultural feedback (returnees, news, commodities) also altered European perceptions and possibilities. The Atlantic was not Europe's sole or always dominant theater (Asia trades competed), but its effects on state power, markets, material life, and self-understanding were profound and enduring through the age of revolutions.

## Historiography: Limits and Best Practice in Light of Old Worlds Evidence

The Part II essays reinforce the volume's five major objections (especially neglect of interiors, over-emphasis on connections, boundedness questions) while illustrating the propositions: regional variation and African agency exemplify the need to balance contrasts; focus on leading sectors (ports, maritime specialists) and values/ideas (creolization, political discourse); chronology matters (uneven integration, pre- and post-1825 phases). Old Worlds material shows Atlantic history's strength in tracing reciprocal impacts without assuming European primacy or uniform transformation.

## National Atlantics: Distinctiveness and Atlantic Lens (Part I)

The volume's Part I examines the five principal European national Atlantics contemporaries themselves recognized. These were competitive, often nationally inflected spheres rather than a seamless integrated space, yet the Atlantic framework reveals both what was distinctive and what was advanced by cross-imperial comparison.

**Spanish Atlantic** (Kenneth J. Andrien): The first great wave of European expansion. Created a vast, "closed" system from the Caribbean through the viceroyalties of New Spain and Peru, centered on silver extraction (Potosí, Zacatecas), a massive professional bureaucracy (viceroys, audiencias, corregidores), strict mercantile regulation via Seville/Cádiz flotas, and Roman Catholic orthodoxy enforced by the Inquisition. Encomienda and later repartimiento structured indigenous labor and tribute. Massive demographic collapse (Mexico from 20–25 million to under 1.5 million) followed by recovery, racial mixing producing a "New World" neither purely European, indigenous, nor African, and creole empowerment through venal office sales. Bourbon reforms (post-1740s) attempted recentralization, free trade (comercio libre), intendants, and Church curbs, provoking revolts (Comuneros, Túpac Amaru) before independence wars 1808–1825 unraveled the trunk-and-feeder market networks. Atlantic perspective highlights inter-imperial competition, contraband porosity, and the system's internal diversity (central zones vs. frontiers). Quote: “the Spanish Atlantic system was ethnically, culturally, and geographically diverse, yet constituted a defined political, economic, and religious space that held together from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century.” (Ch. 2)

**Portuguese Atlantic** (A. J. R. Russell-Wood): Earliest sustained European presence (Ceuta 1415). Distinguished by archipelago hubs (Madeira, Azores, Cape Verdes, São Tomé) as way stations and articulators between North/South Atlantic and hemispheres; symbiotic South Atlantic pairing of Brazil and Angola; early crown captaincies giving way to royal government; high rates of miscegenation and manumission producing creole/mestiço elites and fluid identities; distinctive institutions (senado da câmara municipal councils, Santa Casa de Misericórdia brotherhoods) operating with local negotiation rather than rigid central control. The Atlantic facilitated triangular trades (Portugal–Africa–Brazil), contraband, and cultural exchanges (African crops, Brazilian tobacco/rum to Africa). Unlike Iberian closed models or later northern ones, Portuguese emphasized intercolonial networks often without metropolitan component. Quote: “Brazil and Angola were unparalleled in size... passage between Portuguese Africa and Portuguese America presented no major navigational challenges.” (Ch. 3)

**British Atlantic** (Trevor Burnard): Late entrant (post-1580s, serious settlement 1620s+), rapid eighteenth-century growth to 23 colonies and nearly 2 million people by 1760. Exceptional in rapid population doubling (Franklin), plantation maturation (Barbados model exported), and convergence of creole elites around gentility, consumer goods, and inherited English liberties despite regional diversity (New England vs. Chesapeake vs. Caribbean). Atlantic lens advances incorporation of Africans (majority of migrants; 2.3M vs. 1M Europeans 1600–1800) and the centrality of the West Indies (wealthiest, most diplomatically vital); highlights comparisons (vs. Spanish "Black Legend," French challenge); notes violence, slavery's "dark undercurrent," and limits of assimilationist narratives. Chronology: imagining (1580–mid-1620s), surge/settlement (mid-1620s–1680s), consolidation/integration (early 18th c.), crisis/revolution (late 18th). Quote: “the British Atlantic had by the mid-eighteenth century become so powerful... that farsighted thinkers speculated that before long Britain’s American possessions would outstrip Britain itself.” (Ch. 4)

**French Atlantic** (Laurent Dubois): Geographically diverse crescent from Canada to Caribbean and Guiana, but economic core in the Caribbean plantations (Saint-Domingue "pearl," Martinique/Guadeloupe). Centralized via the Marine, Code Noir (1685/1724), and Coutume de Paris; relatively few voluntary migrants (Canada ~70k total, Louisiana tiny) vs. ~1.1M enslaved Africans imported (heaviest to Saint-Domingue). Intercultural alliances and "middle ground" with Native Americans (fur trade, missions); Jesuits prominent in knowledge production and reductions. The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) as quintessential Atlantic event—ideas, actors, repercussions ricocheting across ocean—destroyed the largest slave society and produced enduring legacies (Québec sovereignty rhetoric, indemnity demands). Atlantic approach connects prerevolutionary Saint-Domingue, Enlightenment circuits, and post-independence cultural dialogues (Negritude, Price-Mars). Quote: debates over rights “reverberate and ricochet back and forth across the ocean.” (Ch. 5, citing Dubois)

**Dutch Atlantic** (Benjamin Schmidt): Brief but intense "exemplary" moment under WIC (1621+), with holdings in New Netherland, New Holland (Brazil), West Africa (Elmina, Luanda), Caribbean entrepôts, and Wild Coast. Distinct origins in anti-Habsburg revolt rhetoric framing the Atlantic as site of "Spanish tyrannies" and potential "alliance" with indigenous "brethren"; rapid success in transit trade and plantation processing more than territorial empire. Post-1654/1664/1674 losses shifted to carrying trade serving other empires' economies (sugar credit, molasses, slaves). Cultural geography shifted from proudly "Dutch" images (Johan Maurits era celebrations, Post, Eckhout) to "universal"/globalist representations in later geographies (Dapper, Montanus, Nieuhof) that effaced or diluted national specificity. "There was no such thing as a Dutch Atlantic" in the sense of sustained imperial model matching British/Spanish; instead a Sonderweg of commercial integration and conceptual globalism. Quote: “the Dutch Atlantic transformed itself... from a promising locus of Dutch empire to a lucrative venue for the Dutch transit trade.” (Ch. 6)

These national essays demonstrate that contemporaries operated within competitive, nationally inflected spheres even while crossing boundaries via contraband, migration, and shared knowledge. The Atlantic lens both reveals distinctive trajectories and facilitates the comparisons the editors recommend.

## Historiography Note

This entry draws directly from the 2009 Greene & Morgan volume, itself a product of practitioner self-reflection after two decades of rapid institutional growth. The field’s trajectory—from perspective to (contested) field—illustrates broader late-20th/early-21st century shifts toward transnational, oceanic, and global scales of analysis. Subsequent work has continued to test the propositions (especially ideas/religion, chronology, and relations to global/continental frames). The volume remains a touchstone for anyone using or critiquing the Atlantic framework.

The Part III material (continental, hemispheric, global/beyond) significantly expands the self-critical dimension: it registers explicit coverage of "continental approach" (pp. 280–81, 300, 308), "hemispheric perspective" (pp. 301, 307–12), "global history, and place for Atlantic history" (p. 321), California School debates (pp. 345–47), world-systems analysis (pp. 346–48), and theories of integration vs. endogenous growth (pp. 350–51).
