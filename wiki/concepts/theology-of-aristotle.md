## Definition and Scope

The **Theology of Aristotle** (*Theologia Aristotelis*, Arabic *Uthūlūjiyā Aristūtālis*) is a pseudonymous Arabic philosophical text compiled in the circle of [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]] in ninth-century Baghdad. It consists primarily of a paraphrased Arabic adaptation of selections from Plotinus' *Enneads* (Books IV–VI), rearranged and rewritten so that their Neoplatonic content — the hierarchy of the One, Intellect, and Soul; the soul's descent into the body and its capacity for ascent; the absolute simplicity of the First Principle — is attributed to Aristotle and presented as his esoteric theology, the crowning achievement of the *Metaphysics*.

The text is not a translation. It is an interpretive rewriting. The authors of the *Theology* — most probably members of al-Kindī's translation circle, possibly including al-Kindī himself in some capacity — freely rearranged, abbreviated, and elaborated upon Plotinus' arguments to suit their theological and philosophical purposes. "Philosophical topics that did not exist as such before Plotinus and were created out of his rethinking of Platonism . . . reappear in the formative period of falsafa. They equal, in the eyes of many Islamic philosophers, the doctrine of the Greeks dealing with divinity, crowning the study of 'what is after the physics'" (D'Ancona, ch. 2, in [[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]]).

**Emic usage**: Medieval Islamic readers understood the text as Aristotle's own theological doctrine, the culmination of first philosophy. This misattribution was not corrected in the Arabic tradition.

**Etic usage**: Modern scholars identify the text as a pseudepigraphon and central document in the Arabic reception of Neoplatonism. The term *Plotiniana Arabica* is used collectively for the Arabic materials derived from Plotinus.

## Content and Structure

The *Theology* opens with a prologue that presents the text as Aristotle's exposition of how the First Cause acts upon creation — not directly but through Intellect and World Soul, maintaining its own absolute simplicity. The main body covers:

- **Soul's descent and ascent**: Plotinus' account of the soul as simultaneously belonging to both the intelligible and sensible worlds (its "amphibious" nature) is presented as Aristotle's psychology. The soul descends into the body without fully abandoning the intelligible realm.
- **Intellect as first creature**: The One or True One creates Intellect as its first image. Plotinus' *nous* (eternal self-thinking mind, identical with the Platonic Forms) becomes the Theology's account of God's first creative act.
- **Creation through being**: The True One acts by the sheer power of its being (*bi-anniyyatihi faqat*, "through its very being alone"), without change or motion — a formula that Cristina D'Ancona has traced to pseudo-Dionysian influence and that became fundamental to subsequent Islamic theological discussion of divine causality.
- **Absolute divine transcendence**: The First Principle is beyond all predication, cannot be named or described, and is accessible only to the highest reaches of intellect in a form of mystical apprehension.

## The Companion Text: Liber de Causis

Closely related is the **Book of Aristotle's Exposition of the Pure Good** (*Kitāb al-īḍāḥ li-Aristūtālis fī al-khayr al-maḥḍ*), an Arabic rearrangement of Proclus' *Elements of Theology*, whose origin within the Kindī circle has been demonstrated by Gerhard Endress. When translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona in twelfth-century Toledo, this work became known as the **Liber de Causis** (*Book of Causes*) and was similarly attributed to Aristotle. Thomas Aquinas was the first Latin scholar to recognize, via William of Moerbeke's translation of Proclus, that the *Liber de Causis* derived from Proclus rather than Aristotle.

Both texts together — the *Theology* (from Plotinus) and the *Liber de Causis* (from Proclus) — gave Islamic and then Latin medieval philosophy a Neoplatonic rational theology they believed to be Aristotelian. This misattribution shaped the entire medieval reading of Aristotle's *Metaphysics*.

## Historical Significance

The *Theology of Aristotle* accomplished something philosophically decisive: it made Neoplatonic metaphysics — the One, emanation, the soul's capacity for return, the absolute transcendence of the First Principle — appear to be the doctrines of Aristotle himself, the acknowledged "First Teacher." This meant that when [[al-kindi|al-Kindī]], [[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]], and [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] read Aristotle's *Metaphysics*, they read it through a Neoplatonic lens that they took to be Aristotle's own.

Key consequences:
1. **Metaphysics as theology**: Al-Kindī interpreted the *Metaphysics* as primarily a study of God, not of being-qua-being. Al-Fārābī and Avicenna later corrected this, but even their corrections operated within a framework saturated by Plotinian Neoplatonism.
2. **Emanationist cosmology**: The hierarchy of Intellect, Soul, and Matter that characterizes Fārābian and Avicennan cosmology derives ultimately from Plotinus via the *Theology*, presented as Aristotelian.
3. **The Ismaili reception**: The [[ismaili-neoplatonism|Ismaili philosophical tradition]] drew heavily on the *Theology* and the Longer Version of it (which incorporates additional material matching Ismaili doctrine) as a philosophical source. See especially al-Nasafī, Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī, and al-Sijistānī.
4. **Latin scholasticism**: Via the *Liber de Causis*, the Proclean strand of Arabic Neoplatonism entered Latin philosophical theology. The *Liber de Causis* was read as Aristotle's highest wisdom until Aquinas identified its true source.

## Historiography

The pseudonymous character of the *Theology* was first demonstrated in modern scholarship by F. W. Zimmermann ("The Origins of the So-Called Theology of Aristotle," 1986). Prior to this, the text had long been known to differ from Aristotle's authentic works, but its exact source and the mechanisms of its production were unclear. Cristina D'Ancona's work has traced the process of Neoplatonic reception in Arabic in detail, showing that the intermingling of Aristotle and Neoplatonism occurred primarily in the Aristotelian works read within a Neoplatonic framework, and only secondarily in pseudo-texts like the *Theology* itself.

The **Longer Version** of the *Theology* (incorporating all of the shorter version plus additional passages) is an important and still imperfectly understood text that shows clear affinities with early Ismaili doctrine, raising questions about whether the Ismailis shaped the Longer Version or drew from a shared earlier source.
