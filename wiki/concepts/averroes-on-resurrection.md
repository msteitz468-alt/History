## Definition and Scope

Discussions 2 and 3 of the Physical Sciences section of [[tahafut-al-tahafut]] address two related questions: (1) whether the philosophers can demonstrate that the human soul is a spiritual substance existing independently of the body, and (2) whether the soul, once in existence, can perish. These discussions form Averroes' response to [[ghazali-on-the-soul]] and bear directly on the question of bodily resurrection that al-Ghazālī raises in Discussion 4. The pages connect directly to [[islamic-philosophy-of-soul]] and [[bodily-resurrection-islam]].

Averroes' position in these discussions is notably qualified and sometimes deliberately agnostic. He defends the philosophers' arguments against al-Ghazālī's dialectical objections, often showing those objections to be based on misreadings, while himself acknowledging that "the discussion of the soul is very obscure" and not fully settled even among philosophers. This makes his treatment distinctive: he neither simply rejects bodily resurrection nor affirms corporeal immortality, but argues that the philosophical question of soul-survival is genuinely hard and that al-Ghazālī's criticism does not succeed in dismantling the philosophers' proofs.

## Discussion 2: Can Philosophers Demonstrate That the Soul Is a Spiritual Substance?

### Al-Ghazālī's Challenge

Al-Ghazālī presents and criticizes ten philosophical proofs that the human rational soul is an incorporeal substance — that is, that intellectual cognition cannot be a function of any bodily organ. These draw largely on Avicenna ([[emanationism-avicennan]], [[active-intellect-arabic-philosophy]]). The soul's faculties are mapped in detail: external senses, common sense, estimative faculty, cogitative faculty, retentive and memorative faculties, motive faculties, and the rational/intellective soul with its speculative and practical aspects.

Al-Ghazālī's overarching dialectical strategy is not to refute these proofs decisively but to show they fall short of *demonstrative certainty* — that equally good objections can be raised against each, so the philosophers are not entitled to claim rational knowledge (as opposed to probable belief) about the soul's incorporeality.

### Averroes' Assessment of the Proofs

Averroes works through each proof, usually finding that al-Ghazālī's objections are based on equivocation or failure to follow the argument properly, but sometimes conceding that specific proofs are incomplete as al-Ghazālī presented them.

**Proof 1 — Indivisibility of intellectual cognition:**  
Philosophers argue that intellectual concepts are indivisible and therefore cannot inhere in a divisible body. Al-Ghazālī counters that the estimative faculty of animals (e.g., the sheep's perception of the wolf's hostility) is also apparently indivisible, yet the philosophers locate it in a bodily organ. Averroes' response: al-Ghazālī has confused two types of divisibility. Attributes in bodies can be divided in two ways — (a) such that every part has the same definition as the whole (like whiteness), or (b) such that the attribute is attached without specific shape and diminishes with the substratum (like the faculty of sight, which is weaker in the sick than the young). The universal intelligibles are not divisible in *either* way, which is the philosophers' actual argument; al-Ghazālī exploited the first sense and made his objection with the second, "and in doing this he committed a sophism; but the science of the soul is too profound and too elevated to be apprehended by dialectics."

**Proof 7 — The intellect is not fatigued by its objects:**  
Bodily perceptive faculties are degraded by strong stimuli — a loud noise reduces subsequent hearing, a bright light reduces subsequent vision. But the intellect behaves oppositely: perceiving a stronger intelligible *strengthens* subsequent perception of a weaker one. Averroes endorses this proof as genuinely compelling:

> "Every substratum which is impressed congruously or incongruously by the inherence of the form in it, be it little or much, is necessarily corporeal, and the reverse is also true, namely that everything corporeal is impressed by the form which is realized in it... And the cause of this is that every becoming is the consequence of a change, and if a form could inhere in a body without a change it might happen that there could be a form whose realization did not impress its substratum."

Since the reception of intelligibles leaves no impression on the intellect (no "wearing out"), the intellect's receptive faculty is not corporeal.

**Proof 8 — The intellect strengthens after forty years:**  
Bodily faculties peak and decline; the intellect typically strengthens in middle and later life even as the body weakens. Al-Ghazālī's objection — that faculties might peak at different times for many reasons — is acknowledged by Averroes as a genuine limitation: "When it is assumed that the substratum of the perceptive faculties is the natural heat, and that natural heat suffers diminution after forty years, then intellect must behave in the same way in this respect; that is, if its substratum is natural heat, then it is necessary that the intellect should become old as the natural heat becomes old." The philosophers' proof is good only *if* the substrata for intellect and sense are the same natural heat.

**Proof 5 — The intellect knows itself:**  
Bodily faculties do not perceive themselves (sight does not see itself). The intellect, however, knows itself as it knows other things. Al-Ghazālī: this might just be a difference among types of senses, not evidence of incorporeality. Averroes' reply shows why this objection fails:

> "Perception is something which exists between the agent and the patient, and it consists of the perceiver and the perceived. It is impossible that a sense should be in one and the same respect its own agent and patient, and the duality of agent and patient in sense arises, as concerns its act, from the side of the form and, as concerns its passivity, from the side of the matter. But no composite can think itself, because if this were so, its essence would be different from that by which it thinks... and since intellect and intelligible are identical, if the composite thought its essence, the composite would become a simple, and the whole the part, and all this is impossible."

**Proof 10 — The intellect apprehends genuine universals:**  
The philosophers argue that the intellect grasps a single universal entity — the quiddity of a species — that is not divided among individuals and is not destroyed by the disappearance of any individual. This entity must be "ingenerable and incorruptible." Al-Ghazālī's nominalist counter: what the intellect retains is not a universal but an individual form that functions as a common representative for similar percepts (just as the imagination retains one image of water that serves for all individual stretches of water). Averroes' response: if al-Ghazālī were correct, "there would be no difference between sense-perception and the apprehension of the intellect" — but this is false, since intellectual cognition genuinely transcends particularity in a way the image does not.

### Averroes on Avicenna's Psychology

Averroes distances himself from Avicenna's detailed faculty-psychology, particularly the Avicennian "estimative faculty" (wahm) as a faculty distinct from the imaginative: "What appears from the theory of the ancients is that the imaginative faculty in the animal is that which determines that the wolf should be an enemy of the sheep... there is no need to introduce another faculty." He follows Aristotle over Avicenna on this point.

On the overall question of soul-survival, Averroes invokes Quranic authority for the soul's continuation after death and notes the comparison of death to sleep:

> "God says: 'God takes to Himself souls at the time of their death; and those who do not die in their sleep.' And the comparison of death with sleep in this question is an evident proof that the soul survives, since the activity of the soul ceases in sleep through the inactivity of its organ, but the existence of the soul does not cease."

This is not a demonstration but an argument suitable "for all" and pointing the learned toward the philosophical question of soul-survival. The overall message of Discussion 2 is that al-Ghazālī has not refuted the philosophers' proofs but has shown them to be dialectical rather than strictly demonstrative when presented out of context; the full demonstrative proof requires mastery of the prior sciences of the soul.

## Discussion 3: Can the Soul Perish After It Has Come Into Existence?

### The Philosophers' Argument for Immortality

Philosophers argue that the soul, once existent, cannot perish through:
1. **Corruption of the body** — for the rational soul (if incorporeal) is separated from the body and its corruption need not entail the soul's.
2. **An internal opposite** — a separate substance has no contrary that could destroy it.
3. **Divine annihilation** — the philosophers hold that God cannot annihilate what exists simply by power.

Al-Ghazālī's counter: (a) he does not grant that the soul is external to the body; (b) on Avicenna's own view that souls are numerically differentiated through bodies, if souls are attached to bodies, they must perish with them.

### Averroes' Discussion of Soul-Individuation

Averroes engages Avicenna's view carefully. Avicenna held that souls are numerically differentiated through their bodies (otherwise Zaid and Amr would share the same soul and the same knowledge). But if souls are thus individuated by bodies, it seems they cannot survive the body's destruction. Averroes' response is to distinguish types of relation: the destruction of one relatum does not necessarily destroy the other, as is shown by the relation between lover and beloved or iron and magnet.

He then introduces a remarkable speculative proposal for how souls can survive individually after the body's death:

> "He who claims the survival and the numerical plurality of souls should say that they are in a subtle matter, namely the animal warmth which emanates from the heavenly bodies, and this is a warmth which is not fire and in which there is not a principle of fire; in this warmth there are the souls which create the sublunary bodies and those which inhere in these bodies."

This invokes the Neoplatonic-Aristotelian framework of heavenly warmth as the medium through which souls are individuated — a "subtle matter" that is not the gross matter of sublunary bodies. The formative soul (the soul that creates and forms the body) is more fundamental than the body it creates:

> "Plato proves that the soul is separated from the body, for the soul creates and forms the body, and if the body were the condition for the existence of the soul, the soul would not have created it or formed it."

This "creating soul" is visible especially in animals generated by spontaneous generation (worms, mice, scorpions), but also in sexual generation. After the body's death, souls return to "their spiritual matter and to the subtle imperceptible bodies."

### Averroes on Bodily Resurrection

Discussion 4 (Averroes' response to al-Ghazālī on what the philosophers say about the afterlife) brings out the sharpest disagreement. The philosophers, on Averroes' reading, do not discuss miracles or post-mortem states as natural-philosophical questions, because these are "the principles on which religion is based and religion is the principle of the virtues":

> "The learned among the philosophers do not permit discussion or disputation about the principles of religion, and he who does such a thing needs, according to them, a severe lesson. For whereas every science has its principles, and every student of this science must concede its principles and may not interfere with them by denying them, this is still more obligatory in the practical science of religion."

He charges that those who attribute to the philosophers a denial of Abraham's miracle (that Abraham was thrown into fire but not burned, on the grounds that fire must burn) are "only asserted by heretical Muslims." The philosophers' position is that such things are religious principles to be accepted on authority, not subjected to philosophical refutation.

This is a distinctive evasion: Averroes neither affirms the philosopher's account of bodily resurrection (which the *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* charges the philosophers deny) nor explicitly denies it. Instead he insulates religious doctrine from philosophical disputation entirely.

## Significance

These discussions make Averroes one of the most careful analysts of the philosophy of mind in classical Islamic philosophy. His critique of Avicenna's faculty-psychology; his distinction between two types of bodily divisibility; his argument that the intellect's non-fatiguability proves its incorporeality; and his careful treatment of soul-individuation all became reference points in Latin Averroism (see [[latin-averroism]] and [[unity-of-intellect]]). The question of whether Averroes held that individual souls survive death, or that only the universal intellect survives, was intensely contested in Latin scholasticism and was a central concern of the [[the-three-condemnations]].

Averroes' refusal to engage directly with bodily resurrection as a philosophical question — his insistence that such questions belong to the jurisdiction of religion and not of natural philosophy — was read by his Latin critics as either evasion or covert denial. It represents his most explicit statement of the division of labor between philosophy and religion, developed further in [[averroes-defense-of-philosophy]].
