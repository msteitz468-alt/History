## Definition and Scope

The Byzantine theology of war is the framework within which the Eastern Roman Empire understood, justified, and conducted armed conflict from Late Antiquity through the twelfth century. It is emphatically **not** a theology of *holy war*. George T. Dennis, in his essay "Defenders of the Christian People: Holy War in Byzantium" (in Laiou and Mottahedeh, *The Crusades from the Perspective of Byzantium and the Muslim World*, Dumbarton Oaks, 2001), argues that the Byzantines rejected both the crusade and the jihad as alien and abhorrent, and that their own wars, while wrapped in religious ceremony, were **just wars** in the Roman imperial tradition — not holy wars in any meaningful theological sense.

Dennis's analytical framework requires three criteria for a war to qualify as "holy":
1. It must be **declared by a competent religious authority** (a pope or a caliph, not merely an emperor).
2. Its **objective must be religious** (protection or recovery of sacred shrines, forced conversion, etc.).
3. **Participants must be promised a spiritual reward** (remission of sins, assurance of paradise).

By all three criteria, Byzantine wars fail. They were declared by the **emperor** (not by the patriarch), fought for the **defense and recovery of imperial territory**, and did not promise participants spiritual merit for the act of killing.

## Origins and Development

### The Roman Imperial Inheritance

Byzantine war doctrine was continuous with ancient Roman practice. Wars were imperial wars, waged on behalf of the emperor and the people subject to him. The legions' adoption of the cross as standard after Constantine did not alter the fundamental character of military action as political and strategic rather than religious. Leo VI's *Taktika* — the most important military manual of the tenth century — states the foundational principle: all men ought to embrace peace, "but since the devil, the original killer of men, the enemy of our race, has made use of sin to bring men around to waging war, contrary to their basic nature, it is absolutely necessary for men to wage war in return against those whom the devil maneuvers and to take their stand with unflinchin resolve against nations who want war." War is necessary evil, not meritorious act.

### Leo VI's *Taktika* and the Just War Framework

Leo VI (r. 886–912) is the most articulate Byzantine theorist of war in the period. In the *Taktika* he articulates what Dennis identifies as a **just war** position:

- War may be waged only when the enemy has initiated hostilities and invaded Byzantine territory: "you do indeed have a just cause, inasmuch as the enemy has started an unjust war."
- Even then, the objective is peace: "The purpose of all wars is peace" (quoting Aristotle, *Politics* 7.13.8, also cited by Anna Komnene in defense of her father Alexios's military campaigns).
- The Byzantines were not to wage war against other peoples unless those others initiated aggression.
- "We must always prefer peace above all else, and refrain from war."

Leo also dismissed the Islamic jihad with imperial contempt: the Saracens "do not campaign out of a sense of military service and discipline, but rather out of a love of gain and license or, more exactly, in order to plunder on behalf of their faith."

### Nikephoros Phokas and the Patriarchal Refusal

The sharpest expression of the Byzantine theological position came in the controversy surrounding Emperor **Nikephoros Phokas** (r. 963–969), who requested that soldiers killed in battle against the Muslims be honored as **martyrs**. The church — specifically the patriarch — refused. The *Synopsis historiarum* of John Skylitzes records the refusal: the church rejected the equation of battlefield death with martyrdom.

This is the decisive institutional distinction from both the crusade and the jihad. The Byzantines observed religious services before battle (prayers, the Eucharistic liturgy), displayed the cross on standards, and used religious war cries such as "God help the Romans" and "The Cross is victorious." But these practices, Dennis argues, no more sanctified their wars than military chaplains and pre-game prayers sanctify modern football. Three liturgical offices (*akolouthiai*) that survive from the period honor soldiers who died in war with prayers for forgiveness of their sins — not with the celebration of their deaths as meritorious acts.

St. Basil's canon — that soldiers who killed in battle were to be refused communion for **three years** — was rarely enforced, but its existence on the books reflects the theological reality: killing, even when justified, was a sin requiring penance, not a holy act deserving spiritual reward.

### Monks, Saints, and "Holy War" of the Spirit

The only sense in which Byzantium recognized a form of holy warfare was in the **spiritual combat** of monks against demons. The **monk** was the frontline soldier in the war against Satan: Gregory of Nazianzos writes that the monk must night and day fight the "spiritual war" (*pneumatikos polemos*). Byzantine hagiography is filled with vivid accounts of saints battling demons in proper military formation — phalanxes of cavalry and infantry with iron breastplates, bows, and arrows, advancing under their commander (strategos) Satan. Saints repelled these attacks with the sign of the cross and wooden crosses made on the spot. The monk actively sought the demon-haunted desert rather than awaiting attack.

This spiritual combat, Dennis notes, could be called "holy" in Byzantine understanding — but it was the combat of monks, not of soldiers, and it was never explicitly called *hieros polemos* ("holy war") in Byzantine sources. The term *hieros polemos* itself, when it appears in Byzantine texts, refers only to the ancient Greek "Sacred Wars" over the oracle at Delphi (590, 449, 355–347 BCE), following Thucydides and Aristophanes.

### The Emperor as Holy — But Not as Priest

In one sense all Byzantine wars were holy because the **emperor** was holy, and wars were declared in his name. The emperor was called to imitate the Lord of heaven, to assure the well-being of his subjects, and to protect them from all dangers. But the emperor's holiness was that of imperial authority, not priestly or prophetic office. **The church left the call to arms entirely to the imperial government.** Unlike the Latin Church, which through Bernard of Clairvaux could preach extermination of the infidel (*delenda penitus*) and declare that killing an enemy of Christ was not homicide but *malecide*, the Byzantine church refused to authorize religious violence. The concept of monks as soldiers of Christ (*stratiotai Christou*, the Latin *miles Christi*) applied to the spiritual war, not to military action.

## The Three Critical Distinctions

Dennis explicitly contrasts Byzantine just war with crusade and jihad:

| Criterion | Jihad | Crusade | Byzantine War |
|---|---|---|---|
| Declared by | Caliph or religious authority | Pope | Emperor |
| Objective | Spread Islam universally by force | Protect/recover holy places; "exalt" Christianity | Defend empire; recover lost imperial territory |
| Spiritual reward for killing | Yes — martyrdom, paradise | Yes — remission of sins | No — killing requires penance |
| Aggressive/defensive character | Aggressive by nature | Initially defensive, became aggressive | Primarily defensive by doctrine |
| Church authorization of violence | Yes | Yes | No |

Dennis concludes: "Muslims believed force might be used to bring all people under the sway of Islam; Western knights believed that they were called not only to defend but to 'exalt' Christianity and that attacks on its enemies could be holy and meritorious. The Byzantines believed that war was neither good nor holy, but was evil and could be justified only in certain conditions that centered on the defense of the empire and its faith."

## Implications for the Byzantine Response to the Crusades

The Byzantine incomprehension of the crusade, documented by Anna Komnene in the *Alexiad*, flows directly from this theology. The Byzantines understood pilgrimage and they understood warfare, "but the conjoining of the two they did not understand." Emperor Alexios I Komnenos treated the crusaders civilly but was always nervous; the Byzantines were more interested in recovering Antioch for its strategic value than Jerusalem for its sentimental value. Constantinople was the New Jerusalem. The preaching of St. Bernard of Clairvaux — calling for the extermination of the infidel and declaring that killing an enemy of Christ was *malecide*, not homicide — would have been deeply alien and abhorrent to Byzantine sensibility. The idea of Templars — monks who wielded lethal weapons — was a scandal.

The events of 1204, when crusaders sacked Constantinople, confirmed Byzantine suspicions that the Latin knights had conquest rather than holy war in mind. Subsequently, Byzantines came to regard the Latins as a greater threat than the Muslims, and Byzantine writers from the seventh to the fourteenth century expressed "utter repugnance" for the jihad doctrine while also rejecting the crusading ideology as foreign.

## Scholarly Debates and Limitations

- Some modern scholars have used Byzantine religious rhetoric to describe later Byzantine campaigns — for example, Manuel I Komnenos's campaign against the Turks in 1176 — as a "sort of crusade." Dennis and the Laiou-Mottahedeh volume treat this as a misreading of the evidence.
- A detailed study of Byzantine "holy war" (*hieros polemos*) by A. Kolia-Dermitzakes (Athens, 1991) provides the most comprehensive treatment of the terminology; Dennis builds on it while reaching more restrictive conclusions.
- The canon of St. Basil was rarely enforced, raising the question of how significant the theoretical prohibition on killing was in practice.
