# Ibn Khaldun's Seven-Zones Geography

The **Seven-Zones Geography** in [[ibn-khaldun-muqaddimah|the Muqaddimah]]'s Second Prefatory
Discussion is [[ibn-khaldun|Ibn Khaldun]]'s detailed account of the physical structure of the
inhabited world (*al-arḍ al-maʿmūra*). It serves as the geographical foundation for his
environmental theory of civilization and his survey of the world's major regions, peoples, rivers,
and seas. Ibn Khaldun adapts this framework from Ptolemy's *Geography* and al-Idrisi's *Book of
Roger* (*Nuzhat al-mushtaq*, c. 1154).

---

## The Framework

The cultivated earth is described as a roughly circular land mass floating in a surrounding ocean
(*al-baḥr al-muḥīṭ*, the Surrounding Sea). It covers approximately one-quarter of the earth's
sphere. Within this cultivated portion, civilization is organized into seven latitudinal bands
(**zones**) running east to west, stacked from the equator northward:

| Zone | Northern boundary (Ptolemy) | Longest day |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 16° N | 13 hours |
| 2 | 20° N | 13.5 hours |
| 3 | 27° N | 14 hours |
| 4 | 33° N | 14.5 hours |
| 5 | 38° N | 15 hours |
| 6 | 43° N | 15.5 hours |
| 7 | 48° N | 16 hours |

Beyond the seventh zone, civilization ends at approximately 64° N because of cold and frost.
South of the equator, waste regions prevail because of excessive heat. Ibn Khaldun notes competing
measurements from Ptolemy, Ishaq b. al-Hasan al-Khazini, and Abu Jafar al-Khazini but does not
adjudicate definitively between them.

Each zone is further subdivided lengthwise into **ten sections** from west to east, giving a
70-cell grid of the inhabited world. For each section, geographers record countries, cities,
mountains, rivers, and traveling distances.

## The Geographical Survey

Chapter I contains an extensive — and for the Muqaddimah somewhat unusual — regional survey of
the known world zone by zone, section by section. Key features:

**Zone 1 (equatorial belt):** Contains the Sudanese Nile (flowing into the Surrounding Sea), the
kingdom of Ghana and Takrur, the Mali empire, the land of Kanem, Nubia, Abyssinia, the Red Sea,
Yemen, and the southern coast of the Indian Ocean. Ibn Khaldun notes that peoples south of Ghana
"are closer to dumb animals than to rational beings," attributing this to environmental extremity
rather than inherent character.

**Zone 2:** Includes the desert routes of the Maghrib to the Sudan, the Fezzan, Upper Egypt
(along the Nile), the Hijaz, Mecca, Medina, and the Persian Gulf coast.

**Zone 3:** The Atlas Mountain region, Maghrib (Morocco, Sus, Sigilmasa), Mediterranean North
Africa, and extending east to parts of the Arabian Peninsula.

**Zones 4–7:** The most densely civilized regions: Mediterranean Europe (Castile, France, Venice,
Rome, Constantinople), the Black Sea, the Caspian, Central Asia (Khurasan, Transoxiana), Persia,
the Caucasus, and eventually the northern steppe peoples (Bulgars, Pechenegs, Bashkirs).

**Major seas described:** The Surrounding Sea; the Mediterranean ("Roman Sea"); the Indian Ocean
(Chinese/Abyssinian Sea); the Red Sea; the Persian Gulf; the Black Sea; the Adriatic; the Caspian
("Sea of Jurjan and Tabaristan").

**Four great rivers:** The Nile (Egyptian and Sudanese branches from the Mountain of the Moon/Qumr);
the Euphrates (from Armenia through Syria); the Tigris (from Armenia through Mosul and Baghdad);
and the Oxus (from Balkh northward through Khurasan to Lake Aral).

## The Canary Islands: An Ethnographic Aside

Ibn Khaldun includes a remarkable passage on the **Canary Islands** (the "Eternal Islands" from
which Ptolemy began measuring longitude):

> "We have heard that European Christian ships reached them in the middle of this century, fought
> with the inhabitants, plundered them, captured some of them, and sold some of the captives along
> the Moroccan coast where they came into the service of the ruler."

The captives, once they had learned Arabic, reported that: the islanders tilled soil with horns
(lacking iron); their bread was barley; their animals were goats; they fought by hurling stones
backwards; they worshipped by prostrating themselves before the rising sun; they had no revealed
religion and had received no missionary activity. Ibn Khaldun remarks that the islands can only be
reached by accident, not by intentional navigation, because no compass chart exists for the
Surrounding Sea.

This passage is one of the earliest Arabic accounts of the pre-conquest Guanche population of the
Canaries and reflects Ibn Khaldun's awareness of European Atlantic expansion in the fourteenth
century.

## Purpose Within the Muqaddimah

The geographical survey is not merely decorative. It serves three functions:

1. **Empirical grounding:** It establishes that Ibn Khaldun is drawing on actual geographical
   knowledge of the inhabited world, not speculative cosmology.
2. **Environmental argument:** The zone-by-zone description provides concrete evidence for the
   claim that civilization is denser in the middle zones and thinner at the extremes.
3. **Scope definition:** The survey maps the spatial domain within which the analytical framework
   of [[ilm-al-umran|ʿilm al-ʿumrān]] applies. Ibn Khaldun explicitly states his primary focus is
   the Maghrib and the Arab East; the global survey establishes that framework before he narrows.

---

## Relationship to al-Idrisi

Ibn Khaldun explicitly names **al-Idrisi** (al-ʿAlawi al-Idrisi al-Hammudi) as his primary
cartographic model. Al-Idrisi composed the *Nuzhat al-mushtaq* ("Book of Roger") for Roger II,
the Norman king of Sicily, around 1154. This work synthesized Islamic geographical knowledge —
drawing on al-Masudi, Ibn Khurradadhbih, and others — and produced a world map oriented with
south at the top (following the convention of Arabic cartography). Ibn Khaldun follows al-Idrisi's
zone-and-section structure and cites his regional data extensively.

The relationship illustrates the transmission of geographical knowledge across the Islamic world
and its incorporation into Ibn Khaldun's analytical framework: Ptolemaic cosmology → Arabic
translation and adaptation → al-Idrisi's synthesis → Ibn Khaldun's sociological application.

## Limitations

- Ibn Khaldun's geography of sub-Saharan Africa (below the Mali empire) is thin and based
  primarily on travelers' accounts filtered through Maghribi commercial knowledge.
- The far east (China, the Korean islands, Southeast Asia) is mentioned but poorly described.
- The Americas are entirely absent, as expected for a fourteenth-century Islamic geographer.
- Northern and Eastern Europe are described schematically.

These limitations are consistent with the collection coverage gap noted in
[[ibn-khaldun-on-environment-and-civilization|the environmental theory page]].
