## Definition and Scope

Ottoman philosophy denotes the tradition of rational inquiry practiced in the [[ottoman-empire]] from the late medieval period through the empire's dissolution. It operated within a distinctive institutional setting: the *ulema* (learned class), closely tied to the Ottoman state, maintained a madrasa curriculum in which logic, mathematics, astronomy, and theological philosophy (*kalām*) were standard subjects alongside law and Koran commentary. Ottoman philosophy was predominantly Sunni — in explicit contrast to the [[school-of-isfahan]] in Shiite Safavid Iran — and engaged heavily with the classical Ashʿarite kalām tradition.

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Chs. 57–59.

## Historical Context

The Ottoman empire took its name from Osman (d. c. 1326), who won a first victory against the Byzantines in 1305. Ottoman expansion into the Balkans and Anatolia, a setback at the Battle of Ankara (1402) against Tamerlane, and the decisive fall of Constantinople in 1453 (Mehmed II) shaped the empire's political and intellectual self-understanding. The sultans' claim to be defenders of Sunni Islam was reinforced when Selīm I (r. 1512–20) took the holy cities of Mecca and Medina (1517) from the Mamluks. The golden age under Süleymān the Magnificent (r. 1520–66) — when Kinalizāde hailed him as a philosopher-king — coincided with the institutional peak of Ottoman madrasa culture.

The Safavid-Ottoman rivalry was not merely political but sectarian: the Safavids' Shiism was precisely what made them natural enemies in Ottoman eyes. Many Sunni scholars relocated from Safavid Iran to the Ottoman realm, bringing with them the philosophical ideas of the "school of Shīrāz" (Dawānī, the Dashtakīs, Mīr Dāmād). See [[school-of-isfahan]].

## Key Figures and Movements

### Early Ottoman Period

**Mollā Fenārī** (d. 1431) — the first *shaykh al-islām* of the Ottomans — carried on the tradition of philosophical Sufism stemming from [[ibn-arabi]] and al-Qūnawī. He defended the "unity of existence" (*waḥdat al-wujūd*) against critics like al-Taftāzānī, using Avicennan philosophical tools to articulate the Sufi metaphysical position. He commented on al-Qūnawī and wrote a commentary on the logical *Introduction* of al-Abharī (completed, he claims, in a single day).

**ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn al-Qūshjī** (fl. 15th c.) — a scientist originally from Central Asia who worked at Ulegh Beg's observatory in Samarqand before settling in Istanbul. He contributed to the autonomy of mathematical astronomy from Aristotelian natural philosophy: the mathematical astronomer need not presuppose Aristotle's physical cosmology; he merely models observed phenomena geometrically. Al-Qūshjī recognized that for all we know the earth might be rotating, since this cannot be excluded by observation.

**Khojazāda** (d. 1488) and **ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī** — two scholars commissioned by Mehmed II to write responses to al-Ghazālī's *Incoherence of the Philosophers*. This was a genuine philosophical contest with monetary reward and a robe for the winner (Khojazāda won). Both were broadly sympathetic to al-Ghazālī's conclusions while critical of his specific arguments. Khojazāda offered an original argument against the eternity of the universe: the infinite past times, though no longer real in the concrete world, exist mentally in God's omniscient mind with an ordered "before and after" — yielding the problematic kind of actual infinity if the past is infinite.

**ʿAlī Çelebī Kinalizāde** (d. 1572) — produced *Ahlāk-ı ʿAlāʾī* (The Exalted Ethics), a reworking of Dawānī's ethics (itself a reworking of al-Ṭūsī's *Ethics for Nāṣir*). Kinalizāde used this framework to provide a philosophical justification for Ottoman social hierarchy: four groups (soldiers, scholars, craftsmen, farmers) must be kept in Platonic balance. He praised Süleymān as a philosopher-king.

**Kātib Çelebī** (d. 1657) — the most significant broad intellectual of the Ottoman seventeenth century. Author of a bibliographical dictionary, a geography, and *Mīzān al-ḥaqq* (The Balance of Truth), which defended a pragmatic, moderate stance in the cultural wars of his day. He argued for tolerance of coffee-drinking and tobacco-smoking, defended Ibn ʿArabī against his critics, and insisted on the value of logic, mathematics, and astronomy for religious scholars. He was critical of the Ḳāḍīzādelī movement (see below) without sharing the extreme anti-rationalism he also opposed.

### The Ḳāḍīzādelī Movement

The **Ḳāḍīzādelīs**, named after the preacher Mehmed Ḳāḍīzāde (d. 1635), were a popular movement opposing religious innovation (*bidʿa*) — including coffee-drinking, tobacco-smoking, hand-shaking in mosques — and the Sufi orders. The movement's founder is credited with the line "Who sheds a tear if a logician dies?" and appears to have been hostile to the rational sciences. Under Sultan Murād IV the movement achieved some policy victories (including a (largely nominal) death penalty for smoking).

However, Adamson's analysis emphasizes that the Ḳāḍīzādelī threat to the rational sciences was limited and short-lived. Like al-Ghazālī, they endorsed mathematics, astronomy, and medicine, and accepted the philosophical ideas already incorporated into Islamic theology. Their heyday (mid-17th c.) was followed by a resurgence of madrasa activity around 1700. The narrative of irreversible anti-rationalist decline initiated by the Ḳāḍīzādelīs does not withstand scrutiny.

### Sufism in the Ottoman Empire

[[Sufism]] was massively popular in the Ottoman empire, and the Sufi orders were significant rivals to both the Ḳāḍīzādelīs and the *ulema*. The tradition of philosophical Sufism (Ibn ʿArabī's school) was continued by Mollā Fenārī and, in the eighteenth century, by **ʿAbd al-Ghanī al-Nābulusī** (d. 1731). Al-Nābulusī defended *waḥdat al-wujūd* against al-Taftāzānī's critique, maintained an irenic attitude toward other religions, and practiced asceticism — while also writing on ḥadīth and composing poetry. He was, unusually, a genuine anti-rationalist: he advised that one should not bother arguing with philosophers, since their heresies spring from reason, and reason is not the proper means to refute them. He urged simple faith and Sufi union instead. But his anti-rationalism was not the mainstream Ottoman position.

### The Taqlīd Debate

A defining tension in Ottoman intellectual life was the debate over **taqlīd** (imitation/following precedent) versus *ijtihād* (independent legal and rational judgment). The Moroccan theologian **al-Sanūsī** (fl. 15th c.) — widely influential in the Ottoman empire and beyond — argued the extreme rationalist position: every Muslim has a duty to reflect rationally on religious beliefs; "lowly taqlīd is at the root of the unbelief of the idolaters." Even thinkers associated with the Ḳāḍīzādelī movement quoted al-Sanūsī approvingly on this. The debate parallels in structure the dispute over the status of religious belief after Maimonides in Jewish philosophy, and the broader question of "externalist" vs. "internalist" accounts of justified belief in epistemology. See [[taqlid-and-ijtihad]].

## Ottoman Astronomy and Science

The Istanbul observatory of **Taqī al-Dīn** (fl. 1570s) was one of the most significant scientific institutions of the Islamic world in the sixteenth century. Taqī al-Dīn designed a plan for a steam engine and in 1577 made observations of Halley's comet parallel to those of Tycho Brahe in Europe in the same year. The observatory was destroyed in 1580 — not, Adamson argues, because of anti-rationalist sentiment from the Ḳāḍīzādelīs (who had not yet arisen as a movement) but probably because of the political fallout from Taqī al-Dīn's failed prediction that the comet portended a military success against the Safavids.

In 1660 **Ibrāhīm Efendi** translated a book on Copernican astronomy from French into Arabic — an early instance of European scientific ideas entering Ottoman intellectual culture. Ibrāhīm did not endorse Copernicanism but saw the translation as potentially useful for correcting problems in Ptolemaic astronomy.

## Encounter with European Thought

From the seventeenth century onward, European scientific and philosophical ideas entered Ottoman intellectual culture through translations and travelers. Key moments:

- Paracelsian chemical theories and pharmacology translated into Arabic in the seventeenth century
- Yanyalı Esad Efendi (18th c., "Tulip Age") translated portions of Aristotle's logic and a Greek commentary on the *Physics* into Arabic, drawing on Averroes' commentary via a Latin version
- The *tanzimat* reforms (mid-19th c.) restructured the Ottoman state and educational system along European lines
- The **Young Ottomans** pushed for constitutional government while still drawing on Islamic texts; the more radical **Young Turks** embraced European materialism and positivism (Comte, Büchner, Durkheim, Darwin)

Ottoman intellectuals who engaged with European ideas ranged from the aggressively secularist Abdullah Cevdet and Ziya Gökalp (Young Turks) to moderate synthesizers like İzmirli İsmail Hakkı (who wanted to do for Comte what Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī had done for Avicenna — weave European thought into Islamic theology) and Said Nursi (fused Sunni theology with Sufi ideas, argued Islam's rationalism made it uniquely suited to survive the European Enlightenment). See [[encounters-with-european-thought]].

## Historiography

The myth of post-classical Islamic philosophical decline has been particularly persistent for the Ottoman case. Ottoman intellectual life has been depicted as dominated by sterile commentary-writing, anti-rationalist populism (Ḳāḍīzādelīs), and institutional sclerosis. Adamson's treatment (Chs. 57–59) argues forcefully against this narrative: madrasa activity in the rational sciences was continuous and productive; the Ḳāḍīzādelīs' impact was limited and temporary; the Ottoman encounter with European ideas in the 17th–19th centuries was nuanced rather than simply reactive. Khaled El-Rouyaheb's work (cited in Ch. 59) has been particularly important in rehabilitating the later Ottoman tradition.
