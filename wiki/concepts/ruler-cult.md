## Definition

**Ruler-cult** is the offering of divine worship — sacrifice, altars, temples,
priesthoods, festivals — to a living or dead king (or queen). It is a characteristic
institution of the [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic world]], and a central mechanism
by which the Greek cities reconciled themselves to the new
[[hellenistic-monarchy|monarchies]]. Walbank (CAH VII.1, ch. 3) distinguishes several
related but distinct practices:

1. **Divine patronage / ancestry** — a dynasty claims a protector god, often as
   ancestor (the Seleucids' Apollo of Miletus, whose anchor was a birthmark on
   Seleucus' thigh; the Antigonids' Heracles; the Attalids' Dionysus Cathegemon).
2. **Assimilation** of a king to a particular god (especially the Ptolemies to
   Dionysus; Arsinoe II and Berenice to Aphrodite).
3. **City ruler-cult** — worship offered *to* the king by an individual city,
   arising (relatively) spontaneously as an act of gratitude.
4. **Dynastic cult** — official worship of the royal house, organised by the kingdom's
   own central administration.

## City Cult: Gratitude in a Time of Need

City ruler-cult, Walbank stresses, **comes primarily from the worshippers, not the
ruler**. It is the response of cities that "stand in constant need of protection and
are, perhaps, less confident than they had been that the traditional gods can provide
this." Early cults are always tied to a **specific benefaction** — rescue from an
enemy, restored freedom, relief of famine — and describe the king's *services*, not
his innate qualities: "By doing the god's work a king qualified to receive the homage
due to the god." For the Greek, the gulf between honouring a great man and worshipping
a god was bridged by many fine gradations, and the sources are often deliberately
ambiguous (whether sacrifice is *to* the king or *for* him; whether an image is a
cult-statue *agalma* or a mere *eikon*).

Landmark instances:
- **Lysander** at Samos (early 4th c.) — by Duris' report the first Greek to whom
  cities raised altars as to a god; the festival Heraea renamed *Lysandreia*.
- **Alexander** — cults in the Greek cities of Asia Minor, and his request for divine
  honours from the cities of European Greece (324/3).
- **[[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus]]** at Scepsis (311), in gratitude for the
  peace and the guarantee of Greek freedom — the earliest dated city ruler-cult of the
  age, and one prompted by a pointed royal letter (the "spontaneity" was encouraged).
- **[[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius Poliorcetes]]** at Athens (307–290): worshipped
  with Antigonus as *Soteres* ("Saviours"), as *Theos Kataibates* ("the god who steps
  down"), tribes named *Antigonis* and *Demetrias*. The notorious **Athenian hymn** of
  290 makes the theology explicit and shocking: "other gods are either far away or have
  not ears, or do not exist... but thee we can see in very presence." This is the
  clearest testimony to ruler-worship as a substitute for failing traditional gods —
  and confirms that, in the third century, such cult was *not* mere routine flattery.
- **[[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]]** worshipped as *Soter* ("Saviour") at Rhodes after
  the [[siege-of-rhodes-305|siege of 305–304]] — a city cult-title that became the
  dynasty's.

## Dynastic Cult: Worship Organised by the Crown

Distinct in origin, **dynastic cult** was instituted by the kingdoms themselves to
give their displaced royal houses, Friends, soldiers, and bureaucrats — people no
longer belonging to any Greek city — a framework of religious observance that
consolidated loyalty around the king. It develops differently in each kingdom:

- **Ptolemaic Egypt** (the most elaborate): begins with the state cult of Alexander
  (eponymous priest used to date contracts); Ptolemy II deifies Ptolemy I and Berenice
  as the *Theoi Soteres* (280), then adds himself and [[arsinoe-ii|Arsinoe II]] as the
  *Theoi Adelphoi* ("Brother–Sister Gods," c. 272/1) — **introducing cult of the
  living monarch**. The Ptolemies' Greek cult-titles also entered the native Egyptian
  temples (the Canopus and Rosetta decrees), cementing the alliance with the priesthood.
- **Seleucid**: a single state cult under Antiochus III (with a separate high-priest in
  each satrapy), worshipping the king and ancestors; living kings included but without
  cult-title until after Antiochus IV.
- **Antigonid Macedon**: notably, **no official dynastic cult** — one of the few real
  differences of the Macedonian monarchy (see [[antigonid-dynasty]]).
- **Commagene** (Nimrud Dagh): shows how the form could fuse with Iranian royal
  ideology (the *hvarena*, royal fortune).

## Interpretation and Debate

What ruler-cult *meant* — religiously and politically — is the central controversy, and
Walbank judges it "even more difficult to find out." Key positions:
- It filled a **real need**, organising relations between free cities and their rulers;
  resistance (Callisthenes against *proskynesis*; Hypereides' outrage) was rare and
  usually a cover for political hostility, not principled impiety.
- Over time the cults became **institutionalised and perfunctory** (S. Price's argument,
  cited approvingly): by the second century the cities had accepted monarchy and no
  longer needed to express the relationship through deification — a trend visible in the
  drift from sacrifices *to* the king toward sacrifices *for* him.
- Adcock's formula, endorsed by Walbank: ruler-cult "was not the root of Hellenistic
  monarchy: it was rather the leaves on the branch."

Ruler-cult is the direct ancestor of the **Roman imperial cult**. See
[[hellenistic-monarchy]], [[hellenistic-world]].
