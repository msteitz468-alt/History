# Meiji Political Parties

The political parties that emerged during the Meiji period (1868–1912) in Japan, organized around
the demand for constitutional government and later as vehicles for parliamentary opposition to the
oligarchic cabinet. They rooted in the [[freedom-and-popular-rights-movement]] (*Jiyū Minken
Undō*) and provided the organized political opposition that contested — and won — Japan's first
elections under the [[meiji-constitution-1889]]. Linked to [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]].

## Definition and Scope

"Meiji political parties" refers primarily to:

- **Jiyūtō** (Liberal Party, founded 1881) — led by [[itagaki-taisuke]]; advocated French-model
  popular sovereignty and universal male suffrage; drew support from rural landlords and
  ex-samurai in western Japan; dissolved 1884 under government pressure; reconstituted 1890
- **Kaishintō** (Progressive/Constitutional Reform Party, founded 1882) — led by
  [[okuma-shigenobu]]; advocated British-model gradual parliamentary reform with a restricted
  franchise; drew support from urban businessmen and intellectuals; merged into the
  Rikken Kaishintō for the 1890 elections
- **Seiyūkai** (Friends of Constitutional Government, founded 1900) — founded by [[ito-hirobumi]]
  himself, in an attempt to co-opt the opposition parties into the governing system

The parties operated within the severe constraints of the Meiji constitutional order: the cabinet
was responsible to the emperor, not to the Diet; the elected House of Representatives could not
initiate the budget or compel cabinet formation. Nonetheless, from the first Diet session in 1890,
opposition parties used budget powers and legislative delay to extract concessions from successive
cabinets.

## Origins

The political parties grew directly from the [[freedom-and-popular-rights-movement]] of the
1870s–1880s. When [[ito-hirobumi]] forced [[okuma-shigenobu]] out of the government in 1881 —
the "Political Crisis of 1881" — and committed Japan to a Prussian-model constitution by 1890,
Ōkuma took his British constitutionalist position outside the government and founded the Kaishintō.
[[itagaki-taisuke]], who had left the government earlier over the Korean issue, founded the
Jiyūtō. Both parties were weakened by government suppression and internal strife in the
mid-1880s, but reconstituted themselves to contest the 1890 elections.

The Meiji oligarchs — especially [[yamagata-aritomo]] — were deeply hostile to party politics,
seeing parties as fractious and self-interested obstacles to efficient governance. Yamagata
worried that officials not given posts worthy of their abilities might "join some political party
that would create problems for the government." This fear shaped the July 25, 1890 law that
simplified party formation while simultaneously restricting political meetings (prohibiting women
and children, banning gatherings near the Diet during sessions).

## The 1890 Election and Its Aftermath

At the [[first-general-election-japan-1890]] on July 1, 1890, the opposition parties won a
substantial majority in the 300-seat House of Representatives. The government had designed the
constitution to limit what they could do with that majority, but the parties proved willing to use
budget obstruction and public rhetoric to pressure successive cabinets.

The governing pattern that emerged: the oligarchic cabinet (drawn from the Satsuma-Chōshū genrō
clique) confronted an opposition House of Representatives in recurring budget battles. Cabinets
responded by dissolving the House, negotiating compromises, or invoking imperial prerogatives.
This produced the volatile executive–legislative dynamic of the 1890s, in which cabinet after
cabinet fell or was reorganized. [[ito-hirobumi]], in his second and third prime ministerial
terms, attempted to work with and then against the parties before finally founding his own party,
the Seiyūkai (1900), which incorporated the remnants of the Jiyūtō.

## Structural Constraints

The Meiji constitutional design systematically limited party power:
1. Cabinet responsible to the emperor, not the Diet
2. If the House rejected the budget, the previous year's budget automatically applied — blunting
   the most powerful parliamentary weapon
3. House of Peers (unelected, stocked with the new meiji peerage) could veto House of
   Representatives legislation
4. Privy Council had wide advisory powers on constitutional matters
5. Military ministers had to be serving generals or admirals — giving the army and navy a veto
   on cabinet formation independent of parliamentary politics

## Long-Term Significance

Despite these constraints, the parties gradually gained leverage. By the Taishō period (1912–26),
the "Taishō Democracy" movement produced a genuine parliamentary system in which cabinets were
formed from majority parties. The Seiyūkai and Minseitō (successor to the Kaishintō tradition)
alternated in power during the 1920s. This development was reversed by [[japanese-militarism]]
in the 1930s, which suppressed party politics between 1940 and 1945.

The parties' early weakness — unable to control the cabinet, the military, or foreign policy —
established habits of governing by oligarchic consensus (*genrō* politics) that civilian
politicians never fully overcame before militarist forces exploited those same constitutional
weaknesses.
