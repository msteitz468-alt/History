# Business Strategy

Business strategy is the youngest of the strategic domains. In Lawrence Freedman's
"strategy from above," the managers — those who "already had power … but had to work out
what to do with it" — became "the recipient of more strategic advice than any other
group, including generals." Yet the word itself was a late arrival: when Drucker drafted
a 1964 book titled *Business Strategies*, his publisher found the title elicited "little
enthusiasm," because "strategy" was "associated with the military … but not with
business," and the book appeared as *Managing for Results*. "Almost the next day,
strategy became the hottest word in management circles." Walter Kiechel dated the
"corporate strategy revolution" to around 1960 and argued that "before this there had
been no business strategy" — a claim, Freedman notes, "comparable to the claim that there
was no military strategy before 1800." This page traces the field from Taylor's shop
floor to Mintzberg's critique that real [[strategy]] is learned, not planned.

## Taylor and the rise of the management class

The verb "to manage" derives from *maneggiare*, "the ability to handle a horse," and
"implied coping, dealing with a state of affairs that could never fully be controlled."
The first business schools (Wharton 1881, Harvard 1908) were preoccupied with "the labor
issue," and the figure who gave management its scientific pretension was **Frederick
Winslow Taylor**. His scientific management held that for "each elemental task … there
should be 'one best way' found through careful analysis and measurement," enforced by a
"sharp distinction between planning and doing." The worker was best "treated as an
unthinking machine"; time-and-motion studies set the rate. Taylor's data (the famous
"Schmidt" story) was later shown to be embellished — "acts of communication rather than
research reports" — but Drucker still called scientific management possibly "the most
powerful as well as the most lasting contribution America has made to Western thought
since the Federalist Papers." Tellingly, [[lenin-vladimir|Lenin]] urged adopting this
"last word in capitalism" for the Soviet Union; the result, "disconnecting planning from
doing," became "Taylorism with teeth." The human relations school (Follett's "power
with," Mayo's Hawthorne studies, Barnard's Pareto-inflected *Functions of the Executive*)
softened Taylor but kept the elite manager's "special leadership role."

## Drucker, Chandler, and the design vs. planning schools

**Peter Drucker** established management "as a discipline" with *The Concept of the
Corporation* (1946) and *The Practice of Management* (1954). He was "skeptical of
scientific management," blaming Taylor for "separating planning from doing" — an "elitist
philosophy" that led Drucker to class Taylor "with Sorel, Lenin and Pareto." His own
philosophy was "rationalist — set ends, find means," compressed in the injunction to
"manage by objectives." Alfred Chandler then "gave the concept of strategy prominence in
a business setting," defining it (from teaching national strategy at the Naval War
College) as "the determination of the basic long-term goals … and the allocation of
resources." His thesis was that **strategy leads to structure**.

From this grew two schools. The **design school** (Kenneth Andrews at Harvard, source of
SWOT analysis) held that "every situation was unique," resisting "templates, formulas, or
frameworks." The **planning school** (Igor Ansoff's *Corporate Strategy*, 1965) sought
to "transform management strategy from an intuitive art into a science" through
exhaustive lists, matrices, and one diagram of "fifty-seven boxes." Planning's apotheosis
was McNamara's Pentagon and corporate planning departments; its collapse came when GE's
Jack Welch abolished his, citing von Moltke — "Strategy was not a lengthy action plan …
Any cookbook approach is powerless to cope … with the unfolding situations of the real
world." Mintzberg's *The Rise and Fall of Strategic Planning* (1994) delivered "the coup
de grace."

## Business as war and the Sun Tzu fad

The reaction against planning drove a "business-as-war" literature — chief executives
"imagining themselves as generals." Books mined "Alexander, Caesar, Napoleon," even
Attila the Hun, for boardroom lessons, mostly "collections of aphorisms … trite, and at
most pithy restatements of best practice." Ries and Trout's *Marketing Warfare* (1986)
borrowed Clausewitz and offered four strategies (defensive, offensive, flanking,
guerrilla) keyed to market share, advising weak firms to fight as guerrillas. The 1980s
brought a shift toward **[[sun-tzu|Sun Tzu]]**, popularized by *Wall Street*'s Gordon
Gekko and Tony Soprano alike. Sun Tzu and Liddell Hart's **[[indirect-approach|indirect
approach]]** "appealed to business strategists for the same reason they appealed to
military strategists. They required intelligence, imagination, and nerve" — outthinking
rather than outspending an opponent. The genre also promised a key to Japanese
competitiveness (Musashi's *Book of Five Rings*; George Stalk's time-based competition,
explicitly linked to John Boyd's OODA loop). Freedman's verdict: "warfare could be
illuminating for business so long as it was not mistaken for the real thing."

## Economics and game theory

The horizontal axis of competition was claimed by economics, "largely in the form of
game theory." Its roots were shared with strategic [[deterrence|deterrence]] — the same
Schelling-style reasoning about interdependent decision under partial information.
**Agency theory**, derived from transaction-cost economics, recast the firm as a "nexus
of contracts" among "self-interested individuals" and, by importing the efficient-markets
hypothesis, argued that "the market for corporate control almost uniformly increases
efficiency and shareholders' wealth." This "reduced the need for strategy and
management," making the manager "just another 'substitutable' commodity … in need of
market discipline." Critics warned the game-theoretic manager was becoming "ruthlessly
hard-driving, strictly top-down, command-and control focused, shareholder-value-obsessed";
Franklin Fisher noted that the elegant models were rarely recognized by practitioners,
and Saloner argued game theory's value was "not literal but rather … metaphorical."

## Porter, Red Queens, and Blue Oceans

The decisive breakthrough was **Michael Porter**'s *Competitive Strategy* (1980). Trained
in industrial-organization economics — the study of imperfect competition — Porter
"turned the theory on its head," asking not how to make markets competitive but "how the
unit within the system could exploit … uncompetitive elements." His **five forces**
framework (rivalry, supplier and buyer power, threats of entry and substitutes) anchored
strategy in **positioning**, via three generic strategies (cost leadership,
differentiation, specialization) and the warning never to be "stuck in the middle." The
trap of competing on operational efficiency alone was the **"Red Queen effect"** — "it
takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place" — a war of attrition in
which imitators erode any advantage. Kim and Mauborgne's answer was to leave the bloody
"red oceans" for **"blue oceans"** of "uncontested market space," recasting Porter's
"structuralist" (environment-determined) approach against a "reconstructionist" one in
which "strategy can shape structure." Freedman flags the recurring "infatuation with
dichotomy — direct/indirect, annihilation/exhaustion … red ocean/blue ocean."
Meanwhile the era's grandest fad, **Business Process Re-engineering**, promised to
"obliterate" old processes with a clean sheet; by 1994 "67% [of projects] were judged as
producing mediocre, marginal, or failed results," and the CSC Index consultancy collapsed
amid a bestseller-list scandal.

## Mintzberg: deliberate vs. emergent

The deepest critique came from **Henry Mintzberg**, who reframed strategy as "a pattern
in a stream of decisions" and distinguished **intended** from **realized** strategy: when
the two coincide, strategy is "deliberate"; "patterns that were realized despite of or in
the absence of intentions were 'emergent.'" A purely deliberate strategy demanded a
"totally benign environment … a 'tall order.'" His conclusion — "strategy formation walks
on two feet, one deliberate, the other emergent" — leaned clearly toward the emergent,
because it "required more of the organization" and demanded continuous learning, akin to
the Greek *mētis*, "particularly important when an environment was 'too unstable or
complex to comprehend.'" The exemplary case was Honda's accidental American success with
the small SuperCub — "miscalculation, serendipity, and organizational learning." This
connects to Freedman's **narrative turn**: with command-and-control fading, managers were
urged to lead through stories (Weick's "sensemaking"), and strategy came to be understood
as "something that people did," a continuing practice rather than a product.

## Limitations and Critiques

Business strategy "lost its way," Freedman argues, falling "prey to temporary
enthusiasms" with "a resulting tendency to prescriptive hyperbole." Unlike the military,
managers had "no agreed framework to provide coherence," and the field fragmented into a
"pre-paradigmatic state" — John Kay quipped that strategy's commonest meaning was now "a
synonym for expensive." Studies of management fads found them "broader-based but
shorter-lived," adopted for "external legitimacy" rather than measurable performance gain.
The Mintzberg/Honda lesson was itself overdrawn: Andrew Mair showed Honda "had always
intended to sell the SuperCub" and that its real triumphs (the brutally attritional
H-Y war) and failures (the NSX) were both flattened to fit the theory — a "halo effect"
(Rosenzweig) confusing correlation with causation. The critical-theory wing (Knights and
Morgan, drawing on Foucault) went further, treating "corporate strategy as a set of
discourses" — "a technology of power" that legitimizes the managerial elite as much as it
guides action. Freedman's closing judgment unifies the domains: business strategy, "like
military and revolutionary strategy, could suffer from its own heroic myths," and "the
hype … exaggerated the importance of the enlightened manager and played down the
importance of chance and circumstances."
