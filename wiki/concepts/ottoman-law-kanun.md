# Ottoman Law (*Shari'a* and *Kanun*)

The **dual legal order** of the Ottoman Empire: the **sacred law (*shari'a*)** of Islam,
administered by the religious-legal scholars (*ulema*), coexisting with the **sultanic secular
law (*kanun*)** that the sultans enacted to govern where the sacred law was silent or
impractical. The reconciliation of the two is one of the defining features of the Ottoman
state. From Colin Imber's
[[imber-ottoman-empire-1300-1650|*The Ottoman Empire, 1300–1650*]].

## Two laws

The ***shari'a*** regulated most day-to-day affairs of Muslims (and many matters for
non-Muslims), administered through a hierarchy of **colleges (*medrese*), muftis, and judges
(*kadı*)**, headed by the chief mufti, the **şeyhülislam**. But it was never the only law in
force. ***Kanun*** — sultanic/customary secular law — governed the areas the *shari'a* left
open or unworkable: above all **criminal law, land tenure, and taxation**. Originating in
**custom**, *kanun* was given written form in the **law-books (*kanunname*)** of the late
fifteenth and sixteenth centuries (from Bayezid II onward) and modified by sultanic decree —
acquiring a "dual character of customary and sultanic law."

## Reconciling the two

A central Ottoman intellectual project was to **harmonize *kanun* with the *shari'a***. As
scholars grew conscious of the disparity between sacred and secular law, jurists such as
**Kemalpaşazade** and especially the great şeyhülislam **Ebussuud** (under
[[suleiman-the-magnificent|Süleyman the Lawgiver]]) redefined the Ottoman laws of land and tax
in the technical terms of the **Hanafi** jurists — producing "legal fictions which, by
relabelling technical terms, satisfied pious aspirations without upsetting legal reality."
Süleyman's title ***Kanunî*** ("the Lawgiver") reflects this codifying achievement.

## The social map of the law

Ottoman secular law rested on a distinction different from the *shari'a*'s categories (free/
slave, Muslim/dhimmi). It divided society into the **tax-paying subjects** and the
**non-tax-paying military/servant class (*askeri*)** — the latter comprising the
[[timar-system|timar-holding cavalry]], the [[janissaries|Janissaries]] and palace cavalry, the
viziers and governors, *and* the religious-legal and teaching professions. Tax-payers were
either townsmen or ***reaya*** ("flock") — peasants holding a tenement by contract with the
fief-holder as the sultan's representative. From Bayezid II's reign the *askeri* gained a
**separate jurisdiction**, making a legal definition of the classes necessary (codified in his
c. 1499 law-book). The *kanunname* typically dealt in concrete cases and exceptions (e.g.
protecting orphans' inherited tenements) rather than abstract principle.

## Significance

The *shari'a*/*kanun* duality let the Ottomans run a vast, religiously plural empire with both
**Islamic legitimacy and pragmatic flexibility** — the sultan upholding the sacred law while
legislating the fiscal and criminal order the state actually needed. The *askeri*/*reaya*
distinction it codified is the legal expression of the empire's whole social structure (the
[[timar-system|timar]] and [[devshirme|kapıkulu]] orders). It is a key Ottoman contribution to
the comparative history of Islamic statecraft.
