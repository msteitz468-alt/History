# Methodical Battle

**Methodical battle** refers to the French operational doctrine that emerged from the failures of 1914–1915 siege and breakthrough attempts: a deliberate, phased, artillery-dominated approach to the attack emphasizing thorough preparation, limited objectives, destruction of enemy positions by massed fires (especially heavy artillery), close infantry-artillery coordination, and consolidation before renewal. It contrasted with rapid "continuous battle" pressure or prewar *offensive à outrance*.

## Definition and Scope

Methodical battle prioritized "slower" operations prepared "down to the smallest detail" with "a large number of artillery rounds ... concentrated on the objective to weaken the enemy and ensure the infantry could advance." Key features:
- Extensive artillery preparation (counterbattery, wire-cutting, trench destruction) before infantry assault.
- Successive, limited phases rather than broad continuous feeding of troops.
- Reserves positioned close for timely exploitation or counterattack response.
- Systematic organization of captured ground before next effort.
- Recognition that "every time a strong point is to be taken, the attack must be prepared by artillery. The infantry should be held back..."

It accepted the realities of industrial firepower and trench depth (multiple bands, reverse slopes).

## Emergence in 1914–1915 (Doughty Ch. 3–4)

Although the term "methodical battle" crystallized later (post-1917 under Pétain), its elements appeared immediately in response to positional war:

- **Joffre Jan 1915 memorandum**: "as a result of the increased effects of firepower and the strength of organized defenses, operations in the future had to be 'slower' than those envisaged in the prewar doctrine." Attack "prepared down to 'the smallest detail'" with massed artillery.
- **De Langle de Cary (Fourth Army, post-Dec 1914 Champagne)**: Advocated "an ensemble of prepared attacks against judiciously chosen points"; "once initial objectives were taken, it was necessary to repeat preparations for another attack, including digging approach trenches and methodically destroying enemy positions with artillery fire." Joffre had "no objection to the methods" but insisted on greater breadth and "incessantly repeated blows."
- **Foch (March 1915)**: Stressed "character of a siege with its method and its slowness" for Vimy attack; careful preparation over haste.
- **Joffre critique and guidance (1915)**: Condemned "uninterrupted succession of partial attacks"; preferred "operations 'prepared by artillery concentrations, separated by delays during which enemy batteries will be discovered and destroyed.'"
- **Artillery coordination**: Shift from 75-mm mobile support role to preparatory massed fires, counterbattery, and disruption of enemy attacks. Introduction of more heavy pieces (105-mm onward) and programs to increase production.

In practice, 1915 offensives (Artois May/June, Champagne Sept) showed incomplete adoption: initial successes followed by failure to methodically reduce second defensive bands; reserves issues; weather interference. Pétain (as corps/army commander in Artois) exemplified closer front-line awareness and later advocated better coordination and limited objectives.

## Interaction and Limits in Range

- Enabled by industrial mobilization (more heavy artillery, ammo: e.g., June 16 1915 Artois doubled heavy expenditure).
- Still insufficient in 1915: "even greater amounts of artillery were necessary, particularly to cut enemy wire."
- Command tension: Joffre retained offensive faith and sometimes pushed "brutal" continuous efforts; subordinates (Pétain: "fools" remark on higher command; de Langle memoirs on interference; Fayolle diary doubts) favored more methodical, realistic approaches.
- Results: Small gains at high cost demonstrated that "method" without overwhelming material superiority yielded only incremental progress.

## Historiography

Doughty highlights the pragmatic, trial-and-error evolution within the French high command, grounded in after-action reports, GQG studies, and archive material. The concept's roots in 1914–15 responses to "siege warfare" fed directly into postwar French doctrine (methodical battle, continuous front, Maginot-era thinking). Critics saw slow adoption as costing lives; defenders note severe prewar under-preparation for industrial war and the strategic imperative to act. Excellent on internal French debates; less on comparative Allied/German doctrinal parallels in this source.
