# Kuge Shohatto (Laws for the Court Nobles)

The *Kuge Shohatto* ("Laws for the Court Nobles") was the legislative code through which the
Tokugawa Shogunate subordinated the imperial court and its aristocracy to bakufu authority during
the [[edo-period|Edo period]]. Issued in parallel with the [[buke-shohatto|Buke Shohatto]] in
1615, it defined the Emperor's permitted activities, regulated court ranks and appointments,
disciplined the *kuge* (court nobility), and ensured that no political initiative could originate
at Kyoto without Shogunal sanction.

## Background and First Code (1613)

The immediate occasion for the first set of laws (five articles, 1613) was a scandal at the
imperial court in 1609, when several young court nobles and a dentist had "enticed several of the
ladies-in-waiting of the Inner Court to an entertainment, at which there had been dancing and
unseemly and inebriate merriment." Emperor Go-Yozei was outraged and asked the Shogunate's
Kyoto deputy (Shoshidai) Itakura Katsushige to punish the offenders. Ieyasu, consulted at
Shizuoka, counseled leniency — the death penalty was remitted; the ladies were banished to the
Izu Islands; the responsible officials were executed — and used the episode to issue the first
formal regulations:

1. Court nobles must apply themselves to their studies, by day and night.
2. Transgression of the law will result in banishment, in proportion to guilt.
3. Guard duty and required attendance must not be neglected.
4. Strolling the streets of the city, whether by night or day, is forbidden.
5. Improper competitive games and the keeping of worthless young samurai in noble households
   are forbidden.

The code specified that breaches reported by the five Regent Families or the *denso* (liaison
officers between court and Shogunate) "shall be dealt with by the military authorities" — making
the Shogunate the enforcer of court discipline.

## The Seventeen Articles (1615)

In 1615, two years after the first code, Ieyasu and Hidetada summoned representatives of the court
nobility to Nijo Castle. The seventeen articles were read aloud by Hirohashi Kanekatsu; court
nobles Nijo and Kikutei attended. Sadler notes that the court nobles Sanjo Saneeda and others
declared the code "very remarkable and complete, which was not strange, seeing that in all
probability they had quite a share in its composition." The articles covered:

1. **The Emperor's arts** — "First of all comes the study of letters." Japanese poetry, the *Kinpi-sho*
   (private imperial records), and classical precedents are to be the Emperor's pursuits. Political
   ideas are implicitly excluded: "In being limited to the contemplation of flowers, birds, snow,
   wind, and moon, the Emperor shared the fate of all from whose minds the Bakufu wished to banish
   political ideas."
2. **Ranking of princes of the blood** — Fixed precedence below the three Great Ministers
   (Dajo-daijin, Sa-daijin, U-daijin), with detailed rules for retired ministers.
3. **Ranking of Pure Flowery Families** — Families including Tokudaiji, Kwazan-in, Sanjo, and
   Saionji governed in their retirement from office.
4. **Ability over birth for Regent and Minister** — "Even members of the Five Regent Families, if
   they lack ability, shall not be appointed to the offices of Regent and Minister." (The
   Shogunate was the sole judge of ability.)
5. **Capable ministers must not resign** — Those capable of office must continue to serve.
6. **Adopted sons must share the family surname** — Preventing imperial-family members from
   becoming ministers (they have no surname).
7. **Military men holding court rank are honorary only** — Such men "shall not go near the Court
   or have anything to do with it."
8. **Naming of year-periods** — Chinese precedents to be used; deviation allowed only with mature
   scholarly justification.
9. **Ceremonial robes** of Emperor, Retired Emperor, and courtiers regulated.
10. **Promotion of court families** — Old customs to be followed, but "scholarship, knowledge of
    ancient customs, and skill in verse are to be encouraged"; promotion for merit is possible.
11. **Obedience to Kampaku, Denso, and Bugyo** — Disobedience by courtiers of any rank results
    in banishment.
12. **Guilt assessed according to the Daiho code** — Criminal jurisdiction mapped onto classical
    precedent.
13–17. **Ecclesiastical hierarchy** — Rules for Lord Abbots, imperial abbots, *Sojo* grades,
    purple-robe conferral, and the title of *Shonin* (Eminence) — all requiring either imperial
    permission or Shogunal oversight, and restricting abuse of rank.

## Strategic Logic

Ieyasu's relationship to the court differed fundamentally from that of Toyotomi Hideyoshi.
Hideyoshi had regarded himself as one of the court nobles and the Emperor as his chief — the
military and court orders overlapping. Ieyasu "regarded himself as a military administrator only,
and the Court and military nobles as two entirely separate groups, the former to be completely
subordinated to the latter." The Kuge Shohatto operationalized this distinction: henceforth the
court was "short-circuited for a couple of centuries, though with its functions unimpaired and its
emoluments to some extent increased and ensured."

Ieyasu reportedly summarized the logic: "Court nobles are like gold and silver, while the military
nobles are like iron and copper. Gold and silver are certainly precious, but they are not as useful
as iron and copper... Warriors must not neglect the sword for the purse."

The financial arrangements reinforced the political subordination. The Imperial Family received a
stipend of 10,000 *koku* — three thousand more than Hideyoshi had provided, but still a modest sum
— while the rebuilding of imperial palaces was delegated as an "expensive privilege" imposed on
daimyo. The consort who became Empress (Hidetada's daughter) brought a 10,000 *koku* dowry, another
form of Tokugawa penetration of the court.

The liaison structure was elaborate: *denso* (transmitters) from court noble families relayed all
communications between the court and Shogunate through the *Shoshidai* (Kyoto Deputy); *Giso*
(administrators of the Imperial Household) from the next tier of court families were nominated by
the Shogun. No approach to the Emperor could bypass these filters.

## Long-term Effect

The Kuge Shohatto effectively confined the imperial court to ceremony, aesthetics, and classical
scholarship for over two centuries. By removing any avenue for the court to intrigue with powerful
*tozama* daimyo (which, as Sadler observes, "might have caused civil war to break out again"), it
was one of the structural conditions of the Edo peace. When the Meiji Restoration finally ended the
Tokugawa order in 1868, the Emperor was able to emerge from the seclusion this code had enforced
and "become the head of the nation once again."
