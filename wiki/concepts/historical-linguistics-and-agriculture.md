## Definition

The use of **historical linguistics** to reconstruct prehistoric subsistence
history — recovering, from the comparative study of living and recorded
languages, evidence about when and where peoples adopted agriculture, what they
grew and herded, and how farming spread. It is the subject of Ch. 3 of CWH Vol.
II (Christopher Ehret) and a complement to archaeology: where archaeology
reveals material life, linguistics can document "the intangible transformations
in technology and ideas."

## How it works

- **Proto-language reconstruction.** By systematically comparing daughter
  languages, linguists reconstruct ancestral **proto-languages** and their family
  trees. The reconstructed vocabulary is a "storehouse" of the things its speakers
  knew and did.
- **Subsistence lexicon as evidence.** If a proto-language can be shown to have
  had words for *cultivating*, *grain*, *herding*, specific crops or livestock,
  then its speakers practised those activities. **Loanwords** track the borrowing
  of techniques and crops between speech communities; the *stratigraphy* of which
  words are inherited vs. borrowed reveals sequence.
- **Linguistic dating and mapping migration.** Relative (and, more
  contestably, absolute) chronologies are inferred from degrees of divergence;
  because language history tracks the movement of *speakers*, it can map the
  spread of farming populations.

## Time-depth varies by region

Reconstructions reach back furthest where families are deep and well studied.
**Africa** is the strongest case: the **Nilo-Saharan**, **Niger–Congo**, and
**Cushitic** families can be reconstructed back into the early Holocene, giving a
stage-by-stage subsistence history (see [[african-language-family-origins]]).
Elsewhere the reach is shallower — the **Austronesian** family extends only to
the later middle Holocene; the Middle East, India, and Mesoamerica likewise to
the middle/later-middle Holocene.

## Key application: farming/language dispersals

The signature hypothesis (Bellwood & Renfrew; "Farmers and their languages,"
Diamond & Bellwood 2003) is that the world's great language families spread
*with* farming populations — Niger–Congo/[[bantu-expansion|Bantu]] across
sub-Saharan Africa, Austronesian across the Pacific, Indo-European, etc. Ehret
argues for the **complementarity** of linguistic and archaeological evidence and
for an increasingly global view.

## Limitations and disputes (why `contested: yes`)

- Some earlier linguistic work made **overly simplistic** assumptions —
  treating agricultural spread as a one-way linear process and equating language
  families neatly with farming migrations. Archaeology now shows rates of change
  varied enormously within and between regions.
- Genetic evidence has repeatedly **complicated** the language-dispersal model:
  the [[austronesian-dispersal]] "express-train" is not supported by the genetics,
  and the [[bantu-expansion]] involved heavy assimilation, not simple replacement.
- Absolute linguistic dating is methodologically fraught (glottochronology is
  widely distrusted); the discipline leans on cross-dating with archaeology.

## Language as historical evidence (CAH method)

[[edwards-cah-v1p1-1970|CAH Vol. I, Part 1]] (Ch. IV, Albright & Lambdin) devotes
a full chapter to the principle that language is a category of historical evidence
in its own right — "a kind of transmitted organism" that, like tools and
institutions, "can be ordered in typologically related sequences" and so reveals
"otherwise undiscernible ethnic and cultural affiliations." It distinguishes
change in **content** (lexicon, tracking cultural change) from change in **form**
(largely self-generated, structurally constrained, and therefore amenable to an
empirical science of historical linguistics), and lays out four models of family
relationship — linear descent, divergence-in-isolation, the **dialect continuum**,
and convergence — while stressing there is "no accepted definition of a dialect as
opposed to a new language." This 1970 statement of method underlies the family-tree
reasoning behind both the [[indo-european-origins]] and [[afroasiatic-homeland]]
debates; see [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
