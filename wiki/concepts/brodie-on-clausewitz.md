# Brodie's Reading of Clausewitz

In the Howard & Paret Princeton edition of *On War* (originally published in 1976, indexed in 1984), the major twentieth-century strategic theorist **Bernard Brodie** (a pioneer of nuclear strategy at RAND and UCLA) provides a book-by-book analytical commentary. Brodie's commentary played a pivotal role in reclaiming Clausewitz for the late Cold War era, shaping how the English-speaking world reads *On War*.

---

## Brodie's Assessment of Clausewitz's Method

Brodie rejects the caricature of Clausewitz as a dogmatic German idealist. While Clausewitz utilizes German philosophical terms—specifically the Kantian and Hegelian language of "idealism," the "absolute," and the "pure concept of war"—Brodie argues that:
> "Fortunately, Clausewitz was of much too pragmatic a fiber to lose himself either deeply or for long in this brand of idealism, but here as the curtain rises on his masterwork, this man who never graduated from a university enters wearing the gown of academe."

The concept of "absolute war" serves simply as a logical construct—the "thing in itself"—which real-world factors then modify.

---

## Reading Book One: The Rejection of the "Apostle of Total War"

Brodie addresses the common misconception that Clausewitz is the "apostle of total war," noting dryly that:
> "This opinion is likely to be confined to those who have never touched the book."

He draws attention to Book One, Chapter Two, where Clausewitz analyzes why and how wars must be kept limited. In limited war, "the value of this [political] purpose must determine the sacrifices to be made for it in magnitude and also in duration. Once the expenditure of effort exceeds the value of the political purpose, the purpose must be renounced and peace must follow."

On Book One, Chapter Three ("On Military Genius"), Brodie emphasizes Clausewitz's insistence on "a strong mind rather than a brilliant one." He notes that a commander’s intuitive judgment (*coup d'oeil*) and moral courage under pressure are what allow him to overcome the friction of the battlefield, rather than mechanical formulas.

---

## The Relevance of Clausewitz to the Nuclear Age

Brodie’s primary contribution as a twentieth-century nuclear strategist was to demonstrate that Clausewitz's core insights remain valid in the age of thermonuclear weapons:

* **Policy as the Supreme Controller:** The dictum that "war is a continuation of policy by other means" is not an endorsement of war, but a warning that military action must never be allowed to escape political control. Brodie notes that "among the contingencies for which the state must be prepared is a war in which every element calls for policy to be eclipsed by violence... He is describing total war, which today would be nuclear, but the acceptance of such a state of affairs is still policy."
* **The Primacy of Limited War:** In the nuclear age, where an unlimited "absolute" war would result in mutual destruction, the only rational form of conflict is limited war. Clausewitz’s analysis of limited aims and the need to scale military effort to the value of the political purpose is, in Brodie's view, the foundation of modern deterrence and crisis management.

---

## The Manuscript Problem and Reception

Brodie repeatedly emphasizes the unfinished nature of the manuscript. Because Clausewitz only fully revised Book One, Chapter One, before his death, the rest of *On War* contains unfinished drafts and seeming contradictions. Later interpreters—such as the younger von Moltke and Liddell Hart—frequently engaged in selective quotation, lifting Clausewitz's passages on destruction and the offensive out of context. Brodie argues that only by reading the work as a whole, keeping the supremacy of policy everlastingly in mind, can one understand Clausewitz’s true theory.
