# Scandinavianism

A mid-nineteenth-century political and cultural movement promoting unity or close
co-operation among the Scandinavian peoples on the basis of shared language,
history, and Lutheran heritage. Emic to its liberal and student adherents in the
1840s–60s, it was both a variant of Romantic [[nationalism]] and a counter-current
to the separate [[scandinavian-nation-state-formation|national projects]] then
crystallising. Its political ambitions collapsed when Sweden–Norway declined to
aid Denmark in the Second Schleswig War (1864), but cultural and inter-Nordic
co-operation persisted. Distinguish the pan-Scandinavian ideal (etic: a failed
unification movement) from the durable practical co-operation it seeded.

## Origins and Course

The movement's symbolic opening was the 1829 "poet's coronation" at Lund, where
Esaias Tegnér pronounced "the time of division is past" (L39813–14), thawing the
post-Napoleonic chill. Cultural expressions followed — Ingemann's *Nordens Aand*
(1833), Hans Christian Andersen's "national song for Scandinavia" (1839) — and
Scandinavian **student congresses** (from 1838, peaking at København 1845 with 500
delegates and Orla Lehmann's "sacred promise" speech). From the 1842–43 congresses
the movement "was about to take a political turn," and the Schleswig question became
"the fixed pivot of political Scandinavianism" (L39902–10), tying Nordic unity to
constitutional reform on the Norwegian model and to defence against Russia and
German nationalism.

## Failure and Limitations

Political Scandinavianism failed its tests. In the First Schleswig War (1848–50)
Swedish-Norwegian troops sent to Fyn ultimately refused active involvement; in 1864
Karl XV's promised military support was "rejected … by the Swedish government," and
Denmark fought Prussia and Austria alone, losing Schleswig and Holstein — which
"brought a halt to political Scandinavianism" (L39957–64). The movement "lacked a
single energetic power which, like the kingdoms of Sardinia and Prussia, could place
itself in the vanguard … and force through the business of union" (L40027–29). It
was "to a large extent nourished by opposition to the German unity movement, but in
the last resort it was also crushed by it" (L40033–35).

Country variations sharpen the critique: Denmark tied it to Eider-border security;
Sweden to anti-Russian sentiment and Bernadotte dynastic ambition; Norway engaged
least, fearing junior-partner status; Finland was "prevented by the Russians from
playing an active role" (L39986–94). Calling it merely "Student Scandinavianism" is
"misleading" — it had genuine governmental and business-elite backing 1848–64
(L40011–20). It is best compared to the German and Italian unification movements in
depth of support, but unlike them it produced no state.

**Emic vs etic:** to its adherents Scandinavianism was a living programme of
unity; historiographically (etic) it is read as a *failed* unification movement
whose real legacy was cultural — "spiritual Scandinavianism" outlived the political
collapse and seeded the durable inter-Nordic co-operation of the modern era
(L39897–01). Distinguish the failed political ideal from this enduring practical
co-operation. See [[scandinavian-nation-state-formation]], which the volume argues
ultimately prevailed over the Scandinavianist alternative.
