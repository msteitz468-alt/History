# Socialist Calculation Debate

The twentieth century's central controversy over whether a socialist economy
without private ownership of the means of production can perform rational
economic calculation. Opened decisively by [[mises-ludwig|Ludwig von Mises]]
(1920); prosecuted in English by [[hayek-friedrich|Hayek]] and Lionel
Robbins; answered by the "market socialists" — [[lange-oskar|Oskar Lange]],
Abba Lerner, H. D. Dickinson — whose "competitive solution" set the terms of
the debate into the Cold War. The primary documentation here follows Hayek's
three survey essays (1935, 1935, 1940) reprinted in
[[hayek-individualism-economic-order-1948]]; his narrative is one side's
account and is attributed as such throughout.

## Pre-history (1850s–1914), as Hayek reconstructs it

Hayek argues the question was long invisible because public discourse
conflated economic allocation with engineering optimization, and because the
German historical school and Marx discouraged "utopian" speculation about
socialist mechanics — "Marx himself had only scorn and ridicule for any such
attempt deliberately to construct a working plan of such a utopia." Early
anticipations he catalogues:

- **H. H. Gossen** (1854): a communist central authority "would soon find
  that it had set itself a task which far exceeded the powers of individual
  men."
- **Edwin Cannan** (1893): socialist aims require abolishing exchange,
  without which value cannot exist.
- **N. G. Pierson vs. Karl Kautsky** (1902): Pierson's impracticability
  thesis provoked Kautsky's Delft lecture — the first Marxist public sketch
  of post-revolutionary economic organization — and Pierson's rebuttal in
  the Dutch *Economist* was, for Hayek, "the first important contribution to
  the modern discussion," long unknown outside Holland.
- **Enrico Barone**, "The Ministry of Production in the Collectivist State"
  (1908), developing Pareto: the first mathematical statement of the
  conditions a planning ministry would have to satisfy. Hayek (following
  Pareto's own disclaimer) insists Barone stated the *conditions* of rational
  allocation, not a method of finding it — he blames Schumpeter for "the
  myth that Pareto and Barone have 'solved' the problem."

## The decisive turn, 1919–1922

Post-WWI socialist governments and the misread lessons of war economies
forced concrete proposals: Otto Neurath's calculation-in-kind (Vienna, 1919),
Otto Bauer's *Der Weg zum Sozialismus*, and the German socialization
commission (Lederer, Heimann, Rathenau), whose Wissell–Moellendorff
memorandum of May 1919 coined the term "planned economy" — a document Hayek
noted in 1935 "closely resembles the proposals for planning now so prevalent
in Great Britain." Against this background three authors converged
independently:

- **[[mises-ludwig|Mises]]** (April 1920, *Archiv für Sozialwissenschaften*):
  rational calculation requires money prices for *all* factors of
  production, not just consumer goods; without them planning is groping in
  the dark. Expanded as *Die Gemeinwirtschaft* (1922; English *Socialism*,
  1936).
- **Max Weber** (*Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft*, posthumous 1921): reached
  the same conclusion independently, warning that calculation failure in a
  fully socialized system could threaten mass starvation.
- **Boris Brutzkus** (Petrograd lectures, 1920–22; *Economic Planning in
  Soviet Russia*, 1935): arrived at parallel conclusions in isolation from
  the West; Hayek and he read the collapse of Soviet "war communism" as
  empirical confirmation.

## Socialist responses and Hayek's taxonomy (1929–1940)

Hayek's 1935 survey classifies the responses: (1) the **mathematical
solution** (F. M. Taylor, W. C. Roper, H. D. Dickinson 1933, after Barone) —
conceded by Hayek as logically conceivable but humanly impracticable
("the mere assembly of these data is a task beyond human capacity"), an
early statement of the [[knowledge-problem]]; (2) **trial-and-error
price-fixing** from inherited capitalist prices; (3) **abandoning consumer
choice** (Maurice Dobb, 1933); (4) the **competitive solution** — market
socialism.

The mature competitive solution came in [[lange-oskar|Lange]] and Taylor,
*On the Economic Theory of Socialism* (1938), and Dickinson, *Economics of
Socialism* (1939): a Central Planning Board (Dickinson: "Supreme Economic
Council") issues parametric factor prices, adjusts them by trial and error
against shortages and surpluses, and instructs plant managers to equate
marginal cost and price, while consumer-goods and labor markets remain real.
Hayek's 1940 *Economica* reply argued the scheme cannot match the speed of
continuous market adjustment, fails entirely for non-standardized goods,
cannot decentralize investment, and dissolves managerial accountability into
"quasi-competition where the persons really responsible will not be the
entrepreneur but the official who approves his decisions." Its most
politically consequential move: seizing on Dickinson's own concession that
under socialism "the economic and the political machinery of society will
fuse into one," which Hayek tagged as "precisely the authoritarian doctrine
preached by Nazis and Fascists" — the germ of the [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]].

## Emic vs. Etic

Emic for the participants — a live scientific dispute of 1920–1940s, fought
in *Archiv für Sozialwissenschaften*, *Economic Journal*, and *Economica*.
Etic as a historiographical frame: "who won the calculation debate" became a
Cold-War and post-1989 interpretive battleground.

## Limitations and Critiques

The mid-century textbook verdict (via Schumpeter and Bergson) held that
Lange had answered Mises; Austrian-school reassessments (Lavoie 1985) and
the post-1989 collapse of planned economies reversed the consensus for many,
while others argue Hayek's victory narrative is itself retrospective
construction. Lange himself, per Hayek's complaint, characterized the
critics as "retreating to a second line of defense" — the two sides never
agreed even on the debate's own history. This page records positions with
attribution and does not adjudicate.
