## Definition and Scope

The German Enlightenment (*Aufklärung*) was the Central European inflection of the broader eighteenth-century European intellectual movement emphasizing human reason, empirical inquiry, religious toleration, and the reform of government and society. It was structurally distinct from the French and British Enlightenments in several respects: it was rooted in universities and territorial church structures rather than in court culture or gentleman scholars; it proceeded through the medium of the German vernacular only gradually displacing Latin; and it operated within a politically fragmented Holy Roman Empire that precluded the centralized salon culture of Paris or the commercial public sphere of London.

The German Enlightenment is inseparable from its characteristic concept of [[german-cosmopolitanism]] — the aspiration to transcend local and national particularity in the name of universal humanity (*Menschheit*), while simultaneously grappling with genuine engagement with the world's diversity.

## Institutional and Social Structure

Contrary to England and France, where universities "receded into insignificance during the eighteenth century, German universities became the power nodes of intellectual and political rejuvenation." The Aufklärung was embedded in two pivot institutions: the territorial school and the territorial church. "Almost all successful writers of the German Enlightenment had to earn their living as teachers or civil servants" — a structural contrast with the French philosophes, who depended on court patronage or the market for publications. Key institutional centers included the University of Göttingen (Gatterer, Schlözer, Michaelis), the Berlin Academy of Sciences, and the learned societies (*Gelehrte Gesellschaften*) scattered across the principalities.

## The Linguistic Turn: German Replaces Latin

A foundational shift within the Aufklärung was the replacement of Latin by German as the medium of intellectual discourse. Just after the Thirty Years' War, Latin-language books outstripped German by 2:1; by 1740 Latin books made up just over a quarter of the Leipzig book fair's total; by 1800 they constituted a mere four percent. Christian Wolff "was the first to propagate German as a language of instruction." Gotthold Ephraim Lessing, "a man of many talents, heralded a cultural transformation of unprecedented magnitude. In the years around 1760, German became the main language of Enlightenment in the principalities and free cities of the Holy Roman Empire."

This shift created a German-language public sphere with a deeper social reach and was inseparable from rising literacy rates. Recent research places literacy in Germany — measured by the ability of marriage partners to sign their names — "well above 50% in 1780," higher in cities and Protestant territories, with class as the most salient differentiating factor.

## Key Figures and Their Contributions

**Gotthold Ephraim Lessing** (1729–1781): Playwright, critic, and philosopher of religious toleration. His famous *Letter Number Seventeen* (1759) signaled the turn away from French court culture toward an independent German literature. His late theological writings (*Nathan the Wise*, *The Education of the Human Race*) made religious toleration a central Enlightenment theme. The Oxford Handbook notes he was "at once actor and emblem of this transition."

**Immanuel Kant** (1724–1804): The defining philosopher of the mature Aufklärung. His cosmopolitan political writings — *Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Aim* (1784), *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795) — elaborated the most rigorous philosophical justification for cosmopolitan norms and international law. His *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) reoriented the epistemological foundations of Enlightenment thought. Kant also penned the most consistent Enlightenment critique of colonialism, "reject[ing] the right to settle on another people's soil except when permitted through a treaty."

**Johann Gottfried Herder** (1744–1803): Kant's former student and the Enlightenment's foremost theorist of cultural particularity and diversity. Herder "repudiated the 'spirit of the times,' which he saw as a presumption of uniformity based upon self-adulatory parochialism." His *Ideas for a Philosophy of the History of Mankind* (1784–1791) argued that each people (*Volk*) possessed its own irreducible cultural character (*Volksgeist*), incommensurable with others: "'Why do we not realize that if we do not have all the vices and virtues of former ages?' it is 'because we are not in their position … nor breathe the same air.'" Herder simultaneously affirmed that "all men are endowed with the power of attaining superior reason."

**Thomas Abbt** (1738–1766): Author of *On Dying for the Fatherland* (1761), the first prose text to demand willingness to die for the territorial state (Prussia). Abbt's work "wedded reason and sentiment" and represented a structural precursor of modern nationalism, linking patriotic sacrifice to enlightened civic virtue. His work was embedded in Prussian war propaganda during the Seven Years' War.

**Johann Christoph Gatterer** (1727–1799) and **August Ludwig Schlözer** (1735–1809): The Göttingen school of universal history, which produced path-breaking attempts at a non-providential, empirically grounded world history. Universal history was written, in Kant's phrase, "with the intention of creating world citizens" (*in weltbürgerlicher Absicht*). Their approach replaced the focus on rulers and dynasties with structural analysis.

## The Göttingen School and Universal History

The University of Göttingen hosted the most internationally connected scholarly network in the German Enlightenment. Schlözer's major works — *Vorstellung seiner Universal-Historie* (1772–1773) and *WeltGeschichte* (1785–1789) — "strove to supersede" both speculative philosophical history and purely compilatory collections, articulating the parameters of a non-providential history of mankind based on "space, time, and the plurality of ethnic groups or civilizations."

Enlightened historians at Göttingen understood that "the powerful gaze turns the aggregate into a system, a system in which world and humanity form a unit." This claim for systematic universal history, grounded in empirical source-criticism, anticipated the later professional historiography of Ranke and the German Historical School.

## The Aufklärung and Religion

The German Enlightenment's relationship to religion was more accommodating than its French counterpart. "German scholarly pursuits contributed to the force and panache of this intellectual revolution, both in Protestant territories and in the Catholic South, which was, contrary to long-held opinions, not stifled by Baroque piety. Religion and religiosity did not simply 'adjust' to 'modernity,' but theological preoccupations fed into enlightened interests." Biblical hermeneutics and Pietism both contributed to the formation of Aufklärung scholarship — Johann David Michaelis's naturalistic approach to Hebrew and his 1761 expedition to Arabia exemplify this theological-philological nexus.

Lessing's promotion of religious toleration, extended in his play *Nathan the Wise* to include Judaism and Islam, represents the most programmatic expression of Enlightenment religious liberalism. Christian Wilhelm Dom's *On the Civic Improvement of the Jews* (1781) applied Enlightenment principles to Jewish emancipation, though the debate over Jewish civic integration "oscillated between patronizing 'betterment,' calls for conversion, and anthropological curiosity."

## Limits and Contradictions

The German Enlightenment was not without its contradictions and internal limits:

1. **Race**: Christoph Meiners (1747–1810), professor of *Weltweisheit* at Göttingen, produced explicitly racist writings on Africans that "have given the German Enlightenment a bad reputation." Even Herder, "rightly considered the paragon of cultural diversity, one comes across occasional comments on non-Europeans, Mongols for example, of a blatantly offensive nature."

2. **Empire**: Because the German states "took no part in the colonial expansion of the time," the critique of empire in Germany was principled and detached rather than politically urgent. By contrast with Britain (the Hastings trial) or France (the 1794 abolition debate), German anti-colonial thought remained primarily philosophical.

3. **Gender**: The Aufklärung's claims for rational autonomy and civic participation were explicitly gendered male. Enlightenment philosophers argued that women's "passivity and lack of self-control" disqualified them from the autonomous reason that underlay citizenship rights. See [[gender-in-modern-germany]].

## The Enlightenment and Nationalism

The relationship between the Aufklärung and German nationalism is complex and contested. The Aufklärung generated both cosmopolitan and nationalist currents simultaneously. Herder's concept of *Volksgeist* — that each people has an incommensurable cultural character expressed in language and custom — was appropriated by later romantic nationalists, though Herder himself was a critic of political nationalism and state aggression. See [[german-cosmopolitanism]].

The transition from Aufklärung cosmopolitanism to romantic nationalism was not a clean break. As the Handbook observes: "The 'nation' frequently invoked around 1800 was not a concept developed in clear contradistinction to cosmopolitism. It rather seems that cosmopolitanism and hyperbolic 'nationalism' carved out separate channels." Goethe's concept of *Weltliteratur* (1827) exemplifies the persistence of cosmopolitan ideals into the post-Enlightenment era.

## Historiography

The historiography of the German Enlightenment has moved away from treating it as a derivative or peripheral expression of French ideas. Scholars now emphasize its distinctive institutional base (universities rather than salons), its productive engagement with Protestant theology, and its early formulation of what would become the defining German idealist tradition in philosophy. Jonathan Israel's emphasis on the "radical Enlightenment" (Spinoza, Bayle) as the foundation of modern secular thought has influenced Aufklärung studies but remains debated.
