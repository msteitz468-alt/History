# Religious Warfare (analytical concept)

A category of organised violence in which contemporaries perceived a direct and defining connection between the war and God's will or purpose. Distinct from (but overlapping with) "just war" theory and institutional crusade. Key markers: claims of divine mandate (Deo auctore), sanctification of the conflict (not necessarily of every act of bloodshed), demonisation of the enemy, engagement with Scripture for operational or legitimating guidance, and the use of religious symbols/communities (Elect People, antemurale) to bind fighters.

## Emic vs Etic

**Emic:** Contemporaries (Walsingham on crusade, Nicholas of Pelhrimov on Hussite defence) spoke of wars as "sanctified." Taborites and Münster Anabaptists treated their struggles as purgative and world-changing.

**Etic:** Modern historians (Housley, Partner, Dupront, Johnson) use the term to capture phenomena that are religiously framed but do not always fit the canonical crusade model (papal bulls, cross, indulgences) or the later confessional Wars of Religion. Useful for analysing 1400–1536 transition cases.

## Core Elements (per Housley)

1. Belief that God is actively and purposefully engaged.
2. Transformation from providential background to immediate mandate.
3. Attributes: Scripture as detailed guide; messianic roles for leaders; symbols (antemurale Christianitatis, crusade privileges filtered through national feeling).
4. Often accompanied by "turkification" (demonisation) of the enemy.
5. Stimulus to critique when claims become radical.

## Limitations and Critiques

- Risk of over-extension: not every religiously tinged war qualifies.
- Boundary problems with "holy war" (narrower, Dupront-style extremities) and crusade (institutional).
- Eurocentric if applied globally without adjustment.
- Post-Enlightenment secularisation makes the category appear exotic; yet 20th–21st c. examples show persistence.
