## Definition and Scope

This page traces the Western scientific tradition of studying how Pacific islands — especially coral atolls — were formed, from the voyages of Bougainville and Cook (1766–79) to the post-WWII nuclear test era. It covers three interlocking themes identified by historian Alistair Sponsel (ch. 8 of [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]): (1) the rise of typological/taxonomic thinking about island types ~1770–1850; (2) how the scale of Pacific travel materially shaped the scope and ambition of theories ~1820–1920; (3) the treatment of Pacific islands as interchangeable scientific "laboratories" from the late 19th century onward, culminating in nuclear weapons testing framed as scientific experiment.

This is a history of Western science, not a comprehensive account of Pacific island knowledge systems. For Indigenous knowledge frameworks, see [[indigenous-environmental-knowledge-pacific]]. For the geological substance of island formation, see [[pacific-island-formation]].

## Phase 1: Typological Thinking (~1766–1831)

### Bougainville's puzzle

Bougainville's 1766–9 circumnavigation took him through the Tuamotu Islands (which he named l'Archipel Dangereux, the Dangerous Archipelago) — atolls whose treacherous reefs enclosed shimmering lagoons beneath barely visible low sandy islands. Surveying them seemed futile: each island appeared generic, indistinguishable. Bougainville himself asked of one: "Is this extraordinary land rising, or is it in ruins?" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8). The question framed two centuries of scientific inquiry.

### Forster's taxonomy (1778)

Johann Reinhold Forster, naturalist on Cook's second voyage (1772–5), was the first Western scientist to offer a systematic answer. Having seen the contrast between the low Tuamotus and the mountainous Society Islands in close succession, he proposed two fundamental island types: "The high ones seem to be the work of fire [i.e. volcanic activity] & the low ones are the work of the Sea & its Inhabitants." He identified the low islands as built by corals — "immense lumps of rocks of the Lythophyta Class" — building upward to sea level, with sand and shells accumulating atop the reefs to form low islands. He published this in Observations Made on a Voyage Round the World (1778), organized taxonomically rather than chronologically (an unconventional format imposed partly by the British Admiralty's prohibition on competing chronological accounts). "The term 'coral reef' came into use as a response to Forster's work" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8).

Forster distinguished two sub-classes of high islands: those encircled by reefs (Tahiti, Fiji) and those without (mainly extra-tropical). He drew on Tahitian oral traditions about the god Maui/O-Maoowe — who "shakes the earth, and causes earthquakes" — as evidence for the volcanic origin of high islands, and interpreted the tradition that Maui "dragged a great land from West to East" as indicating memory of an ancient continent. This early integration of Indigenous testimony into Western scientific argument was not followed systematically by later practitioners.

The impact was immediate: French zoologist Jean Vincent Félix Lamouroux extrapolated Forster's claims into a scenario where corals might grow so thickly in tropical oceans that interhemispheric navigation would become impossible.

### Kotzebue and Indigenous cartography (1816–17)

Otto von Kotzebue's Russian Navy expedition to the Marshall Islands provided a crucial episode in early atoll science that illustrates the relationship between Western cartography and Indigenous geographic knowledge. Kotzebue required weeks of surveying to perceive that the ring-shaped Romanzoff (now Wotje atoll) curved back on itself. His encounter with the Marshall Islander Lagediack was more efficient: Lagediack "drew on the sand a circle, nearly in the form of the group Otdia [the atoll Wotje], placed round the edge of it large and small stones, which represented the islands; and . . . marked the channels", and explained the other islands "in the same sensible manner" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8). Marshall Islanders did not require Western cartography to understand the annular form of their atoll chain. Kotzebue concluded that the "uniformity [of the low island chain] is probably not accidental; but this structure seems to be peculiar to the corals."

Chamisso (expedition naturalist) called for a longitudinal program: "An accurate description of the state of these reefs at different periods, for instance, at an interval of half a century, if it were possible and really undertaken, must contribute to throw light upon many points of natural history" (Chamisso, quoted in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8). This call anticipated the long-term monitoring programs that would not arrive for another century.

### Navy surveying as theory-testing (from 1831)

Francis Beaufort, chief hydrographer of the British Navy, gave detailed instructions from 1831 to all surveyors dispatched to the tropical Pacific to study coral reef formation. Crucially, unlike normal hydrographic instructions, these did not specify particular locations — they required choosing a representative reef and using it to evaluate general theories. This operationalized Forster's typology: individual atolls were treated as representative instances of a class.

The first commander to receive such orders was Robert FitzRoy, preparing for the Beagle voyage (1831–6). Beaufort directed him to survey "circularly formed Coral Islands in the Pacific" and evaluate "the modern and very plausible theory [of Quoy and Gaimard, endorsed by Charles Lyell] that these wonderful formations instead of ascending from the [bottom of the] sea, have been raised from the summits of extinct volcanoes." Beechey and later Belcher received a "boring apparatus" to test what underlay reefs. American naturalists on the US Exploring Expedition (1838–42) under Charles Wilkes also received boring equipment. Both Belcher and Wilkes's lieutenant found boring through coral rock "far more challenging than their superiors had anticipated."

## Phase 2: The Grand Theories (~1831–1900)

### Darwin's subsidence theory

Charles Darwin, FitzRoy's shipmate on the Beagle, developed the dominant theory of atoll formation. At Tahiti in November 1835, gazing across to the equally rugged Mo'orea (Eimeo), encircled by a barrier reef, he was "forcibly struck" with the insight that if the island sank very slowly while corals continued growing upward, the result would be an empty lagoon surrounded by an atoll. He rejected the prevailing crater-rim theory as "a monstrous hypothesis."

Darwin proposed that Forster's three types (high islands; reef-encircled high islands; atolls) were actually three stages in a genealogical sequence driven by crustal subsidence compensating for tectonic uplift of South America. "Atolls were 'monuments' standing above high islands that had sunk and drowned" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8). His treatise The Structure and Distribution of Coral Reefs (1842) became one of his earliest claims to scientific fame and introduced "atoll" (from the Maldivian atholhu) into general Pacific usage.

Darwin's theory was grand — purporting to explain every coral reef on Earth while he had personally studied only three. He spent the rest of his life as "one of the world's foremost scholars of coral reefs, even though he never returned to the tropics or revisited reef formation as a research topic."

### Dana's parallel synthesis

James Dwight Dana, member of the US Exploring Expedition (1838–42), developed a parallel theory very similar to Darwin's but giving greater significance to seawater temperature as a controlling factor in coral growth. He too produced massive post-voyage treatises (Geology, 1849; Zoophytes, 1846) explaining the origin of atolls and barrier reefs on a global scale, plus a specific account of the Hawaiian chain's history.

### Murray's challenge

After HMS Challenger (1872–6) revealed that much of the ocean floor was blanketed in calcareous sediment from microscopic plankton, the ship's naturalist John Murray proposed that atolls might form atop accumulating sediment banks, without requiring large-scale geological subsidence. His theory eliminated the need for Darwin's crustal movements. It attracted vociferous advocates who saw it as a tool to challenge Darwinian influence in British science, spawning decades of "the coral reef problem."

A third theory, R.A. Daly's "glacial-control" theory (1910, 1915), proposed that Pleistocene sea-level fall during glaciations exposed and eroded existing islands, producing platforms at the right depth for coral colonization during subsequent sea-level rise. All three theories — Darwin's subsidence, Murray's sediment-bank, Daly's glacial-control — remained viable into WWII.

## Phase 3: Shorter Expeditions, Narrower Claims (~1880–1940)

### Effects of faster travel

As steam travel accelerated and regularized Pacific crossings from the late 19th century, the pattern of scientific publication changed. Alexander Agassiz (Harvard) and Alfred Goldsborough Mayer (Carnegie Institution) could visit reefs annually rather than once in a lifetime. The result, Sponsel argues, was that "the duration and rarity of those long voyages tended to produce broad and ambitious ideas, whereas quicker expeditions tended to produce shorter publications containing narrower speculations" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8).

Agassiz visited every major coral reef in the world using the Albatross, wrote extensively on individual reef groups, but died in 1910 at sea with no synthesizing manuscript on reef formation despite decades of promises. Mayer created a long-term research station at Loggerhead Key (Dry Tortugas) and made annual Pacific expeditions from 1917–20, producing hundreds of publications but no global theory. Mayer himself summarized the problem: Darwin "saw only one atoll, Dana sailed past many but was permitted to land upon few... and Agassiz was compelled to cover such a vast field that certain of his conclusions, as he states himself, are still tentative."

### Funafuti boring expeditions (1896–8)

The British Association for the Advancement of Science organized three successive boring expeditions to Funafuti atoll (~1,000 km north of Fiji) to definitively resolve Darwin vs. Murray. Darwin himself had urged this approach in 1881: "I wish that some doubly rich millionaire would take it into his head to have borings made in some of the Pacific and Indian Atolls." Sollas commanded the first attempt (1896), targeting 100 fathoms (183 m); boring equipment repeatedly fouled and the deepest bore reached only 32 metres. "The boring is an ignominious failure . . . I am resigned like a dead man to death" (Sollas to Haddon, 1896, quoted in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8). The second expedition (1897, under Sydney geologist T.W.E. David) recovered cores from 213 m but failed to reach bedrock. The third (1898) also failed to resolve the theoretical dispute. British and Australian scientific nationalism entangled with the inquiry.

## Phase 4: Atolls as Military Laboratories (1940s–1973)

### Pacific warfare and atoll knowledge

WWII amphibious warfare demonstrated the strategic significance of reef structure: at Tarawa (November 1943), marines suffered heavy casualties when landing craft foundered on reefs. After US victories at Majuro, Enewetak, and Kwajalein, US Geological Survey scientist Harry Ladd proposed sustained scientific investigation of occupied Pacific archipelagoes, including boring "through a living coral reef at a strategic location."

### Operation Crossroads and the "laboratory" metaphor

The US nuclear tests at Bikini Atoll (Operation Crossroads, summer 1946) were the largest peacetime US military operation in history. The commander, Vice Admiral Blandy, described the enterprise as "a scientific experiment by the United States Government." The scientific corps conducted baseline surveys not only of Bikini but of nearby atolls as "controls" — Enewetak (downwind), Rongerik (upwind), and others. This treatment of atolls as interchangeable experimental units explicitly depended on the same typological thinking Forster had initiated in 1778.

The 167 Bikini Islanders were relocated to Rongerik, sold the move with the promise that "the United States government now wants to turn [the bomb] into something good for the benefit of mankind." The PR strategy embedded "interesting, newsworthy stories" about Darwin and the geological puzzle of atoll formation to "forestall much press criticism and speculation of a harmful nature."

A 1947 press release announced: "Drillers from the oil fields of Oklahoma began working around the clock on Bikini Island today in an operation that may settle a one hundred and ten year old argument among geologists" — the Darwin–Murray controversy — while obscuring the bore's relevance to assessing bomb damage to the atoll's deep structure.

### Enewetak 1952: confirmation and its limits

In 1952 at Enewetak (by then a second US nuclear test site), a drilling crew under Ladd's supervision finally reached volcanic rock at 1,280 metres — seven times deeper than Darwin had guessed necessary. This confirmed Darwin's basic mechanism: the reef was built of shallow-water corals atop a sunken volcanic foundation. However, detailed core analysis revealed a far more complex history than Darwin's sequential model predicted: the atoll had alternated between emergence above sea level and drowning below the coral growth zone multiple times. "It was no longer plausible for any single, elegant theory such as Darwin's to encompass the origins of all atolls, let alone all coral reefs. In turn, there must be limits to how generalizable the knowledge would be that derived from any given island 'laboratory'" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8).

### PACE injunction (1973) and the limits of typological logic

In 1970 the US Air Force planned a Paciﬁc Atoll Cratering Experiment (PACE) at Enewetak, using 500-ton TNT blasts whose results could be compared with nuclear crater data. The exiled Enewetak Islanders (relocated to Ujilang) invoked the 1970 National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) to seek an injunction. The Air Force's environmental impact statement argued PACE could not be conducted elsewhere because no other atoll had "similar geologic conditions to the previous nuclear craters." On 19 January 1973, the US District Court in Honolulu granted the injunction — the first time NEPA was held to apply to US-administered territories outside the USA, and the first time Marshall Islanders (non-US-citizens) were granted standing under it. "There turned out to be limits to how satisfactorily one atoll might be made to stand in for another" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8).

### Mauna Loa and the Keeling Curve

The Mauna Loa observatory on Hawai'i Island has recorded atmospheric CO₂ concentrations continuously since 1958 (established by the US Weather Bureau). The resulting "Keeling Curve" — steadily rising CO₂ — demonstrated that fossil-fuel emissions contribute to greenhouse warming. Sponsel notes: "It is characteristic of the history of Western science in the Pacific that an iconic indicator of the whole planet's health owes its origin to the colonial infrastructure in Hawai'i and depends on the continuous presence of researchers there."

## Historiography

Sponsel's chapter situates Pacific atoll science within a "science and empire" framework: voyaging naturalists were simultaneously engaged in disinterested knowledge-production and imperial expansion. Cook's secret instructions directing him to claim discovered territory for Britain "highlights the contradiction between the then-common rhetoric of science as the pursuit of disinterested knowledge . . . and the underlying reality that scientific activities in the Pacific were often mutually constituted with imperial or commercial undertakings" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 8).

The dominant interpretive tension in contemporary historiography of Pacific science is between celebratory accounts of scientific discovery and critical accounts of epistemological violence — the displacement of Indigenous knowledge systems by Western ones, and of Indigenous peoples by scientific "experiments." The protests against the Thirty Meter Telescope on Mauna Kea, and the critical reassessment surrounding the 250th anniversary of Cook's Pacific entry, are cited as evidence of these legacies remaining active.
