# Egyptian Dynastic Chronology

The framework by which ancient Egyptian history is dated and periodized — the
system of **dynasties** and **kingdoms/intermediate periods** that every narrative
of Egypt uses, together with the king-lists, annals, and astronomical and
radiometric methods that underpin it. Ian Shaw's introductory chapter to the
[[sources/shaw-oxford-history-ancient-egypt-2000|Oxford History of Ancient Egypt]]
is in large part a primer on how this scaffold was built and how far it can be
trusted.

## The dynastic system and its origin

The division of the pharaonic period (c.3100–332 BCE) into numbered **dynasties**
descends from [[actors/manetho|Manetho]], an Egyptian priest who c. the third
century BCE composed the *Aegyptiaca*, grouping rulers "of the same family or the
same town, or sometimes defined by political criteria such as the nature of the
king's relationship to his predecessors … or the location of their principal royal
residence." The grouping into **Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms** separated by
**Intermediate Periods** is a later, modern overlay.

Shaw stresses the system's limits. Manetho's scheme "often strains to contain the
many new chronological trends and currents that can be perceived outside the simple
passing of the throne from one group of individuals to another," and it "created a
misleading air of continuity in the succession of kings and dynasties through his
assumption that only one king could occupy the throne of Egypt at any one time" —
an assumption broken by co-regencies and by periods of parallel rival dynasties.

## Sources for the chronology

- **King-lists.** Recorded on temple walls (the canonical 19th-Dynasty lists of
  Sety I and Ramesses II at [[places/abydos|Abydos]]), in papyri, and in remote rock
  carvings (Wadi Hammamat). Crucially, "the king-lists were not concerned so much
  with history as with ancestor worship" — the living king made offering to
  legitimate predecessors, so usurpers and inconvenient reigns (Hatshepsut, the
  Amarna kings) were silently dropped.
- **The Turin Canon.** A Ramesside papyrus (13th c. BCE) and "the most informative"
  king-list, recording each reign in years, months and days back to the 1st Dynasty
  and into mythical time; it independently "provides some support for Manetho's
  system of dynasties by incorporating a break at the end of the 5th Dynasty."
- **The [[concepts/palermo-stone|Palermo Stone]].** A 5th-Dynasty (c.2400 BCE)
  basalt annal-stele recording reigns back to mythical rulers, with Nile-flood
  heights, cult ceremonies, taxation and building noted year by year. Its Old
  Kingdom dates may count **biennial cattle-censuses** rather than single years, so
  "the number of 'years' in the Old Kingdom dates may well have to be doubled."

## Astronomical and radiometric anchors

The Egyptians dated events by **regnal year** of the current king, not from a fixed
era. Absolute dates therefore depend on a handful of **[[concepts/sothic-cycle|Sothic]]**
(Sirius heliacal-rising) observations: the civil "wandering" year of 365 days drifts
against the solar year by ~1 day per four years, so a recorded Sothic rising fixes a
date once per 1,460-year cycle. Two records — a letter of year 7 of Senusret III
(≈1872 BCE) and Papyrus Ebers under Amenhotep I (≈1541 BCE) — anchor the Middle and
New Kingdom dates by extrapolation. But the anchors are uncertain: the latitude of
observation matters, and scholars dispute whether the readings were taken at Memphis,
Thebes, or Elephantine, leaving "our astronomical linchpins … in reality somewhat
floating" (differences between "high" and "low" chronologies are usually only a few
decades).

Radiocarbon and dendrochronology "almost always provide solid independent support
for the conventional chronology" — they decisively defeated the proposed radical
**revision** of the [[processes/third-intermediate-period|Third Intermediate Period]]
(which would have compressed it from ~400 to ~150 years) — yet "the traditional
calendrical system of dating, whatever its failings, virtually always has a smaller
margin of error than radiocarbon dates."

## Political vs. cultural periodization

Shaw's deeper point is that the dynastic scaffold is a **political artefact** that
fits the cultural and archaeological record only loosely. Naqada III pottery runs on
into the Early Dynastic; Umm el-Qaab excavations reveal a "Dynasty 0" reaching back
into the 4th millennium, so "the last one or two centuries of the 'Predynastic' were
probably in many respects politically and socially 'Dynastic'." Conversely the
"intermediate periods" partly reflect the **patchiness of survival** of records, not
simply political collapse — a theme the volume's specialists press for the
[[processes/first-intermediate-period|First]] and
[[processes/second-intermediate-period|Second Intermediate Periods]].

## Limitations and critiques

- Co-regencies are real but their number and length are disputed; some scholars
  (e.g. Gae Callender) argue certain attested "co-regencies" may never have occurred.
- King-list numbers can be unreliable: the Turin Canon gives Senusret II and III
  reigns of 19 and 39 years, but their highest contemporary regnal dates are only 6
  and 19.
- The whole edifice is **relative** above the astronomical anchors, and the anchors
  themselves carry uncertainty of decades.
