## Overview

The "heirs of Mullā Ṣadrā" designates the tradition of philosophy descending from [[mulla-sadra]]'s *ḥikmat al-mutaʿāliya* ("transcendent wisdom") — his synthesis of primacy of existence, gradation of being, and substantial motion. This tradition was not immediately dominant in Ṣadrā's own lifetime — he faced considerable hostility in Shīrāz — but grew in influence across subsequent generations. Adamson's *Philosophy in the Islamic World* closes by noting that "no philosopher of the Islamic world lives on in the modern day more vividly than Ṣadrā, especially in Iran, where his works continue to be the subject of intense study."

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Chs. 53–55 (and closing passages).

## Immediate Successors

### Saʿīd Qummī (fl. mid–late 17th c.)

A student of Mullā Ṣadrā's son-in-law, Saʿīd Qummī (from the Iranian city of Qom) wrote a major seventeenth-century commentary on the *Theology of Aristotle* — the Arabic version of Plotinus that was a central text for Safavid Neoplatonism. The survival of many manuscripts of this *Theology* in Iran is itself evidence of the Safavid intellectual context in which Ṣadrā's tradition was rooted.

## The Tradition's Distinctive Features

The post-Ṣadrian tradition inherits and develops:

1. **Primacy of existence** (*aṣālat al-wujūd*): The foundational metaphysical claim that existence, not essence, is primary reality; God is pure unrestricted existence; all creatures are existence in varied degrees of intensity.
2. **Gradation of being** (*tashkīk al-wujūd*): Existence comes in degrees, forming a continuous spectrum from God (most intense) to material bodies (least intense), with no ontological gaps or discrete boundaries.
3. **Substantial motion** (*ḥaraka jawhariyya*): All existing things are in continuous change at the level of substance, not merely accidental properties.
4. **Knowledge as identity**: Genuine knowledge involves the knower becoming identical with the known at a higher level of existence.
5. **Synthesis of philosophy, Sufism, and Shiite religious commitment**: The tradition maintains Ṣadrā's conviction that philosophical demonstration, mystical insight, and Koranic revelation point to the same truths.

## Modern Iranian Context

Ṣadrā's works have been studied continuously in Iranian Shiite seminaries (*ḥawza*) from the Safavid period to the present. In the twentieth century, scholars like **Allameh Tabataba'i** (d. 1981) and **Morteza Motahhari** (d. 1979) engaged with Ṣadrā's metaphysics as a living philosophical framework rather than a historical artifact. The tradition became entangled with the ideology of the Islamic Republic after 1979, since Ṣadrā's synthesis of philosophy and Shiite religious commitment provided philosophical resources for clerical political authority.

European scholarship on this tradition was inaugurated primarily by **Henry Corbin** (d. 1978), who situated Ṣadrā within a continuous esoteric tradition from Suhrawardī onward and used the label "School of Isfahan." **Seyyed Hossein Nasr** has been the most prominent Anglophone advocate of the tradition's ongoing vitality. More recent critical scholarship (including Adamson) questions the "school" label while affirming the tradition's philosophical significance.

## Relationship to Islamic Modernism

An interesting tension runs through twentieth-century Islamic philosophy: [[muhammad-iqbal]] and [[muhammad-abduh]], the most influential Islamic modernist thinkers, were both critical of the over-Hellenization of Islamic thought and sought to ground Islamic philosophy more directly in the Koran and empirical engagement with the world. The Ṣadrian tradition, by contrast, is deeply Neoplatonic and theoretical. These represent two different answers to the question of what Islamic philosophy should do in modernity: reform Islam in dialogue with European thought (Abduh, Iqbāl) or deepen and continue the pre-modern synthesis (the Ṣadrian school).

## Historiography

The Ṣadrian tradition has been the subject of more European-language scholarship than any other tradition of later Islamic philosophy, though the field remains small relative to the study of Avicenna or Averroes. The Iranian context has given the tradition political salience that has complicated purely philosophical assessment. Adamson's treatment aims for historical accuracy while situating Ṣadrā among his contemporaries and predecessors rather than as an isolated peak.
