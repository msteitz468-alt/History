# Infiltration Tactics (1918 German Offensive Doctrine)

The German offensive tactical doctrine introduced for the 1918 Spring Offensives on the Western Front. It emphasized small, specialized assault units (stormtroops or *Stoßtruppen*) that infiltrated through enemy lines, bypassed strongpoints, and pushed deep into the rear to disrupt command, artillery, and reserves, supported by precise, short artillery preparations and decentralized initiative. Also known as "Hutier tactics" (after General Oskar von Hutier) or stormtroop tactics.

## Core Principles (per Lupfer and 1918 regulations)

- **Infiltration / bypassing strongpoints**: Assault groups avoid frontal assaults on fortified positions. They penetrate weak spots, exploit gaps, and continue forward, leaving mopping-up of bypassed enemy to follow-on forces.
- **Combined arms and artillery-infantry cooperation**: Very short, intense artillery barrages (often using gas and smoke) to suppress rather than destroy; infantry advances immediately behind the barrage ("creeping barrage" techniques refined); machine guns and light mortars integrated at small-unit level.
- **Decentralized command and small-unit initiative**: Stormtroop squads and platoons are given significant freedom to choose routes and exploit opportunities within the overall mission. Mission-type orders (*Auftragstaktik*) are emphasized.
- **Specialized units and training**: Elite stormtroop detachments receive extra training, equipment (light machine guns, grenades, flamethrowers, etc.), and are used to lead assaults. The doctrine is disseminated through a massive training program to regular units.
- **Deep objectives and tempo**: The goal is not a slow, methodical advance but rapid penetration to paralyze the defender's ability to react.

This doctrine represented a revival of maneuver after years of attrition and was explicitly designed to avoid the pitfalls that had plagued earlier Allied and German offensives (over-reliance on massive artillery, rigid schedules, failure to exploit initial success).

## Relation to Other Concepts

- Built on lessons from the elastic defense-in-depth (many of the same principles of depth, reserves, and counteraction applied in reverse).
- Forerunner to later 20th-century concepts of deep battle, blitzkrieg, and modern combined-arms maneuver.
- Illustrates principles of war: surprise, offensive, maneuver, economy of force, unity of command (at small unit level), and simplicity (in execution once the barrage lifts).
- Contrasts with the more linear, firepower-heavy tactics of 1916–17.

## Development and Dissemination

The tactics evolved from earlier experiments (e.g., at Verdun, Riga, Caporetto) but were systematically refined in winter 1917–18. [[ludendorff]] and OHL directed a vast training effort involving specially selected units that then trained others. The doctrine was not rigid; units were expected to adapt to local conditions.

Range 3 of [[sources/lupfer-dynamics-of-doctrine-1981]] (rest of Ch. 2 + Ch. 3) establishes completion of the 1918 offensive doctrine: it "incorporated many of the virtues of their elastic defense-in-depth" (see [[concepts/elastic-defense-in-depth]]) "and which avoided several of the pitfalls into which the Allied tactical doctrine had fallen." "The new offensive doctrine described the complementary use of firepower and maneuver, using infantry forces which avoided strongpoints and plunged deeply into enemy positions, aided by highly efficient and effective artillery fire."

These changes "were not invented by one mastermind. Rather, they were the product of a thorough collective effort which enlisted the advice and experience of every possible source." "The tactical doctrine was disseminated to the units through a remarkable training program, and the entire procedure was directed with the knowledge that conditions and unique situations would always affect the application of the tactical doctrine." (Synopsis and Ch. 3)

## Limitations and Outcomes

While tactically successful in the initial breakthroughs of March–April 1918 (e.g., Operation Michael), the offensives ultimately failed to achieve strategic decision due to exhaustion of the assault units, lack of reserves and logistics for exploitation, and Allied recovery (including the introduction of tanks and improved defense-in-depth on the Allied side).

## Generalizations on Doctrine Dynamics (from Lupfer Ch. 3)

Per [[sources/lupfer-dynamics-of-doctrine-1981]] Range 3: the Germans developed and applied doctrine inductively rather than deductively. "The process of deriving the doctrine was inductive, and the application was in the same inductive spirit." "Evidence from the battlefield was more respected than the doctrine." "For the Germans all tactical solutions were tentative." "This process still demands much talent and ability, and it still requires a deliberate search for evidence. Glib solutions do not replace hard work."

Contrast to Allies: "Too often on the Allied side, tactics originally derived from experiences ... became deductive formulas indiscriminately deemed appropriate for any situation, despite contradictory evidence or changed conditions." German approach fostered "a healthy curiosity and mental flexibility."

Crown Prince Rupprecht (quoted): “There is no panacea. A formula is harmful. Everything must be applied according to the situation."

Process was corporate: OHL under [[ludendorff]] and [[hindenburg]] selected and employed talent efficiently (e.g. Capt. Hermann Geyer); "under the name of Ludendorff is comprised, exactly what a staff should be, a composite brain." Principles "sufficiently general to apply to a variety of tactical conditions, but sufficiently precise and specific to insure common understanding and unity of effort." "Their flexibility was their strength."
