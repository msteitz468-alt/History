## Definition

The characteristic **social forms of early farming society** — households,
compounds, communally shared spaces, and the residential and labour units within
which agriculture was actually practised. Where [[agricultural-origins]] asks how
foragers *became* farmers, this concept concerns the *consequences* of
established agriculture for social life (Amy Bogaard, CWH Vol. II, Ch. 5). Its
guiding observation: early agricultural societies were superficially very
diverse, but beneath the variety lie **striking convergences**, suggesting
agriculture "tended to constrain and direct social behaviour along certain lines."

## The Flannery convergence

In two seminal papers Kent Flannery compared early-farmer houses and settlements
in **West Asia** and **Mesoamerica** and found remarkable parallels: a recurring
trajectory from loose clusters of simple huts with **shared storage** (occupied
by extended-family/communally oriented groups of forager-cultivators) toward
**nuclear-family households** with their *own* storage as dependence on
agriculture deepened. This convergence across unconnected regions is the core
evidence that agriculture shaped society in patterned ways.

## The household trajectory

- **Communal compounds first:** open, communally oriented arrangements —
  families spread across multiple small dwellings, continuity between indoor and
  outdoor activity, externally located (shared) storage. Little incentive to
  intensify labour; the dietary weight of cultivated food was often limited.
- **Atomization into households:** communities could fragment into small-scale
  households storing their *own* produce — to avoid sharing with non-kin in
  growing settlements, and/or to gain competitive advantage by intensifying
  production. This is a root of **emergent inequality**.
- **Households stayed embedded:** even self-storing households remained tied into
  neighbourhood clusters, settlements, and regional networks for exchanging
  materials, livestock, and marriage partners.

## The role of herding

In **West Asia and China**, household production was quickly linked to combined
**farming + herding**. Livestock spread risk, banked surplus, improved crops
(manuring), and supplied meat for feasts — adding flexibility and "robusticity"
that helped household economies spread (and that eventually loosened the dense
cohesion of early villages and "megasites"). Where herding was *absent* —
**Mesoamerica** and the North American **Southwest** — households diversified
differently, yet still produced dramatic outcomes (the Olmec; the great houses
and kivas of Chaco Canyon). It has even been argued (Diamond) that the absence of
animal "secondary products" contributed to the later collapse of Mesoamerican
civilizations when they met European ones.

## Recurring patterns

- **High labour input per unit area.** Whatever the form — East Asian wet-rice
  paddies, West Asian/European "garden" agriculture, ridged plots — household
  farming converged on labour-intensive cultivation.
- **Reversibility.** In environmentally marginal settings the "communal compound"
  arrangement could re-emerge: risk could shift back from household to community
  depending on ecological and social conditions. The household was a *tendency*,
  not a destiny.

## A dissenting emphasis (Bailey on the Balkans)

The convergence-toward-the-household model emphasises cooperation and shared
trajectories. [[bailey-balkan-prehistory-2000|Douglass Bailey]], working the
[[balkans|Balkan]] Neolithic–Copper Age, accepts the centrality of the
**house and household** but reads their emergence as the creation of **durable,
inflexible boundaries** that locked groups into fixed places and generated
**tension, hoarding, and exclusion** as readily as sharing — see
[[exclusion-incorporation-projection]]. The two are not strictly opposed (both
make the household the pivotal early-farming institution), but Bailey's stress
on division and "illusion" is a useful corrective to any purely communal picture
of Neolithic society.
