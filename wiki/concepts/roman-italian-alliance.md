# The Roman–Italian Alliance System (the *socii*)

The web of alliances through which Rome organised Italy — and which the second
century quietly transformed from a partnership of junior allies into the
subordinate substructure of an empire, storing up the grievance that erupted in the
Social War. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 7 (Gabba).

## Definition and scope

Rome bound the Italian communities not by a single constitution but by individual
**bilateral treaties (*foedera*)** of varying terms — the system built during the
[[roman-expansion-italy|conquest of Italy]] and embodied in the
[[roman-commonwealth-formation-338bce|Roman Commonwealth]]. The allies (*socii*)
supplied troops to the Roman army and kept their internal autonomy; there was **no**
unified "Italy" as a political entity, only a collection of communities defined by
their relation to the dominant partner, Rome.

## The Hannibalic rupture and its aftermath

The defection of much of southern Italy after **Cannae** showed (Polybius) that
Roman military supremacy, not any Italian unity, had held the system together. After
victory Rome punished the defectors mainly by **confiscation of territory** (the
*ager Campanus* in full; proportional shares elsewhere), feeding the
[[agrarian-crisis-roman-republic|expansion of *ager publicus*]]. The alliances were
revived *de iure* but **altered *de facto*** by Rome's new Mediterranean power. The
clause requiring allies to preserve the ***maiestas populi Romani*** ("the majesty
of the Roman people") crystallised in this period — the juridical mark of the
allies' new subordination.

## From partners to substructure

Gabba's central argument: the *socii* were transformed "from junior partners into
constituent parts of the Roman state." Rome consistently **supported the Italian
oligarchies** (the guarantors of stability) while expecting their compliance with
imperial policy. The Italian upper classes, sharing in the profits of conquest,
pursued **economic parity, cultural assimilation, and eventually a demand for direct
participation** — citizenship. By the later second century this demand met a
**hardening elitism** in the Roman governing class; obedience, as Scipio Nasica and
Laelius feared, was increasingly held by fear rather than consent.

Local identity persisted strongly beneath this convergence (Cicero's later doctrine
of the **two *patriae*** — one of place, one of law). The system's deferral of the
citizenship question — together with the [[agrarian-crisis-roman-republic|agrarian
crisis]], since much *ager publicus* was worked by allies under their treaties —
made the *socii* a central problem of the late Republic, erupting in the
**[[social-war-91-88bce|Social War]] (91–88 BCE)**.

## Resolution: the enfranchisement of Italy (CAH IX, Ch. 4, Gabba)

The [[social-war-91-88bce|Social War]] ended the alliance system by **absorbing it**.
Facing an armed demand, Rome granted citizenship — the **Lex Iulia** (90), **Lex
Plautia Papiria** (89), and **Lex Pompeia** (Latin status for Transpadana) — to all
Italy south of the Po. The *socii* ceased to exist as a separate category; the Roman
state was **transformed from a city-state into a state of *municipia***, with Italy
now its territory. Gabba's theme is fulfilled: the junior partners became "constituent
parts of the Roman state," their elites entering the Roman governing class — the
demographic foundation of the late Republic and the Augustan "ideology of Italy."

## Limitations and critique

Gabba warns against anachronism: there was **no Roman programme to unify Italy** and
no unified "Italian" sentiment among the allied elites, who acted from self-interest
without renouncing local traditions. The "ideology of Italy" that culminates under
Augustus was emerging, but must not be read back as policy.
