## Definition

*Fitra* (Arabic: فطرة) is the innate natural constitution or endowment with which a human being is created. In the Qurʾānic base sense (Q 30:30), it refers to the natural disposition (*hanīf*) toward recognition of God implanted in every human soul. In Islamic philosophical usage — especially in Ibn Ṭufayl's [[ibn-tufayl-hayy-ibn-yaqzan]] — it becomes a technical term for the full complement of God-given capacities that constitute a human being's potential for intellectual, moral, and mystical development.

Goodman's introduction renders it: "The Arabs called the sum of a man's natural capacities his '*fitra*'; we might say his nature or his talents. In the case of Hayy, the *fitra* rapidly comes to mean his genius."

## Philosophical Content

In Ibn Ṭufayl's usage, *fitra* has several interlocking dimensions:

**As divine endowment.** The *fitra* is not merely a natural inheritance but a mode of divine being: "the endowment of his *fitra* is a mode of the divine, and man is made 'in the image of God.'" The soul's capacities are activated not by a human teacher but by God through the [[active-intellect-arabic-philosophy]] (identified with the angel Gabriel). This makes the *fitra* simultaneously natural (it is what the creature is) and theological (it is what God has breathed into the creature).

**As hierarchically structured capacities.** The *fitra* in [[ibn-tufayl-hayy-ibn-yaqzan]] is not a single property but a layered set of irreducible capacities — curiosity, boldness, moral sense, modesty, imitative ability, intellectual drive — each introduced into the narrative "with the matter-of-fact tone of an accepted assumption." These capacities are atomic to analysis: they are not made of anything simpler. They are given, not derived. This is Ibn Ṭufayl's deliberate anti-reductionism.

**As progressive actualization.** The *fitra* provides the ground; its realization is a process, not a given. Each of Ḥayy's seven developmental stages represents the actualization of a successive layer of the *fitra*'s potential. God "does not merely found his *fitra*, but continues on with him as the active energizer of his capacities" — the fitra is a living endowment, not a fixed blueprint.

**As the condition for autodidacticism.** The *fitra* is the premise that makes the thought-experiment of [[ibn-tufayl-hayy-ibn-yaqzan]] work. Without postulating a rich endowment of capacities for brilliance, curiosity, and goodness, the autodidactic wager — that a child alone in nature can reach mystical union — would be incoherent. The very name "Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān" (Living, Son of Awake) encapsulates the *fitra*: he is inherently "wide-awake, ready to learn, unrivaled in sensitivity, receptivity, openness to experience."

## Relationship to Free Will

A central problem addressed in Goodman's introduction to [[ibn-tufayl-hayy-ibn-yaqzan]] is whether the *fitra* as divine endowment is compatible with human freedom. If God plants the capacities and activates them, is there room for genuine choice? Ibn Ṭufayl's answer is that what is activated is not "programs of behavior" but "capacities to act with reason and self-awareness as a human being." Freedom is not cancelled by divine endowment but is itself one of the gifts of the *fitra*: "There comes a point of 'moral take-off' when the individual becomes morally self-sufficient and can choose for himself."

The resolution turns on a Neoplatonic interpretation of Islam: the assimilation of individual purpose to the divine will is not a surrender of freedom but its highest expression, since "freedom remains no longer a problem" when human will and divine will coincide through the soul's ascent. This is not fatalism but what Goodman calls the "radical monotheist" account of freedom: "the progressive assimilation of self to God (so far as lies in human power)."

## Contrast with Golding/Freud

Goodman's introduction explicitly sets Ibn Ṭufayl's *fitra*-based anthropology against the Freudian conception of the id — a conception Golding uses in *Lord of the Flies* to reach the opposite conclusion from the same narrative form. Where Ibn Ṭufayl postulates irreducible noble capacities as the elements of the soul, Golding postulates irreducible aggressive drives. The thought-experiment form is the same; the *fitra* loading the premises differs entirely.
