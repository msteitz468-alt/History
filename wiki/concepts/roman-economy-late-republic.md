# Economy and Society of Late-Republican Italy (133–43 BCE)

The production, exchange, and consumption of Roman Italy in the Republic's last
century — and, crucially, how **economy and juridical status** interacted. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 16 (Claude Nicolet).

## Status and economy

Nicolet's governing theme: in ancient society men were not only producers and
consumers but **free or slave, Roman or "ally."** **Status — more civic than economic
— shaped the economy directly**: the state controlled access to land (*ager
publicus*), excluded the upper *ordines* from certain activities, and made slave
labour a function of conquest. The age from the Gracchi to Caesar was one of
near-continuous **crisis** — not only the political "revolutions" but specifically
**economic** ones: grain-supply crises (124/3 onward), credit/financial crises (89–88,
66–63, 48–47), and coinage manipulations.

## Demography

Italy was probably **the most densely populated zone of the Mediterranean after
Egypt** — "the one massive unitary block in a fragmented world," its entire free
population Roman citizens from c. 60 BCE. The evidence is the **census figures**
(*capita civium*, adult males only before 28 BCE), whose great jumps (e.g. 395,000 in
115/4 to 910,000 in 70/69) reflect **enfranchisement** (the Social War's grant), not
natural increase. Nicolet's key revisions:

- **Slavery** rose massively — driven by **enforced immigration** of war-captives
  (150,000 from Epirus in 167; perhaps a million Gauls in 58–52) — peaking in the era
  of the **slave wars** (Sicily 136–132, 106; Italy 73–71); Brunt's ceiling is ~3
  million slaves in ~7.5 million total. Manumission was frequent (perhaps a third
  freed), so the citizen body always contained many ex-slaves and their families.
- **Urbanization** grew strongly (already in the 2nd c., not just the 1st): perhaps
  **40% urban** (the Hellenistic proportion), Rome swelling above all (320,000 on the
  grain dole, 57–46), fed by rural exodus.
- The **"depopulation of Italy"** lamented in the sources (Tiberius Gracchus to
  Augustus) is, Nicolet argues, **largely a myth** — affecting only certain regions
  and the **free peasantry**; the overall population grew ~50% over two centuries.

## Agriculture

Italy was "cultivated like a garden" (Varro), prosperous through **diversity** —
geographical, social, and in "modes of production." Recent research stresses that
**subsistence polyculture remained the rule** at the local level; talk of wholesale
"abandonment" of grain for vine and olive is too crude. The four major products were
**cereals** (everywhere, low-yield, never enough — hence the great **grain import
trade** and the imports-as-tax after 123), **vine** and **olive** (the high-value,
slave-worked specialities of Campania and the coasts), and **pasturage/transhumance**
(the highlands; large-scale ranching in Lucania/Bruttium). The **annexation of the Po
plain (Cisalpina)** opened vast centuriated land to Roman/Italian immigrants — the
great Romanization of the period.

## Contested

Nicolet writes against the older catastrophist picture (the Gracchan "agrarian
crisis" as a simple collapse of the free peasantry before slave-worked *latifundia*).
**Archaeology** (settlement survey, amphora/pottery distribution) has complicated the
literary tradition — revealing diversity, the coexistence of villa and peasant farm,
and the export boom in Italian wine — without wholly overturning the sources'
testimony to real rural distress in some regions. The **scale and demographic effect
of slavery**, and the reliability of the census figures, remain debated.
