# Napoleonic Warfare

The operational system by which **[[napoleon|Napoleon]]** made war **decisive** again — the synthesis
that, for Doughty & Gruber, defines the early nineteenth century. Building on the
**[[nation-in-arms|nation in arms]]**, Napoleon organized mass armies into the **self-contained
corps** (each a miniature army of all arms able to fight independently until the others concentrated),
moved them dispersed for speed and supply ("living off the land"), and **concentrated them on the
battlefield to seek the annihilation of the enemy army** by maneuver onto its flank or rear (the
*manœuvre sur les derrières*) followed by a decisive blow and relentless **pursuit**.

Its hallmarks — **speed, scale, decisiveness, the corps system, unity of command in a single
commander of genius, and the pursuit to destruction** — are displayed at
[[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-austerlitz|Austerlitz]] (1805) and
[[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-jena-auerstedt|Jena–Auerstedt]] (1806).
Doughty & Gruber also chart its **limits** — the [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/russian-campaign-1812|Russian catastrophe of 1812]], the *guerrilla* in Spain, and the coalition mass
that overwhelmed it at [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-leipzig|Leipzig]] and
[[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/napoleonic-wars/battle-of-waterloo|Waterloo]]. The system was the
direct object of study for **Jomini** and **Clausewitz** and the foundation of nineteenth-century
operational thought, until the **[[moltke-helmuth|Moltke]]/Prussian system** adapted it to railways and
mass.
