# Medieval Campaign Style

Etic analytical concept for the characteristic operational pattern of Western European warfare 1000–1300. Contemporaries experienced campaigns as "hostings," "chevauchées," raids, and sieges without using this modern umbrella term.

## Definition and Core Features

The default form of war was destructive raiding (ravaging) punctuated by sieges, with battle as a rare and usually incidental occurrence rather than the planned objective.

> "Across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, war did not change radically. Its staple was destructive raiding punctuated with sieges and, more occasionally, by battle." (Ch. 12)

**Ravaging as integral**: Armies had to live off the land; plunder formed part of soldiers' recompense. Ravaging served military ends: impoverishing the enemy, shaking vassal loyalty ("it was his duty to protect"), and forcing negotiation. Commanders followed Vegetius: "A great strategy is to press the enemy more with famine than with the sword." "It is preferable to subdue an enemy by famine, raids and terror, than in battle where fortune tends to have more influence than bravery." (Ch. 1, operationalized Ch. 12)

**Siege and provocation**: Major field actions frequently arose from sieges (Hattin deliberately provoked by Saladin's siege; Acre punctuated by relief attempts; Cortenova emerged from Frederick II's systematic ravaging and castle-taking around Mantua and Brescia). Castles and fortified camps (e.g., Milanese on the Lusignolo) could deadlock campaigns for weeks.

**Battle as rare decision**: "This was one of those rare occasions when one side was able to impose battle upon another. In general, it needed both sides to resolve upon battle, because running away was fairly easy." (Ch. 12, Cortenova). Battle usually required mutual consent or accident. When it occurred, it might be an ambush (Cortenova), flank attack after initial clash, or result of one side's overconfidence.

**Small-group and retinue norm**: "In this style of warfare, confrontation between small groups was the norm, and the qualities of individual valour and initiative were decisive." Large armies were exceptional and transient. (Ch. 12)

**Geographic and seasonal constraints**: Winter campaigning difficult and expensive (paying off forces); flat Po plain waterways created natural obstacles (Cortenova case); broken or wooded terrain favoured infantry and ambushes.

## Relation to Proprietorial Warfare and Strategy

This campaign style was a direct product of proprietorial social structure: land as wealth, limited government (no standing forces or logistics infrastructure), and the need for proprietors to defend/expand scattered holdings. It contrasts with annihilation-focused or battle-centric strategies of other periods/civilizations. ## Examples Grounded in Source

- Frederick II 1237 Lombardy: classic ravage, pick off strongpoints (Góito, Montichiari with trebuchet, Pontevico), deadlock at fortified camp, then successful ambush at Cortenova when enemy attempted winter withdrawal. (Ch. 12)
- General pattern in Reconquista Spain: "surprisingly few battles... siege and raiding being much more common." (Ch. 12)
- Anglo-Norman and Capetian–Angevin wars: repeated raids and sieges; rare set-piece battles when political stakes forced commitment (e.g., Lincoln 1141, Bouvines 1214 context).

## Limitations

The style was not universal or unchanging. Existential threats or ideological wars (some crusades, Italian city conflicts) could produce larger forces and more willingness to risk battle. Personality could override norms (Edward I's more considered approach in Wales/Scotland). No institutional mechanism existed to codify or transmit lessons systematically.
