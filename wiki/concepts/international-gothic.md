# International Gothic and Court Patronage

**International Gothic** is the art-historical name for the refined, courtly Gothic style that
spread across Europe around 1370–1430 — the culmination of the diversified **court and urban
patronage** of the fourteenth century. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Paul Binski, Ch. 8) treats
the period's art less as a single international style than as one of "extraordinary diversity,"
driven by new regimes of patronage.

## The Diversification of Late Gothic

Binski argues that the hallmark of later-medieval Gothic art is **diversity** rather than
uniformity: while the great courts continued "their signal role in commissioning major works,"
the role of patronage in "the newly powerful cities was growing inexorably." Where the thirteenth
century had a few dominant centres (Paris, London, Rome), the fourteenth saw "a plethora of urban
patronage" — Cologne, Prague, Siena, Bruges — producing "more vigorous art production and a wider
range of stylistic possibilities," alongside vital ecclesiastical and mendicant building.

## Avignon and the Italian Diaspora

A defining episode was the move of the papacy to **Avignon** (from Clement V, 1305), which "signalled
the triumph of French hegemony over the papacy and, in effect, the end of significant art patronage
in Rome for nearly two centuries." Avignon — its **Palais des Papes** decorated by Italian-trained
artists — drew talent from the rising Italian centres (**Siena, Florence, Naples**) that waxed at
Rome's expense. The "diaspora of talent" carried the **Italian painters** — Cavallini, **Giotto**,
**Simone Martini** (who died at Avignon, 1344), the **Lorenzetti** — across Europe, doing "so much
to create the new aesthetic outlook of fourteenth-century western European art." Their naturalism
and spatial illusion, fused with the elegant line of northern court Gothic, are the roots of the
International Gothic.

## Significance

International Gothic is the visual culture of the late-medieval **court** — princely, cosmopolitan,
luxurious, and increasingly secular (the chivalric and chivalric-romance imagery of the
[[chivalry|orders of knighthood]], illuminated *Books of Hours*, the *Très Riches Heures*). It
parallels the period's other "general themes": the rise of [[late-medieval-political-thought|the
princely state]] and the [[rise-of-vernacular-literatures|vernacular]]. Binski's stress on
**diversity and the shift of patronage to the cities** corrects any picture of a single declining
"medieval" style, presenting instead a competitive, mobile, and experimental European art world.

## The Fifteenth-Century Sequel: From International Gothic to the Renaissance (NCMH VII)

[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Paul Crossley, Architecture and Painting, Ch. 15) takes up the
story from the **International Style of c. 1400**, calling the fifteenth century one of "the most
profound changes in visual language in the history of western art" — from late-Gothic courtly
formality to "the accomplished naturalism of Leonardo and Michelangelo." Its two defining features:

- **Acute diversity and a north–south divide.** A Europe of fragmenting polities and vernaculars
  produced "an acute diversity of artistic styles," cut across by the "fundamental split" between the
  **neo-classical language of Quattrocento Italy** (Brunelleschi, Masaccio, Donatello) and a
  **vigorous Gothic** north of the Alps that resisted antique forms into the sixteenth century — even
  as Italian and Netherlandish painting (the oil-painting revolution of van Eyck and his successors)
  exchanged fruitfully.
- **Against Burckhardt and Huizinga.** Crossley rejects the old polarity of a "waning," morbid,
  courtly **northern** Middle Ages versus an optimistic, progressive **Italian** Renaissance,
  stressing instead continuity (Panofsky's medieval "renascences") and cross-Alpine exchange.
- **Art democratised.** Art "broke out from its old, elite setting in church and castle" into
  bourgeois houses (devotional diptychs and panels), city streets (fountains, crosses, statues) and
  the new mass medium of **graphic illustration and [[printing-revolution|print]]**, while pursuing a
  newly "analytical, scientific attitude to nature" (Vasari's "imitation of the truths of Nature").

This is the visual counterpart to the volume's intellectual transition — the rise of
[[renaissance-humanism|humanism]] — and, in **music**, to the era of the Franco-Flemish polyphonists
(Dunstable, Du Fay, Binchois, Ockeghem, Josquin) whose international style dominated fifteenth-century
Europe (Gareth Curtis, Music, Ch. 16).
