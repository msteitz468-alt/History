# Military Administration (Early Modern Standing Armies)

The bureaucratic and logistical apparatus developed in early modern Europe — most fully realized in Bourbon France under Michel Le Tellier and his son Louvois — to convert ad hoc, contractor-recruited, seasonally disbanded mercenary forces into permanent, state-controlled standing armies. Its core innovations included regularized recruitment (including standing militia and foreign regiments), standardized uniforms and equipment, a network of supply magazines and ration depots, the intendant system of civilian field administration accompanying armies, and regularized pay and regimental record-keeping.

This administrative infrastructure was the institutional precondition for the scale of Louis XIV's wars and for the disciplined linear tactics and combined-arms organization (including battalion guns) that characterized eighteenth-century European warfare, as analyzed at length by Hans Delbrück. Comparable administrative reforms — centralized taxation to fund permanent forces, standardized logistics, professional officer corps — subsequently spread to Prussia, Austria, and other European states competing in the emerging state system, marking the broader early modern shift from mercenary-contractor warfare to bureaucratically administered national armies.

## Significance

Early modern military administration represents the institutional bridge between medieval/Renaissance mercenary warfare and the modern nation-state's monopoly on organized violence, converting military capacity from a rented commodity into a permanent function of centralized state bureaucracy.
