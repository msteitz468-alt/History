# Kikongo Language Cluster

The **Kikongo Language Cluster (KLC)** is the group of closely related **Bantu languages** spoken primarily across the Lower Congo region — present-day DRC (Kongo-Central province), Angola, Republic of Congo, and Cabinda — that historically formed the linguistic substratum of the [[kingdom-of-kongo]] and neighboring polities (Loango, Kakongo, Ngoyo). The term was coined by Bostoen & de Schryver (2015) to replace earlier designations such as "Kikongo Dialect Continuum," which understated the degree of internal linguistic divergence.

## Definition and Scope

The KLC comprises forty languages out of ninety-five western Bantu languages examined by de Schryver et al. (2015), constituting a discrete clade within the **West-Coastal Bantu** branch (itself a subgroup of "West-Western Bantu" per Grollemund et al. 2015). Geographically, it stretches from southern Gabon to northern Angola including Cabinda and covers significant parts of southern Congo-Brazzaville and western Congo-Kinshasa.

The KLC is substantially larger than Guthrie's (1948, 1971) referential H.10 "Kikongo" group. It includes not only Guthrie's H.16 Kikongo varieties and the rest of H.10, but also his entire B.40 Shira-Punu and H.30 Yaka groups, plus Kihungan (H.42) and Kisamba (L.12a). Guthrie's H.20 Kimbundu, by contrast, belongs to an entirely distinct clade (South-West Bantu) and is not mutually intelligible with KLC varieties — contradicting assumptions in earlier Kongo historiography that treated Kikongo and Kimbundu as closely related.

### Subgroups

Based on the Bayesian phylogenetic classification of de Schryver et al. (2015) using ninety-two basic-vocabulary items, the KLC divides into five major subclades:

- **South Kikongo** — the heartland subclade, centered on Mbanza Kongo (capital) and surrounding provinces. Key varieties: Kisikongo (Mbanza Kongo), Kisolongo (Atlantic coast, both sides of the Congo delta), Kizombo (eastern, Bazombo traders). Further divides into central (Kisikongo), western (Kisolongo), and eastern (Kizombo and related) subunits; all three had diverged by the seventeenth century.
- **East Kikongo** — spoken east of the Inkisi River; prevailed in the northern provinces of Mbata, Mpangu, and Nsundi. Key variety: Kintandu.
- **Central Kikongo** — a **convergence zone**, not a true genealogical subclade. It arose through intensive language contact between South and East Kikongo varieties in the kingdom's northern provinces. Key variety: Kimanyanga. The zone has higher lexical similarity with South Kikongo than with other subclades, partly because it owes its cohesion to contact rather than common descent. Kindibu, superficially a Central Kikongo variety, genealogically belongs to South Kikongo based on shared phonological innovations.
- **North Kikongo** — spoken in northeastern borderlands and in the Congo-Brazzaville area. Includes Kibembe, Kihangala, Cilaadi (H.16f Laadi), and Bwende.
- **West Kikongo** — spoken in closely related polities to the northwest: Ngoyo, Kakongo, Loango. Includes Kiyombe (H.16c), Fiote/Kisolongo north of Congo, and the Shira-Punu group (B.40). Marginal to the Kongo kingdom proper; present only in its northwestern borderland near modern Boma and Matadi.
- **Kikongoid** — the most distantly related cluster within the KLC, includes Kiyaka (H.30) and related varieties.

### Internal Divergence: Not Dialects of One Language

Bostoen & de Schryver (ch. 3) emphasize that the variation within the KLC is too significant to treat its members as dialects of the same language. Basic-vocabulary similarity rates between varieties from distinct subclades often fall below the 86% threshold conventionally used in Bantu linguistics to distinguish separate related languages from dialects. Representative rates from de Schryver et al. (2015):

| Pair | Similarity |
|---|---|
| Kisikongo – Kiyombe | 70.6% |
| Kisikongo – Kintandu | 70.6% |
| Kisikongo – Cilaadi | 68.5% |
| Kisikongo – Kimanyanga | 79.3% |
| Kisikongo – Yipunu | 38.0% |
| Kisikongo – Kiyaka | 58.7% |

For comparison, Standard Dutch and Modern Standard German share 76.8%. Mutual intelligibility between Kikongo subclades is not natural and depends heavily on exposure. Laman (1936) and Guthrie (1948, 1971) overstated dialectal unity because both worked primarily from Central Kikongo perspectives; Laman's classifications were also shaped by early-twentieth-century colonial-era Kongo ethnic identity formation.

## Bayesian Phylogenetic Tree and Historical Varieties

Bostoen & de Schryver (ch. 3) produced a Bayesian consensus tree incorporating 107 western Bantu varieties (52 from the KLC) plus 21 **historical Kikongo varieties** from the seventeenth century onwards, treating all as if contemporary. This diachronic phylogeny places the three surviving seventeenth-century Kikongo sources (Cardoso 1624; Van Gheel 1652; Brusciotto 1659) firmly within the **central subunit of South Kikongo** — the variety spoken at Mbanza Kongo — rather than in the Soyo coastal variety (Kisolongo) as previous scholars had assumed.

The tree reveals that the eighteenth-century Kikongo from Kakongo (West Kikongo area) belongs to West Kikongo, not South Kikongo — confirming that distinct KLC subclades were already geographically distributed before the documentary record begins.

## Provincial Distribution within the Kongo Kingdom

Mapping the KLC subclades onto the six main Kongo provinces of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries (following Randles 1968):

| Province | Primary Kikongo Subclades |
|---|---|
| Mpemba (capital Mbanza Kongo) | South Kikongo |
| Soyo (coastal, Congo delta) | South Kikongo |
| Mbamba (southern border) | South Kikongo |
| Mbata (eastern) | South + East Kikongo |
| Mpangu (northern) | East + Central Kikongo |
| Nsundi (northernmost) | Central + East + (North) Kikongo |
| Ngoyo, Kakongo, Loango (neighboring kingdoms) | West Kikongo |

South Kikongo and East Kikongo were the principal language groups within the kingdom. West Kikongo was spoken in closely related neighboring polities but was marginal within the kingdom itself.

## Historical Significance: Linguistic Divergence Precedes the Kingdom

A traditional assumption in Kongo historiography held that the linguistic divergence within the KLC was caused — or at least strongly accelerated — by the collapse of the kingdom's centralized structure after the [[battle-of-mbwila-1665]] and through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries (Obenga 1970; Pinçon & Ngoïe-Ngalla 1990; Futi 2012). Bostoen & de Schryver (ch. 3) explicitly refute this view: the major subclades of the KLC were established **long before** the Kongo kingdom emerged. Phonological evidence shows that South Kikongo had already split into at least three distinct subunits (western, central, eastern) by the seventeenth century. The [[kongo-decentralization]] process may have deepened existing dialectal variation but did not trigger it.

The kingdom's political and economic integration did, however, stimulate intensive **language contact** across distinct Kikongo varieties — a process that produced the Central Kikongo convergence zone and left measurable lateral influence visible in present-day phonological irregularities (especially in northern Angola varieties). Sociopolitically, the Kongo court's South Kikongo variety had a strong lateral influence on more western and eastern South Kikongo varieties, blurring inherited augment patterns.

## The Monolingual State Assumption — Contested

Kongo historiography has commonly treated the kingdom as monolingual. Bostoen & de Schryver (ch. 3) identify two challenges to this view. First, de Cadornega's 1680 chronicle *Pauta das Nações do Gentio do Reino de Congo* explicitly describes the kingdom as composed of multiple nations and languages, distinguishing at least nine ethno-linguistic groups: Mexicongos (court nobility), Mexilongos (Soyo vassals), Amzicos, Monjollos, Majacas, Sundis, Sonsos, Mumlumbos, and Mulazas. Second, the phylogenetic evidence shows that inhabitants of the kingdom's northern provinces spoke varieties (East Kikongo, Central Kikongo) distinct enough from the court South Kikongo that they should be treated as separate languages, not dialects. The "monolingual kingdom" model appears to be a construct shaped partly by early-twentieth-century colonial ethnic politics (MacGaffey 2016) rather than a historical reality.

## The KLC and Kongo Origins

Historical Kongo traditions place the kingdom's founders in either Vungu (north of the Congo River, present-day Mayombe — West Kikongo area) or Kongo dia Nlaza (between Inkisi and Kwango rivers — East Kikongo area). Bostoen & de Schryver (ch. 3) note that if the founding dynasty did originate in these areas, it would have spoken West or East Kikongo before shifting to the South Kikongo of Mbanza Kongo. The only possible trace of such origins in South Kikongo would be loanwords like the royal title *ngangula* (of East Kikongo origin). More parsimoniously, the authors suggest, the origin traditions may be legendary, with the kingdom's actual origin situated at Mbanza Kongo itself.

## Relation to Ceramics and Material Culture

The KongoKing project demonstrated correlations between KLC dialect subgroups and the distribution of **ceramics decorated with woven motifs** (Cranshof, Nikis & de Maret 2018) — both appear to track prestige networks associated with Kongo elite culture, suggesting that language and material-culture spread were co-articulated processes tied to political integration rather than random diffusion.

## Historiography

The KLC's scholarly history reflects the broader problems of colonial-era African linguistics. Guthrie (1948, 1971) provided the first systematic classification but based his H.10–H.16 groupings heavily on Laman's (1936) early-twentieth-century dialect map, which was shaped by the then-forming Bakongo ethnic identity within the colonial framework. The result understated internal divergence and placed Kimbundu too close to Kikongo. Vansina (1974, 1995), Nsondé (1995), and Bonvini (1996) examined the historical Kikongo sources in the twentieth century but rarely applied systematic historical-comparative methods to them.

The systematic Bayesian phylogenetic approach began only with the KongoKing project (2012–2016): de Schryver et al. (2015) produced the first rigorous internal classification; Bostoen et al. (2013, 2015) provided the phonological reconstruction; Grollemund et al. (2015) situated the KLC within the broader West-Coastal Bantu phylogeny. The dissertation of Jasper De Kind (2012) applied diachronic phonology to Van Gheel's (1652) *Vocabularium Congense*; Eva Bleyenberg (2012) analyzed the Kakongo dictionary manuscripts. Full digitization of the *Vocabularium Congense* (Guy Ndouli; TLex software, Joffe & de Schryver) and other early sources was a prerequisite for corpus-based research.

The key unresolved question is the depth of divergence within South Kikongo — whether eastern South Kikongo is more closely related to East Kikongo than to central South Kikongo (as the phonological evidence for the *p > h* innovation suggests), or whether the diachronic lexical phylogeny (which clusters them together) should take precedence.
