# Archaic State

## Definition and Scope

The term **archaic state** was used in neo-evolutionist scholarship to refer to the earliest stratified, urbanized, politically centralized societies that emerged after the Neolithic in several world regions. It often carried connotations of a specific "type" or stage: large in scale, ruled by powerful (often divine or despotic) kings, with monopolized control over resources, law, and information.

## Yoffee's Critique

In *Myths of the Archaic State* (2005), Norman Yoffee treats "archaic state" as part of a set of **myths and factoids**. He argues the label misleadingly implies uniformity across early polities and overstates the degree of central despotic control, territorial integration, and simplicity of evolutionary pathways.

Instead, Yoffee stresses:
- Most early polities were **city-states** in clusters sharing a civilization, not large territorial empires.
- Power was multidimensional and limited; rulers competed with other power-holders (temples, kin groups, merchants, etc.).
- Significant agency and variation existed among non-elite roles (priests/priestesses, merchants, soldiers, slaves, women).
- "Evolution" involved simplification as well as complexification.
- Collapse was common and often involved restructuring rather than total dissolution.

## Key Characteristics (Traditional vs. Revised View)

Traditional neo-evolutionist image:
- Large territorial state
- Despotic ruler controlling economy and information
- Simple stage progression

Yoffee / revised view:
- Predominantly city-state systems within broader civilizations
- Fragmented and contested power
- Multiple trajectories and "evolution of simplicity"

## Historiography

The term gained currency in the 1950s–1970s as archaeologists and anthropologists sought cross-cultural regularities. Critiques from the 1980s onward (agency theory, heterarchy, post-processualism, and detailed regional studies) have eroded the monolithic image. Yoffee's work (both his CWH Vol. III contributions and this monograph) is a major synthesis of the revisionist position.
