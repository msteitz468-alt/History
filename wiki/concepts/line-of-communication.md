# Line of Communication (LOC)

The routes (land, river, sea, later rail/air) by which an army receives its supplies, reinforcements, and orders from its base or homeland, and along which it may send back prisoners, wounded, or captured materiel.

In van Creveld's framework, the length, capacity, security, and vulnerability of lines of communication are among the most fundamental constraints on strategy. Before any maneuver or battle can be contemplated, a commander must ensure that his army can be sustained at the end of its LOC.

Key points:
- **Umbilical cord metaphor**: Armies become tethered to their base; the farther they advance, the thinner and more fragile this cord becomes.
- In the age of magazines: short, multiple, protected LOCs.
- Napoleonic: often dispensed with or shortened by living off the country — until Russia 1812 showed the limits.
- Railway age: enormous capacity but extreme rigidity (single tracks, gauge changes, terminals).
- Motorized/mechanized age: trucks offer flexibility but introduce new problems (fuel consumption, spare parts, road destruction, "tyranny of the truck").

The concept explains why many apparently brilliant plans failed in practice and why "logistics is the art of the possible."

See also [[umbilical-cord-of-supply]], [[magazine-system]], [[van-creveld-supplying-war-1977|Supplying War (van Creveld)]].

Van Creveld's closing analysis emphasizes that even the most detailed pre-planned LOC provisions (as in Overlord) proved mismatched to reality; victories often resulted from disregarding rigid plans in favor of improvisation. "To recognize the truth of Napoleon’s dictum that, in war, the moral is to the physical as three to one; in the final account, this may be all that a study of the influence of logistics on mobile operations may be able to teach us." The LOC/umbilical remains the decisive limiter across eras.
