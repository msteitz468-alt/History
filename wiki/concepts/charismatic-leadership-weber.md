# Charismatic Leadership (Weber)

Max Weber's sociological typology distinguished three ideal-typical forms of legitimate authority: **traditional** (resting on the sanctity of long-established custom), **legal-rational** (resting on impersonal rules and offices, the basis of modern bureaucratic states), and **charismatic** (resting on the perceived extraordinary, quasi-supernatural or exemplary personal qualities of an individual leader, recognized and affirmed by followers, typically in conditions of crisis). Charismatic authority is, in Weber's analysis, inherently unstable — it depends entirely on continued follower recognition and the leader's personal presence, and cannot be institutionally guaranteed. For it to outlast the founding leader, it must undergo "routinization" (*Veralltäglichung*), transforming into traditional (hereditary) or legal-rational (bureaucratic office) forms of authority.

## Emic and Etic

Weber's typology is an etic (external, analytical) framework, not a category historical actors themselves used, though charismatic movements often generate their own emic language of extraordinary leadership (e.g., the Nazi *Führerprinzip*).

## Application and Limitations

The concept has been widely applied to 20th-century dictatorships, notably Nazi Germany, where Ian Kershaw's framework of "working towards the Führer" builds directly on Weberian charismatic authority to explain how Hitler's personal, non-bureaucratic style of rule generated radicalizing initiative from subordinates competing to interpret and anticipate his will. Critics note that charismatic authority as an ideal type risks over-individualizing structural and institutional factors, and that "charisma" itself is partly a retrospective effect of successful power rather than a pre-existing, independently measurable quality.
