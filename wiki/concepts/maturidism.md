## Definition and Scope

Maturidism is one of the two dominant schools of Sunni rationalist theology ([[kalam]]), the other being [[asharism]]. Founded intellectually by [[al-maturidi|Abu Mansur al-Maturidi]] (d. 944) of Samarkand, who built upon and gave systematic theological expression to the jurisprudential tradition of [[actors/al-farabi|Abu Hanifa]] (d. 767), Maturidism defined a middle position in Islamic theology between the thoroughgoing rationalism of the [[mutazilism|Mu'tazila]] and the strict traditionism of the Hanbalites. It came to dominate Turkey and, through the Ottoman Empire, large portions of the Islamic world.

The Maturidi school is closely associated with the Hanafi school of law. As Oliver Leaman notes in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], "Maturidi had provided a secure intellectual basis for the Hanafi school of jurisprudence, which made much space for reason and individual judgement."

## Core Doctrines

### Reason and Revelation in Balance

The defining epistemological principle of Maturidism is that knowledge can be acquired through three channels: sensory perception, transmitted reports, and — crucially — reason ('aql). The Maturidis hold that reason is itself a divine gift whose use God expects of human beings. Revelation and reason work in tandem rather than in hierarchy; where a Qur'anic passage is clear, it is accepted as it stands; where two passages appear to conflict, reason is deployed to reconcile them.

This contrasts with the Ash'arite position, which places stricter limits on the competence of unaided reason. The Maturidis argued that "even without religious instruction or revelation we can know that some things are just wrong" — a stance with important implications for the moral accountability of those who never receive Islamic teaching. The Ash'arites, by contrast, would assign such persons to a kind of limbo rather than hellfire, since they cannot be blamed for their actions absent revelation.

### Iman (Faith) and the Murji'i Heritage

Maturidism may broadly be considered a natural derivation from the [[kharijites|Murji'i]] position on the question of faith and sin. The key doctrine holds that *iman* (faith, belief) does not genuinely increase or decrease — unlike *taqwa* (piety), which fluctuates — and cannot be forfeited through sinful action, however grave. The determination of whether a sinner remains a true Muslim is left to God alone ("judgement is God's alone," Qur'an 6:57), a stance the Maturidis trace to Qur'an 9:106.

This "postponement" (*irja'*) of judgment has important political corollaries: it supports a quietist attitude toward impious or sinful rulers and permits a high degree of diversity within the religious community. As Leaman observes, "a regime is likely to tolerate more diversity if it leaves the decision as to precisely who is a believer and who is not to the Almighty." This explains the affinity between Maturidism and the Ottoman imperial project of managing confessional plurality.

### Qur'anic Exegesis

Al-Maturidi himself wrote a pioneering work of theological commentary on the Qur'an (*Ta'wilat al-Qur'an*). Maturidi exegesis holds that anthropomorphic passages cannot be taken literally — to do so would imply that God has a body, which is theologically inadmissible — but also resists the purely traditionalist response of accepting such passages *bi-la kayfa* (without asking how), which the Maturidis regard as intellectually unsatisfying. Reason must be engaged, though it is not sovereign.

## Relation to Ash'arism

Although Maturidism and Ash'arism are the two pillars of Sunni kalam orthodoxy and agree on fundamentals — divine unity, the prophethood of Muhammad, the authority of the Qur'an — they differ on several significant points:

| Issue | Maturidism | Ash'arism |
|---|---|---|
| Scope of unaided reason | Reason can establish basic moral truths without revelation | Reason alone is strictly limited; revelation is required |
| *Iman* and action | Faith does not increase or decrease; sin cannot negate it | Faith fluctuates; actions are relevant to its integrity |
| Moral accountability without revelation | Non-Muslims who act wrongly are culpable | Non-Muslims who lacked revelation are not culpable |
| Primary jurisprudential affiliation | Hanafi | Shafi'i / Maliki |

By the late tenth century, the Maturidi and Ash'ari schools together, along with the traditionists, formed the tripartite basis of what scholars call the "Sunni consensus" — a broad theological common ground that accepted rational investigation while rejecting the extreme positions of the Mu'tazila and the Hanbalites.

## Historical Trajectory

Al-Maturidi was a contemporary of [[al-ashari|al-Ash'ari]] (d. 936) and, like Ash'ari, sought to establish a middle position against Mu'tazilite dominance. Although he "often agreed with Ash'ari, he was by no means a slavish follower, and sought to establish something of a middle ground between the Mu'tazila and the Ash'arites."

The school spread primarily through the Hanafi legal tradition, which had extensive reach across Central Asia, the Iranian plateau, Anatolia, and eventually the Ottoman Empire. The Ash'ari–Maturidi synthesis in Sunni kalam was reinforced by major late-period theologians who drew on both traditions; notably, the Ash'ari thinker al-Taftazani (d. 1389/90) wrote a commentary on Najm al-Din al-Nasafi's *'Aqida*, itself a broadly Maturidi creed.

"Maturidism came to dominate Turkey, and through the Ottoman Empire much of the Islamic world." (Leaman, in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]])

## Historiography

Western scholarship has often presented Ash'arism as the dominant school of Sunni theology and treated Maturidism as a regional variant. Recent scholarship (e.g. Mustafa Ceric, *Roots of Synthetic Theology in Islam: A Study of the Theology of Abu Mansur al-Maturidi*, 1995) has worked to recover Maturidism as an independent intellectual tradition with its own internal logic. The collection coverage for Maturidi primary texts is thin; the best available treatment in the collection is the comparative analysis in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]].
