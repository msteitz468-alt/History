# Oblique Order

**Oblique order** (German *schräge Schlachtordnung*) is a tactical deployment in which an
army concentrates disproportionate strength on one flank, advances that flank aggressively
against a weaker point in the enemy line while deliberately holding the rest of the line
back (refused), and seeks to roll up the enemy's flank and rear before the enemy's stronger
sectors can react — allowing a numerically inferior or equal force to achieve local
superiority at the decisive point.

## Frederick the Great's Use

[[frederick-the-great|Frederick the Great]] made oblique order the signature method of the
Prussian army during the Seven Years' War, most famously at the battles of Rossbach and
Leuthen (1757), where disciplined maneuver and drill allowed his forces to execute the
difficult oblique approach march under fire more reliably than most contemporary armies.
The method demanded a level of tactical drill, unit cohesion, and command control that few
18th-century armies could match, and its reputation contributed significantly to
Frederick's standing as a master tactician — though later in his career (notably at
Kunersdorf and Torgau) its execution proved far less reliable against more experienced
opponents, and historians (e.g. Christopher Duffy) note its practical limits and evolution
over Frederick's career.
