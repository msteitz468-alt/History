# Catharism (Medieval Dualism)

## Definition

**Catharism** was the dualist heresy that flourished in western Europe — especially
**Languedoc, Catalonia, Lombardy, and Tuscany** — in the late twelfth and thirteenth
centuries. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Bernard Hamilton, Ch. 2b) describes a
movement that, though statistically small (a few thousand fully initiated *perfecti*,
implying tens of thousands of sympathizers), could not be disregarded because it had
**an excellent organisation and a coherent system of belief** — a genuine counter-church
with its own bishops, deaconries, and single-sex communities of *perfecti*.

## Belief and Structure

Cathars taught that the **Catholic Church had been founded by the powers of evil** and
that its sacraments could not confer salvation — a position that made compromise
impossible. Their cosmology was **dualist**: the material creation was the work of an
evil principle, the spiritual realm that of the good God. They divided into:

- **Absolute (radical) dualists** — two co-eternal principles (the Albanenses of
  Desenzano near Brescia); their theologians (John of Lugio's *Book of the Two
  Principles*) attempted a rational defence of dualism.
- **Moderate (mitigated) dualists** — one principle from which evil derived (the church
  of Concorezzo near Milan, the largest with ~1,500 *perfecti*).

The elite **perfecti** (the "perfected") renounced the material world — meat, sex,
property — and were the only full members; ordinary believers (*credentes*) received the
*consolamentum* (the sacrament of "liberation") typically on their deathbeds. Because
matter was evil, Cathars **attached sanctity to no place**, which (Hamilton stresses)
refutes the modern romantic myth of Montségur as a sacred Cathar shrine.

## Geographic Spread and Suppression

Catharism's roots lay in **Balkan (Bogomil) dualism** — Bosnia and Bulgaria — and
Hamilton documents that Innocent III viewed it as an **international threat** linking
the Balkans, Italy, and the Midi. Its destruction was the work of three instruments:

- The **[[albigensian-crusade-1209|Albigensian Crusade]]** (1209–1229), which broke the
  political power of the protecting southern lords but barely damaged the underground
  *perfecti*.
- The **[[medieval-inquisition|papal Inquisition]]** (from c. 1233), which over the
  thirteenth century tracked the dispersed Cathar hierarchy — the fall of **Montségur**
  (1244), the exile of bishops to Lombardy, the burning of the late-revival leader
  **Pierre Autier** (1310) and the last *perfectus* **Guillem Bélibaste** (1321).
- In Italy, suppression came later and unevenly, blocked while [[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|
  Frederick II]] lived; the Lake Garda refuge of **Sirmione** (a Lombard parallel to
  Montségur) fell in 1276, its *perfecti* burned at Verona (1278). Catharism had largely
  died out across western Europe by c. 1325.

## Other Dissent

Hamilton sets Catharism beside other thirteenth-century dissent: the **Waldensians**
(close to Catholic belief but subordinating sacraments to lay preaching; persecuted but
surviving to the present), the **rebaptisers**, **Speronists**, **Passagians**, the
**Guglielmites** of Milan (awaiting a woman pope), and "academic" heresies in the
universities — the millenarian **Amalricians** (burned 1210) and the **Averroists** of
Siger of Brabant, condemned at Paris (1270, 1277). He judges **Averroism** potentially
the most radical challenge of all — its claim that reason need not yield to revelation —
yet the least troublesome, because the medieval west "instinctively drew back" from it.

## Why Contested

The frontmatter flags Catharism as **contested** because recent scholarship disputes how
coherent and organized "Catharism" actually was — some revisionists argue that the
elaborate dualist "church" is partly a construct of its inquisitorial sources. Hamilton
(writing within the older consensus) treats the Cathar church as a real, structured
counter-institution; the Bosnian dimension (Fine vs. Šanjek) remains openly disputed.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan treats popular heresy as **lay-centered dissent** in the high Middle Ages and reports the persuasive view that the Cathars were the **most significant medieval heretics** by numbers and superior organization. Movement began in the **1140s**; survived Innocent’s response; death-blow came mainly by **inquisition**, not crusade. Naming: **Cathars** (*katharos*, “pure”) and **Albigensians** (from Albi). Possible **Bogomil** influence and dualist theology (material creation evil; good God of spirit). By late twelfth century communities at Cologne, Champagne, Liège, Bonn, Mainz ([[hildegard-of-bingen|Hildegard]] sent an anti-Cathar sermon), Languedoc from the 1160s (Lombers conference 1165 near Albi), Lombardy (Mark the Gravedigger at Concorezzo), and Italian centres (Desenzano, Vicenza, Verona, Mantua, Florence, Orvieto) — strongest in southern France and northern Italy.

**Organization:** elite *perfecti* (world-renouncing; only full Christians in their own terms) and *credentes*; bishops and hierarchical structure. Extreme vs moderate dualism among groups. **Scale:** not a mass movement — scores of communities, not majority conversion of Languedoc. **Ethic:** world-renunciation as the **only** route to salvation (unlike Catholic monasticism, even Bernard’s rhetoric); dualism also solved the problem of evil/material disaster. **Mutual antagonism** with [[waldensians|Waldensians]], who debated against them.

**Northern vs southern repression before 1198:** Cologne burnings 1144, trials 1163; Bernard’s 1145 preaching at Toulouse/Albi short-term success — northern influence faded. South different: Lombers 1165 failed because secular authorities unwilling; Toulouse trial 1177 with abject merchant recantation yet sons later Cathars — leaving Innocent a “serious, seething problem” in 1198. Innocent made heresy suppression top priority; Diego/[[dominic-de-guzman|Dominic]] barefoot preaching and debates. [[albigensian-crusade-1209|Crusade]] broke political protection but not underground *perfecti*. **Gregory IX (1233)** appointed Dominicans as inquisitors (Toulouse, Albi, Moissac, Cahors); highly active early 1240s; by decade’s end Catharism’s back broken; remnants into early fourteenth century (e.g. Montaillou). Logan notes inquisitorial procedure and mild bulk penances under figures such as **Bernard de Caux** (1245–46) — mass burnings were the exception. Albi Cathedral appears in his visual materials as landscape of post-Cathar recovery.
