# Syro-Palestinian (Biblical) Archaeology

**Syro-Palestinian archaeology** is the professional, secular study of the material remains
of the southern Levant — the discipline that succeeded, and self-consciously broke from,
the older **"biblical archaeology."** The distinction is the methodological foundation of
[[sources/dever-beyond-the-texts-2017|Dever's *Beyond the Texts* (2017)]] and frames the
whole modern debate over the history of [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel]] and
[[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]].

## From "biblical archaeology" to a secular discipline

Classic **biblical archaeology**, dominated by **W. F. Albright** and his student **G. Ernest
Wright** down to the 1960s, treated excavation largely as a means of *illustrating and
confirming* the Bible — Albright's "archaeological revolution" was expected to vindicate the
patriarchs, exodus, and conquest. From the **mid-1970s** Dever and others argued the field
had to become an **independent, secular, professional discipline** modelled on
anthropological archaeology (its practitioners working "always secular, as their counterparts
in" general archaeology), for which he revived the older geographic label **"Syro-Palestinian
archaeology"** — "now the preferred name." The point was not anti-religious but
epistemological: a discipline whose agenda is set by the Bible cannot serve as an independent
witness *to* the Bible.

By the time the **[[the-exodus|"revisionists" / minimalists]]** (Thompson, Davies, Lemche,
Whitelam) demanded a purely "secular history" of Palestine in the early 1990s, Dever's irony
was that *they* were latecomers to a secularization the field's archaeologists had already
accomplished — and that, as nonspecialists, they could not handle the now-"burgeoning"
material record competently.

## Method: text as secondary witness

Dever's governing methodological claims:

- **Archaeology is the primary source.** The material record "alone can serve as an external
  witness to critique, complement, and correct the biblical texts," and is "now our only
  source of genuinely new data." The archaeologist is redefined (after Ian Hodder's
  *Reading the Past*, 1986) as **"a historian of things"** — artifacts can be "read" like
  texts (con-textual, "the inside of events"), though Dever rejects the relativist excesses
  of [[post-processual-archaeology|post-processual / cognitive archaeology]].
- **The Bible is late, selective, and tendentious.** The Pentateuchal J/E sources and the
  Deuteronomistic History (Joshua–Kings) are dated to the late monarchy and reworked in the
  exilic/post-exilic period — "theocratic history... combining fact and fancy" produced by a
  tiny scribal elite "affiliated with the court and temple in Jerusalem" representing "even
  1 percent" of the population. It portrays an "ideal Israel," not "the real Israel of
  history." Hence: usable, but only "when... corroborated by external evidence."
- **The convergence / proven-to-disproven continuum.** Borrowing from **Lester Grabbe**,
  Dever ranks each claim on a continuum from "proven wrong" to "proven correct," privileging
  the **middle ground** of *convergences* between text and artifact (cf. his *What Did the
  Biblical Writers Know?*, 2001). He analogizes history-writing to **jurisprudence** — purported
  event, witnesses (texts), skeptics vs. synthesizers, the verdict "in the lack of positive
  evidence, possibly true" — and invokes Occam's razor and the "most parsimonious explanation."

## Emic vs. etic

The term "biblical archaeology" is **emic** to a confessional tradition that read the spade
as the Bible's handmaid; **"Syro-Palestinian archaeology"** is the **etic**, discipline-defining
label asserting independence from that tradition. Critics note the relabeling is itself
partisan — Dever wields it polemically against both literalists and minimalists — and that no
archaeology of this region is wholly free of the political and religious stakes mapped in
[[nationalism-and-archaeology|nationalism and archaeology]] and
[[socio-politics-of-archaeology|the socio-politics of archaeology]].

## Limitations and critiques

- Material culture under-determines **ethnicity, language, and religion** — the very things
  the biblical debate most wants to recover (see the
  [[controversies/emergence-of-early-israel|emergence-of-Israel]] marker debate).
- The "convergence" method can smuggle the biblical framework back in by choosing which
  claims to test.
- Dever's own centrism is a *position*, not a neutral standpoint: minimalists charge it still
  privileges the biblical narrative; maximalists charge it concedes too much.
