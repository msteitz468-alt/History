## Definition and Scope

The *mazālim* tribunals (sing. *maẓlama*; lit. "boards of grievances") were extra-judicial forums convened by caliphs, governors, and viziers — theoretically on behalf of the caliph — to adjudicate complaints that fell outside the normal Shariʿa court system or to address failures of Shariʿa court enforcement. They were sanctioned by the concept of *siyāsa sharʿiyya* (governance according to religious law), which gave the ruler discretionary authority to maintain equity and justice beyond the letter of *fiqh*.

The *mazālim* tribunals must be understood as a complement to — not a substitute for or encroachment upon — the Shariʿa court system. In Hallaq's words, "by all indications, the mazālim tribunals functioned less as an encroachment on the Shariʿa courts than as a supplement to their jurisdiction."

## Historical Emergence

References to *mazālim* are rare in the sources before the mid-second/eighth century. Hallaq places their appearance "after the middle of the second/eighth century, especially during the reign of the caliph al-Mahdī (158/775–169/785)." The earliest attested *mazālim* magistrate in the Egyptian sources appears in 211/826, when the governor ʿAbd Allāh b. Ṭāhir appointed ʿAttāf b. Ghazwān to the role after the sitting Shariʿa judge died and no replacement could be found. The appointment lasted less than a year before a new qāḍī was secured and ʿAttāf was dismissed.

This origin story is structurally significant: the *mazālim* functioned as a gap-filler rather than as an alternative track.

## Jurisdictional Scope

The *mazālim* tribunals operated within four main spheres:

1. **Prosecution of public service injustices**: oppressive tax collection, non-payment of salaries by government agencies
2. **Claims against government officials**: unlawful appropriation of private property, transgression of official duties
3. **Complaints against Shariʿa judges**: misconduct and corruption — notably, the *mazālim* did not hear *appeals* against Shariʿa decisions, which were final; they addressed the *conduct* of judges, not the correctness of their rulings
4. **Enforcement of Shariʿa court decisions**: where the qāḍī lacked the coercive power to execute a verdict, the *mazālim* tribunal could act

## Procedure and Penalties

The *mazālim* courts applied "a far less stringent procedure" than Shariʿa courts, "admitting, for instance, coercion and summary judgments." Their penalties exceeded the prescribed *ḥudūd* and *taʿzīr* of Shariʿa: they "applied penal sanctions in civil cases, or combined civil and criminal punishments in the same case." This procedural latitude was both their utility and their liability — it enabled quick resolution but departed from the rule-bound character of *fiqh*.

## Relationship to Shariʿa Courts

The Egyptian evidence from the third/ninth century reveals three patterns of interaction:

1. **Substitution, not addition**: In Egypt, *mazālim* tribunals were "instituted not in addition to, but instead of, the Shariʿa courts" during judicial vacancies. The reason was not state usurpation but the "absence of men qualified or willing to serve as Shariʿa judges."

2. **Staffing by Shariʿa judges**: Some *mazālim* magistrates were Shariʿa judges, and joint appointments (*mazālim*-cum-Shariʿa qāḍī) occurred. "Such appointments... were a common phenomenon throughout Islamic history."

3. **Shariʿa review of mazālim decisions**: When a Shariʿa judge succeeded a *mazālim* magistrate, he could and sometimes did reverse the latter's decisions. When Hārūn b. ʿAbd Allāh was appointed qāḍī of Egypt in 216/831, he "reviewed the decisions of the mazālim magistrate Muḥammad b. ʿAbbād and 'revoked many of them'" — probably in response to the wide procedural discretion the *mazālim* had employed.

Hallaq's conclusion is emphatic: "during the entire formative period and long thereafter, the standard and dominant law court was the qāḍī's Shariʿa court. The mazālim tribunals were both sporadic and ephemeral."

## Long-Term Trajectory

The *mazālim* institution became more formalized in later Islamic polities — especially under the Seljuqs, Mamluks, and Ottomans — where it developed into a more regular grievance mechanism. In the formative period, however, it was institutionally thin: "the precise nature of these tribunals is not clear, especially during the formative period. The sources say little about the qualifications of the judges who presided over them, and even less about the procedures and rules they applied."

The later theoretical literature (*siyāsa sharʿiyya* texts, such as Ibn Taymiyya's) would provide the *mazālim* with a more systematic justification; in the early period, the institution was primarily pragmatic.

## Historiography

Evidence for the *mazālim* in the formative period is thin and concentrated in the Egyptian *akhbār* tradition (al-Kindī). This creates a risk of over-generalizing from an Egyptian pattern. Hallaq notes the first Egyptian reference appears in 211/826; other provinces may have developed the institution differently or earlier. The theoretical literature on *mazālim* is substantially later (ninth to twelfth centuries CE) and may project back onto earlier practice a more coherent institutional form than existed at the time.
