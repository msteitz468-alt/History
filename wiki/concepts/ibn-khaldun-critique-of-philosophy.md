## Definition and Scope

Ibn Khaldun's critique of philosophy in Chapter VI of the [[ibn-khaldun-muqaddimah]] is the most sustained refutation of the *falasifa* (Islamic Aristotelians) written from within the Islamic historiographical tradition. Unlike al-Ghazzali's earlier *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (see [[tahafut-al-falasifa]]), which contested specific philosophical propositions on logical grounds, Ibn Khaldun's critique operates at the epistemological level: he argues that the intellect has structural limits that prevent it from reaching the conclusions philosophers claim to reach, particularly in metaphysics. The critique extends to the applied pseudo-sciences — astrology, alchemy, and letter magic — which he treats as corruptions of legitimate inquiry.

This page covers Ibn Khaldun's argument primarily from Sections 26 (metaphysics) and 30 (refutation of philosophy) of Chapter VI, supplemented by his discussion of speculative theology (Section 14), logic (Section 22), astrology (a subdivision of astronomy), and alchemy (Section 29).

## The Epistemological Argument: The Scale and the Mountain

Ibn Khaldun's central argument against the *falasifa* is not that they are unintelligent but that they have misunderstood the scope of the instrument they are using. The intellect is a "correct scale" (*mizan sahih*) — but a scale designed for weighing gold cannot weigh mountains. To demand that it do so is not a failure of the scale; it is a category error by the user.

He states this explicitly in the discussion of speculative theology:

> "The intellect should not be used to weigh such matters as the oneness of God, the other world, the truth of prophecy, the real character of the divine attributes, or anything else that lies beyond the level of the intellect. That would mean to desire the impossible. One might compare it with a man who sees a scale in which gold is being weighed, and wants to weigh mountains in it. The scale is correct and not lying, but it is just not able to weigh mountains."

This distinction — correct instrument, wrong use — is crucial. Ibn Khaldun does not condemn the intellect. He endorses logic as a tool, endorses the mathematical sciences entirely, endorses physics and medicine. His critique is narrowly targeted at what philosophers claim for *metaphysics* specifically.

## The Critique of the Falasifa in Detail

### Who the Falasifa Are

Ibn Khaldun defines philosophers (*falasifa*, plural of *faylasuf* — Greek *philosophos*, "lover of wisdom") as those who believe that "the essences and conditions of the whole of existence, both the part of it perceivable by the senses and that beyond sensual perception, as well as the reasons and causes of those essences and conditions, can be perceived by mental speculation and intellectual reasoning."

In Islam, the chief representatives are [[al-farabi]] (al-Farabi, d. ca. 950, active during the Hamdanid period) and [[ibn-sina]] (Avicenna, d. 1037, active during the Buyid period in Isfahan). He also names Averroes (Ibn Rushd) and Avempace (Ibn Bajja) in al-Andalus. He regards Aristotle as the ultimate source and calls him "the First Teacher."

### The Entry of Greek Philosophy into Islam

Ibn Khaldun situates the *falasifa* tradition historically within the Translation Movement. The Abbasid caliph al-Mansur "requested books on mathematics" and received Euclid's *Elements*. Then al-Ma'mun organized a full translation program — Aristotle and others were translated from Greek and Syriac into Arabic, "and many Muslims investigated them critically." See [[the-translation-movement]].

The result, in Ibn Khaldun's view, was that "scholars whom God led astray adopted their doctrines and defended them in disputations."

### The Three-Part Refutation of Philosophy's Claims

**1. The problem of universals and individual substances.**
All judgments of the mind are *general* (universal), while existentia in the outside world are *individual* in their substances. The gap between the two cannot be bridged by logical argument alone: "Perhaps, there is something in those substances that prevents conformity between the universal judgments of the mind and the individual substances of the outside world." Sensual perception can be verified against observable reality, but it is observation that does the verifying, not the logical argument. Logic is not self-certifying.

**2. The impossibility of logical access to the spiritualia.**
Metaphysics claims to study "existence as such," including the existentia beyond sensual perception. But logic can only work by abstracting intelligibilia from what is perceived by the senses. "We cannot perceive the spiritual essences and abstract, further quiddities from them, because the senses constitute a veil between us and them. We have, thus, no logical arguments for them, and we have no way whatever of affirming their existence." Even Plato "said that no certainty can be achieved with regard to the Divine, and one can state about the Divine only what is most suitable and proper — that is, conjectures." Ibn Khaldun asks: if the goal is certainty and philosophy can only yield conjectures, what use are these sciences?

**3. The false claim that philosophical perception yields happiness.**
The philosophers assert that "happiness consists in arriving at perception of all existing things... with the help of rational speculation and argumentation." Ibn Khaldun rejects this as "a fraudulent statement." The perception that yields genuine joy and fulfillment is not the discursive, brain-mediated perception of logical argument, but the direct spiritual perception of the soul without intermediary — the kind achieved by Sufi practice, "by the removal of the veil of sensual perception and by forgetting all corporeal perceptions." Philosophical study — working through Avicenna's *Shifa'* and *Isharat*, Averroes' abridgements of Aristotle — actually *adds to the obstacles* on the road to that genuine perception, because it exercises and strengthens the very brain-powers (imagination, thinking, memory) that must be stilled for direct spiritual perception to occur.

### The Confusion of Theology and Philosophy

A related error, which Ibn Khaldun attributes specifically to the later tradition (al-Baydawi, Fakhr al-Din Ibn al-Khatib, and those they influenced), is the merger of speculative theology (*kalam*) with philosophy. These are distinct enterprises:

- **Speculative theology**: "defends the articles of faith with the help of rational arguments" against heretics and innovators. Its starting point is *revelation*; reason is the defensive weapon. Its goal is not truth-discovery but faith-defense.
- **Philosophy**: investigates "existence as it is" with the intellect as the primary tool, with no prior commitment to any article of faith.

Recent scholars have conflated the two, producing textbooks that "jumble together" theological and philosophical problems. Ibn Khaldun regards this as an error that misleads students into thinking that philosophical training is theologically necessary or that theological conclusions can be derived philosophically.

This stands in contrast to earlier theology's sharper boundaries. The Ash'arite tradition (see [[asharism]]) and the Mu'tazilite tradition (see [[mutazilism]]) had each used rational tools in service of theological commitments — a practice Ibn Khaldun accepts within limits. The [[kalam]] page covers these traditions.

### What the Critique Does Not Condemn

Ibn Khaldun's critique is targeted and bounded:
- **Logic**: He endorses it as a tool for distinguishing correct from incorrect reasoning, provided it remains a *tool* and not a metaphysical commitment.
- **Mathematical sciences**: He endorses arithmetic, geometry, astronomy (pure), and music unconditionally. They produce genuine knowledge and "enlighten the intellect."
- **Physics and medicine**: He endorses these as legitimate natural sciences. Avicenna's *Shifa'* is, on these topics, valuable.
- **Sufism**: He endorses the Sufi path of direct spiritual experience as genuinely reaching what philosophy falsely claims to reach through argument.

## Astrology: A Science That Corrupts Faith

Ibn Khaldun discusses astrology in connection with astronomy. Astronomy (the mathematical study of celestial motions) is legitimate. Astrology (the inference of earthly fates and characters from stellar configurations) is not — for two reasons:

1. **Epistemological**: The inference from celestial configuration to earthly event is not demonstrated. The influence of heavenly bodies on earthly things is accepted by Ibn Khaldun in principle (celestial heat causes life; lunar cycles affect tides), but "the astrologer claims to know things that no one but God can know." The inferential chain is not established.

2. **Religious**: "The belief in astral influences, referring as it does to beings other than God, corrupts the Muslim faith." Directing attention to the stars as causes of earthly events is a form of *shirk* (associating partners with God), which the Qur'an forbids.

Ibn Khaldun notes that astrology has never been seriously studied by the major jurists — it is legally forbidden. He argues further that astrological predictions, even when they seem to come true, are self-fulfilling: those who believe a prediction may act to bring it about, or may interpret subsequent events selectively. The track record of astrological prediction is poor.

## Alchemy: Pseudo-Science as Crypto-Sorcery

Alchemy claims to be a natural craft: the discovery of the "elixir" that transforms base metals into gold. Ibn Khaldun's verdict is that "those who assume that the achievements of alchemy are the result of a natural craft are wrong." The truth is that alchemy, if it works at all, "is one of the ways in which the spiritual souls exercise an influence and are active in the world of nature. It may belong among the miraculous acts of divine grace, if the souls are good. Or it may be a kind of sorcery, if the souls are bad and wicked."

The chief alchemists (Jabir ibn Hayyan, Maslamah al-Majriti, al-Tughra'i) deliberately expressed themselves in "puzzles and riddles" (*ta'miya*) — not, as is commonly thought, to protect their secrets from the uninitiated, but because they knew alchemy was a form of sorcery and wanted to protect it from the religious condemnation of sorcery. Maslamah's naming his book on alchemy *Rutbat al-hakim* and his book on sorcery *Ghayat al-hakim* confirms this: the *ghaya* (final goal) is sorcery; alchemy is a restricted subset of it.

Ibn Khaldun accepts that sorcery is real — it is confirmed by the Qur'an (the sorcerers of Pharaoh; Harut and Marut). But it is forbidden by religious law, because it involves "directing the psychic powers toward the upper worlds" — specifically toward spirits other than God — in order to obtain earthly effects. Alchemy partakes in this forbidden structure.

## Sorcery and Talismans: Directing Worship to Other than God

Ibn Khaldun addresses sorcery (*sihr*) and talismans (*tilsam*) directly. His classification:

- **Sorcery**: Acts purely from the soul of the sorcerer without material intermediary; the sorcerer, by concentrating his psychic energy, can affect the external world. This is the "downward path" (*istikhdam*) — using higher spiritual forces for worldly effects — and is forbidden.
- **Talismans**: Use celestial configurations at favorable moments to attract specific influences. Maslamah al-Majriti's *Ghayat al-hakim* (the *Picatrix* in Latin translation) is the master text. Talismans also involve directing attention to non-divine powers, hence forbidden.
- **Letter magic (*simiya*)**: Using the powers of letters to affect the soul of a person. Conditionally allowable if used merely to produce psychological effects.
- **The Za'irajah**: A complex combinatorial oracle using celestial positions, the poles of a horoscope, and letter-number permutations to generate answers to questions. Ibn Khaldun presents it in intricate detail while classifying its results as a form of "witty game" — the answers correspond to ideas in the question, not to any supernatural revelation.

## Relationship to Other Ibn Khaldun Concepts

This critique forms part of Ibn Khaldun's broader civilizational theory. The [[ibn-khaldun-on-the-sciences]] page covers his classification of the sciences and their social embeddedness. The present critique answers the question: given that the intellectual sciences exist in civilization, why should they be viewed with caution?

The answer connects to Ibn Khaldun's three-part theory of the intellect:
- **Discerning intellect** (*'aql tamyizi*): distinguishes beneficial from harmful actions — practical, common to most adults
- **Experimental intellect** (*'aql tajribi*): derives from repeated experience the ability to manage social and political affairs
- **Speculative intellect** (*'aql nazari*): abstracts from the sensibilia to reach theoretical perceptions

The *falasifa* claim to operate with the third kind. Ibn Khaldun concedes this within its proper domain (logic, mathematics, physics). His critique is that they extend it into a domain — the divine, the angelic, the supra-sensory — where it cannot go.

## Historiography

Ibn Khaldun's critique belongs to a tradition of Islamic anti-philosophical polemic that includes [[tahafut-al-falasifa]] (al-Ghazzali, d. 1111) and the broader Ash'arite suspicion of falsafa (see [[asharism]]). His distinctive move is to situate the critique epistemologically rather than theologically: he does not argue that philosophy reaches false conclusions about God (al-Ghazzali's strategy) but that philosophy cannot in principle reach any conclusions about God at all, because the intellect lacks the right kind of access to the divine world.

Post-Avicennan philosophy (see [[post-avicennan-philosophy]]) had attempted to reconcile Avicennan philosophy with Islamic theology. Ibn Khaldun's critique implies that this project was misconceived from the outset.

The modern secondary literature generally reads Ibn Khaldun's anti-philosophical stance as strategic: he wanted to protect the autonomy of his historical sociology from theological constraint, and attacking philosophy was one way to do this. Whether the critique reflects genuine epistemological conviction or is partly polemical remains debated.
