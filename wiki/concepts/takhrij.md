# Takhrij (Doctrinal Derivation and Attribution)

Source: [[hallaq-origins-evolution-islamic-law-2005]], chapter 7. Part of [[madhhab-formation]] and the broader account in [[formative-period-islamic-law]].

## Definition

*Takhrij* (pl. *takhrijat*) is the juristic activity of deriving a legal ruling for a case not directly addressed by the founding eponym, by extending or applying the eponym's established principles and methodology. The jurist who engaged in *takhrij* was called a *mukharrij* (pl. *mukharrijun*).

The activity operated in two modes:
1. **Direct takhrij**: deriving a ruling by applying the eponym's explicit methodology to a case the eponym did not address.
2. **Indirect takhrij**: in the absence of the eponym's opinion or a direct analogical path from it, deriving a ruling from the revealed texts according to the eponym's general principles.

In both modes, the resulting ruling was attributed to the school and ultimately, in practice, to the eponym himself — even when it was in fact the independent contribution of a later jurist.

## Takhrij and Authority Construction

Takhrij was the engine of **authority construction** in the [[madhhab-formation|formative period of the doctrinal schools]]. It allowed the schools to:

1. **Expand the eponym's doctrine** without acknowledging the expansion as innovation. A later jurist's contribution — however original — could be presented as what the eponym would have concluded had he addressed the case.

2. **Absorb external doctrinal material** while maintaining school identity. Hallaq's research reveals that early Shafi'i jurists routinely drew on Hanafi doctrine in their *takhrij*, with the resulting opinions credited to Shafi'i's methodology. Similarly, Hanbali *takhrij* frequently drew on Hanafi, Maliki, and Shafi'i materials.

The Shafi'i jurist Ibn al-Qass (d. 336/947) reports dozens of cases where Ibn Surayj (the school's "Khallal") conducted *takhrij* based simultaneously on Shafi'i's and Abu Hanifa's doctrines, with both contradictory outcomes credited to "Shafi'i's way." This practice was acknowledged by the later Shafi'i jurist Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi (d. 476/1083), who devoted a chapter of his *Sharh al-Luma'* to whether it was permissible to attribute a *mukharrij*'s ruling to the eponym — with the school divided between those who permitted and those who denied this attribution.

## The Silence of the Legal Literature

"Generally speaking, *takhrij*, as a process through which later opinions were attributed to the so-called founding imams, was not recognized either in practice or in theory. The legal literature is by and large silent on this feature of constructing doctrine (which may explain modern scholarship's near-total neglect of this important phenomenon)" (Hallaq).

The silence is itself a historical datum. The majority of jurists who attributed *takhrij* rulings to the founding eponym apparently found this attribution "permissible" — the analogy offered is to the standard practice of attributing *qiyas* results to God and the Prophet, since *qiyas* merely extends the divine command by human reasoning. On this view, a ruling derived by *takhrij* from an eponym's principles was effectively the eponym's ruling.

## Takhrij and the Mukharrijun

The generation of jurists who practised *takhrij* most intensively — roughly the 3rd–4th/9th–10th centuries — were known collectively as the *mukharrijun*. Their juristic competence was of the highest rank, but their role was framed as elaboration within the school's tradition rather than independent ijtihad. They were the crucial generation between the personal schools (where individual doctrines were the only content) and the fully formed doctrinal schools (where the cumulative collective tradition was authoritative).

Each school had its equivalent of the Hanbali al-Khallal (d. 311/923) and the Shafi'i Ibn Surayj (d. 306/918): a pivotal jurist who gathered, synthesized, and attributed the school's emerging collective doctrine to the founding eponym. Without these figures, the personal school could not become a doctrinal school.

## Significance for Madhhab Formation

Takhrij is the mechanism that explains the apparent paradox of doctrinal school formation: founders who were historically modest jurists (especially Ibn Hanbal) became the attributed sources of vast doctrinal systems. The solution is not falsification but a systematic and partly acknowledged practice of backward attribution that served the purpose of anchoring an expanding collective doctrine in a single authoritative source.
