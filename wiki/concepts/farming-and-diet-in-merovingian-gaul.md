# Farming and Diet in Merovingian Gaul

**What the post-Roman countryside grew, raised, and ate — recovered from seeds and bones.** Two chapters
of the Oxford Handbook — Paolo Squatriti on "good and bad plants" (Ch. 32) and Jean-Hervé Yvinec & Maude
Barme on livestock and diet (Ch. 33) — reconstruct the Merovingian agrarian economy of northern Gaul from
archaeobotany and archaeozoology, revealing an agricultural revolution (rye) and a striking collapse in
animal size, both consequences of [[merovingian-rural-settlement|ruralization]].

## The Rye Revolution (Archaeobotany)

"The single most dramatic change in Merovingian plant economies was the rapid expansion of rye (*Secale
cereale*)" in northern Gaul, accelerating from the fourth–fifth into the seventh–eighth centuries and
replacing barley and the "dressed" (hulled) wheats — emmer, einkorn, spelt — on many sites. Rye suited the
new small farms: winter-hardy, tolerant of acid/sandy soils, requiring less pit-storage infrastructure and
no specialized threshing. Its weed flora is diagnostic — **corncockle** (*Agrostemma githago*) and
**cornflower** (*Centaurea cyanus*). Swiss Jura samples contain **ergot** (*Claviceps purpurea*),
implicating contaminated rye in early medieval convulsive disease ("St. Anthony's Fire").

Squatriti's analytical point is a **disconnect between literary and archaeological botany**: texts dwell on
symbolically charged "bad plants" — above all **darnel** (*Lolium temulentum*), the *zizania* (tares) of
Matthew 13 — which are nearly absent archaeologically, while the actual rye revolution goes unmentioned.
"Both sources are required but neither is sufficient alone."

## Livestock, Size Collapse, and Diet (Archaeozoology)

Against the climatic backdrop of the **Late Antique Little Ice Age** (c. 536–600, volcanic in origin) and
the genetically-confirmed *Yersinia pestis* plague, the faunal record shows dramatic change:

- **Animal size collapse.** From the Merovingian period there was "an abrupt and geographically widespread
  reduction in the body size" of cattle, pigs, sheep, and goats — attributed to lost zootechnical
  knowledge, family-based production, and reduced long-distance exchange (genetic impoverishment); size
  recovered only c. the sixteenth century. **Horses were the exception**, kept large by elite breeding for
  ceremonial and martial value.
- **The donkey's arrival.** Absent in Gallo-Roman northern Gaul, donkeys appear in the seventh–eighth
  centuries (~12% of sites), exploding to 75% by the end of the Carolingian period — suited to small-scale
  exploitation.
- **Long-distance livestock.** Polycerate (many-horned) sheep in the Seine basin and a possible
  zebu-hybrid bovine at the port of **Quentovic** point to specialized breeds traded across the Channel and
  North Sea (see [[merovingian-emporia]]).
- **Herd composition.** Sheep/goats collapsed from ~20% (Gallo-Roman) to under 7% (Merovingian), recovering
  to 22%+ in the Carolingian period (possibly with wool intensification); cattle came to dominate (near
  two-thirds of remains); pigs briefly predominated in the fifth century.

Diet was socially stratified: "consumers" (urban, elite) favoured **pork and poultry**, "producers" (rural
peasants) ate mostly **beef**; horse meat marks modest rural sites. **Game** was an elite marker — five
times more common on seigneurial than peasant sites — with stag the most abundant and **boar** the
highest-status (and almost absent from ecclesiastical tables "because of its… associations with the secular
aristocratic pastime of hunting," where hare was preferred). **Sturgeon** recurs at elite and monastic
sites (e.g. the Wandignies-Hamage nunnery).

## Plague, Rats, and Cats

Ruralization had a bio-sanitary dimension: the **black rat** declined with urban abandonment and the loss
of stone cellars/latrines, perhaps creating "a sanitary barrier" against plague in the later early Middle
Ages; meanwhile **domestic cats** expanded, present at half of rural sites by the end of the period. The
disappearance of Roman **mass-butchery** techniques followed directly "from the ruralization of society
and the decline of urban centers" (see [[merovingian-cities]]).
