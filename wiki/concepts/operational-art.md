# Operational Art

Operational art is the practical work performed by military staffs to support and sustain tactical action in the pursuit of strategy. It is expressed through a set of disciplines (commonly known as warfighting functions) rather than as a distinct “level” of war.

## Core Argument (per Friedman)

Friedman draws a sharp distinction:
- The **operational level of war** — a relatively modern doctrinal invention (often traced to Soviet theory but adopted widely in the West) that treats “operations” as an intermediate level between tactics and strategy. Friedman argues this construct is problematic: it artificially severs the direct relationship between tactics and strategy, creates dysfunctional staff practices, and distorts civil-military relations by inserting an extra layer of command and planning.
- **Operational art** — the useful, descriptive term for what staffs actually do. It is not a level but a set of enabling activities performed by competent general staffs (a 19th-century innovation) to make tactics effective at the strategic level.

The success often credited to the “discovery” of an operational level is more accurately attributed to the development of professional general staffs capable of handling the increased complexity and scale of warfare since the Napoleonic Wars.

## The Disciplines of Operational Art

Friedman organizes operational art around six core disciplines (staff functions):
- Administration
- Information
- Operations
- Fire Support
- Logistics
- Command and Control

These are the concrete activities through which staffs link tactics to strategy. A modern staff system actualizes operational art when it performs these disciplines effectively.

## Campaign Taxonomy

The book offers a taxonomy for classifying campaigns (offensive vs. defensive; persisting vs. raiding; annihilation vs. cumulative) to help commanders and staffs choose appropriate approaches and understand interactions and hybrids. Campaigns are distinguished from battles; the former are the proper domain for operational art.

## Historical Development

- **Napoleonic Revolution**: Increased scale and complexity of warfare necessitated new staff methods.
- **German Operational Thought**: Rise of the General Staff system (Scharnhorst, Moltke, etc.) as the mechanism for coping with complexity. Emphasis on *Auftragstaktik*, mission command, and staff work.
- **Soviet Operational Thought**: Development of deep operations theory and explicit use of “operational art,” partly as a safe conceptual space under Lenin/Stalin.
- **American Operational Thought**: Adoption of the operational level in doctrine, with critiques regarding its effects on the tactics–strategy link.

## Case Studies

Illustrative applications:
- Austerlitz Campaign (1805)
- Königgrätz Campaign (1866)
- Atlantic Campaign (1914–18)
- Battle of Britain (1940)
- Operation Watchtower (Guadalcanal, 1942)

These demonstrate how staff work (or its absence/failure) in the disciplines affected outcomes.

## Relation to Other Concepts

- Subordinate to and enabling of strategy; directly supports tactics.
- Complements the tactical theory in Friedman’s *On Tactics* (2017): tactics are what commanders do with units; operational art is what staffs do to make those tactics possible and sustainable.
- Critiques the artificial separation introduced by treating “operations” as its own level (see also debates in van Creveld, Naveh, and others on the operational level).
- General staff system is the institutional home of operational art.

## Limitations and Contested Status

The “operational level” remains widely taught in Western doctrine and is defended by many as necessary for large-scale, complex operations. Friedman’s critique is explicit and Clausewitzian: anything that weakens the direct tactics–strategy relationship or encourages staff bloat at the expense of tactical proficiency is suspect.
