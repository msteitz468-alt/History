**Annihilation and exhaustion** is the binary framework devised by the military
historian [[delbruck-hans|Hans Delbrück]] for sorting all military
[[strategy|strategy]] into two basic forms. As Freedman puts it, Delbrück "argued
that all military strategy could be divided into two basic forms": *Niederwerfungsstrategie*,
the **strategy of annihilation**, and *Ermattungsstrategie*, the **strategy of
exhaustion** (sometimes translated as attrition).

## The two strategies

**Annihilation** is the single-pole strategy of the decisive battle. It "conform[ed]
to the majority view of the day" and "demanded a decisive battle to eliminate the
enemy's army." In Freedman's image, "with a strategy of annihilation there was just
one pole, the battle."

**Exhaustion** is the two-pole strategy. It "drew on Clausewitz's note of 1827 which
recognized the possibility for another type of war when the available military means
could not deliver a decisive battle." Here "there was another pole, involving a
variety of ways to achieve the political ends of war, including occupying territory,
destroying crops, and blockading." It balances the possibility of battle against
maneuver, blockade, and the wearing-down of the enemy when a decisive battle is
impractical or politically inappropriate.

Two refinements matter. First, exhaustion is not the strategy of the weak by
definition: "Delbrück did not intend to imply that the strongest was bound to be
attracted by annihilation whereas the weak were fated to do what they could through
exhaustion." Second, exhaustion is not bloodless maneuver — Delbrück "mocked the
idea of a 'pure maneuver strategy that allows war to be conducted without
bloodshed,'" since "there was always a possibility of battle." Freedman stresses
that Delbrück's exhaustion "was more operational than an anticipation of the later
concept of attritional war," which placed "more emphasis on how underlying economic,
industrial, and demographic factors would sustain warfare." The American Civil War
is Freedman's worked example: "In the end this was a war of exhaustion," won because
"the North could survive the losses better than the South."

## Emic vs. etic

The pairing is partly **emic** — Delbrück claimed to be recovering a distinction
latent in [[clausewitz-carl-von|Clausewitz]]'s own thought (the 1827 note) and in
the practice of historical commanders. His most provocative emic claim was
historical: "Delbrück argued that Frederick the Great had practiced
[[limited-war|limited war]] rather than decisive battle," and Freedman judges "the
history was on his side, in that Frederick had become wary of battle and careful in
his ambition." But the scheme is also **etic** — an analytical grid Delbrück
imposed to discipline strategic debate, mapping onto the modern poles of
[[limited-war|limited]] and [[total-war|total war]], with annihilation tending
toward the total-war endpoint and exhaustion toward limitation and bargaining.

## The controversy with the General Staff

The framework was contested from birth. "Delbrück's analysis led him into furious
arguments with the historians of the German general staff," who insisted that all
real strategy was annihilation and could not stomach the suggestion that Frederick
had done anything less. Freedman frames the deeper stakes through the run-up to
1914: the staff's "successors' determination to avoid exhaustion" — "they could not
bring themselves to prepare for an inevitable stalemate" — produced an all-or-nothing
commitment to the decisive offensive, with "neither the appetite nor the resources
for a prolonged war of exhaustion." When that gamble failed, Delbrück himself drew
the obvious inference: "If Germany could not annihilate it would have to exhaust the
enemy." The dichotomy thus doubled as a diagnosis of the General Staff's strategic
blind spot.

## Limitations and Critiques

Freedman registers the framework's chief weakness: the danger of forcing a clean
choice onto messy reality. There remained "a problem with the dichotomous
presentation of complex options" — namely the implication "that a fundamental choice
had to be made in advance about how to comport an army for a coming war, a tendency
that remained evident in strategic debate over the coming century." The two-pole
scheme can mislead by suggesting commanders pick one mode at the outset, when in
practice strategy oscillates between battle, maneuver, and attrition as circumstances
shift. A further limitation is interpretive ambiguity: Delbrück's exhaustion was
"more operational than" the later, economic concept of attrition, so the same label
covers both an operational style of wearing the enemy down and a structural contest
of industrial and demographic endurance — a slippage Delbrück himself felt, since he
"struggled to assess the relative economic impacts on the belligerents."
