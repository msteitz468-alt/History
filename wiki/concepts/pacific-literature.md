## Definition and Scope

Pacific literature designates the body of writing — poetry, fiction, autobiography, petitions, newspapers, and other textual forms — produced by Indigenous Pacific peoples across the region from the early contact era to the present. Te Punga Somerville's contribution to [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]] argues that it should be understood not as a minor literary subfield but as a capacious approach to all texts produced by Pacific people: "Ours is a sea of writing."

The discipline boundary problem: Pacific literary scholarship is often treated as peripheral. Orbell (1979) noted that "in the field of Pacific studies there are many anthropologists and a considerable number of linguists, but very few students of the literature." This marginalisation replicates the casting of the entire region "as a peripheral space by visitors, scholars, politics, and discourse for centuries." Yet the Crocombes observed in 1961 that "the problem is not a lack of texts but a lack of scholars and critical consideration of those texts."

## Foundational Conceptual Frame: Wendt's "Towards a New Oceania"

Albert Wendt's 1976 essay "Towards a New Oceania," written shortly after completing an MA in history and after his first novel and short fiction collection appeared, is the inescapable starting point. The essay is a "break-up letter with history," asserting that "only the imagination in free-flight can grasp its plumage, shape and pain." But Wendt's essay "does not simply repudiate or mock history as a project. Instead, it charts a much broader scope of what counts as history, and the role of creative writers in seeking to produce history in the region."

This essay is "eerily relevant over forty years later" — both because the institutional marginalisation it addresses persists, and because the creative-imaginative mode it defends remains distinct from what disciplinary history typically permits.

## Textual Plurality: Pacific Writing Is Not New

The dominant periodisation — Pacific literature begins in the 1960s–1970s — is demonstrably false, and correcting it is Te Punga Somerville's central contribution. Islander peoples have been producing written texts since literacy arrived in the region; the Crocombes' 1961 call to recognise the "at present largely untapped wealth of historical source material written by the Pacific Islanders" was still going unheeded four decades later.

Te Punga Somerville traces four nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Indigenous Pacific writers as exemplars:

**Mowhee (Māui, Bay of Islands, Māori, c. 1800–1816)**: Taken to Norfolk Island at age nine–ten, educated in New South Wales under Samuel Marsden, returned to the Bay of Islands in 1814, sailed to London 1815. Died December 1816. Asked by Rev. Basil Woodd to write a memoir, Mowhee was partway through the manuscript when he died; Woodd published posthumous versions in missionary journals. "It is a truth that Mowhee is the first published Māori writer." Woodd directly addressed a future Māori readership: "O Native of New Zealand! Whoever thou art that mayest hear or read this little Tract, remember that Mowhee, on his death-bed, remembered and prayed for thee." Te Punga Somerville's finding him in an air-conditioned Birmingham archive underscores both the archival dispersal of Pacific texts and their power to cross centuries.

**Ta'unga (Rarotonga, Cook Islands, 1810s–1890s)**: Prolific missionary writer who produced "unparalleled ethnographic and descriptive accounts of communities he lived with, especially in what we now call New Caledonia." Ron and Marjorie Crocombe located his scattered texts through "serendipitous mistake" and published *The Works of Ta'unga: Records of a Polynesian Traveler in the South Seas, 1833–1896* (1968). H.E. Maude's foreword: "Ta'unga was writing in the earliest, and I submit the finest, age of Polynesian literature … The Works of Ta'unga is not only an important primary source for the student of Pacific history or anthropology; it possesses the additional merit of being an absorbing literary treat."

**Epeli Rokowaqa (Tailevu, Fiji, late 19th century)**: Wrote at Navuloa Methodist Mission Institution; manuscript later published as *Ai Tukutuku Kei Viti* (1926), covering Fijian origins, traditional culture, social organisation, and land-people relationships. Retranslated and republished by Kolinio Rainima Meo in 2013.

**Ligeremaluoga (Kono/New Ireland, PNG, late 19th–early 20th century)**: Memoir translated by missionary Ella Collins and published as *The Erstwhile Savage* (Adelaide, 1932). Beier included an excerpt in his 1974 anthology *Niugini Lives* but dismissed its "literary merit," claiming it was "included here for historical rather than literary reasons." UPNG Press republished it in full in 2016 — "draws our attention to a shift in PNG's sense of its own intellectual history."

These four writers share: all were men; all were deeply connected to Christian missions; all travelled; all wrote in ways that cross the boundary between historical document, ethnographic source, and literary text.

## The South Pacific Literature Bureau (SPLB): A Missing Institution

An important example of how dominant periodisations erase earlier Pacific literary networks. Proposed by H.E. Maude and established under the South Pacific Commission (est. 1947), the SPLB published *Pacific Reading* magazine from Sydney from 1952 — "featuring essays on relevant topics, information about how to acquire reading materials, and a section in which the editor reviewed (quite frankly) a wide range of texts." Neither the SPLB nor *Pacific Reading* "is mentioned in any account of Pacific literary history." A Pacific Literature Bureau (est. 1968) followed in PNG. These institutions connected the region to "broader networks, especially during the mid-century moves towards anti-colonialism and independence" — Banivanua-Mar's point that "the mid-twentieth-century Pacific region was deeply, if not reciprocally, networked with other parts of the colonized world."

## What Literature Can Do That History Cannot

Pacific literary texts produce histories that disciplinary historiography either cannot or does not: "In literary texts, it is possible to link practical, observable, tangible things to spiritual, sacred, cultural influences. Ghosts, ancestors, land, whales: they're all there, active, agentic, and conscious parts of the story."

Key examples from the chapter's literary survey:

- **Chantal Spitz**, *Island of Shattered Dreams* (L'île des rêves écrasés, 1991; trans. 2007): Tahitian novel in which the protagonist "washes away this dirt by writing" — inverting the colonial trope of soap/cleanliness as whiteness. Writing as decolonisation of the mind, echoing Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) and Tracey Banivanua-Mar's *Decolonisation and the Pacific* (2016).
- **Epeli Hau'ofa**: *Kisses in the Nederends* (1987) and *Tales of the Tikongs* (1983) — satirical allegory and social critique impossible in non-fiction conventions.
- **Albert Wendt**, *The Mango's Kiss*: "conveys a turn-of-the-century Sāmoa that is cosmopolitan, complex, and thoughtfully engaged with the cultural, intellectual, economic, and educational opportunities that connect their place with the world."
- **Patricia Grace**, *Chappy*: Māori grandmother whose household weaves together Japanese soldiers, World War II, Aotearoa, and Hawaiʻi.
- **Vincent Eri**, *The Crocodile* (1970): Imagines into the perspective of an Indigenous man from early twentieth-century Papua New Guinea.
- **Grace Mera Molisa**: Poetry articulating "the many intersecting politics negotiated by ni-Vanuatu women in the 1980s."
- **Kathy Jetn̄il-Kijiner**: Poetry connecting "family stories, weapons testing, and anti-Micronesian prejudice in Hawaiʻi."
- **Tusiata Avia**, "Ifoga": Directly addresses the New Zealand prime minister, reframing the apology for colonial wrongs in the Samoan tradition of *ifoga* (seeking forgiveness).
- **Loa Niumeitolu**, "when we tell": Acknowledges English's arrival in Tonga but insists "we've got to have a Tongan way / of doing it."

## Vernice Wineera and the Oceanic Page

Wineera — New Zealand Māori poet, artist, and scholar, long resident in Hawaiʻi — published *Mahanga* (1979), the first collection of poetry by a Māori woman. Her poem "Heritage" ends: "I am taking my place / on this vast marae / that is the Pacific / we call home." Her 1979 poem "Walking on water" articulates a philosophy of Pacific writing: "The poet walks on water, knowing full well / the transparency of the membrane / that keeps him afloat / on an ocean of ideas." The poem ends: "Poetry is walking on water. / Pacific poets know this / more poignantly than all the world."

Crucially, the poem argues that "the worst we can suffer is to forget / the past events that shape us yet / and touch the soul." This is an argument about history made in the negative — "the stakes of forgetting" rather than a prescription to remember. Writing becomes a pathway to history, and to historical thought; seeking Indigenous-authored texts "can also be a nourishing end point of (literary) historical scholarship."

## Institutionalisation and Limits

Origin stories of contemporary Pacific literature cluster around three sites: University of Papua New Guinea (UPNG), University of the South Pacific (USP) at Suva, and Hawaiian/New Zealand universities. These sites are both real (writing communities formed there, resources to publish existed there) and ideologically limiting (they produce origin stories that marginalise writing from islands without major regional institutions).

Pacific literary studies still lacks discipline journals and a scholarly association. It is still "possible to publish critical work that assumes things started in the later twentieth century." Indigenous literary studies influences (Warrior, Womack, Daniel Heath Justice, Chadwick Allen, Lisa Brooks) have broadened the range of texts Pacific scholars engage — toward "all kinds of texts" and "the conscious assemblage of archives as literary archives connected to specific sites, islands, communities, or nations."

## Historiography

Te Punga Somerville situates this chapter in relation to three surveys of Pacific literary history: Subramani (1985), Keown (2007), and Najita (2006). The chapter deliberately does not reproduce this bibliographic work but instead poses methodological questions about why certain texts, institutions, and periods have been invisible to literary history. The chapter's own form (essayistic, interpolated with the author's personal encounters with texts in archives) models the kind of multi-disciplinary, affectively honest scholarship it advocates.
