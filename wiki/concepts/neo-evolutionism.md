# Neo-evolutionism

## Definition and Scope

**Neo-evolutionism** refers to the 20th-century attempt (primarily White, Steward, and later Sahlins & Service) to revive and refine 19th-century unilineal or multilinear evolutionary schemes for explaining the development of human societies from simple to complex forms. It posits stages (bands, tribes, chiefdoms, states) or "culture types" defined by qualitatively new levels of integration, often linked to energy capture (White) or cultural ecology (Steward).

Key features critiqued in later work:
- Typological ladders or step-models of progress.
- Assumption that early states were essentially uniform (large, despotic, territorial).
- Heavy reliance on ethnographic analogies for prehistoric societies.
- Focus on political centralization and top-down control.

## Yoffee's Critique (Myths of the Archaic State)

Norman Yoffee (2005) labels many neo-evolutionist claims about the "earliest states" ("archaic states") as **factoids** — things spoken of as though existing but not supported by evidence. Specific myths include:
- Earliest states were basically all the same.
- They were totalitarian regimes ruled by despots monopolizing goods, services, and information.
- They were large, territorially integrated polities.
- Typologies can measure societies on a ladder of progress.
- Prehistoric societies can be directly correlated with modern ethnographic cases.

Yoffee retains the term "evolution" for the real changes (stratification, urbanism, specialized roles, new ideologies) but rejects the simplifying frameworks. He emphasizes variation, limits on power, bottom-up agency, and multiple trajectories.

## New Rules of Social Evolutionary Theory (from Ch. 8-9)
The old neo-evolutionist game was played on the central assumption that modern “traditional” societies represent stages in the development of the earliest states. The term “rituality” is useful particularly because it is a neologism and owes nothing to neo-evolutionist theory.

Heterarchy simply refers to the existence of many hierarchies in the same society and thus aims to explore arenas of social co-operation and conflict.

Chaco rituality and the Mississippian chiefdoms must be understood on their own terms, not as variability among so-called chiefdoms and not as representatives of stages in the trajectory towards states.

The “embeddedness” of politics and major aspects of the economy in ritual cannot be explained in neo-evolutionist thought.

Although I have traced broad evolutionary schemes in this book, produced social-science-like generalizations and even one 'law,' ... I have tried to resist the reductionist and dehumanizing tendencies inherent in much social evolutionary research. There are many speculations in this book and considerable uncertainty about how societies and their developmental histories can be compared and contrasted, and my 'new rules of social evolutionary theory' are not meant as substitutes for creative thinking about how people understood their lives. I do not apologize for all this distressing openendedness.

## Historiography

19th c. foundations (Tylor, Morgan) carried assumptions of progress and sometimes racial hierarchy. Mid-20th c. revival sought "laws" of cultural evolution (White's energy, Steward's multilinear cultural ecology and "culture types"). Sahlins & Service (1960) tried to reconcile general and specific evolution. By late 20th/early 21st c., many archaeologists moved away from rigid stages toward agency, identity, power negotiations, and historical particularism while still studying long-term change.

Standing critiques include over-generalization, teleology, and the "myth" that early states were despotic palace economies (see also city-state literature).
