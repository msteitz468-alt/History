# Roman Provincial Administration (Augustus to Nero)

The structure of provincial government created by **[[augustus|Augustus']]** settlement of
**27 BCE**, which "gave to the imperial administration in the provinces a fundamental structure
which it was to retain for more than three centuries." Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]]
Ch. 10 (**Alan K. Bowman**). Builds on the late-Republican [[roman-provincial-system|provincial
system]].

## "Imperial" and "public" provinces — not a divided empire

In 27 BCE Augustus received a large ***provincia*** (Spain, Gaul, Syria, Cilicia, Cyprus,
Egypt) to govern through **legates appointed by himself for variable terms**, while the rest
remained **"public" provinces** governed by **proconsuls** chosen annually by lot. Bowman
insists this implies **no division of the empire into two halves or two systems**. The reality
was **flexible**: provinces could be grouped, transferred between proconsular and legatine or
equestrian control, or upgraded; **Egypt** (under an equestrian *praefectus* from 30 BCE) was
the prototype of equestrian governorship, the lesser imperial provinces evolving into the
**procuratorial** governorships standardized under Claudius. Augustus could justify acting in
all provinces by his *imperium proconsulare maius* (from 23 BCE), but at first the *imperium*
was conceptually **separable** from the territories (the *provincia* was renewed at intervals;
Egypt's tribute was reckoned the *aerarium's*; Tiberius censured legates for not reporting to
the Senate). In **practice**, business from *both* kinds of province "gravitated towards the
emperor as the most clearly identifiable and effective source of power."

## The emperor as the focus of power

Bowman's central theme: for provincials, client kings, and tribal chiefs alike, "the embodiment
of Roman power was inescapably and increasingly identifiable as the **emperor**" — and his role
was **active**, not a figurehead's. His **edicts and letters** (*epistulae*) to specific
communities, his **rescripts** answering embassies, and the *mandata* (instructions) given even
to senatorial proconsuls hardened into a growing **body of administrative law** valid beyond a
single reign. Communities took the initiative in consulting him (the Cyrene edicts); the
*patrimonium's* spreading interests reinforced his centrality. As volume grew, **provincial
officials multiplied**, and Claudius had to pronounce (53 CE) that the *res iudicatae* of his
**procurators** carried validity equal to his own.

## Structure: a tiny superstructure on local self-government

The administrative apparatus was strikingly **small** — perhaps **no more than 300 senatorial
and equestrian officials** for the whole empire. The governor (legate, proconsul, or
prefect/procurator) held the full administrative, military, and judicial *imperium*, with a
staff of legates, military tribunes, a **quaestor** (public provinces) or **procurator
provinciae** (imperial finance / the *patrimonium*), and an advisory *consilium* he could fill
with client kings, local magnates, or experts in local law. Subdivision was **patchy** —
Alpine *praefecturae* over groups of *civitates*, the Egyptian *epistrategiae* and nomes,
Thracian *strategiae*, Syrian/Judaean toparchies, and the new revenue-driven *pagi* — with
military organization "inseparably linked" to embryonic civil administration in frontier
provinces.

The system therefore rested on an **infrastructure of local self-government**: in the East the
inherited **poleis**; in the West, the creation of **civitates**, *coloniae*, and *municipia*,
the army playing "a vitally stimulating social, economic and technical role." Bowman's
governing generalization: the aim of Roman government was to **perpetuate or create civic
government resting on the primacy of the town and the supremacy within it of a wealthy
aristocracy** — *urbanization* as "the essence of social and political control." Local elites
were drawn in by **honores** and the burdens of **liturgies / munera** (including
**tax-collection**), a system "systematically linking privilege and obligation" that was the
root (not yet the rigid form) of the later compulsory liturgical order.

## Frontiers and "Romanization"

Roman control "did not end at provincial boundaries": neighbouring tribes supplied soldiers and
imperial-cult priests, hostages were educated at Rome, and tens of thousands were transplanted
across frontiers (Getae into Thrace; Transdanubians into Moesia) — emphasizing "the
insignificance, in important respects, of the frontier between 'Roman' and 'non-Roman'." The
provincial superstructure did not invalidate pre-existing institutions (local *nomoi*, the
Areopagus, the Sanhedrin) but **fostered and moulded** them, civic "freedom" always remaining
"at the disposal of the ruling power."

## Contested

The old reading of a sharp **"senatorial vs. imperial" division of the empire** is rejected by
Bowman as misleading. The degree of **proconsular independence** from the emperor is "all too
easily exaggerated." How far decisions were made by the emperor *in person* versus the palatine
bureaucracy — and how far government was active policy versus reactive response to petitions —
remains debated (cf. [[imperial-court]]).
