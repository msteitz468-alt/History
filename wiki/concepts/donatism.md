# Donatism

**Donatism** was the dominant Christian church of Roman North Africa for much of the fourth
century, a rigorist movement holding that the church must be a communion of the pure and that
sacraments conferred by unworthy (especially *traditor*) clergy are invalid. It takes its name
from **Donatus**, elected rival bishop of Carthage after c. 312. For **[[augustine-of-hippo|Augustine]]**
it was the great pastoral problem of his episcopate — Catholics were a harassed minority in his
own diocese of **[[hippo-regius|Hippo]]** — and the arena in which he developed his theology of
the church and his notorious justification of religious coercion.

## Origins and Character

The schism began in the aftermath of Diocletian's Great Persecution (303–305): some eighty
Numidian bishops declared invalid the ordination of Caecilian of Carthage on the grounds that a
consecrator had been a *traditor*, and elected a rival line. **[[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]**
backed Caecilian; the Numidian grievance hardened into a separate church, "confined to Africa but
firmly rooted." State force under Count Macarius (c. 347) — the bitterly remembered "Time of
Macarius" — and the tolerant reign of **[[julian|Julian the Apostate]]** (361–363) each in turn
entrenched it. By 395 over 300 bishops faced each other on both sides across Africa, Donatism the
established church of Numidia.

Donatist theology drew on Old Testament imagery of ritual purity — the church as Noah's ark
"well-tarred inside and out," keeping in the good water of baptism and out the "defiling waters
of the world" — and cast itself as "the Church of the righteous who are persecuted and do not
persecute." Its extreme wing, the **Circumcellions**, were roving ascetic militants (Brown likens
them to North African marabouts) who venerated the martyrs, carried clubs called "Israels," and
raided Catholic congregations; Catholic atrocity-propaganda amplified their sporadic violence.

## Augustine and Coercion

Augustine and Aurelius of Carthage went on the offensive from the 390s. Initially Augustine
opposed compulsion, fearing it would fill the church with *ficti* (feigned converts). But after
the imperial **Edict of Unity of 405** disbanded the Donatist church as an illegal body, he came
to accept and then theorize coercion as *disciplina* — "a teaching by inconveniences," justified
by the parable's *compelle intrare* ("compel them to come in") and by the argument that even the
Mosaic Law had been imposed by fear on the many. Brown calls him "the first theorist of the
Inquisition," while stressing his consistent opposition to the death penalty and his insistence
that instruction accompany constraint. The great Conference of Carthage (411) ruled against the
Donatists; coercion produced peaceful conformity in the towns but violent Circumcellion
resistance in the countryside — "doctrinaire persecution from above... resisted by mounting
violence from below."

## Contested Interpretation

W. H. C. Frend's influential *The Donatist Church* (1952) read the schism as an ethnic and
social cleavage between a Romanized Catholic elite and an indigenous Berber/peasant Donatist
underclass. Brown rejects this: he finds no significant class, racial, or educational gap between
Augustine and the Donatist bishops, and treats the division as fundamentally an intra-African
dispute over the nature of the church and the memory of persecution. Recovered Donatist voices —
the stenographic record of the 411 conference, newly identified Donatist sermons — now allow the
movement to be heard closer to its own terms.
