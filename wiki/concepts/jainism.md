# Jainism

## Overview

**Jainism** is one of the two great heterodox faiths (with **[[buddhism|Buddhism]]**) that
emerged from the urban ferment of South Asia's **[[vedic-age|Second Urbanization]]** in the
sixth–fifth centuries BCE. In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Kaul, Ch 18) it is, with
Buddhism, a defining expression of the South Asian **[[axial-age|Axial Age]]** — a movement
that, like Buddhism, rejected "the sanctity of the Veda and the ritual authority of the
brahmanas."

## Teaching

Jainism was shaped by **Mahavira**, the last and most famous **Tirthankara** ("ford-maker,"
teacher) of the tradition, a broad contemporary of the **[[buddha|Buddha]]** who belonged to
one of the Vrijji ruling families. CWH IV characterizes Jainism as "an even more austere and
ascetic philosophy" than Buddhism, emphasizing:

- **Ahimsa** (non-violence toward all living beings) — taken to its most rigorous extreme.
- **Celibacy** and **non-covetousness**.
- Severe asceticism and purification as the path to liberation.

Its **monastic orders** (*sangha*, *matha*) drew lay followings sustained by donations from a
cross-section of society, "especially, but not only," the affluent **merchant class**.

## Spread and Patronage

Royal patrons included the early Magadhan kings — **Bimbisara** and **Ajatashatru** are
associated with Jainism (as with Buddhism), and **Chandragupta Maurya** is believed to have
embraced the Jain faith at the end of his life (see [[chandragupta-maurya]],
[[pataliputra]]). The Deccan king **Amoghavarsha I** (Rashtrakuta, 9th c.) — "Ashoka of the
South" — was a notable later promoter. Over time Jainism came to be **concentrated in south
and west India**, while Buddhism spread far beyond the subcontinent.

## Significance

Jainism's radical **ahimsa** left a deep and lasting imprint on South Asian ethics and
culture. As a merchant-supported, anti-Vedic renunciant movement, it parallels Buddhism as
part of the same Axial-Age challenge to brahmanical ritual order — but, unlike Buddhism,
remained a primarily Indian tradition that survives in India to the present.

## Limitations

CWH IV treats Jainism more briefly than Buddhism, reflecting both its narrower geographic
spread and the collection's general thinness on South Asia; the early history of the
tradition, like Buddhism's, rests on later texts and is entangled with hagiography.
