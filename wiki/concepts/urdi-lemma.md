# ʿUrḍī's Lemma

## Definition

ʿUrḍī's Lemma is a geometric proposition introduced by the astronomer-engineer [[al-urdi|Muʾayyad al-Dīn al-ʿUrḍī]] (d. 1266) to resolve the equant problem in Ptolemy's model for the upper planets. The lemma states:

> Given any two equal lines that form equal angles with a base line, either internally or externally, the line joining the extremities of those two lines will be parallel to the base line.

In astronomical application: if two equal-length vectors — representing deferent radii — subtend equal angles with the apsidal line (either exterior angles as in Apollonius's theorem, or interior angles), the line connecting their tips is always parallel to the base line. This parallelism guarantees that a point carried at the extremity of the second vector traces an arc that appears, from the base point, to sweep at a uniform angular rate — even though the actual motion is generated by two physically realizable uniform circular rotations ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Context: The Equant Problem

Ptolemy's model for the upper planets required the deferent sphere — a physical sphere in the Aristotelian universe — to rotate uniformly *around the equant point E*, which lay off-center from the deferent's own center T, at a distance equal to the eccentricity OT. This made the deferent's physical rotation non-uniform around its own center, violating the Aristotelian and mathematical requirement that a sphere moving *in place* rotate uniformly around an axis through its own center. The result was, in Saliba's formulation, that "the existence of such a sphere [was] physically impossible" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

Earlier astronomers had identified this impossibility (Ibn al-Haytham in the *Shukūk*; Abū ʿUbayd al-Juzjānī had attempted reform and failed). Al-ʿUrḍī was the first to introduce new mathematics to resolve it.

## The Construction

Al-ʿUrḍī's solution for the upper planets (as described by Saliba, [[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]):

1. Shift the center of the deferent from T (the Ptolemaic deferent center) to a new point K, located halfway between T and the equant point D. Thus KT = KD = half the Ptolemaic eccentricity.
2. Allow this new deferent (centered at K) to rotate uniformly around K — physically permissible, since the axis now passes through the deferent's own center.
3. The new deferent carries a **small epicycle** whose radius equals TK = KD (i.e., half the eccentricity).
4. The small epicycle moves at the **same speed** as the new deferent, and in the **same direction**.
5. By applying ʿUrḍī's Lemma: the line ZD (joining the tip of the small epicycle's radius to the equant D) is always parallel to KN (joining K to the center of the small epicycle N). Consequently, the point Z — which carries the Ptolemaic epicycle — appears to move uniformly around D, satisfying the observational constraint Ptolemy's equant had been designed to capture, while all spheres now rotate around axes through their own centers.

Saliba's summary: "the center of the Ptolemaic epicycle, now carried at the extremity of ʿUrḍī's small epicycle, look like it was moving uniformly around the Ptolemaic equant. In fact it moved around the center of its own small epicycle, and the center of that epicycle, in turn, moved around the center of the new deferent. With all spheres now moving uniformly, in place, around axis that passed through their centers, ʿUrḍī managed to avoid the absurdity of the Ptolemaic equant altogether" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Relationship to Apollonius's Theorem

The lemma is structurally a **generalization** of Apollonius's theorem. Apollonius had proved that the eccentric and epicyclic models for the sun were mathematically equivalent using exterior equal angles. ʿUrḍī extended this by showing that *interior* equal angles produce the same result of parallelism. This extension was the key innovation: interior angles were precisely what the new Maragha construction required, whereas exterior angles had served only for the solar eccentric–epicycle equivalence.

Saliba writes that the lemma "looked like a generalization of Apollonius's theorem, in that the equal angles needed for the proof of the parallelism of the end line with the base line are no longer restricted to the exterior angles" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Scope of Application

Al-ʿUrḍī introduced the lemma specifically for the upper planets. He did not need it for the sun (which he judged adequately described by the simple eccentric), and he applied different geometric approaches — without requiring any new theorem — to reform the models for the moon and Mercury, adjusting directions and magnitudes of motions while still remaining within the mathematics available from Euclid's *Elements*.

After him, the lemma became a widely deployed tool:

- **Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī** (d. 1311): used it in his lunar model, bisecting the Ptolemaic deferent's eccentricity and adding a small epicycle moving at the same speed and direction.
- **[[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]]** (d. 1375): used it in more than one of his models; Saliba describes it as one of the two "principles" (*uṣūl*) — alongside the Apollonius equation — on which Ibn al-Shāṭir's unified system rests.
- **ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn al-Qushjī** (d. 1474): used it in his model for Mercury.
- **Shams al-Dīn al-Khafrī** (d. 1550): used it twice in his model for the upper planets.
- **Nicolaus Copernicus** (d. 1543): used it for the upper planets, in the same mathematical structure as al-ʿUrḍī's original application.

Saliba: "this relatively simple lemma proved to be an epoch maker, just like the Ṭūsī Couple, which will be mentioned later. And like the Ṭūsī Couple too, once it was discovered, it allowed several generations of astronomers to think differently about Ptolemaic astronomy" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Significance for the History of Science

ʿUrḍī's Lemma, alongside the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī couple]], constitutes one of the two fundamental new mathematical tools produced by the [[maragha-school|Maragha school]] that enabled a physically consistent alternative to Ptolemaic astronomy. Its appearance in Copernicus — for the same model (upper planets), using the same geometric mechanism — is a key element of Saliba's transmission argument: that Islamic mathematical astronomy directly contributed to the European Renaissance rather than merely preserving Greek texts.

The lemma is notable for being a piece of pure geometry whose motivation was entirely astronomical and physical: the requirement that spheres in the Aristotelian universe must rotate on axes through their own centers generated the mathematical need, and the mathematician-astronomer supplied the theorem to meet it.
