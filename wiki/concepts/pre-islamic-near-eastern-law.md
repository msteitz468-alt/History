## Definition and Scope

Pre-Islamic Near Eastern law denotes the body of legal practices, customary norms, commercial contracts, and penal rules that prevailed across the Near East — from Mesopotamia through the Levant, into the Hejaz and Yemen — in the centuries before and during the rise of Islam. It is not a single codified system but a regional legal continuum, rooted in Sumerian and Babylonian origins, that persisted in local variation through Assyrian, Achaemenid, Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, and Sasanid rule. Hallaq's key methodological claim is that this continuum was *indigenous* to the Near East: what appeared as "Roman" or "Byzantine" law in the region was often itself a thin veneer over far older Semitic legal practices.

The concept is historiographically important because it refutes the older thesis — associated with scholars such as Goldziher and partly with Schacht — that Islamic law was primarily derived from Byzantine Roman law through the medium of provincial practice. Hallaq argues that the Arabian Peninsula was already *part of* this Near Eastern legal culture before Islam, making the question of post-conquest "borrowing" misconceived.

## Core Content: Legal Institutions and Practices

**Property and commercial law.** The ancient Near Eastern contracts of sale — involving immediate delivery with deferred payment, or immediate payment for deferred delivery — were prevalent in the pre-Islamic Hejaz and "wholly incorporated (under *araya* and *salam*) into Islamic law" (Hallaq, ch. 1). The Qatabanian kingdom in Yemen possessed a sophisticated trade code including a Law Merchant applicable to foreign merchants as early as the first century BCE. Thamudite and Lihyanite inscriptions from northwest Arabia, predating Islam by several centuries, document property rights over both movable and immovable assets, penal cases, and pecuniary transactions.

**Criminal law and blood-money.** The principle that murder required either commensurate revenge (*qisas*) or payment of blood-money (*diya*) was "an ancient Near Eastern law that was as much present in the pre-Islamic Peninsula (as documented in the Quran) as in ancient Mesopotamia." The parallel to Mesopotamian law — documented by VerSteeg — is one of Hallaq's key pieces of evidence for continuity.

**Family and marriage law.** Meccan marriage practice — specifically the requirement that the bride's family return the dowry to the wife for her financial security — was "nearly identical to Mesopotamian law prevalent since Assyrian times." The Quran adopted this practice and extended it, but did not originate it.

**Compurgation (*qasama*).** The procedure whereby fifty members of a tribe or community swear an oath to exonerate themselves from liability when a murdered person is found in their territory, while nonetheless paying blood-money, is attested in pre-Islamic customary law and survived into mature Sharia. Jurists later justified its retention on the grounds that the Prophet had not repealed it.

**Contractual offer and acceptance.** Hallaq follows Schacht ("From Babylonian to Islamic Law," 1995) in identifying contractual offer-and-acceptance as another element of Babylonian law that survived into Islamic law.

**Arbitration (*hakam*)** . Pre-Islamic Arabian society possessed an institution of charismatic arbitrators — *hukkam* (sing. *hakam*) — men deemed to possess experience, wisdom, and (in pre-Islamic belief) supernatural powers. The first Islamic *qadis* were largely recruited from their ranks and inherited their socially authoritative but non-binding adjudicatory role.

## Geographical Reach and Pre-Islamic Arabia

Hallaq's Chapter 1 argues forcefully that the Arabian Peninsula was not culturally isolated from this continuum. Mecca and Medina lay within what he calls "the cultural continuum that had dominated the Near East since the time of the Sumerians." Commercial links with the Lakhmids of Hira (Sasanid vassals), the Ghassanids of Syria (Byzantine vassals), Yemen, Abyssinia, and Egypt placed Meccan merchants — including Muhammad himself — in continuous contact with Near Eastern legal culture. Pecuniary contracts that entered Islam via the Hejaz — bartering unripe dates against dried dates, sale and futures contracts — had "commonly been practiced in the Near East for centuries, probably as long ago as Babylonian and Assyrian times."

## The Beirut Law School and the Problem of "Roman" Law

Hallaq uses the case of the Beirut (Berytus) law school to argue that what appears Roman in the Byzantine Near East was itself deeply rooted in Semitic legal culture. Quoting Warwick Ball: the school drew on "legal traditions that stretched back to the Judaic traditions of the early first millennium and the Mesopotamian law codes of the early second millennium. Ultimately, therefore, should we be viewing Beirut in the context of Rome or of Babylon?" This argument underpins Hallaq's methodological principle that any claim of cultural transmission between civilizations must demonstrate genuine genealogical origin, not merely the existence of surface resemblance under a later political culture.

## Relationship to Islamic Law

Pre-Islamic Near Eastern law provided the *substrate* on which the Quranic legal revelation operated. Hallaq's model is neither one of radical rupture (Islamic law as something wholly new) nor of passive borrowing (Islamic law as transformed Roman or Byzantine law). Instead:
1. Pre-Islamic Arabian customary and commercial law was already a regional variant of ancient Near Eastern law.
2. The Quran selectively legislated *over* this substrate — prohibiting some practices, reforming others, and leaving the rest in place.
3. What the Quran left untouched — the majority of daily legal life — continued to operate under pre-existing Near Eastern rules, which were later retrospectively sanctioned by jurists as consistent with Prophetic Sunna.

This framework is central to understanding [[formative-period-islamic-law]] and the arguments rehearsed in [[islamic-civilization-historiography]].

## Historiography

The dominant twentieth-century view, most fully articulated by Joseph Schacht (*Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence*, 1950), held that Islamic law owed its technical character largely to the assimilation of provincial Roman and Byzantine legal practice during the conquests. Hallaq systematically challenges this view, arguing it rests on a failure to recognize that "Roman" practice in the Near East was itself substantially Semitic and pre-Roman in origin. His methodological requirement — the "genealogy test" for any transmission claim — has been influential but not universally accepted. The debate intersects with broader historiography on the relationship between Islam and Late Antiquity. See [[islamic-civilization-historiography]] for the wider controversy.
