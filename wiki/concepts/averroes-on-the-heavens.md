## Definition and Scope

Discussions 14–16 of Averroes' *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* constitute the closing metaphysical section of the work. They address three interconnected questions about the nature of the celestial realm:

1. **Discussion 14**: Is heaven a living being (*ḥayawān*) whose circular motion is voluntary and purposive?
2. **Discussion 15**: What is the *aim* (*gharaḍ*) that moves heaven — and is Avicenna's account of it coherent?
3. **Discussion 16**: Do the souls of the spheres observe all particular events in the sublunary world, and is this the correct interpretation of the "Indelible Tablet" (*al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ*)?

Together these discussions represent Averroes' most sustained engagement with Aristotelian cosmology in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*, and they intersect directly with [[averroes-on-gods-knowledge]] (since the mover of the celestial spheres and God's relation to the cosmos are continuous problems). The companion source is [[ibn-rushd-incoherence-of-incoherence]].

## Discussion 14 — Heaven as a Living Being

### The philosophers' argument

The classical philosophical argument for celestial animation runs as follows: heaven is in motion (observable); every moved body has a mover; that mover is either external (constraint), nature, or soul/will. Circular motion cannot be natural because a body moved by nature seeks a definite place and comes to rest — but heaven returns to every position it departs from, so no position can be its natural resting point. The motion cannot be constrained by another external body (there is no body outside heaven, and any such body would require an infinite regress of movers). Therefore the motion must be voluntary, arising from an internal principle — a soul:

> "This movement is the special act for the sake of which heaven is created … the body of the heavens is not created for the sake of this sublunary world. For indeed this movement is the special act for the sake of which heaven is created."

### Averroes' position

Averroes endorses the conclusion — heaven is animated — but criticises both the dialectical packaging Avicenna gave the argument and al-Ghazālī's three alternative hypotheses. He identifies as *self-evident or nearly so* that no external body moves heaven (no body outside heaven exists; any external mover would itself need a body to stand on, generating infinite regress). Al-Ghazālī's second hypothesis — that God moves heaven directly without creating a potency in it — he dismisses as theologically "reprehensible," equivalent to denying that things have natures at all:

> "Such a denial would amount to a denial of the intelligibles, for the intellect perceives things only through their causes."

Al-Ghazālī's third hypothesis — that heaven has a natural potency for circular motion analogous to the heaviness of earth — Averroes partially rehabilitates but ultimately rejects: the key difference is that natural potencies aim at a definite *place*, whereas heaven's circular motion aims at the *movement itself as movement*. This means the mover must be capable of *representing* movement as movement — which requires desire and intellect, not mere nature:

> "What is moved towards movement in so far as it is movement must of necessity desire this movement, and what desires movement must of necessity represent it."

Averroes also adduces a second argument for celestial intellect: some celestial bodies simultaneously exhibit two contrary motions (eastward and westward). A purely natural body cannot perform two opposite acts simultaneously; therefore something beyond nature — intellect and desire — must be the source.

### On the soul as equivocal predicate

Averroes notes that the term "soul" (*nafs*) is predicated only **equivocally** of the souls in celestial bodies and souls in sublunary animals. The nature of celestial perception — whether they sense, imagine, or purely intellectualize — is a separate and more difficult question than whether they are animated at all.

## Discussion 15 — The Aim That Moves Heaven

### The Avicennan account (as summarised by al-Ghazālī)

Al-Ghazālī presents a sophisticated version of the philosophers' theory of celestial teleology: the heavenly bodies aim, by their motion, to *assimilate themselves to God* — to achieve, insofar as a body can, the perfection of the First Principle. Since God's existence is the most perfect, and other existents are imperfect by degrees, the "nearness" the heavens seek is nearness in *qualities*, not in space. The souls of the celestial spheres, being beings in which some potentialities are still unactualized (namely their successive positions), perpetually seek to exhaust all possible positions through their eternal circular motion. As a secondary effect (*bi-l-qaṣd al-thānī*), this motion produces the diversity of celestial configurations — trines, sextiles, oppositions, conjunctions — from which the conditions of the sublunary world emanate.

### Averroes' critique of Avicenna's formulation

Averroes sharply corrects Avicenna's claim that heaven seeks, through its motion, to occupy an **infinite succession of particular positions**:

> "What is infinite cannot be sought, since it cannot be attained. Nobody has held this doctrine but Avicenna."

On Averroes' own reading, what heaven *aims* at is **the movement itself**, not the series of positions the movement passes through. This is the appropriate perfection of a living body free from matter-induced fatigue:

> "The whole life and perfection of those animals which are not affected by tiredness and languor must of necessity lie in their movement; and their assimilation to their Creator consists in this, that by their movement they impart life to what exists in this sublunary world."

He distinguishes the *primary intention* of celestial motion (assimilation to the First, which is the sphere's own perfection) from its *secondary* but real effect (the conservation of sublunary existence through the diversity of celestial configurations):

> "This movement does not occur according to the philosophers in first intention for the sake of this sublunary world … just as the perfection of the ruler in relation to his subject does not lie in his being a ruler, but his being a ruler is only the consequence of his perfection."

### On the direction of celestial motion

Averroes responds at length to al-Ghazālī's objection that the philosophers cannot explain *why* heaven moves in the direction it does (east to west for the outermost sphere, west to east for the planets) rather than the reverse. He argues that simple bodies have their directional natures determined *by those natures themselves*, not by any further cause:

> "For simple existents have no other cause for what proceeds from them than their own natures and their forms … The earth, for instance, has no other cause for its downward movement than its attribute of earthiness."

Heaven, as a living body, has a right and a left determined by its nature — and the noblest direction has been assigned to the noblest body, just as the right side is naturally the side of strength in terrestrial animals. He acknowledges that many details of the wisdom in celestial motions remain unknown to current astronomy but are progressively revealed through long experience, citing the Chaldaeans and the astrologers of his own time as evidence that causal links between celestial motion and terrestrial events are genuinely discoverable.

## Discussion 16 — The Souls of the Heavens and Knowledge of Particulars

### The Avicennan theory of the Indelible Tablet

Avicenna (as summarised by al-Ghazālī) argues that because each particular celestial movement requires a *particular will*, and every will requires a *representation* of its object, the celestial souls must possess representations (*takhayyulāt*) of each definite movement. Since these movements are causal antecedents of all sublunary events, the celestial souls' representations extend through the causal chain to encompass all past and future particular events. This is the philosophical interpretation of the Qur'ānic "Indelible Tablet" (*al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ*) and the "Pen" — the separate intellects are the Pen (bestowers); the celestial souls are the Tablet (receivers). This theory also explains prophetic knowledge: prophets access the Indelible Tablet through the strength of their imaginative faculty, which is not overwhelmed by sensory distraction.

### Averroes' rejection

Averroes makes two decisive moves:

**First**, he denies the theory is attributable to the *philosophers* as such:

> "What he mentions here is, to my knowledge, not said by any philosophers except Avicenna … Alexander of Aphrodisias explains in his book called *The Principles of the Universe* that these bodies have no representations, because representations exist only in animals because of their conservation, and these bodies do not fear corruption."

According to Alexander, representations exist in sublunary animals because they need to remember threats and opportunities for self-preservation. Celestial bodies, being incorruptible, have no such need. Adding imagination to them would require adding sensation (since imagination presupposes sensation), which would make them essentially sublunary animals — absurd.

**Second**, he attacks the core logical move: the inference from "heaven has a particular will for a particular movement" to "heaven has a particular *representation* of that movement." He argues that intellect-governed agents produce particular acts through *universal* representations, not particular ones:

> "No particular act proceeds from beings endowed with intellect, except when this act is represented through a universal representation, and then endless individual things proceed from it — for instance the form of a cupboard proceeds from a carpenter only through a universal representation which does not distinguish one particular cupboard from another."

The celestial sphere, moving in a single continuous circular motion, needs only a **universal will for movement** — it does not need to individually represent each of the infinitely many particular positions it will pass through, any more than a stone needs to represent each instant of its fall:

> "The circular … moves qua circular in one single movement, although from this one movement there follow many different particular motions in the existents below it. These spheres, however, are not concerned with those particular movements, but their only intention is to conserve the species of which these particulars are the particulars."

### Averroes' positive account of heavenly cognition

Averroes' own position is that the celestial bodies' cognition is **neither universal nor individual** in the human sense — the two modes are unified in a knowledge separated from matter:

> "The philosophers assert that these two kinds of knowledge, the universal and the particular, are unified in the knowledge which is separated from matter; and when this knowledge emanates in the sublunary world it divides itself into universal and particular, although this knowledge itself is neither the one nor the other."

This applies to the separate intellects (identified with the philosophical *angels*, the cherubim near God) and, by extension, to the celestial souls that depend on them. The term "knowledge" is again **equivocal** as between divine/angelic cognition and human cognition.

On prophecy and dreams, Averroes says the authentic tradition of the ancient philosophers holds that revelation proceeds from God through an incorporeal intermediary — the **active intellect** (*al-'aql al-fa'āl*), which in the Holy Law is called an *angel*. The elaborate Avicennan theory of the Indelible Tablet as celestial imagination is Avicenna's own innovation, not classical Aristotelianism.

## Cosmological Summary

Across Discussions 14–16, Averroes builds a consistent picture of the celestial realm:

| Level | Nature | Mode of cognition | Relation to motion |
|---|---|---|---|
| God (First Principle) | Pure act, simple, incorporeal | Active intellect; cause of existents | Unmoved mover; object of celestial desire |
| Separate Intellects (cherubim, Pen) | Incorporeal substances; angels | Unified knowledge, neither universal nor individual | Move spheres by being objects of thought |
| Souls of the Spheres (heavenly angels, Tablet) | Animate, intellective, not imaginative | No individual representations; universal–particular unified knowledge | Principle of circular motion; secondary cause of sublunary life |
| Celestial Bodies | Living bodies, eternal in substance | Move both to assimilate to God and to conserve sublunary species | Source of diversity in configuration that produces sublunary change |

This cosmological picture contrasts sharply with Avicenna's version on three key points: (1) celestial souls have no imagination/representation in Averroes; (2) the aim of celestial motion is the movement itself, not an infinite series of particular positions; (3) prophecy and dreams are mediated by the active intellect, not by contact with an imaginative "Indelible Tablet" in the celestial souls.

## Historiography

The question of celestial animation was central to Aristotle's *De caelo* and *Metaphysics* Lambda, and became hotly contested in the medieval reception. Averroes' own lengthy commentaries on Aristotle's physical works are more technically sustained than these passages in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*, which are explicitly dialectical. His rejection of Avicenna's imaginative celestial souls has been confirmed by subsequent historians (Davidson 1992) as representing the mainstream Aristotelian position more faithfully than the Avicennan elaboration.

The Indelible Tablet interpretation and its connection to prophecy became a particular point of tension between philosophical and theological accounts of revelation in al-Andalus, and the question of whether Averroes has a consistent account of prophetic epistemology remains debated.
