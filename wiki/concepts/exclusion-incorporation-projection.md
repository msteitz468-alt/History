# Exclusion, Incorporation and Projection (Bailey)

An **interpretive triad** proposed by Douglass Bailey
([[bailey-balkan-prehistory-2000|*Balkan Prehistory*, 2000]]) to explain what
changed in south-east Europe after **6500 BC**. Bailey argues that the period's
revolution was not fundamentally economic (against Childe's
[[neolithic-revolution|Neolithic Revolution]]) nor demographic (against
Gimbutas's Kurgan invasions), but a transformation in **how people arranged
themselves, their places, and their things**. Three spatial-temporal processes,
all made newly powerful by the durable built environment and the new
materiality of pottery, copper, gold, and figurines, capture that
rearrangement.

The framework is **etic** (Bailey's analytical scheme, not a category used by
prehistoric people) and **contested** — it is one strong post-processual
reading, not a consensus model. Its value in this wiki is as a lens for the
social interpretation of [[balkans|Balkan]] Neolithic–Chalcolithic evidence;
its claims about tension, hoarding, and "illusion" run deliberately against the
older image of Neolithic communalism (cf. [[neolithic-communities]]).

## The three processes

### Exclusion
The physical or symbolic imposition of order by **restricting access** — to
places, things, and information about them. Bailey finds it everywhere in the
post-6500 BC record: impermeable mud/timber/stone walls that block movement,
sound, sight, and smell; internal partitions and "deep" rooms; ditches, banks,
and palisades around villages (Karanovo, Divostin, Nea Nikomedeia) and around
fields; and even closed, lidded pottery and sealed silos that restrict sensory
knowledge of contents. Paradoxically, Bailey holds that exclusion is
**indispensable to individual personhood** — privacy and self-directed thought
depend on it. Both oval pit-huts and rectilinear houses, despite their
differences, were "facilities of exclusion."

### Incorporation
The apparently opposite process of **taking possession** — bringing people,
things, and activities *into* a place or body. Enclosing space simultaneously
creates an inside to incorporate into: houses gather tasks, tools, stores, and
people; the burial of infants under house floors incorporates individuals into
**household** groups; villages incorporate households; extramural cemeteries
incorporate the dead into a community (common body orientation) while shared
prestige-good rituals incorporate distant individuals into a trans-regional
network. Bailey extends incorporation to the **body itself** ("somatic
incorporation"): eating exotic foods and piercing/wearing gold, copper, and
*Spondylus* injected the social essence of those materials into personal
identity.

### Projection
The **injection of an essence into a place or object** — symbolic linkage
between a subject (person, household, community) and a representation. Durable
houses and tells project claims of continuous occupation and legitimate
residence; mound cemeteries project lineage continuity; **miniature clay
models** of houses, ovens, and furniture (e.g. Ovcharovo) project the ideology
of the built environment; and **anthropomorphic figurines** project — and, in
Bailey's reading, *distort* — human identity, transforming unequal people into
a levelled, defamiliarised category.

## The "illusion" argument

Bailey's most pointed claim: a world in which **inflexible**, durable
arrangements define social relationships is one with a high potential for
**illusion** — the advertisement of a "preferred reality" over actual daily
existence. He reads three fifth-millennium phenomena as such projected fictions:

- **Mortuary ritual** — the brilliant [[varna|Varna]]-style graves project
  community-wide distinction (mostly onto male inhumations) against a background
  of cohesion (uniform grave orientation), a "double exposure" painted on the
  blank canvas of the cemetery.
- **Village living** — the ordered, uniform, rebuilt house creates an image of
  stable corporate households that masks a mobile, fissioning, seasonally
  abandoned reality.
- **Anthropomorphic imagery** — figurines "appropriated reality and created
  illusion," making unequal people appear equal.

Against the standard picture of Neolithic sharing, Bailey concludes the Balkans
were "riven with tension and conflict, where hoarding and the exclusion of
people was just as frequent as sharing and helping." The closing vignette of
**Lilka** — a woman who, abandoned by her brother, resolves to build her own,
*bigger* house and found a rival household — dramatises exclusion,
incorporation, and projection at the scale of one ambitious individual.

## Limitations and critique

- **Etic and untestable at the margins.** The triad reframes evidence rather
  than predicting it; "exclusion" and "incorporation" can be read into almost
  any bounded structure, which makes the scheme powerful but hard to falsify.
- **A position, not a consensus.** It is a post-processual interpretation
  contemporaneous with, but distinct from, Whittle's and Chapman's readings of
  the same data; the underlying site facts are not in dispute, the social
  meaning is.
- **"Illusion" is a strong claim.** Treating mortuary and figurine display as
  deliberate fiction imputes intentions that the archaeology underdetermines.
