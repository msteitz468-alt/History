# Te Moana Nui a Kiwa

An Indigenous Māori and pan-Polynesian name and conceptual framework for the
ocean now called the Pacific. Where the European designation "Pacific" (imposed by
Magellan in 1520) names an external, cartographic object, *Te Moana Nui a Kiwa*
names a living genealogical homeland — an environment of relationships, not an
empty expanse.

## Origin and Meaning

The name translates roughly as "the great sea of Kiwa." In Māori cosmogony, the
god Tangaroa (lord of the seas) appointed his brother Kiwa as *overlord* of the
Southern Ocean; further brothers — Kaukau and Rakamaomao — were assigned
subordinate stewardship, with Rakamaomao governing the icy Antarctic extension.
Two guardian gods, Te Puwhakahara and Takaaho, were originally gods of trees but
became gods of sharks and whales and accompanied those species when they were
granted permission to move from lakes to the open ocean.

> "Upon these godly foundations rested the original ocean, Te Moana nui a Kiwa."
> (Ihimaera, ch. 1, in Jones & Matsuda eds., *Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean*,
> Vol. 1, 2022)

The ocean's creation in Māori tradition follows the separation of Ranginui (Sky
Father) and Papatūānuku (Earth Mother) by their son Tāne, who allocated the seas
to his brother Tangaroa. Rain from Ranginui then filled the hollows Papatūānuku
made when she turned over, forming the great seas.

## The Ocean as Genealogical Homeland

For Māori and broader Polynesian peoples, the ocean is not a barrier but the medium
of *whakapapa* — genealogy. All Polynesian peoples trace their origins to Rangiātea
(Ra'iātea in the Leeward Islands of French Polynesia), described as the first homeland
and the "original repository of all Māori myth, folklore, culture, and customs." The
promise embedded in the *Te Moana nui a Kiwa* cosmology is that the descendants
(*mokopuna*) "would never be lost" because they are "seeds sown at Rangiātea."

This genealogical framing binds humans, animals, and the sea together. Māori
followed what they called *hwaelweg* — whale roads — across the ocean. Whales
were understood as ancestral kin: in one version of the Paikea story, the ancestor
Kahutia Te Rangi *shapeshifted into a whale*, because "the whale was his *kaitiaki*,
his nature-self." Whale groups received honorific titles (Tūtara Kauika, Te Kauika
Tangaroa, Wehenga Kauika). Whalers from the North Pacific to New Zealand's South
Island embedded cetacean kinship in their practices, as documented in Philip Hoare's
work cited by Ihimaera.

> "Te Moana nui a Kiwa is not just an ocean. It is an environment, a set of
> relationships, a branching whakapapa involving transactional exchanges between
> man and sea. Thus Te Moana nui a Kiwa is not just a physical entity, it is a human
> one. The future of the one is entrusted in the future of the other."
> (Ihimaera, ch. 1)

## Voyaging and Navigation

Beginning from Ra'iātea around the sixth and seventh centuries CE, Māori ancestors
voyaged by canoe to Aotearoa New Zealand. Ihimaera notes "there may have been
fifty or more canoes which, from the seventh century, began at various times to leave
Ra'iātea for Aotearoa." One ancestor, Hui-te-Rangiora, sailed far into the Southern
Ocean and reportedly saw Antarctic ice cliffs, returning to describe them at Ra'iātea.

The *Tākitimu* canoe carried sacred stones (*mauri*) "to be placed in whare wānanga,
houses of learning, in the new land." Canoes were accompanied by taniwha and
female whales as escorts — underscoring the genealogical intimacy between human
voyagers and cetaceans.

## Colonial "Overlays" and the Framework's Resilience

Ihimaera explicitly frames European colonization and naming as a series of "overlays"
on the original ocean:

> "By a series of 'overlays' — the 1520 naming of the Original Ocean as the Pacific
> Ocean for one, the notions of Polynesia, Melanesia, and Micronesia coined by
> the French explorer Dumont D'Urville in the 1820s for another, those traumas we
> have accepted."
> (Ihimaera, ch. 1)

The concept of *Te Moana nui a Kiwa* thus functions as historiographical counter to
colonial cartography: it insists on the priority of Indigenous naming, genealogy, and
relationship over externally imposed taxonomy. See also [[concepts/chuukese-history-womens-perspectives]]
for a parallel Indigenous decolonization of historiography in Micronesia.

## Stewardship Ethics (*Kaitiakitanga*)

The framework carries explicit obligations. Ihimaera writes that the peoples of the
Pacific are *kaitiaki* (guardians) with a "contract to safeguard the Pacific inventory —
not just the ocean itself but also its many environments and huge histories." This
ethical tradition is embedded in the story of Ruatepupuke: observing the custom of
returning the first fish of the catch to Tangaroa taught "sustainability and replenishing
our resources." Threats enumerated: distant-water illegal fishing, seabed mining,
nuclear radiation, climate change, rising sea levels threatening island nations.

## Historiography

This concept belongs to a genre of Indigenous historiographical intervention,
alongside Epeli Hau'ofa's "Our Sea of Islands" (1994), which recentered Pacific
Islander agency in opposition to colonial "sea of small islands" diminution. Witi
Ihimaera's opening chapter in *The Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean, Vol. 1*
(Jones & Matsuda eds., 2022) deploys traditional speech forms (*mihi*, *korero*,
*kaupapa*, *waiata*) to embed the academic volume itself within an Indigenous
framework — treating the book as a *waka* (ceremonial seagoing canoe) bearing
knowledge to future generations (*mokopuna*).
