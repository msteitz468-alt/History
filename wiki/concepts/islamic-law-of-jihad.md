## Definition and Scope

The juristic theory of jihād — also treated under the title **siyar** (from the root "to walk/march," connoting a military expedition) — is the Islamic law of war as elaborated in classical fiqh. It is distinct from the popular and political uses of the term that have dominated modern discourse. Hallaq's foundational argument is that "jihād as Muslims conceived it in theory and practice for over a millennium no longer exists": the modern Islamist and modernist uses of the concept are qualitatively, not merely rhetorically, different from the pre-modern juristic doctrine.

In legal works, "jihād and siyar always referred to military campaigns by the Abode of Islam against the Abode of War, this latter defined as territory inhabited and ruled by non-Muslims, be they Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians or pagans of all sorts." One influential technical dictionary defined jihād as "a call to the right religion and fighting to implement it when the unbelievers refuse to accept it or refuse a protected status."

This page covers the classical juristic theory and its modern reinterpretations. It does not cover specific historical campaigns, which are filed under event pages.

## The Classical Theory

### Obligation Type and Its Exceptions

Jihād is by universal agreement a duty of the **kifāya** (collective sufficiency) type: if sufficient members of the community perform it, the obligation lapses for the rest. This contrasts with farḍ ʿayn (individual obligation incumbent on every Muslim). Three exceptions convert jihād into a farḍ ʿayn obligation: (1) when battle has already begun and fighters are present on the field; (2) when an invading army enters Muslim territory; (3) when the imam personally calls a specific person to participate. Offensive jihād is far kifāya; defensive jihād in a state of weakness is unanimously regarded as farḍ ʿayn.

### Qualifications of the Jihādist

A jihādist must be: a Muslim male, an adult, free, and of sound body and mind. This excludes non-Muslims, women, minors, slaves, the handicapped, and the insane. Additional circumstantial conditions apply: in a far kifāya jihād, a young volunteer requires parental consent, and an indebted person requires the lender's permission unless a guarantor is secured. Provisions and support for the jihādist and his family must be secured for the campaign's duration.

Importantly, these conditions are not absolute: "the public interest of Islam and Muslims, and various contingencies, may have required enlisting the aid of non-Muslims in jihād wars," and the majority of jurists allowed this, including dhimmīs, people from treaty territories, and even harb̄ıs (inhabitants of the Abode of War).

In Twelver and Zaydite Shīʿism, the imam — a member of the Alid family — holds the sole prerogative to order jihād. Accordingly, no expansionist jihād has technically been possible since the Occultation of the last Imam in 260/873, though historical practice diverged from this theory.

### The Two Abodes and Intermediate Territory

The fundamental binary is between **Dār al-Islām** (Abode of Islam) and **Dār al-Ḥarb** (Abode of War). Between them stands a conceptual intermediate zone with which the Abode of Islam may live in peace under **hudna** (peace treaty). The majority of jurists restrict peace treaties to ten years; some permit indefinite duration if public interest (maṣlaḥa) warrants. Once concluded, treaties bind the imam's successors as well; his death does not dissolve the treaty.

### Treatment of Non-Combatants and Enemy

Prior notice is mandatory before initiating jihād: a call to accept Islam, typically three times over three days (Mālikites). People who cannot fight are forbidden targets: "women, children, farmers, the handicapped, the elderly, the chronically ill, hermaphrodites, monks and all 'church folk' of the monastic kind." The enemy's envoy is also inviolable. The exception: women who directly participate in the war effort.

Ambush, cutting water supplies, destroying forts, and catapulting fire are lawful. Forbidden: "perfidy, treachery, torture and mutilation," burning crops and beehives, uprooting trees and plants, and killing non-combat animals. The governing principle is that "no loss of life or damage to property is permitted to take place behind enemy lines unless it is essential, and directly related, to defeating the enemy's army." All hostilities must cease upon surrender. "Attacks on enemy civilian targets (with the intention of weakening the enemy or otherwise) are forbidden."

The lesser-of-two-evils principle permits firing on human shields when the alternative is the collapse of the jihād, though most jurists require blood-money payment in such cases.

### Prisoners

The elderly, handicapped, and monks are not taken prisoner. Women and children may be taken captive but may not be harmed; parents may not be separated from children. Adult male prisoners are presented before the imam, who may: (1) order execution; (2) enslave; (3) exchange for ransom or Muslim prisoners; or (4) free. Prisoners willing to accept protected status (dhimmī) may be enslaved (not killed or freed without ransom). Prisoners who convert before the imam decides may not be killed, exchanged, or freed — only enslaved (with the option of manumission). A Muslim prisoner released conditionally must honor the terms, including non-betrayal.

### Booty and the Poll-Tax

Booty is distributed as: one-fifth to the imam (spent on orphans, poor, wayfarers, or waqf), one-fifth to foot soldiers, three-fifths to horsemen (the horse itself receiving a two-fifths share among horsemen). The People of the Book who accept protected status pay the **jizya** (poll-tax): 48 dirhams per year for the wealthy, 24 for middle income, 12 for the modest, and nothing for the poor. Conversion at any point in the year cancels the year's obligation.

### Amān (Safe-Conduct)

Any private Muslim — male, female, or slave — has the competence to grant amān (safe-conduct) to up to ten inhabitants of the Abode of War, protecting them in person and property without tax obligation. The imam may grant amān to any number, including entire populations. Amān may be concluded in writing, orally, or by course of conduct; commercial practice is itself sufficient to constitute implied amān. Foreign envoys receive automatic amān. Hallaq notes the "considerable tolerance in the law of non-Muslim denizens in Muslim lands" as starkly contrasting with modern nation-state immigration and rights regimes.

## Structural Characteristics of the Classical Theory

Hallaq identifies six structural features that must be grasped to understand the classical doctrine and distinguish it from modern reinventions:

1. **Narrowly religious goal**: The theory is "driven by a single concern, namely, the conversion of unbelievers to the Islamic faith." Material gain is a minor consideration at most.
2. **Partial scope (inter-bloc only)**: The theory exclusively addresses conflict between Muslim and non-Muslim sovereignties. "Nowhere in the entirety of juristic discourse do the legists deal with wars and conflict between and among Muslim principalities themselves" — despite such wars being frequent historically.
3. **Partial scope (unilateral regulation)**: Jihād law regulates only Muslim conduct; it "has nothing to say about how the enemy should conduct itself." This makes it different from modern international law, which is (in theory) mutually applicable.
4. **Assumption of Muslim victory**: The theory "entirely overlooks the possibility of Muslim defeat … Throughout every discussion of each and every law book, the assumption is one of always winning." Provisions for Muslim populations under enemy rule are virtually absent.
5. **Theory vs. practice gap**: Like other branches of fiqh, the juristic doctrine describes norms, not necessarily historical conduct. The closer the rulers' interests were to a legal domain, the more likely non-Sharīʿa elements would distort application.
6. **Historically bounded origin**: The theory arose from 1st-century Islamic historical experience and was enhanced by successive conquests through the Siege of Vienna (1095/1683). Muslims had little incentive to revise it until colonial modernity.

## Modern Reinterpretations

### Modernist Revisionism

Confronting European colonialism — first in India under British rule — Muslim modernists fundamentally reinterpreted jihād. Sir Sayyid Aḥmad Khān (1817–98) argued that jihād is properly defensive, requiring only the protection of the pillars of Islam (prayer, almsgiving, fasting, pilgrimage, profession of faith) when directly threatened. He concluded that Indian Muslims under British rule were not obligated to wage jihād since the British had not obstructed these pillars. This reinterpretation drew a sharp division between the religious and the political.

Maḥmūd Shaltūt (d. 1963) argued from Quranic exegesis that "there is not a single verse in the Quran which could support the opinion that the aim of fighting in Islam is conversion," making jihād defensive by nature. Muḥammad Saʿīd Ashmāwī distinguished eternal religion from historically contingent religious thought, arguing that Quranic verses calling for unconditional fighting are episodic responses to specific persecution and cannot bind later generations. He concluded that nowhere does the Quran enjoin fighting against People of the Book who have not first attacked Muslims.

### Islamist Reinvention

Some Islamists went beyond the classical theory in opposite directions, arguing that jihād exists to propagate Islam and dismantle oppressive governance. Abū al-Aʿlā Mawdūdī (d. 1979) framed jihād as a global revolution for human happiness and equality, positioning Islam's claim to world governance as categorically distinct from Western colonialism. Sayyid Quṭb argued that Western governance amounts to "the rule of man over man," enslaving humanity, and that jihād liberates people to choose freely — implicitly to choose Islam.

By the 1970s, domestic Muslim-majority regimes became targets alongside Western imperialism. Organizations like the Jamāʿat al-Takfīr wal-Hijra translated Marxian revolutionary discourse into Islamic vocabulary. Hallaq notes that "Christian Arab leaders have invoked the vocabulary of jihād in their support of Arab national movements."

Hallaq's conclusion: "the uses of the concept of jihād in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries are not only widely varied, but also qualitatively different from the consistent pre-modern juristic doctrine. To reduce these differences … to mere differences of style and language … is not only to miss the point but also to conflate the social, economic, political and legal realities of the second/eighth century with those of our own time."

## From Cobb, *The Race for Paradise* (2014) — crusade-era practice of the law

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Paul M. Cobb]] situates classical jihad doctrine in the political and social world of the eleventh–thirteenth-century Mediterranean, without replacing Hallaq’s juristic anatomy above. Overlap with Hallaq is noted only where Cobb adds crusade-period texture.

### Ulema as institutional base

The **ulema** (*ʿulamaʾ*)—more like rabbis than Christian clergy—were scholars of Islamic law without sacerdotal sacraments; informal master–student networks; masters of *ʿilm* (religious knowledge). In 1050 they guarded the *shariʿa* as path for living pleasing to God: jurists, teachers, preachers, qadis, muftis issuing fatwas—putting “equity” into the [[circle-of-justice|circle of equity]]. They were the practical base for jihad doctrine and later anti-Frankish preaching.

### Regulatory function (anti-essentialist framing)

Neither Islam nor Latin Christianity is “inherently” violent; classical jihad doctrine (with school variations) developed in a medieval imperial context of warrior-elites and conquest as a juristic system to **control** violence—conditions for starting/ending war, limit lawlessness, define targets, protect noncombatants, define merit. Core categories Cobb stresses for general readers align with the classical theory above: *fard ʿala al-kifaya* vs *fard ʿala al-ʿayn*; caliph/imam (or representative) as declarer in theory; defensive or offensive/preemptive forms; *mujahidun* assumed male; battlefield dead as *shahid* (martyr). Missionary structure: Abode of Islam vs Abode of War; Jews and Christians unwilling to convert could surrender as *dhimmis* paying *jizya*—the poll-tax most understood as guaranteeing subordinate toleration (see [[dhimmi]], [[dar-al-islam]]).

### Commerce with the Abode of War

Islamic law permitted **commerce with infidels** if not involving prohibited commodities (wine; war materiel—weapons, armor; some interpretations slaves/horses); import for customs duties allowed. The papacy repeatedly (hence unsuccessfully) tried to criminalize Christian trade with Muslims. Ibn Jubayr: armies may array while travelers pass freely between sides. Frankish–Muslim exchange used pre-existing Mediterranean commercial ties (*funduq/fondaco*; condominia/*munasafa* dividing lands, water, harvests). Local politics almost always trumped ideology even under Saladin.

### Jihad as one plank of pious rule (Zangid–Ayyubid cases)

- **Nur al-Din** carefully used epithet *mujahid* in decrees/inscriptions, but jihad against Franks was one small aspect of a broader pious-just-ruler image; inscriptions pair *mujahid* with *zahid* (ascetic) and caliphal loyalty; he often used treaties/tribute; lands taken from **Muslim** rivals outweighed Frankish gains; madrasas and mystic meetinghouses on unprecedented scale.
- By spring **1186**, [[saladin|Saladin]]’s vast sultanate was divided among ambitious kinsmen; jihad vs Franks had been sidelined so long even advisers had misgivings; “dusting off” the *mujahid* title served to assert authority over kin and keep troops busy for plunder—jihad as a political solution whose “time had come” (detail under [[jihad-concept-evolution]] and actor pages).

Comparative institutional contrast with Latin crusade (centralization, temporary vow vs perpetual obligation, land vs souls) is filed under [[holy-war]].

## Historiography

The historiography of jihād is distorted by three compounding problems identified by Hallaq: (1) treating the theory as if it were applied historical reality; (2) attaching the most negative interpretations to the theory; and (3) projecting these interpretations onto contemporary realities that bear no resemblance to the classical era. "Paradigmatic Western scholarship — not to mention mass media — has allowed itself to succumb to all three problematics."

Cobb’s crusade-era narrative reinforces Hallaq’s theory-vs-practice gap: formal sultanic jihad campaigns of “tens of thousands” repeatedly failed through desertion, rivalry, and dual Muslim–Frank alliances; after c. 1115 jihad against Franks became largely a local amir/atabeg affair; and jihad language was calculated and intermittent even for Zangi, Nur al-Din, and Saladin.
