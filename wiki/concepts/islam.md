## Definition

**Islam** is the monotheistic religion founded in the early seventh century by the
preaching of [[muhammad|Muhammad]] (d. 632) in western Arabia, centered on submission
(*islām*) to the one God (Allah) as revealed in the **Qur'an** and embodied in the
practice (*sunna*) of the Prophet. By the close of the [[middle-millennium|Middle
Millennium]] it stretched, in Michael Cook's summary ([[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]],
Ch 15), "east/west from Southeast Asia to West Africa, and north/south from the bend
in the Volga River to Madagascar." It is one of the three "world religions" whose
portability and integrative power structure Part IV of the volume — and, on Cook's
central thesis, the most distinctive of them.

## Cook's Thesis: The Only World Religion That Was Also a World Civilization

Cook's organizing argument is that **Islam is the only world religion that was also a
world civilization**. Christianity and Buddhism spread across, and were shaped by,
several distinct civilizations; Islam, by contrast, generated a single, recognizable
**civilization** coextensive with the faith. He attributes this to a specific early
fusion: the strong **ethnic, religious, and political identity of the Arabs** (forged
in the conquests) combined with the **elaborate literary and administrative
traditions of the Fertile Crescent**. This produced a civilization bound together by
shared components even across vast regional diversity.

## The Binding Components

After surveying the differences among regions (Islam "interacted with and superseded
earlier civilizations and cultural formations" very differently in Iran, the Maghreb,
India, or the Volga), Cook highlights what held the civilization together:

- **A sacred language and scripture.** Arabic as the language of the Qur'an, of
  ritual, and of a vast scholarly literature — a lingua franca of learning from
  al-Andalus to the Indian Ocean.
- **Traditions of scholarship.** The [[educational-institutions|‘ulama’ and the
  madrasa]], the disciplines of law (*fiqh*/*sharia*), hadith, and Qur'anic science,
  and the *isnad* (chain of transmission) as a model of authority that even shaped
  [[science-transmission|the sciences]].
- **Shared institutions and law.** A common legal-commercial framework (partnership
  contracts, the *waqf* endowment), pilgrimage (the **hajj**) as a recurring
  trans-regional gathering, and a esteemed status for commerce — the Prophet himself
  had been a merchant — that made Islam a powerful **commercial ecumene** (see
  [[afro-eurasian-world-system]]).

## How Islam Spread

Cook distinguishes the modes by which Islam expanded, stressing that conquest was
often *not* the mechanism:

- **Conquest and consolidation** in the original Arab-conquest heartland (Iran,
  Iraq, Syria, Egypt, North Africa, Iberia) — though even here conversion was slow
  and followed conquest by generations.
- **Conversion by proximity and trade among the Turks.** The Oghuz/Turks Islamized on
  the fringes of Muslim Central Asia; their (by-then-Muslim) eleventh-century invasion
  of Iran *boosted* the military strength of the eastern Islamic world, and the Muslim
  **Turks** went on to end [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine]] power in Anatolia
  (transforming it from Greek-Christian to Turkish-Muslim) and, as the
  [[ottoman-empire|Ottomans]], to take the Balkans.
- **Commercial diaspora and conversion around the Indian Ocean.** Muslim merchants
  were sailing to southern China by the eighth century; commercial settlement plus
  local conversion carried Islam around the [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean rim]] —
  to East Africa (the [[swahili-coast|Swahili coast]]) and to maritime Southeast Asia
  — and across the Sahara to the [[western-sudan-states|Western Sudan]] (see
  [[trans-saharan-trade]]).
- **Rule over non-Muslim majorities.** In the Balkans and most of India, Muslim
  political rule did *not* produce majority conversion; the resulting minority-power
  situation produced both pragmatic accommodation and syncretism (Mughal-era India)
  and, at times, "fanatical intransigence."

## The Early Modern Expansion (CWH VI)

[[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]] (Nile Green) treats early modern Islam (1400–1800) as
a **set of processes** rather than a Middle Eastern "norm" diffusing outward — the
era of "history's greatest period of mosque construction" under the Ottoman,
Safavid, and Mughal empires, but also, more widely, of an **Islam of the shrine as
much as of the mosque**. Green's organizing argument is that the religion's
expansion across Eurasia and Africa was carried by **Sufi-‘ulama** (the men of
learning and the Sufi masters were often the same persons) through two reproducible
institutions: the **tariqa** (brotherhood, transmitting charisma and doctrine by
genealogy) and the **waqf** (endowment, making saintly "holy families" into
independently wealthy local establishments — "little cities of God"). Because the
authority claims of these lineages (genealogy, *baraka*, *sayyid* descent) were
**intrinsically mobile**, Sufi migrants could settle new frontiers from Morocco to
Java to China (the Chinese *menhuan* corporate lineages), making conversion a
process of **collective acculturation** rather than mass forced conversion.

Green stresses the **limits of "the religion of the book"**: most early modern
Muslims never owned a Quran, it remained largely untranslated and unprinted (a key
contrast with the [[protestant-reformation-1517|Protestant *sola scriptura*]]), and
literate Arabic Islam reached pastoralists and villagers only through Sufi
mediators. The early modern empires drove **confessionalization** (the Safavid
imposition of Shi‘ism; Ottoman and Safavid contestation of the holy families'
autonomy), and the eighteenth-century political and economic crises spawned
**renewal movements** (the Arabian Wahhabiyya and others) that, stressing the
Prophet's *sunna*, challenged the shrine-and-saint establishments — the prelude to
the colonial-era transformations of Islam.

## Limitations and Contestation

Cook's "world civilization" thesis is influential but **contested**: critics question
whether Islamic civilization was as unitary as the thesis implies, given the deep
differences between (say) West African, Persianate, and Southeast Asian Islam, and
whether the religion/civilization distinction holds for Christianity (Latin
Christendom arguably became a civilization too — see [[christianity]]). The
historicity of the earliest Islamic centuries is itself debated (the "skeptical
school"; see the historiography note on [[early-middle-ages]]).

## Islam in Southeast Asia

Anthony Reid's [[reid-southeast-asia-age-of-commerce-v2-1993|*Age of Commerce*]] traces Islam's
trade-borne spread through the **archipelago** — the [[islamization-of-southeast-asia|Islamization of
Southeast Asia]]. After a long "quarantine" stage as a tolerated merchant minority (Pasai, c. 1297),
Islam's great advance came 1400–1650, springing from Muslim [[melaka|Melaka]] along the spice routes; the
militant anti-Portuguese sultanates of Aceh and Banten and the conversion of Mataram and Makassar carried
it into the rural hinterlands. Spread by locally-married merchants and Sufi teachers rather than by
conquest, it was culturally accommodating yet demanded clear markers — the renunciation of **pork** and
**circumcision** — so that "becoming Muslim" fused with "becoming Malay" as a change of ethnic identity.
Today the archipelago (Indonesia, Malaysia, the southern Philippines) is the demographic heartland of the
Muslim world.
