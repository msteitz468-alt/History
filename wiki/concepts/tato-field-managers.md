## Definition

Tato (田堵, also written 田刀) designates the stratum of rural gentry who organized agricultural production in Heian Japan. Neither peasant cultivators nor absentee noble proprietors, they were intermediate managers — wealthy enough to hold private granaries, make suiko rice loans, employ corvee labor, and assume the tax obligations of dependent cultivators, but rooted in the local community in a way absentee nobles were not. By the mid-10th century, tato management had become the general form of Heian agriculture, effectively displacing the ritsuryo allotment-field system.

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]], chapter 4, is the principal source for the analysis here. It treats the tato not as a static social category but as the dynamic outcome of the interplay between the [[ritsuryo-decline]] and the emergence of the [[zuryo-provincial-governors]] system.

---

## Origins and Social Position

Tato grew out of the petty gentry (fugo no yakara, "rich and powerful") who had accumulated wealth through reclaimed fields (konden), private suiko loans, and irregular official positions in provincial headquarters or gun offices. They were sometimes termed rikiden no yakara ("those who maintain the fields"), a phrase borrowed from T'ang China indicating commendable worthies who contributed private wealth to agricultural projects and received tax remissions in return.

The 881 directive on office fields (kanden) in the home provinces is the earliest clear documentary reference to tato as a class of land managers being given possessory interests in land as payment for managing publicly administered fields: "the land-managing tato was to become an increasingly significant force over the next three centuries" (CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4). By the same directive, tato eligibility was extended to migrants (rōnin) as well as native residents.

## Functions

**Tax provision (fumyo role):** By the early 10th century, tato routinely assumed the tribute-tax (chō) and labor-commutation (yō) obligations of the peasants under their economic dominance. They were listed as "capable farmers" (kambyakusho) — those who could guarantee tax payment on behalf of allotment-field peasants, whether the latter actually existed or not. After the Council of State's order of 925, kambyakusho became the government's principal mechanism for acquiring commodity taxes from a largely fictitious registered peasant population.

The 988 Owari Province petition states that each official village had four or five tato responsible for land dues, with roughly 300 such providers across a province of perhaps 55,000 people. These figures show the tato stratum was small but concentrated — a few hundred households bearing the fiscal burden for tens of thousands.

**Agricultural management:** Tato managed both shōen fields for absentee proprietors and publicly administered fields for the kokuga. They rented fields from proprietors at rates assessed by provincial authorities, sometimes under annual written lease agreements. A tato's residential compound (the sho house, or shoke) included private granaries, served as a base for rice loans, and housed the labor force for field work and reclamation. The establishment of branch granaries (shōiri) throughout districts in the 823 Kyushu proposal was explicitly modeled on this private pattern.

**Brokerage of corvee and irregular levies:** From the early 10th century, rinji zōyaku (extraordinary irregular levies) imposed by the kokuga were directed primarily at tato as the bearers of economic capacity. Tato served as the interface between provincial headquarters and the peasantry in collecting these levies. As the Oyama-no-shō documents of 924–935 show, temple proprietors had to negotiate with successive governors on behalf of their tato custodians and managers to secure exemption.

## Named Obligees (Fumyo)

By the late 9th century, the tato-based system had generated a formal accounting category: the "named obligee" (fumyo), a wealthy gentry member whose name was entered on official records as personally responsible for the tax obligations of an assigned district or named holding. By the third decade of the 10th century, fumyo management of entire villages had become standard. The terms fumyo and tato overlapped, with fumyo emphasizing the formal tax-liability dimension and tato the agricultural management dimension. The distinction between the two categories broke down in the late 11th–early 12th centuries, when the term myo (name) came to designate a landholding and its cultivators as a single taxable unit.

---

## Relation to Gunji and Capital Nobility

Tato were not uniformly powerful. The most capable among them were gun-magistrate (gunji) families or their dependents — members of long-established local elite lineages with hereditary prestige and authority over land surveys, tax certification, and armed force. Ninth-century gunji like the Kagami brothers of Mino Province could mobilize over seven hundred men. These upper-tier tato were, as the CHJ states, "destined to become the shiki-holding regional lords of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries."

Lower-ranking tato were often migrants (rōnin) from the capital — former commissioned provincial officers who had illegally taken up permanent residence — or locally born cultivators who had accumulated enough rice wealth to assume tax obligations for others. They occupied a precarious position, owing loyalty to both noble patrons and the kokuga, subject to confiscation of their holdings by governors and dispossession by more powerful rivals.

---

## Significance

"Cultivation by field managers dominated agriculture in the tenth century, gradually displacing allotment-field holdings as the single major source of labor power from land. It was possession by field managers that made land, rather than people, the major object of taxation by both provincial headquarters and shōen proprietors" (CHJ vol. 2, ch. 4). This shift — from taxing people (the ritsuryo system's fundamental logic) to taxing land managed by identified gentry — was the structural transformation that ended the ritsuryo order and created the preconditions for the medieval Japanese estate system and, ultimately, for the warrior lords (bushi) who managed estates militarily.
