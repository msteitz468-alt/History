## Definition

The **Hellenistic polis** is the Greek city-state as it was *transformed and revitalized*
in the [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic age]] — both in old Greece and through "its
relentless spread into area after area of erstwhile non-Greek lands." J. K. Davies (CAH
VII.1 ch. 8) calls this "the greatest cultural phenomenon of the Hellenistic world," and
rejects the old Victorian verdict of the polis's "decline": the city did not die under the
kings but changed its profile, dropping some functions and assuming others. It is the
[[hellenistic-monarchy|monarchies']] counterpart and the chief vehicle of Greek culture's
expansion.

## A Plural Society, Not a Unity

Davies' framing is crucial and revisionist. The "Hellenistic World" is **not a unified
society** but a **plural society** — "a multiplicity of societies, interacting little with
each other," beneath a thin top layer of cosmopolitan [[koine-greek|koine]] culture shared
by courts, intellectuals, and great landowners. (Twenty-two spoken languages in the kingdom
of Pontus alone, he notes, are a better guide to "Hellenistic society" than the koine.) He
likewise doubts that a single "**Hellenistic economy**" existed: most production was local
subsistence (the Mediterranean triad of corn, wine, oil), consumed within ~30 km, occupying
~80% of the labour force — against M. I. Rostovtzeff's older picture of a unified,
market-integrated zone. The term "Hellenistic" itself, he stresses, is a **19th-century
coinage** with no equivalent in contemporary Greek, and the conventional focus on the Greek
component is "basically colonialist."

## How the Polis Changed

The polis was "over-determined" — sustained by six converging interests (settlers, dependent
cities, free leagues, surviving non-Greek cities, hellenizing native elites, and the kings
themselves) — but it diverged from the classical city-state:

- **Democracy hollowed out.** Most Hellenistic cities called themselves "democratic," but
  participatory radicalism gave way to **government by informal elites** working through
  democratic forms; pay for office and assembly (*misthos*) largely vanished, and magistracies
  converged with **liturgies**.
- **Euergetism.** Public finance depended on the **rich**: magistracies became expensive
  honours, and the wealthy provided loans, voluntary gifts (*epidoseis*), and endowed
  **foundations** in return for honours that displayed *philotimia* — the system of civic
  benefaction (see [[euergetism]]). The shift was in **internal power relations**: few
  assemblies could now coerce their rich.
- **The gymnasium** became the **"identifying institution of Greek culture"**, evolving from
  an athletic club into something like a secondary school — and, in colonial Asia Minor, the
  badge of Greekness (and the target of the Maccabean reaction).
- **More permeable boundaries.** Citizenship was sold, granted via *proxenia* and
  *isopoliteia*, lavished on athletes and benefactors — though slaves and non-Greeks stayed
  rigidly excluded. A small, influential class of cosmopolitan **professionals, philosophers,
  and royal officials** moved between city and court (the basis of "Hellenistic
  cosmopolitanism" — real, but a thin minority phenomenon).
- **Withdrawal from war** toward **arbitration** by third powers; the cities increasingly
  resolved disputes without fighting, giving substance to the ideal that war between Greeks
  was civil war.
- **The cities took the initiative in [[ruler-cult]]**, slotting the king as founder, saviour,
  or benefactor into existing categories of honour.

## The Limits of the Polis

Davies stresses that the city frame could not contain everything — "other currents of
religious experience were moving in different directions":

- **Greek gods had their limits**: too few Greeks, too un-effervescent a religious culture,
  for the Greek pantheon to prevail; instead, **interpenetration** with non-Greek cults
  (Isis, [[sarapis|Sarapis]]) that better met needs of **salvation**, healing, and personal
  anxiety.
- A turn toward the **individual** — magic, oracles for private crises, and above all
  **astrology** (from Babylonia) and **scepticism** (and Polybius' deification of *Tyche*).
- **Autonomous associations** escaping the civic frame — philosophical schools (formally
  *thiasoi*), foreign cult-groups, and above all the **technitai of Dionysus** (guilds of
  performing artists), virtually sovereign bodies passing city-style decrees.

## Significance

Davies' chapter is a landmark for treating the Hellenistic world as a **plural, layered
society** rather than a uniform "Hellenistic civilization," and the *polis* as a living,
adaptive institution rather than a relic in decline. The transformed city — euergetistic,
elite-run, gymnasium-centred, ruler-worshipping — would persist, depoliticized, deep into
the Roman period. See [[hellenistic-world]], [[hellenistic-monarchy]], [[euergetism]],
[[greek-federalism]].
