# Sunnaic Practice (al-ʿAmal)

"Sunnaic practice" is Hallaq's analytical term for the living, community-based legal traditions of early Islamic cities — principally Medina, Kufa, Basra, and Damascus — that constituted the operative second source of Islamic law during the first two centuries H (seventh–eighth centuries CE). It is distinct from formal [[hadith|ḥadīth]] (Prophetic narrative with chains of transmission) while also being understood by its practitioners as embodying authentic Prophetic precedent. The displacement of sunnaic practice by formal ḥadīth was the central transformation in the formation of classical Islamic law.

## What Sunnaic Practice Was

Sunnaic practice denoted the cumulative, consensus-validated legal conduct of the Muslim community in a given locale, maintained continuously across generations. The Medinese called it "the matter that the people [of Medina] have continuously upheld," or "the Sunna on which there is no disagreement among us." The Kufans referred to "the opinion on which the people of Kufa agree." In both cases, the claim was not to *individual* authority but to *communal continuity* — the unbroken chain of legitimate Islamic conduct reaching back to the Companions and, through them, to the Prophet.

The key structural feature: sunnaic practice was **self-validating through consensus**. A ḥadīth was tested against the practice; if inconsistent, the ḥadīth was rejected or suspended. The Medinese jurist Ibn al-Qāsim (d. 191/806) expressed this precisely:

> "This tradition [ḥadīth] has come down to us, and if it were accompanied by a practice passed to those from whom we have taken it over by their own predecessors, it would be right to follow it. But in fact it is like those other traditions which are not accompanied by practice . . . the practice was different, and the whole community and the Companions themselves acted on other rules."

For the Medinese, community practice *was* the Prophetic Sunna — not as a claim independent of the Prophet, but as the continuous living application of what the Prophet had actually done and taught in Medina. The formal ḥadīth narratives proliferating from Iraq were, from this perspective, competing claims that lacked the validation of unbroken practice.

## Regional Variants

### Medinan Sunnaic Practice

The strongest and most theoretically coherent form. The Medinese could claim geographic continuity with the Prophet's own life: "it was Medina that the Prophet had made his home, and that it was in Medina that the Qurān was revealed." Their practice was therefore seen as the direct continuation of Prophetic conduct, validated by the consensus of successive generations of Medinan scholars. [[malik-ibn-anas|Mālik b. Anas]] declared Medinan consensus binding on all jurists — not as a provincial claim but as the claim that Medinese practice *was* the authoritative Islamic standard.

Mālik's *Muwaṭṭaʾ* (composed ca. 150/767 or before) records 898 Companion reports alongside 822 Prophetic traditions — the latter deemed authentic *because* they reflected actual Medinan practice, not on independent isnād grounds. Practice authenticated ḥadīth, not the reverse.

### Iraqian (Kufan) Sunnaic Practice

The Kufans could not claim geographic continuity with the Prophet, but forged the link through the Companions who had migrated to Iraq. Their sunnaic practice was characterized by:
- Less use of the term *ʿamal* (practice) than the Medinese
- Reliance on Companion reports and the absence of contradictory Companion opinions as evidence of Prophetic sanction
- Extensive use of istiḥsān and qiyās-type reasoning (without these technical terms)
- Universal consensus claims that were, in fact, Iraqian positions

### Syrian (Damascene) Sunnaic Practice

The Syrian concept, reflected in the doctrine of al-Awzāʿī, was similarly the uninterrupted practice of Muslims from the Prophet through the early caliphs to contemporary scholars — without formal isnāds, simply as the continuous living inheritance of the community.

## The Epistemic Structure

Sunnaic practice and consensus were nearly inseparable: "the force of sunnaic practice could not be separated from the concept of consensus. The former could not have risen to paramountcy without unanimous or near-unanimous agreement." Together they constituted "a compound construct of sunnaic–consensual practice" that stood as the effective second source of the law (after the Qurān) in the second/eighth century.

This compound acted as the *filter* for formal ḥadīth: only ḥadīths consistent with sunnaic practice were accepted as credible. The compound also served as the *check* on raʾy: individual discretionary opinion had to yield to the unanimous or near-unanimous judgment embedded in continuous practice.

## Why Sunnaic Practice Lost

The displacement of sunnaic practice by formal [[hadith|ḥadīth]] had structural causes:

1. **Geographic expansion**: New Muslim communities in Khurasan, Transoxania, Egypt, and North Africa had not inherited the living practices of Medina or Kufa. They needed a source of law not tied to specific urban traditions. Universal Prophetic ḥadīth filled this need; regional sunnaic practice could not.

2. **Scholar mobility**: As legal scholars traveled widely across the Islamic world, loyalty to the specific sunnaic practice of one city became difficult to maintain and impossible to transfer. A Kufan jurist in Egypt would find Kufan sunnaic practice inapplicable to his environment.

3. **Documentation**: Formal ḥadīth claimed to reconstruct Prophetic Sunna as a *historical event* with named transmitters. Sunnaic practice had no equivalently objectified pedigree — its authority rested on the (to outsiders, unverifiable) continuity of communal memory.

4. **Universality of ḥadīth**: The traditionalist movement was mobile and not regionally attached. Its ḥadīth corpus was universally accessible and free of regional peculiarities.

5. **Al-Shāfiʿī's polemic**: [[shafii-muhammad-ibn-idris|Al-Shāfiʿī]] attacked sunnaic practice as a "mass of inconsistencies" compared to authentic Prophetic ḥadīth, and insisted that only formal ḥadīth could validly represent Prophetic Sunna. This argument, though not immediately successful, framed the theoretical stakes in a way that eventually favored the ḥadīth position.

## The Aftermath: Consensus Dissociated

When ḥadīth displaced sunnaic practice as the vehicle of Prophetic Sunna, the compound "sunnaic–consensual practice" was dissolved. Consensus ([[ijma-consensus|ijmāʿ]]) had to be re-theorized as an independent source, no longer defined by its grounding in local practice but by the agreement of all qualified mujtahids of a given generation. Mālikī theory resisted this, continuing to insist on the special authority of Medinan consensus; theorists of other schools argued — eventually prevailing — that no regional consensus could be binding and that only the consensus of the entire community of mujtahids carried legal force. See [[rise-of-legal-theory|Rise of Legal Theory]].

The traces of sunnaic practice did not entirely disappear. Later Mālikite theory maintained a revised form of Medinan consensus as a distinct source; and much Ḥanafī positive law, when re-anchored in formal ḥadīth by Thaljī and later theorists, was substantively the Iraqian sunnaic-practice tradition in ḥadīth form.

## Historiography

The concept of sunnaic practice is central to Hallaq's revision of Joseph Schacht's account of Islamic legal origins. Schacht argued that pre-ḥadīth legal practices were "secular" (*administrative and popular practices of the Umayyads*) and that Islamic law only emerged with the link between these practices and formal Prophetic narrative. Hallaq rejects this: the sunnaic practices of the Companions and early caliphs were understood by their practitioners as *religious* from the outset — as embodiments of what Islam meant. That they were inchoate, dynamic, and changing does not make them non-Islamic; dismissing them as secular "amounts to reducing Islam to a monolith."

The implication: the emergence of formal ḥadīth was not a transition from secular to sacred law, but a *formalization and universalization* of already-religious practice, driven by the structural pressures of an expanding empire.
