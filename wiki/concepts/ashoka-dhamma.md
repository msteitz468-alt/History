# Ashoka's Dhamma

## Definition and Scope

**'Dhamma'** (Pali equivalent of the Sanskrit **dharma**) as employed by [[ashoka|Ashoka]] (c. 269–232 BCE) is distinct from the word's other meanings. Whereas dharma in Vedic thought implies 'a sense of universal law or norm, eternal and unchanging,' and in Buddhism Dhamma can refer either to the teachings of the Buddha or to the philosophical nature of things, 'a more practical interpretation of Dhamma was provided by the emperor Ashoka… The Dhamma of Ashoka, or the law of moral virtues, exhorted people to engage themselves in ethical behaviour and good deeds.'

Crucially, Ashoka's Dhamma was **not Buddhism** as such — it was a deliberately non-sectarian ethical program designed to function as 'the common property of all religions,' capable of binding together 'a vast and variegated society of the empire and providing it with a cultural and spiritual value-system.'

## Content of the Dhamma

The Ashokan edicts, inscribed on rocks and pillars across the empire, articulate several interlocking themes:

1. **Non-violence and animal welfare**: Non-violent behaviour 'by humans towards other humans is emphasised again and again.' Ashoka also extended non-violence to animals: he exhorted people to stop killing animals for wanton pleasure at festivals, stipulated days when various classes of animals and fish could not be hunted, and understood that 'an animal must not be fed with the meat of other animals.'
2. **Welfare**: Ashoka secured 'medical help, from home and abroad, for sick humans and animals, building roads and wells, and planting trees,' and built rest houses for travellers. His officers (mahamattras or rajukas) were charged with overseeing agricultural improvements and promoting law and order.
3. **Justice and humanity towards prisoners**: Prisoners 'need to be treated humanely.' Ashoka personally toured the empire to monitor the work of his officers.
4. **Personal self-discipline**: The edicts emphasise 'the hardest thing for a human being was to recognise the evil that was within his or her own self; only when that was achieved could a genuine conversion to a humanist path begin.'
5. **Religious tolerance**: Ashoka desired 'that all sects might dwell harmoniously everywhere, for all seek self-control and purity of mind.' He urged subjects to 'honour other sects and not to disparage other religious viewpoints,' and 'if there is a cause for criticism [against someone's religious outlook] it should be done in a mild way.' He exhorted his officers to be fair to brahmans, Buddhists, Jains, and Ajivakas alike.
6. **Respect for ritual without substituting it for Dhamma**: He acknowledged that at births, marriages, and long journeys people needed to perform religious ceremonies, 'however trivial and useless,' for their own satisfaction, but 'the Dhamma was of greater value than ritualistic religion.'

## Dhamma and Buddhism

Ashoka was unambiguously a Buddhist patron — he embodied the ideal of the **Chakravartin** ('wheel-turner'), a concept largely Buddhist in inspiration that recasts sovereignty from the Vedic warrior-king model to a promoter of peace and prosperity. He supported the Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra (250 BCE), organized missions to the Hellenistic kingdoms, Tibet, Nepal, south India, and Ceylon (his son Mahinda led the Ceylon mission), and proclaimed unity of the Buddhist sangha against schismatics.

However, as Avari stresses, the major corpus of the rock and pillar edicts expounds **Dhamma**, which 'is not a synonym for Buddhism and was not an attempt to make Buddhism a state religion.' It was deliberately 'vague, non-sectarian' and aimed at social cohesion across a religiously plural empire. By 'talking the language of tolerance and understanding, Ashoka makes his Dhamma the common property of all religions.' The Chakravartin ideal — 'unlike the Vedic warrior king, was to be the promoter of peace and prosperity' — introduced a 'new concept of irenical (peaceful) sovereignty that was later incorporated into Hindu kingship.'

## The Edicts as Primary Sources

The Ashokan edicts constitute the **earliest deciphered writing of imperial South Asia**. They fall into three groups:

- **Fourteen major rock edicts**: inscribed on cliffs and hillocks in Prakrit in Brahmi script at Kalsi, Girnar, Sopara, Yerragudi, Dhauli, Jaugada, Sannathi, and Kandahar; at two sites in Pakistan (Shahbazgarhi, Manserah) in Kharoshthi script; a recently found Kandahar edict in Greek and Aramaic.
- **Three minor rock edicts**: found at nearly twenty different sites covering the Deccan, Ganga valley, and Afghanistan.
- **Seven pillar edicts**: in Prakrit/Brahmi, found at Delhi, Allahabad, Buner, Amaravati, and three Bihar sites (Lauriya-Araraj, Lauriya-Nandangarh, Rampurva). Two Delhi pillars were moved from Meerut and Topra by Sultan Firoz Shah Tughluq in the fourteenth century. Additional minor pillar edicts at Lumbini (Rummindei Pillar Inscription) and Kausambi (Schism Edict).

The geographical spread of the edicts 'clearly delineates the extent of the Mauryan Empire.' Notably, no edicts have been found in Punjab, Sind, the deserts of Rajasthan, or the lands immediately south of the Vindhya hills.

The Brahmi script in which most edicts are written was deciphered by **James Prinsep** in the nineteenth century, rescuing knowledge of Ashoka from centuries of obscurity. H.G. Wells's *The Outline of History* (1920) subsequently spread Ashoka's fame widely in English-speaking audiences.

## Effectiveness and Limits — The Paradox of Mauryan Imperialism

Avari frames the Dhamma within what he calls 'the paradox of Mauryan imperialism': the two contradictory but empire-sustaining methods of governance (bureaucratic coercion and moral exhortation) ultimately proved incompatible. Several historical arguments regarding the Dhamma's role in Mauryan decline:

1. **Brahmanical alienation**: 'Historians have also suggested that the overtly Buddhist pieties of Ashoka had made a large mass of people subscribing to the Brahmanic traditions antipathetic to the empire, thereby weakening the internal cohesion of the state.' Avari notes this 'has been challenged on the grounds that Ashoka dealt fairly towards all in his kingdom.'
2. **Military decline**: 'There may be something in another view that holds that Ashoka's non-militarism undermined the strength of the army, leading to disaffection within the ranks.' The last Mauryan emperor was assassinated by his own commander-in-chief.
3. **Vagueness**: Related to the analysis in [[thapar-history-of-india-v1-1966]] — the program was 'too vague,' leaving structural social tensions untouched, and Ashoka's 'officers of Dhamma' hardened into an interfering quasi-priesthood.

## Significance

The Dhamma experiment represents the ancient world's most ambitious state-sponsored program of ethical governance. It pioneered: (a) the use of royal inscription as an instrument of mass communication across a vast territory; (b) a non-sectarian framework for managing religious pluralism in a diverse empire; (c) the Chakravartin model of irenical sovereignty that influenced later Hindu and Buddhist conceptions of kingship across South and Southeast Asia. The lion capital of an Ashokan pillar is the official emblem of the modern Indian republic.

## Historiography

The Dhamma is uniquely well-documented through Ashoka's own edicts — 'contemporary, first-person royal speech.' Scholarly debate centers on: (a) the degree to which Dhamma is sincere personal conviction versus political strategy; (b) whether it represents Buddhist proselytism or genuine non-sectarian ethics; (c) its role in Mauryan decline. Avari's treatment aligns broadly with Thapar's influential distinction between the Buddhist declarations to the sangha (sectarian) and the major public edicts (non-sectarian Dhamma). The Ceylonese Buddhist chronicles, which depict Ashoka as an ardent Buddhist converting the world, are now treated with caution as retrospective religious construction.
