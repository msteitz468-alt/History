# The Ritsuryō State

The ***ritsuryō*** state is the **Chinese-modeled legal-bureaucratic order** that ancient
[[japan|Japan]] built between the [[taika-reform-645|Taika Reform (645)]] and the eighth
century — the formal name for the centralized imperial state of the [[nara-period|Nara]] and
early [[heian-period|Heian]] periods. It takes its name from the two kinds of law it
codified: ***ritsu*** (penal codes) and ***ryō*** (administrative/civil codes). From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v1-ancient|Cambridge History of Japan]] (Inoue, ch. 3; Naoki,
ch. 4).

## The codes

The ritsuryō system was built up in stages on **Tang Chinese** models: the Ōmi code
(traditionally 668), the Asuka Kiyomihara code (689, under Emperor Temmu and Empress Jitō),
and — definitively — the **Taihō Code (701–702)** and its revision the **Yōrō Code (drafted
718, in force 757)**. These were the legal armature of the state the [[taika-reform-645|Four-
Article reforms]] had only sketched.

## Structure

- **The emperor (*tennō*).** Sovereignty was vested in a sacralized emperor whose authority
  was sharpened under **Temmu** (r. 673–686): a new eight-rank **kabane** title system (684)
  reordered the clans around loyalty and blood-proximity to the throne — "government by
  imperial relatives" creating a "new nobility," and grounding legitimacy in
  [[shinto|descent from Amaterasu]] rather than the Chinese virtue-mandate.
- **Two head organs** — uniquely, a **Department of Worship (*Jingikan*)** for [[shinto|Shintō]]
  rites, formally ranking *above* the **Council of State (*Daijōkan*)** that ran the eight
  ministries and general administration.
- **Provincial structure** — ~66 **provinces (*kuni*)** and ~600 **districts (*gun*)**, staffed
  by centrally appointed governors over the old local elites, tracked by a **household census**
  (every six years).
- **Land and tax** — the **handen** ("allotment field") system: agricultural land held in
  theory by the Crown and allotted to households by size, taxed at a low rate but burdened by
  the **soyōchō** levies (grain, products, and labor/cloth) and **military conscription**.

## Significance and decline

The ritsuryō state was the most thoroughgoing attempt to remake Japan on the Chinese imperial
model — direct rule by an emperor through a written-law bureaucracy and publicly taxed,
state-allotted land. But it rested on **hereditary aristocratic office** rather than the
Chinese merit examination, and it was undermined almost from the start by the growth of
tax-exempt private estates (**[[heian-period|shōen]]**) and by [[fujiwara-clan|Fujiwara]]
familism. By the mid-Heian period the codes survived more as a framework of ranks, offices,
and administrative geography than as a living system of direct rule — yet that framework (the
provinces, the court ranks) persisted into the nineteenth century.
