# Nuclear Strategy

**Nuclear strategy** is the attempt to integrate nuclear weapons into purposeful political-military action — to derive usable policy options from arsenals whose actual employment would likely produce catastrophic, possibly existential, destruction for all parties. As Lawrence Freedman frames the central problem: whether "any useful purpose could be served by employment of devices which invited discussion using words such as 'holocaust', 'doomsday' and 'armageddon'" and whether "any employment could be sufficiently deliberate and controlled to ensure that political objectives were met."

The very phrase is frequently described as paradoxical or contradictory. The weapons' scale and speed collapse traditional distinctions between strategy and tactics, offence and defence, and war and peace. Their introduction did not render prior airpower theory obsolete; early nuclear thinking was "derived from a prior era" — above all the interwar doctrine of [[concepts/strategic-bombardment|strategic bombardment]].

## Core Tensions

- **Deterrence vs. war-fighting.** From the outset (Brodie after Hiroshima), many concluded the chief purpose of nuclear forces must be to avert their use. Others continued to seek ways to fight, limit, or prevail in nuclear war (counterforce, damage limitation, escalation control).
- **Credibility and the rationality of irrationality.** Threats that would be suicidal to execute are inherently incredible. Schelling's solution involved manipulating shared risk ("the threat that leaves something to chance") and committing to courses that would be irrational to complete once begun.
- **Stability vs. advantage.** The search for a stable balance (second-strike survivability, mutual vulnerability) constantly collided with incentives to gain a usable edge (first-strike disarming capability, active defence, counterforce targeting).
- **Extended deterrence.** Protecting allies with nuclear threats is harder than deterring direct attack on the homeland; the "Nth country problem" and European debates (British, French, German, MLF) turned on this asymmetry.

## Major Doctrinal Phases (Freedman framing, with Range 02 detail)

1. **Atomic monopoly and early stalemate** (1945–early 1950s): Bomb as ultimate extension of strategic airpower; limits quickly apparent; Soviet "permanently operating factors" vs. US technological edge. Soviet doctrine allotted only a limited role to strategic bombardment and treated Western theories with derision. "Soviet military science considers that the outcome of war under contemporary conditions is decided on the field of battle by means of the annihilation of the armed forces of the enemy..." Official view: air strikes on deep rear were "only a helpful means" not an end in themselves. The Soviet atom bomb test of August 1949 broke the monopoly and locked the United States into a nuclear strategy. (Freedman 1981: "STRATEGY FOR AN ATOMIC MONOPOLY" and "Strategy for an Atomic Stalemate").

2. **Massive retaliation** (1950s): Dulles/New Look — reliance on nuclear threats to deter both nuclear and conventional aggression at lower cost than large conventional forces. NSC-162/2 (Oct 1953) required "emphasis on the capability for inflicting massive retaliatory damage" and declared nuclear weapons "as available for use as other munitions". British influence via 1952 Chiefs of Staff paper stressing "immediate and crushing retaliation" and priority for long-range air forces. (Freedman 1981: "Massive Retaliation" / "The New Look").

## Formal Strategists and Stable Conflict (Range 05)

The "Formal Strategists" (including Schelling, Kahn) applied game theory, Prisoner's Dilemma, and chicken analogies. Schelling reframed strategy around "the power to hurt" as bargaining power and "latent violence": "Military strategy could no longer be the science of military victory. Rather it would be the art of coercion, intimidation and deterrence." Stable balance of terror as "exchange of hostages." Stability criterion favoured invulnerable, non-counterforce-accurate second-strike weapons. (Freedman 1981, p.193–195).

Arms control emerged as "managing rather than eliminating the arms race," an "enlargement of our military strategy" resting on recognition of "incomplete antagonism" and "induced or reciprocated 'self-control'." Central theme: stability. (ARMS CONTROL chapter).

Bargaining and escalation theory explored how limited nuclear use and controlled escalation could serve political ends via signaling, proportionality, focal points/saliencies, and "the threat that leaves something to chance." Kahn developed the 44-rung escalation ladder with firebreaks and escalation dominance. Schelling emphasized competition in risk-taking and reputation in the game of Chicken. (BARGAINING AND ESCALATION chapter).

City-avoidance under McNamara explored counterforce for damage limitation while preserving bargaining leverage and second-strike reserves before shift in emphasis toward assured destruction. (CITY-AVOIDANCE chapter).

## Critiques and Historiography

Nuclear strategy remained contested because no empirical test of general nuclear war has occurred since 1945. Models abstracted from politics, assumed excessive control and rationality, and struggled with the moral horror of the threatened (or planned) consequences. Freedman notes the cyclical rediscovery of ideas and the persistent gap between elegant theory and the chaotic realities of command, politics, and adversary perceptions.
