# Comitatus

**The comitatus** (Tacitus's term; *Gefolgschaft* in modern German scholarship) was the
Germanic warrior band: a body of young free men who attached themselves to a prestigious
leader, were equipped and maintained by him, and were bound to fight to the death on his
behalf. It is one of the central institutions through which historians have understood
early Germanic society and the lord–retainer bond that runs forward into medieval vassalage.

## Definition and Scope

Per [[geary-before-france-germany-1988|Geary]]'s synthesis of the evidence (chiefly Tacitus, 55–116/120 CE),
the comitatus was **not the fundamental military unit of the tribe**. It was an individual
warrior society organised for "constant plunder and fighting" — a personal following whose
existence could endanger both the internal peace of the tribe and truces with neighbours.
The youths "entered into close, personal bonds with their leader, who was responsible for
providing for them, equipping them, and leading them to victory and booty."

The bond was reciprocal and asymmetric: the leader owed maintenance, arms, and the
opportunity for glory and booty; the retainer owed loyalty unto death.

## Causal Drivers

The comitatus was sustained by an economy of **prestige goods and raiding**. Warfare and
raiding — primarily for cattle and slaves — were "the supreme source of prestige and the
central organizing principle of Germanic economy and society." Allied to this was the
institution of **gift-giving**: gifts appeared voluntary but were obligatory, and prestige
accrued to the *giver*, who thereby displayed superiority and placed the receiver in his
debt — the same logic that structured the lord–retainer relationship.

## Relation to ethnogenesis

The comitatus was a **nucleus of [[ethnogenesis]]**. Successful warrior leaders acquired
prestige and power through the size of their following; tensions within a tribe could cause
a warrior band and its dependents to splinter off and form a new tribe. The institution thus
helped drive the constant fission and fusion of Germanic peoples — see [[frankish-ethnogenesis]]
and [[gothic-peoples]]. As constant warfare promoted war-leaders (*reiks*, *Heerkönig*) over
sacral kings, the cult of Woden as giver of victory tended to displace older fertility cults,
and "anyone who fought with them was a member of their tribe, regardless of previous ethnic,
linguistic, political, or cultic origins."

## Limitations and Critiques

The reliance on Tacitus is itself the central problem. As Geary stresses (see [[ethnogenesis]]),
scholars have too readily applied a **first-century** ethnographic description directly to the
Goths, Franks, and Burgundians of the fourth and fifth centuries — across half a millennium of
profound change. The comitatus as Tacitus describes it should be read as a model of one moment,
not a fixed essence of "the Germanic," and its later forms were reshaped by Roman military
service ([[barbarization-of-the-roman-army]]) and by settlement inside the empire.
