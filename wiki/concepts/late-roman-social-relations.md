# Late Roman Social Relations

The structure of **fourth-century society** — its sharpened hierarchy, its real (if changed) social
mobility, its systems of patronage, and the new place of the poor. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH
XIII]] Ch. 11 (**Arnaldo Marcone**). Marcone's organizing argument resists both the old "caste-system"
model and any rosy picture of fluidity: late-antique society was a **society in transition**, intensely
**vertical** yet still genuinely **mobile**.

## Hierarchy without caste

The most obvious feature is **verticality** — a "marked hierarchization" conditioning thought and every
social relation, with the state, rebuilt after the third-century crisis, tying ever-broader categories of
people (and their children) to their occupations to serve the army and the growing bureaucracy. But
Marcone rejects the "**caste system**" reading: state coercion **coexisted with new channels of social
mobility** opening unexpected routes to advancement — through the imperial service, the army, and the
church. Mobility "was a reality... alongside a policy of coercion," even if its routes differed from
earlier centuries.

## The remade ruling class

A defining change was the **effective destruction of the equestrian order** — not abolished by law but
**absorbed de facto into the enlarged senatorial class** (see [[late-roman-senate]]), with a new internal
hierarchy fixed from **Valentinian I**. The sharp old line between equestrians and the rest now ran between
the **expanded senatorial order** (and, locally, the **[[late-roman-cities|curiales]]**) and everyone
below — the undifferentiated *plebs* (propertyless, artisans, smallholders, tied tenants), too
heterogeneous to call a "class" in the Marxist sense. Hierarchy was inscribed in **unequal treatment before
the law** (punishments graded by rank, the *honestiores*/*humiliores* divide).

## From civic euergetism to Christian charity

Marcone's richest theme is the transformation of **giving**. Classical **euergetism** aimed at *citizens*
as such — public buildings, games and doles that bought prestige and political advancement, distributed
within the civic body (the Roman *plebs* fed "because they were citizens, not because they were poor";
foreigners expelled in famines). **Christian charity** broke with this: directed by **universalistic
ideology** toward groups previously neglected by their *material need* — widows, the sick, prisoners,
foreigners — and administered by the **bishop** from the donations of the faithful. The new institutions
even required **brand-new words** (almshouses, orphanages, hospitals — unattested in classical Greek or
Latin). After Constantine the two converged: local elites who once gave baths now financed **churches**,
and the **bishop became a patron**, taking on the magistrate's traditional responsibilities of protection
and assistance.

## Patronage and *patrocinium*

The same dynamic of protection ran through secular society. As central fiscal pressure mounted, the weak
sought the shelter of the powerful — the shift Marcone (with the chapter's later sections) traces from
ordinary **patronage** to ***patrocinium***: great landowners extending protection (against tax-collectors
and rivals) to peasants and villages, in a way that **drained the state's authority** and, in the long
run, "constituted a factor in the disintegration of the state" (Salvian's poor "preferring barbarians to
Roman tax-collectors"). Society "responded with its own mechanisms of self-defence" to the instruments of
state oppression.

## Significance

Marcone's chapter supplies the **social-relational** dimension of CAH XIII's anti-"decline" reading: the
late-Roman world was not a frozen caste order sliding into collapse, but a **steeply hierarchical society
in genuine transition** — its ruling class remade around imperial service, its old civic euergetism giving
way to Christian charity and the patronage of bishops and magnates, its weak finding protection outside the
state. The cumulative effect — the growth of *patrocinium* and local power at the expense of the centre —
was less a "fall" than a **redistribution of authority** away from the imperial state.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **Citizenship and Romanitas.** Caracalla’s 212 Constitutio Antoniniana extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants, helping forge third-century ideological unity that enabled tetrarchic *Romanitas*. Citizenship itself no longer marked distinction, but majority subjecthood under Roman law underpinned Augustine’s *City of God* contrast with the heavenly kingdom. Amid mid-fifth-century ethnic confusion, “nothing defined *Romanitas* more surely than its written laws” (Priscus’s debate with the Greek-turned-Hun; Orosius on Romans preferring poor freedom among barbarians).
- **Orthodoxy as unifying criterion.** Orthodox Catholic belief joined citizenship; Justinianic army contingents kept ethnic labels (Heruls, Moors, Huns, Armenians, Isaurians) yet were “Romans” flexibly defined, with Chalcedonian Christianity the shared critical quality (Greatrex via Mitchell).
- **Merit/service elite.** Late empire favored merit/service over pure lineage (Justin from illiterate peasant via palace guard). Justinian’s elite mixed Thracian soldiers, Greek legal literati, Cappadocian and Mesopotamian officials, Armenian Narses—held by loyalty to Roman state and shared Christian belief. Extended governing elite ~30,000 empire-wide drawing on Hellenic *paideia* (shrinking relevance), Roman law (favored career path), biblical/clerical literacy, and rising popular culture. Church hierarchy valued poverty, asceticism, holiness alongside secular ranks.
- **State-service elite and privatization of force.** Rising force: salaried officials, soldiers, Constantinopolitan aristocracy displacing curial middle government, building large estates via gold salaries and patronage. Parallel “privatization of force” (*bucellarii*, estate militias) East and West when official protection fails (Leo’s 474 ban on estate armed slaves/*bucellarii*/Isaurians).
