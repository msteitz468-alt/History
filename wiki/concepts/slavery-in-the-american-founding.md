# Slavery in the American Founding

## Definition and Scope

The central paradox of the American Revolution and Constitution: the generation that proclaimed "all men are created equal" and inalienable rights to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness simultaneously entrenched chattel slavery in the new nation's founding documents and territorial expansion. This includes the Declaration's silence, the Constitution's three-fifths clause, fugitive slave provision, and protection of the slave trade until 1808, plus the failure to couple independence with emancipation.

## Emic vs Etic

Etic (historians' term): "the great contradiction," "tragic side of the founding." Emic (founders' language): many privately acknowledged the inconsistency (Jefferson's "wolf by the ear"; Washington's post-war regrets) while publicly defending compromises as necessary for union or deferring the issue to posterity.

## Limitations and Critiques

- Not all founders were slaveholders; some (e.g. certain Quakers, Franklin late in life) opposed.
- The Revolution did produce the first large wave of manumissions in the Upper South and gradual emancipation in New England and mid-Atlantic.
- Indian removal is the second "tragic" failure often paired with slavery in this analysis (see Knox "prior occupants" policy, McGillivray, Treaty of New York 1790, and demographic defeat of federal diplomacy).
- Counterfactuals: was ending slavery politically possible in 1776-1787 without destroying the union? Scholars differ. Ellis: "no document that met our modern-day standard of social justice... could ever have been passed or ratified."

## Ellis (2025) Framing — "Ghost at the Banquet" and Window Closed

At the Convention, "Total silence on the slavery question was itself a loud statement that slavery was a taboo topic." The four compromises (three-fifths giving southern advantage and "Negro president" in 1800; slave trade to 1808 with >200,000 imports; fugitive slave passed unanimously; territorial split) made union possible only by deferral. Franklin (read by Wilson): "I expect no better, and I am not sure it is not the best." "It was more like a marriage ceremony..."

The 1790 petitions (Quakers + PA Abolition Society, Franklin's last public act) and House debate ended with Madison's maneuvers producing a resolution that "Congress have no authority to interfere in the emancipation of slaves..." The 1790 census (US slaves ~694k, up sharply; Virginia pivotal with 292k slaves) showed the window "was not opening, but closing." "The Madisonian maneuver had transformed twenty years into forever... seventy years, and the final purging required the bloodiest war in American history."

Parallel tragedy: Indian policy. Knox: tribes "ought to be considered as foreign nations... prior occupants..." Treaties as equals, guarantees, agriculture transition. McGillivray (mixed Creek leader, plantation/slave owner) negotiated 1790 New York treaty with land guarantees and secret articles. "Settlers kept pouring across... like stopping a flood with a bucket of sponges." "In the end, demography trumped diplomacy." "Indian removal was the inevitable consequence of unbridled democracy in action."
