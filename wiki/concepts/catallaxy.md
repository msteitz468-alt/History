# Catallaxy (The Market Order)

**Etic** coinage by F. A. Hayek (*Law, Legislation and Liberty* II, 1976) for the market order considered as a [[concepts/spontaneous-order|spontaneous order]] rather than an "economy": "A catallaxy is thus the special kind of spontaneous order produced by the market through people acting within the rules of the law of property, tort and contract."

## Etymology and the economy/catallaxy distinction

From the Greek verb *katallattein* (*katallassein*), "which meant, significantly, not only 'to exchange' but also 'to admit into the community' and 'to change from enemy into friend'" — Hayek forms the noun *katallaxia* → "catallaxy" (the order) alongside the older "catallactics" (the science of exchange). The point of the neologism is to break the organizational connotation of "economy": an economy proper (household, farm, firm) allocates given means across a *single* hierarchy of ends; "what is commonly called a social or national economy is in this sense not a single economy but a network of many interlaced economies" with no common scale of ends. Calling the catallaxy an "economy" smuggles in the assumption that it should be run like one — the linguistic root, for Hayek, of planning's plausibility.

## The game of catallaxy

The market process is "wholly analogous to a game, namely a game partly of skill and partly of chance … the game of catallaxy. It is a wealth-creating game (and not what game theory calls a zero-sum game)," fitting the OED's definition of a game as "a contest played according to rules and decided by superior skill, strength or good fortune." Fairness attaches to the rules and conduct of play, never to the score — the hinge to Vol. 2's critique of [[concepts/social-justice]].

Mechanics (the mature statement of the [[concepts/knowledge-problem]]):

- **Prices as knowledge relays.** "The manufacturer does not produce shoes because he knows that Jones needs them. He produces because he knows that dozens of traders will buy certain numbers at various prices" — knowledge of thousands of unknown Joneses reaches him only as a price. Producers switch aluminum for magnesium on relative prices alone, without knowing why they moved.
- **Signals, not rewards.** Remunerations "tell them what in their own as well as in general interest they ought to do," which is why they cannot track merit.
- **Adaptation requires losses.** "The order of the Great Society does rest and must rest on constant undesigned frustrations of some efforts"; when conditions change, "bygones are forever bygones — the past conditions tell us nothing about what is appropriate now." The law protects domains, not values: rules of just conduct "can enable us merely to determine which particular things belong to particular persons, but not what these things will be worth."
- **Policy criterion.** Since there is no common ranking of ends, rational policy "cannot aim at a known maximum of particular results, but must aim at increasing, for any person picked out at random, the prospects" of achieving his unknown ends. "Interference" in the classical sense — "an isolated act of coercion, undertaken for the purpose of achieving a particular result" outside the general rules — "is, therefore, always an unjust act."

## The Great Society as means-connected

The catallaxy is what holds an extended society of strangers together. The decisive civilizational step "was the adoption of barter or exchange," which requires agreement only on rules of property and transfer, not on purposes; peace in the Great Society rests on "agreement only on means and not on ends." Hayek presses the unsentimental corollary: "Most people are still reluctant to accept the fact that it should be the disdained 'cash-nexus' which holds the Great Society together," and acknowledges that "many people regard it as revolting that the Great Society has no common concrete purposes … it is merely means-connected and not ends-connected" — for Hayek its great merit, since it is what makes individual freedom possible. The genealogy he offers runs back through the Stoics and Cicero (public utility = justice), medieval *utilitas publica* (peace and justice), and Harrington's "empire of laws and not of men." The general good in such an order "consists principally in the facilitation of the pursuit of unknown individual purposes"; the public interest cannot be a sum of private interests, since "neither those nor all the circumstances determining them can be known to government or anybody else."

Hayek also adopts Oakeshott's terms: a **teleocratic** order (single binding hierarchy of ends) is necessarily an organization; a **nomocratic** society forms a spontaneous order — mapping onto his own taxis/cosmos and [[concepts/nomos-and-thesis|thesis/nomos]] pairs.

## Limitations and critiques

- The economy/catallaxy line presupposes that firms and households are "true" economies while the whole is not — critics note large firms internally face exactly the dispersed-knowledge problems Hayek reserves for the catallaxy (the Coasean boundary question).
- The game analogy imports normative weight (rules fair → outcomes acceptable) that opponents of the mirage argument reject; see the counters recorded at [[concepts/social-justice]].
- The term itself never displaced "economy" even among sympathizers; its main afterlife is in Austrian-school and constitutional political economy literature (Buchanan's "catallactics").
