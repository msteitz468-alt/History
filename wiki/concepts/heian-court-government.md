# Heian Court Government

Heian court government refers to the institutional structure and practical operations
of the Japanese imperial government from the founding of [[heian-kyo|Heian-kyō]] in
794 through approximately 1070, encompassing the decline of the formal
[[ritsuryo-state|ritsuryō statutory framework]] and its replacement by a more
personal, court-centered system of rule combining statutory offices with new informal
institutions and, ultimately, Fujiwara regency power. [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]]

## The Statutory Foundation

The [[ritsuryo-state|ritsuryō]] framework provided the formal legal structure. At
the apex stood the **Council of State** (*Daijōkan*), which was the highest executive
organ of government, directing eight ministries and issuing directives throughout the
administrative hierarchy. Below it were approximately sixty-six provinces (*kuni*),
subdivided into districts (*gun*), each staffed by centrally appointed governors over
local elites.

At the very beginning of the Heian period, "the major business of government seems
still to have been conducted as prescribed in the statutory code, the emperor and his
ministers gathering each morning to attend to business in governmental councils
(*chōsei*) at the Court of Government (*Chōdōin*)." This morning council system
began atrophying under Saga (r. 809–823) and by the early ninth century "may have
already so dwindled in quantity and scope that they were becoming anachronistic in
any case." By the beginning of Saga's reign they had been relegated to biannual rituals.

## The Chamberlains' Office (Kurōdodokoro)

The key institutional innovation of the early Heian period was the **Chamberlains'
Office** (*Kurōdodokoro*, "Repositors' Office"), established by Emperor Saga in or
about 810, initially to handle legal disputes, maintain the integrity of imperial
documents, and secure the emperor's personal safety during the Kusuko Incident.

Its functions and powers expanded rapidly, especially under Emperors Kōkō (r. 884–887)
and Uda (r. 887–897), until it became "a veritable pivot of court government." The
office:
- Took over the Council of State's functions regarding communication with the emperor
  and the issuance of imperial orders, decrees, and rescripts
- Bypassed the cumbersome Council mechanisms, giving the emperor direct access to
  operating offices
- Administered many of the emperor's personal and household affairs
- Was staffed by men personally close to the emperor, often from his household as
  crown prince

The joining of state and household functions in a single office located at the
emperor's *residential* palace "further blurred the distinction between the imperial
position and the imperial person." Although the Chamberlains' Office began as an
instrument of imperial power, the Fujiwara eventually gained control of it too.

## Guard-Post Judgments (Jinnosadame)

From the latter half of the ninth century, major policy discussions took the form
of **Guard-Post Judgments** (*jinnosadame*) — meetings of Council of State ministers
held at one of the posts of the Imperial Guards in the residential compound, convened
two or three times monthly through the Chamberlains' Office. The process was:

1. Senior minister present convened discussion of specific problems
2. Decisions made after deliberation among ministers
3. Reported back through the Chamberlains' Office as recommendations to the emperor
   or regent
4. Emperor or regent caused necessary orders to be issued

Business covered the full range of court concerns: imperial accessions, foreign
relations, military revolts, appointments, Buddhist and Shintō rites, tax matters,
and provincial government. Guard-Post Judgments remained a vital instrument of
court decision-making "into the latter half of the eleventh century."

## The Kebiishi (Offenses Investigation Agents)

The safety of [[heian-kyo|Kyoto]] was chronically precarious from the early ninth
century. Probably during Saga's reign, a new imperial police force — the ***kebiishi***
("Offenses Investigation Agents") — was established at the emperor's residential
palace. Initially restricted to police work within Kyoto, by 834 (when it became an
independent entity) its powers had expanded to encompass:

- Nearly all police and judicial authority throughout Kyoto and its environs
- Administration of city prisons
- Resolution of disputes concerning agricultural land
- Collection of unpaid land rents
- Establishment of *shōen* (estate) boundaries
- Later: some street repair and maintenance duties

The *kebiishi* were soon imitated in the provinces and remained "a powerful and
ubiquitous force in Heian life through the eleventh century." Their success represented
"a rationalization of the statutory structure that brought together under a single
authority police, judicial, and prison functions that had formerly been divided among
several offices: the Board of Censors, the Capital Offices, the Punishments Ministry,
and the Imperial Guards." However, this rationalization was also "another instance of
the concentration of governmental authority in and around the person of the emperor
and the narrowing of governmental concern to the immediate interests of the court."

## The Fujiwara Regency within the Governmental System

The [[fujiwara-regency|Fujiwara regency]] (*sesshō/kampaku*) was, in the view of
most modern historians cited in the *Cambridge History*, "not the significant
distinguishing characteristic of the period but simply an adjustment in, or relatively
limited transformation of, the central government mechanism centering on the Council
of State." What marked a genuinely new phase was the change in policies toward
provincial administration and taxation at the beginning of the tenth century.

Three regental titles operated within the court governmental framework:
- ***Sesshō***: regent to a minor emperor; could act *as* the emperor in approving
  documents and conducting ceremonies
- ***Kampaku***: regent to an adult emperor; acted for the emperor specifically in
  dealings with the Council of State
- ***Nairan*** ("private inspection"): similar powers to kampaku but with the additional
  advantage of direct participation in Council business; preferred by powerful politicians
  like Michinaga for that reason

The regency was an *ad hoc* arrangement without legal basis in the statutory code,
requiring reappointment by each succeeding emperor.

## Provincial Administration

A key transformation of the tenth century was the shift from the old ritsuryō
system of direct central control over provincial districts to a new arrangement in
which the **provincial governor's office** was given near-autonomous authority to
collect and remit a fixed revenue quota, retaining a portion for its own use and
disbursing the rest to the center. This made provincial appointments highly lucrative,
turning the governor into "a common metaphor in the contemporary literature for
ostentatious new wealth." It also made appointment power — largely in the regent's
hands — a major source of political leverage and personal income.

The subprovincial district (*gun*) gradually ceased to function as an independent
administrative unit in most areas outside the east, replaced by smaller units called
*betsumyō* directly subordinated to the governor's office. Statutory tax names also
disappeared from use by the mid-eleventh century, by which time the fiscal
vocabulary of the ritsuryō system had largely given way to new conventions.

## Fiscal Pressures and Structural Response

The central government of the early Heian period faced a worsening financial
situation driven by:
- Overspending on capital construction (Nagaoka, Heian-kyō) and Emishi campaigns
- Decay of the allotment-land system and growth of tax-exempt private estates (*[[shoen|shōen]]*)
- Emergence in the provinces of a wealthy class that central authorities could not fully
  control or tax

Responses included: constant adjustment of tax laws; reduction of the central
bureaucracy (by the end of the ninth century roughly half the offices provided for
by statute had been abolished); reduction of the imperial clan's size; and beginning
in 879, the dedication of specific land parcels as revenue sources for particular
central offices — a shift toward the privatization of institutional finance that made
some offices "in essential respects institutional equivalents of the *shōen* proprietors."

## Relationship to the Ritsuryō Framework

The *Cambridge History* emphasizes that the statutory framework — the provinces,
the court ranks, the offices — did not disappear. It persisted as the formal
architecture within which all political action was framed: "the court, with its
regularly established offices, its fixed procedures and its documents, remained both
the formal and, for the most part, the actual center of government." The regency
and its innovations operated within and depended on the legitimacy derived from
that statutory order. The result was a hybrid: a statutory shell housing an increasingly
personal and privatized mode of rule.
