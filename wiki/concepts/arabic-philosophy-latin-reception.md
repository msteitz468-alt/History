## Definition and Scope

The reception of Arabic philosophy into the Latin West refers to the multi-century process by which the philosophical and scientific literature composed in Arabic — including Arabic translations of Greek works, original Arabic falsafa, and Arabic commentaries on Aristotle — was translated into Latin and incorporated into the curricula of Western European universities and monastic schools. The process was neither centrally organized nor instantaneous: it unfolded in overlapping waves from approximately 1080 to 1550 CE, with Toledo as its most important institutional center and the scholastic universities of Paris, Oxford, and Cologne as its principal reception sites.

"Arabic texts contributed massively to the building up of a coherent curriculum of Aristotelian philosophy … which was to remain at the center of university training for many centuries to come." (Burnett, in [[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]], ch. 18)

The fact that these texts issued from Muslim lands "did not cause a problem. They were simply the best texts available, and Averroes provided the most dependable and comprehensive commentaries on Aristotle's works." (Burnett, ch. 18)

## The Toledo Translation Enterprise

Toledo, reconquered by Alfonso VI of Castile in 1085, became the preeminent center of translation from Arabic into Latin for several structural reasons:
- It was the metropolitan cultural capital of Iberia, with Arabic as the dominant language of its inhabitants and extensive libraries of Arabic manuscripts.
- It provided refuge to Jewish scholars fleeing the Almohad takeover of al-Andalus (after 1147), supplying an essential layer of bilingual intermediaries.
- It attracted Latin clergy from across Europe seeking access to philosophical and scientific texts.

Two overlapping translation programs operated simultaneously, associated with [[gerard-of-cremona]] (c. 1114–87) and [[dominicus-gundisalvi]] (fl. 1162–90):

**The Aristotelian program (Gerard of Cremona):** Gerard translated Aristotle's works on natural philosophy together with their Arabic commentators — al-Kindī, al-Fārābī, and later Averroes — following the curricular template laid out in al-Fārābī's *On the Classification of the Sciences*, which he also translated. The program covered logic (*Posterior Analytics*), physics, *De Caelo*, *De Generatione et Corruptione*, and the *Meteora*. Gerard's pupils continued the series after his death.

**The Avicennian program (Gundisalvi):** Directed largely by Andalusian Jewish scholars, especially Abraham ibn Dāʾūd ("Avendauth"), this program translated Avicenna's *al-Shifāʾ* (*The Healing*), including the sections on psychology (*On the Soul*) and metaphysics, as well as works by al-Ghazālī, Ibn Gabirol (*Fons Vitae*), and al-Fārābī's works on intellectual philosophy. This introduced into Latin a stream of Neoplatonic Avicennian metaphysics distinct from the straightforwardly Aristotelian corpus.

> "In Toledo, we can see, running parallel, first, a program of translating Aristotle with his Arabic commentators, inaugurated by Gerard of Cremona … Second, there is a program of translating works of the Avicennian tradition, favored by Jewish scholars in Islamic Spain, directed by Dominicus Gundisalvi." (Burnett, ch. 18)

The role of Jewish scholars as bilingual intermediaries was structural: Alfred of Shareshill acknowledged a debt to "the Jew Solomon"; Michael Scot relied on "Abuteus" when translating al-Biṭrūjī; Avendauth collaborated with Gundisalvi on the translation of Avicenna's *On the Soul*.

## Phases of Reception

### Phase 1: Southern Italy and Early Transmission (c. 1080–1130)

The earliest transmission occurred not at Toledo but in southern Italy, via Constantine the African (d. c. 1087), who arrived from Tunisia at the abbey of Montecassino with Arabic medical texts from Qayrawān. His translations — including Isaac Israeli's medical and physical writings — introduced Arabic philosophical concepts into Latin through the channel of medicine. William of Conches and Bernardus Silvestris drew on these translations for their own philosophy of nature.

### Phase 2: The Toledan Translations (c. 1140–1220)

The systematic translation of the full Aristotelian corpus from Arabic, together with the Avicennian parallel program, occurred in Toledo during this period. This phase also produced the *De Causis* (a Proclean Neoplatonic text transmitted as Pseudo-Aristotle by Gerard of Cremona), which was incorporated into the Aristotelian curriculum and remained influential until Aquinas identified its true source using William of Moerbeke's translation of Proclus's *Elements of Theology*.

Michael Scot, working slightly later (before 1220 at Toledo, then at the court of Frederick II), translated Averroes' Long Commentaries on *De Caelo* and Aristotle's *On Animals*, bringing the Averroist commentary tradition to Latin readers and initiating what became [[latin-averroism]].

### Phase 3: The Thirteenth Century — Integration and Controversy

In the thirteenth century "the barrier between Arabic and Latin scholarship was more porous than it had ever been." (Burnett, ch. 18) Arabic philosophical texts circulated in European cathedral schools and the new universities. Scholars such as Albertus Magnus incorporated Arabic sources — including untranslated texts by al-Fārābī — through "seepage through a porous wall." Hermann the German (fl. 1240–56) translated Averroes' *Middle Commentary on the Poetics* and the *Summa Alexandrinorum* (a compendium of the *Nicomachean Ethics*), extending the Arabic–Latin curriculum beyond natural philosophy into ethics and literary theory.

Giles of Rome's *Errores Philosophorum* (c. 1270) illustrates the depth of Arabic philosophical penetration: all the philosophers he criticized by name were Arabic or wrote in Arabic (including Maimonides), with Aristotle as the sole exception.

The Neoplatonic element in the Arabic inheritance is essential to understanding its Latin impact. The Arabic tradition delivered "not the 'pure' Aristotle of the fourth century B.C.E., but rather, as Cristina D'Ancona has shown, the late Neoplatonic curriculum, in which Aristotle's metaphysics was crowned with a rational theology issuing from the Platonic tradition." (Burnett, ch. 18) Hence the easy incorporation of the *De Causis* into the Aristotelian corpus.

### Phase 4: Late Medieval and Renaissance (c. 1300–1560)

From the fourteenth century onward, direct translation from Arabic declined and was increasingly replaced by translation from Hebrew intermediaries, as Jewish scholars (expelled from Spain in 1492 and available in Italy) were more accessible to Christian humanists than Arabic speakers. Jacob Mantino (d. 1549), the most prolific such translator, revised existing Averroes translations and produced new ones for the Giuntine edition of Aristotle with Averroes' commentaries (Venice, 1550–52) — eleven folio volumes representing the culmination of the Arabic–Latin philosophical tradition.

The Giuntine edition appeared just as two countercurrents were strengthening: the humanist turn toward Greek originals, and the professionalization of Arabic studies (with the founding of an Arabic press in Rome in 1584). From the mid-sixteenth century, Arabic philosophy gradually ceased to be part of the European academic mainstream and became instead the object of Orientalist scholarship.

## Relationship to Jewish Philosophy

The reception of Arabic philosophy into the Latin West was inseparable from its prior reception into Jewish philosophy. See [[jewish-philosophy-in-islam]] for the Hebrew-language tradition. The Toledo translations depended structurally on Jewish intermediaries; Renaissance translators relied on Hebrew versions of Arabic texts (see [[latin-averroism]] for the specifically Averroist dimension). The expulsion of Jews from Spain (1492) redirected this human capital to Italy, where it powered the Giuntine edition and related projects.

[[gersonides]]'s supercommentaries on Averroes' commentaries — composed in Hebrew in 1321–24 — were themselves translated into Latin during the Renaissance, representing a third-order transmission: Aristotle → Averroes → Gersonides → Latin.

## Impact on Scholasticism

The Arabic philosophical transmission reshaped Latin intellectual culture in at least four ways:

1. **Curriculum:** It supplied the full Aristotelian *libri naturales* (natural philosophy books), which became the backbone of the university Arts curriculum, supplementing the traditional seven liberal arts.
2. **Metaphysics:** Via Avicenna and the *De Causis*, it delivered a Neoplatonically inflected metaphysics — the doctrine of emanation, the distinction between essence and existence, the concept of the Necessary Being — that shaped the theological metaphysics of Aquinas, Duns Scotus, and their successors.
3. **Psychology:** Avicenna's systematic account of the five inner senses and the faculty psychology of the soul became standard in Latin anthropology, offering what "Aristotle was thought to have failed to discuss." (Burnett, ch. 18)
4. **Controversy:** Averroes' interpretation of the intellect as a single entity shared by all humans generated the controversy of [[unity-of-intellect]] and the condemnations of 1270 and 1277. See [[latin-averroism]].

## Historiography

The field of Arabic–Latin philosophical transmission was shaped by three generations of scholarship. Marie-Thérèse d'Alverny's work on Avicenna in Latin remains foundational. Charles Burnett (ch. 18 of the Companion) has documented the Toledan programs comprehensively. Richard Lemay established Abu Maʿshar's role in conveying Aristotelian physics via astrological texts. Cristina D'Ancona has elucidated the Neoplatonic dimension of the transmitted corpus. Dag Nikolaus Hasse's work on Arabic sciences and philosophy in the Renaissance documents the late phase.

The collection (Bibliotheca Alexandrina) is moderate-to-strong on this topic for the medieval period via the Cambridge histories (NCMH) and specialist series (Brill/Routledge). The source [[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]] (ch. 18, Charles Burnett) provides the most systematic recent synthesis and the translation table used above. The Renaissance phase remains thinner in the collection.
