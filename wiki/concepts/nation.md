# Nation

The **nation**, in Azar Gat and Alexander Yakobson's usage (*Nations*, 2013;
[[sources/gat-nations-2013]]), is the politicized form of an [[ethnicity|ethnos]] —
a people predominantly defined by a shared kin–culture identity that has achieved,
or aspires to, its own statehood. Gat's working formula is that of "nationhood
generally meaning statehood for a people predominantly defined in terms of a shared
kin–culture identity." He "generally accept[s]" the definition of [[nationalism|nationalist]]
theorist Ernest Gellner, that nationhood entails "a congruence of culture or ethnicity
and state" — a congruence "which is never perfect, of course."

The nation is therefore neither identical to the [[ethnicity|ethnos]] nor identical
to the state, but the **conjunction** of the two: a state "roughly encompass[ing] and
remain[ing] largely confined to an entire generally distinct ethnic space." Most ethne
never achieved statehood and so never became nations; most states arose within, and
depended upon, a preexisting ethnic space. The nation is the comparatively rare case
where "a rough congruence between an ethnos and a state occurred."

## From Tribes to Statehood

Chapter 3 of *Nations* reconstructs the path by which an ethnic substratum is built up
and then politicized — the long road "from tribes to statehood." Gat distinguishes the
graduated levels:

- The **tribe** — a kin-based social entity of "anywhere between two thousand to a few
  tens of thousands of people," structured segmentarily (family → clan/*genos* →
  fraternity/*phratria* → tribe). Tribes were neither "primordial" nor static: new
  tribes "branched out and evolved," split, were "dispersed, eliminated, or absorbed."
- The **ethnos** — a wider ethnic space whose members "shared kin–cultural attributes,
  but, unlike tribal groupings, had little or no ties that would make them a social
  entity." Ethne grew through three mechanisms: agricultural expansion (the Bantu, the
  Neolithic spread into Europe, the rice cultivators who became the Japanese, the Slavs),
  pastoralist "elite dominance" expansion (Semitic, Indo-European, Turkic, Arabic), and
  the leveling action of states themselves.
- The **petty-state / city-state**, then the **larger territorial state** — emerging as
  property accumulation, "chief" and "big man" status, chiefdoms, and finally state
  bureaucracy eroded the kin-tribe and replaced tribal domains with "state
  territorial–administrative units."

The key structural claim is that an **ethnically related space is "conducive to the
growth and expansion of larger states."** "State-building in a preexisting ethnic space
was very much easier than ethnos-building." The Zulu kingdom forged under Dingiswayo and
Shaka from the Nguni-speaking Bantu — "an illiterate, preurban, Iron Age society" — is
Gat's paradigmatic worked case: state formation and nation formation proceeding together
on "a common ethnic substratum" entirely outside European or modern conditions.

## Nation Distinguished from Ethnicity and State

Gat insists on keeping three things apart:

- **Nation vs. [[ethnicity|ethnos]]:** an ethnos "is not a political entity." The
  Nguni, the Sumerians, the Greeks, the Aramaeans, the Yoruba were each an ethnos
  "politically divided into separate and often antagonistic units." Most ethne never
  cohered into a single state; the nation is only the politicized, state-bearing case.
- **Nation vs. state:** not all states are nations. Empires "expanded beyond their more
  dependable ethnic core to rule over a larger multiethnic realm," whereas a national
  state "generally remained confined to a specific people." The nation is the state
  roughly congruent with one ethnos. (See [[national-state]].)
- **Nation vs. city-state:** the city-state "encompassed only part of a common ethnic
  space, divided among a multiplicity of city-states," and so "was not a nation" in
  Gellner's and Gat's sense — though scholars of Athens have reasonably called its
  "kin–culture political community of great solidarity" a form of nationhood.

## Emic and Etic

The concept is partly **etic** — an analytic category Gat applies across periods and
continents to identify a recurring congruence of people and polity. But it also rests on
**emic** realities: historical actors themselves perceived peoplehood and resented
foreigners as foreign. Herodotus has the Athenians invoke "the kinship of all Greeks in
blood and speech, and the shrines of gods and the sacrifices that we have in common"; the
Theban prince denounces being "associated with an Asiatic and a Negro"; the Hebrew Bible
"divides humanity into peoples (*am*; *goi*) ... and views them in kin terms as 'families
of the earth.'" Gat reads such language not as mere elite rhetoric but as evidence of a
"premodern 'imagined community' which Benedict Anderson has failed to consider."

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept is **contested**. The dominant **modernist** school (Gellner, Anderson, Kohn
in part) holds that nations and national consciousness are products of modernity —
print capitalism, mass education, industrial society — and that to call premodern
peoples "nations" stretches the term **anachronistically**. On this view premodern
ethnicity, "while existing, was largely devoid of political significance"; states "were
allegedly based on other principles and were supra-ethnic." Modernists also argue, for
cases like China, that "national identity [is] a distinctively modern mode of
consciousness."

Gat concedes that "modernity made a difference, indeed, a huge difference" — premodern
peoples were less "closely integrated and highly mobilized" than modern ones, and
"national identity was not an all or nothing proposition." His rebuttal is that this is a
difference of degree, not of kind: premodern congruences of people and state were "less
potent forms of similar attachments," and so "national by that very definition." The
fuller version of this dispute — and the traditionalist counter-case (A. D. Smith,
Grosby, Roshwald) — is treated under [[antiquity-of-nations]].
