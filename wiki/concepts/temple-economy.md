# The Mesopotamian Temple Economy

The redistributive, temple-centred economic order of the Sumerian city-states, in
which a city's land, labour, and surplus were organized around the household of
its god. It is the economic counterpart of Sumerian
[[divine-kingship|theology of divine ownership]] and the foundation of the
[[early-dynastic-sumer|city-state's]] wealth and power. Reconstructed in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XIII, C. J. Gadd) from the
accounts of one temple — that of the goddess **Baba** at [[lagash|Lagash]].

## The model

The city belongs to its god; the **ensi** manages the god's estate, his wife
manages the goddess's. The temple is a great **household**: it owns land worked by
tenants and dependants, breeds cattle and sheep, runs mills, bakeries,
breweries, weaving workshops, and even a "state" **fishery** (~100 fishermen
delivering to the temple). It feeds, clothes, and houses its god daily — and a
whole section of the city's population that "lived entirely for and by this
institution." In such a society "the distinction between the divine and human
tends to disappear": the god is paralleled by the ensi, his divine family by the
ensi's family, both maintained by the same organization.

## Labour and wages

The Baba temple supported roughly **1,200 dependants**. Most field-workers were
not slaves but **feudatory tenants**, holding allotments in return for a fixed
share of produce *and the duty of military service* — the Baba estate alone could
furnish 500–600 men to the Lagash army. **Wages were paid entirely in kind**,
chiefly barley (the staple), with festival distributions of bread, wool, beer,
milk, and fruit; rations ranged from ~60 litres/month for the best-paid down to
~10 litres for infants and the lowest workers. Women — spinners, weavers, millers,
kitchen and household staff — were largely slaves; some male workers are described
by a term meaning "blind" or "witless."

## The land-ownership debate (a sampling caveat)

Deimel and Schneider, reading the Baba archive, generalized it into a picture of
the whole city as a **"temple-state" (Tempelstadt)** — nearly all land owned by
temples, nearly all people their dependants. Gadd flags this as "precariously
narrow": the Baba lands were ~1 sq. mile, but Lagash's territory stretched "to the
sea." Documents of **private sale of land** (by Enkhegal, Eannatum, and others)
and Urukagina's protection of a "poor man's garden" show substantial property
**outside** the temples — perhaps held by **family communities** alienating land
through their heads with payments to relatives. The temple-state model is thus a
real institution but **not** the whole economy — a correction central to later
scholarship (Diakonoff).

## Liverani: the "temple city" overcorrection, and the palace

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] agrees the **Tempelstadt** model is
untenable as stated — it survives only because temple archives from Early Dynastic IIIb
[[lagash|Lagash]] happen to be what was excavated, while land-sale documents from Early
Dynastic IIIa Shuruppak (Fara, ~2550 BCE) show private property and external seasonal
labour outside temple control — but he warns against overcorrecting into irrelevance:
"Sumerian cities can be considered temple cities in the same way as Late Medieval Venice
can be defined as a commercial city, or nineteenth-century Manchester an industrial city."
He stresses the unifying Sumerian category **é** (Akkadian *bītum*, "house"): temple,
palace, and household were all conceived as "houses," the palace simply a "large house"
(*é-gal* / *ekallum*) reproducing the household plan at scale. Across the Early Dynastic
period administrative centrality **migrated from temple to palace** — the temple kept
ideological supremacy (divine legitimation) while the palace gained executive supremacy —
though the temple's economic role remained essential under palace control. Under the
[[third-dynasty-of-ur|Third Dynasty of Ur]] the temple was finally **absorbed into the
state system**, employing propertyless full-time urban workers rather than "free" village
labour, ideologically underwritten by the deified king replacing the city-gods at the head
of the redistributive system. See [[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]].

## Significance

The temple economy explains how resource-poor southern Mesopotamia financed
monumental building, long-distance [[trade-network-formation|trade]] (timber from
Tilmun, lapis from Badakhshan, carnelian from India, copper from Oman), standing
armies, and the scribal apparatus from which [[writing-and-record-keeping|writing]]
itself grew. It is the institutional matrix of the world's first cities, and the
direct ancestor of the **palace economies** of later Mesopotamia (and a structural
analogue to the redistributive economies of other early states).

## Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World

The Mesopotamian temple economy's long institutional afterlife is documented into the
Hellenistic period by **van der Spek** (Ch. 15): Babylonian temples under Seleucid rule
retained large landholdings, elaborate divided-labor workforces (carpenters, smiths,
weavers, brewers, bakers, scribes, boatmen, and more, attested in Hellenistic
administrative texts), and large sheep flocks. Kings donated land to temples and let them
collect tithes, but also drew income from temple property and could seize temple
treasuries in emergencies — e.g., half the Shamash temple's harvest at Sippar/Larsa was
seized in 309 BCE. See [[processes/hellenistic-economy]] for the broader Seleucid economic
context.
