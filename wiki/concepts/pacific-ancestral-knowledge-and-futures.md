# Pacific Ancestral Knowledge and Futures (Tā-Vā Framework)

## Definition and Scope

This page covers the cluster of Indigenous Oceanian epistemological frameworks through which Pacific peoples understand time, space, ancestors, and environmental responsibility — and specifically how those frameworks orient communities toward the future by placing ancestral knowledge in front (tā-vā philosophy), not behind, the present generation. The page also synthesizes Epeli Hauʻofa's use of these frameworks as the philosophical engine behind his vision of Oceania as home, as well as the concluding reflections on Pacific History offered by Anne Perez Hattori as co-editor of the Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean.

The concept sits at the intersection of **Indigenous philosophy**, **environmental ethics**, and **historiography**: it is simultaneously a metaphysical claim about the structure of time-space, a pedagogical claim about how ancestral knowledge should be transmitted, and a political claim about who is the rightful custodian of the Pacific Ocean.

## The Tā-Vā Philosophy

The foundational conceptual framework is the Moana Nui arrangement of **tā** (time) and **vā** (space). In Tongan, tā means to mark time with beats, rhythms, and actions; vā means the relational space between time-markers. Tā and vā are inseparable: 'Time is spatially constituted while space is temporally marked' ([[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]], ch. 60, Kaʻili).

The critical feature of this temporal-spatial framework is its spatial mapping of past and future relative to the present person:

> 'In historical ways, it logically follows that the past, which has stood the test of time-space, is placed in front of people in the present as guidance, and the unknown future is located in their back in the present, informed by past experiences, with the past and future permanently negotiated in the conflicting present.' (Kaʻili, citing Kaʻili, Māhina, and Addo, *Pacific Studies* 40:1/2 (2017), ch. 60)

This directional arrangement is not merely figurative. It explains why ancestors, elders, grandparents, parents, and elder siblings hold leadership roles across many Moana Nui societies: they are temporospatially positioned in front, as guides. Ancestors occupy the highest level — 'the libraries of Indigenous Moana Nui historical knowledge and information' (ch. 60). Certain ancestors were deified because of extraordinary knowledge and skills; these deified ancestors (tupuʻanga — 'the source of emanation') become permanent guides placed in front of all subsequent generations.

The framework is grounded in a cosmogonic ontology. In the Tongan creation story, the original ancestor of the Tongan people is Vahanoa, the Open Sea, from which emerged Limu (Seaweed) and Kele (Sea Sediment) as the primordial pairs who gave birth to the Tongan people. The sea itself is tupuʻanga: 'the ancestor who provided the source of sustenance and growth for the people who began from the sea' (ch. 60). The people are therefore autochthonous to the ocean: *ko e kakai mei tahi* — the people from the sea.

## The Deified Ancestors: Tangaloa, Māui, Hina

Kaʻili focuses on three major deified ancestors through whom Oceanic peoples understand their relationship to the ocean and land:

**Tangaloa** — The creation deity venerated throughout Moana Nui under variant names (Tangaroa, Tagaloa, Taʻaroa, Kanaloa). In the Tongan tradition, Tangaloa ʻAtulongolongo transformed into a Pacific golden plover (*kiu*, *tulī*) to fly over the ocean in search of land; his brother Tangaloa Tufunga poured down shavings from the Skyworld (Langi) to help coral reefs grow into islands. In different Polynesian traditions he is creation god, master navigator-astronomer, and god of the ocean. In Tonga there are at least seven Tangaloa deities, including Tangaloa Mana — 'Powerful Tangaloa' — who specifically embodies the mana (spiritual power/potency) of the sea (ch. 60). The colonial erasure of Tangaloa — missionaries cast Kanaloa as Satan in Hawaiʻi and de-divinized Tangaloa in Tonga and Sāmoa — is characterized by Kaʻili as directly enabling the colonial ocean's desecration: 'Once Tangaloa was weakened, it was just a matter of time before other forms of debasement followed. As Tangaloa waned into obscurity, the ocean was viewed no longer as an ancestor, a spiritual place, or a source of mana, but rather as a raw material to be extracted for colonial and corporate greed' (ch. 60).

**Māui** — The demi-god hero who fished up islands (using the Mātaʻu Fusifonua, the Land Fishing Hook, from the Tuʻi Manuʻa, Tongamatamoana), snared the sun to give humanity more time, seized fire from the gods to democratize knowledge, and slew monsters to dismantle institutions of domination. Kaʻili interprets Māui's deeds as *heliaki* — figurative expressions of social justice: 'The grand messages of Māui's stories are to advocate for justice by transforming society. Māui's feats are actually heliaki, beautiful poetic expressions, of resisting oppression and fighting injustice for the benefit of humanity' (ch. 60). Māui connects the night sky to navigation — the Scorpio constellation, shaped like a fishhook, is the Mātaʻu Fusifonua, used for navigating to Tonga, Hawaiʻi, and Aotearoa. The haʻamonga ʻa Māui (Burden of Māui), the astronomical observatory at Kolonga in Tonga, marks Māui's first astronomical observatory in Kaʻili's ancestral village.

**Hina** — The oldest and most widely known matriarchal deity in Polynesia, known throughout Moana Nui as Hina, Sina, Hine, or Ina. Goddess of the moon, ocean, sea creatures (particularly sharks and corals), tapa-making, and mat-weaving. In most traditions Hina is the supreme source of Māui's mana — she provides the rope, the adze, and the fishhook. The complementary pairing of Hina (lunar, feminine, nocturnal) and Māui (solar, masculine, diurnal) creates *mālie* — beauty — and embodies the hoa (pairing) principle fundamental to Moana Nui ontology. Hina's ecological significance: as shark goddess, she calls for sustainable shark fishing; as coral goddess, she demands protection of reefs.

## Hauʻofa and the Ancestral Vision

Kaʻili's central argument is that Epeli Hauʻofa's scholarship is most fully understood as the *ecologically based oral traditions* of Moana Nui placed in academic form. Hauʻofa was 'listening to the voices of his ancestors' when he articulated 'Our Sea of Islands' — his argument that Oceania is not a collection of tiny, resource-poor dots on a map but a vast, boundless salt-water continent that its peoples have always navigated freely (ch. 60, citing Hauʻofa, *We Are the Ocean*, pp. 27–40).

Hauʻofa's key claims, as synthesized by Kaʻili:

- 'Oceania is vast, Oceania is expanding' — grounded in deep understanding that oral histories bound people and places to the Pacific Ocean as home and mother, guiding present to future.
- The Pacific Ocean 'purifies the Earth's air, stabilizing the global environment' (citing Sylvia Earle's oceanographic science, which Hauʻofa frames through Indigenous custodianship).
- Pacific Islanders are not 'small island peoples' but *ko e kakai mei tahi* — the people from the sea — with ancestral, cosmogonic responsibilities to the sea as its caretaker, defender, and protector.
- 'No people on earth are more suited to be guardians of the world's largest ocean than those for whom it has been home for generations' (Hauʻofa, *We Are the Ocean*, p. 37).
- The MIRAB 'basket case' discourse and the economic view that 'our future lies in the sea' — meaning 'marine and seabed resources and their development' — are colonial impositions that restrict Pacific peoples from thinking 'outside the bounds of hegemonic capitalist perspectives' (Hauʻofa, *We Are the Ocean*, pp. 27, 54).

Kaʻili argues that Hauʻofa was engaged in a **new Pacific historiography** — interweaving oral and written texts, treating ecologically based oral traditions as sources equal to written documents (following the historian-anthropologist ʻOkusitino Māhina). Hauʻofa also advocated a 'common regional identity' uniting island peoples through 'histories written on the landscape and seascape; carved, stencilled, and wove our metaphors on objects of utility; sang and danced in rituals and ceremonies' (Hauʻofa, *We Are the Ocean*, p. 55).

## Environmental Stakes: Colonial Destruction and Re-emergence

The chapter documents the colonial war against ancestral ocean knowledge in material as well as ideological terms:

- European whaling hunted whales 'to near extinction to obtain meat, oil, and blubber for Europe' while missionaries simultaneously de-divinized Tangaloa.
- Nuclear testing (Marshall Islands, Christmas Island, Johnston Atoll, Mururoa, Fangataufa), military weapons testing, oil drilling, plastic pollution, noise from seismic surveying and military sonar, wall-of-death fishing nets, deep-sea bottom trawling (characterized as 'oceanocide'), shark-finning, sea cucumber over-harvesting, ocean acidification, sand mining, and now seabed mining (begun in Papua New Guinea) are the continuing desecrations of Tangaloa's realm.
- Tonga has 7% of the world's coral reefs and 6% of the world's sea mountains — resources cosmogonically identified with Tangaloa's creation act and now under threat (ch. 60, citing Hauʻofa).

Against this, Kaʻili identifies a **re-emergence of Tangaloa**: His Highness Tui Atua Tupua Tamasese Taʻisi Efi and others are 'resuscitating Tangaloa and the Indigenous spirituality of kinship with all living things.' Tui Atua maintains that Tangaloa is 'the progenitor of all living things on earth (humans, animals, plants, cosmos, sea, land, etc.) and that as such all relationships between these living things are governed by the imperatives of being kin' (ch. 60, citing Tupua Tamasese Taʻisi Tui Atua Efi, *Suʻesuʻe Manogi*, 2009).

## Hattori's Concluding Reflection: 'Choppy Waters'

Anne Perez Hattori (ch. 64, co-editor of the Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean, Vol. 2) offers a framing reflection that complements Kaʻili's ancestral-knowledge argument from the historian's perspective.

Hattori begins with Magellan's 1519 naming of *Pacifico* — a name that encodes the colonizer's misreading of an ocean its peoples have always known as powerful, dangerous, and spiritually charged: 'Those of us who make the Pacific our home know it to be an ocean of vivid contrasts, an ocean whose spirits and power one never takes lightly, regardless of how "pacific" the waters might appear' (ch. 64). The colonial stereotype of 'beach-going, hula-dancing, laughter-filled peoples for whom there is not a care in the world' has served 'mainly to feed the self-interests of those swimming in the warm waters of tourism, colonialism, and militarism' (ch. 64).

Hattori's historiographical claim is that Pacific History has corrected this over more than seventy years of scholarship: 'No longer represented as silent puppets with hands extended to grab the colonizers' handouts, Pacific peoples are acknowledged for tapping into their deep reserves of creativity, diligence, and intelligence to face the challenges before their villages and islands' (ch. 64). The Cambridge volumes, she argues, deliberately position Oceania's past outside the bounds of political and military colonial conquest history, to encompass 'Islanders' relationships with the ocean and the land, art and architecture, song and dance, religion and politics and economics, as well as with foreigners who entered our seascapes and landscapes' (ch. 64).

The 'choppy waters' metaphor frames both the physical ocean and the condition of Pacific peoples navigating colonialism, climate change, and sovereignty struggles: 'Pacific peoples feature as the ever-vigilant guardians of their seas and soil, whether swimming, sailing, or surfing through sometimes choppy waters' (ch. 64).

## Limitations and Critiques

This framework applies primarily to Polynesian (and to some extent Micronesian and broader Austronesian) societies. Kaʻili is explicit that he writes from a Tongan perspective; he acknowledges 'not everyone from Oceania identifies with the ocean' (fn. 8) and 'the sea is an original homeland to some Oceanians, not all' (fn. 41). The tā-vā framework is one among many Moana Nui epistemological frameworks; it has been most fully theorized for Tonga. Application to Melanesian societies — culturally distinct from Polynesian — requires additional caution. The political use of cosmogonic ancestors for environmental advocacy is a recent development and carries risks of essentialism and misrepresentation of communities with diverse orientations to customary knowledge.

## Historiography

Kaʻili's chapter works from Hauʻofa's published essays (*We Are the Ocean*, 2008) as primary text, supplemented by Māhina's theoretical work on tā-vā and his historical reconstruction from oral tradition (Māhina, 'The Tongan traditional history tala-ē-fonua', ANU PhD 1992) and Kaʻili's own ethnographic and textual research (Kaʻili, *Marking Indigeneity*, 2017). The framework draws on Indigenous Tongan heliaki — a figurative, associative, and metonymic literary mode — as both subject and method. Hattori's concluding reflection draws on her positionality as a Chamorro scholar from Guam and six decades of development in Pacific History as an academic discipline. Together the chapters represent the most recent statement of the 'sea of islands' school as it has developed since Hauʻofa's 1994 essay.
