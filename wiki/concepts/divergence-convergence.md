## Definition and Scope

Northrup's divergence-convergence framework proposes a simple but genuinely
global periodization of human history organized around the dominant direction
of cultural, genetic, linguistic, and technological exchange:

**Divergence phase** (most of human history, from the Paleolithic through
roughly 1000 CE): Communities spread across the globe, developing distinct
cultures, languages, genetic profiles, lifeways, and religious traditions.
Long-distance exchanges existed but were limited; most communities constructed
their own distinctive histories. Papua New Guinea at the time of European
contact illustrates the possibilities: 1 million people speaking 500 distinct
languages from 33 different language families. A single proto-Bantu language
split into 250 different languages in under 3,000 years.

**Convergence phase** (intensifying from ~1000 CE to the present): Centripetal
forces for global convergence began to overtake centrifugal divergence. The
tipping point Northrup proposes is ~1000 CE, when — following John Man's
argument — it first became possible to imagine a message circling the entire
inhabited world. Shared diseases, technologies, religions, languages, and
eventually English and Spanish as global languages mark this phase. The total
number of distinct languages is now declining rapidly.

## Divergence Had Earlier Roots in the Paleolithic

Convergence, too, has deep Paleolithic roots. Evidence of long-distance
exchange of goods, ideas, and ritual objects appears in the Paleolithic.
In the last 5,000 years, empires and trading diasporas multiplied exchanges
of goods, ideas, fashions, and religions. Northrup does not date convergence
to the modern era — he traces its gradual emergence across millennia before
identifying 1000 CE as the structural tipping point.

## Significance for Periodization

The framework's value for world history is that it is genuinely global — it
does not rely on European chronology (Ancient/Medieval/Modern) and does not
ignore everything before Sumer. It embraces the full span of human history
and generates questions about future trajectories (will convergence continue?
what are its limits?).

Traditional periodizations that partition the past into Ancient, Medieval, and
Modern periods work for particular regions but poorly for world history.
Northrup's framework cuts across these regional frameworks entirely.

## Limitations

- The tipping point of ~1000 CE is argued, not proven. Others might propose
  the 16th century (first genuinely global networks of exchange) or the 19th
  century (industrial communications technologies) as better candidates.
- "Convergence" is analytically vague: it conflates economic integration,
  cultural homogenization, biological mixing, and political globalization,
  which have very different patterns.
- The framework tends to treat divergence as "natural" and convergence as a
  departure; this may encode assumptions about the naturalness of cultural
  difference.
