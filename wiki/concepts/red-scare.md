# Red Scare

"Red Scare" denotes two distinct periods of intense American public and governmental anxiety over communist, socialist, and anarchist subversion, each producing significant curtailments of civil liberties and prominent purges of suspected radicals from public life.

The First Red Scare (1919–1920) followed the Bolshevik Revolution, a wave of labor unrest, and anarchist bombings (including one at Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer's home), and produced the Palmer Raids — mass warrantless arrests and deportations of suspected radicals and immigrants coordinated by a young J. Edgar Hoover — before subsiding as the perceived threat of imminent revolution in the United States failed to materialize.

The Second Red Scare (c. 1947–1957) accompanied the onset of the Cold War, Soviet acquisition of the atomic bomb, the "loss" of China to communism, and espionage revelations (Alger Hiss, the Rosenbergs), and was institutionalized through the House Un-American Activities Committee, federal loyalty-security programs, and — most notoriously — Senator Joseph McCarthy's investigations, alongside security proceedings such as the [[events/oppenheimer-security-hearing-1954|1954 Oppenheimer security hearing]] that stripped prominent scientists and officials of clearances and reputations on often flimsy evidence. Both episodes are studied as cautionary case studies in the tension between national-security anxiety and civil liberties in American political culture.
