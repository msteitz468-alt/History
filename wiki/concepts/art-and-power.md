# Art and Power in World History

## Definition and Scope

This page treats **art as a category in world history** following
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Robert Bagley, Ch 8). Because a chronological,
cross-cultural narrative of two millennia of world art is impossible, Bagley instead
offers a **definition and a framework of ideas**. His working definition, chosen to
be neither Eurocentric nor parochial: **art is artifacts — from garments to wall
paintings to planned cities — whose functions required that they be designed for
visual effect**: made to attract attention and shape response, to elicit awe,
wonder, exaltation, or delight.

This definition foregrounds **function and intended effect** rather than the
Romantic discourse of self-expression, and it accommodates art from imperial courts,
Irish monasteries, Maya city-states, and Inner Asian nomad tribes alike. It connects
to [[paleolithic-art-symbolic-culture]] (art as a human universal — Picasso applied
"art" to the cave paintings) and to the analysis of [[empire]] and [[imperial-city]].

## Two Polemics

Bagley clears away two fashionable positions:
1. **"Art is a Renaissance European invention."** Rejected as ethnocentric: design,
   like music, is a human universal; if cave paintings are excluded because they had
   a function, so must the Sistine Chapel be.
2. **The "visual culture" leveling** — treating all artifacts as equal to avoid
   "elitism." Rejected because **patrons did not treat objects as equal**:
   Tutankhamun and Hadrian made judgments of quality, and those judgments "are facts
   of history that affected history." We can, cautiously, share them — the Chi-rho
   page of the **Book of Kells** seemed finer than an ordinary gospel-book to its
   makers and to us alike. (The absence of a word for "art" or "beauty" in a culture
   no more proves it lacked the thing than the absence of a word for "economy" proves
   it had none.)

## The Patron as Selector

Bagley's central analytical device: the **patron** (ruler, state, church, wealthy
elite) shapes art "like natural selection." Patrons are not the *source* of variation
— they cannot specify in words the exact appearance of what they want — but their
power to **choose among options**, to hire and fire, "may be absolute": they choose
the variants that survive to have offspring. Durable ancient art is costly and
therefore overwhelmingly the art of those who controlled great resources. The modern
lament that pre-modern artists "lacked artistic freedom" is overstated — museums are
full of masterpieces made to please patrons, and today's "market forces" are no less
a patron. This makes art a direct register of **power**: who commanded resources, and
to what self-representing ends.

## Visual Thinking

Bagley insists that **visual art is the product of visual thinking** — "words are not
the medium of visual thinking any more than they are the medium of musical
thinking." Art may carry or amplify verbal meanings, but it is first and foremost
visual, and should be approached as such.

## Examples Across Civilizations

The chapter proceeds by juxtaposed examples rather than a survey — tomb reliefs of
the vizier **Ramose** in Amenhotep III's Egypt (art to sustain the dead in an
idealized afterlife; the statue represents Ramose by bearing his *name*, not his
likeness); Chinese ritual **bronzes** (Anyang); Assyrian palace reliefs (Assurbanipal
at Nineveh); the inscribed gold of **Achaemenid** Persia (Darius); Roman wall
painting (Pompeii); Maya and Inner Asian works; and the **Book of Kells**. Across all
of them, the common thread is that visual effect on an audience was *consciously
intended and effortfully achieved* — and almost always in the service of rulers,
states, gods, and the dead.

## Contested Status

The definitional questions — what counts as "art," whether quality judgments are
legitimate, whether the category is irredeemably Eurocentric — are live in art
history and anthropology, which is why this concept is marked **contested**. Bagley's
function-and-effect definition is itself a deliberate intervention in that debate.
Compare the broader critique of Western framing in [[eurocentrism]].
