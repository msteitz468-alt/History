# Atlantic System (Bronze–Iron Age)

## Definition and Scope

The Atlantic System refers to the long-term networks of maritime connectivity along the Atlantic façade of Europe—from Morocco to the Shetland Isles—facilitating the movement of commodities (especially metals), ideas, people, and belief systems from the Neolithic through the Iron Age. It formed a distinct cultural continuum characterized by shared warrior ideologies, feasting practices, votive deposition, and eventually the crystallization and spread of the Celtic language as a *lingua franca*.

It is distinct from (though interactive with) the Mediterranean system to the south and east and the developing central European (Hallstatt/La Tène) systems.

## Neolithic and Early Bronze Age Foundations

Coastal waters encouraged short-haul sea journeys. Farming communities using Cardial/Impressed wares reached the Portuguese coast ~5500–5400 BCE and the Atlantic façade of France ~5300 BCE. Passage graves (originating Portugal ~4800 BCE) spread rapidly to Brittany, the Irish Sea zone, and Orkney, carrying shared architecture, burial ritual, art styles, and solar cosmology—evidence of developed connectivity and likely a common *lingua franca* derived from Indo-European roots introduced with early farmers (Cunliffe 2018, Ch. 3, pp. 58–60, cache ~2070–2120).

## Beaker Intensification (c. 2800–2100 BCE)

The Maritime Bell Beaker (Tagus region, Portugal, ~2800–2700 BCE) and associated package (archery equipment, copper metallurgy, individual or collective burial practices) spread rapidly along the Atlantic and into river systems. Analysis shows local production of vessels; the "concept" and associated values and metalworking skills travelled with mobile people and prospectors. By ~2200 BCE Bell Beaker enclaves reached Hungary. This mobility phase, driven in part by the search for metals (copper, gold, tin), is a candidate vector for the eastward spread of Celtic as a *lingua franca* (Cunliffe 2018, Ch. 3, pp. 60–63, cache ~2130–2250).

## Late Bronze Age Peak (1300–800 BCE)

Warrior weapon sets (carp's tongue swords of Huelva and Nantes varieties, shields with V-notch) and feasting gear (riveted bronze cauldrons, flesh hooks, roasting spits) circulated throughout the Atlantic zone from western Iberia to Ireland and southern Britain. Hilltop fortifications appear. Votive deposition in watery places and on land (notably massive Armorican axe hoards of the 7th–6th centuries BCE requiring tonnes of metal) reflect shared chthonic beliefs and social imperatives (Cunliffe 2018, Ch. 4, pp. 67–72, cache ~2350–2500).

Phoenician trading enclaves (from 10th/9th centuries BCE at Huelva/Tartessos, Gadir, Mondego) drew southern Atlantic Iberia into the Mediterranean sphere, loosening older north–south Atlantic links and contributing to the first major dislocation of the system ~900 BCE (Cunliffe 2018, Ch. 4, pp. 73–75, cache ~2510–2590).

## Iron Age Transformations and Celtic Language

The Atlantic lingua franca hypothesis posits that Celtic crystallized here and was carried eastward with Beaker mobility. After dislocations (c. 900 BCE separating Hispano-Celtic; c. 600 BCE isolating Goidelic zones), dialects diverged while connectivity persisted in modified form (Gundlingen swords, horse gear, stamped pottery in Brittany linking to west-central Europe). By the first millennium BCE Celtic speech is attested or inferable across the zone (Cunliffe 2018, Ch. 3–4, passim; Ch. 4, pp. 82–91, cache ~2910–3190).

## Historiography and Limitations

Earlier models treated the Atlantic zone as peripheral or recipient of central European innovations. The Atlantic System concept foregrounds indigenous connectivity and agency. It helps explain the western concentration of Celtic place names and the early date of Celtic inscriptions in Iberia without requiring mass migration from a central European "homeland."

The system was never uniform; regional socio-economic territories and local preferences (e.g., retention of older sword types in Britain) always existed alongside long-distance flows. After ~900 BCE the southern (Iberian) and northern Atlantic zones diverged, with the latter increasingly articulated with west-central European developments.
