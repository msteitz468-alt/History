# Pre-Modern Mixed Economy (the Byzantine Exemplar)

## The Concept

A **mixed economy** combines significant state action with functioning markets, neither
excluding the other. Applied to the pre-industrial world by Angeliki Laiou and Cécile
Morrisson ([[laiou-morrisson-byzantine-economy-2007|*The Byzantine Economy*]], 2007), the
concept is used to argue that the [[byzantine-economy|Byzantine economy]] was "**probably the
most successful example of a mixed economy in the European Middle Ages**" — an interpretive
claim, offered as an *etic* analytical frame, not a category the Byzantines themselves used.

The authors set the concept explicitly against two older readings: the **substantivist /
"embedded"** view (the economy wholly subordinate to the political, à la Polanyi/Finley) and
the image of Byzantium as a **highly protectionist, state-asphyxiated** economy in which
state action and market forces were mutually exclusive. Against both, they argue that
"market forces always operated, with greater or lesser impact," even when state fiscality
was paramount — and that it was precisely the *combination* that, for some seven hundred
years from the seventh century, proved "felicitous," its advantages outweighing its flaws.

## The Argument (emic vs. etic; strengths and limits)

Laiou and Morrisson borrow the vocabulary of modern growth theory: the state supplies
security, infrastructure, legal guarantees and institutional stability (and, they note,
promoted a comparatively high basic literacy), while its "failures" are rigidity, slow
adjustment and capture by narrow interests; the market supplies flexibility and private
initiative, while its "failures" are imperfect information, monopoly, and the generation of
inequality "beyond what each society will tolerate." They stress the **categories are
modern** — but note the same tension was argued in the Chinese **Debate on Salt and Iron**
(81 BCE), which they treat as an analogous pre-modern articulation.

The balance of state and market **shifted over time**: state-dominant after the
seventh-century crisis; progressively liberalising from the tenth century (land market,
relaxed controls) through the eleventh–twelfth; and, after 1204, operating within an
Italian-dominated Mediterranean where the state receded to near-irrelevance (see
[[byzantine-economy|the Byzantine economy]], phases). The authors insist the eventual failure
was **political in the first instance**, not a verdict on the mixed model itself.

## The East–West Comparison

The concept is developed through a structural comparison with the medieval West that
deliberately **avoids the "fallacy of the model"** — i.e. treating Western Europe (because it
"eventually developed capitalism") as the yardstick against which all others are found
wanting. Key contrasts drawn:

- **Broad chronological coincidence** of economic cycles across Byzantium and the West from
  the seventh to the thirteenth century (crisis, recovery, acceleration, then divergence),
  shaped by shared demographic and climatic factors (the "little climatic optimum").
- **Landholding:** Byzantium never developed the Carolingian [[bipartite-manor|bipartite
  manor]] or the *seigneurie banale*; small-scale peasant exploitation stayed dominant, and
  the strong state pre-empted lordly banal rights.
- **Monetization:** far higher and earlier in Byzantium (tax paid in coin), with salaried
  labour and sophisticated estate accounting appearing well before the West.
- **Institutions:** a partnership form (*chreokoinonia*) ~300 years before the Italian
  *commenda*; civil law permitting interest where Western canon law obstructed it.
- **The city:** Byzantium had a **megalopolis** (Constantinople) without Western parallel,
  but the West's later great manufacturing cities (Flanders, Italy) marked the divergence.
- **Western advantages from the twelfth century:** colonisation of resource-rich north-
  eastern lands, new silver mines, the linkage of northern Europe and the Mediterranean, and
  the growth of manufacturing and banking — leaving Byzantium peripheral.

## Contestation

The claim is **interpretive and contested**, and the wiki records it as a position, not a
verdict:
- It reverses an older consensus (state-dominated, protectionist, sedentary-merchant
  Byzantium) and depends on the recent "rehabilitation" of the eleventh–twelfth centuries
  (Kazhdan, Hendy, Lemerle, Morrisson, Harvey) and on the still more recent claim that some
  sectors grew in the thirteenth.
- Laiou and Morrisson explicitly **disagree with M. Hendy** over whether the twelfth-century
  economy was "state-dominated," and decline to adjudicate the **Wickham/Haldon "mode of
  production"** debate.
- The framing that "economics worked where politics did not" (Italian city-states
  integrating economically what Byzantium could not integrate politically) is itself an
  interpretive judgement about causation and counterfactual possibility.
