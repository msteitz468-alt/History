# Optimates and Populares

The two political "tendencies" of the late Roman Republic — and the central
interpretive problem of the period. Analysed in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Chs 1 & 3
(Lintott).

## The Roman terms

These were **not parties** in any modern sense (no organisation, programme, or
membership). Following Gelzer's classic definition: the **optimates** were the
majority — at times the whole — of the governing class, who defended the **authority
of the Senate** and the *status quo* (*otium cum dignitate*); the **populares** were
**individuals** (rarely coherent over time) who chose to work **through the
assemblies**, bypassing the Senate, in the name of the people's welfare and liberty.
The distinction was one of **political method**, not of social class — both came from
the same aristocracy — though Cicero and even Sallust insist the two outlooks were at
bottom **genuinely opposed and irreconcilable**.

Standard *popularis* themes: the physical welfare of the *populus* (land and corn
distributions); the defence of *libertas* (the laws on *provocatio*, secret ballot,
the criminal courts). The recognised canon of *popularis* leaders ran from **C.
Flaminius** (tribune 232) through the **Gracchi** to Saturninus, Clodius, and Caesar.

## The modern debate

Lintott surveys the historiography (the volume opens with it):
- **Polybius**: the Roman mixed constitution, like all constitutions, was subject to
  natural growth and decay; unchallenged empire would breed luxury, greed, and
  ambition, the demagogue exploiting popular discontent toward mob-rule and tyranny —
  prophetic, whether written before 146 or revised in the Gracchan age.
- **The Roman moral tradition** (Sallust, Posidonius, Florus): the fall caused by
  moral corruption after the destruction of Carthage removed the *metus hostilis*.
- **Machiavelli & Montesquieu**: militarism, the extension of citizenship, and the
  prolongation of military commands attaching soldiers to generals.
- **Mommsen**: anachronistically modelled *optimates*/*populares* on British
  Conservatives/Liberals, then (following Sallust) dismissed the contest as an
  unprincipled struggle for power, redeemed only by the monarchy of Caesar.
- **Gelzer & Münzer**: the aristocracy monopolised office through **clientela** and
  kinship; Münzer made small **family factions** the true units of politics (developed
  by **Syme**, *The Roman Revolution*, and Badian).
- **Meier** (*Res Publica Amissa*), **Brunt**, **Gruen**: variously stress the
  stability of the system, the obsolescence of *popularis* ideology, or the genuine
  representation of class interests; Lintott himself (*Violence in Republican Rome*)
  argues the toleration of "a modicum of violence" let real conflicts spiral into
  civil war.

## The tribunate as battleground (the 70s)

The conflict crystallised after Sulla around a **single institution**: the
**tribunate of the plebs**. The [[sullan-constitution|Sullan settlement]] had
deliberately **disabled** it — stripping its power to legislate and barring
ex-tribunes from further office — precisely to choke off the *popularis* method at
source. Through the 70s a succession of tribunes (Sicinius, Quinctius, Licinius
Macer) **agitated for its restoration** against entrenched optimate resistance;
**Cotta** (cos. 75) reopened the *cursus* to ex-tribunes, and in **70** the consuls
**[[pompey]] and Crassus restored the tribunes' legislative power** outright. Seager
underlines that the bitter resistance proved the optimates were **right** to fear it:
the restored tribunate became the instrument by which **Pompey, Caesar, and Crassus**
secured their extraordinary commands — "that excessive growth of individual power
which the oligarchy saw, with some justification, as the greatest threat to its
collective predominance."

## Limitations and critique

The **faction thesis** is contested: Lintott stresses the *lack* of evidence (even in
Cicero's letters) for stable factions, the shifting, cross-cutting network of marriage
and *amicitia*, the impermanence of *clientela* (the *lex de repetundis* recognises
"past and present" patron-client ties), and the rise of **electoral bribery** as
evidence of *weakening*, not strengthening, aristocratic control. The unresolved
questions concern **plebeian self-consciousness** — whether the poor articulated
class grievances, and what *popularis* leaders thought they were doing.

## Flower, *Roman Republics* (2010)

Flower lists *optimates*/*populares* “party politics” among the three standard scholarly focuses on the late Republic, but treats exclusive reliance on that frame as dangerous: it invents a tragic stalemate that presupposes a static fundamental Republic. On her reading, **popular politics after Sulla** — violent interventions by citizens and soldiers, and the restored tribunate of the 60s–50s — tended to **weaken remnants of the Sullan settlement** rather than restore earlier republican culture; “popular politics was not necessarily ‘republican’ in tendency.” The restored tribunate, she argues, fed more violence and multiyear commands of the great generals.

For the **50s** she rejects a coherent party-politics analysis outright (engaging the Taylor tradition): Romans had not had second-century parties; any “party of sorts” under Cinna had been destroyed; only the most powerful generals could form alliances that mattered, and those were backed by military might. She footnotes von Ungern-Sternberg’s claim that **obstructionist optimate tactics** ensured the continuation of *popularis* politics. Source: [[flower-roman-republics-2010]].
