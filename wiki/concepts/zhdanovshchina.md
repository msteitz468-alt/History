# Zhdanovshchina
## Definition
**Zhdanovshchina** (the "Zhdanov doctrine" or "Zhdanov era") refers to a period of intense ideological purification, cultural chauvinism, and political control over intellectual life in the Soviet Union, launched in August 1946 and lasting until Stalin's death in 1953. Directed by Andrei Zhdanov, the campaign demanded that all literature, science, and art conform strictly to Party dogmas, aggressively targeting foreign Western influences ("rootless cosmopolitanism") and enforcing absolute Soviet patriotism.

## Context and Origin
During the Second World War, the Soviet government had relaxed some ideological controls to mobilize patriotism and cooperate with Western allies, introducing a temporary sense of cultural freedom. In 1946, Stalin tasked Zhdanov with re-asserting orthodox control. The campaign opened in August 1946 with a decree condemning the literary journals *Zvezda* and *Leningrad* for publishing the works of the poet Anna Akhmatova and the satirist Mikhail Zoshchenko, both of whom were expelled from the writers' union. The campaign quickly spread to music (denouncing Shostakovich and Prokofiev for "formalism"), philosophy, and science (endorsing Trofim Lysenko's genetics quackery).

## Limitations and Critiques
- **Cultural Stifling:** The campaign placed Soviet scholarship, literature, and the arts in an ideological straitjacket, producing a period of extreme monotony, drabness, and fear.
- **Rootless Cosmopolitanism and Anti-Semitism:** The doctrine's attack on Western influences took a violent, chauvinistic turn, manifesting as state-sponsored anti-Semitism. Yiddish writers and Jewish intellectuals were systematically purged, arrested, or executed.
- **Scientific Decline:** By prioritizing ideological orthodoxy over empirical science (as in the endorsement of Lysenko), the campaign severely set back Soviet biology and genetics, costing the agricultural sector dearly.
