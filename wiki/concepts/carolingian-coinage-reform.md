# The Carolingian Coinage Reforms (Silver Denier)

## Definition

The **Carolingian coinage reforms** were two royal interventions — under [[pippin-iii|Pippin III]]
(754/5) and [[charlemagne|Charlemagne]] (c. 793/4) — that reshaped the money of western Europe around
a single, royally controlled **silver penny** (the **denier** / *denarius*). In
[[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Blackburn, Ch. 20) they are presented as both economic and
political acts: signs of reviving markets and of the reassertion of a **royal monopoly** over coinage.

## From Gold to Silver

The late Roman and Merovingian gold *tremissis* had already, during the 670s, given way across Francia
and England to coins of nearly **pure silver** as gold grew scarce and as the economy shifted toward
low-value everyday transactions (the Anglo-Saxon **[[sceatta|sceattas]]**). The Carolingian reforms
consolidated this silver standard.

## Pippin's Reform (754/5)

Within a few years of taking the crown, Pippin III "implemented a radical reform that changed
fundamentally the appearance and outward status of the coinage." Henceforth every Frankish coin would
name the **royal authority** and usually its **mint**; the small, thick Merovingian flans gave way to
larger, thinner coins struck from cut sheet metal (a technique borrowed from the Visigoths, Lombards,
and Arabs). A capitulary of 754/5 restored the weight standard and decreed that no more than 22 *solidi*
(i.e. 264 deniers) be struck from a pound of silver — establishing the **pound / shilling / penny**
(*libra / solidus / denarius*) reckoning that would survive in parts of Europe into modern times.

## Charlemagne's Reform (c. 793/4)

Charlemagne's second reform **increased the weight** of the denier by some 30% (to c. 1.7 g), imposed
**greater standardisation** (the principal design being the royal **monogram** *KAROLUS*), and **reduced
the number of mints** from perhaps a hundred to about forty — a marked concentration of monetary control.
He extended the broad denier to conquered **Lombard Italy** (probably 781) and the first purely **papal
coinage** at Rome took the Carolingian denier as its model.

## The English Parallel

The Anglo-Saxon coinage underwent two roughly parallel reforms: the East Anglian king Beonna improved the
silver content in the 750s, and **[[offa-of-mercia|Offa]]** introduced a fine, reformed silver penny — the
ancestor of the English penny — that paralleled the Frankish denier.

## Significance

The silver denier became the **dominant coin of western Europe** for centuries, and the *libra/solidus/
denarius* system its enduring accounting framework. NCMH II reads the reforms as evidence of "newly active
internal and external markets" and of kings reasserting authority over a now profit-driven, partially
monetised [[carolingian-economy|economy]].

## Historiography

Blackburn (NCMH II) synthesises the numismatic evidence (hoards, mint attributions, weight standards). The
precise dating of the reforms (754/5; 781 vs. 793/4 for the heavier denier), the exact weight of the early
Carolingian pound, and the interpretation of certain papal and transitional issues remain matters of
specialist debate.
