# The Directed Telescope

The **Directed Telescope** is a command technique where a commander bypasses the formal military hierarchy by deploying trusted staff officers, aides-de-camp, or special liaisons directly to subordinate units to observe operations, verify reports, and gather ground-truth information.

---

## Purpose and Function

Formal military hierarchies are prone to **information pathology**: reports moving up the chain of command are often delayed, filtered, summarized, and sanitized by intermediate commanders who wish to highlight successes or hide failures. During a crisis, this can leave a high-level commander blinded or operating on outdated information.

The directed telescope serves as an informal information gathering mechanism that:
- Cuts through bureaucratic layers to provide rapid, unfiltered reports.
- Verifies the accuracy of formal reports.
- Acts as the "eyes and ears" of the commander on critical sectors of the battlefield.
- Allows the commander to selectively intervene ("optionally control") without permanently micromanaging the chain of command.

---

## Historical Examples

In *Command in War*, Van Creveld highlights several commanders who successfully utilized this technique:

### 1. Napoleon Bonaparte
Napoleon's Imperial Headquarters utilized a team of young, highly trained officers (*officiers d'ordonnance*) who were sent directly to corps headquarters. They had the authority to demand information, observe unit positions, and ride back immediately with updates, allowing Napoleon to reconstruct the battlefield in real time.

### 2. The Duke of Wellington
Wellington relied on "scouting officers" (such as Col. John Waters) who operated independently in Spain, bypassing division commanders to report directly to him on French movements and terrain conditions.

### 3. Helmuth von Moltke the Elder
While Moltke championed decentralized command (*[[concepts/auftragstaktik|Auftragstaktik]]*), he used General Staff officers sent from Berlin to field headquarters to ensure that the independent armies remained coordinated and understood the high-level intent.

### 4. George S. Patton and Dwight D. Eisenhower
During World War II, both Eisenhower and Patton utilized small groups of liaison officers who traveled daily to front-line divisions, providing the army and theater commanders with immediate, unfiltered assessments of combat conditions and unit morale.

---

## Limitations and Risks

While powerful, the directed telescope carries significant risks:
- **Erosion of Trust**: If overused or poorly managed, it can make subordinate commanders feel untrusted and micromanaged.
- **Chain of Command Subversion**: Bypassing intermediate commanders can cause friction, confusion, and duplicate orders.
- **Information Overload**: If not tightly focused (i.e. not "directed" at a specific, critical point), it can overwhelm the commander with excessive tactical detail.

To be effective, the telescope must be used selectively, and the liaison officers must act strictly as observers rather than issuing orders of their own.
