# Wergild

**Wergild** (Old English *wer* = "man" + *gield* = "payment"; also spelled *weregeld* or *wergeld*; Frankish *guidrigild*) is the monetized compensation owed to the kin-group of a killed or injured person under the customary law systems of the early medieval Germanic successor kingdoms. The term functions as both an emic legal category—appearing by name in actual law codes—and as an etic analytical framework used by modern historians to compare the social structures of early medieval societies. It is the central mechanism of the early medieval **leges nationum** (laws of the peoples), and its existence and calibration reveals the social hierarchy and political assumptions of each kingdom.

---

## Definition and Function

In the early medieval legal world, deliberate killing did not automatically trigger state-administered punishment (imprisonment, execution at royal command) as a matter of first resort. Instead, the primary response was a **negotiated cash payment** from the killer's kin-group to the victim's kin-group. This payment—the wergild—was intended to:

1.  **Terminate the feud cycle**: The right to blood vengeance (*faida*) was legally acknowledged. A failure to pay wergild, or a refusal by the victim's kin to accept it, was one of the few legitimate justifications for escalating feud violence.
2.  **Restore social equilibrium**: The payment publicly acknowledged the wrong done and compensated the kin-group for the loss of one of its productive and military members.
3.  **Quantify social worth**: The wergild rate of any individual was a direct expression of their legal standing and social status. The higher the wergild, the more valuable that person was in the eyes of the law.

---

## Wergild and Social Stratification

The tables of wergild rates in the various law codes are among the most precise records of early medieval social hierarchy. The standard structure distinguishes several categories:

| Category | Frankish Salic Law | Lombard *Edictus Rothari* | Anglo-Saxon (Wessex) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Full freeman** | 200 *solidi* | 150 *solidi* | 200 shillings |
| **Half-freeman** (*litus*) | 100 *solidi* | 75 *solidi* | — |
| **Slave** | 30–70 *solidi* (property compensation to master) | property value | — |
| **Roman (in Frankish realm)** | 100 *solidi* | — | — |
| **King's man / *antrustion*** | 600 *solidi* | — | 1,200 shillings (thegn) |
| **Frankish noble** | 600 *solidi* | — | — |

Several critical analytical points emerge from these tables:

*   **Ethnicity is encoded in law**: In the Frankish Salic Code, a *Roman* had a wergild of only 100 *solidi*, exactly half that of a free Frank (200 *solidi*). This was not merely a reflection of existing social difference; it *constituted* and perpetuated a legal hierarchy that distinguished the Roman population from their Frankish rulers.
*   **Royal service elevates status**: A king's sworn companion (*antrustion* in Frankish, *gesith/thegn* in Anglo-Saxon) carried a wergild three to six times that of an ordinary freeman, reflecting the legal weight of royal patronage.
*   **Women had wergild**: Women's wergilds were generally equal to those of men of equivalent status during their childbearing years, reflecting their economic value to the kin-group as mothers; this rate dropped significantly in old age.

## Regional Variation Across the Leges Nationum

**Patrick Wormald** (*NCMH*, Ch. 21) provides a comprehensive survey of the main law codes and their wergild structures:

*   **Salic Law (*Lex Salica*, c. 507–511)**: Issued by the Frankish King Clovis. The most complex and complete surviving Merovingian code. Its wergild structure explicitly differentiates between Franks, Romans, and *laeti* (settled foederati of uncertain status). Its 600-*solidus* wergild for a Frank in royal service directly mirrors the Roman legal tradition of treble damages for violence against a Roman official, demonstrating the ongoing engagement with the imperial model.
*   **Lombard Law (*Edictus Rothari*, 643 CE)**: Issued by King Rothari of the Lombards in Pavia. The first written code of Lombard law. Notable for its particularly systematic organization and detailed wergild tables that differentiate between multiple grades of freedom and servitude.
*   **Visigothic Law (*Liber Iudiciorum*, 654 CE)**: See **[[liber-iudiciorum]]**. Notably, the Visigothic code under Reccesuinth abolished the separate wergilds for Goths and Romans, creating a single territorial rate. This legal unification reflected the collapse of any meaningful ethnic distinction between the Goth and Roman populations of Iberia by the mid-seventh century.
*   **Anglo-Saxon Codes**: The earliest surviving Anglo-Saxon code is that of King Æthelberht of Kent (c. 602 CE), which uses a fine table rather than explicit wergilds for most offenses. Later codes from Wessex (Ine, c. 694 CE; Alfred, c. 880s CE) develop explicit wergild tables with careful gradations between *gesithcund* (noble) and *ceorlisc* (peasant) status.

## Historiographical Significance: Law as Royal Ideology

Wormald (*NCMH* I) argues against reading the barbarian law codes primarily as factual records of actual legal practice. He emphasizes that:

1.  **Written codes were acts of royal ideology**: The Frankish, Lombard, Visigothic, and Anglo-Saxon kings issued written law codes in part to imitate the prestigious Roman legislative tradition—to show that they were legitimate successors to Roman authority. The very act of writing law in Latin (or Latin-influenced vernacular) was a performance of royal power.
2.  **Oral custom pre-dated and persisted alongside written law**: The laws as written were not comprehensive codes replacing oral tradition. Local disputes were still largely settled by local custom and community judgment, with the written code serving as a prestigious authoritative reference rather than an administrative manual.
3.  **Biblical kingship ideology**: From the Visigothic councils of Toledo onward, and in Bede's framing of the Anglo-Saxon kings, the ideological basis of early medieval kingship became increasingly **biblical** and **ecclesiastical**. The model of the Old Testament king—especially David and Solomon—provided an idiom of sacral, divinely endorsed authority that gradually displaced the purely warrior-based legitimation of the Migration Period.
