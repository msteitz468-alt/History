# Classical Liberalism

The political tradition that holds individual liberty under general laws to be
the primary political value: limited constitutional government, [[rule-of-law]],
private property ([[several-property]]) and freedom of contract, religious
toleration, and free trade. Both emic (self-description of 19th-century Liberal
parties, the Manchester School, the American founders' heirs) and etic
(historians' label for the pre-1900 liberal mainstream, coined largely to
distinguish it from twentieth-century welfare liberalism).

This page currently draws chiefly on
[[hayek-constitution-of-liberty-1960|Hayek's *Constitution of Liberty*]] (1960),
the most systematic twentieth-century restatement of the tradition — recorded
throughout as Hayek's attributed positions, not wiki-voice history.

## Core Doctrines (Hayek's restatement)

- **Liberty as absence of coercion**: "that condition of men in which coercion
  of some by others is reduced as much as is possible in society." Hayek traces
  the oldest meaning to the ancient free/slave distinction — Greek manumission
  decrees (Westermann) conferred protected legal status, immunity from arbitrary
  arrest, free choice of work, and free movement.
- **Explicit rejections of rival concepts of liberty**: *political* liberty (the
  franchise — "a free people in this sense is not necessarily a people of free
  men"); *inner* freedom (whose opposite is weakness of will, not coercion); and
  *freedom as power/wealth* — a redefinition Hayek says was "deliberately
  fostered as part of the socialist argument" (naming Dewey and Commons among
  American Progressives), and dangerous because it lets liberty-rhetoric justify
  coercive redistribution. His courtier/peasant example: the luxurious courtier
  at his prince's beck and call is less free than the poor self-directed peasant.
  He accepts the "negative" label (T. H. Green's distinction; Berlin's 1958 "Two
  Concepts") — negative like peace: "It becomes positive only through what we
  make of it."
- **Anti-perfectionism**: liberalism is "a modest and even humble creed" resting
  on a low estimate of human wisdom; "it has been perfectionism of one kind or
  another that has often destroyed whatever degree of decency societies have
  achieved."
- **Liberalism vs. democracy**: "Liberalism is a doctrine about what the law
  ought to be, democracy a doctrine about the manner of determining what will be
  the law." Hayek gives three grounds for democracy — peaceful change ("the only
  method of peaceful change that man has yet discovered"), a historical safeguard
  of liberty, and (following Tocqueville) education of public opinion — while
  insisting it is a means, not an end.
- **Principles over expediency**: endorsing Benjamin Constant's liberalism as a
  *système de principes* — liberty must be an inviolable principle or it is
  destroyed piecemeal by measures whose concrete benefits are visible and whose
  costs to freedom are diffuse.
- **Non-coercion over private morals**: "moral beliefs concerning matters of
  conduct which do not directly interfere with the protected sphere of other
  persons do not justify coercion" — for Hayek the feature most distinguishing
  liberalism from both conservatism and socialism.
- **State action bounded by rule, not volume**: classical-liberal opposition to
  "intervention" (Smith, Mill) meant infringement of the protected sphere by
  specific coercive orders, not absence of all government activity: "Freedom of
  economic activity had meant freedom under the law, not the absence of all
  government action."

## Hayek's "Two Traditions" Thesis (attributed position)

Chapter 4 argues that since the eighteenth century there have been two rival
theories of liberty: one "empirical and unsystematic" (British/evolutionary),
one "speculative and rationalistic" (French) — and that "the rationalist school
was simply and completely wrong."

- **British canon**: the Scottish moral philosophers Hume, Smith, and Ferguson,
  "seconded by" Tucker, Burke, and Paley, rooted in common-law jurisprudence
  (Hale, Coke). Institutions are (Ferguson) "the result of human action but not
  the execution of any human design" — see [[cultural-evolution]] and
  [[true-and-false-individualism]]. Hume is the book's "constant companion and
  sage guide."
- **French/rationalist canon**: Cartesian in temper — the Encyclopedists,
  Rousseau, the Physiocrats, Condorcet; paradigmatically Sieyès urging the
  revolutionary assembly to act like men emerging from the state of nature to
  sign a social contract. The division crosses nationality: Montesquieu,
  Constant, and Tocqueville lean "British"; Hobbes, the English Jacobins
  (Godwin, Priestley, Price, Paine), and Jefferson after his French sojourn lean
  rationalist. See [[constructivist-rationalism]].
- Hayek maps Francis Lieber's 1848 "Anglican" vs. "Gallican" liberty onto the
  same divide, and (via Talmon) treats the rationalist line as the origin of
  totalitarian democracy — feeding the [[road-to-serfdom-thesis]].
- **Critique**: the dichotomy is widely criticized as a partisan genealogy that
  reads the tradition selectively (see Historiography below).

## Sociology of Liberty (chs. 3, 8: attributed)

- Progress "in echelon": rapid advance requires inequality, with the wealthy as
  involuntary "scouts" for consumption patterns later generalized — "Even the
  poorest today owe their relative material well-being to the results of past
  inequality." A planned society would need to designate its own experimenters.
- The rise of the **employed majority**: as the franchise extended to a
  wage-earning majority, policy and social insurance came to reflect the
  employee's outlook, disadvantaging the independent. Hayek rejects the Marxist
  expropriation account of proletarianization: "If 'capitalism has created the
  proletariat,' it has done so... by enabling large numbers to survive and
  procreate."
- The **independent man of means** as a distinct social function: funding
  minority causes (abolition, penal reform) and cultural experimentation the
  market and majority will not yet support; Hayek laments the decline of the
  gentleman-scholar (Darwin, Macaulay, Tocqueville) in favor of state-employed
  intellectuals.
- Freedom of the employed depends on plurality of employers; a state monopoly of
  employment is total coercion (quoting Trotsky: "who does not obey shall not
  eat") — see [[socialist-calculation-debate]].
- **Academic freedom** as plurality of independent centers of research and
  funding, not individual license — analogous to judicial independence.

## Decline, Terminology, and "Why I Am Not a Conservative"

Hayek dates the end of England's original contributions to about 1800, as
Bentham's utilitarians and French-derived political liberty displaced the older
Whig conception of individual liberty under law. In the twentieth century the
word "liberal" itself was captured: in America it came to denote New Deal-era
statism, while in Europe "rationalistic liberalism has long been one of the
pacemakers of socialism."

The Postscript's argument (among the most cited essays in modern political
thought): conservatism "by its very nature cannot offer an alternative to the
direction in which we are moving" — it lacks principles of direction, fears
change ("a timid distrust of the new as such"), trusts authority over rules
("less concerned with the problem of how the powers of government should be
limited than with that of who wields them"), is selectively interventionist
(protectionist and dirigiste in agriculture), obscurantist toward unwelcome
knowledge, and prone to nationalism, "the bridge from conservatism to
collectivism." He replaces the left-right spectrum with a **triangle** —
conservatives, socialists, liberals each at a corner, with conservatives
historically drifting toward the stronger socialist pull. Rejecting both
"liberal" (captured) and "libertarian" ("the flavor of a manufactured term"),
Hayek declares himself "simply an unrepentant Old Whig — with the stress on the
'old'" — the tradition of Burke (who "would have shuddered at the thought of
being regarded as a Tory"), Macaulay, Tocqueville, Acton, and in America
[[madison-james|James Madison]], "not... the radicalism of Jefferson, nor...
the conservatism of Hamilton or even of John Adams." He nonetheless credits
conservative thinkers (Coleridge, Möser, Bonald, De Maistre) with real insight
into spontaneously grown institutions — admired, he says, only in the past.

## The Trilogy Restatement (1973–79): negatives, concessions, and the "no third way"

*Law, Legislation and Liberty* sharpens the doctrine in three directions. **Principles over expediency** (Vol. 1): "freedom can be preserved only if it is treated as a supreme principle which must not be sacrificed for particular advantages," since case-by-case weighing is bound to sacrifice it; classical liberalism's historic defect "was not that it adhered too stubbornly to principles, but rather that it lacked principles sufficiently definite to provide clear guidance" — and utopia is methodologically necessary (Adam Smith thought full free trade in Britain "as absurd as … an Oceana or Utopia," yet it arrived seventy years later). **The three great negatives** (Vol. 3 ch. 18): "The three great negatives of Peace, Freedom and Justice are in fact the sole indispensable foundations of civilization which government must provide"; the supreme authority "can merely say 'no'"; democracy itself is "basically a negative value, a procedural rule which serves as protection against despotism." The basic conception: "government must regard all people as equal, however unequal they may in fact be."

**The concessions** (Vol. 3 ch. 14 — often surprising to readers of the polemics): Hayek explicitly rejects the "minimal state" — "we find it unquestionable that in an advanced society government ought to use its power of raising funds by taxation to provide a number of services which … cannot be provided, or cannot be provided adequately, by the market": collective goods proper (protection against violence, epidemics, natural disasters; roads; standards; land registers, maps, statistics), the guaranteed minimum-income floor as "a necessary part of the Great Society," Friedman-style education vouchers, and Cornuelle's voluntary "independent sector" (Alcoholics Anonymous: "No governmental agency has ever thought out or brought into being so effective an organization"). What he refuses is government *monopoly* of any service — money and post above all ("They were not established in order that people should be served better, but solely to enhance the powers of government"; the Bank of England's 1694 note monopoly was granted "because it was paid for it"), culminating in the denationalization-of-money clause: "Parliament shall make no law abridging the right of anybody to hold, buy, sell or lend, make and enforce contracts, calculate and keep their accounts in any kind of money they choose."

**The hard edges**: "the danger to personal freedom comes chiefly from the left … because the various socialist movements are the only large organized bodies which … want to impose upon society a preconceived design"; "There exists no third principle for the organization of the economics process which can be rationally chosen" between market and central direction; trade-union legal privileges are "rapidly destroying the economic order"; language decay (Dewey's liberty as "the effective power to do specific things") is part of the mechanism; and politics itself "has become much too important, much too costly and harmful" — the "dethronement of politics." All attributed positions; Ortega's epigraph to Vol. 2 ch. 11 captures Hayek's own sense of the doctrine's fragility — liberalism as "the supreme form of generosity … a discipline too difficult and complex to take firm root on earth."

## Limitations and Critiques

- The "two traditions" dichotomy is criticized as a partisan genealogy: it
  reads Bentham, Mill (inventor, per Hayek, of *homo oeconomicus*), and the
  Philosophic Radicals out of a tradition they historically dominated, and
  flattens deep 19th-century disagreements (utilitarians vs. natural-rights
  theorists; free traders vs. imperial liberals).
- The retrospective "classical" label is itself etic and contested; historians
  dispute whether pre-1900 liberalism was as laissez-faire as the 20th-century
  reconstruction implies.
- Hayek's own concessions and tensions: fugitive resources and neighborhood
  effects justify regulation; his denial of tenure to Communists ("tolerance
  should not include the advocacy of intolerance") sits uneasily with his
  non-coercion principle.
