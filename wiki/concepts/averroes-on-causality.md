## Definition and Scope

Averroes' defense of natural causation is the most influential section of the [[tahafut-al-tahafut]] (Incoherence of the Incoherence), responding to [[ghazali-on-causality]] as set out in the *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*. The core claim, attributed to [[al-ghazali]], is that the connection between what is normally called a "cause" and what is normally called an "effect" is not necessary: God could create satiety without eating, burning without contact with fire, or death without decapitation, because each of these is a separate divine creative act rather than a natural consequence. Averroes' reply is the most systematic philosophical defense of real, intrinsic natural causation produced in classical Arabic philosophy, and it became the foundation for Latin scholastic debates about natural necessity and causation after the translation of the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* into Latin in the thirteenth century (see [[arabic-philosophy-latin-reception]] and [[the-three-condemnations]]).

The discussion is framed in Physical Sciences Discussions 1–4 of the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*. It is not a positive elaboration of causal metaphysics but a sustained refutation of [[occasionalism-islamic]] — the Asharite theological doctrine that God directly produces every event without natural intermediaries — on the grounds that this doctrine destroys knowledge, science, and finally theology itself.

## The Occasionalist Position (al-Ghazālī's Argument)

Al-Ghazālī takes the paradigm case of fire and cotton: the burning of cotton when it is brought into contact with fire. For the Asharite occasionalist:

1. The agent of the burning is God, not the fire. Fire is a dead body with no action. What observation proves is only **simultaneity, not causation**: that burning occurs when fire is present does not establish that fire produces the burning.
2. God has the **power to create the cotton becoming ash without fire** or fire-contact without burning.
3. The connection between fire and burning is based on "a prior power of God to create them in a successive order, though not because this connexion is necessary in itself and cannot be disjoined."
4. The burning capacity attributed to fire should instead be attributed to the **bestower of forms** (a separate angelic principle) — a position al-Ghazālī credits to "the true philosophers," ironically.

Al-Ghazālī illustrates the epistemological consequence with the example of a man blind from birth who has the membrane removed from his eyes: he will wrongly believe that opening his eyelids caused his sight until nightfall reveals that sunlight was the true cause. The implication is that all our causal inferences from co-occurrence are equally unreliable.

## Averroes' Reply: The Core Arguments

### 1. Denying Causes is Sophistry that Destroys the Intellect

Averroes opens with his sharpest statement:

> "To deny the existence of efficient causes which are observed in sensible things is sophistry, and he who defends this doctrine either denies with his tongue what is present in his mind or is carried away by a sophistical doubt which occurs to him concerning this question. For he who denies this can no longer acknowledge that every act must have an agent."

This is the foundational charge: occasionalism is not a coherent position but a form of self-refuting sophistry. Denying that things have the natures from which their characteristic acts flow leads immediately to the conclusion that things have no natures at all — and therefore no essences, no names, no definitions. If a thing had no specific nature, "it would not have a special name nor a definition, and all things would be one — indeed, not even one."

### 2. The Epistemological Catastrophe

Averroes makes the argument structural: if there are no necessary causal connections, knowledge itself is destroyed:

> "Intelligence is nothing but the perception of things with their causes, and in this it distinguishes itself from all the other faculties of apprehension, and he who denies causes must deny the intellect. Logic implies the existence of causes and effects, and knowledge of these effects can only be rendered perfect through knowledge of their causes. Denial of cause implies the denial of knowledge, and denial of knowledge implies that nothing in this world can be really known, and that what is supposed to be known is nothing but opinion, that neither proof nor definition exist, and that the essential attributes which compose definitions are void."

This is a transcendental argument: causal necessity is a precondition of rational knowledge. If all causal connections are merely habitual regularities maintained by arbitrary divine volition, then *there is no science* — not only natural science but also theology, since God's nature could not be inferred from the order of the world. Al-Ghazālī's theology thus inadvertently undermines the rational basis of theology itself.

### 3. Critique of "Habit" as a Substitute for Nature

Al-Ghazālī had proposed that while connections are not necessary, God has "created in us the knowledge that He will not do all these possible things," and that "protracted habit time after time fixes their occurrence in our minds." Averroes' reply targets the ambiguity of "habit" (ʿāda):

- If habit means "God's custom," this is impossible: God has no habits, as the Quran itself states ("Thou shalt not find any alteration in the course of God").
- If habit means something in existing things, "habit can only exist in the animated; if it exists in something else, it is really a nature."
- If habit means our subjective regularity of expectation, it is a feature of the soul determined by its nature — but then it is precisely a *natural* determination, not an arbitrary one.

The conclusion is that what the theologians call "habit" either collapses into "nature" (the philosophers' term for real causal structure) or produces incoherence. A world governed only by divine arbitrary will without stable natures would leave permanent knowledge of anything impossible — "we suppose such an agent to rule existents like a tyrannical prince who has the highest power, for whom nobody in his dominion can deputize, of whom no standard or custom is known to which reference might be made."

### 4. Essentialism and the Four Causes

Averroes insists that things have *essences* from which *characteristic acts* flow. A fire that has the nature of fire and yet does not burn is a contradiction, not a miracle:

> "Things have essences and attributes which determine the special functions of each thing and through which the essences and names of things are differentiated. If a thing had not its specific nature, it would not have a special name nor a definition."

He also invokes Aristotle's four causes (agent, form, matter, end) as self-evidently necessary for events. The theologians' own vocabulary of "conditions" and "substrata" (what philosophers call matter and form) already presupposes causal structure: "they here judge the visible and the invisible according to one and the same scheme."

### 5. The Role of External Conditions vs. Denial of Natures

Averroes makes a crucial concession that defuses al-Ghazālī's miracle examples: it is entirely compatible with natural causation to hold that an external condition may *block* a causal power. Fire has the nature to burn, but "it is not impossible that fire may sometimes be brought near cotton without burning it, when something is placed with the cotton that makes it non-inflammable" — as in the case of talc. This is not a denial of fire's burning nature but an acknowledgment that causal powers require the absence of blocking conditions. Miracles need not require God to act directly in the absence of natural causes; they may require only that God or an angel modify the conditions (e.g., create a new property in the fire that limits its action to its own body). As Averroes notes, this is actually Ghazālī's own final position in the third point of his discussion.

### 6. Necessary Connection and the Nature Argument

The deepest argument turns on the structure of knowledge and reality:

> "True knowledge is the knowledge of a thing as it is in reality. And if in reality there only existed, in regard both to the substratum and to the Agent, the possibility of the two opposites, there would no longer, even for the twinkling of an eye, be any permanent knowledge of anything."

Averroes argues that the very existence of prophetic knowledge and revelation presupposes a determinate natural order. The prophet knows that a future event will occur because there is a *nature* in existing things that makes it preponderant — "the knowledge of the existence of this nature which causes the actualization of one of the opposites." If the world were genuinely indeterminate, prophecy would be epistemically incoherent: there would be nothing to know. Eternal divine knowledge and eternal divine will are the *causes* of these natures in existents, not their replacements.

### 7. On the Limits of the Impossible

Averroes and al-Ghazālī reach a partial agreement: the impossible consists in "the simultaneous affirmation and negation of a thing." God cannot create a will without an object willed, cannot create knowledge in the inorganic (which is defined as non-perceiving), cannot transform one genus into another (since there is no common matter between accident and substance). When al-Ghazālī asks whether God could move a dead man's hand so that it writes orderly text — Averroes grants this is not logically impossible, but "only to be denied because the habitual course of nature is in opposition to it." This is itself a concession to natural regularity as the operative norm.

## Relation to the Bestower of Forms

On the question of *where* natural causal efficacy is grounded, Averroes distinguishes philosophers' views:

- Some philosophers say that perceptible existents do not act on each other at all and that causal work is performed exclusively by the "bestower of forms" (a separate intellect/angel) — but Averroes notes this is not the position of "any important philosopher."
- Most philosophers hold that warmth causes warmth, and the four elemental qualities act on each other, while the separate principles act as conditions for the existence of fire and natural processes, not as their sole agents.

Averroes' own position is that natural bodies are genuine (if not entirely self-sufficient) efficient causes: "we need not doubt that some of these existents cause each other and act through each other, and that in themselves they do not suffice for their act, but that they are in need of an external agent whose act is a condition of their act."

## Significance

This discussion established the parameters of subsequent debate in both Islamic and Latin Christian philosophy. Averroes' charge that occasionalism destroys science was taken up in Latin scholasticism. The argument that causal necessity is a precondition of intelligibility — not a limitation on divine power — became the basis of natural philosophy curricula in medieval European universities. The tension between divine omnipotence and natural regularity that Averroes diagnosed here was never fully resolved, and reappeared in Hume's epistemological skepticism about causation and in early modern debates about miracles.

## Historiography

The *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* as a whole, and Discussion 1 in particular, is the most studied text in classical Arabic philosophy. Scholarship divides on whether Averroes' defense of natural causation amounts to a strict necessitarianism (everything that happens is necessary given the natures of things) or a more moderate essentialism allowing for blocking conditions and probabilistic regularities. The passage on fire and cotton has a direct analogue in later European discussions of Humean constant conjunction versus necessary connection. Whether Averroes influenced Hume is a bibliographic question, but the structural parallel is real.

The [[occasionalism-islamic]] position Averroes attacks remained dominant in Asharite theology; the philosophical tradition he defends was increasingly marginalized in the Islamic world after the twelfth century while gaining influence in Latin Europe through the translation movement.
