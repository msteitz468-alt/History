# Ancient Household Economy and Gender

"In the Greco-Roman world the household was the basic unit of production as well as consumption"
(Richard Saller). This page consolidates the household as an economic institution — its structure,
inheritance rules, women's property regimes, guardianship of orphans, and the gendered division of
labor — drawn from Saller's chapter in
[[scheidel-cambridge-economic-history-greco-roman-world-2007|The Cambridge Economic History of the
Greco-Roman World]] (2007).

## The Household as Productive Unit

Saller treats [[xenophon|Xenophon's]] *Oeconomicus* (fourth-century BCE Athenian estate-management
treatise) as "the most influential Greco-Roman work on the 'economy,'" whose central theme — the
gendered division of labor in household production and consumption — scholarship has variously
dismissed as either not real economics or mere moral ideology, but which Saller argues does reflect
genuine practice.

The household (*oikos*/*domus*) was defined by coresidence, kinship, commensality, and economic
cooperation; monogamy was the norm, and divorce was available to both spouses. Marriage timing
followed a later-male/earlier-female pattern: Athenian men typically married in their thirties to
teenage girls; Roman senatorial men married in their early twenties to girls in their early teens;
humbler social strata married somewhat later on both sides. Legal and moral sources assume a nuclear
household — *paterfamilias*, wife, children, and non-working slaves sharing the household stores
(*penus*) — as the ideological norm, and neither Greece nor Rome developed an ideology of extended
multi-family ("frereche") households. In practice, however, especially in the Roman Egyptian census
data, extended multi-family households were common, particularly in rural areas. Partible
inheritance was universal across the Greco-Roman world; neither primogeniture nor ultimogeniture
developed.

Saller's demographic simulation of Roman household structure finds: average age at paternity around
36; roughly one child in three lost their father before puberty; roughly two in three were
fatherless by adulthood (age 25); only about 20% reached adulthood with both parents still alive.
This pervasive fatherlessness is directly linked to the guardianship system discussed below.

Saller distinguishes **two different conceptions of "patriarchy"** operating side by side in the
Greco-Roman world: Athenian patriarchy centered on the husband's domination of the wife (citizen
wives held almost no independent property rights), while Roman patriarchy centered on the father's
domination of children — there was no age of majority under *patria potestas* — even as wives
married *sine manu* held separate property from their husbands.

## Women's Property Rights: Athens

Athenian women operated under a *kyrios* (male guardian — father, then husband, then an adult son
or other kinsman) who controlled property on their behalf; a woman's own legally enforceable
transactions were capped at the value of one *medimnos* of barley, roughly a week's food.

Athenian daughters received **dowries** rather than inheriting real property (which passed to sons)
— mostly movable wealth (cash, furniture, slaves), secured against land marked by *horoi* stones.
Attested dowry values ranged from 500 drachmas to over a talent, representing 5–20% of family
wealth; the husband, as *kyrios*, controlled the dowry but owed 18% annual interest on it if the
marriage ended by death or divorce, until it was returned.

The **epiklerate** governed the special case of a daughter with no brothers (*epikleros*): she
inherited passively, was married to her closest male kinsman, and the estate passed to the sons of
that marriage two years after their puberty. The alternative was adoption of a son who married the
epikleros and forfeited his own patrimony claim in the process. Athenian women rarely acted as
professional moneylenders or in large-scale commerce, and are nearly absent both from the Eleusinian
building accounts (320s BCE) and from Athenian manumission inscriptions as slave-owners in their own
right.

By contrast, the **Gortyn Code** (Crete, fifth century BCE) recognized individualized property
rights for wives and mothers, unlike the unitary Athenian household — daughters there inherited half
a son's share of both paternal and maternal property. Aristotle (*Politics* 1270a23-6) complained
that Spartan women owned roughly 40% of property in fourth-century-BCE Sparta, sufficient to
underwrite ownership of Olympic chariot-racing teams.

## Women's Property Rights: Rome

Classical Roman marriage was typically *sine manu*: the wife remained under her own father's *patria
potestas* and retained her own property rather than transferring it to her husband. On her father's
death she became *sui iuris*, with property rights "nearly equal to men's," subject only to a
*tutor mulieris* (never her husband) for major transactions. Roman dowries ranged from tens of
thousands of sesterces (curial families) to as much as 1 million sesterces (senatorial daughters);
the conventional size was 5–10% of the father's estate — roughly one year's patrimony income — paid
in three annual installments, modest and non-inflating by comparison with some later European dowry
systems. The *Senatus Consultum de Pisone patre* records the senator Piso's daughter Calpurnia
awarded a 1-million-sesterce dowry plus 4 million sesterces in additional property by the Senate,
illustrating these proportions in an actual case.

Legal reform steadily loosened guardianship over Roman women: Augustus's *ius trium liberorum*
exempted women with three children from guardianship, and Claudius abolished mandatory agnatic
guardianship altogether. Saller characterizes the resulting discretion of Roman women over property
and wills as "unparalleled in European history before the twentieth century." Estimates that women
owned roughly 20% of Roman-world wealth are probably too low (Champlin 1991); roughly a third of the
brickyard owners catalogued by Setälä (49 of 149 named owners) were women. The *Senatus Consultum
Velleianum* (first half of the first century CE) barred enforcing women's suretyship on loans,
reflecting a perceived vulnerability to male pressure — while the Murecine wax tablets (Bay of
Naples, mid-first century CE) show women actively engaged in business, loans, slave sales, and
suretyship, always transacted through a male *tutor*, often via slave or freedman agents (Cicero's
freedman Eros, Terentia's freedman Philotimus) who served men and women alike.

## Guardianship of Orphans

Athenian law required guardians for fatherless boys under 17–18 and girls under 14, overseen by
senior magistrates (the *archon eponymous*; the Roman equivalent being the praetor or consul).
Roman guardianship extended, between 200 BCE and 200 CE, from *impuberes* (under roughly 14) to
*minores* (up to age 25). Saller estimates that as much as a third of Athenian children inherited
before adulthood, and as many as two-thirds of Roman males inherited before being deemed competent
adults following the extension of the *curator minorum* — implying that perhaps a sixth of all
property in these societies was, at any given time, owned by orphans, plausibly biasing investment
toward conservative, land-based holdings. A ruling of Septimius Severus (195 CE) barred guardians
from selling wards' rural or suburban properties (*Dig.* 27.9.1 pr.), reinforcing this conservative
freeze on orphans' estates.

## Gendered Division of Labor

An ideology of male-outdoor/female-indoor labor, traceable back to Homer, had "a marked effect in
limiting women's range of occupation," though Saller judges it "not entirely wrong" as a description
of actual practice; the pattern remained broadly stable for roughly seven centuries, from
fifth-century-BCE Athens to second-century-CE Rome.

Athenian manumission inscriptions show 31 of 41 female ex-slaves with recorded occupations were
wool-workers (*talasiourgoi*), with the remainder scattered across eight other occupations.
Treggiari's study of Roman funerary occupation inscriptions found only 35 distinct occupations
attested for women against roughly 225 for men, with women concentrated in service, catering, food
dealing, shopkeeping, prostitution, cloth crafts, and "fiddly" trades (gold-leaf work,
hairdressing, perfumery). Table 4.1 (Joshel's tabulation of *CIL* 6) shows women recorded at 0% in
building, banking, and transportation; 3% in administration; 8% in sales; 15% in manufacture; 16% in
professional occupations; 27% in domestic service; and 47% in skilled service. Roman apprenticeship
contracts from Egypt (mostly weaving, one to five years) record almost no freeborn women as
apprentices — some free boys, some slave boys and girls, but "not one is a freeborn woman" — suggesting
the gender ideology specifically limited formal training and human-capital investment in free women.

Saller draws a comparative contrast with the American antebellum South, which used a higher
proportion of female slaves as field hands (69%, versus 58% male) than Roman estates did; Roman
jurists' concepts of *fundus instructus* and *instrumentum instrumenti* suggest slave women were
treated as domestic "adjuncts" rather than as central field labor, unlike the practice on US
plantations (citing Brunt 1971, Erdkamp 1999, Scheidel 1995). Formal paid education began with the
fifth-century-BCE Greek sophists; by the late Republic and imperial Rome, wealthy households
sometimes gave literacy and numeracy training to slaves — but Saller estimates well under 10% of the
population benefited from this kind of formal investment, given how short life expectancy limited
its returns.

## Significance

Saller's household framework supplies the microeconomic unit underlying the volume's account of
Greco-Roman [[ancient-demography|demography]] and labor markets: property devolution, guardianship,
and gendered labor allocation together shaped patterns of investment, human-capital formation, and
the reach of formal legal institutions treated in [[roman-law-and-economic-institutions]].
