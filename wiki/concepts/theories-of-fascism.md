# Theories of Fascism

Distinct from the *definitional* debate recorded at [[generic-fascism]],
this page records the rival **causal interpretations** of fascism — what
produced it and what it "really" was. The canonical taxonomy is Payne's
twelve-category survey (1980, ch. 8;
[[payne-fascism-comparison-definition-1980]]), prefaced by his
meta-critique: "few of those who attempt to develop a causal theory or
explanatory concept of fascism define exactly what they mean by the
term... The very absence of an empirical definition... has been a major
obstacle to conceptual clarification."

## Payne's twelve categories (1980)

1. **Agent of bourgeois capitalism.** Formulated before Italian Fascism
   was even organized (to explain Mussolini's break from socialism); given
   currency from 1923 by Gyula Sas and Clara Zetkin, becoming the standard
   Comintern line; western exponents R. Palme Dutt, Daniel Guérin; later
   Marxists Kühnl, Poulantzas, Lopukhov, Galkin, Vajda (the last two with
   "serious modifications"). Payne's critique: Marxist interpreters
   generally refuse to distinguish core fascism from right
   authoritarianism — and (from ch. 3) the Turner-line evidence shows the
   right authoritarians, not the Nazis, were the party of big business.
   See [[fascism-and-the-political-left]].
2. **Twentieth-century Bonapartism.** Thalheimer (1930): a crisis of
   "relative class equilibrium" lets dictatorship free itself of class
   domination "transitorily." Payne (ch. 2) rejects
   Bonapartism-as-first-fascism: the Second Empire "never attempted a
   party movement, much less a new political militia," and resembled the
   interwar *nonfascist* rightist systems. (He notes Samuel Farber applies
   the concept to Castro's revolution "not altogether unconvincingly.")
3. **Radicalism of the middle classes.** Salvatorelli's *Nazionalfascismo*
   (1923) on the "humanistic petite bourgeoisie"; De Felice and Volpe;
   Lipset's "extremism of the center." Payne: explains recruitment
   partially but "fails to account for the numbers of fascist supporters
   outside the middle classes" (Germany, Hungary, Romania) — his ch. 3
   data show NSDAP membership one-third blue-collar by 1933 and the SA
   two-thirds blue-collar in 1932–34, with urban middle-class support only
   proportionate; the "losers in modernization" reading (Sauer) founders
   on white-collar employees and teachers being winners.
4. **Unique national histories** (German/Italian "national disease").
   "Increasingly lost support because of the superficiality of their
   analyses... unhistorical and unempirical reductionism." Cf.
   [[german-sonderweg]].
5. **Cultural or moral breakdown** (Croce, Meinecke, Drucker). Explains
   permissive conditions, not specific content; countered by Gregor's *The
   Ideology of Fascism* (1969) — fascism as consequence of specific new
   ideas, "not the product of nihilistic collapse."
6. **Unique metapolitical phenomenon.** Nolte's phenomenology (fascism as
   anti-Marxism adopting Bolshevik techniques, "resistance to
   transcendence") — "few scholars have accepted Nolte's exact
   formulations." Variants: Eugen Weber (a revolutionary project in its
   own right), Mosse (a revolution of the right with transcendental goals
   of its own), Del Noce (revolutionary nationalism of the "first age of
   secularization"; Italian Fascism the competitor of Leninism, Nazism the
   "competitive counterpart of Stalinism"). Merkl adds generational
   revolt as "the one great motivating force" common to the movements.
7. **Neurotic/pathological psychosocial impulses.** Fromm (*Escape from
   Freedom*), Reich (*The Mass Psychology of Fascism* — sexual
   repression), Adorno (*The Authoritarian Personality*). Payne:
   Fromm/Reich "unverifiably speculative"; Adorno more empirical, but
   later work substantiated no clear middle-class or central-European
   personality trait — one study "found communist personalities as
   'authoritarian' as those of fascists."
8. **Rise of amorphous masses.** Ortega, Lederer, Arendt, Kornhauser.
   Payne: obfuscates ideological content; many supporters belonged to
   intact structured sectors (cf. Paxton's "richly structured" Weimar
   society, on [[generic-fascism]]).
9. **Manifestation of totalitarianism.** Friedrich–Brzezinski (1956);
   1950s vogue. Undercut by Arendt herself excepting Fascist Italy and by
   Sauer's Fascism/Nazism-vs-communism differences; Payne dismisses
   Buchheim's definitional efforts ("The essence of fascism is rebellion
   against freedom") as "fatuities." Payne's own strict definition —
   total control over all significant aspects of all major institutions —
   yields: Italy never totalitarian; Germany short of it before 1945;
   "only a socialist or communist system can achieve full totalitarianism."
10. **Resistance to modernization.** Turner (most succinct statement),
    Sauer's "losers," Barrington Moore's "aberrant modernization
    controlled by martial, rural elites," Nolte's anti-transcendence;
    Cassels's "two faces" qualification (modernizing in underdeveloped
    countries, anti-modern in industrial Germany). Vigorously combatted by
    Gregor; Payne's own chs. 1 and 4 side with modernity — fascist ideas
    were "a direct byproduct of aspects of the Enlightenment," and "Nazi
    racism was conceivable only in the twentieth century." Cf.
    [[german-modernism]] and Griffin's modernism thesis.
11. **Stage of socioeconomic growth / developmental dictatorship.**
    Organski (fascism when the industrial sector first equals the primary
    sector — "doesn't apply to Germany at all"); Garruccio ("extremely
    suggestive... but fails to identify... the unique historical features
    of European fascism"); Gregor's developmental-dictatorship theory
    (Italian Fascism as "prototype of the mass mobilizing development
    dictatorship") — which Payne notes is "not specifically applied to
    other presumed fascisms."
12. **Nominalist denial** that generic fascism exists. Allardyce, "What
    Fascism is Not" (1979); partially Bracher and Lukacs. "Depending on
    how rigidly... the category... is defined, they may well be right" —
    Payne's answer is the loose typological description at
    [[payne-typology-of-authoritarian-nationalism]].

## Theories of the Nazi seizure of power (Payne 1980, ch. 3)

A nested application: Payne sorts explanations of Hitler's 1933 triumph
into the **accident theory** (the "back stairs deal" — true as far as it
goes, but silent on why the German center-right had swung authoritarian),
the **agent theory** ("fails to demonstrate specifically just whose agent
Hitler was and on exactly what terms"), and his preferred **mass
mobilization of frustrated nationalism**: Germany as the newest major
industrial power and "by far the lowest in international status," with an
unmatched concatenation of crises (1918 humiliation, 1919–20 revolution,
1923 invasion/inflation, depression) striking a political culture more
deeply marked by the 1890–1914 cultural crisis than any other. He retains
a residue of accident: the Nazi vote was already falling by late 1932 —
"if the general quality of German leadership had not been so poor, Hitler
might well have been resisted one more year," and by end-1933 the
groundswell might have receded. On the big-business question he endorses
Turner against both vulgar-Marxist and "counterrevisionist" readings, with
the barbed dictum that it is "greatly to be regretted" the Marxist view is
wrong — "if German big business had managed to buy Hitler there would
probably have been no general agreement for war."

## Documentary grounding: Griffin's 1995 reader

Griffin's Oxford Reader ([[griffin-fascism-1995]], Part IV) anthologizes
the theorists themselves — Nolte (resistance to transcendence), Mosse
(cultural revolution of the right), Payne (typological description),
Sternhell, Linz, De Felice, Gregor, Eatwell — alongside *contemporary*
interwar reactions (Pareto on nationalism as political religion, early
Marxist verdicts), providing primary excerpts for most positions in
Payne's twelve-category survey above. Griffin's editorial frame treats
all rival theories as competing Weberian ideal types organized around his
own palingenetic core. *(Note: a fuller set of grounded quotations from
this reader was added and lost in a same-day edit collision; re-extract
from Part IV of the reader if depth is needed.)*

## Emic vs. etic; limitations

Entirely etic — a historiographers' taxonomy. Payne's survey is itself a
position: each theory is graded against his own typological empiricism,
and his verdicts (esp. on Marxist and psychohistorical theories) are
contested by the schools concerned. The categories are "not always
mutually exclusive," and post-1980 developments (Griffin's palingenetic
"new consensus," Paxton's processual model, political-religion theory)
postdate the survey — see [[generic-fascism]] for those.
