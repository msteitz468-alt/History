# Virtus

Virtus was the preeminent martial and civic virtue of Roman aristocratic culture, denoting courage, physical and moral excellence, and — in its most literal etymological sense — the qualities proper to a "vir" (a true man). Unlike a purely private ethical disposition, virtus was fundamentally competitive and public: it had to be demonstrated and witnessed, typically through battlefield valor (single combat, decisive charges, wounds received facing the enemy), to translate into the political capital (office, command, honor) that Roman aristocratic careers depended upon.

Classicist Myles McDonnell has argued that virtus in its earlier, more restricted Republican sense referred narrowly to battlefield courage and only later, particularly through Cicero's philosophical adaptations under Greek Stoic and ethical influence, broadened into something closer to a general moral excellence encompassing wisdom, justice, and self-control — a semantic expansion contested among classicists, some of whom see greater continuity in virtus's broader meaning across periods.

Virtus functioned within a wider honor culture — closely related to the "face of battle" dynamics that made personal reputation for courage a currency convertible into political advancement — and was cultivated through public monuments, funeral orations, and family memory (the *imagines* of ancestors), reinforcing an aristocratic ethos in which martial performance and political legitimacy were tightly, and self-reinforcingly, linked.
