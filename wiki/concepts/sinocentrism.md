# Sinocentrism

## Definition and Scope

Sinocentrism is an **etic** historiographical concept — applied by modern scholars — denoting the worldview, embedded in Chinese historical sources and perpetuated in Western scholarship on East Asia, that Chinese civilization represented the highest cultural level in the region, with all surrounding peoples defined by their degree of distance from Chinese moral and political norms. In the context of frontier history and the study of China-nomad interaction, sinocentrism refers specifically to the tendency, both emic (in the original texts) and etic (in modern scholarship that takes those texts at face value), to interpret Chinese–barbarian encounters as a civilizational confrontation — civilization versus barbarism — rather than as political and military competition between polities.

The concept is distinct from mere cultural pride or ethnocentrism. As a historiographical problem, sinocentrism concerns the way the presumption of Chinese cultural centrality has shaped the reading of sources and the framing of questions. The most consequential form of this bias, for Di Cosmo's argument in *Ancient China and Its Enemies* (2002), is the "culturalist" approach: the reading of Zhou-era texts on foreign peoples primarily as evidence of an emerging Chinese cultural consciousness, rather than as reflections of political and military struggles that used cultural rhetoric as a tool.

**Emic vs. etic distinction:** The Chinese worldview of a radiating civilization was genuinely emic — it was how Zhou literati and statesmen represented geographic and social space. The error is etic: scholars who treat this emic rhetoric as transparent evidence of actual cultural boundaries, rather than as political discourse requiring contextual analysis.

## The Emic Worldview: Concentric Zones and Radiating Civilization

The spatial imagination of early Chinese texts was organized around the idea of a center of moral and political authority (the Chou dynastic seat) radiating outward through progressively less-civilized zones. This structure is articulated most explicitly in the "Yü Kung" chapter of the *Shu ching*, though the composition of that text may date to the late Warring States (fourth–third century BCE).

The "Yü Kung" describes **five nested square zones** extending outward from the royal domain:
1. *Tien* — the royal domain
2. *Hou* — the land of feudal vassals
3. *Sui* — the zone of pacification
4. *Yao* — the zone of "vassal foreigners," where the Yi peoples live
5. *Huang* — the zone of uncultivated marshes, where the Man foreigners live

The *Chou li* elaborates variants: a nine-zone (*chiu chi*) division in chapter 29 and a six-zone (*fu*) arrangement in chapter 37. The *Kuo yü* places the Ti and Jung people in the outermost zone. The *Hsün-tzu* states the organizing principle with programmatic clarity: "All the states of Hsia share the same territorial zones (*fu*) and the same customs; Man, Yi, Jung and Ti share the same territorial zones, but have different institutions."

These schemas were not empirical maps. They were **cosmological-ethical schemes** designed simultaneously to demonstrate the spatial equivalence of earth and heaven and to mark the ideal boundary between the Hua-Hsia (Chinese) community and the world outside it. The farther a people from the moral-political center, the less the civilizing influence they received, and the more "alien" they were. Di Cosmo is explicit: "The texts that mention these foreign peoples are expressions of an intellectual world unconcerned with the systematic exploration and empirical description of the surrounding geographical area and 'ethnographic' realities."

## The Ethnonyms: Yi, Di, Rong, Ti, Man, Hu

Early Chinese texts employ a large inventory of ethnonyms for non-Chinese peoples. The principal ones relevant to northern frontier history are:

- **Yi** (夷) — associated with the east, in the *yao* (inner-foreign) zone; the binome Man-Yi indicated allied or assimilated foreigners
- **Man** (蠻) — associated with the south, also in the inner-foreign zone
- **Jung / Rong** (戎) — associated with the west and northwest; the term likely meant "martial" or "military" as much as ethnic identity; the character appears in Western Zhou texts meaning "enemy belligerents." Shaughnessy notes that by the Western Chou period *jung* may simply have designated "enemy belligerents" rather than a specific ethnic group
- **Ti** (狄) — associated with the north; divided in the Spring and Autumn period into at least two major groupings, the Ch'ih (Red) Ti and the Pai (White) Ti; whether "Ti" was a generic word for "northern foreigners," a specific ethnonym, or a political unit "cannot be determined" (Di Cosmo)
- **Hu** (胡) — a later term, applied primarily to the steppe nomads encountered from the Warring States period onward
- **Ch'iang** (羌) — another peripheral-people term used in various contexts

The standard English translation "barbarian" for any of these terms is misleading. There is no single Chinese analog to the Greek *barbaros*. Translating these terms uniformly as "barbarians" obscures a critical distinction embedded in the Chinese sources themselves: the difference between *yao* peoples (allied, assimilated, potentially absorbable within the Chou territorial sphere) and *fan* peoples (independent, resistant, outside Chou authority). The *yao* peoples were associated with the Man-Yi binome; the *fan* peoples with the Jung-Ti binome. Di Cosmo notes: "This distinction is hopelessly obscured when the blanket term 'barbarian' is used indiscriminately."

A second important distinction: the directional assignments of these peoples (Man to the south, Yi to the east, Jung to the west, Ti to the north) were idealized cartographic conventions, not ethnographic descriptions. In practice, these ethnonyms were applied flexibly.

## The "Culturalist" Reading and Di Cosmo's Critique

The **culturalist** or **culturalist-ideological** approach to early Chinese foreign relations holds that during the Spring and Autumn period a clear consciousness emerged of a moral and cultural divide between the Chou states and the surrounding "barbarian" world. On this reading, statements in the *Ch'un ch'iu*, the *Tso-chuan*, and later texts about the bestiality or moral deficiency of the Ti and Jung are evidence of a maturing Hua-Hsia cultural identity — a "Chinese" civilization becoming self-aware by differentiating itself from the alien outside.

Di Cosmo's central argument in Chapter 3 of *Ancient China and Its Enemies* is a systematic refutation of this reading. He argues that:

1. **The rhetoric of cultural differentiation was deployed instrumentally** for political and military purposes. When Kuan Chung, the counsellor of Duke Huan of Ch'i, declared in 661 BCE that "The Ti and the Jung are like wolves, and can never be satisfied; all the Hsia [states] are closely related, and none should be abandoned," this statement needs to be read in its political context: Ch'i was being asked to intervene in defense of Hsing against Ti incursions. Only years later, Wey — a Chou state sharing the same royal surname as Hsing — annexed Hsing itself, violating the very kinship principles Kuan Chung invoked. The rhetoric of civilizational solidarity was instrumentalized when convenient and abandoned when inconvenient.

2. **The Yi-Ti category was not ethnically fixed** — it was a political stigma applicable to any state or people whose behavior violated accepted norms. The *Ku-liang* commentary brands the state of **Chin** as Yi-Ti because it allied with Ti troops to strike at Chou political authority. The state of **Ch'u** is called Yi-Ti because its ruler treacherously ambushed and killed the lord of a Central State. The state of **Wu** is called Yi-Ti on the grounds that "its people shave their hair and tattoo their bodies" — but Wu was accepted into the Chou interstate system and Chin even taught Wu chariot warfare. "We find the boundary between the Yi-Ti and the Chou anything but clearly defined," Di Cosmo concludes.

3. **Fu Ch'en's famous denunciation of the Ti** (636 BCE) — "Those whose ears cannot hear the harmony of the five sounds are deaf; those whose eyes cannot distinguish among the five colors are blind; those whose minds do not conform to the standards of virtue and righteousness are perverse; those whose mouths do not speak words of loyalty and faith are foolish chatterers. The Ti conform to these four evils" — is not evidence of a deep cultural cleavage. Fu Ch'en was a court minister opposing a pro-Ti policy faction; his rhetoric illustrates a *locus classicus* of Chinese politics, the struggle between the outer court (ministers) and inner court (king's wives and concubines). The king ignored him and allied with the Ti anyway.

4. **The "pacivist" vs. "militarist" doctrines** toward non-Chou peoples were not expressions of Confucian or Legalist philosophical orientations. They were pragmatic responses to military and economic circumstances. The association of Confucianism with peace and Legalism with force "cannot be established before the Ch'in-Han period, and perhaps only at the time of the *Discourses on Salt and Iron*" (first century BCE). Before that, Chou states shifted between confrontation and accommodation based on calculation of costs and benefits, not philosophical conviction.

5. **The paucity of genuine ethnographic information** in early Chinese sources about non-Chou peoples is itself diagnostic. The scant attention to cultural difference, compared to the wealth of information about political relations, "suggests that the early Chinese chroniclers were interested almost exclusively in political events, understood as, and dominated by, ethical norms."

The conclusion Di Cosmo draws: "Boundaries between presumed cultural communities in the Eastern Chou period appear to have been drawn ad hoc, according to ever-changing political circumstances... The relatively rare statements that attempted to establish cultural or political boundaries are inadequate for us to determine the substance of the sense of cultural consolidation of the Chou community of states against non-Chou peoples, especially when we consider the historical context."

## Political Contexts: Conquest, Diplomacy, and Incorporation

A key corollary of Di Cosmo's critique is that early Chinese–non-Chou relations operated on three levels that had little to do with cultural demarcation:

**Conquest**: The Chou states regularly conquered Ti and Jung peoples and absorbed them into their territories. The justifications were moral — couched in the language of virtue and just punishment — but the actual objectives were territorial and military expansion. Chin's extinction of the Ch'ih Ti tribe in 594 BCE followed a pretext about the crimes of the Ti minister Feng Shu; the underlying political goal was territorial acquisition. "Having found an excuse that could justify the use of violence and lead to expansion, Chin should use it right away."

**Diplomacy**: Non-Chou peoples participated fully in the Spring and Autumn interstate system — making covenants (*meng*), exchanging hostages, attending political conferences. They were parties to the same treaty protocols as Chou states, including the blood oath ritual. The distinction between Chou and non-Chou mattered for certain ritual purposes (the disposition of war spoils), but it was not an absolute barrier to diplomatic intercourse.

**Incorporation**: As the Chou states expanded, they incorporated non-Chou peoples into their military establishments, administrative units, and frontier societies. By 541 BCE Chin had transformed its chariot army into infantry partly to fight Jung and Ti foot soldiers. By the late sixth century BCE, Ti peoples were "fully integrated in Chinese states' military establishments." The new administrative unit *chou* (county) was explicitly created in the seventh century BCE to govern newly absorbed non-Chou subjects. Marriage diplomacy brought Jung women into princely houses, and half-Jung offspring governed frontier territories precisely because of their dual nature.

The result, as Di Cosmo summarizes: "In their wars against the Jung and Ti, the Chou states did not have to observe rules of virtuous behavior, whose violation might otherwise cause political damage to the perpetrator." Moral sanctions were invoked against the non-Chou as a convenience of conquest, not as the expression of genuine cultural solidarity.

## Limits and Critiques of the Concept

Di Cosmo does not deny that cultural differences existed between Chou and non-Chou peoples. The Jung chief's own speech is explicit: "the food, drink, and clothes of us Jung are all different from those of China (Hua), we do not exchange silk with them, and our languages are not mutually understandable." The point is that this acknowledged cultural distance coexisted with deep political integration: "The Jung were an integral part of Chou politics and an additional source of soldiers and farmers."

Scholars working within the culturalist tradition — including Cho-yun Hsu in the *Cambridge History of Ancient China* — have argued for a genuine emergence of Hua-Hsia cultural consciousness during the Spring and Autumn period. Di Cosmo's counter is that the evidence for this consciousness is largely retrospective: the *Ku-liang* and *Kung-yang* commentaries that most strongly assert an unbridgeable Chou-barbarian divide "reflect ethical positions that were held much later than the events they comment on."

A further limitation: Di Cosmo's argument applies specifically to the Spring and Autumn and early Warring States periods, before the rise of the Xiongnu confederation and the long military confrontation of the Han-Xiongnu era (third–first centuries BCE). The encounter with organized steppe nomads, from the Warring States period onward, did eventually produce clearer and more stable doctrines of foreign relations — doctrines that drew more explicitly on the sinocentric worldview. The *heqin* ("harmony and kinship") policy of the early Han and the debates recorded in the *Discourses on Salt and Iron* represent a more crystallized, if still contested, articulation of how China should relate to the nomadic world.
