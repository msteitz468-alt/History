# The Lockean Consensus (Hartz Thesis)

[[hartz-louis|Louis Hartz]]'s master concept: because America was settled by
emigrants fleeing Europe's feudal and clerical order, it is a **liberal
fragment** — a society "born equal" (Tocqueville) that never experienced
feudalism, and therefore never generated the ideologies that feudalism
produces by reaction. An **etic** analytical framework describing an **emic**
condition Americans themselves do not perceive: their Lockeanism is
"a nationalist articulation of Locke which usually does not know that Locke
himself is involved."

## The core logic

**"No Maistre, no Marx."** "One of the central characteristics of a
nonfeudal society is that it lacks a genuine revolutionary tradition...
And this being the case, it lacks also a tradition of reaction: lacking
Robespierre it lacks Maistre, lacking Sydney it lacks Charles II." America
"begins with Locke... stays with Locke, by virtue of an absolute and
irrational attachment it develops for him, and becomes as indifferent to
the challenge of socialism in the later era as it was unfamiliar with the
heritage of feudalism in the earlier one." Hartz calls this "a kind of
inverted Trotskyite law of combined development, America skipping the
feudal stage of history as Russia presumably skipped the liberal stage."

**Socialism is ideological, not economic.** Against Marx, Hartz holds that
socialism arises not from the objective movement of economic forces but
from "the principles of class and the revolutionary liberal revolt against
them which the old European order inspired... The ancien régime inspires
Rousseau; both inspire Marx." Hence "It is not accidental that America
which has uniquely lacked a feudal tradition has uniquely lacked also a
socialist tradition" — the structural answer to the Sombart question
([[why-no-socialism-in-america]]).

**Irrational Lockeanism and the danger of unanimity.** Because the creed is
unconscious and universal, it turns conformitarian: "Locke has a hidden
conformitarian germ to begin with, since natural law tells equal people
equal things, but when this germ is fed by the explosive power of modern
nationalism, it mushrooms into something pretty remarkable." Hartz's
central ethical claim: "the basic ethical problem of a liberal society
[is] not the danger of the majority which has been its conscious fear, but
the danger of unanimity" — Tocqueville's "tyranny of opinion." Under
external pressure the consensus "transforms eccentricity into sin": the
red scares of 1919–20 (Palmer raids, the New York legislature's expulsion
of five Socialist members) and the 1950s. "The American liberal community
contained far fewer radicals than any other Western society, but the
hysteria against them was much vaster than anywhere else" — a structural
feature, "an absolute 'Americanism' as old as the country itself," not the
pathology of "a single politician, whether he be McCarthy or someone who
might succeed him."

## American politics remapped

American "right" and "left" are both factions *within* European
liberalism: the right corresponds to big-propertied liberalism (English
Whigs, Girondins), the left to the petit-bourgeois tradition — expanded in
America into a hybrid absorbing peasantry and proletariat alike (see
[[jackson-andrew]]).

**Why American Whiggery failed (to 1840).** European Whiggery triumphed in
the 1830s by playing a diversity of enemies off one another — liberal hero
against the aristocracy, conservative hero against the mob. American
Whiggery faced "the absence of an aristocracy to fight, the absence of an
aristocracy to ally with, and the absence of a mob to denounce" (General
Root: "We are all of the same estate — all commoners"). Hamilton's
substitute aristocracy — the fixed Constitution and life-tenured judiciary,
Tocqueville's "bench and bar" — could only defend, never take the
offensive. Comparatively, Hartz argues, this near-laboratory experiment
also solves the old puzzle of democracy's speedy American triumph better
than Turner's frontier (frontiers existed elsewhere) or industrialism
(England and France were more industrial in the 1820s).

**The 1840 "discovery of America" and the law of Whig compensation.** With
the log-cabin Harrison campaign, Whiggery abandoned European strategy and
embraced democratic capitalism — Webster denying any American
masses-vs-classes divide; Calvin Colton: "every American laborer can stand
up proudly, and say, I AM THE AMERICAN CAPITALIST." This "Whig
Counterreformation" (Schlesinger's term) fused Hamilton's capitalist dream
with Jefferson's equal opportunity into the Horatio Alger hero. The
humiliated Whigs were "amply repaid" after the Civil War: the Gilded Age
of [[carnegie-andrew|Carnegie]], Alger, and Sumner is the age of Louis
Philippe come "with a vengeance to America."

**"Americanism" — charm and terror.** Post-Civil-War Whiggery fused Locke
with nationalism: "Two moods then run through the new Whig doctrine: one
rational, the other irrational; one liberal, the other 'Americanistic.'
The difference between them is the difference between William Graham
Sumner and the American Legion." Its second weapon was to frighten the
American democrat with his own absolutism — branding any use of the state
"socialist," i.e. un-American. Conformity is the dark underside: a tyranny
that "did not define itself in terms of jail sentences... a silent quality
in the national atmosphere, not so much blocking alien decisions as
preventing them from ever being made."

## Tests of the thesis

- **The Reactionary Enlightenment** — the antebellum South's attempt at
  genuine conservatism ([[fitzhugh-george]], [[calhoun-john-c]]) failed
  because the South was itself Lockean: "a feudalism that has once been
  liberal can never be really feudal." See
  [[american-civil-war-1861-1865]].
- **The Progressives** — trapped within liberalism; the trust was "just as
  much a psychological creation of the American Progressive mind" as an
  economic fact, letting reformers "keep Locke intact simply by smashing
  it."
- **The Great Depression** — the negative proof: however "objective" the
  conditions for the Marxian apocalypse became, "what emerged to deal with
  the economic problem was a movement within the framework of the liberal
  faith" — the New Deal ([[great-depression]],
  [[roosevelt-franklin-d]]).
- **Foreign policy** — messianism and [[isolationism]] as one dialectic:
  "An absolute national morality is inspired either to withdraw from
  'alien' things or to transform them" ([[wilson-woodrow]]). The book's
  closing question: "Can a people 'born equal' ever understand peoples
  elsewhere that have to become so? Can it ever understand itself?"

## Method

Hartz concedes the analysis is "technically... two factors: the absence of
feudalism and the presence of the liberal idea" but defends single-factor
comparative isolation: "a single factor cannot illuminate all situations,
it can still illuminate many." He subsumes Turner's frontier thesis (the
frontier mattered only as feudalism-free terrain) and capitalist-growth
explanations of suffrage and the Alger ethos.

## Limitations and critiques

The thesis is the defining statement — and the defining target — of
[[consensus-history]]. Major critiques: the republican synthesis (Bailyn,
Wood, Pocock) found the Founders speaking classical-republican, not
Lockean, language; Rogers Smith's "multiple traditions" (*Civic Ideals*,
1997) holds that ascriptive hierarchies — slavery, nativism, patriarchy —
were co-equal American traditions, not deviations; and social historians
object that "consensus" erases the enslaved and dispossessed. Hartz
himself was no celebrant: he wrote the book as a warning that the
consensus, not the majority, was the American danger.
