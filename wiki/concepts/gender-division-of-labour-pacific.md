## Definition and Scope

The gender division of labour in pre-contact Pacific Island societies refers to the culturally encoded allocation of productive and ceremonial tasks between men and women. [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] synthesises this across Melanesia, Polynesia, and Micronesia in Chapter 3, emphasising that no single regional pattern holds and that the conventional public/ceremonial record — almost entirely generated by male ethnographers — systematically under-records women's structural importance.

The central analytical claim: "Complementarity may be the one feature common to the gender division of labour in Pacific societies, and perhaps in all societies." Men and women supplied "categorically different, complementary goods" — but the form of this complementarity, the degree of male dominance, and the esteem accorded to women varied radically across the region and even within the same island group.

## Regional Overview

### Melanesia

In most Melanesian societies "women played a greater role in agriculture and food production than did women in Polynesia." Male dominance "was more explicit and more extreme than in Polynesia, and was often buttressed by ideologies warning of female danger and pollution."

**Highland New Guinea** represents the extreme case. Sweet potato (a "female" crop, contrasted with taro, ginger, and sugarcane as "male" plants) was the Highland staple, and women did most of the planting, tending, and harvesting. Women also raised the pigs on which men's ceremonial exchanges entirely depend: "men's ceremonies intrinsically depend on women's work, and if men like Ongka hold more feasts, women must work much harder." Sexually segregated men's cults conducted initiation rites, "arcane knowledge, and secret men-only rituals," and men "explicitly and publicly denigrate women." A Simbu (Chimbu) woman recorded in the early 1960s itemised her daily burden — cooking, gardening, cleaning, pig-rearing, childcare — and concluded: "Men never help us … Men spend most of their time doing nothing and talking."

Yet the textbook of female subordination contains important corrections. Among the Tombema Enga, women controlled access to pigs in the tee exchange: "Enga would not consider giving pigs to persons without a female tie for there would be no guarantee of return." A man's wife "has direct say over distribution to exchange partners who are not related to her." "Women are essential in shaping and defining the partnerships that nominally belong to men."

**Matrilineal Island Melanesia** (e.g. Nagovisi of Bougainville): women owned and passed on land; both sexes worked gardens. Jill Nash reports "relative equality in rights and control over resources," though matrilineal descent "did not ensure that men respected women, nor that women were equal decision-makers." Nagovisi pig feasts were smaller, emphasising "reciprocity and balance" rather than competitive advantage.

**Sepik River societies**: In some, men do most planting while women catch fish, prepare sago, cook, and operate women-only markets. "Few tasks were strictly reserved for one sex, and men and women helped each other as needed." The general literature emphasises female subordination, but "most ethnographers have been male" — since the 1970s some scholars have suggested women may have "their own alternative cosmology and gender ideology."

**Langalanga (Solomon Islands)**: Shell money production (mass-produced for regional circulation) was women's work here — "gardening was the province of men."

### Polynesia

Polynesian women's "primary work was to manufacture mats and cloth for domestic and ceremonial use," while men performed most agricultural tasks, deep-sea fishing, woodwork, stonework, and cooking (under kapu/tapu). But Polynesian women "were the equals of men in genealogical status and social rank" and "wielded formidable personal and political authority as kinswomen and chiefesses."

**Cloth wealth** — tapa (bark cloth) and pandanus mats — was women's highest cultural production. As Annette Weiner argues: "Oceanic societies with cloth traditions value such wealth not only as a form of currency, but also as a major exchange object, presented to others at births, marriages, deaths, and the inauguration of chiefs." Fine tapa and mats embodied kin group identity, were appropriate gifts to gods and chiefs, and in some societies "particular items embodied the history of families and dynasties." Items "are never really alienated from the families that produced them."

**Samoa**: Le toga (fine mats) were the principal form of woman-made wealth. Women beat pandanus leaves "into wafer-thin strips, which were plaited together into mats." The finest mats "acquired a silky sheen over generations of exposure to dew — and their own names and genealogies." Samoan women "participate equally in discussions with male chiefs over which mats to give at a ceremony." Missionaries and colonial administrators "discouraged, regulated, and even prohibited fine mat exchanges on the grounds that they distracted people from productive work."

**Hawai'i**: Men performed most agricultural tasks (taro), cooking (gender tapu mandated separate ovens for men and women), and deep-sea fishing. Women manufactured pandanus mats, tapa, and feather ornaments; gathered shellfish; and made feathered cloaks — "insignia of high-ranking male chiefs." Fine tapa and mats functioned as tributary offerings indexed to chiefly rank. Samuel Kamakau's summary ("all the work outside the house was performed by the men") was complicated by Maui and Hawai'i island practice where "women worked outside as hard as the men" — possibly explained by the prevalence of dryland sweet potato cultivation requiring intensive labour.

**Trobriand Islands**: Men were primary yam producers; women produced banana-leaf bundles and fibre skirts exchanged at funeral ceremonies. Malinowski's kula account "largely ignored women." Weiner's corrective study shows that women's mortuary exchanges were structurally central — "behind every big-woman who distributes more women's wealth than anyone else during a mortuary ceremony stands a man" who spent months accumulating wealth to give her.

**Cook Islands (Pukapuka)**: "Men work on the periphery and women work in the centre" — women work taro in the atoll's swampy interior, men tend coconuts on the encircling dryland. Women are symbolically "wet," men "dry."

### Micronesia

"The social and political range was equally diverse, from stratified chiefdoms to localised extended-family organisation. Cultural solutions did not necessarily correlate with environmental conditions." The men of Chuuk Lagoon gardened; men in western Chuuk District considered gardening women's work. In Yap and Palau, men fished and women cultivated taro; in Pohnpei, women's wealth was cloth goods and agricultural items (sugarcane, sennit, coconut oil), while male wealth included giant yams, pigs, and kava. Weaving (on back-looms) was "almost always women's work in Micronesia." Pottery was "always a female craft."

## Key Analytical Points

**Production vs. exchange as separate dimensions**: "In some societies women contributed little to subsistence, but produced domestic necessities and valued exchange goods, and played active roles in exchanges." Women's contributions to subsistence did not correlate with high public esteem — Highland New Guinea women produced most staple food yet were most denigrated; Polynesian women primarily manufactured cloth yet held high cultural valuation.

**The source-bias problem**: "These descriptions are only as sound as the sources on which they are based … Nearly all were Western men whose biases led them to ignore or denigrate Island women and their activities." Female activities are "rarely given equal treatment."

**Task division by phase, not categorical exclusion**: "Because productive tasks can readily be divided into stages and phases of preparation, tending, collecting, processing, and so forth, the division of labour facilitates the enactment of perceived differences between social categories." In most societies there were "no ritual prohibitions restricting economic tasks" in agriculture — Highland New Guinea was the "notable exception."

**Age as a variable**: Boys and girls had different work expectations from adults; in Hawai'i, mats and cloth were "made by older women," while younger women took child-care and domestic duties.

**No universal rule of descent**: "There is no apparent relation between the rule of descent, the division of labour, and the esteem accorded to women." Matrilineal descent did not ensure gender equality.

## Historiography

The chapter builds on a feminist ethnographic turn in Pacific studies from the 1970s–1990s: Annette Weiner's Trobriand re-study, Jill Nash on Nagovisi, Feil on Tombema Enga tee exchanges, Linnekin on Hawaiian women. Earlier missionary and traveller sources are used with explicit bias-awareness. Paula Brown's paired male/female testimonies from early 1960s Simbu provide a rare contemporaneous emic double-perspective. The main outstanding gap: intensive studies of female perspectives remain scarce; most ethnographic literature "emphasises female subordination" largely because of the male observer baseline.
