# Law and Politics in the Formative Period — Caliphs, Judges, and Jurists

Source: [[hallaq-origins-evolution-islamic-law-2005]], chapter 8 ("Law and politics: caliphs, judges and jurists") and Conclusion. Part of [[formative-period-islamic-law]].

## Definition and Scope

This concept page addresses the structural relationship between political authority (caliphs, governors, their military and administrative apparatus) and legal authority (private jurists, *qadis*, jurisconsults) during the formative period of Islamic law (roughly 7th–10th centuries CE). The central argument — Hallaq's — is that Islamic law was never a state mechanism; legal authority resided with the private scholarly community, not with the caliphate, and this fact had deep consequences for both legal history and political history.

## The Proto-Qadi Phase (to ca. 700 CE)

In the earliest phase, the *proto-qadi* was not a legal specialist but a government administrator. Judicial appointment was conjoined with functions of tax collection, provincial treasury management, police command, and story-telling. The proto-qadi adjudicated on the basis of *ra'y* (considered opinion), custom (*'urf*), Quranic values, and established precedent (*sunan madiya*). Neither caliphs nor provincial governors made any systematic effort to control or codify the law. "No edicts regulating law are known to have come down from caliphs, no constitutions, and certainly no legal codes of any kind" (Hallaq).

The caliphs of this era were themselves subject to the same sunan and religious values as everyone else. Legal authority was bilateral — caliphs queried judges; judges queried caliphs — not a one-way transmission from political sovereign to legal subordinate.

## The Emergence of the Wedge (ca. 700–750 CE)

With the rise of private legal specialists after the 80s/700s CE, a structural divergence emerged between:
- **Political authority**: the increasingly institutionalized, palace-based, coercive apparatus of Umayyad and then Abbasid rule.
- **Legal authority**: the piety-driven, text-based epistemic authority of the learned jurists.

The divergence was driven by two concurrent developments: (1) the spread of ascetic piety among the legal scholars, who came to define their mission as serving God's law rather than the ruler's interests; and (2) the increasing institutionalization and distancing of the caliphal court from the population.

The result was a remarkable cultural formation: **appointment to the judgeship came to be experienced as a calamity**. Hallaq documents jurists who wept at news of their appointment, went into hiding, submitted to flogging, or pleaded incompetence rather than accept judicial office. Abu Hanifa was imprisoned for persistent refusal. This topos — the virtuous jurist fleeing the corrupting proximity of political power — persisted for nearly a millennium. It was not merely rhetorical; it expressed the genuine institutional logic of a legal culture in which proximity to political power compromised epistemic authority. The Day of Judgment tradition placing judges with sultans in Hellfire and pious jurists with prophets in Paradise captures this logic with eschatological intensity.

## The Symbiosis: Mutual Dependence

Despite the ideological divergence, caliphs and jurists were locked in symbiosis:

**Caliphs needed jurists** because:
- Legal authority was epistemically grounded, and only the jurists possessed the requisite knowledge.
- The jurists served as intermediaries between the ruling elite and the masses — the only effective means of acquiring political legitimacy with a population that did not accept coercive authority as self-legitimating.
- The caliphal court could not staff its judiciary without the jurists' cooperation; judicial appointments required the jurists' recommendation.

**Jurists needed caliphs** because:
- The caliphate provided financial patronage — salaries for *qadis* (by the late 8th century, an Egyptian judge could earn 30 dinars/month, compared with under 100 dirhams/month for a prosperous artisan) and grants for private scholars (the vizier Yahya al-Barmaki gave Sufyan al-Thawri 1,000 dirhams monthly).
- Judicial appointment was a channel for social influence and career advancement, despite its ideological ambiguity.

The caliphs held *majalis* (assemblies) with distinguished jurists, attending funerals of leading scholars and studying hadith. The jurists interceded with governors on behalf of their communities. "True kingship lies in the scholar's hands and hardly with Harun who gathers crowds around him by the force of police and palace guards" — words reportedly spoken by a slave-wife of Harun al-Rashid upon observing that the scholar 'Abd Allah ibn al-Mubarak attracted larger crowds than the caliph himself.

## The Limits of Caliphal Authority Over Law

The caliphs generally did not attempt to legislate substantively. When the Persian secretary Ibn al-Muqaffa' (d. ca. 139/756) proposed that the caliph should become the supreme legal authority, binding his judges through royal edicts, "his suggestion was met with complete disregard." The jurists' control over the law was, by this point, irreversible.

The epistles of *Anbari* (d. 168/785) to the caliph al-Mahdi and *Abu Yusuf*'s treatise to Harun al-Rashid (d. 182/798) both take as a foregone conclusion the caliph's subservience to religious law. The caliph could act as a guide to his judges in hard cases — but this was as a fellow scholar offering learned opinion, not as a sovereign issuing commands. "The caliph's participation in resolving legal questions gives him a religious authority akin to that of the scholars, not one over and above or against theirs" (Zaman, quoted by Hallaq).

## Friction and Encroachment

The relationship was not without conflict. The sources record episodes of caliphal and gubernatorial encroachment:
- A governor releasing a soldier before a judge could complete proceedings, prompting the judge's resignation.
- A governor dissolving a marriage over a judge's explicit objection.
- Harun al-Rashid refusing to dismiss a corrupt judge (al-'Umari of Fustat) on the pretext that he was a descendant of Caliph 'Umar I.

Hallaq argues these incidents were exceptions to an overwhelming pattern of caliphal compliance, and that the sources record precisely because they were unusual — the "routine" of judges' decisions being respected went unrecorded. Statistical inference: documented encroachments are a fraction of a probable hundreds of thousands of cases that proceeded normally.

## The Mihna as a Critical Test

The [[mihna-833]] (218–234/833–848 CE) was the most severe caliphal challenge to the religious scholars' independence. The Abbasid caliphs attempted to impose the Mu'tazilite theological doctrine (Quran as created word of God) on all religious personnel and court witnesses. Many jurists were imprisoned or tortured. Yet the Inquisition failed within fifteen years. Its failure, in Hallaq's reading, was not incidental but structural: the jurists were "too well entrenched for any political power to expunge or even replace." The [[mihna-833]] demonstrated the limits of caliphal authority over law even in its most coercive form, and its aftermath left the caliphate *less* able to challenge the jurists' domain than before. See [[mihna-833]].

## The Rule of Law in Pre-Modern Islam

Hallaq's concluding assessment is striking: "If there was any pre-modern legal and political culture that maintained the principle of the rule of law so well, it was the culture of Islam." This claim rests on the structural separation of law-making from political authority: because the caliphate never controlled the law, compliance with the law could not simply be commanded — it had to be *earned* by political actors who needed legitimacy from the religious-legal establishment.

The dismantling of Islamic law and its institutions during the 19th–20th centuries (colonial legal reforms) destroyed this balance, eliminating the class of professionals who had mediated between the masses and power, and enabling the rise of modern dictatorships unconstrained by an independent legal establishment.

## Contrast with Other Legal Cultures

Hallaq explicitly compares Islamic law to other pre-modern legal cultures:
- In Rome, medieval Europe, and China, law was ultimately a state mechanism — promulgated, codified, or enforced by political authority.
- In Islam, law was a "private enterprise initiated and developed by pious men who embarked on the study and elaboration of law as a religious activity."
- The [[madhhab-formation]] was the institutional solution to the problem of legal authority in a system where the state played no legislative role.

## Historiography

Hallaq draws heavily on M. Q. Zaman's *Religion and Politics under the Early 'Abbasids* (cited frequently in ch. 8) and on S. D. Goitein's identification of a "turning point in the history of the Islamic state." His argument against seeing the caliphs and scholars as antagonists — emphasizing instead the structural symbiosis — is a significant interpretive claim that distinguishes his account from models emphasizing conflict. The rule-of-law claim for pre-modern Islam is normatively charged and contested; Hallaq acknowledges it requires extension beyond this study's scope.
