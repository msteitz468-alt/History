# Civitas Sine Suffragio

A distinctive Roman legal status — "citizenship without the vote" — granted
to conquered or allied Italian communities, combining the obligations of
Roman citizenship (taxation, military levy) with exclusion from Roman
political rights (voting in the assemblies, holding Roman magistracies).

## Definition and Scope

The status was neither full citizenship nor mere alliance: communities
holding it (such as [[caere|Caere]] after 273 BCE) retained local
self-government and law while their inhabitants owed Roman military service
and taxation without a voice in Roman decision-making. It functioned as an
intermediate, often transitional category — some communities were later
upgraded to full citizenship, while others remained in this status for
generations.

## Origin (Emic/Etic)

The institution is an emic Roman legal category, attested in the
historiographical tradition (chiefly Livy) rather than a modern analytical
retrofit, though its systematic use as an instrument of graduated
incorporation is a pattern historians have identified across many separate,
individually attested grants.

## Applications and Examples

Caere is the best-attested early example; the status was later extended,
with variations, to numerous Latin and Italian communities as Rome expanded
across the peninsula, part of the broader flexible alliance-and-citizenship
system that gave Rome its unusually deep reserves of Italian manpower (a key
driver of the [[rise-of-rome|Roman rise to Mediterranean dominance]]).

## Limitations and Critiques

Modern historians debate how uniformly the status was applied and how
quickly individual communities moved toward full citizenship; the sources
are patchy and often reflect Rome's perspective on its own expansion rather
than the experience of the incorporated communities themselves.
