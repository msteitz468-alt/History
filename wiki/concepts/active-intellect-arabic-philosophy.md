## Definition and Scope

The **Active Intellect** (*al-ʿAql al-Faʿʿāl*, literally "the Actuating Intellect" or "Agent Intellect") is the central epistemological and cosmological concept in classical Arabic Aristotelian philosophy. It designates a separate, superhuman, immaterial intellect that performs two distinct functions simultaneously:

1. **Cosmological**: It is the lowest of the celestial intellects in the Neoplatonic-Aristotelian emanationist hierarchy — the intellect associated with the sphere of the Moon, and the last principle before the sublunar world. It confers material forms upon matter, giving the natural world its intelligible structure.

2. **Epistemological**: It illuminates or "actualizes" human intellectual potential, enabling the human mind (*material* or *potential intellect*) to pass from merely potential understanding to actual understanding of universals.

This dual function made the Active Intellect the hinge between cosmology and psychology in Arabic philosophy, and generated the central problems of the tradition: How does the human mind connect to a superhuman principle? What is the relationship between intellectual achievement and prophetic inspiration? Can the individual philosopher achieve union or conjunction (*ittiṣāl*) with the Active Intellect, and if so, what does this mean for personal immortality?

**Emic usage**: Arabic philosophers treated the Active Intellect as a genuine separate substance, philosophically demonstrated. Al-Fārābī identified it with the "Holy Spirit" (*Rūḥ al-Quds*) and with the source of prophetic inspiration — an explicit attempt to bridge Aristotelian psychology and Islamic revelation.

**Etic usage**: Historians of philosophy treat the Active Intellect as the locus of the central tension in Arabic Aristotelianism between Aristotelian naturalism and Neoplatonic mysticism.

## Background: The Aristotelian Problem

Aristotle's *De Anima* III.5 distinguished a "maker intellect" (*nous poiētikos*) from a "material" or "passive" intellect. The maker intellect "makes all things" as light makes colors visible; it is separable, unmixed, and immortal. But Aristotle said almost nothing about what this maker intellect is or how it relates to God, the individual soul, or the process of learning. Late antique commentators, especially Alexander of Aphrodisias (who identified the active intellect with God) and the Neoplatonists, developed elaborate interpretations. These were absorbed into the Arabic tradition via the *De Anima* paraphrase in the Kindī circle and via the *Theology of Aristotle* ([[theology-of-aristotle]]).

## Al-Kindī's Theory

[[al-kindi|Al-Kindī]] developed a four-intellect schema in his treatise *On the Intellect*, drawing on John Philoponus' commentary on the *De Anima*. The four intellects are:

1. **Intellect that is always in act** (the Active Intellect proper — a separate, external, eternal substance)
2. **Intellect in potency** (the human mind before it has learned anything)
3. **Intellect acquired** (human mind having actually come to understand)
4. **Intellect that appears** (human mind actively thinking what it has understood)

For al-Kindī, the Active Intellect is entirely separate from the human soul — it is an external cosmic illuminator. Human intellectual development consists in the soul's progressive alignment with this external principle. Knowledge is ultimately a gift from a divine intellect, not a product of the individual human mind working autonomously.

## Al-Fārābī's Systematization

[[al-farabi|Al-Fārābī]] gave the Active Intellect its most elaborate and influential Arabic formulation. Embedding it within a ten-intellect emanationist cosmology derived from Ptolemaic astronomy, al-Fārābī identified each celestial sphere with a separate intellect; the Active Intellect (*al-ʿaql al-faʿʿāl*) is the tenth and lowest, associated with the sublunary world. It is the principle that:

- Gives material forms to sublunary matter (hence "active" — it actualizes potential matter)
- Gives the human potential intellect its capacity for actual thought
- Gives prophets their direct access to universal truths without the slow work of discursive reasoning

This last point was politically crucial. Al-Fārābī used the Active Intellect to explain prophetic knowledge: the prophet differs from the philosopher not in the source of knowledge (both receive from the Active Intellect) but in the *mode* — the prophet receives knowledge instantaneously and in images (via the imaginative faculty), while the philosopher receives it discursively. This allowed al-Fārābī to maintain that philosophy and prophecy reach the same truth by different routes, with philosophy being the higher mode of access — a claim that would remain contested throughout the tradition.

## Avicenna's Transformation

[[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] inherited al-Fārābī's framework but significantly modified the epistemological role of the Active Intellect. For Avicenna, intellectual abstraction is not a process of the human mind working on sensory data and gradually arriving at universals. Rather, the Active Intellect *directly confers* intelligible forms on the receptive human intellect — a process Avicenna called **emanation of forms** (*ifāḍat al-ṣuwar*).

This has a counterintuitive consequence: sensory experience does not *produce* universal knowledge; it merely *prepares* the soul to receive intelligible forms from the Active Intellect. The soul is like a mirror, and understanding occurs when the mirror is properly oriented to receive the light from the Active Intellect.

Avicenna's account also introduced the concept of **intuition** (*ḥads*): some humans — the prophets, and the most gifted philosophers — can receive intelligible forms from the Active Intellect without the preparatory work of learning and observation. This fast contact with the Active Intellect, at the extreme, describes the Prophet's reception of revelation.

The consequence that most troubled later critics (especially al-Ghazālī in [[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]): if sensory experience does not produce knowledge but merely prepares the soul, then Avicenna's epistemology threatens to make the physical world philosophically irrelevant to intellectual life. This is the target of al-Ghazālī's critique of Avicennan emanationism. Source: Wisnovsky, ch. 6, in [[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]].

## Ibn Bājja's Revision

[[ibn-bajja|Ibn Bājja (Avempace)]] engaged critically with the tradition. For Ibn Bājja, the process by which the human intellect achieves "conjunction" with the Active Intellect (*ittiṣāl al-ʿaql*) is the central problem of philosophy — and the highest human achievement. The philosopher who achieves this conjunction becomes, in a sense, "divine" — not identical with God, but possessing Godlike intellectual qualities.

Ibn Bājja's distinctive contribution was his account of "spiritual forms" (*ṣuwar rūḥāniyya*) as an intermediate category between purely material sensory forms and fully immaterial universal intelligibles. The ascent from particular sensory forms through increasingly abstract spiritual forms to full union with the Active Intellect's universals constitutes the philosophical life. This is also the life of the "solitary" philosopher — the *mudabbar al-mutawaḥḥid* — who pursues intellectual perfection independently of the imperfect city around him.

Ibn Bājja was also more cautious than his predecessors about identifying the Active Intellect with the source of prophetic inspiration, and critical of Sufi claims to have reached a union with the Active Intellect that bypassed rational demonstration.

## Averroes' Rejection and Reconstruction

[[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] is the tradition's most severe critic of the Fārābian-Avicennan Active Intellect theory — while simultaneously depending on it. His objection: Avicenna's version, by making the Active Intellect directly confer forms on the human intellect, effectively removes the role of the senses and the natural world from the process of knowledge. Averroes insisted on a more thoroughly Aristotelian view: knowledge begins with sensory experience, and the intellect abstracts universals from sensory data — it does not receive them from outside.

But Averroes' own solution — his doctrine of the single shared human material intellect combined with the Active Intellect — is also a form of the Active Intellect theory. In his Long Commentary on the *De Anima*, Averroes argues that both the material intellect (the capacity for potential universal thought) and the Active Intellect are single, numerically identical substances shared by all of humanity. The Active Intellect is "form in us" — the highest actualizing power of human cognition. See [[unity-of-intellect]] for this doctrine's detailed treatment and its reception in Latin Christendom.

## The Concept in Context

The Active Intellect doctrine sat at the intersection of three contested domains:
1. **Natural philosophy**: Does the intellect have a bodily basis? How does matter give rise to thought?
2. **Theology**: What is the relationship between human reason and divine revelation? Is prophetic knowledge a species of intellectual knowledge, or categorically different?
3. **Ethics and politics**: If the highest human good is intellectual union with the Active Intellect, what does this mean for political life, for the just city, for those who cannot achieve philosophical union?

Al-Fārābī, Ibn Bājja, and Averroes each gave different answers. The tension between the Active Intellect as a cosmological principle (in Fārābian emanationism) and as an epistemological illuminator (in Avicennan intuitionism) was never fully resolved in the tradition and became one of the defining fault lines of post-Avicennan philosophy ([[post-avicennan-philosophy]]).
