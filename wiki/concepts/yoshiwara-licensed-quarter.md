## Definition and Scope

The licensed pleasure quarter (*yūkaku*) system of Tokugawa Japan was the official policy of concentrating unlicensed prostitution and entertainment into a single enclosed district in each major city, subject to government regulation, physical enclosure, and surveillance. The Yoshiwara in Edo (Tokyo) became the most famous and enduring of these quarters, operating continuously from its formal establishment in 1618 until the late Meiji period.

The system belongs to a broader category of Tokugawa governance through controlled legalization: transforming activities that could not be suppressed into regulated economic and security functions.

## Origin: The Three Jinnai of Edo

Sadler's account of the Yoshiwara's founding centers on three figures known as "the Three Jinnai," operating during the Keicho period (1596–1614) in Edo.

**Kosaka Jinnai** (the first Jinnai) was a highwayman, pupil of the fencing master Miyamoto Musashi, and "as much at home in the water as he was on land." Caught when incapacitated by recurrent fever, he was crucified at the execution place near what became Jinnai Bridge in Asakusa. His dying declaration — that anyone who prayed to him for healing of fever-ague would be cured — made him an enduring popular deity, still remembered as Jinnai Sama.

**Tobisawa Jinnai** (the second Jinnai) was a captured highwayman pardoned on condition he became the officially licensed chief of the second-hand clothes dealers. This position served the Bakufu as an intelligence network: "he would be able to get information about thieves and doubtful characters whose ways he was otherwise well qualified to know." The street named after him (Tomisawa-cho) preserved his memory through the entire Tokugawa era.

**Shōji Jinnai** (the third Jinnai) was of more distinguished background — a former retainer of Hōjō of Odawara turned ronin turned highwayman with a Robin Hood reputation (robbing the evil wealthy, donating to the virtuous). He made a formal proposal in 1613 to the City Magistrate Yonezu Kambei, which was submitted to Honda Masanobu and Ieyasu. His argument ran:

1. Unlicensed entertainment houses in Edo were disorderly and impoverishing clients.
2. Kidnapping and sale of girls to such houses was occurring.
3. Unattached masterless samurai (ronin) — "liable to hatch evil plots and prey on society" — were using them as rendezvous.

Permission was granted in 1618 with the allocation of a piece of land "about five acres in extent" — then "little more than a reedy marsh, and worth nothing, a haunt of foxes and footpads."

## Regulatory Framework

The Bakufu's founding rules for the licensed quarter:
1. No courtesans allowed outside the quarter.
2. No guest to stay more than twenty-four hours.
3. Gold and silver embroidery forbidden; dresses of ordinary dyed materials only.
4. Houses not built in ornamental style; the quarter to have the same officers and headman as any other Edo district.
5. Information about any suspicious person (samurai or citizen) to be reported to the City Magistrate; all guests to be carefully examined.

The quarter was surrounded by walls with a single entrance gate guarded by Yoriki (City Magistrate's officers). Even daimyos were prohibited from entering by palanquin (the sole exception being physicians). No weapons could be carried inside.

## Governance Logic

The system functioned as a double surveillance trap: criminals who escaped detection when disposing of stolen goods through Tobisawa Jinnai's second-hand clothes network were likely to be caught when they went to spend the proceeds in Shōji Jinnai's licensed quarter. Sadler notes that Shōji's concern for his clients' solvency was not pure benevolence: "he did not wish to kill the goose that would lay more golden eggs for him in the future."

Sadler compares the system to Akbar's regulation of prostitution in Mughal India (limiting it to the quarter called "Shaitanpur"), noting that controlled legalization with taxation was a common early modern governance solution. Ieyasu's personal views are illustrated by his handling of the Abekawa quarter in Suruga: when his magistrate proposed moving a similar quarter away from the castle for military discipline reasons, Ieyasu refused on the grounds that the women's livelihoods would be destroyed — then later used the threat of questioning them about his soldiers' behavior to shame the samurai into staying away, achieving the disciplinary result by indirect means.

## Historical Significance

The Yoshiwara system became one of the distinctive institutions of Tokugawa urban life, generating its own culture, literature, and aesthetic world. The founding mechanism — a ronin proposing institutional regulation of an activity he himself practiced, in exchange for a government license — is characteristic of Tokugawa co-optation strategies: converting potential disorder into licensed order that could be monitored, taxed, and used for surveillance.
