# Culminating Point of Victory

The **Culminating Point of Victory** (or **Culminating Point of the Attack**) is a core concept in Carl von Clausewitz's theory of strategy, representing the critical threshold beyond which an attacking army’s superiority is exhausted. Pushing past this turning point does not yield further gains; instead, it triggers a defensive reaction that can reverse previous victories and lead to catastrophic defeat. As Clausewitz notes, detecting this boundary is "the keystone for most plans of campaign."

---

## Conceptual Definition

Clausewitz introduces the concept in Book Seven, Chapter Five ("The Culminating Point of the Attack") and expands its logic in Book Seven, Chapter Twenty-Two ("The Culminating Point of Victory"). The concept is rooted in the physical and psychological dynamics of warfare:

> "Success in attack results from the availability of superior strength, including of course both physical and moral. ... Most [attacks] only lead up to the point where their remaining strength is just enough to maintain a defense and wait for peace. Beyond that point the scale turns and the reaction follows with a force that is usually much stronger than that of the original attack. This is what we mean by the culminating point of the attack."

Because the goal of an offensive is the conquest of enemy territory, attackers are naturally driven to advance until their superiority is entirely spent:
> "Since the object of the attack is the possession of the enemy’s territory, it follows that the advance will continue until the attacker’s superiority is exhausted; it is this that drives the offensive on toward its goal and can easily drive it further."

---

## Causes of Diminishing Offensive Strength

Clausewitz details a dual process in Book Seven, Chapter Twenty-Two where the attacker's strength is eroded by the very act of advancing, even as the defender gathers strength. The principal causes of the invader's loss in strength include:

1. **Sieges and Fortresses:** The attacker must bypass, besiege, or observe enemy fortresses, which severely depletes active forces.
2. **Hostile Operational Theaters:** The invader must establish a presence and garrison conquered territory to control it:
   > "The moment an invader enters enemy territory, the nature of the operational theater changes. It becomes hostile. It must be garrisoned, for the invader can control it only to the extent that he has done so; but this creates difficulties for the entire machine, which will inevitably weaken its effectiveness."
3. **Lengthening Supply Lines:** The attacker moves away from sources of replacement, whereas the defender moves closer to their own:
   > "as the oil that feeds it sinks and draws away from the focus, the light diminishes until at last it goes out altogether."
4. **Political Shifts:** Success may alarm neutral powers, causing them to step in to protect the defeated state, or cause allies to detach themselves if they believe the victor's success is becoming too dominant.
5. **Moral Mobilization of the Defender:** Real danger can arouse intense popular resistance (such as a [[peoples-war-clausewitz|Volkskrieg]]) or a sudden "fit of enthusiasm" that rallies the populace to arms.
6. **Slackening of the Victor's Efforts:** Once initial danger is passed, the attacker's moral urgency often wanes, whereas the defender's focus intensifies.

---

## The Asymmetry and Reaction

Because [[defense-vs-attack-clausewitz|defense is the stronger form of war]], the defender holds an inherent advantage once the attacker's superiority drops to zero. Pushing beyond the culminating point leaves the attacker overextended, poorly organized in newly occupied territory, and highly vulnerable to a counterstroke. Clausewitz warns:

> "If one were to go beyond that point, it would not merely be a useless effort which could not add to success. It would in fact be a damaging one, which would lead to a reaction; and experience goes to show that such reactions usually have completely disproportionate effects."

This disproportionate effect is driven by "the lack of organization in newly occupied territory" and the psychological impact on the attacker's morale when hopes of quick success turn to serious losses. In the absolute form of war, where the conflict is indivisible, this turning point is decisive:
> "conquering Moscow and half of Russia in 1812 was of no avail to Bonaparte unless it brought him the peace he had in view. ... Thus the successful stage was not only wasted but led to disaster."

---

## Strategic Action and Judgment

For the commander, the primary challenge is to identify this turning point before it is reached:

> "What matters therefore is to detect the culminating point with discriminative judgment."

Strategists must design campaigns with the understanding that "the natural goal of all campaign plans, therefore, is the turning point at which attack becomes defense." If a commander realizes that the siege of fortresses has halted the advance or that protecting the rear is draining resources to the extent that the main enemy cannot be defeated, they have reached the culminating point: "Its momentum is exhausted; and if the enemy is still unbroken, there is probably no future in it anyway." They must stop, consolidate their gains, and prepare a defense to negotiate peace from a position of relative strength.
