# Verdict of Battle: Pitched Battle as Legal Institution

**Definition.** The classic pitched battle — a mutually accepted, single-day,
spatially bounded collective combat whose outcome both sides treated as a binding
*verdict* on their dispute. On this reading (Whitman,
[[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012|The Verdict of Battle]], 2012), a pitched battle
was simultaneously a military event and a **legal proceeding**: a large-scale trial
by combat under a "tacit contract of chance," governed by the
[[jus-victoriae|law of victory]].

## The institution's anatomy

- **Aristotelian containment.** The classic battle was limited in time (dawn to
  dusk, one day) and space (a defined field), leaving surrounding society
  untouched. [[battle-of-solferino-1859|Solferino]] (1859) and Waterloo (1815) were
  late, textbook examples: single days of concentrated killing that ended their
  wars.
- **Conflict-resolution mechanism.** However nightmarish for participants, battle
  *limited* war: the alternative was not peace but the chevauchée, the razzia,
  systematic pillage, siege, guerrilla war — Sherman's March. Battle warfare, even
  indecisive, was a kind of rule of law, as litigation is preferable to feud.
- **Consent and wager.** Both commanders had to "accept battle" — the language of
  accepting a wager pervades the sources. Medieval thought made God the judge
  (battle as ordeal; the judicial *duellum*, whose last surviving form — per
  Ayala, Huizinga, and Georges Scelle — was princely battle-war itself, reserved
  to sovereigns who "recognize no superior tribunal"). The secularized eighteenth
  century made chance the arbiter and the outcome contractually binding: the 1789
  *Encyclopédie* article "The Right of Conquest" describes conquest-by-battle as
  subject to "tacit conventions that have the character of a contract of a game
  of chance." The theological hinge was Aquinas: deciding by lot (*sors*) is
  "tempting God" when used to discover truth, but licit to "divide up property or
  put an end to a dispute" — exactly battle's function. Grotius accepted pure
  chance-decision only for a hopelessly weak party and held that a cause worth
  fighting demanded *all* one's forces (echoing Machiavelli's "one should never
  put all one's fortune at risk, without engaging all one's forces" — Whitman:
  "strikingly Clausewitzian... three centuries before Clausewitz"); his own
  eighteenth-century commentators dropped the rigorism and treated battle simply
  as a binding *transactio*. Even the word **jeopardy** (medieval *jeu parti*, "a
  game of even chances") descends from the wager-of-battle world.
- **Verdict and proofs.** Victory was determined by conventional indicia (holding
  the field, enemy retreat, trophies) and conveyed defined legal profits — see
  [[jus-victoriae]].

## Rise and fall

Contained pitched battle was rare but recurrent across premodern history (classical
Greek [[hoplite-warfare]] is one famous battle culture). Its golden age was the
eighteenth century (~1700–1790), when wars between dynastic monopolists of violence
were fought for divisible property stakes ([[proprietorial-warfare]]). It survived
Napoleon (Austerlitz, Leipzig, Waterloo were still day-long verdicts) and lasted
through [[battle-of-solferino-1859|Solferino]] and Königgrätz (1866). It died in
the [[american-civil-war-1861-1865|American Civil War]] and at
[[battle-of-sedan-1870|Sedan]] (1870), when facially decisive verdicts were no
longer accepted by sovereign peoples — see [[death-of-pitched-battle]]. Königgrätz
(1866) is, on Whitman's account, "the last of the fully decisive battles in
Western history," its verdict "fully accepted by the defeated Austrians."
Twentieth-century "battles" (Verdun, Stalingrad, Baghdad 2003 — a successful
Clausewitzian *Vernichtungsschlacht* that brought no settled peace) retained the
name but not the verdict function.

A parallel intellectual shift accompanied the institutional death: battle ceased
to be read as the work of **Fortune** (a wager under a contract of chance) and
came to be read as the revelation of **Destiny** — the Romantic/Hegelian
"world-historical event" of Creasy's *Fifteen Decisive Battles* (1851), Wilhelm
I's description of Sedan as a "world-historical act," and the legend (retold down
to Fukuyama) that history ended at Jena. Montesquieu's lonely 1737 dissent —
"Fortune does not rule the world... there has always been a general cause" —
became the orthodoxy. Whitman: "War ceased to belong to the realm of Fortune and
entered the perilous realm of Destiny." Paradoxically, Whitman argues, legal
verdicts outlast forced ones: eighteenth-century "war in form" settlements
(Silesia, Canada, U.S. independence) proved more durable than Napoleon's genuine
annihilations, whose map of Europe "scarcely lasted a decade" (Weigley).

## Critiques and limits

Military historians object that most early-modern commanders *avoided* battle and
that few battles decided anything (the Jeremy Black line); Whitman answers that
even inconclusive battle-warfare contained violence, and that legal incentives
shaped when battle was accepted. The model is built on European continental land
war; it excludes naval, colonial, and irregular warfare by design. Compare
[[face-of-battle-method]] (Keegan) for the experiential rather than legal analysis
of battle, and [[battle-decision-and-exploitation]] for the operational view.
