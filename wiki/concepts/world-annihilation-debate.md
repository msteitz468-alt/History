## Definition and Scope

The world-annihilation debate is the set of philosophical and theological arguments concerning whether the world (or its material substrate) could, in principle, cease to exist after having come into existence — or, more precisely, whether any coherent account of divine annihilation of the world is available. It is distinct from the [[eternity-of-the-world|eternity-of-the-world debate]] (Discussion 1 of the *Tahāfut*), which concerns whether the world had a beginning. This debate concerns the other temporal boundary: whether it could have an end.

The debate is documented in Discussion 2 of [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī's]] *[[tahafut-al-falasifa|Tahāfut al-Falāsifa]]* (Marmura translation, source [[ghazali-incoherence-of-philosophers]]). Al-Ghazālī's primary interlocutors are [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] and the Avicennan philosophical tradition; the theological positions surveyed represent a range of Kalām schools.

---

## The Philosophers' Core Argument

The Avicennan philosophers hold that the nonexistence of a self-subsisting substance after its existence is *inconceivable* — regardless of whether the substance was pre-eternal or temporally originated. Their argument derives from hylomorphic physics:

- Matter (*hyle*) does not perish; it only changes form.
- What appears to be destruction (water evaporating under fire) is actually transformation: the *hyle* exchanges the form of water for the form of air; cool it, and water returns.
- "It is not the case that a new water has come into being; rather, the materials are shared by the elements. It is only the [successive] turnover of their forms that changes." (Discussion 2, §24)

Since matter is indestructible on this account, and since any composite thing's existence depends on matter persisting in various forms, the world's total annihilation is conceptually impossible. The philosophers also apply this to the individual human soul (which they regard as a self-subsisting, non-material substance): once the soul exists, it cannot be annihilated.

## The Muslim Theological Positions Surveyed and Rejected

Al-Ghazālī surveys four Muslim attempts to articulate divine annihilation, which the philosophers claim are each incoherent on their own terms:

### Position 1 — Annihilation as a Created Existent in God's Essence
Annihilation is an existent that God creates *in His own essence*, through which the world becomes annihilated. Similarly, existence is an act of bringing-into-being created in God Himself.

*Philosopher's objection*: This makes the Eternal a *receptacle of temporal happenings* — which is impossible for an immutable Necessary Existent. Moreover, it is conceptually redundant: the only intelligible meaning of a bringing-about of existence is that the thing stands in relation to Will and Power; affirming anything further (a separate act-existent in God) is unintelligible.

### Position 2 — Endurance as a Separate Created Act
God must actively create endurance for substances to continue existing; if He ceases to create endurance, the substance is annihilated.

*Philosopher's objection*: This generates an infinite regress — if endurance endures, it must do so by virtue of another endurance, which must also endure, *ad infinitum*. Furthermore, on this view the divine attributes themselves would need endurance created for them, which is absurd.

### Position 3 — Ashʿarite Version 1 (Accidents Cannot Endure; Substances Cease Without Motion/Rest)
Accidents cannot endure by themselves. Substances are annihilated when God ceases to create motion, rest, combination, and separation in them — since a body that is neither in motion nor at rest cannot persist.

*Philosopher's objection*: This contradicts sense-perception. The mind perceives blackness to be numerically identical from moment to moment, just as it perceives the hair on a person's head to be identical from day to day, not constantly replaced by new instances.

### Position 4 — Ashʿarite Version 2 (Accidents Cease by Themselves)
Similar to Position 3 but with a slightly different mechanism for accidents. Both Ashʿarite positions effectively treat annihilation as a *refraining from action* rather than a positive act — since they do not conceive nonexistence as something God creates.

## Al-Ghazālī's Counter-Strategy

Al-Ghazālī does not defend any of the four positions in detail. Instead, he opens philosophical space for annihilation by attacking the conceptual status of nonexistence in the philosophers' own system.

### Nonexistence as an Intelligible Occurrence
The philosophers ask: if nonexistence is not a *thing*, how can it "occur" or "come about"? Al-Ghazālī replies:

> "It is not a thing. How, then, did it come about? The only meaning of its proceeding from Him is that what occurs relates to His power. Hence, if its occurrence is apprehended by the mind, why should its relation to [His] power not be apprehended by the mind?" (Discussion 2, §28)

His key evidence: the philosophers themselves acknowledge that accidents cease to exist — and their ceasing is a genuine occurrence, even though nonexistence is not a thing. The concession that nonexistence-of-accidents can occur and be cognized undermines the claim that annihilation of substances is *in principle* unintelligible.

### The Privation Dilemma
Al-Ghazālī presses a dilemma about the philosophers' preferred account of "destruction" (replacement by an opposite existent, not pure nonexistence). When hair turns white, the philosophers say the *occurring thing* is whiteness, an existent — not the privation of blackness.

Al-Ghazālī asks: does the occurrence of whiteness *entail* the privation of blackness, or not?
- If *yes*: the entailed privation is either eternal, temporal, or neither. Eternal is impossible (it was once absent); neither is impossible; therefore temporal. But if it is temporal, it is an intelligible occurrence, and an intelligible occurrence can stand in relation to divine power.
- If *no*: this "would affront what is intelligible."

### Accidents Without Opposites
Some accidents cease to exist without being replaced by their opposite. Motion has no opposite (rest, for the philosophers, is privation of motion, not a positive existent). When motion ceases, pure privation occurs — not a positive opposite. Likewise, sensory forms impressed in the eye or intelligible forms in the soul can cease without their opposite arising. These cases show that "pure privation that has occurred" is intelligible and relatable to power.

### The Decisive Point
Al-Ghazālī's conclusion for Discussion 2: so long as the occurrence of an event by an eternal will is conceivable — which the philosophers cannot deny without undermining their own causal framework — there is no in-principle bar to God willing annihilation. The question of whether annihilation is a "thing" or a "privation" is secondary to the question of whether its occurrence is intelligible; and it is.

## Significance and Connections

The world-annihilation debate is philosophically adjacent to, but distinct from:

- The **eternity debate** (Discussion 1; [[eternity-of-the-world]]): temporal beginning vs. temporal end of the world
- The **occasionalism debates** ([[occasionalism-islamic]]): whether God creates each moment of existence anew, which is directly at stake in Positions 2–4 above
- The **divine power and will debates** ([[divine-attributes-islam]]): the scope of divine omnipotence is implicated in any account of what God can annihilate

The philosophical tradition's hylomorphic argument that matter is indestructible became one of the key points where Avicennan natural philosophy collided with Islamic doctrines about the Day of Judgment (*Yawm al-Qiyāma*), which presuppose that existing things can come to end and that bodily resurrection is possible. Al-Ghazālī's argument here is primarily defensive — he does not establish *that* annihilation will occur, but clears the space for its intelligibility against a strong philosophical objection.

## Historiography

This discussion has received less individual scholarly attention than Discussion 1 (eternity) or the causation discussions. Marmura's notes to the 2000 translation identify the four theological positions as reflecting actual positions in the Ashʿarite and related Kalām traditions, though their precise attribution remains partly uncertain. The argument concerning nonexistence as an intelligible occurrence links to broader debates in Islamic metaphysics about the ontological status of privations (*mafqūdāt*) — a topic with deep roots in Greek and Arabic logical traditions.
