# Ethnicity

## Definition

Azar Gat defines ethnicity as **"a population of shared kinship (real or perceived) and culture."** The term entered English after the Second World War: "The concepts of ethnic group and ethnicity were coined in English in the wake of the Second World War to replace the concept of race that had lost its legitimacy with Nazi racial doctrines." It inherited race's dual sense — "large communities of both cultural and 'blood'-kin relatedness" — while shedding the strictly biological meaning race had assumed since the late nineteenth century.

Gat insists on **two integral elements, kinship and culture**, against definitions that privilege one alone. Against Weber and Walker Connor he specifies *kinship* rather than descent: "Common descent is only one, albeit prevalent, subcategory within the broader category of shared kinship or 'blood relation.'" The constitutive notion is "the notion of extended family which is typical of an ethnos." This lets in-laws and amalgamated founder-groups count as kin — the Romans' fused Latin and Sabine origins, the English Anglo-Saxon/Norman fusion, and modern immigrant nations forged through "extensive intermarriage among the founding groups and the adoption of a common culture." Against those who would exclude culture, Gat replies that "ethnicity overwhelmingly tends to combine both kinship and a common culture": ethnography studies "the culture of kin–culture communities," and "an ethnos very rarely includes populations from different linguistic spaces."

Ethnicity is the substratum from which [[nation|nations]] arise (following Anthony Smith's *ethnie* / *ethnos*), but the two are distinct; on Gat's scheme an ethnos that gains a "sense of common identity, history, and fate" becomes a *people*, and a people that becomes politically sovereign becomes a [[nation]] (often a [[national-state]]).

## Kin–culture communities: the evolutionary basis

Chapter 2 grounds ethnicity in human evolution. The starting point "predates the state and even agriculture" — Homo sapiens has existed ~150,000–200,000 years, so "99.5 or 95 percent, respectively, at the very least, of humans' time on earth was spent before agriculture and the state," lived "in small kin groups as hunter-gatherers." Foragers lived in clans nested in a "regional group, or tribe... about 500 people," which was simultaneously "an endogenic marriage pool" and "a culture unit" with "its own name and a distinct sense of self-identity as a 'people.'"

Two evolutionary mechanisms intertwine. First, **kin selection**: "one's genes are passed on... not only through one's own offspring, but also through other close kin who share the same genes." The same logic that explains sibling solidarity scales — "in principle, the same logic holds true for 32 second cousins, 128 third cousins, or 512 fourth cousins – which is, in fact, roughly what a regional group was." This shares the evolutionary foundation laid out in [[gat-war-in-human-civilization-2006|War in Human Civilization]], and connects to [[evolutionary-origins-of-war]] and [[hunter-gatherer-warfare]] — kin-culture preference "was a very strong selection force... because competition and conflict between individuals and groups were very intense," with adult-male violent mortality "around 25 percent."

Second — and Gat's distinctive emphasis — **culture is not incidental but central**. Culture served as a *cue* for kinship ("Those around me in the tribal group who dressed, behaved, and looked like me were also most likely to be more related") and as a *tool of cooperation* ("Cooperation is dramatically more effective when cultural codes – above all language... are shared"). "Culture, cultural diversity, and... the facility of shared culture cooperation are unique to humans." A "non-specific preference for whatever culture group within which one happens to grow up... became encoded in our genes in the same way as the preference for kin was." This is the key to Freud's "narcissism of minor differences"; the Yanomamö ("They speak crooked; we speak straight, the right way!") and the "Eskimo" calling themselves "real people" illustrate the resulting ethnocentrism. "Biologically we are virtually the same people as our Stone Age forefathers."

## Ethnicity has always been political

"Ethnicity has always been highly political, ever since the emergence of the state and even before." The state and the ethnos shaped each other: **"Ethnicity made the state and the state made ethnicity, in a reciprocal and dialectical process."** Petty-states drew on a shared ethnic space; larger states grew where an ethnically related population eased unification, then "greatly reinforced the ethnic unity of its realm... through deliberate leveling and fusion efforts." Even empires were not ethnically blind: "nearly all of them were grounded in and relied upon a dominant ethnic nucleus." Where "a rough congruence between an ethnos and a state occurred," the result is a [[national-state]] — see also [[premodern-european-nationhood]] and [[antiquity-of-nations]]. This makes [[nationalism]] "one particular form of a broader phenomenon, that of political ethnicity."

## Not neat packages, but real

Ethnicities are not "homogeneous or clear-cut, coming neatly sealed in distinct and fixed packages." They are "variably heterogeneous, 'punctuated' continuums," in which "processes of ethnic fusion and fission occur, shaping and reshaping group boundaries and identities." Yet fuzziness is not unreality. Gat directly confronts the **"essentialism" charge** — "the ultimate detraction in the humanities and social sciences, and for good reason, given the dangers of crude conceptualization" — and answers that "'family resemblance,' relative distances within and between groups, and continuities versus change... are a perfectly valid, indeed indispensable, way of thinking about very genuine realities." His analogy is dialect and language: "the sometimes substantially different dialects of one lingual space... are nonetheless much closer to one another than to a distinctly separate lingual space." "The fact that there are no neatly fixed ethnic packages... does not mean that there are no such significant and quite enduring packages at all." Genetic studies bear this out — "most ethnic communities tend to be genetically related" — even though "genetic continuums regularly flow into or are split between different... cultures," because "what matters, of course, is the *perception* of kinship (which... more often than not has a genuine basis in reality)."

## Emic and etic

Ethnicity is both **emic** and **etic**. Emically, ethnic actors experience their group as an extended family and "relate to the ethnos and nation in familial terms as a reality and metaphor"; consciousness of common identity often crystallized only "with increased contact with outsiders" and an alien "Other" (the Celts, Germans, Slavs, and many colonial-era African ethnicities — which the colonial powers "did not 'invent'... but rather made... conscious"). Etically, "ethnicity" is the analyst's category, "an intellectual grid laid over reality" replacing the discredited term *race*, applied across periods and regions where the actors used their own terms (am, laos, gens, Volk, narod).

## Limitations and Critiques

The principal rival is the **constructivist / instrumentalist (Barthian) view**, which Gat engages throughout. On this account ethnicity is situational, fluid, and constructed — boundaries that shift with circumstance, "invented" by elites or colonial authorities. Gat accepts the genuine kernel: individual ethne "are no more 'primordial' than the nation"; they "form and disappear," emerge through fusion, and are continuously reshaped; identity has "an intrinsically voluntary element." He charges constructivists with a "false dichotomy" — treating real-but-fuzzy as equivalent to unreal, and "invented" as fabrication *ex nihilo* (he cites Nairn that "traditions" are "borne forward from past times... not creation ex nihilo"). His **middle path**: "Ethnicity in general, as opposed to any specific ethnos, is primordial in the sense that it has always been a defining feature of our species," even though no *particular* ethnicity is primordial. The evolutionary account he leans on (van den Berghe) is itself contested — Anthony Smith "withheld judgment," asking that the abstract logic be shown to operate "in the actual historical reality of changing cultural boundaries." Gat concedes the descriptive/normative confusion that fear of ethnicity's "explosive and horrendous manifestations" has bred, while insisting "the evolutionary logic in itself has no normative implications."
