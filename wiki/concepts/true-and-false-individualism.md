# True and False Individualism

Hayek's genealogical distinction between two opposed intellectual traditions
both called "individualism," set out in the Finlay Lecture at University
College Dublin (17 December 1945) and placed first in
[[hayek-individualism-economic-order-1948]]. Epigraph from Tocqueville: from
the eighteenth century and the Revolution flowed two rivers, one leading men
to free institutions, the other to absolute power. Written against the
drift of 1945 — "under the sign of 'neither individualism nor socialism' we
are in fact rapidly moving from a society of free individuals toward one of
a completely collectivist character."

## The two traditions, as Hayek constructs them

**True (British-evolutionary) individualism**: Locke → Mandeville and Hume →
Josiah Tucker, Adam Ferguson, Adam Smith, Burke → Tocqueville and Acton.
Primarily a *theory of society* — social wholes understood through
individual actions, institutions as spontaneous growths ("nations stumble
upon establishments, which are indeed the result of human action but not
the result of human design" — Ferguson). Anti-rationalist and premised on
human fallibility: Smith's system is one "under which bad men can do least
harm"; its "fundamental attitude... is one of humility toward the processes
by which mankind has achieved things which have not been designed or
understood by any individual." A footnote credits Carl Menger with reviving
Smithian "methodical individualism."

**False (Continental-rationalist) individualism**: Descartes (the designing
legislator of the *Discourse on Method*) → the Encyclopedists, Rousseau, the
physiocrats → the French Revolution and the modern "engineer" mentality
toward social problems. Exalting designing Reason, it "always tends to
develop into the opposite of individualism, namely, socialism or
collectivism." Hayek adds that Mill and Spencer were "almost as much
influenced by the French as the English tradition," muddying the classical
liberal canon, and identifies a third, German strand — the
Goethe–Humboldt "cult of the distinct and different individuality" — which
he argues is unrelated to true individualism and, combined with German
political docility, fed the failure to develop free institutions and the
eventual totalitarian outcome.

## Restatement as the "Two Traditions" (1960)

Chapter 4 of [[hayek-constitution-of-liberty-1960|*The Constitution of
Liberty*]] ("Freedom, Reason, and Tradition") gives the mature version — the
"empirical and unsystematic" British vs. "speculative and rationalistic"
French traditions of liberty, with the same canon on each side and the added
claims that reason itself "has grown and can successfully operate" only within
the framework of evolved institutions, and that "the rationalist school was
simply and completely wrong." See [[classical-liberalism]] for the full
statement and [[constructivist-rationalism]] for the rationalist canon.
Francis Lieber's 1848 "Anglican" vs. "Gallican" liberty is added as a
19th-century antecedent, and Talmon's totalitarian-democracy thesis as
supporting scholarship.

## Historical context

A 1945 intervention in the postwar planning argument, continuous with
[[hayek-road-to-serfdom-1944|*The Road to Serfdom*]] (Hayek notes
Tocqueville's "new form of servitude" suggested his title — see
[[road-to-serfdom-thesis]]). Abba Lerner's *Economics of Control* (1944)
appears both as ally (on the price mechanism) and target (as "government by
principle" collapsing into expediency). The equality argument — "only
because men are in fact unequal can we treat them equally" — and the
Tocqueville quotation opposing democratic "equality in liberty" to socialist
"equality in restraint and servitude" became staples of postwar liberal
polemic.

## Emic vs. Etic

Etic and openly polemical: a mid-twentieth-century reconstruction of the
liberal canon, not a category the eighteenth-century authors used.

## Limitations and Critiques

Intellectual historians dispute the neatness of the Anglo/Continental split
(Smith owed much to the physiocrats; Burke sits awkwardly in a liberal
lineage; Tocqueville and Acton are conscripted retrospectively). The
dichotomy is best read as Hayek's self-positioning within the era of the
[[socialist-calculation-debate]] — a genealogy built to give postwar
anti-collectivism an ancestral line.
