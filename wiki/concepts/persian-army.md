# Persian Army

The military system of the Achaemenid Persian Empire in the period of the Greco-Persian Wars: a professional, quality force built around mounted and dismounted archers drawn from the Persian/Median/Parthian national core and warlike retainers, rather than a national mass levy of subject peoples.

## Composition and Armament (per Delbrück)

- Principal weapon: the bow. Even the mounted men were armed with the bow. Swords or short spears served only as auxiliary weapons.
- Armor: light for dismounted troops (probably only a shield of woven straw placed in front while firing). Scale armor mentioned for a portion of the mounted men.
- Dress: "With pants and hats they go into battle" (Aristagoras to the Spartans).
- Structure: Persian Empire composed of Persian national nucleus (present-day Persia, Afghanistan, Baluchistan, large portions of Turkestan—steppes and deserts with oases) and numerous subject peoples. Kings drew warriors from the former, not the latter. Mesopotamians, Syrians, Egyptians, and inhabitants of Asia Minor were viewed as unwarlike tribute-paying masses (except Phoenician and Greek sailors for the fleet).

The truly warlike element was the nomadic branches rather than agricultural ones. The empire was originally established with the nomads. As Persians became masters of distant rich lands they were transformed from warlike shepherds into warlike rulers or knights.

Satraps from the Black Sea to the Red Sea were accompanied by large retinues of warlike national Persian bodyguards. With tribute they retained and augmented these groups with mercenaries from warlike tribes that remained half or entirely independent within the realm. From Persia itself replacements and reinforcements could be levied, recruited, and sent out, more from nomads than farmers.

## Tactical Character

Persians did not form a tactical body comparable to the Greek phalanx. Marksmen lend themselves but little to closed formations; by nature they tend to spread out rather than form a unit. Only particularly highly developed skill can make an intrinsic unit of them. Primarily everything depends on the skill, the vigor, and the courage of the individual.

Marksmen cannot be employed in large masses against hoplites: deep formation prevents effective shooting; spread formation soon puts arrows out of range.

The power of the phalanx rests, in addition to individual courage and equipment, on the steadfastness of the whole tactical formation—the physical and moral pressure of the rear ranks. Persian warriors do not provide this.

## Analogy and Scale

Delbrück draws parallels to the later Arabian Bedouin empire (held together by new religion, formed quality armies rather than mass armies) and to Frankish/Merovingian and German knightly systems: small units of professional or semi-professional warriors occupying and controlling rich areas while the main population remained in place.

Large masses cannot be moved over the great distances of such empires. The Persian Empire in foundation and structure formed quality armies.

## Numbers Debate

Greek accounts contain a basic contradiction: Persians pictured as gigantic but very unwarlike masses that must be driven into battle with whiplashes, and again as extremely brave and skillful warriors. If both mass and warlike skill are correct, the repeated Greek victories would be inexplicable. Only one can be right; superiority is to be found not in numbers but in quality.

Nothing forces assumption of Persian numerical superiority at Marathon and Plataea; it is possible, probable, and in Delbrück's view certain that the Greeks were stronger. The victory of citizen armies over the professional army has been distorted in Greek legend into victory of a small minority over a gigantic majority—a national psychological aberration that recurs (explicit parallel drawn to Burgundian Wars, where muster rolls disprove the "many hundreds of thousands").

Even the men levied from shepherds and peasants for a large war such as Xerxes' were not a popular levy but the more warlike elements. Greek citizen levies (except Spartans) lacked strong military tradition.
