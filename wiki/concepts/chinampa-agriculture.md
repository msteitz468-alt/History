# Chinampa Agriculture

## Definition and Scope

**Chinampas** are raised agricultural fields built in shallow lake or marsh environments by piling organic-rich mud and vegetation into cultivable platforms, often with associated drainage canals. In the southern [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of Mexico]] (lakes Xochimilco and Chalco especially), the Aztec-period system became the most extensive and best-documented example—year-round or near-year-round cultivation supporting dense urban populations including [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]].

The term is often used loosely for **raised fields** and **drained fields** elsewhere in Mesoamerica (Maya lowland wetlands, Gulf Coast, highland lake margins). Those analogues share hydraulic principles but are not identical in construction, scale, or social context. Contestation centers on dating, extent (especially bajo drainage around [[tikal|Tikal]]/El Mirador), and carrying-capacity estimates.

## From CHNPA II.1

### Early Preclassic precursors in the Basin (Grove Ch.3)

Southeastern lake island and peninsula villages of the Early Preclassic—Zohapilco, Terremote-Tlaltenco, Santa Catarina—likely created cultivable land from surrounding marshlands by building **small raised fields** and digging **drainage canals**. Grove stresses limited scope and complexity relative to the later system, yet identifies them as **precursors** to the extensive southern-lake chinampas created nearly 2,500 years later by the Aztecs (Grove 2000; CHNPA II.1 Ch.3). Terremote later specialized in reed baskets and mats—marsh-edge craft economies intertwined with wetland agriculture.

### Basin hydrology and irrigation suite (Cowgill Ch.6)

The Basin floor (~2,240 m) held interconnected shallow lakes (Xaltocan, Zumpango, Xochimilco, Chalco, and lower salty Lake Texcoco without exterior drainage). The region is semi-arid with seasonal rain; irrigation—floodwater, spring-fed canals, and **drainage/chinampa-like systems**—improved maize security. Teotihuacan Valley springs alone irrigated ~3,650 ha as of mid-20th-c. measurement (Cowgill 2000). Chinampa intensification is thus one pole of a broader Central Mexican hydraulic repertoire.

### Maya lowland raised/drained fields (Hammond Ch.5) — analogy

Hammond reports wetland **drained fields** (not recognized in the Maya lowlands until 1972) and **raised fields** built up above the water table further into swamps. Such fields “would have been potentially cultivable year-round in the manner of the chinampas of Aztec times,” with estimated support around **nineteen persons per hectare** at Pulltrouser Swamp and related cases. Preclassic candidates include Cerros and possibly San Antonio Río Hondo; Early Classic near Río Azul. Controversy continues on whether enclosed *bajos* as well as riverine swamps were widely drained; canoe shipping of foodstuffs has also been proposed. Hammond cautions that substantial artificial econiches for intensive agriculture cannot securely be placed earlier than ~A.D. 300 on then-current evidence (Hammond 2000). **Primary home** of the Maya material is [[maya-civilization]] subsistence; chinampa here is the Central Mexican comparative anchor.

### Early–Late Aztec intensification (Charlton Ch.11)

Despite political fragmentation after the fall of Tula (~1150–1428), regional economic integration persisted. Within the **southern Basin**, agricultural intensification was underway through **chinampa construction** (Parsons excavations; Ávila López). These developments, and later constructions, provided a major food source for a rapidly growing population and **underwrote, in part, the later expansion of the Triple Alliance** (Charlton 2000). Early Aztec Basin population rose from about **92,000–120,000 to about 250,000** (Parsons 1989, cited Charlton), with growth greatest in the south and center—the same zone as chinampa expansion.

After [[triple-alliance-formation-1428|1428]], the Triple Alliance consolidated the rest of the Basin including the **southern chinampa lands**, then expanded tributary relations over Central and Southern Mexico. Multi-causal imperial growth rested partly on mid-14th-c. agricultural intensification and demographic growth that continued to the Conquest—major CMSR population growth with chinampa construction and terracing. Basin population rose from Early Aztec c. **250,000** to Late Aztec **800,000–1,200,000**; population was a necessary but not sufficient threshold for imperial expansion (Charlton 2000). Late Aztec archaeology in the Basin includes dedicated chinampa studies alongside rural household and irrigation research.

Post-1428 **core–periphery** restructuring (Nichols & Charlton): within ~30–35 km of [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] (central and southern lake system), city-states intensified **agriculture at the expense of craft production**, with salt as the main nonagricultural specialty—chinampa and lake-edge farming thus underwrote the imperial core while periphery *altepetl* (e.g. Otumba) specialized in noncomestibles (see [[aztec-economy]]).

## Causal Position

- **contributed_to:** demographic thresholds enabling [[aztec-empire|Aztec]] expansion; food security for [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]
- **part_of:** intensive Mesoamerican agricultural package (with terracing, canal irrigation, multi-crop milpa)
- **analogous_to:** Maya lowland raised/drained fields; other New World wetland systems (e.g. Andean raised fields—separate tradition)
- **preceded_by:** Early Preclassic marsh drainage experiments (Zohapilco cluster)

## Colonial land-alienation exception (Cline, CHNPA II.2)

Sarah L. Cline identifies chinampas as the **major exception** to colonial land alienation in central Mexico: artificially built extensions into the southern, freshwater portion of the central lake system, with extremely rich soil, great productivity, and year-round cultivation independent of rainfall, supplying fresh fruits and vegetables to Mexico City markets. Although much native land was alienated to Spaniards, chinampas **remained almost exclusively in native hands well into the twentieth century** because of high labor requirements. Spaniards mainly pursued lower-labor agriculture (wheat) and cattle/sheep ranching and generally did not acquire chinampa land — so natives retained rich agricultural land very near the Spanish capital (though chinampa-zone natives usually also owned other soil types/potentials) (Cline 2000; [[central-mexico-indigenous-colonial]]). Nahuatl soil/land-form vocabulary persisted; *chinampa* itself passed into Mexican Spanish as a loanword (Cline).

Frans J. Schryer extends the social consequence into the national period: within the former San Juan Tenochtitlan *parcialidad*, the humid southern chinampa market-gardening zone (with fodder cutting/selling) kept language and customs well into the twentieth century via endogamy, outsider restriction, resource control, and an intellectual elite — in contrast to the northern Tlatelolco section that lost ethnic cohesion earlier (Schryer 2000; [[central-mexico-indigenous-since-independence]], [[tenochtitlan]]).

## Evans & Webster 2001 (Fedick; Parsons; Feinman; Smith; Calnek; Evans)

Scott L. Fedick, Jeffrey R. Parsons, Gary M. Feinman, Michael E. Smith, Edward Calnek, and Susan Toby Evans in [[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001|Evans & Webster 2001]] add scale, yield, and chronology anchors:

- **Fedick (Agriculture and Domestication):** Chinampas as raised beds in shallow freshwater swamps of the Xochimilco–Chalco Basin; dike-controlled water; compost + canal muck; estimated **~120 km²** of reclaimed wetlands at Spanish contact; residual cultivation continues at Xochimilco. Home gardens (Nahuatl *calmil*) widespread alongside milpa (maize + beans + squash).
- **Parsons (Basin of Mexico):** Middle Postclassic saw the **first large-scale successful swamp drainage and chinampa creation** in Lake Chalco–Xochimilco; full productive potential realized only in the Late Postclassic under Aztec imperial expansion centered at [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]]. Middle Postclassic ceramic variability (Aztec I around Chalco–Xochimilco; Aztec II around Texcoco; northern Mazapan-linked complex) suggests impermeable socio-political borders coeval with early large-scale chinampa experiments.
- **Feinman (Economic Organization):** In wetland chinampa zones at the edge of Lake Texcoco, labor-intensive multi-cropping permitted maize returns per unit land **several times higher** than under other regimes. Long before the Aztecs, last-millennium B.C. swamp drainage created fertile wetland zones in the Maya region (Belize/Petén) for cocoa, cotton, and staples — comparative, not identical systems.
- **Smith (Postclassic Period):** Late Postclassic Central Mexican agricultural intensification included hillside terraces, check dams, canal irrigation of alluvium, **and** transformation of Basin swamps into chinampas; similar intensification across many Mesoamerican areas.
- **Calnek / Willey:** Raised-field/chinampa cultivation possible in and around Basin of Mexico upland lakes, supporting dense urban Teotihuacan/Tula/Tenochtitlan patterns; Mexica settlers transformed Lake Texcoco marshes with packed-earth residential platforms and chinampa garden plots.
- **Evans (Gardens):** Besides house-lot gardens (Aztec *calmil* and milpa), formal and informal pleasure gardens were numerous in the Late Postclassic (palace complexes, zoos/aviaries, monumental retreats at Chapultepec and Texcotzingo, nurseries at Huaxtepec and Acatetelco) — horticultural skill continuous with but distinct from food chinampas; possible influence on early European botanic gardens of the mid–late 16th century.

## Limitations and Critiques

- Carrying-capacity figures (~19 persons/ha) are model estimates for specific wetland configurations, not universal yields.
- Extent of Preclassic chinampa-like systems is easily overstated from sparse canal traces.
- Maya bajo-drainage debate remains open; do not equate every wetland feature with Aztec chinampas.
- Chinampa construction was politically as well as ecologically driven—state and city-state labor organization matter as much as hydrology.
- Colonial “native exception” is labor-driven, not a legal reservation; it coexists with general hacienda pressure on other land types (Cline).
- EW2001’s ~120 km² contact estimate is an encyclopedia synthesis figure; reconcile with site-specific survey totals when both are available.
