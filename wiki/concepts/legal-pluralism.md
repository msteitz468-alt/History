## Definition

**Legal pluralism** is the coexistence, within a single political community, of **more than
one body of law and more than one set of courts**, applied to different populations or
kinds of dispute. It is both an emic reality (the way many premodern subjects actually
experienced law — choosing or being assigned to a forum by language, status, or the
language of their contract) and an etic analytical category (the way historians describe
such overlapping orders rather than assuming a single sovereign law).

In a legally plural order the **state's role is not to author all law** but to **license,
rank, and channel** the plural systems — deciding which court hears which case, which
documents are valid, and how conflicts between the systems are resolved. Managing that
architecture is itself an instrument of rule.

## Legal Pluralism in Ptolemaic Egypt (Manning)

[[sources/manning-last-pharaohs-2010|J. G. Manning]] makes **law central to Ptolemaic
[[ptolemaic-state-formation|state-making]]** — a dimension older economic histories
neglected. [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic Egypt]] ran **two principal legal traditions side by
side**:

- **Egyptian (demotic) law**, administered by the **[[asyut-property-trial-170bce|laokritai]]**
  — panels of Egyptian priest-judges applying native law to cases between Egyptians,
  documented in demotic instruments; and
- **Greek law**, applied to the immigrant Greek-speaking population through Greek courts
  (the *dikasteria* and the royal *chrematistai*), documented in Greek.

The crown sat **above** both as the ultimate source of justice and the author of **royal
ordinances (*prostagmata*) and framework legislation (*diagrammata*)**, and over time it
shifted business toward the royal *chrematistai*, using the **language of the contract**
(Greek or demotic) rather than the ethnicity of the parties to assign jurisdiction —
*"by the end of the second century … the language of legal documents was beginning to
replace the ethnicity of the parties involved as the determinant of the court of
jurisdiction."* There was, Manning stresses, **no fused "Ptolemaic law"**: *"Egyptian and
Greek law never merged. There was no Ptolemaic law"* — and **no concept of "legal
personality"** by which ethnicity fixed the governing law. Manning reads the architecture
not as drift but as deliberate state-building: by guaranteeing enforcement across both
systems the state lowered [[new-institutional-economics|transaction costs]], drew disputes
into royal venues, and bound diverse subjects to a single sovereign order without abolishing
their own law. The **[[asyut-property-trial-170bce|Asyut property trial of 170 BCE]]** (the
book's appendix) is the showcase case of the *laokritai* system in action.

## The Three Forces and the King as "Drawer of Boundaries"

Manning argues "Ptolemaic law" was the product of **three forces, not one**: *"royal
legislation … the underlying legal traditions of the population and … the bureaucracy"*
that adjudicated. Crucially the king was **not** a comprehensive legislator on the modern
model but the **creator of a "cognitive framework," a "drawer of boundaries."** Royal
*prostagmata* and *diagrammata* were *"ad hoc pronouncements and responses,"* not a code;
and with respect to Egyptian private law the great *diagramma* attributed to Ptolemy II
(c. 275 BCE) was *"probably no more than a confirmation of preexisting law, comparable to
the Persian 'codification' of Egypt's legal structures."* This is the same move
[[achaemenid-egypt|Darius I]] made when he had *"Egyptian priests, soldiers, and scribes"*
compile the law valid down to the last year of Amasis — *"not producing a unified, written
code … but rather a collection of prevailing customs and scribal usages."* The Ptolemies,
"like the Persians before them," were **reauthorizing** local law within a new sovereign
frame.

A striking corollary: Ptolemaic law was **not instrumental**. *"It did not seek to 'release
economic energy' … Rather, Ptolemaic activity in the sphere of the law was intended to
preserve the status quo and to assert sovereignty."* Manning offers this as a **counter-
example to the Weberian principle** that law can enable new modes of behaviour — the
Ptolemies *"appear merely to have fixed legal custom in place."* What change there was came
through the **state's fiscal interest in registration**: after 145 BCE demotic contracts
had to be registered (with a Greek summary deposited) to be valid — *"these legal reforms
were a sine qua non for the extraction of 'free floating' resources."*

## The Egyptian Legal Tradition: Ma'at, *hp*, and the Absence of a Code

Manning emphasizes how unlike the Greek (and Mesopotamian) world the Egyptian legal
tradition was. Egypt, *"uniquely among major ancient Near Eastern civilizations, produced no
formal public written code"* comparable to the Codex Hammurabi. Its conceptual foundation
was **Ma'at** — *"closer to 'harmony,' or indeed 'social equilibrium,' the consensus that
bound the lowest member of the community to the king himself."* The Egyptian word **hp**
("law") had *"no technical legal meaning,"* covering *"every kind of rule … 'law,' 'decree,'
'custom,' and even 'contract.'"* The demotic **Hermopolis Legal Code** (pMattha, early
Ptolemaic) was a *"restatement"* — a clarification for scribes and judges, *"authoritative
without necessarily being authorized by the king himself"* — not a sovereign code.

## The Courts

Ptolemy II organized a layered judiciary:

| Court | Jurisdiction |
|---|---|
| ***dikasteria*** | Greek courts for Greek-speaking parties |
| ***chrematistai*** | royal, *ad hoc* courts — grew into the dominant state court by the 2nd c. |
| ***laokritai*** | Egyptian priest-judges applying demotic law to Egyptian disputes |
| ***koinodikion*** | (3rd c. only) mixed Greek–Egyptian cases, by equity |

The **royal *chrematistai* progressively crowded out the local *laokritai*** over the
period. State supervision of even purely Egyptian proceedings ran through the
**[[asyut-property-trial-170bce|*eisagogeus*]]**, a royal officer "seated with" the Egyptian
judges. The **Hermias trial (117 BCE)** shows the developed system: it required advocates,
bilingual documentation, royal ordinances, demotic contracts and *"common Greek law"
(*politikoi nomoi*)* cited together — and the court ruled **for** the Egyptian mortuary
priests against a high-ranking Greek officer, because they held valid written title and
thirty-seven years' undisputed possession: *"neither ethnicity nor personal status mattered
in the adjudication of the property rights at stake."* By the mid-second century the two
traditions had *"evolved into a larger, 'bureaucratized' legal apparatus that incorporated
both legal traditions, as well as royal decrees and edicts."*

## Why It Matters Beyond Egypt

Legal pluralism is a recurring feature of empires and conquest states governing culturally
plural populations — Rome's *ius gentium* alongside local law, Islamic *dhimmi*
arrangements, colonial dual legal systems. The Ptolemaic case is among the best-documented
ancient examples and a model for how a [[premodern-state|premodern state]] turned the
management of plural law into a tool of integration and extraction.

## Limitations and Critiques

- The neat "two systems" picture oversimplifies: in practice forums, document types, and
  norms blended, and individuals exploited the choice of forum strategically.
- The evidence is skewed toward the well-documented (Greek papyri, temple archives); how
  ordinary villagers experienced law is largely inferential.
- Whether royal channeling reflects a coherent "policy" or accumulated *ad hoc* responses
  is debated — the same dispute that surrounds the [[ptolemaic-economy|economy]].
