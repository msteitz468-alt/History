The ʿibādāt (sing. ʿibāda, "acts of worship") are the ritual-legal pillars of Islam that universally open every work of [[fiqh]], occupying "as much as one-quarter to one-third of the entire body" of legal treatises. They comprise the Five Pillars minus the shahādatayn (the testimony of faith, which carries no procedural rules): purification (ṭahāra), prayer (ṣalāt), alms-tax (zakāt), fasting (ṣawm), and pilgrimage (ḥajj). Their placement at the beginning of legal works is both symbolic and functional — they constitute "the moral foundation" that generates willing obedience to the rest of the law governing human affairs. Source: [[hallaq-sharia-2009]], chapter 6.

## Definition and Scope

The ʿibādāt are distinguished from the rest of [[fiqh]] by their "constructionist" character: they are "constituted and created by the believers as devotional acts for the purpose of fulfilling a covenant with God," as opposed to transactional law (muʿāmalāt), where acts relate to worldly objects and persons. Modern Western and Muslim scholarship has tended to treat the ʿibādāt as a "private" sphere of religious belief separate from law proper, but Hallaq argues this distinction fundamentally distorts how [[sharia]] operated: the moral formation achieved through ritual performance was the very precondition for jural observance of the rest of the law.

Underlying all ʿibādāt is the doctrine of **taklīf** — the charge of duty and responsibility incumbent on every human being — and of **ahliyya**, legal capacity. Full performative capacity (ahliyyat al-adāʾ) requires mental and physical maturity (bulūgh). Below full majority, a child attains partial obligation (tamyīz) at around age seven; a child who refuses to pray may be disciplined at age ten.

## Major Divisions

### 1. Purification (Ṭahāra)

Purification is the "key to prayer," a prerequisite for valid prayer and the foundation of the ritual system. Its juristic treatment occupies roughly as much space in legal works as any single pillar. The core Quranic authority is Q. 5:6.

Impurity (najas) is divided into:
- **Ḥadath** (bodily secretion impurity): major (akbar) — spermatic excretion, sexual penetration, menstruation, post-natal bleeding; minor (aṣghar) — urine, feces, gas.
- **Najas** (external impurity): blood, wine, hides of unlawfully slaughtered animals (until tanned), dog hides.

Water is the sole agent of purification. Three grades exist: (1) ṭahūr — naturally pure and capable of purifying others; (2) ṭāhir — pure itself but incapable of purifying others (used water, water mixed with pure substances); (3) polluted water. The Ḥanafites uniquely allow ṭāhir water to purify others. Bodies of water over approximately 216 litres (two qullas) are not rendered impure by contamination. The foremost condition for valid ablution is **niyya** (intention) — an internal state of heart and mind that distinguishes acts of worship from identical mundane acts.

### 2. Prayer (Ṣalāt)

Prayer is "the most important of all religious acts after the shahādatayn." Five daily prayers are obligatory for every sane Muslim adult. Anyone who deliberately abstains from prayer "stands accused of renouncing his religion." Children begin the regimen around age ten.

Constitutive elements of valid prayer:
- **Niyya**: awareness of which prayer is being performed and its obligatory nature
- **Takbīrat al-iḥrām**: the Opening Invocation ("God is Great")
- Recitation of the Fātiḥa at every rakaʿ (bow)
- **Rukūʿ** (bowing): minimum requires placing palms on knees, followed by praise
- **Sujūd** (prostration): exposed forehead must touch the ground, head lower than lower back
- **Tashahhud**: the concluding Testimony of Faith in a seated position

### 3. Alms-Tax (Zakāt)

Zakāt is "unique in that it has a dualistic character: on the one hand, it is an integral part of religious ritual and one of the five 'pillars' of religion; on the other hand, it functions as a substantive legal sphere, constituting itself as a 'tax law.'" Literally meaning "growth," zakāt purifies wealth as ṭahāra purifies the body: "to be wealthy is potentially a moral liability that requires dispensation."

Five conditions for zakāt liability: (a) fully owned property; (b) property capable of growth (cattle, agricultural land, commercial goods — not personal consumption items); (c) excess over subsistence; (d) held for a minimum of one full lunar year (except crops and minerals); (e) free of impediments such as debt.

The standard rate is 2.5% on the growth of wealth above the niṣāb (minimum threshold, which varies by genus). Agricultural produce: 10% for naturally irrigated crops, 5% for artificially irrigated. The Mālikite school is distinctive in imposing zakāt on all livestock and on labor animals. A separate zakāt al-fiṭr (head tax in foodstuffs) is due upon breaking the Ramadan fast.

### 4. Fasting (Ṣawm)

Obligatory during Ramadan; also serves as expiation (kaffāra) for violations of marital obligations and pilgrimage infractions. The fast requires "abstinence from food, drink and sex" from dawn to sunset. Valid fasting requires niyya declared each preceding night and maintained through the full day.

Exemptions: the sick, pregnant and nursing women, the elderly, travelers on arduous journeys. Invalidating acts: interruption of niyya, menstruation, sexual intercourse, ejaculation, masturbation. Intentional violation of food/drink/sex prohibitions incurs penance (kaffāra): freeing a Muslim slave, or fasting two consecutive months, or feeding sixty of the poor.

Ramadan begins upon sighting of the crescent moon, or after thirty days from the start of Shaʿbān if the moon is obscured.

### 5. Pilgrimage (Ḥajj)

Obligatory at least once in a lifetime for those with istiṭāʿa (capability): financial means sufficient to cover travel and provide for dependents left behind, physical health adequate for the journey, safety on pilgrimage routes, and for women, a male family escort and not being in the ʿidda period.

Four essential components (arkān) recognized by all schools:
1. **Iḥrām**: entering a state of ritual consecration via niyya, ritual bath, exchange of sewn garments for white unsewn cloth, and prayer
2. **Wuqūf at ʿArafāt**: standing at Mount ʿArafā on the 9th of Dhū al-Ḥijja
3. **Ṭawāf**: sevenfold circumambulation of the Kaʿba on the 10th of Dhū al-Ḥijja
4. **Saʿy**: rapid walking seven times between al-Ṣafā and al-Marwa

The Ḥanafites recognize only components 2 and 3 as essential. During iḥrām, sewn garments, hair removal, nail clipping, sexual activity, and hunting are all forbidden. Pilgrimage by proxy is valid only for those with a permanent incapacity; the proxy must have previously performed ḥajj personally.

## Analytical Significance

Hallaq's central argument is that the ʿibādāt are not merely devotional addenda but the constitutive moral infrastructure of the entire legal system. "Prayer, with its ever-changing bodily positions, signals submission to a higher power"; "fasting compels identification with the suffering of others"; zakāt "engenders empathy toward the needy." These cumulative moral effects made the believer's willing compliance with transactional and public law — the rest of [[fiqh]] — possible and natural. To treat the ʿibādāt as outside "law proper" is to misunderstand how [[sharia]] functioned as a total normative order.

## Historiography

The primary historiographical challenge has been the Western and Orientalist tendency to bifurcate Islamic ritual from Islamic law, treating the ʿibādāt as belonging to "religion" rather than "law." Hallaq challenges this categorization as a projection of Western church-state dichotomies onto a tradition that never organized itself along those lines. Inter-madhhab variation in ʿibādāt is real (especially on purification water categories, livestock zakāt, pilgrimage components) but the overall structure and moral rationale are shared across Ḥanafite, Mālikite, Shāfiʿite, Ḥanbalite, and Twelver-Shīʿite schools.
