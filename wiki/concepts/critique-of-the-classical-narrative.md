## Definition and Scope

The "classical narrative" of Islamic science is the standard historiographical account, repeated across introductory histories and general works, that reduces the Islamic scientific tradition to three acts: (1) passive reception of Greek, Sasanian, and Indian knowledge through translations during early Abbasid times (c. 750–900 CE); (2) a short-lived "Golden Age" of scientific production that was essentially a re-enactment of Greek achievements; and (3) decline caused by religious reaction, epitomized by al-Ghazali's *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (c. 1095), after which Islamic science ceased to matter for the larger history of science and European civilization reconnected directly with Greek antiquity at the Renaissance.

Saliba's *critique* of this narrative is a sustained, evidence-based dismantling of each component. It argues that the classical narrative "leaves us with some unresolved problems that we cannot afford to leave unsettled if we ever wish to understand the actual process by which Islamic science came into being when it did, and in a more general fashion the process by which science, in general, is born and nourished in any society."

This concept page covers the critique; the alternative positive thesis Saliba proposes is captured in [[islamic-science-origins]].

## The Three Sub-Theories of Origin the Narrative Relies On

Saliba identifies three mechanisms the classical narrative invokes to explain how Greek science reached Islam, and finds all three inadequate:

### 1. Contact Theory
The claim that Islamic civilization absorbed science through direct geographic contact with Byzantium and Sasanian Iran. Saliba objects that the contemporary surrounding cultures had not been producing scientific texts for centuries before the Arab conquests: "the scientific and philosophical texts…contained material that was already written in the classical period of Greek civilization, and most of them were indeed produced before the third or the fourth century A.D." Under such conditions, contact could not have transferred what was no longer being produced or studied at any sophisticated level.

### 2. Pocket Transmission Theory
The claim that classical Greek texts were preserved in isolated centers — Antioch, Harran, Jundishapur — and transferred from there. Saliba counters that "preserving second-century texts for hundreds of years…could not guarantee that there would be people who could understand these texts when they were being sought for translation…about 700 years after they were written." No scientist or philosopher of any demonstrable competence can be documented at any of these putative centers in the intervening centuries.

### 3. Syriac Intermediary Theory
The claim that Syriac-speaking scholars (Paul the Persian, Sergius of Res'aina, Severus Sebokht, George Bishop of the Arabs) transmitted Greek science into Arabic via an intermediate Syriac stage. Saliba accepts that these figures produced scientific work but insists it was elementary relative to classical Greek standards — comparable to the quality of contemporary Byzantine science, not to Ptolemy or Euclid. The Syriac route cannot explain how translators like Hunain b. Ishaq rapidly produced sophisticated, technically precise Arabic translations of the *Almagest* and *Elements*.

## Problems with the Narrative's Account of Beginnings

The classical narrative locates the beginning of Islamic science with early Abbasid caliphal patronage — al-Mansur, al-Ma'mun, and the Mu'tazilite theological climate. Saliba raises a series of specific evidential anomalies that the narrative cannot explain:

**Pre-existing astronomical competence.** When al-Mansur founded Baghdad in 762 CE, he assembled three astronomers — Nawbakht, Masha'allah, and Ibrahim al-Fazari — to cast the city's horoscope. Fazari (an Arab, not a Persian) and his collaborator Ya'qub b. Tariq subsequently translated the Sanskrit *Sindhind*, wrote a theoretical astronomical treatise (*Tarkib al-aflak*), and adapted astronomical parameters to Arab calendar years. "Such activities could not have been accomplished by people who were just learning how to translate under the earliest Abbasids, as the classical narrative claims."

**Mature technical language at the first surviving translation.** Al-Hajjaj b. Matar's Arabic translation of the *Almagest* (completed 829 CE) is written in "impeccably good Arabic, technical terms and all" and even corrects errors in the Greek original. "Neither of these questions is resolvable if we continue to believe the classical narrative that dates the beginning of the serious translations to the time of al-Ma'mun." Early translations typically struggle with terminology; this one presupposes at least a century of prior technical language development.

**Algebra before its Greek sources were translated.** Muhammad b. Musa al-Khwarizmi created the discipline of algebra — in mature form, treating second-degree equations generally — before Diophantus's *Arithmetica* had been translated into Arabic. The coinage of the term *al-jabr* and the formulation of algebra as a discipline distinct from arithmetic required a maturity incompatible with first-generation translation activity.

**New astronomical discipline (*hay'a*) and new trigonometric methods.** Qusta b. Luqa (fl. c. 850) composed original works on theoretical astronomy (*'ilm al-hay'a*) simultaneously with translating Greek texts. Habash al-Hasib (fl. c. 850) devised new planar astrolabe projections preserving direction and distance to Mecca — projections unknown from any earlier civilization — and achieved precision astronomical observations (inclination of the ecliptic at ~23;30°, still accepted today) that presuppose long acquaintance with the instruments and methods.

**Greatest translation volume under an anti-Mu'tazilite caliph.** The classical narrative ties the translation movement's height to al-Ma'mun and Mu'tazilite rationalism. But the greatest number of translations from Greek were produced under al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861), who reversed al-Ma'mun's Mu'tazilite policies. Hunain b. Ishaq, the most prolific translator, worked as al-Mutawakkil's court physician. No single book is documented as having been translated for al-Mutawakkil himself; most were patronized by the Banu Musa brothers — elite bureaucrats, not the caliph.

## Problems with the Narrative's Account of Decline

The classical narrative attributes the decline of Islamic science to al-Ghazali's attack on the philosophers (*Tahafut al-Falasifa*, c. 1095) and the rise of religious orthodoxy. Saliba demonstrates this decline did not occur:

Post-Ghazali scientific production included: al-Jazari's mechanics (1205); the mathematical and astronomical works of al-Abhari (c. 1240), al-'Urdi (d. 1266), al-Tusi (d. 1274), Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi (d. 1311), Ibn al-Shatir (d. 1375), al-Qushji (d. 1474), and al-Khafri (d. 1550); Kamal al-Din al-Farisi's optics (d. 1320); Ibn al-Baitar's pharmacology (d. 1248); and Ibn al-Nafis's discovery of pulmonary circulation (d. 1288). Saliba dubs the post-Ghazali period "the golden age of Islamic astronomy."

The classical narrative's blinkering effect was real and damaging: orientalists who approached post-Ghazali texts with the expectation of decline misread or dismissed results of high significance. Baron Carra De Vaux described the chapter of Tusi's *al-Tadhkira* most directly relevant to Copernicus as exhibiting "de faiblesse, de mesquinerie, quand elle voulait être originale" — a judgment Saliba identifies as a case study in how ideological frames prevent the recognition of historical evidence.

Similarly, Ibn al-Nafis's articulation of pulmonary circulation went uncontextualized within its Islamic intellectual environment (which already included Abu Bakr al-Razi's *Doubts contra Galen*) because the classical narrative made original thought at that late date unthinkable.

## The Erased Chapter: Islamic-Byzantine Scientific Exchange

The classical narrative's focus on the Latin translation movement of the twelfth century causes it to ignore a distinct channel: the transmission of scientific ideas from Arabic into Byzantine Greek, ongoing from at least the tenth century through the fall of Constantinople in 1453. Work by Neugebauer, Pingree, Tihon, and Mavroudi has begun to recover this exchange. This transmission may have been a significant route by which Islamic mathematical astronomy reached Renaissance Europe — a connection the classical narrative structurally cannot accommodate.

Guillaume Postel (1510–1581), whose handwritten annotations on Arabic astronomical manuscripts survive in European libraries, and who may have used those manuscripts to lecture at what became the Collège de France, illustrates the problem: "Whose science was Arabic science in Renaissance Europe?" is a question the classical narrative prevents from being asked.

## Contested Motivations for the Translation Movement

When the classical narrative does explain *why* the Abbasids translated Greek texts, it offers three motivations that Saliba finds implausible:

1. **Persian ascendancy and the desire to reclaim "their" Greek heritage** (via the legend that Alexander stole Persian sciences). Saliba notes this legend was itself a product of early Abbasid intellectual culture — a phenomenon to be explained, not an explanation.

2. **Al-Ma'mun's Mu'tazilite rationalism** creating a philosophical climate favorable to Greek thought. Contradicted by the timing: peak translation activity post-dates al-Ma'mun and occurs under al-Mutawakkil, who suppressed Mu'tazilism.

3. **The Mu'tazilite *mihna* (inquisition)** creating an environment of doctrinal debate that energized philosophical inquiry. Saliba regards this as at best a partial, temporally bounded, and empirically unsupported explanation.

---

## Distinction from Emic vs. Etic Perspectives

The classical narrative largely reflects the categories of the classical Arabic sources themselves, which distinguished "ancient sciences" (*'ulum al-awa'il*) from "Islamic sciences" (*'ulum islamiyya*). Saliba reads this distinction as itself a historical phenomenon requiring explanation — not as a transparent description of how science arrived — and argues that accepting these emic categories at face value has led historians to reproduce the ideological self-understandings of the period rather than analyze them.

## Historiography

Saliba positions his critique against what he calls the "orientalist" tradition of Islamic science historiography, exemplified by figures like Carra De Vaux, Francois Nau, and (implicitly) those who treat Islamic science as a derivative relay station between Greek antiquity and the European Renaissance. His revisionism draws on:

- **Primary source re-reading**: systematic attention to the *Fihrist* of al-Nadim (987–988 CE) and the bio-bibliographical tradition (Ibn al-Qifti, Ibn Abi Usaybi'a) for evidence of actual patronage patterns
- **Mathematical astronomy**: recovery and analysis of post-Ghazali astronomical texts (Tusi, al-'Urdi, Ibn al-Shatir) showing direct mathematical connections to Copernicus
- **Sociological framing**: insistence that scientific production must be connected to "the social conditions in which science is spawned"

The critique is in dialogue with Dimitri Gutas's *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture* (1998), which Saliba acknowledges as "a plausible explanation" for the Persian-ascendancy theory but ultimately finds insufficient for the same reasons he rejects the other classical-narrative explanations.
