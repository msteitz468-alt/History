## Definition and Scope

This concept denotes the typology and critical problems associated with recovering native South American views of the past. Because "no native South American culture practiced writing in the common sense of the word before Iberian contact" (Salomon), the historian faces a layered problem: indigenous memory was encoded in mnemonic technologies alien to European writing, then passed through colonial mediating institutions — chronicles, lawsuits, church extirpation campaigns — that partially preserved and partially transformed it. The concept asks how to use these filtered records to write both "histories of Indians" (what happened to indigenous peoples as European sources tell it) and "Indian histories" (the past as indigenous actors understood and narrated it).

The typology developed by Salomon covers five main source classes:

1. **Native mnemotechnologies** — knotted-cord [[khipu]], dances, chants, pilgrimages, ancestor mummies, tomb architecture, figurative ceramics (notably Moche), and the Cuna pictographic system. These encoded history before 1492 but are accessible mainly through later colonial descriptions.

2. **Oral testimony embedded in Iberian chronicles** — amautas (Inka intellectuals) and harauicos (poets) composed and performed dynastic cantares; Cieza de León and Betanzos recorded fragments. The 1551 account by Betanzos records how "Ynga Yupangue ordered captive Sora lords...displayed before him" and a new victory refrain composed — showing the ceremonial matrix of Inka historical memory. Such testimony is always already "framed within the theme of Spanish victory."

3. **Civil and administrative records** — visitas (house-to-house tribute inspections), lawsuits, wills, and geographical relaciones. These are in many respects richer than chronicles for social history: "lawyers' and administrators' relative unconcern with scholastic norms of historiography...opened their work to considerations that sometimes approach the ethnographic." John Murra's pathbreaking use of visita data exemplifies this vein. Native litigation mobilized genealogies, khipu readouts, and eyewitness memories — e.g., the 1558 Xauxa lawsuit recovered tribute records "down to the last 'partridge'...and pair of sandals" from a quarter century earlier.

4. **Church records** — idolatry-extirpation trials (especially from Huarochirí onwards, 1607–c.1660s), parish visitas, Jesuit annual letters from mission frontiers (Amazonia, Paraguay, Orinoco). Extirpation trials contain "the fullest accounts of ritual and even of visionary experience" but are severely deformed by coercion and the requirement that witnesses use the rubric of "Satanic deception." They are also a rare source on Andean women as religious actors.

5. **Native and mestizo chroniclers** — a short-lived burst (c.1570–1620) of explicitly Andean historical writing: Titu Cusi Yupanqui's 1570 *Relación* (manifesto of Vilcabamba resistance); Garcilaso Inca de la Vega's 1609 *Royal Commentaries* (idealized Inka rule, hugely influential in Europe through the eighteenth century); and the "fully Andean" voices of Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala and Juan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui Salcamaygua, provincial nobles whose works combine Andean oral memory with Renaissance periodization and European epistolary genre. Repression after the 1780 Tupac Amaru II rebellion — banning Quechua publication, Inka-themed drama, even "music for ancient instruments" — sharply curtailed this tradition.

## Emic vs. Etic Dimensions

The emic dimension is irreducibly plural: Inka oral traditions were proprietary property of specific *panakas* (descent corporations), so "historical truth is felt to be relative to, and the property of, the group 'owning' a tradition." When rival panakas gave irreconcilable dynastic lists to Spanish inquisitors, this was not dishonesty but the normal operation of Andean historical epistemology. Similarly, in the Huarochirí manuscript (c.1607–08), the tellers "were not chiefly interested in compiling a chain of human causes for human events. Rather, their main preoccupations were 'mythohistoric'": time structured as a genealogical web linking living people through mummified ancestors to huacas and finally to cosmic forces.

The etic frame — imposed by historians working in Western linear-chronological historiography — must be applied critically and sparingly. Chronological dates extracted from oral genealogies are subject to "structural amnesias" (telescoping of supererogatory data) and the conflation of similar events into single archetypes; the execution of Atahuallpa in 1532, of the last independent Inka Tupac Amaru I in 1572, and of neo-Inka rebel Tupac Amaru II in 1781 collapse in popular memory into a single recurring mythic event.

## Application

Native Andean historical sources are the primary evidence base for:
- Pre-Inka political history of the [[andes]] (c.1000–1438 CE)
- [[inca-empire]] dynastic chronology and administrative history
- [[chimor]] and coastal Andean polities
- The [[spanish-conquest-of-peru-1532]] as experienced from within
- Andean religious history through the extirpation campaigns
- [[ayllu]] and [[mita]] social structures
- Amazonian (frontier) mission history

They feed the [[myth-history-debate-andes]] directly: the tension between treating Inka dynastic traditions as historical chronicle (Rowe's "absolute chronology," 1945) versus as mythic structural encoding (Zuidema's structuralist reading) runs through every use of this source class.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Colonial mediation is total**: every written record of pre-contact indigenous voices passed through the "filter of legalistic form," missionary framing, or chronicle genre conventions. "Native stylistics were silenced. First-person diction and native-language tropes got flattened to a characteristic run-on reportorial style."
- **Geographic unevenness**: the Andes had nearly a century of "indirect rule" allowing transculturation and writing within native society; Amazonian and Pampean regions were not so favored, yielding "very scarce" testimony. "There was no period of 'indirect rule' during which translation, transculturation, and writing could develop within native society."
- **Genre anachronism**: academic "myths and folklore" collections impose a bible-like outline on oral texts that were originally non-sequential; most ethnographic compilations "sacrifice the oral contribution to 'history of Indians' without gaining any advantage for 'Indian histories.'"
- **Literacy bias**: Goody/Ong thesis that literacy is necessary for historical thinking has been challenged (Vansina, Street), but the oral-literate divide did shape what records survived from which communities.
- **Modern sources**: twentieth-century testimonial autobiography (Gregorio Condori Mamani, Domitila Barrios de Chungara) and indigenous-language oral historiography (Rivera Cusicanqui's Oral History Workshop) extend the tradition but are shaped by new political projects.
