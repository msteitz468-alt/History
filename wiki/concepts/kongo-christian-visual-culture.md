## Definition and Scope

Kongo Christian visual culture denotes the distinctive material and symbolic system that the [[kingdom-of-kongo|Kingdom of Kongo]] elites produced through the active transformation of imported European Catholic imagery — crucifixes, saints' pendants, clerical garments, swords bearing Christian ornament — into objects charged with specifically Kongo meanings of authority, legitimacy, and spiritual power. It is neither simple adoption nor simple syncretism: the Kongo elite remade foreign visual forms according to their own categories, embedding them in a local cosmology centred on the relationship between the living and the dead, the visible and the invisible. The concept is both emic (the kingdom genuinely identified as Christian) and etic (modern scholars reconstruct the internal logic of the transformation).

Fromont (ch. 6) argues the analytical power of the concept is best demonstrated by combining three classes of evidence that had previously been studied in isolation: European missionary images, written accounts, and the material culture recovered through the KongoKing archaeological excavations at Kindoki (former capital of Nsundi province) and [[mbanza-kongo|Mbanza Kongo]].

## Origins and Historical Context

Christianity reached the Kongo through the coastal province of Soyo, where Portuguese missionaries landed in the late fifteenth century. [[afonso-i-kongo|Afonso I]] (r. 1509–42), who had first encountered the faith as governor of Nsundi, made Christianity the ideological foundation of his reformed kingship. Nsundi — economically strategic as a gateway to copper and raffia-producing regions north of the kingdom — thus occupied a doubly privileged position: cradle of Kongo Christianity and hub of the prestige goods economy that made Christian objects meaningful.

The Christian material culture that Afonso I institutionalized was never merely religious in a European sense. Crucifixes, medals, and swords were simultaneously political insignia, markers of legitimate rulership in the system Afonso founded. This fusion of sacral and political meaning shaped everything that followed in Kongo visual culture through the nineteenth century.

## Material Evidence from the KongoKing Excavations

Fromont (ch. 6) examines eleven elite tombs at the hilltop of Kindoki, excavated by the KongoKing project in 2012–13 and dating between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries. The burials demonstrate the material vocabulary of Kongo Christian elite identity with unprecedented precision:

**Female elite tombs (8 and 11, c. 1825–45):** Both women were interred with hundreds of glass beads (red, white, blue, silvery), pierced shells, iron ankle chains, gold necklace chains, and copper-alloy hawk bells sewn to a partially preserved shroud. Tomb 11 additionally preserves traces of elaborate coiffure combining textile and decorative metal pins. These goods precisely match the costume visible on women in Friar Bernardino d'Asti's c. 1750 watercolors of Kongo wedding ceremonies — confirming both the accuracy of the missionary images and the continuity of elite female material culture.

**Gendered access to Christian objects:** A notable finding is that the female tombs at Kindoki contain no crucifixes, medals, or swords, while male tombs do. The young male in tomb 6 (estimated 18–22 years old at death) was buried with a sword and a medal. Fromont argues this reflects the gendered structure of Kongo Christian political symbolism: Christian insignia were the public markers of legitimate authority in a political system in which men held the most prominent leadership roles. Women could and did hold political offices, but the emblems indexing that system were predominantly male-coded. Women's prestige derived from overlapping sources — commercial wealth, hereditary status, initiatory associations (tomb 8 contains a distinctive spiral-shaped shell and hawk bells associated with kimpasi and khimba initiation groups) — rather than exclusively from the Christian insignia.

**Copper alloy pendants:** Fromont reconstructs how cast copper pendants depicting the Virgin Mary and male saints (especially Saint Anthony) functioned as mirrors of the Kongo elite who wore them. The Franciscan habit of Saint Anthony, smoothed by wear, increasingly resembled the draped cloth of elite Kongo men; the hood approximated the nkutu shoulder net of prestige; the tonsure mimicked the mpu cap. A female pendant photographed by Pierre de Maret in Nsundi in 1973 (preserved in the archives of the Royal Museum for Central Africa, Tervuren) and closely similar to the Metropolitan Museum's example confirms continued local custody of such objects into the modern period.

**Swords as political insignia:** Swords were among the most emblematic objects of the Kongo, appearing in early visual sources (Pigafetta/Lopes 1591; Dapper 1668) and confirmed by the Kindoki excavations. The sword fragment from tomb 4 at Kindoki bears an angel-face pommel — a motif shared with Kongo crucifixes, confirming that swords and crosses formed a single set of Christian-political insignia. The angel's head is pierced by a nail in a manner Fromont compares to the construction logic of power figures (minkisi), suggesting the sword participated in a broader material culture of object empowerment operating at the end of the kingdom era. The handle's metal-thread wrapping creates a textile-like effect reminiscent of the mpu cap — the sword anthropomorphized as a crowned head.

## The Methodology of Multidisciplinarity

A central argument of Fromont (ch. 6) is methodological: neither class of evidence — images, written sources, or archaeological material — can be fully interpreted in isolation, but each amplifies the analytical potential of the others. Capuchin missionary images document costumes and practices that the written archive incompletely describes; excavated objects give precise dates, geography, and material context that museum-held objects lack; the images allow archaeologists to reconstruct fragmentary grave goods into whole objects. The KongoKing project's combination of archaeology, art history, and documentary history represents a model for the study of early-modern African material culture.

## Gender and Religious Participation

Fromont (ch. 6) traces a structural tension between Kongo elite women's desire for institutional religious engagement and the limitations the Roman Catholic Church imposed on female participation. In 1584, a daughter of King Álvaro I attempted to establish a Carmelite female religious order in Mbanza Kongo after being moved by a statue of the Virgin dressed in Carmelite habit — the first time she had seen the Virgin in clerical garb comparable to the male saints' habits common in the capital. The plan failed; no female convent opened. The female elite instead formed lay associations and occasionally appropriated male Franciscan garb (the prophetess Kimpa Vita, c. 1700; an elderly pious widow of a king; Queen Njinga and her sister Dona Barbara of Ndongo, who were buried in Franciscan habits) as statements combining religious dedication with gender ambiguity serving political ends.

## Transition from Kingdom to Colonial Era

The Kindoki excavations date to the early nineteenth century, covering the terminal phase of the historical kingdom. Fromont (ch. 6) argues that the material from these tombs crucially illuminates the understudied transition between the kingdom era and the colonial period: Christian insignia (swords, medals) still operated as legitimizing symbols, but initiatory associations and commercial wealth provided alternative prestige foundations. Objects like the angel-faced sword fragment "foreshadowed imagery, practices, and emblems that would be recorded in the colonial era," making the archaeological record of this period a bridge between two otherwise disparate documentary worlds.

## Historiography

The study of Kongo Christian material culture has been shaped by several disciplinary traditions:

**Art history:** Earlier scholarship treated Kongo crucifixes and pendants primarily as ethnographic objects or African art, often without attention to their specific political and cosmological functions. Fromont's 2014 monograph *The Art of Conversion: Christian Visual Culture in the Kingdom of Kongo* established the interpretive framework now standard in the field: the Kongo elite were active agents of visual transformation, not passive recipients of European influence.

**Archaeology:** The KongoKing project (launched 2012) provided the first systematic excavation of elite Kongo contexts in 25 to 60 years, depending on the sub-region (Clist et al. 2015c, 2015d, 2015e). Prior knowledge of Kongo material culture derived almost entirely from museum collections and undated surface finds. The project's dated, contextualized assemblages transformed the field.

**Gendered history:** The absence of Christian objects in the female Kindoki tombs is a recent finding (Fromont ch. 6) that opens an important research question — the gendered dimensions of Kongo Christian practice had not previously been addressed through archaeological evidence. The written archive, composed mainly by European men, provides an incomplete view.

**Debates:** The degree to which Kongo Christianity was genuinely Christian versus a formal overlay on indigenous religion has been extensively debated. Thornton (1984, 1998b) argued that the Kongo elite understood themselves as genuinely Christian within their own hermeneutic framework; MacGaffey emphasized the persistence of indigenous Kongo categories. Fromont's approach, focusing on visual culture, sidesteps the doctrinal debate by showing that the material forms functioned simultaneously in multiple registers — Catholic, political, cosmological — without contradiction.
