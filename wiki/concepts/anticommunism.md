# Anticommunism

An organized political ideology and movement opposing communism and
communist political parties, spanning a spectrum from principled
liberal-democratic opposition to authoritarian repression of dissent
conducted in its name.

## Definition and Scope

Anticommunism took markedly different institutional forms across the
twentieth century: as domestic law enforcement and surveillance (in the
United States, associated with figures such as
[[hoover-j-edgar|J. Edgar Hoover]] and the FBI), as foreign policy doctrine
(containment and its various interventionist applications during the Cold
War), and as a mobilizing ideology for movements ranging from mainstream
liberal anti-totalitarianism to the radical political right.

## Origin (Emic/Etic)

Anticommunism is a genuine emic political self-identification adopted by
its adherents, though the label also functions analytically (etic) to group
a diverse range of otherwise distinct political actors and movements united
mainly by their shared opposition rather than a single positive program.

## Applications and Examples

In the United States, anticommunism shaped domestic politics through
episodes including the First and Second Red Scares, the FBI's COINTELPRO
surveillance programs under Hoover, and McCarthyism; internationally, it
underpinned the containment doctrine and associated interventions
throughout the Cold War, from the Truman Doctrine through Vietnam.

## Limitations and Critiques

Critics distinguish anticommunism as principled defense of liberal
democratic institutions from its more repressive applications — surveillance
overreach, blacklisting, and support for authoritarian anticommunist regimes
abroad — a tension at the center of ongoing historical debate over the
Cold War's moral and political legacy.
