# Rois Fainéants ("Do-Nothing Kings")

**The image of the later [[merovingian-franks|Merovingian]] kings as feeble, idle figureheads** —
the *rois fainéants* of French tradition. Geary treats it as a textbook case of **manufactured
historiography**: a hostile portrait created by Carolingian propagandists to justify the usurpation
of 751, then accepted uncritically for over a millennium.

## The Einhard portrait

The canonical image comes from **Einhard's** *Vita Karoli*, which depicts the last Merovingian,
Childeric III, as a theatrical do-nothing: he would "sit on the throne, with his hair long and his
beard flowing, and act the part of a ruler, giving audience to the ambassadors… Whenever he needed to
travel, he went in a cart which was drawn in country style by yoked oxen," coached on what to say.

## Geary's critique

- **It is propaganda, not observation.** Einhard's portrait "was not original but derived from
  Carolingian propaganda." The first continuator of the *Chronicle of Fredegar* reworked the *Liber
  Historiae Francorum* into an Austrasian/Carolingian key; the second continuation was prepared under
  orders of **Charles Martel's half-brother, Count Childebrand**. The hostile image was "intended to
  undermine the prestige of the Merovingian royal family."
- **Many later Merovingians were not idle.** Childeric II "attempted to recover royal authority… he was
  murdered for his efforts"; Theuderic III actually reunited and briefly ruled the kingdom after Ebroin's
  death.
- **The "do-nothing" rituals were real kingship.** The archaic long hair and the ritual **ox-cart** were
  ancient symbols of Frankish royal identity, and the annual assembly where the king received ambassadors
  "as a symbol of the unity of Francia" gave the aristocracy a genuine **non-violent arena** for
  competition — "[it] cannot but remind modern readers of the British monarch in gilded coach."
- **A new political category.** Childeric III "was not deposed for tyranny… he was deposed for simple
  incompetence," introducing the durable category of the *rex inutilis*, the "useless king" — later
  reused against Carolingians and, eventually, Louis XVI.

## Limitations and afterlife

The legend was so successful that even the greatest Merovingian, [[dagobert-i|Dagobert I]], survives in
the mocking nursery rhyme *"Le bon roi Dagobert."* Geary connects the durability of the image to a wider
reluctance to acknowledge Merovingian continuity: Germany looked past the Romanised Franks for more
"authentic" Germanic ancestors east of the Rhine, while France "leapt" from Gallo-Roman Syagrius to
Charlemagne — both answering "no" to the question *"Les Francs sont-ils nos ancêtres?"*

## Why it matters

The *rois fainéants* is a standing warning about **source bias**: the Merovingians are known largely
through the propaganda of the dynasty that overthrew them. It belongs with the partisan portraits of
[[brunhild|Brunhild]] (via her enemies) and the compressed chronology of [[gregory-of-tours|Gregory of
Tours]] as a reminder that the early-medieval record is rarely neutral.

## Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World (2020)

Paul Fouracre's chapter in the [[sources/effros-moreira-oxford-handbook-merovingian-world-2020|Oxford Handbook of the Merovingian World]] is a sustained rebuttal of the *rois fainéants* image, which he identifies as Carolingian propaganda adopted uncritically: "the survival of Francia is the strongest testimony to the stability of its political and religious culture, and it was this stability that was the bedrock of Carolingian power." No other early medieval dynasty could repeatedly survive "rule by children and queen regents" without disintegrating. Charter evidence even complicates the showpiece of decline: after [[battle-of-tertry-687|Tertry]] (687) the kings "still enjoyed support that limited the power of any one magnate group," against the Carolingian sources' claim of powerless kings [CONTRADICTION between narrative and charters].
