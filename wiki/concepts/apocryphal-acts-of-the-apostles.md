# Apocryphal Acts of the Apostles

The **Apocryphal Acts of the Apostles** are a cluster of early-Christian narratives of the
second and third centuries CE — chiefly the Acts of Paul (including the Acts of Paul and
Thecla), Acts of Andrew, Acts of John, Acts of Peter, and Acts of Thomas — that recount the
travels, preaching, miracles, and (usually) martyrdom of an individual apostle. They stand
outside the New Testament canon but mimic the structure of the canonical
[[luke-acts|Acts of the Apostles]] while drawing even more heavily on the conventions of the
[[ancient-novel|Greek and Roman novel]] (spectacular miracles, eroticism, travel, peril).

## Significance for Social History

- **Female characters**: the Apocryphal Acts are notable for prominent, often ascetic and
  independent women (e.g. Thecla), and have been read as voicing — or at least registering —
  women's participation and authority in early Christianity.
- **Slavery**: enslaved characters recur as minor figures, and slavery functions both
  literally and as metaphor. The texts are ambivalent: some episodes endorse the institution
  and its corporal punishment (the tortured slave **Euclia** in the Acts of Andrew, a foil
  Cobb reads against Rhoda), while others (the Acts of Thomas) contain "several critiques of
  slavery, both implicit and explicit" (Glancy), even imagining the apostle himself as
  enslaved. The faithful slave **Iphidama** (Acts of Andrew) is included in the Christian
  community alongside her owner Maximilla.
- They are a principal comparative corpus for reading the social world and narrative art of
  [[luke-acts|Luke-Acts]] and the [[enslaved-truth-teller|enslaved truth-teller]] motif.

## Limitations and Critique

Long dismissed as legendary, they are now valued less as records of apostolic events than as
evidence for the **imagination, controversies, and social composition** of second- and
third-century Christian communities. Whether their prominent women reflect real female
authority or male authorial projection is contested.
