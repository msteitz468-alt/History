## Definition

**Maqāṣid al-sharīʿa** (Arabic: مقاصد الشريعة) means "the purposes" or "objectives" of the Sharīa. In classical [[usul-al-fiqh]], it designates the teleological dimension of legal theory: the claim that God's law aims at the preservation of five (sometimes six) essential human goods — religion, life, intellect, lineage, and property. Legal rules were analyzed partly by reference to whether they served, protected, or threatened these objectives.

The concept occupies a pivotal but contested position in both pre-modern legal thought and 20th-century Islamic legal reform. Its modern career illustrates Hallaq's broader argument about the epistemological transformation of the [[sharia]] under modernity.

## Pre-Modern Significance

In classical jurisprudence, *maqāṣid* served as a secondary, inductively derived framework for reasoning about legal causation (*ta'līl*) and public interest (*maṣlaḥa*). Al-Ghazālī classified *maṣlaḥa* according to whether it was corroborated, contradicted, or left unaddressed by the revealed texts. Al-Shāṭibī systematized the maqāṣid framework and gave *maṣlaḥa* a higher theoretical status through the doctrine of **inductive corroboration** (*istiqrā'*): a general principle could override specific textual rulings if it was confirmed by the overwhelming weight of the entire textual corpus.

Crucially, in pre-modern usage, the *maqāṣid* were *derived from* the existing body of [[fiqh]] — they were an induction over the law, not a superimposed external standard. They functioned within, not above, the [[usul-al-fiqh]] system.

## Modern Transformation

The concept underwent a radical transformation in modern Islamic legal thought, becoming in effect a lever for dismantling the traditional [[usul-al-fiqh]] while retaining the appearance of Quranic grounding.

### Muḥammad Rashīd Riḍā (d. 1935)
Riḍā's landmark appropriation of *maqāṣid* and *maṣlaḥa* detached them from their classical constraints and deployed them as "an overriding principle" that could supersede any revealed text. Hallaq notes that whereas "Ghazālī's and Shāṭibī's *maqāṣid* are inductively inferred from the existing body of fiqh, Riḍā's *maqāṣid* would be progressively reduced to an induction whose subject-matter is the state's *tashrī*" — positive legislation. Necessity (*ḍarūra*) was similarly elevated to a meta-principle: "by elevating the concept of necessity to an inductively drawn principle, the Quran and the Sunna would be subordinated to the *maṣlaḥa* principles inferred from *maqāṣid al-sharīʿa*."

### ʿAbd al-Wahhāb Khallāf (d. 1956)
Khallāf developed a programmatic theory in which matters not covered by clear texts (*lā naṣṣa fīh*) were subject to unlimited ijtihad guided by the *maqāṣid*. Since this encompassed the bulk of legal subject matter, the *maqāṣid* framework effectively replaced classical *qiyās* and *ijmāʿ* methodology with a more open-ended teleological reasoning.

### Muḥammad al-Ghazālī (d. 1996) and the "New Maṣlaḥa School"
A generation of thinkers identified by Hallaq as "new maṣlaḥa scholars" pushed the framework further, arguing that any rule producing genuine harm to the Islamic community could be set aside regardless of its textual pedigree. This approach had the practical effect of subordinating textual authority to consequentialist evaluation — a near-inversion of the classical hierarchy of sources.

### Fazlur Rahman (d. 1988) and the Double Movement
Fazlur Rahman's "Double Movement Theory" employed the *maqāṣid* logic at the level of hermeneutical method: the First Movement extracts general principles from specific Quranic rulings by reconstructing their 7th-century Arabian context; the Second Movement applies those general principles to contemporary conditions. This presupposes that the teleological principle (*maqṣad*) has higher authority than the specific rule (*ḥukm*). Hallaq identifies a critical weakness: "just what the criteria are for rejecting certain 'important respects' [of the present that differ from the Prophetic past] and not others is a crucial question that Rahman does not seem to answer decisively."

## The Crisis of Legal Methodology

Hallaq uses the extended 2006 dialogue between Muḥammad Saʿīd Ramaḍān al-Būṭī (defender of traditional [[usul-al-fiqh]]) and Muḥammad Ḥabīb al-Marzūqī (critic of juristic authority) to illustrate that even the *maqāṣid*-centered reformers have failed to produce a coherent successor methodology:

"While certain trends in fashioning new legal thought can be detected, especially around the themes of *maṣlaḥa*, *maqāṣid* and necessity, the foregoing discussions show a wider multiplicity of orientations that can scarcely agree on a unified set of principles, and much less on a coherent theory that fills the vacuum resulting from the collapse of pre-modern *uṣūl al-fiqh*."

The Būṭī–Marzūqī dialogue — in which the two scholars share no common ground and fail even to engage each other's arguments — demonstrates "the persistent paralysis created by" the 19th-century epistemic breakdown.

## Emic vs. Etic Status

The *maqāṣid* concept is simultaneously **emic** (used by Muslim jurists and reformers to justify legal positions within an Islamic framework) and **etic** (analyzed by scholars like Hallaq as an index of structural transformation). Its modern career shows how a concept with genuine pre-modern roots was radically repurposed to serve the needs of states and reform movements operating in an entirely different institutional environment.

## Contested Dimensions

1. **Whether classical maqāṣid texts support the modern expansion**: Hallaq argues they do not; the modern extension takes them "out of their historical contexts."
2. **Whether the maqāṣid framework can generate binding legal rules** or only general orientations: the Būṭī position holds it cannot substitute for formal *uṣūl* methodology.
3. **Whether maqāṣid-based reasoning is Islamic or covertly utilitarian**: Islamist critics (following Quṭb) charge that it amounts to "human legislation producing a system where men rule over each other."
