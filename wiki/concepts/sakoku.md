# Sakoku (the "Closed Country")

***Sakoku*** ("closed country") is the [[edo-period|Tokugawa]] policy (from 1639) that
severely restricted [[japan|Japan's]] contact with the outside world for over two centuries
— banning Christianity, forbidding Japanese to leave or return, and confining European
trade to a single port. From Mason & Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of
Japan*]] (ch. 13).

## The policy

After the Christian **Shimabara rebellion (1637)** — a rising of ~30,000 mostly Christian
peasants in Kyūshū, suppressed with Dutch naval help — the bakufu under **Iemitsu** enacted
a series of measures (1633–39), known at the time as ***kaikin*** ("maritime prohibitions")
and later as *sakoku*. In substance: **no Japanese could leave the country** (or return);
**no Catholic (Iberian) could enter**; and **all foreign trade and diplomacy were confined
to Nagasaki**. The **Portuguese** were expelled; of the Europeans only the Protestant
**Dutch** remained, confined to the islet of **Dejima** in Nagasaki harbour, trading
alongside the **Chinese**.

## Not total isolation

Mason and Caiger stress that "Japan was never entirely closed." Beyond Nagasaki, the bakufu
authorized controlled foreign relations through three han: **Satsuma** (with Okinawa and so
China), **Tsushima** (with Korea, via a permanent trading post at Pusan), and **Matsumae**
(with the Ainu of the north). Strikingly, relations with **Korea** and the Ryūkyūs were
conducted by regular embassies, and with Korea on a footing of **mutual equality** — Japan
**anticipating** the modern principle of sovereign-state parity and refusing the
Sinocentric tributary order. This farming-out of foreign relations to particular domains is
a model case of how the [[shogunate-bakufu|baku-han system]] worked.

## Why close the country?

Three convergent motives: **economic** (trade increasingly meant draining
[[global-silver-trade|precious metals]] for replaceable goods); **religious-political**
(mistrust of Christianity, which had taken deep root in distant Kyūshū where the Tokugawa
were militarily weak — too reminiscent of the militant Buddhist leagues); and the **shock
of Shimabara**. Once in place, the policy became a "bulwark for vested interests" that
later shoguns dared not reverse.

## Consequences and debate

Sakoku is **contested**. Its costs: Japan **missed the Scientific Revolution and the early
Industrial Revolution** — technologically Europe's equal in 1650, it was far behind by 1850.
Its benefits, in Mason and Caiger's reading: closure let Japan develop its **political,
commercial, and cultural** life "in its own way" and remain **cohesively Japanese**, so
that — unlike much of colonized Asia — its ruling class was not alienated from the masses by
Western influence, and it could **absorb modernization on its own terms after 1850**.
Recent scholarship (Toby, Tashiro) further argues that "sakoku" overstates the isolation —
hence the stress on the active Korean, Ryūkyū, Ainu, and Dutch/Chinese channels — and that
success in foreign relations actually helped **legitimize** Tokugawa rule. The policy ended
under Western pressure (Perry, 1853), helping bring down the bakufu.

## The anti-Christian road to seclusion (Cambridge History of Japan)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v4-early-modern|Cambridge History]] (Jurgis Elisonas, ch. 7)
shows that seclusion grew directly out of the **suppression of [[christianity-in-japan|Japanese
Christianity]]**: the same five **closed-country directives (*sakoku-rei*, 1633–39)** that
expelled the Portuguese were addressed to the Nagasaki commissioners and fused three aims the
shogunate "clearly thought interconnected" — banning Japanese travel abroad (a **capital
crime after 1635**), reducing the European presence, and "the merciless persecution of
Christianity." Elisonas describes the policy's **two faces**: inward, the inquisition
(*shūmon aratame*), the trampling-tests (*fumie*), and compulsory Buddhist temple
registration (*terauke*) that turned anti-Christian surveillance into a permanent instrument
of social control; outward, the conviction that the "Christian peril" could be eliminated only
by **total isolation** from its sources. The expulsion of the Portuguese in **1639** and the
beheading of their 1640 Macao embassy "ended the Christian Century." On this reading, *sakoku*
was less an economic or xenophobic reflex than the **logical endpoint of the Tokugawa decision
to extirpate Christianity** — the prerogative the bakufu reserved to itself as guarantor of
the realm ([[shogunate-bakufu]]).

## The Deshima window: Dutch Learning (Sansom)

Sansom stresses that the Dutch post at **Deshima** was not only a trade outlet but Japan's
**one window onto Western knowledge**. After Shogun **Yoshimune** lifted the ban on foreign
(non-Christian) books in **1720**, scholars built ***rangaku*** ("Dutch learning") from almost
nothing — painfully compiling dictionaries and translating works on **medicine** and
astronomy (**Sugita Genpaku's** anatomy, 1774). So even at its most closed, *sakoku* admitted
a thin but consequential stream of Western science that, by 1800, gave Japan a real grasp of
European learning and contributed to the intellectual ferment behind the
[[meiji-restoration-1868|Restoration]] (see [[tokugawa-intellectual-life]]).
