# Welsh Prose Literature

## Definition and Scope

Welsh prose literature of the Middle Ages includes translations from Latin (religious, apocryphal, classical adaptations) and some original compositions. It encompasses religious instructional texts, saints' lives, apocryphal narratives (e.g. on the Cross, Adam, Pilate), romances, and mystical treatises. Distinct from the dominant poetic traditions (cywyddwyr, Gogynfeirdd), prose often served didactic, devotional, and entertainment purposes in vernacular.

Key characteristics from entries: “Translations from Latin apocryphal texts constitute a sizeable category in the Welsh prose literature of the Middle Ages.” Many works are grouped in manuscripts such as Llyfr Gwyn Rhydderch (Peniarth 5) and Llyfr Coch Hergest.

## Major Examples (Verbatim Grounded)

- **Ymborth yr Enaid (Sustenance of the soul)**: “a Middle Welsh prose treatise on mysticism, being the third and sole surviving book of a larger work called Cysegrlan Fuchedd (Holy living). It was composed by an anonymous Dominican, probably around the middle of the 13th century... Divided into three logically progressive parts, it treats of the purgative, illuminative, and unitive aspects of the soul’s journey towards God. Untypically, it is an original composition... Its most striking feature is a lengthy, highly coloured and heavily rhetorical section in part three; this describes a brilliant vision that the author experienced of the 12-year old Jesus teaching in the temple... For several reasons, this work is unique both in Welsh and European literature (see Welsh prose literature) and is the only known example from the medieval period of an original mystical treatise in a Celtic language.”

- **Ystorya Aªaf (The tale of Adam)** and related: “one of the many examples of Welsh prose literature of the Middle Ages based on Latin Christian apocryphal literature. It is one of a group of Welsh texts—together with Y Groglith (The crucifixion text) and Inventio Sanctae Crucis (The discovery of the Holy Cross)—which focuses on the Holy Rood... Ystorya Aªaf explains the origins of the Cross on which Christ was crucified and its path from Paradise to Mount Calvary.”

- **Ystorya Bilatus (The tale of Pilate)**: “a Middle Welsh text, a translation of the Latin Christian apocryphal text Historia Pilati, which... supplied additional details and background to the biblical accounts of the crucifixion of Jesus Christ. The Ystorya Bilatus explicitly depicts the life and gruesome end of Pontius Pilate.”

- **Ystorya Bown de Hamtwn (‘The Story of Bevis of Hampton’)**: “a Middle Welsh prose adaptation of the Anglo-Norman romance La geste de Boun de Hamtone. The text probably dates from the middle of the 13th century, which would make it the earliest translation of a secular French narrative into Welsh... In content and structure the Welsh version closely follows its source, but it exhibits a clearer emphasis on Christian attitudes than the known Anglo-Norman versions. In idiom and style it follows vernacular narrative norms.”

- **Transitus Beatae Mariae**: “Latin apocryphal texts and their Middle Welsh translations which deal with the life, death, and ascension of Mary... The Welsh Transitus thus provides evidence for the strength of the pan-European Mary cult in the vernacular culture of medieval Wales (Cymru).”

- **Ellis Wynne (1671–1734)**: “recognized as one of the most important writers of Welsh prose literature... In 1703 he published his most famous work, Gweledigaetheu y Bardd Cwsc (‘Visions of the Sleeping Bard’)... Wynne’s Gweledigaetheu has long been acclaimed a prose classic... The book consists of three visions... The range of its style makes the Gweledigaetheu different from all Welsh prose works which preceded it, and its quality has given it the status of a classic.”

## Interaction with Other Processes

Closely linked to contacts with French literature (Arthurian, romances, Charlemagne cycles), Latin Christian learning, and manuscript culture (White Book of Rhydderch, Red Book of Hergest). Contributed to preservation and adaptation of international motifs in Welsh idiom. Later prose (e.g. 17th–18th c.) continued religious and visionary traditions.
