# Stateless Societies (West Africa)

A **stateless society** is one in which political authority is *not* concentrated in
a ruler or governing institution. The category is the deliberate counterpart to the
great states ([[ghana-empire|Ghana]], [[mali-empire|Mali]], [[songhay-empire|Songhay]],
the [[hausa-city-states|Hausa]], [[benin-kingdom|Benin]]) that dominate the
narrative of West African history — and a corrective to the assumption that
"history" means the history of states. Documented for the wiki through **Robin
Horton's** chapter in [[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of West
Africa* Vol. I]] (1971), which insists that perhaps **thirty-five million West
Africans** lived in essentially stateless conditions at the dawn of the colonial era
— the Tiv, most [[igbo-people|Ibo (Igbo)]], the Tallensi, the Lodagaa, the Konkomba,
many Cross River and Liberian-hinterland peoples, and the scattered
[[fulani|Fulani]] pastoralists.

## Horton's four-point definition

A society is *stateless* (an ideal type at one pole of a continuum) where:
1. there is **little concentration of authority** — no individual or small group is
   identifiable as "the ruler";
2. such authority roles as exist affect only a **limited sector** of subjects' lives;
3. the wielding of authority as a **specialised, full-time occupation is virtually
   unknown**; and
4. the unit within which people feel bound to settle disputes by agreed rules rather
   than force is **relatively small**.

Real societies sit at various points on the continuum: the Tiv and central Ibo near
the stateless pole, some Cross River and Liberian groups nearer the middle.

## Varieties: a three-type typology

Horton organises stateless organisation by the role of the **lineage** — the
genealogical "calculus" that is the basic building block of stateless society:

- **Type 1 — Segmentary lineage systems** (Tiv; certain central Ibo). The whole
  society is ordered by a single embracing genealogy, which is the *sole* dominant
  principle. Its defining features are **relativity of political grouping** (units
  fuse and fission according to who opposes them — "bickering brothers who unite
  against an outsider"), the **equivalence of segments** (coordinate segments are
  strict equals and rivals; an over-large segment splits to restore balance), and the
  **predominance of leadership over authority** (representatives who lead the group
  against others, rather than rulers who command within it). The "pure type" is
  actually **rare**, requiring a growing population, evenly available peripheral land,
  and a **dispersed settlement pattern** (defence not overriding the demands of
  small-scale farming).
- **Types 2 & 3** — societies where the lineage is *one* principle among others, and
  where **territorial** principles emerge: common residence on a defined tract of
  land, submission to the laws of the **earth-spirit**, complementary (not equivalent)
  lineages performing distinct services, age-grades, and **secret societies** (Poro,
  Ekpe) cross-cutting the lineages.

## Relation to state formation

The analytically crucial payoff: Types 2 and 3 contain "**germs from which state
organisation and ideology could sprout**" — territorial sovereignty, a
first-occupant/landowning lineage that prefigures a royal lineage, complementary
office-holding lineages that prefigure a council of chiefs, and secret societies that
could incubate the specialisation of political roles. Between the *pure* segmentary
system (Type 1) and the state, by contrast, "there seems a great gulf fixed" — which
is why the Tiv and Ibo remained stateless **even when they entered long-distance
trade**. Horton therefore argues the West African transition from stateless to state
organisation involved **no drastic discontinuity**, only "changes of balance and
emphasis." See [[state-formation]] and [[hamitic-hypothesis-african-states]].

## Why some societies stayed stateless

Stateless societies cluster **away from the great long-distance trade routes** —
their survival in stateless form was a *condition* of their distance from commerce.
Horton's strongest evidence is the contrast within single cultural areas: among the
eastern **[[niger-delta-city-states|Ijo of the Niger Delta]]**, fishing villages
stayed stateless while those that switched to the **Atlantic trade** turned their
founding lineages into royal lineages and their village heads into kings (boosted by
the "comey" protection-money European traders paid them); the same split appears
among the Mbembe of the Cross River. Trade, not alien conquest, was the engine.

## Significance and critique (contested)

- **Methodological:** Horton stresses that one **cannot write a conventional
  "history of" stateless societies** — there are almost no contemporary written
  records (Arab and European visitors gravitated to the trading states), and their
  oral traditions are shallower and less curated than those of the states, which keep
  specialist [[griot-oral-tradition|court historians]]. Their past must be approached
  indirectly, through linguistics, culture-trait mapping, and archaeology.
- **Etic category, with limits:** "stateless society" is "an artificial and
  approximate categorisation," an ideal type, not a natural kind. Horton warns
  against both the anthropologists' "romantic fascination" with such societies and the
  historians' tendency to **ignore** them because standard techniques fail on them.
- The segmentary-lineage model itself (Evans-Pritchard/Fortes) has since been heavily
  criticised in anthropology as an idealisation that overstated genealogical neatness;
  Horton already treats the "pure type" as a rarely-attained limiting case.
