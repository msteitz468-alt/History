# Indirect Approach

The **indirect approach** is [[liddell-hart-basil|Basil Liddell Hart]]'s central strategic
idea, refined across successive titles from *The Decisive Wars of History* to its final
form, *Strategy: The Indirect Approach*. Where the direct approach takes "the obvious route
into a confrontation with a prepared enemy," the indirect approach instead seeks to
"diminish the possibility of resistance." Liddell Hart presented it, as the source notes,
"as an 'indirect approach' as the 'most hopeful and economic form of strategy.'" It is an
emic concept—a prescriptive doctrine its author advocated—that has also become an etic
analytical label historians use to classify maneuver-and-dislocation styles of war.

## Definition and Core Logic

The indirect approach locates the decisive blow in the mind rather than the body of the
enemy: "The vital impact would be in the psychological rather than the physical sphere."
Liddell Hart distinguished two complementary mechanisms—"so while movement might be the key
to catching the enemy out physically, surprise was the key to influencing the enemy's
psychology." Both serve a single end: **dislocation**. "Dislocation is the aim of
strategy," he wrote; "its sequel may either be the enemy's dissolution or his easier
disruption in battle." In the physical sphere, dislocation is achieved by upsetting the
enemy's dispositions through a sudden "change of front"—"separating enemy forces,
endangering supplies, menacing routes of retreat, or combining several of these moves." In
the psychological sphere it requires these effects to be "impressed on the commander's
mind, creating a 'sense of being trapped.'" The strategist therefore seeks "the line of
least resistance," which translates psychologically into "the line of least expectation,"
and keeps multiple options open to put the enemy "on the 'horns of a dilemma'"—for "a plan,
like a tree, must have branches—if it is to bear fruit."

The doctrine's ideal is the avoidance of serious combat altogether. Following [[sun-tzu|Sun
Tzu]], Liddell Hart prescribed "an ideal form of strategy as it should be rather than how
it often turned out in practice," holding that the aim of war was "to subdue the enemy's
will to resist, with the least possible human and economic loss to itself." Its perfect
expression: "The perfection of strategy would be, therefore, to produce a decision without
any serious fighting." Its antecedents are explicit—Sun Tzu's "constant emphasis on doing
the unexpected and pursuing the indirect approach," and T. E. Lawrence's early formulation,
which Liddell Hart read in 1920.

## Contrast with the Direct / Annihilation Approach

The indirect approach was framed against a direct approach that Liddell Hart attributed to
[[clausewitz-carl-von|Clausewitz]] (or his followers): the conviction "that everything must
be geared to decisive battles with the sole aim of destroying the enemy army through
frontal assaults." This is the pursuit of [[annihilation-and-exhaustion|annihilation]]
through battle that Liddell Hart blamed for the Western Front's slaughter, calling
Clausewitz "the 'evil genius of military thought.'" The indirect approach is thus offered
as the antithesis of battle-seeking annihilation—though, Freedman notes, the campaigns
Liddell Hart actually admired "were often attritional, in that they required wearing down
the opponent."

## Link to Deterrence

Carried to its logical end, the indirect approach shades into [[deterrence|deterrence]].
"The ideal indirect strategy created conditions in which the enemy was forced to conclude
that defeat had become inevitable before battle was joined." Once that relationship became
apparent it "encouraged the adversary to become more conciliatory"—and, in Freedman's
words, "the logic pointed to deterrence." If the outcome of battle were known in advance,
"the best advice would be to avoid the original provocation or—at the other extreme—go for
complete, preemptive surprise."

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept is contested. Liddell Hart "claimed that his theory developed through a careful
examination of the whole of military history," but "his approach to history was intuitive
and eclectic rather than... 'scientific,'" and indirectness was stretched so far—"strategic,
tactical, psychological and sometimes even 'unconscious'"—as to drain it of testable
content. The sharpest objection, from Brian Bond, is circularity: "by his definition, a
'decisive victory' was an event which is secured by an 'indirect approach.'" The doctrine
also faces practical puzzles "of how matters would be resolved if both sides were following
an indirect approach, the practical problems of coordination, and the impact of chance and
friction." Finally, its preconditions are demanding: "Societies and their armies could
prove to be extremely resilient," and mounting sustained indirect pressure "requires
effective military dominance—whether at sea, in the air, or on land," which "in turn was
likely to require very direct and decisive contact with enemy forces." The indirect
approach, on this reading, "represented a strategic ideal but one only likely to be
realized in very special circumstances."
