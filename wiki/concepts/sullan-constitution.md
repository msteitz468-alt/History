# The Sullan Constitution (the dictatorship settlement, 82–81 BCE)

The sweeping reconstruction of the Roman constitution by which **[[sulla|Sulla]]**,
as dictator with unlimited tenure and a mandate "to set the state in order and
draft laws," tried to **entrench the supremacy of the Senate** and disable the
forces that had eroded it since 133. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 6
(Seager).

## The office

Sulla revived the obsolete **dictatorship** in an unprecedented form — appointed by
an *interrex* (L. Flaccus) through a *lex Valeria*, **not for the traditional six
months but until the state was restored**, with every act ratified in advance and
the right to put citizens to death without trial. Seager stresses this was **not a
dictatorship for life**: the open-ended mission was itself a kind of term, and Sulla
duly **abdicated** (probably late 81), holding an ordinary consulship in 80 — the
gesture later sources found incomprehensible.

## The reforms

**Restoring and enlarging the Senate.** Halved by the wars and proscriptions, the
Senate was rebuilt to c. **600** by enrolling some 300 *equites*; to keep it at
strength the **quaestorship was raised from 8 to 20** and quaestors entered the
Senate automatically.

**Crippling the tribunate** — the central measure. Tribunes were **stripped of the
right to legislate** (the veto perhaps surviving), and anyone holding the tribunate
was **barred from further office**, so that "men of talent would shun it." This made
the tribunate "a shadow without substance" (Velleius).

**Regulating the magistracies.** The ***cursus honorum*** was enforced by a revived
*lex annalis* (minimum ages — c. 29 quaestor, 39 praetor, 42 consul — and the fixed
order quaestor → praetor → consul); a **ten-year interval** was required before
re-holding any office (aimed at Marian-style iteration). Praetors were raised to
**eight**.

**The provinces and *maiestas*.** A new **law of *maiestas*** restrained
proconsuls: a governor could not leave his province, lead his army beyond it, enter
a foreign kingdom, or make war on his own initiative, and had to quit within 30
days of his successor. Seager: such laws held only "as long as the Senate was in
reality stronger than any individual governor."

**The courts.** Sulla restored **senatorial juries** and, more durably, **revised
and extended the whole system of standing courts (*quaestiones perpetuae*)** — his
most lasting work, surviving into the Principate.

**The people.** **Tribunician legislation abolished**; **grain distributions
abolished**; the people's share in choosing priests (the *Lex Domitia* of 104)
abolished and co-option restored, the colleges raised to fifteen. Plus sumptuary
laws and the settlement of **23 legions** of veterans in colonies, heavily on
confiscated Etruscan and Samnite land.

## Why it failed

Seager's verdict is bleak: the new Senate "lacked the individual authority, the
practical experience, the public spirit and above all the moral self-confidence" to
govern, and fell into "a sterile conformism which exalted mediocrity." The
**veteran settlements** bred the unrest that fed Lepidus (78) and Catiline (63);
the **crippled tribunate** was the most resented measure and was **dismantled within
a decade** — its legislative power restored by [[pompey]] and Crassus in **70**.
Above all, Sulla had himself demonstrated that **an army loyal to its commander
could override any law** — the lesson that doomed his own settlement.

## Contested

Whether the constitution was **reactionary entrenchment** of oligarchy or a
genuine, coherent **reconstruction** is debated (Badian, Gabba, Keaveney). Disputed
points: how much of the dictatorship's programme was already foreshadowed in 88;
the exact date of abdication; whether tribunes kept the veto.
