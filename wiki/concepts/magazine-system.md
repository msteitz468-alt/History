# Magazine System

The dominant logistic system of 17th- and 18th-century European warfare, in which armies were supplied primarily from centralized, pre-positioned depots (magazines) rather than by living off the countryside or long continuous lines of communication.

Key characteristics (per van Creveld):
- Enabled a degree of planning and control.
- Extremely slow and cumbersome; armies were tied to roads, rivers, and the locations of magazines.
- Suited to the "age of linear warfare" and siege operations, where speed and deep penetration were not priorities.
- Required enormous administrative effort (Louvois under Louis XIV was a master of this).
- Limited strategic mobility: an army could rarely operate more than a few days' march from its nearest magazine without risking starvation or disintegration.

Contrasted with the "tyranny of plunder" (earlier predatory systems) and the later "predatory" or "living off the country" system perfected by Napoleon.

The magazine system illustrates how logistics can dictate the very character of warfare: in this case, producing slow, methodical, limited wars rather than rapid decisive campaigns.

See also [[line-of-communication]], [[umbilical-cord-of-supply]], [[van-creveld-supplying-war-1977|Supplying War (van Creveld)]].

The book's final chapter places the magazine system in long perspective: despite technological revolutions (rail, motor), the core constraints on mobility and the need for ad hoc adaptation persist. "most armies seem to have prepared their campaigns as best they could on an ad hoc basis"; theoretical optimum ratios (potentially 100:1 in complex modern cases) defy precise pre-calculation. Logistics realities continue to trump abstract planning or genius, as they did under the magazine regime.

Grounded in van Creveld's analysis of pre-Napoleonic logistics and Ch. 8 synthesis.
