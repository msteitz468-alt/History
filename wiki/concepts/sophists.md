# The Sophists (and the Athenian Enlightenment)

## Athens as a magnet

[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 8h, M. Ostwald) frames fifth-century Athenian culture
as overwhelmingly the work of **foreigners drawn to the imperial city** — painters, poets,
prose writers ([[herodotus]], Hellanicus), natural philosophers, and above all the
**sophists**. The intellectual movement they led "is one of the momentous achievements of
the human mind... an attempt on many fronts to comprehend man, his society and the universe
by rational means with the least possible recourse to supernatural explanations."

## Natural philosophy comes to Athens

Athens "had not yet produced any philosophers of its own." It was **Anaxagoras of
Clazomenae** (associated with [[pericles]], and prosecuted for impiety) who "brought
philosophy into the city," teaching *Nous* (Mind) as the cause of cosmic motion; his books
sold for a drachma and his ideas pervade Euripides. Others passed through —
**Parmenides** and **Zeno** of Elea, **Democritus** of Abdera (the atomist: "I came to
Athens and no one knew me"), **Diogenes** of Apollonia (parodied in Aristophanes' *Clouds*) —
and the first native Athenian philosopher, **Archelaus**, said to be Socrates' teacher.

## The sophistic profession

Because political success in the [[athenian-democracy|democracy]] depended on **persuading**
Council, Assembly, and mass juries, ambitious upper-class young men sought to **buy** the
rhetorical skill that had come naturally to a Themistocles or Pericles. The supply came from
abroad, for a **fee**:

- **Protagoras of Abdera** (c. 433) — the first to call himself *sophistes* and the only real
  philosopher among them. His "**man is the measure of all things**" made human commonsense
  experience, not cosmic speculation, the measure of what is; **no law is truer than another**
  (laws are "valid," not right or wrong), though the wise statesman can make "better" things
  seem just to a city. He claimed to teach **"sound judgement" (*euboulia*)** — the *politike
  techne*, the "art of citizenship" — and (per Diogenes Laertius) that there are **two opposed
  arguments on every question**, which earned him the charge of "making the weaker argument
  the stronger."
- The later "sophists" — **Gorgias** of Leontini (who dazzled Athens as an envoy in 427; pure
  rhetoric, "no claim to inculcate *arete*"), **Prodicus** of Ceos (linguistic precision; a
  rationalizing account of the **origin of religion** that earned him a name for atheism),
  **Hippias** of Elis (the polymath, deviser of the **list of Olympic victors** as a
  panhellenic chronology), **Thrasymachus**, and others — were teachers of **rhetoric** for
  pay, not system-builders. Their pupils were a "Who's Who" of the upper class (Alcibiades,
  Critias, Callicles), hosted in houses like that of the rich **Callias**.

## The *nomos–physis* antithesis

The movement's most consequential idea was the opposition, crystallizing in the last third of
the century, between **nomos** (law, custom, convention — human, mutable, locally valid) and
**physis** (nature — an unchangeable force, whether the innate character of an individual or
"human nature" as such, Thucydides' *anthropine physis*). The antithesis, Ostwald argues, was
born at Athens under sophistic influence, and could be turned to **radical and amoral**
conclusions — that justice is merely "the interest of the stronger" (Thrasymachus in Plato),
or that the superior man should follow nature against the conventions of the herd (Callicles).
This is the dangerous edge Plato dramatized and that conservatives feared.

## Science and medicine

The same rationalist climate fostered **mathematics, astronomy, and medicine**: the
Pythagorean-influenced geometers (Oenopides, Hippocrates of Chios, Theodorus of Cyrene),
the astronomer **Meton**, and the **Hippocratic** medical tradition with its rejection of
magic and its emphasis on observation, environment, and the patient's psychological state.

## The impact and the backlash

The sophists left "the most pervasive and lasting cultural imprint" on Athens, but their
rationalism opened a **rift between intellectuals and the common people** (the impiety trials
of Anaxagoras, Protagoras, and ultimately **Socrates** — who can be called a sophist "only
with strong qualifications," refusing fees and seeking truth rather than persuasion). The
*nomos–physis* debate and the sophistic "enlightenment" frame the political ruthlessness of
the later [[peloponnesian-war]] and the philosophical reaction of Plato and Aristotle.

## Historiography

The picture is "largely filtered through Plato," who was "more trustworthy as a philosopher
than as a historian" and hostile to the sophists; recovering their actual achievement (esp.
Protagoras') means sifting fact from Platonic prejudice. The reputations of "sophistry" and
"atheism" are partly later constructions.

## OHCW (Annas, West, Murray)

[[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]], Ch. 10 (Julia Annas): sophists (Protagoras, Prodicus, Hippias, Antiphon, Thrasymachus, Alcidamas, Lycophron, Gorgias) toured cities offering the only available **higher education for a fee**, teaching rhetoric useful in public politics; Plato depicts them as pretentious manipulators of puzzles without systematic understanding. Zeno’s book of pure arguments and Gorgias’s proof that nothing exists (and if it did could not be known or communicated) marked a new **detachment about argument**; [[aristophanes|Aristophanes’]] *Clouds* reflects public fear that clever argument was a dubious talent indifferent to truth; the *Dissoi Logoi* lumps strong and fallacious pro/con lists without analysis.

**Nomos vs physis.** Sophists popularized the claim that human institutions are *nomos* (convention), not *physis* (nature), and can be changed for different purposes; the vague contrast fostered confusion and amoralist rejections of norms (Callicles in Plato’s *Gorgias* as parade example). West (Ch. 5): mid-fifth-century reconstructions of prehistory and Archelaus’s account of conventional law feed into Protagoras and fee-charging discourses on nature vs custom, morality, education, grammar, and adaptable argument; Gorgias’s “nothing exists” may be playful.

**Public opinion and schools.** Murray (Ch. 9): by the 420s systematic higher education for public life was available at Athens; Plato’s great confrontations (*Protagoras*, *Gorgias*) do not reflect contemporary opinion, which **did not distinguish** Socrates from the sophists. Fourth-century polarity: Plato’s philosophical school vs Isocrates’ rhetorical training — developed systematic logic and rhetoric in [[aristotle|Aristotle]] and a philosophy/rhetoric split dominating later antiquity. Sophistic-trained aristocratic youth in oligarchic coups discredited politics-as-art. Parker (Ch. 11): Protagoras’ “About the gods I cannot declare whether they exist or not” is central to the late-fifth **religious crisis**; “not recognizing the city’s gods” became a prosecutable charge ([[socrates|Socrates]]). Annas: [[aristotle|Aristotle’s]] *Prior Analytics* and *Rhetoric* finally sorted the muddled fifth-century “art of argument.”
