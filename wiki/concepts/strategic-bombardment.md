# Strategic Bombardment

**Strategic bombardment** is the doctrine that an independent air force can win a war *directly* — by bombing an enemy's "vital centers" (cities, industry, transport) and the **morale of its civilian population** — rather than by supporting armies and navies in the field. It is the operational core that links the two air theorists of *[[sources/jablonsky-roots-of-strategy-4-1999|Roots of Strategy, Book 4]]*, [[douhet-giulio|Giulio Douhet]] and [[mitchell-william|William "Billy" Mitchell]], and the doctrine that the wiki's [[strategic-bombing-campaign|Combined Bomber Offensive]] and the [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/second-world-war/strategic-bombing-japan-1944-1945|firebombing of Japan]] would later put to the test.

## Origin: not invented in a vacuum

As David Jablonsky's introduction stresses, neither air theorist *invented* strategic bombardment; both **assembled ideas already current** during and after the First World War. The British general Hugh Trenchard wanted to attack "the enemy's strategic heart by means of a relentless and incessant offensive" without first defeating his army; the official **Smuts Report** of August 1917 concluded that

> the day may not be far off when aerial operations with their devastation of enemy lands and destruction of industrial and population centres on a vast scale may become the principal operations of war, to which the older forms of military and naval operations may become secondary and subordinate.

Douhet's wartime collaboration with Gianni Caproni on long-range bombers, and Mitchell's correspondence with Caproni and friendship with Trenchard, mean the doctrine crystallized as a trans-Allied conversation. The armistice of November 1918 cut short a planned interallied independent bombing force, leaving the central claim **untested** — which is exactly why it became an article of faith rather than a proven method.

## The core wager

The doctrine rests on a single, contestable proposition: that **a society's will to resist is fragile and can be shattered from the air faster than an army can be defeated on the ground.** Douhet drew the line sharply:

> A body of troops will stand fast under intensive bombings, even after losing half or two-thirds of its men; but the workers in shop, factory, or harbor will melt away after the first losses.

Several propositions follow, shared in different measure by the two theorists:

- **Decisiveness.** Command of the air, won by bombing, is "practically certain to win the whole war" (Mitchell); it makes war, in his phrase, "sharper, more decisive, and more quickly finished."
- **The primacy of the offensive.** For Douhet, "**aerial warfare admits of no defense, only offense**"; air defense and interception are near-useless, so the only security is to strike first with a larger bomber fleet (see [[command-of-the-air]]).
- **No sanctuary.** Mitchell: "The frontiers in the old sense — the coast lines or borders — are no longer applicable to the air … Interior cities are now as subject to attack as those along the coast. **Nothing can stop the attack of aircraft except other aircraft.**"
- **Civilians as the target.** Once the theorists rejected aiding surface forces, Jablonsky notes, "civilian populations were the only practicable targets for independent airpower." This is what makes the doctrine inherently a doctrine of **[[total-war|total war]]** — and what set Douhet against the [[limited-war|limited war]] frame of the naval theorists.

## Douhet vs. Mitchell

The two diverged as much as they agreed — a distinction the volume is careful to draw:

| | [[douhet-giulio\|Douhet]] | [[mitchell-william\|Mitchell]] |
|---|---|---|
| Force structure | all-**bomber** "battleplane" fleet | bombers **and** pursuit/interception |
| Air defense | impossible; don't bother | "whip the enemy's air forces in air battles" |
| Geography | European, offense-minded | American, ocean-buffered, defense-framed |
| Targets | enemy population, aerochemical attack on cities | sea lanes, fleets, then (after 1926) cities |
| Limits of law | "swept away like dried leaves on the winds of war" | retained "everything begins and ends on the ground" |

Mitchell's American, hemispheric-defense framing was the more moderate; but after his 1925 court-martial and 1926 resignation his predictions "became increasingly inflated and more closely aligned to Douhet's 'command of the air.'"

## Interwar innovation: doctrine ahead of capability

Murray's case study in *[[sources/murray-millett-military-innovation-interwar-1996|Military Innovation in the Interwar Period]]* (1996) treats strategic bombing as one of the seven test cases of [[military-innovation]], and as the one "particularly influenced by external factors": the breakneck pace of aviation technology, the trauma of the First World War (escape from "another terrible war of attrition on the ground"), and the "romantic" mythology of flight that gave airmen and the public a faith running far ahead of evidence. Its appeal was the promise to "attack the enemy's economic and social base from the opening moments of conflict," pursuing "objects which were beyond, not only the range, but also the strategy of armies and navies."

The comparative verdict is sobering, and it cuts against the doctrine's own confidence:

- **Britain** — Bomber Command made strategic bombing the RAF's reason for independent existence, but **top-down control enforced an unworkable doctrine**: at the outbreak of war the force was "almost completely… inability… to execute any air power missions." Murray uses it as his warning that top-down leadership "will get it very, very wrong" when the underlying idea is wrong.
- **United States** — the Air Corps Tactical School built the rival **daylight-precision / "industrial-web"** doctrine, more discriminating than Douhet but, like the British, untested against the peacetime "imponderables" (Could bombers find and hit targets? Could they defend themselves?).
- **Germany** — the **Luftwaffe** pointedly did *not* build a heavy-bomber strategic force, integrating air power instead into combined-arms continental war ([[close-air-support]], [[armored-warfare]]) — a different reading of the same strategic problem.

The through-line to [[radar-and-air-defense]]: Dowding's integrated air-defense system was the contextual innovation that falsified Baldwin's "the bomber will always get through," and with it the interwar bomber-supremacy doctrine.

## Rescue by the Atom Bomb and Persistence into the Nuclear Age (Freedman 1981)

The great bomber offensives of World War II failed to validate pre-war expectations. The bomber had not always "got through", and when it had the results were less than decisive; air raids were instruments of attrition rather than a means of breaking a deadlock. As Freedman details, the dramatic use of the atom bomb at Hiroshima and Nagasaki "rescued the doctrine of strategic bombardment. Without the atom bomb the theorists of airpower would have been pushed on to the defensive, hard put to justify the pounding of cities for limited rewards." With the atom bomb, "airpower could be said to have come of age."

The old axioms were reformulated: "the rocket would always get through; atomic bombardment offered an independent and swift route to total victory." The terms of the debate on the strategic significance of the new technology were set by the existing debate on the value of strategic bombardment. General Arnold, Chief of Air Staff, stated in his November 1945 Final Report: "The influence of atomic energy on air power can be stated very simply. It has made air power all important . . . [The] only known effective means of delivering atomic bombs in their present state of development is the very heavy bomber . . . This country . . . must recognize that real security ... in the visible future will rest on our ability to take immediate offensive action with overwhelming force. It must be apparent to a potential aggressor that an attack on the United States would be followed by an immensely devastating air attack on him."

The atom bomb was a weapon that could only be delivered by air and against large targets (cities). As long as supplies were limited there would be no point wasting them on small point targets. It made airpower more efficient by decreasing the "cost of destruction". Substituting atomic for conventional explosives made "a given amount of destruction 'at least six times more economical'." The Hiroshima bomb caused "as much damage as 300 planes would have done."

Among airmen an ideology had been created around the heavy bomber; they could not contemplate a truly effective defence against the bomber nor accept value in any role other than strategic bombardment. The immediate impact of Hiroshima and Nagasaki silenced criticism for a couple of years: "Strategic bombing had won its case, and the ignored lessons of World War II could remain ignored by the public, Congress and all others except the inquiring scholar or the parochial Army or Navy man."

This persistence directly shaped early nuclear strategy. See [[concepts/nuclear-strategy]], [[concepts/deterrence]], [[actors/brodie-bernard]], and [[sources/freedman-evolution-of-nuclear-strategy-1981]].

## Soviet Rejection of Strategic Bombardment Primacy (Freedman 1981 Range 02)

Soviet military science took a sharply different view. The focal point was the land battle; respect was given to infantry, tanks, and artillery. Air power's "natural role" was "combat support of ground forces". In the 1930s some interest in strategic bombardment theories existed, but "a number of the most enthusiastic advocates of long-range bombing were purged and greater priority was given to tactical airpower". Even after Stalin was impressed by Anglo-American strategic bombardment, "official Soviet doctrine allotted only a limited role to this sort of activity and treated with derision Western theories of the primacy of strategic airpower."

A 1949 article summarized the official view: "Soviet military science considers that the outcome of war under contemporary conditions is decided on the field of battle by means of the annihilation of the armed forces of the enemy, and that one of the most important tasks of aviation is active assistance to the ground and naval forces in all forms of their combat activity. This definition of the fundamental mission of aviation is not contradicted by the need to employ part of its forces to strike the deep rear of the enemy, on his military-industrial targets, but our military science does not consider such blows an end in themselves, but only a helpful means of creating favourable conditions for the success of the combat operations of the ground and naval forces." (Freedman 1981: "STRATEGY FOR AN ATOMIC MONOPOLY").

These arguments, as much as the lack of the weapon itself, conditioned the initial Soviet response to the atom bomb. Even after the Soviet bomb and long-range aircraft programmes advanced, "there was no significant shift in Soviet pronouncements" while Stalin lived. The intellectual climate did not encourage innovation; the "Greatest Strategist of Modern Times" did not alter his views on the Permanently Operating Factors.
