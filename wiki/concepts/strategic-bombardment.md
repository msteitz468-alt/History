# Strategic Bombardment

**Strategic bombardment** is the doctrine that an independent air force can win a war
*directly* — by bombing an enemy's "vital centers" (cities, industry, transport) and the
**morale of its civilian population** — rather than by supporting armies and navies in the
field. It is the operational core that links the two air theorists of
*[[sources/jablonsky-roots-of-strategy-4-1999|Roots of Strategy, Book 4]]*,
[[douhet-giulio|Giulio Douhet]] and [[mitchell-william|William "Billy" Mitchell]], and the
doctrine that the wiki's [[strategic-bombing-campaign|Combined Bomber Offensive]] and the
[[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/second-world-war/strategic-bombing-japan-1944-1945|firebombing of Japan]] would later put to the test.

## Origin: not invented in a vacuum

As David Jablonsky's introduction stresses, neither air theorist *invented* strategic
bombardment; both **assembled ideas already current** during and after the First World War.
The British general Hugh Trenchard wanted to attack "the enemy's strategic heart by means of a
relentless and incessant offensive" without first defeating his army; the official **Smuts
Report** of August 1917 concluded that

> the day may not be far off when aerial operations with their devastation of enemy lands and
> destruction of industrial and population centres on a vast scale may become the principal
> operations of war, to which the older forms of military and naval operations may become
> secondary and subordinate.

Douhet's wartime collaboration with Gianni Caproni on long-range bombers, and Mitchell's
correspondence with Caproni and friendship with Trenchard, mean the doctrine crystallized as a
trans-Allied conversation. The armistice of November 1918 cut short a planned interallied
independent bombing force, leaving the central claim **untested** — which is exactly why it
became an article of faith rather than a proven method.

## The core wager

The doctrine rests on a single, contestable proposition: that **a society's will to resist is
fragile and can be shattered from the air faster than an army can be defeated on the ground.**
Douhet drew the line sharply:

> A body of troops will stand fast under intensive bombings, even after losing half or
> two-thirds of its men; but the workers in shop, factory, or harbor will melt away after the
> first losses.

Several propositions follow, shared in different measure by the two theorists:

- **Decisiveness.** Command of the air, won by bombing, is "practically certain to win the
  whole war" (Mitchell); it makes war, in his phrase, "sharper, more decisive, and more
  quickly finished."
- **The primacy of the offensive.** For Douhet, "**aerial warfare admits of no defense, only
  offense**"; air defense and interception are near-useless, so the only security is to strike
  first with a larger bomber fleet (see [[command-of-the-air]]).
- **No sanctuary.** Mitchell: "The frontiers in the old sense — the coast lines or borders —
  are no longer applicable to the air … Interior cities are now as subject to attack as those
  along the coast. **Nothing can stop the attack of aircraft except other aircraft.**"
- **Civilians as the target.** Once the theorists rejected aiding surface forces, Jablonsky
  notes, "civilian populations were the only practicable targets for independent airpower."
  This is what makes the doctrine inherently a doctrine of **[[total-war|total war]]** — and
  what set Douhet against the [[limited-war|limited war]] frame of the naval theorists.

## Douhet vs. Mitchell

The two diverged as much as they agreed — a distinction the volume is careful to draw:

| | [[douhet-giulio\|Douhet]] | [[mitchell-william\|Mitchell]] |
|---|---|---|
| Force structure | all-**bomber** "battleplane" fleet | bombers **and** pursuit/interception |
| Air defense | impossible; don't bother | "whip the enemy's air forces in air battles" |
| Geography | European, offense-minded | American, ocean-buffered, defense-framed |
| Targets | enemy population, aerochemical attack on cities | sea lanes, fleets, then (after 1926) cities |
| Limits of law | "swept away like dried leaves on the winds of war" | retained "everything begins and ends on the ground" |

Mitchell's American, hemispheric-defense framing was the more moderate; but after his 1925
court-martial and 1926 resignation his predictions "became increasingly inflated and more
closely aligned to Douhet's 'command of the air.'"

## Limitations and Critiques

Strategic bombardment was, in Jablonsky's words, the doctrine of **visionaries** rather than
theorists — built on "little empirical evidence" selectively drawn from a war in which air
power had been marginal. Its central wager was **the most-tested and most-disappointed claim**
in the volume:

- Civilian morale proved far more *resilient* than predicted; populations endured bombing that
  Douhet assumed would "deny reason itself."
- Douhet **ignored the air battle** required to win command of the air, **dismissed tactical
  air power and air defense**, and **underestimated the continuing need for surface forces** —
  errors exposed in the Second World War.
- The doctrine's logic of preemption and unrestricted attack on cities **denigrated
  international law and morality**, a moral cost that the firebombing campaigns and the atomic
  bombings would make unavoidable.

Yet, Jablonsky argues, the theorists' lasting value is in the **questions** they forced — the
meaning of command of the air, its relation to the other instruments of power, and the use of
air forces as instruments of national policy — even where their answers have not survived.
The doctrine's nuclear-age successors (deterrence, counter-value targeting) and the post–Gulf
War debate over whether precision air power can be independently decisive are direct
descendants of the argument begun here.
