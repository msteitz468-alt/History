## Definition and Scope

A **premodern state** (or "early state") is a centralized polity that extracts surplus and
maintains order **without** the defining capacities of the modern state: a salaried,
disciplined bureaucracy reaching every subject; a monopoly on information; reliable
computational tools for planning; and the coercive saturation that lets a modern
government enforce uniform rules to the village level. Premodern rulers face high
**transaction costs** (see [[new-institutional-economics]]): they cannot directly observe
or control most of what happens in their territory, so they govern through **intermediaries**
and **negotiated arrangements** rather than top-down command.

The analytical payoff is a model of rulership as **bargaining**. Because the ruler depends
on others — landed and priestly elites, soldiers, tax collectors, local notables and
scribes — to mobilize resources and project authority, government is a continual exchange:
the crown grants privileges, exemptions, land, status, and legal recognition in return for
revenue, military service, legitimacy, and local order. The state's reach is real but
**bounded**; its apparent "absolutism" is an ideological claim that coexists with practical
dependence and frequent compromise.

## The Bargaining Model (Manning's Application)

[[sources/manning-last-pharaohs-2010|J. G. Manning]] situates [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic
Egypt]] squarely within this comparative category — its only true parallel, he argues,
being the **Seleukid** kingdom, since both were Macedonian dynasties governing ancient,
deeply-institutionalized populations. Against the old "[[ptolemaic-economy|planned
economy]]" and "oriental despotism" pictures, Manning argues the Ptolemies ruled by
bargaining with key constituencies:

- the **Egyptian priesthood**, whose temples were economic and ideological power centres
  (the trilingual priestly decrees record the terms of the bargain);
- the **soldier-settlers** (cleruchs/*katoikoi*), bound to the crown by land grants;
- **local elites, notables, and scribes**, on whom collection and record-keeping depended.

Coercion existed (a rural police force; the suppression of revolts), but the recurrent
[[asyut-property-trial-170bce|revolt of the Thebaid]] (the "massive and ugly" independent
state of 205–186 BCE) demonstrated the practical limits of state-building and the cost
of pushing the bargain too hard. On this reading "the last pharaohs" were neither
omnipotent planners nor mere predators but **managers of a negotiated order** layered on an
ancient framework — a *"reasonably successful premodern state — a new synthesis of
politics, religion, and economic institutions."*

Manning borrows the model's vocabulary from comparative state theory and applies it
explicitly. He follows **Karen Barkey's "bargained incorporation"** (from her work on the
Ottoman Empire), in which *"the 'capturing' of society by the state generated both
bargaining and opposition."* The Ptolemaic state was a *"dual polity"*: the central court
controlled the military, foreign relations, and tax-farming, while *"agricultural
production, religious practices, tax collection, and the legal system were, by and large,
in the hands of local elites."*

## The Mechanics: Power, Information, and the Cost of Collective Action

Manning's premodern-state model rests on several borrowed distinctions that recur across
the wiki's analytical pages:

- **Despotic vs. infrastructural power** (Michael Mann). A ruler may face no constitutional
  check (high *despotic* power) yet be unable to *"effect political action throughout the
  state"* (low *infrastructural* power). The Ptolemaic question is *"the degree to which the
  Ptolemaic regime had control over or control through Egyptian society."* Manning's answer
  is **through** — *"not over society, but rather through it."*
- **Extensive vs. intensive power.** A premodern empire could mobilize large numbers across
  great distances for *"minimal co-operation,"* but could not *"organize the state
  internally to solve particular problems"* — Patricia Crone's point that *"genuine control
  over provincial affairs simply could not be achieved."*
- **Asymmetric information** as a structural ceiling. The dirigiste claim that the king
  *"knew each day what each of his subjects was worth"* (Tarn & Griffith) is impossible;
  detailed census data *"is really only required at the local level,"* so the crown tracked
  its **local officials**, not its millions of subjects.
- **The "capstone" state** (Crone): a government that sits atop society *"built to control
  rather than built to last … able to prevent, but unable to organize well."*
- **Raising the cost of collective action.** Rather than centralize, the Ptolemies
  *"co-opted elites and created organizations that competed against each other, thus making
  coordination at the local level difficult"* — deliberately fragmenting potential
  opposition. Manning names three coalition strategies: **terror, co-optation, and raising
  the cost of collective action.**

The **dynastic mechanism** itself is read institutionally: adopting pharaonic succession
gave the regime a *"long-term time horizon,"* countering the predatory short-termism of *"an
ageing dictator"* (Stephen Haber). Echoing **Margaret Levi's** theory of predatory rule,
the *"normal tendency of states to over extract … was of course tempered by political
constraints generated by ruling coalitions embedded in ancient institutions."*

## Comparative Anchors

Manning situates Ptolemaic Egypt among other long-lived premodern bureaucratic empires,
explicitly invoking **the Ottoman Empire** (Barkey), **Tokugawa Japan** (whose 268-year
span is *"almost identical in length to the Ptolemies"*), and **Han China** — while noting a
key contrast: unlike China, Egypt *"never developed a civil service examination system"* or
a Confucian-style code of conduct, so it generated prestige through *"personal ties between
the elites and the king"* rather than an impersonal meritocracy. The Ptolemaic dynasty was
*"the longest lasting of the Hellenistic 'successor states'"* and indeed of all Egyptian
history — evidence, for Manning, that the bargaining equilibrium worked.

## Why It Matters Beyond Egypt

The model is a general tool for the wiki: it applies to other ancient and medieval polities
that look "absolutist" in their own propaganda but were, in practice, coalitions held
together by bargaining (compare [[state-formation]], and the comparative pages on
[[empire-formation-and-collapse-patterns]] and the
[[theatre-state-and-the-bases-of-power|theatre state]]). It cautions against reading
royal claims of total control — "all the land is the king's" — as descriptions of reality.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Over-correction** — emphasizing bargaining can understate genuine coercive and
  extractive power; some premodern states really did crush their subjects.
- **Elite bias** — the "constituencies" the model foregrounds are the documented ones;
  the unbargaining mass of cultivators appears mainly as the object of extraction.
- **Definitional looseness** — "premodern state" lumps together polities of vastly
  different scale and capacity (city-states, territorial kingdoms, empires).
