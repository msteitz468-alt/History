# Papyrology

**Papyrology** is the study of the texts written on papyrus, ostraca (potsherds), and similar
perishable media that survive, almost uniquely, in the dry sand of Egypt. Because of
this accident of preservation, Greco-Roman and Late Antique Egypt is — in Bagnall and Rathbone's
words — a society whose inhabitants "are far better known to us than any other group from the
ancient world." The discipline is the documentary foundation of ancient social, economic, legal,
and religious history. (For the specifically Ptolemaic source-base and the Zenon archive, see
[[ptolemaic-papyri]], of which this page is the broader, period-spanning frame.)

## What survives, and from where

Tens of thousands of documents survive — "public and private, written on papyrus in Greek and
other languages" — giving "in random bursts, a wealth of detailed historical information about
events and daily life which is unparalleled in earlier Egyptian history or in any other area of
the Greek and Roman worlds." They include tax records, land leases, loans, contracts, petitions,
official correspondence, private letters, school exercises, accounts, wills, and marriage
settlements, alongside literary and sub-literary texts (Homer, Herodotus, magical papyri,
horoscopes, music).

Most were not archived but **discarded as rubbish** in and around ancient settlements; some, in
the Ptolemaic period, were reused as **cartonnage** (papier-mâché) to wrap mummies. Languages
run the full sequence of Egypt's history: **hieratic, demotic, Greek, Latin, Coptic, Aramaic,
Persian, and Arabic**, from the fourth century BCE to the eleventh century CE.

## The discipline and its biases

The single most important methodological lesson papyrology teaches is that **the evidence is
radically skewed** in time, place, and survival, and must never be read as a neutral sample:

1. **Place-skew.** Papyri survive where ancient settlement was overtaken by desert, above all the
   **[[the-fayyum|Fayyum]]** and **[[oxyrhynchus|Oxyrhynchus]]**. There is *nothing* from
   Alexandria and almost nothing from the Delta — Egypt's richest and most populous region — so
   the documented society is disproportionately that of provincial towns and villages.
2. **Time-skew.** The flow is discontinuous: nearly blank for some reigns, a flood for others
   (e.g. c. 259–215 BCE for the Ptolemaic Fayyum), recovering again in the sixth century with
   great estate archives like that of the **Apion family** at Oxyrhynchus.
3. **Publication-skew.** The published material is only a fraction of the whole, and demotic and
   Coptic texts are under-edited relative to Greek.

Claire Préaux's foundational insistence — to record the **date and provenance of every text** and
to refuse extrapolation "from the flood-lit areas into the dark" — defines responsible practice in
the field.

## How papyrology reshaped ancient history

Papyrology turned Egypt into the **type-site of ancient social and economic history**. It allows
demographic work (life expectancy in the mid-twenties, driven by infant mortality —
Bagnall & Frier, *The Demography of Roman Egypt*, 1994), the reconstruction of village economies
([[karanis|Karanis]], Tebtunis), the study of literacy, law, and the texture of religious change
(the displacement of pagan by biblical personal names tracks [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianisation]]).
The finds also preserve lost literature: half the published papyri of Herodotus come from
Oxyrhynchus, and the **Nag Hammadi** codices (with the [[manichaeism|Manichaean]] texts of
Kellis and Narmouthis) recovered whole bodies of Gnostic and Manichaean writing.

## Limitations and critiques

- The documentary "voice" still favours the literate and propertied; the poorest leave fewer
  traces, though far more than elsewhere in antiquity.
- Survival bias can masquerade as historical change: a village that "disappears" from the record
  may simply have stopped depositing rubbish in the desert (the Fayyum edge, early fourth century).
- Generalising "Roman Egypt" to "the Roman Empire" is hazardous — Egypt's documentary richness is
  itself exceptional, and its institutions partly idiosyncratic.
