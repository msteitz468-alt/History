# Jewish Philosophy in the Islamic World

The tradition of medieval Jewish philosophy that unfolded, between roughly the ninth and fifteenth
centuries, **almost entirely within the lands of Islam** — and is therefore inseparable from the
intellectual history of the [[islamicate|Islamicate]] world. Jewish thinkers wrote mostly in
**Judeo-Arabic**, drew on the same Greek-derived [[islamic-philosophy|falsafah]] and [[kalam|kalam]]
that Muslim thinkers used, and responded to the same problems: the relation of reason to revelation,
proofs of God, the nature of the soul, and the interpretation of scripture.

As Adamson stresses, one cannot understand [[saadia-gaon|Saadia Gaon]] without early Islamic theology,
or [[maimonides|Maimonides]] — the tradition's towering figure — without [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]] and
[[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]; Maimonides and [[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] were the two greatest philosophers of
twelfth-century [[andalusi-philosophy|Andalusia]]. The tradition runs from Saadia and
[[ibn-gabirol|Ibn Gabirol]] through Maimonides to the later [[gersonides|Gersonides]] and
[[hasdai-crescas|Crescas]], alongside the rise of [[kabbalah|Kabbalah]] — and continues, after the
medieval period, into the Renaissance and Spinoza.
