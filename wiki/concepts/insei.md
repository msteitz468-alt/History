## Definition and Scope

**Insei** (院政, "cloister government") denotes the political system operative in Japan from approximately 1086 to 1185 — and persisting in modified form through the Kamakura period — in which abdicated (*jōkō*) or tonsured (*hō*) emperors dominated the Heian court from their private residences (*in*). The term derives from the fact that such emperors conducted politics (*sei*) from their villas or religious cloisters (*in*). Historians call the period 1086–1185 the "Insei period," though the system's roots extend back to the early Heian period and its consequences reached into the Kamakura era.

The CHJ definition: "a political situation in which the retired sovereign, operating as the head of the imperial kin group and 'borrowing' the legitimacy of the titular sovereign, served as the de facto arbiter of political and economic interests of the court" (CHJ, ch. 9).

## Background: Abdication, Regency, and the Japanese Throne (645–1068)

Abdication in Japan was not a late Heian innovation. The first recorded abdication was Empress Kōgyoku's in 645. From 645 to 1073 (Go-Sanjō's abdication), twenty-three of thirty-four or thirty-five sovereigns abdicated. Abdication became standard because "experience proved it the least problematic way of deciding royal succession" — avoiding the armed struggles that had marked Asuka and Nara-period succession contests.

The deep structural context for insei:
1. **The emperor's sacerdotal rather than political primary function**: The emperor was the repository of dynastic authority but normally remained outside the daily scramble for power. Power was routinely delegated — to Fujiwara regents, ex-emperors, or later shoguns.
2. **Separation of authority and power**: "Competition for power was confined to the right to control royal authority rather than to usurp it. This provided relative security for the dynasty and the possibility for other groups to enjoy political hegemony" (CHJ, ch. 9).
3. **Two forms of regency competed in early and mid-Heian**: The Fujiwara sekkanke exercised regency through the maternal line (their daughters as imperial mothers); ex-emperors exercised a paternal regency as head of the imperial kin group. Fujiwara dominance was inherently fragile — it depended on producing daughters who bore imperial sons.

## Go-Sanjō and the Prelude to Insei (1068–1073)

Emperor **Go-Sanjō** (1034–73, r. 1068–72) was the first emperor in 170 years whose mother was not a Fujiwara — his mother was Princess Teishi, daughter of Emperor Sanjō. His accession threatened Fujiwara dominance. Regent Yorimichi tried but failed to prevent it; Go-Sanjō reigned actively with non-sekkanke advisers ("the former three fusas": Oe no Masafusa, Fujiwara no Tamefusa, Fujiwara no Korefusa).

Go-Sanjō's major policy was **estate regulation**: the Enkyū shōen regulation ordinance of 1069, enforced by a new **Records Office** (*Kirokusho*) that examined title documents for all estates established after 1045 and confiscated those with improper documentation or that impeded provincial administration. Approximately 30 percent of estates examined were confiscated. He also issued standardization decrees for the official dry measure (*masu*) and market regulations.

Go-Sanjō abdicated in early 1073, appointing his eldest son Sadahito (Emperor Shirakawa) while simultaneously naming a Minamoto-line infant (Prince Sanehito, born of consort Motoko) as crown prince — deliberately bypassing future Fujiwara maternal connections. "Emperor Go-Sanjō abdicated in order to guarantee the future direction of imperial succession and to protect the imperial house from a possible resurgence of Fujiwara regency power" (CHJ, ch. 9). He died soon after abdication, so the full insei system did not materialize under him.

## Shirakawa and the Normalization of Insei (1073–1129)

**Shirakawa** (1053–1129, r. 1073–87) reigned for fourteen years in the manner of Go-Sanjō, exercising direct personal rule. His abdication in 1086 was motivated not by a desire to create a new system but by a succession maneuver: he moved his own young son Taruhito (Emperor Horikawa) ahead of his Minamoto half-brothers Sanehito (who had died in 1085) and Sukehito, whom Go-Sanjō had designated heirs.

During Emperor Horikawa's reign (1087–1107), Shirakawa did not dominate politics; the evidence (surviving documents show eleven imperial edicts from Horikawa, only one from Shirakawa) indicates that Horikawa exercised genuine authority. It was only after Horikawa died in 1107, leaving the four-year-old Toba on the throne with no designated crown prince, that Shirakawa "moved forcefully into politics" as retired emperor.

From roughly 1107 to his death in 1129, Shirakawa was the undisputed political master of the realm. Contemporary diarist Munetada wrote: "the grandeur of the abdicated sovereign is equal to that of His Majesty, and at the present moment this abdicated sovereign is sole political master." The Heike monogatari would later say the only things outside Shirakawa's control were the Kamo River floods, sugoroku dice, and the unruly Mount Hiei monks.

### Institutional Apparatus: The In-no-Chō

Shirakawa's political power rested on a private bureaucratic organization:
- **In-no-chō** (ex-sovereign's office): household office, attendants' office, servants' office, kitchen, workshop, stable, and several warrior offices
- **In-no-hokumen** (warriors of the northern quarter): Shirakawa's own innovation — a personally recruited elite guard composed of small-scale warrior-landholders and middle-ranking provincial officials
- **Kinshin** (retainers): primarily *zuryo* (provincial governors) who supported Shirakawa through "contributions" (*jōgo* — institutionalized payments rewarded with appointments). Five of the seven notable innovations of Shirakawa's era identified by Munetada concerned zuryo

The in-no-hokumen and the zuryo network gave Shirakawa economic leverage (confiscated and newly established imperial *shoen*) and coercive force (to control armed monks and enforce estate regulation).

## Toba's Hegemony (1129–1156)

**Toba** (1103–56, r. 1107–23) inherited insei rule from Shirakawa in 1129, but their relationship had been antagonistic: Toba was widely believed to not be Shirakawa's biological grandson through Emperor Horikawa, but rather Shirakawa's own son (court gossip recorded that Toba called Emperor Sutoku his "uncle," *ojigo*). During the sixteen years of his reign under Shirakawa's insei, Toba had languished with no political power.

Toba's insei differed from Shirakawa's in three key ways:
1. **Retainers**: He initially dismissed Shirakawa's loyalists, building his own client base; the importance of zuryo diminished as the *chigyokoku* (proprietary province) system developed
2. **Shōen**: Where Shirakawa had relied on court grants and contributions, Toba "wholeheartedly backed acquisition" of new private estates. His period as retired emperor "represented a high point in the process of shōen development." The Noto Province land register of 1221 shows that more than 70 percent of cultivated lands were in shōen, and three-quarters of that was established in Toba's time
3. **Sekkanke relations**: Toba brought the Fujiwara main line back into the political process, but in a clientized rather than dominant role. Regent Tadazane returned to court; the Fujiwara became retainers rather than autonomous political actors

Toba's personal relationships generated the factional rivalries that exploded at his death: his antagonism toward Sutoku (whose paternity he doubted), the split between Fujiwara brothers Tadamichi and Yorinaga, and the Minamoto warrior-league division between Yoshitomo and Tameyoshi — all converged in the **Hōgen Disturbance** of 1156.

## Go-Shirakawa and the Taira (1156–1192)

**Go-Shirakawa** (1127–92, r. 1155–58) came to the throne unexpectedly — his father Toba had not thought him qualified to rule — through a complex factional alignment involving Bifukumon-in, Regent Tadamichi, and his own son Morihito's accession prospects. He abdicated in 1158 in favor of Emperor Nijō, fully expecting to exercise dominant insei power as father of the emperor. But Emperor Nijō (r. 1158–65) was mature enough to resist insei control, and Go-Shirakawa's key political supporter Shinzei was killed in the **Heiji Disturbance** of 1159.

Go-Shirakawa's insei was therefore "less dictatorial and less powerful than those of Shirakawa and Toba" for most of its duration. The key complicating factor was **Taira no Kiyomori** (1118–81): each used the other to further their own goals, cooperating until Kiyomori became powerful enough to dominate his former patron. In 1179, Kiyomori staged a coup — dismissing thirty-nine courtiers and placing Go-Shirakawa under house arrest — and forced Takakura's abdication, enthroning the infant Antoku (Kiyomori's grandson).

Go-Shirakawa reached the height of his power only after Kiyomori's death in 1181 and the Taira's flight from the capital in 1183, when he enthroned his grandson Go-Toba and directed negotiations between competing Genji factions and the remnant Taira. "One can give credit to Go-Shirakawa for helping to effect a courtier-warrior union that would persist through most of the Kamakura period, until the warriors wrested all power from the court" (CHJ, ch. 9).

## Institutional Features: A Summary

| Feature | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Source of authority | Imperial kin-group headship; paternal/familial authority over reigning emperor |
| Formal instruments | *Inzen* (decrees), *kudashibumi* (orders), *chō* (communiqués) from the in-no-chō |
| Economic base | Imperial *shōen* (accumulated under all three insei rulers), *chigyokoku* (proprietary provinces), zuryo "contributions" |
| Military arm | In-no-hokumen (standing guard); warrior clients (Ise Heishi/Taira, Minamoto) |
| Court leverage | Control over appointments (*kinshin-zuryo* network); control over noble council membership |
| Ritual legitimation | Extensive temple-building and shrine patronage; use of multiplex system |

## Structural Limits

The insei system worked best when the reigning emperor was a minor. When a mature emperor existed (Horikawa, Nijō), insei dominance was substantially curtailed. The system thus "tended to perpetuate the tradition of enthroning children" — an institutionalized pressure toward infant succession.

The insei was also subject to manipulation: as the case of Go-Shirakawa shows, the bifurcation of authority between reigning emperor and retired emperor created new vectors for factional competition that ultimately enabled the Taira and then the Minamoto to insert military power into the political center.

## Historiography

The name "insei" and the notion that Go-Sanjō consciously designed the system were first proposed by the medieval Buddhist historian Jien (writing in the thirteenth century). Modern scholarship (Hurst, *Insei*, 1976) has rejected the claim of deliberate institutional design: "A consciously worked out, elaborate, institutional scheme probably never occurred to Go-Sanjō" (CHJ, ch. 9). Each abdication was motivated primarily by succession strategy rather than by programmatic intent.

Kitabatake Chikafusa's claim (c. 1339) that from Shirakawa's time ex-sovereign documents took precedence over imperial edicts is not supported by the documentary record: extant edicts from reigning emperors Horikawa and Toba outnumber retired-emperor documents significantly.
