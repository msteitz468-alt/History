## Definition and Scope

African visual culture is an etic analytical category encompassing the full range of visual practices and their surrounding systems of meaning in African societies: masquerades, shrine sculpture, studio painting, photography, popular art, fashion, architecture, and print and electronic media imagery. The term deliberately supersedes the narrower category of "African art," which had historically been restricted to sculpture and material objects suitable for European museum collections.

As Sidney Kasfir argues in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]], "African visual culture encompasses not only these aesthetic practices but also the visual environment reproduced in print and electronic media." This broader definition admits genres that were systematically excluded from earlier collecting and scholarly attention — bark and leaf masquerades, photography, popular painting — and takes as its subject not the decontextualized object but the performative, social, and commercial contexts in which visual forms are produced and consumed.

## Historical Development as a Scholarly Field

**Phase 1 — Colonial collecting (c.1860–1930)**: The study of African visual objects began with nineteenth-century explorers and colonial administrators who collected for European natural history museums and, after 1900, for the emerging market for modern art. Collectors including Leo Frobenius, Emil Torday, and Alfred Mansfeld sought "authentic" precontact objects. The Paris art world — driven by Picasso, Braque, dealers Kahnweiler and Paul Guillaume, and critics Carl Einstein and Vladimir Markov — valued African sculpture precisely for its difference from academic European art. The concept of the "fetish" mediated African material objects for European audiences.

Much of the early colonial collecting was inseparable from conquest: the British punitive expedition against Benin (1897) scattered hundreds of royal brass heads and plaques to European auction houses; French conquest of Dahomey produced loot that reached the Louvre; the Belgian Congo's Musée de Tervuren accumulated massive holdings. "It is possible to read whole histories of colonialism in European and sometimes American museum collections."

**Phase 2 — Museum ethnology and style geography (1900–1960)**: Scholars in Europe used colonial collections to map style-area geographies of African art without fieldwork. Melville Herskovits and Eckart von Sydow established the "culture area" framework; Carl Kjersmeier identified "style centres" of African sculpture supposedly corresponding to tribal boundaries. The British Museum curator William Fagg promoted a "one-tribe–one-style" model. This framework was ahistorical, treating African art as timeless and failing to account for migration, warfare, long-distance trade, and innovation.

**Phase 3 — Fieldwork and masquerade studies (1960–1990)**: Anthropologists and art historians conducted ethnically based case studies. Masquerades became the central research subject — linking performance, ritual, history, and social theory. Robert Farris Thompson's African Art in Motion argued that masks were efficacious only in performance, not as static objects. Feminist approaches reinterpreted the social asymmetries of masking. A 1984 methodological survey by Jan Vansina exposed shortcomings in the separation of art history from oral history. The "salvage anthropology" paradigm assumed African visual cultures were disappearing.

**Phase 4 — Urban genres and postcolonial critique (1990–present)**: The serious study of urban popular painting, studio photography, and contemporary art displaced the earlier focus on village- and court-based art forms. Paradigm-shifting exhibitions — Africa Explores (1991), The Short Century (2001), Documenta 11 (2002) — moved contemporary African art onto the global art world's agenda. The discourse of "postcoloniality" (drawing on Fanon, Bhabha, Spivak) was incorporated into visual art criticism. The conceptual apparatus of "modern," "contemporary," and "postcolonial" remains contested and temporally fluid.

## Major Aesthetic Domains

**Masquerades**: The pre-eminent expressive form linking aesthetics, ritual, and social life across West and Central Africa. Masquerades range from acoustic "invisible" spirit presences (the oldest form) through textile masquerades (developed after the tenth century CE with the trans-Saharan introduction of the double-heddle loom) to sculptural wooden masks. The Egungun ancestral masquerade among the Oyo Yoruba traced to adoption from Nupe; wooden warrior masks in the Cross River–Benue region developed from earlier dances with human trophy skulls after British conquest outlawed headhunting c.1910. Masquerades have followed urbanization — producing commoditization, new urban violence by politically weaponized masquerades, and continuing creative innovation. The Sande society in Sierra Leone and Liberia remains the primary exception to male ownership of masquerades.

**Photography**: Studio photography emerged in West and East African coastal cities by the late nineteenth century, serving commercial elites, traditional title-holders, and colonial administrators. Seydou Keita's Bamako studio portraits from the 1950s and Malick Sidibé's nightclub and outdoor photography from the 1960s–70s are paradigmatic of the form's dual role as fine art and social document. Photography is "the most egalitarian of visual media, and the primary one through which African modernism is felt and propagated." The photojournalism magazine Drum (founded 1951, South Africa) documented African urban modernity under Apartheid with continent-wide editions.

**Popular painting**: Urban studio and street painting emerged most powerfully in Congolese cities (Kinshasa, Lubumbashi) from the 1970s. Painters Moke and Chéri Samba used moral allegory, historical narrative, and social satire on flour sacking for ordinary workers. "Colonie Belge" history paintings by Tchibumba Kanda Matulu recollected the violence of Belgian colonialism. These forms collapsed distinctions between "high" and "low" art. Since the 1990s, poverty and the dominance of photography have largely ended local patronage for these paintings.

**Fine art and institutional contexts**: Academic fine art programmes in colonial Nigeria (Zaria, later the Nsukka school) produced artists who attempted to synthesize Western modernist media with indigenous forms — the "Natural Synthesis" programme of Uche Okeke, drawing on Igbo uli body decoration and nsibidi script. Senegal under Léopold Senghor (1960–80) deployed state patronage for négritude-inspired visual arts through the École Nationale des Beaux-Arts and large-scale tapestry commissions. International biennales — Dak'Art (Dakar, founded 1990), Johannesburg (1995, 1997), Angola's Trienal de Luanda — institutionalized contemporary African art globally.

## Contested Concepts

**Authenticity**: Colonial and early modernist discourse sought "authentic" precontact objects and treated any evidence of intercultural exchange as contamination. Contemporary scholarship treats hybridity and exchange as the historical norm. The Kuba royal ndop figures changed form immediately upon becoming objects of European collecting (1913). "Pure" African art was always a projection of European primitivism.

**Modernism / postcolonialism / contemporary**: Three overlapping and contested descriptors. "Modern" refers to an aesthetic disposition accompanying modernity, with multiple non-Western modernisms. "Postcolonial" signals principled opposition to colonial ideological legacies, but postcolonial consciousness is beginning to blur as colonial memory recedes. "Contemporary" is a moving temporal marker, now being revised by younger scholars to refer to the period from c.1980. "Non-elite artists continue to produce such expressive forms as masks, a kind of contemporary art couched within a traditional genre."

**Tribal style**: The "one-tribe–one-style" paradigm was institutionally powerful but historically wrong. Populations migrated, traded objects, fled warfare (the Fulani jihads of the 1800s scattered shrine sculpture across the Middle Belt of Nigeria), and adapted styles from neighbours. "Open frontiers" was the actual historical condition of African artistic geography.

## Limitations and Critiques

African visual culture as a field has been criticised for: (1) over-representing sub-Saharan Africa and urban contexts while under-representing North African visual traditions; (2) disproportionate attention to the small cadre of diaspora artists visible to Western art world institutions; (3) the exhibition catalogue format, which produces evocative phrase-making rather than sustained analytical argument; (4) the risk that "a globalizing steam-roller...flattens out distinctions among different visual cultures."

The field also inherits its source biases from European collecting: wooden objects are overrepresented; masks made from bark, leaves, raffia, and cloth were routinely left behind by nineteenth-century collectors; acoustic masquerades leave no museum record at all.
