# Carthaginian (Punic) Religion

## Definition and Scope

The religion of [[carthage|Carthage]] and the western Phoenician ("Punic") world was a
distinct development out of the Levantine [[phoenicians|Canaanite]] tradition. It
retained deep Tyrian roots — children were still named almost exclusively from the
Phoenician divine onomasticon (*Hannibal* = "Grace of Baal"; *Bodaštart* = "In the Hands
of Astarte") — yet reorganised its pantheon around new chief deities, evolving a
recognisably Carthaginian system that persisted under Roman rule into the 4th century CE.
Per [[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010|Miles]], religious ritual "lay at the heart
of Carthage's developing identity," not least as a tool of **elite political control**:
temples were the city's wealthiest institutions, run by elite chief-priesthoods, issuing
tariff lists that fixed sacrifice prices (with fines on cheating priests) and hosting
ritual dining clubs.

## The Pantheon

| Deity | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Baal Hammon** | Chief god of Carthage | "Lord"; *Hammon* perhaps "Lord of the Furnaces" (root *ḥmm*, "to burn"); symbol the crescent moon |
| **Tanit** | Consort, patroness/protector of the city | "Face of Baal," but **not junior** to Baal Hammon — a major promotion from a minor Phoenician deity; her stylised "sign of Tanit" pervades the steles |
| **Melqart** (see below) | Senior god, patron of Tyre/Gades | Never held top rank at Carthage but kept his own temple and *egersis*-practising priests; basis of [[heracles-melqart-syncretism]] |
| **Astarte** | Goddess of love/war | Melqart's celestial consort; great sanctuary at Eryx (Sicily) |
| **Eshmoun, Reshef, Baal Saphon, Hadad** | Lesser deities | The 215 BCE treaty with Philip V (preserved by [[polybius|Polybius]]) lists the gods in three triads, headed by Baal Hammon–Tanit–Reshef |
| **Sid** | Levantine god → divine protector of Sardinia | Paired as "son" of Melqart; temple at Antas fused him with a Nuragic deity to legitimise Punic colonisation |
| **Demeter and Core (Kore)** | Adopted Greek cult, officially from 396 BCE | After plague struck Himilco's army following the sack of their Syracuse temple; Core became ubiquitous on Carthaginian coinage. Miles argues it entered via Punic Sicily already in syncretic form |

### Melqart and the *egersis*

**Melqart** ("King of the City") was elevated at Tyre by King Hiram (10th c. BCE) as the
royal patron god — Miles reads this as a "religious putsch" to curb temple power, the king
becoming "the living embodiment of the great Melqart." The central rite was the annual
spring ***egersis*** ("awakening"): an effigy of the god was burned on a raft drifting out
to sea (fire as death-and-rebirth), foreigners were expelled, and a sacred marriage of
king and consort as Melqart and Astarte followed. Carthage maintained a Tyrian connection
by sending an annual flotilla with a **tithe (one-tenth) of its earnings** to Melqart at
Tyre. See [[heracles-melqart-syncretism]].

## Ritual and the Afterlife

- **Temple practice (Gades model, conservative Phoenician form):** no statues or images of
  the gods; perpetual sacred altar fires; barefoot, celibate, shaven-headed priests in
  linen; women and swine barred from the inner sanctum.
- **Purification:** washing was a ritual act — the Punic town of Kerkouane (Cap Bon) is
  notable for numerous built-in bathrooms.
- **Burial:** the dead were washed, anointed, made up, and given grave goods (tools, food,
  amulets); the afterlife was imagined as resembling earthly life. The soul split in two:
  the *néphesh* (physical, remaining in the tomb) and the *rouaḥ* (spiritual, departing to
  the dead).
- **Crisis cult:** in great emergencies Carthaginians offered extraordinary sacrifices —
  renewed tithes to Melqart, new fortified temples to Tanit and Astarte, and, most
  notoriously, the ***molk* child sacrifice** of the [[carthaginian-child-sacrifice|tophet]].

## Persistence under Rome

Punic religion long outlived the city's 146 BCE destruction. Astarte, Baal Hammon, and
Tanit (often Latinised) continued to be worshipped, *suffetes* held office into at least
the 2nd c. CE, the tophet rite continued with **lambs substituted for children**, and
neo-Punic was spoken into the 4th c. CE. North African elites asserted Punic heritage
*through* Roman membership (e.g. Hannibal Tapapius Rufus of Leptis Magna, priest of the
cult of Augustus, "Lover of Concord").

## Historiographical Caution

Almost all written testimony is external and hostile (Greek and Roman). The lurid set
pieces — child sacrifice above all — come through writers such as **Timaeus**, Diodorus,
Cleitarchus, and Plutarch, whose anti-Punic agenda Miles stresses must be read critically
even where (as he concludes for the tophet) a hostile claim turns out to be largely true.
Material culture (steles, urns, inscriptions, onomastics) is the corrective.
