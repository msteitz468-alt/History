# Territorial State

One of the two basic forms of political organization identified by Bruce G. Trigger (2003) in his comparative study of early civilizations (the other being the city-state).

**Definition (Trigger):** Large, centrally administered polities that directly control a substantial territory containing multiple cities, towns, and rural districts. Power is typically concentrated in a single capital or royal court. The ruler often claims divine or semi-divine status and exercises authority through a bureaucratic or administrative hierarchy that reaches into the provinces.

**Characteristics in early civilizations (from Trigger 2003):**
- Larger total territory and population; multiple levels of provincial/local centers.
- More hierarchical or delegational administration (bureaucratic rotation in Egypt; delegational lineages in Shang; nested hierarchy with rotation/inspectors in Inka).
- Farmers dispersed in villages near fields (central state provided security; less clustering incentive).
- Stronger central/government control of foreign trade, mining/quarrying, exotics, and elite craft production (two-tier economy: local utilitarian vs. state luxury).
- Kingship claims of divinity or semi-divinity often stronger (remote rulers easier to sacralize).
- Greater stylistic uniformity in elite art/architecture promoted by centralized patronage.
- Multiple or shifting capitals common; peripatetic kings for control and ritual.

**Examples from Trigger's seven cases:**
- Old Kingdom and later Egypt (territorial)
- Shang and later Chinese states (territorial)
- Inka Empire (territorial)
- Some phases of Mesopotamian history (e.g., Akkadian, Ur III, Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian)

## China-specific mechanism (clanship to territorial state) from Maisels (1999) Range 05

The Chinese territorial state (guo) emerged via conical clanship and hierarchical segmentation rather than managerial bureaucracy alone. Longshan Chalcolithic burials (e.g. Chengzi) already show four-class stratification within lineage clusters. "the lineage hierarchy was coupled with the hierarchy of the settlements."

"Clans and [their component] lineages... provided a genealogical basis for the differentiation of their members into political and economic classes through the mechanism of hierarchical segmentation. Within each clan there were major and minor lineages determined by genealogical distance."

Apical (senior) lineages gained monopoly via ancestor mediation: "Those deemed to be nearest to the founding ancestor of the clan are the most senior and politically powerful. They control the most important fecundity rituals because they are invested with potency by dint of proximity to the ancestors who are the locus of crucial power, mediating as they do between divinities and men."

State formation threshold: "the process of state-formation thus takes place when the apical lineages (at the top of the cones) no longer mix and marry with their clansmen. Instead they interact with other elevated lineages from other clans..."

Zhou operationalized this as zongfa / fengjian enfeoffment: "The branching lineage system (zā ngfā) provided the perfect framework for feudal land-holding, since each state-forming bequest (fengjian) was in exchange for personal loyalty to the king." "Clans could be assigned territory many hundreds of kilometres away to serve strategic purposes of the Zhou state, by forming new reliable states in sensitive areas."

Resulting polity: "A state, guo, consisted of a network of towns, yi, under a royal capital, du." "The lineage hierarchy would, normatively, coincide with the hierarchy of walled towns." Early "states" numbered in the thousands (c. 2000 BC); reduced over time by absorption/war. Explicitly distinguished from city-state: "neither socially nor economically, militarily nor in terms of settlement structure is this a useful description [city-state]. They began as small territorial states..."

Distinctions reinforced: guo-ren (enfranchised "state people" of the capital) vs ye-ren ("field people"). See also [[actors/shang-dynasty]], [[places/anyang]].

**Contrast with City-State (Trigger 2003):**
City-states are smaller, more autonomous urban polities, often in competition or loose confederation (peer-polity interaction, hegemonic tribute systems that usually left local rulers in place), with power more distributed among elite groups/lineages/councils in addition to kings. Higher proportion of population in urban centers (farmers often resided in cities for protection); more market-oriented exchange and broader access to exotic goods in some cases. See [[concepts/city-state]].

Trigger argues that these two forms were stable alternatives rather than sequential stages; each had its own functional coherence and influenced settlement patterns, economic behaviour, class relations, administration style, and even the degree of ruler sacralization in characteristic ways. Only one type tended to dominate in any given region at the early civilization stage.

## Key Findings from Trigger
- Territorial states enabled massive scale in art/architecture and centralized economic control but featured greater rural continuity and sharper elite/commoner lifestyle gaps.
- Administration often mixed delegational (local elites with substantial autonomy) and bureaucratic elements; kings traveled to maintain oversight.
- No necessary correlation with writing or specific ecology; functional needs of scale explain hierarchy levels.

## Historiography

The distinction has roots in earlier work (e.g., on Greek poleis vs. empires) but Trigger's systematic cross-cultural application to seven unrelated cases makes it a key analytical tool. It challenges purely evolutionary sequences that assume all states pass through the same stages and highlights how political form shapes other aspects of society.
