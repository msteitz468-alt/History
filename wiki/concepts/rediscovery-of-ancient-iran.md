# The Rediscovery of Ancient Iran

## Definition and Scope

This page traces how the **pre-Islamic Iranian past was forgotten and then recovered** —
the historiographical "afterlife" that closes [[sources/wiesehofer-ancient-persia-2001|
Wiesehöfer]]'s *Ancient Persia*. It is the story of why almost everything in this wiki's
[[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid]], [[parthian-empire|Parthian]], and
[[sasanian-empire|Sasanian]] pages had to be **reconstructed by modern scholarship**, and
of the discipline (Iranian/Achaemenid Studies) that did the reconstructing.

## The loss of native memory

By late antiquity Iranians themselves had **lost the Achaemenids**. The
[[sasanian-empire|Sasanian]] "national history" (see [[eranshahr]]) remembered only the
mythical **Kayanian** kings; [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]] and his line "were rediscovered only
in our period and had to serve as questionable 'ancestors' of rulers in search of
legitimacy." Locals called [[persepolis|Persepolis]] *Takht-i Jamshid* ("Throne of
Jamshid") after a legendary king; when the traveller Heinrich von Poser visited in 1644,
"the natives told him that it had been built by King Jamshid." The Sasanian reliefs at
Naqsh-i Rustam were guessed to be "Rustamic," and **"until the nineteenth century no one
connected the ruins of Pasargadae with Cyrus II."**

## European memory before the modern era

Medieval and Renaissance Europe knew ancient Iran only through the **Bible** (the
four-kingdoms scheme of the Book of Daniel) and classical authors, collapsing time so
thoroughly that Cyrus appears as an armoured knight beside a cannon in the Lübeck world
chronicle. "The exclusive assessment of ancient Iran from the outside was inevitable up to
the nineteenth century."

## Early modern travellers

- **Odoric of Pordenone** (c. 1320) gives the earliest European notice of the Persepolis
  ruins ("stately palaces altogether destitute of inhabitants") without identifying them.
- **Josafa Barbaro** (Venetian envoy) wrote the first detailed description of Persepolis,
  Naqsh-i Rustam, and Cyrus's tomb — but "did not connect them with the Achaemenid kings,
  but with biblical personalities."
- **Don García de Silva Figueroa** first identified *Chihil Minar* as Persepolis (Diodorus
  in hand) and gave the first description of cuneiform signs.
- **Pietro della Valle** (1621) made "the first copy of a cuneiform text" (five signs) and
  was "the first to hit upon the correct assumption that the script ran from left to right."
- Later drawings came from Herbert, Chardin, Kaempfer, and de Bruijn — though most
  travellers "found in Persepolis what they wanted to find there."
- **Carsten Niebuhr**, sole survivor of the Danish Arabia expedition, reached Persepolis in
  1765 and made the **first reliable copies of all the inscriptions** — distinguishing the
  three scripts and noting each text appeared once per script. His copies "formed the
  groundwork for deciphering all the cuneiform writing systems"; he went blind in old age
  from the glare off the marble.

## Anquetil-Duperron and the Avesta

In **1762** **Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron** returned to Paris with Avesta
manuscripts from India; his translation of the Zoroastrian scripture "lent the study of
Iranian languages an enormous impetus" — and supplied the Avestan vocabulary later
decipherers needed.

## The decipherment of Old Persian cuneiform

- Precursors **Tychsen** (1798) and **Münter** (1800) identified the word-divider and
  argued the inscriptions must be Achaemenid, akin to the language of the Avesta.
- **Georg Friedrich Grotefend** (1802), a Göttingen philologist, made the breakthrough on
  Niebuhr's inscriptions **B and G** (now **DPa** of [[darius-i|Darius I]] and **XPe** of
  Xerxes). He spotted a recurring word, identified it (via classical sources and de Sacy's
  Sasanian readings) as "king of kings," and inferred the formula *"X, great king, king of
  kings, …, Y's son."* Finding that the opening name of one inscription reappeared in the
  genitive in the other, he reasoned the relationship **father → son** and matched it to
  the only fitting sequence — **Hystaspes (no title) → Darius → Xerxes** — reading several
  signs correctly and borrowing the word for "king" from Anquetil-Duperron's Avestan.
- **Rask**, **Lassen**, and **Burnouf** then applied comparative Indo-Iranian linguistics
  (Sanskrit/Avestan) to fix the remaining signs.
- **Henry Creswicke Rawlinson**, a British officer, copied Darius's giant *res gestae* at
  **[[behistun-inscription|Bisutun]]** at great personal risk (1835–1845), publishing the
  Old Persian (1846/47) and Babylonian (1851) versions; the ancestral genealogy there
  confirmed further sign-values. The Irish parson **Edward Hincks** independently clarified
  the vowel system. A later "absurd and undignified dispute" over priority (Budge crediting
  Rawlinson, Meissner crediting Grotefend) is, Wiesehöfer notes, ill-founded on both sides.

## The birth of Iranian Studies

"The disclosure of the Avesta, the reading of the Middle Persian (and Parthian) versions
of the Sasanian inscriptions and the decipherment of Old Persian cuneiform script marked
the beginnings of the history of 'Iranian Studies' in Europe." Landmark reference works
followed — Bartholomae's *Altiranisches Wörterbuch* (1904), Geiger & Kuhn's *Grundriß*, the
ongoing *Corpus Inscriptionum Iranicarum* — and new finds (Turfan, fresh excavations) keep
renewing the field.

## Limitations and critiques

The narrative is a reminder that the wiki's pre-Islamic Iran rests on a **thin and
relatively recent evidentiary base**, recovered against the grain of both lost native
memory and a hostile/biblical European tradition — a methodological caution that belongs on
every Iranian page.
