# Chanson de Geste and the Rise of Vernacular Literature

## Definition

The ***chanson de geste*** ("song of deeds") is the Old French heroic epic of the
eleventh and twelfth centuries — the most famous being the **_Chanson de Roland_** —
and stands here for the wider rise of **vernacular literature** that
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Jan Ziolkowski, Ch. 18, "Latin and vernacular
literature") treats as one of the defining cultural developments of the period.

## The Latin–Vernacular Paradox

NCMH IV frames the period's literature as an "apparent paradox": **Latin enjoyed a
golden age** — the common language of the educated clergy, reinforcing the cultural and
ecclesiastical unity of Europe and carrying the [[twelfth-century-renaissance|learned
revival]] — while at the same time **the vernaculars grew strong enough to support
creative writing**. As in so many fields, the period opened up the possibility of
**choice**: literate people could now write in the vernacular as well as in Latin.

## The Vernacular Genres

- **The *chansons de geste*** — heroic epics of war and loyalty (the *Chanson de
  Roland*, c. 1100), celebrating the world of the warrior aristocracy. NCMH IV notes
  that, though now written down, they "came to typify a world that was being lost."
- **Troubadour and trouvère lyric** — the courtly love poetry of the langue d'oc and
  langue d'oïl, which transformed the literary representation of love.
- **The romance** — the new genre of [[chivalry|chivalric]] adventure, above all the
  Arthurian and other romances of **Chrétien de Troyes** (later, in German,
  Wolfram von Eschenbach's *Parzival*). NCMH IV stresses that "the writing of romances
  and the cult of chivalry develop together."

## Significance

The twelfth-century flowering of vernacular literature gave the lay aristocracy a
literature of its own and laid the foundations of the European national literatures.
Together with the contemporary cult of [[chivalry]] and the rise of courtly culture, it
marks the emergence of a self-conscious secular, aristocratic culture alongside the
clerical Latin world.
