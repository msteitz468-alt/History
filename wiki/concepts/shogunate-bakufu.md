# The Shogunate (Bakufu)

The ***bakufu*** ("tent government") — the **military government** through which warrior
houses ruled [[japan|Japan]] for nearly seven centuries (1185–1868), headed by the
***sei-i-tai-shōgun*** ("barbarian-quelling great general"). It is the central institution
of Japanese feudal politics and the supreme example of the Japanese pattern whereby
**real power is exercised in the name of, but apart from, a sacred but powerless throne**.
From Mason & Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]].

## Origin and meaning

The title *sei-i-tai-shōgun* originally designated a temporary general sent to fight the
[[heian-period|Ainu frontier]], who surrendered his sword of office when the campaign
ended. [[minamoto-no-yoritomo|Minamoto no Yoritomo]] transformed it: granted the rank in
**1192**, he held it **for life** and passed it to his heirs, and it came to mean
permanent authority to **govern a country at peace**. The government he built (from his
base at **Kamakura**, not the imperial capital) became the model bakufu.

## Structure

Yoritomo's bakufu grew out of wartime administration:

- the **samurai-dokoro** (board of retainers) — controlling the [[samurai|vassals]];
- the **kumonjo/mandokoro** (administration) and **monchūjo** (lawsuits over land);
- and, decisively, the right (won 1185) to appoint **shugo** (provincial military
  constables) and **jitō** (estate stewards) — recognized imperial offices through which
  Kamakura projected order over the whole realm without abolishing the
  [[heian-period|shōen]] system or the court.

Authority rested on the **lord–vassal bond**: the shogun confirmed or granted his vassals'
rights to estate income (*shiki*), and they owed him loyal military service — the core of
[[samurai|Japanese feudalism]].

## The three shogunates and the pattern of delegated power

Mason and Caiger stress a recurring Japanese feature: institutions are controlled not by
their nominal heads but by those who "serve" them. So the bakufu itself was repeatedly
captured:

- **Kamakura bakufu (1185–1333)** — soon run by the **Hōjō** as *shikken* (regents) for
  figurehead shoguns; ruled by the **Jōei Code (1232)**; repelled the
  [[mongol-invasions-of-japan-1274-1281|Mongols]]; fell to Go-Daigo (1333). See
  [[kamakura-period]].
- **Ashikaga (Muromachi) bakufu (1336–1573)** — weaker, dependent on provincial deputies;
  disintegrated in the Ōnin War and the *sengoku* age. See [[muromachi-period]].
- **Tokugawa bakufu (1603–1868)** — the mature, stable shogunate of the early-modern peace
  (the *baku-han* system). *(Cycle 3.)*

Thus power was often **doubly delegated** — emperor → shogun → regent — yet the
**emperor was never deposed**, only sidelined: the throne's [[shinto|sacred legitimacy]]
made it the indispensable source of the shogun's own authority.

## Significance

The bakufu institutionalized **warrior rule** as the normal government of Japan for ~700
years, fusing military power with a delegated imperial sanction. Its final abolition in the
**Meiji Restoration (1868)** — the "restoration" of direct imperial rule — is the great
hinge into modern Japan (Cycle 4).
