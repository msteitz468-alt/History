# Environmentalism

## Definition

**Environmentalism** is the cultural and political movement built on the premises
that pell-mell environmental change is unwelcome and stability preferable, and that
access to clean air, clean water, and a non-toxic environment is a human right. In
[[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], J. R. McNeill presents modern popular
environmentalism as **a young force — a "Hegelian antithesis"** provoked by the
post-1950 [[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]]: the reaction to tumultuous,
accelerating environmental disruption in the second phase of the
[[anthropocene|Anthropocene]].

## Antecedents and emergence

McNeill's chapter traces deep nineteenth-century roots: the "nuisance" suits by which
city-dwellers tried to stop or win compensation for industrial pollution (they
"lost more than they won" for many decades); conservation movements; and the
filtration and sanitation campaigns that cleaned urban water in rich countries by
~1940. But **organized popular environmentalism as a mass political force dates from
after c. 1965**, when the worst Western urban air and water pollution — photochemical
smog, petrochemicals, persistent organic chemicals — peaked and then, under the
combined pressure of environmentalism, government regulation, and fuel substitution,
began to improve in Western Europe, Japan, and North America.

## A reaction to acceleration

Environmentalism is, in this reading, **inseparable from the Great Acceleration it
opposes.** As fossil-fuel-based [[industrialization]] and mass automobility
multiplied pollutants, and as the rate of species extinction rose to its highest
level in 65 million years, the movement crystallized around the claim that growth
and "progress" are not unambiguous goods. It overlaps the older critique of
technology — Luddism, the "small is beautiful" alternative-technology current
(Schumacher), and decentralized, labor-intensive, energy-efficient designs
(see [[technology-and-innovation]]).

## Uneven geography

The clean-up of the 1960s–70s rich world coincided with the *worsening* of pollution
in rapidly industrializing China, India, and Brazil — where, in the early
twenty-first century, a dozen cities ranked among the world's worst for air quality.
Environmentalism's spread and its political traction remain highly uneven, tracking
the shifting geography of the [[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]] itself.

## Limitations and critiques

- **Class and global inequality:** the movement arose first where industrial
  populations were rich enough to demand amenity, raising questions of environmental
  justice between rich and poor countries.
- **Tension between "stability" and development:** the premise that stability is
  preferable sits awkwardly with the developmental aspirations of industrializing
  societies.
- As an analytic category it risks anachronism if projected onto pre-1965 conservation
  or Romantic nature-feeling.
