# Medieval Warfare

Analytical concept capturing the dominant pattern of Western European warfare c. 1000–1300 (with continuities into the early fourteenth century). The term is etic; contemporaries described specific "wars," "feuds," "hostings," or "chevauchées" without a single overarching doctrinal label.

## Definition and Scope

Medieval warfare in the West was shaped by the intersection of proprietorial social structure, limited governmental capacity, defensive technological bias, and western European geography/climate. It was characterized by:

- Small, ad hoc, retinue-based armies raised for short durations.
- Primacy of raiding (ravaging) and siege over pitched battle.
- Castle as the central instrument of power projection and defence.
- Elite (knightly) dominance of the field through mobility and training, but never in isolation from infantry support.
- Battle as a rare, high-risk, usually consensual event rather than the normal object of campaign.
- Command by persuasion, personal example, and unit banners rather than institutionalized staff systems.
- Slow, uneven, non-linear change driven by social and contingent factors rather than systematic institutional learning.

> "Across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, war did not change radically. Its staple was destructive raiding punctuated with sieges and, more occasionally, by battle." (France, Ch. 12)

## Major Characteristics

**Campaign style (raiding and siege dominant)**: Armies lived off the land; plunder was integral to pay and motivation. Ravaging served to impoverish the enemy, undermine vassal loyalty, and force negotiation. "It is preferable to subdue an enemy by famine, raids and terror, than in battle where fortune tends to have more influence than bravery" (Vegetius, cited Ch. 1 and operationalized throughout). Large-scale sieges and battles were exceptional because both were expensive and risky. Most conflict consisted of small-group confrontations during chevauchée-style operations.

**Rarity and nature of battle**: "In general, it needed both sides to resolve upon battle, because running away was fairly easy." Battle often emerged accidentally or from siege provocation (Hattin, Cortenova 1237 as ambush after prolonged ravaging and castle-taking around Mantua/Brescia). When joined, cavalry nearly always held the initiative through mobility, but massed shock charges were uncommon and difficult to control. (Ch. 12)

**Combined-arms reality and infantry role**: Knights valued for mobility and semi-professional training (from adolescence), not solely as "shock weapons-system." Infantry provided mass, defensive solidity, and missile power; properly formed, foot could repel cavalry (examples across 892–1302 including Hastings, Tinchebrai, Brémule, Northallerton, Legnano, Bouvines, Cortenova, Worringen, Courtrai). Urban infantry showed tactical specialization (Italian city sections; Flemish guild differentiation at Courtrai: missile then pike then goedendag). (Ch. 12)

**Command and unit organization**: Armies drawn up in "battles" (larger tactical groups of retinues). Cavalry sub-units (conroi) fought in close order around banners. Trumpets and personal leadership supplemented. The Templar Rule illustrates ideal: small banner-units, repeated charges, reserves with fresh horses. Control was always fragile due to "individualistic ethos." Decisive factor in victory was almost always the commander's ability to maintain cohesion and commit reserves at the right moment (e.g., Guérin at Bouvines; Simon de Montfort at Muret; contrast Otto IV's loss of control). (Ch. 12–13)

**Limits on tactical evolution**: No linear development from "shock knight" to disciplined infantry or gunpowder. "The thirteenth century was truly the age of cavalry" in the limited sense that cavalry actions dictated events on the battlefield — not that infantry vanished or that supremacy was uncontested. Professionalism increased unevenly, chiefly among cavalry first; "indications of a new coherence... appear unevenly and there is little sense of conscious development." Each army was "the product of particular conditions"; "each victory was sui generis"; lessons (e.g., Edward I) died with the commander because "war was not institutionalized" and there was "no institutional memory." "Victory in battle depended... on the personality of the commander... without a staff, without a logistics service and, above all, without an institutional memory to serve him." (Ch. 13)

**Social and institutional embedding**: War was the province of the proprietorial (landed) elite. Chivalry glorified their monopoly claim. Ransom ethics and restraint applied mainly among elites; losers' infantry were often massacred. Castles and knights were proprietorial instruments. (Ch. 1, extended in 12–13)

## Historiography

Older views (Oman, Delbrück emphasis) overstated knightly shock dominance and posited clean technological evolution (couched lance → longbow). France and source-critical work (Verbruggen) demonstrate diversity of experience, persistent infantry importance, organizational limits on "shock," and contingency over linear progress. Battle-centric (Clausewitzian) assumptions misread the period; "Vegetian" raiding/siege norms better fit the evidence.

**Emic/etic**: Contemporaries experienced this as normal lordship enforcement, feuding, and defense of rights. "Proprietorial warfare" and "medieval warfare" as used here are modern analytical constructs.
