# Vassalage and the Benefice

## Definition

**Vassalage** was the personal bond by which a free man (a *vassus*) commended himself to a lord,
swearing fidelity and owing service — above all **military service** — in return for maintenance,
protection, and often a grant of land called a **benefice** (*beneficium*). In
[[mckitterick-ncmh-v2-1995|NCMH II]] (Goetz, Ch. 17) these "social and military institutions" are
examined as the connective tissue of Carolingian society — and as the historical seed of what later
historians have called **[[feudalism]]**.

## Freedom, Lordship, and the Host

Carolingian society was structured by gradations of **freedom and unfreedom** and by ties of
**lordship** (*Herrschaft*) that cut across them. Goetz stresses that "free" and "unfree" were not
simple binary legal categories but a spectrum, and that personal dependence — commendation to a lord —
was becoming a basic organising principle of social and military life. The **host** (the army) was
raised through a combination of public obligation (all free men owed military service to the king) and
these personal bonds: lords brought their vassals, equipped from their benefices, to the royal campaign.
The fusion of **public duty** and **private dependence** is the institutional core of the chapter.

## Benefice and Honor

The **benefice** — land granted in return for service rather than in full ownership — and the *honor*
(office or grant held from the king) linked the [[carolingian-aristocracy|aristocracy]] to the crown and
their own followers to themselves. Charles Martel's reputed grants of (church) land to his warriors were
once seen as the origin of a systematic "feudal" military tenure; NCMH II (like Fouracre, Ch. 3) is
cautious about this once-canonical thesis, treating benefice and vassalage as evolving practices rather
than a designed system.

## Significance

The vassalage–benefice complex provided early medieval rulers and lords with a means of mobilising armed,
loyal followers in the absence of a tax-funded standing army. Generalised, hereditary, and territorialised
after the Carolingian collapse, these bonds became central to the **[[feudalism|feudal]]** order of the
High Middle Ages — though modern scholarship (Reynolds and others) warns against projecting a tidy,
uniform "feudal system" back onto the varied Carolingian reality.

## Historiography

NCMH II (Goetz) reflects the German social-historical tradition's interest in *Freiheit*, *Herrschaft*, and
*Grundherrschaft*. The whole category of "feudalism" is now contested: Susan Reynolds's *Fiefs and Vassals*
(1994), contemporary with this volume, argued that "vassalage" and "fief" as a coherent system were largely
constructed by later jurists and historians — a debate that bears directly on how the Carolingian evidence
in this chapter should be read.
