# The City as the Unit of Empire (Municipal Self-Government)

How the Roman Empire was actually administered: **not** by a bureaucracy but by the
**self-government of thousands of autonomous cities** (*civitates*, *municipia*, *coloniae*,
*poleis*), each running its own affairs through local magistrates and a council of notables. This
is the view "from below" of the administration analysed from above in
[[roman-provincial-administration]]. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 10 (**Hartmut
Galsterer**), the complement to Eck's Ch. 7.

## Administration on a shoestring

Galsterer's framing premise (after Eck): Rome held the lands "between the Caucasus and the Firth
of Forth" with an **astonishingly small administrative apparatus** — a few hundred senators and
knights, plus imperial slaves/freedmen mostly in the emperor's *personal* service. This worked
because the state's **aims were minimal**: keep the peace (and justice), and collect the taxes.
Economy, welfare, education, and culture were "not targets of state intervention." Everything else
rested on **self-administration** at the level of the province, the judicial district (*conventus*),
and above all **the city**.

## Italy and the provincial frame

**Italy** had paid no direct tax and sent no governors since 42 BCE; Augustus' eleven *regiones*
were mere statistical units. Only under Hadrian and Marcus did ***iuridici*** appear for limited
jurisdiction in four Italian districts — the first sign of the **"provincialization of Italy"**
that the Severan *correctores* would complete. In the provinces the governor's recurring jobs were
the same: keep the peace against ubiquitous **robbers**, ride the *conventus* circuit dispensing
justice, and run the **census** so taxes could be assessed (indirect taxes left to *publicani*,
now better understood from the **Code of the Asian Customs Law**, AD 62). The **imperial cult**
gave the provinces their one representative body — the *concilium* / *koinon* of city delegates,
which elected the provincial priest, held the festival, and could praise or accuse the governor.

## A patchwork of communities

Provinces were a mosaic: autonomous **cities and tribes**, imperial **estates, mines and
quarries**, **military land** (with its *canabae* and *vici*), near-autonomous senatorial ***saltus***
(flourishing in Africa, "private possessions on a level with the territories of cities"), and —
especially in the East — petty **principalities**. Cities ranged from million-strong Rome to
poleis of a few hundred, ranked **not by size but by juridical status**: Roman *municipia* and
*coloniae*, versus *peregrine* communities (tribute-paying *civitates stipendiariae*, *liberae*,
or treaty-bound *foederatae*). Yet in **daily local administration the difference was slight** —
even a free, federate city like Amisus was in practice as supervised as any other. And cities grew
**physically alike** (forum, basilica, temples, baths, theatre) — a shared urban civilization.

## Magistrates, council, and the waning *populus*

Civic government followed the ancient city-state pattern, regulated by a *lex provinciae* and
municipal charters — now illuminated by the nearly complete **Flavian *Lex Irnitana*** from
Baetica. Each city had:

- **Magistrates**, elected annually and collegially: the **duoviri** (jurisdiction, peace, giving
  the year its name), **aediles** (corn supply, markets, weights, buildings, streets), and
  **quaestores** (finance) — the local *cursus honorum*. Their old powers (war, foreign policy)
  were gone; **jurisdiction shrank** to petty civil cases as the streamlined **cognitio** procedure,
  reserved to emperor and governor, drew litigants away from local courts (emperors repeatedly
  trying to **stop the bypassing** of municipal justice).
- The **popular assembly**, by the second century reduced almost everywhere to **electing the
  magistrates** — and even that contested only where (as at Pompeii or in Africa) competition
  survived; the *Lex Irnitana* already notes "waning interest" among the qualified.
- The **council** (*ordo decurionum*, *boule*, *curia*) — increasingly dominant: ex-magistrates
  sitting **for life**, gated by a property qualification and (as at Irni) a prerequisite for
  candidacy, ranked by office and seniority like a miniature Senate. The standard 100 (West) was
  the **curial order**, soon one of the three privileged *ordines* of the empire and the reservoir
  for promotion to the equestrian and senatorial orders.

## Liturgies, euergetism, and the burdens of office

The structural problem of city finance was that **income never met expenditure**. Cities had no
direct taxes of their own (the *tributum* they collected went to Rome); income came from customs,
rents, monopolies, and above all from the rich. **Office and council membership carried a *summa
honoraria*** (entrance fee), and decurions were expected to fund buildings, games, and distributions
— **euergetism** as "the privilege of the rich" (*hoc est habere, hoc est vivere*). Personal and
financial obligations — Greek **leitourgiai**, Latin **munera** — financed the rest. Crucially,
distributions of grain and money went **not to the poor but to those entitled *qua* citizens**:
there was no public education, health service, or poor relief (schools and doctors paid by
clients; pre-Christian poor relief "rather exceptional").

The cities also bore three resented **state burdens**: collective **liability for the tributum**
(loaded onto a *decemprimi* / *eikosiprotoi* elite who covered arrears — the seed of the
third-century curial crisis); the upkeep of the **transport system** (*vehiculatio* / *angareia*),
chronically abused by soldiers and officials; and **road maintenance**. The trend across the
period was toward **stricter outside supervision** of city finances by *curatores rei publicae*
(*logistai*) and the governor.

## How city and centre actually connected

Galsterer stresses (from the *Lex Irnitana*) that **governor, Senate, and emperor barely appear**
in the routine of city government. The links ran instead through **letters and embassies** (after
Millar): cities sent envoys — often professional orators, but mostly local notables — on both
particular business and ceremonial occasions (accession, birthday, a governor's arrival),
returning with confirmed privileges; emperors and patrons replied by letter (the Aphrodisias
"archive wall"). **Patrons** (even small Canusium had 31 senatorial patrons) and *curatores*
helped, but were "not part of the administrative routine." The system depended utterly on the
**decurial ruling class**, whom Rome bound to itself by extending citizenship and absorbing their
best families upward — the mechanism by which provincials became first legionaries, then
equestrians, then (by the Severans) senators.

## Significance

The High Empire's "golden age" (Gibbon, Mommsen) rested on this **delegation of governance to a
self-financing municipal aristocracy** — cheap, stable, and self-perpetuating while the cities
were prosperous and the burdens light. Its latent fragility — collective tax liability and the
ratchet of compulsory liturgies falling on the curial order — would, after the wars, plague, and
economic strain at the end of Marcus' reign, become the **crisis of the city** in the third
century, when honour curdled into hereditary burden.
