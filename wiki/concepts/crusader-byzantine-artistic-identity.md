# Art and Identity in the Frankish Morea

## Definition and Scope

Following the Fourth Crusade (1204), the Frankish Principality of the Morea controlled
most of the Peloponnese until 1430. The Byzantine population continued to inhabit
and build Orthodox churches under Latin political rule. This concept examines how
monumental painting in those Orthodox churches — fresco programs, particularly the
representation of military saints — functioned as a carrier of Greek Orthodox
identity under Frankish occupation, and how the shared visual language of the
equestrian saint became a site of both cultural emulation and identity assertion.
Gerstel argues that the Morea constitutes a laboratory for studying the dynamics of
cultural hybridity and resistance in a Crusader context.

## Key Arguments

**Thesis: Byzantine visual culture served as an identity marker under Latin political
domination.** The fresco programs of Orthodox village churches in the southern Morea
during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries were not simply religious decoration
but vehicles of communal self-definition against a Latin overlord. The choices made
by painters and patrons — which saints to depict, how to depict them, what iconographic
details to include — encoded ethnic, religious, and political allegiance.

**The shift from standing to equestrian military saints.** Before the Fourth Crusade,
Orthodox village churches in the southern Morea represented military saints (George,
Demetrios, Theodore) exclusively in frontal standing pose, following the established
Byzantine format. During the thirteenth century — precisely during the period of
Latin overlordship — these saints mounted horses and began to dominate the interior
lower registers. The transition is documented with precision: in Hagios Strategos at
Ano Boularioi (Mani), an equestrian St. George painted in 1274/75 covers a late
twelfth-century standing saint. More than thirty churches in the southern Morea were
decorated with one or more equestrian military figures between the mid-thirteenth and
the fourteenth centuries.

**Two explanatory arguments (not mutually exclusive):**
- *Indigenous response to danger*: equestrian saints already appeared in border
  churches of the Byzantine empire (Cappadocia, Georgia, Egypt); the new border
  status of the southern Morea under Frankish pressure activated this protective
  tradition. Half of the roughly twenty dated churches of the period were dedicated
  to military saints.
- *Cultural emulation and symbiosis*: the Orthodox population observed the Franks
  closely and absorbed elements of chivalric culture — including the equestrian ideal
  central to Latin self-representation. The Chronicle of the Morea and Byzantine
  vernacular romances of the period show linguistic borrowings (kourtesia, ntama,
  lizios) and literary penetration of knightly motifs.

**The equestrian saint as a hybrid image that crossed cultural barriers.** Both Latin
patrons and Orthodox painters could deploy the same iconographic form — St. George
on horseback — while distinguishing their allegiance through small but legible
details: a red cross on white shield for the Latin version; a crescent-and-star motif
(evoking Alexios I Komnenos's bronze coinage) for the Orthodox version. The Nauplion
gatehouse St. George (painted by a local Orthodox artist for Latin patrons) stands at
the intersection: it shares pose and military vocabulary with contemporaneous Orthodox
church paintings but carries the Crusader badge.

**Frankish-sponsored painting programs.** Two examples:
- The archbishop's hospitium at Patras (recorded 1395): its reception hall walls were
  covered with scenes from the destruction of Troy — a canonical subject of courtly
  Latin culture, also used to justify the conquest of Byzantium (Crusaders as
  descendants of Trojans).
- The Nauplion gatehouse (late thirteenth century, patronized by Isabelle of
  Villehardouin and Florent of Hainault): Latin saints and iconography — the Agnus
  Dei, St. James of Compostela with pilgrim's staff and cockle-shell badge, the
  equestrian St. George with Crusader insignia.

**Artistic details as markers of identity.**
- *Pose*: Orthodox equestrian saints in the Morea extend their legs forward in
  stirrups in the Latin fashion, distinct from the Byzantine frontal standing pose
  but also from earlier eastern equestrian traditions.
- *Heraldry*: Latin knights used heraldic shields as identity markers on seals and in
  battle; Byzantine painters in the Morea began marking their saints' shields with
  insignia — crescent-and-star for Orthodox saints, castle tournois (evoking Frankish
  coinage) for soldiers in narrative scenes identified as Latin.
- *Equipment*: some Morea painters adopted the quiver, saddle type, and mail hauberk
  associated with Crusader military imagery (visible in Sinai icons associated with
  Crusader patronage), sometimes misunderstanding their function.
- *Parade format*: the non-combat "parade" pose — lance held aloft, horse in military
  procession — is common in Western seals and Crusader icon painting; it appears in
  the Morea at Vrontamas and spreads to other small churches.

**The Vrontamas frieze as a unique case.** The Old Monastery at Vrontamas (cave church
near Geraki) presents a painted frieze of six military riders — Saints Menas,
Eustathios, George, Niketas, and two others — divided into two groups facing each
other in mock tournament. The equestrian confrontation recalls the Western joust.
Niketas is rendered in standard Byzantine fashion; George in a flatter, liner style
resembling Crusader icons at Sinai. The frieze demonstrates that Byzantine painters
in the Morea were aware of and selectively absorbed Crusader artistic vocabulary.

## Evidence and Examples

**Specific churches with equestrian saints (selected from Gerstel's appendix of
34 documented cases):**
- St. Mamas, Karavas, Mani (1232): earliest documented equestrian saint in the Morea,
  under Frankish rule.
- Hagios Strategos, Ano Boularioi, Mani (1274/75): the key stratigraphic case where
  an equestrian St. George covers a standing predecessor.
- St. John Chrysostom, Geraki (ca. 1300): equestrian St. George placed directly
  opposite the entrance; north wall juxtaposes the saint's crescent-and-star shield
  against soldiers in the Women at the Tomb scene carrying castle-tournois shields.
- Vrontamas Old Monastery (thirteenth century): the tournament frieze.
- St. Demetrios Krokees (1286): three equestrian saints on the north wall.
- Forty Martyrs, Lakedaemona (1304/5): St. Demetrios in full parade format, painter
  named as Constantine Manasses.
- St. George, Geraki (kastro, fourteenth century): a standing St. George whose shield
  combines the Orthodox crescent-and-star (top) with the checkered coat of arms of
  the Frankish lords of Geraki (bottom) — a visual synthesis of the coexistence.

## Scholarly Significance

Gerstel's essay challenges the prevailing view (documented in her footnotes, drawing
on Mouriki) that Byzantine Orthodox painting in the Frankish period was characterized
simply by the *absence* of Latin influence — a conservative resistance born of dogmatic
anxiety and cultural snobbery. She shows instead a more nuanced dynamic: selective
absorption and transformation of Crusader visual vocabulary in the service of Greek
Orthodox communal identity. The equestrian saint was not simply borrowed from the
Latins but reworked as an assertively Orthodox figure — protective, devotional, locally
specific — that happened to share a pose and military vocabulary with the knightly
ideal of the occupiers.

The essay also demonstrates the Morea's value as an analytical laboratory. Here, unlike
in the Palestinian Crusader states or in Constantinople, the documentary record of
patronage (donor inscriptions, chronicle evidence, numismatic data) is rich enough to
correlate specific iconographic choices with moments of political transition — Frankish
occupation, Byzantine reconquest — allowing the art historian to map cultural identity
with unusual precision.
