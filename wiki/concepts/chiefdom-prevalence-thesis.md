## Definition

The **chiefdom prevalence thesis** holds that middle-range chiefdoms — autonomous societies with permanent, centralized, hereditary political hierarchies — were the *predominant* (not exceptional or residual) form of complex society across South America at the time of European contact, and that they persisted structurally well into the colonial period wherever Spanish indirect rule preserved chiefly offices. The thesis directly contests two earlier assumptions: (1) that chiefdoms were transitional staging-posts on the evolutionary road to states, and (2) that the absence of Andean empires in a region implied the absence of political complexity.

A [[chiefdom]] in this framework is defined as "an autonomous society in which there were permanent, centralized political hierarchies headed by chiefs. The chieftainship was institutionalized and hereditary, with the rule of succession usually specified and restricted to elites" (Villamarín & Villamarín). Chiefdoms occupied a wide range of ecological settings: "highland and lowland basins, marshlands, desert, coast, islands, lagoons, floodplains, and piedmont."

## Emic vs. Etic Dimensions

**Emic categories**: sixteenth-century Spaniards distinguished two broad types of native polity:
- *Behetría* — societies without permanent leaders, in which "all are behetría \[without permanent leaders\]"; essentially bands, tribes, and egalitarian societies that were practically impossible for Europeans to tax or exploit.
- *Señoríos, cacicazgos, curacazgos, reinos* — polities with permanent hierarchies, capable of providing food, labor, and tribute. These correspond analytically to chiefdoms and states.

The emic/etic correspondence is imperfect: Spaniards occasionally used *behetría* in a relative sense (Cieza de León applied it to small cacicazgos compared to the Inka), and Spaniards frequently over-hierarchized what were in practice confederations of near-equal chiefs.

**Etic framework**: The Villamaríns situate their argument within the chiefdom concept developed through Service, Sahlins, Fried, Earle, and Carneiro, but explicitly resist the *evolutionary-stage* reading of Service: "Nor should it be assumed that complex chiefdoms naturally tend to become states." The chapter instead emphasizes local histories, cyclical centralization, and long-run stability of chiefdom organization as an adaptive form.

## The Argument

The thesis rests on three interlocking claims.

### 1. Widespread Distribution at Contact
Chiefdoms "constituted a persistent sociopolitical form in South American prehistory" and "at the time of European contact, chiefdoms were widespread throughout the continent." The Villamaríns document this across:
- **Colombia and Venezuela**: Chibcha (Muisca) paramountcies of the eastern highlands (see [[muisca]]), Cauca Valley paramounts (Guaca, Buritica, Popayan, Ceniu, Tairona), Caribbean coastal chiefdoms.
- **Coastal Ecuador**: Manteno, Puna, Daule — "among the most complex chiefdoms in contact-period South America."
- **Greater Amazonia** (see [[amazonia]]): Omagua, Machiparo, Tapajós river chiefdoms on Amazonian floodplains; Quijos region; Llanos de Moxos (Baure, Moxo).
- **Andean regions** (see [[andes]]): hundreds of chiefdoms in the south, central, and north Andes that the Inka had incorporated but which retained their internal structure under indirect rule.

### 2. Time-Depth
Chiefly organization is ancient, not recently assembled: "The earliest known chiefdoms are found at Real Alto in coastal Ecuador (Valdivia V, 2970–2755 B.C.E.) and at Cerro Narrio in the highlands (c. 2500 B.C.E.). On Peru's northern and central coasts, and possibly in the highland regions, chiefdoms are first discernible between 2500 and 1800 B.C.E."

By the first millennium C.E. chiefdoms were "the predominant type of political organization in some areas of Greater Amazonia and in most of Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, and northwest Argentina." Their long record undermines the assumption that complex polities outside the Andes were recent or intrusive.

### 3. Persistence Under Colonialism
The Inka used indirect rule extensively: "outlines of many chiefdoms were still discernible in the sixteenth century." The same logic applied to early Spanish colonialism. Chiefly offices survived as the administrative scaffolding of the *encomienda* system (see [[encomienda]]): tribute and labor still flowed through hereditary chiefs (*caciques*, *kurakas*), now re-labeled colonial functionaries. In the brief interval between Inka collapse and Spanish consolidation, "a wide range of autonomous polities, from chiefdoms to states, reemerged in the altiplano." Structural resilience — not stagnation — explains this pattern.

## The Señoríos Naturales Concept

The chapter's subtitle invokes the Spanish concept of *señoríos naturales* (see [[senorios-naturales]]) — domains whose authority derived from "natural" hereditary right recognized by Iberian legal tradition, as opposed to conquest or delegation. Colonial courts repeatedly adjudicated on which indigenous lords possessed *señoríos naturales*, which gave them standing to negotiate tribute rates, protect subjects from forced relocation, and transmit office to heirs. The Villamaríns show that Chibcha sijipcuas, Andean kurakas, and coastal Ecuadorian paramounts all claimed and received this recognition — demonstrating that the European legal category and the anthropological chiefdom concept tracked the same underlying social reality.

## Chiefdom Internal Structure: The Chibcha Case

The most detailed structural analysis in the chapter concerns the Chibcha (Muisca) of the Colombian altiplano, used to model centralization dynamics more broadly. The hierarchy had four levels:
- **Sijipcua** (cacique): paramount chief, the "political, ceremonial, and judicial focus of the community"; succeeded matrilineally (oldest son of the chief's oldest sister); carried on litters; subject to elaborate sumptuary rules.
- **Sivintiva**: heads of kinship-territorial units (*sivin*), also succeeding matrilineally.
- **Uta headmen**: local lineage officers, performing the same work as commoners, receiving no tribute.
- **Commoners**: paying annual tribute (seven or eight ordinary *mantas* and some gold per *capitán*) in exchange for which chiefs feasted their subjects.

The Zipa (paramount of the southern domain) and Zaque (northern) each commanded populations of "several hundred thousand within their dominions." Their expansion worked by military conquest followed by structural preservation: "When Bogota gained military control over the savannah and adjacent areas, he did not dismantle hierarchical structures but built on them."

## The Amazonian Sub-Debate

A significant embedded debate concerns complex society in Greater Amazonia. Steward's 1940s–1950s "limiting factor theory" held that soils and protein scarcity precluded chiefdom formation: "Subsistence techniques in this environment barely provided enough food, particularly proteins, to meet daily requirements, and it was impossible to accumulate surpluses that could support full-time occupational specialists or ruling classes." The Villamaríns align with Roosevelt and Lathrap in rejecting this: floodplain (várzea) soils are fertile; Omagua, Machiparo, and Tapajós chiefdoms maintained dense populations and elaborate hierarchies; the Llanos de Moxos earthworks (canals, causeways, raised fields over up to 20,235 hectares) demonstrate organized labor on a scale requiring political coordination. "A tendency to discount the existence of chiefdoms in Greater Amazonia can be seen as part of a tradition that predominated through the late 1960s and early 1970s, which saw complex organization in the region as aberrational."

## Warfare, Centralization, and Fragmentation

The chapter engages Carneiro's circumscription/warfare thesis (that chiefdoms "were born out of war") but qualifies it sharply: warfare was important in *late* phases of centralization in some regions (Chibcha, northern Colombia), but also caused political fragmentation (Aburra in Antioquia; Cauca Valley before contact). "In the Colombian Pijao, warfare that continued for over half a century did not promote centralization." The authors conclude that "the role of warfare in political centralization and fragmentation is still difficult to discern" and favor multi-factor analysis (economic, ideological, ritual, and military bases of chiefly power, typically "interlocking" rather than singular).

## Trajectory Variation

A key analytic contribution is the demonstration that chiefdoms did not evolve in one direction. "More centralized polities than those the Europeans encountered had existed in prior epochs in the Moxos region of lowland Bolivia, Marajo Island, and the Orinoco Llanos." In Colombia's Cauca Valley, Calima chiefdoms (C.E. 100–1200) were followed by smaller polities; in the Magdalena Valley, complexity also declined between the first millennium C.E. and the sixteenth century. "The course that centralization and simplification took in different regions and among different peoples strongly points to the importance of placing attention on particular local histories."

## Limitations and Critiques

1. **Source bias**: The primary documentation is sixteenth-century Spanish — visitas, relaciones, litigation. Spaniards systematically over-reported hierarchies useful for tribute extraction and may have imposed hierarchical readings on less-structured political forms (the Chitarrero case: war chiefs converted to permanent *caciques* "with little support on the part of their own people").
2. **Taxonomy slippage**: The chapter acknowledges that "the boundaries between \[chiefdoms and states\] may not always be as clear as one would want." Chibcha polities have been classified as "great chiefdoms," "unusually large chiefdoms," "an incipient state," "nascent state," and "kingdom."
3. **Feinman-Neitzel problem**: The "chiefdom" category may encompass too much diversity to be analytically useful as a single type (see Feinman and Neitzel, "Too Many Types," 1984).
4. **Post-contact distortion**: Disease and colonial disruption may have simplified hierarchies before they were documented, so the contact-period chiefdoms may already represent a reduced state.
