# Learning Institution

A **learning institution** (Nagl's term, drawn from organizational theory) is a military organization that possesses the cultural attributes, structures, and incentive systems necessary to recognize when its existing doctrine is failing against a new form of war, to develop appropriate new doctrine, to disseminate it, and to implement it in the field during an ongoing conflict.

## Core Criteria (from Nagl's framework)

Nagl draws on Downie and Sullivan/Harper to define learning as "a process by which an organization uses new knowledge or understanding gained from experience or study to adjust institutional norms, doctrine and procedures in ways designed to minimize previous gaps in performance and maximize future successes." Institutional memory is "the conventional wisdom of an organization about how to perform its tasks and missions"; "Members come and go, and leadership changes, but organizations' memories preserve certain behaviors, mental maps, norms, and values over time." Doctrine codifies it: "Doctrine is thus an efficient way to track the development of learning in military organizations; changes in doctrine are prima facie evidence of military learning." Figure 1-1 depicts "The Institutional Learning Cycle: The Process of Doctrinal Change."

Nagl poses structured, focused Question Set #1 (Figure 1-2) to test whether an army is a "learning institution":

1. Does the army promote suggestions from the field?
2. Are subordinates encouraged to question superiors and policies?
3. Does the organization regularly question its basic assumptions?
4. Are high-ranking officers routinely in close contact with those on the ground and open to their suggestions?
5. Are Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) generated locally and informally or imposed from the center?

"The answers to the 'structured, focused' questions in Question Set #1 will demonstrate that the British army was in fact a 'learning institution' during the period of this study as a result of its organizational culture. The army developed a successful counterinsurgency doctrine in Malaya largely as a result of its performance as a 'learning institution.' The answers to the same questions will show that the American army was not a learning institution during its involvement in Vietnam but was in fact organizationally disposed against learning how to fight and win counterinsurgency warfare. It was as a result of this failure to become a 'learning institution' that the American army did not learn how to fight a counterinsurgency war in Vietnam, and that the British Advisory Mission to Vietnam was ineffective in passing on counterinsurgency lessons from the recent British success in Malaya to the American army in Vietnam." (Ch. 1).

British example (1902 *Combined Training*): "Success in war cannot be expected unless all ranks have been trained in peace to use their wits. Generals and commanding officers are, therefore, not only to encourage their subordinates in so doing by affording them constant opportunities of acting on their own responsibility but, they will also check all practices which interfere with the free exercise of the judgment, and will break down, by every means in their power, the paralyzing habit of an unreasoning and mechanical adherence to the letter of orders and to routine, when acting under service conditions."

Organizational culture is the key intervening variable (Halperin, Kier): "The organizational culture is the intervening variable between civilian decisions and military doctrine." American essence ("ground combat by organized regular divisional units") produced resistance; British colonial experience and regimental system produced openness. Directly applies and contrasts with Krepinevich's analysis of the U.S. Army's rigid "Army Concept" in Vietnam.

## British Army in Malaya (positive case) — Templer Period and Full Institutional Learning (1952-1957)

Nagl argues the British Army in Malaya qualifies as a learning institution. Early 1948-51 showed mixed results (bottom-up promise via Walker/Ferret Force/Briggs amid higher resistance; see prior section and Ch. 4). Full institutionalization occurred 1952-1957 under Templer.

**Templer’s combined powers and political primacy.** Arrived Feb 1952 with directive: “not only will you fulfill the normal functions of the High Commissioner but you will assume complete operational command over all the Armed Forces...” Brief opened with HMG policy that Malaya “should in due course become a fully self-governing nation.” Reorganized councils to integrate local leaders (Tunku Abdul Rahman/UMNO, Leong Yew Koh/MCA); created compact ops committee including Director of Intelligence. Insisted political progress could not wait: “I am a firm believer in first things first... it is all the more necessary because of the emergency to press on with this measure.” Alliance (UMNO+MCA) harnessed nationalism: “Harnessing nationalism as an issue for the government against the insurgents was the single most vital part of winning the 'hearts and minds' of the population.”

**Civil-military fusion at every level.** DWECs made District Officer “the central and binding force... Chairman of DWEC” coordinating army, police, Home Guard, and civil officers. “Military force cannot change opinion. It can only create a framework in which economic reform and good government can take effect.” Templer’s own staff tiny (~7), with traveling officers reporting reality; he was unafraid of bad news.

**Intelligence as supreme requirement.** “The Emergency will be won by our intelligence system our Special Branch.” Director of Intelligence (Jack Morton) full member of ops committee (“This sounds clumsy, but it worked awfully well”); first efficient SB Training School (“the sluice valve of the Emergency”). “Deep jungle patrolling... was of very little use when there was no specific information to go on.” SEPs turned into sources and guides. Police posts secured first.

**Small-unit operations, minimum force, ATOM doctrine.** Abandoned “shoulder to shoulder” sweeps. “generally no more than a platoon or a couple of sections... goes into the jungle only on first-class information... stays there for a week, two weeks, a month if need be.” Heavy firepower curtailed. “The secret of the change is not so much the new type of training as the integration of Army and Police together as a single anti-Bandit force...” Templer commissioned ATOM (“soldier’s bible”) from Walker covering patrolling/ambushing/intelligence/MCP org. Templer: “It is largely as a result of the publication of this handbook... that we got militant communism in Malaya by the throat.” Adopted force-wide; DWEC courses (150+ teams) and Jungle Warfare School disseminated.

**Food denial / New Villages (3 phases) + Psywar.** “The CTs were beginning to be strangled through food denial operations, made much more far-reaching through the effective policing of the New Villages and by Templer’s focus on expanding 'oil spots' of security.” Phase I: SB infiltration + push CTs into frozen areas. Phase II: central rice cooking, no raw rice, police on villages/fields. Phase III: spectacular successes with SB teams + SEP guides. Psywar (≤30 ex-CTs) used leaflets, voice aircraft, SEP playlets; rewards policy provided “the only hope of acquiring the information which our intelligence so urgently needed.”

**Paramilitary and innovation.** Home Guard to ~250k (defending 72 New Villages); SOVF of SEPs (“To hell with drill. We want them to handle weapons and lay ambushes”); Iban trackers/Sarawak Rangers; 10 helos for insertion. Federation Regiment and Military College for multi-racial independence army.

**Spirit of innovation and honest assessment (Question Set #1).** Bottom-up input welcomed from district advisers, privates, SEPs. “General Templer was smart enough to go out into the jungle with the Gurkhas to find out for himself.” “The British army demonstrated a remarkable openness to learning during the years from 1952 to 1957... spirit of innovation he created... encouraged the taking of risks and the honest assessment of results essential to improving the performance of the organization.” Templer later reflected “jungle bashing on false or no information was a complete waste of time” and food control should have been appreciated earlier. “one of the finest achievements of the British Army since 1945.”

**Metrics of success.** “Major A” incidents: 33 (Jul 1951) → 14 (1952) → 7 (1953) → 0 (Apr 1954). Malacca “White Area” 3 Sep 1953; >13 million in White Areas by Templer’s departure (31 May 1954). Final: CTs 6,698 KIA + 2,675 captured/surrendered vs. SF 1,865 KIA. Independence 31 Aug 1957; Emergency over 31 Jul 1960.

**Before/After tables (Nagl).** See full criteria shift (Victory Doubtful→Yes; Unity No→Yes; Bottom-up Low Level Only→Yes; Local doctrine No→Yes; Small staff No→Yes; Superiors questioned No→Yes). Regimental system + colonial experience + political clarity enabled the culture to remain open to innovation while pursuing clear objectives.

Explicit contrast: British Advisory Mission (BRIAM/Thompson) in Vietnam proved “almost completely ineffective” because “the American military was not interested in learning from it.” (Cross [[krepinevich-the-army-and-vietnam-1986]], [[army-concept]]). The British case demonstrates what a true learning institution looks like in COIN.

## U.S. Army in Vietnam (negative case)

The U.S. Army largely failed the test. It imported the "Army Concept" (see [[army-concept]] and [[krepinevich-the-army-and-vietnam-1986]]), treated the war as a conventional mid-intensity conflict, measured success by body count and large-unit engagements, and subordinated pacification. Career incentives (one-year tours, "ticket punching," preference for command of large formations) actively discouraged the behaviors required for effective COIN. Even after Tet, adaptation was limited and driven more by political necessity than institutional conversion.

**Nagl Ch. 7 evidence (fighting years 1965-1972) — full negative case application:** Despite suggestions percolating from the field (most notably USMC Combined Action Platoons and the PROVN study questioning basic assumptions of the U.S. combat role), “the learning cycle stopped at the level of the Chief of Staff of the Army in Washington and COMUSMACV in Vietnam; innovative thinking from above as well as below foundered on the army’s idea of how wars should be fought. Isolated from the war by their staffs and seeing only what they wanted to see, these generals precluded organizational learning on counterinsurgency.”

Concrete failures per Question Set #1 in the fighting years (Figure 7-4): Bottom-up input Yes (field suggestions continued), but Superiors questioned: “Yes” only at “Low Level Only, CORDS, CAP Only”; Local doctrine development: No; Local training centers: No; Small, responsive staff: No; Theoretical thinking remained “Low Level Only.” Figure 7-3 records parallel failure: the Army developed no successful COIN doctrine on any criterion (Victory, Objective, Unity of command, Minimum force, Mass/appropriate structure all “No”).

Specific barriers documented: CSA Harold K. Johnson Christmas 1965 rejected junior officers’ “bait” small-unit suggestions despite philosophical agreement, citing inability to respond to public outcry on response times or casualty “callousness” charges. The 1966 ARCOV study produced TO&E changes but “all in line with a conventional combat orientation; ‘We were not into pacification at all.’” Institutional blocks: Komer noted no establishmentarian would report to the President “this war stinks. These programs are no good”; civilians in DoD learned earlier because “not fettered with a rigorous belief that firepower yielded results” and careers did not hinge on proving bullets/bombs succeed. “Can-do” culture: subordinates put “all shoulders to the wheel” once decisions made. Westmoreland staff alternate-strategies study (Jan 1966) “nothing significant came out”; conducted by same officers who created current strategy. MACV size/structure made learning “extremely difficult”; Westmoreland blocked intermediate HQ; subordinates screened him (Vann: “even his best subordinates... continually screen him from the realities”). Komer summary: “Viet Nam was a tragedy of bureaucratic inability to adapt to unconventional situations.”

Post-Tet: Political masters forced CORDS and Abrams replacement of Westmoreland, but Abrams confronted “a culture that by then was so entrenched in its attitude that even the MACV commander could not change it.” “The organizational culture of the United States Army precluded organizational learning on counterinsurgency during the Vietnam War.” (Nagl explicitly cross-references Krepinevich sources and analysis throughout Ch. 7.)

Nagl's detailed advisory-years assessment (Ch. 6, 1950-1964): "great deal of input from the advisers in the field" but "the American generals who ran the MAAG and General Harkins as MACV commander were not open to theoretical thinking; they were often openly hostile to suggestions for change from below." "time and the bureaucratization of the officer corps had distorted the memory of how World War II had been won." Harkins' optimism created "chilling effect" suppressing accurate reporting. Ap Bac (Jan 1963) reports (Vann 91-page after-action, Porter on counterproductive ARVN arty/prep fires/no night ops, York) ordered suppressed or ignored; JCS briefing attempt by Vann canceled to keep dissent off record. Adviser guidance: “Avoid gratuitous criticism..." Nagl metrics (Fig 6-3): bottom-up input Yes; superiors questioned/hostile to theory: effectively No; theoretical thinking Low Level Only; local doctrine dev: No; local training centers: No; small responsive staff: No. "The organizational culture of the US Army is very big and hard to change... It’s much easier to change one regiment [in the British Army] than the U.S. Army." Doctrine developed stateside; high command resistant even to Kennedy COIN push, convinced essence "is military" and solvable per WWII/Korea. "advising remains a road to nowhere." Language/cultural preparation absent. Nagl doctrine assessment (Fig 6-2): No on victory, realistic objectives, unity of command (mil to political), minimum force, appropriate structure for threat. "the Americans in Saigon were, in fact, to do everything [with information from Ap Bac] but learn from it." (Halberstam via Nagl). Later fighting years showed same pattern until external pressure (CORDS late, Abrams "One War" attempt largely blocked by culture below). 

## Implications

Nagl's thesis places organizational culture at the center of military effectiveness in irregular war. It is not enough for individuals to learn; the institution must have mechanisms and a culture that reward adaptation. The book became highly influential in the post-9/11 U.S. military reform and the writing of FM 3-24.

## Historiography

The "learning institution" framing is now standard in COIN and innovation literature. Critics argue it overstates British success (Malaya had favorable conditions: smaller scale, ethnic fault lines, colonial authority) and understates political factors in Vietnam's outcome. Nagl's own Iraq service provided a real-world test of the thesis.

## Capstone Synthesis from Nagl Part IV (Ch. 8–9, conclusions; exclusive from range 06)

Nagl's final analysis draws the stark implication: the same organizational culture cannot optimize for both conventional and counterinsurgency war. “The very attributes that allowed the British army to respond to the demands of counterinsurgency in Malaya — decentralization, minimal use of firepower, independent and innovative theater commanders — made it a less effective learning organization on the conventional battlegrounds of World War II.” “The demands of conventional and unconventional warfare differ so greatly that an organization optimized to succeed in one will have great difficulty in fighting the other.” The culture that makes an institution effective in one domain “may blind it to the possibility that its strengths in that field are crippling deficiencies in a different situation — the more debilitating for being so deeply rooted in the culture that they are never even recognized, much less questioned.”

Consequently: “If it is in fact impossible for the same organization to perform effectively two very disparate tasks... then organizations should focus on achieving just one critical mission.” The U.S. Army in Vietnam “is a classic example.”

British success in Malaya flowed directly from a culture “developed over many years of colonial policing” that “not just encouraged but actively expected innovation.” Most of the doctrinal-learning recommendations later formalized by Downie were already “integral parts of the British army’s standard procedure.” Doctrine spread informally “by word of mouth and through the unofficial writing of participants”; the regimental system and colonial experience enabled bottom-up adaptation once senior leadership aligned (Briggs/Templer).

By contrast, the U.S. Army even “during a war in which organizational failures were obvious to much of the army” achieved “no consensus... that change was required.” “Only organizational self-awareness can change organizational culture.” Quotes from senior officers underscore the barrier: culture “can also be a liability”; “innovators are forced out”; generals must “listen even to majors and colonels,” break “compartments,” foster “open, shared thought,” “intellectual curiosity,” “humility.”

For the post-Cold War world: most conflict is internal; “simmering internal war is a permanent condition.” The American army “so successful in waging and winning wars against other states, must adapt to increase its ability to moderate wars within states.” “Armies will have to make the ability to learn to deal with messy, uncomfortable situations an integral part of their organizational culture. In T.E. Lawrence’s metaphor, they must learn how to eat soup with a knife. The process will not be comfortable, but it could not be more important.”

Nagl offers no grand theory of why some forces adapt but supplies a traceable framework centered on organizational culture. Future cases (e.g., French in Indochina/Algeria) merit the same process-tracing approach. Explicitly cross-references Krepinevich for the Vietnam side and positions the work against purely prescriptive COIN theory (Galula).
