# Political Islam (Islamism)

The modern political ideology holding that **Islam should order state and society** — that the
solution to the Arab and Muslim world's ills is a return to Islamic governance. The movement
that, from the 1970s, displaced secular [[arab-nationalism|Arab nationalism]] as the dominant
oppositional force in the [[middle-east-modern|Middle East]]. From Eugene Rogan's
[[rogan-the-arabs|*The Arabs: A History*]].

## Origins: reform and the Muslim Brotherhood

Political Islam draws on the nineteenth-century **Islamic reform** of [[arab-nationalism|al-Afghani
and Muhammad Abduh]], who sought to reconcile Islam with modernity. Its organizational founder
was **Hassan al-Banna**, who created the **Muslim Brotherhood** in Egypt in **1928** — a mass
movement combining religious revival, social welfare, anti-colonial nationalism, and the goal of
an Islamic order. The Brotherhood became the great popular rival to both the secular monarchy and
(after 1952) to [[gamal-abdel-nasser|Nasser's]] regime, which suppressed it.

## Radicalization: Sayyid Qutb

The decisive radical turn came in the 1960s with **Sayyid Qutb** (1906–66), "one of the most
influential Islamic reformers of the century." Sent to study in the **United States (1948–50)**,
Qutb returned repelled by what he saw as its materialism and moral laxity, determined to shield
the Islamic world from such "moral degeneration." Imprisoned and tortured under Nasser, he
developed a militant ideology: that modern Muslim societies had fallen into a new ***jahiliyya***
(pagan ignorance) and that a vanguard must wage **jihad** to restore God's sovereignty.
Executed by Nasser in 1966, Qutb became the **intellectual father of radical Islamism** and
later jihadi movements.

## The rise of political Islam

Rogan situates the movement's rise in the **failures of secular nationalism**. The crushing
[[arab-israeli-conflict|1967 defeat]] discredited [[arab-nationalism|Nasserist pan-Arabism]];
the 1970s oil wealth and the **1979 Iranian Revolution** showed Islam as a mobilizing political
force; and authoritarian, corrupt regimes left the mosque as the one space of opposition. By the
1980s "many in the Arab world turned to the politics of Islam to provide strength and unity
against external powers." A landmark was the **assassination of Egyptian president Anwar Sadat
(6 October 1981)** by the Islamist Khalid al-Islambuli ("I have killed Pharaoh"), condemning
Sadat as a secular ruler and for peace with Israel. Rogan judges that "in any free and fair
election in the Arab world today … the Islamists would win hands down."

## A contested spectrum

Political Islam is `contested:` not a single thing. It spans **mainstream, electoral movements**
(the Muslim Brotherhood and its offshoots; later parties) that work within the system; **militant
and jihadi** currents (descending from Qutb) that pursue violent revolution or, later,
transnational jihad; and **state Islamism** (Saudi Wahhabism; the Iranian Islamic Republic).
Scholars debate whether Islamism is a reaction to **modernity** or a form of it, a product of
**authentic religious revival** or of political and economic **frustration** (Samir Kassir's
"Arab malaise"), and how far the post-9/11 Western conflation of Islamism with **terrorism**
distorts a diverse phenomenon.

## Significance

Political Islam is the defining ideological development of the late-twentieth-century Arab and
Muslim world — the successor to [[arab-nationalism|Arab nationalism]] as the language of
opposition and identity, and central to Rogan's account of the present "age of US domination,"
9/11, and the "war on terror." It remains the most dynamic political force in the region.

## The Asian Parallel Tradition: Mawdudi, Pakistan, and Beyond (Formichi)

Rogan's account centers Egypt and the Arab world; Chiara Formichi's [[formichi-islam-and-asia-2020|*Islam
and Asia: A History*]] (2020) traces a parallel, largely independent South/Central Asian tradition
of political Islam that fed directly into it. **Abu A'la Mawdudi** founded **Jama'at-e Islami (JI)**
in British India in 1941, rejecting both Congress secularism and the Muslim League's Westernized
nation-state demand as "un-Islamic." After Pakistan's creation, Mawdudi promoted the **1949
Objectives Resolution** ("Sovereignty belongs to Allah alone; but He has delegated it to the State
of Pakistan"), which became the 1956 constitutional preamble. Formichi (2020) stresses that
Mawdudi's key concepts — ***hakimiyya*** (God's sovereignty), ***jahiliyya*** as a description of
the contemporary (not just pre-Islamic) world, and legitimate **jihad** — directly shaped Sayyid
Qutb after the two met in 1951 (mediated by Sayyid Abu'l-Hasan Nadwi at Ta'if); the vocabulary of
*hijra* (political non-cooperation with "jahili" society) and *takfir* (declaring fellow Muslims
infidel) as twentieth-century political strategies is attributed jointly to Mawdudi and Qutb.

General **Zia ul-Haq's** 1977 coup, backed by JI's Pakistan National Alliance faction, launched
top-down Islamization (the 1979 Hudud Ordinances) and drove Pakistan's registered madrasas from
900 (1971) to 8,000 (1988) — many Deobandi-run, feeding the Afghan-refugee madrasa network from
which the Taliban emerged (see [[soviet-war-in-afghanistan-1979]]). Formichi also traces political
Islam's spread into Soviet/post-Soviet Central Asia: **SADUM**'s tolerated "gray spaces" of
unregistered study circles absorbed Ibn Taymiyya, Hasan al-Banna, Qutb, and Mawdudi by the 1980s,
feeding both quietist movements like the **Tablighi Jama'at** and militant ones like the **Islamic
Movement of Uzbekistan**. In Indonesia, the 1980s *usra* (tarbiya/da'wa study-circle) movement
likewise read al-Banna, Mawdudi, and Qutb in mosque settings nationwide (see [[pancasila]]).
