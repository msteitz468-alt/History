# Comitia Centuriata

The Comitia Centuriata ("assembly of the centuries") was one of the principal legislative and electoral assemblies of Rome, organized not by simple headcount but by property-based military "centuries" — voting blocs originally corresponding to the equipment classes of the Roman citizen militia, weighted so that the wealthiest classes (equestrians and the first property class) controlled a majority of centuries despite comprising a small minority of citizens.

Traditionally attributed to King Servius Tullius's census reform, the assembly elected Rome's senior annual magistrates (consuls, praetors, censors), declared war, and served as the court of final appeal in capital cases during the Republic. Its centuriate structure meant that formal "majority" votes systematically privileged the propertied classes, since voting proceeded century by century in a fixed order and often concluded once a majority of centuries had been reached, leaving many lower-class centuries never called to vote at all — making it a central mechanism through which Rome's ostensibly popular constitution preserved effective aristocratic and plutocratic control.

The assembly's structure was reformed at least once during the middle Republic (probably in the mid-third century BCE) to bring it into closer alignment with the tribal organization of the Comitia Tributa, somewhat softening its property bias, but it retained its fundamentally wealth-weighted character through the remainder of the Republic before its practical legislative role atrophied under the Principate.
