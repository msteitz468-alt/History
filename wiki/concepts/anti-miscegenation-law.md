# Anti-miscegenation law

Statutes criminalizing marriage and/or sexual relations between persons classified as belonging to different races, typically prohibiting unions between whites and Blacks, Asians, Native Americans, or other designated groups, often with felony penalties, voiding of marriages, and loss of rights.

## Definition and Scope (US)

Core US examples were state laws (not uniform federal statute). By the 1930s, ~30 states had such laws in force; they varied in which groups were targeted and in the precise definition of "race" or "colored" (some used fractions; others informal practice or one-drop conventions). Enforcement ranged from civil invalidation to criminal prosecution. The US Supreme Court struck down remaining statutes in *Loving v. Virginia* (1967).

## Nazi Reception and Use (Whitman)

Nazi lawyers in the early 1930s studied American anti-miscegenation statutes with particular interest as technical precedent for what became the "Blood Law" (Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour, 1935). The criminalization of "Rassenschande" (race defilement) between Jews and "citizens of German blood" had no exact German statutory predecessor of the same form; American models supplied an example of a modern Western state using criminal law to police the sexual/reproductive boundary of the "ruling race." Nazis noted and sometimes rejected American variants they considered excessively harsh (e.g., broad one-drop rules) or poorly theorized.

## Distinctions and Critiques

- Not identical to segregation (public accommodations, education, transport). Anti-miscegenation focused on marriage/sex/reproduction.
- US versions coexisted with formal constitutional equality commitments (Reconstruction Amendments) that Nazis viewed as hypocritical or weak constraints.
- Nazi version was embedded in a far more comprehensive and merciless racial state project.
