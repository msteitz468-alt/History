# Short-War Illusion

The **short-war illusion** refers to the near-universal pre-1914 expectation among policy-makers, military planners, and financial authorities in Europe that any great-power war would be brief—typically three to six months, at most a year—rather than a protracted multi-year conflict. This assumption shaped war planning, munitions stockpiling, financial mobilization, and public expectations, but proved radically at odds with the realities of industrialized warfare in 1914.

## Pre-War Assumptions and Planning

- Most belligerents planned shell and munitions stocks on the basis of short initial campaigns followed by victory-enabled credit. France targeted 1,200–1,500 rounds per 75 mm gun for three months; Britain 1,500 per 18-pounder with reserves for four major three-day battles in the first two months; Germany aimed for resupply within six weeks after an opening engagement consuming ~500 rounds per gun; Russia targeted 1,000–1,500 rounds per gun for four to six months. "The plan was to create a stock of 1,000 rounds, thought to be enough for four to six months fighting." Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE (Strachan Ch. 11).
- Financial orthodoxy reinforced brevity: war would be paid from current income or short-term credit secured on victory; "the Reichsbank in 1913 concluded that mobilization alone would cost 1,800 million marks"; German general staff campaigning costs estimated 10–11 billion marks a year but focused on first thirty days or first victory. "Most official calculations were confined to the period of mobilization only." Locator: FINANCIAL MOBILIZATION (Ch. 10).
- The illusion was not confined to one side or one domain: "Bethmann Hollweg frequently referred to a war of three or four months. Such hopes, derived from the wars of 1866 and 1870, were not challenged in the light of military developments since then." Kitchener anticipated a long war but "wobbled in the face of the intensity of European warfare and its attendant shell shortage." Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE and later analysis.
- Popular belief was even shorter: "The public imagined a short war would be even more brief than that of the most optimistic of policy-makers... 'I hope we won't be in them much longer.'" Predictions often placed end "perhaps in October" (of the same year). Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE.

## Collapse in 1914: Shell Shortage and Consumption Reality

The short-war assumption collapsed not primarily because planners "underestimated the length of the war" but because actual consumption in the opening battles of position warfare vastly exceeded every pre-war model for quick-firing artillery.

- France confronted crisis within weeks of the Marne: reserves of 75 mm shells fell from 530,000 (mobilization) to 33,000 by 10 September; average consumption August 200 rounds per gun, September more than double. By late September Joffre warned "rear now exhausted. If consumption continues at the same rate, impossible to continue fighting for lack of munitions within fifteen days." Each gun limited to 300 rounds. Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE.
- Germany: by early November at Ypres, field guns limited to half a train of shells daily; Falkenhayn estimated four days' fighting left in Flanders. "Some batteries were limited to five rounds a day." Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE.
- Russia and Britain similarly: Russian corps seven days' supply at 50 rds/gun/day; British at First Ypres "the field guns were restricted to firing nine shells for the day"; Gallipoli howitzers two rounds, field guns four. Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE.
- "Shell shortage was not the result of pre-war neglect." Stocks had been doubled from earlier estimates precisely because of lessons from Manchuria and Boer War. "The shells crisis of the autumn of 1914 was emphatically not a manifestation of a belief that the war would be short." The precipitant was "the manner of fighting was so different from pre-war expectation." Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE.

The illusion's collapse forced immediate recognition that production capacity, raw materials allocation, and labour reallocation—not merely initial stocks—would determine staying power.

## Persistence of the Illusion

Even after the Marne and the onset of trench warfare, the short-war mindset persisted in adjusted forms: "When the war was not over within a month, men adjusted by thinking of it as a series of short episodes. The short-war illusion therefore persisted throughout the war." "Not until 1917 would it become common to predict the war’s continuation in terms of years." Locator: SHELL SHORTAGE.

This had strategic consequences: continued optimism about imminent breakthroughs or economic exhaustion of the enemy delayed full societal and industrial mobilization in some quarters and shaped cabinet debates (e.g., British manpower allocation between army and production).

## Historiographical and Analytical Significance

Strachan's analysis distinguishes the assumption's role in pre-war stock levels from its limited explanatory power for the actual 1914 crises. The illusion reflected wishful thinking and failure to integrate military consumption data with economic reality more than deliberate planning error. It contributed to the rapid transition to state-directed war economies (KRA, Ministry of Munitions, etc.) once exposed. The concept remains central to understanding why the war became "total" despite no pre-war power having prepared for it as such.

`contrasts_with:` long-war expectations held by some (elder Moltke analogies of 7–30 years; Bloch on protracted indecisive war); `enabled:` the creation of centralized raw-materials and munitions bureaucracies in 1914–15; `contributed_to:` the framing of 1914–1916 as a race of industrial adaptation rather than decisive battle.
