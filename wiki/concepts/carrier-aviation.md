# Carrier Aviation

## Definition

Carrier aviation is the operation of military aircraft from ships at sea, and the
doctrine that grew up around it during the interwar period (1918–1941) in which the
aircraft carrier displaced the battleship as the arbiter of sea control. Geoffrey
Till's study of the British, American, and Japanese cases argues that "in World War
II, aviation had a decisive effect on the outcome of the naval campaigns against the
Axis powers," extending reconnaissance, protecting shipping against air and submarine
attack, and proving "a potent means of attack on enemy warships and maritime
commerce." Sea-based aircraft were also "essential for the conduct of amphibious
operations because they were the principal means of attacking defensive aircraft and
of securing command of the air." By 1945, "in most Pacific battles, the aircraft
carrier proved itself as the new capital ship" — the U.S. Navy's carrier force became
"the principal means of naval strike."

Crucially, Till resists the popular narrative of a sudden revolution. He argues that
"technological development is less a process of distinct jumps than a prolonged
slither, to which decision makers react incrementally." Naval aviation "was neither
new nor separate"; as the British First Sea Lord Admiral Chatfield put it in 1933, the
air side was "an integral part of our naval operation … not something which is added
on like the submarine, but something which is an integral part of the navy itself,
closely woven into the naval fabric."

## The Comparative Puzzle

The analytical core of the case is a puzzle of differential innovation. The Royal Navy
*pioneered* the carrier — by 1918 it had developed HMS Argus, "the world's first
carrier capable of launching and landing naval aircraft," and operated "a fleet of a
dozen carriers of one sort or another at a time when no other naval power had even
one." The Americans and Japanese "profited to a considerable extent from the
pioneering efforts of the British," with one U.S. officer admitting in 1919 that "so
many of our ideas of naval (air) policy have been gained from the British." Yet by
1939 the British "went to war … with 147 Torpedo/Spotter/Reconnaissance aircraft,
forty-one amphibians, and thirty fighters," while "the Japanese and the Americans each
had in excess of 600 front-line maritime aircraft." Till's verdict: "The British were
the least perceptive, the Japanese, the most" — though the differences were "matters
of degree, not a question of absolutes."

The U.S. and Japanese navies innovated more boldly. "Being newer in the business,"
they "were willing to make bolder departures," following their first carriers "with a
second generation that were three times as large," while Britain "consciously and
deliberately adopted a policy of 'waiting to see'." Both rivals conducted aggressive
fleet exercises: the Japanese "conducted exercises with ruthless realism … sometimes
came at the cost of many lives," and American Fleet Problems (e.g. carrier strikes by
Saratoga against the Panama Canal in 1929 and on Pearl Harbor in 1933) explored
"semi-independent carrier task forces." War-gaming at Newport heavily influenced
advocates like Captain Joseph Reeves.

## Organizational Ownership and Dual Control

Till's central explanatory variable is organizational ownership. The British suffered
under "dual control": the Royal Air Force, created in April 1918, owned the Fleet Air
Arm's aircraft, which "deprived the navy of most of its aviators." Responsibility
"remained uneasily divided, rather than shared, between Admiralty and Air Ministry,"
with "both sides … to agree to every advance" — producing "a vicious and prolonged
twenty-year bureaucratic dispute." Dual control "reduced the flow of recruits …
slowed their training, and impeded their promotion," while the Air Ministry's
priorities "were naturally with the production of fighters and bombers," leaving naval
aircraft "distinctly inferior to American and Japanese standards." The Admiralty's own
instinct to "navalize" its flyers — abhorring "private navies" and insisting general-
service officers, not aviators, command carriers — further "stopped Britain's naval
aviators from developing their own collective sense of mission."

The Americans and Japanese had no independent air force. The U.S. Navy's Bureau of
Aeronautics (BuAer), under Admiral William A. Moffett, was "a conscious bid to
inoculate its airmen … against the idea of an independent air force"; it gave naval
aviation "a coherent policy," lobbied Congress informally, and the Morrow Board of
1925 decreed "that only aviators could command carriers and air stations" — a
"sentiment … not echoed in Britain." Japan's "revolution in naval administration which
began in 1927," creating an independent naval air headquarters, "explains the
impressive surge in the development of Japanese naval aviation from the mid-1930s." All
three navies, however, shared the battleship "big-gun" culture: the U.S. Navy spent
"five times as much on battleship modernization as the British," the Japanese "three
times as much," and the dominant doctrine remained "annihilating the enemy in decisive
battle, with battleships the backbone."

## The Strategic Problem: The Pacific

A clear operational problem "pulled" U.S. and Japanese innovation. Both navies'
"preoccupation … was with each other," and the Pacific was the strategic problem.
Under War Plan Orange thinking the Americans "expected to move westward across the
Pacific without relying on bases in the theater," so "without friendly local air power
in support, the U.S. Navy would clearly have to take its own." Japan faced the mirror
problem of "how to contend successfully against heavy odds," developing
attrition-interception and then a preemptive strategy in which Yamamoto, "inspired by
the performance of Japanese carriers during the war with China" and by the British
Taranto raid of November 1940, formed "carrier task forces to cross the Pacific and
strike the American battle fleet at Pearl Harbor." By contrast, "Britain's strategic
problems were more complex," facing "at least four axes of threat" with no single
agreed adversary — a situation Till likens to "the post–Cold-War world."

## Why a Model Innovation Case

Carrier aviation is a model [[military-innovation|military innovation]] case precisely
because it combines clear organizational ownership, a defined operational problem, and
sustained experimentation. Till concludes that "the bureaucratic/administrative
environment is of decisive importance in the evolution of military capability and the
process of innovation": the Americans and Japanese "benefited from an administrative
system which allowed enthusiastic innovators a high degree of autonomy," producing
"those friends in high places, without whose constant support innovation would have
been more difficult." The British, by dividing responsibility between two services and
lacking a clear strategic incentive, fell behind despite their early lead.

## Limitations and Critiques

Till's most striking critique is *against* the standard revolution narrative. He
rejects the "duel between unimaginative … battleship admirals against young, innovative
… airmen" as a "canard," noting that gunnery officers like Chatfield, Dreyer, and
Keyes "were … at the forefront of the Royal Navy's struggle to recover Fleet Air Arm."
He insists naval aviation "was, and should have been, essentially incremental" — "a
question of constantly tinkering … rather than of wholesale replacement." The
battleship "developed greatly" in the interwar years, and "it is in fact debatable
which … made more progress … the battleship or naval aviation." Wartime confirmed this:
even in 1944 the First Sea Lord called the battleship "the basis of the strength of the
fleet," and "the real lesson of most of World War II was that both were essential
components of a balanced fleet." Finally, Till argues the decisive constraints "were
resource-based, rather than attitudinal or bureaucratic," and that many failures (the
neglect of arrester wires, catapults, and deck-parks; the preference for multipurpose
aircraft) trace "to the innovators themselves," not to reactionary admirals. The
prominence of carrier aviation, on this reading, "was a function of the distinctive
characteristics of the Pacific War."
