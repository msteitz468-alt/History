# Christianization (of the Roman Empire and Beyond)

The long, uneven process by which Christianity moved from a persecuted minority sect to the dominant religion of the Roman Empire and then of most of Europe and parts of the Near East and beyond. Involved top-down imperial legislation, elite conversion, popular mission, destruction or conversion of temples, legal privileges, and the creation of new institutions (bishoprics, monasteries, charitable foundations).

## Key Phases and Debates
- Pre-Constantinian growth despite (and sometimes because of) persecution.
- Constantinian toleration and patronage; Theodosian establishment and coercion.
- "Pagan" survivals, rural vs urban, elite vs popular Christianity.
- Conversion of "barbarian" kingdoms (Franks, Visigoths, Anglo-Saxons, Slavs, etc.) as both religious and political acts.
- Role in the transformation of classical culture, law, art, and ideas of community.

Contested: speed and completeness of the change; degree of coercion vs attraction; continuity vs rupture with classical and "pagan" traditions; how much "Christianization" actually changed daily life and mentalities versus providing a new vocabulary for old structures.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **Survival bias.** Differential survival of Christian vs pagan literature, plus absence of any balanced non-partisan account of Christianity’s rise relative to polytheism, makes it difficult to assess relative importance of the religious cultures and the speed of Christian progress and pagan decline; even Christian secular historians often treated Christianity as an epiphenomenon.
- **Epigraphy vs patristic orthodoxy.** Late antique honorific inscriptions in the East increasingly honor imperial officials rather than local dignitaries (centralization, decline of civic values); Christian epigraphy in Asia Minor reveals heretical/schismatic diversity (Novatians, Encratites, Montanists) that contrasts with patristic literature’s assertion of dominant orthodoxy.
- **Multi-track process.** (1) demographic/social growth and church building acceleration late 4th–early 5th c.; (2) legal/political establishment from Constantine through Theodosius to Justinian; (3) individual conversion biographies of very different types; (4) incomplete cultural displacement (*paideia*, festivals, magic, secular municipal life); (5) identity politics producing internal Christian schisms and Roman vs barbarian Arian markers. Desacralization (Philocalus 354 calendar juxtaposing Christian and civic holidays; Honorius allowing festivals without sacrifice) is as important as coercion. “Christians perpetuated many forms of popular pagan activity by disregarding their religious associations and re-branding them as secular or even as Christian events.” Rejects both instantaneous conversion myth and simple top-down enforcement model.
