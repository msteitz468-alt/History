# Kossinna's Settlement Archaeology (Siedlungsarchäologie)

Method developed by German prehistorian Gustaf Kossinna holding that sharply bounded, recurring groupings of archaeological material culture ("cultures") correspond directly to discrete, bounded ethnic or "peoples" (*Völker*), and that tracing the geographic distribution and change of these culture-areas over time reveals the migrations, expansions, and origins of specific ethnic groups — in Kossinna's own program, above all the Germanic peoples. His famous methodological claim was that "sharply defined archaeological culture areas correspond unquestionably with the areas of particular peoples or tribes."

Kossinna's method became foundational to early 20th-century culture-historical archaeology (later systematized more cautiously by V. Gordon Childe) but was also directly appropriated by Nazi-era German archaeology and the Ahnenerbe organization to support territorial and racial claims about ancient Germanic settlement. This political misuse produced a lasting post-war reckoning within German archaeology (the "Kossinna syndrome") and fueled the post-processual critique that equating material culture with fixed ethnicity is analytically unsound — material culture is actively and variably constituted by social practice, not a passive marker of a bounded ethnic group.

## Significance

Kossinna's method is a central cautionary case in the historiography of archaeology: a methodologically influential but politically catastrophic conflation of material culture with ethnicity, whose legacy shaped both mid-20th-century culture-historical archaeology and the reactive rise of post-processual archaeology after the 1960s.
