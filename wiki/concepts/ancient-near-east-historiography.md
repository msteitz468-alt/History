# Ancient Near Eastern Historiography

How we know — and how we have systematically misknown — the ancient Near East. This
page treats the writing of ANE history as a first-class problem, following Mario
Liverani's framing of "the ancient Near East as a historical problem" ([[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani 2014]]).

## Definition and Scope

The historiography of the ancient Near East differs structurally from that of
Greco-Roman antiquity in two respects Liverani calls a "factor of absence" and a
"factor of presence":

- **Absence of an ancient historiography.** There is no Herodotus or Thucydides of
  Mesopotamia; no native narrative tradition frames the period for us. Liverani treats
  this as a hidden advantage: the modern historian must reconstruct *ex novo* from
  primary sources rather than performing exegesis on a received, biased ancient
  account (as Greco-Roman history so often does).
- **Presence of primary documents in bulk.** Clay tablets survive fire and burial far
  better than papyrus or parchment, so administrative, legal, and commercial archives
  survive in enormous quantity. New excavation (legal and illegal) constantly forces
  revision. The field is therefore "young," fast-moving, and — in Liverani's
  term — still **"pre-paradigmatic."**

A consequence: ANE specialists are overwhelmingly archaeologists and philologists
occupied with first publication of new material; "fully-fledged historians" are rare.
History written in the field tends to be philologically faithful but light on
interpretive problems. Liverani positions his own synthesis against this — a
deliberately interpretive, problem-driven history.

## The Three Myths (emic-into-etic distortions)

Liverani's opening polemic identifies three successive mythologies that Western culture
projected onto the Near East, each of which still distorts research:

1. **The biblical myth.** The Old Testament preserved a continuous memory of the Near
   East, but as a theologically shaped one: the surrounding peoples (Assyrians,
   Chaldeans, Canaanites, Philistines) appear only as instruments in Israel's salvation
   story. Early archaeology was funded and advertised to prove "the Bible was right";
   its earliest practitioners were disproportionately clergy and confessionally
   motivated.
2. **The Orientalist myth ("anthropology by contraposition").** From Herodotus onward,
   the East was defined as the polar opposite of "our" West: despotism vs. democracy,
   immobility vs. progress, occult wisdom vs. rational science. Liverani argues this
   contrastive anthropology must give way to an **anthropology of diversity** — each
   culture different, none superior, including our own.
3. **The "cradle of civilisation" myth.** The modern origin-myth that makes the Near
   East the "dawn" of a Eurocentric world-history backbone (Near East → Greece → Rome →
   medieval Europe → modern West). Liverani accepts that state, city, and writing *first*
   appear here, but rejects the **monogenesis of civilisation**: historical phenomena
   have multiple origins and paths, and an "origin" is only one ring in a long chain.

The program Liverani urges is **normalisation**: studying the ANE like any other period,
neither uniquely holy, uniquely alien, nor uniquely originary.

## Sources Document Ideologies, Not Facts

Liverani's most influential methodological claim concerns royal inscriptions, annals,
and chronicles. These are **not historical sources** in our sense; they are political
texts — propaganda aimed at legitimacy and celebration. Reading them as factual event
records is "a fundamental misunderstanding."

But the response is not to discard them. Their **bias is itself the data**: they
faithfully document the *ideology* of their authors even when they misreport events.
From the same inscriptions Liverani reconstructs the recurring **ideology of kingship**
(see [[divine-kingship]]):

- **Centre vs. periphery / order vs. chaos.** All good is located at the centre (the
  capital as navel of the world, drawing in resources as proof of "universal" control);
  all chaos and barbarism is relegated to the godless periphery. The king is the
  barrier protecting order against chaos.
- **Tripartite time.** A positive mythical origin → a negative interlude of illegitimate
  successors and disorder → restoration by the legitimate, successful king who renews
  right relations with the gods.
- **Two registers, one event.** Internally (royal inscriptions for subjects) the same
  campaign is hegemony and subjugation; externally (letters and treaties between "great
  kings") it is reframed as reciprocity, brotherhood, and equality of status. Comparing
  the two versions of one episode is the sharpest window onto a state's political
  ideology.
- **Reading against the grain.** Because only the victor's voice survives, the defeated,
  the dissenting, and the marginal are recoverable only by a *reversed reading* — the
  refutations and reassurances in propaganda inadvertently preserve their enemies and
  their critics.

## Documentary Bias

A structural warning that governs the whole synthesis: surviving sources radically
misrepresent the society that produced them. A world that was **90–99% illiterate**,
overwhelmingly **rural and agro-pastoral**, and chronically **scarcity-bound** risks
being remembered as a world of cities, palaces, literature, and luxury — simply because
palace and temple archives, monumental art, and elite goods are what survive and what
attract excavation. The historian's duty is to **correct this bias** with quantitative
reasoning and a critical evaluation of what the sources systematically omit. This is the
methodological engine behind Liverani's insistence on society and economy alongside
political narrative.

## Limitations and Critiques

- Liverani's economic-anthropological reading (see [[ancient-near-eastern-modes-of-production]])
  is itself a model imported from outside the field (Polanyi, Marx) and is, by his own
  admission, an interpretive construct rather than a description.
- The "normalisation" program can understate genuine particularity; critics note the ANE
  *is* distinctive in its source base and its place at the threshold of state and writing.
- The "sources are ideology not fact" position, pressed hard, risks a corrosive
  skepticism toward all event-reconstruction; Liverani himself still writes a connected
  political narrative, relying on the very inscriptions he distrusts.
