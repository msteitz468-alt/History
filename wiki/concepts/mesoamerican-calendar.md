# The Mesoamerican Calendar and the Long Count

## Overview

The **Mesoamerican calendar** was the shared timekeeping and cosmological system of the
[[mesoamerican-state-formation|Mesoamerican civilizations]], described in
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Begun & Brashler, Ch 20). Traceable to the
**[[olmec|Olmec]]** and early **[[zapotec|Zapotec]]**, refined by the **[[maya-civilization|Classic Maya]]**, it governed ritual, kingship, agriculture, and the recording of history across the
region.

## The Two Interlocking Cycles

- **The 365-day solar year** (Maya *Ha'ab*): eighteen 20-day "months" plus five "unlucky"
  days (the *Wayeb*).
- **The 260-day ritual cycle** (Maya *Tzolk'in*): twenty 13-day periods.

Read together, the two cycles assign each day a unique designation that repeats only once
every **52 years** — the **Calendar Round**. Even **[[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]]**, with little
surviving script, used the basic 260-day count.

## The Long Count

The 52-year Calendar Round could not anchor events across deep time, so the **Maya** created
the **Long Count**: a linear tally of days built on their **base-20 (vigesimal)** system —
*k'in* (1 day), *winal* (20 days), *tun* (360 days), *k'atun* (7,200 days), *b'ak'tun*
(144,000 days). Counting forward and backward in bundles of twenty, the Maya could record an
**unlimited** span of time. The system depended on a **"null" place-holder equivalent to zero**
— one of the few independent inventions of zero in world history (compare its development in
[[gupta-empire|Gupta India]]). Long Count dates inscribed on **stelae** fixed the deeds of
divine kings in precise chronology; the **last Long Count date** at any Classic Maya site is
**15 January 910 CE** (Itzimté), a marker of the Classic collapse.

## Detailed Mechanics (Snow et al. 2020)

Snow, Gonlin, and Siegel (2020, Ch. 13) provide detailed mechanics of all three Classic Maya
calendar systems:

**The Tzolkin** (260-day ritual calendar) worked through the permutation of 20 day names
and 13 god names cycling simultaneously. One hypothesis for its origin (Earle and Snow 1985)
is that it approximated the period of **human gestation**. The day after the first completion
of the 20-day cycle would be assigned the next god number, and so on for 260 days until
1 Imix began again. One's birthday in this calendar was part of one's name and carried
divinatory predictions — analogous in function to astrological signs in European tradition.

**The Haab** (365-day solar calendar) consisted of 18 months of 20 days each (numbered
0–19, as in modern computing), plus 5 (occasionally 6) extra days to complete the solar
year. The first day was 0 Pohp. All elements (month names, day numbers) had their own glyphs.

**The Calendar Round**: When Tzolkin and Haab cycled simultaneously, every day had a
compound four-element designation (e.g., "1 Ik 0 Pohp"). The same compound designation
repeated every **52 years** (18,980 days). Only 52 of the 260 Tzolkin days can coincide
with 0 Pohp (the four "year-bearer" day names: Ik, Manik, Eb, Caban). Later derivative
calendars used these as shorthand names for the 52 years of the Calendar Round.

**The Long Count** provided a linear count accompanying the 52-year Calendar Round cycles.
The earliest preserved Long Count date is on **Stela C at Tres Zapotes** — an Epi-Olmec
center — equivalent to **September 2, 32 BCE**, demonstrating that the Long Count predates
the Classic Maya and originated with the Olmec. The date reads (from bottom up): 7 Baktun,
16 Katun, 6 Tun, 16 Uinal, 18 Kin. A Classic Maya ruler would often record a great
accomplishment simultaneously in all three calendars.

The **Aztec** (Mexica) equivalent of the Tzolkin was the **Tonalpahualli**, operating on
the same permutation principle. The Maya also understood lunar cycles and the cycle of
**Venus** and could predict **solar eclipses** (Aveni 2018).

## Significance

The Mesoamerican calendar is a prime example of indigenous American **mathematics, astronomy,
and recording systems** developed with no Old World contact. Its precision underwrote both the
agricultural cycle and the ideology of sacred kingship, and its zero ranks among the
independent intellectual achievements of the ancient world. The calendar system spread from
the Olmec through the Zapotec and Classic Maya to the Aztecs, with each culture adapting it
for their own purposes. See [[writing-and-record-keeping]] and
[[snow-gonlin-siegel-archaeology-native-north-america-2020]].
