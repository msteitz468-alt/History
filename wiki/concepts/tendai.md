# Tendai (Japanese Buddhist School)

Tendai (*Tendai-shu*, "Heavenly Platform School") is the Japanese adaptation of the Chinese T'ien-t'ai Buddhist tradition, established on [[mount-hiei|Mount Hiei]] northeast of Kyoto by [[saicho|Saicho]] (Dengyo Daishi) and formally recognized by the imperial court in 806. It was one of the two dominant schools — alongside [[shingon|Shingon]] — of the [[heian-period|Heian period]], and served as the mother tradition from which the great Kamakura Buddhist reformers (Honen, Shinran, Dogen, Eisai, Nichiren) would emerge. Source: [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]].

## Definition and Scope

Tendai in its Chinese form centered on the systematization of Buddhist doctrine accomplished by the monk Chih-i (538–597), who taught at the T'ien-t'ai mountain in southeastern China. His key doctrinal achievement was a classification of all Buddhist scriptures into a graduated scheme culminating in the *Hokekyo* (Lotus Sutra, *Myoho renge-kyo*) as the final and highest teaching of Sakyamuni Buddha. This principle of "progressive revelation" — that all earlier scriptures were provisional means (*upaya*) leading to the Lotus — meant that Tendai could claim to subsume all other Buddhist schools rather than oppose them.

Japanese Tendai differed from its Chinese parent in a crucial structural feature: from its foundation it incorporated **Mikkyo** (esoteric Buddhism) as a co-equal element alongside classical Tendai doctrine. This hybrid arose from the accident of Saicho's China visit (804–805), during which he received low-level esoteric initiations from a master named Shun-hsiao and collected extensive Mikkyo manuscripts. The government's 806 recognition required one of the two annual Tendai novices to specialize in Mikkyo. The reconciliation of Mikkyo with classical Tendai thought — which Saicho himself never fully accomplished — became "one of the principal tasks for future generations of Tendai scholars."

## Founding and the Ordination Controversy

Saicho built the first small hermitage on Mount Hiei after his ordination in 785. Imperial recognition as a court chaplain followed in 797; the landmark Takaosanji lectures before Emperor Kammu in 802 established Tendai as a rival to the Nara schools. After Saicho's China mission and formal school recognition in 806, the central struggle of his remaining sixteen years concerned **ordination independence**.

Saicho argued that the Shibunritsu — the disciplinary code used throughout East Asia to ordain monks — was fundamentally Hinayanist and therefore inappropriate for a Mahayana school. He proposed replacing it with the fifty-eight bodhisattva precepts of the *Bommo-kyo*, administered in an entirely new ceremony unique to Tendai, on an independent ordination platform (*kaidan*) on Hiei beyond Nara's jurisdiction. Three sets of bylaws submitted to the court (818, 818, 819) were met with silence from the Office of Hierarchs and the throne. His major polemic, the *Kenkairon* (820), refuted the hierarchs' objections in fifty-eight articles.

Saicho died on Hiei on 4/VI/822 without achieving his goal. Seven days later Emperor Saga authorized ordinations on Hiei; the first fourteen Tendai ordinands received the *Bommo-kyo* precepts from Saicho's successor Gishin. The ordination hall (*kaidan'in*) was completed in 827. The monastery received the imperial name **Enryakuji** in 823 in memory of Emperor Kammu whose reign era (Enryaku) had been its patron era.

The ordination reform was historically unprecedented in East Asian Buddhism: "Saicho's insistence that Tendai monks live in an 'exclusively Mahayanist monastery' marked the emergence in Japan of the sectarian monastery, which subsequently became one of the hallmarks of Japanese Buddhism."

## Doctrinal Tenets

The core Tendai doctrinal position was the supremacy of the *Hokekyo* and its teaching of the **One Vehicle** (*ichijo*): that the three traditional divisions of Buddhism (Three Vehicles, *sanjo*) were merely expedient devices, and that ultimately all sentient beings possess the Buddha-nature and will attain enlightenment. This universalist soteriology directly conflicted with the Hosso school's teaching that certain beings (*musho*) permanently lacked the Buddha-nature, producing the most significant doctrinal controversy of early Heian Buddhism.

Tendai also incorporated four types of meditation (*shikan*), of which the *jogyo zammai* (ninety-day ambulatory meditation focused on Amida Buddha) would later seed the [[pure-land-buddhism|Pure Land]] movement in Japan.

## Development after Saicho: Ennin, Enchin, Annen

The Tendai school's early post-Saicho decades were difficult. Hiei's monks were overshadowed in court favor by [[kukai|Kukai]]'s Shingon Mikkyo; in 825 the lay superintendent of Hiei wrote desperately to officials requesting placement for "the foodless monks of Hiei." Recovery came through the assiduous acquisition of superior Mikkyo training.

**Ennin** (794–864, posthumous name Jikaku Daishi) traveled to China in 838–847, spending five years in Ch'ang-an studying with second-generation disciples of Hui-kuo (Kukai's master). He received thirteen different initiations including the *soshitsuji kanjo* — a level of initiation not even offered to Kukai. Ennin also encountered the *goe nembutsu* at Wu-t'ai shan (five-tone chanting of Amida's name), which he brought back to Hiei, establishing the [[pure-land-buddhism|Pure Land]] devotional practice within Tendai. Appointed abbot (*zasu*) of Enryakuji in 854 — the first to hold this title signaling independence from Nara jurisdiction — Ennin's court connections broke Shingon's monopoly on esoteric court ritual. He built the first Jogyodo (Pure Land meditation hall) at Hiei in 848. Ennin's version of Mikkyo, arguing that the *Hokekyo* was itself an esoteric scripture ("esoteric in doctrine," *rimitsu*), became known as **Taimitsu** (esoteric Buddhism of the Tiantai/Hiei lineage), distinct from Kukai's **Tomitsu** (esoteric Buddhism of the Toji/Shingon lineage).

**Enchin** (814–891, posthumous name Chisho Daishi) likewise studied in Ch'ang-an and T'ien-t'ai shan (804–859), returning to become abbot of both Onjoji (Miidera, 859) and Enryakuji (868–891). He pushed Mikkyo's status even higher within Tendai, designating the "pure" esoteric scriptures "the king of Mahayana, the most secret of the secret," ranking above the *Hokekyo* itself. Enchin stood in Gishin's lineage (as a disciple of Gishin's disciple), whereas Ennin had been a direct disciple of Saicho — a lineage distinction that would prove explosive.

**Annen** (841–ca. 895), a junior contemporary of Enchin, devised the most elaborate systematization of Tendai Mikkyo. He held that Mikkyo represented the ultimate teachings of Buddhism, ranking above both classical Tendai and Zen. Taking this to its logical conclusion, he referred to his school not as Tendai but as *Eizan Shingon-shu* ("the Shingon school of [Mount] Hiei"), as opposed to Kukai's Shingon.

## Ryogen and Institutional Power

**Ryogen** (912–985, posthumous name Jie Daishi), appointed abbot of Enryakuji in 966, presided over the school's greatest period of institutional consolidation. He rebuilt Enryakuji after devastating fires in 935 and 966 — a reconstruction project backed by the Fujiwara regency (Chancellor Tadahira's son Morosuke had become Ryogen's devoted patron after Ryogen's prayers were credited with the birth of Morosuke's grandson, the future Emperor Reizei). Ryogen also revived classical Tendai scholarship by instituting the *kogaku ryugi* (erudite disputations) five-day debates on the *Hokekyo*, and issued a twenty-six-article disciplinary code (*Nijurokka jo kisho*, 970) to curb clerical abuses — including regulations against monks carrying weapons, which reflected the emergence of the *akuso* (wicked monks) precursor to the later *sohei* (monk warriors). Ryogen was eventually deified as a protector of Hiei.

## The Sammon-Jimon Schism

The most significant internal crisis was the split between the **Sammonha** (Mountain Branch, centered on Enryakuji/Hiei) and the **Jimonha** (Temple Branch, centered on Onjoji/Miidera). Its roots lay in succession disputes among Saicho's immediate disciples; it crystallized from the tension between monks of Ennin's lineage (controlling Hiei from Ryogen's time) and monks of Enchin's lineage (based at Onjoji). The break became violent in 993 when a dispute over the Enryakuji abbotship led Hiei's Ennin-lineage monks to burn more than forty Enchin-faction buildings, driving over a thousand of Enchin's followers to settle permanently at Onjoji.

For the next two centuries the two Tendai factions clashed repeatedly — over abbotship appointments, ordination rights, and temple properties. Onjoji's petition to establish its own ordination platform (first submitted 1039 by abbot Myoson with Regent Fujiwara no Yorimichi's support) was repeatedly denied despite Enryakuji's violent opposition. Hiei burned Onjoji no fewer than four times before the end of the Heian period (1074, 1081, 1121, 1140). Neither side ultimately prevailed: Onjoji never received its independent ordination platform; Hiei could not force Onjoji monks to be ordained on Hiei.

## Relationship to Later Japanese Buddhism

The great Kamakura-period founders were all trained on Hiei: Honen (Jodo school), Shinran (Jodo Shin school), Eisai (Rinzai Zen), Dogen (Soto Zen), and Nichiren (Nichiren school). Tendai's eclectic synthesis — incorporating Lotus doctrine, Mikkyo, Zen meditation, and Pure Land practice — provided the raw material from which each of these reformers extracted a single element and elevated it to primacy. The Tendai school's own institutional character — wealthy, politically connected, militarily capable, intellectually eclectic — was both its strength and the condition against which the Kamakura reformers defined their more focused single-practice teachings.

## Historiography

The Cambridge History account (ch. 7, Weinstein) draws primarily on the *Dengyo Daishi zenshu* (five volumes, 1926–27), the *Tendai zasu ki*, the *Nihon sandai jitsuroku*, the *Nihon kiryaku*, and modern studies including Groner's *Saicho* (1984) and Katsumata Shunkyo's *Mikkyo no Nihon-teki tenkai* (1970). Western scholarship on Tendai is thinner than on Shingon or Zen; Groner's work remains the standard entry point. Japanese scholarship is extensive; Hazama Jiko and Shimaji Daito are key reference points for doctrinal history.
