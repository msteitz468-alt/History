## Definition

The "development gap" is Hodgson's term, used systematically in the Epilogue of *The Venture of Islam* (1974), for the structural divide between societies that have achieved high levels of capital investment and [[technicalism|technicalistic]] integration and those that have not. It is emphatically not a binary between "modern" and "traditional" societies — Hodgson treats all contemporary societies as modern in the sense of being shaped by the [[great-western-transmutation]] — but a divide in the *degree* and *form* that modernity takes.

The term is deliberately chosen over "underdevelopment," "backwardness," or "Third World" because it foregrounds the relational and historical character of the gap: it is not a natural or civilizational given but a product of the same historical process (the Great Western Transmutation) that created the high-investment West. The gap names a relationship within a single world-historical complex.

## Origins and Nature of the Gap

Hodgson roots the development gap in the cumulative advantages that accrued to Western societies from the early stages of technicalization onward: advantages in capital accumulation, in continuous internal cultural tradition (which softened the dislocations of modernity), and in the power to shape the terms of global integration in ways favorable to Western interests.

> "The Muslim peoples in particular must build, as Modernity proceeds ever further, not from economic and social advantages based on yet earlier advantages, but rather from disadvantages based on yet earlier disadvantages." (*Venture of Islam*, Vol. 3, Epilogue)

The gap is self-compounding in two respects. First, the very entry of low-investment lands into technicalistic patterns produces problems (population growth, poverty, cultural dislocation) that can only be addressed by further technicalization, creating a cycle in which earlier disadvantages generate new disadvantages. Second, the cultural break that technicalism imposes is far more severe on the low-investment side: Western societies developed technicalism from within their own traditions (Scholastics to Descartes to Kant etc.; ox-cart to automobile); non-Western lands received it as an external arrival, creating a much sharper rupture in the cultural continuity required for creative response.

## Five Problem Categories Across the Gap

Hodgson analyses five categories of modern world problems, tracking how each takes a different and more acute form on the low-investment side:

| Problem category | High-investment (Western) form | Low-investment (Muslim-majority) form |
|---|---|---|
| Disruption of cultural traditions | Fragmentation, alienation, specialization; manageable by continuity | Drastic breach; external modernity without internal roots |
| Pressure on natural resources | Environmental destruction, wilderness loss | Mass poverty, food insecurity, population-driven famine risk |
| Need for social planning | Tension between liberty and coordination | Same need under conditions of desperate urgency; risk of arbitrary manipulation |
| Lettered mass culture | Lowest-common-denominator vs. elite quality | Single popular audience; translation lag; education for conformity not creativity |
| Unsettling of moral allegiances | Relative leisure for moral experimentation | Overwhelming immediate crisis; heritage functions as slogan not vision |

## The Gap as a Common Problem

Hodgson insists on a counter-intuitive point: the development gap is not only a problem for the low-investment side. For Western societies — most visibly the United States and France in the mid-twentieth century — "military commitments and foreign policy at large (which is largely a function of the development gap) tend to be decisive not only in shaping the national budget but more subtly in the psychological life of the nation." The Algerian independence struggle toppled French governments; anti-communism structured American domestic life. The gap is a constitutive fact of modern world politics for all parties.

From this Hodgson argues that the problems created by the gap "can probably not be fully solved in one place till they are solved everywhere else too." He explicitly rejects both the advice of Western market economists (who tell low-investment lands to wait patiently for convergence through the market mechanism) and the more extreme non-Western theories that treat the gap as purely a product of Western exploitation requiring only political independence to resolve.

## Possible Closure of the Gap

Writing in the early 1970s, Hodgson speculates about the possibility that automation, computing, nuclear or solar energy, and new chemical compounds might eventually make the acquisition of basic technicalistic prosperity "almost sudden, once sufficient education has been introduced." He imagines a future in which manufacturing is as non-decisive for basic economic life as agriculture had become by his own time — making industrialization a "transient affair" in some countries and rendering the gap historically soluble.

This speculation is explicitly tentative; Hodgson's purpose is less to predict than to argue that "adequate human vision must prove relevant to some such future anywhere," and that the moral and cultural dimensions of closing the gap are not separable from its economic and political dimensions.

## Relation to the Islamic Predicament

The development gap is the primary structural context for the [[modern-islamic-predicament]]. Hodgson identifies most Muslim-majority lands as occupying the low-investment side, making the five problem categories especially acute there. But the gap analysis also prevents a purely culturalist account of Muslim difficulties: structural position matters alongside heritage, and the heritage's potential relevance is precisely tested by whether it can provide vision adequate to problems that are in their deepest form common to all humanity.

## Historiographical Status and Critique

Hodgson's "development gap" concept anticipates aspects of later world-systems and dependency theory but is distinct from them in rejecting a purely exploitation-based account: he attributes agency and internal intellectual responsibility to non-Western societies alongside structural analysis. He also differs from modernization theory in treating the Western trajectory not as a model to be replicated but as one historically contingent path whose specific difficulties (including the development gap it produced) are inseparable from its achievements.

The concept implicitly critiques both Cold War blocs: communist central planning and capitalist market mechanisms are, for Hodgson, equally inadequate responses to the gap without adequate moral vision.
