## Definition and Scope

Talud-tablero is the distinctive architectural vocabulary of [[teotihuacan]] pyramid construction: a repeating vertical module composed of a sloping lower panel (the *talud*, literally "slope" or "batter") surmounted by a framed vertical panel (the *tablero*, literally "board" or "panel"). This unit is applied repeatedly to form each body (tier) of a Teotihuacan-style step-pyramid, creating a characteristic visual profile that alternates between receding diagonal slopes and projecting upright framed surfaces. The tablero typically projects out over the talud below it and is framed by a rectangular border or molding. The visual effect contrasts with the pure stepped pyramid profiles of Maya or Oaxacan traditions.

As an etic (analyst-applied) architectural category, "talud-tablero" is the standard archaeological diagnostic for Teotihuacan-associated construction across Mesoamerica.

## Origin and Development at Teotihuacan

The style is fully developed by the Miccaotli phase at Teotihuacan (ca. 100–170 CE), when all major pyramid construction in the civic-ceremonial core employs it. The Moon Plaza is "flanked by eight stepped pyramids in talud-tablero style" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). The Ciudadela's outer platforms support talud-tablero pyramids on all four sides. The Avenue of the Dead is "lined with many large and a few immense structures" in this style.

Precursors to the canonical form may exist in Tzacualli-phase (1–100 CE) construction at Teotihuacan, but the fully standardized module — with the tablero's distinctive projecting framed panel — appears to be a Teotihuacan innovation rather than a direct inheritance from earlier Mesoamerican pyramid traditions, which generally employed plain-sloped or terraced profiles without the projecting tablero.

The [[feathered-serpent-pyramid]] (FSP) applies the talud-tablero form with a unique variant: rather than the rubble-and-concrete fill used on every other Teotihuacan pyramid, its facades are composed of "huge cut stone blocks carefully fitted together, each weighing several hundred kilograms," with sculptural relief and three-dimensional projecting heads on the tablero surfaces (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). This makes the FSP an extreme case illustrating both the canonical form and the degree of variation it could accommodate.

Residential architecture at Teotihuacan also incorporates a lower version of the talud: compound wall bases commonly have "a talud about a meter high added on one or both sides" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6), indicating the style permeated non-monumental as well as monumental construction.

## Spread Beyond Teotihuacan

The presence of talud-tablero structures at sites outside Teotihuacan is one of the primary archaeological diagnostics of Teotihuacan contact, influence, or presence:

- **Morelos**: Hirth and Angulo (1981) document "talud-tablero structures they consider diagnostic of Teotihuacan" at many sites in eastern Morelos (cited in Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6), in the period ca. 100–250 CE. Some larger ceremonial centers are interpreted as Teotihuacan outposts or polities under Teotihuacan dominance.
- **Chingú (Tula region)**: The Ciudadela complex was "never copied except at Teotihuacan outposts, such as Chingú" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6), suggesting that the full Ciudadela-type enclosure was an elite export reserved for controlled outposts.
- **Kaminaljuyú (Guatemala)**: Sacrificial victims buried in cross-legged tailor position appear "in some of the burials in Teotihuacan-style Pyramids A and B at Kaminaljuyú" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6; Kidder et al. 1946). These pyramids employ a talud-tablero variant and are among the clearest evidence for direct Teotihuacan presence in highland Guatemala.
- **Broader Mesoamerica**: By the Early Classic period, talud-tablero elements appear at sites across Mesoamerica from the Gulf lowlands to the Maya highlands, often alongside other Teotihuacan-associated artifacts (green obsidian from Sierra de las Navajas, cylindrical vases, Thin Orange Ware imports). The distribution allows archaeologists to map Teotihuacan's interaction sphere.

## Significance as Historical Marker

Because the talud-tablero profile is architecturally distinctive and did not develop independently elsewhere in Mesoamerica before Teotihuacan's florescence, its presence at a site outside the Basin of Mexico is taken as evidence of one or more of the following: direct Teotihuacan settlement or garrison; elite emulation of Teotihuacan prestige architecture; or Teotihuacan-trained artisans. The interpretation varies by site and must be evaluated against other lines of evidence (ceramic assemblages, obsidian sourcing, burial goods, iconography). The style alone cannot distinguish direct political control from elite emulation or long-distance trade.

Caution is warranted with sites like Morelos, where Cowgill notes that "trade wares, Thin Orange and Granular, as evidence of a Teotihuacan connection" have been critiqued by Nalda (1997), since these goods had multiple distribution pathways (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). Architectural evidence (actual talud-tablero construction) is generally more diagnostic than ceramic evidence alone.

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept is an etic archaeological category; Teotihuacan's own inhabitants presumably had emic terminology for this architectural module, which is not recoverable from available sources. The boundary between "talud-tablero" and "battered-wall-with-cornice" profiles on non-Teotihuacan buildings is not always sharp, and some scholars have debated whether apparent talud-tablero at distant sites genuinely reflects Teotihuacan contact or represents convergent architectural development. The strongest cases for Teotihuacan derivation remain Kaminaljuyú and Chingú, where full Ciudadela-type enclosures or closely Teotihuacan-matched pyramid forms appear.
