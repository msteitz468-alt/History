# Human Rights

The modern framework holding that certain rights and freedoms belong to all human beings by virtue of their humanity, independent of citizenship, law, or government recognition, and that violations of these rights are matters of legitimate international concern rather than purely domestic affairs. While drawing on older natural-rights and national constitutional traditions, the concept in its distinctly international form emerged directly from the revelation of Nazi atrocities and the founding of the United Nations after the Second World War: the UN's new Commission on Human Rights, chaired by [[actors/roosevelt-eleanor|Eleanor Roosevelt]], drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the UN General Assembly in December 1948 as a non-binding statement of aspirational rights (civil, political, economic, social, and cultural) intended to prevent a recurrence of the war's atrocities.

The framework has been contested on several axes: the tension between civil/political rights (emphasized by Western liberal states) and economic/social rights (emphasized by the Soviet bloc and many developing states) during the Cold War; debates over cultural relativism versus universalism (whether human rights as framed reflect a genuinely universal standard or a particular Western liberal tradition presented as universal); and the practical question of enforcement, since the Declaration itself created no binding legal obligations, leaving actual human-rights enforcement to a patchwork of later treaties, regional courts, and international pressure with widely varying effect. Despite these debates, the human rights framework has become the dominant global vocabulary for articulating claims against state abuse and for international humanitarian intervention and monitoring.
