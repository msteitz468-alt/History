# The Roman Imperial Court (*aula*)

The **court** — the social institution, alien to the Republic, through which the
[[principate|Principate's]] monarchical power was actually exercised: the emperor's household
(*domus Caesaris*) "operating as an interface with the society over which he rules." Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 7 (**Andrew Wallace-Hadrill**), the volume's central
analysis of how imperial power worked in practice.

## A new institution

The very word *aula* (from Greek *aulē*, the term for Hellenistic royal courts) was almost
unknown to Republican Latin and established itself rapidly under the early Empire — naming both
the **place** of imperial power (increasingly the **Palatine**, where Augustus fused his house
with the temple of Palatine Apollo and the cult of Vesta) and the perilous **way of life**
around it. Wallace-Hadrill's methodological point: the court is **historiographically veiled**.
Because politics retreated from the open Forum and Senate "behind closed doors" (Cassius Dio),
our information is irreducibly **anecdotal** — gossip, Suetonian biography, the reminiscences of
Seneca, Pliny, and Epictetus. This is "not a personal weakness of our sources, but a structural
consequence" of monarchy. Modern historians, suspicious of "palace plots," over-corrected by
substituting a tidy **bureaucracy** for the unfamiliar reality of a **court society**.

## Access and ritual

The court had **no legal definition** (in contrast to the Senate): membership was constituted
by **proximity to the emperor**, gradated only by social ritual (the morning *salutatio*, the
*cena*, the grades of *admissio*, admission to the *cubiculum*). Its lack of definition "only
added to the power of the court: one of the secrets of power, the *arcana imperii*, was to be
untrammelled by rules."

Wallace-Hadrill's key comparative argument: unlike the **Hellenistic** courts — which set the
king *apart* from civic society through distancing ritual (*proskynesis*, elaborate dress,
cult language) and whose *philoi* were derided as upstart "flatterers" — the early Caesars
**assimilated** the court to the existing Roman aristocracy. They drew their *amici* and
companions from senators and equestrians, greeted senators with the **kiss**, avoided
oriental ritual (Seneca's outrage at Gaius offering his foot to kiss), wore the toga, and built
on Republican forms (the *cohors amicorum*, the *consilium*, the *comites*). The court thus
**embedded** imperial power within Roman social structure rather than standing visibly above
it.

## Patronage, power, and the *familia Caesaris*

What drew men to court was the emperor's unrivalled power to **distribute resources**
(*beneficia*): citizenship, equestrian and senatorial rank, magistracies, army and
administrative posts, fiscal concessions, and judgment. Claudius had shown "how much more
effectively imperial power was secured by favours than by arms." Because the emperor (not any
bureaucracy) was the source, requests were **mediated by brokers** — the freedmen, mistresses,
and *amici* who could secure access. Hence the rapid growth of the freedman **secretariat**:
the Palatine "offices" (*ab epistulis*, *a libellis*, *a rationibus*, *a cognitionibus*, *a
memoria*), whose divisions followed not areas of government but **channels of communication
between subject and ruler** — first prominent under Claudius (Polybius, Narcissus, Pallas).

Wallace-Hadrill insists the *familia Caesaris* is best understood **not as a modern
bureaucracy** but on the analogy of the **medieval/Renaissance royal household**, where private
service (the bedchamber, the table) and public administration are inseparable — a freedman
might rise from food-taster to curator of the aqueducts. The power of the great freedmen flowed
from **proximity**, not office; "separation of *domus* and *res publica* was an empty promise."

## The court as cultural and political centre

The court did not merely reflect society's norms but **dictated its tone** — disseminating
fashions in hairstyle, building, and taste across the empire, and (by suppressing the
Republican channels of prestige, war-glory and popular favour) redirecting elite competition
into **lavish social display**. This "contained the seeds of its own destruction": Nero
outstripped all rivals with the **Golden House** and ruinous luxury, both bankrupting the
aristocracy and provoking a moral revulsion among the municipal men sucked into his orbit. The
court was also the crucible of **opinion** — the watching-game in which fortunes (and the
posthumous "images" of emperors, like Claudius the fool) were made.

## Contested

The "civility" (*civilitas*) for which good emperors are praised has been read as a **charade**
masking autocracy (a screen, like the etiquette of Versailles). Wallace-Hadrill argues the
disjunction of appearance and reality is **exaggerated**: emperors used the court both to
*limit* the upper classes' power and to *strengthen* their own by embedding it in the social
structure — a "complex and ambivalent" relationship, not a mere facade. The degree of
"bureaucratization" of the *familia Caesaris* is likewise debated.
