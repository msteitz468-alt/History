# Oceanic History

The approach to writing history that takes a sea or ocean — rather than a nation,
continent or empire — as its primary unit of analysis, treating the maritime
space as a connective field and, in its strongest form, as a historical agent.

## Definition

Oceanic (or "thalassological") history reframes regions long treated as
peripheral margins of land-based polities as the centres of their own
ocean-spanning worlds of migration, exchange, knowledge and power. For the
[[pacific-ocean|Pacific]], it means foregrounding currents, winds, ecosystems,
islands and voyaging, and reading the ocean as a "sea of islands" that connects
rather than a void that isolates.

## Emic vs. Etic

The approach has strong **emic** roots: Pacific Islander cosmologies (e.g. *Te
Moana Nui a Kiwa*, the great ocean of Kiwa) already understood the sea as
genealogical homeland and relation, not barrier. As an **etic** scholarly
framework it draws on the Annales-derived Mediterranean model (Braudel), Indian
Ocean and Atlantic-world studies, and the "new thalassology," repurposed to
decentre European narratives of discovery.

## Methods

Deliberately multidisciplinary: marine science and geology (island formation,
hazards), genetics and biological anthropology, historical linguistics, oral
tradition, archaeology and experimental voyaging are treated as co-equal sources
alongside written documents — essential where textual records are sparse or
arrive late.

## Limitations and Critiques

Critics note the risk of romanticising connection over conflict and isolation;
the difficulty of integrating radically different disciplinary chronologies
(deep geological time vs. event history); and the danger that an "ocean as actor"
metaphor obscures human agency and inequality. The boundaries of any "ocean" are
themselves a scholarly construction.

## Scale, Lens, and the Bennett Argument

Judith Bennett (ch. 5, "The Pacific World: From Lagoons to the Deep") offers a
practical account of how scale choices shape Pacific historiography. She traces the
"monograph myopia" critique (Kerry Howe, 1979) — the dominance of micro-studies
of individual islands, events, or persons — and the counter-call for longue durée,
regional, and comparative Pacific history. She endorses both lenses as legitimate
but complementary:

> "One is no better than the other; they are just different lenses. We need the former
> to inform the latter, and to help us know who we are and where our roots are, but
> keep in mind it is the latter, the big story which can take us past the specific to
> the more general and often more powerful in understanding forces that crash on
> Pacific shores."
> (Bennett, ch. 5)

Micro-history — island-centred, event-centred, person-centred — is necessary for
gender, agency, the subaltern, and environmental change. The macro lens — Guldi and
Armitage's *History Manifesto* (2014) call for longue durée history — is needed to
address climate change, inequality, and governance in the Pacific.

Bennett identifies outstanding lacunae:
- A comprehensive history of Pacific whaling across island groups (now partly
  addressed by Jones, ch. 3; see [[processes/pacific-whaling-and-cetaceans]])
- A study of the Chinese communities within the island Pacific
- A comparative history of Pacific labour trade and unionism
- Digital history tools (Ngram, GIS, collaborative quantitative methods) to make
  macro-history feasible

She also draws a direct parallel between the Māori practice of "putting the past
before us" and Guldi/Armitage's longue durée: "some Pacific societies, including
Māori, conceptualize their history — not as the past behind them, but as before them."

## Decolonizing Turns in Pacific Historiography

The 1996 Pacific History Association conference in Hilo crystallized the Indigenous
voice debate when a scholar "ran up an independent flag, wanting far more attention
for the Indigenous lens and voice." Since then, nodes of Pacific history research
have appeared in universities within the Pacific Islands (Hawai'i, Fiji, Chuuk,
Guam, Papua New Guinea, Sāmoa).

Key interventions documented in this volume:
- **Witi Ihimaera** (ch. 1): the ocean as *Te Moana Nui a Kiwa* — genealogical
  homeland, not Magellan's named geographical object (see [[concepts/te-moana-nui-a-kiwa]])
- **Myjolynne Kim** (ch. 4): decolonizing Chuukese history through *noos* (weaving
  methodology), *tur* (kinship cloth metaphor), and recovery of women's matrilineal
  authority (see [[concepts/chuukese-history-womens-perspectives]])
- **Ryan Tucker Jones** (ch. 3): inter-species history of humans and whales as the
  best lens for the Pacific as a unified historical space (see
  [[processes/pacific-whaling-and-cetaceans]])
