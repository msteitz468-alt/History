## Definition

Covert action denotes state activity intended to influence political, economic, or military conditions abroad while concealing the sponsoring government's role. NSC 10/2 (1948) formalized the doctrine for the United States, declaring that "the overt foreign activities of the US government must be supplemented by covert operations," specifying "sabotage, anti-sabotage, demolition and...subversion against hostile states" and "support of indigenous anti-communist elements" (quoted in Alfred McCoy, *[[mccoy-cold-war-five-continents-2024|Cold War on Five Continents]]*, 2024). George Kennan's May 1948 memo, written as the State Department's architect of [[containment]], argued for "covert political warfare" involving "clandestine support of 'friendly' foreign elements, 'black' psychological warfare and...encouragement of underground resistance," while urging the US to "dispense with...high-minded international altruism" and "deal in straight power concepts."

## Emic vs. Etic

As an **emic** category, covert action was the term of art used by the [[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]] and allied agencies themselves — a doctrinal and bureaucratic classification distinguishing authorized clandestine operations from open diplomacy or war. As an **etic** analytical category, historians use it to group a heterogeneous range of state activity (propaganda, coups, paramilitary support, assassination, election manipulation) under a single causal lens. McCoy's own coinage, "the covert netherworld," is an etic interpretive term for the historical intersection of intelligence services and criminal syndicates — not a period actor's self-description; CIA operative Lucien Conein's own phrase, "clandestine arts," better captures period-actor self-understanding of shared tradecraft with criminal collaborators.

## Techniques

Documented techniques, per McCoy's archival synthesis, include:
- **Coups and destabilization** — e.g., [[iran-coup-1953]], [[guatemala-coup-1954]], [[congo-crisis-1960]], [[chile-coup-1973]].
- **Propaganda and psychological warfare** — the "Mighty Wurlitzer" media network, cultural-front organizations, fabricated atrocity propaganda (e.g., the Gerwani story disseminated by Indonesian army press and British psywar officers during the 1965–66 killings).
- **Election manipulation and funding of political parties** — e.g., 1948 Italy, 1953 Philippines (Ramon Magsaysay's campaign).
- **Assassination planning and paramilitary action** — e.g., plots against [[lumumba-patrice|Patrice Lumumba]], Operation Mongoose against Castro.
- **Interrogation and mind-control research** — Projects Artichoke and MKUltra (1950–1962), funding 149 research projects at institutions including Columbia, Harvard, and Yale; findings were consolidated into the 1963 "KUBARK Counterintelligence Interrogation" manual, later disseminated to allied security services in South Vietnam, Brazil, Uruguay, Honduras, and Guatemala.
- **Domestic surveillance and disruption** — FBI COINTELPRO (1956–1971; 2,370 documented operations per the Church Committee) and CIA's Operation CHAOS.

## Comparable National Agencies

McCoy's account situates the CIA among a set of Cold War-era intelligence/covert-action services: the Soviet KGB; Britain's SIS/MI6, MI5, GCHQ, and the Foreign Office's Information Research Department (IRD), a psywar unit; and France's SDECE (Service de documentation extérieure et de contre-espionnage).

## Limitations and Critiques

The 1975 Church Committee investigations — prompted by Seymour Hersh's 1974 exposé of CIA domestic operations and the Rockefeller Commission's findings on Operation CHAOS — established the most authoritative public accounting of US covert action's excesses, including MKUltra, assassination planning against foreign leaders, and COINTELPRO. Critics note that covert-action doctrine, by design, evades normal channels of democratic accountability, making its true scope and consequences difficult to assess even after declassification. McCoy's broader interpretive claim that covert action was the *decisive* mechanism of Cold War outcomes — rather than one instrument among conventional diplomacy, economic power, and military deterrence — is itself a contested historiographical position; see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]].
