# War as the Continuation of Policy

The proposition that war is a political instrument is Carl von Clausewitz's most famous and central contribution to strategic theory. In the German original, he writes: *"Der Krieg ist eine bloße Fortsetzung der Politik mit anderen Mitteln"*—commonly translated in the Howard & Paret edition as **"War is merely the continuation of policy by other means."**

This concept is developed in two critical locations in *On War*: first as a logical deduction in Book One, Chapter One (§24), and second as the culminating operational synthesis of the entire work in Book Eight, Chapter Six ("War is an Instrument of Policy").

---

## Book One: The Logical Deduction

Clausewitz introduces this principle in Book One, Chapter One, Section 24, as the crucial conceptual bridge that resolves the tension between absolute war (the abstract logic of extremes) and real war (modified by friction and chance). The moment war ceases to be a pure, theoretical act of total violence, the motive that initiated it must reassert itself:

> "We see, therefore, that war is not merely an act of policy but a true political instrument, a continuation of political intercourse, carried on with other means. What remains peculiar to war is simply the peculiar nature of its means. War in general, and the commander in any specific instance, is entitled to require that the trend and designs of policy shall not be inconsistent with these means. That, of course, is no small demand; but however much it may affect political aims in a given case, it will never do more than modify them. The political object is the goal, war is the means of reaching it, and means can never be considered in isolation from their purpose."

This formulation carries several key implications:
* **The primacy of the object over the means:** The political object (*Zweck*) is the goal, and the military aim (*Ziel*) is merely the means. Just as the means cannot be understood apart from the end, military strategy cannot make sense without its political context.
* **The "grammar" vs. the "logic":** War has its own medium of execution—violence, friction, danger, and chance. Policy must understand this medium (the "grammar" of war), but it remains subordinate to the political intelligence that directs it (the "logic").
* **The political object determines the scale of effort:** In Book One, Chapter One, Section 25 ("The Diverse Nature of War"), Clausewitz explains that "the more powerful and inspiring the motives for war... the closer will war approach its abstract concept... and the more military and less political will war appear to be." Conversely, if the motives are weak, "the conflict will seem increasingly political in character."

## Book Eight: The Operational and Cabinet Synthesis

In Book Eight, Chapter Six (B), Clausewitz expands this concept into a functional guide for cabinet-level planning and war plans. Here, he establishes the unity of war and policy:

> "We maintain, on the contrary, that war is simply a continuation of political intercourse, with the addition of other means. We deliberately use the phrase 'with the addition of other means' because we also want to make it clear that war in itself does not suspend political intercourse or change it into something entirely different... Do political relations between peoples and between their governments stop when diplomatic notes are no longer exchanged? Is war not just another expression of their thoughts, another form of speech or writing? Its grammar, indeed, may be its own, but not its logic."

### The Subordination of the Military stand-point

Clausewitz rejects the notion of a "purely military" view of war:
> "Subordinating the political point of view to the military would be absurd, for it is policy that has created war. Policy is the guiding intelligence and war only the instrument, not vice versa. No other possibility exists, then, than to subordinate the military point of view to the political."

Consequently, he critiques governments that ask soldiers for "purely military advice" or hand over all resources to a commander to draw up a war plan in isolation. Instead:
1. **The Commander in the Cabinet:** "If war is to be fully consonant with political objectives, and policy suited to the means available for war, then unless statesman and soldier are combined in one person, the only sound expedient is to make the commander-in-chief a member of the cabinet, so that the cabinet can share in the major aspects of his activities" (Book Eight, Chapter Six, B).
2. **Policy itself is at fault for military failures:** When people complain of harmful political influence on the management of war, they are usually misidentifying the problem: "Their quarrel should be with the policy itself, not with its influence. If the policy is right—that is, successful—any intentional effect it has on the conduct of the war can only be to the good. If it has the opposite effect the policy itself is wrong."

### The Historical Transformation of War

Clausewitz uses the French Revolutionary wars to prove that military transformations are fundamentally political in origin. The French armies succeeded not because they "freed" themselves from politics, but because a transformed domestic political order mobilized the energy of the French nation:
> "It follows that the transformation of the art of war resulted from the transformation of politics. So far from suggesting that the two could be disassociated from each other, these changes are a strong proof of their indissoluble connection."

---

## The Trinity Context

The political subordination of war is the third pole of Clausewitz's [[remarkable-trinity]] ("its element of subordination, as an instrument of policy, which makes it subject to reason alone"). However, Clausewitz warns that policy is *not* a supreme tyrant that commands the other two elements (popular passion and military chance) at will. Rather, the political aim is constrained: "the political aim is a tyrant. It must adapt itself to its chosen means"—meaning policy must operate within the limits of military feasibility, friction, and the emotional realities of the combatants.
