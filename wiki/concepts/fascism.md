# Fascism

An interwar political ideology and movement-type combining ultranationalism, a rejection of parliamentary liberalism and Marxist class struggle alike, a cult of a charismatic leader, glorification of violence and militarism, and a palingenetic ("rebirth") myth of national renewal through struggle. Named for Mussolini's Italian movement (from *fascio*, a bundle, symbol of unity and the Roman *fasces*), the term was extended by contemporaries and later scholars to describe structurally similar movements across interwar Europe — most importantly German National Socialism, though historians dispute how far Nazism should be treated as a variant of generic fascism versus a categorically distinct phenomenon given its racial-biological core and the unprecedented scale of the Holocaust.

## Historiographical Debate

Whether a coherent "generic fascism" category usefully groups Italian Fascism, German Nazism, and various other interwar movements (Falangism, the Iron Guard, British Union of Fascists, etc.) — or whether these were too nationally distinct, and Nazism too singular in its racial ideology and genocidal outcome, to be usefully generalized — remains a live historiographical debate. See [[concepts/generic-fascism]] for the influential Griffin "five-stage model" and definitional debate, and [[concepts/theories-of-fascism]] for the range of causal/explanatory frameworks (Marxist class-based theories, modernization theory, psychological/mass-society theories, and others) historians have proposed for why fascism emerged when and where it did.

Fascist movements arose across interwar Europe in response to a common cluster of conditions — the trauma and economic dislocation of the First World War, fear of Bolshevik revolution, and the perceived failures of liberal parliamentary systems — but only in Italy and Germany did fully fascist regimes seize and consolidate state power; elsewhere, movements calling themselves fascist either remained marginal or, as [[concepts/para-fascism|para-fascist]] regimes, adopted fascist trappings while remaining fundamentally conservative-authoritarian rather than revolutionary in Griffin's sense.
