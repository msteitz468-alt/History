# U.S. Civilization Program

## Definition

The federal program—missionary, educational, agricultural, legal, and commercial—intended to transform Eastern Native nations into sedentary Christian yeoman communities assimilable to the United States. Michael D. Green (CHNPA I.1 ch. 8) traces it from Henry Knox and George Washington through Jefferson, the factory system, mission schools, and the 1819 civilization appropriation, to its exhaustion and co-optation by Removal ideology in the 1820s–30s.

Framed by policymakers as humanitarian protection, it required cultural transformation so complete that most Native communities rejected or selectively adapted it. It ran in parallel with, and often rationalized, land cession and eventually [[indian-removal-eastern-dispossession-1780-1880|Removal]]. **Etic analytical category** for a bundle of policies historical actors usually called “civilization,” “improvement,” or “education.”

## Knox–Washington “expansion with honor”

Knox blamed western warfare on aggressive state/frontier expansion; peace cheaper and more honorable than war. Essential goal remained expansion into Indian lands, but rooted in honor/justice: recognize right of **occupancy**; purchase freely/voluntarily; mark and enforce boundaries; prevent encroachment. Scenario: settlers fill ceded tracts, game flees, “uncivilized” Indians decline adjacent to “civilized” Americans and willingly sell the next cession. Knox (1794) wrote that American modes of population had been “more destructive to the Indian nations than the conduct of the conquerors of Mexico and Peru”—“utter extirpation of nearly all the Indians in most populous parts of the Union.” Enlightenment premise: Natives equal in mental ability; deficiencies environmental/experiential not racial; educable. Practical side: “civilized” plow-farmers would sell surplus lands for farm investment.

Green’s periodization: **1789–1815** was the most complete trial of expansion with honor—it “succeeded brilliantly in its expansionist goal but failed to bestow the blessings of ‘civilization,’ or to meet the needs of Native Americans, and it ended not in peace but in bloody conflict” (Creek War / western alliance collapse). After 1815, policymakers paid lip service while “honor shrank further into the background as the pressure for expansion became ever more compelling.”

## Instruments

- **Trade and Intercourse Acts (1790–1802 base; 1834 rewrite):** Carefully regulated fair trade; prohibit land purchase except by public federal negotiation; define Indian-country boundary and prohibit encroachment; arrest/punish U.S. citizens for crimes in Indian country; authorize presidential gifts of agricultural tools, animals, spinning wheels, looms. Acts applied to **U.S. citizens, not Indians**—Indians outside legislative jurisdiction; mechanism of relations was the **treaty** from international diplomacy ([[treaty-sovereignty-native-north-america]]).
- **Factory system (1796–1822):** Federally financed trading posts—lower prices, controlled goods, loyalty to U.S., break Spanish Florida and British Canada economic ties; destroyed by private trading companies’ political power in 1822.
- **Agents:** From 1793, federal agents to reside among Indians; **Benjamin Hawkins** among the [[creek-muscogee|Creeks]] 1796–1816 with enormous influence via civilization lessons and political advice.
- **Civilization Act / appropriation (1819):** $10,000/year for mission schools (Calhoun expecting missionary support for government goals).
- **Treaties as vehicles:** Agricultural implements etc. appear from the Treaty of New York 1790 (Creek) and [[treaty-of-greenville-1795]] onward—first official Washington commitment to civilization policy.

## Content: gender, missions, slavery path

Civilization demanded men as farmers, wives spin/weave/keep house; ideally private land (communal-ownership break sporadic); children in school, English, Christian—education generally by evangelical Protestant missionaries; hard to separate Christian and secular teaching. Green stresses that policy **actually meant reversal of Indian gender roles** to Anglo-American norms—fundamental, intimate changes. Real spiritual problem: agricultural ritual knowledge often belonged to women; male farming defied the spiritual universe; men’s ritual knowledge linked to animals—stock raising/horse trading made more sense.

**Southern path:** Deerskin market collapse + deer decline → 1790s severe economic depression. Some southern men farmed; distinctive southern “civilization” included **African slavery**—captives, escapees, Loyalist property entered Cherokee/Creek nations; men became slaveholders as “civilized” path. Jefferson cited them as policy success. **North:** no slavery path + functioning hunt → few saw civilization as useful. Handsome Lake (from 1799) developed a Seneca prophetic **alternative** accomplishing many economic/social goals while preserving Indian identity (to his death 1815).

## Jefferson’s urgency and reversed logic

Jefferson more enthusiastic about civilization yet more deeply committed to rapid expansion (U.S. population 3.9M in 1790 → 7.2M in 1810). William Henry Harrison’s fifteen cessions 1803–1809: after French Louisiana threat passed, frenzy continued partly via **reversed** civilization logic—buy hunting lands from “uncivilized” Indians so economic necessity force civilization or starve.

## Peak “success” and nationalist response

[[cherokee-nation|Cherokees]] widely recognized as most “civilized” southern nation: ~14,000 (1826), 16,542 (1835); Sequoyah syllabary 1821; *Cherokee Phoenix* 1828; constitution 1827 → republican government 1828 proclaiming sovereignty—purpose of southern Indian nationalism: **stave off removal**. Creek/Choctaw/Chickasaw adaptations short of full U.S. model (written laws, police, Choctaw constitution 1826). Successes of civilization + emerging nationalism + land refusal produced McKenney’s “crisis in Indian affairs”: removal emerged as chosen exit. Removal debate inverted the program: civilization “failed” (esp. men refusing abandon hunting—denying women’s agriculture); isolate Indians to protect from “inevitable” decline—or, opponents: civilization worked well enough; assimilation was the implicit promise.

## Contestation

**Contested: yes.** Positions include: (1) sincere Enlightenment humanitarianism corrupted by settler greed; (2) always-instrumental land-acquisition ideology with missionary window dressing; (3) selective Native appropriation producing “too successful” sovereign competitors (Cherokee constitutionalism) that states then crushed; (4) Canadian parallel experiments (Credit River Mississauga; Bond Head’s Manitoulin segregation—“hunt, fish, and die… out of the way”; enfranchisement policy with few takers). Green’s non-extermination-design thesis coexists with documented forced marches and stockade mortality under the same policy complex—do not collapse intention into outcome or vice versa.

## Reservation-era intensification (Hoxie, CHNPA I.2 ch. 12)

After the settlement line closed (c. 1880), civilization rhetoric no longer offered removal “beyond” settlement; it paired with direct assault on remaining lands. In both the United States and Canada, Indian people faced a **common front of government officials and Christian churches**; both advocated suppression of traditional lifeways and taught that there was **no alternative** to adopting private property, capitalism, and Victorian standards of morality. Canada relied explicitly on churches for “civilization”; US mission personnel and Christian values remained integral to every reservation despite church–state separation tradition.

Instruments of this phase:
- Off-reservation boarding and industrial schools; agency day schools; compulsory enrollment (parents hid children).
- Courts of Indian Offenses (US 1883) and reservation police (from 1878) enforcing work, school attendance, and abandonment of tradition.
- Canadian potlatch ban (1884) and Indian Act regulatory regime; North-West Mounted Police.
- Allotment as replacement of communal with capitalist land tenure.
- On early Plains village reservations (Fowler, CHNPA I.2 ch. 9), “civilization” rationalization coexisted with settler theft of timber/stock, court refusal of jurisdiction, railroad speculation, and agent fraud—Susette La Flesche: farce when Whites who came to “civilize” appropriated positions prepared Indians were ready to fill.

Collier’s first-year reforms (religious freedom; end of mandatory boarding-school Christian services) and the [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]] mark a partial federal reverse of civilization-program cultural suppression, without restoring the pre-allotment land base.
