# Classical Greek Historiography (Sources for the Fifth Century)

## Scope

This page maps the **source base and method** for the Greek fifth century, following
[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]]'s opening chapter (D. M. Lewis, "Sources, Chronology,
Method"). It is the historiographical companion to the period's narrative pages and to
the standing [[herodotus-reliability]] controversy. The governing fact is **uneven
illumination**: the century is brightly lit where [[thucydides]] is present and dim
where he is not.

## Three source-zones

Lewis divides the period by the quality of its evidence:

1. **478–435 BCE (the Pentekontaetia).** Thucydides gives only a compressed, undated
   survey (i.89–118); the only continuous narrative of any size is the much later
   **Diodorus Siculus**. This is the most insecure stretch — "we frequently run short of
   events to fill the period."
2. **435–411 BCE.** [[thucydides|Thucydides']] firm, summer-and-winter framework. Here
   reconstruction is more reliable than for almost any other period of antiquity, but is
   wholly dependent on a single, highly selective author.
3. **411–404 BCE.** Thucydides breaks off mid-sentence; two continuous narratives take
   over — **Xenophon's** *Hellenica* (the only complete continuation to survive) and
   **Diodorus**, the latter now often preferred where it preserves the lost, sober
   *Hellenica Oxyrhynchia*.

## The authors

- **[[thucydides]]** (c. 460–c. 400): the critical standard — source-checking, secular,
  organized by war-years; see his own page for method, selectivity, and the
  Pentekontaetia "change of plan."
- **[[herodotus]]**: indispensable for the transition years and for the texture of early
  fifth-century Greece, but with "no open chronological scheme"; his reliability is the
  subject of [[herodotus-reliability]].
- **Diodorus Siculus** (1st c. BCE): his *Bibliotheke* summarizes **one earlier author at
  a time** — for the fifth century, **Ephorus** of Cyme (4th c.). His "years," forced to
  equate Roman consular and Athenian archon years, are mechanically unreliable: "the
  appearance of any event in Diodorus' main narrative under a particular year is not to be
  regarded as evidence for its dating." Yet behind Ephorus lie good sources (Herodotus,
  Thucydides, the *Hellenica Oxyrhynchia*), so Diodorus preserves a real tradition.
- ***Hellenica Oxyrhynchia***: papyrus fragments of a sober, intelligent continuator of
  Thucydides (possibly Cratippus), important above all as the guarantee that "a sober
  historian lies somewhere behind Diodorus."
- **Xenophon**, **Theopompus** of Chios (the polemical *Hellenica* and *Philippica*), and
  **[[plutarch]]** (whose seven relevant *Lives* — Themistocles, Aristides, Cimon,
  Pericles, Nicias, Alcibiades, Lysander — rest on wide reading but are ethical biography,
  not history).
- **Contemporary non-narrative evidence** for the late fifth century: **Old Comedy** and
  **oratory** (usable once one separates the joke or the rhetorical assertion from the
  public fact that makes it intelligible), and the **Atthides** (local Athenian
  chronicles, Hellanicus onward) with the related *Athenaion Politeia* ascribed to
  Aristotle — chiefly valuable for archon-dating.

## The epigraphic spine

Stone inscriptions partly compensate for the literary gaps, and increasingly anchor the
chronology the narratives leave loose: Athenian **decrees**, **building accounts** (the
first Parthenon year fixed to **447/6**, IG i³ 436), and above all the **tribute
quota-lists** recording the one-sixtieth of allied tribute paid to Athena from spring
**453**. The growth of the public written record after the reforms of [[ephialtes]] is
itself a datable historical fact.

## The chronology problem

The Pentekontaetia has **few fixed points**. Secure anchors are the archonship of
Calliades (**480/79**, hence Salamis 480, Plataea/Mycale 479), the war's start in **431**
(confirmed by the solar eclipse of 27 July), the **Thirty Years' Peace** of winter
446/5, and the death of **Xerxes** in August **465** (fixed by a Babylonian text).
Between them, Thucydides dates events only by vague temporal adverbs and an idiosyncratic
notion of "year," so scholars "may legitimately disagree by up to five or six years"
about events like the **battle of the Eurymedon** or the end of the **Helot Revolt** —
and far more about the death of [[pausanias-regent|Pausanias]]. The full reconstruction
turns on cross-clues such as the foundation of [[amphipolis]] (437/6) used to back-date
the disaster at Drabescus.

## Why it matters: the Athenocentric lens

Because the surviving historians, the inscriptions, the comedy and the oratory are
overwhelmingly **Athenian**, the entire period is seen, in Sallust's already-ancient
observation, through Athenian eyes magnified by Athenian literary genius. CAH V treats
this bias not merely as distortion to be corrected but as **itself a historical
phenomenon** — the efflorescence of Athenian literature and art is part of what made the
fifth century "the first Classic age of European civilization." The methodological
discipline the volume draws from Lewis is twofold: fill the gaps Thucydides leaves, but
do not lightly reinterpret what he reports.
