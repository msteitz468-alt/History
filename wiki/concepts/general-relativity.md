# General Relativity

**1915**. Albert Einstein's theory extending [[special-relativity|special
relativity]] to accelerated reference frames and gravitation, describing
gravity not as a force acting through space but as the curvature of
four-dimensional spacetime produced by mass and energy.

## Definition and Scope

Where special relativity applies only to uniform, non-accelerated motion,
general relativity incorporates acceleration and gravity through the
equivalence principle (the local indistinguishability of gravitational
acceleration from acceleration due to force) and a set of field equations
relating spacetime curvature to the distribution of mass-energy. Its most
celebrated early confirmation was the observed gravitational bending of
starlight during the 1919 solar eclipse expedition led by Arthur Eddington,
which brought Einstein international fame.

## Intellectual Genesis

Einstein pursued the theory for roughly a decade after 1905, motivated by
the desire to reconcile special relativity's rejection of absolute
simultaneity with Newtonian gravity's instantaneous action at a distance.
The mathematical formalism of Riemannian differential geometry, introduced
to Einstein by his friend and collaborator Marcel Grossmann, proved
essential to expressing spacetime curvature rigorously; Einstein completed
the final field equations in November 1915 after years of difficult,
repeatedly revised calculation.

## Key Distinctions

General relativity subsumes special relativity as a local approximation in
the absence of significant gravitational fields; it predicts phenomena
absent from Newtonian gravity, including gravitational time dilation,
light bending, the precession of Mercury's perihelion (an anomaly
Newtonian mechanics could not fully explain), gravitational waves, and black
holes.

## Historical and Scientific Significance

Beyond its observational triumphs, general relativity underlies modern
cosmology (the expanding-universe models built on Einstein's field
equations) and practical applications including the relativistic
corrections required for GPS satellite timing, illustrating a rare case of
a highly abstract theoretical framework with direct everyday technological
consequences.
