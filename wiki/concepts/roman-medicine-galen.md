# Medicine in the Roman Empire (and Galen)

The world of **learned medicine** in the High Empire — its high public profile, its competing
sects, its leading practitioners, and above all its towering figure, **Galen of Pergamum**. Treated
in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 33 (**Vivian Nutton**), who insists on restoring the **social
and intellectual context** of medicine and on the **medical pluralism** of the empire.

## Medicine as public culture

Learned Greek medicine enjoyed a **high public profile**: doctors sat with poets at the
Alexandrian Museum and its imitations, wrote history and poetry, gained reputations as
philosophers (Sextus Empiricus), and earned civic honours and benefactions. The boundary between
the physician and the cultured amateur (*philiatros*) was narrow (senators and imperial relatives
attended Galen's demonstrations and asked expert questions). Medicine became **spectacle** —
public anatomical lectures, gymnasium debates, even **medical contests** (the "Olympics" at
Ephesus). This public appetite, Nutton argues, drove the period's chief medical advance: the
**revival of anatomy** (Marinus, Satyrus, then Galen's public dissections of animals).

## The medical sects

Medicine was a **lively arena of debate**, the "sects" distinguished by **theory and philosophy
more than therapy**:

- **Empiricists** — sceptical, rejecting the investigation of causes, treating by recorded
  experience of similar past cases.
- **Methodists** — the body as atoms and pores in three states (constricted, fluid, mixed); disease
  a change in these *koinotetes*; therapy simple and teachable in six months (their accessibility a
  selling point).
- **Rationalists/Dogmatists** — holding that knowledge of a disease's **causation** (difficult, won
  by reasoning) must precede treatment; a loose grouping including Galen.

Many, like Galen, were avowed **eclectics** beholden to no school.

## The four great physicians

- **Soranus of Ephesus** (Methodist) — author of the *Gynaecology*, the largest surviving ancient
  work on women's diseases: cautious, humane, willing to modify ideal treatment for effectiveness.
- **Aretaeus of Cappadocia** (Pneumatist) — celebrated for vivid descriptions of disease (asthma,
  diabetes, tetanus), giving primacy to *pneuma* in an idiom of revived Hippocratic Ionic.
- **Rufus of Ephesus** (revived Hippocratism) — emphasizing **individuality** (the patient's whole
  way of life) and the careful interrogation of patient and neighbours; sympathetic and clear.
- **Galen of Pergamum** — the dominant figure.

## Galen

Born at **Pergamum (AD 129)**, the son of a wealthy architect (turned to medicine by a dream of
Asclepius sent to his father), Galen had "the longest medical education on record" — Pergamum,
Smyrna, Corinth, **Alexandria** (anatomy and Hippocratic study) — possible only because **inherited
wealth** freed him from charging fees. After serving as doctor to the **gladiators** of Pergamum
(cutting fatalities dramatically), he went to **Rome (162)**, where his public dissections before
senators won fame and envy; he left, returned, and became physician to **Marcus Aurelius** and the
court, attending **Commodus** and later **Septimius Severus**, surviving the plague, the tyranny,
and the civil wars to die around AD 199–216.

Nutton's portrait stresses Galen's **paradoxes** and his keys:

- **Philosophy**: "the best physician is a philosopher" — anatomy confirming Plato's tripartite
  soul and Aristotelian purpose; **"scientific demonstration"** his slogan; his anatomy a "hymn to
  the Creator" (*On the Use of Parts*).
- **Erudition** (a huge personal library) married to an insistence on **practical experience** —
  the unity of **reason and experience**, head and hand, against any single approach.
- A **limited view of medical progress**: Hippocrates had laid down the route (as Trajan the roads);
  later physicians only fill holes until medicine reaches perfection. Galen's selective, philological
  **reconstruction of "Hippocrates"** (privileging *On the Nature of Man*) proved long-lasting.
- His **pharmacology** (curiosity-driven sourcing, theory of drug "grades"), **physiology** (the
  doctrine of "faculties" and spirits), and the relentless self-promotion and prolixity that made
  him "in this Galen was no Galenist," leaving loose ends.

## Medical pluralism

Nutton's closing emphasis: the surviving texts of a few great authors **distort** the picture. The
ability to cure was not confined to learned *medici*; competence ranged enormously; **self-
medication was the norm**; magico-medical lore (Xenocrates, the magical papyri) persisted; and
**religious healing** (Asclepius — Aelius Aristides, Alexander of Abonuteichos; and the Christians'
healing message) was a ready alternative or complement. The Roman empire was a world of **medical
pluralism**, in which Galen's Hippocratism was one strand, however dominant it later became in
Byzantium, the medieval West, and Islam.

## Significance

The High Empire was the **golden age of Graeco-Roman medicine** — its learned form fashionable and
publicly celebrated, its sects in vigorous debate, and its supreme synthesizer, Galen, fixing a
medical system (anatomy, physiology, pharmacology, Hippocratic theory) that would **dominate
Western and Islamic medicine for over a millennium**.
