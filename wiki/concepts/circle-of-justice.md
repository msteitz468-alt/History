## Definition and Scope

The "Circle of Justice" (Arabic: dāʾirat al-ʿadl) is a governance ideal, of pre-Islamic Near Eastern origin, that describes the mutually sustaining relationship between ruler, military, fiscal resources, prosperous subjects, and justice. In its Islamic instantiation, the circle was mediated by the [[sharia|Sharīʿa]] — so that justice, the axis of the circle, was definitionally inseparable from the application of divine law through the jurists. The technical compound *siyāsa sharʿiyya* ("governance in accordance with the Sharīʿa") became the juridical expression of this ideal: the ruler's discretionary political authority, exercised within the boundaries set by the Sharīʿa and its scholarly interpreters.

These two concepts — the Circle of Justice as a cultural-political ideal and siyāsa sharʿiyya as its legal-technical implementation — constitute the organizing framework through which Hallaq analyzes Islamic governance from the decline of the ʿAbbāsid caliphate through the late Ottoman and Qajar periods.

## The Circle Explained

Hallaq articulates the Circle's logic as follows:

> "The Circle begins with the idea that no political sovereignty can be attained without the military; yet no military can be sustained without financial resources. These resources furthermore can be raised only through levying taxes, which presupposes continuous economic productivity on the part of the subjects; but to maintain a level of prosperity that can sustain taxable income, justice needs to be ensured, and this in part means controlling the excesses of provincial officials whose vision of justice may be overshadowed by personal power and rapacity. Thus, to be attained, justice requires public order, the all-important social harmony, and control of abusive and greedy government servants. To achieve all this, the Sharīʿa, clearly the axis of governance, points the way. But the Sharīʿa cannot be implemented without political sovereignty, and this cannot be attained without the military. Here, the Circle is joined."

The Circle could be entered from two directions. **From the ruler's perspective**, it began with sovereignty and military power, and moved through fiscal extraction to justice as the means of sustaining revenue-generating prosperity. **From the jurists' perspective**, the Circle began with the Sharīʿa: "Political authority thus becomes at once subservient to, and necessary for, the legal order." On the ground, Hallaq argues, "the reality was a synthesis between the two readings of the Circle, although during the later periods, especially during the Mamlūk and Ottoman regimes, the balance of influence stood in favor of the sovereign's mode."

## Siyāsa Sharʿiyya: Scope and Content

In theory and in practice, *siyāsa sharʿiyya* represented:

1. **Enforcement powers**: The ruler's authority to enforce the qāḍī's judgments — since the Sharīʿa court issued judgments but depended on executive power for their implementation.
2. **Supplementary legislation**: Administrative regulations addressing the regime's machinery — appointments, jurisdictional limits, discipline of officials, tax collection, public order, land use, and certain criminal law matters. The Ottoman *qānūn* (sultanic law) was the most elaborated form of this supplementary legislation.
3. **Curbing official abuses**: Control of provincial governors and tax-farmers whose extortion threatened the fiscal-productive equilibrium the Circle required. The courts of grievances (*maẓālim*) were the institutional expression of this curbing function.

The qualification "sharʿiyya" in the compound was deliberate: "Such powers were not only consistent with the dictates of religious law, but could in no way constitute an infringement thereof if properly exercised." Siyāsa was not secular law set against divine law; it was divinely sanctioned political administration carried out within — and bounded by — the Sharīʿa.

## The Dual Legal Order: Sharīʿa and Qānūn

A key feature of the Circle of Justice in practice was the coexistence of two legal codes:

- **Sharīʿa courts** (*qāḍī*-presided): General jurisdiction over the civilian population in virtually all civil and personal-status matters. See [[qadi-judiciary]].
- **Siyāsa / ʿurf courts** (maẓālim, dīvān-e shāh, etc.): Jurisdiction over government servants, military, and capital crimes that threatened public order.

Hallaq stresses that this was not a conflict between religious and secular law but a complementary duality: "The Sharīʿa and the qānūn had far more in common than they differed upon." The Ottoman *qānūn* of Suleyman the Lawgiver, for example, explicitly prohibited executive officials from imprisoning or injuring any person "without the cognizance of the [Sharīʿa] judge." The standard Ottoman formulaic decree required justice "according to the Shar and qānūn" — consistently conjunctive, never oppositional.

A structural feature of the Ottoman system was that "the qāḍī stood as the exclusive agent of the qānūn's enforcement" vis-à-vis the civilian population, making him simultaneously the interpreter of divine law and the implementing agent of sultanic law.

## The Civilian/Military Divide in Justice

One of the Circle's most striking practical implications was a systematic legal asymmetry: civilian subjects enjoyed the full protections of Sharīʿa due process, while the ruler's own servants — soldiers, governors, officials — were subject to the much harsher and summary sultanic code:

> "While no man or woman, Muslim, Christian or Jew in the civilian population could be punished without a Sharīʿa court trial — largely independent of the sovereign's will — the sultanic code was absolute with regard to the sovereign himself and his men."

This explains the phenomenon of *muṣādara* — confiscation of a statesman's property upon death or dismissal from office — as a systemic feature rather than a tyrannical abuse: "What had been given through the good offices of the salṭana was retrieved by the same offices, and this included property and life as well."

## The Accessibility of Imperial Justice

A culturally embedded feature of the Circle was that the sultan's court of grievances was formally accessible to any subject. Hallaq describes "a line of communication always left open between the tax-paying subjects and the imperial order." The governor's assembly (dīwān) in provincial capitals included the chief [[mufti-and-fatwa|muftī]]s, qāḍīs, tax-collectors, neighborhood shaykhs, and guild leaders alongside imperial officials — making it simultaneously an administrative and a representative body in which local interests were articulated against the center.

## Dynastic Applications

### Mamlūks (648/1250–923/1517)
The Mamlūk Dār al-ʿAdl in Cairo was the highest court, presided over by the sultan or his viceroy, with the four chief justices (one per [[madhhab]]) and a distinguished [[mufti-and-fatwa|muftī]] in permanent attendance. The *ḥājib* (viceroy) tried emirs and government servants in siyāsa proceedings, while the Sharīʿa court handled virtually all civilian litigation. Criminal cases were tried more often at the Dār al-ʿAdl than in Sharīʿa courts — the one area where the siyāsa jurisdiction extended beyond government servants.

### Ottomans (1389–1922)
The Ottomans brought the Circle of Justice to its most elaborated institutional form. Hallaq argues that Bayezid I (r. 1389–1401) initiated a qualitatively new "ruling partnership" with the legists that became an "entrenched paradigm of governance for the two centuries after his death." Key Ottoman contributions to the Circle's implementation:

- Formal adoption of [[hanafi-school|Ḥanafism]] as the official madhhab, creating a unified legal administration across the Empire.
- The office of Şeyhülislam (Shaykh al-Islām) as head of the judiciary, with power to appoint and supervise qāḍīs.
- Commitment of courts to fixed physical courthouses (the first dynasty to do so in Islamic history).
- Preservation of the qāḍī's court register (*dīwān*) as a public document — explaining why vast numbers of Ottoman court records survive while almost none from earlier periods do.
- *Qānūnnāme* legislation at universal, provincial, and local levels that implemented the Sharīʿa's emphasis on local custom while imposing imperial administrative unity.

### Safavid and Qajar Iran
A similar structure obtained in Iran, with Sharīʿa courts under the Shaykh al-Islām handling general jurisdiction and *ʿurf* courts under the governor managing government servants and capital crimes. The Safavids and Qajars differed from the Ottomans in maintaining "a degree of separation between the military/political sovereign and the Sharīʿa establishment" and in not developing a comparably centralized legal aristocracy. Nevertheless, "Persia's Safavid, Zand and Qājār rulers unfailingly continued to uphold their duties as prescribed to them by siyāsa sharʿiyya."

### Mughals
Under the Mughals (1556–1757), a *ṣadr* (chief judicial officer, not a chief muftī) headed the provincial judicial system, appointing qāḍīs, *muḥtasib*s, and [[waqf]] administrators. Some muftīs were appointed in official capacity as legal advisors. The Mughal system's distinctive feature was the power to allocate lands as fiscal remuneration for judicial service — "unknown in the western domains of Islamdom."

## The Jurists' View: Law as the Superior Partner

While rulers read the Circle as beginning with sovereignty, jurists consistently read it as beginning with the Sharīʿa. This was not merely theoretical. The English scholar Alexander Dow observed of the late Mughals that the Sharīʿa "circumscribed the will of the Prince" and that "the House of Timur always observed [the law]; and the practice of ages had rendered some ancient usages and edicts so sacred in the eyes of the people, that no prudent monarch would choose to violate either by a wanton act of power." Hallaq cites this as evidence that the jurists' reading was not merely aspirational — it described real constraints on political conduct.

The "sovereignty" of the Sharīʿa over the political order was embedded in the conception of divine ownership: "Any human claim to earthly possession must thus be either metaphorical or a plain usurpation of the divine Kingdom or a portion thereof. For a man to rule without incriminating himself in the irredeemable sin of usurpation, he must act as the guardian and administrator of the Law."

## Relationship to Other Concepts

- [[sharia]]: The substantive law that defines the content of justice the Circle must deliver.
- [[madhhab]]: The four schools whose jurists staffed the Circle's enforcement apparatus.
- [[mufti-and-fatwa]]: The jurisconsult whose opinions provided the authoritative legal basis for court rulings, including rulings on the conduct of rulers.
- [[qadi-judiciary]]: The court system that was simultaneously the Sharīʿa's institutional home and the Circle's smallest administrative unit.
- [[ulama]]: The scholarly class whose epistemic authority gave the Circle its legitimacy in the eyes of Muslim populations.
- [[waqf]]: The endowment mechanism through which rulers funded the [[madrasa|madrasas]] that trained the jurists who staffed the Circle.
- [[taqlid-and-ijtihad]]: The juristic methodology that governed how law evolved within the Circle's legal order.

## Contested Dimensions

The Circle of Justice concept is analytically contested in modern scholarship in two ways:

1. **Originality**: Hallaq notes the Circle "is said to originate" in pre-Islamic Near Eastern governance ideals. Whether its Islamic instantiation is a genuine transformation of this ideal or its continuation under new theological legitimation is debated.
2. **Descriptive vs. prescriptive**: Whether the Circle accurately described the actual operation of justice in Muslim polities, or was primarily a legitimizing ideology obscuring more brutal realities of extraction and coercion, is a live question. Hallaq's position is that it was both — an operative reality and a legitimizing framework, with the balance shifting across dynasties and periods.

## Historiography

The Circle of Justice has attracted increasing attention in Islamic political and legal history since the 1980s. Hallaq's treatment is distinctive in integrating it as the organizing frame for the entire pre-modern period of Islamic law rather than treating it as a minor political theory trope. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection is strong on the Ottoman implementation through legal history monographs (El-Nahal, Gerber, Marcus, Peirce) and on the Mamlūk period. Safavid and Qajar practice is moderately covered; Mughal coverage is weaker. The theoretical literature on siyāsa sharʿiyya (Māwardī's *al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyya* and Ibn Taymiyya's treatment) is accessible through primary sources in the collection. See [[hallaq-sharia-2009]].
