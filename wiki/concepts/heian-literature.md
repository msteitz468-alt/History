# Heian Literature (Vernacular and Kanbun)

The twin literary traditions of the [[heian-period|Heian]] court: the official, male,
Chinese-language **kanbun** tradition (kanshi verse, dynastic histories, legal codes)
and the vernacular, largely female, [[kana-script|kana]]-script tradition that produced
the most celebrated works of Japanese literature. The two coexisted and interpenetrated;
vernacular poetry, above all, drew constantly on Chinese models even as it became
distinctively Japanese. Documented in the [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian|Cambridge
History of Japan, Vol. 2]].

## Structural overview

There were in effect two kinds of literature at the Heian court:
- **Poetry** (*waka* and *kanshi*): the recognized public genre, appropriate to both
  men and women, governed by imperial anthologies, formal poetry contests, and
  the daily obligation of poetic exchange.
- **Narrative prose**: largely relegated to the rear palace (*kōkyū*) — the consorts'
  and princesses' apartments — and therefore primarily female territory. Imperial
  ladies presided over "the only catholic literary centers of the day, embracing both
  poetry and prose in their interests."

The social mechanism that produced the great prose works was therefore the **salon**
of imperial consorts, who gathered talented women as ladies-in-waiting, patronized
literary activity, and provided the paper (a luxury item) and the audience that made
extended writing possible. Without the salons, [[sei-shonagon]] wrote the *Pillow Book*
only because "her mistress happened to make her a present of a large quantity of paper."

## Chinese-language literature (kanbun)

See [[chinese-learning-heian]] for the full treatment. In poetry:
- Three **imperial kanshi anthologies** were compiled under Emperor Saga (814–827),
  marking the period when Chinese verse displaced Japanese poetry from public life.
- **Honchō monzui** (Fujiwara no Akihira, ca. 1058–1065) became the prestige anthology
  of Japanese kanbun belles-lettres, drawing on the best compositions of the Daigo and
  Murakami courts.

## Poetry: waka and the Kokinshū tradition

The Japanese-language poetic tradition is dominated by the **tanka** (31-syllable short poem),
the sole standard form after the early Heian period. For the full account of the revival
of waka, the [[kokinshu|Kokinshū]] (ca. 905), and the development of the tradition through
the Senzaishū, see [[kokinshu]].

Key features of the waka tradition in Heian:
- **Social function**: every aristocrat was expected to compose and exchange poems
  for greeting, thanks, condolence, love, and ceremony. Poetry was "the main element
  in a note of invitation, thanks, or condolence, or as a well-bred response to a
  moving incident, or as a witty escape from an awkward situation."
- **Poetry contests** (*uta-awase*): competitive events sponsored by court salons,
  in which Left and Right teams presented poems on assigned topics and judges awarded
  victory. These replaced social competitions of other kinds from about 1085.
- **Screen poems** (*byōbu uta*): waka inscribed on folding screens decorated with
  Japanese paintings — the synthesis of literary and visual art.
- ***Mono no aware***: the pervading sensibility — "the pathos of things," the bittersweet
  awareness of transience — that the best waka distills into 31 syllables.

## The literary diary (nikki)

The literary diary, written in kana, is a distinctively Japanese form with no precise
Chinese equivalent:
- **Tosa nikki** (Ki no Tsurayuki, 935): the oldest extant literary diary; the male
  author assumed a female persona, recording a 55-day journey in verse-studded prose.
- **Kagerō nikki** ("Gossamer Journal," ca. 982, by "Michitsuna's mother"): "the misery
  and resentment of a neglected wife" — powerful realism and tight thematic unity.
- **Izumi Shikibu nikki** (ca. 1004?, ascribed to Izumi Shikibu, b. 976?): a lyrical
  account of the protagonist's love affair with an imperial prince.
- **Murasaki Shikibu nikki** (ca. 1010, by [[murasaki-shikibu]]): focuses on activities
  surrounding the birth of an heir to the throne; the prime window onto the Fujiwara
  apogee from the inside.
- **Sarashina nikki** (ca. 1060?, by "Sugawara no Takasue's daughter"): the uneventful
  life of a romantic, religious woman.

Every known major diary author except Tsurayuki was a lady-in-waiting to a consort or
princess at some time in her life — evidence of the salon's custodial function.

## The poem tale (uta monogatari)

Brief, elegant anecdotes about aristocratic life, centering on one or more poems.
- **Ise monogatari** (*Tales of Ise*, anonymous, 10th century): the oldest and best —
  "a classic expression of Heian aesthetic ideals, a poetic handbook, and a compendium
  of types of courtly love, written in simple, chaste language."
- Two other anonymous tenth-century collections. All appear to have been compiled by men.

## The romance (monogatari)

Longer prose fiction, ranging from brief to very long:
- **Taketori monogatari** (*Tale of the Bamboo Cutter*, ca. 900?): the oldest extant
  romance — "a charming fairy tale about a moon maiden and her earthly suitors."
- **Utsubo monogatari** (*Tale of the Hollow Tree*, ca. 970–1000?): a rambling story
  of upper-class life, unified by the miraculous power of music.
- **Ochikubo monogatari** (*Tale of the Sunken Room*, ca. 985?): a Cinderella story
  with "realistic detail and occasional earthy humor."
- **The Tale of Genji** ([[tale-of-genji|Genji monogatari]], ca. 1001–1020): see dedicated page.

Of approximately eighty romance titles known from the ninth and tenth centuries alone,
nearly all have perished. "Most of the romances, like the paintings that illustrated them,
vanished with the old life."

## The miscellany (zuihitsu)

**[[sei-shonagon|Sei Shōnagon's]] *Makura no sōshi*** (*Pillow Book*, ca. 993–1001?) is
the sole surviving example of the miscellany genre — "a jumble of reminiscences about the
author's days at court, random observations on people, nature, and life in general, and
innumerable lists of things." It is the gay, high-spirited counterpart to the psychological
depth of the *Genji*.

## The historical tale (rekishi monogatari)

A vernacular genre created by drawing on the monogatari style for historical subject matter:
- **Eiga monogatari** (*A Tale of Flowering Fortunes*, started ca. 1030?): attributed
  to **Akazome Emon** (b. ca. 960?) — "her country's first vernacular historian," a
  naively enthusiastic celebration of Fujiwara no Michinaga. Invaluable as a social
  document. Inspired a successor:
- **Okagami** (*The Great Mirror*, ca. 1086–1125?): the superior work, written by
  an anonymous male author, which not only describes but explains Michinaga's success
  through anecdote and structural argument.

## Setsuwa (anecdote collections)

The compilation of short factual or religious tales — the *setsuwa* genre — was primarily
a male, Buddhist, and utilitarian enterprise, outside the salon milieu:
- **Konjaku monogatari[shū]** (*Tales of Times Now Past*, ca. 1120?): over a thousand
  tales covering Buddhist and secular subjects from Japan, China, and India; "a
  preachers' manual" and a forerunner of the medieval cultural mix.

## Significance and decline

Heian literature represents, in the Cambridge History's assessment, "the single most
impressive accomplishment of Heian civilization" (meaning the *Genji* in particular),
and demonstrates the paradox that the most enduring Japanese literary achievements
came from women writing in a script and genre that men officially undervalued. The
decline of the Fujiwara salons after about 1075 — as Michinaga's granddaughters ceased
to dominate the imperial household — marks the end of the golden age; later anthologies
like the Goshūishū depart from the Kokinshū style, and the approach of the medieval era
is heralded by the rise of *imayō* songs, Buddhist *setsuwa*, and the warrior class.

## Historiography

The CHJ chapter treats Heian literature with exceptional detail and analytical rigor,
emphasizing the social institutions (salons, poetry contests, the imperial anthology
system) that produced the literature, and the gender dynamics that made women the
principal agents of literary innovation. The collection is strong through the CHJ series,
Sansom's cultural history, and the primary sources (Waley's Genji, McCullough's Kokin
Wakashū). For post-1185 literature (Noh, renga, haiku), the collection is thinner.
