# Sovietization of Eastern Europe (1944–1953)

The process by which the Soviet Union imposed Communist regimes and Stalinist political, economic, and social structures on the countries of Eastern Europe in the aftermath of World War II.

## Key Features

- Installation of "people's democracies" through coalition governments that were gradually purged and subordinated to Moscow.
- Economic integration via Comecon and emulation of Soviet five-year plans.
- Security through bilateral treaties and, later, the Warsaw Pact.
- Suppression of non-Communist parties, churches, and civil society.
- Varied by country (e.g., more violent in Poland and Hungary, different path initially in Yugoslavia and later Albania).

## Historiography

Traditional views saw it as straightforward Soviet expansionism. Revisionists emphasized security concerns and local Communist agency. Post-revisionist accounts (including in Leffler/Westad Vol. I) stress the interplay of Soviet security needs, ideology, local conditions, and Western responses (e.g., Marshall Plan accelerating the process).

From Range 02 (Naimark): Initial 1944–45 "people’s democracy" phase was "one of the most important in the entire history of postwar Eastern Europe" but "difficult to categorize." Policy: "no Communist revolution... no dictatorship of the proletariat; no workers’ councils... Instead, the East European revolution would see the completion of the 1848 bourgeois democratic revolution... 'people’s democracy'" via coalitions. Soviet involvement varied sharply: "absolutely central" in Poland/Romania/Eastern Germany; "predominantly due to internal... causes" in Yugoslavia/Albania.

Acceleration: After Marshall Plan rejection, "The intensification of the Cold War terminated any lingering restraints on the full-scale Sovietization of the region." Cominform (1947) and Comecon (1949) consolidated.

Djilas (recording Stalin): “This war is not as in the past: whoever occupies a territory also imposes on it his own social system. Everyone imposes his own system as far as his army can reach. It cannot be otherwise.”

## From Range 03 (Naimark continuation in Leffler & Westad Vol. I)

- Cominform and shift to full Stalinization (1947): Soviets "countered the perceived threat of the Marshall Plan by calling a meeting of European Communist Parties in Poland at Szklarska Poręba in September 1947. A new organization was set up, the Communist Information Bureau (Cominform), ostensibly to share information among Communist Parties, but in fact to monitor compliance with Soviet directives. Stalin’s deputy for ideological questions, Andrei Zhdanov, delivered his famous 'two camps' speech, in which he insisted that Communist Parties line up with and, by implication, mimic the Communist Party of the Soviet Union." "This brought to an end the period of 'the anti-Fascist, democratic revolution' and the idea of national roads to Communism." "Soon after the Cominform meeting, Soviet officers took a more active part in the development of East European armies." Comecon formed January 1949.
- Acceleration factors: "If the Cold War was born in the tensions surrounding the development of the Marshall Plan, its angry rejection by the Soviets, and the establishment of the Cominform, it was accelerated by the February 1948 coup in Czechoslovakia, the beginning of the Berlin blockade in June 1948, and the eruption of the Soviet–Yugoslav split in the summer of 1948."
- Show trials: "Discipline in the newly emerging Soviet bloc would have to be ensured by the traditional Stalinist means of purges and show trials." "Tito the phantom defendant of the East European trials." Key cases: Albanian Koçi Xoxe (convicted Titoist, executed May 1949); Hungarian László Rajk (Titoist and Western spy, executed); Bulgarian Traicho Kostov (Titoist plot, executed Dec 1949); Romanian Lucrețiu Pătrășcanu (central figure in Gheorghiu-Dej plot, condemned and shot after refusing to confess); Czechoslovak Rudolf Slánský (Oct 1952, "ugly anti-Jewish aspect", sentenced to death and hanged with "Zionist co-conspirators" tied to Noel Field). "Soviet 'advisers' helped prepare and mastermind the purge trials... sometimes directly participated in the interrogations and drew up lists of those to be arrested."
- "Little Stalins": "The result of the purges was that 'Little Stalins' emerged throughout the region, Communist Party chiefs such as Bolesław Bierut (Poland), Rákosi (Hungary), Gheorgiu-Dej (Romania), Ulbricht (GDR), Klement Gottwald (Czechoslovakia), Hoxha (Albania), and Vulko Chervenkov (Bulgaria). Although their powers and domestic images resembled that of Stalin, their persons and policies were strictly subordinated to the Kremlin."
- Soviet advisers and control: "Soviet participation in the organization of the power structures of these countries, in cadre politics, and in implementing economic, cultural, and social policies all served the cause of the successful Sovietization of Eastern Europe." "Soviet advisers in the region – ranging from guest professors of philosophy in the universities to artillery specialists in the militaries – also played a crucial role..." "All the countries of Eastern Europe, from the most highly industrialized, such as Czechoslovakia and the GDR, to the least developed, such as Albania, imported Soviet advisers for an astonishing variety of purposes." "the 'Soviet way' was the only right way to do things."
- Ideological: "In the era of 'high Stalinism' at the end of the 1940s and beginning of the 1950s, the Soviets demanded thoroughgoing ideological imitation... Local Soviet representatives sent 'report cards' back home on how well the East Europeans matched up in their understanding of Marxism-Leninism."
- Marshall Plan pressure (detailed): "In a little over two weeks, the Soviet leadership changed its directives three times in encrypted telegrams... Initially, the instructions stipulated that all the people’s democracies should express interest in the plan. Later, the Soviets suggested that their representatives should participate... but should disagree with its substance and withdraw... Finally, Moscow directed the people’s democracies not to participate at all..." Only complication Czechoslovakia: "Beneš and his associates were completely committed to the Marshall Plan process. But at a meeting in Moscow on July 9, 1947, Stalin ruthlessly pressured the Czechoslovak government delegation, forcing them to reject outright the Marshall Plan..." Masaryk quote as above.
- Historiography: "Some scholars criticize the concepts of Sovietization or Stalinization because they overly simplify the complex processes of give-and-take between the Soviet Union and its East European subordinates. They prefer the idea of 'self-Sovietization' or 'self-Stalinization,' terms that capture the fact that the East Europeans adapted and used Soviet models themselves without direct instructions or coercion."
