# Slavophilism

**Slavophilism** was a romantic nationalist intellectual movement that arose in Russian educated society in the 1830s and 1840s. Opposing the Westernization of Russia, the Slavophiles asserted that Russia possessed a unique, morally superior civilization based on the Orthodox Christian faith, the peasant commune (*obshchina*), and the family, and called for a return to native Russian principles.

## Definition

Slavophilism was an intellectual philosophy that utilized German romantic idealism (particularly Schelling's philosophy of history) to analyze Russian development. Its core tenets included:
- **Sobornost (Spiritual Community):** The concept of organic unity in freedom and love, which they believed existed in the Orthodox Church and the peasant commune. They contrasted this with the Western rationalist principles of legalism, necessity, and compulsion.
- **The Peasant Commune (*Obshchina*):** Celebrated by Constantine Aksakov as "a moral choir," the commune was seen as a harmonious, egalitarian social institution where land was shared in brotherly love, free from Western concepts of individual property and state compulsion.
- **Antagonism toward Peter the Great:** The Slavophiles argued that Peter I had deformed Russia's organic development by introducing Western rationalism, legalism, and bureaucracy, which split educated society from the common people.
- **Consultative Autocracy:** They supported autocracy as a functional necessity to relieve society of the burden of political authority, but insisted that the government must not interfere in the life of the spirit. Their formula was: "To the government, the power of authority; to the people, the power of opinion." They demanded the abolition of serfdom and full freedom of conscience and speech.

## Context and Origin

The movement developed in the salons of Moscow in constant debate with the **Westernizers**. The leading Slavophiles—Aleksey Khomyakov, Ivan and Peter Kireevsky, Constantine and Ivan Aksakov, and George Samarin—were wealthy landowners who were deeply educated in Western philosophy but sought to define a distinct path for Russia.

In their analysis of the West, the Slavophiles argued that the Roman Catholic Church had chosen rationalism and authority over love and harmony, leading to Protestantism and the materialistic, revolutionary disease of Western civilization. They believed that Russia, by returning to its pre-Petrine Orthodox roots, could cure itself and offer salvation to the discordant West.

## Limitations and Critiques

Slavophilism faced significant critiques and practical contradictions:
- **Government Persecution:** Despite their support for autocracy, the Slavophiles' opposition to the bureaucracy, their call for the emancipation of the serfs, and their demand for free speech made them targets of imperial censorship and police surveillance.
- **Romanticization of the Past:** Critics (especially Westernizers) pointed out that the Slavophiles' vision of pre-Petrine Russia was a romantic myth that ignored the historical violence and backwardness of the Muscovite state.
- **Anarchistic Utopianism:** Their complete rejection of legalism and formal institutions ("religious anarchism") was politically unrealistic for a modern empire.
- **Transformation into Pan-Slavism:** In the late nineteenth century, the early, idealistic Slavophilism degenerated into a chauvinistic, state-supported **Pan-Slavism** (as formulated by Danilevsky), which was used to justify imperial expansion in the Balkans.
