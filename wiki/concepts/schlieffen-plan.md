# Schlieffen Plan

The "Schlieffen Plan" refers to the operational concept developed under Alfred von Schlieffen for a rapid, decisive campaign against France in the event of a two-front war, to be followed by a shift of forces eastward against Russia. It is one of the most debated and mythologized documents in military history.

## Definition and Scope

The core idea was to avoid a prolonged two-front war by achieving a quick victory in the west through a massive right-wing envelopment: the bulk of German forces would swing through neutral Belgium and northern France, envelop Paris, cut French communications, and destroy the French armies against their own eastern frontier or the Swiss border. The left wing in Alsace-Lorraine would be deliberately weak, acting as a pivot and possible lure.

It was never a single, fixed operations order. Planning was continuous, incremental, and contingent on railway capacity, annual war games, diplomatic circumstances, and force levels. The famous December 1905 memorandum often called “the Schlieffen Plan” was a private paper written (or redrafted) after Schlieffen’s retirement, partly to argue for army expansion.

## Evolution and 1914 Implementation

- Elder Moltke (pre-1891) had favoured more cautious, counter-offensive approaches and accepted the possibility of long wars. "The protracted French resistance of 1871 suggested to him that a victorious campaign in the west could not be settled sufficiently quickly to allow the possibility of dealing with first one opponent and then the other... he ominously referred to the historical analogies of a war lasting seven or even thirty years." He inclined to ride a French offensive then counter-attack or defend west while offensive east.
- Waldersee and especially Schlieffen shifted toward offensive solutions on both fronts when possible, enabled by expanding rail networks. Schlieffen allocated 48 divisions west/15 east initially (1893-4 plan); increasingly left east to Austria. By 1905 he explored massive right-wing envelopment through Belgium to avoid fixed defences: "Schlieffen’s answer was not to confront France’s fixed defences, but to go round them."
- Schlieffen emphasised the decisive battle of annihilation (Vernichtungsschlacht / Kesselschlacht) on the model of Cannae, but selectively. "At Cannae Hannibal had left his centre weak... But there were methodological problems here too. Hannibal’s success lay in the realm of battlefield tactics; the fire-effect of modern armies might prevent the closing on the centre..." His use of history was "a bogus intellectualism." Staff rides 1904-5 repeatedly failed to deliver decisive victory; right wing often too weak.
- The famous December 1905 "Schlieffen plan" memorandum "is nothing of the sort. It is a memorandum written for his successor and for the war minister... Ostensibly written in December 1905... it was probably actually written in the following month, after he had retired." Partly to argue for army increases. Plans were always contingent: "There was no single solution, no one plan."
- Moltke the Younger modified the 1914 deployment: strengthened left (added 7th Army of 8 divisions for upper Alsace to support Lorraine), narrowed right (no Dutch Maastricht appendix to preserve neutral trade route for long war; must seize Liège by day 3), created multiple options rather than one categorical plan. "Moltke’s plan had at least two different options—the one adumbrated by Schlieffen, and the other in which an advance through Belgium could create the conditions for success elsewhere, probably in Lorraine." Balance shifted from 7:1 (1905 memo) to 3:1 right:left. "Moltke was even more persuaded that the French would not conform to a German agenda." He devoted more to intelligence and anticipated French attacks into Lorraine or Ardennes.

These modifications, combined with French resistance, Belgian resistance at Liège (11 days not 2: "the siege of Liège had lasted eleven days, not two"), British Expeditionary Force intervention, logistical overstretch, intelligence failures, and command frictions (e.g. Kluck subordinated to Bülow), led to the Battle of the Frontiers defeats for the Allies but no German annihilation victory, setting up the Marne. Moltke's pessimism ("We are not superior to the French") contrasted with Schlieffen's fixed convictions. "Schlieffen’s inner certainty about the correctness of his own prescriptions; Moltke, on the other hand, was reluctant to be dogmatic."

## Execution in the Battle of the Frontiers (grounded exclusively in assigned Strachan Ch. 3 range)

The "Battle of the Frontiers" (14–26 August 1914) was the collective opening: French offensives in Alsace-Lorraine (Mulhouse, Morhange) reversed with heavy losses ("The French 1st and 2nd armies advanced into devastating fire"); Ardennes attacks in columns under mist suffered catastrophic casualties (3rd Colonial Division: 228 officers + 10,275 other ranks in one day at Bertrix); Sambre/Charleroi saw Lanrezac committed to unplanned defensive battle after dithering; Mons and Le Cateau were successful British delaying actions enabled by German intelligence failures. By evening 23 August "the battle of the frontiers was over" and Joffre reported the shift "from the offensive to the defensive." "Our object must be to last out as long as possible, trying to wear the enemy out..."

The plan's own assumptions (French passivity, Belgian alignment with Germany, rapid Liège fall, decisive envelopment before Russian readiness) were falsified in execution.

**Marne and transition to stalemate (grounded exclusively in assigned Strachan Ch. 3 range):** Joffre's 25 Aug rail concentration created decisive local superiority on the left: 41 allied divisions vs German right wing by 6 Sep. "On 23 August the three armies constituting the German right wing could muster 24.5 divisions against 17.5 allied divisions: on 6 September they confronted forty-one allied divisions." Kluck (ahead of Bülow) turned south-east after Guise but then shifted two corps 100 km to the Ourcq to envelop Maunoury — opening the gap between 1st and 2nd armies that the BEF entered. "The effect of Kluck’s move was to open a gap between the 1st and 2nd German armies." Hentsch (OHL intelligence head) visited and, after Bülow decided retreat, ordered the 1st Army to conform (due north to avoid BEF). Moltke ordered general retreat to the Aisne. "The Marne was a French victory. The French had to manoeuvre in order to win; they had to fight in order to check the Germans... Germany had failed to secure the quick victory on which its war plan rested. From now on it was committed to a war on two fronts." Falkenhayn continued to seek envelopment on the extreme right but "troops were fed into the right wing in penny packets" due to rail limits and broader front needs. By 25 Nov the western armies were ordered to form defensive positions. Post-Marne, German army "could not recognize that it had been defeated" and blamed individuals (Kluck, Bülow, Hentsch, Moltke, Hausen) rather than the plan or strategy. "In 1914 the right wing was as strong as the logistics of the Schlieffen plan permitted it to be."

## Historiography and Controversy

Post-1918 German accounts (Groener, Kuhl, Förster, and official histories) often portrayed Schlieffen’s concept as a near-perfect blueprint betrayed by Moltke. Liddell Hart popularised a simplified “revolving door” image. Later scholarship (including Strachan) emphasises:

- The plan’s own internal problems (underestimation of space, time, logistics, and enemy will).
- That there was no single sacred “plan” — only evolving staff studies.
- That operational planning was disconnected from grand strategy, economic mobilisation, and political objectives.
- The role of railway timetables in locking in early decisions.

The Schlieffen Plan thus stands as the classic example of operational brilliance pursued in strategic and political isolation — a theme that recurs in 20th-century German (and other) war planning.
