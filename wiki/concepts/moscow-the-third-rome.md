# Moscow the Third Rome
## Definition
**Moscow the Third Rome** is an ideological and religious concept asserting that Moscow (and the Russian Tsardom) is the historical and spiritual successor to Rome and Constantinople as the sole remaining guardian of the true Christian (Eastern Orthodox) faith. 

The core of the doctrine is summarized in Abbot Philotheus's formulation: the first Rome fell due to heresy (the Roman Catholic Church); the second Rome (Constantinople) was conquered by the infidel Ottoman Turks; Moscow is the Third Rome, which will stand permanently, and there will never be a fourth.

## Context and Origin
The doctrine arose in the early sixteenth century in the wake of two major civilizational events:
1. **The Council of Florence (1439 CE):** The Byzantine Greek clergy's acceptance of union with Rome was viewed in Russia as a betrayal of Orthodoxy.
2. **The Fall of Constantinople (1453 CE):** The Ottoman conquest of the Byzantine capital was interpreted by the Russians as a divine punishment for the Greeks' betrayal at Florence.

In **1510 CE**, Abbot **Philotheus** (Filofei) of Pskov wrote a letter to Grand Prince **Basil III** of Moscow, outlining the Third Rome concept. In its original context, the letter was a pastoral and theological warning: it urged the Grand Prince to govern righteously, protect the Orthodox Church, and preserve the true faith, warning that as the last Christian ruler, he bore an immense responsibility before God (l. 1803).

## Limitations and Critiques
* **Ecclesiastical vs. Imperial Meaning:** While modern scholars and polemicists often cite "Moscow the Third Rome" as evidence of early Russian imperialist ideology and aggressive foreign policy, historical analysis indicates that the doctrine was originally ecclesiastical and eschatological, not political. Philotheus was concerned with the preservation of the Church and the purity of faith, not territorial expansion.
* **Lack of State Endorsement:** The grand princes and tsars of Moscow never officially endorsed or incorporated the "Third Rome" doctrine into their state ideology or foreign policy. In their international relations, Muscovite rulers remained pragmatic, focusing on the recovery of the Rurikid patrimony of Kievan Rus' rather than claiming the universal Byzantine imperial inheritance (l. 1803).
