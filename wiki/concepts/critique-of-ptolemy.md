# The Critique of Ptolemy: The Equant Problem and the Principle of Uniform Circular Motion

## Definition and Scope

The "critique of Ptolemy" names the sustained, multi-century programme by astronomers working in Islamic civilization to identify, categorize, and repair the internal contradictions of Ptolemaic astronomy. It is both a body of literature (the *shukūk*, *istidrāk*, and *ḥayʾa* genres) and a theoretical orientation: the conviction that a physically consistent astronomy must reconcile its mathematical models with the cosmological presuppositions on which those models were ostensibly based.

The central fault was the **equant**. George Saliba summarizes the problem: Ptolemy required the deferent sphere — a physical sphere in the Aristotelian universe — to move "uniformly around an axis that did not pass through its center," which made "the existence of such a sphere physically impossible" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]). The principle violated was Aristotle's requirement that every celestial sphere move uniformly *in place* around its own center. Islamic astronomers called the reformation of this contradiction the task of *ʿilm al-hayʾa* (the science of astronomical configurations).

The critique was not peripheral dissatisfaction but a structural demand imposed by the intellectual environment of Islamic civilization, where rival professional groups — religious scholars, natural philosophers, and astronomers themselves — scrutinized any imported science for internal consistency.

## The Aristotelian Framework and Where Ptolemy Violated It

Ptolemaic astronomy operated against an Aristotelian cosmological backdrop that stipulated:

1. The Earth is the fixed center of the universe and the center of heaviness.
2. The celestial realm is composed of the simple element ether.
3. All celestial motions are uniform and circular around fixed centers.

Ptolemy's models violated each of these in turn:

### The Solar Model
Ptolemy proposed either (a) an eccentric sphere — whose center of rotation differed from the Earth, contradicting Aristotle's claim that Earth is the unique center of heaviness — or (b) an epicyclic model, which introduced a subsidiary sphere with its own center, implying complexity in the supposedly simple ether. He chose the eccentric as simpler, but this choice did not remove the contradiction; it merely deferred it.

### The Upper Planets (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Venus)
The deferent sphere for the upper planets rotated "uniformly around an axis that did not pass through its center" — namely, around the equant point E, which lay at a distance from the deferent's center T equal to the eccentricity OT (where O is the center of the universe). Saliba describes this as the equant point's making uniform circular motion "physically impossible": a sphere cannot remain in place while rotating around an axis that bypasses its center ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

### The Lunar Model (the Prosneusis Problem)
The Moon's model compounded the equant with a second violation: the *prosneusis point* (Arabic *nuqṭat al-muḥādhāt*). The Moon's anomalistic motion was measured from a line connecting the moving prosneusis point N — located diametrically opposite the deferent center F with respect to Earth — to the center of the epicycle. Because N itself was in constant motion, the reference line never completed a full revolution, introducing a non-circular, oscillating reference, which was forbidden by Aristotle. Additionally, the model implied that the Moon at quadrature (90° from the mean sun) should appear roughly twice as large as the full Moon — "obviously untrue," and described by Ibn al-Shāṭir as untenable since "the moon was never seen as such (*lam yurā kadhālika*)" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

### Planetary Latitudes
Ptolemy's latitudinal models for the upper planets required the plane of each epicycle to perform a *seesawing* oscillation — tilting north and south as the epicycle moved along the deferent. This motion never completed a full revolution, making it non-circular and therefore impermissible in an Aristotelian universe composed of spheres. For the lower planets Mercury and Venus, the inclined plane itself seesawed. Al-Ṭūsī declared this "beyond what was permitted in the craft (*khārij ʿan al-ṣināʿa*)" in his *Taḥrīr al-majisṭī* (1247). Moreover, Saliba notes that Ptolemy's own "small circles" solution to the seesawing problem would cause a *wobbling* that destroyed the longitudinal component of the motion — an internal mathematical self-destruction.

### Mercury
Mercury's model replicated lunar complications: a crank-like mechanism, an equant placed halfway between the Earth's center and the center of the director (rather than at the standard position), and a deferent that moved uniformly around a point other than its own center.

## The Genre of Critique

The written tradition of critique developed specific genres:

- ***Shukūk*** ("doubts"): texts cataloguing Ptolemy's failures. The canonical example is Ibn al-Haytham's *al-Shukūk ʿalā Baṭlamyūs* (Doubts on Ptolemy), which demonstrated the physical impossibility of the equant sphere.
- ***Istidrāk*** ("supplementations"): works providing corrections or additions to Ptolemaic parameters.
- ***Ḥayʾa*** ("configuration") texts: systematic astronomical treatises in the reformed tradition, which replaced the term *falak* (sphere in the Ptolemaic sense) with models satisfying the physical principles.

Saliba notes that the distinction between a practicing astrologer — who could satisfy himself with the predictive success of Ptolemy's *Handy Tables* — and a serious astronomer was precisely this demand for physical consistency. "No self-respecting astronomer would admittedly want to be cast in an astrologer's garb" ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

## Historical Development

The critique unfolded in recognizable phases:

1. **Observational and parametric correction** (9th–10th c.): Correcting Ptolemy's parameters — precession, inclination of the ecliptic, solar year length — through fresh observation. The Baghdad observatories were central.
2. **Theoretical critique of foundations** (11th c.): Ibn al-Haytham's *Shukūk* identified the equant as a physical impossibility. Abū ʿUbayd al-Juzjānī (d. ca. 1070), student of Avicenna, attempted reform but failed.
3. **New mathematical theorems** (13th c.): [[al-urdi|Muʾayyad al-Dīn al-ʿUrḍī]] (d. 1266) introduced [[urdi-lemma|ʿUrḍī's Lemma]]; [[nasir-al-din-tusi|Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī]] (d. 1274) introduced the [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī couple]]. Both worked at the [[maragha-school|Maragha observatory]].
4. **Systematic model-building** (13th–14th c.): Quṭb al-Dīn al-Shīrāzī (d. 1311) deployed both new theorems for multiple planetary models; [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]] (d. 1375) of Damascus produced a unified geocentric system eliminating all eccentrics.
5. **Mathematical pluralism** (15th–16th c.): ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn al-Qushjī (d. 1474) and Shams al-Dīn al-Khafrī (d. 1550) developed the idea of multiple mathematically equivalent models for the same phenomenon.

## Significance for the European Renaissance

Saliba's central argument is that these reforms were not isolated within Islamic civilization but were transmitted to Renaissance Europe. The models of Ibn al-Shāṭir are mathematically identical to those of Copernicus. The [[tusi-couple|Ṭūsī couple]] reappears in the *Commentariolus* and *De Revolutionibus*. ʿUrḍī's Lemma is used by Copernicus for the upper planets. Saliba argues this transmission is the mechanism behind "the making of the European Renaissance" in the book's title ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]]).

The critique of Ptolemy is therefore not merely internal Islamic intellectual history but the direct predecessor of the Copernican revolution.

## Historiography

Saliba's account is revisionist against the conventional narrative in which Copernicus invented his reforms de novo from Greek sources. He situates the Islamic critique as the indispensable intellectual bridge. The specific claim that Copernicus used the Ṭūsī couple was established by E. S. Kennedy and colleagues in the 1970s–80s and refined by subsequent scholarship. Saliba's contribution is to show that the Islamic reform tradition was continuous from the 9th to the 16th century, not an isolated 13th-century episode, and to argue that the specific mathematical tools (ʿUrḍī's Lemma, Ṭūsī couple) are not independently reinvented but transmitted.

The counter-position — that the mathematical similarities are coincidental convergence — has few defenders among specialists but is not fully refuted by documentary evidence of transmission, which remains circumstantial.
