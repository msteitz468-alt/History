# Aksumite Stelae

## Definition

The **Aksumite stelae** are monolithic granite obelisks erected at [[aksum-city]] and other Aksumite sites as grave-markers for elite and royal burials. The term encompasses both the large "storeyed" stelae — elaborately carved to represent multi-storey buildings with the Aksumite beam-and-window architectural vocabulary — and the numerous smaller, undecorated or simply-carved stelae used across the broader population. The great carved stelae of the Aksum Stelae Park are the largest monoliths ever erected in the ancient world; the fallen **Stela 1** is estimated at c. 520 tonnes or more.

The stelae are simultaneously monuments of royal ideology, documents of Aksumite architectural forms, and the primary chronological markers for the great underground tomb complexes they once stood above.

## Historical Context

Use of stelae as grave-markers was a pre-Aksumite tradition in the northern Horn, attested from earlier periods and widespread across the population. What distinguished the Aksumite royal phase was the sudden, dramatic escalation of scale and elaboration beginning in the latter part of the third century CE.

The process began with the creation of a burial terrace at the foot of Beta Giyorgis hill — now the northern half of the Stelae Park — where platforms with associated graves and stelae have been traced back as far as the first century CE. During the latter part of the third century and into the early fourth century, the scale transformed dramatically. During this roughly one-hundred-year window, a series of monolithic stelae of unprecedented size were quarried, transported, carved, and erected above elaborately constructed underground stone-lined tombs. This phase constitutes the event documented in `[[aksumite-stelae-erection-3c-ce]]`.

The erection of the largest stelae ceased with the adoption of Christianity under Ezana in the early fourth century. The fall of Stela 1 approximately coincided with this transition, and no further stelae of comparable scale were erected. Smaller stelae continued to serve as grave-markers across the population. Several of the great stelae were toppled — tradition attributes this to Queen Gudit around the tenth century. Stela 2 was removed to Rome by Italian forces in 1937 and returned to Ethiopia in 2008.

## Examples and Evidence

### The Six Storeyed Stelae
Six stelae at Aksum were elaborately carved to represent multi-storey building facades, complete with the distinctive Aksumite architectural vocabulary: projecting beam-ends (monkey-heads), recessed windows, and false doors at the base. These are the primary monuments of the tradition.

- **Stela 1** (fallen): The largest, carved to indicate thirteen storeys. Estimated weight c. 520+ tonnes — the largest monolith ever erected in the ancient world by this measure. Now lies where it fell, broken.
- **Stela 2** (33 m; 160 tonnes): Carved with nine storeys; false door at base. Removed to Rome in 1937 after the Italian conquest; returned in 2008. This stela can be attributed on numismatic and stylistic grounds to Ousanas/Ella Amida or to wzb (early fourth century). It stood above the **Mausoleum** — a large underground stone-built tomb.
- **Stela 3** (24 m): Still standing; seven storeys. Above the **Tomb of the Brick Arches**.
- **Stelae 4, 5, 6**: Smaller storeyed examples.

The quarries from which the granite stelae were cut have been identified approximately 4 km from the Stelae Park. Transport was a major logistical achievement — Phillipson reconstructs probable use of timber sledges, rollers, and large labour forces. Carving was completed before erection, as the evidence for the erection sequence demonstrates.

### The Stelae and Their Tombs
Each great stela marked an underground tomb complex. The **Mausoleum** (below Stela 2) and the **Tomb of the Brick Arches** (below Stela 3) are the best documented. The **Tomb of the False Door** is a further elaborate example. These tombs are stone-lined, multi-chambered underground structures reached by a shaft or stairway; they employed fired-brick vaulting and iron clamps (of Graeco-Roman type) to join stone blocks. All known examples had been robbed in antiquity; the Tomb of the Brick Arches yielded significant artefacts including ivory and metalwork despite its looting.

### Smaller Stelae
Beyond the six storeyed examples, the Stelae Park contains numerous smaller, undecorated stelae. Ethnographic and archaeological evidence shows that stelae were used as grave-markers across the Aksumite population, not only the elite. The smaller examples preceded the great carved series chronologically; it has been suggested — though not conclusively demonstrated — that undecorated stelae represented an earlier or lower-status tradition.

### The Funerary Inscription Absence
A striking feature of Aksumite royal funerary culture is the **absence of identifying inscriptions** on the tombs or stelae. None of the surviving monuments name the individuals buried beneath them. Attribution of specific stelae and tombs to named kings relies entirely on numismatic and stratigraphic inference.

## Significance

1. **Scale**: The Aksumite storeyed stelae represent the most ambitious exercise in monolith quarrying, transport, and erection known from the ancient world — surpassing the obelisks of New Kingdom Egypt in single-piece weight.
2. **Royal ideology**: The stelae expressed royal power through controlled demonstration of labour capacity and material command. Their carved facades asserted the king's participation in the elaborate architectural vocabulary of elite Aksumite buildings.
3. **Architectural documentation**: The carved facades record the Aksumite multi-storey building tradition with detail unavailable from the eroded architectural remains themselves (see `[[aksumite-architecture]]`).
4. **Chronological anchor**: The stelae-and-tomb complexes, datable by associated coinage and radiocarbon, provide a key chronological framework for the Aksumite royal sequence.
5. **Religious transition**: The end of large stela erection coinciding with Christianisation under Ezana marks one of the most archaeologically visible expressions of that ideological shift — the pre-Christian funerary ideology was incompatible with Christian burial practice.

## Limitations and Debates

- **Attribution**: No stela can be definitively assigned to a named king by epigraphic evidence alone. All attributions rest on probabilistic numismatic and stratigraphic arguments.
- **Erection sequence and date**: The relative sequence of the six storeyed stelae has been debated; the present scholarly consensus associates the largest (Stela 1) with the early fourth century and the broader monumental phase with c. AD 270–330. Radiocarbon dates from associated tombs are consistent with this range.
- **Fall of Stela 1**: Whether the collapse was accidental or deliberate, and its exact date relative to Ezana's conversion, cannot be established with precision. The association of the fall with Christianisation is archaeologically compelling but not conclusively proven.
- **Queen Gudit tradition**: The attribution of other toppled stelae to the legendary Queen Gudit (c. tenth century CE) is traditional but not archaeologically confirmed.

## Historiography

The stelae were among the first Aksumite monuments documented by European observers. The **Deutsche Aksum-Expedition** (1906; published Littmann et al. 1913) produced the foundational architectural documentation and a tabulation of the six storeyed stelae that has never been surpassed. Phillipson's own scale drawings (D. Phillipson 1997) and the measurements and discussions in *Foundations of an African Civilisation* (2012) are the current standard. The political and religious significance of the stelae as expressions of royal ideology has received growing attention in recent scholarship, moving beyond purely architectural description toward interpretive frameworks of power display and religious transition.
