# Roman Art and Architecture in Transition (193–337)

The visual arts of the [[severan-dynasty|Severan]]-to-[[constantine-the-great|Constantinian]] period —
"one of the most significant" yet "most elusive" phases in the history of Roman art. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 19 (**Janet Huskinson**). The chapter is organized around a single
analytical problem: how to read the **shift from naturalism toward abstraction** without prejudging it.

## The "decline versus changed priorities" debate

Huskinson's central historiographical intervention: the period's drift from the **illusionistic
naturalism** of the Greek tradition toward **flat, linear, schematic** forms has long been read as
artistic **decline** — but should instead be read for itself, as **changed priorities**. The transition
from classical to late-antique/Byzantine style is "not a straight line of evolution but a series of
zigzags," with no single moment when the boundary is crossed (only with the homogeneous Christian art of
the mid-fourth century "is it reasonable to say that the transition has been largely made"). She warns
equally against the circular habit of reading third-century portraits as expressions of "crisis" or
**"anxiety"** without independent criteria — a danger of "predetermining how the art is to be read." The
more neutral lens she proposes is the recurring **tension between flat surface and modelled volume**,
explored across portraiture, mosaic and architecture.

## The stylistic sequence

- **Severan (to c. 235)**: continuation of late-Antonine trends (the column of Marcus Aurelius) —
  **coloristic surfaces, frontality, dramatic poses**, the breaking-up of classical composition (the arch
  of Septimius Severus, 203; the arch of the Argentarii; the Lepcis Magna reliefs; the Baths of Caracalla,
  "the highpoint of Roman construction").
- **The soldier-emperors (c. 235–268)**: powerful portraits of **Maximinus Thrax** and **Philip the
  Arab** combining modelled volume with sharp linear detail; the **"Gallienic renaissance"** — a
  retrospective philhellenism alluding to Augustus, Hadrian and classical Greece (better seen, Huskinson
  argues, as restless change than a true revival); the dramatic, anti-narrative **Ludovisi battle
  sarcophagus**.
- **Tetrarchic and Constantinian (from c. 290)**: "one of the great transitions in the history of western
  art" — forms flatten, figures become **iconic**. The famous **porphyry tetrarchs** (Venice) are
  "abstract, geometric forms" with details *inscribed* not modelled, sacrificing individuality for the
  group; the **arch of Constantine** friezes show the frontal, hierarchically-scaled emperor amid a
  uniform mass. In the **East**, illusionism persisted (the arch of Galerius at Salonica; the modelled
  Diocletian head from Nicomedia).

Architecture revived under **Diocletian** (the great baths; the palace at Split) and Constantine, with
**vast, strongly articulated, symmetrical spaces** (the Trier basilica's austere exterior masking an
elaborate interior), the defensive **walls of Aurelian**, and finally
**[[founding-of-constantinople-330|Constantinople]]**.

## Emperors and the past

Huskinson's first case study: **temporal themes** — emperors anchoring an unstable present to Rome's past.
**Portraiture** carried dynastic claims (Septimius adopting Marcus Aurelius' features; the wide influence
of Caracalla's "scowling" image; Constantine as "neo-Augustus with a neo-Trajanic hairstyle"), while the
tetrarchs used identical features to deny individuality. **Coins and medallions** took over the
commemorative role of vanished public sculpture (Philip's *Saeculum Novum* for Rome's millennium in 248;
the Constantinian silver medallion fusing the Romulus-and-Remus shield with the **chi-rho helmet**). The
**arch of Constantine's spolia** — reliefs lifted from monuments of Trajan, Hadrian and Marcus Aurelius,
their heads recut as Constantine — are read not as decline-by-poverty but as deliberate "sculptural
quotations" linking Constantine to the "good emperors." Constantine then **yoked the Christian past to his
present**: churches at Rome and the Holy Land, the "invention of Christian archaeology" at Golgotha, and
his burial as the **"thirteenth apostle"** at Constantinople.

## The birth of Christian art

Huskinson's second case study tracks **Christian iconography emerging from the funerary context**. Before
the **[[edict-of-milan-313|Peace of the Church]]**, Christian art was confined to catacombs and
sarcophagi; the earliest Christian motifs (from c. 270) — the **Good Shepherd** and the praying **orans** —
were adapted from existing secular figure-types (philanthropy, *pietas*) and inserted inconspicuously into
conventional schemes (strigillated coffins, Seasons, hunts, banquets). After 313 the new stability and
**wealthy Christian patronage** produced larger, double-register sarcophagi with assertively Christian
programmes — the prelude to the homogeneous Christian art of the mid-fourth century.

## Significance

The chapter supplies the wiki's account of how the **visual culture** registered the
[[third-century-crisis|crisis]] and the Constantinian revolution. Its thesis aligns with the volume's
broader revisionism: the move to abstraction and frontality was **not decadence** but a reorientation
toward concept, hierarchy and the transcendent (Huskinson links it to **[[late-polytheism|Neoplatonism]]'s**
stress on inner reality over appearance). Economic and political turmoil left real marks (few public
monuments mid-century; regional divergence; spolia), but the period's art is best understood as a
**dynamic, zigzagging transition** — looking backward and forward at once — that issued in the Christian
art of late antiquity.

## The fourth century: art and the pagan/Christian dichotomy (CAH XIII)

[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 24 (**Jas Elsner**) carries the story to 425, calling late-antique art
"one of the great transitions in the history of western art" — the first wholesale shift of the classical
canon **toward abstraction** since the fifth century BCE. Elsner's arguments:

- **Continuity *and* innovation, not decline.** Imperial art traditions persisted (the colossal bronze of
  Constantius II, the Theodosian marble portraits, the columns of Theodosius and Arcadius, the obelisk base
  of Theodosius, great basilical churches). The apparent "decline" in large-scale sculpture is better read
  as a **transference of patronage to new media** — superb **ivories, silver treasure** (Sevso,
  Kaiseraugst), **wall/vault mosaics**, *opus sectile*, glass cage-cups, and the **illuminated codex**
  (the Vatican Virgil) — not "degeneration of skill."
- **Christianization of the cityscape**: emperors now built **churches not temples**, and in Rome the great
  martyr-churches rose on the **periphery** (St Peter's, S. Lorenzo, S. Agnese *fuori le mura*) — reshaping
  the experience of the processional city.
- **The methodological key — images are "multi-strand."** Elsner attacks the assumption that a
  Christian-themed image proves a Christian patron, or that "single images have single meanings." The same
  motif (the sacrifice of Isaac, say) could be made by, and **carry meaning for, pagans, Jews and
  Christians alike** — dissolving the rigid **pagan/Christian dichotomy** and the static reading of
  spolia. The fourth century was a "high point of religious imagery" shared across sects.

This dovetails with the volume's broader anti-"decline," anti-dichotomy reading (Brown, Cameron): late-
antique visual culture as a **vital, shared, transforming** repertoire, not a degraded one.
