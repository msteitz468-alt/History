## Definition and Scope

Quranic law refers to the body of legally binding injunctions contained within the Quran — the divine revelations received by Muhammad — that govern ritual, family, property, crime, and communal life for the Muslim *Umma*. It is both a historical phenomenon (legislation revealed in the final years of Muhammad's life) and an analytical concept used to distinguish the Quran's legislative dimension from its theological, eschatological, and narrative content.

Quranic law is not a complete code. It is better understood as a set of foundational principles and specific rulings — some highly detailed, others only gestured at — that provided the normative framework within which later jurists developed the full edifice of [[sharia]] and [[fiqh]].

## Historical Development: The Turn to Legislation

The emergence of a distinctly Quranic legal identity was not present from the beginning of Muhammad's mission. Hallaq identifies a decisive turning point around Year 5 of the Hijra (early 626 CE), when Sura 5 of the Quran was revealed. The key verse, 5:48, explicitly states:

> "We have revealed unto you the Book with the Truth, confirming whatever Scripture was before it … so judge between them by what God had revealed … for We have made for each of you a law and a normative way to follow."

Before this point, Muhammad's mission was primarily religious and ethical — calling for monotheism, humility, and generosity. The term *minhaj* used in 5:48 (rendered as "normative way") is a cognate of the Hebrew *minhag* (the Law), signaling the deliberate construction of an Islamic legal parallel to Torah and Gospel. From this point, "the bulk of the substantive legislation seems to have been revealed after the year 5/626, when a distinct body of law exclusive to the Umma was first conceived."

## Character and Scope

**Quantitative weight.** The traditional count of legal verses in the Quran is approximately 500 out of roughly 6,200 total verses. Goitein argued convincingly that this understates the legal weight: the legal verses are not subject to the Quran's characteristic repetition (present elsewhere), making their effective proportion higher; their average length is two to three times that of non-legal verses; and, comparatively, the Quran "contains no less legal material than does the Torah, which is commonly known as 'the Law.'"

**Areas covered.** Quranic legislation addresses, in varying degrees of detail:
- *Ritual*: prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, alms-tax (*zakat*)
- *Family law*: marriage (permitted and prohibited degrees), divorce (*talaq*), the waiting period (*idda*), inheritance (daughters receive half a son's share), dowry and wives' financial independence
- *Criminal law*: penalties for theft, adultery (*zina*), false accusation (*qadhf*), but often without specifying penalties (which early caliphs and later jurists filled in)
- *Commercial and pecuniary law*: prohibition of gambling and risk-speculation, contracts of sale, alms-distribution

**Areas left incomplete.** Critically, Quranic law did not address many areas of daily life, and it left significant gaps even where it legislated. For example, the Quran prohibited wine-drinking but specified no penalty. Umar I ruled the punishment to be eighty lashes, reasoning by analogy (*qiyas*) that inebriation resembled false accusation of adultery — for which eighty lashes was the Quranic penalty. Hallaq uses this example to show how from the very beginning "the Quran provided the framework for legal thinking, bringing its contents to bear upon as many situations as nominally could be justified."

## Relationship to Pre-Islamic Law

Quranic law was not a clean break with the [[pre-islamic-near-eastern-law]] substrate. Muhammad, as a prominent arbitrating judge (*hakam*) in Meccan society, could not have abandoned the legal principles by which he had performed that function. The Quran's engagement with pre-existing law followed three patterns:

1. **Adoption**: Practices already widespread in Near Eastern and Arabian customary law were incorporated — prayer and fasting (pre-Islamic Arabian practice), blood-money rules, dowry and financial security for wives (paralleling Mesopotamian law), bartering contracts (*araya* and *salam*) tracing to ancient Near Eastern commercial practice.

2. **Reform**: Some existing institutions were modified. Inheritance was restructured to include daughters (at half a son's share), which "appears to have been unprecedented in Arabia." The *idda* (waiting period for divorced women) was introduced — before Islam, divorce was complete upon declaration; the Quran imposed a three-menstrual-cycle delay, with the husband obliged to provide domicile and support. Polygamy was restricted in scope.

3. **Prohibition**: Practices like gambling, wine-drinking, and raiding were subjected to outright prohibition or serious limitation.

## The Vulgate and Legal Significance

The creation of a standardized Quranic text was itself a legal act. During Muhammad's life and the caliphate of Abu Bakr, the text existed as fragments; various versions circulated in the conquered territories. The third caliph Uthman (r. 23/644–35/656) commissioned a standard vulgate from Zayd b. Thabit (reportedly Muhammad's scribe), copies of which were distributed to the garrison towns. As Hallaq notes, "The creation of a vulgate must have had a primary legal significance, for it defined the content of the Quran and thus gave the legally minded a *textus receptus* on which to draw."

## Quranic Law vs. Later Islamic Law

Quranic law is a subset of, not identical to, the mature [[sharia]]. The relationship between the two is one of the central problems of Islamic legal history. Key distinctions:

- The Quran is the *first* and supreme source of Islamic law but does not alone constitute it.
- Prophetic Sunna (recorded in [[hadith]]) was the second source, though its independent authority was not fully articulated until well into the second/eighth century.
- The bulk of mature Sharia was developed through juristic reasoning — [[qiyas-analogy]], [[ijma-consensus]], and [[taqlid-and-ijtihad|independent legal reasoning]] — operating on the Quranic and Sunna foundations.
- Quranic law in its earliest application (the first Islamic century) was applied alongside pre-Islamic customary law, caliphal decree, and discretionary opinion (*ray*) — not as a self-sufficient system.

The progressive displacement of non-Quranic sources by a systematized Quran-plus-Prophetic-Sunna framework is the central narrative of [[formative-period-islamic-law]].

## Non-Muslim Recognition

The Armenian Bishop Sebeos, writing in the 660s CE, recognized that Muhammad "upheld a law particular to the new religion, and distinct from other laws" — one of the earliest non-Muslim attestations that Quranic law was perceived as a distinct legal system by contemporaries. This external observation corroborates the Quran's own claims in Sura 5 that the Muslim community had been given a law parallel to and separate from those of Jews and Christians.

## Historiography

The primary scholarly debate concerns the authenticity and dating of Quranic legislation. Revisionist scholars (Wansbrough, *Quranic Studies*, 1977; Crone and Cook, *Hagarism*, 1977) questioned whether the Quran assumed its legal character as early as tradition maintains. Hallaq, while acknowledging these challenges, argues that the external evidence (Sebeos, Syriac sources, Armenian chronicles) and the internal coherence of the legislative sequence support the traditional dating of legal verses to the late Medinan period. The question intersects with debates over whether Quranic law or Prophetic Sunna formed the earlier and more authoritative basis of Islamic legal practice. See [[formative-period-islamic-law]] for the broader argument.
