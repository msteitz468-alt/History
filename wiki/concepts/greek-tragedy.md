# Greek Tragedy

**Greek tragedy** is the formal verse drama of classical [[athens|Athens]] — alongside comedy and satyr play — performed competitively at the **Great Dionysia** and later the Lenaea, taking Homeric themes, irony, justice, and the destruction of the hero into a masked, choral, civic ritual form.

## Definition and Scope

Levi ([[boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW, 1986]], Ch. 7) stresses **ritual-religious origins**: sixth-century animal-mask dancers; early performances around a cart in the agora; actors linked to Dionysiac sanctuaries (Icaria, Eleutherae). The **dithyramb** — processional and choral lyric with narrative themes — is the obvious direct ancestor of theatrical choral lyrics, from which first one, then two, then three actors stepped out while retaining a choral background. [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]]: “We are all eating crumbs from the great table of [[homer|Homer]]” — tragedy took themes, irony, preoccupation with justice, and the inner form of the hero’s destruction from epic. Across the fifth century plays tended toward the more human, secular, and fictional — without ever completing that trajectory even in the fourth century.

## Institutions

- **Great Dionysia** (spring): chief, and early fifth-century only, tragic festival; probably organized under [[pisistratus]], remodelled by [[cleisthenes]]; inscribed winners’ list begun or reorganized by the newborn democracy. Early format: three poets × three tragedies + one **satyr play**.
- **Lenaea** (winter, ~2 February): comedy by 440; tragedy about 432. Comedies five per festival (three in wartime). Both genres later spread through Country Dionysia and the wider Greek world.
- Staging for most of the fifth century very simple (two–three actors; chorus of twelve, later fifteen).
- Contemporary political themes: Phrynichus’s *Phoenissae* and *Fall of Miletus*, Aeschylus’s *Persae*, and later works; [[sophocles|Sophocles’]] *Oedipus at Colonus* needs its production context as Athens “toppled to its fall.”

## Satyr plays

Custom was one satyr play with three tragedies, likely preserving something of drama’s origins: wild countryside, amoral satyr choruses always searching for Dionysus. Only complete surviving satyr play is [[euripides|Euripides’]] *Cyclops* (half a tragedy’s length). Aeschylus was champion of the genre; fragments of *Net-Fishers* (Danae and Perseus) survive.

## Canonical Playwrights

[[aeschylus]], [[sophocles]], [[euripides]] — the three whose work largely defines the surviving corpus. Comedy’s parallel institution (from 488/7 at the Dionysia; previously “volunteers”; Epicharmus already composing in Sicily early fifth century) is treated under [[aristophanes]]. Parker (OHCW Ch. 11): dramatic festivals had moved far from origins but **remained religious ceremonies**. Murray (Ch. 9): Great Dionysia preceded by massive sacrifice (up to ~240 bulls) — audience amid blood-reek and hangover; patronage shifted from tyrant/aristocrat to *demos* via liturgies.
