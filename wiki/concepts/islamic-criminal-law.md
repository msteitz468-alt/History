## Definition and Scope

The branch of Islamic law dealing with offenses against life, body, morality, public conduct, and property is commonly labeled "criminal law" in modern scholarship, but Hallaq argues this is conceptually misleading. Muslim jurists never grouped these offenses under a single unifying category analogous to modern penal law. Instead they recognized distinct horizontal categories, each with its own evidentiary rules, penalties, and juristic logic.

The foundational conceptual distinction is between three categories of offenses: (a) **ḥudūd** (sg. ḥadd) — offenses whose punishments are fixed as God's right; (b) **homicide and bodily harm** (qiṣāṣ/jināyāt) — offenses prosecuted by the victim's kin; and (c) **taʿzīr** — discretionary punishments for all other infractions.

Hallaq stresses that unlike the modern state's penal system, which exercises "systemic and systematic violence" to produce docility, the Sharīʿa's law of offenses operated in a social world governed by divine omniscience and moral internalization: "The world of the Sharīa, by contrast, lived under the full shadow of an omniscient God who — by one of the most cardinal tenets of Islam — knew each and every particular of human conduct and misdemeanor." This divine sanction reduced the need for coercive enforcement and explains the deliberately high evidentiary bars.

The underlying logic of compensation pervades the system: "although this concept of compensation emerged out of the old Semitic and ancient Near Eastern tradition of 'an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth,' it was largely (though not totally) transformed under the Sharīa … into a system of monetary awards for the victim or his/her family." The system remained largely in the realm of private wrongs; the ruler implemented punishments but seldom prosecuted criminals independently.

## Ḥudūd: Fixed Penalties

Ḥudūd offenses are those regulated by Quranic or Sunna texts, whose punishments are fixed and belong to God's right (ḥaqq Allāh). All schools accepted zinā (illicit sexual intercourse), qadhf (false accusation of zinā), shurb al-khamr (drinking alcohol), sariqa (theft), and qatʿ al-ṭarīq (highway robbery) as ḥudūd. The Mālikites also included baghy (rebellion) and ridda (apostasy).

The severe sanctions were "intended to deter (zajr) and were thus infrequently implemented in practice. This is exceedingly clear from the strict evidential procedures required to prove them." The governing maxim, derived from Prophetic hadith, was that ḥudūd penalties "had to be 'averted at the existence of the slightest doubt.'" Procedural rules otherwise standard across fiqh were heightened: confession in ḥudūd (except qadhf) could be retracted, secondary testimony (shahāda ʿalā al-shahāda) was inadmissible, and written inter-judge communications were barred as evidence.

### Zinā (Illicit Sexual Intercourse)

Defined as sexual intercourse involving actual penetration, by legally competent persons, outside lawful rights, and without any doubt (shubha) as to those rights. The penalty for a muḥṣan (a free, adult, sane Muslim who has consummated marriage) is stoning; for non-muḥṣan, one hundred lashes. The evidentiary bar is exceptional: four trustworthy adult male witnesses must appear simultaneously in the same court session and testify in explicit and unambiguous language to having witnessed the act of penetration. Any discrepancy in their testimonies exonerates the accused and exposes the witnesses themselves to the charge of qadhf (eighty lashes). Confession requires four separate utterances by a free, adult, sane, uncoerced person.

### Qadhf (False Accusation of Zinā)

False accusation of zinā, even against the deceased, incurs eighty lashes unless the accuser produces four male witnesses proving the truth of the charge. The exception is the husband's liʿān oath, by which he can accuse his wife without witnesses by swearing under oath, after which the wife may deny under oath and the marriage dissolves — without either party incurring the qadhf penalty.

### Sariqa (Theft)

The ḥadd punishment — amputation of the right hand — applies only to a technically defined sariqa: taking property by stealth (khufya), where the property is licit, imperishable, exceeds a minimum value (niṣāb), and was stored in a ḥirz (a recognized place of custody, such as a house, safe, or shop). If any of these conditions are not met, the offense falls to taʿzīr. The accused must be of adult age, sane, and subject to Islamic rule (thus excluding minors, the insane, and those with no recognized protected status). The poor who steal food and those unfamiliar with Islamic law are explicitly exempted from the ḥadd.

### Shurb al-Khamr (Drinking Alcohol)

Deemed a ḥadd by most schools, regulated by the Sunna rather than the Quran, and "deemed less grave than zinā and qadhf since it represents aggression primarily against oneself and not others." Penalties: forty lashes (Shāfiʿites) or eighty (Ḥanafites, Mālikites, Ḥanbalites). Evidence of intoxication suffices, but drinking must be voluntary.

### Qatʿ al-Ṭarīq / Ḥirāba (Highway Robbery)

Armed and open robbery. Punishment is severing the right hand and left leg; if accompanied by murder, death by sword and crucifixion; if murder without taking property, death; if the amount stolen falls below niṣāb and no bodily harm occurs, banishment.

### Baghy (Rebellion) and Ridda (Apostasy)

Only the Mālikites classify these as ḥudūd, but similar rules apply across schools. Rebels (bughāt) must have a rationally comprehensible creed (taʾwīl ṣāliḥ); the imam must first investigate their grievances and attempt to remove injustice before fighting them. Family members and rebels' property are protected even in suppression. For apostasy, most jurists allow three days for repentance (utterance of the two shahādas); without repentance, the apostate is executed and property confiscated, though Ḥanafites and Twelver Shīʿites exempt women from capital punishment.

## Qiṣāṣ and Jināyāt: Homicide and Bodily Harm

Homicide is "a private wrong, prosecuted only upon the demand of the victim's next of kin." The degree of intentionality is measured by external criteria — primarily the type of implement used — since "knowledge of inner motives (māfī al-bāṭin) [is] well-nigh impossible." Five types are distinguished:

1. **Qatl ʿamd** (intentional homicide): intent to kill plus use of a lethal implement. Liability: retaliation (qiṣāṣ) or diya (blood-money) at the family's option, or pardon.
2. **Shibh ʿamd** (quasi-intentional): intent to harm but non-lethal implement. Liability: extensive blood-money (diya mughallaza — 100 camels over three years), no capital punishment.
3. **Qatl khaṭaʾ** (accidental): e.g., stray arrow while hunting. Liability: mitigated diya (diya mukhaffafa).
4. **Majrā al-khaṭaʾ**: homicide treated like accidental, e.g., rolling over someone in sleep. Same mitigated diya.
5. **Qatl bi-sabab** (indirect killing): e.g., digging a well into which another falls. Lesser blood-money; murderers in this category (unlike the others) are not barred from inheriting from their victims.

When the murderer is unknown, the procedure of **qasāma** applies: the victim's kin may sue the inhabitants of the locale where the body was found, based on evidence of animosity (lawth). The collective defendant must swear fifty oaths of non-culpability or pay diya.

Bodily harm follows the same tripartite structure of retaliation/compensation/pardon. The diya scale: loss of a single-instance organ (nose, tongue, sex organ) = full diya; each of a paired organ (eye, hand, woman's nipple) = half diya; a finger or toe = one-tenth; a tooth = one-twentieth. The retaliation principle is strict equality: "a good eye for a good eye, but not a good eye for a bad eye."

## Taʿzīr: Discretionary Punishment

Any offense not classified under ḥudūd or qiṣāṣ falls to taʿzīr. The range of penalties is wide but may not exceed or match the ḥudūd in severity (lashes range from three to thirty-nine maximum). Death or injury resulting from taʿzīr punishment creates financial liability for the sovereign, since it is typically state officials who implement it.

In practice, taʿzīr was "the most common of all punishments, though often entirely lacking in obvious physical violence. Since personal honor was a precious commodity, with higher stakes at the upper levels of the social order, besmirching this honor was — and remains so in many parts of the Muslim world today — an effective method of inflicting punishment." Social shaming, public humiliation, and reputational damage served as effective sanctions, particularly in Malay and Mughal contexts recorded by Hallaq, where tazīr punishments for serious offenses could consist entirely of public humiliation before a large gathering.

## Historiography

The classical law of offenses has been subject to intense modern misreading. Hallaq's principal historiographical argument is that applying the category "criminal law" to the Sharīʿa is an anachronism imported from the modern state's epistemic framework (following Foucault's analysis of the carceral system). The Muslim jurists did not conceive a unified penal law and did not share the modern state's investment in punishment as correction and social control.

A second major debate concerns the relationship between juristic doctrine and actual practice. The extraordinarily high evidentiary bars for ḥudūd offenses mean that many scholars question how often these penalties were actually applied. The evidence suggests they functioned primarily as moral deterrents backed by eschatological sanction (eternal hellfire for unpunished offenders) rather than as routinely enforced criminal penalties.
