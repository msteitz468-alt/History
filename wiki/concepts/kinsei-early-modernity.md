# Kinsei and Japanese Early Modernity

The historiographical concept of **"early modern" Japan** (Japanese ***kinsei***) — the
periodization of the Tokugawa era and its immediately surrounding years (c. 1580–1877) — and
the debate over what calling the period "early modern" means. From David L. Howell's
Introduction ("Genealogies of Japanese Early Modernity") to the
[[howell-early-modern-japan-asia-world|New Cambridge History of Japan Vol. II]] (2023).

## The concept

Anglophone scholars now routinely call the Tokugawa period (1603–1868) Japan's "early modern"
era, and Japanese scholars use ***kinsei*** for almost exactly the same span (late sixteenth
century to the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji Restoration]], after which *kindai*, "modern,"
begins). Both periodizations rest on a "checklist" of early-modern attributes that the period
displays: the rise of commerce and industry, the spread of a money economy, urbanization, mass
literacy and print, secularization, and an emergent public sphere — set between a medieval
world and full industrial modernity.

## Howell's "genealogy": three claims

1. **Early modernity is older in East Asian than in European historiography.** "Early modern
   Europe" appeared in print only in the 1940s (Violet Barbour, 1940) and became standard only
   in the 1970s; in continental Europe the scheme is medieval → modern → contemporary, with no
   "early modern" at all. But the Japanese **Uchida Ginzō turned *kinsei* into a *historical
   period*** in his *Nihon kinseishi* (1903), dating it from the early Tokugawa precisely
   because that was when the "early modern" changes (commerce, money, cities, intellectual
   vitality) had run their course. East Asian history thus "early modernized" early.
2. ***Kinsei* "has nothing to do with feudalism."** This **overturns the older
   [[cambridge-history-japan-v4-early-modern|CHJ Vol. 4]] framing** (John W. Hall, paraphrasing
   Wakita), which held that Japanese *kinsei* meant "more feudal than modern" while Western
   "early modern" meant "more modern than feudal." Howell shows *kinsei* simply meant "recent
   times / nowadays" in Tokugawa usage and was only later fixed as a period label — carrying no
   built-in claim about feudalism.
3. **Treating Japan as "early modern" is not Eurocentric imposition.** Because the term has an
   independent East Asian genealogy, applying it to Japan reflects Japan's own historical
   experience and its place in **Asian and global circuits**, not the projection of a European
   template.

## Why it is contested

- The Marxist tradition originally tied "early modernity" to a **transition from feudalism to
  capitalism** (a precapitalist, "essentially feudal" stage) — exactly the linkage Howell
  rejects; by the 1970s, however, non-Marxist social and cultural historians had detached the
  term from that narrative, so it "conveys nothing definitive about a user's methods."
- Boundary dates are conventional and disputed: starts proposed from the twelfth century to the
  1610s; ends at 1853 (Perry), 1868 (Restoration), or 1871/1877.
- The deeper stake is the **"surprising career of modernity"** as a global category — whether a
  single yardstick of "modernity" should organize the histories of non-Western societies at
  all.

## Significance

The debate frames the New CHJ Vol. II's whole project: to treat Japanese early modernity **on
its own terms and within Asian/global connections**, rather than as a lagging echo of Europe.
It is the historiographical counterpart to the substantive
[[howell-early-modern-japan-asia-world|volume's]] emphasis on environment, science, status, the
peripheries, and culture.
