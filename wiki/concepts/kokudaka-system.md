# The Kokudaka System and the Taikō Land Survey

The system of land measurement, taxation, and social organization that underlay
early-modern [[japan|Japan]] — born of [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Toyotomi Hideyoshi's]]
nationwide cadastral survey (the **Taikō kenchi**, 1582–98) and the foundation on which the
**[[bakuhan-system]]** rested. In the words of [[cambridge-history-japan-v4-early-modern|CHJ
Vol. 4]], the survey "became the foundation for one of the greatest transformations of the
land system in Japanese history." Its rediscovery as a watershed by **Araki Moriaki** (1959)
"created a stir in Japanese historical circles that has not yet subsided."

## Definition and Scope

**Kokudaka** is the assessment of every parcel of land by its **productive yield (*taka*)
expressed as a quantity of rice in *koku*** (one *koku* ≈ the rice to feed one adult for a
year). Hideyoshi's surveyors measured paddy, dry field, residential plots, and sometimes
forest and marsh; ranked each as superior, medium, or inferior; and recorded for each
parcel the cultivator's name, area, and assessed yield in a **village cadastral register**.
The total national assessment came to roughly **18 million *koku*** (surveyed nationwide in
1591, updated 1593–94).

The survey **standardized measurement**: Hideyoshi imposed the *tan–se–bu* system (1 *tan*
= 300 *bu*, replacing the medieval 360) and the single **Kyoto measure** (*kyō-masu*) for
volume, ending the patchwork of regional and proprietary units. (Regional unevenness
remained: in the developed Kinai surveys were repeated, while in "backward" Kyūshū and the
north old tenurial figures were merely recalculated to fit the new registers.)

## The transformation it produced

- **"One cultivator per parcel" (*isshochi*).** Where medieval land had carried
  **overlapping rights** (the *shiki* hierarchy of the [[medieval-shoen|shōen]]), the survey
  recognized a **single registered cultivator** liable for the tax. The Toyotomi regime
  refused to acknowledge any intermediate landlord–tenant layer between the lord and the
  actual tiller — abolishing at a stroke the multi-tiered proprietorship of the estate
  system and affirming the cultivator's right of occupancy.
- **Hei-nō-bunri (the separation of warrior and peasant).** Coupled with the **sword hunts**
  (*katanagari*, from 1588) that disarmed the countryside, the survey drew a hard legal line
  between the **[[samurai|samurai]]**, withdrawn into the daimyo's castle towns, and the
  **hyakushō** (registered, disarmed, taxpaying peasantry) confined to the self-administering
  village (*mura*). This is the deep social process beneath unification — the freezing of
  the four-status order (samurai / peasant / artisan / merchant).
- **Fief to stipend.** The *kokudaka* figure let the national hegemon and each daimyo
  quantify a domain's resources and assign **military service (*gun'yaku*)** in proportion.
  Over the seventeenth century it underwrote the conversion of samurai from **enfeoffed
  fief-holders** (*chigyō-tori*) exercising direct proprietary authority into **salaried
  stipendiaries** paid from the daimyo's granary — "the evolution from fief to stipend and
  from personal to bureaucratic authority" that Hall calls "one of the crucial subcurrents"
  of the passage into the Edo age. (It was never absolute: in frontier domains like Satsuma,
  Aizu, and Sendai enfeoffed samurai remained on the land — though often the "fief" was a
  prestige fiction masking what was really a stipend.)
- **Supreme proprietorship.** When Hideyoshi's conquest was completed in 1591 he became the
  **supreme proprietor**: all daimyo had, in principle, surrendered their lands to him and
  received them back as fiefs (*chigyō-chi*) after swearing allegiance — a logic the Tokugawa
  inherited, the shogun certifying every domain with his seal.

## Interpretation and debate

- **Araki Moriaki's serfdom thesis.** Araki (1959) claimed the survey created a class of
  small independent farm families under direct military-lord control — a true **serf class**
  — and thereby ushered Japan into **feudalism** in the Marxist sense. His conceptual premise
  is contested, but historians broadly accept that the *kenchi* was of "fundamental
  historical importance."
- **Feudal vs. early modern.** Because the *kokudaka*/*bakuhan* order is so unlike European
  models, Wakita Osamu and others have found the feudalism label unhelpful; the periodization
  dispute (*kinsei* = "late feudal" vs. "early modern") turns largely on how one reads this
  settlement. See [[bakuhan-system]] and [[tokugawa-intellectual-life]].
- **Repressive imposition or social order?** Was the rigid class separation "basically
  repressive, serving the military aristocracy" (E. H. Norman), or did unification policy
  also **release** the populace from medieval fetters (Asao) and create a "rule by status"
  (*mibun* / Bitō's *yaku*) under which those who kept their place were treated impartially
  by law? CHJ Vol. 4 leans toward the second reading without dismissing the first.
