## Definition

The "unity of the human intellect" is the philosophical doctrine, developed by [[ibn-rushd|Averroes (Ibn Rushd)]] in his Long Commentary on Aristotle's *On the Soul* (*De Anima*), that all of humanity shares a single, numerically identical capacity for universal knowledge — the "material intellect." Individual human beings do not each possess their own separate intellect; rather, the single universal intellect draws on the particular experiences and cognitive processes of individual brains and bodies as its basis for actual thinking. The doctrine was condemned as heretical by Catholic church authorities in 13th-century Latin Christendom and attacked in detail by Thomas Aquinas in his *On the Unity of the Intellect Against the Averroists*.

## Background: The Problem of the Intellect in Aristotle

Aristotle's *On the Soul* (III.5) spoke of a "maker intellect" (*nous poietikos*) that makes things intelligible as light makes things visible — a notoriously obscure passage. Medieval commentators, both Islamic and Latin, interpreted this as implying a single superhuman intellect that enables human knowing. Al-Kindi, [[al-farabi|al-Farabi]], and [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]] all posited an "Agent Intellect" — a separate, superhuman intellect that gave forms to matter and enabled human intellectual abstraction. What these thinkers shared was the assumption that each human individual *also* possesses their own individual intellect or rational soul. The Agent Intellect assists but does not replace individual human intellects.

The deeper problem: Aristotle held that genuine knowledge is *universal* (it grasps not particular giraffes but giraffes as such), yet knowledge is apparently *individuated* (you know something I do not). How can a truly universal intellect belong to a particular person?

## Averroes' Development Across Three Commentaries

Averroes wrote three treatments of Aristotle's *On the Soul* — an Epitome, a Middle Commentary (paraphrase), and a Long Commentary — and changed his position significantly:

**First position (Epitome):** Following [[ibn-bajja|Ibn Bajja]], Averroes associated the human "material intellect" (the capacity for potential universal thought) with the imagination — the cognitive faculty that stores and manipulates particular sensory images. These imaginary forms prepare the way for "unification" with the universal Agent Intellect, at which point the human mind achieves actual universal understanding. Averroes admired this view initially.

**Second position (Middle Commentary):** Rejecting the identification of intellect with imagination — because Aristotle argued the intellect cannot have any form of its own, including a bodily connection — Averroes moved to a position like al-Farabi's and Avicenna's: each human has their own individual material intellect, but it is completely free of bodily connection. The Agent Intellect both bestows this individual immaterial power and then activates it.

**Third position (Long Commentary — the notorious doctrine):** Averroes realized the second position made no sense: if my material intellect has no bodily connection, what makes it *mine* rather than yours? And if two people know the same universal truth, are they not knowing numerically the same thing? "The thing you are understanding and the thing I am understanding must be numerically identical, not two individuals of the same type" (Adamson, ch. 26, summarizing Averroes' Long Commentary, p. 411). Averroes concluded: there is only one human material intellect, shared by all humanity. It is not a separate, superhuman thing but rather the highest power that belongs to the human species as a whole.

The *individual* experience of thinking is preserved by Averroes' account of how the single intellect connects with particular human bodies: when the particular thought-processes happening in your brain — imagination, sensory memory, and a lower form of thinking Averroes calls "cogitation" (*fikr/tamyiz*, translated into Latin as *cogitatio*) — are being used as a basis for universal knowledge, then *you* have the experience of universal knowing. These bodily faculties are seated in your particular brain and die with your body. The universal intellect itself is eternal and continuously thinking all possible objects of thought.

## Philosophical Motivation

Averroes was not being eccentric but was following Aristotelian premises to their logical consequences:

1. Intellect must be able to take on *any* form (because everything is thinkable), so it cannot have any form of its own.
2. Matter or body is the "principle of individuation" (what makes things particular). If intellect has no bodily connection, it cannot be individuated — cannot be mine rather than yours.
3. In Aristotelian theory, the intellect *becomes identical with* its object when it thinks. If the object (e.g., the universal nature of giraffes) is universal, the intellect too must be universal.
4. Teaching requires that teacher and student come to possess *the same* knowledge — numerically identical, not merely qualitatively similar.

## Problematic Consequences

**Personal immortality.** If the only part of me that is eternal is the universal intellect — shared with all humanity — then there is no particular "me" that survives death. Only the universal intellect persists, and it has been thinking all possible thoughts continuously throughout all of human history anyway. This was the most theologically offensive consequence in Latin Christendom.

**Individual responsibility.** If there is one shared intellect, who is responsible for what is known or decided? Averroes' response — that particular experiences of thinking are genuinely mine through their bodily basis — was not considered adequate by Aquinas, who repeatedly insisted the doctrine could not explain how "this man understands" (*hic homo intelligit*).

## Reception

**Latin Christendom:** The doctrine was condemned by the Bishop of Paris in 1270 and again in 1277. Aquinas wrote *On the Unity of the Intellect Against the Averroists* (1270) as a detailed rebuttal. The followers of Averroes in the Latin universities — the "Latin Averroists," including Siger of Brabant — maintained the doctrine, producing the controversy of "double truth" (philosophy teaches X; faith teaches not-X; both are somehow acceptable). Averroes himself had no such doctrine and would have been appalled.

**Jewish tradition:** Averroes' commentary project was translated extensively into Hebrew. His theory of the intellect was discussed and commented upon by Jewish thinkers for centuries. The Latin readership drove the perception that Averroes was simply the Aristotelian commentator; Jewish readers engaged with his arguments more directly on their philosophical merits.

**Arabic tradition:** Averroes' commentaries had minimal impact in the Arabic-speaking east after a few generations. The eastern tradition had long since moved to Avicennan synthesis; Averroes' commitment to pre-Avicennan Farabian Aristotelianism made his work "the equivalent of silent films made after the invention of sound" in that context (Adamson, ch. 25).

## Historiography

The doctrine has often been dismissed as a misreading of Aristotle. Adamson (2016) argues that Averroes was not reaching for Platonic universalism but was following Aristotelian premises with unusual consistency, facing a genuine philosophical problem that weaker positions (including his own earlier ones) had failed to solve. Whether the final solution is correct is another matter; that it was philosophically serious is Adamson's main point.

The charge of Platonism — often leveled because Plotinus also posited a single universal intellect — misunderstands what drove Averroes to his position. His starting point was Aristotle's theory of mind, not Neoplatonic metaphysics.
