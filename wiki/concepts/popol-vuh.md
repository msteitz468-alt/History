# The Popol Vuh (the K'iche' Maya Book of Creation)

The **Popol Vuh** ("Council Book" / "Book of the Mat") is the foremost surviving work of
**[[maya-civilization|Maya]]** sacred literature — the creation epic and dynastic charter of the
**K'iche' (Quiché) Maya** of highland Guatemala, and the richest single window into Mesoamerican
mythology and cosmology. Documented for the wiki through Dennis Tedlock's
[[tedlock-popol-vuh|translation]]. It interweaves a **cosmogony** (the creation of the world and of
humankind), the **mythology of the Hero Twins**, and a **genealogical history** of the K'iche' lordly
lineages down to the eve of Spanish rule — three registers the K'iche' themselves treated as "a single,
balanced whole."

## Creation and the failed humans

In the beginning there is "only the sky" and "the calm sea," and the makers — **Heart of Sky**
(**Hurricane**, in three thunderbolt forms) and the **Sovereign Plumed Serpent** — bring forth the
earth, mountains, and animals by their word alone. But the animals "cannot speak" or **praise their
makers**, so the gods attempt to create beings who can: first of **mud** (which dissolves), then of
**wood** (the wooden people move and multiply but have "nothing in their hearts and minds," fail to
honor the gods, and are destroyed in a flood and a revolt of their own tools and animals — the survivors
becoming **monkeys**). The recurring motive is striking: the gods make humans because they need
**worshippers who will "keep the days" and nurture them** — a relationship of mutual obligation that
echoes the [[aztec-religion-and-human-sacrifice|reciprocal debt]] at the heart of Mesoamerican religion.

## The Hero Twins and Xibalba

The mythic heart of the book is the saga of the **Hero Twins, Hunahpu and Xbalanque**. They first
destroy the arrogant false sun **Seven Macaw**; then the story turns back to their father and uncle (One
and Seven Hunahpu), summoned for their noise to the underworld **Xibalba** ("Place of Fright") and slain
by its **Lords of Death** in the ballcourt. One Hunahpu's skull impregnates the maiden **Blood Moon**,
who bears the Twins. Descending to Xibalba in turn, the Twins survive its houses of terror (Dark, Cold,
Jaguar, Fire, Bat), outwit the Lords of Death through cunning and self-resurrection, and finally **defeat
death itself** — rising into the sky as the **sun and the moon**. The cycle is a charter of cosmic order:
the defeat of the underworld and the false sun clears the way for the true dawn.

## Humans of maize

The climax is the **creation of true humanity from maize** (Part Four). Animals reveal the
**yellow corn and white corn** of Broken Place; the grandmother goddess **Xmucane grinds the corn nine
times**, and from this corn-meal-and-water dough the makers model the **first four men** — **Jaguar
Quitze, Jaguar Night, Mahucutah, and True Jaguar** — the founders of the K'iche' lineages. "It was
staples alone that made up their flesh." The first people **see and know too perfectly** — "their vision
passed through trees, through rocks… they understood everything" — so the gods, fearing they would become
"as great as gods," **breathe a fog on their eyes** and limit their sight. Their wives are then created,
and from these four couples descend the great houses of the K'iche'. To **be human is, literally, to be
made of corn** — the most concentrated expression of the Mesoamerican identity of people, [[maize-domestication|maize]],
and the gods.

## A book preserved under conquest

The Popol Vuh announces itself as written "**amid the preaching of God, in Christendom now**," because
"there is no longer a place to see it" — the original hieroglyphic Council Book could no longer be safely
displayed. K'iche' scribes set the oral/glyphic tradition down in **alphabetic K'iche'** in the 1550s;
that manuscript is lost, but the Dominican friar **Francisco Ximénez** transcribed and translated it
c. 1701, preserving the only text. Its final section — the **K'iche' migrations, the receiving of the
gods, and the royal genealogies** down to the Spanish conquest — shows the book's **political function**:
it legitimated the ruling lineages by tracing them to the first maize-men and the dawn of the world.

## Significance

The Popol Vuh is the **single most important surviving Mesoamerican sacred text**, the indigenous
counterpart to the conquest accounts ([[leon-portilla-broken-spears|Broken Spears]], Bernal Díaz) — a
Maya civilization speaking in its **own cosmological voice** rather than through the lens of conquest. It
deepens [[maya-civilization]] from monuments and dynasties to **myth and meaning**, anchors the
pan-Mesoamerican themes of reciprocal debt and people-as-maize, and — through the Hero Twins, attested in
Classic Maya vase-painting centuries earlier — shows a **continuity of Maya religious tradition** from the
Classic period to the colonial K'iche'. It is also simply one of world literature's great creation epics.
