# Crusade Preaching

Crusade preaching was the organized ecclesiastical practice of recruiting participants for crusading campaigns through public sermons, formally institutionalized by Pope Urban II's 1095 Council of Clermont address launching the First Crusade. Preaching was typically accompanied by the granting of indulgences and a formal ceremony of "taking the cross" (*crucesignatio*), by which recruits publicly committed themselves and received a cloth cross to wear. Prominent crusade preachers included Bernard of Clairvaux, whose preaching tour helped launch the Second Crusade, and later the mendicant orders (Franciscans and Dominicans), who took over much crusade recruitment in the 13th and 14th centuries.

## Emic and Etic

Crusade preaching is an emic category, describing an institutional practice historical actors themselves organized, named, and regulated (through papal bulls authorizing preachers and indulgences).

## Persistence and Application

The practice long outlasted the Crusader States in the Levant, being redirected toward the Iberian Reconquista, Baltic crusades against pagan peoples, and later anti-Ottoman campaigns. A notable late instance was the 1514 crusade against the Ottomans proclaimed in Hungary by Cardinal Tamás Bakócz with papal authorization; the peasant army raised through this preaching campaign instead turned into the violent Dózsa Rebellion when noble resistance to its mobilization provoked the recruited peasantry to revolt against the Hungarian nobility rather than march against the Ottomans.
