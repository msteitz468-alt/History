# Monophysitism

**Monophysitism** (from Greek *monophysis*, “one nature”) designates, in conventional Latin and Byzantine polemic, the christology of large parts of **Syria and Egypt** that rejected the full **Chalcedonian** teaching of two natures (divine and human) united in one person of Christ, holding instead that Christ has **one nature**. Logan uses the traditional label; modern scholarship often prefers **Miaphysite** for the non-Chalcedonian churches of the East.

## Emic vs. etic

- **Emic:** Non-Chalcedonian churches understood themselves as defending Cyril of Alexandria’s unity of Christ against a “Nestorianizing” two-natures formula.
- **Etic:** “Monophysite” is an outsider/heresiological term; surviving Oriental Orthodox traditions articulate a refined one-nature (united) christology rather than a crude absorption of humanity into divinity.

## Logan’s summary (Justinianic context)

Much of Syria and Egypt rejected Chalcedon’s two-natures definition. Empress **Theodora** was a Monophysite partisan; Emperor [[justinian|Justinian]] made conciliatory efforts, but monophysite churches endured in Syria, Egypt, and Armenia—deepening the religious fracture that later Islamic conquests would inherit as a political-religious landscape.

> "Rather than believing that the one person, Christ, has two natures, one divine and one human, they believed that Christ has only one nature (monophusus)."

## Interaction with imperial church politics

Justinian’s monophysite problem entangled papal politics: he replaced Pope Silverius with Vigilius via Belisarius (537); the mid-sixth-century bishop of Rome came close to being an imperial puppet in Logan’s judgment—foreshadowing separate East–West courses. ## Limitations and critiques

Logan’s account is a brief textbook sketch for western medieval church history, not a specialist history of Miaphysite theology or the Council of Chalcedon (451) debates. Prefer specialist literature for doctrinal nuance and for the self-designations of the Oriental Orthodox churches.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Source: [[mitchell-later-roman-empire-2015]].

- **Fifth-century path to permanent gulf.** Under Theodosius II impetus from polemical bishops not eirenic emperor. Nestorius (Constantinople 428; Antiochene two-natures school) denied Mary Theotokos—Cyril of Alexandria attacked; Ephesus 431 condemned Nestorius before John of Antioch arrived; Nestorians fled to more tolerant Sasanian Persia. Ephesus “Robber Council” 449 (Dioscorus); Chalcedon 451 under extreme imperial pressure adopted two-natures formula aligned with Tome of Leo; Marcian’s edict declared controversy ended—delusory; Alexandria appalled (saw Chalcedon as crypto-Nestorian). Permanent gulf: Monophysite Syria/Egypt vs Constantinopolitan/Western orthodoxy. Basiliscus encyclical ~470s; Henotikon 482 (Acacius) won Alexandria/Antioch moderates, not extreme Monophysites; provoked Rome’s 484 excommunication (Acacian schism to 519). Anastasius’s Monophysite leanings + 512 trishagion addition sparked Constantinopolitan riots. “Doctrinal unity was achieved by imperial decree, but resulted in the permanent alienation of the Monophysite movement in the eastern empire.”
- **Justinian and Theodora.** Institutes and Nov. 6 preface unite priesthood and empire as God’s twin gifts; Justinian passionately advanced Chalcedonian/theopaschite doctrine and high-handed church control (536: nothing in the Church contrary to the emperor’s will), yet could not extirpate well-organized Monophysites. Theodora’s Monophysite sympathies (Procopius cynically calls the couple’s split a contrivance) enabled Ghassanid request to produce a full Monophysite hierarchy under Jacob Bar-Addai (thirty bishops across eastern Syria and Mesopotamia); John of Ephesus’ Anatolian missions show pragmatic delay before full confrontation.
