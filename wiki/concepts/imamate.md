## Definition

The **imamate** (*imāmah*) is the doctrine concerning who legitimately commands the Muslim community and on what basis. The word *imam* (literally 'one who stands in front') denoted both the prayer leader in worship and, by extension, the political-religious head of the community. Because in Islam there is no institutional separation between religious and political authority, the question of who rightfully held the imamate was simultaneously a theological, legal, and military question.

Hodgson frames this as the central problem of Islamic political thought in the first two centuries: the [[piety-minded-opposition|Piety-minded]] opposition under the [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyads]] agreed that "he who is the admittedly most pious and most knowledgeable of God's will ought to have the command, to be imam of the community; as to whom this implied in practice, they differed" ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v1-1974]]).

## The Three Answers

Early Muslim political thought produced three incompatible answers to the imamate question, each generating a distinct movement:

### 1. Kharijite Answer: Election by the Righteous

The [[kharijites|Kharijites]] held that any sufficiently pious Muslim could be chosen as imam by a righteous community. The imam's legitimacy was entirely conditional on continued moral conduct. A sinning imam forfeited his authority; the community must separate from — and, for the Azraqis, actively fight — all who accepted unworthy rule. This was the most egalitarian and the most destabilizing answer.

### 2. Shi'i Answer: Designation (*Nass*) through the House of Muhammad

The [[shia-islam|Shi'a]] held that the imam must come from Muhammad's house (Hashim) and specifically from the line of 'Ali. Crucially, the doctrine of *nass* (designation) held that each imam was appointed by his predecessor through divine inspiration, not elected by the community. This gave the imam a unique *'ilm* (knowledge) of God's will. Hodgson marks on his genealogical chart those candidates "whose position received, in their lifetime, at least some of its force from the doctrine of *nass*" — beginning with 'Ali himself and running through Husayn, 'Ali Zayn al-'Abidin, Muhammad al-Baqir, and Ja'far al-Sadiq.

The doctrine generated two subsidiary concepts:

- ***Mahdi***: The 'Guided One' expected to return at the end of time and fill the world with justice; applied first by Mukhtar to Ibn al-Hanafiyyah (685 CE), later increasingly eschatological
- ***Ghayba* (occultation)**: The belief that the imam had not died but was in hiding, awaiting the moment of return — first applied to Ibn al-Hanafiyyah; later became the core doctrine of Twelver Shi'ism regarding the Twelfth Imam

### 3. Sunni Answer: Community Consensus and the Historical Caliphate

The emerging Sunni position — crystallized precisely in opposition to the Shi'a after the [[abbasid-revolution-750|Abbasid Revolution]] — accepted the historical caliphate as legitimate even if imperfect, reserved the right to criticize rulers in the name of Islamic norms, and located religious authority in the scholarly *'ulama'* community rather than a divinely-guided single imam. The Sunni term is "Men of the Sunnah and the Jama'ah" — those who follow the established custom of the community.

## The 'Ilm Dimension

A crucial aspect of the imamate debate was the concept of *'ilm* (knowledge, specifically knowledge of God's will). For the piety-minded generally, *'ilm* — accumulated in hadith reports, Qur'anic interpretation, and legal reasoning — was the primary qualification for authority. The debates among Kharijis, Shi'is, and their rivals about who had 'ilm sharpened questions about:

- Free will vs. predestination (*qadar*): If God decrees all acts, how can humans be held morally accountable? The Kharijite emphasis on moral consequence required real human freedom; the Medina school tended toward determinism.
- The nature of faith (*iman*): Must faith produce observable moral consequences (Khariji position) or is inner intention decisive (Murji'i position)?
- The authority of hadith: Whose reports about Muhammad's conduct were reliable, and how could the community adjudicate conflicting claims?

These questions did not receive settled answers until the Shafi'i synthesis of the early ninth century. In the meantime, they generated the rich diversity of early Islamic jurisprudence captured in Hodgson's chart of 'Piety-Minded Groups in Marwani Times', which shows Kufah, Basra, Medina, Syria, Egypt, and Khurasan each with distinct schools taking distinct positions on these questions.

## The Ismaili Fatimid articulation (Jiwa)

The [[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid]] [[ismaili-movement|Ismaili]] imamate gave the *nass* doctrine its most fully
elaborated legal form. Following the line from Ja'far al-Sadiq through his son **Isma'il** (against the Twelver
line of Musa al-Kazim), the Ismaili Imams claimed both esoteric knowledge (*'ilm al-batin*) and, from 909,
actual caliphal office. [[qadi-al-numan|Qadi al-Nu'man]]'s ***Da'a'im al-Islam*** (958–960), written for
[[al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Mu'izz]], made ***walaya*** (devotional allegiance to the Imam) the **first of the
seven pillars** of Islam and identified the Imams as the *ulu'l-amr* ("holders of authority from amongst you,"
Qur'an 4:59) — the sole legitimate source of law and guidance. It also distinguished *iman* (faith, with access
to the *batin*) from *islam* (outward submission). The doctrine that **divine support (*ta'yid*) transfers from
Imam to Imam** — dramatised in Ismaili accounts of al-Mansur's deathbed — expressed the continuity the *nass*
required. See [[jiwa-fatimids-rise-2018]].

## Historiography

The imamate doctrine is contested both historically and among contemporary Islamic scholars. Sunni, Shi'i, and Ibadi traditions all have stakes in how early history is interpreted. Hodgson's treatment is notable for treating all three answers as internally coherent responses to a genuine problem, rather than treating the Sunni answer as default and the others as deviant. His 1955 article 'How Did the Early Shi'a Become Sectarian?' (*Journal of the American Oriental Society*, 75) is the key analytical piece cited in the source.
