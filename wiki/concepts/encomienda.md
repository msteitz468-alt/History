# Encomienda (and the Colonial Labor Systems)

The **encomienda** was the foundational institution of forced labor and tribute in
early [[colonial-latin-america|Spanish America]]: a grant (*encomendar*, "to
entrust") giving a Spaniard (the *encomendero*) the right to **tribute and labor**
from a specified group of Indians, in theory in return for protecting and
Christianizing them. It anchored the colonial economy where Spain's capital and
technology were thin, and it sits at the head of a longer sequence of labor regimes
that Burkholder and Johnson treat as the spine of colonial economic history.

## What it was — and was not

The encomienda was **not** a grant of land; it was a grant of *people* — the right
to their tribute and labor. The conquistadors, drawing on the Reconquest tradition
that military service earned lordship over subject peoples, regarded it as the
**natural reward of conquest** and the basis for becoming a New World aristocracy —
exactly what the Crown feared. It built on genuine pre-conquest institutions: both
the Aztec and Inca states had levied tribute and rotational labor, so in
Mesoamerica and the Andes the early *encomendero* often simply stepped into the
role of the old overlord, collecting through the existing **caciques/kurakas** as
middlemen.

## Caribbean origins and the Laws of Burgos

In the Antilles the encomienda was catastrophic: Indians were moved to the gold
fields, worked to death, and scarcely distinguishable from slaves — a system that
helped exterminate the Caribbean's native peoples. The resulting outcry produced
the **Laws of Burgos (1512)**, the first attempt to regulate treatment of the
Indians (work conditions, food, limits on punishment) — largely unenforced.

## The New Laws and decline

The encomienda's high political crisis came with the **New Laws of 1542**, pushed
by [[las-casas-bartolome|Bartolomé de las Casas]]: they banned Indian slavery,
forbade new encomiendas, and ordered existing ones to **revert to the Crown** on
the holder's death. The reaction was violent — **Gonzalo Pizarro's revolt** in Peru
(see [[spanish-conquest-of-peru-1532]]) — and the Crown relented, granting an extra
"life." But encomiendas reverted anyway through lack of heirs, and by 1570 about
three-quarters of Valley-of-Mexico encomienda income already belonged to the
Crown. The institution faded at the cores but **survived to the late eighteenth
century** in peripheries (Paraguay, Yucatán, Chile, New Granada). Its decline
shifted power from the conquistador aristocracy to royal officials.

## The labor sequence

Burkholder and Johnson lay out a classic (if regionally uneven) **progression** of
labor systems, achieved most fully in New Spain:

1. **Encomienda** → tribute and labor from entrusted Indians.
2. **Repartimiento / [[mita-and-andean-reciprocity|mita]]** — rotational forced-labor
   drafts (called *repartimiento* in New Spain from the 1550s, *mita* in Peru from
   Toledo's 1570s reorganization). Communities supplied a quota of workers for set
   periods; the [[potosi-and-colonial-silver|Potosí mita]] (~13,000–13,500 men/year)
   was the most infamous.
3. **Free wage labor and debt peonage** — as the native population collapsed,
   *hacendados* and miners contracted directly with Indians and *castas*, often
   binding them by advanced wages (debt peonage); by ~1630 free wage labor had
   largely supplanted *repartimiento* in New Spain. Wages were held artificially low
   by employer power and "wage custom" — a true labor market "seldom existed."
4. **Yanaconaje** — in the Andes, *yanaconas* (Indians detached from their *ayllus*)
   settled on Spanish estates as bound labor, often transferred *with* the estate
   when it was sold; by 1600 nearly as numerous as Indians still in traditional
   ayllus.
5. **Indian slavery** — chattel slavery, justified by "just war" and cannibalism,
   common where there was no mineral or agricultural surplus; central to early
   **Brazil** (sugar) and Central America until the New Laws, and persisting on
   frontiers (Chile, northern Mexico, the São Paulo **bandeirante** raids) into the
   eighteenth century.
6. **Repartimiento de bienes** — the *corregidor*'s forced sale of goods (mules,
   cloth) on credit to Indian communities at rigged prices; matured in the late
   seventeenth century and a major spur to Indian rebellion.

Indians were not passive: **flight** was the great weapon. To escape the mita,
tribute, and the *reparto*, hundreds of thousands abandoned their *ayllus*,
becoming *forasteros* (resident "strangers" exempt from the mita) or *yanaconas* —
hollowing out the very communities the system taxed.

## Limitations and critiques

The encomienda is contested terrain. Older accounts treated it as straightforward
slavery; the New Philology and ethnohistory stress **continuity with pre-conquest
tribute** and **indigenous agency** (caciques manipulating the system, mass flight
reshaping demography). Its relation to the *[[hacienda]]* — whether the great
estate descended from the encomienda or arose separately from land grants and the
market — is a classic debate (resolved largely in favor of separate origins). The
whole subject is freighted by the [[black-legend|Black Legend]].

## The indigenous testimony (Guamán Poma)

The fullest **indigenous** indictment of the colonial labour regime is
[[guaman-poma-de-ayala|Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala's]] *[[guaman-poma-nueva-coronica|Nueva corónica
y buen gobierno]]* (c. 1615). Written and illustrated by an Andean nobleman who had served the
colonial administration, it catalogues — case by case, in text and in nearly 400 drawings — the
abuses of *encomenderos*, *corregidores*, priests, and mine-owners under the *encomienda* and
*mita*: extortion, forced labour, sexual violence, and the demographic collapse of the Andean
population. Appealing over the colonists' heads to the king, Guamán Poma protested that the Indians
were treated "worse than slaves brought from Guinea" — a rare interior, indigenous view of what the
encomienda meant to those subject to it (a *situated* witness; see the page for his biases).
