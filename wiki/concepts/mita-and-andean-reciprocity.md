# Mit'a and Andean Reciprocity

The [[inca-empire|Inca empire]] ran **without markets, money, or commerce** over most of its territory —
a fact that astonished Europeans and makes it one of the great alternative models of political economy in
world history. In its place stood a system of **labour tax (*mit'a*)** embedded in a deep Andean ethic of
**reciprocity** and **redistribution**, analyzed above all by **John V. Murra** and synthesized in
Terence D'Altroy's [[daltroy-incas-2004|*The Incas*]].

## Reciprocity and the ayllu

The base unit of Andean society was the **ayllu** — a kin group holding land in common and bound by
**reciprocal labour obligation**: households worked one another's fields and herds in expected mutual
exchange. The Inca state scaled this **household logic up to empire**. Subjects owed not a share of their
goods but a share of their **labour**; in return the state was expected to **reciprocate** — by hosting
laborers at lavish state expense (food and *chicha* maize-beer from the storehouses) and by providing
security and ritual order. Tribute was thus framed not as extraction but as **generalized reciprocity
with the divine ruler**.

## The mit'a

The **mit'a** ("turn") was **rotational labour service** owed to the state: communities sent workers in
turns to farm state and church lands, herd llamas, mine, build and maintain the
**[[qhapaq-nan|roads]]**, bridges, terraces, and storehouses, staff the [[cuzco|provincial centres]],
and — as **military service** — fill the modular conscript armies. Because the obligation was **labour,
not goods**, the state had to **mobilize, feed, and organize** it: hence the **decimal administration**
(kuraka lords over units of 10–10,000), the periodic **census**, and the **[[khipu|khipu]]** records that
tracked who owed what. The state's vast **storehouses (*qollqa*)** were the visible counterpart — the
redistributive reserve that fed workers and armies.

## Vertical complementarity

Murra's other great insight, the **"vertical archipelago"**: in the steep Andes, distinct
**ecological tiers** (high puna for herding and freeze-dried potatoes/*chuño*; valleys for maize; warm
lowlands for coca, chili, cotton) lie close together vertically. Andean communities secured access to all
of them by maintaining **colonies at multiple altitudes** rather than trading through markets — so a
single ayllu or state could draw on the full range of products **without commerce**. The Inca exploited
exactly this when they built royal estates and resettled *mitmaq* colonists to command complementary
zones.

## Significance

Mit'a-and-reciprocity is the answer to how the Inca built roads, terraces, cities, and armies on a
continental scale **without money, markets, or writing**: they commanded **labour**, organized by census
and khipu, legitimated by reciprocity and the sacred kingship, and provisioned by redistribution. It
makes Tawantinsuyu the prime case (with the [[aztec-empire|Aztec]] tribute economy) that the Americas
were a **parallel world of state formation** running on principles — reciprocity, redistribution,
verticality — quite unlike the market-and-coin logic of Afro-Eurasia. The Spanish later **perverted the
mit'a** into the brutal forced-labour draft for the **Potosí silver mines**, severing the reciprocal
obligation that had given it meaning.

## The colonial mita (Burkholder & Johnson)

Burkholder and Johnson detail how the Spanish kept the *name* and rotational *form*
of the mita while inverting its meaning. Viceroy **Francisco de Toledo's
reorganization of 1572** delivered ~**13,000–13,500 Indian men a year** to
[[potosi-and-colonial-silver|Potosí]] (and ~2,000 to the Huancavelica mercury mine):
one-seventh of the adult males of sixteen highland provinces, serving one year in
seven, hauling ore by candlelight amid mercury vapor and (later) blasting. The
authors' verdict: a system that "began as reciprocal obligations within the *ayllu*"
had become "the forced transfer of wealth from poor Indians to rich Spaniards." Its
deepest effect was **demographic flight**: to escape the mita and tribute,
hundreds of thousands abandoned their *ayllus* to become **[[encomienda|*forasteros*
and *yanaconas*]]** (exempt from the draft) — by the 1680s the Indian population of
Upper Peru had halved, and in the mita provinces half the population was reclassified
as forastero/yanacona, intensifying the burden on those who remained. The colonial
mita thus sits within the wider [[encomienda|sequence of colonial labor systems]].
