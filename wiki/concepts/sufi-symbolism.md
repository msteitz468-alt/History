## Definition and Scope

Sufi symbolism refers to the repertoire of conventional images through which Persian (and later Turkish, Urdu, and other Islamicate) mystical poets expressed the ineffable experience of divine love, longing, and union. These symbols function on two simultaneous registers: at the literal level, they invoke sensory experience (the beauty of a rose, the taste of wine, the face of a beloved); at the mystical level, they point to theological realities (the divine essence, the ecstasy of union, the soul's yearning for God). This deliberate ambiguity is not a flaw but a structural feature — the oscillation between the two levels enacts the mystic's own movement between the world of appearances and the world of divine reality.

Schimmel writes that in the poetry of Ḥāfiẓ and his tradition, "the subtle harmony and ultimate equilibrium between the different spheres of being is best maintained... transparent as the air of that town, and fragrant as rose oil distilled from the centuries-old tradition of love of the divine revealed in visible forms" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

## The Core Symbol-Clusters

### The Rose and the Nightingale (gul u bulbul)

The most pervasive symbol pair in Persian Sufi poetry. The nightingale (bulbul) yearns ceaselessly for the rose (gul) but can never possess it; in mystical interpretation, "it is, in mystical language, the soul longing for eternal beauty, as Rūzbihān defined it" (Schimmel, [[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]). The nightingale "infinitely repeats the praise of the rose without tiring, tells of its longing, sings hymns from the Koran of the rose (i.e., its petals), suffers without complaint the stings of the thorns."

The philosophical implication drawn by Iqbal — that longing itself, not union, is the highest state of the soul, because "only longing gives the soul bird the capacity to sing" — represents one extreme of interpretation. The more common reading treats the nightingale's song as praise and the rose's beauty as a veil over the divine.

The rose in this system also carries the color red, which links it to blood, martyrdom, and the burned heart of the lover — especially in Rūmī's imagery where "red tulips with their dark scars in their 'hearts' may grow out of the burned hearts of lovers."

### The Soul-Bird

The equation soul = bird is broader than the nightingale alone. ʿAṭṭār's *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* (Conference of the Birds) systematized this identification: thirty birds quest for the mythical Sīmurgh and discover that *sī murgh* ("thirty birds") is the Sīmurgh — the sought object is the seeker itself. Schimmel calls this "the most ingenious pun in Persian literature, expressing so marvelously the experience of the identity of the soul with the divine essence" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]).

Other bird symbols: the falcon — Rūmī's frequent image for the noble soul exiled among crows, longing to return to its master's fist (the word *bāz*, "falcon," puns on *bāz*, "back again"). The dove calls *kū kū* ("Where, where?") — eternally asking the way to the beloved. Each bird's characteristic sound or behavior becomes a theological statement.

### Wine, Tavern, and Drunkenness

The wine symbolism (šarāb, may) operates as the most theologically fraught of the symbol-clusters because wine is prohibited in Islamic law, giving the imagery a built-in transgressive charge. In Sufi usage:

- **Wine** = the intoxicating experience of divine love or mystical knowledge (maʿrifa)
- **Tavern** (maykada) = the gathering place of those who have abandoned sober religious convention for direct experience
- **Drunkenness** (masti) = the state of absorption in God, the *sukr* (intoxication) that precedes *ṣaḥw* (second sobriety)
- **The Winebringer/Cupbearer** (sāqī) = the spiritual guide who pours out grace
- **The Beloved** (maʿshūq) = simultaneously the human beloved and the divine

The ambiguity is intentional. Schimmel notes that in the poetry of Ḥāfiẓ, ʿIrāqī, and Jāmī, "their ambiguity is intended, the oscillation between the two levels" ([[schimmel-mystical-dimensions-of-islam-1975]]). The mystic plays on the reader's uncertainty: is this love poetry or devotional verse? The answer is: it is both, and the two cannot be separated.

### The Beloved (Maʿshūq) and the Theme of Human Beauty

A structural feature of Persian Sufi poetics is that the human beloved — typically described as a beautiful youth with dark curling hair, languid eyes, and rosy cheeks — functions simultaneously as an earthly person and as a theophany (a manifestation of divine beauty). Rūzbihān Baqlī theorized this most explicitly: the mystic who contemplates beauty in created forms is not deluded but is reading a legitimate sign of God.

This produced the charged and contested figure of *naẓar*, "contemplation" (sometimes "gaze at the unbearded"), which generated controversy within Sufism itself.

The beloved's characteristics become a symbolic lexicon: tresses = the divine mysteries that entangle the seeker; the beauty spot (khāl) = the unique divine essence; the sword-glance = the annihilating power of divine majesty.

### Flowers, Nature, and the Cosmos as Dhikr

The entire created world participates in the symbol system. Every created thing praises God in its characteristic "tongue of the state" (lisān al-ḥāl). The lily praises with ten tongues; the narcissus looks toward the creator with languid eyes; the violet sits in dark Sufi garb meditating. Rain is divine mercy; sun is divine glory; the breeze is loving-kindness making growing things dance.

The ruby is the "heart of the stone transformed through patience and shedding its blood into a priceless jewel" — a microcosmic image of the soul's purification through suffering. The millstone turns restlessly like the Sufi; the waterwheel sighs like the separated lover.

This pervasive theophanic vision — "the eye of the mystic who is enraptured in love sees traces of eternal beauty everywhere" — is explicitly linked in the tradition to Quranic warrant: "We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and in themselves" (Sūra 41:53).

## Historical Development

The symbolic vocabulary crystallized in the poetry of **Sanāʾī of Ghazna** (d. c. 1131), who composed the first Sufi didactic mathnawī, the *Ḥadīqa al-Ḥaqīqa* (Garden of Truth). **ʿAṭṭār of Nishapur** (c. 1145–c. 1221) elaborated the bird symbolism and the allegory of the soul's journey through the *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr*. **Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī** (1207–1273) extended this repertoire, adding the reed flute (*nay*) as a symbol of the soul cut from its divine origin — the image that opens his *Mathnawī*: "Listen to the reed, how it tells a tale of separation." The **ghazal** form — with its compressed lyric intensity and its refrain — became the natural vehicle for the rose-and-nightingale, wine, and beloved imagery. **Ḥāfiẓ of Shiraz** (c. 1315–1390) brought this tradition to its most refined lyric expression. See [[rumi]], [[attar-farid-al-din]], [[persian-sufi-poetry]].

## Turkish Adaptation

The symbol-system was adopted wholesale into Ottoman and Anatolian Turkish mystical poetry, with important inflections. **Yūnus Emre** (c. 1240–1321) naturalized the imagery into Turkish while adding images drawn from Anatolian landscape: the wailing waterwheel cut from its forest, the stone-breasted mountains separating lover from master. The Bektashi poets extended the wine symbolism in antinomian directions, using it as a deliberate transgression against orthodox convention. See [[yunus-emre]], [[bektashi-order]].

## Relationship to Doctrine

The symbol-system is not merely decorative but carries theological freight:

- **Fanāʾ** (annihilation) is symbolized by the lover's total absorption in the beloved, the moth's burning in the flame, the drop's return to the ocean
- **Baqāʾ** (subsistence in God) is the state beyond annihilation, symbolized by the wine of subsistence poured by the divine sāqī
- **Waḥdat al-wujūd** (unity of being, the doctrine associated with Ibn ʿArabī) finds expression in the all-pervading wine metaphor: "Sometimes we call Thee wine, sometimes goblet / Sometimes we call Thee corn, and sometimes snare" (Jāmī)
- **The Prophetic model** is preserved: Muḥammad's night journey (miʿrāj) to divine presence is the archetype for the mystic's journey through the stages to union

## Historiography

The question of sincerity — whether the wine and beloved imagery is "really" mystical or "really" erotic — was a recurrent debate both within Islamic tradition and in Western scholarship. Schimmel's position is that the ambiguity is essential: the two registers cannot be separated, and attempts to reduce the poetry to one or the other level miss the structural point. Rūzbihān Baqlī's *ʿAbhar al-ʿĀshiqīn* (Jasmine of the Lovers) offers the most sustained theological defense of this duality from within the tradition.
