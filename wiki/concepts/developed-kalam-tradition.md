## Definition and Scope

The "developed kalam tradition" designates the mature phase of Islamic dialectical theology that emerged from the tenth century and reached its characteristic form through the work of [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazali]] (d. 1111). It is distinguished from the early kalam of the [[mutazilism|Mu'tazila]] and the first generation of [[asharism|Ash'arites]] by several features:

1. **Systematic absorption of falsafa**: The developed tradition incorporated Avicennan logic, ontological categories (essence/existence, contingent/necessary being), and much of the technical vocabulary of [[islamic-philosophy|Islamic Peripatetic philosophy]], even while kalam theologians like al-Ghazali polemicized against it. As Oliver Leaman observes in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]]: "falsafa's key concerns and methods lived on, and flourished, within developed kalam."

2. **Formal creed literature**: The developed tradition produced a genre of short, memorisable credos (*'aqa'id*, sing. *'aqida*) with their supercommentaries and glosses, which defined and transmitted Sunni orthodoxy in the madrasa curriculum.

3. **Integration with Sufism**: Al-Ghazali's project of "reviving" the Islamic sciences involved incorporating kalam's rational achievements into a spiritually reanimated framework. His *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din* (Revival of the Religious Sciences) became the paradigm for later synthetic works.

4. **The two-school structure**: By the end of the tenth century, the Ash'ari and Maturidi schools together with the traditionists formed the recognized pillars of Sunni theological orthodoxy, providing a stable framework for subsequent centuries of commentary and elaboration.

## The Impact of al-Ghazali

Al-Ghazali (d. 1111) is the pivotal figure in the transition from early to developed kalam. He attacked what he identified as the three positions in Peripatetic philosophy amounting to unbelief (*kufr*): (1) the claim that the world is uncreated (eternal); (2) the denial of God's knowledge of particulars; and (3) the rejection of bodily resurrection. His *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (Incoherence of the Philosophers) demonstrated, on the philosophers' own logical terms, that their conclusions do not follow from their premises — thereby engaging falsafa at its highest level of sophistication rather than simply condemning it.

Crucially, Ghazali's engagement with philosophy actually ensured its continuation in the Islamic world. His second type of philosophical work — expository texts like the *Maqasid al-Falasifa* (Aims of the Philosophers) and *al-Qistas al-Mustaqim* (The Straight Method) — defined an accepted "textbook" genre of falsafa studied in scholastic traditions, particularly in Ottoman institutions. This paradox is noted in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]]: "Ghazali's work actually ensured in more ways than one the continuity of philosophy in Islam."

Ghazali's defining characteristic as a kalam thinker was his reliance on rational argument of a dialectical type — arguing, against the philosophers, that their conclusions fail on their own criteria. This is the method of mature kalam: it does not refuse philosophical argument but beats philosophy at its own game.

## The Ash'ari Systematization: al-Baqillani and al-Juwayni

The developed Ash'ari kalam was systematized through a chain of major thinkers:

- **[[al-baqillani|Abu Bakr al-Baqillani]]** (d. 1013): His *Kitab al-Tamhid* (Prolegomena) systematically laid out the foundational principles of Ash'arism, including the doctrine of divine attributes and the atomic theory of substance.
- **'Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi** (d. 1037): Further refined the Ash'ari synthesis.
- **[[al-juwayni|al-Juwayni]]**: Brought the system to completion as an original form of thought in his *Kitab al-Irshad* (Guidance).
- **[[fakhr-al-din-al-razi|Fakhr al-Din al-Razi]]** (d. 1209): Continued systematic development, now fully integrating Avicennan philosophical categories.

The late period was dominated by creeds and their commentaries. Al-Taftazani (d. 1389/90), al-Sharif al-Jurjani (d. 1413), al-Iji (d. 1355), Abu 'Ali al-Sanusi (d. 1490), and Ibrahim al-Laqani (d. 1641) authored influential texts that formed the standard madrasa theological syllabus, particularly under the Ottomans.

## Atomism and Occasionalism in the Developed Tradition

A defining ontological commitment of the Ash'arite strand of developed kalam is the atomic theory of substance combined with [[occasionalism-islamic|occasionalism]]: the world consists of atoms and accidents whose combination and recombination depends entirely on God's continuous creative act. There is no natural causation — what appears as causal sequence is God's habitual but revocable pattern of creating the appropriate accidents at the appropriate times. This ontology serves as the theological grounding for divine omnipotence and renders any reification of abstract concepts or universal essences doubly problematic. As the text notes: "Since the argument is that the world is at root atomistic, and so is kept together in its present fairly stable form only by the constant intervention of the Deity, the reification of concepts is even more inaccurate than treating the material world as though it were independently subsistent and real." ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]])

## The Maturidi Parallel

[[maturidism|Maturidism]], associated with the Hanafi legal school and dominant in Central Asia and later the Ottoman Empire, developed in parallel with Ash'arism. Though closer to the Ash'arites than to the Mu'tazila, Maturidism gave a greater role to unaided reason in establishing basic moral truths and maintained that faith (*iman*) neither increases nor decreases. The two schools formed a complementary architecture of Sunni orthodoxy: Ash'arism predominant in Shafi'i and Maliki contexts; Maturidism in Hanafi contexts. Late commentaries such as al-Taftazani's commentary on al-Nasafi drew on both traditions.

## Shi'i Parallel Development

The developed kalam tradition was not exclusively Sunni. Part II of Chapter 4 of [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]] (by Sajjad Rizvi) traces the parallel development of Twelver Shi'i theology, which began with the adoption and transformation of Mu'tazilite rational theology by al-Shaykh al-Mufid (d. 1022) and his school. Key figures include:

- **al-Sharif al-Murtada** (d. 1044)
- **al-Shaykh al-Tusi** (d. 1067): His *Tajrid al-i'tiqad* (Epitome of Doctrine) attracted commentaries from Sunni and Shi'i scholars alike.
- **Ibn al-Mutahhar al-Hilli** (d. 1325): Systematizer of Twelver theology, drawing on a more philosophical Mu'tazilism.

Twelver theology developed around two distinctive pillars — the imamate and divine justice ('adl) — absent from Sunni kalam, and eventually achieved its own synthesis with [[post-avicennan-philosophy|post-Avicennan philosophy]] in the [[school-of-isfahan|School of Isfahan]] (17th century).

## Ibn Taymiyya's Critique

[[ibn-taymiyya|Ibn Taymiyya]] (d. 1328) mounted the most systematic attack on the foundations of kalam from within the Hanbali tradition. He argued against the very concept of definition (*hadd*) as presupposing a distinction between essential and accidental properties that is epistemologically unjustified, and against the reliability of syllogism as a method. His nominalism — "universals should be analysed entirely in terms of the individuals which constitute them" — undermined the realist ontology that underpinned both falsafa and kalam. Ibn Taymiyya remained a marginal figure in his own time; only from the eighteenth century with the Wahhabi movement did his anti-rationalist strand become politically significant.

## Historiography

The developed kalam tradition has often been portrayed by Western scholarship (following Ernest Renan's nineteenth-century narrative) as marking the death of philosophical inquiry in Islam. This is rejected by contemporary scholars: falsafa did not die but migrated into the Persian-speaking world and into kalam itself; the Arabic-speaking regions saw a revival in the nineteenth-century *Nahda*. The claim that al-Ghazali "destroyed" philosophy is specifically addressed and rebutted in [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]]: "This is not true; for one thing falsafa as a discipline did not die."

The characterization of developed kalam as "anti-rationalist" is also misleading. Even the critics of kalam defended their arguments rationally. The more illuminating distinction is between different theories of the *scope* of reason — not between rationalism and its absence.
