# Multiple Vietnams

The proposition, most systematically advanced by Christopher Goscha, that the territory and identity now called "Vietnam" has almost always existed as a plurality of overlapping and competing political and cultural projects rather than a single, continuous national essence marching toward inevitable unification. The familiar "S-shaped" Vietnam was a unified polity for only roughly forty-three years in the early nineteenth century (from Gia Long's 1802 consolidation until the French conquests) plus brief moments in 1945–46 and after 1975–76. For most of its history, multiple "Vietnams" coexisted.

## Core Thesis and Scope

Goscha's central claim is that "there was thus nothing necessarily aberrant about the existence of ‘two Vietnams’ during the second half of the twentieth century." The book aims "to provide a new account of the plurality of Vietnams from the past to the present, one that avoids creating a great divide at the ‘French colonial moment’ of 1858." Pre-colonial Dai Viet already concealed "two hiding behind that name—one which had been anchored in the Red River delta for centuries ... the other moving its way southward past Hue." The Nguyen imperial project, French colonial divisions (Tonkin/Annam/Cochinchina), the 1945–54 contest between the DRV and the Associated State / RVN, and post-1975 tensions between the socialist state and highland/diaspora "other Vietnams" are presented as variations on a long pattern of multiplicity, not aberrations from a timeless unity.

This is an etic analytical framework offered to historians; it is not an emic self-description used by most historical actors (though regional identities were keenly felt).

## Historical Configurations

- **Pre-colonial and early modern**: Northern Red River core (Dong Son culture, Chinese domination 111 BCE–938 CE, subsequent independent dynasties) versus the southern expansion (Nam tiến) that produced a distinct Nguyen polity by the seventeenth century, with its own capital, commercial orientation, and relations with Cham and Khmer populations. The Tay Son rebellion (late eighteenth century) briefly constituted a third disruptive center.
- **Nguyen unification (1802–c. 1850s)**: The first sustained "S-shaped" state from the Red River to the Mekong under Gia Long and Minh Mạng; even then, highland and border regions remained only loosely integrated.
- **Colonial era**: French administration deliberately preserved and accentuated regional distinctions (directly ruled Cochinchina colony in the south; protectorates of Annam and Tonkin with nominal Nguyen emperors). The Indochinese Union further embedded Vietnam in a multi-ethnic colonial frame.
- **1945–1975**: The August Revolution briefly raised the prospect of a single broad-front DRV; partition at Geneva created the DRV and RVN as rival state projects. Highland minorities, religious sects (Cao Dai, Hoa Hao), and non-communist republican networks constituted additional "Vietnams."
- **Post-1975**: Official Socialist Republic of Vietnam versus diaspora communities, persistent highland distinctiveness, and internal calls for a return to the pluralist or republican possibilities of 1946.

## Analytical Payoff

The framework recovers contingency and lost alternatives. It challenges both official Vietnamese historiography (which presents a seamless national resistance culminating in the communist victory) and certain Western accounts (e.g., Frances FitzGerald's *Fire in the Lake*) that treat a unitary "Vietnam" as an ancient given. By insisting on "thoughts of the alternatives," it highlights the 1946 constitution's republican experiment, southern regional political cultures, and the possibility that other outcomes (a more plural post-colonial state, a surviving southern republic) were structurally plausible rather than foreclosed by deep history.

## Historiography, Limitations, and Critiques

Goscha draws on the post-1990s opening of Vietnamese, French, and international archives and the growth of regionally focused scholarship. The approach is contested inside Vietnam, where it can be read as undermining the legitimacy narrative of the unified socialist state. Critics may argue that it underplays real cultural, linguistic, and historical threads of connection across regions or that it risks reifying colonial administrative categories. For wiki purposes it is treated as a powerful corrective lens, not the sole truth; it is balanced by sources that emphasize unifying dynamics and the agency of the eventual victors.
