## Definition

A **polis** (plural: *poleis*) was the characteristic form of state and society in ancient Greece. The word simultaneously meant “city,” “state,” and “city-state.” As Mogens Herman Hansen summarises from the Copenhagen Polis Centre’s research:

> “Polis is the ancient Greek word for ‘city’, ‘state’ and the combination of city and state, the ‘city-state’.”

The polis was a small, autonomous (or semi-autonomous) polity consisting of an urban centre (*asty*) and its surrounding territory (*chora*), whose citizens formed a political community that governed itself through institutions (assembly, council, magistrates, courts). There were approximately 1,500 such poleis in the Archaic and Classical periods (c. 650–323 BCE).

## Scope and Characteristics (Hansen 2006)

Hansen’s systematic analysis of the evidence (literary, epigraphic, archaeological, and the Centre’s Inventory of 1,035 poleis) yields these core traits, shared with other city-state cultures but realised on the largest scale in Greece:

- **Not Athens-centric**: Athens was anomalous in size and importance. The typical polis had only a few hundred to a few thousand adult male citizens and a territory of a few dozen to ~2,500 km². Over 80% of poleis had territories ≤200 km².
- **City + State**: The polis was both an urban settlement and a political entity. Of 384 poleis attested as “city,” 364 were also poleis in the political sense; every attested political polis had an urban centre.
- **High urbanisation**: In small and middle-sized poleis a majority (often 60–80% or more) of the population lived inside the walls as *Ackerbürger* (farmer-citizens who commuted daily to their fields). This inverts the modern assumption of dispersed rural populations.
- **Economic base**: Trade- and market-centred economies. Long-distance trade (including grain) was essential to sustain the urban populations.
- **Political decision-making**: Laws and decrees were often passed by majority vote in assemblies after debate (though participation was limited to citizens).
- **Interaction and federalism**: Intense interaction produced leagues, hegemonic empires, and federal states (*koina*) with dual citizenship in some cases.
- **Scale**: The Greek city-state culture was the largest in world history — geographically, demographically (~7.5 million minimum total Greek population in the mid-4th century BCE, possibly 8–10 million), and by duration (roughly 1,200 years of city-state culture).

Four recurring features of city-state cultures (including the Greek):

1. Degree of urbanisation unexampled in macro-states before the Industrial Revolution.
2. Economy based on trade and centred on the city’s market.
3. Political decision-making often by assembly majority after debate (not always monarchical).
4. Interaction producing leagues of states and federal states.

Modern macro-states often resemble ancient city-state cultures more than ancient macro-states in these respects.

## Rise and End of the Greek City-State Culture

The polis emerged from the Greek Dark Age through processes of *synoikism* (political fusion of villages) in the 8th–7th centuries BCE, catalysed by renewed trade, literacy, and the need for corporate action (cultic and military). Archaeological evidence (Smyrna, Eretria, Miletos, etc.) and literary sources (Homer onward) show walled urban centres appearing from the 8th–7th centuries, contemporaneous with state formation.

The culture of independent or semi-independent poleis effectively ended as a system of peer polities with the rise of Macedonian hegemony and the Hellenistic kingdoms. In the Hellenistic and Roman periods most poleis became dependent states, yet retained substantial internal self-government (*autonomia* remained an ideal). The polis as a self-governing political community died out only in late antiquity after Justinian.

## Polis as City

Settlement pattern, size and population studies, demography, and the relationship between the urban centre and its hinterland are treated in detail by Hansen. Key quantitative findings (from the Inventory and surveys):

- Average walled area ~65 ha (median 40 ha).
- >80% of poleis had populations >1,000; at least 10% >10,000.
- Total Greek population estimate: minimum 7.5 million (mid-4th c.), with ~40% living outside the homeland in colonies or Hellenised communities.
- Sparta re-evaluated as a significant conurbation (3–4 km², density ≥100/ha, ~40,000 population) despite lacking Classical walls.

## Polis as State

The polis in the political sense was a *koinōnia politōn politeias* (Aristotle): a community of (adult male) citizens regarding a constitution. 

Institutions present in virtually all poleis: prytaneion (with eternal hearth of Hestia), council (*boulē*), agora (initially political, later with stoas and shops), magistrates (often in boards), courts, and (in most) city walls. 

Constitutions fell into three types (rule of one/tyranny, few/oligarchy, many/democracy). A 4th-century survey yields roughly 59 democracies, 47 oligarchies, and 39 tyrannies. Even non-democracies often had assemblies.

Dependent poleis (*hypēkooi poleis*) retained many internal institutions and the right to self-government in local affairs while lacking full external independence. Clear criteria distinguish them from mere civic subdivisions (*dēmoi*, *kōmai*, *phratriai*, etc.).

## Stasis, Religion, Army, and Inter-Polis Relations

*Stasis* (civil war/factional violence) was endemic: 279 outbreaks recorded in 122 poleis. Group loyalty frequently trumped polis loyalty. Many poleis suffered *andrapodismos* or destruction (121 known cases), though many were later refounded.

Religion was powerful but not the central unifying factor (contra Fustel de Coulanges). Clear sacred/profane boundaries existed; women participated extensively in cults and festivals (including some exclusive to them); the polis organised and funded many cults and festivals.

Every polis maintained its own army, centred on the hoplite phalanx. Service was class-linked (citizens paid for armour); mercenaries became increasingly important.

Inter-polis relations included proxeny, envoys, alliances, hegemonic leagues (Delian, Peloponnesian), and federations (e.g., Boiotian under Theban leadership with clear hierarchy of dependent members and eventual dual citizenship in some federations).

## The Hellenistic Polis

By the later Hellenistic and Roman periods, nearly all poleis were dependent, yet *autonomia* (self-government in internal affairs) remained an ideal. Democracy was initially preferred, then oligarchy revived under Roman rule. Many poleis abandoned armies and navies. Women gained greater (non-political) public visibility. Non-citizens entered the *ephēbeia*, which broadened into a key vehicle of Hellenisation. Monumental public architecture increased; social differentiation became more visible. Hundreds of new inland poleis were founded (especially Seleucid), equipped with Greek institutions but politically dependent.

## Historiography

Prior scholarship was heavily Athens-centric and often treated the polis anecdotally or through the lens of philosophical utopias (Plato, Aristotle) or Sparta. The Copenhagen Polis Centre’s Inventory (2004) and Hansen’s syntheses provided the first comprehensive, evidence-based account covering all known poleis rather than a few famous examples, combined with explicit comparative world-historical framing (37 city-state cultures).

Key revisions: the polis continued as the leading form of state/society well into the Roman period (not “destroyed” in 338 or 323 BCE); most population in typical poleis was urban; the polis was both city and state (near-total overlap); Weber’s ideal type fits the evidence better than the “Finley orthodoxy” allowed; dependence and hierarchy were normal, not exceptional.

This source (Hansen 2006) is the primary modern reference for the definition, typology, quantitative scale, and comparative placement of the Greek polis.
