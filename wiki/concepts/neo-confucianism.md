## Definition

**Neo-Confucianism** is the revitalized and philosophically systematized form of
[[confucianism|Confucianism]] that emerged in [[song-dynasty|Song]] China during the
eleventh and twelfth centuries, providing the dominant intellectual, ethical, and
political framework of late imperial China and the wider East Asian world for the
next eight centuries. [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] treats it as the **"revitalized
Confucian model of imperial sovereignty and civil bureaucracy"** central to Song
state formation (von Glahn, Ch 19) and as the Chinese instance of the
[[cultural-crystallization|tenth-to-thirteenth-century cultural crystallization]]
(Wittrock, Ch 8) — a rearticulation of an [[axial-age|Axial-Age]] legacy into new
institutional and metaphysical form.

## The Reorientation

Classical Confucianism was primarily an ethical and political teaching. Neo-Confucian
thinkers — culminating in the great synthesizer **Zhu Xi** (1130–1200) — recast it as
a comprehensive **metaphysical and cosmological system**, in part to answer the
challenge that [[buddhism|Buddhism]] and Daoism had posed on questions of mind,
cosmos, and self-cultivation. Its key features:

- A metaphysics of **principle (*li*)** and **material force (*qi*)**, giving Confucian
  ethics a cosmological grounding.
- A program of **moral self-cultivation** ("the investigation of things," the
  extension of knowledge) open in principle to all, making the cultivation of virtue
  the basis of social and political order.
- The canonization of the **Four Books** (the *Analects*, *Mencius*, *Great Learning*,
  *Doctrine of the Mean*) as the core curriculum — and, from the later imperial
  period, the basis of the [[educational-institutions|civil-service examinations]].

## State, Society, and the East Asian Sphere

Neo-Confucianism fused intellectual renewal with the institutional needs of the Song
**examination state**, supplying the ideology of a meritocratic scholar-official elite
(the *literati*) and a vision of society as a moral hierarchy governed by the cultivated.
It spread, with the Chinese state model, across the
[[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian cultural sphere]] — becoming the official
orthodoxy of Chosŏn Korea and a major force in Tokugawa Japan and Vietnam. In
Wittrock's framing it is one of the new "institutional macro-arrangements" of the
medieval crystallization that shaped the early modern and modern world.

## The Early Modern Confucian State and Its Challengers (CWH VI)

[[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]] (Menegon & Cogan) shows Neo-Confucianism as the
**ideological backbone of the Ming–Qing "regulatory state."** The Ming founder
**Hongwu** (r. 1368–98) made Confucian ideology, moral codes, and ritual the basis
of both the imperial and local cults, issuing moral edicts and prescribing village
community rituals "with unprecedented intensity," and found ready allies among
fundamentalist Confucian gentry in suppressing "heterodox" Buddhist, Daoist, and
shamanic practice. But orthodoxy was not monolithic: the **School of the Mind** of
**Wang Yangming** (1472–1529) broke from Zhu Xi's emphasis on external "investigation
of things," locating moral knowledge in the **innate mind** and offering an
"unprecedented openness" to the [[buddhism|Buddhist]] ideal of self-realization —
fueling the late-Ming revival of lay Buddhism and a more fluid religious culture.
Across the [[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian sphere]], the early modern period
saw the **Confucian transformation of Chosŏn Korea** (rigid lineage and ancestral
ritual) and the commercial, scholarly flowering of Confucian learning in
**Tokugawa Japan** (Itō Jinsai, Ogyū Sorai) — see
[[intercultural-relations-early-modern]]. The tradition thus remained, into the
eighteenth century, the dynamic framework of statecraft, education, and contested
orthodoxy rather than a frozen inheritance.

## The Tokugawa Japanese career (Cambridge History of Japan)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v4-early-modern|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 4]] (Bitō,
ch. 8; Najita, ch. 12) traces Neo-Confucianism's distinctive course in
**[[edo-period|Tokugawa Japan]]**, where the **Zhu Xi** school — studied earlier in
[[muromachi-period|Muromachi]] Zen monasteries — became the quasi-official philosophy of the
warrior state. Bitō's key observation: the seventeenth century saw thought become
**secular and society-wide**, addressed to all statuses rather than (as in China) confined to
an examination elite — Japan had no civil-service examination, so Confucianism functioned less
as a ladder to office than as the **ethical legitimation of a hereditary status order**
(*yaku*/*mibun*). And precisely because it was not enforced by examinations, Japanese
Confucianism was unusually **plural and contestable**: the **Kogaku ("Ancient Learning")**
scholars **Itō Jinsai** and **Ogyū Sorai** bypassed Zhu Xi's metaphysics for a philological
return to the original classics (Najita's historicism — "the ultimate form of knowledge is
history"), a critical method that in turn seeded the nativist **[[shinto|kokugaku]]**. The
fuller story is on [[tokugawa-intellectual-life]].
