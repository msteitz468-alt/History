# Allotment Policy (Dawes Era)

Federal and related programs dividing tribal/reservation lands into individual parcels and selling “surplus,” presented as civilization reform and functioning as the principal legal engine of Native land loss in the [[reservation-period-1880-1960]].

## Definition

**Etic policy label** for severally: assign individual holdings to tribal members; declare remaining reservation land “surplus”; open surplus to non-Indian purchase/settlement; eventually issue fee patents making land taxable and alienable. Fullest US expression: [[dawes-act-1887]] (General Allotment Act), signed by President Grover Cleveland, sponsored by Sen. Henry L. Dawes—principal vehicle for non-Indian access to Indian lands until repeal in 1934 with the [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]].

part_of: [[reservation-period-1880-1960]]; produced_by: [[dawes-act-1887]]; instrument of: [[us-civilization-program]]; contrasts_with incomplete Canadian parallel (no general allotment statute)

## Acreage and mechanisms (full fidelity to Hoxie)

### Aggregate US loss
- When a final accounting was made in the **1930s**, government officials estimated that the **Dawes Act had brought about the loss of over 90,000,000 acres (36.4 million hectares)** of Indian-owned land.
- Indian Territory was broken up under a **different statute—the Curtis Act**—not Dawes; New York State and arid Southwest communities were **exempted** from Dawes.
- 1880s imposed land-sale agreements (pre- and parallel to full Dawes implementation) took **over 70 million acres** from Colorado Utes, Minnesota Ojibwas, Lakota, Montana Crows/Blackfeet/Assiniboines, interior Salishes, Bannocks/Shoshones, and several Oklahoma peoples.
- Northwest (Fisher/CHNPA): ~**3 million acres** taken from Northwest Indian reserves into European hands within ~30 years of Dawes.

### Surplus + fee-patent second wave
- Beyond the 90+ million acres lost as “surplus,” severally pushed Natives onto small homesteads frequently lost once title issued in fee simple (taxable; exposed to speculators and loan sharks).
- Fee-patenting peaked **1915–1920**: more than **20,000 Indians forced to accept fee patents**—more than twice the number issued since 1887.

### Ojibwa Wisconsin case (parcel + timber)
- After Wisconsin Central reached Ashland (1877), Bad River, Lac Court Oreilles, and Lac Du Flambeau reservations surveyed into **80-acre (32.4 ha)** parcels and allotted; “surplus” sold.
- Through this method **up to half** the land within the Ojibwa reservations passed to Euro-American ownership.
- 1882 Interior order allowed tribal members to sell timber on allotted land; in **1888, 731 contracts** approved removing **over 190 million board feet**—temporary cash, further encroachment, destruction of the forest environment that had supported Ojibwa communities for centuries.

### Market failure on remaining farms
- Remote reservations; homestead-size plots with horses/wagons while non-Indians expanded farms and machinery; aggressive leasing of fertile lands (Montana Crows, Yakimas, Papago/Tohono O’Odham irrigated reserves) left those still farming by 1920 near subsistence only.

### Enabling legal doctrine
- *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* (1903): Supreme Court held “the power exists to abrogate the provisions of an Indian treaty”; Commissioner exulted it would “enable you to dispose of land without the consent of the Indians” (enabled unconsented disposals; related: [[treaty-sovereignty-native-north-america]]).

### Canadian comparison (no general allotment law)
- 1869 Indian Act allowed individual titles to “safe and suitable” reserve residents (first southern Ontario); sales only to band members.
- Canadian reserves averaged **3–4,000 acres** vs **300,000+** in the US → less pressure to dispose of “surplus”; leasing to Euro-Canadians and sermons on individual ownership continued.
- Commissioner **Hayter Reed** (announced **1889**): Prairie Indians should emulate “peasants” with hand tools, home consumption, one or two cows; forced abandonment of machinery and cooperative farming; forty-acre plots; hand broadcasting/scythes/flails. By Reed’s **1897** departure, observers believed Natives incapable of becoming farmers—devastation of earlier Native eagerness for machinery.

### Resistance
- Nez Perce removed boundary markers under Alice Fletcher’s 1889 supervision.
- Redbird Smith (Cherokee) and Chitto Harjo (Creek) continued opposing dissolution of Indian Territory long after it occurred.
- Teton Sioux defeated two 1880s reduction attempts before Crook’s 1889 commission opened millions of acres west of the Missouri.

## Consequences

- Meriam Report (1928) and kin “made clear that the bleakness of Indian life in the 1920s had its origins in the policy of allotment.”
- Reform climate contributed_to: [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]] (abolished allotment; modest land restoration—of 2.75M acres 1934–40 only 20% via purchase/exchange; most desirable lost lands remained beyond reach).
- Fractured land base shaped twentieth-century tribal politics (allottees vs landless majorities in IRA votes).

## Contestation

**Contested: yes.** Contemporary “Friends of the Indian” framed severally as humanitarian reform creating yeoman citizens; Native opponents and later historians treat it as dispossession under reform rhetoric. Hoxie quotes Dawes-era design as “replacing communal with capitalist values.” Curtis Act / Oklahoma path and Canadian Reed program show multiple statutory vehicles with similar assimilation-plus-land-transfer logic. Northwest comparison: Canadian potlatch law and US Dawes as twin 1880s reformist assaults that failed to produce the intended small farmers while transferring land and eroding autonomy.
