# Linear Tactics

The dominant Western infantry doctrine from the late 1600s through the mid-19th century: shoulder-to-shoulder formations (primarily two-rank lines), columns for movement and reserves, complex "evolutions of the line" (wheels, changes of front, passages of lines), and tight command control to maximize the effect of slow-reloading smoothbore (later rifle) muskets while maintaining unit cohesion under fire.

## Core Features
- **Line formation** for maximum firepower and control in combat.
- **Columns** (simple, double, closed) primarily for rapid movement, deployment, and as supports within multiple-line arrangements rather than primary shock attacks (contrast with some Napoleonic practice).
- **Skirmishers** as light screen (increased importance with rifled weapons).
- **Multiple lines / echelons** for depth and reinforcement without exposing the entire force.
- Intensive drill to enable complex maneuvers under fire.

## Civil War Application (per Hess 2015)
Hess demonstrates that the system remained functional and was executed with surprising proficiency by experienced regiments. Combat occurred overwhelmingly at short ranges (50–150 yards), rendering the rifle musket's long-range theoretical advantage largely irrelevant in practice. Lines were strongly preferred for fighting; columns for pre-combat movement and supports. Multiple maneuvers in single engagements show high small-unit articulation.

## Historiography and Contestation
Long associated with the "limited war" professional armies of the 18th century. French Revolutionary/Napoleonic adaptations introduced more flexibility (divisions, corps, greater skirmishing, subordinate initiative). 20th-century Civil War historians (especially McWhiney & Jamieson *Attack and Die*) argued the rifle musket made linear tactics suicidal. Revisionists (Griffith, Nosworthy, Hess) show short-range reality and continued utility.
