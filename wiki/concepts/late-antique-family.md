# The Family in the Late Roman World

How the **Roman family** — its law, morality and structure — was reshaped across late antiquity, above all
by the interaction of **imperial legislation** and **Christianity**, from Constantine to Justinian. Treated
in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 14 (**Antti Arjava**, on the family in general) and Ch. 15 (Ian
Wood, **family and friendship in the West**). The editors note the chapter deliberately spans a wider period
than the rest of the volume, since the subject is best treated on a longer scale.

## Constantine and the moralizing of family law

No emperor before Constantine — not even Augustus — had so strongly sought to **reshape family law as moral
reconstruction** (*pudor tutus, munita coniugia*). Constantine's legislation had three interlinked aims:

- **Safeguarding celibacy** — by *repealing the Augustan marriage laws* that had penalized the unmarried and
  childless (a direct concession to the Christian valuation of virginity).
- **Promoting the *matrimonium iustum*** — repressing socially mixed unions and concubinage, punishing
  abduction severely, restricting adultery accusations to the husband or the wife's close male kin, and
  making **adultery a capital crime**.
- **Protecting infants and minors** — through new rules on *patria potestas*, guardianship, inheritance,
  exposure and the cession of children.

The most sensational measure (331) **restricted divorce** (*repudium*): the old rule that a marriage ended
the moment either spouse withdrew consent was abrogated, divorce permitted only for grave causes, and the
responsible party heavily penalized — wives, notably, **denied the right to repudiate husbands for general
immorality**, an asymmetry revealing the limits of the new "Christian" rigour.

## The Christianity question

A central historiographical dispute, Arjava notes, is **how far Christianity actually drove this
legislation**. One school sees Constantine's laws as "unequivocally" Christian in inspiration; sceptics
attribute the new rigour instead to **pagan philosophical morality**, a revival of traditional Roman values,
or the law's own internal evolution, accepting Christian influence only where there is "perfect identity"
between doctrine and statute. CAH XIV's verdict is that the subject leaves a genuinely **wide margin for
interpretation**: Christianity mattered, but late-Roman family law was not simply canon law avant la lettre,
and much of it (serfdom of the *coloni*, the status rules for children of unequal unions) reflects social
and fiscal pressures, not theology.

## Structure, women and the West

Beneath the law, the late-antique family remained the **conjugal household** with strong agnatic and
inheritance ties; women's **property rights** (the dowry, maternal inheritance) were comparatively robust by
pre-modern standards, even as their public and matrimonial autonomy was constrained. In the **West** (Wood,
Ch. 15), the senatorial aristocracy bound itself through **marriage alliance, kinship and friendship
(*amicitia*)** — the dense personal networks visible in Sidonius Apollinaris — which, as the imperial state
dissolved, became the **load-bearing structure of aristocratic society**, carrying Roman élite identity into
the successor kingdoms.

## Significance

The late-Roman family is the bridge between the **classical Roman household** and the **medieval Christian
family**: the law of marriage, divorce and inheritance codified by [[justinian|Justinian]] (and the Church's
growing claim over marriage) set terms that endured for centuries. It is also a key test case for the larger
debate over **how transformative Christianization really was** — the same tension that runs through CAH
XIV's treatment of religion and culture.
