# Hellenic Identity (Greek and Barbarian)

## The problem

When [[herodotus]] called those who resisted Persia simply "the Greeks," he used a
shorthand that, as [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 2, J. K. Davies) observes,
over-simplified — many Greeks medized, stood aloof, or were already Persian subjects —
yet revealed something real: to Greeks of the time "the Greeks" were **an entity**, and
"Greece" far more than a geographical expression. To define that entity is therefore part
historical analysis and part **reconstruction of a collective consciousness**. The
classic emic statement is Herodotus' *to Hellenikon* (8.144): common **blood, language,
shrines and sacrifices, and customs**. Davies analyses Greek cultural unity under four
heads.

## Four facets of cultural unity

1. **Language.** Despite the four dialect groups (Attic-Ionic, Arcado-Cypriot, Aeolic,
   Doric/Northwest), the dialects were **mutually intelligible**; the perceived gulf lay
   not between dialects but between Greek and all other tongues. Panhellenic institutions
   — the [[panhellenic-games|Games]], oracles, Amphictyonies, multi-city alliances, trade
   — presupposed it.
2. **The *barbaros* boundary.** The word *barbaros* ("babble-speaker") fixed the boundary
   **linguistically** from Homer's "barbarian-voiced Carians" onward, but by the early
   fifth century it had acquired a second, **subjective and evaluative** load: the
   unintelligible were "well on their way to being seen as unintelligent." Aeschylus'
   *Persae* and Herodotus document the new attribution of superior Greek *arete* — "the
   product of wisdom and strict law" — to explain the victory over Persia. Davies notes
   the countercurrent (Herodotus' *philobarbaros* moods, where Persian theology or custom
   comes off better), so the polarity was never simple chauvinism.
3. **Shared *nomoi*.** A web of loaded value-words — *hybris*, *ate*, *time*, *dike*,
   *arete*, *charis* — and shared institutions (blood-guilt, supplication, monogamy,
   chattel slavery, patrilineal descent) constituted "the customs of the Greeks." But
   Davies cautions the phrase "should not be pushed too hard": legal divergence among
   *poleis* was too great for the Greeks to become a "people of the Law" as the Jews did.
   What was felt was the **contrast between Greek freedom under law and the
   arbitrariness** ascribed to monarchy.
4. **Cult and ritual.** Greek religion was not *sui generis* — its components
   (altar/temple, sacrifice, procession, oracle, mysteries) had close analogues
   (Egyptian above all), and many gods were of foreign origin. What was specifically
   Greek was the **interlocking, genealogical structure** of the Olympian family and its
   transparently Greek cult-titles (Zeus Horkios, Athene Ergane), which symbolically
   integrated every social group — lineage, village, tribe, *polis*, trade, age-group —
   into one order. "The sacrificial community is a model of Greek society" (Burkert). That
   festivals could **exclude** non-Greeks (Hdt. 5.22) measures the system's success in
   manufacturing Greek consciousness — and that they could exclude some Greeks shows other
   boundaries also mattered.

## Emic and etic

The fifth-century construction was **emic** — a self-image the Greeks built, "complacently
but with some justice," and partly invented (Hdt. 4.77). Modern historians treat it
**etically** as a historically produced identity, forged in the crucible of the Persian
Wars and propagated by [[herodotus]], Aeschylus, Simonides, and the Athenian funeral
oration. It is contested in the same way all ethnicity is: the unity was real enough to
sustain panhellenic institutions yet never produced political unity, and the *barbaros*
antithesis was as much a rhetorical resource (deployed by Athens against its own Greek
rivals) as a description.
