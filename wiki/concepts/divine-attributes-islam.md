## Definition and Scope

The question of the divine attributes (sifat Allah) is the central problem of classical Islamic speculative theology ([[kalam]]). It asks: in what sense does God possess attributes — omniscience ('ilm), power (qudra), will (irada), life (hayat), hearing (sam'), sight (basar), and speech (kalam) — without compromising divine unity (tawhid) and transcendence (tanzih)? The question is not peripheral: it governs both the doctrine of God's nature and, as a corollary, the status of the Qur'an as God's speech (see [[createdness-of-the-quran]]).

The canonical seven attributes of essence (sifat al-dhat) recognized by the Asharite tradition are: omniscience, life, power, will, sight, hearing, and speech. A controversy arose over whether "will" belongs among these, since it seems to imply action or intention rather than an eternally subsisting quality. Fakhr al-Din al-Razi added "creation" (khalq) as an eighth, distinguishing himself from the customary list.

Source: [[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 6 (Nader El-Bizri).

## The Core Tension

The attributes problem arises from a structural conflict within monotheism:

- If God's attributes (e.g., knowledge, power) are real and distinct from His essence, this implies multiplicity — a "plurality of eternals" — which threatens tawhid.
- If the attributes are simply identical with the essence (as the Mutazilites proposed), God becomes an impersonal, undifferentiated absolute; the personal divine of prayer, fear, and hope is evacuated.
- If the attributes are real but neither identical with the essence nor separate from it, a third position is needed — the one Asharism attempted to articulate.

As El-Bizri summarizes the Asharite resolution: "the attributes are not reduced to the essence itself while being coeternal with it without cause."

## School Positions

### Mutazilite Position

The Mutazilites, upholding radical divine transcendence, reduced the attributes to the essence. Wasil ibn 'Ata (d. 748) rejected the reality of distinct attributes of knowledge, power, will, and life in order to "negate a plurality of eternals." Later Mutazilites further compressed the list: some restricted the attributes to knowledge and power; others collapsed them into unity itself.

The formula employed was: God is "'alim bi-'ilm huwa huwa" — "knowing by a knowledge that is Him." On this view, the divine names used in the Qur'an are accommodations for human understanding, not reports of real distinct realities in the divine essence. The Mutazilites thereby censored, "through rational directives, the classes of meaningful propositions that could be uttered about the divine."

Consequence: this approach drained religious experience of its object. As El-Bizri observes, "by reducing the attributes to the essence, the Mu'tazila seemed to deny worshippers the object of their praise, exaltation and piety" — replacing the personal Exalted One with a neuter qualification closer to the pagan Greek Absolute than to Abrahamic monotheism.

The Mutazilite position also entailed the createdness of the Qur'an (see [[createdness-of-the-quran]]), which became the political flashpoint of the [[mihna-833]].

### Hanbalite Position

The Hanbalites affirmed all divine attributes on the authority of Qur'anic revelation, without speculative elaboration. Their principle was tafwid: consigning the "meaning and howness" (ma'na wa-kayf) of the attributes entirely to God. Attributes exist because God mentioned them; their ontological mode remains veiled.

Ibn Hanbal's position: one affirms that "God spoke to Moses in a way that befits His divine essence" — nothing more may be said. The question "how?" (kayf?) or "why?" (lima?) is impermissible. Kalam speculation risks bid'a (heretical innovation).

Ibn Badran added: "whosoever believes that any of these aforementioned matters are created, should be charged with infidelity." Ibn Hanbal reportedly refused to pray behind or sit near anyone who held the Qur'an's enunciation (lafziyya) to be created.

The Hanbalites relied on "letting [divine words] be without how" (bi-la kayf) — a stance that preceded and persisted alongside Asharism as a rival within Sunni orthodoxy.

### Asharite Position

[[al-ashari]] occupied what El-Bizri calls "an approximately median theological ground between Hanbalism and Mu'tazilism." His key moves:

1. **Against Mutazilism**: The attributes are real, not reducible to the essence. God genuinely possesses knowledge, power, life, will, sight, hearing, and speech as distinct real attributes coeternal with His essence.

2. **Against Hanbalite literalism**: Kalam reasoning is not only permissible but obligatory, since knowing God constitutes knowing the truth (al-haqq). Unreflective deference by taqlid (mimetic assent) is inadequate for Muslims who have the duty to reason about God.

3. **Against anthropomorphism (tashbih/tajsim)**: The attributes are coeternal with the essence "without being marked by otherness (ghayriyya) or privation ('adam)." They are neither accidents subsisting separately nor reductions of the essence. God possesses a "face," "eyes," and "hands" as Qur'an affirms, but these are not corporeal members — their nature is inexplicable and beyond human grasp. "The face is an attribute that God ascribed to Himself and only God knows its significance" (Kitab al-Luma').

A key Asharite distinction: **attributes of action** (sifat al-fi'l) vs. **attributes of essence** (sifat al-dhat or sifat al-nafs). The contraries of attributes of action are permissibly attributable to God — God may be forgiving or unforgiving depending on the creature's state. The contraries of attributes of essence, however, are repugnant: the negation of omniscience entails ignorance; denial of power entails weakness. Attributes of action are thus "negational" (salbiyya); attributes of essence are "existential" (wujudiyya).

On God's speech specifically: "whatever is sensory is created" — so the audible pronunciation (lafziyya) and recitation (qira'a) are created, but "divine speech, as what is recited (maqru'), is uncreated." This allowed Asharism to affirm the eternal Word while acknowledging that its earthly expression in sound and script is temporal.

The classical method Ash'ari deployed for reasoning about God's unseen nature was "al-istidlal 'ala al-gha'ib bi'l-shahid" — inferring from what is phenomenally experienced to what is absent and transcendent.

### Maturidi Position

[[al-maturidi]], associated with Abu Hanifa's legacy in Samarkand, permitted greater rational speculation while maintaining bi-la kayf. His formulation: the attributes are "neither Him nor other than Him" (la huwa wa-la ghayruhu) — God is pre-eternally qualified by all His attributes (mawsuf bi-jami' sifatihi fi'l-azal). It is impossible for the attributes not to be coeternal with God, for that would entail deficiency; yet coeternal status does not mean the attributes are identical with the essence.

The Maturidis also rejected the Mutazilite claim that "God is everywhere" as undermining divine exaltedness; God is "on the Throne" (al-'arsh) and does not commingle with worldly creation.

## Philosophical (Falsafa) Interventions

The philosophers (falasifa) addressed the essence-attribute question through ontological analysis. Avicenna's framework distinguished:

- **Impossible being**: cannot exist
- **Contingent being** (mumkin bi-dhatihi): has potentiality to be or not to be
- **Necessary being** (wajib al-wujud bi-dhatihi): exists necessarily by its own essence, without quiddity/essence (mahiyya) other than existence (wujud)

For Avicenna, the "Necessary Existent due-to-Itself" has no genus, definition, counterpart, or opposite, and is detached from matter, quality, quantity, place, and time. The divine attributes, if real and distinct from the essence, would imply multiplicity — which falsafa, like Mutazilism, resisted.

[[al-ghazali]] attacked this in his Tahafut al-Falasifa (see [[tahafut-al-falasifa]]): if the attributes are not the same as the essence, each one dispenses with the other or needs the other — yielding either multiple necessary existents (impossible) or a deficient First Principle. Ghazali's reply: "the attributes of perfection are not separate from the essence of the Perfect, so as to say that He is in need of another." The attributes are coeternal with the essence without cause.

Later, [[fakhr-al-din-al-razi]] synthesized kalam and falsafa most systematically. He criticized Avicenna's claim that God knows only universals, proposing instead that knowledge involves a "relation qua connection" (ta'alluq) between knower and known, such that a change in what is known alters the relation but not the knower as such — preserving divine immutability while affirming particular knowledge.

Al-Shahrastani (d. 1153) attempted an equipoise between 'aql (intellect) and sam' (audition of revelation), arguing that the enunciation of the divine word (lafz al-kalima) is created while its inherent meaning (al-ma'na al-nafsi) is eternal — and that when rational explications reach their end, attentive listening to revelation must take over.

## Hermeneutic Dimensions

The essence-attribute problem has a necessary hermeneutic corollary: how should qur'anic descriptions of God — "God's hand," "God's face," "God is on the Throne" — be read? Three positions formed:

1. **Literal reading (zahir)**: risks anthropomorphism (tashbih) and corporealism (tajsim)
2. **Allegorical/esoteric reading (ta'wil/batin)**: risks dissolving revealed content into abstraction
3. **Median position (Asharite)**: affirm the reality of what scripture asserts; refuse to say the referent is either a corporeal member or a mere metaphor; note that "a semblance of linguistic affinity in reference to attributes does not affirm a similitude in signification"

The Asharite principle: "God is not in His creatures nor are His creatures in Him." Any "departure from literal readings must be based on valid reasons." But strict literalism carries its own risk; moderation between the two interpretive poles is the goal.

## Significance

The sifat question is not a peripheral scholastic puzzle. It determines:

- The nature of religious experience (whether prayer, fear, hope have a genuine personal divine object)
- The ontological status of the Qur'an as God's eternal speech
- The relationship between reason and revelation in Islamic epistemology
- The boundaries of permissible kalam speculation

The debate was never resolved to consensus. Mutazilism collapsed as a political orthodoxy after the failure of the [[mihna-833]], but its philosophical arguments were absorbed by later kalam. Asharism became the dominant Sunni synthesis. Hanbalite bi-la kayf persisted as an alternative within orthodoxy. Maturidism governed Central and South Asian Sunni thought through the Hanafi school.

## Historiography

The sifat debate is among the best-documented controversies in Islamic intellectual history, owing to the polemical literature of the schools themselves (Ashari's Kitab al-Luma', Ibana; Maturidi's Kitab al-Tawhid; Ibn Hazm's Fisal) and modern scholarship (Gimaret's La doctrine d'al-Ash'ari; Allard's Le problème des attributs divins; Van Ess's Die Erkenntnislehre). The chapter by El-Bizri in Winter's Cambridge Companion offers a philosophically sophisticated synthesis.

A persistent historiographical dispute concerns whether Mutazilite "rationalism" represents a genuinely Islamic intellectual development or primarily a Hellenic import — the latter view now generally rejected in favor of seeing early Mutazilism as an indigenous development from grammatical and linguistic speculation (Burrell, Winter ed., ch. 7).
