# Vertical Archipelago (Andean Verticality)

## Definition

The **vertical archipelago** is the model, formalized by the ethnohistorian **John V. Murra**,
of how Andean societies secured access to the radically different resources of stacked
ecological tiers (*pisos ecológicos*) — coast, valley, high grassland (puna), and eastern
montaña — by placing **permanent colonies (mitmaq / mitimaes)** in distant zones while keeping
them socially and politically attached to a highland nuclear community. Rather than trade
across ecological frontiers, a single [[ayllu|ayllu]] or kingdom directly **controlled
discontinuous "islands"** of production at different altitudes — hence "archipelago."

This is an **etic** analytical model built from **emic** evidence: Murra derived it largely
from the 1567 Spanish *visita* of the Lupaqa ([[aymara-kingdoms|Aymara kingdom]]) of Chucuito,
which recorded altiplano communities maintaining maize colonies on the coast and the eastern
slopes simultaneously.

## Application and Limits

Verticality underpins much of how [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1|Volume III]]
explains Andean economy — from the [[aymara-kingdoms|altiplano señoríos]] to the
[[inca-empire|Inka]] state's redeployment of the principle at imperial scale (mitmaq
resettlement). The volume also flags its **limitations**: verticality was strongest in the
southern highlands and does not capture the **horizontal**, market-and-exchange economies of
the north coast and Ecuador (where specialized merchant traders and shell-money are attested).
Critics warn against treating it as a single pan-Andean essence.
