# Annihilation Through Labor (Vernichtung durch Arbeit)

**"Annihilation through labor"** names the SS policy — most consistently applied to Jewish prisoners in [[auschwitz|Auschwitz]] and [[majdanek|Majdanek]] — of exploiting prisoners' forced labour while deliberately working them to death, rather than sparing them for productivity. It resolved, in the Nazi mind, the apparent contradiction between the war economy's hunger for labour and the drive to exterminate: as [[heydrich-reinhard|Heydrich]] put it at the [[wannsee-conference-1942|Wannsee Conference]], large labour gangs would form in the east and "a large number of them will drop out through natural wastage." Documented from [[wachsmann-kl-concentration-camps-2015|Wachsmann, *KL* (2015)]].

## Definition and Scope

The concept captures a policy, not merely a by-product: registered Jewish slave labourers were, in [[hoss-rudolf|Höss]]'s and the SS's terms, "dead men and women walking," rarely surviving more than a few months. It first appeared in late 1941, when [[himmler-heinrich|Himmler]] allowed fit Soviet "commissars" to be worked to death before killing, and it structured the exploitation of Jews at the IG Farben/Monowitz works, where ~25,000 of ~35,000 prisoners died. It also applied to transferred German state prisoners (whose Buchenwald death rate reached 29% a month in early 1943).

## The Unresolved Contradiction

[[pohl-oswald|Pohl]]'s WVHA embodied the policy's internal tension: from December 1942 Himmler ordered camp mortality to fall (once slave labour became economically vital), yet Pohl simultaneously demanded "literally exhausting" labour and minimum 11-hour workdays. The regional disparity is telling — Majdanek prisoners were ten times more likely to die than Buchenwald prisoners — showing that "annihilation through labour" was a matter of intent and target group, not simply of harsh conditions. The SS never resolved whether prisoners were there to work or to die; for Jews, Himmler's answer was effectively "both."

## Limitations and Critiques

Historians debate how far the term should stretch: Wachsmann notes that calling KL inmates "slaves" is contested, since ordinary slaveholders have an interest in their bondsmen's survival, whereas the SS deliberately destroyed its workforce. He nonetheless defends the vocabulary of slavery and annihilation-through-labour as apt for the war's later stages. The concept is a corrective to any purely economic reading of the camps: forced labour in the KL was a mode of killing as much as of production.
