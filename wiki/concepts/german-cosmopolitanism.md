## Definition and Scope

German cosmopolitanism (*Weltbürgertum*, the state of being a *Weltbürger* or world citizen) was the characteristic political and ethical aspiration of the [[german-enlightenment]] — the claim that membership in the human species as such (*Menschheit*) takes precedence over, or complements, membership in a particular state, religion, or nation. The concept carried several distinct meanings simultaneously: a universal ethics transcending local loyalties; a commitment to freedom of international commerce and the "spirit of commerce" (*Handelsgeist*) as a bond among peoples; a program of universal history written to cultivate world citizens; and a criticism of European colonialism and imperial domination.

As Christoph Martin Wieland articulated it in 1788: cosmopolitans "regard all peoples of the earth as just so many branches of a single family, and the universe as a state in which they are citizens, together with innumerable other rational beings, in order to promote the perfection of the whole." Crucial to this formulation was "the idea of unity in diversity."

German cosmopolitanism was both genuinely universalist and distinctively shaped by the German political context — a fragmented territorial landscape without overseas empire, producing intellectuals who were "keen, but detached observers of other people's empires and colonialism."

## Four Analytical Facets

The Oxford Handbook (chapter 6, Fillafer and Osterhammel) identifies four structural dimensions of enlightened cosmopolitanism in the German lands:

1. **The world as Europe's mirror**: Non-European cultures — Chinese, Persian, Turkish — served as ironic vantage points from which to critique European arrangements. Montesquieu's *Persian Letters* (1721) established the genre; German imitators followed.

2. **Comparative government**: The simultaneous encounter with Americas, Asia, Africa, and the South Seas created a "comparative government" discipline that placed European institutions in global context and helped define "civilization" against "barbarism" through comparative reasoning.

3. **Planetary interconnection**: The Seven Years' War (1756–1763), "fought for global maritime hegemony," made Europeans "clearly recognize that the different parts of the world were interwoven and interconnected to an unprecedented degree." The "spirit of commerce" (*Handelsgeist*) would create bonds of mutual interest. German geographers and statisticians — Matthias Christian Sprengel, August Ludwig Schlözer — specialized in mapping this global interconnection.

4. **Unity of mankind**: The concept of *Menschheit* (Humanity) served as both a utopian emancipatory horizon and an empirical claim about the common capacities of human beings everywhere. Enlightenment intellectuals "not only assumed a common natural right possessed by human beings on all levels of 'refinement,' but also shared cognitive and bodily capacities that made it possible to acknowledge the other's humanity."

## Immanuel Kant: Cosmopolitan Right and Perpetual Peace

Kant provided the most rigorous philosophical foundation for cosmopolitanism. His *Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Aim* (1784) proposed a "regulative idea of the improvement of mankind" that allows for the full development of human reason — a teleological framework for understanding history as purposive without asserting metaphysical certainty about its outcome. His *Toward Perpetual Peace* (1795) elaborated a three-tier framework of cosmopolitan right: republican constitutions within states, a league of free nations, and cosmopolitan right of hospitality between individuals and foreign states.

Kant's critique of colonialism was the sharpest in the German Enlightenment: he "rejected the right to settle on another people's soil except when permitted through a treaty, and he left no doubt that even 'savages' had rights and were capable of acting as legal subjects."

Kant also introduced the concept of "unsociable sociability" (*ungesellige Geselligkeit*) — the antagonism inherent in human nature that paradoxically drives social progress. This concept responded to critics who charged cosmopolitan commerce with promoting "emotional depravation, individual self-aggrandizement, prodigality, and avarice."

## Herder: Unity in Diversity and Cultural Particularity

Johann Gottfried Herder represented a distinct cosmopolitan tradition that emphasized the irreducible plurality of cultures. Against Kant's universalist teleology, Herder argued that each people (*Volk*) expressed a particular *Volksgeist* that could not be measured against a single universal standard of "progress." "The notion of 'deviation' from a given 'standard' is therefore erroneous."

Herder was simultaneously anti-colonial: "All men are endowed with the power of attaining superior reason. Foreign cultures should be treated with respect, not lured into exploitative treaties." His cultural particularism led not to nationalist exclusivism but to a cosmopolitan pluralism that anticipated twentieth-century cultural relativism.

## German Cosmopolitanism Without Empire

A defining characteristic of German cosmopolitanism was that it developed in the absence of colonial empire. "German states did not pursue imperial projects. Although Joseph II contemplated a Pacific voyage, Germans eager to visit other continents sought employment abroad, joining voyages, expeditions, or diplomatic missions organized by the Dutch East India Company, the British Admiralty, the Tsarist Crown, or the Royal Danish government."

This structural position produced a distinctive critique of imperialism — "principled" and grounded in "fundamental terms of justice, legitimacy, and the tolerance of diversity" rather than responding to specific colonial abuses. The most powerful expression was Alexander von Humboldt's extended analysis of the Spanish colonial system in Mexico (*Essai politique sur le Royaume de la Nouvelle-Espagne*, 1808–1812), which went beyond moral denunciation to demonstrate "the inherent irrationality of the colonial system."

German intellectuals engaged with the world "through a vibrant culture of translation. Philosophical treatises, historical surveys, novels, and travel reports from all over the world were translated soon after their first publication." This vicarious cosmopolitanism — absorbing global knowledge through translation rather than colonial administration — gave German Enlightenment thought a distinctive bibliographic character.

## The Göttingen School: Universal History as Cosmopolitan Project

The Göttingen historians Gatterer and Schlözer embedded cosmopolitan ambition within systematic historical scholarship. Universal history was written "with the intention of creating world citizens" (*in weltbürgerlicher Absicht*). Their approach — "The powerful gaze turns the aggregate into a system, a system in which world and humanity form a unit" — aspired to a non-providential, empirically grounded account of humanity's development across space and time.

## The Cosmopolitan-Nationalist Tension, c. 1800

The received narrative of an abrupt turn from Enlightenment cosmopolitanism to romantic nationalism around 1800 is challenged by the Oxford Handbook. "The 'nation' frequently invoked around 1800 was not a concept developed in clear contradistinction to cosmopolitism. It rather seems that cosmopolitanism and hyperbolic 'nationalism' carved out separate channels."

Key evidence for this argument:

- During the Wars of Liberation against Napoleon (1813–1815), "the early nineteenth century did not bring a backlash of stubborn nationalism traditionally attributed to popular revolts against Napoleon. Fraternal bonds to other peoples were reinforced and praise for the Revolution in France remained strong."
- German Philhellenism in the 1820s "also rested on a cosmopolitan scaffold."
- Goethe's concept of *Weltliteratur* (first adumbrated 1827) programmatically extended cosmopolitan values into the post-Napoleonic era, calling for "a constant, and well-organized, activity of awareness and mediation, translation and transfer, a process of linking intellectual communities in distant parts of the world."

Johann Gottlieb Fichte's trajectory does represent a genuine shift: his early cosmopolitan commitments (the period of *The Vocation of the Scholar*, 1794) gave way to the nationalist *Addresses to the German Nation* (1807–1808). But Fichte was exceptional, not representative.

## Romantic Cosmopolitanism

The Romantics maintained cosmopolitan aspirations while rejecting the Enlightenment's rational-commercial framework. Novalis ("Faith and love, not knowing and having"), Joseph von Eichendorff, and Friedrich Schlegel developed specifically Catholic forms of universalism rooted in medieval Christendom. This "tacitly endorsed enlightened ideals of individuality, freedom, and equality, but accused the Enlightenment of having devaluated them, ridiculing sacrifice for the best of mankind and supplanting true charity with greed."

## Limits: Racism within Cosmopolitanism

German Enlightenment cosmopolitanism had internal contradictions it could not fully resolve. Christoph Meiners produced explicitly racist writings. The shift from Enlightenment to post-Enlightenment thought brought "more disparaging attitudes toward other cultures arrested in their respective stages of development" — what John Stuart Mill in 1848 would criticize as "the tendency of attributing the diversities of conduct and character to inherent national differences."

This internal tension between the cosmopolitan ideal of human unity and the comparative judgments embedded in stadial theories of progress represents the deepest structural contradiction of German Enlightenment cosmopolitanism — and a site of active historiographical debate.

## Historiography

The chapter by Fillafer and Osterhammel in the Oxford Handbook represents a revisionist position in Aufklärung studies, challenging both the earlier dismissal of German Enlightenment cosmopolitanism as derivative and the post-1960s postcolonial critique that reduces it to a smokescreen for European hegemony. The postmodern critique (Spivak and others) "gives short shrift to Enlighteners' recognition of diversity and calls for toleration, and sees eighteenth-century universalism as a smokescreen of benevolence to camouflage Europe's rise to world supremacy." The Handbook rejects this reductionism while acknowledging the contradictions.

The relationship between German cosmopolitanism and the emergence of [[nationalism]] remains a productive area of scholarly disagreement. See also [[german-sonderweg]] for the argument that German cosmopolitanism's failure to take political root contributed to Germany's distinctive path to modernity.
