## Definition

A permanent, professional, state-paid military force maintained in peacetime as well as war, as opposed to feudal levies, temporary militia call-ups, or contracted mercenary bands disbanded once a campaign ended. The concept marks a central institutional watershed in early modern military history: the shift from ad hoc, seasonal war-making toward year-round, centrally administered military establishments.

## Emic vs. Etic

Largely an etic historiographical category, though contemporaries (e.g., seventeenth-century French administrators) explicitly debated the fiscal and political implications of maintaining large permanent forces under direct royal control.

## Historical Development

France under [[actors/louvois-francois-michel-le-tellier|Michel Le Tellier and his son Louvois]] built the paradigmatic early modern standing army, reorganizing recruitment, supply, and discipline under direct royal administration and vastly expanding force size — a model historians such as Hans Delbrück treat as the benchmark against which Prussian, Habsburg, and other developments are measured. Standing armies required — and drove — the fiscal-administrative growth of the early modern state, since permanent forces demanded regularized taxation, magazine logistics, and standing bureaucracies rather than one-off feudal levies or contract financing.

## Significance

Enabled the sustained, large-scale warfare of the seventeenth–eighteenth centuries; shifted political power toward centralizing monarchies at the expense of feudal nobility and urban militias; and laid institutional groundwork for the mass conscript armies of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
