# Counterinsurgency

## Definition and Scope

Counterinsurgency (COIN) is the mirror image of [[guerrilla-warfare|guerrilla warfare]] — the occupier's problem of how to contain an insurgency "rather than how it could be contained" from the rebel's side. Where Lawrence and Mao theorized how to *fight* irregular war, the counterinsurgent inherits the harder, slower task that Lawrence captured in his enduring image: making war on rebellion "is messy and slow, like eating soup with a knife."

The imperial origin lies in nineteenth-century "imperial policing." The classic early text was "C. E. Calwell's *Small Wars*, published in 1896, which observed that as a general rule, 'the quelling of the rebellion in distant colonies means protracted, thankless, invertebrate war.'"

## Causal Drivers

The central insight of modern COIN is the **primacy of politics and population over firepower**. Two strands run through Freedman's account:

**Population-centric ("hearts and minds").** The Americans were "influenced by the successful British experience in Malaya as described by Robert Thompson." Under Sir Gerald Templer, a communist insurgency was contained: "'The shooting side of the business is only 25 percent of the trouble,'" Templer observed, "'and the other 75 percent lies in getting the people of this country behind us.'" The answer was not "pouring more troops into the jungle" but lay, in his famous phrase, "'in the hearts and minds of the people.'" General Edward Lansdale similarly understood that "without popular support there was 'no political base for supporting the fight'" — people had to be convinced "their lives could be improved through social action and political reform," which required "a responsive, non-corrupt government; well-behaved armed forces; and a cause in which they could believe."

**David Galula and "insurgency."** The French officer Galula, drawing on the failures in Vietnam and Algeria, "popularized the concept of 'insurgency'" and "stressed the importance of the loyalty of the population." His method: "Victory would require pacifying one area after another, each serving as a secure base from which to move to the next." Yet his own Algerian experience "was mixed" — his efforts "were not matched by many of his fellow officers," and "his theory fitted neither the local political structures nor army culture."

## Major Phases

**The enemy-centric counter-pull.** Against the population-centric school stood the U.S. military's "search and destroy" preference: "wars were won by eliminating enemy armed forces and frustrating their operations." This led to "shelling and bombing areas where the enemy was believed to be hiding, though the enemy had often moved on and the attacks led to civilian deaths and popular resentment." The military "was doubtful that this was really a new type of insurgency and preferred to treat it as old-fashioned aggression."

**The diagnosis/cure gap.** The challenge "was seen to be less with the diagnosis... than in working out what to do about it." Counterinsurgency theory, "like nuclear strategy, developed as a special body of expertise geared to discussing special sorts of military relationships as if they were special types of war." One participant later lamented the "somewhat simplistic" assumptions about a "monolithic" threat following the script of a "war of national liberation," which lost sight of the insurgency's "domestic origins and root causes" and treated it as "a clearly articulated military force instead of the apex of a pyramid deeply embedded in society."

## Interaction

COIN's deepest problem was structural: "ameliorating the 'worst causes of discontent'" required reform by the local government, "yet the measures being proposed threatened to undermine the government's position because they would involve altering the country's social structure and domestic economy." Original doctrine "assumed that the main work would be undertaken by local forces, assisted by American resources and advisors. The use of American forces on a large scale was to be avoided" — South Vietnam was "the exception, but it was an exception that clouded all later thoughts on counterinsurgency theory and practice." See [[indirect-approach|The Indirect Approach]] for the wider strategic frame.

**"Counterinsurgency American-Style" (Krepinevich Ch. 7):** In Vietnam the U.S. Army applied the Army Concept to COIN: "the infantry found the enemy and the artillery and the air killed the enemy"; Westmoreland's one-word answer to insurgency was "Firepower." Search-and-destroy, body-count metrics, H&I fires, defoliation (RANCH HAND), and short tours substituted material/technological solutions for sustained population security and local-force building. "Pacification was the 'other' war, the ARVN’s war." "It was far easier to fall back on the logistical and technological elements of strategy—sensors, ground radars, firepower on call, helicopter gunships, herbicides and defoliants." Even CORDS (Ch. 8) was a civil-military hybrid created by "stealing from the military" resources, with the big-unit war retaining primacy. This produced the opposite of classical COIN: alienation of population, inflated metrics, and resource allocation that starved the "other war." Contrasts directly with population-centric approaches (e.g. Marine CAPs, which Westmoreland opposed).

## End Conditions

Vietnam, on which the civilian strategists "had relatively little of value to say," "marked the end of the 'golden age' of strategic studies" and "poisoned the academic well." The asymmetry Lawrence had named never resolved: the counterinsurgent must win politically what the insurgent need only avoid losing militarily.

## Nagl's Framework (Ch. 2 of *Learning to Eat Soup with a Knife*)

Nagl grounds modern COIN in the Clausewitz–Jomini–Mao lineage and distinguishes two enduring approaches.

**Historical lineage (verbatim grounding):**
- Guerrilla/asymmetric warfare is ancient ("as old as the word guerrilla itself"); essential features: avoid enemy main forces, strike outposts/logistics from unexpected directions, use terrain (swamps, mountains, forests) to neutralize conventional advantages.
- Clausewitz placed "people in arms" in context of Napoleon's "remarkable trinity" (people, army, government); "the people increased in relative importance"; "people in arms" as "smoldering embers" that "consume the basic foundations of the enemy forces."
- Jomini's emphasis on "massive battles of annihilation" and "scientific principles" was widely misattributed to Clausewitz and "influenced other thinkers about strategy as well as generations of practitioners." "The belief that annihilating the enemy army was the certain route to victory was corrupted before World War I into the concept that offense was both practically and morally superior to defense." Direct relevance: "Jomini emphasizes the destruction of the enemy army in the field, despite Clausewitz’s understanding that the true power of armies... rests in the people and their government." Armies "ever since have clung to what they continue to describe as 'Clausewitzian' (actually Jominian) principles."
- Mao extended the trinity: "The richest source of power to wage war lies in the masses of the people." Enforced via "Three Rules and the Eight Remarks" of the Eighth Route Army. Three phases of revolutionary war: 1. organization/consolidation/preservation (limited ops, cadre training); 2. progressive expansion (attack isolated outposts, "storehouse of the revolution"); 3. decisive (guerrillas transform into conventional forces). "This question of the political mobilization of the army and the people is indeed of the greatest importance... victory is impossible without it." "Insurgents start with nothing but a cause and grow to strength, while the counter-insurgents start with everything but a cause and gradually decline in strength and grow to weakness." Post-WWII changes (nationalism, media as "the greatest weapon in the armory of the modern commander," portable weapons) allowed revolutionary warfare to become "a strategically offensive form of warfare in its own right."

**Two approaches (direct vs. indirect):**
- Direct: "A war is a war is a war" (Harry Summers); focus on destruction of enemy armed forces via firepower and big units; "primarily offensive in nature."
- Indirect: "separate the fish from the water" (Mao/Kitson/Thompson); attack the support of the people. Kitson: "If a fish has got to be destroyed it can be attacked directly... But if rod and net cannot succeed by themselves it may be necessary to do something to the water which will force the fish into a position where it can be caught." "the counter-insurgent must direct his efforts, not toward seeking combat with the insurgent’s guerrilla forces, but at the insurgent political infrastructure."

**Thompson's Five Principles of Counterinsurgency (Figure 2-1):**
1. "The government must have a clear political aim: to establish and maintain a free, independent and united country which is politically and economically stable and viable."
2. "The government must function in accordance with law."
3. "The government must have an overall plan."
4. "The government must give priority to defeating the political subversion, not the guerrillas."
5. "In the guerrilla phase of an insurgency, a government must secure its base areas first."

**Evaluating success (Question Set #2, Figure 2-2):**
1. Victory: Did the doctrine adopted achieve national goals in the conflict?
2. Objective: Did the army contribute to the setting of realistic national goals in the conflict?
3. Unity of Command: Did the military accept subordination to political objectives?
4. Minimum Force: Did the military use the minimum amount of force necessary to accomplish the mission?
5. Mass: Did the military structure itself in an appropriate manner to deal with the threat at hand?

Nagl argues these derive directly from insurgency theory and practice. "These five questions... have the merit of being based on both the history and the theory of insurgency and guerrilla warfare." British performance in Malaya will be evaluated against them; American in Vietnam will be shown to have defaulted to the direct/Jominian approach embodied in the [[army-concept]] (see [[krepinevich-the-army-and-vietnam-1986]]).

## Capstone: Organizational Culture Determines Adoption (Nagl Part IV conclusions, Range 06)

Nagl's final chapters establish that even correct doctrine (the indirect approach, Thompson's principles, "separate the fish from the water") is useless without an organizational culture that permits learning and implementation. The British Army's colonial/regimental culture made it a "learning institution" that could develop, test, and disseminate effective COIN methods in Malaya; the U.S. Army's conventional "essence" made it structurally resistant even when failures were obvious and British lessons were offered via the Advisory Mission.

Nagl's dramatic implication: cultures optimized for one form of war (conventional mid-intensity) actively cripple performance in the other. “The U.S. Army in Vietnam is a classic example.” Building adaptability requires “profound cultural shifts,” self-awareness of past failings, promotion of innovators, open dialogue, and humility — practices already embedded in the British system but alien to the American one in the 1960s.

Post-Cold War reality: “the vast majority of armed conflict today occurs inside states”; armies “must learn how to eat soup with a knife.” The metaphor (T.E. Lawrence) captures both the awkwardness of the task and its necessity. Nagl cross-references Krepinevich for the mechanics of U.S. failure and positions his work as the cultural complement to Galula's prescriptive framework. The learning-institution lens explains why some forces can actually execute population-centric COIN while others default to direct, firepower-heavy approaches regardless of the evidence.
