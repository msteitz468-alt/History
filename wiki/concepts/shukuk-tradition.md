# Shukūk Tradition (Doubts Against Ptolemy)

The ***shukūk*** tradition refers to a genre of Islamic scientific writing devoted to systematically enumerating and analyzing **"doubts" (Arabic *shukūk*, sing. *shakk*) or problems (*ishkālāt*)** detected in the works of classical Greek authorities — above all in the astronomical and cosmological writings of Ptolemy. The tradition spans from the mid-9th century through at least the early 17th century and constitutes, according to George Saliba ([[saliba-islamic-science-renaissance-2007]], ch. 3), the intellectual engine that drove Islamic astronomy's progressive divergence from, and eventual supersession of, the Ptolemaic system.

## Genesis and Social Context

The tradition did not arise from purely abstract intellectual curiosity. As Saliba argues, the social environment of Abbasid science — characterized by intense competition between proponents of "foreign" and "Islamic" sciences — meant that the importers of Greek astronomy had compelling reasons to ensure their imported knowledge was free of demonstrable flaws. Opponents could discredit the entire scientific enterprise by pointing to errors. This defensive imperative generated a culture of critical scrutiny that became the *shukūk* tradition.

The earliest doubts were not yet a genre: Muḥammad b. Mūsā b. Shākir (d. 873), one of the major patrons of Greek translations and himself a scientist, wrote a treatise on the absurdity of Ptolemy's ninth celestial sphere barely two decades after al-Ḥajjāj's first Arabic translation of the Almagest. The Ptolemaic problems were being noticed even as the texts were being translated.

## The Genre's Codification

The *shukūk* genre was formally established by **Abū Bakr al-Rāzī** (Latin Rhazes, d. 925) in his *al-Shukūk ʿalā Jālīnūs* (Doubts Against Galen) — a medical text, not an astronomical one. This reveals the cross-disciplinary character of the tradition: the impulse to enumerate and criticize the inconsistencies of Greek authorities extended across all the "foreign sciences."

The astronomical branch of the *shukūk* genre received its fullest early elaboration from **[[ibn-al-haytham|Ibn al-Haytham]]** (Alhazen, d. c. 1040) in his *al-Shukūk ʿalā Baṭlamyūs* (Dubitationes in Ptolemaeum). Unlike al-Rāzī's medical doubts, Ibn al-Haytham's astronomical *shukūk* remained untranslated into Latin, limiting its direct European reception.

## The Equant as the Central Problem

Almost all strands of the *shukūk* tradition converged on the **equant problem** as the central absurdity of Ptolemaic astronomy. The equant was Ptolemy's device for handling the observed non-uniform motion of planetary epicycles: he stipulated that the epicyclic center moved uniformly not around the center of its deferent sphere but around a separate "equant point" at a different location on the line of apsides.

In physical terms, this required a physical sphere (the deferent) to move uniformly around an axis that did not pass through its own center. This was:

- **Kinematically impossible** in Aristotelian physics, where uniform circular motion required an axis through the body's center.
- **Self-contradictory**: Ptolemy himself stated in *Almagest* III that a body moving uniformly around one point cannot move uniformly around any other point — yet the equant required exactly this.
- **Explicitly acknowledged by Ptolemy** as "contrary to accepted principles" (*Almagest* IX.2), an admission that only strengthened later critics' positions.

Ibn al-Haytham's condemnation was unambiguous:

> "What we have reported is the truth of what Ptolemy had established for the motion of the upper planets; and that is a notion that necessitates a contradiction." (*al-Shukūk ʿalā Baṭlamyūs*)

By the 11th century, the equant problem was circulating from al-Andalus in the far west to Bukhara in the east, reaching not just specialist astronomers but philosophers like **Ibn Sīnā** (Avicenna, d. 1037). Abū 'Ubayd al-Jūzjānī (d. c. 1070), Ibn Sīnā's student, reported discussing the equant with his teacher — and added, archly, that he did not believe his teacher had ever actually resolved it.

## Relationship Between Critique and Construction

The *shukūk* tradition forked into two related but distinct activities:

1. **Enumeration and critique** — documenting Ptolemaic problems without necessarily proposing solutions. Ibn al-Haytham is the most prominent example; his *Shukūk* contains no alternative models. He was criticized for this by later astronomers including [[al-urdi|Muʿayyad al-Dīn al-ʿUrḍī]] (d. 1266).
2. **Constructive alternatives** — proposing new mathematical models that resolved the problems. 'Urḍī, [[nasir-al-din-tusi|al-Ṭūsī]] (with his *Ṭūsī couple*), [[ibn-al-shatir|Ibn al-Shāṭir]], and al-Khafrī all belong here. Al-Khafrī's 16th-century works went farthest, offering multiple distinct mathematical models for the same planetary motions — demonstrating that mathematics was a language with multiple valid dialects for describing the same physical reality.

## Continuity and the "Decline" Thesis

Saliba explicitly uses the *shukūk* tradition to refute the thesis that Islamic science declined after the 12th century. The tradition continued vigorously through the 16th and 17th centuries:

- Al-Akhawayn's 15th-century anthology codified half a millennium of problems and solutions as a teaching text.
- 16th-century Persian astronomers (Dashtaghī, al-Khafrī, al-Ḥalabī) produced new critiques and original alternative constructions.
- A 17th-century marginal note in a copy of al-'Āmilī's *Tashrīḥ al-aflāk* summarized the entire history of the *shukūk* tradition from al-Jūzjānī to Dashtaghī, showing that practitioners were themselves historically conscious of the genre.

By the early 16th century, per Saliba: "no self-respecting astronomer would have continued to uphold the long-discarded and obsolete astronomy of Ptolemy."

## Significance for the Copernican Revolution

The *shukūk* tradition's significance extends beyond Islamic science. The same mathematical devices developed to resolve the Ptolemaic problems — above all the Ṭūsī couple and Ibn al-Shāṭir's planetary models — appear in Copernicus's *De revolutionibus* (1543), a correspondence that cannot be coincidental. The *shukūk* tradition thus forms a causal chain leading from the 9th-century Abbasid reception of Greek astronomy to the astronomical revolution associated with the European Renaissance.
