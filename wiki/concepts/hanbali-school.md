## Definition and Scope

The Ḥanbalī school (Arabic: al-madhhab al-Ḥanbalī) is the fourth of the four canonical Sunnī legal schools ([[madhhab]]) of [[sharia|Islamic law]]. Named after Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal (d. 241/855), the Baghdad-based ḥadīth scholar who endured persecution (the *miḥna*, or inquisition) under the Muʿtazilite-influenced ʿAbbāsid caliphs, the school is the most scripturally rigorous of the four, prioritizing explicit Quranic and Prophetic ḥadīth evidence and the positions of the Companions over the rational legal reasoning (raʾy) more prominent in the [[hanafi-school|Ḥanafī]] and [[shafii-school|Shāfiʿī]] traditions. Historically concentrated in Iraq and parts of Syria, the school achieved enduring dominance in the Arabian Peninsula, where it undergirds the Wahhābī reform movement of the 18th century onward.

## Founding Context

Ibn Ḥanbal's biography intertwines with one of the defining theological crises of early Islamic history: the *miḥna* (833–848 CE) under ʿAbbāsid caliph al-Maʾmūn and his successors, who attempted to impose the Muʿtazilite doctrine that the Quran was a created (rather than eternal) text. Ibn Ḥanbal's principled refusal, endured at the cost of imprisonment and flogging, made him the exemplar of scholarly independence from political power — the very theme Hallaq identifies as the defining tension in the entire history of Islamic legal education. His resistance became a touchstone for the broader principle that the [[ulama]]'s authority rested on epistemic-religious credentials, not political appointment, and that political power could not dictate the content of religious law.

## Doctrinal Character

Within [[fiqh]], the Ḥanbalī school is distinguished by:

- **Maximal use of ḥadīth**: Ibn Ḥanbal's massive *Musnad* (a collection of Prophetic traditions) reflects the school's orientation toward textual evidence over analogical extension.
- **Minimal role for raʾy**: Where Ḥanafī law permitted considerable reliance on rational reasoning in the absence of explicit textual evidence, Ḥanbalī jurists preferred weak ḥadīth over analogical inference.
- **Flexible marriage contract stipulations**: Paradoxically, Ḥanbalī [[fiqh]] permitted the widest range of contractual stipulations in marriage contracts. Studies of 16th-century Ottoman Cairo show that "both Ḥanafite and Mālikite judges adjudicated cases in which the marriage contracts routinely included contractual terms otherwise thought to be permissible only in the Ḥanbalite school" — women inserting conditions about polygamy, relocation, concubinage, and spousal support defaults. This indicates that Ḥanbalī doctrine on personal status served as a reservoir of flexibility that other schools drew upon in practice.

## Presence under the Mamlūks and Ottomans

Under the Mamlūk sultanate, all four chief justices — Ḥanafī, Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, and Ḥanbalī — were represented in major cities. The Dār al-ʿAdl hearings in Cairo included a "chief justice" from each school. The Ḥanbalī chief justice participated on the same formal footing as the other three, and Ḥanbalī courts operated in the commercial city-center as one of the four schools' courts. See [[qadi-judiciary]] and [[circle-of-justice]].

Under the Ottomans, the adoption of [[hanafi-school|Ḥanafism]] as the official imperial madhhab created a hierarchy in which Ḥanbalī (along with Mālikī and Shāfiʿī) jurists occupied secondary positions in the official apparatus. Hallaq notes that in Ottoman cities, "some of these deputy-judges were non-Ḥanafites who held court in neighborhoods and large villages whose inhabitants were either Shāfiʿīte, Ḥanbalite or Mālikite" — confirming that Ḥanbalī communities persisted even under Ottoman dominance, served by non-official deputy qāḍīs. The broader Ottoman policy was that "any advancement in a government legal career presupposed Ḥanafite legal education as well as membership in the Ḥanafite school."

## Practical Legal Pluralism

Despite the Ḥanbalī school's reputation for rigidity in legal theory, court practice across pre-modern Islamic cities was far more fluid. Nelly Hanna's study of Ottoman Cairo captures the phenomenon precisely: "It was not unusual for one person to buy a house one day according to Ḥanbalī law and get married next day according to Mālikī or Shāfiʿī law. By assessing the specific differences between the schools of law … people deliberately chose the school that best defended their interests in any particular case or transaction." The Ḥanbalī school's relatively expansive recognition of contractual stipulations made it attractive for parties seeking the widest protective clauses in personal-status transactions.

## Later Significance

The Ḥanbalī school's later historical significance — particularly the Wahhābī and Salafī reform movements rooted in Ibn Taymiyya's (d. 1328) Ḥanbalī reformism and Ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb's (d. 1792) revival — falls largely outside Hallaq's pre-modern focus but is foreshadowed by the school's consistent emphasis on scriptural authority against what it regarded as accumulated juristic accretion. See [[taqlid-and-ijtihad]] for the broader theoretical debate around this tension.

## Historiography

The Ḥanbalī school's formative period and its connection to the *miḥna* are well covered in Islamic intellectual history. The school's medieval Baghdad presence is recoverable through biographical literature. The Arabian Peninsula tradition and the school's role in later reform movements are better covered in modern scholarship than in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina's pre-modern sources. The collection is weak on post-1500 Arabian Ḥanbalī scholarship. See [[hallaq-sharia-2009]].
