## Definition and Scope

The 'Sunni-Shi'a split' is the conventional label for the most consequential division in Islamic history: the polarization of Muslim allegiance into those who accepted the historical caliphate and the community consensus (*jama'ah*) as legitimate — the **Sunnis** — and those who held that legitimate leadership belonged exclusively to the house of 'Ali b. Abi Talib — the **Shi'a**.

Hodgson's crucial historiographical contribution is to argue that this division, while having antecedents back to the [[first-fitna-656|First Fitna]] (656 CE), achieved its definitive, empire-wide, explicitly sectarian character only *after* the [[abbasid-revolution-750|Abbasid Revolution]] of 750 CE. Before 750, the piety-minded opposition had been "relatively local, and their rejection or acceptance of the ruling dynasty had varied in many degrees. There had been no overall schism in the community" ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v1-1974]]). The 'Abbasid betrayal of Shi'i expectations, and the subsequent forced choice between accommodation and open revolt, is what converted a spectrum of political preferences and theological tendencies into a durable binary.

## The Terms: What They Actually Mean

**Sunni** (*Ahl al-Sunnah wa'l-Jama'ah*, 'Men of the Sunnah and the Community'): Those who accept the religious authority of all of Muhammad's companions (*sahaba*) without distinction — including Abu Bakr, 'Umar, and 'Uthman, whose legitimacy the Shi'a denied. The Sunni position also accepted the *jama'ah* principle: that the historical consensus of the community, however imperfect, carried binding authority. This is what had been called the *jama'ah* (community) position throughout Umayyad times — the position of those who accepted the ruling dynasty as at least provisionally legitimate while reserving the right of criticism.

**Shi'i** (*Shi'at 'Ali*, 'the party of 'Ali'): After 750, Shi'is crystallized the position that the first three caliphs (Abu Bakr, 'Umar, 'Uthman) had been usurpers who deprived 'Ali of his right. Correspondingly, the *'ilm* (religious knowledge) of those early companions who had not supported 'Ali was tainted and could not be authoritative. This was a position hitherto held only by the radical Shi'a (those recognizing *nass* designation); after 750 it became the marker of Shi'i identity generally.

## The Crystallization Process

Hodgson's account shows the split hardening in stages:

1. **Pre-750**: Multiple shades of opinion coexisted. Even those sympathetic to 'Alid claims often used hadith from companions who had opposed 'Ali. The spectrum ran from radical 'Alid partisans (Ghulat) through moderate Shi'is (Zaydis), through piety-minded critics of the Umayyads who preferred 'Alids without rejecting other companions, to Murji'is who suspended judgment on all fitna-era actors, to Jamalis who accepted the Umayyads fully. See Hodgson's chart 'Piety-Minded Groups in Marwani Times' for the geographic distribution of these positions.

2. **750–754**: The 'Abbasid victory initially presented an opening for 'Alid power. Abu Salamah hoped to install an 'Alid caliph; the ambiguity of Abu Muslim's mobilization had preserved that possibility. The systematic elimination of Shi'i leaders by al-Saffah and al-Mansur closed it.

3. **754–762**: The failure of Muhammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyyah's revolt (and Basra's bloody suppression) demonstrated that open Shi'i political opposition was no longer viable. The piety-minded faced the choice Hodgson describes: "to continue their overt opposition but on some new historical basis; or to accept the settlement offered by the 'Abbasids as a half-loaf."

4. **Post-762**: "Gradually the majority accepted the 'Abbasi settlement and adopted the black robes which, like the black flags, were the emblem of the dynasty." Those who remained unreconciled "found themselves frankly in a minority position." The binary hardened: Sunni acceptance of the 'Abbasid caliphate and the authority of all companions vs. Shi'i rejection of the first three caliphs' religious authority.

## Emic vs. Etic Dimensions

The terms 'Sunni' and 'Shi'i' were partly retroactively applied. Hodgson notes that "each side adopted a historical position in justification of its present attitude" — meaning the theological positions crystallized partly *after* the political polarization, as each side worked out what it believed in light of what had happened. Using 'Sunni' or 'Shi'i' to describe the full early Islamic spectrum before 750 CE is an anachronism that flattens the diversity Hodgson's chart of Marwani-era groups reveals.

The third major tendency — the [[kharijites|Kharijites]] — refused the binary entirely. Neither the Sunni acceptance of community consensus nor the Shi'i claim to a divinely-guided 'Alid imam satisfied their demand for a righteous community that actively enforced moral standards. Kharijites remained a significant force in the Maghrib (Ibadis) and peripheral regions precisely because they operated outside the Sunni-Shi'a binary.

## Historiography

The dating of the Sunni-Shi'a split is one of the most contested questions in early Islamic historiography. Older Western scholarship (Wellhausen, Goldziher) tended to project the full Sunni-Shi'a distinction back to the earliest Islamic period. More recent scholarship (Hodgson, Watt, Madelung) emphasizes the gradual nature of the crystallization and the diversity of pre-750 positions. Hodgson's 1955 article 'How Did the Early Shi'a Become Sectarian?' (*Journal of the American Oriental Society*) is the key statement of this revisionist position.

The question is not merely academic: each tradition's claims to historical legitimacy depend on how the early divisions are characterized. For Sunnis, the *jama'ah* consensus of the community has been authoritative from the beginning; for Shi'is, the community majority was in error from Abu Bakr's caliphate onward.
