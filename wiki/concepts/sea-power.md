# Sea Power

**Sea power** is the analytical concept introduced by [[mahan-alfred-thayer|Alfred Thayer Mahan]] in *The Influence of Sea Power Upon History, 1660–1783* (1890) to name the cluster of conditions, capabilities, and policies by which a nation makes itself "great upon the sea or by the sea." Mahan argued that "sea power had exerted a decisive influence upon the development and prosperity of nations throughout history," and built the entire historical survey of 1660–1783 around demonstrating its "exact bearing at a particular juncture."

## Definition

Mahan never gave a single crisp definition; he defined sea power by enumerating what it is made of and by showing it at work. He opens by characterizing the sea itself as "a great highway; or better, perhaps, of a wide common, over which men may pass in all directions, but on which some well-worn paths show that controlling reasons have led them to choose certain lines of travel rather than others." Sea power in his "broad sense" is two-sided at once: it "includes not only the military strength afloat, that rules the sea or any part of it by force of arms, but also the peaceful commerce and shipping from which alone a military fleet naturally and healthfully springs, and on which it securely rests." It is therefore simultaneously a commercial-economic fact and a military instrument — the navy being meaningless without the trade that gives rise to it.

## The Triad: Production, Shipping, Colonies

The economic core of the concept is a chain of three mutually reinforcing elements. In Mahan's own formulation: "In these three things—production, with the necessity of exchanging products, shipping, whereby the exchange is carried on, and colonies, which facilitate and enlarge the operations of shipping and tend to protect it by multiplying points of safety—is to be found the key to much of the history, as well as of the policy, of nations bordering upon the sea." Production generates goods to trade; shipping carries the exchange; and colonies and bases provide "stations along the road … not primarily for trade, but for defence and war" — Mahan instances "the Cape of Good Hope, St. Helena, and Mauritius … Gibraltar, Malta, Louisburg." The navy is the protective fourth term: "The necessity of a navy, in the restricted sense of the word, springs … from the existence of a peaceful shipping, and disappears with it."

## The Six Elements of Sea Power

In Chapter I, "Discussion of the Elements of Sea Power," Mahan lists "the principal conditions affecting the sea power of nations":

- **I. Geographical Position** — A nation "neither forced to defend itself by land nor induced to seek extension of its territory by way of the land" enjoys "all advantage" (Britain over France and Holland); position may "promote a concentration, or … necessitate a dispersion, of the naval forces," and may confer a "central position and a good base."
- **II. Physical Conformation** — Coastline, harbors, and rivers: "the easier the access offered by the frontier to the region beyond … the greater will be the tendency of a people toward intercourse with the rest of the world." A long seaboard "entirely without a harbor" yields "no sea trade … no shipping, no navy." Includes climate and natural resources; a too-rich land (France) can turn a people inward.
- **III. Extent of Territory** — "Not the total number of square miles … but the length of its coast-line and the character of its harbors." Coastline is strength or weakness "according as the population is large or small" — "the garrison must be proportioned to the enceinte" (illustrated by the Confederacy's blockade).
- **IV. Number of Population** — "Not only the grand total, but the number following the sea," the reserve "fitted for … military life" and for "the creation of naval material." This "staying power, or reserve force" decides whether a nation can recover from a lost fleet.
- **V. National Character** — "Aptitude for commercial pursuits"; a people must "more or less, love money" and seek it by trade and production rather than by plunder (Spain/Portugal) or hoarding (France), plus "the capacity for planting healthy colonies."
- **VI. Character of the Government** — "The wise or unwise action of individual men" and "particular forms of government … have exercised a very marked influence." Government corresponds to "the exercise of the intelligent will-power"; in peace it fosters the commerce "upon which alone … a thoroughly strong navy can be based," in war it maintains "an armed navy, of a size commensurate with the growth of its shipping."

## Operational Doctrine

Sea power as a *military* programme means command of the sea won by the concentrated battle fleet in decisive battle. Mahan repeatedly insists on "concentration of effort" as "the essence" of regular naval operations, contrasting it with the "diffusion of effort" of commerce-destroying. He dismisses commerce-raiding (the French *guerre de course*) as indecisive: it is "an irregular secondary operation," for "commerce-destroyers scatter, that they may see and seize more prey," and history shows that "such evasions are always possible, to some extent, to the weaker party, however great the inequality of naval strength" without overturning "the general control of the sea." The decisive instrument is instead the massed fleet brought to battle — the lesson he draws from Nelson at the Nile ("to choose that part of the enemy's order which can least easily be helped, and to attack it with superior forces") and Trafalgar, where "it was not Villeneuve that failed, but Napoleon that was vanquished."

## Emic vs. Etic

In Mahan's own (emic) usage, "sea power" is both a description of how maritime nations rose and fell and a normative argument that the United States should choose the sea over continental development and "build again her sea power." In later (etic) analytical usage, the term became a category for classifying states and a benchmark in naval-construction debates, frequently detached from Mahan's specific historical claims — the editor notes the added first chapter "has defined the perception of Mahan's writings ever since," meaning the concept as received is largely the geopolitical abstraction of that chapter rather than the operational analysis that fills the rest of the book.

## Limitations and Critiques

Mahan presents sea power as a **necessary but not sufficient** cause. He explicitly refuses to "claim for it an exclusive influence," allowing that command of the sea "did not exclude maritime raids" and that English predominance owed much "to French error as well." His method is vulnerable on its own terms: he reasons deductively from a fixed conclusion, treats sailing-era conditions as near-permanent "principles," and writes the United States sections as present-day advocacy. He himself concedes that "a precedent is different from … a principle," and that a single decisive defeat ("a Trafalgar") could be ruinous to a nation wholly dependent on its fleet — an admission that exposes the fragility of a strategy resting entirely on the battle fleet.

The concept's looser rival is found in [[corbett-julian|Julian Corbett]]'s account of [[maritime-strategy|maritime strategy]], which substitutes "control of communications" for Mahan's emphasis on the concentrated battle fleet and decisive battle, treating command of the sea as usually limited and contested rather than absolute. (Mahan's continental and air-power critics — the geopolitics of land power, and the submarine and aircraft — fall outside this slice; what the source supports is his own concession that sea power is one "principal factor," not the whole.)
