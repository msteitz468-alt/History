## Overview

Contrary to a common assumption that Islamic societies excluded women entirely from intellectual life, women have participated in Islamic scholarship across multiple centuries and in multiple modes — though their participation was more restricted than men's, and the modes available to them changed over time. Peter Adamson's survey identifies four principal channels: (1) early Sufi asceticism and mysticism, (2) ḥadīth transmission, (3) poetry, and (4) modern political and feminist philosophy. Several men discussed throughout the history of Islamic philosophy were taught by women.

Sources: [[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]], Ch. 60.

## Sufism and Asceticism

### Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya (d. c. 801)

The earliest and most influential woman in the Islamic intellectual tradition was Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya of Basra, a foundational figure in [[sufism]]. She introduced the theme of passionate, selfless love for God (*maḥabba*) into Islamic mysticism — a theme that would shape the Sufi tradition for centuries. Born into poverty (she was a freed slave), she lived a life of rigorous asceticism and remained celibate, unusual among Muslim women mystics. She was quoted by many male Sufis and imitated by many female ones.

Rābiʿa's authority was recognized in terms that reveal the gendered limits of that recognition: she was praised by male scholars as having "transcended her gender," as being "no longer a woman" because of her spiritual knowledge. This pattern — the exceptional woman sanctioned by being symbolically reclassified as male — is characteristic of the treatment of women mystics across medieval Islamic sources.

### Umm ʿAlī of Balkh (9th c.)

Umm ʿAlī, from a wealthy and educated background, was a Sufi figure who broke gender expectations in several ways: she aggressively pursued her husband-to-be, teased him for unmanliness in eluding her, and unveiled herself while studying with a male Sufi teacher — justifying this on the grounds that her teacher was a "spiritual partner" alongside her husband. Her teacher paid her the "compliment" that she was "a man wearing women's clothes." Like Rābiʿa, her departure from normative femininity was recognized by regendering her identity.

### ʿĀʾisha al-Bāʿūniyya (fl. 15th–early 16th c.)

Born in Damascus in the Mamluk period, al-Bāʿūniyya was author of a manual of Sufi practice and many poems in praise of the Prophet. She represents the combination of Sufi practice and literary achievement that was one of the more accessible intellectual channels for women.

## Women and Ḥadīth Transmission

The wives of the Prophet Muḥammad were the earliest and most authoritative female transmitters of *ḥadīth* — reports about the Prophet's sayings and practices. The most important was **ʿĀʾisha bint Abī Bakr** (d. c. 678), Muḥammad's favorite wife, whose transmitted reports are foundational for Islamic law and daily life. More than a thousand authenticated ḥadīths trace back to chains beginning with her.

ʿĀʾisha's reports also provided an early model for women's critical engagement with the tradition. She publicly rejected a ḥadīth claiming prayer is interrupted if a woman, donkey, or dog comes between the worshipper and Mecca, on the basis of personal experience: the Prophet had prayed while she lay in front of him. This is arguably the earliest example of a Muslim woman using reasoning from experience to challenge a report.

Women were prominent among the earliest ḥadīth transmitters but gradually faded from the role as the discipline became more specialized and required extensive travel for authentication — conditions that excluded most women. They returned to the role in the late tenth century once the hadith corpus was better established.

Women also served as teachers of men in later periods. **Bint al-Ibārī** taught ʿAbd al-Laṭīf al-Baghdādī (12th–13th c.); **Fāṭima of Cordoba** taught [[ibn-arabi]], the greatest of the philosophical Sufis.

## Women and Poetry

Female poets writing in Arabic predate Islam, and this tradition continued through the Umayyad and ʿAbbāsid eras. Medieval anthologies frequently included poems by women, and a large (now lost) collection called *Accounts of Women Poets* once existed. Poetry was one of the most accessible literary channels for women throughout the Islamic world, in part because it could be composed and circulated without the institutional affiliations required for law, theology, or philosophy.

## Women Scholars in the Late Ottoman Period

The most significant shift in women's intellectual opportunities came with late Ottoman modernization. The Young Ottoman and Young Turk movements (late 19th c.) pushed for women's education on instrumental grounds: women were "an untapped economic resource." As the Tanzimat reforms reshaped Ottoman education, new periodicals written for and by women appeared — including the *Ladies' Own Gazette* and other magazines whose contributors were often daughters of the Ottoman bureaucratic class.

A 1895 article by the novelist **Fatma Aliye** referenced the British "Bluestocking" movement of salon culture, affirming that Muslim women should adopt their intellectual activities while not imitating their "immodest behavior." This is characteristic of the selective, negotiated engagement with European feminist models.

## Modern Women Philosophers and Scholars

### ʿĀʾisha ʿAbd al-Raḥmān (Bint al-Shāṭiʾ) (1913–1998)

An Egyptian scholar who published under the pen-name *Bint al-Shāṭiʾ* (Daughter of the Shore). Daughter of a conservative religious scholar and an illiterate mother, she attended Cairo University and had an academic career beginning during the Second World War. She is reportedly the first woman to have produced extensive Koranic exegesis. Her strategy was notably cautious: she chose "difficult, theological Koranic verses with no social implications whatsoever" — in Ruth Roded's analysis, "the strategy of an ambitious woman carefully invading a traditional male domain." Her treatise *The Wives of the Prophet* drew on classical ḥadīth scholarship while reimagining the Prophet's wives' inner lives, but did not advocate feminist positions; Roded suggests it trades in "almost misogynist stereotypes" in its repeated depiction of the wives' jealousy and pettiness.

### Fatema Mernissi (1940–2015)

The most philosophically bold of the modern women thinkers discussed by Adamson. Born in Fez, Mernissi held a professorship of sociology in Morocco and wrote several pioneering works on women in Islam.

Her most famous work, *Beyond the Veil* (1973), inverts the standard Western feminist diagnosis: Islamic society does not oppress women because it considers them biologically inferior (as Freud assumed). Rather, Islamic society fears women precisely because they are the "stronger sex." Drawing on al-Ghazālī's depiction of women as sexually active and demanding (in contrast to Freudian passivity), Mernissi argues that veiling and spatial segregation exist to protect the *weaker* sex — men — from their own uncontrollable desires. The practice of veiling is a concession to male weakness, not female immodesty.

In *Women and Islam* (1991), Mernissi applies hadith-critical methods to challenge anti-feminist reports. The famous saying attributed to the Prophet — "those who entrust their affairs to a woman will never know prosperity" — was, she argues, first reported by a man (Abū Bakra) whose reliability is questionable, since he was once convicted of bearing false testimony. Classical ḥadīth scholars authenticated the report because of their sexist assumptions, not because the evidence warranted it. Similarly, the "verse of the ḥijāb" (Koran 33:53) was revealed in response to wedding guests overstaying their welcome, not as a commandment for general female veiling.

Mernissi paradoxically resembles [[ibn-arabi]]'s critic Ibn Taymiyya in her method: she too urges a return to the authentic teaching of Islam, stripping away later distortions — but in her case, the distortions are patriarchal rather than theological.

## Structural Analysis

Adamson identifies several recurring features of women's participation in Islamic intellectual life:

1. **Channels vary by period**: Sufi asceticism and ḥadīth transmission were the earliest channels; poetry was accessible throughout; formal philosophical education was essentially closed until modern reforms.
2. **Symbolic reclassification**: The most widely recognized women scholars were often praised by being symbolically gendered male ("she is no longer a woman"), which licensed their authority while simultaneously reaffirming the norm that authorized intellectual activity was male.
3. **The exceptional case**: Women's access typically depended on exceptional personal circumstances (wealth, supportive fathers, widowhood providing unusual freedom) rather than structural opportunities.
4. **Modern reformism and women's education**: The Young Ottoman and Young Turk reforms made women's education politically salient; subsequent generations of women intellectuals ranged from cautious traditionalists (Bint al-Shāṭiʾ) to bold feminists (Mernissi).

## Historiography

The question of women's intellectual role in Islam has been shaped by both apologetic and polemical distortions. Adamson's survey aims for accuracy: women were not entirely excluded, but their participation was genuinely more restricted than men's, and the channels available to them were constrained by institutional and social structures. Modern feminist scholarship (Mernissi; Ruth Roded's study of Bint al-Shāṭiʾ) has significantly expanded knowledge of women's roles. The history of female ḥadīth transmitters has been systematically studied by Mohammad Akram Nadwi (though his work is not confirmed in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection).
