# Great Tradition / Little Tradition

A distinction borrowed by anthropologist **Robert Redfield** to describe how, in complex
agrarian civilizations, a reflective, literate, centrally cultivated **"Great Tradition"**
(the temple theology, the canonical art, the court) coexists with the diffuse, local,
largely oral **"Little Tradition"** of village and household practice. The two are not
sealed off: they borrow from and reshape one another.

## Use in Egyptology (Kemp)

Barry Kemp made this pairing central to
[[sources/kemp-ancient-egypt-anatomy-2018|*Anatomy of a Civilization*]] (esp. Ch. 3, "The
dynamics of culture"). He reads pharaonic high culture — the royal-temple ideology of
[[actors/maat|Maat]], the formal canon of art and architecture
([[concepts/egyptian-formalism]]) — as a **"Great Culture"** that overlay, standardized
and partly absorbed the varied folk religion and material practice of ordinary
communities. The relationship is dynamic: popular practice feeds motifs upward, while the
Great Tradition disciplines and formalizes them. This lets Kemp explain both Egypt's
durable surface uniformity and the persistent local variety beneath it.

Kemp's mechanics: "Great culture, which requires patronage and the direction of labour,
**originates in courts**," where splendour and craft are "instruments of rule, encouraging
deference." To become dominant a Great Tradition "has to **colonize the minds of the
nation**. Whatever does not succumb becomes 'folk culture.'" He frames this as two competing
sources of order — **self-organization** (informal, spontaneous) versus a **codified style**
taught from authoritative models — with the self-defining steadily losing ground. The court
did not flip a switch at Dynasty 1: for "about a third of its history" (c. 3085–2117 BCE)
Egypt ran "two sources of cultural creativity... almost amounting to two cultures." Folk
culture is mostly invisible (it "uses music, oral tale and dance," lost to archaeology) and
surfaces only when official patronage adopts it — the domestic god **Bes** from the New
Kingdom, the Late Period boom in **sacred-animal** burial. The reciprocal is
[[concepts/egyptian-formalism|formalism]]; the "before" state is
[[concepts/preformal-egypt|Preformal Egypt]].

## Limitations and Critiques

The model risks a too-tidy two-tier picture and can underweight the many intermediate
levels (provincial elites, temple staff) between court and village. Kemp uses it as a
heuristic, not a rigid sociology.
