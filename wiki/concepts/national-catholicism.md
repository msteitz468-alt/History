# National Catholicism (nacionalcatolicismo)

The legitimating ideology of [[actors/francoist-spain|Francoist Spain]], especially in its 1945–59 phase: the identification of Spanish national identity with Catholicism, the Civil War as a religious "Crusade," confessional monopoly over education, morality, and public life, and the neotraditionalist revival of Golden Age Spain (Hispanidad). Payne (1987) calls the Francoist attempt to revive traditionalism and religious fundamentalism "unprecedented by any other European regime... almost more akin to Islamic revivalism than to Italian Fascism" — the key feature distinguishing the regime from generic fascism.

## Emic / Etic

Emic components — Crusade rhetoric, the Concordat of 1953, Franco's entry into churches under canopy (*bajo palio*) — were the regime's own; the label *nacionalcatolicismo* is largely an etic, often critical, coinage of later analysts. As an analytical category it explains: the Church's role as a pillar-family of the regime; the depth of defascistization after 1945 (Catholic corporatism replacing national syndicalism); and the shock of Vatican II (1962–65), which delegitimized the confessional state from within and turned much of the clergy into regime opponents by the late 1960s — Payne treats this as one of the regime's terminal solvents.

## Detail (Payne 1987)

Payne traces the deep genealogy to Alfonso II of Asturias (791–842) and the reconquest-crusade ideology, revived by 19th-century Carlism and given its fullest modern statement in Maeztu's *Defensa de la Hispanidad* (1934). The Civil War sacralization was clergy-led and reactive: Bishop Olaechea (23 August 1936: "This is not merely a war that is being waged but a Crusade") and Gomá's *El Caso de España* preceded the bishops' Collective Letter (1 July 1937), which endorsed the "civic-military movement" while declining to endorse any specific regime form or foreign model — 6,832 clergy having been killed in the Republican zone. Regime measures ran from the *plato único* decree (October 1936) through the 1938 school restorations to the 1953 Concordat (state confessionality, royal presentation of bishops, Church juridical autonomy). Theorists (Azpiazu's "Christian totalitarian state," Pemartín's claim that "Spain was already fascist four full centuries before" under the Catholic Kings) show the fusion's anti-generic-fascist logic. The 1940s religious revival (mass missions drawing near a quarter-million) coexisted with friction — Gomá's disillusion, censorship even of *Mit brennender Sorge*, Cardinal Segura comparing "caudillo" to a bandit-chief. The term itself was coined derisively by opposition writers in the late 1960s (possibly Aranguren), mimicking "nacionalsindicalismo"; Guy Hermet's analysis (endorsed by Payne) holds the church–state fusion served the regime's legitimacy more than the Church's interests. After Vatican II — whose impact was greater in Spain than any other Catholic country — the clergy became de facto opposition leaders: a special "Concordat jail" for priests at Zamora, ultra attacks on the "red clergy," Carrero's 1972 "hammer-blow" to Tarancón ("The Church leaders may have forgotten, but God does not forget"), and seventeen sees left vacant by Vatican strategy at Franco's death.

## Limitations and Critiques

The concept can overstate ideological coherence (the regime's eclecticism spanned families with different religious politics) and understate the fascist component of 1936–45.
