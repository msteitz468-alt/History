# The Sects of Second Temple Judaism

The three great "philosophies" of Hasmonean-era Judaism — **Pharisees, Sadducees,
and Essenes** — whose crystallization, accelerated by the political upheavals of the
[[hasmonean-dynasty|Hasmonean dynasty]], shaped Jewish religious life down to the
destruction of the Temple in 70 CE. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 8b
(Tessa Rajak).

## The groupings

Josephus first names the three *haireseis* under Jonathan and locates their
emergence in the early Hasmonean period:

- **Pharisees** — from a Hebrew root "to separate"; pursued **ritual purity and
  strict tithing** within enclosed table-fellowships (*havuroth*), and championed an
  **Oral Law** supplementing the Torah. Unlike the Essenes they **did not withdraw**
  from Jerusalem, so were repeatedly in **direct political opposition** to the rulers;
  their interpretations spread through the populace and (Josephus claims) dominated
  public practice by 70 CE.
- **Sadducees** — the **aristocratic, high-priestly** party (their name linked to
  **Zadok**); denied the Oral Law, fate, and the afterlife; generally **supported the
  ruling house** and supplied its agents.
- **Essenes** — a **separatist, monastic** movement of extreme purity, hierarchy, and
  a revised calendar, **withdrawing from the Temple cult** as defiled. Now generally
  **identified with the Qumran community** of the **Dead Sea Scrolls**.

## Roots and political entanglement

The pursuit of piety predated the sects: already in the 160s the **hasidim** ("the
pious") joined Judas Maccabaeus but broke with him. Rajak's argument is that
**sectarianism grew out of disagreements over political and social change** — the rise
of a worldly, Hellenizing military monarchy — **expressed largely in religious
terms** (the status of the Oral Law, the purity of the Temple, the legitimacy of the
Hasmonean high priesthood). Politics and religion were **not separable spheres**.

The flashpoints recur: **John Hyrcanus** broke with his early Pharisee teachers (the
"banquet" quarrel — the demand that he surrender the high priesthood); **Alexander
Jannaeus** fought a mass civil war against (probably) Pharisaic-led opposition,
culminating in the **crucifixion of 800**; and **Salome Alexandra** reversed course
and **shared power with the Pharisees**.

## The Qumran / Dead Sea Scrolls

The Scrolls give "unmediated access" to one sect's thought-world. Their cryptic
history tells of a **Teacher of Righteousness**, a priest who led the elect (the
"sons of Zadok") into withdrawal, against a **"Wicked Priest"** (plausibly
**Jonathan the Hasmonean**) and the **"seekers of smooth things"** (probably the
Pharisees). The **Temple Scroll's** law of kingship is a pointed Bible-based critique
of Hasmonean rule. The community's withdrawal dates plausibly to the mid-2nd century;
the **Nahum Commentary's** "furious young lion" who crucifies his enemies is read as
**Jannaeus**.

## Contested

The **chronology** of the Qumran sect, the **identity** of the Teacher and Wicked
Priest, and the degree to which Josephus' Greek-philosophical framing distorts the
sects' real concerns are all debated. Whether the *hasidim* were even a coherent
group is disputed. Rajak warns against the modern false dichotomy of "political
vs. religious" parties.
