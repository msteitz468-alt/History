# Iron Age Warfare

## Definition and Scope

Iron Age warfare among the Celts of temperate Europe encompassed raiding, large-scale migrations, mercenary service, single combat, headhunting, and the use of chariots and distinctive equipment. It was deeply embedded in social hierarchy, with the size of a leader’s entourage and success in war serving as key indicators of status. The range details its transformation from the migration period (raiding and conquest) to the post-200 BCE developed world (restricted raiding, clientage with Rome, and new display mechanisms).

## Key Features Grounded in Cunliffe 2018 Range

- **Social role of warfare and entourage:** "the size of a leader’s following was seen as an indicator of his status. In earlier times... the raid provided the opportunity to demonstrate leadership and the feast was the occasion for the display of status before the assembled company." By the developed period this was replaced in parts of Gaul by "a form of potlatch—a process of conspicuous giving and consumption" exemplified by Arvernian chief Louernius who "built a large square enclosure and there stockpiled food and set up wine presses to feast all comers over a period of many days. He also drove about the countryside in his chariot scattering ‘little pieces of gold and silver’ to the horde who followed eagerly in his wake." (the developed celtic world 318–19)
- **Chariot and mobility:** Chariots used in display and earlier combat; "a few [warriors’ tombs] were provided with parts of chariots or chariot fittings." Louernius example above. British forces noted for "the mobility of the British cavalry and chariots in battle." (range contexts)
- **Warrior equipment and status markers:** Warriors’ tombs with "fittings appropriate to the feast" plus "short swords with anthropoid hilts. Widely distributed from Hungary to Britain, such swords may have been an emblem of a particular social class." Elite "wearing a tunic of chain mail and a torc and carrying a shield." (the developed celtic world 343–5; statue references)
- **Headhunting and trophy practices:** Detailed classical account (Diodorus, presumably Poseidonius): "They cut off the heads of enemies slain in battle and attach them to the necks of their horses. These blood-stained spoils they hand over to their attendants and carry off as booty, while striking up a paean and singing a song of victory, and they nail up these first fruits upon their houses... They embalm in cedar-oil the heads of the most distinguished enemies and preserve them carefully in a chest and display them with pride to strangers..." Context: "to own and to display a distinguished head was to retain and control the power of the dead person, which was part of the inheritance of the lineage." Livy example of Boii mounting a consul’s skull as a cup for the deity. Skulls in Celto-Ligurian sanctuaries interpreted in this light. (religious systems 309)
- **Mercenary service and Roman clientage:** "an aspiring young warrior might place himself and his fighting entourage in the hands of Rome to serve as auxiliaries in the Roman army, much as his ancestors might have been engaged in a less formal way as mercenaries by the Mediterranean powers. Knowledge of Roman fighting methods and acceptance of Roman ways would have provided another opportunity to demonstrate social superiority." (the developed celtic world 319)
- **Sanctuary deposition of war gear:** "the ditch... was used as a repository for sacrificed offerings, including some 2,000 broken weapons and large quantities of animal bones" at Gournay-sur-Aronde. Similar at La Tène, Llyn Cerrig Bach, Hjortspring. (religious systems 294, 297)
- **Eastern fringes dynamics:** Dacian expansion under Burebista (60/59 BCE) "moving first against the Scordisci and then against the Boii and Taurisci... The defeat of the Celtic tribes was so devastating..." leading to displacement (Boii into Noricum and Helvetian territory). Cimbri and Teutones migrations "inflicting further defeats on Roman armies" and destabilizing Celtic groups. (the developed celtic world 327–9)

## Social Hierarchy and Change

Warfare was central to hierarchy in the migration period but transformed after ~200 BCE as Roman power and Dacian/Germanic pressures restricted traditional raiding. New mechanisms: potlatch, coinage as largesse ("Gold and silver coins were used for gift exchange rather than commerce... the coin... would be seen to emanate ultimately from his authority"), client kingship, and oppida as route-control centres. Three social zones identified: core (elected magistrates), intermediate (kings + oppida), periphery (sumptuous burial rites for elite status). (the developed celtic world 345)

## Historiography and Limitations (per range)

Range draws heavily on Poseidonius (via Diodorus, Strabo) for warrior ethos and classical stereotypes, balanced against archaeological evidence of burials, weapon deposits, and oppida. Emphasizes that "the Celts were, in Caesar’s words, extremely superstitious" linking religion and war (head trophies, sanctuary offerings). Limitations: detailed tactical accounts or equipment typologies not primary focus here (cross-ref classical sources); emphasis on social/economic embedding of war rather than battlefield mechanics. Dacian threat and Cimbri episode show external pressures reshaping Celtic warfare capacity.
