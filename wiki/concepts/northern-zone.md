# Northern Zone (Archaeological Complex)

## Definition and Scope

The "Northern Zone" (Chinese: pei-fang ti-ch'ü, 北方地區) is an etic — that is, analytically imposed, not historically self-described — term used in Chinese and Western archaeology to designate the broad cultural zone lying north of the Central Plain civilization of China. Geographically, it runs from the Liao River basin in the northeast, westward through northern Hebei (Hopei), northern Shanxi (Shan-hsi), and northern Shaanxi (Shensi), to the Ningxia–Qinghai region in the northwest, encompassing most of present-day Inner Mongolia. The zone roughly corresponds to the arc of territory that would later be demarcated by the Qin and Han Great Walls, though the Wall itself was a political construction imposed on a culturally fluid landscape.

The critical point in Di Cosmo's usage is that the term designates a zone of shared material culture, NOT a homogeneous or politically unified culture:

> "The term 'Northern Zone,' therefore, should not suggest a homogeneous culture, but a broad area in which different peoples shared a common metallurgical tradition, one that typified the north and marked a cultural boundary between the north and the civilization of the Central Plain." (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 49)

This formulation deliberately pushes back against two older distortions: first, the classical Chinese historiographical tendency to lump all northern peoples together under labels like "barbarians" (reflected in emic categories such as Rong, Di, and Hu); and second, the more recent archaeological tendency — what Lothar von Falkenhausen calls the "regionalist paradigm" — to treat each local culture as a purely local phenomenon disconnected from the broader zone of interaction in which it was embedded.

The Northern Zone was already an independent cultural unit during the Shang dynasty. It acted as a filter and link between the Chinese Central Plain and Central and northern Asia — receiving, adapting, and retransmitting influences in multiple directions, not simply as a passive recipient of Chinese cultural diffusion.

## Historical Application

### Progenitor Cultures (Late Third to Early Second Millennium BCE)

The Northern Zone complex did not emerge from nothing. Three major early Bronze Age cultures formed its prehistory, each partially distinct but contributing to a recognizable bronze-using cultural complex north of the Central Plain.

**The Ch'i-chia Culture** (Qijia, c. late third millennium BCE) is the earliest Bronze Age culture within present-day China. Its main sites are in Gansu, but it extended into Inner Mongolia, the upper Yellow River Valley, and the upper Wei River and Huang River valleys. The Ch'i-chia were sedentary agriculturalists whose subsistence also included stock breeding. Some sites (Ta-he-chuang and Ch'in-wei-chia) yield evidence of horse domestication. Their bronze production progressed from forged copper tools (knives, awls, chisels) to cast objects in open molds (knives and axes) to complex composite-mold casting (mirrors and socketed axes). The sudden appearance of advanced bronze metallurgy and horse domestication with no analogues in eastern pre-Shang cultures strongly suggests extensive contacts with cultures to the north and west. Close parallels with the Seimo-Turbino cultural complex — in socketed axes, handled knives, and hafting methods — point to connections linking Ch'i-chia to South Siberia and the western steppe horizon. Following the Ch'i-chia core, subsequent cultures in the northwest (No-mu-hung in Qinghai; Huo-shao-kou/Ssu-pa, Hsin-tien, and Ssu-wa in Gansu) show a gradual trend from mixed agro-pastoral economies toward more purely pastoral ones, driven by intensified contacts with peoples to the north and west rather than by Chinese influence or environmental deterioration alone.

**The Chu-k'ai-kou Culture** (mid-third to mid-second millennium BCE) developed in the north-central zone — northern and central Inner Mongolia, northern Shaanxi, and northern Shanxi — with the Ordos region at its center. The Chu-k'ai-kou people were primarily agriculturalists (millet) who also raised sheep, pigs, and cattle. By the end of the third millennium they displayed pottery decorative motifs — including a snake pattern and the flower-shaped edge of the li vessel — that later archaeologists recognize as characteristic of the area's nomadic peoples. Significantly, oracle bone divination, closely associated with Shang statecraft, was already practiced within the Chu-k'ai-kou culture in the first half of the second millennium BCE. In its final phase (Chu-k'ai-kou V, c. 1500 BCE, roughly contemporary with early Shang), this culture produced an indigenous metallurgical tradition that included Shang objects (ko dagger-axes), pure Northern Zone items (including the earliest bronze dagger of the Northern Zone type), and hybrid objects combining Shang and northern features. This makes Chu-k'ai-kou the probable predecessor of the Northern Zone bronze complex.

**The Lower Hsia-chia-tien Culture** (c. 2000–1300 BCE) extended across southeastern Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, and northern Hebei, with the Peking region forming a broad transitional belt between it and Shang culture to the south. The Lower Hsia-chia-tien emerged at the initial phase of metalworking and produced limited small objects. Its economy was primarily agricultural (millet), supplemented by stock raising and deer hunting. Despite its modest metallurgical production, this culture formed part of the network from which the Northern Zone complex emerged.

### The Northern Zone Bronze Complex (Shang Period to Western Zhou)

From these three progenitors, a more coherent Bronze Age complex emerged in the Northern Zone during the Shang period. The standard typology of the Northern Zone metal inventory, established by Lin Yun, includes daggers, knives, axes with short sockets, axes with tubular sockets, mirrors, and "bow-shaped" objects (Di Cosmo 2002, p. 50).

**Daggers** (short swords) feature integral casting of hilt and double-edged blade with a relatively narrow, straight hand guard. Early types (middle and late Shang) have a curved hilt, often geometric-decorated, with a terminal in the shape of an animal head — horse, ram, eagle, or ibex. **Knives** are integrally cast (unlike Shang knives, which have a separate stem inserted into a handle of different material). Pommels take mushroom, animal-head, ring, and loop forms. **Axes** are long and thick with a narrow cutting edge; the most distinctive type has a tubular socket set perpendicular to the blade — a hafting system completely different from the Shang method of attaching handle to a protruding flat tang. **Mirrors** have a smooth front surface, a knobbed back sometimes bearing geometric or solar decoration; a star-shaped mirror from Qinghai suggests a Central Asian solar cult connection. Some mirrors found in Shang burials (including the tomb of Lady Fu Hao, c. 1200 BCE) have decorative motifs inconsistent with Shang artistic vocabulary. The mysterious **"bow-shaped" objects** — a slightly bent decorated bar with curved lateral sections — appear both in Yin-hsü (Anyang) culture and in the Minusinsk region; Lin Yun suggests they were "reins holders," probably invented in the Northern Zone and thereafter transmitted in both directions.

The defining decorative tradition is the **animal style** (shou-mien wen), shared across the Northern Zone from at least the thirteenth century BCE and common to the Karasuk culture of South Siberia (1200–800 BCE) and early Central Asian nomadic cultures. At this early stage in the Northern Zone, animal style consisted primarily of ornamental animal heads rendered in the round and attached to the ends of knife handles and dagger hilts. The animal style marks the single most visible cultural boundary between Northern Zone and Chinese Central Plain traditions.

Geographically, the Northern Zone complex clusters around type sites at Li-chia-ya (Shaanxi) and Ch'ao-tao-kou (Hebei), and covers the broad area between the Yellow River bend and the Liao River basin — northern Hebei, Shanxi, and Shaanxi. These cultures are known primarily through funerary sites, where bronze objects appear in consistent combinations. Non-Chinese bronzes in Shang tombs at An-yang (a knife with animal-head pommel from Hou-chia-chuang; a knife with ring head from Hsiao-t'un; a pickax from Ta-ssu-k'ung; a northern-style knife and mirrors in Fu Hao's tomb) provide direct material evidence of contact and exchange. The site of Pai-fu north of Peking has been convincingly reclassified from a Shang site to a Northern Zone site, partly on the presence of Chinese ke blades and chariot-type bronze helmets — objects that show technology exchange rather than cultural subordination.

### Evolution of Early Nomadic Cultures (Three Phases)

Di Cosmo identifies three broad evolutionary stages in the Northern Zone, based on changes in funerary assemblages, typological analysis, and radiocarbon dating (Di Cosmo 2002, pp. 57–59):

**Phase One (c. 9th–7th century BCE):** Horse fittings and weapons increase markedly in burial goods, reflecting an economy where animal husbandry grows dominant (though agriculture continues). The northeast appears most advanced in this phase, while the central and northwestern areas lag. The **Upper Hsia-chia-tien culture** (c. 11th–4th century BCE, spanning Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, and Hebei) provides the clearest evidence: its site at Nan-shan-ken in Inner Mongolia yielded the first iconographic evidence of horse riding in northern China — a bronze ring adorned with two hunters on horseback pursuing a hare. Horse and chariot technology at this site includes harnesses with cheek pieces, two types of bits, tinkling bells, and head ornaments. The Ta-ching mining site in Lin-hsi county (Inner Mongolia), dated to the 10th century BCE by radiocarbon analysis, shows sophisticated metallurgical knowledge alongside smelting furnaces and stone casting molds. In the northwest, the **K'a-yüeh culture** (Gansu and Qinghai, c. 1500 BCE to Han period) evolves in this phase from mixed farming and pastoral toward predominantly nomadic: animal sacrifices shift from pigs (in early sites like Shang-sun) to cattle and horses (in middle-period sites like A-ha-t'e-la), indicating the livestock mix characteristic of pastoral nomads.

**Phase Two (c. 6th–4th century BCE):** The "Scythian triad" — weapons, horse gear, and objects decorated in animal style — appears clearly. Bronze daggers, abundant horse fittings, and animal-style plaques concentrate especially in two areas: (1) the Ordos region and the zone between Inner Mongolia and Liaoning east of the T'ai-hang Mountains; and (2) the territory spanning Qinghai, Gansu, and Ningxia. Iron metallurgy appears in this phase — but iron tools precede iron weapons, and iron's social impact appears limited compared to its technological potential. Bimetallic weapons (bronze hilts, iron blades) emerge. Sites such as **T'ao-hung-pa-la** (late Spring and Autumn, 6th–5th century BCE) and **Mao-ch'ing-kou** (its Phase I, also Spring and Autumn) represent the earliest "true" nomadic foci in the north-central area, and may be directly ancestral to the Xiongnu cultural complex. The site of Chün-tu-shan near Peking reveals a fully nomadic burial assemblage including one hundred straight-blade daggers, ko dagger-axes, and extensive horse fittings, suggesting that a martial horse-riding elite dominated the area. In the northwest, **Ku-yüan county** (Ningxia) and **Ch'ing-yüan** (Gansu) emerge as comparable centers of Scythian-type pastoralism.

**Phase Three (c. mid-4th–3rd century BCE):** Sites concentrated in the Ordos area, generally referred to as "Xiongnu" in the archaeological literature, show a decisive shift in funerary content: precious metals (especially gold) predominate over weapons in aristocratic burials. At **A-lu-ch'ai-teng** (north of T'ao-hung-pa-la), two late Warring States tombs yielded 218 gold and 5 silver objects, including a unique four-piece gold headdress set — identified by excavators as a royal burial, possibly of the Lou-fan people. At **Hsi-kou-p'an** (also in the Ordos), gold and silver ornaments abound in one burial while other assemblages show traditional nomadic-aristocracy objects. Simultaneously, iron agricultural tools (hoes, adzes, pickaxes) appear in adjacent settlements, indicating a symbiotic relationship between nomadic aristocracy and farming tributaries. The presence of Chinese characters on golden plates and silver ornaments, and silver rein rings bearing workshop marks from the Chinese state of Zhao, suggests that by this period much of the gold and precious-metal wealth originated as trade goods from China — payments for horses, cattle, furs, and other northern products.

### Sub-Regional Variation and the Problem of Cultural Boundaries

A critical methodological observation underlies Di Cosmo's entire treatment: the Northern Zone was characterized by multiple, partially overlapping cultural centers that interacted and influenced each other but did not fuse into a single unit. The northeastern zone (Upper Hsia-chia-tien), the north-central Ordos zone, and the northwestern Kansu-Ningxia zone each had distinctive trajectories. Watson's suggestion — based on the T'ai-hang mountain range as a watershed — that the zone divided into a western (continental steppe-oriented) and eastern (forest-grassland-maritime Manchurian) sub-zone has merit that Chinese archaeological literature, focused on the master paradigm of the Northern Zone as a unit, has tended to overlook. The "regionalist paradigm" in Chinese archaeology, noted by von Falkenhausen, has partly obscured these sub-regional dynamics by assigning local cultures to the archaeological "identity" of Chinese successor states (Zhao, Yan, etc.) rather than treating them as parts of a trans-Sinitic cultural continuum.

The coexistence of different burial types within the same site (earthen pits and catacomb burials; pits lined with stone slabs; wooden coffins) reflects the ethnic and social complexity that Di Cosmo identifies as central to understanding the Northern Zone: nomadic pastoral aristocracies ruled over economically diverse subordinated communities that included farmers, hunters, and various agro-pastoral peoples.

## Limits and Critiques

**The homogeneity problem:** Despite its value, "Northern Zone" risks implying more cultural coherence than the evidence supports. Di Cosmo himself warns against this reading. The metallurgical tradition is genuinely shared — but political organization, burial customs, language, and subsistence mixes varied substantially across the zone. The same material assemblage (weapons + horse fittings + animal-style ornaments) was produced by ethnically and organizationally diverse groups.

**The Chinese-centrism problem:** Lin Yun's original typology, which remains the standard, was formulated primarily in relation to Shang culture — defining what is "not Chinese." This potentially biases the analysis toward defining northern cultures negatively rather than understanding them on their own terms. Di Cosmo partially addresses this by examining internal evolution within northern cultures rather than only their relationship with the Chinese core.

**The migration vs. local-evolution debate:** The origins of pastoral nomadism in the Northern Zone remain contested. Di Cosmo surveys theories ranging from climate-driven aridification (which drove communities toward pastoralism) to migrations from the Sayano-Altai or northeastern Manchuria, to purely internal social evolution stimulated by contact with more advanced pastoral communities. The Shelach model — that intensive Chinese agriculture stimulated complementary pastoral specialization in marginal zones — is critiqued as inconsistent with the evidence that Hsia-chia-tien cultures were themselves sedentary farming societies, not peripheral to Chinese agriculture.

**The "Scythian triad" as a diagnostic tool:** Archaeologists use the "Scythian triad" (weapons, horse gear, animal-style decoration in funerary assemblages) as the defining marker of "true" nomads. Di Cosmo applies this criterion but acknowledges its limitations: it produces a potentially circular definition of nomadism and may misclassify communities that were pastoral but not fully nomadic, or nomadic but whose burials preserve weapons less well.

**Periodization uncertainty:** For most Northern Zone sites, chronologies rest on typological analysis and limited radiocarbon dates, not secure documentary evidence. Dates given for phases are approximate and contested.
