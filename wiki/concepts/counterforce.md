# Counterforce

**Counterforce** targeting directs nuclear strikes primarily against the enemy's military forces and capabilities — especially nuclear delivery systems, command centers, airfields, missile silos, and supporting infrastructure — rather than against population and economic centers (**countervalue**).

## Rationale and Appeal

- **War-fighting and damage limitation.** If deterrence fails, counterforce strikes might disarm or blunt the opponent's ability to retaliate, thereby "winning" or at least limiting damage to one's own side.
- **Credibility.** A threat to strike enemy weapons (rather than cities) may be more believable because it does not require the threatener to commit national suicide in the opening move.
- **Pre-emption and "the importance of being first."** In the late 1950s, as both sides acquired growing nuclear arsenals, analysts on both sides concluded that the side that struck first against the other's strategic forces might dramatically reduce the damage it would suffer. This logic drove interest in accurate, prompt hard-target-kill systems and in pre-emptive doctrine.

McNamara's 1962 Ann Arbor speech articulated the counterforce preference: principal objectives "should be the destruction of the enemy’s military forces, not of his civilian population." This would give the opponent "the strongest possible incentive to refrain from striking our own cities."

## Technological Shift (Range 08 Grounding)

Improvements in guidance systems during the 1970s made hard-target counterforce practical. CEP accuracies moved "from half of a mile to under a sixth of a mile". MIRVed systems reversed unfavorable exchange rates: previously at least two ICBMs needed to destroy one enemy silo; with accuracy and MIRV "one MIRVed ICBM could take out one or more enemy ICBMs". Poseidon (SLBM) and Minuteman III (ICBM) exemplified the trend. Fixed targets became vulnerable to accurate small-yield warheads. (Freedman 1981, NEW TECHNOLOGIES / SELECTIVE OPTIONS, p.352-353, 673-724).

ICBMs thereby "took on the attributes of first-strike weapons for which bombers had been criticized in the late 1950s." They remained vulnerable in silos "and a threat to the equivalent force of the enemy once launched." SLBMs retained status as the "only pure second-strike weapon" (with qualifications for ported boats). Cruise missiles offered eventual high-accuracy single-shot options but at slow speed. (Freedman 1981, p.354, 756-773).

## ICBM Vulnerability Debate and Escalation Dominance

The flip side of improved US counterforce was growing Soviet hard-target capability against US Minuteman. By late 1970s the canonical scenario was a Soviet counterforce first strike eliminating US ICBMs while preserving Soviet reserve. Only the ICBM leg could respond in kind against hard targets. Loss of it would force the US President into city attacks, leaving the USSR with escalation dominance and greater remaining countervalue capacity. (Freedman 1981, ICBM VULNERABILITY, p.388-391).

Gray argued this would make prudent US Presidents deterred from initiating; any US initiation would likely end after a Soviet-favorable counter-military exchange. Schlesinger warned the development "could bring into question our ability to respond... in a controlled, selective, and deliberate fashion" and "give the Soviets a capability that we ourselves would lack". MX was pursued to restore both survivability and counterforce. (Freedman 1981, p.389, 392).

## Soviet and European Dimensions

Soviet doctrine showed limited interest in Western-style controlled counterforce. Emphasis remained on decisive destruction of enemy forces and war-making capacity. Sidorenko: nuclear employment would make them "the main means for destroying the enemy in battle". (Freedman 1981, p.381).

For Europe, Schlesinger doctrine extended counterforce logic to theatre strikes supporting conventional battle (airfields, marshalling yards, oil). This raised "Euro-strategic" issues: whether US strategic counterforce options strengthened or weakened coupling, and the 1979 decision for 572 ground-based US missiles as a regional counter to Soviet systems "designed solely for European use". To a German, "practically any nuclear weapon is potentially ‘strategic’ in its consequences." (Freedman 1981, OPTIONS FOR A EUROPEAN WAR, p.385-387).

## Critiques and Limits (Freedman Verdict)

Counterforce always risked becoming cover for larger forces and first-strike capabilities. Technological improvements did not solve command/control for moving targets (submarines) or guarantee limitation once exchanges began. Freedman notes the fundamental uncertainty: "That fallible and unpredictable human beings might be faced with such a choice has always been the fundamental uncertainty upon which nuclear deterrence has depended." (Freedman 1981, p.391).

In the Conclusion, the pursuit of refined counterforce options exemplified the shift to "second- and third-order issues". No strategy escaped the risk that controlled strikes would degenerate into "a furious exchange of devastating and crippling blows against the political and economic centres". (Freedman 1981, CONCLUSION, p.2582-2586).
