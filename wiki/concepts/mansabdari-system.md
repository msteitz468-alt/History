## Definition and Scope

The mansabdari system was the graded bureaucratic-military ranking structure by which [[akbar]] organized the imperial administration of the [[mughal-empire]] from approximately 1575 onward. *Mansab* means rank or position; a *mansabdar* was an officer holding such a rank. The system integrated fiscal assignment, military obligation, and imperial hierarchy into a single unified structure, replacing the looser arrangements inherited from the Delhi Sultanate and Afghan Suri rulers.

Hodgson (in [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974]]) describes it as transforming "the old Mongol-Timurid way of regarding the central power as one great army at the personal behest of the sultan" into "a very flexible system of graded rankings of office holders, manabdârs, in a bureaucracy." The military origins were formally retained: each officer's rank was expressed as the number of horsemen he was nominally responsible for equipping (five hundred, one thousand, five thousand), though "actual contingents were not expected in clearly civilian cases."

## How It Worked

Each mansabdar was assigned a *jagir* — specific lands whose assessed revenue was calculated to yield a sum corresponding to his rank-salary. The jagir was the Indian equivalent of the *iqta* system in earlier Islamicate states (see [[iqta]]). However, Akbar introduced several features that distinguished it from prior arrangements:

1. **Cash-basis assessment**: Salaries were fixed on a predetermined cash basis derived from the revenue survey introduced in 1575–80 (a standardized ten-year average yield assessment).

2. **Frequent rotation**: Jagir assignments were rotated regularly — both to adjust for promotions (which required reassigning larger revenue parcels) and as a mechanism of central control. Frequent rotation meant mansabdars had less incentive to improve their lands (since they would not hold them long) and could not entrench as local magnates.

3. **Central audit**: The central revenue bureaus maintained oversight of what each jagir should yield. Officers had to supervise collection within their assigned lands under imperial revenue regulations.

4. **Military muster control**: Akbar "attempted to reduce the scale of fraud at military musters (where officers were wont to pass off their troops or animals as more numerous than they actually were)."

## Multi-Confessional Character

The mansabdari system was structurally religion-neutral — rank was assigned on the basis of service to the Timurid throne, not Muslim identity. This was essential to [[akbar-religious-policy]]: the Rajput military chieftains and Hindu administrative officers who were integrated into the system received mansabs alongside Muslim nobles. The system thus institutionalized the interconfessional character of Mughal rule in an administrative mechanism, not merely in cultural or rhetorical terms.

## Structural Problems and Decline

Hodgson identifies two compounding problems that emerged over time:

1. **Short-horizon management**: Officers rotated through jagirs had no incentive to invest in agricultural improvement. "Critics of the time seem to have ascribed it to the mismanagement of their land assignments by the military officers, who... were inclined to exact maximum revenue despite effects on future yield." This contributed to long-term agricultural decline and peasant flight.

2. **Demand inflation**: As the empire expanded and the noble class grew, the emperor's ability to find sufficient jagir lands for all mansabdars became strained. "The great emperor had difficulty finding lands sufficient for the assignments that the great ruling apparatus was demanding." This fiscal strain was acute under Aurangzeb, whose expansion into the Deccan multiplied the army and administration without a proportional increase in available revenue lands.
