# Heracles–Melqart Syncretism

## Definition

**Heracles–Melqart syncretism** is the progressive identification, from at least the
7th–6th centuries BCE, of the Tyrian city-god **[[carthaginian-religion|Melqart]]**
("King of the City") with the Greek hero-god **Heracles**. Both Greeks and Phoenicians
came to treat the two as the same divine figure across the western and central
Mediterranean. In Richard Miles's reading ([[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010]])
this shared deity became the principal **ideological battleground** of the
pre-Roman and Roman west: a hero whose "ownership" successively legitimated
Carthaginian, Sicilian-Greek, Barcid, and finally Roman claims to dominate the
region — making the figure of Heracles the running emblem of the book's argument
that the Mediterranean was a zone of cultural fusion, not a Greco-Roman monopoly.

## Why the Two Gods Merged (emic logic)

Three structural affinities underpinned the identification:

- **Both were colonisation founders** (*archegete*). Greek colonists carried Heracles
  west as the hero who cleared the land of monsters and brigands and sired offspring
  with local elites; Melqart was the divine founder of [[tyre|Tyre]] and patron of its
  colonies ([[gades-tartessos|Gades]], Lixus, [[phoenician-colonization|the western network]]).
- **Both crossed the human/divine boundary.** Heracles earned godhood through his
  labours and his apotheosis on the pyre; Melqart was a god who was also remembered as
  Tyre's first king, his living embodiment the reigning king.
- **Both centred on regenerative fire.** Heracles' immolation parallels the Tyrian
  ***egersis*** ("awakening"), the spring festival in which an effigy of Melqart was
  burned on a raft at sea to enact death and rebirth.

The **Heraclean Way** — the hero's westward route driving Geryon's cattle (labour 10),
elaborated from Stesichorus' *Geryoneis* (6th c.) — was a mythic itinerary that kept
being extended to track new colonies. Geryon's island Erythia was generally located by
the 6th c. at Gades, site of Melqart's greatest western temple. A **bilingual
inscription from Malta** dedicates the same shrine to "Melqart, Lord of Tyre" (Phoenician)
and to "Heracles *archegete*" (Greek) — direct evidence of the equation.

## Contested Appropriations (the political history)

| Holder | Use of Heracles–Melqart | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Tyre / western Phoenicians | Melqart as royal/colonial patron; *egersis*; Gades tithe to Tyre | Miles Ch.1, 3 |
| Sicilian Greeks | Heracles as Greek founder vs the "non-Greek Heracles" (Melqart) at Eryx; Dorieus' mission | Miles Ch.3 |
| Alexander the Great | 332 BCE sack of Tyre replaced the *egersis* with Hellenic Heracles games — "smothering Melqart in the ashes" | Miles Ch.5 |
| The Barcids / [[hannibal|Hannibal]] | Heracles–Melqart on Barcid coinage; the Italian march staged as a retracing of the Heraclean Way "in the footsteps of Heracles" | Miles Ch.9–11 |
| Rome | Hercules Invictus (state cult 312 BCE); 270 BCE coinage joins she-wolf with Greek Heracles; [[augustus|Augustus]] times his triple triumph to the festival of Hercules and builds the Via Augusta from Gades | Miles Ch.6, 15 |

Miles's central narrative claim is that **Hannibal weaponised the syncretism**: his Greek
historians ([[#related|Silenus, Sosylus]]) cast Heracles–Melqart as his divine guide,
presenting him as saviour of the old Greek-Punic-indigenous west against the Roman
interloper (inverting **Timaeus**'s earlier Greek-vs-barbarian axis); his Greek
historians **Silenus** and **Sosylus** built the literary case. Rome's deepest,
most lasting response, on this reading, was not the battlefield victory but the
**capture of the Heraclean legacy for itself** — see [[hellenization-of-rome]] and
[[punic-faith]].

## Limitations and Critique

This is a strong interpretive frame, not settled consensus. The identification of
specific monuments (e.g. the Sant'Omobono statue in Rome as Heracles–Melqart) and the
extent to which Hannibal ran a coherent "propaganda" programme are debated; both
Polybius and Livy complain of Hannibalic divine-favour claims, which Miles reads as
evidence of their success but which could reflect ordinary Hellenistic ruler-imagery.
Treat the Heracles thread as Miles's organising argument: illuminating, sometimes
maximalist, and dependent on hostile sources read against the grain.
