# Ancient Graffiti

## Definition

**Graffiti** in antiquity are informal, non-official texts and images —
scratched (incised with a stylus/*graphium*), painted, inked, or traced in
charcoal or chalk — applied to available surfaces: the walls of civic and
private buildings (baths, brothels, latrines, shops, temples, theatres, tombs),
architectural elements (columns, doorposts, lintels), and portable objects of
clay (pots, tiles, votives, *ostraca*), metal, stone and wood. Peter Keegan,
following A. J. Peden, treats them as "a form of communication that is both
personal and free of the everyday social restraints" — and therefore as a
distinctive window onto the lived experience of ordinary people, free and
enslaved, otherwise invisible in canonical literary and monumental sources.
Keegan reads each graffito as a social/cognitive **"script"** — a sequence of
perception, evaluation and response — asking what it *does* socially and
psychologically, not merely what it says.

### Emic vs. etic

The category is partly **etic**: "graffiti" is a modern label, and its modern
legal definition (illicit, public defacement / "anti-social behaviour") does
*not* map onto antiquity, where marking surfaces was widespread, tolerated, and
governed by the property-owner's consent rather than by a blanket ban.
Prohibitions existed (e.g. *CIL* IV.7521 at Pompeii: "If someone writes
something here, may he rot…"; *CIL* VI.52 at Rome, the temple of Sol) but were
exceptional. At the same time the practice is **emic**: ancient inscribers acted
within their own conventions of address, insult, votive dedication and
commemoration, aware their marks would be read. In late-8th-c. BCE Greece and in
Egypt the words for "draw" and "write" were identical (Greek *graphein*, Egyptian
*s-sh*) — the conceptual link between sketching, painting and inscribing was
integral.

## Techniques, Tools, and Corpus

Marking required a cutting device (stylus/*graphium* or reed/stone/ivory/metal
point), a painting applicator (*penicillus*, brush), and pigments, charcoal or
inks. The genre sits on a continuum with older mark-making — Palaeolithic cave
art (Chauvet, c. 30,000 BCE) and petroglyphs (from c. 10,000 BCE) — and with the
third-millennium writing systems (cuneiform, hieroglyphic) and first-millennium
alphabets. Subject matter ranges over *abecedaria* (alphabet exercises),
numerical notations, names with praise or insult, commercial and taxation marks,
figural drawings (gladiators, ships, animals, phalluses), and verse.

The single richest corpus is the **Vesuvian region**: **10,916** texts
catalogued in *CIL* IV — c. 3,348 painted on exterior walls (*dipinti*,
including electoral *programmata*), c. 4,664 incised or ink/charcoal
inscriptions, and c. 2,242 marks on vessels (amphorae, *hydriae*, *urcei*).
Catalogued by street and by *regio*/*insula*/*ianua*, preserved by the eruption
of 79 CE. See [[pompeii]] and [[eruption-of-vesuvius-79ce]]. The Athenian Agora
excavations yielded over 3,000 inscribed sherds/lamps; see [[athens]].

## Literacy

The volume and social range of graffiti bear directly on debates over ancient
literacy. Keegan distinguishes **functional/craft literacy** — the ability to
figure out and transmit a short message — from **scribal literacy** (reading and
writing literary works), arguing that slave/ex-slave epitaphs, *programmata*,
advertising, and graffiti support wider (including female and servile) literacy
than purely quantitative estimates allow. Egyptian literacy is put at 1–5% of
the population overall, though the urban estimate at [[deir-el-medina|Lahun]] may
rise to ~15%. Graffiti thus evidence a **spectrum of literacies** (economic,
military, political, religious) rather than a single binary. See
[[roman-literacy]].

## Thematic Domains of Evidence

Keegan organizes the material into four parts — Traditions (history, literature,
art), Beliefs (religion, magic, mythology), and Lifestyles (politics, sport,
commerce, sexuality):

- **History.** Graffiti supplement and complicate the literary record. Egyptian
  examples span protodynastic scenes at **Gebel Tjauti** (c. 3200 BCE, earliest
  Horus title) to New Kingdom pilgrimage texts. Political graffiti quoted by
  historians include the **Brutus** appeals of 44 BCE (Dio), the anti-Nero and
  anti-Agrippina statue graffiti, and the Domitian "arches" pun (*arcei*). At
  Pompeii, Oscan *eituns* mobilization notices date to the Social War (91–88 BCE)
  and a painted "Spartacus" (Oscan *Spartaks*) scene may reference the revolt of
  73 BCE. See [[herodotus]] on ancient historical method.
- **Literature.** Verse graffiti quote and parody Virgil, Ovid, Tibullus and
  Propertius — e.g. the *fullonica* parody of the *Aeneid* incipit at Pompeii
  ("I sing of fullers and a screech-owl…", *CIL* IV.9131) and the recurring
  "admiror… that you have not fallen" wall-weariness couplet. Pilgrim verse
  survives on the **Colossi of Memnon** (women poets Julia Balbilla and Caecilia
  Trebulla, commemorating Hadrian's visit) and Greek travelers' thanks to Pan at
  **El Kanais**. The Bronze-Age **Wâdi el-Hôl** inscriptions (c. 1900 BCE) mark
  the origin of alphabetic writing.
- **Art and architecture.** Figural graffiti (ships, gladiators, animals,
  architectural plans) appear from Egyptian eastern-desert rock art through the
  House of Menander renovation plan and the Pantheon-porch 1:1 drafting outlines
  in Rome.
- **Religion.** Pilgrimage/adoration texts (*proskynemata*) at Abydos, Philae,
  Talmis and the pyramids; votive graffiti at the Hymettos sanctuary of Zeus and
  on the **Dipylon Jug** (c. 740 BCE, among the earliest Greek alphabetic
  inscriptions); Tyche-cult graffiti at [[dura-europos]]; Christian and Jewish
  catacomb graffiti at Rome (intercession pleas to Peter and Paul; menorahs);
  and the **Alexamenos graffito** (Palatine, 2nd/3rd c. CE), a donkey-headed
  crucifixion caricature — one of the earliest depictions of the Crucifixion and
  an anti-Christian mockery.
- **Magic.** Curse tablets and apotropaic marks — see [[defixiones]]. The
  apotropaic phallus (*fascinum*) and "*cacator cave malum*" latrine warnings
  blend magic and religion.
- **Mythology.** Labels naming Trojan and mythic figures; Orphic bone plaques at
  Olbia ("life–death–life… the Orphics"); the Mithraic and Sabazius cult
  graffiti of the frontier.
- **Politics.** Athenian ostracism ballots (see [[ostracism]]) and Pompeian
  electoral *programmata* — including endorsements by trade guilds, women, and
  satirical "sleepers/petty-thieves/late-drinkers" mock-endorsements.
- **Sport.** Gladiatorial tallies in the House of the Gladiators at Pompeii
  (fighters recording their own wins), *edicta munerum* game announcements, the
  Nemea stadium-tunnel athletes' boasts, and word-games (the **Sator Square**,
  ROMA/AMOR palindromes).
- **Commerce.** Pot-marks, amphora labels (Scaurus's *garum*; the Sestii wine
  network), market-day (*nundinae*) calendars, and the Caecilius Iucundus and
  Herculaneum wax-tablet archives documenting auctions — including of enslaved
  people (the literate slave Nymphius witnessing his own sale, 54 CE). Trade
  graffiti at [[naucratis]], [[pithecusae]] (Nestor's Cup; Aramaic pot-marks),
  and Ephesus.
- **Sexuality.** Explicit erotic graffiti articulate the ancient **active/passive
  "principle of superiority"** rather than a modern hetero/homosexual binary;
  see [[history-of-sexuality]]. Examples run from the archaic pederastic Thera
  graffiti (temple of Apollo Carneius) and Athenian Agora *kalos*-inscriptions to
  the ~500 erotic texts of Pompeii (lupanar, Basilica, gladiator quarters) and
  rare female-voiced verse (*CIL* IV.5296).

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Survival bias.** The record privileges durable surfaces and catastrophically
  or aridly preserved sites (Pompeii, Herculaneum, Egypt); it is not a
  representative sample of ancient communication.
- **Attribution and dating.** Authorship, intent and date are frequently
  irrecoverable; earlier "anecdotal" scholarship often slid into speculation, a
  tendency Keegan criticizes.
- **Interpretive framing.** Reading graffiti as social "scripts" is Keegan's own
  cognitive-cultural framework; the balance among philological, archaeological
  and literary-critical approaches is itself contested. Literary references to
  graffiti (e.g. Catullus threatening to draw phalluses on a tavern) are
  *tropes*, not direct evidence of practice.
