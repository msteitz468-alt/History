## Definition and Scope

The term *jihad*, often loosely translated as "holy war on behalf of Islam," refers in Islamic legal discourse to a complex of obligations — individual and communal — concerning the use of force in relation to non-Muslims. The standard reference work *Encyclopaedia of Islam* (article by Emile Tyan) presents jihad as a stable, perpetual religious duty derived from the principle that Islam, "along with the temporal power which it implies, ought to embrace the whole universe, if necessary by force." Roy Parviz Mottahedeh and Ridwan al-Sayyid, in their contribution to the Dumbarton Oaks volume *The Crusades from the Perspective of Byzantium and the Muslim World* (2001), argue that this standard picture is misleading: differences about the status and nature of jihad were a **marked feature of early Islamic law**, and the development toward the "normative" perpetual-offensive-jihad doctrine was a historically contingent process, not a given from the outset.

Crucially, there was no authoritatively codified Islamic law before the nineteenth century. The body of law (more precisely, of legal thinking) is *accretional*, with minority opinions preserved alongside majority ones, and with more than one widely held position possible at any given time.

## Origins and Development

### The Earliest Sources: Maghazi and Siyar Literature

The first half of the second Islamic century (ending c. 766 CE) produced two genres of writing bearing on jihad:

1. **Maghazi literature** — accounts of the military expeditions of the Prophet in the Medinan period and of the early caliphs. Though ostensibly historical, these served as sources for Islamic rules of war. Key authors include al-Zuhri (d. 741), 'Urwah b. al-Zubayr (d. 714), and Musa b. 'Uqbah (d. 758). The jurist Malik b. Anas directed students to use Musa b. 'Uqbah's maghazi rather than Ibn Ishaq's.

2. **Siyar literature** — books on the conduct of state and the legal rules governing war. The most important surviving early example is the work of **Abu Yusuf** (d. 798), which preserves (via refutation) the lost siyar of the Syrian jurist **al-Awzaʿi** (d. 773) and parts of a work attributed to **Abu Hanifa** (d. 767). The *Siyar* of Abu Ishaq al-Fazari (d. c. 801), a section of which has been found in Morocco, is another example.

### The Hijazi vs. Syrian Schools

Mottahedeh and al-Sayyid identify a fundamental divergence between two early regional schools:

**The Syrian school** (centered on al-Awzaʿi and his circle) was oriented toward the active Byzantine frontier and naturally favored the view that aggressive war — offensive jihad aimed at expanding the realm of Islam — was obligatory. Syrian jurists promoted pious stories about the merits of frontier warfare (*ribat*), and their tradition saw a continuous line of legitimate rulers from the Prophet through the Umayyads whose authority was validated by their expansion of Islamic territory.

**The Hijazi/Medinan school** (centered on Mecca and Medina) was more skeptical. Key evidence:

- **Sufyan al-Thawri** (d. 778), the Syrian-trained Kufan jurist, held that only *defensive* jihad was obligatory — a view Tyan's *EI* article treats as idiosyncratic but which Mottahedeh and al-Sayyid show was representative of a broader tendency.
- The *Muwatta'* of **Malik b. Anas** (d. 795), in al-Shaybani's compilation, appears to lack any endorsement of frontier warfare in a jihad context. The Cordovan recension attributes to Malik a hadith identifying assiduous prayer and mosque attendance — not warfare — as the true *ribat*.
- The *Musannaf* of 'Abd al-Razzaq al-San'ani (d. 826), which gathered materials from Hijazi and Syrian teachers, shows a group of respected Hijazi jurists around Ibn Jurayj (d. 762) who rejected the obligatory nature of jihad for all individuals and gave primacy to other religious acts.
- Malik b. Anas himself, quoted in the foundational Maliki law text *al-Mudawwanah*, was "extremely cautious — even doubtful" about the legitimacy of a Muslim offering his services in border warfare led by the Syrian Umayyads, whose rule many Hijazi jurists considered of questionable legitimacy. When asked repeatedly whether fighting jihad alongside Umayyad rulers against the Byzantines was permissible, Malik replied "There is no harm" — not a duty, merely a permissible meritorious act.

### The Emergence of Normative Offensive Jihad Doctrine

The shift toward a normative doctrine of offensive jihad as obligatory occurred in Iraq in the second half of the eighth century, driven by several forces:

- **'Abd Allah b. al-Mubarak** (d. 797), a Khurasanian who came to Syria to study under al-Awzaʿi, wrote the *Kitab al-Jihad*, one of the earliest standalone books on jihad. He integrated jihad into a framework of ascetic piety and spiritual discipline, treating it as a form of asceticism.
- **Al-Shafiʿi** (d. 820) gave the Syrian doctrine its broadest acceptance, elevating "the destruction of unbelief" to be the primary justification for jihad. His stamp of approval made this motivation much more commonly accepted.
- The normative view was consolidated through the *Kitab al-Siyar al-Kabir* of **Muhammad al-Shaybani** (d. 804), the most comprehensive early legal treatment of the conduct of war, later commented upon by al-Sarakhsi.

This Iraqi synthesis transformed the Syrian frontier-warrior ideology into the majority position. Yet minority opinions continued to be preserved in the tradition, and the Hijazi skeptical attitude toward offensive jihad reappeared in later Islamic thought when circumstances favored it — notably in the early fourteenth-century arguments of Rashid al-Din Fadl Allah, who argued that the Quranic "verse of the sword" (IX:29) applied only to Arab polytheists.

### Fard 'Ayn vs. Fard Kifaya

One enduring juristic distinction concerned whether jihad was:

- **Fard 'ayn** (individual duty): each Muslim was personally obligated to participate in offensive war against unbelievers.
- **Fard kifaya** (collective/communal duty): the obligation was discharged if a sufficient number of the community participated, relieving the remainder.

The majority view in the normative Sunni tradition treated offensive jihad as a collective duty (*fard kifaya*) when the community was not under direct attack. Defensive jihad, when Islamic territory was invaded, became a universal individual obligation (*fard 'ayn*). The Hijazi school's reluctance about obligatory aggressive war was, in part, consistent with the collective-duty formulation — individual participation in Syrian Umayyad frontier campaigns was permissible but not individually required.

### The Dar al-Islam / Dar al-Harb Distinction

As the early Islamic conquests stabilized and it became clear that universal Islamic dominion was not imminent — Constantinople remained unconquerable, Muslim armies suffered setbacks in western Europe and Central Asia under Hisham (724–743) — jurists developed the conceptual framework of **Dar al-Islam** (realm of Islam) and **Dar al-Harb** (realm of war). This division appears as early as Muhammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyah (d. 762) and implicitly acknowledges a state of relative equilibrium. It is, as Mottahedeh and al-Sayyid note, "more an expression of something that jurists had to deal with after it had occurred and not as an expression of what they thought should happen." By the time of al-Shafiʿi, a juridical third category — **Dar al-'Ahd** (realm of treaty relations) — had also emerged to govern truce and trade relations.

### Jihad and Legitimacy

A persistent structural tension ran through jihad doctrine: the majority of jurists held that jihad was lawful only if led by a **legitimate Muslim ruler** — in principle, a legitimate caliph/imam. Yet by the ninth and tenth centuries it was increasingly unclear on which ruler that title properly rested. Military leaders exploited the image of jihad in popular piety, claiming that their success in jihad confirmed their legitimacy, sometimes implying caliphal sanction. This made jihad "an unpredictable variable in the internal politics of Islamic lands just as it had become in their relations with non-Islamic lands." The practical importance of this dynamic was illustrated during the Second Crusade (c. 1148), when Ibn 'Asakir gave public readings of 'Abd Allah b. al-Mubarak's *Kitab al-Jihad* in Damascus, inspiring volunteers to help relieve the siege of Ascalon.

## Scholarly Debates

- Whether Sufyan al-Thawri's defensive-jihad position was truly idiosyncratic or representative of a broader Hijazi school — Mottahedeh and al-Sayyid argue strongly for the latter against the *EI* consensus.
- Whether the "verse of the sword" (Quran IX:29) was intended to abrogate earlier verses permitting peaceful relations — Tabari's commentary, Mottahedeh and al-Sayyid note, gives no indication that it does so.
- The dating of the Dar al-Islam / Dar al-Harb distinction: Fred Donner places its appearance in the late second Islamic century (late 8th c. CE).
- The extent to which the normative offensive-jihad doctrine preceded or followed the political stabilization of Islamic frontiers — Mottahedeh and al-Sayyid argue that juristic theory largely followed historical reality.

## Limitations and Critiques

- The essay by Mottahedeh and al-Sayyid explicitly disclaims comprehensiveness: well over half the relevant legal manuscripts remain unpublished, and the essay cannot weigh the comparative importance of different theories at different periods.
- The reconstruction depends heavily on fragmentary and mediated sources (refutations of lost books, later compilations of earlier opinions).

## From Cobb, *The Race for Paradise* (2014) — crusade-era evolution in practice

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Paul M. Cobb]] continues the conceptual story from Mottahedeh and al-Sayyid into the eleventh–thirteenth centuries, when Frankish Mediterranean expansion tested how jihad was invoked, sidelined, or blended with reformist kingship. Classical juristic detail remains under [[islamic-law-of-jihad]]; comparative holy-war institutions under [[holy-war]].

### Semantic range

Jihad rests on Qurʾan and sunna/hadith; core meaning “struggle,” often “in the path of God” (*fi sabil Allah*), including war against infidels and inner struggle against sin (“Greater Jihad”). Cobb notes Greater Jihad is often overemphasized by modern apologists but has authentic scriptural roots; nonetheless **medieval Muslims almost always meant armed struggle against infidels** when discussing jihad. Living veterans gained spiritual/social honor; jihad dovetailed with commanding the good and forbidding the wrong as pinnacle of altruistic moral activism. Ulema often went to the Abode of War as legal advisers, preachers, mystics, or scholar-warriors; volunteers (*mutatawwiʿa*) arrived uncommanded from distant places. Jihad evoked Prophetic/Companion warfare—“a reenactment of the deeds of Islam’s Greatest Generation.”

### Multi-response model (anti-reductionist)

Not all Muslim warfare against Franks was framed as jihad, nor was warfare the only response. Pragmatism—accommodation, grudging tolerance, alliance, even friendship—coexisted with holy war. Ulema-authored sources preferred jihad discourse and condemned pragmatism. Jihad was dominant but never sole; it was invoked for specific reasons (spiritual-renewal preaching, enfeebled rulers mobilizing subjects, upwardly mobile politicians building new states with Islamic credentials). Frankish invasion necessitated jihad only when combined with such factors. Cobb notes at least one Frankish-war observer who never called *his own* fighting jihad.

### Reformist regimes: aspirational ideal to platform plank

- **Abu Bakr al-Turtushi** (Andalusi jurist; *Lamp of Kings*, 1122, for a Fatimid vizier) framed justice, Circle of Equity, and “putting infidels in their place” as aspirational under a failing Fatimid regime—ideals later realized in Near East and Maghrib by rulers Turtushi could not have imagined.
- Under **Nur al-Din**: final melding of frontier Turcoman military worldview with Sunni urban kingship and religious-reform goals; after Damascus 1154, titles stress *al-Malik al-ʿAdil* (“The Just King”) more than pure warrior role; jihad against Franks one small aspect of broader pious image (see [[islamic-law-of-jihad]]).
- For **Almohads** as for Saljuqs/Ayyubids east, jihad was one aspect of broader social reform and state legitimation (building, fortification, policing orthodoxy—e.g. trial of Averroes/Ibn Rushd at Córdoba under al-Mansur).
- **Saladin**: after years sidelining jihad while subduing Muslim rivals, “dusting off” the *mujahid* title (c. 1186) was a political solution for asserting authority over kin and mobilizing troops—not the continuous identity of a career “countercrusader.”

Cobb’s terminal claim for the process: the Crusades did not make Islam militant; they helped justify the dominion of military elites who used jihad-against-Franks as a platform plank. See [[processes/crusades]] and [[crusade-historiography]].
