# Population Politics (Biopolitics)

## Definition

**Population politics** is the making of human numbers — fertility, mortality,
migration, and their distribution — into an object of state knowledge and
intervention. Alison Bashford, in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], argues that
"population" has been political across time and place because it engages two
fundamental matters: **life and death** (fertility and mortality, objects of
intervention from the intimate to the international) and **food** (land use, economy,
and energy). Long analyzed as **"biopolitics,"** the regulation of population, she
insists, always entailed **geopolitics** as well — and was always **gendered**, since
the reproductive labor that creates populations is done by women. Bashford calls for
"an integrated and global gendered political economy of population."

## Emic / Etic

Both. **Biopolitics** and **demographic transition** are etic, analysts' categories.
But the actors themselves theorized population: Malthus, Hong Liangji, Marx and
Engels, neo-Malthusians, eugenicists, and birth-control campaigners all made
explicit, emic arguments about numbers, land, and reproduction.

## The political economy of population, c. 1750–1850

Two scholars observed population and land independently at the end of the eighteenth
century: **Hong Liangji** (1746–1809) in Qing China and **Thomas Malthus**
(1766–1834) in England. Both saw a mismatch between the capacity to reproduce and the
capacity to produce food; Hong's remedy was state-led agricultural intensification
and frontier settlement, Malthus's was a grim "principle of population" in which
numbers oscillate against the food (and land) supply, leaving some always "in want."
Bashford reads Malthus's *Essay* as an early **world history** and a distant
precursor of development economics. Against the prevailing mercantilist view that
state strength required large populations, "political arithmetic," **statistics**, and
the **census** (US 1790, France 1836, Brazil 1872) emerged as tools of population-aware
states — and, in colonies (notably India), as instruments of colonial knowledge and
rule.

## Reproductive politics, c. 1850–1950

A "major element of global convergence" in the modern period, Bashford argues, was
**the rendering public — the business of the state — of what had been religiously,
privately, or familially negotiated matters of reproduction.** Sex became "core
business" for states as welfare and warfare became massed and centralized.
Nineteenth-century **neo-Malthusians** (J. S. Mill among them) folded contraception
into political economy; socialists and communists, by contrast (Marx held
overpopulation "relative," a product of capitalism's need for surplus labor),
rejected Malthusian limits. The era also produced **eugenics**, assimilationist and
"breed-out" policies, and the colonial concern with indigenous **depopulation** —
a "Pacific problem" of decline (venereal and respiratory disease, low fertility)
running alongside the global problem of growth, and shading into the
[[genocide|removal and "extermination"]] logics of settler colonialism.

## Twentieth-century contests

Pomeranz and McNeill stress (against a technological reading) that the **decline in
birth rates is where politics looms largest**, and that outcomes have varied because
the public winners of population debates "could not necessarily override the private
ideas and desires of couples." **Pro-natalist** policies have failed more often than
succeeded; some sterilization campaigns were strongly and effectively resisted. Even
China's coercive post-1980 **one-child policy** forced significant compromises with
rural norms. In most of the world, falling fertility has chiefly resulted from people
making their own decisions amid shifting norms and incentives.

## Limitations and critiques

- **Biopolitics can flatten agency:** Bashford and Pomeranz both stress that states
  repeatedly failed to override private reproductive choice.
- The field's **dark legacies** — eugenics, coercive sterilization, colonial
  population transfer — make "population policy" an ethically charged category.
- The economic and the gendered literatures have tended to talk past each other,
  which Bashford's "gendered political economy of population" seeks to repair.
