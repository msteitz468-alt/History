# Statelessness in Africa (Modern Period)

## Definition and Scope

**Statelessness in Africa** — the existence of politically decentralized societies without rulers or hierarchical governing institutions — is not a museum piece but a dynamic, persistent feature of African history across the early modern, colonial, and postcolonial periods. This page covers the modern analytical treatment of the concept and its role in the historiography of states and stateless societies from c. 1400 to the present, as synthesized by Walter Hawthorne in [[parker-reid-oxford-modern-african-history-2013]].

For the classic typological and structural analysis of stateless societies (Horton's four-point definition, the three-type typology, and the segmentary lineage model), see the foundational page [[stateless-societies]]. This page builds on that analysis to cover: the relationship between statelessness and the slave trade; stateless society responses to colonial imposition of authority; and the persistence of stateless structures into the postcolonial period.

**Key analytical distinction**: The category is a **spectrum, not a binary**. Scholars agree that there is no pure form of either state-based or stateless society; that states and stateless societies share essential functions (organizing production, regulating exchange, resolving disputes, protecting members); and that African societies have moved between state and stateless forms throughout history. The interest of the category lies in how the *degree* of centralization and hierarchy shaped a society's engagement with trade, warfare, and colonial rule.

## Emic vs. Etic

"Stateless society" is an **etic category**, coined by social anthropologists (Evans-Pritchard, Fortes) and applied to African history by Robin Horton. Societies classified as stateless did not understand themselves as lacking anything; they organized authority, distributed resources, and regulated behaviour through lineage structures, age grades, secret societies (Poro, Ekpe), territorial principles, and earth-spirit cults — all functionally adequate to their conditions. The etic label captures only the absence of one *European* form of political organization.

## Stateless Societies and the Atlantic Slave Trade (c. 1400–1800)

The relationship between stateless societies and the [[atlantic-slave-trade]] was more complex than the simple picture of stateless peoples as passive victims of state raiding:

### Stateless societies as the primary raided

Patrick Manning described stateless societies as "the raided" — the primary source of captives for both internal African slave markets and the external trades. Predatory states (the Segu Bambara state, Oyo, Kongo, Dahomey) organized warfare against stateless neighbours to produce captives for Atlantic markets. Martin Klein argued that for such states, slave raiding became "the way the state reproduced itself": warfare produced captives, captives were traded for firearms and iron weapons, which enabled further warfare.

### Active defence strategies

Revisionist scholarship (Hawthorne, Hubbell, Baum) has shown that **stateless societies were not simply passive victims** but developed elaborate defensive strategies:

- **Fortification**: The Samo of Burkina Faso built defensive walls; coastal Guinea-Bissau peoples erected palisaded *tabancas* around villages; "live fences" of prickly bushes blocked invaders.
- **Ecological refuge**: Stateless peoples located themselves in inaccessible terrain — the Samo, Kabre, Dogon, Dagara, and Konkomba on massifs, outcroppings, and riverbanks; communities in the Dar al-Kuti region in highland refuges.
- **Hostile reception**: Floup, Balanta, and Bijago killed European and mixed-race traders until they discovered the utility of ransoming them; Dagara and Lobi were known for shooting strangers who entered their territories.

### Stateless societies as slave producers

More controversially, stateless societies also **participated in slave production** — not merely endured it. Hawthorne's research on the Balanta of coastal Guinea-Bissau shows that, when threatened by the predatory Kaabu state, the Balanta built fortified rice-farming settlements in mangrove swamps and produced captives to trade at coastal entrepôts for iron tools and weapons. The Diola raided distant communities for captives ransomed for cattle. In south-eastern Nigeria, the Aro network — operating through politically decentralized Igbo communities — managed large-scale slave capture and trade from the mid-eighteenth century. The key analytical revision: **stateless societies that traded slaves did not necessarily become states**. They enforced strict prohibitions against the accumulation of excessive wealth and power through secret societies, "witch"-finding institutions, and peer sanctioning — preserving egalitarianism even while participating in the slave economy.

## The Nineteenth Century: Legitimate Commerce, Jihads, and State Formation

The ending of the Atlantic slave trade (1807 for Britain; gradually thereafter) did not end stateless societies' engagement with external commerce. It redirected trade toward "legitimate" commodities — palm oil, groundnuts, kola nuts, wax — which required new organizational forms. Some states underwent "crisis of adaptation"; others adjusted. Stateless societies likewise adapted.

The nineteenth century also saw the expansion of Islam and the rise of **militarized jihad states** (the Sokoto Caliphate in West Africa; Umar Tall's empire; the Mahdist state in Sudan). These states raided stateless neighbours for captives and converts, adding new pressure. See [[west-african-jihads]].

In East Africa, the plantation economy on the Swahili coast (centered on Zanzibar and the mainland) drew labour through coercive processes that disrupted stateless inland societies. See [[indian-ocean-trade]] and [[slave-society]].

## Colonial Imposition and the "Invention" of Chiefs

Colonial rule posed a distinctive challenge for stateless societies: European administrators needed identifiable "chiefs" with whom to negotiate and through whom to govern. In state-based societies, colonial powers could appropriate existing hierarchies (the Buganda kingdom's kabaka and functionaries were retained and used as an administrative system by the British). In stateless societies, there were no such figures.

The response was the **administrative creation of chiefs**:

- **British (Nigeria)**: Colonial administrators imposed the "warrant chief" system on Igbo and other decentralized peoples, grouping hundreds of communities into "native court areas" and empowering appointed (not traditional) chiefs with British authority. Resistance ensued: the **Aba Women's War of 1929** was the most dramatic episode, as were the earlier Ekumeku movement and later the Biafran War. G. I. Jones interpreted these as "episodes in a continuous struggle between the central government and the local [decentralized] people."

- **British (East Africa)**: In Lango (Uganda), the British demarcated a district for the decentralized Langi, then created both chiefdoms and chiefs to rule them. By 1920, thirty-seven chiefs ruled groups of 2,000–6,000 Langi; responses ranged from acceptance to migration and armed resistance.

- **French**: Despite republican anti-aristocratic ideology, the French found ruling families "more ready to be of service than any other class" and generally retained them in state-based areas. In stateless regions, they created equivalent warrant-chief systems. In Maka (Cameroon), the French forced people from politically independent villages into larger ones near roads, appointing *chefs de village* and *chefs supérieurs*. The legitimacy of such imposed chiefs was rarely accepted; many were "thought to be excessively cruel in their efforts to enforce the will of the French."

- **Portuguese (Guinea-Bissau)**: Finding no traditional rulers on the coast, the Portuguese installed African soldiers — often Mandinka and Fula — as *régulos* over Balanta and Floup villages that had no prior political connection to them. Balanta resisted through migration, tax refusal, labour boycotts, and assassination. The result was what Joshua Forrest calls "a terrorist state" — one maintainable only through extreme violence. This history of resistance made Guinea-Bissau's stateless coastal peoples among the most enthusiastic participants in Amilcar Cabral's liberation struggle.

## Postcolonial Persistence

The postcolonial nation-state inherited the administrative structures that colonial rule had imposed on stateless regions — but the underlying social structures persisted. As Forrest writes of Guinea-Bissau: "the various manifestations of rural civil societal authority have preserved their relative autonomy from state-centric forces." Lineages, secret societies, age grades, and local community structures "maintain their own dynamism and relevance beneath — and sometimes beyond — the nation-state."

Hawthorne's conclusion (2013): "It has long been possible for societies without rulers to maintain the relative equality of their members and to mediate pressures from the outside world. By working through their own unique social and political structures, Africans have shaped their histories and the nature of the communities in which they live. They did so in the era of Atlantic slavery, in the nineteenth-century era of legitimate commerce, in that of colonial rule — and in many ways continue to do so today."

## Relation to the Hamitic Hypothesis

The Hamitic Hypothesis (critiqued as early as the 1970s, now fully discredited) held that sub-Saharan African states could only have been created by non-black outsiders — implying that indigenous Africans had never achieved "complex" political organization. Statelessness was used in this framework as evidence of racial incapacity. Robin Horton's 1971 essay refuted this directly by showing stateless societies as possessing "germs from which state organisation and ideology could sprout" — African state formation as endogenous, not imported. See [[hamitic-hypothesis-african-states]].

## Contested Dimensions

1. **The categorization problem**: No pure stateless society exists; the concept is an ideal type at one end of a spectrum. Horton's three-type typology partially addresses this but has been criticized for overstating the neatness of segmentary lineage structures.

2. **Agency vs. victimhood**: The revisionist emphasis on stateless societies as active participants in slave raiding has been criticized for underweighting the enormous harm that raiding inflicted on targeted communities.

3. **Continuity vs. rupture**: Hawthorne sees great continuity in Guinea-Bissau's stateless structures across the colonial divide; historians of indigenous states tend to see colonialism as causing a more definitive break. The balance between these readings remains an open research question.

4. **Categorization vs. analysis**: Some scholars argue that the state/stateless binary, however refined, remains an over-schematized analytical grid for what were in practice continuously variable forms of political organization.
