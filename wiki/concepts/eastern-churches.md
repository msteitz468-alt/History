# The Eastern Christian Churches and the Question of Union

## Definition

The **eastern churches** are the diverse family of Christian communions of the eastern
Mediterranean and Near East — distinct from the Latin (Roman) Church and from one
another in doctrine, liturgy, and language. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Jean
Richard, Ch. 15, "The eastern churches") surveys them in the eleventh and twelfth
centuries, when most lived under Muslim rule and the Latin West — through the
[[crusades|Crusades]] and the reform [[papacy]] — pressed with new insistence for their
**union** under Rome.

## The Principal Churches

- **The Chalcedonian / Greek Orthodox Church** — the imperial Church of
  [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]], in communion with the other Chalcedonian patriarchates
  (Alexandria, Antioch, Jerusalem). Its relations with Rome, long strained, hardened into
  the **East–West Schism** conventionally dated to **1054** (the mutual excommunications
  of the legates of Leo IX and the patriarch Michael Keroularios) and deepened
  irreparably after the Latin sack of Constantinople in 1204.
- **The Miaphysite ("Monophysite") churches** — which rejected the Council of Chalcedon
  (451): the **Syriac Orthodox** (Jacobite), the **Armenian Apostolic**, and the
  **Coptic** Church of Egypt (with its Ethiopian daughter). Richard notes that Byzantine
  pressure to impose the Chalcedonian faith, especially after Basil II, often drove
  Miaphysites to prefer **Muslim** rule, under which they were tolerated.
- **The Church of the East ("Nestorian")** — the dyophysite church of Mesopotamia and
  Persia, which had carried Christianity across Asia to India and China.
- **The Maronites** of Lebanon, who would enter union with Rome during the crusader
  period.

## The Question of Union

The reform papacy and the crusader presence in the Levant made **church union** a live
issue. The Latins established their own hierarchy in the conquered territories
(parallel Latin and Greek patriarchs), pursued theological dialogue (Anselm of Havelberg
with Byzantine theologians), and achieved partial unions (the Maronites; later, briefly,
the Armenians). But Latin pressure generally **widened** rather than healed the divisions:
the imposition of Latin bishops over resentful Orthodox populations after 1204 hardened
the Greek schism, while the eastern churches beyond the reach of Byzantium and the
crusades followed their own trajectories within the Islamic world.

## Significance

Richard's chapter is part of NCMH IV's deliberate insistence that **Christendom and
Europe are not coterminous**: large Christian populations lived under Islam, and the
religious map of the eleventh- and twelfth-century world cannot be reduced to a
Latin–Muslim binary. The eastern churches are central to the history of Byzantium, the
crusader states, and Christian–Muslim coexistence.
