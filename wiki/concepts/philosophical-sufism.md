## Definition and Scope

Philosophical Sufism (also called the "Akbarian tradition," from Ibn ʿArabī's honorific *al-Akbar*, "the greatest") refers to the strand of Islamic mystical thought that integrated the visionary and metaphysical writings of Ibn ʿArabī with the technical philosophical vocabulary of [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]. It constitutes one of the four major branches of [[post-avicennan-philosophy]] and was a primary vehicle for Islamic metaphysical thought in the 13th–17th centuries.

The tradition is "philosophical" in more than its borrowed terminology: it engages directly with the central metaphysical questions of post-Avicennan philosophy — particularly the nature of existence and the status of essences — while offering distinctively mystical answers. Adamson notes that "philosophical mysticism" is now regularly applied to al-Qūnawī and his heirs ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

## Central Doctrine: Oneness of Existence (*Waḥdat al-Wujūd*)

The characteristic metaphysical doctrine is *waḥdat al-wujūd* — "oneness of existence." Adamson stresses that the phrase is not prominent in Ibn ʿArabī himself; it was systematized by **Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī** (c. 1207–1274) and subsequent thinkers.

The doctrine holds that God's existence is absolute and unrestricted, while created things are "specifications" (*taʿayyun*, from *ʿayn*, "individual") — limited, determinate expressions of divine being. Al-Qūnawī uses multiple metaphors: created things as reflections in a mirror; created things as waves in an infinite sea of divine reality; things in the universe as names or images of God.

Crucially, al-Qūnawī denies the charge (pressed most forcefully by [[ibn-taymiyya]]) that this makes God and creation identical. The created universe is to God as an image is to the real thing, or as a name is to what it names. There is a real difference — it is the difference between absolute, unrestricted being and its limited manifestations.

### The Non-Existent as Hidden in God

Al-Qūnawī offers a distinctive solution to the problem of non-existent things (debated in Avicennan scholasticism). Before God creates something, that thing resides in God's knowledge as a "paradigm" or "pattern" — more real, in a sense, than the contingent created thing, which is merely an image or specification of this divine paradigm. The created thing is therefore *less* real than its pre-existent form in the divine mind. This distinguishes al-Qūnawī from the Muʿtazilites (who also spoke of non-existent "things"), a point al-Qūnawī himself made to [[nasir-al-din-tusi]] in their correspondence.

## Key Figures

### Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī (c. 1207–1274)

The systematizer of Ibn ʿArabī's mysticism into a technical philosophical idiom. Al-Qūnawī lived and taught in Konya (Anatolia), where he was in close personal relationship with [[rumi]]; al-Qūnawī said the prayer at Rūmī's burial (1273) and died the following year, buried nearby. He corresponded with [[nasir-al-din-tusi]], who noted that al-Qūnawī's views on essences resembled Muʿtazilite positions. Al-Qūnawī responded that the comparison was incomplete: for him, pre-existent paradigms in the divine mind are *more* real than created things, not merely possible things awaiting creation. He consistently maintained that mystical insight transcends what philosophical argument can reach: "the mystic's insights lie beyond the reach of discursive argument, and can be communicated only in hints and allusions."

### [[rumi|Jalāl al-Dīn al-Rūmī]] (1207–1273)

The great Persian Sufi poet, whose vast verse collections communicate philosophical and mystical ideas through powerful images rather than systematic argument. Rūmī agreed with al-Qūnawī that the "philosophers" (i.e., Avicennians) were limited: he famously called Avicenna "a donkey on ice" and said that "the leg of the reasoners is wooden." Yet he also agreed that Sufism contains and transcends philosophy rather than simply rejecting it.

Rūmī's philosophical contributions include:

- **Two kinds of intellect**: "acquired intellect" (knowledge from books and teachers, including philosophy) versus "universal intellect" (the divine light reflected in the heart). Philosophy belongs to the first category and must be transcended.
- **Annihilation (*fanāʾ*) as metaphysical**: the mystic's self-destruction is not merely ethical self-discipline but a metaphysical return to the ground of being. "Oh chickpeas, boil in tribulation, so that neither your existence nor your selfhood may remain."
- **Created existence as specification**: like al-Qūnawī, Rūmī sees creaturely limitations as veils between the mystic and God — "veils that must be torn asunder if we are to know the single reality of God face to face."
- **Drunkenness and erotic metaphors for divine union**: Rūmī's most celebrated images represent mystical ecstasy in sensual terms. "The self is like a cork, and when removed we find the wine within."

Rūmī's chain of teaching traced back to al-Ghazālī's brother Aḥmad. His family fled Balkh ahead of the Mongol invasion when he was a child, eventually settling in Konya. His most transformative relationship was with the wandering Sufi Shams al-Dīn al-Tabrīzī, whose departure (driven away by Rūmī's jealous students) is the emotional center of much of his verse.

### Later Akbarian Thinkers

Al-Qūnawī's heirs include Shams al-Dīn Lāhijī and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Jāmī (15th c.). The Akbarian tradition also flowed into [[school-of-shiraz]] and ultimately into Mullā Ṣadrā's synthesis.

## Relation to [[illuminationism]]

Philosophical Sufism and Illuminationism share important features: both privilege direct experience over discursive proof; both use the metaphor of light for divine reality; both engage critically with Avicennan metaphysics while using his vocabulary. The two traditions increasingly merged in the Safavid period. Adamson identifies them as distinct branches of [[post-avicennan-philosophy]] that converge at Mullā Ṣadrā.

## Ibn Taymiyya's Critique

[[ibn-taymiyya]] attacked philosophical Sufism more ferociously than he attacked any other tradition. He interpreted *waḥdat al-wujūd* as implying that God and creation are literally identical — a form of pantheism. This would make the universe eternal (sharing God's eternal existence), and would make knowledge of the cosmos equivalent to knowledge of God. Al-Qūnawī's distinction between absolute and specified existence would not, in Ibn Taymiyya's reading, rescue the doctrine from these consequences. The Sufis' supporters retaliated by having Ibn Taymiyya imprisoned in Alexandria.

## Historiography

The full history of Sufism in Persian, Ottoman, Mughal, and Chinese contexts "would burst the seams of this volume," as Adamson acknowledges. Coverage is focused on the Anatolian-eastern Islamic synthesis of the 13th century. Sufism as a broad phenomenon has a separate wiki page ([[sufism]]); this page covers only the specific philosophical strand of the Akbarian-Qūnawī tradition and its integration with Avicennan metaphysics.
