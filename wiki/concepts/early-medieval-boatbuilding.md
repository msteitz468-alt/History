# Early Medieval Boatbuilding

## Definition and Scope

Early medieval boatbuilding (c. A.D. 400–1000) encompasses the construction of small boats and larger vessels used in central and northern Europe. Traditions include dugout (logboat) vessels, expanded dugouts, clinker-planked (lapstrake) "keel" style craft, and some carvel-like planking in western areas. Knowledge derives from surviving hulls, tool marks, experimental archaeology, and workshop evidence. Level of study is uneven: southern Scandinavian planked craft better known than traditions to the south, east, and west.

## Major Traditions and Techniques

**Dugout boats:** Most common small boats (2.5–7 meters long). Built from large whole or halved oak trunks (0.6–1.0 m diameter). By tenth–eleventh centuries, highest quality large oaks were reserved for planked ships in intensively settled regions (England, Denmark). Built with axes, adzes, and splitting techniques rather than fire hollowing in many cases. Fire used in some regions to soften thin hulls for expansion (widening with uplifted ends for seaworthiness), then often extended with overlapping planks. Example: Vaaler Moor boat (northwestern Germany) with one added plank per side. Low-status craft; rarely depicted.

**Expanded and planked hybrids:** Larger cargo craft in Low Countries and southern North Sea ("hulcs"). Tenth-century fragments of seagoing trading vessel found in London. Overlapping planks waterproofed with moss held by battens secured with small iron staples (sintels). Utrecht boat (Netherlands) is a well-preserved inland example.

**Clinker-planked keel vessels:** Dominant for large trading, fishing, and war vessels in northern Europe. Shell of partially overlapping planks fastened to central keel and end posts; pointed at both ends. Planks split (not sawn) from large trees. Light frames. Also used for some small boats (e.g. 4 m tenth-century Arby boat, central Sweden). In Slav/Baltic lands and England, treenails (wooden pegs) common for fastening alongside heavier frames.

**Other:** Skin-covered boats (coracles, curraghs) likely used on western fringes (Britain, Ireland) with slight archaeological evidence. Rafts used on some inland waterways with light pines/firs/spruces.

**Tooling and experimental evidence:** English experimental work on replicas follows tool-mark analysis. Clapton boat (tenth century, London): 3.75 m long, could carry crew + ~110 kg cargo. Graveney boat (tenth/eleventh century, Kent): ~14–15 m trading vessel, flat rounded bottom, straight sloping stern post. Port-Berteau II wreck (Charente River, SW France): edge-to-edge planking (carvel-like); possibly frame-first construction; dated before A.D. 1000.

## Economic and Social Role

These often humble boats played a key role in developing the early medieval economy in lands with poor roads. Used for ferrying, local travel, fishing, fowling, hunting, and carrying cart- or packhorse-equivalent loads of produce or traded goods. Larger planked vessels supported long-distance trade.

## Workshops and Preservation

Larger ships required high-quality timber reserved from smaller builders. Evidence uneven geographically.
