# Slave Society vs. Society with Slaves

## Definition and Scope

A foundational analytical distinction in the comparative history of [[slavery]], formalised by
**Moses Finley** and structuring the entire [[cambridge-world-history-slavery-v1|Cambridge World
History of Slavery]]. Almost every complex society in history was a **society with slaves** —
slavery existed but was one subordinate status among many, marginal to the central productive
economy. Only a handful were **genuine slave societies**, in which slaves formed a large share of
the population (often cited as 20–30%+) and slave labour dominated the central productive sector,
so that the society's economy, social structure, and self-understanding *rested* on slavery.

Two criteria qualify a slave society (per the volume's Athens chapter):
1. **Quantity** — the sheer number of slaves relative to the whole population.
2. **Centrality** — the qualitative role of slaves in production; the material basis of the
   society absolutely depends on slave labour. For some scholars a **social** criterion matters
   more than the economic one: slaves are not merely workers but the constitutive "other" against
   whom the free define themselves as free, powerful, and honoured.

## The Canonical Five

World history is conventionally said to have produced **five** genuine slave societies:

- **Classical Athens** (c. 500–300 BCE)
- **Roman Italy and Sicily** (c. 200 BCE–200 CE)
- **The Caribbean** sugar colonies (British, French, Dutch — 17th–19th c.)
- **Colonial/imperial Brazil**
- **The U.S. South** (antebellum)

The first two are ancient ([[cambridge-world-history-slavery-v1|CWHS Vol. 1]]); the last three are
products of the [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic system]] and the [[plantation-economy|plantation
complex]]. That the canonical list is **Western and split between antiquity and the Atlantic** is
itself analytically significant: it frames the [[atlantic-slave-trade|New World slave societies]]
as structural successors to the Greco-Roman ones, not as aberrations.

## Emic vs. Etic

The category is **etic** — an analyst's tool, not a term used by ancient or early-modern actors.
What was emic was the slave/free dichotomy itself: in a slave society like Athens, citizen freedom
was articulated precisely against the figure of the enslaved (chattel as "socially dead," in
[[slavery|Patterson's terms]]). The *concept* of a "slave society" is a 20th-century comparative
abstraction laid over that lived dichotomy.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **The binary is too rigid.** The [[cambridge-world-history-slavery-v2|medieval]] and global
  volumes of the CWHS explicitly *resist* the dichotomy: it captures neither the borderline cases
  (Sparta's [[helots]]-like serf populations, Ottoman *devshirme*, military slavery) nor the
  continuum of dependency and coerced labour that the global approach foregrounds. Joseph Miller
  and others argue the categories are "an impediment to understanding" the real range of slaving.
- **Eurocentrism of the canon.** Restricting "genuine slave societies" to five Western cases may
  reflect the evidentiary and conceptual lens rather than reality; large-scale slaving in Africa,
  the Indian Ocean world, and Southeast Asia complicates the list.
- **Quantity thresholds are arbitrary** and the demographic data (especially for antiquity) are
  too poor to apply them precisely.
