## Definition

The 'devotional revolution' is the term coined by historian Emmet Larkin in 1972 to describe the rapid and comprehensive transformation of Irish Catholic practice and organizational structure in the decades following the [[great-famine-ireland-1845]], particularly under the direction of Archbishop Paul Cullen (effective head of the Irish Catholic Church 1849–78). The concept describes the shift from an imperfectly instructed, geographically dispersed, and liturgically irregular Catholicism to a highly visible, disciplined, ultramontane institution with:

- standardized church structures and a disciplined clergy
- high levels of religious instruction and practice
- Church control of Catholic education and social activities
- visible allegiance to Rome
- distinctive clerical dress (synod of Thurles, 1850)
- removal of devotional practices from the home and into the church

## Historical Context

Paul Cullen was firmly ultramontane and 'impatient of the idiosyncrasies which had characterized the Irish church.' He was appointed Archbishop of Armagh (1849), then Dublin (1852), and shaped episcopal appointments across Ireland in his own image for three decades. The first national synod since 1642, held at Thurles in 1850 under Cullen's effective leadership, marked the decisive institutional turning point.

The transformation occurred against a backdrop of post-Famine demographic collapse: with roughly half the pre-Famine population gone through death and emigration, a smaller and somewhat more prosperous rural society was more amenable to the Church's disciplinary ambitions. The Catholic Truth Society (founded 1827) had already produced five million books in its first decade.

## The Critique

Marianne Elliott (in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]]) notes that the concept 'has received some criticism, largely based on the fact that the changes were already under way in the eighteenth century and his study concentrated on "orthodox" Catholic practice.' Specifically:

- practices now associated with the 'devotional revolution' (regular mass attendance, Marian devotion, structured catechesis) can be documented in Leinster and Munster from the mid-eighteenth century
- the traditional Catholic popular culture — holy wells, patterns (patron day celebrations), domestic 'stations,' prophetic literature, Marian devotion — was not created by the post-Famine transformation but incorporated and regularized by it
- Gaelic-speaking areas retained older attitudes even as English-speaking areas underwent 'orthodox' transformation

David Miller's influential article 'Irish Catholicism and the Great Famine' (1975) endorsed and extended Larkin's thesis, connecting the revolution to the social disruptions of the Famine. The debate over timing and causation is one of the foundational methodological disputes in Irish religious history.

## Significance and Limits

Despite the critique, 'devotional revolution' remains useful as a shorthand for the visible transformation of Irish Catholic institutional life between 1850 and 1900. By the end of the nineteenth century, the Irish Catholic Church had acquired almost all its modern characteristics. The concept helps explain:

- the Church's social authority in independent Ireland after 1922
- the institutional basis for Church control of education and welfare
- the basis for political Catholicism in Northern Ireland (where Catholic cohesion under hierarchy was if anything stronger than in the south)

The concept is less useful for understanding popular religion, the spiritual experience of the laity, or the persistence of pre-Tridentine practices into the twentieth century.
