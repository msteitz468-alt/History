Islamic family law encompasses the juristic rules governing marriage (nikāḥ), divorce (ṭalāq and khulʿ), marital obligations, child custody (ḥaḍāna), family maintenance (nafaqa), bequests (waṣiyya), and inheritance (mīrāth / ʿilm al-farāʾiḍ). It is the branch of [[fiqh]] most extensively elaborated in legal treatises, most directly experienced by ordinary Muslims, and most frequently contested in modern reform debates. Source: [[hallaq-sharia-2009]], chapter 8.

## Marriage (Nikāḥ)

### Foundations and Social Function

Marriage was "seen in Islam as the cornerstone of social order and communal harmony," simultaneously regulating sexual, moral, and familial relationships. Its primary purposes in quasi-legal literature were preservation of pedigree and sexual fulfillment for both men and women. In strictly legal terms, however, "the nikāḥ contract excluded what we might call the elements of companionate marriage" and "limited itself to regulating, in strictly contractual ways, only those aspects of the matrimonial institution that pertained to the lease of services."

The juristic stance on the obligation of marriage is relativist: wholly obligatory for individuals whose sexual desires are "uncontrollable or nearly so" (failing to marry constitutes a sin); recommended for those with an average sex drive; outright reprehensible for those incapable of marriage due to infirmity or strong disinclination. This gradation reflects the fiqh's characteristic adaptation of legal norms to human difference.

Extramarital sexuality (zinā — covering both fornication and adultery) stands as the "primary cause of social discord" and is what marriage is specifically designed to prevent. Temporary marriage (mutʿa), practiced by some early jurists, was rejected by Sunnite Islam from the first/seventh century. The Twelver-Shīʿite school retains it.

### Contract Formation

Marriage rests on an indefinite contract (permanent, not time-limited) that must involve: two contracting parties, two witnesses, and a guardian (walī) representing the woman. Essential elements: offer (ījāb) and acceptance (qabūl) in explicit contractual language.

**Language requirements:** The Ḥanafites permitted the widest latitude (accepting metaphorical terms such as "gifting" or "selling"). The Shāfiʿites, Ḥanbalites, and Twelver-Shīʿites required the explicit terms nikāḥ or tazwīj ("pairing/marrying"), rejecting ownership-language on the reasoning that "the 'owner' can never stand with that which is 'owned' in such a relationship of pairing."

**The guardian (walī):** The majority of schools require a guardian for women; the Ḥanafites uniquely permit a free, compos mentis adult woman to conclude her own marriage contract without one. Except for the Ḥanbalites, all schools required the guardian to obtain the bride's approval — creating what they called wilāyat mushāraka ("guardian-partnership"). The father-guardian is limited by conditions: no manifest discord with his ward, securing a kufūʾ (compatible) husband, and ensuring adequate dower. Compatibility (kafāʾa) is defined in terms of socio-economic status and personal suitability. The Twelver-Shīʿites limit compatibility to financial capacity and shared religion.

**Witnesses:** A minimum of two male witnesses or one male and two female witnesses. Their function is both attestation and public advertisement of the marriage, precluding any suspicion of zinā. The Mālikites preferred concluding the contract in the mosque for maximum publicity. Slaves and non-Muslims cannot witness Muslim marriages (with Ḥanafite exceptions for certain mixed marriages). The Ḥanafites uniquely did not require witnesses to be ʿadl (upright in religious conduct).

### Dower (Mahr)

The dower is paid by the husband to the wife and is normally divided into immediate (paid at conclusion of contract) and delayed portions (due upon repudiation or death). The wife is under no obligation to spend it on anyone other than herself, not even her children. If repudiation occurs before consummation, the wife receives only half the dower. Most schools permit dower to take the form of usufruct (the Ḥanafites dissent). Dower is "not the point of marriage" — but it is its standard protection for the wife.

### Prohibited Degrees

Marriage is prohibited between persons in defined categories of consanguinity, affinity, and fosterage; these create permanent bars. Temporary bars include the ʿidda waiting period (after divorce or husband's death) and simultaneous marriage to more than four wives.

## Divorce

### Repudiation (Ṭalāq)

The husband's unilateral right of divorce by pronouncing repudiation. Ṭalāq takes three forms by number of pronouncements:
- A single revocable repudiation (ṭalāq rajʿī): the husband may rescind it during the ʿidda (waiting period — three menstrual cycles for non-pregnant women; until delivery for pregnant women; four months and ten days for widows). No new contract is needed; sexual intercourse during the ʿidda itself constitutes revocation according to the Ḥanafites.
- An irrevocable repudiation short of three pronouncements: dissolves the marriage; remarriage requires a new contract.
- Three pronouncements (ṭalāq bāʾin kubrā): absolutely final; the couple cannot remarry unless the wife first marries another man in a genuine marriage (ḥallāl) and is subsequently widowed or divorced.

Ṭalāq may be pronounced conditionally ("if you leave the house, you are divorced") or through allusive language, but the latter requires niyya to be effective. A pronouncement under duress (ikrāh) and one made in extreme anger are contested — majority view is that a genuine inability to form intention may vitiate the pronouncement. Repudiation during the wife's menstrual period is reprehensible (ṭalāq bidʿī) because it prolongs the ʿidda unnecessarily, but most schools hold it effective.

### Divorce by Mutual Release (Khulʿ)

Khulʿ is the wife-initiated dissolution of marriage by payment of consideration (ʿiwaḍ) to the husband — typically the return of the dower. The juristic presumption is that khulʿ "is caused by the husband's misconduct," making it "reprehensible for him to receive any consideration" in such cases (Ḥanafite view). The majority hold that the consideration in khulʿ should not exceed the amount of the dower.

Five elements of khulʿ: (1) a husband or his agent; (2) a wife competent to transact and able to pay consideration; (3) an intact, unrepealed marriage; (4) consideration; (5) contractual language of offer and acceptance.

If the husband has coerced his wife into seeking khulʿ — including by intentional withholding of nafaqa — he is deprived of any remuneration.

### Oath of Sexual Abstinence (Īlāʾ)

A husband may swear to abstain from sexual intercourse with his wife for four months or more. If the period lapses without resumption of sexual relations, the oath has the force of a final ṭalāq. If he resumes intercourse within the period, he must perform penance (kaffāra) for causing his wife undue hardship.

### Oath of Ẓihār

A husband pronounces his wife sexually forbidden to him "as any intimate part of his mother's body." This does not dissolve the marriage but is reprehensible and requires penance (freeing a slave; or fasting two consecutive months; or feeding sixty poor persons for one day) before sexual relations may resume.

## Child Custody (Ḥaḍāna)

Mothers have an unqualified right to custody over minor children. Order of priority in the mother's absence: the mother's mother, the father's mother, the father's full sister, maternal aunts. A mother's remarriage to an ajnabī (non-relative) causes her to lose custody, but it is restored upon dissolution of that marriage.

**Age limits for custody:** Ḥanafite position — mothers retain boys until age seven or nine; Mālikites — until majority for boys, until marriage for girls; Ḥanbalites — first seven years for both. The father retains his paternal rights (he may not remove the children to another town) regardless of custody.

## Family Maintenance (Nafaqa)

Once the marriage is consummated, the husband owes his wife a duty of support (nafaqa) calibrated to her socio-economic status before marriage: quality of food, clothing, housing (separate from extended family and affording privacy), and servants. She is entitled to own the substance (ʿayn) of her food but only the usufruct of clothing and shelter. The wife is obliged neither to cook nor to clean; these are not "part of her contractual duties, for all she is obligated to fulfill is (the husband's right to) sexual enjoyment."

Failure to provide nafaqa entitles the wife to petition the qāḍī for an injunction to borrow against the husband's estate.

Maintenance extends to: divorced wives during their ʿidda (unless they were at fault), children (the father pays for wet nurses), parents and grandparents when in need, and by extension to other family members whose potential inheritance entitles them to support proportionate to their expected share.

Masters are legally obligated to provide nafaqa to their slaves; failure enables slaves to seek independent employment and temporarily extinguishes the master's claim to their services.

## Bequests (Waṣiyya)

Before the estate is distributed to heirs, three deductions are made in priority order: (a) funeral expenses; (b) outstanding debts; (c) bequests. The Sunnite schools hold the maxim "no bequest can be made to an heir" (lā waṣiyyata li-wārith), preventing augmentation of shares set by Quranic succession. The Twelver-Shīʿites permit bequests to heirs.

Maximum bequest: one-third of the estate after debts. If Quranic sharers are poor, the moral norm is to bequeath less than one-third. Bequests to non-Muslims are permitted by the Shāfiʿites; opposed by the Ḥanafites. A bequest for public works (mosques, hospitals, bridges) is valid.

A legatee convicted of the propositus's murder may still receive a bequest under the Shāfiʿite, Mālikite, and Twelver-Shīʿite views, though they are barred from inheriting through succession.

## Inheritance (Mīrāth / ʿIlm al-Farāʾiḍ)

Islamic inheritance law is among the most technically elaborate fields of classical jurisprudence. The Quran (primarily Q. 4:11–12 and 4:176) specifies fixed fractional shares for defined categories of heirs, with the remainder going to agnates (ʿaṣaba).

### Bars to Inheritance

Three universal bars: (1) homicide of the propositus; (2) difference in religion; (3) slavery. Some add apostasy. Non-Muslims cannot inherit from Muslims (and vice versa) by Prophetic ḥadīth reasoning: mutual inheritance rights would transfer property and strength to non-Muslim communities.

### Quranic Shares (Aṣḥāb al-Farāʾiḍ)

Fixed shares allocated by the Quran:
- **½**: sole daughter; husband with no children; full sister with no children
- **¼**: husband with children; wife without children
- **⅛**: wife with children
- **⅔**: two or more daughters
- **⅓**: mother without children; maternal half-siblings (two or more together)
- **⅙**: mother with children; father with children; son's daughter(s)

### Agnates (ʿAṣaba)

Males related to the propositus without a female link. Priority: (a) male descendants how low so ever; (b) male ascendants how high so ever; (c) brothers and sons of brothers; (d) paternal uncles; (e) mawlās (clients through patronage). Unless Quranic sharers exhaust the estate, agnates take the residue. Female heirs (daughter's son's daughter, etc.) acquire ʿaṣaba status through their brothers (ʿaṣaba bil-ghayr) or in conjunction with other females (ʿaṣaba maʿa al-ghayr).

### Mathematical Adjustments

- **ʿAwl** (proportionate reduction): when fixed Quranic shares aggregate to more than 1, each is reduced proportionately
- **Radd** (return): when Quranic sharers do not exhaust the estate, residue is returned to them in proportion to their shares. The Mālikites reject radd, sending residue to the Public Treasury (bayt al-māl).

Sunnite law does not acknowledge **right of representation**: the son of a predeceased son does not inherit the share his dead father would have received; the agnatic brother excludes the daughter's son entirely (in contrast to Twelver-Shīʿite law, which arranges heirs in generational classes that exclude lower classes, leading to dramatically different outcomes in contested cases).

### Twelver-Shīʿite Divergences

The Twelver-Shīʿite school entirely excludes agnates, distributing through three generational classes (parents + lineal descendants; grandparents + siblings + their descendants; paternal and maternal uncles + aunts). Nearer members exclude more distant ones within each class. Bequest rules also differ: Shīʿites give the testator complete freedom to allocate the one-third bequest to any heir.

## Historiography

Islamic family law is the most studied, and most politically charged, branch of [[sharia]] in the modern period. Classical scholarship by Schacht and Anderson established the standard Western account; late twentieth-century scholarship (Pearl and Menski, Powers, Tucker, Rapoport) has substantially revised understanding of how law was applied in practice — courts often departed from the strict doctrine on matters of dower, divorce, and custody. The relation between Quranic inheritance rules and pre-Islamic tribal agnatic succession is itself a subject of scholarly debate (Kimber; Powers, Studies).

Modern reform controversies center particularly on: (1) the husband's unilateral ṭalāq; (2) polygamy; (3) custody age-limits; (4) inheritance shares for daughters vs. sons. All post-colonial Muslim-majority states have addressed these issues through either codification of reformed sharia, secular family law codes, or dual systems.
