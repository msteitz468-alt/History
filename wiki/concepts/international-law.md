# International Law (the Law of Nations)

## Definition

**International law** — the older term is the **"law of nations"** — is, in Anthony
Clark Arend's chapter in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], "a set of rules that are
**binding** on international actors." Three elements: the rules are concrete (a
twelve-mile territorial sea, diplomatic immunity, the duty to honor treaties); they
are **binding** (unlike etiquette or morality, their enforcement is regarded as
legitimate); and they bind a widening range of **actors** — states above all, but also
intergovernmental organizations (the UN, [[world-health-organization|WHO]]),
supranational bodies (the EU), NGOs, corporations, sub-state groups, and even
individuals (pirates, war criminals).

Arend's framing caveat: the contemporary system is a **Western creation** — heir to
the European tradition that, "for good or ill," globalized along with the European
sovereign-state system. Other legal orders existed (Islamic law "served as
international law in large parts of the world for many centuries"), but the current
system descends from the Western line. This is a direct counterpoint to
[[interpolity-law]] (Benton & Clulow, in [[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]]), which
recovers the *messier, jurisdictionally plural* legal encounters of the early modern
world that the tidy Westphalian story obscures.

## From natural law to positivism

- **Before Westphalia — natural law.** Binding rules were grounded in **natural law**
  (the "law of nature") — eternal, universal norms discoverable by reason, traced from
  Stoic philosophy through Aquinas to the Scholastics **Vitoria** and **Suárez**, who
  asked whether the conquistadores were bound by any norms toward the non-Christian
  peoples of the Americas. **Hugo Grotius** (1625–35), writing amid the Thirty Years'
  War, was the first to articulate a law binding on **states as such** — created not
  only by nature but by their **mutual consent** for "the great society of states."
- **Westphalia (1648) and positivism.** The Peace of Westphalia recognized the
  **sovereign territorial state** with sharp borders as the primary actor — juridically
  equal and bound by **no higher law without its consent**. This consent-based view is
  **legal positivism**: positive law exists only where states have created it. Its
  classic statements: *The Antelope* (1825), where the US Supreme Court held the slave
  trade immoral (against natural law) but **not illegal** because states still consented
  to it; and *The Lotus* (1927) — "restrictions upon the independence of States cannot
  be presumed."

## The anarchic, self-help system

A hallmark of the Westphalian order was the **absence of centralized enforcement** —
the system later theorists called "anarchic," with "no common power" (Hobbes). Law was
enforced by **self-help**, notably the **reprisal**: an act that would otherwise be
unlawful but is permitted in response to a prior illegal act, up to and including
military force.

## Sources: treaties and custom

Positivism recognizes two ways states consent to create law:

- **Treaties** (bilateral, or multilateral "conventions" — the Genocide Convention,
  the conventions on human rights and the rights of the child).
- **Custom** — a state practice that, becoming near-universal *and* accompanied by a
  belief that it is obligatory (*opinio juris*), "ripens" into binding law (the
  *Paquete Habana* test).

Arend's chapter traces the trajectory from this state-centric core toward the
**twentieth-century expansion** of international institutions and law — the League of
Nations and the United Nations, human-rights and humanitarian law, and the
proliferation of non-state actors — partially qualifying pure Westphalian sovereignty.

## Limitations and critiques

- **Eurocentrism:** the global system universalized a Western tradition, often at the
  expense of Islamic and other legal orders (cf. [[interpolity-law]]).
- **Enforcement:** the persistent gap between binding norms and the absence of reliable
  enforcement fuels skepticism about whether international law is "really" law.
- **Sovereignty vs. universal norms:** the unresolved tension between consent-based
  positivism and natural-law/human-rights claims that bind states *regardless* of
  consent (e.g. the prohibition of [[genocide]]).
- **The missing law of victory (Whitman):**
  [[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012|Whitman (2012)]] argues the modern law of war —
  jus ad bellum, jus in bello, international criminal law, an emerging jus post
  bellum — has *no* functioning [[jus-victoriae]]: no law determining who won a
  war or what the victor may claim, only the "very poor legal doctrine" of
  unconditional surrender (a WWII-era U.S. State Department invention). On his
  account this moral maximalism makes wars harder to *end*: "Good law makes
  peace... We need a jus victoriae to... permit a brokered peace." He also reads
  the standard triumphal historiography of humanitarian law as tragedy — wars
  have grown worse, not better, since the old law of victory died
  ([[death-of-pitched-battle]]) — and notes the anachronism of asking only
  "were the humanitarian rules obeyed?" of premodern warfare, which understood
  war as a settlement procedure ([[verdict-of-battle]]).
