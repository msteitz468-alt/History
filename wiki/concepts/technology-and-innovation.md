## Definition and Scope

Daniel Headrick (Ch. 7) defines technology as "the use of materials, energy, and
living beings for practical purposes." While technologies can be identified in
many non-human species, humans are so prolific at inventing technologies — and
technological innovation has given the species such power over its surroundings
and over each other — that technological innovation counts as a defining feature
of humanity and a fundamental driver of human history.

The central historical question Headrick poses: if technological innovation is a
powerful driver of change, **what drives technological innovation?** Marx's
famous formulation (in *The Poverty of Philosophy*, 1847) — "The windmill gives
you society with the feudal lord; the steam-mill, society with the industrial
capitalist" — raised this question and the danger of technological determinism.

## Key Analytical Distinctions

- **Technology as driver vs. technology as product**: Does technology drive
  historical change, or is it itself driven by social, economic, and cultural
  forces? The mature field rejects simple technological determinism in favor of
  studying the social roots of innovation.
- **"Whiggish" history of technology**: The 19th-century popular view assumed
  that technological/scientific change represented progress and that technological
  determinism drove history. Modern scholarship is less Whiggish — more interested
  in technological *failures*, in cultural diffusion, and less in the national
  origins of particular technologies.
- **Innovation vs. diffusion**: Global studies of particular technologies (e.g.,
  firearms) demonstrate the extent to which innovation depended on — or was limited
  by — cultural diffusion rather than independent invention.

## Historiographical Development

- **Enlightenment origins**: The *Encyclopédie* (1751–72). The first serious
  scholarly treatment was J.H.M. von Poppe's *History of all the Inventions and
  Discoveries* (1837). Marx and others took up technology as a driver of change.
- **19th-century popular interest**: Great exhibitions (from the 1844 French
  industrial exposition), science/technology museums, *Scientific American* (1845).
  This interest was "Whiggish" and nationalistic (disputes over whether photography
  was invented by a Briton or Frenchman, flight by a Frenchman or the Wrights).
- **Early 20th century**: George Sarton's multi-volume history of science (notably
  broad, highlighting Islamic science; founded the journal *Isis*, 1927). Classic
  studies by Lewis Mumford and Abbott Payson Usher.
- **Post-WWII maturation**: The Society for the History of Technology (SHOT); the
  field became less Whiggish, more global, more interested in failure. The
  International Committee for the History of Technology (1968).
- **Joseph Needham**: His monumental work on Chinese science and technology
  (possibly the most important scholarship in the field in the 20th century)
  raised the possibility that China was the world's technological leader until
  the 15th century — a direct challenge to [[eurocentrism]].
- **Vaclav Smil, Leslie White, Ruth Schwartz Cowan**: Energy-focused, anthro-
  pological, and social/gender approaches. Cowan showed how labor-saving
  technologies could *increase* the burden of domestic labor.

## Relation to Fire and Energy

Technology in human history is intimately tied to energy capture. The control of
fire is arguably the first technological threshold (see [[fire-management]]).
Fire technologies drove all three of the "great technological revolutions"
identified in CWH Vol. I:
1. The control and use of fire (Paleolithic) — see [[fire-management]]
2. Agrarianization / the Neolithic Revolution — see [[neolithic]]
3. Industrialization (fossil fuels — Sieferle's "subterranean forest")

This energy-centric framing connects the history of technology to [[big-history]].

## Technology in the Middle Millennium (CWH V)

[[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]] (Dagmar Schäfer & Marcus Popplow, Ch 12) reframes
technology for 500–1500 against the older model of **sudden breakthroughs**. Their
thesis: change "actually occurred continuously," as people invented and adapted
tools, machines, and processes "deliberately to address new environmental or other
circumstances, and also playfully or because of changing tastes." Key points:

- **Artisans, not inventors, are the base.** State or elite interests often acted as
  *catalysts*, but "artisanal experts and everyday practitioners held the basis of
  technical knowledge and stabilized its circulation." Motivations and modes of
  transmission were manifold.
- **No culture was hostile to technology.** Innovations served both **reform and the
  continuation of traditions** — there was no general pre-modern technophobia.
- **Resource-saving, not machine-fancying.** Workshop owners and officials favored
  "simple and resource-saving solutions" (the Near Eastern *qanat*; intensive-labor
  cotton and silk production in India and China relying on "a multitude of minor
  organizational and managerial innovations") rather than complex machinery — a
  contrast with the later European mechanization that drove industrialization.
- **Asia first.** Gunpowder, printing, and the compass were used in Asia for
  centuries before reaching Europe. **Woodblock printing**, emerging in seventh-century
  China through Buddhist proselytization, caused major social and political change in
  China *earlier* than print did in Europe — reshaping attitudes to learning,
  religion, history, and "things (*wu*)."

This complements the "theoretical" knowledge traced in
[[science-transmission|the transmission of science and philosophy]] (Burnett, Ch 13):
technology spread chiefly through *practice* and adaptation, science through *texts*
and translators — both along the [[afro-eurasian-world-system|world system's]] webs.

## Wages, Machinery, and the Great Divergence (CWH VI)

[[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]] (Sugihara & Wong, after Robert Allen) sharpens
Schäfer & Popplow's "resource-saving versus machine-fancying" contrast into a
causal argument about industrialization. Why did **labor-saving machinery** emerge
in England rather than in the equally commercialized, equally "industrious"
Yangzi Delta? Allen's answer is **factor prices**: England was a **high-wage
economy**, which made it profitable to substitute machines for expensive labor,
whereas low-wage economies (China, and even the high-wage but differently
structured Netherlands) lacked that incentive and continued the
[[industrious-revolution|labor-intensive intensification]] that Schäfer & Popplow
describe for the Middle Millennium. Technology here is neither autonomous driver
nor mere product but a **response to economic incentives** — the technological
hinge of the [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]], and a direct rebuke to
technological-determinist accounts of the rise of the West.

## Industrial Technology, 1750–present (CWH VII)

[[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]] (Paul Josephson, Ch 5) carries the history of
technology into the industrial and modern eras. Josephson's organizing claims:

- **Not one revolution but a series.** The Industrial Revolution was "a series of
  revolutions in production, power generation, and distribution," beginning with
  textiles, iron and steel, and coal mining (c. 1770–1900), then continually renewed
  by new prime movers — steam, then electricity, the internal combustion engine,
  hydroelectricity, and nuclear power.
- **Systems, not just machines.** Modern technology is increasingly **large-scale
  systems** — business, financial, construction, and state institutions, plus armies
  of routinized, **de-skilled** labor. The **American system** of interchangeable
  parts (born in federal armories) led to **Fordist** assembly-line mass production
  and the de-skilling of the workforce.
- **Exploitative across regimes.** Josephson stresses that large-scale technological
  systems "will nearly always be exploitative no matter the political and economic
  system" — Gary, Indiana and Stalin's Magnitogorsk alike. This holds across
  capitalist, socialist, and fascist states (cf. [[communism-and-fascism]]).
- **Science–industry fusion.** The growing, formalized bond between **industrial R&D**
  and corporate/state laboratories — exemplified by the German chemical industry
  (Perkin's coal-tar dyes 1856 → BASF → I. G. Farben, which supplied Zyklon B for the
  Holocaust) — drove the synthesis of dyes, **plastics**, and pharmaceuticals.
- **Mega-engineering and its costs.** River engineering and giant dams (the
  Mississippi works, Aswan, Three Gorges — which ousted 1.3 million people) spread
  through a transnational engineering community, repeatedly destroying ecosystems and
  displacing populations — a technological face of the [[anthropocene|Anthropocene]].
- **Critics of technology** run through the period: the **Luddites**; Marx and Engels
  (who criticized technology's *concentration as capital*, not technology per se, and
  were themselves determinists who saw it as the engine of history); technological
  determinists (Ellul); and the "small is beautiful" alternative-technology current
  (Schumacher) that fed modern [[environmentalism]].

This deepens the wiki's account of technology as both driver and product: Josephson
shows modern technology as a **socio-technical system** whose scale, not merely whose
artifacts, shapes labor, environment, and politics — and connects it to
[[industrialization]], the [[fossil-fuel-energy-transition]], and the
[[great-acceleration]].

## Emic vs. Etic

"Technology" as an analytical category and "the history of technology" as a field
are etic and modern. But tool-making and innovation are emic activities present
throughout human history, deep into the Paleolithic.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Technological determinism**: the persistent temptation to treat technology as
  an autonomous driver, ignoring the social forces that shape which technologies
  are developed and adopted.
- **Nationalism**: much early scholarship was distorted by claims to national
  priority in invention.
- **Eurocentrism**: the field long underestimated non-European (especially
  Chinese and Islamic) technological achievement until Needham, Sarton, and others.
