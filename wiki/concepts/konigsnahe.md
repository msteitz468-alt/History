# Königsnähe

**Königsnähe** ("nearness to the king") is the analytical category for royal or imperial favour as a distinct, negotiable criterion of aristocratic status and power, especially visible at the margins where ancestry or large-scale landowning were absent or insufficient. It is transactional: ambitious men without traditional resources succeeded (or failed) through negotiating skills, military success, and luck with kings and other aristocrats. In Merovingian Francia, kings were the direct sources of all political patronage and lavish gifts; being a *conviva regis* (royal table-companion) was the quintessence of Königsnähe in all periods.

## Origin

Wickham draws on German historiographical usage for the early medieval period and makes it one of his six interdependent criteria for the ideal-type aristocrat (alongside ancestry, landed wealth, official hierarchy, peer recognition, and lifestyle). The term highlights how fluid and performance-based aristocratic identity remained before the late Middle Ages.

## Applies to periods and regions

Primarily Late Antiquity and Early Middle Ages in Gaul/Francia and related successor kingdoms. Less central where tax-based or strongly landed aristocracies made royal proximity one among many stable resources (e.g. late Roman East or Byzantine thematic hierarchies).

## Application in Wickham

At the margins of aristocratic recruitment, Königsnähe was decisive. In post-Roman Gaul the politics of land (royal gifts, confiscations, family *proprium* vs *fiscus*) made proximity to the king the fastest route to landed power. The military world was king-centred because kings controlled patronage. This pattern persisted into the Carolingian period and helps explain the speed with which new men could rise (and fall) in Frankish politics. It also explains why "being a *conviva regis*" carried such weight in narrative sources.

## Historiography

The concept is standard in Merovingian and Carolingian studies (German scholarship especially). Wickham uses it to underscore fluidity and the transactional nature of power in land-based polities, against romantic or overly institutional readings of early medieval "nobility." It fits the broader materialist emphasis on how surplus and patronage actually operated.
