## Definition and Scope

Islamic theological ethics concerns the question of whether moral categories — good (*hasan*) and evil (*qabih*) — are objective features of acts discoverable by unaided human reason, or whether they are entirely constituted by God's command. This dispute is not merely academic: it determines whether the obligation to engage in theological reasoning (*nazar*) can be established prior to revelation, and thus whether someone who has never received a prophet's message can be held morally responsible.

The two principal positions in classical *kalam* are:

- **Mu'tazilite ethical realism** (or *tahsin wa-taqbih al-'aqli*): good and evil are intrinsic, objective qualities of acts, knowable by reason independent of revelation. Wrongdoing is evil because it is a form of wrongdoing, and wrongdoing is intrinsically evil.
- **Ash'arite divine command theory** (or *voluntarism*): moral value is constituted solely by God's command. There are no intrinsic moral facts prior to revelation; duties exist only when religion has been established through prophecy.

## The Mu'tazilite Position

Mu'tazilites grounded their ethical realism in the claim that reason can identify harm and benefit, and that a rational agent is obligated to avoid unjustified harm. Al-Malahimi (d. 1141), a later Basran Mu'tazilite, advanced two arguments for why theological reflection (*nazar*) is obligatory even without revelation:

1. **The fear argument**: A sensible person, encountering evidence that the world has an intelligent Maker, will experience eschatological fear. Since it is obligatory to avoid unjustified harm one expects to befall oneself, one is obligated to investigate whether the feared punishment is real.
2. **The duty-enabling argument**: Knowing God's existence is itself a duty (because this knowledge motivates moral conduct); reflection is necessary to attain this knowledge; what is necessary for fulfilling a duty thereby becomes a duty.

Crucially, for Mu'tazilites, theological reflection is "neither intrinsically obligatory, nor an end in itself" — its obligation derives from prior ethical duties established by reason alone. This is self-consciously pre-revelatory: the entire structure must hold without appeal to scripture, since accepting scripture already presupposes belief in God. ([[winter-cambridge-companion-islamic-theology-2008]], ch. 10)

The Mu'tazilites further insisted that this duty to reflect was the "most primary (*awwal*) duty of all" — a claim that generated internal tensions, since the foregoing arguments establish it as conditional and derivative rather than primary.

## The Ash'arite Position

Early Ash'arites accepted that theological reflection is a duty, but held it to be a *religious* (*shar'i*) rather than a rational duty. Duties, on this account, are engendered exclusively by revealed religion; unaided reason cannot generate moral obligations. Al-Juwayni (d. 1085) articulated this view: one who has never encountered any revealed religion has no absolute obligation to reflect or to do good. Only when prophecy has been established does knowing God become obligatory on those who receive the message.

The Ash'arites deflected the Mu'tazilite objection that a non-believer can simply refuse to investigate by arguing that a prophet's performance of miracles provides sufficient motivation for a sensible person to take the prophet's claims seriously — even without prior acceptance of God's existence. "The truthfulness of prophecy does not depend on reflection, but on miracles."

Ash'arites also turned an argument back on the Mu'tazilites: since the Mu'tazilites do not treat the duty to reflect as immediately known, they face the regress that a non-believer will know reflection is obligatory only once he reflects — meaning he can simply refuse the first move.

Later Ash'arism, especially under the influence of Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 1210), departed from early divine command ethics in favour of a **subjectivist consequentialism**: a rational person who hears of possible afterlife punishment will find it *prudentially* necessary to investigate. This is explicitly anti-Mu'tazilite (rejecting objectivist ethics) and also anti-early-Ash'arite (rejecting purely religious obligation), arriving instead at a subjective, prudential account of theological duty.

## Relationship to Revelation and Law

The ethics/revelation nexus was noted by Yahya Michot (ch. 9 of the same volume): "it is relative to religion, not metaphysics, and thus beyond His seignioriality, that God's godhead can properly be investigated." Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) drew the distinction between God as Lord (*rabb*) — the creative-ontological relation — and God as deity (*ilah*) — the relation constituted by worship, love, and command. Revelation, on this view, introduces moral distinctions into created reality; without it, there are no commands and thus (for Ash'arites) no duties.

This means the Ash'arite position formally makes the existence of obligation dependent on prophethood — creating a tight structural link between ethics, revelation, and the argument for God's existence. The Mu'tazilite position reverses this dependency, making reason the foundation on which the obligation to seek God rests.

## The Ibn 'Arabi Strand

A third position, associated with Muhyi al-Din Ibn 'Arabi (cited in Chittick, *Sufi Path of Knowledge*, p. 208), dissolves the dispute by locating moral knowledge in mystical illumination rather than in either rational analysis or textual command. For Ibn 'Arabi, the sacred law (*sharia*) contains depths accessible only to those who have achieved the extinction of ego (*fana'*), not to rationalists working from first principles.

## Historiography

The definitive modern study of the rationalism/voluntarism axis in Islamic ethics is George F. Hourani, *Reason and Tradition in Islamic Ethics* (Cambridge, 1985). A. Kevin Reinhart, *Before Revelation: The Boundaries of Muslim Moral Thought* (Albany, 1995) investigates how far reason was held to bind in the pre-revelatory condition. The Ash'arite side is illuminated by Sherman Jackson, "The alchemy of domination? Some Ash'arite responses to Mu'tazilite ethics", *IJMES* 31 (1999). Fakhr al-Din al-Razi's shift from divine command ethics to teleological consequentialism is traced in Ayman Shihadeh, *The Teleological Ethics of Fakhr al-Din al-Razi* (Leiden, 2006).

The dispute maps imperfectly onto Western meta-ethical categories. "Ethical realism" in the Mu'tazilite sense is not the same as secular moral realism: it holds that reason recognises moral facts that God also recognises and that God would be acting wrongly to violate. The Ash'arite position is not simple voluntarism either, since later thinkers like al-Razi recast it in prudentialist terms.
