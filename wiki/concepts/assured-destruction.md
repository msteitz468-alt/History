# Assured Destruction

**Assured destruction** (often **mutual assured destruction** or MAD) was the declaratory and planning concept associated with US Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara in the mid-to-late 1960s. It held that stable deterrence rested on each side possessing the certain capability to inflict "unacceptable damage" on the other even after absorbing a first strike.

McNamara's working definition of unacceptable damage was roughly the destruction of 25% of the opponent's population and 50% of its industrial capacity. Beyond that threshold, additional forces produced diminishing returns ("all you are going to do is make the rubble bounce").

## Logic and Implications

- **Second-strike emphasis.** The force structure priority shifted toward survivable, relatively invulnerable retaliatory forces (Polaris/Poseidon SLBMs, Minuteman in hardened silos, later bombers on alert or airborne). Accurate, vulnerable, or "first-strike" systems were de-emphasized in declaratory policy.
- **City-avoidance vs. retreat.** Early McNamara thinking explored "city-avoidance" or "no-cities" targeting (counterforce strikes against military targets while sparing population centres to preserve bargaining leverage and limit damage). This was abandoned in favor of a clearer assured-destruction posture as Soviet capabilities grew and as the technical and political difficulties of controlled counterforce campaigns became apparent.
- **Action-reaction phenomenon.** McNamara and analysts argued that each side's defensive or counterforce efforts (ABM systems, MIRVs for hard-target kill) simply stimulated the other to build more or better offensive forces, producing an action-reaction arms race dynamic. Arms control (SALT) was pursued in part to dampen this.

Pre-MAD thinking in the late 1950s already pointed toward mutual destruction as the likely outcome. The Killian Report anticipated that "Eventually the two super-powers would reach a position where ‘attack by either side would result in mutual destruction’. A surprise attack would produce no dividends" because "each country will have the residual offensive power to break through the defenses of the other country and destroy it regardless of whether the other country strikes first." In the 1960s "a state of mutual assured destruction came to be considered reassuringly stable."

The Gaither Report shared anxiety over SAC vulnerability but saw the balance tending toward "an ‘extremely unstable equilibrium’" due to the offence-defence race. Neither report believed the first strike need be critical "if appropriate measures were taken in time."

## Soviet and Other Views

The Soviets never fully adopted the American language of assured destruction as a stable end-state. Their doctrine retained a stronger war-fighting and damage-limitation element even while accepting the reality of mutual vulnerability. They invested heavily in civil defence, hard-target capabilities, and (at times) ABM systems around Moscow. Soviet military writers continued to stress the destruction of the enemy's armed forces and war-making potential rather than pure population/industrial punishment.

Early formalizations of stable stalemate emphasized invulnerable retaliatory forces. Oskar Morgenstern stated the principle: "‘In order to create a nuclear stalemate under conditions of nuclear plenty it is necessary for both sides to possess invulnerable retaliatory forces.’" Amster and Sherwin (1954/1956) argued that with long-range missiles "forces are very costly to attack, and cities are very cheap to attack, the optimum force will not be very large." A force of invulnerable submarines "could guarantee deterrence."

French and British independent deterrents were explicitly "second-best" minimum deterrents aimed at the "Moscow criterion" or "dissuasion pure et dure" — enough to threaten unacceptable damage to the Soviet heartland even if the superpowers had already devastated each other.

## Critiques (Grounded in Freedman 1981, Range 08)

- **Moral.** Critics (including some within the strategic community) charged that planning for the deliberate slaughter of tens of millions of civilians was morally obscene and strategically bankrupt. Fred Ikle in Foreign Affairs (1970s): "The jargon of American strategic analysis works like a narcotic. It dulls our sense of moral outrage about the tragic confrontation of nuclear arsenals, primed and constantly perfected to unleash widespread genocide. It fosters the current smug complacency regarding the soundness and stability of mutual deterrence. It blinds us to the fact that our method for preventing nuclear war rests on a form of warfare universally condemned since the Dark Ages - the mass killing of hostages." Ikle noted the threat was not credible for scenarios short of massive surprise attack and neglected "lunacy and recklessness at the top" or "fatal accident or unauthorized act". (Freedman 1981, THE CONSENSUS UNDERMINED / "The Shame of Assured Destruction").
- **Mutual assured vulnerability.** Donald Brennan: mutual assured destruction meant mutual assured vulnerability. "We should rather prefer live Americans to dead Russians, and we should not choose deliberately to live forever under a nuclear sword of Damocles." Deterrence could be sustained even with damage-limiting capacities if both sides faced equivalent damage. (Freedman 1981, p.348-349).
- **Instability.** Pure MAD left no usable options if deterrence "failed" at any level short of all-out spasm. It appeared to many as a doctrine of despair rather than a strategy. Wohlstetter series attacked measuring adequacy "in terms of the number of civilians they can kill" and "making 'murder respectable' in such chaste phrases as 'counter-value attacks'". (Freedman 1981, p.347-348).
- **Technological erosion.** Improvements in accuracy, MIRVs, and depressed-trajectory SLBMs began to undermine the invulnerability assumptions that made assured destruction "assured." Herbert York and Wolfgang Panofsky viewed the mutual-hostage relationship as a "tension of modern life" or "physical fact" "grossly insensitive to any change in strategic policy". Reductions would not end it, only limit the assured deaths. (Freedman 1981, p.350-351).
- **Political science failure.** By late 1970s the liberal consensus around pure assured destruction was eroding. The 1969 ABM debate and Soviet build-up confirmed that "the principles of Mutual Assured Destruction and the associated views on the dynamics of the arms race" had gained "canonical status" more through politicization of procurement than intellectual compulsion. (Freedman 1981, MILITARY-INDUSTRIAL COMPLEXITIES, p.340).

By the 1970s the consensus around pure assured destruction was eroding, leading to the Schlesinger Doctrine's search for selective, limited nuclear options. Freedman concludes that no operational nuclear strategy has yet been devised that does not carry enormous risk of degenerating into uncontrolled escalation or city exchanges.
