# The Shugo and the Rise of the Daimyo

The ***shugo*** (守護, "protector / constable") — the provincial military governor of the
[[shogunate-bakufu|bakufu]] — is the office through which warrior authority climbed from
estate-level stewardship to **regional territorial rule**, producing the **daimyo** who
would dominate [[muromachi-period|Muromachi]] and Warring-States [[japan|Japan]]. Its
trajectory is the institutional spine of the period: a [[minamoto-no-yoritomo|Kamakura
appointee]] charged with policing and mustering the vassals of a province slowly became,
under the Ashikaga, a quasi-sovereign provincial lord. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v3-medieval|Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 3]] (John W. Hall
on the Muromachi bakufu; Imatani Akira on shugo and kokujin; Nagahara Keiji on the shōen's
decline).

## The Kamakura shugo

[[minamoto-no-yoritomo|Yoritomo]] created the shugo in 1185 as a **limited police and
military office** — one per province, charged with mustering the bakufu's vassals
(gokenin), keeping the peace, and pursuing rebels (the "great criminal matters"). The
Kamakura shugo had **no general fiscal or proprietary authority** over the province's land;
that lay with the [[medieval-shoen|shōen]] proprietors and the provincial governors. The
office was supervisory, not territorial.

## The Muromachi transformation

Under the Ashikaga the shugo accumulated the powers that turned office into domain. John W.
Hall's chapter **rehabilitates the [[muromachi-period|Muromachi bakufu]]** against the old
"weak shogunate" view: under **Yoshimitsu** and **Yoshinori** a military government for the
first time held *all* aspects of secular authority, and — crucially — Hall argues that
growing shugo power was **not inherently a threat to the bakufu**: so long as shugo and
shogun "worked together in a context that admitted the shogun's primacy," strong shugo
*advanced* bakufu rule. Shogunal control was reinforced by the **kanrei** (deputy-shogun)
post rotating among three great shugo houses, by the practice of **shugo residence in
Kyoto**, and by military action against recalcitrant constables.

Imatani Akira details the powers that made the shugo a **regional hegemon**:

- ***Hanzei*** — the right to levy **half the proprietary dues** of the [[medieval-shoen|shōen]]
  in the province, which let shugo siphon off and ultimately **absorb** estate income to
  support their own vassals;
- ***tansen*** — a **provincewide land tax**, extending the shugo's reach over public and
  private land alike;
- expanded authority to **enforce land-dispute judgments**; and
- the increasingly **hereditary** character of the post.

Together these built an economic and military base independent of the estates — the
**shugo-daimyo** (shugo who had become territorial lords) and, beyond them, the **shugo
domain (*shugo-ryōgoku*) system**.

## The failure of the periphery

Imatani stresses that the bakufu could not hold its edges. The **Kantō *kubō*** (a regional
deputy, given to an Ashikaga branch family) and the **Kyūshū *tandai*** became sources of
rebellion rather than control; their failure drove the **decentralization of the periphery**
and helped produce the period's chronic warfare.

## Shugo vs. kokujin: a historiographical dispute

`contested:` Scholars debate who was the *central figure* of the late-medieval order. **Nagahara
Keiji** sees the **shugo** as the axis that built the shugo-domain system; **Kurokawa
Naonori** instead foregrounds the **kokujin** (*kokujin-ryōshu*, the local overlords —
many of them former [[medieval-shoen|jitō]] grown into holders of contiguous local power
bases). Imatani concludes that it was the **shugo who exerted regional power** who drove the
changes of the late-fifteenth and early-sixteenth centuries.

## From shugo-daimyo to sengoku daimyo

The [[muromachi-period|Ōnin War (1467–77)]] shattered the system from above: many shugo
houses, absent in Kyoto, lost their provinces to their own deputies and kokujin vassals in
the *gekokujō* ("the low overturning the high"). The territorial lords who emerged from the
wreckage — the **sengoku daimyo** — ruled compact, defensible domains by their own house
law, no longer needing a bakufu commission. This is the political form that
[[oda-nobunaga|Nobunaga]], [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Hideyoshi]], and [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]]
would consolidate into the early-modern *baku-han* order.
