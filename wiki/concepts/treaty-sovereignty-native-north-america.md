# Treaty Sovereignty in Native North America

## Definition

The practice whereby European powers and the United States conducted relations with Native nations through **treaties**—acts of sovereigns—thereby recognizing a form of Native sovereignty even as demographic and military power shifted. Michael D. Green (CHNPA I.1 ch. 8): “Treaties are the acts of sovereigns. By engaging in treaty relations with Native nations, Europeans recognized the actuality of sovereignty even as they frequently rejected its legality.” **Autonomy** (self-government free from outside control) is central to sovereignty. When the U.S. embraced the treaty system it learned it must also extend recognition of sovereignty that underlay colonial treaties.

Green’s conclusion: treaties, “frequently extorted, often broken,” nevertheless “became the spine on which the body of relations grew.” Until **1871**, assumption that Native nations could be expected to behave as sovereigns influenced federal policy and Native histories. Congress then formally denied Native nation sovereignty as prerequisite to final dismemberment of political institutions and dispossession of lands.

## Dual Character

- **Recognition:** Treaties presuppose corporate Native polities capable of binding agreement; nation-to-nation business elevates Indians above states in Knox’s framing.
- **Instrument of dispossession:** Cession treaties—dictated right-of-conquest instruments, unrepresentative signatures, bribed chiefs, unauthorized Treaty Parties—were the principal legal tool of land transfer east of the Mississippi.

## Constitutional and statutory architecture

- **Articles of Confederation:** Congress “sole and exclusive right of … managing all affairs with the Indians,” modified by “not members of any of the states”—ambiguity inviting state interference.
- **Constitution (1787):** Indians appear only excluded from tax levies and population counts, or (with states and foreign countries) subject to Congressional commerce regulation. Western peoples treated as foreigners, nations not subject to U.S. laws except diplomatically/militarily.
- **Art. I Sec. 10:** “No State shall enter into any Treaty…”—Knox used this to strip states of pretence to legitimate Indian treaties (after Georgia’s right-of-conquest Creek treaties helped spark southern war).
- **1790 Senate:** Indian treaties handled like European treaties. Implication: parties are sovereign. Knox: independent nations/tribes “ought to be considered as foreign nations, not as the subjects of any particular State.”
- **Trade and Intercourse Acts:** Relations mechanism remains treaty; Indians outside ordinary legislative jurisdiction.
- **~400 Senate-ratified treaties to 1871** established legal/judicial precedent that continues to recognize Indian nation sovereignty.

## Policy sequence (Green)

1. **1780s conquered-nations policy:** Alliances with defeated Britain allegedly forfeited land rights within U.S. boundaries; dictated Fort Stanwix 1784, Fort McIntosh 1785, Fort Finney 1786; Hopewell 1785–86 south of Ohio recognized borders but asserted U.S. sovereignty in place of England’s. Northwest Ordinance 1787 “utmost good faith” pledge repudiated pure right of conquest.
2. **Treaty of New York 1790 (Creek):** Peace; U.S. “protection”; three Georgia right-of-conquest treaties treated as invalid; partial cession of lands already occupied by Georgians; federal monopoly of Indian affairs under Constitution; civilization implements—template of post-conquest purchase diplomacy.
3. **[[treaty-of-greenville-1795]]:** Cession of most of Ohio; U.S. recognized rights to unceded lands (another repudiation of right of conquest) and paid money/goods/services.
4. **Post-1815 challenge:** [[jackson-andrew]] (1817): reject sovereignty, stop “absurd” treaty system, legislate boundaries—Indians subjects not sovereigns. Calhoun (1818): “Our views of their interest, and not their own, ought to govern them.” Monroe: to civilize them and prevent extinction, “their independence as communities should cease.” **Congress unresponsive**—continued sovereignty and treaties.
5. **Removal era:** State jurisdiction laws + Jackson’s non-enforcement of protective treaty provisions converted “voluntary” treaty removal into coerced expulsion; Treaty of [[new-echota]] ratified despite unauthorized signers.
6. **Marshall Court:** *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831)—“domestic dependent nation,” ward–guardian analogy; not a foreign nation for original jurisdiction. **[[worcester-v-georgia-1832]]**—Georgia jurisdiction in Cherokee Nation null and void; Cherokee retained sovereignty outside enumerated surrenders (foreign relations, trade with Americans); unenforced.
7. **1871:** Congress ends treaty system; express denial of Native nation sovereignty.

## Native articulations (examples from Green)

- **Alexander McGillivray** (Creek, letter 10 July 1785 to Spanish West Florida): Peace of Paris could not impair Creek territorial rights—Indians not parties; Britain never possessed Creek territories by cession, purchase, or conquest; irreducible minimum: right of “a free Nation” to protect inheritance “Since the beginning of time.”
- **Western alliance (1793 Detroit talks):** “Money, to us, is of no value, and … no consideration whatever can induce us to sell our lands”—proposed dividing U.S. offer among poor settlers instead.
- **[[tecumseh]]:** God gave land to all Indians; no small group of unauthorized treaty chiefs can sell (same pan-Indian stand as 1780s alliance).
- **Creek National Council:** Law forbidding national-domain sale without full Council—basis for executing William McIntosh after Indian Springs 1825 (treason-like national law).
- **John Ross / Cherokee Nationals:** Petition that virtually entire population opposed New Echota; signers lacked authority—Senate ratified anyway.

## Contestation

**Contested: yes.** Legal historians debate how far “domestic dependent nation” qualifies or preserves sovereignty; political historians dispute whether treaty form was primarily recognition or primarily a land-title machine; ethnohistorians stress Native ceremonial and council protocols that U.S. commissioners often ignored. Green holds that recognition “so fully permeated relations” that Congress felt constrained to deny it formally in 1871. Atrocity and coercion under treaty forms (stockades after New Echota; Choctaw Eaton threat of war; Fort Gibson Seminole document) are part of the same record—not external to “sovereignty.”

## Plains treaty practice and post-1871 assault (CHNPA I.2)

### Intermediaries and “touching the pen” (Fowler ch. 9)
Annexations (Texas 1845, Oregon 1846, Mexican War lands 1848) and California gold drove trails through Arapaho/Cheyenne/Teton and Comanche/Kiowa hunting grounds. Major councils: **1851 Fort Laramie** (Arapahos, Cheyennes, Tetons, Crows, Assiniboines, etc.); **1853 Fort Atkinson** (Kiowas, Comanches, Kiowa-Apaches); **1855 Judith River** (some Siksikas, some Bloods, most Piegans, Gros Ventres)—peace, defined “tribal” territories (often unrealistically), and supply issues for game destruction. Councils mirrored Native trade ritual; “touching the pen” later enabled fraudulent insert/omit of provisions. U.S. expected few leaders to speak for all; Teton many autonomous subdivisions made selecting few representatives hard, so government tried to influence intermediary selection.

### *Lone Wolf* and unconsented disposals (Hoxie ch. 12)
After 1871 ended new treaties, older promises remained litigation fodder. In *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* (1903) the Supreme Court declared that “the power exists to abrogate the provisions of an Indian treaty.” The Commissioner of Indian Affairs immediately reported that Lone Wolf “will enable you to dispose of land without the consent of the Indians”—doctrine that enabled: [[allotment-policy]] surplus sales and related disposals. Treaty-based claims nonetheless structured pan-Indian politics (Black Hills Council 1911; NCAI treaty-rights language; Sioux preference for land return over cash).

### Twentieth-century treaty renaissance (Washburn ch. 15)
From c. 1960, courts increasingly held that nineteenth-century treaty rights must be respected by twentieth-century institutions. Landmark: *Washington et al. v. Washington State Commercial Passenger Fishing Vessel Assn.* (1979) upheld that 1854–55 treaty phrase “right of taking fish … in common with all citizens of the Territory” meant present-day Indians and non-Indians share the take **equally**. Canadian path differed: government never comfortable with “aboriginal title” as phrase; 1969 White Paper sought to abolish treaty special status (withdrawn 1971); Constitution Act 1982 later affirmed existing aboriginal and treaty rights (contrasts_with US treaty-suite while concurrent_with [[termination-policy]] Canadian analogue). contrasts_with Canadian aboriginal-title unease; concurrent_with [[northwest-native-history-to-1880s]] fishing rights; part_of later [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]] legal layer.
