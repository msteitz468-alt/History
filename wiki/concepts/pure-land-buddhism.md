# Pure Land Buddhism (Heian Japan)

Pure Land Buddhism (*Jodo-kyo*) — the devotional tradition centered on faith in Amida Buddha (Sanskrit: Amitabha or Amitayus) and aspiration to rebirth in his Pure Land (*Gokuraku*, "Paradise") — developed from an adjunct Tendai practice into a major religious force during the [[heian-period|Heian period]], laying the foundation for the independent Jodo, Jodo Shin, and Ji schools of the Kamakura era. Its Heian trajectory moved from monastic contemplative practice (Ennin's *nembutsu* on [[mount-hiei|Mount Hiei]]) through aristocratic lay societies, to popularization among common people through wandering *hijiri* (holy men). Source: [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]].

## Amida and the Pure Land Tradition

Amida Buddha is "one of the most popular divinities in the Buddhist pantheon, his name or that of his Pure Land appearing in more than 270 scriptures, roughly one out of every three works in the Mahayanist canon." In Mahayana theology, Amida is a bodhisattva who, through accumulated merit, created a Pure Land offering rebirth to all beings who demonstrated faith through devotional acts. His foundational vow — to receive into Pure Land all who sincerely called his name — made him a figure of universal salvific power.

In China, the Pure Land cult grew after 402 when the monk Hui-yuan founded the first Pure Land lay-clerical devotional society. The independent Pure Land school — distinguishing itself from sectarian interpretations that treated Pure Land as secondary practice — crystallized in the sixth century under T'an-luan and culminated in Shan-tao's teaching that vocal recitation of Amida's name (*nembutsu*) was the primary devotional act leading to rebirth in Pure Land.

In Nara-period Japan, Amida was worshiped primarily as a deliverer of deceased relatives' souls; images were commissioned mostly by laypeople seeking to ensure a dead relative's rebirth in Pure Land, "relatively few instances are recorded of individuals commissioning statues of Amida in the hope that they themselves might be able to enter his Pure Land after death." Pure Land remained marginal — a Nara phenomenon of personal piety, not yet a movement.

## Ennin and the Introduction of *Nembutsu* on Hiei (848)

The systematic practice of invoking Amida's name for one's own rebirth in Pure Land was introduced to Japan by [[tendai|Tendai]] monk **Ennin** (794–864, Jikaku Daishi), who encountered the *goe nembutsu* — five-tone rhythmical chanting of Amida's name — at the Chu-lin ssu on Wu-t'ai shan (China) in 840. The monastery had been founded by the Pure Land master Fa-chao (died ca. 820), who combined traditional Amida meditation with fervent name-invocation following Shan-tao. Ennin encountered the practice again at the Tzu-sheng ssu in Ch'ang-an, where he resided nearly five years.

In 848, immediately on his return to Japan, Ennin built a **Jogyodo** (Hall of Perpetual Practice) in the Toto (Eastern Pagoda) complex on Hiei. The Jogyodo was exclusively devoted to the *jogyo zammai* — a ninety-day meditation in which one concentrated thoughts on Amida while invoking his name and circumambulating his image. Classical Tendai had taught the *jogyo zammai* primarily as a meditative technique for enhancing concentration; for Ennin, "the *jogyo zammai* was not simply a meditative exercise but an act of devotion to Amida that facilitated entry into his Pure Land." In his will, Ennin instructed disciples to begin *fudan nembutsu* ("uninterrupted contemplation of Buddha").

## The *Fudan Nembutsu* and Its Dissemination

First performed in 865 (the year after Ennin's death), the **fudan nembutsu** was held annually at the Jogyodo from the morning of the eleventh to the night of the seventeenth of the eighth month. It comprised three elements: (1) continuous invocation of Amida's name; (2) circumambulation of Amida's image; (3) mental concentration on Amida — representing the verbal, physical, and mental dimensions of human activity respectively. "By dedicating his verbal utterances, physical movement, and mental processes to Amida in this fashion, the devotee immersed himself completely in Amida, which guaranteed his rebirth in Pure Land."

The fudan nembutsu spread steadily: a second Jogyodo was built in the Saito (Western Pagoda) complex with imperial sponsorship in 893; in 968 **Ryogen** established a third Jogyodo at Yokawa, enabling the roughly two thousand monks on Hiei to participate in the annual ceremony. Provincial clerics who trained on Hiei built Jogyodo in their home regions; by end of the tenth century, Jogyodo could be found in Kyoto, Otsu, Tonomine, and the Izu peninsula. A document of 970 drily observes that although novices had been expected to learn all four Tendai meditations during their twelve-year training, they "now limit themselves to the practice of *jogyo zammai* alone."

## Genshin and the *Ojo Yoshu* (985)

The decisive theoretical breakthrough came with **Genshin** (942–1017), a disciple of Ryogen who chose seclusion at Eshin'in in Yokawa over the high ecclesiastical career expected of Hiei's elite. His *Ojo yoshu* (*Anthology on Rebirth in Pure Land*, 985) "marked the beginning of a new phase in the development of Japanese Pure Land Buddhism." The work quotes from more than 160 Buddhist texts, particularly the Chinese Pure Land masters Shan-tao and Tao-ch'o.

What distinguished Genshin from his predecessors was his conviction that the world was about to enter the **mappo age** (*mappo*, "latter days of the Dharma") — a period of irreversible spiritual decline in which traditional practices like meditation, precept-keeping, and cultivation of wisdom would be "exceedingly difficult, if not impossible, for most people." The appropriate teaching for the mappo age was Pure Land, which Amida had created as "a refuge for all beings, both good and evil, who sought rebirth there."

While Genshin in principle accepted Tendai meditation on Amida as the highest practice, he recognized that most people in the mappo age could not sustain the demanding visualizations of Amida's thirty-two physical signs. For such "spiritually weak people — and Genshin included himself in this group" — he advocated *isshin shonen* ("wholehearted invocation of Amida's name combined with contemplation"). He placed particular weight on **rinju gyogi** (deathbed rites), in which the dying person, surrounded by friends urging him to think of Amida, repeatedly invoked Amida's name while holding a cord attached to an Amida image.

The *Ojo yoshu*'s structure organized the argument through vivid contrast: "our present world is one of defilement and pain in contrast to the Pure Land of Amida, which is a blissful realm free from taint or suffering." Graphic descriptions of the sufferings of beings trapped in transmigration against ecstatic descriptions of Pure Land's palaces, lakes, and gardens both warned against attachment to transitory pleasures and awakened yearning for Amida's realm.

## Aristocratic Lay Societies

Genshin's work appears to have been intended as a guide for the **Nijugo zammai-e**, a mutual-help society formed in 986 by twenty-five Yokawa monks who vowed to assist one another in attaining rebirth in Pure Land. Meeting on the fifteenth of each month, the society opened with a Lotus Sutra lecture, then moved to Amida meditation and chanting. If a member fell ill or appeared to be dying, the group would bring him to their Ojoin (Chapel for Rebirth), where they would encourage him to chant Amida's name while envisioning Amida's arrival with a retinue of bodhisattvas. The rules were drafted by Yoshishige no Yasutane, a converted literary man who had in 964 also founded the **Kangaku-e** — a mixed clerical-lay society of twenty monks from Hiei and twenty aristocrats or literary figures, meeting twice yearly to promote Pure Land practice among the upper classes.

Fujiwara no Michinaga (966–1027), the most powerful aristocrat of his age, was "an avid reader of the *Ojo yoshu*" and devoted Pure Land believer. After taking the tonsure in 1019, he built an **Amida Hall** (*Muryojuin*), installing nine sixteen-foot gilded Amida images representing the nine divisions of Pure Land. He died at his Amida Hall "in the manner of Genshin, clutching a silk cord that linked him to the nine images of Amida." His son Yorimichi built the famous **Hoodo** (Phoenix Hall) at Byodoin in Uji, designed as a physical re-creation of Pure Land. Between Michinaga's 1020 dedication and 1192, at least ninety-five Amida Halls were established across Japan.

## Popular Dissemination: The Hijiri

The Amida Halls and aristocratic *mukae-ko* ceremonies ("welcoming services" in which costumed young monks played bodhisattvas welcoming patrons into Paradise) had little to do with popular Pure Land faith. Common people found the faith through the **hijiri** — "holy men" who chose a religious calling outside the main monasteries, practicing esoteric rites simple enough for individual use, devotion to the Lotus Sutra, and faith in Amida as a savior. Some hijiri wore deerskins and lived in mountain retreats; others (*yugyo hijiri*, wandering holy men) roamed the countryside preaching and collecting small donations (*kanjin*), enabling common people to "establish affinity" (*kechieri*) with a hijiri and partake of his merit.

The most important early hijiri was **Kuya** (903–972, also known as Koya), who spent early years in mountain austerities, then became an itinerant proselytizer, building roads, bridges, wells, and collecting corpses for cremation. "Known as Amida hijiri because of his continuous chanting of the name of Amida — even the wells he dug came to be called 'Amida wells'" — Kuya settled in Kyoto in 938 as *ichi hijiri* ("the hijiri of the marketplace"). The late-tenth-century *Nihon ojo gokuraku ki* observes that before the Tengyo era (938–47) few common people practiced Amida devotions, but thanks to Kuya "who invoked the name himself and made others invoke the name, the whole of Japan came to devote itself to nembutsu."

By the eleventh century warriors, farmers, and fishermen — occupations whose activities violated Buddhist precepts — increasingly saw nembutsu as the primary path to rebirth in Pure Land. The *ojo den* (rebirth biography) genre collected stories of sinners saved by nembutsu: "there was not an evil act that he did not commit," says one account of a man who nonetheless attained Pure Land through reciting Amida's name 100,000 times daily after age sixty. The concept that *nembutsu* alone sufficed — without meditation, precepts, or scholarly understanding — and that even evil persons could be saved, laid the "foundation for the Pure Land schools that were established in the Kamakura period."

## Bridge to Kamakura Buddhism

Shinran (1173–1262), founder of the Jodo Shin school, acknowledged his debt to Heian lay piety, declaring that he modeled himself after Kyoshin (d. 866), a tonsured but married layman who expressed Pure Land faith simply by reciting Amida's name as often as possible in daily life. The theological move from Genshin's "deathbed rites and meditative nembutsu" to Honen's (1133–1212) "exclusive nembutsu" and Shinran's "nembutsu as expression of gratitude for already-assured salvation" all passed through the Heian trajectory of escalating emphasis on name-invocation as the accessible practice for an age of spiritual decline.

## Historiography

The Cambridge History treatment draws on Inoue Mitsusada's foundational *Nihon Jodo-kyo seiritsu-shi no kenkyu* (1956) and *Nihon kodai no kokka to Bukkyo* (1971), Hori Ichiro's studies of the *hijiri* concept, and primary sources including the *Sambo ekotoba* (984, translated by Edward Kamens as *The Three Jewels*, 1988), the five *ojo den* collections, and the *Ojo yoshu* itself (T, vol. 84). The chapter by Weinstein covers Pure Land as a sub-tradition within aristocratic Buddhism rather than as a separate movement, which is consistent with the Heian reality where Pure Land remained embedded within Tendai until Honen broke it free in 1175.
