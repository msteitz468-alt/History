## Definition

[[ibn-khaldun]]'s argument, developed in Chapter IV of [[the-muqaddimah]], that cities and towns are not spontaneous products of human preference but are causal effects of royal authority backed by dynastic group-solidarity ([[asabiyyah]]). Urbanization is not prior to the state; the state is prior to urbanization.

The argument has three components: (1) why royal authority is necessary to found cities; (2) why royal authority is necessary to sustain cities; (3) why large monuments require successive dynasties rather than a single one.

---

## Why Royal Authority Is Necessary to Found Cities

City-building is "not among the things that are necessary matters of general concern to human beings, in the sense that all human beings desire them or feel compelled to have them. As a matter of fact, (human beings) must be forced and driven to (build cities)." Two mechanisms operate:

- **Coercion**: "The stick of royal authority is what compels them."
- **Compensation**: payment sufficiently large "that only royal authority and a dynasty can pay for it."

Both mechanisms presuppose concentrated political and fiscal power. Bedouin groups and small polities lack both the authority to mobilize coordinated labor at scale and the surplus to compensate it. Only a dynasty that has already converted [[asabiyyah]] into territorial dominion can fund city-building.

---

## The Dynasty as Urban Life-Support

Ibn Khaldun's formulation: "the life of the dynasty is the life of the town." When a dynasty is short-lived, urban life terminates with it. When a dynasty endures, the city grows through continuous construction. The dependency runs in one direction: the city does not sustain the dynasty; the dynasty sustains the city.

Two exceptions qualify this:

1. **Geographic reprieve**: Cities set in productive territories with large rural hinterlands can draw in new population after their founding dynasty falls, and thus persist independently. Fez (drawing from Moroccan highland populations) and Bougie survive this way.
2. **Dynastic succession**: A new dynasty may occupy an existing city rather than building a new capital, giving the city renewed life. This happened with Cairo under successive Islamic dynasties.

Cities founded without geographic reprieve and abandoned by their dynasties — al-Kufah, al-Basrah, al-Qayrawan, old Baghdad — decay rapidly. "Its civilization will gradually decay, until its population is dispersed and gone."

## Large Monuments and Multi-Dynastic Construction

Section 4 of Chapter IV extends the argument: very large monuments cannot be built by a single dynasty alone, because they exceed the combined capacity of even great concentrated power. They require:

- The starting effort of one dynasty
- Continuation by successors who follow the original plan
- Engineering multipliers (pulleys, machines) that compensate for the limits of human bodily strength

Ibn Khaldun explicitly rejects the folk belief that ancient monuments were built by giants ("the common people call most of the monuments of the ancients found at this time, 'Adite monuments"). He explains: "Many a traveled person can confirm what we have stated from his own observation of building (activities) and of the use of mechanics to transport building materials." The size of monuments is proportionate to the organizational power of the dynasties that built them, not to the physical size of the builders.

Historical examples: the Dam of Ma'rib (begun by Saba' b. Yashjub, completed by Himyarite successors); the aqueduct at Carthage; the Iwan Kisra (Reception Hall of Khosraw), which Caliph ar-Rashid attempted and failed to demolish even with pickaxes, fire, and vinegar. The Iwan's resistance to destruction confirmed that its construction had required power exceeding that of any single caliphate — and its survival after Islamic conquest was itself evidence of the greatness of the Persian dynasty that built it.

This argument has a political corollary: "Only a strong royal authority is able to construct large cities and high monuments." The size of a dynasty's monuments is an index of its original power. Conversely, the monuments that survive a dynasty serve as evidence for historians of that dynasty's former greatness.

## Historiography

The claim that states precede cities rather than emerge from them was a minority position in early 20th-century historiography (V. Gordon Childe's "urban revolution" model assumed the reverse). Ibn Khaldun's 14th-century formulation anticipates later state-formation scholarship that emphasizes the coercive dimension of urbanization — notably Charles Tilly's work on war-making and state-making, and James C. Scott's arguments about early states as population-concentration machines. The Muqaddimah is not cited in this literature because it was unknown to it; the parallel is structural, not genealogical.
