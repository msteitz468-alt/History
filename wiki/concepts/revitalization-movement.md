# Revitalization Movement

Etic anthropological concept, imported into fascist studies by Roger Griffin in [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007|*Modernism and Fascism* (2007)]] and there elevated to a definitional component of [[generic-fascism]].

## Wallace's Anthropological Model

Anthony Wallace's revitalization movement is what Victor Turner calls a "social anti-structure": when a society's crisis is too deep to be resolved through its own symbolic resources (a **liminoid** rather than liminal condition), a group secedes to "seek the glow of communitas among those with whom they share... their most signal mark of identity." A "prophet" who has had "an ecstatic vision or revelation," now "personally rejuvenated," "undertakes the salvation of the community by imposing through preaching and proselytizing 'a syncretism of both ancient and new-fangled elements.'" Wallace's term for this syncretic recombination is **"mazeway resynthesis"** — the mazeway being "to the individual what culture is to society" (Tollefson: "a new synthesis, a new mazeway, or a new Gestalt"). Underlying machinery: van Gennep's rite-of-passage triad (separation / liminality / reaggregation) as refined by Turner and Maurice Bloch, whose *From Prey to Hunter* reads the ritual's function as society's regeneration — "the replenishing of its supply of transcendence."

Canonical premodern cases in Griffin's exposition: the Münster Anabaptists of 1534 (via Norman Cohn's *propheta* "intent on turning this one particular upheaval into the apocalyptic battle, the final purification of the world") and the 1890 Sioux Ghost Dance, crushed at Wounded Knee — a "ludic recombination" of foundation myths and anti-colonial war imagery. Standard features that carry into the modern political cases: a charismatic prophet-leader; the mythic exclusiveness of the new communitas; demonization of the old order *and of groups identified as the cause of its decadence*; intense syncretism; and emergence "during epochs of marked cultural change and its accompanying personal distress."

## Griffin's Application to Fascism and Nazism

Under modernity's protracted liminoidality (see [[modernism]]), programmatic modernisms "tend naturally to assume characteristics of contemporary revitalization movements." Griffin's fascist applications:

- **Mussolini** is read as an individual undergoing mazeway resynthesis (his 1908–09 conversion through *La Voce*; post-Caporetto assumption of the propheta persona), and the 1919 Fasci di combattimento as "an anti-party and a movement" — the embryo of a new communitas. See [[mussolini-benito]].
- **Nazism as a revitalization movement** (the book's ch. 9): Hitler transformed the post-putsch NSDAP into a mass movement of national "uprising" (*nationale Erhebung*) rather than a party; recruits sought communitas against Spengler's Weimar "quagmire"; the Depression triggered "not just an economic and political crisis but a nomic crisis" (NSDAP: 2.6% in May 1928 → 37.3% in July 1932, with over 50% of votes going to revolutionary anti-democratic parties). The Feldherrnhalle martyr liturgy (Vondung) substituted "the immortal Volksgemeinschaft of the racial elite" for the Christian ecclesia; Hess's "The Party is Hitler! Hitler however is Germany, just as Germany is Hitler!" and Kershaw's "working towards the Führer" are read as a secular *participatio mistica* turning the [[volksgemeinschaft|Volksgemeinschaft]] into a "charismatic community." Heidegger's and Robert Ley's conversions are the book's case studies in individual mazeway resynthesis under nomic crisis.
- In the conclusion, "the revitalization movement" is proposed (with [[modernism]] and the "gardening state") as a **definitional component of generic fascism**, within Emilio Gentile's cluster of intersecting concepts (totalitarianism, political religion, charismatic politics, palingenetic myth, anthropological revolution, organic nationalism). The [[iron-guard|Iron Guard]] is re-read as "a modern revitalization movement based on charismatic politics" (Eugen Weber's cargo-cult comparison), not an anti-modern religious atavism.

A structural corollary Griffin stresses: fascism "re-imagines the third stage of the ritual triadic process as a permanent second stage, as perpetuated liminality" — a cult of permanent revolution with no exit strategy, which is why neither fascist regime could routinize or transfer charismatic authority.

## Limitations and Critiques

Wallace's model was built on small-scale societies; its transfer to industrial mass politics is analogical, and Griffin concedes the whole primordialist apparatus is "speculative but sustainable through transdisciplinary research." Anthropology itself treats the mid-century functionalist literature (Wallace, Turner) with reflexive caution. Historians sceptical of "political religion" approaches (Kershaw) extend the same scepticism here.
