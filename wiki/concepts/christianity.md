## Definition

**Christianity** is the monotheistic religion centered on Jesus of Nazareth as the
incarnate Word of God, which by the [[middle-millennium|Middle Millennium]] had
become one of the three "world religions" structuring [[kedar-cwh-v5-2015|CWH V]]'s
Part IV. Miri Rubin's chapter (Ch 16) treats it not as a single bloc but as a set of
**regional systems** — a family of Christianities, differing in language, liturgy,
and theology, linked (and sometimes divided) by trade, crusade, and missionary
movement. Its defining double potential, in Rubin's conclusion, is that Christian
institutions and teaching held the capacity **both for inclusion** (accommodating
diversity) **and for exclusion** — for "encouraging polities to become persecuting
societies."

## A Family of Christianities

Rubin's central corrective is the **diversity** of medieval Christendom, far beyond
the Latin West:

- **Latin (Western) Christendom.** Centered on Rome and the [[papacy]], Latin in
  liturgy; the expanding, increasingly centralized and reformist Christendom of
  western Europe — the world of bishops, monasteries, the
  [[medieval-university|universities]], the cult of the saints and the Virgin Mary,
  and (from 1095) the [[crusades|Crusades]].
- **Greek / Byzantine Orthodoxy.** The imperial Orthodoxy of
  [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]], from which the Slavic Orthodox churches were
  evangelized; the Melkite and Maronite communities of Syria.
- **The Church of the East ("Nestorian").** Following Theodore of Mopsuestia and
  Nestorius (condemned at Ephesus, 431), centered first at Edessa then Nisibis and
  led by the *catholicos* (seat at Seleucia-Ctesiphon, then [[baghdad|Baghdad]] from
  780). Its missionaries, "easily travelling with the merchants of the southern
  [[silk-roads|Silk Road]]," reached **southern India** (the Malabar/St Thomas
  Christians), Central Asia (a metropolitan see at Kashgar), and **China** — the
  easternmost Christian reach of the age.
- **The Oriental Orthodox (non-Chalcedonian) churches.** The Syriac (Jacobite),
  **Coptic** (Egypt), **Ethiopian**, Nubian, and Armenian churches, separated over
  the Christological formulations of 451 and maintaining their own liturgical
  languages and hierarchies (the Ethiopian church depending on the Coptic).

## Three Conditions of Christian Life

Rubin surveys the world's Christians by their political situation:
1. **Areas governed by Christian rulers** (Latin Europe, Byzantium, Ethiopia,
   Christian Caucasus).
2. **Large tolerated indigenous communities under Muslim rule** (Copts, Syriac
   Christians, the Christians of Iberia and the Levant) — protected as *dhimmis* but
   subordinate.
3. **Small Christian groups further east** (India, Central Asia, China), sustained by
   trade routes and missionary networks.

These were linked by **movements** — trade, crusading, and missionary activity —
that knit the Christian regions together even across political and confessional
divides.

## Inclusion and Exclusion

The chapter's analytical edge is Rubin's argument (drawing on R. I. Moore's *Formation
of a Persecuting Society*) that medieval Christianity carried an inherent **double
potential**. Its rituals, saints, sacraments, and clergy could **include** and
accommodate enormous diversity; but the same institutions and teaching could
**exclude**, sanctioning the persecution of Jews, heretics, lepers, and others, and
pushing polities toward becoming "persecuting societies" — a tendency that sharpened
in the High and [[late-middle-ages|Late Middle Ages]] (anti-Jewish violence,
expulsions, the crusade against heretics). This bears on the wiki's treatment of
[[gender-history|gender]] (women mystics and the limits set on them) and of the
"expanding webs of conflict" in the volume's title.

## The Early Modern Transformation (CWH VI)

[[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]] (R. Po-chia Hsia) carries the story into 1400–1800 —
the era when, against all expectation, the divided and embattled Christianity of
1400 became a globally expanding "world religion." In 1400 it stood in **three
branches**: a crisis-ridden **Latin** Church (the Great Schism, 1378–1415);
**Greek Orthodoxy**, under siege as Byzantium shrank (the mantle of apostolic
succession passing to Muscovy after 1453); and the **Oriental Orthodox** churches
(Coptic, Ethiopian, Armenian, Syrian), mostly tolerated minorities under Islam.

Latin Christianity survived two further profound crises — the Schism (healed at the
Council of Constance, 1417, with a re-centralized, increasingly Italian papacy) and
the [[protestant-reformation-1517|Protestant Reformation]] (1517–1648), which
**permanently divided** Western Christendom and produced the bloodiest century in
Christian history. Hsia stresses Christianity's **deep entanglement with Christian
polities**: just as Constantine's conversion had made it a world religion, the
Iberian *padroado/patronato* (papal grants of church control to the crowns, after
the 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas) tied its **global expansion** to Spanish and
Portuguese empire. Overseas, this produced the syncretic Christianities of the
Americas and Kongo and the Jesuit missions in Asia (see
[[intercultural-relations-early-modern]]) — the Ricci accommodation in China and the
**Chinese Rites Controversy**, the brief Japanese "Christian century" and its brutal
suppression. Even Russian Orthodoxy expanded eastward with the tsars, so that, as
Hsia opens, Catholic Jesuits and Russian Orthodox icons could meet on the Mongol
steppe between two expanding Christian empires.

## Limitations and Contestation

Treating Christianity as a set of "regional systems" resists the older Latin-centric
narrative but raises its own question — whether so diverse a family shares enough to
be a single object. Cook's claim (see [[islam]]) that Islam alone was a "world
civilization" is implicitly challenged by the case that Latin Christendom, too,
cohered into a civilization. The "persecuting society" thesis is itself debated among
medievalists.
