# Guerrilla Warfare

## Definition and Scope

Guerrilla warfare is the weaker side's method of survival: a strategy of exhaustion fought on home terrain with the advantages of popular support and local knowledge. Freedman frames it as the counterpoint to nuclear strategy — "If nuclear weapons pulled military strategy away from conventional warfare in one direction, guerrilla warfare moved it in another." Where nuclear weapons "threaten society with extreme force," guerrilla warfare was "about the response of an enraged society to illegitimate military force." Its "basic attraction was as a method that could help weaker sides survive."

The name derives not from novelty but from one campaign: "guerrilla warfare gained its name from the tactics of ambush and harassment used during the 'little war' fought by Spaniards against French occupation forces at the start of the nineteenth century." The form was older — already used in the American War of Independence.

The defining paradox is the inversion of offense and defense. Guerrilla warfare is **strategically defensive but tactically offensive**: "Though a strategically defensive concept, the tactics of guerrilla warfare had to be offensive, aiming to catch the enemy unawares." It is "geared to a strategy of exhaustion, gaining time in the hope that the enemy would tire or that something else would turn up." See [[annihilation-and-exhaustion|Annihilation and Exhaustion]].

## Causal Drivers

Guerrilla warfare is rarely decisive on its own. Freedman stresses that "such warfare was unlikely to be successful on its own. Irregular forces worked most effectively when providing a distraction to an enemy also facing regular forces in a more conventional campaign." Napoleon "suffered in Spain because he also faced the British army"; Russian peasants merely "made life additionally miserable for French forces in 1812." Effectiveness depended on rough, inaccessible interior terrain, and an uprising had to remain "nebulous and elusive, because as soon as it became concrete it could be crushed."

## Major Phases

**Clausewitzian/Jominian ambivalence.** [[clausewitz-carl-von|Clausewitz]], who observed the Spanish insurrection and the French debacle in Russia, treated guerrilla warfare "as a form of defense" — the "last and desperate resort" of an otherwise defeated people — and "did not see irregular militias as being of much value in the absence of regular forces." [[jomini-antoine-henri|Jomini]] recoiled outright, calling such wars "organized assassination," confessing his "prejudices were with the good old times when the French and English guards courteously invited each other to fire first."

**The socialist hesitation.** Engels saw the Spanish guerrillas as "more of a mob than an army," motivated by "hatred, revenge, and love of plunder." [[lenin-vladimir|Lenin]] "refused to dismiss guerrilla warfare entirely" but held it "could only be a subordinate form of struggle, not the main method." Trotsky, following Delbrück, contrasted the stronger side's pursuit of annihilation with the weaker side's attempt "to disorganize the stronger using light, mobile units" — but "was clearly in favor of annihilation." See [[revolutionary-strategy|Revolutionary Strategy]].

**Lawrence's "war of detachment."** T. E. Lawrence, leading the Arab revolt against the Ottomans, did "the most to develop principles about how guerrilla warfare should be fought rather than how it could be contained." His metaphors define the field: armies were "like plants, 'immobile as a whole, firm-rooted,'" while the Arab irregulars were "'a thing intangible, invulnerable, without front or back, drifting about like gas.'" The asymmetry was cognitive: the Turks "would not realize 'to make war upon rebellion is messy and slow, like eating soup with a knife.'" Tactics were "tip and run, not pushes, but strokes," depending on "speed, concealment, accuracy of fire." His algebra of insurgency: "Rebellions can be made by 2 per cent active in a striking force, and 98 per cent passive sympathetically." Yet he conceded his campaign was "a 'side show upon a side show'" — the real defeat of the Ottomans came from Allenby's conventional push.

**Mao's protracted war.** [[mao-zedong|Mao]] "saw guerrilla warfare as an acceptable strategy when on the defensive but not as an independent route to victory," relying on it "whenever his immediate need was simply to survive." He put political education at the heart of "people's war" — "Politics, therefore, always had to be in command" — and played down material factors: "It is people, not things that are decisive." The fish/sea image is canonical: the mobilized people "would be 'a vast sea in which the enemy will be swallowed up' but in which their army would thrive like fish." Mao's optimum strategy ran in three stages — defensive, stalemate, then offensive — and "the third stage demanded regular forces."

## Geographic Spread

From Iberia to Italy (Garibaldi as "a gifted guerrilla commander"), to the Arabian deserts, to China and Vietnam. General Vo Nguyen Giap, "the most assiduous follower of Mao," called Lawrence's *Seven Pillars of Wisdom* his "fighting gospel," and innovated by moving flexibly between Mao's stages rather than treating them as sequential.

## Interaction and End Conditions

The through-line "from Engels to Giap" is that "guerrilla warfare was therefore never seen as sufficient in itself. It was a way of holding out until it was possible to develop a true military capacity... if the aim was to seize power, the regular forces of the state would have to be defeated." Guerrilla warfare needs a regular force or political endgame to be decisive.

## The Legal Lineage (Whitman)

Whitman ([[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012|The Verdict of Battle]], 2012) traces
the modern Western guerrilla to the American Revolution: rebel riflemen's
ambush tactics outraged British regulars as "ungenerous," verging on murder
("What an unfair method of carrying on a war!"). The lineage runs through
Napoleonic Spain, the Mexican-American War, the Confederacy's **Partisan Ranger
Act (April 1862)** — which conferred formal combatant status on Southern
irregulars, forcing the first new legal doctrines of irregular war (Lieber,
Bluntschli) — and the francs-tireurs after [[battle-of-sedan-1870|Sedan]]. His
structural point: guerrilla war is the signature of wars where a defeated
*people* refuses a battlefield verdict — the mode of conflict that replaced the
[[verdict-of-battle|pitched-battle settlement system]] and marks its
[[death-of-pitched-battle|death]]; and one of the "hard war" alternatives
(chevauchée, razzia, siege, bombing) that contained battle warfare had always
held at bay.
