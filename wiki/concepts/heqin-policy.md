# Heqin Policy (和親 — Peace-and-Kinship)

## Definition and Scope

The *heqin* (Wade-Giles: *ho-ch'in*; "peace-and-kinship") policy was the Han dynasty's foundational appeasement strategy toward the [[xiongnu|Xiongnu]] steppe empire, formalized c. 198 BCE following the [[battle-of-baideng-200bce|Battle of Baideng]] (200 BCE) and maintained, with periodic renewals, until Emperor Wu ([[han-wudi|Han Wudi]]) repudiated it in 133 BCE. It governed Han–Xiongnu relations across five imperial reigns of the Former Han.

The policy had four structural components:
1. **Annual tribute** of silk, grain, and other goods delivered by the Han to the Xiongnu chanyu
2. **Marriage alliances** — Chinese princesses (or women styled as such) given as consorts to the chanyu
3. **Political symbolism** — the two rulers represented as nominal equals, "brothers," with their kingdoms as "two complementary universes" — Earth and Heaven
4. **Border markets** (kuan-shih) opened as controlled exchange zones

The framework was explicitly not permanent. From a Han perspective, *heqin* was a long-term strategy aimed at absorbing the Xiongnu leadership into the Han political sphere and neutralizing the Xiongnu as an independent state — "an evolution of Spring and Autumn marriage-diplomacy policies" in which Han was "ready to wait and to sacrifice part of their revenues to achieve a long-term political objective" (Di Cosmo). The goal was what Di Cosmo calls "unite and rule": strengthen the chanyu's central authority through economic support so he could enforce peace on his own subordinates, and over time draw the ruling clan into the Han "family."

## Historical Application

### Adoption (200–198 BCE)

The heqin was inaugurated because the Han was militarily incapable of fighting the Xiongnu after years of civil war against Qin remnants and rival warlords. The defeat at [[battle-of-baideng-200bce|Baideng]] demonstrated that Chinese armies could not sustain campaigns in nomadic territory, where the Xiongnu's superior mobility made it impossible to compel a decisive engagement. The formal treaty followed c. 198 BCE.

The diplomatic correspondence of 198–133 BCE consistently employed language that "specifically excludes the existence of sources of authority other than the emperor and the ch'an-yü" — the Han deliberately chose to strengthen the chanyu's position by recognizing only him as the Xiongnu political authority, rejecting the "divide and rule" strategy of assigning the title of chanyu to multiple tribal leaders. This was a calculated effort to create a single interlocutor capable of guaranteeing peace.

### The Operational Record (198–133 BCE)

The policy was renewed multiple times — after the reigns of Gaozu (Liu Bang), Hui, Wen, and Jing — but consistently failed to prevent border violence. The pattern was:
- Treaty concluded → chanyu accepts terms
- Tribute delivered, markets opened
- Border raids continue by subordinate leaders or defected Chinese commanders
- Han complains; chanyu expresses frustration but cannot stop his own people
- New treaty concluded, tribute often increased

In 176 BCE the "wise king of the right" violated the treaty on his own initiative and invaded China south of the Yellow River. Emperor Wen-ti's edict explicitly blamed the subordinate leader: "The Han and the Hsiung-nu made a brotherly pact... Today the wise king of the right has left his state and has led his people to settle in the region south of the river. This is not in accord with the agreements" (*Han shu* 94A, 3756). The chanyu's reply acknowledged his own frustration — the wise king had "preferred to follow the advice of other Hsiung-nu leaders" without informing him. The same pattern recurred in 162 BCE, when Wen-ti reminded the chanyu that "wicked and evil people on his side were 'severing the harmony' between the two rulers" (*Shih chi chu-yi* 110, 2324–5; *Han shu* 94A, 3762–3).

Later the heqin itself became a tool of Han divisive strategy: Xiongnu subordinate leaders used the promise of a separate heqin agreement as justification for defection to the Han, bypassing the chanyu's authority. The "surrender" of disaffected leaders was "nothing else but an attempt to bypass the ch'an-yü's authority and set up separate ho-ch'in agreements with the Han" (Di Cosmo).

### The Debates of 135–134 BCE

The most cogent contemporary diagnosis of heqin's limitations is preserved in the two-phase policy debate reported in *Han shu* chapter 52, 2398–2403. As [[han-wudi|Emperor Wu]] acceded (140 BCE) and the chanyu requested renewal of the treaty, two officials argued the fundamental question:

**Wang Hui** (a border official with expertise in nomadic affairs): "When the Han conclude a peace [agreement] with the Hsiung-nu, usually after just a few years the Hsiung-nu violate the treaty. It would be better to reject their promises and send soldiers to attack them." Wang's second-round argument: the military circumstances had changed since Baideng, the Xiongnu political base was less stable than it appeared ("the Hsiung-nu did not have such a firm hold over their subject peoples"), and a single decisive strike against the chanyu would trigger internal collapse and submission of formerly vanquished peoples like the Yuezhi.

**Han An-kuo** (the "pacifist" argument): Heqin had "benefited five generations" since Baideng. Deep military engagement in nomadic territory was futile: "the Hsiung-nu depend upon the hooves of their own horses... They move around like a multitude of birds and are difficult to capture and control. Fighting at a distance of a thousand li, the army will not gain any profit" (*Han shu* 52, 2398). His counter to Wang's military optimism: "the clash of two strong winds will weaken [them] to the point that they cannot raise a hair or a feather; an arrow shot from a strong bow at the end of its flight would not be able to pierce the white plain silk of Lu" — even the strongest army weakens over time.

An-kuo's "pacifist" argument won the 135 BCE round and the treaty was renewed. But by 134 BCE a border notable (Nie Weng-yi) had proposed an ambush at the frontier city of Ma-yi designed to lure the chanyu into a trap. The plan failed disastrously — the chanyu detected the stratagem, the Han armies were humiliated at Ma-yi — but paradoxically confirmed Wang Hui's argument: if treaties did not work and limited military action also failed, "there was no other solution but to confront them on their own terms, through full-scale military engagement."

Di Cosmo's conclusion: "The issue that forced the change of policy was not the ho-ch'in policy per se, but that it was not working."

## Di Cosmo's Structural Incompatibility Thesis

The centerpiece of Di Cosmo's analysis is that heqin failed for structural reasons that no amount of tribute or diplomatic skill could overcome. The argument proceeds from a comparison of the two polities' notions of sovereignty and treaty authority.

### Han Sovereignty (Four Constitutive Elements)

Di Cosmo identifies four aspects of Han sovereignty that equipped the Han emperor to observe an international agreement:

1. **Supreme lawgiver**: The emperor's ordinances and decrees had the authority of law and could overrule customary usages. "The code of laws became then the ultimate source of authority within the body politic, and the only institutional power located above it was the emperor" (Hulsewé, cited by Di Cosmo).
2. **Unity of religious and political sanction**: The doctrine of Heaven's Mandate, though developing slowly, made the emperor's religious and temporal powers inseparable. The title *huang-ti* "already excluded, from the very beginning of the imperial period, the idea that the ruler's religious and temporal powers could be separated."
3. **Highest political authority, unchallengeable**: "The emperor also represented the highest source of political authority in the state. He could issue edicts and decrees, could appoint or dismiss ministers, and was the supreme judicial authority." The Han bureaucratic hierarchy ensured policies were implemented through a "chain of subordination within which each link was responsible to the higher one."
4. **Bounded political community**: The authority of the Han emperor, while cast in universal terms philosophically, was in practice bounded by the norms of foreign relations. A claim to universal rulership had been "suppressed because it was unrealistic and contradicted the policy of negotiated accommodation."

These four features meant that when the Han emperor signed a treaty, he had the institutional capacity to enforce its terms throughout his polity.

### Xiongnu Authority (The Structural Deficit)

In traditional Inner Asian societies, "kin bonds, reliance on customary law, and segmentation of political power among clans and tribes in steppe pastoral societies prevented the emergence of any absolute, indivisible, and legally recognized authority that would be the expression of a given political community in its entirety" (Di Cosmo). The chanyu was not a sovereign in the Han sense but rather "the first among equals, whose position of primacy rested ultimately on the consent obtained from other chieftains and members of the tribal aristocracy."

Key features of Xiongnu political authority:
- The chanyu was "primus inter pares, rather than the sovereign... the agent of the interests of the community of aristocrats by whom he was supported"
- Customary and traditional law remained the basis of Xiongnu law — "to which even the ch'an-yü had to conform"
- High offices (Hu-yen, Lan, Hsu-pu clans held the highest ranks) were hereditary and could not be displaced
- The chanyu's "consent could be extracted by force or suasion but could not be compelled through adherence to a firmly established state ideology or controlled through the machinery of a state bureaucracy"
- Succession struggles were not uncommon (e.g., the Lu-li king of the left who proclaimed himself ruler at the death of the Chün-ch'en chanyu in 126 BCE)
- Even the attempt to execute insubordinate subordinates required more than "a simple imperial order" (the case of the Hun-yeh and Hsiu-t'u kings in 121 BCE)

### The Asymmetry and Its Consequences

"The crux of the matter is that when treaties were concluded, their observance could not exceed the limits of the authority of each contracting party. As the Han and the Hsiung-nu notions of central authority were asymmetric, so were their respective abilities to observe the provisions of the treaties" (Di Cosmo).

The best evidence: raids continued despite the Han's delivery of tribute to the chanyu precisely because the perpetrators were subordinate leaders or Chinese defectors whom the chanyu could not effectively restrain. Han statesman Hou Ying made this structural point explicit in 51 BCE: "although China possesses the teachings of propriety and morality and has the death penalty, the masses still violate prohibitions. How then could we expect the ch'an-yü to keep his followers from violating the treaty?" (*Han shu* 94B, 3804).

Di Cosmo's conclusion: "No amount of Chinese support could change the core structure of nomadic society, and because a basic notion of sovereignty could not possibly develop without being preceded by radical transformations in the nomadic state's social and political texture, the position of the ruler was destined to remain fragile... peace proved aleatory."

The Han strategy of "unite and rule" — increasing tribute to strengthen the chanyu's internal position — was therefore fundamentally misconceived. "Unfortunately no amount of tribute could accomplish that, and the security of the frontier was constantly threatened."

## Repudiation and the Turn to War

The abandonment of heqin was not simply a function of Han growing strong enough to fight — it was the realization that the policy could never achieve its stated goals. Emperor Wu's decision to pursue military engagement from 133 BCE followed from the combination of:
1. Military capability: Han development of cavalry forces (36 breeding stations with 300,000 horses by 140 BCE), iron armor, advanced crossbows, and a professional ping soldier class
2. Internal political consolidation: resolution of the *chu-hou-wang* challenge, death of the empress dowager in 135 BCE
3. Xiongnu political vulnerabilities: internal divisions among the Xiongnu aristocracy that Wang Hui had correctly identified as exploitable
4. The Ma-yi disaster of 133 BCE: confirming that neither renewed diplomacy nor limited military action could solve the border problem

The formal end of the bipolar Han–Xiongnu system came with Hu-han-yeh chanyu's acceptance of a position of inferiority to Han emperor Hsüan-ti in 51 BCE — the direct result of Wu-ti's campaigns.

## Limits and Critiques

Scholarly debate on heqin has produced three main positions:

**Tactical arrangement view** (Shih Wei-ch'ing): Both Han and Xiongnu accepted heqin as a temporary arrangement from the start. The Han were forced to accept tributary status for lack of strength; the Xiongnu found it economically advantageous. Neither renounced their separate interests.

**Permanent warfare context view** (Chang Ch'ang-ming): Heqin was pure expediency within a general context of permanent warfare. The "ho-ch'in treaties constituted only one side of a broader and more complex relationship" and genuine reconciliation was never seriously pursued by either party.

**Holistic benefits view** (Lo Ta-yün): The policy deserves credit for enabling Han economic growth and political consolidation, as well as promoting friendly relations and "cultural exchanges" during the early Former Han.

Di Cosmo's structural incompatibility thesis cuts across all three: it argues that the question of diplomatic intention is secondary to the structural question of treaty enforcement capacity. Even if both sides wanted peace, the Xiongnu political system made durable peace impossible to guarantee. The "insatiable" nomad stereotype was "a convenient rationalization, not the least because it prepared the Chinese psychologically for the unavoidable consequence of the failure of peace: a painful and prolonged war against a powerful enemy."

## Comparison to Later Chinese Appeasement Strategies

The heqin established a template — tribute and marriage alliance in exchange for nominal border peace — that recurred in Chinese frontier relations through the Tang, Song, and Ming periods. Later dynasties (particularly Tang and Ming) relied far more heavily on horse imports from the steppe than the early Han, which attempted autarchic horse breeding. The later pattern of assigning the title of chanyu to multiple tribal leaders ("divide and rule"), broadly enacted by Wang Mang and the Later Han, was explicitly *not* part of the earlier heqin strategy — the Former Han deliberately chose "unite and rule."
