# Democratic Centralism
## Definition
**Democratic Centralism** is the organizational principle of communist parties, combining freedom of discussion with unity of action. In theory, it balances two aspects:
1. **Democratic:** All party members have the right to debate and discuss policy options, and all party organs are elected from the bottom up.
2. **Centralist:** Once a decision has been reached by a majority vote or by a higher body, all discussion must cease, and all members and lower party organs must execute the decision without question. In short, "freedom of discussion, unity of action."

## Context and Origin
Lenin introduced the concept to ensure that his Bolshevik faction could act as a highly disciplined, unified vanguard party in the autocratic environment of imperial Russia. Under Stalin, the "democratic" aspect of the principle was completely extinguished, and it became a mechanism of absolute centralism. The general secretary and the Politburo issued directives that had to be obeyed without discussion, and any criticism was labeled "factionalism"—a charge that Stalin exploited during the succession struggle of the 1920s and the Great Purge to eliminate his rivals (Line 5857, 6129).

## Limitations and Critiques
- **Mechanism of Dictatorship:** In practice in the USSR, democratic centralism led to a rigid, top-down command structure. The "centralist" side completely dominated, preventing any genuine debate or self-criticism, and rendering party congresses mere rubber stamps for the leadership.
- **Suppression of Pluralism:** Under the banner of preventing "factionalism," the principle was used to suppress any political pluralism or alternative opinions within the Party, establishing a monolithic system that struggled to adapt to changing social and economic realities.
