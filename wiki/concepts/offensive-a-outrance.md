# Offensive à Outrance

The "offensive à outrance" ("offensive to the utmost" or "all-out offensive") was the dominant French doctrinal concept on the eve of the Great War. It elevated the will to advance, moral superiority, and relentless attack as the decisive factors in battle, downplaying or rejecting the defensive except as a temporary expedient.

## Definition and Scope

The doctrine held that "Only the offensive yields positive results" and that "Battles are above all moral contests." Defeat was inevitable when hope for victory ceased; success went to the side with the steadiest will and most highly tempered morale rather than the one suffering least losses. The 1913 regulations asserted that an energetic commander-in-chief "will launch operations of such violence and fury that the enemy, weakened in its morale and paralyzed in its action, will be reduced, perhaps, to remaining on the defensive." The defense could contain the enemy only for a limited time; it could never gain success by itself. Combat, once begun, was to be "pushed to the end" with extreme energy.

It explicitly rejected earlier regulations that saw the defense as a means of "drawing the enemy onto terrain where one can fight him under the best conditions."

## Causal Drivers

- Intellectual roots: Charles Ardant du Picq's emphasis on "moral ascendancy" or "resolution to advance" as decisive even against equal or superior material. Ferdinand Foch (1903) argued modern firearms favored the attacker by enabling greater volume of fire from the offensive side. Henri Bonnal stressed that the first great battle of mass conscript armies would be decisive due to panic in inexperienced troops.
- Institutional push: Lt. Col. Louis Loyseau de Grandmaison, head of the Operations Bureau, in February 1911 lectures criticized the "nearly complete atrophy" of the offensive spirit in the French army.
- Joffre's adoption: After his July 1911 appointment as chief of the General Staff, Joffre "firmly integrated the offensive à outrance into the thinking of the French army" through maneuvers, map exercises, and the 1913 regulations (October for large units; December for smaller). He bears ultimate responsibility for raising it to new heights.

## Major Phases / Application in Plan XVII Era

The October and December 1913 regulations codified it. Artillery's role shifted: it no longer "prepares attacks" (as in 1895 regs) but "supports them." Massive pre-assault bombardment was discouraged; artillery fired during the infantry advance, with economical ammunition use. Infantry regulations (April 1914) declared the bayonet the "supreme weapon"; troops in blue coats and red trousers, officers leading, drums/bugles sounding the charge, were to close and finish with superior courage. The 75-mm gun was expected to provide rapid, intense fire support.

This doctrine aligned with Joffre's preference for an immediate offensive (revising Plan XVI toward greater offensive emphasis in 1911 and Plan XVII in 1913–14) rather than the prior defensive-offensive posture.

## Interaction with Other Concepts / Limitations

It contrasted with more cautious approaches (e.g., Michel's emphasis on reserves and integration of active/reserve forces). It contributed to under-preparation for positional warfare and heavy artillery needs, as mobile battles and moral factors were prioritized. Post-1914 experience forced evolution toward rupture, continuous battle, and eventually methodical battle (see related concepts).

`contrasts_with:` [[concepts/schlieffen-plan|Schlieffen Plan]] (German emphasis on envelopment and material factors in planning); later French adaptations after 1914.

## Historiography

Doughty (2005) presents the doctrine as a deliberate choice by Joffre and senior officers, not mere national temperament, with Joffre using his new powers to codify it. Joffre later defended the strategic/operational offensive while blaming tactical commanders for poor execution ("not taking into account the nature of modern war"). Critics (including Michel before his ouster) saw it as pernicious. It produced "glorious" but costly actions that bled elite units in August 1914. The concept's effects fed postwar debates on French doctrine leading to 1940.
