# Trireme

The **trireme** (*triērēs*) was the premier warship of the Classical Greek world: a fast,
oared galley with three horizontal banks of oars, a bronze ram, and a crew of about
**200** (about **170 oarsmen** plus deck crew and marines). Highly maneuverable for
**ramming** tactics rather than boarding, it was central to Athenian naval power
([[delian-league|Delian League]] / Athenian Empire), the Persian Wars
([[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]), and the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]].
Broader tactics and politics: [[greek-naval-warfare]]; finance: [[financing-greek-warfare]].

## Design and crew

Out of a total crew of ~200, **170 were oarsmen** arranged in three horizontal rows, one
man per oar (Hunt, [[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]]
I.5). Other crewmen included **ten hoplite marines**, **four archers**, a carpenter, a
piper, and officers and deck-hands (helmsman, lookout, rowing-master, purser, shipwright).
Hulls were lightly built for speed; nightly (and often midday) beaching for food, water,
and sleep was normal. Rough dimensions in modern literature: roughly 37 m long by 6 m
beam, speeds under oar historically estimated at average 7–8 knots (well over 100 miles
in a day possible under ideal conditions).

No sufficiently detailed ancient artistic depiction resolved scholarly disputes about the
operation of the three rower tiers; the modern reconstruction **Olympias** used partial
images plus engineering speculation (Whitby, CHGRW I.3). Sea-battles leave little for
underwater archaeology — triremes used crew weight as ballast and rarely sank to silted
seabeds; they were overwhelmed in storms, wrecked on shore, or incapacitated in battle
without ending on the seabed. Trials clarified the importance of training, performance
factors, and weather susceptibility; seat cushions on Olympias were soaked and grimy after
rowing; the boatswain needed an assistant to be heard aft.

## Origins and spread

Evolution: Homeric long ships → bireme / penteconter → trireme; waterline ram perhaps
eighth century. Greek trireme development mid-seventh century at **Corinth** per
Thucydides’ tradition. Expense slowed adoption — the **pentekontor** remained more common
until the mid-sixth century. The wealth generated by the creation of the Persian empire in
the mid-sixth century seems to have played a key role in the original spread of the
trireme among Aegean Greeks and Phoenicians (Strauss, CHGRW I.7).

**Themistocles’** 483 plan used a Laurium silver-mine windfall to build **200 Athenian
triremes** — the core of Greek victory at Salamis 480; Persian fleet defeat forced
withdrawal of most of the land army; Mycale followed up by sea. Manning 200 ships
implied ~**40,000** crew — beyond any single *polis*’s citizen demography, Athens
included; combat effectiveness was highly skill-dependent (Gabrielsen, CHGRW I.8).

## Tactics and command

Signals by flags, trumpet, shield flash; local wing victories common. Double line used as
defence against breakthrough (Heraclides of Mylasa; Athenians at Arginusae 406). Athenian
doctrine favoured ramming manoeuvre (*diekplous*, *periplous*) with light marine
contingents; older “land battle on deck” style (Sybota 433) used heavier marine
complements. To be successful, a fleet needed good men as well as good ships, and a
general who was part commander, coach, and trainer — oarsmen intolerant of mistreatment;
pay disputes could prompt desertion or riots (Thuc. 7–8). See [[greek-naval-warfare]] for
Phormio, blockade limits, and amphibious use.

## Successors: polyremes

Dionysius of Syracuse built “fours” and “fives” early fourth century; Athens was building
fours/fives by the 330s. Rating numbers indicate **files of oarsmen per side**, not
additional oar levels above three. Polyremes dominated Hellenistic fleets but were less
nimble than triremes; combat shifted toward boarding, marines, and deck artillery. The
trireme remained the Classical paradigm against which later types are measured.
