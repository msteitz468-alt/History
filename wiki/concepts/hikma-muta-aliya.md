## Definition and Scope

*Al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya* — conventionally translated as "transcendent philosophy" or, more precisely, "metaphysical philosophy" — is the name Mullā Ṣadrā gave to his synthetic philosophical system, most fully expounded in his magnum opus *al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya fī al-asfār al-arbaʿa al-ʿaqliyya* (usually called *Asfār* — "The Four Journeys of the Intellect"). The system defines the dominant intellectual tradition of Shīʿite scholasticism from the seventeenth century to the present, and continues to shape the curriculum of Iranian seminary education.

Hossein Ziai — in [[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]], ch. 19 — emphasizes that the term should be translated as "metaphysical philosophy" rather than "transcendent philosophy" (as in Henry Corbin's influential but misleading rendering *sagesse orientale*): "Given Ṣadrā's explicitly philosophical aims, this term is to be preferred to the prevalent 'transcendent philosophy'." The system is described in the source as "rational and 'scientific' (*ʿilmī*) in intention."

## Structural Features

### Departure from Avicennan Organization

Unlike Avicenna's *al-Shifāʾ*, the *Asfār* has no separate section on logic or physics. It thus departs from the tripartite Peripatetic division (logic, physics, metaphysics) that structured Avicenna's encyclopedic works. Instead, the emphasis falls entirely on the study of being (*wujūd*), the subject of the *Asfār*'s first book.

The *Asfār* also differs structurally from Suhrawardī's *Philosophy of Illumination*. Ṣadrā rejects a number of specific Illuminationist doctrines, especially on the question of the primacy of quiddity vs. the primacy of being (see below), while maintaining the Illuminationist epistemological framework and cosmological outlook.

### The Four Thematic Domains

Ziai identifies four interlocking domains within *al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya*:

**a) Epistemology:** "Primary intuition" (*ishrāq*, knowledge by presence) replaces Aristotelian definition (*horos/ḥadd al-tāmm*) as the foundation of science. Knowledge by presence (*al-ʿilm al-ḥuḍūrī*) is held to be prior to predicative knowledge (*al-ʿilm al-ḥuṣūlī*). Ṣadrā dispenses with the Active Intellect as the tenth intellect of a numbered discrete cosmology — following the Illuminationist position — in favor of a multiplicity of intellects that form a continuum. This generates his famous theory of the "unity of knower and known" (*ittiḥād al-ʿāqil wa al-maʿqūl*), "perhaps the most discussed theory in all recent philosophical writings in Arabic and Persian." (Ziai, ch. 19)

**b) Ontology:** Ṣadrā endorses the "primacy of being" (*aṣālat al-wujūd*) against the Illuminationist "primacy of quiddity" (*aṣālat al-māhiyya*). The division of metaphysics into *metaphysica generalis* (study of pure being) and *metaphysica specialis* (study of qualified being), established by the Illuminationists, is incorporated and refined. Ṣadrā's distinctive contribution is the doctrine of "substantial motion" (*ḥaraka jawhariyya*) — the view that change operates at the level of substance itself, not merely accidents — which gives his ontology a dynamic character not found in Avicenna.

**c) Science and Religion:** Ṣadrā's theory of knowledge is more Illuminationist than Peripatetic: knowledge is grounded in the knowing subject's self-consciousness (*al-ʿanāʾiyya al-mutaʿāliya*) rather than in sensation and abstraction of universals. The knowing "I" recovers primary notions of time-space intuitively and confirms the existence of God through self-consciousness. This makes the system "more congenial to religious doctrine" and "paves the way for the triumph of *al-ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya* in the scholastic Shīʿite centers of Iran." (Ziai, ch. 19)

**d) History of Philosophy:** Ṣadrā provides a systematic classification of philosophical schools — Pythagoreans and Platonists; earlier Peripatetics; later Peripatetics (including al-Fārābī and Avicenna); Illuminationists (whom he associates with the Stoics). This classification framework, drawing on Shahrazūrī's earlier philosophical history, becomes standard in the later tradition.

## Theory of Causality: A Case Study

Ziai examines in detail Ṣadrā's discussion of "priority in terms of Truth" (*taqaddum bi-al-ḥaqq*) from the *Taʿlīqāt ʿalā Sharḥ ḥikmat al-ishrāq* (Glosses on the Commentary on the Philosophy of Illumination), a set of glosses on Quṭb al-Dīn Shīrāzī's commentary on a Suhrawardian work. Ṣadrā argues that beyond the five famous types of priority (as enumerated by Aristotle in the *Categories*), there is a further type: the priority of the ranks of being emanating from "the First." This cannot be captured by any standard causal notion (essential, natural, mathematical, or temporal) and can only be known by the subject's immediate understanding of "truth" (ḥaqq) — that is, by knowledge by presence.

Ziai notes a parallel with Hume's rejection of rationalist causality — Ṣadrā too argues that there is no logical or metaphysical relationship between cause and effect — but distinguishes Ṣadrā's position as more realist than Hume's: "S.adrā does accept 'real priority' (*taqaddum bi-al-ḥaqīqa*), which he states to be priority of a thing over that which is existent because of it." (Ziai, ch. 19)

## Political and Religious Implications

The epistemological priority of "knowledge by presence" in *al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya* has political consequences that extend to the present. If universal propositions (Avicennan "laws of science") must be "always true" — but the contingency of the future means that any law may be overturned by future exceptions — then the foundation of knowledge must be renewed in each era by "living sages" with heightened intuitive faculties. In Shīʿite political philosophy, this role has been assigned to the most learned Shīʿite jurist (*marjaʿ al-taqlīd* — "source of imitation") of each age.

> "If we ponder the impact of Ṣadrā's system on Shīʿite political doctrine, we may fathom how intellectual Shīʿism, as the dominant recent trend in philosophy, has embraced the primacy of practical reason over theoretical science, especially in the last century." (Ziai, ch. 19)

This is the philosophical grounding of the doctrine of *wilāyat al-faqīh* (governance of the jurist), which underpins the political theology of the Islamic Republic of Iran.

## Relationship to Illuminationism and Avicennism

*Al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya* stands at the junction of three traditions:

- **[[avicennism]]**: Provides the technical philosophical language, the Peripatetic framework, and the problems (essence-existence distinction, modalities of being, Necessary Being)
- **[[illuminationism]]**: Provides the epistemological framework (knowledge by presence prior to predicative knowledge), the cosmological vision (continuity of intellects, mundus imaginalis), and the division of metaphysics into general and special
- **Shīʿite rational theology**: Provides the religious commitment that the system is designed to harmonize with philosophy

Ṣadrā's synthesis does not simply juxtapose these traditions. He adjudicates between Avicennan and Illuminationist positions in detail, "sometimes providing a third, more refined position." (Ziai, ch. 19)

## Continuity to the Present

The *Asfār* remains the capstone text of the "intellectual sciences" (*ʿulūm-e ʿaqlī*) curriculum in Iranian Shīʿite seminaries. Higher study of philosophy in the hawza (seminary) system proceeds through standard textbooks (notably Athīr al-Dīn al-Abharī's *Hidāya al-ḥikma* with its numerous commentaries) to Ṣadrā's *Asfār*. The twentieth-century Shīʿite philosopher Seyyed Jalāl al-Dīn Āshtiyānī continued and extended the Ṣadrān tradition in both philosophical and editorial work.

## Precursor: Ṣāʾin al-Dīn and the Harmony of Philosophy and Religion

Ziai identifies Ibn Torkeh ʿAlī b. Muḥammad Khojandī Iṣfahānī (d. c. 1432), known as Ṣāʾin al-Dīn, as an important precursor who "did much to recover the idea of the harmony between religion and philosophy, as well as mysticism." Ṣāʾin al-Dīn's *Tamhīd al-qawāʿid* — a work on phenomenology and philosophy of religion — was widely studied by seventeenth-century Shīʿite scholars and contributed to the acceptance of "intellectual sciences" within clerical culture. He is the first figure identified in the source as drawing systematically on Illuminationist epistemology (knowledge by presence) to give priority to intuitive and inspired knowledge in the grounding of religion. See [[ibn-torkeh-isfahani]].

## The Liar Paradox and Sixteenth-Century Logic

Among the specific philosophical problems Ziai documents in the later tradition is a debate over the liar paradox (*shubha kull kalāmī kādhib* — "all of my statements are false") in sixteenth-century Shīrāz between Ṣadr al-Dīn Dashtakī and Jalāl al-Dīn Dawwānī. Dashtakī, anticipating modern metalinguistic analysis, distinguished between first- and second-order truth and falsity of the proposition and insisted on the distinction between natural language and meta-language. This debate shows that the scholastic tradition of glosses and super-glosses could generate genuinely analytical philosophical innovation. It is documented in the journal *Kherad-nāmeh-ye Mullā Ṣadrā* and discussed in Ziai, ch. 19.

## Historiography

Hossein Ziai's chapter (ch. 19 of [[adamson-taylor-cambridge-companion-arabic-philosophy-2005]]) argues explicitly against the Orientalist tendency to describe post-Avicennan Arabic and Persian philosophy using vague terms like "theosophy," "Oriental wisdom," or "mystical experience" (phrases associated with Henry Corbin's influential but misleading framework). Ziai insists on the "purely intellectual and philosophical hard core" of the tradition (citing Fazlur Rahman). The chapter provides an internal philosophical account of the *Asfār*'s doctrines, with particular attention to ontology, epistemology, and causality.

The collection's coverage of this tradition is thin: the primary resource in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina is the Adamson-Taylor Companion itself. The tradition's continuation to the present makes it a live research area inadequately served by pre-2000 scholarship.
