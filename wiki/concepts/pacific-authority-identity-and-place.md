## Definition and Scope

Mayo's "authority–identity–place" framework proposes that Pacific history — from roughly the twelfth century to the present — is best understood through the triangulated nexus of (1) concrete physical entities and processes (environments, languages, landscapes); (2) contestation and struggle between those who exercise power and those who experience it; and (3) imaginative and symbolic construction (how places and identities are projected and inhabited). The framework rejects both Eurocentric teleology and a narrowly island-focused "Sea of Islands" model, insisting instead on an "associative history" that traces linkages across the island Pacific, the Pacific coasts of the Americas, and East and Southeast Asia without imposing a false unity.

Mayo argues: "The Pacific Ocean is simultaneously a material reality, an object of contest, and an imaginative projection" — and that no single account can exhaust what the idea of "the Pacific" means in different places and times.

## Origin and Intellectual Context

Mayo situates this framework in dialogue with Epeli Hau'ofa's celebrated "Sea of Islands" vision and against the older binary of Pacific Islander vs. European contact narratives. His methodological label is "associative history": writing by association between widely separated zones rather than imposing divisions or unities. He likens this to driftwood logs washed across the Pacific by ocean currents, "forces that combine macro regularities and macro patterns with happenstance" — once made into canoes, vital to the societies that incorporated them. The framework draws on and extends previous Pacific historiography by Matsuda (*Pacific Worlds*, 2012), D'Arcy (*The People of the Sea*, 2006), and the spatial turn in cultural geography.

## Core Analytical Claims

**1. The tripartite historical domain.** Histories involve concrete processes, contestation/struggle, and imaginative engagement simultaneously. This is especially visible in institutions, which are "concrete entities whose existence is difficult to deny," sites of contestation, and structures for "acts of imagining."

**2. The twelfth-to-thirteenth-century baseline.** Mayo dates the modern Pacific system to circa 1200–1300 for three reasons: (a) Polynesian migration reached its known limits in this period, establishing Hawai'i, Rapa Nui, and Aotearoa New Zealand as distinct societies; (b) political structures foundational to present-day Fiji, Tonga, Sāmoa, and the hierarchical eastern Carolines (Kosrae, Pohnpei) consolidated; (c) the Mongol conquests in East and Southeast Asia created an interlocking system of regional entities from Siberia to eastern Indonesia. Aztec and Inca foundations also solidify in this period. These three zones form "a lattice of separate but internally interlinked societies and cultures."

**3. Authority and identity are not produced by Europeans alone.** The dominant historiographical error, in Mayo's view, is framing Pacific history primarily as what happened when Europeans arrived. Colonization and economic development are "wrongly construed as things that are uniquely or distinctively European." The pre-existing authority–identity–place nexus in Oceania has deep roots; its resilience in the face of industrial empire is "not simply a response to the effects of colonial and neo-colonial power, but is rather something much older."

**4. Mineral resources as historical connectors.** Mayo uses three case studies — Bolivia (loss of Pacific coastline after the War of the Pacific, 1879–1884; nitrates), Banaba/Ocean Island (phosphate mining, WWII Japanese occupation, displacement to Rabi in Fiji), and Haizhou in China (Jinping phosphate mine, post-Qing industrialization) — to demonstrate that the authority–identity–place nexus in disparate Pacific locales has been shaped by the same trans-Pacific commodity chains of minerals (guano, nitrate, phosphate) and industrial warfare. "An historical narrative whose central focus was phosphates would be able to plot a story of trans-Pacific linkages between zones of phosphate production."

**5. Radical asymmetries and hinterland formation.** Industrial modernity creates two broad types of places: those linked to the industrial order and those left behind. Bolivia exemplifies the "hinterland" in economic geography — "a place that has a subordinated status in relation to a port zone which is the focus of commercial activity and wealth accumulation." Banaba exemplifies an island society destroyed so that Australia and New Zealand could have chemical fertilizer for pastoral agriculture: "The lands of meat and milk that settlers of European background created for themselves… were dependent on chemical fertilizer inputs that were procured by transforming Banaba and places like it into barren wastelands." Haizhou, by contrast, is cast by Chinese nationalism and liberal modernization theory as a "success," though Mayo questions that framing.

**6. The Banaba pattern.** Banaban history is presented as paradigmatic for the Pacific: "a cultural entity possessing a strong sense of place, identity, and a set of authority relations which structure communal life, but which is subsumed into the structures of one or more larger political entities that limit its autonomy." The displacement of Banabans to Rabi, Fiji, following Japanese occupation and phosphate exhaustion, illustrates the cumulative layering of cultural modification that nonetheless fails to extinguish place-based identity.

**7. Plurality of Pacific identities.** The concept acknowledges that "dramatically different forms of Pacific identity" coexist: Polynesians for whom the ocean creates shared identity; Micronesians who share that oceanic sense; people from British Columbia, Colombia, Queensland, and Vladivostok who invoke Pacific identity primarily against inland or Atlantic-oriented counterparts; Bolivians who define themselves by Pacific exclusion. No single narrative accounts for all these simultaneously valid identities.

## Limitations and Critiques

Mayo himself anticipates key objections: (1) A "Pacific" history spanning Americas, Oceania, and East Asia is too large to be analytically tractable — better a fully global or more regionally restricted frame. (2) The starting date of ~1200 is arbitrary since the Pacific only became a zone of direct inter-regional interaction in the late eighteenth century. (3) The category "hinterlands" dilutes the oceanic focus. He answers by arguing that "associative history" is precisely a response to these objections — it does not claim a unified Pacific system but rather traces connections that are real without being total. The approach risks what he calls "the narrative equivalent of a food court, in which the juxtapositions of cultures and their histories is the result of proximities that are more-or-less arbitrary." Whether the three case studies (Bolivia, Banaba, Haizhou) are genuinely linked or merely juxtaposed remains a contested methodological question.

The framework does not address gender as a structural variable and is thin on Southeast Asian island societies beyond brief references to Islamization and Dutch/Spanish rivalry. Its engagement with the Banaban case rests heavily on Katerina Teaiwa's *Consuming Ocean Island* (2014) and Sigrah and King's *Te Rii ni Banaba* (2001).

## Historiography

Mayo positions the chapter as a contribution to the move beyond the "Pacific Islander–European dichotomy" in English-language Pacific historiography (critiquing works like Bernard Smith's *European Vision and the South Pacific* [1989] and K.R. Howe's *Where the Waves Fall* [1984]). He acknowledges that much late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century Anglophone Pacific scholarship is "characterized by a critique of imperialism and colonialism and their effects on societies across and around the Pacific" — a self-aware irony given that this critical discourse is itself a product of Anglophone cultural expansion. The framework engages with Woodside's *Lost Modernities* (2006) for the Chinese examination-elite model of "post-feudal" modernity predating European industrialism, and with Matsuda's *Pacific Worlds* (2012) for the multi-seas model. The associative history method is original to Mayo within this volume.
