# Concert of Europe

## Definition and Scope

The **Concert of Europe** is the historians' term for the informal but durable diplomatic tradition, dating from the post-Napoleonic settlement, by which the Great Powers (Britain, France, Prussia/Germany, Austria/Austria-Hungary, Russia, and later Italy) managed international crises collectively rather than allowing them to escalate to general war. It rested on no permanent institution or binding treaty of arbitration — the sovereign state remained, in principle, "the final judge of its own interests" — but on a shared expectation among Great Power statesmen that a crisis involving a lesser or non-Great-Power state should be jointly contained and settled by negotiation among the Powers (Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century*, 5th ed., ch. 1–2).

It is not to be confused with the modern machinery of binding international law (the Hague arbitration system, the League of Nations, the UN); the Concert operated through ad hoc congresses, ambassadorial conferences, and informal Great Power consultation, and states accepted its restraints only when convenient to their own interests.

## Causal Drivers

- The shared trauma and settlement logic of the post-1815 order, which sought to prevent any single power (as Napoleonic France had done) from overturning the European balance.
- The Great Powers' common interest in preventing the ambitions of any one member, or the instability of the Ottoman, Habsburg, or Russian multinational empires, from dragging all of them into war.
- The relative absence, before 1914, of rigid two-bloc alliance commitments that would force automatic Great Power involvement in a local dispute.

## Major Phases

- **Congress of Vienna (1814–15)** and subsequent congresses (Aix-la-Chapelle, Troppau, Verona) — the founding phase of collective management, aimed chiefly at containing revolutionary and nationalist upheaval.
- **Congress of Berlin (1878)** — the Great Powers, led by Bismarck's Germany, trimmed back Russia's gains from its war with the Ottoman Empire and compensated Austria-Hungary with influence over Serbia and the right to administer Bosnia-Herzegovina, defusing a Russo-Austrian confrontation over the Balkans.
- **Hague Peace Conference (1899)** — voluntary international arbitration machinery, alongside other conveniences of the era (international postal conventions, the Red Cross, agreed rules of war), accepted by states without surrendering ultimate sovereignty.
- **Localization of the Balkan Wars (1912–13)** — the Great Powers again worked, in the Concert tradition, to keep the fighting among lesser Balkan states from spreading to a general war.
- **Collapse, July 1914** — the Austro-Serbian crisis following the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand (see [[july-crisis-1914]]) was, in Paxton's analysis, "neither a Great Power confrontation, as in 1908, nor a war among lesser states, as in 1912 and 1913. This was an attempt by one Great Power, supported by another, to weaken a small neighbor" — a form of conflict the Concert's traditional mechanisms proved unable to contain, especially once Germany actively blocked British-proposed mediation in order to preserve a "localized" Austro-Serbian war.

## Geographic Spread

Confined to relations among the recognized European Great Powers, though its crisis-management logic was applied above all to containing the disintegration of the Ottoman Empire and the resulting Balkan disputes.

## Interaction

Directly related to [[new-imperialism|imperial rivalry]] (which the Concert also occasionally managed, e.g., the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911) and to the rigidifying alliance system of the [[first-world-war-1914|pre-1914 period]] (Triple Alliance vs. Franco-Russian alliance and the Entente cordiale), which is generally credited with undermining the flexibility the Concert required to function.

## End Conditions

The Concert of Europe effectively ended with the July Crisis of 1914: mediation proposals by British Foreign Secretary Sir Edward Grey were repeatedly blocked by German insistence on a "localized" war, and the crisis escalated through mobilization timetables and alliance commitments rather than negotiated Great Power settlement. See [[july-crisis-1914]] for the crisis itself and the historiographical debate over why traditional Concert diplomacy failed in 1914.
