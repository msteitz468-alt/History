# The Crusades and the Development of Islamic Art

## Definition and Scope

This concept addresses the question of whether the Crusades — the two-century Latin
presence in Syria, Palestine, and Lebanon — caused or shaped the major transformations
visible in Islamic art during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The question is
posed at multiple levels: direct formal borrowing, shared techniques, cross-cultural
iconographic exchange, and the broader "macro" climate of creative energy. Grabar's
essay resists the assumption, common in art history, that a momentous political event
automatically produces a proportional cultural impact.

## Key Arguments

**The main finding: the Crusades had surprisingly little direct influence on Islamic
art.** Grabar distinguishes between micro-effects (isolated instances of Western forms
appearing in Islamic monuments) and a possible macro-effect (an enabling impulse
generated by the Crusades that energized the broader Mediterranean world).

**What changed in Islamic art during the Crusade period — and why.** The twelfth century
witnessed revolutionary transformations in Islamic art: hundreds of new madrasahs,
caravanserais, and bazaars; the spread of the muqarnas ceiling from Iraq and Central
Asia all the way to Morocco; new metalwork techniques (silver inlaying) enabling
detailed figural decoration; the first dated illustrated Arabic manuscripts; expanded
patronage from mercantile elites. Grabar attributes these changes not to the Crusades
but to expanded patronage, the industrialization of paper production, and techniques
developed in northeastern Iran and Baghdad.

**The Crusades as an "impulse of energy, not of forms."** Grabar's most important
formulation: whatever influence the Crusades exerted was not a transfer of visual
forms but an activation of creative energy. The Latin establishment of a new focus of
power in an artistically underdeveloped region (Syria and Palestine) catalyzed adjacent
areas — a revitalized Syrian interior, Anatolia bursting with building, northern
Mesopotamia growing new cities along reestablished trade routes.

**Resistance to the diffusionist model.** Grabar explicitly rejects the assumption that
the Crusaders brought Western art forms to the East and that Islamic art adopted them.
The traffic went more in the other direction: Islamic forms appeared in Western art
far more than Western forms in Islamic art, and even those Western-to-Islamic cases
are mostly unique, explicable by special circumstances, not systematic diffusion.

**The "othering" dynamic.** By around 1250 the climate of creative interaction was
closing down. Grabar observes that "the creation of man became Muslim or Christian,
Saracenic or Infidel, ours or alien." The Crusade period, paradoxically, accelerated
the hardening of a visual boundary between Christian and Muslim culture — the "modern
age," in his phrase, began with this partition.

**Norman Sicily as the lost alternative.** The most spectacular Islamic art of the Crusade
period was produced in Norman Sicily under Roger II and William II — the Cappella
Palatina with its muqarnas ceiling, the coronation mantle (Palermo, 1133–34, Arabic
inscription, Latin form, astrological decoration). This Norman synthesis of muqarnas,
figural programs, astronomical imagery, and Arabic inscriptions represented a genuinely
ecumenical visual culture. Like the Crusades themselves, it left no posterity.

## Evidence and Examples

**Micro-effects (direct traces):**
- Cairo: the complex of Muhammad al-Nasir (ca. 1303) incorporates a Gothic portal
  brought from Acre by boat as a trophy.
- Cairo: certain features of the slightly earlier complex of Qala'un — exterior facade
  articulation, windows — may owe something to Western architecture, or may reflect
  forms associated with Jerusalem rather than Latin culture.
- Anatolia: the mosque and hospital at Divrigi (ca. 1229) have unusual facades with
  elaborate gates and stone sculpture in high relief that recalls late Romanesque art
  from southwestern France to east of Moscow.
- Jerusalem, Hebron, Bethlehem, Beirut, Tripoli: many examples of Crusader
  architectural elements reused in Mamluk and later Muslim monuments.
- Military architecture: Crusader fortifications demonstrably influenced citadels in
  Syria, Jordan, and Anatolia.

**The Ayyubid bronzes with Christian scenes.** The clearest case study: eighteen
surviving inlaid bronzes from northern Mesopotamia and Syria, datable to the first
half of the thirteenth century, decorated with Christian iconography. Key features:
(1) they use the same technique (silver-inlaid brass) and the same functional forms
(lighting, serving, writing, washing) as standard aristocratic Islamic metalwork;
(2) their inscriptions are generic good-wishes rather than dedications to Christian
patrons; (3) the Christian scenes derive from local Syriac and possibly Byzantine
sources, not from Western art; (4) they disappear after ca. 1250, probably due to a
revival of aniconism. Grabar interprets them as evidence of a "quiet pietism" — the
momentary acceptability of Christian motifs as areligious decoration within a certain
Muslim courtly stratum, possibly enabled by the political climate after Frederick II's
1229 treaty with al-Kamil.

**Odd objects and the muqarnas problem:**
- The Louvre peacock aquamanile: inscribed in both Arabic (a Christian maker's name)
  and Latin (*opus Solomonis erat*), possibly dating to 962–972 rather than the twelfth
  century — which removes the Crusade connection entirely.
- The Innsbruck Urtuqid plate (1114–42): enameled on both sides with Byzantine,
  Georgian, and Limousin parallels — evidence of traveling craftsmen serving feudal
  patrons, not of Crusade influence per se.
- The mantle of Roger II (Palermo, 1133–34): Arabic inscription, Latin form, symbolic
  decoration — part of the Norman ecumenical synthesis.
- The muqarnas: spread westward (Tinmal, Fez, the Cappella Palatina ceiling by 1142)
  just as the Latin kingdom was establishing itself. Grabar does not claim the Crusades
  caused this, but notes the temporal coincidence.

## Scholarly Significance

Grabar's essay performs a methodological check on the "Napoleon's law" assumption
(major events automatically affect culture). His conclusion — that the Crusades
mattered less as a direct cultural force than as one energizing element in a broader
twelfth-century Mediterranean creative explosion — is a corrective to triumphalist
or diffusionist art history. The essay also provides a pre-history of the modern
civilizational divide, locating its crystallization in the second half of the thirteenth
century rather than in the original Crusade encounter. This makes the Crusade period
not the birth of the clash of civilizations but, for a moment, its opposite: a shared
Mediterranean world that failed to reproduce itself.
