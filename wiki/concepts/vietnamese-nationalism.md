# Vietnamese Nationalism

Vietnamese nationalism in the modern era was never a single coherent ideology or movement. Goscha and other scholars distinguish at least two major competing streams with different intellectual genealogies, social bases, and visions of the state: an Atlantic-republican current (drawing on French revolutionary, American, and constitutionalist models) and a Marxist-Leninist current (adapted from the Soviet and Chinese revolutions and channeled through the Indochinese Communist Party / Vietnamese Workers' Party).

## Two Streams

**Atlantic-republican nationalism**

- Early exemplars: Phan Chu Trinh and other reformers who petitioned at Versailles (1919) in a republican idiom, demanded constitutional government, and looked to French and Western models of representative institutions.
- High point: the 1946 constitution of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. This document, drafted by a fragile multiparty coalition under Ho Chi Minh, enshrined universal suffrage, an independent National Assembly with real legislative power, an independent judiciary, freedoms of speech/press/association, and protections for private property. It was "an entirely Vietnamese initiative" and "a milestone in Vietnamese political theory." For a brief moment in 1945–46 a "fragile democratic state was born."
- Later echoes: non-communist republicans, southern political parties, Buddhist activists, and post-1975 dissidents who have invoked the 1946 framework against single-party authoritarianism. The 1946 constitution remained a reference point for those arguing that representative government was an indigenous Vietnamese aspiration, not a foreign imposition.

**Marxist-Leninist / communist nationalism**

- Emerged from the frustrations of colonial reformism. Ho Chi Minh's own trajectory (Paris cook, 1919 republican petition, 1920 adherence to the French Communist Party at Tours, Comintern work in Moscow and Guangzhou) illustrates the shift: colonial experience convinced many that republican reform within the French empire was impossible; only revolution modeled on Lenin and later Mao offered a path to genuine independence and social transformation.
- Institutionalized after 1950 with Chinese aid and the shift from the broad Viet Minh front to explicit party control. The 1960 constitution (and party statutes) subordinated the state to the Vietnamese Workers' Party (VWP, later Communist Party of Vietnam), establishing the single-party framework that has persisted.
- Core features: democratic centralism, class analysis (initially subordinated to national liberation), land reform and collectivization as nation-building tools, and internationalist solidarity with the socialist camp.

The two streams clashed repeatedly over the form of the independent state: coalition/republican versus vanguard-party centralism.

## Key Moments of Contestation

- 1919–1930s: republican reformers versus emerging communists and radical nationalists (Yen Bay 1930 VNQDD uprising crushed).
- 1945–1946: the August Revolution and 1946 constitution represented the high-water mark of the republican-pluralist possibility; communists participated in coalition but viewed it tactically.
- Post-1950: with Chinese military and political support, the communist stream consolidated control in the north; non-communist and republican elements were sidelined or eliminated.
- 1955–1975: the RVN under Diem and successors embodied (in distorted form) an anti-communist republican claim, while the NLF/DRV presented the Marxist-Leninist path as the authentic national revolution.
- Post-1975 and especially after Đổi Mới (1986): renewed calls, inside and outside the country, for a return to elements of the 1946 constitutional vision (multi-party or at least genuine assembly power, rule of law) alongside official insistence on the leading role of the Party.

## Relation to "Multiple Vietnams"

The nationalist cleavage mapped onto (and reinforced) regional and social pluralities. Southern and urban/coastal elites, certain religious communities, and highland groups often had different affinities than the northern Red River core that supplied much of the communist leadership. The multiplicity of polities provided space for rival nationalist projects to take institutional form.

## Historiography and Contestation

Official Vietnamese historiography presents the communist victory as the culmination of a unified national will in which "real nationalists became communists." Goscha and others note that this erases a generation of Vietnamese republicans who continued to advocate representative government. Western scholarship has moved from Cold-War-era binaries toward recognition of the internal diversity of Vietnamese nationalism(s), though debate continues over the relative weight of ideological commitment, personal networks, and structural factors in the communist triumph.

The concept remains contested: some scholars emphasize the pragmatic flexibility of the ICP/VWP (Ho's "not born Red," coalition-building skill); others stress the disciplined Leninist core that never relinquished the goal of party supremacy.
