Islamic metaphysics (*al-falsafa al-ula*, "first philosophy," or *ilm al-ilahiyyat*, "divine science") is the tradition of systematic inquiry into the most fundamental features of being — existence, unity, causation, God, and the structure of reality — developed by the Arabic-speaking Hellenizing philosophers (*falasifa*) from the 9th through the 12th centuries CE. The tradition is complex: it weaves together Aristotelianism (especially *Metaphysics* books II and XII), Neoplatonism (especially the Plotiniana Arabica and the *Liber de Causis* derived from Proclus), and original contributions — above all Avicenna's distinction between essence and existence — that mark genuine philosophical advances beyond the Greek sources.

The tradition should not be reduced either to paraphrase of Aristotle or to a "blend" of Aristotelianism and Neoplatonism. As Cristina D'Ancona has shown, the *falasifa* used Greek sources "carefully and creatively." And Richard Frank argued (1979) that *falsafa* is not immune to influence from [[kalam]] (Islamic theology), which had developed its own ontology independently; al-Ghazali himself, the famous opponent of the philosophers, is in some respects deeply influenced by Avicenna.

## Subject Matter: The Central Debate

The central historiographical and philosophical question of Arabic metaphysics is the subject matter of first philosophy: is it natural theology (the science of God) or ontology (the science of being qua being)?

Early Islamic thinkers — above all al-Kindi — read the *Metaphysics* as a form of natural theology, seeking God as the cause of existence. Al-Farabi made the decisive move of recognizing that the *Metaphysics* is primarily about being qua being and only incidentally about God. Avicenna himself describes his confusion before reading al-Farabi's treatise explaining the purpose of the *Metaphysics*: he had read it forty times without understanding, until al-Farabi's short work revealed "that the main aim was not the study of God." This moment — reconstructed by Gutas and Bertolacci from Avicenna's autobiography — marks a turning point in Islamic intellectual history.

## Al-Kindi: First Philosophy as Natural Theology

Al-Kindi (d. ca. 870), the first *faylasuf*, drew primarily on *Metaphysics* book II (exceptional in the Arabic tradition, which received it as the book's introduction) and book XII, chapters 6–10 (the Prime Mover passage). From book II he derived the thesis that the cause of "the existence and continuance of everything is the True One" — equating philosophy's aim with finding God as the cause not merely of initial creation but of the *continuous* maintenance of existence.

Al-Kindi's God, shaped by the Plotiniana Arabica, transcends Aristotle's Prime Mover: the One is beyond being and intellect, perfectly simple, uncaused, without accident or substrate. True agency — bringing existence from utter non-being — belongs to God alone; creatures are "agents" only metaphorically, either because their effects are simultaneous with them (walking) or because their effects persist after the cause ceases (house-building). This distinction between metaphorical types of agency was later used by Avicenna to differentiate physical from metaphysical causation.

Despite his Neoplatonist theology, al-Kindi defended creation *in time* against Aristotelian eternalism, appealing both to John Philoponus' arguments and to the kalam position that even non-being is a "thing." His ontology thus blended Neoplatonist transcendence with Islamic creationism.

## Al-Razi: Five Eternal Beings

Abu Bakr al-Razi (d. ca. 925) represents a deviant position within the *falsafa* tradition. Preferring Plato to Aristotle, he posited five coeternal beings: God, Soul, time, space, and matter. Originally static, this quintet was disrupted when Soul "succumbed to a passionate desire to get enmeshed in matter," introducing disorderly motion. God, being merciful, endowed Soul with intellect to recognize its mistake and organize the chaos. This metaphysics excludes creation ex nihilo and any revealed religion (revelation is always particular, therefore unjust to peoples and times it excludes). Al-Razi used his metaphysics — especially God's three main attributes of compassion, justice, and intellect — as the foundation for a normative ethics concerned with common moral values across religious diversity.

## Al-Farabi: Metaphysics as Ontology Completed by Emanation

[[al-farabi]] understood metaphysics as having three parts: (1) beings qua beings (ontology proper); (2) the principles of the theoretical sciences, including the origin of first intelligibles; and (3) beings that are neither bodies nor in bodies — immaterial substances — leading to the First or One, which gives existence, unity, and truth to all other beings. He gives one line to the first part (Aristotle had handled it adequately), a paragraph to the second, and two pages to the third — revealing where his interests and the tradition's unsolved problems lay.

Al-Farabi's signature move is completing Aristotle's inadequate metaphysics with Neoplatonic emanation. In his *Political Regime* (*al-Siyasa al-Madaniyya*) he posits six hierarchical kinds of principles: the First Cause, secondary causes (the emanated intelligences governing the celestial spheres), the Agent Intellect (governing the sublunar world), the soul, form, and matter. From the First Cause, which knows only itself, emanation proceeds eternally and necessarily: the First gives rise to the secondary intelligences, which in turn give rise to the celestial spheres and the Agent Intellect; the motions of the spheres give rise to prime matter; the Agent Intellect is a "giver of forms" (*wahib al-suwar*), emanating material and intelligible forms into the sublunar world.

Crucially, al-Farabi equates some features of Aristotle's Prime Mover with the Neoplatonic One while *distinguishing* God from the Agent Intellect — the First knows only itself (pure self-intellection), while the Agent Intellect is responsible for the sublunar world. This distinction addresses a persistent problem: how can a wholly transcendent God know or create the material world?

Al-Farabi also speaks of "first intelligibles" — principles common to all humans, not derived from experience, which serve as the ultimate premises of every science. These are not ontological claims derived from experience; their existence requires explanation through the Agent Intellect's emanation of them as conditions for the intelligibility of experience.

## Avicenna: Essence, Existence, and the Necessary Being

[[ibn-sina]] (d. 1037) produced the most influential and original Islamic metaphysics, departing from both al-Kindi and al-Farabi in significant respects while deepening the emanationist framework.

**Essence-existence distinction.** Avicenna's central metaphysical innovation is the strict distinction between the *essence* (*mahiyya*, "whatness") of a thing and its *existence* (*wujud*). A thing's essence is what it is — the content of its definition — and is in itself neither existent nor non-existent. Existence is "added" to essence as an accident (except in God, where essence and existence are identical). This allows Avicenna to analyze contingency: most things exist contingently (their essence does not include existence, so they require an external cause to exist) while God alone exists necessarily (*wajib al-wujud*, the Necessary Existent).

**Proof of the Necessary Existent.** From the contingency of everything we encounter, Avicenna argues to the existence of a Necessary Existent. If we admit the existence of even one thing, there must be a Necessary Existent grounding the chain of contingent causes; otherwise existence itself would be ungrounded. God is unique as the being whose existence is identical to its essence — pure existence without quiddity.

**Emanation refined.** Avicenna retains al-Farabi's emanationist cosmology but integrates it with the essence-existence distinction. The First Being, pure existence, thinks itself; from this self-thought emanates the first Intelligence (which thinks the First, thinks itself as necessary, and thinks itself as possible), producing successively the celestial sphere, its soul, and the next Intelligence. This tripartite intellection at each level produces the descending series of intelligences, spheres, and souls down to the Agent Intellect and the sublunar world.

**God's knowledge.** A major controversy within Avicenna's metaphysics (and the focus of al-Ghazali's attacks in the *Tahafut al-Falasifa*) is how God can know particular things in the sublunar world if God's self-knowledge is eternal and unchanging. Avicenna maintained that God knows particulars "in a universal way" — a position al-Ghazali found insufficient.

**World eternity vs. creation.** Avicenna maintained the Aristotelian-Neoplatonist position that the world is eternal (since an eternal God necessarily and eternally produces its effect), while firmly insisting that the world is existentially dependent on God at every moment (contingent, not necessary). This is "eternal creation" — not creation at a moment in time, but continuous dependence on the Necessary Existent. Al-Ghazali attacked all three positions: eternal world, God's knowledge of particulars, and bodily resurrection.

## Averroes: Return to Aristotelian Orthodoxy

[[ibn-rushd]] (d. 1198) sought to purify Arabic metaphysics of its Neoplatonist accretions and return to what he took to be genuine Aristotelianism. He criticized al-Farabi's and Avicenna's emanationism as an illegitimate importation of Neoplatonism, and questioned Avicenna's essence-existence distinction. His Long Commentary on the *Metaphysics* (*Tafsir ma ba'd al-tabi'a*) is the primary text. Averroes insisted on the eternity of the world on Aristotelian grounds and rejected the falasifa who "claim that there is one philosophical truth reflected in a plurality of simultaneously true religions" — though he was himself committed to some version of the double-truth problem.

Averroes also revised the emanationist account of causation, arguing that Avicenna's emanationism owed too much to the Neoplatonist tradition rather than Aristotle's own considered view of efficient causation.

## The Harmonization Problem

A running theme in Arabic metaphysics — shared from al-Farabi to Averroes — is the effort to harmonize Plato and Aristotle. Al-Farabi's text *The Harmonization of the Two Opinions of the Two Sages: Plato the Divine and Aristotle* illustrates the "old Alexandrian tradition" that profoundly influenced the *falasifa*: the claim that Plato and Aristotle ultimately agree, differing only in the paths they take toward the same truths. This harmonizing impulse drove the creative synthesis of Aristotelianism and Neoplatonism that characterized Arabic metaphysics as a whole.

## Relation to Kalam

Richard Frank argued that *falsafa* absorbed kalam ontological categories despite presenting itself as purely philosophical. Al-Kindi in particular adapted Mu'tazilite positions on divine attributes and creation (defending creation in time, engaging with the kalam view that non-being is a "thing"). Avicenna's essence-existence distinction may owe something to kalam discussions of accidents and existence. The border between Islamic theology and Islamic philosophy in matters of ontology is more porous than the *falasifa* themselves acknowledged.

## Historiography

Therese-Anne Druart's chapter in Adamson and Taylor's *Cambridge Companion to Arabic Philosophy* (2005) provides the most recent systematic overview. The foundational problem of Arabic metaphysics — the subject matter of first philosophy and the relation between ontology and natural theology — has been studied by Amos Bertolacci (*The Reception of Aristotle's Metaphysics in Avicenna's Kitab al-Shifa'*, 2006) and Cristina D'Ancona. Muhsin Mahdi's interpretation of al-Farabi as concealing a purely philosophical, non-theological metaphysics behind emanationist language remains contested. The influence of kalam on *falsafa* ontology (Frank's thesis) is contested but increasingly accepted in qualified form.
