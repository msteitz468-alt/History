# The Federalist Papers

A series of 85 essays, published under the pseudonym "Publius" in New York newspapers between October 1787 and August 1788, arguing for ratification of the newly drafted United States Constitution. Written chiefly by [[actors/hamilton-alexander|Alexander Hamilton]] (who conceived and organized the project and wrote the majority of the essays), with substantial contributions from [[actors/madison-james|James Madison]] and a smaller number from John Jay, the essays defended the proposed federal structure against Anti-Federalist objections that it created a dangerously powerful central government at the expense of the states and individual liberty.

The essays addressed specific structural questions — the separation of powers, the presidency, the judiciary, the danger of factions (Madison's Federalist No. 10 being especially influential), and the case for union against the risks of disunion or confederal weakness — and, though written as immediate political persuasion rather than abstract theory, became the single most authoritative contemporary explanation of the Constitution's intended meaning and design. American courts, including the Supreme Court, have long treated the Federalist Papers as primary evidence of the framers' original intent in constitutional interpretation, giving the essays a continuing legal and political significance far beyond their original ratification-campaign purpose.
