## Definition

The *contribución indígena* (Indian contribution, or Indian head tax) was the post-independence Andean republican name for the colonial tribute levy on indigenous men, repackaged as a fiscal instrument compatible with republican legal frameworks. It was the central mechanism through which newly independent Andean states simultaneously acknowledged and perpetuated the colonial-era racialized distinction between Indians and non-Indians. The term encodes a key contradiction: it cast a compulsory racial tax as a voluntary civic "contribution."

Unlike the colonial [[mita|mita]]-and-tribute system, which had rested on a community-based collective quota tied to reciprocal obligations and corporate rights, the *contribución indígena* was an individual head tax. It stripped away the moral-reciprocal dimension of the older tribute while preserving and intensifying its fiscal extraction.

## Origin and Context

The colonial tribute had already been individualised between 1690 and 1730, when collective *tasa* payments gave way to individual *padrones de tributarios* (fiscal censuses listing tributary heads of household). The independence wars disrupted tribute collection across the Andes, but the new republics quickly restored it under a new name because they could not finance provincial bureaucracies without it.

Brooke Larson notes that "Indian tribute, the symbolic and material mainstay of the dual-republic regime already partially restored after independence, had become but a minor source of state revenue by mid-century" — yet the symbolic as much as the fiscal stakes kept it politically explosive. Peru abolished the *contribución* in 1854 under President Ramón Castilla, followed by Ecuador in 1857 and Bolivia in 1874.

## Abolition and Restoration Cycle

The abolition/restoration cycle revealed the fiscal dependency of Andean states on Indian taxation. In Peru, Castilla discovered almost immediately that guano revenues were insufficient to fund provincial bureaucracies that had subsisted on tribute revenue. He "quietly tried (and failed) to reinstate it" within two years of abolition. A subsequent regime temporarily restored it in 1866, provoking the altiplano rebellion of Huancané, which was met with a "Law of Terror" authorising the massacre and deportation of insurgent Indian communities. The tax reappeared as an emergency "war tax" in 1879 at the onset of the War of the Pacific.

In Bolivia, tribute (as the *contribución indígena*) persisted longer than in the other republics because Andean communities had turned it to their own advantage: "Bolivia sustained colonial structures of tribute, corporate landholding, and caste longer than the other republics. Andean communities and ayllus continued to manipulate colonial-customary terms of the 'tributary pact' until well into the nineteenth century." Bolivian communities used their status as tribute-paying *contribuyentes* (contributors) as legal evidence of corporate land rights — a form of strategic self-identification that worked as long as the state honoured the reciprocal dimension of the pact.

## Contestation

The concept is contested along several axes:

1. **Fiscal vs. moral economy.** Colonial tribute had carried "a deep feeling that combined personal relationships of dependency with a logic of exchanging reciprocal gifts, and with loyalties to the leadership of the *kurakas*" (Glave, Ch. 22). Republican tribute stripped away this moral economy while retaining the extraction, producing what Glave calls the critical moment when tribute became "an economic imposition, pure and simple."

2. **Tool of resistance vs. instrument of oppression.** In the Bolivian case, *contribución* payment had been deployed by indigenous communities as proof of corporate land rights and ethnic self-governance. When Bolivia abolished the tax in 1874, it simultaneously eliminated the legal basis for communal landholding — a move deliberately designed to open indigenous lands to liberal privatisation.

3. **Race and citizenship.** The *contribución indígena* made race the legal basis of a discriminatory fiscal category in republics that formally proclaimed individual equality. As Larson observes, this double standard — "hurled into the market, but shut out of the political system" — was Colombia's and Peru's fundamental contradiction of republican citizenship.
