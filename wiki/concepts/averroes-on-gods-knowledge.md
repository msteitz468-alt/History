## Definition and Scope

Averroes' treatment of God's knowledge occupies Discussions 12 and 13 of the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*, his point-by-point response to [[al-ghazali]]'s *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*. The two discussions address distinct but related charges: (12) that the philosophers cannot demonstrate that God knows Himself at all; and (13) that the philosophers are forced to say God is ignorant of particular, temporally-indexed individuals and events. Averroes defends the philosophical tradition while also criticising the specific formulation offered by [[ibn-sina]] (Avicenna), whom he regards as having introduced confusions that made al-Ghazālī's attacks possible.

This page covers Averroes' own positive doctrine on divine knowledge as reconstructed across these two discussions. It belongs to the broader cluster of [[islamic-metaphysics]] and connects directly to the controversy page [[god-knowledge-of-particulars]].

## Background: Al-Ghazālī's Charges

### Discussion 12 — God's self-knowledge

Al-Ghazālī argues that if the First Principle acts not by will but by necessity — as the philosophers claim — there is no basis for attributing self-knowledge to it. He holds that the theologians derive God's self-knowledge from His will (God wills the world → God knows His will → God is alive → every living being knows itself). Without will, the chain collapses. He further charges that the philosophers' claim that "everything free from matter is intellect by itself" is an *arbitrary assertion without proof*.

### Discussion 13 — Knowledge of particulars

Al-Ghazālī expounds Avicenna's position: God knows particulars "in a universal way" — He knows the eclipse, its causes, and all its temporal attributes, but not *that it is happening now* or *that it has now passed*, since such indexical knowledge would require change in the Divine essence as the object changes. Al-Ghazālī then attempts to show this position is both philosophically incoherent (it cannot consistently be held alongside admitting universal knowledge) and religiously unacceptable (it entails God cannot know whether Muḥammad proclaimed himself a prophet at a definite time).

---

## Averroes' Response on Self-Knowledge (Discussion 12)

Averroes rejects al-Ghazālī's theological argument as merely persuasive (dialectical), not demonstrative. He argues that the inference from *temporal becoming of the world* → *will* → *knowledge* is itself unproven: natural things can come into being through nature, will, or chance, not will alone.

More positively, Averroes affirms:

> "Since they ascertained by proofs that God thinks only Himself, His essence must of necessity be intellect. And as intellect, in so far as it is intellect, can only be attached to what exists … it was necessary that His intellect should be attached to them."

The key move is to distinguish God's knowledge from human knowledge structurally: **God's knowledge is the *cause* of existents; human knowledge is their *effect***. This asymmetry means God's knowing Himself *is* the thinking of all existents, because all existents are contained in His essence as their source:

> "In so far as God does not think other things as being other than Himself His essence is not passive knowledge, and in so far as He thinks them as being identical with His essence, His essence is active knowledge."

Averroes also endorses the argument from life: since some principles possess knowledge and others do not, the most noble principle must possess the most noble attribute, which is knowledge. The First Principle's superiority to what sees and hears lies not in lacking perception but in possessing a perception *superior* to sight and hearing, namely knowledge — and nothing is superior to knowledge.

On al-Ghazālī's charge that "everything free from matter thinks itself" is merely assumed, Averroes acknowledges the proof loses force when removed from its proper demonstrative context, but insists the principle holds within the full body of philosophical science.

## Averroes' Response on Knowledge of Particulars (Discussion 13)

### The problem stated

Al-Ghazālī's eclipse example crystallises the difficulty: knowing that an eclipse *will occur*, that it *is occurring*, and that it *has occurred* require three numerically distinct pieces of knowledge, each involving a different temporal relation. If God's knowledge must track the object of knowledge, and the object changes, God changes — which is impossible. Avicenna's solution (knowledge "universal in mode") merely defers the problem.

### Averroes' positive resolution

Averroes describes the philosophers' summary position:

> "The most competent philosophers therefore do not call God's knowledge of existents either universal or individual, for knowledge which implies the concepts of universal and individual is a passive intellect and an effect, whereas the First Intellect is pure act and a cause."

The key distinction: **human knowledge is passive** (the world impresses itself on the knower; changes in the world change the knowledge); **divine knowledge is active** (it is the cause of what exists, not caused by it). Therefore the categories of "universal" and "individual" as applied to human cognitive modes simply do not apply to divine knowledge. The term *knowledge* is predicated of God and humans **equivocally** — in a way structurally analogous to the equivocal predication of *existence* across the First Cause and effects.

Averroes then attacks al-Ghazālī's internal argument: al-Ghazālī claims that if God can have unified knowledge of all *species and genera* (which are indefinitely many and radically different from one another), He ought also to be able to have unified knowledge of an individual across three temporal moments. Averroes accepts the analogy's direction but rejects its conclusion: the diversity between genera and species *does* cause a real plurality in knowledge of universals, which is precisely why the best philosophers refuse to attribute either mode to the First Intellect.

### The Sufi convergence

Averroes notes a convergence with mystical tradition: the ancient philosophers' position — that God is "the totality of the existents which He bestows on us in His bounty" — resonates with what "the chiefs of the Sufis say: there is no reality besides Him." He explicitly marks this as esoteric knowledge that "must not be written down and it must not be made an obligation of faith." This passage represents one of the rare moments in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* where Averroes gestures toward a convergence of philosophical and mystical theology.

### Against Avicenna's "universal mode" formula

Averroes distances himself from Avicenna's specific formulation. He considers Avicenna's solution to remain within the framework of human-analogue knowledge (just "universalized"), which reproduces the problem it was meant to solve. Genuinely divine knowledge is neither universal nor particular — it is a third mode that Averroes admits cannot be fully characterised within human conceptual resources.

## Relation to the Philosophers' Broader Cosmology

In Discussion 13, Averroes also characterises the Avicennan picture of how God's knowledge relates to cosmic order: God knows the celestial system He has produced — the spheres, their movements, the separate intellects (*angels*) that move them, the causal chain descending to the sublunary world — all in one eternal, unified act of intellection:

> "God knows that the sun and moon move in circles … and nothing of this escapes God's knowledge. However, God's knowledge before, during, and after the eclipse is all of one kind without any differentiation and without any implication of a change in His essence."

This eternal, comprehensive, non-temporal cognition is what the Avicennan tradition calls divine knowledge of particulars "in a universal way." Averroes accepts the structural intention while rejecting the specific vocabulary as misleading.

---

## Relation to Averroes' Critique of Avicenna

Across both discussions, Averroes distinguishes his own view from Avicenna's at several points. He regards Avicenna as having introduced unnecessary and erroneous elements into the Aristotelian framework, particularly:
- The doctrine that existence is an attribute *additional* to essence (even in composites), which makes the unity of quiddity and existence in the First Principle puzzling where it should be obvious.
- The "necessary through itself / through another" distinction, which Averroes calls "superfluous and erroneous."
- The theory that particular knowledge of the world is mediated through Heavenly Souls' representations of individual events (treated in Discussion 16; see [[averroes-on-the-heavens]]).

These divergences make Averroes' response to al-Ghazālī more than a defence: it is simultaneously a correction of the philosopher al-Ghazālī claimed to refute.

## Historiography

The problem of divine knowledge of particulars is among the most intensively studied in medieval Islamic and Jewish philosophy. Al-Ghazālī's formulation — that the philosophers make God ignorant of individuals — became the canonical statement of the theological charge. Avicenna's response, and the inadequacy Averroes diagnoses in it, shaped subsequent discussions through Moses Maimonides and into the Latin scholastic tradition (via Michael Scot's translation of the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* in the 13th century).

Modern scholarship (Davidson, Kogan, Leaman) debates whether Averroes has a fully coherent alternative to Avicenna or whether his position amounts to an *apophatic* refusal: divine knowledge is neither universal nor particular, and no positive characterisation is available. The Sufi convergence passage has attracted attention from historians of Islamic mysticism as evidence of Averroes' willingness to acknowledge limits of philosophical language.
