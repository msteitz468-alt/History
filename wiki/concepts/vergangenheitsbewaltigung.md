## Definition and Scope

**Vergangenheitsbewältigung** (roughly: "working through," "coming to terms with," or "mastering" the past) denotes the ongoing, deeply contested process by which German society — primarily but not exclusively West Germany — has confronted, memorialized, and drawn legal, political, and moral consequences from the Nazi period, the Holocaust, and the Second World War. The term is both an emic concept (used self-reflexively by Germans debating their obligations to the past) and an etic analytical category (used by scholars to compare different national memory cultures).

The term is contested because "mastering" (Bewältigung) implies a completion that critics argue is impossible — the Holocaust cannot be "mastered" or put behind. Alternative terms — *Aufarbeitung* (working through), *Erinnerungskultur* (culture of memory), *Gedenkkultur* (commemoration culture) — reflect different assumptions about what the process aims at and whether it can succeed.

## Distinction from GDR Memory Culture

The West German process is intelligible only against the GDR's fundamentally different approach. The GDR's founding ideology was **anti-fascism**: the claim that the socialist state's very creation represented a categorical break with the Nazi past. East German citizens were, in this framework, the victims or resisters of fascism — not the heirs of its perpetrators. This displaced serious confrontation with the Holocaust, reduced Jewish victimhood to a subcategory of anti-fascist struggle, and rendered the Mitscherlich diagnosis (see below) formally inapplicable to the East. In practice, the GDR suppressed Holocaust memory as irrelevant to socialist identity construction, and early anti-Semitic episodes in the 1950s (a brief period of requiring Jewish registration) exposed the limits of official anti-fascism. West Germany's contested, painful, and ultimately deeper engagement with the Nazi past stands in direct contrast to this.

## Major Phases and Milestones

**Phase 1 (1945–1958): Displacement and Denazification.** Allied denazification trials removed the most obviously implicated Nazis from public positions, but the process was rapidly wound down as the Cold War made West German rearmament a priority. The Adenauer government's personnel included former Nazi lawyers, diplomats, and officials — most controversially Hans Globke, Adenauer's State Secretary, who had helped draft the commentary on the Nuremberg Laws. The dominant cultural tendency, articulated by the Mitscherlichs (1967, looking back), was flight into prosperity rather than mourning. The 1950s Heimatfilm, idealized rural settings, and the cult of the Wirtschaftswunder substituted comfort for confrontation. Paul Celan's "Death Fugue" (1948) was a remarkable exception — the artistic norm was avoidance.

**Phase 2 (1958–1968): Legal Confrontation.** The institutional framework for systematic prosecution of Nazi crimes was established in this period. The Central Office for the Investigation of National Socialist Crimes (Zentrale Stelle der Landesjustizverwaltungen) was founded in Ludwigsburg in 1958, enabling coordinated prosecution. The **Eichmann Trial** in Jerusalem (1961), though an Israeli court, brought the extermination program into global and German consciousness as a deliberate, bureaucratically organized project of genocide. Hannah Arendt's "Eichmann in Jerusalem" and her "banality of evil" concept shaped the reception. The **Auschwitz Trials** in Frankfurt (1963–1965), prosecuting Auschwitz guards under German law, brought the machinery of industrial killing before German courts and German public attention for the first time systematically. Peter Weiss's play *The Investigation* (1965) restaged the Auschwitz trial testimony. Yet serious, widespread German confrontation with the Holocaust was still blocked: the mass German public acknowledgment of Holocaust guilt was not yet present.

**Phase 3 (1968–1979): Generational Confrontation.** The [[the-1968-generation-germany]] forced the confrontation that the parent generation had evaded. Young Germans demanded accounting: how had their parents participated in, tolerated, or ignored the crimes? The generational dynamic — children confronting parents — gave Vergangenheitsbewältigung its emotional charge and social intensity. The Mitscherlichs' *The Inability to Mourn* (1967) provided the psychoanalytic vocabulary. Literature (Böll, Grass, Wolf) probed the Nazi inheritance. The statutory limitations debate — would the statute of limitations on murder apply to Nazi crimes? — produced significant political controversy, and was ultimately resolved in favor of no limitations.

A paradox: the 1960s cultural confrontation with the past, however genuine, was largely limited to educated elites. Mass cultural confrontation came later.

**Phase 4 (1979–1990): Mass Culture and the Holocaust.** The turning point for mass public confrontation was the American NBC miniseries *Holocaust*, broadcast in West Germany in January 1979. As chapter 31 of Smith observes, no other cultural artifact before it had "caused Germans of all ages and social strata to face the horrors of the death camps." High-brow critics decried the serialized melodramatic format; but its effect was unprecedented and arguably more democratizing than thirty years of literary confrontation had been. The program triggered a mass outpouring of formerly repressed memories.

The **Historians' Dispute (Historikerstreit, 1986)** crystallized the political stakes of Vergangenheitsbewältigung. Ernst Nolte's suggestion that the Holocaust could be understood as a reaction to (and copy of) Soviet Stalinist terror, and that German national memory should relativize Nazi crimes in a comparative framework, was attacked by Jürgen Habermas as a self-serving normalization that threatened constitutional patriotism's foundations. The debate was not resolved but defined the parameters of legitimate historiographical discourse.

**Phase 5 (1990–present): Institutionalized Memory Culture.** Unified Germany inherited and deepened West German memory culture. Major milestones: the Holocaust Memorial (Denkmal für die ermordeten Juden Europas) in Berlin, opened 2005; Germany's extensive network of memorials, documentation centers, and school curricula on the Holocaust; the Jewish Museum Berlin. The concept of Erinnerungskultur (memory culture) and Gedenkstättenarbeit (memorial site work) became institutionalized features of German political culture. Simultaneously, controversies continued: the Walser-Bubis debate (1998) over whether the Holocaust should remain central to German identity vs. serving merely as a "moral cudgel"; the Daniel Goldhagen controversy (*Hitler's Willing Executioners*, 1996); the Wehrmacht Exhibition controversy over ordinary soldiers' complicity.

## Emic vs. Etic Dimensions

As an emic term, Vergangenheitsbewältigung implies a moral obligation and a process with evaluable stages. West Germans debated whether their society was doing it adequately, whether it was possible, and what form it should take.

As an etic analytical category, it has been applied comparatively: Japan's far weaker confrontation with wartime crimes (no equivalent process); South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission; post-communist Eastern European memory politics. Germany's process, despite its incompleteness and continued controversies, is generally considered the most sustained national confrontation with mass atrocity in recorded history.

## Limitations and Critiques

- **Completion impossibility:** The Holocaust cannot be "mastered." The process is ongoing and generationally renewed, not completed.
- **Instrumentalization:** Vergangenheitsbewältigung can become performative — ritualized memorial observance that substitutes for genuine accountability. The Walser critique: excessive focus on the Holocaust as a defining national identity marker may crowd out other identity possibilities.
- **East German deficit:** The GDR conducted no genuine Vergangenheitsbewältigung; the anti-fascist founding myth displaced the process. Post-1990 Germany inherited significant deficits in eastern Germans' Holocaust education and memory culture.
- **Migrant exclusion:** As Şenocak observed, immigrants cannot access a history that is framed as ethnic collective memory. Vergangenheitsbewältigung as practiced can exclude the very minorities it ostensibly protects. See [[german-multiculturalism]].

## Historiography

The historiography of Vergangenheitsbewältigung is itself a major field. Key texts: Alexander and Margarete Mitscherlich, *Die Unfähigkeit zu trauern* (1967); Jeffrey Herf, *Divided Memory* (1997) — contrasting West and East German memory cultures; Charles Maier, *The Unmasterable Past* (1988) — on the Historians' Dispute; Jan-Werner Müller, *A Dangerous Mind: Carl Schmitt in Post-War European Thought* (2003); Aleida Assmann's extensive work on cultural memory. From the source, Lutz Koepnick's chapter 31 in Smith provides the aesthetic/cultural history angle.

**Source coverage:** [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]] chapters 28 and 31 provide treatment. Chapter 28 covers the 1968 generational confrontation; chapter 31 (Culture in the Shadow of Trauma) analyzes postwar cultural responses to Auschwitz in depth. The collection is strong on intellectual and cultural history of the FRG.
