## Definition and Scope

Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) — also termed Indigenous Ecological Knowledge (IEK) or Aboriginal Ecological Knowledge (AEK) — is a cumulative body of knowledge, practice, and belief, evolving by adaptive processes and handed down through generations by cultural transmission, about the relationship of living beings (including humans) with one another and with their environment (Marshall, cited in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 6). It is inseparable from cultural law, language, ceremonial life, and cosmology. The concept is explicitly distinguished from "unchanging" tradition: ecological environments evolve, and so must the knowledge systems that track them.

The chapter in this source is authored by Chels Alby Marshall, a Gumbaynggirr scholar from the mid-north coast of New South Wales, writing from both an insider cultural position and academic ecology. This dual standpoint is itself constitutive of the chapter's epistemological argument.

## Core Components

Marshall identifies three nested layers in any TEK system (following Berkes):

1. **Observational knowledge** — local, long-term empirical observation of species behaviour, phenology, atmospheric indicators, and landscape dynamics.
2. **Practice** — the way communities carry out resource use: firestick farming, rotational harvesting, multi-species management, landscape patch management.
3. **Belief** — how people fit into or relate to ecosystems; the cosmological framework that gives law and practice their authority.

"Traditional knowledge is a knowledge–practice–belief complex" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 6, citing Berkes).

The graphic representation in Figure 6.1 (Marshall, ibid.) identifies a "Human Realm of Eco-centric Consciousness" as the integrating layer connecting mental/spiritual, behavioural, and technological dimensions of knowledge.

## Australian Aboriginal Case

### Depth of time

Human occupation of Australia extends at least 65,000 BP (Madjedbebe Rock Shelter excavations; Clarkson et al., Nature, 2017). Continuous ecological knowledge from this deep past is encoded in oral traditions, law, and songlines. "The academy is now increasingly acknowledging over 3,000 generations of Aboriginal understandings of history and the environment as both insightful and needing to be understood within their own terms" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 6).

Creation stories, dreaming trails, and songlines carry embedded geological and climatic data: changes in sea level, megafauna interactions, species extinctions. For example, a Gumbaynggirr story from the north coast of New South Wales describes a time of sea-level rise and animal movement — oral transmission of physical events occurring after the last glacial maximum.

### Megafauna knowledge

Archaeologists now confirm that megafauna roamed Australia until at least 46,000 BP, well after human settlement. Archaeological site Cuddie Springs (NSW) provides evidence of both human tools and megafauna bones, suggesting coexistence for 13,500–17,000 years. Aboriginal oral histories of giant kangaroos (nunguu mirral), enormous fish, giant frogs (tiddalick), and the rainbow serpent encode ecological memory of this coexistence. The cause of megafauna extinction remains debated: Aboriginal burning practices altering trophic dynamics, climatic drying from 46,000 BP, or a combination.

### Language and ecological knowledge

"Language is embedded in the landscape and is inseparable from the connection, kinship, ceremony, and law of country" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 6). At European contact, more than 250 Aboriginal languages existed in Australia, each encoding fine-grained ecological distinctions of a particular country. Colonial dispossession caused catastrophic language loss, simultaneously destroying ecological knowledge systems.

### Fire management (firestick farming)

The deliberate and controlled use of fire is identified as "the distinguishing factor in the evolution of landscapes and species across millennia of human occupation" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 6). Aboriginal fire-managed landscapes were more resistant to wildfires and more effective in water management than post-colonial equivalents. The catastrophic 2019–20 Australian bushfire season prompted government reviews which "almost uniformly concluded that bush managed by Traditional owners often fared better than other areas." The removal of Aboriginal fire management during colonization — through population collapse from disease and massacre, and deliberate suppression of cultural practices — directly contributed to landscape degradation.

### Seasonal calendars

Traditional seasonal calendars are a key applied form of TEK: integrated representations of ecological cycles, biological indicators, astronomical events, and resource management obligations. They differ fundamentally from Western Gregorian calendars in being "ecological time" rather than "structural time" — based on environmental shifts rather than fixed dates (Table 6.1, Marshall, ibid.). For example, among the Brambuk people, the appearance of the emu constellation in the Milky Way signals the spring abundance of birds' eggs and triggers major gatherings for trade, marriage arrangement, and ceremony.

## Pacific Island and Filipino Parallels

Ch. 6 draws broader Pacific analogies. In the Philippines, Visayan culture practised a lunar calendar for agriculture and fishing calibrated to moon cycles, constellations, and flowering plants. Specialist elders could predict typhoon severity and track storms several days in advance from cloud formations, sky colour, and lunar phase (Scott, Barangay, cited in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 9). Behavioural signs from animals — rats, snakes, crabs emerging from the ground, pigs and chickens fleeing Mayon volcano — were observed as hazard indicators. Pacific Island navigators similarly read flora, fauna, sea, and sky for storm prediction at sea.

## Distinction from Western Science

"Science is aware that it is only one type of knowledge among others and that knowledge is always embedded in cultural and historical settings" (Rist and Dahdouh-Guebas, cited in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 7). The same chapters argue for inter-cultural rather than assimilationist integration: TEK is local, practice-specific, and holistic, while Western science seeks broadly applicable general principles through inductive reasoning. The two are complementary rather than commensurable: TEK's strength is precisely in particulars that generalized scientific analogies often get wrong.

Key structural contrasts:
- TEK knowledge is embedded in law and kinship; Western science is institutionally separated from governance.
- TEK transmits through immersion, ceremony, and intergenerational oral practice; Western science through peer publication and replication.
- TEK is overtly eco-centric; Western environmental science has historically been anthropocentric.

## Contemporary Relevance and Threats

Climate change threatens TEK through three mechanisms: (1) species movement and phenological shifts break the ecological cues on which calendars and indicators are based; (2) habitat destruction and language loss destroy the transmission systems; (3) colonially-imposed land and sea management regimes exclude Indigenous custodians from the decision-making needed to maintain practices. "At present there is little if any recognition of these diverse and integrated components of Aboriginal climatic adaptations in nearly all current government adaptation and resilience documents" ([[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], ch. 6).

At the same time, TEK offers a proven adaptive framework tested across tens of thousands of years of climatic variability, including events comparable in scale to current projections.

## Limitations and Critiques

- "There is currently no universal definition of Indigenous ecological knowledge" — the heterogeneity of TEK systems across cultures is a fundamental feature, not a flaw, but it resists easy policy integration.
- The 'traditional' label can misleadingly imply stasis; all TEK systems are dynamic and adaptive.
- Western academia's increased interest in TEK risks tokenism or appropriation without genuine Indigenous governance authority over its application.
- Agrawal's critique (1995) warns that generalist definitions of TEK erase the distinctiveness of particular knowledge systems embedded in particular histories and places.

## Contested Status

The field is contested on epistemological grounds (whether TEK constitutes 'science' in a meaningful sense), political grounds (intellectual property, governance rights), and practical grounds (integration mechanisms with Western resource management). The chapter explicitly argues against subordinating TEK to Western scientific validation — the knowledge should stand "within its own terms."
