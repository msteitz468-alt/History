# Soldiers and Ghosts (Lendon)

Analytical and historiographical concept from J. E. Lendon’s 2005 study: the idea that Greek and Roman soldiers, officers, and commanders were powerfully motivated and constrained by the "ghosts" of earlier warriors and models of combat.

## Definition and Scope

The living "soldiers" of any generation fought (or refused to fight in certain ways) under the eyes and in the imagined presence of past heroes. These ghosts were not abstract; they were concretely present through:

- Epic poetry (Homer’s Iliad as the primary "nursery of ghosts" for Greeks)
- Ancestral custom and exempla (Roman mos maiorum)
- Famous historical battles and commanders (Thermopylae, Alexander, the heroes of the Punic Wars)
- Cultural ideals of honor, courage, and performance (timē, kleos, virtus, aemulatio)

The thesis is that this emulation/competition with the past was often a stronger driver of tactical choices, formation preferences, command aggressiveness, and willingness to accept risk than calculations of efficiency, new technology, or adaptation to current threats.

## Key Elements

- **Nursery of Ghosts**: The Iliad (and later literary/historical models) provided scripts, values, and standards against which later fighters measured themselves.
- **Aemulatio / Competitive Emulation**: Greek and Roman fighters sought to equal or surpass the performance of past models in public, witnessed action.
- **Cultural drag and channeling**: Some "obsolete" practices persisted because abandoning them would mean abandoning the ghosts and the status they conferred. Innovation occurred when it could be framed as fulfilling or improving upon the model.
- **Mismatches**: In late antiquity, the aggressive, decisive-battle culture inherited from earlier ghosts became dangerous when the army itself had become smaller, more professional, and less able to absorb catastrophic losses (e.g., context of Adrianople).

## Application and Examples (from Lendon)

- Greek hoplite warfare and its long persistence explained in part by competition to live up to Iliadic standards.
- Spartan behavior at Thermopylae and elsewhere as extreme performance of a culturally constructed ideal.
- Alexander at Issus consciously playing the role of Homeric/Achillean hero while also becoming the new ghost for successors.
- Roman preference for rapid, aggressive seeking of battle even when caution was tactically or strategically wiser (examples from Trebia through the Jewish War to the 4th century).
- Late Roman reversion toward denser, shield-wall infantry tactics interpreted as partial return toward earlier (Greek-influenced) models under the weight of cultural memory.

## Distinctions and Critiques

- Not a denial that functional considerations (weapons, terrain, numbers, logistics) mattered — Lendon acknowledges them — but an argument that cultural models heavily shaped *which* functional solutions were chosen or retained.
- Contrasts with purely technological or "progress" narratives of military history (phalanx → manipular → cohort as simple improvement stories).
- Complements but differs from "face of battle" experiential approaches (Keegan) and quantitative modeling (Sabin) by foregrounding the literary/cultural scripts soldiers carried into combat.
- The thesis is strongest for Greeks (Homer as living authority) and has been debated for its application to Roman and especially late Roman practice.
