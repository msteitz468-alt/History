# The Marian Army Reforms

The transformation of the Roman legion from a property-based citizen militia into a
**professional, recruited, general-loyal army** — traditionally credited to
**[[marius]]**, though CAH IX stresses the changes were **gradual and evolutionary**.
Analysed in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 2 (Lintott).

## What changed

Between the **manipular legion** described by Polybius (three age-and-wealth-graded
ranks — *hastati*, *principes*, *triarii* — plus *velites* and citizen cavalry) and the
army of **Caesar's** Gallic War, the legion was fundamentally remade:
- the tactical unit became the **cohort** (~480 men), replacing the maniple;
- the **age/equipment grading vanished** (the names survived only for centurions);
- **citizen light infantry and cavalry** were replaced by **allied auxiliaries**
  (Gaulish, Spanish, Numidian);
- the **eagle** became the chief standard; the *pilum* was redesigned to bend on
  impact; soldiers carried their own kit ("Marius' mules").

Most decisively, Marius enrolled the **propertyless *capite censi*** (from 107),
breaking the principle that the army's defence belonged to men with a property stake —
the same principle that justified the wealthy's dominance of the *comitia centuriata*.

## CAH IX's revision

Lintott resists the textbook picture of a single Marian revolution. The **cohort**
already appears in Spanish campaigns before 190; the last manipular ranks are in
Sallust's *Jugurtha*. The change was driven by the **recruitment crisis** (declining
*assidui*, reluctance to serve, shortage of re-enlisting veterans), the demands of
**Spanish guerrilla and German mass warfare**, and the **absence of Hellenistic
phalanxes** to fight after Pydna. Marius **completed and standardised** an evolution —
training every legionary in one repertoire — that was "as much a decline in fighting-
power as a response to new challenges."

## Significance and the contested legacy

Later historians blamed Marius for filling the army with propertyless men "ideal
material for aspirants to dictatorial power" — the seed of the warlord armies of the
civil wars. Lintott (with Brunt) cautions against hindsight: land for veterans still
required the **Senate's co-operation**, not a general's command, and the
self-seeking soldier was arguably **created by civil war**, not its cause. The
army's loyalty to generals over the state became decisive only after the Social War.
