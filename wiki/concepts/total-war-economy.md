## Definition

The specifically economic dimension of [[processes/total-war-mobilization|total war]]: the reorganization of a belligerent state's entire productive capacity — industry, agriculture, labor, and finance — around the priorities of war production, typically involving centralized planning boards, rationing of consumer goods, conversion of civilian industry to munitions manufacture, and extensive state direction of the labor force (including large-scale recruitment of women into industrial employment).

## Emic vs. Etic

Largely emic: contemporary planners and economists (in Germany, Britain, the US, and the USSR alike) explicitly designed and debated these systems as deliberate policy, using terms like "war economy" and "total mobilization" themselves.

## Historical Significance

The Battle of Britain-era German and British war economies, and the far larger American and Soviet war-production efforts of the Second World War, demonstrated how thoroughly modern industrial states could redirect output toward military ends — a capacity that also made the enemy's war economy itself a legitimate strategic target, driving the Allied strategic bombing campaigns aimed at German and Japanese industrial capacity. The disparity in war-economic capacity between the major combatants (particularly the vast productive advantage the United States and Soviet Union brought once mobilized) is widely regarded by historians as decisive to the outcome of the Second World War.
